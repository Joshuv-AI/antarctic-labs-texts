Relevance to Utility: Process-Supervised Rewrite for RAG
Jaeyoung Kim, Jongho Kim, Seung-won Hwang, Seoho Song, Young-In Song, IPAI, Seoul National University, Naver Corp {jae.young, jongh97, seungwonh}@snu.ac.kr{song.seoho, song.youngin}@navercorp.com

Abstract
Retrieval-Augmented Generation systems often suffer from a gap between optimizing retrieval relevance and generative utility: retrieved documents may be topically relevant but still lack the content needed for effective reasoning during generation. While existing ‘‘bridge’’ modules attempt to rewrite the retrieved text for better generation, we show how they fail to capture true document utility. In this work, we propose R2U, with a key distinction of directly optimizing to maximize the probability of generating a correct answer through process supervision. As such direct observation is expensive, we also propose approximating an efficient distillation pipeline by scaling the supervision from LLMs, which helps the smaller rewriter model generalize better. We evaluate our method across multiple open-domain question-answering benchmarks. The empirical results demonstrate consistent improvements over strong bridging baselines. 1 1 1 Source code: https://anonymous.4open.science/r/R2U-D547/README.md .

As direct observation is costly, existing work observes

Proposed Bridging Objective

In this section, we first formalize the role of bridge modules in RAG system, outlining where existing approaches fall short (§
2.1
). We then present our formulation of the bridging objective and how our objective captures true document utility. (§
2.2
–§
3.2
).

2.1
Motivation: Existing Bridging Models

In RAG system, the generator
\phi
produces an answer
a
given query
q
and retrieved documents
d
as follows:

a\sim P_{\phi}(a|q,d)

(1)

Previous bridging works attempted to rewrite the documents to improve their utility,
typically by reordering the documents
Pradeep et al. 2023
;
Xiao et al. 2023
, filtering out irrelevant contents
Yoon et al. 2024
;
Li et al. 2024
, or reconstructing the inputs
Chen et al. 2025
using
q
and
d
:

a\sim P_{\phi}(a|q,d^{\prime}),~\textrm{where}~d^{\prime}\sim P_{\theta}(d^{\prime}|q,d)

(2)

Here,
\theta
denotes either a fine-tuned or off-the-shelf rewriting module, and
d^{\prime}
represents the rewritten version of the documents.

We argue that such bridges,
P_{\theta}(d^{\prime}|q,d)
, cannot capture the true definition of document utility
P_{\phi}(a\mid q,d^{\prime})
, which measures whether the rewritten document effectively supports the process of
answer generation
of
a
.

2.2
Proposed: Process Supervision

The key objective of R2U is to learn a bridge model that rewrites documents to maximize their
true utility
, i.e., probability of generating a correct answer, formalized as
max_{d^{\prime}}P(a|q,d^{\prime})
.

However, this notion of true utility is inherently unobservable. To optimize it directly, one would need an ideal rewriter that samples from
P_{\theta}(d^{\prime}|q,d,a)
, i.e., a rewriter that conditions on the target answer
a
during rewriting. Training a model with direct access to
a
constitutes a shortcut that the rewriter could simply encode the answer instead of genuinely learning to support answer generation. Thus, the challenge is how to approximate this ideal but unobservable distribution using practically observable signals, without shortcuts.

Our contribution is twofold. To address the above issue, we provide a derivation (§
2.2.1
) that shows how the ideal rewrite distribution, otherwise unobservable, can be grounded in observable quantities obtained from LLM reasoning traces toward the answer.
A key prerequisite for this derivation is that rewritten documents

d^{\prime}
appear explicitly in these traces, allowing the distribution to be anchored in actual observations.

Therefore, our second contribution is to demonstrate that sufficiently robust generators, e.g., 70B or larger, already satisfy the prerequisite for this derivation. During their internal reasoning process, they naturally perform the desired rewriting where the model produced rewrites
d^{\prime}
tailored to the target answer.
In our manual inspection, this behavior occured in 95% of cases, as shown in the examples in Table

2.2.1
Formulation of R2U

This section develops the first contribution, building on the prerequisite established above. We will show that what we observed from the LLM serves as the basis from which the ideal rewrite distribution can be derived.
With the ideal distribution, we obtain the document utility that directly aligns rewritten documents with answerability and avoids shortcuts.

In practice, what we
actually
observe from the teacher LLM’s reasoning traces are joint samples,

(a,d^{\prime})\sim P_{\phi}(a,d^{\prime}\mid q,d),

(3)

where
q
is the query and
d
is the retrieved documents.
This joint reflects the LLM’s process supervision as it generates
d^{\prime}
as part of its internal reasoning toward
a
.
By the chain rule, the joint can be factorized in two equivalent ways:

\begin{split}&P_{\phi}(a,d^{\prime}\mid q,d)\\
&=P_{\phi}(d^{\prime}\mid q,d,a)\;P_{\phi}(a\mid q,d)\\
&=P_{\phi}(a\mid q,d^{\prime},d)\;P_{\phi}(d^{\prime}\mid q,d)\\
\end{split}

Under the natural
sufficiency assumption
that once the rewritten documents
d^{\prime}
are given from a sufficiently robust LLM, the original documents
d
barely provide information about
a

4
4

4

We empirically validate that the performance difference with and without
d
is negligible, as shown in Table
6
.
, which is formally

P_{\phi}(a\mid q,d^{\prime},d)\approx P_{\phi}(a\mid q,d^{\prime})
, and thus:

P_{\phi}(a,d^{\prime}\mid q,d)\approx P_{\phi}(a\mid q,d^{\prime})\;P_{\phi}(d^{\prime}\mid q,d)

(4)

From this identity, the ideal rewrite distribution,
P_{\phi}(d^{\prime}\mid q,d,a)
, can be written as:

P_{\phi}(d^{\prime}\mid q,d,a)=\frac{P_{\phi}(a\mid q,d^{\prime})\;P_{\phi}(d^{\prime}\mid q,d)}{P_{\phi}(a\mid q,d)}

(5)

which describes how the LLM rewrites documents when aiming to produce an answer
a
.

Thus, when we sample
d^{\prime}
from the LLM’s reasoning traces, we are implicitly sampling from a distribution close to

P_{\phi}(d^{\prime}\mid q,d,a)
. Feeding such
d^{\prime}
back into the generator yields the bridge process:

a\sim P_{\phi}(a\mid q,d^{\prime}),~\text{where}~d^{\prime}\sim P_{\phi}(d^{\prime}\mid q,d,a)

(6)

We can now reformulate the bridging objective
\max_{d^{\prime}}P_{\phi}(a\mid q,d^{\prime})
as an expectation over the ideal rewrite distribution:

\begin{split}&\textrm{maximize}~\mathbb{E}_{d^{\prime}\sim P_{\phi}(d^{\prime}|q,d,a)}\left[\log P_{\phi}(a|q,d^{\prime})\right]\\
&\quad\quad=\mathbb{E}_{d^{\prime}\sim P_{\phi}(d^{\prime}|q,d)}\left[\frac{P_{\phi}(d^{\prime}|q,d,a)}{P_{\phi}(d^{\prime}|q,d)}\log P_{\phi}(a|q,d^{\prime})\right]\\
&\left(\because\textrm{Importance-sampling identity}\right)\\
&\quad\quad=\mathbb{E}_{d^{\prime}\sim P_{\phi}(d^{\prime}|q,d)}\left[\frac{P_{\phi}(a|q,d^{\prime})}{P_{\phi}(a|q,d)}\log P_{\phi}(a|q,d^{\prime})\right]\\
\end{split}

(7)

The key insight emerges from the importance weight,

\frac{P_{\phi}(a|q,d^{\prime})}{P_{\phi}(a|q,d)}
,
which has a clear interpretation: it measures the relative
answerability
of the rewritten documents, compared to the original.

Unlike prior objectives that ignore the answer during rewriting, our formulation directly observes answerability.
The prior rewriting objective in Eq. (
2
) relies solely on a query and documents without incorporating the target answer
a
.
While some works
Yoon et al. 2024
;
Li et al. 2024
use answers as a supervision to post-hoc filter rewritten documents, they only treat
a
as a prior by adding
P(a)
to Eq. (
2
) rather than explicitly aligning for answerability.
In contrast, our formulation confirms that rewritten documents
d^{\prime}
, sampled from the LLM’s internal reasoning process, maximize answerability.
Therefore, sampling
d^{\prime}
from the LLM approximates ideal rewritten documents for the generator, without introducing shortcut signals.

3
Proposed Approach: R2U

In this section, we introduce the core design of R2U (§
2.2
–§
3.2
).
Using a large reasoning model (e.g., 70B parameters) as a bridge is a straightforward solution but comes with high computational cost, especially when the answer generator is much smaller (e.g., 3B).
To address this, we train a smaller model to imitate the rewriting behavior of a larger teacher model.

3.1
Distilling Scaled Process Supervision

To avoid high computational cost of direct observation, we train a smaller relevance-to-utility rewriter model (R2U) to imitate direct observation.
Based on the formulation in §
2.2
, we collect
(d,d^{\prime})
pairs to construct training dataset for the small rewriter, where
d^{\prime}
is obtained from the LLM’s reasoning traces.

Still, smaller models have a limitation in that they remain susceptible to positional bias, which requires caution when distilling rewritten documents.
When they imitate from
P_{\textrm{LLM}}(d^{\prime}\mid q,d,a)
,
they may be hallucinated by under-emphasized documents in middle positions
Liu et al. 2024
, leading to overlooked content and lower distillation quality.

We propose
inference scaling

for expanding each training example into
k
separate LLM calls, as shown in the upper part of Figure
2
.
The key idea is to augment each document to mitigate bias, inspired by unbiased training from summarization
Hwang et al. 2024
that rewrites each sentence individually based on the entire document.
Specifically, for each of the top-
k
retrieved documents, we prompt the LLM to rewrite one target document of
P_{\textrm{LLM}}(d_{i}^{\prime}\mid q,d,a)
at a time, conditioned on the query and the remaining documents.
During this process, the target document is placed at the end of the context to mitigate positional bias as well. While this increases inference cost, this robust dataset generation is done only once offline.
Figure
3
repeats the experiment from Figure
1
, showing that both our scaled rewriting and its ablation outperform NaiveRAG, with our full method achieving a significantly larger gain.

Then, we design a revised CoT prompt template to observe the desired document distribution from the reasoning process. A part of our prompt is described below.
To ensure better supervision quality, we have made several design choices:
(1) Separate the document rewriting step from the reasoning and answering step, and discard the latter step to prevent leakage. (2) Restrict rewrites to retrieved content. (3) Encourage cross-document interaction. (4) Allow skipping irrelevant documents.

Think step by step to answer the query based solely on the target document, while using the other documents to help clarify and improve the target document. […]

[Guidelines]

If the document does not contain any content relevant to the query, return exactly:
[NO_REWRITE]
[…]

Output format:

Step 1. Document rewrite:
[Rewritten document]

Step 2. Explain and answer:
[Explain and answer]

3.2
R2U Training

As shown in the lower part of Figure
2
, we train an SLM with a standard language modeling objective to imitate the rewriting behavior of the teacher LLM. We begin by fine-tuning the SLM on the supervision dataset described in Section
3.1
.
Then, as shown in the right part of Figure
2
,
we apply Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)
Rafailov et al. 2023
to let the SLM adapt its rewriting preferences.

3.2.1
Supervised Fine-Tuning

First, the SLM learns to generate the rewritten document set
d^{\prime}
conditioned on the original retrieved documents
d
and the query
q
.
Training is performed by minimizing the negative log-likelihood over the entire token sequence of
d^{\prime}
, generated in a single forward pass:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{SLM}}=-\log P_{\textrm{R2U }}(d^{\prime}_{1},...,d^{\prime}_{k}\mid q,\{d_{1},...,d_{k}\})

(8)

Here,
d^{\prime}_{1},...,d^{\prime}_{k}
denotes the token sequence representing the complete set of rewritten documents, and
\{d_{1},...,d_{k}\}
represents the original retrieved documents.
Note that we concatenate all
k
augmentations for students to generate a single rewrite of
k
documents in a single inference.

3.2.2
Preference Optimization

We further optimize the SLM with DPO
Rafailov et al. 2023
to align its rewriting preferences with those that lead to correct answers. While the teacher model provides high-quality rewritings, these may not always align with what is optimal for a smaller generator
Ke et al. 2024
.
DPO enables smaller models to adjust toward their own optimal preferences rather than strictly imitating the teacher.

The key challenge in DPO is that preference pairs must be constructed over
sets
of rewritten documents, not
individual
documents.
A naive construction
Meng et al. 2024
would only collect individual documents that always yield correct answers (F1 = 1) and contrast with those that always fail (F1 = 0), but it severely underutilizes the available supervision.
The analysis in SubSec.
4.5
shows that even when individual rewritten documents are partially informative in isolation, their combination can result in a correct answer.

To overcome this challenge, we augment the preference pairs at the set level by leveraging documents with varying individual answer correctness.
Specifically, we begin by evaluating
individual
rewritten document,
d^{\prime}_{i}
, in isolation, categorizing it into one of three groups based on its F1 score: (a) F1 = 0, (b)
0<
F1
<1
, and (c) F1 = 1. We then construct document
sets
,
d
, by combining these individual documents according to their categories—for example, sets containing only type (a), a mix of (a) and (b), a mix of (b) and (c), or other combinations including type (c). Each document set is then used as input to the model to determine whether it produces a correct answer. If the generated answer achieves F1 = 1, the document set is labeled as a positive example; else if F1 = 0, it is labeled as negative. These positive and negative document sets are used to construct win–lose pairs dataset
\mathcal{D}
for DPO training:

\begin{split}\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}&=-\mathbb{E}_{(d,d^{\prime}_{w},d^{\prime}_{l})\sim\mathcal{D}}\\
&\left[\log\sigma\left(\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(d^{\prime}_{w}|d)}{\pi_{\phi}(d^{\prime}_{w}|d)}-\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(d^{\prime}_{l}|d)}{\pi_{\phi}(d^{\prime}_{l}|d)}\right)\right]\\
\end{split}

(9)

\pi_{\theta}
denotes the policy model that needs to be optimized, and
\pi_{f}
represents the original policy model with fixed parameters.

\mathcal{D}
is the training set, where the input of each data is
d
and the output is a pair of rewritten document lists, with
d^{\prime}_{w}
and
d^{\prime}_{l}
as the winner set and loser set, respectively.

Comparison with Distilled Baselines

While baselines such as RankZephyr
Pradeep et al. 2023
and Refiner
Li et al. 2024
also leverage knowledge distillation from large language models for document rewriting, their performance remains lower than that of R2U.
This indicates that the observed gains primarily stem from aligning document utility with answerability, rather than from distillation alone.
Notably, R2U achieves superior performance with a comparatively small 3B module, showing that our approach enables effective distillation even with limited model capacity.

Deeper Analysis

To further investigate the role of document utility in complex query resolution, we categorize CRAG queries into four types: aggregation, multi-hop, post-processing-heavy, and others
Yang et al. 2024
.
This categorization allows us to disentangle the varying levels of document interaction and reasoning required by different query types. We compare R2U against Naive and RankZephyr, the latter achieving the strongest overall performance among existing baselines on CRAG.

As shown in Figure
4
, R2U attains the highest performance across all query types, with particularly pronounced gains in scenarios demanding higher document utility.
Specifically, R2U outperforms alternatives on aggregation and multi-hop queries, where the effective integration of multiple documents is crucial.
The improvement is even more substantial for post-processing-heavy queries, which require both multi-document reasoning and significant post-processing.

These findings highlight that by enhancing the true utility of retrieved documents through the bridge module, R2U enables the generator to more effectively leverage relevant evidence, thereby yielding substantial improvements over competitive baselines.

4.5
RQ 3: Does each component of R2U contribute to overall effectiveness?

We conduct an ablation study to validate the effectiveness of each component of R2U.

First, we validate that our performance gains stem from effectively capturing true document utility rather than merely improved training procedures. To isolate the effect of our rewriting strategy with training procedures, we compare our prompts for process supervision against prompts adapted from ComPACT
Yoon et al. 2024
, which instruct the model to summarize query-relevant information from the document. We prompt Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct model directly as a rewriter.
Due to the cost issue, we use 1,000 randomly sampled examples per dataset. In MS MARCO, this set includes 500 abstractive queries and 500 extractive queries.

The results are presented in Table
3
.
Our rewriting method consistently outperforms the baseline rewriting by a significant margin.
These findings support our claim that our rewriting approach, which explicitly leverages reasoning processes, captures true document utility more effectively.

Second, we validate the effectiveness of our training procedures. We start by evaluating performance without DPO training (w/o DPO). As shown in Table
4
, this variant still outperforms many baselines, indicating that our rewriting method, only with supervised fine-tuning alone, provides substantial improvements. Then, incorporating DPO further amplifies these gains.

Next, we assess the role of preference augmentation by introducing a naive variant,
\text{DPO}_{naive}
. In this version, preference pairs are constructed solely from individual documents that either succeed (
\text{F1}=1
) or fail (
\text{F1}=0
). The results in Table
4
show that R2U outperforms R2U w/
\text{DPO}_{naive}
.
Importantly, the naive variant yields only 4K preference pairs, whereas our augmentation procedure expands this to 11K pairs. The observed performance gain highlights that incorporating sets with partially informative documents meaningfully contributes to the effectiveness of preference-based optimization.

5
Related Works

5.1
Retrieval and Generation Gap in RAG

Retrieval modules
Robertson and Zaragoza 2009
in RAG systems are commonly optimized using ranking metrics such as nDCG and MRR, which rely on relevance judgments based on expected human behavior
Järvelin and Kekäläinen 2002
.
However, these metrics do not fully capture the needs of generative models consuming the retrieved context.
For example, LLMs often exhibit positional biases distinct from humans, disproportionately focusing on tokens at the beginning and end of contexts while neglecting those in the middle
Liu et al. 2024
.
Furthermore, LLMs are sensitive to distracting passages
Amiraz et al. 2025
;
Cuconasu et al. 2024
, and performance degradation from distracting passages becomes particularly severe when paired with strong retrievers
Jin et al. 2025
.
Consequently, a bridging model
Ke et al. 2024
is necessary to align retrieval outputs with the generative model’s requirements, thereby reconciling human-based retrieval optimization with generation needs to improve the RAG performance.

5.2
Bridging the Gap

Optimizing relevance in the retriever may not align with what the generation needs. Bridging models aim to mediate this gap by re-ranking or rewriting retrieved content before it is passed to a generator.

One direction is rerankers, which reorder documents to reflect usefulness to the query better. Pointwise rerankers such as BGE
Xiao et al. 2023
score each document individually, while listwise approaches like RankZephyr
Pradeep et al. 2023
consider global ordering, often distilled from strong LLMs like GPT
-
4. Recent works such as Rank
-
K
Yang et al. 2025
go further by distilling reasoning
-
oriented models (e.g., DeepSeek
-
R1). However, the rerankings are performed independently of the generator’s answer generation.

A second line of bridging work uses QFS, either selection-based or generation-based. Selection methods select salient segments directly from retrieved text, as EXIT
Hwang et al. 2024
, which uses GPT
-
4o pseudo
-
labels to train sentence selectors but risks spurious success when reasoning shortcuts appear. Generation-based methods instead rewrite evidence with LLMs: RECOMP
-
Abst
Xu et al. 2023
distills GPT
-
3.5 summaries, while later works like CompAct
Yoon et al. 2024
and Refiner
Li et al. 2024
scale to stronger (GPT-4) or multiple LLMs. Some QFS methods also filter the training dataset based on answer correctness.
Hwang et al. 2024
;
Yoon et al. 2024
;
Chirkova et al. 2025
.

Our work follows the generation-based direction but differs by exploiting the reasoning traces of LLMs. Instead of only distilling summaries, we capture how the model reasons about the query to ensure document rewriting reflects the reasoning path that yields higher answerability.

6
Conclusion

In this work, we address the gap between retriever and generator in RAG.
We propose to directly observe
reasoning process as process supervision, then distill to a smaller model for practical deployment. Moreover, R2U enables more effective use of retrieved information for answer generation. Empirical results on diverse datasets confirm that our rewritten documents significantly improve performance over strong baselines.
