On Fundamental Theorems of Super Invariant Theory


Abstract
The purpose of this paper is to prove the First and Second Fundamental Theorems of invariant theory for the complex special linear supergroup and discuss the superalgebra of invariants, via the super Plücker relations.

Introduction

Since its origins, in the
XIXth century
[
6
]
,
classical invariant theory rapidly provided important results,
whose applications involve both, mathematics and physics
(see
[
3
]
for an historical review of the theory).

One of the classical invariant theory main questions,
which led to the spectacular result by Hilbert
[
16
]
, is the search for invariants,
with respect to a given group action, in the ring of polynomial functions
(
[
22
,
23
]
).

We can look, for example, at the polynomial functions on the set of
r\times p

(r\leq p)
complex matrices
\mathrm{M}_{r\times p}
, that
are invariant under the action of the complex
special linear group
\mathrm{SL}_{r}(\mathbb{C})
:

\begin{CD}\mathbb{C}[\mathrm{M}_{r\times p}]\times\mathrm{SL}_{r}(\mathbb{C})@>{}>{}>\mathbb{C}[\mathrm{M}_{r\times p}]\\
(f,g)@>{}>{}>f.g,\end{CD}

where

(f.g)(M):=f(gM)

and
\mathbb{C}[\mathrm{M}_{r\times p}]
denotes the algebra of polynomials
functions on the entries of
\mathrm{M}_{r\times p}
.

Let
X_{i_{1}\dots i_{r}}
denote the
r\times r
minor formed with the columns
(i_{1},\dots,i_{r})
of a matrix in
\mathrm{M}_{r\times p}
.
The First and Second fundamental Theorems of invariant
theory for
\mathrm{SL}_{r}(\mathbb{C})

state the following (see
[
13
,
12
,
11
]
and Refs. therein
for a full account).

Theorem 1.1
.

Let the notation be as above.

1.

First Fundamental Theorem (FFT) of invariant theory.

The ring of invariants
\mathbb{C}[\mathrm{M}_{r\times p}]^{\mathrm{SL}_{r}(\mathbb{C})}

is generated by the minors
X_{i_{1}\dots i_{r}}
of the
r\times r
submatrices in

\mathrm{M}_{r\times p}
.

2.

Second Fundamental Theorem (SFT) of invariant theory.

We have a presentation of the ring of invariants via the ideal
\mathrm{I}
of the
Plücker relations:

\displaystyle\mathbb{C}[\mathrm{M}_{r\times p}]^{\mathrm{SL}_{r}(\mathbb{C})}\cong\mathbb{C}[X_{i_{1}\dots i_{r}}]\,\big/\,\mathrm{I},\qquad\hbox{ with}

\displaystyle\mathrm{I}:=\left(\sum_{k=1}^{r+1}(-1)^{k}X_{i_{1}...i_{r-1}j_{k}}X_{j_{1}\dots\tilde{\jmath}_{k}\dots j_{r+1}}\right)

(1)

where

\displaystyle 1\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{r-1}\leq p

\displaystyle 1\leq j_{1}<\cdots<\tilde{\jmath}_{k}<\dots<j_{r+1}\leq p

and

\tilde{\jmath}_{k}
means that the index is removed.

In this work we want to provide a generalization of this result to supergeometry.
The generalization of some questions of invariant theory to the super setting appeared
early in the literature: for example, the Young super tableaux
and techniques regarding their manipulation, appeared first in

[
18
]
and at the same time in
[
1
]
, to address questions of
representation theory of Lie superalgebras and the double commutant theorem, a fundamental result, originally proven, in the ordinary setting,
by Schur
[
22
]
.

In particular,
in
[
1
]
, the authors prove also a version of
the straightening algorithm for Young super tableaux, of uttermost
importance in representation theory and directly linked to the Plücker relations
[
11
]
.
Later on, in the papers
[
15
,
4
]
,
appeared such algorithm in the super setting.
Their idea, involving the so called
virtual variables method
, provides an elegant approach
which encompasses both, the ordinary and the super setting at once.
More recently in
[
24
,
9
]
appeared generalizations to other supergroups
as the orthosymplectic one and in
[
7
]
a categorical approach to super invariant
theory allowed to go beyond
the characteristic zero setting (see also
[
8
]
).

In the present work, however, we are interested in a related, and yet different
topic.
It has its significance rooted into the geometric meaning of
these combinatorial questions. In the ordinary setting Theorem
1.1
provides with
a presentation of the ring of invariants via the Plücker relations, which
give an embedding of the Grassmannian manifold into a suitable projective space.
In supergeometry, super Grassmannians do not admit, in general,
projective embeddings (see
[
19
]
, Chapter 4).
However, in
[
20
]
, the authors define a new graded
version of super projective space, with negative grading, which allows for such embedding.
Indeed, the Berezinians of sub-supermatrices replace truly the notion of minors,
which give the projective coordinates
for the classical Plücker embedding. Then, they allow to proceed
and give the correct supergeometric counterpart of the Plücker embedding and Plücker relations
[
20
]
. Furthermore, in
[
21
]
, these super Plücker relations has been studied from the perspective of constructing super cluster algebras. However, due to the very nature of Berezinians, which are defined only when the supermatrix is invertible, one is forced to restrict the analysis to an open set. Once this geometric constraint is set in place, one can state and prove the super version of the First Fundamental Theorem and progress towards the full proof of the more elusive Second Fundamental Theorem.

The paper is organized as follows.

In Section
2
, we introduce the notation and a few
facts concerning the Berezinians. We have included in Appendix
A

some very basic notions of supergeometry. We also state the super Cramer rule,
first proved in
[
2
]
with different methods,
which is instrumental for the proofs of the results in the next sections. Due to its importance to the present work, and in order to be self contained, we have included our
new proof of super Cramer’s rule in Appendix
B
, which is very much in line with the reasoning of the rest of the paper.

In Section
3
, we state and prove the
First Fundamental
Theorem of super invariant theory
for the special linear supergroup, Theorem
3.1
.

In Section
4
, we introduce and prove a super version
of the Jacobi identiy (a classical determinant identity, for details see Appendix
C
), Theorem
4.1
, one of our main results, which is instrumental for the following sections.

In Sections
5
,
6

we derive the
super Plücker relations
from the super Jacobi identity in a special,
yet illustrative, case (i.e. of
\mathrm{SL}(1|1)
). We also prove
Second Fundamental Theorem of super invariant theory
for
\mathrm{SL}(1|1)
.

In Section
7

we derive the
super Plücker relations
for the case of
\mathrm{SL}(r|s)
and conjectured
Second Fundamental Theorem of super invariant theory
for
\mathrm{SL}(r|s)
. It is important to note that the super Plücker relations we derived are same as the one given in
[
20
]
, however, we used a different construction based on super Jacobi identity.

B_{1}

Definition 2.1
.

A
fake supermatrix of type-I
is a matrix obtained by inserting an odd vector at an even vector’s position of an even supermatrix.
A
fake supermatrix of type-II
is a matrix obtained by inserting an even vector at an odd vector’s position of an even supermatrix.

As usual, for any even supermatrix or fake supermatrix, as in (
2
), we define its
parity reversed matrix
,
denoted by
B^{\Pi}
by swapping
B_{1}
with
B_{4}
and
B_{2}
with
B_{3}
, i.e.

\begin{pmatrix}B_{1}&\vline&B_{2}\\
\hline\cr B_{3}&\vline&B_{4}\end{pmatrix}^{\Pi}=\begin{pmatrix}B_{4}&\vline&B_{3}\\
\hline\cr B_{2}&\vline&B_{1}\end{pmatrix}.

Notice that if
B
is a fake supermatrix of type I, then
B^{\Pi}
is a fake supermatrix of type II and viceversa.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
B
be an even supermatrix or a fake supermatrix of type-I:

B=\begin{pmatrix}B_{1}&\vline&B_{2}\\
\hline\cr B_{3}&\vline&B_{4}\end{pmatrix}.

(3)

Assume that
B_{4}
is invertible.
We define the
Berezinian
of
B
as:

\mathrm{Ber}B:=\det B_{4}^{-1}\det(B_{1}-B_{2}B_{4}^{-1}B_{3})

Notice that, although this definition is the same as the first line of (
47
), in Appendix
A
,
here we use it in a more general context, when
B
can be a fake supermatrix of type-I.

Definition 2.3
.

Let
B
be an even supermatrix or a fake supermatrix of type-II:

B=\begin{pmatrix}B_{1}&\vline&B_{2}\\
\hline\cr B_{3}&\vline&B_{4}\end{pmatrix}

(4)

Assume that
B_{1}
is invertible.
We define the
inverted berezinian
of
B
:

\mathrm{Ber}^{*}B:=\mathrm{Ber}B^{\Pi}.

It is clear that for an invertible supermatrix
B
:

\mathrm{Ber}^{*}B=\mathrm{Ber}B^{-1}.

(5)

We state now the super version of Cramer’s rule. Recall that, in ordinary linear algebra, for an invertible matrix
T
of size
n\times n
and a column vector
\mathbf{b}
of size
n
, the solution to the equation:

T\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b}

is a column vector
\mathbf{x}
whose entries are given by:

x_{i}=\dfrac{\det T_{i}(\mathbf{b})}{\det T}

where
T_{i}(\mathbf{b})
denotes the matrix obtained by replacing the
i
-th column of
T
with
\mathbf{b}
.

In super linear algebra, we have the following theorem:

Theorem 2.5
.

Super Cramer’s rule
.
Let
M\in\mathrm{GL}(r|s)
be an invertible even supermatrix and let
\mathbf{b}\in\mathbf{k}^{r|s}
be an even vector of size
r|s
. Then, the solution to the equation

M\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b}

is given by:

\displaystyle x_{i}=\dfrac{\mathrm{Ber}M_{i}(\mathbf{b})}{\mathrm{Ber}M}\qquad\hbox{for}\quad i=1,...,r

\displaystyle x_{\hat{\jmath}}=\dfrac{\mathrm{Ber}^{*}M_{\hat{\jmath}}(\mathbf{b})}{\mathrm{Ber}^{*}M}\qquad\hbox{for}\quad\hat{\jmath}=\hat{1}\cdots\hat{s}

where
M_{i}(\mathbf{b})
is the supermatrix obtained by replacing
i
-th even column of
M
with
\mathbf{b}
and
M_{\hat{\jmath}}(\mathbf{b})
is the fake supermatrix of type-II obtained by replacing
\hat{\jmath}
-th odd column of
M
with
\mathbf{b}
.

This is a known result, see
[
2
]
. However, for completeness, we have included in Appendix
B
a proof of it, obtained with different, elementary methods.

As stated in Remark
B.1
, the same expressions hold for the equation
M\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b}
if
\mathbf{b}
is an odd vector of size
r|s
.

Example 2.6
.

Let
g=\begin{pmatrix}a&\vline&\alpha\\
\hline\cr\beta&\vline&b\end{pmatrix}\in\mathrm{GL}(1|1)
. Suppose we need to find
\begin{pmatrix}x\\
\hline\cr\eta\end{pmatrix}
such that:

g\begin{pmatrix}x\\
\hline\cr\eta\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}c\\
\hline\cr\gamma\end{pmatrix}.

Then, using super Cramer’s rule, one gets:

x=\dfrac{\mathrm{Ber}\begin{pmatrix}c&\vline&\alpha\\
\hline\cr\gamma&\vline&b\end{pmatrix}}{\mathrm{Ber}\begin{pmatrix}a&\vline&\alpha\\
\hline\cr\beta&\vline&b\end{pmatrix}}=\dfrac{c-\alpha b^{-1}\gamma}{a-\alpha b^{-1}\beta}

and

\eta=\dfrac{\mathrm{Ber}^{*}\begin{pmatrix}a&\vline&c\\
\hline\cr\beta&\vline&\gamma\end{pmatrix}}{\mathrm{Ber}^{*}\begin{pmatrix}a&\vline&\alpha\\
\hline\cr\beta&\vline&b\end{pmatrix}}=\dfrac{\gamma-\beta a^{-1}c}{b-\beta a^{-1}\alpha}.

3
First Fundamental Theorem of invariant

theory for
\mathrm{SL(r|s)}

In ordinary geometry, we have a natural right action of the complex special linear group
\mathrm{SL}_{r}(\mathbb{C})

on the algebra of functions
\mathbb{C}[\mathrm{M}_{r\times p}]
of the
r\times p
complex matrices
\mathrm{M}_{r\times p}
:

\begin{CD}\mathbb{C}[\mathrm{M}_{r\times p}]\times\mathrm{SL}_{r}(\mathbb{C})@>{}>{}>\mathbb{C}[\mathrm{M}_{r\times p}]\\
(f,g)@>{}>{}>f.g,\end{CD}

(6)

where
(f.g)(M):=f(gM)
, for all
M\in\mathrm{M}_{r\times p}(\mathbb{C})
.

The First Fundamental Theorem of ordinary invariant theory realizes the ring of
invariants with respect to this action,
as the subring of
\mathbb{C}[\mathrm{M}_{r\times p}]
generated by the determinants
of the
r\times r
minors in
\mathrm{M}_{r\times p}(\mathbb{C})
.

We now extend the action (
6
) to the super setting and compute
the superalgebra of invariants. Let
\mathrm{M}_{r|s\times p|q}
be the super vector
space of supermatrices and let
\mathcal{O}
be the superalgebra of ‘functions’ on

\mathrm{M}_{r|s\times p|q}
(see Observation
A.1
).
For notational purposes, let us organize the generators of
\mathcal{O}
(or ‘coordinates’) in a supermatrix, as follows:

A=\begin{pmatrix}x_{11}&...&x_{1p}&\vline&\alpha_{1\hat{1}}&...&\alpha_{1\hat{q}}\\
.&...&.&\vline&.&...&.\\
.&...&.&\vline&.&...&.\\
x_{r1}&...&x_{rp}&\vline&\alpha_{r\hat{1}}&...&\alpha_{r\hat{q}}\\
\hline\cr\beta_{\hat{1}1}&...&\beta_{\hat{1}p}&\vline&y_{\hat{1}\hat{1}}&...&y_{\hat{1}\hat{q}}\\
.&...&.&\vline&.&...&.\\
.&...&.&\vline&.&...&.\\
\beta_{\hat{s}1}&...&\beta_{\hat{s}p}&\vline&y_{\hat{s}\hat{1}}&...&y_{\hat{s}\hat{q}}\end{pmatrix},

(7)

where the hat denotes the odd indices.
Then,

\mathcal{O}=k[x_{ij},y_{\hat{k}\hat{l}},\alpha_{i\hat{k}},\beta_{\hat{l}j}].

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a commutative superalgebra. For every supermatrix in the set of
\mathcal{A}
-points of
\mathrm{M}_{r|s\times p|q}
(see Appendix
A
),
M\in\mathrm{M}_{r|s\times p|q}(\mathcal{A})
and

f\in\mathcal{O}
one can compute an element of
\mathcal{A}
. This is denoted, in the terminology of the
A
-points, as
f(M)
instead of the, perhaps more appropriate,

M(f)
.

Let
\mathrm{SL}(r|s)
be the special linear
supergroup (see, for example,
[
5
]
, Chapter 1). Its
\mathcal{A}
-points are the matrices in
{\mathrm{GL}}(r|s)(\mathcal{A})

with Berezinian equal to 1.
In terms of
\mathcal{A}
-points, one can write a left action of the special linear supergroup on the supermatrices:

\begin{CD}\mathrm{SL}(r|s)(\mathcal{A})\times\mathrm{M}_{r|s\times p|q}(\mathcal{A})@>{}>{}>\mathrm{M}_{r|s\times p|q}(\mathcal{A})\\
(g,M)@>{}>{}>gM.\end{CD}

One can also define a right action of
\mathrm{SL}(r|s)
on
\mathcal{O}
, exactly as in the ordinary case (
6
). For
g\in\mathrm{SL}(r|s)(\mathcal{A})
and
f\in\mathcal{O}
one has

(f.g)(M):=f(gM),\quad M\in\mathrm{M}_{r|s\times p|q}(\mathcal{A}).

We want to be able to define the Berezinians of all
r|s\times r|s
minors appearing in the matrix (
7
). We then have to localize
\mathcal{O}
at the determinants

D_{i_{1},..,i_{r}}:=\det\begin{pmatrix}x_{1i_{1}}&...&x_{1i_{r}}\\
.&...&.\\
.&...&.\\
x_{ri_{1}}&...&x_{ri_{r}}\\
\end{pmatrix},\qquad D_{\hat{\jmath}_{1}\cdots\hat{\jmath}_{s}}:=\det\begin{pmatrix}y_{\hat{1}\hat{\jmath}_{1}}&\cdots&y_{\hat{1}\hat{\jmath}_{s}}\\
\cdot&\cdots&\cdot\\
\cdot&\cdots&\cdot\\
y_{\hat{s}\hat{\jmath}_{1}}&\cdots&y_{\hat{s}\hat{\jmath}_{s}}\\
\end{pmatrix},

in the diagonal blocks, in the expression (
7
).
We can now look at the action of the supergroup
\mathrm{SL}(r|s)
on the localization

\tilde{\mathcal{O}}:=\mathcal{O}[D_{i_{1}\cdots i_{r}}^{-1},D_{\hat{\jmath}_{1}\cdots\hat{\jmath_{s}}}^{-1}].

(8)

This corresponds to take the subset
\tilde{\mathrm{M}}_{r|s\times p|q}(\mathcal{A})\subset\mathrm{M}_{r|s\times p|q}(\mathcal{A})
where all the corresponding minors are invertible.

In this way we can formulate the super version of the First Fundamental Theorem of
invariant theory. As we shall see in the next section, it is related with
the Grassmannian and its super Plücker embedding, and we adopted a very similar notation for generators as in
[
20
]
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
A_{i_{1}\cdots i_{r}|\hat{\jmath}_{1}\cdots\hat{\jmath}_{s}}
be the supermatrix formed with the columns
(i_{1},\dots,i_{r}|\hat{\jmath}_{1},\dots,\hat{\jmath}_{s})
of the supermatrix
A
in (
7
). Then, the ring of invariants
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}^{\mathrm{SL}(r|s)}

is generated by the super minors

\displaystyle X_{i_{1}\cdots i_{r}|\hat{\jmath}_{1}\cdots\hat{\jmath}_{s}}:=\mathrm{Ber}A_{i_{1}\cdots i_{r}|\hat{\jmath}_{1}\cdots\hat{\jmath}_{s}}

\displaystyle X^{*}_{i_{1}\cdots i_{r}|\hat{\jmath}_{1}\cdots\hat{\jmath}_{s}}:=\mathrm{Ber}^{*}A_{i_{1}\cdots i_{r}|\hat{\jmath}_{1}\cdots\hat{\jmath}_{s}}

(9)

and the ‘fake’ super minors,

\displaystyle X_{i_{1}\cdots i_{k-1}\hat{\jmath}_{k}i_{k+1}\cdots i_{r}|\hat{\jmath}_{1}\cdots\hat{\jmath}_{s}}

\displaystyle:=\mathrm{Ber}A_{i_{1}\cdots i_{k-1}\hat{\jmath}_{k}i_{k+1}\cdots i_{r}|\hat{\jmath}_{1}\cdots\hat{\jmath}_{s}}

(10)

\displaystyle X^{*}_{i_{1}\cdots i_{r}|\hat{\jmath}_{1}\cdots\hat{\jmath}_{l-1}i_{l}\hat{\jmath}_{l+1}\cdots\hat{\jmath}_{s}}

\displaystyle:=\mathrm{Ber}^{*}A_{i_{1}\cdots i_{r}|\hat{\jmath}_{1}\cdots\hat{\jmath}_{l-1}i_{l}\hat{\jmath}_{l+1}\cdots\hat{\jmath}_{s}}

(11)

where
1\leq i_{1}\leq\cdots\leq i_{r}\leq p
and
1\leq\hat{\jmath}_{1}\leq\cdots\leq\hat{\jmath}_{s}\leq q
.

Proof.

Firstly, it is clear, from the multiplicative property of Berezinian, that super-minors and fake super-minors are invariants.

To show that they generate the ring
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}^{\mathrm{SL}(r|s)}
, we decompose the matrix of coordinate functions (
7
) as
A=\tilde{A}_{1\cdots r|\hat{1}\cdots\hat{s}}B
, where

\tilde{A}_{1\cdots r|\hat{1}\cdots\hat{s}}=\begin{pmatrix}\dfrac{x_{11}}{X_{1\cdots r|\hat{1}\cdots\hat{s}}}&\cdots&x_{1r}&\vline&\alpha_{1\hat{1}}&\cdots&\alpha_{1\hat{s}}\\
\cdot&\cdots&\cdot&\vline&\cdot&\cdots&\cdot\\
\cdot&\cdots&\cdot&\vline&\cdot&\cdots&\cdot\\
\dfrac{x_{r1}}{X_{1\cdots r|\hat{1}\cdots\hat{s}}}&\cdots&x_{rr}&\vline&\alpha_{r\hat{1}}&\cdots&\alpha_{r\hat{s}}\\
&&&\vline&&&\\
\hline\cr&&&\vline&&&\\
\dfrac{\beta_{\hat{1}1}}{X_{1\cdots r|\hat{1}\cdots\hat{s}}}&\cdots&\beta_{\hat{1}r}&\vline&y_{\hat{1}\hat{1}}&\cdots&y_{\hat{1}\hat{s}}\\
\cdot&\cdots&\cdot&\vline&\cdot&\cdots&\cdot\\
\cdot&\cdots&\cdot&\vline&\cdot&\cdots&\cdot\\
\dfrac{\beta_{\hat{s}1}}{X_{1\cdots r|\hat{1}\cdots\hat{s}}}&\cdots&\beta_{\hat{s}r}&\vline&y_{\hat{s}\hat{1}}&\cdots&y_{\hat{s}\hat{s}}\end{pmatrix}.

Therefore, we have,

B=\tilde{A}_{1\cdots r|\hat{1}\cdots\hat{s}}^{-1}A.

Notice that, we are working over
\mathcal{O}[D_{i_{1}\cdots i_{r}}^{-1},D_{\hat{\jmath}_{1}\cdots\hat{\jmath_{s}}}^{-1}]
, hence,
\tilde{A}_{1\cdots r|\hat{1}\cdots\hat{s}}^{-1}
is well-defined.
Using super Cramer’s rule we can find the entries of
B=\begin{pmatrix}U&\vline&V\\
\hline\cr W&\vline&Z\end{pmatrix}
:

\displaystyle U_{ij}=\begin{cases}X_{j\cdots r|\hat{1}\cdots\hat{s}}&\hbox{for }i=1,1\leq j\leq p,\\
X^{*}_{1\cdots r|\hat{1}\cdots\hat{s}}X_{1\cdots(i-1)j(i+1)\cdots r|\hat{1}\cdots\hat{s}}&\hbox{for }1<i\leq r,\,1\leq j\leq p\end{cases}

(12)

\displaystyle W_{\hat{k}j}=X_{1\cdots r|\hat{1}\cdots\hat{s}}X^{*}_{1\cdots r|\hat{1}\cdots(\hat{k}-\hat{1})j(\hat{k}+\hat{1})\cdots\hat{s}}\qquad\hbox{for }1\leq k\leq s,\,1\leq j\leq p.

(13)

\displaystyle V_{i\hat{l}}=\begin{cases}X_{\hat{l}\cdots r|\hat{1}\cdots\hat{s}}&\hbox{for }i=1,\,1\leq l\leq q,\\
X^{*}_{1\cdots r|\hat{1}\cdots\hat{s}}X_{1\cdots(i-1)\hat{l}(i+1)\cdots r|\hat{1}\cdots\hat{s}}&\hbox{for }1<i\leq r,\,1\leq l\leq q.\end{cases}

(14)

\displaystyle Z_{\hat{k}\hat{l}}=X_{1\cdots r|\hat{1}\cdots\hat{s}}X^{*}_{1\cdots r|\hat{1}\cdots(\hat{k}-\hat{1})\hat{l}(\hat{k}+\hat{1})\cdots\hat{s}}\qquad\hbox{for }1\leq k\leq s,\,1\leq l\leq q.

(15)

Let
\mathcal{A}
be any superalgebra and consider an arbitrary element
T\in\tilde{M}_{r|s\times p|q}(\mathcal{A})
. We can write
T
as a product,

\displaystyle T=\tilde{T}_{1\cdots r|\hat{1}\cdots\hat{s}}R

(16)

where,

\tilde{T}_{1\cdots r|\hat{1}\cdots\hat{s}}
is an element of
\mathrm{SL}(r|s)(\mathcal{A})
and
R\in\tilde{M}_{r|s\times p|q}(\mathcal{A})
is computed in terms of superminors and fake superminors. Explicitly, each entry of
R
can be computed by evaluating on
T
the corresponding superminor or fake superminor as in
B
above.

Let

4
The super Jacobi identity

In this section we want to obtain a super Jacobi identity, a generalization of the Jacobi identity, Theorem
C.1
in the Appendix
C
, to the super setting. We first introduce some notation.

Notation.
Let
A
be an invertible supermatrix of size
p|q\times p|q
. Let
u=(p-r+1,\dots,p|{\hat{q}-\hat{s}+\hat{1}},\dots,\hat{q})
and
v=(1,\dots,r|\hat{1},\dots,\hat{s})
be two sets of indices,
r<p
,
\hat{s}<\hat{q}
.
Let
A^{u}_{v}
denote the matrix obtained from
A
by deleting the rows and columns
contained in
u
and
v
, respectively,
i.e.
by deleting the last
r
even rows, the last
\hat{s}
odd rows, first
r
even columns and first
\hat{s}
odd columns. Also, set
\tilde{u}=(1,\dots,p-r|\hat{1},\dots,{\hat{q}-\hat{s}})
and
\tilde{v}=(r+1,\dots,p|\hat{s}+\hat{1},\dots,\hat{q})
as the complements of
u
and
v
, respectively.

Theorem 4.1
.

The super Jacobi Identity
.
Let the notation be as above.
If
A^{u}_{v}
and
(A^{-1})^{\tilde{v}}_{\tilde{u}}
are invertible, then

\displaystyle\mathrm{Ber}A\,\mathrm{Ber}(A^{-1})^{\tilde{v}}_{\tilde{u}}=(-1)^{t}\mathrm{Ber}A^{u}_{v}

(17)

where
t=r(p+1)+s(q+1)
.

We call (
17
) the
super Jacobi identity
.

Proof.

A similar strategy to what we used in the proof of Theorem
C.1
(see Appendix
C
) will work here. Let

A=\begin{pmatrix}[a_{ij}]&\vline&[a_{i\hat{k}}]\\
\hline\cr[a_{\hat{l}j}]&\vline&[a_{\hat{l}\hat{k}}]\end{pmatrix}\qquad\hbox{and}\qquad A^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}[b_{ij}]&\vline&[b_{i\hat{k}}]\\
\hline\cr[b_{\hat{l}j}]&\vline&[b_{\hat{l}\hat{k}}]\end{pmatrix}.

According to our notation,

(A^{-1})_{\tilde{u}}^{\tilde{v}}=\begin{pmatrix}b_{1(p-r+1)}&\cdots&b_{1p}&\vline&b_{1(\hat{q}-\hat{s}+\hat{1})}&\cdots&b_{1\hat{q}}\\
\cdot&\cdots&\cdot&\vline&\cdot&\cdots&\cdot\\
\cdot&\cdots&\cdot&\vline&\cdot&\cdots&\cdot\\
b_{r(p-r+1)}&\cdots&b_{rp}&\vline&b_{r(\hat{q}-\hat{s}+\hat{1})}&\cdots&b_{r\hat{q}}\\
\hline\cr b_{\hat{1}(p-r+1)}&\cdots&b_{\hat{1}p}&\vline&b_{\hat{1}(\hat{q}-\hat{s}+\hat{1})}&\cdots&b_{\hat{1}\hat{q}}\\
\cdot&\cdots&\cdot&\vline&\cdot&\cdots&\cdot\\
\cdot&\cdots&\cdot&\vline&\cdot&\cdots&\cdot\\
b_{\hat{s}(p-r+1)}&\cdots&b_{\hat{s}p}&\vline&b_{\hat{s}(\hat{q}-\hat{s}+\hat{1})}&\cdots&b_{\hat{s}\hat{q}}\end{pmatrix}

One can see that

\mathrm{Ber}(A^{-1})_{\tilde{u}}^{\tilde{v}}=\mathrm{Ber}T

(18)

where
T
is the super matrix of size
p|q\times p|q
described as follows:

T_{j}=\begin{cases}(A^{-1})_{p-r+j}\qquad&\hbox{for}\qquad 1\leq j\leq r\\
e_{j}\qquad&\hbox{for}\qquad r+1\leq j\leq p\end{cases}

where
T_{j}
denotes the
j
-th even column of
T
,
(A^{-1})_{k}
is the
k
-th even column of
A^{-1}
and
e_{j}
denotes an even vector of size
p|q
, whose entries are all
0
except at the
j
-th even position, where the entry is
1
. On the other hand,

\displaystyle T_{\hat{l}}=\begin{cases}(A^{-1})_{\hat{q}-\hat{s}+\hat{l}}\qquad&\hbox{for}\qquad\hat{1}\leq\hat{l}\leq\hat{s}\\
e_{\hat{l}}\qquad&\hbox{for}\qquad\hat{s}+\hat{1}\leq\hat{l}\leq\hat{q},\end{cases}

where
T_{\hat{l}}
denotes the
\hat{l}
-th odd column of
T
,
(A^{-1})_{\hat{k}}
is the
\hat{k}
-th-odd column of
A^{-1}
and
e_{\hat{l}}
denotes an odd vector of size
p|q
whose entries are all
0
except at the
\hat{l}
-th odd position, where the entry is 1.

In other words,
T
is a supermatrix whose first
r
even columns coincide with the last
r
even columns of
A^{-1}
, the first
\hat{s}
odd columns coincide with the last
\hat{s}
odd columns of
A^{-1}
and the remaining even and odd columns are
e_{j}
and
e_{\hat{l}}
, respectively.

Therefore, using Equation (
18
) and the multiplicative property of Berezinian, we have:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Ber}A\,\mathrm{Ber}(A^{-1})_{\tilde{u}}^{\tilde{v}}=\mathrm{Ber}A\,\mathrm{Ber}T=\mathrm{Ber}(AT).

(19)

Let us denote
C=AT
; then, using above notation:

\displaystyle C_{j}=\begin{cases}e_{p-r+j}\qquad&\hbox{for}\qquad 1\leq j\leq r,\\
A_{j}\qquad&\hbox{for}\qquad r+1\leq j\leq p,\end{cases}

and

\displaystyle C_{\hat{l}}=\begin{cases}e_{{\hat{q}-\hat{s}+\hat{l}}}\qquad&\hbox{for}\qquad\hat{1}\leq\hat{l}\leq\hat{s}\\
A_{\hat{l}}\qquad&\hbox{for}\qquad\hat{s}+\hat{1}\leq\hat{l}\leq\hat{q}.\end{cases}

Then, one can compute:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Ber}(AT)=\mathrm{Ber}C=(-1)^{r(p+1)+s(q+1)}\mathrm{Ber}A^{u}_{v}

(20)

and by putting this into Equation (
19
) the desired identity is obtained.
∎

Appendix A
Supergeometry

We briefly recall some notions regarding supergroups, superspaces
and their
\mathcal{A}
-points. For more details see, for example, Ref.
[
5
]
, Chapters 1 and 3 and
[
10
]
, Chapter 1.

Let
\mathbf{k}
denote the ground field (
\mathbf{k}=\mathbb{R},\mathbb{C}
). A

super vector space
is a
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-graded
vector space over
\mathbf{k}

V=V_{0}\oplus V_{1}
.
We call the pair of numbers
\dim V_{0}|\dim V_{1}
the
super dimension
of
V
and we define

\mathbf{k}^{m|n}:=\mathbf{k}^{m}\oplus\mathbf{k}^{n}
.

We say that
\mathcal{A}
is a
commutative superalgebra

if it is a
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-graded algebra,
with grading preserving multiplication and

ab=(-1)^{p(a)p(b)}ba
, for all (homogeneous)
a,b\in\mathcal{A}
, being
p(a),p(b)=0,1
according to the grading (
p(a)
is the
parity
of an element
a\in\mathcal{A}
).
An important example of commutative superalgebra
is the
superalgebra of polynomials
:

\mathbf{k}[x_{1},\dots,x_{m},\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n}]:=k[x_{1},\dots,x_{m}]\otimes\wedge(\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n})

where Latin letters denote the
even
(commuting) variables,
while Greek letters denote the
odd
(anticommuting) ones.

For a commutative superalgebra
\mathcal{A}
, we define the free
\mathcal{A}
-module
\mathcal{A}^{m|n}=\mathcal{A}\otimes k^{m|n}
, where

\mathcal{A}^{m|n}_{0}:=\mathcal{A}_{0}\otimes\mathbf{k}^{m}\oplus\mathcal{A}_{1}\otimes\mathbf{k}^{n}\quad\hbox{and}\quad\mathcal{A}^{m|n}_{1}:=\mathcal{A}_{0}\otimes\mathbf{k}^{n}\oplus\mathcal{A}_{1}\otimes\mathbf{k}^{m}

are, respectively, its even and odd parts.

Given a commutative superalgebra
\mathcal{A}
,
the
\mathcal{A}
-points
of the supervector space
V=V_{0}\oplus V_{1}
are:

V(\mathcal{A})=(\mathcal{A}\otimes V)_{0}=\mathcal{A}_{0}\otimes V_{0}+\mathcal{A}_{1}\otimes V_{1}

We call the elements in
(\mathcal{A}\otimes V)_{0}

even vectors
, while the elements
in
(\mathcal{A}\otimes V)_{1}
are
odd vectors
.

For example, the
\mathcal{A}
-points of the supervector space
k^{m|n}
are:

k^{m|n}(\mathcal{A}):=\mathcal{A}_{0}\otimes k^{m}\oplus\mathcal{A}_{1}\otimes k^{n}=\left\{(a_{1},\dots,a_{m},\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n})\right\}

(46)

Hence
(a_{1},\dots,a_{m},\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n})
is an even vector while

(\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{m},b_{1},\dots,b_{n})
is an odd one.

Observation A.1
.

We equivalently interpret the
\mathcal{A}
-points as follows:

\displaystyle\mathbf{k}^{m|n}(\mathcal{A})

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathrm{(salg)}}(\mathbf{k}[x_{1},\dots,x_{m},\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n}],\mathcal{A})

where

denotes the

that we shall not pursue here. The above superalgebra,

[
5
]
, Chapter 3.

We now take into exam a special case,
important in our treatment.

\hline\cr Z&\vline&W\end{pmatrix}\right\},\qquad\hbox{with}

x_{\hat{\jmath}}=\dfrac{\mathrm{Ber}^{*}F_{\hat{\jmath}}(\mathbf{c})}{\mathrm{Ber}^{*}F}=\dfrac{\det\left(W_{\hat{\jmath}}(\mathbf{c}^{(o)})-VU^{-1}Y_{\hat{\jmath}}(\mathbf{c}^{(e)})\right)}{\det(W-VU^{-1}Y)}.

(53)

On the other hand,

\displaystyle\dfrac{\mathrm{Ber}D_{i}(\mathbf{b})}{\mathrm{Ber}D}=

\displaystyle\dfrac{\det\left((U+XV)_{i}(\mathbf{b}^{(e)})-(Y+XW)W^{-1}V_{i}(\mathbf{b}^{(o)})\right)}{\det(U-YW^{-1}V)}=

\displaystyle\dfrac{\det\left((U+XV)_{i}(\mathbf{b}^{(e)})-XV_{i}(\mathbf{b}^{(o)})-YW^{-1}V_{i}(\mathbf{b}^{(o)})\right)}{\det(U-YW^{-1}V)}=

\displaystyle\dfrac{\det\left(U_{i}(\mathbf{c}^{(e)})-YW^{-1}V_{i}(\mathbf{b}^{(o)})\right)}{\det(U-YW^{-1}V)}

(54)

and

\displaystyle\dfrac{\mathrm{Ber}^{*}D_{\hat{\jmath}}(\mathbf{b})}{\mathrm{Ber}^{*}D}=\dfrac{\det\left(W_{\hat{\jmath}}(\mathbf{b}^{(o)})-V(U+XV)^{-1}(Y+XW)_{\hat{\jmath}}(\mathbf{b}^{(e)})\right)}{\det\left(W-V(U+XV)^{-1}(Y+XW)\right)}=

\displaystyle\dfrac{\det\left(W_{\hat{\jmath}}(\mathbf{b}^{(o)})-V(U^{-1}-U^{-1}XVU^{-1})\left(Y_{\hat{\jmath}}(\mathbf{c}^{(e)})+XW_{\hat{\jmath}}(\mathbf{b}^{(o)})\right)\right)}{\det\left(W-V(U^{-1}-U^{-1}XVU^{-1})(Y+XW)\right)}=

\displaystyle\dfrac{\det(W_{\hat{\jmath}}(\mathbf{b}^{(o)})-VU^{-1}Y_{\hat{j}}(\mathbf{c}^{(e)})-VU^{-1}XW_{\hat{\jmath}}(\mathbf{b}^{(o)})-VU^{-1}XVU^{-1}Y_{\hat{\jmath}}(\mathbf{c}^{(e)}))}{\det(W-VU^{-1}Y-VU^{-1}XW+VU^{-1}XVU^{-1}Y)}=

\displaystyle\dfrac{\det(I-VU^{-1}X)\det\left(W_{\hat{\jmath}}(\mathbf{c}^{(o)})-VU^{-1}Y_{\hat{\jmath}}(\mathbf{c}^{(e)})\right)}{\det(I-VU^{-1}X)\det(W-VU^{-1}Y)}=

\displaystyle\dfrac{\det\left(W_{\hat{\jmath}}(\mathbf{c}^{(o)})-VU^{-1}Y_{\hat{\jmath}}(\mathbf{c}^{(e)})\right)}{\det(W-VU^{-1}Y)}.

(55)

By comparing Eqs. (
52
), (
53
), (
54
) and (
55
), one concludes that the matrices of type
F
satisfy the Theorem.

One can easily observe that every even supermatrix
M\in\mathrm{GL}(r|s)
is of type
F
and this completes the proof.
∎

Remark B.1
.

It is important to note that, in the above proof, we never use the fact that
\mathbf{b}
is even. The same expressions work for the equation
M\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b}
if
\mathbf{b}
is an odd vector of size
r|s
.

Appendix C
The Jacobi identity and the Plücker relations

We start by recalling a determinant identity due to Jacobi, the
Jacobi complementary minor theorem

[
17
]
or
the
Jacobi identity

for short.

Theorem C.1
.

The Jacobi complementary minor theorem or Jacobi identity.

Let
A
be an invertible
n\times n
matrix. Fix the two sets of indices:

u=(n-r+1,...,n)\qquad and\qquad v=(1,...,r)

Let
\tilde{u}
and
\tilde{v}
denote the complements of
u
and
v
respectively
in
(1,\dots,n)
. Then:

\displaystyle\det A\det(A^{-1})^{\tilde{v}}_{\tilde{u}}=(-1)^{r(n+1)}\det A^{u}_{v}

(56)

where
A^{u}_{v}
denotes the matrix obtained from
A
by deleting its rows and columns whose indices are contained in
u
and
v
, respectively.

Proof.

We denote

A=\begin{bmatrix}a_{ij}\end{bmatrix}_{n\times n}

and,

A^{-1}=\begin{bmatrix}b_{kl}\end{bmatrix}_{n\times n}.

Therefore,

(A^{-1})^{\tilde{v}}_{\tilde{u}}=\begin{pmatrix}b_{1(n-r+1)}&\cdots&b_{1n}\\
\cdot&\cdots&\cdot\\
\cdot&\cdots&\cdot.\\
b_{r(n-r+1)}&\cdots&b_{rn}\end{pmatrix}.

Notice that,

\displaystyle\det(A^{-1})^{\tilde{v}}_{\tilde{u}}=

\displaystyle\det\begin{pmatrix}b_{1(n-r+1)}&\cdots&b_{1n}\\
\cdot&\cdots&\cdot\\
\cdot&\cdots&\cdot\\
b_{r(n-r+1)}&\cdots&b_{rn}\end{pmatrix}=

\displaystyle\det\begin{pmatrix}b_{1(n-r+1)}&\cdots&b_{1n}&0&\cdots&0\\
\cdot&\cdots&\cdot&\cdot&\cdots&\cdot\\
\cdot&\cdots&\cdot&\cdot&\cdots&\cdot\\
b_{r(n-r+1)}&\cdots&b_{rn}&0&\cdots&0\\
b_{(r+1)(n-r+1)}&\cdots&b_{(r+1)n}&1&\cdots&0\\
\cdot&\cdots&\cdot&\cdot&\cdots&\cdot\\
\cdot&\cdots&\cdot&\cdot&\cdots&\cdot\\
b_{n(n-r+1)}&\cdots&b_{nn}&0&\cdots&1\end{pmatrix}_{n\times n}

(57)

Let us denote the
n\times n
matrix that appeared in Equation (
57
) by
T
. Hence, using the multiplicative property of the determinant we get:

\displaystyle\det A\det(A^{-1})^{\tilde{v}}_{\tilde{u}}=

\displaystyle\det A\det T=\det(AT)=

\displaystyle\det\begin{pmatrix}0&\cdots&0&a_{1(r+1)}&\cdots&a_{1n}\\
\cdot&\cdots&\cdot&\cdot&\cdots&\cdot\\
\cdot&\cdots&\cdot&\cdot&\cdots&\cdot\\
0&\cdots&0&a_{(n-r)(r+1)}&\cdots&a_{(n-r)n}\\
1&\cdots&0&a_{(n-r+1)r+1}&\cdots&a_{(n-r+1)n}\\
\cdot&\cdots&\cdot&\cdot&\cdots&\cdot\\
\cdot&\cdots&\cdot&\cdot&\cdots&\cdot\\
0&\cdots&1&a_{n(r+1)}&\cdots&a_{nn}\end{pmatrix}=

\displaystyle(-1)^{r(n+1)}\det\begin{pmatrix}a_{1(r+1)}&\cdots&a_{1n}\\
\cdot&\cdots&\cdot\\
\cdot&\cdots&\cdot\\
a_{(n-r)(r+1)}&\cdots&a_{(n-r)n}\end{pmatrix}=

\displaystyle(-1)^{r(n+1)}\det(A^{u}_{v}).

as desired.
∎

There are several and more general versions of the Jacobi identity,
(see
[
14
]
, Section 6 and references therein). However, we show now that the Jacobi identity, as in
(
56
), is enough to recover the ordinary
Plücker relations as in Equation (
1
) of Theorem
1.1
.

Proposition C.2
.

Let
X_{i_{1}\cdots i_{r}}
denote the minor of
M\in\mathrm{M}_{r\times p}
(
r\leq p
) corresponding
to the submatrix formed by the columns
(i_{1},\dots,i_{r})
of
M
. Then, the relations

\sum_{k=1}^{r+1}(-1)^{k}X_{i_{1}\cdots i_{r-1}j_{k}}X_{j_{1}\cdots\tilde{\jmath}_{k}\dots j_{r+1}}=0

come directly from the Jacobi identity (
56
).

Proof.

Fix an
r\times p
matrix:

\displaystyle C=\begin{pmatrix}a_{11}&a_{12}&\cdots&a_{1p}\\
\cdot&\cdot&\cdots&\cdot\\
\cdot&\cdot&\cdots&\cdot\\
\cdot&\cdot&\cdots&\cdot\\
a_{r1}&a_{r2}&\cdots&a_{rp}\end{pmatrix}

Set any two sets
\{i_{1},...,i_{r}\}
and
\{j_{1},...,j_{r}\}
of ordered indices from
\{1,...,p\}
and define the following
2r\times 2r
matrix:

\displaystyle A=\begin{pmatrix}a_{1i_{1}}&\cdots&a_{1i_{r}}&a_{1j_{1}}&\cdots&a_{1j_{r}}\\
\cdot&\cdots&\cdot&\cdot&\cdots&\cdot\\
\cdot&\cdots&\cdot&\cdot&\cdots&\cdot\\
a_{ri_{1}}&\cdot\cdot\cdot&a_{ri_{r}}&a_{rj_{1}}&\cdot\cdot\cdot&a_{rj_{r}}\\
0&\cdots&0&1&\cdots&0\\
\cdot&\cdots&\cdot&\cdot&\cdots&\cdot\\
\cdot&\cdots&\cdot&\cdot&\cdots&\cdot\\
0&\cdots&0&0&\cdots&1\\
\end{pmatrix}\quad\text{which satisfies}\quad\det A=X_{i_{1}\cdots i_{r}}.

Suppose that
X_{i_{1}\cdots i_{r}}
is invertible. Now, fix

u=(r+1,\dots,2r)\qquad\hbox{and}\qquad v=(1,\dots,r),

which implies,

\tilde{u}=(1,\dots,r)\qquad\hbox{and}\qquad\tilde{v}=(r+1,\dots,2r).

Therefore,

A_{v}^{u}=\begin{pmatrix}a_{1j_{1}}&\cdots&a_{1j_{r}}\\
\cdot&\cdots&\cdot\\
\cdot&\cdots&\cdot\\
a_{rj_{1}}&\cdots&a_{rj_{r}}\end{pmatrix}\quad\text{and}\quad\det A_{v}^{u}=X_{j_{1}\cdots j_{r}}.

Putting this in Equation (
56
) one gets

\displaystyle X_{j_{1}\cdots j_{r}}=(-1)^{r}X_{i_{1}\cdots i_{r}}\det(A^{-1})^{\tilde{v}}_{\tilde{u}}.

(58)

Now,

\displaystyle(A^{-1})^{\tilde{v}}_{\tilde{u}}=\begin{bmatrix}(A^{-1})_{kl}\end{bmatrix}_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=1,...,r\\
l=r+1,...,2r\end{subarray}}

Using Cramer’s rule, we know that:

\displaystyle(A^{-1})_{kl}=\dfrac{\det A_{k}(e_{l})}{\det(A)}.

Therefore, one can easily compute,

\displaystyle(A^{-1})^{\tilde{v}}_{\tilde{u}}=\begin{pmatrix}-X_{j_{1}i_{2}\cdots i_{r}}X_{i_{1}\cdots i_{r}}^{-1}&-X_{j_{2}i_{2}\cdots i_{r}}X_{i_{1}\cdots i_{r}}^{-1}&\cdots&-X_{j_{r}i_{2}\cdots i_{r}}X_{i_{1}\cdots i_{r}}^{-1}\\
-X_{i_{1}j_{1}i_{3}\cdots i_{r}}X_{i_{1}\cdots i_{r}}^{-1}&-X_{i_{1}j_{2}i_{3}\cdots i_{r}}X_{i_{1}\cdots i_{r}}^{-1}&\cdots&-X_{i_{1}j_{r}i_{3}\cdots i_{r}}X_{i_{1}\cdots i_{r}}^{-1}\\
\cdot&\cdot&\cdots&\cdot\\
\cdot&\cdot&\cdots&\cdot\\
-X_{i_{1}i_{2}\cdots j_{1}i_{r}}X_{i_{1}\cdots i_{r}}^{-1}&-X_{i_{1}i_{2}\cdots j_{2}i_{r}}X_{i_{1}\cdots i_{r}}^{-1}&\cdots&-X_{i_{1}i_{2}\cdots j_{r}i_{r}}X_{i_{1}\cdots i_{r}}^{-1}\\
-X_{i_{1}i_{2}\cdots i_{r-1}j_{1}}X_{i_{1}\cdots i_{r}}^{-1}&-X_{i_{1}i_{2}\cdots i_{r-1}j_{2}}X_{i_{1}\cdots i_{r}}^{-1}&\cdots&-X_{i_{1}i_{2}\cdots i_{r-1}j_{r}}X_{i_{1}\cdots i_{r}}^{-1}\end{pmatrix}

The Laplace expansion of the determinant, using the first row, gives us:

\displaystyle\det(A^{-1})^{\tilde{v}}_{\tilde{u}}=(X_{i_{1}\cdots i_{r}})^{-1}\sum_{t=1}^{r}-X_{j_{t}i_{2}\cdots i_{r}}c_{1,t}

(59)

where
c_{1,t}
is the cofactor of the entry
\left((A^{-1})^{\tilde{v}}_{\tilde{u}}\right)_{1t}
. Therefore, substituting these in Equation (
58
) we get:

\displaystyle X_{j_{1}\cdots j_{r}}=(-1)^{r+1}\sum_{t=1}^{r}X_{j_{t}i_{2}\cdots i_{r}}c_{1,t}.

(60)

Now, to calculate
c_{1,1}
, consider the same calculations with a matrix
A^{\prime}
where we fix
j_{1}=i_{1}
. This gives us:

\displaystyle(A^{\prime-1})^{\tilde{v}}_{\tilde{u}}=\begin{pmatrix}-1&-X_{j_{2}i_{2}\cdots i_{r}}X_{i_{1}\cdots i_{r}}^{-1}&\cdots&-X_{j_{r}i_{2}..i_{r}}X_{i_{1}\cdots i_{r}}^{-1}\\
0&-X_{i_{1}j_{2}i_{3}..i_{r}}X_{i_{1}\cdots i_{r}}^{-1}&\cdots&-X_{i_{1}j_{r}i_{3}..i_{r}}X_{i_{1}\cdots i_{r}}^{-1}\\
.&.&\cdots&.\\
.&.&\cdots&.\\
0&-X_{i_{1}i_{2}\cdots j_{2}i_{r}}X_{i_{1}\cdots i_{r}}^{-1}&\cdots&-X_{i_{1}\cdots j_{r}i_{r}}X_{i_{1}\cdots i_{r}}^{-1}\\
0&-X_{i_{1}i_{2}\cdots i_{r-1}j_{2}}X_{i_{1}\cdots i_{r}}^{-1}&\cdots&-X_{i_{1}i_{2}\cdots i_{r-1}j_{r}}X_{i_{1}\cdots i_{r}}^{-1}\end{pmatrix}

and from Equation (
56
) it follows that:

\displaystyle X_{i_{1}j_{2}\cdots j_{r}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(-1)^{r}X_{i_{1}\cdots i_{r}}\det(A^{\prime-1})^{\tilde{v}}_{\tilde{u}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(-1)^{r+1}X_{i_{1}\cdots i_{r}}\,c_{1,1}

Hence, we get:

\displaystyle c_{1,1}=(-1)^{r+1}X_{i_{1}j_{2}\cdots j_{r}}(X_{i_{1}\cdots i_{r}})^{-1}.

Similarly, one can get:

\displaystyle c_{1,t}=(-1)^{r+1}X_{j_{1}\cdots j_{t-1}i_{1}j_{t+1}\cdots j_{r}}(X_{i_{1}\cdots i_{r}})^{-1}.

By putting this in Equation (
60
) we get:

\displaystyle X_{i_{1}\cdots i_{r}}X_{j_{1}\cdots j_{r}}=\sum_{t=1}^{r}X_{j_{t}i_{2}\cdots i_{r}}X_{j_{1}\cdots j_{t-1}i_{1}j_{t+1}\cdots j_{r}}

(61)

which are exactly the Plücker relations in (
1
), up to renaming some indices.
∎

We illustrate this result with an example given below.

Example C.3
.

Fix a
2\times 4
matrix

\displaystyle C=\begin{pmatrix}a_{11}&a_{12}&a_{13}&a_{14}\\
a_{21}&a_{22}&a_{23}&a_{24}\end{pmatrix}

and set
X_{ij}:=\det\begin{pmatrix}a_{1i}&a_{1j}\\
a_{2i}&a_{2j}\end{pmatrix}.
Suppose that
X_{12}
is invertible. Now, consider the following matrix:

\displaystyle A=\begin{pmatrix}a_{11}&a_{12}&a_{13}&a_{14}\\
a_{21}&a_{22}&a_{23}&a_{24}\\
0&0&1&0\\
0&0&0&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad\text{which satisfies}\qquad\det(A)=X_{12}.

By fixing
u=(3,4)
and
v=(1,2)
, we get:

\displaystyle\det A_{v}^{u}=\det\begin{pmatrix}a_{13}&a_{14}\\
a_{23}&a_{24}\end{pmatrix}=X_{34}.

Moreover, using Cramer’s rule, one can easily compute:

\displaystyle(A^{-1})^{\tilde{v}}_{\tilde{u}}=\begin{pmatrix}(A^{-1})_{13}&(A^{-1})_{14}\\
(A^{-1})_{23}&(A^{-1})_{24}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}X_{23}X_{12}^{-1}&X_{24}X_{12}^{-1}\\
-X_{13}X_{12}^{-1}&-X_{14}X_{12}^{-1}\end{pmatrix}.

Hence, substituting these values in Jacobi’s identity,

\displaystyle\det(A_{v}^{u})=\det(A)\det(A^{-1})^{\tilde{v}}_{\tilde{u}}

we get,

\displaystyle X_{34}=X_{12}\det\begin{pmatrix}X_{23}X_{12}^{-1}&X_{24}X_{12}^{-1}\\
-X_{13}X_{12}^{-1}&-X_{14}X_{12}^{-1}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\qquad X_{12}X_{34}-X_{13}X_{24}+X_{14}X_{23}=0

which is exactly the Plücker relation in this case.
