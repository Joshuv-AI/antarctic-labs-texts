Logit Distance Bounds Representational Similarity
Beatrix M. G. Nielsen, Emanuele Marconato, Luigi Gresele, Andrea Dittadi, Simon Buchholz

Abstract
For a broad family of discriminative models that includes autoregressive language models, identifiability results imply that if two models induce the same conditional distributions, then their internal representations agree up to an invertible linear transformation. We ask whether an analogous conclusion holds approximately when the distributions are close instead of equal. Building on the observation of 46 that closeness in KL divergence need not imply high linear representational similarity, we study a distributional distance based on logit differences and show that closeness in this distance does yield linear similarity guarantees. Specifically, we define a representational dissimilarity measure based on the models’ identifiability class and prove that it is bounded by the logit distance. We further show that, when model probabilities are bounded away from zero, KL divergence upper-bounds logit distance; yet the resulting bound fails to provide nontrivial control in practice. As a consequence, KL-based distillation can match a teacher’s predictions while failing to preserve linear representational properties, such as linear-probe recoverability of human-interpretable concepts. In distillation experiments on synthetic and image datasets, logit-distance distillation yields students with higher linear representational similarity and better preservation of the teacher’s linearly recoverable concepts.

Introduction

It is widely believed that the success of deep learning models depends on the data representation they learn
6
; yet it is unclear what properties “good” representations have in common
5
.
Accordingly, prior work studies when models with comparable performance exhibit similar internal representations
43
;
32
;
31
and how human-interpretable concepts are encoded within them
10
;
17
. Empirically, many such concepts can be predicted from internal representations by simple linear probes
2
;
29
, suggesting substantial linear structure in the representations of successful models
42
;
50
. However, this regularity is not universal
14
;
35
, and it remains unclear to what extent linear representational properties are consistently shared across models that perform comparably well on the same task.

We study this question for a broad family of discriminative models, including autoregressive next-token prediction. Prior identifiability results show that, under a suitable diversity assumption, if two such models induce the same conditional distribution, then their representations are equal up to an invertible linear transformation
28
;
57
;
34
, and one can characterize which linear properties are shared across the equivalence class
(
38
)
. A key question is whether these conclusions hold approximately when two models induce distributions that are close but not equal
(
11
)
.
46
show that the answer depends on how distributional closeness is measured: in particular, models can be arbitrarily close in KL divergence while their representations remain far from linearly related. On the other hand, robustness can be recovered under suitable divergences for which distributional closeness does imply representational similarity.

In this work, we ask: when two models in this family induce similar conditional distributions, to what extent do their representations agree up to an invertible linear map? We introduce a distributional distance based on logit differences and prove quantitative representation-level guarantees: small logit distance (i) implies high linear representational similarity, yielding an explicit lower bound on mCCA between representation spaces
54
;
43
and (ii) upper-bounds a representational dissimilarity measure that we design to respect the model family’s equivalence class. Finally, we clarify the (limited) extent to which KL control can recover such guarantees: if model probabilities are bounded away from zero, then the KL divergence upper-bounds the logit distance, but the resulting tight robustness bounds are insufficient for practical settings.

Our results have an immediate implication for knowledge distillation. Standard approaches train a student to match a teacher’s predictions by minimizing a KL divergence between output distributions
21
; yet our theory implies that a student can be very close to its teacher in KL while still learning representations far from being linearly aligned.
This motivates alternative objectives, and our theory suggests that minimizing logit differences—an approach considered in prior distillation work
(
3
;
41
;
30
)
—
should yield representations that are closer to being linearly equivalent.
Empirically, we compare vanilla (KL-based) distillation to logit-distance distillation and find that the latter preserves the teacher’s representational structure substantially better, both in terms of representational similarity measured by mCCA and our metric (on a synthetic dataset and
CIFAR

100

(
33
)
) and in terms of downstream linear properties recoverable by probing (on the SUB dataset
(
4
)
).
Overall, our work clarifies when linear properties are approximately shared across models whose distributions are close, and suggests a practical distillation takeaway: if representational similarity matters, KL is the wrong loss; a logit-distance objective is a better one.

Our contributions can be summarized as follows:

•

We analyze a logit-difference distance between conditional distributions and show it is a proper metric for the model family we consider (
Section

3
).

•

We show KL divergence can upper-bound logit distance under strong assumptions (
Section

3.1
), but the resulting bounds do not yield meaningful representation guarantees in typical regimes.

•

We propose a representation dissimilarity measure which is zero if and only if models are in the same linear equivalence class (
Section

3.3
).

•

We prove that small logit distance implies high linear representational similarity,
giving quantitative guarantees both for mCCA (
Section

3.2
), our dissimilarity measure (
Section

3.4
) and for linearly encoded concepts (
Section

4
).

•

In distillation experiments, we show empirically that logit matching preserves the teacher’s representational structure better than KL-based distillation (
Section

5
).

Preliminaries

In this section, we introduce the notation and model family, recall its linear identifiability under a diversity assumption, and formulate the question of approximate identifiability that motivates our analysis.

Let
\mathcal{X}
denote the input space, either continuous or discrete.
We assume a data distribution
p_{\bm{\mathrm{x}}}
supported on
\mathrm{supp}(p_{\bm{\mathrm{x}}})\subseteq\mathcal{X}
, and a finite label set
\mathcal{Y}
of size
k
, such as tokens in a language model or class labels in a classification problem. We focus on a broad model family which encompasses autoregressive language models
(

)
, methods for self-supervised pretraining for image classification
(

We denote this model family by

\displaystyle p_{{\bm{\mathrm{f}}},{\bm{\mathrm{g}}}}(y\mid{\bm{\mathrm{x}}})=\frac{\exp({\bm{\mathrm{f}}}({\bm{\mathrm{x}}})^{\top}{\bm{\mathrm{g}}}(y))}{\sum_{y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Y}}\exp({\bm{\mathrm{f}}}({\bm{\mathrm{x}}})^{\top}{\bm{\mathrm{g}}}(y^{\prime}))}\;.

(1)

We also indicate the logits generated by the model

{\bm{\mathrm{u}}}({\bm{\mathrm{x}}}):=\big({\bm{\mathrm{f}}}({\bm{\mathrm{x}}})^{\top}{\bm{\mathrm{g}}}(y_{1}),\,\ldots,\,{\bm{\mathrm{f}}}({\bm{\mathrm{x}}})^{\top}{\bm{\mathrm{g}}}(y_{k})\big)^{\top}\,,

(2)

so the probability distribution can be equivalently
described as a categorical distribution with probability vector
\mathrm{softmax}({\bm{\mathrm{u}}}({\bm{\mathrm{x}}}))
.
Without loss of generality, we assume the unembeddings to be centered with zero mean,

1

\textstyle\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}{\bm{\mathrm{g}}}(y)=\mathbf{0}\implies\sum_{i=1}^{k}u({\bm{\mathrm{x}}})_{i}=0\,.

(3)

When studying this model family, it is convenient to introduce the

of a model
({\bm{\mathrm{f}}},{\bm{\mathrm{g}}})\in\Theta
. For any pivot label

, these are given by defining
\tilde{\bm{\mathrm{g}}}(y)\coloneq{\bm{\mathrm{g}}}(y)-{\bm{\mathrm{g}}}(\tilde{y})
.
We then introduce the matrix of shifted unembeddings

\tilde{\bm{\mathrm{g}}}

\tilde{\bm{\mathrm{L}}}_{\mathcal{J}}:=\begin{pmatrix}\tilde{\bm{\mathrm{g}}}(y_{1})&\cdots&\tilde{\bm{\mathrm{g}}}(y_{m})\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}\,.

(4)

Identifiability of the model class.

Several works
(
28
;
57
;
34
;
38
;
56
;
46
)

have investigated the symmetries belonging to the model class for which, two different choices of embeddings and unembeddings, say
({\bm{\mathrm{f}}},{\bm{\mathrm{g}}}),({\bm{\mathrm{f}}}^{\prime},{\bm{\mathrm{g}}}^{\prime})\in\Theta
, generate the same conditional probability distribution.
In our setting, similar to
(
46
)
, we study the case where labels are abundant and their number exceeds that of the representations, i.e.,
k>m
.
Below, we report the linear identifiability result from
(
57
)
.
This requires an extra condition, known as diversity, which can be interpreted as a restriction to models that span the whole representation space
(
38
)
.

Assumption 2.1

(Diversity)
.

A model
({\bm{\mathrm{f}}},{\bm{\mathrm{g}}})\in\Theta
satisfies the diversity condition if

\mathrm{dim}\big(\mathrm{span}\{{\bm{\mathrm{u}}}({\bm{\mathrm{x}}}):{\bm{\mathrm{x}}}\in\mathrm{supp}(p_{\bm{\mathrm{x}}})\}\big)=m\,.

(5)

In particular, there exists a choice of
\tilde{y}\in\mathcal{Y}
and
\mathcal{J}\subseteq\mathcal{Y}\setminus\{\tilde{y}\}
, for which
\tilde{\bm{\mathrm{L}}}_{\mathcal{J}}
(
Eq.

4
) is invertible.

This condition gives the following result.

Theorem 2.2

(Linear Identifiability)
.

For two models
({\bm{\mathrm{f}}},{\bm{\mathrm{g}}}),({\bm{\mathrm{f}}}^{\prime},{\bm{\mathrm{g}}}^{\prime})\in\Theta

that satisfy the diversity condition (
2.1
),
let
\tilde{y}\in\mathcal{Y}
and
\mathcal{J}\subseteq\mathcal{Y}\setminus\{\tilde{y}\}
be a choice of pivot point and
m
labels such that
\tilde{\bm{\mathrm{L}}}_{\mathcal{J}}
is invertible. Let
{\tilde{\bm{\mathrm{A}}}_{\mathcal{J}}\coloneqq\tilde{\bm{\mathrm{L}}}_{\mathcal{J}}^{-\top}\tilde{\bm{\mathrm{L}}}_{\mathcal{J}}^{\prime\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}}
.
Let
\sim_{L}
be the equivalence relation in
\Theta
, such that

({\bm{\mathrm{f}}},{\bm{\mathrm{g}}})\sim_{L}({\bm{\mathrm{f}}}^{\prime},{\bm{\mathrm{g}}}^{\prime})
if and only if there exists a matrix
{\bm{\mathrm{A}}}
such that

\displaystyle{\bm{\mathrm{f}}}({\bm{\mathrm{x}}})={\bm{\mathrm{A}}}{\bm{\mathrm{f}}}^{\prime}({\bm{\mathrm{x}}}),\quad\tilde{\bm{\mathrm{g}}}(y)={\bm{\mathrm{A}}}^{-\top}\tilde{\bm{\mathrm{g}}}^{\prime}(y)\,.

(6)

Then we have that

\displaystyle\begin{split}&p_{{\bm{\mathrm{f}}},{\bm{\mathrm{g}}}}(y\mid{\bm{\mathrm{x}}})=p_{{\bm{\mathrm{f}}}^{\prime},{\bm{\mathrm{g}}}^{\prime}}(y\mid{\bm{\mathrm{x}}}),\;\forall({\bm{\mathrm{x}}},y)\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}\\
&\iff({\bm{\mathrm{f}}},{\bm{\mathrm{g}}})\sim_{L}({\bm{\mathrm{f}}}^{\prime},{\bm{\mathrm{g}}}^{\prime})\,.\end{split}

(7)

In particular, we can set
{\bm{\mathrm{A}}}=\tilde{\bm{\mathrm{A}}}_{\mathcal{J}}
.

This result shows that when the distributions generated by two models are equal and satisfy the diversity condition, their representations are related by an invertible linear transformation given by
\tilde{\bm{\mathrm{A}}}_{\mathcal{J}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
.
We show in
Proposition

C.1
that the form of
\tilde{\bm{\mathrm{A}}}_{\mathcal{J}}
does not depend on the choice of
\tilde{y}
and
\mathcal{J}\subseteq\mathcal{Y}\setminus\{\tilde{y}\}
as long as the matrix
\tilde{\bm{\mathrm{L}}}_{\mathcal{J}}
is invertible.

Closeness in distribution and representational similarity.

The result in
Theorem

2.2
assumes
exact
equality of conditional distributions. In practice, however, independently trained models will almost never satisfy this condition exactly, even when trained on the same data. This raises the question of whether the identifiability conclusion is
robust
: namely, whether models whose distributions are merely close, rather than equal, must also have similar internal representations up to the model class’s intrinsic symmetry (an invertible linear change of basis).

This question was first systematically investigated by
46
. Addressing it requires specifying (i) a notion of distance between conditional distributions and (ii) a notion of dissimilarity between representations. For the latter, we require the dissimilarity to vanish for
\sim_{L}
-linearly equivalent models. This is because, for any
p_{{\bm{\mathrm{f}}},{\bm{\mathrm{g}}}}(y\mid{\bm{\mathrm{x}}})
, different reparametrizations of
({\bm{\mathrm{f}}},{\bm{\mathrm{g}}})
exists that do not change the probability distribution (by
Theorem

2.2
).
With this requirement in place, our goal is to identify distributional distances for which small discrepancies in conditional distributions provably imply small representational dissimilarity.

3
The Logit Distance and Connection to Representational Similarity

We now define a metric between distributions from our model family.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
p_{{\bm{\mathrm{f}}},{\bm{\mathrm{g}}}},p_{{\bm{\mathrm{f}}}^{\prime},{\bm{\mathrm{g}}}^{\prime}}^{\prime}
be two sets of conditional distributions arising from two models from our model class. We denote by
d_{\mathrm{logit}}
the following logit distance

\displaystyle d_{\mathrm{logit}}^{2}(p_{{\bm{\mathrm{f}}},{\bm{\mathrm{g}}}},p_{{\bm{\mathrm{f}}}^{\prime},{\bm{\mathrm{g}}}^{\prime}}^{\prime})=\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{\mathrm{x}}}\sim p_{\bm{\mathrm{x}}}}\left\|{\bm{\mathrm{u}}}({\bm{\mathrm{x}}})-{\bm{\mathrm{u}}}^{\prime}({\bm{\mathrm{x}}})\right\|_{2}^{2}\;.

(8)

Notice that
d_{\mathrm{logit}}
coincides with the square root of the mean squared error between models’ logits. This can be written in terms of log-probabilities (see Appendix
D.1
for details), and the expression

\lVert{\bm{\mathrm{u}}}({\bm{\mathrm{x}}})-{\bm{\mathrm{u}}}^{\prime}({\bm{\mathrm{x}}})\rVert_{2}
corresponds to the Aitchison distance (see
Section

D.2
) of the conditional distributions

p_{{\bm{\mathrm{f}}},{\bm{\mathrm{g}}}}(\cdot|{\bm{\mathrm{x}}})
and
p_{{\bm{\mathrm{f}}}^{\prime},{\bm{\mathrm{g}}}^{\prime}}(\cdot|{\bm{\mathrm{x}}})

which is a metric in compositional data analysis

(
1
;
51
)
.
We show in
Section

D.3
that
Definition

3.1
is a metric (potentially with infinite value) between distributions from models in our model class. A sufficient condition for
d_{\mathrm{logit}}(p,p^{\prime})<\infty
, is that the probabilities are lower bounded by some
\tau>0
for all
y\in\mathcal{Y}
and
{\bm{\mathrm{x}}}\in\mathcal{X}
.

3.1
KL Divergence Bounds Logit Distance

We consider the relationship between the
logit distance
and the Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence which both measure the closeness between two distributions.
Following
46
,
given
({\bm{\mathrm{f}}},{\bm{\mathrm{g}}}),({\bm{\mathrm{f}}}^{\prime},{\bm{\mathrm{g}}}^{\prime})\in\Theta
,
we consider

d_{\mathrm{KL}}(p_{{\bm{\mathrm{f}}},{\bm{\mathrm{g}}}},p_{{\bm{\mathrm{f}}},{\bm{\mathrm{g}}}^{\prime}})\coloneq\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{\mathrm{x}}}\sim p_{\bm{\mathrm{x}}}}[\mathrm{KL}(p_{{\bm{\mathrm{f}}},{\bm{\mathrm{g}}}}(\cdot\mid{\bm{\mathrm{x}}})||p_{{\bm{\mathrm{f}}}^{\prime},{\bm{\mathrm{g}}}^{\prime}}(\cdot\mid{\bm{\mathrm{x}}}))]\,,

(9)

which is related to the cross-entropy loss

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{p}({\bm{\mathrm{f}}}^{\prime},{\bm{\mathrm{g}}}^{\prime})=\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{\mathrm{x}}}\sim p_{\bm{\mathrm{x}}},y\sim p(y\mid{\bm{\mathrm{x}}})}[-\log p_{{\bm{\mathrm{f}}}^{\prime},{\bm{\mathrm{g}}}^{\prime}}(y|{\bm{\mathrm{x}}})]

(10)

for a well-specified model through

\displaystyle d_{\mathrm{KL}}(p_{{\bm{\mathrm{f}}},{\bm{\mathrm{g}}}},p_{{\bm{\mathrm{f}}},{\bm{\mathrm{g}}}^{\prime}})=\mathcal{L}_{p_{{\bm{\mathrm{f}}},{\bm{\mathrm{g}}}}}({\bm{\mathrm{f}}}^{\prime},{\bm{\mathrm{g}}}^{\prime})-\mathcal{L}_{p_{{\bm{\mathrm{f}}},{\bm{\mathrm{g}}}}}({{\bm{\mathrm{f}}}},{{\bm{\mathrm{g}}}})\;.

(11)

This means that learning a model
({\bm{\mathrm{f}}}^{\prime},{\bm{\mathrm{g}}}^{\prime})\in\Theta
on data sampled from a distribution
p_{{\bm{\mathrm{f}}},{\bm{\mathrm{g}}}}
in the model class, will be equivalent in expectation to minimizing
d_{\mathrm{KL}}(p_{{\bm{\mathrm{f}}},{\bm{\mathrm{g}}}},p_{{\bm{\mathrm{f}}}^{\prime},{\bm{\mathrm{g}}}^{\prime}})
.
However,
46
show that the equivalence relation
\sim_{L}
is not robust to small changes in the probability distribution as measured by
d_{\mathrm{KL}}
. Specifically, they construct pairs
({\bm{\mathrm{f}}},{\bm{\mathrm{g}}})
and
({\bm{\mathrm{f}}}^{\prime},{\bm{\mathrm{g}}}^{\prime})
with arbitrarily small KL divergence, yet whose representations cannot be made close by any invertible linear map (low
m_{\mathrm{CCA}}
). This can happen because, without additional regularity assumptions on
\Theta
, models may assign extremely small probabilities to some events.
Changes concentrated on such near-zero-probability events can correspond to large (potentially nonlinear) changes in representation geometry while incurring only a negligible increase in
d_{\mathrm{KL}}
.
To rule out these corner cases, we restrict attention to models
({\bm{\mathrm{f}}},{\bm{\mathrm{g}}})\in\Theta
whose probability assignments are uniformly lower bounded by some constant
\tau>0
.

Assumption 3.2

(
\tau
-lower bounded)
.

For
\tau>0
, we say that a model
({\bm{\mathrm{f}}},{\bm{\mathrm{g}}})\in\Theta
is
\tau
-lower bounded if, for
p_{\bm{\mathrm{x}}}
-almost every
{\bm{\mathrm{x}}}\in\mathcal{X}
, we have

\min_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}p_{{\bm{\mathrm{f}}},{\bm{\mathrm{g}}}}(y\mid{\bm{\mathrm{x}}})\geq\tau\,.

(12)

We also show in
Section

E.1
that this is equivalent to having bounded embeddings and unembeddings.
We can now prove the next result
(full proof in
Section

E.2
).

Theorem 3.3
.

For two models
({\bm{\mathrm{f}}},{\bm{\mathrm{g}}}),({\bm{\mathrm{f}}}^{\prime},{\bm{\mathrm{g}}}^{\prime})\in\Theta
,
we have

d_{\mathrm{logit}}^{2}(p_{{\bm{\mathrm{f}}},{\bm{\mathrm{g}}}},p_{{\bm{\mathrm{f}}}^{\prime},{\bm{\mathrm{g}}}^{\prime}}^{\prime})\geq 2d_{\mathrm{KL}}(p_{{\bm{\mathrm{f}}},{\bm{\mathrm{g}}}},p_{{\bm{\mathrm{f}}}^{\prime},{\bm{\mathrm{g}}}^{\prime}})\,.

(13)

If there exists
0<\tau<1/3
such that both
({\bm{\mathrm{f}}},{\bm{\mathrm{g}}})
and
({\bm{\mathrm{f}}}^{\prime},{\bm{\mathrm{g}}}^{\prime})
are
\tau
-lower bounded (
3.2
), then

d_{\mathrm{logit}}^{2}(p_{{\bm{\mathrm{f}}},{\bm{\mathrm{g}}}},p_{{\bm{\mathrm{f}}}^{\prime},{\bm{\mathrm{g}}}^{\prime}}^{\prime})\leq\frac{4\log(\tau)^{2}}{\tau}d_{\mathrm{KL}}(p_{{\bm{\mathrm{f}}},{\bm{\mathrm{g}}}},p_{{\bm{\mathrm{f}}}^{\prime},{\bm{\mathrm{g}}}^{\prime}})\,.

(14)

In contrast to the general setting discussed above—where arbitrarily small
d_{\mathrm{KL}}
may still correspond to highly dissimilar representations—
Theorem

3.3
shows that, under the additional
\tau
-boundedness assumption, the KL divergence
does
bound the logit distance. As we show below, this in turn implies that
d_{\mathrm{KL}}
also bounds representation dissimilarity. However, the upper bound in (
14
) is only quantitatively meaningful when
d_{\mathrm{KL}}=O(\tau)
, since the bound scales as
\tau^{-1}
up to logarithmic factors. As
\tau\leq 1/k
in a
k
-class setting and is typically much smaller in practice, this condition can be restrictive.
We remark that a similar but weaker bound holds when only one of the models (e.g., a constrained student) is
\tau
-bounded (see
Lemma

E.4
). Moreover, the dependence on
\tau
is tight up to logarithmic terms.

3.2
Logit Distance Bounds Mean CCA

We next show that the representations are approximately linearly related when the distance between the logits of two models is small. Canonical Correlation Analysis (CCA)
(
22
)
is a common way of measuring whether sets of vectors are linearly related (see
Section

B.1
).
We denote by
m_{\mathrm{CCA}}
the mean canonical correlation which is 1 when two vector-valued variables are almost surely linear transformations of each other. In our setting, the pair of variables will be either a pair of embeddings or unembeddings from two different models.

Theorem 3.4
.

Let
m
be the dimension of the representations.
Let
\mu_{m}
be the
m
-th eigenvalue of the matrix
{\bm{\mathrm{\Sigma}}}_{{\bm{\mathrm{u}}},{\bm{\mathrm{u}}}}\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{\mathrm{x}}}\sim p_{{\bm{\mathrm{x}}}}}\big[{\bm{\mathrm{u}}}({\bm{\mathrm{x}}}){\bm{\mathrm{u}}}({\bm{\mathrm{x}}})^{\top}\big]-\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{\mathrm{x}}}\sim p_{{\bm{\mathrm{x}}}}}\big[{\bm{\mathrm{u}}}({\bm{\mathrm{x}}})]\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{\mathrm{x}}}\sim p_{{\bm{\mathrm{x}}}}}\big[{\bm{\mathrm{u}}}({\bm{\mathrm{x}}})^{\top}\big]
.
Assume
\mu_{m}>0
. Then we have

\displaystyle m_{\mathrm{CCA}}({\bm{\mathrm{f}}}({\bm{\mathrm{x}}}),{\bm{\mathrm{f}}}^{\prime}({\bm{\mathrm{x}}}))\geq 1-\frac{d_{\mathrm{logit}}^{2}(p_{{\bm{\mathrm{f}}},{\bm{\mathrm{g}}}},p_{{\bm{\mathrm{f}}}^{\prime},{\bm{\mathrm{g}}}^{\prime}}^{\prime})}{m\mu_{m}}

(15)

and the same bound holds for
m_{\mathrm{CCA}}({\bm{\mathrm{g}}}(y),{\bm{\mathrm{g}}}^{\prime}(y))
.

Full proof in
Section

F.4
.
We emphasize that this result is invariant to
linear transformations
({\bm{\mathrm{f}}},{\bm{\mathrm{g}}})\mapsto({\bm{\mathrm{A}}}{\bm{\mathrm{f}}},{\bm{\mathrm{A}}}^{-\top}{\bm{\mathrm{g}}})
.
The theorem shows that, with a small difference in distributions in terms of
d_{\mathrm{logit}}
of two models, we get similar representations in terms of
m_{\mathrm{CCA}}
. In particular, if the distributions of
p_{{\bm{\mathrm{f}}},{\bm{\mathrm{g}}}}
and
p_{{\bm{\mathrm{f}}}^{\prime},{\bm{\mathrm{g}}}^{\prime}}
agree, we conclude that
m_{\mathrm{CCA}}({\bm{\mathrm{f}}}({\bm{\mathrm{x}}}),{\bm{\mathrm{f}}}^{\prime}({\bm{\mathrm{x}}}))=1
,
which means that there exists a matrix
{\bm{\mathrm{B}}}
such that
{\bm{\mathrm{f}}}({\bm{\mathrm{x}}})={\bm{\mathrm{B}}}{\bm{\mathrm{f}}}^{\prime}({\bm{\mathrm{x}}})
for all
{\bm{\mathrm{x}}}

and similarly
{\bm{\mathrm{g}}}(y)=\tilde{\mathbf{B}}{\bm{\mathrm{g}}}(y)
for another matrix
\tilde{\mathbf{B}}
.
Therefore this result generalizes the linear identifiability of
{\bm{\mathrm{f}}}
and
{\bm{\mathrm{g}}}
in
Theorem

2.2
to models that are similar in terms of
d_{\mathrm{logit}}
or
d_{\mathrm{KL}}
(via Theorem
3.3
).
However, having
m_{\mathrm{CCA}}({\bm{\mathrm{f}}}({\bm{\mathrm{x}}}),{\bm{\mathrm{f}}}^{\prime}({\bm{\mathrm{x}}}))=1
and small KL divergence is not sufficient to conclude that the joint representations

({\bm{\mathrm{f}}},{\bm{\mathrm{g}}})
and
({\bm{\mathrm{f}}}^{\prime},{\bm{\mathrm{g}}}^{\prime})
are similar (see
Appendix

G
for an example), and while we can conclude that
{\bm{\mathrm{B}}}=\tilde{\mathbf{B}}^{-\top}
holds for
d_{\mathrm{logit}}=0
and thus recover the identifiability result, no
rates for the error can be obtained when
d_{\mathrm{logit}}
is positive (see Appendix
F.3
).
This motivates the study of further dissimilarity measures which naturally incorporate the relation of embeddings and unembeddings and therefore better match the linear identifiability result.

3.3
Linear Identifiability Dissimilarity

In this section we define a dissimilarity measure,
d_{\mathrm{rep}}
, between representations inspired by
Theorem

2.2
, in the sense that it is zero if and only if the representations are linearly equivalent.
We first notice that linear equivalence in
Theorem

2.2
, mediated by
\tilde{\bm{\mathrm{A}}}_{\mathcal{J}}
, necessitates a choice of pivot point and
m
other labels so that
\tilde{\bm{\mathrm{L}}}_{\mathcal{J}}
is invertible. Since we do not want the invertibility to depend on the choice, we introduce the following assumption:

Assumption 3.5
.

A model

\mathrm{span}\{{\bm{\mathrm{f}}}({\bm{\mathrm{x}}}):{\bm{\mathrm{x}}}\in\mathrm{supp}(p_{\bm{\mathrm{x}}})\}=\mathbb{R}^{m}

This assumption naturally requires that for any choice of pivot point,
\tilde{y}

\tilde{\bm{\mathrm{g}}}(y)
are in

Corollary 3.6
.

For two models
({\bm{\mathrm{f}}},{\bm{\mathrm{g}}}),({\bm{\mathrm{f}}}^{\prime},{\bm{\mathrm{g}}}^{\prime})\in\Theta
that satisfy

. If

Definition 3.7
.

Let

\displaystyle d_{\mathrm{rep}}^{2}(({\bm{\mathrm{f}}},{\bm{\mathrm{g}}}),({\bm{\mathrm{f}}}^{\prime},{\bm{\mathrm{g}}}^{\prime}))\coloneq\frac{1}{kJ}\sum_{\tilde{y}\in\mathcal{Y}}\sum_{\mathcal{J}\subseteq\mathcal{Y}\setminus\{\tilde{y}\}}\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{\mathrm{x}}}\sim p_{\bm{\mathrm{x}}}}\left\|{\bm{\mathrm{f}}}({\bm{\mathrm{x}}})-\tilde{\bm{\mathrm{A}}}_{\mathcal{J}}{\bm{\mathrm{f}}}^{\prime}({\bm{\mathrm{x}}})\right\|_{2}^{2}\;.

The

in

3.7
averages the squared distance between
{\bm{\mathrm{f}}}({\bm{\mathrm{x}}})
and
\tilde{\bm{\mathrm{A}}}_{\mathcal{J}}{\bm{\mathrm{f}}}^{\prime}({\bm{\mathrm{x}}})
over every choice of pivot and labels and every input. We see that by

({\bm{\mathrm{f}}},{\bm{\mathrm{g}}})

3.4
Logit Distance Bounds Representational Distance

In this section, we show that, for our model family, the logit distance is zero if and only if the linear identifiability dissimilarity vanishes and that the former bounds the latter.
As a corollary, we show that this bound extends also to KL.
Full proofs for this section are in
Appendix

H
.

We note that, for two models
({\bm{\mathrm{f}}},{\bm{\mathrm{g}}}),({\bm{\mathrm{f}}}^{\prime},{\bm{\mathrm{g}}}^{\prime})\in\Theta

that satisfy
3.5
,
the squared logit distance can be written as a sum of terms of the form
\big\|{\bm{\mathrm{B}}}^{-\top}\big({\bm{\mathrm{f}}}({\bm{\mathrm{x}}})-\tilde{\bm{\mathrm{A}}}_{\mathcal{J}}{\bm{\mathrm{f}}}^{\prime}({\bm{\mathrm{x}}})\big)\big\|_{2}^{2}
for a matrix
{\bm{\mathrm{B}}}

related to the SVD decomposition of
\tilde{\bm{\mathrm{L}}}_{\mathcal{J}}

(see
Theorem

H.1
for the precise statement).
This shows that the logit difference is naturally connected to the linear identifiability dissimilarity and gives us the following corollary:

Corollary 3.8
.

For
({\bm{\mathrm{f}}},{\bm{\mathrm{g}}}),({\bm{\mathrm{f}}}^{\prime},{\bm{\mathrm{g}}}^{\prime})\in\Theta
satisfying
3.5
, we have

\displaystyle d_{\mathrm{rep}}(({\bm{\mathrm{f}}},{\bm{\mathrm{g}}}),({\bm{\mathrm{f}}}^{\prime},{\bm{\mathrm{g}}}^{\prime}))=0\iff d_{\mathrm{logit}}(p_{{\bm{\mathrm{f}}},{\bm{\mathrm{g}}}},p_{{\bm{\mathrm{f}}}^{\prime},{\bm{\mathrm{g}}}^{\prime}})=0\,.

Moreover,
we can upper (and lower, see

and the singular values of

Theorem 3.9

Let

(17)

Interestingly, this result highlights that the connection between distributional distance and representational dissimilarity is tightly connected to both embeddings and unembeddings.
In fact, the bound depends on the minimum singular value among the unembeddings matrices

i.e.

\sigma_{\min}

In particular, small values of

This also links to a bound on

Applications: Distillation and Interpretability

Knowledge distillation
(KD) aims to train a compact student model to match the predictive distribution of a larger teacher.
In its original formulation
(
21
)
, KD combines a supervised cross-entropy loss,
\mathcal{L}_{p_{\mathcal{D}}}
, on the data distribution
p_{\mathcal{D}}(y\mid{\bm{\mathrm{x}}})
with a KL divergence term that aligns the student distribution with that of a fixed teacher
({\bm{\mathrm{f}}},{\bm{\mathrm{g}}})
with distribution
p_{{\bm{\mathrm{f}}},{\bm{\mathrm{g}}}}
. The student
({\bm{\mathrm{f}}}^{\prime},{\bm{\mathrm{g}}}^{\prime})
with distribution
p_{{\bm{\mathrm{f}}}^{\prime}{\bm{\mathrm{g}}}^{\prime}}
is obtained by solving

({\bm{\mathrm{f}}}^{\prime},{\bm{\mathrm{g}}}^{\prime})\in\argmin_{({\bm{\mathrm{f}}}^{*},{\bm{\mathrm{g}}}^{*})\in\Theta}\big(\mathcal{L}_{p_{\mathcal{D}}}({\bm{\mathrm{f}}}^{*},{\bm{\mathrm{g}}}^{*})+d_{\mathrm{KL}}(p_{{\bm{\mathrm{f}}},{\bm{\mathrm{g}}}},p_{{\bm{\mathrm{f}}}^{*},{\bm{\mathrm{g}}}^{*}})\big)

typically using only a small subset of training samples.
A large body of work explores refinements of this objective to better match the teacher distribution
(
37
)
,
including alternative formulations of the KL term
(
65
)

or by considering an equivalent loss to
d_{\mathrm{logit}}

(
44
;
30
)
.
When available, the teacher’s intermediate activations can further guide training
(
58
;
23
)
, with regression on embeddings or unembeddings encouraging closer representational alignment.

Our analysis highlights that, when only teacher probabilities are available,
the original
d_{\mathrm{KL}}
minimization does not significantly bound representational similarity (as measured with
d_{\mathrm{rep}}
) between teacher and student.
When only teacher probabilities or logits are available,
a better learning target is to minimize an equivalent loss to
d_{\mathrm{logit}}
.
Apart from using
d_{\mathrm{logit}}
as a loss directly, we consider training with a
L_{1}
variant of the logit distance between teacher logits
{\bm{\mathrm{u}}}
and student
{\bm{\mathrm{u}}}^{\prime}
, that is

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{logit}}^{1}(p_{{\bm{\mathrm{f}}},{\bm{\mathrm{g}}}},p_{{\bm{\mathrm{f}}}^{\prime},{\bm{\mathrm{g}}}^{\prime}})\coloneq\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{\mathrm{x}}}\sim p_{\bm{\mathrm{x}}}}\left\|{\bm{\mathrm{u}}}({\bm{\mathrm{x}}})-{\bm{\mathrm{u}}}^{\prime}({\bm{\mathrm{x}}})\right\|_{1}\,,

(19)

Using
\mathcal{L}^{1}_{\mathrm{logit}}
improves training stability
(
52
)
and, importantly,
still leads to decreasing
d_{\mathrm{logit}}(p_{{\bm{\mathrm{f}}},{\bm{\mathrm{g}}}},p_{{\bm{\mathrm{f}}}^{\prime},{\bm{\mathrm{g}}}^{\prime}})
:

Proposition 4.1
.

For two models
({\bm{\mathrm{f}}},{\bm{\mathrm{g}}}),({\bm{\mathrm{f}}}^{\prime},{\bm{\mathrm{g}}}^{\prime})\in\Theta
which both satisfy Assumption
3.2
for some
\tau>0
,
then

\displaystyle d_{\mathrm{logit}}^{2}(p_{{\bm{\mathrm{f}}},{\bm{\mathrm{g}}}},p_{{\bm{\mathrm{f}}}^{\prime},{\bm{\mathrm{g}}}^{\prime}})\leq 2|\log(\tau)|\mathcal{L}^{1}_{\mathrm{logit}}(p_{{\bm{\mathrm{f}}},{\bm{\mathrm{g}}}},p_{{\bm{\mathrm{f}}}^{\prime},{\bm{\mathrm{g}}}^{\prime}})\,.

(20)

In particular, with this result we have that
minimizing
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{logit}}^{1}
will naturally reduce
d_{\mathrm{logit}}
between the teacher and student distributions, independently of the representation dimensionality of the two. Full proof in

Interpretable properties.

Prior works have investigated whether model embeddings linearly encode latent, interpretable concepts
(
42
;
29
;
14
)
.
For example,
40
showed that the truth value of a statement
{\bm{\mathrm{x}}}
can be approximated by a linear function of its language model embedding
{\bm{\mathrm{f}}}({\bm{\mathrm{x}}})
.
Similar forms of linear separability have also been studied in pretrained neural networks for visual concepts in image classification
(
2
)
.

Formally,
we define a
categorical concept
as a function
{\bm{\mathrm{h}}}:\mathcal{X}\to\Delta^{C-1}
,
where
\Delta^{C-1}

is the simplex over
C\geq 2
values, giving a distribution over concept values

p_{\bm{\mathrm{h}}}(c\mid{\bm{\mathrm{x}}}):={\bm{\mathrm{h}}}({\bm{\mathrm{x}}})_{c},\;\forall c\in\{1,\ldots,C\}\,.

(21)

Inspired by previous works
(
2
;
55
)
,
we propose a definition that captures linear separability of concepts from model embeddings:

Definition 4.2

(Linearly encoded concept
{\bm{\mathrm{h}}}
in
{\bm{\mathrm{f}}}
)
.

For a concept
{\bm{\mathrm{h}}}:\mathcal{X}\to\Delta^{C-1}
, with
C\geq 2
,
giving a distribution
p_{\bm{\mathrm{h}}}
as in
Eq.

21
,
we say that that
{\bm{\mathrm{h}}}
is
linearly encoded
in
{\bm{\mathrm{f}}}
if
there exist
C
linear weights
{\bm{\mathrm{w}}}_{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
and biases
b_{c}\in\mathbb{R}
such that, for all
c\in\{1,\ldots,C\}
,

\frac{\exp\big({\bm{\mathrm{w}}}_{c}^{\top}{\bm{\mathrm{f}}}({\bm{\mathrm{x}}})+b_{c}\big)}{\sum_{j=1}^{C}\exp\big({\bm{\mathrm{w}}}_{j}^{\top}{\bm{\mathrm{f}}}({\bm{\mathrm{x}}})+b_{j}\big)}=p_{\bm{\mathrm{h}}}(c\mid{\bm{\mathrm{x}}})\,.

(22)

Then, we indicate with
{\bm{\mathrm{W}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times C}
the matrix of weights
{\bm{\mathrm{w}}}_{c}
and with
{\bm{\mathrm{b}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{C}
the vector of biases
b_{c}
.

In words,
Definition

4.2
states that the probability assignments over concept values defined by
p_{\bm{\mathrm{h}}}
can be recovered through a linear function of the model embeddings.
This also implies that the embedding space is partitioned linearly into convex regions, each corresponding to the most likely concept label
(
60
)
, as illustrated in
Fig.

1
(left).
Crucially, we show that
d_{\mathrm{logit}}
bounds the degree to which concepts are shared between models that are close:

Theorem 4.3
.

For two models
({\bm{\mathrm{f}}},{\bm{\mathrm{g}}}),({\bm{\mathrm{f}}}^{\prime},{\bm{\mathrm{g}}}^{\prime})\in\Theta
and a concept
{\bm{\mathrm{h}}}:\mathcal{X}\to\Delta^{C-1}
with
C\geq 2
,
if
{\bm{\mathrm{h}}}
is linearly encoded in
{\bm{\mathrm{f}}}
,
then

\displaystyle\textstyle\min_{{\bm{\mathrm{W}}}^{\prime},{\bm{\mathrm{b}}}^{\prime}}d_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(p_{\bm{\mathrm{h}}}

\displaystyle,\mathrm{softmax}\big({\bm{\mathrm{W}}}^{{}^{\prime}\top}{\bm{\mathrm{f}}}^{\prime}(\cdot)+{\bm{\mathrm{b}}}^{\prime})\big)\leq\frac{1}{2}\lVert{\bm{\mathrm{A}}}\rVert_{op}^{2}d_{\mathrm{logit}}^{2}(p_{{\bm{\mathrm{f}}},{\bm{\mathrm{g}}}},p_{{\bm{\mathrm{f}}}^{\prime},{\bm{\mathrm{g}}}^{\prime}})\,,

(23)

where
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}
denotes the operator norm,
{\bm{\mathrm{A}}}
is determined by
{\bm{\mathrm{W}}}={\bm{\mathrm{L}}}{\bm{\mathrm{A}}}
,
{\bm{\mathrm{L}}}
is the unembedding matrix, and
{\bm{\mathrm{W}}}

{\bm{\mathrm{b}}}
are obtained from
Definition

4.2

This result (proof in
Appendix

(

6
Discussion and Conclusion

Limitations.

(i)

As in prior works
(
28
;
57
;
46
)
,
we assume that the number of labels exceeds the representation dimension plus one. This is satisfied for language models with large vocabularies, but may be restrictive in standard image-classification settings with few classes.
In future work, we would thus like to do experiments on language models, where our assumptions are likely to be met.
Extending our results to regimes where the number of labels is equal to, or smaller than, the representation dimension
(
38
)
is another key next step.

(ii)

In our
Definition

3.7
, we assumed our models to be in general position (
3.5
). This resembles the diversity assumption (
2.1
), but it is stronger. Similar to diversity, it is satisfied with high probability for random vectors in high dimension (
m
large) under mild conditions.

Notions of representational similarity.

Our results show that having models whose distributions are close in KL divergence does not provide strong guarantees for
linear
representational similarity. If closeness in KL induces a robust notion of representational similarity, it is therefore likely to be of a different kind. Relatedly,
24
report that standard linear similarity metrics reveal limited convergence, whereas a mutual
k
-nearest-neighbor metric on representation space uncovers strong alignment across models. Other similarity notions are discussed in
Appendix

A
.

Drivers of representational similarity.

Our
Theorem

3.3
implies weak linear representational similarity guarantees for KL, which is connected to the cross entropy loss (
11
); nonetheless, some works report empirical observations of linear representational similarity under the cross entropy loss—e.g.,
(
56
)
. There may thus be additional factors that drive the observed similarity, for example, learning dynamics in early phases of training (e.g.,
15
;
26
), see also
Appendix

A
.

Distillation and representational similarity.
There is also a line of work on similarity-based knowledge distillation that explicitly matches hidden states between teacher and student, for example by aligning embedding spaces or internal features with auxiliary losses
(
59
;
64
)
. Our analysis is complementary: rather than adding representational regularizers, we study how purely distributional objectives—KL divergence and our logit-based distance—control (or fail to control) linear representational similarity.

Conclusion.

We showed that the logit distance is a metric between model distributions and, under a lower bound on conditional probabilities, can be controlled by the KL divergence. Building on this, we introduced the linear identifiability dissimilarity, tightly linked to the identifiability of the model family, and proved that logit distance upper-bounds both this dissimilarity and the mCCA between representations. Empirically, minimizing the logit distance yields student models whose representations are substantially more linearly similar to their teachers’ than when training with standard KL-based distillation.

{\bm{\mathrm{Y}}}

\displaystyle{\bm{\mathrm{\Sigma}}}_{{\bm{\mathrm{X}}}{\bm{\mathrm{Y}}}}=\mathbb{E}\left(({\bm{\mathrm{X}}}-\mathbb{E}({\bm{\mathrm{X}}}))({\bm{\mathrm{Y}}}-\mathbb{E}({\bm{\mathrm{Y}}}))^{\top}\right)

(27)

the cross correlation.

For
m=m^{\prime}
We consider the mean CCA

(28)

Variables

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{i}b_{m+1-i}\leq\tr({\bm{\mathrm{A}}}{\bm{\mathrm{B}}})\leq\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{i}b_{i}.

D.2

\displaystyle{\bm{\mathrm{f}}}({\bm{\mathrm{x}}}_{i})\cdot\left({\bm{\mathrm{g}}}(y_{i})-{\bm{\mathrm{g}}}(\bar{y}_{i})\right)={\bm{\mathrm{f}}}({\bm{\mathrm{x}}}_{i})\cdot\max_{y,\bar{y}\in\mathcal{Y}}\left({\bm{\mathrm{g}}}(y)-{\bm{\mathrm{g}}}(\bar{y})\right)\geq\alpha\lVert{\bm{\mathrm{f}}}({\bm{\mathrm{x}}}_{i})\rVert.

(104)

This implies that

\displaystyle p_{{\bm{\mathrm{f}}},{\bm{\mathrm{g}}}}(\bar{y}_{i}|{\bm{\mathrm{x}}}_{i})=p_{{\bm{\mathrm{f}}},{\bm{\mathrm{g}}}}({y}_{i}|{\bm{\mathrm{x}}}_{i})e^{-{\bm{\mathrm{f}}}({\bm{\mathrm{x}}}_{i})^{\top}\left({\bm{\mathrm{g}}}(y_{i})-{\bm{\mathrm{g}}}(\bar{y}_{i})\right)}\leq 1\cdot e^{-\alpha\lVert{\bm{\mathrm{f}}}({\bm{\mathrm{x}}}_{i})\rVert}\to 0

(105)

as
i\to\infty
. Therefore, probabilities cannot be
\tau
-bounded for any
\tau>0
.

E.2
Bounding logit distance with KL divergence

In this section, we start by considering two generic probability distributions
{\bm{\mathrm{p}}}=(p_{1},\ldots,p_{d})
and
{\bm{\mathrm{q}}}=(q_{1},\ldots,q_{d})
with
d
outcomes, such that
p_{i},q_{i}\geq 0
. We show how to provide bounds for
the logit differences
\sum_{i}(\log(p_{i})-\log(q_{i}))^{2}
in terms of the KL-divergence

\sum_{i}p_{i}\log(p_{i}/q_{i})

While these bounds are generally standard, we could not locate the required bounds in the literature and therefore provide complete proofs here.

Let us first clarify that this is not generally possible.
We consider the two probability
distributions with two outcomes (
d=2
) whose probabilities are given by
(p_{1},p_{2})=(\tau,1-\tau)

and
(q_{1},q_{2})=(\tau^{2},1-\tau^{2})
for some
0\leq\tau<1
.
Then, on one hand, we find

\displaystyle\sum_{i}(\log(p_{i})-\log(q_{i}))^{2}\geq\log(\tau)^{2}

(106)

and on the other hand

\displaystyle\mathrm{KL}({\bm{\mathrm{p}}}||{\bm{\mathrm{q}}})=\tau\log(\tau/\tau^{2})+(1-\tau)\log((1-\tau)/(1-\tau^{2}))\leq\tau|\log(\tau)|.

(107)

Since the former diverges as
\tau\to 0
while the latter converges to
0
it is generally not possible
to bound the squared logit difference by a function of the KL-divergence only. This is also the root of the observation that small KL-divergence between models in general does not imply similar representations
[
46
]
.

We therefore consider assuming a lower bound on
p_{i}

for such a bound. We first assume in addition that

q_{i}
is also lower bounded. Before stating the bound in this case we provide one auxiliary lemma that we will need in the proof.

Lemma E.1
.

For
\tau\leq 1/3
the following bound
holds for all
x\geq\tau

\displaystyle\log(x)^{2}\leq 4\log(\tau)^{2}(x\log(x)-x+1).

(108)

Proof.

We define the function

\Phi:\mathbb{R}_{+}\setminus\{1\}\to\mathbb{R}
given by

\displaystyle\Phi(x)=\frac{\log(x)^{2}}{x\log(x)-x+1}.

(109)

We observe that the function at thet denominator
x\to x\log(x)-x+1

is convex, minimized at
x=1
, and non-negative
on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
and thus
\Phi
is well defined.
We notice that
\Phi
can be extended to a continuous function at
1
by setting
\Phi(1)=2
.
To prove the lemma we need to show that

\Phi(x)\leq 4\log(\tau)^{2}
for
x\geq\tau
.
We will proceed in two steps: First we show that
\Phi
is non-increasing and then we bound
\Phi(\tau)
.
To show that
\Phi
is non-increasing we will need the following auxiliary fact: The function
\Psi:\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}
given by

\displaystyle\Psi(x)=\log(x)-1+\frac{1}{x}-\frac{\log(x)^{2}}{2}

(110)

is decreasing on
(0,\infty)
and satisfies

\Psi(1)=0
, so in particular
\Psi(x)\geq 0

for
x\leq 1
and
\Psi(x)\leq 0
for
x\geq 1
.
To show this we note that

\displaystyle\Psi^{\prime}(x)=\frac{1}{x}-\frac{1}{x^{2}}-\frac{\log(x)}{x}=-\frac{1}{x^{2}}\left(x\log(x)-x+1\right)\leq 0.

(111)

Now we calculate
\Phi^{\prime}
using
(\log(x)x-x+1)^{\prime}=\log(x)
and find

\displaystyle\begin{split}\Phi^{\prime}(x)=\frac{2\frac{\log(x)}{x}\left(x\log(x)-x+1\right)-\log(x)^{2}\cdot\log(x)}{\left(x\log(x)-x+1\right)^{2}}&=\frac{2}{\left(x\log(x)-x+1\right)^{2}}\log(x)\left(\log(x)-1+\frac{1}{x}-\frac{\log(x)^{2}}{2}\right)\\
&=\frac{2}{\left(x\log(x)-x+1\right)^{2}}\log(x)\Psi(x)\leq 0\end{split}

(112)

where we used that
\log(x)
and
\Psi(x)
both change their sign at
x=1
so the product is always non-positive.
Therefore we conclude that

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in[\tau,\tau^{-1}]\setminus\{1\}}\Phi(x)=\Phi(\tau)=\frac{\log(\tau)^{2}}{\tau\log(\tau)-\tau+1}\leq 4\log(\tau)^{2}

(113)

where we used
\tau\log(\tau)-\tau+1\geq 1/3\log(1/3)-1/3+1\geq 1/4
for
\tau\leq 1/3
in the last step.
∎

We can now state and prove the upper bound.

Lemma E.2
.

Let
{\bm{\mathrm{p}}}
and
{\bm{\mathrm{q}}}
be two probability distributions satisfying
p_{i}\geq\tau
and

q_{i}\geq\tau
for all
i
and some
0<\tau\leq 1/3
.
Then

\displaystyle\sum_{i}(\log(p_{i})-\log(q_{i}))^{2}\leq\frac{4\log(\tau)^{2}}{\tau}\mathrm{KL}({\bm{\mathrm{p}}}||{\bm{\mathrm{q}}}).

(114)

Remark E.3
.

The bound is tight up to the logarithmic terms. Indeed, consider

{\bm{\mathrm{p}}}=(2\tau,1-2\tau)

and
{\bm{\mathrm{q}}}=(\tau,1-\tau)
. Then the left hand side is bigger than
\log(2)^{2}
while the KL-divergence of these two distributions is bounded by
\log(2)\tau
.

Proof.

We write
p_{i}=x_{i}q_{i}
. By assumption,
p_{i}=x_{i}q_{i}\geq\tau
, which means that
x_{i}\geq\frac{\tau}{q_{i}}\geq\tau
.
Also,
x_{i}=\frac{p_{i}}{q_{i}}\leq\frac{1}{q_{i}}\leq\frac{1}{\tau}
.
So we have
x_{i}\in[\tau,\tau^{-1}]
.
The starting point is to rewrite the KL-divergence as follows

\displaystyle\mathrm{KL}({\bm{\mathrm{p}}}||{\bm{\mathrm{q}}})=\sum_{i}p_{i}\log(p_{i}/q_{i})=\sum_{i}q_{i}(x_{i}\log(x_{i})-x_{i}+1).

(115)

Here we used in the last step that

\sum_{i}q_{i}x_{i}=\sum_{i}p_{i}=1=\sum_{i}q_{i}
.
Recall that the function
x\to x\log(x)-x+1

is convex, minimized at
x=1
, and non-negative.
We apply (
108
) from Lemma
E.1
to control

\displaystyle\begin{split}\sum_{i}(\log(p_{i})-\log(q_{i}))^{2}&=\sum_{i}\log(x_{i})^{2}\leq 4\log(\tau)^{2}\sum_{i}(x_{i}\log(x_{i})-x_{i}+1)\\
&\leq 4\log(\tau)^{2}\sum_{i}\frac{q_{i}}{\tau}(x_{i}\log(x_{i})-x_{i}+1)=\frac{4\log(\tau)^{2}}{\tau}\mathrm{KL}({\bm{\mathrm{p}}}||{\bm{\mathrm{q}}}).\end{split}

(116)

∎

We can similarly show a slightly weaker bound without assuming that
{\bm{\mathrm{q}}}
is lower bounded.

Lemma E.4
.

Let
{\bm{\mathrm{p}}}
and
{\bm{\mathrm{q}}}
be two probability distributions satisfying
p_{i}\geq\tau
for all
i
and some
0<\tau\leq 1/3
.
Then

\displaystyle\sum_{i}(\log(p_{i})-\log(q_{i}))^{2}\leq\frac{12\log(\tau)^{2}}{\tau}\mathrm{KL}({\bm{\mathrm{p}}}||{\bm{\mathrm{q}}})+\frac{9}{\tau^{2}}\mathrm{KL}({\bm{\mathrm{p}}}||{\bm{\mathrm{q}}})^{2}.

(117)

Remark E.5
.

The second term can not be avoided. Note that
for
{\bm{\mathrm{p}}}=(\tau,1-\tau)
and
{\bm{\mathrm{q}}}=(\varepsilon,1-\varepsilon)
we find

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{\sum_{i}(\log(p_{i})-\log(q_{i}))^{2}}{\log(\varepsilon)^{2}}=1

(118)

while

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{\mathrm{KL}({\bm{\mathrm{p}}},{\bm{\mathrm{q}}})}{|\log(\varepsilon)|}=\tau.

(119)

Proof.

As before we write
p_{i}=x_{i}q_{i}

but in contrast to before
x_{i}
is not upper bounded but only satisfies
x_{i}\geq p_{i}\geq\tau
. The main idea is to control
\log(x_{i})^{2}
separately, when
{\bm{\mathrm{x}}}
is large we use a new bound based on a similar approach while we can apply the approach from before when
{\bm{\mathrm{x}}}
is small because this lower bounds
q_{i}
.
We start with the former.
Note that for
x\geq 3
we find

\displaystyle\frac{\log(x)x}{3}\leq x\log(x)-x+1.

(120)

Indeed, note that for
x=3

\displaystyle\frac{2}{3}x\log(x)-x+1=\frac{2}{3}\cdot 3\cdot\log(3)-3+1>0

(121)

and

\displaystyle\left(\frac{2}{3}x\log(x)-x+1\right)^{\prime}=\frac{2}{3}\log(x)+\frac{2}{3}-1\geq\frac{1}{3}

(122)

for
x\geq 3
.
This implies that

\displaystyle\begin{split}\sum_{i:\,x_{i}>3}\log(x_{i})^{2}_{i}&\leq\sum_{i:x_{i}>3}\left(\frac{3}{x_{i}}\frac{x_{i}\log(x_{i})}{3}\right)^{2}\\
&\leq\sum_{i:x_{i}>3}\left(\frac{3p_{i}}{\tau x_{i}}(x_{i}\log(x_{i})-x_{i}+1)\right)^{2}\\
&\leq\frac{9}{\tau^{2}}\sum_{i}\left(q_{i}(x_{i}\log(x_{i})-x_{i}+1)\right)^{2}\\
&\leq\frac{9}{\tau^{2}}\left(\sum_{i}q_{i}(x_{i}\log(x_{i})-x_{i}+1)\right)^{2}=\frac{9}{\tau^{2}}\mathrm{KL}({\bm{\mathrm{p}}}||{\bm{\mathrm{q}}})^{2}.\end{split}

(123)

Here we used in the last step that all terms in the sum are non-negative which implies that all cross terms in the expansion of the square of the sum are non-negative.

We now consider the indices
i
so that
x_{i}<3
.
Note that
q_{i}=p_{i}/x_{i}\geq\tau/3
.
Therefore we obtain using
x_{i}\geq\tau

and (
108
)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\sum_{i:x_{i}\leq 3}\log({\bm{\mathrm{x}}})^{2}_{i}&\leq 4\log(\tau)^{2}\sum_{i:x_{i}\leq 3}\left(x_{i}\log(x_{i})-x_{i}+1\right)\\
&\leq 4\log(\tau)^{2}\sum_{i:x_{i}\leq 3}\frac{q_{i}}{\tau/3}\left(x_{i}\log(x_{i})-x_{i}+1\right)\\
&\leq\frac{12\log(\tau)^{2}}{\tau}\sum_{i}{q_{i}}\left(x_{i}\log(x_{i})-x_{i}+1\right)=\frac{12\log(\tau)^{2}}{\tau}\mathrm{KL}({\bm{\mathrm{p}}}||{\bm{\mathrm{q}}}).\end{split}

(124)

Combining (
123
) and (
124
) ends the proof.
∎

Remark E.8

Without assuming a lower bound on

F.3
Relating embeddings and unembeddings

The results in Theorem
F.1
can be used to show that
{\bm{\mathrm{f}}}
and
{\bm{\mathrm{f}}}^{\prime}

and
{\bm{\mathrm{g}}}
and
{\bm{\mathrm{g}}}^{\prime}
are linearly related
when the model logits are similar. Concretely we bounded the canonical correlations and we can also bound the residual (see Remark
F.3
).
However, while these are quantitative versions of the linear identifiability of
{\bm{\mathrm{f}}}
and
{\bm{\mathrm{g}}}

this alone is not sufficient to recover the identifiability result as this also requires that

{\bm{\mathrm{f}}}=\tilde{\mathbf{B}}{\bm{\mathrm{f}}}^{\prime}
,
{\bm{\mathrm{g}}}={\bm{\mathrm{B}}}{\bm{\mathrm{g}}}^{\prime}

and
\tilde{\bm{\mathrm{B}}}_{\mathcal{J}}^{-\top}=\tilde{\mathbf{B}}
.
Therefore, a natural question is whether
we can show quantitative bounds for

{\bm{\mathrm{B}}}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{B}}-\bm{1}_{m^{\prime}\times m^{\prime}}
(this matrix can also be considered for
m^{\prime}\neq m
).
However, when considering the equivalent model
({\bm{\mathrm{A}}}^{\top}{\bm{\mathrm{f}}}^{\prime},{\bm{\mathrm{A}}}^{-1}{\bm{\mathrm{g}}}^{\prime})
for
{\bm{\mathrm{A}}}\in\mathrm{GL}(m^{\prime})
we find that the transition matrix becomes

{\bm{\mathrm{A}}}^{-1}{\bm{\mathrm{B}}}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{B}}{\bm{\mathrm{A}}}
.
However, if
{\bm{\mathrm{B}}}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{B}}
is not a multiple of the identity we have

\displaystyle\sup_{{\bm{\mathrm{A}}}\in\mathrm{GL}(m^{\prime})}\lVert{\bm{\mathrm{A}}}^{-1}{\bm{\mathrm{B}}}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{B}}{\bm{\mathrm{A}}}\rVert=\infty

(188)

and thus no meaningful bounds on
\lVert{\bm{\mathrm{B}}}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{B}}\rVert
in terms of

\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{\mathrm{x}}}\sim p_{{\bm{\mathrm{x}}}}}\lVert{\bm{\mathrm{u}}}({\bm{\mathrm{x}}})-{\bm{\mathrm{u}}}^{\prime}({\bm{\mathrm{x}}})\rVert^{2}

are possible. Instead, we can only show a weaker statement for the relation of
{\bm{\mathrm{B}}}
and
\tilde{\mathbf{B}}
.
To achieve this we argue as in Lemmas
F.2
and
F.6
.

Lemma F.10
.

Appendix G
Example of Models with small KL Divergence and Equal Embeddings, but Dissimilar Unembeddings

and

For a subset of input data, a reference model

assigns high probability to one among the first four labels, as displayed by embeddings aligning to unembeddings directions

All in all, we have that for any choice of

{\bm{\mathrm{x}}}

\displaystyle\frac{\|\tilde{\bm{\mathrm{B}}}_{\mathcal{J}}^{-\top}\left({\bm{\mathrm{f}}}({\bm{\mathrm{x}}})-\tilde{\bm{\mathrm{A}}}_{\mathcal{J}}{\bm{\mathrm{f}}}^{\prime}({\bm{\mathrm{x}}})\right)\|_{2}}{\sigma_{\tilde{\bm{\mathrm{L}}}_{\mathcal{J}}\min}}\geq\left\|{\bm{\mathrm{f}}}({\bm{\mathrm{x}}})-\tilde{\bm{\mathrm{A}}}_{\mathcal{J}}{\bm{\mathrm{f}}}^{\prime}({\bm{\mathrm{x}}})\right\|_{2}

And since

We can make a similar argument by defining

as the diagonal matrix with the maximum singular value of

Also, from

218

\displaystyle\frac{\|\tilde{\bm{\mathrm{B}}}_{\mathcal{J}}^{-\top}\left({\bm{\mathrm{f}}}({\bm{\mathrm{x}}})-\tilde{\bm{\mathrm{A}}}_{\mathcal{J}}{\bm{\mathrm{f}}}^{\prime}({\bm{\mathrm{x}}})\right)\|_{2}}{\sigma_{\tilde{\bm{\mathrm{L}}}_{\mathcal{J}}\min}}

In this Section we show that when

between two models is small, then concepts in one model are approximately represented in another model, in particular we prove Theorem
4.3
.
We recall that
{\bm{\mathrm{L}}}
is the matrix of unembeddings and that a concept
{\bm{\mathrm{h}}}
defines a conditional distribution
p_{\bm{\mathrm{h}}}(c\mid{\bm{\mathrm{x}}})
, over
C\geq 2
values.

Consider a model
({\bm{\mathrm{f}}},{\bm{\mathrm{g}}})
, such that
{\bm{\mathrm{h}}}
is linearly represented in
{\bm{\mathrm{f}}}
.
For every
c\in\{1,\ldots,C\}
,
we write the concept vectors
{\bm{\mathrm{w}}}_{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}

as
{\bm{\mathrm{L}}}{\bm{\mathrm{\alpha}}}_{c}
for some
{\bm{\mathrm{\alpha}}}_{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}

which we call

as they are the weights when writing
{\bm{\mathrm{w}}}_{c}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}{\bm{\mathrm{g}}}(y_{i})(\alpha_{c})_{i}
.
Since we assume that
{\bm{\mathrm{L}}}
has full rank, this is possible for all
{\bm{\mathrm{w}}}_{c}
.
Moreover, this is a natural way to parametrize the concepts as it is invariant to linear transformations of the embedding space and concepts of interest are generally aligned with the unembeddings
{\bm{\mathrm{g}}}(y)
.
We also define the matrix
{\bm{\mathrm{A}}}=({\bm{\mathrm{\alpha}}}_{c})_{c\in\{1,\ldots,C\}}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times C}

We now show that these concepts can be approximately realized on the embedding space of another model
({\bm{\mathrm{f}}}^{\prime},{\bm{\mathrm{g}}}^{\prime})\in\Theta

when the logit distance to

Indeed, we consider the concept matrix

\displaystyle{\bm{\mathrm{W}}}^{\prime}={\bm{\mathrm{L}}}^{\prime}{\bm{\mathrm{A}}}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times C}

(246)

and therefore the individual concept vectors are given by

\displaystyle{\bm{\mathrm{w}}}^{\prime}_{c}={\bm{\mathrm{L}}}^{\prime}{\bm{\mathrm{\alpha}}}^{\prime}_{c}.

(247)

We can then define (the biases remain invariant

\displaystyle p^{\prime}_{{\bm{\mathrm{h}}}}(c|{\bm{\mathrm{x}}})=\frac{\exp\big({\bm{\mathrm{w}}}_{c}^{\prime\top}{\bm{\mathrm{f}}}^{\prime}({\bm{\mathrm{x}}})+b_{c}\big)}{\sum_{j=1}^{C}\exp\big({\bm{\mathrm{w}}}_{j}^{\prime\top}{\bm{\mathrm{f}}}^{\prime}({\bm{\mathrm{x}}})+{\bm{\mathrm{b}}}_{j}\big)}

(248)

We then get the following result.

K.2

We use

To evaluate

Depending on the number of labels, evaluating all possible choices of pivots and subsets of labels can be unfeasible.
We thus consider selecting at random the pivots and the subset of labels to reduce the computational cost.
For

, we evaluate

for all possible labels and pivots.
For
CIFAR
we select all possible pivots and for each of them we consider

For

subsets of labels selected at random among the

\binom{33-1}{10}\approx 6\cdot 10^{7}

possible combinations. This number of samples is very small compared to the total number of combinations possible, but the most important thing is that we get information from all possible labels and with

(

(

(

Aggregation of measures.

Reported values on teachers are aggregating by evaluating the mean and standard deviations over
5

different seeds for both the

student and the

We then aggregate all mean and variances by considering the inverse-variance weighting. The mean is given by

(262)

whereas the standard deviation is obtained as

\bar{\sigma}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{\sum_{i}\omega_{i}}}\,.

(263)

The final results are reported as
