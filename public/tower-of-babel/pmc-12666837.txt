Post-traumatic pseudoaneurysm of the common carotid artery associated with arteriovenous fistula to the internal jugular vein: A case report
Authors: Henriquy Aguiar Coelho, Lucas Cardoso Siqueira Albernaz, Lucas Soares de Aguiar, Andrey Salgado Moraes Filho, Mariana Willes Santana Soares, Helena Barbi, João Felipe Federici de Almeida, Alexandre Foratto, Alex Aparecido Cantador, Simone Reges Perales
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractTraumatic arteriovenous fistulas (AVFs) occur in up to 30 % of cases involving the cervical region, with the concomitant presence of AVF and pseudoaneurysm being a rare but clinically significant condition. This case report describes a 32-year-old male victim of a stab wound in Zone II of the right cervical region, who presented with a palpable thrill and subcutaneous emphysema. Cervical computed tomography angiography (CTA) revealed a pseudoaneurysm of the right common carotid artery and an AVF with the right internal jugular vein. The patient underwent successful endovascular repair using a covered stent, achieving complete resolution of the condition without any neurological or functional deficits. This case underscores the rarity of traumatic AVFs associated with pseudoaneurysms and emphasizes the importance of a systematic evaluation in penetrating cervical trauma. Additionally, it shows CTA as a crucial non-invasive diagnostic tool and demonstrates the effectiveness of endovascular treatment as a safe, minimally invasive, and highly efficient alternative to conventional surgical approaches.Keywords: Cervical trauma, Endovascular treatment, Arteriovenous fistula, Pseudoaneurysm

Introduction

Traumatic arteriovenous fistulas (AVFs) occur in up to 30 % of cases in the cervical region, with approximately half resulting from stab wounds or gunshot wounds [1,2]. The concomitant presence of AVF and pseudoaneurysm formation is a rare and underreported condition in the medical literature [3,4].

A significant number of cases of AVF associated with pseudoaneurysm remain undiagnosed during initial trauma evaluation, increasing the risk of neurological sequelae [5]. Endovascular treatment, a less invasive alternative to conventional surgical repair, has gained prominence due to its lower risk of bleeding and reduced morbidity and mortality rates [6].

This case report describes the case of a patient who suffered a stab wound to the cervical region, which progressed with the formation of a pseudoaneurysm of the right common carotid artery and an arteriovenous fistula to the right internal jugular vein, a very rare condition.

Case report

A 32-year-old male patient with no comorbidities was referred to the Hospital de Clínicas of UNICAMP due to a stab wound in Zone II of the right cervical region. During the initial assessment, he presented with a patent airway, mild hoarseness, hemodynamically stable, with a Glasgow Coma Scale score of 15 and eupneic on room-air. A 3 cm wound was observed in Zone II of the right cervical region, accompanied by minor venous bleeding, slight subcutaneous emphysema, non-pulsatile perilesional edema, and a palpable thrill. No other traumatic injuries were identified.

Given the patient's hemodynamic stability, cervical computed tomography angiography (CTA) was performed, which revealed the following findings: traumatic laceration of the proximal segment of the right common carotid artery associated with a pseudoaneurysm (4.5 × 1.6 cm, projecting inferiorly towards the mediastinum, with a lateral component measuring 2.9 × 1.6 cm) on its anterior wall, contained by an inferior mediastinal hematoma and subcutaneous tissue laterally (Fig. 1); presence of an AVF with the right internal jugular vein, confirmed by early contrast enhancement of the internal jugular vein in the arterial phase of CTA (Fig. 2), without signs of tracheal compression (Fig. 3).

Pseudoaneurysm of the internal carotid artery on the coronal section, extending towards the mediastinum and contained laterally by subcutaneous tissue.

Early contrast enhancement of the internal jugular vein in the arterial phase (suggestive of AVF).

Absence of extrinsic tracheal compression signs.

Endovascular repair of the AVF and pseudoaneurysm was indicated, with the placement of a covered stent in the right common carotid artery. Endovascular access was obtained via catheterization of the right common femoral artery with an 8Fr introducer, progressing to the right brachiocephalic trunk using a 0.035 mm hydrophilic guidewire and an MPA catheter. The presence of the pseudoaneurysm and carotid-jugular fistula was confirmed by arteriography, without additional lesions compared to the CTA (Fig. 4). The hydrophilic wire was substituted for an Amplatz guidewire, and a 6x60mm covered self-expandable stent was deployed. Self-expandable prosthesis was accommodated with an intravascular balloon. Post-procedural arteriography demonstrated complete resolution of the AVF and pseudoaneurysm (Fig. 5).

Cervical region arteriography showing arteriovenous fistula between the right common carotid artery and the right internal jugular vein (yellow arrow), with a pseudoaneurysm in the right common carotid artery (red arrow-head).

Post-stent placement arteriography showing resolution of the AVF and pseudoaneurysm.

The procedure was uneventful and at the end of the anesthesia, the patient woke up without neurological deficits. The postoperative course was favorable, and the patient was discharged from the hospital three days later on dual antiplatelet therapy.

Discussion

Arteriovenous fistulas (AVFs) may be congenital or acquired, with most cases being iatrogenic and only a minority resulting from trauma [7]. Acute presentation occurs in 25–30 % of cases, though delayed presentation up to six years post-trauma has been reported [8]. The concomitant presence of a traumatic pseudoaneurysm is rare, with an incidence of up to 5.4 % in the carotid artery, as reported in a study of 291 patients with trauma-acquired AVFs [2].

Clinical presentation varies and may include local manifestations such as edema, hematoma, pain, pulsatile mass, and palpable thrill, as well as systemic effects such as hemodynamic alterations due to arteriovenous shunting, respiratory issues due to mass effect causing airway compression, and neurological symptoms ranging from headache and altered consciousness to ischemic stroke [1,4].

Penetrating cervical trauma should be systematically evaluated following ATLS guidelines, preferably in trauma centers. Initial assessment involves visual inspection of the wound and identification of platysma muscle involvement to determine the depth of injury. Superficial wounds are managed with cleaning and suturing, while deep wounds require advanced imaging or surgical exploration [9].

Hemodynamic instability is an indication for exploratory cervicotomy, as is the presence of “hard signs,” which include pulsatile or expanding hematoma, active bleeding, air leakage from the wound, massive hematemesis or hemoptysis, respiratory insufficiency, and neurological deficits suggestive of ischemia. In hemodynamically stable patients without these signs, additional investigation with imaging, endoscopy, or bronchoscopy is indicated [9].

Angiography remains the gold standard for diagnosis but is invasive and less accessible compared to vascular doppler ultrasound and CTA [10]. Doppler ultrasound is cost-effective and widely accessible but is operator-dependent and has limitations in assessing Zones I and III of the cervical region. Additionally, its accuracy can be compromised in the presence of subcutaneous emphysema, hematoma, or obesity. [10]. CTA is a less invasive and more widely available method, non-dependent operator, with a sensitivity of 90–100 % and specificity of 96,4 % a 98,4 % for diagnosing of vascular injuries [[10], [11], [12]].

Endovascular management of isolated traumatic pseudoaneurysms lacks definitive evidence regarding the superiority of covered versus bare metal stents, with patient characteristics and lesion location guiding the choice. In penetrating injuries, where there is a higher risk of full-thickness arterial injury, covered stents are generally preferred. They are also favored in cases with concomitant AVF, as they provide fistula exclusion. In other cases, bare-metal stents may be considered due to a lower risk of thrombosis, although additional coil embolization may sometimes be necessary [[13], [14], [15]].

Patients undergoing endovascular carotid revascularization are at risk of both short and long-term complications. The most common early complication is stroke, occurring in up to 3.6 % of cases within 30 days [16,17]. Other complications include myocardial infarction, acute renal dysfunction, and late stent-related issues such as stenosis, thrombosis, or fracture. Stent stenosis or thrombosis occurs in approximately 6 % of cases at one year [18] and 6.8 % at ten years [19], while stent fractures are observed in 29 % of cases within 18 months [20].

Conclusion

Post-traumatic arteriovenous fistula associated with carotid artery pseudoaneurysm is a rare but high-risk condition. Prompt diagnosis using advanced imaging modalities such as CTA, coupled with effective endovascular management, is essential for optimizing patient outcomes.
