Alteration of gut microbiota contributes to peritoneal fibrosis through increased production of trimethylamine N-oxide
Authors: Weizhen Xie, Jiangzi Yuan, Chenhong Feng, Huinan Shi, Chenhong Zhang, Taofang Xi, Jiaying Huang, Wei Fang, Zhaohui Ni, Leyi Gu, Na Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPeritoneal fibrosis is a common complication in peritoneal dialysis (PD) patients, which results in ultrafiltration failure (UFF) and PD withdrawal. PD patients demonstrate altered structural and functional profiles of the gut microbiota. Herein, we investigated the role of the gut microbiota and trimethylamine N-oxide (TMAO), a bacterial metabolite, in the pathogenesis of PD-associated peritoneal fibrosis. PD mice displayed mesenchymal transition features and fibrosis in the peritoneum, which were accompanied by an altered gut microbiota profile and elevated serum TMAO levels, and these peritoneal histologic abnormalities were ameliorated by gut microbiota depletion. Fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) from PD patients induced mesenchymal and fibrotic alterations within the peritoneum of wild-type mice, and the effect was more pronounced in mice receiving FMT from PD patients with UFF. Intraperitoneal supplementation with TMAO enhanced PD-induced peritoneal fibrosis in wild-type mice. On the contrary, PD- or FMT-induced mesenchymal features and fibrosis within the peritoneal membrane were lessened in flavin-containing monooxygenase 3 gene knockout mice, which were incapable of synthesizing TMAO. TMAO treatment enhanced high glucose-mediated phenotypic transition and fibrogenesis in cultured peritoneal mesothelial cells and fibroblasts, partly by increasing TGF-β1 synthesis and secretion and subsequent phosphorylation of Smad2/3 and activation of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway. Collectively, we found that altered gut microbiota plays an important role in the development of PD-associated peritoneal fibrosis through dysregulated production of the bacterial metabolite TMAO.Keywords: Chronic kidney disease, peritoneal dialysis, peritoneal fibrosis, gut microbiota, trimethylamine N-oxide

Introduction

Peritoneal dialysis (PD) is a successful kidney replacement therapy for patients with kidney failure that utilizes the peritoneal membrane to remove uremic solutes and extra water from the circulation.1 Thus, the long-term success of PD depends on the structural and functional integrity of the peritoneal membrane. However, peritoneal morphology is altered over the course of PD, with interstitial fibrosis being the most common alteration.2 Peritoneal fibrosis ultimately leads to dysfunction of the peritoneum, characterized as reduced solute and fluid clearance in PD patients.3,4 Peritoneal ultrafiltration failure (UFF) is a common reason for PD withdrawal.5 Moreover, peritoneal fibrosis sometimes results in encapsulating peritoneal sclerosis, which is a life-threatening disease for long-term PD patients.6

The pathogenesis of PD-associated peritoneal fibrosis has not been fully elucidated to date. Recent studies have demonstrated that the gut microbiota participates in the development of various fibrotic diseases.7-9 Trillions of bacteria reside in the intestinal tract of human beings, constituting the gut microbiota. The homeostasis of the gut microbiota is influenced by diet, infection, drugs, and health status. Dysbiosis of the gut microbiota, characterized by disturbed microbial composition and metabolite profiles, has been shown to contribute to fibrosis in multiple organs, including the kidney, heart, and lung.7-9 The gut microbiota is altered in patients with chronic kidney disease (CKD), primarily due to the accumulation of uremic toxins, and this alteration persists even after the initiation of maintenance dialysis.10,11 We previously reported that the gut microbiota varied in PD patients with different levels of dialysis duration, peritoneal glucose exposure and residual kidney function.12 However, the role of the gut microbiota in the pathogenesis of PD-related peritoneal fibrosis has not been studied.

Trimethylamine N-oxide (TMAO) is a metabolic end product of the gut microbiota that has been shown to possess profibrotic properties.13,14 It may partly mediate the pathogenic effect of altered gut microbiota in fibrogenic diseases. Serum TMAO increases in patients with kidney failure due to elevated synthesis by enhanced cloning of TMAO-producing bacteria in the gut, as well as lowered clearance from the circulation through urine excretion.15 Recent studies have demonstrated that serum TMAO levels remain elevated even in patients undergoing dialysis and that this elevation is associated with an increased risk of peritoneal inflammation and peritonitis in PD patients.16,17 However, its role in PD-related peritoneal fibrosis is not known. In the current study, we explored the effects of the gut microbiota and TMAO on the development of PD-related peritoneal fibrosis.

Materials and methods

Animal experiments

Wild-type and flavin-containing monooxygenase 3 (FMO3) gene knockout PD mouse models

Eight-week-old female wild-type (C57BL/6J, Chinese Academy of Sciences, Shanghai, China) and FMO3 gene knockout (C57BL/6-FMO3em1Smoc, NM-KO-200734, Shanghai Model Organisms Center, Inc.) mice were used. All the mice were derived from the same litter to ensure experimental consistency, and 5–6 mice were housed together in one cage under a 12-h light/dark cycle at room temperature with free access to food and water throughout the study. The C57BL/6-FMO3em1Smoc mice used in this study showed comparable body weights and general behaviors to wild-type littermates under the experimental conditions. They were viable and fertile and demonstrated no spontaneous pathological changes.

The PD murine model was established using modified methods as we reported previously in rats.18 Briefly, the mice were first subjected to 2/3 left-side nephrectomy (day 1) and then right-side nephrectomy a week later (day 8). Then mice were randomly assigned to each experimental arm on day 15 by the same animal technician, who was blinded to the study design, and each group contained 8 mice. The sample size was limited by practical constraints (e.g., the availability of transgenic animals and cost). We utilized the maximum number of animals available to us for this investigation while adhering to the principle of reduction. 4.25% glucose PD solution (Dianeal@, Baxter, China; 100 ml/kg body weight) was injected into the peritoneal cavity daily for 8 weeks (day 15‒70, PD group). Saline, instead of PD solution, was injected into uremic mice as a control (uremia group). Serum urea and creatinine concentrations were measured on days 1, 14 and 71 to assess kidney function. Serum, stool, parietal peritoneum, and colon samples were collected on day 71 after the mice were euthanized. The animal experiments were approved by the Animal Care Committee at Renji Hospital, Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine, China.

Peritoneal fibrosis, mesenchymal transition features, and angiogenesis were assessed, and the detailed methods were provided in the Supplementary methods. Moreover, methodologies for fecal DNA extraction and 16S rRNA gene sequencing, serum gut microbiota metabolomic profiling, TMAO concentration determination, intestinal mucosal integrity assessment, Western blot and quantitative real‐time polymerase chain reaction (q-PCR) were described in the Supplementary methods. The project has been registered at NCBI, and the raw data have been submitted to the BioSample database (No.: PRJNA1212711).

Gut microbiota depletion

PD mice were fed with drinking water containing ampicillin (1 g/l), vancomycin (0.5 g/l), neomycin (0.5 g/l), gentamicin (100 mg/l), and erythromycin (10 mg/l) for 4 weeks to deplete the gut microbiota (day 43–70).19 Antibiotic-containing drinking water was refreshed every 3 d. Grape juice was added to reduce the bitter taste of antibiotics. Fecal smear examination was conducted to confirm the efficacy of the antibiotics after 2 weeks of administration.

Peritoneal TMAO supplementation

TMAO (Sigma, purity ≥ 99.0%) was reconstituted in PBS and administered intraperitoneally to mice together with saline or 4.25% glucose PD solution for 2 weeks (150 mg/kg body weight, day 57‒70).20

Fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT)

Human fecal samples were collected from pre-dialysis uremic patients (uremia group) and PD patients (PD with or without UFF group) after written informed consent (Ethics Approving No.: KY2024-106-C). Patient selection and exclusion criteria were provided in Supplementary methods. The clinical characteristics of the recruited subjects were retrieved. Fecal specimen was obtained in sterile containers in the morning, delivered to the laboratory within 2 h, resuspended in Ringer's solution under anaerobic conditions, and then mixed with sterile defatted milk powder. Fecal suspension samples were stored at −80 °C until use. Additional fecal, serum and overnight dialysate samples were collected and sent for 16S rRNA gene sequencing of the gut microbiota, determination of the serum gut microbiota metabolome profile, and measurement of the serum and dialysate TMAO concentrations. The detailed methodology was stated in the Supplementary methods.

For FMT, the mice were first treated with antibiotic-containing drinking water for 4 weeks to deplete the gut microbiota (days 1–28). Then, 200 μl fecal suspension was given to the gut microbiota-depleted mice via oral gavage, which was conducted 3 times a week for a duration of 4 weeks as previously reported (days 29–56).21 In addition, 100 μl fecal suspension was applied onto the fur of the mice. Mice that did not receive FMT were used as controls (control group).

Cell culture

Cells and treatment

Primary human peritoneal mesothelial cells (HPMCs) were isolated from omental specimens obtained from non-uremic patients who underwent elective abdominal surgery after written informed consent (Ethics Approval No.: KY2023-081-B). Patients with abdominal malignancy and peritoneal metastasis were excluded. HPMCs were isolated as previously described.22 In brief, the omental tissue was washed in PBS to remove contaminating red blood cells, cut into small pieces, and digested with 0.25% trypsin/0.01% EDTA (Gibco) at 37 °C for 20 min The tissue and cell suspensions were centrifuged at 1000 rpm for 15 min. The omental tissue was collected, and the process was repeated once more. The cell pellet was resuspended and cultured in Medium 199 (Gibco) containing 15% FCS. All experiments were conducted using cells from the third passage. Human fibroblasts (CRL 2091 cell line, ATCC) were cultured in DMEM medium (ATCC) supplemented with 10% FCS.

HPMCs and fibroblasts at 70% confluence were growth-arrested in serum-free medium for 24 h and then incubated with TMAO (Sigma, 50 or 100 μM) in the presence or absence of 4.25% D-glucose (Sigma) for a further 24 h. In parallel experiments, cells were pre-treated with a TGF-β1 neutralizing antibody (BE0057, Bioxcell), a TGF-β receptor I (TGF-βRI) inhibitor (SB431542, Selleckchem) or a Wnt inhibitor (ETC-159, Selleckchem) 1 h prior to incubation with TMAO and glucose.

Fibrogenesis, as well as phenotypic transition, including mesothelial-to-mesenchymal transition in HPMCs and the myofibroblast transition in fibroblasts, was assessed by evaluating the expression of matrix proteins (fibronectin [FN] and collagen type 1 [Col1]), mesenchymal molecules (α-smooth muscle actin [α-SMA], Snail, and FSP-1), and epithelial markers (E-cadherin) via Western blot and qPCR. The expression of TGF-β1, TGF-βRI, and the activation of the Smad2/3 and Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathways were also assessed by Western blot. TGF-β1 concentrations in the cell supernatants were measured by Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay. Detail methods were provided in the Supplementary methods.

Statistical analysis

All the experiments were repeated at least 3 times. Data were expressed as mean ± SD or median (interquartile range). Statistical analyses were performed using Prism 10.0 (GraphPad, Inc., California, USA). Student's t-test, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey's multiple comparisons test were conducted to compare differences between two or more groups for normally distributed continuous data. For non-normal distribution data, Mann‒Whitney U test was conducted for comparisons between two independent groups. Kruskal‒Wallis H test followed by Dunn's multiple comparisons test with Bonferroni correction was performed for analyses involving three or more groups. Alpha diversity was analyzed using the “vegan” package in R software and expressed as Simpson and Shannon diversity indices. Beta diversity was analyzed using R software, and a principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) plot was generated based on the visualization of unweighted UniFrac distances. Orthogonal partial least-squares-discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) and partial least-squares-discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) were performed to identify differentially expressed metabolites between groups. Permutational analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) was used to compare differences in PCoA and OPLS-DA. The microbiota and metabolites with significantly different abundances among the groups were displayed in a heatmap. Pearson correlation analysis was performed to assess the correlation between microbial and metabolite abundances. p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Gut microbiota profile is altered in PD mice

We developed a PD mouse model by administering daily peritoneal injections of 4.25% glucose solution to mice that had undergone 5/6 nephrectomy (Figure 1 A). The serum urea and creatinine levels increased significantly following 5/6 nephrectomy, and the elevations persisted following PD (Figure S1). Higher Simpson and Shannon indices were observed in PD mice, indicating greater microbial diversity and richness compared with uremia group (Figure 1B). Significant separation of the microbial composition was observed between uremic and PD mice (Figure 1C). Several amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) in the genera Muribaculaceae, Lactobacillus and Incertae Sedis, etc., were enriched in PD mice, whereas some ASVs in the genera Clostridium sensu stricto 1, Sphingomonas, and Sediminibacterium, etc., were reduced (Figure 1D). Furthermore, reduced expression of tight junction and increased macrophage infiltration were shown in the colon epithelium of PD mice (Figure S2, IHS of claudin-1 and F4/80).

Depletion of gut microbiota attenuates peritoneal fibrosis in PD mice

We next question whether the altered gut microbiota in PD mice plays a pathogenic role in PD-related peritoneal fibrosis. The gut microbiota in PD mice was depleted with broad-spectrum antibiotics (ABX) (Figure 2A), as demonstrated by a significant reduction of microbial diversity and richness (Figure S3A), coupled with a separation of microbial communities compared with those of mice without ABX treatment (Figure S3B). The abundances of most microbial ASVs were decreased in ABX-treated mice (Figure S3C).

PD mice presented exacerbated peritoneal fibrosis, as evidenced by increased thickness of the peritoneal submesothelial layer and upregulated expression of FN and Col1. These mice also displayed notable mesenchymal transition features within the peritoneal membrane, including elevated α-SMA and Snail expression and reduced E-cadherin expression. Interestingly, gut microbiota depletion attenuated PD-induced histologic alterations within the peritoneum (Figure 2B–D). Increased number of CD31-positive vessels were shown in mouse peritoneum of PD group, and the elevation was abolished after gut microbiota depletion (Figure 2B). Furthermore, ABX treatment ameliorated intestinal mucosal barrier injuries and inflammation in PD mice (Figure S2).

Serum gut microbiota metabolomics profile is altered in PD mice

OPLS-DA of the gut microbiota metabolite profiles revealed discrete differences between uremic and PD mice (Figure 3A). Metabolites were analyzed by untargeted LC–MS/MS, and values are presented as relative abundances. Compared with uremic mice, PD mice exhibited higher serum abundances of the gut microbiota-derived uremic toxin TMAO and its precursor metabolites, including trimethylamine (TMA), L-carnitine, creatinine and betanine (Figure 3B). Whereas serum abundances of streptidine, p-acetaminobenzoic acid, and indoleacrylic acid were significantly lower (Figure 3B). Indoxyl sulfate and p-cresyl sulfate, two additional gut microbiota-derived uremic toxins, showed no significant differences in their serum abundances between uremic and PD mice (Figure 3C). Serum TMAO and TMA abundances were positively correlated with the abundances of several ASVs in the genera Muribaculaceae, Lactobacillus and Incertae Sedis, but negatively correlated with Sphingomonas ASV 2084, Clostridium sensu stricto 1 ASV 663 and Sediminibacterium ASV 2175 (Figure 3D). Targeted LC‒MS/MS further confirmed that the serum TMAO concentrations were higher in PD mice than in uremic mice and were reduced after gut microbiota depletion (Figure 3E).

TMAO aggravates peritoneal fibrosis in PD mice

To explore the role of TMAO in PD-associated peritoneal fibrosis, TMAO was injected into the peritoneal cavity of the mice for 2 weeks (Figure 4A), which was accompanied by a significant increase in the serum TMAO concentrations (Figure S4). TMAO exacerbated PD-induced mesenchymal transition features and fibrosis in the peritoneum (Figure 4B–D) and enhanced peritoneal angiogenesis (Figure 4B). In the colon epithelium, while TMAO on its own mildly disrupted colon mucosa integrity, it greatly enhanced PD-induced tight junction loss and macrophage infiltration (Figure S5).

Inhibition of TMAO production attenuates peritoneal fibrosis in PD mice

The synthesis of TMAO from its gut microbial metabolite precursor TMA requires the host hepatic enzyme FMO3.23 To investigate the role of TMAO in PD-induced peritoneal fibrosis, FMO3 gene knockout mice (C57BL/6-FMO3em1Smoc) were used as a TMAO-deficient model (Figure 5A). Serum TMAO concentrations were significantly lower in C57BL/6-FMO3em1Smoc mice compared with wild-type mice (Figure S6). Blockade of TMAO production significantly alleviated PD-induced mesenchymal features, fibrosis, and angiogenesis within the peritoneal membrane (Figure 5B–D).

Fecal samples were collected from sex- and age-matched donors in three groups (uremia, PD without UFF, and PD with UFF, n = 5 per group). Compared with patients without UFF, PD patients with UFF showed longer PD duration, lower eGFR, and worse peritoneal ultrafiltration capacity (Table S2). Although there were no significant differences in microbial diversity and richness (Figure S7A), significant separation of the gut microbiota composition was shown among the three groups (Figure S7B). A number of ASVs in the genera Blautia, Oscillibacter and Muribaculaceae were enriched in the gut microbiota of PD patients, especially in those with UFF (Figure S7C). Conversely, the abundances of Blautia ASV 104, Lachnospiraccae NK4A136 group ASV 22 and Muribaculum ASV 359 were reduced in PD patients (Figure S7C). Metabolomic analysis revealed a distinct separation of serum metabolites between the groups (Figure S7D). The serum TMAO abundance and concentration were significantly higher in PD patients with UFF, as determined by untargeted and targeted LC-MS/MS analyses, respectively (Figure S7E and H). No significant differences were observed in the serum abundances of indoxyl sulfate and p-cresyl sulfate among the three patient groups (Figure S7F). The serum TMAO and TMA abundances were correlated with the abundance of ASV 72 of genus Bacteroides (Figure S7G). TMAO concentrations in peritoneal effluent were higher in PD patients with UFF than in those without (Figure S7I).

Peritoneal fibrosis, mesenchymal transition features, and angiogenesis were noted in wild-type mice receiving FMT from PD patients, with more pronounced structural alterations in those transplanted with fecal microbiota from UFF PD patients (Figure 6B–D). On the contrary, in C57BL/6-FMO3em1Smoc mice, FMT from PD patients did not trigger significant morphological changes in the peritoneum (Figure 6B–D). Interestingly, FMT from uremic patients did not induce significant peritoneal fibrosis in either type of mouse (Figure 6B–D). Meanwhile, FMT from both uremic and PD patients increased the serum TMAO concentrations in wild-type mice, but not in FMO3 knockout mice (Figure S8). The increase was more remarkable in wild-type mice receiving FMT from PD patients with UFF.

TMAO enhances high glucose-mediated phenotypic transition and fibrogenesis in HPMCs and fibroblasts in vitro

We then investigated the effects and underlying mechanisms of TMAO on the phenotypic transition and fibrogenesis in HPMCs and fibroblasts, two key resident peritoneal cell types (Figures 7A and 8A). TMAO alone only altered the expression of a small number of matrix proteins and mesenchymal markers in both cell types (Figures 7B and C, 8B and C). However, when co-treated with 4.25% glucose, TMAO exacerbated the upregulation of matrix proteins (FN and Col1) and mesenchymal markers (α-SMA, Snail and FSP-1) in both HPMCs and fibroblasts, as well as the downregulation of E-cadherin in HPMCs (Figures 7B and C, 8B and C).

In addition, TMAO enhanced high glucose-induced upregulation of TGF-β1 synthesis and secretion, as well as TGF-βRI expression (Figures 7D and 8D, S9). These changes were accompanied by increased phosphorylation of Smad2/3 and elevated expression of Wnt5a/b and active β-catenin in HPMCs and fibroblasts (Figures 7D and 8D). Notably, blockade of TGF-β1 binding to its receptor or inhibition of TGF-βRI or Wnt signaling partially abolished the synergistic effect of TMAO and high glucose on fibrogenesis in both HPMCs and fibroblasts (Figures 7E and F, 8E and F).

Discussion

PD is an established kidney replacement therapy that has been applied in the clinic for decades. Peritoneal fibrosis, which frequently develops in long-term PD patients, is associated with ultrafiltration failure and ultimately results in the discontinuation of this effective therapy.5 The pathogenesis of PD-related peritoneal fibrosis has not been fully elucidated. Herein, we demonstrated a link between the gut microbiota and peritoneal fibrosis following PD (Figure S10). We found that uremic mice receiving PD displayed different gut microbiota profile as compared with those not receiving dialysis. The depletion of the gut microbiota with antibiotics alleviated peritoneal fibrosis, while inoculating PD-associated gut bacteria triggered mesenchymal and fibrotic features within the peritoneum in wild-type mice. We further identified TMAO as an indispensable metabolite in the development of peritoneal fibrosis in vivo and in vitro. Supplementing TMAO to PD mice exacerbated mesenchymal transition features and fibrosis within the peritoneal membrane, whereas blocking TMAO synthesis in C57BL/6-FMO3em1Smoc mice alleviated these pathological alterations. In peritoneal mesothelial cells and fibroblasts, TMAO synergistically enhanced high glucose-mediated phenotypic transition and fibrogenesis, partly by increasing TGF-β1 synthesis and secretion and subsequently activating the Smad2/3 and Wnt/β-catenin molecular pathways. These evidences are the first of its genre to reveal the causal effect of gut dysbiosis in PD-associated peritoneal fibrosis.

More and more studies point to the importance of dysbiosis in organ fibrosis. Nevertheless, no research has been done to illustrate the connection between dysbiosis of the gut microbiota and peritoneal fibrosis, particularly in the setting of PD. Uremic conditions have been shown by several studies to alter gut microbiota hemostasis in CKD patients.24-27 This CKD-associated gut dysbiosis in return acts on the microenvironment of the kidney and then aggravates fibrosis. A kidney‒gut‒peritoneum axis theory has recently been proposed by Stepanova, hypothesizing that CKD-associated gut dysbiosis might lead to peritoneal fibrosis even before PD and that PD initiation exacerbates gut dysbiosis, which will further enhance peritoneal fibrosis.28 In line with this theory, our data showed FMT from long-term PD patients with UFF induced profound mesenchymal and fibrotic alterations in the mouse peritoneum.

However, under the experimental conditions of the current study, CKD-associated gut microbiota did not trigger pronounced peritoneal fibrosis in mouse models, suggesting that gut microbiota dysbiosis associated with PD initiation is critical for the development of peritoneal fibrosis. In addition to uremic status, PD therapy per se alters the gut microbiota by perturbing the local physiological environment of the peritoneal cavity as well as the systemic metabolic condition. First, continuous dwell of bioincompatible PD fluid poses chemical stress and mechanical pressure on the intestinal tract, which may impair the integrity of the intestinal mucosa.29,30 Moreover, high glucose absorption into the circulation will change the growth condition of the intestinal microbiome.31 In addition, it has been reported that PD patients with and without a history of peritonitis have different characteristics of the gut microbiota.32 In the present study, 16S rRNA sequencing of the gut microbiota in mouse stool samples revealed discrete differences between PD mice and uremic mice not receiving PD, with differential abundances of certain ASVs. Similarly, the composition of the intestinal microbiota and their metabolites differed significantly between pre-dialysis uremic patients and PD patients, a finding that may explain the exacerbated peritoneal fibrosis observed in mice that received FMT from PD patients as stool donors. It would be worthwhile to compare the gut microbiota and bacterial metabolites in the same cohort of CKD patients prior to PD initiation, following the commencement of PD, and subsequent to the development of UFF. Such a longitudinal analysis would enable the identification of specific gut microbiota dysregulations that directly contribute to the pathogenesis of peritoneal fibrosis.

Altered microbiota and chronic kidney failure per se lead to the accumulation of toxic metabolites in the serum. Our analysis identified TMAO and its related precursor metabolites TMA, L-carnitine and betaine were among the top altered bacterial metabolites in PD mice and patients. We also examined other gut microbiota-derived uremic toxins, including indoxyl sulfate and p-cresyl sulfate. However, no significant between-group differences were observed in either the murine models or the human subjects, which may be attributed to the small sample size and individual heterogeneity. Therefore, we focused our investigation on the role of TMAO in the pathogenesis of PD-related peritoneal fibrosis in the present study. Mouse serum TMAO abundance was positively correlated with the abundances of some ASVs in the genera Muribaculaceae and Lactobacillus, and negatively associated with the abundances of ASVs in the genera Clostridium sensu stricto 1 and Sphingomonas, findings that are consistent with the characteristics of the gut microbiota composition in PD mice. The quantitative relationship between certain bacterial metabolites and the gut microbiota is highly complicated and varies between studies and animal models. It has been reported that Lactobacillus crispatus and casei reduce the serum TMAO level in CKD rats, whereas another strain, rhamnosus in the genus Lactobacillus could increase TMAO levels in the liver of mice treated with human baby flora.33,34 The molecular mechanisms mediating the effects of specific bacterial strains on serum TMAO production in the setting of PD remain to be investigated in future research.

TMAO has been shown to cause fibrosis in different organs, such as the heart and kidney, but its effect on peritoneal fibrosis has not been studied.13,14,35 In our study, we proved that TMAO augmented PD-induced peritoneal fibrosis in mice. Nonetheless, TMAO by itself only triggered marginal peritoneal fibrosis in uremic mice not receiving PD solution injection, indicating it probably exerts a fibrosis-enhancing effect by coupling with the high glucose content in PDF. Indeed, in in vitro experiments, we showed that TMAO activated phenotypic transition and fibrogenesis in peritoneal mesothelial cells and fibroblasts only in the presence of high glucose incubation. Moreover, although dialysate TMAO concentrations were not measured directly, it is noted that serum TMAO concentrations in uremic mice following TMAO administration were substantially lower than those in PD mice, which may also explain the above findings.

We then identified that the synergistic pro-fibrotic effect of TMAO combined with high glucose in HPMCs and fibroblasts was mediated, at least in part, by increased TGF-β1 synthesis and secretion. Both TMAO and upregulated TGF-β1 contribute to the downstream phosphorylation of Smad2/3 and the activation of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway. These findings were consistent with those of previous studies in other cell types.36-38 Further dissection of how TMAO upregulates TGF-β1 synthesis and secretion, as well as identification of its direct receptor(s), will be critical to fully understand its biological functions in HPMCs and fibroblasts. Deciphering these molecular events will lead us to the discovery of drug targets for peritoneal fibrosis.

Mesothelial-to-mesenchymal transition in HPMCs and myofibroblast transition in fibroblasts are critical molecular events that initiate peritoneal fibrosis.39,40 In the current study, we found that TMAO promoted phenotypic transition in these two key resident peritoneal cell types, a finding supported by both in vivo and in vitro experiments. These results were in line with those of a previous study which showed that TMAO promotes epithelial‒mesenchymal transformation in hepatocellular carcinoma cell lines.41 Additionally, we demonstrated that TMAO supplementation aggravated intestinal mucosal barrier injury in PD mice, as evidenced by a marked reduction in claudin-1 expression in the colon epithelium, a recognized molecular event in epithelial‒mesenchymal transition.42 In agreement with our findings, Chen et al. reported that TMAO significantly decreased claudin-1 expression in the jejunum, ileum, and colon in a mouse model of frailty.43 Disruption of the intestinal mucosal barrier will lead to bacterial translocation and the entry of microbial metabolites into the systemic circulation, including TMAO itself, which may further exacerbate the fibrogenic process.44

In addition to fibrosis, angiogenesis is another recognized histologic feature leading to impaired peritoneal function and the development of UFF.45 In the present study, we found that TMAO exacerbated angiogenesis, as evidenced by increased CD31-positive microvessel density, in the peritoneum of PD mice. These findings are consistent with a prior study demonstrating that TMAO promoted cell proliferation and angiogenesis in colorectal cancer.46 Thus, it is of interest to further explore the effect of TMAO on vascular endothelial cells biology in a PD context, which will help us to understand more comprehensively how PD-related gut dysbiosis and elevated TMAO lead to UFF after long-term PD.

There are some limitations in our study that need to be discussed. First, a PD animal model was established using mice because FMO3 gene knockout mice were needed. However, PD effluent samples were not collected from the mice to measure TMAO concentrations or to assess the transport characteristics of the peritoneum via a peritoneal equilibrium test. Second, the current study only used 4.25% glucose PD solution to trigger peritoneal fibrosis. So it did not allow a comparison with different glucose concentrations, which would provide more information on the effect of glucose on alteration of gut microbiota and metabolites. Third, although FMT conducted by gavage administration and fur application is an established method,21 we only measured serum TMAO concentrations but did not assess the profile of the gut microbiota in mice post-transfer. Furthermore, although murine models enable mechanistic investigation of gut microbiota‒host interactions, species-specific differences in microbial metabolism and host FMO3 activity may limit the direct translation of these findings to PD patients. Finally, the human stool samples used for microbiome transplantation were harvested from age- and sex-matched subjects controlled for key clinical covariates that influence the microbiome profile, including concurrent medications and intestinal comorbidities. Although all recruited patients followed a routine diet without enteral nutritional supplements, dietary diaries were not systematically recorded, which might introduce residual confounding.

Collectively, in the current study, we discovered a strong link between the gut microbiota and PD-associated peritoneal fibrosis, and TMAO was found to be a critical bacterial metabolite that mediated dysbiosisinduced peritoneal fibrosis. Based on these novel findings, FMT from healthy donors to PD patients may be a potential therapeutic solution for peritoneal fibrosis, thereby prolonging the effective therapeutic course of PD in patients with kidney failure. Further characterization of the dysregulated strains of gut bacteria in PD patients with UFF could hopefully provoke development of probiotics that remedy dysbiosis and alleviate or delay peritoneal fibrosis.
