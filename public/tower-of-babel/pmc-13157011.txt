Spontaneous closure of a full-thickness macular hole in highly myopic eye: A case report
Authors: Angelica Aja Dangca Alcoreza, Junyeop Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

Introduction

Full-thickness macular holes (FTMHs) are often managed surgically via pars plana vitrectomy with internal limiting membrane peeling and gas tamponade, with a 90% anatomic success rate.1 FTMH in highly myopic eyes results from complex tractional forces involving the vitreomacular interface and progressive posterior scleral ectasia. Despite advances in surgical techniques, the repair of myopic FTMH remains technically challenging and often yields variable visual outcomes.2 However, spontaneous closure of myopic macular holes is rare.3

Case report

A 68-year-old diabetic and hypertensive female with a known history of high myopia, visited our clinic for routine annual ophthalmologic evaluation. The patient had undergone bilateral cataract extraction with posterior chamber intraocular lens (PCIOL) implantation ten years prior to consultation, with a documented refractive error of −20.00 diopter sphere in the right eye and −21.00 diopter sphere in the left eye prior to surgery. Post-operatively, refraction was measured on a −5.00 diopter sphere with a +2.00 diopter cylinder at 118° in the right eye and a −6.00 diopter sphere with a +2.50 diopter cylinder at 60° in the left eye, which has been consistent for years. At presentation, she reported stable vision with no recent symptoms, such as metamorphopsia, central scotoma, or visual loss.

On examination, the best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) was 0.5 (Snellen equivalent 20/40) in both eyes. The anterior segment was unremarkable, with well-centered PCIOL in situ bilaterally. The patient's intraocular pressure was 20 mmHg in both eyes. Fundus examination of both eyes revealed a tessellated, pale, myopic fundus with characteristic chorioretinal thinning and a tilted optic disc. No signs of retinal detachment or neovascularization were noted (Fig. 1A). Using the IOL master (Zeiss), axial length of the right and left eye of the patient were measured at 30.96mm and 30.44mm respectively.

Optical coherence tomography (OCT) by Heidelberg Engineering was performed as part of routine surveillance. In the right eye, a thin epiretinal membrane (ERM) was identified, accompanied by subtle changes in the foveal contour suggestive of progression toward an impending macular hole (Fig. 1B). For the left eye, an incidental finding of full-thickness macular hole was noted with minimum linear diameter of 204 μm and base diameter of 550 μm. A linear membrane extending from the temporal margin of the macular hole consistent with an epiretinal membrane combined with an incomplete posterior vitreous detachment with residual posterior hyaloid cortex were observed. Prominent intraretinal cystic spaces were noted along the hole margin, suggesting an acute phase of hole formation (Fig. 1C).

Surgical intervention was recommended to the patient but since the patient had no subjective complaints, a decision was made to observe. At the 3-month follow-up, BCVA on the right eye was 0.5 (Snellen equivalent 20/40) and 0.32 (Snellen equivalent 20/63) on the left eye. Repeat OCT demonstrated spontaneous closure of the macular hole with restoration of the foveal contour. Notably, the membrane attached to the temporal margin of the hole, which was initially presumed to be the cause of the full-thickness macular hole, remained in place without separation (Fig. 2A and B).

Since no clear evidence of active vitreomacular traction was observed, fluorescein angiography (FA) was performed to evaluate the possibility of an underlying retinal vascular disorder. However, FA revealed no vascular abnormalities at the macular area, including telangiectatic changes, that could account for the macular hole or macular edema. Only peripheral retinal areas of capillary nonperfusion without leakage or neovascularization were identified with absence of diabetic retinopathy or diabetic macular edema (Fig. 2C and D). Indocyanine green angiography (ICGA) revealed dilated choroidal vessels suggestive of choroidal vascular remodelling, which is common in myopic degeneration (Fig. 2E). To further quantify metamorphopsia, microperimetry (Centervue) and M-chart were requested with scores of 0.4 on the vertical and 0.5 on the horizontal meridian.

The patient remained under close observation with stable BCVA of 0.5 (Snellen equivalent 20/40) on the right eye and 0.4 (Snellen equivalent 20/50) on the left eye on the 5th month follow-up and sustained anatomical closure of the macular hole with improvement of the ellipsoid zone integrity on serial OCT scans. There was also improvement in microperimetry (Fig. 3) and M-chart values to 0 on both horizontal and vertical meridians.

Discussion

In highly myopic eyes, axial elongation leads to progressive thinning of the retina and choroid, predisposing them to structural changes such as posterior staphyloma, chorioretinal atrophy, vitreoretinal traction, and macular hole formation.4

Macular hole (MH) closure is influenced by several factors. Anatomic factors include a small hole diameter, release of vitreomacular traction, changes in the contour of the posterior hyaloid, presence of epiretinal membranes or cystic structures at the hole edge, and posterior vitreous detachment (PVD). Young patient age is also a predictive factor. Cellular mechanisms also play a key role, with Müller cells proliferating at the external limiting membrane to form a bridging structure across the hole, along with glial cell proliferation and tissue bridging that promote closure. Complete PVD helps reduce tractional forces, while the formation of a contractile epiretinal membrane contributes to hole shrinkage and cellular repair at its base.3

Spontaneous closure of highly myopic macular holes has been exceedingly rare.3 Yu et al. described the case of spontaneous closure of a macular hole with complete retinal reattachment in a highly myopic eye with posterior staphyloma.5 A case by Li et al. reported spontaneous closure of highly myopic macular hole associated with retinal detachment. The macular hole closed spontaneously but retinoschisis and vitreomacular traction persisted. The absence or reduction of vitreomacular traction supports a favourable environment for natural healing.6

In both of the previously reported cases, the retinoschisis showed improvement but the photoreceptor layer did not fully recover, indicating incomplete anatomical restoration. This differs in our case, in which macular hole closure was achieved with structural and functional improvement of the photoreceptors.

Subsequently, a few cases of spontaneous macular hole closure similar to ours have been reported; however, no epiretinal membrane was present in those cases. The closure was presumed to result from tangential forces following peripheral barrier laser in dome-shaped macula, and the process required more than five years.3 In contrast, our case achieved closure within three months despite the acute structural features of the MH, suggesting that a distinct recovery mechanism may have operated in the staphylomatous eye.

Although the precise mechanism underlying spontaneous closure remains unclear, it is conceivable that changes in the posterior hyaloid contour during peripheral or nasal posterior vitreous detachment may have altered the tangential forces acting on the fovea, thereby facilitating hole closure in this unique anatomical context.

The present case is noteworthy for several reasons. The epiretinal membrane attached to the temporal margin of the FTMH may not have acted as a true tractional force, contrary to the initial assumption. Moreover, the macular hole closed spontaneously within a relatively short period of three months, representing a rapid anatomical improvement. However, the relatively short follow-up period is a key limitation of the present report hence long-term follow-up is essential specially in highly myopic eyes where progressive maculopathy or macular holes may recur.

This case underscores the importance of individualized patient management. In select asymptomatic patients with small macular holes and that may not have tractional forces, close observation with serial OCT can be an appropriate approach. However, timely surgical intervention remains essential when visual acuity decreases or when the hole enlarges.

Conclusion

Routine imaging in myopic patients can reveal silent but potentially vision-threatening lesions such as macular holes. Although uncommon, spontaneous closure of FTMH in myopic eyes can occur and warrants consideration in patient counselling and management decision with appropriate caution regarding long-term stability.
