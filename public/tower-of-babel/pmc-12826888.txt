Differential Effects of Intravenous Versus Intracoronary Adenosine on Non‐Culprit Lesion Assessment Following Acute Coronary Syndrome
Authors: Jonathan Los, Frans B Mensink, Guus A de Waard, Lokien X van Nunen, Mohamed M Reda Morsy, Peter Damman, Tim J F ten Cate, Cyril Camaro, Aukelien C Dimitriu‐Leen, Marleen H van Wely, Aysun Cetinyurek‐Yavuz, Niels van Royen, Robert‐Jan M van Geuns
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDuring acute coronary syndrome (ACS), the validity of initial non‐culprit fractional flow reserve (FFR) measurements remains controversial because of microvascular dysfunction and potential diminished adenosine response. Because the hyperemic effects of adenosine have been predominantly studied in stable patients, and high‐dose intracoronary administration may produce stronger hyperemia than intravenous delivery, we compared non‐culprit FFR between the acute and stabilized phases of ACS using both administration routes.MethodsPatients presenting with ACS underwent repeated physiological assessment of relevant non‐culprit lesions using both intracoronary adenosine boluses (≥100 μg) and intravenous adenosine (140 μg/kg/min) during index hospitalization and at 12‐week follow‐up.ResultsA total of 53 patients (mean age 65.1±9.3 years, 7 [13.2%] women) were included in the current analysis. A significant interaction between the route of adenosine administration and change in FFR was found (P=0.035). Baseline FFR was lower when measured with intracoronary adenosine (mean difference: −0.02, 95% CI, −0.03 to −0.01). Over time, FFR measured with intravenous adenosine decreased (mean change: −0.02, 95% CI, −0.04 to −0.00), while intracoronary adenosine showed stable values (mean change: −0.00, 95% CI, −0.02 to 0.01). Concurrently, more patients were initially classified as having a non‐flow‐limiting lesion with intravenous adenosine (27 [50.9%] versus 15 [28.3%], P=0.008), which was no longer evident at follow‐up (P=0.146). No significant change in microvascular function was observed.ConclusionsDuring ACS, adequate intracoronary adenosine boluses elicit a stronger hyperemic response than intravenous adenosine administration, yielding lower initial FFR values that remain stable over time. Further research is warranted to investigate the dose‐dependent hyperemic effects of intravenous adenosine during the acute phase of ACS.Keywords: acute coronary syndrome (ACS), adenosine, fractional flow reserve (FFR), microvascular function, non‐culprit vesselsSubject Categories: Percutaneous Coronary Intervention, Catheter-Based Coronary and Valvular Interventions

Clinical Perspective

What Is New?

Among patients presenting with acute coronary syndrome and multivessel coronary artery disease, adequate intracoronary adenosine administration for non‐culprit lesion assessment induces enhanced hyperemia and lower initial fractional flow reserve values, which remain stable over time, in contrast to the decreasing values observed with intravenous adenosine.

Fractional flow reserve (FFR) has been considered the gold standard to assess functional significance of intermediate epicardial coronary artery lesions and to guide coronary revascularization. 1 It serves as a measurement tool to assess the aggregate effect of the loss of blood flow across atherosclerotic lesions. 1 During the initial phase of acute coronary syndrome (ACS), myocardial blood flow is impaired in culprit vessel territory. 2 Atherothrombotic embolization, myocardial edema, neurohumoral vasoconstriction and elevated left ventricular end‐diastolic pressure induce microvascular dysfunction, which in turn could lead to reduced blood flow and falsely elevated FFR values. 3 , 4 , 5 Therefore, culprit vessel FFR is regarded unreliable during the acute phase of ACS. 6 However, the extent of microvascular dysfunction in patients presenting with ACS is unclear. 7 Some clinical studies demonstrated reduced myocardial blood flow in remote areas as well. 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 In addition, non‐culprit vessel adenosine sensitivity seems diminished after myocardial infarction. 4 Notwithstanding, other trials report stable non‐culprit myocardial blood flow and FFR measurements between acute and stabilized phases of ACS. 2 , 12 Recent trends indicate a shift toward the use of higher intracoronary adenosine boluses, which have been shown to produce more profound hyperemia. 13 , 14 In contrast, the standard intravenous adenosine dosing regimen has largely remained unchanged and has been less extensively studied in the ACS setting. The aim of the present analysis was to evaluate the changes in non‐culprit FFR over time. Patients presenting with ACS underwent repeated physiological assessment of a relevant non‐culprit lesion using both high‐dose intracoronary and intravenous adenosine during index hospitalization and at 12‐week follow‐up.

METHODS

Data Availability

The data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

Study Design

The present analysis involves all included patients in the FITTER (Functional Improvement of non‐infarcT relaTed coronary artery stenosis by Extensive LDL‐C Reduction with a proprotein convertase subtilisin/kexin type 9 Antibody) trial (NCT04141579) in whom successful baseline and follow‐up intracoronary physiology assessment using both intracoronary and intravenous adenosine was performed. 15 The design of the trial was previously described. 16 In short, patients with ACS were included after successful percutaneous coronary intervention of the culprit vessel and if they demonstrated another relevant lesion in a non‐infarct related artery with an FFR of 0.67–0.85. Major exclusion criteria were prior coronary artery bypass grafting, untreated functional left main stem stenosis (FFR ≤0.80), or severe kidney dysfunction. After 12 weeks, patients underwent repeat angiography. Ethical approval was given by the Dutch Ethical Review Board (METC Oost Nederland). The trial was conducted in full accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki, and all patients provided written informed consent.

Physiological Indices

Intracoronary physiological testing included measurements of intracoronary pressure and estimates of coronary flow. A guidewire equipped with a pressure‐ and temperature‐sensitive sensor (PressureWire X, Abbott Vascular, Santa Clara, CA) was used to assess coronary pressures and bolus thermodilution mean transit times (Tmn). After introduction of the guiding catheter, an intracoronary bolus of 200 to 300 μg nitroglycerin was administered to prevent coronary vasoreactivity. After equalization, the wire was positioned distal from the atherosclerotic lesion. Trial eligibility was assessed by measuring non‐culprit FFR using an intracoronary bolus of ≥100 μg adenosine (150 μg was advised). When non‐culprit FFR was 0.67 to 0.85, additional intracoronary physiological testing was performed to assess microvascular function during stable maximum hyperemia, induced by continuous intravenous infusion of adenosine (140 μg/kg/min) using an antebrachial vein or large vein on the forearm. Basal distal and aortic resting pressures (Pdrest and Parest, respectively) and hyperemic distal and aortic pressures (Pdhyp and Pahyp, respectively) were noted as well. Concurrently, coronary microcirculatory function assessment was performed in accordance with the bolus thermodilution technique using repeated 3 mL intracoronary saline boluses both in rest and during stable maximum hyperemia induced by intravenous adenosine. 17 Bolus thermodilution curves were considered adequate when an unimodal shape was observed with <30% variation across the 3 measurements (following the default Coroventis CoroFlow system setting). At follow‐up, all measurements were repeated similarly, with the wire at the same location as baseline measurements. A drift of ≤0.02 was considered acceptable. If drift was >0.02, measurements were repeated. The FFR values were calculated as the lowest distal pressure/aortic pressure ratios during a period of stable maximal hyperemia, and the lowest stable FFR values were used. The coronary flow reserve (CFR) was defined as the ratio between hyperemic mean transit time (Tmnhyp) and resting mean transit time (Tmnrest). Baseline microvascular resistance values were derived as the product of Pdrest and mean Tmnrest. Inherent to the inclusion criterion of an FFR 0.67 to 0.85, a large proportion of patients exhibited significant epicardial stenosis. Therefore, to correct for the influence of collateral flow without coronary wedge pressure measurement, index of microvascular resistance (IMRc) values were calculated following Yong’s formula: IMRc=Pahyp×Tmnhyp×([1.35×Pdhyp/Pahyp]–0.32). 18 The microvascular resistance reserve capacity (MRR) was calculated as follows: (CFR/FFR)×(Parest/Pahyp).

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables are presented as mean±SD or median (25th to 75th percentile). Categorical variables are displayed as frequency (%). Normality was assessed through visual inspection of histograms and Q‐Q plots. Comparisons of intracoronary pressure parameters between intracoronary and intravenous adenosine from baseline to follow‐up were performed using mixed model repeated measures, including the route of administration, time, and their interaction as fixed effects assuming an unstructured variance covariance matrix structure for within subject residuals. Kenward‐Roger degrees of freedom were used in all calculations. The interaction P value is reported as the primary parameter of interest. In addition, pairwise contrasts of least squares means are presented to compare the route of administration at each time point and to assess changes from baseline to follow‐up. These pairwise results are considered exploratory to support the primary findings, and no adjustment for multiplicity is applied. Linearity of FFR values at baseline, at follow‐up, and from baseline to follow‐up were assessed using simple linear regression. The McNemar test was used to compare the classifications (FFR>0.8 and ≤0.8) and classification agreements of both FFR measurements at baseline and follow‐up. Additionally, Cohen’s kappa was calculated to assess the level of classification agreement. Differences in coronary microcirculatory function parameters (Tmnrest, Tmnhyp, CFR, baseline microvascular resistance, IMRc, and MRR) from baseline to follow‐up were assessed using the Wilcoxon signed‐rank test. The Spearman rank correlation test was used to evaluate the relationship between infarct size and changes in coronary microcirculatory parameters. Assessment of changes from baseline to follow‐up between non–ST‐segment–elevation myocardial infarction (NSTEMI) and ST‐segment–elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) patients was performed using mixed model repeated measures following the same approach, including infarct type, time, and their interaction as fixed effects. All reported P values are 2‐sided. A P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were conducted using statistical product and service solutions (IBM SPSS 29.0).

RESULTS

Out of the initial study population of 150 patients enrolled between November 2020 and August 2023, a total of 53 patients recruited at the Radboud University Medical Center (Nijmegen, the Netherlands), with 53 non‐culprit vessels, were included in the current subgroup analysis (Figure S1). The mean number of days between presentation and intracoronary physiology assessment was 1.4±2.0. Baseline characteristics are presented in Table 1. Mean age was 65.1±9.3 years, and 7 patients (13.2%) were women. Most of the patients presented with NSTEMI (66.0%) and without any signs of heart failure (Killip class I, 92.5%). The left anterior descending coronary artery was the most interrogated non‐culprit vessel (67.9%) for physiological assessment.

Assessment of Intracoronary Physiology During Hyperemia

The observed per‐patient changes in intracoronary pressures in response to adenosine administration are displayed in Figure S2. From baseline to follow‐up, Pahyp decreased by −6.0 mm Hg (95% CI, −10.3 to −1.7) with intracoronary adenosine, whereas with intravenous adenosine the decrease was −1.5 mm Hg (95% CI, −5.9 to 2.9) (Table 2). The difference in change between intracoronary adenosine and intravenous adenosine was significant (P for interaction=0.024). These findings are consistent with a stronger hyperemic response at follow‐up with intracoronary adenosine, considering the comparable Pahyp values at baseline (mean difference compared with intravenous adenosine: −0.0 mm Hg, 95% CI, −3.3 to 3.3) (Table 2). For Pdhyp, values were numerically lower with intracoronary adenosine at baseline (mean difference compared with intravenous adenosine: −1.5 mm Hg, 95% CI, −4.3 to 1.3), and significantly lower at follow‐up (mean difference compared with intravenous adenosine: −3.3 mm Hg, 95% CI, −5.7 to −1.0) (Table 2). However, the difference in change over time between the route of administration was not significant (P for interaction=0.245). A statistically significant interaction between the change in FFR and route of adenosine administration was found (P for interaction=0.035) (Table 2, Figure 1). At baseline, FFR values were lower with intracoronary adenosine compared with intravenous adenosine (mean difference: −0.02, 95% CI, −0.03 to −0.01). In addition, FFR decreased with intravenous adenosine (mean change: −0.02, 95% CI, −0.04 to −0.00) over time, whereas no relevant change was observed with intracoronary adenosine (mean change: −0.00, 95% CI, −0.02 to 0.01) (Table 1, Figure 1). Figure S3 provides an overview of all observed FFR values at baseline and follow‐up. Figure 2C and 2D demonstrate the linear relationship between baseline and follow‐up FFR for both intracoronary (β=1.06, 95% CI, 0.67–1.46, P<0.001) and intravenous adenosine (β=0.66, 95% CI, 0.27–1.05, P=0.001). However, intracoronary adenosine demonstrated a stronger correlation (R=0.61) than intravenous adenosine (R=0.43). Bland–Altman analysis revealed a smaller mean difference and narrower limits of agreement for intracoronary adenosine (mean difference: −0.00, 95% CI, −0.12 to 0.12) compared with intravenous adenosine (mean difference: −0.02, 95% CI, −0.17 to 0.13). In line, the correlation of FFR at follow‐up (R=0.84) was higher than at baseline (R=0.58) (Figure 2A and 2B).

At baseline, 27 patients (50.9%) demonstrated a normal FFR (>0.8) with intravenous adenosine, compared with 15 (28.3%) with intracoronary adenosine (P=0.008). Of these patients, 17 (32.1%) patients were reclassified at follow‐up as having a significant stenosis (FFR≤0.80) with intravenous adenosine, compared with 7 (13.2%) patients with intracoronary adenosine. Subsequently, no difference in classification of lesion significance was present at follow‐up (24 [45.3%] with intracoronary adenosine and 18 [34.0%] with intravenous adenosine; P=0.146). Concurrently, classification agreement between intracoronary adenosine and intravenous adenosine was higher at follow‐up (77.4%; Cohen’s kappa: 0.53, 95% CI, 0.31 to 0.76) compared with baseline (66.0%; Cohen’s kappa: 0.33, 95% CI, 0.10 to 0.55), although not statistically significant (P=0.263) (Figure 2A and 2B). Furthermore, classification agreement for baseline to follow‐up FFR was higher for intracoronary adenosine (71.7%; Cohen’s kappa: 0.41, 95% CI, 0.18 to 0.65) compared with intravenous adenosine (52.8%; Cohen’s kappa: 0.06, 95% CI, −0.19 to 0.32) (P= 0.041, Figure 2C and 2D).

No significant non‐culprit improvements in CFR (2.74±1.56 versus 2.96±1.78, P= 0.131), IMRc (24.57±20.56 versus 20.11±10.67, P=0.180), or MRR (3.61±2.23 versus 4.01±2.51, P=0.097) were observed from baseline to follow‐up (Table 3).

Differential Adenosine Response by Clinical Presentation

Out of all patients, 35 (66.0%) and 14 (26.4%) presented with NSTEMI and STEMI, respectively (Table 1). The change in FFR measured with intravenous adenosine was −0.03 (95% CI, −0.06 to −0.00) for patients with STEMI and −0.03 (95% CI, −0.06 to −0.00) for patients with NSTEMI (P for interaction=0.722) (Table 4). Both patients with NSTEMI‐ and STEMI demonstrated quantitative improvements in CFR, IMRc, and MRR, though not statistically significant. Also, there were no significant differences in change between patients with NSTEMI and STEMI for CFR (P for interaction = 0.463), IMRc (P for interaction=0.750), or MRR (P for interaction=0.785) (Table 4). DISCUSSION

This substudy was performed to evaluate non‐culprit FFR variability between intracoronary and intravenous adenosine following ACS. Only relevant lesions with an FFR of 0.67 to 0.85 were included. The present findings indicate that intracoronary adenosine elicits a more pronounced hyperemic response than intravenous adenosine at the commonly used doses of ≥100 μg and 140 μg/kg/min, respectively. During the acute phase, administration of intravenous adenosine results in a relatively smaller drop in distal intracoronary pressure compared with intracoronary adenosine, which leads to elevated FFR values. Despite the differences in Pahyp and Pdhyp at follow‐up, similar FFR values were found for intracoronary and intravenous adenosine. Moreover, significantly more patients were classified as having a non‐flow limiting lesion at baseline with intravenous adenosine. In contrast to intravenous adenosine, the use of intracoronary adenosine resulted in consistent FFR measurements over time. Subsequently, classification agreement was higher with intracoronary adenosine.

Dose‐Dependent Effect of Intracoronary and Intravenous Adenosine

Intravenous adenosine, preferably administered by central venous infusion, is considered the gold standard for measuring FFR. 19 , 20 De Bruyne et al demonstrated that the hyperemic effect of adenosine was similar for femoral and antecubital venous adenosine infusions in patients with chronic coronary syndrome scheduled for percutaneous coronary intervention. 21 Increasing the adenosine dose >140 μg/kg/min did not result in a further decline of FFR, despite a continued reduction in aortic pressure. 21 The aforementioned was confirmed in a study by Alexopoulos et al, which included patients with chronic coronary syndrome and ACS. 22 Accordingly, a dosage of 140 μg/kg/min per mi of intravenous adenosine is recommended. 1 , 13

The use of intracoronary versus intravenous adenosine has been subject of debate. 19 Intracoronary adenosine has become increasingly used because it is less time‐consuming, less expensive, and associated with fewer adverse events. 19 Intracoronary adenosine dose responsiveness has been extensively studied. A meta‐analysis by Rigattieri et al, which included 11 studies in which FFR was predominantly performed in patients with chronic coronary syndrome, found a significant difference of 0.02 FFR units in favor of IV adenosine. However, no difference was found when using high doses (≥100 μg) of intracoronary adenosine. Therefore, considering potential side effects, intracoronary doses ranging from 100 μg to 200 μg are recommended to induce hyperemia for evaluating stenosis severity. 19 , 20 A recent study by Mejia‐Renteria et al, involving patients with myocardial ischemia without obstructive coronary artery disease, found that intracoronary boluses of 100 μg to 200 μg induced significantly greater magnitudes of hyperemia, demonstrated by shorter Tmnhyp, compared with intravenous adenosine at a dose of 140 μg/kg/min.14

Non‐Culprit FFR During ACS

Specifically in ACS, the validity of initial non‐culprit FFR measurements is a point of contention. 23 After myocardial infarction, the sensitivity of adenosine receptors has shown to be reduced, resulting in a diminished adenosine response that contributes to microvascular dysfunction. 4 However, our findings indicate that intracoronary adenosine boluses ≥100 μg can overcome this heightened sensitivity threshold to some extent, as we demonstrated lower baseline FFR values using intracoronary adenosine. Nonetheless, the changes in aortic pressure and distal pressure in response to intracoronary adenosine were numerically higher at follow‐up compared with baseline, indicating some degree of microvascular dysfunction at index. Apart from this analysis, few studies have assessed the temporal variability of non‐culprit FFR in patients with ACS. Van der Hoeven et al demonstrated a significant reduction in FFR at 1 month compared with the acute phase. 11 On the contrary, Ntalianis et al, Musto et al, and Jo et al showed stable non‐culprit FFR measurements over time. 2 , 12 , 24 Yet, a trend toward reduction in FFR could be acknowledged in the study by Jo et al. 2 The major difference between the studies is the induction of hyperemia, which was achieved with intravenous adenosine in the studies by Van der Hoeven et al and Jo et al. Therefore, the use of intravenous adenosine during ACS might, to some extent, contribute to previously reported underestimation of lesion severity.

Non‐Culprit Microvascular Function During ACS

FFR should not be regarded as stand‐alone but rather evaluated within broader context. While FFR is focused on epicardial coronary stenosis, CFR, IMR, and MRR are the main metrics to characterize the coronary microcirculation. 25 In particular, CFR, which describes the ratio by which coronary blood flow can be augmented, and IMR, which is an effective parameter to assess microvascular resistance, have been established as validated markers to identify microvascular dysfunction. 25 In reality, epicardial and microvascular disease form a continuous spectrum. Microvascular dysfunction caused by myocardial infarction, which can be expressed by augmented IMR and decreased CFR, could lead to falsely elevated FFR values in culprit territory. 1 , 6 It is conceivable that similar findings may occur when abnormal IMR and CMR are detected in remote regions as well. In our trial, only slight numerical improvements in non‐culprit CFR, IMR, and MRR were observed. On the contrary, van der Hoeven et al found significant improvement of non‐culprit CFR and IMR as well, which seems related to their difference in FFR. 11 A crucial point of contrast is that Van der Hoeven et al only included patients with STEMI. Also, their patients presented with considerable larger peak creatinine kinase levels (median of 229 U/L versus 1086 U/L). In culprit vessels, it has been demonstrated that IMR values are higher in patients with STEMI compared with patients with NSTEMI. 26 Therefore, our data support the concept that non‐culprit microvascular function is reduced in patients with large STEMI and relatively unaffected in patients with NSTEMI or small STEMI.

Clinical Relevance

Based on our results, intracoronary adenosine boluses of ≥100 μg induce a stronger hyperemic response compared with intravenous adenosine at 140 μg/kg/min, which during the acute phase of ACS results in lower FFR values. Despite the moderate non‐culprit microvascular dysfunction observed in our study population, baseline FFR measurements using intravenous adenosine were significantly higher compared with follow‐up. Therefore, our observations support the use of adequate intracoronary adenosine boluses over intravenous adenosine at 140 μg/kg/min in the acute setting of ACS. Current findings reinforce the use of recommended intracoronary adenosine boluses (≥100 μg) based on previous studies. 19 , 20 However, caution is still warranted when interpreting non‐culprit FFR values of patients with large STEMI, in which considerable non‐culprit microvascular dysfunction is suspected. Higher intravenous adenosine doses might be needed to acquire a similar degree of hyperemia. Interestingly, Lindstaedt et al observed a stronger hyperemic response with intravenous adenosine administration at 170 μg/kg/min via the antecubital vein compared with 140 μg/kg/min, which is in contrast to the aforementioned studies by de Bruyne et al and Alexopoulos et al. 21 , 22 , 27 Therefore, future studies evaluating the dose‐dependent effects of both intravenous and intracoronary administration over the course of ACS would be of significant interest.

LIMITATIONS

Several limitations should be considered. First, the present analysis is exploratory in nature, as the study protocol was not specifically designed to address FFR variability. Patients were not required to withhold vasoactive medication or caffeine‐containing products before any procedure. Second, no core lab was involved in assessment of the pressure curves. Third, although prior studies have used similar sample sizes, our cohort of 53 patients remains relatively small. In addition, the number of included right coronary artery vessels was low, limiting the generalizability to the entire coronary tree. Fourth, baseline measurements were conducted during the index hospitalization but not always during the initial procedure itself. Therefore, we may have missed peak microvascular dysfunction in some patients. Fifth, merely FFR was measured during hyperemia with intracoronary adenosine. Assessment of microvascular function using intracoronary adenosine has recently been shown to be feasible and safe. 14 This would have been valuable for interpreting our findings. Also, only adenosine was used to induce hyperemia. Nonetheless, adenosine is widely favored over other hyperemic agents like papaverine or nicorandil. However, in recent years, continuous thermodilution is becoming increasingly popular to assess FFR, CFR,and MRR. For CFR and MRR, continuous thermodilution yields higher reproducibility. 28 On the other hand, IMR and CFR, measured using bolus thermodilution, have more established clinical experience and validated thresholds. 25 Finally, peak laboratory values were not available for all patients.

CONCLUSION

In the setting of ACS, adequate (≥100 μg) intracoronary adenosine boluses induce a more pronounced hyperemic response compared with intravenous adenosine at 140 μg/kg/min, yielding lower initial FFR values that remain stable over time. When only mild microvascular dysfunction is present, early non‐culprit FFR measurements using intracoronary adenosine appear to be a valid method for assessing non‐culprit lesion severity. Future studies investigating the dose‐dependent effect of intravenous adenosine during ACS are needed to assess the degree of induced hyperemia.
