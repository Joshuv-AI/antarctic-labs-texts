The impact of electronic health literacy on emotional management ability among college students: the mediating roles of peer relationships and exercise self-efficacy
Authors: Weidong Zhu, Jun Liu, Hu Lou, Fanzheng Mu, Bo Li
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aims to explore electronic health literacy’s impact on college students’ emotional management ability and analyze the chained mediating effects of peer relationships and exercise self-efficacy.MethodsThe study used stratified, cluster, and staged sampling methods to collect demographic information. Electronic health literacy, peer relationships, exercise self-efficacy, and emotional management ability data were collected among college students using the Questionnaire Star software. A total of 30,105 valid questionnaires were obtained, with a valid response rate of 98.7%. Using Process Model 6, investigated how electronic health literacy sequentially influences emotional management ability through mediators such as peer relationships and exercise self-efficacy.ResultsThere was a significant positive correlation between electronic health literacy and emotional management ability (r = 0.496, P < 0.01), between electronic health literacy and peer relationships (r = 0.226, P < 0.01), and between electronic health literacy and exercise self-efficacy (r = 0.180, P < 0.01). Peer relationships were significantly positively correlated with exercise self-efficacy (r = 0.142, P < 0.01) and emotional management ability (r = 0.396, P < 0.01). Exercise self-efficacy was significantly positively correlated with emotional management ability (r = 0.339, P < 0.01). Electronic health literacy significantly and positively predicted emotional management ability (β = 0.254, P < 0.001). There was a mediating effect of peer relationships between electronic health literacy and emotional management ability, accounting for 58.2% of the impact, 95%CI: [0.036,0.042]. Exercise self-efficacy also mediated the relationship between electronic health literacy and emotional management ability, accounting for 61.5% of the effect, 95%CI: [0.022,0.027]. Moreover, a chained mediating effect of peer relationships and exercise self-efficacy was observed between electronic health literacy and emotional management ability, accounting for 17.1% of the effect, 95%CI: [0.004,0.005].ConclusionElectronic health literacy among college students can positively predict emotional management ability. Both peer relationships and exercise self-efficacy play a chained mediating role between electronic health literacy and emotional management ability.Keywords: College Students, Electronic Health literacy, Emotional management ability, Exercise Self-efficacy, Mediating Effect, Peer relationships

Introduction

Emotional management ability was proposed by psychologists Peter Salovey and John D. Mayer. These abilities represent an individual’s comprehensive ability to recognize, understand, regulate, and express emotions [1]. Studies indicate that emotional management ability is a critical domain for individuals with mental health disorders, often manifesting as syndromes starting from adolescence (after the age of 12) [2, 3]. Within this context, student populations are navigating a critical phase characterized by rapid psychological and social identity changes [4]. particularly college students who face unique challenges and pressures, especially as they transition from student life to societal roles [5]. Poor emotional management can lead to long-term negative states, impairing cognitive functions, manifesting as slow thinking, and reduced willpower, among other issues [6]. In severe cases, it can lead to psychological issues like depression and potentially suicidal tendencies, causing significant negative impacts on schools, families, and society [7]. The level of emotional management ability dictates college students’ state in academic and campus life. Scholar Brown contends that schools exert the most potent influence over adolescents’ emotional issues and are thereby the prime venues for effective improvement and interventions [8]. Current research on emotional management ability highlights significant psychological trauma, peer evaluations, and life experiences as potential influencing factors [9–11]. Emotional management ability has become a focal point in research by psychologists globally, especially the exploration of its impact factors relating to health [12]. In the digital era, cultivating college students’ electronic health literacy has emerged as an effective means to improve emotional management ability [13].

Electronic health literacy refers to individuals’ capacity to seek, understand, evaluate, and make reasoned health-related decisions based on health information obtained through electronic media [14], and in the context of the digital age, electronic health literacy is seen as an extension of health literacy [14]. With the proliferation of the internet and the widespread use of digital devices, China’s netizen population has reached 1.067 billion [15], yet there’s a varied degree of penetration in electronic health literacy across the population [16]. Due to a lack of adequate education and public health information strategies, a significant gap remains, especially among elderly populations and those with lower educational levels [17–19]. Furthermore, reliance on electronic information has intensified due to unexpected public health events, significantly increasing the public demand for health information, thus raising higher requirements for electronic health literacy [20]. In the rapidly evolving digital age, the internet has become one of the primary channels for accessing psychological health resources and emotional support [21]. Recently, as the importance of electronic health literacy in the health sector has become more prominent, it has attracted widespread attention from academia [22]. Although emotional management-related mental disorder syndromes are considered incurable, understanding and managing emotional health information could lead to earlier diagnoses and better treatment outcomes [3].

Peer relationships refer to interpersonal connections formed among individuals of similar age or psychological development levels during social interactions [23]. These relationships significantly impact individual socialization and psychological development, especially during adolescence and college years [24]. In China, college students generally have favorable peer relationships, though issues like social anxiety and group exclusion exist [25]. Students with good peer relationships often have stronger social ability, enabling more support and assistance in study and life, thus improving academic performance and personal adaptability [26]. Conversely, students with poor peer relationships may feel lonely and ostracized, adversely affecting their academic performance and potentially leading to psychological issues such as depression and anxiety [27]. These students often lack adequate social ability, finding it challenging to forge and maintain stable relationships. Prior research has shown a significant negative correlation between peer relationships and emotional disorders [28]. Thus, peer relationships may have a positive impact on electronic health literacy and emotional management ability.

Self-efficacy refers to an individual’s confidence in their ability to complete specific tasks, showcasing their self-confidence and autonomy [29]. Exercise self-efficacy, as a crucial component, focuses on an individual’s confidence in persisting with physical fitness activities [30]. College years mark a sensitive period for students’ psychological development. In recent years, as positive psychology in sports exercise rapidly develops, enhancing exercise self-efficacy has begun to emerge as an important means to promote individual mental and physical health development [31]. Students with higher exercise self-efficacy are likely to exert more effort in various exercises and maintain persistence, believing that exercise can bring long-term mental and physical health benefits, and are more likely to enjoy pleasure from exercising [32, 33]. Conversely, students with lower exercise self-efficacy are more prone to replace exercise with pleasure-seeking activities, and when faced with setbacks, are more likely to doubt their abilities, leading to a negative cycle of decreasing exercise intent [34]. Previous studies have shown that exercise has a significant negative correlation with emotional disorders [35].

Within the domain of “health behaviors,” smoking and drinking behavior emerge as critical areas of inquiry due to their profound implications for both psychological and physiological health [36]. These behaviors not only pose significant risks to physical well-being but are also intricately linked to emotional management, thereby warranting focused investigation in the context of college students’ health behaviors [37]. Extant research illustrates that smoking and drinking consumption may contribute to challenges in emotional regulation, thereby escalating the risk of anxiety and depression [38]. The college years represent a pivotal phase for the establishment and reinforcement of life habits [39]. Thus, comprehending how these behaviors interact with electronic health literacy, peer relationships, and exercise self-efficacy to affect emotional management ability is of substantial theoretical and practical importance.

Although extensive research has explored college students’ emotional management ability and their influencing factors, studies on how electronic health literacy enhances psychological health by affecting emotional management ability are relatively limited. Specifically, using peer relationships and exercise self-efficacy as mediating variables, delving into these studies is crucial for understanding and utilizing electronic health resources to enhance college students’ emotional management potential. Peer relationships, an essential component of the social environment, directly influence college students’ psychological and emotional states [40]. Meanwhile, exercise self-efficacy, reflecting students’ confidence in continuing physical activities, can effectively alleviate negative emotions and enhance mental health [41]. How these factors influence emotional management ability in the context of electronic health literacy is yet to be fully explored. Therefore, investigating how peer relationships and exercise self-efficacy mediate between electronic health literacy and emotional management ability presents a new field of research, potentially offering more empirical support and theoretical guidance for promoting college students’ psychological health.

The Media Dependency Theory, proposed by DeFleur and Ball-Rokeach in 1976, posits that individuals’ dependency on media is contingent upon the media’s significance in fulfilling their specific needs [42]. This theory provides a framework for analyzing how people rely on media information to meet their needs and how such media dependency affects individuals’ perceptions, cognition, and behaviors [43]. According to the Media Dependency Theory, the degree of dependency on media depends on its importance in satisfying specific needs [44]. As a capacity that enables individuals to effectively use electronic media to search for, obtain, and evaluate health information, electronic health literacy can significantly affect individuals’ psychological health status [45]. Given that electronic health literacy levels may impact college students’ efficiency in obtaining support and managing emotions through online platforms, this literacy, through a media dependency mechanism, indirectly influences the quality of peer relationships, thereby affecting emotional management ability.

Methods

This study was conducted using the national data from the “Chinese College Student Health Tracking Survey” for the year 2022 [46, 47].

Participants

The study primarily utilized an epidemiological survey method. 104 universities in 31 provinces, autonomous regions, and municipalities across China were surveyed. The list of these institutions was derived from the “National List of Regular Higher Education Institutions” issued by the Ministry of Education, valid up to August 1, 2022. In August 2022, the survey was carried out electronically using the Wenjuanxing software (version 2.0.96), with questionnaires distributed according to administrative classes. A total of 30,513 questionnaires were retrieved.

Determination of sampling sites

To ensure representativeness, three sampling sites were allocated to each province (or autonomous region or municipality). The selection included one provincial capital as a “Type 1” site. Two additional cities were chosen based on geographical location and socioeconomic development: one with an average socioeconomic level as a “Type 2” site and another with a lower level as a “Type 3” site. For directly administered municipalities, cluster sampling was primarily used.

Selection of sampling units

The sampling units were selected based on three key criteria. First, only higher education institutions registered with the Ministry of Education, including both regular and vocational/technical colleges, were eligible to participate. Second, these units needed to meet specific sampling criteria, including student age, the number of enrolled students, and the distribution of students across different grades. Lastly, participating institutions had to designate responsible individuals for questionnaire distribution and demonstrate a willingness to engage in long-term monitoring. The survey was conducted after students had returned to campus for the fall semester. All surveys were conducted during physical education classes.

Stratification and sample size

The sample population was divided into two primary groups based on gender (male and female) and further stratified into eight categories according to grade level. Each category required a minimum sample size of 45 individuals. The total sample size per province (or autonomous region or municipality) was set at 1,080 participants, aiming for a national total of 33,480 participants.

Quality control

Data cleaning rules: Data cleaning was conducted to ensure the external validity of the analyzed data. In the data preprocessing stage, logical errors, omissions, inaccuracies, and indiscernible responses were identified and either retested or excluded to ensure data accuracy and validity. The following rules were applied for the inclusion of valid questionnaires: (1) questionnaires with unidentifiable school full names were deleted, resulting in the exclusion of 45 questionnaires. (2) questionnaires with codes indicating complete consistency for at least 21 consecutive entries were deleted, resulting in the exclusion of 153 questionnaires. (3) questionnaires with completion times falling within the ranges of (0, 0.5%] and (99.5%, 100%], given that the average completion time was 6 min and 12 s, were excluded. Note that there is overlap in the exclusion numbers from points 1 and 2. Finally, a total of 30105 valid questionnaires were included in the analysis. Out of the total 30513 questionnaires distributed, 30105 were deemed valid after screening, resulting in a response rate of approximately 98.7%.

The research plan was approved by the Ethics Committee of Nantong University (2022 [70]). Informed consent was obtained from all subjects included in the study. Measurement

Electronic health literacy

The Electronic Health Literacy Scale (eHEALS) was developed by Norman et al. and adapted into Simplified Chinese by Guo [48, 49]. The scale consists of 8 items, covering the ability to apply online health information and services (items 1–5), judgment ability (items 6–7), and decision-making ability (item 8). Each item uses a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from “strongly disagree” (1) to “strongly agree” (5), with the total score ranging from 8 to 40. Higher scores indicate higher levels of electronic health literacy. In this study, the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of the scale was 0.913, the cumulative contribution rate was 62.38%, and the KMO value was 0.904 [48].

Peer relationship

The Peer Relationship Scale (PRS) is based on the Student Peer Relationship Scale developed by Asher and revised by Zhang, encompassing dimensions of acceptance, rejection, and loneliness [50, 51]. This scale includes 16 items scored on a 4-point Likert scale, ranging from “completely disagree” (1) to “completely agree” (4), where higher scores indicate better peer relationships. Reverse scoring items are 2, 4, 6, 8, 9, 11, 12, 13, 14, and 16. The Cronbach’s alpha for this measure is 0.870, the KMO value is 0.899, and the cumulative contribution rate is 53.0% [50].

Physical exercise self-efficacy

Measurement was carried out using the Physical Activity Self-efficacy Scale (PASS), developed by Jiang [52], which includes two dimensions: situational motivation and subjective support, comprising 10 items. It uses a 5-point Likert scale, from “1 = strongly disagree” to “5 = strongly agree”. Higher item scores indicate higher self-efficacy in physical exercise. The cumulative variance contribution rate of the two factors is 67.782%, and the confirmatory factor analysis shows that the two-factor model fits well. The overall scale’s Cronbach’s alpha, split-half reliability, and test-retest reliability are 0.908, 0.819, and 0.531, respectively [52].

Emotional intelligence scale

The Emotional Intelligence Scale (EIS), translated by the Chinese scholar Wang, was used to measure university students’ emotional management abilities [53, 54]. EIS consists of 33 items across 4 subscales. This study utilized the emotional management abilities subscale, composed of questions 2, 6, 7, 10, 12, 14, 21, and 28, using a 5-point Likert scale from 1 to 5, ranging from “strongly disagree” to “strongly agree”. The overall scale’s Cronbach’s alpha was 0.907, with construct validity ranging between 0.827 and 0.884 [55].

Health behavior

Following the World Health Organization (WHO) definitions, smoking behavior was categorized into “never smoked”, “occasional smoking”, and “frequent smoking” for analysis [56].

Reflecting the American Youth Risk Behavior Surveillance System, drinking behavior was analyzed based on the number of days drunk and the number of days intoxicated in the past month, categorized into “never drank”, “drank without getting drunk”, and “drank and got drunk” [57].

Results

Test for common method bias

In this study, due to the utilization of self-reported subjective scales for data collection, to counteract common method bias, Harman’s single-factor test was employed for an exploratory factor analysis of all questionnaire items related to electronic health literacy, peer relationships, exercise self-efficacy, and emotional management abilities, after data acquisition. The results yielded five principal components with eigenvalues greater than 1, where the largest factor explained 29.3% of the variance, which is lower than the commonly set threshold of 40% [59]. Consequently, this study does not suffer from common method bias.

Overview of descriptive analysis results

Electronic health literacy

The total score for college students’ electronic health literacy is 28.608 ± 7.845. There are no significant differences in terms of gender, smoking, and drinking, but there is a significant difference at the grade level (P < 0.001). Freshmen’s total score for electronic health literacy (29.282 ± 7.438) is significantly higher than that of sophomores (28.442 ± 7.824), juniors (28.114 ± 8.201), and seniors (28.020 ± 8.392).

Emotional management ability

The total score for emotional management ability is 29.564 ± 5.118, with no significant difference at the gender level, but there are significant differences at the levels of grade, smoking, and drinking (P < 0.001), with the effect size at the grade level being small (η2 = 0.010). Freshmen’s scores for emotional management ability (30.323 ± 4.752) are significantly higher than those of seniors (28.989 ± 5.582). Students who smoke regularly have significantly lower scores for emotional management ability (28.592 ± 6.123) than those who never smoke (29.607 ± 5.049) and those who smoke occasionally (29.203 ± 5.771), and this pattern is also observed in drinking behavior. College students who have been drunk have significantly lower scores for emotional management ability (28.932 ± 5.771) than those who have never drunk (29.633 ± 5.091) and those who have drunk but never been drunk (29.069 ± 5.177).

Peer relationships

The total score for college students’ peer relationships is 46.648 ± 8.696, with significant differences in terms of gender, grade, smoking, and drinking (P < 0.001). At the gender level, females’ scores for peer relationships (47.131 ± 8.352) are significantly higher than males’ (45.948 ± 9.128). At the grade level, freshmen have the highest scores for peer relationships among the four grades (47.771) and are significantly higher than the other three grades (P < 0.001). In terms of smoking status, it can be seen that students who smoke regularly have significantly lower scores for peer relationships (44.835 ± 9.035) than those who have never smoked (46.709 ± 8.644) and those who smoke occasionally (46.260 ± 9.335). In terms of drinking status, scores for peer relationships gradually decrease as drinking behavior increases (P < 0.001). Students who have never drunk have significantly higher scores for peer relationships (46.794 ± 8.709) than those who have drunk without getting drunk (45.676 ± 8.436) and those who have been drunk (44.918 ± 8.903).

Exercise self-efficacy

The total score for college students’ exercise self-efficacy is 37.188 ± 8.639, with significant differences in terms of gender, grade, and smoking (P < 0.001), but no significant difference in terms of drinking. From the gender perspective, males’ scores for exercise self-efficacy (39.192 ± 8.363) are significantly higher than females’ (35.803 ± 8.555). At the grade level, the scores for exercise self-efficacy of freshmen (37.462 ± 8.252) and sophomores (37.233 ± 8.658) are significantly higher than those of juniors (36.808 ± 8.992) and seniors (36.864 ± 9.072). In terms of smoking status, students who smoke occasionally have significantly higher scores for exercise self-efficacy (39.275 ± 8.381) than those who have never smoked (37.048 ± 8.607) and those who smoke regularly (37.765 ± 10.037) (P < 0.001).

Discussion

This study aimed to reveal the relationship between electronic health literacy and emotional management ability among college students by introducing peer relationships and exercise self-efficacy as variables, constructing and verifying a chained mediation model between college students’ electronic health literacy and emotional management capability. The study found that the total effect of electronic health literacy on emotional management ability was 0.321, with a direct effect of 0.254 and an indirect effect of 0.067.

Descriptive results analysis

No significant differences were observed in emotional management abilities among college students based on gender, grade, smoking, or drinking, which is inconsistent with previous studies on gender differences in college students’ emotional management abilities [60]. Research has shown significant gender differences in emotional management abilities among younger students, with girls generally outperforming boys [61]. This may be due to the lower secretion of female hormones compared to males, as hormones are an important dimension influencing the body’s aggression and control desires [60]. As students age and mature, these differences likely diminish [62]. Regarding grade level, students’ scores on emotional management abilities increased and then decreased, possibly due to growing employment concerns as they advanced in college. Worries about job scarcity, career development concerns, and job-related expectations can create stress and anxiety, impacting emotional management abilities [63]. In terms of smoking and drinking, college students who smoke and drink regularly or have been drunk scored significantly lower in emotional management abilities, consistent with prior research [64]. Smoking and drinking have been significantly correlated with poorer emotional management abilities in many studies [65]. There were no significant differences in electronic health literacy across gender, smoking, and drinking habits among college students, which differs from previous findings [66, 67]. This may be because the study samples were college students who generally received similar levels of education, including access to health information and electronic resources [68]. Higher education provides equal access to information, thereby reducing disparities in electronic health literacy [69, 70]. Peer relationships showed no significant differences across gender, grade, smoking, and drinking. This contradicts previous research findings [71–73], possibly because the ubiquity of the internet and social media has led to more diverse and tolerant methods of forming peer relationships among college students, blurring the boundaries between genders, grades, and lifestyle habits like smoking and drinking [74]. There were no significant differences in exercise self-efficacy across gender, grade, smoking, and drinking habits [75], likely because the sample consisted mainly of college students, the majority of whom had never experienced drinking.

The study found a significant positive prediction of electronic health literacy on emotional management ability (β = 0.254), confirming the study’s hypothesis H1. Moreover, electronic health literacy and its sub-dimensions were significantly positively correlated with emotional management ability. This aligns with previous studies [76], showing that college students with higher electronic health literacy have better abilities in obtaining, understanding, evaluating, and applying health information, which helps them manage their emotions and stress more effectively. In today’s digital age, effectively utilizing online resources for health knowledge and emotional regulation is particularly important [77]. To improve the impact of electronic health literacy on emotional management ability, it is recommended to strengthen the digital health courses offered in universities, teaching students how to effectively use electronic resources to obtain health information. Additionally, provides online mental health resources and tools to help students self-assess and manage their emotions. Encouraging students to learn emotional management strategies independently and share their experiences through interactive platforms will also be beneficial.

Positive mediating effect of peer relationships

This study discovered that peer relationships play a positive mediating role between electronic health literacy and emotional management ability among college students, with a significant mediation effect value of 0.039, accounting for 58.2% of the total effect, thus confirming hypothesis H2. Moreover, both peer relationships and their sub-dimensions showed a significant positive correlation with electronic health literacy and its sub-dimensions, as well as with emotional management ability. This suggests that peer relationships act as a crucial bridge between college students’ electronic health literacy and their ability to manage emotions. As a key psychological variable, peer relationships have long been employed in various studies [78]. By utilizing peer relationships as a mediating variable, this study explores how electronic health literacy influences emotional management abilities. The findings indicate that the positive mediating effect of peer relationships enhances the impact of electronic health literacy on emotional management capabilities. This supports previous research [79], highlighting that the strength of peer relationships can shape individuals’ emotional management abilities in diverse contexts [80].

College students with higher levels of electronic health literacy can share and access health information through broader social networks via interactions with peers, effectively enhancing their emotional management abilities [13]. The support and positive feedback from peers provide emotional comfort and strategic insights, helping individuals better handle stress and challenges by managing their emotions more proactively [80]. This process of mutual help and collaboration among students extends beyond academics and health, permeating various aspects of daily life and enhancing their social cohesion and intimacy. This positive emotional exchange offers everyone a safe environment to openly share their feelings and challenges, thus gaining understanding and support from their peers [81]. Such support comes not only from verbal encouragement, understanding, and empathy but also through concrete actions and the establishment of long-term stable relationships, providing ongoing emotional support and practical help [82]. These factors collectively play a role in enhancing and manifesting the mediating role of peer relationships between electronic health literacy and emotional management ability. Moreover, this mediating effect is not limited to a specific event but is a continuous, positive process that helps form good self-regulation patterns and long-term psychological adaptability [83]. Therefore, interventions that strengthen peer relationships are particularly vital in promoting the overall psychological health and emotional regulation abilities of college students. By encouraging and reinforcing positive peer relationships among college students, not only can their electronic health literacy be improved, but their overall emotional management and psychological health levels can be comprehensively enhanced. This is crucial for nurturing high-quality talents capable of adapting to modern complex societal challenges. Given the positive mediating effect of peer relationships on electronic health literacy and emotional management ability, future interventions should focus on strengthening these relationships. Specific suggestions include: establishing peer support mechanisms to encourage students to share and exchange health information and emotion regulation skills within groups; conducting peer training programs to enhance students’ psychological support and health communication capabilities; utilizing social media to promote information sharing and emotional exchanges; and regularly evaluating and optimizing peer support programs. These measures will help further enhance the positive impact of peer relationships, improve students’ electronic health literacy and emotional management abilities, and promote their overall mental health and well-being.

Positive mediating effect of exercise self-efficacy

This study demonstrates that exercise self-efficacy significantly mediates the relationship between electronic health literacy and emotional management ability among college students, confirming hypothesis H3 with a mediation effect value of 0.024, accounting for 61.5% of the total effect. Additionally, exercise self-efficacy and its sub-dimensions exhibit significant positive correlations with electronic health literacy and its sub-dimensions, as well as with emotional management ability. These results indicate that in the context of electronic health literacy, an increase in college students’ exercise self-efficacy positively enhances their emotional management abilities. This finding aligns with research by Wang [84]. Students with higher self-efficacy are often more confident in their ability to perform certain actions and achieve desired outcomes, and they are better at perceiving and assessing emotions in specific settings.

College students with higher electronic health literacy are more adept at accessing information about effective exercise, boosting their self-efficacy and confidence in maintaining a healthy lifestyle [85, 86]. As exercise self-efficacy increases, students are more likely to engage in regular physical activities, improving both physical fitness and emotional regulation [87]. Exercise is widely recognized as an effective means to relieve stress and regulate emotions [88], releasing endorphins that help alleviate stress and anxiety, thereby enhancing emotional stability and mental health [89]. This process allows students with high electronic health literacy to access more professional information about ongoing physical activity and healthy living, making it easier for them to understand and apply this information [90]. Therefore, when students believe they can achieve expected health outcomes through regular physical activity, their exercise self-efficacy improves accordingly [91]. This enhanced confidence encourages them to continue participating in sports activities, which in turn affects their emotional management abilities, improving emotional stability [92]. The series of positive effects brought by exercise self-efficacy are not limited to boosting individual physical fitness and emotional management; they also enhance overall life quality [93]. Given the significant role of physical self-efficacy in the relationship between electronic health literacy and emotional management ability, intervention measures should focus on enhancing exercise self-efficacy in college students. This can be achieved by providing comprehensive resources, organizing training courses, implementing personalized coaching, creating supportive campus environments, and incorporating physical self-efficacy activities into learning management systems. These measures will help improve students’ emotional regulation and mental health.

Chain mediating effect of peer relationships and exercise self-efficacy

This study reveals a chain mediating effect of peer relationships and exercise self-efficacy between electronic health literacy and emotional management ability, with an effect value of 0.004 and an effect proportion of 17.1%, validating hypothesis H4. Correlation analysis indicates significant positive correlations between electronic health literacy, peer relationships, and exercise self-efficacy, further confirming the chain mediation model. The results suggest that electronic health literacy positively influences peer relationships, which in turn enhances exercise self-efficacy, further positively promoting college students’ emotional management abilities.

Bronfenbrenner’s ecological systems theory emphasizes that individual development is influenced by the interactions of multiple environmental systems [94]. In this study, college students with high electronic health literacy effectively retrieve, evaluate, and utilize health information, enhancing communication and information sharing with peers on the microsystem level [95]. This enhanced communication and peer acknowledgment, according to Bandura’s social cognitive theory, further strengthens individual self-efficacy, particularly in exercise [96]. Good peer relationships provide social support, which is crucial for boosting exercise self-efficacy. This support is not only a significant supplement to intrinsic motivation but also serves as an external motivator in Vroom’s expectancy theory, driving behavior [97]. peer support not only fosters confidence in engaging in regular exercise but also supports consistency in such activities, aligning with Maslow’s need for self-actualization [98]. Regular physical exercise enhances individual physical health but also brings about positive changes at the emotional level [99]. By improving exercise self-efficacy, which promotes participation in healthy physical activities, further positive impacts on emotional management abilities are realized [100]. Regular physical activity has been proven to significantly enhance emotional stability, reduce symptoms of anxiety and depression, and strengthen stress-coping ability [101]. Therefore, electronic health literacy, by optimizing peer relationships and enhancing exercise self-efficacy through chain mediation paths, ultimately promotes more effective emotional management ability, offering a significant avenue to improve the overall health and welfare of college students. Our research found that electronic health literacy influences peer relationships, which in turn enhances college students’ exercise self-efficacy, ultimately improving their emotion management ability. Therefore, educational policies should prioritize digital literacy workshops, peer support mechanisms, and team fitness activities. These measures will strengthen the positive impact of electronic health literacy on peer relationships, boost exercise self-efficacy, and ultimately enhance emotion management ability and overall mental health.

Limitations of this study include (1) The use of self-reported information, which may introduce bias and affect the reliability of the results. (2) There may be significant endogeneity issues in the analytical framework, including potential reverse causality. This complicates the direction of causal relationships between the explanatory variables and may lead to misinterpretations of the analysis. Even if statistical correlations exist, they should be interpreted with caution, as the exact causal relationships remain unclear. (3) Further research is needed to establish causal relationships. Future studies should consider longitudinal designs or experimental interventions better to understand the directionality and causality of these pathways.

Conclusion

In summary, this study suggests that college students’ electronic health literacy is associated with enhanced emotional management abilities. Additionally, it appears to influence emotional management indirectly through potential chain-mediated effects involving peer relationships and exercise self-efficacy. These findings highlight the potential importance of improving electronic health literacy among college students in the digital age and exploring strategies that may help maintain and promote mental health through peer interactions and exercise habits. At the same time, these results provide valuable insights for understanding educational and health promotion strategies in the 21st century, particularly within higher education institutions, further longitudinal and experimental research is needed to establish causal relationships and optimize the use of electronic health resources in supporting students’ emotional regulation and mental health management.
