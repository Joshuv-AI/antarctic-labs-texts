Successful Translational Prediction of Bedaquiline's Phase 2a Clinical Trial Results against Tuberculosis: The Zebrafish as a New Approach Methodology
Authors: Bart van Lieshout, Aanisah Hanuun, J G Coen van Hasselt, Rob C van Wijk
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTuberculosis remains an urgent global health crisis that requires an accelerated development of novel regimens. Rodent and rabbit studies currently inform early translational predictions. However, the zebrafish (Danio rerio) embryo/larva, as a New Approach Methodology, provides an ethical, cost‐effective, and high‐throughput non‐mammalian disease model to study early anti‐tuberculosis drug pharmacology and bridge the gap between in vitro drug screens and (confirmatory) rodent experiments. This study aimed to translate the bedaquiline exposure–response relationship from zebrafish to humans to predict two phase 2a trial results in order to strengthen the use of zebrafish as a New Approach Methodology for translational anti‐tuberculosis pharmacology. An established exposure–response relationship for bedaquiline in zebrafish against Mycobacterium marinum as a tuberculosis disease model was combined with a clinical population pharmacokinetic model to predict bacterial burden profiles from two published phase 2a clinical trials (25 to 400 mg daily). This translation incorporated the minimum inhibitory concentration ratio between Mycobacterium marinum and Mycobacterium tuberculosis to scale the zebrafish bedaquiline EC50 to humans. Clinical simulations based on the scaled zebrafish exposure–response relationship demonstrated that accounting for in vitro drug sensitivity resulted in close alignment with observed bacterial burden across all doses and timepoints, with a mean absolute percentage error of 10.37% and a root mean squared error of 0.74 log10 colony‐forming units per milliliter of sputum. These findings demonstrate that early‐phase clinical results for bedaquiline in tuberculosis can be successfully predicted from pharmacokinetics–pharmacodynamics in the zebrafish tuberculosis disease model. These results help advance the zebrafish as a resource‐efficient and sustainable New Approach Methodology for tuberculosis drug development.

Study Highlights

WHAT IS THE CURRENT KNOWLEDGE ON THE TOPIC?

The prediction of clinical efficacy from animal models is essential for the development of anti‐tuberculosis (TB) drugs. However, mammalian models are costly, slow, and pose ethical burdens. Zebrafish (Danio rerio) embryos/larvae offer a potential high‐throughput, ethical New Approach Methodology (NAM), with a proof of principle for translating the efficacy of isoniazid.

WHAT QUESTION DID THIS STUDY ADDRESS?

Can the NAM zebrafish model successfully predict the phase 2 clinical early bactericidal activity (EBA) across anti‐TB drugs, specifically bedaquiline?

WHAT DOES THIS STUDY ADD TO OUR KNOWLEDGE?

This study demonstrates that clinical EBA for bedaquiline can be successfully predicted using the innovative NAM approach of longitudinal bacterial burden measurements in zebrafish, by utilizing translational pharmacokinetic–pharmacodynamic modeling and translational factors accounting for infection phase and drug sensitivity.

HOW MIGHT THIS CHANGE CLINICAL PHARMACOLOGY OR TRANSLATIONAL SCIENCE?

These findings help establish zebrafish embryos/larvae as a highly predictive NAM for tuberculosis. Zebrafish as a NAM can contribute to reduced reliance on traditional mammalian studies for anti‐TB drug candidate selection, early clinical efficacy predictions, and dose optimization in TB drug development.

Tuberculosis (TB), the leading cause of death by an infectious disease, remains an urgent global health crisis. 1 The standard treatment for TB consists of an extensive antimicrobial chemotherapy regimen, associated with a high incidence of adverse drug effects and poor adherence. Developing shorter regimens and novel anti‐TB modalities is therefore critical, yet this involves substantial time and high financial risks. Both computational and experimental innovations are necessary to address these challenges. 2 Model‐informed drug development has emerged as an essential strategy to enhance the translation of preclinical results in TB drug development. Computational models can simulate host–pathogen–drug dynamics over time to predict clinical outcomes such as the early bactericidal activity (EBA). 3 These predictions are informed by data from preclinical studies in mammalian species (e.g., rodents, lagomorphs). However, the ethical challenges, high costs, and length of these mammalian studies require New Approach Methodologies (NAMs) to replace, reduce, and/or refine them. 4 , 5

Phylogenetically lower, non‐mammalian models offer a NAM to traditional animal use by combining in vivo complexity with in vitro qualities such as high‐throughput capabilities, and the zebrafish (Danio rerio) is the most predominant in recent submissions. 6 Zebrafish share key genetic and developmental pathways with humans and develop TB‐like granulomas when infected with Mycobacterium marinum. 7 Crucially, zebrafish embryos and larvae enable early assessment of anti‐TB pharmacology in smaller‐scale facilities under less restrictive biosafety conditions. Furthermore, their optical transparency facilitates detailed longitudinal pharmacodynamic (PD) imaging, yielding rich data essential for pharmacological modeling. Taken together, these characteristics position the zebrafish as a rapid in vivo method complementary to in vitro methods like the hollow fiber infection model, which excels at mimicking dynamic concentration–time profiles for pharmacokinetic–pharmacodynamic studies but lacks complex host–pathogen interaction beyond inoculated pre‐infected immune cells. 8 As a proof of principle, zebrafish embryos and larvae have successfully been used to predict the clinical efficacy of isoniazid, 9 and a standardized workflow for their use has been proposed. 10

To strengthen the translational modeling approach of zebrafish, further evaluation by retrospective computational predictions of clinical trials for different standard anti‐TB drugs is required. 4 Establishing the reliability of zebrafish‐to‐human clinical outcome predictions could help reduce and refine preclinical mammal studies in TB drug development. This study aimed to translate the exposure–response relationship of the currently most promising anti‐TB drug, bedaquiline (BDQ), from zebrafish to humans to assess predictive accuracy based on two dose‐ranging phase 2a clinical trials. 11 The clinical EBA observed in the trials was simulated based on a developed model combining established human pharmacokinetics (PK) with zebrafish‐derived PD. The alignment between the model's predictions and observed results was evaluated to ultimately demonstrate the zebrafish's potential as a resource‐efficient and sustainable non‐mammalian NAM for TB drug development.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Bedaquiline pharmacodynamics against Mycobacterium marinum in zebrafish embryos and larvae

The exposure–response relationship of bedaquiline against M. marinum in zebrafish embryos/larvae, used to predict the clinical EBA, was derived from a previously published zebrafish PKPD study where infected zebrafish within 5 days post‐fertilization were exposed to waterborne BDQ concentrations ranging from 0 to 50 μM. 10 In short, PK was determined from pooled whole‐body homogenates, assuming a volume of distribution equal to total larval volume, 12 and PD was measured longitudinally with fluorescence. The concentration–response relationship was described by a saturable maximum equation with a 50% effective concentration (EC50) of 26.5 mg/L and a maximum effect equal to 109% of the bacterial growth rate constant, which increased to 137% after 24 hours of BDQ exposure. 10 , 12

Simulations of the clinical pharmacokinetics of bedaquiline

A published human bedaquiline PK model simulated clinical exposures for the translation of zebrafish PD 13 . The same structural components, covariate relationships (weight, age, and race), and inter‐individual variability on structural PK parameters were kept, but inter‐occasion variability was omitted. Mean study covariate values were used for simulations unless individual‐level information was available.

Clinical bedaquiline study characteristics

Two randomized, double‐blind, placebo‐controlled phase 2a clinical trials of BDQ were available to evaluate the translational model: a 7‐day study with aggregated data and a 14‐day study with individual‐level data. 14 , 15 Both studies determined the longitudinal bacterial burden in colony‐forming units per milliliter (CFU/mL), collected from the sputum of participants not previously treated with BDQ.

The aggregate‐level data were derived from reported values of 45 participants across 25, 100, and 400 mg daily regimens of BDQ. 14 The individual‐level data were obtained from the TB‐PACTS clinical data‐sharing platform. 15 , 16 This trial included 60 participants and four BDQ regimens, consisting of 2 loading doses followed by daily maintenance doses of 200/100/100 mg, 400/300/200 mg, 500/400/300 mg, and 700/500/400 mg (loading dose day 1/loading dose day 2/maintenance dose from day 3), respectively.

Clinical baseline bacterial burden for aggregate data was imputed from simulated values based on the mean reported bacterial burden per regimen and the pooled variance of all timepoints, 14 or from estimated individual Empirical Bayes Estimates (EBE) of a mixed‐effects intercept model on individual observations before BDQ treatment start. 15 , 16 L2‐level modeling was included for correlations between technical replicate measurements. Population baselines were estimated independently for each dosing regimen but had a shared inter‐individual variability.

Translational predictions of the clinical early bactericidal activity of bedaquiline from zebrafish embryos and larvae

The interspecies scaling between zebrafish and clinical trial participants was performed based on two common assumptions: no net bacterial growth of Mycobacterium tuberculosis occurs during the clinical studies due to the stage of infection, and a drug susceptibility difference between M. tuberculosis and M. marinum. 4 , 9 , 17

Considering the assumed equilibrium of growth and death of M. tuberculosis during chronic infections, the maximum drug effect in zebrafish, which was proportional to the bacterial growth rate constant (109–137% of 0.0853 h−1), 10 was reparametrized to 0.093–0.117 h−1. For the difference in susceptibility, in vitro minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) scaling was implemented (Eq. 1). 9 , 17 This approach used the ratio between the MICs of M. tuberculosis (MICMtb) and M. marinum (MICMm) to scale the drug potency from zebrafish (EC50,ZFL) to humans (EC50,human). The geometric mean of EUCAST‐reported MICs (~0.04 mg/L) was utilized for MICMtb, aligning with the clinical trial populations' susceptibilities (< 0.1 mg/L). 15 , 18 For the MICMm, 0.1 mg/L was used. 19

As a sensitivity analysis for the discordance between zebrafish whole‐body and human plasma concentrations, further translational factors were evaluated. These included the adjustment of the EC50 by a calculated zebrafish central volume through allometric scaling from humans and, oppositely, the use of human whole‐body concentrations to drive the pharmacodynamic effect. Full details of the translational model code can be found in the Supplementary Materials .

Simulations (n = 2000) were generated for each participant of the 14‐day study and each trial arm of the 7‐day study to compare their prediction intervals (PI) against the mean clinical observations, in addition to assessing the mean absolute prediction error (MAPE) and the root mean squared error (RMSE). In this article, the time of pre‐exposure observations was set to day 0. The bactericidal effect was further assessed by evaluating the observed versus 95% PI of the EBA in log10(CFU/mL) from day 0 to 14 (EBA0‐14d) for individual‐level clinical data. The estimated baseline EBE was used as the day 0 measurement, and 8 individuals without a reported day 14 measurement were omitted from this comparison. Lastly, the overall correlation between individual measurements and their simulated bacterial burden was assessed.

Software and analysis

The baseline estimation, carried out using Laplacian estimation with interaction, and simulations of the clinical EBA were performed through NONMEM (v7.5.1, RRID: SCR_016986) with PsN (v5.3.1) and the Pirana (v23.10.1) interface. 20 , 21 , 22 Data processing and graphical imaging were conducted with R (v4.3.3, RRID: SCR_001905) using RStudio (v2023.12.1 + 402, RRID: SCR_000432) as the integrated development environment and the ggplot2 (v3.5.0, RRID: SCR_014601) package. The graphical assessment for the comparison between the translational predictions and the clinical observations of the EBA over time was generated with the vpc (v1.2.2) package. 23

RESULTS

Simulated exposures and baselines

The plasma concentration–time profiles of the dosing regimens in the phase 2 EBA studies were simulated to drive the response using the clinical PK model, which encompassed their reported pharmacokinetic observations (Figure S1 ). 13 , 14 , 15 The estimated baselines (residual standard error) of the 14‐day study (Figure S2 ) were quantified as 5.96 (3.7%), 5.69 (3.9%), 5.82 (3.8%), and 6.20 (3.6%) log10(CFU/mL) for the 100, 200, 300, and 400 mg dosing regimen groups, respectively, with a coefficient of variation (CV) of 98.6% (10%). 15 , 16 The reported baselines of the 7‐day clinical data were slightly higher at 6.66, 6.32, and 6.82 for the 25, 100, and 400 mg dosing groups, respectively, with the variance calculated to be 68.2% CV. 14

Translational bacterial burden predictions reflect clinical observations

After coupling the simulated PK to a scaled zebrafish exposure–response relationship, the resulting simulated prediction interval for the decline in bacterial burden over the 14‐day treatment period closely aligned with the observed clinical trajectories across the regimens (Figure 1 ), with a slight overprediction of the drug effect in the highest dosing group (MAPE: 12.94%, RMSE: 0.83 log10(CFU/mL)). The predictions of the 7‐day study demonstrated a higher accuracy (MAPE: 7.8%, RMSE: 0.63 log10(CFU/mL)). Combined, this resulted in a MAPE of 10.37% and an RMSE of 0.74 log10(CFU/mL).

Visual predictive checks of simulations of the studies (n = 2000) that were performed with the translational model to produce simulations of the bacterial burden in colony‐forming units per milliliter (CFU/mL) over time per treatment arm. 14 , 15 , 16 The time of pre‐exposure measurements was set to day 0. Shown are the mean observations (black circles), observed standard deviation (black solid lines), the simulated median (dashed dark blue line), and the 95% prediction interval (blue ribbon).

The individual‐level EBA0‐14d comparison included 52 participants with bacterial burden measurements up to day 14. The translational model precisely predicted the EBA0‐14d, with all observed median values falling within the 95% PI of the simulated outcomes (Figure 2 a ). For the 14‐day 100, 200, 300, and 400 mg groups, the observed means versus the 95% PI were 0.828 (0.287–1.09), 0.975 (0.595–2.05), 1.18 (0.825–2.81), and 1.61 (1.12–3.61) log10(CFU/mL), respectively.

Comparison of observed and simulated clinical outcomes and measurements of the 14‐day clinical early bactericidal activity study. 14 , 15 , 16 (a) Boxplots and density plots displaying the distribution of the observed and simulated (n = 2000) 0‐ to 14‐day early bactericidal activity (EBA0‐14d) values, expressed in colony‐forming units per milliliter (CFU/mL) and stratified by treatment arm. (b) Scatterplot showing the correlation between individual observed and simulated measurements.

The graphical assessment of the individual measurements per participant demonstrated a strong agreement between the observations and individual simulations over time for all regimens, confirming the model's robustness on the individual level (Figure 2 b ). This individual‐level accuracy was further confirmed by a visual predictive check with individual observational data (Figure S3 ). Alternative translational factors that accounted for whole‐body versus plasma PK demonstrated inferior predictive performance, overpredicting the observed bacterial burden reduction (Figure S4 ).

DISCUSSION

We developed a translational PKPD approach to translate the exposure–response relationship of bedaquiline from zebrafish embryos and larvae to human EBA to explore the suitability of zebrafish as a non‐mammalian New Approach Methodology in translational anti‐TB drug development studies. As a vertebrate model organism with longitudinal individual observations, the zebrafish bridges in vitro throughput with in vivo pharmacological interpretation. When used in simulating bacterial decline across a range of drug exposures, the simulated trajectories and EBA outcomes closely matched clinical observations, supporting the utility of zebrafish as a NAM to forecast clinical efficacy.

A key component of our translational approach was addressing the difference in bacterial susceptibility between M. marinum in zebrafish and M. tuberculosis in humans. While M. marinum causes granuloma‐like infections in zebrafish, it has a different susceptibility to bedaquiline than M. tuberculosis. 7 The in vitro MIC‐based scaling approach for this difference demonstrated strong predictive performance, in line with previous applications of this scaling approach. 9 , 17 Notably, in vitro MIC values are straightforward to obtain, making this an accessible and practical translational factor to incorporate in early‐stage drug evaluation. 24

A homogeneous drug distribution as measured in the zebrafish model was assumed for the exposure–response translation. This assumption was a simplification given the PK of BDQ, which has a large volume of distribution and accumulates extensively in peripheral tissues. 13 , 25 By directly bridging human plasma with zebrafish whole‐body PK, which reflects drug exposure across tissues and infection‐relevant compartments, the translational model averaged out the distribution phase and tissue‐specific gradients. The sensitivity analysis that addressed whole‐body versus plasma differences resulted in poor predictive accuracy. Therefore, while this assumption neglects the complexities of bedaquiline distribution at the site of infection, it resulted in a sufficient fit‐for‐purpose approximation. 25 , 26

The minor remaining overprediction observed at the tail end of 400 mg dose predictions could be attributed to bacterial subpopulations for which the treatment duration‐dependent effect of BDQ in zebrafish may not have sufficed. 10 M. tuberculosis displays dynamic phenotypic subpopulations with differing drug susceptibilities. This behavior has been described in the multistate tuberculosis pharmacometrics model and was successfully applied to translate isoniazid's effect from zebrafish to humans. 9 , 27 Additionally, the lack of explicit scaling for interspecies protein‐binding differences remains a limitation that could contribute to the prediction discrepancy, as zebrafish lack albumin. 28 Regardless, this type of model and other unaccounted‐for factors come with a higher experimental and modeling burden that makes it difficult to utilize for early TB drug discovery, in contrast to the in vitro MIC, which was ultimately sufficient.

Our findings highlight that a translational PKPD model combining human PK and zebrafish‐derived PD can yield accurate and precise EBA predictions for BDQ, warranting further prospective evaluations. While zebrafish might not fully replace confirmatory rodent and rabbit studies altogether, the demonstrated correlation between zebrafish efficacy and clinical outcomes could significantly help reduce and refine them. This study thereby reinforces zebrafish as a relevant fit‐for‐purpose preclinical NAM to partially replace and complement rodent infection models. Furthermore, this study lays the foundation for a more systematic framework to produce and further evaluate early human anti‐TB efficacy predictions from zebrafish, with the potential to de‐risk early‐stage compounds, optimize study dosing, and ultimately accelerate TB drug development.
