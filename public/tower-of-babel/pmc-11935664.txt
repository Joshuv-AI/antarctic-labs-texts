Lipoprotein(a) and High‐Sensitivity C‐Reactive Protein Compound the Risk of Hypoattenuating Leaflet Thickening After Transcatheter Aortic Valve Replacement
Authors: Wence Shi, Dejing Feng, Xiangming Hu, Can Wang, Guannan Niu, Zhenyan Zhao, Hongliang Zhang, Moyang Wang, Yongjian Wu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe mechanism for hypoattenuating leaflet thickening (HALT) after transcatheter aortic valve replacement is still not well elucidated, and the role of Lp(a) (lipoprotein[a]) and hs‐CRP (high‐sensitivity C‐reactive protein) has rarely been studied. This study sought to test the hypothesis that the risk of HALT is associated with an elevated level of Lp(a) or hs‐CRP.Methods and ResultsA total of 307 consecutive individuals who underwent a transcatheter aortic valve replacement procedure were included. All patients received their first postoperative computed tomography scans within 12 months, and raw data were analyzed on 3mensio software. HALT was defined as visually identified increased leaflet thickness with typical meniscal appearance and at least 2 different multiplanar reformation projections. Associations of Lp(a) or hs‐CRP with the risk of HALT were evaluated using multivariable logistic regression analysis. The incidence of HALT within 12 months after transcatheter aortic valve replacement in this study was 36.2%, and the risk of HALT was associated with higher baseline Lp(a) (the multivariable adjusted odds ratio [OR] for every 10 mg/dL change was 1.18 [95% CI, 1.09–1.29]) and hs‐CRP level (the multivariable adjusted OR for every 1 mg/L change was 1.08 [95% CI, 1.00–1.27]). Compared with individuals out of the top 25th percentile for both Lp(a) and hs‐CRP, the multivariable adjusted OR for HALT was 4.74 (95% CI, 1.65–14.37) for the top 25th percentile. This result remained consistent after excluding patients receiving anticoagulant therapy.ConclusionsThe top 25th percentile of Lp(a) level (≥40 mg/dL) combined with the top 25th percentile of hs‐CRP level (≥3.5 mg/L) conferred a 4.74‐fold risk of HALT.

The safety and effectiveness of transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) have been well established, and latest guideline‐recommended TAVR is suitable for older patients (>75 years of age) or those who are at high risk for surgery. 1 , 2 As more evidence has emerged, it seems that individuals with a low risk for surgical treatment could also benefit from TAVR. The Evolut Low‐Risk trial 3 and PARTNER 3 (Placement of Aortic Transcatheter Valves) trial 4 indicated that TAVR is noninferior to surgery for adverse outcomes, and more low‐risk patients with severe aortic stenosis (AS) underwent a TAVR procedure. Therefore, the durability of a prosthesis valve has gradually attracted people's attention. 5

Hypoattenuated leaflet thickening (HALT) detected noninvasively by 4‐dimensional computed tomography (CT) showed a high degree of concordance with transesophageal echocardiography findings and is commonly recognized as a hallmark of subclinical leaflet thrombosis. 6 HALT, which is reported to have an incidence of 10% to 20% in previous studies, 7 , 8 has aroused considerable interest due to the effectiveness of pharmacological intervention with anticoagulation. 9 Research also showed that HALT is not only related to biological valve dysfunction 10 or symptomatic hemodynamic deterioration 11 but also related to adverse clinical outcomes. 12 , 13 Therefore, it is vital to identify individuals with a high risk for HALT after a TAVR procedure.

Although the biological process and mechanism is still unclear, much effort has been made to elucidate the risk factors for HALT. We noticed that several articles have revealed the relationship between postprocedure prosthesis valve morphology and HALT, 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 whereas few of them pay attention to the role of biochemical indicators. Additional evidence has demonstrated that elevated Lp(a) (lipoprotein[a]) levels are associated with adverse clinical outcomes among patients with AS, 17 , 18 whereas it remains unclear whether patients with AS with elevated Lp(a) levels have a higher risk of secondary complications after undergoing valve replacement surgery. Moreover, the association between inflammation and thrombosis was also well clarified, and the activation of neutrophils and the release of various inflammatory mediators can further regulate various aspects of the coagulation system activation, including platelet activation, fibrin aggregation, and endothelial cell damage, 19 and hs‐CRP (high‐sensitivity C‐reactive protein) seemed to modulate Lp(a)‐associated cardiovascular risk. 20 Whether biochemical indicators such as Lp(a) and hs‐CRP also exert a thrombotic effect on patients undergoing TAVR is not clear. Therefore, we conducted this analysis to test the hypothesis that HALT is associated with an increased level of Lp(a) or hs‐CRP.

METHODS

Patient Population and Study Design

We started a retrospective study in which all patients at our hospital (Fuwai Hospital, Beijing, China) underwent an ECG‐gated, contrast‐enhanced cardiac CT within 12 months after TAVR to evaluate the frame morphology. This project was approved by the institutional review board central committee at Fuwai Hospital, Beijing, China. All patients provided informed consent, with their data being protected at all times (approval number 2020‐1290). From May 1, 2014 to September 30, 2022, patients with severe, symptomatic AS, regardless of morphology or surgical access, were included. A total of 385 consecutive patients who underwent TAVR in accordance with guidelines and after the heart team's evaluation were included. 1 , 2 A total of 3 independently trained members analyzed the CT data after TAVR, and only when all 3 independently determined it as HALT was it assigned to the HALT group. Disagreements were resolved in a panel discussion of the 3 members when there was controversy. Study exclusion criteria were valve‐in‐valve procedure (n=36), inadequate CT image quality for assessment (n=38), and procedure failure (n=2). Emergency TAVR (n=2) was also excluded due to inadequate preprocedure assessment and stress state. Finally, 307 patients were included in the analysis (Figure 1).

CT Image Acquisition and Analysis

The CT data included in our study were all patients' first postoperative CT scans. Siemens SOMATOM Definition Flash and SOMATOM Force dual‐source photon CT scanners were used for post‐TAVR patient imaging evaluation, and iopromide (iodine concentration 370 mg/mL) was selected as the contrast agent. A retrospective ECG‐gating sequence was applied. Tube voltage was settled at 120 to 140 kV, and tube current was adjusted according to Care 4D and an in‐house algorithm. Tube rotating speed was 0.28 s/r. ECG milliampere modulation technology was used during exposure, and the high‐dose exposure area was a 35% to 75% R‐R interval. The exposure dose of other exposure areas was set to 20% of the high‐dose area. After the scan was completed, the machine automatically gave the optimal systolic period and optimal diastolic period based on coronary artery movement, which was used to obtain coronary artery information. Manual reconstruction was needed when coronary artery observation was not ideal.

Patient Serum Samples Acquisition and Analysis

All patients' blood samples were collected 1 to 3 days before the TAVR procedure and were transported to the laboratory at Fuwai Hospital within 3 hours for analysis. All biochemical indicators were analyzed using a fully automated biochemical analyzer (Hitachi Labospect 008AS). The reagent kit for Lp(a) determination was manufactured by SHIMA Laboratories, whereas the reagent kit for hs‐CRP determination was produced by Aidian Oy. Although the patients included in the study spanned a wide time range, the laboratory at Fuwai Hospital has not changed its equipment or reagent kits, and regularly calibrated its experimental facilities, ensuring the comparability of all patients' biochemical indicators.

Definition of HALT

All of the CT data analyses were conducted using 3mensio software (Pie Medical Imaging's portfolio, version 10.3 SP2) independently by 3 expert CT radiologists blinded to baseline data. The prosthesis valve leaflets were carefully assessed throughout the diastolic period. HALT was defined as visually identified increased leaflet thickness with typical meniscal appearance and at least 2 different multiplanar reformation projections. 6 , 14

Briefly, we identified the centerline of the prothesis valve frame and straightened the image. Then, based on the 3 lowest points at the inflow level of the valve frame, we determined the basal plane of the prothesis valve frame and slid it upward to observe and evaluate HALT in 2 different axial views (shown in Figure S1). When discrepancies in HALT determination arose, group discussions were conducted, and another senior researcher made the final decision on the imaging results.

Definition of Prosthesis Valve Expansion and Ellipse Index

For both balloon‐expanded valves and self‐expanded valves (SEV), measurements of the external stent‐frame area and major and minor diameter were performed at the waist. Expansion was calculated as follows: (bioprosthesis valve measured area at the waist/nominal bioprosthesis valve area)×100%. Ellipse index was calculated as follows: (bioprosthesis valve measured major diameter at the waist/bioprosthesis valve measured at minor diameter at the waist)×100%. The nominal bioprosthesis valve area was provided by the valve company according to the size used in the patients.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables are expressed as mean±SD or median (25th and 75th percentiles), and categorical variables are presented as percentages. Differences in baseline characteristics in patients with and without HALT were assessed using the χ2 test or Fisher exact test for categorical variables and ANOVA or the Wilcoxon rank sum test for continuous variables. Independent factors associated with HALT were identified using multivariable logistic regression analysis. To verify the impact of other lipids and inflammation indicators on the conclusion, low‐density lipoprotein, white blood cell count, and procalcitonin level were also included in the final model. Moreover, other important clinical or prosthesis characteristics, such as age, sex, medication, valve type, expansion, and ellipse index, were also included. We also made subgroup analysis in the antiplatelet or anticoagulant group, bicuspid or tricuspid group, and SEV or balloon‐expanded valve group to discover whether the role of Lp(a) and hs‐CRP showed consistent results.

Moreover, restricted cubic splines with 3 knots based on Akaike information criterion were conducted to present the impact of Lp(a) and hs‐CRP on HALT on continuous scales, and the reference value was the 25th percentile (2 mg/dL for Lp(a) and 0.1 mg/L for hs‐CRP). Furthermore, the level of plasma Lp(a) and hs‐CRP were divided into 3 parts. Odds ratios (ORs) for HALT of the top 25th percentile and 25th to 74th percentile were evaluated with reference to the bottom 25th percentile population. Finally, we combined the top 25th percentile level of Lp(a) and hs‐CRP to assess a potential synergistic effect on HALT.

ORs were presented with their 95% CIs. All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.2.2, and a 2‐tailed P<0.05 was considered statistically significant. All P values were to be considered exploratory or hypothesis generating in nature. The data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

RESULTS

After excluding 78 patients, a total of 307 consecutive patients with AS who underwent TAVR at Fuwai Hospital were analyzed. The mean age of the cohort was 74.37±7.43 years, 54.4% were men, and the body mass index was 23.94±4.47. In addition, the proportion of patients with atrial fibrillation was 14.3%, and some of them received oral anticoagulants (61.4%). As to the TAVR procedure, bicuspid patients with AS accounted for 28.3% of the cohort, and an SEV was implanted in 85% of patients.

A postprocedural 4‐dimensional CT scan was performed within the 12‐month follow‐up at a median of 157 days (interquartile range, 35–246), and 111 (36.2%) individuals were identified as having HALT. These incidences were comparable for SEVs and balloon‐expanded valves (36.4% versus 34.8%, P=0.965), and bicuspid and tricuspid (29.9% versus 38.6%, P=0.191) (Table 1 and Figure S1). Moreover, baseline clinical characteristics, imaging, biochemical indicators, TAVR procedure characteristics, and medication at discharge were comparable between patients with and without HALT, except for the level of Lp(a) and hs‐CRP. Risk Factors for HALT

As shown in Table 2, Lp(a) (OR, 1.18 for every 10 mg/dL increase [95% CI, 1.09–1.29]; P=0.010) and hs‐CRP (OR, 1.08 for every 1 mg/L increase [95% CI, 1.00–1.17]; P=0.049) were still strongly associated with the risk for HALT after multivariable adjusted analysis, and similar results were observed in the population that received antiplatelet therapy at discharge, as shown in Table S1. For every 10 mg/dL increase in Lp(a), the risk of HALT increased by 15% (95% CI, 1.06–1.26; P=0.001), whereas for every 1 mg/L increase in hs‐CRP, the risk of HALT increased by 8% (95% CI, 1.00–1.17; P=0.050). Considering the impact of other lipid or inflammation indicators on the conclusion, low‐density lipoprotein, white blood cell count, and procalcitonin were included in the final model, along with other important variables such as age, sex, medication, valve type, prosthesis expansion, and ellipse index. Figure 2 indicates that multivariable‐adjusted OR for HALT increased with both higher Lp(a) and hs‐CRP levels, and consistent results were found on continuous scales.

Among different subgroups, patients with higher levels of Lp(a) and hs‐CRP had a higher incidence for HALT (Figure S2). Moreover, a higher level of hs‐CRP was associated with increased risk for HALT in the balloon‐expanded valves group and tricuspid group, and a higher level of Lp(a) was associated with increased risk for HALT in the SEV group. Possibly due to the small size of these subgroups, this trend did not occur in the anticoagulants group and bicuspid group (Table S1).

Compared with individuals with Lp(a) levels in the first to 24th percentiles (≤8 mg/dL), the multivariable adjusted OR for HALT was 0.90 (95% CI, 0.44–1.97) for individuals in the 25th to 74th percentiles (95% CI, 8–40 mg/dL) and 1.86 (95% CI, 0.83–4.22) for individuals in the 75th to 100th percentiles (>40 mg/dL; Figure 3). Compared with individuals with hs‐CRP levels in the first to 24th percentiles (≤0.5 mg/L), the multivariable‐adjusted OR for HALT was 0.98 (95% CI, 0.47–2.09) for individuals in the 25th to 74th percentiles (95% CI, 0.5–3.5 mg/L) and 3.06 (95% CI, 1.34–7.20) for individuals in the 75th to 100th percentiles (>3.5 mg/L; Figure 3).

Combination of Lp(a) and hs‐CRP

Compared with individuals in the first to 74th percentiles for both Lp(a) (≤40 mg/dL) and hs‐CRP (≤3.5 mg/L) levels, the multivariable adjusted OR for HALT increased with both higher Lp(a) levels and higher hs‐CRP (Figure 4). With the same reference group, multivariable‐adjusted ORs for HALT were 4.74 (95% CI, 1.65–14.37) for individuals with both Lp(a) levels (>40 mg/dL) and hs‐CRP levels (>3.5 mg/L) in the 75th to 100th percentiles (8.1% of all individuals). For individuals with Lp(a) in the first to 74th percentiles and hs‐CRP in the 75th to 100th percentiles (16.9% of all individuals), the adjusted OR was 2.72 (95% CI, 1.18–6.31), and for those with Lp(a) in the 75th to 100th percentiles and hs‐CRP in the first to 75th percentiles (17.9% of all individuals), the adjusted OR was 2.85 (95% CI, 1.40–5.85). In an analysis excluding individuals who received anticoagulants (detailed baseline clinical characteristic can be found in Table S2), the results were similar. The adjusted OR for HALT for individuals with both Lp(a) levels and hs‐CRP levels in the 75th to 100th percentiles was 5.13 (95% CI, 1.74–16.23; Figure S3) (7.1% of all individuals).

DISCUSSION

In our population that underwent TAVR, individuals with high Lp(a) and hs‐CRP levels showed a relationship with the risk of HALT, and a combination of the top 25th percentile Lp(a) and hs‐CRP levels conferred a 4.74‐fold risk of HALT (as shown in Figure 5). These findings are novel.

Combined elevated lipoprotein(a) and hs‐CRP levels increased the risk of HALT.

Our study reported a relatively high incidence of HALT (36.2%), compared with the incidence rate of 10% to 20% described in previous studies. 7 , 8 , 14 , 21 The following are possible reasons accounting for this difference. First, we noticed that anticoagulant medication was more frequent in Fukui et al's study 14 (34%), Chakravarty et al's study 7 (25%), and Vollema et al's study 22 (35%). Although there is currently debate on post‐TAVR anticoagulation strategies, the 4‐dimensional CT analysis from the ATLANTIS (Anti‐Thrombotic Strategy to Lower All Cardiovascular and Neurologic Ischemic and Hemorrhagic Events After Trans‐Aortic Valve Implantation for Aortic Stenosis) trial 23 confirms that anticoagulants can reduce the incidence of HALT. In our study, only 8% of patients received anticoagulants at discharge, which to some extent explains why the incidence of HALT is relatively high in our cohort postoperatively. Second, previous studies reported a higher body mass index in patients with severe AS undergoing TAVR (29 in Fukui et al's study, 14 29 in Garcia et al's study, 12 and 31.5 in Khan et al's study 24 ), whereas in our cohort, patients had a lower body mass index (23.9). Jilaihawi et al 6 pointed out that there may be some benefit in increasing scanner acquisition voltage to 140 kV, particularly in the presence of a large body habitus, although this may be at the expense of contrast delineation of hypoattenuation. Although previous studies have rarely reported the voltage used for CT assessments after TAVR, this potential population difference could explain why the incidence of HALT is higher in our study. Moreover, most of these studies recognized HALT on CT at 30 days to identify a risk factor for acute thrombus, 25 which was mainly caused by prosthesis characteristic, 14 , 26 whereas we enrolled patients with CT available within 12 months after TAVR to better detect the impact of serum indicators on HALT. As reported in the PARTNER 3 CT subgroup 8 and Evolut Low‐Risk substudy 21 analysis, HALT incidence at 1 year was 28% and 30.9%, respectively, which indicated time might be a driver for the formation of HALT.

Lp(a) is a low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol‐like particle bound to apolipoprotein(a), which shares many structural similarities with plasminogen, and research has reported that it could cause interference with the physiological fibrinolysis process and therefore contribute to a thrombotic process. 27 , 28 Considerable evidence has shown that Lp(a) is associated with atherosclerosis 18 and valve disease, 29 whereas no study assessed the impact of Lp(a) level on HALT risk following TAVR. We found that the risk of HALT increased with Lp(a) changed every 10 mg/dL (OR, 1.18), and the top 25th percentile Lp(a) level seemed associated with a higher risk (presented a trend with an OR of 1.86). The role of inflammation in thrombus formation has been well established, 30 , 31 but no evidence of infective endocarditis was found in our cohort. The white blood cell count and procalcitonin level were comparable between the HALT group and the no‐HALT group, and both covariates were included in the final multivariable model. However, to our surprise, hs‐CRP seemed to be independently related to HALT, and a dose effect was detected.

Next, we combined these 2 indicators. As we expected, the OR for HALT was 4.74 (95% CI, 1.65–14.37) for individuals with both Lp(a) and hs‐CRP levels in the 75th to 100th percentiles, which indicated a synergistic effect on the risk for HALT. The conclusion remains consistent after the exclusion of individuals receiving anticoagulants. Our novel results have the following important implications. First, the risk factors attributed to prosthesis morphology after TAVR related to the occurrence of HALT at 30 days have been well clarified, whereas the underlying mechanism for organized thrombosis within a long follow‐up is less known. Our study provides a risk stratification of HALT incidence in patients after TAVR, and it seemed that individuals with a high level of serum Lp(a) and hs‐CRP (threshold of 40 mg/dL and 3.5 mg/L in our population, respectively) might benefit from anticoagulants. Second, Lp(a) as a marker of residual cardiovascular risk has been well recognized. Recent research involving target therapy for Lp(a) showed inspiring results on reducing adverse clinical outcomes. The post hoc analysis from the ODYSSEY (Evaluation of Cardiovascular Outcomes After an Acute Coronary Syndrome During Treatment With Alirocumab) study outcomes demonstrated that even with optimized statin therapy, patients with ACS with elevated Lp(a) can still benefit from PCSK9 (proprotein convertase subtilisin/kexin type 9) inhibitors, and a 1 mg/dL reduction in Lp(a) with alirocumab was associated with a hazard ratio of 0.994 (95% CI, 0.990–0.999; P=0.0081). 32 , 33 Additionally, it is encouraging that in recent years, new pharmacologic therapies 34 , 35 targeting the reduction of Lp(a) have emerged, enabling convenient management of Lp(a) levels in patients at risk for cardiovascular disease and aortic stenosis. Therefore, whether intensified therapy targeting Lp(a) can lead to a reduction in HALT events deserves more attention. Based on ATLANTIS 23 and ADAPT‐TAVR (Anticoagulation Versus Dual Antiplatelet Therapy for Prevention of Leaflet Thrombosis and Cerebral Embolizaiotn After Transcatheter Aortic Valve Replacement) trials, 36 it seemed that simplified antithrombotic treatment is suitable for patients undergoing TAVR. Although anticoagulants could reduce prosthesis thrombosis, 23 increasing concern for a threatening bleeding event limited its use among individuals without having an established indication for anticoagulation. Single antiplatelet add target therapy for Lp(a) might reduce prosthesis thrombosis without extra bleeding risk. Third, colchicine also showed safety and effectiveness for the treatment of cardiovascular disease, 37 which seemed associated with a reduction of hs‐CRP levels. 38 Trying to prevent HALT with colchicine is worth considering. Furthermore, Small et al 39 revealed the significant roles of Lp(a) and hs‐CRP in the primary and secondary prevention of patients with ASCVD, and our study further supplements this by highlighting the potential importance of Lp(a) and hs‐CRP in the lifelong management of patients with AS following TAVR.

Although univariable and multivariable logistic analysis did not show correlation among prosthetic valve type, anticoagulants, the bicuspid aortic valve, and HALT, we still conducted subgroup analysis to assess whether a consistent conclusion could be observed. Possibly due to a relatively small number of enrolled patients, some subgroups only show trends, and more large‐sample data are needed in the future to verify our conclusions.

Limitations

First, this is a single‐center, retrospective study, and potential racial disparities limit the conclusion's applicability to other populations. Second, prosthesis morphology might still exert an impact on HALT formation at long‐term follow‐up, and some data related to prosthesis morphology included in our study might neglect potential morphology risk factors. Third, although our center is already the largest TAVR center in China, the number of patients enrolled is still relatively small. The top 25th percentile‐level patients with Lp(a) and hs‐CRP in our cohort is only 25 (accounting for 8.8% in the cohort), the calculated confidence intervals are wide, which may affect the accuracy of the results to some extent. Larger‐scale studies are warranted in the future to verify this finding. Nevertheless, our results provide a new insight into the novel mechanism for HALT. The role of Lp(a) in aortic valve stenosis has been widely described, but now our findings identified that Lp(a) still plays a role after patients undergo TAVR. We think this study is a great addition to the current field and expands upon the available knowledge, and can change medication therapy after TAVR in the future.

CONCLUSIONS

The incidence of HALT within 6 months in our cohort is 36.2%. The risk for HALT increased with higher Lp(a) and hs‐CRP levels. The top 25th percentiles of Lp(a) level (≥40 mg/dL) combined with the top 25th percentile of hs‐CRP level (≥3.5 mg/L) conferred a 4.74‐fold risk of HALT. These results remain in the population excluding individuals receiving anticoagulants.
