Introduction

Suppose that
S
is a compact surface, possibly with boundary.
We define
\MCG(S)
, the
mapping class group
of
S
, to be the group of isotopy classes of homeomorphisms of
S
.
Since this group is finitely generated
[
10
]
one may ask for a solution to its
word problem
:
an algorithm that, given a word of length
n
in the generators and their inverses, decides whether the resulting mapping class is trivial.

Our main result is the following.

Theorem 1.1
.

There is an algorithm that solves the word problem for
\MCG(S)
in
O(n\log^{3}(n))
time.
Furthermore, the implied constants are bounded by a fixed polynomial in
|\chi(S)|
.

This answers a question of Farb
[
15
, Question 3.1]
;
it also gives the best solution to date to Thurston’s Problem 20
[
45
, Page 380]
.
We prove
Theorem

1.1
using divide-and-conquer, ideas of Dynnikov
[
13
]
, and the following.

Theorem 1.2
.

There is an algorithm to compute the geometric intersection number of two curves (given in
\Delta
–coordinates) in
O(n\log^{2}(n))
time.
Furthermore, the implied constants are bounded by a fixed polynomial in
|\chi(S)|
.

Our proof of
Theorem

1.2
is inspired by the
half-GCD algorithm

[
29
]
;
this uses divide-and-conquer to accelerate the euclidean algorithm.
As a special case of
Theorem

1.2
, we also obtain the best solution to date for a question (posed as a “remarque”) of
[
17
, Exposé 4, page 66]
.

Theorem 1.3
.

There is an algorithm for
curve shortening
in
S
which runs in
O(n\log^{2}(n))
time.
Furthermore, the implied constants are bounded by a fixed polynomial in
|\chi(S)|
. ∎

The final divide-and-conquer, giving the last factor of
\log(n)
, comes from the recent improvement in large integer multiplication
[
23
, Theorem 1.1]
.
Note, however, that our paper uses integer multiplication as a “black box”.
Swapping in a different algorithm for integer multiplication, say running in time
M(n)
, changes the running time in
Theorem

1.1
to
O(M(n)\log^{2}(n))
and in Theorems

1.2
and
1.3
to
O(M(n)\log(n))
.

1.4
Reduction to a special case

The general case of
Theorem

1.1
reduces, in linear time, to the following special case:

•

The surface
S
is connected and oriented,

•

the surface
S
has
\chi(S)\leq-2
,

•

the mapping class
f
preserves orientation, and

•

the mapping class
f
preserves the boundary of
S
, componentwise.

We henceforth assume that
S=S_{g,b}
: the surface of genus
g
with
b
boundary components.
We use the notation
\Mod(S)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\MCG^{+}_{\partial}(S)
for the group of such mapping classes.
It is a theorem from Dehn’s Breslau lectures
[
11
]
(see also
[
10
,
27
,
26
]
) that
\Mod(S)
is finitely generated by Dehn twists.
Fix
F
, a finite generating set of
\Mod(S)
.

1.5
Corollaries

Remark 1.6
.

There is another common variant of the mapping class group,
\MCG^{+}(S,\partial S)
, where homeomorphisms and isotopies fix
\partial S
pointwise.
The word problem in this case is reduced to the previous one by attaching pairs of pants to all boundary components of
S
.
∎

Remark 1.7
.

Thus for any fixed value of
r
we obtain an
O(n\log^{3}(n))
algorithm to solve the word problem in
B_{r}
:
the
braid group
on
r
strands.
This answers Problem B13 on the list maintained by Shpilrain
[
39
]
.
∎

Remark 1.8
.

The order of torsion in
\MCG(S)
is bounded, solely in terms of the topology of
S
;
this follows from the classification of hyperbolic orbifolds and a result of Nielsen
[
32
, page 24, Staz]
.
Thus we have an
O(n\log^{3}(n))
algorithm to decide whether a given mapping class has finite order.
∎

In a pair of papers

A naïve analysis shows that his second algorithm is quadratic time.

Parts of Dehn’s work were recovered, and parts greatly extended,

]

We refer to Erickson–Nayyeri

2.1
Matrices and further reduction

Suppose that
d
is a non-negative integer.
We treat
\GL(d,\mathbb{Z})
as both a motivating example and as a useful tool.
Fast integer multiplication gives fast matrix multiplication, as follows.

Corollary 2.2
.

Suppose that
A
and
B
are matrices in
\GL(d,\mathbb{Z})
with
||A||,||B||\leq n
.
Then we can compute the product
A\cdot B
in
O(n\log(n))
time.
Furthermore, the implied constants are bounded by a fixed polynomial in
d
. ∎

From
[
25
]
, and
[

, Proposition 2]
, we have the following.

Proposition 2.3
.

Suppose that
(E_{n},\ldots,E_{2},E_{1})
is a list of elementary matrices in
\GL(d,\mathbb{Z})
.
Then we can compute their product in
O(n\log^{2}(n))
time.
Furthermore, the implied constants are bounded by a fixed polynomial in
d
.

Proof.

We split the list
(E_{i})
in half and recurse.
The two subproducts have complexity at most
O(n)
, by induction.
Thus we can compute the final product in time
O(n\log(n))
by
Corollary

2.2
.

Let
t(n)
bound the time needed to compute the product of
n
elementary matrices.
We deduce that

t(n)\leq 2t(n/2)+O(n\log(n))

Thus

by the master theorem
[

, Theorem 4.1]
.
∎

Corollary 2.4
.

The word problem for
\GL(d,\mathbb{Z})
, as generated by elementary matrices, can be solved in
O(n\log^{2}(n))
time.
Furthermore, the implied constants are bounded by a fixed polynomial in
d
.
∎

Recall that

is our fixed finite generating set for

Corollary 2.5
.

Suppose that

is a list of generators and inverses of generators.

Let

As usual and when needed, we will blur the distinction between a multi-curve and its isotopy class.
For

We use

In our construction of pants decompositions, dual curves, and double duals we closely follow

Fix a

Note that

The curves

4.1
Index

Suppose that
Q
is a

:
a compact riemannian surface with piecewise smooth boundary (perhaps empty).
We assume that at each non-smooth point of
\partial Q
the two adjacent arcs of the boundary make an interior angle of either zero or 90 degrees.
We call the former points
(outward) cusps
and the latter
(outward) corners
.
We define the
index
of

following
[
31
, page 57]

\ind(Q)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\chi(Q)-\frac{1}{2}(\textrm{number of cusps in $\partial Q$})-\frac{1}{4}(\textrm{number of corners in $\partial Q$})

The second and third terms give a combinatorial version of the contribution of geodesic curvature in the Gauss–Bonnet formula.
Note that if
Q=R\sqcup R^{\prime}
, then
\ind(Q)=\ind(R)+\ind(R^{\prime})
.

With
Q
a region as above, suppose now that
\alpha
is a properly embedded simple arc, or closed curve, in

.
If
\alpha
is an arc, then we suppose that its endpoints

•

avoid the cusps and corners of
\partial R

and

•

are tangent or perpendicular to
\partial R
.

In a small abuse of notation, we define
\overline{Q-\alpha}
to be the completion (in the induced path metric) of
Q-\alpha
.
We can now state the “additivity” property of the index
[
31
, pages 57–58]
.

Lemma 4.2
.

With
Q
and
\alpha
as above,
\ind(\,\overline{Q-\alpha}\,)=\ind(Q)
. ∎

is a clean pair of weighted train tracks.
Suppose that
\boldsymbol{\tau}^{\prime}=(\sigma^{\prime},\mu^{\prime},\tau^{\prime},\nu^{\prime})\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\move^{k}(\boldsymbol{\tau})
for some

Then

|\sigma^{\prime}\pitchfork\tau^{\prime}|-|\sigma\pitchfork\tau|\leq 13\mathcal{B}^{3},

which, in particular, is independent of

.

Proof.

A new crossing point between
\sigma^{\prime}
and
\tau^{\prime}
is only created when a separation or split of type 2, 4, 5, 7, 8c, 10c or 11c is performed.
However, all of these moves except a split of type 7 reduce
\#\boldsymbol{\tau}
by at least the number of crossing points that they introduce.
Thus the total number of crossing points introduced by separations or splits of type 2, 4, 5, 8c, 10c or 11c is at most
3\mathcal{B}
.

To bound the number of crossing points introduced by splits of type 7, suppose that
\#\boldsymbol{\tau}^{\prime}=\#\boldsymbol{\tau}
.
Now if
|\sigma^{\prime}\pitchfork\tau^{\prime}|-|\sigma\pitchfork\tau|>4\mathcal{B}^{2}
then there a branch-end of
\sigma
and a branch-end of
\tau
which perform least five splits of type 7 together.
Without loss of generality, we may assume that the
\sigma
branch-end is part of a left
\sigma
–switch and
\tau
branch-end is part of a right
\tau
–switch.

Noting that we are only performing tightness-preserving moves, since this pair of branch-ends perform a split of type 7 together they must connect into the same smooth component of
\partial(S-n(\sigma\cap\tau))
.
Furthermore, since they perform at least two splits of type 7 together this component must actually be a circle.
Finally, since they perform at least five splits of type 7
this component, when considered as a train cycle
c
in
\sigma\cap\tau
, cannot meet any shared switches, right
\sigma
–switches, or left
\tau
–switches.
However this means that
c
is an incompatibly combed train cycle.
Thus, if any of the branches along it were ever considered for splitting then the move would separate the entire cycle instead.
This contradicts the assumption that
\#\boldsymbol{\tau}^{\prime}=\#\boldsymbol{\tau}
.

Hence a sequence of tightness-preserving moves contains at most
4\mathcal{B}^{2}
splits of type 7.
However any sequence of moves contains at most
3\mathcal{B}
sub-sequences of tightness-preserving moves.
Therefore the number of crossing points introduced by splits of type 7 is at most
12\mathcal{B}^{3}
.

Hence
|\sigma^{\prime}\pitchfork\tau^{\prime}|-|\sigma\pitchfork\tau|\leq 12\mathcal{B}^{3}+3\mathcal{B}\leq 13\mathcal{B}^{3}
as required.
∎

Since we start with a clean pair of weighted standard train tracks and these have at most
