Nurses' Experiences Caring for People Presenting to the Emergency Department With Mental Health Concerns
Authors: Caitlin Kent, Catherine Stephen, Elizabeth Halcomb
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

Nurses were recruited if they were employed at a single tertiary Emergency Department in the Northern Territory, Australia. Introduction

Globally, the prevalence of poor mental health in the community is increasing, affecting the demand of health services (World Health Organisation 2025). In Australia, one in five people experiences a mental health condition (Australian Bureau of Statistics 2023), a broad term encompassing both diagnosable mental illness and other forms of psychological distress. While in America, approximately 57.8 million adults (22.8%) experience a diagnosable mental illness (National Alliance on Mental Health 2023). Beyond those diagnosed with specific mental illnesses, many other people experience episodes of poor mental health, usually in response to life events or personal crises that prompt engagement with health services.

Mental illness is often characterised by periods of exacerbation and remission with varying frequency (Carey and Cole 2024). During exacerbations, when individuals are experiencing a mental health crisis, challenges accessing community‐based services mean emergency departments (EDs) often are the first point of contact with the health system (Wand et al. 2021; Sacre et al. 2022). However, EDs are primarily designed to respond to acute physical conditions and are often poorly suited to address the complex needs of people in psychological distress (Navas et al. 2022). ED staff face constant challenges in finding safe care pathways for patients with mental illness or episodes of poor mental health due to increasing demand for inpatient beds and a lack of appropriate community support services (Duggan et al. 2020). Delays in admission to psychiatric services can result in prolonged waiting times, stigma, and inconsistent quality of care for patients presenting with mental health issues (Peart et al. 2023). The rising prevalence of mental illness at a population level translates into increasing mental health‐related presentations to EDs. This places an increasing burden on ED services. The Australasian College for Emergency Medicine (2019) has identified that the strain on EDs due to mental health care is unsustainable. This is supported by national data indicating that in 2023–2024 there were approximately 310,200 mental health‐related presentations to Australian public hospital EDs, representing an increase of approximately 22% since 2014–2015 (Australian Institute of Health and Welfare 2025).

Background

People with mental health issues often require treatment from specialist clinicians and/or inpatient services. Therefore, EDs frequently serve only as a transitional point to direct patients to this specialist care. Despite this intention, people with mental health issues experience disproportionately longer ED stays (120%) compared to people with other presentations (Chisholm et al. 2025). Consequently, ED nurses play a critical role in supporting people presenting with mental health issues by ensuring efficient assessment, high‐quality care, and timely referral to mental health services. However, ED nurses frequently encounter significant challenges when attempting to provide safe and effective mental health care within this setting (Peart et al. 2023).

The ED environment is characterised by high patient turnover, requiring staff to respond to constant medical demands and provide care without established patient‐provider relationships (Isbell et al. 2025). This high‐pressure, time‐limited setting forces ED nurses to have brief interactions with patients and strains staff capacity to meet the emotional needs of people with mental health issues (García‐Carpintero Blas et al. 2023). These constraints can create gaps between patient expectations of care and the care that ED nurses can provide. Previous research indicates that people with mental health issues want nurses to understand their care needs, comprehend the care process in the ED, and demonstrate effective skills (Cullen et al. 2019). Yet while most ED nurses receive extensive training in acute and critical care, few have specialised mental health training (García‐Carpintero Blas et al. 2023). If ED nurses feel unprepared, it can affect their confidence and willingness to engage in mental health care (Chou and Tseng 2020), ultimately impacting patient outcomes.

As mental health‐related presentations to EDs continue to increase (Carey and Cole 2024), the workload and emotional demands placed on ED nurses escalate, within an already stressful environment. This escalation has been associated with higher rates of dissatisfaction with care and increased exposure to challenging behaviours, including agitation, aggression, and violence (Landa‐Ramirez et al. 2024). Such experiences can contribute to the stigmatisation of people living with mental health issues and lead to physical and emotional fatigue among nurses (Chou and Tseng 2020). Over time, cumulative stress and burnout have emerged as key contributors to workforce retention across ED nursing, highlighting the broader impact of addressing these issues (Chernoff et al. 2019). Notably, Chernoff et al. (2019) reported burnout rates of up to 78% among ED nurses, surpassing those of other health care professionals in non‐ED settings.

Given these pressures, understanding ED nurses' perspectives on providing mental health care is essential, not only to improve patient outcomes but also to support nurses' well‐being and workforce sustainability. Although substantial literature supports the challenges in ED mental health care from the patient's perspective (Perry et al. 2020), there is limited research from the viewpoint of ED nurses, despite their central role in care delivery (Derblom et al. 2022; Kent and Halcomb 2025). In their recent review, Kent and Halcomb (2025) found that ED nurses faced a range of challenges in providing care for people presenting with mental health issues, although the paucity of literature necessitated further investigation of these challenges.

The Study

Aim

This study aimed to explore the experiences of ED nurses when caring for people with mental health issues. For the purposes of this study, mental health issues were defined broadly to include any presentations that involved mental health as a contributing diagnosis. Gaining insight into these experiences will inform strategies to enhance staff support, reduce burnout, and promote ED environments that are better equipped to respond to the complex needs of patients experiencing mental health distress.

Methods

Study Design

This study used a qualitative descriptive approach to guide data collection and analysis. This approach was chosen as it is pragmatic and practice‐focussed, valuing participants' subjective experiences and meanings. This approach allows participants to tell their stories in their own words and researchers to remain closely connected to the data, ensuring an accurate reflection of the participants' authentic voices (Doyle et al. 2020).

Setting and Recruitment

The study was undertaken in a 45‐bed tertiary ED in the Northern Territory, Australia. This ED has an embedded mental health liaison team that assesses patients referred by ED doctors or known to the service. After initial medical clearance by ED doctors, a mental health team assumes responsibility for patient care. Patients who are legally detained due to their mental health receive one‐to‐one monitoring from dedicated external security staff, while hospital security responds to events, such as violence or aggression, requiring a security presence.

A convenience sample of participants was recruited over a 12‐week period. Posters displayed in the workplace invited ED nurses to contact the researcher directly, while snowball referrals occurred through professional networks, managers and previous participants, with interested nurses initiating contact. Recruitment was pragmatic and sought to include nurses across a range of experience levels. Potential participants were provided with study information and, if they consented to participate, a mutually convenient interview time was scheduled. All potential participants agreed to participate, and no participants refused or ceased the interview.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

Participants were recruited if they were either a diploma‐prepared enrolled nurse or a baccalaureate‐prepared (or equivalent) registered nurse who had worked in an ED for more than 6 months. This time period was pragmatically chosen to ensure that participants had some experience in both the ED environment and the care of patients presenting to the ED with mental health issues, while remaining inclusive of early‐career nurses. Specialist mental health nurses were excluded due to their expertise, which would have changed the way in which they experienced providing care to patients presenting with mental health issues.

Data Collection

Participants were individually interviewed face‐to‐face using a semi‐structured approach. One interview was undertaken with each participant to minimise burden. An interview schedule was developed based on a literature review (Kent and Halcomb 2026) and expert input. Questions asked about participants' experiences in caring for ED patients with mental health issues, including facilitators and barriers to providing care. Open‐ended questions were used to provoke meaningful discussion, for example, “What has been your experience in managing mental health patients in ED?” and “What could be done differently to improve mental health care in the ED?” The interview guide was pilot tested and minor changes were made to the wording of some questions and the interviewer's technique. While basic demographic data, namely age, gender, and years of experience, were collected, these data were kept to a minimum to avoid potential participant identification.

All interviews were conducted in a private room off‐site from the ED to maintain confidentiality. Interviews were conducted by the first author (C.K.), a female registered nurse with clinical experience in ED nursing, who was undertaking a research training course. This professional background informed the researcher's understanding of the emergency care context and supported rapport with participants during data collection and interpretation. However, this also meant that the first author (C.K.) was known to some participants in the clinical setting. To minimise potential bias, strategies were implemented including passive recruitment, emphasising voluntary participation and confidentiality, maintaining reflexive awareness of how professional familiarity may have influenced responses, and working closely with research supervisors (C.S. and E.H.) throughout the interpretation of results. Interviews were audio‐recorded and transcribed verbatim by a professional transcription company. The interviewer kept field notes of their thoughts around each interview to aid data analysis by providing reflexive memos and provide an audit trail. Interviews ranged in duration from 25 to 43 min (Mean 34 min).

Interviews were undertaken until it was felt that no new information was being revealed. Sample size was guided by the concept of information power (Malterud et al. 2016), taking into consideration the study aim, sample specificity, and the quality and richness of the data. Following interview 8, the research team discussed the potential for the data holding information power and conducted a further 2 interviews to confirm that no new concepts were emerging and that themes remained stable. After 10 interviews, it was confirmed the data held sufficient information power and was considered sufficiently rich and conceptually informative to address the research question (Malterud et al. 2016), and data collection ceased. Given the potentially sensitive nature of the topic, member checking was not used. However, peer debriefing was used to support credibility.

Data Analysis

Interviews were analysed using the reflexive thematic analysis approach advocated by Braun and Clarke (2022). Researchers initially read the transcripts multiple times to familiarise themselves with the data. Then, the first author (C.K.) extracted the main messages and phrases by identifying codes and used Microsoft Word to organise the data in a table with a column for the quotation and another for the relevant code. Microsoft Word was used, given its accessibility to all researchers and the time limitations of the project as part of a research training course. As the coded data were grouped, all researchers collaborated to discuss the initial themes. Field notes were used, where required, to provide insights into the researcher's initial reflections of particular interviews or comments. This was achieved by discussion between the first author (C.K.) and the research supervisors (C.S. and E.H.), two female nurse academics with expertise in qualitative research. Themes were then reviewed to ensure the collated information formed a coherent pattern representative of the data. Data were then categorised into final themes that were defined and named before being presented to create a written description of the findings.

Ethical Considerations

The Human Research Ethics Committee (HREC) of the Northern Territory (NT) Department of Health and Menzies School of Health Research (Approval no 2024‐4997), and the University of Wollongong HREC approved the study's conduct (Approval no 2025/0535). Although participants were advised they could stop the interview or withdraw from the study at any time, no one did. While no participant was identified as experiencing distress during the interviews, a distress plan was in place, with referrals to free counselling services made available if required. Pseudonyms are used in reporting for confidentiality.

Rigour and Reflexivity

Rigour was ensured using Lincoln and Guba's (1985) criteria for trustworthiness: credibility, transferability, dependability, and confirmability. All data were discussed at regular peer debriefing to ensure credibility, clarify the interpretation of meaning, and minimise the risk of bias. Dependability and confirmability were maintained by keeping field notes alongside audio recordings throughout data collection and analysis to capture coding iterations and theme maps, and to maintain an audit trail. Transferability is demonstrated through thick description within the findings, which allows the reader to understand the context.

It is acknowledged that the first author (C.K.), who conducted the interviews, held preconceived views and values shaped by prior experience as a registered nurse working in the participating ED. To enhance reflexivity and reduce potential bias, audio recordings of interviews were audited by the research supervisors (C.S. and E.H.). Additionally, the research supervisors (C.S. and E.H.) oversaw the data analysis to minimise any potential bias. This supported a balanced interpretation and credibility of findings.

Findings

Characteristics of Participants

Two (20%) of the 10 participants identified as male (Table 1), and the mean age was 33.5 years (range 24–48 years). While most participants (n = 7, 70%) had over 5 years nursing experience, only two participants (20%) had over 5 years' experience employed at the study site ED. Consistent with the concept of information power (Malterud et al. 2016), the sample was considered to have sufficient diversity to support the study aim and to generate rich, relevant insights into ED nurses' experiences of caring for patients with mental health presentations.

Themes

Four interrelated themes were identified that reflect participants' experiences of providing mental health care within the ED, namely: systemic factors; emotional impact and staff culture; influence of communication; and education and training (Figure 1). Subthemes within each theme provide deeper insights.

Thematic structure.

Systemic Factors

All participants described how the challenges experienced in providing mental health care in EDs are embedded in broader systemic issues. This includes both environmental and organisational/process constraints. Elements of the ED, such as the physical environment, patient load, staff shortages, and limited bed availability, influenced participants' ability to deliver optimal mental health care. Casey explained: “It's not the nurses themselves, it's bigger than that.”

Environmental Context

The ED environment was described as overstimulating, chaotic and untherapeutic for individuals presenting with mental health concerns. Participants described how mental health rooms were situated in high‐traffic areas “jammed between resus and the ambulance bay,” which meant “it's noisy, it's busy, it's bright lights, there's no privacy. It's very overstimulating” (Sam). Harley noted, “nobody can settle in that environment, let alone somebody who's already overstimulated.”

The mental health room design was considered unsuitable, as the rooms were isolating, lacked natural light, and failed to meet basic needs. Drew noted, “There's no windows, no sense of the time… it would drive you insane.” Additionally, Jamie acknowledged, “You put me in a room and I stare at a blank wall forever, it's not going to be good for anyone's mental health… it's good in principle but not actually in practice.” Others empathised with how this contributed to patient distress, particularly when patients were in the ED for prolonged periods: “… I know I would go off my nut if I had to sit in there for 24 hours” (Taylor).

The physical ED space and the complexities in managing patients with mental health issues were consistently raised. A common challenge encountered by participants was navigating involuntary patients wanting to go outside to smoke. Jamie reported, “We have a concern for their safety and we need to recognise that, but no one ever does well when you restrict their life privileges.” The direct association between stringent restrictions on involuntary patients and subsequent escalation of behaviours was apparent. Sam noted the importance of an outdoor courtyard for patients to have a cigarette break, “absolutely groundbreaking in terms of de‐escalating behaviours,” highlighting how small, patient‐focused strategies could make a significant difference.

Several participants also noted that while meeting basic needs could be crucial in patient‐centred care, limited resources resulted in minimal access to after‐hours food and comfort measures. “They're already probably coming from a situation of low economic status… So looking at the basic needs as well, which is often overlooked” (Harley). Casey explained, “we never have any food to give them… Everyone gets angry when they're hungry.” Failing to address these fundamental needs was identified as a key contributor to the escalation of negative behaviours.

Overcrowding and Staffing Pressures

Participants described organisational pressures from high patient loads and staff shortages, which left them with less time for interaction and the emotional support required by this group.

“I don't have the time to sit down and go, ‘What do you need right now? Do you want to have a shower? You haven't showered in days. Are you hungry? Do you want a cup of tea?” (Avery)

Taylor described how these factors can result in missing critical intervention opportunities. “They'll sit in the waiting room. Two hours later, they're gone, and then you've missed that individual”.

Increasing numbers of mental health presentations also resulted in prolonged wait times for specialist assessments, and “priorities are stretched between other patients… [which] may limit the care they give to these patients” (Jordan). This was seen to negatively affect care, with Avery sharing a patient's escalation after feeling ignored by staff. “‘Why hasn't anyone come to see me?’ And then [she] was like threatening to stab herself.”

Many participants reported the added difficulty of ‘double‐bunking’, a systemic challenge in the study setting where two patients are placed in a single ED cubicle instead of one. This practice was described as increasing nurse workload, reducing safety and limiting patient privacy. “If you've got suicidal thoughts and someone puts you in a double‐bunked bed, you're not going to feel safe” (Sam). Increased nurse‐to‐patient ratios were described as common, placing immense strain on staff capacity.

“It's not uncommon that staff could have one to four, one to five, one to six at times. And I would probably say, more often than not, if someone was having a ratio of one to four, it would involve at least one mental health patient.” (Jordan)

Length of Stay

Prolonged ED length of stay for patients presenting with mental health concerns, driven by limited bed availability, was described as a pressing systemic issue related to organisational/process constraints. Participants described patients in the ED for days while awaiting admission or transfer. Avery explained, “[I] have people in ED who've been in there for four days.”

Extended stays in an overstimulating ED environment were perceived to exacerbate distress, agitation, and at times aggressive behaviour. Jamie reflected, “We're exacerbating the problem… You lock someone in the room for days and see how your mental health is” Similarly, Harley noted, “anyone spending that amount of time in ED is not only dangerous, but it's distressing.”

Many participants expressed frustration with delays in admission to specialised units/facilities, stating that these delays affected their ability to deliver meaningful care. Sam questioned, “Really, what are we offering them?,” while Casey described a recurring cycle where patients remained in ED due to the absence of alternative beds.

Disjointed communication pathways further compounded these challenges. Delays in review and admission meant patients were required to repeat themselves to multiple clinicians before receiving specialist care. Sam described the system as “slow” and “clunky”, while Casey empathised with the frustrations of patients, “You're just another person. Is anybody talking to each other?”

Although some participants expressed confidence in their individual nursing skills, many highlighted frustrations with the systemic limitations. Jamie explains, “I feel confident in kind of my nursing care side of it, but the whole system side of it is I feel like where we have the issues.” This contributed to a sense of futility and an experience of care as “an individual battle, rather than a system supporting that person's care” (Harley).

Lack of Clear Mental Health Protocols

Uncertainty around optimal care pathways and processes was also identified due to the lack of a standardised approach. Many participants described unclear local policies, protocols and mental health pathways, as well as inconsistencies in care.

“I think that also makes treating people hard because there's no systematic protocol or pathway that's, I guess, universal in the department.” (Jordan)

“I tried to look up the protocol and it's currently not there… I wish that it was there so I could have… looked at it.” (Avery)

This lack of consistency was described as creating confusion about best practice and resulted in differing treatment pathways depending on individual clinicians.

“One of my biggest issues was that … no one seems to know exactly what the protocol should be when a mental health patient comes… Everyone has a different theory… you just listen to them and do as they tell you.” (Morgan)

The need for clear guidance in a user‐friendly format was highlighted to promote consistent quality care. “We need a proper guideline… If a patient with suicidal thoughts comes in, this is what you do…” (Morgan).

Emotional Impact and Staff Culture

ED culture and staff attitudes significantly shaped participants' experiences of providing mental health care. Participants described an emotional toll when suboptimal care was provided. Fear of adverse patient outcomes, safety concerns, and exposure to distressing situations influenced care delivery and staff engagement with patients. While some participants linked emotional fatigue to negative attitudes towards mental health patients, others discussed how ED cultures can influence reactive approaches to care, shaping staff attitudes and ultimately affecting patient management.

Impact on Nurses

Feelings of distress and remorse were identified when inadequate care was delivered or poor patient outcomes occurred. Participants expressed concern about poor care contributing to patients' trauma and not aligning with their values as nurses.

“It feels really confronting and I think a lot of the time it feels like we are contributing to people's trauma.” (Avery)

“It's distressing, because you can't do a good job. And we often do more damage than good, I think, sometimes.” (Harley)

Some participants highlighted the physical and emotional toll of caring for patients in mental health crises.

“It's incredibly traumatising watching that… as someone who wants to help people and seeing it get to that is really difficult.” (Avery)

Despite the emotional toll involved, participants discussed a lack of structured support, in some cases, following these stressful situations. Avery described, “there isn't an opportunity for us as nurses to be able to be like, ‘That really affected me’”. This absence of emotional support for nurses was seen to contribute to burnout. “The culture is a suck it up and deal with it. There's definitely no feedback. There's definitely no support” (Harley).

ED Culture and Staff Attitudes

Participants expressed a fear of professional consequences if a mental health patient self‐harmed or was hurt under their care. Morgan shared, “The really sad thing is… you're scared of getting in trouble if they hurt themselves”. Jamie added, “We had safety officers and stuff like that, but at the end of the day, it's our registration that is on the line.”

Safety concerns for both patients and staff also influenced participants' attitudes towards people with mental health issues and care delivery. Exposure to violent incidents during mental health crises was described by participants as shaping staff attitudes.

“There's been quite a shift… we are seeing increasing levels of mental health patients and significantly increasing levels of violence… I think there's an increased level of fear.” (Sam)

This fear was described as contributing to defensive, and sometimes depersonalised approaches to care where “you kind of don't interact, you just put a wall up” (Casey). Harley explained that “if you're scared of someone, you're not going to spend more time with them than you have to.”

The language participants used also reflected a culture associated with reactive and detached approaches. Many participants referred to the de‐escalation of mental health patients involving physical restraint and/or chemical sedation as “take‐downs”. However, some noted that it was confronting. “I hadn't heard the terminology of take‐down until being [here]… it's really heavy‐handed” (Drew). Participants described how this language and its practice represented significantly broader cultural issues. “I've had junior nurses say to me, ‘Let's just take them down and get this done with,’ and I think that's an awful perspective” (Sam).

Some participants acknowledged that some nurses' negative attitude towards caring for mental health patients was often linked to pressures of high patient loads. “People don't feel like they've got the time to do it properly” (Jamie). While other participants noted it could be due to burnout or compassion fatigue. “It takes a different approach to when you're just medically approaching somebody, so it's mentally and physically exhausting” (Harley). Stigma towards mental health was also associated with negative attitudes towards patient care. “There's definitely an attitude of ‘I don't want to deal with that’” (Avery) and “it's viewed as a burden” (Jamie). However, other participants suggested it wasn't the role of ED nurses to provide mental health care. Avery explained “we aren't going to be able to provide them any care in ED, it is just a stepping stone to be able to get down to either a ward or to get in touch with other services.”

Influence of Communication

Clear communication was central in participants' positive experiences managing mental health patients, and poor communication negatively affected care quality. Communication between clinicians and patients was described as crucial for creating therapeutic relationships, and miscommunication resulted in a lack of trust, frustration, and patient escalation. Good communication between multidisciplinary team members was described as being vital in minimising confusion and optimising the care pathway.

Building Rapport With Patients

Participants consistently emphasised the importance of spending time with people presenting with mental health issues to build rapport through meaningful communication. Validating patient experiences and establishing trust helped achieve positive outcomes. As Jordan reflected, “having someone appreciate you giving the time to them… I think is what makes that a positive outcome”. Drew noted the positive impact when he “had the time and ability to actually listen and help”. Although having the time to do so was described as an ongoing challenge.

Participants also recognised the importance of teamwork when building rapport with people presenting with mental health issues and how certain staff members were more suited to provide appropriate care.

“You recognise the nurses… that have this beautiful kind of nature… make that patient feel safe versus other staff who are just going to be a bit daunted and overwhelmed by that.” (Sam)

Additionally, participants highlighted that when patients felt unheard, they often became agitated. Casey reflected on a scenario where a communication breakdown led to a mental health patient becoming heightened, “Everyone was sort of talking at him. He had all these security guards around him, and no one was quite listening to him.”

Gaps in Communication

Participants described frustration when patients were not included in their care, noting that they were often excluded, especially when under involuntary treatment orders. “I just notice so many times people get sectioned and the doctor—or whoever has done it—has not told the patient” (Avery). This communication deficit often led to patient frustration and behavioural escalation. Harley reflected, “it ended up with security being there, finally the consultant going, ‘Yes, I need to prescribe some medication,’… but by the time it got to that point, it was very messy. It affected the whole department. It affected the person. It was a very poor outcome.”

Many participants also discussed how the number of teams involved in individuals' care can contribute to breakdowns in communication between health professionals. Morgan explained, “there's a lot of miscommunication between doctors, mental health staff and the nurses.” Such miscommunication confused staff and contributed to negative patient outcomes.

“mix‐up of communication, where somebody thought he was going to be able to go and somebody was like, ‘No, he can't.’ … the patient jumped off the bed, slammed his head into the wall… It was complete chaos.” (Sam)

Participants also described confusion over which medical team had responsibility for mental health patients and the burden this placed on delivering effective care. “ED doctor said, “We've referred to mental health.” Mental health said, “We're still waiting for them to be medically cleared.” And so I'm running back and forth…trying to keep the patient calm” (Morgan). This was raised as a particular concern during high‐risk scenarios, where establishing responsibility of care and engaging in proactive approaches was identified as crucial for improving patient outcomes.

Communication gaps between health professionals often left participants feeling unsupported. Morgan reflected on being unaware of the patient's plans of care: “You look really unprofessional when you yourself don't know what's going on… In reality, I'm actually not sure what we're waiting on here.” Harley agreed, saying: “there is no feedback… there's no discussion about how we should be managing it and who to talk to, to get things.”

Effective Communication

Teamwork and clear communication between medical teams and patients were also identified as crucial in delivering safe and effective care. Participants advocated for communication strategies that promoted transparency of care to the patient. As encouraged by Avery, “it would be really helpful if when that happens…the doctor who makes the decision to section the patient comes and gets the nurse and you go in together and have a conversation so that…you know that you're all on the same page.” Similarly, Sam described how a cohesive approach among clinicians allows different perspectives on treatment to come together, making the patient feel supported, “Both him and the AOD [drug and alcohol] clinician, who both know this patient, came together… had some conversations, made a plan.”

Participants favoured this open communication and proactive approach to care, as it initiated conversations with patients to build rapport, rather than waiting for escalation to occur before intervening. Sam reflected on their positive experience, “what's happening some days, which is brilliant, is they watch the triage screen and seen when… a patient that's known to them has arrived and [they] are actually going and seeing them from the waiting room straightaway.” Participants also described how clear care plan documentation from mental health specialists reduced the confusion and stress associated with complex care delivery. As explained by Morgan, “I feel like I have more control…they write a very clear plan… This is the progress note. This is where we're going next.”

Education and Training

Participants consistently reported a lack of mental health‐specific education for nurses, both during undergraduate education and ongoing professional development. This created a knowledge gap that affected participants' confidence in providing care for patients experiencing mental health distress. “I don't think we've got enough education around it” (Jamie).

Limited Mental Health Training

Participants discussed their limited exposure to mental health education during undergraduate studies and the impact this had on their preparedness for practice. Taylor recalled, “The mental health aspect that you have in university, it literally is just a drop in the bucket”. This lack of foundational training left some participants feeling unprepared for work in the ED.

“When I was a grad in ED, it was very confronting… you didn't know how to interact because you didn't want to ask the wrong questions… it was a bit scary.” (Casey)

Participants also described limited mental health training in ongoing professional development within the ED.

“I think even internally… there's very little support or, I guess, training that's given to staff.” (Jordan)

“Your exposure in further education in the department might be a 30‐min briefing maybe once a year, which is not really beneficial.” (Taylor)

Knowledge Gaps Affecting Confidence

Inadequate education meant that participants described feeling overwhelmed and fearful when managing people with mental health issues. “There's a lot of fear… especially if it's a patient with a history of aggression… I don't feel like I've got the skill at times” (Morgan). Without confidence and skills, participants were more likely to avoid patient interactions.

“I think nurses leave mental health patients to last—as their last job on their list because (a) it's overwhelming, (b) it might take time, they're not sure what's going to happen.” (Sam)

Most participants agreed that education would enhance nurses' confidence, knowledge and ability to provide quality mental health care. Sam reflected that, “if people felt like they had that knowledge and felt empowered to use those skills, then they might feel less overwhelmed.” Jamie also explained that “We need a lot further education… how to deal with them, how to de‐escalate, and how to just do a mental health assessment.” Taylor added, “I think it's [education] crucial for any ED nurse engaging with a mental health patient.”

Knowledge Gaps Affecting Patient Care

Gaps in knowledge and skills also contributed to broader issues in mental health care delivery. Participants described a reactive approach to care, where interventions often occurred only at the point of crisis.

“I think they have less de‐escalation skills … I think the clinical staff are quite reactive to mental health patients… a lower threshold for tolerating challenging behaviours.” (Sam)

“They usually let it go until there's violence involved, where it should be de‐escalated and identified way earlier than that.” (Harley)

“Instead of just starting that process, we're like, let's just wait until they get really angry or try to leave, and then we'll just do a take‐down. That's not a nice process.” (Drew)

In contrast, other participants described how stronger mental health skills and knowledge could enhance care. Sam explained, “staff that are actually trained to provide mental health care would be huge for the patients that are bed‐locked.” Drew added, “I would love to know how we can actually start treatment in ED for mental health. Because I feel like we're just trying to keep things calm until it's someone else's problem.”

Informal Learning

Beyond formal education, participants expressed the value of experiential learning and observing peers.

“Seeing how other individuals engage with mental health people…you look and see what people are doing and you take a little positive thing from this one… and make up your own sort of practice.” (Taylor)

Participants acknowledged the importance of learning skills from mental health clinicians, “that's their bread and butter. They know it better than we do” (Riley). Drew identified that they, “would love to even have cross‐education between ED and [mental health ward].”

Beyond other informal learning opportunities, participants who had personal experience of mental health issues were able to draw on this to build their confidence in patient interactions. Sam discussed how, “My own personal lived experience… makes a huge difference” in her care delivery. Similarly, Avery noted, “I have experienced mental health issues myself… I feel like I can support people.”

Discussion

This study has identified the structural, cultural and educational challenges ED nurses face when providing mental health care. Data reveals the need to consider the implementation of mental health care models and environmental modifications to better meet patient needs, as well as targeted mental health training for ED staff to increase awareness of mental health and decrease stigma. Translating these findings into practice has the potential to enhance the quality of care experienced by patients (Chou and Tseng 2020), as well as promote job satisfaction and reduce stress among nurses (Sexton et al. 2021).

Study findings demonstrated that structural challenges within EDs made it increasingly difficult for nurses to deliver therapeutic mental health care. This reinforces growing literature describing a misalignment between the capacity and resources of EDs and the complex needs of people with mental health issues (Bommersbach et al. 2023). Participants in this study highlighted the issue of extended lengths of stay in ED for mental health patients, reflecting the broader systemic issues of service gaps in community health services and inpatient settings (Isbell et al. 2025; Smith et al. 2025). A coordinated approach between community health services and tertiary ED services can help reduce the demand for ED mental health care. As suggested by Carey and Cole (2024), an increase of one annual mental health visit through community centres was associated with a 16% reduction in ED presentations. Introducing dedicated emergency psychiatric assessment units within EDs has also been demonstrated to significantly reduce wait times and lengths of stay. Kim et al. (2022) reported a two‐thirds reduction in boarding time, which in turn eased pressure on ED services and staff. These findings suggest that improving mental health care requires collaboration between community‐based services, acute psychiatric care, and crisis management services, which can create a safe space that provides timely health care outside of the ED (Ayano et al. 2017). This would not only improve the care mental health patients receive in times of crisis by providing an environment that is fit for purpose but also reduce the pressure on ED services and nursing workload.

Despite ED mental health presentations rising, this study found that participants felt inadequately prepared to provide optimal mental health care. Participants described minimal exposure to mental health content during their undergraduate studies, which can lead to nurses feeling underprepared to manage the complexities of mental health presentations in ED (Perrone McIntosh 2021). The need for additional mental health content in nursing curricula has been recognised, as the current knowledge gap creates challenges for graduates attempting to translate limited knowledge into nursing practice (Chukwuere et al. 2025; Yamamoto et al. 2021). Beyond undergraduate education, participants identified ongoing barriers to accessing professional development opportunities that provided structured, practical training specific to the ED context. Perrone McIntosh (2021) described how ED nurses expressed that this lack of ongoing education and updating of skills in caring for patients with mental illness can impact care delivery. Ongoing professional development in ED settings is particularly challenging, with barriers including inaccessibility due to rostering conflicts, workload demands, resource constraints, and cost of back‐filling positions (Pawaskar et al. 2022). These findings suggest that both universities and health care organisations play an important role in ensuring that nurses are appropriately prepared and skilled through ongoing mental health education. Supporting staff to develop and maintain mental health knowledge and skills is not only vital for their confidence in caring for mental health patients, but also for improving the safety of care and outcomes for this vulnerable group.

This study highlighted the emotional impact of caring for patients with mental health concerns in an under‐resourced and reactive environment. Nurses, as the largest group of healthcare professionals, are at the frontline and greatly impacted by the increasing number of patients seeking mental health care in ED (Perrone McIntosh 2021). Without adequate support, such additional stressors will contribute to nurses leaving the setting (Naidoo and Schoeman 2023). In the study, participants described feelings of guilt and moral distress when unable to provide best practice care to mental health patients, with minimal opportunities for formal debriefing and emotional support. Limited debriefing can lead to staff burnout and compassionate fatigue, which can perpetuate into negative attitudes and behaviours towards mental health patients (Knaak et al. 2021). To address these concerns and the broader issue of workforce retention, workplaces need to promote cultures that validate and support the emotional labour of ED nursing (Jachmann et al. 2025). Integrating trauma‐informed principles into ED practice can reduce stigma, alleviate burnout, and improve job satisfaction (Knaak et al. 2021; Smith et al. 2025). Embedding these approaches into routine service delivery and professional development not only enhances mental health care for patients but also protects the well‐being of the ED nursing workforce.

Strengths and Limitations

This study provides valuable insights into the experiences of ED nurses when caring for people with mental health issues, addressing a key gap in the literature. While the sample size was modest, information power was achieved, demonstrating sampling adequacy. The qualitative approach enabled rich accounts of nursing experiences to be recorded, and the supervisory oversight minimised any interpretation bias related to the first author's (C.K.) experience. As the study was conducted in a single hospital in the unique setting of the Northern Territory, other hospitals or jurisdictions may have different community contexts, physical environments, or policies and procedures that may impact nurses' experiences. Additionally, the use of snowball sampling may have introduced a level of selection bias. However, the participants involved reflected a cross‐section of ED nurses with varying levels of experience and roles, offering diverse perspectives on the topic.

Implications for Policy and Practice

Understanding the experiences of ED nurses provides important insight into the challenges of providing care for people presenting to the ED with mental health concerns. Improving mental health care in EDs requires a coordinated hospital‐wide approach. Developing easily accessible, evidence‐based care pathways and consistent protocols that outline best practice care for various presentation types may reduce clinical variation and optimise the delivery of best practice care. The creation of supportive environments for both patients and staff by integrating debriefing practices into ED culture would reduce negative psychological impacts and enhance outcomes. Ongoing mental health education and reflective practice should be embedded to build staff confidence, reduce burnout, and promote compassionate care. ED nurses should be actively involved in developing and implementing these strategies to support sustainable practice change.

Recommendations for Further Research

Future research should be undertaken with larger, more diverse samples across multiple sites to explore the implementation and impact of the strategies identified in this study on staff and patient outcomes. Such strategies could include mental health specific education such as early intervention and de‐escalation training, alternative mental health care pathways or evidence‐based care pathways. Future studies should also evaluate various models of care to better support mental health care both in the ED and within the community when exacerbations occur. Although this study focused on ED nurses, future research should consider how multidisciplinary teams can be engaged to support people presenting with mental health issues. This research should not only engage health professionals but also consumers and their carers to ensure that services are developed in a person‐centred manner that meets the needs of this population.

Conclusion

ED nurses face significant systemic, cultural, and educational barriers when caring for mental health patients. These barriers compromise not only quality patient care, but also contribute to the stress, emotional fatigue, and burnout of ED nurses. This study highlights that addressing these challenges requires a multi‐layered approach. Health system executives and hospital leadership must drive systemic reforms, including redesigning EDs and integrating community psychiatric services into care pathways, while ED management must provide structured support for staff well‐being and providing targeted mental health education for ED nurses. Only by embedding accountability and resources at every level, from policy to frontline management, can ED nurses be equipped to deliver safe, therapeutic, and patient‐centred care in the context of increasing mental health presentations.
