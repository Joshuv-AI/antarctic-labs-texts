Early diagnostic value of serum procalcitonin, C-reactive protein, white blood cell count and the neutrophil percentage in bacterial and fungal bloodstream infections
Authors: Shihui Zhai, Chunhua Luo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBloodstream infections (BSIs) are severe systemic disorders caused by pathogenic microorganisms, including bacteria and fungi, invading the bloodstream. Associated with high morbidity and mortality, BSIs represent one of the leading causes of death globally. There is an urgent demand for simple, rapid, and accurate laboratory biomarkers to facilitate the diagnosis and differential diagnosis of patients with suspected BSIs, thereby guiding timely and targeted antimicrobial therapy.ObjectiveTo evaluate the value of serum procalcitonin (PCT), C-reactive protein (CRP), white blood cell count (WBC), and neutrophil percentage (NEUT%) for early and differential diagnosis of bacterial vs. fungal bloodstream infections (BSIs), a cross-sectional study was conducted.MethodsA total of 1649 patients with suspected BSI were included in this retrospective study. Of these, 1357 patients with positive blood cultures were classified as the BSI group (experimental group), and 292 patients with negative blood cultures were assigned to the non-BSI group (control group). Serum PCT, CRP, WBC/NEUT% were measured using a bioMerieux VIDAS 30 analyzer, SIEMENS ADVIA 2400 and by Sysmex XN-9000, respectively. Microbial identification was based on bioMerieux VITEK 2 systems. The diagnostic performance of these biomarkers in the discrimination in bacterial and fungal bloodstream infections were compared using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves.ResultsFor BSI discrimination, a PCT level > 0.687 ng/mL could demonstrate the optimal performance (AUC = 0.788, sensitivity 61.7%, specificity 80.1%). The PCT level < 0.5 ng/mL for 3 consecutive days could effectively rule out BSI (sensitivity 98.1%, specificity 79.5%). Elevated PCT (> 1.78 ng/mL) and CRP (> 79.76 mg/L) levels were associated with bacterial BSIs (AUC = 0.686 for the combined markers). A PCT level > 1.98 ng/mL suggested Gram-negative BSI (sensitivity 68.4%). The positive diagnostic rates of the PCT + CRP+NEUT% combined test for G- bacterial and G+ bacterial BSIs reached 96.17% and 92.86% respectively, which significantly improved the diagnostic positive rate compared with single diagnosis.ConclusionsPCT could reliably excludes BSI (3-day < 0.5 ng/mL rule) and aids differentiation (bacterial/fungal: PCT + CRP; G-/G+: PCT+NEUT%). Introduction

Bloodstream infection (BSI) is a serious systemic disease caused by the invasion of pathogenic microorganisms such as bacteria and fungi, characterized by high morbidity and mortality, and is one of the leading causes of human mortality worldwide [1]. It has been documented that the incidence of BSI in hospitalized patients in the United States is 5.9% and the morbidity and mortality rates are about 15.6% [2]. BSI is classified as community-acquired BSI or hospital-acquired BSI depending on the source of the pathogen [3]. In general, community-acquired BSI in immunocompetent adults is usually caused by drug-susceptible bacteria, while hospital-acquired BSI is usually caused by multiple drug resistance strains [4]. Studies have shown that isolates from elderly BSI patients show higher rates of antibiotic resistance [5]. In addition, with the widespread use of central venous catheter and peripherally inserted central catheter, catheter-associated BSI has become more common in clinical practice. Catheter-related BSI has become one of the most frequent causes of hospital-acquired BSI [6]. The most common pathogenic microorganisms causing BSI are bacteria and fungi, and the clinical medication used to treat BSI caused by different pathogenic microorganisms is also different, so it is crucial to identify the pathogens as soon as possible [7]. At present, pathogen blood culture results are still the gold standard for diagnosis of BSI. However, the detection speed and positive detection rate of blood culture cannot meet the demand for “rapid diagnosis” and treatment of critically ill patients [8]. Thus, it is particularly important to choose simple, rapid and accurate laboratory indicators to diagnose and differentiate the type of BSI in patients, and to carry out targeted treatment.

A number of biomarkers have been used to diagnose and guide treatment of BSI, with procalcitonin (PCT) and C-reactive protein (CRP) being two of the most commonly studied biomarkers [9–11]. PCT is a polypeptide chain consisting of 116 amino acid molecules and is converted by biological enzymes to form calcitonin, which lowers blood calcium. The concentration of PCT in the blood of healthy humans is usually less than 0.05 ng/mL. When infected with viruses or mild local bacterial infections, PCT levels rarely exceed 0.5 ng/mL; when the PCT level is greater than 2.0 ng/mL, it highly indicates systemic bacterial infection, sepsis, and severe focal bacterial infection. PCT is detectable 2–4 h after the onset of infection, with peak levels occurring 12–24 h later; its half-life is 24 h [12]. Studies have shown that PCT-guided antibiotic therapy is effective in shortening the duration of treatment and reducing in-hospital and 28-day mortality in patients with BSI [13]. CRP, a classical acute-phase response protein, is commonly used in clinical practice to screen for microbial infections and to assess the activity of inflammatory diseases. A European study of community-acquired BSI has shown that serial CRP testing after infection can help in the clinical identification of patients with a poorer prognosis [14]. The white blood cell count (WBC) and neutrophil percentage (NEUT%) are traditional laboratory tests in patients with BSI and usually show an elevated trend for most infectious diseases. Currently, there are more domestic and international studies on the differential laboratory diagnosis of Gram-positive (G+) and Gram-negative (G−) bacterial BSI [15–18], but fewer studies have looked at the diagnosis of fungal BSI [19].

In present study, we collected and retrospectively analyzed the data of suspected BSI patients admitted to Yichang Central People’s Hospital from December 2018 to December 2022 who had blood culture test results. The experimental group (BSI group) with positive blood culture test results and a single pathogenic agent totally 1,357 cases, while the control group with negative blood culture test results comprised 292 cases. The PCT, CRP, WBC and NEUT% levels of the BSI group and the control group were compared. The BSI group was divided into bacterial BSI and fungal BSI groups, and the PCT, CRP, WBC and NEUT% levels of patients with bacterial BSI and fungal BSI compared. To further evaluate the diagnostic value of PCT for fungal BSI, we retrospectively collected data from 80 healthy physical examination subjects for intergroup comparison and ROC analysis. The aims were to explore the value of individual and combined detection of each index for the early diagnosis and differential diagnosis of BSI. This will facilitate the identification of the infecting pathogen at an early stage and guide appropriate targeted treatments.

Information and methods

Study subjects

The study subjects were patients with suspected BSI who were hospitalized in Yichang Central People’s Hospital between December 2018 to December 2022 and the protocol was approved by our hospital’s ethics committee.

Inclusion criteria

The BSI group: Patients were sent for at least 1 blood culture, PCT, CRP, WBC and NEUT% test within 48 hours of the onset of 1 or more systemic symptoms of infection (including a temperature > 38ºC or < 36ºC, chills, hypotension, oliguria and high lactate levels)[8]. The BSI group's blood culture was positive. And the blood culture of BSI patients yielded only one pathogenic species without polymicrobial infection.

The BSI group's blood culture was positive. And the blood culture of BSI patients yielded only one pathogenic species without polymicrobial infection.

The non-BSI group (control group): Patients were sent for at least 1 blood culture, PCT, CRP, WBC and NEUT% test within 48 hours of the onset of 1 or more systemic symptoms of infection (including a temperature > 38ºC or < 36ºC, chills, hypotension, oliguria and high lactate levels)[8]. The control group's blood culture was negative.

The control group's blood culture was negative.

Research methodology

Data collection

This was a retrospective case-control study. We collected the information of patients’ gender, age, clinical diagnosis, pathogen culture identification results and peripheral venous blood PCT, CRP, WBC and NEUT% test results.

Instruments and reagents

Blood culture was performed using a bioMerieux BACT/ALERT 3D automatic blood culture instrument. Microbial identification was conducted on a bioMerieux VITEK 2 MS automatic rapid microbial mass spectrometer and VITEK 2 Compact automatic bacterial identifier. Routine blood tests were performed using a Sysmex XN-9000 automatic hematology analyzer. CRP was measured by latex-enhanced immunoturbidimetry on a SIEMENS ADVIA 2400 automatic biochemistry analyzer. PCT was measured by enzyme-linked fluorescent assay on a bioMerieux VIDAS 30 automatic fluorescence immunoassay analyzer. All the reagents that were used were matching and employed in accordance with the instrument manufacturer’s instructions, with the data of indoor quality and inter-room quality assessment under control. According to the reagent instructions, WBC ≥ 10 × 109/L, NEUT% ≥ 75%, CRP ≥ 10 mg/L and PCT ≥ 0.5 ng/mL were determined as positive.

Statistical analysis

SPSS ver. 26.0 was used for all statistical analyses. The data results of measures that conformed to a normal distribution are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) and were compared using the two independent samples t-test. Data that did not conform to a normal distribution are given as the median and interquartile range (M, Q), and were compared using the nonparametric Mann-Whitney U test. Count data are presented as frequencies and percentages (n, %) and were compared using the chi-squared test. A P-value < 0.05 was considered to be a statistically significant finding.

Diagnostic value of PCT, CRP, and NEUT% to identify BSI and control groups

Plotting ROC curves to represent the diagnostic value of PCT, CRP and NEUT% in patients with BSI (Fig. 2). The area under the curve (AUC) of PCT for diagnosing BSI was 0.788 (95% CI: 0.749–0.827). The optimal cut-off value of PCT for diagnosing BSI vs. non-BSI was 0.687 ng/mL, suggesting that BSI was diagnosed when the PCT concentration was > 0.687 ng/mL in patients suspected of having BSI, with a diagnostic sensitivity of 61.7% and a specificity of 80.1%. The AUC of CRP for diagnosing BSI was 0.650 (95% CI: 0.617–0.684). The optimal cut-off value of CRP for diagnosing BSI vs. non-BSI was 51.29 mg/L, suggesting that BSI was diagnosed when the CRP concentration was >51.29 mg/L in patients suspected of having BSI, with a diagnostic sensitivity of 65.2% and a specificity of 58.6%. The AUC of NEUT% for diagnosing BSI was 0.590 (95% CI: 0.558–0.621). PCT has a high value for the exclusion diagnosis of BSI, and CRP and NEUT% have limited value as exclusion markers for BSI.

Diagnostic value of serial testing of PCT values for the exclusion of BSIs

In this study, a total of 433 patients underwent serum PCT testing for 3 consecutive days after the onset of one or more systemic symptoms of infection, of which 262 were in the BSI group and 171 in the control group. After comparing the levels of PCT concentrations in the BSI and control groups on 3 consecutive days respectively, it was found that all patients in the BSI group had significantly higher PCT levels on 3 consecutive days than those in the control group (P < 0.001) (Table 3). Temporal trends in PCT levels were further analyzed using serial measurements over 3 consecutive days (Table 3). In BSI patients, the median (interquartile range) PCT levels were 8.78 (37.37) ng/mL on Day 1, 11.66 (199.95) ng/mL on Day 2, and 4.61 (19.56) ng/mL on Day 3. PCT levels increased from Day 1 to Day 2, reaching a peak on Day 2, and then declined on Day 3. In contrast, PCT levels in the control group remained persistently low (median: 0.18–0.19 ng/mL) across all three days, with no obvious temporal variation. These dynamic changes indicate that peak PCT levels occur approximately 24–48 h after infection onset in BSI patients, supporting the value of serial PCT monitoring for assessing the course of infection. In the BSI group, there were 5 patients (1.9%) with 3 consecutive days’ PCT values < 0.5 ng/mL and 136 patients (79.5%) with 3 consecutive days’ PCT values < 0.5 ng/mL in the control group; the difference between the two groups was statistically significant (P < 0.001). The sensitivity of the diagnosis was 98.1% and the specificity was 79.5%. The positive predictive value (PPV) was 88.01% and the negative predictive value (NPV) was 96.45%, indicating favorable diagnostic reliability for both positive and negative results (Table S3).

Discussion

The clinical manifestations of BSI include fever, sudden chills, shortness of breath, tachycardia, etc. In severe cases, shock, disseminated intravascular coagulation and multiple organ failure may result, which may threaten the patients’ lives and safety [20]. While bacteria are the most common pathogens, fungal BSI incidence has risen due to prolonged use of broad‑spectrum antibiotics and antitumor agents, especially in immunocompromised patients, increasing morbidity, mortality, and hospital stay. Fungal and bacterial BSI show similar clinical features, leading to frequent misdiagnosis and delayed treatment [21, 22]. Exploring simple and specific laboratory tests that rapidly identify bacterial and fungal BSI provides important reference values for choosing appropriate treatment regimens for BSI at an early stage.

PCT is a peptide composed of 116 amino acid molecules with a molecular weight of 13 kDa, which is the precursor substance of calcitonin and has no hormonal activity itself [23]. After bacterial infection, endotoxin is released into the body, which causes the release of pro-inflammatory factors. At this stage, the liver, kidney, muscle cells, etc. begin to secrete PCT in large quantities into the bloodstream, so that its serum concentration rises rapidly and reaches a peak in about 12 h, with a half-life of about 24 h [24]. Therefore, PCT can respond to changes in BSI over time, and its serum concentration can be used as an indicator for judging the clinical condition and treatment prognosis [25].

A single-center retrospective study in France found that PCT helps to exclude the diagnosis of BSI faster in elderly patients compared to blood cultures [26]. In the present study, we found that the AUC of PCT for the diagnosis of BSI was 0.788, with a specificity of 80.1% when the PCT concentration was < 0.687 ng/mL. At the same time, we analyzed the PCT concentrations of 433 patients tested for 3 consecutive days after the appearance of one or more systemic infection symptoms, For patients with suspected BSI, continuous PCT testing is feasible, and if PCT < 0.5 ng/mL is detected for 3 consecutive days after the onset of clinical symptoms, the diagnosis of BSI can be basically excluded.

Pieralli et al. [27] reported that the serum PCT values in patients with bacterial BSI were significantly higher than those in patients with fungal BSI, and that PCT had a good differential diagnostic value between bacterial and fungal BSI. The results of this study demonstrated that patients in the bacterial group exhibited significantly higher PCT levels compared with those in the fungal group (M, Q: 2.17 [11.57] ng/mL vs. 0.68 [5.72] ng/mL, Z=-2.546, P = 0.011), which is consistent with the results of previous research. In addition, PCT is of great value in differentiating between G+ or G- bacterial BSI. This is because the cell wall structure of G- bacteria is different from that of G+ bacteria, as the cell wall of G- bacteria contains lipopolysaccharide (endotoxin), which acts as a stimulating factor that can trigger PCT production by an organism, thus causing a significant elevation of PCT in the patients with G- bacteria. The mechanism of the elevation of PCT may be due to the fact that bacterial invasion into the bloodstream stimulates the production of large amounts of PCT by extra-thyroidal organs or tissue cells, and the PCT produced by these cells cannot be converted to calcitonin, thus leading to the accumulation of PCT [24].

CRP is an acute time-phase response protein produced by hepatocytes induced by cytokines, and is a sensitive indicator of whether the body is infected or not and also the severity of the infection. it is widely used for the diagnosis of infectious diseases in children and adults [28, 29]. CRP exists in plasma mainly in the form of a pentamer of 115 kD, and it is able to bind to phosphoryl choline on the cell membranes of bacteria, fungi and damaged cells of the body and plays roles in activating complement, promoting phagocytosis and the regulation of immunity [30]. CRP is an acute temporal response protein whose levels increase dramatically in response to infection or tissue damage, but the elevation is nonspecific. Previous studies have suggested that elevated CRP had some diagnostic value in differentiating BSI from non-BSIs, but the specificity was low [31]. The study by Qiang Miao et al. [32] reported that serum CRP levels were significantly lower in fungal BSI than in bacterial BSI, supporting CRP as a differential marker. Our results suggested that the diagnostic efficacy of CRP for differential diagnosis of bacterial and fungal BSI was 0.663 (95% CI: 0.606–0.719), which has some diagnostic value and is consistent with the results of previous studies.

WBC and NEUT% are routine testing indicators for infectious diseases [33]. As key components of the immune defense system, they eliminate invasive pathogens via phagocytosis and antibody production. Bacterial infection typically induces elevated WBC and NEUT% levels, which are widely used to support the diagnosis of bacterial infection [34]. Despite their relatively low specificity, WBC and NEUT% remain traditional tests for BSI and improve diagnostic accuracy when combined with other infectious biomarkers.

In this study, the positive detection rates of PCT and CRP for bacterial BSI were higher than those for fungal BSI, and their combined detection significantly improved the positive rate for both types of BSI. A PCT concentration > 1.78 ng/mL or CRP > 79.76 mg/L indicated a higher probability of bacterial BSI, suggesting that PCT and CRP can be used for the early differential diagnosis of bacterial versus fungal BSI and reduce underdiagnosis. Our results are consistent with those reported by Liu et al. [35] and the combined use of PCT and CRP showed better diagnostic performance than either marker alone. Early monitoring of serum PCT and CRP levels helps improve BSI detection, identify pathogenic types, guide rational antibiotic use, reduce the risk of drug resistance and adverse reactions, and lower treatment costs [36].

In this study, 1,282 patients with bacterial BSI were included, of which 862 were patients with G− bacterial BSI. We found that PCT, CRP, NEUT% levels and test positivity were significantly higher in patients in the G− bacterial group compared to the G+ bacterial group (P < 0.01), findings consistent with the results of a previous study [37]. This may be due to the fact that the cell wall of G− bacteria contains lipopolysaccharide (endotoxin) that induces the production of PCT in the organism, thus causing a significant increase in PCT in patients with G− bacteria [38]. Further categorizing G− bacterial BSIs, the detection rates were, in order, Escherichia spp. (35.00%), Staphylococcus spp. (15.40%), Klebsiella spp. (15.11%), Streptococcus spp. (9.58%), Enterococcus spp. (5.82%), Pseudomonas spp. (3.17%), Enterobacteriaceae spp. (2.87%), Salmonella spp. (1.18%), Aspergillus spp. (0.73%), similar to common bacteria in other studies [39]. The National Bloodstream Infection Bacterial Resistance Surveillance 2021 Annual Report [40] showed that Escherichia coli and Klebsiella pneumoniae caused more than 50% of BSI, a similar finding to the more than 50% found in the present study.

In recent years, the literature on BSI has mostly focused on bacterial BSI, and there are fewer studies on fungal BSI, and the sample sizes of the relevant studies are small. This study was a large-sample study, which analyzed the differential diagnostic value of PCT, CRP, NEUT% and WBC for bacterial and fungal BSI, and G− and G+ bacterial BSI. The results provide reference value for the early differential diagnosis of clinical BSI. In addition, the study innovatively used the PCT value for 3 consecutive days after one or more systemic infection symptoms that appeared in patients for the exclusion of a diagnosis of BSI. It was found that the PCT concentration of < 0.5 ng/mL over 3 consecutive days for an exclusion diagnosis of BSI yielded a sensitivity of 98.1%, a specificity of 79.5%, a PPV of 88.01%, and an NPV of 96.45% for excluding the diagnosis of BSI, which provides new diagnostic criteria for the exclusion of a diagnosis of suspected BSI.

In summary, for patients suspected of having BSI, PCT was performed within 48 h after the onset of one or more systemic symptoms of infection. The AUC of PCT for the diagnosis of BSI was 0.788 (95% CI: 0.749–0.827), suggesting that PCT has a higher diagnostic value for BSI. When PCT concentration was > 0.687 ng/mL, a diagnosis of BSI was more likely, and the sensitivity and specificity of diagnosis were a more than satisfactory 61.7% and 80.1%, respectively. The relatively low sensitivity suggests that PCT alone is insufficient to rule out BSI, whereas the moderate specificity supports its role as a supplementary diagnostic marker. A PCT concentration of < 0.5 ng/mL over 3 consecutive days showed high sensitivity, specificity, PPV, and NPV for excluding BSI, providing a new criterion for ruling out suspected BSI. PCT, CRP and NEUT% are valuable for the early differential diagnosis of bacterial and fungal BSI, and the combined test can improve the positive detection rate and differential diagnosis value. In our study, we found that in patients with suspected bacterial and fungal BSI, the possibility of bacterial BSI was higher when the serum PCT concentration was > 1.78 ng/mL or CRP > 79.76 mg/L. Compared with healthy subjects, fungal BSI patients had higher PCT levels, with PCT showing excellent diagnostic performance at a cut-off of 0.11 ng/mL. In patients with suspected G− bacterial or G+ bacterial BSI, the possibility of G− bacterial BSI was higher when PCT was > 1.98 ng/mL. However, this should only be used as an auxiliary indicator, and diagnosis must be made in combination with clinical findings.

There were some limitations to this study. Firstly, it was a single-center retrospective study with some study design anomalies. Although the total sample size was large, some cases were not included due to incomplete data in the process of data collection, which may have had an impact on the results. As a retrospective observational study, some patients had received antibiotics before admission, and relevant records were incomplete or not systematically documented in the medical system, making it impossible to collect full and accurate information on pre-sampling antibiotic treatment. The findings will be further improved by a multicenter prospective study which we will conduct in the near future. Secondly, given the low sensitivity of blood culture, using it as the gold standard may be insufficient, and some blood culture-negative patients with bloodstream infection may have been misclassified into the control group. Furthermore, we did not include SOFA scores, hemodynamic status, or organ function assessment, which limits the clinical applicability of our results. Thirdly, confounding factors such as cytokine release syndrome, CPR status, and infection focus were not adjusted for, which may restrict the robustness of the conclusions. In subsequent experiments, we will comprehensively evaluate and diagnose bloodstream infections by integrating the SOFA scores, patients’ clinical conditions, infection biomarkers and other relevant indicators, while excluding potential confounding factors that may exert effects on infection biomarkers. Finally, we did not analyze disease severity or alternative diagnoses due to the retrospective design and incomplete records. Future studies may stratify patients by disease severity (based on blood culture time to positivity and clinical status) for further comparison and validation. Meanwhile, document alternative diagnoses in the control group.

Conclusions

PCT has high diagnostic and exclusionary value for BSI. For patients suspected of BSI, PCT detection should be performed immediately after the appearance of one or more systemic infection symptoms. The diagnosis of BSI can be basically excluded when the patients’ serum PCT concentration is < 0.5 ng/mL for three consecutive days. PCT, CRP and NEUT% have certain diagnostic value for the early differential diagnosis of bacterial and fungal BSI. Multi-index combined detection can improve the positive detection rate of bacterial and fungal BSI. In patients with suspected bacterial or fungal BSI, when PCT is > 1.78 ng/mL and CRP > 79.76 mg/L, the possibility of diagnosis of bacterial BSI was significantly increased. Compared with healthy subjects, fungal BSI patients had higher PCT levels, with PCT showing excellent diagnostic performance at a cut-off of 0.11 ng/mL. PCT has certain value for the differential diagnosis of G− bacterial and G+ bacterial BSI. In patients suspected of G− bacterial or G+ bacterial BSI, when the PCT concentration is > 1.98 ng/mL, the likely diagnosis of a G− bacterial BSI was significantly increased.
