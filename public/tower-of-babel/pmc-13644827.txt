Dimensionally Engineered Nanotherapeutics for Pulpitis and Periodontitis: From Structural Design to Artificial Intelligence-Enabled Precision Therapy
Authors: Fuzhong Zhang, Wenyue Zhang, Ting Lu, Yao Meng, Meihui Su, Qixiong Zhang, Houkui Shi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPulpitis (Pul) and periodontitis (PD) are oral infectious diseases with extremely high global prevalence. These pathologies can induce severe pain, significantly elevate the risk of tooth mobility and eventual tooth loss, and have been confirmed to be closely correlated with multiple chronic systemic disorders including diabetes mellitus and cardiovascular diseases. Conventional clinical interventions for these diseases are frequently characterized by incomplete debridement and inefficient drug delivery. Additionally, the growing threat of antimicrobial resistance presents an ongoing challenge for traditional treatments, leading to overall unsatisfactory therapeutic outcomes. Nanomaterials offer an innovative approach to overcoming the aforementioned limitations of conventional treatments, owing to their distinctive physicochemical properties, intrinsic antibacterial, anti-inflammatory and antioxidant activities, as well as their ability to facilitate bone and cementum regeneration. However, the development of nanotherapeutics for Pul and PD currently remains inefficient. Most existing nanomaterial systems exhibit poor adaptability to the complex oral microenvironment, and are unable to achieve closed-loop dynamic regulation during the entire therapeutic process. The rapid development of artificial intelligence (AI) has brought unprecedented new opportunities to resolve these bottlenecks. By enabling an intelligent “sense-analyze-respond” feedback loop, AI can optimize the key performance parameters of nanomaterials and facilitate dynamic adjustment of clinical therapeutic regimens. This review firstly sorts out the pathogenesis of Pul and PD, as well as the corresponding targeted intervention strategies enabled by nanomaterials. Subsequently, the existing nanomaterial systems are categorized based on their dimensional classification (0D to 3D), and their therapeutic applications and current limitations in the treatment of both diseases are analyzed. Finally, this paper discusses the research prospects of AI-enabled nanomaterials in structural design and in the management of Pul and PD. This review aims to provide theoretical reference for the development of more precise and effective therapeutic strategies for these two prevalent oral diseases.

Introduction

Oral infectious diseases are among the most prevalent pathologies in clinical dental practice. Among these conditions, pulpitis (Pul) and periodontitis (PD) are particularly common in clinical practice. These two diseases impose severe detrimental impacts on both oral function and systemic health, and have thus been identified as critical global public health concerns.1 Pul typically initiates when deep caries breaches the hard-tissue barrier, enabling pathogenic microorganisms to invade the pulp chamber. In the absence of timely and effective clinical intervention, Pul may progress to pulp necrosis or apical periodontitis.2 In contrast, PD is primarily triggered by the long-term accumulation of plaque biofilm at the gingival margin.3 The sustained chronic inflammatory response induced by plaque biofilm progressively damages periodontal supporting tissues, resulting in the formation of periodontal pockets, loss of connective tissue attachment, and irreversible alveolar bone resorption, which constitutes the leading cause of tooth loss among the adult population. Epidemiological surveys demonstrate that the combined prevalence of Pul and PD affects billions of individuals globally. Furthermore, substantial evidence confirms that the occurrence and progression of these two diseases are closely associated with chronic systemic disorders including diabetes and cardiovascular diseases.4,5 Accordingly, optimizing therapeutic strategies and improving clinical outcomes for Pul and PD carries great public health significance.

At present, the standard clinical management of Pul and PD is mainly composed of root canal therapy or basic periodontal therapy represented by scaling and root planing (SRP), combined with local drug administration or periodontal surgery.6–9 These conventional therapeutic strategies still face multiple non-negligible limitations. First, the root canal system contains complex anatomical structures such as isthmuses and lateral canals, and periodontal pockets extend deep into the periodontal tissue. Mechanical debridement alone cannot completely eliminate bacterial biofilms hidden in these difficult-to-reach areas.8 Second, the low permeability of dense dentinal tubules restricts the diffusion of conventional anti-infective agents into deep tissues, which may lead to incomplete disinfection of the root canal system.9 Meanwhile, the problem of antimicrobial resistance is becoming increasingly prominent. In addition, conventional treatment modalities cannot actively induce the regeneration of damaged periodontal and pulp tissues.10 Finally, existing diagnostic systems lack objective molecular typing criteria and accurate quantitative indicators, which hinders the implementation of personalized precision therapy. The combination of these limitations leads to uncertain clinical efficacy and high recurrence rates of diseases.

Notably, progress in nanotechnology has provided a novel approach to overcoming the aforementioned therapeutic limitations. Nanomaterials are defined as materials with at least one dimension falling within the range of 1–100 nm. Benefiting from nanoscale effects, high specific surface area, adjustable surface charge, and convenient functional modification, nanomaterials can achieve properties superior to those of conventional materials.11–14 In the field of oral treatment, nanomaterials can simultaneously exert multiple biological functions including broad-spectrum antibacterial activity, sustained anti-inflammatory effect, oxidative stress regulation, and induction of bone and cementum regeneration.15–17 Nevertheless, three major limitations still impede the clinical translation of nanomedicines for the treatment of Pul and PD. First, the biological performance of nanomaterials is co-regulated by multiple parameters such as particle size, morphology, surface charge and surface coating, and these parameters exhibit complex nonlinear interactive relationships.18 Traditional nanomaterial development is heavily dependent on trial-and-error strategies, which require extensive repeated experiments and substantially prolong development cycles while increasing research and development costs. Second, the oral microenvironment is characterized by dynamic heterogeneity, with local pH, enzyme concentration and bacterial metabolites constantly changing over time.19 However, conventional nano-preparations have fixed drug release kinetics and response modes after synthesis, and cannot achieve on-demand adaptive adjustment of therapeutic strategies along with lesion progression. Third, after the administration of nanosystems, it is generally impossible to realize real-time monitoring of local pathological status and therapeutic response, and dynamically adjust subsequent intervention schemes based on real-time feedback information. The lack of a “perception-analysis-response” closed-loop system is still a key bottleneck restricting the clinical translation of nanomaterials.

Recent developments in AI algorithms and computing power are expected to provide potential solutions to the aforementioned limitations. Existing studies on cancer nanomedicine and neurological diseases have confirmed that deep learning and machine learning approaches can quantitatively model the cross-scale relationships among the structure, properties and function of nanomaterials.20,21 This data-driven research paradigm can effectively reduce the time consumption and economic cost of traditional trial-and-error development modes, while extract meaningful feature information from complex nanosensor signals, and supporting the transformation of clinical diagnosis and treatment to dynamic decision-making and precise regulation.22 In Pul, AI-assisted diagnostic systems have been able to achieve high-accuracy identification of periapical lesions and root fractures.23 In PD, deep learning technology has also exhibited excellent performance in predicting disease progression and assisting clinical maintenance care.24 Accordingly, the construction of AI-enabled nanotheranostic systems for Pul and PD has clear research value and clinical translation potential.

This review first elaborates on the pathophysiological mechanisms of Pul and PD. Systematically analyzes the shared molecular pathways of the two diseases and their disease-specific pathological differences, and further discusses the potential intervention targets for nanomaterial-based therapy. On this basis, the review categorizes currently reported nanosystems according to their dimensionality (ranging from 0D to 3D), and summarizes the research progress of application and existing limitations of different dimensional nanosystems in the treatment of the two diseases. Finally, this review discusses the future development prospects of AI-enabled intelligent nanotheranostic systems targeting Pul and PD. The overall research aim is to provide theoretical reference for the development of more accurate and effective clinical treatment strategies for the two diseases (Figure 1). Pathogenesis of Pul and PD and Targeted Design of Nanomaterials

The Healthy Oral Environment and Pathological Infected State

The oral and maxillofacial region contains both open and relatively enclosed anatomical compartments. It maintains connectivity with the external environment while sustaining a highly coordinated state of microbial homeostasis.25 Under physiological conditions, intact oral mucosa and dental hard tissues constitute a stable physical barrier. Meanwhile, host defensive factors including salivary clearance, pH homeostasis regulation, and antimicrobial peptides collectively maintain local tissue homeostasis.26 Furthermore, the dynamic equilibrium between commensal microorganisms and host immunity restricts the overgrowth of pathogenic microorganisms.27 Both the pulp-dentin complex (DPC) and periodontal tissues possess tissue-specific immune surveillance systems and endogenous repair capabilities, which can maintain tissue structural integrity under mild pathogenic stimulation.28 When the above-mentioned balance is disrupted by external factors, pathogenic microorganisms can colonize the root canal wall and form persistent biofilms, driving the host immune response to shift from a protective defense phenotype to a destructive inflammatory phenotype, and ultimately leading to irreversible tissue necrosis.29 Accumulating evidence indicates that microbial dysbiosis is a core driving factor in the pathogenesis of both Pul and PD.30 Moreover, the two diseases share multiple common pathogenic molecular pathways. Accordingly, in-depth exploration of these pathogenic mechanisms is a fundamental prerequisite for the rational design of well-matched targeted nanodelivery systems.

Common Pathogenic Mechanisms Underlying Pul and PD

Pul and PD are both biofilm-mediated infectious inflammatory diseases, and their pathogenic progression follows a sequential continuous cascade of infection-inflammation-tissue destruction (Figure 2A). Oral pathogens initiate the pathogenic process by invading host tissues, activating host immune responses, and triggering downstream inflammatory signaling pathways, which ultimately leads to irreversible structural damage to adjacent dental and periodontal soft and hard tissues.31 In Pul, pathogenic microorganisms typically invade the dental pulp via hard tissue defects induced by deep caries, and form biofilms through adhesin-mediated microbial coaggregation.32 PD originates from the formation and colonization of subgingival plaque biofilms.33 In addition to physical interactions between different microorganisms, virulence factors represented by gingipains and the scaffold function of specific bacteria synergistically promote the heterogeneous aggregation of plaque. This established biofilm attenuates mechanical clearance effects and evades host immune recognition and clearance, thereby forming a physical barrier that protects pathogens from host immune defense.34

At the inflammatory stage, both diseases are characterized by the activation of host immune responses and the amplification of inflammatory signaling cascades. The acidic metabolites produced by biofilm microorganisms form an acidic microenvironment that drives the enrichment of acid-tolerant strains. Subsequent hard tissue demineralization disrupts the structural integrity of dental hard tissues, creating an invasion pathway for anaerobic pathogens to penetrate deep dentin and reach the pulp chamber.34,35 Pathogens and their associated virulence factors function as pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs). After being recognized by pattern recognition receptors expressed on host immune cells, downstream signaling pathways including nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB) and mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) are activated, which further induces the transcriptional expression and secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines and chemokines.36 These inflammatory mediators recruit immune effector cells to the lesion site, initiating a series of immune responses including phagocytosis, pathogen clearance and antigen presentation.37 However, persistent inflammatory stimulation triggers a cascade reaction that leads to progressive tissue damage. Continuous activation of immune cells induces excessive production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs). While these effector molecules eliminate invading pathogens, they also induce oxidative damage to host cells and mediate degradation of the extracellular matrix (ECM).38,39 Additionally, the acidic, hypoxic and hyper-inflammatory microenvironment upregulates the expression of hypoxia-inducible factor 1 alpha (HIF-1α), which exerts an inhibitory effect on the biological functions of reparative cells such as fibroblasts and osteoblasts, ultimately impairing the regenerative potential of the DPC and periodontal tissues.40

During the process of tissue destruction in Pul, the anatomical structure of the enclosed pulp chamber and the limited collateral circulation of dental pulp tissue are critical influencing factors. Inflammatory responses increase vascular permeability and interstitial tissue pressure, rapidly impairing local microcirculation, and ultimately induce pulp necrosis via hypoxia and nutrient deprivation.28,41 Necrotic pulp tissue subsequently provides a favorable microenvironment for further proliferation of pathogenic microorganisms, and the infection can spread through the apical foramen and lateral root canals to periapical tissues, resulting in apical periodontitis, focal alveolar bone resorption, and persistent clinical pain.42 In PD, the core pathological characteristics are degradation of periodontal connective tissue and alveolar bone resorption.43,44 These pathological processes are primarily regulated by the receptor activator of nuclear factor-κB/receptor activator of nuclear factor-κB ligand (RANK/RANKL) and osteoprotegerin (OPG) signaling system. Persistent inflammatory upregulates RANKL expression in osteoblasts and activated T cells, while simultaneously inhibiting OPG synthesis. The resultant imbalance in the RANKL/OPG ratio leads to excessive activation of osteoclast-mediated bone resorption.45 Besides, Sustained activation of HIF-1α signaling can further upregulate RANKL expression and promote osteoclastic bone resorption.46 It is worth noting that the pulp and periodontal tissues maintain anatomical connectivity via the apical foramen, lateral root canals, and dentinal tubules. Accordingly, pulp infection may induce periodontal attachment loss and alveolar bone destruction. Meanwhile, pathogenic microorganisms and their metabolites in periodontal pockets can also retrogradely invade the pulp tissue. The endodontic-periodontal combined lesions caused by this interconnection are particularly challenging in clinical diagnosis and treatment.47

Distinct Pathogenic Mechanisms of Pul and PD

Although Pul and PD share a number of overlapping pathological pathways, they differ substantially in both lesion microenvironment and microbial community composition (Figure 2B and C). Pul develops within the closed pulp chamber. Its blood supply is dependent on terminal vessels traversing the narrow apical foramen, and mature dental pulp is characterized by the absence of effective collateral circulation. Consequently, the dental pulp maintains a relatively hypoxic state under physiological conditions.48 During the progression of inflammation, accumulated inflammatory metabolites further promote local microenvironmental acidification.49 By contrast, PD arises in the relatively open periodontal pocket, which is equipped with an abundant vascular network. The persistent flow of gingival crevicular fluid establishes a unique fluid microenvironment that supports host-microbe interactions.50

The trajectory of microbial succession during Pul pathogenesis is also relatively well characterized. In the early stage of infection, facultative anaerobes are the dominant taxa, and they initiate bacterial adhesion and biofilm formation. As local hypoxia intensifies, obligate anaerobes become the predominant population, and amplify inflammatory responses via metabolic cooperation and virulence factor expression. PD is predominantly driven by Gram-negative anaerobic rods. These pathogens construct complex biofilm structures via interbacterial coaggregation and adhesion to the host matrix, and subsequently trigger excessive activation of host immune responses through lipopolysaccharide (LPS), proteases, and other virulence factors.51

Targeted Design of Nanomaterials

Based on the commonalities and disparities in the pathogenic mechanisms underlying Pul and PD, the targeted design of nanomaterials can follow a hierarchical design strategy, developing broad-spectrum intervention strategies that target the core pathological pathways shared by the two diseases, and subsequently achieving precise functional adaptation tailored to the unique microenvironmental characteristics and disease progression stages of each specific condition.

To interrupt the shared pathological cascade of infection-inflammation-tissue destruction, an ideal nano-theranostic platform should integrate antibacterial, anti-inflammatory, antioxidant and regenerative biological functions. In the infectious pathological microenvironment, nanomaterials can clear pathogenic biofilms through multiple distinct mechanisms. Silver-based nanomaterials exert biofilm clearance effects via releasing metal ions that disrupt bacterial membrane structure, induce elevation of intracellular ROS, and interfere with normal microbial metabolism processes.52,53 Polyphenol-based nanocarriers can disrupt bacterial cell membrane stability, impair membrane function and protein synthesis, inhibit the activity of bacterial efflux pumps, downregulate virulence gene transcription, and reverse antibiotic resistance phenotypes of pathogenic bacteria, thereby weakening the protective barrier effect of biofilms.54 In addition, stimuli-responsive drug delivery systems can specifically identify characteristic signaling molecules within the biofilm microenvironment, including acidic pH, specific enzymes and hypoxic conditions, and achieve controlled on-demand drug release to improve local drug bioavailability.55

During the inflammatory phase, regulation of oxidative stress status and inflammatory signaling pathways constitutes a core therapeutic target. Cerium oxide nanozymes take advantage of the reversible valence conversion between Ce3+ and Ce4+ in their crystal lattice, which endows the nanozymes with superoxide dismutase (SOD)-like and catalase (CAT)-like enzymatic activities, enabling efficient scavenging of excess ROS.56 Ceria nanoparticles can also induce polarization shift of macrophages from the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype to the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype, thereby remodeling the periodontal immune microenvironment.57 Polyphenol-based nanomaterials can inhibit the activation of the NF-κB signaling pathway, and downregulate the expression of key inflammatory mediators including tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α), interleukin 1 beta (IL-1β) and interleukin 6 (IL-6).58,59

In the phase of tissue destruction, nanomaterial-based therapeutic strategies center on bioactive functional regulation and in situ tissue regeneration. Metal-organic frameworks (MOFs) can achieve controlled degradation, and continuously release osteoinductive ions including Zn2+, Mg2+, and Cu2+, which further activate the signaling pathways associated with osteoblast differentiation.60 Three-dimensional porous scaffolds can be functionalized via biomimetic mineralization or decellularized ECM coating modification; the modified material surface can mimic the biochemical microenvironment of natural bone matrix, and provide favorable conditions for the adhesion, proliferation and directed differentiation of bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells.61,62 Polydopamine-modified reduced graphene oxide (RGO@PDA) integrates the anti-inflammatory activity of dopamine with the osteoinductive potential of reduced graphene oxide, which can scavenge reactive oxygen species, downregulate pro-inflammatory mediators, upregulate osteogenic gene expression, and promote the osteogenic differentiation of bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells.63

Given the heterogeneity of pathogenic mechanisms underlying Pul and PD, the functional design of nanomaterials generally adopts differentiated targeted regulation strategies. In the case of Pul, the enclosed pulp cavity creates a structural barrier, thus small-sized metal nanoparticles are preferred, as they can penetrate dentinal tubules and reach deep-seated infection foci in the root canal system.64,65 Additionally, oxygen-generating nanosystems can alleviate hypoxic conditions in poorly perfused pulp lesions, while hypoxia-responsive drug carriers can achieve on-target drug release at lesion sites.66,67 For PD, the lesion site is well-perfused and continuously exposed to gingival crevicular fluid. Therefore, nanomaterials with positively charged surfaces (such as chitosan-based nanoparticles) or biomimetic adhesive surface coatings (such as polydopamine) can prolong the retention time on the inner wall of periodontal pockets and resist the clearance effect of gingival crevicular fluid.68,69 Besides, Living cell-based platforms represent another route to active PD targeting. Liu et al reported phytate/Zn2+-loaded gelated macrophages (PZ-GMs) that retain both membrane integrity and the innate molecular recognition of native macrophages, thereby enabling sustained sequestration of bacterial toxins and inflammatory mediators.70 Coupled with Zn2+-mediated bactericidal activity and phytate-driven osteogenesis, this construct integrates neutralization, eradication, and osteopromotion within one platform, interrupting the periodontitis cascade at multiple nodes.

To sum up, the “seeking common ground while reserving differences” design strategy can support the construction of a continuous therapeutic system for Pul and PD. In the following section, we classify the mainstream nanomaterials and systematically review their research applications in both diseases.

Mechanisms and Applications of Nanomaterials

Nanosystems of Different Dimensions

Nanomaterials are conventionally defined as materials where at least one spatial dimension falls within the 1–100 nm range.71 According to their dimensional characteristics, nanomaterials can be categorized into four classes: zero-dimensional (0D) particles, one-dimensional (1D) fibers or tubes, two-dimensional (2D) sheets, and three-dimensional (3D) scaffolds that contain nanopores or nanonetworks. 0D nanomaterials possess multiple distinct advantages, including abundant interfacial contact, satisfactory catalytic activity, and facile implementation of surface functionalization (Figure 3). 1D nanostructures demonstrate high adaptability to sustained drug delivery systems and biomimetic supporting applications. 2D nanosheets can provide large specific interfacial areas and achieve high drug loading capacity. 3D nanonetworks are designed to prioritize two key functions, namely spatial structural support and multi-functional integration.72 The actual application value of these nanomaterials in the management of Pul and PD is jointly determined by multiple practical factors, including lesion anatomical characteristics, administration route, therapeutic dose window, in vivo degradation behavior, and other related variables. Zero-Dimensional (0D) Nanomaterials

0D nanomaterials refer to particulate nanostructures where all three spatial dimensions are confined within the nanoscale range. Representative types of 0D nanomaterials include noble metal nanoparticles, metal oxide nanoparticles, mesoporous silica nanoparticles, carbon dots and quantum dots (Figure 4).73–75 Owing to their high specific surface area and easily modified surface properties, 0D nanomaterials can exert biological effects via multiple mechanisms, including metal ion release, catalysis, photothermal effect, photodynamic effect, and loading and delivery of active therapeutic agents.76 In the oral microenvironment, the small particle size of 0D nanomaterials enables them to penetrate and contact biofilms distributed in root canal isthmuses, lateral root canals and dentinal tubules.77 In addition, 0D nanomaterials can be incorporated into liposomes, polymers or hydrogel systems to prolong local retention time, improve the stability of loaded active ingredients, and achieve controllable regulation of drug release kinetics.78

In Pul, 0D nanoparticles can function as active functional components of pulp sealers. Their intrinsic antibacterial activity, immunoregulatory capability, favorable biocompatibility with pulp cells, and the potential to maintain pulp vitality collectively indicate strong research value for further investigation. Xie et al prepared chromium-based nanoparticles (NanoCr) via water-in-oil droplet-confined precipitation followed by thin-film dispersion, and then incorporated the obtained nanoparticles into pulp sealers.79 It was verified that NanoCr exhibited good biocompatibility with dental pulp stem cells (DPSCs), could effectively scavenge ROS and inhibited the activation of the MAPK and NF-κB signaling pathways. Meanwhile, NanoCr possessed SOD-like and CAT-like activities, thus enabling it to mimic the endogenous antioxidant defense system of organisms. Additionally, NanoCr exerted inhibitory effects on oral pathogens by disrupting bacterial cell wall structure, interfering with intracellular energy metabolism, and depleting intracellular adenosine triphosphate (ATP).

Duan et al synthesized cerium-doped mesoporous bioactive glass nanoparticles (Ce-MBGNs) via a microemulsion-assisted sol-gel method.80 The as-prepared nanoparticles were verified to inhibit the polarization of macrophages toward the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype, downregulate the expression of inflammatory mediators in an LPS-stimulated THP-1 monocyte model, and scavenge intracellular ROS. In addition, Ce-MBGNs could promote the odontoblastic differentiation of DPSCs. Therefore, this nanoparticle system achieves the integration of immunoregulatory function and dentin regeneration induction in in vitro experiments.

For local therapeutic delivery applications, Zhang et al constructed a sodium alginate (SA) based hydrogel system incorporated with L-arginine-modified calcium phosphate/zinc phosphate nanoparticles (L-(CaP-ZnP) NPs).81 The sustained release of L-arginine, calcium ions and zinc ions from the hydrogel not only promoted odontoblastic differentiation and inhibited inflammatory response, but also enhanced the odontogenic differentiation ability of DPSCs. Liao et al integrated carbon-dot nanozymes (C-NZ) into gelatin methacryloyl (GelMA) to construct a composite therapeutic system (Figure 5).82 Benefiting from the high electron transfer efficiency and excellent catalytic activity of C-NZ, the composite system effectively scavenged reactive oxygen and nitrogen species (RONS). By reshaping the oxidative microenvironment of inflamed pulp tissue, the C-NZ/GelMA system provides a feasible approach for the realization of minimally invasive pulp regeneration. 0D nanoparticles can also assist in achieving deep disinfection of the root canal system. Given that mature biofilms often persist in lateral canals and dentinal tubules, effective penetration to the infected site is of greater clinical significance than achieving a high antibacterial rate in simple in vitro experiments. Guo et al co-loaded magnetic nanoparticles and the antibacterial monomer DMADDM into an EndoREZ root canal sealer.83 The application of an external magnetic field promoted the penetration of the nanoparticles within the root canal system, and the constructed therapeutic system effectively inhibited infection in an animal model of persistent apical periodontitis. Zeng et al integrated photodynamic bactericidal therapy with nitric oxide (NO) release, achieving disruption of root canal biofilms via complementary action of multiple mechanisms (Figure 6).84 Bukhari et al leveraged the peroxidase-like activity of iron oxide nanoparticles to enhance the killing effect on Enterococcus faecalis biofilms in extracted teeth and bacteria colonized within dentinal tubules in the presence of hydrogen peroxide (H2O2).85 After effective infection control, Dal-Fabbro et al applied biomimetic high-density lipoprotein nanoparticles for the treatment of periapical inflammation.86 In vivo animal experiments verified that this treatment reduced NF-κB activation, inflammatory cell infiltration, and alveolar bone resorption. Collectively, the existing studies systematically address three core issues in root canal treatment, deep targeted delivery, catalytic disinfection, and host inflammatory response regulation. Xu et al constructed a metal-phenolic nanozyme composite capable of on-demand release of copper-tannic acid nanosheets, which integrates triple functions of antibacterial activity, ROS scavenging and osteogenic promotion.87 Aiming to attenuate cytotoxic damage to host cells caused by excessive catalytic activity, Zhang et al modified manganese ferrite nanozymes via ligand engineering, and loaded the modified nanozymes into a hydrogel system (Figure 7).88 This system successfully maintains the inherent antioxidant activity while effectively reducing cytotoxicity. Jin et al further developed a macrophage-targeted self-assembled metal-polyphenol nanosystem.89 Following internalization by macrophages, the nanosystem can eliminate intracellular pathogens and remodel the local immune microenvironment. Adhesive matrix materials can effectively improve the local bioavailability of 0D nanoparticles. Hu et al incorporated MnO2 nanozymes into a catechol-modified hydrogel, which extends the in vivo retention time of the nanozymes and endows the composite system with multiple biological functions including ROS regulation, oxygen generation, and near-infrared photothermal therapy.90 In a rat model of PD, this system significantly alleviated local inflammatory response and reduced alveolar bone resorption. Li et al reported a pathogen-responsive bismuth-doped carbon dot/berberine therapeutic system.91 In this system, the metabolites secreted by P. gingivalis can trigger the degradation of the carrier, after which the loaded two therapeutic agents synergistically inhibit bacterial proliferation, reduce gingipain activity, and alleviate epithelial barrier damage. For periodontal tissue repair applications, Zong et al pretreated periodontal ligament stem cells with a gold nanoparticle composite, then embedded the pretreated cells in collagen scaffolds for transplantation.92 This treatment effectively reduced bone loss in a rat model of alveolar bone resorption.

One-Dimensional (1D) Nanomaterials

1D nanomaterials possess two dimensions confined at the nanoscale, with the third dimension being significantly extended.71 Common morphological categories of 1D nanomaterials include nanowires, nanorods, nanotubes and nanofibers, and representative examples are gold nanorods, metal nanowires, TiO2 nanotubes, carbon nanotubes, and electrospun nanofibers. In the treatment of Pul and PD, 1D nanomaterials are usually constructed into continuous fibrous networks or hollow drug-loading nanotubes, which are applied for local sustained drug delivery and serve as tissue engineering scaffolds. Their high aspect ratio and continuous topological structure can simulate the ECM microenvironment, and effectively support cell adhesion, migration and oriented arrangement. The electrospinning technique allows flexible regulation of fiber diameter, porosity, orientation and drug distribution, enabling the resulting material to integrate barrier function and sustained drug release capability.93,94

In Pul, 1D materials primarily connect root canal disinfection with the reconstruction of regenerative microenvironment. Palasuk et al co-loaded metronidazole and ciprofloxacin into polydioxanone nanofibers.95 The prepared nanofibers exhibited significant inhibitory effects against E. faecalis, P. gingivalis, and F. nucleatum, while did not induce obvious cytotoxicity to human dental pulp stem cells (hDPSCs), which supports the local antibacterial application of such materials in regenerative endodontics. Subsequently, Ribeiro et al loaded chlorhexidine into hollow halloysite nanotubes and dispersed the drug-loaded nanotubes in a MMP-responsive GelMA matrix (Figure 8).96 Infection-induced overexpressed MMPs degraded the responsive matrix, triggering the release of chlorhexidine-loaded nanotubes. In this system, the halloysite nanotubes realized drug storage and sustained release, while the GelMA matrix endowed the whole system with injectability and in-situ local fixation capability. In PD, 1D fibrous materials are primarily utilized for local drug delivery into periodontal pockets and function as guided tissue regeneration membranes. Di et al successfully incorporated quercetin (QU) into polylactic acid-based nanofibers, and the results demonstrated that acidic microenvironments could promote drug release, inhibit biofilm maturation, and attenuate LPS-induced inflammatory responses in human gingival fibroblasts.97 As an adjuvant therapy to SRP, Chaturvedi et al applied metronidazole-loaded polycaprolactone (PCL) nanofibers into the periodontal pockets of patients with chronic PD.98 This nanofiber system achieved sustained drug release for approximately 11 days, and short-term clinical outcomes showed that it outperformed monotherapy with basic treatment in reducing probing depth, plaque index and gingival index. Li et al fabricated a chitosan/polyvinyl alcohol (PVA)/graphene oxide (GO) nanofibrous membrane loaded with astaxanthin, and the sustained release of astaxanthin from the membrane reduced inflammatory response and promoted the repair of alveolar bone defects in animal models.99 Deepak et al developed a type of electrospun fiber that incorporated a silver-metronidazole complex and nano-hydroxyapatite (nHA).100 The silver-metronidazole complex exerted sustained broad-spectrum antibacterial activity, while nHA provided a biomimetic inorganic component for filling periodontal bone defects.

Two-Dimensional (2D) Nanomaterials

2D nanomaterials are defined as layered or sheet-structured nanomaterials that only have one dimension confined at the nanoscale, with the thickness generally less than 5 nm, while the other two orthogonal dimensions extend to mesoscopic or macroscopic scale. This class of materials has drawn extensive research attention owing to its atomic-level thickness, ultra-high specific surface area, and abundant surface functional groups. For instance, graphene possesses a theoretical specific surface area of up to 2630 m2/g, which can provide sufficient space for drug loading and functional modification.101 Its typical derivative, GO, contains abundant carboxyl, hydroxyl and epoxy groups on its surface, which not only improves the aqueous dispersion of the material, but also provides reactive sites for covalent and non-covalent modification.102 In addition, Black phosphorus (BP) is a newly emerged 2D material with a layered structure and a narrow band gap. It has excellent photothermal conversion efficiency and electrical conductivity, making it suitable for photothermal therapy and biosensing applications. It is particularly crucial that BP can degrade gradually into non-toxic phosphate under physiological conditions, which constitutes a critical potential advantage in terms of biosafety.102,103

For Pul, Sun et al realized the in-situ assembly of GO on porous titanium substrates via microarc oxidation (MAO) to prepare the Ti-MAO-GO composite.104 In vitro experimental results indicated that modification with 1.0 mg/mL GO could upregulate the expression of dentin sialophosphoprotein (DSPP) and alkaline phosphatase (ALP), promote the odontogenic differentiation of hDPSCs, and enhance the formation of mineralized nodules. Kang et al extended this research strategy to transition-metal carbides and nitrides (MXenes).105 It was found that MXene nanosheets can promote the osteogenic and neural differentiation of mesenchymal stem cells through direct interaction with cell membranes and regulation of intracellular signaling pathways. This discovery provides a feasible approach for realizing neurovascular regeneration of DPC. To address the demands of dentinal tubule sealing and dentin remineralization, Wang et al incorporated BP nanosheets into GelMA via photocrosslinking. In this system, BP enables near-infrared (NIR) photothermal conversion, which can achieve local antibacterial therapy and regulate cellular activity (Figure 10).106 Meanwhile, the gradual oxidation of BP can release phosphate ions (PO43-), which induces local supersaturation of phosphate and further promotes hydroxyapatite nucleation and biomimetic remineralization. Ni et al fabricated silicene nanosheets via iodine intercalation exfoliation, and constructed a silica (SiO2)-coated silicene delivery system loaded with 2,2’-azobis(2-amidinopropane) dihydrochloride (AIPH).110 Under irradiation by 1064 nm light within the second near-infrared window (NIR-II), mild heating to 40–42 °C triggers the release of free radicals from AIPH. Jiang et al employed dopamine as both a reducing agent and a surface modification agent to synthesize rGO coated with polydopamine.63 This composite integrates the anti-inflammatory activity of polydopamine and the osteoinductive property of rGO. It can scavenge ROS, inhibit the overactivation of the NF-κB signaling pathway, and downregulate the expression of inflammatory mediators. In vivo studies demonstrate that this approach can achieve dynamic balance between osteoclast population suppression and new bone formation, which verifies the feasibility of the “anti-inflammatory-osteogenesis” synergistic treatment strategy.

Three-Dimensional (3D) Nanomaterials

3D nanomaterials refer to a class of structural materials assembled from D particles, 1D fibers, or 2D sheets via ordered assembly, in situ growth, or template-directed synthesis strategies.116 The main advantage of 3D nanomaterials lies in their ability to integrate macroscopic supporting structures with nanoscale interfacial biological activity. Although the overall dimensions of 3D nanomaterials exceed 100 nm, their internal pores, pore wall thicknesses, and crosslinking nodes still retain nanoscale dimensions. This hierarchical macro-nano porous architecture constitutes the defining structural feature of this class of nanomaterials.55 Common morphological types of 3D nanomaterials include porous microspheres, dendritic structures, foams, aerogels, crosslinked nanorod arrays, and hierarchical macro/meso/microporous structures. In Pul and PD, the interconnected pore structure and high specific surface area of 3D nanomaterials enable high-capacity loading and controlled release of antibacterial agents, growth factors, and anti-inflammatory drugs. Additionally, the graded pore structure of 3D nanomaterials can mimic the topological structure of ECM, and provide a suitable mechanical microenvironment for the adhesion, proliferation, and differentiation of DPSCs and PDLSCs. The interconnected pore network can reduce mass transfer resistance, and may alleviate the aggregation and rapid clearance issues that commonly occur in low-dimensional nanomaterials. In Pul, 3D MOF-derived nanozymes have been investigated as functional components of pulp-capping materials. Xie et al incorporated a chromium-aluminum bimetallic MOF-based nanozyme (MOF@Cr-Al) into GelMA to construct a photocurable injectable hydrogel system (Figure 13).117 In vitro cytotoxicity assays confirmed that the hydrogel did not reduce the viability of hDPSC or alter cell morphology even at a nanozyme concentration up to 500 μmol/L. 3D mesoporous silica materials are also utilized as delivery carriers in root canal treatment. Wang et al demonstrated that positively charged mesoporous calcium silicate nanoparticles (MCSNs) can bind to negatively charged bacterial cell membranes, and silver/zinc co-doping further enhances the antibiofilm activity of the nanoparticles.118 The silicate ions released from the material can induce dentin mineralization, and the inorganic dopants do not compromise the flexural strength or elastic modulus of the material. In addition, mesoporous silica nanoparticles (MSNs) can also be loaded with natural antibacterial agents such as rutin and curcumin, or conjugated with sonosensitizers such as protoporphyrin. These design strategies support the implementation of sonodynamic therapy (SDT), enable combined drug loading, and reduce the cytotoxicity of the system as well as the development of bacterial drug resistance. Further modification with redox-responsive groups endows the system with the capability of intracellular controlled release, which facilitates the eradication of E. faecalis biofilms and promotes dentin remineralization. Limitations in the Design and Application of Nanomaterials

Although nanosystems with different dimensionalities have exhibited promising therapeutic potential for Puls and PD, there remain substantial development barriers to their clinical translation (Figure 17). Currently, the selection of structural and performance parameters still relies heavily on trial-and-error strategies. Multiple parameters including chemical composition, particle size, morphology, metal valence, surface charge, surface coating, drug loading capacity and administered dose exhibit complex nonlinear interactions with each other. Meanwhile, clinically applicable biomaterials are required to satisfy multiple performance requirements simultaneously, including efficient antibacterial activity, favorable host biocompatibility, long-term local retention capacity, and pro-regenerative bioactivity. Single-factor experimental designs can only isolate individual local effects, but fail to uncover complex multiparameter interaction networks or determine the optimal efficacy-safety balance window.19,77 Furthermore, there is a general lack of unified standardized protocols for delivery system studies in terms of production batch preparation, reference strain selection, biofilm model construction and outcome evaluation, which greatly limits cross-study comparison and meta-analysis.126,127 Even for nanosystems that achieve satisfactory performance under specific experimental conditions, challenges still exist in terms of reproducible preparation, scalable manufacturing, and verification of comparative advantages over existing therapeutic alternatives.

Furthermore, following in vivo administration, nanomaterials are further required to maintain normal function within the dynamically changing oral microenvironment. Pulpal pH, oxidative stress level and enzyme activity all exhibit dynamic variations with the progression of inflammation. In periodontal pockets, nanomaterials additionally have to withstand the constant impact of gingival crevicular fluid flow, microbial community succession and mechanical loading. Although existing pH-, ROS-, or enzyme-responsive carriers can achieve triggered drug release at preset stimulus thresholds, a single chemical response trigger cannot accurately identify the specific pathological stage of the target lesion. Even staged nanosystems designed to sequentially complete infection control and osteogenic induction still adopt an open-loop regulation mode. Their regulatory program is fixed during the manufacturing process, and cannot adjust the drug dose or release timing in real time based on the actual lesion state of an individual patient.

Systematic long-term biosafety evaluations of such nanosystems also remain insufficient. Even though nanomaterials with improved antibacterial activity may achieve better therapeutic effects, uncontrolled release of metal ions, ectopic accumulation in soft tissues, excessive photothermal dosage and toxic degradation products may collectively narrow the host safety window.77 Currently, most existing evidence on biosafety is obtained from in vitro cell experiments or preclinical animal studies, and long-term follow-up data in human subjects are still lacking.128 In a triple-blind randomized controlled trial enrolling 60 patients diagnosed with symptomatic apical periodontitis, the addition of jasminum-derived TiO2 nanoparticles to calcium hydroxide dressing was found to improve pain control within 4–96 hours post-treatment. This finding confirms that this nanomaterial formulation can provide effective short-term symptom relief, but it fails to validate the long-term performance of the nanoparticles in terms of degradation behavior, in vivo biodistribution, metabolic pathways, and healing quality in periapical tissues.129

In short, conventional experience-based development strategies cannot adequately address the multiple challenges including multiparameter coupling, adaptation to dynamic lesions, real-time regulation and long-term biosafety. Therefore, a more systematic and intelligent data-driven research framework is urgently required to overcome these existing translational barriers.

This bottleneck essentially reflects the urgent demand of the current research and development system for deep integration of multi-source heterogeneous data and dynamic decision-making algorithms, and also lays a solid logical foundation for the extensive involvement of AI in the reverse design of nanomaterials, real-time lesion perception and intelligent therapeutic efficacy regulation. The core value of AI lies in its capability to explore the implicit correlations among material characterization data, in vitro results, in-situ sensing signals and clinicopathological characteristics, and to decode the nonlinear mapping rule of “structure-property-biological effect” from high-dimensional multi-source datasets, so as to empower high-throughput screening of candidate materials, real-time analysis of complex sensing signals and decision-making of personalized treatment schemes.

At present, the research paradigm of AI-driven nanosystem optimization has achieved groundbreaking advancement in non-oral medical and materials fields. For example, in the field of rational design of crystalline porous nanomaterials, Xie et al systematically summarized the innovative applications of generative AI algorithms, including Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs), Variational Autoencoders (VAEs), diffusion models and Reinforcement Learning (RL), in the structural design of MOFs, covalent organic frameworks (COFs) and zeolites.10 This category of algorithms can reversibly generate candidate material structures in accordance with target pore structure requirements or specific adsorption, separation and delivery performance demands, which exponentially reduces the chemical space that requires practical experimental synthesis and verification, and provides readily available algorithmic tools for addressing the core challenge of “difficulty in efficient screening of high-dimensional parameter spaces” encountered in oral nanomaterial development.

In the field of disease-specific nanotheranostics, Fan et al proposed a multi-omics data-driven nanomedicine development framework to address the challenge of tumor heterogeneity.20 Through integrating drug target information, physicochemical parameters of nanocarriers, and metabolic characteristics of the tumor microenvironment, they constructed a machine learning model to screen nanomedicine combinations that can precisely regulate tumor metabolic pathways, which provides an innovative research approach for overcoming chemotherapy resistance. Jeevanandam et al further incorporated molecular imaging, disease stratification, and intelligent nanodelivery systems, and proposed thatAI can be utilized to identify radiomics and biomarker signatures, so as to assist in screening nanotheranostic strategies capable of efficiently crossing the blood-brain barrier.21 This work provides important reference for targeted delivery adapted to the complex dynamic oral microenvironment and anatomical structure, as well as precise staging-based therapy.

In the field of nanosensing technology, Yin et al investigated high-dimensional spectral and imaging data acquired from nanoparticle superstructure photonic sensors, and verified that deep learning algorithms can effectively extract weak sensing signals and suppress background noise interference, which enables high-sensitivity detection of trace biomarkers.22 This study provides algorithmic support for the development of AI-enabled intelligent oral sensing systems.

Collectively, the aforementioned cross-disciplinary research and practical applications have fully verified that artificial intelligence can achieve profound empowerment throughout the entire research chain of nanomaterials, ranging from rational design to clinical evaluation. Accordingly, the application of artificial intelligence technologies to the development of nanotherapeutic strategies for oral diseases carries substantial academic significance and practical application value (Figure 18). AI-Enabled Rational Design of Nanomaterials

The value of AI-enabled design lies in its capacity to accurately identify key determinants of material performance in multi-parameter, highly coupled experimental design systems, thus breaking through the efficiency bottleneck of the traditional single-factor trial-and-error approach (Figure 19). Mirzaei et al integrated data from 60 in vitro research articles, and adopted multidimensional parameters including nanoparticle core size, surface physicochemical properties, exposure dose and target bacterial strain as input variables for model construction.130 The resultant machine learning model attained a coefficient of determination R2 of up to 0.78 in the validation dataset. Subsequent feature importance analysis indicated that core size, exposure dose and target bacterial strain are the three core variables that affect the prediction of the antibacterial performance of nanomaterials. This study confirms that the antibacterial efficacy of nanomaterials cannot be discussed independently of specific experimental conditions, among which the effects of strain specificity and dose dependence are particularly significant, and this feature is more worthy of attention in the polymicrobial microenvironment of the oral cavity. On this basis, standardized metadata collection specifications should be established in the field of oral nanomedicine to completely record key information including particle structural characterization, action concentration, contact time and target bacterial strain during experiments, so as to provide a solid data foundation for the construction of high-quality machine learning training datasets. Nevertheless, constrained by the inherent heterogeneity of different literature in terms of preparation process, antibacterial activity evaluation methods and experimental condition control, such models constructed based on integrated literature data are more applicable to the preliminary screening stage, and can be used to rapidly narrow the search range of candidate parameters.

To eliminate the interference of cross-study data heterogeneity on model performance, establishing refined prediction models within a single material system is recognized as a more reliable research strategy. Taking cerium-doped ZnO nanoparticles as the research object, Perfecto-Avalos et al systematically correlated the structural parameters, morphological characteristics, and optical properties of the material with its antibacterial activity against E. coli, S. aureus, and Pseudomonas aeruginosa.131 The results showed that the prediction error of the Extremely Randomized Trees model can be as low as 1.8%, enabling high-accuracy identification of the key parameter combinations that determine antibacterial performance. Although this study did not include the microbiota associated with Pul or PD, its methodological contribution lies in the complete demonstration of a closed-loop optimization process of “doping and preparation parameter adjustment-antibacterial outcome prediction-confirmatory experimental validation”. For common oral material systems such as nHA and Ce-MBGNs, the aforementioned strategy can be referenced in future work to screen particle size, doping ratio, and exposure dose. Nevertheless, the endpoint evaluation indicators of the model need to be shifted from the single-strain bacteriostatic rate to indicators with higher clinical translational value, such as multi-species biofilm clearance rate, ROS regulation ability, and biocompatibility with DPSCs and PDLSCs.

Furthermore, Bilgi et al collated 2077 data points corresponding to 193 Au nanoparticle samples across 59 studies, and adopted ensemble learning paradigms including random forest and gradient boosting tree to establish a prediction model for the cellular uptake efficiency of nanoparticles.132 The experimental results indicated that the optimized model could explain 80%–90% of the total variance in the test dataset, and identified particle size, ζ-potential, particle concentration and exposure time as the dominant contributors to cellular uptake efficiency. This work provides a quantitative foundation for the safety-oriented design of nanomedical materials. Nevertheless, it should be emphasized that the training dataset of the model covers a heterogeneous collection of cell sources, and the derived prediction results cannot be directly extrapolated to DPSCs, PDLSCs or periodontal macrophages. For research targeting the application scenarios of Pul and PD, it is necessary to construct domain-specific datasets based on relevant oral cell types, and synchronously record multi-dimensional information including cellular uptake kinetics, cell viability, cellular inflammatory phenotype, mineralized nodule formation ability and expression level of odontoblastic differentiation markers, so as to eliminate the simplistic linear cognitive bias that blindly assumes a positive correlation between cellular uptake level and therapeutic effect.

Regarding optimization objectives, there exist distinct differences requirements between the therapeutic regimens for Pul and PD. Pul prioritizes the penetration depth of nanoparticles into dentinal tubules, root canal lateral branches and the apical triangular region, together with local retention duration, anti-biofilm activity, and biosafety toward periapical tissues.77,126 By comparison, for PD, simultaneous assessments are required for nanomaterials in terms of drug retention capacity within periodontal pockets, stability against gingival crevicular fluid irrigation, immunomodulatory function, and the potential to promote alveolar bone regeneration and repair.126,127 Accordingly, a standardized workflow for AI-enabled nanomaterial design should first set minimum admission thresholds for cytocompatibility, ion release rate, and tissue safety. Among candidate materials that satisfy these safety specifications, comparative analysis is further performed on antibacterial efficacy, anti-inflammatory activity, tissue penetration capacity, local retention capability, and the ability to promote tissue regeneration. In scenarios where the initial sample size is insufficient, the model can screen the next batch of formulations with the maximum information content based on an active learning strategy for experimental verification. Newly obtained experimental data are subsequently introduced to complete iterative updating of the model, enabling the solution to gradually approach the global optimum. The finally screened candidate materials still need to undergo systematic verification using independent preparation batches, multi-species biofilm models, and corresponding in vivo models.

The complexity of the oral microenvironment, characterized by the intermixing of saliva, gingival crevicular fluid, and blood components as well as constant vulnerability to interference from daily physiological activities including feeding and vocalization, renders sensing signals inevitably subjected to severe high background noise and baseline drift. Taking the detection of MMP-8, a key biomarker for PD, as an illustrative example, Jeon et al constructed an electrochemical sensor based on molecularly imprinted polymers (MIPs) with GO employed as the conductive interface.129 In this study, electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) measurements were transformed into standardized dual-channel images, and a convolutional neural network (CNN) regression model was applied to achieve concentration prediction of MMP-8, followed by transfer learning for the classification and diagnosis of clinical saliva samples. In the proposed system, each functional module performs clearly defined functions. GO undertakes the roles of signal conduction and interface stabilization, MIP recognition sites are responsible for target-specific binding, and the deep learning model executes the tasks of signal denoising, quantitative regression, and clinical classification. The effectiveness of this research has been verified via experiments on clinical saliva samples collected from patients, and it stands as a comparatively complete “nanointerface-biomarker- algorithm output” research paradigm in the PD research field. In a similar manner, Wang et al coupled a peptide-responsive photonic crystal hydrogel to a deep-learning-assisted smartphone application for MMP-9 detection, affording both visual and quantitative readouts within 10 min at a detection limit of 0.62 ng/mL, with results showing close concordance with ELISA in complex biological fluids, including saliva.133 Because salivary MMP-9 is an established biomarker of PD, this system directly links a material-level optical response to a clinically meaningful analyte, thereby completing the nanointerface-biomarker-algorithm output chain.

A further challenge to the oral microenvironment lies in its pronounced dynamic nature. The pH level and concentrations of specific bacterial metabolites within the microenvironment undergo continuous fluctuations, driven by food intake, salivary secretion cycles, and circadian rhythms. Conventional single-point measurement approaches are limited in capturing such dynamic spatiotemporal characteristics. Alternatively, nanosensor-based continuous monitoring integrated with time-series deep learning models offers a promising solution to this limitation. Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs), their derivative Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks, and the Transformer architecture exhibit advantages in processing sequential continuous sensing signals. These models can effectively learn long-term dependencies and periodic patterns embedded in time-series data, thereby enabling three functional capabilities: i) Trend prediction: forecasting future caries risk windows based on historical pH fluctuation data. ii) Anomaly detection: identifying abrupt signal variations that indicate acute exacerbation of periodontal inflammation within periodontal pockets from baseline drift. iii) Signal denoising: eliminating transient interference introduced by physiological activities including eating and speaking, and extracting feature signals that authentically reflect pathological states. As a representative example, in real-time monitoring of the characteristic metabolites of P. gingivalis, the LSTM model can recognize specific signal patterns associated with bacterial activity from complex background noise, which provides dynamic support for the early warning of PD.

Colorimetric sensor arrays constructed from nanomaterials and quantum dot fluorescence arrays have attracted extensive attention in the field of oral point-of-care testing (POCT) owing to their ease of operation and capability to deliver visually distinguishable detection results. However, interfering components contained in oral samples, including pigments, food residues, and mucin, typically introduce remarkable interference to optical detection signals. Computer vision (CV), particularly CNNs, provides an efficient solution to this problem. Through sufficient model training, CV is able to automatically extract subtle signal variations of nanoscale colorimetric sensors or fluorescence arrays from complex background interference, realizing high-sensitivity quantitative detection of multiple biomarkers such as MMP-8, IL-1β, and P. gingivalis-specific antibodies. In comparison with manual visual interpretation or conventional simple RGB thresholding methods, CV models can learn the nonlinear combination characteristics of hue, saturation, and value in the color space, which significantly improves detection accuracy and result reproducibility for complex oral samples.

In the domain of oral imaging diagnostics, AI has exhibited extraordinary potential in translating complex pathological features into quantifiable metrics. Orhan et al input 153 cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) images of periapical lesions obtained from 109 patients into a CNN model.130 The model successfully identified 142 lesions, reaching a detection rate of 92.8%, and the measured lesion volumes exhibited strong consistency with the manual segmentation results. Based on panoramic dental radiographs of 8462 teeth from 320 patients, Xue et al adopted a combined framework of YOLOv8, Mask R-CNN, and TransUNet to complete tooth positioning, tissue segmentation, alveolar bone loss measurement, and periodontitis staging, with an overall diagnostic accuracy of 89.45%.131 Although the aforementioned imaging AI models do not intrinsically integrate nanomaterials, they are capable of converting the scope of periapical lesions or the degree of periodontal bone loss into quantifiable and traceable indicators of disease status. In the future, the integration of imaging AI and localized nanosensing technology is expected to construct a complementary multimodal diagnostic system. Specifically, imaging can provide structural dimension information of tissue damage, while nanosensors supplement functional dimension information of inflammatory alteration, and the two modalities jointly establish a more comprehensive characterization profile of disease.

For PD, a single biomarker detection result cannot provide sufficient evidence to guide the determination of treatment intensity. Consequently, implementing longitudinal dynamic monitoring and realizing multimodal data integration constitute the approach to improving the predictive performance of disease progression models. In the multicenter longitudinal cohort study conducted by Furquim et al, a total of 113 periodontally healthy participants and 302 PD patients were enrolled, with a 12-month follow-up completed.132 Clinical indicators, including probing depth and clinical attachment loss, along with demographic characteristics and 10 salivary analytes, were incorporated into the predictive model for periodontitis progression. The results of this study demonstrated that the probabilistic graphical model that integrates clinical measurements, salivary IL-1β levels, age, and sex achieved an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) of 0.88, and its predictive performance outperformed that of the standalone multilayer perceptron (MLP) model. This work reveals that nanosensors are particularly suitable for serving as one of the core input components for multimodal prediction models.

All in all, the key breakthrough in this field lies in the construction of a “closed-loop regulation” system. In this framework, nanosensors first realize real-time monitoring of the key parameters of the oral microenvironment. Subsequently, an AI model integrates sensor-derived data, imaging information and clinical medical records to achieve comprehensive evaluation of disease status. Ultimately, the system triggers a targeted therapeutic response based on the assessment and decision-making outputs, which is implemented via a nanoscale platform. AI-Enabled Adaptive Regulation and Clinical Decision-Making

Notably, the implementation of a “closed-loop regulation” decision-making framework necessitates the application of AI to empower nanoscale systems, facilitating their functional transformation from “passive monitors” to “active interveners” (Figure 21). Specifically, AI can not only identify disease statuses, but also dynamically adjust the therapeutic protocol of the nanoscale system based on real-time monitoring feedback. In this respect, RL exhibits distinct application advantages. The core mechanism of RL is that an agent learns the optimal decision strategy through continuous interactions with the environment, based on reward and penalty signals generated by environmental feedback.128 For Pul and PD, the local inflammatory biomarker concentration, pH value, and reactive oxygen species level continuously acquired by nanosensors can be used as state inputs for the reinforcement learning model. The action set output by the model includes decisions such as adjusting drug release rate and determining the optimal timing of pulsed drug administration. When the nanosensor detects a decrease in local inflammatory activity at the lesion site or an increase in the concentration of tissue repair-related biomarkers, the model receives a positive reward signal and thus retains the current administration strategy. In the opposite case, the model reduce the drug release rate, which helps reduce the risk of drug resistance and local toxicity caused by traditional fixed-dose administration regimens. This mechanism enables adaptive adjustment of treatment protocols in response to dynamic changes in the individual microenvironment of patients, overcoming the performance limitations in conventional “predefined protocol”-based stimulus responsive materials. Furthermore, closed-loop machine learning frameworks such as “Lab-in-the-Loop” have shown the potential to achieve autonomous optimization and significantly improve iterative efficiency through the closed-loop integration of biological data and deep RL, which provides a methodological foundation for the dynamic regulation of oral nanomedicines.134

Besides, the application value of AI-enabled therapeutic decision-making is further embodied in the development of integrated Clinical Decision Support Systems (CDSS).135 Such systems achieve multimodal data fusion by integrating information stored in patients’ electronic medical records (EMR), including systemic health status, medication history, and previous treatment response profiles, in combination with CBCT imaging data and continuous monitoring outputs obtained via nanosensors. This data integration enables the system to provide clinicians with individualized therapeutic decision suggestions. For instance, for elderly patients with chronic PD complicated by diabetes mellitus, CDSS can comprehensively analyze multiple relevant factors, namely the patient’s glycemic control level, periodontal probing indicators, local inflammatory load detected by subgingival nanosensors, and the patient’s historical tissue response to specific nano root canal sealants, and then output structured decision-making suggestions such as “whether the patient is indicated for this nano root canal sealant” or “the recommended drug loading concentration”.

For the long-term safety assessment of nanoscale systems, the AI-based evaluation framework can be implemented progressively along a pathway spanning from offline performance evaluation to real-world clinical utility verification. In the algorithm development phase, training datasets and test datasets shall be stratified at the patient level, and the model’s discriminative performance, calibration performance, external validation results, and bias risk assessment outcomes shall be fully reported.136,137 Second, a “toxicity penalty term” should be integrated into the reward function design for RL and the risk assessment module of the clinical decision support system. If the model predicts that the cumulative concentration of nanoparticles approaches the toxicity threshold or induces a risk of microbial dysbiosis, the system is required to automatically assign a negative reward and downregulate the administered dose. Finally, the DECIDE-AI statement provides a standardized reporting framework for the early evaluation of AI-powered clinical decision support systems.138 The CONSORT-AI statement explicitly stipulates that, when conducting AI-involved interventional clinical trials, it is mandatory to elaborate in detail on the input and output formats of the algorithm, human-computer interaction mechanisms, and contingency plans for algorithm failure.139

Conclusions and Future Perspectives

Pul and PD are prevalent infectious oral diseases, and pulp-periodontal tissue destruction caused by these two conditions is the leading cause of tooth loss. For the development of targeted nanotherapeutic systems for Pul and PD, it is necessary to take the common pathological progression pathway of “infection-inflammation-tissue destruction” as the fundamental basis, while fully considering the significant differences between the pulpal cavity microenvironment and the open periodontal pocket microenvironment. Based on the above, this study classifies nanomaterials according to their 0D-3D dimensional characteristics, aiming to clarify how the material properties of different dimensions can be utilized to address specific clinical therapeutic difficulties.

Specifically, 0D nanoparticles have extremely small hydrodynamic size, high specific surface area and are easy to achieve surface functionalization, thus they can efficiently penetrate dentinal tubules and reach the deep part of complex root canal systems. They mainly realize targeted antibacterial activity, ROS scavenging and on-demand drug release through mechanisms such as metal ion release, nanozyme catalysis and microenvironment-responsive phase transition. 1D nanofibers and nanotubes, by virtue of their significant anisotropic properties, can accurately regulate drug delivery and cell adhesion behavior along the axial direction, which provides a structural basis for localized long-acting drug release, contact-guided cell growth. Certain 1D nanomaterials with photothermal conversion capability can further improve the therapeutic effect against deep-seated infections through near-infrared light-controlled thermal action. Benefiting from their in-plane isotropy and out-of-plane confined two-dimensional topological characteristics, 2D nanosheet materials provide huge active interfaces and high loading capacity, and can realize efficient loading and integration of therapeutic drugs, functional ions or photothermal components. The unique edge effect and abundant interfacial reactivity of some 2D materialssupport the multi-mechanism synergistic antibacterial effect that combines physical destruction and chemical killing. 3D nanocomposite networks construct highly interconnected pore structures and simulate ECM-like microenvironments, which can realize localized high loading of bioactive factors, optimization of mechanical support, and time-series staged drug release. This design effectively maintains the spatial framework required for tissue regeneration, and is particularly suitable for providing support for reparative cells and promoting structural reconstruction of complex pulp-periodontal complexes. These nanomaterials with different dimensional characteristics can be designed to achieve functional complementarity according to different clinical needs, and jointly form a multi-dimensional material science solution for pulp-periodontal tissue regeneration.

Although dimensionality-based differentiated nanomaterial design has provided diverse therapeutic strategies for Pul and PD, the efficiency of its clinical translation still relies heavily on the nonlinear coupling of multiple variables, including intrinsic material parameters, administration dosage, in vivo degradation kinetics, and lesion pathological staging. Reliable guidance for determining optimal intervention strategies cannot be achieved by relying solely on single-dimensional characteristics or isolated in vitro endpoint indicators. The traditional trial-and-error research and development paradigm is incapable of elucidating the interactive relationships among multiple parameters, nor can it predict the dynamic behavior of nanomaterials in the complex oral microenvironment. This has led to a consistently low translation efficiency of candidate nanomaterials during the transformation from laboratory research to clinical application. The introduction of AI technology has created a breakthrough opportunity to bridge the translational gap between “material design” and “clinical efficacy”. At the level of rational design, AI can employ machine learning and deep learning algorithms to systematically excavate the complex structure-activity relationships between the physicochemical properties of materials and their biological effects, which promotes the transformation of the research and development paradigm from the traditional empirical trial-and-error approach to data-driven reverse precision design. At the level of data analysis, AI can efficiently process multimodal high-dimensional biological and clinical data, and accurately identify the dynamic evolution pattern of the disease microenvironment and core intervention targets. At the level of regulatory decision-making, AI can optimize the spatiotemporal distribution and administration strategy of drug delivery by establishing intelligent prediction models, thereby providing standardized scientific decision support for personalized diagnosis and treatment as well as closed-loop feedback therapy. In general, the deep integration of AI technology not only substantially shortens the research and development and iterative cycle of new nano-therapeutic systems, but also strongly promotes the clinical application of Pul and PD treatment toward a more precise, intelligent and personalized direction.

Looking ahead, AI-driven nanotheranostic systems are set to emerge as the mainstream development direction in the clinical treatment of Pul and PD. AI can decipher the nonlinear correlations between high-dimensional material parameters and macroscopic biological properties via deep learning algorithms, thereby enabling global optimization and rapid iteration of nanomaterial formulations. The integration of material structural features, clinical imaging information, and biomarker data facilitates the construction of standardized multimodal datasets. Further, with the aid of transfer learning and explainable artificial intelligence (XAI) techniques, the environmental adaptability and clinical interpretability of the models can be continuously enhanced. Ultimately, propelled by the deep interdisciplinary convergence of materials science, biomedical engineering, and AI, the clinical treatment of Pul and PD will embrace in a new chapter of transition from empirical medicine to precision intelligent medicine.
