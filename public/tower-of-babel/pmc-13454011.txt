Development and validation of an interpretable machine learning model for predicting postoperative complications after transanal total mesorectal excision: a multicenter study
Authors: Z. Jingtao, Y. Jun, C. Shaoqin, L. Chunlin, C. Zihang, L. Weilong, L. Zhixiong, Z. Guangwei, Y. Jianxin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis study aimed to develop and externally validate an interpretable machine learning (ML) model for predicting postoperative complications after transanal total mesorectal excision (taTME).MethodsWe conducted a multicenter case–control study of 245 consecutive patients undergoing taTME at three centers in China. Patients from one center formed the development cohort (n = 185); two independent centers provided an external validation cohort (n = 60). The primary endpoint was any 30-day complication (Clavien–Dindo). Candidate variables were screened with least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) and entered into multivariable logistic regression to define features. In total, 11 ML algorithms were trained with tenfold cross-validation; the final model was selected by area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve (AUC). Discrimination, calibration, decision-curve analysis (DCA), and clinical-impact curves were assessed. Model interpretability was examined with Shapley additive explanations (SHAP).ResultsOverall, 27.8% of patients experienced postoperative complications. Six predictors—postoperative C-reactive protein (CRP), preoperative total protein (TP), smoking history, age group (> 80 years), neoadjuvant therapy, and ASA score—were retained. The AdaBoost classifier performed best (development AUC 0.822; 95% CI, 0.751–0.892). External validation demonstrated higher discrimination (AUC 0.872; 95% CI, 0.771–0.972), with accuracy 83.3%, sensitivity 56.25%, and specificity 94.7%. Calibration and DCA supported clinical utility, and clinical-impact curves indicated favorable threshold behavior. The SHAP analysis identified postoperative CRP as the most influential predictor and provided transparent, individualized risk explanations.ConclusionsWe developed and externally validated a robust and interpretable ML model to predict complications following taTME. By leveraging routinely available clinical variables and providing transparent predictive explanations, this model can aid clinicians in identifying high-risk patients for targeted interventions, thereby potentially improving perioperative outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10151-026-03344-2.

Background

Colorectal cancer is the third most common malignancy globally, with 1.9 million new cases in 2020 [1]. Rectal cancer accounts for more than one third of these cases [1], typically treated with a multidisciplinary approach, where total mesorectal excision (TME) is the standard for mid–low rectal cancers [2]. Laparoscopic TME is favored for its quicker recovery and lower complication rates, with comparable long-term outcomes to open surgery [3, 4]. However, it faces challenges in obese male patients with narrow pelvic anatomy, potentially affecting resection quality and increasing complications and conversion rates [5]. Transanal total mesorectal excision (taTME) offers a solution with its “bottom-up” approach, overcoming anatomical limitations of laparoscopic techniques in challenging cases such as narrow pelvis, obesity, or bulky tumors [6]. By improving distal rectum visualization, taTME allows precise transection and better margin identification for optimal resection [7]. Despite this, safety concerns have been raised, particularly regarding local recurrence, prompting a nationwide moratorium in Norway [8, 9]. However, the TaLaR trial demonstrated that taTME achieves noninferior 3-year disease-free survival compared with laparoscopic surgery, confirming its oncological safety [10].

While oncologic concerns have been partially addressed, perioperative safety has attracted increasing attention. Approximately 30% of patients experience postoperative complications after taTME, with anastomosis-related events accounting for about 15%, including pelvic collections, sinus tract formation, anastomotic leakage, and stenosis [11, 12]. These complications impair recovery, prolong hospitalization, and increase healthcare utilization. Most current research focuses on risk factors for anastomotic leakage after taTME [13, 14], However, risk factors for overall postoperative morbidity and validated clinical prediction models for composite complications remain underexplored.

Machine learning (ML) is capable of identifying complex, nonlinear patterns in high-dimensional clinical data, enabling accurate predictions with high reliability [15, 16]. It has been successfully applied to predict surgical risks, including those in gastric cancer procedures [17, 18]. However, despite its potential, there are currently no ML models specifically developed to predict complications in taTME. The “black-box” nature of ML models, which limits transparency, poses challenges for clinical trust and widespread adoption [19]. Shapley additive explanations (SHAP) offer a solution to this issue by combining optimal credit allocation with local explanation analysis, allowing for the quantification of each variable’s contribution to the model’s output, thus enhancing interpretability and fostering greater clinical acceptance [20].

This study presents the first development and validation of an interpretable machine learning model for predicting postoperative complications following taTME, utilizing multicenter data and SHAP-based explanations to assist clinicians in high-risk patient identification and individualized intervention.

Material and methods

Data source

This multicenter case–control study included 245 patients who underwent taTME between February 2017 and December 2024 at three high-volume cancer surgery centers in China: the First Affiliated Hospital of Fujian Medical University, the First Affiliated Hospital of Xiamen University, and Mengchao Hepatobiliary Hospital of Fujian Medical University. The training cohort comprised 185 patients from the First Affiliated Hospital of Fujian Medical University, and the external validation cohort consisted of 60 patients from the other two centers.

Inclusion criteria were: (1) histologically confirmed rectal adenocarcinoma; (2) tumor located in the mid-to-lower rectum is defined as one with its inferior margin within 10 cm from the anal verge or positioned below the peritoneal reflection, as confirmed by pre-neoadjuvant therapy magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) or, if MRI was unavailable, by colonoscopy, computed tomography (CT), or digital rectal examination; (3) no invasion of adjacent organs and no distant metastasis; and (4) adequate cardiopulmonary function. Exclusion criteria were: (1) contraindications to laparoscopy; (2) emergency presentation (obstruction, perforation, or hemorrhage); (3) planned multiorgan resection; and (4) incomplete clinical or pathologic data. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the First Affiliated Hospital of Fujian Medical University (Approval No. XMYY-2024KY034). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants or their legal guardians.

Surgical technique

All procedures were performed by experienced gastrointestinal surgical teams. taTME was undertaken as a laparoscopic-assisted procedure with simultaneous abdominal and transanal teams; both teams had experience with > 200 laparoscopic rectal resections. All operations adhered to the principles of total mesorectal excision [21]. Patients received mechanical bowel preparation the day before surgery and intravenous prophylactic antibiotics within 60 min before incision.

Data collection

Clinical and pathologic data were extracted from electronic medical records at each center and independently verified by two investigators. The following variables were collected: sex, age, height, weight, body mass index (BMI), comorbidities (hypertension, diabetes), prior abdominal surgery, smoking history, American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) classification, neoadjuvant therapy, and tumor distance from the anal verge (measured by MRI, or by colonoscopy/CT when MRI was unavailable). Preoperative laboratory parameters obtained within 7 days before surgery included serum albumin (ALB), total protein (TP), hemoglobin (HB), alpha-fetoprotein (AFP), carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA), carbohydrate antigen 19-9 (CA19-9), carbohydrate antigen 125 (CA125), and glucose. Intraoperative variables included operative times (abdominal and transanal), anastomotic technique (manual or stapled), anastomotic height from the anal verge, number of lymph nodes harvested, estimated blood loss, and creation of a protective ileostomy. Postoperative measures included C-reactive protein (CRP) on postoperative day 1 and pathologic characteristics such as tumor diameter and pathologic stage.

Anastomotic leakage was defined as a defect in the integrity of the intestinal wall at the anastomosis site (including both suture and staple lines of neorectal reservoirs) that allowed communication between the intra- and extraluminal compartments, as per the definition and grading criteria of the International Study Group of Rectal Cancer [22]. This diagnosis was confirmed by rectal contrast radiographic evidence of extravasation or by digital rectal examination within 30 days postoperatively.

The primary endpoint was any complication within 30 days after surgery, graded according to the Clavien–Dindo classification [23]: grade I, no pharmacologic treatment beyond routine agents (e.g., antipyretics/analgesics); grade II, requiring additional pharmacologic therapy; grade III, requiring surgical, endoscopic, or radiologic intervention; grade IV, life-threatening complication; and grade V, death.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables with normal distributions were summarized as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and compared between groups using the t-test. Non-normally distributed continuous or ordinal variables were summarized as median (interquartile range, IQR) and compared using nonparametric tests (Mann–Whitney U). Categorical variables were reported as counts and percentages and compared using the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate.

Feature selection was performed with least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression. By imposing an L1 penalty on the sum of the absolute regression coefficients, LASSO conducts variable selection and dimensionality reduction, removing irrelevant or redundant predictors and mitigating overfitting, thereby improving model robustness and generalizability [24]. Variables retained by LASSO were then entered into multivariable logistic regression, and predictors with P < 0.05 were kept as the final model features.

We trained and evaluated 11 machine-learning algorithms: random forest, gradient boosting, support vector machine (SVM), logistic regression, K-nearest neighbors (KNN), neural network, extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost), naive Bayes, discriminant analysis, LASSO regression, and adaptive boosting (AdaBoost). Tenfold cross-validation was used for model development and internal evaluation. Hyperparameters were tuned by grid search, and the final model was selected according to the highest area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve (AUC). All models were trained on the training set and assessed on an independent validation set. Discriminative performance was summarized by AUC, sensitivity, specificity, precision, F1 score, and accuracy. Clinical utility was evaluated using decision-curve analysis (DCA) and calibration plots. Clinical-impact curves (CICs) were constructed to determine the optimal decision threshold and to appraise the model’s potential value in practice [25].

Model interpretability was examined using SHAP. Population-level summary plots were generated to depict the contribution of each feature to the predicted outcome. For representative cases, SHAP force plots illustrated feature-level effects on individual predictions, thereby clarifying the model’s decision process.

Statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS software for Windows (version 26.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and R software (version 4.5.1; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

Baseline characteristics

The study cohort comprised 245 patients; 68 (27.8%) developed postoperative complications and 177 (72.2%) remained complication-free. Pulmonary infection was the most frequent complication (n = 24, 9.8%). Anastomosis-related events—including leakage, hemorrhage, and stenosis—occurred in 20 patients (8.1%). According to the Clavien–Dindo classification, the distribution of complications was as follows: grade I in 21 patients (8.6%), grade II in 31 patients (12.7%), grade III in 14 patients (5.7%), grade IV in 2 patients (0.8%), and grade V in no patients (0%). Overall, 16 patients (6.5%) experienced severe complications (Clavien–Dindo ≥ III).

Table 1 summarizes clinicopathological characteristics for the entire cohort. The population included 166 males (68.4%) with a mean age of 60.0 ± 11.1 years. The median BMI was 23.1 (21.1–25.3) kg/m2. Comorbidities and risk factors included smoking history in 36 patients (14.7%), hypertension in 61 (24.9%), diabetes mellitus in 36 (14.7%), and previous abdominal surgery in 16 (6.5%). Preoperative neoadjuvant therapy was administered to 66 patients (26.9%). The median operative time was 220.0 (195.0–250.7) minutes, with a median blood loss of 50.0 (40.0–50.0) mL. A protective ileostomy was performed in 210 patients (85.7%). According to postoperative TNM staging, tumor stages were distributed as follows: stage 0 in 4 patients (1.3%), stage I in 98 (40.0%), stage II in 62 (25.3%), stage III in 77 (31.4%), and stage IV in 4 (1.3%). Feature selection

All independent variables were screened using LASSO regression. The penalty parameter was chosen by tenfold cross-validation to minimize prediction error. LASSO produced a coefficient-path plot of variable selection (Fig. 2A) and a cross-validated plot identifying the optimal λ (Fig. 2B). Model development and performance on the training set

The six independent risk factors (age group, smoking history, neoadjuvant therapy, TP, CRP, and ASA score) were used as inputs to 11 machine-learning algorithms to develop a predictive model for complications after taTME. As shown in the forest plot (Fig. 3A), all models demonstrated acceptable discrimination; the AdaBoost classifier performed best, achieving an AUC of 0.822 (95% CI, 0.751–0.892). Random forest and a boosting method followed closely, both with an AUC of 0.809. ROC curves corroborated these findings (Fig. 3B), and additional performance metrics are summarized in Fig. 3C. On the basis of this performance, we selected AdaBoost as the final model for external validation. Discussion

TaTME has transformed the management of mid-to-low rectal cancer through its distinctive “bottom-up” approach. By enhancing visualization of the distal rectum, it effectively overcomes the anatomic constraints of conventional laparoscopy in patients with a narrow pelvis, obesity, or bulky tumors [6, 7]. Although the TaLaR randomized controlled trial established the oncologic safety of taTME [10], the procedure’s technical complexity and steep learning curve continue to contribute to substantial postoperative morbidity [26]. Our observed complication rate of 27.8% aligns with prior taTME series [12, 27–29], underscoring persistent safety concerns surrounding this technique.

This multicenter study presents the first interpretable ML model, developed and validated across multiple institutions, to predict 30-day complications after taTME. By integrating the SHAP framework, our model provides transparent, global, and individualized explanations for each prediction, fostering clinical trust [30]. Leveraging the AdaBoost algorithm and six readily available variables, the model demonstrated superior discriminative performance, robust generalizability, and significant clinical utility.

Inflammatory responses are central to postoperative complications in colorectal surgery [31]. SHAP analysis identified postoperative CRP as the most influential predictor after taTME. As a sensitive acute-phase protein, elevated CRP reflects the magnitude of systemic inflammation after surgical trauma [32], and prior studies have linked CRP monitoring to serious complications such as anastomotic leakage [33, 34]. Notably, the present model incorporates postoperative day-1 CRP to identify patients at risk within 24 h after surgery, prior to the onset of clinically apparent deterioration, thereby enabling proactive rather than reactive management.High postoperative CRP may indicate occult anastomotic microleakage, pelvic infection, or tissue ischemia, each of which can amplify systemic inflammation [35]. Our multivariable approach highlights the synergistic value of combining CRP with other clinical features to provide a more comprehensive risk assessment than any single biomarker. TP also emerged as a crucial predictor, reflecting nutritional status and systemic inflammation. Low protein states—including hypoalbuminemia—indicate limited physiologic reserve and impaired tissue repair and immune function [36]. Prior evidence shows that reduced protein/albumin levels exacerbate inflammatory responses, promote infection, and portend worse outcomes in colorectal cancer [37, 38]. The combination of elevated CRP and low TP may identify a vulnerable immunonutritional state that substantially increases complication risk [37]. Smoking history independently predicted postoperative complications in our cohort. Nicotine and carbon monoxide cause vasoconstriction, reduce tissue perfusion, and induce cellular hypoxia, collectively impairing oxygen delivery and delaying wound healing [38]. Smoking also damages respiratory epithelium, impairs mucociliary clearance, and disrupts alveolar macrophage function, increasing the risk of pulmonary complications [36]. Neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy introduces further challenges by inducing tissue edema and vascular alterations in the operative field, thereby increasing technical difficulty, prolonging operative time, and delaying postoperative tissue repair—all of which can raise complication risk [39]. Advanced age, particularly beyond 80 years, was another independent predictor. Older patients typically exhibit reduced organ reserve, slower tissue repair, and attenuated immune responses, which heighten vulnerability to surgical stress [40, 41]. In the technically demanding, confined operative space of taTME, limited tolerance of prolonged pneumoperitoneum and positional changes, together with increased pelvic tissue fragility, may further magnify risk in this population. Finally, the ASA classification has become integral to perioperative assessment and correlates closely with postoperative complications and mortality [42, 43]; our findings confirm that higher ASA scores are associated with increased complication rates after taTME.

While prior studies in taTME have predominantly focused on single outcomes such as anastomotic leakage, our model captures the broader spectrum of postoperative morbidity. When compared with traditional logistic regression models often reported in literature [27], our AdaBoost-based approach demonstrated a potent ability to capture intricate interactions between variables. The model’s high specificity is particularly valuable in clinical practice, as it efficiently identifies high-risk patients with a low rate of false positives, facilitating the targeted allocation of preventive resources.The primary value of this predictive tool lies in its potential to guide personalized postoperative management. The model’s output, complemented by SHAP explanations, can shift clinical decision-making from reactive to proactive. For instance, for a high-risk patient, a clinician can drill down into the contributing factors: if low preoperative TP is a major driver, aggressive preoperative nutritional support could be initiated to optimize the patient’s metabolic state. Similarly, a high predicted risk driven by elevated postoperative CRP would warrant heightened vigilance, potentially prompting earlier imaging, prolonged antibiotic coverage, or delayed oral feeding. This targeted, individualized approach aims to mitigate complications, enhance recovery, and reduce healthcare utilization.

To demonstrate the model’s practical utility, consider the profile of a representative patient: age 82 years (age group: ≥ 80), positive smoking history, history of neoadjuvant therapy, preoperative total protein 68 g/L, postoperative CRP 82 mg/L, and ASA class III. Using the nomogram (Supplementary Fig. S1), the clinician would assign points for each predictor on the basis of the nomogram axes, sum them, and locate the total on the bottom axis to estimate the predicted complication risk. For this profile, the estimated risk exceeds 70%. This high-risk estimate would prompt intensified postoperative monitoring, including more frequent clinical assessments, serial CRP measurements, and a low threshold for diagnostic imaging.

Several limitations should be considered. First, the retrospective design and exclusion of cases with incomplete data introduce selection bias and potential unmeasured confounding. Second, although external validation across independent centers strengthens our findings, the size of the external validation cohort remains limited (n = 60 with 17 complication events). Combined with the retrospective design and the regional origin of all data, this limitation may affect the model’s generalizability to broader populations and different healthcare settings. Therefore, our model should currently be regarded as a robust proof of concept. A critical and immediate next step is its prospective validation in larger, multiregional, and ethnically diverse cohorts before it can be recommended for routine clinical implementation. Additionally, given the limited number of severe complications (n = 16) in our cohort, we adopted a composite endpoint encompassing all complication grades; future studies should specifically prioritize severe complications as the primary outcome.

Conclusions

We developed and validated an interpretable machine learning model for predicting postoperative complications following taTME using multicenter data. The AdaBoost model, incorporating six clinical variables, demonstrated strong performance (AUC 0.822 in development, 0.872 in validation) and high specificity, making it valuable for identifying high-risk patients and enabling targeted interventions. Key predictors included postoperative CRP, preoperative TP, smoking history, neoadjuvant therapy, age group (> 80 years), and ASA score. By integrating SHAP, the model offers both global and individualized risk assessments, enhancing clinical decision-making.
