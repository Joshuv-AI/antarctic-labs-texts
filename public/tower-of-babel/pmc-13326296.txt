Mesenchymal stem cell derived exosomes mitigate COVID-19 cytokine storm via Annexin A1 and TGF-β mediated MAPK pathway inhibition
Authors: Nesrine Ebrahim, Hajir A. Al Saihati, Arigue A. Dessouky, Yasmeen Mohammed Ismail, Ashraf A. Shamaa, Shereen A. Mohamed, Mohamed E. Mohamed, Nermine Nosseir, Mohamed Ahmed Eladl, Gianpiero Di Leva, Omnia A. Badr
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSevere COVID-19 is marked by a dysregulated inflammatory response, known as a cytokine storm, resulting in acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) and multiple organ failure. Mesenchymal stem cell-derived exosomes (MSC-Exos) have demonstrated potential as immunomodulatory agents. This work investigates the possibility of MSC-Exos to mitigate excessive inflammation in COVID-19 by targeting the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) signalling pathway.MethodologyWe integrated molecular docking analysis between TGF-β and Annexin A1 as exosomal proteins and key component proteins of the MAPK pathway (p38, ERK1/2, JNK1). The in-silico results were then validated in vivo using a Syrian hamster model of SARS-CoV-2 infection. Quantitative PCR (qPCR), western blotting, and histological examination were employed to evaluate the effects of MSC-Exos therapy on MAPK pathway activation, cytokine production, and lung tissue pathology.ResultsThe in-silico study revealed extensive hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic interactions at the protein–protein interfaces between exosomal proteins and MAPK components. These interactions suggest that exosomal proteins may modulate MAPK signaling pathways. In vivo, MSC-Exos administration led to marked downregulation of pivotal genes in the MAPK signaling pathway (MEKK1, MEKK2, MEKK3), diminished phosphorylation of JNK1, p38, and ERK1/2, and lowered production of pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α). Histopathological examination demonstrated ameliorated lung tissue structure, characterized by diminished alveolar wall thickness and decreased immune cell infiltration.ConclusionMSC-Exos elicit immunomodulatory effects in SARS-CoV-2-Infected hamsters, partially by directly targeting and blocking the MAPK signaling pathway. These findings offer a compelling justification for the clinical assessment of MSC-Exos as a therapeutic approach to alleviate the cytokine storm and enhance outcomes in severe COVID-19 by targeting the ACE2-Independent pathway.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13287-026-04980-z.

Introduction

The COVID-19 pandemic, induced by the severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2), has created an extraordinary worldwide health emergency [1]. Although vaccination initiatives have demonstrated substantial efficacy in mitigating severe illness, the rapid emergence of viral variants capable of evading neutralizing antibodies underscores the need for alternative and adjunctive therapeutic strategies [2]. Severe COVID-19 is now recognized as the consequence of a multifactorial pathogenic process in which an exaggerated inflammatory response (cytokine storm) represents one prominent component alongside endothelial dysfunction, coagulopathy, and metabolic dysregulation, collectively contributing to acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), multiorgan injury, and death [3]. One of the central signaling networks governing host responses to viral infection is the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) pathway, which encompasses the ERK1/2, p38, and JNK branches [4]. This pathway integrates multiple extracellular stress and immune signals and plays a pivotal role in regulating cytokine production, apoptosis, and immune cell activation [4, 5]. In the context of SARS-CoV-2 infection, aberrant activation of MAPK signaling, particularly through the p38 MAPK, ERK1/2, and JNK branches [6, 7]. However, accumulating evidence indicates that MAPK signaling is not unidirectionally pro-inflammatory; rather, its functional output is highly context, cell type, and time-dependent. In myeloid and other innate immune cells, ERK1/2 activation downstream of pattern‑recognition and cytokine receptors clearly promotes the production of classical pro‑inflammatory mediators, including TNF, IL‑1β, IL‑6 and IL‑12, and is frequently upregulated in chronically inflamed tissues [8]. At the same time, the ERK1/2 module can support anti-inflammatory or pro-resolving programs, for example by enhancing IL-10 expression, restraining excessive IL-12 production, and modulating T-cell differentiation in a way that limits sustained Th1-skewed responses. In non‑immune compartments, ERK1/2 signaling has likewise been implicated in cytoprotection and limitation of tissue damage, as shown in experimental models of acute myocardial injury and other inflammatory settings [9]. Systems‑level analyses of the MAPK network further underscore that distinct combinations and temporal patterns of ERK, p38 and JNK activation, driven by specific MAP3Ks, can switch the net outcome of MAPK signaling from inflammatory amplification toward survival, repair and resolution. These observations collectively support a more nuanced view of MAPK as a pleiotropic regulator of inflammation and tissue homeostasis, which is particularly relevant when considering MAPK-targeted or MAPK-modulating interventions [6, 7]. During the viral infection, MAPK activation can arise as part of the host’s intrinsic antiviral and stress‑response machinery, for example by coordinating interferon‑stimulated gene expression, promoting apoptosis of infected cells, and supporting tissue repair and barrier restoration. At the same time, numerous viruses, including SARS‑CoV‑2, can take over p38, ERK1/2, and JNK modules to support their own replication, amplify pro‑inflammatory cytokine production, and subvert type I interferon signaling, thereby tipping the balance from controlled antiviral defense toward pathogenic hyperinflammation and tissue injury [6]. This duality is particularly relevant for therapeutic targeting, because interventions that modulate MAPK activity must account for both its protective host functions and its virus‑exploited, disease‑promoting roles in COVID‑19. SARS-CoV-2 infection causing inflammatory changes in various organs, highlighting the systemic nature of COVID-19 immunopathology. So, indicating the need for therapies that modulate host responses alongside antiviral mechanisms [10].

Exosomes derived from mesenchymal stem cells (MSC-Exos) have a great attention as a promising cell-free therapy for COVID-19, mainly due to their immunomodulatory effect via its cargo which contain varieties of molecules including TGF-β, Annexin A1, IL-10, and many microRNAs [11]. These nanovesicles modulating the host immune responses, suppressing the proinflammatory signaling, and promoting the tissue repair [12]. Supporting this, studies on conditioned medium-enriched MSCs from umbilical cord have shown that MSC secretomes containing several bioactive factors capable of modulating the inflammation and promoting tissue regeneration in preclinical studies of injury [13]. In early clinical and preclinical COVID 19 studies, MSC Exos have been associated with improved oxygenation, reduced circulating cytokine levels, and attenuation of lung injury, while recent mechanistic work suggests that MSC EVs may modulate key signaling hubs such as JAK/STAT and interfere with viral entry by targeting ACE2 and TMPRSS2 [13, 14]. Against this background, elucidating whether and how MSC Exos can modulate MAPK signaling during SARS CoV 2 infection is of considerable interest for refining their therapeutic application in severe COVID 19.

This research aims to examine whether MSC-Exos influence immunomodulation in COVID-19, at least partially, by affecting the MAPK signaling pathway. The current study proposes that MSC-Exos proteins engage with pivotal MAPK pathway components causing pathway inhibition of pro-inflammatory cytokines production. This theory is assessed via a combination of in silico modeling and in vivo validation in a Syrian hamster model of SARS-CoV-2 infection, aiming to justify the clinical application of MSC-Exos in treating severe COVID-19.

Materials and methods

In silico modelling

To explore the potential interactions between mesenchymal stem cell-derived exosomal proteins and key regulators of the MAPK signaling pathway, we conducted molecular docking analyses using TGF-β and Annexin A1. These exosomal proteins, identified in the ExoCarta database (TGF-β, Annexin A1), possess well-documented immunomodulatory properties. The selected target proteins for docking—JNK1, p38, and ERK1/2—are essential components of the MAPK cascade and play a critical role in SARS-CoV-2-induced inflammation.

The three-dimensional (3D) structures of TGF-β (PDB ID: 1KSQ), Annexin A1 (PDB ID: 1MCX), JNK1 (PDB ID: 4U8M), p38 (PDB ID: 1A9U), and ERK1/2 (PDB ID: 4QTB) were retrieved from the Protein Data Bank (PDB) (https://www.rcsb.org).

Molecular docking simulations were performed using HADDOCK2.4 (HADDOCK) to investigate protein–protein interactions. HADDOCK ranks docking conformations based on energy scores, including electrostatic and van der Waals interactions, and clusters the best conformations based on structural similarity. The docking approach incorporated Ambiguous Interaction Restraints (AIRs) derived from literature and sequence conservation. The top-ranked complexes were selected based on binding energy (ΔG), buried surface area (BSA), and electrostatic energy, where lower binding energy indicates stronger interactions, BSA reflects the interaction interface, and electrostatic energy accounts for charge-based interactions.

The selected complexes were then visualized using Discovery Studio (BIOVIA Discovery Studio) to examine binding interactions in three dimensions. To further characterize the interactions at the atomic level, LigPlot + (LigPlot +) was used to identify hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic interactions between the docked proteins. These analyses provided insights into the stability and molecular basis of the protein–protein interactions.

In vivo validation

Mesenchymal stem cell derived exosomes (MSC-Exos)

Human Wharton’s jelly-derived mesenchymal stem cell-derived lyophilized exosomes were purchased from Bioluga® (Canada) with Certificate ID: TU35-5ZN4. The exosomes were reconstituted in 5 mL distilled water, where 1 mL contained MSC-Exos derived from 0.5 × 106 MSCs [14–16]. Lyophilization process as stated in the COA of the product was by freeze drying and sublimation using a cryoprotectant stabilizer which improves its stability and temperature tolerance and increases the shelf life and extends preservation periods of exosomes without losing its intrinsic cargo or harmful effects on the exosomal membranes [14, 16]. The vendor confirmed that the original Wharton’s jelly donations were obtained with prior ethics approval and written informed consent, as documented in the product COA and provider materials; procurement was via Prof. Ashraf Shamaa under vendor research-use terms (no MTA). All experiments were conducted using the same production batch (Batch No: WC0478) to ensure consistency across all treated groups. Exosome characterization performed using BCA assay, DLS, zeta potential analysis, TEM, flow cytometry, and ELISA was guided by the MISEV2018 (Minimal Information for Studies of Extracellular Vesicles) guidelines to ensure rigorous assessment of exosome identity, purity, and structural stability.

Experimental animals

Syrian hamsters were chosen as the in vivo model for SARS-CoV-2 infection due to their close resemblance to human COVID-19, including efficient respiratory viral replication, alveolar damage, and strong lung inflammation [18–21] offering a relevant platform to study how MSC-derived exosomes modulate MAPK-mediated inflammation and cytokine responses in vivo. So, we used adult male Syrian hamsters (Mesocricetus auratus), 8–10 weeks old, weighing 100–150 g, procured from Egypt's National Research Center (NRC). The animals were housed in clean cages under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions with unrestricted access to safe drinking water and standard rodent nutrition. Animals were acclimated to a habitat characterized by a 12-h light cycle starting at 8:00 AM and a room temperature of 23 °C. Preparation of SARS-CoV-2 virus

The hCoV-19/Egypt/NRC-03/2020 strain of SARS-CoV-2 (GISAID Accession: EPI_ISL_430819) was selected due to its early isolation during the first wave of the pandemic in Egypt and its established use in local preclinical infection models. The virus was propagated at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 0.005 in Vero E6 cells (ATCC No. CRL-1586), which were cultured in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO₂ and monitored daily via light microscopy. The sample possesses the GISAID accession number EPI_ISL_430819. The virus-infected culture supernatant was harvested 72 h post-infection and subjected to centrifugation twice for 15 min at 4 °C for clarity, after which the supernatant was aliquoted and titrated via a plaque assay [16]. VERO-E6 cells were infected in a 6-well cell culture plate utilizing the virus, which was serially diluted tenfold in cell maintenance media and incubated for one hour at 37 °C with 5% CO2. The uninfected wells functioned as controls. Subsequent to infection, the plates were incubated for 72 h at 37 °C with 5% CO2. Following this period, 3 mL of overlayer medium containing agarose was introduced to each well, and the plates were maintained at room temperature to facilitate solidification. Fixation and viral inactivation were performed using a 10% formalin solution for one hour. After fixation, the overlay was eliminated, and a 0.1% crystal violet solution was utilized for staining. Viral plaques appeared as transparent, colorless entities on a violet backdrop of marked cells. The formula employed for viral titer measurement is as follows: plaque-forming units (PFUs)/mL = number of plaques × virus-injected volume × viral dilution × 10.

Hamster infection trial

A viral microneutralization assay was used to confirm the seronegative status of all male Syrian hamsters prior to infection. Animals were anesthetized using an intraperitoneal injection of ketamine (100 mg/kg) and xylazine (10 mg/kg). Each hamster was intranasally inoculated with 50 μL of SARS-CoV-2 suspension containing approximately 1 × 104 plaque-forming units (PFU), as quantified by plaque assay. This dose was selected based on prior preclinical studies showing it induces moderate, reproducible pulmonary pathology in Syrian hamsters without excessive morbidity [22, 23]. The virus used was obtained from a single production batch of hCoV-19/Egypt/NRC-03/2020 (GISAID: EPI_ISL_430819), propagated under BSL-3 conditions in Vero E6 cells, titrated, and stored in aliquots at − 80 °C to ensure batch consistency. Animals were monitored daily for 7 days post-infection. A single intraperitoneal injection of 0.5 mL of MSC-derived exosomes (MSC-Exos) containing 100 μg/mL of total exosomal protein (50 μg per animal) was administered to the treatment group 8 h post-infection. The intraperitoneal route was selected over intravenous administration due to its proven capacity to mediate systemic distribution and pulmonary delivery of extracellular vesicles in rodent models, with reduced technical invasiveness and improved safety under repeated handling [24, 25]. The selected dose of 100 μg/mL was based on earlier studies demonstrating effective anti-inflammatory and tissue-protective effects of MSC-derived exosomes in models of acute lung injury and viral infection [26, 27].

Titration of viral shedding

A sterile 0.5 mL of 1 × PBS, supplemented with a 2% antibiotic-antimitotic mixture, was administered through the nasal cavity to obtain nasal washes from infected hamsters (group II) and infected hamsters treated with MSC-Exos [28]. Subsequently, 200 μL of the sample underwent total RNA extraction prior to RT‒qPCR analysis targeting the ORF1b–nsp14 gene, as previously delineated [29, 30].

Viral microneutralization assay (VMN)

The serum samples were heat-inactivated for 30 min at 56 °C and stored at 20 °C. Serum samples from each animal were diluted twofold in 50 μL of maintenance cell culture medium, ranging from 1:10 to 1:1280. 100 TCID/mL Egypt/NRC-03/2020 hCoV-19 the virus and SARS-CoV-2 isolate were combined in equal proportions with serially diluted serum. The mixture was applied duplicate to 96-well plates containing Vero-E6 cells and incubated for one hour at 37 °C before removal. Following the removal of the inoculum, maintenance DMEM enriched with 2% BSA was administered. The plates were incubated for three days at 37 °C with 5% CO2 in a humidified environment. The neutralizing antibody titer was the highest serum dilution that effectively neutralized the virus.

Animal experimental design

This animal model and group allocation were previously described by Al Saihati [16] and applied in our related studies [38]. After two weeks of acclimatization, 28 male Syrian hamsters were randomly divided into three groups according to Al Saihati [16]:

Group I (Control group; n = 14): The hamsters were divided equally into two subgroups:

Subgroup Ia: Standard laboratory conditions without any intervention.

Subgroup Ib: Single intraperitoneal injection of 0.2 mL of phosphate-buffered saline.

Group II (Covid group; n = 7): Intranasally administered 100 μL of 50 μL of SARS-CoV-2. Hamsters were sacrificed after 7 days of infection challenge (at the end of the experiment).

Group III (MSC-Exos group; n = 7): Eight hours after infection challenge, the hamsters were injected intraperitoneally with 0.5 mL of MSC-Exos per hamster at a concentration of 100 μg protein/mL [15].

Daily measurements of body temperature and weight were conducted for each group. No mortality or morbidity were observed. Nasal washes from the anesthetized animals were collected at 1-, 3-, 5-, and 7-days post-infection. Seven days post-viral challenge, the hamsters had a 12-h fasting period before anesthesia. We performed euthanasia on the hamsters utilizing a two-step technique in strict adherence to our institutional rules for humane animal research. The hamsters received an intramuscular injection of a ketamine (100 mg/kg) and xylazine (10 mg/kg) combination to achieve profound surgical anesthesia. Upon confirmation that the animals were in a profound state of anesthesia, shown by the absence of withdrawal reflexes and unresponsiveness to stimuli, we proceeded with euthanasia. Euthanasia was conducted through exsanguination (cardiac puncture) by obtaining a terminal blood sample from the retro-orbital sinus, leading to the fast reduction in blood volume and subsequent death of the animal under deep anesthesia. The complete euthanasia procedure was conducted by qualified staff in a specialized euthanasia room, employing methods that reduce any possible pain or suffering for the animals. All methods received approval from our Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) to ensure compliance with the highest standards of humane animal research and were reported in accordance with the ARRIVE 2.0 principles [31]. Blood samples were collected in sterile ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) vacutainer tubes, and all animal sera were separated and preserved at − 20 °C until required. Arterial perfusion fixation was performed via the left ventricle while the hamsters were restrained. Three segments of lung tissue were isolated. For histological studies, the first segment was fixed in 10% formaldehyde, subsequently washed, dried, and embedded in paraffin blocks. To assess the viral titters in each collected organ, the second segment was preserved in RNeasy before homogenization. The third segment was placed in SDS buffer.

Histopathological analysis

4–6 µm thick paraffin sections were mounted on glass slides and stained using H&E and Masson's trichrome to assess fibrosis. Fixed sections stained with H&E were dehydrated using ascending doses of ethanol. The dehydrated sections were washed twice with distilled water and underwent H&E staining. Two blinded professional investigators utilized a Leica DMR 3000 light microscope to inspect, analyze, and picture the lung sections [33]. Morphometric study

Two experienced histologists evaluated the H&E, Masson's trichrome, and immune-stained sections without knowledge of the experiment. Morphometric measurements were performed on at least two hamster sections using ImageJ® Version 1.52a28 and Fiji ImageJ [35]. Three non-overlapping fields were examined from each area. To quantify lung tissue pathology, Huang, Shi et al.’s 2022 lung injury rating system was used. The alveolar wall thickness, congestion, pulmonary edema, bleeding, and neutrophil infiltration into the airspace or vessel wall were assessed. Lung hemorrhage, lung interstitial edema, alveolar wall thickness, and inflammatory cell infiltration were graded as follows. Masson's trichrome-stained sections at × 100 were used to assess the mean area percentage [35] of the collagen fibers. The immune reactivity area percentage was also measured. The image type was changed to RGB stack-created grayscale images of each channel to distinguish immune staining. The threshold for adjusting the image was set to 0–87 for the green channel. The analysis measures the measured area, area fraction, threshold limit, and display label. Immunohistochemical quantitative evaluation was performed using the Allred score (0–1 = negative, 2–3 = mild, 4–6 = moderate, and 7–8 = strongly positive). QuPath (0.1.2) adds the percentage of positive cells and staining intensity classes (0–3) to calculate the score [36].

Western blot

Frozen lung tissues were chopped and homogenized in ice-cold RIPA lysis buffer with a protease inhibitor cocktail (4%, Roche Diagnostics, Manheim, Germany) using hypodermic needles (20G, 22G, and 26G). Lysates of lung tissue were incubated on ice for 30 min with gentle agitation. To obtain the supernatant, the lysates were centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 30 min. The protein concentration in each sample was determined using the Folin-Lowry spectrophotometric method (Beckman Coulter Inc., Indianapolis, Indiana). Protein extracts were incubated in Laemmli buffer for 5 min at 95 °C. Next, 50 g protein samples were placed onto a 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) polyacrylamide gel for gel electrophoresis. After that, the proteins were blotted onto polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes (Amersham Biosciences, Bucks, United Kingdom).

To inhibit nonspecific reactions, the membranes were incubated for 1 h in 5% nonfat dry milk in 1% TBS with Tween 20. The membranes were then treated with the selected primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C (Table 3). Next, the membranes were washed three times and incubated for one hour at room temperature with the appropriate alkaline phosphatase-labeled secondary antibody (Biospes, 1:5000). ImageJ analysis software was used to perform densitometric analysis of the immunoreactive bands, while actin serving as the internal control.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

The Levels of TGF-β1 and Annexin A1 were quantified in MSC-derived exosomes, while COL1A1 was measured in lung tissue homogenates, using commercial ELISA kits according to manufacturer protocols. Lyophilized exosomes were reconstituted in sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to a final concentration of 1 mg/mL total protein. Protein content was verified using the bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat. No. 23225), with absorbance measured at 562 nm using a Synergy HT or Epoch™ Microplate Reader (BioTek Instruments, USA). For protein extraction, both exosome suspensions and lung tissue homogenates were lysed in RIPA buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat. No. 89900) supplemented with a protease inhibitor cocktail (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat. No. P8340). Lysates were incubated on ice for 30 min with intermittent vortexing, then centrifuged at 14,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C to remove debris. Supernatants were collected and normalized based on total protein concentration to ensure consistency across assays.

TGF-β1 levels were measured using a Quantikine ELISA kit (R&D Systems, Cat. No. DB100B). Samples were acid-activated according to the manufacturer’s instructions to convert latent TGF-β1 into its immunoreactive form, and 100 µL of each sample or standard was analyzed in duplicate. Absorbance was recorded at 450 nm with correction at 570 nm.

Annexin A1 concentrations were determined using a specific ELISA kit (MyBioSource, Cat. No. MBS2028703). Each sample or standard (100 µL) was incubated with biotinylated antibody and HRP-streptavidin, followed by TMB substrate addition. Absorbance was read at 450 nm. Six-point standard curves were generated for both TGF-β1 (0–32 ng/mL) and Annexin A1 (0–16 ng/mL), and concentrations were interpolated using linear regression. All data were normalized to total exosomal protein content to minimize loading variation.

COL1A1 levels in lung homogenates were assessed using a rodent-specific ELISA kit (Cloud-Clone Corp., Cat. No. SEA571Ra). Approximately 30 mg of lung tissue was processed as above, and the resulting protein extracts were assayed in duplicate. Optical density was recorded at 450 nm using a microplate spectrophotometer, and concentrations were derived using a four-parameter logistic regression standard curve. Data were expressed relative to total protein content and reported as mean values from biological replicates.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using R software (version 4.1.2). Prior to parametric testing, the normality of all datasets was verified using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and all variables met the assumption of normality (p > 0.05). One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was conducted to determine statistical differences among groups, followed by Tukey’s Honestly Significant Difference (HSD) post hoc test to assess pairwise comparisons. Significant differences (p < 0.05) were annotated using distinct letter assignments, where groups sharing the same letter were not significantly different. Data are expressed as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). A bar plot was generated using ggplot2, with mean expression values represented by bars and standard errors depicted as error bars.

Molecular docking analysis

Molecular docking analysis using HADDOCK predicted notable interactions between exosomal proteins and MAPK pathway components, as outlined in Table 4. Among the examined complexes, Annexin A1/p38 demonstrated the strongest binding affinity, with a highly negative binding energy (ΔG = − 346.508 kcal/mol) and a substantial electrostatic contribution (− 438.012 kcal/mol). Similarly, TGF-β/ERK1/2 and Annexin A1/ERK1/2 showed moderate binding stability, with binding energies of − 257.282 kcal/mol and − 226.354 kcal/mol, respectively. Notably, TGF-β/ERK1/2 displayed the largest interaction surface (BSA = 1731.27 Å2), while Annexin A1/ERK1/2 had a slightly higher BSA (1738.22 Å2), reinforcing their potential role in mediating protein–protein interactions. These findings suggest structural compatibility and potential binding interfaces between exosomal proteins and MAPK components. However, since molecular docking is a predictive tool, the results should be interpreted with caution. Discussion

Mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) have long been investigated for their regenerative capacity [37] and immunomodulatory potential in diseases such as viral infections and inflammatory disorders [38]. Accumulating evidence suggests that a substantial part of MSC therapeutic activity is mediated by paracrine signaling through exosomes (Exos) [39], which deliver bioactive proteins, lipids, and nucleic acids to recipient cells. MSC-derived Exos (MSC-Exos) retain key parent-cell benefits, including immunomodulation, tissue repair, and anti-fibrotic activity, while offering practical advantages over live MSCs: because Exos lack nuclei and cannot self-replicate, they are associated with reduced tumorigenicity and lower risk of immunological rejection. In addition, Exos are relatively stable, can be stored long-term, and are less likely to be trapped in the lung microvasculature after systemic administration. Collectively, these properties supported by preclinical and early clinical evidence position MSC-Exos as a safer and more scalable cell-free therapeutic approach with strong immunomodulatory potential for inflammatory and infectious diseases, including COVID-19 [38].

In this study, the exosomal proteins TGF-β and Annexin A1 were selected for molecular docking analyses with MAPK pathway components due to their documented presence in MSC-Exos and their roles in immune regulation and cellular signaling. According to the ExoCarta database (http://www.exocarta.org/), a comprehensive and widely accepted repository of experimentally verified exosome contents, both TGF-β and Annexin A1 are confirmed to be present in MSC-Exos from human Wharton’s Jelly. Prior studies also support functional links between these proteins and MAPK signaling: Ding et al. [40] reported that Annexin A1 inhibited p38 MAPK activation in microglia and reduced neuroinflammation in a mouse intracerebral hemorrhage model via the FPR2/p38 pathway, while Liu et al. [41] showed that MSC-derived exosomes activated ERK1/2 signaling in vascular smooth muscle cells, influencing inflammation and proliferation and supporting exosomal regulation of the ERK1/2 pathway. Consistent with this axis, Zhu et al. [42] further highlighted the connection between TGF-β and ERK signaling by demonstrating a nucleolar mechanism that modulated TGF-β/ERK signaling with downstream effects on organismal proteostasis.

The in-silico findings suggest that MSC-exosomal proteins may inhibit MAPK signaling through stable molecular interactions. Annexin A1 showed strong binding affinity to p38 (ΔG = − 346.5 kcal/mol), which may interfere with p38 phosphorylation, consistent with the reduced p-p38 levels observed in treated hamsters (Fig. 8e). This pattern also aligned with decreased IL-1β and TNF-α levels (Fig. 8a–c), cytokines that are regulated downstream of p38-mediated MAPK signaling [40]. Similarly, docking of exosomal TGF-β with ERK1/2 supported a potential regulatory mechanism whereby exosomal TGF-β could modulate ERK1/2 activity linked to proliferation and differentiation, consistent with reported roles in tumor suppression and fibrosis regulation [41]. The prominent electrostatic contributions and large buried surface areas further supported complex stability, which may be important for the proposed inhibitory effects.

Comparable studies indicate that exosomal proteins can negatively regulate MAPK pathways across different cellular contexts. Exosomal Annexin A1 has been reported to suppress MAPK activation in macrophages by inhibiting p38 and JNK phosphorylation, thereby reducing inflammatory responses [43]. In addition, exosome-associated TGF-β signaling has been linked to ERK1/2 inhibition in certain cancer cells, contributing to reduced tumor progression and enhanced apoptosis [41]. Consistent with these reports, the interactions observed in this study suggest that exosomal proteins may function as regulators of MAPK signaling, potentially through stabilizing less active kinase conformations. Notably, the predicted inhibitory interaction of exosomal Annexin A1 with JNK1 supported by strong hydrogen bonding and electrostatic contributions may represent a mechanism to limit stress-induced MAPK activation, which is implicated in neurodegenerative diseases and chronic inflammation [44]. Overall, these docking-based findings provide molecular-level support for the inhibitory potential of exosomal Annexin A1 and TGF-β against p38, ERK1/2, and JNK1, which may help prevent excessive MAPK activation associated with inflammation-driven damage and aberrant proliferation.

The current study showed that MSC-Exos modulated the MAPK signaling pathway and reduced inflammation in a Syrian hamster model of SARS-CoV-2 infection. MSC-Exos have received considerable attention as therapeutic candidates for diverse diseases, including viral infections [45]. Al Saihati et al. [16] from the same research group demonstrated that MSC-Exos can inhibit SARS-CoV-2 infection by targeting viral entry receptors, and ACE2 decoy exosomes have been proposed as an alternative entry-targeting mechanism [46]. Given the emergence of SARS-CoV-2 variants capable of evading antibody neutralization [47], these observations support the need for multifaceted therapeutic strategies that extend beyond viral entry. Accordingly, this study focused on whether exosomes may influence host responses during SARS-CoV-2 infection by targeting MAPK signaling as a means to mitigate cytokine-storm–associated inflammation.

In contrast to studies primarily centered on viral entry or direct antiviral activity, we proposed that exosomal proteins could directly interact with MAPK components, thereby modulating viral protein–host signaling interactions and attenuating inflammatory responses driven by p38 MAPK and NF-κB activation [48]. To test this hypothesis, we integrated in silico docking and structural modeling with in vivo validation in a hamster model, providing a combined computational experimental evaluation of MSC-Exos as a strategy for targeting host-cell signaling pathways. The in-silico results suggested direct interactions between exosomal proteins and key MAPK signaling components, and we therefore evaluated exosomal treatment effects on MAPK pathway activation and inflammatory cytokine production in a Syrian hamster model of SARS-CoV-2 infection, extending the work of Al Saihati et al. [16]. This in vivo work enabled assessment of MSC-Exos–mediated modulation of host inflammatory responses through mechanisms independent of the ACE2 and TMPRSS2 pathways described by Al Saihati et al. [16].

To further interpret the observed immunomodulatory effects, we examined how exosomal cargo therapy influenced the MAPK signaling pathway, a central regulator of cellular stress responses, inflammation, and virus replication [49]. MAPK signaling contributes to the induction of pro-inflammatory cytokines and chemokines that are key features of the cytokine storm observed in severe COVID-19 [50, 51]. Accordingly, we integrated qPCR, Western blotting, and histopathological assessment to evaluate MAPK-pathway modulation and downstream lung tissue outcomes following exosome treatment in the Syrian hamster infection model, using an approach comparable to that applied by Al Saihati et al. [16] for MSC-EVs.

qPCR results showed downregulation of major MAPK-cascade genes in the exosome-treated group (Fig. 8), including reduced mRNA expression of MEKK1, MEKK2, and MEKK3, which function as upstream kinases initiating MAPK signaling. MEKK1, acting as a MAP3K, can activate ERK1/2 and JNK/p38 signaling in response to cellular stress, growth factors, and inflammatory stimuli [52], and MEKK2 and MEKK3 have also been implicated in activating downstream MAPKs and shaping cellular responses [53]. MEKK3 is particularly implicated in transmitting extracellular cues to downstream effectors and regulating processes such as proliferation, differentiation, and apoptosis [54]. Beyond MEKK1–3, MSC-Exos treatment markedly downregulated ASK1 (MAP3K5) and TAK1 (MAP3K7) transcripts in lung tissue, two upstream kinases that drive MAPK activation under stress and inflammatory conditions. ASK1 promotes JNK and p38 signaling and thereby amplifies apoptotic and inflammatory responses [55], whereas TAK1 integrates multiple pro-inflammatory inputs, including TGF-β and toll-like receptor signaling, and is required for activation of NF-κB and MAPKs during infection [56, 57]. Together, these coordinated reductions are consistent with attenuation of early MAPK-cascade activation and cellular stress- and cytokine-driven signaling.

In parallel, MSC-Exos–treated lungs showed significant upregulation of DUSP1 (dual specificity phosphatase 1), a key negative regulator that dephosphorylates and inactivates MAPKs including p38, JNK, and ERK, thereby limiting excessive inflammation [58, 59]. qPCR also revealed elevated IL-10 expression in the MSC-Exos–treated group. IL-10 is a central anti-inflammatory cytokine that suppresses pro-inflammatory mediator release and promotes tissue repair and homeostasis, particularly during viral pneumonia [60]. In addition, reduced IL-18R1 expression suggested a possible dampening of IL-18–driven inflammatory signaling, given that IL-18R1 mediates responses to the pro-inflammatory cytokine IL-18 [61]. This observation is relevant to COVID-19, where IL-18 has been associated with heightened inflammation and lung injury [62]. Mechanistically, these transcriptional effects may involve MAPK-associated regulators such as ATF2, RHO, Ras, and Raf-1. Collectively, these data indicate that MSC-Exos exert multi-nodal regulation of MAPK-driven inflammation by suppressing key upstream kinases (ASK1, TAK1, MEKK1–3) while enhancing counter-regulatory and reparative mediators (DUSP1 and IL-10). This combined inhibitory and pro-resolution profile may underlie the anti-inflammatory and tissue-protective effects observed in the SARS-CoV-2 hamster model and is consistent with emerging preclinical evidence supporting MSC-Exos in severe COVID-19 [38]. Together, these findings support the rationale for targeting host inflammatory pathways, consistent with the framework highlighted by Al Saihati et al. [16] and Derafsh et al. [63]. Although our docking analysis and in vivo findings suggest that MSC-derived exosomal proteins such as Annexin A1 and TGF-β may modulate MAPK pathway activity, the mechanistic link remains correlative. Future studies are needed to establish whether these effects are due to direct molecular interactions or secondary to reduced viral burden and inflammation. Immunoprecipitation (IP) experiments targeting MAPK proteins (e.g., p38, ERK, JNK) from lung tissue or exosome-treated cells could help determine whether exosomal proteins are physically associated with MAPK components, as predicted by docking. Additionally, MAPK inhibition or activation assays and cell-type–specific experiments will be essential to dissect the pathway involvement and validate the functional significance of these interactions.

Consistent with the transcriptional data, Western blotting showed reduced phosphorylation of JNK1 (p-JNK1), p38 (p-p38), and ERK1/2 (p-ERK1/2) in the exosome-treated group, while total protein levels changed minimally, indicating suppression of MAPK activation rather than global protein loss. Because MAPK activity is regulated primarily by phosphorylation, the reductions in p-JNK1, p-p38, and p-ERK1/2 supported the qPCR findings. This interpretation aligned with reports that SARS-CoV-2 infection activates (phosphorylates) p38 MAPK and that p38 signaling promotes production of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-6, contributing to cytokine storm severity [62]. Moreover, p38 activation has been implicated in viral propagation by supporting viral entry, replication, and assembly, underscoring MAPK inhibition as a plausible antiviral strategy [64]. In agreement, Al Saihati et al. [16] also observed decreased inflammatory markers following MSC-EV treatment, suggesting convergent anti-inflammatory effects.

Histological analysis of lung tissues from the Control and MSC-Exos–treated groups was performed to evaluate whether exosome-associated MAPK modulation corresponded with improved pulmonary pathology. Consistent with the molecular findings, the exosome-treated cohort showed improved lung architecture, including reduced alveolar wall thickening, decreased immune-cell infiltration, and fewer features suggestive of hyaline membrane formation, hallmarks associated with ARDS and severe COVID-19. Such pathological changes are linked to impaired gas exchange and respiratory failure in critically ill COVID-19 cases [65, 66]. These observations were in agreement with Al Saihati et al. [16], who reported reduced lung injury following MSC-EV treatment, supporting the potential of MSC-Exos to attenuate SARS-CoV-2–associated lung damage.

Immunohistochemical analysis indicated reduced Annexin A1 expression in the lungs of SARS-CoV-2–infected hamsters, whereas MSC-Exos treatment restored and enhanced Annexin A1 immunoreactivity, particularly in alveolar epithelial cells and interstitial macrophages. Annexin A1 is an endogenous pro-resolving mediator that can regulate leukocyte trafficking and support inflammation resolution, thereby limiting tissue injury [67]. Consistent with reports that Annexin A1 expression is dynamically regulated during lung inflammation and may increase in areas of leukocyte infiltration and tissue damage during viral infection [67], the observed restoration of Annexin A1 after MSC-EV delivery may have contributed to reduced inflammatory infiltration and preservation of alveolar architecture noted in the histological assessment. Moreover, the increased Annexin A1 signal in the MSC-Exos group was consistent with reports that exosomal Annexin A1 can influence MAPK signaling and reduce pro-inflammatory cytokine production, thereby supporting resolution processes in acute lung injury. Together, these observations suggest that Annexin A1 restoration may contribute, at least in part, to exosome-associated immunomodulatory effects in this model.

Given the coordinated downregulation of MAPK-pathway transcripts, reduced phosphorylation of MAPK proteins, and improved lung histopathology in the exosome-treated group, we next assessed downstream inflammatory mediators. MSC-Exos treatment was associated with reduced expression of IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α, along with decreased expression of upstream components including TLR4 and TGF-β. The reduction in TLR4 expression is notable because TLR4 functions as a pattern-recognition receptor that initiates innate immune responses to pathogen-associated signals [68], while TGF-β is a profibrotic mediator implicated in lung fibrosis, a potential chronic sequela of COVID-19 [69]. In agreement, Al Saihati et al. [16] reported reduced cytokine-storm indicators following MSC-EV treatment.

Interestingly, although MSC-Exos contained measurable TGF-β1 (Fig. 6d), lung tissue from treated animals showed reduced TGF-β expression. This apparent discrepancy is consistent with prior evidence that exosomal TGF-β can trigger negative-feedback mechanisms that attenuate endogenous TGF-β signaling in inflamed tissues [70, 71]. Rather than functioning as a simple additive cytokine, exosomal TGF-β may modulate immune-cell programs (e.g., regulatory T cells and macrophages), contributing to downregulation of fibrotic and inflammatory signaling loops. Such context-dependent effects have been reported across injury and fibrosis models, supporting the view that exosomal cargo can alter recipient-cell signaling without directly increasing tissue cytokine levels [72, 73]. Accordingly, the reduced tissue TGF-β observed here may reflect an indirect, host-mediated anti-fibrotic response. Because elevated TGF-β has been linked to severe COVID-19 pathogenesis and worse outcomes [74, 75], these findings further suggest that exosome-delivered TGF-β may behave differently than circulating or endogenously overexpressed TGF-β, potentially acting in a localized and regulated manner that does not amplify systemic profibrotic signaling. In addition to MAPK signaling, TGF-β can also activate the JAK/STAT3 pathway, which is implicated in immune regulation, tissue remodeling, and inflammation-driven fibrosis in severe viral infections including COVID-19 [76, 77]. Although STAT3 activation was not assessed in this study, the potential interplay between MAPK and STAT3 signaling warrants further investigation to clarify mechanisms underlying MSC-Exos therapeutic effects.

Moreover, In the present study, MSC‑Exos administration was associated with a significant reduction in pulmonary IFN‑R1 and IFN‑R2 transcript levels, accompanied by attenuated MAPK activation, lower expression of IL‑1β, IL‑6 and TNF‑α, and histological improvement of lung injury, whereas the decrease in viral load was modest in magnitude. Taken together, this pattern argues against a purely epiphenomenal downregulation of interferon receptors secondary to viral clearance. Rather, when viewed alongside our previous work showing that MSC‑EVs directly target host viral entry receptors (ACE2/TMPRSS2) [16] via vesicle‑borne microRNAs and constrain JAK/STAT [38] activation and downstream interferon‑responsive mediators in related COVID‑19 models, the present data support the interpretation that MSC‑Exos modulate the interferon axis at multiple levels. We therefore consider the reduced IFN‑R1/IFN‑R2 expression in MSC‑Exos–treated lungs to reflect an integrated effect of (i) direct immunoregulatory actions of exosomal cargo on interferon‑receptor/JAK‑STAT/MAPK signaling and (ii) indirect consequences of a partially diminished viral burden, jointly contributing to the resolution of sustained interferon‑driven inflammatory signaling rather than indiscriminate suppression of antiviral defenses.

The cytokine storm in COVID-19 has been linked to MAPK signaling, a central regulator of inflammation and cellular stress responses [78]. SARS-CoV-2 infection can activate MAPK cascades and promote excessive production of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α, which are core features of cytokine storm [79]. Mechanistically, activation of p38 MAPK and NF-κB may be initiated by recognition of viral components through pattern-recognition receptors such as TLR4, with downstream engagement of upstream MAP3Ks (e.g., MEKK1, MEKK2, and MEKK3) [80]. Subsequent phosphorylation of MAPKs including JNK1, p38, and ERK1/2 can amplify inflammatory signaling and enhance transcription of pro-inflammatory cytokine genes [81]. Collectively, these observations support MAPK-pathway inhibition as a potential strategy to mitigate cytokine storm–associated lung injury, consistent with the anti-inflammatory outcomes reported by Al Saihati et al. [16] and with our in silico predictions of interactions between exosomal proteins and MAPK components.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that MSC-Exos effectively reduce SARS-CoV-2-induced lung injury by modulating the host inflammatory response, potentially through regulatory effects on MAPK signaling components. MSC-Exos were associated with the downregulation of critical upstream kinases (MEKK1–3, ASK1, TAK1), restoration of the MAPK inhibitor DUSP1, suppression of pro-inflammatory cytokines, and enhancement of anti-inflammatory IL-10 expression. Together, these changes correlated with mitigation of the cytokine storm, reduced fibrosis, and improved lung histology in a COVID-19 hamster model. While our molecular docking data suggest possible interactions between exosomal proteins and MAPK pathway members, further functional studies are needed to confirm direct inhibition. Nonetheless, our findings support MSC-Exos as a promising host-targeted therapeutic strategy to control hyperinflammation and promote lung recovery independently of ACE2-mediated viral entry.

Study limitations

While our findings offer promising insights, several limitations should be considered. First, all in vivo experiments were performed using the Syrian hamster model, which, although closely mimicking many aspects of human COVID-19 lung pathology, cannot fully capture the complexity of human immune responses. Second, the predicted interactions between exosomal proteins (Annexin A1 and TGF-β) and MAPK pathway components were based on molecular docking and have not yet been validated using biochemical methods such as co-immunoprecipitation or surface plasmon resonance. Third, due to the lack of nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA), exosome dosing was based on total protein concentration rather than vesicle number, which may affect the precision of dose standardization. Finally, this study used a single dose and administration route, without investigating dose response dynamics, biodistribution, pharmacokinetics, or long-term safety for future translational studies.

Future directions

Future studies will build upon study limitations by assessing the pharmacokinetics, biodistribution, and cellular uptake of MSC-Exos using in vivo labeling approaches, as well as exploring the effects of varied dosing and administration schedules. Additionally, long‑term studies will evaluate the safety and durability of MSC-Exos ‑mediated benefits, while targeted gain‑ and loss‑of‑function approaches will further define the contributions of specific exosomal cargo (e.g., Annexin A1, TGF‑β) to MAPK pathway modulation. Together, these efforts will refine the therapeutic application of MSC-Exos and support their translation into clinical treatments for respiratory viral infections and hyperinflammatory conditions.
