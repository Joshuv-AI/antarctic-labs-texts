Large Language Models as Automatic Annotators and Annotation Adjudicators for Fine-Grained Opinion Analysis
Gaurav Negi, M.A. Waskow, Paul Buitelaar

Abstract
Fine-grained opinion analysis of text provides a detailed understanding of expressed sentiments, including the addressed entity. Although this level of detail is sound, it requires considerable human effort and substantial cost to annotate opinions in datasets for training models, especially across diverse domains and real-world applications. We explore the feasibility of LLMs as automatic annotators for fine-grained opinion analysis, addressing the shortage of domain-specific labelled datasets. In this work, we use a declarative annotation pipeline. This approach reduces the variability of manual prompt engineering when using LLMs to identify fine-grained opinion spans in text. We also present a novel methodology for an LLM to adjudicate multiple labels and produce final annotations. After trialling the pipeline with models of different sizes for the Aspect Sentiment Triplet Extraction (ASTE) and Aspect-Category-Opinion-Sentiment (ACOS) analysis tasks, we show that LLMs can serve as automatic annotators and adjudicators, achieving high Inter-Annotator Agreement across individual LLM-based annotators. This reduces the cost and human effort needed to create these fine-grained opinion-annotated datasets.



1
Introduction

There has been a considerable evolution in the field of fine-grained opinion analysis, which has led to many theoretical and practical formulations of opinions and sentiment.
These fine-grained methods provide a clearer picture of not just the sentiment polarity (positive, negative, neutral) but also the contextual elements of the aforementioned sentiments. Consider the following example:

“I had hoped for better battery life, as it had only about 2-1/2 hours doing heavy computations (8 threads using 100% of the CPU)”

In the text, a
negative
sentiment is being expressed. However, a fine-grain annotation, unlike sentence or document-level annotation, has the opinion span expressing the polarity, i.e.
“hoped for better”
, along with an explicit instantiation of the target in the text called an aspect term
“battery life”
, forming an aspect sentiment triplet as specified according to the Aspect Sentiment Triplet Extraction (ASTE) task
[
16
]
. Moreover, the annotation can also include a more coarse-grained property
“Battery#Operational_performance”
, which is an aspect category, and expresses the attribute of the entity towards which the opinion is specifically directed. Aspect categories enable the encoding of cases where the aspect term is missing and the target sentiment is expressed implicitly. In addition to triplets elements, the quadruple annotations are assigned categories too, adhering to Aspect-Category-Opinion-Sentiment (ACOS) guidelines
[
1
]
.

The existing datasets used for fine-grained opinion mining tasks are derived from SemEval tasks and are relatively small, limited to specific domains, and collected exclusively from review websites. As highlighted in
[
18
]
, these datasets are helpful in comparing different ABSA methods but are not sufficient for addressing real-world use cases. The subjective annotation of opinions by human annotators is a non-trivial task and requires strict adherence to annotation guidelines, as documented in
[
13
]
.

LLMs are few-shot learners
[
8
]
and exhibit instruction following and reasoning capabilities
[
2
]
, which warrants the assessment of their use for annotating opinions. In this work, we investigate the use of LLMs to annotate text with fine-grained opinions. We also propose a novel LLM-based Adjudication approach that combines multiple opinion annotations for a given text input to resolve inter-annotator disagreement and obtain final opinion annotations. Our main contributions include:

•

Evaluation of fine-grained opinion annotation accuracy of LLMs (on ASTE and ACOS)

•

Declarative LLM-driven opinion annotation pipeline using DSPy.

•

We propose a method for aggregating annotations from multiple LLM-driven artificial annotators to produce final annotations using LLM as an annotation adjudicator.

2.3
Prompt Engineering

LLMs are known to be sensitive to the prompts
[
19
]
and exhibit a higher level of variability in performing complex tasks
[
14
]
. To minimise the spurious interaction between the prompt and model selection as a confounding variable, we adopt
DSPy

[
6
]
and the approach of
“programming LLMs”
rather than prompting them naively. It shifts the focus from performing string manipulation on prompt strings to programming with structured, declarative modules that automatically prepare prompts using a small sample of annotated examples.

In this work, we propose a declarative pipeline based on LLMs for fine-grained opinion annotation. In addition to utilising individual LLMs to annotate text with fine-grained opinions, we also propose a novel LLM-based Adjudication approach to combine multiple annotations to resolve inter-annotator disagreement and obtain final annotations.

3.2
Datasets

To align our experiments with those of the original ASTE and ACOS tasks, we use subsections of the original dataset splits, as shown in Table
1(b)
. We disregard the training splits for each dataset as we are not fine-tuning or training the LLMs in our pipeline. For the development splits, we use random sampling to select half of the entries as possible in-context learning (ICL) examples required by the optimal prompt, then use the other half of the development set for evaluating which number of ICLs (5, 10, or 15) in the prompt produces the best performing model. This division is equal across all datasets’ development sets, except for ASTE’s lap14 and ACOS’s restaurant datasets, which each have one more example in their prompt evaluation than in their prompt creation. Finally, the test splits are kept the same to evaluate performance across multiple LLMs in our experiments.

4
Method

The fine-grained opinion annotation pipeline has multiple stages: (i) Preparing and evaluating LLM-specific prompts, (ii) Redundant Opinion annotation using multiple LLMs, and (iii) Annotation adjudication using LLM to create collaborative opinion annotations.

4.1
Problem Statements

The two steps of annotating opinions using the fine-grained opinion formulations involve identifying a set of opinions expressed in the input text
T_{i}
. As with human annotators, the process generates multiple annotations for the same input
T_{i}
, followed by adjudication that resolves disagreements between annotators to produce final annotations.

LLM as opinion annotators
Given the input text
Ti
from corpora
\mathcal{T}
, our objective is to extract all expressed opinions
Oi=\{o_{1},o_{2},..o_{n}\}
. The elements of single opinion (
o_{j}
) is task dependant:

•

ASTE opinions are formulated as the triple
o_{j}=\{at,s,op\}
, where
at
is the aspect term,
s
is the sentiment and
op
is the opinion term.

•

ACOS opinions are quadruples
o_{j}=\{at,s,op,ac\}
, where
at
,
s
and
op
encode similar concepts as ASTE and
ac
is aspect category made up of entity
E
and attribute
A
(expressed as
E\#A
).

The LLM-driven annotation pipeline maps input corpora
\mathcal{T}
to
\mathcal{O}^{k}
using
k
LLM-based annotators.

LLM as annotation adjudicators
We use LLM to perform the adjudication to combine opinions annotated by
k
different LLMs to obtain the final opinion annotation, i.e.,
\mathcal{A}\mathrm{dj}_{\mathrm{LLM}}:(\mathcal{T},\mathcal{O}^{1},\dots,\mathcal{O}^{k})\rightarrow\mathcal{O}
. This method is motivated by an ensemble machine learning technique called stacking
[
15
]
.

4.2
Declarative Annotation Pipeline

Figure 2
:
LLM-Based Annotation Pipeline using DSPy (Left) with LLM-as-adjudicator (Right)

The proposed annotation pipeline as illustrated in Fig
2
uses LLMs for automatic annotation. However, LLMs are known to be sensitive to the prompts
[
19
]
and exhibit a higher level of variability in performing complex tasks
[
14
]
. To minimise the spurious interaction between the prompt and model selection as a confounding variable, we adopt the
DSPy

[
6
]
method of programming LLMs rather than prompting them naively. DSPy’s pipeline outputs an optimised prompt and the mechanism to facilitate data input and generated output in our workflow.

Prior to annotating the dataset, we use DSPy to generate an optimal prompt. It uses a data model to provide a general description of its inputs (text) and expected output, along with the data types. We use a small number of annotated examples from the Dev set for this purpose, giving one half to the DSPy program as a pool of ICL candidates to construct optimal prompts for the LLM using 5, 10, or 15 annotated examples from the Dev set. Next, we use the other half of the Dev set to evaluate each prompt and determine the number of ICL examples that is most effective for the model. Once the final configuration has been selected, the optimal DSPy model is run on the test set to produce a set of opinion annotations. It should be noted that in our pipeline, we never fine-tune the LLM. We repeat this across multiple LLMs across multiple sizes to obtain multiple annotations for the test data. Finally, we repeat this process to optimise an adjudication prompt that resolves redundant annotations into the final annotations.

In our experiments, we used models of three different sizes: mini ( 4B parameters), small ( 14B parameters), and medium ( 32B parameters). The assignment of LLMs as

\mathcal{A}_{1}
, which is the notation assigned to the best-performing LLM-based annotator.

Each experiment was run on a single Nvidia A40 GPU, with vLLM
[
9
]
used to serve each model through the various steps of our pipeline, as required by DSPy for OpenAI-compliant API access. We keep the LLM hyperparameters constant across all models. The temperature is set to 0.0 to ensure the most deterministic generation. The model’s context window determines the input sequence length, and the generated sequence’s output length is set to 16384. Since the objective of the experiments is to conduct annotations using LLMs’ common-sense understanding, we do not use the training data. We use half of the small evaluation set provided for DSPy prompt optimisation to generate prompts with 5, 10, and 15 ICL examples, then use the remaining evaluation set examples to select the best-performing DSPy-wrapped prompt for annotating the test split.

We evaluate the performance of multiple LLM-based annotation models on the ASTE and ACOS tasks using standard exact match metrics. For each dataset, we report precision (P), recall (R), and F1-score across different data splits or subsets, labelled as Mini, Small, and Med.

8
Conclusion

This work demonstrates that Large Language Models can effectively serve as automatic annotators, reducing the high cost and effort of creating human-annotated datasets for fine-grained opinion analysis. By implementing a declarative DSPy pipeline for the annotation process and introducing a novel LLM-based adjudication method, we have shown that LLMs can produce high-quality, reliable annotations for ASTE opinion triplets and ACOS quadruples. We evaluated the human alignment of the produced annotations using the F1-score and found that ACOS tasks show greater deviation from human annotations than ASTE tasks do. We further investigate and discuss the challenges that make ACOS more difficult than ASTE. As customary with human annotators, we report the Kirppendorff
\alpha
to assess the reliability of the annotated dataset. Our results across various model sizes confirm the viability of this approach, paving the way for a more scalable, cost-effective method to build detailed datasets by aiding human annotators or providing additional annotators.
