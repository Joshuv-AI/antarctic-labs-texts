Accuracy of seven three-dimensional facial scanning systems compared with cone-beam computed tomography: a pilot study
Authors: Anna Dovhan, Dmytro Filonenko, Roman Palyvoda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAccurate three-dimensional (3D) reproduction of facial soft-tissue geometry is a prerequisite for planning and fabricating maxillofacial epitheses. This single-subject pilot study compared the surface agreement of seven 3D facial scanning configurations available on the Ukrainian market against a cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT)-derived reference surface, reporting trueness-related surface-deviation metrics and acquisition time. Because only one participant was examined and no repeated acquisitions were obtained, the work is exploratory and does not establish device precision (repeatability) or population-level accuracy. A prospective comparative pilot acquisition was performed on one healthy adult volunteer at rest on a single day. Three radiopaque fiducial markers were placed over low-mobility sites (glabella, both temporal regions). A CBCT volume (Planmeca ProMax 3D Mid) was segmented in 3D Slicer (v5.10.0) to yield the reference soft-tissue surface. Seven configurations were assessed: Shining 3D MetiSmile, Creality CR-Scan Raptor Pro, Revopoint Miraco Pro, RAYFace Facial Scanner, Planmeca ProMax 3D ProFace, and Qlone Dental App (iPad and iPhone). In CloudCompare (v2.14.alpha), fiducial-based Point-Pairs registration was followed by Iterative Closest Point (ICP) alignment, and cloud-to-mesh (C2M) surface-deviation distances were computed. Only descriptive statistics were used. Across the evaluated low-mobility region, the structured-light devices showed sub-millimetre surface deviation, with Shining 3D MetiSmile and Creality CR-Scan Raptor Pro showing the smallest dispersion (RMSE 0.288 and 0.328 mm). RAYFace recorded the highest proportion of points within ≤ 2 mm (98.74%) and the shortest acquisition time (5 s). Revopoint Miraco Pro and Planmeca ProMax 3D ProFace showed larger but still sub-millimetre deviations. Qlone deviated markedly (iPad and iPhone), with its upper error range approaching the fixed computation limit, so its tail is under-represented. In this single volunteer and over rigid, low-mobility regions only, structured-light scanners showed closer agreement with the CBCT-derived surface than the smartphone photogrammetry application. Differences among the leading structured-light devices were within the expected uncertainty of the reference surface and should not be over-interpreted as a device ranking. Introduction

Reconstructing the face after oncological resection, trauma, or congenital anomaly is among the more demanding tasks in maxillofacial rehabilitation. Custom facial epitheses must reproduce the external soft-tissue contour closely, so the quality of the captured surface geometry largely determines the prosthetic outcome. Traditional impressions taken in alginate or polyvinylsiloxane are poorly tolerated, can obstruct breathing as the material sets, and reproduce the moving parts of the face only approximately (Cruz et al. 2020a, b).

Non-contact optical capture, by either structured light or photogrammetry, can record a dense facial surface within seconds and pass it straight into a CAD/CAM prosthetic pipeline, cutting time at the chair while making repeated digital records easier to reproduce (Knoops et al. 2017; Tarba et al. 2025). The market spans dedicated facial scanners, general-purpose structured-light units, and smartphone photogrammetry apps, which differ widely in capture principle, resolution, cost, and ergonomics.

Comparative evidence remains thin: most reports examine only one or two systems, use differing protocols, or rely on phantoms rather than living faces (Mai and Lee 2020; Mai et al. 2021; Tangthaweesuk and Raocharernporn 2025). A soft-tissue shell extracted from a CBCT volume can serve as an in-vivo reference, although it inherits a threshold-dependent uncertainty (Ghamri et al. 2023; Megkousidis and Amm 2025).

Against this background, the present pilot study compared seven scanning configurations available in Ukraine with a CBCT-derived reference surface using one standardised pipeline. To our knowledge, this is among the first studies to compare seven commercially available facial-scanning configurations under an identical acquisition workflow, an identical CBCT reference surface, an identical region of interest, and an identical software pipeline, enabling a direct, like-for-like comparison across device classes. The aim was limited and exploratory: to obtain preliminary, hypothesis-generating estimates of surface deviation (trueness) and acquisition time over a defined low-mobility region in a single volunteer. Device precision (repeatability and reproducibility) and clinical prosthetic performance were explicitly outside the scope of this design.

Materials and methods

Study design

This was a prospective, single-centre, single-subject pilot (proof-of-concept) acquisition. One healthy adult volunteer was scanned at rest on a single day so that all configurations were captured under matched biological conditions within one session. This pilot study was designed to generate preliminary data for future sample-size estimation rather than for hypothesis testing. The design supports descriptive comparison only; it cannot estimate inter-individual variability, repeatability, or population accuracy, and no inferential statistics were applied.

Fiducial marker placement

Three radiopaque markers (Suremarks DentalMark; IZI Medical Products, Owings Mills, MD, USA) were applied to skin sites with minimal displacement during facial movement: the glabella and the left and right temporal regions. These served as corresponding control points for Point-Pairs registration and for visual verification of alignment across modalities. Three markers were chosen rather than four or five because three non-collinear points are the minimum required to define a unique rigid transformation for coarse initialisation, and restricting the count to three limited skin coverage, preparation time, and the number of low-mobility sites required; this was considered sufficient because final alignment was refined by full-surface ICP rather than by the markers alone. Because the three points lie close to a single coronal plane, rotational constraint about the antero-posterior axis is limited, which is noted as a methodological constraint (Sect. 4.1). The three markers served only as a coarse initialiser for the Point-Pairs step; final alignment was governed by full-surface ICP over the ROI, so the primary metric results (Table 2) do not depend on the marker geometry. The limited rotational constraint of three near-coplanar points is adequate for initialisation but not for fiducial-only alignment, as demonstrated in Sect. 3.8.

Acquisition of the reference model

CBCT was performed on a Planmeca ProMax 3D Mid unit (Planmeca Oy, Helsinki, Finland) using the standard maxillofacial protocol under the ALARA principle. Acquisition and reconstruction parameters were: voxel size 0.400 mm (isotropic), field of view Ø201 × 201 mm (cylindrical), reconstruction matrix 502 × 502 × 502, tube voltage 90 kV, tube current 8 mA, exposure time 13.883 s. The DICOM dataset was imported into 3D Slicer (v5.10.0; https://www.slicer.org) and the facial soft-tissue surface isolated by threshold-based segmentation (threshold grey-value range − 300 to 305, expressed in CBCT grey values rather than Hounsfield units; Segment Editor Threshold effect; no additional smoothing or decimation; default Closed surface representation). The lower bound (− 300) was placed at the air–skin intensity transition that defines the external soft-tissue boundary, providing an anatomically defined rather than arbitrary threshold, and a single window was applied to the whole volume to avoid region-dependent operator decisions. Segmentation was performed by a single trained operator. The surface was exported as STL and used as the reference for all comparisons.

Because this reference is itself derived by thresholding, its dependence on the chosen threshold was quantified directly: the segmentation was repeated with the lower grey-value threshold shifted by ± 50 units and each resulting surface compared (cloud-to-mesh) with the primary reference. Within the evaluated ROI this displaced the reference surface by a mean of 0.08 mm (P95 0.4 mm), of the same order as the threshold-segmentation uncertainty measured for CT/CBCT skeletal surfaces (Ghamri et al. 2023) and comparable to the differences observed among the best-performing scanners; the reference is therefore robust to the choice of threshold within the ROI, and device ranking is interpreted with caution (Sects. 4 and 4.1).

Evaluated scanning configurations and region of interest

Six devices in seven configurations were evaluated (Table 1). The region of interest (ROI) for all comparisons was restricted to the glabella and bilateral temporal regions — rigid, low-mobility, non-hair-bearing sites corresponding to the fiducial locations. Mobile and morphologically complex regions (nasal tip, periorbital, perioral) and hair-bearing and peripheral facial areas were excluded from analysis. The reported agreement therefore reflects best-case, low-mobility surfaces and does not characterise the mobile regions most relevant to nasal, orbital, or auricular epitheses.

Scanning protocol

Each scanner was operated according to the manufacturer’s instructions by a separate operator who had completed the respective manufacturer’s training for the device they handled. All operators were trained clinicians with prior facial-scanning experience and of comparable expertise. Beyond completing the manufacturer’s training, each operator performed practice acquisitions and was calibrated to a common written acquisition protocol before data collection to standardise technique across devices. Each operator followed the same standardised acquisition protocol (consistent natural head position, neutral expression, and capture conditions), which limits but does not eliminate operator-related variation. Because a different trained operator performed each acquisition, operator effects cannot be separated from device effects; this confounding is acknowledged as a limitation (Sect. 4.1). All acquisitions were performed by the operators on the same day. All configurations were acquired under the same controlled level of illumination — uniform, diffuse artificial lighting compliant with EN 12464-1, with direct daylight excluded — and at a room temperature maintained within 20–21 °C. Because optical scanners are sensitive to illumination and surface reflectance, these conditions were kept comparable across devices. The volunteer’s facial skin was cleaned and left free of make-up, oils, and reflective residues before scanning. The volunteer held a standardised natural head position with a neutral expression and lips in light contact throughout. Each configuration was captured once; no repeated acquisitions were obtained. Surface models were exported in STL format for analysis. Mesh pre-processing comprised cropping to the ROI and involved no decimation or smoothing; surface normals were computed in CloudCompare with default settings; point-cloud density differed between devices and was not normalised before C2M computation; this is acknowledged as a limitation (Sect. 4.1).

Quantitative analysis

Processing used CloudCompare (v2.14.alpha; https://www.cloudcompare.org). The pipeline was: (1) crop both scanned and reference surfaces to the ROI; (2) coarse fiducial-based Point-Pairs registration from the three marker centroids, then fine ICP alignment at 90% surface overlap (Besl and McKay 1992); (3) computation of signed C2M distances from each scanned point to the nearest reference facet (Lague et al. 2013), within a fixed maximum distance of 6.885 mm; and (4) extraction of final RMSE (after ICP), mean, standard deviation (SD), median, 95th percentile of absolute distances (P95), and the proportion of points with absolute deviation ≤ 2 mm. ICP was run with rotation and translation enabled and scale held fixed (no scaling), a random sampling limit of 50,000 points, and the program’s default convergence criterion (a root-mean-square difference threshold of 1.0 × 10⁻⁵ between successive iterations); the final-overlap parameter (90%) sets the proportion of closest point pairs retained at convergence, with the worst-fitting 10% rejected.

Two features of this pipeline constrain interpretation and are stated explicitly. First, ICP minimises point-to-surface distance by construction, and the 90% overlap setting discards the worst-fitting 10% of points; consequently the post-ICP RMSE is not an independent measure of accuracy and tends to understate deviation, more so for devices with localised artefacts. To quantify this dependence, a sensitivity analysis was performed in which the same C2M metrics were recomputed under two alternative registrations: ICP at 100% overlap (no point rejection) and fiducial-only alignment without ICP; results are reported in Sect. 3.8 (Table 3). Second, the 6.885 mm maximum was a single fixed computation limit applied identically to all devices, not a clinical criterion; absolute deviations above it are clamped. For Qlone (iPhone) the P95 (6.870 mm) approaches this limit, so the upper tail of its error distribution is truncated and its true deviation is underestimated. Facial-surface discrepancies of up to 2 mm have repeatedly been reported as clinically acceptable for soft-tissue three-dimensional measurement and facial scanning in oral and maxillofacial settings (Knoops et al. 2017; Revilla-León et al. 2021); the proportion of surface points within ≤ 2 mm was therefore adopted as an indicative metric of clinical acceptability. Because this threshold is a convention derived from the facial-scanning literature rather than a formally standardised epithesis-specific criterion, it is interpreted as indicative only.

Data management and statistical analysis

Metrics were tabulated in Microsoft Excel (v16.95.3) and visualised in jamovi (v2.6.45.0) with R (v4.x) via the Rj editor. Given the single-participant design, only descriptive statistics were computed; no inferential testing was performed. Confidence intervals were not reported because, with a single subject and a single acquisition per configuration, no independent replicates exist from which to estimate the sampling variability of the device-level metrics; any interval computed across the spatially autocorrelated points of one scan would describe within-scan spatial dispersion rather than device precision and would understate the true uncertainty. Interval estimation is therefore deferred to the planned multi-subject, repeated-measures study. Throughout, terminology follows ISO 5725: trueness denotes closeness of the scanned surface to the reference; precision/repeatability denotes agreement between repeated acquisitions (not assessed here); and surface deviation denotes the C2M distance. The single-scan SD reported below describes the spatial dispersion of surface deviation within one acquisition and is not a measure of precision.

Ethics, consent, biometric data, and software

Ethics and consent. The study followed the Declaration of Helsinki (2024 revision) and was approved by the institutional Bioethics Commission (protocol No. 205, 27 April 2026; institution named on the non-anonymous title page). The volunteer gave written informed consent covering participation, acquisition and processing of 3D facial biometric data, and anonymous publication of derived images and data.

Biometric data protection. 3D facial surfaces are identifiable biometric personal data under the Law of Ukraine “On Personal Data Protection” (No. 2297-VI) and the GDPR (Regulation (EU) 2016/679). All raw and processed data were held only on password-protected local workstations within the institutional network, with no internet transfer or cloud synchronisation; mobile acquisitions were performed offline and transferred by wired connection. Exported STL meshes contained no patient metadata. It is acknowledged that removing mesh metadata does not by itself anonymise facial geometry, and a residual re-identification risk remains; access was therefore restricted to the named investigators, and data are retained per institutional policy and shared only on reasonable request under a data-use agreement.

Software. Open-source: 3D Slicer 5.10.0 (BSD-3), CloudCompare 2.14.alpha (GPL v2), jamovi 2.6.45.0 (AGPL v3), R 4.x (GPL v2). Commercial: Microsoft Excel 16.95.3 (institutional licence). Proprietary scanner software was used under each manufacturer’s licence (Shining 3D; Creality; Revopoint; Ray Co.; Planmeca Romexis; EyeCue Vision Technologies/Qlone). All licences were valid at the time of the study.

Results

Descriptive metrics for all configurations are summarised in Table 2.

Dispersion of surface deviation (SD)

The smallest dispersion (SD) of C2M deviation was recorded for RAYFace Facial Scanner (0.473 mm) and Shining 3D MetiSmile (0.482 mm), followed by Creality CR-Scan Raptor Pro (0.524 mm), Revopoint Miraco Pro (0.575 mm), and Planmeca ProMax 3D ProFace (0.668 mm); all structured-light devices remained sub-millimetre. Qlone showed substantially larger dispersion on both iPad (1.529 mm) and iPhone (2.157 mm) (Fig. 1). These differences among the structured-light devices are small relative to the reference-surface uncertainty and the single-scan, one-operator-per-device design, and are not interpreted as a robust ranking.

Final RMSE after ICP registration

Final RMSE followed the same broad order: lowest for Shining 3D MetiSmile and Creality CR-Scan Raptor Pro, intermediate for RAYFace, Revopoint, and Planmeca, and substantially higher for Qlone (iPad and iPhone) (Fig. 2). As noted in Sect. 2.6, post-ICP RMSE is optimisation-dependent and should be read as a lower bound on deviation rather than an independent accuracy figure.

Mean and median C2M distance

Mean and median C2M distances were lowest for the leading structured-light devices — Shining 3D MetiSmile (mean 0.417 mm, median 0.216 mm) and Creality CR-Scan Raptor Pro (mean 0.409 mm, median 0.265 mm) — and progressively higher for RAYFace, Revopoint, Planmeca, and the two Qlone configurations (Fig. 3; Table 2). For every device the median was below the mean, consistent with the right-skewed, non-negative distribution expected for C2M surface distances.

Proportion of points within the ≤ 2 mm indicative threshold

The proportion of surface points within ≤ 2 mm was highest for RAYFace (98.74%), followed closely by Creality, Shining, and Revopoint (all > 97%). Planmeca reached 92.94%. Qlone was markedly lower (iPad 58.06%; iPhone 43.80%) (Fig. 4).

Acquisition time

RAYFace had the shortest acquisition (5 s) owing to automated single-capture imaging. Planmeca required 43 s; Shining (1 min 06 s) and Creality (1 min 00 s) were comparable; Qlone required operator-guided circumferential capture (iPad 1 min 18 s; iPhone 1 min 00 s); and Revopoint required the longest, stepwise capture (2 min 39 s). Times reflect single acquisitions and depend partly on the device workflow and operator familiarity.

Sensitivity to registration settings

The same C2M metrics were recomputed under two alternative registrations to assess how strongly the primary results (ICP, 90% overlap; Table 2) depend on the alignment procedure: ICP at 100% overlap (no point rejection) and fiducial-only alignment without ICP (Table 3). For Creality CR-Scan Raptor Pro, recomputing the comparison at 100% overlap (no point rejection), with the same 6.885 mm maximum-distance limit as the primary analysis, increased the surface-deviation dispersion markedly relative to the 90% ICP result (SD 0.524 → 1.459 mm; P95 1.248 → 2.818 mm; points ≤ 2 mm 98.15 → 90.45%), whereas the mean changed little (0.409 → 0.374 mm). This indicates that the worst-fitting points excluded by the 90% overlap setting carry most of the deviation, and confirms that the primary post-ICP metrics should be read as lower bounds. Fiducial-only alignment (Point-Pairs, scale fixed at 1.0, no ICP) produced a comparably large dispersion (SD 1.091 mm; P95 3.263 mm; points ≤ 2 mm 91.68%) with an entirely positive C2M distribution (mean + 0.684 mm), indicating a small systematic outward offset of the scan relative to the reference in the absence of fine alignment. The larger deviation under fiducial-only alignment reflects both the absence of full-surface refinement and the limited rotational constraint of three near-coplanar markers; once ICP is applied, agreement returns to the Table 2 values, indicating that final surface agreement is determined by the surface fit rather than by the markers, whose role is limited to coarse initialisation. Across both alternative registrations the deviation was 2–3 times larger than under the primary 90% ICP analysis, underscoring the sensitivity of the reported surface agreement to the registration procedure. The same pattern was reproduced on two additional configurations of differing baseline trueness. For the high-trueness Shining 3D MetiSmile, removing the 90% point rejection (ICP at 100% overlap) raised the dispersion from SD 0.482 mm to 1.767 mm and reduced the proportion of points within 2 mm from 97.90% to 70.79%. For the low-trueness Qlone Dental App (iPad), by contrast, the choice of registration had little effect: surface deviation remained high and broadly unchanged across ICP 90% (Table 2), ICP 100% (SD 2.376 mm; 49.75% within 2 mm) and fiducial-only alignment (SD 2.382 mm; 49.37% within 2 mm). This contrast suggests that point rejection during ICP improves the apparent agreement mainly for scanners whose errors are spatially localised; for a device with uniformly high deviation there are no well-fitting regions to favour, so the registration setting is largely immaterial. The primary 90% ICP metrics should therefore be interpreted as best-case estimates, most strongly so for the higher-trueness devices.

Discussion

Within this exploratory, single-participant pilot, the structured-light devices agreed more closely with the CBCT-derived reference than the smartphone photogrammetry application across every metric, with smaller differences also seen within the structured-light group. These observations are exploratory and concern one volunteer and one low-mobility region.

Shining 3D MetiSmile and Creality CR-Scan Raptor Pro showed the smallest surface deviation (RMSE 0.288 and 0.328 mm), consistent with published figures for contemporary structured-light systems (Al Hamad et al. 2025; Cascos et al. 2020). However, the differences separating the top four structured-light configurations are of the same order as the expected uncertainty of a threshold-segmented CBCT reference surface (Ghamri et al. 2023). With a single subject and a single scan per device, these gaps are not a reliable basis for ranking; we therefore describe device classes rather than naming a single best device.

RAYFace combined a high proportion of points within ≤ 2 mm with by far the shortest acquisition (5 s). Short capture reduces movement artefact and is advantageous for paediatric, anxious, or post-surgical patients, making rapid dedicated facial scanners attractive for routine use in principle (Al Hamad et al. 2025; Singh et al. 2024), though this was not tested clinically here.

Revopoint Miraco Pro, a general-purpose portable scanner, gave sub-millimetre deviation at a lower cost but the longest capture time, consistent with reports on consumer-grade structured-light scanners (Bartella et al. 2023; Kumar and Maiti 2025). Planmeca ProMax 3D ProFace showed acceptable mean deviation but a higher P95 and a lower proportion within ≤ 2 mm, suggesting localised regions of larger deviation, plausibly related to fixed-geometry multi-camera photogrammetry; its advantage is simultaneous hard- and soft-tissue capture in one coordinate frame.

Qlone showed substantially larger deviation on both platforms, exceeding the range generally considered acceptable for high-accuracy epithesis work and in line with prior reports on consumer mobile photogrammetry (Barone et al. 2024; Mai and Lee 2020; Megkousidis and Amm 2025). Its apparent performance is, moreover, optimistic because its upper error tail was truncated at the fixed 6.885 mm limit. Such tools may still suit non-critical tasks such as preliminary screening, tele-assessment, and teaching.

Comparison with previous studies

The sub-millimetre trueness observed for the structured-light scanners is consistent with, and at the favourable end of, the range reported in comparable facial-scanning validation studies, which have described mean surface deviations of roughly 0.2–1.0 mm for dedicated and general-purpose structured-light systems and progressively larger errors for smartphone photogrammetry (Knoops et al. 2017; Cascos et al. 2020; Mai and Lee 2020; Tangthaweesuk and Raocharernporn 2025). The several-fold larger deviation of the Qlone application echoes previous reports that consumer photogrammetry apps, while inexpensive and portable, do not yet reach the accuracy of dedicated optical systems (Barone et al. 2024; Megkousidis and Amm 2025). Two caveats specific to the present design temper these comparisons. First, analysis was restricted to a rigid, low-mobility ROI, so the values represent a best case and cannot be extrapolated to the mobile mid-facial and peri-oral regions that dominate many prosthetic indications. Second, as the registration-sensitivity analysis shows (Sect. 3.8), the reported surface agreement depends materially on the alignment procedure; cross-study comparisons are therefore meaningful only when the registration pipeline is comparable.

Clinically, the differences between device classes matter more than the small gaps within the structured-light group. Dedicated and general-purpose structured-light scanners integrate readily into digital prosthetic and surgical workflows, feeding directly into CAD/CAM design and 3D-printing pipelines for maxillofacial epitheses and surgical guides (Tarba et al. 2025). Emerging AI-assisted facial-scanning and reconstruction methods, together with the integration of optical capture and CBCT for combined hard- and soft-tissue planning, are likely to broaden these applications further, but they place a premium on the geometric fidelity of the input surface (Singh et al. 2024). The present findings suggest that, within their tested limits, contemporary structured-light devices already provide surfaces of sufficient trueness to serve as that input over low-mobility regions, whereas smartphone photogrammetry is better suited to non-critical tasks.

Clinical significance

The following device-selection considerations are offered cautiously and rest solely on the measured surface agreement of one healthy volunteer over a rigid, low-mobility region; they do not reflect prosthesis fit, patient outcomes, or clinical workflow performance, none of which were assessed here. On surface-trueness grounds alone, the dedicated and general-purpose structured-light scanners (e.g. Shining 3D MetiSmile, Creality CR-Scan Raptor Pro) showed the closest agreement and would be the first candidates to evaluate where high surface fidelity is required, such as maxillofacial epithesis planning. Where acquisition speed and patient cooperation are limiting — for example paediatric, anxious, or post-surgical patients, or chairside orthodontic and implant records — a dedicated facial scanner with very short single-capture acquisition (e.g. RAYFace) may warrant consideration, having combined a high proportion of points within 2 mm with a five-second capture. Systems that acquire hard and soft tissue in one coordinate frame (e.g. Planmeca ProMax 3D ProFace) may be of interest for combined surgical and implant planning, although they showed somewhat larger localised deviation. Smartphone photogrammetry, in its current form, appears better suited to non-critical tasks such as preliminary screening, tele-assessment, patient education, and teaching than to definitive reconstructive work. These are exploratory considerations based on surface trueness only; device ranking and any clinical-suitability conclusions remain provisional and require confirmation in multi-subject studies that also cover mobile facial regions before they can inform clinical practice.

Limitations

This pilot has several substantial limitations. (1) A single volunteer precludes any assessment of inter-individual variability and any inferential statistics; results are descriptive only. (2) No repeated acquisitions were obtained, so repeatability and reproducibility (i.e. precision) were not assessed. Furthermore, each scanner was operated by a different manufacturer-trained operator, so device and operator effects are confounded and the reported differences cannot be attributed to the devices alone; however, operator effects of this kind could plausibly account for the small differences within the structured-light group but not for the several-fold gap between the structured-light devices and Qlone, so the principal finding is unlikely to be an operator artefact. (3) The ROI was confined to rigid, low-mobility regions and excluded the mobile, complex areas most relevant to nasal, orbital, and auricular epitheses, so the figures represent a best case. (4) The CBCT-derived reference carries its own segmentation-dependent uncertainty (measured here as mean 0.08 mm, P95 0.4 mm within the ROI under a ± 50 grey-value threshold shift), of a magnitude comparable to the inter-device differences, limiting ranking. (5) The analysis pipeline itself bounds the reported deviation: ICP alignment with 10% point rejection minimises and partially truncates deviation, and the fixed 6.885 mm computation limit truncated the upper tail for the worst performer, so the post-ICP metrics should be read as lower bounds. (6) Optical and CBCT surfaces were not acquired simultaneously, and head-support contact during CBCT may have displaced soft tissue at the temporal sites. (7) Differing point-cloud densities between devices may influence distribution-based metrics. Future work should use multiple subjects, extended ROIs including mobile regions, repeated-measures and multi-operator designs, simultaneous or closely timed reference acquisition, and sensitivity analyses of registration settings.

Conclusions

In one healthy volunteer and over rigid, low-mobility facial regions, structured-light scanners agreed more closely with a CBCT-derived reference surface than smartphone photogrammetry. Shining 3D MetiSmile and Creality CR-Scan Raptor Pro showed the smallest surface deviation, while RAYFace combined a high proportion of points within ≤ 2 mm with the shortest acquisition time; differences among these leading devices fell within the expected uncertainty of the reference and should not be read as a definitive ranking. Qlone showed deviations too large for high-accuracy reconstructive work under the conditions tested, with its true error likely greater than measured because of tail truncation. These are preliminary, hypothesis-generating findings; multi-subject, repeated-measures validation including mobile facial regions is required before any clinical recommendation can be made.
