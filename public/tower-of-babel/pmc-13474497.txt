Clinical features, triggers, and risk factors of severe gastrointestinal involvement in anti-NXP2-positive juvenile dermatomyositis: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Xin Yao, Wei Zhang, Xinning Wang, Ying Chi, Yuan Liu, Xiaolei Wang, Wenquan Niu, Jianguo Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study aimed to examine the clinical characteristics, triggers and risk factors for gastrointestinal (GI) complications in anti-NXP2-positive JDM (anti-NXP2-JDM) patients.MethodsA retrospective analysis was performed on 57 anti-NXP2-JDM patients, including 19 with severe GI complications (GI group) and 38 without GI involvement (Non-GI group).ResultsThe GI group had a male-to-female ratio of 6:13 and a mean onset age of 5.83 years, with abdominal pain as the most common initial symptom. All severe GI involvement occurred during active JDM, and 16 patients (84%) had prodromal infections (potential triggers). Compared with the Non-GI group, the GI group showed significantly lower proximal muscle strength grade, CMAS score, BMI, hemoglobin, albumin, creatinine and lymphocyte count, and higher rates of severe JDM at onset, malnutrition, infection, skin ulcer, edema, livedo reticularis. The GI groups also had higher CAT-A score, CAT-D score, WBC, CRP, ESR, ferritin and LDH. Multivariate analysis identified severe JDM at onset, infection, malnutrition, CAT-A score, skin ulcer, edema and ESR as independent risk factors for GI complications, and lymphocyte count, proximal muscle strength, CMAS score, hemoglobin and albumin as protective factors. Kaplan-Meier analysis showed significantly different survival rates between groups. The proportion of deaths was 26.3% (5/19) in the GI group compared to 0% (0/38) in the non-GI group (p = 0.002).ConclusionsAnti-NXP2-JDM patients require vigilant monitoring and early intervention to mitigate GI complications and improve outcomes. Infection may be an important trigger. Risk factors for GI complications include severe JDM at onset, malnutrition, skin ulceration, edema, low CMAS score, high CAT-A score, lymphocytopenia, anemia, hypoalbuminemia and elevated ESR.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12969-026-01219-2.

Introduction

Juvenile dermatomyositis (JDM) is a rare inflammatory myopathy in children, marked by muscle weakness and skin symptoms [1–4]. The anti-nuclear matrix protein 2 (anti-NXP2) antibody is linked to severe manifestations and gastrointestinal (GI) complications, which occur in 22% to 37% of cases [5, 6], increasing morbidity and mortality. Despite the association with anti-NXP2 antibody, the specific characteristics and risk factors for GI complication in these patients are unclear, as previous studies have focused on muscle and skin involvement. Therefore, a retrospective analysis of clinical data from anti-NXP2-positive JDM (anti-NXP2-JDM) patients with GI issues is needed to identify risk and protective factors for better outcomes and timely interventions.

The study retrospectively collected clinical and laboratory data from anti-NXP2-JDM patients to compare those with and without GI involvement. Multivariate logistic regression was performed to identify independent predictors of GI issues, findings that enhance understanding of anti-NXP2-JDM clinical course and inform the development of improved management strategies to reduce complications and mortality.

Materials and methods

Patients

Patients with JDM who were hospitalized and diagnosed (based on Bohan and Peter criteria [7, 8]) at our hospital between November 1, 2016 and November 1, 2024, were included. Among these, 19 patients with anti-NXP2-JDM developed GI involvement (GI group). Cases 1–5 were previously published as a case series [9]. Thirty-eight anti-NXP2-JDM patients without GI involvement were randomly matched as controls (Non-GI group). GI involvement was defined as wall thickening, obstruction, ulcer, or perforation. GI involvement was diagnosed based on surgical findings, endoscopic evaluations, or clinical manifestations supported by radiological evidence (Table 1). All patient data were anonymized, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Capital Institute of Pediatrics (SHERLL2023028). This study was supported by the Research Foundation of Capital Institute of Pediatrics (Project NO. LCYJ-2023-11) and the Capital Funds for Health Improvement and Research (Project NO. 2020-2-2102).

Clinical and laboratory data

Clinical data collected included gender, age, disease duration, severity at JDM onset, disease status prior to GI symptoms, nutritional status, body mass index (BMI), infection, symptoms, proximal muscle strength, and skin lesions (such as ulcer, livedo reticularis, and calcinosis), Childhood Myositis Assessment Scale (CMAS) score, Cutaneous Assessment Tool-Activity (CAT-A) and Cutaneous Assessment Tool-Damage (CAT-D) score, and relevant laboratory findings. Severe JDM is defined as the presence of any of the following high-risk manifestations: (1) CMAS score < 15, or MMT8 score < 30, or presence of aspiration or dysphagia (to the point of inability to swallow); (2) Gastrointestinal vasculitis (as determined by imaging or presence of bloody stools); (3) Myocarditis with decreased ejection fraction; (4) Lung injury or rapidly progressive interstitial lung disease (RP-ILD) accompanied by dyspnea or hypoxemia; (5) Central nervous system disease (defined as decreased level of consciousness or seizures); (6) Severe skin ulceration or significant calcification leading to functional impairment; (7) Macrophage activation syndrome [10, 11]. The initial stage of the disease was defined as within three months of diagnosis. The progressive stage was defined as disease worsening despite treatment for at least three months. The recurrence stage was defined as the reappearance or worsening of rash and/or myositis following initial improvement that was sustained for more than three months with treatment. Malnutrition in this study refers to moderate and severe malnutrition, defined as below − 2 SD from the median (Z-score below − 2) for weight-for-height or BMI-for-age, according to WHO standards and Chinese data [12, 13]. Laboratory data included creatine kinase (CK), lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), hemoglobin (HGB), albumin (ALB), serum creatinine (SCr), C-reactive protein (CRP), erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), serum ferritin (SF), white blood cell (WBC) count, lymphocyte count, and lymphocyte subpopulation profiles. Laboratory data for the GI group were collected during the most severe episodes of GI involvement, whereas those for the Non-GI group were obtained during peak JDM activity. And all laboratory data were collected before the administration of CTX on that occasion. All 57 patients had measured WBC, lymphocyte count, CK, LDH, AST, ALT, HGB, ALB, SCr, CRP, ESR testing, and GI ultrasonography. Fifty-five patients had measured SF, while 54 had lymphocyte subpopulation analysis, including T, B, NK, CD4+T, and CD8+T cells. Naïve B cell testing was performed in 48 patients. Among the 19 patients with GI involvement, 8 underwent surgical exploration, 10 had endoscopic examinations, and 9 received histopathological evaluations. The follow-up period was defined as the time from the onset of GI involvement to either death or the end of the observation on November 1, 2024.

Autoantibody analyses

The myositis-specific antibodies (MSAs) test were performed on the patients’ first admission. Sixteen MSAs (MDA-5, NXP2, Jo-1, PM-SCL100, PL-7, PL-12, EJ, OJ, anti-Ro52, Ku, PM-Scl, SRP, SAE1, TIF1γ, Mi-2α and Mi-2β) were tested by immune dot blot hybridization using a commercial kit based on goat anti-human IgG antibodies (BlueDot Myositis 12 IgG, #MYO12D-24, D-TEK, Belgium).

Pathogen examination

Respiratory pathogen testing was performed using sputum or bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) specimens for nucleic acid detection of the following pathogens: influenza A virus (H1N1, H3N2), influenza B virus, respiratory syncytial virus (RSV), human parainfluenza virus, adenovirus, Mycoplasma pneumoniae, and Chlamydia pneumoniae. Sputum or BALF specimens were also subjected to bacterial culture and fungal culture. Stool pathogen testing included detection of norovirus, rotavirus, and Clostridioides difficile, as well as bacterial culture and fungal culture. Skin secretion testing included bacterial culture and fungal culture.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 27.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) and R version 4.4.3. Group comparisons involving demographic characteristics, clinical symptoms, and laboratory parameters were performed using the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables and the Mann-Whitney U test or independent t-test for continuous variables, with normality assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were used to identify predictors of GI involvement in anti-NXP2-JDM patients. To minimize false-positive results, a significance threshold of 0.001 was applied for variable selection in the logistic regression analyses. A nomogram was constructed to visualize the predictive value of exposure variables on clinical outcomes. Survival curves were generated using the Kaplan-Meier method, and the log-rank test was employed to compare survival between groups. Unless otherwise specified, a p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Clinical characteristics of the GI group

Demographics

Among the 19 anti-NXP2-JDM patients with GI involvement, the male-to-female ratio was 6:13, and the mean age at onset was 5.83 years. Clinical features are detailed in Supplementary Table 1.

Timeline and clinical status of GI

As shown in Fig. 1A, the mean time from JDM diagnosis to the onset of GI symptoms was 14 months. The shortest interval occurred at disease onset, while the longest was 68 months. The mean duration from symptom onset to severe GI events was 11 weeks, ranging from 1.5 weeks to 22 months. The average time from the onset of severe GI events to GI symptoms relieved was 14 weeks, ranging from 2 weeks to 6.4 months. Case 16 experienced intermittent hematochezia that persisted throughout the 25-month follow-up period.

All 19 patients developed GI complication during the active phase of JDM, occurring in the initial stage (3 cases), progressive stage (12), or recurrence stage (5). In Case 2, GI perforation occurred twice: once during the progressive stage and once during the recurrence stage. None of the patients experienced GI involvement during the stable phase of JDM (Supplementary Table 1).

Triggers

This study analyzed potential triggers of GI complication, including JDM disease activity, infection, cyclophosphamide (CTX). Firstly, as previously described, GI complication occurred during active disease stages. Secondly, sixteen patients experienced infection prior to GI involvement: eight cases occurred within three days of infection, seven between one week and two months after infection (mean interval 18.4 days), and one case had unclear infection timing. Cases 14 and 15 developed intestinal infection following the poor hand or dietary hygiene, subsequently presenting with fever, anorexia, abdominal pain, distension, and diarrhea, and ultimately progressed to GI ulceration with bleeding. Infections were located in the respiratory tract (11 cases), skin (5), GI system (5), and perianal area (1). Thirdly, patients developed severe GI complications after CTX administration within three days (3 cases), and others between one and fifteen months - with an average duration of five months (10). For the former, notably, each had mild abdominal pain before CTX, which progressed to GI bleeding or perforation following the first dose. For the latter, interestingly, some patients who developed GI complications after CTX infusion also experienced symptom relief following continued CTX therapy (6). Therefore, the relationship between CTX and gastrointestinal involvement cannot be determined.

Clinical features of GI

During the course of GI involvement, all 19 patients experienced abdominal pain. Additionally, it was the most common initial presenting symptom. Other symptoms are shown in Fig. 1B.

Based on the most severe GI lesion in each patient, the 19 cases were classified as perforation (11), ulcer (5), bowel wall thickening (2), and intestinal obstruction (1). As shown in Supplementary Table 2, the colon and duodenum were the most frequently affected sites of perforation and ulcer. Other affected sites included: the ileum, the ileocecum region, stomach, jejunum, cecum, and rectum. Four patients had perforations involving multiple GI sites. Among these, Case 11 presented with six simultaneous perforations: the anterior wall of the duodenal bulb, cecum, ascending colon, transverse colon, descending colon, and sigmoid colon. The perforation site in Case 6 could not be identified. Figure 1C and D show imaging findings of bleeding ulcer and bowel wall thickening, respectively.

Histopathological examination revealed that chronic inflammatory cell infiltration in the mucosa (9 cases), chronic inflammatory cell infiltration in the submucosa (3), vascular proliferation and small vessel vasculitis in the mucosa and submucosa (2), vascular dilation and congestion in both the mucosa and submucosa (1), and focal hemorrhage in the submucosa (1). Most samples showed disordered or incomplete bowel wall structure. Figure 1E shows the histopathological image from Case 10.

Therapy

The partial therapy both before and after GI complication, including glucocorticoids (GCs), CTX and surgical treatment, is summarized in Supplementary Table 1. Surgical intervention was performed in eight patients with GI perforation, with six undergoing enterostomy.

Comparison of clinical features of the GI and Non-GI groups

To identify potential risk factors for severe GI involvement, we compared baseline demographics, clinical findings, laboratory data, and treatment regimens between the GI and Non-GI groups (Table 1).

Comparison of baseline data

There was no statistically difference between the two groups in gender, age at onset, and course. However, significant differences were noted in proportion of severe JDM diagnosed at onset, infection, malnutrition, BMI, proximal muscle strength, skin ulcer, edema, and livedo reticularis, CMAS score, CAT-A and CAT-D score (p < 0.05).

Comparison of laboratory data

Compared to the Non-GI group, the GI group showed significantly elevated WBC, CRP, ESR, SF, and LDH levels, while HGB, ALB, SCr, lymphocyte count, T cell, B cell, NK cell, CD4+T cell, CD8+T cell, and naïve B cell counts were markedly decreased (p < 0.05) (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Comparison of therapy

We analyzed the primary drugs administered to the two groups over the preceding 3-month, 6-month periods (Supplementary Table 3). The GI group received higher doses of prednisone and a greater proportion of CTX than the Non-GI group (p < 0.05).

Exposure factors associated with GI involvement in anti-NXP2-JDM patients

Risk factors and protective factors

As shown in Table 2, after adjusting for age, gender, and disease course, the independent risk factors for GI complication included severe JDM at onset, preceding infection, malnutrition, CAT-A score, skin ulcer, edema, and ESR in anti-NXP2-JDM patients. Protective factors included lymphocyte count, proximal muscle strength, CMAS score, HGB, and ALB.

Risk prediction

The nomogram (Fig. 2A) illustrates the predictive weight of each independent risk factor. To improve clinical applicability, the nonogram incorporates severe JDM at onset, CMAS score, skin ulcer, edema, HGB level, and lymphocyte count at disease onset to estimate risk probability. Given its strong predictive influence, lymphocyte count was converted to an ordinal variable (1: ≤ 1150, 2:1151–1700, 3:1700–2700, 4: > 2700). For instance, a 5-year-old (score 1.25) female (score 2.25) with severe JDM at onset (score 2.5), a CMAS score of 10 (score 2.25), skin ulcer (score 3), HGB level of 100 g/L (score 4.5), and a lymphocyte count < 1000 cells/µL (score 10) would have a total score of 25.75, statistically, corresponding to a > 95% risk of severe GI involvement.

Outcome and Kaplan-Meier survival analysis

In this study, the case-fatality rate of GI involvement among patients with JDM was 26.3% (5 of 19 cases). With growing clinical experience, GI-related deaths in our center declined over time, and no fatalities occurred in the last 5 years (Fig. 2B). Of the 14 surviving patients, one died during the reversal of colostomy due to adrenal cortical insufficiency, two had successful reversal of ileostomy, and two remained in status post ileostomy.

Kaplan-Meier survival curves differed significantly between the GI groups and the Non-GI group (Fig. 2C). The log-rank test confirmed a statistically significant difference between the two groups (p = 0.0007).

Discussion

JDM with GI involvement is a potentially fatal complication, with limited related reports. The underlying triggers and risk factors remain unclear. Through clinical analysis of preceding infection, medication use, and mortality risk factors, this study aims to summarize the key findings that may guide early detection, diagnosis of GI complication in JDM, thereby improving prognosis. Our study covered 19 anti-NXP2-JDM patients with GI involvement. This is the largest cohort to date in terms of the number of anti-NXP2-JDM with GI involvement. Meanwhile, this study is also the first study to identify independent risk factors for severe GI involvement.

In this study, the mean age of GI group at onset was 5.83 years and females were more frequently affected, representing a younger demographic than the onset age of overall JDM [1, 4, 6].

We speculate that the potential triggers for severe GI events might include infection, disease activity, and medication.

Firstly, the most important and direct trigger is infection. In this study, eight patients developed GI lesions within 1–3 days of infection onset, with infection involving the respiratory tract, gastrointestinal system, and suppurative skin lesions. The mechanisms underlying infection-induced GI damage are not fully understood, but immune dysregulation triggered by infection may contribute to disease reactivation [6, 14, 15]. Several hypotheses have been formulated to explain the possible mechanism by which pathogens may induce autoimmune response in dermatomyositis (DM)/polymyositis (PM): (1) Modification of self-antigens by direct interaction between microbial and host proteins. In the GI tract, the gut epithelial barrier is maintained through tight junction proteins. Disassembly of these proteins can result in increased intestinal permeability, also known as “leaky gut”, leading to the passage of luminal factors such as bacterial and dietary components into the gut mucosa and systemic circulation, thus triggering inflammation [16]. (2) Release of clustered host proteins displaying cryptic epitopes [17]. (3) Induction of human antibodies carrying pathogenic idiotypes [17]. (4) Sequence homology between host proteins and microbial antigenic sites (molecular mimicry) [17]. Although these hypotheses explain the mechanism by which infection leads to immune system activation, the mechanism by which infection induces GI damage needs further study.

Secondly, in this study, all patients developed GI complications during active disease, including progressive stage and recurrence stage. Whether disease activity is caused by infection or other causes, the resulting imbalance of the immune system leads to further aggravation of inflammation and further damage to target organ. This differs from adult DM, where GI involvement typically occurs after the resolution of myositis, and the severity of GI damage is generally milder than in JDM, with a lower incidence of GI involvement than JDM [18, 19]. This may be due to the lower positivity rate of anti-NXP2 antibodies in adult DM than in JDM [20]. There is limited research on GI involvement in adult DM. Professor Shuang Ye’s team in China reported 56 cases of DM with anti-NXP2 antibody positivity, among which 10 patients exhibited GI involvement, with a GI incidence rate of 17.86% and 8 cases of GI perforation [19]. Another cohort study involving 114 patients with DM, reported by Professor Mohannad Abu-Hilal’s team in Canada, demonstrated a GI incidence rate of 23.68%. The manifestations of GI involvement included gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD) in 12.3%, inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) in 4.39%, and irritable bowel syndrome (IBS) in 3.51%; no cases of perforation were observed [21]. This suggests that the incidence of GI involvement in adults is similar to that in children, but the severity is less pronounced. This may be attributed to GI involvement in adult DM often occurring after the resolution of myositis, at which point the use of GCs and immunosuppressants is reduced, thereby lowering the risk of infection or perforation as complications of GI involvement.

Thirdly, the GI group received higher doses of glucocorticoids (GCs) and more frequent administration of CTX, likely reflecting the disease severity. Although some studies associate GI complications with GC use [22, 23], our data suggest symptoms improved in six patients following high-dose methylprednisolone pulse therapy. Therefore, we propose that GCs are unlikely to be the direct cause of GI involvement. Additionally, CTX may shorten intestinal villi and disrupt the mucosal barrier [24]; however, its role remains uncertain, as some patients showed improvement after CTX treatment. This suggests possible selection bias, as patients with more severe cases may have been more likely to receive CTX. Further studies using prospective cohorts and experimental models are needed to clarify the pharmacological impact on JDM-associated GI involvement. Notably, intensive immunosuppressive therapy can lead to lymphopenia, which is a key risk factor for GI involvement in JDM. In this study, perforations in three patients were suspected to be associated with CTX administration. Whether this is attributable to the impact of CTX on lymphocyte counts warrants further investigation. Consequently, selection of treatment strategy needs careful consideration following episodes of GI involvement in JDM.

Compared to those without GI involvement, the GI group had lower proximal muscle strength grade, higher prevalence of severe JDM at onset, skin ulcers and malnutrition, alongside lower CMAS score, BMI, ALB and lymphocyte counts, all of which demonstrated predictive value. The mean CK levels appear to remain within the normal range in both groups, indicating that they cannot reflect the disease activity level or degree of organ damage in JDM. This is consistent with previous research findings [25, 26]. Notably, skin ulcer, especially perianal ulcer, were more frequent in the GI group—a difference consistent with our clinical observations. The strong association between skin ulcer raises questions about a shared pathogenesis, potentially involving small-vessel vasculitis or a gut-skin immune and/or microbial axis [16, 27, 28]. The mechanism, however, remains poorly understood and requires further exploration.

A decreased peripheral lymphocyte count was the most important and strongest predictor of GI complication. Peripheral lymphocytopenia has previously been documented in patients with DM with GI or lung involvement [19, 29, 30], however, there was no abnormality in the distribution of bone marrow lymphocytes [31]. These studies on adult DM-ILD suggest that lymphocyte migration may be a primary cause of peripheral blood lymphopenia [30]. In this study, patients in the GI Group showed significantly reduced peripheral blood lymphocyte counts, with chronic inflammatory cell infiltration observed in intestinal histopathology. Based on the above, we speculate the potential mechanisms for peripheral lymphopenia in JDM-GI may include: (1) intrinsic immune dysregulation such as lymphocyte migration and (2) immunosuppressive effects of continuous therapy and aggressive therapy. However, the relationship and mechanism between lymphopenia and severe GI complication requires further study. In addition, lymphopenia increases susceptibility to opportunistic infection, which may act as a trigger for exacerbating GI injury. As the disease progresses, escalating immunosuppressive therapy may further exacerbate lymphopenia, creating a vicious cycle. Therefore, in clinical practice, pediatricians must balance the benefits and risks when administering immunosuppressants—particularly lymphocyte-depleting agents—to JDM patients with GI involvement.

Although several case reports and small series have described GI involvement in JDM, it is still difficult to identify GI complications at an early stage in clinical practice. JDM with GI involvement often presents with abdominal pain, vomiting, and GI bleeding [9, 18]. In this cohort, early abdominal pain symptoms were frequently atypical, with some patients reporting only intermittent or mild discomfort. This suggests that early GI symptoms in JDM may be insidious and are easily overlooked. The sudden onset of severe abdominal pain may indicate GI perforation and warrants urgent evaluation. In addition, pediatricians should remain alert to symptoms such as loss of appetite, diarrhea, melena, and anemia of unknown cause, which may precede overt abdominal pain. If any suspicious symptoms occur, clinicians should assess by ultrasound, abdominal contrast-enhanced CT, gastrointestinal radiography, and even endoscopic examination for detect bowel wall thickening, obstruction, or ulcer, until the ulcer or perforation site is identified. Early detection of GI lesions relies on experienced ultrasound physicians as well as the timely use of endoscopy and contrast imaging.

Previous reports suggest that GI involvement in JDM most commonly affects the duodenum [9, 18, 32–35]. However, in this cohort, the colon was the most frequently involved site, followed by the duodenum. Some patients experienced multisite or recurrent perforations. Although the exact pathogenesis of GI involvement in JDM remains unclear, vasculopathy is widely considered central to its development [36, 37]. In DM patients, vasculopathy can affect any segment of the GI tract. Consistent with current reports, histopathological of this study showed chronic inflammatory cell infiltration and vascular proliferation in both the mucosa and submucosa, some of them with features characteristic of vasculitis. The activation of complement, the formation of membrane attack complex (MAC), release of von Willebrand factor, endothelial activation, upregulation of type I interferon expression, and activation of the epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) pathway may be involved in the mechanism of gastrointestinal vascular lesions [18, 19, 38].

Currently, no standardized treatment protocol exists for GI involvement in JDM. In this pediatric cohort, medications included methylprednisolone pulse therapy, immunosuppressants, and biologic agents. Surgical intervention was performed in eight patients with GI perforation, with six undergoing enterostomy. The need for surgical intervention depends on the location of GI involvement and the clinical urgency.

As is known to all, GI involvement can be fatal in both JDM and DM patients [18, 39, 40]. Ana Matas-Garcia reported that the mortality rate due to GI complication in DM reached 41.7%, with massive hemorrhage as the leading cause of death [18]. In our JDM cohort, the case-fatality rate of GI involvement was 26.3%, with all deaths attributed to perforation and associated hemorrhage. With the accumulation of clinical experience, our institution has achieved a steady decline in mortality; notably, no deaths have occurred in the past five years, even among patients with multiple-site perforations. Our experience emphasizes early identification of GI involvement and prompt dietary control. If perforation has occurred, aggressive anti-infection therapy should be initiated, while the use of GCs and immunosuppressants should be minimized. Surgical intervention should be scheduled as soon as possible when indicated.

This study has several limitations. First, as a retrospective study, some case data were missing, though statistical adjustments helped mitigate this issue. Second, the cohort consisted exclusively of Chinese children, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other ethnic or geographic populations.

In conclusion, GI involvement is an important consideration in anti-NXP2-JDM patients; infection is the most important and direct trigger, particularly in those with poor initial treatment response or refractory disease with persistent activity. Abdominal pain may indicate severe GI involvement. In addition, pediatricians should be alert to symptoms such as loss of appetite, diarrhea, melena and anemia of unknown cause. If any suspicious symptoms occur, early detection with ultrasound, contrast-enhanced abdominal CT, gastrointestinal radiography, and/or endoscopic examination may be needed. Additionally, lymphocytopenia, skin ulcer, malnutrition and other risk factors can help us predict the occurrence of GI in patients. Further research is warranted to elucidate underlying mechanisms and inform targeted therapeutic strategies.
