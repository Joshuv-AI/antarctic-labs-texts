Trends in lung cancer burden, risk factors, and disease projections in China and the United States, 1990–2023
Authors: Lanwei Guo, Yaxin Li, Lin Cai, Huamei Luan, Jiani Yuan, Chenxin Zhu, Hongda Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLung cancer is among the most burdensome malignancies worldwide, with marked disparities between China and the United States in terms of its epidemiological characteristics, risk factors, and prevention outcomes. In this study, the long-term trends in lung cancer burden and risk factors in China and the United States from 1990 to 2023 were systematically analyzed using Global Burden of Disease (GBD) Study 2023 data, and disease burden was projected through 2050, aiming to provide evidence for optimizing lung cancer prevention and control strategies in both countries.MethodsData, including incident cases, deaths, prevalence cases, disability-adjusted life years (DALYs), age-standardized incidence rates (ASIRs), age-standardized mortality rates (ASMRs), age-standardized prevalence rates (ASPRs), and age-standardized DALY rates (ASDRs) for lung cancer in China and the United States, along with data on smoking, air pollution, occupational exposure, and metabolic and dietary risk factors, were extracted from the GBD 2023 database. Joinpoint regression was used to calculate annual percentage changes (APCs) and average annual percentage changes (AAPCs). The three-factor Das–Gupta decomposition analysis was used to quantify independent contributions of population size, population aging, and epidemiological change to variations in the absolute case burden of lung cancer in China and the United States, and Bayesian age–period–cohort models were used to project disease burden trends from 2024 to 2050.ResultsFrom 1990 to 2023, the numbers of lung cancer incident cases, deaths, prevalent cases and DALYs in China increased substantially and were far greater than those in the United States by 2023; the ASIR remained relatively stable (36.91 vs. 37.28 per 100,000), while ASMR decreased from 36.95 to 32.86 per 100,000, the ASPR increased from 40.04 to 53.03 per 100,000, and ASDR decreased from 935.08 to 742.26 per 100,000. In the United States, the ASIR, ASMR, and ASDR declined continuously and substantially, with a significant reduction in disease burden. With respect to risk factors, China experienced a continuous increase in the burden attributable to smoking (DALYs increased by 109.97%), ambient particulate matter pollution (DALYs increased by 414.84%), secondhand smoke (DALYs increased by 79.99%), high fasting plasma glucose (DALYs increased by 102.05%), and occupational exposure (including asbestos, diesel engine exhaust, and silica, with attributable DALY increasing by 219.97%, 164.39% and 110.59%, respectively), with only household air pollution from solid fuels declining (DALYs decreased by 85.58%). In the United States, the DALY burden attributable to all major risk factors decreased significantly. Sex-specific analyses revealed that the DALY burden was consistently higher in males than in females in both countries; the sex disparity remained stable in China but narrowed substantially in the United States. Projections through 2050 indicate that the absolute case burden of lung cancer in China will continue to increase, whereas that in the United States will continue to decrease, further widening the disparity between the two countries.ConclusionsFrom 1990 to 2023, China and the United States exhibited diametrically opposite lung cancer burden trajectories. China remains in a phase of increasing risk factors and rising absolute case burden, whereas the United States has entered a phase of comprehensive risk factor control and sustained burden reduction. China should implement long-term stringent tobacco control, strengthen ambient PM2.5 management, target middle-aged and elderly populations for focused interventions, and promote universal low-dose computed tomography (LDCT) screening. The United States should consolidate tobacco control achievements and enhance screening among high-risk persons. The experiences of both countries provide stratified references for countries at different stages of development.

Introduction

Lung cancer is among the most prevalent and deadly malignancies worldwide and exhibits strong invasiveness and poor prognosis. It can result in the destruction of normal lung tissue and function, and can metastasize to multiple organs and tissues, including the pleura, bones, and brain, through hematogenous and lymphatic routes, posing a serious threat to patients’ health and lives, and it represents one of the core cancer types contributing to the global cancer burden.1,2 The development and progression of lung cancer are associated with multiple factors, including smoking, air pollution, occupational exposure, and genetics.3,4 Its high incidence and high mortality rate not only impose a huge burden on human health, but also place tremendous pressure on healthcare systems and socioeconomic development worldwide.5 Since 1990, lung cancer has consistently been a major challenge and a priority in global cancer prevention and control efforts.6

The burden of lung cancer is unevenly distributed globally, with significant disparities in incidence patterns and mortality profiles between developed and developing countries. These differences are closely related to the regional human development index, healthcare accessibility, intensity of risk factor control, and level of diagnostic and therapeutic technologies used.7,8 China, as the world’s most populous developing country, is facing severe challenges in lung cancer prevention and control amid accelerated population aging, lifestyle transitions, and gradual shifts in the cancer spectrum, with the lung cancer burden exhibiting notable stage-specific characteristics.9 In contrast, the United States has made extensive efforts in lung cancer screening promotion, risk factor control (e.g., tobacco control), and diagnostic and therapeutic innovations, achieving remarkable progress in reducing the lung cancer burden.10 Although numerous studies have compared differences in overall cancer incidence and mortality rates between China and the United States,11 the updated literature specifically focusing on lung cancer—a highly prevalent malignancy—and using the latest Global Burden of Disease (GBD) data is lacking. Therefore, systematically analyzing the evolving characteristics of lung cancer burden and differences in risk factors between the two countries and exploring the strengths and weaknesses of their prevention and control strategies on the basis of the latest data hold significant practical importance for optimizing lung cancer prevention policies and reducing the disease burden in both countries.

In this study, we retrieved the most recent data from the GBD database to systematically delineate the epidemiological characteristics of lung cancer from 1990 to 2023, with a focus on comparing and analyzing the trends in age-standardized incidence, mortality, prevalence, and disability-adjusted life year rates between China and the United States.12 We further investigated population distribution differences, conducted decomposition analyses, and assessed changes in risk factors for lung cancer burden, and projected the lung cancer burden over the period 2024–2050, with the aims of elucidating the current status and disparities in lung cancer prevention and control between the two countries, evaluating the effectiveness of existing control strategies, and providing scientific evidence for optimizing preventive measures and reducing the lung cancer burden while also offering references and lessons for lung cancer control efforts in other developing countries.

Methods

Data sources

This study was based on the GBD 2023 database, from which we systematically extracted data on tracheal, bronchial, and lung cancer (hereafter referred to as lung cancer) in China and the United States from 1990 to 2023. The GBD database employs a standardized, reproducible, and cross-nationally comparable epidemiological modeling framework that integrates data from cancer registries, vital registration systems, population-based epidemiological surveys, academic literature, and health statistics from countries worldwide. The database uniformly corrects for underreporting, misclassification, and missing data, ultimately generating disease burden estimates stratified by time, sex, and age. Data quality and reliability have been validated in numerous global oncological epidemiological studies.

The core indicators extracted in this study included incident cases, deaths, prevalence cases and DALYs, as well as the age-standardized incidence rate (ASIR), age-standardized mortality rate (ASMR), age-standardized prevalence rate (ASPR), and age-standardized DALY rate (ASDR), which were calculated using the GBD 2023 world standard population. All indicators were stratified by country (China and the United States), sex (male, female, both sexes), age group (<5, 5–9, 10–14, 15–19, 20–24, 25–29, 30–34, 35–39, 40–44, 45–49, 50–54, 55–59, 60–64, 65–69, 70–74, 75–79, 80–84, 85–89, 90–94, ≥95 years), and year (1990–2023) to minimize confounding effects arising from population structure, age distribution, and temporal changes. Additionally, we extracted GBD 2023 data on risk factor-attributable DALYs for lung cancer, including smoking, household air pollution from solid fuels, secondhand smoke, ambient particulate matter pollution, a diet low in fruits, high fasting plasma glucose, residential radon and 9 occupational exposures (silica, asbestos, diesel engine exhaust, arsenic, nickel, polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons, chromium, cadmium, and beryllium) for comparative analysis of risk factor burden between China and the United States.

Statistical analysis

Joinpoint regression analysis

Joinpoint regression analysis was employed to examine the temporal trends in age-standardized rates from 1990 to 2023. A permutation test was used to identify the joinpoints and segmental trends with the best goodness of fit. The annual percentage changes (APCs) with their 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for each segment, and the average annual percentage changes (AAPCs) with their 95% CIs, for the entire study period were calculated.13 AAPCs with their 95% CIs and P values were calculated using the weighted average of segmental APCs based on the normal approximation method in Joinpoint software (https://surveillance.cancer.gov/joinpoint/).

Decomposition analysis

The Das–Gupta demographic decomposition method was applied to decompose the absolute changes in disease burden into three components: population size, population aging, and changes in age-specific rates.14 The decomposition formula was as follows: Total = Pop + Age + Epid, where Total denotes the overall change in incidence/deaths/DALYs from 1990 to 2023; Pop denotes the change in cases attributable solely to population size changes; Age denotes the change in cases attributable solely to population aging; and Epid denotes the change in cases attributable to fluctuations in age-standardized rates (epidemiological changes). The contribution proportion of each effect was calculated as follows: contribution proportion of each effect = absolute value of individual effect/sum of absolute values of the three effects × 100%.

Risk factor analysis

Data on absolute numbers of risk factor-attributable DALYs and percentage indicators were extracted from the GBD risk factor module. The GBD employs a comparative risk assessment framework to estimate the attributable burden of current exposure scenarios relative to counterfactual minimum risk exposure scenarios on the basis of risk exposure distributions, risk–outcome relative risks, theoretical minimum risk exposure levels, and total disease burden. For risk factors with mediating pathways, a mediation matrix was used for adjustment. The attributable risk percentage in GBD represents the proportion of risk factor-attributable DALYs to the total DALYs of that disease, which is expressed as the proportion of risk-attributable DALYs in this study. This indicator was estimated by the GBD comparative risk assessment model, and this study did not recalculate population-attributable fractions on the basis of individual-level data.

Bayesian age–period–cohort model

The Bayesian age–period–cohort (BAPC) model is an extension of the traditional age–period–cohort model within the Bayesian framework. The BAPC model estimates trends by imposing smoothness priors on age, period, and cohort effects within the Bayesian framework.15 This study incorporated GBD official scenario-based projections of future population size and age structure to estimate the disease burden trends of lung cancer in China and the United States from 2024 to 2050. Specifically, the “BAPC” R package (https://cran.r-project.org/package=BAPC), which implements the integrated nested laplace approximation (INLA) method, was used for model fitting and prediction analysis. The INLA directly approximates marginal posterior distributions, effectively avoiding the common chain mixing and convergence issues associated with traditional Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) methods. This approach maintains high computational efficiency and has been widely applied in global disease burden projection studies.

All data cleaning, visualization, and statistical analyses were performed using the R software (version 4.4.2, https://www.r-project.org/). Age-standardized rates are expressed per 100,000 population, and temporal trends are presented as APC, AAPC, and their 95% CIs. A two-sided P-value <0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

Results

Lung cancer burden

Globally, lung cancer remained one of the leading causes of cancer incidence and mortality in 2023. Estimates from the GBD 2023 study revealed substantial differences in lung cancer burden between China and the United States in 1990 and 2023 (Table 1). Overall, the number of incident cases, deaths, prevalent cases and DALYs due to lung cancer in China increased significantly from 318,666.71 (95% uncertainty interval [UI]: 270,891.32–407,364.30), 308,904.84 (95% UI: 262,233.50–397,226.38), 363,896.45 (95% UI: 308,321.54–444,215.88) and 8,563,532.30 (95% UI: 7,275,497.70–10,871,660.23) in 1990 to 872,463.45 (95% UI: 739,142.24–1,009,478.00), 763,722.67 (95% UI: 657,067.00–885,257.41), 1,253,322.96 (95% UI: 1,044,131.80–1,529,979.30) and 17,312,708.96 (95% UI: 14,753,240.86–20,333,096.98) in 2023, respectively. The ASIR of lung cancer in China increased slightly (36.91 vs. 37.28 per 100,000), while ASMR decreased from 36.95 to 32.86 per 100,000; the ASPR increased from 40.04 to 53.03 per 100,000, and ASDR decreased from 935.08 to 742.26 per 100,000. In the United States, the number of incident cases increased slightly, and deaths remained relatively stable, but all age-standardized indicators declined sharply. From 1990 to 2023, DALYs in the United States decreased from 3,799,542.57 (95% UI: 3,616,393.81–3,962,567.01) to 3,286,070.70 (95% UI: 3,054,416.55–3,520,942.48), and the ASDR decreased from 1,259.93 to 555.88 per 100,000. With respect to sex, the lung cancer burden was much greater in males than in females in both countries. In China, the ASIR, ASMR, ASPR, and ASDR in males were consistently more than twice those in females, with a stable sex disparity. In the United States, the male-to-female ratios decreased from 2.23, 2.36, 1.94 and 2.21 in 1990 to 1.22, 1.31, 1.06 and 1.28 in 2023, respectively. As of 2023, males in the United States had lower ASMR and ASDR for lung cancer than their Chinese counterparts, and females in the United States had higher ASMR and ASDR than those in China. With respect to age, the lung cancer burden was highly concentrated in people aged 50 years and older in both countries (Supplementary Tables 1 and 2). In China, the number of incident cases, deaths, prevalent cases and DALYs among those aged ≥50 years increased markedly during the study period. In 2023, the ASIR and ASDR in the 65–69 age group in China were 204.68 and 4173.13 per 100,000, respectively, much higher than those in the United States (172.59 and 3243.47 per 100,000, respectively). As of 2023, the ASPR for all age groups from 55 to 94 years in China was lower than that in the United States. For instance, the ASPR for the 75–79 age group was 411.75 per 100,000 in China and 473.11 per 100,000 in the United States in 2023. Annual changes in lung cancer burden

Significant differences were observed in the temporal trends of age-standardized rates of lung cancer between China and the United States from 1990 to 2023. In China, the ASIR among the total population fluctuated, with an AAPC of 0.14% (95% CI: 0.04% to 0.24%, P < 0.05) and a marked increase between 2020 and 2023 (APC = 4.57%, 95% CI: 3.13% to 7.35%, P < 0.05) (Fig. 1, Supplementary Table 3). Sex-specific analysis revealed that in China, the ASIR remained relatively stable in males (AAPC = 0.01%, 95% CI: −0.11% to 0.14%) and declined slightly in females from 1990 to 2023 (AAPC = −0.07%, 95% CI: −0.14% to 0); however, the ASIR increased significantly in females between 2020 and 2023 (APC = 4.89%, 95% CI: 3.94% to 6.04%) (Supplementary Table 3). In the United States, the ASIR decreased much more rapidly in males than in females, with AAPCs of −2.69% (95% CI: −2.73% to −2.64%) in males and −0.94% (95% CI: −0.97% to −0.91%) in females, and both sexes tended to decrease (Supplementary Table 4). The total number of optimal model joinpoints, the corresponding years for each joinpoint, and all segmental APCs with their 95% CIs are presented in Supplementary Table 3 and 4.

In China, the ASMR and ASDR decreased between 1990 and 2023, with AAPCs of −0.22% (95% CI: −0.33% to −0.10%) and −0.57% (95% CI: −0.68% to −0.46%), respectively (Supplementary Tables 5 and 6). The decreases were greater in females than in males: the ASDR AAPC was −0.99% in females (95% CI: −1.05% to −0.94%) and the ASDR AAPC was −0.41% in males (95% CI: −0.53% to −0.29%). In the United States, the ASMR and ASDR decreased rapidly and consistently, with AAPCs of −1.99% (95% CI: −2.02% to −1.95%) and −2.44% (95% CI: −2.48% to −2.39%), respectively (Supplementary Tables 7 and 8). Compared with females, males exhibited significantly greater declines, with stable and consistent downward trends across all periods.

Decomposition analysis of lung cancer burden

Quantitative decomposition analysis based on the three-factor model of population size, aging, and epidemiological changes (Fig. 2) revealed that the increase in lung cancer burden in China was driven primarily by population aging and population size. Taking incident cases as an example, between 1990 and 2007, aging contributed 72.17%, population size contributed 20.80%, and epidemiological changes contributed only 7.02% toward the increase in lung cancer burden (Supplementary Table 9). From 2020 to 2023, incident cases increased again, with aging, population size, and epidemiological changes contributing 45.19%, 2.06%, and 52.75%, respectively. Sex-stratified analysis showed that population aging contributed 82.07% and population size contributed 18.82% to the increase in lung cancer incidence among Chinese males from 1990 to 2023. Epidemiological changes accounted for only −0.90%, indicating a marginal decrease in burden. In Chinese females, the contribution pattern of epidemiological changes fluctuated considerably across periods, ranging from −46.79% between 2000 and 2009 to 469.74% between 2017 and 2020, and shifting to 56.10% between 2020 and 2023, reflecting the dynamic process of lung cancer risk factor control and changes in diagnostic and treatment levels. The decomposition pattern in the United States was distinctly different. Although population size and aging contributed positively, these effects were consistently offset by substantial improvements in epidemiological changes except for the period before 2001, resulting in net declines in most periods. Decomposition of total population incident cases revealed that between 2014 and 2019, the contribution of epidemiological changes reached 221.97%, far exceeding the positive effects of aging and population size; between 2019 and 2023, the contribution was even greater at 615.56% (Supplementary Table 9). Differences in risk factors

As shown in Fig. 3, from 1990 to 2023, the risk factor profiles of lung cancer burden in China and the United States exhibited distinctly different evolutionary trajectories. In China, smoking consistently remained the leading risk factor, with the disease burden continuing to increase. In 2023, the number of smoking-attributable DALYs increased by 109.97% compared with that in 1990. Ambient particulate matter pollution replaced household air pollution from solid fuels as the second leading risk factor in 2023, with DALYs increasing by 414.84%. Secondhand smoke (DALYs increased by 79.99%), high fasting plasma glucose (DALYs increased by 102.05%), and occupational exposures (including asbestos, diesel engine exhaust, and silica, with corresponding DALY increases of 219.97%, 164.39% and 110.59%, respectively) showed substantial increases, whereas only household air pollution from solid fuels declined markedly (DALYs decreased by 85.58%). In the United States, the prevalence of most major risk factors decreased comprehensively. Although smoking remained the leading risk factor in 2023, smoking-attributable DALYs and ASDRs decreased substantially, with reductions of 24.93% and 61.83%, respectively. The burden from ambient particulate matter pollution, secondhand smoke, and other exposures decreased, with ambient particulate matter pollution-attributable DALYs decreasing by 75.33% and secondhand smoke-attributable DALYs decreasing by 48.87%. The burden from occupational asbestos exposure and residential radon declined by 21.47% and 13.92%, respectively. The burden from household air pollution from solid fuels became negligible and dietary-related risks also continued to decrease.

In terms of sex (Supplementary Fig. 1), both Chinese males and females were predominantly affected by smoking, ambient particulate matter pollution, and secondhand smoke in 2023. The number of DALYs among females increased by 53.67%, 466.80%, and 89.11% for secondhand smoke, ambient particulate matter pollution, and high fasting plasma glucose, respectively, with ASDR changes of −39.15%, 117.56%, and −28.38%. Among males, the number of DALYs increased by 116.34%, 396.15%, and 110.43% for smoking, ambient particulate matter pollution, and secondhand smoke, respectively, with ASDR changes of −16.96%, 92.08%, and −18.02%. Both sexes underwent structural risk transitions.

In the United States, the ASDRs for all major risk factors declined in both males and females over the study period. The ASDR for secondhand smoke declined by 66.34% in females and 77.56% in males; the ASDR for high fasting plasma glucose declined by 16.56% in females and 54.54% in males; and the ASDR for smoking declined by 47.68% in females and 69.49% in males. In the United States, males exhibited greater declines in ASDR than females for all major risk factors. Nevertheless, the overall burden levels in both sexes remained far below those in China.

From 2024 to 2050, the lung cancer burden trajectories in China and the United States will be markedly different. In China, the ASDR is projected to increase slightly from 771 per 100,000 population in 2024 to 849 per 100,000 population in 2039 and then decline modestly to 819 per 100,000 population by 2050. The ASMR first increases slowly from 32.47 to 34.68 per 100,000 population over the period 2024–2039, and then trended downward from 2040 to 2050, while the ASIR is projected to increase continuously from 37.69 per 100,000 population to 48.43 per 100,000 population from 2024 to 2050. Driven by population aging and population size, over the period 2024–2050, the absolute number of incident cases in China is projected to increase from 933,721 to 7,870,519, the number of deaths is projected to increase from 805,539 to 7,602,070, and DALYs are projected to increase from 18,680,180 to 96,532,757 person-years, indicating sustained and rapid growth. In the United States, age-standardized rates are projected to continue to decline rapidly: the ASDR is projected to decrease from 515 to 235 per 100,000 population, the ASMR is projected to decrease from 23.25 to 10.31 per 100,000 population, and the ASIR is projected to decrease from 29.24 to 14.14 per 100,000 population from 2024 to 2050. Absolute incident cases are projected to decrease from 198,651 to 145,129, deaths are projected to decrease from 161,906 to 109,740, and DALYs are projected to decrease from 3,203,467 to 1,874,210 person-years. These projections suggest that the absolute burden of lung cancer in China may continue to grow rapidly over the coming decades, driven primarily by population aging and size. In contrast, it is expected that the United States could achieve a sustained and significant decline in lung cancer burden, facilitated by continued effective tobacco control and advancements in diagnosis and treatment.

With respect to sex, the disparity in lung cancer burden trends between China and the United States is substantial. In Chinese males, the ASIR is projected to increase initially and then stabilize, rising from 53.57 per 100,000 population in 2024 to 64.15 per 100,000 population in 2050. Incident cases are projected to grow from 635,332 to 4,727,631, indicating that males will continue to constitute the primary burden. In Chinese females, both age-standardized rates and absolute burden are projected to increase rapidly and continuously, with the incidence rate rising from 22.65 to 41.32 per 100,000 population and the number of incident cases increasing from 292,277 to 3,579,051, suggesting that lung cancer prevention and control among women would face even greater challenges. In the United States, age-standardized rates for both males and females are projected to decline continuously from 2024 to 2050: the ASIR in males is projected to decrease from 32.02 to 10.77 per 100,000 population, and the ASIR in females is projected to decrease from 26.78 to 17.60 per 100,000 population. The number of incident cases is projected to decrease from 99,898 to 55,800 in males and slightly increase from 98,476 to 98,637 in females, reflecting the sustained effectiveness of tobacco control in both sexes, although the decline in female lung cancer will lag behind that in males.

Discussion

Based on the GBD 2023 database, the long-term trends in lung cancer burden and risk factors in China and the United States from 1990 to 2023 were systematically analyzed and trends were projected through 2050. The results revealed marked differences in the epidemiological characteristics, trajectories, and risk factor structures between the two countries, indicating that they are at different stages of disease transition. These differences are closely associated with tobacco control policies, environmental governance, occupational protection, screening coverage, and socioeconomic development levels.16,17

In terms of overall disease burden trends, China’s absolute lung cancer burden has continued to increase, with age-standardized rates stabilizing but remaining at high levels. Although ASMR and ASDR have slightly declined, the substantial increases in incident cases, deaths, and total DALYs suggest that population aging and cumulative risk factor exposure are the primary drivers.18,19 In contrast, the United States has experienced sustained and substantial declines in age-standardized incidence, mortality, and DALY rates, indicating that the disease burden has entered a phase of stable reduction, reflecting the decisive impact of long-term, rigorous public health interventions.20

In terms of sex differences, the disease burden was greater in males than in females in both countries, but the trajectories differed markedly. In China, the lung cancer burden in males has remained persistently high, while that in females has exhibited an upward trend in recent years. Most female lung cancer patients in China have no history of active smoking, distinguishing them from the tobacco-driven female population in the United States. Indoor and outdoor environmental exposures to cooking oil fumes, secondhand smoke, and PM2.5 are core drivers of the increasing burden among Chinese females, which is amplified by population aging. The establishment of a female-specific stratified prevention and control system, focusing on primary prevention through comprehensive smoke-free legislation in public places to reduce involuntary secondhand smoke exposure, promotion of affordable high-efficiency kitchen exhaust facilities through subsidies, and dissemination of low-temperature cooking techniques to reduce cooking fume exposure, thereby decreasing long-term carcinogen accumulation in women, is recommended.21 Implementing urban–rural and age-differentiated interventions, promoting clean energy transitions for rural women, and providing community-based integrated management of pulmonary and metabolic chronic diseases for middle-aged and elderly women would effectively mitigate the upward trend in the lung cancer burden among Chinese women in the coming years.22 In the United States, both male and female burdens have decreased significantly, with greater reductions in males, leading to a rapidly narrowing sex gap, which is consistent with the findings of previous studies.23 These disparities are primarily attributable to higher tobacco exposure and more concentrated occupational exposures among males, as well as differences in the population coverage and implementation intensity of tobacco control measures between the two countries.24,25

The age distribution revealed that the lung cancer burden was highly concentrated in individuals aged 50 years and older in both countries, with the risk increasing sharply with age. Age-standardized rates in older age groups in China were significantly higher than those in the United States in 2023, suggesting longer durations of risk factor exposure and room for improvement in prevention and early diagnosis, and treatment coverage among the elderly population in China.26 Strengthening high-risk screening, risk factor control, and health management in middle-aged and elderly populations represents a shared critical priority for reducing the lung cancer burden in both countries.27

The evolution of risk factors constitutes the core of the disparity between the two countries. China is in a transitional phase of risk factors: Smoking remains the leading risk factor with a continuously increasing burden; the number of DALYs for ambient particulate matter pollution, secondhand smoke, high fasting plasma glucose, and occupational hazardous exposures are rapidly increasing; only traditional risks such as household air pollution from solid fuels have declined significantly. Overall, this presents a transitional pattern characterized by the decline in traditional risks and the rapid increase in modern environmental and metabolic risks, which is highly consistent with China’s rapid industrialization and urbanization processes.28 China officially promulgated the Air Pollution Prevention and Control Action Plan (“Ten Measures for Air”) in September 2013, achieving the first-phase emission reduction targets by 2017. However, particulate matter exposure has a 10–20-year lag window for lung cancer onset.29 Our 1990–2023 data reveal that ambient PM2.5-related DALYs increased by 414.84%, and populations exposed during the high-pollution pre-2013 industrialization period successively entered the ≥50-year-old age group with high lung cancer incidence. Therefore, air quality improvements after 2013 did not immediately reverse the upward trend in lung cancer incidence, resulting in a distinctive inflection point characterized by “policy effectiveness with lagging burden elevation”. The stage-specific increase in the ASIR among Chinese females during 2020–2023 is also directly associated with the COVID-19 pandemic and cumulative exposure to long-term indoor cooking fumes and outdoor PM2.5, which contributed to the updated 2023 GBD database estimates.30

The United States has entered a phase of comprehensive risk factor control: Smoking, ambient air pollution, secondhand smoke, occupational exposure to asbestos, residential radon, and other major risk factors have exhibited comprehensive and significant decreases in both DALYs and age-standardized rates. Household indoor air pollution has become negligible, and metabolic and dietary risks have continued to decrease. The sustained decline in the lung cancer burden in the United States is largely attributable to the federal tobacco control legislation and nationwide smoking cessation promotion implemented over the decades since 1964. The United States has proposed regulations on menthol tobacco, but these have yet to be implemented—an issue that may warrant attention in future tobacco control efforts.31 The 1964 Surgeon General’s Report confirming the carcinogenicity of smoking, the 1971 ban on tobacco advertising on television and radio, and the 2009 Family Smoking Prevention and Tobacco Control Act authorizing FDA regulation of the entire tobacco chain, have sustained decades of decreasing adult smoking rates.32, 33, 34 Corresponding to our joinpoint results, the United States age-standardized rates have continuously decreased across all measures since 1990, with accelerated declines during 1990–1995 and 2004–2014, reflecting the cohort lag effect as earlier tobacco-exposed populations gradually entered older age groups with synchronously declining lung cancer risk. Beyond tobacco control, strict environmental standards and comprehensive lung cancer screening systems can also provide important references for China.35,36

Joinpoint and APC/AAPC trend analyses revealed that China’s lung cancer age-standardized rates exhibited pronounced fluctuations with some periods of rebound, suggesting that prevention effects are vulnerable to external factors. This aligns with the fact that, despite the 2013 Action Plan, populations exposed to severe industrial pollution before 2013 have gradually reached the age of 50 years or older, when lung cancer incidence rises sharply. In contrast, the United States exhibited stable downward trends across all periods without significant rebound, indicating the continuity and stability of its intervention systems.37 Application of Bayesian APC models further revealed that lung cancer burden changes in both countries are jointly driven by age effects, period effects, and birth cohort effects. The 2011 National Lung Screening Trial revealed that low-dose computed tomography (LDCT) can reduce lung cancer mortality by 20%38; the 2013 U.S. Preventive Services Task Force (USPSTF) recommendation for annual screening in heavy smokers aged 55–80 years and the 2015 Medicare coverage of LDCT screening correspond to the maximum APC decline (−4.43%) in age-standardized rates for the United States during 2014–2019 in our study. Screening expansion has driven early detection of lung cancer and has reduced the proportion of late-stage cases, directly lowering population mortality and DALY burdens.39,40

China is more significantly influenced by birth cohort cumulative risk factor exposure. Strong tobacco control in males, universal LDCT coverage for high-risk populations aged ≥50 years, and improvements in cancer registry systems are recommended.41 Population structure transition is the core driver of China’s increasing lung cancer burden, while risk factor control and public health interventions are key to burden reduction in the United States. Given that China’s aging population, especially the elderly people, has low health awareness and limited affordability for medical expenses, national policies should increase health awareness and screening efforts among the elderly, and implement universal annual free LDCT screening nationwide to enable early identification of pulmonary lesions, thereby reducing mortality and disability burdens from lung cancer in the elderly.42

Consistent with previous studies (most of which projected only through 2030), our extended forecasts to 2050 indicate that the disparity between China and the United States in lung cancer burden will further widen. China’s burden is projected to remain persistently high with slow upward fluctuations and sustained overall pressure. The United States, lung cancer burden is projected to continue declining steadily, entering a phase of sustained optimization in disease control. Without strengthened comprehensive prevention and control, China’s lung cancer prevention pressure will remain higher than that of the United States in the long term.43

This study relies on GBD 2023 global unified modeling estimates. Compared with annual cancer registry reports from the National Cancer Center of China, the GBD database offers several advantages. First, the National Cancer Center covers only hospital-based populations in registered institutions, with substantial underdiagnosis and missing primary care cases, whereas GBD integrates multiple data sources, including vital registration, cohort studies, registries, and literature, to correct for underreporting. Second, domestic registry systems cover only urban areas and large-medium medical institutions, with insufficient rural population data, whereas GBD supplements rural lung cancer burden estimates through stratified modeling. Finally, the statistical definitions differ slightly between the two systems (tumor–node–metastasis staging and death attribution rules).44

This study has several limitations. The GBD database provides modeled estimates that are influenced by the quality of registration data, underreporting, and model assumptions. Quantitative analyses of social factors such as regional economic levels, healthcare resource distribution, and screening coverage were not included. Projection models are based on historical trends and fitted to historical data, without incorporating uncertainties from future policy interventions, medical technological breakthroughs, or changes in environmental improvement intensity. Future studies could integrate cancer registry data, cohort studies, and policy data for more refined analyses.

Conclusions

From 1990 to 2023, China and the United States exhibited diametrically opposite trends in terms of lung cancer burden. In China, the absolute numbers of lung cancer cases, deaths, and DALYs have continued to increase, with age-standardized rates stabilizing but remaining high. Risk factors are rapidly transitioning from traditional indoor pollution to ambient air pollution, tobacco, metabolic, and occupational exposure, with the disease burden facing continued upward pressure. In the United States, age-standardized rates decreased substantially across all the measures, with comprehensive risk factor control achieved, and entered a stable post-transition phase of decline.

Sex and age stratification revealed that males and middle-aged/elderly populations bear the predominant lung cancer burden in both countries, but China has more pronounced sex disparities and a greater burden in older age groups. Comparisons of risk factors indicate that sustained, long-term implementation of tobacco control, environmental governance, occupational protection, and early screening and diagnosis constitute the core experience underlying the reduction in the burden of lung cancer in the United States.

Projections through 2050 indicate that the disparity in the lung cancer burden between China and the United States will further increase. China should adopt proven strategies from the United States, including reinforced tobacco control, sustained air quality improvement, stringent occupational health supervision, expanded early screening, and targeted interventions for middle‑aged and elderly populations, as part of a comprehensive life‑course prevention framework. The consistent implementation and strengthening of these measures are pivotal to curbing the increasing lung cancer burden and facilitating its eventual decline. Nevertheless, given the inherent lag in health outcomes following policy implementation, the full benefits of these interventions will likely manifest over the long term and warrant ongoing evaluation. In the United States, which relies on decades of sustained tobacco control, nationwide LDCT screening expansion, and strict industrial pollution and occupational protection regulations, long-term substantial declines in age-standardized lung cancer indicators have been achieved. However, challenges remain, including a slow rebound in female lung cancer patients and gaps in menthol tobacco regulation. Future efforts should strengthen the screening of high-risk women, implement comprehensive bans on flavored cigarettes, and improve interventions for young and middle-aged, at-risk populations. The prevention and control experiences of both countries are mutually informative and can provide stratified references for lung cancer control in countries at different stages of development globally.
