Diabetes care goals and diabetic retinopathy in adults at tertiary hospitals, Rwanda: a cross-sectional hospital-based study
Authors: Aloys Tuyizere, Bitew Yifter Helen, Emile Sebera, Bosco Bangamwabo, Olive Dushimimana, Chantal Mukantwali, Liberathe Uwamaliya, Sylvie Igihozo, Belay Enyew Kibret, Louise Murorunkwere, Raja Mustafa, Mohammed Almisiry, Charlotte M. Bavuma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAttainment of diabetes mellitus (DM) care goals is essential to improving quality of life and preventing complications in patients living with this increasingly prevalent non-communicable disease. Despite the existence of evidence-based national and international guidelines, achievement of glycaemic, blood pressure, and lipid targets remains suboptimal, particularly in resource-limited settings. This study was designed to assess the extent to which diabetes care goals are achieved at major tertiary healthcare facilities in Rwanda, in accordance with established process and intermediate outcome indicators, and to determine the prevalence of and factors associated with diabetic retinopathy.MethodsThis prospective, observational cross-sectional study was conducted between September 2023 and April 2024 at four tertiary hospitals in Rwanda: King Faisal Hospital Rwanda, Centre Hospitalier Universitaire de Kigali, Rwanda Military Hospital, and Centre Hospitalier Universitaire de Butare. A total of 400 patients were enrolled using consecutive sampling type 1 diabetes patients with at least 5 years of follow-up and type 2 diabetes patients with at least 12 months of follow-up in outpatient departments. Data were collected through structured interviews, clinical examinations, and review of laboratory results using a validated data collection tool. Performance of process indicators and achievement of intermediate outcome indicators (HbA1c < 7%, blood pressure ≤ 130/80 mmHg, LDL cholesterol ≤ 2.6 mmol/L) were assessed against American Diabetes Association (ADA) 2023 standards. Retinopathy was classified by dilated fundoscopy. Statistical analysis used STATA Version 17 SE; binary logistic regression identified factors associated with poor glycaemic control and retinopathy at 95% CI, p < 0.05.ResultThe majority of participants were female (69.5%) and most had type 2 diabetes (96.75%). The overall performance of process indicators was: 100% for blood pressure measurement at every visit, 90.75% for 3- to 6-monthly glycated haemoglobin testing, 69.25% for LDL cholesterol testing, and 56.5%, 26%, and 19% for annual eye, dental, and foot examinations respectively. The proportions of participants who achieved optimal HbA1C (< 7%), blood pressure (below 130/80 mmHg), and LDL cholesterol (< 2.6 mmol/L) were 44.5%, 65.25%, and 55% respectively. In multivariate analysis, treatment type was the primary determinant of glycaemic control, with patients on oral therapy showing significantly lower odds of poor control compared to those on insulin alone (aOR: 0.306, 95% CI: 0.157–0.596, p = 0.001); diabetes duration was the strongest predictor of retinopathy, with progressively increasing risk for patients with 6–20 years (aOR = 1.985, 95% CI: 1.217–3.236, p = 0.006) and more than 20 years duration (aOR = 2.488, 95% CI: 1.100–5.630, p = 0.029).ConclusionBlood pressure monitoring and HbA1c testing were consistently performed; however, foot (19%) and dental (26%) examinations remained critically underperformed across all sites. Achievement of the three diabetes care goals glycaemic, blood pressure, and lipid control was suboptimal in a substantial proportion of patients. Oral therapy was associated with better glycaemic control than insulin alone, while longer disease duration and concomitant hypertension were independently associated with higher odds of diabetic retinopathy. These findings call for targeted systemic interventions, including mandatory integration of ophthalmology, podiatry, and dental screening into routine diabetes protocols at Rwandan tertiary facilities.Clinical trial number Not applicable.

Background

Diabetes is among the rapidly growing health emergencies affecting almost 537 million adults’ individuals (10.5%) worldwide in 2021 with the projection to affect 643(11.3%) and 783 million individuals (12.2%) by 2030 and 2045 respectively [1]. Individuals with diabetes are at increased risk of cardiovascular complications and related mortality, compared to the general population [2]. Diabetes care aims to prevent complications through glycemic control, lipids and blood pressure optimization [3].

The burden of diabetes is particularly concerning in Africa, where limited resources compound the challenge of disease management [4]. In Africa 1 in 22 adults (24 million) adults are living with diabetes [5]. The total number of people with diabetes is predicted to increase by 129% to 55 million by 2045 [5]. Over 1 in 2 (54%) people living with diabetes are undiagnosed [5]. Studies have shown that a person may spend 5–6 years in an asymptomatic phase of pre-diabetes and type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) before being diagnosed, during which time micro- and macrovascular complications may develop [6]. The Sub-Saharan region faces unique challenges, including limited access to diagnostic tools, essential medicines, and specialized care services [7].

Process indicators assessing the performance of diabetes related tests and exams (glycated hemoglobin-HbA1c, blood pressure measurement, microalbuminuria level, serum cholesterol levels, eye and foot examinations, diabetes education) and intermediate outcome indicators for optimization of glycemic, lipids and blood pressure, constitute the cornerstone references to evaluate diabetes care across the world [8]. Their performance has increased across countries and associated with improved outcomes [9]. Gaps exist regarding the achievement of diabetes care goals globally and more substantial in developing countries with large and growing diabetes populations [10].

In Rwanda, the numerical results show that the prevalence of type 2 diabetes increases from 2.8% in 2015 to reach 12.65% in 2020 and to 22.59% in 2025 [11]. The country has made substantial efforts to improve diabetes care through the integration of non-communicable disease services into primary healthcare. However, like many developing nations, Rwanda faces challenges in achieving optimal diabetes care outcomes. The management of diabetes primarily aims to prevent complications through strict glycemic control, blood pressure management, and lipid optimization [12]. Evidence consistently shows that adherence to process indicators (regular monitoring of HbA1c, blood pressure, lipids, and screening for complications) and achievement of treatment targets significantly reduces the risk of eye/retinal complications [13].

In Rwanda, according to data in non-communicable diseases risk factors surveillance survey conducted in 2022, diabetes prevalence in adult population was 3%, with Kigali city having highest prevalence (9.8%) which is more than 4 times higher than the national prevalence [14]. National guidelines for the diabetes care were developed to be implemented at different levels of health facilities including tertiary hospitals where accessibility to laboratory tests and specialized services (ophthalmology, dental, nutrition) has improved. Despite the existence of comprehensive management guidelines, the achievement of diabetes care goals remains suboptimal globally, with particularly concerning rates in developing countries. In Rwanda, while the healthcare system has evolved significantly, there is limited data on the achievement of diabetes care goals and the burden of eye/retinal complications at tertiary care facilities. Therefore, this study aims to assess three key aspects of diabetes care at Rwandan tertiary hospitals: adherence to process indicators, achievement of diabetes care goals (intermediate outcome indicators), and the prevalence of diabetic retinopathy (a eye/retinal complication).

Methods

Study design

This cross-sectional, hospital-based study assessed the achievement of diabetes care goals and prevalence of retinopathy in Rwanda’s tertiary hospitals.

Study setting

The study was conducted across four tertiary hospitals in Rwanda: King Faisal Hospital Rwanda (KFHR), Rwanda Military Hospital (RMH), and Centre Hospitalier Universitaire de Kigali (CHUK) in Kigali city, and Centre Hospitalier Universitaire de Butare (CHUB) in Huye district, Southern province. While CHUB primarily serves the South and Western provinces, CHUK, RMH, and KFHR serve patients nationwide. All four facilities offer comprehensive diabetes care services including ophthalmologist-conducted retinal examinations, dental surgeon assessments, and laboratory testing for glycated haemoglobin and lipid profiles, covered at 90% by community health insurance (Mutuelle de Santé).

Study population

The study population comprised adults aged 18 years and above attending outpatient diabetes clinics at the four study sites during the data collection period.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria: [1] confirmed diagnosis of type 1 or type 2 diabetes mellitus; [2] type 2 diabetes patients with at least 12 months of follow-up at the study site; [3] type 1 diabetes patients with at least 5 years of follow-up; [4] age 18 years or older; and [5] willingness to participate and ability to provide written informed consent. Exclusion criteria: [1] patients with incomplete medical records precluding assessment of the primary outcome variables; [2] patients who declined to participate; and [3] pregnant women, given the distinct glycaemic targets applicable during pregnancy.

Sample size and sampling procedure

The sample size was calculated using Leslie Kish formula for single population proportion: N = Z²P(1-P)/e². With a 95% confidence interval (Z = 1.96), estimated proportion of 0.50, and margin of error of 0.05, the calculated sample size was 384. The sample was rounded up to 400 to allow equal allocation of 100 participants per study site, enabling facility-level comparisons. Participants were recruited consecutively from outpatient clinics during the data collection period (September 2023 to April 2024); all eligible patients presenting during operating hours were invited to participate until the site quota was reached.

Data collection methods

Data collection was conducted through interviews and examinations by trained registered nurses at each site, with verification by the principal investigator. Data collection was conducted from September 2023 to April 2024. The process included gathering sociodemographic data, process indicators (HbA1c, LDL-cholesterol, blood pressure, retinal, dental, and foot examinations), and reviewing electronic medical records. Recent laboratory results were considered: HbA1c within 3–6 months, lipid profile within 6–12 months, and current blood pressure readings. Optimal targets were defined as HbA1c < 7%, blood pressure ≤ 130/80 mmHg, and LDL cholesterol ≤ 2.6mmol/L. For retinopathy assessment, we used documented retinal exam results from the past 12 months; patients without recent examinations were referred to ophthalmology, with 10% cost coverage provided when needed. Retinopathy was classified as absent, background, non-proliferative, or proliferative.

Variable definitions

The dependent variables in this study were two binary outcomes: glycaemic control, defined as HbA1c below 7% (good control) versus HbA1c of 7% or above (poor control) per ADA 2023 standards; and diabetic retinopathy status, classified as present (background, non-proliferative, or proliferative) or absent based on dilated fundoscopy findings documented within the preceding 12 months.

The independent variables comprised sociodemographic and clinical characteristics hypothesized to influence the dependent outcomes. Sociodemographic variables included age, sex, place of residence, education level, and occupation. Clinical independent variables included diabetes type, duration of diabetes (1–5, 6–20, and above 20 years), treatment modality (insulin alone, oral therapy, or combination), compliance to treatment, body mass index, and presence of hypertension. For the retinopathy model, current blood pressure measurement (normal versus elevated, below or above 130/80 mmHg) and statin use were additionally considered as independent predictors.

Data management and analysis

Data were collected on paper forms, screened for completeness by the principal investigator, and entered into Microsoft Excel. Double data entry and range checks were performed during data cleaning prior to analysis. Cleaned data were exported to STATA Version 17 SE for analysis. Descriptive statistics (frequencies, proportions, means, and standard deviations) were computed for sociodemographic and clinical characteristics. The outcome variable for glycaemic control was coded as 1 = poor control (HbA1c > 7%) and 0 = good control (HbA1c ≤ 7%); for retinopathy, 1 = present and 0 = absent. Binary logistic regression was performed to identify factors associated with poor glycaemic control and retinopathy. Variables significant at p < 0.20 in bivariate analysis were entered into the multivariate model. Model fit was assessed using the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test (p > 0.05 for both models). Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05 with a 95% confidence interval.

Ethical considerations

This study was conducted following ethical approval from the university of Rwanda, college of medicine and health sciences CMHC Institutional Review Board and the hospital study site’s ethical committee. Written informed consents were obtained from the participants or next of kin. Participation was entirely voluntary, and participants retained the right to withdraw at any time without affecting their ongoing medical care. This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of our institutional review board and with the 1964 Helsinki Declaration and its later amendments.

Results

Socio-demographic characteristics

A total of 400 participants from four University teaching Hospitals in Rwanda (KFHR, CHUK, RMH, CHUB), participated in the study. As shown in Table 1, Participants were evenly distributed across all four hospitals, each accounting for 25% of the total sample (100 participants). Majority of participants were female (69.5%). Most of the participants were over 50 years old (71.75%), and majority lived in urban areas (74.75%). Over half of the participants had secondary or tertiary education (50.75%), 42.75% had primary school education, and 6.5% had no formal education. Of the participants, 49.75% had a normal BMI, 23.75% were overweight, 23.75% were obese, and 2.75% were underweight.

Diabetes mellitus characteristics

Almost all participants had type 2 diabetes (96.75%). Majority of participants have had diabetes for 6–20 years (51.5%), 40.5% for 1–5 years, and 8% for over 20 years. A larger proportion of participants were on oral therapy (71.5%), followed by insulin 16.25% and 12.25% combined therapy. The majority reported full compliance to diabetes treatment (82%), defined as taking all prescribed medications consistently; partial compliance (18%) was defined as occasional missed doses. No participant reported complete non-compliance. Among participants, 65% had co-existing hypertension, and only 30.25% were on statin therapy. A proportion of 31.25% had a prior history of retinopathy (as shown in Table 1).

Achievement of diabetes care goals (Process and intermediate outcome indicators)

Performance of process indicators

The Table 2, presents the attainment of process indicators for diabetes care across four healthcare facilities (CHUB, CHUK, KFH, and RMH). Generally, the performance of measuring glycated hemoglobin every 3 to 6 months was high (90.75%), with a small proportion having glycated hemoglobin measured once in a year (9.25%). The results show notable variations in the performance of these process indicators. KFH, RMH and CHUK had the highest proportion of patients who had their HbA1c measured every 3 months within the last year:93%,69% and 62% respectively, while CHUB had the lowest (37%). Similarly, CHUK, RMH and KFH had the highest performance rates of LDL cholesterol exams (89%,81% and 69% respectively), urine protein testing (89%,83% and 69%), while CHUB lagged behind in these metrics. Foot and dental exams were particularly low across all facilities, with only 19% and 26% of patients receiving these checks, respectively. The blood pressure of all patients was measured at every visit, demonstrating consistent adherence to this process indicator.

Achievement of diabetic care goals (intermediate outcomes indicators)

As shown in Table 2, achievement of diabetes care goals was assessed based on three intermediate outcome indicators (glycaemic, blood pressure, and LDL cholesterol control), benchmarked against ADA 2023 targets. The Table 2 also presents the intermediate outcome indicators for diabetes management across the four healthcare facilities. In terms of glycemic control, 44.5% of the total patients had an HbA1c level below 7%, indicating good control. However, this proportion varied significantly across the facilities, with CHUK having the highest percentage of patients (53%) with HbA1c below 7%, followed by CHU and KFH (both at48%), while RMH had the lowest (29%). Similarly, 65.25% of all patients had blood pressure in normal range (below 130/80 mmHg), but again, the facility-level differences were notable, ranging from 62% at KFH to 67% at CHUB. Regarding lipid profile, 55% of patients had an optimal LDL cholesterol level (below 2.6 mmol/L), with the highest proportion at KFH (75%) and the lowest at RMH (44%). Triglyceride levels were largely within the optimal range (below 2.3 mmol/L) across the facilities, with an average of 91% of patients achieving this target.

Factors associated with glycemic control among participants

A binary bivariate logistic regression model was conducted and revealed that type and duration of diabetes, type of diabetes treatment, and patient’s BMI significantly were associated with glycaemic control (as shown in Table 3). Results showed that patients with type 2 diabetes were less likely to have poor glycemic control comparing to those with type 1diabetes [cOR 0.21, 95% CI: 0.47, 0.99, p = 0.04]. Participants with diabetes for 6–20 years were 1.78 more likely to have unoptimized glycaemic control compared to those with 1–5 years [cOR 1.78, 95% CI: 1.17, 2.7, p = 0.007]. Participants who were on oral therapy alone were less likely to have poor glycaemic control [cOR 0.26, 95% CI:0.14, 0.44, p = < 0.0001]. Obese patients were 3.99 times more likely to have poor glycaemic control [cOR 3.99, 95% CI: 0.99, 16.04, p = 0.05].

The multivariate logistic regression analysis, after adjusting for other variables, the results show that the type of diabetes treatment remains the most significant factor associated with glycemic control (as shown in Table 3). Specifically, patients on oral therapy had significantly lower odds of glycemic control compared to those on insulin alone (aOR: 0.306, 95% CI: 0.157–0.596, p = 0.001). Interestingly, the type of diabetes (Type 1 vs. Type 2), which was significant in the bivariate analysis, lost its significance in the multivariate model (aOR: 0.561, 95% CI: 0.111–2.829, p = 0.484).

Risk factors associated with diabetic retinopathy

To determine the retinopathy factors, a bivariate logistic regression analysis was conducted. The results showed that duration of diabetes, type of diabetes treatment, and blood pressure level were associated with diabetic retinopathy significantly (Table 4). Participants with diabetes for 6–20 years and those with diabetes for above 20 years are more exposed retinopathy comparing to those with diabetes for 1–5 years [cOR 2.13, 95% CI:1.31, 3.45, p = 0.002, and cOR 2.77, 95% CI:1.24, 6.21, p = 0.01] respectively. Patients who were on oral therapy alone had a lower risk of getting retinopathy compared to those on Insulin therapy [cOR 0.56, 95%CI:0.31, 1.01, p = 0.05]. Presence of hypertension (cOR = 1.708; p = 0.027), and elevated blood pressure (cOR = 1.5, 95% CI:1.01,1.4, p = 0.04) were all associated with higher odds of diabetic retinopathy significantly.

The multivariate logistic regression analysis revealed that the duration of diabetes was the most significant factor associated with diabetic retinopathy (as shown in Table 4). Patients with 6–20 years of diabetes duration had nearly twice the odds of developing diabetic retinopathy compared to those with 1–5 years of duration (aOR = 1.985, CI: 1.217, 3.236; p = 0.006), and this risk further increased for those with more than 20 years of diabetes (aOR = 2.488, 1.100, 5.630; p = 0.029).

Discussion

The present study is the first in the country to assess the performance of process and intermediate outcomes indicators in adults with diabetes at tertiary hospitals, with the aim to look at the attainment of diabetes care goals and magnitude of retinopathy). The study enrolled 400 participants across RMH, KFH, CHUK, and CHUB, with comparable sociodemographic and clinical characteristics across sites (Table 1). Key findings include suboptimal achievement of glycaemic control, significant gaps in foot and dental examination performance, and a strong dose-response relationship between diabetes duration and retinopathy risk.

This study found the overall performance of 3 to 6 months glycated hemoglobin testing to be high (90.75%), annual LDL cholesterol testing relatively high (69.25%). These findings are much better than those in a study done in Israel using annual performance data of quality indicators for 222,235 diabetic patients in 2005 where the adherence rate was 70% for HbA1C and LDL Cholesterol annual testing [15]. The highest performance of glycated hemoglobin every 3 to 6 months could be attributed to the accountability of doctors to comply to diabetes care standards but also to the availability of the test at tertiary levels. This may contribute to the good follow up of patients and achieve the diabetes care goals.

Blood pressure measurement was performed to all study participants at every visit across all four hospitals, this would be attributed to the fact that taking BP is part of basic vital signs at nursing station where blood pressure machines are always available and also to the accountability of professional Nurses. This is consistent with findings from a cross-sectional study which was done among 500 participants in specialized diabetes care clinics in Bahrain kingdom in Middle East Asia, where blood pressure measurements were performed at 100% [16]. From the same study, eye and foot exams were performed at 87% and 97% respectively, which were much better than our findings where eye and foot exams were 56.5% and 19% respectively. These disparities underscore the need for system-level accountability mechanisms to ensure adherence to ophthalmology referral and podiatric examination protocols at all facilities.

The lowest performances of almost all process indicators recorded at CHUB, may be attributed to the fact that clients with diabetes are seen by Internal Medicine Residents and Internists with an inconsistent follow up due to the turnover of the staff. The majority of patients with diabetes at KFH, CHUK and RMH are followed by Endocrinologists who are regularly available. The low annual performance of eye, dental, and foot examinations across all hospitals is not attributable to lack of service availability, as all four facilities offer these services covered under community and private insurance. Systemic factors — including high patient-to-physician ratios, documentation burden, and inconsistent subspecialty referral pathways — are more plausible contributors and warrant targeted investigation in future health systems research.

In this study, the overall achievement of an optimal glycemic control (HbA1C below 7%) was relatively low (44.50), while optimal lipid control was relatively high (LDL Cholesterol:55%). Blood pressure control below 130/80mmHg was achieved at 65% globally. Different studies conducted in both developed and developing countries showed suboptimal attainment of diabetes care goals. A study analyzed achievement of diabetes care goals in United States diabetic patients from 1999 to 2010 showed almost a half of patients didn’t meet the diabetes care goals despite there was an improvement in adherence to preventive practices [17].

Assessment of achievement of diabetes care goals in Nepal on 336 diabetic patients in 2020 showed optimal glycemic control at 56.6%, LDL cholesterol at 56.3% and blood pressure at 72% [18], it was 26.1% and 75.2% for glycemic and blood pressure respectively in Ethiopia at Tikur Anbessa Hospital in a study done on 421 diabetic patients in 2020 [18]. In a cohort analysis of 7th wave of international diabetes management studies in Kenya on 194 individuals with diabetes in 2016, an optimal Hba1C was attained only by 36.6% of participants [19],and it was 21% in a systemic review and metanalysis study done across Africa referring to 109 published articles on the attainment of diabetes management goals, most of the articles were from Eastern African countries. From the same source, an optimal blood pressure and LDL Cholesterol was 38% and 42% respectively [20]. From these observations, diabetes treatment goals attainment is suboptimal across the world, most in developing countries. Further investigations are needed to have details and plan accordingly to improve at the maximum possible the diabetes treatment goals achievement.

The bivariate logistic analysis showed that duration of diabetes and diabetic treatment to be the factors associated with glycemic control in this study. Having diabetes between 6 and 20 years was 1.51 more likely for poor glycemic control compared to 1 to 5 years [cOR 1.78, 95% CI: 1.17, 2.7, p = 0.007] and this is similar to the findings in a cross-sectional study done in Menelik II referral hospital in Ethiopia for 245 patients with diabetes who were having diabetes for more than 10 years exposed to suboptimal glycemic control [aOR 2.3,95%ci,1.028,5.08] [21]. Patient with type 2 diabetes were less likely to have poor glycemic optimization compared to those with type 1 diabetes [cOR 0.21, 95% CI: 0.47, 0.99, p = 0.04], this was similar to the findings in a cross-sectional study in Northwest Ethiopia on 423 patients with diabetes where having type 1 diabetes led to suboptimal glycemic control [aOR: 2.53, 95% CI:1.47,4.37] p value < 0.05 [18]. From the same study, duration of diabetes for more than 10 years led to poor glycemic control 2 times than those with less than 5 years [aOR: 2.12, 95% CI: 1.09, 4.12)], which was comparable to the findings from our study. The patient on oral therapy alone were less likely to have poor glycemic control compare to those on insulin alone [cOR 0.26, 95% CI: 0.14, 0.44, p < 0.0001]. Most individuals with type 2 diabetes are controlled on oral medications alone and this may increase the level of compliance to treatment compared to those taking Insulin as an injectable treatment.

Moreover, through binary bivariate analysis the factors associated with retinopathy were assessed, it was found that patients with diabetes for 6–20 years had 2.13 times the odds, and those with diabetes for more than 20 years had 2.77 times the odds of retinopathy compared to those with diabetes for 1–5 years [cOR 2.13, 95% CI:1.31, 3.45, p = 0.002, and cOR 2.77, 95% CI:1.24, 6.21, p = 0.01]. Patients who were on oral therapy alone had a lower risk of getting retinopathy compared to those on Insulin therapy [cOR 0.56, 95%CI:0.31, 1, p = 0.05]. And also, patient with hypertension have 1.708 odd of having retinopathy compared to those with no hypertension (cOR = 1.708, p = 0.027). These findings are consistent with those in the Wisconsin Epidemiologic Study of Diabetic Retinopathy (WESDR) where the increased prevalence of any retinopathy was associated with the long duration of diabetes [22].

Multivariate logistic regression analysis, showed that the patients on oral therapy had significantly lower odds of glycemic control compared to those on insulin alone (aOR: 0.306, 95% CI: 0.157–0.596, p = 0.001). Patients with 6–20 years of diabetes duration had 1.985 the odds of developing diabetic retinopathy compared to those with 1–5 years of duration (aOR = 1.985, p = 0.006), and this risk further increased for those with more than 20 years of diabetes with 2.488 odds of having diabetic retinopathy (p = 0.029). This was similar to the findings in a cross-sectional study in Brunei on 341 patients with diabetes where diabetes for more 10 years was associated with retinopathy [aOR 3.88, 95% CI:1.78,8.46] with a P value < 0.014 [23]. Moreover, these findings are consistent to the study which was conducted in sub-Saharan Africa which revealed that factors associated with the development of diabetic retinopathy were duration of diabetes, uncontrolled blood pressure in a review of diabetic retinopathy factors in sub-Saharan Africa that combined findings of 25 studies and reviews [24]. These findings collectively emphasize that sustained glycaemic management and regular retinal screening particularly for patients with disease duration exceeding six years and those with concomitant hypertension — are critical to minimizing retinopathy onset and preventing progression to sight-threatening stages within Rwandan tertiary care settings.

Strengths of the study

This study was the first in the country to assess the performance of process and intermediate outcomes indicators in adults with diabetes. It was done in well characterized tertiary hospitals with the ability to the maximum care to patients with diabetes, and included equal number of participants with almost similar sociodemographic and diabetes characteristics. This study provided data on achievement of diabetes care goals, with them policy makers and Clinicians may plan to further improve diabetes care. Again, the study looked at factors associated with poor glycemic control and retinopathy as well. Finally, the study provides facility-level comparative data across the four main tertiary referral hospitals in Rwanda, offering a representative picture of diabetes care quality at the highest level of the health system.

Limitations of the study

The study looked at the performance of process indicators as well intermediate outcome indicators and the magnitude of retinopathy. It could not collect relevant data on the determinants of glycemic, lipids and blood pressure control which we expect would influence the study findings regarding the impact of clinical practice to the clinical outcome of individuals with diabetes in Rwanda. It didn’t consider the factors influencing glycated hemoglobin like anemia, chronic kidney disease, polycythemia. Again, we were limited to diabetic retinopathy, while we could include all eye/retinal complications of diabetes for the completeness of the study. We do recommend further study looking at all mentioned aspects in the future. This study employed cross sectional, cannot establish causality between the factors examined and the outcomes observed, as it lacks the temporal sequence necessary for causal inference. Additionally, the cross-sectional nature limits the ability to assess changes in care processes or outcomes over time, which could be important for understanding the long-term effectiveness of diabetes management strategies. The findings from this study cannot generalize the whole country, because the patients from the primary health care level were not included. The study did not collect detailed socio-economic data such as household income, employment category, or level of insurance coverage, which may mediate access to care, adherence to follow-up, and the achievement of diabetes care goals. Future studies should incorporate these variables to better characterize the structural determinants of diabetes outcomes in Rwanda. Lifestyle-related variables including dietary patterns, physical activity, tobacco use, and alcohol consumption were not assessed in this study; these factors are known to influence glycaemic control, blood pressure, and lipid parameters, and their absence limits the completeness of our etiological understanding of suboptimal diabetes care goal achievement. Out-of-pocket expenditure was not explicitly assessed; residual co-payments may nonetheless constitute a barrier for some patients, potentially contributing to the observed gaps in annual eye, foot, and dental examination performance. The analysis did not account for potential clustering of outcomes by hospital site; future studies should employ multilevel modelling to address intra-facility correlation. The equal allocation of 100 participants per site, rather than proportional allocation based on each facility’s total patient volume, may limit the representativeness of aggregate prevalence estimates. The very small number of Type 1 diabetes participants (n = 13, 3.25%) limits the reliability of subgroup regression estimates for this group, and findings pertaining to diabetes type should be interpreted with caution.

Conclusion

While this study revealed strong performance in certain process indicators like blood pressure monitoring and glycated hemoglobin testing across tertiary hospitals in Rwanda, it also highlighted significant gaps in comprehensive diabetes care, particularly in eye, foot, and dental examinations. The suboptimal achievement of glycemic, cholesterol, and blood pressure control targets, coupled with the correlation between disease duration and poor outcomes, calls for urgent systemic changes. Establishing dedicated diabetic centers, implementing comprehensive medical education programs, and conducting regular clinical audits aim to address these challenges is recommended. Further research is crucial to understand the barriers to achieving optimal treatment goals and their relationship with clinical outcomes in the Rwandan population. These findings and recommendations provide a roadmap for strengthening diabetes care delivery and improving patient outcomes within Rwanda’s healthcare system. Specifically, national policy should prioritize the mandatory integration of ophthalmology, podiatry, and dental screening into routine diabetes follow-up protocols at all tertiary facilities, supported by dedicated funding mechanisms, trained multidisciplinary teams, and standardized audit tools to ensure accountability and sustained improvement in comprehensive diabetes care delivery.
