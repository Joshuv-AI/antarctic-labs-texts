Combined application of biochar-compost and cow urine enhances organic rice production
Authors: Sharaban Tahura, Shamim Mia, Md Harun Rashid, Md Kamal Hossain, Mohammad Mahmudur Rahman, Keiji Jindo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBoosting rice (Oryza sativa) yield under organic farming remains challenging, particularly due to nitrogen (N) limitations that hinder primary productivity. This study assessed the effects of cow urine combined with biochar-compost on the growth and yield of three indigenous aromatic rice cultivars (Kalizera, Chinigura, and Shhakharkhora). Five organic amendment treatments were tested: compost, cow urine, compost + cow urine, biochar-compost, and biochar-compost + cow urine. Chinigura showed the highest grain yield (3.13 t/ha), which increased significantly to 4.03 t/ha under the compost + cow urine treatment. The addition of cow urine to biochar-compost enhanced N availability in both soil and plant tissues. Principal component analysis (PCA) revealed strong positive correlations between grain yield and nutrient concentrations. These findings suggest that integrating N-rich organic inputs—particularly cow urine—with compost can improve the performance of local aromatic rice cultivars and enhance nutrient availability in organic systems.Keywords: Biochar-compost, Cow urine, Fine rice, Organic management practicesSubject terms: Ecology, Environmental sciences

Introduction

Rice (Oryza sativa L.) is one of the world’s major food crops, providing an estimated 20% of the dietary energy to most people in Asia, including Bangladesh1,2. Although increasing bold rice productivity with modern agricultural practices, including agrochemicals, remains the main agenda for many developing countries, the demand for aromatic rice has also been growing3. In recent years, aromatic rice, also called fine rice, scented rice or fragrance rice, has received special attention in the global market. However, the aromatic rice production in Bangladesh is low, estimated at 188 thousand tons. The demand and price of organically produced rice is even higher than that of inorganically produced rice, which provides a net benefit to farmers while sustaining environmental quality4,5. The widespread use of synthetic Nitrogen (N) fertilizers contributes to greenhouse gas emissions, nutrient leaching, run-off, and degradation of aquatic ecosystems, particularly in regions with fragile coastal watersheds. In areas where smallholder farmers have limited access to synthetic inputs, recycling of N from locally available resources such as cow urine offers a practical alternative that can reduce environmental degradation compared to synthetic fertilizers, while supporting soil fertility and climate resilience.

Organic agriculture provides multiple benefits over inorganic agriculture, including improved product quality, ensuring human health and leaving less environmental footprint3. Moreover, it sustains soil health3,4. However, the yield of organically produced rice is often less than inorganically produced rice. One of the main reasons for this low yield is the supply of insufficient amounts of certain nutrients to support crop growth and yield6.

In organic agriculture, nutrients are usually supplied from organic origin, including compost and other animal manures7. However, the nutrient supply rate, especially N, from these sources is often insufficient to support crop growth. N is required in large quantities (1–5% of dry biomass) while its loss is also high due to volatilization, leaching, and de-nitrification8. Therefore, the N supply to plant can be promoted by the application of N-rich substances such as urine to soil along with organic amendments.

Urine from animals (e.g., cow urine) can be a good alternative to synthetic chemical fertilizers since it is less expensive and is usually not recycled for agricultural nutrition. Hence, it can be considered an alternative plant nutrition source9,10, although the volatilization loss of N as ammonia from urine adversely affect the environment. When cow urine is applied together with other organic amendments (e.g., compost), N in the urine may be utilized more efficiently11,12. This improved efficiency could be due to: (1) reduced N loss through enhanced nutrient retention and water-holding capacity provided by the added organic inputs, or (2) the complementary effects of the co-applied organic inputs (e.g., pH buffering, and micronutrient supply)13.

Compost is one of the potential organic amendments for organic agriculture14. However, the nutrient-supplying capacity of compost has been shown to be increased when additives, including biochar or nutrient-rich substances, are added15,16. Biochar is an aromatic carbon-(C) rich porous material. Adding biochar to the composting biomass, it can increase microbial activity, oxidize biochar surfaces, and reduce nutrient loss during composting, improving both biochar and compost quality17,18. Moreover, adding cow urine to biochar-compost may increase the retention of the N in the urine due to its adsorption on the biochar surfaces , which can then be supplied to plants when applied to the soil.

Plant species, including rice, exhibit variations in N uptake and utilization efficiency. The coastal region of Bangladesh, which encompasses approximately 30% of the country’s net cultivable area (estimated at around 0.83 million hectares) hosts numerous rice landraces, many of which are aromatic. Notable examples include Balam, Kalizera, Shhakharkora, and Chinigura19. Local rice landraces are adapted to the region’s unique tidal climatic conditions but typically produce low yields. Given the high market value of aromatic rice, cultivation of such varieties may offer greater benefits to farmers. However, their response to organic and inorganic fertilizers remains understudied.

Despite biochar being one of the most researched topics in recent years, the preparation of compost using biochar as an additive and their performance evaluation for improving crop yield in the field settings are not many20. For instance, other scientists21 examined the performance of biochar (applied alone, mixed with compost, or as composted biochar) on lettuce grown in two soils (Luvisol and gleyic Fluvisol) using a pot trial. However, biochar loaded with liquid N from cow urine has not been studied under field settings22. Combined application of readily available N from urine with a slow cycling N from compost in presence of biochar would improve N use efficiency and grain yield. Because the N in the urine might be adsorbed onto the biochar which might then accelerate the N priming from compost ultimately resulting in a greater N supply. To our knowledge, we are not aware of any studies that examined the effect of composted biochar and cow urine in organic rice cultivation system. In this study, we examined the performance of selected aromatic rice varieties (e.g., Kalizera, Shhakharkora, Chinigura) under a combination of biochar-compost and cow urine treatments using a field trial. We hypothesized that combined application of biochar-compost and cow-urine would improve rice yield through enhanced N use efficiency.

Materials and methods

Field trial

The experiment was conducted at the Agronomy Field Laboratory, Patuakhali Science and Technology University, Dumki, Patuakhali (22.464997 °N and 90.3895586 °E) from June to December 2019. The experiment involved two factors: (a) three local aromatic rice varieties (V1- Kalizera, V2- Chinigura, and V3- Shhakharkhora), and (b) six different treatments (T1- control, which is without amendments; T2- compost; T3- cow urine; T4- compost + cow urine; T5- biochar-compost; T6- biochar-compost + cow urine). The treatments were assigned using a Randomized Complete Block Design with three replications. The rice used in the experiment was obtained from our previous study19. Biochar was produced from sawdust in a slow pyrolysis biochar kiln for 10 h at ~ 400 °C17. The biochar-compost was prepared from kitchen waste, sawdust and biochar at a ratio of 2:1:1 on dry basis while the compost only treatment had kitchen waste and sawdust at a ratio of 1:1.The initial properties of the compost and biochar-compost were presented in our earlier article23. In brief, the compost had C and N concentrations of 27.5 ± 0.1 and 2.1 ± 0.0, respectively, while the values for biochar-compost were 44.9 ± 0.0 and 1.9 ± 0.1, respectively. The cation exchange capacity of the biochar-compost was 38.2 ± 1.1 mol charge/kg dry mass, slightly higher than that of the compost (32.5 mol charge/kg dry mass). The compost and biochar-compost were applied at a rate of 2 t/ha (dry basis), and cow urine was added at a similar rate of 2000 L/ha. The cow urine was collected in the same day of field application from a cattle farm. Compost and biochar-compost was mixed with cow urine (1 kg: 1 L) and applied right after thorough mixing. The biochar, compost and biochar-compost properties presented elsewhere (Hasan et al. preparation). The N concentration in the cow urine was approximately 10 g N/L urine, equivalent to a N application of 20 kg/ha. The soil is clay loam in texture (sand- 35%, silt-38%, clay-27%), and its nutrient status is medium (total N − 0.071%, Olsen phosphorus (P) − 8.49 µg/g, K − 0.8 cmol (+)/kg, available S − 13.40 µg/g, B − 0.59 µg/g soil)23. The treatments were applied to one square meter plots before transplanting different rice cultivars using 33-day-old seedlings. Weeding was done twice while irrigation was provided when the soil moisture was below 60% field capacity. Rice was harvested when 80% of the grain turned into yellow in color.

Plant performance and N concentration determination

Various plant performance indicators were measured, including plant height, number of effective tillers, number of non-effective tillers, panicle length, number of grains per panicle, number of filled grains, number of unfilled grains, 1000 seed weight, and grain yield. The N concentration in the leaves was determined using a Soil Plant Analysis Development chlorophyll meter (SPAD) meter (SPAD-502 plus). The N concentration in the grains was determined using a modified Kjeldahl method24.

Soil properties analysis

At harvest, soil samples were collected from the top 20 cm soil layers, and soil pH, EC value, available NH4+-N and NO3−-N, total N, and available P were measured. Soil pH and EC were measured in soil–water suspension (1:5, m/v) while the soil was extracted with 1 M KCl at 1:5 (m/v) to determine available NH4+-N and NO3−-N. The NH4+-N concentration was determined using the indophenol blue colorimetric method25, while the NO3−-N was measured using a modified colorimetric method26,27. Available P was determined after extraction with 0.5 mol L− 1 NaHCO3 (pH 8.5) at a soil/solution ratio of 1:10 (m/v) using the colorimetric method. We measured the organic matter (OM) content as well as aromatic OM by the loss on ignition method28.

Statistical analysis

A two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was conducted using JMP 8.0 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA) to evaluate the main and interactive effects. Tukey’s HSD test at a significance level of 5% was employed to compare means. Model assumptions, such as normality and equal variance, were assessed, and if the assumptions were not met, appropriate data transformations were applied. Additionally, the linear regression analysis and principal component analysis were conducted.

Ethical approval

No live vertebrates were used in this study, and therefore, no ethical approval was required.

Results

Soil properties

The soil pH value significantly varied due to different organic amendments (p < 0.01, Table 1). Among all the organic amendment treatments, the plots receiving cow urine had the highest soil pH (8.32), while the control condition had the lowest result (7.80). Similarly, soil pH also differed among different rice varieties (Table S1), but there was no significant interaction between variety and organic amendment (Table S2). The soil amendment had a significant impact on soil electrical conductivity (EC) value (p < 0.01), while it was not significant for the interaction between different varieties and organic amendments. Soil inorganic N concentrations (NH4+-N and NO-3–N) varied for different soil amendments and rice varieties, although their interaction effect was not significant. Across all varietal treatments, the biochar-compost + cow urine treatments had the highest NH4+-N (44.92 mg/kg) and NO3−-N (34.64 mg/kg) concentrations. In contrast, the control treatment had the lowest values, i.e., 29.77 mg /kgand 18.60 mg/kg, respectively (Table 1, S1, and S2). Similarly, P concentrations were higher in the plots where organic amendments were combinedly applied with cow urine , but the interaction effect was not significant (Table 1, S1, and S2).

Plant performance

The results demonstrated that the variety had a significant impact on various parameters, including plant height, the total number of tillers per hill, effective tillers per hill, non-effective tillers per hill, panicle length, number of filled grains per panicle, number of unfilled grains per panicle, 1000-grain weight, and grain yield (p < 0.05; Table S3). The Chinigura plant exhibited the tallest height (147.31 cm), the highest total number of tillers per hill (16.65), the highest number of effective tillers per hill (14.65), the highest number of filled grains per panicle (127.77), the longest panicle length (22.67 cm), the highest 1000-grain weight (17.64 g), and the highest grain yield (3.13 t/ha). Additionally, it had the lowest number of non-effective tillers per hill (1.97). Conversely, the Shhakharkhora plant had the lowest values for these parameters.

The organic amendments also had a significant effect on plant height, the total number of tillers per hill, effective tillers per hill, non-effective tillers per hill, panicle length, number of filled grains per panicle, number of unfilled grains per panicle, 1000-grain weight, and grain yield (p < 0.05, Table S4). The treatment with biochar-compost + cow urine showed superiority, exhibiting the tallest plants (151.3 cm), the highest total number of tillers per hill (19.64), the highest number of effective tillers per hill (18.6), the highest number of filled grains per panicle (133.7), longest panicle length (23.43 cm), highest 1000-grain weight (19.44 g), and highest grain yield (4.03 t/ha). It also had the lowest number of non-effective tillers per hill (1.0). Conversely, the control treatment (without organic amendments) yielded the lowest results for these parameters.

The interaction between variety and fertilizer treatments had a significant effect on the total number of tillers per hill, number of filled grains per panicle, number of unfilled grains per panicle, 1000-grain weight, and grain yield (Table S5, Fig. 1). The highest number of total tillers per hill (20.46), the highest number of filled grains per panicle (148.82), highest 1000-grain weight (19.53 g), and highest grain yield (4.09 t/ha) were observed in the interaction between the biochar-compost + cow urine treatment and the Chinigura variety. It also had the lowest number of unfilled grains per panicle (10.49). Conversely, the Shhakharkhora variety with the control treatment yielded the lowest results for these parameters.

N concentration in plant and grain

The N concentration in the leaves, as measured by SPAD value, exhibited significant variation (Table S3-S5). At 53 days after transplantation (DAT), the highest SPAD value (40.57) was observed in the plots treated with biochar-compost + cow urine, while the lowest SPAD value (38.11) was recorded in the control condition. Similarly, the grain N uptake was significantly higher in the combined application of compost and cow urine, although the N concentration did not show a significant difference (Fig. 2). A linear association was found between grain yield and SPAD value, which serves as a measure of leaf N content (r2 = 0.5247, p < 0.01). However, no significant relationship was observed between grain yield and grain N concentration.

Principal component analysis (PCA)

Principal component analysis was employed to analyze and generalize the variations among different explanatory factors. The results demonstrated that principal component 1 (PC1) accounted for 62.0% of the total observed variations among the characters, while PC2 explained 13.5%. The plot of PC1 and PC2 revealed that the lengths and directions of the arrows of several variables are seen in the same locations, such as an effective tiller, yield, panicle length, NH4+, and 1000-grain weight (Fig. 3).

Discussion

Variability in rice yield among cultivars

Rice yield potential varies among cultivars19. Averaged across organic amendment treatments, the highest grain yield was obtained from the Chinigura variety, followed by Kalizera and Shhakharkhora. This higher yield was primarily attributed to more effective tillers per hill, more grains per panicle, and increased individual grain weight29. Similar findings were reported in previous research19, which showed variations in yield potential among local landraces in the coastal area.

Enhanced rice yield with combined application of compost and cow urine

Organic agriculture offers a sustainable approach to achieve high-quality crop production. In our study, we investigated the effectiveness of different organic amendments in increasing the yield of local aromatic rice. Among the organic amendments, the highest grain yield was recorded in plots receiving the biochar-compost + cow urine treatment, followed by the compost + cow urine treatment. The application of biochar-compost + cow urine demonstrated superiority in terms of plant height, the total number of tillers per hill, effective tillers per hill, panicle length, number of filled grains per panicle, and grain yield (Table S3-5, Fig. 2). This treatment likely provided sufficient nutrients to the plants, resulting in improved performance. The biochar compost and cow urine carried both fast and slow cycling N. Specifically, cow urine contained readily available N while compost carried mineralizable N. Biochar-compost might have adsorbed the readily available N from the cow urine. It is plausible since biochar had a greater CEC and specific surface area, which allows it to retain cationic nutrients, including N30. The additional N might have promoted N priming. Soil N priming, i.e., release of N from the mineralization of labile OM, both in the soil and applied through the addition of biochar-compost31. A greater N retention and cycling might also have reduced the N loss32 This increased supply of N might have provided sufficient N provision for improving rice yield33–35. Our findings support these notions, as we observed higher soil N content (Table 1), higher SPAD values (indicating better plant N status) (Fig. S1 & 2), and significantly higher N uptake in the biochar-compost and compost treatments (Fig. 2). Furthermore, a positive relationship was observed between grain yield and SPAD values (Fig. 4). It is also possible that other plant nutrients including P might have contributed to the yield increment. Soil-available P was considerably higher in the biochar-compost treatments, suggesting that P may have also contributed to the grain yield. Our results imply that integration of readily available N from cow urine with slow cycling N from compost can potentially enhance the retention ofN on the biochar and OM surfaces, which then can be taken up by crops, resulting in a significant improvement in grain yield.

It should be noted that this study is limited by the use of a single type of biochar and two types of organic amendments (cow urine and compost). Future research should investigate a wider variety of biochar types in combination with diverse organic inputs, such as vermicomposts, to better understand their interactive effects on soil properties and plant performance36,37. Additionally, further studies are needed to examine whether biochar can positively influence soil properties and rice productivity under saline conditions, which characterize many agricultural regions in Bangladesh38.

Conclusion

While organic production systems are often challenged by lower yields compared to conventional methods, our findings demonstrate that combining nutrient-rich amendments such as cow urine with biochar-compost can enhance the productivity of aromatic rice in Bangladesh’s coastal region. Specifically, the co-application of biochar-compost and cow urine significantly improved grain yield, likely due to greater N retention on exchange sites and improved mineralization. The results contribute to a growing body of evidence that showed integration of organic materials with N rich materials can improve N availability and crop performance, offering practical value for enhancing food security in climate-vulnerable regions.
