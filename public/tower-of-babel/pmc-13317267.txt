Clinical impact and cost-effectiveness of the WHO integrated care for older people (ICOPE) Intervention for older adults with intrinsic capacity decline in primary health care facilities in China: study protocol for a cluster-randomised controlled trial
Authors: Xinyan Zhang, Liu Sun, Liwei Jing, Shuqin Xiao, Huan Fan, Yanling Wang, Shuai Jin, Yuanyuan Zhao, Zun Wang, Jun-E Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis cluster-randomised trial aims to evaluate the effectiveness of the WHO Integrated Care for Older People (ICOPE) intervention for community-dwelling older adults with declining intrinsic capacity in China, incorporating a cost-effectiveness analysis from a healthcare system perspective.MethodsThis study will employ a community-based, cluster randomised controlled trial design. A total of 1,400 participants with declining intrinsic capacity aged ≥ 60 years will be recruited from 37 health service stations affiliated with seven community health service centres (CHCs) in Beijing. Within each CHC, clusters will be randomised to either the ICOPE intervention group or the standard health management care group. The ICOPE intervention will comprise a 4-month program featuring structured group health education sessions, group practice activities and online health education delivered via WeChat. Educational content will target six domains of intrinsic capacity: cognitive function, vitality/nutrition, sensory ability, psychological well-being, mobility and traditional Chinese medicine-based approaches. The primary outcomes will include health status and economic evaluations assessed at 12 and 24 months post-intervention. Health outcomes will encompass physical function, cognitive capacity, vitality, mobility, vision capacity, hearing capacity and psychological health. The economic evaluation will assess incremental cost-effectiveness based on quality-adjusted life-years gained, as measured by the EuroQol five-dimensional questionnaire (EQ-5D), using a 12-month time horizon.DiscussionThis trial will generate critical evidence on the effectiveness and cost implications of the WHO ICOPE integrated care model when implemented in a Chinese community setting.This study will provide research evidence on effectiveness and cost-effectiveness of the ICOPE intervention among community-dwelling older adults with declining intrinsic capacity in China. Findings will inform policy and practice development.Trial registrationThe trial has been registered in the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ID: ChiCTR 2400090521). Background

The global population is ageing rapidly [1]. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), adults aged 60 years and older comprised 12% of the global population in 2015, a proportion projected to rise to 22% by 2050. However, this increase in life expectancy has not been paralleled by a corresponding extension of healthspan [2], with significant implications for healthcare systems and society at large [3, 4]. In response to these challenges, the WHO introduced the concept of intrinsic capacity (IC) in its Global Report on Aging and Health. IC refers to the composite of an individual’s physical and mental abilities, representing a paradigm shift from disease-centred to function-centred care for older adults [5]. Emerging evidence indicates that focusing on IC is more effective in promoting healthy ageing than targeting specific chronic diseases [2–4]. Furthermore, reduced IC is strongly associated with adverse outcomes, including disability [6–8], hospitalisation [6], falls [9, 10] and mortality [6–8, 11].

To operationalise this concept, the WHO issued the Integrated Care for Older People (ICOPE) guidelines in 2017, aiming to maintain or slow declines in intrinsic capacity. These guidelines propose a novel, function-centred, and person-centred model of care for older adults living in the community. The ICOPE framework emphasises optimising intrinsic capacity—encompassing all mental and physical faculties—as the central strategy to promote healthy ageing and prevent care dependency [12–14]. The ICOPE care pathway is structured around the assessment and longitudinal monitoring of six core domains of intrinsic capacity: locomotion, vitality, vision, hearing, cognition and psychological well-being [15–17]. It comprises five steps: Screening for potential declines in one or more IC domains. In-depth assessment of individuals identified with domain-specific declines during initial screening. Development of a personalised care plan, co-designed with the individual and their caregivers, addressing IC decline, comorbid conditions, and socio-environmental needs. Implementation of a referral pathway and regular monitoring of the care plan, with appropriate linkage to specialised geriatric services. Engagement of community-based resources and caregiver support systems to sustain care delivery and social integration.

In China, the population aged 65 years and older reached approximately 220 million in 2023, representing nearly one-quarter of the global elderly population—nearly one in every four older adults worldwide. The dual burden of a rapidly ageing population and rising rates of unhealthy longevity has placed mounting pressure on healthcare systems and insurance expenditures. The IC framework offers a proactive opportunity to identify and implement targeted interventions that promote healthy aging among individuals at high risk of functional decline [1]. Nevertheless, evidence on effective strategies to maintain or enhance IC remains limited [2]. In recent years, China has actively explored localised implementation pathways for the ICOPE model, aligning with the WHO’s vision of healthy aging by delivering integrated, person-centred clinical, rehabilitative, and psychosocial services for older adults.

Preliminary evidence suggests that timely assessment and implementation of integrated nursing care can prevent adverse outcomes and improve health status among older adults receiving primary care services [12–14]. Currently, practice-based projects centered around intrinsic capacity are being gradually launched to improve the quality of care for older adults. These ICOPE practice projects have been implemented in multiple countries worldwide, including France [18] and South Korea [19], achieving significant results. In China [20], researchers conducted a study on middle-aged and older adults enrolled in the ICOPE-China project, using the ICOPE screening tool for intrinsic capacity assessments. Overall, while prior studies have largely emphasised the feasibility of large-scale clinical screening and assessment of IC subdomains in primary care settings based on the WHO’s ICOPE guidelines,,robust economic evaluations of such interventions within primary care are notably scarce. This study protocol is innovative in its rigorous evaluation of the WHO ICOPE model within China’s primary healthcare system by employing a cluster-randomised controlled trial design to generate high-level causal evidence on both clinical effectiveness and cost-effectiveness. Additionally, we will examine whether the intervention could both mitigate IC decline and reduce healthcare expenditures for community-dwelling older adults from a healthcare system perspective, with the broader aim of informing public health policy and care model development to promote active and healthy ageing.

Methods

The trial is registered with the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ID: ChiCTR2400090521). Ethical approval was obtained from the Medical Ethics Committee of Capital Medical University (Approval ID: Z2024YS025).

Study design and setting

Participants will be recruited from seven CHCs located in six districts of Beijing: Fengtai, Haidian, Chaoyang, Tongzhou, Miyun and Xicheng. As of 2023, Beijing’s population aged 60 years and above reached 4.948 million, with the selected districts representing over 60% of the city’s elderly population. We will employ purposive sampling to ensure geographic and demographic diversity across suburban, peri-urban, and central urban zones. Written informed consent will be obtained from all participants prior to enrollment.

Procedures

The recruitment process will involve a two-step screening procedure:

The ICOPE framework and study protocol will first be introduced to the target communities through extensive outreach campaigns, including posters and announcements disseminated via both traditional and social media channels [21]. In Step 1, a brief screening for IC decline will be administered via the online platform Wenjuanxing, consisting of 12 questions evaluating cognitive function, mobility, nutrition, vision, hearing, and depressive symptoms. This self-administered or assisted questionnaire will require approximately 5–10 min to complete and will not necessitate any software installation, thus lowering accessibility barriers for older adults, family caregivers, and community health workers. Individuals with negative screening results will be excluded. Those identified with potential IC decline will proceed to Step 2, which involved comprehensive assessments of their physical environment, social needs, and personal health goals. Step 2 results will be deemed positive if any of the following criteria—derived from the ICOPE guidelines—are met: Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) < 25 (cognitive function), Short Physical Performance Battery (SPPB) ≤ 9 (mobility), Mini Nutritional Assessment Short-Form (MNA-SF) < 12 (vitality), Geriatric Depression Scale-5 (GDS-5) ≥ 2 (psychological health), Hearing Handicap Inventory for the Elderly Screening Version (HHIE-S) > 8 (hearing capacity), or impairment detected via WHO distance and near vision screening charts (visual capacity) [18]. All cut-off values are set according to the ICOPE handbook [21]. This two-step screening process will ensure inclusion of participants with measurable IC decline, who are at greater risk of further deterioration.

The integrated care intervention will consist of two key phases. The first will be the planning phase, during which clusters will identify local challenges and formulate implementation strategies tailored to contextual needs. This will be followed by the action phase, during which the intervention activities will be carried out and sustainability mechanisms established (Fig. 1).

ICOPE implementation sequence

The core components of the implementation intervention are as follows: (1) establishment of local implementation structures and clarification of objectives by the research team, (2) baseline screening and comprehensive assessment of intrinsic capacity (IC) to support person-centred integrated care, (3) formation of multidisciplinary and management teams across specialisations, (4) promotion of knowledge about integrated care and its implementation among older adults and (5) continuous monitoring and support of the implementation process (Fig. 1). Each of these components involves more specific implementation activities, described in the sections below.

Identifying local structures and defining the aim

To initiate the intervention, local implementation structures and management personnel will be identified and established. Each city district is expected to form a dedicated project management team responsible for overseeing resource allocation, operational planning, on-the-ground implementation, and follow-up activities. These managers will play a central role in defining the local governance structure and will operate under the initiative titled ‘Leadership in Implementation of the Integrated Care Model Project in Beijing’.

Establishing a multidisciplinary team

The managers will also be tasked with assembling a multidisciplinary team comprising 10–15 healthcare professionals from diverse backgrounds. This team will be responsible for delivering personalised care and influencing multiple sectors within the municipal healthcare system. The team will lead the design and implementation of the intervention at the CHC level through structured workshops and a series of three training sessions facilitated by ICOPE researchers.

Promoting knowledge on implementation and Integrated Care intervention

The training component of this study will be conducted in the form of structured workshops. Each multidisciplinary team will participate in seven half-day workshops, conducted across seven CHCs. The primary goal of these workshops will be to equip teams with the knowledge, tools, and strategies required for effective intervention delivery. A central output of these workshops will be an implementation plan informed by the WHO-endorsed ICOPE model and the Knowledge to Action framework [21]. Two additional workshops will be held: one to follow up on implementation progress and another to share experiences, present interim findings, and refine the long-term sustainability strategy.

Supporting the implementation process

To further support CHCs, each team will be provided with an implementation toolkit, including educational posters, PowerPoint materials, daily content push cards, and brochures promoting the enhancement of intrinsic capacity among older adults. During the intervention period, the project team will remain available to offer support as needed.

Randomisation

This study is a cluster-randomised controlled trial conducted across multiple service stations under CHCs. Participants will be randomly allocated in a 1:1 ratio to intervention and control groups. Allocation will be disclosed to the research team only after participants are deemed eligible and have provided informed consent. Cluster randomisation within CHCs helps ensure demographic and socioeconomic comparability—particularly in terms of age distribution and household income levels—while also minimising inter-cluster differences at baseline.Specifically, we will employ a stratified randomization procedure based on the minimization method. First, based on the household registration population records of the communities where the service stations are located, we will collect key demographic and socioeconomic characteristics of the eligible service stations during the baseline period. The “average age” and “proportion of low-income households” will be selected as the core variables for stratified randomization. Subsequently, using a computer-generated random sequence, under the constraint of minimizing the total difference in these two key indicators between the two groups, the service stations will be dynamically allocated to either the intervention group or the control group. This process will be carried out by an independent statistician who was not involved in the subsequent implementation and evaluation of the trial. Recruitment of participating city districts will commence in October 2024 and conclude in January 2025.

Blinding

Blinding of care providers and participants will not be feasible given their direct involvement in intervention workshops, which necessarily discloses group allocation. However, outcome assessors will remain blinded to group assignments to reduce the risk of assessment bias.

Sample size

Sample size estimation is based on the primary outcome using the cluster randomised trial formula [22]. A conservative intra-cluster correlation coefficient (ICC, ρ) of 0.06 and an average cluster size of 100 participants are assumed [23]. To control the Type I error rate, we assume an expected between-group standard deviation (SD) of 0.33, a within-group standard deviation (SD_w) of 2.69 for the primary outcome, based on pilot data, a significance level of 5%, and a power of 80%. Accounting for an anticipated 10% attrition over 12 months of follow-up, we aim to recruit at least 312 participants per group. In total, 1,400 participants will be enrolled from seven communities—700 each in the intervention and control groups.

Study population

A broader cohort of 3,500 community-dwelling adults aged ≥ 60 years will be recruited for baseline assessments in Beijing starting October 2024. Eligibility criteria include being 60 years or older, residing in the community for at least 6 months, and providing signed informed consent. Exclusion criteria comprise terminal illness with life expectancy under 6 months, malignancies, dementia (Clinical Dementia Rating ≥ 1 or a prior diagnosis), significant cognitive impairment (5-min MoCA ≤ 5), major psychiatric disorders, and severe sensory or communication impairments. From this initial sample, 200 older adults with declining IC will be selected from each CHC for detailed evaluation. Individuals currently enrolled in other clinical intervention studies will be excluded to avoid confounding effects.

Recruitment and allocation

Each CHC will establish a multidisciplinary team comprising general practitioners, nurses, traditional Chinese medicine practitioners, dietitians, and rehabilitation specialists, in accordance with the WHO ICOPE Handbook. In-community recruitment will be carried out by designated members of these teams, typically nurses or other healthcare professionals. Recruitment for baseline assessment will continue for 4 months or until 500 participants are enrolled per CHC. From these, 200 older adults identified with declining IC in each CHC will be randomly assigned to either the intervention or control group.

Study flow

Eligible participants will undergo comprehensive baseline and in-depth assessments before the intervention. These include clinical evaluations, physiological and biochemical tests, cognitive and psychological assessments, vitality assessments, sensory function (vision and hearing) and quality of life measures. Participants will then be randomised to the integrated care intervention or control group. The intervention period will span 4 months, with follow-up outcome measurements at 6, 12 and 24 months post-intervention.

Trial design

Our reporting in this protocol adheres to the SPIRIT checklist [24] and CONSORT checklist for cluster RCTs [25].

Interventions

An individualised care plan will be developed for each participant in the intervention group [21], based on the ICOPE generic care pathway and aligned with person-centred goals. Participants in this group will receive integrated care interventions guided by the WHO ICOPE guidelines [21]. Meanwhile, participants in the control group, like those in the intervention group, also received the first service session, which involved explaining the concept of ICOPE, the assessment report and the care plan to each of them. However, the control group will only access standard services without additional ICOPE-based care.Intervention content at each CHC will be delivered by experienced community generalists, following ICOPE-prescribed health management and care actions. For those in the intervention group, the program includes monthly group health education sessions and practice activities over 4 months, targeting all six domains of intrinsic capacity: cognition, vitality/nutrition, sensory function, psychological well-being, mobility, and traditional Chinese medicine interventions.As recommended in the ICOPE approach [2], all care plans will be developed based on a comprehensive geriatric assessment. Based on the assessment results, personalized intervention plans will be formulated to address specific risks and needs identified for each older adult. Interventions may include physical exercise guidance, nutritional support, cognitive training, auditory and visual training, depression management, medication review, and social engagement activities, among others, all customized according to the individual’s assessment profile. Health education content will also be delivered via daily WeChat messages. Each education session will last approximately 40–60 min. The implementation consists of a 4-month intervention phase followed by a 24-month follow-up period, while the control group will continue with usual care throughout.

The core trial is a multi-centre, single-blind, parallel-arm, cluster-randomised controlled study. Each cluster will undergo a 4-month recruitment period, followed by individual participant follow-ups for 24 months. A process evaluation will be conducted alongside the trial (see Fig. 2 for the study flowchart).

Summary of study design

Registration of ICOPE intervention adherence

In each group, community-based multidisciplinary team members will document the implementation of each intervention session using attendance forms. These records will include the session date, health education content delivered and the participation status of each older adult. Additionally, to enhance adherence, daily health education messages will be disseminated through WeChat groups, along with access to medical health management and counselling services.

Data collection

Data will be collected at five time points: baseline (T0: January 2025, before randomisation), post-intervention (T1: May 2025), and at 6-month (T2: November 2025), 12-month (T3: May 2026) and 24-month (T4: May 2027) follow-ups. Data sources will include participant-completed questionnaires and healthcare administrative systems. At each time point (T0–T4), questionnaires will be distributed to participants recruited from each CHC. Total healthcare costs and resource utilisation will be extracted from primary health care information systems. One designated individual (a manager or health professional) at each CHC will serve as a study coordinator responsible for data collection.

Outcome measures

Primary and secondary outcomes will be derived from medical records and self-reported questionnaires. The primary outcomes include health status and cost-effectiveness evaluated at 12 and 24 months post-intervention. Health outcomes will be assessed using tools adapted from the WHO ICOPE Handbook, all of which have been localised and validated in Chinese [20, 21, 26]. Physical function will be measured using the 14-item Activities of Daily Living (ADL) 14-questionnaire scale [27] and the Fried Frailty Phenotype (FP). Cognitive capacity will be assessed with the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) [20]. Vitality will be evaluated using body mass index (BMI) and the Mini Nutritional Assessment—Short Form (MNA-SF) [28]. Mobility will be assessed through the Chair Rise Test and the Short Physical Performance Battery (SPPB) [27]. Psychological health will be measured using the five-item Geriatric Depression Scale (GDS-5) [27]. Vision will be evaluated with WHO distance and near vision charts [29], and hearing will be assessed via the Whisper Voice Test and the Hearing Handicap Inventory for the Elderly—Screening version (HHIE-S). The study will also assess participants’ intervention-related costs and healthcare resource utilisation.

Intervention costs

An economic evaluation will be conducted to estimate the costs associated with implementing the intervention. Data will include time spent by multidisciplinary team members, participation in seminars, and involvement in project promotion meetings. Information will be collected on the number, job titles, and income levels of primary healthcare professionals participating in these activities. The economic evaluation will adopt a healthcare sector perspective and a 12-month time horizon. Direct healthcare costs will be considered and discounted at an annual rate of 3% [30, 31]. Total patient-level healthcare costs during the intervention will be obtained from each CHC’s hospital information system (HIS). Cost-effectiveness will be assessed by combining the incremental cost with the quality-adjusted life years (QALYs) gained as a result of the intervention [32]. The effectiveness of the intervention was assessed using the EuroQol five-dimensional questionnaire (EQ-5D), a standardised instrument for measuring generic health status. The EQ-5D comprises five dimensions—mobility, self-care, usual activities, pain/discomfort and anxiety/depression—rated on a three-point scale (no problems, some or moderate problems, extreme problems). It generates preference-based scores for health-related quality of life, which were used to calculate QALYs [33]. In addition, the study will evaluate intervention-related costs and healthcare resource utilisation. Secondary outcomes include medical service utilisation 12 months post-intervention, measured by the annual number of outpatient visits and inpatient admissions.

Statistical analysis

Numerical variables will be summarised as means ± standard deviations for normally distributed data (normality assessed using kernel density estimation), and as medians with interquartile ranges for non-normally distributed data. Categorical variables will be reported as frequencies with percentages. Between-group differences in the mean change in the primary outcome will be assessed at each time-point using linear mixed models. Models will include fixed effects for treatment group (intervention vs. control), time (baseline, 12-month, and 24-month follow-up), and a time-by-treatment group interaction term. A random level intercept for CHC will be included to account for the clustered design of the study. A random intercept for older adults nested within CHC will also be included to account for potential repeated measures by older adults. The linear mixed models will use all available data, assuming missing data is at random. The model will adjust for the baseline value of the outcome and a priori specified potential confounders, including demographic characteristics (age, sex, education level and insurance), and clinical profiles (the number of comorbidities and medications at baseline). Statistical significance will be defined as a two-tailed P value < 0.05. All statistical analyses will be conducted by a statistician blinded to group allocation. To minimise potential confounding and enhance robustness, sensitivity analyses will be conducted using 1:1 propensity score matching (PSM) based on all baseline covariates [34]. Specifically, we will match the two groups of older adults according to their characteristics and community health service facility characteristics. Older adults in the control group will be matched 1:1 to older adults in the intervention group by the propensity score using the greedy matching algorithm. The propensity scores will be derived from a logistic regression model that includes the following covariates: age, sex, education level, smoking, drinking, level of facility, insurance, region, number of Charlson comorbidities and medications. Region and level of facility refer to the facility characteristics in the present study.

To assess the economic impact of the intervention, a detailed accounting of incurred costs will be performed to identify components of direct healthcare expenditure. Cost-effectiveness will be evaluated using the incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER), defined as the ratio of the mean cost difference between the intervention and control groups to the difference in their mean effects [35]. Sensitivity analyses will be conducted to address uncertainties in cost and effect estimates. For cost-utility analysis, the ICER represents the average incremental cost per QALY gained, thereby addressing the key policy question: how much additional health benefit is achieved per additional unit of cost compared with usual care. If the ICER is negative, the intervention is cost-saving while improving health outcomes. If the ICER is less than the country’s gross domestic product (GDP) per capita, the intervention is considered highly cost-effective. An ICER below three times the GDP per capita is typically regarded as cost-effective [30, 36]. This threshold-based approach is widely applied in economic evaluations globally [31, 37, 38].

Dissemination plans

The findings of the study will be published in peer-reviewed scientifc journals. We will present the findings at national and international conferences and use it for education in primary health care services. The results will be disseminated regardless of the direction or magnitude of the effects.

Discussion

This study will evaluate the effectiveness and cost-effectiveness of a person-centred integrated intervention within a resource-constrained primary healthcare system typical of low- and middle-income countries. The intervention was designed by optimising existing primary care resources and implementing WHO-recommended ICOPE pathways to ensure efficient resource allocation. Findings will offer valuable insights for policymakers in developing targeted intervention strategies for ageing populations in China, with direct relevance for scaling up multi-centre implementation and informing global public health policy.

This study will be conducted as a randomised controlled trial guided by ICOPE principles and will selectively adapt and localise intervention components for integration into real-world practice. Specifically, it will incorporate culturally tailored health education interventions based on traditional Chinese medicine, including acupuncture, tuina, taijiquan, baduanjin and yoga programmes [39, 40]. Health services grounded in traditional Chinese practices—such as Chinese diet, therapeutic diets, lifestyle routines, emotional regulation and exercise—are increasingly popular among community-dwelling older adults [41]. This culturally contextualised approach offers a practical framework for implementing ICOPE in low- and middle-income countries, particularly within primary care settings. The results are expected to underscore the feasibility of developing innovative and locally adapted models to strengthen system readiness for delivering integrated care to older populations with limited resources.

Although efforts to prevent IC decline have long been part of primary care practice in China, challenges remain in effectively integrating resources [42, 43]. Globally, the preservation of IC is critical for transforming clinical practice and promoting healthy ageing, as IC decline is a strong predictor of adverse health outcomes such as disability and mortality [5, 44, 45]. The ICOPE-integrated care model has shown promise in preventing, delaying, and even reversing IC decline among community-dwelling older adults and has been successfully piloted in several high-income countries. As one of the WHO’s initial seven global pilot countries for ICOPE implementation, China is well positioned to embed this model into local primary care systems and advance its nationwide dissemination. Accordingly, evaluating the economic benefits of this intervention is of substantial importance.

A key strength of this study lies in the research team’s access to a large collaborative network of CHCs across Beijing, which is expected to foster trust and active engagement among participants. However, this study will also face some challenges. First, the integrated care intervention program covers multiple health aspects, requiring primary healthcare workers to have advanced knowledge and skills. However, inconsistent training in some community health service centers may limit the effectiveness of the program. To address this, we plan to develop a comprehensive training program, offer expert-led courses, and regularly assess the performance of multidisciplinary team members [46].Second, the lack of blinding among participants and care providers may introduce bias, particularly as both groups will be involved in health education interventions, making complete masking infeasible. However, outcome assessors and data analysts will be blinded to group allocation, thereby minimising the risk of measurement and analytical bias.

In conclusion, this study adopts a community-based cluster randomised controlled trial design to evaluate the effectiveness and cost-effectiveness of an integrated care intervention compared with existing models of care for community-dwelling older adults in China with IC decline. The trial will be conducted in real-world primary care settings, offering robust evidence for the broader applicability of the intervention. The findings are expected to inform health policymakers in developing countries on how to operationalise national guidelines for healthy aging. Ultimately, we anticipate that the ICOPE framework will serve as a transformative approach to enhancing geriatric care, advancing implementation strategies, and supporting nursing practice and policy for the promotion of healthy ageing.
