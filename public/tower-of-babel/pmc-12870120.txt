Global burden of hemolytic disease and neonatal jaundice from 1990 to 2021 with projections to 2050: a systematic analysis of the GBD 2021 data
Authors: Kaihu Huang, Bo Gao, Lijiao Zhang, Manhua Xiao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNeonatal hemolytic disease and jaundice represent a persistent global health challenge, accounting for substantial avoidable mortality and long-term neurodevelopmental impairment among infants worldwide, with inequitable impacts across resource regions. This study aimed to evaluate the global, regional, and national burden of hemolytic disease and neonatal jaundice from 1990 to 2021, and project future trends up to 2050.MethodsData on prevalence, disability-adjusted life years (DALYs), and risk factors were drawn from the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) 2021 study. Socio-demographic disparities were assessed via the socio-demographic index (SDI). Temporal trends were examined, inequalities were quantified by the slope index of inequality and concentration index, and future burden was projected to 2050.ResultsGlobally, the ASPR of neonatal jaundice increased by 41.5% between 1990 and 2021, while ASDR decreased by 56.8%. DALYs declined overall, but absolute case numbers rose due to population growth and improved survival. The burden was consistently higher in males than females and disproportionately concentrated in low and middle SDI regions. Key risk factors included low birthweight, preterm birth, and air pollution. Projections suggest that prevalence will continue to rise through 2050, while mortality is expected to decline further.ConclusionsA global trend of increasing ASPR but decreasing ASDR from neonatal jaundice-related conditions was found. However, low SDI regions still have a disproportionate burden, underscoring the urgent need for targeted perinatal interventions and mitigation of environmental risks.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12887-025-06367-0.Keywords: Neonatal, Hemolytic, Risk factor, Global burden of disease study 2021, Socio-demographic index, Health inequalities, Forecasting, Disability-Adjusted life years

Introduction

Neonatal hemolytic disease and other jaundice remain major causes of neonatal morbidity and mortality worldwide, especially in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). Neonatal jaundice, a frequent condition in newborns caused by abnormal bilirubin metabolism, can progress to acute bilirubin encephalopathy or kernicterus in severe cases, resulting in irreversible neurological injury or death [1–3].

In LMICs, neonatal jaundice frequently goes undetected due to inadequate healthcare infrastructure and insufficient screening capacity, especially at the primary care level [4]. While most cases are self-limiting, delays in diagnosis and treatment substantially increase the risk of bilirubin-induced neurological injury [5, 6]. This contributes significantly to both under-five mortality and neurodevelopmental delay [7]. Such complications contribute significantly to under-five mortality and long-term neurodevelopmental impairment, posing a major barrier to achieving the Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) target of reducing neonatal mortality to at least as low as 12 per 1,000 live births and under-five mortality to at least as low as 25 per 1,000 live births [8].

These clinical challenges are compounded by pronounced regional disparities in disease burden. South Asia and sub-Saharan Africa record the highest prevalence and mortality, driven by a high frequency of Rh incompatibility, widespread glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) deficiency, and elevated rates of preterm birth [9–11]. Although global jaundice-related mortality has declined by more than half since 2000, LMICs have achieved only a modest reduction, reflecting persistent inequities in healthcare access and quality [12]. The lack of basic phototherapy equipment and trained personnel in rural areas further compounds these disparities [13]. The shortage of phototherapy equipment and trained personnel in rural areas exacerbates these inequalities, as documented in recent digital screening and computer vision studies for non-invasive jaundice detection.

Despite its global significance, important evidence gaps remain. Most studies focus primarily on mortality data, with limited estimates of prevalence and disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) [1]. Neonatal jaundice causes considerable non-fatal health loss through neurodevelopmental impairment, which mortality indicators alone cannot reflect [14]. Furthermore, existing meta-analyses often exclude data from LMICs, leading to a systematic underestimation of disease burden [15]. Few studies have explored sex-specific differences, regional inequality patterns, or projected future trajectories of neonatal jaundice. These gaps hinder the formulation of equitable and forward-looking neonatal health strategies.

This study advances the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) framework through a unified analytical approach that integrates temporal trend assessment, health inequality measurement, and long-term burden projection. By employing complementary statistical models, we characterize multidimensional disease dynamics, elucidate underlying socioeconomic determinants, and forecast future epidemiological trajectories. By adopting a multidimensional and methodologically rigorous approach, this study offers evidence-based insights to inform global neonatal disease prevention strategies and policy development.

Materials and methods

Data source

Health loss estimates were derived from the GBD 2021, led by the Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluation (IHME), which represents the most comprehensive and standardized global assessment of disease burden to date [16]. The GBD framework systematically quantified the burden of 371 diseases and injuries, as well as 88 risk factors, across 204 countries and territories from 1990 to 2021. Data on prevalence and DALYs rates related to neonatal jaundice were extracted from the publicly available GBD 2021 database. All data underwent rigorous quality control and standardization, including harmonized case definitions, adjustments for underreporting, and age standardization (Supplementary Appendix 1). As only anonymized, publicly available data were used, ethical approval was not required [17].

In the GBD 2021 cause hierarchy, “hemolytic disease and other neonatal jaundice” are classified as Level 3 causes under the parent category of neonatal disorders. Specifically, hemolytic disease of the newborn encompasses jaundice secondary to ABO and Rh incompatibility, G6PD deficiency, and other immune-mediated hemolytic mechanisms. Other neonatal jaundice refers to non-hemolytic hyperbilirubinemia due to prematurity, hepatic immaturity, infection, or metabolic dysfunction. Outcomes such as bilirubin encephalopathy and kernicterus are not coded as separate diseases but are incorporated into the DALYs estimates for these underlying conditions, consistent with GBD 2021 methodology. All definitions and mappings followed the standardized GBD 2021 cause list curated by the IHME.

Socio-demographic index

We employed the socio-demographic index (SDI), a composite measure reflecting a population’s average educational attainment, income per capita, and total fertility rate, to gauge socio-economic development levels [18]. SDI values range from 0 (lowest development) to 1 (highest development). Countries and territories were stratified into five SDI categories: low, low-middle, middle, high-middle, and high. Stratified analyses by SDI level assessed disparities in disease burden and explored associations between socio-economic development and disease distribution.

Joinpoint regression analysis

Temporal trends in disease burden for hemolytic disease and other neonatal jaundice from 1990 to 2021 were assessed using Joinpoint regression (Version 5.1.0, Statistical Research and Applications Branch, National Cancer Institute, USA) [19]. This method employs Monte Carlo permutation tests to objectively identify significant inflection points in time series data, offering enhanced sensitivity in detecting subtle epidemiological shifts over extended periods compared to conventional linear or polynomial models. Sex-stratified analyses were conducted to estimate annual percentage changes (APCs) and average annual percentage changes (AAPCs), where APCs describe trends within individual segments and AAPCs summarize long-term patterns. The Joinpoint framework treats time as the sole explanatory variable; therefore, the resulting APC and AAPC values represent unadjusted temporal trends.

Inequality assessment

To quantify socioeconomic inequalities in disease burden for hemolytic disease and other neonatal jaundice, absolute and relative inequality indices were applied. The slope index of inequality (SII) was derived from weighted least squares regression, with population size as weights, modeling the relationship between age-standardized rates and the cumulative relative rank of each country based on its SDI. Countries were ranked from the most disadvantaged (rank 0) to the most advantaged (rank 1) according to their SDI values, with the ranking weighted to reflect the proportional population distribution within each country. The concentration index (CI) was calculated using the concentration curve approach, employing fractional ranks based on SDI and applying population weights to correct for small-country bias. The SII represents the absolute difference in disease burden between the extremes of the socioeconomic spectrum, while the CI quantifies the degree of relative inequality across the distribution [20]. These WHO-recommended indices have been validated in previous GBD-based health inequality research.

Risk factors

Low birth weight and short gestation, defined relative to the theoretical minimum-risk exposure levels (TMRELs) for birth weight (3500–4500 g) and gestational age (38–42 weeks), respectively. Household air pollution was specifically attributed to exposure to PM2.5 resulting from the combustion of solid fuels like wood, coal, or dung in domestic settings. The assessment of ambient particulate matter pollution was based on the population-weighted annual average PM2.5 concentration, with the TMREL threshold set between 2.4 and 5.9 micrograms per cubic meter. Risk factor contributions to DALYs were also decomposed by sex to capture differential vulnerability to low birthweight, short gestation, and environmental exposures.

Future projections

Future burden trends from 2022 to 2050 were projected using a Bayesian age-period-cohort (BAPC) model implemented with the Integrated Nested Laplace Approximation (INLA) approach. The model was fitted to historical data from 1990 to 2021, including counts of prevalent cases and disability-adjusted life years (DALYs), as well as corresponding age-standardized prevalence (ASPR) and DALY rates (ASDR). The BAPC framework incorporates population aging, cohort effects, and temporal dynamics, with posterior estimates derived via Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) sampling [21, 22]. Projections were based on the following core assumptions: a gradual demographic transition consistent with United Nations population forecasts, moderate improvements in neonatal care coverage, and stability in key socioeconomic covariates. Major exogenous disruptions such as pandemics or large-scale conflicts were not incorporated, in line with standard BAPC-based burden forecasting practices, to ensure interpretable extrapolation under baseline epidemiological conditions. Within the INLA framework, random walk priors were used to smooth age, period, and cohort parameters, with default log-Gamma priors specified for hyperparameters. Sex-specific projections were generated by fitting separate models while preserving consistent temporal structures. All results are reported with 95% credible intervals (CrI), defined by the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the posterior distribution.

Statistical analysis

The estimated annual percentage change (EAPC) was used to characterize temporal patterns in ASR of hemolytic disease and other neonatal jaundice [23]. EAPCs and their 95% CIs were derived from log-linear regression models. An increasing trend was identified when both the EAPC and the lower CI exceeded zero, whereas a decreasing trend was inferred when both were below zero. Stable trends were assigned when CIs spanned zero. All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.4.1).

Results

Global burden of hemolytic disease and other neonatal jaundice

In 2021, a total of 1,995,135 (1,831,120 to 2,197,792) prevalent cases of hemolytic disease and other neonatal jaundice were reported globally in neonates, comprising 1,008,160 (924,773 to 1,111,701) males and 986,975 (905,565 to 1,087,179) females. The corresponding number of DALYs was 3,754,621 (3,009,225 to 4,575,408), including 2,132,112 (1,697,419 to 2,734,658) in males and 1,622,509 (1,297,289 to 2,035,442) in females. The burden remained consistently higher in males than in females. From 1990 to 2021, the ASPR of hemolytic disease and other neonatal jaundice demonstrated a significant upward trend (EAPC: 1.35; 95% CI: 1.32 to 1.38), whereas ASDR declined markedly (EAPC: −3.06; 95% CI: −3.15 to −2.96) (Supplementary Table S1).

Among the 5 SDI regions, the low-middle SDI regions exhibited the highest burden of hemolytic disease and other neonatal jaundice in 2021, with 714,026 (656,008 to 785,561) prevalent cases and 1,755,405 (1,345,905 to 2,196,606) DALYs cases, while the high SDI regions had the lowest burden, reporting 59,002 (53,526 to 66,360) prevalent cases and 26,318 (20,065 to 33,609) DALYs cases. Across the 7 GBD super-regions, South Asia reported the greatest burden of hemolytic disease and other neonatal jaundice, with 711,589 (651,861 to 784,438) prevalent cases and 2,180,054 (1,660,180 to 2,750,759) DALYs cases. Conversely, the Commonwealth of Dominica experienced the lowest burden. From 1990 to 2021, most regions exhibited rising ASPR trends, except for the Commonwealth of Dominica, where ASPR declined significantly (EAPC: −0.11; 95% CI: −0.12 to −0.11).

Among the 21 GBD regions, South Asia, Southeast Asia, and East Asia had substantial disease burden for hemolytic disease and other neonatal jaundice, while Australasia had the lowest in 2021, with 585 (95% UI: 521–662) prevalent cases and 243 (95% UI: 181–320) DALYs cases. Declining ASPR trends from 1990 to 2021 were observed in Central Asia (EAPC: −0.06; 95% CI: −0.07 to −0.05), Central Europe (EAPC: −0.46; 95% CI: −0.51 to −0.41), and Andean Latin America (EAPC: −0.04; 95% CI: −0.05 to −0.04). Notably, ASPR in 1990 exceeded 2021 levels in regions such as Central Asia and Andean Latin America, suggesting a declining burden of hemolytic disease and other neonatal jaundice. In contrast, ASPR trends showed increasing levels in Tropical Latin America, North Africa, the Middle East, and South Asia. ASDR declined across all 21 GBD regions when comparing 2021 with 1990 (Fig. 1).

Regional trends in the burden of hemolytic disease and other neonatal jaundice, 1990–2021

In 2021, among 204 countries and territories, the highest ASPR for hemolytic disease and other neonatal jaundice was recorded in Albania, North Macedonia, Afghanistan, and Libya, whereas the lowest was in Australia. The highest ASDR was observed in Pakistan, India, and South Sudan, and the lowest in Australia, Andorra, and Sweden. From 1990 to 2021, ASPR for hemolytic disease and other neonatal jaundice increased significantly in Ethiopia and Nigeria, but declined in Latvia, Montenegro, and Romania. ASDR increased only in Zimbabwe and South Sudan, while decreasing elsewhere, particularly in Thailand and Iran (Fig. 2).

Joinpoint regression

Temporal trend modeling from 1990 to 2021 revealed a significant increase in the ASPR of hemolytic disease and other neonatal jaundice in both sexes. The AAPC was 1.25 (95% CI: 1.20–1.29) overall, with comparable values for females (1.24; 95% CI: 1.20–1.28) and males (1.26; 95% CI: 1.21–1.31) (Supplementary Table S2). The most pronounced increases were noted between 2010 and 2015 (APC: 1.61 in both sexes; 1.63 in females). In contrast, the ASDR demonstrated a consistent downward trend, with an overall AAPC of −3.05 (95% CI: −3.13 to −2.98), −3.00 (95% CI: −3.08 to −2.92) in females, and − 3.10 (95% CI: −3.19 to −3.02) in males. Notably, the sharpest reductions were observed from 2012 to 2021 in both sexes (APC: −4.12), from 2019 to 2021 in females (APC: −5.36), and from 2012 to 2018 in males (APC: −4.15) (Supplementary Fig S1).

Rising ASPR trends for hemolytic disease and other neonatal jaundice were also detected across all five SDI regions (Supplementary Table S3). At the global level, ASPR increased significantly during 1994–2010 (APC: 1.40) and 2010–2015 (APC: 1.61). In the low-middle SDI regions, consecutive increases were recorded over 1995–2010 (APC: 1.43), 2010–2015 (APC: 1.78), and 2015–2021 (APC: 0.80). A notable exception was observed in the high-middle SDI regions, where ASPR declined during 1990–1996 (APC: −0.21). ASDR consistently declined across all regions, with a sharp global decrease between 2012 and 2021 (APC: −4.11), and the steepest regional reduction observed in the high-middle SDI regions during 2002–2007 (APC: −6.59) (Supplementary Fig S2).

SDI trend analysis

A significant inverse relationship was identified between SDI levels and the burden of hemolytic disease and other neonatal jaundice from 1990 to 2021. Spearman correlation coefficients indicated negative associations for ASPR (ρ = −0.27) and ASDR (ρ = −0.81), suggesting that burden declined as socioeconomic development improved. Observed burdens in South Asia and Eastern Europe exceeded SDI-based expectations, while Australasia, Central and Western Sub-Saharan Africa, and East Asia exhibited lower-than-expected values (Fig. 3).

Health inequality

Assessment of health inequality from 1990 to 2021 revealed a widening absolute burden gap between high and low SDI regions. The SII for prevalence decreased from − 9.06 in 1990 to −12.62 in 2021, while the SII for DALYs rates declined from − 44.32 to −134.14, underscoring the growing concentration of burden in less developed regions. The CI for prevalence dropped from − 0.22 to −0.27, reflecting intensified relative inequality, whereas the CI for DALYs showed a modest increase from − 0.45 to −0.44, indicating a marginal reduction in relative disparity (Fig. 4).

Risk factors

Low birthweight and short gestation remained the primary contributors to DALYs attributable to hemolytic disease and other neonatal jaundice. Globally, low birthweight accounted for 46.48% of female DALYs and 47.73% of male DALYs, while short gestation contributed 21.99% in females and 23.06% in males. In the low-middle SDI regions, the highest attributable proportions were noted for low birthweight (males: 54.5%; females: 52.31%) and short gestation (males: 26.01%). Additionally, ambient particulate matter pollution had a greater impact than household air pollution in the middle SDI regions. Among the 21 GBD regions, South Asia reported the highest DALYs shares attributable to low birth weight (males: 59.94%; females: 57.63%) and short gestation (males: 31.22%; females: 28.76%). In contrast, household air pollution contributed no measurable burden in Western Europe and Australasia (Fig. 5).

Future projections

Projections generated using the BAPC model indicated rising trends in both the number of prevalent and ASPR through 2050. The global prevalent cases are expected to rise from 2,076,243.64 in 2022 to 2,889,809.36 by 2050, with ASPR increasing from 25.81 per 100,000 (95% CrI: 25.49–26.12) to 30.26 per 100,000 (95% CrI: 10.61–49.90). Although the number of DALYs is projected to rise from 3,672,849.82 to 7,339,104.1, ASDR is anticipated to decline substantially from 45.65 (95% CrI: 39.88–51.42) to 7.68 (95% CrI: −0.32 to 15.69). Sex-specific forecasts suggested that male neonates will continue to have a greater burden than females. Among males, ASDR is expected to decline from 50.48 (95% CrI: 43.63 to 57.33) to 8.27 (95% CrI: −0.82 to 17.36), whereas a reduction from 40.66 (95% CrI: 35.77 to 45.54) to 7.34 (95% CrI: 0.12 to 14.56) is projected in females (Fig. 6).

Discussion

This study advances the epidemiological understanding of neonatal hemolytic disease and jaundice through three major contributions. First, the BAPC projections indicate a 17.2% increase in the global ASPR by 2050, highlighting a paradoxical pattern of rising prevalence but declining mortality. Second, systematic quantification of socioeconomic disparities reveals a progressively widening burden gap between high and low SDI regions from 1990 to 2021. Third, the study identifies distinct sex disparity patterns, with male predominance most evident in South Asia and sub-Saharan Africa.

Between 1990 and 2021, the global ASPR for neonatal jaundice increased steadily, whereas the ASDR decreased markedly. This indicates reduced lethality but an expanding affected population. The trend is consistent with findings from GBD 2019 and reflects advances in perinatal diagnostics, referral systems, and the effectiveness of interventions such as phototherapy and exchange transfusion [12]. The decline in mortality primarily reflects improvements in perinatal diagnostics, referral systems, and interventions such as phototherapy and exchange transfusion. Two key factors underpin this divergence. First, global expansion of neonatal care, including phototherapy scale-up and improved management of preterm births, has markedly enhanced survival among affected neonates, especially in middle SDI regions [24]. Second, improved diagnostic capacity and broader bilirubin screening have increased detection of mild and moderate cases, particularly in high SDI regions [25]. While refinements in GBD modeling and changes in ICD coding may partly explain the observed increase in prevalence, these represent secondary effects compared with genuine clinical progress and improved survival [26]. This epidemiological transition signifies major public health success in reducing mortality but also introduces a new challenge: health systems, particularly in resource-limited regions, must now provide long-term neurodevelopmental follow-up and rehabilitation for an expanding population of survivors [4].

A strong inverse correlation was observed between SDI and both ASPR (ρ = −0.27) and ASDR (ρ = −0.81), confirming a structural inequality in disease burden. The SII declined from 1990 to 2021, suggesting that the disease burden for neonatal haemolytic disease and other jaundices is becoming increasingly concentrated in poorer countries [27]. This may reflect a “detection-intervention gap”, although diagnostic coverage has expanded, access to critical therapies, such as exchange transfusion and neonatal intensive care, remains severely limited [28]. In Ethiopia, for example, most primary health centers can test for bilirubin; yet, only a small fraction are equipped to perform exchange transfusions, leading to frequent referral delays [29, 30].

The assessment of health inequality from 1990 to 2021 revealed a progressively widening absolute burden gap between high and low SDI regions. These robust metrics quantifying the growing disparity signal an urgent need for targeted health system policies aligned with international guidelines. WHO and UNICEF jointly advocate scaling up low-cost phototherapy, universal bilirubin screening, and routine G6PD testing in high-risk regions, evidence-based interventions that directly address the inequities quantified by our SII and CI analyses [31, 32]. Strategic implementation of these measures, particularly through the scaling-up of basic phototherapy equipment, prioritized G6PD screening in endemic areas such as South Asia and sub-Saharan Africa, and strengthened Rh immunoprophylaxis programs, would substantially narrow the absolute and relative gaps in neonatal jaundice burden between socioeconomic regions. The effectiveness of such targeted interventions is supported by recent implementation studies demonstrating that coordinated scale-up of phototherapy and G6PD screening can reduce severe jaundice cases in resource-limited regions.

The analysis also identified consistent sex disparities, with males bearing a higher burden of neonatal jaundice. This is partly attributable to the X-linked nature of G6PD deficiency, which affects approximately 4.9% of males and only 0.7% of females globally [33]. G6PD deficiency predisposes to oxidative hemolysis, contributing to hemolytic jaundice. Social and cultural factors further exacerbate this disparity. In northern India, male infants were reportedly 2.3 times more likely to receive care for jaundice than females, reflecting entrenched gender biases that disadvantage female newborns [34]. These findings underscore the need for a dual-pronged public health strategy. In regions with high G6PD prevalence, such as sub-Saharan Africa and the Mediterranean, screening programs should account for the heightened biological susceptibility of male neonates. Concurrently, and critically, targeted interventions, such as community education and gender-neutral care-seeking incentives, are imperative to counteract the identified gender biases and ensure equitable healthcare access for vulnerable female infants.

Low birth weight and preterm delivery were identified as the leading risk factors, contributing approximately 50% and 25% of global DALYs, respectively, in alignment with previous research [12, 35]. These factors can be mitigated through improved prenatal nutrition, infection prevention, and antenatal care [36, 37]. In middle SDI countries, environmental exposures, particularly to particulate matter, played a significant role. Prenatal exposure to PM2.5 has been shown to trigger oxidative stress and systemic inflammation in pregnant women, leading to alterations in placental transport and fetal hepatic development. Studies in regions such as Taiwan have demonstrated that prenatal exposure to PM2.5 and carbon monoxide is associated with elevated neonatal bilirubin levels [38, 39]. These converging lines of evidence support a mechanistic link between maternal air pollution exposure and disrupted bilirubin metabolism, underscoring the need for integrated maternal–child health and environmental policies in high-burden regions.

Projections to 2050 suggest that the number of prevalent cases will rise to 2.89 million, representing a 39% increase from 2022, primarily driven by population growth in high-burden regions such as South Asia and sub-Saharan Africa. However, ASDR is projected to decline by 83%, from 45.65 per 100,000 in 2022 to 7.68 per 100,000 in 2050. These projections highlight the potential for scalable public health interventions to reduce mortality, even as absolute caseloads rise. BAPC modeling revealed a nonlinear trajectory, with increasing prevalence but declining fatality, suggesting that neonatal jaundice is transitioning into a manageable but persistent health issue. While enhanced perinatal investment may reduce mortality, without addressing structural drivers such as environmental hazards and under-resourced health systems, the overall disease burden may continue to grow in absolute terms [36]. In resource-limited settings, this portends a rise in non-fatal, chronic burdens that will challenge future health systems.

Continued reliance on exchange transfusion in resource-poor regions underscores the need for expanded phototherapy infrastructure. Evidence from Myanmar and Nigeria demonstrates the effectiveness of regional phototherapy deployment, frontline health worker training, and community-based early-warning systems in reducing severe complications. The strategic integration of IoT-enabled connected health solutions, such as wireless bilirubin sensors and smart phototherapy units, can provide the foundational data capture and remote management capabilities necessary to effectively implement and scale these evidence-based interventions at the primary care level [40].

Achieving the SDG 3.2 target to end preventable neonatal deaths by 2030 requires scaling equitable neonatal screening, phototherapy, and G6PD testing. The observed inequality patterns emphasize that technological advancement alone is insufficient without parallel investment in system-level equity.

These projections highlight not only epidemiologic trends but also the policy urgency for global coordination in neonatal health, particularly in low- and middle-income regions. Informed by these findings, several strategic directions appear warranted. A tiered resource allocation strategy could prioritize scaling up basic phototherapy equipment in LMICs, as cost-effectiveness analyses from other studies have demonstrated that each additional 100 devices could prevent approximately 38 cases of kernicterus [41]. Fostering regional collaborative networks, perhaps modeled after successful Southeast Asian initiatives, could enhance resource sharing and streamline referral pathways for high-risk neonates. The integration of environmental health is particularly crucial in East Asia, suggesting a need for coordinated policies that link air pollution control with maternal-child health programs. Finally, community-level interventions focusing on training female health volunteers may help improve healthcare access for vulnerable populations, particularly female infants who face cultural barriers to care [42].

Several limitations warrant consideration. As with all GBD-based studies, our estimates rely on modeled data where primary inputs are scarce, particularly in low-income regions. This may lead to under- or overestimation of prevalence and mortality due to data sparsity or misclassification between hemolytic and non-hemolytic etiologies [43]. Second, while the GBD 2021 dataset represented the most complete and methodologically consistent iteration available at study initiation, subsequent releases (e.g., GBD 2023) may offer updated estimates and refinements. Third, the comparative risk assessment assumes consistent exposure-response relationships across populations. This potentially obscures region-specific variations, so the attributable risk factors should be interpreted as global averages rather than precise estimates for any single population. Additionally, the Joinpoint approach used in this study cannot adjust for covariates, so the detected breakpoints reflect unadjusted time trends. Future work could apply piecewise Poisson, mixed-effects segmented, or Bayesian change-point models to jointly estimate trend shifts and the effects of socioeconomic or environmental factors. Finally, uncertainties inherent in BAPC projections should be interpreted with caution, given the potential propagation of prior distributional assumptions. Although our BAPC projections incorporate statistical uncertainty, real-world dynamics such as climate change influencing preterm birth rates, population displacement due to conflict, and expanding use of digital diagnostics could substantially affect future burden [44]. Therefore, our projections should be viewed as indicative trends under current assumptions rather than deterministic forecasts. Future research should explore the interaction of genetic susceptibility and environmental exposures through molecular epidemiology; employ artificial intelligence to refine disease forecasting models; and establish long-term follow-up cohorts to assess neurodevelopmental outcomes in survivors of neonatal jaundice [45].

Conclusions

The burden of Neonatal jaundice and neonatal jaundice remains concentrated in low- and middle-SDI regions, reflecting persistent inequities in neonatal care. Although mortality has declined markedly, the widening prevalence gap underscores that progress remains uneven across socioeconomic strata. Low birthweight and preterm birth continue to dominate global DALYs, while environmental exposures, particularly particulate matter pollution, pose emerging challenges in urbanizing regions.

Global action must now move beyond observation to implementation. Achieving the SDG 3.2 target of ending preventable neonatal deaths by 2030 demands equitable access to screening, phototherapy, and G6PD testing across all regions. Integration of IoT-enabled monitoring, maternal-child health investment, and clean-air policies could transform outcomes for millions of newborns. Collaboration among governments, the WHO, and UNICEF is urgently needed to ensure that every child, regardless of birthplace, has the opportunity for a healthy start in life.
