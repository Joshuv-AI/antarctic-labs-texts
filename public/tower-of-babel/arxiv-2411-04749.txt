Berry phase in axion physics, SM global structure, and generalized symmetries
Qing-Hong Cao, Shuailiang Ge, Yandong Liu, Jun-Chen Wang

Abstract
We investigate the Berry phase arising from axion-photon and axion-fermion interactions. The effective Hamiltonians in both systems share the same form, enabling a unified description of the Berry phase and providing a novel perspective on axion experiments. We conceptually propose a new photon-ring experiment for axion detection. Furthermore, we demonstrate that measuring the axion-induced Berry phase offers a unique method for probing the global structure of the Standard Model gauge group and axion-related generalized symmetries.

Implications for SM global structure and generalized symmetries
–
In conventional experiments of axion detections, the experimental observables are typically proportional to
g_{\gamma}/f_{a}
. The signal strength of axion events is suppressed by
f_{a}
, and it is challenging to disentangle
g_{\gamma}
from
f_{a}
.
The Berry phase in Eq. (
12
) depends only on
g_{\gamma}
and provides a unique method to measure
g_{\gamma}
.
The key reason lies in the fact that at the core of a domain wall, the axion field satisfies
a/f_{a}\sim\pi/N_{\rm DW}
, ensuring that the Berry phase generated by particles crossing an axion wall (or more generally, a closed-loop structure) depends solely on the coupling
g_{\gamma}
. Consequently, this Berry phase serves as a powerful tool for probing non-perturbative aspects of axion physics, including the global structure of the Standard Model (SM) gauge group.
Theoretically,

is predicted as
[
35
]

g_{\gamma}=\frac{\alpha_{\rm EM}}{\pi}\left(E-1.92N\right),

(14)

originate from the axion-photon and axion-gluon couplings, respectively.
Regardless of many UV models, couplings

and

are quantized due to the axion periodicity

[
24
,
22
,
70
,
3
]

, with

[

Additional symmetry structures will further constrain the quantization of

Axion physics is rich in generalized symmetries, including the higher-group symmetry arising from the mix of axion winding 2-form symmetry and electric 1-form symmetry, and the non-invertible 1-form symmetry as the axion-modified SM center 1-form symmetry consistent with axion periodicity. As a concluding remark for this section, we comment on the feasibility and the principal challenges of the proposed photon-ring experiment. First, several aspects of current optical technology suggest that such an experiment is in principle viable. Polarization-maintaining optical fibers can preserve linear polarization over long distances and strongly suppress unwanted birefringence originating from temperature gradients or mechanical stress
[
60
,
36
,
23
]
. This ensures that any observed polarization rotation predominantly reflects the axion-induced signal rather than Standard Model birefringence or material imperfections. Modern polarization-maintaining fibers have therefore become suitable platforms for precision phase measurements.

In addition, the optical medium characterized by the tensor in Eq. (
22
) behaves as a controllable birefringent material. Systems such as crystalline media or stressed fibers naturally support distinct refractive indices for the two orthogonal polarization modes
[
21
,
29
,
44
]
. The birefringence parameter
2\chi
can be continuously tuned by external pressure, strain, or temperature control
[
17
,
65
]
. Experimental studies have demonstrated that
\chi
can be stabilized and adjusted at the level of
10^{-9}
–
10^{-5}

[
74
]
, which comfortably covers the parameter region relevant for the photon-ring configuration and enables enhancement of the dynamical phase.

Furthermore, photon attenuation along long optical paths can be compensated using solid-state or doped-fiber optical amplifiers
[
31
,
7
]
. Such amplifiers restore the photon intensity while leaving the polarization state largely unaffected, making it possible to extend the photon propagation time without significantly degrading coherence. Together with modern low-loss fibers, this allows effective optical lengths sufficient to accumulate a measurable phase shift
[
52
,
48
,
13
,
27
]
.

Despite these favorable features, several experimental challenges remain. A central difficulty is achieving long coherent propagation times in the optical ring so that the dynamical phase can accumulate to an observable level. From Eq. (
25
), the phase scales linearly with the propagation time
t_{\rm exp}
(or the effective optical length
L_{\rm eff}=t_{\rm exp}/n
). Although optical amplifiers and low-loss fibers can extend
L_{\rm eff}
, maintaining polarization coherence over such distances requires careful characterization and suppression of noise, dispersion, and other decoherence mechanisms. Determining the maximal achievable coherent propagation time therefore represents one of the key practical challenges of the setup.

Another major challenge is satisfying the resonance condition with sufficient accuracy, as this condition suppresses Standard Model contributions while maximizing the axion-induced dynamical phase. In practice, mechanical vibrations, temperature drifts, and slow environmental fluctuations can shift the resonance away from its ideal value. Achieving stable operation thus requires active stabilization, such as precise temperature control, mechanical isolation, and laser-frequency locking, to ensure that relevant optical parameters remain within the narrow tolerance required by the resonance. Assessing the performance of such stabilization techniques is essential for determining the realistic sensitivity of the photon-ring experiment.

Conclusion
–
Due to axion being a pseudoscalar and Nambu-Goldstone boson, the Berry phase naturally arises in axion physics. We present a unified description of the Berry phase in axion-photon and axion-fermion systems, as they share the same effective Hamiltonian form in the low-energy limit.
We study the Berry phase in two scenarios: one in which photons and fermions experience a closed loop of the axion field and the other in which the motion of photons and fermions in the axion background forms a closed loop in the momentum space.

A nontrivial Berry phase can be derived from axion periodicity. Detecting this Berry phase enables a direct measurement of the dimensionless axion coupling
g_{\gamma}
without the suppression from
f_{a}
, which is a portal for probing the SM global structure and axion-related generalized symmetries.
Additionally, our study of the Berry phase offers new insights into axion experiments, such as photon-birefringence and storage-ring experiments.
Inspired by the unified description of axion-photon and axion-fermion systems, we conceptually proposed a photon-ring experiment, the details of which will be left in our future work
[
18
]
.

Appendix A
Derivation of the axion-photon effective Hamiltonian

We derive the effective Hamiltonian governing axion-photon interactions by starting with the standard photon kinetic term,
-\frac{1}{4}F^{\mu\nu}F_{\mu\nu}
, and the axion-photon coupling term,
\frac{g_{\gamma}}{4f_{a}}\,a\,F^{\mu\nu}\tilde{F}_{\mu\nu}
, where
a
is the axion field,
f_{a}
is the axion decay constant, and
g_{\gamma}
is the coupling constant. Combining these terms modifies Maxwell’s equations in the presence of an axion field, as derived in Refs.
[
76
,
77
]
:

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot\left(\bm{E}-\frac{g_{\gamma}}{f_{a}}\,a\,\bm{B}\right)

\displaystyle=0,

(26)

\displaystyle\nabla\times\left(\bm{B}+\frac{g_{\gamma}}{f_{a}}\,a\,\bm{E}\right)

\displaystyle=\partial_{t}\left(\bm{E}-\frac{g_{\gamma}}{f_{a}}\,a\,\bm{B}\right),

(27)

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot\bm{B}

\displaystyle=0,

(28)

\displaystyle\nabla\times\bm{E}+\partial_{t}\bm{B}

\displaystyle=0.

(29)

To proceed, we adopt a plane-wave ansatz for the electromagnetic fields:

\displaystyle\bm{E}(t,\bm{x})

\displaystyle=\bm{E}_{0}(t)\,e^{-i\omega t+i\bm{k}\cdot\bm{x}},

(30)

\displaystyle\bm{B}(t,\bm{x})

\displaystyle=\bm{B}_{0}(t)\,e^{-i\omega t+i\bm{k}\cdot\bm{x}},

(31)

where
\bm{k}
is the wave vector and
\omega=|\bm{k}|
is the angular frequency.

We assume the geometric-optics regime, in which the axion field
a
varies slowly compared to the electromagnetic oscillation scale. Consequently, second derivatives of
a
are negligible
[
39
,
79
]
. Taking the time derivative of Eq. (
81
) and substituting Eq. (
83
), we obtain:

\partial_{t}^{2}\bm{E}+\frac{g_{\gamma}}{f_{a}}\,\partial_{t}a\,\nabla\times\bm{E}+\nabla(\nabla\cdot\bm{E})-\nabla^{2}\bm{E}-\frac{g_{\gamma}}{f_{a}}\,\nabla a\times\partial_{t}\bm{E}\simeq 0.

(32)

Using Eqs. (
80
) and (
82
), the term
\nabla(\nabla\cdot\bm{E})
can be expressed as:

\displaystyle\nabla(\nabla\cdot\bm{E})

\displaystyle=\nabla\left(\frac{g_{\gamma}}{f_{a}}\,\nabla a\cdot\bm{B}\right)

\displaystyle\simeq\frac{g_{\gamma}}{f_{a}}\left[\nabla a\times(\nabla\times\bm{B})+(\nabla a\cdot\nabla)\bm{B}\right].

(33)

Substituting Eqs. (
81
), (
83
), and the ansatz Eq. (
31
) into Eq. (
33
), and neglecting terms of order
\mathcal{O}(g_{\gamma}^{2}/f_{a}^{2})
, we find:

\nabla(\nabla\cdot\bm{E})\simeq\frac{g_{\gamma}}{f_{a}}\,\nabla\times\partial_{t}\bm{E}+\frac{g_{\gamma}}{f_{a}}\,(\nabla a\cdot\nabla)\,\frac{\nabla\times\bm{E}}{i\omega}.

(34)

Inserting Eq. (
34
) into Eq. (
32
) yields:

\partial_{t}^{2}\bm{E}+\omega^{2}\bm{E}+i\,\frac{g_{\gamma}}{f_{a}}\left(\partial_{t}a+\frac{\bm{k}\cdot\nabla a}{|\bm{k}|}\right)(\bm{k}\times\bm{E})=0.

(35)

Since
\partial_{t}a+\frac{\bm{k}\cdot\nabla a}{|\bm{k}|}
represents the total time derivative along the photon trajectory, we define
\dot{a}(t)\equiv\frac{da}{dt}
. Within the plane-wave ansatz Eq. (
30
), the second time derivative becomes:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}^{2}\bm{E}

\displaystyle=(-\omega^{2}\bm{E}_{0}-2i\omega\partial_{t}\bm{E}_{0}+\partial_{t}^{2}\bm{E}_{0})\,e^{-i\omega t+i\bm{k}\cdot\bm{x}}

\displaystyle\simeq(-\omega^{2}\bm{E}_{0}-2i\omega\partial_{t}\bm{E}_{0})\,e^{-i\omega t+i\bm{k}\cdot\bm{x}},

(36)

where the WKB approximation (
\partial_{t}^{2}\bm{E}_{0}\ll\omega\partial_{t}\bm{E}_{0}
) justifies neglecting the
\partial_{t}^{2}\bm{E}_{0}
term. Substituting into Eq. (
35
), we obtain:

\partial_{t}\bm{E}_{0}=\frac{g_{\gamma}}{2f_{a}}\,\dot{a}(t)\,\frac{\bm{k}\times\bm{E}_{0}}{|\bm{k}|}.

(37)

Expressing Eq. (
37
) in component form:

i\partial_{t}E_{0,\alpha}=i\frac{g_{\gamma}}{2f_{a}}\,\dot{a}(t)\,\frac{1}{|\bm{k}|}\,\epsilon^{\alpha\beta\delta}k_{\beta}E_{0,\delta},

(38)

where
\epsilon^{\alpha\beta\delta}
is the Levi-Civita symbol (
\alpha,\beta,\delta=1,2,3
). Defining the spin-1 matrices
(S^{\beta})_{\alpha\delta}=i\epsilon^{\alpha\beta\delta}
, which satisfy:

[S^{\alpha},S^{\beta}]=i\epsilon^{\alpha\beta\delta}S^{\delta},\quad(S^{1})^{2}+(S^{2})^{2}+(S^{3})^{2}=2,

we treat
\bm{E}_{0}=(E_{0,1},E_{0,2},E_{0,3})^{T}
as a quantum state
|\psi\rangle
. Equation (
38

i\partial_{t}|\psi\rangle=H_{a\gamma\gamma}|\psi\rangle,

(39)

with the effective axion-photon Hamiltonian:

H_{a\gamma\gamma}=\frac{g_{\gamma}}{2f_{a}}\,\dot{a}(t)\,\frac{\bm{k}\cdot\bm{S}}{|\bm{k}|}.

(40)

This matches the Hamiltonian presented in the main text.

Appendix B
Derivation of Non-Adiabatic Berry Phase in Scenario I

Consider a photon propagating along the
\hat{z}
-direction in an axion background. The Hamiltonian from Eq. (

H_{a\gamma\gamma}=\frac{g_{\gamma}}{2f_{a}}\,\dot{a}(t)\,S^{3}=\frac{g_{\gamma}}{2f_{a}}\,\dot{a}(t)\begin{pmatrix}0&-i&0\\
i&0&0\\
0&0&0\end{pmatrix}.

(41)

Since the third component is decoupled, we focus on the

H_{a\gamma\gamma}=\frac{g_{\gamma}}{2f_{a}}\,\dot{a}(t)\begin{pmatrix}0&-i\\
i&0\end{pmatrix}.

(42)

The corresponding time-evolution operator is:

U(t)=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\left(\frac{g_{\gamma}}{2f_{a}}\Delta a\right)&-\sin\left(\frac{g_{\gamma}}{2f_{a}}\Delta a\right)\\
\sin\left(\frac{g_{\gamma}}{2f_{a}}\Delta a\right)&\cos\left(\frac{g_{\gamma}}{2f_{a}}\Delta a\right)\end{pmatrix},

(43)

where
\Delta a(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\dot{a}(t^{\prime})\,dt^{\prime}
. Assuming
\dot{a}(t)
is periodic with period

, we decompose
\Delta a(t)
as:

\Delta a(t)=\tilde{a}(t)+At,

(44)

where
\tilde{a}(t)
is
T

A
is a constant determined by
\dot{a}(t)
. Substituting into Eq. (
43
), we write
U(t)=Z(t)e^{iMt}
, with:

Z(t)=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\tilde{\beta}&-\sin\tilde{\beta}\\
\sin\tilde{\beta}&\cos\tilde{\beta}\end{pmatrix},\quad M=-\frac{g_{\gamma}}{2f_{a}}A\begin{pmatrix}0&-i\\
i&0\end{pmatrix},

(45)

and
\tilde{\beta}\equiv\frac{g_{\gamma}}{2f_{a}}\tilde{a}(t)

The cyclic states, eigenstates of
M
, are
\left|v_{L}\right\rangle=(1,-i)^{T}/\sqrt{2}
(left-circular) and
\left|v_{R}\right\rangle=(1,i)^{T}/\sqrt{2}
(right-circular). The Berry phase is:

\displaystyle\alpha_{\text{Berry}}

\displaystyle=i\int_{0}^{T}\left\langle v_{L/R}\right|Z^{\dagger}(t)\frac{d}{dt}Z(t)\left|v_{L/R}\right\rangle\,dt

\displaystyle=m\frac{g_{\gamma}}{2f_{a}}\left[\tilde{a}(T)-\tilde{a}(0)\right],\quad m=\pm 1.

(46)

Since

(with

\mathbb{S}^{1}

\alpha_{\text{Berry}}=\pm N_{w}\pi g_{\gamma},

(47)

consistent with the main text.

Appendix C
Basis-independence of the Berry phase

In this appendix, we demonstrate explicitly that the axion-induced Berry phases obtained in the main text are independent of the basis choice. Our main point is that the effective nonrelativistic Hamiltonians used in the main text are derived at the classical (tree-level) level, whereas the chiral rotation that removes the axion–fermion Yukawa interaction necessarily invokes the quantum anomaly, a loop effect. Consequently, to maintain physical equivalence between different bases, the loop-induced contributions—particularly the anomaly-generated axion–photon coupling—must be taken into account.

To make this explicit, we start from a DFSZ-type axion model
[

For simplicity, here we consider a toy model with one Dirac fermion.
After spontaneous breaking of

Appendix D
Derivation of Non-Adiabatic Berry Phase in Scenario II

Consider a time-dependent Hamiltonian
H(t)=\bm{V}(t)\cdot\bm{j}
, where
\bm{V}(t)
has constant magnitude and rotates uniformly:

\frac{\bm{V}(t)}{|\bm{V}|}=\left(\sin\theta\cos\omega t,\sin\theta\sin\omega t,\cos\theta\right),

with period
T=2\pi/\omega
. Following Ref.
[
82
]
,
H(t)=e^{-ij_{z}\omega t}H_{0}e^{ij_{z}\omega t}
, where
H_{0}=H(0)
. The Schrödinger equation
i\partial_{t}|\psi(t)\rangle=H(t)|\psi(t)\rangle
transforms to:

i\frac{\partial}{\partial t}|\tilde{\psi}(t)\rangle=H(\omega)|\tilde{\psi}(t)\rangle,

(69)

where
|\psi(t)\rangle=e^{-ij_{z}\omega t}|\tilde{\psi}(t)\rangle
and
H(\omega)=H_{0}-\omega j_{z}
.
This corresponds precisely to a coordinate transformation from the original, static coordinate system to a comoving frame that rotates synchronously with the vector
\mathbf{V}(t)
.
Defining
\overline{\bm{V}}
such that:

H(\omega)=\overline{\bm{V}}\cdot\bm{j},\quad\overline{\bm{V}}=\overline{V}(\sin\overline{\theta},0,\cos\overline{\theta}),

with

\overline{V}=\sqrt{V^{2}+\omega^{2}-2V\omega\cos\theta},

(70)

and

\sin\overline{\theta}=\frac{V\sin\theta}{\overline{V}},\quad\cos\overline{\theta}=\frac{V\cos\theta-\omega}{\overline{V}}.

Thus, Eq. (
69
) becomes time-independent, yielding:

|\psi(t)\rangle=e^{-ij_{z}\omega t}e^{-iH(\omega)t}|\psi(0)\rangle.

(71)

The time-evolution operator is:

U(t)=e^{-ij_{z}\omega t+ij\omega t}e^{-i[H(\omega)t+j\omega t]},

(72)

matching
U(t)=Z(t)e^{iMt}
, with:

M=-[H(\omega)+j\omega],\quad Z(t)=e^{i(j-j_{z})\omega t}.

The eigenvalues
\xi_{z}
and eigenstates
|\phi_{z}\rangle
of
M
are:

\xi_{z}=-j_{z}\overline{V}-j\omega,\quad|\phi_{z}\rangle=e^{-i\overline{\theta}j_{y}}|j_{z}\rangle,

(73)

where
j_{z}|j_{z}\rangle=j_{z}|j_{z}\rangle
(
j_{z}=-j,-j+1,\dots,j
). The Berry phase is:

\displaystyle\alpha_{\text{Berry}}

\displaystyle=i\int_{0}^{2\pi/\omega}\left\langle\phi_{z}\right|e^{-i(j-j_{z})\omega t}\frac{d}{dt}e^{i(j-j_{z})\omega t}\left|\phi_{z}\right\rangle\,dt

\displaystyle=2\pi(j_{z}\cos\overline{\theta}-j)=-2\pi j_{z}(1-\cos\overline{\theta}).

(74)

Additionally, we have the total phase

\alpha_{\rm tot}=j_{z}\overline{V}T.

(75)

Appendix E
Derivation of
\alpha_{\rm tot}
in the photon ring experiment

Including the axion–photon coupling together with the standard photon kinetic term, we obtain the axion-modified Maxwell equations. Unlike Eqs. (
80
)-(
83
), we now also include an external charge density
\rho
and current density
\mathbf{j}
, yielding

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot\left(\mathbf{E}-\frac{g_{\gamma}}{f_{a}}a\mathbf{B}\right)=\rho,

(76)

\displaystyle\nabla\times\left(\mathbf{B}+\frac{g_{\gamma}}{f_{a}}a\mathbf{E}\right)-\partial_{t}\left(\mathbf{E}-\frac{g_{\gamma}}{f_{a}}a\mathbf{B}\right)=\mathbf{j},

(77)

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot\mathbf{B}=0,

(78)

\displaystyle\nabla\times\mathbf{E}+\partial_{t}\mathbf{B}=0,

(79)

For photons propagating in the medium,
\rho
and
\mathbf{j}
can be expressed in terms of the electric polarization
\mathbf{P}
as
\rho=-\nabla\cdot\mathbf{P}
and
\mathbf{j}=\partial_{t}\mathbf{P}
. Introducing the electric displacement field
\mathbf{D}\equiv\mathbf{E}+\mathbf{P}
, the equations become

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot\left(\mathbf{D}-\frac{g_{\gamma}}{f_{a}}a\mathbf{B}\right)=0,

(80)

\displaystyle\nabla\times\left(\mathbf{B}+\frac{g_{\gamma}}{f_{a}}a\mathbf{E}\right)-\partial_{t}\left(\mathbf{D}-\frac{g_{\gamma}}{f_{a}}a\mathbf{B}\right)=0,

(81)

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot\mathbf{B}=0,

(82)

\displaystyle\nabla\times\mathbf{E}+\partial_{t}\mathbf{B}=0.

(83)

In a general linear medium,
\mathbf{D}=\varepsilon\mathbf{E}
, where the dielectric tensor can be decomposed as

\varepsilon=\varepsilon_{n}I+\kappa,

(84)

with
\varepsilon_{n}I
the isotropic part (
\epsilon_{n}=n^{2}
) and
\kappa
the anisotropic part, where
I
is the identity matrix and
n
is the refractive index.
As in Scenario II, we neglect the axion momentum and assume
\partial_{t}a
varies slowly over the experimental timescale. It can therefore be treated as approximately constant, with
\partial_{t}a\simeq\sqrt{2\rho_{\rm DM}}\sin(m_{a}t_{0}+\varphi)
, where
t_{0}
denotes the time at which the experiment is performed and
\varphi
is the initial phase of the axion field.
We further adopt the ansatz for the electric field

\mathbf{E}(t,\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{E}^{a}(t)e^{-i\omega t+i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{x}},

(85)

where
\mathbf{k}
is the photon wave vector and
\omega=|\mathbf{k}|/n
. Taking a time derivative of Eq. (
81

\partial_{t}^{2}\left(\varepsilon\mathbf{E}\right)+\eta_{a}\nabla\times\mathbf{E}+\nabla(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{E})-\nabla^{2}\mathbf{E}=0,

(86)

where we have defined
\eta_{a}\equiv g_{\gamma}\sqrt{2\rho_{\rm DM}}\sin(m_{a}t_{0}+\varphi)/f_{a}

Substituting the ansatz Eq. (
85

86

\displaystyle-2i\omega\varepsilon\partial_{t}\mathbf{E}^{a}+\varepsilon\partial_{t}^{2}\mathbf{E}^{a}-\kappa\omega^{2}\mathbf{E}^{a}

(87)

\displaystyle+i\eta_{a}\mathbf{k}\times\mathbf{E}^{a}-\left(\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{E}^{a}\right)\mathbf{k}=0.

Within the WKB approximation we neglect the second time derivative of
\mathbf{E}^{a}
. Assuming a weak anisotropy, we approximate
-2i\omega\varepsilon\partial_{t}\mathbf{E}^{a}\simeq-2i\omega\varepsilon_{n}\partial_{t}\mathbf{E}^{a}

\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{E}^{a}\simeq 0

87

i\partial_{t}\mathbf{E}^{a}=-\frac{\omega}{2\varepsilon_{n}}\kappa\mathbf{E}^{a}+i\frac{\eta_{a}}{2\varepsilon_{n}\omega}\mathbf{k}\times\mathbf{E}^{a}.

(88)

Writing
\mathbf{E}^{a}
and
\kappa

i\partial_{t}E^{a}_{\alpha}=-\frac{\omega}{2\varepsilon_{n}}\kappa^{\alpha\beta}E^{a}_{\beta}+i\frac{\eta_{a}}{2\varepsilon_{n}\omega}\epsilon^{\alpha\beta\gamma}k_{\beta}E^{a}_{\gamma}.

(89)

are the spin operators with spin quantum number 1. If we regard

as a quantum state, i.e.,

H=-\frac{\omega}{2\varepsilon_{n}}\kappa+\frac{\eta_{a}}{2\varepsilon_{n}\omega}\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{S}.

(91)
