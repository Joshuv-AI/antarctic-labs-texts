Four‐Dimensional Electrochemiluminescence Microscopy Enables Depth‐Resolved Imaging of Single, Living Spheroids
Authors: Elisa D'Arrigo, Vanshika Gupta, Bertrand Goudeau, Jeffrey E. Dick, Neso Sojic
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThree‐dimensional (3D) cell culture systems called spheroids have emerged as a valuable alternative to traditional two‐dimensional cell cultures due to their ability to closely mimic the complexity of in vivo environments. In this study we propose a hybrid “confocal” electrochemiluminescence (ECL) approach that harnesses the optical sectioning ability of confocal microscopy and the high spatial resolution of ECL, overcoming the surface‐confinement barrier of conventional ECL and enabling depth‐resolved mapping of living spheroids up to 150 µm from the electrode while resolving the temporal evolution of ECL. By exploiting the sluggish heterogeneous electron‐transfer kinetics of the 2‐(dibutylamino)ethanol coreactant at ITO electrodes, homogeneous redox events driven by the sequential oxidation of [Ru(bpy)3]2+ propagate deep into the cellular architecture. Spatiotemporal mapping reveals the evolution of these processes across the spheroid volume, establishing time as an additional dimension for visualizing ECL reaction‐diffusion behavior within living 3D systems over time. We demonstrate that the 3D microenvironment acts as a reaction‐diffusion chamber that confines reactive intermediates, enhancing the signal in internal regions. This method achieves single‐cell resolution and provides a non‐invasive tool to probe internal permeability and redox metabolic states, transitioning ECL from a surface technique to a powerful volumetric imaging modality for tissue‐engineered models.

Introduction

Understanding the complex interactions between cells and their microenvironment is a critical step toward early diagnosis and development of targeted therapies [1, 2]. Cell cultures are indispensable tools for advancing the investigation of cellular physiology and molecular pathways in complex biological systems [3, 4]. Traditionally, the study of these systems has relied on in vitro 2D and in vivo animal models [5]. However, both approaches have significant limitations [6]. For example, in vitro 2D models are unable to replicate the microenvironmental complexity of real tissues. On the other hand, while animal models can mimic the tissue hierarchy of the in vivo environment, they are expensive to obtain, time‐consuming to maintain, and often are unreliable due to species‐specific differences that may not translate to humans [7, 8, 9]. These shortcomings limit their ability to effectively report on the efficacy of pharmacological treatments for patients.

These limitations highlight the need for advanced models capable of replicating real cell structure, metabolism, and the complexity of the surrounding microenvironment. Consequently, there is a growing interest in three‐dimensional (3D) culture systems known as spheroids [10, 11, 12]. These models more accurately reproduce the natural cell microenvironment in terms of cell‐to‐cell and cell‐extracellular matrix interactions, as well as cell density, differentiation, and spatial organization due to their multi‐layer architecture. Moreover, they allow for the integration of various physiological components including fibroblasts, endothelial, and immune cells within an organized matrix. For this reason, they are widely adopted in cancer research, drug monitoring, fundamental biological research, and stem cell regeneration [13, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18]. Although spheroids better approximate the cell density found in vivo, studying 2D cultures is relatively straightforward whereas analyzing 3D structures requires more sophisticated techniques. Spheroids are often evaluated through histological or histochemical staining, which involves fixing and sectioning the tissue. These processes destroy the sample precluding potential temporal analysis of biochemical reactions [19]. A less invasive alternative is fluorescence imaging which utilizes light‐excitable fluorophores to enable the evaluation of cell viability at various depths within a spheroid. Confocal microscopy is the most commonly used technique for this purpose, as it allows for optical sectioning and high‐resolution imaging throughout the 3D structure [13, 20]. While promissing, this technique presents significant drawbacks when applied to live‐cell imaging. For example, high laser excitation power is needed for the visualization of fluorescent dyes loaded onto the sample. This often leads to photobleaching and photodamage that can alter the homeostasis of the cells in the sample [21, 22]. Notably, prolonged exposure to intense visible light significantly impacts mitochondrial respiration, alters gene expression, and induces DNA damage, compromising the physiological relevance of the model [23, 24, 25].

In this study, we propose ECL as a complimentary imaging modality for the evaluation of live spheroids. ECL is a luminescence phenomenon where the excited state of a luminophore is produced via electrochemical reactions at an electrode surface, resulting in light emission without the need for external light excitation. Starting with electron transfer at the electrode surface which drives a cascade of highly energetic redox reactions with other molecules in solution, an excited luminophore is generated. This excited species then releases a photon as it decays back to its ground state configuration. The kinetics of this process can be controlled by modulating the potential applied to the electrode which in turn precise control of the location and timing of light emission [26, 27, 28, 29]. As a result, ECL is a sensitive chemistry‐based technique with low background and exceptional spatiotemporal regulation of luminescence [30, 31, 32, 33, 34, 35].

ECL has been extensively applied across a wide range of imaging applications [36, 37, 38, 39, 40, 41, 42, 43]. Over the past few years, individual cells have been successfully observed at the micron length scales, particularly by targeting plasma membranes through a surface‐confined microscopy approach [44]. In addition, ECL has been employed to investigate 3D cellular models such as spheroids [45, 46], facilitating the evaluation of metabolic activity, monitoring drug‐induced responses [47], and detecting diffusible biocompounds via straightforward signal measurement on ECL platforms [48, 49, 50]. Furthermore, initial efforts toward “confocal” ECL imaging have been conducted by varying axial focal depths in single cells and through simulations on tissue models [51].

The versatility of ECL imaging arises from the ability to implement different imaging configurations depending on the experimental objective. In particular, two main modes can be distinguished: positive ECL imaging and negative or shadow ECL imaging. In the positive ECL mode, the target object is typically labelled with ECL‐active luminophores (e.g. [Ru(bpy)3] 2+) [52]). These species interact with diffusing electroactive reagents generated at the electrode surface, producing light emission localized at the position of the object. As a result, the labelled object appears as a bright feature on a dark background [53, 54]. Conversely, in the negative ECL mode, the object acts as an insulating obstacle that locally blocks the diffusion of electroactive species toward the electrode surface. ECL emission therefore occurs only in the surrounding regions where the electrochemical reactions can take place, producing a bright background with a dark shadow corresponding to the object [44, 55]. This label‐free imaging approach is particularly attractive for the observation of subcellular structures [56, 57, 58, 59, 60, 61, 62].

To optimize the ECL process, a sacrificial species known as a co‐reactant is commonly employed [63]. This species undergoes electrochemical reactions, producing reactive intermediates that interact with the luminophore to yield photon emission [64, 65, 66]. The choice and concentration of this species are therefore critical parameters for optimizing ECL performance [67, 68, 69]. In systems where the co‐reactant and the luminophore diffuse freely in solution, the relative concentration between these two species plays a major role in determining the efficiency and spatial distribution of the emission. One key parameter affected by this ratio is the thickness of the ECL layer, which corresponds to the region between the electrode and the bulk aqueous phase where reactive species diffuse and emit light. When the concentration of [Ru(bpy)3]2+ is high relative to the coreactant, its oxidation can occur farther from the electrode surface [70]. In this case, the ECL‐emitting layer expands, and the overall process becomes limited by the diffusion of the luminophore rather than the short lifetime of the coreactant radicals. In contrast, at low [Ru(bpy)3]2+ concentrations, the short‐lived radicals derived from the coreactant determine the extent of the emission zone, resulting in a thinner ECL layer confined near the electrode surface [71, 72]. Among the alternative coreactants investigated, 2‐(dibutylamino)ethanol (DBAE) exhibits ECL intensities up to 10‐fold higher than those obtained with the commonly used tri‐n‐propylamine [29]. Additionally, DBAE is environmentally friendly, with lower toxicity, greater solubility in aqueous solutions, and reduced volatility compared to tri‐n‐propylamine, rendering it a safer and more practical option for biological imaging applications [73, 74].

Therefore, in this study we selected the [Ru(bpy)3]2+/DBAE ECL system and employed optically transparent ITO working electrodes to investigate the spatiotemporal distribution of ECL emission within live spheroids (Figure 1). By applying a constant potential, we were able to perform ECL imaging across multiple focal planes using a “confocal” z‐stack scanning approach. This integration of temporal information with three‐dimensional spatial mapping enables four‐dimensional visualization of ECL within living spheroids. Furthermore, the method allowed us to acquire detailed three‐dimensional information about the luminescent emission within untreated spheroids and DBAE‐incubated spheroids (DBAE@spheroids). Remarkably, we were able to successfully reconstructed ECL signals extending beyond 150 µm from the electrode surface, significantly surpassing the thickness of the conventional emissive layer typically accessible by surface‐based electrochemical techniques. This demonstrates the potential of the approach to probe deeper into the complex architecture of 3D tumor models, offering a promising alternative or complement to conventional confocal microscopy techniques.

Results and Discussion

The intrinsic spatiotemporal resolution of ECL was integrated with the axial sectioning capability of confocal microscopy to resolve ECL emission across three spatial dimensions and time in living spheroids (Figure 1) to yield better four‐dimensional understanding of ECL in spheroid systems. Using optically transparent ITO working electrodes, the spatial distribution of ECL emission could then be examined across multiple focal planes along the z‐axis, covering a total depth of 265 µm within the spheroid (Figure 1b). This depth was selected because it corresponded to the widest region of the spheroid, beyond which the spheroid began to curve inward, and the dense cellular structure prevented further fluorescence imaging. CHO‐K1 cells were used as a model system in this study to generate spheroids. These cells have been widely characterized for their cellular behavior and, thus, were chosen as the ideal candidate for benchmarking our system [75]. Briefly, cells were first grown on 2D substrates until confluent. Next, they were detached from the flasks using trypsin and seeded in ultra‐low‐attachment multi‐well plates at a density of 2 × 104 cells per well to promote three‐dimensional aggregation. After five days of culturing, well‐defined spheroids were formed, exhibiting the typical rounded morphology and dense core characteristic of such 3D structures. It is important to note that despite being assumed as spherical, these cell structures can exhibit deviation from perfect symmetry due to the packing ability of difference cells in the spheroid and due to the inherent heterogeneity present in cell shape, growth, and interaction with the surrounding environment even within a genetically homogeneous sample (Figure 1c). A schematic of the combined ECL platform is provided in Figure S1. An electrochemical cell was constructed by adhering a PDMS well onto an optically transparent ITO electrode, which served as the working electrode. The cell was mounted on an inverted microscope equipped with motorized z‐positioning for depth control. Prior to 3D imaging, the electrochemical behavior of the spheroid under ECL conditions was evaluated. A single CHO spheroid was positioned directly on the ITO electrode, and the potential was swept from 0 to 1.5 V at 100 mV s− 1 while simultaneously monitoring light emission. The system was characterized with and without prior incubation of the spheroid in DBAE (Figure S2a,b). Next, to confine the emission within the cellular aggregate, spheroids were first incubated for 15 min in a solution containing the sacrificial DBAE coreactant at a 750 µM concentration, according to previously established ECL imaging protocols [76]. Then, the DBAE@spheroids were transferred to the ECL imaging chamber where ECL microscopy experiments were conducted immediately in a 1X DPBS solution with 500 µM [Ru(bpy)3]2+. Each frame was acquired with an exposure time of 300 ms.

Cyclic voltammograms of the DBAE@spheroids were recorded in 1X DPBS buffer (Figure S2e). No significant oxidation features arise from the DBAE@spheroids indicating that DBAE is largely confined within the spheroid. CVs and corresponding ECL signals (Figure S2e,f) of solutions containing [Ru(bpy)3]2+ alone show the characteristic reversible oxidation of [Ru(bpy)3]2+ at 1.05 V and the concomitant ECL emission. Similar features are observed for DBAE@spheroids in presence of [Ru(bpy)3]2+ (Figure S2e,f). The ECL intensity profiles (Figure S2f) reveal for both conditions a maximum emission at ∼1.0 V. One can observe that ECL of the DBAE@spheroids is generated at the oxidation potential of the [Ru(bpy)3]2+. In other words, the ECL mechanism operating in such spheroids requires the direct oxidation of [Ru(bpy)3]2+ at the ITO electrode. Notably, measurable ECL was detected even in the absence of a coreactant (Figure S2c,f), suggesting that endogenous redox‐active species such as reactive oxygen species or membrane‐associated functionalities can transiently participate in coreactant‐type pathways. This observation is consistent with previous reports that have demonstrated that biological substrates can sustain ECL emission without exogenously added coreactants [48, 49]. Upon 15‐min incubation in DBAE, the ECL intensity of the DBAE@spheroids resulted in an approximately 10‐fold increase. Representative micrographs acquired at 1 V (Figure S2d,f), clearly illustrate the enhanced photon flux for DBAE@spheroids compared to spheroids that were not been incubated with DBAE. These results collectively confirm that DBAE substantially amplifies emission efficiency without altering the electrochemical onset potential, thereby establishing suitable and optimized conditions for subsequent depth‐resolved ECL imaging under constant‐potential operation.

The invariance of the maximum emission potential upon incubation in DBAE, together with the correspondence between the ECL signal and the oxidation of [Ru(bpy)3]2+ at the ITO electrode, offers a mechanistic insight into the role of the coreactant. This behavior, in fact, can be rationalized by the sluggish heterogeneous electron‐transfer kinetics between DBAE and ITO [65, 77, 78]. Although the oxidation of the tertiary amine is thermodynamically accessible within the potential window, due to its inner‐sphere nature, the slow kinetics prevent efficient direct oxidation of the amine at the electrode interface. Additionally, the spheroid occupies only ∼1%–2% of the total geometric area of the ITO electrode (considering the total electrode area approximately 1.77 cm2), further limiting the amount of DBAE in direct proximity to the electrode surface. As a result, DBAE does not significantly contribute to the faradaic response and the electrochemical step remains exclusively associated with the oxidation of [Ru(bpy)3]2+ (Figure S2e, black curve). These observations support a sequential reaction pathway in which, upon application of a positive potential, [Ru(bpy)3]2+ is first oxidized at the electrode to form [Ru(bpy)3]3+ (Equation 1). This species then diffuses into the spheroid and oxidizes DBAE to its radical cation (DBAE• +) through a homogeneous redox reaction (Equation 2). The resulting radical cation rapidly deprotonates to generate a strongly reducing α‐amino radical (DBAE•) (Equation 3). The DBAE‐derived radical subsequently reacts with a second [Ru(bpy)3]3+ species to generate the excited‐state [Ru(bpy)3]2+* luminophore and the iminium ion (Im+) (Equation 4). Relaxation of this excited state to the ground state produces photon emission (Equation 5). However, this first set of voltammetric and ECL experiments does not provide insights on the location and spatial distribution of the ECL emission. Therefore, we performed ECL microscopy experiments of the DBAE@spheroids deposited on ITO electrodes placed on a typical microscopy setup. The choice of ITO as working electrode provides a dual advantage in our experimental setup. Primarily, the optical transparency of ITO ensures compatibility with inverted microscopy, allowing simultaneous electrochemical monitoring and ECL imaging. By leveraging the kinetics of ITO toward DBAE oxidation, alongside the retention of coreactant within the three‐dimensional cellular structure, a selective emission pathway is favored. Therefore, although this reaction sequence is initiated at the electrode surface, the coupled homogeneous redox events allow the ECL layer to extend beyond a few micrometers from the electrode surface as usually observed [42, 79, 80]. Under appropriate concentration regimes, the spatial distribution of reactive intermediates permits axial (i.e. along z‐axis) propagation and confinement of emission into the DBAE@spheroid volume. This enables the ECL signal to probe regions at increasing distances from the ITO substrate (Figure 1b).

The coreactant concentration strongly governs the formation, thickness, and intensity of the ECL emission layer. Therefore, its influence was systematically investigated. To enable this, the DBAE concentration used during the incubation step was varied to maximize signal while preserving spatial resolution at the DBAE@spheroid|ITO interface. Chronoamperometry was performed at a constant potential of 1.15 V for 180 s to monitor the stability of the ECL signal over time while imaging DBAE@spheroids incubated previously with increasing DBAE concentrations (125 µM, 250 µM, 750 µM, and 1 mM; Figure S3). The potential value was chosen to ensure the quantitative oxidation of [Ru(bpy)3]2+ while operating within the diffusion‐limited regime to achieve the highest possible ECL intensity. The recorded emission intensity increased with DBAE concentration up to 750 µM, beyond which no further enhancement was observed (Figure S3). At this concentration, the ECL signal was both intense and highly resolved as it allowed us to resolve localized dark features approximately 10‐12 µm in diameter at the spheroid surface. The dimensions of these features were consistent with individual CHO cells, indicating that the technique achieves single‐cell‐level resolution at the spheroid–electrode interface. In contrast, at 1 mM DBAE, the micron‐scale interfacial features were markedly less pronounced (Figure S3e). The reduced visibility of single‐cell signatures at higher coreactant concentration suggests an excessive diffusion of radicals, which leads to a “blurring” of the CHO single cells. This loss of spatial contrast, despite the increase in overall intensity, indicates that the emissive zone is no longer strictly confined to the spheroid and spatial contrast is diminished. These results demonstrate that maximizing brightness alone does not ensure optimal resolution; rather, precise modulation of coreactant concentration is required to balance radical flux with the preservation of high‐contrast spatial features.

The stability of the ECL response at the optimal 750 µM concentration was further assessed by monitoring the emission intensity over the 180 s chronoamperometric step (Figure S4) at defined time points (Figure 2). This temporal study was conducted to characterize the evolution of the signal under constant potential bias, ensuring that the spatial features observed at the DBAE@spheroid‐electrode interface remain stable and well‐resolved for the entire duration of the imaging process. Monitoring the ECL response over time revealed the dynamic evolution of emission under constant potential bias, complementing the spatial information obtained across x, y, and z and thereby providing the temporal coordinate required for four‐dimensional ECL imaging. Immediately upon application of the potential, a sharp increase in ECL intensity was observed, due to high local DBAE concentration at the spheroid‐electrode interface, promoting efficient generation of [Ru(bpy)3]2+*. The emission then gradually decreases with a shallower signal decay slope after approximately 30 s as the DBAE concentration at the interface is exhausted and the oxidation of DBAE on the walls of the spheroid begins (Figure 2). This decay is driven by the progressive consumption of DBAE at the interface until a diffusion–reaction equilibrium is established. The corresponding micrographs reveal that the micron‐scale interfacial features attributed to individual cells remain clearly discernible throughout the potential application period. The preservation of single‐cell‐level contrast over time demonstrates that the ECL emission remains sufficiently confined to the electrode‐DBAE@spheroid interface and does not progressively blur or broaden under sustained bias. This can be rationalized by considering the bimolecular reaction rate between [Ru(bpy)3]3+ and the reactive intermediate of DBAE. Indeed, DBAE generates a highly unstable intermediate, exhibiting reaction kinetics approximately ten times faster than those of TPA•+ [81]. This rapid deprotonation step significantly limits both the thickness of the emission layer and the diffusion of [Ru(bpy)3]3+, thereby enhancing spatial confinement of the ECL signal and enabling visualization of well‐defined features within the spheroid. After the applied potential is switched off, a measurable ECL persists, generating an “afterglow” emission (Figure 2b). One can observe a clear ECL emission even 60 s after stopping the potential. This delayed emission suggests that electrochemically generated [Ru(bpy)3]3+ complexes accumulate in the solution phase and within the DBAE@spheroids. They diffuse slowly in the spheroid matrix and continue to react with accumulated DBAE that is present in significant concentration in the spheroid. The signal decays only once the oxidized luminophore is fully regenerated to [Ru(bpy)3]2+, further supporting the contribution of homogeneous reaction pathways extending beyond the immediate electrode surface. In fact, these systems can act as reservoirs for reactive species. Together, these results confirm that the optimized [Ru(bpy)3]2+/DBAE system provides both temporal stability and spatial confinement within the spheroid structure, establishing a robust foundation for probing the axial distribution of ECL emission within three‐dimensional architectures. This observation also suggests that DBAE is not significantly released into the surrounding electrolyte after transfer to DBAE‐free solution. Considering the amphiphilic nature and limited water solubility of DBAE [82], transient partitioning into hydrophobic domains of the spheroid, including lipid‐rich cell membrane and possible intracellular components, may contribute to its retention within the three‐dimensional architecture. Furthermore, the retention of the coreactant is likely also governed by the molecules’ diffusion‐limited transport within the spheroid microenvironment. The absence of detectable DBAE‐related electrochemical features indicates that significant leaching into the bulk solution does not occur within the experimental timescale. Such leakage would be expected to increase nonspecific ECL emission outside the spheroid and reduce spatial confinement of the emissive region. However, the preserved localization and temporal stability of the ECL signal demonstrate that DBAE retention is sufficient under the employed experimental conditions. It is a remarkable result because developing a long‐lasting ECL emission can be considered as a challenging task. Indeed, classically in the ECL field, stopping the imposed potential ends the ECL emission instantly. However, a few specific approaches have been developed for long‐lasting ECL. By storing reactive intermediates in confined or interfacial environments, such as gas bubbles [83, 84], droplets [85] or multiphase boundaries [86, 87], it is possible to sustain redox reactions and ECL emission even after the potential is switched off. Moreover, in the present case, the long‐lasting ECL is achieved with a biological matrix and not with pure chemical systems as reported previously.

The spatial evolution of the emission captured through successive z‐planes (Figures 3a and S6a), reveals an extended reaction zone that propagates beyond the immediate electrode interface. This finding deviates from the widely accepted view that the ECL layer in [Ru(bpy)3]2+‐based systems is confined to within ∼3 µm of the electrode surface [40]. We hypothesize that an extension of the diffusion layer is visualized here because [Ru(bpy)3]3+ diffuses into the interstitial spaces of the spheroid and becomes retained within its internal cavities, the ECL reaction is not a purely surface‐confined event but a volume‐distributed process. This depth‐dependent propagation could only be revealed through z‐stacking, as conventional wide‐field imaging would integrate emission along the optical axis and obscure the true spatial extent of the ECL emission. Together with the x–y spatial distribution and the temporal evolution of the ECL emission described above, the z‐resolved measurements introduced here provide the third spatial coordinate required for four‐dimensional (4D) visualization of the emissive process, revealing both where ECL occurs within the spheroid and how the underlying reaction‐diffusion process evolves over time.

The 3D reconstruction of the complete z‐stack (Figure 3b, Movie S1) provided a volumetric visualization of the ECL signal, revealing the remarkable depth to which the emission permeates the DBAE@spheroid architecture. While the primary intensity maximum is expectedly observed at the ITO surface, where the electrochemical generation of [Ru(bpy)3]3+ is most concentrated, the axial profile along z (Figure 3c) uncovered a more complex distribution of the emissive area. Moving away from the electrode, the signal did not simply decay; instead, the second‐highest intensity maximum was located approximately 25 µm from the surface. Beyond this secondary maximum, the emission gradually decreased with increasing distance. However, discernible ECL intensity persisted up to approximately 150 µm from the electrode before ultimately diminishing to background levels. These observations provide direct evidence of in‐spheroid ECL generation. The extended emission layer observed substantially exceeds previously reported thicknesses for surface‐confined ECL systems and underscores the importance of resolving the axial dimension. Indeed, ECL emission is usually confined to the vicinity of the electrode surface, typically a few microns, due to the very short lifetime of the electrogenerated radicals in solution [80, 88, 89, 90, 91]. However, the approach reported here with DBAE@spheroid demonstrates the possibility to extend the ECL region a few hundreds of microns far from the electrode surface.

It is important to note that the depth discrimination achieved by the present approach is not yet equivalent to that provided by the physical pinhole of confocal fluorescence microscopy [13, 20]. In the latter, out‐of‐focus light is rejected independently of the sample chemistry, whereas in the present platform the effective axial resolution is governed by the reaction‐diffusion length of the electrogenerated intermediates. Consequently, axial resolution and imaging depth are intrinsically coupled rather than independently tunable. In the present system, the extended penetration depth demonstrated here is achieved at the expense of a broader emissive reaction zone. An additional strategy could be to covalently confine the coreactant to the cell membrane of the spheroid as opposed to the incubation strategy presented here. In that case, there would be no leaching of the reagent overtime and the ECL induced glow would remain confined to individual cells of the spheroid indefinitely. Lastly, our ECL imaging approach can be combined with advanced optical sectioning modalities or higher‐resolution imaging configurations to enable resolutions similar to those of the confocal microscope [41]. In this work, we pose confocal ECL microscopy as a complimentary tool to confocal microscopy for studying these complex three‐dimensional biological systems.

To better understand the relationship between the extended ECL emission and the internal structure of the spheroid, we performed correlated fluorescence imaging using Hoechst reagent to stain cellular nuclei. CHO spheroids were incubated with Hoechst reagent, producing blue fluorescence localized to cell nuclei. By acquiring fluorescence z‐stacks at the same depth intervals as the ECL measurements, a direct morphological correlation could be established (Figures 3d and S6b). A notable feature emerges when focusing at the center of the DBAE@spheroid, where we observe a much stronger ECL emission compared to the surrounding regions. By examining the confocal images, we notice that at a depth of 60 µm there is a visible cavity in the center area of the spheroid while at 144 µm some nuclei are visible in the central region. This concave microarchitecture likely promotes localized confinement of reactive intermediates including DBAE and electrochemically generated [Ru(bpy)3]3+, between the electrode surface and the inner core, leading to an enhanced ECL signal. To determine whether the observed emission pattern was determined by the specific structure of the spheroid, the same experiment was performed under identical conditions using a different DBAE@spheroid (sample 2) with a distinct morphology (Figure S7a). Visualization of the z‐stack along different orientations (Figure S7c–e) shows that the emission is consistently confined to the DBAE@spheroid and extends beyond 100 µm, although the spatial structure of the emission differs between samples due to inherent biological variability. An interesting feature that emerges from the analysis is that the ECL intensity profiles along the z‐direction for both samples display a characteristic shape, with a first intensity maximum followed by a second one (Figure S7b).

Following the electrochemical experiments, 3D confocal imaging was employed to assess cellular health through a live/dead assay. Hoechst stain was used to map the total cell population by staining all nuclei, while Propidium Iodide selectively identified cells with compromised membranes (Movie S2, Figure S8). In post‐ECL measurements, an increase in the dead cell population was observed in the spheroid. However, despite the presence of dead cells, the spheroid integrity was maintained as evidenced by the consistent shape and the large area of live cells that remain after interrogation. Complementary photoluminescence (PL) measurements monitoring light emission at the basal region of a spheroid, provided further insight into the luminophore dynamics within the 3D architecture (Figure S9). Upon application of the potential necessary to oxidize [Ru(bpy)3]2+, a progressive increase in PL intensity was observed, specifically localized within the spheroid core, while the surrounding bulk solution remained dark. This phenomenon can be attributed to a synergistic combination of transport and environmental factors.

First, the time‐dependent rise in signals suggests a progressive accumulation of the luminophore, likely driven by specific chemical interactions between the [Ru(bpy)3]2+/3+ species and the biomolecular components of the spheroid [48]. Second, the preferential emission from the core aligns with the presence of a necrotic, less densely packed center, which offers lower resistance to luminophore infiltration compared to the highly cohesive peripheral layers [92]. While this outer “shell” limits the rate of diffusion for bulk species, the internal degradation creates a more permeable microenvironment, allowing for a higher local accumulation of the [Ru(bpy)3]2+/3+ species. Moreover, it has been shown how damage of DNA results in an increased reaction rate with [Ru(bpy)3]3+ [93]. Finally, the pronounced contrast between the bright core and the dark exterior is indicative of a differential oxygen quenching effect. In the hypoxic microenvironment of the inner spheroid, the excited state [Ru(bpy)3]2+* is shielded from oxygen‐mediated non‐radiative deactivation, significantly enhancing its luminescence [94, 95].

To directly probe the influence of microarchitectural confinement, we modified the spheroid–electrode interface by promoting active cellular adhesion to the ITO substrate. This was achieved through the surface functionalization of ITO with fibronectin (FN), a key glycoprotein of the extracellular matrix that mediates cell‐substrate attachment via specific integrin‐receptor recognition [96]. Following FN deposition, spheroids were incubated on the treated electrode for 1 h. This procedure resulted in firm attachment of the spheroid base to the substrate, effectively eliminating the concave interfacial geometry observed in untreated samples. The adhered spheroids were subsequently incubated for 15 min in DBAE before ECL measurements. DBAE@spheroid was interrogated using the same ECL “confocal” imaging protocol (Figure S10). In contrast to freely positioned DBAE@spheroids, the ECL maps of fibronectin‐adhered samples displayed a pronounced dark region at the DBAE@spheroid center. This central attenuation indicates that neither [Ru(bpy)3]2+ nor DBAE‐derived intermediates were able to access or accumulate at the spheroid–electrode interface once the concave cavity was removed, leading to a characteristic shadow ECL effect. Importantly, the peripheral ECL distribution remained consistent with that observed in non‐adhered DBAE@spheroids, confirming that the overall emission mechanism was preserved while the localized accumulation effect was suppressed. Moreover, in this case, unlike in previous observations, the integrated ECL intensity profile along the z‐direction exhibits a strong initial emission followed by a gradual decrease, with no further peaks observed (Figure S11).

The “confocal” z‐stacking capability of the integrated platform enabled progressive yet spatially precise imaging of ECL emission beginning at the electrode surface and extending into the spheroid volume. Together, these results demonstrate that the previously observed deep ECL enhancement arises from localized accumulation of reactive intermediates within a confined three‐dimensional microenvironment. Elimination of this structural confinement abolishes the central intensity maximum while preserving the broader emission profile, confirming that architectural features within the spheroid strongly modulate radical retention and enable sustained ECL propagation far from the electrode surface.

Conclusion

We report a “confocal” ECL imaging strategy that enables spatially resolved mapping of redox activity within intact three‐dimensional spheroids. By coupling electrochemically generated luminescence with axial z‐stacking, this platform overcomes the traditional surface confinement limits of ECL, providing depth‐resolved insights into reaction–diffusion processes across complex cellular architectures. By leveraging the sluggish heterogeneous electron‐transfer kinetics between DBAE at ITO electrodes, we achieved unique spatial regulation of the emissive zone. Through systematic optimization of the [Ru(bpy)3]2+/DBAE system, we achieved single‐cell‐level resolution at the spheroid–electrode interface while maintaining high temporal stability under constant potential application.

“Confocal” sectioning revealed ECL propagation up to ∼150 µm from the electrode, providing direct evidence of in‐spheroid ECL generation. In addition, long‐lasting ECL generation is reported within such a biological matrix. Correlated fluorescence imaging and architectural perturbation experiments demonstrate that the three‐dimensional microstructure governs radical confinement and sustains emission deep within the spheroid volume. Together, these results establish “confocal” ECL as a powerful tool for mapping redox activity within biological assemblies and redefine the accessible dimensionality of electrochemiluminescence in three‐dimensional systems.
