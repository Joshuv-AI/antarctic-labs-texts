Stridor Misleading as Asthma: Unveiling Case of Shabbir Syndrome in an Atopy‐Prone 2.5 Years Old Pakistani
Authors: Nabeel Ahmad, Ali Gohar, Abdul Rehman Shahid Khan, Armaghan Hanif, Rohail Sani, Khizra Saeed, Masab Ali, Muhammad Husnain Ahmad, Fida Hussain
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTLaryngo‐onycho‐cutaneous syndrome (LOCS), or Shabbir's syndrome, linked to mutations in the LAMA3 gene, is predominantly observed in children from Punjabi families. We present the case of a 2.5‐year‐old male who was initially misdiagnosed with asthma due to progressive hoarseness and stridor. Further evaluation revealed biphasic stridor, paronychia, conjunctival inflammation, nail dystrophy and widespread mucocutaneous lesions. Laryngoscopy showed glottic granulation tissue and subglottic stenosis. While genetic testing was unavailable due to resource limitations, histopathological findings supported a clinical diagnosis of LOCS. Through this case report, we aim to highlight the importance of considering LOCS diagnosis in children with atypical respiratory symptoms complemented by mucocutaneous findings, especially in atopy‐prone populations. Early diagnosis is critical to prevent complications like airway obstruction. Management remains supportive, involving surgical and ophthalmologic interventions.

Introduction

Laryngo‐onycho‐cutaneous (LOC) syndrome, also known as Shabbir's syndrome, is a rare autosomal recessive disorder primarily affecting the mucocutaneous surfaces of the body, including the skin, nails, larynx and ocular regions [1]. First identified in the Punjabi Muslim population by Prof. Syed Ghulam Shabbir in 1986, this condition is most commonly seen in consanguineous families [2]. LOC syndrome is a subtype of junctional epidermolysis bullosa (JEB), and its hallmark is the formation of hypergranulation tissue, leading to recurrent erosions, particularly in the larynx and conjunctiva [1].

Mutations in the LAMA3 gene, which encodes the α3 chain of laminin‐332, essential for the structural integrity of epithelial tissues, are responsible for LOC syndrome [3]. Disruption of this protein results in early symptoms of blistering and scarring, especially in skin and mucous membranes [2, 3]. Later laryngeal involvement occurs, which often manifests as airway stenosis requiring tracheostomy [1, 2].

Untreated ocular complications like conjunctival erosions and symblepharon may cause blindness, while chronic blood loss from hypergranulated wounds can lead to anaemia [1]. Management of LOC syndrome is complex and hence requires a multidisciplinary approach, involving surgical excision of granulation tissue, airway management and ocular surface reconstruction with amniotic membrane transplantation [1, 2]. This case report will discuss the clinical course of a patient with LOC syndrome, highlighting the diagnostic challenges and management strategies while increasing awareness of this rare but serious condition.

Case Report

A 2.5‐year‐old Punjabi Muslim Pakistani male, the third child of a consanguineous marriage, was brought to the emergency department with complaints of progressive noisy breathing and hoarseness of voice (stridor) over the past 2 years. Initially, the child had normal speech development; however, around the age of 15 months, his parents noticed increasing hoarseness, which gradually progressed to biphasic stridor and respiratory distress. He had multiple admissions to local health facilities with a presumed diagnosis of bronchial asthma, influenced by his father's asthma history. The child was frequently administered inhaled bronchodilators with only transient improvement. However, despite therapy, the breathing difficulty and hoarseness continued to worsen, raising concern for an alternative diagnosis.

There was no associated history of fever, coryza, recent upper respiratory tract infections, breathing pattern changes, or feeding difficulties. No similar illnesses were noted in the family. Physical examination revealed biphasic stridor, a harsh hoarse voice, intermittent suprasternal and subcostal recessions and equal bilateral air entry. The oral cavity appeared clear without obvious obstruction. Six months prior, the child developed recurrent painful paronychia, followed by small, erythematous, vesicular and ulcerative lesions around the ears and nostrils (Figure 1). These bled easily and healed with scarring and pigmentation. He had intermittent spontaneous epistaxis but no gum bleeding, hemarthrosis, or post‐circumcision haemorrhage. Skin involvement worsened over time, requiring hospitalisation for secondary bacterial infections. Nail examination revealed brittle nails with onycholysis (Figure 2b) involving both fingers and toes, a detail previously overlooked. Over recent weeks, the child also developed photophobia and increased lacrimation. Ophthalmologic evaluation revealed bilateral conjunctival hyperemia, corneal vascularisation and early pseudopterygium formation (Figure 2a)—findings consistent with chronic conjunctival irritation and scarring, typical of LOCS.

The constellation of findings—persistent stridor with glottic involvement, mucocutaneous fragility, onychodystrophy and ocular inflammation with corneal changes—prompted consideration of laryngo‐onycho‐cutaneous syndrome (LOCS), also known as Shabbir syndrome. Flexible laryngoscopy showed ulceration and granulation tissue over the glottis and supraglottic region, with subglottic stenosis and anterior webbing (Figure 3). Biopsy of the granulation tissue showed chronic inflammatory infiltrate with granulation tissue but no evidence of malignancy or infection (Figure 4). While genetic confirmation was not pursued due to financial constraints, the diagnosis of LOCS was made based on the characteristic clinical picture and histopathological findings. The child underwent microlaryngoscopic debulking of the granulation tissue with CO2 laser by the ENT team. A short course of systemic corticosteroids was initiated to reduce mucosal inflammation, followed by maintenance inhaled budesonide therapy. Serial endoscopic assessments were planned to monitor recurrence.

Initial ophthalmologic management included topical antibiotics and steroids (Moxifloxacin eye drops and betamethasone 0.1%), but the patient was not improving as corneal scarring was extensive and there were symblepharons. Later the ophthalmology department suggested symblepharectomy, which was done after the patient was discharged from our vicinity. Nail care with emollients and topical antibiotics was advised by the rheumatology department to prevent secondary infection. During his inpatient stay, nutritional support was initiated, including protein‐rich feeds and micronutrient supplementation, under paediatric dietary guidance. At follow‐up, the child showed improved weight gain, decreased skin lesion recurrence and partial resolution of ocular symptoms.

Gene Xpert was done for tuberculosis to rule it out and the results came negative. The genetic studies were not done due to resource limitation at our centre.

Discussion

LOCS is characterised by the hypergranulation tissue formation in the auricular, ocular and laryngeal regions, leading to recurrent erosions. While the multiple erosions in the fingernails and toenails can lead to loss of digits in the early months of life, the life‐threatening complication of this syndrome is laryngeal stenosis, secondary to granulation tissue formation in the larynx. If left untreated, it potentially leads to premature death [4]. The ocular manifestations of the syndrome include recurrent minimal blistering and granulation formation, contributing to the formation of an ocular mass that causes corneal scarring, ultimately leading to blindness.

To date, there is no effective pharmacotherapy available for LOCS. The disease is primarily managed conservatively with surgical procedures such as granulation tissue removal, ocular surface reconstruction with amniotic membrane transplantation and thalidomide for the eyes and granulation tissue excision or tracheostomy for laryngeal stenosis [5]. The fatality rate of the disease peaks during early childhood; however, children who survive the initial flare may experience remission in the second decade of life [2].

Through our current case, we want to point out that the clinical diagnosis of LOCS, especially in the subcontinent, is difficult due to the increased prevalence of atopy in this part of the world, mainly during the summer season. The initial symptoms of the disease, such as breathlessness due to laryngeal narrowing, can mimic allergic asthma exacerbations in atopic patients, leading to misdiagnosis due to availability bias. Given the severity of LOCS and its laryngeal manifestations, which often necessitate tracheostomy [4].

To conclude, LOCS is a rare but critical syndrome, and differentiating it from asthma can be challenging. Through our case, we aim to highlight the critical importance of its early recognition and diagnosis, especially in prevalent areas.
