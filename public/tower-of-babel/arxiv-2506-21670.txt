Spontaneous Quantum Turbulence in a Newborn Bose-Einstein Condensate via the Kibble-Zurek Mechanism
Seong-Ho Shinn, Matteo Massaro, Mithun Thudiyangal, Adolfo del Campo

Abstract
The Kibble-Zurek mechanism (KZM) predicts the spontaneous formation of topological defects in a continuous phase transition driven at a finite rate. We propose the generation of spontaneous quantum turbulence (SQT) via the KZM during Bose-Einstein condensation induced by a thermal quench. Using numerical simulations of the stochastic projected Gross-Pitaevskii equation in two spatial dimensions, we describe the formation of a newborn Bose-Einstein condensate proliferated by quantum vortices. We establish the nonequilibrium universality of SQT through the Kibble-Zurek and Kolmogorov scaling of the incompressible kinetic energy.



Understanding turbulence is one of the long-standing unsolved problems in physics, with broad applications in biology, medicine, industry, and weather prediction. Feynman pioneered the idea of Quantum Turbulence (QT) as an analog in superfluids proliferated by a tangle of vortices. With the development of technology to create, manipulate, and probe Bose-Einstein condensates (BECs), the study of QT emerged as a new research field at the frontiers of the nonequilibrium physics of complex systems. QT has applications from atomtronics to astrophysics and arises in superfluid Helium, BECs, polaritonic BEC, and neutron stars
Tsubota 2008a
;
Tsatsos et al. 2016
.

Quantum turbulence describes the complex, chaotic dynamics of interacting quantized vortices, which are topological defects. This definition is loose, and as we shall see, different criteria for its diagnosis have been put forward, including the scaling of the energy spectrum on the wavenumber, velocity autocorrelation functions, circulation statistics, etc. The situation is reminiscent of the use of various inequivalent definitions of quantum chaos, emphasizing complementary features brought out by a diversity of diagnostic tools (spectral statistics, semiclassical orbits, out-of-time order correlators, etc.)
Nandy et al. 2025
.

The study of QT is expected to be simpler than that of its classical counterpart. It was discovered in low-temperature physics studies of superfluid Helium. However, the remarkable experimental progress in the mid-90s offered a new highly controllable and tunable platform for its exploration: Bose-Einstein Condensates (BECs) of ultracold atomic gases
Ueda 2010
and the closely related quantum fluids of light
Carusotto and Ciuti 2013
. The experimental measurement of quantized vortices in BEC was crucial in probing their superfluid character. A BEC is characterized by a macroscopic wavefunction that acts as a complex-valued order parameter that can be conveniently written in a polar decomposition
\Psi(\mathbf{r})=\sqrt{n(\mathbf{r})}\ e^{i\theta(\mathbf{r})}
. A defining feature of a superfluid is that the circulation
\Gamma[C]=\oint_{C}\mathbf{v}\cdot d\bm{\ell}
(that is, the contour integral of the velocity around a given contour
C
) is quantized as a result of the single-valued character of the macroscopic wavefunction and the relation between the superfluid velocity
\mathbf{v}
(equivalently, the vorticity
\mathbf{\omega}=\nabla\times\mathbf{v}
) and the condensate phase,
\mathbf{v}(\mathbf{r})=\hbar\nabla\theta(\mathbf{r})/m
. QT differs from its classical counterpart in that quantum vortices are true topological defects and thus more stable than eddies, having conserved and quantized circulation
Tsubota et al. 2013
;
Tsatsos et al. 2016
.

The phenomenology of turbulence dramatically varies with the spatial dimension. In the quantum case, the spatial dimensionality of the superfluid can be varied by tuning the anisotropy of the external confinement in BEC experiments. In three spatial dimensions (3D), turbulence is characterized by the cascade of energy from large to small scales, with eddies breaking into ever-decreasing whirlpools. By contrast, two-dimensional (2D) turbulence is governed by the presence of an additional invariant of motion known as the enstrophy, and gives rise to an inverse energy cascade, with energy flowing from small to large scales. As a result, 2D turbulence leads to the clustering of vortices in bigger patches, predicted by Onsager and experimentally reported in BEC
Seo et al. 2017
;
Gauthier et al. 2019
and exciton-polariton superfluids
Panico et al. 2023
.

Several protocols are used to induce QT in a superfluid sample. They generally rely on continuous pumping of energy. A predominant approach involves sweeping a repulsive potential
Kwon et al. 2014
;
Navon et al. 2016
. This procedure is often too violent, complicating the study of QT, as a result of the generation of a variety of excitations.
As an alternative, one may wonder whether QT can emerge spontaneously, as topological defects emerge across a continuous phase transition. In this context, the Kibble-Zurek mechanism (KZM) constitutes a powerful paradigm for describing universal critical dynamics
Kibble 1976
;
Kibble 1980
;
Zurek 1985
;
Zurek 1993
;
del Campo and Zurek 2014
. It does so by exploiting equilibrium properties such as the divergence of the correlation length
\xi=\xi_{0}/|\varepsilon|^{\nu}
and the relaxation time
\tau=\tau_{0}/|\varepsilon|^{z\nu}
in the neighborhood of the critical point
del Campo and Zurek 2014
. Here,
\nu
is the correlation length critical exponent,
z
is the dynamic critical exponent, and
\varepsilon=\lambda-\lambda_{c}
for the control parameter
\lambda
(e.g., temperature) with critical value
\lambda_{c}
. For a linearized quench
\varepsilon=t/\tau_{Q}
, the KZM introduces the universal scaling of the nonequilibrium correlation length
\hat{\xi}
with the quench time
\tau_{Q}
, i.e.,
\hat{\xi}=\xi_{0}(\tau_{Q}/\tau_{0})^{\nu/(1+z\nu)}
. For point-like defects, such as solitons in a cigar-shaped BEC
Damski and Zurek 2010
;
Lamporesi et al. 2013
, and vortices in a pancake BEC
Weiler et al. 2008
, the KZM further predicts the formation of vortices at a density that scales universally with the quench time
n\propto 1/\hat{\xi}^{D}=\left(1/\xi_{0}^{D}\right)\left(\tau_{0}/\tau_{Q}\right)^{D\nu/(1+z\nu)}
, where
D
denotes the spatial dimension. In addition, it dictates that the response of the system is delayed by the freeze-out time scale
\hat{t}=(\tau_{0}\tau_{Q}^{z\nu})^{1/(1+z\nu)}
. In scenarios characterized by
U(1)
symmetry breaking that lead to vortex formation, the KZM has been widely tested in theoretical studies
del Campo et al. 2011
;
Chesler et al. 2015
;
Liu et al. 2020a
;
Zeng et al. 2023
;
Thudiyangal and del
Campo 2024
, with several experiments also supporting its role in superfluid formation and the BEC transition
Weiler et al. 2008
;
Navon et al. 2015
;
Ko et al. 2019
;
Kim et al. 2022
.

In this Letter, we describe spontaneous quantum turbulence (SQT) in a newborn BEC prepared by a thermal quench in finite time. We establish the Kolmogorov scaling of the incompressible kinetic energy and demonstrate its universal scaling for different values of the quench time when rescaled by the nonequilibrium correlation length predicted by the Kibble-Zurek mechanism.

Kibble-Zurek dynamics of the BEC transition.
To describe the non-equilibrium condensate dynamics and the emergence of QT, we consider the two-dimensional (2D) stochastic projected Gross-Pitaevskii equation
Gardiner and Davis 2003
;
P. B. Blakie, A. S. Bradley, M. J. Davis, R. J.
Ballagh and C. W. Gardiner 2008
;
Rooney et al. 2012
;
Rooney et al. 2014
;
Bradley et al. 2015
;
McDonald et al. 2020
.
This formalism has been widely used to model the BEC transition, with numerous studies demonstrating its quantitative agreement with experimental observations
Weiler et al. 2008
;
Liu et al. 2020b
.
Specifically, it describes the evolution of the low-energy coherent modes of the system via a complex order parameter
\Psi_{\mathcal{C}}
, which evolves according to

d{\Psi_{\mathcal{C}}}=\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{C}}\left[-(i+\gamma)\left(H_{sp}+g|\Psi_{\mathcal{C}}|^{2}-\mu\right)\Psi_{\mathcal{C}}\,dt+d\eta\right].

(1)

Here,
H_{sp}=-\left(1/2\right)\nabla^{2}+V(\mathbf{r})
denotes the single-particle Hamiltonian, where
V(\mathbf{r})
is the external trapping potential. In this work, we focus on a periodic homogeneous system with
V(\mathbf{r})=0
. Further details of Eq. (
1
) and its numerical implementation are provided in
url will be inserted by publisher
.

To drive the phase transition, we consider a linear quench of the chemical potential,
\mu(t)=\mu_{i}+\left(\mu_{f}-\mu_{i}\right)t/\tau_{Q}
, with
\mu_{i}
and
\mu_{f}
denoting its initial and final values, respectively. This protocol drives the system through the critical point
\mu_{c}
at a finite rate
\tau_{Q}
, resulting in the formation of a BEC populated with vortices. A vortex is characterized by a quantized circulation of the superfluid velocity,

\oint\mathbf{v}(\mathbf{r},t)\cdot d\bm{\ell}=\left(2\pi\hbar/m\right)w
, where
w\in\mathbb{Z}
is the winding number.
The associated singularity of the velocity field enforces a vanishing condensate density at the vortex center, the healing length determining the core size.
An example of a single realization of the newborn BEC is shown in Fig.
1
, where vortices can be recognized by the depletion of the BEC density at their core.
The associated phase map indicates that only the values
w=\pm 1
are generated in the course of the BEC transition. This is consistent with the fact that higher winding numbers are energetically costly and unstable against decay
Tsubota 2008b
.

Using the mean field values of the critical exponents
\nu=1/2
and
z=2
, the KZM predicts the scaling of the freeze-out time, which sets the time scale in which the growth of the order parameter lags behind the crossing of the critical point set by the quench of
\mu(t)
. The equilibration time,
when the growth of the number of bosons in BEC changes from exponential to linear
Chesler et al. 2015
;
Thudiyangal and del
Campo 2024
;
url will be inserted by publisher
,
is proportional to the freeze-out time and scales universally as
t_{eq}\propto\hat{t}\propto\tau_{Q}^{1/2}
. This scaling is confirmed by the numerical results shown in the inset of Fig.
1
(c), where
\langle t_{eq}\rangle=\left(3.843\pm 0.460\right)\tau_{Q}^{0.573\pm 0.028}
. At the equilibration time, the KZM further predicts that the average vortex density scales as
\langle n\rangle\propto\tau_{Q}^{-1/2}
, in agreement with the fit shown in Fig.
1
(c), where
\langle n\rangle=\left(225.140\pm 19.016\right)\tau_{Q}^{-0.502\pm 0.025}

\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{r})=|\Psi_{\mathcal{C}}(\mathbf{r})|\mathbf{v}(\mathbf{r})

E=\frac{m}{2}\int|\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{r})|^{2}\,d^{2}\mathbf{r}.

(2)

Using the canonical decomposition

(3)

It is particularly useful to express Eq. (

(4)

the Fourier transform of

Kolmogorov scaling of the energy spectrum in SQT.
The occurrence of SQT in the newborn BEC is diagnosed by the Kolmogorov scaling of the incompressible kinetic energy spectrum

E_{i}\left(k\right)

Tsubota et al. 2013
;
Neely et al. 2013
, which has been experimentally measured in 2D BECs
Zhao et al. 2025
.
The Kolmogorov scaling is originally derived for homogeneous isotropic turbulence where large eddies (with high Reynolds number) are continuously broken into smaller eddies (with small Reynolds number), and there exists a range of length scales (inertial range) where the details of energy injection and dissipation can be neglected and the eddies follow universal statistical properties
Frisch 1995
;
Barenghi et al. 2023
.
With the additional assumptions that, in the inertial range,
the change of the kinetic energy per unit time
is independent of
k
and the viscosity does not affect
E_{i}(k)
,
dimensional arguments imply a
k^{-5/3}
scaling of the incompressible kinetic energy spectrum in the inertial range, independent of spatial dimensionality
Vinen and Niemela 2002
;
Barenghi et al. 2023
.
This Kolmogorov scaling
is a direct reflection of the velocity-velocity autocorrelation function
Kolmogorov 1941
;
Frisch 1995
.
At higher momenta, the
inertial range
over which the Kolmogorov scaling extends is interrupted by a second power law,
E_{i}(k)\propto k^{-3}
, that reflects the short-range properties of the vortex core and is thus unrelated to turbulence
Bradley and Anderson 2012
;
Tanogami 2021
.
As the radius of the vortex core is of the order of the condensate healing length
\xi_{h}:=\sqrt{\hbar^{2}/2m\mu_{f}}

Ginzburg and Pitaevskii 1958
;
Pitaevskii and Stringari 2003
;
Tanogami 2021
, the inertial range lies below
k<2\pi/\xi_{h}
.
For moderated quenches in which the KZM describes the dependence of the vortex density on the quench time,
the Kolmogorov scaling is verified as shown in Fig.

(a) and (b).

Having established that the Kolmogorov scaling describes thermal quenches for two different values of

According to KZM, the kinetic energy associated with localization over a length scale set by the KZ correlation length

(5)

Naturally, other non-universal processes beyond KZM and its extensions, governing the evolution after the formation of the BEC, such as atom losses, vortex-antivortex annihilation and coarsening, are expected to suppress the Kolmogorov scaling and SQT.

Discussion and conclusion.
In summary, we have shown that superfluid formation triggered by a finite-time quench gives rise to SQT. To this end, we have established that the incompressible kinetic energy of the emergent nonequilibrium BEC exhibits Kolmogorov scaling with respect to the wavevector. Moreover, using the nonequilibrium correlation length predicted by KZM and the associated energy scale, we have shown that the Kolmogorov scaling admits a universal collapse
for different values of the quench time.
Our findings highlight the rich interplay between distinct yet complementary notions of universality: the universal scaling of energy spectra in turbulent flows and the universal defect dynamics across continuous phase transitions.

B.1
Two-dimensional stochastic projected Gross-Pitaevskii equation

The stochastic projected Gross-Pitaevskii equation (SPGPE) is a description based on
c
-field methods
P. B. Blakie, A. S. Bradley, M. J. Davis, R. J.
Ballagh and C. W. Gardiner 2008

\mathcal{C}
), which includes the low-lying, highly populated levels, and an incoherent region of high-energy, sparsely occupied states that act as a thermal reservoir. The evolution of
\mathcal{C}
is captured by a classical field
\Psi_{\mathcal{C}}
, which evolves according to

d{\Psi_{\mathcal{C}}}=\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{C}}\left[-(i+\gamma)\left(H_{sp}+g|\Psi_{\mathcal{C}}|^{2}-\mu(t)\right)\Psi_{\mathcal{C}}\,dt+d\eta\right],

(6)

where length, time, and temperature are measured in units of
l\coloneqq\sqrt{\hbar^{2}/mE_{sc}}
,
\hbar/E_{sc}
, and
E_{sc}/k_{B}
, respectively. Here,
E_{sc}
defines the energy scale.

H_{sp}=-(1/2)\nabla^{2}+V(\mathbf{r})
is the single-particle Hamiltonian, and its eigenfunctions
\phi_{n}(\mathbf{r})
serve as a basis to describe the
\mathcal{C}
region.
The noise term
d\eta
is a complex Wiener process, with an autocorrelation function proportional to the temperature
T

\langle d\eta(\mathbf{r},t)\rangle=0,\quad\langle d\eta(\mathbf{r},t)d\eta^{*}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime},t)\rangle=2T\gamma\,\delta_{\mathcal{C}}(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\,dt,

(7)

where
\delta_{\mathcal{C}}(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime})=\sum_{n\in\mathcal{C}}\phi_{n}(\mathbf{r})\phi^{*}_{n}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime})

is the delta function restricted to the low-energy subspace. This subspace is spanned by the set of eigenfunctions
\{\phi_{n}\}_{n\in\mathcal{C}}
with energies
\epsilon_{n}
below a properly chosen cut-off
\epsilon_{cut}
.
Finally,
\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{C}}
denotes the projector operator,
which restricts the dynamics to the low-energy subspace.
Specifically,

\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{C}}
acts on a generic field

by projecting it onto the
\mathcal{C}
region
P. B. Blakie, A. S. Bradley, M. J. Davis, R. J.
Ballagh and C. W. Gardiner 2008

\mathcal{P}_{C}f(\mathbf{r})=\sum_{n:\epsilon_{n}<\epsilon_{\text{cut}}}\phi_{n}(\mathbf{r})\int f(\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\phi^{*}_{n}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\,d^{2}\mathbf{r}^{\prime}.

(8)

Finally, the equilibration time is defined as
t_{eq}=t_{2}
.

B.2
Determination of the equilibration time

The equilibration time
t_{eq}
is identified by analyzing the time evolution of the norm of the order parameter,
N_{\mathcal{C}}=\int|\Psi_{\mathcal{C}}(\mathbf{r})|^{2}\,d^{2}\mathbf{r}
.
In the spirit of
Chesler et al. 2015
, we locate the global maximum of
d^{2}N_{\mathcal{C}}/dt^{2}
and identify the subsequent interval
(t_{1},t_{2})
where
d^{2}N_{\mathcal{C}}/dt^{2}\leq-\Delta
, with
\Delta
set to
10\%
of the maximum. We then take
t_{eq}=t_{2}
. The procedure is illustrated in Fig.
4
.

B.3
Compressible and incompressible energies

Given the weighted superfluid velocity
\mathbf{u}
, we outline the procedure to extract its irrotational and solenoidal components,
\mathbf{u}_{c}
and
\mathbf{u}_{i}
, respectively.

Using the Green’s function
Watanabe 2015
, any
two-dimensional vector field
\bm{V}\left(\bm{r}\right)
can be expressed as

\bm{V}\left(\bm{r}\right)=\int\bm{V}\left(\bm{r}^{\prime}\right)\delta\left(\bm{r}-\bm{r}^{\prime}\right)\,d^{2}\mathbf{r}^{\prime}=\nabla^{2}\left[\frac{1}{2\pi}\int\bm{V}\left(\bm{r}^{\prime}\right)\ln\left(\frac{\left|\bm{r}-\bm{r}^{\prime}\right|}{l}\right)\,d^{2}\mathbf{r}^{\prime}\right],

(9)

where
\delta\left(\bm{r}\right)
is the two-dimensional Dirac delta function.
Applying Eq. (
9
) to
\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{r})
yields
\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{r})=\nabla^{2}\mathbf{b}(\mathbf{r})
, where
\bm{b}\left(\bm{r}\right)\coloneqq\left(1/2\pi\right)\int\bm{u}\left(\bm{r}^{\prime}\right)\ln\left(\left|\bm{r}-\bm{r}^{\prime}\right|/l\right)\,d^{2}\mathbf{r}^{\prime}
.
Using the vector identity
\nabla\times\left(\nabla\times\bm{A}\right)=\nabla\left(\nabla\cdot\bm{A}\right)-\nabla^{2}\bm{A}
, we can thus decompose
\mathbf{u}
into:

\bm{u}_{c}\left(\bm{r},t\right)=\nabla\left[\nabla\cdot\bm{b}\left(\bm{r},t\right)\right],\quad\bm{u}_{i}\left(\bm{r},t\right)=-\nabla\times\left[\nabla\times\bm{b}\left(\bm{r},t\right)\right],

(10)

satisfying

\nabla\times\bm{u}_{c}\left(\bm{r},t\right)=0
and

\nabla\cdot\bm{u}_{i}\left(\bm{r},t\right)=0
.

In Fourier space, we have
\bm{\tilde{u}}(\mathbf{k})=-{k}^{2}\bm{\tilde{b}}(\mathbf{k})
, with
\bm{\tilde{b}}\left(\bm{k},t\right)=\left(1/2\pi\right)\int e^{-i\bm{k}\cdot\bm{r}}\bm{b}\left(\bm{r},t\right)\,d^{2}\mathbf{r}
.
Inserting this into Eq. (
10
) gives:

\bm{u}_{c}\left(\bm{r},t\right)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int\frac{\bm{k}}{k^{2}}\left[\bm{k}\cdot\bm{\tilde{u}}\left(\bm{k},t\right)\right]e^{i\bm{k}\cdot\bm{r}}\,d^{2}\mathbf{k},\quad\bm{u}_{i}\left(\bm{r},t\right)=-\frac{1}{2\pi}\int\frac{\bm{k}}{k^{2}}\times\left[\bm{k}\times\bm{\tilde{u}}\left(\bm{k},t\right)\right]e^{i\bm{k}\cdot\bm{r}}\,d^{2}\mathbf{k},

(11)

where
k\coloneqq\left|\bm{k}\right|
.
Thus, in momentum space, the compressible and incompressible components of
\mathbf{u}
read

\bm{\tilde{u}}_{c}\left(\bm{k},t\right)=\bm{e}_{k}\left[\bm{e}_{k}\cdot\bm{\tilde{u}}\left(\bm{k},t\right)\right]
, and

\bm{\tilde{u}}_{i}\left(\bm{k},t\right)=\bm{\tilde{u}}\left(\bm{k},t\right)-\bm{\tilde{u}}_{c}\left(\bm{k},t\right)
where
\bm{e}_{k}\coloneqq\bm{k}/k
.
Finally, the compressible and incompressible energy spectra are given by

E_{c}\left(k\right)=\frac{m}{2}k\int_{0}^{2\pi}\left[\bm{e}_{k}\cdot\bm{\tilde{u}}^{*}\left(\bm{k},t\right)\right]\left[\bm{e}_{k}\cdot\bm{\tilde{u}}\left(\bm{k},t\right)\right]\,d\varphi_{k},\quad E_{i}\left(k\right)=\frac{m}{2}k\int_{0}^{2\pi}\bm{\tilde{u}}^{*}\left(\bm{k},t\right)\cdot\bm{\tilde{u}}\left(\bm{k},t\right)\,d\varphi_{k}-E_{c}\left(k\right),

(12)

where
\varphi_{k}
is the azimuthal angle of
\bm{k}
, i.e.,
\bm{e}_{k}=\left(\cos\varphi_{k},\sin\varphi_{k}\right)
.

To numerically evaluate
E_{c,i}(k)
as given in Eq. (
12
),
we used the Julia package
FFTW.jl
, which provides an interface to the
FFTW3
library
Frigo and Johnson 2005
, allowing efficient computation of the fast Fourier transform to obtain
\bm{\tilde{u}}\left(\bm{k}\right)
.
To perform the angular integral over
\varphi_{k}
, we employed the quadrature method introduced in
P. B. Blakie, A. S. Bradley, M. J. Davis, R. J.
Ballagh and C. W. Gardiner 2008
.
Denoting by
k_{m}
the maximum wavenumber considered for evaluating
E_{i,c}(k)
, and by
N_{g}
the number of grid points along each spatial direction for a periodic system of size
L\times L
, we compute and plot
E_{c}(k)
and
E_{i}(k)
for different values of
N_{g}
in Fig.
5
.

Appendix C
Compressible kinetic energy and the universality

One may also expect to see the universality of
E_{c}(k)
for small
k
, assuming that phonon emission is dominant in that region.
The phonon energy spectrum is proportional to the speed of sound, and that speed is proportional to the square root of the chemical potential
Pitaevskii and Stringari 2003
. As we consider the system at the equilibration time, the chemical potential is proportional to
t_{eq}/\tau_{Q}\propto\tau_{Q}^{-1/2}
, leading to
E_{p}(k)\propto k\tau_{Q}^{-1/4}
where
E_{p}(k)
is the phonon energy spectrum. Fig.
6
(a) shows that the intercept of
\log_{10}E_{c}(k)
decreases as
\tau_{Q}
increases, which is expected from
E_{p}(k)
.
Next, we plot
E_{c}(k)\tau_{Q}^{1/4}
as a function of
k\hat{\xi}\propto k\tau_{Q}^{1/4}
as in Fig.
6
(b).
However, the curves do not collapse, implying that the simple picture using the phonon emission does not fully explain the behavior of
E_{c}(k)
.
