Iterative Tilting for Diffusion Fine-Tuning
Jean Pachebat, Giovanni Conforti, Alain Durmus, Yazid Janati, [0.8em] CMAP, École Polytechnique

Abstract
We introduce iterative tilting, a gradient-free method for fine-tuning diffusion models toward reward-tilted distributions. The method decomposes a large reward tilt exp ⁡ ( λ ​ r ) \exp(\lambda r) into N N sequential smaller tilts, each admitting a tractable score update via first-order Taylor expansion. This requires only forward evaluations of the reward function and avoids backpropagating through sampling chains. We validate on a two-dimensional Gaussian mixture with linear reward, where the exact tilted distribution is available in closed form.

Introduction

Diffusion models are now a standard tool for generative modelling in images, audio, and related domains
(
16
;
10
;
19
)
.
They learn a time-dependent score (or denoiser) that inverts a simple noising process and produces new samples from noise.
Following their success, it has been proposed to fine-tune pretrained diffusion models, taking into account new data preferences and/or constraints by simply shifting the learnt weights accordingly.

To achieve this, ideas from language-model alignment have recently started to be applied to diffusion:
Either shifting the pre-trained weights using methods based on reward models
(
24
)
or reinforcement learning policies
(
3
;
9
)
, or adapting the architecture using lightweight adapters such as LoRA
(
12
)
.
In addition, diffusion-specific techniques like classifier-free guidance
(
11
)
or improved noise schedulers
(
15
)
provide complementary control mechanisms.

In this paper we introduce
iterative tilting
, a gradient-free method for reward-based fine-tuning that shifts a diffusion model toward a reward-tilted density without differentiating through the reward function.
During the preparation of this work, a concurrent paper was submitted to ICLR
(
2
)
. Their method is essentially the same as ours but formulated using the stochastic interpolants framework and developed in continuous time. In contrast, our approach is based on a diffusion framework and is derived in discrete time.

Section
2
recalls the theoretical framework of Denoising Diffusion Models (DDM) and reviews existing reward fine-tuning formulations, Section
3
develops the Iterative Tilting method from a theoretical standpoint, and Section
4
reports controlled experiments on a Gaussian synthetic fine-tuning task, before we conclude.

2
Preliminaries on Denoising Diffusion Models and Fine-Tuning

2.1
Diffusion models

Denoising diffusion models (DDMs)
(
16
;
18
;
10
)
define a generative process for a data distribution
p_{0}
by constructing a continuous probability path
(p_{t})_{t\in[0,1]}
of distributions between
p_{0}
and a tractable reference
p_{1}:=\mathrm{N}(0,\mathrm{I}_{d})
.

2.1.1
Forward noising process

The path is defined via the marginals
p_{t}=\mathrm{Law}(X_{t})
, where

X_{t}=\alpha_{t}X_{0}+\sigma_{t}X_{1},\qquad X_{0}\sim p_{0},\quad X_{1}\sim\mathrm{N}(0,\mathrm{I}_{d}).

(2.1)

Here
X_{0}
and
X_{1}
are independent, and
(\alpha_{t})_{t\in[0,1]}
and
(\sigma_{t})_{t\in[0,1]}
are deterministic schedules such that
\alpha_{t}
is non-increasing,
\sigma_{t}
is non-decreasing, with boundary conditions
(\alpha_{0},\sigma_{0})=(1,0)
and
(\alpha_{1},\sigma_{1})=(0,1)
. The path
(p_{t})_{t\in[0,1]}
thus defines an interpolation that gradually transforms the clean data distribution
p_{0}
into the Gaussian reference
p_{1}
.

From (
2.1
), the conditional distribution of
X_{t}
given
X_{0}=x_{0}
, denoted
q_{t|0}
, is Gaussian:

q_{t|0}(x_{t}\mid x_{0})=\mathrm{N}(x_{t};\,\alpha_{t}x_{0},\,\sigma_{t}^{2}\mathrm{I}_{d}).

(2.2)

Regarding the choices of schedules
(\alpha_{t},\sigma_{t})
, two standard choices are the
variance-preserving (VP)
schedule with
\alpha_{t}^{2}+\sigma_{t}^{2}=1

(
10
;
5
)
, which ensures
\mathrm{Var}(X_{t})=\mathrm{Var}(X_{0})
when
X_{0}
has unit variance, and the
linear (flow matching)
schedule with
(\alpha_{t},\sigma_{t})=(1-t,t)

(
14
;
8
)
, corresponding to a straight-line interpolation between data and noise.

2.1.2
Reverse process and DDIM transitions

To generate new samples, DDMs simulate a time-reversed Markov chain. Given a increasing sequence
(t_{k})_{k=0}^{K}
of time steps with
t_{K}=1
and
t_{0}=0
, reverse transitions are iteratively applied to map a sample from
p_{t_{k+1}}
to one from
p_{t_{k}}
, progressively denoising until convergence to
p_{0}
.

DDIM framework.

The DDIM framework
(
17
)
introduces a general family of reverse transitions. It defines a schedule
(\eta_{t})_{t\in[0,1]}
satisfying
\eta_{t}\leqslant\sigma_{t}
for all
t\in[0,1]
, along with transition densities given for
s<t
by

p_{s|t}^{\eta}(x_{s}\mid x_{t})=\mathbb{E}\left[q_{s|0,1}^{\eta}(x_{s}\mid X_{0},X_{1})\,\middle|\,X_{t}=x_{t}\right],

(2.3)

where

q_{s|0,1}^{\eta}(x_{s}\mid x_{0},x_{1}):=\mathrm{N}\!\left(x_{s};\,\alpha_{s}x_{0}+\sqrt{\sigma_{s}^{2}-\eta_{s}^{2}}\,x_{1},\,\eta_{s}^{2}\mathrm{I}_{d}\right)

(2.4)

and the random variables
(X_{0},X_{t},X_{1})
are defined as in (
2.1
). By construction, this family satisfies the marginalisation property
p_{s}(x_{s})=\int p_{s|t}^{\eta}(x_{s}\mid x_{t})\,p_{t}(x_{t})\,\mathrm{d}x_{t}
: by the law of total expectation, the right-hand side equals
\mathbb{E}_{(X_{0},X_{1})}[q_{s|0,1}^{\eta}(x_{s}\mid X_{0},X_{1})]
, and since
X_{1}\sim\mathrm{N}(0,\mathrm{I}_{d})
is independent of
X_{0}
, sampling from the bridge (
2.4
) yields
X_{s}=\alpha_{s}X_{0}+\sqrt{\sigma_{s}^{2}-\eta_{s}^{2}}\,X_{1}+\eta_{s}\epsilon
with
\epsilon\sim\mathrm{N}(0,\mathrm{I}_{d})
; the sum
\sqrt{\sigma_{s}^{2}-\eta_{s}^{2}}\,X_{1}+\eta_{s}\epsilon
has variance
\sigma_{s}^{2}\mathrm{I}_{d}
, so
X_{s}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\alpha_{s}X_{0}+\sigma_{s}\tilde{X}_{1}
with
\tilde{X}_{1}\sim\mathrm{N}(0,\mathrm{I}_{d})
, matching the definition of
p_{s}
. This ensures that
(p_{t_{k}|t_{k+1}}^{\eta})_{k=0}^{K-1}
defines a consistent set of reverse transitions.

Denoiser.

The transitions (
2.3
) are intractable and have to be approximated.
To this end, it has been proposed to replace
X_{0}
and
X_{1}
by their conditional expectations given
X_{t}
. Let
\hat{x}_{0}^{\theta}(\cdot,t)
and
\hat{x}_{1}^{\theta}(\cdot,t)
denote parametric estimators of

\hat{x}_{0}(x_{t},t):=\mathbb{E}[X_{0}\mid X_{t}=x_{t}],\qquad\hat{x}_{1}(x_{t},t):=\mathbb{E}[X_{1}\mid X_{t}=x_{t}].

(2.5)

From the forward interpolation (
2.1
), these two quantities are related by
\hat{x}_{1}(x_{t},t)=(x_{t}-\alpha_{t}\hat{x}_{0}(x_{t},t))/\sigma_{t}
, yielding the parametric relationship

\hat{x}_{1}^{\theta}(x_{t},t)=\frac{x_{t}-\alpha_{t}\hat{x}_{0}^{\theta}(x_{t},t)}{\sigma_{t}},\qquad\hat{x}_{0}^{\theta}(x_{t},t)=\frac{x_{t}-\sigma_{t}\hat{x}_{1}^{\theta}(x_{t},t)}{\alpha_{t}}.

(2.6)

The parametric DDIM transition then becomes

p_{t_{k}|t_{k+1}}^{\eta,\theta}(x_{t_{k}}\mid x_{t_{k+1}}):=q_{t_{k}|0,1}^{\eta}\!\left(x_{t_{k}}\mid\hat{x}_{0}^{\theta}(x_{t_{k+1}},t_{k+1}),\,\hat{x}_{1}^{\theta}(x_{t_{k+1}},t_{k+1})\right).

(2.7)

For
k=0
, the transition
p_{0|t_{1}}^{\eta,\theta}(\cdot\mid x_{t_{1}})
is simply defined as the Dirac mass at
\hat{x}_{0}^{\theta}(x_{t_{1}},t_{1})
.

Sampling procedure.

Given timesteps
(t_{k})_{k=0}^{K}
with
t_{K}=1
and
t_{0}=0
, DDIM sampling is summarised in Algorithm

. Setting
\eta_{t}=0
yields deterministic sampling; setting
\eta_{t}=\sigma_{t}\sqrt{1-\alpha_{t}^{2}/\alpha_{s}^{2}}
(for the VP schedule) recovers the stochastic DDPM sampler
(
10
)
.

2.1.3
Score function and training

Score and denoiser relationship.

The
score
of the noised distribution is
\nabla\log p_{t}(\cdot)
. For the Gaussian forward kernel (
2.2
), the score admits a closed-form expression in terms of the denoiser. Exchanging expectation and gradient under standard regularity assumptions:

\nabla_{x_{t}}\log p_{t}(x_{t})=\mathbb{E}\left[\nabla_{x_{t}}\log q_{t|0}(x_{t}\mid X_{0})\,\middle|\,X_{t}=x_{t}\right]=-\frac{\hat{x}_{1}(x_{t},t)}{\sigma_{t}}.

(2.8)

Thus, the denoiser
\hat{x}_{1}^{\theta}
directly provides a score estimator
s_{\theta}(x_{t},t)=-\hat{x}_{1}^{\theta}(x_{t},t)/\sigma_{t}
, and conversely
\hat{x}_{1}^{\theta}(x_{t},t)=-\sigma_{t}s_{\theta}(x_{t},t)
.

Training objective.

The denoiser can be trained by regressing either
X_{0}
or
X_{1}
from the noised sample
X_{t}=\alpha_{t}X_{0}+\sigma_{t}X_{1}
. The
X_{0}
-prediction loss reads

\mathrm{L}_{X_{0}}(\theta)=\int_{0}^{1}\mathbb{E}_{X_{0}\sim p_{0},X_{1}\sim\mathrm{N}(0,\mathrm{I}_{d})}\left\|\hat{x}_{0}^{\theta}(\alpha_{t}X_{0}+\sigma_{t}X_{1},t)-X_{0}\right\|^{2}\mathrm{d}t,

(2.9)

while the
X_{1}
-prediction loss is

\mathrm{L}_{X_{1}}(\theta)=\int_{0}^{1}\mathbb{E}_{X_{0}\sim p_{0},X_{1}\sim\mathrm{N}(0,\mathrm{I}_{d})}\left\|\hat{x}_{1}^{\theta}(\alpha_{t}X_{0}+\sigma_{t}X_{1},t)-X_{1}\right\|^{2}\mathrm{d}t.

(2.10)

Since
\hat{x}_{1}^{\theta}=-\sigma_{t}s_{\theta}
by (
2.8
), the
X_{1}
-prediction loss is equivalent to denoising score matching
(
13
;
23
)
: minimising (
2.10
) amounts to learning the score
\nabla\log p_{t}
. In practice, the integral is approximated via Monte Carlo: sample
t\sim\mathrm{Unif}[0,1]
,
x_{0}\sim p_{0}
,
x_{1}\sim\mathrm{N}(0,\mathrm{I}_{d})
, form
x_{t}=\alpha_{t}x_{0}+\sigma_{t}x_{1}
, and regress either
x_{0}
or
x_{1}
.

2.1.4
Connection to SDEs

The interpolation (
2.1
) can be viewed as discretising a continuous-time process. Given a
noise schedule

\beta(t)>0
, consider the Itô SDE

\mathrm{d}X_{t}=-\tfrac{1}{2}\beta(t)X_{t}\,\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{\beta(t)}\,\mathrm{d}W_{t},\qquad X_{0}\sim p_{0},

(2.11)

where
(W_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
is a standard Brownian motion in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. For the VP schedule, setting
\alpha_{t}=\exp(-(1/2)\int_{0}^{t}\beta(s)\,\mathrm{d}s)
and
\sigma_{t}=\sqrt{1-\alpha_{t}^{2}}
, the marginal
\mathrm{Law}(X_{t})
coincides with
p_{t}
from (
2.1
). The time-reversed process satisfies
(
1
;
19
)

\mathrm{d}\bar{X}_{t}=\left[-\tfrac{1}{2}\beta(t)\bar{X}_{t}+\beta(t)\nabla_{x}\log p_{t}(\bar{X}_{t})\right]\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{\beta(t)}\,\mathrm{d}\bar{W}_{t},

(2.12)

where
\bar{X}_{t}:=X_{1-t}
and
\bar{W}_{t}
is a standard Brownian motion. The deterministic
probability-flow ODE

(
19
)
with identical marginals reads

\mathrm{d}x_{t}=\left[-\tfrac{1}{2}\beta(t)x_{t}+\tfrac{1}{2}\beta(t)\nabla_{x}\log p_{t}(x_{t})\right]\mathrm{d}t.

(2.13)

DDIM with
\eta_{t}=0
can be seen as a discretisation of (
2.13
), while DDPM corresponds to (
2.12
).

2.2
Fine-tuning formulations

Given a positive reward function
r
and a pretrained diffusion model sampling from the base distribution
p_{0}^{\mathrm{b}}
, we aim to fine-tune the model to sample from the
tilted distribution
, defined for
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
:

p_{0}^{\lambda r}(x)=Z_{\lambda r}^{-1}\exp\!\left(\lambda r(x)\right)p_{0}^{\mathrm{b}}(x),

(2.14)

where
\lambda>0
is the tilt strength and
Z_{\lambda r}=\int\exp(\lambda r(x))\,p_{0}^{\mathrm{b}}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x
is the normalising constant. This tilted distribution emphasises regions with high reward. Several formulations have been developed to tackle this goal.

Direct reward alignment (DRaFT).

DRaFT
(
4
)
treats the diffusion sampler as a differentiable computation graph.
For network parameters
\theta
, the reverse sampling process is a deterministic function of
\theta
and the i.i.d. Gaussian noises
\xi=(Z_{K},\ldots,Z_{1})
, denoted
(\theta,\xi)\mapsto\psi_{0}(\theta,\xi)
,
i.e.
,
\psi_{0}(\theta,\xi)=\hat{X}_{0}
where
\hat{X}_{0}
is the output of Algorithm
1
.

DRaFT maximises the expected reward:

J(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\left[r\!\left(\psi_{0}(\theta,\xi)\right)\right]

(2.15)

by backpropagating the reward gradient through the entire chain of reverse steps:

\nabla_{\theta}J(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\left[\left(\frac{\partial\psi_{0}(\theta,\xi)}{\partial\theta}\right)^{\top}\nabla_{x}r\!\left(\psi_{0}(\theta,\xi)\right)\right].

(2.16)

Full backpropagation is memory-intensive. DRaFT-
K
reduces cost by differentiating only through the final
K
steps (typically
K\in\{1,4\}
), detaching earlier gradients. DRaFT-LV further refines
K=1
with a control-variate correction for lower variance while remaining unbiased.

Stochastic optimal control and Adjoint Matching.

An alternative approach casts fine-tuning as stochastic optimal control (SOC)
(
22
;
21
;
6
)
. Rather than backpropagating through sampling (as in DRaFT), SOC methods add a drift control
u_{t}
to shift the generative process toward high-reward regions. For a controlled SDE
\mathrm{d}X_{t}=[b(X_{t},t)+g(t)u_{t}(X_{t})]\,\mathrm{d}t+g(t)\,\mathrm{d}W_{t}
, where
g(t)
denotes the diffusion coefficient (distinct from the noise schedule
\sigma_{t}
in (
2.1
)), the objective balances control effort against terminal reward:

\min_{u}\;\mathbb{E}\left[\tfrac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}\|u_{t}(X_{t})\|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t-\lambda\,r(X_{1})\right].

(2.17)

By Girsanov’s theorem, the quadratic control cost equals the KL divergence between controlled and base processes. However, naïvely solving (
2.17
) yields
p^{*}(X_{0},X_{1})\propto p^{\mathrm{base}}(X_{0},X_{1})\exp(\lambda r(X_{1})+V(X_{0},0))
, where
V
is the value function. This does not marginalize to the tilted distribution (
2.14
) unless the initial noise
X_{0}
and generated sample
X_{1}
are independent.

6
show that a
memoryless
diffusion coefficient, one that ensures
X_{0}\perp X_{1}
, removes the value function bias. Under this schedule, the optimal control is
u_{t}^{\star}(x)=g(t)^{\top}\tilde{a}_{t}(x)
, where the
lean adjoint

\tilde{a}_{t}
satisfies the backward ODE

\tfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\tilde{a}_{t}=-[\nabla_{x}b(x,t)]^{\top}\tilde{a}_{t},\qquad\tilde{a}_{1}=\lambda\nabla_{x}r(X_{1}).

(2.18)

Adjoint Matching learns a parametric control
u_{\phi}
by minimizing:

\mathrm{L}_{\mathrm{AM}}(\phi)=\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{1}\|u_{\phi}(X_{t},t)+g(t)^{\top}\tilde{a}_{t}(X_{t})\|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t\right].

(2.19)

After training, the learned control is absorbed into the drift, yielding a fine-tuned score or velocity field. Sampling can then use any diffusion coefficient, including deterministic sampling with
g(t)=0
.

3
Iterative Tilting Method for Fine-Tuning Diffusion Models

The methods reviewed in Section
2.2
share a common limitation: they require differentiating through the reward function
r
. In DRaFT, reward gradients appear explicitly in (
2.16
) via backpropagation through the sampling chain. In stochastic optimal control, they appear in the adjoint terminal condition
a_{0}=\lambda\nabla_{x}r(X_{0})
. When
r
is a large pretrained model (such as a vision transformer or human preference classifier trained on millions of examples), computing these gradients becomes prohibitively expensive, both in memory and compute. Moreover, many reward functions of practical interest are non-differentiable or defined only through black-box evaluations.

We introduce
iterative tilting
, a method that targets the reward-tilted distribution (
2.14
) using only forward evaluations of
r
, without any gradient computation. The key idea is to construct a path of intermediate distributions
(p_{0}^{k})_{k=0}^{N}
connecting the base
p_{0}^{\mathrm{b}}
to the tilted
p_{0}^{\lambda r}
, each representing a gentle reweighting of the previous one. At each step, we reweight samples from the current distribution toward higher rewards, gradually approaching the target along this path. This sequential approach trades off a single expensive gradient-based update for multiple cheaper gradient-free updates.

This section develops the method in continuous time, which makes the structure of the approximation explicit and connects naturally to the score-based and control perspectives introduced in Section
2
.

3.1
Score of the tilted distribution

Suppose we have a data distribution
X_{0}\sim p_{0}^{\mathrm{b}}
(the base distribution) and wish to sample from the tilted distribution (
2.14
). We use the forward noising kernel
q_{t|0}
from (
2.2
), whose score is available in closed form:

\nabla_{x_{t}}\log q_{t|0}(x_{t}\mid x_{0})=\frac{\alpha_{t}x_{0}-x_{t}}{\sigma_{t}^{2}}.

(3.1)

Noising the tilted distribution, the marginal at time
t
is:

p_{t}^{\lambda r}(x_{t})=\int q_{t|0}(x_{t}\mid x_{0})\,p_{0}^{\lambda r}(x_{0})\,\mathrm{d}x_{0}.

(3.2)

For any prior density
p_{0}^{\circ}
(e.g., base
p_{0}^{\mathrm{b}}
or tilted
p_{0}^{\lambda r}
) and the fixed forward noising kernel
q_{t|0}(x_{t}\mid x_{0})
, we write
p_{0|t}^{\circ}(x_{0}\mid x_{t})
for the posterior (inference) density of the clean sample
X_{0}
given its noised version
X_{t}=x_{t}
at time
t
:

p_{0|t}^{\circ}(x_{0}\mid x_{t})\propto p_{0}^{\circ}(x_{0})\,q_{t|0}(x_{t}\mid x_{0}).

(3.3)

Proposition 3.1

(Score of the tilted distribution)
.

Assume that
r
has at most linear growth, i.e.,
\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\;|r(x)|\leqslant C(1+\|x\|)
for some
C>0
, and that
q_{t|0}
is the Gaussian forward kernel (
2.2
). Then for all
t\in(0,1]
and
x_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\nabla_{x_{t}}\log p_{t}^{\lambda r}(x_{t})=\frac{\mathbb{E}_{X_{0}\sim p_{0|t}^{\mathrm{b}}(\cdot\mid x_{t})}\!\left[\nabla_{x_{t}}\log q_{t|0}(x_{t}\mid X_{0})\,\exp(\lambda r(X_{0}))\right]}{\mathbb{E}_{X_{0}\sim p_{0|t}^{\mathrm{b}}(\cdot\mid x_{t})}\!\left[\exp(\lambda r(X_{0}))\right]}.

(3.4)

Proof.

Exchanging differentiation and integration in (
3.2
) (justified by dominated convergence under the stated assumptions) yields

\nabla_{x_{t}}p_{t}^{\lambda r}(x_{t})=\int p_{0}^{\lambda r}(x_{0})\,\nabla_{x_{t}}q_{t|0}(x_{t}\mid x_{0})\,\mathrm{d}x_{0}.

(3.5)

Dividing by
p_{t}^{\lambda r}(x_{t})
and using
\nabla_{x_{t}}p_{t}^{\lambda r}(x_{t})=p_{t}^{\lambda r}(x_{t})\nabla_{x_{t}}\log p_{t}^{\lambda r}(x_{t})
, we obtain Fisher’s identity:

\nabla_{x_{t}}\log p_{t}^{\lambda r}(x_{t})=\mathbb{E}_{X_{0}\sim p_{0|t}^{\lambda r}(\cdot\mid x_{t})}\!\left[\nabla_{x_{t}}\log q_{t|0}(x_{t}\mid X_{0})\right].

(3.6)

The key observation is that the tilted posterior can be written as a reweighting of the base posterior:

\displaystyle p_{0|t}^{\lambda r}(x_{0}\mid x_{t})=\frac{p_{0}^{\lambda r}(x_{0})\,q_{t|0}(x_{t}\mid x_{0})}{p_{t}^{\lambda r}(x_{t})}

\displaystyle=\frac{Z_{\lambda r}^{-1}\exp(\lambda r(x_{0}))\,p_{0}^{\mathrm{b}}(x_{0})\,q_{t|0}(x_{t}\mid x_{0})}{\int p_{0}^{\lambda r}(x_{0}^{\prime})\,q_{t|0}(x_{t}\mid x_{0}^{\prime})\,\mathrm{d}x_{0}^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\exp(\lambda r(x_{0}))\,p_{0|t}^{\mathrm{b}}(x_{0}\mid x_{t})}{\mathbb{E}_{X_{0}^{\prime}\sim p_{0|t}^{\mathrm{b}}(\cdot\mid x_{t})}\!\left[\exp(\lambda r(X_{0}^{\prime}))\right]},

(3.7)

where in the last equality, the marginal
p_{t}^{\mathrm{b}}(x_{t})
cancels out. The tilted posterior
p_{0|t}^{\lambda r}(\cdot\mid x_{t})
is thus an exponential tilt of the base posterior
p_{0|t}^{\mathrm{b}}(\cdot\mid x_{t})
. Substituting (
3.7
) into (
3.6
) gives (
3.4
).
∎

Iterative tilts.

To efficiently approximate the score of the tilted distribution, we decompose the full tilt into a sequence of smaller tilts. Fix
N\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
(typically large) and define, for
k=0,\ldots,N
,

p_{0}^{k}(x):=Z_{k}^{-1}\exp\!\left(\tfrac{k}{N}\lambda r(x)\right)p_{0}^{\mathrm{b}}(x),

(3.8)

where
Z_{k}
is the normalising constant. We have
p_{0}^{0}=p_{0}^{\mathrm{b}}
and
p_{0}^{N}=p_{0}^{\lambda r}
. The key observation is that consecutive distributions are related by a small tilt:
p_{0}^{k}(x)\propto\exp(\lambda r(x)/N)\,p_{0}^{k-1}(x)
.

Corollary 3.2

(First-order score approximation)
.

Under the assumptions of Proposition
3.1
, for all
x_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
t\in(0,1]
, define the posterior
\pi_{k-1}(\cdot):=p_{0|t}^{k-1}(\cdot\mid x_{t})
and the covariance

C_{k-1}(x_{t},t):=\mathrm{Cov}_{X_{0}\sim\pi_{k-1}}\!\left(\nabla_{x_{t}}\log q_{t|0}(x_{t}\mid X_{0}),\,r(X_{0})\right).

(3.9)

Then, for large
N
,

\nabla_{x_{t}}\log p_{t}^{k}(x_{t})=\nabla_{x_{t}}\log p_{t}^{k-1}(x_{t})+\frac{\lambda}{N}\,C_{k-1}(x_{t},t)+O\!\left(\frac{1}{N^{2}}\right).

(3.10)

Proof.

Applying Proposition
3.1
with base
p_{0}^{k-1}
and tilt strength
\lambda/N
:

\nabla_{x_{t}}\log p_{t}^{k}(x_{t})=\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{k-1}}\!\left[g(X_{0})\,\exp(\tfrac{\lambda}{N}r(X_{0}))\right]}{\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{k-1}}\!\left[\exp(\tfrac{\lambda}{N}r(X_{0}))\right]},

(3.11)

where
g(X_{0}):=\nabla_{x_{t}}\log q_{t|0}(x_{t}\mid X_{0})
. Taylor-expanding
\exp(\lambda r/N)=1+\lambda r/N+O(N^{-2})
in both numerator and denominator:

Numerator

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{k-1}}[g]+\tfrac{\lambda}{N}\,\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{k-1}}[g\,r]+O(N^{-2}),

Denominator

\displaystyle=1+\tfrac{\lambda}{N}\,\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{k-1}}[r]+O(N^{-2}).

Dividing and keeping first-order terms yields
\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{k-1}}[g]+(\lambda/N)\,C_{k-1}(x_{t},t)+O(N^{-2})
. By Fisher’s identity,
\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{k-1}}[g]=\nabla_{x_{t}}\log p_{t}^{k-1}(x_{t})
.
∎

3.2
Neural approximation and algorithm

Corollary

3.2
motivates a recursive algorithm for estimating the sequence of score functions

\{\nabla_{x_{t}}\log p_{t}^{k}\}_{k=1}^{N}
starting from
k=0
, for
which
p_{t}^{0}=p^{\mathrm{b}}_{t}
. Suppose that at iteration

k
, we have access to an approximation for

\nabla_{x_{t}}\log p_{t}^{k}
, denoted by
s_{\theta^{k}}^{k}
. The resulting generative models associated to
s_{\theta^{k}}^{k}
is called the teacher at step
k
.

Corollary

3.2
provides a natural procedure to obtain an approximation for
\nabla_{x_{t}}\log p_{t}^{k+1}
: we train a new network
s_{\theta}^{k+1}
with weights
\theta
, so that it matches the target score from (
3.10
). The updated parameter
\theta^{k+1}
is thus obtained as an approximate minimizer of the loss

\mathrm{L}_{\mathrm{IT}}(\theta;k+1)=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{\mathcal{X}}w(t)\left\|s_{\theta}(x_{t},t)-\left(s_{\theta^{k}}^{k}(x_{t},t)+\frac{\lambda}{N}\,C_{k}(x_{t},t)\right)\right\|^{2}p_{t}^{k}(x_{t})\,\mathrm{d}x_{t}\,\mathrm{d}t\;.

(3.12)

In practice, we make three simplifications. First, we use a Monte Carlo estimate of the loss using a
teacher-generated dataset
\{X_{0}^{i}\}_{i+1}^{N}
at the beginning of each step
k
. Second, for
X_{t}^{i}\sim q_{t|0}(\cdot|X_{0}^{i})
,
the covariance
C_{k}(X_{t}^{i},t)
is approximated by a single-sample estimate
g^{i}=r(X_{0}^{i})(\nabla_{x_{t}}\log q_{t|0}(X_{t}^{i}\mid X_{0}^{i})-s_{\theta^{k}}(X_{t}^{i},t))
, where
\nabla_{x_{t}}\log q_{t|0}
is computed via (
3.1
). Finally, we multiply by
\sigma_{t}^{2}
to stabilize training across noise levels. This leads to the following empirical loss for a single sample time
t
and pair
(X_{0}^{i},X_{1}^{i})
: setting
\delta=\lambda/N
,

\mathrm{L}_{\mathrm{prac}}(\theta;k+1)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sigma_{t}^{2}\left\|s_{\theta}^{k+1}(X_{t}^{i},t)-\sigma_{t}^{2}\left(s_{\theta^{k}}^{k}(X_{t}^{i},t)+\delta\,r(X_{0}^{i})\left(g^{i}-s_{\theta^{k}}^{k}(X_{t}^{i},t)\right)\right)\right\|^{2}\;,

(3.13)

Finally, since we expect that
\nabla_{x_{t}}\log p_{t}^{k}
to be close to
\nabla_{x_{t}}\log p_{t}^{k+1}
, the score network at step
k+1
,
s_{\theta}^{k+1}
is initialized at
s_{\theta^{k}}^{k}
.

4
Experiments

In this section, we validate the iterative tilting method on a two-dimensional Gaussian mixture with a reward function. This setup has a key advantage: tilting a Gaussian (or mixture of Gaussians) with a quadratic reward (including linear as a special case) yields another Gaussian (or mixture), for which we can compute the exact tilted density and score analytically.

4.1
Gaussian data with quadratic rewards

One Gaussian.

Suppose the base density is Gaussian:

p^{\mathrm{b}}_{0}(x)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d/2}|\Sigma|^{1/2}}\exp\left\{-\tfrac{1}{2}(x-\mu)^{T}\Sigma^{-1}(x-\mu)\right\}=\mathrm{N}(x;\mu,\Sigma).

(4.1)

and the reward function
r
is quadratic, i.e.:

r(x)=\tfrac{1}{2}x^{T}Ax+b^{T}x+c.

(4.2)

Then the product
\exp(r(x))p^{\mathrm{b}}_{0}(x)
writes:

\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d/2}|\Sigma|^{1/2}}\exp\left\{-\tfrac{1}{2}(x-\mu)^{T}\Sigma^{-1}(x-\mu)+\left(\tfrac{1}{2}x^{T}Ax+b^{T}x+c\right)\right\}.

(4.3)

Focusing on the term in the exponent:

\displaystyle-\tfrac{1}{2}(x-\mu)^{T}\Sigma^{-1}(x-\mu)+\left(\tfrac{1}{2}x^{T}Ax+b^{T}x+c\right)

\displaystyle=-\tfrac{1}{2}\left[x^{T}(\Sigma^{-1}-A)x-2(\Sigma^{-1}\mu+b)^{T}x\right]+c-\tfrac{1}{2}\mu^{T}\Sigma^{-1}\mu.

(4.4)

With
\Sigma^{\prime}=(\Sigma^{-1}-A)^{-1}
and
\mu^{\prime}=\Sigma^{\prime}(\Sigma^{-1}\mu+b)
(we assume
\Sigma^{-1}-A
is positive definite so that the tilt is normalizable), the exponential can be rewritten as

\exp(r(x))p^{\mathrm{b}}_{0}(x)=\left(\frac{|\Sigma^{\prime}|}{|\Sigma|}\right)^{\!1/2}\exp\left\{\tfrac{1}{2}\big[\mu^{\prime T}\Sigma^{\prime-1}\mu^{\prime}-\mu^{T}\Sigma^{-1}\mu\big]+c\right\}\,\mathrm{N}(x;\mu^{\prime},\Sigma^{\prime}).

(4.5)

Consequently,

\int\exp(r(x))p^{\mathrm{b}}_{0}(x)\,dx=\left(\frac{|\Sigma^{\prime}|}{|\Sigma|}\right)^{\!1/2}\exp\left\{\tfrac{1}{2}\big[\mu^{\prime T}\Sigma^{\prime-1}\mu^{\prime}-\mu^{T}\Sigma^{-1}\mu\big]+c\right\}.

(4.6)

Mixture of Gaussians.

Suppose that we now have a mixture of Gaussians with
(\omega_{1},\ldots,\omega_{K})\in\Delta_{K-1}
where

\Delta_{K-1}=\{(\omega_{1},\ldots,\omega_{K})\in\mathbb{R}^{K}_{+}:\sum_{i\in[K]}\omega_{i}=1\}

(4.7)

is the simplex and
(\mu_{1},\Sigma_{1}),\ldots,(\mu_{K},\Sigma_{K})
corresponding means and covariances.

p^{\mathrm{b}}_{0}(x)=\sum_{i\in[K]}\omega_{i}\,\mathrm{N}(x;\mu_{i},\Sigma_{i}).

(4.8)

Then, we have:

\displaystyle\exp(r(x))p^{\mathrm{b}}_{0}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in[K]}\omega_{i}\,\exp(r(x))\,\mathrm{N}(x;\mu_{i},\Sigma_{i})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in[K]}\omega^{\prime}_{i}\,\mathrm{N}(x;\mu^{\prime}_{i},\Sigma^{\prime}_{i}),

(4.9)

where the parameters of the tilted Gaussians are

\left\{\begin{aligned} \Sigma^{\prime}_{i}&=(\Sigma_{i}^{-1}-A)^{-1},\\
\mu^{\prime}_{i}&=\Sigma^{\prime}_{i}(\Sigma_{i}^{-1}\mu_{i}+b),\\
\omega^{\prime}_{i}&=\omega_{i}\left(\frac{|\Sigma^{\prime}_{i}|}{|\Sigma_{i}|}\right)^{\!1/2}\exp\left\{\tfrac{1}{2}\big[\mu^{\prime}_{i}{}^{T}\Sigma^{\prime-1}_{i}\mu^{\prime}_{i}-\mu_{i}^{T}\Sigma_{i}^{-1}\mu_{i}\big]+c\right\}.\end{aligned}\right.

(4.10)

We consider the following mixture of two Gaussians in dimension
d=2
as base distribution:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}K=2\\
\mu_{1}=(-2,0);\,\mu_{2}=(2,0)\\
\Sigma_{1}=\Sigma_{2}=\sigma^{2}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{I}};\,\sigma^{2}=1/2\\
\omega_{1}=\omega_{2}=1/2\end{array}\right.

(4.11)

and we consider the linear reward
r(x)=b^{T}x
with
b=(0,4)
(corresponding to
A=0
,
c=0
in the general quadratic form).
In this case, the resulting distribution is a mixture of two Gaussians with parameters given by Section
4.1
:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}K^{\prime}=2\\
\mu^{\prime}_{1}=(-2,2);\,\mu^{\prime}_{2}=(2,2)\\
\Sigma^{\prime}_{1}=\Sigma^{\prime}_{2}=\sigma^{2}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{I}};\,\sigma^{2}=1/2\\
\omega^{\prime}_{1}=\omega^{\prime}_{2}=1/2.\end{array}\right.

(4.12)

5
Conclusion

This paper introduced iterative tilting, a gradient-free method for fine-tuning diffusion models toward reward-tilted distributions. The core idea is to decompose a single large tilt
\exp(\lambda r)
into
N
sequential tilts
\exp(\lambda r/N)
, each producing a tractable score update. At iteration
k
, the score is approximated by

\nabla_{x}\log p^{k}_{t}(x)\approx\nabla_{x}\log p^{k-1}_{t}(x)+\frac{\lambda}{N}\,\mathrm{Cov}_{X_{0}\sim p^{k-1}_{0|t}(\cdot\mid x)}\!\left(\nabla_{x}\log q_{t|0}(x\mid X_{0}),\,r(X_{0})\right),

valid for small
\lambda/N
via first-order Taylor expansion. Crucially, the method requires only forward evaluations of
r
, avoiding the cost of backpropagating through sampling chains or solving adjoint equations.

We validated the approach on a two-dimensional Gaussian mixture with linear reward, where the exact tilted distribution is available in closed form. The experiments confirmed that the method successfully recovers the target distribution for
N\in\{20,50,100,200\}
, with score error (RMSE) converging at each tilt and runtime scaling linearly with
N
.

Compared to DRaFT
(
4
)
and stochastic optimal control
(
7
)
, which both require differentiating through the reward, iterative tilting trades a single gradient-based update for a sequence of gradient-free updates. This makes it well suited to settings where
r
is a large pretrained model or is only available through black-box evaluations.

Several directions remain open: reducing variance in the single-sample covariance approximation, establishing principled criteria for selecting
N
, extending to high-dimensional domains with learned reward models, and combining with parameter-efficient methods such as LoRA
(
12
)
.
