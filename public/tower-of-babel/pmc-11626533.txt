Introduction

Solitary plasmacytoma of the breast is an exceedingly rare manifestation of plasma cell neoplasms, with only a handful of cases reported in the medical literature to date [[1], [2], [3], [4], [5], [6], [7], [8]]. Plasmacytomas typically originate in the bone marrow and are commonly associated with multiple myeloma, a systemic disease characterized by the proliferation of malignant plasma cells. However, when occurring as a solitary lesion, particularly in an unusual site such as the breast, diagnosis and management pose unique challenges for clinicians. Here, we present a case report of solitary plasmacytoma of the breast, discussing its clinical presentation, diagnostic workup, treatment strategies, and outcomes, along with a comprehensive review of the existing literature on this rare entity. This rarity poses significant diagnostic challenges, as its clinical and radiological features can mimic those of more common breast malignancies, necessitating a meticulous approach involving histopathological examination, immunohistochemistry, and advanced imaging modalities for accurate diagnosis. Additionally, the successful management of this case with surgical excision followed by adjuvant radiotherapy, resulting in no recurrence over a two-year follow-up period, provides valuable insights into effective treatment strategies for this uncommon condition.

Case presentation

A woman in her early 60s, presented to the Surgery outpatient department in 2022 with a chief complaint of an enlarged mass in her right breast for the last six months, which has been gradually increasing in size. There were no associated symptoms of pain, nipple discharge, skin changes, or axillary lymphadenopathy.

She had undergone regular mammographic screenings in the past, with the most recent one being two years ago, which showed no significant findings. There was no significant family history of breast cancer. Upon physical examination, a firm, non-tender, and mobile mass measuring approximately 20 × 18 cm in diameter was palpable in the upper outer quadrant of the right breast. There were no signs of skin dimpling, nipple retraction, or palpable axillary lymph nodes. The left breast examination was unremarkable.

A mammogram revealed a well-defined oval-shaped mass with circumscribed margins in the right breast. No microcalcifications or architectural distortions were noted. Computed tomography exhibited a large well-defined mass with enhancement raising the possibility of a breast neoplasm. A fine needle aspiration biopsy was done which exhibited sheets of mature and immature plasma cells. The immature forms displayed enlarged nuclei with an increased nucleo-cytoplasmic ratio. Few binucleate and multinucleate forms were also noted. A possibility of plasmacytoma was suggested [Fig. 1]. A core needle biopsy was done and simultaneously the patient was worked up for multiple myeloma. Complete blood counts with peripheral smear, serum calcium, serum electrophoresis, bone marrow biopsy, bone scan, renal function tests, urine analysis, and whole-body positron emission tomography-computed tomography (PET-CT) scan were unremarkable and ruled out the possibility of multiple myeloma. The peripheral smear did not show any rouleaux formation, while the bone marrow biopsy showed only 1–2 % plasma cells.

The patient was referred to an oncologist for further evaluation and management. Given the localized nature of the disease, the multidisciplinary team recommended a combination of surgical excision followed by adjuvant radiotherapy to achieve optimal local control. The patient was scheduled for surgery followed by close surveillance for disease recurrence or progression. The patient underwent surgical excision of the breast mass without any perioperative complications. Histopathological examination of the excised specimen confirmed the diagnosis of solitary plasmacytoma, with clear margins. Subsequently, she received adjuvant radiotherapy to the affected breast area (40 Gy in 20 fractions). Currently, the patient is on follow-up 3 times per year (with physical examination and complete blood count with peripheral smear, including serum and urine protein electrophoresis and skeletal survey) for two years with no signs of relapse.

Discussion

Solitary plasmacytoma of the breast is an exceptionally rare entity, with only a handful of cases reported in the contemporary medical literature [[1], [2], [3], [4], [5], [6], [7], [8]]. Our case presentation reported according to SCARE guidelines adds to this limited pool of knowledge, underlining the diagnostic and therapeutic complexities associated with this uncommon diagnosis [9].

Solitary plasmacytomas typically arise in the bone marrow and are frequently linked with multiple myeloma. The occurrence of a solitary lesion in the breast, without systemic involvement, is exceedingly rare, with only a handful of cases documented in the medical literature. This unusual presentation poses significant diagnostic challenges as it can mimic other more common benign or malignant breast conditions, making accurate diagnosis crucial for appropriate management. Diagnosing solitary plasmacytoma of the breast necessitates a broad approach encompassing histopathological examination, immunohistochemistry, and advanced imaging modalities. Diagnosing solitary plasmacytoma of the breast can be particularly challenging due to its histological similarity to several other tumors. Invasive ductal carcinoma (IDC), the most common type of breast cancer, which may display cellular architecture that can resemble that of plasmacytoma. However, these cells typically express epithelial markers such as cytokeratin and estrogen receptors, which are absent in plasmacytoma. Immunohistochemistry can help differentiate these entities by showing strong positivity for CD138 and CD38 in plasmacytoma cells, markers not present in IDC. Further, invasive lobular carcinoma (ILC) is an important differential for plasmacytoma due to plasmacytoid cells. ILC does not exhibit the cartwheel chromatin of plasma cells, while it shows presence of intracytoplasmic vacuoles in perinuclear distribution [10]. Another consideration for the differential diagnosis is solid papillary carcinoma, which can also exhibit plasmacytoid morphology. This tumor's characteristic solid papillary growth pattern, along with keratin positivity and neuroendocrine marker positivity, can aid in distinguishing it from plasmacytoma. Including solid papillary carcinoma as a differential diagnosis is essential, particularly given the histological overlap that may be present in complex breast lesions. Primary breast lymphoma, particularly diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL), is another important differential due presence of large, atypical cells similar to those found in plasmacytoma. Immunohistochemical staining is crucial in this differentiation, as lymphomas usually express leukocyte common antigen (LCA) and CD20.

Additionally, IgG4-related sclerosing disease should be considered, as it can present with a plasma cell-rich mass that might mimic plasmacytoma. This entity requires IgG and IgG4 staining, with diagnostic features including storiform fibrosis and obliterative phlebitis. These unique histological features, coupled with a high IgG4+/IgG+ plasma cell ratio, help to distinguish IgG4-related sclerosing disease from plasmacytoma, ensuring accurate diagnosis.

Metastatic melanoma can also present a diagnostic challenge, as it occasionally exhibits plasmacytoid morphology. However, immunostains for melanoma markers like S-100, HMB-45, and Melan-A can identify metastatic melanoma, which would be negative in plasmacytoma. Another potential mimicker is an extramedullary myeloid tumor, also known as granulocytic sarcoma, which can display similar histological features. These tumors express myeloid markers like myeloperoxidase (MPO) and CD117, which are not found in plasmacytoma. The plasma cells in plasmacytoma are characteristically positive for CD38 and CD138 with immunoglobulin light chain restriction, typically lambda or kappa. Additionally, chronic inflammatory conditions such as chronic mastitis, plasma cell mastitis or granulomatous mastitis can be confused with plasmacytoma due to the presence of numerous plasma cells. However, these inflammatory conditions lack the monoclonality that characterizes plasmacytomas. Clonality can be assessed by immunohistochemical staining for light chain restriction or by molecular techniques [1,2].

To accurately diagnose plasmacytoma and differentiate it from these mimickers, a comprehensive diagnostic approach is essential. This approach includes histopathological examination, immunohistochemical profiling, and advanced imaging techniques to rule out systemic involvement, such as multiple myeloma. The combination of these diagnostic tools allows for the correct identification of plasmacytoma and the exclusion of other similar-appearing tumors, ensuring appropriate and effective treatment.

Histologically, plasmacytomas are typified by the infiltration of monoclonal plasma cells, expressing markers such as CD138, CD38, and CD56. Our case corroborates these findings, with immunohistochemical analysis demonstrating robust positivity for CD138 and CD38, confirming the diagnosis [2].

Moreover, imaging plays a pivotal role in delineating disease extent and guiding therapeutic decisions. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and PET-CT serve as invaluable tools for detecting local recurrence and distant metastases. In our case, a CT scan revealed a well-defined mass with enhancement, consistent with imaging features observed in solitary plasmacytoma. Treatment strategies for solitary plasmacytoma of the breast range from surgical excision and radiotherapy to systemic therapies. While surgical resection may afford local control, radiotherapy remains the cornerstone of treatment, offering excellent disease control and preserving breast function. Additionally, systemic therapies, including chemotherapy and immunomodulatory agents, may be considered in cases of disease progression or relapse [3]. In our case, the patient underwent surgical resection followed by adjuvant radiotherapy, resulting in complete remission without evidence of recurrence during follow-up.

Prognostically, solitary plasmacytoma of the breast generally carries a favorable outlook, with a low risk of progression to multiple myeloma. However, evidence is limited due to the rarity of this neoplasm presenting in breast. Nonetheless, vigilant long-term surveillance is imperative due to the potential for local recurrence or distant metastases. Regular clinical assessments, imaging studies, and serum protein electrophoresis are recommended to monitor disease status and detect any signs of progression.

Very few cases of solitary plasmacytoma of the breast have been reported in literature. [[1], [2], [3], [4], [5], [6], [7], [8]] In this case, a 60-year-old female presented with a gradually enlarging breast mass, a symptom common to many breast pathologies. The mammographic and clinical findings were non-specific, emphasizing the need for a thorough diagnostic workup. Fine needle aspiration biopsy indicated a plasmacytoma, which was confirmed by core needle biopsy and extensive diagnostic tests that ruled out multiple myeloma. The immunohistochemistry results, showing CD38 and CD138 positivity with lambda restriction, were consistent with solitary plasmacytoma. This aligns with the findings in other reported cases, such as those by McCormack et al. and Shiva et al., where similar immunohistochemical markers were used to confirm the diagnosis [1,2].

Comparatively, other documented cases, such as the one by Cao et al., involved synchronous conditions like infiltrating ductal carcinoma, highlighting the heterogeneity in clinical presentations [6]. Taylor et al. reported a case involving axillary lymph node metastasis, underscoring the variability in disease progression and metastatic potential [7]. Dağlı et al. reported a case of bilateral breast plasmacytomas [8]. Our case, with a favorable outcome following surgical excision and adjuvant radiotherapy, supports the effectiveness of localized treatment strategies in managing solitary plasmacytoma of the breast. These reports underscore the heterogeneity in clinical presentation, diagnostic approaches, and treatment outcomes, underscoring the urgent need for further research to delineate standardized guidelines for managing this rare condition effectively.

This case report highlights several critical lessons. Firstly, it underscores the necessity of including solitary plasmacytoma in the differential diagnosis for breast masses, especially when initial diagnostic findings are inconclusive. Secondly, it demonstrates the importance of a multidisciplinary approach in diagnosis and management, utilizing histopathological examination, immunohistochemistry, and advanced imaging techniques to accurately identify and treat the condition. Thirdly, the favorable prognosis with no recurrence over a two-year follow-up period underscores the potential for successful management with localized treatment modalities, such as surgical excision followed by radiotherapy.

Conclusion

The novelty of this case lies in its rare presentation and the successful outcome achieved through meticulous diagnostic and therapeutic strategies. This case contributes to the limited literature on solitary plasmacytoma of the breast and emphasizes the importance of continued research and documentation to aid in diagnosis and inform standardized guidelines for managing this rare condition effectively.
