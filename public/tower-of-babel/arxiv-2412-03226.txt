Information thermodynamics for Markov jump processes coupled to underdamped diffusion: Application to nanoelectromechanics
Ashwin Gopal, Nahuel Freitas, Massimiliano Esposito

Abstract
We extend the principles of information thermodynamics to study energy and information exchanges between coupled systems composed of one part undergoing a Markov jump process and another underdamped diffusion. We derive integral fluctuation theorems for the partial entropy production of each subsystem and analyze two distinct regimes. First, when the inertial dynamics is slow compared to the discrete-state transitions, we show that the steady-state energy and information flows vanish at the leading order in an adiabatic approximation, if the underdamped subsystem is governed purely by conservative forces. To capture the non-zero contributions, we consistently derive dynamical equations valid to higher order. Second, in the limit of infinite mass, the underdamped dynamics becomes a deterministic Hamiltonian dynamics driving the jump processes, we capture the next-order correction beyond this limit. We apply our framework to study self-oscillations in the single-electron shuttle - a nanoelectromechanical system (NEMS) - from a measurement-feedback perspective. We find that energy flows dominate over information flows in the self-oscillating regime, and study the efficiency with which this NEMS converts electrical work into mechanical oscillations.

Introduction

Thermodynamic principles have been crucial for developing macroscopic engines that extract useful work from various nonequilibrium resources, and optimizing their designs. Such machines, usually operating at steady-state, consume a constant input of free energy which is converted from one form to another to carry out the desired function. The notion of information as a thermodynamic resource was first proposed by James Clerk Maxwell through his renowned thought experiment, the Maxwell demon

The apparent violation of the second law in Maxwell demons is caused by ignoring the entropic transfers between the demon and the system which modify the second law of the system while leaving its first law unaffected. Over the past two decades, stochastic thermodynamics has emerged as a natural framework for incorporating concepts from information theory - such as Shannon entropy and mutual information - into its formalism. Maxwell demons are analyzed as measurement-feedback systems, where the demon acts as a controller coupled to the system. It detects microscopic fluctuations, increasing the mutual information between itself and the system, and later utilizes this information to perform feedback on the system. Together, the system and the controller obey the second law, but the mutual information exchanged among them modifies their own local second laws. The controller produces mutual information that is then used as a resource by the system to perform useful tasks. This formalism has been extended beyond Maxwell demon setups to encompass more general coupled systems that exchange both energy and information between the system and the controller. II
General Model

We consider a composite system composed of two subsystems, namely
\mathbb{X}
and
\mathbb{Y}
. The subsystem
\mathbb{X}
is described by a continuous state space,
\vec{\Gamma}\equiv(x,v)

\mathbb{Y}
has discrete coarse-grained states

represents the number of molecules of the

[
65
]

denotes the number of charges in the

conductor
[
59
]
. This representation is general and can be applied to any system where the state can be described as a vector of discrete quantities.

The total energy of the system can be decomposed into contributions from the individual subsystems and an interaction term:

\displaystyle\hat{E}(\vec{\Gamma},y)=\frac{1}{2}mv^{2}+\hat{U}(x,y)=\frac{1}{2}mv^{2}+\hat{U}_{\mathbb{X}}(x)+\hat{U}_{\mathbb{Y}}(y)+\hat{U}_{\mathbb{XY}}(x,y),

(1)

where
\hat{U}(x,y)
represents the potential energy that depends on both subsystems, and is further decomposed into the potential energy
\hat{U}_{\mathbb{X}}(x)
of individual subsystems
\mathbb{X}(\mathbb{Y})
, and the interaction energy
\hat{U}_{\mathbb{XY}}(x,y)
between
\mathbb{X}
and
\mathbb{Y}
.

The bare internal energies of the individual subsystems
\mathbb{X}
and
\mathbb{Y}
are then given by:

\displaystyle\hat{E}_{\mathbb{X}}(\vec{\Gamma})=\frac{1}{2}mv^{2}+\hat{U}_{\mathbb{X}}(x),\quad\quad\hat{E}_{\mathbb{Y}}(y)=\hat{U}_{\mathbb{Y}}(y).

(2)

II.1
Langevin Dynamics

At the trajectory level, the stochastic dynamics of the composite system can be described by coupled Langevin dynamics. The stochastic dynamics for the subsystem
\mathbb{X}
is modeled by underdamped diffusive (UD) dynamics. The position
x_{t}
and the velocity
v_{t}
of subsystem
\mathbb{X}
satisfy the following Langevin dynamics:

\displaystyle dx_{t}

\displaystyle=v_{t}dt,

(3)

\displaystyle m\>dv_{t}

\displaystyle=\left[-\partial_{x}\hat{U}(x_{t},y_{t})-\gamma v_{t}+g(x_{t},y_{t})\right]dt+\sqrt{2\gamma\beta_{\mathbb{X}}^{-1}}\,.\,dB_{t},

where

is a non-conservative force,
\gamma
is the damping coefficient and

is the thermal Gaussian noise with zero mean and autocorrelation function
\langle dB_{t}dB_{t^{\prime}}\rangle=dt\>\delta(t-t^{\prime})
.

Similarly, the Langevin dynamics in the discrete state space is modeled as Markov jump (MJ) processes. The corresponding stochastic trajectory can be obtained by solving the following equations:

\displaystyle dy_{t}=\sum_{\rho,y^{\prime}}(y^{\prime}-y_{t})\>d\mathcal{N}_{\rho}^{y^{\prime},y_{t}}(x_{t}),

(4)

where
d\mathcal{N}_{\rho}^{y^{\prime},y}(x)\in\{0,1\}
is a Poisson random variable with mean
\langle d\mathcal{N}_{\rho}^{y^{\prime},y}(x)\rangle=\lambda_{\rho}^{y^{\prime},y}(x)dt
starting from
y
and ending at
y^{\prime}
. Note that here we assume that the jump rates
\lambda_{\rho}^{y^{\prime},y}(x)
only depend on the position of the underdamped particle, as observed in specific example systems of
[
62
,
60
,
66
]
. Moreover, we consider the ideal case with only one jump occurring at any time.
Since the jumps between the states are mediated by the reservoir
\rho
of the discrete subsystem
\mathbb{Y}
, each transition

needs to have a reverse transition
y^{\prime}\to y
with it corresponding rates
\lambda_{\rho}^{y,y^{\prime}}(x)
, to satisfy the microscopic reversibility. In addition, these bidirectional processes also satisfy the local detailed balance condition (LDB) for thermodynamic consistency. The LDB associated with the process,
(y^{\prime}\xrightarrow{\rho}y)
, for any given state
\vec{\Gamma}
of
\mathbb{X}
, is given as

\displaystyle\log\frac{\lambda_{\rho}^{y,y^{\prime}}(x)}{\lambda_{\rho}^{y^{\prime},y}(x)}=-\beta_{\mathbb{Y}}\left\{\left[\hat{\phi}_{\mathbb{Y}}(\vec{\Gamma},y)-\hat{\phi}_{\mathbb{Y}}(\vec{\Gamma},y^{\prime})\right]-w^{y^{\prime}\to y}_{\rho}(x)\right\},

(5)

where
\hat{\phi}_{\mathbb{Y}}(\vec{\Gamma},y)=\hat{E}(\vec{\Gamma},y)-T_{\mathbb{Y}}s_{i}(y)
is the Helmholtz free energy of state
(\vec{\Gamma},y)
associated with the thermal reservoir at the inverse temperature
\beta_{\mathbb{Y}}=(k_{B}T_{\mathbb{Y}})^{-1}
, and
s_{i}(y)
is the internal entropy for each discrete state
y
. We thus assumed that the sole contribution to the internal entropy changes is due to the discrete state. The reservoir
\rho
also performs non-conservative work
w^{y^{\prime}\to y}_{\rho}(x)=-w^{y\to y^{\prime}}_{\rho}(x)
in the transition
y^{\prime}\to y
. Examples of non-conservative work by the reservoir
\rho
include the work done by its chemical or electrical potential
[
67
,
59
]
. Therefore, the transition rates, in general, can be written as
\lambda_{\rho}^{y,y^{\prime}}(x)=\Lambda^{y,y^{\prime}}(x)\exp\left({\frac{-\beta_{\mathbb{Y}}\left\{\left[\hat{\phi}_{\mathbb{Y}}(\vec{\Gamma},y)-\hat{\phi}_{\mathbb{Y}}(\vec{\Gamma},y^{\prime})\right]-w^{y^{\prime}\to y}_{\rho}(x)\right\}}{2}}\right)
, where
\Lambda^{y,y^{\prime}}(x)=\sqrt{\lambda_{\rho}^{y,y^{\prime}}\lambda_{\rho}^{y^{\prime},y}}
is the symmetric (kinetic) part of the rates, which may also depend on the position
x
.

II.2
Master Equation

At the ensemble level, the dynamics of the probability density
P_{t}(\vec{\Gamma},y)
is described through a master equation, given as:

\displaystyle d_{t}P_{t}(\vec{\Gamma},y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\rho}\sum_{y^{\prime}}J_{\rho}^{y,y^{\prime}}(\vec{\Gamma})-\nabla.\;\vec{J}_{\vec{\Gamma}}^{y},

(6)

where

is the probability current due to the jumps associated with
\rho

is probability current associated with the UD dynamics. For the Langevin dynamics described by Eq. (
3
) and Eq. (
4

\displaystyle J_{\rho}^{y,y^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lambda_{\rho}^{y,y^{\prime}}(x)P_{t}(\vec{\Gamma},y^{\prime})-\lambda_{\rho}^{y^{\prime},y}(x)P_{t}(\vec{\Gamma},y),

(7)

\displaystyle\vec{J}_{\vec{\Gamma}}^{y}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\vec{\mathcal{L}}^{\rm{det}}+\vec{\mathcal{L}}^{\rm{diss}}\right)P_{t}(\vec{\Gamma},y),

where the underdamped probability current is further split into deterministic and dissipative contributions, such that:

\displaystyle\vec{\mathcal{L}}^{\rm{det}}\equiv\begin{bmatrix}v\\
\frac{1}{m}\left[-\partial_{x}\hat{U}(x,y)+g(x,y)\right]\end{bmatrix},\;\;\;\vec{\mathcal{L}}^{\rm{diss}}\equiv\begin{bmatrix}0\\

For the ease of notation in the following section, we will identify the independent force

III
Thermodynamics of the composite system

III.1
1st Law

The first law of thermodynamics describes the balance of energy flows in the system. At the trajectory level, the change in total energy
\hat{E}(\vec{\Gamma},y)

1

at time

\displaystyle d\hat{E}

\displaystyle=-\gamma[v^{2}_{t}+(\beta_{\mathbb{X}}m)^{-1}]dt-\sqrt{2\gamma\beta_{\mathbb{X}}^{-1}}v_{t}\>.\>dB_{t}+g(x_{t},y_{t})\>.\>dx_{t}+\sum_{\rho,y^{\prime}}\left[\hat{E}(\vec{\Gamma}_{t},y^{\prime})-\hat{E}(\vec{\Gamma}_{t},y_{t})\right].\>dN^{y^{\prime},y_{t}}_{\rho}(\vec{\Gamma}_{t})

In the last step, we distinguish the different energy flows into the system at the trajectory level. The work done on the system by the reservoir
\rho

At the average level, the changes in energy of the system can be computed by taking the ensemble average of Eq. (

\displaystyle d_{t}E=\underbrace{\vphantom{\sum_{\rho}}\dot{Q}_{\mathbb{X}}+\dot{W}_{\mathbb{X}}}_{d_{t}E_{\mathbb{X}}+\dot{\mathcal{E}}^{\mathbb{X}}}+\underbrace{\sum_{\rho}\left[\dot{Q}_{\rho}+\dot{W}_{\rho}\right]}_{d_{t}E_{\mathbb{Y}}+\dot{\mathcal{E}}^{\mathbb{Y}}}.

\displaystyle\dot{Q}_{\mathbb{X}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\gamma\left[\langle v^{2}\rangle-\frac{1}{m\beta_{\mathbb{X}}}\right],

\displaystyle\dot{W}_{\mathbb{X}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\SumInt P_{t}(\vec{\Gamma},y)\>v\>g(x,y).

Similarly, the average heat

\displaystyle\dot{Q}_{\rho}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{y>y^{\prime}}\int d\vec{\Gamma}\>J^{y,y^{\prime}}_{\rho}(\vec{\Gamma},t)\left\{\left[U(x,y)-U(x,y^{\prime})\right]-w^{y^{\prime}\to y}_{\rho}(x)\right\},

\displaystyle\dot{W}_{\rho}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{y>y^{\prime}}\int d\vec{\Gamma}\>J^{y,y^{\prime}}_{\rho}(\vec{\Gamma},t)\>w_{\rho}^{y^{\prime}\to y}(x).

III.2
2nd Law

In this section, we will focus on entropic changes and their implications on thermodynamics. At the trajectory level, the nonequilibrium system entropy at any given time
t
given as:

\displaystyle\hat{s}=s_{i}(y_{t})-k_{B}\log P_{t}(\vec{\Gamma}_{t},y_{t}),

(35)

which accounts for the internal entropy
s_{i}(y)
and the uncertainty
-k_{B}\log P_{t}(\vec{\Gamma},y)
at a given state. This quantity depends on both the given trajectory and also on the whole ensemble due to its dependence on the instantaneous probability density
P_{t}(\vec{\Gamma}_{t},y_{t})

\hat{s}(t)
and the entropy associated with the reservoirs. For equilibrated baths, the change in reservoir entropy is given by the heat exchanged divided by its temperature,
-\delta\hat{Q}/T

\displaystyle\delta\hat{\sigma}_{\rm{tot}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle d\hat{s}-\frac{\delta\hat{Q}_{\mathbb{X}}}{T_{\mathbb{X}}}-\sum_{\rho}\frac{\delta\hat{Q}_{\rho}}{T_{\mathbb{Y}}}

(36)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\underbrace{d\hat{s}_{\mathbb{X}}-\frac{\delta\hat{Q}_{\mathbb{X}}}{T_{\mathbb{X}}}}_{\delta\hat{\sigma}_{\mathbb{X}}}+\underbrace{d\hat{s}_{\mathbb{Y}}-\frac{\sum_{\rho}\delta\hat{Q}_{\rho}}{T_{\mathbb{Y}}}}_{\delta\hat{\sigma}_{\mathbb{Y}}}.

(37)

In the above expression, the instantaneous entropy production
\delta\hat{\sigma}_{\rm{tot}}
is divided to partial contributions,
\delta\hat{\sigma}_{\mathbb{X}}
and
\delta\hat{\sigma}_{\mathbb{Y}}
, due to the dynamics in subsystems
\mathbb{X}
and
\mathbb{Y}
respectively. This quantity satisfies an integrated fluctuation theorem, as shown in Sec.

. Similarly, we also identify

as the time-variation of the system entropy

exclusively due to the
\mathbb{X}(\mathbb{Y})

\displaystyle d\hat{s}_{\mathbb{X}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-k_{B}\nabla\log P_{t}(\vec{\Gamma}_{t},y_{t})\>.\>d\vec{\Gamma}_{t}+k_{B}\>\frac{\nabla\>.\>\vec{J}^{y}_{\vec{\Gamma}}(t)}{P_{t}(\vec{\Gamma}_{t},y_{t})}dt,

(38)

\displaystyle d\hat{s}_{\mathbb{Y}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[s_{i}(y^{\prime})-s_{i}(y_{t})-k_{B}\log\frac{P_{t}(\vec{\Gamma}_{t},y^{\prime})}{P_{t}(\vec{\Gamma}_{t},y_{t})}\right]\>.\>dN^{y^{\prime},y_{t}}_{\rho}(x_{t},t)-k_{B}\left[\sum_{\rho,y^{\prime}}\frac{J^{y_{t},y^{\prime}}_{\rho}(\vec{\Gamma}_{t},t)}{P_{t}(\vec{\Gamma}_{t},y_{t})}\right]dt.

(39)

Now moving to the average description, the nonequilibrium system entropy of the composite system, is given as:

\displaystyle S(t)=\SumInt P_{t}(\vec{\Gamma},y)[s_{i}(y)-k_{B}\log P_{t}(\vec{\Gamma},y)].

can be obtained by taking the average over Eq. (

\displaystyle\dot{\sigma}_{\rm{tot}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle d_{t}S-\frac{\dot{Q}_{\mathbb{X}}}{T_{\mathbb{X}}}-\sum_{\rho}\frac{\dot{Q}_{\rho}}{T_{\mathbb{Y}}}

(49)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\underbrace{\vphantom{\sum_{\rho}}\frac{\gamma}{T_{\mathbb{X}}}\SumInt P_{t}(\vec{\Gamma},y)\left[v+(m\beta_{\mathbb{X}})^{-1}\partial_{v}\log P_{t}(\vec{\Gamma},y)\right]^{2}}_{\dot{\sigma}_{\mathbb{X}}\geq 0}+\underbrace{\sum_{\rho}\int d\vec{\Gamma}\sum_{y>y^{\prime}}J^{y,y^{\prime}}_{\rho}(\vec{\Gamma},t)\log\frac{\lambda_{\rho}^{y,y^{\prime}}(x)P_{t}(\vec{\Gamma},y^{\prime})}{\lambda_{\rho}^{y^{\prime},y}(x)P_{t}(\vec{\Gamma},y)}}_{\dot{\sigma}_{\mathbb{Y}}\geq 0}\geq 0.

This quantity is zero only when the system is at equilibrium, such that the dynamics is detailed balanced with zero irreversible fluxes. In the average description, the partial entropy productions
\dot{\sigma}_{\mathbb{X}/\mathbb{Y}}
due to individual subsystems
\mathbb{X}/\mathbb{Y}
are themselves non-negative. Combining it with the first law (Eq. (
12
)), we can rewrite the second law as follows:

\displaystyle\dot{\sigma}_{\rm{tot}}=\left[d_{t}S-\beta_{\mathbb{Y}}\;d_{t}E\right]+\beta_{\mathbb{Y}}\left[\dot{Q}_{\mathbb{X}}\Big(1-\frac{\beta_{\mathbb{X}}}{\beta_{\mathbb{Y}}}\Big)+\dot{W}_{\rm{tot}}\right]\geq 0,

(59)

where the total work done on the system is,
\dot{W}_{\rm{tot}}=\dot{W}_{\mathbb{X}}+\sum_{\rho}\dot{W}_{\rho}\equiv\dot{W}_{\mathbb{X}}+\dot{W}_{\mathbb{Y}}
. In the steady-state, since the entropy and the average energy do not change, i.e.
d_{t}S=d_{t}E=0
, the second law imposes the following bound on its working:

\displaystyle\dot{Q}_{\mathbb{X}}\Big(1-\frac{\beta_{\mathbb{X}}}{\beta_{\mathbb{Y}}}\Big)+\dot{W}_{\rm{tot}}\geq 0.

(60)

Hence, for non-isothermal conditions with
\beta_{\mathbb{X}}>\beta_{\mathbb{Y}}
, we recover the Carnot efficiency for converting heat into work, i.e
-\dot{W}_{\rm{tot}}/\dot{Q}_{\mathbb{X}}\leq[1-(\beta_{\mathbb{X}}/\beta_{\mathbb{Y}})]
. In an isothermal setting (
\beta_{\mathbb{X}}=\beta_{\mathbb{Y}})
, the expression is simplified and the efficiency of a work transducer is upper bounded by 1, i.e.
\eta_{T}=-\dot{W}_{\mathbb{X}}/\dot{W}_{\mathbb{Y}}\leq 1
.

III.3
Mutual Information and information flows

In addition to the internal energy flows, there are also continuous information flows between the subsystems that create and / or destroy correlations between the two subsystems. The measure of correlations at any time
t
can be tracked by mutual information. At the trajectory level, the instantaneous mutual information
\hat{i}_{t}
, is given as:

\displaystyle\hat{i}(\Gamma_{t}:y_{t})=k_{B}\log\frac{\mathbb{P}_{t}(\Gamma_{t}|y_{t})}{P_{t}^{\mathbb{X}}(\Gamma_{t})}=k_{B}\log\frac{\mathbb{P}_{t}(y_{t}|\Gamma_{t})}{P_{t}^{\mathbb{Y}}(y_{t})}.

(61)

This quantity measures the distance between the conditional distribution and its corresponding marginal distribution and satisfies the following equality
\hat{i}=\hat{s}_{\mathbb{X}}^{\text{marg}}(t)+\hat{s}_{\mathbb{Y}}^{\text{marg}}(t)-\hat{s}(t)
. Here,
\hat{s}_{\mathbb{X}}^{\text{marg}}=-k_{B}\log{P_{t}^{\mathbb{X}}(\vec{\Gamma})}
is the marginalized system entropy for the subsystem
\mathbb{X}
, and
\hat{s}_{\mathbb{Y}}^{\text{marg}}=[s_{i}(y)-k_{B}\log{P_{t}^{\mathbb{Y}}(y)}]
is the marginalized system entropy for the subsystem
\mathbb{Y}
. Similar to the other thermodynamic quantities, it time variation can also be divided into two contributions due to the dynamics in
\mathbb{X}
and
\mathbb{Y}
, given as:

\displaystyle d\hat{i}^{\mathbb{X}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle d\hat{s}_{\mathbb{X}}^{\text{marg}}(t)-d\hat{s}_{\mathbb{X}}(t)

(62)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle k_{B}\left[\nabla\log P_{t}(\vec{\Gamma}_{t},y_{t})-\nabla\log P^{\mathbb{X}}_{t}(\vec{\Gamma}_{t})\right]\>.\>d\vec{\Gamma}_{t}+k_{B}\>\frac{\partial_{v}\left[f_{\mathbb{XY}}(x_{t},y_{t})P_{t}(\vec{\Gamma}_{t},y_{t})\right]}{P_{t}(\vec{\Gamma}_{t},y_{t})}-k_{B}\>\frac{\partial_{v}\left[\langle f_{\mathbb{XY}}(\Gamma_{t})\rangle_{y}P^{\mathbb{X}}_{t}(\vec{\Gamma}_{t})\right]}{P^{\mathbb{X}}_{t}(\vec{\Gamma}_{t})}dt,

\displaystyle d\hat{i}^{\mathbb{Y}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle d\hat{s}_{\mathbb{Y}}^{\text{marg}}(t)-d\hat{s}_{\mathbb{Y}}(t)

(63)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[\hat{i}_{t}(\Gamma_{t}:y^{\prime})-\hat{i}(\Gamma_{t}:y)\right].dN_{\rho}^{y^{\prime},y}(x_{t},t)+k_{B}\sum_{\rho,y^{\prime}}\frac{J^{y_{t},y^{\prime}}_{\rho}(\vec{\Gamma}_{t},t)}{P_{t}(\vec{\Gamma}_{t},y_{t})}-k_{B}\sum_{\rho,y^{\prime}}\int d\vec{\Gamma}\frac{J^{y_{t},y^{\prime}}_{\rho}(\vec{\Gamma},t)}{P^{\mathbb{Y}}_{t}(y_{t})},

(64)

where
\langle f_{\mathbb{XY}}(\Gamma_{t})\rangle_{y}=\sum_{y}f_{\mathbb{XY}}(x_{t},y)\mathbb{P}_{t}(y|\Gamma_{t})
is the average interactive force that the underdamped particle experiences.

In the average description, the mutual information becomes the Kullback–Leibler divergence between the joint probability density
P_{t}(\vec{\Gamma},y)
and the product of marginal densities
P_{t}^{\mathbb{X}}(\vec{\Gamma})\otimes P_{t}^{\text{e}}(y)
, given as

\displaystyle I(t)\equiv I(\vec{\Gamma}:y;t)=k_{B}\SumInt P_{t}(\vec{\Gamma},y)\log\frac{P_{t}(\vec{\Gamma},y)}{P^{\mathbb{{X}}}_{t}(\vec{\Gamma})P^{\mathbb{Y}}_{t}(y)}.

This quantity is positive (as
\log P\leq P-1
) and is the non-additive contribution to the Shannon entropy
S(t)
of the composite system, i.e.
I(t)=S_{\mathbb{X}}^{\text{marg}}(t)+S_{\mathbb{Y}}^{\text{marg}}(t)-S(t)
, where
S_{\mathbb{X}(\mathbb{Y})}^{\text{marg}}=\langle\hat{s}_{\mathbb{X}(\mathbb{Y})}^{\text{marg}}\rangle
are the marginal Shannon entropy. By differentiating Eq. (
III.3
) with respect to time, we identify the information flows
\dot{\mathcal{I}}^{\mathbb{X}(\mathbb{Y})}
due to the dynamics in the subsystem
\mathbb{X}(\mathbb{Y})
:

\displaystyle\dot{\mathcal{I}}^{\mathbb{X}}

\displaystyle=\frac{k_{B}}{m}\int d\vec{\Gamma}\;P_{t}^{\mathbb{X}}(\vec{\Gamma})\sum_{y}\left\{f_{\mathbb{XY}}(x,y)\partial_{v}\mathbb{P}_{t}(y|\vec{\Gamma})-\left(\frac{\gamma}{\beta m}\right)\mathbb{P}_{t}(y|\vec{\Gamma})\left[\partial_{v}\log\mathbb{P}_{t}(y|\vec{\Gamma})\right]^{2}\right\},

(74)

\displaystyle\dot{\mathcal{I}}^{\mathbb{Y}}

\displaystyle=k_{B}\sum_{\rho}\sum_{y\geq y^{\prime}}\int d\vec{\Gamma}\;J_{\rho}^{y,y^{\prime}}(\vec{\Gamma})\log\frac{\mathbb{P}_{t}(\vec{\Gamma}|y)}{\mathbb{P}_{t}(\vec{\Gamma}|y^{\prime})},

(75)

which satisfies
d_{t}I=\dot{\mathcal{I}}^{\mathbb{X}}+\dot{\mathcal{I}}^{\mathbb{Y}}
. The information flow
\dot{\mathcal{I}}^{\mathbb{Y}(\mathbb{X})}
is the rate of increase in mutual information
I(t)
exclusively due to the dynamics of
\mathbb{Y}(\mathbb{X})
. It provides a qualitative insight into the measurement-feedback mechanism in such autonomous systems. For example, a positive
\dot{\mathcal{I}}^{\mathbb{X}}
implies that the UD dynamics in
\mathbb{X}
at a fixed state
y
increases correlations on average and is equivalent to a measurement process. Therefore, such dynamics can be interpreted as the subsystem
\mathbb{Y}
transmitting information to the subsystem
\mathbb{X}
or the subsystem
\mathbb{X}
learning about the subsystem
\mathbb{Y}
. In general, the magnitudes of the information flows at a given time
t
may differ, that is,
|\dot{\mathcal{I}}^{\mathbb{X}}|\neq|\dot{\mathcal{I}}^{\mathbb{Y}}|
. However, in the stationary state, the mutual information converges to a constant, such that
d_{t}I=\dot{\mathcal{I}}^{\mathbb{X}}+\dot{\mathcal{I}}^{\mathbb{Y}}=0
, similar to other time-independent observables, such as energy.

Combining changes in marginal entropy
d_{t}S_{\mathbb{X}(\mathbb{Y})}^{\text{marg}}
with information flow
\dot{I}^{\mathbb{X}(\mathbb{Y})}
leads to local second laws, given as:

\displaystyle\dot{\sigma}_{\mathbb{Y}}

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\left[d_{t}S_{\mathbb{Y}}^{\text{marg}}-\sum_{\rho}\frac{\dot{Q}_{\rho}}{T_{\mathbb{Y}}}\right]}_{\dot{\Sigma}_{\mathbb{Y}}}-\dot{\mathcal{I}}^{\mathbb{Y}}=\dot{\Sigma}_{\mathbb{Y}}-\dot{\mathcal{I}}^{\mathbb{Y}}\geq 0,

(76)

\displaystyle\dot{\sigma}_{\mathbb{X}}

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\left[d_{t}S_{\mathbb{X}}^{\text{marg}}-\frac{\dot{Q}_{\mathbb{X}}}{T_{\mathbb{X}}}\right]}_{\dot{\Sigma}_{\mathbb{X}}}-\dot{\mathcal{I}}^{\mathbb{X}}=\dot{\Sigma}_{\mathbb{X}}-\dot{\mathcal{I}}^{\mathbb{X}}\geq 0.

Only keeping track of an individual subsystem
S_{\mathbb{X/Y}}^{\text{marg}}
along with changes in associated reservoirs
\dot{Q}_{\rho}/T_{\rho}
leads to the estimation of marginal entropy production
\dot{\Sigma}_{\mathbb{X}(\mathbb{Y})}
, which can violate the second law (Appendix.
B
). The positive quantity satisfying the local second law is the partial entropy production
\dot{\sigma}_{\mathbb{Y}/\mathbb{X}}
, capturing the entropy produced by the local dynamics in subsystem
\mathbb{Y}/\mathbb{X}
when the other subsystem
\mathbb{X}/\mathbb{Y}
remains fixed. Information flows represent the non-additive component of partial entropy production, reflecting changes in correlations between subsystems. Hence, we find that bipartite information thermodynamics
[
12
,
17
,
10
]
naturally extends to these systems.

The information flow
\dot{\mathcal{I}}^{\mathbb{X}}
due to
\mathbb{X}
can be further divided as
\dot{\mathcal{I}}^{\mathbb{X}}=\dot{\mathcal{I}}^{\mathbb{X}}_{S}+\dot{\mathcal{I}}^{\mathbb{X}}_{F}
. We identify these two terms as the entropic
\dot{\mathcal{I}}^{\mathbb{X}}_{S}
and the force contribution
\dot{\mathcal{I}}^{\mathbb{X}}_{F}
, given as

\displaystyle\dot{\mathcal{I}}^{\mathbb{X}}_{S}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{\gamma k_{B}}{T_{\mathbb{X}}m^{2}}\int d\vec{\Gamma}\;P_{t}^{\mathbb{X}}(\vec{\Gamma})\sum_{y}\mathbb{P}_{t}(y|\vec{\Gamma})\left[\partial_{v}\log\mathbb{P}_{t}(y|\vec{\Gamma})\right]^{2}\leq 0,

(77)

\displaystyle\dot{\mathcal{I}}^{\mathbb{X}}_{F}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{k_{B}}{m}\int d\vec{\Gamma}\;P_{t}^{\mathbb{X}}(\vec{\Gamma})\sum_{y}f_{\mathbb{XY}}(x,y)\>\partial_{v}\mathbb{P}_{t}(y|\vec{\Gamma}).

(78)

Therefore, in steady state operation, when the interacting force
f_{\mathbb{XY}}=0
, the subsystem
\mathbb{Y}
will always behave as a measurement device, as
\dot{\mathcal{I}}^{\mathbb{X}}=\dot{\mathcal{I}}^{\mathbb{X}}_{S}\leq 0
. In such a setting, the UD dynamics in
\mathbb{X}
becomes completely independent of the subsystem
\mathbb{Y}
and relaxes to equilibrium. However, the MJ process in
\mathbb{Y}
still depends on the state of
\mathbb{X}
through the jump rates, thus performing the measurement (
\dot{\mathcal{I}}^{\mathbb{Y}}\geq 0
) on the state of
\mathbb{X}
. This gained information cannot be used due to the lack of a feedback mechanism in the subsystem
\mathbb{X}
(
f_{\mathbb{XY}}=0
). Hence, the subsystem
\mathbb{Y}
acts solely as a sensor. This scenario applies to a recent toy model
[
66
]
and its electronic implementation
[
60
]
of classical escapement clocks, where the UD dynamics drives the MJ process without any back-action. Conversely, the subsystem
\mathbb{X}
can be a measurement device (i.e. with
\dot{\mathcal{I}}^{\mathbb{X}}>0
) only with an interacting force
f_{\mathbb{XY}}(x,y)\neq 0
.

III.4
Integral Fluctuation theorem for partial entropy production

At the average level, the local second laws (Eq. (
76
)) impose restrictions on thermodynamic flows in these autonomous systems. In this section, we generalize the fluctuation relations for partially masked dynamics, derived earlier for bipartite MJ processes
[
22
]
and bipartite UD dynamics
[
21
]
, to composite systems with coupled MJ-UD dynamics. This generalization establishes fundamental bounds on fluctuations in the information processing capabilities of these systems.
Specifically, we show the existence of integral fluctuation theorems for the partial entropy production satisfying the dynamics in Eq. (
6
), given as:

\displaystyle\langle e^{-\hat{\sigma}_{\mathbb{Y}}(t)/k_{B}}\rangle=\langle e^{-[\hat{\Sigma}_{\mathbb{Y}}(t)-\Delta{\hat{I}}^{\mathbb{Y}}(t)]/k_{B}}\rangle=1,\hskip 42.67912pt\langle e^{-\hat{\sigma}_{\mathbb{X}}(t)/k_{B}}\rangle=\langle e^{-[\hat{\Sigma}_{\mathbb{X}}(t)-\Delta{\hat{I}}^{\mathbb{X}}(t)]/k_{B}}\rangle=1,

(79)

where
\hat{o}_{\mathbb{X}(\mathbb{Y})}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\delta\hat{o}_{\mathbb{X}(\mathbb{Y})}(\tau)
represents the time-integrated quantities along individual trajectories, corresponding to the partial entropy production
\delta\hat{\sigma}_{\mathbb{X}(\mathbb{Y})}=d\hat{s}_{\mathbb{X}(\mathbb{Y})}-\frac{\delta\hat{Q}_{\mathbb{X}(\mathbb{Y})}}{T_{\mathbb{X}(\mathbb{Y})}}
(Eq. (
39
)) and marginal entropy production
\delta\hat{\sigma}_{\mathbb{X}(\mathbb{Y})}=d\hat{s}^{\text{marg}}_{\mathbb{X}(\mathbb{Y})}-\frac{\delta\hat{Q}_{\mathbb{X}(\mathbb{Y})}}{T_{\mathbb{X}(\mathbb{Y})}}
(see Sec.
III.3
). Here,
\Delta{\hat{I}}^{\mathbb{X}(\mathbb{Y})}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}d\hat{I}^{\mathbb{X}(\mathbb{Y})}(\tau)
is the mutual information accumulated due to the dynamics of the UD (MJ) in the individual subsystem
\mathbb{X}(\mathbb{Y})
. In Appendix.
C
, we provide a detailed proof of the above generalized fluctuation theorems for such composite systems with mixed dynamics.

IV
Timescale separation

In this section, we will consider the case when the jump dynamics in the subsystem
\mathbb{Y}
is much faster than the inertial dynamics in the subsystem
\mathbb{X}
. For the dynamics described by Eq. (
6
), we can associate the time scale of the dynamics in subsystem
\mathbb{Y}
as
\tau_{\mathbb{Y}}
and a timescale due to the potential
\hat{U}_{\mathbb{X}}
in the subsystem
\mathbb{X}
as
\tau_{\mathbb{X}}
. This allows us to define a dimensionless parameter
\epsilon=\tau_{\mathbb{Y}}/\tau_{\mathbb{X}}
. Using the characteristic length scale
l_{\mathbb{X}}
of the dynamics in subsystem
\mathbb{X}
, we can adimensionalize the master equation using the following variable transformation:

\displaystyle\tilde{t}

\displaystyle=t/\tau_{\mathbb{Y}},\quad\tilde{x}=x/l_{\mathbb{X}},\quad\tilde{v}=v\frac{\tau_{\mathbb{X}}}{l_{\mathbb{X}}},

(80)

\displaystyle\quad\tilde{U}(\tilde{x},y)=\frac{\tau_{\mathbb{X}}^{2}}{m\,l_{\mathbb{X}}^{2}}U(x,y),\quad\tilde{g}(\tilde{x},y)

\displaystyle=\frac{\tau_{\mathbb{X}}^{2}}{m\,l_{\mathbb{X}}}g(x,y),\quad\tilde{\gamma}=\frac{\tau_{\mathbb{X}}}{m}\gamma,\quad\tilde{\beta}_{\mathbb{X}(\mathbb{Y})}=\frac{ml_{\mathbb{X}}^{2}}{\tau_{\mathbb{X}}^{2}}\beta_{\mathbb{X}(\mathbb{Y})},\quad\tilde{\lambda}^{y,y^{\prime}}_{\rho}(\tilde{x})=\tau_{\mathbb{Y}}\,\lambda_{\rho}^{y,y^{\prime}}(x).

Dropping the tilde notation, the adimensional master equation is given as

d_{t}P_{t}(\vec{\Gamma},y)=\sum_{\rho,y^{\prime}}\lambda_{\rho}^{y,y^{\prime}}(x)P_{t}(\vec{\Gamma},y^{\prime})+\epsilon\underbrace{\Big[-v\partial_{x}-[f_{\mathbb{X}}(x)+f_{\mathbb{XY}}(x,y)]\partial_{v}+\gamma\partial_{v}(v+\beta_{\mathbb{X}}^{-1}\partial_{v})\Big]}_{\vec{\mathcal{L}}=\vec{\mathcal{L}}^{\rm{det}}+\vec{\mathcal{L}}^{\rm{diss}}}P_{t}(\vec{\Gamma},y),

(81)

where we have defined the escape rate
\lambda_{\rho}^{y,y}(x)\equiv-\sum_{\rho,y^{\prime}}\lambda_{\rho}^{y^{\prime},y}(x)
for state
y
. The adimensional forces are divided to identify the purely underdamped contributions
f_{\mathbb{X}}(x)=-\partial_{x}\hat{U}_{\mathbb{X}}(x)
and the interacting ones
f_{\mathbb{XY}}(x,y)=-\partial_{x}\hat{U}_{\mathbb{XY}}(x,y)+g(x,y)
. To see the changes due to interaction terms, we further divide the Fokker-Planck operator
\vec{\mathcal{L}}=\vec{\mathcal{L}}^{\rm{det}}+\vec{\mathcal{L}}^{\rm{diss}}=\vec{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathbb{X}}+\vec{\mathcal{L}}_{\rm{int}}
, such that

\displaystyle\vec{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathbb{X}}=-v\partial_{x}-f_{\mathbb{X}}(x)\partial_{v}+\gamma\partial_{v}(v+\beta_{\mathbb{X}}^{-1}\partial_{v}),\hskip 22.76228pt\text{and}\hskip 22.76228pt\vec{\mathcal{L}}_{\rm{int}}=-f_{\mathbb{XY}}(x,y)\partial_{v}.

(82)

Assuming a MJ dynamics to be much faster than the UD dynamics, i.e.
\epsilon\ll 1
, we can do a perturbative expansion of both the conditional
\mathbb{P}_{t}(y|\vec{\Gamma})
and marginal distribution
P_{t}^{\mathbb{X}}(\vec{\Gamma})
in
\epsilon
, as

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{t}(y|\vec{\Gamma})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{t}^{(0)}(y|\vec{\Gamma})+\epsilon\mathbb{P}_{t}^{(1)}(y|\vec{\Gamma})+\epsilon^{2}\mathbb{P}_{t}^{(2)}(y|\vec{\Gamma})+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{3})

\displaystyle P_{t}^{\mathbb{X}}(\vec{\Gamma})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle P_{t}^{\mathbb{X},0}+\epsilon P_{t}^{\mathbb{X},1}(\vec{\Gamma})+\epsilon^{2}P_{t}^{\mathbb{X},1}(\vec{\Gamma})+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{3}),

(83)

such that we get back
P_{t}(\vec{\Gamma},y)=\mathbb{P}_{t}(y|\vec{\Gamma})P_{t}^{\mathbb{X}}(\vec{\Gamma}).

Normalization of the probabilities implies that
\sum_{y}\mathbb{P}_{t}^{(0)}(y|\vec{\Gamma})=1
,
\int dx\,dvP_{t}^{\mathbb{X},0}(\vec{\Gamma})=1
,
\sum_{y}\mathbb{P}_{t}^{(n)}(y|\vec{\Gamma})=0
and
\int dx\,dvP_{t}^{(n)}(\vec{\Gamma})=0
for all
n\geq 1
. Due to the slower changes in UD dynamics of the
\mathbb{X}
, the thermodynamic quantities will have a similar consequence, and the (adimensional) first and second law satisfies:

\displaystyle d_{t}E

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\epsilon\left[\dot{Q}_{\mathbb{X}}+\dot{W}_{\mathbb{X}}\right]+\sum_{\rho}\dot{Q}_{\rho}+\sum_{\rho}\dot{W}_{\rho}

(84)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\epsilon\left[d_{t}E_{\mathbb{X}}+\dot{\mathcal{E}}^{\mathbb{X}}\right]+d_{t}E_{\mathbb{Y}}+\dot{\mathcal{E}}^{\mathbb{Y}},

(85)

\displaystyle\dot{\sigma}_{\rm{tot}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\epsilon\left[d_{t}S_{\mathbb{X}}-\beta_{\mathbb{X}}\dot{Q}_{\mathbb{X}}\right]+\left[d_{t}S_{\mathbb{Y}}-\beta_{\mathbb{Y}}\sum_{\rho}\dot{Q}_{\rho}\right]=\epsilon\dot{\sigma}_{\mathbb{X}}+\dot{\sigma}_{\mathbb{Y}}\geq 0,

(86)

\displaystyle d_{t}I

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\epsilon\left[\dot{\mathcal{I}}^{\mathbb{X}}_{S}+\dot{\mathcal{I}}^{\mathbb{X}}_{F}\right]+\dot{\mathcal{I}}^{\mathbb{Y}},

(87)

where
d_{t}S_{\mathbb{X}(\mathbb{Y})}
is the time-variation of the Shannon entropy
S
exclusively due to
\mathbb{X}(\mathbb{Y})
dynamics (Appendix.
A
). Putting Eq.
83
back into the master equation, we can systematically derive dynamical equations governing the marginal and conditional probabilities at different orders of timescale separation parameter
\epsilon
. We will also characterize the internal thermodynamic flows at each order of approximation.

IV.1
Adiabatic approximation

When the dynamics is fully time-scale separated (adiabatic approximation), i.e.,
\epsilon\to 0
, we obtain:

\displaystyle d_{t}P_{t}^{\mathbb{X},0}(\vec{\Gamma})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0,

(88)

\displaystyle d_{t}\mathbb{P}_{t}^{(0)}(y|\vec{\Gamma})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\rho,y^{\prime}}\lambda_{\rho}^{y,y^{\prime}}(x)\mathbb{P}_{t}^{(0)}(y^{\prime}|\vec{\Gamma}).

(89)

In this regime, the dynamics in the subsystem
\mathbb{X}
appears frozen, in comparison to the fast dynamics in
\mathbb{Y}
. To account for the effect of the dynamics of
\mathbb{Y}
back on
\mathbb{X}
, we need to consider the dynamical equation for the next-order term in the timescale parameter
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{1})
, giving us:

\displaystyle d_{t}P_{t}^{\mathbb{X},1}(\vec{\Gamma})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\vec{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathbb{X}}P_{t}^{\mathbb{X},0}(\vec{\Gamma})-\partial_{v}\left[\sum_{y}\mathbb{P}_{t}^{(0)}(y|\vec{\Gamma})f_{\mathbb{XY}}(x,y)\right]P_{t}^{\mathbb{X},0}(\vec{\Gamma}).

(90)

If the two subsystems are initially uncorrelated, i.e.
\mathbb{P}_{0}(y|\vec{\Gamma})=P^{\mathbb{Y}}_{0}(y)
, then the adiabatic dynamics (Eq. (
89
)) leads to a velocity-independent distribution
\mathbb{P}_{t}(y|x)
at any time
t
. Combining Eq. (
89
) with Eq. (
88
) and Eq. (
90
), we obtain a closed set of equations capturing the adiabatic dynamics:

\displaystyle d_{t}\mathbb{P}_{t}(y|x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\rho,y^{\prime}}\lambda_{\rho}^{y,y^{\prime}}(x)\mathbb{P}_{t}(y^{\prime}|x)+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon),

(91)

\displaystyle d_{t}P_{t}^{\mathbb{X}}(\vec{\Gamma})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\epsilon\left[-v\partial_{x}-[f^{\rm{eff}}_{\mathbb{X}}(x)+g^{\rm{eff}}(x)]\partial_{v}+\gamma\partial_{v}(v+\beta_{\mathbb{X}}^{-1}\partial_{v})\right]P_{t}^{\mathbb{X}}(\vec{\Gamma})+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{2}),

(92)

where
f_{\mathbb{X}}^{\rm{eff}}(x,t)
and
g^{\rm{eff}}(x,t)
are the effective forces from the conservative potential
U(x,y)
and non-conservative force
g(x,y)
, given as:

\displaystyle f_{\mathbb{X}}^{\rm{eff}}(x,t)

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle-\left[\partial_{x}\hat{U}_{\mathbb{X}}(x)+\sum_{y}\mathbb{P}_{t}(y|x)\partial_{x}\hat{U}_{\mathbb{XY}}(x,y)\right],

(93)

\displaystyle g^{\rm{eff}}(x,t)

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\sum_{y}g(x,y)\mathbb{P}_{t}(y|x).

(94)

The fast dynamics in the subsystem
\mathbb{Y}
quickly relaxes to its stationary state
\pi_{y}(x)
, satisfying
d_{t}\pi_{y}(x)=\sum_{\rho,y^{\prime}}\lambda_{\rho}^{y,y^{\prime}}(x)\pi_{y}(x)=0
. Hence, the slow adiabatic dynamics of
\mathbb{X}
is equivalent to an underdamped Brownian particle with effective conservative potential
\hat{U}_{\mathbb{X}}^{\rm{eff}}(x)=-\int\!dx\;f_{\mathbb{X}}^{\rm{eff}}(x)
and an effective non-conservative force
g^{\rm{eff}}(x)
obtained using
\pi_{y}(x)
. The underdamped dynamics of
\mathbb{X}
thus corresponds to an averaging of the fast dynamics, capturing a quasi-static evolution of the subsystem
\mathbb{X}
.

For systems with purely conservative interactions, i.e.
g(x,y)=0
, the adiabatic dynamics in
\mathbb{X}
relaxes to equilibrium with a modified Gibbs state
P^{\mathbb{X}}_{\rm{ad}}(\Gamma)\propto\exp{\left(-\beta_{\mathbb{X}}[(v^{2}/2)+\hat{U}_{\mathbb{X}}^{\rm{eff}}(x)]\right)}
at the same temperature
T_{\mathbb{X}}
. Although the fast dynamics in
\mathbb{Y}
can be far from equilibrium, it is unable to drive the slow underdamped dynamics out of equilibrium under the adiabatic approximation. This is reflected in the vanishing energy (Eq. (
III.1
)) and information flows (Eq. (
74
)) in the steady-state, which are nonequilibrium resources capable of driving a system out of equilibrium:

\displaystyle\dot{\mathcal{I}}=\epsilon\dot{\mathcal{I}}^{\mathbb{X}}=-\dot{\mathcal{I}}^{\mathbb{Y}}=0+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{2}),

(95)

\displaystyle\dot{\mathcal{E}}=\epsilon\dot{\mathcal{E}}^{\mathbb{X}}=-\dot{\mathcal{E}}^{\mathbb{Y}}=0+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{2}).

(96)

Hence, there is a need to go beyond the adiabatic approximation to capture the flows between the subsystems and the associated nonequilibrium phenomenology.

IV.2
Beyond Adiabatic approximation

Going to the next order in
\epsilon^{2}
, we derive the following equations from the perturbative expansion of the master equation Eq. (
81
):

\displaystyle d_{t}\mathbb{P}_{t}^{(1)}(y|\vec{\Gamma})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\rho,y^{\prime}}\lambda_{\rho}^{y,y^{\prime}}(x)\mathbb{P}_{t}^{(1)}(y^{\prime}|\vec{\Gamma})-v\partial_{x}\mathbb{P}_{t}^{(0)}(y|\vec{\Gamma})+\frac{\mathbb{P}_{t}^{(0)}(y|\vec{\Gamma})\partial_{v}P_{t}^{\mathbb{X},0}(\vec{\Gamma})}{P_{t}^{\mathbb{X},0}(\vec{\Gamma})}\left[\left(\sum_{y}\mathbb{P}_{t}^{(0)}(y|\vec{\Gamma})f_{\mathbb{XY}}(x,y)\right)-f_{\mathbb{XY}}(x,y)\right],

(97)

\displaystyle d_{t}P_{t}^{\mathbb{X},2}(\vec{\Gamma})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\vec{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathbb{X}}P_{t}^{\mathbb{X},1}(\vec{\Gamma})-\left[\sum_{y}\mathbb{P}_{t}^{(0)}(y|\vec{\Gamma})f_{\mathbb{XY}}(x,y)\right]\partial_{v}P_{t}^{\mathbb{X},1}(\vec{\Gamma})-\partial_{v}\left[\sum_{y}f_{\mathbb{XY}}(x,y)\mathbb{P}_{t}^{(1)}(y|\vec{\Gamma})P_{t}^{\mathbb{X},0}(\vec{\Gamma})\right].

(98)

Combining Eqs. (
88
,
89
,
90
) with the above Eqs. (
97
,
98
), we derive a closed dynamical equation for the marginal probability density
P_{t}^{\mathbb{X}}(\vec{\Gamma})
and the conditional distribution
\mathbb{P}_{t}
:

\displaystyle d_{t}\mathbb{P}_{t}(y|\vec{\Gamma})\!\!

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\!\!\sum_{\rho,y^{\prime}}\lambda_{\rho}^{y,y^{\prime}}(x)\mathbb{P}_{t}(y^{\prime}|\vec{\Gamma})+\epsilon\!\left\{\!-v\partial_{x}\mathbb{P}_{t}(y|\vec{\Gamma})+\mathbb{P}_{t}(y|\vec{\Gamma})\left[\!\left(\sum_{y}f_{\mathbb{XY}}(x,y)\mathbb{P}_{t}(y|\vec{\Gamma})\right)\!-\!f_{\mathbb{XY}}(x,y)\right]\partial_{v}\log P_{t}^{\mathbb{X}}(\vec{\Gamma})\right\}\!+\!\mathcal{O}(\!\epsilon^{2}\!),

(99)

\displaystyle d_{t}P_{t}^{\mathbb{X}}(\vec{\Gamma})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\epsilon\left[\vec{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathbb{X}}P_{t}^{\mathbb{X}}(\vec{\Gamma})-\partial_{v}\left(\sum_{y}f_{\mathbb{XY}}(x,y)\mathbb{P}_{t}(y|\vec{\Gamma})P_{t}^{\mathbb{X}}(\vec{\Gamma})\right)\right]+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{3}).

(100)

Hence, going beyond the adiabatic approximation, we can capture the influence of the slow UD dynamics in
\mathbb{X}
on the fast MJ dynamics in
\mathbb{Y}
, leading to coupled dynamical equations of Eq. (
99
) and Eq. (
100
). To make analytical progress, we will again make the assumption that the conditional distribution has already relaxed to its steady-state, for any given marginal density
P_{t}^{\mathbb{X}}
at time
t
. This assumption implies
d_{t}\mathbb{P}_{t}^{(0+1)}=0
in Eq. (
99
). The steady-state conditional distribution
\mathbb{P}_{t}^{(0+1)}(y|\vec{\Gamma})
, Applying matrix perturbation methods to Eq. (
99
) around its adiabatic solution
\pi_{y}
, is then given as

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{t}^{(0+1)}(y|\vec{\Gamma})=\pi_{y}(x)+\epsilon\left[v\;\sum_{y^{\prime}}\mathcal{A}^{-1}_{y,y^{\prime}}(x)\partial_{x}\pi_{y^{\prime}}(x)+\partial_{v}\log P_{t}^{\mathbb{X}}(\vec{\Gamma})\sum_{y^{\prime}}\mathcal{A}^{-1}_{y,y^{\prime}}(x)f_{\mathbb{XY}}(x,y^{\prime})\pi_{y^{\prime}}(x)\right]+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{2}).

(101)

Here,
\mathcal{A}^{-1}_{y,y^{\prime}}(x)
represents the generalized inverse of the generator
\mathcal{L}_{\mathbb{Y}}=\sum_{\rho}\lambda_{\rho}^{y,y^{\prime}}\ket{y}\bra{y^{\prime}}
of the dynamics in the subsystem
\mathbb{Y}
. It is explicitly given as
\mathcal{A}^{-1}_{y,y^{\prime}}(x)=\sum_{\chi_{i}/\{0\}}\frac{\ket{R_{\chi}^{i}}\bra{L_{\chi}^{i}}}{\chi_{i}\braket{L_{\chi}^{i}|R_{\chi}^{i}}}
, where
\bra{L_{\chi}^{i}}
and
\ket{R_{\chi}^{i}}
represent the left and right eigenvectors corresponding to the nonzero eigenvalues
\chi_{i}
of the generator
\mathcal{L}_{\mathbb{Y}}=\sum_{\rho}\lambda_{\rho}^{y,y^{\prime}}(x)\ket{y}\bra{y^{\prime}}
governing the jump dynamics. Inserting it back into Eq. (
100
) results in a closed Fokker-Planck equation for the marginal dynamics. This modified Fokker-Planck equation in adimensional form, is given as:

\displaystyle d_{t}P_{t}^{\mathbb{X}}(\vec{\Gamma})=\epsilon\Big[-v\partial_{x}-\partial_{v}[f_{\mathbb{X}}^{\rm{eff}}(x)+g^{\rm{eff}}(x)-\gamma^{\rm{eff}}(x)v]+D^{\rm{eff}}(x)\partial_{v}^{2})\Big]P_{t}^{\mathbb{X}}(\vec{\Gamma})+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{3}),

(102)

with the same effective conservative
f_{\mathbb{X}}^{\rm{eff}}(x)
(Eq. (
93
)) and the non-conservative
g^{\rm{eff}}(x)
(Eq. (
94
)) force. However, the drift
\gamma^{\rm{eff}}(x)
and diffusion coefficient
D^{\rm{eff}}(x)
are modified due to the interactive forces, given as:

\displaystyle\gamma^{\rm{eff}}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\gamma-\epsilon\sum_{y,y^{\prime}}f_{\mathbb{XY}}(x,y)\mathcal{A}^{-1}_{y,y^{\prime}}(x)\partial_{x}\pi_{y^{\prime}}(x),

(103)

\displaystyle D^{\rm{eff}}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\gamma}{\beta_{\mathbb{X}}}+\epsilon\sum_{y,y^{\prime}}f_{\mathbb{XY}}(x,y)\mathcal{A}^{-1}_{y,y^{\prime}}(x)f_{\mathbb{XY}}(x,y^{\prime})\pi_{y^{\prime}}(x).

(104)

In general, this effective UD dynamics does not need to satisfy the detailed balance condition
\gamma^{\rm{eff}}/D^{\rm{eff}}\neq\beta_{\mathbb{X}}

[
61
]
. Therefore, it is possible to drive the subsystem
\mathbb{X}
out of equilibrium through the interactive force
f_{\mathbb{XY}}
with
\mathbb{Y}
. We also notice that the additional terms in the damping and the diffusion coefficient vanish by taking
\epsilon\to 0
, thereby recovering the adiabatic dynamics of Eq. (
92
). It is important to note that the modified Fokker-Planck equation presented above has been previously derived in the context of the 2-level electron shuttle model
[
61
]
, which will be discussed in detail in Sec.
VI
. Here, we extend this modified Fokker-Planck formalism to more general composite systems, where the MJ process occurs over any arbitrary state space.

In the absence of non-conservative force
g^{\rm{eff}}=0
, we derive the lowest-order contribution of energy and information flows in the steady-state. For the information flow
\dot{\mathcal{I}}
, we obtain:

\dot{\mathcal{I}}=-\epsilon^{2}\left[k_{B}\int\!d\vec{\Gamma}\;P_{\rm{ad}}^{\mathbb{X}}(\vec{\Gamma})\left\{\sum_{y}\nu(x,y)\;\partial_{x}\hat{U}_{\mathbb{XY}}(x,y)\right\}\right]+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{3}),

(105)

where
\nu(x,y)=\left[\sum_{y^{\prime}}\mathcal{A}^{-1}_{y,y^{\prime}}(x)\partial_{x}\pi_{y^{\prime}}(x)-\beta_{\mathbb{X}}\sum_{y^{\prime}}\mathcal{A}^{-1}_{y,y^{\prime}}(x)f_{\mathbb{XY}}(x,y^{\prime})\pi_{y^{\prime}}(x)\right]
represents the first-order perturbation in the conditional distribution
\mathbb{P}_{t}(y|\vec{\Gamma})
and
P_{\rm{ad}}^{\mathbb{X}}
is the marginal density of
\mathbb{X}
from the adiabatic approximation of Eq. (
92
). Hence, the force part
\dot{\mathcal{I}}=\dot{\mathcal{I}}^{\mathbb{X}}_{F}
captures the essential contribution to the information flow up to
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{2})
, and the entropic contributions
\dot{\mathcal{I}}^{\mathbb{X}}_{S}
only appear at higher order.

Similarly, for the energy flows
\dot{\mathcal{E}}
, we obtain:

\displaystyle\dot{\mathcal{E}}=\epsilon^{2}\left[\int\!d\vec{\Gamma}\;P_{ss}^{\mathbb{X},1}(\vec{\Gamma})\;v^{2}\right]+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{3}),

(106)

where
P_{ss}^{\mathbb{X},1}=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\left[P_{ss}^{\mathbb{X}}-P_{\rm{ad}}^{\mathbb{X}}\right]/\epsilon
represent the first order perturbation in the marginal density
P^{\mathbb{X}}
. This term captures the nonequilibrium dissipation at
\mathbb{X}
due to energy flows from
\mathbb{Y}\to\mathbb{X}.
Hence, systematically going beyond the adiabatic approximation, we captured the lowest-order contributions to the energetic and information flows from
\mathbb{Y}
needed to push the dynamics of
\mathbb{X}
out of equilibrium.

V
Large mass limit

In this section, we consider the dynamics and thermodynamics in the large (but finite) mass limit for the underdamped system. To avoid trivial dynamics in a flat potential, in the limit
m\to\infty
, we assume that the conservative potential
\hat{U}_{\mathbb{X}}(x)
scales with the mass. This ensures that the subsystem
\mathbb{X}
experiences a finite conservative acceleration,
\mathcal{O}(\partial_{x}\hat{U}_{\mathbb{X}}/m)=1
, while the contribution from the interaction potential vanishes
\lim_{m\to\infty}(\partial_{x}\hat{U}_{\mathbb{XY}}/m)=0
. This approximation effectively separates the energy scales of the two subsystems, i.e. the energy
E_{\mathbb{X}}(t)
of the underdamped subsystem
\mathbb{X}
is much higher than the energy of the dot
E_{\mathbb{Y}}(t)
and the interaction energy
U_{\mathbb{XY}}(t)
at any given time
t
, such that the subsystem
\mathbb{Y}
and the environment of
\mathbb{X}
only weakly influence the underdamped dynamics of
\mathbb{X}.

Rewriting the initial master equation in Eq. (
6
) to identify contributions for different orders of
\mathcal{O}(1/m^{n})
, we get the following:

\displaystyle d_{t}P_{t}(\vec{\Gamma},y)=\sum_{\rho,y^{\prime}}\lambda_{\rho}^{y,y^{\prime}}(x)P_{t}(\vec{\Gamma},y^{\prime})+\Big\{\left[-v\partial_{x}-a_{\mathbb{X}}(x)\partial_{v}\right]+\frac{1}{m}\left[f_{\mathbb{XY}}(x,y)+\gamma\partial_{v}\;v\right]+\frac{1}{m^{2}}\gamma\beta_{\mathbb{X}}^{-1}\partial_{v}^{2}\Big\}P_{t}(\vec{\Gamma},y),

(107)

where we have defined the conservative acceleration
a_{\mathbb{X}}=-\partial_{x}\hat{U}_{\mathbb{X}}/m
and the interactive forces
f_{\mathbb{XY}}(x,y)=-\partial_{x}\hat{U}_{\mathbb{XY}}(x,y)+g(x,y)
. In the diverging mass
m\to\infty
limit, the subsystem
\mathbb{X}
has Hamiltonian dynamics
\dot{x}_{t}=v_{t}
and
\dot{v}_{t}=a_{\mathbb{X}}(x_{t})=-\partial_{x}\hat{U}_{\mathbb{X}}(x_{t})/m
due to its initial energy
E_{\mathbb{X}}=(1/2)mv_{0}^{2}+\hat{U}_{\mathbb{X}}(x_{0})

[
54
]
. Here, we go beyond this limit to the next order contribution to the dynamics for systems with large, but finite mass
m
. Similar to the previous section, we perturbatively expand the conditional distribution
\mathbb{P}_{t}(y|\vec{\Gamma})
and marginal probability density
P_{t}^{\mathbb{X}}(\vec{\Gamma})
, leading to the following dynamical equation up to
\mathcal{O}(1/m)
:

\displaystyle d_{t}P_{t}^{\mathbb{X}}(\vec{\Gamma})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\{\left[-v\partial_{x}-a_{\mathbb{X}}(x)\partial_{v}\right]+\frac{1}{m}\left[\gamma\partial_{v}\;v-\sum_{y}\mathbb{P}_{t}(y|\vec{\Gamma})f_{\mathbb{XY}}(x,y)\right]\right\}P_{t}^{\mathbb{X}}(\vec{\Gamma})+\mathcal{O}(m^{-2}),

(108)

\displaystyle d_{t}\mathbb{P}_{t}(y|\vec{\Gamma})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\rho,y^{\prime}}\lambda_{\rho}^{y,y^{\prime}}(x)\mathbb{P}_{t}(y^{\prime}|\vec{\Gamma})+\left[-v\partial_{x}-a_{\mathbb{X}}(x)\partial_{v}\right]\mathbb{P}_{t}(y^{\prime}|\vec{\Gamma})+\mathcal{O}(m^{-1}).

(109)

Due to the lack of a diffusive term up to
\mathcal{O}(m^{-1})
, if we initialize the system in an uncorrelated state such that
P_{0}(\vec{\Gamma},y)=P^{\mathbb{Y}}_{0}(y)\delta(x-x_{0})\delta(v-v_{0})
, then the marginal dynamics evolves to
P_{t}^{\mathbb{X}}(\vec{\Gamma})=\delta(x-x_{t})\delta(v-v_{t})
at time
t
, satisfying the deterministic dynamics:

\displaystyle dx_{t}

\displaystyle=v_{t}dt,

(110)

\displaystyle dv_{t}

\displaystyle=a_{\mathbb{X}}(x_{t})dt+\frac{1}{m}\left[-\gamma v_{t}+\sum_{y}\mathbb{P}_{t}(y|\vec{\Gamma}_{t})f_{\mathbb{XY}}(x_{t},y)\right]dt+\mathcal{O}(m^{-2}).

The above dynamical equation goes beyond the constant energy timescales of Hamiltonian dynamics of subsystem
\mathbb{X}
at the infinite mass limit. It captures the lowest order contribution due to damping and interaction with subsystem
\mathbb{Y}
for large but finite mass. However, the above equation Eq. (
110
) is not closed as it is still dependent on the conditional probability
\mathbb{P}_{t}(y|\vec{\Gamma}_{t})
. To close the dynamical equation up to
\mathcal{O}(1/m)
, we need the conditional probability
\mathbb{P}_{t}(y|\Gamma_{t})
along a deterministic trajectory
\vec{\Gamma}_{t}
up to
\mathcal{O}(1)
at any time
t
. This can be obtained by solving:

\displaystyle d_{t}\mathbb{P}_{t}(y|\vec{\Gamma}_{t})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\rho,y^{\prime}}\lambda_{\rho}^{y,y^{\prime}}(x)\mathbb{P}_{t}(y^{\prime}|\vec{\Gamma}_{t})+\mathcal{O}(m^{-1}).

(111)

The term due to the Hamiltonian flow in Eq. (
109
) drops out when the conditional probability
\mathbb{P}_{t}(y|\Gamma_{t})
is only along the deterministic trajectory
\mathbb{P}_{t}(y|\vec{\Gamma}_{t})=\int d\vec{\Gamma}\;\mathbb{P}_{t}(y|\vec{\Gamma})\delta(\vec{\Gamma}-\vec{\Gamma}_{t})=\int d\vec{\Gamma}\;\mathbb{P}_{t}(y|\vec{\Gamma})P_{t}^{\mathbb{X}}(\vec{\Gamma})
. In this setting, the conditional distribution
\mathbb{P}_{t}(y|\vec{\Gamma}_{t})
is equal to the corresponding marginal probability
P_{t}^{\mathbb{Y}}(y)
, i.e.
P_{t}^{\mathbb{Y}}(y)=\mathbb{P}_{t}(y|\vec{\Gamma}_{t})+\mathcal{O}(1/m)
. Hence, combining Eq. (
110
) along with Eq. (
111
), we obtain the closed form dynamics consistent upto
\mathcal{O}(1/m)
, given as:

\displaystyle dx_{t}

\displaystyle=v_{t}dt,

(112)

\displaystyle dv_{t}

\displaystyle=a_{\mathbb{X}}(x_{t})dt+\frac{1}{m}\left[-\gamma v_{t}+\sum_{y}P_{t}^{\mathbb{Y}}(y)f_{\mathbb{XY}}(x_{t},y)\right]dt+\mathcal{O}(m^{-2}),

\displaystyle d_{t}P_{t}^{\mathbb{Y}}(y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\rho,y^{\prime}}\lambda_{\rho}^{y,y^{\prime}}(x_{t})P_{t}^{\mathbb{Y}}(y)+\mathcal{O}(m^{-1}).

Hence, we effectively obtain a deterministic underdamped dynamics for the subsystem
\mathbb{X}
, which drives the stochastic MJ dynamics through its dependence on the Poisson rates
\lambda_{\rho}^{y,y^{\prime}}(x)
. This dynamics is equivalent to the meanfield dynamics
[
64
,
61
,
47
]
, obtained by doing an ad-hoc approximation
\lambda_{\rho}^{y,y^{\prime}}(x)\approx\lambda_{\rho}^{y,y^{\prime}}(\langle x\rangle)
, which leads to a closed form equation for
\langle x\rangle,\langle v\rangle
and
P_{t}^{\mathbb{Y}}(y)
. It is important to highlight that the dynamics of Eq. (
112
) is essentially deterministic and need not have a time-independent stationary state. Instead, the nonlinear dynamics (Eq. (
112
)) may exhibit a time-dependent attractor, such as a limit cycle. In contrast, the full stochastic dynamics Eq. (
6
) has a unique steady-state in the long time limit
P_{ss}(\mathbf{\Gamma},y)=\lim_{t\to\infty}P_{t}(\mathbf{\Gamma},y)
. This indicates that to accurately capture the stationary state, one must consider higher order terms, beyond
\mathcal{O}(m^{-1})
, accounting for the diffusive terms in the UD dynamics. Note that the derivation of the mean-field dynamics relies on the assumption that the energy of the underdamped subsystem
\mathbb{X}
is much larger than the energy scales at any given time, i.e
E_{\mathbb{X}}(\vec{\Gamma}_{t})\gg E_{\mathbb{Y}}(y_{t}),\hat{U}_{\mathbb{XY}}(\vec{\Gamma}_{t},y_{t})
, which is naturally achieved for
m\gg 1
and
\Gamma_{t}\neq(0,0)
.

The deterministic dynamical equations of Eq. (
112
) leads to an incomplete description of the thermodynamics, as the higher-order diffusion term plays an important role in the thermodynamic quantities. For instance, the heat
\dot{Q}_{\mathbb{X}}
flow in
\mathbb{X}
contains a
term
-\gamma/\beta m
arising from the thermal diffusion term
of
\mathcal{O}(m^{-2})
in the master equation Eq. (
107
). Due to our truncation to
\mathcal{O}(m^{-1})
in the derivation of MF dynamics, these diffusive effects are not captured. To rigorously derive thermodynamic quantities, the dynamics must be extended to at least
\mathcal{O}(m^{-2})
. Despite this, thermodynamic quantities can still be estimated from MF dynamics using the localized joint probability density
P_{t}(\vec{\Gamma},y)=P^{\mathbb{Y}}_{t}(y)\delta(x-x_{t})\delta(v-v_{t})
, as done in
[
61
]
. The first law at the average level due to meanfield dynamics satisfies:

\displaystyle d_{t}\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muE\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu=\vphantom{\sum_{\rho}}\dot{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muQ\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}_{\mathbb{X}}+\dot{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muW\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}_{\mathbb{X}}+\sum_{\rho}\left[\dot{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muQ\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}_{\rho}+\dot{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muW\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}_{\rho}\right],

(113)

where each of thermodynamic quantities are computed from the meanfield dynamics:

\displaystyle\dot{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muQ\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}_{\mathbb{X}}

\displaystyle=-\gamma v_{t}^{2},\hskip 28.45274pt\dot{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muW\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}_{\mathbb{X}}=\sum_{y}P^{\mathbb{Y}}_{t}(y)\>v_{t}\>g(x_{t},y),

(114)

\displaystyle\dot{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muQ\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}_{\rho}=\sum_{y>y^{\prime}}\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muJ\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu^{y,y^{\prime}}_{\rho}(\vec{\Gamma}_{t})

\displaystyle\left\{\left[U(x_{t},y)-U(x_{t},y^{\prime})\right]-w^{y^{\prime}\to y}_{\rho}(x_{t})\right\},\hskip 14.22636pt\dot{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muW\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}_{\rho}=\sum_{y>y^{\prime}}\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muJ\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu^{y,y^{\prime}}_{\rho}(\vec{\Gamma}_{t})\>w_{\rho}^{y^{\prime}\to y}(x_{t}),

\displaystyle\dot{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mathcal{E}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}^{\mathbb{X}}(t)=\sum_{y}P^{\mathbb{Y}}_{t}(y)

\displaystyle v_{t}\,\partial_{x}\hat{U}_{\mathbb{XY}}(x_{t},y),\hskip 14.22636pt\dot{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mathcal{E}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}^{\mathbb{Y}}(t)=\sum_{\rho,y>y^{\prime}}\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muJ\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu^{y,y^{\prime}}_{\rho}(\vec{\Gamma}_{t})\left[\hat{U}_{\mathbb{XY}}(x_{t},y)-\hat{U}_{\mathbb{XY}}(x_{t},y^{\prime})\right],

where
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muJ\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{\rho}^{y,y^{\prime}}=\lambda_{\rho}^{y,y^{\prime}}(x_{t})P^{\mathbb{Y}}_{t}(y^{\prime})-\lambda_{\rho}^{y^{\prime},y}(x_{t})P^{\mathbb{Y}}_{t}(y)
is the average probability current. In particular, the heat flow
\dot{\bar{Q}}_{\mathbb{X}}
to its reservoir
\mathbb{X}
, as described by the mean-field (MF) dynamics, accounts only for the interaction with the reservoir through the damping force
-\gamma v_{t}
.

Here, the deterministic nature of the subsystem
\mathbb{X}
with the localized probability density
P_{t}(\vec{\Gamma},y)
, the joint entropy
S(t)
has contributions only from the subsystem
\mathbb{Y}
, i.e.
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muS\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu(t)\approx S_{\mathbb{Y}}^{\text{marg}}(t)=-k_{B}\sum_{y}P^{\mathbb{Y}}_{t}(y)\log P^{\mathbb{Y}}_{t}(y).
As a result, it also fails to account for changes in correlations, since the joint distribution is an independent product of the marginal densities:
P_{t}(\vec{\Gamma},y)=P^{\mathbb{Y}}_{t}(y)P^{\mathbb{X}}_{t}(\vec{\Gamma})=P^{\mathbb{Y}}_{t}(y)\delta(x-x_{t})\delta(v-v_{t})
. Hence, the mutual information and information flows vanish in this limit, i.e.
d_{t}I(t)=\dot{\mathcal{I}}^{\mathbb{X}}(t)+\dot{\mathcal{I}}^{\mathbb{Y}}(t)=0
(See Sec.
III.3
).

The entropy production rate is the sum of changes in the system entropy
d_{t}S_{\mathbb{Y}}^{\text{marg}}(t)
and changes in the reservoir entropy, leading to:

\displaystyle\dot{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\sigma\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}_{\rm{tot}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle d_{t}\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muS\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu-\frac{\dot{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muQ\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}_{\mathbb{X}}}{T_{\mathbb{X}}}-\sum_{\rho}\frac{\dot{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muQ\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}_{\rho}}{T_{\mathbb{Y}}}

(115)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\rho}\left[\lambda_{\rho}^{y,y^{\prime}}(x_{t})P^{\mathbb{Y}}_{t}(y^{\prime})-\lambda_{\rho}^{y^{\prime},y}(x_{t})P^{\mathbb{Y}}_{t}(y)\right]\log\frac{\lambda_{\rho}^{y,y^{\prime}}(x_{t})P^{\mathbb{Y}}_{t}(y^{\prime})}{\lambda_{\rho}^{y^{\prime},y}(x_{t})P^{\mathbb{Y}}_{t}(y)}+\frac{\gamma}{T_{\mathbb{X}}}v_{t}^{2}\geq 0.

(116)

Thus, we obtain consistent thermodynamic laws for meanfield dynamics as it corresponds just to the large mass limit
m\gg 1
.

VI
Example: Electron shuttle

In this section, we consider one of the simplest nanoelectromechanical systems, called the electron shuttle. The system consists of a quantum dot hosted by an underdamped harmonic oscillator, placed under a voltage bias between two leads. Electron shuttling is a form of periodic transport or “shuttling” of charges between the leads due to the interplay between the mechanical oscillations and sequential tunneling events. It is the nanoscopic equivalent of the classical bell experiment, in which a metallic ball bounces back and forth between two leads at constant high voltage drops, resulting in periodic charge transfer at the macroscale
[
68
]
. However, with modern nanomechanical setups at ultra-low temperatures, charge shuttling has been observed experimentally at the single-electron level
[
68
,
69
,
70
,
71
,
72
,
48
]
. These setups have been extensively studied theoretically, both in the classical and quantum regimes
[

74

75

76

77
,
78

79

80

81
,
82
,
83
,
84
,
85
,
86
]
. Recently, the classical setup has been thermodynamically studied
[
61
]
, and various autonomous thermodynamic cycles using the self-oscillations have been proposed
[
53
,
62
,
54
]
.

Here, we consider the classical description of the electron shuttling as a result of incoherent quantum tunneling between the leads and the oscillating dot. For simplicity, we assume the dot to be in the ultra-strong Coulomb blockade regime (i.e. ultra-low temperatures and/or small capacitances) such that the dot can accommodate only a single electron. In this context, the dot can be modeled as a single-electron level with addition energy
\mathbb{e}_{0}
, whose state is described by its occupancy
y\in\{0,1\}
. The dot is further hosted by a nanomechanical oscillator, with mass
m
and natural frequency
\omega

is the coupling strength with the dimension of the inverse distance. In these nanoelectromechanical (NEM) systems, this force can deform the mechanical component of the system due to electromechanical coupling, which to the lowest order has energy
\hat{U}_{\mathbb{XY}}=-\int\!dx\,f_{\mathbb{XY}}=-\alpha Vy\,x

\displaystyle\hat{E}(\vec{\Gamma},y)=\frac{1}{2}mv^{2}+\frac{1}{2}m\omega^{2}x^{2}+\mathbb{e}_{0}y-\alpha Vy\,x\;.

(117)

We can identify the energetic contribution due to the isolated electronic subsystem as

VI.1
Stochastic dynamics

The changes in dot state
y
are due to tunneling events between the dot and the two leads
\rho\in\{L,R\}
, assumed to be fermionic reservoirs with chemical potentials
\mu_{L}=\mathbb{e}_{0}+V/2
,
\mu_{R}=\mathbb{e}_{0}-V/2
and inverse temperatures
\beta=(1/k_{B}T)
. The chemical potential defines the work done by the reservoir
\rho
to add an electron
(y=0\to 1)
to the dot, i.e.,
w_{\rho}(0\to 1)=q_{e}\mu_{\rho}
. The oscillator also has a dissipative reservoir with inverse temperature
\beta_{\mathbb{X}}
. In this section, we will consider isothermal conditions, i.e
\beta_{\mathbb{X}}=\beta_{\mathbb{Y}}\equiv\beta
. The jump rates
\lambda_{\rho}^{y,y^{\prime}}(x)
for individual tunneling events in the dot are given as

\displaystyle\lambda_{L}^{1,0}(x)

\displaystyle=\Gamma_{b}e^{-x/\lambda}f_{L}(\mathbb{e}_{0}-\alpha Vx),\hskip 48.36958pt\lambda_{L}^{0,1}(x)=\Gamma_{b}e^{-x/\lambda}[1-f_{L}(\mathbb{e}_{0}-\alpha Vx)];

(118)

\displaystyle\lambda_{R}^{1,0}(x)

\displaystyle=\Gamma_{b}e^{x/\lambda}f_{R}(\mathbb{e}_{0}-\alpha Vx),\hskip 52.35297pt\lambda_{R}^{0,1}(x)=\Gamma_{b}e^{x/\lambda}[1-f_{R}(\mathbb{e}_{0}-\alpha Vx)];

where
f_{\rho}(e)=[\exp(q_{e}\beta(e-\mu_{\rho}))+1]^{-1}
is the Fermi distribution,
\lambda
is the characteristic tunneling length and
\Gamma_{b}
is the bare tunneling rate. Since the tunneling probability exponentially decays with the distance from leads, the tunneling amplitude (or kinetic component) of the jump rates also depends on position
x
as
\sqrt{\lambda_{\rho}^{1,0}\lambda_{\rho}^{0,1}}=\Gamma_{b}e^{\pm x/\lambda}
. Note that the above rates are thermodynamically consistent, satisfying the local detailed balance condition (Eq. (
5
)). Hence, the stochastic dynamics of the dot and the oscillator are coupled both through their energetic interaction
\hat{U}_{\mathbb{XY}}(x,y)=-\alpha Vy\,x
and also through the position-dependent tunneling amplitude
\Gamma_{b}e^{\pm x/\lambda}
. The master equation (Eq. (
6
)) for the electron shuttle follows:

\displaystyle d_{t}P_{t}(\vec{\Gamma},y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(2y-1)[J_{L}^{1,0}(\vec{\Gamma})+J_{R}^{1,0}(\vec{\Gamma})]+\left[-v\partial_{x}+\frac{1}{m}\partial_{v}(m\omega^{2}x+\gamma v-\alpha Vq)+\frac{\gamma}{m^{2}\beta_{\mathbb{X}}}\,\partial_{v}^{2}\right]P_{t}(\vec{\Gamma},q).

(119)

Here, the probability currents
J_{L/R}^{1,0}(\vec{\Gamma})
flowing through left
(L)
and right
(R)
leads, are given as

\displaystyle J_{L/R}^{1,0}(\vec{\Gamma})=\lambda_{L/R}^{1,0}(x)P_{t}(\vec{\Gamma},0)-\lambda_{L/R}^{0,1}(x)P_{t}(\vec{\Gamma},1).

(120)

The stochastic dynamics of an electron shuttle can be qualitatively understood as follows. Due to tunneling events from the leads, an unoccupied dot becomes occupied and makes the grain charged. Hence, at finite voltage
V
across the leads, the grain experiences a force
f_{\mathbb{XY}}
, pushing the grain towards the right lead with lower potential
\mu_{R}
. As the grain moves closer to the right lead with
x>0
, the tunneling rates increase exponentially
\lambda_{R}\propto e^{+x/\lambda}
, facilitating the transfer of charge from the dot to the right lead and leaving the grain temporarily uncharged. Subsequently, the restoring force of the oscillator pulls the grain back beyond its equilibrium position toward the left lead, leading to
x<0
. As the grain moves closer to the left lead, the tunneling amplitude to the left lead,
\lambda_{L}\propto e^{-x/\lambda}
, increases. This enables the charge to tunnel from the left lead to the dot, thereby recharging the grain and repeating the process. Hence, the applied voltage
V
induces a self-oscillatory motion of the grain, driven by the electromechanical force
f_{\mathbb{XY}}
.

The transition to self-oscillation depends on the interplay between damping, characterized by a damping coefficient
\gamma
, and the strength of the electromechanical force
f_{\mathbb{XY}}
, which occurs beyond a critical voltage
V_{\rm{cr}}

[
85
,
61
]
. For low voltages
V<V_{\rm{cr}}
, the damping dominates, confining the grain near its equilibrium position
(x,v)=(0,0)
. The tunneling dynamics of the dot thus resembles that of a stationary dot. However, beyond the critical voltage
V>V_{\rm{cr}}
, the electromechanical force
f_{\mathbb{XY}}
overcomes the damping resulting in self-oscillations. As the voltage
V
increases, the force
f_{\mathbb{XY}}
drives the oscillator to larger amplitudes, and the dot is likely to be occupied,
y=1
, when the grain is closer to the left lead,
x/\lambda\ll 0
, and vice versa. For very large voltages
V\gg V_{\rm{cr}}
, the amplitude of the oscillations becomes so large that the dot effectively does not experience the region around
x/\lambda\sim 0
, where tunneling is equally probable for both leads. This prevents short-circuit current flow between the leads. In this high-amplitude regime, the tunneling with the dot predominantly occurs due to a single lead at any time, effectively transferring a single electron from the left to the right lead in a single oscillation. This defines the “shuttling” regime of this electromechanical setup. Note that the stochastic dynamics of Eq. (
119
) does not have a sharp transition to self-oscillation mainly due to the thermal fluctuations in the oscillator. But the meanfield dynamics (discussed in the next Sec.
VI.1.1
) provides a reliable estimate of the transition, as previously shown in
[
61
]
.

In Fig.
2
(b), we plot a sample trajectory for the position
x
and the state of the dot
y
below and above the critical voltage
V_{\rm{cr}}/V_{T}
. Here, we consider the timescale-separated case, with bare tunneling rates significantly higher than the oscillator’s natural frequency, i.e.
\Gamma_{b}\gg\omega
. For small applied voltages
V<V_{\rm{cr}}
(top panel), the oscillator oscillates irregularly due to its thermal fluctuations around
x=0
, similar to the equilibrium scenario (
V=0
). In this case, the dot behaves as in stationary condition with many tunneling events from both leads. However, above a critical voltage
V>V_{\rm{cr}}
(bottom panel), the amplitude of the oscillations is larger and the oscillations also become more coherent, which characterizes the regime of self-oscillations. Notice also that there are fewer tunneling events as the dot interacts mainly with a single lead.

The onset of self-oscillations can clearly be seen in the marginal distribution
P(x,v)
of the oscillator. In Fig.
2
(c) (top panel), we plot the marginal distribution
P_{\rm{ss}}^{\mathbb{X}}(x,v)
in the steady-state as we increase the applied voltage
V
. At zero voltage drop
V=0
, the oscillator and the dot relax independently to its equilibrium, with its stationary behavior of the oscillator described by its Gibbs density
P^{\mathbb{X}}(x,v)\propto\exp(-\beta(m\omega^{2}x^{2}+mv^{2})/2)
. As the voltage increases below the critical voltage
0<V<V_{\rm{cr}}
, the probability density still peaks around zero, but with a larger standard deviation. However, above the critical voltage
V>V_{\rm{cr}}
, the probability density peaks around a circular orbit, reflecting the self-oscillating regime. In this regime, further increasing the voltage
V
leads to increasing amplitude of oscillations. For the timescale separated parameters considered in Fig.
2
(b) and (c), achieving the shuttling behavior requires very large oscillation amplitudes, which occur at voltages far above the critical threshold,
V\gg V_{\rm{cr}}
, beyond the range of our analysis.

) of the electron shuttle with the predictions from the dynamical equations derived by taking the large mass limit (Sec.
V

IV

VI.1.1
Large mass limit : Meanfield dynamics

In Sec.
V
, we established that the stochastic dynamics described by Eq. (
119
) is equivalent to a meanfield (MF) approximation in the large mass limit, by keeping terms up to
\mathcal{O}(m^{-1})
in the full master equation. The MF approximation effectively neglects fluctuations of the oscillator in the jump dynamics, simplifying the tunneling rates, i.e.
\lambda_{L/R}(x)\approx\lambda_{L/R}(\langle x\rangle)

[
61

\displaystyle\dot{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mux\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}(t)

\displaystyle=\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muv\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu(t),

\displaystyle m\dot{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muv\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}(t)

where

is the the probability current to the left
(L)
and right (
R

V_{\rm{cr}}

[

]

. The critical voltage

corresponds to the voltage at which the real part of Jacobian of the MF dynamics Eq. (
121

[
61

However, the MF dynamics (Eq. (
121
)) captures the limit cycle attractor, it fails to reach a time-independent stationary state, which requires the higher order
\mathcal{O}(m^{-2})
diffusive term.

VI.1.2
Fast dot dynamics limit: Timescale separated dynamics

In the limit of fast tunneling compared to the oscillation time period, i.e. when
\tau_{\mathbb{Y}}=\Gamma_{b}^{-1}\ll\tau_{\mathbb{X}}=(2\pi)/\omega
, the timescale separated (TS) Fokker-Planck equation (Eq. (
102
)) for the marginal density of the oscillator in the dimensional form is given as:

\displaystyle d_{t}P^{\mathbb{X}}_{t}=\Big[-v\partial_{x}-\frac{\partial_{v}[f_{\mathbb{X}}^{\rm{eff}}(x)+g^{\rm{eff}}(x)-\gamma^{\rm{eff}}(x)v]}{m}+D^{\rm{eff}}(x)\partial_{v}^{2})\Big]P^{\mathbb{X}}_{t}+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{2}),

(122)

with effective conservative force
f_{\mathbb{X}}^{\rm{eff}}(x)
, non-conservative force
g^{\rm{eff}}(x)
, drift
\tilde{\Gamma_{b}}(x)
and diffusion coefficient
D^{\rm{eff}}(x)
, given as:

\displaystyle f_{\mathbb{X}}^{\rm{eff}}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-m\omega^{2}x-\alpha V\pi_{1}(x),

(123)

\displaystyle\gamma^{\rm{eff}}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\gamma+\frac{\alpha V}{\Gamma_{b}\chi(x)}\partial_{x}\pi_{1}(x),

(124)

\displaystyle D^{\rm{eff}}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\gamma}{m^{2}\beta}+\frac{\alpha^{2}V^{2}\pi_{1}(x)}{m^{2}\Gamma_{b}\chi(x)}[1-\pi_{1}(x)].

(125)

In the above expressions,
\pi_{1}(x)
is the stationary conditional probability that the dot is occupied (
y=1
) using the adiabatic approximation, i.e.
\pi_{1}(x)\equiv\mathbb{P}_{ss}^{(0)}(1|\vec{\Gamma})=[e^{-x/\lambda}f_{L}(\mathbb{e}_{0}-\alpha Vx)+e^{x/\lambda}f_{R}(\mathbb{e}_{0}-\alpha Vx)]/\chi(x)
with
\chi(x)=2\cosh{(x/\lambda)}
being the escape rate and the normalization condition imposes
\pi_{0}(x)=1-\pi_{1}(x)
. The effective dynamics (Eq. (
122
)) does not satisfy the detailed balance condition
\gamma^{\rm{eff}}(x)/(D^{\rm{eff}}(x)m^{2})\neq\beta
, which was not possible at the adiabatic level
\Gamma_{b}/\omega\to 0
, and also captures self-oscillations (Fig.
D.1
). However, for very large voltages
V\gg V_{\rm{cr}}
, the TS approximation fails, since there is basically one jump per half oscillation. In this regime, the oscillator acts as a shuttle, transferring one electron per period of an oscillation from the left (
L
) lead to the right (
R
) lead.

VI.2
Steady-state information thermodynamics

into the oscillator
\mathbb{X}

\displaystyle\dot{\mathcal{E}}^{\mathbb{X}}

VII
Conclusion and Discussion

In this article, we extended the framework of classical information thermodynamics to autonomous systems composed of two interacting subsystems: one governed by a Markov jump process and the other by underdamped diffusion. We derived the thermodynamic flows with respect to external heat, work reservoirs, and entropy production. We consistently derived the first and second laws that govern the thermodynamics of the composite system. Next, we derived the internal energetic and informational flows between the subsystems, both at the trajectory and average level. In doing so, we established local second laws for each subsystem, highlighting a measurement-feedback interpretation of their interactions. At the fluctuating level, we derived integrated fluctuation relations for the partially masked dynamics of the subsystems. Specifically, the partial entropy production, involving the information flows, due to the individual subsystem, satisfies this fluctuation relation. Hence, we showed that the traditional bipartite formalism for purely jump and diffusive dynamics, can be extended to such composite system with mixed dynamics.

We also analyzed two practically relevant regimes of operations of these systems: one with slow underdamped dynamics relative to the discrete jump dynamics, and the other in the large mass limit of the underdamped subsystem. Unlike purely jump or diffusive dynamics, we showed that the nonequilibrium dynamics of the fast (discrete) subsystem cannot drive the slow (underdamped) subsystem out of equilibrium purely through interactions under the adiabatic approximation, resulting in zero energy and information flows. To address this, we perturbatively derived dynamical equations beyond the adiabatic approximation, capturing the lowest-order contributions to energy and information flows and the associated nonequilibrium effects in slow subsystem. In the large-mass regime, we systematically extended the effective dynamics, capturing the lowest-order effects of damping and interactions beyond traditional Hamiltonian dynamics in the infinite-mass limit. We found that the dynamics in this regime correspond to a mean-field approximation, where non-zero energy flows are present, but information flows vanish due to the deterministic nature of the underdamped system.

Finally, we applied our theoretical framework to a paradigmatic model of a nanoelectromechanical system - a single-electron shuttle - in which self-oscillation arises from the coupling between electron tunneling through a quantum dot and the mechanical motion of a nanoresonator. Here, we demonstrated that in the large, but finite-mass regime, the mean-field dynamics effectively capture the transient stochastic behavior at the average level, as the system relaxes into a limit cycle within the self-oscillating regime. Similarly, we showed that the timescale-separated dynamics accurately represent the steady-state probability distribution and the mutual information, even in the self-oscillating regime with small amplitudes where multiple tunneling events occur per oscillation. We analyzed the energy and information flows, as the shuttle transitions towards the self-oscillations above a critical voltage. Under isothermal conditions, we demonstrated that for any applied voltage across the leads, the oscillator acts as a sensor, measuring the state of the dot, but using the energy supplied by the dot due to its tunneling events. We also found that the self-oscillating regime is primarily driven by energetic exchanges, with the information flows saturating as the dot dynamics becomes synchronized with mechanical oscillations for large voltages. Lastly, we analyzed the transduction efficiency of the shuttle in converting electrical work into mechanical oscillations.

Our thermodynamic framework opens up the avenue to study hybrid CMOS circuits
[
59
]
, coupling with inductors
[

,

and/or quartz crystals

Using timescale separation between jump and diffusive dynamics, we derived a modified Fokker-Planck equation for the underdamped subsystem, which requires the full spectral information of the generator of jump dynamics. Applying this approach to systems with macroscopic state spaces, such as CMOS circuits, remains an area for future exploration, with potential simplifications by considering only the slowest relaxation modes. In this article, we assume that the MJ process in the discrete subsystem depends only on the position of the underdamped subsystem. In contrast, if the jump dynamics were velocity-dependent, the fast Markov jump (MJ) dynamics could drive the slow underdamped (UD) subsystem out of equilibrium, even under the adiabatic approximation. This behavior does not occur when the jump dynamics depend solely on the position, as discussed in Sec.
IV
. However, a careful evaluation of the local detailed balance condition is necessary to ensure that the correct equilibrium state is reached, due to the odd parity of the velocity under time-reversal
[
90
,
91
]
. Additionally, further analysis is required to verify the validity of the generalized integrated fluctuation theorem (IFT) under these conditions.

In the isothermal case of the electron shuttle, we observed that self-oscillations are primarily driven by energy transduction rather than information flows. Since the minimum energetic cost to generate it and its maximum transduced energy (due to coherent oscillations) are scaled by its corresponding temperatures, the information flow becomes more significant in non-isothermal settings
[
92
,
10
]
. Further studies are required to identify the design principles such that the shuttle functions effectively as an information engine and assess its transduction efficiency in these contexts. Additionally, on the theoretical front, anomalous contributions to thermodynamic quantities have previously been observed when considering the overdamped approximation, neglecting inertia
[
93
,
94
]
. The systematic derivation of such anomalous contributions to information thermodynamics in the overdamped limit in such mixed dynamics is a topic of future research.

Appendix A
Second Law : Average Description

At the average description, the nonequilibrium system entropy at a given time
t
of the composite system is given as,

\displaystyle S(t)=\SumInt P_{t}(\vec{\Gamma},y)[s_{i}(y)-k_{B}\log P_{t}(\vec{\Gamma},y)].

Taking the time derivative of the Shannon entropy, we can distinguish the partial contributions from the individual subsystems and identify the total entropy production rate:

\displaystyle d_{t}S

\displaystyle=\SumInt d_{t}P_{t}(\vec{\Gamma},y)[s_{i}(y)-k_{B}\log P_{t}(\vec{\Gamma},y)]

\displaystyle=\SumInt\left(\sum_{\rho}\sum_{y^{\prime}}J_{\rho}^{y,y^{\prime}}(\vec{\Gamma})-\nabla.J_{\vec{\Gamma}}^{y}\right)[s_{i}(y)-k_{B}\log P_{t}(\vec{\Gamma},y)]

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\int\!d\vec{\Gamma}\!\sum_{\rho,y>y^{\prime}}J^{y,y^{\prime}}_{\rho}(\vec{\Gamma},t)\left[s_{i}(y)-s_{i}(y^{\prime})-k_{B}\log\frac{P_{t}(\vec{\Gamma},y)}{P_{t}(\vec{\Gamma},y^{\prime})}\right]}_{\dot{S}_{\mathbb{Y}}}+\underbrace{\vphantom{\sum_{\rho,y>y^{\prime}}}k_{B}\SumInt\nabla.\left[\vec{\mathcal{L}}^{\rm{diss}}P_{t}(\vec{\Gamma},y)\right]\log P_{t}(\vec{\Gamma},y)}_{\dot{S}_{\mathbb{X}}}.

The first term in the expression above
\dot{S}_{\mathbb{Y}}
denotes changes in the Shannon entropy of the system due to dynamics in subsystem
\mathbb{Y}
. It is the sum of the entropy flow to the reservoirs
\rho
,
\dot{\Sigma}_{\rho}^{r}=-\dot{Q}_{\rho}/T_{\mathbb{Y}}
, and the corresponding partial entropy production rate,
\dot{\sigma}_{\mathbb{Y}}
,

\displaystyle\dot{S}_{\mathbb{Y}}=\int d\vec{\Gamma}\sum_{\rho,y>y^{\prime}}J^{y,y^{\prime}}_{\rho}(\vec{\Gamma},t)\left[s_{i}(y)-s_{i}(y^{\prime})-k_{B}\log\frac{P_{t}(\vec{\Gamma},y)}{P_{t}(\vec{\Gamma},y^{\prime})}\right]=\frac{1}{T_{\mathbb{Y}}}\sum_{\rho}\dot{Q}_{\rho}+\dot{\sigma}_{\mathbb{Y}},

(196)

\displaystyle\dot{\sigma}_{\mathbb{Y}}=\sum_{\rho}\int d\vec{\Gamma}\sum_{y>y^{\prime}}J^{y,y^{\prime}}_{\rho}(\vec{\Gamma},t)\log\frac{\lambda_{\rho}^{y,y^{\prime}}(x)P_{t}(\vec{\Gamma},y^{\prime})}{\lambda_{\rho}^{y^{\prime},y}(x)P_{t}(\vec{\Gamma},y)}\geq 0.

(197)

The second term corresponding to the underdamped dynamics can be further split into the corresponding entropy change in reservoir
\mathbb{X}
,
-\langle\dot{Q}_{m}\rangle/T_{\mathbb{X}}
and partial entropy production rate
\dot{\sigma}_{\mathbb{X}}
,

\displaystyle\dot{S}_{\mathbb{X}}=k_{B}\SumInt\nabla.\left[\vec{\mathcal{L}}^{\rm{diss}}P_{t}(\vec{\Gamma},y)\right]\log P_{t}(\vec{\Gamma},y)=\frac{\langle\dot{Q}_{\mathbb{X}}\rangle}{T_{\mathbb{X}}}+\dot{\sigma}_{\mathbb{X}},

\displaystyle\dot{\sigma}_{\mathbb{X}}=\frac{\gamma}{T_{\mathbb{X}}}\SumInt P_{t}(\vec{\Gamma},y)\left[v+(m\beta_{\mathbb{X}})^{-1}\partial_{v}\log P_{t}(\vec{\Gamma},y)\right]^{2}\geq 0.

Appendix B
Marginalized dynamics and thermodynamics

Marginalization to one of the subsystems can be understood as a coarse-graining procedure that retains only the degrees of freedom specific to that subsystem at any given time. In this section, we explore the effective dynamics and thermodynamics of such coarse-grained states.

B.1
Marginal UD dynamics

We will first analyze the case with only continuous degrees of freedom
\vec{\Gamma}
, while integrating out the discrete electronic state
y
. This situation arises when one can only access the position and velocity of the subsystem
\mathbb{X}
, whereas changes in the discrete states are inaccessible. This is equivalent to the marginalization done for a coupled underdamped system in
[
17
]
. From the knowledge of the full dynamics, the corresponding Fokker-Planck equation for the marginal probability density,
P_{t}^{\mathbb{X}}(\vec{\Gamma})=\sum_{y}P_{t}(\vec{\Gamma},y)
, is given as:

\displaystyle d_{t}P_{t}^{\mathbb{X}}(\vec{\Gamma})=-\nabla.\Big(\left[\vec{\mathcal{L}}^{\rm{det}}_{\mathbb{X}}+\vec{\mathcal{L}}^{\rm{diss}}_{\mathbb{X}}\right]P_{t}^{\mathbb{X}}(\vec{\Gamma})\Big).

(214)

Here, the effective underdamped probability current is further split into deterministic and dissipative contributions:

\displaystyle\vec{\mathcal{L}}^{\rm{det}}_{\mathbb{X}}=\begin{bmatrix}v\\
\frac{1}{m}\left[-\partial_{x}\hat{U}_{\mathbb{X}}(x)+g^{y}(\vec{\Gamma},t)\right]\end{bmatrix},\;\;\;\vec{\mathcal{L}}^{\rm{diss}}_{\mathbb{X}}\equiv\begin{bmatrix}0\\
v+(\beta m)^{-1}\partial_{v}\log P_{t}^{\mathbb{X}}(\vec{\Gamma})\end{bmatrix}.

(215)

Comparing it with Eq. (
9
), the key difference is the emergence of an additional time-dependent non-conservative force
g^{y}(\vec{\Gamma},t)
, given as

g^{y}(\vec{\Gamma},t)\equiv\sum_{y}\mathbb{P}_{t}(y|\vec{\Gamma})\left[-\partial_{x}\hat{U}_{\mathbb{XY}}(x,y)+g(x,y)\right]=\sum_{y}\mathbb{P}_{t}(y|\vec{\Gamma})f_{\mathbb{XY}}(x,y),

(216)

due to the influence of the discrete state dynamics through the interaction force
f_{\mathbb{XY}}(x,y)
. Note that the dynamics in Eq. (
214
) is not a closed equation, as it still depends on the time-dependent conditional distribution
\mathbb{P}_{t}(y|\vec{\Gamma})
.

The marginalized dynamics of Eq. (
214
) mimics that of an underdamped particle with internal energy
E_{\mathbb{X}}=(1/2)mv^{2}+\hat{U}_{\mathbb{X}}(x)
influenced by a non-conservative force
g^{y}(\vec{\Gamma},t)
in a thermal bath with inverse temperature
\beta_{\mathbb{X}}
. The first law for the marginal dynamics is given as,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}E_{\mathbb{X}}

\displaystyle=-\gamma\left[\langle v^{2}\rangle-\frac{1}{\beta m}\right]+\int\!\!d\vec{\Gamma}\;P^{\mathbb{X}}_{t}(\vec{\Gamma})\>g^{y}(\vec{\Gamma},t)\>v

\displaystyle=\dot{Q}_{\mathbb{X}}+\dot{W}_{\mathbb{X}}-\dot{\mathcal{E}}^{\mathbb{X}}.

(217)

Similar to the composite system, the marginal entropy production can be identified from the sum of entropy change in the subsystem
\mathbb{X}
along with its reservoir entropy, leading to:

\displaystyle\dot{\Sigma}_{\mathbb{X}}

\displaystyle=d_{t}S_{\mathbb{X}}^{\text{marg}}-\beta_{\mathbb{X}}\dot{Q}_{\mathbb{X}}=\dot{\sigma}_{\mathbb{X}}+\dot{\mathcal{I}}^{\mathbb{X}}_{F}+\dot{\mathcal{I}}^{\mathbb{X}}_{S}.

(218)

However, as mentioned in main text, this quantity can take negative values leading to apparent violations of the second law.

A similar apparent violation of the second law has previously been observed in the entropy production
\dot{\Sigma}_{\mathbb{X}}
of molecular refrigeration systems
[
95
,
96
,
97
]
, where a velocity-dependent force performs the feedback cooling. The second law is restored in such systems by introducing an ”entropy pumping” term. As noted in
[
17
]
, this entropy pumping contribution is exactly equal to the force contribution of the information flow
\dot{\mathcal{I}}^{\mathbb{X}}_{F}=\frac{k_{B}}{m}\int d\vec{\Gamma}\;P_{t}^{\mathbb{X}}(\vec{\Gamma})\partial_{v}\;g^{y}(\vec{\Gamma},t)
, arising from interactions with the subsystem creating the force. This leads to a positive corrected entropy production
\dot{\Sigma}^{\rm{pump}}_{\mathbb{X}}
at
\mathbb{X}
, given as:

\displaystyle\dot{\Sigma}^{\rm{pump}}_{\mathbb{X}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle d_{t}S_{\mathbb{X}}^{\text{marg}}-\beta_{\mathbb{X}}\dot{Q}_{\mathbb{X}}-\dot{\mathcal{I}}^{\mathbb{X}}_{F}

(219)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\beta_{\mathbb{X}}\gamma\SumInt P_{t}^{\mathbb{X}}(\vec{\Gamma})\left[v+(\beta_{\mathbb{X}}m)^{-1}\partial_{v}\log P_{t}^{\mathbb{X}}(\vec{\Gamma})\right]^{2}\geq 0

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dot{\sigma}_{\mathbb{X}}+\dot{\mathcal{I}}^{\mathbb{X}}_{S}\leq\dot{\sigma}_{\mathbb{X}}.

(229)

However, this corrected entropy production
\dot{\Sigma}^{\rm{pump}}_{\mathbb{X}}
only provides a lower bound to the true partial entropy production at
\mathbb{X}
, i.e.
\dot{\Sigma}^{\rm{pump}}_{\mathbb{X}}\leq\dot{\sigma}_{\mathbb{X}}
, because it neglects the entropic part of the information flow
\dot{\mathcal{I}}^{\mathbb{X}}_{S}\leq 0
. This entropic part of the information flow,
\dot{\mathcal{I}}^{\mathbb{X}}_{S}\leq 0
, is lost through such a marginalization procedure. Thus, while the entropy pumping term corrects the negative entropy production, it does not account for all the entropy exchanged due to subsystem interactions, and the partial entropy production at a subsystem must include both force and entropic contributions of the information flow.

Putting it all together, we can reformulate the marginal entropy balance as follows:

\displaystyle d_{t}S_{\mathbb{X}}^{\text{marg}}=\beta_{\mathbb{X}}\dot{Q}_{\mathbb{X}}+\dot{\Sigma}_{\mathbb{X}}+\dot{\mathcal{I}}^{\mathbb{X}}_{F}.

(230)

This is equivalent to the entropy balance done for molecular refrigerator using feedback control of their velocities
[
95
]
.

B.2
Marginal MJ dynamics

Now we will shift our focus to the complementary case where we can only keep track of the electronic degrees of freedom
y
. Here, we will be left with the marginal distribution
P_{t}^{\mathbb{Y}}(y)=\int d\vec{\Gamma}\>P_{t}(\vec{\Gamma},y)
, after integrating out continuous degrees of freedom. Integrating Eq. (
6
), one finds that the dynamics in the discrete states can still be described by a Markov jump process, given by

\displaystyle d_{t}P_{t}^{\mathbb{Y}}(y)=\sum_{\rho}\sum_{y^{\prime}}\left[W_{\rho}^{y,y^{\prime}}(\vec{\Gamma},t)P_{t}^{\mathbb{Y}}(y^{\prime})-W_{\rho}^{y^{\prime},y}(\vec{\Gamma},t)P_{t}^{\mathbb{Y}}(y)\right],

(231)

where we have defined marginalized jump rates
W_{\rho}^{y,y^{\prime}}(\vec{\Gamma},t)\equiv\int d\vec{\Gamma}\,\mathbb{P}_{t}(\vec{\Gamma}|y^{\prime})\lambda_{\rho}^{y,y^{\prime}}(x)
. As in the previous case, Eq. (
231
) cannot be solved independently due to the dependence of
W_{\rho}
on the conditional distribution
\mathbb{P}_{t}(\vec{\Gamma}|y^{\prime})
. However, there is no trivial identification of new non-conservative force from the effective dynamics in Eq. (
231
). Assuming that the energy of the electronic subsystem is described
\hat{U}_{\mathbb{Y}}(y)
, we can write the first law as:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}E_{\mathbb{Y}}(y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\rho,y>y^{\prime}}\int d\vec{\Gamma}\>J^{y,y^{\prime}}_{\rho}(\vec{\Gamma})\left[\hat{U}_{\mathbb{Y}}(y)-\hat{U}_{\mathbb{Y}}(y^{\prime})\right]

(232)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dot{Q}_{\mathbb{Y}}+\dot{W}_{\mathbb{Y}}-\dot{\mathcal{E}}^{\mathbb{Y}}.

Assuming that one can measure the changes in the environment entropy
-\beta_{\mathbb{Y}}\langle\dot{Q}_{\rho}\rangle
, the marginal entropy production can be computed as:

\displaystyle\dot{\Sigma}_{\mathbb{Y}}=d_{t}S_{\mathbb{X}}^{\text{marg}}-\beta_{\mathbb{Y}}\sum_{\rho}\dot{Q}_{\rho}.

(233)

From the entropy balance for the marginal dynamics in subsystem
\mathbb{Y}
, we can connect this to the partial entropy production
\dot{\sigma}_{\mathbb{Y}}
, as:

\displaystyle\dot{\sigma}_{\mathbb{Y}}=d_{t}S_{\mathbb{X}}^{\text{marg}}-\beta_{\mathbb{Y}}\sum_{\rho}\dot{Q}_{\rho}-\dot{\mathcal{I}}^{\mathbb{Y}}=\dot{\Sigma}_{\mathbb{Y}}-\dot{\mathcal{I}}^{\mathbb{Y}}.

(234)

where
\dot{\mathcal{I}}^{\mathbb{Y}}
is the directional information flow to the electronic subsystem, whose explicit form is given in Eq. (
A
).

Appendix D
Electron Shuttle: Beyond adiabatic approximation in the energy space

In this section, we show the derivation of the modified Fokker-Planck equation for the electron shuttle using the general framework of Sec.
IV.2
. The generator
\mathcal{L}_{\mathbb{Y}}
of the jump dynamics due to tunneling events is given as,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathbb{Y}}=\begin{bmatrix}-[\lambda_{L}^{0,1}(x)+\lambda_{R}^{0,1}(x)]&\lambda_{L}^{1,0}(x)+\lambda_{R}^{1,0}(x)\vskip 5.69046pt\\
\lambda_{L}^{0,1}(x)+\lambda_{R}^{0,1}(x)&-[\lambda_{L}^{1,0}(x)+\lambda_{R}^{1,0}(x)]\end{bmatrix}.

(247)

The eigenvalues of the above generator are
0
and
-\Gamma_{b}\chi(x)
, where
\Gamma_{b}\chi(x)=\sum_{\rho,y,y^{\prime}}\lambda_{\rho}^{y,y^{\prime}}(x)=2\Gamma_{b}\cosh(x/\lambda)
is the escape rate. The left
\bra{L_{0}}
and right
\ket{R_{0}}
eigenvectors to the
0
eigenvalue are:
\bra{L_{0}}=(1,1)
and
\ket{R_{0}}=(\pi_{0}(x),\pi_{1}(x))^{\mkern 2.0mu\dagger}
respectively. Note that the right eigenvector
\ket{R_{0}}
is the steady-state probability at the adiabatic limit. Similarly, the left
\bra{L_{\chi}}
and right
\ket{R_{\chi}}
eigenvectors corresponding to the remaining eigenvalue
-\Gamma\chi(x)
are:
\bra{L_{\chi}}=[\pi_{1}(x),\,\pi_{0}(x)]
and
\ket{R_{\chi}}=[1,-1]^{\mkern 2.0mu\dagger}
. The generalized inverse
\mathcal{A}^{-1}_{y,y^{\prime}}(x)
is then given as,

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}^{-1}_{y,y^{\prime}}(x)=\frac{1}{\Gamma\chi(x)}\begin{bmatrix}-\pi_{1}(x)&\pi_{0}(x)\\
\pi_{1}(x)&-\pi_{0}(x)\end{bmatrix}.

(248)

Substituting the
\mathcal{A}^{-1}_{y,y^{\prime}}(x)
to Eq. (
97
), the conditional distribution
\mathbb{P}_{ss}^{(0+1)}(y|\vec{\Gamma})
for the electron shuttle simplifies to:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\rm{TS}}^{(0+1)}(y|\vec{\Gamma})=\pi_{y}(x)+v\frac{(2y-1)\nu(x)}{\Gamma_{b}}+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{2}),

(249)

where
\nu(x)=\left[-\partial_{x}\pi_{1}(x)+\alpha V\beta_{\mathbb{X}}\pi_{1}(x)\pi_{0}(x)\right]/\chi(x)
is the contribution due to the oscillator dynamics. In this expression, we have used the equilibrium adiabatic solution for the marginal density, satisfying
\partial_{v}\log P_{\rm{ad}}=-\beta mv+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon)
. As expected from the dynamics, the velocity dependence of
\mathbb{P}_{\rm{TS}}^{(0+1)}(y|\vec{\Gamma})
implies that the dot is more probable to be occupied at
x=0
only when coming from the left lead, i.e.
v>0
. We also derive the modified FP equation for the shuttle (Eq. (
122
)) using
\mathcal{A}^{-1}_{y,y^{\prime}}(x)
to Eq. (
102

To further gain insight on the oscillatory dynamics, one can equivalently work in energy-phase
(E_{\mathbb{X}},\phi_{\mathbb{X}})
space. For the electron shuttle, the transformation is the following:
\hat{E}_{\mathbb{X}}=(1/2)m\omega^{2}x^{2}+(1/2)mv^{2}
and phase
\tan\phi_{\mathbb{X}}=\omega x/v

{\mathbb{X}}
for ease of notation here, i.e.
(E,\phi)\equiv(\hat{E}_{\mathbb{X}},\phi_{\mathbb{X}})

E
used here is not the total energy of the composite system. This allows us to parametrize the position
x
and the velocity
v

(E,\phi)

\displaystyle x=\sqrt{\frac{2E}{m\omega^{2}}}\sin{\phi},\hskip 28.45274ptv=\sqrt{\frac{2E}{m}}\cos{\phi}

\tau_{E}\gg\tau_{\phi}
