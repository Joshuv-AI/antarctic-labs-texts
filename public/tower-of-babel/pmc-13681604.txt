Minimally invasive Ayurveda management of recurrent and acute-on-chronic non-healing venous leg ulceration – a Case Report
Authors: AS Santhi Krishna, Sujith Eranezhath, Arun T Namboothiri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSlow healing and recurrent venous leg ulcers resulting from varicose veins due to lower limb chronic venous insufficiency are common vascular complications. If left untreated, they may lead to severe outcomes such as hemorrhage and, in extreme cases, limb amputation.We report the case of a 61-year-old male with bilateral lower limb varicosities of 10 years’ duration, presenting with multiple painful venous leg ulcers that recurred several months after phlebectomy due to patient non-compliance. Physical examination revealed exquisitely tender, exudative, irregular-bordered, multiple shallow ulcers with sloping edges and a putrid odor. The surrounding tissue was oedematous and indurated, predominantly involving the areas in and around the ankle joints. Aerobic wound swab culture showed heavy growth of Klebsiella pneumoniae and Proteus hauseri, with moderate growth of Staphylococcus aureus and Streptococcus pyogenes.One month of Ayurvedic intervention employing vrana sodhana and vrana ropana cikitsa resulted in accelerated wound healing in recurrent, acute-on-chronic, non-healing venous leg ulcerations.Keywords: Acute-on-chronic non-healing venous leg ulceration, Ayurveda, Varicose veins

Introduction

Venous leg ulcers (VLUs), also known as varicose ulcers, are chronic, open ulcerations that are often recurrent. They are most prevalent among individuals with concomitant chronic venous insufficiency or chronic venous disease (CVD) and account for nearly 80% of all lower-extremity ulcerations [1]. The prodromal stage of the disease is characterized by damaged venous valves and impaired venous return due to venous occlusion, which subsequently triggers an inflammatory response. These pathological processes collectively contribute to the complex heterogeneity of ulcer-initiating events, including endothelial damage and platelet aggregation, eventually leading to intracellular edema and venous leg ulcer formation [2].

From an Ayurvedic perspective, the pathology of VLU can be understood through the concepts of siragranthi (nodular growths within the body channels) [SAT-D.8651] and dushta vrana (putrefied or chronic ulcer) [SAT-K-10]. Siragranthi is considered a later manifestation of vitiation of doṣa (regulatory functional factors), rakta, mamsa, and medo dhatu (major structural components of the body). This pathological state predisposes the body to cellular and tissue damage, particularly in the dependent parts. With chronicity and further exacerbation of the condition, siragranthi progresses into dushta vrana, the management of which is comprehensively described in the Susruta Samhita [3].

Although several conventional, surgical, and mechanical treatment modalities are available for the management of VLU [4], prevention of recurrence remains a significant challenge, highlighting the need for alternative therapeutic approaches. This case report demonstrates that one month of Ayurvedic interventions facilitated wound cleansing (vrana sodhana) and wound healing (vrana ropana), thereby accelerating re-epithelialization and neovascularization. These effects may help prevent exacerbation and reduce the recurrence rate of non-healing VLUs. While there are reports on the use of leech therapy in such cases [5], despite leech therapy being a recognized Ayurvedic modality, the present case focused on managing the condition using minimally invasive Ayurvedic interventions.

Patient information

A 61-year-old male with a 10-year history of chronic bilateral lower limb varicosities presented to our clinic in March 2022. The patient exhibited an acute-on-chronic presentation of multiple venous leg ulcerations associated with pain, occurring five months after undergoing phlebectomy. He had a history of beedi smoking (a cheap, unfiltered cigarette made of tobacco flakes wrapped in tendu or Piliostigma racemosum leaves), consuming 3–5 packets per day. The patient also reported a family history of varicose veins and followed an unhygienic lifestyle.

The patient was habitually required to stand for prolonged periods (8–10 hours/day). He developed an initial ulceration over the left lower leg in 2016, for which he underwent phlebectomy; however, recurrence was noted five months following the procedure. Subsequently, multiple small ulcerations developed and progressively worsened due to patient negligence and failure to seek timely and adequate medical care.

The patient had previously consulted the same clinic in January 2019 for a non-healing venous leg ulcer. Following eight months of Ayurvedic intervention, along with ulcer care, dietary regulation, and lifestyle modifications, wound healing was achieved with residual hyperpigmented atrophic scarring. From September 2019 onward, the patient remained on medication for the management of varicose veins without ulcer recurrence. However, treatment compliance gradually declined once ulcer healing was achieved.

Toward the end of 2021, influenced by word-of-mouth information regarding vegetable therapy, the patient independently initiated this therapy without the consent of the treating physician. The intervention involved dietary practices contrary to pathya-apathya (wholesome and unwholesome regimens). Within two weeks of undergoing vegetable therapy, the patient developed recurrence of venous leg ulcers with increased severity in both lower limbs, prompting him to report back to our clinic in April 2022.

On clinical examination, multiple ulcers were observed over the medial, anterior, and lateral aspects of the distal third of both lower limbs. There was no history of diabetes mellitus, hypertension, or other systemic conditions contributory to the current disease pathophysiology.

Clinical findings

At the time of admission, the patient was well built, well nourished, and of masculine habitus, with a height of 5 feet 6 inches and a body weight of 185 pounds. Detailed examination and consultation revealed that the patient had vata–pitta prakrti (constitutional type), uttama Sara (excellent tissue quality), pravara samhanana (well-developed physique), and madhyama satva (moderate psychological strength). The patient was satmya (habituated) to tea consumption (5–6 cups/day) and exhibited pravara jarana sakti (good digestive capacity) and pravara ahara sakti (adequate food intake), with avara vyayama sakti (low physical endurance).

All systemic examinations were within normal limits. Varicosities were present in both lower limbs, with a positive Moses’ sign, and pedal pulses were palpable. Cutaneous examination revealed exquisitely tender, exudative, irregular-bordered, multiple shallow ulcers with pale granulation tissue over the medial, anterior, and lateral aspects of the distal third of the lower limbs, exhibiting sloping edges. The ulcers had a putrid odor with seropurulent discharge, and both forelegs were oedematous with severe tenderness. The surrounding skin showed pigmentation, induration, and tenderness.

Diagnostic assessment

The patient presented to our outpatient department with multiple exudative wounds associated with discharge and edema involving both lower limbs. Past history revealed a similar episode of ulceration in 2019, which was managed with Ayurvedic medications and subsequently healed, although the wound exhibited delayed healing.

Clinical examination revealed ulcers that were tender, warm, erythematous, swollen, and discharging pus, suggesting a slow-healing process. The patient's clinical presentation at the time of admission was characterized by putrid discharge mixed with blood (puti-asrk srava), chronicity of the ulcer (cira-sthiti vrana), markedly widened wound margins (ati-vivrta), excessive hardness on palpation (ati-kathina), increased local temperature (atyusna), a gruesome wound appearance (bhairava darsana), offensive odor (amanojna gandha), severe pain (atyartha vedana), intense burning sensation (atyartha daha), excessive suppuration (atyartha paka), severe itching (atyartha kandu), and marked swelling (atyartha sopha).

Considering the patient's occupation as a tea shopkeeper working in an unhygienic environment, inpatient admission was advised. Treatment was initiated upon admission. After six days of combined Internal and external therapies (as detailed in Table 1), pain had reduced; however, the wound response remained suboptimal. Following consultation with a microbiologist and suspecting polymicrobial infection, an aerobic wound swab culture was performed. The culture revealed heavy growth of gram-negative organisms, Klebsiella pneumoniae and Proteus hauseri, along with moderate growth of gram-positive organisms, Staphylococcus aureus and Streptococcus pyogenes. Bacterial identification was carried out using proteomic analysis (MALDI-TOF/MS Biotyper). These findings indicated the need to target gram-negative bacterial predominance, prompting modification of the treatment strategy.

Based on the pathological features and current clinical presentation, the condition was diagnosed as dusta vrana (putrefied wound) [[6]; [7]], and the treatment plan was formulated accordingly. The patient was a known case of siragranthi (bilateral lower limb varicosities) for nearly 10 years, with dusta vrana manifesting as a complication. The long-term prognosis of venous leg ulcers includes progression to chronic non-healing or granulating ulcers with potential systemic infections, significantly affecting quality of life (QoL). However, recurrence and disease progression can be effectively managed through periodic follow-up and adherence to modified dietary and lifestyle guidelines.

Therapeutic intervention

Based on the severity and clinical presentation, dusta vrana chikitsa (line of management for putrefied wounds) was adopted in the present case. Ayurveda advocates sasti upakrama (sixty therapeutic measures for wound management) [[8]], among which parisheka (therapeutic streaming), bandhana karma (wound bandaging), and the use of kasaya (medicated decoctions) and taila (medicated oils) for vrana sodhana (wound cleansing) and vrana ropana (wound healing) were employed. The patient underwent 14 days of Ayurvedic Oral medications and external therapies, with no concomitant conventional medications administered during the hospital stay. Strict adherence to dietary regulations and treatment schedules was ensured.

The patient was administered a specially prepared kasayam [9], consisting of 10 g each of Yastimadhu (Glycyrrhiza glabra), Manjista (Rubia cordifolia), Asana (Pterocarpus marsupium), Nimba-tvak (bark of Azadirachta indica), Aragvadha-mula (root of Cassia fistula), and Guduci (Tinospora cordifolia). These drugs were boiled in 1200 mL of water and reduced to 150 mL; the decoction was administered twice daily along with Kaiśora Guggulu [10,11]. This regimen was intended to initiate wound cleansing and healing while enhancing cellular rejuvenation (rasayana) and blood purification (rakta sodhaka), thereby pacifying pitta and promoting resolution of wound-related disorders (sarva vrana nasanam).

Triphala Guggulu [[12]] was administered to mitigate the acute inflammatory phase (sopha-hara), thereby limiting cellular damage. Nyagrodhadi Kasayam [[13]] was prescribed to facilitate holistic tissue healing, while Gandharvahastādi Eranda Tailam [14] was administered daily to provide anti-inflammatory (sopha-hara), analgesic (sula-hara), and bowel-regulating effects (vata anulomana). Additionally, Dasamularistam[15], Pippalyasavam[16], and Dhanvantaram Gutika [[17]] were included during inpatient care to impart anti-inflammatory (sopha-hara), analgesic (sula-hara), antimicrobial (krimihara), strength-promoting (balya), hemopoietic (rakta vardhaka), immunomodulatory (vyadhi ksamatva), and antipyretic (jvara-hara) effects. As wound exudation and inflammatory signs were prominent, Triphala Kasaya vrana parisheka (therapeutic streaming with medicated decoction) was administered for 14 days. However, owing to the presence of gram-positive cocci and gram-negative bacilli, the wound response remained suboptimal despite kasaya dhara. To counteract the persistence of gram-negative bacilli, Apamarga kshara karma (therapeutic application of caustic alkali) was performed for three days, followed by vrana pariṣeka to achieve optimal bioburden reduction, particularly targeting gram-positive cocci.

Vrana bandhana (wound dressing) using Jatyadi Taila [18] was carried out nightly for 14 days, with removal in the morning. During the final seven days of inpatient care, simple gauze bandaging following vrana parisheka was advised.

Follow-up and outcomes

Recurrence of venous leg ulcers was observed due to patient negligence and non-compliance with the treating physician's advice, following which inpatient care was recommended. The patient's condition was evaluated based on clinical features and wound measurements recorded at baseline and at the endpoint during inpatient admission, as well as during follow-up outpatient visits. No recurrence reported up to Last patient visit to hospital on 11-01-2023.

Complete healing of the venous leg ulcer lesions with active scar tissue formation was observed within 30 days of initiation of treatment. Discussion

Ayurvedic management of venous leg ulcers (VLUs) is well established, minimally invasive, and commonly practiced in Ayurvedic hospitals. However, such cases remain underreported in the scientific literature due to factors such as individualized treatment approaches and the limited emphasis on research and publication among general Ayurvedic practitioners. The present case highlights a personalized Ayurvedic management strategy for a complex VLU, in which non-compliance and unauthorized therapeutic practices undertaken under the guise of naturopathy and Ayurveda adversely affected the patient's condition. This report demonstrates that Ayurveda is capable of effectively managing VLUs even following iatrogenic complications arising from unauthorized medical interventions. A long-standing history of bilateral varicosities complicated by recurrent ulceration was successfully managed using Ayurvedic medications in conjunction with dietary and lifestyle modifications.

At the time of admission, following comprehensive assessment, therapeutic streaming with Triphala Kasaya (vrana parisheka) was initiated to cleanse the wound and promote re-epithelialization. However, after six days, the wound response was suboptimal, necessitating revision of the treatment plan. This inadequate response was later attributed to the heavy growth of gram-negative bacteria identified on wound culture. These microbiological findings enabled an informed clinical decision, leading to the incorporation of therapeutic application of caustic material (kshara karma) to counteract the thick, viscous pathogenic growth at the wound site.

Kshara karma exerts therapeutic excision (chedana), scraping (lekhana), incision (bhedana), and antimicrobial (krimighna) actions, thereby preventing unhealthy granulation tissue formation and reducing wound bioburden. This intervention helps negate the mechanisms underlying delayed wound healing and recurrence. Following Apamarga kshara karma and subsequent wound bandaging (vrana bandhana) with Jatyadi Taila, the ulcers demonstrated marked improvement, with evidence of re-epithelialization at the wound margins and the development of healthy granulation tissue.

The present case report demonstrates the benefits of one month of Ayurvedic intervention employing vrana sodhana and vrana ropana cikitsa in accelerating re-epithelialization, neovascularization, and overall wound healing. Significant symptomatic relief was observed within 13 days of inpatient care, with complete healing of VLUs noted within two weeks of follow-up, and no recurrence reported up to last patient visit to hospital on 11-01-2023. The patient's quality of life (QoL) improved considerably, with a marked reduction in anxiety levels and restoration of functional independence. Objective wound assessment, including measurements, was systematically documented (Table 2). After one month of treatment, the VLUs showed substantial reduction in size and depth, minimal exudation, and near-complete closure of the wounds.

Importantly, this case also underscores the value of integrating Ayurvedic diagnostic principles with selected conventional diagnostic tools, such as wound swab culture. Such integration enables Ayurvedic physicians to make informed, evidence-supported clinical decisions while remaining aligned with traditional treatment frameworks.

As a single case report, a direct, definitive causal relationship cannot be universally established, and further controlled studies are warranted.

Conclusion

Recurrent and acute-on-chronic non-healing venous leg ulcerations were better managed with minimal invasive Ayurveda interventions. However, understanding the reasons for non-healing of wound by integrating the diagnostic tools and expertise of Ayurveda with conventional advanced methods played a vital role in the current treatment protocol. Ayurveda interventions with vrana sodhana (wound cleansing) and ropana cikitsa (wound healing) has accelerated the reepithelialization, neovascularization and wound healing activity and has also prevented the exacerbation and rate of recurrence of non-healing VLU. In conclusion, this case is also an example to demonstrate that Ayurveda is capable of managing VLUs even after an iatrogenic complication due to unauthorized medical intervention.

Patient perspective

"I have been suffering from problems in my legs for many years. I had swollen veins and wounds that kept coming back, even after surgery. The wounds were very painful, had bad smell, and made it difficult for me to stand or work properly. I was worried and frustrated because nothing seemed to give lasting relief. When I came for this treatment, I stayed in the hospital (from 06/04/22 to 19/04/22) and followed the doctor's advice carefully. The medicines, dressings, and food restrictions were explained clearly to me. Slowly, the pain reduced, the discharge stopped, and the wounds started closing. Within a few weeks (by 28/04/22), the wounds healed and I felt much better. Now I can stand and move more comfortably, and I am able to manage my daily activities without fear or anxiety. I have understood the importance of following medical advice, proper diet, and lifestyle changes. This treatment has given me relief, confidence, and a better quality of life."
