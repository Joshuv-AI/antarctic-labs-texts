Field performance of inclined H-beam strength composite piles as internal bracing in a deep soft soil excavation
Authors: Lijun Sun, Yekuan Geng, Jun Liu, Yun Miao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The design of internal bracing is critical for controlling deformations in soft-soil foundation pits, especially in congested urban areas where ground anchors are prohibited. This study investigates a novel combined support system consisting of 45° inclined H-beam strength composite piles (SCPs) and triaxial deep-mixing columns (TDCs). A full-scale field investigation was conducted in a 6.15–7.10 m deep excavation in Nanjing mucky silty clay, adjacent to sensitive metro tunnels and dense underground utilities. Field load tests on three inclined SCPs demonstrated ultimate vertical capacities of 1,350–2,090 kN, with total settlements limited to 14.07–15.17 mm. Regression analysis using a power-function model (s = aQb) predicted extrapolated ultimate capacities of 2,299–3,682 kN, indicating that current technical specifications (JGJ/T 327) provide reliable but conservative estimates (approximately 62%–84%). Continuous field monitoring revealed that the maximum lateral wall deflection was confined to 6.36 mm (less than 0.1%H), significantly superior to conventional cantilever systems. These results validate a structural system transformation from a quasi-cantilever to a quasi-simply supported configuration enabled by the inclined bracing. This study provides a practical benchmark and quantified performance data for the application of inclined SCPs as a low-carbon, high-efficiency alternative to traditional internal bracing in complex soft-soil environments.

Introduction

With the extensive development of urban underground space, excavation projects are becoming increasingly prevalent1. However, excavation support in soft soils has long presented a persistent challenge in geotechnical engineering2–4. Conventional support systems, such as deep mixing columns, diaphragm walls, and pile-anchored structures, often face critical limitations. For instance, installing top supports can be impractical in large-scale projects, while ground anchors and tiebacks frequently extend beyond property lines5–7. These constraints often force the retaining system to function as a cantilever, subjecting it to excessive earth pressures and deformations. Furthermore, traditional methods struggle to align with modern low-carbon goals, leading to significant cost inefficiencies even among excavations of a similar depth. Given these issues, the design of internal bracing becomes critical, especially for the long straight sections of large excavations. Consequently, developing support systems that ensure structural safety, cost-effectiveness, and strict boundary compliance remains a paramount objective in geotechnical engineering8–10.

To address these challenges, Strength Composite Piles (SCPs) have emerged as a novel engineering solution for internal bracing. As defined in the current Technical Specification for Strength Composite Piles (JGJ/T 327)11, SCPs are constructed by compositing granular piles, flexible piles, and rigid piles to achieve synergistic reinforcement. The rigid central component, referred to as the inner core (e.g., H-beams, steel pipes), is surrounded by the outer core (e.g., jet-grouted cement-soil piles). By installing this core pile into a pre-existing soil–cement mixing (SMW) piles, the overall bearing capacity is significantly increased and deformation in soft clay is greatly reduced, owing to the high strength and stiffness of the inner cores12–14. While SCPs represent a relatively recent innovation in soft soil improvement, their application specifically as internal bracing in excavations remains limited15–17. Nevertheless, their excellent constructability in soft soils offers considerable potential for simplifying on-site installation for internal bracing of foundation pit.

The performance of underground structures is highly sensitive to their geometric configuration and inclination. For instance, Sarfarazi and Tabaroei18 demonstrated that the shape of an underground Qanat significantly affects stress concentration and ground subsidence. By analogy, the inclination angle of SCPs is expected to play a critical role in load transfer and deformation control. According to similar studies, the fundamental mechanical principles governing inclined SCPs as internal bracing are based on the synergistic action of five principal mechanisms: (1) Soil Arching Effect—An arched soil structure forms between adjacent inclined SCPs, transferring most of the lateral earth pressure directly to the pile bodies and thereby reducing the loads acting on waling beams or other support components19–21. (2) Optimized Mobilization of Passive Earth Pressure—The inclined configuration allows for a more extensive mobilization of soil resistance, providing greater support capacity with smaller displacement compared to vertical bracings22,23. (3) Application of Prestress—Prestress can be applied to the inclined SCPs before excavation, and this preloading partially offsets the horizontal thrust from the retained soil that develops after excavation begins24. (4) Structural System Transformation—By providing top support to the SMW piles, the inclined SCPs alter the system from a quasi-cantilever to a quasi-simply supported configuration, thereby reducing bending moments and enhancing the overall stability of SMW piles25. (5) System Synergy—The SMW piles and inclined SCPs work in synergy to form an integrated system that effectively combines structural support, waterproofing, and deformation control functions26.

While conventional methods are widely used, their behavior is inherently three-dimensional, and simplified plane-strain analyses may not fully capture the deformation mechanisms, especially at corners or near boundaries27,28. The behavior of conventional contiguous pile walls under staged excavation has been well-documented through physical modeling29,30. However, the performance of inclined composite piles that combine a high-strength steel core with a cement-soil matrix, and which function as both retaining and bracing elements, is fundamentally different and lacks field validation31. Predicting and controlling excavation-induced deformations is a central concern. However, recent studies have developed simplified frameworks to estimate deformation patterns, highlighting the significant influence of support inclination32. Furthermore, the performance of such inclined support systems under seismic loading has also been explored33. These findings underscore the importance of validating the deformation control capability of inclined SCPs under static service loads, which is a primary focus of our field monitoring. To address the lack of field‑validated evidence for inclined strength composite piles (SCPs) as internal bracing in deep soft soil excavations, this study presents a full‑scale investigation of 45° inclined H‑beam SCPs under stringent urban constraints (adjacent to live metro tunnels and dense utilities). The wide-flange H-beam, through the incorporation of stiffeners on the top flange or by increasing the sectional moment of inertia, significantly improves the overall stability, flexural load-bearing capacity, and deformation performance of the beam. In addition, its ease of use and construction efficiency make it a preferred choice for structural and foundation engineering applications. Due to these advantages, the inner core of the SCPs in this project utilizes wide-flange H-beams, as illustrated in Fig. 5. The 45° inclination balanced geometric constraints with mechanical efficiency. According to Rankine’s theory, the active failure surface in retained soil develops at 45° + φ/2. For soft clay (φ ≈ 0°–15°), this angle is 45°–52.5°. The SCPs at 45° intersect this surface at a near-perpendicular angle, effectively mobilizing soil shear strength. This angle also enables the horizontal axial component to directly counteract lateral thrust, acting as an internal strut without occupying excessive workspace.Fig. 5Wide-flange H-beam used as the inner core of the SCPs.

Wide-flange H-beam used as the inner core of the SCPs.

Analysis of field test results

The load–settlement (Q–s) and settlement-time (s–lgt) curves are primary indicators for evaluating the interaction between the inclined SCPs and the surrounding soft soil. As shown in Fig. 20, the Q–s curves for all tested piles (SX2, SX3, and SX4) exhibit a ‘gradual-change’ (creep-dominant) characteristic without a distinct plunging point. This behavior is primarily attributed to the high sensitivity and low shear strength of the mucky silty clay in Nanjing. As the axial load increases, the shaft friction is mobilized progressively, transferring from the upper stiff-plastic layers to the deeper soft-plastic layers.Fig. 20Measured Q-s curves of the tested piles.

Ultimate capacity evaluation and prediction

As the field static load tests were terminated upon fulfilling the design strength requirements rather than reaching a physical failure state, the recorded maximum test loads (up to 2090 kN) do not represent the actual ultimate bearing capacity (Qu) of the SCPs. To quantitatively assess the untapped bearing potential, several extrapolation methods can be adopted for Q–s curves exhibiting gradual variation, including the hyperbolic method, bi-linear method, and various regression models37–39. In this study, a power-function model, s = aQb, (where s is settlement, Q is applied load, and a and b are regression coefficients), was specifically adopted for this extrapolation. This model is particularly well-suited for capturing the non-linear, strain-softening response typically observed at the pile-soil interface in soft clay deposits. As illustrated in Fig. 22, the regressed curves demonstrate a high degree of fit with the field-measured data, providing a reliable basis for capacity extrapolation.Fig. 22Regression analysis of the Q–s response in the loading stage.

Regression analysis of the Q–s response in the loading stage.

According to the Technical Code for Testing of Building Foundation Piles (JGJ 106), for Q–s curves exhibiting a gradual-change characteristic, the ultimate capacity can be determined as the load corresponding to a settlement threshold of 5%d (where d = 800 mm, i.e., s = 40 mm). By applying this criterion to the extrapolated curves in Fig. 22, the predicted ultimate capacities for SX2, SX3, and SX4 were determined to be 3682 kN, 2894 kN, and 2299 kN, respectively.

A quantitative comparison was then performed between these extrapolated ultimate values and the specification-based predictions (Ra in Table 8). The results indicate that the calculated characteristic capacities (Ra) represent approximately 62%–84% of the regressed ultimate values. This comparison confirms that the estimation formula provided by the Technical Specification for Strength Composite Piles (JGJ/T 327) is not only reliable but also relatively conservative. Such findings demonstrate that the inclined SCP system provides a substantial safety margin, which is essential for ensuring the stability of deep excavations in complex and sensitive soft-soil environments.

Discussion on applicability and limitations

The proposed 45° inclined H-beam SCP combined with the TDC system is highly effective for shallow to medium-deep excavations (5–10 m) in soft soils. It is particularly advantageous in congested urban environments characterized by strict spatial constraints (e.g., restricted property lines prohibiting ground anchors) and stringent deformation limits (e.g., adjacent to sensitive metro infrastructures). Despite its demonstrated feasibility, several limitations should be noted to guide future research:Geological and Depth Constraints: The current findings are based on a 6.15–7.10 m excavation in mucky silty clay. The system’s performance and installation feasibility in stiffer soils (e.g., dense sand), as well as in extra-deep excavations (> 15 m) where the vertical load component from inclined bracings might induce excessive wall settlement, remain unverified.Parameter Optimization: This study focused on a fixed 45° inclination. Parametric studies evaluating different angles (e.g., 30°–60°) are necessary to further optimize structural behavior and economic efficiency.Bearing Capacity Evaluation: As the field tests were terminated upon reaching design loads, the physical failure mode of the pile-soil interface was not directly observed. Future studies should incorporate full-scale failure tests and 3D numerical modeling to develop comprehensive design guidelines.Predictive Modeling Advancements: While our study relies on traditional regression analysis of Q–s curves to estimate ultimate capacities, the increasing application of machine learning techniques offers new avenues for predicting complex excavation responses40. Our field dataset could serve as a valuable benchmark for training or validating such intelligent models in future studies.

Conclusions and suggestions

The combined support system consisting of inclined H-beam strength composite piles (SCPs) and triaxial deep-mixing columns (TDCs)—which achieves carbon emission reduction from both design and construction perspectives—was successfully applied to the Nanjing Yinxia Health Industry Project (Area B), a site characterized by soft soil conditions, dense underground utilities, and stringent deformation control requirements near metro facilities. The main conclusions and suggestions are as follows:Three test piles (SX2, SX3, SX4) achieved ultimate capacities of 1,350–2,090 kN with settlements of 14.07–15.17 mm, fully satisfying the design requirements (1.8Nk). The linear Q–s responses and high rebound rates (64.9%–67.4%) confirm quasi-elastic behavior.Extrapolated ultimate capacities (2,299–3,682 kN at 5%d settlement) are 0.62–0.84 times the specification-based predictions (JGJ/T 327), confirming that the current code provides a conservative yet practical safety margin (16%–38% below extrapolated capacity).Horizontal displacements at critical sections remained within –2.7 to 6.8 mm (< 0.1% of excavation depth, H = 7.1 m), significantly stricter than typical soft-soil limits (0.2%–0.5%H).Field evidence validates the structural system transformation from quasi-cantilever to quasi-simply supported behavior, reducing bending moments and enabling the observed low displacements without conventional struts or anchors.The system reduced construction time compared to conventional strut systems, avoided encroachment outside the red line, and aligned with green-construction objectives. Wider application is recommended for soft-soil excavations where conventional internal bracing is difficult to install.
