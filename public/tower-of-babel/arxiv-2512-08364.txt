Generalized Discrepancy of Random Points
Erich Novak, Friedrich Pillichshammer

Abstract
We study the L p L_{p} -discrepancy of random point sets in high dimensions, with emphasis on small values of p p . Although the classical L p L_{p} -discrepancy suffers from the curse of dimensionality for all p ∈ ( 1 , ∞ ) p\in(1,\infty) , the gap between known upper and lower bounds remains substantial, in particular for small p ≥ 1 p\geq 1 . To clarify this picture, we review the existing results for i.i.d. uniformly distributed points and derive new upper bounds for generalized L p L_{p} -discrepancies, obtained by allowing non-uniform sampling densities and corresponding non-negative quadrature weights. Using the probabilistic method, we show that random points drawn from optimally chosen product densities lead to significantly improved upper bounds. For p = 2 p=2 these bounds are explicit and optimal; for general p ∈ [ 1 , ∞ ) p\in[1,\infty) we obtain sharp asymptotic estimates. The improvement can be interpreted as a form of importance sampling for the underlying Sobolev space F d , q F_{d,q} . Our results also reveal that, even with optimal densities, the curse of dimensionality persists for random points when p ≥ 1 p\geq 1 , and it becomes most pronounced for small p p . This suggests that the curse should also hold for the classical L 1 L_{1} -discrepancy for deterministic point sets.

Numerical integration, worst-case error, discrepancy,
curse of dimensionality, quasi-Monte Carlo

Introduction

The
L_{p}
-discrepancy of point sets in
[0,1]^{d}
is one of the central
quantities in quasi-Monte Carlo (QMC) theory. It measures the worst-case
integration error for functions belonging to the Sobolev space underlying the
Koksma-Hlawka inequality. In this paper we study the generalized

L_{p}
-discrepancy associated with the multivariate Sobolev space
F_{d,q}
,
where
p
and
q
are Hölder conjugates. This space consists of functions that
are once differentiable in each coordinate, whose mixed derivative lies in

L_{q}([0,1]^{d})
, and which satisfy the boundary condition
f({\bm{x}})=0
whenever at
least one coordinate of
{\bm{x}}\in[0,1]^{d}
equals
1
. These spaces appear naturally in
multivariate integration and tractability theory.

A classical result (see, e.g.,
[
23
]
) states that for QMC algorithms
with equal weights, the worst-case error in
F_{d,q}
equals the classical

L_{p}
-discrepancy of the underlying point set. For general linear algorithms
with non-negative (but not necessarily equal) weights, one obtains the more
flexible notion of a
generalized
L_{p}
-discrepancy
. This extended
framework is particularly useful in high dimensions: it allows one to
incorporate non-uniform sampling densities and to interpret the analysis in
terms of importance sampling for the Sobolev space
F_{d,q}
.

It is known that the classical
L_{p}
-discrepancy suffers from the curse of
dimensionality for all
p\in(1,\infty)
when restricted to non-negative
weights
[
18
,
19
]
. More precisely, the number of required sample points
grows exponentially in
d
when aiming at a fixed relative error
reduction. Nevertheless, the best available upper bounds
are still far from matching
these lower bounds. The gap is especially large for small values of
p
, and
the case
p=1
remains the least understood. Existing explicit bounds for the
average
L_{p}
-discrepancy of uniformly distributed random points are known
only for even integers
p\geq 2
, and asymptotic results for general

p\in[1,\infty)
are due to Steinerberger
[
29
]
and Hinrichs–Weyhausen

[
15
]
. Much less is known about how one can improve these bounds by
choosing the sampling distribution in a non-uniform way.

The present paper investigates the effect of non-uniform random sampling.
Instead of choosing points independently and uniformly in
[0,1]^{d}
, we draw
them independently according to a product density
\varrho_{d}=\varrho^{\otimes d}
and evaluate the integrand with weights proportional to
1/\varrho_{d}
.
This leads to the generalized discrepancy
L_{p,N}(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A})
,
whose expectation with respect to the density
\varrho_{d}
we analyze in
detail. The probabilistic method then implies the existence of deterministic
point sets with discrepancy no larger than this expectation.

Our first goal is to clarify to what extent a suitable change of measure can
improve the probabilistic upper bounds in high dimensions. The answer turns
out to depend strongly on
p
. For
p=2
we completely solve the underlying
variational problem and recover the optimal density already found implicitly
in earlier work of Plaskota, Wasilkowski, and Zhao
[
25
]
. This yields
explicit optimal constants and improves the classical bound by a factor of

(4/3)^{d/2}
instead of
(3/2)^{d/2}
. For general
p\in[1,\infty)
we show
that the optimal one-dimensional density is uniquely determined by a
nonlinear equation and leads to a base of the form

\alpha_{p}=\Bigl(\tfrac{p+2}{p+1}\Bigr)^{1/2}

in the exponential factor of the
d
-dependence. This improves the
corresponding base
((2p+2)/(p+2))^{1/p}
from the classical uniform-sampling bound.
The effect is most pronounced for small
p
and diminishes as
p\to\infty
.

A second goal is to clarify how random information compares with optimal
deterministic constructions. Our upper bounds show that random points drawn
from optimally chosen product measures can significantly outperform all
currently known deterministic point sets with respect to generalized
discrepancy. At the same time, even for the optimal density, the curse of
dimensionality persists for all
p\geq 1
. Indeed, the exponential growth rate
in
d
becomes
more
severe as
p\to 1
. This suggests that the curse
should also hold for the classical
L_{1}
-discrepancy with deterministic
points—a problem that is still open.

The paper is organized as follows. In Section
3
we study the case

p=2
in detail, solve the associated variational problem explicitly, and
derive the optimal density. Section
4
extends the analysis to
general
p\in[1,\infty)
, leading to an implicit characterization of the
optimal density and sharp asymptotic bounds for the average discrepancy. In
Section
5
we discuss the stability of the resulting
quadrature formulas in the norm of
F_{d,q}
and present open problems.
But first we give some further background.

Background

We study numerical integration for the function space that is the canonical
space underlying the Koksma-Hlawka inequality as presented in
[
23
, Chapter 9, Section 9.8]
.
The goal is to investigate how probabilistic arguments can improve upper
bounds for generalized discrepancy measures and the corresponding integration problem.

The function space setting.

Throughout this paper let
p,q\geq 1
be Hölder conjugates, i.e.,
1/p+1/q=1
.
For
d=1
, let
W_{q}^{1}([0,1])
denote the space of absolutely continuous
functions whose first derivatives belong to
L_{q}([0,1])
.
For
d>1
we consider the
d
-fold tensor product space

W_{q}^{\bm{1}}:=W_{q}^{(1,1,\ldots,1)}([0,1]^{d}),

which is the Sobolev space of functions on
[0,1]^{d}
that
are once differentiable in each variable and whose mixed derivative

\partial^{d}f/\partial{\bm{x}}
has finite
L_{q}
-norm, where
\partial{\bm{x}}=\partial x_{1}\partial x_{2}\ldots\partial x_{d}
.

Now consider the subspace of functions satisfying the boundary conditions
1
1

1

In analysis and stochastics the boundary condition
f(0)=0
is more common.
Of course it does not really matter, since one can always change a variable

f({\bm{x}})=0
if at least one component of

equals

and equip this subspace with the norm

\|f\|_{d,q}:=\Biggl(\int_{[0,1]^{d}}\Bigl|\frac{\partial^{d}}{\partial{\bm{x}}}f({\bm{x}})\Bigr|^{q}\,{\rm d}{\bm{x}}\Biggr)^{1/q}\quad\text{for }q\in[1,\infty),

and

\|f\|_{d,\infty}:=\sup_{{\bm{x}}\in[0,1]^{d}}\Bigl|\frac{\partial^{d}}{\partial{\bm{x}}}f({\bm{x}})\Bigr|\quad\text{for }q=\infty.

That is,

F_{d,q}:=\Bigl\{f\in W_{q}^{\bm{1}}:f({\bm{x}})=0\text{ if }x_{j}=1\text{ for some }j\in[d],\ \|f\|_{d,q}<\infty\Bigr\},

where

Integration and linear algorithms.

Now consider multivariate integration

I_{d}(f):=\int_{[0,1]^{d}}f({\bm{x}})\,\,{\rm d}{\bm{x}}\quad\text{for }f\in F_{d,q}.

(1)

We approximate
I_{d}(f)
by linear algorithms of the form

A_{d,N}(f)=\sum_{k=1}^{N}a_{k}f({\bm{t}}_{k}),

(2)

where
{\bm{t}}_{1},\ldots,{\bm{t}}_{N}\in[0,1)^{d}
are sample points and

a_{1},\ldots,a_{N}
are real integration weights.
If
a_{1}=\dots=a_{N}=1/N
, then (
2
) is a quasi-Monte Carlo (QMC)
algorithm, denoted by
A_{d,N}^{\ast}
.

In this paper we derive new upper bounds for
non-negative
quadrature formulas, i.e. those with

a_{k}\geq 0
for all
k\in\{1,\dots,N\}
.
To emphasize that only non-negative weights are used we write
A_{d,N}^{+}
.
Such non-negative integration weights are often favored in practice
because of their monotonicity and numerical stability (see Section
5
).

For lower bounds, the restriction to positive quadrature formulas makes the results weaker,
but here we focus entirely on
upper
bounds.

Worst-case error and generalized discrepancy.

Define the worst-case error of an algorithm (
2
) by

e(F_{d,q},A_{d,N})=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\in F_{d,q}\\
\|f\|_{d,q}\leq 1\end{subarray}}\bigl|I_{d}(f)-A_{d,N}(f)\bigr|.

(3)

For a QMC algorithm
A_{d,N}^{\ast}
it is well-known (see, e.g.,
[
23
, Section 9.8.1]
) that

e(F_{d,q},A_{d,N}^{\ast})=L_{p,N}^{\ast}(\mathcal{P}),

where
L_{p,N}^{\ast}(\mathcal{P})
is the classical
L_{p}
-discrepancy of the
point set
\mathcal{P}=\{{\bm{t}}_{1},\ldots,{\bm{t}}_{N}\}
.

For general linear algorithms (
2
) the worst-case error equals the

generalized
L_{p}
-discrepancy

e(F_{d,q},A_{d,N})=L_{p,N}(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}),

(4)

where
\mathcal{A}=\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{N}\}
are the integration weights corresponding to
\mathcal{P}
.
This equality follows from the Koksma-Hlawka inequality; see
[
23
, Section 9.8.1]
.

For points
\mathcal{P}=\{{\bm{t}}_{k}\}
and weights
\mathcal{A}=\{a_{k}\}
, the discrepancy function is

\Delta_{\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}}({\bm{x}})=\sum_{k=1}^{N}a_{k}\,\mathbf{1}_{[\bm{0},{\bm{x}})}({\bm{t}}_{k})-x_{1}x_{2}\cdots x_{d},

for
{\bm{x}}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})\in[0,1]^{d}
, and the generalized
L_{p}
-discrepancy is

L_{p,N}(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A})=\Biggl(\int_{[0,1]^{d}}|\Delta_{\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}}({\bm{x}})|^{p}\,{\rm d}{\bm{x}}\Biggr)^{1/p}\qquad\mbox{for $p\in[1,\infty)$,}

with the usual modification for
p=\infty
. If
a_{1}=\dots=a_{N}=1/N
, we recover the classical definition
L_{p,N}^{\ast}(\mathcal{P})
.

We define the
N
th minimal worst-case error

e_{q}(N,d):=\min_{A_{d,N}}e(F_{d,q},A_{d,N}),

where the minimum ranges over all algorithms of the form (
2
).
The initial error (i.e.,
N=0
) equals

e_{q}(0,d)=\sup_{\|f\|_{d,q}\leq 1}|I_{d}(f)|=\begin{cases}(p+1)^{-d/p}&\mbox{if $q\in(1,\infty]$,}\\[3.00003pt]
1&\mbox{if $q=1$.}\end{cases}

Using the duality (
4
), one obtains

e_{q}(N,d)={\rm disc}_{p}(N,d):=\min_{\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}}L_{p,N}(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}),

(5)

and we refer to
{\rm disc}_{p}(N,d)
as the
N
th minimal
L_{p}
-discrepancy
.

Information complexity.

We define the information complexity as the minimal number of function
evaluations needed to reduce the initial error by a factor
\varepsilon
, where
\varepsilon>0
:

N_{q}^{\rm int}(\varepsilon,d):=\min\{N\in\mathbb{N}:e_{q}(N,d)\leq\varepsilon\,e_{q}(0,d)\}.

By duality,

N_{q}^{\rm int}(\varepsilon,d)=N_{p}^{\rm disc}(\varepsilon,d),\quad N_{p}^{\rm disc}(\varepsilon,d):=\min\{N:{\rm disc}_{p}(N,d)\leq\varepsilon\,{\rm disc}_{p}(0,d)\}.

In the QMC case (
a_{k}=1/N
), we add a superscript
{\ast}
,
writing
e_{q}^{\ast}(N,d)
,
{\rm disc}_{p}^{\ast}
,
N_{q}^{{\rm int},{\ast}}
, etc.
When we restrict to non-negative weights (
a_{k}\geq 0
), we write the superscript
+
.
Obviously,

e_{q}(N,d)\leq e_{q}^{+}(N,d)\leq e_{q}^{\ast}(N,d),\qquad N_{q}^{\rm int}(\varepsilon,d)\leq N_{q}^{{\rm int},+}(\varepsilon,d)\leq N_{q}^{{\rm int},{\ast}}(\varepsilon,d),

and analogous relations hold for the corresponding discrepancies.

An important question now is, how quickly the information complexity
N_{q}^{{\rm int}}(\varepsilon,d)
increases,
when
d\rightarrow\infty
and/or
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
(i.e., when
d+\varepsilon^{-1}\rightarrow\infty
).
Such questions are typically studied in the field “Information Based Complexity” (see
[
22
,
23
,
24
]
).
If
N_{q}^{{\rm int}}(\varepsilon,d)
grows at least exponentially fast in
d
for a fixed
\varepsilon>0

more precisely, if there exists a real
C>1
such that
N_{q}^{{\rm int}}(\varepsilon,d)\geq C^{d}

for infinitely many

, then the integration problem is said to suffer from the curse of dimensionality.

If, on the other hand,
N_{q}^{{\rm int}}(\varepsilon,d)
grows at most sub-exponentially fast
in
d
and
\varepsilon^{-1}
for

problem is said to be tractable and there are various notions of tractability in order
to classify the grow rate of

more accurately
2

The probabilistic method in discrepancy theory.

The probabilistic method was first used in a deep result of Hlawka
[
16
]
(1947)
to prove the existence of a lattice sphere packing with large density,
building on Siegel’s mean value theorem
[
26
]
.
Almost simultaneously, Erdős
[
8
]
employed the method for
existence theorems in combinatorics and graph theory.

Regarding the
L_{\infty}^{\ast}
- (star) discrepancy, Heinrich et al.
[
10
]
proved

N_{\infty}^{{\rm disc},\ast}(d,\varepsilon)\leq C\,d\,\varepsilon^{-2},

where
C>0
is an absolute constant.
Equivalently, for every
d
and
N
there exists an
N
-element point set
\mathcal{P}\subseteq[0,1)^{d}
such that

L_{\infty,N}^{\ast}(\mathcal{P})\leq\sqrt{C}\,\sqrt{\tfrac{d}{N}}.

For this and related results on average
L_{p}^{\ast}
-discrepancies (for even
p
),
they applied the probabilistic method to i.i.d. uniformly distributed points in
[0,1]^{d}
.
Let
\mathcal{P}_{N}
denote an i.i.d. sample of
N
uniform random points in
[0,1]^{d}
.
Then, with high probability,

L_{\infty,N}^{\ast}(\mathcal{P}_{N})\leq C\sqrt{\tfrac{d}{N}},

see Aistleitner and Hofer
[
1
]
. Doerr
[
7
]
showed that this order cannot be improved
substantially: there exist absolute constants
C_{1},C_{2}>0
with

C_{1}\sqrt{\tfrac{d}{N}}\leq\mathbb{E}[L_{\infty,N}^{\ast}(\mathcal{P}_{N})]\leq C_{2}\sqrt{\tfrac{d}{N}}.

In weighted function spaces, where the relevance of
coordinates decays, QMC methods can achieve dimension-independent bounds.
If, in the sense of Sloan and Woźniakowski
[
27
]
, coordinate weights
\bm{\gamma}=(\gamma_{j})
control variable importance, then randomized lattice rules
and digital nets satisfy, for every
\delta\in(0,1)
, the existence of a positive
C(\bm{\gamma},\delta)
such that

\mathbb{E}[L_{\infty,N,\bm{\gamma}}^{\ast}(\mathcal{P}_{N})]\leq C(\bm{\gamma},\delta)\,N^{-1+\delta},

leading to strong polynomial tractability (see, e.g.,
[
4
,
5
,
6

21

]
).

Randomized shifts and scrambles are analyzed probabilistically,
and expectation bounds again imply the existence of good deterministic point sets.
These results ensure polynomial or strong polynomial tractability (see
[
22
,
23
]
).
For some, though not all, of these upper bounds there exist constructive versions.

In this paper we concentrate on the integration problem related to the classical
L_{p}^{\ast}
-discrepancy for
finite
p
, i.e.,
p\in[1,\infty)
.
We use random i.i.d. points
\{{\bm{t}}_{1},\ldots,{\bm{t}}_{N}\}
in
[0,1]^{d}
.
However, uniform sampling is not appropriate for the underlying Sobolev space
F_{d,q}
,
because functions in
F_{d,q}
satisfy
f({\bm{x}})=0
whenever any coordinate
x_{j}=1
;
hence their values are more significant for small coordinates (where
f
can be large)
than for large ones (where
f
must be small). Consequently, regions near the origin contribute most to the integration error.

This observation motivates a change of measure, analogous to importance sampling in Monte Carlo methods
[
17
]
.
Even though we are ultimately interested in deterministic upper bounds for quadrature
formulas (or, equivalently, for the
L_{p}^{\ast}
-discrepancy), we employ a different
averaging strategy in the probabilistic method. There is, however, one caveat for the
discrepancy setting: we must replace the equal QMC weights
1/N
by new non-negative
weights
a_{k}=1/(N\varrho_{d}({\bm{t}}_{k}))
, where
\varrho_{d}
is a suitably optimized density. Hence our new upper bounds apply to the
generalized

L_{p}
-discrepancy.

In more detail, we use random i.i.d. points
\mathcal{P}_{N}=\{{\bm{t}}_{1},\ldots,{\bm{t}}_{N}\}
in
[0,1]^{d}

drawn according to a density function
\varrho_{d}
on
[0,1]^{d}
, and consider algorithms of the form

A_{d,N}(f)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\frac{1}{\varrho_{d}({\bm{t}}_{k})}f({\bm{t}}_{k}).

(6)

We write
\mathcal{A}_{\varrho_{d}}
for the set of these weights. We then focus on the
p
-average

{{\rm av}}_{p}(N,d,\varrho_{d}):=\mathbb{E}\bigl[(L_{p,N}(\mathcal{P}_{N},\mathcal{A}_{\varrho_{d}}))^{p}\bigr]^{1/p},

and its normalized version

{{\rm n\text{-}av}}_{p}(N,d,\varrho_{d}):=(p+1)^{d/p}\,\mathbb{E}\bigl[(L_{p,N}(\mathcal{P}_{N},\mathcal{A}_{\varrho_{d}}))^{p}\bigr]^{1/p},

where in both cases the expectation is with respect to the density
\varrho_{d}
.
The classical QMC-case corresponds to
\varrho_{d}=1
. We then write simply
{{\rm av}}_{p}^{\ast}(N,d)
and
{{\rm n\text{-}av}}_{p}^{\ast}(N,d)
, respectively.

Note that an upper bound of the form

{{\rm n\text{-}av}}_{p}(N,d,\varrho_{d})\leq C_{p}\,\alpha_{p}^{d}\,N^{-1/2},

for some non-negative

, implies

N_{p}^{{\rm disc}}(\varepsilon,d)\leq C_{p}^{2}\,\alpha_{p}^{2d}\,\varepsilon^{-2}.

(7)

We aim to contribute to two fundamental questions:

1.

How can we establish sharper

Throughout, for functions

3
Change of measure for
p=2

Consider the integration problem (
1
) for functions from the Sobolev-Hilbert space
F_{d,2}
. The reproducing kernel of this space is the tensor product of
K_{1}(x,y)=1-\max(x,y)
, and the representer of the integral is

h_{d}({\bm{x}})=\prod_{j=1}^{d}\tfrac{1}{2}(1-x_{j}^{2})

with
\|h_{d}\|_{d,2}=3^{-d/2}
.

We use random i.i.d. points
{\bm{t}}_{1},\ldots,{\bm{t}}_{N}\in[0,1]^{d}

drawn according to a density function
\varrho_{d}
on
[0,1]^{d}
,
and consider algorithms of the form

A_{d,N}(f)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\frac{1}{\varrho_{d}({\bm{t}}_{k})}f({\bm{t}}_{k}).

Using
[
23
, Eq. (9.46)]
it is straightforward to compute the squared worst-case error:

e(F_{d,2},A_{d,N})^{2}=\frac{1}{3^{d}}-\frac{2}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}h_{d}({\bm{t}}_{k})\frac{1}{\varrho_{d}({\bm{t}}_{k})}+\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{k,\ell=1}^{N}K_{d}({\bm{t}}_{k},{\bm{t}}_{\ell})\frac{1}{\varrho_{d}({\bm{t}}_{k})\varrho_{d}({\bm{t}}_{\ell})}.

We assume that

C(K_{d},\varrho_{d}):=\int_{[0,1]^{d}}K_{d}({\bm{t}},{\bm{t}})\frac{1}{\varrho_{d}({\bm{t}})}\,\,{\rm d}{\bm{t}}<\infty.

(8)

Taking the expectation over the random points yields

\mathbb{E}[e(F_{d,2},A_{d,N})^{2}]=\frac{C(K_{d},\varrho_{d})-3^{-d}}{N}.

Hence, by the mean-value argument,
for certain deterministic points
{\bm{t}}_{1},\ldots,{\bm{t}}_{N}
we have

e(F_{d,2},A_{d,N})^{2}\leq\frac{C(K_{d},\varrho_{d})-3^{-d}}{N}.

(9)

If we take uniformly distributed points, i.e.,
\varrho_{d}=\lambda_{d}
,
then we recover the well-known
equation

{{\rm n\text{-}av}}_{2}^{\ast}(N,d)=3^{d/2}\sqrt{\tfrac{2^{-d}-3^{-d}}{N}}\,\lesssim\,\Bigl(\tfrac{3}{2}\Bigr)^{d/2}N^{-1/2},

(10)

for all
N
and
d
.

Comparing (
8
) and (
9
),
it is evident that we want to minimize
C(K_{d},\varrho_{d})

over all densities
\varrho_{d}
.
This optimization problem has a unique solution,
which follows from convexity via the Euler-Lagrange equations.
For
d=1
one needs to minimize

C(K_{1},\varrho)=\int_{0}^{1}(1-t)\frac{1}{\varrho(t)}\,\,{\rm d}t,

and the solution is (see also Theorem
2
)

\varrho^{\ast}(t)=\tfrac{3}{2}\sqrt{1-t}.

(11)

We then obtain the minimal value
C(K_{1},\varrho^{\ast})=\tfrac{4}{9}
. The density (
11
) was already used in
[
23
,
25
]
, here we clearly see that it is optimal.

For
d\in\mathbb{N}
the optimal density
\varrho_{d}^{\ast}
is the
d
-fold tensor product
\varrho^{\ast,\otimes d}:=\bigotimes_{j=1}^{d}\varrho^{\ast}
, leading to the following result, which improves (
10
):

Theorem 1
.

For the optimal density
\varrho_{d}^{\ast}

{{\rm n\text{-}av}}_{2}(N,d,\varrho_{d}^{\ast})=3^{d/2}\sqrt{\tfrac{(4/9)^{d}-3^{-d}}{N}}\,\lesssim\,\Bigl(\tfrac{4}{3}\Bigr)^{d/2}N^{-1/2}.

(12)

We have
\varrho_{d}^{\ast}=\varrho^{\ast,\otimes d}
, where
\varrho^{\ast}
is given in (
11
).

The factor
3^{d/2}
in (
12
) stems from normalization.

Remark 1
.

It is known that the weights
1/(N\varrho_{d}({\bm{t}}_{k}))
are not optimal:
for small
N
one should use smaller weights.
A simple modification is to introduce a constant
c>0
and define

A_{d,N}^{(c)}(f):=\frac{c}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\frac{1}{\varrho_{d}({\bm{t}}_{k})}f({\bm{t}}_{k}).

Computing

\mathbb{E}[e(F_{d,2},A_{d,N}^{(c)})^{2}]
and optimizing over
c
gives

c_{\ast}=\frac{N}{N-1+3^{d}C(K_{d},\varrho_{d})}.

With this choice we obtain

\mathbb{E}[e(F_{d,2},A_{d,N}^{(c_{\ast})})^{2}]=\frac{N}{N-1+3^{d}C(K_{d},\varrho_{d})}\,\mathbb{E}[e(F_{d,2},A_{d,N})^{2}].

Hence, for
N\leq 3^{d}C(K_{d},\varrho_{d})
a significant improvement is achieved,
whereas for very large
N
the benefit becomes negligible.

Remark 2
.

It was shown by Hinrichs
[
11
]
that for
p=2
the curse of dimensionality
(for the normalized error) can be avoided in the randomized setting using
importance sampling with a suitable density, generally unknown in closed form.
Here one can proceed similarly to the survey
[
12
, pp. 159-160]
:
sampling with the representer
h_{d}({\bm{x}})=\prod_{j=1}^{d}\tfrac{1}{2}(1-x_{j}^{2})
.
Then the Monte Carlo error is bounded by the norm of integration
(the initial error) times
N^{-1/2}
;
thus there is
no curse for the normalized error
.
The proof closely follows
[
12
, p. 160]
.
In fact, the normalized density proportional to
h_{d}
is optimal
in the randomized setting for importance sampling.

Interestingly, the two optimal densities—for the deterministic worst-case
setting and for the randomized setting—are similar but not identical.
The optimal density (
11
) minimizes the worst-case error
for deterministic algorithms but is not optimal for randomized importance sampling
and cannot remove the curse entirely.

(23)

Proof.

We follow the proof of
[
15
, Theorem 2]
,
introducing positive integration weights
a_{k}=1/(N\varrho_{d}({\bm{t}}_{k}))

with product density
\varrho_{d}
.
We then study

\displaystyle\mathrm{av}_{p}(N,d,\varrho_{d})^{p}

\displaystyle=\!\!\int_{[0,1]^{Nd}}\!\int_{[0,1]^{d}}\Biggl|\lambda_{d}([{\bm{0}},{\bm{x}}))-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\frac{1}{\varrho_{d}({\bm{t}}_{k})}\mathbf{1}_{[{\bm{0}},{\bm{x}})}({\bm{t}}_{k})\Biggr|^{p}\,{\rm d}{\bm{x}}\prod_{j=1}^{N}\varrho_{d}({\bm{t}}_{j})\,{\rm d}{\bm{t}}_{j},

and interchange the order of integration.

For fixed
{\bm{x}}
define the random variables

X_{i}=\mathbf{1}_{[{\bm{0}},{\bm{x}})}({\bm{t}}_{i})\varrho_{d}({\bm{t}}_{i})^{-1}
.
Their mean and variance are

\mathbb{E}[X_{i}]=\lambda_{d}([{\bm{0}},{\bm{x}}))=:\lambda,\qquad V(X_{i})=\int_{[0,1]^{d}}\!\mathbf{1}_{[{\bm{0}},{\bm{x}})}({\bm{t}})\tfrac{1}{\varrho_{d}({\bm{t}})}\,\,{\rm d}{\bm{t}}-\lambda^{2}.

Let

X_{N,\lambda}=\Bigl(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}X_{i}-\lambda\Bigr)\sqrt{N}.

By the central limit theorem,

X_{N,\lambda}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{D}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\Bigl(\int_{[0,1]^{d}}\!\!\mathbf{1}_{[{\bm{0}},{\bm{x}})}({\bm{t}})\tfrac{1}{\varrho_{d}({\bm{t}})}\,\,{\rm d}{\bm{t}}-\lambda^{2}\Bigr)^{1/2}Y,\quad Y\sim N(0,1).

Proceeding as in
[
15
]
, we obtain

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}N^{p/2}\mathrm{av}_{p}(N,d,\varrho_{d})^{p}

\displaystyle=\frac{2^{p/2}}{\pi^{1/2}}\Gamma\!\Bigl(\tfrac{p+1}{2}\Bigr)\!\!\int_{[0,1]^{d}}\!\Biggl(\int_{[0,1]^{d}}\mathbf{1}_{[{\bm{0}},{\bm{x}})}({\bm{t}})\tfrac{1}{\varrho_{d}({\bm{t}})}\,\,{\rm d}{\bm{t}}-\lambda^{2}\Biggr)^{p/2}\!\!\!\!\,{\rm d}{\bm{x}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2^{p/2}}{\pi^{1/2}}\Gamma\!\Bigl(\tfrac{p+1}{2}\Bigr)\!\!\int_{[0,1]^{d}}\!\Biggl(\int_{[0,1]^{d}}\mathbf{1}_{[{\bm{0}},{\bm{x}})}({\bm{t}})\tfrac{1}{\varrho_{d}({\bm{t}})}\,\,{\rm d}{\bm{t}}\Biggr)^{p/2}\!\!\!\!\,{\rm d}{\bm{x}}.

(24)

Because of the product structure of
\varrho_{d}
,

\displaystyle\int_{[0,1]^{d}}\left(\int_{[0,1]^{d}}\mathbf{1}_{[{\bm{0}},{\bm{x}})}({\bm{t}})\tfrac{1}{\varrho_{d}({\bm{t}})}\,{\rm d}{\bm{t}}\right)^{p/2}\,{\rm d}{\bm{x}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{[0,1]^{d}}\left(\int_{[0,1]^{d}}\prod_{j=1}^{d}\mathbf{1}_{[0,x_{j})}(t_{j})\tfrac{1}{\varrho(t_{j})}\,{\rm d}{\bm{t}}\right)^{p/2}\,{\rm d}{\bm{x}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{[0,1]^{d}}\prod_{j=1}^{d}\left(\int_{0}^{x_{j}}\tfrac{1}{\varrho(t_{j})}\,{\rm d}t_{j}\right)^{p/2}\,{\rm d}{\bm{x}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\prod_{j=1}^{d}\int_{0}^{1}\left(\int_{0}^{x_{j}}\tfrac{1}{\varrho(t_{j})}\,{\rm d}t_{j}\right)^{p/2}\,{\rm d}x_{j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\int_{0}^{1}\left(\int_{0}^{x}\frac{1}{\varrho(t)}\,\,{\rm d}t\right)^{p/2}\,{\rm d}x\right)^{d}.

Hence we must minimize, for given

J(\varrho)=\int_{0}^{1}\left(\int_{0}^{x}\frac{1}{\varrho(t)}\,\,{\rm d}t\right)^{p/2}\,{\rm d}x

subject to
\varrho\geq 0

\int_{0}^{1}\varrho(t)\,\,{\rm d}t=1
.
Define
S(x)=\int_{0}^{x}\varrho(t)^{-1}\,{\rm d}t
,
so that
S^{\prime}(x)=1/\varrho(x)\geq 0
.

Introducing a Lagrange multiplier
\lambda

we minimize

\mathcal{L}(S)=\int_{0}^{1}\!\bigl(S(x)^{p/2}+\lambda\varrho(x)\bigr)\,{\rm d}x=\int_{0}^{1}\!\Bigl(S^{p/2}+\frac{\lambda}{S^{\prime}}\Bigr)\,{\rm d}x.

Since the integrand does not depend explicitly on

the Euler-Lagrange equation reduces to the Beltrami identity

S^{p/2}+\frac{2\lambda}{S^{\prime}}=\mu,

with constant
\mu
(

on

Integrating gives

2\lambda x=\mu S(x)-\frac{2}{p+2}S(x)^{p/2+1}.

(25)

Setting

(26)

The normalization constraint

1=\int_{0}^{S_{1}}\Bigl(\frac{\mu-v^{p/2}}{2\lambda}\Bigr)^{2}\!\,{\rm d}v=\frac{1}{(2\lambda)^{2}}\biggl(\mu^{2}S_{1}-\frac{4\mu}{p+2}S_{1}^{p/2+1}+\frac{S_{1}^{p+1}}{p+1}\biggr),

(2\lambda)^{2}=\mu^{2}S_{1}-\frac{4\mu}{p+2}S_{1}^{p/2+1}+\frac{S_{1}^{p+1}}{p+1}.

(27)

Changing variables in the objective gives

J(\varrho)=\frac{1}{2\lambda}\Bigl(\frac{2\mu}{p+2}S_{1}^{p/2+1}-\frac{1}{p+1}S_{1}^{p+1}\Bigr).

After eliminating
\lambda

\mu=\frac{S_{1}^{p/2}}{p+2}\Bigl(2+\frac{p}{\sqrt{p+1}\sqrt{S_{1}-1}}\Bigr),\qquad 2\lambda=\frac{p}{(p+2)\sqrt{p+1}}\frac{S_{1}^{p/2+1}}{\sqrt{S_{1}-1}}.

Substituting these into

\frac{p}{p+1}(S_{1}^{\ast})^{p/2}x=(S_{1}^{\ast})^{p/2}S(x)-\frac{2}{p+2}S(x)^{p/2+1}.

Differentiating with respect to

Thus,

5
Stability, and open problems

We conclude with a discussion of stability, which in our context must be understood
relative to the function space
F_{d,q}
rather than in the classical sup-norm sense.

QMC rules
A_{d,N}^{\ast}
(see Section
2
) integrate constant functions exactly and are traditionally regarded as stable because

\|A_{d,N}^{\ast}\|_{\infty}=1.

Here the operator acts on
C([0,1]^{d})
, and its norm coincides with that of the integration operator itself. Considering linear quadrature formulas of the form

A_{d,N}(f)=\sum_{k=1}^{N}a_{k}f({\bm{t}}_{k}),

a frequently used indicator of stability is

\|A_{d,N}\|_{\infty}=\sum_{k=1}^{N}|a_{k}|,

(32)

which ideally equals 1 (or is close to 1) for a well-balanced formula.

In the present framework, however, the measure (
32
)
is not appropriate because we work in
F_{d,q}
.
Note that the integration operator on
F_{d,q}
has norm
(p+1)^{-d/p}
,
which is exponentially small in
d
.
A stable quadrature formula for
F_{d,q}
should therefore also have
an exponentially small operator norm, and there is no requirement that
it integrates constants exactly, since constant functions are not contained in
F_{d,q}
.

It can be verified that the norm of a QMC rule
A_{d,N}^{\ast}

in
F_{d,q}
depends strongly on the node set.
The inequality

0\leq\|A_{d,N}^{\ast}\|_{F_{d,q}}\leq 1

is sharp: if each point
{\bm{t}}_{k}
has at least one coordinate equal to 1 we obtain the lower bound,
while for
{\bm{t}}_{k}=\bm{0}
for all
k
we obtain the upper bound.
Since
1
is exponentially larger than the initial error
(p+1)^{-d/p}
,
some QMC algorithms possess very large operator norms and are thus unstable on
F_{d,q}
;
naturally, they also exhibit large integration errors.

In contrast, the algorithms considered in this paper use comparatively large
(non-negative) weights, which may lead to arbitrarily large values of
\|A_{d,N}^{+}\|_{\infty}
.
Consequently, these quadrature formulas are not stable in the classical sense.
Nevertheless, they achieve small integration errors, and their

F_{d,q}
-stability
, measured by
\|A_{d,N}^{+}\|_{F_{d,q}}
, remains small.
Thus, in our setting, algorithms
A_{d,N}^{+}
with small integration error

I_{d}-A_{d,N}^{+}
automatically satisfy small
F_{d,q}
-norms by the triangle inequality.

As a simple example, for
p=2
and
d=1
the optimal one-point rule is

A_{1,1}(f)=\tfrac{2}{3}f(\tfrac{1}{3}),

with worst-case error
1/\sqrt{27}
.
If the weight is fixed to
a_{1}=1
, the optimal node is
t_{1}=\tfrac{1}{2}
,
and the error equals
1/\sqrt{12}
.
With the optimal density
\varrho^{\ast}
one obtains

\sup_{f\in F_{1,2}}\frac{f(t)}{\varrho^{\ast}(t)}=\frac{2}{3}\qquad\mbox{for }t\in[0,1),

so all possible contributions
\tfrac{f(t_{k})}{N\varrho^{\ast}(t_{k})}
lie in the same interval
[-2/(3N),2/(3N)]
;
that is, each function value contributes uniformly to the result.

Remark 7
.

A practical issue arises when some of the weights
a_{k}
are large:
if the available data are noisy or the function
f
does not belong to
F_{d,q}
,
the algorithm may behave poorly.
This risk can be mitigated by replacing the optimal density
\varrho^{\ast}
with
a slightly regularized version that stays bounded away from zero by a positive constant.

Open problems.

Several questions remain open and suggest directions for further work:

1.

Can the asymptotic bounds (
16
), (
17
),
(
18
), and (
20
), be converted into explicit non-asymptotic bounds valid for all
N
?

2.

Can the upper bounds be improved when arbitrary quadrature
formulas are permitted, i.e., when some weights
a_{k}
may be negative?

3.

Can one close the gap between upper and lower bounds by
establishing sharper lower bounds, particularly for
p<2
?

4.

Does the curse of dimensionality hold for
p=1
?
