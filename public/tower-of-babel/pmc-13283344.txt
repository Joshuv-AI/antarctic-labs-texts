Metastatic Crohn’s Disease and Acute Myeloid Leukemia: A Diagnostic Paradox in a Severe Hyperinflammatory State
Authors: Francisco David Roman Delgado, María del Carmen Rivera García, Missael Isaac Ruiz Godinez, Ana Elena Escorcia Saucedo, Giovanni Elivt De La Rosa Patlan, Juan Carlos Anda Garay
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Crohn's disease (CD) is a chronic immune-mediated inflammatory disorder in which extraintestinal manifestations occur in up to 40% of patients. Metastatic Crohn's disease (MCD), characterized by noncaseating cutaneous granulomas anatomically remote from the gastrointestinal tract, is among the rarest of these manifestations. Its coexistence with occult acute myeloid leukemia (AML) and secondary hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (sHLH) in a treatment-naïve patient constitutes an exceptional clinical triad with profound diagnostic and therapeutic implications.We report a 56-year-old woman without prior immunosuppressive exposure who presented with severe panenteric CD, MCD manifesting as necrotic lower-extremity nodules, and a submandibular abscess. Profound bicytopenia (hemoglobin: 5.3 g/dL, reference range: 13-18 g/dL; platelets: 12,000/μL, reference range: 150,000-450,000/μL) prompted a bone marrow evaluation, which revealed hypercellularity with 9% myeloid blasts (CD34+, CD117+). Although these findings required an urgent repeat marrow evaluation, cytogenetic/molecular testing, and hematology clearance due to the high risk of an indeterminate or pre-leukemic state, the presence of active, refractory gastrointestinal bleeding prompted the immediate initiation of infliximab induction. Given the absence of prior therapy and the presence of marked systemic inflammation, including concurrent pneumonia and active CD, these findings were initially attributed to reactive myelodysplastic mimicry. However, while systemic inflammation from pneumonia and active CD can mimic myelodysplasia, a 9% blast count with severe cytopenias should not be safely dismissed as merely reactive. Within days of the first infliximab infusion, the patient developed explosive leukocytosis (40,000/μL). Repeat flow cytometry demonstrated 37% immature myeloid blasts (CD34+, CD117++, HLA-DR+, CD123+), establishing a diagnosis of AML without maturation (FAB M1). Concurrently, overt hemophagocytosis with extreme hyperferritinemia confirmed concomitant sHLH (HScore: 227). This case highlights a critical and underrecognized risk: TNF-α may act as an endogenous immunological brake against clonal expansion. In our patient, TNF-alpha blockade may have contributed to the loss of immune control over an occult myeloid clone, dismantling this surveillance mechanism and precipitating a rapid transition to fulminant leukemia while triggering sHLH. Although causality cannot be proven from this single case and a direct triggering effect by infliximab remains speculative unless other potential triggers are thoroughly evaluated, the temporal association is striking. Severe systemic inflammation can create a reactive bone marrow phenotype indistinguishable from early myeloid neoplasia or myelodysplastic syndrome, posing a life-threatening diagnostic trap. Comprehensive hematologic evaluation-including repeat bone marrow assessment under controlled inflammatory conditions-should be considered mandatory before initiating biologic therapy in patients with CD and unexplained cytopenias or borderline blast counts.

Introduction

Crohn's disease (CD) is a chronic, immune-mediated inflammatory disorder characterized by transmural gastrointestinal inflammation and a broad spectrum of extraintestinal manifestations (EIMs), affecting up to 40% of patients [1,2]. Among these manifestations, metastatic Crohn's disease (MCD), defined as noncaseating granulomatous inflammation of the skin in sites anatomically discontinuous from the gastrointestinal tract, is a rare and frequently underdiagnosed entity whose pathogenesis remains incompletely understood, with emerging evidence implicating the interleukin (IL)-23/T-helper (Th)17 axis in both intestinal and cutaneous granuloma formation [1,2]. The hematologic landscape of CD is similarly complex. While anemia is common, severe refractory bicytopenia warrants a broader differential diagnosis that includes drug-induced suppression, autoimmune cytopenias, and primary bone marrow disorders [3]. Importantly, chronic inflammatory states can drive reactive bone marrow changes, including transient dysplasia and hypercellularity, that closely mimic myelodysplastic syndromes (MDS), creating substantial diagnostic uncertainty [3]. Although IBD has been epidemiologically linked to an increased risk of MDS and acute myeloid leukemia (AML) [4,5], current evidence indicates that this association is largely mediated by cumulative immunosuppressive exposure rather than by the inflammatory disease per se [5], making clonal hematologic disease in treatment-naïve patients a particularly uncommon and diagnostically confounding event [3]. Superimposed on this complexity, secondary hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (sHLH), a life-threatening hyperinflammatory syndrome driven by uncontrolled macrophage activation, can be triggered simultaneously by infections, hematologic malignancies, and autoimmune conditions [6]. Its occurrence in IBD, while rare, has been disproportionately reported during biologic induction, suggesting that immune dysregulation inherent to severe CD may create conditions permissive for hyperferritinemic collapse [3]. We present what we believe to be an exceptional case of a treatment-naïve patient with severe panenteric CD and MCD who, following infliximab induction for refractory disease, rapidly evolved to overt AML and sHLH conditions harbored silently beneath a florid inflammatory phenotype. This case illustrates the diagnostic pitfalls of borderline bone marrow findings in hyperinflammatory states and argues for mandatory hematologic clearance before biologic escalation in patients with CD and unexplained cytopenias.

Case presentation

A 56-year-old woman with a history of acute myocardial infarction (revascularized in 2019), systemic arterial hypertension, and prediabetes presented with a one-month history of rhinorrhea, intermittent fever, night sweats, and weight loss. She subsequently developed painful oral ulcers, a right submandibular abscess, and ulcerated nodular skin lesions with necrotic centers involving the lower extremities and gluteal region (Figure 1). On admission, she was hemodynamically stable. Laboratory evaluation revealed a marked systemic inflammatory response (C-reactive protein 279 mg/L, ferritin 5908 ng/mL) and severe cytopenias (hemoglobin 5.3 g/dL; platelets ranging from 12,000/µL to 20,000/µL). Broad-spectrum antibiotics were initiated. Given suspicion for a granulomatous or vasculitic disease, an incisional skin biopsy was performed, demonstrating noncaseating granulomatous inflammation and multinucleated giant cells. Microbiological stains were negative.

Endoscopic evaluation revealed erosive gastropathy and ulcerations involving the esophagus, ileum, and colon (Figure 2). The initial diagnostic approach focused on stabilizing suspected panenteric Crohn's disease and characterizing the cutaneous lesions, which were confirmed on biopsy as noncaseating granulomatous inflammation. Cross-sectional imaging showed nonspecific findings; however, contrast-enhanced CT of the abdomen and pelvis demonstrated segmental stenoses at múltiple levels of the gastrointestinal tract, supporting the diagnosis of extensive CD involvement (Figure 3). Behçet's disease was ruled out based on a negative pathergy test and absence of the HLA-B51 marker gene. Additionally, there was no evidence of cytomegalovirus infection.

An urgent repeat bone marrow evaluation with flow cytometry demonstrated 37% blast with low CD45 expression, positive for CD34, strongly positive for CD117, CD105, CD123, HLA-DR, CD13, CD33, and CD38, and negative for cytoplasmic myeloperoxidase and lymphoid markers. These findings established the diagnosis of AML without maturation (FAB M1). Concomitantly, bone marrow examination revealed overt hemophagocytosis (Figure 4). In the setting of extreme hyperferritinemia, this supported the diagnosis of secondary HLH with an HScore of 227.

While initially considered reactive mimicry, the bone marrow karyotype, performed only after AML evolution, demonstrated a complex cytogenetic profile. This profile, characterized by monosomy 5, chromosome 7 rearrangement involving t(3;7)(q21;q36), trisomy 8, chromosome 12p abnormalities, trisomy 13, and a marker chromosome, confirmed the presence of an underlying high-risk clonal myeloid neoplasm that was not captured earlier in the diagnostic timeline.

Flow cytometry revealed myeloid and monocytic maturation asynchrony, with an immature population comprising 9% of total cells. Overall, these findings suggest multifactorial cytopenias in the context of systemic inflammation, with a possible underlying myelodysplastic process.

Discussion

The clinical trajectory described in this report encapsulates a diagnostic and therapeutic paradox of exceptional rarity: a treatment-naïve patient in whom the biological therapy selected to control a life-threatening inflammatory condition simultaneously dismantled the endogenous immune surveillance restraining an occult leukemic clone. The simultaneous coexistence of metastatic Crohn's disease, acute myeloid leukemia, and secondary hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis-each individually rare, each mutually amplifying-constitutes, to our knowledge, a previously unreported clinical triad with profound implications for the management of severe inflammatory bowel disease complicated by unexplained cytopenias.

Metastatic Crohn's disease, defined by noncaseating granulomatous inflammation at sites in the skin anatomically remote from the gastrointestinal tract, is among the rarest and most diagnostically elusive extraintestinal manifestations of CD [1,2]. Its pathogenesis is incompletely characterized, though current evidence increasingly implicates the IL-23/Th17 axis as the central immunological driver of both intestinal and cutaneous granuloma formation [1]. The broad and overlapping differential diagnosis, encompassing vasculitis, sarcoidosis, pyoderma gangrenosum, and cutaneous infections, frequently contributes to diagnostic delays, as was initially the case in our patient [1,2]. Confirmation ultimately required histopathological demonstration of noncaseating granulomatous inflammation with sterile microbiological cultures.

The central diagnostic dilemma of this case was the interpretation of 9% myeloid blasts in the context of severe systemic inflammation. Current evidence establishes that chronic inflammatory states, recurrent infections, and pathological cytokine signaling can generate reactive bone marrow changes, including transient dysplasia, myeloid hyperplasia, and increased immature precursors, that are morphologically and immunophenotypically indistinguishable from early myelodysplastic neoplasia [3]. Crucially, WHO diagnostic criteria for MDS mandate the exclusion of reactive etiologies prior to establishing a clonal diagnosis, providing the conceptual and practical framework within which our initial interpretation of reactive myelodysplastic mimicry was both defensible and methodologically appropriate [3,4]. The absence of prior immunosuppressive exposure in our patient further reinforced this interpretation: population-based analyses have demonstrated that the excess risk of myeloid neoplasms in IBD patients attenuates to statistical non-significance after adjusting for cumulative exposure to thiopurines and anti-TNF agents [5,6], suggesting that the inflammatory disease per se is a considerably weaker leukemogenic driver than its pharmacological management.

The explosive leukemic evolution following infliximab induction highlights a critical, underrecognized immunobiological risk. Tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α occupies a dual position in the tumor microenvironment: while it mediates inflammatory tissue injury in diseases such as CD, it simultaneously coordinates cytotoxic immune surveillance of malignant and pre-malignant cells through direct apoptotic signaling and activation of natural killer cell and T-lymphocyte responses [7]. In patients harboring a pre-leukemic clone, endogenous TNF-α may therefore function as an immunological restraint on clonal expansion-a brake that, when pharmacologically neutralized, permits unchecked leukemic transition. The temporal correlation between infliximab induction and the rapid progression from 9% to 37% blasts in our patient is consistent with this mechanism [7] and is corroborated by published reports of AML development, including acute promyelocytic leukemia, shortly after initiating anti-TNF therapy in patients with CD [8,9]. At the population level, meta-analytic data from randomized controlled trials confirm that anti-TNF therapy is associated with a more than threefold increase in malignancy risk (OR 3.3; 95% CI, 1.2-9.1), with the highest excess risk observed at induction doses [10], precisely the clinical scenario encountered in our patient.

The severe infectious burden sustained by our patient, concurrent pneumonia and a submandibular abscess superimposed on an active CD flare, likely functioned as an additional pathological amplifier, driving pro-inflammatory cytokine release that simultaneously promoted clonal expansion and activated macrophage hyperactivation pathways [3]. This infectious stimulus, combined with anti-TNF-mediated immunosuppression, generated the hyperinflammatory milieu necessary for the emergence of secondary HLH. A systematic review by Coburn et al. documented that HLH in IBD occurs predominantly in CD (83% of cases) and that 34.5% of episodes arise during biologic induction [11], a pattern strikingly concordant with our case. As established by Henter et al., the classical triggers of sHLH are infections, hematologic malignancies, and autoimmune conditions [12]; our patient harbored all three concurrently, creating a uniquely permissive environment for macrophage hyperactivation. The HScore of 227, corresponding to a diagnostic probability exceeding 93%, provided objective confirmation of sHLH [13] in a clinical context where hyperferritinemia alone would have been insufficient, given its well-recognized lack of specificity in the setting of active inflammatory disease [11,12].

The therapeutic decision-making process in this case merits careful analysis, as it reflects a broader clinical dilemma likely to recur as biologic therapy becomes increasingly prevalent in severe IBD. Ustekinumab, an IL-12/23 inhibitor, represented a theoretically superior alternative: its safety profile with respect to serious infections is favorable compared to anti-TNF agents [14], it targets the IL-23/Th17 pathway implicated in both intestinal and cutaneous granuloma formation in MCD [1,15], and it has demonstrated efficacy in IBD associated with myelodysplastic processes, offering potential hematological benefit without the leukemogenic risk of TNF-α blockade [15]. However, the clinical urgency imposed by recurrent life-threatening gastrointestinal bleeding, combined with the more established evidence base for infliximab in acute severe luminal CD, led the multidisciplinary team to prioritize rapid disease control [14]. The decision was further informed by evidence that TNF-α mediates premature apoptosis of hematopoietic precursors in MDS, and that infliximab has been investigated as a strategy to improve hematopoiesis in low-risk MDS [16], a rationale that, while biologically coherent, did not account for the possibility that the marrow blasts represented not a reactive MDS phenotype but an occult leukemic clone in which TNF-α suppression would prove catastrophic.

This case generates several lessons with direct clinical applicability. First, severe systemic inflammation can produce a reactive bone marrow phenotype, including increased blasts and dysplastic changes, that is morphologically and immunophenotypically indistinguishable from early myeloid neoplasia, making repeat evaluation under controlled inflammatory conditions essential before any definitive hematological diagnosis is established or excluded [3]. Second, in patients with CD and unexplained cytopenias or borderline blast counts, the initiation of potent immunosuppression, particularly anti-TNF induction, must be preceded by a comprehensive multidisciplinary hematological assessment, including repeat bone marrow evaluation whenever clinically feasible [3,14]. Third, this case provides clinical evidence supporting the hypothesis that endogenous TNF-α serves as an immunological restraint on pre-leukemic clonal expansion, and that its pharmacological neutralization may precipitate fulminant leukemic transformation in susceptible patients [7,10]. Fourth, the simultaneous presence of infection, hematologic malignancy, and autoimmune disease-all established triggers of sHLH-should prompt early application of validated diagnostic criteria such as the HScore [13], even in the absence of a confirmed trigger, as diagnostic delay in sHLH is independently associated with mortality [11,12]. Future research should prioritize the identification of molecular biomarkers capable of distinguishing reactive myelodysplastic mimicry from true clonal evolution in hyperinflammatory states, and the prospective evaluation of the hematological safety of anti-TNF agents in patients with IBD and concurrent cytopenias. Until such data are available, a structured multidisciplinary approach integrating gastroenterology, hematology, and hematopathology represents the minimum standard of care in this exceptional but increasingly relevant clinical scenario [3,7,14].

Conclusions

The coexistence of severe Crohn's disease with unexplained cytopenias and borderline bone marrow blasts represents an exceptionally high-risk clinical scenario. This case serves as a stark warning: while anti-TNF agents are essential for inducing remission in severe inflammatory bowel disease, they can inadvertently disrupt vital immune surveillance. In patients with intense inflammation, this immunosuppression can act as a molecular catalyst, unmasking a hidden malignant clone and triggering its fulminant progression to overt acute myeloid leukemia and secondary hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis. Therefore, in patients with Crohn's disease who present with unexplained cytopenias or extreme hyperferritinemia, clinicians must implement a highly structured, multidisciplinary diagnostic approach. A comprehensive bone marrow evaluation to rule out primary malignancies is essential before initiating potent immunosuppression. Future research should focus on identifying reliable molecular biomarkers capable of distinguishing reactive myelodysplastic mimicry from true clonal evolution in hyperinflammatory states.

Currently, there are insufficient studies to confirm the hypothesis of an "immunological brake" provided by TNF-α, which, when blocked, triggers blast proliferation. Therefore, it is crucial to investigate the hematological safety of anti-TNF-α biologics in patients susceptible to hematological malignancies and the efficacy of alternative targeted therapies, such as IL-12/23 inhibitors, in this vulnerable population. Diagnostic improvement remains a critical unmet need. Importantly, subsequent cytogenetic analysis revealed a complex karyotype including monosomy 5, chromosome 7 abnormalities involving 3q21 rearrangement, trisomy 8, and 12p alterations, strongly supporting the presence of an underlying clonal myeloid neoplasm rather than purely reactive inflammatory dysplasia. These findings suggest that the patient likely harbored an occult high-risk myeloid process during the initial inflammatory presentation, with severe systemic inflammation and active Crohn´s disease obscuring early diagnostic interpretation.
