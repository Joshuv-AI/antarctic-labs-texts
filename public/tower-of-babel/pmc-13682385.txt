Climate change and sexual and reproductive health: A mixed methods scoping review of possible mechanisms
Authors: Luissa Vahedi, Susan Pradhan, Sakina Zaidi, Vanessa Brizuela, Sabina Faiz Rashid, Maha El Adawy, Rana Hajjeh, Michelle Gaffey, Zulfiqar A. Bhutta
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryThis scoping review synthesized empirical evidence on the relationships and possible pathways through which climate change could impact sexual and reproductive health and rights (SRHR) in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). Eligible sources had to investigate climate change and SRHR using any study design. Two independent reviewers conducted screening; data extraction captured exposures, outcomes, associations/relationships, and possible mediators and contextual moderators. From 40,503 database-identified sources, we included 256 peer-reviewed publications across 108 LMICs. We identified 14 potential pathways and developed a typology of mediators and contextual moderators to inform the mechanisms. Five mediator domains were identified: (1) systems and infrastructure, (2) demographic shifts, (3) socioeconomic resources, and (4) behavioral and (5) biological. Six contextual moderator types were isolated: (1) patriarchal norms, (2) cultural power structures, (3) socioeconomic realities, (4) gender-inequitable climate governance, (5) place-based factors, and (6) pre-existing systems/infrastructure. As climate shocks intensify, advancing a mechanistic understanding will be critical to designing responsive, inclusive, and sustainable systems of protection.

Introduction

About 3.6 billion people from low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) reside in areas most affected by climate change.1 Between 2000 and 2019, extreme weather events (EWEs) (defined as weather events that are rare at a particular place and time of the year such as temperature extremes, floods, heavy precipitation, extreme storms and cyclones, and droughts)2 caused an estimated 1.2 million deaths and affected over 4 billion people globally, with the burden disproportionately falling on LMICs.3 Further, mortality due to EWE was 15 times higher in LMICs compared to high-income countries.4 Climate change is also worsening food and water insecurity, with 2 billion people lacking access to safe water and 770 million, mostly in Africa and Asia, facing hunger due to climate-related disruptions.4

One of the most overlooked yet urgent issues concerning the climate crisis are impacts on sexual and reproductive health (SRH) and rights (SRHR) including infrastructure related to empowerment and safety like stable housing, water sanitation and hygiene, and employment.5 Health and well-being depend on whether rights like making choices about one’s body, having control over reproduction, and living free from violence are protected and respected.6 Globally, SRHR indicators are not on track to achieve Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) 3: Good Health and Well-Being and SDG 5: Achieve Gender Equality and Empower all Women and Girls.

The consequences of climate change on SRHR unfold through various pathways. For example, EWEs and long-term alterations in climate can adversely impact SRHR due to physical healthcare infrastructure damage, disrupted medical supply chains, and forced migration.7 EWEs can lead to household-level economic shocks, which are known catalysts for gender-based violence (GBV) and child marriage.7,8 Other harmful pathways may involve biological considerations. For example, rising temperatures can increase the risk of fetal distress, preterm births, low birth weight, and stillbirths.9

Despite growing awareness of the linkages between climate change and SRHR adversities, there are gaps in synthesizing the evidence on the underlying pathways of harm within specific contexts, including the mechanisms through which harm unfolds. While several other reviews on climate change and SRHR exist,8,9,10,11,12,13,14,15,16 existing syntheses are limited in three ways. First, existing reviews tend to synthesize evidence from acute climate disasters like floods or storms in isolation from chronic alternations in climate indicators like temperature.9,15 Second, considering that SRHR is an umbrella term that encompasses a variety of different outcomes, many existing reviews focus on selected SRHR outcomes like maternal aspects of reproductive health,10 GBV,14 or HIV.17 Third, existing reviews tend to summarize the overall relationship between climate change and SRHR (either through meta-analysis or thematic summaries), without sufficient consideration given to classifying mechanisms of harm, context differences, and possible mediating paths.

Building on emerging work focused on empirically investigating harmful pathways,13,18,19,20,21,22 this mixed methods scoping review strengthens a mechanistic understanding of climate change and SRHR in LMICs. This review organizes the evidence base into distinct pathways. We consider possible mediators (factors affected by climate change that have downstream effects on SRHR) and contextual moderators (pre-existing realities of a particular location or community that modify the effects of climate change on SRHR). Our review includes a range of both acute and chronic climate change exposures and SRHR outcomes. Our work extends beyond biomedical SRHR outcomes to also encompass sociocultural and psychosocial dimensions of SRHR. By including diverse study methods, we can synthesize quantitative associations between climate change and SRHR alongside qualitative evidence of the lived experiences of individuals and communities.

Methods

The purpose of the review was to explore evidence of relationships/associations between climate change and SRHR in LMICs and elucidate the potential mechanisms underlying those relationships. We used a convergent integrated mixed-methods scoping review design23 and adopted the PRISMA ScR guidelines.24 No review protocol was registered or published.

A priori eligibility criteria were used. Regarding inclusion, studies had to empirically investigate climate change exposures and SRHR outcomes among human populations in LMICs, as defined by the World Bank.25 Panel 1 outlines the definitions used to assess climate change exposures and SRHR outcome eligibility. Studies were excluded if they were not peer reviewed and published in non-English languages and before 2000. We included all study designs and methods. The literature search was implemented on January 21, 2025. Four databases were selected to cover medical, social science, public health, and interdisciplinary fields: (1) Medline, (2) Embase, (3) Web of Science, and (4) Scopus. Search strategies were customized for each database using keywords and thesaurus-specific terms related to (1) climate change, (2) SRHR, and (3) LMICs (refer to Tables S1–S3 and Text S1).

Covidence was used to manage the article screening process and remove duplicate records before the title/abstract screening phase. During the title and abstract (inter-rater reliability for title and abstract k = 0.82) and full-text screening phases (inter-rater reliability for full-text review k = 0.66), researchers independently assessed sources to determine eligibility. Conflicts were resolved through discussion and group consensus at both screening stages. Data extraction was conducted using structured charting forms depending on whether the article was (1) quantitative, (2) qualitative, or (3) mixed methods. Two research assistants independently extracted information on (1) study characteristics (including climate change exposures of SRHR outcomes), (2) results, (3) mechanisms, and (4) context. Pilot extraction was conducted on 20 articles. A discussion-based team consensus process resolved any differences in the extracted information/data.

Informed by an epidemiological perspective,32 we defined mediators as conditions that are worsened by climate change and have harmful downstream effects on SRHR. We separately extracted quantitative (e.g., statistical analyses of mechanisms or related associations, mediation, and mechanistic interaction) and qualitative evidence (e.g., ethnographies, lived experiences, and perceptions) of possible mediation. We also extracted information related to potential contextual moderators. We define context as pre-existing elements of the physical, social, and political environments that are not impacted by climate change but influence the overall relationship between climate change and SRHR. Context can influence the relationship in terms of impact strength and significance. Contextual elements (empirical studied via moderation, effect heterogeneity, thematic analyses or ethnographies describing particularities of a geographical region, culture, and power structures) helped to determine which populations or regions are most and least affected. In this way, contextual moderation tells us under which conditions the underlying causal effects may be occurring.

The synthesis of quantitative, qualitative, and mixed methods studies occurred simultaneously based on the extracted information. We integrated quantitative measures of association (extracting measures of association and abstracting textual descriptions of results), qualitative perceptions, lived experiences, ethnographies, and mixed methods studies to better understand the pathways and underlying mechanisms through which climate change can influence SRHR. We did not privilege one type of evidence over another. Our synthesis focused on breadth and triangulation rather than a hierarchy of evidence. Our integration of quantitative and qualitative evidence informed a conceptual framework that we present in the results.

Extracted information was analyzed using a variety of approaches. First, we summarized the distribution of articles by general characteristics, including funding organizations. Second, we categorized empirical evidence by pathway, wherein a pathway was eligible for reporting in our analysis if it was studied by three separate sources. This approach ensured that the pathways presented were studied in at least three different articles, allowing us to identify patterns in results and gaps, rather than reporting evidence from a single source. From the studies reporting on each pathway, we synthesized extracted information on mediators and contextual moderators and presented these components using flow diagrams, to visualize the underlying mechanisms of harm. In this review, we distinguish between pathways and mechanisms. A pathway refers to a set of at least three articles examining a specific climate change exposure and SRHR outcome pair, whereas a mechanism explains how, why, and under what conditions the harmful relationship between climate change and SRHR occurs.

While our extraction framework was informed by concepts from causal inference, the evidence informing the synthesis varied. Some studies explicitly examined mediation, moderation, interaction, effect heterogeneity, or stratification through quantitative analyses. Other studies, particularly qualitative and mixed methods research, described perceived pathways and contextual conditions through participant lived experiences and interpretations. Results

A total of 40,503 articles were identified by the search. Duplicates (n = 7,129) from the database-identified sources were removed, culminating in 33,474 articles for screening. Following the title and abstract screening, the full texts of 1,942 articles were assessed for eligibility. A further 1,686 articles were excluded during the full text review for reasons such as not reporting a climate change exposure or an SRHR outcome or not using an empirical study design. General study characteristics

Most studies employed quantitative methods (n = 173), followed by qualitative methods (n = 55), and lastly mixed methods, which combined both qualitative and quantitative approaches (n = 28). Various funding organizations were disclosed among the included studies (national governments, internal university grants, and private foundations), 70% of which originated from three countries: United States (n = 65), China (n = 66), United Kingdom (n = 40), and Australia (n = 16). Other notable funders were Norway (n = 9), Canada (n = 9), Germany (n = 7), and India (n = 5). Of the climate change exposures studied (panel 1), primary exposures were the most commonly reported: temperature/heat effects (raising temperatures over time and temperature shocks) (n = 125), floods (n = 79), droughts (n = 57), storms (cyclones and hurricanes) (n = 32), precipitation (in excess or lack thereof) (n = 27), humidity (n = 8), and wildfires (n = 4). Synthesis of pathways

The 256 included studies were grouped into 14 distinct pathways involving 6 climate change exposure types (rainfall shocks/variability, floods, drought and water insecurity, storms, salinity, and temperature) and 8 SRHR-related outcomes (VAWG including IPV and sexual violence; child marriage; menstrual health and hygiene; contraception; gestational hypertension; maternal-infant outcomes like pregnancy loss, preterm birth, low birth weight, and post-partum health; fertility; and HIV/AIDS).

Our synthesis incorporates both empirically tested mechanisms, measures of association indicating the direction and significance of relationships, and evidence-informed pathways suggested by qualitative and mixed methods research. The quantitative evidence was concentrated on estimating associations between maternal-infant outcomes and (1) temperature changes/shocks and (2) floods as well as associations involving drought and GBV/harmful practices. The qualitative evidence was concentrated on pathways leading to menstrual health and hygiene, drawing on lived experiences of adolescent girls affected by climate disasters like floods and cyclones and salinization.

Conceptual model of mechanisms involving possible mediators and contextual moderators

To synthesize findings, we developed a conceptual model illustrating our typology of mediators and contextual moderators (refer to Figure 4). The mediators are situated conceptually on the hypothesized causal pathways between climate and SRHR and can be grouped into 5 domains, namely, (1) systems and infrastructure, (2) demographic shifts, (3) economic resources, and (4) coping behaviors and (5) biology. The systems and infrastructure domain includes damage to physical infrastructure (transportation, healthcare, water sanitation and hygiene, and housing) and system disruption (dysregulated systems of safety/rule of law, education, health, supply chains related to menstrual hygiene, medication, contraception, etc.).Figure 4Conceptual model linking climate change and SRHR in LMICs

Conceptual model linking climate change and SRHR in LMICs

Physical damage to (1) healthcare and (2) transportation infrastructure, and (3) health system disruption were most studied. The pathways that investigated system damage and disruption involved EWE like floods and storms and SRHR outcomes that require access to health supplies (medication, contraception, and menstrual hygiene products) or healthcare providers (i.e., ante- and postnatal care, facility births, and HIV/AIDS treatment and counseling). For example, when discussing the impact of floods, healthcare providers from Malawi described antiretroviral therapy (ART) stockouts, damaged medications, flooded roads, and increased demand for replaced medication.49 Further, qualitative evidence from Nigeria noted that rainstorms flooded local communities, sweeping raw sewage and urine into homes.90 This contaminated water and instruments used during home deliveries.90 As a result of exposure to unhygienic and unsafe environmental conditions in the first minutes of life, participants recounted newborn children who developed neonatal tetanus infection and died shortly after.90

The demographic shift domain involves movement and migration due to forced displacement (internal and external) and male outmigration as mediators, both of which can result in family separation, loss of social support, and confinement in the home or emergency shelters, with implications for SRHR. For example, male outmigration often increases domestic responsibilities for women and girls, who are left to manage household tasks and care for family members. This dynamic reinforces and deepens existing gender inequities. Commonly studied was displacement to emergency shelters/camps, appearing in half of the pathways. Male outmigration (referring to displacement of men/boys and their influx into host communities) and family separation, loss, and reduced social support each appeared in over one-quarter of the pathways studied. Male out migration and family separation can increase the risk factors for different forms of GBV like sexual violence and sexual harassment as well as child marriage. For example, in a qualitative study conducted in Chad, a local women’s advocate observed that drought-induced famine resulted in men fleeing in “exodus,” resulting in familial abandonment and men looking to marry young girls from poor families.45

The socioeconomic resources domain encompasses both economic shocks (magnifying unemployment, precarious labor domestic/parenting burdens, and reducing agriculture, livestock, and fishing yield) and food and water insecurity, which reduce diet quality and increase hunger, distance to water sources, and household water conflicts. Economic/employment shocks, agricultural/fishing shocks, food insecurity, and hunger each appeared in over half of the pathways identified. Household economic shocks were reported as precipitating factors for violence perpetration and the use of child marriage as a coping mechanism as well as reduced healthcare seeking in the pre- and postnatal periods. For example, quantitative evidence linking drought and child marriage was strongest in Bangladesh and coastal Andhra Pradesh in South India (regions where household resources are sensitive to drought).43 A qualitative study from Ghana described child marriage as a household coping strategy during climate-related resource scarcity.44 Further, food and water insecurity were related to reduced HIV treatment adherence and to sexual risk behaviors that can increase the risk of HIV transmission. In South Africa, water scarcity disrupted ART adherence by constraining the ability to ingest medication.118

Mediators in the behavioral coping category include an increase in potentially harmful behaviors like substance use, transactional sex, and multiple sex partners as well as reduced healthcare seeking, empowerment, and mental and emotional health. Mediators studied in over one-third of the pathways were (1) deteriorated mental and emotional well-being and (2) men’s substance use (mainly alcohol). Deteriorated mental/emotional well-being and substance use among men appeared in the mechanisms between climate change and domestic violence/ IPV. For example, qualitative evidence from Kenya described household conflict and emotional distress arising from water insecurity and associated domestic burdens.39 In Bangladesh, qualitative research following cyclones documented emotional distress and heightened sexual harassment among women and girls in emergency shelters.33 A study from India linked drought-induced male unemployment to increased violence against women.36 Multi-country evidence from Lesotho, Tanzania, Uganda, and Zambia suggested that drought-related poverty and food insecurity increased engagement in high-risk sex behaviors among rural women.47

Reported mediators situated at the biological level are physiological processes related to thermoregulation, oxytocin release and uterine contraction, intrauterine growth restriction, sperm count and morphology, fetal heart rate and movement, and sodium intake during pregnancy. Mechanisms involving biological mediators investigated maternal-infant outcomes (pregnancy loss, pre-term birth, low birth weight, gestational hypertension) and fertility. Biological mediators were most apparent in studies examining temperature and salinity exposures. For example, studies from Gambia,65 Iran,64 Ethiopia,62,68 and other LMIC settings63,66,67 linked heat exposure during pregnancy to physiological processes associated with pregnancy loss, preterm birth, low birth weight, and neonatal mortality. Evidence from coastal Bangladesh linked saline drinking water and elevated sodium intake during pregnancy to gestational hypertension and pre-eclampsia.69 Studies from India and Argentina further associated high temperatures with reduced sperm quality, concentration, motility, and morphology.66,67

Also featured in our typology of mechanisms based on the literature (Figure 4) are pre-existing contextual realities (i.e., those that predate the climate change exposure) grouped into 6 domains: (1) patriarchal norms, (2) cultural power structures, (3) socioeconomic realities, (4) gender inequitable climate governance, (5) place-based factors, and (6) pre-existing systems/infrastructure. These contextual factors can amplify the harmful impacts of climate change on women and girls compared to men and boys and disproportionately affect those at intersecting axes of marginalization, such as poverty and ethnicity.

Patriarchal norms place men in positions of power by limiting women’s and girls’ movement, decision-making, and bodily control as well as promoting harmful gender roles and the shame and stigma around women’s/girls’ bodies and sexualities. Women’s/girls’ limited decision-making and harmful gender norms concerning domestic labor were studied in about half of the pathways. For example, in situations of forced displacement, women and girls have less control over whether, where, and when to migrate compared to men. In crowded emergency camps, menstruation-related stigma marginalizes adolescent girls; pre-existing practices of sexual harassment (eve teasing) creates environments where girl child marriage is perceived as protective against GBV and a means to protect family honor. In Bangladesh, adolescent girls described feelings of shame and difficulty managing menstruation during flood-induced forced displacement due to gendered expectations around privacy and bodily cleanliness.61 Studies from Kenya,39 Tanzania,38 and Chad98 showed how drought-related water insecurity, male outmigration, and resource scarcity intensified domestic burdens for women and girls (reflecting the presence of gender inequitable norms).

Cultural power structures concern the ideologies that justify discrimination, such as colonialism, as well as context-specific forms of discrimination, such as the caste system. Dowry practices appeared in pathways investigating child marriage and GBV. Discrimination by caste or Indigenous identity were apparent relative to maternal-infant outcomes given discrimination-related differential access to care, social stigmatization, and the inequitable patterning of the social determinants of health.

Socioeconomic realities include existing gradients determined by poverty/debt, family structure and size, education level, and reliance on subsistence farming or agricultural work. Pre-existing gradients (related to income, education, and employment) and reliance on subsistence farming/agricultural work were most dominant, each studied by half of the pathways. For example, rural households relying on subsistence farming or fishing are among the most vulnerable to climate-related exposures, lacking economic and social protections for women and girls and persons living with HIV/AIDS. Further, pre-existing socioeconomic gradients moderated the risk of child marriage following drought. For example, evidence from Bangladesh noted that weather shocks (defined as dry months with precipitation significantly below the long-term average) had a larger (and more statistically significant) relationship with child marriage among the poorest upazilas compared to the less-poor upazilas.94 Rural communities that rely on subsistence farming or cash crops like cane sugar are particularly vulnerable to the socioeconomic consequences of drought. In Nepal, socially disadvantaged women faced heightened vulnerability due to chronic stressors that amplified the biological response to heat, including an acute cortisol surge entering placental circulation.159 In Pakistan, maternal nutritional supplementation prior to pregnancy emerged as a potential protective factor, attenuating the impact of heat on neonatal birth length.142

Gender-inequitable climate governance concerns gender-unequal and blind climate governance of warning systems, disaster preparedness, aid distribution, and water access. Warning and preparedness systems were deemed gender inequitable in some of the literature reviewed because women’s and girls’ lived realities were not considered in their development. For example, this was evident in limited consultations with women and girls, insufficient targeting of communication to reach them, and inadequate dissemination of information through their social and kinship networks. The mechanism linking climate disasters and salinity to menstrual health and hygiene was shaped by multiple gender-inequitable climate policies, including those related to warning systems, aid distribution, and water governance.79 The presence of early disaster warning systems and community-based educational opportunities have the potential to disrupt the harmful impacts of climate disasters on child marriage. Evidence emerged from an Indonesian study, which analyzed various contextual factors: availability of early warning systems, welfare status of villages, education facilities, and infrastructure.114 Early disaster warning systems reduced the probability of child marriage by 2.9 percentage points (p < 0.05).114 Secondly, the availability of community-based secondary school and skills training institutions reduced the probability of a girl entering child marriage.114 Each additional facility reduced the probability of a girl to be a child bride by 1.3% (secondary school) and 0.46% (skills training institutions).114

Place-based modifiers of climate change effects on SRHR reported in the reviewed literature included densely populated settlements and geographical realities like rural areas, living near or on riverbanks/coastal settlements, and lowland and island housing. For example, island communities in Bangladesh were also far away from healthcare facilities and relied on boats for transport, even prior to the onset of a climate disaster.82 The place-based factor reported most was living in rural areas, represented by half of the pathways investigated, relating to the differential effects of climate change on gestational hypertension, maternal-infant outcomes, fertility, and HIV/AIDS in rural compared to urban areas.

Contextual moderators in the category of pre-existing systems and infrastructure concerned the degree of resilience in water, sanitation, and hygiene (WASH); healthcare; transportation; energy; and education systems, as well as in the labor force. Weak medical infrastructure and water and sanitation systems as well as women’s/girls’ pre-existing education levels and employment access were each represented by about one-third of the pathways studied. Where systems and infrastructure are not resilient in the face of climate change exposures, there is greater risk for gender-inequitable SRHR outcomes. For example, an unreliable energy grid increases the risk of birth complications and magnifies risk of opportunistic violence against displaced women and girls in emergency shelters. When schools cannot resume their function following an EWE, adolescent girls are deprived of educational opportunities that delay marriage and childbearing.

Discussion

This scoping review synthesized evidence from 256 empirical studies exploring the relationship between climate change and SRHR in LMICs. The LMIC-focused literature is concentrated on quantitative research conducted in South and East Asia (mainly China and Bangladesh) and Sub-Saharan Africa. Most evidence focused on temperature, floods, and droughts. Maternal infant health outcomes emerged as the most frequently studied SRHR domain. In contrast, domains such as abortion, psychosexual health, and SRHR education were rarely addressed. We synthesized 14 possible pathways linking climate-related exposures to SRHR outcomes. The pathways are underpinned by a typology of mediators and contextual moderators, which could advance a mechanistic understanding of climate change impacts on SRHR.

Just as climate impacts are shaped by context, the evidence available to understand the relationship between climate change and SRHR is also shaped by geopolitical and funding contexts. Certain climate exposures and SRHR outcomes dominate not only because they are important but also because research funding priorities shape what gets studied. The concentration of funding organizations in a relatively small number of countries highlights that knowledge production on climate change and SRHR is itself embedded within global funding structures. These patterns may influence which research questions are prioritized, which populations are studied, and which pathways receive empirical attention. Consequently, the gaps identified in this review may reflect not only evidence deficits but also broader inequities in research investment and agenda setting.

In 2023, a Delphi consultation established expert-identified research priorities across on climate change and SRHR in LMICs and high-income countries.160 This initiative called for investments in better SRHR indicators, intersectional analytical frameworks, and rigorous methodologies, particularly longitudinal studies and impact evaluations to map the casual pathways between climate change and SRHR outcomes.160 Funders can additionally support research that tests and refines our understanding of the mechanisms underlying harmful associations for the purpose of designing relevant adaptation responses.

Investigating theoretical causal pathways between climate change and SRHR enables researchers and practitioners to more deeply understand underlying processes of harm and strategic intervention points in a contextually relevant manner. Accordingly, our work contributes to the development of more effective, gender-responsive climate action and adaptation plans that fully incorporate SRHR dimensions.

The findings from our review could advance intervention and policy design in several ways: (1) contextualization of harmful associations (harmful effects are not equally harmful across populations and regions), (2) target improvement (by strategically identifying and acting on mediators that induce harm), and (3) revealing important heterogeneity regarding opportunities for protection based on contextual features. As our synthesis demonstrates, analysis of mediators helps explain how or why climate change yields deleterious SRHR outcomes and analysis of contextual moderations helps explain under what conditions and for whom effects are most harmful.

Future research can apply causal thinking to more deeply explore the mechanisms between climate change and SRHR in distinct LMICs, making broader connections to other aspects of quality of life. For example, attention should be given to investigating the possible mediating paths identified in this review as well as effect moderation and statistical interaction based on contextual moderators. Understanding these mechanisms is essential for identifying how climate change exacerbates downstream SRHR outcomes and designing interventions that prevent and mitigate harmful pathways.160 Moreover, additional research is needed to further distinguish pathways of harm involving extreme events and gradual changes in climate indicators. In our review, chronic temperature changes were related to biological mechanisms that affected birth outcomes like pregnancy loss and anthropometry and fertility. Evidence for a more diverse set of SRHR outcomes was available with respect to droughts and EWEs like floods and storms. Lastly, adopting a polycrisis lens can clarify the interrelated relationship between climate change and other macro-level stressors in LMICs, like conflict.161,162 Though conflict did not emerge as a contextual or mediating construct in the studies analyzed, its patterning in climate-affected countries warrants further research.

Further, interventions should be developed in discussion and dialog with diverse community members and frontline workers at the field level in LMICs, reflecting the lived experiences of those most affected.160,163 In terms of intervention adaptation, implementation science can prioritize both contextual moderators and pathway mediators, while centering localized knowledge to effectively embed SRHR into climate adaptation strategies.164 Investing in social science research in these areas can support the development of more contextually relevant, equitable, climate-resilient health systems that protect and promote SRHR in the face of ongoing climate change disasters.

In our review, climate change disasters and alterations in climate indicators engendered infrastructure damage, threatened livelihoods, and intensified stress and lost social support due to migration and mortality. Pre-existing and contextual gendered power structures that reduced women’s decision-making, autonomy, and degree of household/parenting labor also intensified. Thus, in the absence of gender protections, informed by a mechanistic understanding of harmful pathways, opportunities to advance the human capital of women, girls, and gender minorities dwindle as climate change continues to unfold. These consequences are most pronounced in communities where women and girls are already marginalized and unable to experience their full human rights.

As climate shocks intensify, so do existing structural inequalities: discriminatory norms, restricted access to reproductive care, and intensified domestic labor.165 These barriers heighten vulnerabilities, disrupt education and SRHR services, and threaten fundamental rights and bodily autonomy.166 Yet climate governance remains overwhelmingly gender blind, meaning it does not consider the existing gender norms and social relations, reinforces gender-based discrimination, and ignores gender differences in resource allocation.167

Given both the intensification of climate change and gender equality backsliding, policies and future research must move toward transformative change.168,169 There are several ways to promote transformation, informed by causal thinking. For example, in our synthesis, floods, storms, and drought adversely affected men’s traditional gender role of being an economic provider, primarily among households relying on agriculture and fishing in rural areas. There is an opportunity to prevent incident and lethal cases of violence against women under these conditions by focusing on men’s mental/emotional well-being and maladaptive coping via substance use and by providing social protection programs to female household members who are above a certain age (to disincentivize child marriage as a mechanism for creating more households).

Future research should be locally grounded and gender focused. Funding should prioritize participatory research models that center the experiences of key groups.170 Adolescence is a sensitive period of physical, emotional, and social development for girls and presents a window for policy action. For example, over 880 million adolescent girls and young women globally face menstruation within the wider context of food insecurity, early marriage and early childbearing, and sexual violence, all of which are intensified by climate shocks.171 In Kenya, girls are pushed into exploitative relationships during droughts to access basic needs like menstrual supplies, yet menstrual health under climate stress remains poorly understood.22 In Northern Ghana, for instance, women reported using rags, leaves, or tissues during menstruation due to climate-induced economic strain, leading to adverse health effects.170 Similarly, in flood-prone regions of Bangladesh, women reported prolonged use of hormonal contraceptives to avoid menstruation, as they lacked access to WASH infrastructure.170 These examples illustrate how climate change directly affects menstrual health: a subject that remains critically under-researched in general and from a mechanistic perspective.172

This review has several limitations. First, although studies represented 108 LMICs, the evidence base was geographically concentrated in a relatively small number of countries, particularly China, Bangladesh, India, Uganda, Pakistan, Malawi, and Kenya, which may limit the transferability of findings to less-studied settings. Second, the literature was heavily concentrated on maternal-infant outcomes, temperature, floods, and droughts, while comparatively little evidence was available for abortion, psychosexual health, and menstrual health and hygiene, and no studies examined the outcomes related to comprehensive sexuality education and information. This uneven distribution of evidence influenced the pathways and mechanisms that could be identified. Third, while our review sought to advance causal thinking by synthesizing reported mediators and contextual moderators, most included studies were observational, cross-sectional, or qualitative in design, limiting the ability to draw causal conclusions regarding the pathways identified. Further, the pathways presented in this review should not be interpreted as universally established causal mechanisms. Although some included studies investigated mechanisms, many proposed pathways were derived from qualitative evidence describing lived experiences or from synthesis-level integration across studies of varying study designs. Accordingly, the framework is best understood as identifying plausible and evidence-informed mechanisms requiring further empirical testing rather than confirming causal pathways. Fourth, the review was restricted to English-language, peer-reviewed publications and excluded gray literature; therefore, relevant evidence from non-English sources, government reports, technical documents, and programmatic evaluations may not have been captured. Consistent with scoping review methodology, we did not conduct a formal quality appraisal or risk-of-bias assessment, and the findings should, therefore, be interpreted as a mapping of the available evidence rather than an assessment of evidence certainty. Finally, publication bias may have affected the available evidence base, as studies reporting significant or novel findings are more likely to be published than studies reporting null or inconsistent results. Together, these limitations underscore the need for more geographically diverse, methodologically rigorous, and under-represented areas of SRHR research within the climate change literature.

Conclusions

Overall, our work underscores the need to integrate SRHR into climate change discourse, policy, and action, particularly in LMICs. Mechanistic thinking offers a powerful lens to move beyond correlation and toward a causal understanding of how and why SRHR inequities persist and are magnified by climate change. As climate shocks intensify and global funding landscapes shift, it is imperative that SRHR is not sidelined. Future research can deepen causal inquiry, expand geographic and thematic coverage, and elevate under-represented voices with respect to designing and testing interventions.
