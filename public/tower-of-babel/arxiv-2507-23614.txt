Elliptic unique continuation below the Lipschitz threshold
Cole Jeznach

Abstract
. In this article, we investigate unique continuation principles for solutions u u of uniformly elliptic equations of the form − div ( A ∇ u ) = 0 -\divv(A\nabla u)=0 when A A is less regular than Lipschitz. For general matrices A A , we prove that strong unique continuation holds provided A A has modulus of continuity ω \omega satisfying the Osgood condition, ∫ 0 1 ω ​ ( t ) − 1 ​ 𝑑 t = ∞ \int_{0}^{1}\omega(t)^{-1}dt=\infty , plus some other mild hypotheses. Along with the counterexamples of [ Man98 ] , this shows that the sharp condition on A A that guarantees unique continuation is essentially that A A is log-Lipschitz. In the class of isotropic equations (i.e., A ⁡ ( x ) = a ⁡ ( x ) ​ I A(x)=a(x)I for some scalar function a a ) we show that Hölder continuity of a a of the order α ∈ ( 2 / 3 , 1 ) \alpha\in(2/3,1) is sufficient to guarantee strong unique continuation. This latter result contrasts counterexamples known for anisotropic equations, and disproves a conjecture of Miller from 1974 [ Mil74 ] .

1.
Introduction

Questions regarding unique continuation for solutions of linear PDEs are among the most fundamental. A linear partial differential operator
\mathcal{L}
, defined in a connected domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, is said to satisfy the
weak unique continuation principle
if every solution
u
of
\mathcal{L}u=0
in
\Omega
that vanishes on an open subset of
\Omega
must vanish identically:
u\equiv 0
. If all solutions are
L^{2}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\Omega)
, then we say that the operator
\mathcal{L}
satisfies the
strong unique continuation principle
provided that whenever
u
vanishes to infinite order (in the
L^{2}
-sense) at some point
x\in\Omega
, i.e.,

(1.1)

\displaystyle\lim_{r\rightarrow 0}r^{-2N}\fint_{B_{r}(x)}u^{2}\,dx=0\text{ for all }N>0,

then
u\equiv 0
. If the condition above fails for large
N
, then the
vanishing order of
u
at
x
is defined as the supremum over all
N\geq 0
for which the limit in (
1.1
) vanishes. Understanding how the size and regularity of the coefficients defining
\mathcal{L}
impact unique continuation remains a subtle and rich area of investigation, with many fundamental questions still open. Moreover, unique continuation principles (along with the techniques used to develop them) have found widespread applications, including in inverse problems
[
KSU07
,
Hab15
,
CR16
]
, harmonic measure and nodal geometry
[
DF88
,
Log18a
,
Log18b
,
LMNN21
,
Tol23
]
, quantitative uniqueness results for PDE
[
ESvS03
,
ESS03
,
KSW15
,
LMNN25
]
, and Anderson localization
[
BK05
]
.

The focus of this article is linear, symmetric, uniformly elliptic operators

(1.2)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}u=-\divv(A\nabla u),\,\,\text{ in }B_{1}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}.

Of course when
A=I
is the identity, solutions of (
1.2
) are harmonic, and hence analytic; as such,
-\Delta
satisfies the strong unique continuation principle. In his pioneering work
[
Car39
]
, Carleman proved that strong unique continuation also holds for operators of the form (
1.2
) in two dimensions, provided that
A\in C^{2}(B_{1})
. The method he introduced via weighted inequalities–now known as Carleman estimates– has become a ubiquitous tool for proving unique continuation results across a wide range of PDEs, well beyond the elliptic setting. Since then, the role of the smoothness condition on
A
has largely been clarified. When
n=2
, strong unique continuation holds even for
A
which are merely bounded measurable
[
BN55
,
Sch98
]
. For dimensions
n\geq 3
, improvements by
[
Aro57
,
Agm66
]
, and finally
[
GL86
]
showed that Lipschitz regularity of
A
suffices to guarantee strong unique continuation. Such results are valid even when
\mathcal{L}
has singular lower-order terms; if
\mathcal{L}u=-\divv(A\nabla u)+W\cdot\nabla u+Vu
with
A
Lipschitz,
W\in L^{n}
, and
V\in L^{n/2}
then strong unique continuation still holds
[
JK85
,
Wol92
,
KT01
]
. Moreover, these exponent thresholds are essentially sharp.

In the other direction, Miller showed in dimensions
n\geq 3

[
Mil74
]
that weak unique continuation for (
1.2
) may fail if we only assume
A
is Hölder continuous (see also the earlier example of Plis in non-divergence form
[
Pli63
]
). Later, Mandache improved the counterexample of Miller and showed that there is a matrix
A
which is Hölder continuous of every exponent
\alpha\in(0,1)
but for which
\mathcal{L}
does not satisfy the weak unique continuation principle. In fact, Mandache provided for each modulus of continuity
\omega:[0,1]\rightarrow[0,\infty)
satisfying

(1.3)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\dfrac{1}{\omega(t)}\,dt<\infty,

an associated symmetric, uniformly elliptic matrix
A
with modulus of continuity
\omega
for which weak unique continuation fails for the operator
\mathcal{L}

[
Man98
]
. Taking
\omega(t)=t\log(1/t)^{p}
for
p>1
then gives the claimed matrix
A
. It is worth noting that the examples of both
[
Mil74
]
and
[
Man98
]
are anisotropic, i.e.,
A(x)
cannot be written as
A(x)=a(x)I
for any scalar function
a
.

Unlike Carleman’s, the methods of
[
GL86
,
GL87
]
relied on monotonicity properties of the so-called
Almgren frequency function

N_{u}^{A}(r)
, which for
\mathcal{L}=-\Delta
takes the form

\displaystyle N_{u}^{I}(r)=\dfrac{r^{2}\fint_{B_{r}}\left\lvert\nabla u\right\rvert^{2}\,dx}{\fint_{\partial B_{r}}u^{2}\,d\sigma}.

This frequency function, which measures in a scale-invariant way the dominant homogeneity of
u
in
B_{r}
, is known to be almost-monotone and bounded in
r
if
A
is Lipschitz. Such properties of this frequency function and its relatives have proven to be essential in quantitative unique continuation and free boundary problems. Indeed, bounds on the frequency function allow one to deduce doubling inequalities for the measure
u^{2}\,dx
and obtain structural information on the infinitesimal behavior of the solution
u
about the origin. It is impossible to list all such applications, so instead we point the reader to some of the recent results in quantitative unique continuation such as
[
CNV15
,
NV17a
,
NV17b
,
LM18a
,
LM18b
,
Log18a
,
Gal23
,
CLMM24
,
Fos25
]
, as well as the references
[
LM20
,
Spo22
,
SVG24
]
for further reading. See also the recent results of
[
Dav25
]
, where the author reproves elliptic unique continuation results originally obtained via the Carleman method using instead monotonicity properties of the Almgren frequency.

The purpose of this article is to study the monotonicity properties of the Almgren frequency (and associated unique continuation principles) when
A
is less regular than Lipschitz. In the anisotropic setting, we shorten the gap between the counterexamples for unique continuation provided by
[
Man98
]
and the sufficiency of the Lipschitz condition used in
[
GL86
]
to prove strong unique continuation. Our main result applies to matrices
A
which admit a modulus of continuity
\omega
for which (
1.3
) fails (such
\omega
are said to satisfy the
Osgood
condition).

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
u\not\equiv 0
solve

(1.4)

\displaystyle-\divv(A\nabla u)=0\text{ in }B_{1}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n},

where
A(0)=I
,
A
is symmetric and uniformly elliptic with constant
\lambda\in(0,1)
, and
A
has modulus of continuity
\omega(t)
for which
\omega(t)
and
\phi(t)\coloneqq\omega(t)/t
satisfy

(1)

\int_{0}^{1}\omega(t)^{-1}\,dt=\infty
,

(2)

\int_{0}^{1}\phi(s)\,ds<\infty
,

(3)

\phi(st)\leq C\phi(s)\phi(t)
for some
C>1
and all
0<s,t\leq C^{-1}
.

Then the vanishing order of
u
at
0
does not exceed
N
, where
N
is some value depending just on
n
,
\lambda
, the modulus of continuity
\omega
, and the frequency
N_{u}^{A}(1)
of
u
in
B_{1}
.

See Definition
2.5
for the expression
N_{u}^{A}(1)
. In view of the examples of
[
Man98
]
and Theorem
1.1
, the threshold for unique continuation is more accurately described as
A
having
Osgood
modulus of continuity
\omega
. Indeed in practice the conditions (
2
),(
3
) are likely satisfied by many moduli of continuity
\omega
which satisfy (
1
), and Theorem
1.1
applies to, for example
\omega(t)=t\log(1/t)
. Along with
[
Man98
]
, we then infer that if
A
has modulus of continuity given by
\omega(t)=t\log(1/t)^{p}
, then unique continuation for
\mathcal{L}
holds for
p=1
but may fail for any choice of
p>1
.

In the isotropic setting, i.e., when
A(x)=a(x)I
for some scalar function
a
, we obtain stronger results. In particular, we show that if
a\in C^{0,\alpha}(B_{1})
for
\alpha>2/3
, then strong unique continuation holds for the operator
\mathcal{L}
:

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
u\not\equiv 0
solve

\displaystyle-\divv(a\nabla u)=0\text{ in }B_{1}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n},

where
a:B_{1}\rightarrow[\lambda,\lambda^{-1}]
is
\alpha
-Hölder continuous with constant
C_{h}
for
\alpha>2/3
. Then there exists constants
C>1
and
r_{0}\in(0,1)
depending just on the dimension
n
,
\lambda
,
C_{h}
, and
\alpha
so that the vanishing order of
u
at
0
does not exceed
(N_{u}^{a}(r_{0})+2)^{C}
.

This disproves a conjecture of Miller from
[
Mil74
]
on unique continuation for isotropic elliptic equations, and shows in particular that the threshold for unique continuation is different for anisotropic and isotropic elliptic equations. With a clever factoring technique, one can use Theorem
1.2
to prove strong unique continuation for
\mathcal{L}u=-\divv(a\nabla u)+Vu
with
V\in L^{p}(B_{1})
for
p>n
. The author thanks Max Engelstein for showing him this fact. For a precise statement, see Theorem
3.14
and the discussion that precedes it. We emphasize that in
[
Mil74
]
, the author claims that their initial counterexample to unique continuation for
parabolic
equations in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
n\geq 3
, was for an operator of the form

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}u=u_{t}-\divv(a\nabla u)+Vu,

where
a
is isotropic and Hölder continuous, and
V\in L^{\infty}
. Theorem
3.14
shows then that no such similar example exists in the elliptic setting, as long as the Hölder exponent of
a
is larger than
2/3
.

One cannot expect a version of Theorem
1.2
to hold without assuming some regularity of the coefficients
a
. Indeed, as explained in the final Remark in
[
Mil74
]
, the results of
[
MS69
]
show that solutions of anisotropic elliptic equations can be uniformly approximated by solutions of isotropic equations (with slightly worse ellipticity constant). As such, if the conclusion of Theorem
1.2
were true for
a
just bounded and measurable, then this would contradict the Hölder continuous anisotropic counterexamples to unique continuation constructed in
[
Mil74
]
.

Finally, it is worth noting that since our methods give uniform bounds on the frequency function
N_{u}^{A}(r)
, either in the context of Theorem
1.1
or Theorem
1.2
, then we can also obtain uniform doubling properties of solutions and apply the recent results of
[
HJ23
]
to obtain the following:

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
u\not\equiv 0
solve

(1.5)

\displaystyle-\divv(A\nabla u)=0\text{ in }B_{1}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n},

where
A
is Hölder continuous of order
\alpha\in(0,1)
and one of the following holds:

(1)

A
satisfies the hypothesis of Theorem
1.1
, or

(2)

A=aI
with
a
scalar, and
\alpha>2/3
.

Then the critical set
\mathcal{C}(u)\coloneqq\{x\in B_{1}\,:\,\nabla u(x)=0\}
is
(n-2)
-rectifiable and has locally finite
(n-2)
-Minkowski content
B_{1}
.

The point of Theorem
1.3
is that the solution
u
is not assumed apriori to be doubling, such as in the main results of
[
HJ23
]
. Theorem
1.3
can be made more quantitative (using the more quantitative conclusions of Theorems
2.11
and
3.13
), but we do not expect the estimates to be optimal, especially in the isotropic setting. It is likely that an analogue Theorem
1.3
holds for the nodal set
\mathcal{N}(u)\coloneqq\{x\in B_{1}\;:\;u(x)=0\}
of solutions as well (replacing the dimension
(n-2)
with
(n-1)
), but since
[
HJ23
]
do not study the size of the nodal set, we content ourselves just with stating the application to the size of critical sets.

1.1.
Discussion of the main results and outline of the paper

Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
are new in that they show unique continuation in a class of operators without requiring estimates (or structure) on
\nabla A
. Indeed it is worth pointing out that unique continuation results have been shown for Lipschitz coefficients
A
with jump discontinuities across a hypersurface
[
LRL13
,
DCFL+17
,
FVW22
]
, or degenerate-singular elliptic equations when
\nabla A
has some homogeneous structure
[
CS07
,
GSVG14
,
Yu17
,
STT20
,
EJS25
]
. Additionally, in the isotropic setting (
A=aI
), if
a\in W^{1,p}(B_{1})
for
p\geq n
, then strong unique continuation holds as a consequence of the results in
[
Wol92
,
KT01
]
, since
u
solves
-\divv(a\nabla u)=0
if and only if

(1.6)

\displaystyle-\Delta u

\displaystyle=\nabla(\log(a))\cdot\nabla u.

Since
W^{1,p}
embeds into
C^{0,1-n/p}
for
p>n
, it is reasonable to expect that Theorem
1.2
holds for all
\alpha\in(0,1)
. When
a
is only Hölder continuous though, we cannot use this embedding, and indeed our arguments do not rely on equations of the type (
1.6
).

Interestingly, the reformulation (
1.6
) turns out to be an essential part of studying uniqueness results for isotropic conductivities in the Calderón problem, where one tries to reconstruct a conductivity
a
from knowledge of the Dirichlet-to-Neumann map associated to elliptic operator

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}=-\divv(a\nabla\,\cdot\,),

for
a\in W^{1,n}
. It has been proved that uniqueness holds for
n=3,4
, but remains open in higher dimensions
[
Hab15
]
. This conjecture is motivated by the unique continuation results that hold operators of the type (
1.6
), since the Calderón problem and unique continuation are connected morally (and in some ways technically, since both pass through Carleman inequalities). In view of Theorem
1.2
then, it is natural to wonder whether uniqueness in the Calderón problem holds for isotropic, Hölder continuous conductivities. On the other hand, it seems new ideas are needed to extend Theorem
1.2
to the range
\alpha\in(0,2/3]
, and thus it remains an interesting open question even whether strong unique continuation holds for the operator
(
1.2
)
with
A=aI
and
a\in C^{0,\alpha}(B_{1})
,
\alpha\in(0,2/3]
.

While the proofs of the two main Theorems
1.1
,
1.2
look quite different from afar, the strategies are the same. In essence, we use the regularity of the coefficients on
A
to control the size of the Almgren frequency function associated to a solution,
u
, from the scale
r
to the scale
r(1-1/N)
, where
N=N_{u}^{A}(r)
is the frequency of
u
at the scale
r
. After repeating this many times, we show that in fact
N_{u}^{A}(r)
is bounded for all sufficiently small
r
which readily implies the bound on the vanishing order.

In the anisotropic setting with Osgood modulus of continuity, we can directly smooth the coefficients
A
and pass estimates on a solution
v
to an equation with smooth coefficients directly to the solution
u
. The key estimate in this section is Lemma
2.10
, which is in some sense an integrated version of almost-monotonicity of
N_{u}^{A}(r)
. The Osgood condition is well-suited to this context, and guarantees slow enough growth from
r
to
r(1-1/N)
to ensure that
N_{u}^{a}(r)
does not blow up at a finite radius
r^{\prime}
; see Lemma
2.3
. In the isotropic setting with Hölder continuous coefficients, the same approximation necessarily fails, and so we need to employ a different method that uses the structure of the equation.

The key difference in the isotropic setting is that we have a true monotonicity formula for solutions of isotropic equations with
homogeneous
equations (Lemma
3.1
). Using this true monotonocity, we are able to show apriori that the Almgren frequency function
N_{u}^{a}(r)
can blow up at most at a slow polynomial rate (see Theorem
3.10
). Proving this small polynomial blowup is what requires the most work, and new ideas. In particular, we first introduce a modified frequency function,
\tilde{N}_{u}^{a}(r,\rho)
, which depends on two scales
0<\rho<r
and appears to be better suited to approximation arguments. In particular, in Lemma
3.8
we prove a version of almost-monotonicity for
\tilde{N}_{u}^{a}
which holds for coefficients
a
which are Hölder of order
\alpha>1/2
, and we use this almost-monotonicity to pass the small polynomial blowup back to the original frequency
N_{u}^{a}(r)
when
\alpha>2/3
. Once we obtain the small polynomial blowup of
N_{u}^{a}(r)
, we can further use the Hölder regularity to deduce that in fact
N_{u}^{a}(r)
is bounded for all sufficiently small
r
(and in fact, this is a general result which works for anisotropic equations, see the proof of Theorem
3.13
). Thus the difficulty in studying unique continuation properties for isotropic equations is reduced to showing the small-polynomial blow-up, and indeed it is Theorem
3.10
which requires the most work.

The entirety of this paper is valid in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
for
n\geq 2
. As is usual in analysis papers, we denote by
C>1
and
0<c<1
large and small constants which may change from line to line, and only depend on the dimension

unless otherwise specified. We use the symbol
A\lesssim_{D}B
to mean that there is some constant
C>1

and

to mean that

CA

A=B+O(D)

\left\lvert A-B\right\rvert\lesssim D

\displaystyle\lambda\left\lvert\xi\right\rvert^{2}\leq A\xi\cdot\xi,\qquad\left\lvert A\xi\right\rvert\leq\lambda^{-1}\left\lvert\xi\right\rvert,\,\,\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

2.
Anisotropic equations

In this section, we prove the main result about anisotropic equations, Theorem
1.1
. The main tools in the proof consist of elementary boundedness results for functions satisfying a differential inequality (Lemma
2.3
) and almost-monotonicity of the Almgren frequency function when
A
is Lipschitz (Lemma
2.6
). Through a standard approximating argument, we combine them to arrive at the main result, which is Theorem
2.11
.

First, let us introduce some notation and basic properties related to moduli of continuity.

Definition 2.1
.

We say that
\omega:[0,1]\rightarrow[0,\infty)
is a modulus of continuity if
\omega
is a continuous, non-decreasing, concave function, and
\omega(0)=0
. We further say that
\omega
satisfies the
Osgood condition
provided that

(2.1)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\dfrac{dt}{\omega(t)}=\infty.

Associated to the modulus of continuity
\omega
, we introduce the function
\phi(s)=\omega(s)/s
which has the following properties.

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
\omega:[0,1]\rightarrow[0,\infty)
be a modulus of continuity, and write
\omega(s)s=\phi(s)
,
\psi(s)=\phi(1/s)
. Then
\phi
is decreasing,
\psi
is increasing, and
1/(s\psi(s))
is decreasing.

From the previous proposition, we know that
\lim_{s\rightarrow 0^{+}}\phi(s)
exists. Since the case when
\lim_{s\rightarrow 0^{+}}\phi(s)<\infty
corresponds to Lipschitz functions (which, from the work of
[
GL86
]
is well-understood), we may as well restrict our attention to non-Lipschitz modululi
\omega
, i..e, the case when

(2.2)

\displaystyle\lim_{s\rightarrow 0^{+}}\phi(s)=+\infty.

First, let us make the following elementary observations, which will be the main tool in the proof of Theorem
2.11
.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
\omega
be an Osgood modulus of continuity, and let
f:[0,1]\rightarrow[0,\infty]
be a decreasing function (locally Lipschitz whenever it is finite) satisfying the differential inequality

(2.3)

\displaystyle f^{\prime}(t)\geq-C_{1}f(t)\psi(f(t))g(t),\,\,\text{a.e. }t\in(0,1],\,\,f(t)<\infty,

for some nonnegative
g\in L^{\infty}_{\mathrm{loc}}(0,1)
and some constant
C_{1}>0
. If
f(1)<\infty
, then
f
is bounded on compact subsets of
(0,1]
. If in addition
\int_{0}^{1}g(t)\,dt<\infty
, then
f
is bounded on
[0,1]
.

Proof.

Recall the notation
\omega(s)=s\phi(s)
,
\psi(s)=\phi(1/s)
. Let
h(t)=\int_{1}^{t}1/(s\psi(s))\,ds
, so that
h^{\prime}(t)=1/(t\psi(t))
, and
h(t)
is an increasing function. By assumption, we have

\displaystyle\dfrac{d}{dt}h(f(t))\geq-C_{1}g(t),

so that

\displaystyle h(f(t))\leq h(f(1))+C_{1}\int_{t}^{1}g(s)\,ds,\,\,t\in(0,1).

Since
g\in L^{\infty}_{\mathrm{loc}}(0,1)
, we see that
h\circ f
is bounded on compact subsets of
(0,1]
. Recalling that
\omega
satisfies the Osgood condition (
2.1
), we know that

\displaystyle\infty=\int_{0}^{1}\dfrac{ds}{\omega(s)}=\int_{1}^{\infty}\dfrac{ds}{s\psi(s)},

and thus
f(t)
is bounded on compact subsets of
(0,1]
as well. In addition this also shows that if
g\in L^{1}(0,1)
, then
h\circ f
(and thus
f
) is bounded on
[0,1]
, which completes the proof.
∎

It is a simple Corollary of Lemma
2.3
that if one instead assumes an integrated version of the differential inequality (
2.3
), then the same conclusion holds. The proof consists in simply replacing the function
f
by its linear interpolant in the scales
[t_{k}(1-1/f(t_{k})),t_{k}]
for an appropriately chosen sequence
t_{k}\downarrow 0
and applying the Lemma above.

Corollary 2.4
.

Let
\omega
be an Osgood modulus of continuity, and let
f:[0,1]\rightarrow[0,\infty]
be a decreasing function satisfying

(2.4)

\displaystyle f\left(t\left(1-1/f(t)\right)\right)\leq f(t)+C_{1}t\psi(f(t))g(t),\,\,t\in(0,1),\,\,f(t)<\infty,

for some nonnegative
g\in L^{\infty}_{\mathrm{loc}}(0,1)
and some constant
C_{1}>0
. If
g
is doubling in the sense that

\displaystyle g(s)\leq C_{1}g(\gamma s),\,\,\gamma\in(1/2,1),

and
f(1)<\infty
, then
f
is bounded on compact subsets of
(0,1]
. If in addition
\int_{0}^{1}g(t)\,dt<\infty
, then
f
is bounded on
[0,1]
.

Proof.

Let
N_{0}\coloneqq f(1)<\infty
,
t_{0}\coloneqq 1
, and define inductively the sequences
t_{k+1}\coloneqq t_{k}(1-1/N_{k})
,
N_{k+1}\coloneqq f(t_{k+1})\leq N_{k}+C_{1}t_{k}\psi(N_{k})g(t_{k})
. Notice that we may assume
N_{0}\geq 2
, otherwise we simply choose
t_{0}
slightly smaller. Consider the function
h
, which on each interval
I_{k}\equiv[t_{k+1},t_{k}]
interpolates linearly between
N_{k+1}
and
N_{k}
:

\displaystyle h(t)\equiv N_{k+1}+\dfrac{N_{k}-N_{k+1}}{t_{k}-t_{k+1}}(t-t_{k+1}),\,\,t\in I_{k}.

Since
f
is decreasing, we see that
h
also is, and that
h(t)\geq N_{k}
for
t\in I_{k}
. Moreover, we see that

\displaystyle h^{\prime}(t)=(N_{k}/t_{k})(N_{k}-N_{k+1})\geq-C_{1}N_{k}\psi(N_{k})g(t_{k}),\,\,t\in I_{k}.

Using that
\psi
is increasing and the doubling condition on
g
, the above implies readily that

\displaystyle h^{\prime}(t)\geq-Ch(t)\psi(h(t))g(t),\,\,t\in I_{k}

and thus we may apply Lemma
2.3
to the function
h
. Since
h(t_{k})=f(t_{k})
and
f
is decreasing, the boundedness properties of
h
from that Lemma transfer directly to
f
, completing the proof of the Lemma.
∎

Our goal is to apply Corollary
2.4
to a suitable variant of the Almgren frequency function for a solution
u
of the uniformly elliptic equation

(2.5)

\displaystyle-\divv(A\nabla u)

\displaystyle=0\text{ in }B_{1}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n},

where
A
has an Osgood modulus of continuity
\omega
. Unique continuation principles for solutions will then follow quickly from boundedness of this frequency function. The frequency
N_{u}^{A}(r)
, which we define now, measures in some sense the dominant homogeneity of
u
inside the ball
B_{r}
.

Definition 2.5
.

If
u
solves (
2.5
) in
B_{R}
, we define the Almgren frequency

(2.6)

\displaystyle N_{u}^{A}(r)\coloneqq\dfrac{r\int_{B_{r}}\langle A\nabla u,u\rangle\,dx}{\int_{\partial B_{r}}u^{2}\mu\,d\sigma}\equiv\dfrac{rD_{u}^{A}(r)}{H_{u}^{A}(r)},\,\,0<r<R,

where
\mu(x)\coloneqq\langle A(x)x/\left\lvert x\right\rvert,x/\left\lvert x\right\rvert\rangle
.

When the matrix
A
is Lipschitz, the Almgren frequency function was shown to be almost-monotone in
[
GL86
]
. Since we need slightly more precise estimates, we provide a detailed proof in the following Lemma. In the end these estimates will be applied to solutions of
\divv(A_{\varepsilon}\nabla v)=0
where
A_{\varepsilon}=A*\eta_{\varepsilon}
,
\eta
is an approximate identity, and
\varepsilon
is a small parameter depending on the frequency of the solution
u
at scale
r
.

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
u
be a solution of (
2.5
) in
B_{R}
, and suppose that
A
is symmetric and uniformly elliptic with constant
\lambda>0
, and
A
satisfies the smoothness estimates

\displaystyle\left\lvert\nabla A(x)\right\rvert\leq M,\qquad\left\lvert A(x)-I\right\rvert\leq\delta,\,\,x\in B_{R},

for some
M,\delta>0
. Then we have the estimate

\displaystyle\dfrac{d}{dr}\log\left(N_{u}^{A}(r)\right)\geq-C_{1}(M+\delta/r),\,\,r\in(0,R),

for some constant
C_{1}>0
depending just on the dimension
n
and
\lambda
. In addition, we have the estimate

\displaystyle\dfrac{d}{dr}\log(r^{-n+1}H_{u}^{A}(r))

\displaystyle=2N_{u}^{A}(r)/r+e(r),\,\,r\in(0,R),

with error
\left\lvert e(r)\right\rvert\leq C_{1}(M+\delta/r)
.

Proof.

Recall from the definition of
N_{u}^{A}
, we have that
\mu(x)\equiv\langle A(x)x/\left\lvert x\right\rvert,x/\left\lvert x\right\rvert\rangle
. Define the vector field
\beta(x)\coloneqq A(x)x/\mu(x)
. Since the unit outer normal
\nu(x)
to
\partial B_{r}
is given by
\nu(x)=x/\left\lvert x\right\rvert
, then we have by definition of
\mu
that

(2.7)

\displaystyle A(x)\nu(x)\equiv\mu(x)\beta(x)/r,\,\,\beta(x)\cdot\nu(x)=r,\,\,x\in\partial B_{r},

Straight-forward arguments using the ellipticity and smoothness conditions on
A
give us the following estimates on
\mu
and
\beta
:

(2.8)

\displaystyle\mu(x)\simeq 1,\,\left\lvert\nabla\mu(x)\right\rvert\lesssim M+\delta/\left\lvert x\right\rvert,\,\left\lvert\mu(x)-1\right\rvert\lesssim\delta,

(2.9)

\displaystyle\left\lvert\beta(x)-x\right\rvert\lesssim\delta\left\lvert x\right\rvert,\,\left\lvert D\beta(x)-I\right\rvert\lesssim M\left\lvert x\right\rvert+\delta,

with implicit constants depending just on the dimension and
\lambda
. Of course in order to prove the estimates above, one uses that if
A\equiv I
, then
\mu(x)\equiv 1
and
\beta(x)\equiv x
.
For convenience, write
N(r)=N_{u}^{A}(r)
,
D(r)=D_{u}^{A}(r)
and
H(r)=H_{u}^{A}(r)
. One computes

(2.10)

\displaystyle\dfrac{d}{dr}\log(N(r))

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{r}+\dfrac{D^{\prime}(r)}{D(r)}-\dfrac{H^{\prime}(r)}{H(r)},

and we deal with each term separately.

First, as for
D^{\prime}(r)
we have from (
2.7
) and the divergence theorem that

\displaystyle D^{\prime}(r)

\displaystyle=\int_{\partial B_{r}}\langle A\nabla u,\nabla u\rangle

\displaystyle=r^{-1}\int_{\partial B_{r}}\langle A\nabla u,\nabla u\rangle\langle\beta,\nu\rangle

\displaystyle=r^{-1}\int_{B_{r}}\divv(\langle A\nabla u,\nabla u\rangle\beta),

and so using the symmetry of
A
,

\displaystyle rD^{\prime}(r)

\displaystyle=\int_{B_{r}}\divv(\beta)\langle A\nabla u,\nabla u\rangle+\int_{B_{r}}\sum_{i,j,k}\beta^{k}(A_{ij})_{x_{k}}u_{x_{i}}u_{x_{j}}+2\int_{B_{r}}\sum_{i,j,k}\beta^{k}A_{ij}u_{x_{i}}u_{x_{j}x_{k}}

\displaystyle\coloneqq T_{1}+T_{2}+T_{3}.

By courtesy of (
2.9
) and the fact that
\mathrm{Trace}(I)=n
, we have

\displaystyle T_{1}=nD(r)+O(Mr+\delta)D(r),\,\,T_{2}=O(Mr)D(r).

As for
T_{3}
, we integrate by parts, use that
u
solves (
2.5
), and again apply (
2.7
), (
2.9
) to obtain

\displaystyle T_{3}

\displaystyle=-2\int_{B_{r}}\sum_{ijk}(\beta^{k})_{x_{j}}A_{ij}u_{x_{i}}u_{x_{k}}+2\int_{\partial B_{r}}\langle\beta,\nabla u\rangle\langle A\nabla u,\nu\rangle

\displaystyle=-2D(r)+O(Mr+\delta)D(r)+2r\int_{\partial B_{r}}\mu^{-1}\langle A\nabla u,\nu\rangle^{2}.

Finally, we use once again that
u
is a solution to rewrite

(2.11)

\displaystyle D(r)

\displaystyle=\int_{B_{r}}\divv(uA\nabla u)=\int_{\partial B_{r}}u\langle A\nabla u,\nu\rangle.

Combining all of the the previous equalities yields

(2.12)

\displaystyle D^{\prime}(r)/D(r)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{n-2}{r}+O(M+\delta/r)+2\dfrac{\int_{\partial B_{r}}\mu^{-1}\langle A\nabla u,\nu\rangle^{2}}{\int_{\partial B_{r}}u\langle A\nabla u,\nu\rangle}.

Moving on to
H^{\prime}(r)
, one easily computes with the estimate on
\mu
from (
2.8
) that

\displaystyle H^{\prime}(r)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{n-1}{r}H(r)+\int_{\partial B_{r}}\partial_{\nu}(u^{2}\mu)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{n-1}{r}H(r)+O(M+\delta/r)H(r)+2\int_{\partial B_{r}}u\partial_{\nu}u\mu.

Next we notice that
A(x)\nu(x)-\mu(x)\nu(x)\equiv r^{-1}\mu(x)(\beta(x)-x)
is a tangent vector field to
\partial B_{r}
which satisfies

\displaystyle\left\lvert\divv_{\partial B_{r}}(A\nu-\mu\nu)\right\rvert\lesssim M+\delta/r.

Indeed the estimate above again comes from the estimates (
2.8
), (
2.9
). Thus we can apply the divergence theorem on
\partial B_{r}
and use the symmetry of
A
to obtain

\displaystyle\left\lvert\int_{\partial B_{r}}2u\partial_{\nu}u\mu-2u\langle A\nabla u,\nu\rangle\right\rvert

\displaystyle=\left\lvert\int_{\partial B_{r}}\langle\nabla(u^{2}),\mu\nu-A\nu\rangle\right\rvert

\displaystyle=\left\lvert\int_{\partial B_{r}}u^{2}\divv_{\partial B_{r}}(\mu\nu-A\nu)\right\rvert=O(M+\delta/r)H(r).

Moreover, we can use this to rewrite

(2.13)

\displaystyle H^{\prime}(r)

\displaystyle=\left(\dfrac{n-1}{r}+O(M+\delta/r)\right)H(r)+2\int_{\partial B_{r}}u\langle A\nabla u,\nu\rangle.

With the estimates (
2.12
) and (
2.13
), we substitute into (
2.10
) to obtain

\displaystyle\dfrac{d}{dr}\left(\log N(r)\right)=2\left(\dfrac{\int_{\partial B_{r}}\mu^{-1}\langle A\nabla u,\nu\rangle^{2}}{\int_{\partial B_{r}}u\langle A\nabla u,\nu\rangle}-\dfrac{\int_{\partial B_{r}}u\langle A\nabla u,\nu\rangle}{\int_{\partial B_{r}}u^{2}\mu}\right)+O(M+\delta/r),

and conclude the proof of the Lemma by Cauchy-Schwarz.
∎

Remark 2.7
.

Although they are not the most important parts of Lemma
2.6
, we record for future use the estimates shown above that the vector field
\beta(x)
and conformal factor

satisfy

\displaystyle\left\lvert A\nu-\mu\nu\right\rvert\lesssim\delta,\,\,\left\lvert\divv_{\partial B_{r}}(A\nu-\mu\nu)\right\rvert\lesssim M+\delta/r

on

under the assumptions of Lemma
2.6
.

With our main tools collected, let us first provide a proof of the unique continuation principle for solutions
u
that solve (
2.5
) in
B_{1}
whenever
A
has an Osgood modulus of continuity

with some extra structure. To this end, we continue directly with the growth properties of the frequency function

defined in Definition

. Our goal is to show that

for all

by appealing to Corollary
2.4
. First, we collect some estimates regarding a non-trivial solution

and its distance to solutions with smooth coefficients.

In the remainder of this section, we shall frequently assume:

(1)

is a non-trivial solution of (
2.5
),

(2)

A
is a symmetric, uniformly elliptic matrix with constant
\lambda>0
, and
A(0)=I
,

(3)

has modulus of continuity

which satisfies the Osgood condition (

),

(4)

0<r<1
is fixed and

,

where

is a large constant depending just on the dimension

. As in Proposition

, we write
\phi(s)\coloneqq\omega(s)/s
and

to be a smooth bump function with

Lemma 2.8
.

Assume
1
-
4
, and suppose that
0<\varepsilon<r/2<1/4
. Let
\overline{A}\coloneqq A*\eta_{\varepsilon}
, and let
v
be the solution of

(2.14)

\displaystyle-\divv(\overline{A}\,\nabla v)

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\text{ in }B_{r},

\displaystyle v

\displaystyle=u

\displaystyle\text{ on }\partial B_{r}.

Then for some constant
C_{1}>0
depending just on
n
,
\lambda
, and the choice of
\eta
we have:

\displaystyle\fint_{B_{r}}\left\lvert\nabla(u-v)\right\rvert^{2}\,dx

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}\omega(\varepsilon)\fint_{B_{r}}\left\lvert\nabla u\right\rvert^{2}\,dx,

\displaystyle\fint_{\partial B_{tr}}(u-v)^{2}\,d\sigma

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}(1-t)\omega(\varepsilon)r^{2}\fint_{B_{r}}\left\lvert\nabla u\right\rvert^{2}\,d\sigma,\,\,tr\in(r(1-\varepsilon),r).

Proof.

Since
A
is uniformly elliptic with constant
\lambda>0
, then we readily see that
\overline{A}
is also uniformly elliptic and symmetric with the same constant. Applying Lemmas
4.1
and
4.3
, we immediately obtain the estimate

(2.15)

\displaystyle\fint_{B_{r}}\left\lvert\nabla(u-v)\right\rvert^{2}\,dx\lesssim\omega(\varepsilon)\fint_{B_{r}}\left\lvert\nabla u\right\rvert^{2}\,dx

with implicit constant depending just on the dimensions and
\lambda
. As for the second estimate, notice that if
x\in\partial B_{r}
, and
t\in(1-\varepsilon,1)
, then we can estimate by the fundamental theorem of calculus and the fact that

on

,

\displaystyle\left\lvert(u(tx)-v(tx))\right\rvert^{2}\leq\left(\int_{\gamma_{x,t}}\left\lvert\nabla(u-v)\right\rvert\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}\right)^{2}\lesssim\mathcal{H}^{1}(\gamma_{x,t})\int_{\gamma_{x,t}}\left\lvert\nabla(u-v)\right\rvert^{2}\,d\mathcal{H}^{1},

where
\gamma_{x,t}=[tx,x]
is the segment connecting
tx
and
x
. Integrating the above for
x\in\partial B_{r}
, recalling that
t\simeq 1
, and applying the coarea formula leaves us with

\displaystyle\int_{\partial B_{tr}}(u-v)^{2}\,d\sigma\lesssim(1-t)r\int_{B_{r}}\left\lvert\nabla(u-v)\right\rvert^{2}\,dx,

which, after combining with (
2.15
), gives the second desired estimate.
∎

Remark 2.9
.

Since it shall be used several times in the arguments that follow below, let us set aside the fact that this last argument with the co-area formula told us that if
u\equiv v
on
\partial B_{r}
, then for
t\in(0,1)
with

, we have the estimate

(2.16)

\displaystyle\fint_{\partial B_{tr}}(u-v)^{2}\,d\sigma\leq C(1-t)r^{2}\fint_{B_{r}}\left\lvert\nabla(u-v)^{2}\right\rvert\,dx.

With the previous estimate in hand, we can prove the following dichotomy: either the frequency function is bounded at scale
r
, or otherwise it does not grow too quickly from scale
r
to scale
r(1-1/N(r))
.

Lemma 2.10
.

Assume the hypotheses
1
-
4
and write
N_{u}^{A}(t)=N(t)
. Then the following alternative holds:

\displaystyle N(r)<N_{0},\text{ or }N(r(1-s/(N(r))\leq N(r)+C_{1}r\psi(N(r)/r),\,\,s\in[0,1],

where
C_{1},N_{0}>0
are constants depending only on the dimension and the ellipticity constant
\lambda
.

Proof.

Let
r\in(0,1)
be fixed, and set
N\coloneqq N_{u}^{A}(r)
. We need only consider the case
N>N_{0}
, where
N_{0}
is large and fixed. Since the hypothesis and conclusion of the Lemma are invariant under multiplication by scalars, we may normalize

so that

\displaystyle r^{2}\fint_{B_{r}}\langle A\nabla u,\nabla u\rangle\,dx=\dfrac{\sigma(\partial B_{1})}{\left\lvert B_{1}\right\rvert}N,\qquad\fint_{\partial B_{r}}\mu_{A}u^{2}\,d\sigma=1,

where
\mu_{A}(x)=\langle A(x)x/\left\lvert x\right\rvert,x/\left\lvert x\right\rvert\rangle
.

Define
\varepsilon=N^{-1}r
. As in Lemma
2.8
, we let
v
be a solution of (
2.14
), and denote by
N_{v}^{\overline{A}}
the frequency function corresponding to
v
. If we denote by
\mu_{\overline{A}}=\langle\overline{A}(x)x/\left\lvert x\right\rvert,x/\left\lvert x\right\rvert\rangle
, then Lemma
4.1
readily gives

\displaystyle\left\lvert A-\overline{A}\right\rvert+\left\lvert\mu_{A}(x)-\mu_{\overline{A}}(x)\right\rvert\lesssim\omega(\varepsilon)\ll 1,\,\,\left\lvert\nabla\overline{A}\right\rvert\lesssim\varepsilon^{-1}\omega(\varepsilon)=\phi(\varepsilon),

and thus from the normalization of

and Lemma
2.8
it is easy to verify that

(2.17)

\displaystyle N_{v}^{\overline{A}}(r)\leq N(1+C\omega(\varepsilon)).

We also know that
\left\lvert\overline{A}-I\right\rvert\lesssim\omega(2r)
, since
A(0)=I
, and thus Lemma
2.6
applied to the solution

tells us

\displaystyle\dfrac{d}{dt}\log\left(N_{V}^{\overline{A}}(t)\right)\geq-C(\phi(\varepsilon)+\omega(2r)/t)\geq-C\phi(\varepsilon),\,\,t\in(r-\varepsilon,r),

since
\phi(s)=\omega(s)/s
is a decreasing function. Integrating the above from
r_{s}=r(1-s/N)
to
r
and applying (

is large)

(2.18)

\displaystyle N_{v}^{\overline{A}}(r_{s})\leq e^{C\varepsilon\phi(\varepsilon)}N_{v}^{\overline{A}}(r)\leq N_{v}^{\overline{A}}(r)(1+C\omega(\varepsilon))\leq N(1+C\omega(\varepsilon)).

Finally, using the same estimates from Lemma
2.8
to obtain (
2.17
) gives us in a similar fashion

\displaystyle N_{u}^{A}(r_{s})

\displaystyle=\dfrac{(r_{s})^{2}\fint_{B_{r_{s}}}\langle A\nabla u,\nabla u\rangle}{\fint_{\partial B_{r_{s}}}\mu_{A}u^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\dfrac{(r_{s})^{2}\fint_{B_{r_{s}}}\langle\overline{A}\nabla v,\nabla v\rangle}{\fint_{\partial B_{r_{s}}}\mu_{\overline{A}}\,v^{2}}+C\omega(\varepsilon)N

\displaystyle=N_{V}^{\overline{A}}(r_{s})+CN\omega(\varepsilon)\leq N(1+C\omega(\varepsilon)).

Recalling that

∎

As a Corollary of Lemma
2.10
and Corollary
2.4
, we have the following. It is straightforward to see that a bound on

also readily gives a bound on the doubling properties of

; for more details see the proof of Theorem

.

Theorem 2.11
.

Assume the hypotheses
1
-
3
, and assume in addition that
\omega
is such that for
\psi(s)\equiv s\omega(1/s)
and
\phi(s)=\omega(s)/s
, we have the estimates

(2.19)

\displaystyle\psi(xy)

\displaystyle\leq C_{M}\psi(x)\psi(y),\,\,x,y,\geq 1,

(2.20)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\phi(s)\,ds

\displaystyle<\infty.

Then we have the uniform boundedness

\displaystyle\sup_{0<r\leq 1}N_{u}^{A}(r)

\displaystyle\leq N<\infty,

\displaystyle\fint_{\partial B_{r}}u^{2}\,d\sigma

\displaystyle\leq 2^{N}\fint_{\partial B_{r/2}}u^{2}\,d\sigma,\,\,0<r\leq 1,

where
N
is a constant depending just on the dimension
n
, ellipticity constant
\lambda
associated to
A
,
\omega
, and
N_{u}^{A}(1)
.

Proof.

Take
N_{0}\gg 1
large depending just on
\lambda
and
n
so that Lemma
2.10
is satisfied, and write
N(r)\equiv N_{u}^{A}(r)
. If we define the function

\displaystyle\tilde{N}(t)\coloneqq\sup_{r\in(t,1)}N(t),

then clearly
\tilde{N}
is decreasing, and if
\tilde{N}(t)\leq N_{0}
for all

, then the conclusion of the Theorem holds trivially. On the other hand, if
r_{0}\in(0,1]
is the largest value for which
\tilde{N}(r_{0})\leq N_{0}
, then it is easy to check that
\tilde{N}(t)
satisfies the hypothesis of Corollary

(since otherwise, we can decrease

without changing

, and then use that

is decreasing). For such values of

and the assumption (

\displaystyle N(t(1-s/N(t)))\leq N(t)+C_{1}t\psi(N(t)/t)\leq N(t)+C_{1}C_{M}t\psi(N(t))\phi(t),\,\,s\in[0,1],

and thus by definition of
\tilde{N}
and the fact that

,

\displaystyle\tilde{N}(t(1-1/\tilde{N}(t)))\leq\tilde{N}(t)+C_{1}C_{M}t\psi(\tilde{N}(t))\phi(t).

We may then apply Corollary

with

, since

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in(0,1)}\tilde{N}(t)=\sup_{t\in(0,1)}N(t)<\infty,

∎

3.
Isotropic equations

In this section, we apply similar arguments to study equations of the type (
1.2
), where now
A
is isotropic, i.e., solutions
u
of

(3.1)

\displaystyle-\divv(a\nabla u)

\displaystyle=0\text{ in }B_{1}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n},

for some scalar elliptic function
a:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow[\lambda,\lambda^{-1}]
. The structure of such equations allows us to obtain better results than in the anisotropic setting, and the key reason for this is the following monotonocity result for solutions for isotropic,
homogeneous
coefficients. The proof is exactly the same as in the case for harmonic functions, since
\partial_{r}\overline{a}=0
when
\overline{a}
is a
0

2.6
to deduce the following.

Lemma 3.1
.

Suppose that
v
is a solution of

\displaystyle-\divv(\overline{a}\nabla v)=0\text{ in }B_{1}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n},

where
\overline{a}(r,\theta)=\overline{a}(\theta)\simeq_{\lambda}1
is homogeneous of degree
0
. Then the frequency function
N_{v}^{\overline{a}}(r)
satisfies

\displaystyle\dfrac{d}{dr}\left(\log(H_{v}^{\overline{a}}(r)/r^{n-1})\right)=\dfrac{2N_{v}^{\overline{a}}(r)}{r},\;\;\;\dfrac{d}{dr}N_{v}^{\overline{a}}(r)\geq 0.

We prove Theorem
1.2
in two steps. The first step is to show that for any nontrivial solution
u
,
N_{u}^{a}(r)
has arbitrarily small polynomial growth for small values of
r
, i.e.,
N_{u}^{A}(r)\leq r^{-\delta}
for all
0<r<r_{0}\ll 1
(see Corollary
3.11
). This step is the bulk of our work, and requires us to introduce a modified frequency function,
\tilde{N}
(see Definition
3.2
). This frequency appears to be better suited to passing doubling properties of solutions of “homogeneous equations” to the original solution
u
. Once we have this slow growth of
N_{u}^{a}(r)
, we can use the Hölder regularity of
a
once again to follow the scheme of the proof of Theorem
2.11
and deduce that
N_{u}^{a}(r)
is bounded for all sufficiently small
r
. This step is much easier, and does not require the isotropic nature of the operator
\mathcal{L}=-\divv(a\nabla\,\cdot\,)
.

Let us begin in earnest to prove the slow growth of
N_{u}^{a}(r)
for equations of the type (
3.1
). Since we shall assume them frequently, let us state some underlying assumptions on the solution
u
to refer back to later:

(1)

u
is a non-trivial solution of (
3.1
) in
B_{1}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

(2)

a:B_{r}\rightarrow[1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon]
is
\alpha
-Hölder continuous with constant
C_{h}\leq 1
,
\alpha\in(2/3,1)
, and
\varepsilon\in(0,1/10)
sufficiently small

(3)

0<r<1
is fixed and
1\ll N_{0}\leq N_{u}^{a}(r)<\infty
,

(4)

N_{u}^{a}(t)>N_{0}
,
r/2<t\leq r
,

Our goal is to show that
N_{u}^{a}(r)
is finite implies that
N_{u}^{a}(r/2)
is finite, with some rather weak quantitative control. In doing so, we can often assume

(5)

For some fixed constant
A>1
and

, we have that

\displaystyle\int_{B_{r(1-A\log(N)/N)}}\left\lvert\nabla u\right\rvert^{2}dx\leq N^{-2p-1}\int_{B_{r}}\left\lvert\nabla u\right\rvert^{2}\,dx,

where

.

The first step in approaching our goal is to introduce the right notion of the frequency function in this context, more amenable to coefficients
a
which are not Lipschitz.

Definition 3.2

Suppose that

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}C_{h}N^{\tau-\beta\tau}\ll C_{2}N^{\tau-\beta\tau},

where in the second to last inequality we again use that
M\simeq N^{1-\tau+\eta}
. Combining this estimate with (
3.49
) and recalling the definitions of the
N_{k,i}
, we see

\displaystyle\tilde{N}_{u}^{a_{k+1}}(r_{j_{k}},r_{j^{*}})

\displaystyle\leq(1+C_{1}\varepsilon)N+C_{1}C_{2}(N_{k,1}+N_{k,2})

\displaystyle\qquad+MC_{2}N^{1-2\beta\tau+\eta}+C_{1}C_{h}N^{\tau-\beta\tau}

\displaystyle<(1+C_{1}\varepsilon)N+C_{1}C_{2}N_{k+1,1}.

Using the upper bound above with the equality (
3.48
) and
\overline{a}=a_{k+1}
, we see that there must exist some
1\leq p\leq m
for which

(3.50)

\displaystyle\tilde{N}^{a_{k+1}}_{u}(r_{j_{k}+p},r_{j_{k}+p-1})\leq\tilde{N}^{a_{k+1}}_{u}(r_{j_{k}},r_{j^{*}})<(1+C_{1}\varepsilon)N+C_{1}C_{2}N_{k+1,1}.

From (
3.47
) we similarly see that

(3.51)

\displaystyle N^{a}_{u}(r_{j_{k}+p})<(1+C_{3}\varepsilon)((1+C_{1}\varepsilon)N+C_{1}C_{2}N_{k+1,1}).

Then the estimates (
3.50
),(
3.51
) tell us that
j_{k+1}=j_{k}+p
is a valid choice of the next chosen integer (with the corresponding choice of

), thus completing our induction claim, and the proof of the Theorem. ∎

We just need one last Lemma before proving the main Theorem, the analogue of Lemma

Lemma 3.12
.

Let
u\not\equiv 0
solve

\displaystyle-\divv(a\nabla u)=0\text{ in }B_{1}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n},

where

is
\alpha
-Hölder continuous with constant

. Then there exists
0<r_{0}<1
small and

depending on the dimension

,

and

(3.52)

\displaystyle N_{u}^{a}(r(1-s/N))\leq N_{u}^{a}(r)+C_{1}C_{h}r^{\alpha/2},\,\,s\in[0,1],

where

is a dimensional constant. Moreover, in this latter case we also have

\displaystyle N_{u}^{a}(r(1-1/N))\leq(r(1-1/N))^{-\alpha/2}.

Proof.

We fix
0<r<r_{0}
with

sufficiently small to be determined. We also assume that

The proof is essentially the same as the proof of Lemma
2.10
. Renormalize

so that with

\displaystyle r^{2}\fint_{B_{r}}a\left\lvert\nabla u\right\rvert^{2}\,dx\equiv\dfrac{\sigma(\partial B_{1})}{\left\lvert B_{1}\right\rvert}N,\qquad\fint_{\partial B_{r}}u^{2}\,d\sigma\equiv 1.

Applying Lemma
3.6
we deduce that if

, and

is the solution of (

), then for each

,

(3.53)

\displaystyle N_{v}^{\overline{a}}(r(1-s/N))\leq N_{v}^{\overline{a}}(r)

\displaystyle\leq(1+CC_{h}r^{\alpha})N,

(3.54)

\displaystyle r^{2}\fint_{B_{r}}\left\lvert\nabla(u-v)\right\rvert^{2}\,dx\leq CC_{h}r^{\alpha}N

\displaystyle\leq CC_{h}r^{\alpha/2}\ll 1,

(3.55)

\displaystyle\fint_{\partial B_{r(1-s/N)}}(u-v)^{2}\,d\sigma

\displaystyle\leq sCC_{h}r^{\alpha}\ll 1,

provided that

). Using the estimates (

), (
3.55
), and the fact that

is Hölder continuous, one readily checks that

\displaystyle N_{u}^{a}(r(1-s/N))

\displaystyle\leq N_{u}^{\overline{a}}(r(1-s/N))+N\left\lVert a-a(0)\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(B_{r})}

\displaystyle\leq N+CC_{h}r^{\alpha/2},

where

. The last estimate now is a simple computation as long as we choose

With the small polynomial estimate from Corollary

and the alternative from Lemma

Theorem 3.13
.

Let
u\not\equiv 0
solve

\displaystyle-\divv(a\nabla u)=0\text{ in }B_{1}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n},

where
a:B_{1}\rightarrow[\lambda,\lambda^{-1}]
is
\alpha
-Hölder continuous with constant
C_{h}
for
\alpha>2/3
. Then there exists constants
C,N_{0}>1
depending just on the dimension
n
,
\lambda
,
C_{h}
, and
\alpha
so that if
0<s<C^{-1}
, then for
N\equiv N_{u}^{a}(s)
, we have the estimates

(3.56)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\sup_{0<t<s/\max\{N_{0},N^{C}\}}N_{u}^{a}(t)&\leq\max\{N_{0},N^{C}\},\\
\log\left(\dfrac{\fint_{\partial B_{t}}u^{2}\,d\sigma}{\fint_{\partial B_{t/2}}u^{2}\,d\sigma}\right)&\leq\max\{N_{0},N^{C}\},\,\,0<t\leq s/\max\{N_{0},N^{C}\}.\end{split}

Proof.

First we infer from Corollary
3.11
that
u
cannot vanish on any open subset of
B_{1}
, and so
N_{u}^{a}(r)<\infty
for each
0<r<1
. Notice that after multiplying
a
by a constant if necessary, we may choose an initial radius
0<r_{0}<1
depending on
\lambda
,
C_{h}
, and
\alpha
so that

\displaystyle\left\lvert a(x)-1\right\rvert\leq\alpha/C_{1},\,\,x\in B_{r_{0}}

where
C_{1}
is a large constant. By choosing
r_{0}
smaller if necessary, we may assume that Lemma
3.12
holds in
B_{r}
for
0<r<r_{0}
, and that
N_{0}\leq r_{0}^{-\alpha/2}
, where
N_{0}
is large and to be determined. Our goal is to argue iteratively, similar to Theorem
2.11
, though unfortunately the details are slightly messier in this setting and do not fit so neatly into the framework of Corollary
2.4
. As such, we more or less argue the proof of that Corollary applied to this context.

First, Corollary
3.11
tells us that as long as
N_{0}
is chosen large enough,

(3.57)

\displaystyle N_{u}^{a}(\rho_{k})\leq\rho_{k}^{-\alpha/2},\,\,k\geq C_{1}\log(\max\{N_{u}^{a}(r_{0}),N_{0}\})/\alpha,

for some sequence of
\rho_{k}\in[\delta^{k+1}r_{0},\delta^{k}r_{0}]
and some
\delta\in(0,1)
depending on just the dimension,
C_{h}
, and
\alpha
.
Fix the first such index
k\in\mathbb{N}
where (
3.57
) holds, and set
\rho^{\prime}\equiv\rho_{k}
. If
N_{u}^{a}(\rho^{\prime})<N_{0}
, then take
\rho=\sup\{s\in[0,\rho^{\prime}]:N_{u}^{a}(s)\geq N_{0}\}
(if the supremum does not exist because the set is empty, we are done) and otherwise set
\rho=\rho^{\prime}
. Finally, we set
\tilde{N}(t)\coloneqq\sup_{s\in[t,\rho]}N_{u}^{a}(s)
, and notice that

\displaystyle N_{0}\leq\tilde{N}(\rho)\equiv N_{u}^{a}(\rho)\leq\rho^{-\alpha/2},

in any case, whether by (
3.57
) if
\rho=\rho_{k}
, or otherwise since
N_{0}\leq r_{0}^{-\alpha/2}\leq\rho^{-\alpha/2}
.

Set
N\equiv\tilde{N}(\rho)
,
t_{0}=\rho
, and
t_{j+1}=t_{j}(1-1/\tilde{N}_{u}^{a}(t_{j}))
. At this stage, we begin our iteration with
\tilde{N}(t)
, and claim that for each integer
1\leq j\leq\lceil N\rceil
we have

(3.58)

\begin{split}\tilde{N}(t_{j})&\leq N+jCC_{h}\rho^{\alpha/2},\\
\tilde{N}(t_{j})&\leq t_{j}^{-\alpha/2}.\end{split}

Indeed the estimates above hold for
j=1
by Lemma
3.12
and the choice of the initial radius
\rho=t_{0}
. Then inductively, if
j\geq 1
is such that (
3.58
) holds, then we let
s=\sup\{0<t\leq t_{j}\,:\,N_{u}^{a}(t)=\tilde{N}(t)\}
and apply Lemma
3.12
again at the scale
s
in place of
t_{j}
to see that (
3.58
) holds for
j+1
as well. After iterating
j=\lceil N\rceil
times we remark that
N\leq\rho^{-\alpha/2}
gives us that

\displaystyle\log(t_{\lceil N\rceil}/\rho)\leq\sum_{\ell=1}^{\lceil N\rceil}\log\left(1-\dfrac{1}{N+\ell CC_{h}\rho^{\alpha/2}}\right)\leq\dfrac{-\lceil N\rceil}{N(2+CC_{h})}\leq\dfrac{-1}{4+4CC_{h}},

so that in particular

(3.59)

\begin{split}\tilde{N}(\rho(1-\tau(\rho)))&\leq\tilde{N}(\rho)(1+CC_{h}\rho^{\alpha/2}),\\
\tilde{N}(\rho(1-\tau(\rho)))&\leq(\rho(1-\tau(\rho)))^{-\alpha/2},\end{split}

for some
\tau(\rho)\in(\tau_{0},1)
with
0<\tau_{0}<1
depending just on the dimension,
C_{h}
, and
\alpha
.

Finally we argue inductively, just as in our steps to prove (
3.59
), to show that for each
j\geq 1
, there exists a corresponding
\tau_{j}\in(\tau_{0},1)
so that with
s_{j}=\rho\left(\prod_{\ell=1}^{j}(1-\tau_{j})\right)
we have

(3.60)

\displaystyle\tilde{N}\left(s_{j}\right)\leq\tilde{N}(\rho)\prod_{\ell=1}^{j}\left(1+CC_{h}s_{j}^{\alpha/2}\right)\leq\tilde{N}(\rho)\prod_{\ell=1}^{\infty}(1+CC_{h}s_{j}^{\alpha/2}).

However, notice that
s_{j}\leq\rho(1-\tau_{0})^{j}
, and so we readily see that

\displaystyle\prod_{\ell=1}^{\infty}(1+CC_{h}s_{j}^{\alpha/2})\leq M<\infty

for some constant
M
depending just on
C_{h}
,
\alpha
, and
\tau_{0}
. Taking
j\rightarrow\infty
in (
3.60
) and recalling the definition of
\tilde{N}
gives us that

\displaystyle\sup_{0<t<\rho}N_{u}^{a}(t)\leq M\tilde{N}(\rho).

This concludes the proof of the first claim in the Theorem once we recall that
\rho_{k}
was chosen as the first such index for which (
3.57
) holds, and so

\displaystyle\tilde{N}(\rho)=N_{u}^{a}(\rho)=N_{u}^{a}(\rho_{k})\leq N_{u}^{a}(r_{0})^{C}

for some large
C>1
in the case that
N_{u}^{a}(r)\geq N_{0}
.

To see that the first claim implies the second, we appeal to Lemma
3.7
. Setting
N^{*}=\max\{N_{0},N_{u}^{a}(r_{0})^{C}\}
and
\overline{a}\in H_{0}(a,\rho,0)
, we see that for any
0<r<\rho
,

\displaystyle\fint_{\partial B_{r}}\overline{a}u^{2}\,d\sigma\leq C\fint_{\partial B_{r(1-s/N^{*})}}u^{2},d\sigma,\,\,s\in[0,1].

Applying the above inequality on the order of
N^{*}
times, we deduce that

\displaystyle\fint_{\partial B_{r}}\overline{a}u^{2}\,d\sigma\leq C^{N^{*}}\fint_{\partial B_{r/2}}\overline{a}u^{2}\,d\sigma.

∎

It is possible to extend Theorem
3.13
to solutions

of

\displaystyle-\divv(a\nabla u)+Vu=0\text{ in }B_{1}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}

for

, with

. In fact, one can prove unique continuation for

with

in Theorem

, so instead we provide the shorter proof just for

. The author thanks Max Engelstein for showing him the proof that follows.

First, let

be such a non-trivial solution of (

) such that

. Since

, we know that the Serrin maximum principle (
[

, Theorem 4]
) applies (as does Hölder continuity of solutions, so that

is well-defined). In particular, there exists some radius
r_{0}
small enough depending just on
n
and
\left\lVert V\right\rVert_{L^{p}}
so that if
v
is the solution of

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} -\divv(a\,\nabla v)&=0&&\text{ in }B_{2r_{0}},\\
v&=2&&\text{ on }\partial B_{2r_{0}},\end{aligned}

then we have that

\displaystyle\sup_{B_{2r_{0}}}v\leq 2+C\left\lVert V\right\rVert_{L^{p}}\leq C_{1},

where
C_{1}
depends just on
\left\lVert V\right\rVert_{L^{p}}
. Moreover, since
(2-v)
solves

, and

on

\displaystyle\sup_{B_{2r_{0}}}(2-v)\leq Cr_{0}\left\lVert V\right\rVert_{L^{p}},

since

. Choosing
r_{0}
small enough depending on
\left\lVert V\right\rVert_{L^{p}}
, we see that

(3.62)

\displaystyle 1\leq v\leq C_{1}\text{ in }B_{2r_{0}}.

Since
V\in L^{p}(B_{1})
with
p>n
, and since
a
is Hölder continuous, standard Schauder estimates
[
FRRO22
, Theorem 2.28]
tell us that there is some small
\beta\in(0,1)
depending on

so that
v\in C^{1,\beta}(B_{r_{0}})
, and for which we have

(3.63)

\displaystyle\left\lVert\nabla v\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(B_{r_{0}})}\leq CC_{1}

Setting
w\coloneqq u/v
, one checks that
w
satisfies the isotropic elliptic equation

(3.64)

\displaystyle-\divv(av^{2}\nabla w)=0\text{ in }B_{r_{0}}.

Moreover, by (

) this equation is uniformly elliptic, and the coefficients

are Hölder continuous of order

since

is bounded and Lipschitz in

. In particular, we may apply Theorem

to see that

(and thus

. Moreover, we estimate using (
3.62
) and (

) to see that for any
0<r<r_{0}
,

\displaystyle\fint_{B_{r}}av^{2}\left\lvert\nabla w\right\rvert^{2}\,dx

\displaystyle\leq CC_{1}\left(\fint_{B_{r}}\left\lvert\nabla u^{2}\right\rvert+u^{2}\left\lvert\nabla v\right\rvert^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq CC_{1}\fint_{B_{r}}\left\lvert\nabla u\right\rvert^{2},

by the Poincaré inequality and clearly,

\displaystyle\fint_{\partial B_{r}}av^{2}w^{2}\,d\sigma=\fint_{\partial B_{r}}au^{2}\,d\sigma.

Combining these two estimates show that
N_{w}^{av^{2}}(r)\simeq_{C_{1}}N_{u}^{a}(r)
for

, so we still get the same dependence on the frequency function as in the following Theorem:

Theorem 3.14

Let
u\not\equiv 0

Here we list some elementary estimates for solutions of uniformly elliptic equations. The first is a basic approximate-identity computation which we omit.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
f:B_{r}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
have modulus of continuity
\omega
, and let
\eta\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
be a nonnegative function satisfying
\spt\eta\subset B_{1}
, and
\int\eta\,dx=1
. Writing
f_{\varepsilon}=f*\eta_{\varepsilon}
for
\varepsilon\in(0,r)
, we have the estimates

\displaystyle\left\lvert f_{\varepsilon}(x)-f(x)\right\rvert\leq\omega(\varepsilon),\,\,\left\lvert\nabla f_{\varepsilon}(x)\right\rvert\leq C\varepsilon^{-1}\omega(\varepsilon),\,\,x\in B_{r-\varepsilon},

where
C
depends just on the dimension
n
and
\int\left\lvert\nabla\eta\right\rvert\,dx
.

Next, we have a simple quantitative stability for solutions of elliptic equations, which for completeness we prove. First, we remind the reader of the notation

\displaystyle\left\lvert\nabla_{A}u\right\rvert^{2}\coloneqq A\nabla u\cdot\nabla u,

whenever
A
is uniformly elliptic and symmetric.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
A_{0},A_{1}
be two uniformly elliptic, symmetric matrices (both with ellipticity constant
\lambda_{0}\in(0,1)
) in
B_{1}
. Then if
u_{0}=u_{1}
on
\partial B_{1}
and
-\divv(A_{i}\nabla u_{i})=0
in
B_{1}
for
i=0,1
, then we have the energy estimate

(4.1)

\displaystyle\int_{B_{1}}\left\lvert\nabla(u_{0}-u_{1})\right\rvert^{2}\leq\lambda_{0}^{-1}\left(\int_{B_{1}}\left\lvert\nabla_{A_{0}}u_{1}\right\rvert^{2}-\left\lvert\nabla_{A_{0}}u_{0}\right\rvert^{2}\right).

Proof.

Using that
u_{0}
is a solution, we know that

\displaystyle\int_{B_{1}}A_{0}\nabla u_{0}\cdot\nabla(u_{0}-u_{1})=0,

so that
\int_{B_{1}}\left\lvert\nabla_{A_{0}}u_{0}\right\rvert^{2}=\int_{B_{1}}A_{0}\nabla u_{0}\cdot\nabla u_{1}
. Similarly,
\int_{B_{1}}\left\lvert\nabla_{A_{1}}u_{1}\right\rvert^{2}=\int_{B_{1}}A_{1}\nabla u_{0}\cdot\nabla u_{1}
, and thus expanding
\left\lvert\nabla_{A_{0}}(u_{0}-u_{1})\right\rvert^{2}
, we see that

\displaystyle\int_{B_{1}}\left\lvert\nabla_{A_{0}}(u_{0}-u_{1})^{2}\right\rvert

\displaystyle=\int_{B_{1}}\left\lvert\nabla_{A_{0}}u_{0}\right\rvert^{2}-2A_{0}\nabla u_{0}\cdot\nabla u_{1}+\left\lvert\nabla_{A_{0}}u_{1}\right\rvert^{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{B_{1}}\left\lvert\nabla_{A_{0}}u_{1}\right\rvert^{2}-\left\lvert\nabla_{A_{0}}u_{0}\right\rvert^{2}.

Using the ellipticity of
A_{0}
completes the proof of the Lemma.
∎

As a corollary, we have the simple estimate regarding solutions of equations whose coefficients are close (where the majority of the mass of the solution lives).

Corollary 4.3
.

Let
A_{0},A_{1}
be two uniformly elliptic, symmetric matrices (both with ellipticity constant
\lambda_{0}\in(0,1)
). Let
u_{0}=u_{1}
on
\partial B_{1}
and
-\divv(A_{i}\nabla u_{i})=0
in
B_{1}
for
i=0,1
, and suppose that for some
r\in(0,1)
, we have that

(1)

\left\lvert A_{0}-A_{1}\right\rvert\leq\varepsilon
in
B_{1}\setminus B_{r}
,

(2)

\int_{B_{r}}\left\lvert\nabla u_{i}\right\rvert^{2}\leq\delta\int_{B_{1}}\left\lvert\nabla u_{i}\right\rvert^{2}
for
i=0,1
,

for
\varepsilon,\delta\in(0,1)
sufficiently small. Then

(4.2)

\displaystyle\int_{B_{1}}\left\lvert\nabla(u_{0}-u_{1})\right\rvert^{2}\leq C_{1}(\varepsilon+\delta)\min\left\{\int_{B_{1}}\left\lvert\nabla u_{0}\right\rvert^{2},\int_{B_{1}}\left\lvert\nabla u_{1}\right\rvert^{2}\right\}.

with constant
C_{1}>0
depending on the dimension
n
and
\lambda
.

Proof.

Since
u_{0}=u_{1}
on
\partial B_{1}
, it is straight-forward to see via energy minimization and ellipticity of
A_{0},A_{1}
that

\displaystyle\int_{B_{1}}\left\lvert\nabla_{A_{0}}u_{0}\right\rvert^{2}\leq\int_{B_{1}}\left\lvert\nabla_{A_{0}}u_{1}\right\rvert^{2}\leq\lambda_{0}^{-2}\int_{B_{1}}\left\lvert\nabla_{A_{1}}u_{1}\right\rvert^{2},

\displaystyle\int_{B_{1}}\left\lvert\nabla_{A_{1}}u_{1}\right\rvert^{2}\leq\int_{B_{1}}\left\lvert\nabla_{A_{1}}u_{0}\right\rvert^{2}\leq\lambda_{0}^{-2}\int_{B_{1}}\left\lvert\nabla_{A_{0}}u_{0}\right\rvert^{2},

so that
\int_{B_{1}}\left\lvert\nabla u_{0}\right\rvert^{2}\simeq_{\lambda_{0}}\int_{B_{1}}\left\lvert\nabla u_{1}\right\rvert^{2}
. Next, we estimate using
1
,
2
and energy minimization

\displaystyle\int_{B_{1}}\left\lvert\nabla_{A_{0}}u_{1}\right\rvert^{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{B_{1}\setminus B_{r}}\left\lvert\nabla_{A_{0}}u_{1}\right\rvert^{2}+\int_{B_{r}}\left\lvert\nabla_{A_{0}}u_{1}\right\rvert^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{B_{1}\setminus B_{r}}\left\lvert\nabla_{A_{1}}u_{1}\right\rvert^{2}+(\lambda_{0}^{-1}+1)\int_{B_{r}}\left\lvert\nabla u_{1}\right\rvert^{2}+\varepsilon\int_{B_{1}}\left\lvert\nabla u_{1}\right\rvert^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{B_{1}}\left\lvert\nabla_{A_{1}}u_{0}\right\rvert^{2}+(\delta(\lambda_{0}^{-1}+1)+\varepsilon)\int_{B_{1}}\left\lvert\nabla u_{1}\right\rvert^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{B_{1}}\left\lvert\nabla_{A_{0}}u_{0}\right\rvert^{2}+(\delta(\lambda_{0}^{-1}+1)+\varepsilon)\int_{B_{1}}\left\lvert\nabla u_{0}\right\rvert^{2}+\left\lvert\nabla u_{1}\right\rvert^{2}.

Recalling that
\int_{B_{1}}\left\lvert\nabla u_{0}\right\rvert\simeq_{\lambda_{0}}\int_{B_{1}}\left\lvert\nabla u_{1}\right\rvert^{2}
, we conclude the proof by invoking Lemma
4.2
.
∎

We also have a similar version of Corollary
4.3
which only requires that one of the two solutions have the majority of its mass in a thin annulus, but we obtain a slight loss in the approximation.

Corollary 4.4
.

Let
a_{0},a_{1}:B_{1}\rightarrow[1/2,2]
and suppose that
u_{0}=u_{1}
on
\partial B_{1}
and
-\divv(a_{i}\nabla u_{i})=0
in
B_{1}
for
i=0,1
. If for some
r\in(0,1)
,
\varepsilon,\delta\in(0,1)
, we have

(1)

\left\lvert a_{0}-a_{1}\right\rvert\leq\varepsilon
in
B_{1}\setminus B_{r}
,

(2)

\int_{B_{r}}\left\lvert\nabla u_{0}\right\rvert^{2}\leq\delta\int_{B_{1}}\left\lvert\nabla u_{0}\right\rvert^{2}
,

then we have the estimate

(4.3)

\displaystyle\int_{B_{1}}\left\lvert\nabla(u_{0}-u_{1})\right\rvert^{2}\leq C_{1}(\varepsilon+\delta^{1/2})\int_{B_{1}}\left\lvert\nabla u_{0}\right\rvert^{2}

with constant
C_{1}>0
independent of
N
, as long as
\delta
is small enough.

Proof.

Write
N\equiv\int_{B_{1}}a_{0}\left\lvert\nabla u_{0}\right\rvert^{2}
, and notice that as in the proof of Lemma
4.2
,
\int_{B_{1}}a_{1}\left\lvert\nabla u_{1}\right\rvert^{2}\simeq N
, and
\int_{B_{1}}a_{0}\nabla u_{0}\cdot\nabla(u_{0}-u_{1})=0
since
u
is a solution. Hence we may estimate

\displaystyle N=\int_{B_{1}}a_{0}\left\lvert\nabla u_{0}\right\rvert^{2}

\displaystyle\equiv\int_{B_{1}}a_{0}\nabla u_{0}\cdot\nabla u_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\int_{B_{1}\setminus B_{r}}a_{0}\left\lvert\nabla u_{0}\right\rvert^{2}\right)^{1/2}\left(\int_{B_{1}\setminus B_{r}}a_{0}\left\lvert\nabla u_{1}\right\rvert^{2}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\left(\int_{B_{r}}a_{0}\left\lvert\nabla u_{0}\right\rvert^{2}\right)^{1/2}\left(\int_{B_{r}}a_{0}\left\lvert\nabla u_{1}\right\rvert^{2}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq N^{1/2}\left(\int_{B_{1}\setminus B_{r}}a_{0}\left\lvert\nabla u_{1}\right\rvert^{2}\right)^{1/2}+C\delta^{1/2}N.

As long as
\delta
is sufficiently small, we rearrange to find

\displaystyle\int_{B_{1}\setminus B_{r}}a_{0}\left\lvert\nabla u_{1}\right\rvert^{2}\geq N(1-C\delta^{1/2})^{2}\geq N(1-C\delta^{1/2})

so that finally with
1
, we deduce

(4.4)

\displaystyle\int_{B_{1}}a_{1}\left\lvert\nabla u_{1}\right\rvert^{2}\geq\int_{B_{1}\setminus B_{r}}a_{0}\left\lvert\nabla u_{1}\right\rvert^{2}-\varepsilon\int_{B_{1}}\left\lvert\nabla u_{1}\right\rvert^{2}\geq N-CN(\varepsilon+\delta^{1/2}).

On the other hand, energy minimization of
u_{1}
gives

(4.5)

\begin{split}\int_{B_{1}}a_{1}\left\lvert\nabla u_{1}\right\rvert^{2}&\leq\int_{B_{1}}a_{1}\left\lvert\nabla u_{0}\right\rvert^{2}\\
&\leq\int_{B_{1}\setminus B_{r}}a_{0}\left\lvert\nabla u_{0}\right\rvert^{2}+\int_{B_{1}\setminus B_{r}}\left\lvert a_{0}-a_{1}\right\rvert\left\lvert\nabla u_{0}\right\rvert^{2}+\int_{B_{r}}a_{1}\left\lvert\nabla u_{0}\right\rvert^{2}\\
&\leq N+CN(\varepsilon+\delta),\end{split}

so combining (
4.4
) and (
4.5
) gives us

\displaystyle\left\lvert N-\int_{B_{1}}a_{1}\left\lvert\nabla u_{1}\right\rvert^{2}\right\rvert\leq CN(\varepsilon+\delta^{1/2}).

Using the above with (
4.5
) again then tells us

(4.6)

\displaystyle\left\lvert\int_{B_{1}}a_{1}\left\lvert\nabla u_{1}\right\rvert^{2}-\int_{B_{1}}a_{1}\left\lvert\nabla u_{0}\right\rvert^{2}\right\rvert\leq CN(\varepsilon+\delta^{1/2}),

so we apply Lemma
4.2
to conclude. ∎
