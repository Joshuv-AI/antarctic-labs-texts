Attention guided convolutional neural network with explainable AI for papaya leaf disease detection in edge and drone agricultural systems
Authors: Raiyan Gani, Maherun Nessa Isty, Mohammad Rifat Ahmmad Rashid, Jubaer Ahmed, Tasmia Islam, Mahamudul Hasan, Raihan Ul Islam, Shamim H. Ripon, Ahmed Wasif Reza
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Existing disease discovery in papaya leaves is most significant in achieving yield and profitability stability in the tropics but has proven difficult in the presence of deficiencies in manual exploration and tailored crop models in crop-AI systems. Therefore, this study introduces PapayaNet, a lightweight attention-guided convolutional network specifically structured for the automated classification of six papaya leaf states, including major diseases and healthy leaves. For real-world deployment in scarce-resource farming contexts, PapayaNet adopts batch norm and hierarchical attention steps in five convolution stages and accelerates both computational celerity and discriminability. Trained on 6618 manually annotated orchard images sourced from orchards in Bangladesh at a very high resolution, it has a 98.79% classification accuracy, all of which was realized using 483,926 parameters and an average infer time of 0.01 s, which is significantly better when evaluated using EfficientNetB6, DenseNet121, and VGG16. XAI methods, including Grad-CAM and LIME, showed model decisions towards the biologically informative parts of the leaf, thus boosting interpretability and user confidence. Systematic ablation analysis also confirmed the importance of distributed attention in ensuring robust generalization towards visually similar disease classes. An in-browser diagnostic portal deployed using Gradio provides intra-browser predictive deployment and interpretability overlay in real time, thus inviting field practicability. Given its low-latency inference and minimal computational footprint, PapayaNet is well-suited for integration into edge devices and drone platforms, offering a scalable solution for real-time in-situ crop health monitoring. This study advances the field of precision agriculture by delivering a crop-specialized, explainable, and deployable AI system for sustainable management of papaya diseases.

Introduction

Ensuring food security at the international level is a vital and complex undertaking, particularly in the agricultural sector, where the sustainable production of fruit crops is essential for economic stability and nutritional sufficiency. Among the various challenges in fruit production, foliar diseases are a significant constraint that adversely affect plant physiology by interfering with photosynthetic functions, inhibiting nutrient transport, and eventually reducing the quantity and quality of fruit1. Therefore, plant leaf health plays a vital role in upholding crop productivity and maintaining the integrity of food systems.

Traditionally used methodologies in agriculture for leaf disease recognition rely significantly on human observation or rudimentary methods through image processing. However, they have universally acknowledged drawbacks: they require huge amounts of time and human power and are prone to human errors; therefore, they cannot be used on a large scale or in real time1. In the past decade, advancements in artificial intelligence (AI), particularly through the emergence of deep learning, have revolutionized the potential of plant disease recognition at very large accelerations and with precision2. In addition, transfer learning models and Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) have been leading this sector first and foremost. For example, models utilizing CNNs have been observed to have highly successful recognition in mango leaf disease diagnosis3, and through deep learning, methodologies have been observed to be highly successful in quick apple leaf disease recognition4. In addition, the interweaving of AI and Internet of Things (IoT) paradigms has enabled real-time environmental observation and tele-disease diagnosis, thus empowering decision-making processes through precision agriculture4–6.

Notwithstanding this work, the void in disease recognition in papaya leaves (Carica papaya) has yet to be significantly exploited. Papaya is a tropical crop with commercial and nutritional value that is extensively produced throughout Asia, America, and Africa. Its vulnerability to some diseases in its leaves is one of the major obstacles in continued crop production, often triggering diminished harvest, loss of fruit quality, and enormous financial losses. This continues to be a major issue, particularly in communities where papaya is one of the major sources of food and economy, and the reason for the need for automated and scalable systems in diagnosis, specifically designed for deployment in this crop.

The methods for disease detection in papaya are severely limited. Conventional practices perform poorly in early detection and are ineffective and late in achieving speed and accuracy in successful and timely interventions. Second, there is a sheer absence, if not non-emergence, of sophisticated, specialized deep learning systems specifically coded for the recognition and detection of papaya leaf diseases. Generalized models inevitably perform poorly when applied to papaya leaves because of the distinct morphological characteristics and disease modes of expression of the crops. Third, the lack of scalable systems for disease monitoring in image warts preventive management interventions avoids losses in crop quality and production.

This study was designed to overcome these limitations by building a deep learning model specifically used to classify and detect diseases in papaya leaves. PapayaNet exploits transfer learning techniques to find the best representation of features and better classification precision, and provides real-world solutions for pragmatic crop diagnosis in agriculture. It was trained and tested using a meticulously crafted dataset of papaya leaf images with a very high resolution and accurately benchmarked using traditional CNN architectural frameworks to test its performance. The primary goal of this study was to develop a pragmatic automated system that provides early disease detection, timely agronomic intervention, and increased productivity in papaya plantations. Related works

The application of deep learning to plant leaf disease detection has emerged as a transformative approach in agricultural diagnostics, offering significant advantages over conventional methods in terms of accuracy, scalability, and automation. Recent advances in convolutional neural network (CNN) architectures, optimization strategies, and feature representation techniques have substantially improved the disease classification performance across a wide range of crops. Early investigations into CNN-based disease detection established the viability of fundamental architectures. Rajitha et al.7 demonstrated the effectiveness of models such as VGGNet, ResNet, and InceptionNet in classifying bacterial and fungal infections, consistently reporting accuracies exceeding 90%. Building on this, Al-Akkam et al.8 developed a refined CNN model for tomato, pepper, and potato diseases, achieving an initial accuracy of 98.09%, which was later enhanced to 98.34% through architectural improvement. Pandian et al.9 proposed a 14-layer deep CNN trained using multi-GPU configurations, attaining a peak accuracy of 99.97%. These foundational studies have laid the groundwork for more sophisticated crop-specific solutions. Parallel to deep learning, researchers have explored hybrid approaches that integrate traditional machine learning with feature engineering. Sudakar et al.10 applied Random Forest regression using multi-descriptor features such as texture, color, and shape for papaya leaf disease classification, achieving 95.93% accuracy. Although such methods offer interpretability and reduced computational overhead, their scalability and robustness under real-world conditions remain limited compared with end-to-end deep learning models. Recent efforts have focused on enhancing the CNN architecture through architectural innovation and performance optimization.

Xiao et al.11 proposed SE-VRNet, which can be summarized as a lightweight integrated CNN with in-field leaf image-trained and squeeze-and-exciting modules. Achieving 99.98% accuracy, they optimized and mobilized it for a real-time featurestep deployment. Similarly, Andrew et al.12 cited DenseNet-121 to obtain very good classification accuracy (99.81%) in certain test instances, and Albahli et al.13 proposed the CornerNet DenseNet-77 model for tomato leaf deformity recognition, achieving 99.98% accuracy. EfficientNet gained popularity in a similar manner through scale-optimized capacities. Babu et al.14 employed EfficientNetB3, fortified through batch normalization and regularization, to obtain 99% classification accuracy. Vengaiah et al.15 and Arathi et al.16 employed DenseNet-121 pre-trained using ImageNet for tomato and cotton disease recognition, respectively, and obtained 98.3% and 91% accuracy, respectively. It can be discerned from this literature that the potential of deep feature hierarchies and transfer learning expands model generalizability. Novtahaning et al.17 surveyed applying a deep learning ensemble in coffee leaf disease detection and applied transfer learning using pre-trained CNNs to improve categorization and obtained 97.31% validation accuracy. Ahmad et al.18 proposed an automated identification of plant diseases using SVM, providing an outstanding accuracy of 98.79%. Anuj Yadav et al.19 proposed AFD-Net, developed with EfficientNet B3 and B4, including the lambda layer, gaining the highest accuracy of 98.7% for apple leaf disease classification. Upadhyay and Gupta20 provided a comprehensive survey of AI-driven agricultural disease detection by reviewing various machine-learning and deep-learning methods. They noted that deep learning consistently achieved higher accuracy (over 90%) on primarily controlled datasets, but highlighted limitations such as reliance on curated data, lack of uniform evaluation, and limited real-world robustness, emphasizing the ongoing need for research into generalizable and robust solutions for diverse field conditions.

The development of crop-specific models has gained momentum in response to the limitations of general-purpose architecture. Mahum et al.21 utilized an enhanced DenseNet-201 with an additional transition layer to classify potato leaf diseases, achieving a 97.2% accuracy on PlantVillage data. Similarly, Nazir et al.22 introduced EfficientPNet, a hybrid model combining EfficientNet-V2 with spatial channel attention, which achieved a 98.12% accuracy on potato datasets. Wagh et al.23 applied a CNN-AlexNet architecture for grape leaf disease detection and reported 98.23% accuracy in the binary classification of bacterial spots and powdery mildew. Another study24 applied a personalized DenseNet-CNN architecture for potato leaf disease detection and achieved 96.0% accuracy for healthy leaves, early blight, and late blight. EfficientNet has emerged as a prominent architecture for plant disease recognition, owing to its efficacy in image classification. The authors of25 proposed a new approach utilizing EfficientNet for plant disease recognition. S¸ahin et al.26 proposed an automated leaf classification system using global and local features, achieving 99.11% accuracy with Inception-V3 on the Flavia dataset. Pandian et al.27 utilized the ResNet197 model for leaf disease detection, achieving 99 average classification accuracy of 99.58%, although acknowledging its higher computational density compared to models such as MobileNet and VGG19Net. Jung et al.28 presented a CNN algorithm integrating images of healthy and diseased plant pairs with five pre-trained models, achieving 97.09% accuracy in both crop type and disease classification, with VGG19 achieving a perfect 100.00% for potato diseases. Khalid et al.29 proposed a deep learning process for real-time plant disease identification using CNN and YOLOv5 models, achieving 93% accuracy. Narmadha et al.30 proposed a DenseNet169-MLP model for rice plant disease identification, obtaining a maximum accuracy of 97.68 Other studies have emphasized computational efficiency and generalizability across environmental conditions. Hossain et al.31 developed a modified MobileNet trained on a custom dataset featuring complex natural backgrounds, achieving 99.55% accuracy. Chen et al.32 introduced an EfficientNet model adapted for cassava disease detection using low-bandwidth IoT image sensors, achieving 89% accuracy while maintaining computational constraints. The importance of data diversity and robustness was also addressed through augmentation and metalearning. Upadhyay and Gupta33 employed data augmentation techniques to mitigate dataset imbalance in potato disease detection, attaining 99.2% accuracy with an enhanced VGG-19 model. In a subsequent study34, they adopted a meta-learning strategy to estimate mango crop maturity under limited labeled data, further emphasizing the growing need for adaptive learning in agricultural AI.

Hybrid and ensemble models have also been explored to enhance accuracy and resilience. Srinidhi et al.35 reported 99.8% accuracy using pre-trained DenseNet121 and EfficientNetB7 for apple disease detection. Kotwal et al.36 introduced a hybrid AD-ENet model that combined deep learning with traditional techniques, achieving 99.91% accuracy. Other advanced models, including ViT, DeIT, and Bi-CNN37,38, have demonstrated strong performance but often at the cost of computational complexity or limited interpretability. Fungal disease detection and cross-crop generalizability have recently received attention. Upadhyay and Gupta39,40 achieved ¿98.9% accuracy for fungal disease classification in apples, guavas, and custard apples using modified ResNeXt models and fuzzy segmentation, although they noted constraints in transferring performance across different crops or environmental settings. These findings reflect the broad challenge of developing universally robust models for heterogeneous field conditions. Daniya et al.41 suggested an Rider Water Wave-based Neural Network (RWW-NN) for rice plant disease detection, integrating Rider Optimization Algorithm (ROA) and Water Wave Optimization (WWO), which demonstrated an impressive accuracy of 90.8%. Despite the breadth of existing research, several limitations persist, including overreliance on lab-controlled datasets, inadequate real-time capability, limited crop-specific specialization, and challenges in deploying lightweight scalable models in resource-constrained environments. Moreover, few studies have addressed the diseases of papaya, a crop of considerable nutritional and economic importance.

The research introduces an attention-augmented CNN to process Alzheimer’s disease and justify forecasts through various XAI methodologies42 by learning from publicly released structural MRI datasets (Alzheimer’s 4-class and ADNI axial scans). With transfer learning, its 26-layer CNN attained 98.24% and 99.70% accuracy for detecting and subclassifying dementia, respectively. The major limitation is single-modality MRI dependency along with relatively modest architecture hindering generalizability. On the other hand, the author43 focuses on enhancing intra-aural disease classification from otoscopic images by introducing an attention-endowed deep learning system comprising a Vision Transformer (ViT) for feature learning and machine learning classifiers for final classification. With a dataset consisting of 880 eardrum images in four classes (CSOM, earwax, myringosclerosis, normal) divided into training (70%), verification (10%), and test (20%) sets, the approach attained 99.00% classification accuracy when coupled with a k-nearest neighbor classifier. One limitation is that disease variety is constrained and images from one otoscope are employed, which might hinder generalizability across wider clinical contexts. The authors of paper44 presents an explainable attention-augmented segmentation system with a U-Net backbone and state-of-the-art encoders (ResNet, DenseNet, EfficientNet) and attention layers (CBAM, non-local) for sketching out breast tumor regions. The model was trained and tested on the BUSI (Breast Ultrasound Image) dataset with segmentation masks and ground-class labels. Attention-augmented models achieved improved metrics (e.g. high Dice, IoU, precision/recall) and enhanced interpretability by Grad-CAM than non-attention baseline models. A key limitation is that it relies on single-modality ultrasound images and has no cross-site verification and thus may not generalize well to diverse clinical settings.

Latest developments in edge computing have been instrumental in facilitating intelligent and environmentally friendly Internet of Things (IoT) infrastructures. Zhang and Feng systematically survey edge-assisted IoT systems with emphasis on latency-aware analytics45, resource orchestration, and energy-aware data processing from smart cities to e-health and intelligent transport. Their exposition reveals crucial open issues such as scaling with explosive device proliferation, secure processing, and sustainable energy usage along with emphasizing shortage of empirical proof and deployment studies in natural environments as one limitation. The paper46 presents a system based on deep learning to detect early symptoms of Grapevine Red Blotch (GRBD) and Leafroll Disease (GLD) from foliar images and runs it on edge devices for in-venue deployment. It uses a dataset of 3198 grape leaf images (with molecular diagnoses for 800 specimens) and compares CNN models (YOLOv3, YOLOv5, YOLOv8, ResNet-50) on edge platforms (e.g. Jetson Nano). The method finds YOLOv5 optimal (95.36% Precision, Recall: 95.77%, F1-score: 95.56%) running on Jetson Nano with tolerable latency for inference but is limited by single-modality visual symptoms (sc. it cannot detect asymptomatic infections) and environmental variability in image appearance.

Maußner et al. propose explainable AI models for improving trust in water demand forecasts through use of the Battle of Water Demand Forecasting dataset with daily use, calendar, and weather information47. They compare transparent models and black-box models (RF, LSTM) with SHAP/LIME for explainability and find increased accuracy from obscure models but increased robustness from transparent models. A limitation is that they employ one regional dataset and one day-ahead prediction scale, limiting generalizability to other scales or broader settings. Nikiforidis et al. survey explainable AI in smart manufacturing in Industry 4.0/5.0 settings by mapping major XAI methodologies (local, global, interactive) to industrial use cases and emphasizing visualization methods to enhance understanding48. They survey XAI deployment in fault prediction, predictive maintenance, quality inspection, and human–AI interaction in plants with special focus on how interpretability is crucial in establishing trust. They mention how the majority of work remains benchmarked in highly controlled labs with narrowly defined use cases and without common evaluation frameworks or real-world deployment proof.

Data description

The papaya leaf disease dataset49 has been carefully collected from various papaya orchards in Bangladesh while maintaining quality standards. The images consist of a dataset captured in a way that can be used for deep learning research. The dataset is created in a standard format, displaying a comprehensive collection of diseased and healthy papaya leaf samples for comparison. The images are precisely categorized, ensuring easy navigation and access to subfolders of various diseases. This organization increases the usability of the dataset for in-depth research and analysis. This dataset is based on detection and analysis of six categories like ring spot, leaf curl, mosaic, mealybug, red mite and healthy leaves. The dataset contains 1400 papaya leaf images captured using smartphone cameras from four different papaya orchards in Bangladesh. Consequently, augmented datasets that use data augmentation methods to expand the dataset show a significant increase in the number of images per class, thus increasing the diversity and power of the dataset for deep learning purposes.

Papaya ringspot disease, a common affliction in the leaves of papaya plants and this disease of reduced productivity is easily recognized by the distinct irregular pattern appearing on the young leaves. The leaf pattern characterized by ring spots often manifests as yellow with translucent areas, signaling a leaf disorder, and is distributed across the leaf surface. Next, papaya leaf curl disease, caused by virus propagation, causes significant biochemical and cellular changes in the host plant, thereby severely affecting fruit production. Infection results in leaves showing distinctive curling patterns, bending downwards and inwards, with pronounced thickening. These structural changes do not affect plant appearance but disrupt flower and fruit development.

Consequently, Papaya mosaic disease is characterized by yellow discoloration of papaya leaves that affects plant growth and health. Affected leaves depart from their normal structure, altering the plant’s appearance, and young leaves are initially affected, showing slight deformities and dark green, blister-like spots that indicate the presence of the disease. Furthermore, Papaya Mealybug infestation, insects secrete honeydew on papaya plants, which is a clear symptom of cotton bollworm disease on the underside of leaves. Mealybugs stunt plant growth and increase pest pressure, further weakening the plant which inhibits plant growth and health. Infestations caused by Paracoccus marginatus appear as cottony clusters on leaves. Therefore, leaf mite infestations on papaya plants typically characterized by fine silk webbing located under the leaves and significantly damaging the health of both stems and leaves are a definite indication of a mite-related problem. Deep learning models architecture

DenseNet121 architecture

Convolutional Neural Network based architecture DenseNet121 uses dense connectivity in between layers to encourage feature re-use, decrease the number of parameters, and alleviate the vanishing gradient problem at training. It follows an architecture comprising 121 layers, each one being fed by all previous ones. This connectivity pattern ensures that each layer is directly fed with the feature maps of every previous layer, which results in lesser feature reuse and also decreases the problem of vanishing gradients. A convolutional layer and several dense blocks follow in the model’s hierarchy. Afterwards, a completely linked layer at the conclusion, and a global average pooling layer. Between every dense block, transition layers with convolution and pooling are used to minimize the feature maps’ dimensions to maintain the computational effectiveness. By this way, DenseNet121 achieves significantly improved performance on tasks like image classification using its small-sized structure while being highly connected, requiring lesser parameters and computational resources than a traditional deep neural network of the same depth. Because of this capability to model fine-grained details in visual data with computational efficiency, the design has been greatly borrowed into applications. Very recent improvements in DenseNet, including hardware optimization and optimization of training techniques50, result in further gains in its performance on real-world tasks.

MobileNetV2 architecture

MobileNetV2 is designed to improve computational efficiency in various vision applications on mobile and embedded systems. It extends the depth-wise separable convolution introduced in MobileNetV1. However, it also introduces a key innovation due to the utilizing linear bottlenecks and inverted residuals. It has two kinds of blocks: a narrow bottleneck, involving depth wise separable convolutions, and a larger expansion phase, which allows the complexity of features to be better modeled. The inverted residual structure-around the shortcut connections bypassing the expanded layers-keeps the computation light while maintaining the capability for rich feature extraction. This design helps reduce a considerable amount of parameters and computing costs, which makes it highly appropriate for resource-constrained environments without performance loss. ReLU6 is also used as the activation function in the hidden layers of MobileNetV2 to avoid saturating the network during training and, hence, enhancing its generalization capability across diverse tasks. It is due to the excellent balance between performance and computation efficiency that this architecture has found wide adoption on a variety of assignments, from image classification and object detection to semantic segmentation51, which are highly desirable for real-time applications at edge devices.

EfficientNet-B6 architecture

EfficientNet-B6 is a sophisticated convolutional neural network architecture found in the EfficientNet line, characterized by its compound scaling technique. The technique evenly scales the depth, width, and resolution based on specific scaling coefficients52. Through the use of the mobile inverted bottleneck convolution (MBConv) structure alongside squeeze-and-excitation optimization, EfficientNet-B6 greatly enhances the capability to extract features whilst maintaining computational efficiency. The baseline network for the architecture is the EfficientNet-B0, created by Neural Architecture Search (NAS), scaled to B6 using the compound coefficient technique afterwards. The network has around 43 million parameters, finding the perfect balance between efficiency and accuracy, hence enabling it to be used in high-resolution classification tasks of images, such as the classification of diseases in medical imaging datasets or agricultural datasets. Additionally, EfficientNet-B6 achieves the latest standard in accuracy from the different tests whilst requiring less resource than other networks sharing the same accuracy standards.

VGG16 architecture

VGG16 is one of the advanced deep convolutional neural network architectures developed by the Visual Geometry Group at the University of Oxford. The architecture is composed of 16 weight layers, consisting of 13 convolutional layers in sequence with three fully connected layers, using tiny 3 × 3 convolutional filters along with 2 × 2 maxpooling layers. The application of the same filter size contributes to the simplification of the architecture by enabling deep extraction of features53. VGG16 has been optimized to handle input images of 224 × 224 × 3 and is known for its simplicity and effectiveness in image classification activities. Regardless of its 138 million-odd parameters, making it computationally intensive, VGG16 achieves outstanding performance on large datasets like ImageNet. Its high depth and regular layer structure make it a powerful standard for transfer learning and for classification of medical images or agricultural images.

PapayaNet architecture

The proposed PapayaNet is a deep convolutional neural network (CNN) architecture tailored for the automatic classification of papaya leaf diseases. The design integrates classical convolutional layers with a lightweight attention mechanism and batch normalization, thereby optimizing both the performance and interpretability. As illustrated in Fig. 3, PapayaNet comprises five hierarchical convolutional stages, each paired with an attention module, followed by a global feature aggregator and classification head. The attention mechanism adaptively highlights disease-relevant regions, thereby enhancing the discriminative power of the model, while maintaining computational efficiency.Fig. 3PapayaNet architecture.

Input and convolutional feature hierarchy

The input to PapayaNet is an RGB image x ∈ R3 × H × W, where H and W represent height and width, respectively. The network processes this input through five sequential convolutional blocks. Each block consists of a 2D convolution layer with a 3 × 3 kernel, followed by batch normalization (BN), rectified linear unit (ReLU) activation, and a max pooling layer with a stride of 2. These blocks progressively abstract the spatial features from low-to high-level disease-specific patterns. Attention-guided feature modulation

Each convolutional block is followed by a lightweight spatial attention module to enhance the focus of the network on the salient disease regions. This module computes an attention map using 1 × 1 convolution, followed by batch normalization and sigmoid activation to constrain the output between 0 and 1. The resulting attention map is multiplied element-wise with the input feature map to modulate the activation intensity in a data-dependent manner. Explainable AI (XAI) technique

LIME is one of the explainable AI (XAI) techniques to enhance interpretability for complex models in machine learning. Slightly simplified, LIME approximates a black-box model around the neighborhood with an interpretable surrogate model, usually linear. LIME aims to derive explanations by perturbing the input data slightly and observing the consequences of these changes on the model predictions in order to understand the decision-making process of a model for an individual prediction. As this is a model-agnostic technique-meaning any machine learning model can use it in explaining predictions on tasks that include classification and regression. The LIME objective function is defined as (Eq. 4),4\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\xi \left( x \right) = \arg \mathop{\min}\limits_{g \in G} L\left( {f,g,\pi_{x} } \right) +\Omega (g)$$\end{document}where ξ(x) is the interpretable model approximation around the instance x, f is the original, complex model, g is an interpretable model (e.g., a linear model), G is the class of possible interpretable models, L(f, g, πx) is a loss function that measures the difference between f and g in the local neighborhood defined by πx, πx is a proximity measure that weights the locality around x, Ω(g) is a complexity term to ensure that g remains simple for interpretability.

On the other hand, Grad-CAM (Gradient-weighted Class Activation Mapping) is a visualization technique employed to enhance interpretability in Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs). This method functions by identifying and highlighting regions within an input image that are most salient for a model’s prediction. Grad-CAM accomplishes this by computing the gradient of the target class score with respect to the feature maps of a specific convolutional layer. Through the weighting of these gradients, Grad-CAM generates a class-discriminative heatmap that is superimposed on the input image, thereby illustrating the spatial regions that contribute most significantly to the model’s decision. This visualization technique is particularly valuable in applications where comprehending the model’s focus can facilitate the validation of its reliability or elucidate potential biases in its decision-making process. Discussion

The findings of the present study highlight the profound benefits of tailoring deep learning architectures to the niche area of papaya leaf disease classification, which has hitherto garnered limited attention in the field of agricultural artificial intelligence. The proposed PapayaNet model, which can act as an attention convolutional neural network (CNN), exhibited a significantly enhanced classification accuracy of 98.79% in comparison to conventional CNN benchmarks such as MobileNetV2 (89.44%), DenseNet121 (89.59%), VGG16 (97.13%), and highly optimized EfficientNetB6 (97.73%). This enhancement occurs through a synergistic combination of architectural advancements consisting of layer-specific attention protocols, group normalization standards, and dropout regularization, each validated in an ablation study.

The results confirmed that deep learning models specifically designed for particular crops and well-fine-tuned models achieved significantly improved diagnostic accuracy. The persistent convergence patterns in the training and validation phases clearly indicate that PapayaNet possesses excellent generalization capabilities, whereby over-fitting is successfully avoided, even with a relatively small parameter size (483,926) and a low inference time (0.01 s). This balance of computational cost and precision makes the model ready for real-time deployment in edge-deployed systems such as integration with monitoring systems using low-power drones.

Notably, explainable AI (XAI) analyses using Grad-CAM and LIME have illustrated how PapayaNet targets visually and biologically relevant characteristics, such as curling of leaves, ring patterns, and necrotic lesions, rather than irrelevant background noise or image artifacts. This interpretability is crucial for practical implementation in real-world settings because the extent of trust placed on AI-made decisions can significantly determine the uptake rates among agricultural practitioners. The model’s performance per class further supports its trustworthiness; for example, PapayaNet achieved 99.05% accuracy in detecting Mosaic disease—a class where MobileNetV2 and DenseNet121 struggled, with only 77.31% and 50% accuracy, respectively. This disparity could be because PapayaNet is more adept at identifying subtle chromatic and textural patterns through the attention layers, whereas the baseline models are prone to overfitting the more salient patterns in easier-to-classify classes.

This study had several limitations that are worth discussing. First, although PapayaNet worked extremely well on a carefully curated dataset, real-world variations in lighting differences, occlusions, and leaf orientations could add more complications. Even in hypothetical scenarios involving drone-captured images at multiple angles, further validation across orchards under different geographical and climatic conditions is necessary to confirm the generalizability of the model. Second, the current implementation does not directly address model calibration issues when exposed to domain shifts or an unseen disease variety. Incorporating semi-supervised or few-shot learning approaches can increase the robustness of future developments.

Practically speaking, PapayaNet’s lightweight structure, coupled with its inference time of 0.01 s, makes it extremely well-positioned for integration with mobile and embedded systems common to precision agriculture. Although model development and testing are highlighted in the present study, one emergent direction for future research involves integrating PapayaNet into drone-based aerial monitoring systems to enable real-time identification of papaya leaf disease. Such an application requires consideration of real-world limitations such as energy usage, operational viability, and limitations in the bandwidth available for delivering high-resolution images. However, PapayaNet’s small parameter size (483,926) and fast inference make it highly beneficial for onboard computation on platforms such as NVIDIA Jetson or Raspberry Pi, which are commonly used in unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs).

The use of aerial imagery enables widespread and automatic surveillance of crops, providing timely and context-aware diagnostic information to farmers and agronomists. In addition, integrating explainable AI results, such as Grad-CAM heatmaps, into PapayaNet enables better validation and interpretability by indicating key regions linked to plant diseases in images acquired using drones. To achieve this goal, future work should simulate scenarios using aerial imaging, evaluate inference quality by accounting for embedded hardware restrictions, and build optimized deployment systems designed expressly for UAV applications. In doing so, these efforts ensure compliance with worldwide trends toward intelligent agriculture, and place PapayaNet on a technically viable and scalable footing for autonomous plant health surveillance.

Conclusion

This study presents PapayaNet, a lean convolutional neural network with attention mechanisms tailored for disease detection in papaya leaves. This topic embodies a significant component of tropical agriculture that has been underexplored in the current artificial intelligence literature. In the past, owing to the limitations of standard diagnostic methods and the shortcomings of generic convolutional neural networks, PapayaNet leveraged crop-specific features and prioritized architectural simplicity. Consequently, it attained state-of-the-art performance with a remarkable accuracy rate of 98.79%, outperforming previous standards on all critical measures.

Through extensive experimental evaluations involving comparisons with several models, ablation studies, and explainability evaluations, PapayaNet demonstrated significant benefits regarding both interpretability and generalization performance, highlighting its applicability in agricultural situations. The low number of parameters and very low inference latency (0.01 s) make it a prime candidate for edge deployment in scenarios involving limited power availability in devices on which deep models must be run. Although the current research was conducted in a controlled setup, the design and functionality of the model show high promise for adaptability in disease monitoring at the field level. Therefore, a promising direction for future research involves integrating PapayaNet with drone-based aerial surveillance systems to enable real-time and autonomous assessments of crop health in large papaya orchards. This integration, coupled with device explainability, can significantly improve early stage disease detection, intervention effectiveness, and sustainable agricultural practices. In summary, this study adds to the scientific framework of precision agriculture by introducing an applied deep learning system with crop specificity that successfully bridges laboratory precision with practical implementation. Future research on cross-domain generalization, emerging hardware innovations, and optimal deployment system development will determine how these outcomes can be applied to impactful agronomic practices.
