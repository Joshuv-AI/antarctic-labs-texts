In silico analysis of L- and G-type lectin receptor kinases in tomato: evolution, diversity, and abiotic responses
Authors: Makarim Elfadil M. Osman, Rieham Sallah H. Osman, Sara A. A. Elmubarak, Mohanad A. Ibrahim, Hana Badreldin Mohamed Abakar, Amina I. Dirar, Emadeldin Hassan E. Konozy
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Solanum lycopersicum (family: Solanaceae) is a crucial crop and model organism for many phenotypic traits, and its sequenced genome provides valuable insights into plant biology and crop improvement. This study investigated lectin receptor-like kinases (LecRLKs) in tomato, focusing on L-type and G-type families. Mining the tomato genome (ITAG2.4) revealed 161 putative lectin genes across seven families, with GNA-related genes being the most abundant. Gene duplication analysis indicated that tandem and segmental duplications were the primary mechanisms driving LecRLK gene family expansion, particularly for G-type LecRLKs. These duplicated genes showed evidence of both purifying and negative selection, suggesting functional conservation and sub-functionalization. L-type and G-type LecRLKs exhibited diverse domain rearrangement architectures and subcellular localizations, with G-type LecRLKs showing greater expansion and architectural diversity. Differential expression analysis during abiotic stress (drought, heat, and cold stress) revealed key responsive genes. During drought stress, 63.2% of L-type and 18.5% of G-type LecRLK genes were expressed, with L-type Solyc09g005000.1 and G-type Solyc03g078360.1 genes showing significant 2-fold upregulation. Heat stress (42 °C) induced the upregulation of L-type Solyc04g071000.1 and G-type Solyc03g078360.1 and Solyc04g008400.1, particularly after 12–24 h of exposure. Promoter analysis revealed numerous stress-related cis-elements. Transcription factor predictions and miRNA targeting sites suggest complex regulatory mechanisms. This comprehensive in silico characterization of tomato LecRLKs, including their expansion patterns and evolutionary pressures, provides insights into their potential roles in abiotic stress responses and lays the groundwork for enhancing crop resilience through targeted breeding or genetic engineering approaches.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-024-11014-6.

Introduction

Lectins, a diverse group of carbohydrate-binding proteins, have been isolated from various sources, including plants, animals, and microbes. They possess at least one noncatalytic domain that facilitates their reversible binding to specific glycan structures [1]. Their importance in plant biology extends to enhancing plant resilience against both abiotic and biotic stresses [2]. Studies have shown that lectins aid plants in coping with environmental challenges such as drought, salinity, extreme temperatures, pests, diseases, and microbial infections. By binding to specific carbohydrate molecules, lectins can trigger defense mechanisms within plants, thereby contributing to their overall health and survival under adverse conditions [3]. One class of plant lectins, which are found in seeds and storage tissues, act as storage proteins and herbivore defenses and are synthesized with a signal peptide for the secretory pathway. Another type is expressed at low concentrations under stress and interacts with glycans in the nucleus and cytoplasm to trigger signaling pathways [4]. Plant lectins are typically classified into twelve families on the basis of their carbohydrate recognition domain (CRD). Examples of these families include ABA, amaranthins, CRA, cyanovirin, EUL, GNA, hevein, JRL, legume lectins, LysM domain lectins, Nictaba-like lectins, and ricin B lectins. Each family possesses a unique amino acid sequence, allowing for its distinction. In addition to their essential role in carbohydrate recognition, CRDs serve crucial functions in lectins, including facilitating defense mechanisms, cell signaling, and interactions between cells [5]. Plant lectins often contain one or more additional protein domains that may be unrelated to their primary function [6]. These additional domains can bestow various functional properties upon lectins. The anchoring of these lectins in the cell membrane is vital for their function, as it allows them to interact effectively with external signals and transmit information into the cell, thereby participating in various biological processes. One notable domain found in some of these lectins is the kinase motif, which facilitates enzymatic activity, transferring phosphate groups to specific substrates. The presence of kinase motifs implies that these lectins may also play roles in phosphorylation processes, further increasing their functional versatility [7]. Among the 12 plant lectin families, many members of four types are equipped with kinase motifs (LecRLKs) and are known to participate in stress regulation: legume (L-type), GNA (G-type), LysM, and CRA (C-type) [4].

Tomato (Solanum lycopersicum), which belongs to the Solanaceae family, is a vital crop that is valued for its nutritional benefits, including fiber, antioxidant compounds, and high levels of vitamins A and C [8]. Economically, it supports a significant agricultural industry, driving revenue through fresh market sales, processing for sauces and canned goods, and the export trade [9]. The cultivated tomato Solanum lycopersicum has an extensive range of sub-cultivars, with thousands of varieties grown worldwide [10]. These cultivars are typically classified according to various attributes, such as morphology, color intensity, nutritional quality, firmness, flavor, and aroma [11]. Tomato cultivation faces significant challenges because of the multitude of stresses affecting the plant, ranging from abiotic to biotic factors. Abiotic stresses, such as drought, extreme temperatures, and unfavorable soil conditions, pose considerable obstacles. On the other hand, biotic stresses, including pests, diseases, and weed competition, also exert substantial pressure on tomato plants, leading to diminished yield and quality at harvest [12, 13]. In recent decades, tomato breeding has emerged as a vital technique for developing crop varieties that are resilient to both abiotic stresses, ensuring stable production, reducing input dependency, increasing food security, and promoting sustainable agricultural practices [14].

Given the economic importance of tomato crops, studying tomato lectins with kinase motifs is crucial. This study seeks to explore the evolutionary history, gene duplication mechanisms, and domain architecture of the L- and G-type LecRLKs, as well as their potential roles in response to abiotic stresses like drought and heat. By leveraging bioinformatics tools, we aim to identify key regulatory elements and expression profiles that could inform future breeding strategies for improved stress tolerance in tomato.

Methodology

Identification of L- and G-type putative lectin homologs

The Solanum lycopersicum genome assembly (ITAG2.4, ID390) [15] found in the public repository Phytozome v13 (https://phytozome-next.jgi.doe.gov/) [16] was searched for the presence of two lectin-like receptor kinase (LecLRK) families (L-type (Legume, PF00139 – UniProt/SwissProt: P05046.1) and G-type (GNA, PF01453 – UniProt/SwissProt: P30617.1)) by using the incorporated BLAST tool (E value < 0.0001). The LecLRK sequence, which shares the highest identity with the model sequences, was used to perform a second BLAST, and the identified sequences, chromosomal location, gene starting and ending points in the chromosome, 1500 bp upstream promoter region, CDS, and genomic sequences were retrieved via the Phytozome-BioMart tool. Each sequence was then checked individually using the InterProScan 5 (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/interpro/) [17], and each sequence containing at least one lectin domain was retained along with the information related to the lectin domains and other fused domains’ start and end points and their identified Pfam IDs.

Characterization of LecLRK putative sequences

Each LecLRK putative sequence was characterized by using a set of web tools. The presence of the signal peptide, transmembrane domains, and N- and O-glycosylation was checked via SignalP 5.0 (https://services.healthtech.dtu.dk/services/SignalP-5.0/) [18], TMHMM 2.0 (https://services.healthtech.dtu.dk/services/TMHMM-2.0/) [19], NetNGlyc 1.0 (https://services.healthtech.dtu.dk/services/NetNGlyc-1.0/) [20], and NetOGlyc 4.0 (https://services.healthtech.dtu.dk/services/NetOGlyc-4.0/) [21], respectively. The molecular weight (Mw) and isoelectric point (pI) for each sequence were calculated using pI/Mw computation tool of ExPASy (https://web.expasy.org/compute_pi/) [22]. Furthermore, possible subcellular localization and cellular compartment targeting were predicted via WoLF PSORT (https://wolfpsort.hgc.jp/) [23] and the OutCyte 1.0 server (http://www.outcyte.com/) [24], respectively.

Expansion, evolutionary relationships, and gene collinearity

Expansion and gene duplication

G- and L-type lectin gene duplication and expansion events were identified based on their protein sequences and the GFF file (Linkage group, gene ID, and start and end positions of the gene). To identify potential homolog pairs, BLASTp was performed (E value < 1e− 10; the first 5 matches were chosen). The tabular BLASTp file obtained along with the GFF file was then analyzed via the MCScanX package (MCScanX wrapper with a built-in tool (duplicate_gene_classifier) found in TBtools-II). Wide genome duplication (WGD)/segmental and tandem duplicated pairs were directly identified from the tool’s output data; however, further analysis was performed to determine the pair for genes duplicated by proximal or dispersed (transposon) events. The duplicate pair for each gene identified as proximal was obtained from the BLASTp gene homolog pool and located on the same chromosome within a short distance from its pair genes. The dispersed pairs were selected based on their identity percentage, and their respective locations on a different chromosome or within the same chromosome (located at least > 1 million base pairs).

Estimation of the substitution rate and the divergence time

To calculate the synonymous substitution (Ks) for each GNA- and legume-related gene and their duplicates. Fasta format files of the protein and CDS sequences along with a list of the linked pairs were uploaded to the TBtools-II [25] Ka/Ks calculator and only values with Ka/Ks ratios < 1 were retained (only 1 pair was excluded). The type of selection which is defined based on the Ka/Ks ratio was defined as follows: (Ka/Ks < 1 purifying negative selection, Ka/Ks > 1 diversification positive selection, and Ka/Ks = 1 neutral selection). To estimate the divergence time (T) for each gene pair, the synonymous mutation rate of substitution (Ks) per the number of synonymous substitutions rate per site per year (x) was calculated for the tomato genome via the following equation (T = Ks/2x; where x = 6.56 × 10− 9), 2 is the factor that accounts for both gene copies accumulating substitutions independently after they diverge from a common ancestor [26].

Synteny analysis

To analyze the synteny relationship between S. lycopersicum (ITAG2.4) L- and G-type lectins and Arabidopsis thaliana (TAIR10), genomic data files (GFF3, FASTA format protein sequence, and genomic sequence) were retrieved from the Phytozome-JGI Data Portal. Synteny analysis and visualization were performed using one-step MCScanX and dual synteny for MCScanX-associated TBtools toolkits to merge files from both genomes and obtain new files (chromosome linkage groups, GFF3, and collinearity) and then provide a visualization plot.

Phylogenetic tree construction

To analyze the phylogenetic relationships between LecLRK homologs, trimmed lectin domains as well as the entire lectin sequence were aligned separately using T-coffee, followed by phylogenetic tree construction using JTT matrix-based substitution model and the maximum likelihood method, with the final bootstrap consensus tree inferred from 1000 replicates via the IQ-TREE web server (http://iqtree.cibiv.univie.ac.at/) [27]. The following genes were used as orthologs: A. thaliana (TAIR10) L-type genes (LecRK-VI.1 (AT3G08870.1), LecRK-VI.2 (AT3G3810.1), LecRK-VI.3 (AT5G01550.1), LecRK-VI.4 (AT5G01560.1), LecRK-VIII.1 (AT3G380.1), LecRK-VIII.2 (AT5G03140.1), Glycine max lectin (UniProt: P05046), and Galanthus nivalis agglutinin (UniProt: P30617).

Gene structure, cis-elements, transcription factor, and miRNA analyses

Gene structures were analyzed via the Gene Structure Display Server (GSDS 2.0) (https://gsds.gao-lab.org/index.php) [28] using the genomic and CDSs fasta format files retrieved for each LecLRK. The 1500 bp upstream promoter sequence for the LecLRK genes was analyzed to identify the type and number of cis-acting regulatory element sites using PlantCARE (https://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/webtools/plantcare/html/) [29]. S. lycopersicum transcription factors with potential regulatory interactions with LecLRK genes were identified using PlantRegMap/PlantTFDB v5. (https://planttfdb.gao-lab.org/index.php?sp=Sly) [30, 31] using gene IDs and promoter sequences. The possible miRNA molecules predicted to silence LecLRK genes were retrieved from psRNATarget, a plant small RNA target analysis server (https://www.zhaolab.org/psRNATarget/) [32].

Differential expression of LecLRK genes during abiotic stress

The transcriptional datasets produced by high-throughput sequencing analysis and curated in the gene expression omnibus (GEO) database were searched for datasets of S. lycopersicum (genotype M82) under abiotic stresses. The FPKM-normalized transcription values for 38-day-old tomato seedlings under heat (42°C) and drought (GSE151277) [33] and 18-day-old tomato seedlings under cold (GSE226856) [34] were retrieved. The seedlings’ leaves were incubated at 42 °C for heat stress, and samples were collected at 0, 2, 4, 12, and 24 h. For drought, a water-withholding experiment was performed for 5 days, and leaf samples were collected at 0, 1, 2-, 3-, 4-, and 5 days post-drought. The chilling experiment at 4 °C was performed on seedling shoots (0, 30, and 180 min) and roots (0 and 30 min). LecLRK gene IDs for G- and L-type lectins were searched against the datasets, and the corresponding FPKMs were retrieved. iDEP 2.01 (http://bioinformatics.sdstate.edu/idep/) [35] was used for the data analysis using DEG1 (differential expression of genes) which employs DESeq2 package which employs the statistical analysis Wald test to determine the significance of gene expression between control and treatment after modeling the RNA-Seq data with a negative binomial distribution. iDEP2.0 default threshold for differentially expressed genes was used (log2FC ≥ 1 with at least 2-fold change in expression, adjusted P value (FDR ≤ 0.05 for statistical significance, and a minimum expression level to filter out low expression < 0.5.

Results

Overview of Solanum lycopersicum L- and G-type putative lectin homologs

Plants possess 12 unique lectin families with evolutionarily distinct domain architectures. The S. lycopersicum genome (ITAG2.4) was initially mined for the presence of members of each family. Seven families were identified (JRL (PF01419), LysM (PF01476), EUL (PF14200), Hevein (PF00187), Nictaba (PF14299), CRA (PF01453), G-type GNA (PF00704), and L-type Legume (PF00139)) with a total of 161 putative genes. GNA-related genes were the most abundant, accounting for approximately 53.4%, 43.7% of which were G-type LecRLK homologs containing protein kinase domains. The members of this family are distributed across all chromosomes. L- and G-type LecLRK members were predicted to be glycoproteins, with L-type protomers having relatively smaller sizes (62.86–93.35 kDa). Different domain architectures were evident between members of the same family and between the two families. All the Leg_B and Bulb B lectin domains are fused by their C-termini directly or through other domains into a kinase protein domain. Compared with LecRLK genes encoding protein tyrosine/serine/threonine kinases (PF07714), genes encoding protein kinases (PF00069) were more predominant (89% L-type and 51.6% G-type). Other protein domains have been reported in relation to G-type LecRLK, such as the S-locus domain, which is part of the plant pollen self-/nonself-recognition system [36]; the domain involved in bumping secondary metabolites and toxins across the cell membrane (MATE), hence regulating plant growth and development [37]; the cytochrome P450 domain, which mainly participates in the oxidative degradation of various environmental compounds such as toxins and mutagens [38]; and the FAD-binding domain, which catalyzes the oxidation of a variety of substrates. Most LecLRK genes are predicted to target the plasma membrane; however, their targeting and subcellular localization in the ER, Golgi apparatus, vacuoles, extracellular space, cytoplasm, and nucleus have also been reported. The diversification of the LecRLK homologs (especially the G-type) in terms of the architectural build of the protein itself and the subcellular targeting and localization allows these proteins to have distinct functions and participate in specific pathways (Table 1; Figs. 1 and 2, and Supplementary File 1, Table S1).

Expansion, evolutionary relationship, and gene collinearity of LecLRK

L-type and G-type lectin pairs exhibit various types of duplications, including proximal, tandem, and dispersed duplications, reflecting different mechanisms of gene expansion. 122 out of 162 gene pairs from both the L-type and the G-type have several key characteristics: (a) gene pairs with synonymous codon substitutions (Ks > 1) are the result of ancient duplication events, dating back over 95 million years; (b) these pairs share low sequence homology (< 50%); and (c) the Ka/Ks ratio is < 1, indicated purifying selection.

Among these ancient, expanded genes five gene pairs from the G-type lectin family appear to have arisen from whole-genome duplication (WGD) or segmental duplication events. These gene pairs exhibit significant collinearity, suggesting that they are syntenic duplicates. The synonymous substitution rate (Ks) for these pairs ranges from 1.22 to 2.69, which corresponds to estimated divergence times of approximately 93–205 million years ago. The nonsynonymous substitution rate (Ka) and Ka/Ks ratios suggest that all the gene pairs are under purifying selection, with Ka/Ks values between 0.18 and 0.34. The relatively low Ka/Ks ratios across all pairs indicate that although these genes have diverged over time, strong selective pressure has acted to preserve their core functions. Additionally, the consistent duplication depth value of 1 across all gene pairs implies that these duplications occurred during a single WGD or segmental duplication event. These findings suggest a high degree of evolutionary conservation and functional constraint among WGD-derived gene pairs in the tomato genome.

In contrast, 40 gene pairs did not exhibit the same characteristics as the other analyzed pairs (122). Nonetheless, the Ka/Ks ratios indicate that nearly all gene pairs are under purifying selection, with values consistently less than 1, suggesting that deleterious mutations are being removed and that these genes maintain their essential functions. The Ka/Ks ratios range from 0.13 to 1.18, with most values under 0.5, signifying strong evolutionary constraints. Tandem duplications are the most common type, particularly in the GNA family, indicating that many of these gene pairs likely arose through local duplications. For instance, the Solyc02g079540.1 and Solyc02g079550.1 pairs present a Ka/Ks ratio of 0.25 and an estimated evolutionary time of approximately 68 million years, indicating a significant evolutionary distance yet retained function. Proximal duplications are also present, as seen with pairs such as Solyc04g058110.2 and Solyc04g008400.1, which diverged approximately 22 million years ago with a Ka/Ks ratio of 0.32. Additionally, dispersed duplications are observed in both the GNA and legume families, suggesting long-range genomic duplication events. One example is the Solyc10g005440.1 and Solyc02g079570.2 pair, with a Ks value of 0.86 and an evolutionary time of approximately 66 million years, reflecting a significant divergence period. Despite the variation in duplication mechanisms and evolutionary times, all gene pairs are under purifying selection, indicating that these lectin-related genes have preserved important biological roles throughout evolutionary history.

A collinearity analysis between the genomes of Solanum lycopersicum (ITAG2.4) and Arabidopsis thaliana (TAIR10) revealed shared conserved syntenic blocks across different chromosomes. Several GNA and legume-type lectin genes in Solanum lycopersicum show conserved syntenic relationships with Arabidopsis genes, particularly AT3g33801.1, AT5g01550.1, and AT5g03410.1. L-type Solyc09g005000.1 shares 50% identity and synteny with members of the kinase receptor subfamily A4 (AtLecRK-VI.1 (AT3G08870.1), AtLecRK-VI.2 (AT3G3810.1), AtLecRK-VI.3 (AT5G01550.1), and AtLecRK-VI.4 (AT5G01560.1)), whereas Solyco10g084250.1 (LecRK-VIII) has 67% homology with AtLecRK-VIII.1 (AT3G380.1), and 63.7% homology with AtLecRK-VIII.2 (AT5G03140.1) syntenic counterparts. This highlights the conserved evolutionary trajectories between the two species and suggests that certain lectin genes have been preserved across large evolutionary distances.

The phylogenetic analysis illustrates the evolutionary relationships of G- and L-type lectin genes in S. lycopersicum, with a focus on the GNA bulb (G-type lectin) and Leg_B (L-type lectin) domains. These key functional regions were used to construct phylogenetic trees, providing insights into the evolutionary trajectories within these lectin families. In the phylogenetic tree, the G-type lectin domains cluster into distinct clades, indicating that while the GNA domain is conserved, there has been considerable diversification within the S. lycopersicum G-type lectin family. However, the clustering of S. lycopersicum G-type lectins shows a close evolutionary relationship, sharing a common origin that has since undergone specific variations. When the GNA ortholog (P03017) is compared with the G-type lectin domains, a clear pattern of divergence emerges, suggesting that while the core functionality of the GNA domain is retained, sequence modifications have optimized it for different biological functions. For the L-type lectins, the orthologous Leg_B domains from Glycine max (P05046) and Arabidopsis thaliana (LecRK-VI.1, LecRK-VI.2, LecRK-VI.3, LecRK-VI.4, LecRK-VIII.1, and LecRK-VIII.2) cluster together in distinct subtrees, reflecting a closer evolutionary relationship. In contrast, the S. lycopersicum L-type lectin domains show greater divergence from these orthologs, suggesting that species-specific evolutionary pressures have led to the diversification of L-type lectins in tomato. Differential expression of LecLRK genes during abiotic stress

Two different experimental settings that measure the effects of heat and drought (GSE151277) and cold (GSE226856) applied separately to M82 tomato seedling cultivars revealed differential expression of certain genes from both the L-type and G-type lectin families. During the application of drought stress, 12 (63.2%) out of 19 L-type genes were expressed, whereas only 13 (18.5%) out of the 86 entire G-type LecRLK gene sets were relatively expressed. L-type Solyc09g005000.1 and G-type Solyc03g078360.1 were significantly (2-fold) upregulated throughout almost all the days where drought was applied (D1–D5), and their expression was immediately downregulated on the recovery day (day 6), indicating a rapid response to the cessation of drought stress. However, when the tomato seedlings were subjected to heat treatment (42°C), other lectins were upregulated. L-type Solyc04g071000.1 and G-type Solyc03g078360.1 and Solyc04g008400.1 significantly increased, especially after 12 to 24 h of heat exposure. Moreover, cold stress, applied to both roots and shoots, also resulted in the differential expression of 11 genes from both the L-type and G-type lectin families (57.8 and 15.7%, respectively). However, the observed changes in gene expression during cold treatment were less pronounced, with no significantly upregulated genes in response to cold stress, implying a more subdued lectin-mediated response to this condition (Fig. 4).

Gene structure, cis-elements, transcription factor, and miRNA analyses

The primary structure and architecture of the five drought- and heat stress-upregulated genes (L-type Solyc09g005000.1, Solyc04g071000.1, Solyc10g084250.1 and G-type Solyc03g078360.1 and Solyc04g008400.1) indicate that, similar to many members of both families (i.e., legume- and GNA-related), their protomers are chimerolectins. A single exon encodes each gene of the three L-types LecRLK homologs. Architecturally, the ectopic Leg_B (PF00139) domain is fused to a cytoplasmic protein kinase domain (PF00069) through a transmembrane domain, allowing the cell to receive stress or any environmental signal through the lectin domain and carry it downstream through the Pkinase domain. This is supported by their cellular component gene ontology (GO:0005886). The promoter region of the Solyco09g005000.1 gene contains approximately 31 cis-element motifs, 25.8% of which are hormone-responsive elements and 29% of which are environmentally responsive elements, including 2 MBS cis-element motifs that respond to the MYB transcription factor and induce gene expression during the drought response. The Solyc09g005000.1 gene is predicted to be targeted by the Solyc01g108080.2.1 transcription factor from the family bZIP, which contains a basic-leucine zipper domain (PF00170). Based on the gene ontology of this TF, it functions in biological processes such as responds to water deprivation (GO:0009414), salt stress (GO:0009651), and the abscisic acid-activated signaling pathway (GO:0009738). Its differential expression profile during drought showed that despite reporting the highest levels at the beginning of the experiment (Checkpoint), its expression was maintained through the days of drought treatment, followed by a sudden drop upon receiving water on the recovery day. Solyc04g071000.1 is a heat-responsive LecRLK that shares primary architecture attributes to the Solyc09g005000.1, however, its promoter (1500 bp) contains half the number of cis-elements (16 elements) reported for Solyc09g005000.1. It is regulated by transcription factors that function in relation to hormonal pathways (AP2/ERF; PF00847), possibly during heat stress, as well as other TFs that function in relation to growth and development. Both Solyc09g005000.1 and Solyc04g071000.1 have sites for microRNA targeting (i.e., miR394, miR156, miR169, miR164, and mi390) for negative gene regulation. These miRNAs mainly regulate these genes during development, growth, and maturation. However, the family of miR169s (i.e., Sly miR169a, Sly miR169b, and Sly miR169c) that target Solyc04g071000.1 is known to regulate various abiotic stresses (heat, drought, salt, and cold) [39]. G-type LecRLK genes (Solyc03g078360.1 and Solyc04g008400.1) are also chimeric lectins with a single-exon gene configuration that encodes a B_lectin–S-locus–Pkinase protomer for (Solyc03g078360.1) and Solyc04g008400.1 architecture (12 exons gene) that results from two gene fusion events with protomers built from a successive (B_lectin–S-locus–PAN–Pkinase) configuration separated by a transmembrane domain. Each gene’s promoter region contains several cis-elements and is targeted by transcription factors related mainly to light responsiveness and growth and development processes. However, no miRNAs were predicted to regulate the expression of these genes. (Figures 5 and 6; Supplementary File 3: Table S4, S5, and S6).

Discussion

Conventional approaches to studying plant lectins [6] have provided valuable insight into the structure, function, and possible applications of isolated lectins [40–43]. However, with the emergence of the functional genomics field, a global view of entire gene sets from a specific family (such as lectins), their evolutionary relationship and the interactive network became possible [4, 44, 45]. In 2012, the tomato genome annotation (ITAG2.4) was released, the assembly covered approximately 84% of the genome, and 34,725 protein-coding genes were identified [15], of which 161 (0.46%) were lectin gene homologs from seven evolutionarily distinct plant lectin families. A total of 63.3% belong to the legume (L-type) and GNA (G-type) families. Many members of these families, along with the LysM and C-type genes, are considered LecRLKs because the kinase domain is linked via its N-terminus to an ectopic carbohydrate-binding domain, which makes these genes essential components of the cellular functions that mediate many central processes in metabolic signaling and the stress response [46].

The tomato genome underwent two significant genome-wide duplications. The first, an ancient paleohexaploidy event shared with other rosids approximately 135–140 million years ago, was a foundational event contributing to the diversification of flowering plants [47]. The second, more recent, occurred approximately 71 million years ago, specific to the Solanum lineage, predating the divergence of the Solanaceae family, which includes tomato, potato, pepper, etc. There may have been additional duplications specific to the tomato genome following this event [15]. These genome duplications are reflected in the L- and G-type lectins, where approximately 75% of these genes have synonymous substitution (Ks) values greater than 1. However, 99.4% of the lectin genes presented Ks/Ka ratios of less than 1, suggesting that evolutionary pressure led to the retention of beneficial mutations while purging deleterious mutations. This pattern is consistent with sub-functionalization (the division of original functions) rather than neofunctionalization gene evolution (the evolution of new functions) [48]. The duplication events inferred from the Ks values suggest that both ancient duplications (Ks > 1) are shared among eudicot plants, while the more recent duplications (Ks < 1) are shared within the Solanaceae lineage have expanded these lectin families. Additionally, tandem and segmental duplications are key drivers of lectin family expansion in other plant species, such as A. thaliana, G. max, and Oryza sativa. However, the contribution of these mechanisms varies across the genetic repertoire and species of lectin families, including the L- and G-type lectins in S. lycopersicum [49]. One of the evolutionary dynamics of plant lectins is linear domain rearrangement, where many maintain their core components while others undergo progressive changes and expansion within their protomer architecture [50]. G-type lectins are sophisticated examples of the adaptive power of domain rearrangement and functional diversification in S. lycopersicum and other plants. By combining lectin-like carbohydrate recognition with kinase-driven signal transduction, LecRKs have evolved to participate in a wide array of plant processes, from pathogen defense to reproductive regulation. The fusion of the lectin and S-domains with a kinase domain allows novel interactions with environmental and biological stimuli [51].

These evolutionary mechanisms promote functional specialization to adapt to environmental pressures that prompt a highly regulated mechanism of action against biotic and abiotic stresses. LecRLKs are environmental sensors that receive external signals through their lectin domain and transmit them via a cascade of downstream signals to ensure appropriate responses [52]. Although tomato L- and G-type LecRLKs constitute a large group of lectins, only a few are expressed in tomato seedlings under drought and heat stress conditions. This study reports low number of LecRLK from both families were expressed in seedling’s leaves out of which only L-type LecRLKs Solyc09g005000.1, Solyc04g071000.1, and Solyc10g084250.1 and G-type LecRLKs Solyc03g078360.1 and Solyc04g008400.1 were significantly upregulated under drought and heat conditions. The differential expression of this type of lectins (L- and G-type) is well documented in other plant species such as Populus trichocarpa, and in different tissues/organs and at variable plant stage [53]. L-type AtLecRK-IX.2 (ATG65600.1) from A. thaliana, shares 32.7, 45, and 36.6% with tomato Solyc09g005000.1, Solyc04g071000.1, and Solyc10g084250.1, respectively, was reported to be up-regulated under drought stress in roots throughout the experimental time except for the point at 3 h where a sudden drop in expression occurs. Contrary to the expression pattern in shoots where only at points 3 and 24 h the gene is upregulated. Authors also found that this gene is induced more in roots compared to shoots under a variety of stress conditions [54].

Upon comparing the evolutionary relationship of L-type LecRLKs from tomato with A. thaliana, Wang et al. reported that LecRLKs from tomato are closely related to those of other species, indicating a conserved evolutionary path within certain clades (IV, VI, VII, VIII, and IX) However, LecRLKs in tomato also show some unique lineage-specific expansions, suggesting that the evolutionary dynamics in tomatoes differ from other species like Arabidopsis. Members of the clades (I, II, III, and V) of AtLecRLK are believed to confer resistance against plant-damaging oomycetes Phytophthora and other bacterial plant pathogens [55]. AtLecRK-VI.2 (AT3G3810.1), shares 50% homology and a syntenic relationship with Solyc09g005000.1. AtLecRK-VI.2 participates in abscisic acid (ABA) signaling, stomatal regulation, and the expression of other stress-related genes. It enhances the accumulation of proline, an important osmolyte in stress responses, and primes a positive pattern-triggered immunity (PTI) response [56]. Another example is a G-type S-locus LecRLK named GsSRK, which was isolated from Glycine soja (wild soybean) and was identified as an alkaline-responsive gene encoding a 93.22 kDa protein. This gene is primarily induced by abscisic acid (ABA) treatment and is also responsive to salt and drought stresses. Its role in the stress response possibly involves mechanisms for maintaining chlorophyll levels and preserving cellular integrity [57]. Furthermore, transgenic pigeon pea (Cajanus cajan (L.) Millsp.) plants overexpressing rice’s OsLec-RLK gene exhibited enhanced tolerance to salinity stress, demonstrated by improved physiological responses under salt conditions, these transgenic plants also displayed a significant increase in seed yield compared to non-transgenic controls [58].

The regulation of S. lycopersicum LecRLK gene expression during abiotic stress mirrors the well-established mechanisms observed in model species such as A. thaliana. Several cis-regulatory elements associated with stress responses, including MYB-binding sites (MBSs), have been identified to regulate LecRLK [54]. These elements are crucial for triggering gene expression in response to drought stress, primarily through the action of MYB transcription factors, which play pivotal roles in ABA-mediated stress pathways [59]. Additionally, transcription factors such as bZIP and AP2/ERF [60, 61], known for regulating stress-related genes, were found to control the expression of S. lycopersicum LecRLK genes under drought and heat stress conditions, facilitating the activation of these genes during adverse environmental conditions. These findings suggest that a highly conserved regulatory mechanism across different plant species is essential for adaptive stress responses. While LecRLKs primarily act as environmental sensors, their activation is tightly linked to hormonal regulation, particularly via abscisic acid (ABA), a hormone crucial for stress adaptation. ABA not only regulates the expression of MYB and other transcription factors, but also directly influences LecRLKs, enabling plants to fine-tune their response to drought, heat, and other stresses [61]. The modulation of S. lycopersicum LecRLKs under drought and heat stress and their differential expression during cold stress underscore their potential importance in broader abiotic stress adaptation pathways.

The identification of key L- and G-type LecRLKs that are differentially expressed under drought and heat stress provides valuable genetic markers that could be incorporated after systematic validation into breeding programs. By selecting these stress-responsive genes, breeders can develop tomato varieties with improved resilience to environmental stresses, ultimately leading to more stable yields under challenging conditions.

Conclusion

This study provides new insights into the role of L- and G-type LecRLKs in tomato, particularly under abiotic stress conditions such as drought and heat. Phylogenetic, duplication and domain architecture analyses reveal significant evolutionary expansion, driven by purifying selection, to preserve essential stress response functions. The observed differential expression patterns during drought and heat stress emphasize the importance of these genes in adaptive mechanisms, positioning them as key players in improving stress tolerance in tomato plants.

However, the study has some limitations. First, there is a lack of experimental validation for the differential expression and upregulation of the identified genes, which would strengthen the evidence for their biological relevance. Second, the study offers limited functional analysis, as it does not explore LecRLK interactions with molecular components beyond transcription factors, which could provide deeper mechanistic insights. Lastly, the focus on a single crop (tomato) limits the broader applicability of the findings. Comparative studies with other Solanaceae species or crops could expand the relevance of these results to a wider range of plants. Despite these limitations, the findings offer valuable potential for tomato breeding and future functional studies.
