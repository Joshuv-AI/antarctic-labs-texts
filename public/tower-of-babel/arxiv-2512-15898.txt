Higher-form entanglement asymmetry.


Abstract
Entanglement asymmetry is a relative entropy that faithfully diagnoses symmetry breaking in quantum states, possibly within a spatial subregion. In this work, we extend such framework to higher-form symmetries and compute entanglement asymmetry in theories with spontaneously-broken continuous zero- and higher-form symmetries. One of our central results is an entropic Coleman–Mermin–Wagner theorem, for 0- and p p -form symmetries, valid also on subregions, which forbids spontaneous breaking of continuous p p -form symmetries in spacetime dimensions d ⩽ p + 2 d\leqslant p+2 . Our theorem not only qualifies symmetry breaking, it also quantifies it: spontaneous breaking triggers a nonvanishing entanglement asymmetry that grows monotonically towards the infrared, and counts the number of Goldstone fields. Along the way, we derive standalone results concerning the entanglement entropy of Goldstone bosons and gauge fields.

Introduction and summary

Over the past decade, our understanding of quantum many-body systems and quantum field theory (QFT) has advanced substantially thanks to the generalisation of symmetry far beyond the standard textbook treatment. From a modern point of view, symmetries are given by the set of topological operators of a theory — each of which implements a symmetry action — together with a prescription of what happens when these operators meet. Standard symmetries are implemented by codimension-

To fix ideas, we briefly recall the definition of entanglement asymmetry in the simplest setting, involving ordinary (i.e., zero-form and invertible) symmetries. Suppose we have a quantum mechanical theory that enjoys a global symmetry given by a group
G
. Consider a state, described by a trace-normalised density matrix
\rho
, which can be pure or mixed, defined on a subsystem or on the entire system. If one wants to test whether or not the symmetry is broken in the state
\rho
, one can compare it to its symmetrisation, which by construction preserves the symmetry:

\rho_{\mathsf{S}}\coloneqq\frac{1}{\absolutevalue{G}}\,\sum_{g\in G}\,U_{g}\;\rho\;U_{g}^{\dagger}~.

(1.1)

Here
U_{g}
are the operators that implement the symmetry action on the Hilbert space. Eqn.
equation

1.1
then describes a quantum channel that projects to the neutral singlet representations of
G
. For brevity we will sometimes refer to this map
\rho\mapsto\rho_{\mathsf{S}}
as a
symmetriser
. The above formula is written for a discrete group, but the generalisation to continuous symmetries is straightforward and it involves an integral in place of the sum.

A simple and, as it turns out, useful way to compare the two states
\rho
and
\rho_{\mathsf{S}}
is the relative entropy between them:

\Delta\mathcal{S}_{G}(\rho)=\mathcal{S}\quantity(\vphantom{\big|}\rho\,\middle\Vert\, \rho_\sfS)=\tr\bigl(\rho\,\quantity(\log\rho- \log\rho_\sfS)\bigr)~.

(1.2)

This is entanglement asymmetry. By the properties of relative entropy, we immediately have
\Delta\mathcal{S}_{G}\geqslant 0
on any state and moreover
\Delta\mathcal{S}_{G}=0
if and only if
\rho=\rho_{\mathsf{S}}
. Hence it is a faithful measure of symmetry breaking. Moreover, since the map
\rho\mapsto\rho_{\mathsf{S}}
is a projector,
\Delta\mathcal{S}_{G}
admits an alternative expression as a difference of von Neumann entropies:

\Delta\mathcal{S}_{G}(\rho)=\mathcal{S}\quantity(\rho_\sfS)-\mathcal{S}(\rho)~.

(1.3)

This equivalent presentation has certain computational benefits — of which the main one is enabling Rényi versions of the asymmetry, by means of the replica trick.

While originally studied on the lattice, entanglement asymmetry displays a number of important features when imported into QFT. To begin with, as a relative entropy, it is free of ultraviolet (UV) divergences, at least whenever the symmetry breaking is spontaneous or explicit but soft. Furthermore, it depends solely on the state and not on the Hamiltonian, and so it captures universal data. Universal and UV-finite observables are notoriously scarce in QFT, which makes this one particularly valuable.

Another remarkable trait is the following. Monotonicity of relative entropy
[
57
,
58
]
implies that entanglement asymmetry cannot increase under any quantum channel
\mathcal{E}
that commutes with the symmetriser:

\Delta\mathcal{S}_{G}(\rho)\geqslant\Delta\mathcal{S}_{G}\bigl(\mathcal{E}(\rho)\bigr)~.

(1.4)

Consider now the situation of typical interest in QFT: a state
\rho_{\mathcal{A}}
defined on a region
\mathcal{A}
. For any subregion
\mathcal{B}\subseteq\mathcal{A}
, the partial trace over the complement of
\mathcal{B}
is such a quantum channel.

\Delta\mathcal{S}_{G}(\rho_{\mathcal{A}})\geqslant\Delta\mathcal{S}_{G}(\rho_{\mathcal{B}})~,

(1.5)

for any

and for all states. This is already an interesting structural constraint. Specialising further to a specific class of regions, it becomes even more powerful. For instance, let

. It is clear that tracing out the complement of

for the entanglement asymmetry of the reduced state (which we leave implicit) on a ball of radius

\derivative{r}\,\Delta\mathcal{S}_{G}(r)\geqslant 0~.

(1.6)

The above establishes entanglement asymmetry as an RG monotone.

Entanglement asymmetry detects symmetry breaking for both ground and excited states. Whenever
\rho
is the ground state, the question is one of
spontaneous
symmetry breaking (SSB). In the standard Landau paradigm, SSB is diagnosed in effective field theory by a local operator, an
order parameter
, whose expectation value is nonvanishing in the broken phase. One of the standard textbook accounts of SSB goes as follows. Start with the underlying theory at finite volume
V
and turn on a symmetry-breaking source
h
. Calculate the expectation value
\expectationvalue{\cO}_{V,h}
of an order parameter
\mathcal{O}
. If

\lim_{h\to 0}\,\lim_{V\to\infty}\expectationvalue{\cO}_{V,h}\neq 0~,

(1.7)

the theory exhibits SSB. Note that the limits do not commute: at finite
V
, sending
h\to 0
first forces
\expectationvalue{\cO}=0
by selection rules. Let us now give an alternative way of detecting SSB through the asymmetry. Consider a generic (clustering, excited) symmetry breaking density matrix
\rho_{V}

at finite volume
V
and evolve it in Euclidean time
\beta
, with the system’s Hamiltonian:

\rho_{V,\beta}=\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\beta H}\,\rho_{V}\,\mathrm{e}^{-\beta H}}{\tr(\rho_V\, \ex{-2\beta H})}~.

(1.8)

The limit
\beta\to\infty
projects to the vacuum. At finite volume this is unique, but taking
V\to\infty
first can lead to SSB. Hence SSB occurs whenever

\lim_{\beta\to\infty}\lim_{V\to\infty}\Delta\mathcal{S}_{G}\quantity(\rho_{V,\beta})\neq 0~.

(1.9)

This is the precise sense in which
\Delta\mathcal{S}_{G}
is a good entropic order parameter (using the terminology of
[
30
]
).

Entropic symmetry-breaking theorems

A resounding result in quantum field theory is the theorem due to Mermin–Wagner
[
79
]
and Coleman
[
80
]
(hereby the
CMW theorem
) that continuous symmetries cannot break spontaneously in spacetime dimensions
d\leqslant 2
. A similar statement exists for higher-form symmetries
[
1
,
81
]
, stating that a continuous
p
-form symmetry cannot break spontaneously when
d\leqslant p+2
. As an application of our formalism, we examine the SSB patterns of quantum field theories with continuous symmetries and establish an
entropic
CMW theorem both for 0-form and higher-form symmetries. A benefit of our entropic theorems is that they are also
quantitative
: we show how SSB is controlled by the system size.

Let us state this result more precisely. We consider effective field theories (EFTs) in which a continuous symmetry — Abelian or non-Abelian, 0-form or
p
-form — is realised nonlinearly, and compute the entanglement asymmetry of a pure clustering ground state
\rho
. We start by considering the entanglement asymmetry of the full system (as opposed to a subsystem). We find that it behaves as follows (stated here in the general case of a
p
-form symmetry):

\Delta\mathcal{S}(\rho)=\begin{cases}0~,&d\leqslant p+2~,\\[1.99997pt]
\dfrac{\mathcal{N}(d-p-2)}{2}\,\log(\mu R)~,&d>p+2~.\end{cases}

(1.10)

Here
R
is the linear system size, taken to infinity in accordance with the thermodynamic limit, and
\mu
is an energy scale: the UV cutoff of the EFT.
4
4

4

We also consider symmetry breaking of
p
-form symmetries in CFTs. In that case, instead of by a cutoff, symmetry breaking is controlled by the ratio between the size of a
(d{-}p{-}1)
-dimensional manifold and that of a compact
p
-dimensional manifold, in accordance with SSB patterns of higher-form symmetries
[
81
,
82
,
83
]
.

This is a first incarnation of our entropic CMW theorem: in
d\leqslant p+2
, the symmetry is unbroken and the ground state is unique. But in
d>p+2
, there exists a moduli space of symmetry-breaking vacua. Entanglement asymmetry detects this by picking up a logarithmic growth.

The dimension of this moduli space is encoded in the coefficient
\mathcal{N}
of the logarithmic scaling. Specifically, we find that
\mathcal{N}
is the number of broken generators of the symmetry under consideration. For instance, in the case of
\mathrm{U}(1)
symmetry breaking we find

\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{U}(1)}=1~,

(1.11)

while for a general pattern

the coefficient is:

\mathcal{N}_{G/H}=\dim(G/H)=\frac{\dim G}{\dim H}~.

(1.12)

In the 0-form case, our results match with results obtained on the lattice using excited matrix product states
[
39
]
.

More strikingly, this result provides an indirect proof of a conjecture by Metlitski and Grover

(1.13)

Here

\mu
is the EFT scale. The area-law coefficient

Moreover, our results extend to higher-form symmetries. In this case the role of the Goldstones is played by higher-form fields: photons and

Subregion theorems

A further advantage of entanglement asymmetry is that it can detect symmetry breaking in a subsystem. This can either consist of a subset of the degrees of freedom, or of the degrees of freedom associated to a spatial subregion. The latter is the case that we focus on in the present work. Having a local description of symmetry breaking could be very useful in situations where some of the degrees of freedom are inaccessible. A prototypical example of such a situation is in quantum gravity, where degrees of freedom are often hidden behind a black hole or a cosmological horizon.

We utilise entanglement asymmetry to explore such situations in quantum field theory and we establish a subregion CMW theorem. For concreteness we focus on
\mathrm{U}(1)

p
-form symmetries. To get a flavour of our result, suppose that
\rho
is a clustering ground state at infinite volume, and
\rho_{\mathcal{A}}
is its restriction to a subregion
\mathcal{A}
of size
R
. The entanglement asymmetry of that state has the following behaviour:

\Delta\mathcal{S}_{{\mathrm{U}(1)}^{\quantity[p]}}(\rho_{\mathcal{A}})=\begin{cases}0~,&d\leqslant p+2~,\\[1.99997pt]
f(\mu R)~,&d>p+2~.\end{cases}

(1.16)

Here
f(\mu R)
is a monotonic function of
\mu R
that interpolates between no symmetry breaking at small scales:

f(\mu R)\,\sim\,0\qquad\quad\mbox{when}\quad\mu R\ll 1~,

(1.17)

and full symmetry breaking at large scales, when the subregion covers the entire Cauchy slice:

f(\mu R)\,\sim\,\frac{d-p-2}{2}\;\log(\mu R)\qquad\quad\mbox{when}\quad\mu R\gg 1~.

(1.18)

In other words, for
d>p+2
, the subregion entanglement asymmetry coincides at leading order with the one of the full system when the region is big, and it vanishes when the region is small. Recalling that the inverse size of the subregion,
R^{-1}
, is an RG flow scale is clarifying. In the deep infrared (IR) the entanglement asymmetry coincides with the one of the full system. In the ultraviolet (UV) the symmetry is restored and the entanglement asymmetry vanishes.

Entanglement asymmetry for higher-form symmetries

The aim of this section is to extend the notion of entanglement asymmetry to encompass higher-form symmetries in
d
spacetime dimensions. This generalisation falls into place once we rewrite the definition
equation

1.1
in a more geometric language. For concreteness, let us take
G
to be an Abelian continuous compact group. We begin with global states, namely those defined on the entire
(d-1)
-dimensional spatial slice
\Sigma
. On such a slice, the topological defects that implement a standard 0-form symmetry are labelled by a
(d-1)
-cycle in the homology group
H_{d-1}(\Sigma;G)
with coefficients in
G
. Since we are primarily concerned with connected spatial slices (the generalisation to multiple connected components being straightforward), we have the isomorphism
H_{d-1}(\Sigma;G)\cong G
.

With this identification, the symmetrised density matrix can be written as

\rho_{\mathsf{S}}=\int_{H_{d-1}(\Sigma;G)}\differential{\mu(a)}\,U_{a}\;\rho\;U_{a}^{\dagger}~.

(2.1)

The measure

\differential{\mu(a)}=\frac{\differential{a}}{\opbraces{\volume}\bigl(H_{d-1}(\Sigma;G)\bigr)}

(2.2)

is the pushforward of the Haar measure of
G
by the map implementing the isomorphism
G\xlongrightarrow{\sim}H_{d-1}(\Sigma;G)
. It is normalised so that the total measure is unity, ensuring that
\rho_{\mathsf{S}}
remains trace-normalised. When
G
is discrete, its Haar measure is naturally the counting measure and
equation

2.1
reduces to
equation

1.1
.

6

2.1

\rho_{\mathsf{S}}

Now suppose we focus not on global states, but rather on reduced states

With suitable boundary conditions on the regulated entangling surface, the symmetry operators can act on the factorised Hilbert space:

U_{a}(\Sigma)\,\to\,U_{a}(\mathcal{A})\;U_{a}(\mathcal{A}^{\prime})~,

Rényi asymmetries
. These are defined in terms of the Rényi entropies as

\Delta\mathcal{S}_{\mathsf{n},{G}^{\quantity[p]}}(\rho)\coloneqq\frac{1}{1-\mathsf{n}}\log\frac{\tr(\rho_\sfS^\n)}{\tr(\rho^\n)}~,

(2.9)

and are indexed by an integer
\mathsf{n}\geqslant 2
. The symmetrised contribution takes the following form:

\tr(\rho_\sfS^\n)=\int_{H_{d-p-1}(\Sigma;G)}\,\prod_{i=1}^{\mathsf{n}}\differential\mu_{i}(a_{i})\,\mathcal{E}_{\mathsf{n}}(\boldsymbol{a})~,

(2.10)

where
\boldsymbol{a}
denotes the
\mathsf{n}
-component vector of elements
a_{i}\in H_{d-p-1}(\Sigma;G)
. The
\mathcal{E}_{\mathsf{n}}(\boldsymbol{a})
are the
charged moments
:

\mathcal{E}_{\mathsf{n}}(\boldsymbol{a})=\tr[\,\prod_{i=1}^{\mathsf{n}}U_{a_{i}}\,\rho\,U_{a_{i}}^{\dagger}]~.

(2.11)

The connection to entanglement asymmetry comes naturally through the replica trick. Specifically, once the Rényi asymmetries are calculated, analytically continuing
\mathsf{n}
and taking the limit
\mathsf{n}\to 1
, one recovers the entanglement asymmetry:

\Delta\mathcal{S}_{{G}^{\quantity[p]}}(\rho)=\lim_{\mathsf{n}\to 1}\;\Delta\mathcal{S}_{\mathsf{n},{G}^{\quantity[p]}}(\rho)~.

(2.12)

3
An entropic Coleman–Mermin–Wagner theorem

It is a classic result
[
79
,
93
,
80
]
that a continuous global symmetry in
d\leqslant 2
spacetime dimensions is necessarily preserved in the ground state. Following the literature, we call this result the Coleman–Mermin–Wagner (CMW) theorem. Most standard derivations show that a putative order parameter,
v
, must vanish in two dimensions. In
[
79
]
this is the magnetisation of the Heisenberg model, while in
[
80
]
it is the commutator of the conserved charge with a generic scalar operator. With the advent of generalised symmetries, the CMW theorem has been extended to higher-form symmetries: a continuous
p
-form symmetry in
d
spacetime dimensions must remain unbroken in the ground state if
d\leqslant p+2

[
1
,
81
]
. In what follows we revisit this classic problem from a different angle, computing the entanglement asymmetry in the ground state of a would-be Goldstone
p
-form gauge field.

We consider a
\mathrm{U}(1)

p
-form symmetry, denoted as
{\mathrm{U}(1)}^{\quantity[p]}_{\text{e}}
, of a QFT on a Euclidean (closed)
d
-dimensional spacetime
X_{d}
, and assume it to be spontaneously broken. The universal IR effective description of such a phase is provided by a free
p
-form Maxwell theory. Its lowest-derivative action, in Euclidean signature, takes the form:

S=\frac{\lambda\,\mu^{\Delta}}{2}\int_{X_{d}}{f}_{p+1}\wedge\star\,{f}_{p+1}~.

(3.1)

Here
{f}_{p+1}
is the
(p+1)
-form field strength. Being closed, it admits a decomposition

{f}_{p+1}={f}_{p+1}^{(h)}+\differential{\f{a}{p}}~,

(3.2)

where
{f}_{p+1}^{(h)}\in 2\pi\,H^{p+1}(X_{d};\mathbb{Z})
is the harmonic part, while
{a}_{p}
is a globally-defined
\mathrm{U}(1)

p
-form gauge connection. The coupling
\lambda
is dimensionless, while
\mu
is an arbitrary energy scale. We also introduced the shorthand
\Delta=d-2(p+1)
.
10
10

10

Note that the action has no scale when
\Delta=0
: the theory is indeed conformal. This is for example the case of a compact scalar in
d=2
or free electromagnetism in
d=4
. Whenever
\Delta\neq 0
, the theory has a scale, and flows to a scale-invariant theory at the two ends of the RG flow. Note, however, that the fixed points are not always conformal
[
94
,
95
,
96
]
.

In this effective theory the
{\mathrm{U}(1)}^{\quantity[p]}_{\text{e}}
electric symmetry is realised non-linearly, through shifts of the gauge connection:

{a}_{p}\mapsto{a}_{p}+2\pi\,{\Omega}_{p}~,\qquad\quad{\Omega}_{p}\in H^{p}(X_{d};\mathbb{Z})~.

(3.3)

Besides the electric
{\mathrm{U}(1)}^{\quantity[p]}_{\text{e}}
symmetry, the effective action
equation

3.1
also enjoys an emergent
{\mathrm{U}(1)}^{\quantity[d-p-2]}_{\text{m}}
magnetic symmetry. This symmetry is likewise spontaneously broken in the phase of interest. Moreover, the two symmetries are linked by a mixed anomaly. It is precisely this interplay that guarantees the existence of massless excitations, even in regimes where the CMW theorem rules out spontaneous symmetry breaking
[
97
,
98
]
. For our purposes, however, the magnetic symmetry will play almost no role and we will not mention it further. Hence, in what follows we denote the electric symmetry simply as
{\mathrm{U}(1)}^{\quantity[p]}
.

3.1
Quantisation of
p
-form gauge theory

We now turn to the quantisation of the theory on a general spatial slice
\Sigma
, following
[
99
,
100
]
. Denoting the coordinate transversal to
\Sigma
by
t
, the field strength decomposes as
11
11

11

We use the mostly-plus convention in Lorentzian signature, so that
\star\,(\differential{t}\wedge{\omega}_{p})=-\star_{\Sigma}{\omega}_{p}
and
\star\,\omega_{p}=(-1)^{p}\,\differential{t}\wedge\star_{\Sigma}\,{\omega}_{p}
for a
p

{\omega}_{p}
on
\Sigma
.

{f}_{p+1}=\differential{t}\mathbin{\scalebox{0.8}{$\wedge$}}{E}_{p}+(-1)^{d(p+1)}\star_{\scriptscriptstyle\Sigma}{B}_{d-p-2}~,

(3.4)

where
{E}_{p}
and
{B}_{d-p-2}
are forms on
\Sigma
. They are the electric and magnetic fields, respectively, as we first learn them in electrodynamics. In terms of these, the Hamiltonian on
\Sigma
can be written as

H_{\Sigma}=\frac{\lambda\,\mu^{\Delta}}{2}\quantity(\norm{\f{E}{p}}^2+\norm{\f{B}{d-p-2}}^2)~,

(3.5)

where the norm denotes
\norm{\omega}^{2}=\int_{\Sigma}\omega\mathbin{\scalebox{0.8}{$\wedge$}}\star_{\scriptscriptstyle\Sigma}\,\omega

Already at the semiclassical level, both electric and magnetic fluxes are quantised.
12
12

12

Magnetic fluxes are obviously quantised as follows from elementary differential geometry. For electric fluxes, a transparent way to see that they are quantised is to consider an open path integral preparing the state in question. The path-integral sums over magnetic fluxes
\oint\!f\in 2\pi\mathbb{Z}
. This sum generates a delta function that quantises the electric fluxes.

For concreteness let us fix bases
\quantity{C^i_{p+1}}
and
\quantity{\hat{C}^{\hat{\jmath}}_{d-p-1}}
of
(p+1)
- and
(d-p-1)

\Sigma

\displaystyle\Phi_{\text{m}}^{i}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\int_{C^{i}_{p+1}}{f}_{p+1}=(-1)^{d(p+1)}\int_{C^{i}_{p+1}}\star_{\scriptscriptstyle\Sigma}\,{B}_{d-p-2}~,\quad\mbox{and}\quad

(3.6)

\displaystyle\Phi_{\text{e}}^{\hat{\jmath}}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\int_{C^{\hat{\jmath}}_{d-p-1}}\star\,{f}_{p+1}=-\int_{C^{\hat{\jmath}}_{d-p-1}}\star_{\scriptscriptstyle\Sigma}\,{E}_{p}~.

(3.7)

Flux quantisation then implies that:

\Phi_{\text{m}}^{i}=2\pi\,m^{i}~,\quad\mbox{and}\quad\Phi_{\text{e}}^{\hat{\jmath}}=\frac{1}{\lambda\,\mu^{\Delta}}\,k^{\hat{\jmath}}~,\quad\mbox{with}\quad m^{i},k^{\hat{\jmath}}\in\mathbb{Z}~.

(3.8)

One of the main observations of
[
101

102

is that, whenever

\Sigma

The topological sector of a state is thus specified by its fluxes through the chosen cycles. As discussed in
[
100

\boldsymbol{m}
and

\displaystyle\Phi_{\text{m}}^{i}\ket{\vec{m},\vec{k}}

\displaystyle=2\pi\,m^{i}\ket{\vec{m},\vec{k}}~,

(3.9)

\displaystyle\Phi_{\text{e}}^{\hat{\jmath}}\ket{\vec{m},\vec{k}}

\displaystyle=\frac{k^{\hat{\jmath}}}{\lambda\mu^{\Delta}}\ket{\vec{m},\vec{k}}~.

(3.10)

These states are taken to be orthonormal. The energy of the state
\ket{\vec{m},\vec{k}}
, as measured by the Hamiltonian
equation

3.5

E_{\boldsymbol{m},\boldsymbol{k}}=2\pi^{2}\lambda\mu^{\Delta}\,m_{i}G_{ij}m_{j}+\frac{1}{2\lambda\mu^{\Delta}}k_{\hat{\imath}}\hat{G}_{\hat{\imath}\hat{\jmath}}k_{\hat{\jmath}}~.

(3.11)

In this formula,

\hat{G}_{\hat{\imath}\hat{\jmath}}

[

[

103

For our purposes it will suffice to consider spatial slices of the form

\Sigma=S_{r}^{p}\times S_{R}^{d-p-1}~,

(3.12)

where the subscripts

13
13

13

For simplicity, we define the “radius”

For generic

, all states have

and therefore they are fully specified by their the electric flux on

.
14
14

14

In some cases more care is needed.
If
d=2p+1
, one can turn on electric fluxes on both spheres, but in the limit of interest the extra states have energies that diverge in units of

and thus do not become vacua.
On the other hand, if
d=2p+2
, nonvanishing magnetic flux on
S^{d-p-1}_{R}
is allowed, and the situation is subtler. States carrying magnetic flux (or mixed electric–magnetic flux) have vanishing energy at infinite volume. Moreover, a

We denote these states by
\ket{k}
, indicating that they carry electric flux
k/(\lambda\mu^{\Delta})
. Such states are prepared by a Euclidean path integral on
\mathbb{R}_{-}\times\Sigma
with an insertion of a charge

.
15
15

15

In fact, one needs to dress the Wilson lines with squeezing operators, see

. We will omit this step as it will not affect our results.

We note that the states

U_{\alpha}\quantity(S^{d-p-1})=\exp( \ii\alpha\, \lambda\mu^\Delta\int_{S^{d-p-1}} \star\, \f{f}{p+1})

(3.13)

acts on the states as:

U_{\alpha}\quantity(S^{d-p-1})\ket{k}=\mathrm{e}^{i\alpha k}\ket{k}~.

(3.14)

As detailed above, these states are also energy eigenstates with energy:

E_{k}=\frac{1}{R}\,\frac{k^{2}}{2\lambda}\,\frac{\quantity(\mu r)^{p}}{(\mu R)^{d-p-2}}~.

(3.15)

An interesting question is whether these states are degenerate. Generically they are not, but if we consider the limit in which

1/R
as long as

R\,E_{k}\to 0\quad\quad\text{if}\quad\quad d>p+2~.

(3.16)

Let us pause with this result. This is a decompactification limit, in which

while
\mu r
is kept fixed. In other words, the volume of

, which is where the Wilson lines
W_{k}(\tau_{0},S^{p})
are inserted to prepare the states, is kept fixed. Overall, this is a large volume limit akin to the standard one used for
0

16
16

16

Note that, indeed, a spontaneously broken
p
-form symmetry does not lead to a degeneracy of states on
\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
, but rather on spatial slices of the form

such that

is very large

We conclude that in the large volume limit the ground state is degenerate if and only if

. This agrees with the higher-form Coleman–Mermin–Wagner theorem, but does not yet constitute a proof, since we have not addressed whether the
{\mathrm{U}(1)}^{\quantity[p]}
symmetry is spontaneously broken or not.

We end by commenting on the nongeneric cases where more states are available due to the interplay between
p
and

. In the case

, where electric flux on

is also allowed, the result is unchanged since, in the limit, the extra states have energies that diverge in units of

. On the contrary, the states with only electric flux on

have energies that, in units of
1/R
, vanish for

(indicating degenerate vacua at infinite volume), are constant for
p=1
(indicating a scale-invariant effective theory with a single vacuum), and diverge for
p=0
(indicating a gapped effective theory). On the other hand, in the case

, the theory is conformal and there is no energy scale

(indeed

. The states with magnetic flux on

as those of the purely electric states. We can still take the limit

\frac{R}{r}\,\to\,\infty~.

(3.17)

For
p\geqslant 1
, all energies go to zero in units of
1/R
and thus all dyonic states
\ket{k,m}
are degenerate at infinite volume. This hints at the magnetic and electric symmetries — which have the same degree in this case — being both spontaneously broken. We will come back to this point in the next section. For
d=2
and
p=0

1/R
and there is no limit in which the states become degenerate, indicating that the symmetry remains unbroken in a scale-invariant theory with a single vacuum.

3.2
Asymmetry in
p
-form gauge theory

We have yet to address the spontaneous breaking (or not) of the
{\mathrm{U}(1)}^{\quantity[p]}
symmetry, which we do in this section by explicitly computing the entanglement asymmetry. For this purpose we introduce a new basis of states (see also
[
103
]
for a related discussion):

\ket{\theta}=\sum\nolimits_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathrm{e}^{ik\theta}\ket{k}~.

(3.18)

The
{\mathrm{U}(1)}^{\quantity[p]}
symmetry acts on these states nontrivially:

U_{\alpha}\quantity(S^{d-p-1})\ket{\theta}=\ket{\theta+ \alpha}~.

(3.19)

More importantly, the states
\ket{\theta}
are the ones that satisfy cluster decomposition and are thus the relevant ones in order to study the spontaneous breaking of the
{\mathrm{U}(1)}^{\quantity[p]}
symmetry.
17
17

17

Consider for simplicity the case
p=0
for
d>2
. A vertex operator
V_{q}(x)=\mathrm{e}^{iq\phi(x)}
of charge
q
acts as
V_{q}(x)\,|k\rangle\sim|k+q\rangle
on the states with fixed flux, which implies
\bra{k}V_{q}(x)\ket{k}=0
. This is not surprising: the flux sourced by
V_{q}(x)
has nowhere to go in
S_{R}^{d-1}
. Conversely,

\bra{k}V_{q_{1}}(0)\,V_{q_{2}}(x)\ket{k}\,\propto\,\delta_{q_{1},-q_{2}}\,\exp(-\frac{q_1 q_2}{\lambda\abs{\mu\, x}^{d-2}})~,

(3.20)

which is nonvanishing as
\absolutevalue{x}\to\infty
when
q_{1}=-q_{2}
. This violates cluster decomposition. Similar arguments hold for generic

. In contrast, one can check that cluster decomposition is preserved on
S^{d-p-1}_{R}
in the
\ket{\theta}
states. In those cases without vacuum degeneracy one always achieves cluster decomposition after projection to the unique vacuum.

An interesting property of the states
\ket{\theta}
is that generically they are not energy eigenstates, but become eigenstates with zero energy in the large volume limit explored in the previous section, as long as
d>p+2
. That is the case where we expect the symmetry to be spontaneously broken in the large volume limit and the states
\ket{\theta}
are good candidates to describe the symmetry breaking vacua. However, these states are only delta-function normalisable. Inspired by computations in quenched CFTs
[
104
,
105
,
106
,
107
]
, topological order and boundary CFT
[
108
,
109
,

,
111
,

, we regulate the states using the quadratic Hamiltonian

3.5

\ket{\theta_\epsilon}\coloneqq\mathrm{e}^{-\epsilon R\,H_{\Sigma}}\ket{\theta}~.

(3.21)

Also these states transform nontrivially under the
{\mathrm{U}(1)}^{\quantity[p]}
symmetry action, but they are normalisable. What was sacrificed in the process is orthogonality of the states at different angles, but this is something we can keep track of. It follows from
\theta\sim\theta+2\pi
and
equation

3.19
that, as long as
d>p+2
so that these states are degenerate in the large volume limit, the moduli space of vacua is a circle
S^{1}
.

This brings us to the computation of entanglement asymmetry of the states
\ket{\theta_\epsilon}
. The corresponding trace-normalised pure density matrix is

\rho_{\theta}=\frac{\outerproduct{\theta_\epsilon}{\theta_\epsilon}}{\Theta\quantity(q_\epsilon)}~.

(3.22)

We introduced the Jacobi theta constants

\Theta(q)=\sum\nolimits_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}q^{n^{2}}~,

(3.23)

and we defined

q_{\epsilon}=\exp(-\frac{\epsilon}{\lambda} \frac{\qty(\mu r)^p}{(\mu R)^{d-p-2}})

(3.24)

as a shorthand. According to
equation

2.6
, the symmetrised density matrix takes the form

\rho_{\theta,\mathsf{S}}=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{\differential{\alpha}}{2\pi}\;U_{\alpha}\quantity(S^{d-p-1})\,\rho_{\theta}\,U^{\dagger}_{\alpha}\quantity(S^{d-p-1})=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{\differential{\alpha}}{2\pi}\;\rho_{\theta+\alpha}~.

(3.25)

The integral can be evaluated, yielding

\rho_{\theta,\mathsf{S}}=\frac{1}{\Theta\quantity(q_\epsilon)}\,\sum\nolimits_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\;e^{-2\epsilon R\,E_{k}}\outerproduct{k}{k}~.

(3.26)

To compute the entanglement asymmetry we employ the replica trick, as in
equation

2.12
. A straightforward check shows that
\tr(\rho_\theta^\n)=1
and that the symmetrised trace evaluates to

\tr( \rho_{\theta,\sfS}^\n)=\frac{\Theta\quantity(q_\epsilon^\n)}{\Theta\quantity(q_\epsilon)^{\mathsf{n}}}~.

(3.27)

Substituting this expression into
equation

2.9
we obtain the Rényi asymmetries:

\Delta\mathcal{S}_{\mathsf{n},\,{\mathrm{U}(1)}^{\quantity[p]}}\bigl(\rho_{\theta}\bigr)=\frac{1}{1-\mathsf{n}}\log\frac{\Theta\quantity(q_\epsilon^\n)}{\Theta\quantity(q_\epsilon)^{\mathsf{n}}}~.

(3.28)

The limit
\mathsf{n}\to 1
can be carried out explicitly to yield finally
the entanglement asymmetry:

\Delta\mathcal{S}_{{\mathrm{U}(1)}^{\quantity[p]}}\bigl(\rho_{\theta}\bigr)=\log\Theta\quantity\big(q_\epsilon)-q_{\epsilon}\log q_{\epsilon}\;\frac{\Theta^{\prime}\quantity(q_\epsilon)}{\Theta\quantity(q_\epsilon)}~,

(3.29)

where
\Theta^{\prime}
denotes the derivative of the Jacobi theta constant with respect to its argument. Let us briefly comment on the case
d=2p+2
. The clustering states analogue to
equation

3.18
have two sums, one over electric and one over magnetic charges, with different angles
\theta_{\text{e}}
and
\theta_{\text{m}}
. Denote the regularised state
\ket{\theta_\t{e}, \theta_\t{m}}_{\epsilon}
. In the large volume limit for
p\geqslant 1
these states are degenerate and the moduli space is a torus instead of a circle. However, acting with the electric symmetry still generates a circle
U_{\alpha}\ket{\theta_\t{e}, \theta_\t{m}}_{\epsilon}=\ket{\theta_\t{e} + \alpha, \theta_\t{m}}_{\epsilon}
and the corresponding entanglement asymmetry is the one we just computed.

3.2.1
Various physical limits

Despite its simplicity, the model has many available knobs we can adjust to probe interesting physics. The behaviour of entanglement asymmetry as we tune the various parameters at their extremes uncovers distinct physical phenomena. We take the chance here to briefly explain some of them. In what follows, we dial one parameter at a time, keeping all other parameters fixed at finite values. Multi-scaling limits could of course be explored as well, but we will not pursue them here.

Very hot and very cold.

The regulated states
\rho_{\theta}
are pure, however their symmetrisation (
3.26
) is thermal in the Hilbert space of primary states, with a temperature

T=\frac{1}{\beta}=\frac{1}{2\epsilon R}~.

(3.30)

We can thus draw a connection between asymmetry and thermal physics.
In the limit
\epsilon\to 0
, indeed,
\rho_{\theta,\mathsf{S}}
is a highly excited state, probing all of the primary states
\ket{k}
, even those with increasingly large energy as
\absolutevalue{k}
grows. It explores the full non-linearly realised
\mathrm{U}(1)^{[p]}
symmetry, and the asymmetry reflects this as

\Delta\mathcal{S}_{{\mathrm{U}(1)}^{\quantity[p]}}\,\sim\,\frac{1}{2}\log T\,+\,\text{const}~,\qquad\quad\mbox{for}\quad T\quad\text{large}~.

(3.31)

Indeed, since the seed state
\rho_{\theta}
is pure, the entanglement asymmetry exactly reduces to the von Neumann entropy of
\rho_{\theta,\mathsf{S}}
. The behaviour in (
3.31
) is therefore entirely natural: for thermal states the von Neumann entropy coincides with the thermodynamic entropy, and at high temperature the “partition function”
\Theta(q_{\epsilon})
reduces to that of a free one-dimensional gas with
\beta=2\epsilon R
since
q_{\epsilon}=\exp( - \beta\, E_{k=1})
.

In the opposite limit, when we “cool down the system” by taking
\epsilon\to\infty
, only the true ground state — unique at finite volume — contributes. Indeed:

\Delta\mathcal{S}_{{\mathrm{U}(1)}^{\quantity[p]}}\to 0~,\qquad\quad\mbox{for}\quad T\quad\text{small}~.

(3.32)

From a path-integral perspective, this behaviour is of course unsurprising, as this low temperature limit corresponds to a very long Euclidean time evolution.

We note in passing that the limit
\mathsf{n}\to\infty
, conjectured to define a (semi) universal entropic quantity
[
59
]
, has a simple physical meaning in this model. To see this, recall that for Rényi entropies the limit
\mathsf{n}\to\infty
simply gives

\lim_{\mathsf{n}\to\infty}\mathcal{S}_{\mathsf{n}}(\rho)=-\log\lambda_{\text{max}}~,

(3.33)

where
\lambda_{\text{max}}
is the largest eigenvalue of
\rho
. Since in our setup the symmetrised state is thermal, the largest eigenvalue is just
\lambda_{\text{max}}=1/\Theta(q_{\epsilon})
from the vacuum. Hence, the entanglement asymmetry in this limit takes the form:

\Delta\mathcal{S}_{\infty}=\log\Theta\quantity\big(q_\epsilon)~.

(3.34)

To connect this result with the broader problem, recall that partition functions of
p
-form gauge theories factorise into instanton and oscillator contributions
[
112
,
113
]
(cfr. also
sections

4
and
5
). The corresponding thermal partition functions were computed in
[
99
,
100
]
, where it was shown that the instanton sector takes precisely the form of a Jacobi theta constant — or, more generally, a Siegel theta function (see again
sections

4
and
5
). Reconciling this with our present result, we conclude that the
\mathsf{n}\to\infty
Rényi asymmetry captures the instanton free energy of
p
-form gauge theory on
S^{p}_{r}\times S^{d-p-1}_{R}
:

\Delta\mathcal{S}_{\infty}=\log\mathcal{Z}^{\text{inst}}\quantity[\S^1_\beta\times\S^p_r\times\S^{d-p-1}_R]~.

(3.35)

Very large and very small (target).

Another useful control parameter is the coupling constant
\lambda
. Upon rescaling the
p
-form gauge field
{a}_{p}
to canonically normalise its kinetic term, one sees that
\lambda
controls its periods, or equivalently, the radius of the
\mathrm{U}(1)
target space. Hence, the limit
\lambda\to\infty
corresponds to a noncompact gauge theory with gauge group
\mathbb{R}
. The entanglement asymmetry tracks this decompactification:

\Delta\mathcal{S}_{{\mathrm{U}(1)}^{\quantity[p]}}\,\sim\,\frac{1}{2}\log\lambda\,+\,\text{const}~,\qquad\quad\mbox{for}\quad\lambda\gg 1~.

(3.36)

In this limit the symmetrised state is maximally mixed, and we may infer that the dimension of the Hilbert space of primary states grows as

\dim\mathcal{H}_{\text{primary}}\overset{\lambda\to\infty}{\sim}\sqrt{\lambda}~,

(3.37)

characterising the growing degeneracy of low-energy modes in the noncompact theory.

Conversely, the limit
\lambda\to 0
describes a somewhat exotic theory — a gauge field without holonomies
[
114
]
. Here we find

\Delta\mathcal{S}_{{\mathrm{U}(1)}^{\quantity[p]}}\to 0~,\qquad\quad\mbox{when}\quad\lambda\to 0~.

(3.38)

In this limit all charged states are too heavy to be excited and thus decouple. Only the symmetry-preserving vacuum, with
k=0
, survives inside
\rho_{\theta}
and hence the asymmetry vanishes.

Finally, one may tune the size of the spatial slice. Since this connects directly to the question of spontaneous symmetry breaking, we will treat it in detail in the next section.

3.3
The Coleman–Mermin–Wagner theorem

Since are interested in spontaneous symmetry breaking, we need to take a large volume limit, which as explained in
section

3.1
consists in taking:

\mu R\to\infty~,\qquad\quad\mbox{with}\quad\mu r\quad{\text{fixed}}~.

(3.39)

This limit yields different results, depending on
d
and
p
. We distinguish three cases.

•

\boldsymbol{d>p+2\,.}
The state
\ket{\theta_\epsilon}
becomes an energy eigenstate with zero energy. Explicit computation of the
\mu R\to\infty
limit yields
18
18

18

In computing this limit the modular property of the Jacobi theta constant is handy:

\Theta\bigl(\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{\pi i}{\tau}}\bigr)=(-i\tau)^{\frac{1}{2}}\,\Theta\quantity(\ex{\pi\ii\tau})~.

(3.40)

\Delta\mathcal{S}_{{\mathrm{U}(1)}^{\quantity[p]}}(\rho_{\theta})\,\sim\,\frac{d-p-2}{2}\,\log(\mu R)\,+\,{\text{const}}~,

(3.41)

where the additive constant collects the finite subleading contributions from
r,\lambda
and
\epsilon
. A subtlety arises if
d=2p+2
(and
p>0
) because the theory is conformal. In this case
\mu
does not exist and the relevant limit is
R/r\to\infty
. In that limit one finds:

\Delta\mathcal{S}_{{\mathrm{U}(1)}^{\quantity[p]}}(\rho_{\theta})\,\sim\,\frac{p}{2}\,\log\frac{R}{r}\,+\,{\text{const}}~.

(3.42)

We conclude that for
d>p+2
entanglement asymmetry is nonvanishing and diverges with the radius
R
of the spatial
(d-p-1)
-sphere. Since
\ket{\theta_\epsilon}
is a ground state, this signals spontaneous breaking of the
{\mathrm{U}(1)}^{\quantity[p]}
symmetry.

For later comparison, we note that the coefficient of the logarithmic divergence is
\frac{d-p-2}{2}
times the number of broken generators, a fact that becomes manifest when one considers, for instance, a
\mathrm{U}(1)^{N}
gauge group.

•

\boldsymbol{d=p+2\,.}
In this critical case,
\mu R
drops out of the expression of the energy levels and the large volume limit has no effect. Furthermore,
\ket{\theta_\epsilon}
are not energy eigenstates. To ensure that
\ket{\theta_\epsilon}
are ground states, we notice that the regulator in
equation

3.21
is implementing a Euclidean time evolution for a time
\tau=\epsilon R
. Such an evolution will project to a ground state if
\epsilon RE_{k=1}\gg 1
, where
E_{k=1}
is the energy gap, see
equation

3.15
. We thus need

\epsilon\gg\frac{2\lambda}{(\mu r)^{p}}~.

(3.43)

Under this assumption, the limit trivialises and one finds

\Delta\mathcal{S}_{{\mathrm{U}(1)}^{\quantity[p]}}(\rho_{\theta})\,\to\,0~.

(3.44)

We conclude that there is a unique ground state with vanishing entanglement asymmetry. This implies that, for
d=p+2
, the
{\mathrm{U}(1)}^{\quantity[p]}
symmetry is not spontaneously broken.

•

\boldsymbol{d<p+2\,.}
As in the previous case,
\ket{\theta_\epsilon}
is not an energy eigenstate and a Euclidean evolution is needed to project onto a ground state. In this case we need

\epsilon\gg\frac{2\lambda}{(\mu r)^{p}\,(\mu R)^{p+2-d}}~,

(3.45)

which is automatic in the large volume limit
\mu R\to\infty
. Under this assumption, one finds again a vanishing entanglement entropy. We conclude that, for
d<p+2
, the
{\mathrm{U}(1)}^{\quantity[p]}
symmetry is not spontaneously broken.

We finish by summarising the essential point. For a free
p
-form gauge theory in
d
spacetime dimensions, quantised on
\Sigma=S_{r}^{p}\times S_{R}^{\,d-p-1}
, the electric
p
-form symmetry
{\mathrm{U}(1)}^{\quantity[p]}
is spontaneously broken in the

R\to\infty
limit if and only if
d>p+2
. This conclusion distils the analysis of the previous subsections and provides a direct proof of the
p
-form Coleman–Mermin–Wagner theorem, with entanglement asymmetry serving as an entropic order parameter.

3.4.1
Complete symmetry breaking

Consider a quantum field theory with a continuous non-Abelian symmetry given by a compact Lie group

If no additional light degrees of freedom are present, or if these decouple, the low energy dynamics is described by a non-linear

In the interest of simplicity, we consider here the principal chiral model, i.e., the NLSM with bi-invariant metric on the group manifold

For the time being, we restrict attention to

The Peter--Weyl theorem

\boldsymbol{d>2\,.}

We conclude that the entanglement asymmetry of the ground state diverges with the size of the system, signalling the spontaneous breaking of

•

\boldsymbol{d=2\,.}
There is no scale
\mu

\Delta\mathcal{S}_{\mathsf{n},G}\rightarrow 0\qquad\qquad\text{for $R$ large but $R\lesssim\Lambda_{\text{IR}}^{-1}$}~.

(3.68)

This result is valid at large volume, but the volume cannot be taken arbitrarily large while remaining at weak coupling. The wary reader is thus vindicated: the asymmetry vanishes up to a certain large distance

, but it cannot be computed perturbatively at larger distances. The principal chiral model is known to be gapped with a single vacuum at infinite volume, therefore the entanglement asymmetry should vanish, but again, this cannot be determined perturbatively. Note that the Abelian case does not suffer from this limitation: it is Gaussian and the higher modes are trivially decoupled.

•

\boldsymbol{d=1}\,.
The low-energy effective theory is already the quantum rigid rotor,

3.49

(3.69)

\Delta\mathcal{S}_{\mathsf{n},G}\to 0~,

(3.70)

in agreement with the uniqueness of the vacuum in quantum mechanics.

Partial symmetry breaking

is the Laplacian on the homogeneous space
G/H
, with a left-invariant metric under
G
. It is simpler to work directly in position basis, i.e., to construct the states analogue to
\ket{g}
. Following
[
128
]
, these states are described as

\ket{xH}=\int_{H}\differential{h}\ket{x\,h}~,

(3.72)

where
x\in G
, so
xH
are the left cosets of
H
in
G
. It is immediate to see that the right action of
H
is trivial, while the left action of
G
is unaffected. These are the symmetry-breaking states. The regulated trace-normalised symmetry-breaking density matrix in this case is:

\rho_{x}=\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\epsilon R\,H_{0}}\outerproduct{xH}{xH}\mathrm{e}^{-\epsilon R\,H_{0}}}{\Theta_{G/H}\quantity(q_\epsilon)}~,

(3.73)

where

\Theta_{G/H}\quantity(q_\epsilon)=\matrixelement{xH}{\ex{- 2 \epsilon R\, H_0}}{xH}~,

(3.74)

with
q_{\epsilon}
as above.

The Rényi asymmetries for this symmetry-breaking pattern read straightforwardly:

\Delta\mathcal{S}_{\mathsf{n},G/H}\quantity(\rho_x)=\frac{1}{1-\mathsf{n}}\,\log\,\frac{\Theta_{G/H}\quantity(q_\epsilon^\n)}{\Theta_{G/H}\quantity(q_\epsilon)^{\mathsf{n}}}~.

(3.75)

It is not too difficult to obtain an explicit expression of
\Theta_{G/H}(q_{\epsilon})
following the technology developed in
[
128
]
and the Peter–Weyl theorem for
G/H

[
129
]
. However, since we are interested in the large volume limit
\mu R\to\infty
, we do not need such an expression. It suffices to notice that
\Theta_{G/H}
is a heat kernel and that (in
d>2
) the infinite volume limit corresponds to a short time expansion of the heat kernel. As such, we have

\Theta_{G/H}(q_{\epsilon})\,\sim\,C_{G/H}\quantity( \frac{ \lambda\, (\mu R)^{d-2} }{ \epsilon} )^{\!\frac{\dim(G/H)}{2}}

(3.76)

at
\mu R\gg 1
, and
C_{G/H}
is a constant. Hence, with
d>2
and in the infinite volume limit:

\Delta\mathcal{S}_{\mathsf{n},\,G/H}(\rho_{x})\,\sim\,\frac{(d-2)\dim(G/H)}{2}\,\log(\mu R)\,+\,{\text{const}}~.

(3.77)

A similar behaviour of asymmetry, featuring the coefficient
\dim(G/H)
, was reported in lattice models for matrix product states
[
39
]
. For
d\leqslant 2
the discussion is identical to the one for
G\to\quantity{1}
and we refer the reader to it.

We close this section by giving a unified perspective on our results. First, we have proven in the Abelian case, and provided evidence for in the non-Abelian case, that entanglement asymmetry provides a rigorous entropic version of the CMW theorem. Moreover, whenever SSB is allowed (for
d>p+2
),
\Delta\mathcal{S}
exhibits a characteristic behaviour summarized as:

\Delta\mathcal{S}_{{G}^{\quantity[p]}}=\frac{\mathcal{N}\,(d-p-2)}{2}\,\log(\mu R)~,\qquad\quad\mbox{as}\quad\mu R\gg 1~,

(3.78)

were
\mathcal{N}
is the number of broken generators of
{G}^{\quantity[p]}
in the symmetry broken phase. In the 0-form case, and under the assumption that the effective theory of the spontaneously-broken phase is Lorentz invariant,
\mathcal{N}
coincides with the number of Goldstone modes. This behaviour was conjectured by Metlitski and Grover
[
84
]
to capture the logarithmic contribution of Goldstone fields to entanglement entropy. Here we have shown that entanglement asymmetry isolates precisely this term, and moreover we have confirmed the conjecture of
[
84
]
in the 0-form case. Extending this reasoning to the higher-form case, it becomes evident that what this coefficient is actually capturing is broken generators, rather than the individual gapless degrees of freedom — which one would naturally associate with the number of Goldstone modes
[
130
]
. The latter would count polarisations of the
p
-form gauge field and would have given
\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}d-2\\
p\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)\,\mathcal{N}
instead.

4
Subregion theorem for
\mathrm{U}(1)
0-form symmetry

As explained in the introduction, entropic order parameters (here entanglement asymmetry) have a key advantage over conventional diagnostics: they can probe symmetry breaking within a spatial subregion. This section makes that concrete by computing the entanglement asymmetry of a compact scalar field in
d
spacetime dimensions in a subregion
\mathcal{A}
. We will draw the ensuing conclusions in the form of a subregion CMW theorem. Our computation is closely related to the computation of entanglement entropy, which was carried out for
d=3
in
[

. In

(p+1)
-form gauge fields that describe the low-energy dynamics of spontaneously-broken

To set the stage, consider a compact scalar field whose action is the
p=0

equation

3.1
:

S[\phi]=\frac{\lambda\,\mu^{d-2}}{2}\int_{X_{d}}{f}_{1}\wedge\star\,{f}_{1}~.

(4.1)

Here we have denoted by
{f}_{1}=\differential{\phi}
the field strength of the compact scalar, which is periodically identified
\phi\sim\phi+2\pi
and taken canonically dimensionless. As before,
\lambda
is a dimensionless coupling constant and
\mu
an arbitrary energy scale. From a sigma model perspective,
(\lambda\,\mu^{d-2})^{1/2}
is the dimensionful target-space radius (when
d=2
,
\mu
does not appear in the Lagrangian and
\lambda^{1/2}
is the dimensionless radius). We will be interested in the
\mathrm{U}(1)
shift symmetry

\phi\rightarrow\phi+c~.

(4.2)

Let us quantise this theory on a trivial spatial slice
\Sigma=\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
. We prepare a global pure state
\rho\propto\outerproduct{\theta}{\theta}
on
\Sigma
by specifying Dirichlet boundary conditions at infinity:
23
23

23

The same state can be prepared by a Euclidean path integral on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with an insertion of an operator
\mathcal{O}_{\theta}=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathrm{e}^{ik\theta}V_{k}
, where
V_{k}
are the vertex operators of the theory
[
103
]
.

\phi(x)\to\theta\quad\mbox{as}\quad\absolutevalue{x}\to\infty~.

(4.3)

Without loss of generality we choose
\theta=0
henceforth. Note that while the boundary conditions break the
\mathrm{U}(1)
symmetry, this is not enough to guarantee SSB. Indeed, in two dimensions, even when the field is fixed at a point, long-range order is destroyed by fluctuations as one moves away from that point

80
]
.

We then restrict our state to a subregion. For simplicity, we choose a tractable, yet rich enough, setup. We take our subregion to be a ball of radius
R

\mathcal{A}=\mathbb{B}^{d-1}_{R}\subset\Sigma
. On this subregion we compute the entanglement asymmetry of the resulting reduced state
\rho_{\mathcal{A}}
. This leads ultimately to one of the core results in this paper,
the subregion CMW theorem
.

Along the way, we extend known results on the entanglement entropy of compact scalars to the settings relevant to our analysis. Before diving into the computation, we briefly collect a few elementary but recurring ingredients that will streamline what follows.

4.1
Partition functions and winding sectors

Suppose one is interested in the partition function of the theory defined by
equation

4.1
on a generic spacetime manifold
X_{d}
. In our applications
X_{d}
will be either
\mathbb{R}^{d}
or the replica manifold (see
section

2
) and we will be interested in cases where
X_{d}
has a boundary. In those cases, one must specify boundary conditions compatible with the variational principle: either Dirichlet,
\evaluated*{\f{f}{1}}_{\pd X_d}=0
, or Neumann,
\evaluated*{\qty(\star\f{f}{1})}_{\pd X_d}=0
.
24
24

24

We do not include the possibility of edge modes. Allowing them enlarges the set of admissible boundary conditions, see e.g.
[
132
]
.

We proceed using Dirichlet boundary conditions, as these will turn out to be relevant in the actual computation, and we briefly comment on the other choice in the end.

As in the case without boundary (which we explored in
section

3
), one may split
{f}_{1}
as

{f}_{1}={f}_{1}^{(h)}+\differential{\phi}~.

(4.4)

Here
\phi
is a globally-defined noncompact scalar, while
{f_{1}}^{\!(h)}
is a harmonic contribution whose role is to capture the windings.
25
25

25

Two field configurations of
\phi(x)
that differ by a global shift of
2\pi
are considered gauge equivalent.
With the chosen boundary conditions,
{f_{1}}^{\!(h)}
is a representative of the relative cohomology
H^{1}(X_{d},\partial X_{d})
(with Neumann boundary conditions it would be
H^{1}(X_{d})
). See also
appendix

A
for details. When
X_{d}
has no boundary, the discussion reduces to that of
section

3
.

Using the splitting
equation

4.4
of the field strength and the orthogonality between harmonic and exact forms, the partition function on
X_{d}
factorises,

\mathcal{Z}[X_{d}]=\mathcal{Z}^{\text{inst}}[X_{d}]\;\mathcal{Z}^{\text{osc}}[X_{d}]~,

(4.5)

where
\mathcal{Z}^{\text{inst}}[X_{d}]
captures the sum over
{f}_{1}^{\text{(h)}}
(the winding sectors/instantons), while
\mathcal{Z}^{\text{osc}}[X_{d}]
is the oscillator contribution from
\phi
. Explicitly:

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}^{\text{inst}}[X_{d}]

\displaystyle=\int\fdifferential{{f}_{1}^{(h)}}\,\exp(-\frac{\lambda\, \mu^{d-2}}{2}\int_{X_d} \f{f}{1}^{(h)} \w\star\, \f{f}{1}^{(h)})\quad\mbox{and}\quad

(4.6)

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}^{\text{osc}}[X_{d}]

\displaystyle=\int\fdifferential{\phi}\;\exp(-\frac{\lambda\, \mu^{d-2}}{2}\int_{X_d} \dd{\phi}\w\star\dd{\phi})~.

(4.7)

The oscillator piece is a standard one-loop determinant that is invariant under shift symmetry and will therefore play no role in the computations below. The instanton contribution deserves closer attention.

By flux quantisation, the path integral over winding sectors reduces to a discrete sum. Let
\quantity{\tau_i}
be a basis of Dirichlet harmonic 1-forms on
X_{d}
(i.e., such that
\tau_{i}\big|{}_{\partial X_{d}}=0
and with integer periods) with
i=1,2,\ldots,b_{1}
and where
b_{1}=\dim H^{1}(X_{d},\partial X_{d})
. For details, see
appendix

A
.
Then
{f_{1}}^{\!(h)}
is expressed as

{f}_{1}^{(h)}=2\pi w^{i}\tau_{i}~,\qquad w^{i}\in\mathbb{Z}~,

(4.8)

with implicit summation over
i
. The integers
w^{i}
label the winding numbers on each (relative) cycle and are summed over in the path integral. The instanton action, appearing in the exponent of
equation

4.6
, becomes

S^{\text{inst}}=\frac{\lambda\,\mu^{d-2}}{2}\int_{X_{d}}{f}_{1}^{(h)}\mathbin{\scalebox{0.8}{$\wedge$}}\star\,{f}_{1}^{(h)}=2\pi^{2}\lambda\,\mu^{d-2}\;w^{i}\,\mathbb{M}_{ij}\,w^{j}~,

(4.9)

where the matrix
\mathbb{M}_{ij}
denotes the Gram matrix of this basis:

\mathbb{M}_{ij}=\int_{X_{d}}\tau_{i}\mathbin{\scalebox{0.8}{$\wedge$}}\star\,\tau_{j}~.

(4.10)

We will refer to
\mathbb{M}_{ij}
as the
instanton matrix
of
X_{d}
. In
appendix

B
we perform a careful computation of
\mathbb{M}_{ij}
for the relevant replica geometry by translating it to an equivalent problem of calculating a capacitance matrix in electrostatics, building upon
[
131
]
.

With
\mathbb{M}_{ij}
specified, the instanton partition function becomes:

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}^{\text{inst}}[X_{d}]=\sum\nolimits_{\begin{subarray}{c}w^{i}\,\in\,\mathbb{Z}\\[0.81949pt]
i=1,\ldots,b_{1}\end{subarray}}\,\exp(-2\pi^2 \lambda\, \mu^{d-2}\; w^i\, \bbM_{ij}\, w^j)=\Theta\quantity( 2\pi\lambda\, \mu^{d-2} \, \bbM_{ij})~.

(4.11)

In the last step we introduced the Siegel theta function. For a strictly positive-definite symmetric
n\times n
matrix
\mathbb{A}
, it is defined as:

\Theta(\mathbb{A})=\sum\nolimits_{\boldsymbol{w}\,\in\,\mathbb{Z}^{n}}\,\mathrm{e}^{-\pi\,\boldsymbol{w}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbb{A}\,\boldsymbol{w}}~.

(4.12)

Finally, we note that in trivial topology
{f}_{1}=\differential{\phi}
globally and hence
\mathcal{Z}^{\text{inst}}[X_{d}]=1
.

4.2
Rényi entropies of a compact scalar in
d
dimensions

We build up towards entanglement asymmetry by first computing the Rényi entropies of a compact scalar field on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Our analysis follows and extends
[
131
]
, which treated the case
d=3
in detail and sketched partial results for general
d
. Consider the subregion
\mathcal{A}=\mathbb{B}^{d-1}_{R}
. We aim to evaluate the Rényi entropies:

\mathcal{S}_{\mathsf{n}}\coloneqq\frac{1}{1-\mathsf{n}}\,\log\frac{\tr(\rho_\aent^\n)}{(\tr\rho_{\mathcal{A}})^{\mathsf{n}}}~.

(4.13)

Here the traces of
\rho_{\mathcal{A}}
and its powers are computed via a Euclidean path integral, as explained in
section

2.1
. Owing to the factorisation in
equation

4.5
, the Rényi entropies naturally decompose into winding and oscillator contributions:

\mathcal{S}_{\mathsf{n}}=\mathcal{S}_{\mathsf{n}}^{{\text{inst}}}+\mathcal{S}_{\mathsf{n}}^{{\text{osc}}}~.

(4.14)

As explained earlier, for our purposes it will suffice to compute the winding contribution alone. With that in mind, we evaluate

\mathcal{S}_{\mathsf{n}}^{{\text{inst}}}=\frac{1}{1-\mathsf{n}}\,\log\frac{\mathcal{Z}^{{\text{inst}}}[X_{\mathsf{n}}]}{\quantity(\parti^{\t{inst}}[X_1])^{\mathsf{n}}}~.

(4.15)

The geometry in question is the replica manifold
X_{\mathsf{n}}
. It is constructed by taking
\mathsf{n}
copies of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and cyclically gluing them along the
\mathsf{n}
balls
\mathbb{B}^{d-1}_{R}
, as illustrated in
figure

1
. The result is an
\mathsf{n}
-fold branched cover of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, whose branch locus is the entangling surface
\partial\mathcal{A}=S^{d-2}_{R}
. Note here that
X_{\mathsf{n}}
possesses
\mathsf{n}
asymptotic boundaries — which we shall denote as
B_{i}
(
i=1,\ldots,\mathsf{n}
) — one on each sheet. We will refer to the full asymptotic boundary of
X_{\mathsf{n}}
as

B_{\text{asym}}=B_{1}\sqcup\cdots\sqcup B_{\mathsf{n}}~.

(4.16)

Further details on the topology of
X_{\mathsf{n}}
are provided in
appendix

A
.

Figure 1:
A cartoon of the replica manifold
X_{\mathsf{n}}
. The coloured discs denote the location of the branch cuts
\mathbb{B}_{R}^{d-1}
, which are identified cyclically.

As discussed in
section

2.1
, we must regulate the entangling surface by excising a tubular neighbourhood of radius
\epsilon
around it. This procedure introduces an additional
entanglement boundary
, on top of the asymptotic boundaries already present on the replica manifold:

B_{\text{ent}}=S^{1}_{\mathsf{n}\epsilon}\times S^{d-2}_{R}~.

(4.17)

On the entanglement boundary we impose boundary conditions chosen to ensure a clean factorisation of the Hilbert space. At this stage, for the computation of Rényi entropies, these are given by Dirichlet or Neumann boundary conditions. Both are equally valid. However, once we turn to the calculation of Rényi asymmetries, there is an additional requirement: they must allow the involved topological operators to end topologically on the entanglement boundary. Such conditions are referred to as symmetric boundary conditions in the literature.

In general,
\mathcal{S}_{\mathsf{n}}
has divergent contributions as the cutoff
\epsilon
is removed
[
59
]
. However, as we show in
appendix

C
, the winding contribution remains well behaved in this limit. Therefore, from here on, we take
\epsilon\to 0
and remove the cutoff. In addition, we need to impose boundary conditions on the asymptotic boundaries. Specifically, in accordance with the discussion at the beginning of this section, we impose

\phi\big|_{B_{i}}=0

(4.18)

at each asymptotic boundary
B_{i}
. This choice is consistent with the replica construction. In particular, since
\mathcal{Z}[X_{\mathsf{n}}]=\tr(\rho_\aent^\n)
and
\rho_{\mathcal{A}}=\tr_{\mathcal{A}^{\prime}}\outerproduct{0}{0}
, the field
\phi
must take the same value across all asymptotic boundaries.

For consistency, imposing Dirichlet conditions for
\phi
also enforces Dirichlet conditions on the harmonic piece
{f_{1}}^{\!(h)}
. This allows us to compute the winding contribution using the formalism developed earlier. Topologically,
X_{\mathsf{n}}
is a
d
-dimensional sphere with
\mathsf{n}
balls excised (corresponding to the
\mathsf{n}
asymptotic boundary components). This geometry harbours
\mathsf{n}-1
non-trivial relative 1-cycles that connect those balls. Central to the computation is the
(\mathsf{n}-1)\times(\mathsf{n}-1)
instanton matrix
\mathbb{M}_{\mathsf{n},d}
associated with this geometry. This matrix turns out to have a surprisingly interesting structure, as we explain in
section

B.2
. Its components are given by:

\quantity[\bbM_{\n,d}]_{j\ell}=\frac{1}{\mathsf{n}}\,\sum_{k=1}^{\mathsf{n}-1}\,\mathrm{e}^{2\pi i\frac{(j-\ell)k}{\mathsf{n}}}\,C_{d}\quantity(\frac{k}{\n})~.

(4.19)

The function
C_{d}\quantity(k/\n)
is computed by solving the Laplace equation in
d
dimensions with monodromies that depend on
k
along the entangling surface. We devote
sections

B.4
,
B.5
and
B.6
to this computation. When the dust settles we find:

C_{d}\left(\frac{k}{\mathsf{n}}\right)=\frac{2^{3-d}\,\pi^{d/2}\,\Gamma\Bigl(\frac{d-1}{2}\Bigr)^{\!2}}{\Gamma\Bigl(\frac{d}{2}\Bigr)^{\!2}\,\Gamma\Bigl(\frac{d}{2}-1\Bigr)}\;u^{\mathsf{T}}\quantity[\mathbf{1} + \cot[2](\frac{\pi\, k}{\n})\, T^{(d)}]^{-1}u~,

(4.20)

where

T^{(d)}
are defined in
sections

B.4
,
B.5
and
B.6
. In
[
131

, an expression for
C_{d}\left(\frac{k}{\mathsf{n}}\right)
in

was proposed:

C_{3}\left(\frac{k}{\mathsf{n}}\right)=-4\left(1-\frac{2\,k}{\mathsf{n}}\right)\tan(\frac{\pi\,k}{\n})~.

(4.21)

We do not have a closed formula for other
d
, but numerical evaluation is possible, see
section

B.6
. Let us proceed, since we will see that most of our results do not depend on the form of
C_{d}\left(\frac{k}{\mathsf{n}}\right)
. From the above, we see that something special happens in
d=2
: the instanton matrix reduces identically to the zero matrix. This signals that all instanton configurations contribute equally and are unsuppressed, each carrying a vanishing action. We will argue in
section

4.4

Proceeding with the computation, we now have all the ingredients to determine the instanton partition function. It reads:

\mathcal{Z}^{\text{inst}}[X_{\mathsf{n}}]=\Theta\quantity(2\pi\lambda\, (\mu R)^{d-2} \, \bbM_{\n,d})~.

(4.22)

Note here the explicit dependence on the radius

\mathcal{A}

\mathcal{S}_{\mathsf{n}}^{\text{inst}}=\frac{1}{1-\mathsf{n}}\,\log\Theta\quantity( 2\pi\lambda\, (\mu R)^{d-2} \, \bbM_{\n,d})~.

(4.23)

It is sometimes useful to rewrite
equation

4.23
in a different way. A natural generalisation of
equation

3.40
yields

\Theta\bigl(\mathbb{A}\bigr)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\det\mathbb{A}}}\;\Theta\quantity(\inv{\bbA})~.

(4.24)

Using this identity we get the equivalent expression:

\mathcal{S}_{\mathsf{n}}^{\text{inst}}=\frac{1}{2\,(\mathsf{n}-1)}\log\det( 2\pi\lambda\, \qty(\mu R)^{d-2} \,\bbM_{\n,d})+\frac{1}{1-\mathsf{n}}\log\Theta\quantity( \frac{1}{2\pi\lambda\, (\mu R)^{d-2}} \, \bbM_{\n,d}^{-1})~.

(4.25)

4.2.1
Simplifying limits and entanglement entropy

While
equations

4.23
and
4.25
give exact expressions for the Rényi entropies, they can be rather unwieldy when it comes to extracting explicit dependence on the size of the subregion. More significantly, they offer little help in computing the entanglement entropy itself, because analytically continuing a Siegel theta function is generally intractable. Nevertheless, each of
equations

4.23
and
4.25
is useful in different limiting regimes of the subregion radius
R
.

In the regime
\mu R\gg 1
, we use the asymptotic behaviour

\lim_{s\to\infty}\Theta(s\mathbb{A})=1

(4.26)

to deduce, from
equation

4.23
, that the instanton contribution to the Rényi entropy vanishes:

\mathcal{S}_{\mathsf{n}}^{\text{inst}}\to 0~,\qquad\quad\mbox{as}\quad\mu R\to\infty~.

(4.27)

In contrast, in the opposite regime
\mu R\ll 1
, we appeal to
equation

4.25
which gives:

\mathcal{S}_{\mathsf{n}}^{\text{inst}}\sim\frac{1}{2}\log(2\pi\lambda\, \qty(\mu R)^{d-2})+\frac{1}{2\,(\mathsf{n}-1)}\log\det\mathbb{M}_{\mathsf{n},d}~.

(4.28)

Using the explicit structure of
\mathbb{M}_{\mathsf{n},d}
from
equation

4.19
, we obtain:

\mathcal{S}_{\mathsf{n}}^{\text{inst}}\sim\frac{d-2}{2}\,\log(\mu R)\,+\,{\text{const}}~,

(4.29)

where the additive constant depends on the details of the function
C_{d}
. These limits give us a handle on the entanglement entropy, since in both regimes the continuation
\mathsf{n}\to 1
is straightforward. Altogether, we find:

\mathcal{S}^{\text{inst}}=\lim_{\mathsf{n}\to 1}\,\mathcal{S}_{\mathsf{n}}^{\text{inst}}\sim\begin{cases}0~,&\mu R\gg 1~,\\
\frac{d-2}{2}\log(\mu R)\,+\,{\text{const}}~,&\mu R\ll 1~.\end{cases}

(4.30)

Note that
\mathcal{S}^{{\text{inst}}}
is negative for all
\mu R
. This is easy to see at
\mu R\ll 1
, while for general
\mu R
it follows from monotonicity of the Siegel theta function. This feature is not a cause for concern: the full entanglement entropy of the theory is
\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{S}^{{\text{inst}}}+\mathcal{S}^{{\text{osc}}}
, where
\mathcal{S}^{{\text{osc}}}
denotes the oscillator contribution. As discussed above, the oscillator contribution must be computed in the presence of a UV regulator and typically diverges as a power of the cutoff. Consequently,
\mathcal{S}^{{\text{inst}}}
is always subleading. In this light,
\mathcal{S}^{{\text{inst}}}
is best seen as a negative universal subleading correction, much like the topological entanglement entropy

[
17
,
18
]
in topologically ordered phases.

4.3
Rényi asymmetries of a compact scalar in
d
dimensions

We now turn to the central object of interest for us: the Rényi asymmetries, defined in
equation

2.9
as

\Delta\mathcal{S}_{\mathsf{n},\mathrm{U}(1)}=\mathcal{S}_{\mathsf{n},\mathsf{S}}-\mathcal{S}_{\mathsf{n}}~.

(4.31)

Here
\mathcal{S}_{\mathsf{n},\mathsf{S}}
denotes the contribution from the symmetrised state
\rho_{\mathcal{A},\mathsf{S}}
:

\mathcal{S}_{\mathsf{n},\mathsf{S}}=\frac{1}{1-\mathsf{n}}\,\log\mathcal{Z}_{\mathsf{n},\mathsf{S}}=\frac{1}{1-\mathsf{n}}\,\log\tr(\rho_{\aent,\sfS}^\n)~.

(4.32)

This relation also defines the symmetrised partition function
\mathcal{Z}_{\mathsf{n},\mathsf{S}}
. In terms of the charged moments
\mathcal{E}_{\mathsf{n}}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})
defined in
equation

2.11
, the symmetrised partition function takes the form

\mathcal{Z}_{\mathsf{n},\mathsf{S}}=\,\prod_{i=1}^{\mathsf{n}}\,\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{\differential{\alpha_i}}{2\pi}\;\mathcal{E}_{\mathsf{n}}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})~.

(4.33)

To evaluate the charged moments, we proceed as follows. We begin by inserting symmetry operators, as shown schematically in
equation

2.17
, into the path integral computed in the previous subsection. It is important to note that, in order to define the charged moments as in
equation

2.17
, it was essential to regulate the entangling surface and impose symmetric boundary conditions there.

Symmetric boundary conditions require that the topological operators

U_{\alpha}(\gamma)=\exp(\ii\alpha\int_\gamma\star\, \f{f}{1})

(4.34)

can end topologically on the entangling boundary. It is then evident that this requirement is satisfied precisely by imposing Neumann boundary conditions on
B_{\text{ent}}
. Once this is done, the topological operators may be smoothly deformed so that they encircle the punctures corresponding to the asymptotic boundaries. Following the discussion in
section

\Delta\mathcal{S}_{\mathsf{n}}=\frac{1}{\mathsf{n}-1}\,\log\Theta\quantity( \frac{1}{2\pi\lambda\, (\mu R)^{d-2}} \, \bbM_{\n,d}^{-1})~.

Let us close with a few comments. We have evaluated the Rényi asymmetries for a spherical subregion. However, the conceptual picture remains unchanged for subregions

4.4
The theorem

We now return to the subtle case of
d=2
and examine the fate of asymmetries in two dimensions. As established above, in this case all components of the instanton matrix vanish identically. It follows that the instanton partition function
\mathcal{Z}^{\text{inst}}\quantity[X_\n]
is left invariant by the symmetriser and entanglement asymmetry vanishes. Since
\mathcal{Z}^{\text{inst}}\quantity[X_\n]
is formally divergent, though, let us be more careful by reintroducing the regulating tube
\mathbb{B}^{2}_{\mathsf{n}\epsilon}\times S^{d-2}_{R}
around the entangling surface, always taken with Neumann boundary conditions for the reasons discussed earlier. Its role here is to regularise the instanton contribution:

\mathcal{Z}^{\text{inst}}\quantity[X_\n]=\Theta\quantity( 2\pi\lambda\, (\mu R)^{d-2} \, \epsilon^2 \, \bbA_\n)~,

(4.48)

where
\mathbb{A}_{\mathsf{n}}
is an
(\mathsf{n}-1)\times(\mathsf{n}-1)
matrix with entries of order one, encoding the corrections arising from the entanglement boundary, such as
equation

C.10
. With
\epsilon\ll 1
we then obtain, to leading order in
\epsilon
:

\mathcal{Z}^{\text{inst}}\quantity[X_\n]=\quantity[\det( 2\pi\lambda\, (\mu R)^{d-2} \, \epsilon^2\, \bbA_\n)]^{-1/2}~.

(4.49)

By the same token, and from eqn.
equation

4.41
, the symmetrised partition function
\mathcal{Z}_{\mathsf{n},\mathsf{S}}^{\text{inst}}
is given by the same expression. Hence, their ratio is unity and we find

\Delta\mathcal{S}_{\mathsf{n},\mathrm{U}(1)}=0

(4.50)

in
d=2
, for any value of the radius
R
. Finally, in
d<2
the situation trivialises. Now we do not have the possibility of a meaningful subregion, as the full spatial slice is at most a collection of points. For any subregion (a collection of fewer points) we can appeal to
section

3
and conclude that
\Delta\mathcal{S}_{\mathsf{n},\mathrm{U}(1)}=0
in this case too.

This result allows us to formulate a sharp, entropic version of the CMW theorem. Phrased loosely, it states that no matter how one tunes the system in an attempt to realise spontaneous symmetry breaking (SSB), it is simply not allowed in
d\leqslant 2
.
Specifically, we consider a theory describing a would-be Goldstone boson. If the symmetry were spontaneously broken, this would be the effective theory of the light degrees of freedom. We then prepare a global pure state on
\Sigma=\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
by imposing boundary conditions that explicitly break the symmetry,
\phi(x)\to\theta
as
\absolutevalue{x}\to\infty
.
Next, we restrict attention to a subregion
\mathcal{A}=\mathbb{B}_{R}^{d-1}
and compute the Rényi asymmetries of the reduced state
\rho_{\mathcal{A}}
. As explained above, these asymmetries provide a faithful measure of spontaneous symmetry breaking: if SSB were present, the Rényi asymmetries would detect it. And we find that

\Delta\mathcal{S}_{\mathsf{n},\mathrm{U}(1)}(R)=\begin{cases}0~,&d\leqslant 2~,\\[6.0pt]
\dfrac{1}{\mathsf{n}-1}\,\log\Theta\quantity(\dfrac{\bbM_{\n,d}^{-1}}{2\pi\lambda\, (\mu R)^{d-2}})~,&d>2~,\end{cases}

(4.51)

from which the following statements immediately follow:

•

Even when restricting attention to a finite portion of spacetime, spontaneous symmetry breaking does not occur in
d\leqslant 2
.

•

When SSB does occur (for
d>2
), the expression
equation

4.51
provides a quantitative entropic measure of its strength within a subregion.

•

The degree of symmetry breaking is monotonically increasing with the size of the subregion. Equation
equation

4.51
interpolates smoothly and monotonically between the two limiting values,

\displaystyle\Delta\mathcal{S}_{\mathsf{n},\mathrm{U}(1)}(0)

\displaystyle=0~,\quad\mbox{and}\quad

(4.52)

\displaystyle\Delta\mathcal{S}_{\mathsf{n},\mathrm{U}(1)}(R)

\displaystyle\sim\,\frac{d-2}{2}\,\log(\mu R)~,\qquad R\gg{{\mu}^{-1}}~.

(4.53)

(5.17)

It follows immediately that the integrated charged moments take the form

\mathcal{Z}_{\mathsf{n},\mathsf{S}}=\quantity[\det(\frac{2\pi\lambda(\mu R)^{d-p-2}}{ (\mu r)^{p}} \bbM_{\n,d-p})]^{-\frac{1}{2}}~.

(5.18)

Finally, substituting these quantities into the definition
equation

2.9
yields the entanglement asymmetry of
p
-form Maxwell theory,

\Delta\mathcal{S}_{\mathsf{n}}=\frac{1}{2(\mathsf{n}-1)}\log\det(\frac{2\pi\lambda(\mu R)^{d-p-2}}{ (\mu r)^{p}} \bbM_{\n,d-p})+\frac{1}{\mathsf{n}-1}\log\Theta\quantity(\frac{2\pi\lambda(\mu R)^{d-p-2}}{ (\mu r)^{p}} \bbM_{\n,d-p})~,

(5.19)

or equivalently:

\Delta\mathcal{S}_{\mathsf{n}}=\frac{1}{\mathsf{n}-1}\log\Theta\quantity(\frac{ \qty(\mu r)^p}{2\pi\lambda(\mu R)^{d-p-2}}\bbM_{\n,d-p}^{-1})~.

(5.20)

5.3
The theorem

In
d=p+2
dimensions, the same important remark arises as in the zero-form case at
d=2
: the matrix
\mathbb{M}_{\mathsf{n},d}^{(p)}=\mathbb{M}_{\mathsf{n},d-p}
vanishes identically. The resolution is identical in spirit. One must again fatten the entangling region to
\mathbb{D}^{2}_{\mathsf{n}\epsilon}\times\partial\mathcal{A}
in order to regulate the calculation. Upon removing the cutoff, it follows that

\Delta\mathcal{S}_{\mathsf{n},{\mathrm{U}(1)}^{\quantity[p]}}=0~,

(5.21)

in
d=p+2
dimensions. Similarly, the asymmetries also vanish identically in
d<p+2
.

This result brings us naturally to the final expression for the Rényi asymmetries of higher-form symmetries. We find

\Delta\mathcal{S}_{\mathsf{n},{\mathrm{U}(1)}^{\quantity[p]}}=\begin{cases}0~,&d\leqslant p+2~,\\[6.0pt]
\dfrac{1}{\mathsf{n}-1}\log\Theta\quantity(\dfrac{ \qty(\mu r)^p}{2\pi\lambda(\mu R)^{d-p-2}}\bbM_{\n,d-p}^{-1})~,&d>p+2~.\end{cases}

(5.22)

In short, this computation confirms that even when restricted to a finite subregion, the higher-form analogue of the CMW theorem
[
1
,
81
]
continues to hold: a continuous
p
-form symmetry cannot undergo spontaneous breaking in spacetime dimensions
d\leqslant p+2
. All further consequences of
equation

4.51
in the zero-form case — such as the monotonic growth of symmetry breaking with the subregion size — carry over verbatim to the higher-form setting encapsulated by
equation

5.22
. We therefore refrain from repeating them here and refer the reader to the discussion in
section

4
.

In the limit
\mu R\gg 1
, where the entangling region effectively covers the entire spatial slice, the entanglement spectrum flattens out — that is, the Rényi entropies become independent of the replica index
\mathsf{n}
. In this regime, extracting the entanglement asymmetry is straightforward. For
d>p+2
, we find

\Delta\mathcal{S}_{{\mathrm{U}(1)}^{\quantity[p]}}=\frac{d-p-2}{2}\log(\mu R)+{\text{const.}}~,\qquad\mu R\gg 1

(5.23)

while, as expected, it vanishes identically for
d<p+2
. For completeness, in the case that
d=2p+2
, where the theory becomes conformal, the above limit ceases to apply. The appropriate scaling limit is instead
R/r\gg 1
, in which case the asymmetry becomes:

\Delta\mathcal{S}_{{\mathrm{U}(1)}^{\quantity[p]}}=\frac{d-p-2}{2}\log(\frac{R}{r})+{\text{const.}}~,\qquad\frac{R}{r}\gg 1~.

(5.24)

The additive constant (slightly different between the generic and conformal cases) denotes a subleading correction, dependent on the geometry of the entangling surface
\partial\mathcal{A}
and on the coupling
\lambda
. In all instances, this asymptotic regime happily reproduces the results of
section

3
, where the entanglement asymmetry was computed for the entire spatial slice.

6
Discussion

In this work we have defined entanglement asymmetry for higher-form symmetries, and explored its behaviour in phases with spontaneously broken continuous invertible
p
-form symmetries. Along the way we
encountered a number of entropic statements about symmetry breaking and the renormalisation group. There are several natural directions to extend our analysis.

A first, technical generalisation concerns the definition of higher-form
entanglement asymmetry itself, introduced in
section

2
. Throughout this work we assumed, for simplicity, that the spatial slice has no torsion in its homology. For many physical settings this is a harmless assumption, but torsional cycles are known to carry subtle information. In supersymmetric gauge theories, for example, they are sensitive to the global structure of the gauge group
[
135
]
. It would therefore be interesting to understand whether entanglement asymmetry can diagnose more delicate features of symmetry-breaking patterns by probing such torsional cycles. There is a natural extension of our definition that incorporates torsion. In the main text, we reduced all sums over
H_{d-p-1}(\Sigma;\mathrm{U}(1))
to integrals over
\mathrm{U}(1)^{b_{p}(\Sigma)}
. However, in the presence of torsion, the universal coefficient theorem gives
H_{d-p-1}(\Sigma;\mathrm{U}(1))\cong\mathrm{U}(1)^{b_{p}(\Sigma)}\oplus\operatorname{Tor}\quantity(\H_{d-p-2}(\Sigma;\Z))
(non-canonically). Thus, in our definition of entanglement asymmetry
equation

2.6
, we would have a sum over torsional defects, on top of the integrals we considered here. We expect this to be the correct generalisation of our proposal, though we have not attempted a detailed analysis here. We leave an understanding of the role of torsion cycles in entanglement asymmetry as an open question.

A second natural extension concerns the behaviour of entanglement asymmetry for continuous non-Abelian symmetries when evaluated on subregions. In this work we restricted our non-Abelian analysis to the full spatial slice, where the construction is comparatively straightforward. For subregions one expects the entanglement asymmetry to approach the value for the global state (computed in
section

3.4
) as the region becomes large, while in the opposite limit of small subregions it should vanish, reflecting the symmetry restoration in the UV. Understanding the details of this crossover would be interesting.

A more ambitious programme is to understand entanglement asymmetry for more general symmetry structures. One natural direction is that of higher-group symmetries
[
136
,
137
]
. This case is especially interesting, as such systems exhibit a hierarchy of symmetry-breaking scales
[
136
]
. It would be interesting to understand how entanglement asymmetry detects this, particularly in light of its interpretation as an RG monotone. A closely related arena is that of noninvertible symmetries, where analogous hierarchies of breaking patterns are known
[
138
,
139
,
140
]
. In several examples the associated Goldstone theory is already understood
[
136
,
141
]
, opening the door to a similar analysis to the one developed here. Along these lines, continuous noninvertible (higher-form) symmetries — appearing, for
instance, in the orbifold branch of a compact scalar or in
\mathrm{O}(2)
gauge theory — offer another fertile testing ground.

We hope that our work has convinced the reader that entanglement asymmetry — and, more broadly, entropic order parameters — are of great interest for quantum field theory. Surprisingly, we have only started uncovering their possibilities. We leave the further exploration of their applications as an invitation to the interested reader.

Appendix A
The topology of the replica manifold

In this appendix we collect some basic topological facts about the replica manifold
X_{\mathsf{n}}
introduced in
section

4
. Recall that
X_{\mathsf{n}}
is defined as the
\mathsf{n}
-fold branched cover of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with branch cut along a unit
(d-1)
-ball. Topologically this space is equivalent to a
d

\mathsf{n}

\mathsf{n}

\mathsf{n}

d

For the calculation of Rényi entropies, we require its homology and relative homology groups. For our purposes real coefficients will suffice. The absolute homology follows directly from the observation that
X_{\mathsf{n}}
is homotopically equivalent to a wedge sum of
(\mathsf{n}-1)

(d-1)

X_{\mathsf{n}}\cong S^{d}\smallsetminus\quantity{\B^d_1 \sqcup\cdots\sqcup\B^d_\n}\overset{{\text{\tiny hom}}}{\simeq}\bigvee^{\mathsf{n}-1}S^{d-1}~.

(A.1)

From this equivalence the homology groups are immediate:

H_{k}\quantity(X_\n)=\begin{cases}\mathbb{R}&k=0~,\\
0&0<k<d-1~,\\
\mathbb{R}^{\mathsf{n}-1}&k=d-1~,\\
0&k=d~.\end{cases}

(A.2)

We now turn to the relative homology of the replica manifold with respect to its boundary
\partial X_{\mathsf{n}}=S^{d-1}_{1}\sqcup\cdots\sqcup S^{d-1}_{\mathsf{n}}
. A standard route is to use the relative homology long exact sequence:

\cdots\to H_{k}(\partial X_{\mathsf{n}})\to H_{k}(X_{\mathsf{n}})\to H_{k}(X_{\mathsf{n}},\partial X_{\mathsf{n}})\to H_{k-1}(\partial X_{\mathsf{n}})\to\cdots~,

(A.3)

but in our case it is much simpler to relate relative homology to reduced homology:

H_{k}(X_{\mathsf{n}},\partial X_{\mathsf{n}})=\widetilde{H}_{k}(X_{\mathsf{n}}/\partial X_{\mathsf{n}})~,

(A.4)

where
\widetilde{H}_{k}
denotes the reduced homology, and the quotient
X_{\mathsf{n}}/\partial X_{\mathsf{n}}
is the space obtained from
X_{\mathsf{n}}
by collapsing its boundary to a single point. See
[
142
]
for details. Noting that

X_{\mathsf{n}}/\partial X_{\mathsf{n}}\overset{{\text{\tiny hom}}}{\simeq}S^{d}\vee\bigvee^{\mathsf{n}-1}S^{1}~,

(A.5)

we immediately obtain:

H_{k}(X_{\mathsf{n}},\partial X_{\mathsf{n}})=\begin{cases}0&k=0~,\\
\mathbb{R}^{\mathsf{n}-1}&k=1~,\\
0&1<k<d~,\\
\mathbb{R}&k=d~.\\
\end{cases}

(A.6)

For later use, we also record the cohomology groups, obtained by Poincaré–Lefschetz duality:

H_{k}(X_{\mathsf{n}})\cong H^{d-k}(X_{\mathsf{n}},\partial X_{\mathsf{n}})\quad\quad\mbox{and}\quad\quad H_{k}(X_{\mathsf{n}},\partial X_{\mathsf{n}})\cong H^{d-k}(X_{\mathsf{n}})~.

(A.7)

Thus we have:

H^{k}(X_{\mathsf{n}})=\begin{cases}\mathbb{R}&k=0~,\\
0&0<k<d-1~,\\
\mathbb{R}^{\mathsf{n}-1}&k=d-1~,\\
0&k=d~.\end{cases}\qquad\quad H^{k}\quantity(X_\n,\pd X_\n)=\begin{cases}0&k=0~,\\
\mathbb{R}^{\mathsf{n}-1}&k=1~,\\
0&1<k<d~,\\
\mathbb{R}&k=d~.\\
\end{cases}

(A.8)

Finally, recall that on manifolds with boundary the de Rham picture requires some care. It is no longer true that
\opbraces{\kernel}\Delta=\opbraces{\kernel}\differential\cap\opbraces{\kernel}\differential^{\dagger}

[
143
]
. Following
[
144
]
, we refer to forms in
\opbraces{\kernel}\differential\cap\opbraces{\kernel}\differential^{\dagger}
as strongly harmonic, and we distinguish two natural boundary conditions:

\displaystyle\operatorname{Harm}_{\text{D}}^{k}(M)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\quantity{\omega\in\Omega^k(M) \suchthat\dd\omega= 0 ~,\;\; \cdd\omega= 0 ~,\ \t{and} \;\; \omega\big|_{\pd M} = 0}

(A.9)

\displaystyle\operatorname{Harm}_{\text{N}}^{k}(M)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\quantity{\omega\in\Omega^k(M) \suchthat\dd\omega= 0 ~,\;\; \cdd\omega= 0 ~,\ \t{and} \;\; \star\, \omega\big|_{\pd M} = 0}~.

(A.10)

These are the Dirichlet and Neumann (strongly) harmonic forms, respectively. One has the identifications
[
143
]

\operatorname{Harm}_{\text{D}}^{k}(M)\cong H^{k}(M,\partial M)\quad\quad\mbox{and}\quad\quad\operatorname{Harm}_{\text{N}}^{k}(M)\cong H^{k}(M)~.

(A.11)

Applied to the replica manifold, this means:

•

one Neumann harmonic 0-form: the constant function,

•

(\mathsf{n}-1)
Dirichlet harmonic 1-forms:
\tau_{i}
,
i=1,\cdots,\mathsf{n}-1
, normalised against a basis of relative one-cycles
\quantity{\alpha_i}
by

\int_{\alpha_{i}}\tau_{j}=\delta_{ij}~.

(A.12)

•

(\mathsf{n}-1)
Neumann harmonic
(d-1)
-forms. A basis of them is given by
\star\,\tau_{i}
.

•

one Dirichlet harmonic top-form: the volume form
\opbraces{\volume}_{X_{\mathsf{n}}}=\star\,1

Appendix B
The instanton matrix from electrostatics

In this appendix we derive useful expressions for the matrix
\mathbb{M}_{\mathsf{n},d}
that appears in the main text (in particular in

5

Let
\quantity{\tau_i}_{i=1}^{\mathsf{n}-1}

appendix

A
. Define
\mathbb{M}_{\mathsf{n},d}
to be the Gram matrix of the above basis:

\quantity[\bbM_{\n,d}]_{ij}\coloneqq\int_{X_{\mathsf{n}}}\tau_{i}\mathbin{\scalebox{0.8}{$\wedge$}}\star\,\tau_{j}~.

(B.1)

By the general discussion of

4
and
5

\mathbb{M}_{\mathsf{n},d}

B.1
An equivalent electrostatics problem

It is helpful to map the above calculation to a capacitance problem in electrostatics. Denote each of the asymptotic boundaries of

as

,

and consider

(

\Delta v_{i}=0\quad\mbox{with boundary conditions}\quad\evaluated{v_i}_{B_j}=\begin{cases}\delta_{ij}~,&j=1,\ldots,\mathsf{n}-1~,\\
0,&j=n~.\end{cases}

(B.2)

Here
\Delta
denotes the scalar Laplacian on
X_{\mathsf{n}}
. The
\mathsf{n}
-th boundary acts as “ground”, reflecting that
X_{\mathsf{n}}
carries only
\mathsf{n}-1
non-trivial one-cycles.

We may then define the one-forms
\tau_{i}
as

\tau_{i}\coloneqq\differential{v_i}~,\qquad i=1,\ldots,\mathsf{n}-1~.

(B.3)

These are automatically closed. They are also coclosed, as
v_{i}
satisfy the Laplace equation. Furthermore they vanish on
\partial X_{\mathsf{n}}
, as
v_{i}
are constant on each boundary component of
X_{\mathsf{n}}
. Hence they form a basis of
H^{1}(X_{\mathsf{n}},\partial X_{\mathsf{n}})
.

X_{\mathsf{n}}
drawn as a

\mathsf{n}
boundaries, as explained in
appendix

. The boundaries, denoted by crossed circles get mapped to the conductors. Of these,

is grounded. The relevant basis,

Moreover, let
\quantity{\gamma_i}

to
B_{i}
as depicted in

. Then

(B.4)

and hence

With this identification the desired matrix reads

\displaystyle=\int_{X_{\mathsf{n}}}\tau_{i}\mathbin{\scalebox{0.8}{$\wedge$}}\star\tau_{j}=\int_{X_{\mathsf{n}}}\differential{v_i}\mathbin{\scalebox{0.8}{$\wedge$}}\star\differential{v_j}=\int_{\partial X_{\mathsf{n}}}v_{i}\mathbin{\scalebox{0.8}{$\wedge$}}\star\differential{v_j}

where we integrated by parts and used the constancy of

B.2
Unfolding and the structure of
\mathbb{M}_{\mathsf{n},d}

To completely determine the matrix
\mathbb{M}_{\mathsf{n},d}
we must solve the Laplace problem posed in
equation

B.2
. This task becomes considerably more tractable if we “unfold” the problem to an equivalent one on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

We begin by contracting the harmonic basis vectors
v_{i}
with an arbitrary constant vector
\beta^{i}
(with
i=1,\dotsc,n-1
), to avoid carrying free indices. This yields a harmonic scalar field:

v_{\beta}\coloneqq\beta^{i}v_{i}:X_{\mathsf{n}}\to\mathbb{R}~,

(B.6)

subject to the following boundary conditions on the asymptotic components of the replica manifold:

\evaluated{v_\beta}_{B_i}=\beta^{i}~\quad\mbox{and}\quad\evaluated{v_\beta}_{B_\n}=0~.

(B.7)

Now, let
x
be a point in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
(excluding the branch locus), and denote by
x_{j}
its image on the
j
-th sheet of
X_{\mathsf{n}}
. We then define a set of complex scalar potentials on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
:

\widetilde{v}_{k}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{C},

(B.8)

indexed by
k=1,\ldots,n
, given explicitly by:

\widetilde{v}_{k}(x)\coloneqq\frac{1}{\mathcal{N}_{k}}\sum_{j=1}^{\mathsf{n}}\mathrm{e}^{2\pi i\frac{jk}{\mathsf{n}}}v_{\beta}(x_{j}),\quad\mbox{with}\quad\mathcal{N}_{k}\coloneqq\sum_{j=1}^{\mathsf{n}-1}\mathrm{e}^{2\pi i\frac{jk}{\mathsf{n}}}\beta^{j}~.

(B.9)

Each
\widetilde{v}_{k}
is thus a harmonic function on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and is held at unit potential at the asymptotic boundary:

\evaluated{\widetilde{v}_k}_{\S^{d-1}_\infty}=1~.

(B.10)

Let us now observe a few key properties of the potentials
\widetilde{v}_{k}
. First, by construction, each
\widetilde{v}_{k}
satisfies the Laplace equation on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. In the replicated geometry, traversing from one sheet to the next amounts to:

v_{\beta}(x_{j})\mapsto v_{\beta}(x_{j+1})~.

(B.11)

In the unfolded picture, this induces a monodromy in the complex potential:

\widetilde{v}_{k}(x)\mapsto\mathrm{e}^{-2\pi i\frac{k}{\mathsf{n}}}\widetilde{v}_{k}(x)~,

(B.12)

upon encircling the unit
S^{d-2}
. It follows immediately that
\widetilde{v}_{\mathsf{n}}
is invariant under this transformation and hence trivial. We also define:

C_{d}\quantity(\frac{k}{\n})=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\quantity(\dd{\widetilde{v}_k})^{*}\mathbin{\scalebox{0.8}{$\wedge$}}\star\differential{\widetilde{v}_k}~,

(B.13)

which represents the energy stored in the field
\widetilde{v}_{k}
. By the same reasoning used in the previous section, this integral can be equivalently evaluated as a flux through the boundary at infinity:

C_{d}\quantity(\frac{k}{\n})=\int_{S^{d-1}_{\infty}}\star\differential\widetilde{v}_{k}~.

(B.14)

We now have all the ingredients needed to determine the matrix
\mathbb{M}_{\mathsf{n},d}
. On the one hand, by definition,

\int_{X_{\mathsf{n}}}\differential{v_\beta}\mathbin{\scalebox{0.8}{$\wedge$}}\star\differential{v_\beta}=\quantity[\bbM_{\n,d}]_{j\ell}\beta^{j}\beta^{\ell}~.

(B.15)

On the other hand, by unfolding the integral across the sheets:

\displaystyle\int_{X_{\mathsf{n}}}\differential{v_\beta}\mathbin{\scalebox{0.8}{$\wedge$}}\star\differential{v_\beta}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{\mathsf{n}}\int_{{\text{$j$-th sheet}}}\differential{v_\beta(x_j)}\mathbin{\scalebox{0.8}{$\wedge$}}\star\differential{v_\beta(x_j)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mathsf{n}}\sum_{k=1}^{\mathsf{n}-1}\absolutevalue{\cN_k}^{2}C_{d}\quantity( \frac{k}{\n})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j,\ell=1}^{\mathsf{n}-1}\quantity[\frac{1}{\n}\sum_{k=1}^{\n-1} \ex{2\pi i \frac{(j-\ell)k}{\n}} C_d\qty(\frac{k}{\n})]\beta^{j}\beta^{\ell}~.

(B.16)

In the final step, we have again used that
\widetilde{v}_{\mathsf{n}}
is trivial and does not contribute. Since
\beta^{j}
is arbitrary, it follows that the elements of the instanton matrix are given by:

\quantity[\bbM_{\n,d}]_{j\ell}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{\mathsf{n}-1}\mathrm{e}^{2\pi i\frac{(j-\ell)k}{\mathsf{n}}}C_{d}\quantity(\frac{k}{\n})~.

(B.17)

Let us also offer an alternative viewpoint. The matrix
\mathbb{M}_{\mathsf{n},d}
as obtained in
equation

B.17
has the structure of a symmetric Toeplitz matrix. More precisely it can be viewed as the leading principal submatrix of an
\mathsf{n}\times\mathsf{n}
symmetric circulant matrix. This is precisely the form expected for the capacitance matrix in cyclic arrangements of conductors
[
147
]
. Grounding one of the conductors, thereby reducing the number of independent potentials, we recover exactly our
\mathbb{M}_{\mathsf{n},d}
.

Let us pause to take stock of what happened. We have shown that solving the Laplace problem on
X_{\mathsf{n}}
as in
equation

B.2
is equivalent to solving the following problem on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
:

\Delta\widetilde{v}_{k}=0\quad\mbox{with}\quad\widetilde{v}_{k}\mapsto\mathrm{e}^{-2\pi i\frac{k}{\mathsf{n}}}\widetilde{v}_{k}\quad\mbox{around the unit $S^{d-2}$.}\quad

(B.18)

In short: we have traded a partial differential equation on a complicated geometry with simple boundary conditions, for one on flat space with a non-trivial monodromy.

Hyperfunky coordinates

In the following section we will go on to solve the Laplace problem we set up above. That problem will simplify greatly, upon introducing the correct coordinate system. We take a moment here to do so.

Appendix C
Shrinking the entanglement boundary

In this appendix we argue that the winding/instanton contribution to the replica partition function remains smooth upon shrinking the entanglement boundary. As a reminder, in order to compute this contribution, one needs to evaluate the instanton matrix
\mathbb{M}_{\mathsf{n}}
, i.e. the induced metric on
H^{1}(X_{\mathsf{n}},\partial X_{\mathsf{n}})
. Here we show that this matrix depends smoothly on the length,
\epsilon
, of the entanglement boundary,
B_{\text{ent}}
, as
\epsilon\ll 1
.

We begin by noting that
B_{\text{ent}}
introduces an additional relative one-cycle in the homology
H_{1}(X_{\mathsf{n}},\partial X_{\mathsf{n}})
, on which we impose “clear-cut” boundary conditions
[
59
]
. Those are given by Dirichlet or Neumann BCs. Let us examine each case separately.

First consider the case of Dirichlet BCs

{f}_{1}\Big|_{B_{\text{ent}}}=0~,

(C.1)

and denote by
\mathbb{M}_{\mathsf{n}}
the instanton matrix of
X_{\mathsf{n}}
prior to regulating. After introducing the cutoff, the relevant matrix is the Gram matrix of
H^{1}(X_{\mathsf{n}},\partial X_{\mathsf{n}})
. It takes the augmented form:

\widetilde{\mathbb{M}}_{\mathsf{n}}=\matrixquantity(\lx@physics@matrix\bbM_\n& \vec{m} \\[4pt] \vec{m}^\sfT& \widetilde{m}\endlx@physics@matrix)~.

(C.2)

The new entries, the vector
\boldsymbol{m}
and the number
\widetilde{m}
, arise due to the cutoff and depend on
\epsilon
.
28
28

28

Strictly speaking,
\mathbb{M}_{\mathsf{n}}
also receives corrections from the introduction of the cutoff. However these are subleading and vanish in the limit
\epsilon\to 0
, so we neglect them for this argument.

Our aim is to show that both
\boldsymbol{m}
and
\widetilde{m}
vanish in the
\epsilon\to 0
limit.

In
section

B.1
we reinterpret
\mathbb{M}_{\mathsf{n}}
as the capacitance matrix of a system of
\mathsf{n}
conducting
(d{-}1)
-spheres. Once the regulating surface is introduced, the problem becomes that of
\mathsf{n}
spherical conductors alongside a very thin, toroidal conductor: the excised tube
D^{2}_{\mathsf{n}\epsilon}\times S^{d-2}_{R}
. Thus,
\widetilde{m}
corresponds to the self-capacitance of this toroidal capacitor. Given that
\epsilon\ll R
, the geometry is effectively cylindrical. In
d=3
, this is nothing but a coaxial cable, whose capacitance behaves as:

\widetilde{m}_{d=3}\,\propto\,\frac{1}{\log\epsilon}\quad\mbox{as}\quad\epsilon\to 0~.

(C.3)

For
d>3

\widetilde{m}_{d>3}\,\propto\,\epsilon^{d-3}\quad\mbox{as}\quad\epsilon\to 0~.

(C.4)

The

\widetilde{m}_{d=2}\,\propto\,\frac{1}{\log\epsilon}\quad\mbox{as}\quad\epsilon\to 0~.

(C.5)

Hence, in all cases, the self-capacitance

\absolutevalue{m_i}\leqslant\widetilde{m}\,[\mathbb{M}_{\mathsf{n}}]_{ii}\to 0\quad\mbox{as}\quad\epsilon\to 0~.

(C.6)

In other words, we see that with Dirichlet BCs, the instanton matrix reduces effectively to

\widetilde{\mathbb{M}}_{n,d}=\matrixquantity(\lx@physics@matrix\bbM_{\n,d} & \vec{0} \\[4pt] \trans{\vec{0}} & 0\endlx@physics@matrix)

in the limit
