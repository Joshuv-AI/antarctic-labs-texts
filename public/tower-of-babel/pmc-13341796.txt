Whole genome resequencing reveals the genetic basis of stature in short-statured Indian cattle
Authors: Sheikh Firdous Ahmad, Ovais Aarif, Mir Mehroz Hassan, Roshni Chand, Munish Gangwar, Sarath Kumar T, Amit Kumar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIndia possesses a rich diversity of indigenous cattle that are well adapted to varied agro-climatic regions. These populations exhibit remarkable variation in stature (height at withers), ranging from short-statured types such as Vechur, Punganur, Malnad Gidda, and Khariar to tall and heavy breeds like Kankrej, Ongole and other milch breeds (Sahiwal, Gir, etc.). The short-statured breeds offer potential advantages in feed efficiency, disease resilience, and cultural value besides being economical to maintain. However, their genetic basis for stature remains underexplored. This study leverages whole-genome resequencing (WGS) data on short-statured (n = 19) and tall (Kankrej as representative; n = 19) Indian cattle to delineate the copy number variation (CNV) landscape and selection signatures underpinning stature divergence. Post-quality control, CNVs were detected from duplicate-marked bam files using CNVnator with read-depth methodology, filtered (q0 < 0.5, p < 0.01, size 1 kb–5 Mb), and concatenated into CNV regions (CNVRs). Selection signatures were identified using cross population extended haplotype homozygosity (XP-EHH) methodology for inter-population comparison of short-statured cattle with tall cohort. Genes harboured under CNVRs and sweep windows were annotated using GALLO, with functional mining from literature databases. In short-statured cattle, 41,913 CNVs were concatenated into 10,075 CNVRs, with 8.01% genomic coverage. A total of 25 genes were found to be common across two analyses i.e., unique (non-overlapping) CN regions in 70% short-statured individuals and scan of selection signature. Key genes across the analyses included IGF1R (cell proliferation), FGFR3 (skeletal growth), SOX6 (body size), EXT2/LGR4 (bone density), PRKCD (developmental regulation), ADAMTSL2 (extracellular matrix integrity), SLC25A6 (glucose metabolism), and SDHA (energy supply). Unique non-overlapping copy number regions (e.g., 78 regions found in 100% of dwarf individuals) harbored several genes, including ARL13B (osteogenesis), AXIN2 (bone remodeling), CCND2 (myogenesis), and TNNT1 (muscle contraction). This comprehensive CNV map and scan for signatures of selection unveil stature-associated genomic variants, informing conservation strategies for threatened short-statured breeds by enhancing their socio-economic value through targeted breeding. Introduction

India is home to a large livestock inventory, coupled with rich genetic diversity and a vast number of indigenous populations that are adapted to different agro-ecological zones1. The National Bureau of Animal Genetic Resources (ICAR-NBAGR), Karnal, India has registered 242 indigenous breeds of livestock and poultry species, including 55 cattle breeds (http://14.139.252.116:8080/appangr/openagr.htm). Each livestock population develops distinct adaptive characteristics in response to the specific agro-climatic zones under which it is reared, evident as a peculiar evolutionary contour that has shaped its genome and ultimately the phenotype. Indian cattle have significant place in its cultural and economic setup, playing a vital role in milk production, draught power, traditional practices, and providing job opportunities to marginal farmers. Indian cattle are categorized into different groups based on their production capabilities i.e., milch breeds like Sahiwal, Gir, Red Sindhi, and Tharparkar; dual purpose breeds like Hariana, Kankrej, and Deoni; and draught purpose breeds like Amritamahal, Kanagayam, Pullikulam and Khillari2. The Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO) and the International Society for Animal Genetics (ISAG) have emphasized the conservation of indigenous breeds, courtesy their genetic uniqueness, resilience, and adaptability traits3.

Apart from their functional classification, Indian cattle also exhibit significant variations with regard to their stature (height at withers). Certain breeds, such as Kankrej, Ongole, and Amritmahal are tall4, while several other breeds are short-statured such as Vechur, Punganur and Kasargod5,6. Due to their small size, short-statured breeds require minimal maintenance and fodder support, making them highly efficient in feed utilization and ideal for small-scale farming5. They are well adapted to harsh environments, particularly in hot and humid areas, and exhibit natural resilience to few tropical diseases4. In India, their cultural and socio-economic relevance is also significant, as many rural households consider them auspicious and rear them as family animals or pets7. Despite their small size, breeds like Vechur and Punganur produce milk with high-fat content, making them suitable for production of dairy products such as ghee8. Their milk is believed to possess medicinal properties, likely due to traditional feeding and higher lactoferrin levels as compared to heavier (large-sized) dairy breeds5. On the other hand, Kankrej is considered as one of the heaviest cattle breeds in India9.

Apart from Indian milch breeds, other cattle populations face tremendous challenge for their sustenance. The conservation value of these breeds with lower milk productivity can be improved by attaching certain attributes to their rearing. The short-statured breeds like Vechur, Punganur, Malnad Gidda and Khariar have recently attracted attention of the common masses who consider rearing these animals as family animals or pets. These short statured breeds are economical to rear for landless and marginal farmers as their maintenance requirements are quite lower than other heavier cattle populations, especially crossbreds that are reared across India. Improved conservation value can, in fact, prove to be a game changer in the conservation of these populations and encourage farmers for their rearing, besides improving their profitability. Investigating these populations is also important as any knowledge pertaining to their unique gene pool will add to their conservation value, besides leading to their propagation, especially in the era of rapidly changing agro-climatic trends.

According to national database of India for livestock maintained at ICAR-NBAGR (Karnal), the Indian cattle vary in stature with an average height at withers ranging from 90 to 147 cm (Accessed: April, 2025). The short-statured (like Malnad Gidda, Vechur, Lakhimi, Kasargod, Motu, Punganur, Konkan Kapila and Khariar) and tall (like Kankrej and Ongole) cattle breeds have developed unique genomic adaptations over millions of years of evolutionary contour to survive and thrive in their specific agro-climatic zones. The molecular markers such as single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs), microsatellites, and copy number variations (CNVs) play a pivotal role in identifying genetic determinants (structural and molecular variations) of various phenotypes10. Climate-mediated selective pressures have been reported to further influence species distributions, phenotypic variation, and allele frequency patterns11. However, different environmental factors such as temperature, precipitation, altitude, and solar radiation exert distinct effects on the extent and nature of genetic variations within species over the evolutionary timescale. These factors may help shape the distribution and frequency of CNVs, thereby influencing adaptive potential and the ability of populations to respond to environmental challenges12.

SNP markers can provide high-resolution genetic insights into the population structure and evolutionary dynamics, while CNVs can help elucidate large genomic alterations that contribute to stature variations. These genetic tools enable precise breed characterization, aiding in conservation and selective breeding efforts. On the similar lines, whole-genome resequencing (WGS) offers comprehensive insights into the genetic composition of an individual as compared to the conventional techniques like microsatellite markers or RAD-seq13. It is based on generation of high-throughput data across the entire genome, typically at coverage of ~10×-30×, producing millions of short reads that are subsequently aligned to the reference genome14. This enables simultaneous characterization of both functional and neutral genetic variants, facilitating the elucidation of genetic basis of diverse phenotypic (physiological and pathological) traits15. WGS generates information on dense genome-wide markers, including single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs), insertions and deletions (InDels), and structural variants such as chromosomal rearrangements and copy number variations (CNVs)16. It is particularly useful for detecting rare mutations17 and a broad spectrum of genetic variants and functional genes18 that influence key breed characteristics. In addition, WGS supports quantitative trait loci (QTL) mapping and comprehensive analyses of genetic diversity, population structure, and evolutionary dynamics19. Owing to its scale and resolution, WGS serves as a robust platform for variant discovery, identification of selection signatures18, and precise genotype–phenotype association studies20, thereby advancing genomic research and precision breeding strategies.

Despite the relevance of stature variation in Indian cattle, its genetic basis remains poorly understood. It was hypothesized in the present study that comparative integrated genomic analysis of short-statured breeds against taller counterpart(s) would help identify specific copy number variations (CNVs) and genomic regions under selection (selective sweeps), enriched for genes and pathways associated with reduced body size and other adaptive traits. While previous studies have predominantly focused on single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs), the combined influence of CNVs and selection signatures in regulating stature has not been systematically investigated through integrative genomic analyses. Therefore, the present study aimed to undertake comparative genomic analyses of short-statured Indian cattle in relation to a taller reference breed (Kankrej), with an emphasis on CNV mapping and selection signatures to address this critical research gap.

Materials and methods

Data and population

Two contrasting datasets were used in the present study, i.e., short-statured cattle (n = 19) and tall population (n = 19). The dataset included short statured cattle from multiple locations across India including Karnataka, Kerala, Assam, Odisha, Goa, Odisha, and Andhra Pradesh, along with tall-statured breed i.e., Kankrej from Gujarat (Fig. 1). The data were mined from multiple publications and repositories4,21,22. On the basis of distribution of Indian cattle breeds, the animals with average height at withers < 105 cm (< 3.5 ft.) were considered short-statured; the average height at wither (HAW) of Kankrej being 141.5 cm (> 4.64 ft.). To understand the genetic factors influencing stature in these cattle populations, it is crucial to compare breeds reared under similar environmental conditions and purposes. This approach shall help differentiate genetic determinants from environmental influences, providing a better understanding of the hereditary traits that govern variations related to stature in Indian cattle. Kankrej is considered as one of the heaviest cattle breeds of India; it was therefore chosen for different comparisons in the present study. Preprocessing, alignment and variant calling

Standardized tools and pipelines were used in the present study for alignment and variant calling. FastQC v0.12.0 (https://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/fastqc/; Accessed: April, 2025) was used to process the raw sequencing data for quality assessment, and TrimGalore v0.6.5 was used to remove adapters and low-quality reads23. FastQC v0.12.0 was used to re-evaluate the quality of the trimmed reads. The trimmed reads were aligned to the latest ARS-UCD2.0 domestic cattle reference genome assembly (GCA_002263795.4) following genome indexing. The Burrows-Wheeler Aligner (BWA) tool v0.7.17 was used with default parameters and multiple threads to execute the alignment of filtered reads against the reference genome24. SAMtools v1.1725 was then used to sort and index the binary alignment map (BAM) files. The mapped reads were then marked with SM read tags. ‘MarkDuplicates’ feature of Picard tools v2.25.1 (https://github.com/broadinstitute/picard/releases/) was used to identify the PCR duplicates from the BAM files (https://broadinstitute.github.io/picard/). Duplicate-free BAM files were used for elucidation of structural variants i.e., CNVs (see below).

The Genome Analysis Toolkit (GATK) v4.1.8.0 (https://gatk.broadinstitute.org/hc/en-us; Accessed: April 2025) was then utilized for standard variant calling. HaplotypeCaller function of GATK was utilised to delineate the genetic variants (SNP and InDels) from WGS data and reconstruct the haplotypes using probabilistic modelling on 38 cattle samples. Multiple variant call format (VCF) files were then combined together using the CombineVCF GATK function for further downstream analysis. Genomic VCF (GVCF) files were converted back into a regular VCF file using the GenotypeGVCFs function. A subset of SNP variants were extracted from the VCF file using the SelectVariants function of GATK. Subsequently, quality control (QC) was undertaken on genome-wide SNP data using VariantFiltration function in GATK. Variants with low confidence, mapping issues, or strand bias (if any) were eliminated using GATK, while applying thresholds based on expression and mapping quality. Quality depth (QD) score < 2, Phred-scaled genotype quality (QUAL) score < 30, mapping quality (MQ) score < 40, Fisher strand (FS) bias score < 60, strand odds ratio (SOR) score < 3, mapping quality rank sum test (MQRankSum) score < − 12.5, and a Read Position Rank Sum Test (ReadPosRankSum) score of < − 8 were among the various thresholds used for variant filtering. Post removal of InDels, the VCF dataset was processed for further QC in PLINK v1.926. SNP markers located on sex chromosomes and mitochondria, as well as those lacking specific coordinates on the cattle genome were eliminated. Furthermore, the current study employed the conventional quality control levels for a number of metrics, including genotyping rate (10% threshold), SNP call rate (10% threshold), minor allele frequency (5% threshold). SNP markers that provided redundant information due to their physical proximity close to each other on the corresponding chromosomes were eliminated after filtering for linkage disequilibrium (LD). The markers in high LD with a r2 estimate higher than 0.2 were pruned. Markers were grouped with a physical distance of 5 base pairs using a window size of 50 SNPs for LD pruning. Subsequent analyses were undertaken using the filtered dataset, which included independent (LD-pruned) and non-redundant markers.

CNV mapping and concatenation

The CNVnator program v.0.4.127 was used for CNV detection, based on read-depth (RD) methodology and analysis thereof. The basic principle of CNVnator is to divide the genome into user-specified non-overlapping bins (in multiples of 100) and subsequently count the mapped reads in each bin to get the RD signals. PCR duplicate-free BAM files (see above) were used to create copy number histograms for CNV call partitioning and statistical analysis in the current study. Bin sizes of 100–3000 bp (in multiples of 100) were used for initial CNV calling on per sample basis, while the ratio of the read depth to its variance was used to determine the optimal bin size, with ratio of 4–527. In order to remove the GC bias due to lowered depth coverage at GC-rich areas, wave correction was carried out within CNVnator program using reference files28. Subsequent to CNV detection, pruning and quality check were carried out using the thresholds on CNV size, zero mapping quality (q0), and p-value. In order to filter confident CNV calls only, the study used the multiple criteria: variations with mapping quality < 0.5, p-value determined by t-test statistics < 0.01, and the size of CNVs (within the range 1 kb to 5 Mb). The current study employed a q0 filter (fraction of mapped readings with zero quality) of 0.5, which indicates the level of confidence that a read originates from the site to which it is actually linked.

The overlapping CNVs (with at least one bp overlap) were concatenated into copy number variation regions (CNVRs) using a customized Python script. Depending on whether the events in the proximal CNVs were all deletion, all duplication, or a combination of deletion and duplication events, the merged CNVRs were labelled as deletion, duplication, or mixed, respectively. The RIdeogram package29 was utilised to plot the distribution of CNVRs against gene density using gene information file (gff file corresponding to the genome assembly ARS-UCD2.0). In brief, the gene density parameters were determined using a window of 1 Mb from the gff file that corresponded to the genome assembly utilised in the study. The assembly metadata was used to obtain the chromosomal coordinates. Finally, plotting of the CNVR data over the gene density parameters was undertaken in R-programming environment.

Signature (unique and non-overlapping) CNV regions in specific populations

The CNV data were also analyzed using BedTools ‘multiinter’ function for various downstream statistics. BedTools v2.26.030 was used to detect the common (having exactly the same chromosomal coordinates), overlapping (having at least one bp overlap), and unique CNVRs, based on the chromosomal coordinates of structural variants from various populations. Additionally, the genomic coverage of CNVRs across two cohorts (tall and short) was determined by comparing it with the reference genome assembly. Furthermore, the unique non-overlapping CNVRs secured with BedTools were assessed for elucidation across 10%, 20%, 30%, 40%, 50%, 70%, 90% and 100% of individuals. Additionally, CNVs as well as CNVRs were compared across two population groups i.e., short-statured and tall cattle.

Selection signature analysis

Genomic regions, subjected to positive selection within dwarf cattle breed, were identified using inter-population approach to capture selective sweeps. The cross-population extended haplotype homozygosity (XP-EHH) statistic was employed to compare dwarf cattle breed against reference populations (tall breed), highlighting loci under differential selection. The phased genome-wide SNP data were generated from VCF files using BEAGLE v5.231. The cross-population extended haplotype homozygosity (XP-EHH) statistic32 was employed in this study to identify selection fingerprints in comparison of two Indian cattle groups (short and tall statured). The extended haplotype homozygosity (EHH) profiles of the target and reference populations were compared in order to determine the regions under selection pressure. The haplotype-phased VCF files were used in the rehh package33,34 under R-programming environment to compare the genome-wide EHH estimates surrounding each SNP between two groups under comparison. The log ratio of the integrated EHH (iES) values from the two populations under comparison was represented by the unstandardised XP-EHH score for each SNP. The mean and dispersion (standard deviation) of all unstandardised scores (unXP-EHH) were used to standardise the XP-EHH scores. A two-sided logarithmic scale was then used to determine the p-values. The selective sweeps in observed and reference populations were denoted by positive and negative XP-EHH estimates, respectively. XP-EHH scores and matching p-values (logarithmic scale) were computed for each SNP on genome-wide scale. The results were plotted as Manhattan plots for visualisation.

Annotation of CNV regions and selective sweeps

Gene profiling of genomic regions, found to harbour CNVs or selection signatures associated with stature in different populations, was undertaken using GALLO package35. A mining approach was applied across several datasets to ascertain the functional significance of individual genes. In order to gain further insights into the functional significance of these genes, information was mined from PubMed and other relevant sources.

Results and discussion

Copy number variations (CNVs) scored across dwarf and tall cattle cohorts

Elucidation of CNVs and CNVRs have earlier been reported based on genotyping and WGS data in cattle36–38; buffaloes39; swine40; cat41; chicken42; sheep43; and geese44. Various factors including underlying data and sequencing platform (array, genotyping or sequencing), algorithms used, sample size, genomic coverage, sequencing depth/ marker density and population involved (in terms of its structure) affect the reliability of CNV calling and downstream analysis39,40,45,46. WGS promises advantages for detecting CNVs as it detects majority of breakpoints41,42.

Accurate detection of CNVs in WGS data relies on robust computational tools, and CNVnator has emerged as a leading method for this purpose. CNVnator is a read-depth–based tool specifically optimized for CNV detection and has been extensively validated in livestock genomics. Multiple studies have demonstrated its utility for sensitive and reliable elucidation of CNVs across a broad size spectrum; it has been reported to provide higher resolution than targeted sequencing or array-based approaches36,37,40–44,47. In the present study, genomes from dwarf population revealed 41,913 unique CNVs distributed across various chromosomes (including gonosomes) with 31,231 deletion and 10,682 duplication events. The sex chromosomes contributed 13,016 CNVs (11042 on X; 1974 on Y), accounting for 31.06% of the overall CNV load. Among all autosomes, BTau_15 exhibited the highest number of CNVs (2162), while BTau_22 showed the lowest (354). Across autosomes, 18,870 deletion and 10,023 duplication events were scored. Maximum deletions (1201) were present on BTau_15, while BTau_25 showed the least count (145). Similarly, maximum duplications (961) were present on BTau_15, while BTau_24 showed least duplication events (92). Copy number variation regions (CNVRs) in short-statured Indian cattle

Following the concatenation of overlapping CNVs (with at least one bp overlap), 10,075 distinct CNVRs were identified in short-statured cattle, distributed across various chromosomes (including sex chromosomes) with 7294 deletion, 2639 duplication and 142 mixed events. In the present study, the average CNVR length was found to be 0.022 Mb (22.06 kb) across all chromosomes. However, the average CNVR length across autosomes was 0.010 Mb (10.12 kb). The total genomic coverage of these CNVRs was approximately 8.27% . The sex chromosomes harboured 1562 CNVRs (1230 on X; 332 on Y), accounting for (15.50%) of the overall CNVR load. However, the autosomal coverage of CNVRs was 3.46% . The genomic coverage of CNVs across different studies has been reported to vary across different studies with individual estimates of 0.18%48, 0.71%49, 1.5%50, 2.36%51, 4.05%52, and 9.49%53 across different livestock species. Numerous studies have characterized the distribution and genomic landscape of CNVs and CNV regions (CNVRs) in cattle, revealing substantial variability in their number and genomic coverage54,55. For instance, recent WGS-based studies by Dang et al.56 reported 97,564 CNVs in Nanyang Cattle populations. Similarly, Zhong et al.57 identified 197,434 CNVs and 5852 CNVRs in 30 Chinese Hainan yellow cattle, while Butty et al.58 detected 124,302 CNVs and 8974 CNVRs in 96 Holstein animals. In another study, Gao et al.47 identified 14,821 CNVs and 487 CNVRs in eight Holstein bulls, while Peripolli et al.55 detected 24,945 CNVs and 463 CNVRs in Brazilian cattle. The observed variation in CNV and CNVR counts across studies, including the present work, likely reflects differences in sample size, sequencing depth, population structure, and analytical methodologies among other factors.

Vast majority of CNVRs were short, concentrated within the 1–5 kb range. The finding that deletion events were more frequent than duplication events in our CNV analysis aligns with similar observations across livestock species59,60. This phenomenon is largely attributable to a technical bias inherent in the CNV calling algorithms, particularly those based on sequencing read depth. Simply put, the computational tools are more sensitive and robust at detecting deletions61. When a segment of DNA is missing (a deletion), it results in a clear and measurable drop in the number of sequence reads mapping to that region, providing a high-confidence signal. Conversely, when a segment is inserted (a duplication event), it causes only a slight increase in read depth, which is harder for the algorithms to distinguish reliably from sequencing noise or technical variation, leading to fewer confident duplication calls being reported52,62. Among all autosomes, BTau_1 and BTau_15 harboured the highest number of CNVRs (481), while BTau_24 scored the least count (147). Various earlier studies have reported BTau_15 to be a CNV-hotspot in cattle63,64. Genes overlapping CNV regions (CNVRs) in short-statured Indian cattle

The CNVRs in short statured cattle were found to be harbour 6115 unique genetic elements (protein-coding genes, long non-coding RNA, pseudogenes, etc.) including 3906 protein coding gene (of which 3187 were annotated). Figure 5 presents the empirical distribution of CNVRs along the genome of short statured Indian cattle in relation to the gene density. Among different genes harboured under CNVRs, various genes including IGF1R (deletion at BTau_21: 7.77–7.78 Mb), FGFR3 (duplication at BTau_6: 116.73-116.78 Mb), SOX6 (deletion at BTau_15: 35.993–35.997 Mb), EXT2 (duplication at BTau_15: 74.21–74.22 Mb), LGR4 (deletion at BTau_15: 58.14–58.15 Mb), PRKCD (duplication at BTau_22: 47.75–47.80 Mb), ADAMTSL2 (deletion at BTau_11: 104.42-104.43 Mb), SLC25A6 (deletion at BTau_26: 15.20-15.23 Mb), and SDHA (deletion at BTau_20: 71.81–71.88 Mb) were relevant to phenotype under consideration. The IGF1R (Insulin-like growth factor 1 receptor) gene (BTau_21:7.77–7.78 Mb) has been reported to play a central role in cell proliferation, muscle development, and body size, with reduced expression linked to impaired growth65. In the context of short stature, reduced IGF1R expression or functional alterations may lead to de-activation or diminished activation of these pathways, resulting in decreased chondrocyte proliferation and differentiation in the growth plate, ultimately impairing longitudinal bone growth. Additionally, altered IGF1R signaling may affect endocrine regulation of the growth hormone (GH)–IGF axis, further contributing to reduced body size66–68. Similarly, FGFR3 (Fibroblast growth factor receptor 3) gene (BTau_6:116.77-116.79 Mb), a tyrosine kinase receptor, regulates chondrocyte proliferation and differentiation, affecting skeletal growth and limb formation69. Its enhanced expression has been reported to trigger an intracellular signaling cascade that limits the rate of chondrocyte proliferation and differentiation in the epiphyseal growth plate70. Hyperactivation of FGFR3 signaling has been reported to induce STAT1-mediated cell cycle arrest, reducing chondrocyte proliferation and impairing endochondral ossification, ultimately contributing to short stature71. It has been reported to regulate cartilage development and has been associated with variation in ear size in Duolang sheep72. The expression of SOX6 (SRY-box transcription factor 6) gene (BTau_15:35.84–36.55 Mb) has been associated with wither height and chest girth in Ashidan yaks, highlighting its role in body size regulation73. Genes such as EXT2 (Exostosin Glycosyltransferase 2; BTau_15:74.20-74.35 Mb) and LGR4 (Leucine-Rich Repeat-Containing G Protein-Coupled Receptor 4; BTau_15:58.09–58.20 Mb) have been reported to contribute towards skeletal muscle maintenance through chondrocytosis, where mutations cause short stature or altered bone density74,75. LGR4 gene (BTau_15:58.09–58.20 Mb) knockout mice has been reported to exhibit hypomorphic phenotype with retarted growth characteristics. In cattle, PRKCD (Protein kinase C delta) gene (BTau_22:47.75–47.78 Mb) is considered essential for FGF2-induced interferon tau expression, linking it to developmental regulation76. Its expression causes cell cycle arrest at S-phase and induces apoptotic signals in cells77. Similarly, the ADAMTSL2 (ADAMTS-like protein 2) gene (BTau_7: 2.01–2.26 Mb) is considered critical for skeletal growth and extracellular matrix integrity, with mutations causing dwarfism-like phenotypes and connective tissue dysplasia78. Its expression has been reported to regulate extracellular matrix organization by interacting with microfibrillar components such as fibrillin and modulating TGF-β signaling. Disruption of ADAMTSL2 function has also been associated with dysregulated TGF-β activity, resulting in abnormal extracellular matrix deposition, impaired chondrocyte differentiation, and defective endochondral ossification79,80. SLC25A6 (BTau_26: 15.19–15.21 Mb) is part of the SLC25 gene family, encoding different solute mitochondrial carriers that help in regulation and maintenance of efficient cell metabolism, thereby affecting growth through ADP-ATP interchange across the mitochondrial membrane81. The SDHA (succinate dehydrogenase complex flavoprotein subunit A) gene (BTau_20: 71.70–71.82 Mb) encodes a key enzyme of the mitochondrial respiratory chain that links the TCA cycle with oxidative phosphorylation and acts as a major gene related to growth and energy supply82.

Copy number variation regions (CNVRs) in tall Indian cattle

In tall Kankrej cattle, 7969 distinct CNVRs were identified following concatenation that were distributed across various chromosomes (including gonosomes) with 5271 deletion, 2593 duplication and 105 mixed events. The genomic coverage of these CNVRs was approximately 8.21% . The sex chromosomes harboured 1154 CNVRs (924 on X; 230 on Y), accounting for (14.48%) of the overall CNVR load. The autosomal coverage of CNVRs in Kankrej cattle was 3.54%. Among the autosomes, BTau_5 harboured the highest number of CNVRs (372), while BTau_ 27 harboured least count (111). The longest CNVR was a deletion, mapped on BTau_10 (1 Mb). Across autosomes, 4254 deletion, 2492 duplication and 69 mixed events were scored. Maximum deletions (281) were scored on BTau_1 while BTau_25 showed least count (37). Similarly, maximum duplications (143) were present on BTau_18 while BTau_28 showed least number of loss events (25). Furthermore, maximum mixed events (8) were scored on BTau_23. The longest duplication was 0.18 Mb, mapped on BTau_1 while the longest mixed event 0.12 Mb, mapped on BTau_3. Genes overlapping CNV regions (CNVRs) in tall Kankrej cattle

The CNVRs, scored on concatenation of overlapping CNVs, in tall Kankrej cattle were found to harbour multiple genes (5817), 3065 of which were protein-coding and annotated. Among these, IGF2BP1 (deletion), PLCL1 (duplication), ROR2 (duplication), IGFALS (duplication), CNMD (deletion), IKBKB (duplication), and TRRAP (duplication) were relevant to the phenotype of interest. Several of these genes have been identified as key regulators of growth and development in farm animals. The IGF2BP1 (insulin-like growth factor 2 mRNA-binding protein 1) gene has been linked to body weight and size in sheep through insertion/deletion (InDel) variants83. Its expression has been related to regulation of facultative stem cell states across various tissues84. On the other hand, PLCL1 (Phospholipase C-Like 1) gene (BTau_2: 86.31–86.69 Mb) has shown significant association with monthly body weight in Hu sheep85. In Holstein cattle, the ROR2 (Receptor tyrosine kinase-like orphan receptor 2) gene has been linked to interdigital hyperplasia and growth plate disruptions, thereby affecting skeletal growth86. Similarly, IGFALS (Insulin-like Growth Factor Binding Protein, Acid Labile Subunit) gene (BTau_25: 1.36–1.37 Mb) is considered essential for regulating circulating IGF-I and IGF-II factors, which are critical for growth and development, while its polymorphisms have been associated with body weight, average daily gain, and carcass traits in cattle87. The CNMD (Chondromodulin) gene, encoding a cartilage-derived glycoprotein, has been reported to contribute to chondrocyte proliferation (chondrogenesis) and endochondral bone formation88,89. It expression has been reported to promote chondrocyte proliferation and matrix synthesis while inhibiting angiogenesis in cartilages with its reduced function associated with impaired growth plate development and endochondral ossification, leading to reduced longitudinal bone growth90. The IKBKB (inhibitor of nuclear factor kappa B kinase subunit beta) gene (BTau_27: 37.12–37.18 Mb) has been associated with increased muscle fiber formation and the regulation of late-stage myogenesis in Landrace pigs91. Additionally, the expression of TRRAP gene (BTau25: 37.26–37.35 Mb) has been linked to skeletal and muscular system development92. Signature CNVs across dwarf individuals

Among all the unique non-overlapping CNV regions in dwarf cattle (54,388), 8,474 were singletons. Similarly, 78 regions were found in 100% of the dwarf individuals, which may be considered as putative signatures of short-statured phenotype. The details on other unique (non-overlapping) regions have been presented in Table 3. The unique non-overlapping regions were found to harbour important protein-coding genes and regulatory elements. Among these, ARL13B (BTau_1:38.27–38.35 Mb), AXIN2 (BTau19: 62.21–62.24 Mb), CCND2 (BTau_5: 105.76-105.78 Mb), CD1B (BTau_3: 11.713–11.727 Mb), CDH18 (BTau_20: 53.33–53.93 Mb), GIMAP1 (BTau_4: 113.307-113.317 Mb), WDPCP (BTau_11: 61.72–62.02 Mb), and TNNT1 (BTau_18: 62.27–62.28 Mb) were relevant with respect to their role in phenotype of interest. The role of ARL13B (ADP-ribosylation factor-like 13B) gene has been implicated in bone development by promoting osteoblast proliferation, migration, and osteogenesis93. It has been reported to regulate primary cilia–mediated Hedgehog and Wnt signaling, that are regarded essential for skeletal development; its disruption has been associated with impaired osteoblast function and endochondral ossification, leading to reduced bone growth and short stature94,95. Similarly, AXIN2 (Axis Inhibition Protein 2) gene, a negative regulator of β-catenin signaling, plays a key role in bone remodeling, where its knockout enhances β-catenin activity, accelerating osteoblast proliferation and inhibiting osteoclast formation via increased OPG expression96. The CCND2 (Cyclin D2) gene regulates cell cycle progression through CDK4/CDK6 interaction and has been identified as a downstream target of MyoD1 via the PI3K-Akt pathway, contributing to myogenesis in cattle97. Another gene i.e., CDH18 (Cadherin 18) gene has been associated with growth traits, with polymorphisms in Hu sheep linked to body weight and size, highlighting its role in muscle development83. On the other hand, WDPCP (WD repeat containing planar cell polarity effector) gene is crucial for ciliogenesis and Hedgehog signaling, with loss-of-function mutations in mice causing severe congenital defects98. Finally, TNNT1 (Troponin T1) gene, encoding slow skeletal troponin T, contributes to muscle contraction and development, with polymorphisms linked to growth traits and meat quality in livestock99,100. Overall, these genes were mainly related to growth, and osteogenesis, and ultimately related to their phenotype of these short- statured breed.

Among all the unique non overlapping regions in Kankrej cattle scored (44,379) through BedTools, 7,333 were singletons. Furthermore, 44,379, 32,513, 26,059, 21,020, 16,699, 7286, and 1343 unique non-overlapping regions were found in 10%, 20%, 30%, 40%, 50%, 70%, and 90% of individuals, respectively. A parallel analysis using BedTools resulted in 54,388 unique regions in dwarf cattle and 44,379 in Kankrej cattle, compared to 10,075 CNVRs in dwarf and 7,970 regions in Kankrej identified by our custom Python script. The discrepancy between these numbers is expected, as our custom script consolidates adjacent CNVs into single regions, while BedTools multiinter reports distinct, non-overlapping sub-intervals. While the BedTools multiinter approach is highly valuable for fine-scale analysis of CNV overlap, it can lead to over-fragmentation of results when the goal is to define broad regions of interest. By consolidating CNVs, our approach provides a more biologically interpretable set of regions for downstream functional annotation and enrichment analyses. For example, a single long CNV region might be fragmented into several sub-intervals by BedTools, potentially diluting its signal in a gene enrichment analysis. There was no common/overlapping CNVR shared between short statured Indian cattle and tall Kankrej cattle. However, the harboured genes under unique non-overlapping genes showed some overlap with 101 shared genes (38 and 74 harboured genes were unique for dwarf and tall cattle on this basis). On the other hand, the genes harboured CNVRs showed overlap for 2212 genes while 975 and 853 genetic elements were unique for short-statured and tall cattle, respectively. Besides the protein coding genes that are annotated, significant proportion of genetic elements harboured under CNVs and CNVRs were non-annotated and regularly sequence. It thus stresses the significant of further intense research on the subject and their role in phenotype of interest (short- stature) of cattle.

Selection signature analysis

The XP-EHH analysis between the short-statured versus tall cattle revealed several SNPs exhibiting strong selection signals in both groups (Fig. 8), located near protein-coding genes associated with adaptability and growth. Overall, 251 genes were identified within selective sweep regions in the Indian dwarf cohort, while 86 genes were found near such regions in the Indian tall cattle population.

The genes found to overlap within the analyzed genomic windows included ACER3, ARL13B, PRKG1, NBAS, RAPGEF5, RhoBTB2, WC1.3, Siglec1, and DEFB7. The ACER3 (Alkaline Ceramidase 3) gene has been reported to regulate ultra-long chain (ULC) ceramide metabolism, which directly affects cell proliferation and survival. Dysregulation of ACER3 elevates ULC ceramide levels, thereby activating the growth-arrest regulator p21CIP1/WAF1, leading to reduced cell proliferation and impaired growth. Interestingly, ACER3 knockdown protects cells from apoptosis under stress, indicating a dual role in growth arrest and cell survival through interactions with ACER2 and sphingolipid pathways101. Similarly, ARL13B (ADP-ribosylation factor-like 13B) encodes a small ciliary GTPase, essential for Hedgehog signaling and skeletal development. Studies in mice and zebrafish have shown that ARL13B promotes osteoblast proliferation, migration, and differentiation, while its knockdown shortens cilia and suppresses osteogenesis, resulting in reduced skeletal growth. Moreover, mechanical strain upregulates ARL13B expression, underscoring its role in bone mechano-transduction95. The PRKG1 (Protein Kinase, cGMP-Dependent, Type I) gene further contributes to growth by mediating NO/cGMP signaling inside bones. Osteoblast-specific knockout studies in mice have revealed that loss of PRKG1 may lead to defective fracture healing due to reduced expression of VEGF and BMP2/4, impaired angiogenesis, and diminished bone regeneration. Interestingly, pharmacological activation of PKG restored VEGF/BMP expression and normal fracture repair even under diabetic conditions, highlighting PRKG1 as a key molecular link between angiogenesis and osteogenesis102. In addition PRKG1 gene is associated with growth, muscle development, and metabolic pathways, influencing both milk production and meat traits in small ruminants103. Adding another layer to skeletal development, the NBAS (Neuroblastoma Amplified Sequence) gene has been reported to be involved in nonsense-mediated mRNA decay and Golgi-to-ER retrograde transport, playing a vital role in collagen secretion. Biallelic mutations in NBAS, initially associated with SOPH syndrome and liver failure, have recently been identified as a cause of bone fragility and atypical osteogenesis imperfecta. Studies in humans and zebrafish models have demonstrated that NBAS deficiency disrupts collagen synthesis, impairs chondrocyte organization, and causes recurrent fractures and short stature104. The RAPGEF5 (Rap guanine nucleotide exchange factor 5) gene is another crucial developmental regulator, activating small GTPase Rap proteins to control cell adhesion, growth, and cytoskeletal dynamics. Through modulation of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway, RAPGEF5 ensures proper β-catenin nuclear translocation and Wnt target gene expression, essential for embryonic patterning and tissue integrity105,106. In contrast, RhoBTB2 (Rho-related BTB domain-containing protein 2) acts as an E2F1-regulated gene that fine-tunes cell cycle progression and apoptosis. Short-term RhoBTB2 upregulation enhances proliferation, whereas sustained expression inhibits growth, the role that is consistent with its tumor suppressor role. By regulating cyclin D1 levels and recruiting proteins to the Cul3 ubiquitin ligase complex for degradation, RhoBTB2 balances cell growth and proliferation107. Beyond growth-related genes, several immune genes also exhibit important functional variation.

A total of 25 genes were found to be common across two analysis i.e., unique (non-overlapping) CN regions in 70% short-statured individuals and selection signature analysis. These genes were mainly related to growth and development (ACER3, ARL13B, NBAS, PRKG1, RAPGEF5, TSPEAR, and RhoBTB2) in animals along with immune response (DEFB7, IFNAA, KIR3DL2, IFNW1, MGC140461, SIGLECL1, and WC1.3) and olfaction (OR2D4, OR4C1N, OR51A5, OR51P1B, OR6A2, and OR6C264). Limitations include sample size constraints and the need for functional validation. Future studies should integrate multi-omics and larger cohorts to refine stature prediction models, fostering resilient livestock genomics.

In the present study, the short-statured cattle group (Height at withers - HAW < 105 cm; n = 19) represented multiple indigenous dwarf cattle populations as described in the source studies. It is acknowledged that variability in population origin and management conditions may exist due to the multi-study nature of the dataset. However, all genomic data were processed using a uniform bioinformatics pipeline for CNV and SNP detection to ensure comparability across datasets. The study included elucidation of the structural variants, present in 70%, 90% and 100% of individuals and subsequently, the results were complemented with selection signature analyses. Since the analyses are based on germline genomic variation, the impact of environmental differences on the structural genomic signals is expected to be minimal. The principle of population genomics as applied in the present study with CNV as well as SNP-based analyses undertaken at the population level adds to the criterion, thereby nullifying the effect.

Conclusion

The present study provides comprehensive insights into the genomic basis of stature variation in Indian Bos indicus cattle using whole-genome resequencing. Distinct CNV architectures were identified between short-statured and tall cattle, including 10,075 CNVRs in short-statured breeds and 7970 CNVRs in Kankrej, with no shared CNVRs between groups, highlighting divergent structural variation underlying body size. Integration of CNV and selection signature analyses revealed key candidate genes, including IGF1R, FGFR3, SOX6, and ADAMTS2, along with adaptive loci such as WDTC1 and ZNF410, implicating pathways related to growth regulation, skeletal development, and metabolic efficiency. These findings have direct applications in conservation and molecular breeding. The identified CNVRs and candidate genes provide genomic markers for the characterization and preservation of endangered short-statured cattle breeds, enabling more precise management of genetic diversity. In addition, these markers can be incorporated into marker-assisted and genomic selection programs to develop cattle that combine small body size, feed efficiency, and climate resilience traits that are particularly valuable under resource-limited and changing environmental conditions.
