Collaborative Autoethnography: A Research Method to Investigate Professional Identity of Medical Radiation Practitioners
Authors: Michelle Fenech, Nadia Mead, Paula Kinnane
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTProfessional identity (PI) encompasses the values, knowledge, skills and attitudes aligned with an individual's role in the workplace and the expectations of a professional group. Due to the changing landscape of healthcare and the extended scope of practice of medical radiation practitioners (MRPs), their PI is also evolving. A well‐formed PI can assist individuals, organisations and professions. For individuals, understanding PI can provide role clarity, a clear sense of purpose and direction, career progression opportunities, enhance work motivation and satisfaction, and improve well‐being. For professions or organisations, a well‐defined PI can increase workforce retention, improve work productivity and professional performance, support the professional development of individuals, and the way the profession is perceived by society. An understanding of the evolving PI of MRPs is required. Collaborative autoethnography (CAE) qualitative research can be feasibly used by MRPs to investigate their PI. To undertake CAE, the steps involved in the research process must be appreciated.Keywords: evolving professional identity, lived experience, narrative inquiry, professional credibility, professional evolution, role clarity, work satisfaction

Introduction

Professional identity (PI) comprises values, knowledge, skills and attitudes associated with a professional's role in the workplace and expectations of the wider profession [1, 2]. It is an important mechanism that impacts how a professional acts and feels; a clear PI impacts role clarity, job satisfaction, workplace behaviour, career planning and longevity [3]. PI subsequently influences professional purpose, meaning, self‐esteem and well‐being [4]. A positive PI can be a source of resilience, helping individuals to navigate professional challenges and manage occupational stress [5]. Likewise, a negative PI can contribute to professional burnout and impact an individual's decision to leave the profession [3].

At a profession level, PI can shape how society views it; hence, it impacts a profession's status, public recognition and credibility [6, 7, 8]. PI can influence professional codes of conduct, guidelines, standards, scope of practice, workforce development, education, research and innovation, all of which enhance professional quality and evolution [6, 8]. A positive PI therefore works to attract, recruit and retain professionals, and enhance practice and service quality [2, 7, 9]. Conversely, an ill‐defined PI can undermine work motivation, teamwork and performance, particularly in multidisciplinary environments, where role boundaries must be clear [2, 3, 9]. As the healthcare landscape evolves, so does the role complexity of allied health professionals (AHPs), including medical radiation practitioners (MRPs) [1, 10]. MRPs work across diverse public, private and non‐clinical settings, often within hierarchies of influence centred on medicine and nursing [6]. While PI has been extensively explored for doctors and nurses, there is a paucity of research regarding the PI of MRPs.

PI can be difficult to define, in part due to internal tensions within, and between professions and professionals, including territorial matters surrounding scopes of practice, and the multi‐discoursal nature of methods required to explore it [3, 8]. To capture and unpack how PI is formed and transformed in response to local organisational cultures and broader healthcare system changes, a contemporary approach is required [3]. Research to investigate PI is typically qualitative in nature; this can involve competing theoretical frameworks regarding professionalisation and professional socialisation, which tend to fall under the paradigm of social constructionism and social identity theory [11].

Research methods to investigate PI often use interviews, surveys, questionnaires, focus groups and literature or systematic reviews [8]. When investigating PI at a profession level, research often investigates functionalist‐based professional attributes, including how professions negotiate roles, occupational boundaries, professional territory, ownership of professional knowledge and skills, and where the profession sits relative to others [8]. However, the voice of individual professionals may become buried.

Collaborative autoethnography (CAE) offers an alternate, qualitative research approach to explore PI at the individual and profession level, which captures the voices of individuals [12]. Research data are gained from life stories of researcher‐participants [13]. Data include their professional experiences, and analysis allows a deeper understanding of their PI, at the individual and professional (group) level, gained within the sociocultural contexts in which they work [13, 14]. CAE, therefore, offers an effective approach to investigate the PI of MRPs.

The aim of this paper is to unpack CAE as a research approach to investigate PI and encourage MRPs to use it to investigate and clarify their dynamically evolving PI. A narrative review was conducted to gather information, points of view and unique perspectives related to CAE investigating PI [15, 16, 17]. Inductive content analysis, a ‘bottom‐up’ research method was used to support knowledge building and information gathering and interpretive analysis of the literature findings was conducted [18, 19, 20]. White literature was searched (from years 2000 to 2026) and analysed to allow the theoretical foundations of PI and CAE to be synthesised and contextualised for MRPs [19]. A step‐by‐step guide to conduct CAE to investigate PI is provided. CAE can allow individuals, professional bodies and educators to gain an understanding of PI which can then provide greater role and career clarity, enhance work satisfaction and inform the future direction of their profession.

Defining ‘Profession’ and ‘Professional’

To understand PI, the terms ‘profession’ and ‘professional’ must be clarified.

A profession is defined as a disciplined group of individuals, accepted by the public as possessing specialised knowledge and skills in a recognised body of learning, derived from high level research, education and training, who apply knowledge and exercise skills in the interest of others [21]. A profession has its own organisational structure and hierarchy, including administrative layers, policy formation and implementation; they are governed by ethical codes and standards to ensure accountability to society [22]. Such codes require behaviour and practice beyond personal moral obligations and demand behaviour standards in respect to the services provided and in dealing with professional colleagues [21]. A profession as a whole can have a collective PI.

A professional is a member of a profession, and they are accountable to those they serve and society [21]. Professionals are governed by their code of conduct and ethics; they profess commitment to competence, integrity and morality, and the promotion of the public good within their expert profession domain [23]. Professionals possess individual traits, often related to their training and job experience, but also their own character traits [22]. Hence, personal attributes and beliefs of an individual can impact their professionalism and PI [10]. An individual professional's PI defines the way they assign meaning to themselves, shaping their work attitudes, behaviour, productivity, work quality and effectiveness [24].

PI for Medical Radiation Practitioners (MRPs)

In Australia, the term medical radiation science (MRS) is used by the Australian Society of Medical Imaging and Radiation Therapy (ASMIRT) to represent health professionals from four allied health disciplines/professions which provide a range of diagnostic imaging and therapeutic procedures using ionising and non‐ionising radiation [25]. These professions include those working in the fields of (1) Diagnostic radiography/Medical imaging, (2) Radiation therapy, (3) Nuclear medicine technology and (4) Medical ultrasound [25]. These professions have professional titles which include the diagnostic radiographer, medical imaging technologist, radiographer, nuclear medicine scientist, nuclear medicine technologist, radiation therapist and sonographer [25].

The collective MRP term encapsulates these four professions. Each MRP profession has distinct qualification pathways, requires expert discipline‐specific scientific knowledge and skill acquisition, performs individual specialised roles in healthcare, and is governed differently [25]. Overlap between these professions can exist; there may be a common theoretical background in physics, anatomy and professional practice, but the training for each profession and work involved typically deviates beyond that [25].

These professions must have their own PI to enable safe and effective practice and person‐centred care, but also allow further professional evolution [1, 3]. A collective MRP PI is also required to ensure collaborative work between these professions. Developing a greater understanding of a collective MRP PI can allow individual professionals to understand where they dynamically fit within the organisational structure under the MRP banner and the wider healthcare system [5].

Clarifying the PI of individual MRP professions allows similarities, differences, synergies and potential barriers between these professions and professionals, including where they sit relative to each other and other health professions to be defined [1]. When individual professionals struggle with professional classification sensemaking, such as the use of terms and titles used to define them, it can lead to feelings of professional ambiguity [3]. Protected titles of professions denote professional regulation, legal restriction of professionals with that title and protection of public safety [26]. Protected titles and regulation also denote that qualified professionals meet specific education, training and registration requirements, and therefore can act to strengthen PI [26]. Currently the professional titles of all MRPs, except for that of the sonographer, are protected [25, 27].

Research is needed to investigate areas of overlap and differences across the four MRP professions including their professional needs, wants and PI. This may help identify pathways for effective, multidisciplinary collaboration that advances all four professions and supports them to navigate PI challenges. Research that captures the nuanced and lived experience of MRPs can help unpack the complexity of PI formation and transformation and clarify where and how all MRP professions fit together.

Some sonographers hold dual MRP qualifications and work across professions [24]. All MRPs, except for sonographers, are required to be registered with the Australian Health Practitioner Regulation Agency (AHPRA) to practise in Australia, which provides professional regulation [25, 28]. Sonographers who do not hold an additional MRP qualification (qualifying them to use ionising radiation) do not require AHPRA registration. The MRP title, therefore, does not appropriately represent the professional work of sonographers without dual MRP qualifications, or those that have dual MRP qualifications but only practice as sonographers, which can impact their PI. Research regarding sonographer PI is limited, including how they fit under the MRP banner, and this is required.

Factors That Impact PI

PI formation is a complex construct: it implies an individual responsibility to uphold the public perception of a profession, regardless of context, affecting judgements, behaviours and attitudes within and beyond the workplace [1, 5, 10]. Changing environments and experiences shape an evolving PI and can be impacted by internal and external influences [2, 9].

Individual PI

For individuals, internal influences on PI can involve what they personally feel and believe; this can reflect personal values, politics, ethics and feelings, including individual motivations to continuously improve, relative to their role [29]. Many intersectional factors impact and shape individual PI such as gender, self‐image, career stage, professional motivations, career goals, professional socialisation and culture, organisational support, and the impact of mentors and role models [30]. An individual's PI evolves through ongoing, self‐reflection of the ways they think, feel and act [31]. The personal dimension of PI may, at times, be in conflict with professional values, which can cause internal friction [3]. Hence, a person's professional role and work life can impact their personal well‐being [3, 10]. Research to investigate individual PI should consider identity theory, identity status paradigm and social learning theory [32, 33, 34, 35].

Profession Level PI

PI at a profession level can allow reporting lines and accountability, both within a profession and relative to other professions, to be defined [3]. It can also guide how supervision, organisational culture and leadership of specific professions can be formed, maintained, revised and further evolve [3, 36]. In healthcare, as PI is a key factor influencing the functioning of interprofessional teams, defining an evolving PI allows role clarity, defines professional boundaries, behaviour expectations, and allows professions and professionals to grow and develop [5].

External Influences on PI

External influences on PI are related to what people outside the profession perceive it to be, and this also needs to be explored [29]. The process of defining a contemporary PI, involves looking at professional relationships and organisational and institutional structures that provide context to the profession, and this is broadly referred to as ‘socialisation’ [2]. The sociocultural perspective in which professions and professionals work, can depend on the social structure and relationships with other communities or disciplines [37]. The experiences of professionals and the meaning that they, and others, attach to these experiences, play an important role in defining PI [34]. PI within health professions must consider the dynamic nature where identities are ‘assembled’ and ‘disassembled’, socially defined, influenced and intertwined with ones' self‐concept and one's multiple personal identities [38].

Obstacles to effective teamwork in healthcare teams include conflicting power relationships associated with professional cultures, philosophy, educational requirements, status and backgrounds of different professional groups, which have competing priorities and agendas [5, 9]. Development of regulatory standards, accreditation and policies by professional societies can shape, but also constrain PI, and hence, research to further investigate PI to inform further profession development is required.

A clearly articulated sense of PI allows professional agency and autonomy (distinct entities) of the profession as a whole, and individual professionals to be gained [39]. It is argued that professional autonomy can be achieved by motivating professional agency [40]. When individuals and professions have a clear PI, it can guide professional responsibility, which can improve work performance and satisfaction [24]. Considering current MRP workforce shortages, understanding the evolving PI can allow ways to improve work satisfaction and workforce sustainability to be identified.

PI Formation

PI formation includes the development of a sense of belonging to a profession and begins during training [24]. Higher education curriculum, therefore, plays an important role in PI development of student health professionals [38]. PI formation is accelerated during work integrated learning/clinical placements, where students put into action their developing knowledge and skills, and undertake clinical practice under supervision of qualified professionals [30, 41]. Much of the literature pertaining to health professional PI development is focused on the development of PI during student training [4, 10, 30, 41, 42, 43, 44].

Once qualified, health professionals develop their PI based on their experiences; it is impacted by those they work colleagues, employers, patients, professional societies and the way they feel they are perceived as professionals by society [10]. The majority of research related to qualified health professional PI is related to medicine and nursing, with less attention paid to other qualified health professionals [4, 10, 30, 41, 42, 43, 44]. There is a paucity of research investigating PI evolution post qualification, accreditation or registration of MRPs, and this is required [10].

Ways to Investigate and Define PI

There are many different approaches employed to explore PI, and they can be overwhelming and confusing, which can deter research [10]. The theoretical basis, language used and methodology in some approaches can make the research process feel intangible and too difficult a task to undertake. A suitable, feasible, easy to implement and effective qualitative method to explore and define PI is required, and CAE can address this need. CAE can deepen understanding of both individual and profession (group) PI, allowing reflections on how professionals intentionally and autonomously act within their discipline and professional context to be gained [44]. Data involve life stories of researcher‐participants, including their personal and professional experiences [13]. Analysis of data allows an understanding of sociocultural contexts in which researcher‐participants work to be appreciated, and this is used to gain an understanding of their PI [45].

Collaborative Autoethnography (CAE)

To understand CAE, it is important to first appreciate autoethnography.

Autoethnography (AE)

Autoethnography is a qualitative research method, predominantly used in the social sciences [46]. AE uses a single researcher's life stories to understand and interpret a cultural phenomenon [12]. It connects individual narratives to broader social, cultural and political means through self‐reflection (reflexivity) and narrative writing [47]. It is autobiographical in content, ethnographic in terms of its methodology and interpretively oriented [48] (Figure 1).

AE not only retells personal narratives, it uses a carefully organised research design, where data are systematically collected and analysed [49]. A single researcher‐participant recounts their lived experience, coupled with an ethnographic analysis of the cultural context and implications of that experience [48]. AE therefore provides an ‘insider’ perspective of experiences [50]. As an inquiry approach, it addresses the issues of representing, speaking for or appropriating the voice of others [48]. Data analysis can explain how the researcher responds to their environments and how their perspectives, behaviours and decisions have been shaped by their sociocultural contexts [12]. This research approach is useful for understanding PI; however, it only reflects an individual perspective [29, 47].

Collaborative Autoethnography (CAE)

Collaborative or collective AE is an evolving qualitative research method that extends AE by bringing together two or more researcher‐participants who co‐construct multivocal narratives and interrogate each other's accounts to allow for iterative thematic analysis [29]. It is a form of narrative inquiry, where researcher‐participants collect and share personal lived experiences to collectively gain meaningful understandings of a shared sociocultural phenomena [14, 45, 47, 51]. Collective data analysis allows themes and meanings to be identified, revealing commonalities and differences, shared insights and understanding beyond that of an individual perspective [50, 52]. CAE researcher‐participants bring different PI perspectives; they may come from different professional hierarchical levels, which are dismantled through the CAE process to ensure equal value is provided to all input [13].

CAE researcher‐participants interrogate both their own data and the data of co‐researchers, addressing some methodological and ethical issues associated with AE; hence, CAE can be interpreted as having greater research rigour [48]. Co‐constructed narratives from multiple points of view include those of enduring or pragmatic social value that may have been missed by a single researcher in AE [53]. This is of particular importance when exploring PI and workplace‐ or profession‐specific dynamics [49]. CAE therefore allows others to question something a single researcher may have missed or glossed over [45, 47, 51].

Implementing CAE: A Step‐by‐Step Guide

To undertake PI CAE, the research design, methodology and data analysis involved must be appreciated to guide research proposal development, ethics applications, data collection and analysis, and communication and publishing of the results, which can ultimately guide professional action. The steps involved in CAE include: (1) building the CAE research team, (2) crafting a focussed CAE research question, (3) designing a CAE study, methodology and defining what constitutes data, (4) navigating human research ethics approval for CAE research, (5) collecting CAE data, (6) analysing CAE data and (7) writing‐up, communicating and disseminating CAE findings. These steps are outlined below.

Building the CAE Research Team

In CAE, as the researchers are also the participants. The research team, therefore, involves those providing AE data, defining the sample size (n) of the study [45]. For PI research, the researcher‐participants include professionals from specific profession/s being investigated. One researcher from the team must have qualitative research experience, to design, lead and manage the research process, including thematic analysis [54]. Researcher‐participants involved must feel comfortable discussing personal data with each other, as there is a degree of vulnerability inherent in sharing personal information [45].

Research Team Size

The most suitable size of CAE teams is often three to five researcher‐participants [49]. Whilst CAE's strength lies in collaboration, too many voices can hinder and slow the research progress, particularly where there are considerable ideological differences [45, 49]. CAE often generates substantial qualitative data, all requiring systematic analysis and coding to identify themes and sub‐themes [13]. The number of researcher‐participants should therefore be carefully considered to ensure manageable data analysis [45].

Understanding Researcher Reflexivity

It is important that CAE researcher‐participants are aware of researcher reflexivity and are willing to exercise it [14]. Reflexivity is one of the most notable differences between qualitative and quantitative research methodologies [55]. Reflexivity is the act of examining one's own identity, assumptions, beliefs and judgement systems and how this can influence a research process [55]. It involves continuous self‐reflection, self‐awareness and self‐inquiry, and is essential in CAE to ensure research rigour [29]. Reflexivity is not a one‐time consideration but an ongoing commitment that occurs at every CAE stage [55, 56].

Researcher reflexivity allows one to appreciate how their identity shapes CAE data [57, 58]. It is required to ensure data reflects the diversity of all researcher‐participant experiences and social practices, not just their own [56]. CAE researcher‐participants, therefore, must appreciate that they are not neutral observers, but rather active participants, whose subjectivity can shape the research process [56]. Each researcher‐participant must continuously examine their own beliefs, assumptions, experiences and positionality to allow them to identify potential biases, influence that may have on the study to enhance transparency [56, 57]. When performed appropriately, each researcher‐participant must ask themselves, ‘what is the research process and how am I influencing it?’ [55]. Failure to attend to reflexivity can result in the research rigour, trustworthiness of the study and reputation of researchers involved being questioned [56].

Team Diversity, Dynamics and Power Considerations

Diversity of a research team is beneficial for CAE and ensuring reflexivity [55, 56]. In PI research, researcher‐participant diversity can involve those who come from a variety of disciplines, different career stages or career levels. Team members should embrace diverse perspectives [49]. When investigating the PI of MRPs, representation from different professions can provide a multi‐lens input. All researcher‐participants must use reflexivity to recognise any power imbalances in the research team and ensure nuanced, ethical and power balanced decisions are made during the research process [56].

Crafting a Focussed CAE Research Question

The overarching research aim of CAE is usually defined first, rather than specific research questions [45]. For CAE investigating PI, the aim may be to explore a broad area of PI of a single profession or group of professions. Alternatively, it may involve investigating the PI of professionals at a specific career stage or an event/experience in a professional's career that had a lasting impact on them [10, 50]. Aspects of PI, such as how it is formed, challenged, and evolved, can be investigated. Alternatively, CAE may investigate how specific elements such as values, skills, experience or perceptions shape PI.

Research questions subsequently evolve after the main aim is defined [13]. A literature review to investigate existing knowledge and identify knowledge gaps informs the development of meaningful, clear, focussed research questions that will be answerable through data collection and analysis [10]. Examination of the literature can also illustrate the ways AE outputs and journal articles are presented, as they may not always resemble the format of traditional scientific papers [50].

Designing a CAE Study, Methodology and Defining What Constitutes Data

CAE uses a transformative narrative methodology, underpinned by the foundational principles of AE [13]. This involves participatory research and narrative inquiry, which involves making meaning of researcher‐participants' stories and experiences, and for PI research, these include professional related experiences [29]. As CAE involves multiple researcher‐participants and their stories, it leads to the co‐creation of knowledge [54]. CAE dynamically reacts to contexts, and subsequently methodological decisions can alter throughout the research process, sometimes due to unforeseen circumstances [56].

What Constitutes Data in CAE

When designing a CAE study, what constitutes data must be firstly considered, and then how the data will be collected is subsequently defined [12]. CAE data is generally the recording of stories and experiences of researcher‐participants [13]. Data can include written data such as self‐generated memory data, diary entries, self‐reflections [29, 56]. If diary or journal entries are used, researcher‐participants must maintain a research diary during the data collection period to keep a record of their ideas, notes, reflections and actions [59].

Data can also include discussions between the research team [29, 56]. Discussions can be held face to face or online such as Zoom or Teams meetings which are usually recorded, so transcriptions can be gained [14, 54]. Alternatively, written notes from meetings or researcher‐participant discussions can also be used as data [54]. Discussions can involve all group members, or sub‐group discussions; using both approaches allows for variations in group dynamics and can act to diminish the impact of any power imbalances [54]. Memos and notes can be recorded by researcher‐participants during or after discussions, and these can be used to document critical interpersonal dynamics impacting participants and build on moments of analytic insight and form data [56]. Additionally, conversations or correspondence with others outside the research team can also be used as data [12]. If this occurs, external parties involved must be completely de‐identified, and their words can only be used as data with voluntary consent [48].

Navigating Human Research Ethics Approval for CAE Research

As CAE involves human participants, it requires human research ethics approval [29]. Informed voluntary consent of all researcher‐participants involved must be gained [29]. Risks to researcher‐participants, and the institutions supporting the research need to be considered prior to conducting CAE, and plans to mitigate or address risks must be outlined [29, 48]. In PI research, any potential researcher power imbalances must be clearly identified and strategies to manage associated risks outlined at the outset [48].

Protecting Researcher‐Participant Well‐Being

A supportive, collegial approach by all research team members is required to ensure well‐being preservation; particularly as researcher‐participants can share vulnerable stories [14]. Hence, a trauma‐informed approach and safe research process should be considered [29, 48]. A protocol for regular check‐ins to gain well‐being updates on all researchers is useful, with a plan for counselling services to be accessed, if required [29].

Ensuring a Non‐Hierarchical Culture of Researcher‐Participants

For ethical and effective CAE, it is important that the research team is non‐hierarchical and all researcher‐participants are free to express experiences and opinions without negative consequences [48]. This is particularly important for PI research, where researcher‐participants may be at different professional hierarchical levels, causing a pre‐existing power differential [48]. Therefore, as previously discussed, power dynamics in the research team must be carefully addressed in the research design, ethical applications and managed continually throughout the CAE process [48].

Collecting CAE Data

CAE data collection depends on the type of data to be gained. Discussion data must be effectively transcribed or documented in a consistent manner, to allow subsequent appropriate data analysis [54]. Individual written reflections must be shared between all co‐researchers [45]. A collective data storing and management system is needed as a repository for data [48]. Such instruments can include, but are not limited to, a Microsoft Teams site or a Google Doc [48].

Discussing and probing written data can provide insights into sociocultural phenomena related to PI that researcher‐participants are experiencing as part of their personal, work and professional lives [45]. Whilst data sources from individual researcher‐participants inform initial data collection, in CAE, there is also the opportunity to collect further data as the researcher‐participants interact with each other's initial individual data and have discussions about it [45]. This is because, during the data collection process, researcher‐participants can uncover differing perspectives on the same topic. Further individual and group reflections will allow the collective to dynamically dig deeper and invite more robust critique of the issues raised in individual and collective researcher‐participant narratives, and generate further data [53].

Researcher‐participants usually create a shared location where they upload their own data and make it accessible to others in the team [14]. Others in the team then read and reflect on their collaborator's data sources, adding to the data [12]. Ongoing data can be in the form of meetings or email correspondence, all of which must be recorded [14]. Researcher‐participants can then read and reflect on feedback they have received from others and add further reflections [13]. This revisiting process means that researcher‐participants are compelled to reflect more deeply on issues that resonate with the team. It is also a time where similarities and differences between perspectives can emerge [29].

Analysing CAE Data

CAE, as a form of narrative inquiry, focuses on understanding and interpreting human experiences conveyed through stories [54]. Analysis of CAE data is performed through thematic analysis, which identifies and analyses patterns in the qualitative data, and different styles of thematic analysis can be utilised [12, 60]. CAE data analysis can be conducted both independently and collaboratively [54]. An established method for the process of data analysis must involve all researcher‐participants; this is essential to ensure uniformity in analysis, interpretation and write‐up [54]. The analytical approach should be decided on by group consensus [54]. Cooperative work of the research team allows all researcher‐participants to interact and have critical dialogue about data related to PI and its analysis [14].

In CAE, researcher‐participants need to be familiar with all data, and this may have already begun when they start to reflect and comment on each other's data [45]. However, the systematic process of reviewing data, categorising data into initial codes and then identifying emerging themes usually starts at the completion of data collection [49]. For data analysis, a macro review and then a micro review of data are recommended [45].

The macro‐data review and analysis involves revising data at the holistic level, to reveal potential overarching themes/broader patterns of meaning [45]. When investigating PI, CAE researcher‐participants investigate aspects of their professional work, from work motivation to role description and role hierarchy [10]. Analysis can reveal how personal values are aligned or non‐aligned with those of their profession [10]. Co‐researchers may have contradicting results from their own data, and this can lead to rich conversations about what it is to be a professional in the given context [12]. Group consensus must be reached initially on overarching emerging themes in response to research questions [54].

The micro‐data review and analysis in CAE can be conducted across the categorised groups of data, organised into more defined themes, which then allows a more detailed analysis at the coding level [45]. All codes are agreed on collaboratively by the research group. Researcher‐participants record their observations, review data and how it fits into the emerging themes during this more detailed analysis process [48]. Codes are applied to the dataset to help organise the data and analyse it.

Each researcher‐participant has a defined role and responsibility in the data analysis process, with a common understanding of how coding will occur to ensure consistency and coherence throughout the analysis [54]. A two‐tiered analysis generally occurs, where researcher‐participants independently analyse data to look for emergent alternative or sub‐themes, in their own work first and then across all data [50]. Internal peer‐reviewing occurs through the CAE data analysis and interpretation sessions [49]. Researcher‐participants can expand on, affirm and question each other's reflective writing, recollection of memories and experiences [48, 49]. Collaborative review, discussion and agreement on final themes and potential sub‐themes, including naming the themes allows the analysis to eventually comprise the co‐constructed analysis [54, 60].

Using collaborative reflexivity, researcher‐participants ask difficult questions about assumptions and decisions of themselves and each other [56]. The themes selected should reveal answers to research questions; however, usually further questions that had not been considered previously also emerge [29]. Insights gained through data analysis help researcher‐participants make meaning from their collective data and possibly transform perspectives [54]. In PI research, this can provide new knowledge about themselves and their individual and collective identity, both personal and professional [12]. This iterative, co‐constructive process leads to a more robust and nuanced narrative, and the research group will be able to perceive aspects of the narrative that may have been missed in isolation [54].

Writing‐Up, Communicating and Disseminating CAE Findings

The output of PI CAE research is usually structured as a final narrative, which discusses identified themes, sub‐themes and their sociocultural context [54]. Quotes from researcher‐participants are selected that appropriately represent themes and sub‐themes, and these are used in the write‐up of results and research outputs [45]. During the write‐up phase, researcher‐participants revisit original data, when looking for quotes, to gain and discuss essential context [54]. Individual researcher‐participants can have input into different sections of the write‐up; however, it is essential that each researcher‐participant's voice comes through in the final narrative [48]. Frequent and open communication between researcher‐participants is required in the write‐up and editing phase to allow the group to collaboratively refine the final narrative to ensure they have all accurately represented the themes and the co‐constructed essence of the study [54].

CAE outputs can have a different structure to more traditional papers and manuscripts for publication [56]. When writing up CAE PI research, the people involved and their locations may be de‐identified [48]. Some researcher‐participants may use pseudonyms to help reduce vulnerability, especially if they may have disclosed experiences that could have potential negative career consequences [48].

Benefits and Limitations of Using CAE for PI Research

CAE is a useful research method to investigate PI, including that of MRPs [45]. Without a well‐defined PI, individuals may feel like they do not know where they ‘fit’ within their profession, organisation or larger working landscape, and this can impact their professional agency and autonomy [4, 6]. Conflict between personal and professional expectations can result, potentially leading to an identity crisis, affecting both their professional and personal lives [5].

CAE investigating PI enables a multivocal account of sometimes vulnerable and marginalised professional and personal experiences [45, 61]. MRPs can use CAE as a process to reflect on perceptions and expectations from patients, colleagues, other health care professionals and the environment they work within, which can influence their PI [54]. Professionals using CAE have the opportunity to pause, look back at the terrain they have travelled together and gain clarity on how to further shape themselves [13]. CAE investigating PI can therefore assist to reignite a passion for working in a profession and retain professionals in the workforce [50]. As CAE involves self‐reflection, researcher‐participants need to undertake some reflective writing and a lack of comfort or experience with this can be a limitation. The process, however, can be therapeutic for researchers; sharing stories can foster mutual understanding and collective learning and even healing, which can bring joy to the research [29, 54].

CAE allows diverse perspectives of professionals to be illuminated, discussed and reflected upon, which can inform the evolution of professional practice, standards, workforce development and interprofessional collaboration [50]. As the roles of health professions, and the context in which they work, including workforce structures transform in parallel to the broader healthcare system, revising PI within and across health professions, particularly for MRPs, needs to be continually investigated [5]. The multi‐vocal analysis of CAE offers insider perspectives; however, research requires a time commitment and careful facilitation to manage divergent viewpoints. Ethical risks include inadvertent identification of individuals or organisations who wish to remain anonymous, which necessitates robust anonymisation and consent procedures.

When PI is not well defined or understood, role confusion can result; this can lead to professional and interprofessional teamwork challenges [62]. In healthcare teams, role confusion may subsequently result in an ‘us versus them’ mentality between professions and professionals. This can impact work‐related decision‐making for individuals and professions, ultimately impacting delivery of high‐quality person‐centred care [5]. Exploring personal narratives can help individuals cope with challenging professional experiences, and make sense of their role and reactions in specific contexts in which they work [54]. By professionals knowing who they are, what their professional purpose is, and acknowledging satisfactions they gain from their role, undertaking CAE PI research can be an empowering process.

Conclusion

As the roles and responsibilities of MRPs evolve within the broader healthcare environment, there is a need for research to understand PI formation and transformation. CAE allows stories of researcher‐participants, including their multiple points of view and experiences, to be captured, analysed, and workplace and social structures and issues related to PI to be explored. CAE offers a transformative process whereby researcher‐participants create a community, advance understanding and become empowered within their social and professional context. By following the simple steps of CAE, in‐depth insights related to PI can be uncovered. Clarifying PI has implications for individual practitioners, professional associations, educators and workplaces. Further defining PI can impact professional resilience, workforce development and evolution of professional roles, scope of practice, policy and standards, workforce sustainability and continued high‐quality MRP delivered person‐centred care.
