Comprehensive Neonatal Screening for Genetic Disorders in Tribal Populations of Central India
Authors: Rakesh K. Jha, Meghali Kaple, Ranjit S. Ambad, Archana Dhok, Ashsish Anjankar
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground:: Genetic disorders, including sickle cell disease (SCD), thalassemia, and glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) deficiency, pose significant health challenges in central India, especially among tribal populations. Comprehensive neonatal screening is crucial for early diagnosis and management.Aim and Objective:This study aims to evaluate the prevalence of SCD, thalassemia, and G6PD deficiency in newborns from tribal regions of central India and assess the impact of comprehensive neonatal screening programs.Materials and Methods:A total of 382 newborns were screened for SCD and thalassemia using high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) and for G6PD deficiency using a colorimetric assay. Demographic data were collected, and statistical analyses were performed.Results:The screening identified 28 cases of Sickle cell disease (SCD) (7.3%), 35 cases of thalassemia (9.1%), and 34 cases of G6PD deficiency (8.9%). The study found a significant relationship between maternal education and the prevalence of genetic disorders.Conclusion:The findings highlight the importance of implementing comprehensive neonatal screening programs in tribal populations to improve early detection and management of genetic disorders.

INTRODUCTION

Genetic disorders represent a significant global health burden, affecting millions of people worldwide. In regions with limited healthcare infrastructure, such as central India, genetic disorders pose a formidable challenge to healthcare systems, particularly in rural and tribal areas. Comprehensive neonatal screening has emerged as an effective intervention to identify and manage genetic disorders early in life, enabling timely intervention to prevent severe morbidity and mortality. In central India, particularly among tribal populations, the prevalence of genetic disorders such as sickle cell disease (SCD), thalassemia, and glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) deficiency is alarmingly high.[1] These disorders disproportionately affect tribal populations, largely due to genetic drift, geographical isolation, and consanguinity.[2] Implementing a comprehensive neonatal screening program in this region could significantly reduce the public health burden posed by these disorders.

The tribal populations of central India are among the most vulnerable to genetic disorders, particularly hemoglobinopathies such as sickle cell disease and thalassemia.[3] Sickle cell disease is caused by a single-point mutation in the beta-globin gene, leading to the production of abnormal hemoglobin S (HbS).[4] Individuals who inherit the HbS gene from both parents develop sickle cell disease, which is characterized by the production of abnormally shaped red blood cells. These sickle-shaped cells are prone to causing blockages in blood vessels, leading to vaso-occlusive crises, severe pain, hemolytic anemia, and long-term complications such as stroke, organ damage, and early mortality.[5] In contrast, individuals who inherit only one copy of the HbS gene are carriers (sickle cell trait), typically asymptomatic but capable of passing the trait to their offspring.[6]

Thalassemia, another common hemoglobinopathy, results from mutations that impair the production of one or more globin chains that make up hemoglobin.[7] The most common form in India is beta-thalassemia, where the production of the beta-globin chain is either reduced or completely absent. This leads to severe anemia, requiring regular blood transfusions to maintain normal hemoglobin levels.[8] Without adequate management, beta-thalassemia major can result in severe complications such as iron overload, heart disease, and early death.[9] Beta-thalassemia trait (minor, though usually asymptomatic) is of great concern from a genetic counseling perspective because two carriers have a 25% chance of having a child with beta-thalassemia major.[10] Given the high prevalence of consanguineous marriages among tribal communities in central India, the risk of both parents being carriers is elevated, increasing the risk of passing the disorder to offspring.[11]

Glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) deficiency is another common genetic disorder in central India, especially among the tribal population.[12] G6PD deficiency is an X-linked recessive disorder that affects the red blood cells’ ability to protect themselves from oxidative stress.[13] When exposed to certain triggers, such as infections, certain drugs, or foods like fava beans, individuals with G6PD deficiency experience hemolysis, leading to episodes of acute hemolytic anemia.[14] This can result in jaundice, fatigue, shortness of breath, and in severe cases, life-threatening anemia.[15] Although G6PD deficiency is typically asymptomatic, neonatal screening is essential to identify affected individuals and provide preventive advice, particularly regarding the avoidance of known triggers.[16]

The prevalence of these genetic disorders in tribal populations of central India is significantly higher than in other parts of the country.[17] Several factors contribute to this increased burden, including the high rate of consanguineous marriages, which increases the likelihood of inheriting autosomal recessive disorders like SCD and thalassemia.[18] Additionally, the social and geographical isolation of tribal communities limits access to adequate healthcare services, further exacerbating the impact of these genetic disorders.[19] The lack of awareness and education about genetic disorders also plays a critical role in the continued high prevalence of these conditions in tribal areas.[20] Many affected families are unaware of the genetic nature of these disorders, and as a result, they may not seek genetic counseling or carrier testing, which could help prevent the transmission of these disorders to future generations.[21]

Neonatal screening is a well-established public health strategy to detect genetic disorders early in life.[22] In many high-income countries, comprehensive neonatal screening programs have been implemented for decades, successfully identifying disorders like sickle cell disease and thalassemia shortly after birth.[23] Early detection through neonatal screening allows for prompt intervention, such as prophylactic penicillin in children with SCD to prevent infections or regular blood transfusions for children with beta-thalassemia.[24] These interventions significantly reduce morbidity and mortality, improving the quality of life for affected individuals.[25] However, in India, particularly in tribal regions, neonatal screening programs are still in their infancy.[26] Despite the availability of diagnostic tools, the implementation of screening programs remains inconsistent, especially in rural and tribal areas, where healthcare resources are limited.[27]

The benefits of neonatal screening in tribal populations extend beyond individual health outcomes. From a public health perspective, screening programs can provide valuable epidemiological data on the prevalence of genetic disorders in these populations, informing future healthcare policies and resource allocation.[28] Furthermore, early identification of carriers of genetic disorders like sickle cell disease and thalassemia can enable genetic counseling services to be offered to affected families, helping to reduce the incidence of these disorders in future generations.[29] In the long term, the establishment of comprehensive neonatal screening programs could play a pivotal role in reducing the overall burden of genetic disorders in central India’s tribal populations.[30]

The implementation of comprehensive neonatal screening programs in tribal populations faces several challenges, including limited healthcare infrastructure, lack of trained personnel, and cultural barriers.[31] However, with the right investments in healthcare resources, education, and community engagement, these barriers can be overcome.[32] Increasing awareness about genetic disorders, improving access to healthcare, and providing genetic counseling to affected families are crucial steps in ensuring the success of these programs.[33] Additionally, collaborations between government agencies, non-governmental organizations, and local communities will be essential in establishing sustainable screening programs that reach all newborns in tribal areas.[34]

MATERIAL AND METHODS

This cross-sectional study was conducted in central India between January and December 2023, involving a total of 382 newborns from various tribal communities. Newborn screening for SCD and thalassemia was conducted using high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) to identify abnormal hemoglobin variants. For G6PD deficiency, a quantitative colorimetric assay was employed.

Data were collected through structured questionnaires to capture demographic information, including maternal education, family history of genetic disorders, and socio-economic status. The prevalence of each disorder was calculated, and statistical analyses, including chi-square tests and logistic regression, were performed to assess associations between variables.

DISCUSSION

The comprehensive neonatal screening for genetic disorders in the tribal populations of Central India represents a significant public health initiative aimed at addressing the disproportionately high burden of inherited diseases in this marginalized group. Genetic disorders such as sickle cell disease, thalassemia, and glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) deficiency are common in tribal communities due to the interplay of various socio-cultural, genetic, and environmental factors. Early identification through neonatal screening provides an opportunity to initiate timely interventions, reducing morbidity and mortality associated with these conditions.[35]

Sickle cell disease (SCD) is a particularly prevalent genetic disorder in the tribal regions of Central India. Several studies, including the present one, confirm the high frequency of the sickle cell trait in these populations. The autosomal recessive nature of SCD means that the disease manifests in individuals who inherit the mutant hemoglobin S gene from both parents, while carriers (heterozygotes) usually remain asymptomatic. Neonatal screening for SCD allows for the early identification of affected individuals, enabling the commencement of interventions such as penicillin prophylaxis and immunization to prevent infections, which are a major cause of mortality in young children with SCD. Additionally, hydroxyurea therapy has been shown to reduce the frequency of vaso-occlusive crises and improve the overall quality of life in patients with SCD. However, access to such therapies is limited in rural and tribal regions, where healthcare infrastructure is often inadequate, and healthcare workers may lack the training necessary to manage such chronic conditions. Despite the potential benefits of early diagnosis, the lack of consistent follow-up and treatment remains a major challenge in ensuring the long-term success of neonatal screening programs for SCD.[36]

Thalassemia, particularly β-thalassemia, is another genetic disorder with a high prevalence in Central India’s tribal populations. β-thalassemia results from mutations that affect the production of the β-globin chain of hemoglobin, leading to ineffective erythropoiesis and chronic anemia. Children born with thalassemia major require lifelong blood transfusions and iron chelation therapy to manage their condition. In the absence of such treatments, affected individuals face severe complications such as growth retardation, organ damage due to iron overload, and early mortality. Neonatal screening for thalassemia not only helps in early diagnosis but also offers families the option of genetic counseling to reduce the likelihood of future births of affected children. Genetic counseling is crucial in tribal populations, where consanguineous marriages and a lack of awareness about genetic disorders contribute to the high prevalence of thalassemia. However, the success of screening programs is contingent on the availability of follow-up care, including regular blood transfusions, iron chelation therapy, and comprehensive genetic counseling services. These services are often lacking in rural and tribal areas, making it difficult to effectively manage thalassemia in these populations.[37]

G6PD deficiency, the most common enzyme deficiency worldwide, is also highly prevalent in Central India’s tribal communities. G6PD deficiency predisposes individuals to hemolytic anemia in response to certain triggers, such as infections, drugs (e.g., antimalarials, sulfonamides), and foods (e.g., fava beans). Neonatal screening for G6PD deficiency allows for the early identification of at-risk individuals, enabling preventive measures to be taken to avoid hemolytic episodes. Parents can be educated about the triggers of hemolysis and how to avoid them, which can prevent potentially life-threatening episodes of anemia. However, despite the simplicity of preventive strategies, the lack of awareness and education in rural and tribal areas often results in preventable hemolytic crises in individuals with G6PD deficiency. This highlights the need for community-based education programs to complement neonatal screening efforts, ensuring that parents and caregivers are equipped with the knowledge necessary to protect their children from avoidable harm.[38]

Beyond the specific genetic disorders covered in this screening program, the broader challenges of implementing neonatal screening in tribal populations cannot be overlooked. The success of such programs depends on several factors, including community engagement, government support, and the availability of healthcare resources. In tribal communities, healthcare infrastructure is often inadequate, with limited access to diagnostic facilities, trained healthcare personnel, and necessary medications. Geographic isolation further exacerbates the challenges, as many tribal villages are located in remote areas with poor transportation links to healthcare centers. These logistical barriers make it difficult to ensure timely follow-up care for infants diagnosed with genetic disorders.[39]

Moreover, cultural factors play a significant role in the success of neonatal screening programs in tribal populations. Traditional beliefs and practices, as well as a general mistrust of modern medicine, can hinder the acceptance of screening programs. It is essential to involve local community leaders and healthcare workers in the design and implementation of screening programs to build trust and encourage participation. Community-based education programs are also crucial in raising awareness about genetic disorders, the importance of early diagnosis, and the availability of treatment options.[34]

Another challenge is the lack of sustained government support for neonatal screening programs. While pilot programs and short-term initiatives have demonstrated the feasibility of screening in tribal populations, long-term success requires consistent funding and policy support. The government must prioritize the expansion of neonatal screening programs and the development of healthcare infrastructure in tribal areas. Additionally, there is a need for greater investment in training healthcare workers in the management of genetic disorders, as well as the provision of essential medications and therapies.[40]

CONCLUSION

The study highlights the pressing need for comprehensive neonatal screening programs for SCD, thalassemia, and G6PD deficiency among tribal populations in central India. Early detection and timely interventions can drastically improve health outcomes for affected newborns. Collaborative efforts among healthcare providers, government agencies, and community leaders are crucial to establish effective screening programs and raise awareness about genetic disorders.
