Social frailty and related social vulnerabilities among older adults in the Philippines: A narrative review
Authors: Julienne Ivan Soberano, Jed Montayre
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesTo synthesize the evidence on social frailty among older adults in the Philippines, with attention to sociocultural and structural determinants, assessment approaches, and policy and program responses, and to derive implications for nursing practice and research.MethodsA narrative review with scoping elements was informed by the Scale for the Assessment of Narrative Review Articles (SANRA), with selected PRISMA-ScR items used to enhance reporting transparency. Electronic searches of PubMed, CINAHL, Web of Science, PsycINFO, and Google Scholar identified peer-reviewed and relevant verifiable discussion papers written in English from January 2015 to December 2025 (final search: 5 January 2026). The search, title, and abstract screening, full-text review, and data extraction were performed independently by two reviewers, with disagreements resolved through consensus. Eligible studies focused on community-dwelling Filipinos aged ≥60 years and examined social frailty or closely related constructs, including social networks, participation, support, isolation, loneliness, and social determinants of health and functioning. Data were synthesized thematically using Bunt et al.’s four domains of social frailty.ResultsThirty-five studies were included, most using qualitative or cross-sectional designs. Evidence clustered in the domains of general resources and social behavior activities, particularly access, participation, mobility, disaster related disruption, and emerging digital engagement. Fewer studies addressed social resources and self-management abilities. Family, kinship, and faith-based networks, and community organization were important protective systems, but were increasingly strained by migration, economic insecurity, service limitations, and environmental disruption.ConclusionsSocial frailty among older Filipinos emerges at the intersection of structural disadvantage, changing informal support systems, community participation, digital access, and self-management capacities. Addressing social frailty requires multidimensional assessment, culturally grounded measurement, and coordinated nursing, community and policy responses

Introduction

The aging population is increasing rapidly worldwide. By 2030, one in six people will be aged 60 years or older, increasing to approximately 2.1 billion by 2050 (World Health Organization [WHO], 2025). In the Philippines, the share of older adults aged 65 years or older is projected to exceed 7.0% by 2032 as fertility declines and life expectancy increases (Demographic Research and Development Foundation, 2025; UNDESA, 2015). This demographic transition is expected to increase vulnerability in later life and intensify the demand for chronic and geriatric care.

Frailty is a multidimensional geriatric syndrome linked with adverse outcomes such as disability, hospitalization, and mortality (Bunt et al., 2017). While traditionally conceptualized physiologically, contemporary frameworks recognize frailty as a multidimensional concept, encompassing physical, psychological, and social components that interact over time (Freitas & Salibi, 2023; Gobbens et al., 2010; Kastner et al., 2024; Venturini et al., 2021). Within this perspective, social frailty is defined as “a continuum of being at risk of losing, or having lost, social and general resources, activities or abilities that are important for fulfilling one or more basic social needs during the life span” (Bunt et al., 2017). SF is associated with a higher risk of cognitive and physical decline (Ma et al., 2018), depressive symptoms (Huang et al., 2024b; Li et al., 2023), institutionalization (Lee et al., 2020), and mortality (Huang et al., 2025a). SF intersects with other concepts such as loneliness, social isolation, social vulnerability, and resilience. While sharing features and influence, SF remains analytically distinct (Andrew, 2015; Gale et al., 2018; Jeste et al., 2013; Kastner et al., 2024; Lee et al., 2020).

Because this review includes assessment approaches, distinguishing social frailty specific tools from broader social-domain instruments helps clarify which measures directly identify social frailty and which assess related constructs. Social-frailty specific tools, including social frailty indices, aim to identify vulnerability arising from reduced social resources, participation, and abilities (Hamid et al., 2024; Huang et al., 2025a; Webber et al., 2025). Related tools such as the Lubben Social Network Scale, the social relationships domain of the WHO Quality of Life-BREF, and social domains within comprehensive geriatric assessment capture the overlapping constructs, including network size, perceived support, social participation, and quality of life (Chong et al., 2022; Li et al., 2024). However, these tools may not always capture the combined structural, relational, behavioral, and subjective dimensions of social frailty. Existing Asia-Pacific tools highlight networks, activities, support, financial difficulty, and roles, and recognize contact through digital platforms as part of networks (Webber et al., 2025). In the Philippine context, measurement also needs to consider family structure, faith-based participation, senior citizens’ organization, migration, informal caregiving, and digital communication (Bessa et al., 2018; Huang et al., 2025a; Webber et al., 2025).

Epidemiologically, SF is already prevalent in Asia. A systematic analysis by Zhang et al. (2023) estimated a pooled prevalence of SF at 18.8% in community and 47.3% in hospital settings across Asia. Estimates vary by setting: China reports rates between 4.9 and 15.2% (Qi et al., 2023; Sun et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2023); Hong Kong accounts 24% (Montayre et al., 2024); Japan ranges from 11.1% to 21.1% (Kume et al., 2022; Qi et al., 2023); and Singapore reports 18.4% (Qi et al., 2023; Sun et al., 2023). Where SF estimates are sparse, overall frailty data suggest substantial vulnerability in LMICs, for example Indonesia has a pooled frailty prevalence of 26.8% (Pradana et al., 2023); Malaysia reports 6–56% frailty, with higher prevalence in institutional settings and rural cohorts (Shaharudin and Abd Rahman, 2022); and Vietnam with rural community studies report 21.7% frailty (Nguyen et al., 2019).

In the Philippines, rapid aging overlaps with socioeconomic vulnerabilities such as economic disadvantages, existing health challenges, and limited healthcare access (Demographic Research and Development Foundation, 2025), which may heighten social frailty risk. To our knowledge, no prior review has synthesized evidence on social frailty in the Philippines, and primary studies remain limited.

This narrative review therefore synthesizes Philippine evidence on social frailty and related constructs among community-dwelling older adults. Using an established four broad domains of social frailty by Bunt et al. (2017), this review aimed to: (1) map the scope and characteristics of existing studies; (2) identify dominant themes and gaps; and (3) discuss implications for nursing practice, policy, and future research.

Methods

Study design

This study was conducted as a narrative review with scoping elements informed by the Scale for the Assessment of Narrative Review Articles to enhance rigor and transparency (Baethge et al., 2019). A narrative design was selected because the available Philippine evidence is heterogeneous in design, outcomes, and terminology, and therefore required conceptual and contextual synthesis. Selected items from the PRISMA Extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) were incorporated to improve reporting clarity, particularly with eligibility criteria, information sources, search strategy, selection of evidence, data charting, and synthesis of results (Tricco et al., 2018). A protocol was not registered; ethical approval was not required because the review used published literature. See Supplementary A-SANRA; Supplementary B-PRISMA-SCr Reporting Summary.

Search strategy

A three-step approach was implemented by two reviewers (JIS and JM). First, an initial search of published articles from PubMed was conducted using the keywords: social frailty, socially frail, social vulnerability, social isolation, social participation, social engagement, social support social networks, loneliness, older adults, senior citizens, geriatric, Philippines, and Filipino. Second, text words in the titles and abstracts of relevant articles, and index terms were used to develop a search strategy. Third, the final strategy combined social frailty terms, related social constructs, older-adult terms, and Philippine-context terms using Boolean operators. The PubMed search combined three concept blocks: social frailty/social vulnerability terms, older adult terms, and Philippine-context terms. The final PubMed string was: ("social frailty" OR "socially frail" OR "social vulnerability" OR "social isolation" OR loneliness OR "social participation" OR "social engagement" OR "social support" OR "social network*" OR caregiving OR "living arrangement*" OR "community engagement") AND ("older adult*" OR elderly OR geriatric OR "senior citizen*" OR ageing OR aging) AND (Philippines OR Filipino*). This was also adapted to CINAHL, Web of Science, PsycINFO, and Google Scholar for English-language sources (January 1, 2015–December 31, 2025; final search January 5, 2026). Search and reporting was informed by PRISMA-ScR (Tricco et al., 2018) guidance where feasible. See Supplementary File C for search strings.

Eligibility criteria

The eligibility criteria were developed following the Population–Interest–Outcome framework consistent with Joanna Briggs Institute methodology (Munn et al., 2018). The population was community-dwelling Filipinos aged ≥ 60 years, defined as non-institutionalized older adults in the Philippines. Studies of Filipino diaspora populations living outside of the Philippines were excluded unless the data specifically referred to residence and community life in the Philippines. The interest was social frailty or related concepts, such as loneliness, social isolation, social participation, social engagement, social support, social network size and quality, availability of a confidant, caregiving, living arrangements, community engagement, digital participation, mobility, service access, and social determinants affecting functioning in later life. Outcomes included structural and sociodemographic risk factors, cultural and community influences, assessment tools, policy responses, and public health or community programs relevant to social frailty. Eligible sources included peer-reviewed empirical studies, reviews, and discussion papers written in English. All studies involving institutionalized or hospitalized older adults, did not examine social frailty and it related constructs, no full text available, and sources cannot be verified were excluded.

Selection and extraction strategy

Following the search, all identified citations were collated within an EndNote library (The EndNote Team, 2013) and uploaded to Covidence (Covidence systematic review software, 2025). Duplicates were either automatically removed by the system or manually by the two reviewers (JIS and JM). Searches, title and abstract screening, full-text review, data charting, and domain mapping were conducted by the two reviewers independently. Disagreements were resolved through discussion until consensus was reached. Data were charted on design, year, population, social frailty related constructs, measures, outcomes, and mapping to Bunt et al.’s domains.

Analysis and synthesis

Included studies were descriptively summarized and mapped to Bunt et al.’s (2017) social frailty four broad domains framework: general resources (indirect but enabling factor for social fulfillment), social resources (refers to people and services a person can depend on, including the size and quality of their social network), social behavior activities (activities like going out, group activities, hobbies, and volunteering), and self-management abilities (taking initiative, investing in relationships and resources, coping with loss, maintaining variety and multifunctionality in resources) (Bunt et al., 2017; Hamid et al., 2024; Huang et al., 2025b, 2024a; Pek et al., 2020). This framework was selected because: (1) it defines social frailty as multidimensional condition involving structural resources, relationships, participation, and capacities needed to fulfill basic social needs (Kastner et al., 2024); (2) preferred over narrower models focused only on isolation, loneliness, or support because it allows interpretation of how structural barriers, relational networks, social participation, and perceived capacities interact in later life (Bessa et al., 2021; Rojas-Avila et al., 2025); and (3) this was suitable for the Philippine evidence base because limited studies used the term social frailty directly, while many examined adjacent construct that could be mapped conceptually to Bunt’s framework. Moreover, the two reviewers followed the definition and description of each domain in domain mapping. When studies addressed more than one domain, the primary domain was assigned according to the study’s main objective and dominant outcome or construct. This approach mirrors mapping strategies employed by Kastner et al.’s (2024) scoping review on social frailty.

Thematic synthesis by social frailty domain

General resources

There are 12 studies under this domain (Abalos, 2018; Carandang et al., 2019; Reyes et al., 2019; Alipio and Pregoner, 2020; Siongco et al., 2021; Abrigo et al., 2022; Nakamura et al., 2022; Albougami et al., 2023; Balikuddembe et al., 2024; Carandang et al., 2024a; Garcia et al., 2025, Garcia et al., 2025).

Studies mapped to this domain point to gaps in financial protection (Abalos, 2018; Abrigo et al., 2022; Reyes et al., 2019), geographic and cost barriers to care (Alipio and Pregoner, 2020; Carandang et al., 2019), uneven service availability (Carandang et al., 2024; Garcia et al., 2025), and workforce constraints that can limit participation and reciprocity in later life (Nakamura et al., 2022; Siongco et al., 2021). Weak health data systems and limited long-term care readiness further constrain the planning needed to address these gaps (Garcia et al., 2025,Garcia et al., 2025). Climate related disasters may heighten social frailty through several pathways: displacement from familiar neighborhoods, interruption of transport and health services, loss of livelihood or household resources, damage to community meeting places, and increased dependence on already strained family caregivers (Albougami et al., 2023; Balikuddembe et al., 2024). For older adults with mobility limitations, disasters can also reduce access to senior citizens’ activities, church gatherings, and routine primary care. These findings suggest that social frailty among older Filipinos is shaped by both structural conditions and opportunities for everyday participation (Balikuddembe et al., 2024).

Social resources

There are 7 studies in this domain (Alvarez et al., 2017; Abalos et al., 2018; Sta Maria et al., 2018; Loa et al., 2023; Estioko et al., 2023; Felipe-Dimog et al., 2024; Takagi et al., 2022). These studies emphasized intergenerational caregiving and kin networks as primary buffers against social loss but also a source of strain within the context of migration and economic difficulties. Family ties often provided older adults with practical assistance, emotional connection, and a sense of continuity. However, these same arrangements could also become sources of strain when caregiving responsibilities, financial pressures, or migration-related separation placed added demands on households (Estioko et al., 2023; Felipe-Dimog et al., 2024).

Beyond the family, faith-based communities were cited as social anchors that provide identity and opportunities for engagement (Sta Maria et al., 2018). Similarly, contact with migrant children may remain emotionally present through Facebook, Messenger, video calls, and group chats helped sustain emotional presence across distance. However, this depends on digital access, affordability, and confidence.

Overall, the studies suggest that social resources were not uniformly available or reliable. Migration, economic difficulty, caregiver burden, living alone, and changing household arrangements could weaken everyday support and reduce opportunities for reciprocity (Abalos et al., 2018; Takagi et al., 2022). Even in a culture where family plays a central role, co-residence may not equate to emotional support. Older adults may live with relatives but still lack a confidant with whom to discuss sensitive concerns, especially when family members are stressed, caregiving is burdensome, or decision-making is dominated by younger household members (Estioko et al., 2023; Loa et al., 2023).

Social behavior activities

There are 11 studies categorized on this domain (Diño and de Guzman, 2015; Carandang et al., 2020; de Guzman and Diño, 2020; Parial et al., 2021, Parial et al., 2022, Parial et al., 2023; Trinidad and A., 2022; Ebardo et al., 2023; Carandang et al., 2024; Gallardo et al., 2024; Tan et al., 2026). In Philippine literature, this domain is reflected in studies of digital and telehealth engagement, group-based physical activity, life-space and mobility, peer support, religious participation, and everyday informal contact such as regular conversation. These activities may reduce social frailty by preserving routine, mobility, confidence, identity, and valued social roles. Conversely, reduced participation may weaken contact, role continuity, and belonging (Carandang et al., 2020; Diño and de Guzman, 2015; Parial et al., 2022, Parial et al., 2021).

Most studies, however, assessed component indicators rather than social frailty as a unified construct. This highlights both the breadth of locally relevant evidence and the continuing gap in direct measurement. A notable development is the validation of the 6-item Social Frailty Index-Philippines, which represents an important step toward measuring social frailty in the local context (Tan et al., 2026). This tool was mapped under the behavioral domain because its items primarily assess participation, interaction, and everyday social engagement, although some overlap with social resources and self-management ability is evident. While the tool offers a brief and practical local screening measure, its focus on observable social engagement may not fully capture the broader structural, relational, and self-management factors that shape social frailty among older Filipinos. In particular, its brevity may limit its ability to account for wider resources, relationship quality, coping capacity, and contextual barriers.

Furthermore, employment and formal volunteering were less frequently examined in the included studies. Nevertheless, the available evidence suggests that productive roles, community engagement, senior citizens’ organization activities, faith-based service, and peer support can help preserve role continuity and social recognition (Carandang et al., 2020; Sta Maria et al., 2018). These protective activities may be constrained by reduced mobility, economic strain, transport barriers, low digital confidence, and disaster-related disruption, all of which may increase social frailty risk (Carandang et al., 2024; Ebardo et al., 2023).

Self-management abilities

Five studies were mapped to this domain (Soriano et al., 2016; Carandang et al., 2020; Salvador and Alqahtani, 2020; Kim et al., 2021; Tan et al., 2025). In the included studies, this domain was often addressed indirectly through themes such as spirituality, role adaptation in later life, subjective well-being, affection, remarriage, quality of life, and community recognition. Meaning and purpose appeared to be sustained through faith practices, reciprocal roles, and a sense of belonging, whereas losses in role mobility or connection were linked to diminished purpose and lower life satisfaction.

Compared with other domains, this aspect of social frailty appears less directly studied in the Philippine literature and is often embedded in broader quality-of-life or aging narratives. Nevertheless, this domain is important because older adults may remain socially vulnerable even when they are not formally isolated. Social vulnerability may arise when older adults experience diminished recognition, weakened reciprocity, or reduced social worth (Bunt et al., 2017; Carandang et al., 2020).

Across the five studies in this domain, self-management abilities were reflected in how older adults coped with aging-related losses, maintained affection and intimate relationships, adapted to changing social roles, sustained subjective well-being, and sought recognition through family and community participation. Filipino-specific patterns included reliance on spirituality, family reciprocity, community belonging, and digital contact with geographically distant relatives or peers (Carandang et al., 2020; Tan et al., 2025). In general, these findings suggest that social frailty is shaped not only by the presence or absence of social contact, but also whether older adults continue to experience purpose, recognition, reciprocity, and social value in later life.

Discussion

Principal findings

This narrative review suggests that social frailty among community-dwelling older adults in the Philippines is shaped by structural disadvantage, changing family and community arrangements, uneven access to resources, and limited social participation opportunities. The strongest evidence focuses on general resources and social behavior activities, while fewer studies address social resources and self-management abilities. This suggests the literature has focused on observable social conditions like care access, financing, mobility, participation, and digital engagement, but has less often examined relational and experiential dimensions of social frailty such as belonging, perceived social worth, reciprocity, confidants, and sustaining meaningful social roles.

Structural determinants or barriers refer to system-level conditions that shape opportunities for social connection and support, including financing, transportation, service availability, health workforce capacity, digital infrastructure, disaster exposure and continuity of health and social care (Holt-Lunstad, 2022; Meehan et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2023). In the Philippine context, these structural determinants shape the other social frailty domains beyond immediate system-level barriers that constrain older adults’ ability to use their relationships, participate in community life, and access needed care and support (Reyes et al., 2023). For instance, an older adult may have family support but still become socially frail if transport is unavailable, health services are fragmented, digital communication is inaccessible, or disasters disrupt routines and care.

Filipino older adults commonly interact with the wider community through senior citizens associations, barangay-based activities, faith-based organizations, peer groups, family networks, and local government programs (Sta Maria et al., 2018; Carandang et al., 2019). More than social contact, these spaces foster support identity, information sharing, access to benefits, spiritual life, and reciprocal roles. Social frailty may reduce participation in these spaces by limiting mobility, confidence, resources, or available support, thereby weakening both informal and formal protective systems (Hamid et al., 2024; Rojas-Avila et al., 2025).

Interpretation in relation to the broader literature

The present findings align with international literature showing that SF is linked to adverse outcomes, with social determinants, participation, and support systems crucial for healthy ageing (Lee et al., 2020; Li et al., 2023; Ma et al., 2018). However, the Philippine evidence adds contextual specificity. Family-centered support functions as both a protective resource and a vulnerability site when younger family members migrate for work, caregiving intensifies, or household resources are insufficient (Abalos et al., 2018; Carandang et al., 2019; Estioko et al., 2023). Faith-based communities provide routine interaction, identity, social recognition, and meaning, contributing to social resources and self-management capacities like belonging and role fulfillment (Sta. Maria et al., 2018; Soriano et al., 2016; Salvador and Alqahtani, 2020). Digital technologies, including mobile phones, Facebook, messaging applications, telehealth, and online communities, are emerging tools for maintaining relationships and accessing care, especially when family members are geographically dispersed; however, affordability, trust, skills, and confidence remain barriers (de Guzman and Diño, 2020; Gallardo et al., 2024; Carandang et al., 2024). Financing gaps, workforce limitations, uneven service availability, and disaster-related interruptions can all reduce older adults’ capacity to maintain mobility, engagement, and reciprocity (Abrigo et al., 2022; Nakamura et al., 2022; Siongco et al., 2021). In the Philippine setting, where floods and typhoons may disrupt daily routines and access to community support, social frailty may be intensified by shocks that interrupt care delivery and ordinary social life (Balikuddembe et al., 2024).

Overall, the availability of general resources shapes all other social frailty domains. Social resources, participation, and self-management abilities depend on transport, income security, accessible services, communication infrastructure, and disaster resilience (Huang et al., 2024b; Li et al., 2024). This interdependence explains why older adults with similar family structures may experience different social frailty risks depending on local resource availability.

Measurement and conceptual considerations

This review highlights important conceptual and measurement issues. Many studies examined component constructs like social support, loneliness, living arrangements, mobility, technology use, or well-being.

The 6-item SF Index-Philippines is an important advance, shifting from proxy indicators to direct assessment and providing a foundation for future screening and outcomes research in the local setting (Tan et al., 2026). However, future validation should examine associations with disability, hospitalization, health service use, quality of life, and mortality across Philippine subgroups (Goto et al., 2024; Hamid et al., 2024; Tan et al., 2026).

Social frailty tools are also conceptually distinct from existing social-domain measures. For instance, the Lubben Social Network Scale focuses on network size and perceived support, while the WHO Quality of Life-BREF includes a social relationships domain. Social frailty tools add value by integrating participation, support, vulnerability to disruption, and capacities needed to fulfill social needs (Hamid et al., 2024; Rojas-Avila et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2023). Similarly, within comprehensive geriatric assessment, social domains are typically considered alongside medical, functional, cognitive, and psychological domains which may be considered as a contextual information (Bessa et al., 2018; Webber et al., 2025). Social frailty tools are distinct as it is purposely built to identify vulnerability arising from the combined deficits in social resources, participation, relationships, general resources, and the capacity to fulfil social needs (Huang et al., 2025a).

Moreover, the relative scarcity of studies on self-management abilities is important. This domain encompasses the subjective and relational capacities through which older adults maintain affection, respect, meaningful contact, and valued social roles (Bunt et al., 2017). In the Philippine literature, these issues were often embedded within broader discussions of spirituality, adaptation, life satisfaction, or role change rather than being examined directly as part of social frailty (Soriano et al., 2016; Salvador and Alqahtani, 2020; Carandang et al., 2020). This gap matters because older adults may remain socially vulnerable even when not formally isolated, particularly if they experience diminished recognition, weakened reciprocity, or reduced sense of purpose. Greater attention to this domain would deepen understanding of how social frailty is lived and perceived, not only how it is externally observed.

Implication for nursing practice and policy

The findings have implications for nursing, primary care, and community-based ageing policy. Social frailty sensitive assessment should be considered during routine contacts with older adults, especially in primary care, community outreach, and post-disaster settings. Assessment should consider access barriers, caregiving reliability, mobility, digital exclusion, and meaningful social roles, not just living alone or activity participation. A multidimensional approach aligns with the reviewed literature and gerontological perspectives on frailty screening and assessment (Gobbens and Uchmanowicz, 2023; Walston et al., 2018).

Evidence supports cautious exploration of community-based responses to strengthen participation and connection. Potential approaches include group activities, mobility support, digital literacy, telehealth, and referral to community or faith-based groups. These should be framed as promising and contextually plausible strategies, not yet evidence-established solutions for the Philippine setting. Given the limited and descriptive local evidence base, intervention models should be carefully adapted and evaluated using pragmatic outcomes relevant to older adults’ daily lives (Kastner et al., 2024; Paquet et al., 2023; Percival et al., 2022; Smith et al., 2019).

Existing Philippine senior-citizen responses include benefits and entitlements such as discounts, Philippine Health National Insurance related coverage, social pensions mechanisms, Office of the Senior Citizens Affairs (OSCA) coordination, and barangay and local-government senior-citizen activities (Reyes et al., 2019). These mechanisms address important material and service needs, but the findings of this review suggests that they may not systematically identify older adults with weakened networks, lack of confidants, digital exclusions, caregiver strain, disaster-related social disruption, or loss of meaningful roles. Social frailty screening and referral pathways could therefore complement existing benefit-oriented approaches.

At the practice level, community health nurses can screen for social frailty during routine visits and connect older adults to senior citizen groups, faith-based networks, peer support, telehealth assistance, and caregiver navigation. At the local government level, barangays and OSCA can strengthen transport support, digital-literacy activities, disaster continuity plans, and referral pathways. At the national policy and education level, the Department of Health and nursing education and regulatory bodies can integrate social frailty assessment, caregiver support, and digital inclusion into older-adult care standards and training.

Limitations

As a narrative review, this study prioritized conceptual synthesis over formal risk-of-bias appraisal. The broad and transparent search strategy may have missed relevant local literature, especially studies published outside indexed databases. The evidence base was dominated by qualitative and cross-sectional studies, limiting causal inference and conclusions about directionality or intervention effectiveness. Many included studies assessed constructs adjacent to social frailty, which broadened the scope but introduced conceptual heterogeneity. Some findings may not fully reflect recent policy, technological, or service changes, but they remain useful for understanding enduring features of ageing and social vulnerability in the Philippines.

Conclusion

Social frailty among older Filipinos is not simply a matter of isolation; it emerges at the intersection of structural disadvantage, changing family support, community participation, digital access, and self-management capacities. Addressing social frailty requires integrated assessment and culturally sensitive measurement that reflect Filipino family, community, faith-based, economic, caregiving, migration, and digital contexts. Nursing practice and policy should strengthen community systems while reducing barriers to mobility, participation, communication, caregiving continuity, and access to support. Future research should validate Philippine social-frailty tools across diverse subgroups, and evaluate community-based interventions that preserve meaningful roles, relationships, and social participation in later life.
