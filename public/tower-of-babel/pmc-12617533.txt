The Value of Chest and Abdominal CT Scans in Deceased Organ Donor Screening: A Prospective Nationwide Cohort Study
Authors: Kinita A. Chotkan, Ludo F.M. Beenen, Jacobus W. Mensink, Martin B.A. Heemskerk, Joyce Deggens, Niels P. van der Kaaij, Willemijn N. Nijboer, Wojtek G. Polak, Ian P.J. Alwayn, Thomas G.V. Cherpanath, Luuk B. Hilbrands, Robert A. Pol, Andries E. Braat
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background.Standard computed tomography (CT) scanning in deceased organ donor screening may offer advantages over conventional chest radiograph and abdominal ultrasound.Methods.A nationwide prospective cohort study in the Netherlands was conducted, including all deceased organ donors between July 2022 and April 2024. There were 2 groups: those who received standard chest and abdominal CT scan (study group, n = 330) with a new standardized scan protocol and those screened according to the current protocol (chest radiograph and abdominal ultrasound, n = 224, or chest or abdominal CT scan already performed in the clinical workup, n = 162, together the control group, n = 386). Outcomes included the detection of suspicious lesions during screening or procurement, the incidence of procurement-related injuries, explantation time, and transplant outcomes.Results.During screening, suspicious lesions were identified in 19 donors (5.8%) in the study group and in 2 donors (0.5%) in the control group (P < 0.01). This resulted in more biopsies, but the majority were benign. During procurement, suspicious and unexpected lesions were identified in 2 donors (0.7%) in the study group and in 7 donors (2.2%) in the control group (P = 0.13). Kidneys from the study group had a lower rate of procurement-related injuries (16% versus control 23%; P < 0.01). No significant difference was observed for livers and pancreases. Nephrectomy (36 versus 39 min) and hepatectomy (31 versus 33 min) times were significantly shorter in the study group. No significant difference in kidney function up to 1-y posttransplantation was seen.Conclusions.In conclusion, CT scanning improves deceased donor screening by reducing unexpected findings during procurement, thereby lowering the risk of procurement-related kidney graft injuries and shortening explantation times.

INTRODUCTION

The Eurotransplant (ET) protocol currently mandates abdominal ultrasound (US) and chest radiograph (CXR) as the minimum radiological workup for deceased organ donor screening. However, these modalities have limitations in assessing organ quality, vascular abnormalities, and detecting malignancies.1,2 Currently, chest and abdominal computed tomography (CT) scans have been introduced as alternative, radiological screening methods in several countries, as they can provide more detailed information on detecting malignancies, visualizing vascular aberrant anatomy, assessing organ quality, and size matching in lung and liver transplantation.2-4 Limited comparative data exist between the 2 radiological screening methods.5-8 A few advantages of CT scanning have been described. First, screening with chest and abdominal CT has been shown to reduce intraoperative malignant tumor detection by 30%, as earlier detection during the screening process can result in early termination of the donation.2 Although the estimated risk of tumor transmission in solid organ transplantation is low (0.01%–0.05%), the impact on transplant recipients if this occurs can be devastating.9-13

Secondly, aberrant vascular anatomy of the kidney and liver is common, occurring in 24%–28% and 25%–45%, respectively.14-18 Preoperative knowledge of these anatomical variations could result in improved preoperative planning and consequently reduce procurement-related injury, explantation time, and graft loss.19-22 Additionally, aberrant arterial vascularization between the liver and pancreas may affect transplantation for the suitability of these organs.23

Third, in liver transplantation, size matching is important, as a significant size mismatch can lead to functional complications.24,25 While CT scans are routinely performed in living liver donation to assess total liver volume, the application in deceased donation could help prevent large-for-size syndrome and improve graft survival.26-32 In lung transplantation, current size evaluation relies on predicted total lung capacity, as a surrogate marker of total lung capacity, calculated using a formula known with limited accuracy.33 Chest CT could improve lung size prediction, enhance donor-recipient size matching and preoperative planning.6,33

Next to the positive side, there are also some challenges to be met. First, CT scans are more expensive than CXR and US. Secondly, incidental findings on chest and abdominal CT are common, with a reported prevalence of 40%–75%, depending on the population characteristics. Among these findings, 2.5%–12% required additional evaluation.34-37 This could lead to more diagnostic procedures, a delay in procurement, or even canceling organ procurement without adequate knowledge and substantiation. Third, to assess parenchyma and vascular anatomy, contrast-enhanced CT is necessary. The administration of contrast medium has been associated with contrast-induced acute kidney injury, but different studies have shown that contrast medium administered to potential donors before procurement does not influence transplant outcome.38-41 Finally, transporting critically ill patients to the radiology department poses logistical challenges, increases the workload of intensive care employees, and may introduce additional risks.42

This study aims to assess the impact of standard chest and abdominal CT scans on procurement-related injury of abdominal organs and the incidence of detecting suspicious lesions during screening and procurement, as compared with the current protocol, which includes only abdominal US and CXR.

MATERIAL AND METHODS

Study Design and Population

A nationwide prospective cohort study was conducted, including all deceased donors reported in the Netherlands between July 1, 2022, and April 30, 2024, comparing standardized chest and abdominal CT scans according to the new scan and reporting protocol (study group), to the current protocol (control group). According to the current protocol, an abdominal US and CXR are minimal requirements, but in some cases, a chest and/or abdominal CT scan was already performed during admission (eg, trauma screening). Since these CT scans were not made according to the new scan and reporting protocol and were not reviewed by the procurement surgeon, these donors are part of the control group. The control group, therefore, consists of 2 subgroups: the control-CXR + US group and the nonstudy CT group.

The implementation of the new screening protocol followed a stepwise approach. The Netherlands is divided into 3 distinct procurement regions, with the new radiological workup introduced in stages: the first region adopted screening in January 2023, the second after 6 mo, and the third after 12 mo. All donors in the region where the new method was introduced were automatically included in the study group. Donors who could not undergo a CT scan because of hemodynamic instability were excluded.

The study protocol was approved by the Central Ethics Review Board of the University Medical Center Groningen (registration no. 202200100) and adhered to the principles outlined in the world medical association Declaration of Helsinki and Declaration of Istanbul. Written consent for all procedures was obtained from patients and/or their bereaved, according to Dutch legislation.

CT Scan Protocol

For the CT scans in the study group, at least an enhanced chest CT, followed by an enhanced CT abdomen in the arterial and portal venous phases, was performed according to the standard protocol of the local center. The minimum required information for the structured report is outlined in Appendix A (SDC, https://links.lww.com/TXD/A808). The CT scans were reviewed by the radiologist on-call in the donor hospital, who was not specifically trained. There was no difference in scan protocol between donation after circulatory death and donation after brain death donors.

In the region where the new screening method had not yet been implemented, a standardized template for the CXR and abdominal US was used (Appendix B, SDC, https://links.lww.com/TXD/A808). In the cases that a CT scan was available because of other clinical indications, the existing report was uploaded to the donor information system, or, if no prior report was available, the CT scan was reviewed and reported using the abdominal US template (according to agreements of the current protocol).

Sample Size Calculation

The necessary sample size was calculated based on the incidence of procurement-related organ injury. The incidence of procurement-related injuries in the Netherlands is approximately 25% for procured abdominal organs.21 For sample size calculation, a reduction in surgical injury to 15% was assumed. Sample size calculation using these estimates was performed, with α = 0.05 and a power of 80%, using power calculation for categorical data. According to this calculation, 250 organs per group (total n = 500) would be needed to achieve statistical significance.

Data Source

Donor baseline characteristics were retrieved from the ET database, and transplant recipient follow-up data were obtained from the Netherlands Organ Transplant Registry (NOTR). Including only follow-up of Dutch transplant recipients. If follow-up data from a recipient were missing, the recipient was excluded. Additional information regarding the donation procedure, including information regarding the impact of the imaging method on the donation process, was collected through a preformed questionnaire sent to donation professionals. The CT scans could not be shared with transplant centers, only the structured radiology report was available. However, the procuring surgeon was able to review the CT scan at the donor hospital before the procurement. If this were not possible, organs from these donors were excluded from the analyses of procurement-related injury.

Study Endpoints and Definitions

Primary outcome measures were the incidence of procurement-related organ injury and the incidence of suspicious (malignant) lesions identified during procurement. Secondary outcome were explantation time and short-term transplant outcome of kidney and liver transplantation (see for these definitions Appendix C [SDC, https://links.lww.com/TXD/A808]). Additionally, the impact of imaging methods on decision-making regarding the organ suitability was assessed.

A suspicious lesion was defined as a lesion found during screening, which is malignant or could be malignant. Suspicious lesions were defined according to the guidelines of the American Radiology Committee on incidental findings for liver, renal masses, and pancreatic cysts.43-45 For incidental pulmonary findings, the Fleischner criteria were used.46 Procurement-related injury was defined as a possible preventable injury.

In the Netherlands, procurement surgeons are nationally trained. The procurement technique used is described in a national protocol.47 Data were collected using the “Quality Forms” application, which was completed by the procuring surgeon and reviewed on agreement by the transplanting surgeon. The Quality Form includes the assessment of arterial and venous anatomy as well as organ injury.21

Explantation time was defined as the interval from the start of cold aortic perfusion to the removal of the organ from the donor’s body.1 Aberrant vascular anatomy of the kidney was defined as the presence of multiple arteries and/or veins. Aberrant vascular anatomy of the liver was defined according to Hiatt’s classification.48 Definition of other terms can be found in Appendix C (SDC, https://links.lww.com/TXD/A808).

Statistical Analyses

Continuous data are presented as mean ± SD. Categorical data are presented as percentages (%) and absolute numbers. Parametric tests were used to assess the differences between continuous variables. The chi-square test was used to assess differences between categorical data. Statistical significance was set at P ≤ 0.05. Kaplan Meyer survival analysis was performed to assess the difference in 6-mo survival rate between the groups.

For statistical analyses, IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows was used (IBM Corp. Released 2022. Version 29.0).

RESULTS

Between July 2022 and April 2024, a total of 716 potential organ donors in the Netherlands were reported to ET. Of these, 330 donors in the study group and 386 donors were included in the control group (Figure 1; Table 1). One donor in the study group did not receive a CT scan because of high mechanical ventilation pressure settings and was excluded. Additionally, in 13 donation procedures the CT scan was unavailable for review by the procuring surgeon before the procedure. Consequently, the organs from these donors were excluded from the procurement-related injury and explantation time analyses. From the region where the new method was not (yet) introduced (the control group), 386 were reported, of which 224 donors (58%) received an abdominal US and CXR (control-CXR + US group), whereas 162 donors (42%) had already received a CT scan before inclusion in the study (nonstudy CT group). Indications for these CT scans were trauma screening during admission (53%, n = 83), screening for organ donation after euthanasia (14%, n = 22), further diagnostics because of findings on the US or CXR (10%, n = 16) or medical history considerations, that is, severe obesity precluding reliable US (2%, n = 2) or a previous history with malignancy (5%, n = 8).

Baseline donor characteristics were comparable between the control group and the study group (Table 1). However, when comparing the control-CXR + US group with the study group, the latter had a significantly higher incidence of trauma-related causes of death (Table 1). This difference is likely because of the higher proportion of trauma-related cases in the nonstudy CT group (23%), compared with those who only underwent CXR and US (7%), as trauma screening was the most frequent indication for performing a CT scan in the control group.

Eventually, 117 donation procedures did not lead to a procurement procedure, in 79% of the cases because of an agonal phase >2 h (Table 2). In 33 of the donors, no organs were transplanted after procurement (13 procedures in the study group). The percentage of donors, of which at least 1 organ was transplanted, was significantly higher in the study group compared with the control-CXR + US group (81% versus 74%; P = 0.03).

Influence of the Imaging Method on Policy Changes

In the study group, a total of 55 organs (10 kidneys, 12 livers, 4 pancreases, and 29 pairs of lungs) were declined because of the quality of the organ as seen on the CT scan (Table 3). Table S2 (SDC, https://links.lww.com/TXD/A809) provides detailed information about the specific findings leading to this decision. Relevant findings influencing organ acceptance seen on CT were, for example, renal cysts seen on CT, renal atrophy, cirrhosis, steatosis, atherosclerosis, and lung quality (emphysema, fibrosis). In total, 88 donation procedures were prematurely ceased, before being reported to ET and are therefore not included in the analyses of this study. Because of the premature cessation of the procedure, limited donor characteristics are available. In 41 of the cases, this was because of the medical history, which turned out to be unacceptable (of which 18 were in the region of the study group). Of these 41 cases, 14 donors were not reported solely because of on imaging findings (Table S3, SDC, https://links.lww.com/TXD/A809). No frozen sections were performed, so in case of indeterminate lesions, there was no confirmation of malignancy.

Suspicious Lesions Found During Screening or Procurement

During screening, suspicious lesions were identified in 1 of 224 (0.4%) in the control-CXR + US group and in 1 of 162 (0.6%) of the cases in the nonstudy CT group. In the study group, 19 of 330 (5.8%) suspicious lesions were found, which was significantly more compared with the control group (Table 2). Three lesions were identified during bronchoscopy for evaluation of the lungs.

During procurement (considering only the number of donors that had a procurement procedure), suspicious lesions, not previously seen during screening, were found in 5 of 176 (2.8%) cases in the control-CXR + US group and 2 of 143 (1.4%) in the nonstudy CT group. In the study group, 2 of 280 (0.7%) cases had an undetected suspicious lesion was found, this difference was not statistically significant compared with the control-CXR + US group (P = 0.07; Table 2).

Details of the suspicious lesions are provided in Table S4 (SDC, https://links.lww.com/TXD/A809). In 30 cases, a frozen section was taken during procurement (9 in the control group and 21 in the study group). Malignancy was confirmed in 8 cases (4 in each group). In 1 case, the definite pathology result contradicted the frozen section; the frozen section had ruled out a malignancy, while the definitive pathology reported a lymphoma. In the study group, 2 procedures were ceased, and no organs were transplanted because of the result of the frozen section. In the CXR-US group, this happened 4 times. No donors with benign lesions were excluded.

Procurement-related Injury, Explantation Time, and Aberrant Anatomy

Kidney

Of all the included donors, 1063 kidneys were procured, of which 937 were transplanted. In total, 126 kidneys were not transplanted; reasons for not transplanting a procured kidney are described in Table S5 (SDC, https://links.lww.com/TXD/A809).

In the study group, the percentage of procurement-related injuries in kidney grafts was significantly lower compared with the control group (16% versus 23%; P < 0.01) and lower compared with the CXR + US group (16% versus 28%; P < 0.01; Table 4). In the control group, in 69% of the kidneys, the type of injury was not specified, in 6% arterial injury was reported, in 7% venous injury, and in 17% parenchymal injury. In the study group, in 64% the type of injury was not reported, in 11% arterial injury was reported, in 8% venous injury, and in 15% parenchymal injury. In the control group, 1 graft was specifically reported to be discarded because of procurement-related injury and in the study group, 2 grafts (Table S5, SDC, https://links.lww.com/TXD/A809).

Liver

A total of 459 livers were procured, of which 320 were transplanted. In total, 139 livers were not transplanted; reasons for not transplanting a procured liver are described in Table S5 (SDC, https://links.lww.com/TXD/A809). The percentage of procurement-related injury did not differ significantly (Table 4). In the control group, 18% (n = 12) of the livers had arterial injury, 3% (n = 2) had venous injury, 62% (n = 42) had parenchymal injury, and in 18% (n = 12), the type of injury was not reported. In the study group, 23% (n = 10) had arterial injury, 5% (n = 2) had venous injury, 65% (n = 28) had parenchymal injury, and in 7% (n = 3), it was not reported. These differences were not significantly different between the groups. In the control group, no graft was specifically reported to be discarded solely because of procurement-related injury, and in the study group, 1 graft (Table S5, SDC, https://links.lww.com/TXD/A809).

However, the explantation time was shortest in the study group compared with the CXR + US group (31 versus 33 min; P < 0.01).

Pancreas

A total of 134 pancreases were procured, of which 33 were transplanted. Reasons for not transplanting a procured pancreas are described in Table S5 (SDC, https://links.lww.com/TXD/A809). The percentage of procurement-related injury and explantation time in pancreas grafts did not differ significantly between the groups (Table 4).

Short-term Kidney Transplant Outcome

Of 937 kidneys transplanted, 779 were transplanted in the Netherlands, and follow-up was registered in the NOTR. Recipient characteristics were comparable between the 3 groups (control-CXR + US, nonstudy CT, and study group), except for the preservation method, more kidneys in the control-CXR + US and nonstudy CT groups were placed on cold storage (Table S6, SDC, https://links.lww.com/TXD/A809). After stratifying for recipients who received a kidney from donation after brain death and donation after circulatory death donors, no significant differences were found in immediate graft function, delayed graft function, and primary nonfunction among the 3 groups (Table 5). Recipient estimated glomerular filtration rate at 3 mo and at 1-y follow-up did not differ between the groups.

Short-term Transplant Liver Transplant Outcome

Of the 320 transplanted livers, 259 were transplanted in the Netherlands, and follow-up was registered in the NOTR. Recipient characteristics were comparable between the 2 groups (control versus study group), except for cold ischemic time in hours, which was the shortest in the study group (Table S7, SDC, https://links.lww.com/TXD/A809). At 3 mo after transplantation, no differences were observed in liver enzymes (aspartate aminotransferase, alanine transaminasen, bilirubin, gamma-glutamyltransferase, and alkaline phosphatase), but at 6 mo, the levels of aspartate aminotransferase and alanine transaminasen were the lowest in the study group (Table S8, SDC, https://links.lww.com/TXD/A809).

Kaplan-Meier survival analysis showed no significant differences in death-censored graft survival 6 mo posttransplantation between the control and study groups (P = 0.42; Figure 2). A retransplantation was performed in 8% (n = 12) of the recipients in the control group versus 3.5% (n = 4) in the study group (P = 0.11).

DISCUSSION

This is the first study, to our knowledge, that compares the impact of 2 types of radiological diagnostics on the organ donation process (CXR + US versus chest + abdominal CT scan) in a large cohort with a control group. Procurement-related organ injury was lower in the study group, seen in all abdominal organs taken together, specifically in kidney grafts, where the injury rates decreased from 28% to 16%. Nephrectomy and hepatectomy times were significantly shorter in the study group. Standard screening of deceased organ donors with CT scan more frequently revealed (potential malignant) lesions and resulted in fewer lesions detected during organ procurement. Also, in the study group, the utilization rate was higher compared with the control group. Kidney graft function was similar in both groups, indicating that exposure of donor kidneys to contrast medium does not negatively impact transplant outcome.

Compared with our study, in France, 2 small single-center studies found that performing whole-body computed tomography angiography (CTA) led to cessation of the donation procedure in 6% and 13% of cases because of a medical contraindication identified on CT. They concluded that CTA is valuable in graft selection during multiple organ donation procedures and in assisting the procuring surgeon to anticipate technical difficulties.5,7 Although, these studies did not include a control group, they undermine the value of included CT scans in the deceased organ donor screenings process. In 2020, Lormans et al4 conducted a retrospective study of 25 donors of whom 52% underwent chest and abdominal CT scan and 48% underwent CXR and abdominal US. This study proposed to perform CT scanning for extended criteria donors (ie, body mass index > 30, cardiovascular/metabolic comorbidity, or age >60 y) or when detailed anatomic information was needed. However, because of the small sample size, it is difficult to draw any conclusions from this study.4

Our study indicates that knowledge of vascular anatomy beforehand reduces injury and shortens explantation time. Since the explantation time is only shortened by a couple of minutes, the clinical relevance of this reduction is small. Nevertheless, CT scanning was of added value in specific cases, as assessing organ quality in lung donors, detecting severe atherosclerosis, and identifying cirrhosis and steatosis. Furthermore, CT scanning improves organ donation safety by providing more detailed imaging, excluding the possibility of potential malignancies, or facilitating further investigation, such as a targeted biopsy, with frozen section, either before or during the donor surgery. Knowing whether a frozen section is needed improves logistics and enhances time efficiency. Also, we saw a significantly higher utilization rate in the study group. A possible explanation could be that performing a CT scan contributes to better selection of donor quality and contraindications during screening, leading to decreasing nonutilization rate. This suggests that a CT scan could be a valuable tool in optimizing the use of healthcare resources.

On the other hand, the use of a CT scan could have a negative impact on organ donation. Incidental findings could lead to the nonuse of organs or donors even without pathological confirmation of these findings. When the radiologist classifies a lesion as indeterminate (uncertain malignant or benign), this could inadvertently lead to cessation of a donation procedure. This occurred in only a few cases (n = 5 in the study group, n = 6 in the control group). An increase in incidental findings also led to an increase in the number of frozen sections during procurement, which increases the costs of pathology services. Yet, when a frozen section shows signs of a malignancy, this could prevent transmission of malignancy to the recipient, avoiding the tragic consequences of such transmission, as reported in some prior cases.11-13 Additionally, procedures that are ceased prematurely save costs, although a CT scan is more expensive compared with CXR and abdominal US. The calculation of the cost/benefit ratio of standardizing CT scans is complex because of the immense variation in costs associated with each donation procedure (intensive care unit admission duration, diagnostics used, procurement equipment, and machine perfusion) and demands a dedicated cost-effectiveness analysis, which falls beyond the scope of this article. Nevertheless, important to mention that performing a chest and abdominal CT scan costs approximately €400 in comparison with a CXR and abdominal US, which is < €150.49

Another possible disadvantage of a standard CT scan is the potential negative impact of contrast medium administration on the donor kidneys before procurement. This study showed that there was no impact on the incidence of delayed graft function or on estimated glomerular filtration rate values at 3-mo and 1-y posttransplantation, which is in accordance with existing literature.39-41 Also, only 1 donor could not be transported to the CT scanner because of hemodynamic instability, a potential problem that was anticipated, but proved to be rare. Furthermore, no incidents occurred during transport to or at the CT scanner. Nevertheless, performing CT scans on patients on life support is demanding for intensive care unit staff and radiologists (on-call).

Several potential limitations of this study should be addressed. Although the images of the CT scans were available for review by the procuring surgeon before the procurement procedure, they were not available to the transplant physician/center receiving the organ offer because of lacking the existence of the infrastructure meeting the criteria of the competent authority to safely share the images together with the information of the organ offer to the transplant center. Therefore, transplant centers relied on written reports of the CT scans, which made reviewing the anatomy, organ quality, and size matching, essential in lung and liver transplantation, more difficult. When sharing of the images during the allocation process will be possible, this will even further enhance the potential benefit of donor screening with CT scanning.

Unfortunately, not every case followed the template for reviewing CT scans. In some cases, discrepancies were noted between the information in the CT reports and the donor records filled out by the procuring surgeon. There were marked discrepancies that influenced organ acceptance in several cases (n = 7, for which this was specifically reported). Examples included the identification of multiple renal arteries during procurement but not reported during initial allocation, a renal aneurysm not reported, or wrongly reporting a short length of the kidney. The potential benefit of reviewing the CT scans by a centralized and dedicated radiologist is currently being investigated.

Another limitation was the exclusion of analyses for transplant outcomes in pancreas and lung recipients because of small numbers and limited follow-up data available in the NOTR, respectively.

Standardized CT scan use was introduced stepwise. In regions where the new method was not yet implemented, a higher-than-expected percentage of donors (42%) had already received a CT scan. Since these CT scans were not reported according to the new template, this group was treated and analyzed as a separate group. Because of this high number of CT scans in the control group, the intended control group (donors who received CXR + abdominal US) was smaller than we expected. The intended sample size of 250 organs to find a significant difference in procurement-related injury was more than achieved. However, with a larger sample size for each organ specific, for liver grafts, a significant difference may have been found as well. Also, the differences in finding undetected suspicious lesion during procurement between the control-CXR + US group and the study group was near significance. Since the incidence of suspicious lesions found during procurement was relatively low, it was difficult to reliably power the study on this outcome beforehand.

To assess the suitability of a heart for transplantation, cardiac US and coronary CTA or coronary angiography (reference standard) are required. Previous research has shown that coronary CTA has a high negative predictive value, while the positive predictive value varies in the literature between 51% and 97%.50-52 Since the experience with performing coronary CTA in combination with chest CT and enhanced abdominal CT varies considerably between hospitals, the value of coronary CTA in the screening process was not explored. An interesting possibility for the near future could be to replace the invasive coronary angiography by a noninvasive coronary CTA, as increasingly is done in regular patient care to exclude coronary pathology.53

Ultimately, each organ procurement organization should weigh the pros, cons, and limitations of use of CT scans in the deceased organ donation process. We do not see barriers for broad implementation of this work method. Before the start of this study, there was some hesitation regarding the risk and workload on transporting a donor to the CT scanner on the radiology department, the influence on kidney function and the impact of incidental findings. However, this study showed that these affairs form no issue for the donation process, and even has a positive influence on the utilization rate. After the study period was finished, partly thanks to the results of this study, performing a CT scan in the donation process was widely implemented in the Netherlands.

Currently, CT scans are routinely performed for presurgical planning and are widely used for screening in living kidney and liver donations.26,35,54,55 The future possibility of sharing CT scans, together with the organ offer and 3D imaging software, in size-matched lung and liver grafts, could potentially further reduce organ reallocation and shorten allocation time altogether.56-60

In conclusion, CT scanning proved to be beneficial in deceased donor screening. Procurement-related injuries to kidney grafts were reduced, and explantation time was shorter. Fewer suspicious lesions were found during procurement. No impact was observed on 1-y kidney transplant outcomes, indicating that contrast administration is safe for donor kidneys.
