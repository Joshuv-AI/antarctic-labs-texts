Association of HbA1c levels with neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio, monocyte-to-lymphocyte ratio, and platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio in patients with diabetes mellitus: a case–control study
Authors: Esra D Kahvecioglu, Gulsen Sener, Basak Can, Ihsan Ayhan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe prevalence of diabetes mellitus in the general population has been increasing in recent years, and diabetes-related complications contribute substantially to morbidity, mortality, and healthcare system burden. This study aimed to evaluate the relationship between glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) levels and inflammatory markers, including the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), monocyte-to-lymphocyte ratio (MLR), and platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR), in individuals diagnosed with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM).MethodsThis retrospective case–control with longitudinal follow-up study included 138 patients with T2DM followed at the Internal Medicine Outpatient Clinic of Başakşehir Çam and Sakura City Hospital (HbA1c > 7%: n = 37; HbA1c < 7%: n = 101) and 118 healthy control subjects. HbA1c, white blood cell (WBC) count, NLR, MLR, and PLR values obtained at baseline and follow-up outpatient visits were retrospectively retrieved from the hospital information system and compared between groups.ResultsGlucose and HbA1c levels differed significantly among the groups, with the highest values observed in the uncontrolled diabetes group (p < 0.001). WBC and neutrophil counts were also significantly higher in patients with uncontrolled diabetes (p < 0.001). Monocyte counts, as well as NLR and MLR levels, were significantly increased in this group (p < 0.05). At the third-month follow-up, patients in the uncontrolled diabetes group exhibited significant changes in glucose, HbA1c, neutrophil, lymphocyte, NLR, and PLR levels (p < 0.05), whereas the change in MLR did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.083). Correlation analysis demonstrated significant positive associations between diabetes duration, glucose, and HbA1c levels and both NLR and MLR (r = 0.316–0.354, p < 0.001). Additionally, moderate positive correlations were observed among NLR, MLR, and PLR at both baseline and third-month measurements (p < 0.001).ConclusionNLR, MLR, and PLR are low-cost and easily accessible inflammatory markers that change in parallel with HbA1c levels and may be useful for monitoring glycemic control and disease progression in T2DM. These findings support the potential clinical utility of inflammation-based risk assessment in patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Keywords: Type 2 diabetes mellitus, Glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), Neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio, Platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio

Introduction

Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) is a metabolic disorder characterized by chronic hyperglycemia resulting from inadequate insulin secretion, impaired insulin action, or a combination of both. Accounting for approximately 90–95% of all diabetes cases worldwide, T2DM has become one of the leading chronic diseases imposing a substantial economic and societal burden on healthcare systems. Chronic inflammation plays a pivotal role in the pathogenesis of T2DM by promoting insulin resistance and pancreatic β-cell dysfunction, ultimately leading to impaired glycemic control [1].

Plasma glycated hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) is a well-established and reliable parameter widely used to assess long-term glycemic control in patients with diabetes. Elevated HbA1c levels are closely associated with poor disease control and an increased risk of diabetes-related complications. In recent years, accumulating evidence has demonstrated that inflammatory processes are critically involved in the pathophysiology of T2DM, often preceding the clinical onset of the disease and contributing to its progression [2, 3].

Beyond being a metabolic disorder, T2DM is a complex systemic disease associated with a wide range of microvascular and macrovascular complications [4]. Hyperglycemia- and insulin resistance–induced proinflammatory pathways play a major role in the development of these complications. Activation of various immune cells leads to a state of chronic, low-grade inflammation, which has been shown to correlate not only with the development of T2DM but also with disease progression and the severity of its complications [5].

Inflammation is now recognized as a key mechanism in the pathogenesis of many chronic diseases, extending far beyond infectious conditions. Among routinely available hematological parameters, the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), monocyte-to-lymphocyte ratio (MLR), and platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR) have emerged as accessible biomarkers reflecting systemic inflammatory status [6, 7]. Previous studies have reported significant elevations in NLR and MLR in various chronic conditions, including malignancies, acute coronary syndromes, chronic kidney disease, diabetic nephropathy, and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease [8, 9]. Notably, NLR has been highlighted as an early marker of subclinical inflammation and disease progression, making it a potentially valuable indicator for assessing inflammatory burden in metabolic disorders such as T2DM [10]. Several studies have further emphasized a significant association between elevated NLR levels and both microvascular and macrovascular complications related to diabetes [1].

In this context, the present study aimed to investigate the relationship between HbA1c levels and the inflammatory markers NLR, MLR, and PLR in patients with T2DM, both with and without adequate glycemic control, as well as in individuals without chronic disease. In addition, we sought to evaluate the potential utility of these low-cost and readily accessible inflammatory indices in the clinical management and monitoring of disease progression in T2DM. Importantly, whereas previous studies have primarily focused on the association between HbA1c and NLR at a single time point, our study simultaneously examines NLR, MLR, and PLR, and further assesses their short-term changes over a three-month follow-up period. This approach provides a more comprehensive perspective on the dynamics of inflammation in relation to glycemic control.

Materials and methods

This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of Başakşehir Çam and Sakura City Hospital (Approval No: 28.08.2024/196). This retrospective case–control with longitudinal follow-up study evaluated patients who attended the Internal Medicine Outpatient Clinic of Başakşehir Çam and Sakura City Hospital between October 2024 and December 2024. Patient data were retrospectively obtained from the hospital information management system and laboratory records in electronic format.

Patients with a newly established or previously diagnosed T2DM were included in the study. In addition, individuals without any known chronic disease who attended the outpatient clinic during the same period were included as the control group. Patients younger than 30 years or older than 80 years, pregnant women, and individuals with malignancy, subacute and chronic infections, obesity, smoking and renal failure, autoimmune disease, or refractory heart failure were excluded from the study.

Baseline data at the initial outpatient visit and follow-up data obtained at the 3-month control visit were retrieved from patient records. The collected variables included age, sex, duration of diabetes, use of insulin and/or oral antidiabetic drugs (OADs), HbA1c levels, fasting plasma glucose, hemoglobin, WBC, platelet count, neutrophil, lymphocyte, and monocyte counts. Inflammatory indices, including the NLR, MLR, and PLR, were calculated from complete blood count parameters.

Patients with T2DM were stratified into two groups based on glycemic control: controlled diabetes (HbA1c < 7%) and uncontrolled diabetes (HbA1c ≥ 7%). Biochemical and hematological parameters were compared between these groups.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). A p value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Prior to the study, an a priori sample size and power analysis was conducted using G*Power to determine the minimum required sample size. Based on an expected effect size of 0.407, a statistical power of %95 and an alpha level of 0.05, the required sample size was calculated as 256 participants. The final sample size of 256 patients was obtained.

Normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. As the data did not meet the assumption of normal distribution, non-parametric statistical methods were applied. Descriptive statistics were presented as median and minimum–maximum values. Comparisons between two independent groups were conducted using the Mann–Whitney U test, while comparisons among more than two independent groups were performed using the Kruskal–Wallis H test, followed by Dunn–Bonferroni post hoc analysis when appropriate. The Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used to compare dependent (paired) measurements. Spearman’s rank correlation analysis was performed to assess the strength and direction of associations between variables.

According to the Spearman correlation analysis, diabetes duration demonstrated a very strong positive correlation with HbA1c1 (r = 0.819, p < 0.001) and a strong positive correlation with Glucose1 (r = 0.736, p < 0.001). Its associations with age (r = 0.448, p < 0.001) and NLR1 (r = 0.354, p < 0.001) were moderate, whereas the correlation with MLR1 (r = 0.228, p < 0.001) was weak but statistically significant. Glucose1 showed a very strong positive correlation with HbA1c1 (r = 0.837, p < 0.001) and a moderate positive correlation with NLR1 (r = 0.314, p < 0.001). Notably, HbA1c1 demonstrated a moderate positive association with NLR1 (r = 0.316, p < 0.001), while its relationship with MLR1 was weaker. No direct correlation between HbA1c1 and PLR1 was observed at baseline. Among inflammatory markers, NLR1 exhibited moderate positive correlations with both PLR1 (r = 0.542, p < 0.001) and MLR1 (r = 0.526, p < 0.001). Additionally, MLR1 and PLR1 were moderately correlated (r = 0.364, p < 0.001), indicating internal consistency among inflammatory indices rather than uniform associations with glycemic parameters. At the three-month follow-up, Glucose2 and HbA1c2 remained strongly correlated (r = 0.600, p < 0.001). NLR2 demonstrated moderate positive correlations with both MLR2 (r = 0.577, p < 0.001) and PLR2 (r = 0.493, p < 0.001). Overall, these findings suggest that NLR and to a lesser extent MLR at baseline are associated with glycemic control, whereas PLR appears to be more closely related to other inflammatory markers and may reflect dynamic changes over time rather than a direct relationship with HbA1c.

Discussion

Although HbA1c is widely used as a primary parameter for monitoring metabolic control in patients with diabetes mellitus, it is well recognized that this marker alone may be insufficient to fully capture the pathogenesis and progression of microvascular and macrovascular complications over the long-term course of the disease. Chronic low-grade inflammation has increasingly been shown to play a key role in the development of type 2 diabetes-related complications, such as diabetic nephropathy, proliferative diabetic retinopathy, and diabetic peripheral neuropathy. In this context, readily accessible and cost-effective systemic inflammatory markers, including NLR, MLR, and PLR, have attracted attention due to their associations with both malignancies and diabetes-related complications, particularly diabetic nephropathy. This study aimed to evaluate the relationship between these inflammatory indices and HbA1c, exploring their potential contribution as additional biomarkers reflecting the inflammatory burden of diabetes and the risk of complications beyond glycemic control.

In our study, the mean age of the uncontrolled DM group was higher than that of the controlled DM group. Consistently, fasting glucose and HbA1c levels were significantly elevated in the uncontrolled group, suggesting that glycemic control may become more challenging with advancing age. This is in line with the large-scale study by Selvin et al., which reported poorer glycemic control among individuals aged 65 years and older. Our findings support the literature and underscore the influence of age on diabetes management and glycemic regulation [11].

We also observed significantly higher NLR and MLR levels in the uncontrolled DM group compared to the controlled group. Similarly, Dayama et al. reported elevated NLR and MLR levels in patients with uncontrolled T2DM. These findings support the concept that type 2 diabetes should be considered not only as a metabolic disorder but also as a systemic disease characterized by chronic low-grade inflammation. The association of increased inflammatory response with poor glycemic control suggests that NLR and MLR may serve as potential biomarkers reflecting the inflammatory burden of diabetes and the risk of complications.

Our study demonstrated a positive correlation between HbA1c levels and both NLR and MLR, consistent with the findings of Sefil et al., who reported a significant positive relationship between HbA1c and NLR in poorly controlled diabetes patients [12]. These results indicate that the inflammatory response increases in parallel with glycemic dysregulation, supporting the critical role of inflammation in the pathogenesis and progression of diabetes. Therefore, systemic inflammatory markers such as NLR and MLR may serve as early indicators for predicting disease progression and complication risk in patients with inadequate glycemic control.

While most studies assess inflammatory markers at a single time point, our study identified significant changes in NLR and PLR at the three-month follow-up in the uncontrolled DM group. These findings suggest that these inflammatory parameters not only reflect disease status but may also provide clinically meaningful information through their temporal dynamics. Indeed, Yazan et al. reported significant associations between HbA1c and both NLR and PLR [13]. Our findings indicate that NLR and PLR reflect an inflammatory response that parallels glycemic control and may serve as dynamic biomarkers for monitoring treatment response, thereby supporting clinical decision-making.

The present study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the retrospective case–control design may introduce selection bias and limits the ability to establish causal relationships between glycemic control and inflammatory markers. Second, the study was conducted at a single center with a relatively limited sample size, which may reduce the generalizability of the findings. In addition, unequal group sizes may have affected the robustness of intergroup comparisons. Although significant associations were observed between HbA1c and inflammatory markers, the analyses were primarily univariate, and adjustment for potential confounders such as age, diabetes duration, treatment modality, and comorbid conditions was not feasible because of the retrospective nature of the study and incomplete clinical data. Furthermore, information regarding treatment modifications and medication adherence during the 3-month follow-up period was not consistently available, limiting the interpretation of longitudinal changes in inflammatory markers. Measurements of NLR, MLR, and PLR were obtained at limited time points and may not fully reflect temporal biological variability. Therefore, the observed associations should be interpreted cautiously, and larger prospective studies with multivariable analyses are needed to confirm the independent clinical utility of these inflammatory markers in patients with T2DM.

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that inflammatory markers particularly NLR and MLR are significantly associated with poor glycemic control in patients with uncontrolled T2DM, while PLR may exhibit temporal variation during short term follow-up. These findings reinforce the concept that chronic low-grade inflammation is closely linked to deteriorating glycemic regulation. Given that NLR, MLR, and PLR are inexpensive, easily accessible, and routinely measured laboratory parameters, they hold promise as practical and supportive inflammatory indicators for monitoring glycemic status, assessing disease progression, and potentially aiding in the early detection of complications in clinical practice. However, considering the retrospective design, the relatively modest strength of the observed associations, and the absence of comprehensive adjustment for confounding variables, these markers should not yet be regarded as definitive tools for clinical decision making. Therefore, large-scale, prospective studies are warranted to establish their independent prognostic value and to clarify their role in the routine follow-up and management of patients with T2DM.
