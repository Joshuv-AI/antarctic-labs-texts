Introduction

Flipped learning has gained increasing attention in health professions education for its potential to enhance student engagement and learning outcomes [1–3]. In flipped learning, also known as the flipped or inverted classroom, students engage with learning materials prior to class, allowing in-class time to focus on collaborative problem-solving tasks. During these sessions, students are encouraged to question, analyze, evaluate, infer, and reflect on their decisions while engaging in activities. Instructors take a facilitator role—stimulating students’ interest, promoting active engagement, and providing timely feedback [4]. These features make flipped learning particularly well-suited for supporting the development of clinical reasoning skills.

Developing clinical reasoning is a fundamental yet complex goal in medical education and there is growing consensus that it should be explicitly addressed across all phases of medical training [5]. For novice learners—particularly preclinical students with limited medical knowledge and clinical exposure—clinical reasoning development requires carefully structured learning environments. Instructional strategies such as the whole-task approach, peer interaction, reflective practice, timely feedback, and knowledge-oriented methods (e.g. illness script theory) have each demonstrated effectiveness in supporting clinical reasoning development [5–8]. However, these strategies are often studied in isolation, with little attention to how they can be coherently integrated into a single, unified instructional design that can provide the multiple forms of support novice learners require.

Case-based flipped learning offers a promising framework for bringing these diverse strategies together into a cohesive and structured learning experience [8]. In this approach, clinical cases serve as the primary learning content, with the ultimate goal of fostering clinical reasoning. Although flipped learning has been increasingly applied in medical education, existing implementations often reduce it to relocating lectures outside the classroom and adding in-class activities [7,9,10], while overlooking broader aspects of instructional design such as systems approaches, whole-task approaches, learner support, cognitive load management, and structured clinical reasoning activities. Some recent approaches have combined flipped learning with team-based learning, but these primarily emphasize cooperative team activities [11,12], rather than systematically addressing the instructional supports needed for novice learners. Because clinical reasoning is cognitively demanding and complex, novices require multiple forms of instructional support that must be deliberately integrated into flipped learning design. While general design guidelines for flipped learning, and some theory-informed studies exist [7,9,13], there remains little specific guidance on how to design effective case-based flipped learning that employs theory-informed instructional strategies to support novice learners in developing clinical reasoning.

This commentary seeks to address this gap by presenting eight practical strategies for designing case-based flipped learning tailored to the development of clinical reasoning in preclinical medical students. These recommendations are grounded in learning science theories and further informed by my teaching and research experience [6,8]. They aim to support medical educators in creating pedagogically sound and engaging flipped learning environments that are responsive to the specific needs of novice learners.

Use an instructional systems development model to systematically design case-based flipped learning

To create an effective, efficient, and repeatable flipped learning experience, it is critical to adopt a systems approach. Designing flipped learning involves more than simply shifting content from face-to-face to online formats or selecting what to teach and how to teach it. One of the key advantages of flipped learning is that it allows for greater instructional support during complex learning tasks, rather than expecting students to apply concepts independently [4]. In this model, educators can guide learners as they apply knowledge in a structured environment—facilitated by peer collaboration and timely feedback. Achieving this requires a systems approach to the design of flipped learning.

A system can be defined as a set of interrelated components working together toward a defined goal, and the instructional process can similarly be viewed as a system designed to achieve learning goals [14]. Designing flipped learning within this framework requires careful alignment of learning objectives, contents, activities, instructional strategies, and assessments throughout the entire educational process. Without such alignment, flipped learning may fall short of achieving its intended outcomes. A systems approach to instructional design promotes this coherence, and the use of an instructional systems development model offers a visual and practical guide for designing flipped learning systematically [14,15].

It is important to consult instructional systems development models to guide a systematic and intentional planning process. Although instructors may intuitively envision a sequence of steps, designs created without reference to established models often lack coherence and structure. Formal adoption of instructional systems development models such as the widely used ADDIE model—which includes the phases of Analysis, Design, Development, Implementation, and Evaluation—helps ensure that each component of flipped learning is logically connected and pedagogically sound [12,16].

When applied to case-based flipped learning, the ADDIE model begins by analyzing the needs of faculty and learners, the learning environment, and the intended learning objectives and tasks. During the design and development phases, instructors define learning objectives and create flipped learning activities and assessments that are aligned with those learning objectives and instructional strategies appropriate to novice learners. In the implementation phase, the focus is on ensuring smooth delivery and providing necessary instructional support. Finally, the evaluation phase involves collecting feedback from students and faculty, as well as assessing the extent to which the learning objectives have been achieved to inform future course refinement. By applying this systems approach to instructional design, all components of case-based flipped learning can be deliberately aligned with intended outcomes, ultimately enhancing the quality and effectiveness of clinical reasoning education.

Grounded flipped learning activities in theory for clinical reasoning development

Case-based flipped learning activities should be grounded in learning science theories that support the development of clinical reasoning, particularly for novice learners. Recent research highlights the effectiveness of knowledge-oriented instructional approaches such as self-explanation, deliberate reflection, and the illness script method, all of which emphasize the importance of structured knowledge acquisition in clinical reasoning education [8,17–20]. Embedding these theoretical foundations into flipped learning activities can ensure that instructional practices are evidence-based and pedagogically sound for clinical reasoning development.

For example, when designing flipped learning activities based on the illness script method, instructional focus should be on helping students construct structured clinical knowledge, or illness scripts. According to illness script theory, these scripts are goal-directed knowledge structures that experienced clinicians use to interpret patient information and make clinical decisions [21]. These scripts evolve through repeated integration of biomedical and clinical knowledge over time [20]. For novice learners to begin reasoning like experts, the first step is building foundational illness scripts, which can then be refined through applied practice with clinical cases [22].

One example of this approach is shown in Figure 1, which presents a clinical reasoning worksheet that integrates an illness script table with related components such as differential diagnoses, enabling conditions, pathophysiological mechanisms, and clinical manifestations [8,18,23]. In-class activities using this tool can guide students to actively construct their own basic illness scripts while practicing clinical reasoning in a structured format. When alternative theoretical frameworks are employed, flipped learning activities should likewise be aligned with their key principles. This theoretical grounding ensures that flipped learning does not merely promote engagement, but also supports the deeper cognitive processes required for clinical reasoning development.

Use authentic clinical cases as learning tasks, adjusting their complexity for novice learners

Using authentic, real-life clinical cases as learning tasks is critical for developing clinical reasoning abilities in medical education [12,24]. Whole-task approaches, which employ clinical case that resemble real clinical situations, are more effective than fragmented approaches for clinical reasoning training according to complex learning theory [25–27]. However, due to the inherent complexity of such tasks—often requiring the simultaneous processing of multiple information sources—it is essential to adjust their difficulty for novice learners.

Simple, typical, and important clinical cases that are common in practice, pedagogically valuable, and accessible to novice learners should be selected as learning tasks. These cases should be sequenced from simple to complex, allowing learners to gradually build competence and confidence [26,27]. In terms of presentation, beginning with text-based case formats can offer a sense of psychological safety and help prevent cognitive overload. As learners gain experience and fluency, instruction can transition to more immersive modalities, such as virtual patients or standardized patient encounters. This gradual increase in both difficulty and modality helps novice learners manage cognitive load effectively and aligns with the principles of instructional design for complex learning [25,28].

Design flipped learning activities across the pre-, in- and post-class phases

In flipped learning, where lectures are shifted outside the classroom, in-class time should be used strategically to promote active learning, peer interaction, instructor guidance, and formative assessment to monitor students’ understanding and progress [7,27,29]. This shift requires a deliberate design of activities across all phases—pre-class, in-class, and post-class—to optimize learning and support the development of clinical reasoning.

Pre-class learning materials presented before class should be cognitively manageable for novice learners. To prevent cognitive overload, instructors should provide concise and focused content—such as short online videos (preferably under 10–15 min) or targeted reading packages—that directly support the learning objectives [30]. These materials should be explicitly connected to the clinical cases to be addressed during in-class activities. To ensure student preparedness and identify misconceptions, comprehension-based quizzes can be administered at the beginning of the class [8,27]. Instructors can then offer brief clarifications before transitioning to collaborative tasks.

In-class activities should focus on case-based clinical reasoning in groups. These tasks require students to analyze, synthesize, and evaluate clinical information while making diagnostic decisions. Instruction should guide learners in constructing their own illness scripts, drawing on knowledge acquired during pre-class preparation. Student performance should be assessed using clear, rubric-based criteria to ­provide constructive and timely feedback [30]. Ongoing formative assessments throughout the course can further support and monitor student progress [31].

Post-class activities, particularly structured reflection, are essential yet often overlooked in medical education. Reflective journaling enhances clinical reasoning by encouraging students to consolidate and internalize their learning, identify areas needing further study, and articulate the rationale behind their clinical decisions [20]. Students should be encouraged to reflect on key takeaways, unresolved questions, and the reasoning behind their initial diagnostic hypotheses. This reflective process fosters metacognitive awareness and supports the transfer of learning to future clinical contexts [20]. Incorporate instructional strategies to reduce cognitive load during in-class activities

Novice learners in clinical reasoning often struggle to process multiple pieces of information simultaneously, which can result in cognitive overload [32]. The central instructional challenge is therefore managing cognitive load during clinical reasoning tasks to enable successful learning. According to cognitive load theory, learning is impaired when cognitive demands exceed the limited capacity of working memory [32]. Instructional strategies grounded in this theory are essential for optimizing learning in flipped learning, particularly for novice learners.

One effective strategy is to provide procedural information in a just-in-time manner-such as a clinical reasoning worksheet (Figure 1)-during clinical reasoning activities. Delivering structured guidance at the moment it is needed allows learners to focus on specific reasoning steps without being overwhelmed by the overall task [26]. This scaffolding helps reduce intrinsic cognitive load associated with task complexity [26,32]. Additionally, providing worked-out examples—instructor-completed versions of the worksheet—before class can serve as helpful models for reducing extraneous cognitive load caused by unguided clinical reasoning practices [26,32,33]. These examples offer learners clear guidance on how to approach clinical problems and apply reasoning steps effectively. Collaborative group work using these worksheets can further reduce individual cognitive demands. This approach takes advantage of the collective working memory effect, enabling group members to share their cognitive resources and process information more efficiently [32].

These scaffolding strategies provide structured support and guidance to help students successfully engage with tasks that might otherwise exceed their current level of competence. However, such support should be gradually withdrawn as students gain expertise [26,32]. This fading of scaffolding promotes the development of autonomous clinical reasoning skills and facilitates a smooth transition from guided to independent problem solving.

Design feedback strategies to support the development of clinical reasoning

Providing timely and meaningful feedback is essential for effective clinical reasoning training. However, in practice, delivering sufficient feedback is often constrained by clinicians’ limited availability. To address this challenge, feedback strategies should be planned in advance to ensure they are both time-efficient and educationally effective.

Feedback can be typically categorized into two types: corrective feedback, which addresses specific errors or misconceptions, and cognitive feedback, which prompts learners to reflect on their reasoning processes and outcomes [26]. Both types are important for helping students recognize mistakes, refine their thinking, and develop stronger illness scripts [34].

One practical approach is for instructors to share completed versions of clinical reasoning worksheets by themselves at the end of each class, following the group presentations of students’ work. This allows students to compare their own reasoning with an expert example, promoting self-assessment and helping them identify gaps in their clinical reasoning [33]. Group presentations also provide a useful opportunity for peer-based cognitive feedback. By observing how other teams approach the same case, students are exposed to alternative ways of thinking and decision-making. Instructors can further enhance this process by using a clear rubric to provide both corrective and cognitive feedback to each group’s performance [34]. This multifaceted feedback approach can foster a reflective learning environment, which is essential for the progressive development of clinical reasoning ability.

Evaluate course quality beyond student performance

After implementing flipped learning, it is important to evaluate not only student performance but also the overall course design and instructional effectiveness. Such evaluations provide critical feedback for refining future iterations and enhancing instructional quality [14]. Evaluation should address multiple dimensions, including the alignment of instructional design with learning objectives, the achievement of learning outcomes, and student and faculty satisfaction [14], rather than relying solely on perceptions measured with Likert scales, which is often used in practice [7]. Appropriate methods may include comprehension quizzes, clinical reasoning worksheet assessments, group presentations, and structured satisfaction surveys [27].

Faculty feedback, gathered through surveys or interviews, can reveal areas for improvement in course design, learning activities, and course delivery. Similarly, student feedback—also collected through surveys and interviews—can provide essential information about what worked well, where confusion arose, and what could be improved. In addition, the learning environment itself should be assessed. This includes the accessibility of learning materials, ease of use of technological tools, and the quality of in-class interactions. These elements directly affect engagement and learning outcomes. By systematically collecting and analyzing feedback from diverse sources, educators can iteratively improve flipped learning implementation, ensuring that future courses align more closely with pedagogical goals and the evolving needs of learners.

Prepare both students and faculty for flipped learning

Effective implementation of flipped learning requires clear, consistent, and ongoing communication among all participants [31]. Orientation sessions for both students and faculty are critical to ensure shared understanding and commitment. While student-centered learning is widely promoted in medical education, many faculty members may be more accustomed to traditional lecture formats. Moreover, in case-based flipped learning, which often involves multiple instructors from diverse clinical backgrounds, coordinated preparation is essential. All participating instructors should be familiar with the goals, structure and pedagogical rationale of case-based flipped learning. A detailed syllabus outlining course components, learning objectives, instructional strategies, and assessment can help maintain instructional consistency across sessions.

For students, flipped learning requires active engagement not only in pre-class learning but also in in-class and post-class activities. Learning objectives should be clearly communicated and reinforced throughout the course. These objectives help students understand what is expected, why each task matters, and how it contributes to their learning progression [31].

Conclusion

Designing effective case-based flipped learning for novice learners requires more than simply reversing traditional instruction. Because clinical reasoning is a cognitively demanding and complex skill, its development should be supported through structured, theory-informed instructional design. This commentary outlined eight practical strategies for creating case-based flipped learning environments that foster clinical reasoning in preclinical students. Grounded in learning science theory as well as my research and teaching experience, these strategies provide guidance on applying a systems approach to flipped learning design, incorporating clinical reasoning theory into learning activities, managing cognitive load, implementing a whole-task approach, and embedding feedback loops and course quality control mechanisms.

Despite the growing adoption of flipped learning in health professions education, its application to clinical reasoning development remains limited in both theory and practice. The eight strategies presented here provide a practical foundation for enhancing clinical reasoning instruction; however, further research and scholarly dialogue are needed to refine and adapt these design strategies across diverse medical education contexts. Ultimately, theory-informed and systematically designed flipped learning can better prepare novices learners for the complexities of clinical practice.

Reflective journal writing

Reflection is an essential part of the learning process in clinical reasoning. Through reflective activities, learners internalize, conceptualize, and make personal meaning of what they have learned in class. This process supports the development of individual illness scripts, which are essential for building clinical reasoning skills. In addition, reflective journal writing serves as a valuable assessment tool to evaluate each learner’s level of understanding and progress throughout their learning experience in the course.

Please reflect on your learning experiences in class and describe your thoughts and feelings by referring specifically to your own learning experiences. Respond to the questions below within one A4 page.

Was the most likely diagnosis for today’s patient case the same as what you initially expected? On what basis did you arrive at your initial diagnosis? Did you gain any new insights or realizations during today’s clinical reasoning process? If you encounter a similar patient case in the future, what additional knowledge, skills, or attitudes do you think you will need?
