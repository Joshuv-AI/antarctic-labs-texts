Evaluation of the efficacy of microneedling alone and in combination with injectable hyaluronic acid in augmentation of peri-implant soft tissues: A randomized controlled trial
Authors: Pranjali Vijaykumar Bawankar, Prabhnoor Tuli, Abhay P. Kolte, Rajashri Abhay Kolte
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Objectives:The objective of this study was to evaluate the efficacy of injectable hyaluronic acid (HA) combined with microneedling (MN) in augmenting peri-implant soft tissue.Materials and Methods:Sixty-five patients were enrolled and divided into two groups. Group 1 received MN alone, while Group 2 received HA following MN on the peri-implant mucosa. Various parameters – including the plaque index (PI), gingival index (GI), peri-implant probing depth, clinical attachment levels, Sulcus Bleeding Index, mucosal thickness (MT), keratinized tissue width (KTW), interproximal width (IPW), and papillary recession height (PRH) – were evaluated at baseline, 3 months, and 6 months postoperatively. Patient-reported outcome measures were assessed to gauge treatment effectiveness and patient satisfaction.Results:Group 2 patients exhibited reduced PI, GI, SBI, PIPD, and CAL compared to Group 1. Both groups showed a significant increase in MT and KTW after 6 months; MT increased to 1.03 ± 0.11 mm in Group 1 and 2.48 ± 0.45 mm in Group 2, while KTW increased to 2.44 ± 0.84 mm in Group 1 and 4.34 ± 1.02 mm in Group 2. In addition, the mean PRH was 1.67 ± 0.32 mm in Group 1 and 0.88 ± 0.25 mm in Group 2, with a mean IPW of 2.19 ± 0.52 mm in Group 1 and 0.90 ± 0.16 mm in Group 2.Conclusion:HA combined with MN leads to increased MT and KTW, reduced inflammation, and improved papillary fill. Thus, HA with MN may serve as an effective short-term implant maintenance modality, assisting clinicians in achieving peri-implant soft tissue regeneration.

INTRODUCTION

There has been considerable interest in conserving and regenerating the lost soft tissue architecture surrounding natural teeth and dental implants for both esthetic and functional reasons. The stability of soft tissue is pivotal for peri-implant health, and modifications in its phenotype can enhance implant survival.[1] In efforts to create beautiful smiles, the intricate characteristics of soft and hard tissues must be considered as guidelines for restorative and implant procedures.[2] Numerous surgical procedures and biomaterials have been employed to enhance the gingival biotype, color, texture, volume, and amount of mucosal margin with appropriate papillary presence, all of which have significant effects on the final esthetic result.[34]

Peri-implant soft tissue thickness and width of keratinized mucosa have a major impact on the esthetic appearance, marginal bone stability and health of implant/prosthetic reconstruction. In unpredictable compliers, <2 mm of keratinized mucosa around implants appears to be linked to an increased risk of peri-implantitis.[5] The aforementioned elements must be in line with those of soft tissue around adjacent teeth to obtain a harmonious and natural-looking restoration. Soft tissue augmentation is recommended in esthetic regions where a certain amount of buccal bone remodeling is expected, like immediate implant placement, in situations with thin biotype or thin buccal bone plate.

A paradigm shift in periodontal research has led researchers to search for new biomaterials enhancing and accelerating the intrinsic healing potential.[6] Hyaluronic acid (HA) is one such natural extracellular matrix component, which plays a vital role in maintaining structural and homeostatic integrity of tissue. It activates metalloproteinase inhibitors, thus suppressing the tissue breakdown enhancing soft tissue wound healing.[78] It also participates in several important biological procedures, including mediating cellular signaling, regulating cell adhesion and proliferation, and manipulating cell differentiation.[910]

One study[11] revealed that HA had bacteriostatic effect patterns on microorganisms such as Porphyromonas gingivalis, Actinobacillus actinomycetemcomitans, and Staphylococcus aureus, leading to a decreased risk of postsurgical infection and promoting more predictable regeneration. A recent study[12] reported positive effects of MN combined with injectable HA gel on soft tissue regeneration in a rat gingival recession model. Microneedling (MN) is used as a transdermal drug delivery agent for the oral mucosa by penetrating the oral epithelial barrier of the dermis. It can establish microchannels perpendicular to the oral mucosa for delivery of the drug into the body’s circulation through vascular channels, allowing the drugs to exert local or systemic therapeutic effects.[1314] MN is also known as transdermal collagen induction therapy and has been shown to initiate a wound healing cascade by applying microneedles to the mucosa, which stimulates the release of multiple growth factors that continuously affect the proliferation of cells such as monocytes, keratinocytes, and fibroblasts.

Despite the growing interest in enhancing peri-implant soft tissue around dental implants, there is a notable lack of research on the effectiveness of combining injectable HA with MN for this purpose as part of treatment and maintenance therapy. This led to the design of our study, driven by the hypothesis that using HA alongside MN could yield better clinical outcomes for peri-implant soft tissue. Thus, the present randomized controlled clinical trial aimed to compare the efficacy of injectable HA with MN against MN alone in augmenting peri-implant soft tissue.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Following the ethical standards outlined by the World Medical Association Declaration of Helsinki, revised in 2013, and adhering to the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials guidelines for biomedical research, this comparative study obtained written informed consent from all participating patients. The trial protocol received approval from the institutional ethics committee of our institute and was registered with the clinical trial registry of India (Reg No. CTRI/2023/06/053517). The inclusion criteria of the study population consisted of (a) systemically healthy male and female patients aged 18–59 years, (b) with healthy and stable soft tissue architecture, (c) presence of at least one implant with functional restoration and keratinized mucosa < 1 mm, (d) patients who were able to maintain oral hygiene around dental implants, (e) patients with thin gingival phenotype, (f) cooperative, motivated, and hygiene conscious patients, (g) patients with peri-implant probing depth (PIPD) <3 mm, and (h) patients with no signs of pre-exsisting peri-implantitis. On the contrary, the patients with (a) systemic diseases influencing bone or soft tissue metabolism, (b) pregnant or lactating females, (c) tobacco smokers, (d) untreated existing periodontal conditions, and (e) patients treated with any medication known to cause gingival hyperplasia were excluded.

Based on the results of a previous study,[15] a power analysis was established by G * Power version 3.0.1 (Franz Faul universitat, Kiel, Germany). Total minimum calculated sample size of 34 (17 samples per group; total 2 groups) would yield 80% power to detect significant differences, with effect size of 0.5 and significance level at 0.05. With the assumption that some of the patients may be lost to follow-up, the sample size was planned at 40 patients which during the course of the study rose to 65 patients.

All the sites fulfilling the inclusion criteria were selected and randomly allocated to one of the two groups using computer-generated random numbers at the time of the procedure. Presurgical hygiene therapy including oral hygiene instructions and scaling root planning was performed.

Patients in Group 1 were treated with MN using round lancet needle 30 Gauge (0.255 mm) (SMARTCARE, Indore, India) which was vertically inserted into the tissue until the hard tissue was reached. MN was carried out on keratinized mucosa of the implant to be treated. The microchannels were prepared as previously reported.[15] [Figure 1a]. Patients in Group 2 were treated with MN followed by HA filler injection. Before the intervention and measuring of mucosal thickness (MT), a topical anesthetic spray was applied over the mucosa. A commercially available pure cross-linked mild type, HA from GENOSS (MONALISA, Genoss, Gyeonggi R and DB Centre IF, Suwon-si Yeongtong-gu, Gyeonggi-do, Korea) [Figure 1b] was used for injecting in peri-implant mucosa. The concentration of HA was 20 mg/ml and the particle size was 400 µm. It was supplied in 1.0 ml syringe for clinical usage. A volume of 0.2 mm of HA (20 mg/ml) was administered at specific locations 3–5 mm around the soft tissue of dental implants. Injecting HA [Figure 1c] was done into the sulcus, in attached mucosa at 3 mm apical to free gingival margin on the facial aspect of the implant until blanching of the mucosa was evident. To ascertain that the gel was distributed evenly, the region was gently massaged for 2 to 3 min. The patients were given postoperative instructions like need of adhering to a soft diet. The following clinical parameters were evaluated at baseline, 3, and 6 months, postoperatively using UNC 15 Periodontal probe (PCPUNC 15; Hu Friedy, Chicago IL, USA).

Two pre-calibrated examiners (PT and PB) conducted the clinical measurements with meticulous care. The reliability of these measurements was assessed through inter- and intraexaminer analyses using the intraclass correlation coefficient, which yielded values ranging between 0.93 and 0.97 across different study parameters. Each examiner repeated their readings and subsequent intraobserver reproducibility analysis was conducted. To maintain the integrity of the study, a masked approach was adopted. Each patient was assigned an examination file and a treatment file upon inclusion. The examination file, containing the patient’s case history and clinical measurements, was accessible solely to the examiner who remained blinded to the study group. Conversely, the treatment file, containing randomization data, was only accessible to the operator.

Baseline demographic data was summarized using descriptive statistics such as mean, standard deviation, and standard error. To compare the means of different parameters between the two groups, a t-test for independent samples was conducted at all three time points. In addition, within-group comparisons for each parameter across time were made using repeated measure analysis of variance (ANOVA). Pairwise comparisons of each parameter between two time points were performed, with P values adjusted using the Bonferroni multiple testing correction method. Visual analog scale scores (PROMS) in both groups were assessed using the Chi-square test. All analyses were conducted using SPSS version 26.0 (IBM, USA), with statistical significance evaluated at the 5% level.

RESULTS

The study was conducted from January 2023 to September 2023 and involved 65 patients including 33 females and 32 males. In Group 1, the mean age was 29.33 ± 2.23 years, while in Group 2, it was 28.17 ± 2.58 years. The primary outcomes were the clinical parameters – PI, GI, SBI, PIPD, CAL, MT, KTW, IPW, and PRH, whereas the secondary outcomes were the PROMs. Descriptive statistics detailing the periodontal parameters for both groups, including the mean and standard deviation at all three time points along with intragroup comparisons, are presented in Tables 1 and 2. Significant differences in PI were noted in both Group 1 (F = 103.614, P = 0.000) and Group 2 (F = 166.465, P = 0.000) across the specified time periods. These findings underscore notable variations in plaque levels within each group, highlighting the dynamic nature of oral hygiene practices.

DISCUSSION

Maintaining or establishing a sufficient width of keratinized mucosa is crucial for ensuring the long-term survival of implants and meeting patients’ aesthetic expectations.[19] The findings of the present study reveal a significant decrease in GI, SBI, PIPD, and CAL after a 6-month follow-up in Group 2 compared to Group 1. This suggests that utilizing MN with HA injection not only helps reduce peri-implant inflammation but also improves peri-implant health. These results align with findings from other studies, further affirming the efficacy of this approach.[2021]

In the present investigation, an increase in the KTW and MT along with reduction in PRH and IPW was observed in both the groups; however, the magnitude of these changes was significantly greater in Group 2. This not only highlights the proliferative effects of HA but also the stimulatory potential of MN. Increased collagen and elastin fiber production has been reported following MN procedures, with a 40% thicker stratum spinosum layer observed 6 months posttreatment.[22] Histological analysis performed by Aust et al.,[23] one year after an MN session, showed increased collagen and elastic fiber accumulation in the reticular dermis, a thickened epidermis, and a normal stratum corneum.

In a study evaluating the additional contribution of a MN procedure to the use of topical Vitamin A and C, a 22% increase in the epidermal thickness after the use of only topical Vitamin A and C and a 140% increase in the epidermal thickness after the use of MN and topical Vitamin A and C were reported at 8 weeks after the procedure when compared with the baseline.[24] The results of our study likely reflect similar histological changes within the tissues in both groups, although these changes may have been more pronounced in Group 2 than in Group 1.

Similarly, a considerable reduction of IPW was observed in Group 2 suggesting peri-implant mucosal filling due to the combination of injectable HA and MN when compared to Group 1. The findings of the present study clearly highlight the additive effect of HA and MN in improving the peri-implant mucosal phenotype and supporting peri-implant soft tissue regeneration. These results are consistent with those of previous studies.[2526] Furthermore, this study is likely one of the few to evaluate MT, KTW, PRH, and IPW, along with PROMs, in patients treated with HA.

HA is a natural glycosaminoglycan and an excellent scaffold for periodontal regeneration present in connective tissue. Moreover, it seems to have an antimicrobial and anti-inflammatory effect. The principal factor that makes HA a promising biomaterial is its viscoelastic property and its capacity to absorb a substantial amount of water. This renders HA a periodontal filler, and in several situations, it has a protective function as a barrier for bacteria and viruses. For implant patients, especially those prone to occasional tissue inflammation, regular follow-up and treatment with chemotherapeutic agents in the form of rinses, gels, or solutions are extremely essential. Site-specific application of topical HA injections will not only specifically treat the target area by improving the KTW but also help to reduce peri-implant inflammation if present. This was confirmed from the findings of the present study where intergroup comparison showed statistically significant changes in terms of GI, SBI, PIPD, and CAL. These findings are in accordance with the findings of the previous studies.[2728]

A systematic review from Eliezer et al.[29] suggested that the addition of HA to non-surgical and surgical periodontal therapy may have additional clinical effects on the CAL, probing depth, and bleeding on probing. Regarding the use of HA in peri-implant defects, several studies suggest the benefit in microflora diversity, and at the same time, HA acts as a protective shield against bacteria colonization.[11]

In the present study, injections of cross-linked HA were suggested as a means of augmenting the peri-implant mucosa, as HA has the potential to stimulate the proliferation of fibroblasts within the soft tissue, enhance collagen production, and ultimately result in soft-tissue augmentation. Cross-linked HA’s delayed degradation pattern allows it to be present at different stages of wound healing, encouraging regeneration rather than repair.

The Group 2 patients in the present study also showed significant increases in KTW and MT during the follow-up periods, suggesting proliferative changes in the peri-implant tissues and adjacent attached mucosa. The presence of an adequate amount of KM around implants (>2 mm) facilitates the maintenance of oral hygiene, which can lead to reduced plaque accumulation and a lower incidence of peri-implant mucositis. Furthermore, KM is associated with soft and hard tissue stability, resulting in a decreased incidence of peri-implant mucosal recession, marginal bone resorption, and attachment loss. The PRH also exhibited considerable reduction after the HA injection during follow-up period. These findings are mirroring and supporting those of previous studies.[2526]

On the contrary, the study by Bertl and colleagues[30] found no significant differences in HA injection compared to saline injection groups during the follow-up periods. The specific material and concentration employed, variations in drug administration techniques (injection location, injection technique), and the apparent differences in pretreatment papillary loss size and type might all contribute to these discrepancies in findings. After any surgical procedure, inflammation resulting from tissue damage is the primary cause of acute postoperative discomfort. Pain was rare because of the conservative nature of this procedure, which is consistent with the findings of Becker et al.[31]

The ANOVA results highlight significant intragroup variations for most parameters, emphasizing the need for continuous monitoring and intervention to maintain optimal oral health. The specific dynamics observed in each parameter provide valuable information for understanding how different aspects of oral health may change over time within each group.

The consistent significant differences in IPW at all time points (P < 0.001) suggest differential augmentation of the soft tissues having implications for oral hygiene practices. These differences may indicate unique oral health characteristics and varied responses to treatment or observational periods between Group 1 and 2. Understanding these variations is crucial for tailoring dental interventions and improving our comprehension of the impact of time on oral health parameters in distinct study groups.

When the PROMs of both experimental groups were compared, no significant differences were observed. This may be attributed to the MN technique, which was performed similarly in both groups, leading to comparable PROM scores. No significant changes in functional, psychological, or behavioral impact were reported when comparing the two groups. Similarly, no differences in pain or discomfort were noted among Group 2 patients, likely due to the use of a fine-bore, thin-walled needle that allows for smooth injections.

These findings suggest that HA injections combined with MN in the peri-implant soft tissue area to improve soft tissue volume can be a promising approach in implant maintenance programs, offering optimal patient satisfaction. Assessing PROMs through a questionnaire system introduces the potential for individual bias, influenced by the comprehension of question significance and response accuracy. In addition, the study’s focus on a specific age range limits its ability to address age-related variations, racial differences, and gingival phenotype disparities, all of which could impact the observed parameter values. Moreover, site-specific variations must be considered, as different intraoral sites may yield diverse results with MN and HA injections. To validate our findings, long-term split-mouth study designs are warranted. These would provide a more comprehensive understanding of the efficacy and implications of our interventions over time.

CONCLUSION

Within the study constraints, the results confirm that the use of HA in peri-implant soft tissue improves IPW and KTW, increases MT, and promotes soft tissue regeneration. Injectable HA, when used after MN as in Group 2, facilitates greater improvements in these parameters, indicating an overall increase in soft tissue mass that ultimately leads to a change in phenotype. Moreover, HA injections significantly reduce peri-implant inflammation, further enhancing soft tissue health. Patients reported satisfactory experiences with HA injections, noting minimal pain and discomfort. These noninvasive techniques and biomaterials offer promising avenues for achieving superior functional and esthetic outcomes, thereby improving success in managing soft tissue dehiscence or thin biotypes in implant therapy.
