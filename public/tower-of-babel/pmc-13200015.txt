Machine Learning for Revision Joint Arthroplasty: A Systematic Review of Distinct Challenges, Current Performance, and Clinical Readiness
Authors: Teja Yeramosu, Logan K Laubach, Raveena Joshi, Sonya Feeser, Jacob Wait, John Cyrus, Jibanananda Satpathy, Gregory J Golladay, Nirav K Patel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGiven the high complication rates and economic burden of revision total joint arthroplasty, machine learning (ML) models may offer a tool to improve outcomes. This study aims to review the application of ML for predicting outcomes following revision total hip arthroplasty and total knee arthroplasty.MethodsA comprehensive literature search using Ovid MEDLINE, Embase, and Web of Science was conducted in May 2024. Studies were screened in Covidence. Primary exclusion criteria included (1) systematic reviews; (2) not utilizing ML; and (3) not involving revision arthroplasty. Thirteen studies met the inclusion criteria.ResultsStudies focused on patient complications (n = 7), inpatient status and length of stay (n = 3), and readmissions and discharge dispositions (n = 2). One study looked at all 3 outcomes. Studies involved revision total knee arthroplasty (n = 6), revision total hip arthroplasty (n = 4), or a combination (n = 3). Eleven studies reported predictive success using the area under the receiver-operating characteristic curve. 6.7% were in the poor range, 36.7% in the acceptable range, 48.3% in the excellent range, and 8.3% in the outstanding range. Ten studies were internally validated, and 3 were externally validated.ConclusionsML algorithms used in revision total joint arthroplasty are complicated by greater heterogeneity, data sparsity, and outcome imbalance. They have utility in clinical settings for risk stratification, predicting complications, length of stay, and discharge disposition. However, these algorithms should be critically assessed before clinical adoption.Keywords: Artificial intelligence, Exchange arthroplasty, Machine learning, Predictive modeling, Revision arthroplasty, Two-stage revision

Introduction

Revision total joint arthroplasty (rTJA) is necessitated when a primary TJA fails and often results in poorer patient outcomes, higher complication rates requiring additional surgeries, and increased economic burdens to the healthcare system. Anticipated trends indicate a significant rise in demand for rTJA, with projected increases of 42% in revision total hip arthroplasty (rTHA) and 149% in revision total knee arthroplasty (rTKA) by 2040 [1]. As such, improving the ability to predict outcomes following rTJA is crucial for minimizing both adverse patient outcomes and healthcare expenditures.

Machine learning (ML) is a branch of artificial intelligence (AI) that leverages data to make predictions about future outcomes. It has extensive applications in fracture detection, bone image segmentation, and osteoarthritis prediction [[2], [3], [4], [5]]. While previous studies have systematically reviewed the utility of ML in predicting outcomes for primary TJA, a significant gap remains in evaluating its applicability in the context of rTJA [2,6,7]. Studies on outcome prediction for primary TJA have demonstrated that ML is adept at managing complex data relationships and often outperforms traditional logistic regression models, providing a data-driven approach to value-based care [[8], [9], [10]].

In the context of rTJA, ML has been utilized to predict various outcomes, including discharge disposition, length of stay (LOS), and opioid usage [2,7,9,11,12]. However, experts caution against the widespread adoption of ML for predicting clinical outcomes until these methods have been rigorously assessed and validated [13,14]. A recent systematic review of ML applications for predicting primary TJA outcomes found that only 30% of the studies demonstrated improvements over traditional logistic regression models. Furthermore, only 30% of these studies underwent external validation [6]. Unlike primary TJA, revision procedures present distinct challenges for outcome prediction. These challenges stem from the heterogeneous nature of revision indications, factors such as prior implantation or bone loss, and the frequent need for staged procedures. As a result, the generalizability of predictive models is more difficult to achieve than in primary TJA.

As ML increasingly shapes outcome prediction in orthopaedics, the key issue in rTJA is not just whether it has been used, but also how algorithms have been deployed and how studies have addressed the inherent complexity of this population. As such, this study aims to systematically review published studies on ML in rTHA and rTKA, characterizing their algorithms, target outcomes, predictive performance, and methodological quality.

Material and methods

This systematic review evaluated the literature on the application of AI or ML to predict patient outcomes after revision hip or knee arthroplasty. The Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses guidelines were followed throughout this study [15]. This review uses publicly available data without identifiable info; therefore, institutional review board approval was not necessary. The review was registered with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO; CRD420251118797).

Search strategy

A comprehensive literature search was conducted using the electronic databases Ovid MEDLINE, Ovid Embase, and Web of Science from the inception of the databases to May 30, 2024. A research librarian with experience in systematic reviews developed a search strategy that included a combination of keywords and controlled vocabulary for the concepts of hip or knee arthroplasty, revision surgery, and AI or ML. The full search strategy for each database is provided in the Appendix.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Studies selected for inclusion were those that applied AI or ML methodologies to rTHA or rTKA and predicted patient outcomes. The search strategy was designed to broadly capture all AI-related studies, including rule-based systems, natural language processing, and deep learning. There were no restrictions on the publication year, but studies were required to be in English. Systematic reviews were excluded from this analysis. Studies were also excluded if they focused on joints other than the hip and knee, did not involve rTJA, did not incorporate AI or ML, or lacked predictive patient outcome data. Editorial notes, technical notes, case reports, and animal models were excluded from the study.

Study selection

After conducting the search, 3 authors independently reviewed both titles and abstracts of each reference. Any disagreements or conflicts among the 3 reviewers were addressed through discussion with a fourth reviewer.

Data collection

Outcomes compiled were author and year of publication, data size set, data source, input feature types, joints, revision vs primary + revision, primary outcome, missing data handled, AI modes used, AI performance, comparison to logistic regression, and external validation, which Table 1, Table 2, Table 3 show. External validation was assessed by reviewing each manuscript and identifying any articles that referenced the study. No minimum training-to-testing data split ratio was prespecified as an inclusion criterion. For studies that included logistic regression models, the authors reported the area under the receiver-operating characteristic curve (AUC) as a measure of diagnostic performance and compared it with the AUC of the ML algorithm. The algorithm with the higher AUC was deemed superior. If logistic regression results were unavailable, “N/A” (not available) was noted in the corresponding table entry.

Data analyses

AUC was considered the primary measure of AI performance. When evaluating ML models strictly by performance, an AUC less than 0.5 indicates no discrimination; 0.5-0.7 indicates poor discrimination; 0.7-0.8 indicates acceptable discrimination; 0.8-0.9 indicates excellent discrimination; and an AUC greater than 0.9 indicates outstanding discrimination [21]. In cases where a single study reported multiple AUC values from different ML predictions, each AUC was treated as a distinct outcome. When summarizing results, each outcome was assigned equal weight across studies, with medians and ranges calculated accordingly. Our analysis examined the distribution of AUC outcomes across all studies, categorizing them by performance level: poor, acceptable, excellent, and outstanding. If AUC was not reported, AI performance was noted by either the area under the precision-recall curve or the concordance statistic (C-statistic).

Search results

Following the removal of duplicates and ineligible formats, including editorials, letters, conference abstracts, and animal studies, 1285 articles remained for screening. A total of 57 full-text studies were reviewed for eligibility. Of these, 44 were excluded for not meeting inclusion criteria: 28 studies did not involve the use of ML to predict patient outcomes (eg, focused on diagnosis, imaging, implant design, or other unrelated applications); 12 studies did not involve revision arthroplasty; 2 focused on joints other than the hip or knee; 1 lacked outcome prediction entirely; and 1 had no full-text available. Ultimately, 13 studies met all inclusion criteria and were selected for final analysis (Fig. 1).

Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA) diagram.

To account for clinical and methodological heterogeneity, we prespecified a structured narrative synthesis, grouping results (i) by joint and (ii) by outcome domain, such as complications, inpatient status/LOS, and readmissions/discharge disposition. Seven of the studies focused on patient complications, 2 on inpatient status and LOS, 3 on readmissions and discharge dispositions, and 1 on all 3 outcome categories. Of the studies reviewed, 6 (46.2%) focused exclusively on rTKA, 4 (30.8%) on rTHA, and 3 (23.0%) on both operations. In total, 11 (84.6%) studies examined solely revision arthroplasty, while 2 (15.4%) included both primary and revision arthroplasty. Regarding data sources, 7 studies (53.8%) utilized national databases, primarily the American College of Surgeons National Surgical Quality Improvement Program, while 6 (46.2%) relied on single-institution datasets. Included studies employed a range of validation strategies, including hold-out splits and k-fold cross-validation. Five of the studies compared their ML models to logistic regression (38.5%). All 5 studies performed better than logistic regression. Three of the studies were externally validated (23.1%). Across 13 studies reporting an AUC and a total of 60 predictions, we found that 6.7% were in the poor range, 36.7% in the acceptable range, 48.3% in the excellent range, and 8.3% in the outstanding range.

Results

Complications

Eight of the studies focused on utilizing ML to assess the risk of complications following rTJA [7,9,10,[12], [13], [14],16,17] (Table 1). Topics of interest included recurrent periprosthetic joint infection, postoperative 30-day mortality, postoperative blood transfusion, postoperative 90-day venous thromboembolism (deep vein thrombosis and pulmonary embolism), cardiac and respiratory complications, rerevision, reoperation, and surgical site infection, in addition to overall complication risk. The median sample size was 8010 patients (range, 618-53,533). Seven of the studies utilized similar training inputs, including demographics, comorbidities, and perioperative variables. However, one study used free text from electronic health record (EHR) data and clinical notes. Three studies found that various ML algorithms, including bidirectional long short-term memory neural networks, random forest (RF), and least absolute shrinkage and selection operator analysis, achieved the best predictive performance. The best-performing ML models were artificial neural networks (ANNs) and extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost), which appeared in 2 and 3 studies, respectively. Success was defined as an ML model achieving a higher AUC than logistic regression, observed in 3 studies. However, only 2 research groups externally validated their algorithms. Six studies reported the AUC with a median of 0.85 (range, 0.76-0.94), 1 study reported the area under the precision-recall curve (0.79), and 1 study reported the C-statistic with a median of 0.82 (range, 0.80-0.88).

Inpatient status and length of stay

Three studies applied ML to predict LOS following rTJA (Table 2), with a median sample size of 5600 patients (range, 2577-7425) [7,8,17]. One study focused on prolonged LOS, another on same-day discharge, and the third addressed a combination of complications, LOS, and readmissions. The top-performing algorithms included neural networks, RF models, and XGBoost. Of the 3 studies, 2 used logistic regression for comparison, both demonstrating the superior performance of the ML models. None were externally validated. All of these studies considered performance using AUC, with a median value of 0.87 (range, 0.81-0.89).

Readmissions and discharge disposition

ML has been applied to predict readmissions and discharge disposition, with 4 studies identified on these topics [2,10,12,17] (Table 3). The median sample size for patients undergoing rTJA was 4826 (range, 1628-17,443). Two studies focused on readmissions, 1 on discharge disposition and 1 on both discharge disposition and associated outcomes such as complications and LOS. The most effective ML models included ANNs, XGBoost, and histogram-based gradient boosting. Two studies incorporated logistic regression for comparison, both demonstrating improved performance with ML. One of the 4 studies was externally validated using an institutional dataset. All studies reported model performance using AUC, with a median AUC of 0.88 (range, 0.78-0.95).

Discussion

This systematic review emphasizes the emerging role of ML in predicting outcomes after rTJA. While ML has shown promise in primary TJA, its application in the revision context remains relatively limited. This review isolates rTJA, a population inherently different from primary TJA, and demonstrates that overall, models performed best when they could handle the complexities of revision arthroplasty data, including mixed predictor types, nonlinear risk relationships, and imbalanced outcomes. Tree-based and ensemble methods were particularly well-suited to these conditions, often outperforming traditional regression methods. Performance also varied by outcome domain, with complications, LOS, and readmission each posing distinct challenges, underscoring the need to interpret results within each category rather than as a whole.

Among the evaluated algorithms, RFs, ANNs, elastic-net penalized logistic regression, and gradient-boosted machines were the most commonly used. Each algorithm has unique strengths: RFs are resistant to overfitting and handle outliers effectively, while neural networks excel in modeling complex, nonlinear patterns with minimal human feature engineering [22]. However, these models often operate as “black boxes,” making their decision-making processes unclear [23]. Explainability tools such as SHapley Additive exPlanations values have emerged as a means to address this limitation by quantifying the contribution of individual features to model predictions, thereby improving clinician trust and facilitating clinical adoption [24].

One notable concern is overfitting, which occurs when an algorithm is over-trained and no longer learns, instead memorizing the data, resulting in poor generalization when applied to external populations [25].

ML algorithms have been shown to identify patterns in complex datasets and have frequently been compared to logistic regression models. Logistic regression remains important in clinical prediction due to its simplicity, interpretability, and robustness with smaller datasets. However, compared to traditional logistic regression, ML models have the theoretical advantage of capturing more complex relationships in the data and may outperform logistic regression when significant nonlinearity exists between predictors and outcomes. Notably, in our review, only 5 of the 13 studies (38.5%) compared ML models to logistic regression, and all 5 reported better performance for the ML models [7,14,17,19,20]. Logistic regression models are better suited to handle smaller datasets, as ML algorithms can result in overfitting [26]. As a result, the model is not generalizable and may struggle to perform well on new, unseen data. ML algorithms, in turn, have been shown to have higher accuracy and are better suited for larger datasets [27]. This finding suggests that while ML can enhance predictive accuracy, direct comparisons with logistic regression are underreported and that selecting the right analytical method is crucial for achieving optimal results.

Despite growing interest, the clinical adoption of ML algorithms has been slow, primarily due to concerns about transparency, reproducibility, and external validity. In this review, most studies reported an AUC. Eleven of the 13 studies reported an AUC to assess the predictive performance of their algorithms [2,7,9,10,14,[17], [18], [19], [20],28]. When considering the 60 AUCs reported across all studies, we found that 6.7% fell in the poor range, 36.7% in the acceptable range, 48.3% in the excellent range, and 8.3% in the outstanding range. 56.6% reported an AUC in the excellent to outstanding range (AUC, 0.8-1). Most studies reported predictive accuracy using AUC values, with 56.6% achieving excellent or outstanding discrimination (AUC > 0.9). This represents a significant improvement over prior reviews of ML in primary TJA, such as the study by Karlin et al., in which fewer than 35% of models met similar performance thresholds [6]. However, AUC alone may not fully reflect a model's clinical usability, and high performance in internal validation does not guarantee success in broader populations.

External validation is crucial for ensuring the real-world applicability of predictive models, yet it remains rare in the current literature. In our review, only 3 of the 13 studies (23.1%) validated their models on independent datasets, highlighting a significant gap between model development and clinical readiness. Without external validation, it is difficult to determine whether a model's performance reflects true predictive ability or simply reflects patterns unique to the training data. This concern is particularly relevant in rTJA, where patient populations, surgical techniques, and perioperative protocols can vary considerably across institutions. Models trained exclusively on single-institution data may risk overfitting to local practice patterns, while those built on national registries may lose relevance to specific institutional nuances [29]. Bridging this divide will require collaborative, multicenter validation efforts that test models across diverse clinical environments and patient demographics [30].

A further challenge specific to revision arthroplasty is the inherent difficulty in assembling large, high-quality datasets. Unlike primary TJA, which is performed in high volumes at most arthroplasty centers, revision procedures are less frequent and often concentrated at tertiary referral centers [31]. Patients frequently present as transfers from outside institutions with limited or incomplete preoperative records, introducing missing data and reducing the pool of usable training cases. Moreover, the heterogeneity of revision indications, ranging from aseptic loosening to periprosthetic joint infection to periprosthetic fracture, further fragments already limited datasets into smaller subgroups [32,33]. This data scarcity is a fundamental barrier to developing robust ML models, as these algorithms generally require large training datasets to avoid overfitting and achieve reliable generalizability [26]. National databases such as American College of Surgeons National Surgical Quality Improvement Program partially address the volume limitation, as demonstrated by the 7 studies in this review that utilized such registries; however, these databases may lack procedure-specific variables critical to revision cases, such as bone loss severity, fixation method, or prior surgical history [34]. Future efforts to develop multi-institutional, revision-specific databases that build on existing infrastructure, such as the American Joint Replacement Registry, and include granular clinical variables would meaningfully advance ML applications in this population [35].

Reproducibility represents another major obstacle to the clinical translation of ML in rTJA. Even minor variations in how missing data are handled, which features are selected, and how hyperparameters are tuned can substantially alter model performance, making it difficult to compare results across studies or replicate findings at new sites. In our review, the heterogeneity of algorithms, input variables, and validation approaches across the 13 included studies underscores this challenge. Adopting standardized reporting practices could help address this issue [36]. However, the most effective approach would be to directly share algorithmic models, allowing for independent evaluations across different clinical sites. The Transparent Reporting of a multivariable prediction model for Individual Prognosis or Diagnosis Initiative provides a widely accepted framework of guidelines for reporting studies that develop or validate prediction models [37]. Beyond standardized reporting, directly sharing trained model architectures and code would enable independent evaluation across clinical sites, further enhancing reproducibility and trust in published results [38].

Overall, while ML models in rTJA show promising performance metrics, widespread clinical adoption will depend on greater transparency, reproducibility, robust external validation, and a clear demonstration of added clinical value over existing methods. As the field advances, a critical focus must shift from purely technical performance to the practical integration of clinical workflows.

Limitations

This systematic review has several limitations. While our search terms were carefully chosen to encompass all known terminology, significant variations in terminology across ML, statistics, and computer science may have led to the overlooking of some relevant studies. Additionally, although most high-impact studies in this field are published in English, limiting our search to English-language publications may have introduced language bias. Furthermore, the review focused on studies evaluating ML applications for predicting patient outcomes after rTJA, which may have excluded other relevant applications in this population; however, narrowing our focus allowed for a more targeted and detailed synthesis. Finally, given the novelty and emerging interest in ML within rTJA, the relatively small number of included studies (n = 13) limited our ability to draw robust conclusions or conduct a meta-analysis; however, this highlights opportunities for future research to further enrich the available dataset.

Conclusions

ML algorithms, particularly XGBoost and neural networks, show significant promise for accurately predicting patient outcomes following rTJA. These models can serve as valuable tools to support personalized surgical planning, improve preoperative risk assessment, and guide postoperative management. However, before gaining widespread acceptance in clinical practice, several challenges need to be addressed, including the need for robust external validation, increased transparency in model interpretability, and standardized reporting of predictive performance. Future research should focus on evaluating these algorithms across diverse institutions and patient populations to ensure their generalizability, reliability, and practical relevance in real-world orthopaedic care.
