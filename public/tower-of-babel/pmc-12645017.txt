Microbiota and their functional profiling across the life cycle of the blow fly Hemipyrellia ligurriens
Authors: Pluemkamon Phuwanatsarunya, Rutchanee Rodpai, Sophit Khanthawong, Ketsarin Thipphet, Rapee Thummeepak, Sudarat Onsurathum, Kabkaew L Sukontason, Wanchai Maleewong, Tongjit Thanchomnang, Nophawan Bunchu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe blow fly, Hemipyrellia ligurriens (Diptera: Calliphoridae), is an ecologically and medically important species, particularly in forensic entomology, where its developmental stages aid in minimum postmortem interval estimation. Microbial diversity in insects is known to fluctuate dynamically across developmental stages. However, the mechanisms governing microbial community assembly, symbiont transmission, and functional roles in H. ligurriens remain largely unexplored. This study utilized 16 S ribosomal RNA gene amplicon sequencing to characterize the microbiota structure and functional metabolic potential across all life stages of H. ligurriens. Microbial composition varied significantly across developmental stages, with distinct assemblages characterizing each stage. Proteobacteria (synonym Pseudomonadota), Firmicutes (synonym Bacillota), and Bacteroidota consistently dominated across all life stages and rearing media, suggesting their essential role in host development. Notably, natural strain flies exhibited significantly greater microbial diversity than laboratory-derived counterparts. While microbial diversity was comparable between male and female flies from both strains, sex-specific microbiota appeared exclusively in the natural strain. Functional metabolic profiling suggests that microbiota co-evolve with their host, playing vital roles in immune regulation and pathogen defense. This study provides the first comprehensive microbiota profile of H. ligurriens, offering novel insights into its potential applications in forensic entomology, maggot debridement therapy, and insect health management.Keywords: Hemipyrellia ligurriens, Blow fly, 16S ribosomal RNA gene, Insect microbiota, Life stagesSubject terms: Microbiology, Molecular biology, Zoology

Introduction

Insects harbor diverse microbial communities that profoundly influence their physiology, development, immunity, and ecological fitness1,2. Recent advances in insect microbiome research have highlighted the critical roles of these microbial symbionts in facilitating nutrient absorption, regulating host immune responses, and providing defense against pathogens, with far-reaching implications for biotechnology, infectious disease control, and forensic science. The composition of these microbial communities is highly dynamic, shaped by factors such as diet, sex, developmental stage, and environmental exposure3. These microbial dynamics are especially evident in holometabolous insects, whose metamorphosis involves anatomical and physiological remodeling that coincides with microbial succession, ultimately influencing host biology4,5.

Blow flies (Diptera: Calliphoridae) represent an ecologically and medically important group of holometabolous insects. Their role as early colonizers of decomposing remains makes them indispensable in forensic entomology, especially for estimating the minimum postmortem interval (minPMI)6. The larval stages of blow flies develop in nutrient-rich, microbe-laden environments, necessitating strong immune defenses, which microbial symbionts often modulate1,2. Recent investigations have demonstrated that microbial communities associated with blow flies not only contribute to host development and survival but may also serve as novel sources of antimicrobial compounds and as biomarkers for minPMI estimation7. Among forensically important species, Hemipyrellia ligurriens (Wiedemann) is of particular interest in Southeast Asia, especially Thailand8, where it commonly occurs in both urban and rural environments, such as sewers, animal remains, and forests9. This species has been implicated in cases of myiasis10,11 and has been increasingly reported in forensic investigations involving human remains6,12,13. Despite its frequent occurrence in microbe-rich environments and its relevance to forensic casework, the microbiota associated with H. ligurriens remains uncharacterized.

Previous research on other dipteran species, including the black soldier fly (Hermetia illucens)14, house fly (Musca domestica)3, flesh flies (Sarcophaga spp.)15 and blow flies such as Chrysomya megacephala16, Lucilia cuprina17,18, and Lucilia sericata17–19, has revealed stage-specific and environment-driven microbial patterns. These studies consistently reported the predominance of bacterial phyla such as Proteobacteria, Bacteroidetes, and Firmicutes. In addition, microbial communities shifted significantly throughout the life cycle of an insect and responded dynamically to external factors, reflecting tightly regulated host-microbe interactions that contributed to host fitness and ecological adaptation3. Previous study showed highly similar bacterial profiles across different life stages of closely related species, L. cuprina and L. sericata17. Similarly, other research reported no significant differences in the bacterial communities between life stages of blow flies, L. sericata and Phormia regina7. Notably, although house flies and blow flies belong to different dipteran families, they share over 50% of their associated bacterial taxa20. Interestingly, laboratory-reared and naturally collected flies were found bacterial community differences21–23. Previous predictive metabolic profiling evidence indicated that microbiota and their functional roles develop in parallel with the host, forming distinct interactions at each life stage3. For example, in M. domestica flies, microbial functions shifted from basic pathways, such as carbohydrate and nucleotide metabolism in larvae, to more complex functions like amino acid metabolism, xenobiotic degradation, and signal transduction in pupae and adults3. These findings revealed that microbiota functions were closely linked to the physiological state of host and gradually adapted throughout the development of host. Based on this previous evidence, we hypothesize that H. ligurriens will harbor stage-specific microbiota that perform specialized functions during its development. Moreover, their microbiota between laboratory-reared and filed-collected flies will be different due to variations in environmental exposure and behavior.

To prove our hypotheses, the present study aimed to comprehensively characterize the microbiota associated with H. ligurriens across all life stages using 16 S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing. In addition, we also compared laboratory-reared (laboratory strain) and field-collected adult flies (natural strain), as well as between male and female individuals. We applied functional prediction tools to infer the potential roles of microbiota in host metabolic and immune functions. To our knowledge, this is the first study to systematically investigate microbiota dynamics in H. ligurriens with developmental stage, sex, and environmental exposure, providing new insights into insect-microbe coevolution and potential applications in forensic science and other aspects.

Results

Molecular identification of H. ligurriens

Molecular identification of H. ligurriens (10 sequences), using the cytochrome oxidase subunit 1 (COI) gene, confirmed the morphological identification. Analysis of the 624 bp COI gene revealed 99.84–100% similarity between all sequences in this study and known H. ligurriens sequences in GenBank. The Neighbor-Joining (NJ) analysis distinctly separated the H. ligurriens group from other genera in the family Calliphoridae (Fig. 1). All COI sequences from this study grouped with those previously reported from various countries including China, Czechia, India, Malaysia, Malta, Pakistan, Peru, Taiwan, Thailand, and the United States of America (USA) (Fig. 1). Both delimitation methods used Assemble Species by Automatic Partitioning (ASAP) and Poisson Tree Processes (PTP) yielded consistent results, identifying six Molecular Operational Taxonomic Units (MOTUs) (100% match), not including the outgroup two MOTUs. (Fig. 1).

Microbial diversity across life stages

A total of 42 samples were analyzed via 16 S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing, comprising 36 fly specimens of all developmental stages (eggs, first-, second-, and third-stage larvae, prepupae, pupae, and adults) and six rearing medium samples. After sequencing, a total of 8,564,670 raw reads were obtained. Following filtering processes, 8,016,211 effective reads remained for analysis. The number of sequences varied across sample types, with adult females from the natural strain (AFN) having the lowest number (168,317 sequences), while F1 adult female (AF) samples contained the highest number (201,493 sequences). The microbial community richness revealed a cumulative trend across the developmental stages (Table 1; Fig. 2), with microbial diversity ranging from 131 to 1,024 Amplicon Sequence Variants (ASVs) per sample. The egg samples from the laboratory strain displayed unexpectedly high diversity (482 ASVs), while the highest overall diversity was found in AFN, containing 1,024 ASVs. Higher Shannon index values, indicating greater community diversity and more even species distribution, were most prominent in AFN and egg samples from the laboratory strain (E). ASVs distribution across life stages of H. ligurriens are visualized in Fig. 2, providing insights into both shared and stage-specific ASVs. Only two core ASVs were found among all sample categories. The adult males of natural strain (AMN) displayed the highest number of category-specific ASVs (723 ASVs). In contrast, AF exhibited the lowest number (39 ASVs). Comparing microbial diversity between laboratory and natural strains, the Venn diagram shows distinct patterns of ASV distribution among adult H. ligurriens flies (Fig. 3). Natural-strain flies exhibited the highest number of unique ASVs, with 786 in AFN and 737 in AMN. In contrast, laboratory-reared flies had fewer unique ASVs, with 114 in AM and 54 in AF. A total of 176 ASVs were shared between AFN and AMN. Notably, several more minor overlaps were observed, such as four ASVs shared between AF and AFN, 20 ASVs between AM and AFN, five ASVs between AM and AMN, and 26 ASVs between AM and AF. Only 15 ASVs were shared across all four groups, while no ASVs were exclusively shared between AMN and AF. Both sexes from laboratory and natural strains had some unique ASVs.

Dominant bacterial taxa in H. ligurriens

Composition of the microbial community across the developmental stages of H. ligurriens is shown in Fig. 6 and Supplementary Table S1. Overall, the microbiota across fly samples consisted of taxa from 150 phyla, 215 classes, 453 orders, 746 families, 1,106 genera, and 477 species (Supplementary Table S1). The relative abundance of bacterial phyla associated with H. ligurriens across different developmental stages and sample groups is shown in Fig. 6A. Proteobacteria (synonym Pseudomonadota) dominated the microbial communities in all samples, consistently accounting for over 50% of the total composition. Firmicutes (synonym Bacillota) and Bacteroidota were the following most abundant phyla. Other phyla, including Myxococcota, Patescibacteria, Cyanobacteria, Verrucomicrobiota, Fusobacteriota, Actinobacteriota, and Desulfobacterota, were detected in trace amounts in some groups. The fresh liver samples used as controls revealed a bacterial community composition distinct from that of decomposed liver tissues. Fresh liver samples demonstrated higher abundances of Proteobacteria. In contrast, tainted liver samples showed higher abundances of both Proteobacteria and Firmicutes. The relative abundance of ten dominant bacterial genera across developmental stages and sample groups of this fly species is shown in Fig. 6B; the composition varied substantially by stage and condition. Laboratory strain eggs were colonized predominantly by Acinetobacter, while newly hatched larvae stage L1 were colonized primarily by Myroides. Ignatzschineria was the dominant genus in larval stages (L2 and L3), prepupa, pupa, and tainted liver. Proteus and Myroides were the highest abundance in prepupa and pupa. Commensalibacter and Asaia were more prevalent in adult samples, particularly in the laboratory strain. In contrast, adult flies from the natural strain showed different dominant genera: Acinetobacter and Pseudomonas in males, and Proteus and Myroides in females. Fresh liver samples were primarily colonized by Acinetobacter and Aeromonas, whereas tainted liver samples harbored abundant Ignatzschineria and Atopostipes. Additionally, Bacteroides was present in low abundance in certain groups. Across all sample categories, a large proportion of sequences were assigned to “Others”, indicating the presence of many low-abundance genera. Expanding the analysis to the top 35 genera provided more profound insights into the microbiota composition. The heatmap illustrates the distinct microbial profiles across the developmental stages and sample groups of H. ligurriens (Fig. 6C).

The analysis aligns samples according to developmental stage and strain, and organizes functional categories by similarity. Red areas indicate higher relative abundance, while blue areas indicate lower abundance. Early developmental stages, notably eggs and larvae (first-, second- and third-stage larvae), revealed enrichment in pathways related to lipid transport and metabolism (K00059, K00626, K00257), transcription (K03088, K03704), secretion systems (K07345), replication and repair (K07497), and carbohydrate metabolism (K01990, K01992 and K07024). Furthermore, third-stage larvae exhibited enriched defense mechanisms (K01990, K01992, K02004, K06147). In contrast, prepupal and pupal stages demonstrated distinct patterns, including increased abundance of pathways such as signal transduction (K02016), and inorganic ion transport (K02016). Adult stages exhibited elevated functional activity in sugar transporters, amino acid and energy metabolism (K02029, K00100, K08137, K02030), signal transduction (K02483, K03316, K00936, K02030, K03406), transcriptional regulation (K02529), and carbohydrate and sucrose metabolism (K07024). Discussion

This study presented the first comprehensive characterization of microbiota composition and predicted functional profiles throughout all developmental stages of H. ligurriens, uncovering stage-specific microbial patterns, clear distinctions between laboratory-reared and field-collected adult flies, and functional predictions related to host development and immune defense. The COI gene barcoding confirmed species identity and revealed minimal genetic variation between natural and laboratory strains. Phylogenetic analysis placed H. ligurriens in a distinct cluster within the Luciliinae subfamily, alongside species such as Hypopygiopsis infumata, Lucilia papuensis, and L. sericata, corroborating previous taxonomic findings24,25. Despite low genetic divergence, the potential influence of host genetic background on microbial composition cannot be ruled out, as prior studies have shown associations between host genotype and microbiota structure26. Further research involving a larger number of natural strains is recommended to clarify these relationships.

The microbial diversity patterns observed across developmental stages of H. ligurriens revealed dynamic changes in community structure, strongly influenced by host development and environmental exposure. Alpha diversity analysis showed a clear trend of increasing microbial richness from eggs to late larval stages, with the highest ASV count detected in adult females from the natural strain. This finding suggests that prolonged exposure to microbe-rich environments and active foraging behavior may contribute to elevated microbial diversity in field-collected adult flies. This observation aligned with findings in M. domestica, where early stages harbored high microbial diversity3. Notably, 40% of the top 35 dominant microbial genera were shared across feeding stages (first through third-instar larvae and prepupae) and pork liver (rearing media), suggesting a strong dietary influence on microbiota composition. However, the marked reduction in microbial diversity observed in tainted pork liver was likely attributable to the accumulation of nitrogenous waste during decomposition, larval excretions, and the production of antimicrobial compounds by specific bacterial taxa17,27–29. Only two core ASVs were consistently detected across all developmental stages, while adult males from the natural strain harbored the highest number of strain- and sex-specific ASVs, reflecting host-environment interactions. Beta diversity and PCoA analyses further supported the stage-specific clustering of microbial communities, with early-stage samples clustering with fresh liver and third-instar larvae aligning with tainted liver, indicating the influence of rearing media during feeding stage. The microbiota of the prepupal and pupal stages (non-feeding stage) clustered closely together in beta diversity analysis, likely due to their similar physiological status and developmental proximity. In contrast, the adult samples separated by rearing condition (natural vs. laboratory) showed that laboratory-reared adults exhibited significantly lower diversity, indicating the role of environmental exposure in shaping the adult microbiota. This result was similar to the previous observation in M. domestica3,22 and Cochliomyia hominivorax23. Despite the marked variation in microbiota across developmental stages and environmental conditions, no significant differences in community composition were observed between male and female flies within the same strain. Additionally, microbial diversity levels were comparable between F1 and F2 laboratory generations. These findings were consistent with previous studies in M. domestica, which reported overall similarities in microbiota between sexes and across generations. These suggest that host-associated microbial communities may be stably transmitted across generations in flies, possibly through vertical or environmental transmission mechanisms3. Collectively, these findings suggested that microbial communities were primarily shaped by developmental stage and environmental conditions30, rather than by host sex or genetic background.

The bacterial community composition of H. ligurriens demonstrated marked variation across developmental stages and between laboratory and natural strains, reflecting dynamic host-microbe interactions shaped by physiological and environmental influences. Proteobacteria dominated across all life stages, consistent with patterns reported in other blow fly species, such as L. cuprina and L. sericata17. Proteobacteria have also been widely identified across various insect species, including ants, bees, beetles, dragonflies, and mosquitoes4,31,32, suggesting they may form a core microbiota of insects. These bacteria play key roles in nutrient acquisition and immune regulation throughout the development of insects1. Firmicutes and Bacteroidota also exhibited increased representation during specific stages, notably in late larval forms and natural adult flies, suggesting roles in digestive capacity or environmental adaptation. The genus Ignatzschineria was a hallmark of the larval, prepupal, and pupal stages, supporting its established role in carrion colonization and development of blow flies. In contrast, adult microbiota composition was highly strain-dependent: Commensalibacter and Asaia dominated in laboratory-reared adults, while Proteus, Myroides, Acinetobacter, and Pseudomonas were enriched in field-collected flies. These differences highlighted the significant effect of environmental exposure on microbial acquisition and assembly during the adult stage. The egg samples were particularly rich in bacteria from the phyla Acidobacteria and Actinobacteria. Acidobacteria, known for genes involved in cellulose and protein metabolism, may contribute to biofilm formation and protect against desiccation and pathogens33. Actinobacteria, often associated with production of antimicrobial compound, likely play a defensive role during early embryogenesis27,28. As larvae matured, Ignatzschineria became dominant, a trend also observed in L. sericata, L. cuprina17, and C. megacephala34, suggesting a conserved role in larval gut function or cuticle development. The chitinolytic activity of this genus may contribute to metamorphic remodeling35, while its association with myiasis points to its forensic diagnostic value36,37. Myroides and Proteus were particularly abundant during prepupal and pupal stages, consistent with previous findings17, and may provide antimicrobial biosurfactants to protect vulnerable pupae38,39. In adults, Providencia and Morganella, both producers of bacteriolytic enzymes, were frequently detected, likely contributing to host immune defense40–42. Females uniquely harbored Faecalibacterium, which suggests an association with environments contaminated by human and animal feces. Males uniquely carried Rhodococcus, indicating a preference for decomposing organic matter. These bacteria may serve specific roles in reproduction or physiological processes. For instance, Faecalibacterium has anti-inflammatory properties that could influence female reproductive health in flies43,44. We also identified a suite of lactic acid bacteria (LAB), including Lactobacillus, Latilactobacillus, Lactococcus, and Leuconostoc, particularly in natural strain adults. These LAB taxa, common in M. domestica3 and other calliphorids17, are known for food fermentation45 and antagonism against pathogenic microbes17. Their potential application as insect-derived probiotics aligns with findings in animal agriculture, where LAB supplementation has been shown to enhance gut health and nutrient absorption46–50. Furthermore, the detection of antimicrobial-producing taxa such as Bacillus and members of the Enterobacteriaceae family suggests a potential biomedical role for H. ligurriens. Given its phylogenetic proximity to L. sericata, a species widely used in maggot debridement therapy (MDT)51, H. ligurriens may represent a promising alternative source of insect-associated antimicrobial agents30,52. Forensic applications of these findings of this study are also significant. The distinct bacterial profiles observed between fresh and decomposed liver substrates, especially the enrichment of Ignatzschineria and Atopostipes in tainted liver, mirrored microbial succession during decomposition. These genera have shown potential as postmortem interval (minPMI) biomarkers7. The integration of such microbiota-based indicators with metagenomic analysis may refine forensic models and enhance the precision of minPMI estimation53,54.

The predicted functional profiling of H. ligurriens microbiota across developmental stages revealed clear stage-specific enrichment patterns, reflecting microbial adaptation to the physiological and metabolic needs of host during growth, metamorphosis, and adult life. Functional predictions based on PICRUSt2 indicated that early developmental stages, particularly eggs and larval stages, were enriched in genes associated with carbohydrate metabolism, lipid transport, transcription, replication, and repair, which are essential for rapid growth, molting, and tissue synthesis. These results are consistent with the high metabolic demands of larvae and their reliance on carbohydrate-rich diets as a primary energy source during this active feeding phase. Notably, third-instar larvae exhibited enrichment of genes associated with defense mechanisms, including antimicrobial peptide production, which likely protects in highly microbe-rich, decomposing environments55. The prepupal and pupal stages demonstrated a functional shift, characterized by increased activity in signal transduction and inorganic ion transport pathways, which support cellular differentiation and morphogenetic remodeling during metamorphosis. In adult stages, microbial communities exhibited higher predicted activity in sugar and amino acid metabolism, transcriptional regulation, and energy-related pathways, including oxidative phosphorylation. These functions are crucial for supporting energy-demanding behaviors, such as flight, mating, and reproduction56,57. These functional transitions echo findings from other insect models, such as M. domestica3, Aldrichina grahami58, and Spodoptera littoralis57, where microbial functions dynamically shift to match host development and environmental interactions. The stage-specific functional enrichment observed in H. ligurriens thus supports the hypothesis that microbiota not only respond to but also support host physiological changes throughout the life cycle.

While this study provided the first complete overview of microbiota composition and predicted functions across all developmental stages of H. ligurriens, several limitations were identified. These included environmental and individual variation, reliance on 16 S rRNA sequencing and computational inference, and the unresolved influence of host genetic background. Future studies should include more diverse natural populations and employ metagenomic, transcriptomic, and experimental approaches to validate microbial functions. Despite these limitations, our findings establish a foundational framework for using microbiota in forensic applications, including microbial-based minPMI estimation, and highlight the potential of H. ligurriens as a source of insect-derived probiotics and antimicrobial agents.

Materials and methods

Blow fly samples

Laboratory strain

The colony of H. ligurriens used in this study originated from the Department of Parasitology, Faculty of Medicine, Chiang Mai University, Chiang Mai province, Thailand. The maintenance procedure in the laboratory was as described previously59. The colony was maintained in the medical entomology laboratory of the Department of Microbiology and Parasitology, Faculty of Medical Science, Naresuan University, Phitsanulok province, Thailand. Each stage of H. ligurriens was photographed using a Leica M205C Stereo Microscope (Leica Microsystems; Switzerland).

Natural strain

Adult flies in the natural strain were collected in September 2023 by using a sweeping net in open areas of Naresuan University, Phitsanulok province, Thailand (16°44′21′′N; 100°11′31′′E). All adults of H. ligurriens were initially identified based on their morphology using the taxonomic key60.

Molecular identification of fly specimens from laboratory and natural strains

Ten adult flies (six from the laboratory strain and four from the natural strain) were selected randomly from all specimens collected for species identification by molecular methods, as described in a previous study25. All of the sequences in this study were edited using SeqMan II, and then multiple sequences were aligned in Clustal W within the MEGA Version 7.0 program61. The COI sequences obtained were compared against the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) database (http://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Blast.cgi) using the BLAST tool to confirm species identification. Genetic distance analyses were performed using the NJ method, based on the Kimura Two-Parameter (K2P) model62 with bootstrap support from 1,000 replications in the MEGA Version 7.0 program61. The COI sequences of Drosophila melanogaster (Accession No. GQ2299519.1 and MK795998.1) were used as outgroups. The sequences obtained in this study were submitted to GenBank under accession numbers OR646670-OR646675 (laboratory strain) and OR758491-OR758494 (natural strain). Finally, two species delimitation methods were used to analyze the number of MOTUs: ASAP63 and PTP64. The ASAP method was conducted online (https://bioinfo.mnhn.fr/abi/public/asap/) with default settings (Simple Distance, p-distances), while the PTP analysis was also performed online via https://mptp.h-its.org/#/tree.

Blow fly microbiota analysis

Bacterial DNA extraction and 16 S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing

Bacterial DNA was extracted from samples representing each developmental stage (Table 1). Fresh and naturally decomposed pork livers (tainted over six days under rearing conditions) were collected from the rearing box and then processed to extract bacterial DNA, which served as control samples for the microbiome analysis. The number of samples used in this study is shown in Table 1. Samples from all developmental stages (eggs, first-, second-, and third-stage larvae, prepupae, pupae, and adults) were inactivated by freezing at -20 °C for 10 min. All samples except for the pork liver were rinsed with sterile water and 70% ethanol to eliminate surface contaminants. Each sample was then transferred to a 1.5 ml microcentrifuge tube and stored at -80 °C until DNA extraction was performed.

DNA was extracted from each frozen sample using the NucleoSpin® tissue kit (Macherey-Nagel GmbH & Co. KG, Duren, Germany) according to the manufacturer′s guidelines. The V3-V4 region of the 16 S rRNA gene was amplified by Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) using the specific primers, 341 F (5′-CCT AYG GGR BGC ASC AG-3′) and 806R (5′-GGA CTA CNN GGG TAT CTA AT-3′) (NovogeneAIT Genomics Pte. Ltd., Singapore), with sample-identifying barcodes following Novogene′s self-sequencing conditions65. Negative controls were prepared by substituting genomic DNA with deionized water. The PCR products, ranging from 400 to 450 bp in length, were quantified and assessed for quality before library preparation. Quality control protocols were applied throughout each step to ensure reliable sequencing data by the Illumina sequencing system (NovogeneAIT Genomics). Library preparation was conducted with the NEBNext® Ultra DNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina according to the manufacturer’s protocols (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA). The DNA fragments were end-repaired and A-tailed prior to Illumina adapter ligation. Quality assessment of the libraries was performed using both the Qubit 2.0 Fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) and the Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100 system (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA). Sequencing was completed on the Illumina Novaseq 6000 platform (Illumina, USA) with 250 bp paired-end reads. High-quality sequences were obtained from triplicate measurements of both the fly specimens and media samples. All sequence data have been deposited in the NCBI database under BioProject accession number: PRJNA1224942.

Bioinformatics and statistical analyses

Bioinformatics and statistical analyses followed previously established methodologies66–68. Sample identification was performed by assigning paired-end reads to their corresponding unique barcodes, followed by truncation to remove the barcode and primer sequence. FLASH (Version 1.2.11, http://ccb.jhu.edu/software/FLASH/)67 was used to merge paired-end reads where overlap existed between reads generated from opposite ends of the same DNA fragment. The resulting merged sequences, termed Raw Tags, were followed by quality filtering using fastp (Version 0.20.0) software to generate Clean Tags. The Clean Tags were then compared to reference databases (Silva database, https://www.arb-silva.de/) using Vsearch (Version 2.15.0) to detect chimera sequences. Then, the chimera sequences were removed to obtain Effective Tags66. Denoising was performed using DADA2 or deblur within QIIME2 software (Version QIIME2-202006) to generate ASVs, with the default method being DADA2. AVSs with abundances below five were filtered out68. Species annotation was performed using QIIME2 software with the Silva databases for 16 S rRNA gene analysis. Phylogenetic relationships and dominant species variations among samples were examined through multiple sequence alignment in QIIME2, with ASV abundances normalized prior to diversity analyses.

Alpha and beta diversity analyses were conducted to evaluate community structure. Alpha diversity, which measures the diversity and uniformity within sample communities, was assessed using three indices in QIIME2: Observed_otus, Chao1, and Shannon. Beta diversity, which evaluates community composition complexity and between-group differences, was calculated based on weighted UniFrac distances in QIIME2. Dimensionality reduction was conducted through principal component analysis (PCA) using R software (Version 3.5.3). Principal Coordinate Analysis (PCoA) was then performed to generate principal coordinates and visualize differences in complex multi-dimensional sample data. The weighted UniFrac distance matrix was transformed into orthogonal axes, with the first principal coordinate representing maximum variation, followed by the second principal coordinate, and so on. Three-dimensional PCoA results were displayed in QIIME2, while two-dimensional results were visualized using R software (Version 2.15.3) with ade4 and ggplot2. Species differences were identified across all taxonomic levels (phylum, class, order, family, genus, and species). MetaStat and T-test analyses were performed using R software (Version 3.5.3). PICRUSt2 software69 (Version 2.1.2-b) was used to perform functional analysis of community roles across groups by comparing representative sequences from 16 S rRNA gene amplicon data against the KEGG database70. The predicted functional profiles were visualized using a heatmap.
