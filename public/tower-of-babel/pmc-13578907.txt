Recurrent Asthma Exacerbations Secondary to Gastric Stenosis After Gastric Band Slippage: Case Report and Literature Review
Authors: Gerardo Arwi, Adriana Avram, Philip Bardin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTRecurrent asthma exacerbations despite optimal therapy warrant investigation for alternative causes. In this setting, silent aspiration is a key but often forgotten trigger of airway inflammation that should be considered in the differential diagnosis. We present a patient with long‐standing severe Type 2 asthma complicated by recurrent exacerbations of an infective nature that was ultimately found to be associated with gastric band slippage causing gastric stenosis and delayed gastric emptying. Exacerbations ceased after surgical intervention and removal of the band. The report highlights the importance of considering gastrointestinal causes, particularly in people with prior gastric surgery, as a reason for poor asthma control and repeat exacerbations. We review literature pertaining to this field and emphasise the need for a multidisciplinary approach (including surgical intervention) to identify and effectively treat silent aspiration.

Introduction

Recurrent asthma exacerbations still occur in people with severe asthma despite optimised anti‐inflammatory inhaler and more recently biologic therapies [1]. Silent aspiration is an established trigger of airway inflammation, often leading to bacterial secondary infection, which may contribute to repeated exacerbations [2]. This situation is especially likely in patients who have had gastric surgery, an increasingly common intervention for obesity, treatment‐resistant reflux disease and other intrinsic gastric pathologies. This case report highlights the importance of considering complications from gastrointestinal procedures as a treatable cause of refractory asthma exacerbations and underscores the need for a multidisciplinary approach to improve outcomes in severe asthma.

Case Report

A 71‐year‐old female with a long‐standing history of severe Type 2 asthma is presented. Over the recent 3–4 years, she started to experience repeated (2–3 annually) asthma exacerbations of an infective nature requiring repeated courses of antibiotics and oral corticosteroids. Past medical history included allergic rhinitis, chronic rhinosinusitis and obesity with BMI > 31 kg/m2. Laparoscopic adjustable gastric banding (LAGB) for weight loss was performed 11 years prior, and she was a lifelong non‐smoker with no other relevant occupational or social history.

There was good adherence with use of a fluticasone furoate/umeclidinium/vilanterol 200/62.5/25 inhaler. Biologic therapy was initially given with mepolizumab 100 mg every 4 weeks. She was then switched to dupilumab 200 mg every 2 weeks for troublesome chronic rhinosinusitis. The switch from mepolizumab to dupilumab was primarily undertaken due to persistent symptoms despite mepolizumab therapy. Despite this optimised treatment, she continued to experience 2–3 asthma exacerbations per year.

Between exacerbations, asthma was well controlled, with Asthma Control Questionnaire‐5 (ACQ‐5) scores consistently 1.0–1.5. Pulmonary function measurements had remained largely stable. Initial FEV1 1.44L (70% predicted), FVC of 2.48L (96% predicted), FEV1/FVC ratio 0.55 and diffusion of 96% predicted. Recent measurements found FEV1 1.40L (74% predicted), FVC of 2.44 L (101% predicted) and FEV1/FVC ratio 0.57. Biomarkers demonstrated a FeNO of 81 ppb, a blood eosinophil count of 0.26 × 109/L and IgE was 102 kU/L. Evidence of atopy was present, with positive skin prick testing to Dermatophagoides pteronyssinus (house dust mite).

Initial chest imaging showed clear lung fields with a small hiatus hernia. During an asthma exacerbation, right upper lobe consolidation on CT chest was observed (Figure 1A). There was no CT evidence of bronchiectasis, mucous plugging, or mosaic attenuation to suggest allergic bronchopulmonary aspergillosis. Bronchoscopy with washings revealed polymorphonuclear cells with normal flora and washings were negative for acid‐fast bacilli. After antibiotics CT chest showed resolution of the lung consolidation and other changes (Figure 1B).

(A) Chest computer tomography (CT) scan axial views showing consolidation of the right upper lobe (upper image). Imaging post‐antibiotic treatment demonstrates resolution of consolidation.

Despite the absence of reflux symptoms, the lung changes were deemed to be secondary to aspiration pneumonia. The differential diagnosis for right upper lobe consolidation in this clinical context included bacterial pneumonia, aspiration pneumonia, or pulmonary malignancy. Malignancy was considered but was felt to be unlikely given complete radiological resolution following antibiotic therapy. Although aspiration pneumonia classically involves dependent lung regions, upper lobe involvement is well recognised, particularly in ambulant or semi‐recumbent individuals, in whom the distribution of aspirated material may vary according to body position at the time of aspiration. She was reviewed by gastroenterology and subsequently underwent a gastroscopy. This revealed a markedly distorted gastric anatomy due to malpositioning of the gastric band. The band had migrated, slipped over the fundus and then migrated along the greater curvature, resulting in a double‐lumen stomach with two large lumens separated by scarred connective tissue (Figure 2). Consensus was that the recurrent asthma exacerbations were caused by silent aspiration and gastric band slippage causing gastric stenosis and delayed gastric emptying.

Upper gastrointestinal endoscopy demonstrating multiple perforations within the tissue wall separating the two large lumens of the stomach. The adjustable gastric band had slipped over the gastric fundus and migrated toward the greater curvature. Subsequent band tightening resulted in sustained apposition of the fundus and greater curvature, leading to dense fibrotic adhesion, progressive erosion and perforations traversing the apposed gastric wall.

Surgery was performed with gastric band removal. Intraoperative findings revealed extensive adhesions involving the falciform ligament and the left lobe of the liver, extending between the omentum, liver, parietal peritoneum and stomach. Dense adhesions were also noted surrounding the band. The band was dissected and removed, after which the stomach expanded naturally. Histopathological examination of tissue samples showed normal gastric antral mucosa.

The patient recovered well post‐operatively. In the 12 months following gastric band removal, she experienced no further asthma exacerbations and required no courses of oral corticosteroids, compared with 2–3 exacerbation episodes per year prior to surgery. Post‐operative biomarkers demonstrated a FeNO of 18 ppb and blood eosinophil count of 0.07 × 109/L, representing a notable reduction in markers of Type 2 inflammation. There was also a mild improvement in spirometry, with post‐operative spirometry demonstrating an FEV1 of 2.00L (76% predicted) with a 10% bronchodilator response, an FVC of 103% predicted and an FEV1/FVC ratio of 0.62.

Overall, the abrupt and sustained cessation of infective exacerbations following surgery strongly supports gastric band slippage as a major contributor to recurrent aspiration events. Biologic therapy with dupilumab was continued unchanged, as the treating team considered that the observed clinical improvement could not be attributed solely to the surgical intervention given the patient's underlying Type 2 inflammatory phenotype. Furthermore, the potential risks of biologic cessation were considered to outweigh the potential benefits in the short term.

Discussion

We report silent aspiration caused by longstanding gastric band slippage leading to gastric stenosis, delayed gastric emptying and reflux. Despite optimisation of inhaler and biologic therapy, significant improvement in asthma management occurred only after surgical intervention. This case underscores the importance of considering gastrointestinal surgical complications as a reversible cause of recurrent asthma exacerbations.

Despite the advent of advanced monoclonal biologic therapies targeting Type 2 inflammation, managing asthma exacerbations remains a significant clinical challenge [1]. Although pharmacological management has improved outcomes for many, a subset of patients continues to experience frequent exacerbations, necessitating a thorough evaluation for alternative or contributory causes. Silent aspiration, defined as the inhalation of oropharyngeal or gastric contents into the lower airways without overt symptoms, is an under‐recognised precipitant of recurrent airway inflammation and infection [2]. This is especially pertinent in patients with altered upper gastrointestinal anatomy and physiology following surgical interventions [3]. Gastrointestinal surgeries, notably bariatric procedures such as LAGB, Roux‐en‐Y gastric bypass (RYGB) and sleeve gastrectomy, are increasingly common and may predispose patients to micro‐aspiration by way of anatomical distortion, gastric outlet obstruction and reflux of gastric contents [3]. Despite the rising prevalence of these surgeries, awareness of their potential respiratory sequelae remains limited.

There is an increasing prevalence of gastrointestinal surgical interventions, driven largely by rising rates of obesity and GERD [4]. Bariatric surgeries are widely performed to treat obesity, with the main techniques being LAGB, which involves placing an adjustable band around the stomach to restrict intake. Bariatric surgery has been shown to improve pulmonary function and sleep architecture [5]. As a result, it is reasonable to assume that it also improves obesity‐related respiratory diseases such as GERD, extra‐thoracic restriction, obesity hypoventilation syndrome, or obstructive sleep apnoea. However, long‐term complications such as band slippage and gastric stenosis can cause delayed emptying and regurgitation, increasing silent aspiration risk [6]. Although LAGB placed immediately beneath the lower oesophageal sphincter (LES) functions as an effective anti‐reflux barrier, it has been hypothesised that device malposition resulting in excess stomach above the band, as seen in our case, increases the risk of reflux [7]. The mechanistic link between LAGB displacement and aspiration pneumonia is that band slippage can result in gastric outlet obstruction, impaired gastric emptying and retention of gastric contents, thereby predisposing to regurgitation and recurrent aspiration events.

Other bariatric surgeries such as RYGB and sleeve gastrectomy also pose unique aspiration risks [8]. RYGB creates a small gastric pouch connected to the jejunum, reducing acid but increasing bile reflux, which can damage the airway and cause non‐acidic aspiration [9]. Sleeve gastrectomy reduces tension of the LES, impairs gastric emptying and decreases gastric volume [10]. These changes may contribute to worsening GERD, promote micro‐aspiration and subsequently trigger asthma exacerbations. Antireflux surgeries, such as Nissen fundoplication, restore LES function to reduce GERD. However, complications such as wrap failure, tight or slipped wraps, oesophageal dysmotility and gas‐bloat syndrome can paradoxically increase aspiration risk [11]. Dysphagia due to oesophageal dysmotility may cause food stasis and impaired clearance, leading to micro‐aspiration. Gas‐bloat syndrome, arising from the inability to vent gas from the stomach into the oesophagus due to impaired relaxation of the recreated oesophageal‐gastric junction valve, can subsequently raise intragastric pressure, promote regurgitation and increase aspiration risk. Consequently, respiratory outcomes following Nissen fundoplication are variable, with some patients experiencing improvement, while others develop worsening symptoms [12].

This case highlights the importance of distinguishing true severe asthma from difficult‐to‐treat asthma, as an underlying mechanical contributor was ultimately identified. It emphasises the need to consider gastric band‐related complications as a potential contributor to difficult‐to‐control asthma, particularly in patients with a history of prior bariatric surgery. Clinical features that should prompt consideration of this diagnosis include recurrent infective exacerbations, inadequate response despite optimised asthma therapy and history of bariatric interventions. Complications of LAGB, including band slippage, can occur months to many years after placement, emphasising the importance of specifically enquiring about previous gastric surgery even when it occurred remotely [6]. Given the increasing prevalence of bariatric surgery, respiratory physicians are increasingly likely to encounter patients with late complications of LAGB and awareness of this potential complication may facilitate earlier recognition of aspiration‐related respiratory disease and appropriate multidisciplinary management.

Management of aspiration risk in the context of gastric band complications requires consideration of both medical and anatomical factors. In this case, proton pump inhibitor therapy and conservative measures, including dietary modification and positional strategies, would not be expected to correct the mechanical pathology resulting from band slippage, namely gastric outlet obstruction and the formation of a double‐lumen gastric anatomy. Given the degree of adhesion formation and the complexity of the altered anatomy, surgical band removal was considered appropriate. The proposed mechanism of benefit was restoration of gastric emptying and removal of the reservoir of retained gastric contents that was thought to contribute to recurrent aspiration events.

There are several limitations to this case report. First, baseline biomarkers prior to initiation of mepolizumab, including blood eosinophil count, FeNO and total serum IgE, were unavailable. This limits retrospective characterisation of the underlying inflammatory phenotype before biologic therapy. Second, aspiration was not directly confirmed through formal swallowing assessment, such as videofluoroscopic swallow study or fibreoptic endoscopic evaluation of swallowing. However, a multidisciplinary consensus was reached among the respiratory physician, gastroenterologist and general surgeon that the gastric abnormality was a clinically relevant contributing factor, supported by the marked clinical improvement observed following correction of the underlying anatomical issue. Third, a formal sleep study was not performed. Obstructive sleep apnoea (OSA) is a recognised comorbidity among patients with obesity and may contribute to nocturnal airway inflammation and suboptimal asthma control. Although clinical suspicion for OSA was considered low in this case based on the absence of reported daytime somnolence, the diagnosis cannot be definitively excluded without objective assessment. OSA remains an important consideration in patients with difficult‐to‐control asthma and obesity and future evaluations should include assessment for sleep‐disordered breathing when clinically indicated.

Suspecting silent aspiration in refractory asthma requires vigilance, especially in patients with prior gastrointestinal surgery. Key indicators include frequent respiratory infections and disease exacerbations resistant to treatment. A detailed surgical history and imaging review are essential to guide management. Investigations that can assist with diagnoses include upper GI endoscopy for anatomical assessment, barium swallow for structural changes, 24‐h pH impedance monitoring for reflux detection, chest imaging for aspiration‐related lung changes and gastric emptying studies to evaluate delayed transit [2]. Management often requires a multidisciplinary team including respiratory, gastroenterology and surgical specialists. Interventions may involve surgical correction, device removal and acid suppression with proton pump inhibitors. The case serves as a reminder that in patients with apparent severe asthma who do not respond as expected to biologic therapy, a systematic search for remediable comorbidities and alternative triggers, including gastrointestinal pathology, should be undertaken before attributing ongoing exacerbations to inadequate treatment response.

In conclusion, recurrent asthma exacerbations can be caused by aspiration of gastric contents. Gastrointestinal surgical procedures, while often beneficial for their primary indications, can lead to delayed gastric emptying with recurrent asthma exacerbations. This case report and literature review therefore highlights the key link of silent aspiration with poor asthma control. Recognising and addressing aspiration can improve asthma outcomes, reduce recurrent respiratory infections and asthma exacerbations and alleviate the burden of refractory disease.
