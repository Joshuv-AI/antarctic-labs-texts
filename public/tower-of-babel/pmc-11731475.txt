Identification of key genes and regulatory networks involved in the Comorbidity of atrial fibrillation and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease
Authors: Shan Xiong, Qiming Liu, Shenghua Zhou, Yichao Xiao
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe underlying molecular processes of atrial fibrillation (AF) and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) are frequently linked to increased morbidity and mortality when they co-occur. However, their underlying molecular mechanisms are questioned due to their incomplete analysis.ObjectiveThis study aimed to identify common differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in AF and COPD patients and investigate their potential biological functions and pathways. We hope to complement and update previous research through clearer figure presentation and different bioinformatic analysis methods with different datasets.MethodsWe used statistical analysis to identify DEGs in the expression profiles of AF and COPD patients using datasets from the Gene Expression Omnibus database. To ascertain whether the common DEGs were functionally enriched, Gene Ontology and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes pathway analyses were used. In addition, we generated protein‒protein interaction networks and identified significant hub genes. Furthermore, the hub genes were used to analyze transcription factor (TF)-gene interactions and TF-miRNA coregulatory networks, and their expression levels were validated in additional datasets.ResultsWe identified a total of 15 DEGs that were upregulated, whereas 36 were downregulated in AF and COPD patients. The DEGs were commonly expressed in both AF and COPD patients, with functional enrichment analysis revealing their involvement in metabolic processes and neuron-to-neuron synapses. We identified significant hub genes, including TGM2, ITPR1, CHL1, ALDOC, RPS3, FBLN2, NDUFS2, ITGA5, CTNNB1, RBP1, CLSTN2, FABP5, EPHA4, LDHA, and HNRNPL, and analyzed their coexpression and biological functions. TF-gene interaction and TF-miRNA coregulatory network analyses revealed the regulatory relationships of the hub genes. Additional datasets were analyzed to validate hub gene expression, and ALDOC, HNRNPL, and NDUFS2 displayed similar processes in AF and COPD patients.ConclusionsIn our study, we demonstrate that metabolic processes and neuron-to-neuron synaptic connections may contribute to the cooccurrence of AF and COPD. The identified hub genes and regulatory networks may act as potential biomarkers and therapeutic targets for these diseases.

Introduction

Atrial fibrillation (AF), a frequent kind of cardiac arrhythmia, is characterized by erratic electrical impulses in the atria, resulting in inefficient atrial contraction [1,2]. The incidence of AF increases with age and is higher in men than in women [3]. It is expected that the number of AF patients in the United States will increase to 12.1 million by 2030 [4]. Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is a progressive respiratory disease that has a high morbidity and mortality rate [[5], [6], [7], [8]]. Cigarette smoking and aging are the major risk factors for COPD [9]. The prevalence of COPD is high among AF patients, which leads to poorer outcomes and increased comorbidities [10,11]. However, the molecular mechanisms underlying the cooccurrence of AF and COPD are not well understood.

Bioinformatics is a crucial tool for integrating and analyzing biological data [12,13], including gene expression information. A publicly accessible database, Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO), provides genomic-scale data, including gene expression data [14]. In this study, we obtained four datasets from the GEO database and then identified differentially expressed genes that are commonly expressed in both AF and COPD. To clarify the molecular mechanisms underlying the co-expressed differentially expressed genes (co-DEGs), an enrichment analysis of Gene Ontology (GO) terms, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG), and protein‒protein interaction (PPI) network analysis were conducted. We also explored the possible mechanisms and predicted the functions of commonly expressed hub genes. Additionally, transcription factor (TF)-gene interaction networks and TF-miRNA coregulatory networks were constructed to analyze the hub genes.

Since previous articles did not explore the underlying mechanisms between AF and COPD in detail, the significance of this study is to uncover common molecular mechanisms between AF and COPD and to establish a theoretical basis for the clinical diagnosis and treatment of these conditions. We analyzed the two diseases using different data and analytical methods and found almost completely different molecular mechanisms than those proposed in a previous study [15]. The study offers other possible mechanisms, and we hope to complement the previous results based on them specifically. The findings of our study could have implications for the prevention and treatment of AF and COPD in the future [16].

Materials and methods

Data download and processing

Four datasets (GSE2240 [17,18], GSE115574 [19], GSE76925 [20], and GSE38974 [21]) were retrieved using the keywords "atrial fibrillation" and "chronic obstructive pulmonary disease" from the GEO database. Specifically, we utilized the GSE2240 dataset to explore the DEGs in AF and sinus rhythm, which consisted of 10 patients with AF and 20 individuals with sinus rhythm. For validation of the DEGs, we used GSE115574, consisting of 15 samples for sinus rhythm and 15 for AF. The GSE76925 dataset included 110 patient samples with COPD and 40 control smoker samples, and the dataset was used to identify the DEGs in COPD and healthy people. Additionally, the COPD GSE38974 dataset consisted of 23 lung samples from COPD patients and 9 lung samples from control smokers.

Selection and identification of DEGs

To explore DEGs in datasets GSE2240 and GSE76925, we opted to employ the "limma" [22] package with a fold-change larger than 1.2 and a P value within 0.05 in the GSE2240 and GSE76925 datasets [23]. Upregulated genes were defined as those with log2FC > 0, while downregulated genes were defined as those with log2FC < 0. To visualize AF-related DEGs and COPD-related DEGs, we generated volcano plots and heatmaps utilizing the R platform. The common genes in both AF and COPD were identified using Venn diagrams.

Analysis of common DEGs (co-DEGs) for functional enrichment

To explore potential biological pathways of co-DEGs, we used the "clusterProfiler" R package and performed Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses [24]. Statistical significance was determined for the top functional items and pathways among co-DEGs by a P value of 0.05.

Protein–protein interaction (PPI) network module analysis

We constructed PPI networks [25] using the STRING database (Version 11.0) with a minimum required interaction score of 0.15. Visualization of PPI networks was performed using Cytoscape (version 3.9.1), and we analyzed the molecular complex detection (MCODE) technology available as a plugin for Cytoscape to identify the key functional modules with K-core set to 2, degree cutoff set to 2, max depth set to 100, and node score set to 0.02.

Selection and functional enrichment of hub genes

We utilized the cytoHubba [26] plugin in Cytoscape, which provides 11 topology analytical methods, including degree, edge percolated component (EPC), maximum neighborhood component (MNC), density of maximum neighborhood component (DMNC), and maximal clique centrality (MCC). In general, proteins with a high degree tend to be key proteins. Therefore, we ranked co-DEGs by degree to identify the 15 hub genes [27]. Furthermore, we built a coexpression network and predicted the function of the hub genes on the basis of GeneMANIA [28]. Using the "clusterProfiler" [24] package and the R programming language, we ran GO and KEGG enrichment analyses and produced bubble and bar diagrams to better comprehend the probable biological roles of the hub genes. To be statistically significant, P values adjusted below 0.05 were considered.

Transcription factor (TF)-gene interactions

We utilized NetworkAnalyst 3.0 [29], an online platform for gene expression analysis, functional enrichment analysis, and gene-miRNA network construction, to investigate TF-gene interactions. We analyzed the common genes and transcription factors for interaction and obtained transcription factor targets from the JASPAR TF binding site profile database [29]. The resultant networks were downloaded as SIF files and visualized using Cytoscape software.

TF–miRNA coregulatory network

The TF-miRNA coregulatory network was developed by using NetworkAnalyst 3.0 to analyze the common genes. Additionally, we identified miRNAs using curated regulatory interaction data from RegNetwork [30].

Hub gene validation

Validation of the hub genes identified in our study is necessary, and we used two additional datasets (GSE115574 and GSE38974) as validation cohorts. Using the "limma" package in R, we identified DEGs using the threshold of |log2FC| > 1.2 and an adjusted P value of 0.05. Violin diagrams of the R platform were used to display the validation results. Furthermore, we used a T test to compare the expression levels of the hub genes between the control and disease groups. We considered a P value of 0.05 statistically significant, indicating that our bioinformatics analysis was reliable.

Analysis of common DEGs for functional enrichment

We analyzed the biological functions of the co-DEGs through Gene Ontology (GO) analysis using the ClusterProfiler package. The enriched biological process (BP) pathways included regulation of binding, prostaglandin metabolic process and prostanoid metabolic processes, while the cell composition (CC) pathways were mainly related to neuron-to-neuron synapses and synaptic densities. In the molecular function (MF) category, the enriched pathways were monocarboxylic acid binding and carboxylic acid binding (Fig. 2A and B). Supplemental Table S2 contains more information about the GO analysis. KEGG pathway analysis revealed the top enriched pathways to be arachidonic acid metabolism, the glucagon signaling pathway, and proteoglycans in cancer (Fig. 2C). More details of the KEGG analysis are shown in Supplemental Table S3. In addition, we conducted GO analysis of upregulated or downregulated genes, and the results showed that upregulated genes were mainly enriched in pathways such as regulation of angiogenesis and prostaglandin metabolic process in BP pathways, cell projection membrane in CC pathways and cadherin binding in MF pathways (Supplemental Fig. S3). However, the downregulated genes were mainly enriched in positive regulation of protein serine/threonine kinase activity (BP), postsynaptic density (CC) and monocarboxylic acid binding (MF). There were no shared GO pathways between upregulated or downregulated genes. These genes included Transglutaminase 2 (TGM2), Inositol 1,4,5-Trisphosphate Receptor Type 1 (ITPR1), Cell Adhesion Molecule L1 Like (CHL1), Aldolase, Fructose-Bisphosphate C (ALDOC), Ribosomal Protein S3 (RPS3), Fibulin 2 (FBLN2), NADH: Ubiquinone Oxidoreductase Core Subunit S2 (NDUFS2), Integrin Subunit Alpha 5 (ITGA5), Catenin Beta 1 (CTNNB1), Retinol Binding Protein 1 (RBP1), Calsyntenin 2 (CLSTN2), Fatty Acid Binding Protein 5 (FABP5), EPH Receptor A4 (EPHA4), Lactate Dehydrogenase A (LDHA), and Heterogeneous Nuclear Ribonucleoprotein L (HNRNPL), as visualized in Fig. 4. Using the GeneMANIA database, we analyzed these genes to predict their co-expression, colocalization, shared protein domains, and physical interactions. Discussion

AF and COPD are two prevalent health conditions that are associated with high morbidity and mortality [[31], [32], [33]]. The prevalence and incidence of these diseases have been reported in recent studies, and patients with both conditions are more likely to have comorbidities [34,35]. However, the molecular mechanisms underlying the coexistence of these two diseases remain unknown. To investigate the potential molecular mechanisms linking COPD and AF, we conducted a bioinformatics analysis of multiple microarray datasets obtained from the NCBI GEO databases. In total, we found 51 overlapping DEGs, of which 15 were upregulated and 36 were downregulated, that were expressed in both AF and COPD.

The enrichment analysis of biological processes in the Gene Ontology (GO) revealed several significant terms, including regulation of binding, prostanoid metabolic process, prostaglandin metabolic process, unsaturated fatty acid metabolic process, and icosanoid metabolic process, which are associated with inflammation and tissue regeneration. For instance, prostaglandins (PGs) produced by the metabolism of unsaturated fatty acids in cells, such as prostaglandin I2 (PGI2), prostaglandin D2 (PGD2), prostaglandin E2 (PGE2), and prostaglandin F2 alpha (PGF2α), are involved in tissue regeneration and inflammation [[36], [37], [38]]. Imbalances in PGI2 and thromboxane A2 (TXA2) levels have been associated with pulmonary hypertension in COPD patients who smoke cigarettes [39]. Conversely, PGI2 signaling inhibits Treg reprogramming to a pathogenic phenotype in COPD and reduces the incidence of reperfusion arrhythmia in dogs [40].

According to our GO analysis, the common DEGs were found to be enriched in specific cellular components, such as postsynaptic density, asymmetric synapse, postsynaptic specialization, and neuron-to-neuron synapse. The pathways of AF and COPD are influenced by the connections between synapses [41]. Neural networks rely on synapses to transmit action potentials between neurons, and an imbalance in cardiac sympathetic synapse plasticity may trigger the development of atrial fibrillation [42,43].

Furthermore, according to our GO analysis, these common genes were primarily involved in carboxylic acid and monocarboxylic acid binding, while KEGG analysis showed that arachidonic acid metabolism, the glucagon signaling pathway, and proteoglycans in cancer were significantly enriched. Arachidonic acid metabolites have been linked to cardiovascular disease, and an increase in epoxyeicosatrienoic acids, one of the arachidonic acid metabolites, has been shown to attenuate atrial structural and electrical remodeling, thereby preventing atrial fibrillation [[44], [45], [46]]. Perturbations in arachidonic acid and linoleic acid metabolism have also been linked to the development of COPD [47]. Glucagon plays a crucial role in the regulation of glucose homeostasis, amino acid metabolism, and lipid metabolism, and its dysregulation can cause insulin resistance, which can lead to diabetes [[48], [49], [50]]. Recent studies have indicated that chronic lung disease is associated with metabolic dysfunction, as indicated by the triglyceride-glucose index [51]. In addition, dysregulated blood glucose metabolism or diabetes may promote the occurrence of cardiac arrhythmias such as atrial fibrillation [52,53]. Hub genes play an important role in regulating certain biological processes. In specific scientific research, hub genes often have the strongest regulatory effect on differentially expressed gene sets (DEGs) [54,55]. Changes in hub genes are closely related to diseases. Hence, we further identified and selected 15 hub genes ranked by degree, including TGM2, ITPR1, CHL1, ALDOC, RPS3, FBLN2, NDUFS2, ITGA5, CTNNB1, RBP1, CLSTN2, FABP5, EPHA4, LDHA, and HNRNPL. We then predicted their functions using GeneMANIA and found that they were significantly associated with glucose metabolism in both COPD and AF. Furthermore, according to the analysis of gene ontology enrichment, these hub genes were involved in positive regulation of cell adhesion in biological processes and retinoid binding, isoprenoid binding, and fatty acid binding in molecular function terms. Dysregulation of cell adhesion molecules has been linked to both AF and COPD, and the upregulation of cell adhesion may be a fundamental factor in both diseases [[56], [57], [58], [59], [60]]. KEGG enrichment analysis of these hub genes revealed their involvement in various pathways in both diseases, including proteoglycans in cancer, Huntington's disease, Alzheimer's disease, pathways of neurodegeneration in multiple diseases, glycolysis and gluconeogenesis, bacterial invasion of epithelial cells, arrhythmogenic right ventricular cardiomyopathy, the glucagon signaling pathway, the HIF−1 (hypoxia-inducible factor 1) signaling pathway, and retrograde endocannabinoid signaling.

We investigated the regulatory mechanisms underlying COPD and AF by constructing a TF-gene interaction network using NetworkAnalyst. We identified 48 TFs associated with both diseases, and LDHA, HNRNPL, and FABP5 showed high interaction rates with other TF genes. Additionally, GATA binding protein 2 (GATA2) and CAMP responsive element binding protein 1 (CREB1) had the highest degree in the network. We also constructed a TF-miRNA coregulatory network to identify the relationship between shared genes, TFs, miRNAs, and proteins [61]. MYC proto-oncogene (MYC), Sp1 transcription factor (SP1), and MYC associated factor X (MAX) had the highest degree among the TFs, while human serum albumin microRNA (has-miR) 128, has-miR-128b, has-miR-153, and has-miR-19a had the highest degree among the miRNAs. MYC is an important TF in the p38MAPK-c-myc signaling pathway of patients with COPD and plays a crucial role in the development of AF [62,63].

In the validation set, we confirmed the involvement of the hub genes in both COPD and AF, with ALDOC, HNRNPL, and NDUFS2 showing similar underlying processes in both conditions. Elevated levels of ALDOC, a key enzyme in glycolysis, have been associated with various human diseases and cancers, as well as certain lung pathologies [64]. HNRNPL plays a crucial role in RNA splicing, mRNA stability, and translation regulation, and its dysregulation may be linked to myocardial injury and inflammation [[65], [66], [67], [68]]. Meanwhile, NDUFS2 emerges as a core subunit intricately involved in the pathways of the mitochondrial membrane respiratory chain NADH dehydrogenase, and its functioning is essential for hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction [69].

While our study offers valuable insights into potential molecular mechanisms linking COPD and AF, it is important to note some limitations. First, our threshold for selecting differentially expressed genes may have been too low, leading to the selection of nonsignificant genes. Second, the use of publicly available data may introduce variability in data quality, preprocessing, and normalization. Furthermore, we did not consider some confounding factors, such as age, sex, smoking history, and comorbidities, which could have impacted the genes and pathways we identified. Third, the study omitted experimental validation of the identified genes and pathways, limiting the ability to draw definitive conclusions about their clinical relevance. Fourth, the validation set used in the study was relatively small, limiting the generalizability of the findings. Fifth, this study is limited to identifying potential molecular mechanisms underlying the cooccurrence of COPD and AF, and additional research is needed to confirm the results and develop diagnostic and therapeutic approaches.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our research uncovered intricate molecular mechanisms underlying the coexistence of COPD and AF, specifically the roles of glucose metabolism, cell adhesion, and proteoglycans in both diseases. The study also identified key transcription factors and their interactions with other genes and miRNAs, revealing the regulatory mechanisms underlying these diseases. Despite limitations such as the use of publicly available data and a lack of experimental validation, researchers may be able to use these findings to discover potential therapeutic targets for COPD and AF. Further studies are needed to validate the identified genes and pathways, control for confounding factors, and develop diagnostic and therapeutic strategies. Ultimately, this work has the potential to improve our understanding of the molecular mechanisms underlying COPD and AF and may lead to improved treatment and management for patients with these conditions.
