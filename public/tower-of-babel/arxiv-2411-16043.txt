Downlink MIMO Channel Estimation from Bits: Recoverability and Algorithm
Rajesh Shrestha, Mingjie Shao, Mingyi Hong, Wing-Kin Ma, Xiao Fu

Abstract
In frequency division duplex (FDD) massive MIMO systems, a major challenge lies in acquiring the downlink channel state information (CSI) at the base station (BS) from limited feedback sent by the user equipment (UE). To tackle this fundamental task, our contribution is twofold: First, a simple feedback framework is proposed, where a compression and Gaussian dithering-based quantization strategy is adopted at the UE side, and then a maximum likelihood estimator (MLE) is formulated at the BS side. Recoverability of the MIMO channel under the widely used double directional model is established. Specifically, analyses are presented for two compression schemes—showing one being more overhead-economical and the other computationally lighter at the UE side. Second, to realize the MLE, an alternating direction method of multipliers (ADMM) algorithm is proposed. The algorithm is carefully designed to integrate a sophisticated harmonic retrieval (HR) solver as subroutine, which turns out to be the key of effectively tackling this hard MLE problem. Extensive numerical experiments are conducted to validate the efficacy of our approach.

Contributions.
This work aims to address these challenges. Our contributions are as follows:

\bullet

Recoverabilty-Guaranteed CSI Acquisition from Bits
: We propose to compress the channel matrix at the UE and then use a Gaussian dithering based quantizer to convert the entries of the channel matrix to bits.
This way, the original complex-valued channel matrix is reduced to a substantially smaller amount of binary values.
A
maximum likelihood estimator
(MLE) is formulated at the BS accordingly. We propose two different compression schemes and a unified recovery method: The first compression scheme uses simple least squares operations to acquire the channel matrix at the UE and enjoys low feedback overheads for CSI recovery; the second uses fewer computation flops at the UE, with the tradeoff of costing more feedback overhead.
Notably, our approach does not use discretized spatial dictionaries, and thus circumvents the scalability issues encountered in previous works, e.g.,
[
23
,
15
,
14
,
8
]
.
More importantly, we show that recoverability of the downlink MIMO channel matrix under both schemes is guaranteed.

\bullet

ADMM Algorithm via RELAX-based Subproblem Solving
: We propose to tackle
the formulated MLE problem under both feedback schemes using a unified optimization strategy.
Under the double directional channel model, the MLE amounts to a challenging quantized and compressed matrix recovery problem—due to antenna array manifold constraints.
Standard optimization strategies such as gradient descent were found to struggle to attain reasonable solutions (as shown later),
possibly because the complicated problem structure renders an undesired optimization landscape.
We propose an efficient
alternating direction method of multipliers
(ADMM) algorithm.
Our method “decouples” the complex recovery problem into manageable subproblems, enabling us to separately address the quantization effects and the constraints imposed by the array manifold.
In particular, it allows us to use sophisticated
harmonic retrieval
(HR) algorithms, e.g., RELAX
[
24
]
, to efficiently handle the subproblem.

The preliminary version of this work appeared at Asilomar 2022
[
25
]
.
The conference version includes one of the compression schemes and the ADMM algorithm design.
The journal version additionally includes (i) a new compression scheme, (ii) comprehensive recoverability analysis for both schemes, and (iii) more extensive experiments.

II-A

Signal Model

We consider the downlink channel estimation problem in an FDD MIMO system as shown in Fig.
1
.
The BS has
N
transmit antennas, and the UE has
M
receive antennas.
The downlink channel from the BS to the UE is assumed to remain static during the channel coherence time.
In FDD systems, the BS acquires the downlink CSI based on the UE’s feedback.
To put this into the context, in the downlink training phase, the BS transmits pilot signals to the UE, and the received signal at the UE side can be written as

\begin{split}{\bm{y}}_{t}={\bm{H}}{\bm{s}}_{t}+{\bm{n}}_{t},\quad t=1,\ldots,T,\end{split}

(1)

where
t
is the index of the pilot sequence,
{\bm{y}}_{t}\in\mathbb{C}^{M}
is the received signal at the UE side,
{\bm{s}}_{t}\in\mathbb{C}^{N}
is the transmitted pilot signal,
{\bm{n}}_{t}
is the additive complex Gaussian noise, i.e.,
{\bm{n}}_{t}\sim{\cal CN}(\bm{0},\sigma_{n}^{2}{\bm{I}})
, and

{\bm{H}}\in\mathbb{C}^{M\times N}
is the downlink channel.
The pilot signal
{\bm{S}}:=[{\bm{s}}_{1},\ldots,{\bm{s}}_{T}]
is known by both the BS and the UE.
Suppose that both the BS and the UE are equipped with uniform linear arrays (ULA).
We consider the widely-adopted double directional channel model, specifically, the
finite scatterer channel model comprising of
K
paths
[
26
]
:

\begin{split}{\bm{H}}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\beta_{k}{\bm{a}}_{r}(\theta_{k}){\bm{a}}_{t}(\phi_{k})^{\rm{H}},\end{split}

(2)

where
\beta_{k}\in\mathbb{C}
is the channel path loss of the
k
th path;

\theta_{k},\phi_{k}\in[-\pi,\pi]
are the angle of arrival (AoA) and angle of departure (AoD) of the
k
th path, respectively;

{\bm{a}}_{r}(\theta_{k})
and
{\bm{a}}_{t}(\phi_{k})
are the steering vectors associated with the AoA
\theta_{k}
and AoD
\phi_{k}
, respectively, which are given by

\begin{split}{\bm{a}}_{r}(\theta)=[1,e^{-\mathfrak{j}\frac{2\pi d}{\lambda}\sin(\theta)},\ldots,e^{-\mathfrak{j}\frac{2\pi d}{\lambda}\sin(\theta)(M-1)}]^{\top},\\
{\bm{a}}_{t}(\phi)=[1,e^{-\mathfrak{j}\frac{2\pi d}{\lambda}\sin(\phi)},\ldots,e^{-\mathfrak{j}\frac{2\pi d}{\lambda}\sin(\phi)(N-1)}]^{\top},\end{split}

(3)

in which
d
is inter-antenna spacing; and
\lambda
is the carrier wavelength.
One can express the channel model in a matrix form:

{\bm{H}}={\bm{A}}_{r}(\bm{\theta})\mbox{Diag}(\bm{\beta}){\bm{A}}_{t}(\bm{\phi})^{\rm{H}},

(4)

where the notation
\bm{\theta}=[\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{K}]^{\top}
,
\bm{\phi}=[\phi_{1},\ldots,\phi_{K}]^{\top}
, and
\bm{\beta}=[\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{K}]^{\top}
.

In FDD systems, a naive feedback scheme is as follows:
the UE estimates the downlink channel
{\bm{H}}
from the received signal
{\bm{Y}}:=[{\bm{y}}_{1},\ldots,{\bm{y}}_{T}]
using the known pilot sequences
{\bm{S}}
; this often can be done with relatively light computations, e.g., via least squares.
Then, the UE sends the estimated channel
\widehat{{\bm{H}}}
to the BS via a feedback channel.
However, in the massive MIMO regime, the channel matrix
{\bm{H}}
has a large dimension.
In such circumstances, designing limited feedback-based high-accuracy CSI recovery at the BS is well-motivated.

II-B

Limited Feedback-based CSI Estimation: Prior Art

For large-scale
{\bm{H}}
’s, quantization techniques such as VQ and codebook-based recovery at BS can be adopted to reduce the feedback overhead
[
3
,
2
]
.
VQ maps all the channel matrix realizations to several “centroids” and only feeds back the index of the centroid for each instance.
Beyond VQ, there are primarily two types of methods to feedback
\bm{H}
using limited overheads.
The first type lets the UE estimate the channel parameters
\bm{\theta}
,
\bm{\phi}
and
\bm{\beta}
from
\widehat{{\bm{H}}}

[
18
,
14
,
16
,
19
,
22
]
.
Then, the UE only sends these parameters to the BS, which has a much lower dimension than that of
{\bm{H}}
.
However, estimating these parameters often amounts to solving problems related to multi-dimensional harmonic retrieval, which are non-convex and challenging
[
27
,
28
,
29
,
22
,
16
,
19
,
22
,
30
]
.
Consequently, this type of approaches come at the cost of increased computational burden at the UE side. This is not ideal, as computational resources—e.g., power, CPU capacity, and memory—at the UE side are usually limited.
The second type of approaches shifts the computational burden to the BS side. This is arguably more desirable, as the BS has resources to perform computationally more intensive operations.
A line of work discretizes the angular space of
\bm{\theta}
and
\bm{\phi}
, forms a large angular dictionary, and transforms the parameter estimation problem into a compressive sensing (CS) problem
[
23
,
15
,
14
,
18
]
.
The CS problem is then tackled at the BS side.
Nevertheless, in order to accurately estimate the channel parameters, it requires the angular discretization to be sufficiently dense, which in turn could significantly increase the problem dimension and computational/memory complexity.

Besides the difficulties in methodology design,
another notable challenge lies in theoretical understanding.
For example, under the double directional channel model (and its variants), it has been unclear how many bits are sufficient to recover the downlink CSI at the BS to a target accuracy. However, establishing such understanding is essential for building reliable CSI recovery algorithms.

III
Proposed Approach

In this section, we propose a compression-and-quantize strategy at the UE and an MLE-based CSI recovery criterion at the BS. We develop two schemes under this framework.

III-A

Scheme 1: Estimation, Compression, and Quantization

III-A
1
Operations at UE

In this scheme, we assume that the UE first estimates
\bm{H}
via relatively simple operations, e.g., least squares.
In practice, one can choose the pilot signal
{\bm{S}}
to be row orthogonal, i.e.,

{\bm{S}}{\bm{S}}^{\rm{H}}={\bm{I}}.

As a result, the LS estimation of
{\bm{H}}
under (
1
) can be efficiently computed via

\widehat{{\bm{H}}}={\bm{Y}}{\bm{S}}^{\rm{H}}.

(5)

Note that estimating
\bm{H}
is relatively easy compared to estimating the key parameters
\bm{\theta}
,
\bm{\beta}
, and
\bm{\phi}

[
29
,
16
]
.
To proceed, we express the channel matrix as a real-valued vector

\widehat{{\bm{h}}}=\mbox{vec}(\overline{{\bm{H}}}),\quad\overline{{\bm{H}}}=\begin{bmatrix}\Re(\widehat{{\bm{H}}})\\
\Im(\widehat{{\bm{H}}})\end{bmatrix},

(6)

where
\Re(\widehat{{\bm{H}}})
and
\Im(\widehat{{\bm{H}}})
represent the real and imaginary parts of
\hat{{\bm{H}}}
, respectively.
We denote
\widehat{{\bm{h}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{J}
as the vectorized
\widehat{{\bm{H}}}
with
J=2MN
.
We compress the channel
\hat{{\bm{h}}}
by a random matrix
{\bm{A}}\in\mathbb{R}^{R\times J}
with
R\ll J
and

a_{i,j}\sim{\cal N}\left(0,\frac{1}{R}\right),\quad i=1,\ldots,R,\penalty\ j=1,\ldots,J.

The compression matrix
{\bm{A}}
is known to both the BS and the UE.
The compressed channel is given by

{\bm{x}}={\bm{A}}\hat{{\bm{h}}}.

(7)

Further, we quantize the compressed channel
{\bm{x}}
through the following:

{\bm{r}}={\cal Q}({\bm{x}}+{\bm{v}}),

(8)

where
\cal Q
is the uniform quantizer with
Q
-level output
\mathcal{U}:=\{q_{1},q_{2},\ldots,q_{Q}\}
, i.e.,

{\cal Q}(x)=q\Leftrightarrow x\in[\underline{b}[q],\bar{b}[q]),\penalty\ q\in\mathcal{U},

where
\underline{b}[q]
and
\bar{b}[q]
are the quantization boundaries associated with the quantized output
q
, and
\bm{v}
is a dithering noise with

{\bm{v}}\sim{\cal N}(\bm{0},\sigma_{v}^{2}{\bm{I}})
.
Dithering is widely used in quantization, which improves the recovery performance via making the quantization error less correlated with the input signal and more uniformly distributed
[
31
,
32
,
33
]
.
The UE sends the compressed and quantized counterpart
{\bm{r}}
, instead of the full channel matrix
\widehat{{\bm{H}}}
, to the BS.
Obviously, the feedback of
{\bm{r}}
requires much less communication overhead than that of
\widehat{{\bm{H}}}
.

III-A
2
MLE Recovery at BS

The BS receives the compressed and quantized CSI
{\bm{r}}
through a designated signaling channel.
Subsequently, the BS estimates the full channel matrix
{\bm{H}}
from
{\bm{r}}
.
Based on the compression and quantization process in (
5
)-(
8
),
the likelihood function
p({\bm{r}}|{\bm{h}})
is given by

\begin{split}p({\bm{r}}|{\bm{h}})=&\penalty\ \prod_{i=1}^{R}p(r_{i}|{\bm{h}}),\\
p(r_{i}|{\bm{h}})=&\penalty\ \Phi\left(\frac{\bar{b}[r_{i}]-{\bm{a}}_{i}^{\top}{\bm{h}}}{\sigma_{v}}\right)-\Phi\left(\frac{\underline{b}[r_{i}]-{\bm{a}}_{i}^{\top}{\bm{h}}}{\sigma_{v}}\right),\end{split}

(9)

where
\Phi(x)=\int_{-\infty}^{x}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-t^{2}/2}\ dt
is the cumulative distribution function of standard Gaussian distribution. This formulation arises from the dithering process, which induces a distribution
{\bm{x}}_{i}\sim{\cal N}({\bm{a}}_{i}^{T}{\bm{h}},\sigma_{v}^{2})
. Consequently, the probability of the quantized value
r_{i}
corresponds to the probability mass of this Gaussian distribution within the boundaries
(\underline{b}[r_{i}],\bar{b}[r_{i}])
, as expressed in (
9
).

As a result, the MLE of
{\bm{h}}
can be expressed as follows:

\begin{split}\minimize_{{\bm{z}},{\bm{H}}}&\penalty\ -\log p({\bm{r}}|{\bm{h}})\\
\mbox{subject\penalty\ to}&\penalty\ {\cal G}({\bm{z}})={\bm{H}},\\
\end{split}

(10)

where we stack the channel parameters
{\bm{z}}=(\bm{\theta},\bm{\phi},\bm{\beta})
, and the operator
\cal G
denotes the channel model (
4
) that maps the channel parameters
{\bm{z}}
to the channel
{\bm{H}}
.

III-B

Scheme 2: Direct Compression and Quantization

Scheme 1 performs
\bm{H}
estimation and feedback in a sequential manner. Acquiring
{\bm{H}}
using (
5
) requires
T\geq N
(i.e., that the pilot length is equal to or greater than
N
), but
N
could be large in massive MIMO settings. As a result, the
\bm{H}
-estimation process at UE inevitably introduces delays in the feedback, which is undesired in fast varying wireless environments.
In this subsection, we propose to compress and quantize the received signal at UE, and send them back to BS without estimating
\bm{H}
first.
This scheme reduces the “preparation time” before feeding back the channel; see
[
14
,
9
,
10
]
.

III-B
1
Operations at UE

At the UE, we compress the received signal vector
{\bm{y}}_{t}=\bm{H}\bm{s}_{t}+\bm{n}_{t}
using the following

x_{t}={\bm{a}}_{t}^{\top}\tilde{{\bm{y}}}_{t},

(11)

where
\tilde{{\bm{y}}}_{t}=[\Re({\bm{y}}_{t})^{\top},\Im({\bm{y}}_{t})^{\top}]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{2M}
is the real-valued expression of
{\bm{y}}_{t}
;

{\bm{a}}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{2M}
is the compression vector, which is assumed to follow
{\bm{a}}_{t}\sim{\cal N}(\bm{0},1/T^{2}\bm{I})
.
In Scheme 2, we choose the random pilot signal
{\bm{s}}_{t}\sim{\cal CN}(\bm{0},{\bm{I}})
.
Then, the compressed signal
x_{t}
is further quantized to a few-bit scalar for feedback via

r_{t}={\cal Q}(x_{t}+v_{t}),

(12)

where
v_{t}\sim{\cal N}(0,\sigma^{2}_{v})
.
Then, the UE sends
r_{t}
for
t=1,\ldots,T
.
Compared to Scheme 1, we no longer need to estimate the channel by the LS at the UE side.

III-B
2
MLE Recovery at BS

Note that
\tilde{{\bm{y}}}_{t}
in (
11
) can be written as

\tilde{{\bm{y}}}_{t}=\tilde{{\bm{H}}}\tilde{{\bm{s}}}_{t}+\tilde{{\bm{n}}}_{t},

where

\tilde{{\bm{H}}}=\begin{bmatrix}\Re({\bm{H}})&-\Im({\bm{H}})\\
\Im({\bm{H}})&\Re({\bm{H}})\end{bmatrix},\penalty\ \tilde{{\bm{s}}}_{t}=\begin{bmatrix}\Re({\bm{s}}_{t})\\
\Im({\bm{s}}_{t})\end{bmatrix},\penalty\ \tilde{{\bm{n}}}_{t}=\begin{bmatrix}\Re({\bm{n}}_{t})\\
\Im({\bm{n}}_{t})\end{bmatrix}.

(13)

Therefore,
{\bm{x}}
can be expressed as

\begin{split}{\bm{x}}=&\penalty\ \begin{bmatrix}{\bm{a}}_{1}^{\top}\tilde{{\bm{H}}}\tilde{{\bm{s}}}_{1}\\
\vdots\\
{\bm{a}}_{T}^{\top}\tilde{{\bm{H}}}\tilde{{\bm{s}}}_{T}\end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix}{\bm{a}}_{1}^{\top}\tilde{{\bm{n}}}_{1}\\
\vdots\\
{\bm{a}}_{T}^{\top}\tilde{{\bm{n}}}_{T}\end{bmatrix}={\bm{D}}\check{{\bm{h}}}+\check{{\bm{n}}},\end{split}

(14)

where

\begin{split}{\bm{D}}=\begin{bmatrix}\tilde{{\bm{s}}}_{1}^{\top}\otimes{\bm{a}}_{1}^{\top}\\
\vdots\\
\tilde{{\bm{s}}}_{T}^{\top}\otimes{\bm{a}}_{T}^{\top}\end{bmatrix},\quad\check{{\bm{h}}}=\mbox{vec}(\tilde{{\bm{H}}}),\penalty\ \check{{\bm{n}}}=\begin{bmatrix}{\bm{a}}_{1}^{\top}\tilde{{\bm{n}}}_{1}\\
\vdots\\
{\bm{a}}_{T}^{\top}\tilde{{\bm{n}}}_{T}\end{bmatrix},\end{split}

and we have used

{\bm{a}}_{i}^{\top}\tilde{{\bm{H}}}\tilde{{\bm{s}}}_{i}=(\tilde{{\bm{s}}}_{i}^{\top}\otimes{\bm{a}}_{i}^{\top})\mbox{vec}(\tilde{{\bm{H}}})

[
34
]
.
Then, the feedback signal model can be rewritten as

{\bm{r}}={\cal Q}({\bm{D}}\check{{\bm{h}}}+\check{{\bm{v}}}),

where
\check{{\bm{v}}}=\check{{\bm{n}}}+{\bm{v}}
and it can be shown that

\check{{\bm{v}}}\sim{\cal N}(\bm{0},\check{\sigma}^{2}{\bm{I}})

for
\check{\sigma}^{2}=\sigma_{n}^{2}+\sigma_{v}^{2}
.
Similar to Scheme 1, the BS estimates
\check{{\bm{h}}}
through the following ML estimation problem

\begin{split}\minimize_{{\bm{z}},{\bm{H}}}&\penalty\ -\log p({\bm{r}}|\check{{\bm{h}}})\\
\mbox{subject\penalty\ to}&\penalty\ {\cal G}({\bm{z}})={\bm{H}},\\
\end{split}

(15)

where the likelihood function is given by

\begin{split}p({\bm{r}}|\check{{\bm{h}}})=&\penalty\ \prod_{i=1}^{N_{S}}p(r_{i}|\check{{\bm{h}}})\\
p(r_{i}|\check{{\bm{h}}})=&\penalty\ \Phi\left(\frac{\bar{b}[r_{i}]-{\bm{d}}_{i}^{\top}\check{{\bm{h}}}}{\check{\sigma_{v}}}\right)-\Phi\left(\frac{\underline{b}[r_{i}]-{\bm{d}}_{i}^{\top}\check{{\bm{h}}}}{\check{\sigma_{v}}}\right),\end{split}

and again
the mapping
{\cal G}
represents the generation process from the channel parameter
{\bm{z}}
to the channel
{\bm{H}}
; the correspondence between
{\bm{H}}
and
\check{{\bm{h}}}
is shown in equations (
13
) and (
14
).
It is seen that Problems (
15
) and (
10
) take the same form.

Remark 1
.

In general, Scheme 1 has more operations performed by the UE as it involves estimating
{\bm{H}}
, compression, and quantization.
These give rise to
O(MNT+MNR)
flops.
Scheme 2 saves time at UE as it directly compresses the
{\bm{H}}\bm{S}
without extracting
{\bm{H}}
first, which only uses
O(MT)
flops.
Note that the flop reduction at the UE by Scheme 2 can be substantial relative to Scheme 1, as
N
could be (much) larger than
M
.
Nonetheless, as one will see in the next section, Scheme 2 gains in efficiency but loses in sample complexity—that is, Scheme 2 requires slightly more feedback bits to attain the same CSI recovery accuracy of Scheme 1.

IV
Recoverability Analysis

This section analyzes the recoverability of the proposed channel feedback schemes.

IV-A

Preliminaries

In the channel model (
2
), it is reasonable to assume that the channel path loss
\beta_{k}
is bounded. We denote the maximum amplitude of
\beta_{k}
as
\kappa
, i.e.,
|\beta_{k}|\leq\kappa
for all
i=1,\ldots,K
.
The parameter space of
{\bm{z}}
, denoted by
\mathcal{Z}
, is then described by

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}=

\displaystyle\penalty\ \mathcal{Z}_{\theta}\times\mathcal{Z}_{\phi}\times\mathcal{Z}_{\beta},

(16)

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}_{\theta}=

\displaystyle\penalty\ [-\pi,\pi]^{K},\penalty\ \mathcal{Z}_{\phi}=[-\pi,\pi]^{K},\penalty\ \mathcal{Z}_{\beta}=\{\bm{\beta}\mid\|\bm{\beta}\|_{\infty}\leq\kappa\},

where
\mathcal{Z}_{\theta}
,
\mathcal{Z}_{\phi}
and
\mathcal{Z}_{\beta}
represent the parameter space of
\bm{\theta}
,
\bm{\phi}
and
\bm{\beta}
, respectively.
It is seen that the set
\mathcal{Z}
is compact.
In addition, the parameter space of
{\bm{h}}
and
\check{{\bm{h}}}
, generated by
{\bm{H}}={\cal G}({\bm{z}})
for
{\bm{z}}\in\mathcal{Z}
, are denoted by

\Gamma:=\{{\bm{h}}\mid{\bm{h}}=g({\cal G}({\bm{z}})),\penalty\ {\bm{z}}\in\mathcal{Z}\}

(17)

\check{\Gamma}:=\{\check{{\bm{h}}}\mid\check{{\bm{h}}}=\check{g}({\cal G}({\bm{z}})),\penalty\ {\bm{z}}\in\mathcal{Z}\}

(18)

where
g
and

Our analysis will utilize the covering number of the above sets, which is defined as follows:

Definition 1

(Covering Number
[
35
]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a set of vectors. Then, a set

is said to be an

with respect to the Euclidean distance if for any

such that
\|{\bm{x}}-{\bm{x}}^{\prime}\|_{2}\leq\epsilon
.
The smallest cardinality of the
\epsilon
-net among all the possible
\epsilon
-nets of
\mathcal{X}
is called the covering number of the
\mathcal{X}
and is represented as
\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{X},\epsilon)
.

In addition, we have the following lemma:

Fact 1
.

There exists a finite
L_{\cal G}<\infty
such that

\displaystyle\|{\cal G}({\bm{z}})-{\cal G}({\bm{z}}^{\prime})\|_{\rm{F}}\leq L_{\cal G}\|{\bm{z}}-{\bm{z}}^{\prime}\|_{2};

(19)

that is, the function
{\cal G}
is Lipstchiz continuous.

Note that

\bm{z}
; see Appendix

in the supplementary material of this paper.

It is not hard to show the following facts:

Fact 2
.

The covering numbers of the sets
\cal Z
and

\begin{split}{\mathcal{C}}(\mathcal{Z},\epsilon)\leq\left(\frac{8\pi\kappa}{\epsilon^{2}}\right)^{2K},\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \mathcal{C}(\Gamma,\epsilon)\leq\left(\frac{8\pi\kappa L_{\cal G}^{2}}{\epsilon^{2}}\right)^{2K}.\end{split}

The proof is in Appendix

Fact 3
.

Assume that the channel path losses satisfy
|\beta_{k}|\leq\kappa
for
k=1,\ldots,K
, where
\kappa>0

\|{\bm{H}}\|_{\rm{F}}\leq K\sqrt{MN}\kappa.

Proof
: We have

\begin{split}\|{\bm{H}}\|_{\rm{F}}=&\penalty\ \|{\bm{A}}_{r}(\bm{\theta})\mbox{Diag}(\bm{\beta}){\bm{A}}_{t}(\bm{\phi})^{\rm{H}}\|_{\rm{F}}\\
\leq&\sum_{k=1}^{K}|\beta_{k}|\left\|{\bm{a}}_{r}(\theta_{k}){\bm{a}}_{t}(\phi_{k})^{\rm{H}}\right\|_{\rm{F}}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}|\beta_{k}|\sqrt{MN}\\
\penalty\ \leq&K\sqrt{MN}\|\bm{\beta}\|_{\infty},\end{split}

where the first inequality is by applying basic properties of norms (i.e., the triangle inequality and homogeneity).
\blacksquare

Our analysis requires the characterization of the likelihood function.
Consider the model in (
8
)–(
9
).
Denote

f_{r}(x)=\Phi\left(\frac{\bar{b}[r]-x}{\sigma_{v}}\right)-\Phi\left(\frac{\underline{b}[r]-x}{\sigma_{v}}\right).

(20)

Note that when
x
is bounded, the negative log-likelihood function satisfies

\sup_{x}\max_{r\in\mathcal{U}}-\log f_{r}(x)\leq U_{f}

(21)

for a certain constant
U_{f}>0
.
In addition, as the gradient of
f_{r}(x)
is continuous, by the extreme value theorem we have

\sup_{x}\max_{r\in\mathcal{U}}\left|\frac{f_{r}^{\prime}(x)}{f_{r}(x)}\right|\leq L_{f}

(22)

for some constant
L_{f}>0
, where
L_{f}
being small implies that the function
f_{r}(x)
does not change too rapidly.
Similarly, we have

\inf_{x}\max_{r\in\mathcal{U}}\frac{(f_{r}^{\prime}(x))^{2}}{f_{r}(x)}\geq F_{f}

(23)

for some constant
F_{f}>0
.
The constant
F_{f}
being bounded away from zero means that the function
f_{r}(x)
does not change too slowly.

We make the following assumption.

Remark 3

Compared with Scheme 1 (cf. Theorem

Remark 4
.

Despite its importance, the recoverability of array manifold-structured matrices from quantized and compressed measurements had not previously been established.
The topic is related to low-rank matrix/tensor completion from quantized measurements (see, e.g.,

[
36
,
37
,
38
,
33
]
).
The works in
[
39
,
40
,
33
]
established recoverability over nonlinear manifolds represented by neural networks and multilinear structures, which is closely related to our setting.
Nonetheless, some key analytical tools (e.g., Hoeffding’s inequality) used under completion settings cannot be applied to our Gaussian compression case.
The latter was only studied in quantized compressive sensing for sparse signals
[

,
42
,
43
,
44

However, their proofs are based on

Our analysis builds upon a set-restricted eigenvalue condition

This new proof pipeline also provides a viable path for establishing guarantee in problems with other complex structural constraints under Gaussian compression.

V
Proposed Channel Estimation Algorithm

This section presents an algorithm for solving (
10
) and (
15
).
We describe the algorithm based on (
10
) and will briefly comment on the latter that can be handled by the same algorithm.
A natural thought is to use the gradient descent (GD) algorithm to tackle (
10
),
as the constraint can be “absorbed” into the objective, resulting in differentiable form shown in (
27
):

\minimize_{{\bm{z}}}\penalty\ -\log p({\bm{r}}|{\cal G}({\bm{z}}))

(27)

However, gradient descent performs unsatisfactorily (see Fig.
3
)—possibly because the Vandermonde manifold-induced landscape has too many local minima.
In this work, we propose an ADMM algorithm
[
46
]
to tackle problem (
10
) that leverages sophisticated harmonic retrieval (HR) solvers to handle the array manifold.

To begin with, the augmented Lagrangian function associated with problem (
10
) is expressed as follows:

{\cal L}({\bm{z}},{\bm{H}},\bm{\Lambda})=-\log p({\bm{r}}|{\bm{h}})\\
+\langle\bm{\Lambda},{\cal G}({\bm{z}})-{\bm{H}}\rangle+\frac{\rho}{2}\|{\cal G}({\bm{z}})-{\bm{H}}\|_{\rm{F}}^{2},

(28)

where
\bm{\Lambda}
is the Lagrangian dual variable and
\rho>0
is a hyperparameter.
In each iteration, the ADMM algorithm performs the following operations:

ADMM for Solving (
10
):

\displaystyle{\bm{z}}^{\ell}=\underset{{\bm{z}}}{\arg\min}\left\|{\cal G}({\bm{z}})-\left({\bm{H}}^{\ell-1}-\frac{\bm{\Lambda}^{\ell-1}}{\rho}\right)\right\|_{\rm{F}}^{2},

(29a)

\displaystyle{\bm{H}}^{\ell}=\underset{{\bm{H}}}{\arg\min}-\log p({\bm{r}}|{\bm{h}})+\frac{\rho}{2}\left\|{\bm{H}}-{\cal G}\left({\bm{z}}^{\ell}\right)-\frac{\bm{\Lambda}^{\ell-1}}{\rho}\right\|_{\rm{F}}^{2},

(29b)

\displaystyle\bm{\Lambda}^{\ell}=\bm{\Lambda}^{\ell-1}+\rho\left({\cal G}({\bm{z}}^{\ell})-{\bm{H}}^{\ell}\right),

(29c)

where
\ell
denotes the iteration number.
As mentioned, the MLE problem (
10
) is challenging because of the manifold structure (represented by
{\cal G}(\bm{z})
). Under the ADMM update rule, estimating
\bm{z}
from
{\cal G}(\bm{z})
is now only present in the subproblem (
29a
), having a relatively “clean” least squares-based denoising form.
Next, we describe how to solve the subproblems (
29a
) and (
29b
), respectively.

V-A

Modified RELAX for Subproblem (
29a
)

The subproblem (
29a
) can be viewed as an HR problem, if one regards (
{\bm{H}}^{\ell-1}-\bm{\Lambda}^{\ell-1}/\rho
) as a noisy multi-dimensional harmonic signal
[
27
,
30
]
.
This is because, similar to HR, Eq. (
29a
) also aims to recover the frequency components
(\bm{\theta},\bm{\beta})
and amplitudes
\bm{\beta}
from the noisy two-dimensional signal
({\bm{H}}^{\ell-1}-\bm{\Lambda}^{\ell-1}/\rho)
.
Noisy multi-dimensional HR poses a challenging nonconvex optimization problem.
Nonetheless, as a classical task in array processing, many sophisticated HR algorithms exist.
We propose to employ the celebrated RELAX method
[
24
]
to tackle (
29a
). Note that the original RELAX algorithm essentially discretizes the spatial domain (i.e.,
[-\pi,\pi]
) and estimates
\theta_{i}
and
\phi_{i}
over discrete spatial grids. This is not desired in CSI recovery. Hence, we also propose a simple modification step to the RELAX iterations as remedy.

To explain the modified RELAX algorithm, note that the subproblem in (
29a
) can be written as

{\bm{z}}=\underset{(\bm{\beta},\bm{\phi},\bm{\theta})}{\arg\min}\left\|\bm{\zeta}-\sum_{k=1}^{K}{\bm{a}}_{t}^{*}(\phi_{k})\otimes{\bm{a}}_{r}(\theta_{k})\beta_{k}\right\|_{2}^{2},

(30)

where
\bm{\zeta}=\mbox{vec}({\bm{H}}-\bm{\Lambda}/\rho)
.
The RELAX algorithm’s hierarchy consists of two levels: The outer level estimates
\{\beta_{i},\phi_{i},\theta_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\eta}
and varies
\eta
from
1
to
K
incrementally.
Within each outer step
\eta
, the inner loop uses
block coordinate descent
(BCD) to estimate
(\beta_{1},\phi_{1},\theta_{1}),\ldots,(\beta_{\eta},\phi_{\eta},\theta_{\eta})
together. This way, RELAX gradually finds the most primary harmonics and was numerically found to be robust against convergence to poor local minima.
To be more specific, the outer iteration
\eta
(where
\eta\in\{1,\ldots,K\}
) deals with the following:

\minimize_{\{\beta_{i},\phi_{i},\theta_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\eta}}\left\|\bm{\zeta}-\sum_{i=1}^{\eta}{\bm{a}}_{t}^{*}(\phi_{i})\otimes{\bm{a}}_{r}(\theta_{i})\beta_{i}\right\|_{2}^{2}.

(31)

The inner iterations update

(\beta_{i},\phi_{i},\theta_{i})
for
i\in\{1,\ldots,\eta\}
in an alternating optimization manner by solving

(\beta_{i},\phi_{i},\theta_{i})=\underset{(\beta_{i},\phi_{i},\theta_{i})}{\arg\min}\left\|\bm{\zeta}_{i}-{\bm{a}}_{t}^{*}(\phi_{i})\otimes{\bm{a}}_{r}(\theta_{i})\beta_{i}\right\|_{2}^{2},

(32)

where

\bm{\zeta}_{i}=\bm{\zeta}-\sum_{j=1,j\neq i}^{\eta}{\bm{a}}_{t}^{*}(\phi_{j})\otimes{\bm{a}}_{r}(\theta_{j})\beta_{j}.

Problem (
32
)
is shown to be equivalent to the following:

\displaystyle(\phi_{i},\theta_{i})

\displaystyle=\arg\max_{\phi_{i},\theta_{i}}\left|{\bm{a}}_{r}(\theta_{i})^{\rm{H}}\mat(\bm{\zeta}_{i}){\bm{a}}_{t}(\phi_{i}))\right|^{2},

(33)

where we have
\beta_{i}=\nicefrac{{({\bm{a}}_{t}(\phi_{i})\otimes{\bm{a}}_{r}(\theta_{i}))^{\rm{H}}\bm{\zeta}_{i}}}{{MN}}
and the matricization function “
{\rm mat}(\cdot)
” is the inverse operation of “
{\rm vec}(\cdot)
”. The original RELAX algorithm approximates the solution of (
33
) using 2D-FFT.
RELAX is widely recognized as an effective method for handling 2D HR
[
27
]
.
Nonetheless, the 2D-FFT step works over a discretized array manifold, which means that the accuracy of RELAX is limited by the discretization resolution (i.e., the size of the DFT matrix used).
To pull the RELAX-found solutions back to the continuous space, we further refine the solution by running gradient ascent for solving (
33
); that is,

\displaystyle(\phi_{i},\theta_{i})\leftarrow(\phi_{i},\theta_{i})+\alpha\nabla f_{i,\eta}^{\rm HR}(\phi_{i},\theta_{i})

(34)

where
f_{i,\eta}^{\rm HR}(\phi_{i},\theta_{i})=|{\bm{a}}_{r}(\theta_{i})^{\rm{H}}\mat(\bm{\zeta}_{i}){\bm{a}}_{t}(\phi_{i}))|^{2}
.
The gradient updates in (
34
) follows the 2D-FFT step for solving (
33
). The modified RELAX (
Mod-RELAX
) algorithm is summarized in Appendix
A
.

\displaystyle g_{i}^{j}=

\displaystyle\penalty\ {\bm{a}}_{i}^{\top}{\bm{h}}^{j}-\sigma\frac{\phi\left(\frac{\bar{b}[r_{i}]-{\bm{a}}_{i}^{\top}{\bm{h}}^{j}}{\sigma_{v}}\right)-\phi\left(\frac{\underline{b}[r_{i}]-{\bm{a}}_{i}^{\top}{\bm{h}}^{j}}{\sigma_{v}}\right)}{\Phi\left(\frac{\bar{b}[r_{i}]-{\bm{a}}_{i}^{\top}{\bm{h}}^{j}}{\sigma_{v}}\right)-\Phi\left(\frac{\underline{b}[r_{i}]-{\bm{a}}_{i}^{\top}{\bm{h}}^{j}}{\sigma_{v}}\right)},\penalty\ \forall i\in[R],

(35)

\displaystyle{\bm{h}}^{j+1}

\displaystyle=\underset{{\bm{h}}}{\arg\min}\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\|{\bm{A}}{\bm{h}}-{\bm{g}}^{j}\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{\rho}{2}\|{\bm{h}}-{\bm{u}}^{\ell}\|_{2}^{2},

(36)

VII
Conclusion

In this paper, we revisited the limited feedback-based CSI estimation problem in FDD MIMO systems.
We proposed a random compression and noise dithering-based quantization framework for CSI feedback.
Our approach features two different compression schemes that demonstrate a trade off between UE preparation time and feedback overhead.
Under our framework, most of the heavy computations are carried out by the BS.
We showed that, through MLE-based recovery at the BS, the proposed schemes can provably recover the downlink CSI under reasonable conditions, providing valuable insights into sample complexity of recovering array manifold-constrained channels.
To handle the challenging optimization problem associated with the MLEs,
we proposed an efficient ADMM algorithm, namely,
REDEEM
, that judiciously employs a sophisticated, classic HR algorithm to tackle its hard subproblem.
We conducted extensive numerical simulations to test the performance of our method, showcasing promising channel estimation accuracy.
