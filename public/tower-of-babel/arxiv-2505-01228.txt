Ind-cluster algebras and infinite Grassmannians
Sira Gratz, Christian Korff

Abstract
. A prototypical examples of a cluster algebra is the coordinate ring of a finite Grassmannian: using the Plücker embedding the cluster algebra structure allows one to move between ‘maximal sets’ of algebraically independent Plücker coordinates via mutations. Fioresi and Hacon studied a specific colimit of the coordinate rings of finite Grassmannians and its link with the infinite Grassmannian introduced by Sato and independently by Segal and Wilson in connection with the Kadomtsev-Petiashvili (KP) hierarchy, an infinite set of nonlinear partial differential equations which possess soliton solutions. In this article we prove that this ring is a cluster algebra of infinite rank with the structure induced by the colimit construction. More generally, we prove that cluster algebras of infinite rank are precisely the ind-objects of a natural category of cluster algebras.

1.
Introduction

Infinite-dimensional Grassmann manifolds were introduced by Sato in
[
Sat81
,
Sat83
]
and by Segal, Wilson in
[
SW85
]
as well as by Pressley, Segal in
[
PS85
]
. The constructions in
[
Sat81
,
Sat83
]
and
[
SW85
]
are motivated by the observation that their points are in one-to-one correspondence with solutions of an integrable hierarchy of non-linear partial differential equations (PDEs), the Kadomtsev-Petiashvili (KP) hierarchy, which describes shallow water waves and possesses soliton solutions. The KP hierarchy plays a central role in the Riemann-Schottky problem and the proof of the Novikov conjecture
[
Shi86
]
. It connects the area of integrable systems with algebraic geometry
[
KS11
]
but it is also of interest in representation theory of loop groups
[
PS85
]
and in connection with the boson-fermion correspondence as well as vertex operator algebras and symmetric functions, see e.g.
[
MJD00
,
KRR13
]
as well as references therein.

Key to understanding the link between the KP-hierarchy and the underlying algebraic and geometric structure of infinite dimensional Grassmannians is the notion of a
\tau
-function introduced by the Kyoto School in the 1990s, which allows one to formulate the PDEs in an algebraic form, known as Hirota’s bilinear identity, that is equivalent to the union of the Plücker relations of all finite Grassmannians; see e.g. the textbook
[
MJD00
]
and references therein. The solutions of the KP-hierarchy can therefore be identified with points on an infinite Grassmannian (defined independently in
[
Sat81
,
Sat83
]
and
[
SW85
]
), which is sometimes in the literature simply called the ‘Sato Grassmannian’ although the constructions in these sources differ in some technical aspects; we refer the reader to
[
SW85
, §10]
and
[
PS85
]
for details.

For the sake of concreteness we recall the definition of the infinite Grassmannian
\mathrm{Gr}(H)

. That is, we can think of the subspace

of finite order elements as the space

of formal Laurent series in the variable
z
and the latter is dense in

. Consider the decomposition

1.1.
Plücker relations and cluster algebras

There is a one-to-one correspondence between the Plücker relations defining the coordinate ring
\mathbb{C}[\mathrm{Gr}]
and the PDEs making up the KP hierarchy
[
Sat83
]
. However, as is already known from the finite case, the set of Plücker coordinates is algebraically dependent. This is where cluster algebras come in: their clusters consist of maximal sets of algebraically independent Plücker coordinates from which, by a purely combinatorial procedure called mutation, all other Plücker coordinates can be obtained. For the coordinate rings
\mathbb{C}[\mathrm{Gr}_{m,n}]
of finite Grassmannians
\mathrm{Gr}_{m,n}
the cluster algebra structure was worked out by Scott
[
Sco06
]
. In particular, Scott’s work describes clusters of Plücker coordinates and their mutations in explicit combinatorial terms using Postnikov diagrams.

To capture the algebraic-combinatorial structure of the coordinate ring
\mathbb{C}[\mathrm{Gr}]
(as introduced in
[
Sat81
,
Sat83
]
and studied by Fioresi and Hacon in
[
FH04
]
), finite-rank cluster algebras will not do. In this article the focus is instead on infinite rank cluster algebras whose study was initiated in
[
GG14
]
inspired by work on infinite rank cluster categories
[
HJ12
]
, and has since been extended and applied in the context of combinatorial models
[
BG18
]
, Teichmüller theory and cluster algebras from infinitely marked surfaces
[
cF19
]
, quantum affine algebras
[
HL16a
]
,
[
HL16b
]
,
[
GHL24
]
as well as from a representation theoretic perspective notably with the introduction of discrete cluster catgories of type
A
in
[
IT15
]
, as well as their combinatorial
[
PY21
]
and categorical
[
CG24
]
completions, and Grassmannian cluster categories of infinite rank in
[
ACF+23a
]
, inspired by the construction from
[
JKS16
]
. These categories have been studied from a cluster combinatorial perspective for example in
[
BKM16
]
,
[
GHJ19
]
,
[
PY21
]
,
[
ACF+23b
]
and
[
cKP24
]
.

1.2.
Main result: Ind-Cluster Algebras

In this paper, we tackle infinite rank versions of cluster algebras by studying ind-objects in a category of finite rank cluster algebras. The approach to study infinite rank cluster algebras as ind-objects was first employed in
[
Gra15
]
, where it was shown that every cluster algebra of infinite rank arises as a colimit of cluster algebras of finite rank in the category
\mathsf{mCl}
of
inducible melting cluster morphisms
, introduced, with slightly different conventions, in
[
ADS14
]
. Its objects are rooted cluster algebras, that is, cluster algebras with a fixed initial seed, with clusters of arbitrary cardinality, and its maps are morphisms which commute with mutation starting at the initial cluster. The freedom we have is that we are allowed sending cluster variables to integers (“specialising”), as well as sending frozen cluster variables to exchangeable cluster variables (“melting”), but not vice versa. We provide a converse to
[
Gra15
, Theorem 1.1]
.

Theorem 1.3

(Theorem
2.12
)
.

The category
\mathsf{mCl}
of inducible melting cluster morphisms is closed under filtered colimits.

We show that the compact objects of
\mathsf{mCl}
are precisely the rooted cluster algebras of finite rank. In particular, if we stick with the common convention that stipulates that a cluster algebra has initial cluster of finite cardinality, it makes sense to initially consider the full category
\mathsf{mCl}^{\mathrm{f}}
of
\mathsf{mCl}
whose objects are finite rank rooted cluster algebras. We then obtain

Corollary 1.4
.

The rooted cluster algebras of infinite rank are the ind-objects of
\mathsf{mCl}^{\mathrm{f}}
.

We are thus motivated to call cluster algebras of infinite rank
ind-cluster algebras
, to distinguish them from classical cluster algebras. That is, an ind-cluster algebra is just an ind-object of
\mathsf{mCl}^{\mathrm{f}}
, where we forget the datum of the initial seed.

Remark 1.5
.

While cluster algebras of infinite rank successfully deal with the situation where we encounter infinite clusters—a situation that naturally arises both from representation theoretic and combinatorial perspectives—it fails to take into account the possibility of infinite mutations, which were successfully studied from combinatorial, and representation theoretic perspectives in
[
BG18
]
,
[
cF19
]
and
[
ACF+23a
]
. We close this gap in a forthcoming paper
[
GK
]
, by also introducing
pro-cluster algebras
; the idea of which was first introduced in
[
Wie22
]
.

1.3.
Application: the coordinate ring of the Sato-Segal-Wilson Grassmannian

We approach the cluster algebra structure of the coordinate ring
\mathbb{C}[\mathrm{Gr}]
from this perspective of ind-cluster algebras:
as already mentioned, the ring
\mathbb{C}[\mathrm{Gr}]
studied in
[
FH04
]
is defined as a colimit
\mathbb{C}[\mathrm{Gr}]=\mathop{\mathchoice{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\hfil$\displaystyle\operator@font colim$\hfil\cr\kern 1.5pt\cr\rightarrow\cr\kern-1.0pt\cr\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\hfil$\textstyle\operator@font colim$\hfil\cr\kern 1.5pt\cr\rightarrow\cr\kern-1.0pt\cr\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\hfil$\scriptstyle\operator@font colim$\hfil\cr\kern 1.5pt\cr\rightarrow\cr\kern-1.0pt\cr\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\hfil$\scriptscriptstyle\operator@font colim$\hfil\cr\kern 1.5pt\cr\rightarrow\cr\kern-1.0pt\cr\cr}}}}\displaylimits\mathbb{C}[\mathrm{Gr}_{m,n}]
of the homogeneous coordinate rings of finite dimensional Grassmannians which carry a classical cluster algebra structure, as proved in
[
Sco06
]
. It is this natural cluster algebra structure which induces the ind-cluster algebra on the coordinate ring
\mathbb{C}[\mathrm{Gr}]
.

It has been argued in
[
FH04
, Prop 2.10 (ii)]
that
\mathbb{C}[\mathrm{Gr}]
can be viewed as a
coordinate ring of
\mathrm{Gr}
, in the sense that the closed points of
\mathrm{Proj}\;\mathbb{C}[\mathrm{Gr}]
are in one-to-one correspondence with the points of the ‘universal Grassmann manifold’ postulated by Sato and which is closely related to the one considered by Wilson and Segal
[
SW85
]
. Using Theorem
1.3
, we show the following:

Theorem 1.6
.

The ring
\mathbb{C}[\mathrm{Gr}]
is a cluster algebra of infinite rank. Its cluster structure is induced by the classical cluster structures on homogeneous coordinate rings of finite dimensional Grassmannians.

Using Sato’s results on the connection between points on the infinite Grassmannian
\mathrm{Gr}
and solutions of the KP-hierarchy, it then follows that the general solutions,
\tau
-functions, of the KP-hierarchy carry an ind-cluster algebra structure. Using the ind-cluster algebra we will prove the following:

Theorem 1.7
.

Let
W\in\mathrm{Gr}
be a point on the Sato-Segal-Wilson Grassmannian. Then the associated Plücker coordinates
\Delta_{\lambda}(W)
, where
\lambda
ranges over all partitions, are Laurent polynomials with non-negative coefficients of those coordinates for which
\lambda
has a Young diagram of rectangular shape.

We will recall the precise definition of the coordinates
\Delta_{\lambda}(W)
later in Section
4.2
.

Remark 1.8
.

Various types of
\tau
-functions have been studied extensively in the literature. Perhaps most famously, multi-soliton solutions of the KP-hierarchy have been constructed (see e.g.
[
MJD00
]
,
[
KRR13
]
as well as references therein) and each such solution corresponds to a point
W
on the Sato-Segal-Wilson Grassmannian
\mathrm{Gr}
. Other celebrated examples include
\tau
-functions obtained from algebraic curves; see e.g.
[
Kri77a
,
Kri77b
,
Dub81
,
HÈ11
]
and
[
Kod24
]
. Another point of view is to interpret
\tau
-functions of integrable hierarchies, and the ones of the KP-hierarchy in particular, as generating functions for objects in enumerative geometry; see e.g. the review
[
KL15
]
as well as references therein.

More recently, Kodama and Williams
[
KW11
,
KW14
]
connected a special class of soliton solutions of the KP-equation to the combinatorics of plabic graphs which describe the cluster algebra structure for the coordinate ring of
finite
Grassmannians. These data of the finite Grassmannians describe the asymptotics of these soliton solutions and are not connected to the Plücker coordinates of the Sato-Segal-Wilson Grassmannian which are the focus of this article; see the comments in Section
4.3
at the end of this article.

There is a growing literature and research activity on ‘positive geometries’ emerging from physics and related mathematics and combinatorics; see e.g.
[
Lam24
,
RST25
]
for some recent overview articles as well as references therein. On the mathematics side the probably best studied examples are related to Lusztig’s notion of
total positivity

[
Lus94
,
Lus98
]
in the case of (finite-dimensional) Grassmannians; for a small sample of works see e.g.
[
SW05
,
Pos06
,
KW14
,
Wil21
]
. Inspired by the connection between total positivity of finite Grassmannians and KP solitons
[
KW14
]
we generalise in this article the definition of total positivity to the infinite Sato-Segal-Wilson Grassmannian:

Definition 1.9
.

Define the totally positive Sato-Segal-Wilson Grassmannian
\mathrm{Gr}^{+}
to be the set of all points
W\in\mathrm{Gr}
such that their Plücker coordinates
\Delta_{\lambda}(W)>0
for all partitions
\lambda
.

As an immediate corollary of the ind-cluster algebra structure on
\mathbb{C}[\mathrm{Gr}]
and the above theorem we can then conclude the following:

Corollary 1.10
.

We have
W\in\mathrm{Gr}^{+}
if and only if
\Delta_{\lambda}(W)>0
for all rectangular partitions
\lambda
.

It would be interesting as a future application to further investigate positivity aspects of the known stratification of the Sato-Segal-Wilson Grassmannian
[
SW85
,
PS85
]
and solutions of the KP-hierarchy by making use of the ind-cluster algebra structure similar as finite rank cluster algebras are being used to discuss positivity for finite Grassmannians; see e.g.
[
Wil21
]
.

2.
Ind-cluster algebras

2.1.
Rooted cluster algebras

A holistic definition of a structure preserving map between cluster algebras is hard to come by: The recursive combinatorial definition of a cluster structure on an algebra, which contributes to its computational attractiveness, also makes it difficult to pin down what a map preserving this structure should look like.

A version of a category of pointed cluster algebras has been proposed by Assem, Dupont and Schiffler
[
ADS14
]
. They introduce the category
\sf{Clus}
of rooted cluster algebras. Its object are
rooted cluster algebras
, that is, cluster algebras with a fixed initial seed. This allows for a convenient definition of what it means for a ring homomorphism to commute with mutation. Before we treat the morphisms, we give a brief introduction to (rooted) cluster algebras. We follow the convention and notation from
[
ADS14
]
; the definitions in this section go, unless otherwise stated, back to Fomin and Zelevinsky
[
FZ02
]
. While for the reader encountering cluster algebras for the first time, the definitions might appear at first quite technical, they will soon become more natural when observed in concrete examples.

We explicitly include seeds of infinite rank, as introduced in
[
GG14
]
. For this purpose, we consider
infinite integer matrices
: Specifically, given a set
\mathrm{X}
of arbitrary cardinality, and a subset
\mathrm{ex}\subseteq\mathrm{X}
, we consider tuples of the form
B=(b_{vw})_{v\in\mathrm{ex},w\in\mathrm{X}}
from
\mathbb{Z}
, and call this an integer matrix with rows labelled by
\mathrm{ex}
and columns labelled by
\mathrm{X}
. The submatrix
B\mid_{\mathrm{ex}}=(b_{vw})_{v,w\in\mathrm{ex}}
is called the
\mathrm{ex}
-part of
B
. The matrix
B
is called
\mathrm{ex}
-locally finite
, if every row has only finitely many non-zero entries, that is, for all
v\in\mathrm{ex}
there are only finitely many
w\in\mathrm{X}
such that
b_{vw}\neq 0
. It is called
skew-symmetriseable
if the
\mathrm{ex}
-part of
B
is skew-symmetriseable, that is, there exist positive integers
\{d_{v}\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}\}_{v\in\mathrm{ex}}
such that for all
v,w\in\mathrm{ex}
we have
d_{v}b_{vw}=-b_{wv}d_{w}
.

Definition 2.1
.

A seed is a triple
\Sigma=(\mathrm{X},\mathrm{ex},B)
, where
\mathrm{X}
is a set of indeterminates,
\mathrm{ex}\subseteq\mathrm{X}
and
B=(b_{vw})_{v\in\mathrm{ex},w\in\mathrm{X}}
is an
\mathrm{ex}
-locally finite skew-symmetriseable integer matrix.

The set
\mathrm{X}
is called the cluster, the elements of the set
\mathrm{ex}
the exchangeable variables, the elements of the set
\mathrm{X}\setminus\mathrm{ex}
the frozen variables, and the matrix
B
the exchange matrix of
\Sigma
. The cardinality
|\mathrm{X}|
is the rank of
\Sigma
. The
\mathrm{ex}
-part of
B
is called the principal part of
B
.

Remark 2.2
.

Conventionally, the cluster
\mathrm{X}
is assumed to be finite. We explicitly allow the infinite rank case. The only concession we make is the
\mathrm{ex}
-local finiteness condition on the exchange matrix
B
. This will ensure that the exchange relations (
2.1
) from Definition
2.4
are well-defined.

Remark 2.3
.

If the principal part of the exchange matrix
B
of a seed
\Sigma=(\mathrm{X},\mathrm{ex},B)
is skew-symmetric, that is, if for all
v,w\in\mathrm{ex}
we have
b_{vw}=-b_{wv}
, we can consider the
associated ice quiver
instead: It is the quiver with vertices given by
\mathrm{X}
and with
b_{uv}
arrows from vertex
u
to vertex
v
whenever
b_{uv}>0
and
-b_{uv}
arrows from
u
to
v
whenever
b_{uv}<0
. The vertices corresponding to frozen cluster variables are marked as frozen, symbollically this is done by drawing a box around the vertex. When working in concrete examples, it will sometimes be useful to allow arrows between frozen variables (cf. Section
3.3
). These are of no importance for the seeds, and are not recorded in the exchange matrix.
The ice quiver associated with a seed encodes all the information of the seed. In examples of seeds, we will often present the ice quiver in lieu of the triple of cluster, exchangeable variables, and exchange matrix. For example, the seed

\Sigma=(\{x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4}\},\{x_{1},x_{2}\},B=\begin{bmatrix}0&1&1&1\\
-1&0&-2&1\end{bmatrix})

can be represented by either of the two ice quivers

\mathrm{ex}

Definition 2.4
.

Let
\Sigma=(\mathrm{X},\mathrm{ex},B=(b_{uv}))
be a seed, and let
x\in\mathrm{ex}
. The mutation
\mu_{x}(\Sigma)
of
\Sigma
at
x
is the seed

\mu_{x}(\Sigma)=(\mu_{x}(\mathrm{X}),\mu_{x}(\mathrm{ex}),\mu_{x}(B)),

where, setting

(2.1)

\displaystyle x^{\prime}=\frac{\prod_{b_{xu}>0}v^{b_{xu}}+\prod_{b_{xv}<0}v^{-b_{xv}}}{x}\in\mathbb{Q}(\mathrm{X})

we have

•

\mu_{x}(X)=(X\setminus\{x\})\cup x^{\prime}
;

•

\mu_{x}(\mathrm{ex})=(\mathrm{ex}\setminus\{x\})\cup x^{\prime}
;

•

\mu_{x}(B)
is Fomin-Zelevinsky mutation of the matrix
B
at the row and column labelled by
x
, i.e. we have
\mu_{x}(B)=(b^{\prime}_{uv})_{u\in\mu_{x}(\mathrm{ex}),v\in\mu_{x}(\mathrm{X})}
, where

b^{\prime}_{uv}=\begin{cases}-b_{xv}&\text{ if $u=\mu_{x}(x)$}\\
-b_{ux}&\text{ if $v=\mu_{x}(x)$}\\
b_{uv}+\frac{1}{2}(|b_{ux}|b_{xv}+b_{ux}|b_{xv}|)&\text{otherwise.}\par\end{cases}

Equation (
2.1
) is referred to as an exchange relation. Furthermore, given
y\in\mathrm{X}
we write

\mu_{x}(y)=\begin{cases}x^{\prime}&\text{if $y=x$}\\
y&\text{else},\end{cases}

so that we have
\mu_{x}(\mathrm{X})=\{\mu_{x}(y)\mid x\in\mathrm{X}\}
.

We can naturallly iterate mutation from a given seed. This prepares the definition of a rooted cluster algebra later.

Definition 2.5
.

Let
\Sigma=(\mathrm{X},\mathrm{ex},B=(b_{vw}))
be a seed. A
\Sigma
-admissible sequence is a finite sequence
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{\ell})
such that
x_{1}\in\mathrm{ex}
and for all
2\leq i\leq\ell
we have

x_{i}\in\mu_{x_{i-1}}\circ\cdots\circ\mu_{x_{1}}(\mathrm{ex}).

We call
\ell
the length of the sequence
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{\ell})
.
Two seeds
\Sigma
and
\Sigma^{\prime}
are called mutation equivalent, if there exists a
\Sigma
-admissible sequence
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{\ell})
such that

\Sigma^{\prime}=\mu_{x_{\ell}}\circ\cdots\mu_{x_{1}}(\Sigma).

Given a
\Sigma
-admissible sequence
\underline{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{\ell})
we write
\mu_{\underline{x}}
for the composite
\mu_{x_{\ell}}\circ\cdots\circ\mu_{x_{1}}
. By convention, mutation along the empty sequence
\varnothing
of length
\ell=0
does not change the seed:
\mu_{\varnothing}(\Sigma)=\Sigma
.
Indeed, mutation equivalence defines an equivalence relation on the class of all seeds. Given a seed
\Sigma
, we denote by
[\Sigma]
its mutation equivalence class.

Definition 2.6

(
[
FZ02
, Definition 2.3]

[
ADS14
, Definition 1.4]
)
.

Let
\Sigma=(\mathrm{X},\mathrm{ex},B)
be a seed. The cluster algebra associated to
\Sigma
is the subalgebra

\mathcal{A}(\Sigma)=\mathbb{Z}\Bigl[\bigcup_{(\tilde{\mathrm{X}},\tilde{\mathrm{ex}},\tilde{B})\in[\Sigma]}\tilde{\mathrm{X}}\Bigr]\subseteq\mathbb{Q}(\mathrm{X})

of the field of rational functions in
\mathrm{X}
, generated by the elements of clusters of seeds in
[\Sigma]
.

•

The elements in
\bigcup_{(\tilde{\mathrm{X}},\tilde{\mathrm{ex}},\tilde{B})\in[\Sigma]}\tilde{\mathrm{X}}
are called the cluster variables of
\mathcal{A}(\Sigma)
.

•

The elements in
\bigcup_{(\tilde{\mathrm{X}},\tilde{\mathrm{ex}},\tilde{B})\in[\Sigma]}\tilde{\mathrm{ex}}
are called the exchangeable cluster variables of
\mathcal{A}(\Sigma)

•

The elements in
\mathrm{X}\setminus\mathrm{ex}
, are called the frozen variables of
\mathcal{A}(\Sigma)
.

•

The rank of
\mathcal{A}(\Sigma)
is
|\mathrm{X}|
, the rank of
\Sigma
.

•

The seeds in
[\Sigma]
are called the seeds of
\mathcal{A}
.

•

The pair
(\mathcal{A}(\Sigma),\Sigma)
is called the rooted cluster algebra with initial seed
\Sigma
.

Note that the cluster algebra associated to
\Sigma
only depends on the mutation equivalence class of
\Sigma
. We remove this freedom when passing to the rooted cluster algebra, which comes with a fixed initial seed. By abuse of notation, we write
\mathcal{A}(\Sigma)
for the rooted cluster algebra
(\mathcal{A}(\Sigma),\Sigma)
.

Notation 1
.

For ease of notation, for any
\Sigma
-admissible sequence
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})
and any integer
m\in\mathbb{Z}
we will allow the expression
\mu_{x_{k}}\circ\cdots\circ\mu_{x_{1}}(m)
to mean

\mu_{x_{k}}\circ\cdots\circ\mu_{x_{1}}(m)=m,

that is, mutation of an integer along an admissible sequence leaves the integer unchanged.

2.2.
A category of rooted cluster algebras

Let
\mathcal{A}(\Sigma)
and
\mathcal{A}(\Sigma^{\prime})
be rooted cluster algebras with initial seeds
\Sigma=(\mathrm{X},\mathrm{ex},B)
and
(\mathrm{X}^{\prime},\mathrm{ex}^{\prime},B^{\prime})
respectively. Given a ring homomorphism
f\colon\mathcal{A}(\Sigma)\to\mathcal{A}(\Sigma^{\prime})
and a
\Sigma
-admissible sequence
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{\ell})
, we call the sequence
(f,\Sigma,\Sigma^{\prime})
-
biadmissible
if its image
(f(x_{1}),\ldots,f(x_{\ell}))
is
\Sigma^{\prime}
-admissible. When the rootings are clear from context, we usually just write that a sequence
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{\ell})
is
f
-biadmissible
. We now have the tools to define structure preserving maps between cluster algebras.

Definition 2.7
.

[
[
ADS14
, Definition 2.2]
]
A melting cluster morphism
f\colon\mathcal{A}(\Sigma)\to\mathcal{A}(\Sigma^{\prime})
between rooted cluster algebras with seeds
\Sigma=(\mathrm{X},\mathrm{ex},B)
and
\Sigma^{\prime}=(\mathrm{X}^{\prime},\mathrm{ex}^{\prime},B^{\prime})
respectively, is a unital ring homomorphism satisfying the following:

CM1:

f(\mathrm{X})\subseteq\mathrm{X}^{\prime}\cup\mathbb{Z}
;

CM2:

for any
f
-biadmissible sequence
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{\ell})
and for all
y\in\mathrm{X}
, we have

f(\mu_{x_{\ell}}\circ\cdots\circ\mu_{x_{1}}(y))=\mu_{f(x_{\ell})}\circ\cdots\circ\mu_{f(x_{1})}(f(y));

MCM:

f(\mathrm{ex})\subseteq\mathrm{ex}^{\prime}\cup\mathbb{Z}
.

Axioms CM1 and CM2 are not enough to ensure composability of cluster morphisms, hence further assumptions are required. The first M in the name of axiom MCM stands for
melting
: We are allowed to
melt
frozen cluster variables, that is, send a frozen cluster variable to an exchangeable. However, we are not allowed to freeze: An exchangeable cluster variable in the domain’s initial seed
\Sigma
must be sent to an exchangeable cluster variable in the target’s initial seed
\Sigma^{\prime}
. In
[
Wie22
]
the opposite case is treated, where we are allowed
freezing
(sending exchangeable cluster variables to frozen variables), but not melting.

Proposition 2.8

(
[
ADS14
, Proposition 2.5]
)
.

There is a category
\sf{Clus}
which has as objects rooted cluster algebras and as morphisms melting cluster morphisms.

However, melting cluster morphisms may exhibit degenerate behaviour; they do not necessarily preserve the cluster structure in a meaningful way, as the following example illustrates.

Example 2.1
.

Consider the seed
\Sigma
of rank
3
and the seed
\Sigma^{\prime}
of rank
1
determined by their ice quivers, the latter with a single vertex:
\Sigma=
x
y_{1}
y_{2}
\Sigma^{\prime}=
z

We have
\mathcal{A}(\Sigma)=\mathbb{Z}[x,y_{1},y_{2},\frac{y_{1}+y_{2}}{x}]
and
\mathcal{A}(\Sigma^{\prime})=\mathbb{Z}[z,\frac{2}{z}]
. The map

\displaystyle f\colon\mathcal{A}(\Sigma)

\displaystyle\to

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}(\Sigma^{\prime})

\displaystyle x

\displaystyle\mapsto

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle y_{1}

\displaystyle\mapsto

\displaystyle 1

\displaystyle y_{2}

\displaystyle\mapsto

\displaystyle-1

\displaystyle\frac{y_{1}+y_{2}}{x}

\displaystyle\mapsto

\displaystyle r

is a rooted cluster morphism for any
r\in\mathcal{A}(\Sigma)
. The cluster structure is not meaningfully preserved.

Therefore, we restrict our attention to melting cluster morphisms which guarantee that the cluster structure is preserved, and the morphism is controlled by its values on the initial cluster. These morphisms were first studied by Chang and Zhu in
[
CZ16
]
.

Definition 2.9

(
[
CZ16
, Definition 2.8]
)
.

A melting cluster morphism
f\colon\mathcal{A}(\Sigma)\to\mathcal{A}(\Sigma^{\prime})
between rooted cluster algebras with initial seeds
\Sigma=(\mathrm{X},\mathrm{ex},B)
and
\Sigma^{\prime}=(\mathrm{X}^{\prime},\mathrm{ex}^{\prime},B^{\prime})
respectively is called inducible if it satisfies the following axiom:

iMCM

f(\mathrm{ex})\subseteq\mathrm{ex}^{\prime}\cup\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
.

By the Laurent phenomenon
[
FZ02
, Theorem 3.1]
, which asserts that every cluster variable can be expressed as a Laurent polynomial in its initial cluster, with denominator a product of exchangeable cluster variables, an inducible cluster morphism is uniquely determined by its values on the initial cluster.

Definition 2.10
.

We denote by
\mathsf{mCl}
the wide subcategory of
\sf{Clus}
consisting of inducible melting cluster morphisms, and call this the category of melting cluster morphisms.

We recall that a
wide
subcategory of a category of
\mathcal{C}
is a subcategory of
\mathcal{C}
which has the same objects as
\mathcal{C}
, but not necessarily all of its morphisms. We are now ready to discuss the main new objects, ind-cluster algebras.

2.3.
Cluster algebras of infinite rank are ind-cluster algebras

Cluster algebras of infinite rank have first been studied in
[
GG14
]
. It was shown in
[
Gra15
]
that any cluster algebra of infinite rank
\mathcal{A}
can be realised as a colimit of cluster algebras of finite rank in
\mathsf{mCl}
.

Theorem 2.11

(
[
Gra15
]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a cluster algebra of infinite rank, and let
\Sigma
be any seed of
\mathcal{A}
. The rooted cluster algebra
\mathcal{A}(\Sigma)
is isomorphic to a filtered colimit of rooted cluster algebras of finite rank in
\mathsf{mCl}
.

Proof.

In
[
Gra15
, Theorem 4.6]
it is shown that every rooted cluster algebra of countably infinite rank is isomorphic to an
\mathbb{N}
-shaped colimit of rooted cluster algebras of finite rank in
\mathsf{mCl}
. It follows from Remark
2.15
that every rooted cluster algebra of infinite rank is isomorphic to a filtered colimit of rooted cluster algebras of countable rank.
The assertion follows.
∎

The converse also holds: filtered colimits of finite rank cluster algebras exist in
\mathsf{mCl}
. In fact:

Theorem 2.12
.

The category
\mathsf{mCl}
has filtered colimits.

Proof.

By
[
AN82
, Theorem 1]
the existence of directed colimits implies the existence of filtered colimits. The claim now follows from the explicit construction from Theorem
2.31
, which is proved in Section
2.6
.
∎

In Sections
2.5
and
2.6
, we explicitly construct directed colimits in
\mathsf{mCl}
, which requires us to consider exchangeably connected components.

2.4.
Neighbours and exchangeably connected components

Each seed consists of distinct exchangeably connected components.

Definition 2.13
.

Let
\Sigma=(\mathrm{X},\mathrm{ex},B)
be a seed. Two cluster variables

and
y\in\mathrm{X}
are called
B
-neighbours if
b_{xy}\neq 0
. For any subset

we write

N_{B}(S)=\{y\in\mathrm{X}\mid\text{$y$ is a $B$-neighbour of $z$ for some $z\in S$}\}.

If

for

.
Two elements

are called

(x_{i})_{i=0}^{n+1}
with

•

v=x_{0}
and
w=x_{n+1}
;

•

x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\in\mathrm{ex}
;

•

for all

the consecutive elements
x_{i}
and
x_{i+1}
are
B
-neighbours.

Remark 2.14
.

Note that two frozen variables in
\mathrm{X}
cannot be

Let

into its

Remark 2.15
.

Every rooted cluster algebra
\mathcal{A}(\Sigma)
can be realised, in
\mathsf{mCl}
, as a filtered colimit of its exchangeably connected components, and its isolated frozens. In particular, every rooted cluster algebra can be realised in this manner as a colimit of countable rank rooted cluster algebras in
\mathsf{mCl}
, since by
[
Gra15
, Remark 3.18]
every exchangeably connected component is countable, and every isolated frozen component has finite rank
1
.

Concretely, let the index set
I_{1}
label the exchangeably connected components
\Sigma_{\alpha}=(\mathrm{X}[\alpha],\mathrm{ex}[\alpha],B\mid_{\mathrm{X}[\alpha]})
of
\Sigma
, where
\alpha\in I_{1}
, and let the index set
I_{2}
label its isolated frozen components
\Sigma_{\beta}=(\mathrm{X}_{\beta}=\{x_{\beta}\},\varnothing,\varnothing)
, where
\beta\in I_{2}
. Set
I=I_{1}\sqcup I_{2}
and consider the set
\mathcal{F}(I)\subseteq 2^{I}
of finite subsets of
I
. For
f\in\mathcal{F}(I)
we set
\Sigma_{f}=(\mathrm{X}_{f}=\bigcup_{\alpha\in f}\mathrm{X}[\alpha],\bigcup_{\alpha\in f}\mathrm{ex}[\alpha],B\mid_{\mathrm{X}_{f}})
. If
f\subseteq g\in F(I)
, the inclusion
\mathrm{X}_{f}\to\mathrm{X}_{g}
induces a map
\mathcal{A}(\Sigma_{f})\to\mathcal{A}(\Sigma_{g})
in
\mathsf{mCl}
. Hence we obtain a filtered diagram
\mathcal{F}(I)\to\mathsf{mCl}
, of which
\mathcal{A}(\Sigma)
is the colimit.

We can read off from the initial seeds whether two rooted cluster algebras are isomorphic in
\mathsf{mCl}
. The key is the notion of
similarity
.

Definition 2.16
.

[
[
Gra15
, Definition 3.24]
]
Two seeds
\Sigma=(\mathrm{X},\mathrm{ex},B=(b_{uv})_{u,v\in\mathrm{X}})
and
\Sigma^{\prime}=(\mathrm{X}^{\prime},\mathrm{ex}^{\prime},B^{\prime}=(b^{\prime})_{u^{\prime}v^{\prime}})_{u^{\prime},v^{\prime}\in\mathrm{X}^{\prime}}
are called similar, if there exists a bijection
\varphi\colon\mathrm{X}\to\mathrm{X}^{\prime}
such that

•

the map
\varphi
restricts to a bijection
\varphi\colon\mathrm{ex}\to\mathrm{ex}^{\prime}
;

•

for every exchangeably connected component
\Sigma[\alpha]=(\mathrm{X}[\alpha],\mathrm{ex}[\alpha],B[\alpha])
of
\Sigma
there exists a
\sigma_{\alpha}\in\{+1,-1\}
such that following holds: If
u\in\mathrm{ex}[\alpha]
and
v\in\mathrm{X}[\alpha]
, then

b_{uv}=\sigma_{\alpha}b^{\prime}_{\varphi(u)\varphi(v)}.

If the map
\varphi
is the identity map, then we call the seeds strongly similar.

Proposition 2.17
.

[
[
Gra15
, Theorem 3.25]
]
Two rooted cluster algebras are isomorphic in
\mathsf{mCl}
if and only if their initial seeds are similar.

Remark 2.18
.

Mutation at an exchangeable variable
x\in\mathrm{ex}
only affects the exchangeably connected component containing
x
: If
\Sigma_{\alpha}
is an exchangeably connected component of
\Sigma
with
x\in\mathrm{ex}\setminus\mathrm{ex}[\alpha]
, then
\mu_{x}(\Sigma_{\alpha})=\Sigma_{\alpha}
.

Lemma 2.19
.

[
[
Gra15
, Lemma 3.28]
[
Gra15
, Proposition 3.8]
]
Let
\Sigma=(\mathrm{X},ex,B=(b_{vw})_{v,w\in\mathrm{X}})
and
\Sigma^{\prime}=(\mathrm{X}^{\prime},\mathrm{ex}^{\prime},B^{\prime}=(b^{\prime}_{vw})_{v,w\in\mathrm{X}^{\prime}})
be seeds.
Let
f\colon\mathrm{X}\to\mathrm{X}^{\prime}
be a melting cluster morphism. The following hold.

(1)

If
x,y\in\mathrm{X}
with
x\neq y
but
f(x)=f(y)\in\mathrm{X}^{\prime}
, then
x,y\in\mathrm{X}\setminus\mathrm{ex}
are frozen variables.

(2)

For every exchangeably connected component
\Sigma^{\prime}_{\alpha}=(\mathrm{X}^{\prime}[\alpha],\mathrm{ex}^{\prime}[\alpha],(B^{\prime})^{\alpha})
of
\Sigma^{\prime}
there exists a
\sigma_{\alpha}\in\{+,-\}
such that the following holds: For every
v\in\mathrm{ex}
with
f(v)\in\mathrm{ex}^{\prime}[\alpha]
and every
w^{\prime}\in\mathrm{X}^{\prime}
we have

b_{f(v)w^{\prime}}=\sigma_{\alpha}\sum_{f(w)=w^{\prime}}b_{vw}.

Moreover, the summands in the sum are either all positive or all negative.

We conclude the section with an observation on possible specialisations of neighbours of exchangeable cluster variables: Neighbours of
x\in\mathrm{ex}
with
f(x)\in\mathrm{ex}^{\prime}
can only be specialised to either
1
or
-1
, and the product of all of their images is
1
.

Lemma 2.20
.

Let
f\colon\mathcal{A}(\Sigma)\to\mathcal{A}(\Sigma^{\prime})
be a melting cluster morphism, where
\Sigma=(\mathrm{X},\mathrm{ex},B=(b_{uv}))
and
\Sigma^{\prime}=(\mathrm{X}^{\prime},\mathrm{ex}^{\prime},B^{\prime}=(b^{\prime}_{uv}))
. Denote by
\mathrm{X}_{\mathbb{Z}}=f^{-1}(\mathbb{Z})\cap\mathrm{X}
the initial cluster variables which get sent to integers. If
x\in\mathrm{ex}
with
f(x)\in\mathrm{ex}^{\prime}
, then

f(\prod_{y\in{\mathrm{X}_{\mathbb{Z}}}\colon b_{xy}>0}y^{b_{xy}})=1=f(\prod_{y\in{\mathrm{X}_{\mathbb{Z}}}\colon b_{xy}<0}y^{-b_{xy}}).

In particular, setting
\mathrm{X}_{cl}=\mathrm{X}\setminus\mathrm{X}_{\mathbb{Z}}=\{x\in\mathrm{X}\mid f(x)\in\mathrm{X}^{\prime}\}
, we have

f(\mu_{x}(x))=\frac{\prod_{u\in\mathrm{X}_{cl}\colon b_{xu}>0}u^{b_{xu}}+\prod_{v\in\mathrm{X}_{cl}\colon b_{xv}<0}v^{-b_{xv}}}{x}.

Proof.

Set
f(x)=x^{\prime}
. Since the sequence
(x)
is
f
-biadmissible, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\prod_{y\in\mathrm{X}\colon b_{xy}>0}f(y)^{b_{xy}}+\prod_{y\in\mathrm{X}\colon b_{xy}<0}f(y)^{-b_{xy}}}{f(x)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f(\mu_{x}(x))=\mu_{f(x)}(f(x))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\prod_{y^{\prime}\in\mathrm{X}^{\prime}\colon b^{\prime}_{x^{\prime}y^{\prime}}>0}{y^{\prime}}^{b^{\prime}_{x^{\prime}y^{\prime}}}+\prod_{y^{\prime}\in\mathrm{X}^{\prime}\colon b^{\prime}_{x^{\prime}y^{\prime}}<0}{y^{\prime}}^{-b^{\prime}_{x^{\prime}y^{\prime}}}}{f(x)}

Since
\prod_{y^{\prime}\in\mathrm{X}^{\prime}\colon b^{\prime}_{x^{\prime}y^{\prime}}>0}{y^{\prime}}^{b^{\prime}_{x^{\prime}y^{\prime}}}
and
\prod_{y^{\prime}\in\mathrm{X}^{\prime}\colon b^{\prime}_{x^{\prime}y^{\prime}}<0}{y^{\prime}}^{-b^{\prime}_{x^{\prime}y^{\prime}}}
are coprime, by algebraic independence of
\mathrm{X}^{\prime}
we must have

\prod_{y\in\mathrm{X}\colon b_{xy}>0}f(y)^{b_{xy}}=\prod_{y^{\prime}\in\mathrm{X}^{\prime}\colon b^{\prime}_{x^{\prime}y^{\prime}}>0}{y^{\prime}}^{b^{\prime}_{x^{\prime}y^{\prime}}}\;\text{ and }\prod_{y\in\mathrm{X}\colon b_{xy}<0}f(y)^{-b_{xy}}=\prod_{y^{\prime}\in\mathrm{X}^{\prime}\colon b^{\prime}_{x^{\prime}y^{\prime}}<0}{y^{\prime}}^{-b^{\prime}_{x^{\prime}y^{\prime}}}

or vice versa. Hence, all of these are monic monomials; the claim follows.
∎

Construction of ind-seeds

Throughout this section, we fix a directed system

. For all

set
F(i)=\mathcal{A}(\Sigma_{i})
with seed
\Sigma_{i}=(\mathrm{X}_{i},\mathrm{ex}_{i},B^{i}=(b_{vw}^{i})_{v,w\in\mathrm{X}_{i}})
, and for a morphism

Definition 2.21
.

The initial cluster functor
\mathbb{X}
is the functor

which assigns to each rooted cluster algebra

\mathcal{A}((\mathrm{X},\mathrm{ex},B))
the set
\mathrm{X}\cup\mathbb{Z}
, and to each melting cluster morphism

its restriction
f\mid_{\mathrm{X}\cup\mathbb{Z}}\colon\mathrm{X}\cup\mathbb{Z}\to\mathrm{X}^{\prime}\cup\mathbb{Z}
.

Similarly, the exchangeable initial cluster functor
\mathbb{E}
is the functor which assigns to each rooted cluster algebra
\mathcal{A}((\mathrm{X},\mathrm{ex},B))
the set
\mathrm{ex}\cup\mathbb{Z}
, and to each melting cluster morphism

its restriction

We will use these functors to define seeds for ind-cluster algebras. Their clusters and exchangeable variables can be computed using colimits in the category of sets.

Remark 2.22
.

We want to consider the sets
\mathrm{X}=(\mathop{\mathchoice{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\hfil$\displaystyle\operator@font colim$\hfil\cr\kern 1.5pt\cr\rightarrow\cr\kern-1.0pt\cr\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\hfil$\textstyle\operator@font colim$\hfil\cr\kern 1.5pt\cr\rightarrow\cr\kern-1.0pt\cr\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\hfil$\scriptstyle\operator@font colim$\hfil\cr\kern 1.5pt\cr\rightarrow\cr\kern-1.0pt\cr\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\hfil$\scriptscriptstyle\operator@font colim$\hfil\cr\kern 1.5pt\cr\rightarrow\cr\kern-1.0pt\cr\cr}}}}\displaylimits\mathbb{X}\circ F)\setminus\mathbb{Z}
and
\mathrm{ex}=(\mathop{\mathchoice{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\hfil$\displaystyle\operator@font colim$\hfil\cr\kern 1.5pt\cr\rightarrow\cr\kern-1.0pt\cr\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\hfil$\textstyle\operator@font colim$\hfil\cr\kern 1.5pt\cr\rightarrow\cr\kern-1.0pt\cr\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\hfil$\scriptstyle\operator@font colim$\hfil\cr\kern 1.5pt\cr\rightarrow\cr\kern-1.0pt\cr\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\hfil$\scriptscriptstyle\operator@font colim$\hfil\cr\kern 1.5pt\cr\rightarrow\cr\kern-1.0pt\cr\cr}}}}\displaylimits\mathbb{E}\circ F)\setminus\mathbb{Z}

where for

and

if and only if

for some

with

. Setting

, we can also write

\mathrm{X}\cong\bigsqcup_{i\in\mathcal{F}}\tilde{X}_{i}\big/\sim

Similarly, we have

For every

The sets

and

from Remark

will give us the cluster, and set of exchangeable variables respectively, for the seed of an ind-cluster algebra. Defining the exchange matrix of this seed requires a bit more finesse, and we set this up via a series of lemmata below.

Lemma 2.23

(2)

(3)

Let

\tilde{B}
-exchangeably connected component and let

Remark 2.25

Proof.

To show that a cluster algebra of infinite rank is not compact, consider a seed
\Sigma=(\mathrm{X},\mathrm{ex},B)
with

, and the identity map

Corollary 2.35
.

The ind-objects of
\mathsf{mCl}^{\mathrm{f}}
are precisely the cluster algebras of infinite rank.

Proof.

We have a fully faithful embedding
\mathsf{mCl}^{\mathrm{f}}\to\mathsf{mCl}
, and
\mathsf{mCl}
has filtered colimits by Theorem
2.12
. By Theorem
2.34
the finite rank cluster algebras are the compact objects in
\mathsf{mCl}
. By Theorem
2.11
all objects in
\mathsf{mCl}
are filtered colimits of compact objects. The claim follows by
[
KS06
, Corollary 6.3.5]
.
∎

Motivated by Corollary
2.35
, we will refer to cluster algebras of infinite rank as
ind-cluster algebras
. This fits seamlessly into the classical definition of a cluster algebra, which asserts that the initial cluster of a ‘true’ cluster algebra is to be finite.

3.
The coordinate ring
\mathbb{C}[\mathrm{Gr}]
is an ind-cluster algebra

Recall the definition of
\mathrm{Gr}
as stated in Def
1.1
in the introduction which follows the conventions in
[
SW85
]
and
[
PS85
]
. Our main focus though shall be on its respective coordinate ring as first introduced in Sato’s seminal papers
[
Sat81
]
and
[
Sat83
]
in connection with the solutions of the KP-hierarchy.

Fioresi and Hacon investigate in
[
FH04
]
a ring
\mathbb{C}[\mathrm{Gr}]
, which they argue can be viewed as the ‘coordinate ring’ for the ‘Sato Grassmannian’, in the sense that there is a bijection between closed points of
\mathrm{Proj}(\mathbb{C}[\mathrm{Gr}])
and points of
\mathrm{Gr}
whose coordinates satisfy the union of all Plücker relations of the finite Grassmannians; see
[
FH04
, Proposition 2.10]
. This is the same ring as discussed in
[
Sat83
]
using the Plücker embedding; see also the discussion of the Plücker embedding of
\mathrm{Gr}
in
[
SW85
, §8,§10]
and
[
PS85
, §7.5]
where additional convergence conditions on Plücker coordinates are imposed.

We show in Section
3.1
that the ring
\mathbb{C}[\mathrm{Gr}]
has a natural structure of an ind-cluster algebra, induced by the classical finite rank Grassmannian cluster algebra structures introduced by Scott
[
Sco06
]
. One of the applications of our definition and description of the ind-cluster algebra structure of this coordinate ring is that it identifies among the infinite set of equations making up the KP-hierarchy
natural sets of relations for the
\tau
-function, the solution of the KP-hierarchy, which generate all the remaining ones. The mutations of its clusters provide a natural combinatorial framework to encode the algebraic dependence among the infinite relations of the KP-hierarchy. In fact, the KP-equation itself can be seen as a mutation in the coordinate ring
\mathbb{C}[\mathrm{Gr}]
.

3.1.
The coordinate ring of the Sato-Segal-Wilson Grassmannian

Figure 3.1.
A graphical depiction of the bijection between Maya sequences and Young diagrams: each black go-stone in the left half of the Figure corresponds to a

downward step and a white go-stone to a

degree upward step when drawing the outline of a Young diagram. In the example shown the partition is

We recall the construction of
\mathbb{C}[\mathrm{Gr}]
from

the Grassmannian of
m

\mathbb{C}^{m+n}
, and by
\mathbb{C}[\mathrm{Gr}_{m,n}]
the homogeneous coordinate ring of
\mathrm{Gr}_{m,n}
viewed as a projective variety under the Plücker embedding. The
\mathbb{C}
-algebra

is generated by the Plücker variables

\{d_{j_{1},\ldots,j_{m}}\mid-m\leq j_{1}<\ldots<j_{m}\leq n-1\}\;.

The latter are the minors obtained by taking the rows
-m,\ldots,-1
and columns

in a

matrix which corresponds to a point in
\mathrm{Gr}_{m,n}

m
-tuple
I
from
[-m,n-1]=\{-m,\ldots,n-1\}
by setting for any permutation
\sigma
in the symmetric group
\mathfrak{S}_{m}

d_{i_{\sigma(1)},\ldots,i_{\sigma(m)}}=\begin{cases}\mathrm{sign}(\sigma)d_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}}&\text{if }i_{1}<\ldots<i_{m}\\
0&\text{ else}\end{cases}\;.

In what follows we will sometimes use the interval notation
[a,b]=\{a,a+1,\ldots,b\}
for subsets of consecutive integers to ease the notation. With these conventions in place we can formulate the Plücker relations in the usual manner: For any
(m-1)
-tuple
I=(i_{1},\ldots,i_{m-1})
with
-m\leq i_{1}<\ldots<i_{m-1}\leq n-1
and any
(m+1)
-tuple

with
-m\leq j_{1}<\ldots<j_{m+1}\leq n-1
we have the quadratic relation

(3.1)

\sum_{\ell=1}^{m+1}(-1)^{\ell}d_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{m-1},j_{\ell}}d_{j_{1},\ldots,j_{\ell-1},j_{\ell+1},\ldots,j_{m+1}}=0\;.

Consider the following directed system of
\mathbb{C}
-algebras: for each

and

let

be defined as the algebraic extension of

(3.2)

\displaystyle r_{m,n,m^{\prime},n^{\prime}}\colon d_{j_{1},\ldots,j_{m}}\mapsto d_{-m^{\prime},\ldots,-m-1,j_{1},\ldots,j_{m}}.

In words, we simply identify the Plücker coordinates
d_{j_{1},\ldots,j_{m}}
of the ‘smaller Grassmannian’
\mathrm{Gr}_{m,n}
with Plücker coordinates
d_{-m^{\prime},\ldots,-m-1,j_{1},\ldots j_{m}}
of the larger one by pre-pending the integers in the interval
[-m^{\prime},-m-1]
. Alternatively, we will label below the Plücker coordinates using Young diagrams which fit inside a rectangle of height
m
and width

and the above map simply embeds the same Young diagram into a larger

Definition 3.1

(

The coordinate ring

.

4.
The connection with the KP-hierarchy

In this section, we briefly recall some known facts about the connection of the coordinate ring
\mathbb{C}[\mathrm{Gr}]
with
\tau
-functions, the solutions of the KP hierarchy, in order to make the connection between the latter and the ind-cluster algebra
\mathbb{C}[\mathrm{Gr}]
explicit.

4.1.
Schur functions and
\tau
-functions of the KP-hierarchy

We start by recalling the definition of Schur functions which form an important
\mathbb{Z}
-basis in the ring of symmetric functions
\Lambda
; see
[
Mac95
, Chap. I]
. The latter is the projective limit of the rings of symmetric polynomials
\Lambda_{n}=\mathbb{Z}[x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}]^{S_{n}}
in
n
variables in the category of graded rings. That is, setting
\Lambda_{n}=\bigoplus_{k\geq 0}\Lambda^{k}_{n}
, where
\Lambda^{k}_{n}
is the degree
k
subspace, we consider the projective system
\pi_{mn}:f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})\mapsto f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},0,\ldots,0)
for
m>n
. Then we have that

\Lambda=\bigoplus_{k\geq 0}\Lambda^{k},\qquad\Lambda^{k}=\lim\limits_{\longleftarrow}\Lambda_{n}^{k}

where
\Lambda
is a free
\mathbb{Z}
-module with basis
\{m_{\lambda}~:~\lambda\in\Pi\}
, the monomial symmetric functions, and where
\Pi
is the set of all partitions of integers. Let
\Bbbk
be any unital associative and commutative ring, then we shall adopt the notation
\Lambda_{\Bbbk}=\Lambda\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\Bbbk
.

In particular, if we set
\Bbbk=\mathbb{Q}
and let
p_{r}=m_{(r)}
, the
p_{r}=\sum_{i\geq 1}x_{i}^{r}
are called the Newton power sums, then
\Lambda_{\mathbb{Q}}\cong\mathbb{Q}[p_{1},p_{2},\ldots]
and the
p_{\lambda}=p_{\lambda_{1}}p_{\lambda_{2}}\cdots
with
\lambda\in\Pi
form a
\mathbb{Q}
-basis of
\Lambda_{\mathbb{Q}}
, see e.g.
[
Mac95
, Chap.I]
. Using the Frobenius formula for the irreducible characters
\chi_{\lambda}
of the symmetric group
S_{n}
with
|\lambda|=n
one has

(4.1)

s_{\lambda}(p)=\sum_{\mu\vdash|\lambda|}\frac{\chi^{\lambda}(\mu)}{z_{\mu}}\;p_{\mu_{1}}p_{\mu_{2}}\ldots p_{\mu_{\ell}},\qquad z_{\mu}=\prod_{i>0}i^{m_{i}}m_{i}(\mu)!\,,

where
\chi^{\lambda}(\mu)
is the value of the irreducible character on permutations of cycle type
\mu
and
z_{\mu}
is the cardinality of the centraliser of a permutation of cycle type
\mu
in
S_{n}
.

Remark 4.1
.

We alert the reader to the fact that we have broken standard conventions with large parts of the integrable systems literature: commonly the ‘flow parameters’
t_{r}=p_{r}/r
with
r\geq 1
are used instead in the physics literature with
t_{1}=x
,
t_{2}=y
being identified as the spatial coordinates and
t_{3}=t
as the time parameter. The remaining flow parameters belong to higher integrals of motion; see e.g.
[
MJD00
]
for details.

There is a natural non-degenerate bilinear form
\Lambda\otimes\Lambda\to\mathbb{Z}
, called the Hall inner product, with respect to which the Schur functions form an orthonormal basis,
\langle s_{\lambda},s_{\mu}\rangle=\delta_{\lambda,\mu}
; see e.g
[
Mac95
, Chapter I]
. Denote by
s^{\ast}_{\lambda}
the adjoint of the multiplication operator
s_{\lambda}
with respect to this Hall inner product. Explicitly, we have the following realisation of
s^{*}_{\lambda}
as a differential operator in
\Lambda_{\mathbb{Q}}
,

s^{\ast}_{\lambda}=s_{\lambda}(\partial),\qquad\partial=\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial p_{1}},2\frac{\partial}{\partial p_{2}},3\frac{\partial}{\partial p_{3}},\ldots\right)=\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial t_{1}},\frac{\partial}{\partial t_{2}},\frac{\partial}{\partial t_{3}},\ldots\right)\;.

We are now ready to state the definition of a ‘formal solution’ of the KP-hierarchy. To this end we return to considering the extension of the ring of symmetric functions by an arbitrary unital associative and commutative ring
\Bbbk
,
\Lambda_{\Bbbk}=\Lambda\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\Bbbk
and denote by
\Lambda_{\Bbbk}^{*}
its (algebraic) dual space. Using the obvious extension of the Hall inner product
\Lambda_{\Bbbk}\otimes_{\Bbbk}\Lambda_{\Bbbk}\to\Bbbk
we naturally identify Schur functions with elements in the dual via
s_{\lambda}\mapsto\langle s_{\lambda},\bullet\rangle
. N.B. under this identification the dual
\Lambda_{\Bbbk}^{*}
allows for ‘infinite’ linear combinations in Schur functions, while
\Lambda_{\Bbbk}
only contains finite linear combinations.

Definition 4.2
.

By a
\Bbbk
-valued
\tau
-function we mean an element
\langle\tau,\bullet\rangle
in
\Lambda_{\Bbbk}^{*}
such that the
\langle\tau,s_{\lambda}\rangle\in\Bbbk
satisfy the Plücker relations (
3.7
) for all
m,n\in\mathbb{N}
and
\lambda\in\Pi
, i.e. the map
d_{\lambda}\mapsto\langle\tau,s_{\lambda}\rangle
defines a ring homomorphism
\mathbb{Z}[\mathrm{Gr}]\to\Bbbk
.

We note that in this formal setting we impose no convergence condition on the elements in the set
\{\langle\tau,s_{\lambda}\rangle~:~\lambda\in\Pi\}\subset\Bbbk
. Moreover, each ring homomorphism
f:\mathbb{Z}[\mathrm{Gr}]\to\Bbbk
defines an associated formal
\tau
-function by setting
\langle\tau^{f},s_{\lambda}\rangle=f(d_{\lambda})
for all
\lambda\in\Pi
and as the Schur functions
s_{\lambda}
form a basis of
\Lambda_{\Bbbk}
this fixes
\tau^{f}\in\Lambda^{*}_{\Bbbk}
uniquely.

Example 4.1
.

The simplest examples of
\tau
-functions are the Schur functions: namely set
\tau=s_{\lambda}
then one has
\langle\tau,s_{\mu}\rangle=\delta_{\lambda\mu}
, which trivially solves the Plücker relations (
3.7
) as the latter are only containing quadratic terms
d_{\alpha}d_{\beta}
with
\alpha\neq\beta
. N.B. this solution of the Plücker relations corresponds to the ideal in
\mathbb{Z}[\mathrm{Gr}]
generated by
d_{\lambda}-1
and
d_{\mu}
for
\mu\neq\lambda
.

Employing the Hall inner product the Plücker relations (
3.7
) can be written over the base
\Bbbk=\mathbb{Q}
as an infinite set of nonlinear partial differential equations – which make up the KP-hierarchy – using that,

(4.2)

\langle\tau,s_{\lambda}\rangle=\langle s_{\lambda}^{*}\tau,1\rangle=s^{*}_{\lambda}(\tau)(0)=s_{\lambda}(\partial)\tau|_{p_{1}=p_{2}=\cdots=0}\;.

We now demonstrate on the simplest non-trivial example how to recast the Plücker relations (
3.7
) as differential equations for the
\tau
-function and how these relations arise from the mutation at a vertex in the quiver
Q_{\infty}
of the ind-cluster algebra
\mathbb{Z}[\mathrm{Gr}]
.

Example 4.2
.

Fix
m=2
. Then (
3.7
) reduces to the following three term relation,

-d_{(\ldots,-3,0,1)}d_{(\ldots,-3,-2,-1)}+d_{(\ldots,-3,-1,1)}d_{(\ldots,-3,-2,0)}-d_{(\ldots,-3,-2,1)}d_{(\ldots,-3,-1,0)}=0\;.

This is the only non-trivial Plücker relation coming from
\mathbb{Z}[\mathrm{Gr}_{2,2}]
. Recall that each of the Maya sequences (of charge 0) appearing in the above equation encodes the outline of a Young diagram; see Remark
3.2
. Labelling the coefficients in terms of partitions instead we obtain

(4.3)

-d_{(2,2)}d_{\varnothing}+d_{(2,1)}d_{(1)}-d_{(1,1)}d_{(2)}=0\;.

We recognise in this labelling that this relation is obtained by mutating at the vertex labelled by a single box in Figure
3.7
. The resulting Postnikov diagram and quiver is shown in Figure
3.8
. In terms of Schur functions this Plücker relation translates into

(4.4)

\langle\tau,1\rangle\langle\tau,s_{(2,2)}\rangle-\langle\tau,s_{(2,1)}\rangle\langle\tau,s_{(1)}\rangle+\langle\tau,s_{(1,1)}\rangle\langle\tau,s_{(2)}\rangle=0,

which is the KP-equation. The latter can be expressed in its Hirota bilinear form by expanding each Schur function into power sums using (
4.2
); see e.g.
[
MJD00
, Example 10.1]
. More generally, using the relation (
3.10
) from Example
3.3
, we obtain (using Frobenius notation for the partitions) the following set of 3-term relations for solutions
\tau
of the KP-hierarchy,

(4.5)

\langle\tau,1\rangle\langle\tau,s_{(a,a-1|b,b-1)}\rangle-\langle\tau,s_{(a|b)}\rangle\langle\tau,s_{(a-1|b-1)}\rangle+\langle\tau,s_{(a-1|b)}\rangle\langle\tau,s_{(a|b-1)}\rangle=0,

which for
a=b=1
reduces to the KP-equation above.

4.2.
The Sato-Segal-Wilson Grassmannian and
\tau
-functions

We shall now specialise to the case
\Bbbk=\mathbb{C}
and consider the ring
\mathbb{C}[\mathrm{Gr}]
. Recall Definition
1.1
of the Grassmannian
\mathrm{Gr}(H)
and of its connected component
\mathrm{Gr}
of virtual dimension zero. For technical details we refer the reader to
[
SW85
, Section 8]
and
[
PS85
, Chapter 7]
.

We recall that any subspace
W\in\mathrm{Gr}
contains a sequence
\{w_{i}\}_{i\geq 1}
called an
admissible basis
in
[
SW85
, §3]
and
[
PS85
, Def. 7.5.1]
such that

•

the associated map
w:H_{\varnothing}\to H
which maps
z^{-i+1}\mapsto w_{i}
for all
i\geq 1
has image
W
and is continuous as well as injective;

•

the matrix transforming
\{\mathrm{pr}(w_{i})\}
into
\{z^{i+1}\}
differs from the identity by an operator who has a determinant (i.e. it differs from the identity by an operator of trace class).

In particular, one can choose for each
W\in\mathrm{Gr}
a particular admissible basis making use of the following known stratification of
\mathrm{Gr}(H)
in terms of Maya diagrams
[
SW85
, §2,§3]
and
[
PS85
, §7.3]
: for each
W\in\mathrm{Gr}
there exists a unique (minimal) Maya diagram
a_{\bullet}
of virtual cardinality zero (or equivalently a partition
\lambda
) such that the orthogonal projection
W\to H_{a_{\bullet}}
is an isomorphism. Here
H_{a_{\bullet}}
is the subspace spanned by
\{z^{a_{i}+1}\}_{i\geq 1}
. That is,
W
contains elements
w_{i}
of the form

w_{i}=z^{a_{i}+1}+\sum_{n>i}w_{n,i}z^{n+1},\qquad w_{n,i}\in\mathbb{C},

which span the dense subspace
W^{\rm fin}\subset W
of finite order elements in
W
. For a given fixed Maya diagram
a_{\bullet}
(or, equivalently, a partition
\lambda
) the set of all subspaces
W\in\mathrm{Gr}
obeying the latter property form a submanifold, the stratum
\mathrm{Gr}_{\lambda}
. In particular,
W=H_{\varnothing}\in\mathrm{Gr}_{\varnothing}
which explains our notation.

Example 4.3
.

The last example generalises to all partitions
\lambda
: let
a_{\bullet}=a_{\bullet}(\lambda)
be the unique Maya sequence of virtual cardinality zero corresponding to
\lambda
; c.f. Remark
3.2
. Then the subspace
H_{\lambda}=H_{a_{\bullet}}
with admissible basis
\{z^{a_{i}+1}\}_{i\geq 1}
lies in
\mathrm{Gr}_{\lambda}
. The subspaces
H_{\lambda}
correspond to the simplest solutions of the KP-hierarchy
\tau=s_{\lambda}
discussed above.

Using the concept of admissible bases, one can assign to each
W\in\mathrm{Gr}
and partition
\lambda
(or equivalently Maya diagram of charge 0) the following ‘infinite determinant’ called ‘the Plücker coordinates of
W
’ in
[
SW85
, §8]
,

(4.6)

\Delta_{\lambda}(W):=\det(w_{\lambda_{i}-i,j})_{i,j\geq 1}\in\mathbb{C}\;.

In fact, using the admissible basis above for
W\in\mathrm{Gr}_{\lambda}
, one can show that
\Delta_{\lambda}(W)
reduces always to a finite determinant and is therefore well-defined; see
[
SW85
, §8]
. Moreover, analogous to the finite dimensional case the Plücker coordinates (
4.6
) define a projective embedding; c.f.
[
SW85
, §10]
and
[
PS85
, §7.5]
.

The following summarises the key result of the connection between the infinite dimensional Grassmannian
\mathrm{Gr}
and the ring
\mathbb{C}[\mathrm{Gr}]
; we refer the reader to
[
Sat83
]
and
[
SW85
]
for details.

Theorem 4.3

(Sato, Segal-Wilson, Pressley-Segal)
.

For each
W\in\mathrm{Gr}
the collection
\{\Delta_{\lambda}(W)\}_{\lambda\in\Pi}\subset\mathbb{C}
satisfies the Plücker relations (
3.7
) and is square-summable, i.e.
\sum_{\lambda\in\Pi}|\Delta_{\lambda}(W)|^{2}<\infty
.

The last theorem defines a holomorphic embedding
\mathrm{Gr}\to\mathrm{Proj}(\ell^{2}(\Pi))
, where
\ell^{2}(\Pi)
denotes the Hilbert space of square summable sequences labelled by partitions
\lambda\in\Pi
; see
[
SW85
, Chapter 7, Prop. 7.5.2]
. This map has been called the Plücker embedding in
loc. cit.
and should be thought of as a generalisation of the Plücker embedding from the finite to the infinite case.

Remark 4.4
.

Recall the presentations from
[
FH04
, Prop.2.8 (ii) and Prop 2.10 (ii)]
of the coordinate ring
\mathbb{C}[\mathrm{Gr}]
. In
loc. cit.
the maximal ideals of
\mathbb{C}[\mathrm{Gr}]
are identified as points on Sato’s ‘universal Grassmann manifold’
\widetilde{UGM}
; see
[
FH04
, Remark 2.11]
. In this case no convergence conditions are imposed on the Plücker coordinates.

Example 4.4
.

The simplest subspaces
H_{\lambda}\in\mathrm{Gr}
which contain the admissible basis
\{z^{\lambda_{i}-i}\}_{i\geq 1}
have Plücker coordinates
\Delta_{\mu}(H_{\lambda})=\delta_{\lambda\mu}
. They trivially solve the Plücker relations (
3.7
). More generally, we have for
W\in\mathrm{Gr}_{\mu}
that
\Delta_{\mu}(W)\neq 0
and
\Delta_{\lambda}(W)=0
when
\lambda\subset\mu
; see e.g.
[
PS85
, Prop.7.5.4]
. Thus, in general there will be infinitely many nonzero Plücker coordinates for a generic point
W\in\mathrm{Gr}
.

Instead of working with the (algebraic) dual space
\Lambda^{*}_{\mathbb{C}}
, as in the previous section, we now consider the Hilbert space completion
\overline{\Lambda}_{\mathbb{C}}
of
\Lambda_{\mathbb{C}}=\Lambda\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{C}
with respect to the Hall inner product
\Lambda_{\mathbb{C}}\otimes_{\mathbb{C}}\Lambda_{\mathbb{C}}\to\mathbb{C}
which we assume to be anti-linear in the first factor. The Hilbert space completion allows for infinite linear combinations of Schur functions but it also imposes a convergence condition on the Plücker coordinates
\langle\tau,s_{\lambda}\rangle
for any
\tau\in\overline{\Lambda}_{\mathbb{C}}
: namely, they must be square summable and this condition then matches with the convergence condition imposed on the Plücker coordinates
\Delta_{\lambda}(W)
of a point
W
on the Sato-Segal-Wilson Grassmannian. The following definition of the Sato-Segal-Wilson
\tau
-function, a solution of the KP-hierarchy, is Proposition 8.3 in
[
SW85
]
(see also
[
MJD00
]
and references therein)
3
3

3

We note that in
[
SW85
, Section 3]
the
\tau
-function is defined in terms of sections of the dual determinant bundle instead and using explicit determinant formulae. Here we have used
[
SW85
, Prop. 8.3]
for the definition instead, the expansion of a
\tau
-function into Schur functions, in order to make the connection with the Plücker coordinates explicit.
.

Definition 4.5
.

Let
W\in\mathrm{Gr}
. Then the associated
\tau
-function
\tau^{W}\in\overline{\Lambda}_{\mathbb{C}}
is the series

(4.7)

\tau^{W}(p)=\sum_{\lambda\in\Pi}\Delta_{\lambda}(W)s_{\lambda}(p),

where the Plücker coordinates
\Delta_{\lambda}(W)\in\mathbb{C}
of
W
are the ones defined in (
4.6
) and the
s_{\lambda}(p)
are the Schur functions (
4.1
).

This definition allows us to recall Sato’s original observation
[
Sat81
,
Sat83
]
of the relation between solutions of the KP hierarchy and infinite dimensional Grassmannians in the setting of the Sato-Segal-Wilson Grassmannian.

Theorem 4.6

(Sato
[
Sat81
,
Sat83
]
)
.

Any element
\tau\in\overline{\Lambda}_{\mathbb{C}}
which satisfies the KP-hierarchy, i.e. the coefficients
\langle\tau,s_{\lambda}\rangle
satisfy the Plücker relations (
3.7
), corresponds to a point of the Sato-Segal-Wilson Grassmannian
\mathrm{Gr}
. That is, there exists
W\in\mathrm{Gr}
such that
\langle s^{*}_{\lambda}(\tau),1\rangle=\Delta_{\lambda}(W)
.

As another application of the ind-cluster algebra structure we are now considering positivity questions for the Sato-Segal-Wilson Grassmannian. Some of our motivation comes from combinatorics as well as the close connection between
\mathrm{Gr}
and the representation theory of loop groups; see
[
PS85
]
.

The following closely resembles the definition of the
totally positive Grassmannian
in the finite case; see e.g.
[
FWZ24
, Def. 1.2.1]
.

Definition 4.7
.

Define the totally positive Sato-Segal-Wilson Grassmannian
\mathrm{Gr}^{+}
to be the set of all points
W\in\mathrm{Gr}
such that
\Delta_{\lambda}(W)>0
for all
\lambda\in\Pi
.

Recall that in algebraic combinatorics an important concept is that of Schur positivity: an element
f\in\Lambda
in the ring of symmetric functions is called
Schur positive
if it has an expansion into Schur functions where each non-zero coefficient is positive, i.e.
\langle f,s_{\lambda}\rangle\geq 0
. We extend this notion to the Hilbert space completion
\overline{\Lambda}_{\mathbb{C}}
in the obvious manner.

Corollary 4.8
.

Let
\tau=\tau^{W}
be a solution of the KP-hierarchy with
W\in\mathrm{Gr}_{\varnothing}
. Then
\tau^{W}
is totally Schur positive, i.e. the Plücker coordinates
\Delta_{\lambda}(W)=\langle s_{\lambda}^{\ast}(\tau^{W}),1\rangle
in the expansion (
4.7
) are all positive, if and only if
\Delta_{\mu}(W)>0
for any rectangular partition
\mu
. In particular, in this case we have that
W\in\mathrm{Gr}^{+}
.

Remark 4.9
.

The previously known explicit descriptions of the Plücker coordinates
\Delta_{\lambda}(W)
in the literature are in determinant form such as (
3.8
); see e.g.
[
HÈ11
, Cor.2.1]
and also
[
NOS17
, Thm 1.1]
for Plücker coordinates belonging to points
W\in\mathrm{Gr}_{\lambda}
in strata with
\lambda\neq\varnothing
. These determinant formulae are derived using the Plücker embedding and Wick’s Theorem for free fermions and are therefore not manifestly positive. They also do not allow one to address the question of algebraic independence of relations; c.f. Cor
3.5
.

The notion of positivity for
\tau
-functions considered here is different from the one considered in
[
kodam2011kp
,
KW14
]
for (a certain class of) multi-soliton solutions of the KP-equation (
4.4
), which corresponds to the simplest Plücker relation (
4.3
). In the latter work solutions describing multi-solitons are described in terms of monomials of exponential functions in the flow parameters
t_{1}=p_{1},t_{2}=p_{2}/2,t_{3}=p_{3}/3
with positive coefficients (that come from the Plücker coordinates of finite Grassmannians). There also exist more general multi-soliton solutions which satisfy the entire KP-hierarchy (see e.g.
[
MJD00
]
and
[
KRR13
]
), i.e. all of the Plücker relations (
3.7
) and correspond to the
\tau
-functions considered here, but in general it is not expected that the expansions of these more general multi-soliton solutions (or the special class considered in
[
kodamakodama2011kp
]
) into Schur functions gives
positive
or
non-negative
Plücker coordinates
\Delta_{\lambda}(W)
for the corresponding point
W
on the Sato-Segal-Wilson Grassmannian. To clarify this point, we briefly recall the multi-soliton solutions discussed in
[
KW11
,
KW14
]
.

4.3.
Multi-Soliton Solutions and Finite Grassmannians

There is a class of special multi-soliton solutions of the KP-equation, which are obtained from points on
finite
Grassmannians
\mathrm{Gr}_{m,n}
; see e.g.
[
Kod17
]
. This particular subset of solutions and their connection with the cluster algebra structure of the (finite) coordinate rings
\mathbb{C}[\mathrm{Gr}_{m,n}]

[
Sco06
]
has been the subject of study in the works by Kodama and Williams
[
KW11
,
KW14
]
. In order to avoid confusion and highlight the difference between the finite Plücker coordinates entering these special multi-soliton solutions as combinatorial data and their Plücker coordinates when viewed as points on the Sato-Segal-Wilson Grassmannian we briefly comment on these solutions; for details we refer to
[
KW11
,
KW14
]
and
[
Kod17
]
as well as references therein.

Fix some integer
N\geq 2
, some free parameters
\zeta=(\zeta_{1},\ldots,\zeta_{N})
and a subspace
V\in\mathrm{Gr}_{m,n}
with
m+n=N
. Then the special
N
-soliton solution associated to these data in
[
KW11
]
is given by

(4.8)

\tau^{V,\zeta}(x,y,t)=\sum_{I\subset[N]}c_{I}(\zeta)\prod_{i\in I}e^{\zeta_{i}x+\zeta_{i}^{2}y+\zeta_{i}^{3}t},\quad c_{I}(\zeta)=\Delta^{(m,n)}_{I}(V)\prod_{i,j\in I}(\zeta_{i}-\zeta_{j})\,,

where the sum runs over all
m
-subsets
I
of
[N]=\{1,\ldots,N\}
and
\Delta^{(m,n)}_{I}(V)
are the Plücker coordinates of
V\in\mathrm{Gr}_{m,n}
. For generic choice of parameters it is not clear that these are solutions of the higher Plücker relations (
3.7
) other than (
4.3
) and, thus, in general will not correspond to points on the Sato-Segal-Wilson Grassmannian (despite this we have labelled these solutions with the letter
\tau
although they fall in general outside of our definition of a ‘
\tau
-function’). However, general multi-soliton solutions which satisfy all of the relations (
3.7
) are known, see e.g.
[
MJD00
]
and
also
[
Nak18
, Thm 2]
for an extension of the soliton solutions from
[
KW11
]
to the Sato Grassmannian as well as references therein. In order to obtain the Plücker coordinates of these multi-soliton
\tau
-functions when viewed as points
W\in\mathrm{Gr}
on the Sato-Segal-Wilson Grassmannian, they need first to be rewritten in terms of Schur functions according to (
4.7
). This rewriting can be achieved by using the Cauchy identity (see e.g.
[
KX21
, Sec. 2.2]
for such a discussion in the case of certain soliton solutions).

From the expansion (
4.7
) one sees that in general, i.e. for generic points
W\in\mathrm{Gr}
, one has infinitely many non-zero Pücker coordinates
\Delta_{\lambda}(W)
. The Plücker relations of the latter are described by the ind-cluster algebra
\mathbb{C}[\mathrm{Gr}]
, while the finite number of relations satisfied by
\Delta^{(m,n)}_{I}(V)
in the special solutions (
4.8
) of the KP-equation are described by the finite rank cluster algebra
\mathbb{C}[\mathrm{Gr}_{m,n}]
.
