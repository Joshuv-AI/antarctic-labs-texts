EXO/hydrogel system for sequential regulation of endogenous hyaline cartilage regeneration
Authors: Kang Wu, Rong Gong, Tianyu Gao, Yong Hu, Hongrui Wang, Weiye Zhang, Xiaoxu Wang, Yifan Wang, Ning Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractArticular cartilage repair remains a clinically significant challenge due to its limited regenerative capacity and vulnerability to inflammation-induced damage and fibrocartilage formation. Current therapeutic strategies frequently fail to enhance intrinsic repair capacity or overcome the dual barriers of injury/inflammation-driven fibrosis and insufficient hyaline cartilage regeneration, often yielding suboptimal outcomes. Here, we developed an exosome-integrated hydrogel system for temporally programmed drug delivery. Kartogenin (KGN) and halofuginone (HF) were encapsulated into human umbilical cord MSC-derived exosomes (EXOKGN/EXOHF). EXOHF was specifically immobilized in GelMA hydrogel via CP05-CD63 binding, while EXOKGN was incorporated unbound. This design achieved rapid release of EXOKGN versus sustained retention of EXOHF for sequential therapeutic action. The rapidly released EXOKGN initiated bone marrow-derived mesenchymal stem cell (BMSC) migration and chondrogenic differentiation in the repair process. Concurrently, the sustained release of EXOHF demonstrated anti-inflammatory and anti-fibrotic effects during later regenerative phases. This sequential release profile—first promoting BMSC recruitment and chondrogenesis, then suppressing inflammation and fibrocartilage formation—synergistically enhanced hyaline cartilage repair. Importantly, we identified that HF dynamically modulates inflammatory chemokines through the cGAS-STING pathway, maintaining a pro-regenerative microenvironment. In conclusion, our exosomal dual-drug hydrogel with sequential release successfully coordinated chondrogenesis promotion and inflammation/fibrosis suppression, significantly enhancing hyaline cartilage regeneration. Also revealed was HF's cGAS-STING immunomodulatory mechanism, demonstrating strong potential for clinical translation.Keywords: Hyaline cartilage regeneration, Temporally programmed drug delivery, Halofuginone, Kartogenin, cGAS-STING pathway

Introduction

Articular cartilage primarily consists of hyaline cartilage that is composed of water, proteoglycans, and type II collagen. It has a limited chondrocyte population (about 2 %), is avascular and aneural, and lacks lymphatic tissue, resulting in very poor intrinsic healing capacity [1,2]. The etiology of cartilage injury is complex, involving trauma, abnormal mechanical stress, and inflammation [3]. Injury typically involves localized tissue degeneration, progressive deterioration, subchondral bone damage, and can lead to osteoarthritis (OA) [4,5]. Current clinical treatments, such as microfracture and autologous or allogeneic osteochondral transplantation, have achieved some success. However, despite clinical successes with contemporary surgical approaches, persistent challenges still effect long-term outcomes [6,7]. First, cartilage lesions undergo serious depletion of functional chondrocytes, with ≥85 % cell loss observed in traumatic defects [8]. Concurrently, the hypoxic microenvironment redirects reparative BMSCs toward aberrant osteogenic differentiation via HIF-1α overactivation instead of essential chondrogenesis [9]. While interventions like microfracture recruit BMSCs, their failure to undergo chondrogenesis means cartilage-lineage treatment must be actively promoted—potentially through targeted pharmacological modulation.

Second, even when repair occurs, fibrocartilage predominates over functional hyaline cartilage [10]. This inferior tissue—rich in type I collagen (COL1)—exhibits compromised biomechanics including reduced elasticity, compressive modulus, and reduced durability, ultimately accelerating joint degeneration [11]. Critically, emerging evidence reveals that fibrocartilage formation is driven by injury-induced inflammatory progression: elevated Interleukin 1 beta (IL-β) and Matrix metallopeptidase 13 (Mmp13), concentrations at the lesion site create a catabolic microenvironment that suppresses chondrogenic markers while stimulating chondrocyte dedifferentiation and hypertrophy [9,12,13]. Therefore, successful regeneration requires dual-pathway interventions that simultaneously promote chondrogenesis in the early stage and inhibits inflammatory mediators and prevents fibrosis in the later stage.

Kartogenin (KGN) is a small molecule promoting chondrogenic differentiation of MSCs, with applications in cartilage regeneration, tendon-bone healing, wound healing, and limb development [14]. It significantly enhances the chondrogenesis of BMSCs reaching the injury site. Halofuginone (HF) specifically inhibits COL1 synthesis and has strong potential for cartilage repair, shown to slow OA progression by inhibiting TGF-β and IL-1β activity [15]. Its approval by the US FDA for other clinical indications enhances its safety profile [16]. We hypothesize that a KGN and HF temporally staged release will overcome: (1) inadequate chondrogenic priming of reparative cells during early regeneration, and (2) progressive fibrocartilaginous metaplasia impairing functional hyaline matrix deposition during the later stage. To overcome the poor aqueous solubility of both KGN and HF, they were encapsulated in EXO, termed as EXOKGN and EXOHF. EXO are bioactive nanovesicles with a phospholipid bilayer capable of traversing cell membranes, offering excellent encapsulation and protection for hydrophobic drugs, significantly boosting delivery efficiency [17,18]. Compared to other drug carriers like liposomes and nanoparticles, EXO are relatively simple to obtain and often require less engineering [[19], [20], [21]].

Gelatin Methacryloyl (GelMA) served as the delivery vehicle for EXOKGN and EXOHF. As an engineered photocrosslinkable hydrogel derived from gelatin, GelMA is widely employed in tissue regeneration owing to its retention of gelatin's low immunogenicity, combined with injectability, structural plasticity, and biocompatibility [[22], [23], [24]]; it's particularly suitable for repairing irregular cartilage defects [25,26]. To achieve sequential KGN and HF release, CP05 peptide is employed, which specifically recognizes and binds the CD63 surface protein on EXO [27]. CP05 was covalently conjugated to the GelMA matrix to establish a three-dimensional polymer network scaffold. EXOHF was specifically immobilized in GelMA hydrogel via CP05-CD63 binding, while EXOKGN was incorporated unbound. This design achieved rapid release of EXOKGN and sustained retention of EXOHF for sequential therapeutic action. The focus is to achieve temporal-phase-specific effects through differential release kinetics: rapid EXOKGN release drives BMSC recruitment and chondrogenic differentiation during the early phase, while sustained EXOHF release facilitates cartilage remodeling in the later phase by modulating chronic inflammation and aberrant fibrosis [15].

Materials and methods

Preparation and characterization of hUCMSCs-EXO

Human umbilical cord mesenchymal stem cells (hUCMSCs) were cultured in serum-free medium. The conditioned medium was harvested after 48 h. EXO were isolated by sequential centrifugation: at 300×g for 10 min to remove cells, followed by centrifugation at 2000×g for 20 min to remove debris, and finally ultracentrifugation at 100,000×g for 70 min at 4 °C to pellet EXO. EXO morphology was examined using transmission electron microscopy (TEM). Particle size distribution and concentration were determined by nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA).

To evaluate cellular uptake and intracellular localization, purified drug-loaded exosomes (EXOHF/EXOKGN) were labeled with the red fluorescent cell membrane probe DiI. Unbound DiI was removed via ultracentrifugation (100,000×g, 70 min) to ensure labeling specificity. The labeled DiI-EXO were co-incubated with BMSCs or RCs under light-protected conditions for 24 h. After incubation, cells were gently washed three times with PBS to remove non-internalized EXO, fixed with 4 % paraformaldehyde for 15 min at room temperature, and permeabilized with 0.1 % Triton X-100 for no more than 5 min to prevent leakage of internalized exosomes. F-actin was stained using Actin-Tracker Green-488, and nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. All procedures were performed under light-protected conditions.

Image acquisition was carried out using an Olympus FV3000 confocal microscope, with fluorescence signals collected through separate channels for red (DiI-EXO), green (F-actin), and blue (DAPI). Colocalization analysis was performed using ImageJ software to qualitatively assess the intracellular distribution of EXO. A straight line spanning the cytoplasmic and nuclear regions was drawn on merged three-channel images, with the direction indicated from point “1” to point “2”. Fluorescence intensity profiles along this path for each channel were extracted using the “Plot Profile” function.

Preparation and characterization of drug-loaded exosomes

We developed a dual-drug loading strategy by encapsulating HF and KGN separately into exosomes derived from hUCMSC-EXO. This approach was designed to overcome the poor aqueous solubility of both drugs and enhance their delivery efficiency, aligning with our proposed research hypothesis that temporally controlled release can synergistically promote chondrogenesis and inhibit fibrosis.

We employed an optimized electroporation protocol to load HF and KGN into the EXO. Briefly, purified hUCMSC-EXO were mixed with HF and KGN at a 5:1 mass ratio in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). A 100 μL aliquot of this mixture was transferred into a pre-chilled 4 mm electroporation cuvette and subjected to electroporation under the following parameters: 300 V, 250 μF, 4 pulses (10 s per pulse with 5-s intervals). These conditions were optimized to achieve maximum drug encapsulation while preserving EXO integrity. After electroporation, the sample was incubated at 37 °C for 30 min to facilitate membrane recovery, followed by overnight storage at 4 °C to stabilize the drug-loaded EXO. To remove unencapsulated drugs, the sample underwent three cycles of ultrafiltration using a 100 kDa molecular weight cut-off membrane, with PBS serving as the washing buffer. The resulting drug-loaded EXO were resuspended in PBS for subsequent experiments [[28], [29], [30], [31], [32]].

We conducted systematic characterization to verify successful drug loading and the preservation of EXO properties. Morphological assessment was performed using transmission electron microscopy (TEM), while nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) was employed to determine particle size distribution and concentration. The concentration of free drugs in the ultrafiltrate was determined using liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS) and uv spectrophotometer (UV–Vis) spectroscopy to quantitatively evaluate drug loading efficiency, encapsulation efficiency, and drug loading capacity.

Preparation and characterization of GelMA-CP05-EXOHF + EXOKGN composite hydrogels

The CP05 peptide, with the amino acid sequence CAHSGMTVTSAL and a molecular weight of 1515.8 Da, was utilized due to its specific affinity for the exosomal surface marker CD63. The CP05 peptide modified with Allyl-L-glycine (hereinafter referred to as CP05) was custom-synthesized by China Peptides Co., Ltd. (Hangzhou, China) using standard solid-phase Fmoc chemistry, followed by purification via high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) to a purity of ≥95 %. Under 405 nm photoinitiation, the allyl group of the CP05 peptide can covalently couple with the methacryloyl groups of GelMA. Additionally, a separate batch of CP05 conjugated with FITC was synthesized for fluorescence-based detection and distribution analysis. Based on prior experiments, an optimal volume ratio of 5:1 (GelMA:CP05) was determined to ensure sufficient incorporation and distribution of the peptide within the hydrogel network without compromising its crosslinking kinetics or structural integrity (Fig. S1A, B and C).

GelMA powder was dissolved (10 % w/v) in a (0.25 % w/v) photoinitiator solution by vortex mixing followed by incubation at 65 °C in a light-protected water bath for 25 min with intermittent mixing to achieve complete dissolution. CP05 peptide solution and the GelMA solution were then added at a 5:1 vol ratio, homogenized, and centrifuged (500×g, 5 min) to remove air bubbles. The precursor solution was sterile filtered (0.22 μm pore size). Drug-loaded exosomes were sequentially incorporated into the chilled (4 °C) precursor solution—first by adding EXOHF and incubating for 10 min, followed by the addition of EXOKGN before photopolymerization. GelMA-CP05-EXOHF + EXOKGN (Gel-C-EH + EK) composite hydrogels were then formed via photo-crosslinking (405 nm, 5 min). Four hydrogel formulations were ultimately prepared and characterized: (1) GelMA (Gel), (2) GelMA + EXO (Gel + E), (3) GelMA/CP05 + EXO (Gel/C + E), and (4) GelMA-CP05 + EXO (Gel-C + E). The precursor solutions were placed in 1 cm × 1 cm × 1 cm cubic molds for curing and demolding to observe gelation behavior and macroscopic appearance. The microstructure of each lyophilized sample was examined using scanning electron microscopy (SEM).

All EXO used in the aforementioned groups were maintained in a drug-free (unloaded) state. To evaluate whether drug loading in EXO (EXOHF/EXOKGN) would influence hydrogel properties, the morphology and size of EXO were re-characterized after drug loading. Additionally, a hydrogel formulation—GelMA-CP05 + drug-loaded EXO (EXOHF/EXOKGN), designated as Gel-C + EH/K—was prepared. The microstructure of this group was directly examined using SEM, and its pore size was statistically analyzed and compared with that of the unloaded EXO group (Gel-C + E) for validation.

To evaluate the effect of successful CP05 conjugation on EXO enrichment capacity, a comparative assessment was conducted between the GelMA/CP05 physical mixture group (Gel/C + E) and the GelMA-CP05 covalent conjugation group (Gel-C + E). EXO were stained with DiI, and CP05 was labeled with FITC. A fixed volume (1 μL) of each precursor solution was formed into microdroplets, cured, and incubated with EXO suspensions. After washing, fluorescence microscopy was used to visualize and quantitatively analyze differences in EXO enrichment efficiency.

For mechanical testing, hydrogel samples shaped into 1 cm × 1 cm × 1 cm cubes were tested in a dried state under unconfined compression at a constant speed of 1 mm/min (n ≥ 4 per group). The measured force was normalized to the original cross-sectional area to obtain stress (σ), while strain (ε) was calculated as the change in height divided by the original height. The compressive modulus was then determined from the slope of the initial linear region of the resulting stress–strain curves.

To further evaluate the physicochemical properties of the hydrogel, we systematically characterized its swelling behavior, degradation kinetics, and drug release profiles. These analyses were conducted to examine whether the hydrogel system could achieve controlled drug release while maintaining structural stability, thereby validating its potential as a sustained therapeutic delivery platform. The equilibrium swelling capacity and degradation behavior were assessed to evaluate the structural integrity and responsiveness of the hydrogel under physiological conditions. The equilibrium swelling ratio (%) was calculated as [(Ws − Wd)/Wd × 100 %], where Ws represents the swollen mass (after 24 h of incubation in phosphate-buffered saline PBS at 37 °C) and Wd denotes the initial dry mass. This parameter was used to evaluate the water absorption capacity of the hydrogel, which directly influences its mechanical properties and drug diffusion pathways. In vitro degradation kinetics were monitored through mass loss analysis using the formula [(W0 − Wt)/W0 × 100 %], where W0 refers to the hydrated mass after equilibrium swelling, and Wt is the residual mass at time point t (n ≥ 3 per group; time points: days 0, 3, 7, 14, 21, 30; under conditions of PBS, 37 °C, and gentle shaking). This assessment is critical for predicting in vivo durability and ensuring sustained drug release without premature degradation.

To verify the ability of the hydrogel to control drug release in a biomimetic environment, the drug release profiles of HF and KGN from the Gel-C-EH + EK hydrogel were evaluated (n = 3). The hydrogels were placed in dialysis tubes (molecular weight cutoff: 10 kDa) immersed in PBS (37 °C, 100 rpm). This dialysis setup simulated physiological diffusion conditions, allowing drug release while retaining the hydrogel matrix. The release medium was sampled and replaced with fresh PBS at intervals from 0.5 to 30 days to maintain sink conditions. The concentrations of released HF and KGN were quantified using UV–Vis spectrophotometry at 230 nm and 340 nm, respectively, based on standard calibration curves. These wavelengths were selected according to the characteristic absorption peaks of HF and KGN, enabling accurate quantification of their release kinetics.

Isolation and culture of bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells and chondrocytes

BMSCs (STCC5011P, Servicebio) were cultured in α-MEM medium (Gibco) supplemented with 10 % fetal bovine serum (FBS, Procell) and 1 % penicillin–streptomycin at 37 °C under 5 % CO2. Cells were passaged at 70–80 % confluency, with the medium refreshed every 3 days. BMSCs at passages 3–4 were used for subsequent experiments.

To evaluate chondrogenic differentiation under different conditions, the cells were divided into the following groups: Control group (Ctrl): cultured in α-MEM basal medium; Standard chondrogenic induction group (CM): induced with chondrogenic differentiation medium (G417, Servicebio); Hydrogel group (Gel-C-EH + EK): cultured in α-MEM basal medium supplemented with the Gel-C-EH + EK hydrogel system. After 21 days of induction, chondrogenic differentiation was assessed by Alcian blue staining and RT-qPCR analysis of key chondrogenic markers (SOX9, COL2, and ACAN). The Gel-C-EH + EK hydrogel demonstrated optimal chondrogenic differentiation efficacy (Fig. S3A and B)

To better simulate the in vivo chondrogenic microenvironment and ensure a consistent inductive background across all experimental groups, all subsequent groups—including Ctrl, Gel-C-E, Gel-C-EH, and Gel-C-EH + EK groups—were cultured in the standard chondrogenic induction medium (G417, Servicebio).

For pellet-based chondrogenic differentiation, BMSCs (4 × 105 cells per tube) were pelleted by centrifugation (150×g, 5 min) in 15 mL conical tubes. The supernatant was replaced with a 0.5 mL of chondrogenic medium. The tubes were centrifuged again (150×g, 5 min), and the caps were loosened to facilitate gas exchange. The pellets were then cultured upright at 37 °C under 5 % CO2. After 24 h of stabilization, the pellets were gently dislodged at 48 h and maintained in culture for 21 days, with medium changes every 2–3 days. Finally, cryosectioned pellets were validated by H&E, Alcian blue, and toluidine blue staining.

Knee articular cartilage was harvested from 7-day-old Sprague-Dawley(SD)rats, minced, and digested sequentially with 0.5 % trypsin-EDTA for 2 h and 1 % type II collagenase (Solarbio) overnight at 37 °C. The resulting cell suspension was filtered through a 200 μm strainer and centrifuged (400×g, 10 min) to pellet the cells. The cells were then cultured in DMEM/F12 medium supplemented with 10 % fetal bovine serum and 1 % penicillin/streptomycin at 37 °C under 5 % CO2. Chondrocyte identity was confirmed by Western blot detection of COL2 and SOX9 expression.

To evaluate the maintenance of chondrocyte phenotype under both normal and inflammatory conditions, the cells were divided into the following experimental groups: Ctrl, Gel-C-E, Gel-C-EH and Gel-C-EH + EK groups. Cells in each group were cultured under two conditions: Standard culture condition: maintained in complete DMEM/F12 medium; Inflammatory stimulation condition: treated with either LPS (100 μg/mL) or IL-1β (50 ng/mL) to simulate a damaged microenvironment.

After 48 h of treatment, proteins and RNA were extracted from each group. The expression levels of chondrogenic markers (ACAN, COL2, SOX9) and fibrotic markers (COL1) were analyzed by Western blot and RT-qPCR.

Under IL-1β stimulation, immunofluorescence (IF) staining was performed to assess the expression of COL1 and COL2 in each group. To further evaluate intracellular oxidative stress levels, chondrocytes were pre-treated with LPS for 24 h to simulate injury, followed by incubation with the DCFH-DA fluorescent probe. Reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels were quantitatively analyzed across experimental groups using fluorescence microscopy and flow cytometry.

Biocompatibility assessment

To evaluate the indirect effects of hydrogels on cell viability and proliferation. The viability of BMSCs and RCs exposed to Gel-C-EH + EK hydrogel and other groups was detected using a CCK-8 assay (Beyotime). Cells were seeded in 96-well plates (5 × 103 cells/well) and cultured with material extracts (prepared in DMEM) for 1, 4, and 7 days. Control groups received standard DMEM medium. After incubation, 10 μL CCK-8 reagent was added per well and incubated for 2 h (37 °C, 5 % CO2). Absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader. BMSCs and RCs were cultured in the lower chamber of Transwell plates (Corning). Test hydrogels (100 μL) were polymerized in upper chambers and co-cultured for 72 h. Live/dead staining was performed using Calcein-AM (green) and propidium iodide (red) for 20 min, followed by fluorescence microscopy.

To evaluate the direct impact of hydrogels on cell–matrix interactions, BMSCs and RCs were seeded onto the surfaces of the respective experimental hydrogel formulations, with control groups cultured on standard tissue culture dishes. All groups were maintained under identical culture conditions using the same medium. On days 1 and 3 post-seeding, cells from both the Gel-C-EH + EK hydrogel groups and control groups were fixed with 4 % paraformaldehyde for 10 min, permeabilized with 0.1 % Triton X-100 for 15 min, and stained with rhodamine-phalloidin (green) and DAPI (blue). Fluorescence images were then acquired using a confocal microscope (Olympus FV3000), and cytoskeletal morphology was quantitatively analyzed with ImageJ software (n ≥ 3 biological replicates).

Real-time quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR) analysis

To investigate the molecular mechanisms underlying the chondrogenic differentiation and anti-inflammatory effects of the hydrogel system, we analyzed the expression of key genes associated with cartilage formation, extracellular matrix composition, and inflammatory response. This investigation was designed to test our hypothesis that the sequential release of KGN and HF from the hydrogel promotes hyaline cartilage-specific gene expression while suppressing fibrotic and inflammatory markers. Total RNA was extracted from adherent cultures of BMSCs and RCs using the FastPure Cell Total RNA Isolation Kit V2 (Vazyme) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The RNA extraction process was carefully optimized to ensure high RNA purity and integrity, which are critical for reliable downstream gene expression analysis.

Subsequently, the expression levels of specific genes—including COL2, COL1, ACAN, Mmp13, IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α—were quantified using quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR). These genes were selected based on their established roles in chondrogenesis, fibrosis, cartilage degradation, and inflammatory pathways, allowing us to evaluate the hydrogel's ability to modulate biologically relevant pathways.

Total RNA was reverse-transcribed into complementary DNA (cDNA) using the Evo M-MLV RT Mix Kit with gDNA Clean (Vazyme Biotech), which includes a dedicated step for genomic DNA (gDNA) removal to prevent amplification artifacts. The qRT-PCR reactions were performed in at least three independent biological replicates to ensure statistical robustness and reproducibility. Threshold cycle (Ct) values were obtained from amplification curves, and relative gene expression levels were determined using the comparative ΔΔCt method, with normalization to the reference gene β-actin. Western blot analysis

To elucidate the protein-level mechanisms through which the hydrogel system modulates chondrogenic differentiation, extracellular matrix synthesis, and inflammatory signaling pathways, we performed Western blot analysis to quantify the expression of specific target proteins.

Cellular proteins were extracted from BMSCs and RCs using RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors to preserve protein phosphorylation states and prevent degradation. Protein concentrations were determined using a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay to ensure equal loading across samples. Subsequently, proteins (20 μg per lane) were separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and electrophoretically transferred to activated polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes (Millipore). After blocking with 5 % non-fat milk to prevent nonspecific binding, the membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies: CD9 (Rabbit, 1:1000, Beyotime), CD81 (Rabbit, 1:1000, Beyotime), CD63 (Mouse, 1:1000, Proteintech), TSG101 (Rabbit, 1:1000, Beyotime), and Calnexin (Rabbit, 1:1000, Beyotime) to characterize exosomal and cellular markers; Aggrecan (Rabbit, 1:500, Abcam), Collagen II (Rabbit, 1:1000, Abcam), SOX9 (Rabbit, 1:1000, Abcam), and Collagen I (Mouse, 1:1000, Proteintech) to evaluate chondrogenic and fibrotic phenotypes; and cGAS (Rabbit, 1:1000, Proteintech), STING (Rabbit, 1:1000, CST), p-STING (Rabbit, 1:1000, CST), TBK1 (Mouse, 1:2000, Proteintech), p-TBK1 (Mouse, 1:2000, Proteintech), TAK1 (Rabbit, 1:1000, Abclonal), p-TAK1 (Rabbit, 1:1000, Abclonal), p65 (Rabbit, 1:1000, CST), p-p65 (Rabbit, 1:1000, CST) to probe key inflammatory and intracellular signaling pathways. β-actin (Rabbit, 1:5000, Abcam) was used as a loading control.

Thereafter, membranes were incubated with species-appropriate horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies (diluted 1:1000 in antibody dilution buffer) for 1 h at 37 °C. Following three 10-min washes with TBST buffer (TBS containing 0.1 % Tween-20) to remove unbound antibodies, immunoreactive bands were detected using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) developing solution and visualized under a Tanon imaging system using default acquisition settings. This approach enabled quantitative comparison of protein expression changes underpinning the hydrogel-mediated effects on cartilage regeneration and anti-inflammatory responses.

Immunofluorescence staining

To visually assess and quantify the phenotypic changes and extracellular matrix composition in BMSCs and RCs following hydrogel treatment, immunofluorescence staining was performed to evaluate the expression of key proteins.

Cultured BMSCs and RCs were fixed with 4 % paraformaldehyde for 15 min and permeabilized with 0.5 % Triton X-100 for 10 min to facilitate intracellular antibody penetration. Non-specific binding sites were blocked with 5 % bovine serum albumin (BSA) for 1 h at room temperature. The samples were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with appropriate primary antibodies targeting specific proteins of interest to evaluate the functional effects of hydrogel treatment. After washing with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), the cells were incubated with Alexa Fluor®-conjugated secondary antibodies (488/594 nm, diluted 1:100) at room temperature for 1 h in the dark. Nuclei were counterstained with 4′6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) at a concentration of 1 μg/mL for 5 min. Fluorescence images were acquired using an Olympus confocal microscope under consistent imaging parameters to ensure comparability. Quantitative analysis of fluorescence intensity was performed using ImageJ software, enabling objective assessment of treatment-induced alterations in protein expression.

Assessment of migratory capacity in BMSCs

To evaluate the effects of different hydrogel formulations on BMSC migration, we conducted scratch wound healing assays and Transwell migration assays.

In the scratch assay, BMSCs were seeded in the lower chambers of 6-well plates and cultured until full confluence was achieved. Standardized cross-shaped scratches were created in the cell monolayer using a sterile 200 μL pipette tip. Subsequently, 200 μL of each hydrogel formulation was added to the upper chambers and allowed to polymerize. Cell migration was assessed at 0 and 24 h using phase-contrast microscopy, and wound closure rates were quantified to evaluate cellular motility. For the Transwell migration assay, BMSC suspensions (100 μL, 1 × 105 cells/mL) were added to the upper chambers of 24-well Transwell plates. The experimental groups received 200 μL of hydrogel extracts diluted in serum-free medium in the lower chambers, while the control groups received serum-free medium alone. After 24 h of incubation, migrated cells were fixed with 4 % paraformaldehyde and stained with 0.1 % crystal violet. Cell counts were obtained from at least three random fields per chamber using ImageJ software. All experiments were performed with a minimum of three biological replicates to ensure statistical reliability.

In vitro oxidative stress analysis

To investigate whether the hydrogel system alleviates oxidative stress, intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels in chondrocytes were evaluated under LPS-induced inflammatory conditions. ROS generation was measured using a Reactive Oxygen Species Assay Kit (S0033M, Beyotime). Chondrocytes were pretreated with LPS at a concentration of 100 μg/mL for 24 h to simulate an inflammatory microenvironment and induce oxidative stress. Subsequently, DCFH-DA fluorescence (excitation/emission wavelengths = 488 nm) was quantified using a BD FACSymphony™ flow cytometer. Data were obtained from at least three independent experiments to ensure reproducibility and statistical significance.

Cartilage regeneration in SD rat defect models

A total of 40 four-week-old male SD rats were obtained and randomly divided into five groups (n = 8 per group). Under anesthesia, a medial parapatellar arthrotomy was performed on left knees to expose the femoral trochlear groove. Full-thickness osteochondral defects (2 mm diameter) penetrating the subchondral bone were created at the trochlear center using a biopsy punch. Sham-operated controls underwent capsulotomy without defect creation, while defect-only controls received no further intervention. The experimental groups received intra-defect injections of 100 μL prepared composite hydrogel, which was photo-crosslinked for 60 s under 405 nm light. At 4 and 8 weeks post-operation, rats were euthanized. Knee joints were harvested and fixed in 4 % (w/v) paraformaldehyde for 72 h at 25 °C, followed by 4-week decalcification in EDTA (0.5 M, pH 7.4). Decalcified specimens were paraffin-embedded and sectioned for histology.

Following fixation, the harvested knee joint specimens were carefully removed from the paraformaldehyde solution, and any residual liquid was gently blotted away with gauze. Micro-CT scanning was performed using a micro-CT scanner. Each sample was placed securely in the scanning chamber, and images were acquired with the following parameters: a source voltage of 80 kV, a current of 0.06 mA, a transverse field of view (FOV) of 50 mm, an axial FOV of 16 mm, and a CT threshold of 1200. These settings were consistently applied across all samples to ensure comparability. The scanning region of interest (ROI) was meticulously centered on the surgically created full-thickness osteochondral defect (2 mm diameter) in the femoral trochlear groove.

The acquired projection images were reconstructed into three-dimensional (3D) models using the dedicated reconstruction software (Recon). For quantitative analysis, the reconstructed 3D images were imported into the data analysis software (Avatar). A standardized volume of interest (VOI) was defined to encapsulate the entire defect region, ensuring identical VOI placement across all samples. The following parameters were automatically calculated by the software within the defined VOI: Bone Volume/Total Volume (BV/TV, %), Trabecular Number (Tb. N, 1/mm), Trabecular Thickness (Tb. Th, mm), Trabecular Separation (Tb. Sp, mm), and Connectivity Density (Conn. Dn, 1/mm3). The resulting data were then exported for further analysis.

All histological slides were evaluated by an experienced investigator who was blinded to the experimental group assignments throughout the entire scoring process to minimize bias. The scoring was performed based on the established histological scoring system for cartilage repair (ICRS score). The specific criteria for each score were pre-defined according to the chosen scoring system. This study was approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of The Second Qilu Hospital of Shandong University (Approval No. KYLL20250716), and all efforts were made to minimize animal suffering.

Statistical analysis

All data are presented as mean ± SD from ≥3 biological replicates. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10.0 (GraphPad Software). Between-group comparisons used two-tailed unpaired Student's t-tests. Significance thresholds: ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, ∗∗∗p < 0.001, ∗∗∗∗p < 0.0001.

Results and discussion

Isolation and characterization of human umbilical cord mesenchymal stem cell-derived EXO

EXO were isolated from a serum-free conditioned medium of hUCMSCs using differential ultracentrifugation (Fig. 1A). Western blot analysis confirmed the enrichment of exosome-specific fractions, as evidenced by positive expression of canonical exosomal markers (CD9, CD81, CD63, TSG101) and the concurrent absence of the endoplasmic reticulum marker calnexin (Fig. 1B), thus indicating successful isolation free from significant organellar contamination. NTA revealed a homogeneous size distribution profile characteristic of EXO, with a mean diameter of 130.4 ± 6.2 nm (Fig. 1D). Consistently, TEM images confirmed the presence of vesicles exhibiting the classic cup-shaped morphology with intact bilayer membranes (Fig. 1C).

HF and KGN were electroporated into purified exosomes to generate drug-loaded exosomes designated as EXOHF and EXOKGN, respectively. Liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS) quantification of unencapsulated drug and subsequent standard curve extrapolation established drug loading efficiencies of approximately 10 % for both formulations. Crucially, the electroporation process did not compromise vesicle integrity, as validated by post-loading analyses using TEM and NTA (Fig. 1C and D). These analyses confirmed the retention of structural fidelity, demonstrating no significant size variation and maintaining intact bilayer membrane structures comparable to unloaded vesicles. This structural preservation underscores a key advantage of EXO as natural drug carriers: their bioactive phospholipid bilayer architecture inherently facilitates membrane traversal while offering excellent encapsulation and protection for hydrophobic therapeutic agents like HF and KGN, thereby significantly boosting intracellular delivery efficiency and bioavailability compared to many synthetic systems [[33], [34], [35]]. Moreover, the relative simplicity of exosome acquisition from cellular sources like hUCMSCs, often requiring less complex engineering than synthetic carriers such as liposomes and polymeric nanoparticles [36,37], enhances their practical utility for drug delivery applications.

After 24 h of co-culture with BMSCs and RCs, fluorescence microscopy revealed significant intracellular uptake of EXOHF and EXOKGN in both cell types. Further high-resolution imaging showed a pronounced tendency of the drug-loaded exosomes to accumulate in the perinuclear region. Importantly, these exosomes exhibited clear spatial separation from the nuclear envelope (Fig. 1E), indicating preferential perinuclear localization without direct penetration into the nucleus. To further characterize the intracellular distribution, colocalization analysis was performed using ImageJ software to qualitatively assess the localization of EXO within cells. A straight line spanning the cytoplasmic and nuclear regions was drawn on merged three-channel images, oriented from point “1” to point “2”. Fluorescence intensity profiles along this path were extracted using the “Plot Profile” function. Strong overlap (peak coincidence) between the red (EXO) and green (F-actin) signals within the cytoplasmic region indicated cytoplasmic localization of EXO, while the absence of overlap or clear separation between the red and blue (DAPI) signals in the nuclear region confirmed the exclusion of EXO from the nucleus (Fig. 1F and G).

This analysis qualitatively demonstrated efficient uptake of EXO in both cell types, with predominant cytoplasmic distribution, consistent with the expected behavior of a drug delivery system.

Preparation and characterization of gel-C-EH + EK hydrogel

GelMA was functionalized through covalent conjugation of CP05 peptide (abbreviated as CP05, modified with Allyl-L-glycine) to the polymer side chains (Fig. 2A). Photo-crosslinking at 405 nm wavelength solidified all hydrogel groups while preserving structural integrity (Fig. 2C). Incorporation of CP05 induced subtle alterations in hydrogel color and transparency. SEM revealed interconnected porous architectures across all hydrogel groups (Fig. 2D), which provide mechanical support for cellular growth while enabling sustained EXO release. Furthermore, both SEM microstructures and average pore sizes indicated no significant differences between Gel-C + E and Gel-C + EH/K hydrogels, suggesting that drug-loaded EXO did not alter the hydrogel properties (Fig. S2A and B). The covalent conjugation between CP05 and GelMA yielded a new three-dimensional polymeric network scaffold. Significantly, this architecture retains GelMA's inherent photo-induced crosslinking ability and native cell-adhesive motifs while simultaneously compensating for the deficient bioresponsive behavior of single-component GelMA systems [[38], [39], [40]].

Fourier transform infrared FTIR analysis demonstrated characteristic peak shifts following the introduction of EXO and CP05 peptides. Notably, the covalently crosslinked GelMA-CP05 group exhibited distinct additional alterations, confirming effective integration of functional components via covalent anchoring (Fig. 2F). This structural modification enhanced EXO enrichment within the scaffolds, evidencing retention mechanisms driven by specific anchoring interactions rather than mere physical entrapment (Fig. 2E and J).

Given that substrate mechanical properties have been established to regulate cellular functions and differentiation [[41], [42], [43], [44], [45]], mechanical compression testing of the hydrogel groups demonstrated significantly higher compressive modulus in Gel + E, Gel/C + E and Gel-C + E systems compared to the pure GelMA group. Specifically, the Gel-C + E hydrogel group exhibited a compressive modulus range of 0.37 ± 0.04 MPa (Fig. 2H and K). This mechanical reinforcement is likely attributable to reduced pore dimensions and strengthened polymer networks resulting from covalent reactions between GelMA and CP05 [46,47], while simultaneously providing appropriate mechanical support at defect sites that more closely mimics natural cartilage [[48], [49], [50]].

Swelling behavior and degradation kinetics constitute critical determinants for practical applications, governing solute diffusion, mass transport, mechanical performance, and cellular responses [51,52]. Stability assessments under physiological conditions (37 °C, PBS, agitation) indicated that the GelMA group exhibited a maximal swelling ratio (27.7 % ± 3.0 %) and the most rapid degradation (23.9 % ± 4.2 % mass retention at day 14). Conversely, Gel-C + E displayed significantly reduced swelling (20.7 % ± 2.1 %) and delayed degradation (66.4 % ± 7.3 % mass retention at day 14) (Fig. 2G and I). This enhanced stability confirms structural reinforcement via covalent conjugation integration.

Drug release profiles of encapsulated HF and KGN evaluated by UV–Vis spectrophotometry demonstrated sustained release kinetics over 30 days (Fig. 2L). HF showed slow initial release (4.9 % ± 0.6 % at 24 h versus 25.7 % ± 5.2 % for KGN), followed by an extended-release phase (70.2 % ± 4.4 % cumulative release at day 30 versus 87.8 % ± 4.6 % for KGN). This differential release behavior stems from distinct modulation of diffusion kinetics by the CP05 peptide anchoring effects. Within this shared sustained-release framework, the CP05 anchoring mechanism ensures dual functionality: HF achieves sufficient effective concentrations during late phases to suppress fibrosis, while simultaneously permitting KGN's rapid early-phase release to facilitate BMSC recruitment and differentiation.

While this temporally programmed release profile is well-suited for the specific full-thickness chondral defect model (2 mm diameter) employed in the present study, it is important to note that the optimal release kinetics may vary for cartilage defects of different sizes, depths, or etiologies. Larger or more complex defects, such as those involving extensive subchondral bone damage or irregular geometries, might necessitate a longer or more adaptable release window to accommodate the increased regenerative volume and prolonged inflammatory phase. Therefore, future studies should focus on tailoring the release kinetics—potentially by modulating the density of CP05 anchoring sites or adjusting the hydrogel degradation rate—to match the specific temporal sequence-dependent healing requirements of diverse cartilage injury scenarios.

Cytocompatibility in vitro

The Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay revealed distinct proliferation dynamics of BMSCs and RCs cultured with different hydrogel extracts. On day 1, no significant differences in cell viability were observed among the groups. By day 4, the viability of both cell types had increased across all groups. By day 7, cells cultured in the Gel-C-EH + EK hydrogel system exhibited the highest proliferation activity for both cell types (Fig. 3A). Live/Dead staining after 3 days of co-culture in the Transwell system revealed no significant cytotoxicity of the hydrogels toward either BMSCs or RCs (Fig. 3D). F-actin cytoskeleton analysis further demonstrated that cells grown on the Gel-C-EH + EK hydrogel surface displayed more extensive spreading behavior and stronger cell–matrix interactions compared to control groups (Fig. 3B, C, E and F). Specifically, both BMSCs and RCs cultured on the Gel-C-EH + EK hydrogel showed larger spreading areas, suggesting enhanced cell adhesion and diffusion facilitated by the hydrogel (Fig. 3Ca, c and Fa, b). Compared to controls, BMSCs on the hydrogel exhibited higher aspect ratios (Fig. 3Cb), whereas RCs showed lower aspect ratios (Fig. 3Cd). For BMSCs, a higher aspect ratio is associated with enhanced migration capacity and polarization, which aligns with our design intent of rapid EXOKGN release to promote BMSC recruitment and early chondroinduction. For RCs, a lower aspect ratio is favorable, indicating the maintenance of a stable, differentiated phenotype and prevention of dedifferentiation [[53], [54], [55]]. Together, these comprehensive data verify the favorable biocompatibility of the Gel-C-EH + EK hydrogel and demonstrate its positive effects on cellular transcriptional activity and physiological functions.

In vitro assessment of the capacity to promote bone marrow mesenchymal stem cell migration and chondrogenic differentiation

To evaluate the effect of the Gel-C-EH + EK hydrogel on the migration capacity of BMSCs serving as seed cells, scratch and Transwell assays were performed. After 24 h, scratch assay results indicated the smallest residual wound area and significantly higher migration rates in the Gel-C-EH + EK group compared to other groups (Fig. 4C and D). Similarly, Transwell assays demonstrated a significantly greater number of BMSCs penetrating the polycarbonate membrane in the Gel-C-EH + EK group (Fig. 4E and F), indicating that this hydrogel system effectively promotes BMSCs migration and recruitment. BMSCs constitute ideal seed cells for cartilage repair due to their anatomically privileged location at the bone marrow cartilage interface [[56], [57], [58], [59]]. Consequently, the Gel-C-EH + EK hydrogel has been demonstrated to harness and augment this positional advantage.

To evaluate chondrogenic differentiation, BMSCs were cultured within the Gel-C-EH + EK hydrogel for 21 days. Biochemical analyses (Western blotting and RT-qPCR) revealed the highest expression levels of chondrogenic markers (ACAN, COL2, SOX9) in the Gel-C-EH + EK group (Fig. 4A and B). This indicates that the sustained delivery of EXOKGN from the Gel-C-EH + EK scaffold significantly enhanced the chondrogenic differentiation capacity of BMSCs. Concurrently, in Gel-C-EH and Gel-C-EH + EK hydrogels, expression of the fibrotic marker COL1 was significantly downregulated, whereas the COL2 expression remained at a high level (Fig. 4A, B and Fig. S4A). This specific inhibitory effect on COL1 can be attributed to HF encapsulated within EXO. Through its anchorage to the CP05 peptide within the hydrogel system, HF exhibited a sustained release rate, consequently maintaining therapeutic concentrations during the late stage. Consequently, HF maintained therapeutically relevant concentrations during the later phases, effectively inhibiting the TGF-β/Smad signaling pathway [15]. This inhibition reduced fibroblast-to-myofibroblast transformation and aberrant COL1 production, while concurrently preserving COL2 synthesis [60], thereby stabilizing the phenotype of the neocartilage.

Histochemical analysis further corroborated the enhanced chondrogenic differentiation. Alcian blue staining revealed a more extensive and intense staining area within the Gel-C-EH + EK hydrogel constructs, indicating the highest level of sulfated glycosaminoglycan (sGAG) production (Fig. 4G). Histological evaluation of BMSCs spheroids (using H&E, Alcian blue, and Toluidine blue staining) confirmed significantly augmented sGAG deposition and enhanced cartilage ECM organization within the Gel-C-EH + EK group compared to control groups (Fig. 4H). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that the Gel-C-EH + EK hydrogel effectively enhances chondrogenic differentiation by promoting the expression of key chondrogenic genes and proteins, increasing ECM synthesis, and suppressing fibrotic propensity, thereby functionally directing BMSCs towards chondrogenesis.

In vitro assessment of the capacity to maintain stable chondrocyte phenotypes

Building upon previously demonstrated dual functionality of Gel-C-EH + EK hydrogels for BMSCs, this study further examined their capacity to maintain chondrocyte phenotype stability under simulated pathological conditions. Primary chondrocytes cultured under standard conditions and LPS/IL-1β-stimulated microenvironments underwent comprehensive biochemical analyses. Western blotting and RT-qPCR revealed a significantly enhanced expression of chondrogenic markers (ACAN, COL2, SOX9) accompanied by suppressed COL1 expression in the Gel-C-EH + EK group under normal conditions (Fig. 5D–F and Fig. S4B). Crucially, under inflammatory stimulation, this formulation maintained chondrogenic marker stability while eliminating injury-induced COL1 upregulation (Fig. 5E, F and Fig. S4C), confirming exceptional phenotype-preserving capability. Immunofluorescence (IF) analysis further demonstrated collagen imbalance in the control and Gel-C-E groups, characterized by reduced COL2 and abnormally elevated COL1 proportions (Fig. 5A–C). In contrast, the Gel-C-EH group and the Gel-C-EH + EK group maintained normal COL2/COL1 ratios through HF-mediated specific suppression of COL1 synthesis. Moreover, DCFH-DA fluorescence staining (Fig. 5G and H) and flow cytometry (Fig. 5I and J) confirmed that the Gel-C-EH + EK hydrogel significantly attenuated LPS-induced ROS generation, reduced early oxidative stress levels, and mitigated injury-associated microenvironment disruption.

This system concurrently maintains chondrocyte phenotypic stability and mitigates collagen imbalance-induced fibrotic manifestations.

Therapeutic effects of gel-C-EH + EK hydrogels on cartilage defects in vivo

In vitro studies demonstrated the favorable biological properties of the Gel-C-EH + EK hydrogel. Consequently, for the in vivo experiments, adult male SD rats were selected, and full-thickness chondral defects were surgically created. Forty rats were randomly allocated into five groups: Sham (no defect), Untreated Control (natural regeneration), Gel-C-E, Gel-C-EK and Gel-C-EH + EK. All treated groups received a post-operative intralesional injection of the respective hydrogel formulation followed by photo-crosslinking (405 nm). Samples were harvested at 4 and 8 weeks for multi-modal assessment.

Macroscopic evaluation at 4 weeks revealed irregular tissue morphology with pronounced depression in all groups, most severe in the control group. By week 8, defects in the Gel-C-EH + EK group were completely repaired, exhibiting seamless integration with the adjacent native cartilage. In contrast, the Untreated Control and Gel-C-E groups displayed irregular defects lacking continuity, while the Gel-C-EK group showed hyperplastic white fibrous tissue at the defect site (Fig. 6A). The ICRS scoring confirmed that the Gel-C-EH + EK group achieved excellent repair quality at both the 4-week and 8-week time points (Fig. 6B). Micro-computed tomography (micro-CT) 3D reconstructions further demonstrated near-anatomical articular surface restoration without a subchondral cavity in the Gel-C-EH + EK group at week 8 (Fig. 6E), contrasting sharply with the persistent structural defects observed in the control group and other groups. Quantitative micro-CT analysis revealed significant enhancements (p < 0.01) in bone volume fraction (BV/TV) (Fig. 6F), trabecular number (Tb. N) (Fig. 6G), trabecular thickness (Tb. Th) (Fig. 6J), and trabecular connectivity (Fig. 6H) within the Gel-C-EH + EK group compared to the control group and other groups, indicating enhanced subchondral reconstruction.

Histopathological analysis corroborated these findings (Fig. 6K). H&E staining at week 8 displayed well-organized chondrocyte columns and a continuous tide mark in the Gel-C-EH + EK group, in stark contrast to the disorganized tissue architecture observed in the control group. Safranin O/Fast Green and Toluidine Blue staining revealed the most abundant proteoglycan deposition and a continuous, homogenous distribution of chondrocyte ECM in the Gel-C-EH + EK group, consistent with normal articular cartilage. Quantitative analysis of collagen content in the regenerated Elisa (Fig. 6C) found an elevated COL1/COL2 in the neo-tissue of the Untreated Control (Fig. 6D), Gel-C-E and Gel-C-EK groups. Conversely, the Gel-C-EH + EK group restored a near-normal COL2/COL1 ratio, validating hyaline cartilage regeneration. Therefore, the Gel-C-EH + EK hydrogel enables functional cartilage repair through the coordinated regeneration of hyaline-like cartilage and subchondral bone.

Notably, while the present study demonstrates promising repair outcomes in a rat model, it is important to acknowledge the inherent limitations of small animal models for cartilage regeneration research. Rats exhibit substantial differences from humans in terms of articular cartilage thickness, biomechanical loading, and healing capacity. Therefore, further validation in large animal models (such as rabbits or miniature pigs) that better mimic human joint physiology and defect pathophysiology is necessary to more robustly assess the translational potential and long-term safety of the Gel-C-EH + EK hydrogel system.

Inherent regulatory mechanism of chondrocyte behavioral dynamics under inflammation stimulation

Chondrocytes stimulated with LPS served as the model group, while normally cultured chondrocytes constituted the control group. Subsequent mRNA sequencing revealed 1533 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) (P < 0.05; | log2 (fold change) | > 1) in the LPS-treated group compared to controls, comprising 1049 upregulated and 484 downregulated genes (Fig. 7A). Notably, key genes including cGAS and STING exhibited significant alterations (Fig. 7B and C). GO and KEGG enrichment analyses demonstrated significant enrichment of the cytosolic DNA-sensing pathway (cGAS-STING signaling), suggesting its pivotal role in cartilage injury and inflammation (Fig. 7D and E).

Based on these transcriptomic findings implicating the cGAS-STING pathway in inflammation-driven cartilage pathology, we next sought to investigate whether targeted intervention of this pathway could mitigate inflammatory responses and chondrocyte dysfunction.

Gel-C-EH + EK hydrogel alleviates inflammatory responses by targeting the cGAS-STING pathway

To investigate the anti-inflammatory mechanism of the Gel-C-EH + EK hydrogel system under LPS stimulation, this study evaluated its effects on key inflammatory mediators. Elisa (Fig. 8Aa-d) and RT-qPCR (Fig. 8Ca-d) revealed that both Gel-C-EH and Gel-C-EH + EK significantly inhibited the expression/secretion of IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α, and Mmp13, showing significant differences compared to the control group. In contrast, Gel-C-E demonstrated negligible effects, highlighting the indispensable roles of HF and KGN.

HF exerted the primary anti-inflammatory effect. Individualized treatment of LPS-stimulated chondrocytes confirmed that HF could significantly downregulate the expression of inflammatory cytokines (RT-qPCR) (Fig. 8Ba-d). Western blot further indicated that HF restored the normal expression levels of chondrogenic markers (ACAN, COL2, SOX9) under inflammatory stress, while not altering their basal levels in normal chondrocytes (Fig. 8D and Fig. S4D). Furthermore, HF reversed LPS-induced suppression of SOX9/COL2 and effectively suppressed the overexpression of COL1 (Fig. 8G and Fig. S4G), suggesting a dual function of HF in maintaining chondrocyte phenotype and inhibiting fibrotic remodeling. Given that mitochondrial DNA leakage during cartilage injury activates the cGAS-STING signaling pathway [[65], [66], [67], [68]], the interaction between HF and cGAS was probed further. This interaction was validated by co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) and thermal shift assays (CETSA), confirming the formation of a robust HF-cGAS complex (Fig. 8E–H and Fig. S4G). Therefore, HF directly targets cGAS to block STING pathway activation. Mechanistically, HF pretreatment suppressed LPS-induced cGAS upregulation and phosphorylation of downstream effectors (STING, TBK1, TAK1, NF-κB p65), effectively blocking pathway activation (Fig. 8I, J and Fig. S4F). Collectively, these results indicate that the Gel-C-EH + EK hydrogel combats cartilage degeneration by inhibiting cGAS-STING signaling, preventing feedback amplification, and providing early intervention targeting the upstream node of the inflammatory cascade.

Conclusion

In summary, the Gel-C-EH + EK hydrogel provides an effective therapeutic platform for the regeneration of functional hyaline cartilage at defect sites. Its advantage lies in the controlled and sequential delivery of KGN and HF within the GelMA matrix, achieved via EXO encapsulation and the CP05 peptide anchoring strategy. This intelligent, time-dependent gradient release profile better aligns with the specific biological requirements of each stage in the cartilage repair process. During the first stage, HF reduces local oxidative stress levels, while KGN continuously drives the recruitment, chondrogenic differentiation of BMSCs and ECM synthesis. Subsequently, the delayed release and sustained presence of HF play a critical role in the later stage by maintaining chondrocyte phenotypic stability, suppressing chronic inflammatory responses, and inhibiting fibrocartilage formation, thereby ensuring the generation of hyaline cartilage with normal biomechanical function. Facing a series of challenges during the repair process, such as insufficient endogenous chondrogenesis, chronic persistent inflammation, and abnormal fibrosis, this hydrogel system demonstrates significant therapeutic potential. It notably enhances the regeneration rate of hyaline cartilage while presenting a promising translation strategy.
