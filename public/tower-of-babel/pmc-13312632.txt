Association between the blood urea nitrogen to albumin ratio and all-cause mortality in critically ill patients with liver cirrhosis
Authors: Jian Liao, Maojuan Wang, Mingyang Du, Yuan Wang, Dingyu Lu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe prognosis for critically ill patients with liver cirrhosis in the intensive care unit (ICU) remains uncertain. This study aimed to investigate the association between the Blood Urea Nitrogen to Albumin Ratio (BAR) at ICU admission and mortality in patients with liver cirrhosis.MethodsA retrospective cohort study was conducted using data from the MIMIC-IV database. The primary and secondary outcomes were 28-day and 180-day all-cause mortality after ICU admission, respectively. Patients were stratified into high- and low-BAR groups based on an optimal cutoff value determined by ROC analysis. To evaluate the association between BAR and all-cause mortality, we employed multivariate Cox regression models with comprehensive covariate adjustments. The survival experience stratified by BAR was visualized using Kaplan-Meier curves. Furthermore, we utilized restricted cubic splines to examine the potential non-linear relationship between BAR and the risk of mortality, complemented by interaction and subgroup analyses to assess the consistency of this association.ResultsA total of 3,268 adult patients with liver cirrhosis were included. The optimal BAR cutoff was 9.25. Compared to the low-BAR group, patients in the high-BAR group (≥ 9.25) had significantly higher 28-day (46.5% vs. 17.5%) and 180-day (54.0% vs. 24.9%) mortality rates. After multivariable adjustment, a high BAR remained independently associated with increased risks of 28-day (HR: 1.65, 95% CI: 1.42–1.92) and 180-day mortality (HR: 1.41, 95% CI: 1.21–1.64). BAR demonstrated good predictive ability for both 28-day mortality (AUC: 0.752) and 180-day mortality (AUC: 0.744), which was comparable to APS III and significantly better than SOFA, OASIS, GCS, and Charlson scores. Restricted cubic spline analyses revealed the mortality risk increased only modestly at BAR levels below approximately 9.25, but rose steeply and nonlinearly once BAR exceeded this threshold, indicating a potential threshold effect.ConclusionThe BAR is an independent predictor of short-and long-term mortality in critically ill cirrhotic patients. Its calculation at ICU admission may serve as a potential tool for early risk stratification, potentially facilitating timely clinical interventions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12876-026-04887-2.

Introduction

Chronic liver disease (CLD) poses a considerable challenge to public health on a global scale, as it leads to a high rate of morbidity and mortality among populations worldwide [1]. As reported by the World Health Organization, around 2 million fatalities each year are attributed to liver diseases, with cirrhosis and liver cancer as the primary contributors [2]. Cirrhosis significantly contributes to worldwide illness and death, with yearly fatalities and hospital admissions consistently increasing [3, 4].

The issue of liver disease is responsible for a growing share of admissions to the Intensive Care Unit (ICU) as well as hospitals [5]. Episodes of acute decompensation in cirrhosis, including gastrointestinal bleeding and hepatic encephalopathy, are linked to elevated rates of both multi-organ failure and short-term mortality [6–8]. In fact, 10% of those admitted to the hospital due to complications arising from cirrhosis necessitate admission to the intensive care unit [9]. The mortality rates observed in the ICU for individuals suffering from cirrhosis have varied between 29% and 87% [10–12].

At present, there are no established prognostic scoring instruments for quickly estimating ICU outcomes in patients suffering from cirrhotic liver disease in a general ICU environment. Current liver scoring systems are tailored for particular objectives. For example, the Child-Turcotte-Pugh (CTP) score was formulated to forecast mortality after surgical intervention for esophageal varices [13], whereas the United Kingdom model for end-stage liver disease (UKELD) was designed to evaluate patients awaiting transplantation in the UK [14]. The MELD score is utilized to prioritize individuals awaiting liver transplants and is calculated based on three intricate factors: creatinine, total bilirubin, and the international normalized ratio [15]. In recent years, more specialized scoring systems such as CLIF-SOFA [16] and CLIF-C ACLF scores [17] have been developed for acute-on-chronic liver failure (ACLF), the primary syndrome leading to ICU admission in cirrhotic patients. However, their predictive performance in ICU settings may be constrained. Consequently, identifying effective, non-invasive, and readily accessible biomarkers for predicting clinical results in patients with liver cirrhosis is crucial.

Blood urea nitrogen (BUN) mainly indicates changes in kidney function and protein metabolism, alongside minor fluctuations in cardiac output and neurohumoral activity. Increased levels of BUN correlate with unfavorable outcomes in numerous conditions, such as heart failure, renal disorders, and various critical illnesses [18, 19]. Albumin (ALB) mainly indicates the body’s nutritional condition and fulfills several vital physiological functions which include preserving osmotic equilibrium, facilitating molecular transport, providing antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties, as well as helping to stabilize the function of vascular endothelium [17]. Previous research has shown that ALB levels serve as a spredictor of mortality and prognosis in cardiovascular diseases and acute pancreatitis [20–22]. Patients with liver cirrhosis often present with hypoalbuminemia and elevated creatinine levels. The ratio of BUN to ALB (BAR) serves as a broad measure of renal function, inflammation, nutritional health, and the condition of the endothelium. Since it was first introduced, BAR has shown significant associations with various conditions, such as pneumonia, sepsis, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), COVID-19, cancer, gastrointestinal bleeding and acute ischemic stroke [23–29].

Nevertheless, the predictive value of BAR in liver cirrhosis remains inadequately investigated, particularly among patients admitted to the ICU. Considering the elevated mortality associated with ICU patients suffering from liver cirrhosis, along with the necessity for dependable and easy-to- use prognostic indicators, we proposed that BAR could serve as a useful and easily computable predictor to help clinicians in evaluating both short- and long-term risk of mortality for patients with liver cirrhosis.

Methods

Data source

Data were extracted from the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care IV (MIMIC-IV v3.0), a critical care database approved by Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center (BIDMC) and the Massachusetts Institute of Technology (MIT), which can be found at https://mimic.mit.edu [30]. The study specifically examined medical records of patients admitted to the BIDMC ICU between 2008 and 2022. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center (2001-P-001699/14) and the Massachusetts Institute of Technology (No. 0403000206) and individual consent for this retrospective analysis was waived. Patient’s data were de-identified to ensure privacy by the Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center. All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations. This retrospective study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Patient inclusion

The patient cohort for this research comprised individuals with a confirmed diagnosis of liver cirrhosis, following the guidelines outlined in the International Classification of Diseases, 9th and 10th Revision (5715, 5712, K7469, K7031, K7030, K7460, K743, 5716, K745, K744, K717, K741). Patients were included based on a documented diagnosis of liver cirrhosis using ICD-9/10 codes, regardless of the primary reason for ICU admission, because cirrhosis itself is a chronic condition that may influence patient outcomes irrespective of the acute precipitating event. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) those who were not admitted to the ICU for the first time; (2) ICU stay ≤ 24 h; (3) patients with end-stage renal disease or malignant tumors; (4) patients with transplantation of liver; (5) patients who lacked adequate data on their first day of admission. (Supplementary file 1).

Data collection

The data extraction tools included PostgresSQL software (version 13.7.2) and pgAdmin 4 tool (version 7.5). Data extraction focused on four key variable categories: demographics, vital signs, laboratory parameters, illness severity scores, comorbidities and treatments. All vital signs and laboratory tests data were measured for the first time within 24 h of ICU admission. BAR was calculated using the BUN/ALB ratio obtained from the worst measurements within 24 h of ICU admission. The exact timing of each measurement was recorded using the charttime field in the MIMIC-IV database, and the time interval from ICU admission was calculated using the DATETIME_DIFF function. To mitigate any potential bias, variables containing missing values surpassing 20% were eliminated, including C-reactive protein (CRP) and Interleukin-6 (IL-6) (Supplementary file 2, Table S1). To handle secondary variables (such as vital signs and laboratory tests) with less than 20% missing data, we employed the multiple imputation (miss Forest R package) technique, five complete data sets each with 5 iterations were imputed with predictive mean matching using available covariates while excluding the outcome variables. Using MIMIC-IV’s labevents table, we extracted the first BUN (itemid 51006) and albumin (itemid 50862) measurements with charttime within 24 h of intime from the icustays table. Patients with missing values for either BUN or albumin were excluded.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was all-cause mortality within 28 days of ICU admission. Time zero was defined as the time of ICU admission. Patients were followed from ICU admission until death, day 28, or censoring. The secondary outcome was all-cause mortality within 180 days of ICU admission. Patients were followed from ICU admission until death, day 180, or censoring. Survival status after hospital discharge was ascertained using the Social Security Death Index (SSDI) linked to the MIMIC-IV database, which provides death dates for patients regardless of whether death occurred in-hospital or after discharge.

Statistical analysis

The normality of continuous variables was assessed graphically. As all variables deviated from normality, they are presented as median (IQR) and compared using the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical data were presented as frequencies and proportions. For comparisons, one-way ANOVA (normally distributed data), the Kruskal-Wallis test (non-normal data), or the chi-square test (categorical data) were employed.

The association between BAR levels at admission and 28-day mortality in patients with cirrhosis was assessed using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis. Patients were then categorized into lower BAR group and higher BAR group according to the optimal cutoff value identified by maximizing the Youden index.

Kaplan-Meier survival curves were constructed to compare the risk of primary outcome events between the two groups, and differences were assessed using the log-rank test.

Cox regression analysis was conducted to investigate the association between BAR and the risk of mortality. We included all variables in the survival analysis. Model 1: unadjusted; Model 2 was adjusted for age, gender, BMI, temperature, heart rate (HR), mean arterial pressure (MAP), and saturation of peripheral oxygen (SPO2), respiratory rate (RR), white blood cell (WBC), platelet, hemoglobin, red blood cell distribution width (RDW), sodium, potassium, lactate, prothrombin time (PT), fibrinogen, activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT), international normalized ratio (INR), total bilirubin, lactic dehydrogenase (LDH), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and creatinine; Model 3 was further adjusted for Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA), Acute Physiology Score III (APS III), Oxford Acute Severity of Illness Score (OASIS), glasgow coma scale (GCS), Charlson Comorbidity Index (Charlson), esophagealvariceal bleeding, AKI, sepsis, hypertension, diabetes, heart failure, hepatitis, pneumonia, hyperlipemia, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), seroperitoneum, hepatic encephalopathy, the use of diuretic, vasoactive drugs, continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT) and ventilation. To assess the robustness of our findings, we constructed an additional multivariable Cox model (Model 4) that adjusted for all covariates in Model 3 but excluded the comprehensive illness severity scores (SOFA, APS III, and OASIS) to avoid potential overadjustment for mediators or colliders. To evaluate whether the addition of covariates significantly improved model fit, we performed likelihood ratio (LR) tests comparing nested models (Model 2 vs. Model 1, and Model 3 vs. Model 2). We assessed multicollinearity by calculating the variance inflation factor (VIF) for all covariates in the multivariable Cox models. A VIF > 5 was considered evidence of significant collinearity. A two-sided p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Additionally, given the high prevalence of sepsis in our cohort and its profound physiological impact, we conducted a subgroup analysis restricted to patients without sepsis to determine if the prognostic value of BAR persisted independently of this condition.

To flexibly model the potential nonlinear relationship between BAR and all-cause mortality, we fitted multivariable Cox proportional hazards models using restricted cubic splines with five knots placed at the 5th, 27.5th, 50th, 72.5th, and 95th percentiles of BAR distribution. These models were fully adjusted for all covariates included in Model 3 of the primary analysis.

The predictive abilities of BAR, SOFA, APS III, OASIS, GCS, and Charlson scores for 28-day and 180-day all-cause mortality were assessed using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis. The area under the curve (AUC) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) was calculated for each score. To compare the predictive performance between BAR and each of the other scores, we performed DeLong’s test for two correlated ROC curves. A two-sided p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

To evaluate whether the association between BAR and mortality was consistent across key patient characteristics, we performed prespecified subgroup analyses. For each subgroup, we fitted separate multivariable Cox proportional hazards models that included the same covariates as in Model 3 of the primary analysis. BAR was analyzed as a dichotomous variable using the optimal cutoff of 9.25. Finally, subgroup analyses were performed to assess the consistency of the BAR’s effect across populations stratified by age (< 65 vs. ≥65 years), gender (female vs. male), and medical history-including esophageal variceal bleeding, acute kidney injury, sepsis, hypertension, diabetes, heart failure, hepatic encephalopathy, as well as use of continuous renal replacement therapy and mechanical ventilation. Interaction effects were evaluated using P-values for interaction terms. The data analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.2.2) and SPSS statistical software (Version 26.0). For all analyses, a 2-side P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics

After applying strict inclusion and exclusion criteria, 3268 patients with cirrhosis were enrolled in this study (Fig. 1). Based on the optimal BAR cutoff value, the cohort was stratified into the lower BAR group (< 9.25) and the higher BAR group (≥ 9.25) (Fig. 2). Patients in the higher BAR group were older, had higher BMI, and exhibited more severe clinical indicators including elevated levels of WBC, PT, APTT, INR, total bilirubin and creatinine. The higher BAR group showed significantly higher illness severity scores (SOFA: 9.6 ± 4.1 vs. 7.1 ± 3.8; APSIII: 69 ± 23 vs. 49 ± 22; OASIS: 35 ± 9 vs. 32 ± 8; all p < 0.001) and greater comorbidity burden (Charlson score: 6.58 ± 2.77 vs. 5.48 ± 2.58, p < 0.001). Notable differences in comorbidities included higher rates of esophagealvariceal bleeding (33.5% vs. 30.1%), AKI (90.4% vs. 80.5%), sepsis (81.0% vs. 70.0%), heart failure (23.0% vs. 13.2%), seroperitoneum (66.6% vs. 54.1%), and hepatic encephalopathy (12.8% vs. 6.5%) in the higher BAR group (all p < 0.05). Treatment during hospitalization differed significantly, patients in the higher BAR group received more human albumin (66.4% vs. 51.1%), norepinephrine (56.9% vs. 41.8%), and mechanical ventilation (52.0% vs. 41.5%) (all p < 0.001). Compared to the lower BAR group, patients in the high-BAR group exhibited higher utilization rates of plasma, albumin, diuretics, vasoactive drugs, CRRT and mechanical ventilation, along with a longer duration of mechanical ventilation. In terms of clinical outcomes, patients in the high-BAR group exhibited longer hospital and ICU stays, along with higher 28-day (46.5% vs. 17.5%) and 180-day mortality rates ( 54.0% vs. 24.9%), compared to those in the lower BAR group (Table 1).

Survival analysis

In this study, 1039 of the 3268 patients (31.8%) with liver cirrhosis died within 28 days and 1282 died (45.2%) within 180 days. The Kaplan-Meier survival curve showed that the survival rate for 28-day increased for the lower BAR group compared to the higher BAR group (log-rank test p < 0.001). Moreover, the 180-day survival curve demonstrated similar results (Fig. 3).

Associations between BAR and the risk of mortality

Using multivariate Cox regression models, we examined the association between the BAR and all-cause mortality at day 28 and day 180 in patients with liver cirrhosis.

The results showed that BAR had a positive association with increased risk of mortality when expressed as a continuous variable in Model 3 after adjusting for all potential confounders. A higher BAR was significantly associated with an increased risk of mortality across all time points compared to the lower BAR group. In the unadjusted Model 1, HR were 2.21 (95% CI: 1.93–2.53; p < 0.001) at day 28 and 2.12 (95% CI: 1.85–2.42; p < 0.001) at day 180. This association remained statistically significant in Model 2 after preliminary adjustments. In the fully adjusted Model 3, a higher BAR was associated with a 65% increase in mortality risk at day 28 (HR:1.65; 95% CI: 1.42–1.92) and a 41% increase at day 180 (HR:1.41; 95% CI: 1.21–1.64). Likelihood ratio tests demonstrated that both Model 2 (LR χ² = 156.7, df = 18, p < 0.001) and Model 3 (LR χ² = 89.2, df = 12, p < 0.001) provided significantly better fit than their respective nested models, confirming the incremental prognostic value of the added covariates (Supplementary file 3, Table S2). When the comprehensive illness severity scores (SOFA, APS III) were removed from the fully adjusted model (Model 4), the association between higher BAR and 28-day mortality remained robust (HR: 1.66; 95% CI: 1.41–1.92), similar to the estimate from Model 3 (HR: 1.65). This suggests minimal overadjustment bias introduced by these scores (Table 2). The VIF values ranged from 1.04 to 4.19. All VIF values were below the conventional threshold of 5, indicating no significant multicollinearity among the predictor variables in our models (Supplementary file 4, Table S3). Critically, in the subgroup of patients without sepsis (n = 805), a higher BAR remained significantly associated with increased 28-day mortality (HR: 1.72; 95% CI: 1.35–2.19) after full adjustment, with a magnitude of effect similar to that observed in the overall cohort. This indicates that the predictive value of BAR is not solely driven by the presence of sepsis (Supplementary file 5, Table S4).

Non-linear relationship detection

After multivariable adjustment for all covariates in Model 3, restricted cubic spline analyses revealed a non-linear positive association between BAR and all-cause mortality at both 28 days and 180 days (p for non-linearity < 0.001 for both). Specifically, the mortality risk increased only modestly at BAR levels below approximately 9.25, but rose steeply and nonlinearly once BAR exceeded this threshold, indicating a potential threshold effect. Predictive value of BAR for 28-day all-cause mortality and 180-day all-cause mortality

ROC analysis was performed to evaluate the predictive performance of BAR, SOFA, APS III, OASIS, GCS, and Charlson for 28-day all-cause mortality (Fig. 5; Table 3). BAR achieved an AUC of 0.752 (95% CI: 0.73–0.77), which was higher than those of the other scores. However, DeLong test comparisons revealed that while the AUC of BAR was significantly larger than those of SOFA, OASIS, GCS, and Charlson (all p < 0.001), it was not significantly different from that of APS III (AUC difference = 0.004, p = 0.58) (Supplementary file 6, Table S5). Thus, BAR showed comparable predictive performance to APS III and superior performance to the remaining scores for 28-day mortality. For 180-day all-cause mortality, BAR also demonstrated good predictive ability with an AUC of 0.744, which was again significantly higher than those of SOFA (0.677), OASIS (0.630), GCS (0.452), and Charlson (0.580) (all DeLong p < 0.001). The AUC of BAR was slightly lower than that of APS III (0.728), but the difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.24). (Supplementary file 7, Table S6 and Fig. S1).

Subgroup analysis

We further assessed the association between BAR and all-cause mortality at 28 day, and 180 day across various subgroups of patients with liver cirrhosis. We selected the following as research variables for the stratified subgroup analysis: Age, gender, esophagealvariceal bleeding, AKI, sepsis, hypertension, diabetes, heart failure, epatic encephalopathy, CRRT, and ventilation (Fig. 6). Forest plots demonstrated no significant interactions between BAR and most subgroups (p > 0.05). However, a strong interaction was observed at day 28 specifically in patients with sepsis and those receiving CRRT ( p < 0.001). Meanwhile, a similar interaction pattern was observed for 180-day all-cause mortality. These findings supported the robustness of the main conclusions.

Discussion

In this large ICU cohort of liver cirrhosis patients, we observed that a higher blood urea nitrogen-to-albumin ratio (BAR) at admission was significantly associated with both short- and long-term mortality. Patients with elevated BAR (≥ 9.25) demonstrated markedly higher crude 28-day mortality (46.5% vs. 17.5%) and 180-day mortality (54.0% vs. 24.9%). Importantly, the associations persisted after multivariable adjustment (HR 1.65 for 28-day and 1.41 for 180-day mortality). In this study, we chose 180-day all-cause mortality as a secondary endpoint to capture the longer-term prognostic implications of the BAR. While short-term mortality is critically important in the ICU setting, a longer follow-up period can provide valuable insights into the sustained impact of the physiological derangements present at admission on patients with underlying chronic liver disease. As shown in Fig. 3, the survival curves begin to plateau after approximately 60 days, suggesting that the major impact of the acute illness on mortality occurs within the first two months. However, the continued (albeits lower), divergence of the curves up to 180 days indicates that the initial risk stratification by BAR also has relevance for longer-term survival in this population with a high burden of chronic disease. In terms of predictive performance, BAR achieved an AUC of 0.752 for 28-day mortality, which was not significantly different from that of APS III (p = 0.58) but outperformed other commonly used severity scores. Similar findings were observed for 180-day mortality. These results suggest that BAR is a simple and effective tool for early risk stratification, with predictive capability at least as good as complex scoring systems. Notably, the threshold effect observed in the RCS analysis (around 9.25) coincides closely with the optimal cut-off value identified by ROC analysis. This convergence reinforces that a BAR level of 9.25 may serve as a clinically meaningful boundary for identifying patients at significantly elevated risk of short- and long-term mortality.

BAR integrates two dimensions of pathophysiology central to cirrhosis outcomes. Blood urea nitrogen reflects renal function and catabolic stress [31], while hypoalbuminemia captures both nutritional status and systemic inflammation [32].

BUN is a nitrogen-containing substance found in plasma, and it serves as an important indicator for evaluating renal function, nutritional status, blood volume, and protein metabolism in the body [33]. Increased levels of BUN are often noted in critically ill individuals, including patients with acute ischemic stroke, diabetes, and acute heart failure, and acute coronary syndrome [34–36]. BUN is the primary end product of protein metabolism. Under normal physiological conditions, it is filtered by the glomeruli and subsequently reabsorbed by the renal tubules. However, insufficient renal perfusion or significantly impaired renal function results in the accumulation of BUN, which serves as an indicator of the extent of renal damage. In critically ill patients with cirrhosis, renal insufficiency is common, and hepatorenal syndrome may even be present [37]. Our study revealed that the incidence of AKI in cirrhotic patients admitted to the ICU was as high as 85.4%.

ALB not only indicates the nutritional status of the body but also plays essential roles in preserving intravascular colloid osmolality while providing anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects [38]. Due to the liver’s diminished ability to synthesize proteins, individuals suffering from liver cirrhosis often exhibit lower serum albumin levels (hypoalbuminemia). Furthermore, liver cirrhosis not only results in decreased albumin levels but also induces significant alterations in its molecular structure. These alterations are associated with the severity of cirrhosis and negatively affect the diverse functions of albumin [39]. In individuals suffering from decompensated cirrhosis, the ability of albumin to bind with numerous ligands is markedly impaired. Consequently, both quantitative and qualitative alterations in albumin can lead to a substantial decrease in the level of “effective albumin” (eAlb) present in the bloodstream [40]. The disruption of these functions in individuals suffering from hypoalbuminemia can result in increased death rates both during hospitalization and over the long term. Lower levels of albumin are indicative of chronic or significant malnutrition and inflammation, frequently associated with negative prognoses and outcomes [41]. A meta-analysis reveals that hypoalbuminemia serves as an independent predictor of long-term mortality in patients with AIS [42].

BAR is a multifunctional composite parameter that integrates the clinical values of BUN and albumin. It reflects a patient’s comprehensive status, including renal and liver function, systemic inflammation, nutritional status, endothelial function, and blood volume. Due to this integrative nature, BAR has emerged as a prognostic tool in critical care. For instance, Zhang et al. demonstrated that an elevated BAR(≥ 10.42) independently predicts mortality in ICU patients with acute ischemic stroke [43]. Zhao et al. found that increased levels of BAR upon admission to the ICU were associated with a greater risk of four-year all-cause mortality in patients with AMI, implying that BAR acts as a standalone predictor [44]. Nam et al. found a strong association between BAR and different forms of cerebral small-vessel disease in people undergoing health assessments. BAR serves as a more dependable predictor compared to BUN or ALB individually [45]. Our findings demonstrate a positive correlation between serum BAR levels and both 28-day and 180-day risk of all -cause mortality in patients with liver cirrhosis, even after accounting for various potential confounding factors. Early assessment of the BAR can help clinicians identify patients at high risk for poor outcomes and enable timely interventions to improve prognosis. Several studies have similarly investigated the prognostic value of BAR in cirrhotic patients. Zhang et al.focused on HBV-associated decompensated cirrhosis (n = 161) [46], they found that the predictive power of BAR was superior to that of the other four albumin-related biomarkers and similar to that of MELD score. while Duan et al. examined ICU patients with cirrhosis and ascites (n = 1,516) [47]. Our study extends these findings in several important aspects. First, we included all adult cirrhotic patients admitted to the ICU regardless of etiology or complications (n = 3,268), enhancing generalizability to real-world practice. Second, our extended follow-up to 180 days provides more comprehensive prognostic information compared to the 30- and 90-day outcomes assessed previously. Third, we found the mortality risk increased only modestly at BAR levels below approximately 9.25, but rose steeply and nonlinearly once BAR exceeded this threshold, indicating a potential threshold effect. Fourth, our analyses incorporated extensive adjustments for illness severity scores, treatments, and comorbidities, strengthening BAR’s independence as a predictor. Finally, utilizing MIMIC-IV version 3.0 (2008–2022) ensures our findings reflect contemporary ICU management.

In our subgroup analyses, we observed significant interactions between BAR and both sepsis and CRRT use (both p for interaction < 0.001), suggesting that the predictive value of BAR may vary across these high-risk subgroups. Sepsis is a powerful physiological insult that independently elevates BUN (through acute kidney injury and catabolic stress) and reduces albumin (through capillary leakage, decreased synthesis, and increased degradation) [48]. In septic cirrhotic patients, BAR may therefore reflect not only the severity of underlying liver disease but also the superimposed burden of sepsis-related organ dysfunction. The significant interaction (p < 0.001) suggests that the prognostic value of BAR may be amplified in septic patients, as a high BAR in this context captures a “double hit” of both cirrhosis-related and sepsis-related pathophysiology [49]. Clinically, this implies that an elevated BAR in a septic cirrhotic patient should raise particular concern and prompt aggressive management of both the infection and its sequelae. CRRT directly lowers BUN levels through dialysis, artificially reducing the BAR value and potentially masking the patient’s true endogenous renal function and catabolic state. Therefore, in patients receiving CRRT, the calculated BAR may not accurately reflect their underlying physiological status. Moreover, patients requiring CRRT represent the sickest subgroup, with extremely high baseline mortality risk [50]. Clinically, this suggests that BAR should be interpreted with caution in patients already on CRRT, and its predictive value may be best utilized before renal replacement therapy is initiated. In specific high-risk subgroups-particularly those with sepsis or requiring CRRT-its interpretation should be contextualized.

BAR integrates two dimensions of pathophysiology that are central to cirrhosis outcomes, however, its interpretation in this population requires nuanced consideration of cirrhosis-specific metabolic alterations. While BUN is conventionally viewed as a marker of renal function, its interpretation in cirrhotic patients is more complex. BUN represents the end product of protein catabolism, synthesized by the liver via the urea cycle and excreted by the kidneys [33]. In cirrhosis, several unique factors influence BUN levels. First, hepatic urea synthesis capacity is substantially impaired, Rudman et al. demonstrated that maximal urea synthesis rate in cirrhotic patients averages only 27 mg urea N/h per kg metabolic weight, less than half of normal values [51]. This impairment means that even with significant protein loading or catabolism, BUN may not rise appropriately, potentially masking the severity of liver dysfunction. Second, gastrointestinal bleeding-a frequent complication of portal hypertension-delivers a massive protein load to the gut, markedly elevating BUN [52]. Third, hypercatabolism in advanced cirrhosis increases endogenous protein breakdown, contributing to BUN elevation [53]. While reduced hepatic synthesis is fundamental-albumin production may decrease to 30–50% of normal in decompensated cirrhosis [54], several other mechanisms contribute. Expanded distribution volume is critical: cirrhosis is characterized by plasma volume expansion due to sodium and water retention, which dilutes albumin concentration independent of synthesis [55]. Systemic inflammation further depresses albumin through capillary leakage, increased catabolism [38]. Moreover, as noted by Domenicali et al., cirrhosis induces qualitative alterations in albumin’s molecular structure, impairing its binding, antioxidant, and detoxification functions [36]. The significance of BAR differs across clinical contexts. In heart failure, BAR primarily reflects cardiorenal syndrome and hemodilution, with BUN elevation driven by reduced cardiac output and neurohormonal activation [18, 19]. In acute ischemic stroke, BAR indicates dehydration, catabolic stress, and perhaps renal hypoperfusion [43]. In sepsis, BAR captures the combined effects of acute kidney injury, capillary leakage, and inflammatory consumption [21]. In cirrhosis, however, BAR uniquely integrates hepatic synthetic failure, portal hypertension-related complications, hepatorenal interactions, and systemic inflammation of liver disease. This liver-specific context makes BAR particularly valuable in cirrhotic patients, as it simultaneously captures multiple dimensions of disease progression. A notable observation from Table 1 warrants specific discussion: while BUN levels differed markedly between the low- and high-BAR groups (15 mg/dL vs. 45 mg/dL), albumin levels were surprisingly similar (2.94 ± 0.59 vs. 2.84 ± 0.68). This pattern reflects the distinct pathophysiology that BAR captures in cirrhotic patients. First, albumin exhibits a “floor effect” in decompensated cirrhosis-by the time patients require ICU admission, hepatic synthetic capacity is already maximally impaired, leaving little room for further decline. Second, BUN captures acute superimposed events such as acute kidney injury (90.4% vs. 80.5%), gastrointestinal bleeding (33.5% vs. 30.1%), and sepsis (81.0% vs. 70.0%), which are the primary drivers of short-term mortality. Thus, BAR in cirrhosis functions primarily as a marker of acute superimposed insults rather than chronic nutritional status.

Nonetheless, the research has certain limitations. First, the retrospective cohort design may lead to selection bias, given that the researchers depended on existing data and records, which may include inaccuracies, utilizing data from electronic health records, there may exist possible inaccuracies in how data is entered, and missing information that could influence the validity of the study. Second, BAR was calculated using only the first measurement within 24 h of ICU admission. We did not account for dynamic changes in BAR during the ICU stay, which might provide additional prognostic information. Third, although we adjusted for a comprehensive set of covariates, we could not fully account for certain cirrhosis-specific factors such as pre-admission nutritional status, precise etiology of cirrhosis (e.g., viral, alcoholic, NASH), or quantitative measures of liver function reserve (e.g., MELD-Na score components beyond those available). These unmeasured factors may confound the observed association. While we adjusted for several nutritional proxies including BMI, hemoglobin, and platelet count, we lacked pre-admission nutritional assessment data. Malnutrition is highly prevalent in cirrhosis and may influence both BAR components. Although our sensitivity analyses using available nutritional proxies suggested that the BAR-mortality association remained robust, we cannot entirely exclude residual confounding by nutritional status. Fourth, our study spanned 14 years (2008–2022), during which advances in cirrhosis management may have influenced outcomes. While we did not adjust for admission year in our primary analysis, the consistency of our findings with prior MIMIC-IV studies that similarly did not account for temporal trends suggests that this limitation is unlikely to have fundamentally altered our conclusions. Nonetheless, future studies should explicitly evaluate whether the predictive performance of BAR varies across different time periods. In addition, while the MIMIC-IV database provides high-quality, granular data from a single academic medical center in the United States, this also limits the generalizability of our findings. The patient population, clinical practices, and outcomes may differ in other regions or healthcare settings. Therefore, our results require external validation in independent cohorts from diverse geographic locations and healthcare systems. Finally, due to the observational nature of this study, we can only establish associations, not causality. Prospective multicenter studies are needed to confirm the prognostic value of BAR in critically ill cirrhotic patients and to evaluate whether BAR-guided risk stratification can improve clinical decision-making and patient outcomes.

Conclusion

In this large retrospective cohort study of critically ill patients with liver cirrhosis, we demonstrated that the blood urea nitrogen-to-albumin ratio (BAR) at ICU admission is independently associated with both 28-day and 180-day all-cause mortality. BAR exhibited a threshold effect at 9.25, beyond which mortality risk increased sharply and nonlinearly. Its predictive performance was comparable to the complex APS III score and superior to SOFA, OASIS, GCS, and Charlson comorbidity index. However, future studies that focus on prospective research are essential to confirm our results and clarify the relationship between BAR and the risk of mortality in this population.
