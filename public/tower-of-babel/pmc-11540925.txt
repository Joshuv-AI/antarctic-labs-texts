A new mouse model for PRPH2 pattern dystrophy exhibits functional compensation prior and subsequent to retinal degeneration
Authors: Breyanna L Cavanaugh, Michelle L Milstein, R Casey Boucher, Sharon X Tan, Mario W Hanna, Adam Seidel, Rikard Frederiksen, Thomas L Saunders, Alapakkam P Sampath, Kenneth P Mitton, Dao-Qi Zhang, Andrew F X Goldberg
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractMutations in PRPH2 are a relatively common cause of sight-robbing inherited retinal degenerations (IRDs). Peripherin-2 (PRPH2) is a photoreceptor-specific tetraspanin protein that structures the disk rim membranes of rod and cone outer segment (OS) organelles, and is required for OS morphogenesis. PRPH2 is noteworthy for its broad spectrum of disease phenotypes; both inter- and intra-familial heterogeneity have been widely observed and this variability in disease expression and penetrance confounds efforts to understand genotype–phenotype correlations and pathophysiology. Here we report the generation and initial characterization of a gene-edited animal model for PRPH2 disease associated with a nonsense mutation (c.1095:C>A, p.Y285X), which is predicted to truncate the peripherin-2 C-terminal domain. Young (P21) Prph2Y285X/WT mice developed near-normal photoreceptor numbers; however, OS membrane architecture was disrupted, OS protein levels were reduced, and in vivo and ex vivo electroretinography (ERG) analyses found that rod and cone photoreceptor function were each severely reduced. Interestingly, ERG studies also revealed that rod-mediated downstream signaling (b-waves) were functionally compensated in the young animals. This resiliency in retinal function was retained at P90, by which time substantial IRD-related photoreceptor loss had occurred. Altogether, the current studies validate a new mouse model for investigating PRPH2 disease pathophysiology, and demonstrate that rod and cone photoreceptor function and structure are each directly and substantially impaired by the Y285X mutation. They also reveal that Prph2 mutations can induce a functional compensation that resembles homeostatic plasticity, which can stabilize rod-derived signaling, and potentially dampen retinal dysfunction during some PRPH2-associated IRDs.

Introduction

Mutations in PRPH2 trigger inherited retinal degenerations (IRDs) that are amongst the most common (3%–4.6%) [1, 2] and clinically heterogeneous [3] reported for genes causing non-syndromic IRDs. The broad disease spectrum hinders a neat rationalization of genotype–phenotype associations, and prevents a clear understanding of the mechanisms that drive disease pathogenesis. Although some pathogenic mutations produce a relatively narrow range of disease phenotypes, the majority include substantial inter- and intrafamilial heterogeneity [3–5], indicating that environmental and/or genetic modifiers can interact with particular alleles to alter disease etiology. To understand and predict the likely impacts of particular PRPH2 mutations on retinal function and health, a more complete knowledge of peripherin-2 protein structure/function and an improved understanding of how other contributing factors affect disease phenotype and penetrance are needed.

The PRPH2 gene (TSPAN22) encodes PRPH2, commonly known as peripherin-2 [6], an integral membrane tetraspanin that generates and maintains the membrane curvature that shapes rod and cone OS disk rims [7]. It does so by assembling into an extended tridentate scaffold [8], which includes the homologous interaction partner rom1 [9, 10]. Multi-step assembly of peripherin-2 and rom1 is mediated by each protein’s extracellular-2 (EC2) domain [10, 11], and an early study identified defective subunit assembly as a proximal cause for PRPH2-associated retinitis pigmentosa (RP) [12]. Numerous engineered mouse models have since highlighted the importance of EC2 integrity for peripherin-2 oligomerization and for support of OS structure and photoreceptor viability [13, 14].

The protein’s cytoplasmic C-terminus has received relatively less attention, despite being the site of numerous pathogenic mutations (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/clinvar/prph2). The bulk of the C-terminal domain is intrinsically disordered [15], but its central region includes a membrane-active inducible amphipathic helix [16, 17]. This feature participates in protein trafficking and targeting to OS disk rims [18, 19], and it is important for regulating peripherin-2 membrane shaping activity [7]. Because the significance of the peripherin-2 C-terminus remains a key knowledge gap for understanding protein structure/function and the etiology of PRPH2-associated diseases, we have created and initiated characterization of a new gene-edited mouse model for instances of pattern dystrophy associated with a PRPH2 nonsense mutation (c.1095:C>A, p.Y285X), predicted to completely truncate the protein’s C-terminal domain [20]. The studies reported here confirm Y285X pathogenicity, and demonstrate that loss of OS structural integrity severely compromises both rod and cone photoreceptor light responses in young animals prior to significant photoreceptor cell loss. Despite significantly reduced rod photoreceptor function, downstream b-wave signaling was largely preserved This result introduces the possibility that a functional compensation that resembles homeostatic plasticity may help to preserve vision during PRPH2-associated retinal disease.

Results

Generation of Prph2Y285X gene-edited mice

Previous PRPH2 disease models have focused on missense mutations within the EC2 domain, which has long been known to mediate protein self-assembly [11, 12]. Although numerous pathogenic mutations impact the protein’s cytoplasmic C-terminus and cause a variety of IRDs, the in vivo significance of this domain remains to be elucidated. To address this knowledge gap, we created a new PRPH2 mouse model, which incorporates a nonsense mutation (c.1095:C>A, p.Y285X) implicated in instances of human pattern dystrophy [20].

CRISPR/Cas9 gene editing was used to generate a guide RNA (Fig. 1A) that included the in-frame nonsense codon, and three silent mutations that introduced a new restriction site, to facilitate PCR identification of founder mice (Fig. 1B). Founders were confirmed by DNA sequencing (Fig. 1C), and then were backcrossed to generate a congenic line on the commonly used C57BL/6J strain. The mutated Prph2 allele was predicted to truncate the protein at Y285, completely eliminating the cytoplasmic C-terminal domain (Fig. 1D). Heterozygous and homozygous Prph2Y285X mice were of normal physical appearance, behavior, litter size, sex ratios, and body mass.

Truncation of the peripherin-2 C-terminus prevents stable protein expression and reduces levels of wild-type (WT) peripherin-2 and of interacting proteins

Protein content in P21 retinas from Prph2Y285X mice and their wild-type (WT) controls, as well as from age-matched retinal degeneration slow (rds) mice, was assessed by western blotting. The rds strain (Prph2rds) includes a null mutation in Prph2 [21, 22], and is a common control for investigating engineered Prph2 mice [13]. The rds defect is caused by a spontaneous mutation (insertion of viral DNA) that disrupts Prph2 exon II preventing mRNA nuclear export, and protein expression [23, 24]. Figure 2A shows that levels of full-length peripherin-2 protein (~39 kDa) in retinal extracts from Prph2Y285X/WT disease model mice were reduced more than 2-fold vs. WT controls, when probed with an antibody directed to the protein’s N-terminus. Interestingly, no truncated protein product was detected at the predicted mobility of 32.6 kDa. In addition, no peripherin-2 whatsoever was detected in retinal extracts from homozygous Prph2Y285X/Y285X mice.

Primary antibody details are provided in Table S1. Statistically significant differences were calculated using t-tests (*P < 0.05). Together, these data suggest that the Prph2Y285X allele produces an unstable product that can reduce abundance of the full-length protein produced by the WT allele.

We next utilized a C-terminal-directed antibody that lacks reactivity with truncated protein to specifically assay the full-length product. We again found levels of full-length peripherin-2 reduced more than 2-fold in each of the disease model mice relative to WT, with levels reduced in Prph2Y285X/WT > Prph2rds/WT animals (Fig. 2B). Finally, we assayed for full-length peripherin-2 expression in homozygous Prph2Y285X/Y285X and Prph2rds/rds mice, and found no detectable protein whatsoever. Altogether, these findings indicate that the Prph2Y285X allele does not generate measurable levels of truncated peripherin-2 protein, and may slightly reduce steady-state levels of the full-length protein produced by the WT allele in heterozygous animals.

We next assayed levels of the peripherin-2 interactors—rom1 and glutamic acid-rich proteins (GARPs). Rom-1 is a peripherin-2 homolog that forms non-covalent heterodimers with peripherin-2 EC2 domain interactions [10], while GARPs associate with peripherin-2 at disk rims by interacting with its C-terminus [17, 25]. In the Prph2Y285X/WT disease model, rom1 was present at ~50%, GARPs were present at ~40%, and the most abundant OS protein, rhodopsin, was present at ~50% of WT protein levels (Fig. 2C–E). In homozygous Prph2Y285X/Y285X mice, only very minor levels of rom1 and GARPS could be detected (~10% of WT).

Prph2Y285X/WT disease model mice develop near-normal numbers of rod and cone photoreceptors

Because the reduced levels of photoreceptor proteins present in Prph2Y285X/WT retinas could in part be due to fewer cells, we assayed photoreceptor numbers in mutant retinas. Figure 3A shows images of hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) stained ocular tissue sections from P21 Prph2Y285X homo- and heterozygotes, compared to Prph2rds homo- and heterozygotes, and WT controls. Figure 3B presents corresponding spider plots, which compare outer nuclear layer (ONL) thickness between the five genotypes. Photoreceptor numbers in the Prph2Y285X/WT disease model were not significantly different from WT in the central retina (blue circles vs. black squares), the region most comparable to the human macula in the murine retina [26]. In contrast, homozygosity for Prph2Y285X or Prph2rds reduced ONL thickness by ~25% at P21. Combined with the protein expression data (Fig. 2), these results demonstrate that the substantial loss of peripherin-2 in the Prph2Y285X/WT disease model mice cannot be a sole consequence of reduced photoreceptor numbers.

Flat-mounted retinal explants, structured illumination microscopy, and automated image analysis were utilized to count cone OSs (as a proxy for cone cells) in reconstructed 3D volumes. Photoreceptor ultrastructure, but not protein localization, is affected in a subtype and dose-dependent manner

Because peripherin-2 and its protein interactors are important for normal OS organelle structure, we used transmission electron microscopy (TEM) to examine rod and cone ultrastructure of the heterozygous Prph2Y285X/WT disease model and of the homozygous Prph2Y285X/Y285X mice. We observed robust and distinctive phenotypes for each of the Prph2Y285X mutant genotypes. In contrast to the well-organized rod OSs of WT mice, the Prph2Y285X/WT disease model produced a variety of dysmorphic rod OS membrane shapes, with laminar whorls being the most common (panels B, B′ vs. A). Cone OS structures in the Prph2Y285X/WT mutant were less profoundly disrupted than were those of rods (panels D, D′ vs. B, B′). The extent of rod and cone OS disruption in the new model was not noticeably different in form or extent to that which we have observed previously for Prph2rds/WT mice on the C57BL/6J background [27].

Far larger impacts on OS structure were observed in homozygous Prph2 Y285X/Y285X mutant retinas (panels E, E′). In this case, only ciliary stubs, often surrounded by vesicles and retinal pigment epithelia (RPE) apical membrane processes, were observed. This phenotype was not noticeably different from that which we (and others) have previously observed for Prph2rds/rds animals [27]. Altogether, the findings demonstrate that the Prph2Y285X defect compromises OS ultrastructure in a photoreceptor subtype- and dose-dependent fashion. In the new Prph2Y285X/WT disease model, both rods and cone OS structures were affected, with rods experiencing more substantial disorganization than cones.

Because disruptions of OS structure can affect photoreceptor protein transport and localization, we performed IHC analyses of peripherin-2 and interactors, using fixed ocular tissue sections, previously validated antibody reagents (Table S1), and laser scanning confocal microscopy (LSCM). Figure 4 (RH panel) shows maximum projection images that localize peripherin-2, rom1, GARPs, and rhodopsin in retinal sections from Prph2Y285X/WT and control animals. Despite significant disruption of rod and cone OS ultrastructures, normal targeting and localization of peripherin-2, rom1, and GARP proteins were retained in the disease model mice. A slight mislocalization of rhodopsin into photoreceptor cell bodies was observed in very occasional cells.

In vivo rod photoreceptor function is severely compromised, but rod-derived downstream signaling is preserved

Given the loss of OS structure and protein content seen in the Prph2Y285X/WT disease model, we hypothesized that retinal function would be compromised by a reduction in rod-driven signaling. Figure 5A shows in vivo scotopic ERG photoresponses from P21 Prph2Y285X/WT mutant mice, relative to those recorded from Prph2WT/WT littermates and Prph2rds/WT age-matched controls. Representative traces show a dramatic reduction in rod-driven a-wave amplitudes for each of the mutants. The intensity-response plot (Fig. 5B) illustrates that the Prph2Y285X/WT disease model, like the heterozygous null Prph2rds/WT mouse, lost ~51% of its scotopic a-wave responses. Despite the ~2-fold reduction in rod a-wave amplitudes, downstream signaling as reflected by b-wave amplitudes (primarily mediated by rod bipolar cells; RBCs), was preserved in the Prph2Y285X/WT mutant (Fig. 5C). A similar behavior was observed for the Prph2rds/WT mice at all but the highest flash intensity. Figure 5D shows the substantial increase in b-wave/a-wave ratio for each Prph2 mutant line relative to WT, particularly at reduced luminance. These data imply a potentiation of rod-RBC signaling, which was unexpected because such behavior has not been described for Prph2 models previously.

We also recorded in vivo ERG b-waves under photopic conditions for each of the three genotypes (Fig. 5E). The paucity of cone numbers in the murine retina limits a-wave amplitudes and precludes reliable a-wave analyses. Plots of b-wave amplitudes as a function of flash luminance (Fig. 5F) show that Prph2Y285X/WT disease model photopic responses were indistinguishable from WT, while those produced by Prph2rds/WT mice were slightly reduced from WT at the two highest flash intensities.

In sum, in vivo ERG recordings from the Prph2Y285X/WT disease model demonstrate a preservation of rod-driven b-wave responses, despite a greater than 2-fold reduction of a-waves. Cone b-wave responses were likewise unaffected in the Prph2Y285X/WT mutant mice; however, technical limitations prevented assessment of possible mutant impact on cone a-waves.

Ex vivo ERG finds that rod and cone photoreceptor function are each severely compromised, but rod-derived downstream signaling is preserved

We were intrigued by the preservation of rod-mediated b-waves in the Prph2 disease model mice, and were also motivated to explore cone-mediated responses more fully, because cone a-waves have not been reported for any Prph2 mutant mice to date. We therefore recorded ex vivo transretinal ERGs, with and without pharmacological blockade of photoreceptor-to-bipolar cell signal transmission, to isolate a-wave and b-wave components under scotopic and photopic conditions.

Scotopic flash series traces (Fig. 6A) and intensity-response plots (Fig. 6B) show large a-wave amplitude reductions (~66%) and significant, but smaller impacts on b-wave amplitudes (~30%) for the Prph2Y285X/WT disease model vs. WT. Photopic flash series traces (Fig. 6C) and intensity-response plots (Fig. 6D) also show substantial a-wave amplitude reductions (~50%), but lesser (non-significant) impacts on b-wave amplitudes. These data are noteworthy, because they (to our knowledge) represent the first cone photoreceptor a-wave recordings reported for a Prph2 mutant animal model. Moreover, they provide a first demonstration that cone function is severely compromised in young animals. This was unexpected because the Prph2rds/WT mouse cone ERG has been reported to be spared relative to the rod ERG [24]. Furthermore, Fig. 6E (left) shows that the scotopic b-wave/a-wave ratio was significantly elevated for the Prph2Y285X/WT mutant over WT, which reflects a robust functional compensation that preserves rod-driven signaling. Figure 6E (right) shows that photopic b-wave/a-wave ratio was also increased for Prph2Y285X/WT vs. WT; however, these differences did not reach statistical significance. Altogether, the ex vivo ERG data are consistent with and reinforce the findings obtained using in vivo ERG, and suggest that loss of rod function in pre-degenerative mice may trigger a change in rod-RBC coupling, which provides functional compensation and preserves downstream signaling.

The source of b-wave preservation remains to be identified

As a first step towards understanding the mechanism of functional compensation, we examined several outer plexiform layer (OPL) features, the region where photoreceptor-bipolar cell (BiP) synaptic transmission occurs. Histological analysis found no significant alteration in OPL layer thickness (Fig. 7A). Although a previous study reported increased synaptic ribbon numbers in degenerating Prph2rds/WT mice [29], no significant changes in rod photoreceptor ribbon numbers were found for Prph2Y285X/WT or Prph2rds/WT P21 mice in this study (Fig. 7B). Finally, IHC/LSCM analysis found no changes apparent in BiP cell dendritic structures (Fig. 7C).

The Prph2Y285X/WT model degenerates with age and loses additional rod function, but maintains b-wave functional compensation

Because the PRPH2Y285X mutation has been associated with progressive retinal disease, we sought to determine whether aged Prph2Y285X/WT mice had undergone IRD by analyzing ONL thickness in stained ocular tissue sections from P90 animals. The data show a significant reduction in ONL thickness compared to WT (Fig. 8A), and a roughly 40% loss of photoreceptors between P21 and P90 (Fig. 8B). These findings confirm this line as a new model for IRD. Because PRPH2Y285X disease includes a progressive loss of visual function [20], we evaluated in vivo scotopic ERG function at P90. The data (Fig. 8C) show that scotopic a-wave amplitudes in the aged Prph2Y285X/WT mutant mice were reduced ~65%, relative to those of WT littermates. This indicates a worsening of rod function with age, given that P21 animals showed a ~51% reduction relative to WT (Fig. 5B). Finally, because a remarkable preservation of in vivo ERG scotopic b-wave responses was seen at P21, we also analyzed b-waves (Fig. 8D) and b/a-wave ratios at P90 as a function of luminance (Fig. 8E), and then did a comparison to P21 (Fig. 8F). The elevated b/a-wave ratios seen in the pre-degenerative Prph2Y285X/WT P21 mice were retained in the P90 animals, which had lost > 40% of their photoreceptors. This finding indicates that the rod-mediated functional compensation present at P21 was retained with aging and IRD, and may reflect a form of homeostatic plasticity.

Discussion

Although mutations in PRPH2 are a relatively common cause of IRDs, disease pathophysiology is not well understood and effective treatments are not yet available. Here we generated and validated a novel Y285X Prph2 gene-edited mouse model for human IRD, as a first step towards understanding disease natural history. Our phenotyping of young (P21) Prph2Y285X/WT animals documented near normal photoreceptor numbers, but dysmorphic photoreceptor OSs that contained less than haploinsufficient levels of full-length peripherin-2, and a complete lack of truncated peripherin-2. The abnormal OS structures severely compromised rod and cone ERG a-waves; however, the young animals showed a remarkable preservation of b-waves. Although aged (P90) mice showed significant photoreceptor loss, they too showed b-wave preservation and functional compensation. Because Prph2rds/WT mice displayed similar behavior, the overall findings suggest the possibility that pre-degenerative loss of photoreceptor signaling caused by dysmorphic OSs can induce a form of homeostatic plasticity that strengthens rod-RBC coupling and preserves retinal signaling.

The importance of peripherin-2 for rod and cone OS structure has long been known [6,30,31]; however, its molecular function was only recently demonstrated. Peripherin-2 generates membrane curvature via a multi-step self-assembly mechanism mediated by its EC2 domain [7, 8, 10]. This functional activity directly shapes OS disk rim curvature, and it is required for normal disk morphogenesis and for mature disk stability. This new understanding explains how PRPH2 mutations can disrupt OS architecture in a genetically dominant and dose-dependent manner to trigger IRDs. It does not however provide more detailed insights into disease pathophysiology or the broad phenotypic disease spectrum reported for PRPH2 mutations. In this regard, it is helpful to note that several knock-in mouse models of EC2 missense mutations show residue-specific losses of protein structure, stability, and/or interactions that can produce distinctive retinal phenotypes [13,32]. Although the peripherin-2 C-terminus is also affected by numerous pathogenic mutations (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/clinvar/prph2), a lack of animal model natural history studies has limited understanding of how defects in this domain manifest in vivo.

The Y285X gene-edited mouse model described here was predicted to truncate peripherin-2 and delete the protein’s entire C-terminal domain. Instead, we found that retinas from Prph2Y285X/WT mice did not contain any truncated Y285X products, and that retinas from Prph2Y285X/Y285X mice did not contain any peripherin-2 at all. We further observed that levels of WT peripherin-2 in the heterozygous Prph2Y285X/WT mice were reduced slightly below those of haploinsufficient Prph2rds/WT mice, though differences did not reach statistical significance. Because both Prph2Y285X/WT and Prph2rds/WT mice possessed near normal photoreceptor numbers at P21, we conclude that the Y285X allele prevents stable protein expression. Although data is not available on this point, the lack of mutant gene product could potentially be a product of cell autonomous nonsense-mediated decay and/or protein-level quality control mechanisms, since it occurred prior to the onset of significant retinal degeneration. The results also suggest the possibility that small quantities of truncated and unstable Y285X protein may assemble with and lead to degradation of a portion of the full-length protein pool produced by the WT allele. Similar findings were reported in a C214S transgenic mouse study, which found a complete absence/loss-of-function of mutant gene product [33]. Expression of C214S on the Prph2rds/WT background slightly reduced levels of protein produced by the WT allele, but like Prph2Y285X/WT, it did not appear to produce other dominant negative phenotypic effects.

Reduced peripherin-2 levels in Prph2Y285X/WT mice produced disruptions of OS ultrastructure that were consistent in scope and magnitude with previous studies [34,35]. Because peripherin-2 functions in a stoichiometric manner to generate OS disk rim curvature and internalize OS disks [7,36], the losses of normal OS ultrastructure can be rationalized by a simple model in which inadequate protein levels impede timely completion of these processes. OS disk morphogenesis is thought to require two sequential steps [37–41]. Actin-driven evagination of the OS plasma membrane creates prominin-1- [42] bounded membranous lamellae, which are remodeled by peripherin-2 during full (as in rods) or partial (as in cones) disk internalization [7,37]. Taken together, these considerations suggest that photoreceptors lack a well-coupled feedback mechanism that can adequately adjust membrane evagination rates in the event that disk rim formation and internalization are significantly slowed or interrupted by inadequate peripherin-2 availability.

Reduced peripherin-2 levels were accompanied by decreased levels of other rod OS proteins, including rom1, GARPs and rhodopsin. Rom1 and GARPs rely on peripherin-2 for trafficking to the OS [43,44], so the reduced levels of those proteins was not surprising. Although some PRPH2 disease model mice do display low levels of mislocalized rhodopsin [13], the Prph2Y285X/WT mice did not. The observed reduction in their rhodopsin level was most likely a product of reduced protein density in OS membranes and/or degradation of protein not sequestered into OSs.

The most striking phenotype observed for the new Prph2Y285X/WT disease model mice was a robust preservation of scotopic signaling, despite a nearly 2-fold congenital loss of rod photoreceptor function—as reflected by in vivo ERG b- and a-waves respectively. A similar phenomenon was observed for the Prph2rds/WT line. This was surprising, because to our knowledge, functional compensation of reduced rod signaling has not been previously reported for any Prph2 animal model to date. To confirm the in vivo ERG results with an alternative technique, and investigate whether a similar preservation of downstream function also occurs under photopic conditions, we used ex vivo transretinal ERG recordings. This approach allows for the pharmacological isolation of photoreceptor and bipolar cell responses, and simultaneously provides the sensitivity needed to accurately assay cone a-wave responses in the mouse retina [45,46]. Transretinal recordings reinforced the observation of in vivo scotopic b-wave preservation, and found it was maximal at low luminance levels. Similar behavior has been documented for P23H rats [47,48], and was most recently the subject of an in-depth study using a P23H rhodopsin knock-in mouse IRD model [49]. This investigation, by Leinonen et al., suggests that early rod degeneration can induce a form of homeostatic plasticity that potentiates rod-RBC cell signaling. Homeostatic plasticity, as used here, is the concept that “destabilizing influences are counterbalanced by homeostatic plasticity mechanisms that act to stabilize neuronal and circuit activity” [50]. Interestingly (and in contrast to Leinonen, et al), we observed that functional compensation initially occurred prior to the onset of retinal degeneration (at P21). It is therefore conceivable that the initial “destabilizing influence” may have been the large drop in photoreceptor signaling that accompanied congenital OS dysmorphogenesis (vs. photoreceptor loss). Because the earliest time point used by Leinonen et al. [49] was P30, it is possible that earlier assay of P23H mice (i.e.—at P21) might produce a similar finding.

The mechanism underlying the functional compensation described here remains to be determined. We found no evidence of outer retinal rewiring, such as that induced in response to engineered photoreceptor or bipolar cell loss [51,52], nor for alterations in ribbon content, such as those described for retinas in degenerating Prph2 (formerly rds) mutant mice [29]. Instead, we speculate that the functional compensation we report here for two lines of Prph2 mutant mice may resemble that occuring in the P23H knock-in mice, in which a potentiation of rod-to-RBC signaling was observed [49]. In that case, homeostatic plasticity appears to involve strengthening of existing, rather than creation of new synapses, and the authors suggest that changes in post-synaptic scaling and/or RBC excitability may be responsible. Future studies will required to address this question for Prph2 mutant mice.

In addition to highlighting functional compensation of rod signaling, the ex vivo transretinal ERG recordings reported here also revealed that cone function is significantly compromised in Prph2 mutant mice. This contrasts with an early study of Prph2 mice, which suggested that cone function is only marginally affected by Prph2 mutations [53]. This conclusion was based an early untested assumption that the photopic b-wave is a valid proxy for the cone a-wave in Prph2 mutant mice [24]. The present findings demonstrate that rods (and possibly cones) of Prph2 mutant mice participate in a form of functional compensation in response to reduced photoreceptor signaling, and therefore photopic b-waves cannot be taken as a reliable proxy for cone a-waves. This is a particularly important point for the reporting of animal disease natural histories and pre-clinical intervention studies.

Several hundred mutations in PRPH2 have been linked to IRDs, and clinical presentations include: patterned and vitelliform macular dystrophies, central areolar choroidal dystrophy, retinitis punctate albescens, and retinitis pigmentosa (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/clinvar/prph2; https://omim.org/entry/179605?search=179605&highlight=179605). Attempts to neatly parse human genotype–phenotype relationships have not been successful; however, some clinical studies have identified important roles for genetic modifiers for generating phenotypic heterogeneity [54,55]. Animal model investigations provide a valuable complementary approach for understanding and predicting the likely impacts of particular PRPH2 mutations on retinal function and health. The new Prph2Y285X/WT knock-in mouse described here is a model for instances of IRD associated with a PRPH2 (c.1095:C>A, p.Y285X) nonsense mutation (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/clinvar/variation/98714/) for which limited data is available. Two cases have been reported for siblings of a single German family, with initial vision loss in the third decade, and diagnosis of autosomal dominant pattern (or macular) dystrophy in the fifth decade [20]. The mutation was present in both affected siblings, but not in either of two unaffected siblings. Additional cases have not been reported since (S. Kohl, personal communication). The studies presented here provide direct experimental evidence for pathogenicity of the Y285X variant, and a well-defined animal model that can be utilized to advance Prph2 natural history and intervention studies.

The finding that functional compensation can mask primary rod and cone signaling defects in Prph2 disease models by preserving downstream signaling provides a second precedent (beyond the P23H rhodopsin knock-in mouse), for a positive adaptive retinal response to IRD mutations. Additional studies will be required to clarify the extent to which the functional compensation observed for Prph2 models represents examples of homeostatic plasticity, document significance for disease natural history, and consider implications for the development of therapeutic interventions.

Materials and methods

Generation and genotyping of gene-edited mice

All experiments performed in this study were carried out with the approval of Oakland University’s Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) and conformed to the ARVO Statement for the Use of Animals in Ophthalmic and Vision Research. Mice were housed at Oakland University in a facility approved by the Association for Assessment and Accreditation of Laboratory Animal Care International (AAALAC).

Prph2 gene-edited mice were generated using the CRISPR/Cas9 system in conjunction with pronuclear microinjection into single-cell zygotes. Prph2 exon 3 was targeted for cleavage proximal to Tyr285, and a ssDNA donor template was utilized to introduce a C>A transversion, mimicking a putatively pathogenic PRPH2 mutation associated with pattern dystrophy disease [18]. An online CRISPR Design Tool (http://tools.genome-engineering.org; no longer available) was used to identify a highly specific (CFD score = 90) single guide RNA (sgRNA) target five base pairs upstream of Tyr285 with the sequence, 5′ TGTGTGGAGGTAGCGGAGTC (PAM = CGG) 3′ [56]. The sgRNA was cloned into plasmid pX330-U6-Chimeric_BB-CBh-hSpCas9 (Addgene.org plasmid #42230) as described [57]. The plasmid was mixed with a 125 nucleotide oligonucleotide (Fisher Scientific) designed to replace Tyr285 with an ochre stop codon. The oligonucleotide sequence was 5′ GCCTCATGTCTGCTCTAAGTCTCCTCTCTCCCTACCAGGTGAGCATCACTGCtGGtCTaaGgTAaCTCCACACAGCGCTGGAGAGTGTGTCTAACCCGGAGGACCCCGAGTGTGAGAGTGAGGGC 3′. Lower case letters indicate silent coding mutations that disrupt sgRNA binding and introduce a MaeIII restriction site (underlined gTAaC). Bold letters indicate the stop codon at position 285. A solution containing 5 ng/ul of circular pX330 plasmids and 10 ng/ul of donor oligonucleotides were microinjected into the pronuclei of fertilized mouse eggs obtained from B6SJLF1/J mice (Jackson Laboratory stock number 100012) and transferred to pseudopregnant B6D2F1/J mice (Jackson Laboratory stock number 100006) as described [58]. Founder mice carrying the Y285X mutation were identified via endpoint PCR (using Prph2Ex3-for: 5′-TCGCAATGTGACCATCTGCC-3′ and Prph2Ex3-rev: 5′-GTAGGTGACCAGCTTTCTCGG-3′ primers) and capillary DNA sequencing, and then were backcrossed onto the C57BL/6J strain (The Jackson Laboratory, Stock No. 000664) for more than seven generations. The congenic line was confirmed to be free of common murine mutations causing IRDs, via genotyping at the Pde6b (rd1) and Crb1 (rd8) loci. Mice were maintained under 12 h light/12 h dark light cycles with food and water provided ad libitum, and all studies used roughly equal numbers of age-matched male and female mice. For tissue collections, mice were sacrificed by asphyxiation with CO2 under normal room illumination at postnatal day 21 (P21), 3–5 h after light onset, except where otherwise noted.

Retinal histology

The superior hemisphere of each eye was marked prior to enucleation. Processing of mouse eyes for histology was performed as described [59], with slight modification. Whole eyes were fixed for 48 h at RT in modified Davison’s fixative (10% formaldehyde, 6% acetic acid, 20% isopropanol) and stored in 70% EtOH until use. Fixed tissues were embedded in paraffin, and 5 μm retina cross-sections through the optic nerve were stained with hematoxylin and eosin. Images were acquired using a 20X objective lens and Nikon Optiphot-2 equipped with SPOT digital camera and NIS Elements software. Photomicrographs from individual sections were montaged using Adobe Photoshop, and retinal layer thicknesses were measured [60], using ImageJ [61]. Measurements were initiated at 250 μm from the optic nerve, and then were repeated every 250 μm thereafter, in 14 contiguous fields along the entire retina. Three measurements were taken at each location, and averaged values were utilized for between-group statistical comparisons. A minimum of 4 animals were assessed for each group, and data were analyzed with two-way ANOVA and a Tukey post hoc test, using a significance threshold of P ≤ 0.05.

Whole mount immunohistochemistry for cone counting

Retinas were processed for retinal flat mount immunohistochemistry essentially as previously described [62]. Eyecups collected from P21 mice were fixed overnight in 4% PFA in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), then retinas were dissected and washed in PBS + 0.1% Tween, and incubated in blocking buffer (PBS, 5% donkey serum, 4% BSA). Cone OSs were labeled using antibodies diluted in blocking buffer, directed against S-opsin (Abcam ab235274) and M-opsin (Millipore AB5405), and detected with donkey α-goat Alexa Fluor 647 and donkey α-rabbit Alexa Fluor 568 secondary antibodies (Invitrogen). Primary antibodies are detailed in Table S1. After mounting with VECTASHIELD (Vector Laboratories), the retinas were imaged with a Zeiss AxioImager Z2 Apotome microscope. Z-stacks (1 μm step size) were acquired using a 40x objective, representing four anatomical positions (superior, 2 × medial, and inferior), relative to the superior to inferior gradient of S-opsin expression and the optic nerve. Cone outer segments in each ROI were counted using the 3D object counter (3DOC) ImageJ plugin [63], and medial regions for each retina were averaged. To remove noise, the counts were filtered to exclude objects < 0.5 or > 500 μm3. Results were analyzed by one-way ANOVA and Tukey’s HSD, using a significance threshold of P < 0.05, and were validated by comparison against a previously-established 2D counting method [64].

Analysis of photoreceptor ultrastructure

Detailed procedures followed those described previously [27]. Following sacrifice, mice were immediately perfused with fixative, then eyes were enucleated and anterior segments removed, and eyecups were immersion fixed for an additional 4 h to overnight. Tissues were washed, post-fixed in osmium tetroxide, washed again, then dehydrated through a graded ethanol series. Following infiltration with Polybed 812 (Electron Microscopy Sciences), samples were polymerized at 60°C for 24–48 h. Thin sections (80 μm) were cut and post-stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate. Imaging was performed using a FEI Morgagni transmission electron microscope at 100 kV, using a side-mount Hamamatsu Orca-HR digital camera driven by AMT Image Capture Engine V601 software. Individual image brightness and contrast were adjusted when needed to facilitate comparisons. At least three animals were analyzed for each genotype.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) and laser scanning confocal microscopy (LSCM)

IHC analyses were performed on ocular vibratome sections (100 μm) from wildtype and heterozygous Prph2Y285X/WT mice, essentially as described [65]. Briefly, agarose-embedded PFA-fixed sections were blocked with 5% normal goat serum in IHC buffer containing: 1× PBS, 0.2% Triton X-100, 0.5% BSA, 0.02% sodium azide (pH 7.4). Primary antibodies diluted in IHC buffer were added to the sections for 36–48 hrs at 4°C. Antibodies were reactive against peripherin-2, rom1, glutamic acid rich proteins (GARPs), Na+,K+-ATPase, Protein Kinase C (PKC) and rhodopsin, and are detailed in Table S1. After washing, sections were incubated for 24 h (4°C) in IHC buffer containing goat α-mouse Alexa Fluor 488 and goat α-rabbit Alexa Fluor 568 secondary antibodies (Invitrogen), and Hoechst 33342 nuclear stain (Sigma). Washed sections were mounted on glass slides, using glass coverslips and SlowFade Glass Soft-Set Anti-Fade mounting media (Invitrogen). A Nikon C1 LSCM was used to acquire sub-saturated image Z-stack images, using a 60x oil objective (1.4 N.A.) and 30 μm confocal aperture, with 70 nm pixel and 0.25–0.5 μm Z-step sizes. Unless otherwise indicated, presented images are single optical sections taken from a Z-stack midpoint.

Western blotting

Mice were sacrificed by CO2 asphyxiation and eyes were immediately enucleated. Retinas were dissected and flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen, then stored at −80°C until use. Retinal homogenates were prepared by mechanical disruption in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) solution containing 8 M urea, 10 mM DTT, and protease inhibitors, using a plastic microtube pestle driven by a hand-held homogenizer (Kontes). Homogenates were centrifuged at 14 000 × g to remove insoluble material, and supernatant protein concentrations were determined as the average of triplicate A280 measurements using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Samples were subjected to SDS-PAGE (10% acrylamide; Bio-Rad) and western blotting, essentially as previously described [7]. The primary antibodies used are detailed in Table S1. Proteins were detected using near-infrared secondary antibodies IRDye 680RD and IRDye 800CW (Li-Cor Biosciences). Image acquisition and densitometry/quantitation were conducted using a Sapphire Biomolecular Imager and AzureSpot software (Azure Biosystems). Signals were normalized using the AzureRed total protein stain following manufacturer’s protocol (Azure Biosystems).

In vivo electroretinography

Mice were dark-adapted overnight (> 12 h) prior to electroretinography (ERG). ERGs were recorded under dim red light using a Celeris system (Diagnosys, Inc.). Pupils were dilated by application of tropicamide (1%) and phenylephrine (2.5%) to each eye for 10 min. Mice were anesthetized with isoflurane (2.5% in oxygen at 2.5 liters per minute), and a thin layer of 2.5% hypromellose (GONAK; Akorn, Inc.) was applied to each eye. Mice were placed onto a 37°C heated platform and anesthesia was maintained at reduced isoflurane (2.0% in oxygen at 2.5 liters per minute). Sterile reference and ground electrodes were introduced subcutaneously into the scalp and hind flank respectively. Stimulator/recording electrodes were positioned over each eye in contact with the hypromellose solution, and ERG stimulus/response recordings were collected alternately from each eye. Scotopic photoresponses were acquired under dark-adapted conditions, using flashes of LED white light at four increasing flash luminances (−0.8 to 1.9 log cd.s/m2). For photopic photoresponses, animals were light-adapted with rod-saturating white background light (30 cd/m2) for 8 min, and then averaged cone photoresponses were recorded, using 50 successive flashes at four increasing flash luminances (−3.68 to 1.29 log cd.s/m2). ERG responses were measured from a minimum of 10 animals for each condition, and data were analyzed with two-way ANOVA and a Tukey post hoc test for comparison of means between groups, using a significance threshold of P < 0.05.

Ex vivo electroretinography

Mice were dark-adapted overnight (>12 h) and euthanized by CO2 overdose and cervical dislocation. Under dim red illumination, right and left eyeballs were enucleated from a mouse for dark-adapted and light-ERG recording, respectively. The eyeballs were transferred to a petri dish filled with Ames’ medium (US Biological, Salem, MA) bubbled with 95% O2-5% CO2. In the medium, the whole retina was dissected from an eyeball and then placed with the ganglion cell side down in a recording chamber mounted on the stage of an upright conventional fluorescence microscope (BX51WI, Olympus, Tokyo, Japan). Oxygenated Ames’ medium continuously ran over the retina at a rate of 2–3 ml/min with a temperature of 32–34°C. ERGs were recorded with two Ag/AgCl electrodes placed in the photoreceptor and retinal ganglion cell sides, respectively. A reference electrode was inserted into an electrode space of the recording chamber connected to the ganglion cell side through a 0.1-mm hole. The recording electrode was placed on the surface of the photoreceptor layer under infrared illumination.

ERG a- and b- waveforms were elicited in the presence of 100 μM barium (to block contributions from Müller glia) by full-field light stimuli of increasing intensity at a wavelength of 470 nm (20 ms duration) with a maximum intensity (log I = 0) of 2.20 × 1016 photons/cm2/s [66]. The waveforms were amplified using a Grass amplifier (Grass Telefactor, Warwick, RI) and acquired using Clampex 10 software (Molecular Devices, Sunnyvale, CA). The same series of intensities were then used to elicit a-waves only, while the b-waves were blocked with the mGluR6 agonist L-(+)-2-Amino-4-phosphonobutyric acid (L-AP4) [67]. Dark-adapted b-waves at each light intensity were obtained by subtracting the waveform in the presence of L-AP4 from that in the absence of L-AP4. The a- or b- wave amplitudes were plotted against the log stimulus intensity to construct intensity-response curves. Alternatively, b-wave/a-wave ratios were plotted against the log stimulus intensity to generate functional compensation curves.

To obtain light-adapted ERG, we delivered steady full-field 470 nm illumination to the retina with an intensity of 3.32 × 1013 photons/cm2/s (log I = −2.82) to saturate rod-mediated responses [68]. Simultaneously, a 470 nm light pulse of 20 ms duration (log I = 0) was delivered with a repetition frequency of 1 Hz for 5 min to induce the light-adapted state of the retina. Upon light adaptation, light-adapted ERG a- and b-waves in the presence of steady background illumination were obtained with a series of light intensities with and without L-AP4 and their intensity-response curves were constructed using the procedures described above for dark-adapted ERGs.
