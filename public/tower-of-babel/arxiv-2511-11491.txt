Cubic points on dynamical modular curves
John R. Doyle, Alexander Galarraga

Abstract
. We consider the family of dynamical modular curves associated to quadratic polynomial maps and determine precisely which of these curves have infinitely many cubic points. We use this to prove a classification statement on preperiodic points for quadratic polynomials over cubic fields, extending previous work of Poonen, Faber, and the first author and Krumm.



2020 Mathematics Subject Classification
Primary 37P15, 37P35; Secondary 11G30, 14G05

1.
Introduction

Let
K
be a number field, let
\overline{K}
be its algebraic closure, and let
f(x)\in K(x)
be a rational function. Write
f^{0}
for the identity map on
\mathbb{P}^{1}
, and for each
n\geq 1
set
f^{n}:=f\circ f^{n-1}
. We say that a point
P\in\mathbb{P}^{1}(\overline{K})
is
periodic
for
f
if there exists
n\geq 1
such that
f^{n}(P)=P
; the minimal such
n
is the (
exact
)
period
of
P
. More generally, we say that
P
is
preperiodic
for
f
if there exist integers
m\geq 0
and
n\geq 1
such that
f^{m+n}(P)=f^{m}(P)
; the minimal such
m
and
n
are the
preperiod
and
eventual period
of
P
, respectively. The
preperiodic portrait
for
f
(over
K
) is the functional graph
G(f,K)
of the restriction of
f
to the set of
K
-rational preperiodic points for
f
, that is, the directed graph whose vertices are the
K
-rational preperiodic points for
f
and whose edges are all ordered pairs of the form
(P,f(P))
. Throughout, we will use the term “portrait” to refer to a functional graph on a finite set.

Northcott
[
northcott:1950
]
showed that if
f
has degree at least
2
, then
f
has finitely many
K
-rational preperiodic points; in particular, the preperiodic portrait
G(f,K)
is a finite graph. Based on an analogy between preperiodic points for rational maps and torsion points on elliptic curves, Morton and Silverman posed the following dynamical analogue of the strong uniform boundedness conjecture for torsion points on elliptic curves (now a theorem of Merel
[
merel:1996
]
).

Conjecture 1.1

(Morton-Silverman
[
morton/silverman:1994
, p. 100]
)
.

Fix integers
d\geq 1
and
D\geq 2
. There exists a constant
B=B(d,D)
such that if
K
is a number field of absolute degree
d
and
f(x)\in K(x)
is a rational function of degree
D
, then the number of
K
-rational preperiodic points for
f
is at most
B
.

Conjecture
1.1
is completely open—it is not known for any values of
d
or
D
—though we mention that Looper
[
looper:2021
,
looper
]
has proven a version of the uniform bounedness conjecture for polynomials assuming a stronger variant of the
abc
conjecture. To emphasize the difficulty of Conjecture
1.1
, we note that just the case
D=4
would suffice to prove Merel’s theorem; see
[
silverman:2007
, §3.3]
.

1.1.
Preperiodic portraits for quadratic polynomials

Perhaps the most well-studied family of rational functions in complex and arithmetic dynamics is that of quadratic polynomials. We say that two rational functions
f
and
g
are
linearly conjugate
over
K
if there exists
\gamma\in\operatorname{PGL}_{2}(K)
such
g=\gamma^{-1}\circ f\circ\gamma
. This is a natural notion of equivalence in dynamics since conjugation commutes with iteration; in particular, if
f,g\in K(x)
are linearly conjugate over
K
, then the portraits
G(f,K)
and
G(g,K)
are isomorphic. Every quadratic polynomial in
K[x]
is linearly conjugate over
K
to a unique polynomial of the form

f_{c}(x):=x^{2}+c

with
c\in K
; see
[
silverman:2007
, §4.2]
, for example. Thus, the space of quadratic polynomials
up to linear conjugation
is a one-parameter family, which we view as analogous to the fact that elliptic curves up to isomorphism form a one-parameter family (parametrized by the
j
-invariant).

Conjecture
1.1
asserts that the number of
K
-rational preperiodic points for a quadratic polynomial should be bounded above by a constant depending only on
[K:\mathbb{Q}]
. We rephrase this in terms of preperiodic portraits.

Conjecture 1.2
.

Fix an integer
d\geq 1
. There exists a (minimal) finite set
\overline{\Gamma}=\overline{\Gamma}(d)
of portraits such that for every number field
K
of absolute degree
d
and every quadratic
f(x)\in K[x]
, the preperiodic portrait
G(f,K)
is isomorphic to a portrait in
\overline{\Gamma}
.

Thus, the problem is to determine for which portraits
\mathcal{P}
there exists a degree-
d
number field
K
and a quadratic polynomial
f(x)\in K[x]
for which
G(f,K)\cong\mathcal{P}
.
For the
d=1
case, Poonen
[
poonen:1998
]
has shown that if there are no quadratic polynomials over
\mathbb{Q}
with rational points of period larger than
3
, then
\overline{\Gamma}(1)
consists of exactly the twelve portraits labelled

\rm\emptyset,\ 2(1),\ 3(1,1),\ 3(2),\ 4(1,1),\ 4(2),\ 5(1,1)a,\ 6(1,1),\ 6(2),\ 6(3),\ 8(2,1,1),\text{ and }8(3)

in Appendix
A
. It is not known whether there exists a quadratic polynomial over
\mathbb{Q}
with rational points of period larger than
3
; however, it is known that a quadratic polynomial in
\mathbb{Q}[x]
cannot have rational points of period
4

[
morton:1998
]
, period
5

[
flynn/poonen/schaefer:1997
]
, or, assuming the Birch and Swinnerton-Dyer conjecture for a certain abelian fourfold, period
6

[
stoll:2008
]
. Further computational evidence
[
hutz/ingram:2013
]
suggests that
3
the largest possible period arising in this setting.

For the
d=2
case, based on a large amount of computational evidence together with techniques similar to those used in
[
poonen:1998
]
, it was suggested in
[
doyle/faber/krumm:2014
, p. 514]
and explicitly conjectured in
[
doyle:2018quad
, Conjecture 1.4]
that
\overline{\Gamma}(2)
consists of precisely
46
portraits, which can be found in
[
doyle/faber/krumm:2014
, Appendix B]
.

1.2.
Portraits realized infinitely often

Given
d\geq 1
, a complete classification of those preperiodic portraits that can be realized by quadratic polynomials defined over number fields of degree
d
—that is, determining the set
\overline{\Gamma}(d)
—seems to be quite difficult. Instead, we consider the following variant: Informally, given
d\geq 1
, which preperiodic portraits can be realized infinitely often by quadratic polynomials over degree-
d
number fields?

As phrased, this is not quite the right question. Indeed, any preperiodic portrait realized by even one quadratic polynomial over
K
will be realized infinitely often, since linearly conjugate maps have isomorphic preperiodic portraits. Thus, we wish to know which portraits can be realized by infinitely many
equivalence classes
of quadratic polynomials over degree-
d
number fields.
For this problem, the analogue of Conjecture
1.2
is known. The following statement was proven in
[
doyle/krumm:2024
]
using the main results of
[
doyle/poonen:2020
]
. For
d\geq 1
, we define

\mathbb{Q}^{(d)}:=\{\alpha\in\overline{\mathbb{Q}}:[\mathbb{Q}(\alpha):\mathbb{Q}]\leq d\}

to be the set of algebraic numbers of degree at most
d
.

Theorem 1.3

(
[
doyle/krumm:2024
, Theorem 1.11]
)
.

Fix an integer
d\geq 1
. There exists a finite set
\Gamma=\Gamma(d)
of portraits such that, for every portrait
\mathcal{P}
, the following are equivalent:

(a)

There are infinitely many
c\in\mathbb{Q}^{(d)}
such that
G(f_{c},K)\cong\mathcal{P}
for some degree-
d
number field
K
containing
c
.

(b)

The portrait
\mathcal{P}
is isomorphic to a portrait in
\Gamma
.

The sets
\Gamma(1)
and
\Gamma(2)
are known. Again referring to the labels of portraits in Appendix
A
, Faber showed in
[
faber:2015
]
that
\Gamma(1)
consists of the seven portraits

\emptyset
, 4(1,1), 4(2), 6(1,1), 6(2), 6(3), and 8(2,1,1),

and the first author and Krumm showed in
[
doyle/krumm:2024
]
that
\Gamma(2)
consists of the
17
portraits

\emptyset
, 4(1,1), 4(2), 6(1,1), 6(2), 6(3), 8(1,1)a/b, 8(2)a/b, 8(2,1,1), 8(3), 8(4), 10(2,1,1)a/b, 10(3,1,1), and 10(3,2).

In this article, we determine
\Gamma(3)
.

Theorem 1.4
.

For an abstract portrait
\mathcal{P}
, the following are equivalent:

(a)

There are infinitely many
c\in\mathbb{Q}^{(3)}
such that
G(f_{c},K)\cong\mathcal{P}
for some cubic number field
K
containing
c
.

(b)

The portrait
\mathcal{P}
is isomorphic to one of the following
19
portraits from Appendix
A
:

\emptyset
, 4(1,1), 4(2), 6(1,1), 6(2), 6(3), 8(1,1)a/b, 8(2)a/b, 8(3), 8(4), 8(2,1,1), 10(1,1)a, 10(2,1,1)a/b, 10(3,1,1), 10(3,2), 12(3,3).

In particular,
\Gamma(3)
is the set of portraits listed in part
(b)
.

Each realization of a portrait
\mathcal{P}
as the
K
-rational preperiodic portrait of a polynomial
x^{2}+c\in K[x]
yields a
K
-rational point on a certain
dynamical modular curve

X_{1}(\mathcal{P})
. We defer the definitions to Section
2
, but we note that these curves are, as the name suggests, dynamical analogues of the classical modular curves which parametrize isomorphism classes of elliptic curves together with marked torsion points of a given order. Much of the work in proving Theorem
1.4
is in proving the following:

Theorem 1.5
.

For a
generic quadratic portrait

\mathcal{P}
(as defined in Section
2.2
), the following are equivalent:

(a)

There are infinitely many cubic points on
X_{1}(\mathcal{P})
.

(b)

The portrait
\mathcal{P}
is isomorphic to one of the following
19
portraits from Appendix
A
:

\emptyset
, 4(1,1), 4(2), 6(1,1), 6(2), 6(3), 8(1,1)a/b, 8(2)a/b, 8(3), 8(4), 8(2,1,1), 10(1,1)a, 10(2,1,1)a/b, 10(3,1,1), 10(3,2), 12(3,3).

Theorem
1.4
does not follow immediately from Theorem
1.5
due to one important subtlety: Roughly speaking, a general
K
-rational point on
X_{1}(\mathcal{P})
corresponds to a map of the form
f_{c}(x)=x^{2}+c
for which
G(f_{c},K)
contains a
subgraph
isomorphic to
\mathcal{P}
. In order to show that, infinitely often,
G(f_{c},K)
is actually
isomorphic
to
\mathcal{P}
requires some additional work. This additional work, which was used in
[
doyle/krumm:2024
]
but is quite different from the approach from
[
faber:2015
]
, involves Hilbert’s irreducibility theorem coupled with a local statement about periodic points for maps of good reduction modulo a prime; the relevant arguments are in Section
6
.

1.3.
Rationality questions

As in
[
doyle/krumm:2024
]
, we also offer a slightly more refined version of Theorem
1.4
, where we consider the degrees of the infinitely many
c\in\mathbb{Q}^{(3)}
appearing in the statement of Theorem
1.4
.

Consider, for example, the portrait
\mathcal{P}=\rm 4(1,1)
. Faber showed in
[
faber:2015
]
that there are infinitely many
c\in\mathbb{Q}
for which
G(f_{c},\mathbb{Q})\cong\mathcal{P}
. Now fix such a
c\in\mathbb{Q}
. Since the set of preperiodic points for
f_{c}
has bounded height, for all but finitely many cubic fields
K
we will also have
G(f_{c},K)\cong\mathcal{P}
. In this case, we are able to conclude that
\rm 4(1,1)\in\Gamma(3)
simply because
\rm 4(1,1)\in\Gamma(1)
. In an effort to avoid this sort of triviality, we are interested in the following two questions for each
\mathcal{P}\in\Gamma(3)
:

•

Are there infinitely many
c\in\mathbb{Q}
for which there is a cubic field
K
such that
G(f_{c},\mathbb{Q})\subsetneq G(f_{c},K)\cong\mathcal{P}
?

•

Are there infinitely many
c\in\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
with
[\mathbb{Q}(c):\mathbb{Q}]=3
such that
G(f_{c},\mathbb{Q}(c))\cong\mathcal{P}
?

The next two results answer these questions.

Theorem 1.6
.

For an abstract portrait
\mathcal{P}
, the following are equivalent:

(a)

There are infinitely many
c\in\mathbb{Q}
such that
G(f_{c},\mathbb{Q})\subsetneq G(f_{c},K)\cong\mathcal{P}
for some cubic number field
K
.

(b)

The portrait
\mathcal{P}
is isomorphic to one of the following four portraits from Appendix
A
:

6(3), 10(3,1,1), 10(3,2), 12(3,3).

Theorem 1.7
.

For an abstract portrait
\mathcal{P}
, the following are equivalent:

(a)

There are infinitely many
c\in\mathbb{Q}^{(3)}\smallsetminus\mathbb{Q}
such that
G(f_{c},\mathbb{Q}(c))\cong\mathcal{P}
.

(b)

The portrait
\mathcal{P}
is isomorphic to one of the
18
portraits in
\Gamma(3)\smallsetminus\{{\rm 12(3,3)}\}
.

We prove these statements in Section
7
by more carefully analyzing the cubic points on the corresponding dynamical modular curves.

2.
Dynamical modular curves

In this section, we construct the curves which serve as moduli spaces for quadratic polynomial maps with preperiodic “level structure.”

2.1.
Dynatomic polynomials

For a polynomial
f
defined over a field
K
, the points of period
n
for
f
are roots of the polynomial
f^{n}(x)-x
. However,
f^{n}(x)-x
also has among its roots the points of period properly dividing
n
, so there is a factorization

f^{n}(x)-x=\prod_{d\mid n}\Phi_{f,d}(x),

where the factor
\Phi_{f,d}
generally has as its roots the points of period
d
for
f
. By Möbius inversion, one obtains the formula

\Phi_{f,n}(x)=\prod_{d\mid n}(f^{d}(x)-x)^{\mu(n/d)},

and we refer to this polynomial as the
n
th
dynatomic polynomial
.

As we are interested in the family
f_{c}(x)=x^{2}+c
, we consider the polynomial in two variables given by

\Phi_{n}(c,x):=\Phi_{f_{c},n}(x)\in K[c,x].

Borrowing notation from the classical modular curves, we let
Y_{1}(n)
be the affine plane curve defined by
\Phi_{n}(c,x)=0
, and we denote by
X_{1}(n)
the normalization of the projective closure of
Y_{1}(n)
. A general point
(c_{0},x_{0})
on
Y_{1}(n)
corresponds to a map of the form
f_{c_{0}}(x)=x^{2}+c_{0}
together with a marked point
x_{0}
of period equal to
n
for
f_{c_{0}}
. However, for each
n\geq 2
, there are finitely many exceptional points
(c_{0},x_{0})
where the period of
x_{0}
for the map
x^{2}+c_{0}
is a proper divisor of
n
. For example, we have

\Phi_{2}(c,x)=\frac{f_{c}^{2}(x)-x}{f_{c}(x)-x}=x^{2}+x+c+1,

so one can verify that
(c,x)=(-3/4,-1/2)
is a point on
X_{1}(n)
despite the fact that
-1/2
is a fixed point for
x^{2}-3/4
.

If
z
has period
n
for
f_{c}
, then
f_{c}(z)
also has period
n
for
f_{c}
, and from this we obtain an automorphism of
Y_{1}(n)
of order
n
defined by
(c,z)\mapsto(c,f_{c}(z))
; we write
\sigma_{n}
for the extension of this automorphism to
X_{1}(n)
. We denote the quotients of
X_{1}(n)
and
Y_{1}(n)
by the automorphism
\sigma_{n}
by
X_{0}(n)
and
Y_{0}(n)
, respectively, and we note that—again, with finitely many exceptions for each
n
—points on
X_{0}(n)
correspond to maps
f_{c}
together with a marked periodic
cycle
of length
n
.

For
n\geq 1
, define

D_{1}(n):=\sum_{k\mid n}\mu(n/k)2^{k}\quad\text{and}\quad D_{0}(n):=\frac{D_{1}(n)}{n}.

Then
D_{1}(n)
and
D_{0}(n)
are the degrees of the maps
c:X_{1}(n)\to\mathbb{P}^{1}
and
c:X_{0}(n)\to\mathbb{P}^{1}
, respectively. These quantities grow exponentially with
n
: it is not difficult to show that

(2.1)

2^{n-1}\leq D_{1}(n)\leq 2^{n},\quad\text{hence}\quad\frac{2^{n-1}}{n}\leq D_{0}(n)\leq\frac{2^{n}}{n},

for all
n\geq 1
, with strict inequalities whenever
n\geq 3
.

The points on the curve
X_{1}(n)
parametrize maps
f_{c}
with a
single
marked periodic point; we now extend this to multiple marked periodic points. Fix a nonincreasing sequence
n_{1},n_{2},\ldots,n_{m}
of positive integers, and assume that each positive integer
n
appears at most
D_{0}(n)
times in the sequence. We denote by
X_{1}(n_{1},n_{2},\ldots,n_{m})
the curve whose general points correspond to tuples
(c,\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{m})
where each
\alpha_{i}
has period
n_{i}
for the map
f_{c}
and no two
\alpha_{i}
are in the same orbit under
f_{c}
.

Example 2.1
.

For
n=3
, we have
D_{1}(3)=6
, which implies that a general quadratic polynomial has six points of period
3
partitioned into two
3
-cycles. A general point on the curve in
\mathbb{A}^{3}
defined by

(2.2)

\Phi_{3}(c,x)=0=\Phi_{3}(c,y)

corresponds to a map
f_{c}
together with two points (
x
and
y
) of period
3
for
f_{c}
, but without the additional (Zariski open) restriction that
x
and
y
lie in distinct orbits under
f_{c}
. In fact, the curve defined by (
2.2
) has exactly four geometrically irreducible components—a genus-
4
component birational to
X_{1}(3,3)
, and three components defined by

(2.3)

\begin{cases}\Phi_{3}(c,x)&\!\!\!\!=0\\
\hfill y&\!\!\!\!=x\end{cases},\quad\begin{cases}\Phi_{3}(c,x)&\!\!\!\!=0\\
\hfill y&\!\!\!\!=f_{c}(x)\end{cases},\quad\text{and}\quad\begin{cases}\Phi_{3}(c,x)&\!\!\!\!=0\\
\hfill y&\!\!\!\!=f_{c}^{2}(x)\end{cases},

respectively. Note that each curve from (
2.3
) is isomorphic to
Y_{1}(3)
.

2.2.
General dynamical modular curves

We now define our most general notion of dynamical modular curve. We give only an informal description here, and we refer the reader to
[
doyle:2019
]
for details.

The structure of the preperiodic points for a map can be illustrated using a functional graph, which we refer to as a (
preperiodic
)
portrait
. The following definition provides the class of portraits that we will be interested in.

Definition 2.2
.

A
generic quadratic portrait
is a directed graph
\mathcal{P}
satisfying the following properties:

(a)

Every vertex of
\mathcal{P}
has out-degree
1
and in-degree
0
or
2
.

(b)

For each
n\geq 1
, the number of
n
-cycles in
\mathcal{P}
is at most
D_{0}(n)
.

(c)

If
\mathcal{P}
has at least one fixed point, then
\mathcal{P}
has exactly two fixed points.

Remark 2.3
.

The term “generic quadratic portrait” was adopted in
[
doyle/krumm:2024
]
; in the earlier paper
[
doyle:2019
]
, the first author had used the term “strongly admissible”.

Our reason for restricting to such portraits is summarized in the following statement:

Lemma 2.4
.

Fix an integer
d\geq 1
. There are only finitely many
c\in\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
with
[\mathbb{Q}(c):\mathbb{Q}]\leq d
for which there is a number field
K\supseteq\mathbb{Q}(c)
such that
G(f_{c},K)
does not satisfy properties
(a)
,
(b)
, and
(c)
of Definition
2.2
.

Remark 2.5
.

A weaker form of Lemma
2.4
, where the number field
K
is fixed, is stated in
[
doyle:2019
, Corollary 2.6]
. The proof is the same, relying on the fact that the set of exceptional
c\in\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
is a set of bounded height; see the proof of
[
silverman:2007
, Proposition 4.22]
.

Given a generic quadratic portrait
\mathcal{P}
, we denote by
X_{1}(\mathcal{P})
the smooth projective curve whose general points correspond to tuples
(c,\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{k})
, where
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{k}
are preperiodic points for
f_{c}(x)=x^{2}+c
which, informally, form a portrait isomorphic to
\mathcal{P}
. We refer to
[
doyle:2019
]
for a formal definition of the curve
X_{1}(\mathcal{P})
; here, we give a slightly simpler description that is more in the spirit of
[
doyle/silverman:2020
]
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a generic quadratic portrait, and label its vertices using the integers
1,2,\ldots,N
. For each directed edge
{\boldsymbol{e}}=(i,j)
, define the polynomial

F_{\boldsymbol{e}}(c,x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}):=f_{c}(x_{i})-x_{j}=x_{i}^{2}+c-x_{j}\in\mathbb{Z}[c,x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}].

Note that, since
\mathcal{P}
is a functional graph, there are precisely
N
edges. Let
U_{1}(\mathcal{P})
be the subvariety of
\mathbb{A}^{N+1}
cut out by the
N
equations
F_{\boldsymbol{e}}=0
and the
\binom{N}{2}
Zariski open conditions
x_{i}\neq x_{j}
with
1\leq i<j\leq N
. We then take
Y_{1}(\mathcal{P})
to be the Zariski closure of
U_{1}(\mathcal{P})
in
\mathbb{A}^{N+1}
and
X_{1}(\mathcal{P})
to be the smooth projective curve birational to
U_{1}(\mathcal{P})
.

2.3.
Morphisms between dynamical modular curves

The construction
X_{1}(\cdot)
is an assignment that takes functional graphs (or, more specifically, generic quadratic portraits) to algebraic curves (over
\mathbb{Q}
). The next statement says that this assignment is functorial. For what follows, we use the term
portrait morphism
to mean an
injective
graph homomorphism
\mathcal{P}\hookrightarrow\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
of (generic quadratic) portraits, that is, an injective map from the vertex set of
\mathcal{P}
to the vertex set of
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
that preserves edge relations.

Proposition 2.7
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
be two generic quadratic portraits, and write
c_{\mathcal{P}}
and
c_{\mathcal{P}^{\prime}}
for the projection-to-
c
maps from
X_{1}(\mathcal{P})
and
X_{1}(\mathcal{P}^{\prime})
, respectively, to
\mathbb{P}^{1}
. Suppose there exists a portrait morphism
\psi:\mathcal{P}\hookrightarrow\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
. Then there is a dominant morphism of curves
F_{\psi}:X_{1}(\mathcal{P}^{\prime})\to X_{1}(\mathcal{P})
, defined over
\mathbb{Q}
, satisfying the following properties:

(a)

The map
c_{\mathcal{P}^{\prime}}
factors through
F_{\psi}
; more precisely,
c_{\mathcal{P}^{\prime}}=c_{\mathcal{P}}\circ F_{\psi}
.

(b)

If
\psi
is not surjective, then
\deg F_{\psi}\geq 2
.

(c)

If
\psi_{1}
and
\psi_{2}
are distinct portrait morphisms
\mathcal{P}\hookrightarrow\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
, then the corresponding morphisms
F_{\psi_{1}}
and
F_{\psi_{2}}
are distinct.

Proof.

This essentially follows from
[
doyle:2019
, Proposition 3.3]
and
[
doyle/silverman:2020
, Theorem 10.1(c)]
, but we include the short proof in this case for completeness.

Label the vertices of
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
as
\{1,\ldots,n\}
and
\{1,\ldots,N\}
, respectively, with
n\leq N
. We claim that the projection map

(2.5)

\begin{split}\pi:\mathbb{A}^{N+1}&\longrightarrow\mathbb{A}^{n+1}\\
(c,x_{1},\ldots,x_{N})&\longmapsto(c,x_{\psi(1)},\ldots,x_{\psi(n)})\end{split}

restricts to a map from
U_{1}(\mathcal{P}^{\prime})
to
U_{1}(\mathcal{P})
with finite fibers; the desired morphism
F_{\psi}
is then the extension of this map to
X_{1}(\mathcal{P}^{\prime})
.

Let
(c,x_{1},\ldots,x_{N})\in U_{1}(\mathcal{P}^{\prime})
. Because
\psi
is a portrait morphism, if
(i,j)
is a directed edge in
\mathcal{P}
, then
(\psi(i),\psi(j))
is a directed edge in
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
. Thus, for every relation
f_{c}(x_{i})=x_{j}
defining

, we also have
f_{c}(x_{\psi(i)})=x_{\psi(j)}
. Furthermore, the conditions
x_{\psi(i)}\neq x_{\psi(j)}
are met for all

by injectivity of

and the fact that, on the Zariski open subset

, we have
x_{k}\neq x_{\ell}
for all
k\neq\ell
. Thus,
\pi(c,x_{1},\ldots,x_{N})\in U_{1}(\mathcal{P})
. Finally, the fibers are finite since, for any given
c
, there are only finitely many preperiodic points for
f_{c}
with an orbit of length at most
N
. Thus, our claim is proven.

Statement (a) is immediate from the construction in (
2.5
), and (c) is straightforward as well: If
\psi_{1}\neq\psi_{2}
, then there exists

such that

, hence

. Statement (b) is not difficult, but we refer the reader to the proof of

.
∎

We record two useful consequences of Proposition

.

of a portrait

is the nonincreasing sequence of cycle lengths appearing in

Corollary 2.8
.

If
\mathcal{P}
has cycle structure

, then there is a morphism

.

For a curve

In other words,

Proposition 2.9
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a generic quadratic portrait. Then

\operatorname{Aut}_{\mathbb{Q}}\big(c:X_{1}(\mathcal{P})\to\mathbb{P}^{1}\big)\cong\operatorname{Aut}(\mathcal{P}).

Proof.

Let

be the function field of

over
\mathbb{Q}
. The extension

is generated by preperiodic points

for

is determined by its action on

\operatorname{Aut}_{\mathbb{Q}}(c:X_{1}(\mathcal{P})\to\mathbb{P}^{1})\cong\operatorname{Aut}(L/\mathbb{Q}(c))

that every element of

yields an automorphism of

∎

3.
Useful tools for algebraic curves

We begin by stating some general results towards determining if
X/K
has infinitely many cubic points.

3.1.
Background

If a curve
X/K
has infinitely many points of degree
d\leq 5
, then Kadets and Vogt
[
kadets/vogt:2025
]
show that either
X/\overline{K}
is a degree-
d
cover of
\mathbb{P}^{1}
or an elliptic curve, or
X/K
is a “Debarre-Fahlaoui curve.” We use their result for

:

Theorem 3.1

(
[
kadets/vogt:2025
, Theorem 1.2]
)
.

Let
X
be a smooth projective curve of genus greater than
4
defined over a number field
K
. If
X
has infinitely many cubic points over
K
, then
X
admits a
K
-morphism of degree at most
3
to
\mathbb{P}^{1}
or to an elliptic curve with positive rank over
K
.

We can often rule out maps of low degree to curves of low genus via the Castelnuovo-Severi inequality; see
[
stichtenoth:2009
, Theorem 3.11.3]
, for example.

Theorem 3.2

(Castelnuovo-Severi inequality)
.

Let
X
,
Y_{1}
, and
Y_{2}
be curves of genus
g
,
g_{1}
, and
g_{2}
, respectively. Suppose that for each
i=1,2
we have a map
\varphi_{i}:X\to Y_{i}
of degree
d_{i}
. Then at least one of the following must hold:

(a)

the inequality

g\leq d_{1}g_{1}+d_{2}g_{2}+(d_{1}-1)(d_{2}-1)

is satisfied, or

(b)

there is a curve
Y
and a morphism
\psi:X\to Y
of degree at least
2
such that both
\varphi_{1}
and
\varphi_{2}
factor through
\psi
.

Given a curve
X
, one step in determining which elliptic curves are covered by
X
involves reducing modulo various primes of good reduction. We will rely on the following computationally useful facts about reduction:

Lemma 3.3

(
[
liu:2002
, Corollary 10.1.25]
)
.

Let
X
be a smooth projective curve defined over a number field
K
, and let
\mathfrak{p}
be a maximal ideal in the ring of integers
\mathcal{O}_{K}
. Let
X_{\mathfrak{p}}
be the reduction of
X
modulo
\mathfrak{p}
. If the normalization of the projective closure of
X_{\mathfrak{p}}
has the same genus as
X
, then
X
has good reduction at
\mathfrak{p}
.

The following is well known; we refer to
[
xarles:2012
, Proposition 5]
for a proof and additional references.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
X
be a smooth projective curve defined over a number field
K
, let
\mathfrak{p}\subset\mathcal{O}_{K}
be a prime of good reduction for
X
, and let
\mathbb{F}_{q}
be the residue field of
\mathfrak{p}
. Let
\gamma
and
\widetilde{\gamma}
be the gonalities of
X
and
X_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}
, respectively. Then

\gamma\geq\widetilde{\gamma}\geq\frac{|X(\mathbb{F}_{q})|}{q+1}.

All of our dynamical modular curves are defined over
\mathbb{Q}
, so we will be reducing modulo rational primes
p
. Once we have a prime
p
of good reduction, we can compute the zeta function of the mod-
p
reduction. Irreducible factors—possibly raised to a nontrivial power—of the numerator of the zeta function correspond to factors (up to isogeny) of the Jacobian
[
tate:1966
, Theorem 1]
. Then, we use the following result of Kani
[
kani:1994
]
, but before we state the result, we set some terminology and notation.

Let
X
be a curve, and let
J(X)
be its Jacobian. An
elliptic subgroup
of
J(X)
is an elliptic curve contained in
J(X)
which is also a subgroup with respect to the addition on
J(X)
. (Note that it is not enough to assume the elliptic curve
E
is simply a subvariety of

, since the group operation on
E
need not be compatible with the addition law on
J(X)
.) An
elliptic subcover
of
X
is a morphism from
X
to an elliptic curve

which does not factor through a nontrivial isogeny of

. Note that if
X
has genus greater than
1
, then any prime-degree morphism

is an elliptic subcover.

Theorem 3.5

(
[
kani:1994
, Corollary 4.3]
)
.

For a curve
X
with theta divisor
\Theta_{X}
and an integer
d\geq 1
, the map
f\mapsto f^{*}J(E)
induces a one-to-one correspondence between isomorphism classes of degree-
d
elliptic subcovers
f:X\to E
and elliptic subgroups
E\leq J(X)
such that
E.\Theta_{X}=d
. (Here
E.\Theta_{X}
denotes the intersection number of
E
and
\Theta_{X}
.)

In the other direction, if
X/K
has very low genus, then we expect

to have infinitely many cubic points. For example, the following is a straightforward consequence of Riemann-Roch:

Lemma 3.6

(
[
jeon/kim/schweizer:2004
, Lemma 2.1]
)
.

Let
X/K
be a curve of genus
2
with at least three

-rational points. Then

admits a degree-

map to

defined over

. In particular,

has infinitely many cubic points over

.

3.2.
General lemmas

3.2.1.
Degrees of points with respect to two maps

We prove a suitable version of
[
levin:2016
, Theorem 4.2]
for later application.

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
X/K
be a curve, let
\varphi
and
\psi
be two dominant morphisms to
\mathbb{P}^{1}
, and let
d=\deg\varphi
. Then there is a finite set
S
of maximal ideals in
\mathcal{O}_{K}
for which the following is true: For all maximal ideals
\mathfrak{p}\notin S
, and for any finite collection of curves
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
with dominant morphisms
\pi_{i}:X_{i}\to X
, there are infinitely many points
P\in X(\overline{K})
such that

(i)

[K(P):K]=d
,

(ii)

the fiber
\pi_{i}^{-1}(P)
is irreducible over
K
for all
i=1,\ldots,n
, and

(iii)

for all maximal ideals
\mathfrak{q}\mid\mathfrak{p}
in
\mathcal{O}_{K(P)}
, we have
\operatorname{ord}_{\mathfrak{q}}(\psi(P))\geq 0
.

Proof.

Let
\pi:X\to Y
be a morphism of maximal degree such that both
\varphi
and
\psi
factor through
\pi
, and let
\varphi^{\prime},\psi^{\prime}:Y\to\mathbb{P}^{1}
be such that

\varphi=\varphi^{\prime}\circ\pi\quad\text{and}\quad\psi=\psi^{\prime}\circ\pi.

Let
e,d^{\prime}\geq 1
be the degrees of
\pi
and
\varphi^{\prime}
, respectively; in particular,
d=d^{\prime}e
.

Let
x
and
y
be the elements of the function field of
Y
corresponding to the maps
\varphi^{\prime}
and
\psi^{\prime}
, respectively. Then, since
\varphi^{\prime}
and
\psi^{\prime}
do not factor through a common map of degree at least
2
, the curve
Y
has a plane model defined by the vanishing of a polynomial of the form

F(x,y)=a_{d^{\prime}}(x)y^{d^{\prime}}+a_{d^{\prime}-1}(x)y^{d^{\prime}-1}+\cdots+a_{1}(x)y+a_{0}(x)\in K[x][y].

Choose
x_{0}\in\mathcal{O}_{K}
such that
a_{d^{\prime}}(x_{0})\neq 0
, and let
S
be the set of maximal ideals
\mathfrak{P}\subset\mathcal{O}_{K}
such that either
\operatorname{ord}_{\mathfrak{P}}(a_{d^{\prime}}(x_{0}))\neq 0
or one of the coefficients of at least one
a_{i}(x)\in K[x]
has negative
\mathfrak{P}
-adic valuation. Note that
S
is a finite set. Now let
\mathfrak{p}
be any maximal ideal not in
S
, and let

[x_{0}]_{\mathfrak{p}}:=\{t\in K:t\equiv x_{0}\ \left(\operatorname{mod}\ {\mathfrak{p}}\right)\}

be the residue class of
x_{0}
modulo
\mathfrak{p}
.

By Hilbert Irreducibility, if
\mathcal{T}\subset K
is a thin set (in the sense of Serre), then
[x_{0}]_{\mathfrak{p}}\smallsetminus\mathcal{T}
is infinite.
Thus, if we let
\pi_{i}:X_{i}\to X
with
1\leq i\leq n
be a collection of maps as in the statement of the lemma, then the set

\Sigma:=\{t\in[x_{0}]_{\mathfrak{p}}:(\varphi\circ\pi_{i})^{-1}(t)\text{ is irreducible for all }i=1,\ldots,n\}

is infinite. We claim that every point in the infinite set
\varphi^{-1}(\Sigma)\subset X(\overline{K})
satisfies the three conditions of the lemma.

Thus, let
P\in\varphi^{-1}(\Sigma)
. For every
i=1,\ldots,n
and every preimage
Q_{i}\in\pi_{i}^{-1}(P)
, we have

[K(P):K]=\deg\varphi=d\quad\text{and}\quad[K(Q_{i}):K(P)]=\deg\pi_{i},

so that conditions (i) and (ii) are satisfied.
Finally, write
t=\varphi(P)
and
u=\psi(P)
, so that
F(t,u)=0
. Note that
t\in[x_{0}]_{\mathfrak{p}}
by assumption, so each
a_{i}(t)
is integral at
\mathfrak{p}
and
a_{d^{\prime}}(t)
is a
\mathfrak{p}
-adic unit. In particular, this implies that
u
is integral over each maximal ideal
\mathfrak{q}\subset\mathcal{O}_{K(P)}=\mathcal{O}_{K(u)}
lying over
\mathfrak{p}
, so condition (iii) is satisfied as well.
∎

3.2.2.
Factoring maps between curves

We would like to thank James Rawson for suggesting the following short lemma, which allows us to study the image of degree-
d
points arising from a map
X\to Y
under a second map
X\to Z
. See
[
derickx/rawson:2025
]
for the case
Z=\mathbb{P}^{1}
.

Lemma 3.8
.

Let
X/K
,
Y/K
, and
Z/K
be curves with morphisms
\theta:X\to Y
and
\pi:X\to Z
. Suppose that for infinitely many
y\in Y
,
\pi(\theta^{-1}(y))
is a single geometric point. Then
\pi
factors through
\theta
.

Proof.

Consider the map
\theta\times\pi:X\to Y\times Z
, let
V
be the image of
X
in
Y\times Z
, and let
\gamma:X\to V
be the map induced by
\theta\times\pi
.
By assumption, for infinitely many
y\in Y
, there exists
z\in Z
such that
\pi(\theta^{-1}(y))=\{z\}
, so that the fiber
\gamma^{-1}((y,z))
contains
\theta^{-1}(y)
,
and thus has degree (as a divisor) at least
\deg\theta
. Thus, there are infinitely many
v\in V
such that
\gamma^{-1}(v)
has degree at least
\deg\theta
; in other words, the degree of
\gamma
is at least the degree of
\theta
. But
\theta
factors through
\gamma
, so
\theta
and
\gamma
have the same degree.

Finally, let
\pi_{Y}
and
\pi_{Z}
be the projection maps from
Y\times Z
to
Y
and
Z
, respectively. Since
\theta=\pi_{Y}\circ\gamma
, and since
\deg\theta=\deg\gamma
, the restriction
\pi_{Y}|_{V}
is a birational map, hence
\pi=\pi_{Z}\circ\pi_{Y}|_{V}^{-1}\circ\theta
.
∎

3.2.3.
Curves with finitely many cubic points

We now provide some sufficient conditions, computable in Magma, for
X/K
to have finitely many cubic points. Theorem
3.1
shows that if the genus of
X
is greater than
4
and
X
does not admit a degree-
3
map to a curve of genus at most
1
, then
X/K
has finitely many cubic points. Often Theorem
3.2
is enough to rule out degree-
3
maps to
\mathbb{P}^{1}
, and thus we are primarily concerned with maps of degree
3
to an elliptic curve.

If the isogeny decomposition of
J(X)
does not contain an elliptic curve, then
X
does not admit a degree-
3
map to an elliptic curve. (In fact,
X
does not admit
any
maps to an elliptic curve in this case.) When the isogeny decomposition of
J(X)
does contain an elliptic curve
E
, Theorem
3.5
shows that there will exist a map
X\to E
, and we must determine its degree.

Lemma 3.9
.

Let
X/K
be a curve of genus
g>1
, and fix an integer
d>1
. Suppose that the isogeny decomposition of
J(X)
is
E_{1}\times\cdots\times E_{n}\times A
, where the
E_{i}
are pairwise non-isogenous elliptic curves and
A
does not contain any elliptic curves. Further, suppose that for each
i
with
1\leq i\leq n
there exists an elliptic curve
F_{i}
isogenous to
E_{i}
and a morphism
\varphi_{i}:X\to F_{i}
of degree coprime to
d
. Then,
X
does not admit a degree-
d
morphism to any elliptic curve.

Proof.

We first show that the only elliptic subgroups of
J(X)
are (up to isogeny) the
E_{i}
. If
E\leq J(X)
is an elliptic subgroup, then as
A
does not contain any 1-dimensional subgroups, the image of
E
under the projection
\pi_{A}:J(X)\to A
must be the identity. Thus,
E\leq\pi^{-1}_{A}(0)=E_{1}\times\cdots\times E_{n}\times 0
. For each
i
with
1\leq i\leq n
, the projections
\pi_{i}:J(X)\to E_{i}
give group homomorphisms
\theta_{i}:E\to E_{i}
, which are isogenies if and only if they are non-constant. As the
E_{i}
are non-isogenous, we see that at most one—hence
exactly
one—of the maps
\theta_{i}
is non-constant. On the other hand,
\theta_{i}(E)
is a subgroup of
E_{i}
, so if
\theta_{i}
is constant, then
E
is contained in the kernel of
\theta_{i}
. Intersecting all the kernels containing
E

By Theorem

, there is a one-to-one correspondence between isomorphism classes of degree-

such that

there is a unique morphism

for

an elliptic curve must factor through one of the

, the degree of

is coprime to

, as the degree of

must divide the degree of the map

. But every map to an elliptic curve would factor through one of the

∎

When the isogeny decomposition of

contains repeated elliptic factors, the situation is more complicated. Indeed, suppose that

contains an abelian subvariety isogenous to

. By Theorem

, maps to elliptic curves are equivalent to elliptic subgroups of

, and

contains infinitely many elliptic subgroups: let

.
Instead of attempting to classify the degrees of the infinitely many maps arising from these infinitely many subgroups, we instead note that if

, then

Proof.

By Theorem

4.
Dynamical modular curves with finitely many cubic points

Our procedure for determining precisely which dynamical modular curves have infinitely many cubic points requires two main steps: First, we show that if
\mathcal{P}
has sufficiently many periodic points, then
X_{1}(\mathcal{P})
has only finitely many cubic points. We do this by considering curves of the form
X_{0}(n)
,
X_{1}(n)
, and more generally
X_{1}(n_{1},\ldots,n_{m})
, as defined in Section
2.1
. The result will be that if
X_{1}(\mathcal{P})
has infinitely many cubic points, then
\mathcal{P}
has no cycle of length greater than
4
and has at most six periodic points.

Once we have restricted our attention to portraits with at most six periodic points, we fix an allowable cycle structure, start with the smallest portrait of that cycle structure, and, gradually increasing the number of vertices, we create larger and larger portraits until all such portraits with a given number of vertices yield curves with finitely many cubic points. Since a portrait morphism
\mathcal{P}\hookrightarrow\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
yields a morphism
X_{1}(\mathcal{P}^{\prime})\to X_{1}(\mathcal{P})
, once
X_{1}(\mathcal{P})
has only finitely many cubic points, the same is true for any portrait containing
\mathcal{P}
. In this way, we are able to determine all curves
X_{1}(\mathcal{P})
with infinitely many cubic points.

The proof of the proposition will rely on the Hilbert irreducibility theorem (via Lemma
3.7
) as well as the following statement about periodic points for maps with good reduction, for which we refer to
[
silverman:2007
, Theorem 2.21]
.

Proposition 6.2
.

Let
K
be a number field, and let
\mathfrak{p}
be a maximal ideal in
\mathcal{O}_{K}
with residue field of cardinality
q
. Then there exists a bound
B=B(q)
such that for all
c\in K
with
\operatorname{ord}_{\mathfrak{p}}(c)\geq 0
, every
K
-rational periodic point for

has period less than
B
.

Proof of Proposition
6.1
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a portrait for which
X_{1}(\mathcal{P})
has infinitely many cubic points. For all such
\mathcal{P}
, there is a degree-
3
map
\varphi:X_{1}(\mathcal{P})\to\mathbb{P}^{1}
defined over
\mathbb{Q}
, so we may apply Lemma
3.7
with
X=X_{1}(\mathcal{P})
,
d=3
, and the maps
\varphi
and
\psi=c
. Let
S
be the finite set of (rational) primes guaranteed by Lemma
3.7
, and let
p
be any rational prime not in
S
.

Set
B=\max\{B(p),B(p^{2}),B(p^{3})\}
, with
B(\cdot)
as in Proposition
6.2
.
Define
\Sigma(\mathcal{P},B)
to be the set of generic quadratic portraits
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
such that

•

\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
properly contains
\mathcal{P}
,

•

there are no intermediate subportraits
\mathcal{P}\subsetneq\mathcal{P}^{\prime\prime}\subsetneq\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
, and

•

\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
has no cycles of length greater than
B
.

Note that
\Sigma(\mathcal{P},B)
is finite, as each
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}\in\Sigma(\mathcal{P},B)
can be obtained from
\mathcal{P}
by either adding a new cycle of length at most
B
or by adding two preimages to a vertex in
\mathcal{P}
. Let
\mathcal{P}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{P}_{n}
be an enumeration of the set
\Sigma(\mathcal{P},B)
.

By Proposition
2.7
, for each
i=1,\ldots,n
, there is a morphism
\pi_{i}:X_{1}(\mathcal{P}_{i})\to X_{1}(\mathcal{P})
of degree at least
2
. Thus, by Lemmas
2.4
and
3.7
, there are infinitely many cubic points
P
on
X_{1}(\mathcal{P})
such that

•

the preperiodic portrait
G(f_{c(P)},\mathbb{Q}(P))
is generic quadratic,

•

for all
i=1,\ldots,n
, the points in the fiber
\pi_{i}^{-1}(P)
are defined over proper extensions of
\mathbb{Q}(P)
, and

•

\operatorname{ord}_{\mathfrak{q}}(c(P))\geq 0
for all primes
\mathfrak{q}
in
\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{Q}(P)}
lying over
p
.

To complete the proof, it now suffices to show that if
P
is one of these infinitely many cubic points, then
G(f_{c(P)},\mathbb{Q}(P))
is isomorphic to
\mathcal{P}
. Thus, let
P
be such a point, and let
K=\mathbb{Q}(P)
. Since
P
is a
K
-rational point on
X_{1}(\mathcal{P})
, and since
G(f_{c(P)},K)
is generic quadratic, the portrait
G(f_{c(P)},K)
has a subgraph isomorphic to
\mathcal{P}
; we claim that
G(f_{c(P)},K)
can be no larger than
\mathcal{P}
.

Let
\mathfrak{q}
be any prime ideal of
\mathcal{O}_{K}
lying over
p
. Since the residue field of
\mathfrak{q}
has cardinality
p^{k}
for some
1\leq k\leq 3
, and since
\operatorname{ord}_{\mathfrak{q}}(c(P))\geq 0
, it follows from Proposition
6.2
that
f_{c(P)}
has no
K
-rational periodic points of period greater than
B
. Thus, since we have assumed that
G(f_{c(P)},K)
is a generic quadratic portrait, it must be that either
G(f_{c(P)},K)\cong\mathcal{P}
or
G(f_{c(P)},K)
contains a portrait isomorphic to
\mathcal{P}_{i}
for some
1\leq i\leq n
. But the latter is impossible, as this would imply that
\pi_{i}^{-1}(P)
contained a
K
-rational point, which contradicts our assumption that
\pi_{i}^{-1}(P)
is irreducible over
K
. Therefore,

.
∎

8(4).

We claim that there are only finitely many weakly cubic points on
X_{1}({\rm 8(4)})
. One could give an argument similar to the one used for 8(3), though it is a bit more involved; instead, we provide a different (and shorter) proof in this case that relies more on the geometry of
X_{1}({\rm 8(4)})
than on dynamical properties.

Suppose for the sake of contradiction that
X_{1}({\rm 8(4)})
has infinitely many weakly cubic points. The curve
X_{1}({\rm 8(4)})
is isomorphic to the
classical
modular curve
X_{1}(16)
(see
[
morton:1998
, p. 93]
), so its Jacobian has rank
0
over
\mathbb{Q}
. Thus, there are finitely many degree-
3
morphisms
X_{1}({\rm 8(3)})\to\mathbb{P}^{1}
(up to automorphisms on
\mathbb{P}^{1}
), and hence by the pigeonhole principle infinitely many weakly cubic points must be linearly equivalent. That is, there must exist some degree-
3
morphism
\varphi:X_{1}({\rm 8(3)})\to\mathbb{P}^{1}
such that for infinitely many

, the fiber
\varphi^{-1}(y)
is (the Galois orbit of) a weakly cubic point. By definition, if
z\in X_{1}({\rm 8(3)})
is a weakly cubic point, then

is rational point, so that
c(\varphi^{-1}(y))
is a single geometric point. By Lemma
3.8
, we see that the
c
map must factor through
\varphi
; however, computation of the Galois group of (the Galois closure of) the map
c:X_{1}({\rm 8(3)})\to\mathbb{P}^{1})
shows that the map

does not factor through any degree-
3
map.

10(3,1,1) and 10(3,2).
Let
\mathcal{P}
be either of these portraits. Then
X=X_{1}(\mathcal{P})
has genus
2
, and the Jacobian of
X
has rank zero over
\mathbb{Q}
. The curve
X
admits a cubic map
\varphi:X\to\mathbb{P}^{1}
which is disjoint from
c:X\to\mathbb{P}^{1}
, so the cubic preimages of rational points under
\varphi
will generally satisfy
c\notin\mathbb{Q}
by Hilbert irreducibility.
In other words, we get infinitely many strictly cubic points.

On the other hand, the map
c:X\to\mathbb{P}^{1}
also factors through a cubic map defined over
\mathbb{Q}
. More precisely, if we let
\sigma\in\operatorname{Aut}(X)
be the order-
3
automorphism of
X
corresponding to a cyclic permutation on the
3
-cycle in
\mathcal{P}
, then the quotient of
X
by
\sigma
is isomorphic to
\mathbb{P}^{1}
, hence the map
c:X\to\mathbb{P}^{1}
factors through a degree-
3
map
\varphi:X\to\mathbb{P}^{1}
. Therefore,
X
has infinitely many weakly cubic points.

12(3,3).
We claim that the genus-
4
curve
X=X_{1}({\rm 12(3,3)})
has finitely many strictly cubic points. More precisely, if
K
is a cubic field,
c\in K
, and
G(f_{c},K)
contains 12(3,3), then with finitely many exceptions, actually
c\in\mathbb{Q}
and
G(f_{c},\mathbb{Q})
contains 6(3).

We first show that the Jacobian
J(X)
has rank
0
over
\mathbb{Q}
. What follows comes mainly from
[
morton:1992
, §4]
(the function field of
X
is the field
N
from
[
morton:1992
]
) plus some Magma computations.

The curve
X
covers two distinct curves of genus
2
(
N_{-}
and
N_{+}
in
[
morton:1992
]
); more precisely, if we think of
X
as given by the model (
2.4
), the genus-
2
curves are the quotients of
X
by the automorphisms

(c,x,y)\mapsto(c,f_{c}(x),f_{c}(y))\quad\text{and}\quad(c,x,y)\mapsto(c,f_{c}^{2}(x),f_{c}(y)).

The Jacobians of both curves have rank
0
, so it suffices to show that
J(X)
is, up to isogeny, a product of the Jacobians of the two genus-
2
curves. Indeed, the first genus-
2
quotient has an elliptic subcover, hence has an elliptic isogeny factor, but a Magma computation shows that the second genus-
2
quotient is simple. Thus,
J(X)
has rank

are pullbacks of rational points on
\mathbb{P}^{1}
by a degree-

We now show that there are only two degree-

, up to automorphisms of
\mathbb{P}^{1}
. Indeed, there are two natural projection maps

X=X_{1}({\rm 12(3,3)})\to X_{1}({\rm 6(3)})\cong\mathbb{P}^{1},

each of which has degree

, where by “distinct” we mean that the pullbacks of
\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{1}}(1)
are distinct, or equivalently in this case, that one cannot be obtained from the other by postcomposition by an element of
\operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{P}^{1})

These two projection maps are distinct, hence they are the only cubic maps to
\mathbb{P}^{1}
.

X
are preimages of rational points on
X_{1}({\rm 6(3)})
; in other words, with finitely many exceptions, if

is a cubic field,

and

contains 6(3,3).

Proof of Theorem
1.6
.

Let
\mathcal{P}\in\Gamma(3)
, and suppose that there are infinitely many

such that

for some cubic number field

Now let
\mathcal{P}
be one of these four portraits. In each case, the map

with

. Choose
