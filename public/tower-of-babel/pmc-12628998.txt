Fzd2 orchestrates canonical and non-canonical Wnt signaling to regulate lung development and fibrosis
Authors: Jiayu Zhou, Xiayuan Lou, Zichen Wei, Xuhua Mao, Jingbo Zhang, Xiaoye Jin, Bo Li, Chao Zheng, Xuerong Siwu, Yi Yang, Yong Zhang, Wenjun Chen, Ming Jiang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Wnt signaling is a fundamental regulator of embryonic development and tissue regeneration, and its dysregulation contributes to diseases such as pulmonary fibrosis. However, the mechanisms by which Wnt signaling is differentially activated in lung cell populations and how it regulates lung development and disease pathogenesis remain unclear. Here, we identify distinct, spatiotemporal expression patterns of Wnt ligands and Frizzled receptors in the developing lung, with Fzd2 broadly expressed in epithelial, mesenchymal, and endothelial compartments. Using conditional knockout models, we demonstrate that Fzd2 plays cell type-specific roles through distinct Wnt pathways. Canonical Wnt/β-catenin signaling via Fzd2 is essential for endothelial proliferation and vascular development, while non-canonical Fzd2 signaling regulates mesenchymal cell proliferation and survival. Loss of Fzd2 in mesenchymal cells leads to impaired regeneration following bleomycin-induced injury, characterized by a transition from alveolar fibroblast 1 (AF1) to alveolar fibroblast 2 (AF2) cells. This transition suppresses Wnt2 expression, preventing alveolar type 2 (AT2) cell expansion and compromising alveolar regeneration. In addition, Fzd2-mediated PI3K-AKT signaling maintains the AF1 identity and restrains fibrotic AF2 transition. Together, these findings reveal that Fzd2 integrates canonical and non-canonical Wnt signaling to coordinate the crosstalk between mesenchyme, endothelium, and epithelium during lung development and repair, providing critical insights into fibroblast plasticity and potential therapeutic targets for fibrosis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12964-025-02501-8.

Background

Wnt signaling is an essential pathway that orchestrates stem cell proliferation, lineage specification, tissue homeostasis, and regeneration [1]. In the lung, Wnt signaling is critical for both development and repair processes, particularly in pulmonary fibrosis [2]. Lung morphogenesis and injury repair depend on close coordination between the endoderm and mesoderm, mediated in part by Wnt ligands and their receptors [3]. Canonical Wnt ligands such as Wnt7b and Wnt2/2b play distinct roles in lung development [4–7]. Loss of Wnt7b in the epithelium disrupts canonical Wnt signaling, resulting in lung hypoplasia and severe defects in vascular smooth muscle development [4]. Wnt2/2b, expressed in mesenchyme, are required for lung endoderm specification [7]. Cooperation between these ligands is crucial for mesenchymal lineage development and endodermal progenitor differentiation [8]. In the adult lung, AT2 cell self-renewal and the prevention of trans-differentiation into AT1 cells are supported by nearby Wnt2-expressing fibroblasts [9].

Non-canonical Wnt signaling also contributes to lung development and homeostasis. Disruption or overexpression of Wnt5a leads to imbalances in epithelial-mesenchymal interactions, causing lung hypoplasia [10, 11]. It also influences tracheal elongation and cartilage formation through Ror2-dependent signaling [12], and modulates AT2 and AT1 lineage specification during the perinatal period [13]. Foxf1 mutations promote abnormal AT2 proliferation by downregulating Wnt5a, suggesting a regulatory axis between Foxf1 and Wnt5a [14]. Pdgfra+ alveolar fibroblasts are key sources of Wnt5a to facilitate the self-renewal of Axin2+ AT2 cells during homeostasis [9].

Beyond Wnt ligands, Wnt pathway components like traffickers, receptors, and effectors are also essential [15]. Gpr177 (encoded by Gpr177 or Wntless), necessary for Wnt ligand transport, is essential for embryogenesis and lung morphogenesis [16, 17]. β-catenin is critical for foregut development and airway patterning. Loss of β-catenin causes lung agenesis and esophageal progenitor identity, while its constitutive activation leads to tracheoesophageal defects and abnormal lung progenitor expansion [7, 18]. Activation of β-catenin in early SPC+ cells leads to distal conducting airway dilation without differentiation into airway cell lineages [19]. Moreover, epithelial Wnts activate mesenchymal β-catenin to regulate cartilage and basal cell development [20]. In mesenchyme, β-catenin enhances Fgf10⁺ progenitor expansion without affecting their differentiation into smooth muscle cells, whereas endothelial cell proliferation and differentiation depend on β-catenin [21]. During lung regeneration, β-catenin is reactivated in basal cells and promotes their proliferation [22]. Wnt signals promote myoepithelial cell contribution to proximal airway regeneration following severe injury [23]. AT2 cells activate autocrine Wnt signaling to promote progenitor expansion during H1N1 injury [9]. In addition, Frizzled (Fzd) receptors mediate Wnt ligand responses and play cell type-specific roles in lung biology. Fzd2 regulates epithelial morphology via the small GTPase RhoA [24]. Gata6 activates Fzd2 to restrict bronchioalveolar stem cell expansion through inhibition of canonical Wnt signaling [25]. Fzd5/6 are essential for AT2 stem cell function and regeneration post-injury [26]. However, dysregulation of Wnt-Fzd interactions contributes to lung developmental abnormalities and fibrosis, yet their precise roles remain incompletely defined.

In this study, we profiled the expression of Wnt ligands and Fzd receptors across developmental stages and adult lung compartments. Using transgenic mouse models with conditional deletion of Fzd2, we demonstrated that Fzd2 is essential for lung mesenchymal and vascular development and plays distinct, cell type-specific roles through either canonical or non-canonical Wnt signaling pathways. Loss of Fzd2 in mesenchyme impaired lung regeneration in bleomycin-induced fibrosis, leading to a transition from AF1 to AF2 cells. This transition disrupted Wnt2 secretion, further impairing AT2 cell proliferation and alveolar regeneration. Finally, Fzd2 mediates PI3K-AKT signaling to regulate this fibroblast transition, highlighting Fzd2 as a potential therapeutic target for fibrotic lung diseases.

Materials and methods

Mice

All mouse experiments conducted in this study were ethically approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Zhejiang University (approval number: 20221576). The Dermo1-Cre, Tbx4-Cre, Flk1-Cre, Spc-CreER, Fzd2loxp/loxp, β-Cateninloxp/loxp, Flk1loxp/loxp, R26tdTomato, Pdgfra-CreER mouse strains and genotyping methods have been reported previously [17, 27–29]. Vegfa-hypo and Vegfaloxp/loxp mice were kindly provided by Dr. Hoon-Ki Sung of The Hospital for Sick Children. All mice are kept on a mixed genetic background comprising C57BL/6 and BALB/cJ substrains.

These Cre driver lines target distinct cell populations: Dermo1-Cre targets mesenchymal progenitor cells including cardiac mesenchymal cells; Tbx4-Cre specifically targets lung mesenchymal cells; Flk1-Cre targets endothelial cells and some mesenchymal cells, while Spc-CreER and Pdgfra-CreER provide tamoxifen-inducible targeting of alveolar epithelial cells and fibroblasts, respectively. The broad mesenchymal targeting of Dermo1-Cre, particularly in cardiac tissues, may contribute to the observed cardiac phenotypes in addition to lung defects.

Dermo1-Cre, Tbx4-Cre, Flk1-Cre, Spc-CreER and Pdgfra-CreER mice were crossed with Fzd2loxp/loxp mice, β-cateninloxp/loxp or Vegfaloxp/loxp to generate tissue-specific conditional knockout animals. Tamoxifen (T5648, Sigma-Aldrich) was dissolved in corn oil (C8267, Sigma-Aldrich) at 20 mg/ml concentration and administered via intraperitoneal injection. Spc-CreER;Fzd2loxp/loxp, Spc-CreER Vegfaloxp/loxp,and Pdgfra-CreER;Fzd2loxp/loxp mice received tamoxifen at 75 mg/kg body weight on days −8, −6, and −4 relative to bleomycin administration. Age-matched control littermates (Fzd2loxp/loxp mice) received identical tamoxifen treatment to control for non-specific effects of tamoxifen administration. To verify recombination efficiency and confirm Fzd2 deletion, immunohistochemical staining for tdTomato was performed on lung tissue sections from tamoxifen-treated mice. For bleomycin administration, 6- to 8-week-old mice were anaesthetized by intraperitoneal injection of tribromoethanol (S4508, Selleckchem) at a dose of 200 mg/kg. Under anesthesia, a 30-Gauge needle was gently inserted along the tracheal wall to a depth of approximately 0.5–1 cm, and bleomycin (S1214, Selleckchem) was intratracheally instilled in a volume of 100 μL at a dose of 1.5 unit/kg body weight [30, 31]. After instillation, mice were promptly placed in an upright position and gently tapped on the back to ensure even distribution of the solution into both lungs. Control mice were administrated sterile PBS intratracheally. Samples were harvested for analysis at different time points after bleomycin treatment as indicated in the text. For drug treatment, mice were exposed to bleomycin and then treated with 10 mg/kg IWR-1 (Wnt inhibitor, S7086, Selleckchem) dissolved in corn oil at days 6, 8, and 10 following bleomycin treatment [32, 33]. Lungs were collected for analysis at various time points as indicated in the text. Fibrotic areas were quantified from whole-slide digital images of lung lobes using image analysis software ImageJ.

Mouse embryonic lung isolation

Timed pregnant mice were euthanized by CO2 asphyxiation followed by cervical dislocation. Under sterile conditions, the uterus was rapidly removed and placed in ice-cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Individual embryos were carefully dissected from the uterine horns and amniotic sacs under a dissecting microscope. Embryonic staging was confirmed based on crown-rump length and morphological landmarks.

For lung isolation, embryos were positioned ventral side up and the chest wall was opened along the midline using fine surgical scissors. The ribcage was gently separated to fully expose the thoracic cavity contents. Bilateral lung lobes were accurately identified and carefully separated from adjacent tissues (heart, esophagus, trachea, etc.) using fine forceps. Throughout the dissection procedure, particular attention was paid to maintaining the structural integrity of the lung lobes and avoiding mechanical damage. The isolated lung tissues were immediately transferred to appropriate culture medium or preservation solution for subsequent experimental processing.

Tissue preparation, histology and immunostaining

Mice were euthanized by CO2 inhalation following cervical dislocation. Tissue samples were harvested in PBS and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) at 4 ℃overnight. For lung samples, the lungs were perfused with ice-cold PBS through the right ventricle. Lungs were then inflated and fixed in 4% PFA in PBS overnight at 4 ℃. After fixation, samples were dehydrated with ethanol and embedded in paraffin for sectioning as previously described [27]. 5 um sections were then prepared from the paraffin blocks. To prepare cryosections, fixed tissue sample were placed in 30% sucrose and embedded in OCT. 10 um sections were cut from the embedded tissues. For immunostaining analysis, primary antibodies used include Rat anti-Sox2 (1:500, 14–9811-82, Invitrogen), Goat anti-Sox9 (1:500, AF3075, R&D Systems), Rabbit anti-Nkx2.1 (1:1000, ab76013, Abcam), Rabbit anti-Nkx2.1 (1:1000, ab76013, Abcam), Rabbit anti-FZD2 (1:200, PA5-33,544, Invitrogen), Rabbit anti-Cc10 (1:500, 06–263, Millipore), Mouse anti-acetylated-tubulin (1:2000, T7451, Sigma), Rabbit anti-Spc (1:200, ab90716, Abcam), Hamster anti-T1a (1:1000, 8.1.1, Developmental Studies Hybridoma Bank), Rat anti-CD31 (1:100, 550,274, BD Biosciences), Mouse anti-αSMA (1:500, A2547, Sigma), Mouse anti- Anti-β-catenin (1:500, 610,153, BD Biosciences), Rabbit anti-Flk1 (1:200, 9698S, Cell Signaling Technology), Goat anti-tdTomato (1:1000, orb182397, Biorbyt), Mouse anti-Ki67 (1:500, 550,609, BD Biosciences), Rat anti-phosphorylated Histone H3 (pHH3) (1:500, H9908, Sigma), Rabbit anti-cleaved caspase 3 (1:200, 9664S, Cell Signaling), PDGFRa (1:200, ab203491, Abcam), PDGFRb (1:200, AF1042, R&D Systems). Biotinylated or fluorescent secondary antibodies were used for detection and visualization. For hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining, paraffin sections were deparaffinized and rehydrated. Sections were stained with Mayer’s hematoxylin for 1 min, followed by stained with Alcoholic-eosin for 1 min. Alcian blue staining were performed as previously described [27]. Briefly, sections were pretreated with 3% acetic acid solution for three minutes, then stained in Alcian blue solution (A3157, Sigma) for five minutes and counterstained with Nuclear Fast Red (N8002, Sigma). Masson’s trichrome staining was performed by using commercial staining kit (Sigma, H15-1KT) according to the manufacturer’s instruction. Images were obtained using Nikon SMZ1500 Inverted microscope (Nikon). Confocal images were obtained with a Zeiss LSM T-PMT confocal laser-scanning microscope (Carl Zeiss).

Cell culture, gene knockdown and luciferase assay

Human lung microvascular endothelial HULEC-5a cells were maintained in 10% FBS MCDB131 medium with 10 ng/ml EGF, 1 μg/ml Hydrocortisone and 10 mM Glutamine. Flk1 shRNAs were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. shRNA-mediated knockdown was performed as previously described [17]. To test whether the Flk1 promoter is regulated by Wnt/β-catenin signaling, a 2 kb promoter region of the Flk1 gene was cloned into the pGL3 luciferase report vector, and then co-transfected into HULEC-5a cells with CatcLEF, Wnt3a or Wnt5a plasmids. Point mutation of the potential binding sites was generated with QuickChange II XL Site-Directed Mutagenesis kit (Aligent). Luciferase activity was determined 24–48 h after transfection using a dual reporter luciferase kit (Promega).

Lung mesenchymal cells isolation and Fluorescence-Activated Cell Sorting (FACS) for drug treatment

Mice were perfused with PBS from right ventricles to wash out blood in the lungs. Lung tissue digestion was performed as previously described [27]. Single cell suspension was obtained after passing through a 40 mm cell strainer and centrifuged at 1200 rpm for 5 min. Cell pellet was resuspended in a red blood cell (RBC) lysis buffer for 1 min to remove RBCs. Cells were centrifuged and resuspended in 100 ml sorting buffer (5% FBS, 0.2 mM EDTA, and 1% PS in PBS buffer). Dead cells were excluded by LIVE/DEAD Fixable Violet Dead Cell Stain Kit (L34963, Thermo Scientific). Cells were sorted with tdT into the sorting buffer. Data were obtained by using BD LSRII and analyzed with FlowJo software. For drug treatment, sorted mesenchymal cells were treated with 5 uM LY294002 (PI3K inhibitor, S1105, Selleckchem), 1 uM MK-2206 (AKT inhibitor, S1078, Selleckchem), 10 uM Y-27632 (Rho inhibitor, S1049, Selleckchem), 50 uM NSC23766 (Rac inhibitor, S8031, Selleckchem), 10 uM SP600125 (JNK inhibitor, S1460, Selleckchem) or 2 uM AEB071 (PKC inhibitor, S2791, Selleckchem) for 48 h, after which the treated cells were collected for RNA extraction.

Reverse transcription and quantitative real-time PCR

Tissues and Cells were lysed with TRIzol reagent (15596026, Thermo Scientific), and RNA was purified using the RNeasy Mini Kit (74,104, QIAGEN). RNA reverse transcription was performed with EvoM-MLV Premix for qPCR (AG) with 500 ng RNA. cDNA was quantified by real-time PCR using the iTaq Universal SYBR Green Supermix (1,725,122, Bio-Rad) andBio-Rad CFX96 for detection (Bio-Rad, CA, USA). The transcript levels of genes were normalized to Actin expression. All real-time quantitative PCR experiments were performed at least triplicate. PCR primers were designed using the Lasergene Core Suite (DNASTAR Inc.), and the sequences of primers are listed in Supplementary Table 1.

Mouse and human single-cell RNA sequencing

E10.5 lung tissues were digested and then processed following the 10 × Genomics protocol. Cell Ranger pipeline was used to process raw sequencing data, and Cell Ranger R package and Seurat v2.0 pipeline were used to perform downstream scRNA-seq analysis. Differentially expressed genes in the heatmap were identified with the expression levels > 1.5 fold differences between the cell subpopulations. For embryonic (E16.5 and E18.5) and adult mouse single-cell data downloaded from LungMAP (LMEX0000004397), these are preprocessed single-cell RNA sequencing expression matrix data that have already undergone cell clustering [34]. To validate the findings from the mouse scRNA dataset, Human normal lung and Idiopathic Pulmonary Fibrosis (IPF) scRNA data (GSE135893) were downloaded from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO). The data were further processed and analyzed using the Seurat R package. The Harmony function was used to correct the batching effect between samples. All cells were categorized into four major clusters: immune, mesenchymal, endothelial, and epithelial. The mesenchymal cell population was extracted for further subclustering analysis.

Differential expression analysis and functional enrichment

Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using the FindMarkers or FindAllMarkers function from the Seurat R package, which employs the Wilcoxon rank-sum test with Bonferroni correction for statistical analysis. The parameters were set as follows: logfc.threshold > 0.25, p < 0.05, min.pct > 0.25. These criteria were selected to ensure that the identified DEGs exhibited significant differences in expression levels and were expressed at a certain proportion within the cell populations. For each cell cluster or subset, GO biological process enrichment analysis of DEGs was conducted using the ClusterProfiler package (version 4.12.0). In adult mice, DEGs in AF1 and AF2 cells are involved in multiple cellular functions. Through PANTHER (protein class) analysis, cellular functions with counts greater than 25 were selected for plotting.

Trajectory analysis

Monocle2 (version 2.32.0) was utilized to reconstruct the lineage trajectories of AF1, AF2, and myofibroblast cells. Adhering to the workflow of Monocle2, a “CellDataSet” object was constructed. The “differentialGeneTest” function was employed to extract differentially expressed genes (DEGs) from each cell cluster, with only those exhibiting a q-value < 0.01 being retained and subsequently ranked. For the pseudotime analysis, the top 1000 DEGs were selected to order the cells. Following dimensionality reduction and cell ordering, the differentiation trajectories were inferred using the default parameters of Monocle2.

Collagen gene expression analysis

Collagen gene expression levels were assessed by selecting cells labeled as "AF1" or "AF2" from the human scRNA sequencing dataset. The analysis focused on a set of collagen genes with notable expression levels, specifically Col1a1, Col1a2, Col3a1, Col5a1, Col5a2, Col6a1, Col6a2, and Col6a3. The expression data for these genes in AF1 or AF2 cells were extracted using the GetAssayData function and subsequently converted into a long-format data frame to facilitate visualization and analysis. Boxplots were generated using the ggplot2 package (3.5.1) to compare the expression levels of the selected collagen genes between the "Control" and "IPF" groups. Additionally, the Wilcoxon rank-sum test was applied to each gene to statistically evaluate the expression differences between the two groups.

Quantification and statistical analysis

For cell counting in the slides at least five random fields (20 × magnified images) were captured for each section. The data was analyzed using GraphPad Prism version 9.5 software and presented as mean ± SD, derived from experiments with a minimum of three replicates. p values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. *p < 0.05; ** p < 0.01; *** p < 0.001; ns, no significance.

Results

Loss of mesenchymal Fzd2 leads to lung hypoplasia

To elucidate the role of Wnt signaling in lung development, we assessed expression of 16 Wnt ligands and 10 Fzd receptors in E10.5 and E12.5 lungs. Canonical ligands (Wnt2 and Wnt7b) and non-canonical ligands (Wnt5a and Wnt5b) were prominently expressed at both time points (Supplementary Fig. 1 A). Fzd2 and Fzd3 were highly expressed in early lung (Supplementary Fig. 1B). Single-cell RNA sequencing analysis on 5 individual lung tissues at E10.5 revealed that Wnt2 was highly expressed in mesenchyme [7], and Wnt7b was exclusively expressed in epithelium [4] (Fig. 1A-B and Supplementary Fig. 1 C). Non-canonical ligands Wnt5a and Wnt5b were widely distributed in lung mesenchyme and epithelium (Fig. 1B-C and Supplementary Fig. 1D). Fzd genes, especially Fzd2 and Fzd3, were abundantly expressed in both compartments (Fig. 1D and E). Further analysis of ligands and receptors in E16.5 lung tissues [34] revealed dynamic changes in the expression patterns compared to earlier stages of lungs (Fig. 1F-H and Supplementary Fig. 1E), notably reduced mesenchymal Wnt5b and Fzd3 but increased Fzd1 (Fig. 1G-J). Epithelial Fzd2-mediated non-canonical Wnt signaling is crucial for lung epithelial morphology and branching morphogenesis [24]. To determine the role of Fzd2 in the lung mesenchyme, we deleted Fzd2 using the Dermo1-Cre (Fig. 1K and Supplementary Fig. 2 A) and found that Fzd2-null mutants were viable but had markedly smaller lungs by E18.5 [control (28.3 ± 1.4 mg) vs hetero (16.1 ± 2.1 mg) vs homo (8.8 ± 1.4 mg) (p < 0.01)]) with preserved tracheoesophageal separation and branching (Fig. 1L and Supplementary Fig. 2B). In contrast to Dermo1-Cre;β-cateninloxp/loxp mutants, which exhibited embryonic lethality around E14.5 due to severe defects in cardiac development and vasculogenesis [21], Fzd2-null mutants displayed reduced heart size, accompanied by a thinner myocardium and hypoplastic intraventricular septum (Supplementary Fig. 2C-E). Notably, the overall morphology of the liver seems preserved (Supplementary Fig. 2 F). The development of the heart and lungs areclosely coordinated [35, 36]. To determine whether the reduced lung size is due to heart defects, we generated lung-specific Tbx4-Cre;Fzd2loxp/loxp mutants and confirmed that lung hypoplasia occurred independently of cardiac defects (Fig. 1M and Supplementary Fig. 2G-I). Histological analysis revealed that the lung structure was intact in both wildtype and mutant lungs (Fig. 1L-M and Supplementary Fig. 2 J-K).

Mesenchymal Fzd2 is dispensable for differentiation of lung epithelial and mesenchymal lineages

Canonical Wnt signaling is essential for lung specification and trachea-esophageal separation [7, 18]. The specification of lung and esophageal epithelium was unaffected in Fzd2 knockout mutants, as evidenced by the proper expression of Sox2 and Nkx2.1 (Supplementary Fig. 3 A). Proximal–distal patterning appeared normal, with the expected distribution of Sox2 + and Sox9 + cells in the E14.5 mutant lungs (Supplementary Fig. 3B). Consistently, Sox2 expression was preserved in the proximal airways, while Sox9 expression was present in the distal lung in both mutants and controls at E17.5 (Supplementary Fig. 3B). The numbers of club and ciliated cells were comparable between mutants and controls (Fig. 2A and Supplementary Fig. 3 C). The differentiation of alveolar cells remained unaffected (Fig. 2B). No significant differences were revealed in the density of blood vessels (CD31+) between mutant and control lungs at E18.5 (Fig. 2C and Supplementary Fig. 3D). Additionally, the number of smooth muscle cells (SMA+) surrounding both blood vessels and airways and pericytes (PDGFRβ+) surrounding the microvasculature were also preserved (Fig. 2D and Supplementary Fig. 3D). Similarly, the differentiation of both epithelial and mesenchymal components remained intact in Tbx4-Cre;Fzd2loxp/loxp mutants (Supplementary Fig. 3E-F). Loss of Fzd2 reduces proliferation and increases apoptosis in lung development

The hypoplastic phenotype in Fzd2-null lungs prompted us to examine the proliferation and apoptosis. At E10.5, proliferation in both epithelial and mesenchymal compartments was significantly decreased in Fzd2 mutant lungs (Fig. 2E-F). This decreased proliferation persisted at E14.5 and E18.5, accompanied by elevated apoptosis (Fig. 2G-I). Tbx4-Cre;Fzd2loxp/loxp mutants showed similar results (Supplementary Fig. 3G-I), suggesting that mesenchymal Fzd2 supports lung growth by regulating cell proliferation and survival rather than differentiation.

Endothelial Fzd2 is essential for vascular development

Mesenchymal Fzd2 deletion did not disrupt vasculature formation, leading us to examine whether Fzd2 directly regulates endothelial fate. We generated an endothelial-specific deletion of Fzd2 using the Flk1-Cre knock-in allele. The Flk1-Cre;Fzd2loxp/+ heterozygous mutants survived until E12.5-E13.5 and caused severe central nervous system (CNS) hemorrhaging (Supplementary Fig. 4 A), and a marked reduction in the vascular network was observed (Supplementary Fig. 4B). Notably, Flk1 expression was diminished while CD31 expression was retained in the remaining endothelial cells (Supplementary Fig. 4B-C).

We further found that the Fzd2-null lungs exhibited a reduction in the vascular network and a significant decrease in the expression of Flk1 in endothelial cells, resembling the phenotype observed in the CNS tissues (Fig. 3A-C). Similar defects were also found in the hearts (Supplementary Fig. 4D-E). These defects were recapitulated in Flk1-Cre;β-cateninloxp/loxp mutants, which indicates a complete loss of vasculature in CNS tissues [37], suggesting canonical Wnt/β-catenin signaling via Fzd2 is essential for endothelial development (Fig. 3D-E and Supplementary Fig. 4 F). However, αSMA immunostaining did not reveal any obvious differences in smooth muscle structure surrounding blood vessels and airways (Fig. 3F). The vasculature defects observed upon loss of Fzd2 resemble those seen in Shh-Cre;Gpr177loxp/loxp mutants, Wnt7a/7b-null mutants or Flk1-Cre; β-cateninloxp/loxp mutants [6, 17, 37], suggesting that epithelial secreted Wnt7a/7b predominantly signals through Fzd2 to activate canonical Wnt/β-catenin signaling in the embryonic endothelial cells. Mesenchymal Fzd2 is required for lung regeneration following bleomycin injury

Re-analysis of previously published scRNA-seq data revealed that Fzd2 was highly expressed in epithelial and mesenchymal populations (Supplementary Fig. 7 A and B), particularly in multiple mesenchymal cells including alveolar fibroblast 1 (AF1), alveolar fibroblast 2 (AF2) and myofibroblast cells in PND 14–28 (Fig. 5A-B). Pdgfra was widely expressed across these mesenchymal subpopulations, whereas Pdgfrb showed relatively lower expression in AF1 cells (Fig. 5B). Loss of Fzd2 by Pdgfra-CreER;Fzd2loxp/loxp mutants did not affect lung homeostasis (Supplementary Fig. 7C-E). To determine the role of mesenchymal Fzd2 in lung regeneration, we subjected Pdgfra-CreER;Fzd2loxp/loxp mutants to bleomycin (BLM)-induced lung injury. Following BLM administration, both control and Fzd2-loss mutants exhibited significant body weight loss, followed by gradual recovery over a 3–4-week period (Fig. 5C). Remarkably, fibrotic areas were significantly increased in Pdgfra-CreER;Fzd2loxp/loxp mutants when analyzed 14 days post-BLM treatment (28.2% ± 2.9% versus 42.9% ± 5.9%, p < 0.01) (Fig. 5D). Masson’s trichrome staining revealed extensive collagen deposition within the interstitial spaces of mutant lungs (Fig. 5E). The transcript levels of Col1a1 and Col1a2 were also increased in Fzd2 mutant lungs with BLM treatment (Fig. 5F). In addition, mucus metaplasia was observed in the airway epithelium following BLM treatment, with a more pronounced phenotype in Pdgfra-CreER; Fzd2loxp/loxp mutants (21.8% ± 2.9% versus 36.0% ± 2.8%, p < 0.01) (Supplementary Fig. 7F-H). Moreover, severe fibrosis persisted in mutant lungs 8 weeks post-BLM treatment, whereas control lungs displayed partial regeneration of fibrotic lesions and epithelium by this time point (Fig. 5G). Further analysis of the fibrotic areas revealed a significant loss of alveolar type II (AT2) and type I (AT1) cells (Fig. 5H), accompanied by an accumulation of fibroblasts (αSMA+) and a substantial reduction in vasculature (CD31+) intensity (Fig. 5I). A recent study has shown that the transition from AF1 to AF2 is critical for alveolar regeneration following H1N1-induced injury [29]. To examine whether the loss of Fzd2 mediates AF1 to AF2 transition, we performed a signaling pathway enrichment analysis in the fibroblast populations. AF1 cells were characterized by high Pdgfra expression, whereas AF2 predominantly expressed Pdgfrb [29]. We observed increased Pdgfrb expression and decreased Pdgfra expression in Pdgfra-CreER;Fzd2loxp/loxp;RosatdTomato mutants compared with Pdgfra-CreER;RosatdTomato controls treated with BLM (Fig. 6G-H and Supplementary Fig. 9 C). Gene expression profiling of AF1 and AF2 cells identified 466 differentially expressed genes. PANTHER (protein class) analysis identified a significant number of genes encoding components of biological processes such as metabolism, protein interactions and cytoskeletal regulation, some of which have already been implicated in fibroblast development (Supplementary Fig. 9D). Pathway analysis indicated that AF1 cells were enriched in the PI3K-AKT signaling pathway, which potentially functions as a downstream of Fzd2-mediated non-canonical Wnt signaling, whereas AF2 cells are enriched in TGF-β signaling, which contributes to fibrotic pathogenesis (Fig. 6I).

Previous studies have reported that Fzd2 interacts with Dishevelled (DVL) proteins to activate downstream signaling (Gordon and Nusse, J. Biol. Chem., 2006). To investigate this mechanism in our model, we compared the expression of Dvl genes in mesenchymal cells from Pdgfra-CreER;Fzd2loxp/loxp;Rosatdt mutants compared to Pdgfra-CreER;Rosatdt controls. We observed reduced expression of Dvl3, but not Dvl1 or Dvl2, in the mutant cells, suggesting that Fzd2 may primarily signal through a Dvl3–PI3K cascade in lung mesenchymal cells (Supplementary Fig. 9E). To test whether inhibition of the PI3K-AKT signaling affects the transition of AF1 to AF2 cells, we isolated and cultured Pdgfra-CreER;RosatdTomato positive mesenchymal cells and treated with PI3K inhibitor LY294002 or AKT inhibitor MK-2206. We detected decreased expression of Pdgfra and increased expression of Pdgfrb, indicating a reduction in AF1 cells and a concurrent increase in AF2 cells (Fig. 6J-K). Furthermore, Wnt2 expression was reduced following the treatment with PI3K and AKT inhibitors (Supplementary Fig. 9F-G). To explore the potential involvement of other non-canonical Wnt signaling pathways, we tested various inhibitors and found that SP600125 reduced both Wnt2 and Pdgfra expression, whereas AEB071 selectively reduced Wnt2 expression (Supplementary Fig. 9H-K). Furthermore, AF2 cells expressed higher levels of collagens, suggesting their contribution to collagen deposition during fibrotic pathogenesis (Fig. 6L-M).

Discussion

The intricate cellular architecture of the respiratory system highlights the necessity of understanding cell-specific Wnt signaling pathways that regulate lung development and regeneration. Our study elucidates the essential and context-dependent roles of Fzd2-mediated Wnt signaling in mesenchymal and endothelial compartments of the lung. By mapping spatiotemporal expression patterns and dissecting cell-type-specific functions, we show that Fzd2 mediates both canonical and non-canonical Wnt signaling pathways to govern mesenchymal and vascular development. Our results further reveal that mesenchymal Fzd2 is essential for lung injury repair. Conditional deletion of Fzd2 in mesenchymal cells led to impaired lung regeneration in BLM-induced fibrosis by promoting the transition from AF1 to AF2 cells and possibly blocking the reverse trans-differentiation of AF2 cells back to AF1 cells. This shift disrupts Wnt2 secretion and hinders alveolar regeneration. Furthermore, Fzd2-mediated PI3K-AKT signaling is essential for blocking the transition of AF1 into AF2 cells, resembling the key features of human idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) (Fig. 7H). These findings provide new insight into the molecular mechanisms of lung fibrosis and highlight Fzd2 as a critical regulator of fibroblast plasticity.

The roles of Wnt signaling in lung development and regeneration are well-characterized, especially the canonical Wnt pathway [43, 44]. However, the complexity of Wnt signaling arises from the combinatorial interactions of Wnt ligands and Frizzled receptors, which interact in a context-specific manner. Fzd2 is known to bind both Wnt3a and Wnt5a/b, enabling dual regulatory capacity of both canonical and non-canonical Wnt signaling [45–47]. Fzd2 structural similarity to Fzd1 and Fzd7 further complicates interpretation, as redundancy often masks the effect of single-gene loss [21, 48]. Prior studies demonstrate that combined inhibition of Fzd1/2 improves cardiac remodeling in heart infarction models [49]. Wnt5a-Fzd2 signaling promotes the differentiation of immortalized cardiac fibroblasts into myofibroblasts [50]. Wnt-Fzd2 signaling also facilitates the aggregation and organization of cardiomyocytes in the neonatal heart [51]. Given the lethality observed in β-catenin knockout models using Nkx2.5-Cre [52, 53], loss of Fzd2 mediated by Dermo1-Cre results in reduced heart size, suggesting that Fzd2 primarily regulates cardiomyocytes and fibroblasts rather than influencing the formation of heart progenitors. Both Fzd2 and Fzd3 receptors are present in pulmonary mesenchyme. Although Fzd2 has been shown to play a critical role in noncanonical Wnt signaling during lung development [24, 54]. However, we cannot exclude the possibility that Fzd2 also mediates canonical Wnt signaling. Future studies employing combined Fzd2/Fzd3 loss-of-function models will be necessary to directly test this compensatory mechanism, as genetic redundancy among Frizzled receptors has been reported in other developmental contexts [55, 56].

Previous studies also showed that Tbx family genes have been implicated in regulating mesenchymal–epithelial signaling during lung development. Lüdtke et al. reported that loss of Tbx2 leads to downregulation of Wnt activity and impaired lung growth, phenotypes that could be rescued by pharmacological reactivation of Wnt signaling [57, 58]. These findings highlight the importance of Tbx–Wnt interactions in maintaining proper developmental signaling. Consistent with this, our observations that Wnt activation promotes regenerative responses following bleomycin-induced injury further underscore the robustness of this signaling axis across both developmental and injury contexts.

In our study, Fzd2 deletion in endothelial cells recapitulated vascular defects seen in β-catenin knockout models, suggesting that canonical Wnt/β-catenin signaling via Fzd2 is essential for endothelial proliferation and Flk1 expression. Flk1 is a well-known regulator of vascular progenitors and is required for angiogenesis during early development [59–61]. We show that Fzd2 regulates Flk1 transcription directly through β-catenin-mediated promoter activation, confirming its canonical signaling function in the endothelium. We further observed vasculature defects in both single-allele knockout of Fzd2 and double-allele knockout of β-catenin driven by the Flk1-Cre, suggesting a dose-dependent role of Fzd2 in vasculature development.

Fibroblasts exhibit remarkable plasticity and dynamic behavior in tissue repair and homeostasis, serving as a crucial source of growth factors that establish a microenvironment supporting various cell types, including themselves [62]. Spanjer et al. showed that Fzd8 amplifies TGF-β–driven profibrotic signaling in response to bleomycin, thereby contributing to myofibroblast activation [63]. Our data demonstrate that mesenchymal Fzd2 regulates lung regeneration by sustaining a reparative fibroblast niche. In response to injury, AF1 cells, enriched in Pdgfra and Wnt2, promote epithelial repair, while AF2 cells, marked by Pdgfrb and pro-fibrotic genes, contribute to fibrosis [64, 65]. Loss of Fzd2 leads to reduced PI3K-AKT signaling and elevated TGF-β1 signaling, causes a transition of AF1 toward AF2, and suppresses Wnt2 production. Pharmacological inhibition of PI3K-AKT recapitulated this transition, affirming a functional relation between non-canonical Fzd2 signaling and fibroblast identity. Notably, similar fibroblast transitions were observed in human IPF, supporting the critical role of fibroblast dynamics and regenerative capacity in the progression of fibrotic lung diseases. These findings point toward a broader role for Frizzled receptors in modulating TGF-β signaling. These parallels reinforce the concept that Wnt–TGF-β crosstalk is a critical determinant of fibrotic progression.

Given the distinct roles of Fzd2 and the diversity of Wnt signaling pathways, there is an urgent need to identify precise interventional targets for tissue regeneration and pathological processes. Notably, Clostridium difficile infection, a condition characterized by diarrhea, abdominal pain, and, in severe cases, toxic megacolon, is mediated through non-canonical Wnt receptors. Triple knockout of Fzd1/2/7 confers increased resistance to its toxin, highlighting a potential link between Wnt signaling and bacterial pathogenesis [66, 67]. In Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease (COPD) models, elevated Wnt5a expression by fibroblasts suppresses epithelial regeneration by antagonizing canonical Wnt signaling activity, while anti-Wnt5a neutralizing antibody therapy restores tissue repair [68]. A similar result has been observed in a hyperoxia-induced lung injury model [69]. These findings strongly suggest that targeting Fzd2-mediated Wnt pathways by a potent Fzd2 agonist could serve as a potential therapeutic approach for lung fibrosis.

Conclusions

This study reveals that Fzd2 plays dual and distinct roles in regulating lung development, vascular formation, and post-injury regeneration. Fzd2 orchestrates fibroblast identity and vascular integrity by integrating canonical and non-canonical Wnt signaling in a cell-type-specific manner. The dynamic balance between AF1 and AF2 fibroblast states, governed by Fzd2-PI3K-AKT signaling, emerges as a pivotal mechanism in lung repair and fibrosis. Targeting this axis may offer new opportunities for therapeutic intervention in fibrotic lung disease.
