Unexpected Long‐Term Disease Control in Lung Cancer With Sacral Metastases After Conventional Radiotherapy and Chemotherapy Despite Discontinuation of Maintenance Therapy: A Case Report
Authors: Reyhaneh Bayani, Mahdi Khakpour, Behnaz Jahanbin, Nima Mousavi Darzikolaee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTNovel treatments, including stereotactic body radiotherapy (SBRT), targeted therapies, and immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), have improved the prognosis and outcomes of patients with metastatic lung cancer. However, not all patients are eligible for these treatments; furthermore, these novel therapies are not readily available in developing countries. We report a 54‐year‐old man who presented with a sacral mass, initially suspected as a chordoma and underwent subtotal resection. Further evaluation revealed a mass in the lung, confirming the pulmonary origin of the tumor. He received palliative radiotherapy (RT) to the residual sacral lesion, chemotherapy with bevacizumab, and 3D‐RT to a lung lesion. Bevacizumab was discontinued after one year of maintenance therapy following a serious gastrointestinal perforation. Surprisingly, two years after stopping all treatment, he has had no sign of disease progression.

Summary Although the prognosis of lung cancer with bone metastases is generally poor, particularly in patients without access to novel treatments such as stereotactic body radiotherapy, immune checkpoint inhibitors, or eligibility for targeted therapies, some patients may achieve long‐term disease control with a conventional non‐ablative dose of radiotherapy and chemotherapy, even after discontinuing maintenance therapy.

Introduction

Non‐small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) is the leading cause of cancer‐related death all over the world; more than 40% of patients are diagnosed at stage IV [1]. The skeleton is one of the most common sites of NSCLC metastases, with approximately 34% of patients experiencing bone metastases [2]. Compared to patients with non‐small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) without bone metastases, those with bone metastases exhibit a significantly poorer prognosis, characterized by a median overall survival of approximately 5 months and a 5‐year survival rate of 5% [3].

Current management of bone metastases in non‐small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) involves addressing both the primary tumor and metastatic lesions. Several studies have shown that surgical removal or radiotherapy targeting the primary tumor can improve survival outcomes in patients with oligometastatic NSCLC. Treatment of bone metastases typically includes a combination of surgery, radiotherapy, bone‐targeting agents (BTAs), chemotherapy, targeted therapies, and immunotherapy [4, 5, 6].

Preclinical studies have shown that a murine monoclonal antibody targeting VEGF can inhibit the growth of human tumor xenografts, either on its own or in combination with chemotherapy [7, 8, 9, 10]. A humanized version of this antibody has shown clinical activity in human cancers and improves overall survival when combined with standard chemotherapy [11]. Novel treatments, including stereotactic body radiotherapy (SBRT), targeted therapies, and immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), have improved patient outcomes. However, not all patients are eligible for these treatments; furthermore, these novel therapies are not readily available in developing countries.

In this report, we presented a lung cancer patient with sacral metastases and no targetable mutation who demonstrated unusually prolonged progression‐free survival despite subtotal resection of the bone lesion, palliative radiotherapy, and early discontinuation of maintenance therapy.

Case History/Examination

A 54‐year‐old man presented in February 2021 with complaints of progressive low back pain. He did not complain of impairment in force or paresthesia. At referral for oncologic treatment, he complained of severe lower back pain and difficulty sitting. His medical history was unremarkable, with no prior malignancy, recent trauma, or systemic symptoms such as fever, weight loss, or night sweats. He had a 10‐pack‐year history of smoking and no family history of cancer. His Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status was 3 at the time of presentation.

Methods (Differential Diagnosis, Investigations, and Treatment)

Pelvic MRI revealed an abnormal lesion at the S1/S2 level with bone marrow changes, ill‐defined margins, and mass effect on the thecal sac. A core needle biopsy did not identify malignancy. In April 2021, the patient underwent surgery for a presumed chordoma at S1, including hemilaminectomy at L5, total laminectomy at S1/S2, discectomy at L5/S1/S2, and subtotal tumor resection.

Surgical pathology demonstrated fibroconnective tissue infiltrated by a malignant neoplasm with epithelioid features. Immunohistochemistry (IHC) failed to confirm the exact lineage but suggested metastatic carcinoma rather than chordoma. A comprehensive workup—including endoscopy, colonoscopy, and PET‐CT—was performed to identify the primary tumor. While both endoscopy and colonoscopy were unremarkable, PET‐CT (Figure 1) revealed a 20 × 30 mm hypermetabolic mass in the left upper lobe (LUL) hilum (SUV max = 13), two smaller nodules in the LUL (6 mm and 11 mm, SUV max = 1.3), and viable bone metastases in S2 and the left sacral ala (SUV max = 9.9).

A lung biopsy (Figure 2) confirmed poorly differentiated carcinoma. IHC was positive for TTF‐1 and negative for P63, supporting a diagnosis of lung adenocarcinoma. Molecular testing was negative for EGFR mutations and ALK/ROS1 fusion transcripts. Immune checkpoint inhibitors were not affordable for the patient, so PD‐L1 testing was not performed.

Lung parenchyma infiltrated by invasive large cells with hyperchromatic nuclei and light acidophilic cytoplasm. They revealed negative immunostaining for P63 (A, 400X magnification) and positive immunostaining for TTF1 (B, 400X magnification).

The multidisciplinary tumor board recommended palliative radiotherapy (RT) for the sacral lesion, delivered with 3D conformal planning to a total dose of 39 Gy in 13 fractions. SBRT was not available. This was followed by systemic therapy comprising Carboplatin (administered to achieve an AUC of 5 mg/min/mL), Pemetrexed (500 mg/m2), and Bevacizumab (7.5 mg/kg) every three weeks for six cycles. Zoledronic acid was administered every 4 weeks. Bevacizumab was used for maintenance therapy. The patient completed 12 cycles of Bevacizumab, but during maintenance therapy, the patient developed a gastric perforation secondary to bevacizumab toxicity and presented to the emergency department with abdominal pain. Emergent exploratory laparotomy revealed a perforated gastric ulcer, which was managed surgically with primary repair and an omental (Graham) patch. Three months after surgery, the patient had sufficiently recovered to resume treatment for his metastatic lung cancer.

After the life‐threatening event, the patient declined any further systemic therapy and was lost to follow‐up for six months. When he returned, imaging revealed persistence of the primary lung mass and associated stable pulmonary nodules. Based on these findings, the multidisciplinary tumor board recommended definitive chemoradiotherapy (CRT) targeting the thoracic disease. The patient received thoracic radiotherapy at a total dose of 60 Gy in 30 fractions, concurrently with Carboplatin and Paclitaxel. Post‐CRT imaging demonstrated a partial response in the pulmonary lesions and stability of the previously noted sclerotic and lytic bone lesions in the left iliac region.

Conclusions and Results (Outcome and Follow‐Up)

Following CRT, the patient declined further therapy but remained on surveillance—initially every three months, then every six months. Two years later, without any additional treatment, the patient continues to show no evidence of disease progression (Figures 3 and 4). The case timeline is shown in Figure 5.

Discussion

This report presents a patient with a sacral lesion initially presumed to be a chordoma, later identified as metastasis from a primary lung malignancy. The sacral metastatic lesion was managed with subtotal surgical resection followed by conventional palliative radiotherapy. It should be noted that stereotactic body radiation therapy (SBRT) was not readily available at the time. A combination regimen of carboplatin, pemetrexed, bevacizumab, and zoledronic acid was then administered. Bevacizumab was continued as maintenance therapy. Additionally, the patient received conventional radiotherapy to the primary lung tumor. Maintenance therapy was held after a while due to the development of gastrointestinal perforation, which necessitated an emergent surgical intervention. Notably, the patient who had no targetable mutation and did not receive an ablative dose of radiation to the bone lesion is now alive and symptom‐free over four years after diagnosis.

Bone is a common site for metastasis in non‐small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), with approximately 34% of patients experiencing bone metastases [2]. The prognosis for patients with NSCLC and bone metastasis is generally poor, with the one‐year cumulative survival rate after bone metastasis reported at 31.6% [12, 13]. Prognosis is further worsened in patients who lack targetable mutations. Typically, treatment involves a combination of modalities, such as surgery, radiotherapy, chemotherapy, immunotherapy, and targeted therapy.

When examining chemotherapy regimens, none of the four major combinations—cisplatin and paclitaxel, cisplatin and gemcitabine, cisplatin and docetaxel, or carboplatin and paclitaxel—have demonstrated a significant advantage over the others [14]. Recent studies suggest that the addition of bevacizumab to chemotherapy can lead to improved progression‐free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS) [11]. Furthermore, existing literature provides a theoretical rationale for the use of bevacizumab as maintenance therapy, suggesting that it may extend PFS [15].

This case is noteworthy because it challenges the expected poor prognosis typically associated with NSCLC patients with bone metastases, especially in the absence of targetable mutations and no access to ablative SBRT and ICIs (Immune Checkpoint Inhibitors). This is especially important for patients in developing countries with limited access to these novel treatments. Another point worth mentioning is that while PFS has typically been reported as 6 to 10 months for patients receiving bevacizumab maintenance therapy, with disease progression commonly occurring after discontinuation of bevacizumab [15], our patient has demonstrated no evidence of disease progression over two years after cessation of maintenance therapy. This can suggest that a subtotal resection of metastases and a palliative dose of radiation could lead to durable symptom control and local disease stability in some NSCLC patients with bone involvement. Furthermore, it raises the possibility that continuous maintenance therapy until disease progression may not be necessary for all patients. This finding implies that the concept of “planned treatment breaks” or “temporary cessation” of systemic therapy can be a viable option for some patients, especially when considering potentially life‐threatening treatment‐related side effects, as seen in our case [16, 17, 18]. Moreover, this case highlights that clinical stage alone may not adequately predict outcomes among patients with advanced lung cancer. Patients presenting with similar clinical features, such as bone metastases, may harbor distinct biological profiles that significantly impact disease behavior and treatment response.

Comparable long‐term survival in NSCLC with bone metastases has been reported only rarely in the literature. Hou et al. reported a 76‐year‐old woman with NSCLC and spinal bone metastasis who underwent cementoplasty and local radiotherapy combined with long‐term gefitinib plus zoledronic acid; she achieved survival exceeding eight years while continuing targeted therapy. Similarly, Shintani et al. described a 70‐year‐old man with EGFR‐mutated NSCLC and bone metastases who achieved a pathological complete response under ongoing Osimertinib monotherapy. In contrast to these cases, our patient had no actionable mutations and discontinued maintenance bevacizumab after only one year due to life‐threatening toxicity, yet demonstrated sustained disease stability for over two years without systemic therapy. This comparison underscores that while durable control of bone metastases is typically reported in the context of continuous targeted therapy and favorable molecular profiles, selected patients lacking targetable mutations may also achieve unexpectedly prolonged progression‐free survival after multimodality conventional treatment and early therapy cessation [19, 20].

Conclusions

We presented a lung cancer patient with sacral metastases and no targetable mutation, lacking access to ICIs and SBRT, who demonstrated unusually prolonged progression‐free survival despite subtotal resection of the bone lesion, palliative radiotherapy, and early discontinuation of maintenance therapy due to a life‐threatening complication possibly related to anti‐VEGF treatment. This case indicates that, for some patients, conventional non‐ablative radiotherapy and chemotherapy might contribute to durable disease control in bone metastases. It also suggests that continuous maintenance therapy until disease progression might not be necessary in every case. Additionally, it underscores the potential importance of underlying molecular and genetic factors in influencing prognosis, which may complement traditional clinical staging.

Limitation

The unusually prolonged progression‐free survival observed may be influenced by individual biological and genetic factors that were not fully characterized, as comprehensive molecular testing was not performed. The lack of PD‐L1 testing and the absence of immune checkpoint inhibitor therapy limit comparisons with contemporary standard‐of‐care approaches.
