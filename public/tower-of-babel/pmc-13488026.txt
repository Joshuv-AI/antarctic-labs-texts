Quality of Temporomandibular Disorder Videos on Social Media
Authors: Fei He, Xuteng Kang, Peiding Wang, Haoqi Tu, Yihao Ding, Chonghao Yao, Zhe Zhang, Xin Yang, Junyi Wu, Junbo Tu, Sijia Na
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction and aimsVideo-sharing platforms are key health information sources, but poor oversight and variable quality may mislead patients. This study assesses the quality, reliability, and dissemination of TMD videos on major platforms, and examines whether user engagement aligns with information quality.MethodsA cross-sectional content analysis was conducted of TMD-related videos retrieved from YouTube, TikTok, and Bilibili using platform-specific search strategies. The top 100 videos from each platform were screened, and 260 eligible videos were included. Two independent reviewers assessed information quality using the modified DISCERN (mDISCERN), Global Quality Scale (GQS), and Video Information and Quality Index (VIQI). Video characteristics, uploader type, content features, and engagement metrics were extracted. Between-platform comparisons were performed using nonparametric tests, and associations between quality scores and engagement metrics were examined using Spearman correlation analysis.ResultsOverall information quality was moderate and varied significantly across platforms. YouTube videos scored higher on mDISCERN, GQS, and VIQI than TikTok and Bilibili (all P < .001). Medical professional videos were of higher quality than those from patients or self-media. However, higher-quality videos did not correlate with greater dissemination; lower-quality videos tended to attract more views, likes, comments, and shares, with only weak correlations observed.ConclusionsTMD-related health information on video-based social media platforms shows substantial variability in quality and a clear mismatch between informational value and public reach. Platform-specific dissemination patterns may allow lower-quality content to achieve substantial reach, underscoring the need for strategies that improve the visibility of credible medical information.Clinical relevanceTMD-related video quality varies widely across major social media platforms, and high engagement does not equate to reliability. Joint efforts by clinicians, educators, and platform designers are needed to deliver trustworthy and accessible health information to those who need it most.

Introduction

In the digital age, online video platforms have become a primary channel for patients seeking preliminary health information, reflecting a broader shift from traditional clinician-led consultations toward proactive self-education.1, 2, 3 While this trend can empower health consumers, it also introduces considerable risks due to the lack of content regulation and the highly variable quality of available videos. Such conditions facilitate the spread of misinformation, which may adversely influence patients’ health decisions and clinical outcomes.4, 5, 6 These concerns are especially relevant in the context of temporomandibular disorders (TMD) – a set of conditions involving pain and dysfunction of the masticatory muscles and temporomandibular joint.7 As the most common form of orofacial pain, TMD frequently presents with localized facial or preauricular pain, restricted mandibular movement, and audible joint sounds during function.8,9 Such symptoms often drive individuals to search for explanatory information online.

Despite the abundance of TMD-related videos on major platforms – including the global service YouTube and the regionally dominant platforms TikTok (short-form) and Bilibili (long-form) – a systematic evaluation of their content quality remains lacking. This gap is critical, as the distinct structure and user incentives of each platform may shape the type and reliability of information presented. To address this issue, we conducted a cross-sectional analysis of TMD-related videos across these three platforms. This study aims to systematically profile uploader sources and content features, and to assess video quality using validated evaluation tools.10, 11, 12 The ultimate goal is to help patients identify reliable health information and to offer evidence-based recommendations for content creators and platforms to improve the quality of public health communication.

Methods

Search strategy and selection approach

On August 28, 2025, a systematic search was performed across three video-sharing platforms: YouTube, TikTok, and Bilibili. The search term ‘Temporomandibular disorder’ was used on YouTube, while the Chinese equivalent ‘颞下颌关节紊乱’ was employed on TikTok and Bilibili. To mitigate the influence of algorithmic personalization, all searches were conducted in a logged-out state and after clearing browser history and cookies. The default sorting order was adopted for each platform without applying any filters to simulate the typical experience of a new user. During the initial screening, promotional or advertising content was excluded. Consistent with previous studies,10,12 the top 100 videos from each platform were retained for further screening. A subsequent 1-week screening period was implemented, during which videos were excluded based on the following criteria: (1) irrelevance to TMD; (2) duplication or substantial content overlap; (3) publication within the preceding 7 days (to ensure stable viewership metrics); and (4) partial data missing. Evaluating methodologies of the videos

The reliability and content quality of the videos were evaluated using the modified DISCERN scale (mDISCERN), the Global Quality Score (GQS) scale, and the Video Information and Quality Index (VIQI). Before the evaluation, the participating physicians involved in the assessment familiarized themselves with the basic knowledge of TMD and the official instructions of these three scales, so that they could evaluate the accuracy and completeness of the video content while following the guidelines.

The mDISCERN was adapted from the DISCERN tool and is more suitable for assessing video material.13 It consists of five questions related to the reliability of the video. Each question is scored 1 for ‘yes’ and 0 for ‘no’. Higher scores correspond to more excellent reliability. The GQS, a 5-point scale, assessed overall video quality, ranging from poor (1) to excellent (5).14 The VIQI tool encompasses four dimensions: information flow (VIQI 1), information accuracy (VIQI 2), quality (videos including one point for each image, animation, interview, video captions, and summary) (VIQI 3), and precision (level of coherence between video title and content) (VIQI 4). Each criterion is rated on a scale of 1 to 5, with higher scores indicating better quality.15 The mDISCERN, GQS, and VIQI instruments have been validated in previous studies for assessing health information quality on social media platforms.16,17

Data collection and evaluation of video reliability and content quality

The primary outcomes of this study were: (1) platform-specific video characteristics (including uploader profiles and production styles), (2) cross-platform differences in quality scores, and (3) the correlation between video quality and audience interaction. Differences in quality scores across uploader types and video styles constituted the secondary outcomes.

Key video information was systematically documented for subsequent analysis. Quantitative engagement data were systematically recorded, including video length, duration, cumulative views, thumbs up, comments, collections, and shares. To capture recent trends, 30-day metrics for views, thumbs up, comments, collections, and shares were also collected where available. Due to platform-specific parameter differences, two data points were unavailable: (1) the number of collections and shares on YouTube, and (2) the number of views on TikTok. Furthermore, given the varying user engagement and market penetration across platforms, the magnitude of the collected data may differ accordingly. For each video, uploader information was also documented, comprising a unique identifier (ID), platform verification status, and classification. Uploaders were categorized into one of five types: doctor, other medical worker/student, patient, nonprofit organization (eg, hospitals, academic departments, professional associations), and self-media. Furthermore, content attributes pertaining to originality and production style were evaluated. Videos that were direct reposts, translations, or generic informational compilations were deemed nonoriginal. Styles of video shooting were defined as solo narration, question-and-answer (Q&A), PPT/Class, animation/action, or others.

The quality of all included videos was independently rated by two senior physicians using the predefined assessment tools. The inter-rater reliability between the two physicians was assessed using Cohen’s kappa (κ) statistic. For any scoring discrepancies between the two initial raters, a third senior physician served as an adjudicator to reach a final consensus. In this study, Spearman’s rank-order correlation was used to examine the relationship between video quality and its interaction indicators. However, the VIQI score inherently incorporates traffic-related components such as views and thumbs up. To avoid potential confounding from these factors, the VIQI score was excluded from the correlation analysis, which was therefore conducted solely on the mDISCERN and GQS scores.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (Version 27.0). The normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Normally distributed data were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), while non-normally distributed data were summarized as median with minimum, maximum, and interquartile range (P25, P75), denoted as M (P25, P75). Cohen’s kappa (κ) statistic was used to quantify the inter-rater agreement between the two physicians for categorical outcomes. According to the criteria proposed by Landis and Koch: κ values >0.80 indicated excellent agreement; 0.61 to 0.80, substantial; 0.41 to 0.60, moderate; and ≤0.40, poor agreement.18 For group comparisons, the Mann-Whitney U test was employed for non-normally distributed continuous variables. Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages (n, %), and compared using the Chi-square test, with continuity correction or Fisher’s exact test applied as appropriate. A pairwise comparison approach was utilized to identify specific differences among the three video platforms. The relationship between audience engagement metrics and video quality scores was examined using Spearman’s rank-order correlation. The correlation coefficient (r) indicated the direction and strength of the relationship: |r| ≤ 0.2, negligible; 0.2 < |r| ≤ 0.4, weak; 0.4 < |r| ≤ 0.6, moderate; 0.6 < |r| ≤ 0.8, strong; and |r| > 0.8, very strong. A two-tailed P-value of less than .05 was considered statistically significant for all tests. Missing data were handled using pairwise deletion. Consequently, all available data for each specific pair of variables were utilized in the correlation and regression analyses to maximize statistical power. In the results, statistical significance is denoted as follows: *P < .05, **P < .01, and ***P < .001. Given the relatively small sample size, no adjustment for multiple comparisons (eg, Bonferroni correction) was applied.

Significant disparities in video basic characteristics were observed across the 3 platforms. Bilibili hosted the longest videos (260.5 [120.25-516.25] seconds), significantly exceeding the length of videos on TikTok (89.5 [55-151] seconds) and YouTube (57 [29.25-269] seconds). Regarding content longevity, videos on YouTube had been online for the longest duration (984.5 [399.75-1625] days), significantly longer than those on Bilibili (581.5 [205.75-1278] days) and TikTok (116.5 [54.75-242] days). User engagement patterns also varied substantially. While view count data for TikTok was unavailable, YouTube videos amassed significantly higher cumulative view counts (114,806 [36,448.75-485,757.25] times) than those on Bilibili (13,000 [3006-136,250] times). Analysis of recent interaction trends (30-day metrics) revealed that TikTok consistently led in all engagement increments, including thumbs up, comments, collections, and shares (all P < .001 for relevant pairwise comparisons). Specifically, the median 30-day thumbs up on TikTok (310.5 [42.25-1375.25] times) far surpassed those on YouTube (21.5 [0-83.25] times) and Bilibili (3 [0-12] times). In contrast, YouTube demonstrated notably lower 30-day interaction increments across most metrics compared to its substantial historical cumulative data.

Uploader characteristics

This study identified 72 unique uploaders on YouTube, 76 on TikTok, and 46 on Bilibili, respectively (Table 2). The proportion of doctors among uploaders was comparable across all three platforms (YouTube: 26.4%; TikTok: 22.4%; Bilibili: 26.1%), with no statistically significant differences. Notable disparities were observed in other uploader categories. Patients constituted a significantly larger proportion of uploaders on TikTok (40.8%) than on YouTube (0%) or Bilibili (4.3%). Nonprofit organizations posted content exclusively on YouTube (9.7%). Self-media accounts represented the primary source of videos on both YouTube (54.2%) and Bilibili (54.3%), a proportion significantly higher than that on TikTok (30.3%). The composition of verified uploaders also varied by platform. Among verified accounts, doctors comprised the vast majority on TikTok (85.0%) and Bilibili (73.3%). Video categorisation

Analysis of video originality and production styles revealed distinct platform-specific patterns. Original videos constituted the majority on all three platforms (Table 3). YouTube hosted the highest proportion of original content (97.9%), which was not significantly different from TikTok (92.6%) but was significantly greater than that on Bilibili (80.6%), where nonoriginal content (eg, reposted or translated videos) was more prevalent. Substantial variation was observed in video shooting styles. Solo narration was the most common format on TikTok (45.7%), with its prevalence being significantly higher than on both YouTube (20.2%) and Bilibili (27.8%). The PPT/Class format was nearly exclusive to YouTube (16.0%), being entirely absent on TikTok (0%) and not significantly different in presence from Bilibili (9.7%). Information quality and reliability

Inter-rater agreement was substantial, with a Cohen’s kappa of 0.637. Detailed scoring criteria and item-specific results are provided in Table 4. Overall, YouTube videos demonstrated significantly higher quality across most dimensions compared to TikTok and Bilibili, while the latter two platforms exhibited relatively similar quality levels. In terms of informational reliability, although YouTube videos achieved the highest median mDISCERN total score (4), this difference from TikTok and Bilibili (both 3) was not statistically significant. However, significant interplatform differences emerged in specific items. YouTube videos substantially outperformed both TikTok (61.7%) and Bilibili (72.2%) in clearly stating treatment aims (mDISCERN-1, 95.7%). Conversely, YouTube videos showed a critical deficit in mentioning alternative treatment options (mDISCERN-4, 8.5%), with a proportion far lower than that of TikTok (58.5%) and Bilibili (56.9%). Regarding overall quality, the median Global Quality Scale (GQS) score of YouTube videos (4) was significantly higher than that of TikTok and Bilibili (both 3). Discussion

Principal findings

The rapid expansion of video-based social media platforms has fundamentally altered how patients access and interpret health information, yet whether widely disseminated content provides reliable guidance remains uncertain, particularly for common chronic conditions such as temporomandibular disorders. In this cross-platform analysis of YouTube, TikTok, and Bilibili, we found that the overall quality of TMD-related videos was moderate and highly variable, with content produced by medical professionals demonstrating higher informational quality but substantially lower public reach than lower-quality videos. This discordance between information quality and dissemination may reflect, at least in part, the influence of platform-specific recommendation mechanisms, although further research is needed to establish causal relationships. However, this study did not directly measure platform algorithms, and this interpretation should therefore be regarded as hypothesis-generating. From a health promotion and education perspective, these findings suggest that good information does not always reach the people who need it. Helping patients find accurate and reliable videos should be a priority for improving public health education.

Video characteristics

This comparative analysis reveals three distinct content ecosystems for TMD-related videos. Video length patterns reflect platform orientations: Bilibili specializes in medium‑to‑long content prioritizing depth, TikTok emphasizes short‑form consumption, and YouTube occupies an intermediate position with flexible format adaptation. Content longevity further differentiates them. YouTube’s significantly longer median video survival time reinforces its role as a stable knowledge repository, whereas TikTok’s rapid content turnover exemplifies a ‘fast‑consumption’ model. This temporal dimension has crucial implications for health information accessibility – YouTube preserves educational content over time, while TikTok’s ephemeral nature may limit long‑term utility despite its engagement advantages.

The paradox between engagement and information quality warrants attention. TikTok shows strong short‑term interactions but only moderate professional quality scores, revealing a ‘high‑engagement, medium‑quality’ gap. This may be partially driven by platform‑specific recommendation mechanisms favouring interaction potential, though this remains a hypothesis. Similar patterns have been observed for other health topics, including H. pylori infection, gallstone disease, mpox, and lung cancer.19, 20, 21, 22 For patients seeking TMD information, engagement metrics like likes and shares are not reliable proxies for clinical accuracy. Over‑reliance on popular videos could lead to misleading advice, inappropriate self‑management, and worse outcomes. As for platform traits, YouTube’s longer content lifecycle and broader viewership may support sustained health communication, while TikTok’s format suits concise, visually engaging messages.21,23 These differences suggest tailored strategies: professionals could create systematic educational content on YouTube and bite‑sized evidence‑based tips on TikTok, helping patients access trustworthy information across platforms.

Uploader characteristics

The three platforms show markedly different uploader structures, reflecting distinct content sourcing paradigms. Physicians maintain a consistent presence across platforms (22.4%-26.4%), forming a stable foundation for professional content. TikTok, however, exhibits a distinctive patient‑led composition: patient uploaders account for 40.8% of its contributors – far exceeding the other platforms. This pattern reflects TikTok’s low creative threshold and strong social interactivity, which promote personal experience sharing as the dominant health communication mode, consistent with findings across multiple disease domains.17,24,25 While overall verification rates vary little across platforms (26.3%-36.1%), the composition of verified uploaders reveals profound differences in verification priorities. TikTok and Bilibili prioritize authenticating medical professionals: verified doctors represent 85.0% and 73.3% of verified accounts, respectively, establishing a profession‑oriented ecosystem. In contrast, YouTube’s verification system appears more creator‑focused, with over half (53.8%) of verified accounts belonging to self‑media creators and only 23.1% to certified doctors. This divergence may reflect platform‑specific verification policies,16,26 though disease‑specific factors cannot be excluded – as seen in a laryngeal carcinoma study where YouTube had the highest proportion of verified non‑profit organizations.16

Critically, verification signifies only account authenticity, not content quality or professional rigor. These findings yield important implications: users should evaluate content reliability through the combined lens of uploader type and verification status, while platform operators should consider refining verification systems to facilitate clearer identification of healthcare professionals, thereby enhancing the overall quality and trustworthiness of health information ecosystems.

Video categorisation

While original videos dominate across all three platforms, Bilibili has a notably lower proportion of original content. Although content originality has often been overlooked in prior research, it is important for both creators and platforms. However, the lack of a standardized assessment for originality remains a limitation, and this study is subject to some subjective judgment. Existing literature suggests that Bilibili’s copyright policy may partially explain its lower originality rate.16 Accordingly, content creators should maintain strong copyright awareness and consider protective measures such as watermarking. Caution is also needed when reposting or translating third-party materials to avoid infringement. Platforms, in turn, should strengthen protective mechanisms to incentivize high-quality original content.

Video production styles differ notably across platforms. TikTok favours simple solo narration for personal storytelling, a format popular on the platform.24,27,28 YouTube, with higher production value, more often features animated or action-oriented content. Bilibili accommodates a broader diversity of formats, reflecting its hybrid community structure. From a health promotion perspective, these stylistic differences affect how effectively health information reaches audiences. For example, the PPT/Class format enables systematic delivery of health information but its longer duration and higher production demands may limit accessibility. In contrast, formats like solo narration or animation can be strategically combined to create concise, engaging, and patient-friendly content, improving comprehension and retention. Understanding these format-specific strengths can help health educators and creators tailor messages for maximum educational impact across platforms, ultimately supporting better-informed patients and more effective public health communication.

Information quality and reliability

Research shows that evaluation tool choice significantly influences health video assessment, a finding consistent with our study.29,30 Although mDISCERN, GQS, and VIQI are widely used, our results reveal notable limitations when applied to cross‑platform and short‑form content, especially on TikTok. As shown in Table 4, YouTube scored significantly higher than TikTok and Bilibili on all three tools but was markedly lower on ‘mention of alternative treatment options’ (mDISCERN‑4). This raises whether existing tools adequately capture information completeness and clinical decision support, or inadvertently favour certain formats. The applicability of these instruments to short‑video platforms deserves scrutiny. TikTok videos performed moderately on ‘clearly stating treatment goals’ (mDISCERN‑1) but had low overall reliability and production quality – partly due to many non‑professional patient uploaders,17,24,25 and partly because the short format inherently limits information depth. Constrained by duration, short videos struggle to elaborate on complex pathology or treatment risks, placing them at a natural disadvantage. In contrast, YouTube, with more professional creators and a stable content environment, led in information accuracy (VIQI‑2) and title‑content consistency (VIQI‑4). Bilibili scored in the mid‑range on most metrics, and its lower‑originality ecosystem may cap overall quality. From a health promotion perspective, these findings highlight the need to develop or adapt evaluation frameworks that account for different video formats’ strengths and limitations, thereby providing more meaningful guidance for patients and educators navigating diverse health video content.

Platform quality disparities may stem from design and cultural contexts. YouTube’s algorithm may favour content that sustains longer engagement, potentially to maximize watch time. TikTok’s feed appears designed for continuous novelty, prioritizing immediate appeal over depth – though this remains speculative – and its low production barriers, combined with the viral potential of emotional narratives, could further drive dissemination. Bilibili’s model seems to prioritize distribution within close-knit follower networks and shared interest tags, encouraging original content but also tolerating reposts. This insular dynamic, with limited external exposure, may contribute to its moderate quality. Beyond algorithms, a cross-cultural perspective matters. YouTube dominates in Western countries, where TMD knowledge and medical education may be more advanced than in China, where TikTok and Bilibili lead. This disparity in professional consensus and training quality could independently affect TMD content reliability and depth, regardless of algorithms. Thus, improving online health information requires not only algorithmic adjustments but also stronger professional education and cross-cultural exchange. Helping patients access trustworthy, evidence-based content across platforms remains a shared goal for researchers, educators, and platform designers.

Based on Figure 2, video quality is strongly associated with uploader background. Doctors and non‑profit organizations consistently achieved higher quality scores, whereas patient‑uploaded videos often rely on personal anecdotes that may inadvertently mislead others.19,31, 32, 33, 34, 35 Video style also matters: animation/action outperformed solo narration in information delivery and production quality, while PPT/class‑style videos – though comprehensive – were less common due to higher production and comprehension demands.24,27,28 To advance health education, we offer the following recommendations. Medical practitioners should be encouraged to produce more high‑quality content. Video platforms could add labelling or guidance for patient‑uploaded videos (eg, disclaimers about anecdotal nature) and provide templates, tutorials, or incentives for evidence‑based formats. End‑users need digital literacy programs emphasizing verification of uploader credentials and cross‑referencing with trusted sources. Content creators should adopt engaging yet accurate styles, such as combining animation with clear narration. Finally, fostering collaboration among clinicians, educators, and platform designers is essential to ensure reliable, accessible health information reaches those who need it most.

In summary, the quality of TMD-related videos on YouTube surpassed that of Bilibili and TikTok, a result consistent with comparative studies of these platforms across other medical topics.16,26,28 Given differences in platform accessibility and user ecosystems across regions, cross-platform dissemination of high-quality TMD information may help broaden access to reliable educational content. The following cross-platform dissemination strategy is recommended: high-quality content creators on YouTube should consider distributing their videos on domestic platforms to benefit Chinese audiences, while exemplary domestic uploaders are encouraged to translate and upload high-quality Chinese videos to YouTube to facilitate global knowledge exchange on TMD.

Relationship between video quality and information flow

Based on empirical evidence, video interaction metrics demonstrate strong internal consistency, as do the GQS and mDISCERN instruments in assessing video quality. However, only weak-to-moderate correlations were observed between user engagement indicators and professional quality ratings – a finding consistent with prior research.36, 37, 38, 39, 40, 41 Thus, high engagement does not reliably indicate high informational quality. Videos may gain popularity through entertainment, emotional resonance, or other non‑quality factors unrelated to accuracy or comprehensiveness. For the public, over relying on likes or shares when evaluating health information is risky. Greater emphasis should be placed on source professionalism and intrinsic accuracy. For platform operators, integrating professional quality assessments alongside engagement signals in recommendation algorithms may be worth considering. Such a balanced approach could better support the dissemination of content that is both appealing and scientifically sound.39,42,43 Due to sample size limitations, our correlation analysis was performed on the combined dataset. Future studies with larger samples should examine whether these correlations differ across platforms (eg, YouTube vs TikTok), which would inform platform‑specific quality enhancement strategies.44

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, despite using three validated tools and having three trained physicians evaluate the videos, some subjective bias remains inevitable. Second, although we employed strategies to minimize personalized recommendations, the inherent complexity of platform algorithms means this influence cannot be fully eliminated. Third, we did not analyse textual elements such as video descriptions or user comments, and some engagement metrics were unavailable due to platform restrictions. These missing data limited our ability to capture potentially key or negative audience feedback. Additionally, effect sizes for platform comparisons were not calculated; we reported only P‑values. Although our main findings had extremely low P‑values (all <.001), the lack of effect sizes (eg, Cohen’s d or η²) limits interpretation of the practical magnitude of observed differences. Fourth, because the data were collected within a specific temporal and cultural context, generalizability to other linguistic or regional settings may be constrained. Future research could improve by using natural language processing to analyse video transcripts and descriptions, or by developing algorithms to automatically assess video quality, reducing reliance on manual scoring and enabling larger‑scale evaluations.

Conclusions

This study systematically examined the characteristics and quality ratings of TMD-related videos across three major platforms. Overall, YouTube demonstrated superior video quality with extended content longevity. Short-form platforms such as TikTok exhibited stronger interactivity but moderate informational quality, potentially due to the high proportion of patient uploaders. Bilibili maintained its long-form video platform characteristics while showing a relatively lower proportion of original content. The monologue and animation/action formats emerged as the most prevalent production styles across platforms. These findings offer practical value for multiple stakeholders. The public can utilize the quality assessment results as a reference for identifying appropriate educational content on TMD. Content uploaders are encouraged to develop higher-quality videos that align with both user preferences and platform-specific characteristics. Platform operators should consider refining recommendation algorithms to balance popularity with scientific reliability in health information dissemination.
