Reducing health inequalities in the deaf community: a priority setting exercise
Authors: Anna Selby, Alison Jones, Helen Radford, Daniel Hind
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe Deaf community experiences health inequalities including higher mortality rates, increased prevalence of chronic conditions, and poorer access to healthcare services compared to the general population. Despite calls for urgent research investment and meaningful community engagement, no research agenda developed by and for the Deaf community exists to address these inequalities. Traditional priority-setting exercises often exclude or dilute the perspectives of underrepresented groups. This study aimed to identify and prioritise research questions to address health inequalities experienced by Deaf British Sign Language (BSL) users in the UK, using Research Prioritisation by Affected Communities (RPAC) methodology to ensure community voices were centred without external filtering.MethodsA Deaf-led steering group adapted RPAC methodology for BSL accessibility. Two rounds of focus groups were conducted entirely in BSL led by Deaf facilitators. Round one (January 2025) included 43 Deaf BSL users across eight groups who shared experiences of healthcare receipt across seven NHS services. The 294 extracted statements were categorised using a conceptual framework for understanding healthcare disparities in disability, then refined into nine research themes by Deaf public contributors. In round two, 38 participants (87% retention) prioritised themes using a resource allocation exercise where each participant distributed £1 million across themes. Themes were ranked by total allocation.ResultsEnvironmental factors (222 statements) substantially outnumbered individual factors (72 statements) in round one. The highest priority theme was ‘process’ (28% of votes), focusing on enabling direct NHS communication without telephone barriers. ‘Deaf awareness for NHS staff’ ranked second (25%), followed by ‘interpreters’ (17%) and ‘technology’ (13%). Individual-level interventions received minimal prioritisation: ‘Deaf health empowerment’ (1%) and ‘Deaf health champions’ (0.6%). Participants emphasised systemic failures including non-compliance with the Accessible Information Standard and lack of BSL interpretation.ConclusionsDeaf BSL users prioritised systemic NHS changes over individual interventions, indicating that institutional barriers must be addressed before individual-level approaches can be effective. The emphasis on process improvements and staff awareness training has immediate implications for NHS policy and Accessible Information Standard (AIS) enforcement. These findings should inform research commissioning, with priority given to co-designed interventions addressing communication processes and developing effective Deaf awareness training for healthcare professionals.

Background

The Deaf community is a diverse and vibrant linguistic and cultural minority, with British Sign Language (BSL) at its centre [1]. Although the Census reports BSL as the main language for 22,000 people in England and Wales, this significantly underestimates the Deaf population; more robust estimates suggest figures closer to 151,000 [2]. Against this demographic backdrop, a substantial body of evidence shows that Deaf people experience persistent and wide-ranging health inequalities.

Systematic reviews and meta-analyses demonstrate that Deaf people have poorer overall health and increased all-cause and cardiovascular mortality when compared with the general population [3]. Across the NHS CORE20PLUS5 priority clinical areas, Deaf adults, children and young people experience disproportionate burdens of disease [4, 5]. Higher prevalence of obesity, heart disease and diabetes has been consistently documented, alongside greater likelihood of receiving a late-stage cancer diagnosis [6, 7]. These disparities are mirrored in preventive care. A systematic review of cancer screening shows that disability, including deafness, is associated with reduced odds of screening attendance [8]. Similarly, Deaf pregnant women receive fewer antenatal visits, experience longer post-delivery hospitalisations and have an increased risk of caesarean delivery [9]. Oral health follows the same pattern: Deaf adults and children have lower rates of toothbrushing and higher rates of tooth decay [10]. Alongside physical health inequalities, the Deaf community carries a higher prevalence of anxiety and depression [11, 12], prompting ongoing work to review signed mental health assessments [13].

Evidence suggests that these patterns arise from structural and systemic barriers rather than individual behaviour. A conceptual model of hearing health inequalities highlights the interaction between socioeconomic disadvantage, health behaviours, limited health literacy and exposure to lower-quality care [14]. Deaf people repeatedly encounter barriers at the point of accessing services: 36% report it is “not worth” seeing their GP due to communication difficulties [15]. When they do attend, consultations often rely on lip-reading or written notes instead of BSL, undermining both trust and care quality. Even when interpreters are present, they may be unqualified or junior, while family members are sometimes used instead—raising concerns about confidentiality, autonomy and decision-making [15]. A recent scoping review confirmed the clinical impact of these barriers, linking poor communication with negative health outcomes [16].

Communication inequities extend beyond individual encounters to public health messaging. Deaf people generally have lower levels of written literacy and reduced exposure to biological and health concepts [17–19]. Public health campaigns—designed for hearing audiences—often use metaphors, visual symbolism or spoken communication that fail to translate directly into signed languages. As a striking example, some French Deaf people in the 1990s believed that suncream could prevent HIV because the virus was depicted as a sun in campaign materials [19]. During the COVID-19 pandemic, Deaf individuals were more likely to rely on social media rather than official government communication [18]. Compounding these issues, NHS services frequently fail to comply with the Accessible Information Standard (AIS) [20], leaving over 80% of Deaf people reporting negative impacts on their physical and mental health due to inaccessible communication [21].

Mental health disparities cut across all these themes. Higher rates of anxiety and depression reflect not only broader social determinants but also chronic exposure to communication barriers and inaccessible services [11, 12]. The lack of validated mental health tools in BSL further widens the gap, prompting current attempts to develop and evaluate signed assessments [13].

Some progress has been made. A systematic review identified several intervention approaches, including routine use of BSL during care, adaptation and validation of clinical tools, Deaf awareness training, development of tailored healthcare facilities, digital and e-health solutions, and accessible educational programmes [22]. There has also been movement toward producing BSL versions of key mental health questionnaires, such as the Generalised Anxiety Disorder Assessment (GAD-7) and Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9) [13]. Models promoting health equity for Deaf populations have been developed in the United States, demonstrating that structured approaches are possible [22].

Yet despite these efforts, systematic reviews consistently conclude that research involving Deaf people requires urgent investment and must meaningfully engage Deaf communities throughout design, implementation and evaluation [19, 23]. Existing research has largely been conducted on Deaf people rather than with them, seldom centring their expertise on which inequalities matter most or how they should be addressed [16].

Crucially, there is no Deaf-led research agenda to guide investment or service development. Priority-setting exercises often marginalise underrepresented groups [24, 25], and for the Deaf community these challenges are magnified by communication barriers and hearing-centric research approaches. Without a structured, accessible and community-driven method of identifying priorities, research risks continuing to overlook the issues most important to Deaf people.

This study was developed as a partnership between the Deaf community, NHS and academic researchers. The study was funded by a NIHR programme development grant - Developing Innovative, Inclusive and Diverse Public Partnerships Call (NIHR207088). The oversight group agreed the geographical, time and population scope of the work including the beneficiaries and target audience of the priority exercise and established a leadership, management and governance plan.

Traditional priority setting partnerships, the most well established being the James Lind Alliance, rely on academic literature and clinical/academic stakeholders with community representation from patient advocacy groups [26]. However, these partnerships do not address the challenge of including under-represented groups and therefore the priorities set may not reflect the uncertainties of minority groups [24]. Where under-represented groups have been included, they may have their views diluted during the multiple prioritisation phases [25]. This limitation is particularly problematic for the Deaf community, where communication barriers and power imbalances with hearing professionals can further marginalise their perspectives. To address the aim of identifying priorities directly from Deaf BSL users without external filtering, a methodology was required that would centre their lived experience throughout the process.

Newer methodologies are being utilised to overcome these barriers including Research Prioritisation by Affected Communities (RPAC) protocol [27]. This method avoids privileging the views of professionals or academic stakeholders and enables the lived experience of the underrepresented communities to shape the research agenda without external filtering.

RPAC methodology was developed to prioritise the unanswered research questions of the affected community and has been used to set priorities for women of colour at risk of pre-term birth, young people with chronic illness and parents with opioid dependence [28–30]. The RPAC method involves 5 stages: planning, round 1 of facilitated group work, interim analysis, round 2 of facilitated group work including prioritisation and finally summary analysis.

Aim and objective

This study was part of a wider National Institute for Health and Care Research (NIHR) programme development grant exploring factors that influenced receipt of healthcare by Deaf people. The study aimed to identify and prioritise research questions that address the health and healthcare inequalities experienced by Deaf BSL users in the UK. The objective was to engage directly with the Deaf community to co-develop a research agenda using (RPAC) methodology. This method centres the affected community’s voice to identify and prioritise research questions. This ensures these priorities inform future research commissioning and service development.

To meet our objective of co-developing a research agenda with the Deaf community, we adapted the RPAC approach to ensure full accessibility and cultural appropriateness. These included conducting all Deaf facilitator led focus groups in BSL with qualified BSL interpreters, Deaf relay workers and bilingual (English/BSL) users providing translation into spoken English for transcription purposes. This approach directly addressed our aim of engaging with the Deaf community by removing communication barriers that typically exclude them from research participation [27].

Method

Scope and governance

A steering group was established consisting of Deaf public contributors (n = 5), university academics (n = 1) and an NHS manager (n = 1). The steering group defined the scope and context of the priority setting exercise focusing on the experiences of healthcare receipt in the UK for Deaf people with BSL as their primary language. Recordings of the focus groups were transcribed from BSL to written English and a thematic analysis conducted to elicit themes. At a second meeting participants were asked to prioritise the themes. The target audience of the priorities are researchers, policy makers and healthcare delivery organisations. The prioritisation exercise aimed to develop a research agenda to inform future research commissioning by UK research funders (including NIHR), policy-makers (NHS England, Department of Health) and healthcare delivery organisations (NHS Trusts). Research priorities encompassed all types of research questions including health services research, implementation research, and intervention development, though this was not pre-defined. Priorities were intended as 5-year goals with plans for review and update. The methodology used is outlined in Fig. 1.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the University of Sheffield (local ethics number 064184) prior to commencement of the first round of focus groups and in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. To aid inclusion, any written material, including participant information sheets and consent forms, were interpreted in BSL signed videos. These adaptations were essential to achieving our objective of ensuring that the priorities identified would genuinely reflect the Deaf community’s perspectives and could inform future research commissioning and service development. Informed consent was acquired prior to commencement of the focus groups.

Focus Groups Round 1 - experiences of healthcare receipt

Participant recruitment

The prioritisation exercise was advertised on social media (Facebook), through an interpreter charity client databases and Deaf networks. People who expressed an interest were invited to attend. All those who expressed an interest were invited to attend a group. Language proficiency was not assessed but all those who attended were first language BSL users. Face to face groups met at the Derby Deaf community hall and online groups were via Zoom. A separate group aimed at 16–19 year olds was carried out at the Royal School for the Deaf Derby, although participants did not have to be connected to the school. In total, eight focus groups were conducted at round 1 and two at round 2. Reimbursement for time was £75 per participant per focus group. This honorarium compensated participants for their time and expertise in line with NIHR public involvement payment guidance.

Participant inclusion

Participants were included in the focus groups if they were first language Deaf BSL users living in the United Kingdom.

Participants demographics

Postcodes were used to determine a deprivation score using a web based GB Postcode Deprivation Finder. Inclusion criteria specified Deaf BSL users residing in the UK, with no exclusions based on age, ethnicity, or socioeconomic status. The strategy deliberately excluded healthcare professionals and researchers to avoid diluting community perspectives, diverging from traditional priority setting approaches. Forty-three individuals participated in round one and 38 in round two (87% retention). Organisations involved included Communication Unlimited and Royal School for the Deaf Derby.

Focus group delivery

Following training, members of the Deaf community who served on the steering group facilitated the focus groups.

Focus group participants were asked to share their experiences of healthcare receipt in 2023, 2024 and 2025. This timeframe was selected to capture current healthcare inequalities that should inform immediate research priorities, aligning with our aim of developing an actionable research agenda. This enabled current experience, particularly post COVID pandemic, to be captured. Deaf people report healthcare receipt post COVID to be poorer than pre-COVID as ongoing adaptations to healthcare made during the pandemic, including mask wearing, has had a detrimental effect on a Deaf person’s ability to access healthcare.

Participants were asked to share their experiences of receipt of seven discrete aspects of the English NHS health delivery provision: General Practitioner (GP), dentist, acute medical services (including emergency department, same day care and 111 telephone triage service), hospital inpatient stay, hospital outpatient appointments, community pharmacy and mental health services. Questions set prior to the group were not used, instead participants were asked to share their experiences of healthcare in the seven discrete areas. Participants, BSL interpreters and focus group facilitators were able to ask questions and take ad hoc breaks throughout the focus group. Focus groups lasted an average of 2 h each with timing proportional to the number of participants present. Each participant attended only one focus group and all participants were encouraged to share their thoughts on each of the seven NHS service areas. The focus group was conducted in BSL. Focus groups were video recorded, including audio from BSL interpreters present, for subsequent transcription and analysis. At least two BSL interpreters were present at each focus group. All BSL interpreters were registered with the National Register of Communication Professionals working with Deaf and Deafblind People (NRCPD). All video recordings were held on a secure server that only the study team could access.

Data extraction and categorisation

Focus group recordings were transcribed verbatim into a Microsoft Word document. Each transcription was independently checked to ensure accuracy. Statements about receipt of healthcare were extracted into a Microsoft Excel spreadsheet. Data extraction and categorisation was performed independently by two coders with 20% overlap for quality assurance. Discrepancies were resolved by discussion and where a consensus could not be reached, the senior author arbitrated. Statements were categorised according to a conceptual framework for understanding healthcare disparities experienced by individuals with disabilities [31]. Overall categories were individual (patient) level factors and environmental (systematic) factors. In line with the conceptual model, individual factors were then subdivided into biological, social and cultural, impairment related, or psychological factors. Environmental factors were subdivided into products and technology, natural and human-made changes to environment, support and relationships, attitudes, health delivery system and services, systems and policies. All 294 statements were retained and incorporated into the nine themes to ensure comprehensive representation of participant views. The steering group discussed and agreed the nine themes in line with the conceptual framework. The steering group did not check whether identified priorities had already been answered through existing research, as the focus was on community-identified needs rather than research gaps.

Refining of statements into research themes

The categorised statements were reviewed by the steering group and under PPI guidance divided into nine research themes. Four themes were based on the individual factors and five themes on the environmental factors. Titles for the themes were carefully chosen to ensure the same meaning in written English and BSL signs. The individual themes were Health information, Deaf health empowerment, NHS and Deaf culture, and Deaf health aware. The environmental themes were Interpreters, Process, Deaf awareness for NHS, Technology and Deaf health champions.

Focus Groups Round 2 - prioritisation of research themes

The prioritisation phase was crucial to meeting the objective of identifying which research areas the Deaf community considered most important for addressing their health inequalities. This ensured that future research commissioning could be directed toward the areas of greatest need as defined by the community itself.

Participants who attended the first round of focus groups were invited to attend the second stage focus groups. All focus groups at stage two were face to face. Facilitation was carried out by the same Deaf steering group members as round one. The themes were discussed and participants had the opportunity to ask questions and clarify the different themes. Themes were presented in a random order at each focus group to reduce bias. Participants were given 30 min after theme discussions to consider importance before voting. Each participant was then given 4 stickers that represented £1 million and asked to allocate their stickers to one or more of the themes. They were able to use their allocation either all on one theme or divided across multiple themes. Participants allocated their stickers independently. At the end of voting at each session, the results were then shared with the participants.

Prioritisation criteria

Participants allocated resources based on their assessment of which themes would most improve healthcare access and outcomes for Deaf people. No explicit criteria such as feasibility or novelty were imposed, allowing participants to apply their own lived experience in determining importance.

Research theme ranking

The number of stickers allocated to each theme at the second round focus groups were counted and an overall number calculated for each theme. The research themes were then ranked from top priority to lowest priority.

Evaluation and dissemination

Participant feedback evaluating the focus group process was not formally collected. Dissemination plans included a BSL results video published on social media, particularly via Deaf online communities, publication in peer-reviewed health service journals, direct communication with NIHR and other research funders, and BSL-interpreted summaries distributed through Deaf community networks.

Implementation and impact

Implementation strategies include direct engagement with research commissioners, integration into NIHR funding calls, and advocacy through Deaf organisations. Impact will be evaluated through tracking of funded research projects addressing identified priorities and periodic review of policy changes.

Discussion

Statement of principal findings

Deaf BSL users prioritised systemic NHS process changes and Deaf awareness training for NHS staff over individual-level interventions such as health empowerment or health champions.

Process changes were highlighted as a top priority as participants felt that enabling Deaf people to be able to communicate directly with the NHS would be most beneficial. The NHS predominately communicates with patients via telephone or letters. This results in Deaf people being either unable to book or change appointments without using an interpreter or attending in person to book an appointment and relying on pen and paper to communicate. Participants reported that NHS departments often phone Deaf people to arrange appointments despite their patient record stating that they are Deaf. Similarly, healthcare professionals often call a patient’s name when taking them from the waiting room to their appointment. This leaves Deaf people feeling anxious about whether they have missed their name being called. Participants aged 18–20 relied heavily on family to book medical appointments for them and often family acted as the interpreter rather than a professional. These participants want processes to change so they are able to book and attend appointments alone especially to discuss private health concerns.

Deaf participants also felt that NHS appointment letters are often unclear and containing lots of medical jargon that they find inaccessible. The Accessible Information Standard (AIS) is a legal requirement in the UK for NHS and adult social care organisations to provide access to information and communication in a format that that all those with a disability or sensory impairment can understand [21]. Currently, there is no process in the NHS to ensure that this legal requirement is met.

Participants ranked Deaf awareness amongst healthcare professionals as a second priority. They highlighted that if healthcare professionals are aware of a Deaf person’s needs then they would be able to adapt their care accordingly. For example, arranging an interpreter to be present during consultation and treatment or ensuring that continuity of care is maximised for Deaf people. Healthcare professionals often do not understand Deaf culture and how to relate to a Deaf person, for example, knowing how to wake them up when they are asleep in a hospital bed. Participants felt that it was important that Deaf awareness was not only for current NHS staff but future staff with the incorporation in healthcare professional training courses.

The top two priorities identified above align with a recent systematic review showing persistent communication barriers and lack of cultural competence in healthcare delivery to Deaf patients [16]. Rogers et al. similarly identified organisational and structural barriers as primary concerns [16]. Multiple reviews report healthcare professionals’ lack of Deaf awareness resulting in insensitive practices [32, 33]. Communication between healthcare professionals and Deaf people has been shown to be rarely effectively, except in very simple specific situations [10, 34]. Deaf people report less value in healthcare consultations [35] along with fear, mistrust and frustration [32].

Interpreters and technology were ranked third and fourth. These two themes are linked. Participants wanted more interpreters trained as they preferred a face to face interpreter but recognise the need for a virtual interpreter out of hours or for urgent care. However, participants had poor experiences of virtual interpretation as often hardware like tablets or laptops were unavailable to stream the interpretation, or Wi-Fi was unstable. Participants also expressed concerns about the appropriateness of using virtual BSL interpretation to deliver bad or complex health diagnoses. According to the Royal National Institute for the Deaf (RNID) there is a shortage of trained BSL interpreters with approximately 1 BSL interpreter per 60 BSL users [36]. Literature supports the role of interpreters, with BSL interpreters reducing communication barriers [10, 19, 34, 37, 38]. Conversely, a lack of interpreter provision impacted access to services [39]and exacerbated difficulties in understanding health information [32]. While other studies have suggested that telehealth services may overcome barriers to interpreter access, improve healthcare access [40] and communication [34, 37, 41–43] for Deaf people. However, how face to face and remote access to interpreters compare is unknown [44]. A systematic review on the efficacy and effectiveness of telemedicine intervention concluded that there is a significant evidence gap and called for further research co-designed with Deaf people [44]. There are also concerns about the use of artificial intelligence to create BSL avatars as this could lead to inaccurate or inappropriate interpretation and a loss of rich language heritage [45].

Participants felt that if NHS processes and Deaf awareness for NHS staff was improved then the Deaf community would feel more confident in accessing healthcare. They raised concerns about the scope of Deaf health champions and felt that healthcare professionals were better placed to offer advice. Some BSL interpreted health awareness videos are available [46] and, while participants wanted more, they did not feel that it was as high a priority.

Strengths and weaknesses of the study

Strengths of this study include the use of RPAC methodology which centred Deaf community voices without external filtering, Deaf-led facilitation throughout, full BSL accessibility, and high retention between rounds (87%). The methodology successfully adapted to meet the cultural and communication needs of the Deaf community.

Weaknesses include limited geographic diversity, with participants predominantly from one UK region despite the Deaf community being geographically dispersed. The study lacked representation from coastal communities, recognised as underserved in CORE20PLUS5 [4]. The study participants were also predominately white and further research should be done to explore the experiences of non-white Deaf populations.

Unlike traditional JLA priority setting partnerships, healthcare professionals and other stakeholders were excluded, which enhanced community voice but limited triangulation with clinical perspectives. The study was also limited to BSL users and did not include deaf people who use other communication methods.

Meaning of the study

The prioritisation of process changes and staff awareness over individual empowerment suggests that institutional barriers must be addressed before individual-level interventions can be effective. The top priority of the need for Process improvements indicates that basic accessibility failures—such as telephone-only booking systems—represent the most urgent barrier to healthcare equity. This has immediate implications for NHS compliance with the AIS, suggesting enforcement rather than education may be needed. The low prioritisation of Deaf health champions challenges assumptions that peer support models are universally valued and suggests that Deaf people want qualified healthcare from trained professionals, not substitutes.

The lower prioritisation of themes like Deaf health empowerment suggests that addressing institutional barriers may be a necessary precursor to individual-level interventions. Several literature reviews have shown that while health education, particularly cancer related, delivered with sign language adaptation increases knowledge among Deaf people, it is unknown whether these interventions result in Deaf patients seeking and receiving related healthcare [47, 48].

These prioritised themes will be disseminated to relevant research funders, policy-makers and healthcare organisations to inform future commissioning decisions, in line with RPAC’s emphasis on actionable community-driven research agendas [27].

The themes identified in this priority setting partnership are similar to those in a recently published systematic review exploring Deaf Sign Language users’ experiences as patients where issues were themed according to patient centred (individual) and health care delivery system (structural) categories [16]. Fitting with the top priority of process, the review identified organisational and structural barriers to healthcare access. In addition, the review showed many healthcare professionals do not demonstrate cultural knowledge, competency and skills when treating Deaf people. The confirms the need for Deaf awareness training across all sectors of healthcare [16].

Unanswered questions and future research

Further research should be conducted with the Deaf community to understand the rationale behind their prioritisation choices to ensure that any policy and practical changes match the expectations of the community.

Key questions remain about how to implement the prioritised changes effectively. Intervention studies are needed, co-designed with Deaf people and healthcare providers to test process improvements and staff training models. Research should examine whether addressing top priorities improves health outcomes and healthcare satisfaction. The optimal balance between face-to-face and virtual interpretation requires investigation, particularly for complex consultations. Cost-effectiveness analyses of systemic changes versus individual interventions are needed to inform commissioning decisions. Future work should also explore whether these priorities are consistent across different UK regions and whether they apply to deaf people using communication methods other than BSL.

Conclusion

Deaf BSL users prioritised systemic NHS changes over individual-level interventions, with Process and Healthcare professionl Deaf awareness rated highest. Deaf people’s top priorities reflect legal failures in communication access (AIS), suggesting immediate policy relevance. Findings support targeting systemic and staff-level barriers rather than public health education alone. Results validate the RPAC model for Deaf BSL users and highlight its potential scalability to other sensory-impaired communities. Further work should test implementation of high-ranked themes (e.g. NHS communication process redesign).
