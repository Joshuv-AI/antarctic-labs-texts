Research in context

Evidence before this study

Intrahepatic cholestasis of pregnancy (ICP) is a pregnancy-specific liver disorder associated with adverse foetal outcomes. While genetic and hormonal factors have been implicated in ICP pathogenesis, the role of gut microbiota in ICP development remains largely unexplored. Previous studies have primarily focused on post-diagnostic evidence, lacking insights into early pregnancy microbial patterns and their temporal dynamics throughout pregnancy.

Added value of this study

Using a large prospective birth cohort with repeated microbiota data across three pregnancy periods, we identified distinct gut microbial signatures associated with ICP development in early pregnancy. We demonstrated that early microbial alterations were significantly associated with ICP development and severity, and timing of onset. Our network-based analyses and longitudinal investigations revealed progressive gut microbiota dysbiosis in women with ICP, which highlights the dynamic nature of ICP-associated microbial changes. Importantly, we identified hepatic markers as crucial mediators in the microbiota-ICP relationship and established a potential mechanistic link between gut microbiota perturbations and adverse pregnancy outcomes.

Implications of all the available evidence

Our findings demonstrate that women with ICP are characterised by distinct alterations in the gut microbiota composition. The identification of specific microbial signatures and their temporal dynamics offer substantial insights into the ICP pathogenesis. This evidence supports the potential value of early pregnancy microbiome screening and suggests that interventions targeting gut microbiota might be a potential strategy for ICP prevention and management.

Introduction

Intrahepatic cholestasis of pregnancy (ICP) is characterised by unexplained distressing pruritus and elevated bile acid in pregnant women during the second or third trimester of pregnancy. It poses significant risks to the foetus, including sudden intrauterine foetal demise, preterm labour, and meconium-stained amniotic fluid.1, 2, 3, 4, 5 Given that the aetiology and pathophysiology of ICP remain largely unknown, identifying risk factors for ICP has strong clinical relevance for the prevention and treatment of ICP.

Gut microbiota has been reported to be involved in the pathogenesis of ICP.6, 7, 8, 9, 10 Compared with uncomplicated pregnancies, an altered composition of the gut microbiota has been demonstrated in women with ICP.6,9,11, 12, 13 In particular, Blautia and Bacteroides fragilis were reported to be positively associated with ICP development, while Megamonas and Ruminococcaceae showed inverse associations.6,9,11,13 The altered microbial community structure in women with ICP could potentially lead to pathological changes in bile acid metabolism and various lipid and protein metabolism pathways.8,11,14 However, existing studies on this topic have utilised either a cross-sectional or case–control design, and have only assessed the microbiota alterations after ICP onset.6,9,11,13 Moreover, these studies were limited by relatively small sample sizes, ranging from 40 to 91 participants (including 20 to 50 women with ICP).6,9,11 The differences in study design, sample sizes as well as demographic characteristics could also lead to discrepancies in the microbial taxa identified as being associated with ICP. In addition, the association of the gut microbiota with ICP in early pregnancy and the dynamic microbial changes during pregnancy remain unclear.

To address the knowledge gap, using repeated 16S rRNA sequencing data collected at three timepoints throughout pregnancy (early, middle and late pregnancy) in a large cohort, we aimed to (1) assess the associations of gut bacteria in early pregnancy with the development of ICP; (2) assess the dynamic alterations of gut bacteria in women with ICP compared to those without ICP; and (3) explore the role of hepatic markers in mediating the influence of gut microbiota on the development of ICP.

Methods

Population and design

The ongoing cohort study is part of the Tongji-Huaxi-Shuangliu Birth Cohort (THSBC) that has been conducted at the Shuangliu Maternal and Child Health Hospital since 2017 in Chengdu, China. The criteria for participant inclusion and exclusion have been detailed in previous publications.15 A total of 7281 pregnant women aged 18–40 years, attending their initial prenatal appointments early in pregnancy (within 15 weeks of gestation) were enrolled. Participants attended regular antenatal visits, during which blood and fecal samples were collected in early (<16 gestational weeks; T1), middle (24−28 gestational weeks; T2), and late pregnancy (>29 gestational weeks; T3). Structured questionnaires were administered to collect comprehensive data on sociodemographic characteristics, lifestyle behaviours, and reproductive history. For the current analyses, we included 4596 women with available 16S rRNA sequencing data from early pregnancy.

Ethics statement

The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Tongji Medical College, Huazhong University of Science and Technology, Wuhan, China (approval number: 2017-S225). All procedures were conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrolment.

Diagnosis of ICP

ICP was diagnosed in women with pruritus and a fasting serum total bile acids (TBA) level ≥10 μmol/L.2,4,5 To diagnose ICP, other potential causes of pruritus or any abnormalities in laboratory indicators were ruled out. Mild ICP was diagnosed when pregnant women exhibit fasting serum TBA levels between 10 and 39 μmol/L, with pruritus as the primary clinical symptom and no significant additional symptoms. Severe ICP was characterised by serum TBA levels of ≥40 μmol/L. Due to the limited number of ICP cases with onset before 28 weeks of gestation, we used 32 weeks of pregnancy as the cutoff point to investigate the association between gut microbiota alterations and ICP onset timing.

Birth outcomes

Gestational age was determined based on the weeks since the first day of the last menstrual period, with subsequent adjustment using ultrasound findings. Preterm birth (PTB) was defined as delivery occurring before 37 completed weeks of gestation. Iatrogenic preterm birth (IPB) was defined as medically indicated delivery before 37 weeks of gestation due to maternal and/or foetal complications requiring medical intervention, whereas spontaneous preterm birth (SPB) was defined as spontaneous onset of labour or premature rupture of membranes resulting in delivery before 37 weeks of gestation.

Clinical measurements

Hepatic markers including alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), total bile acid (TBA), and glycocholic acid (GCA) were assessed using a Hitachi 7180 automatic biochemical analyser with commercial kits from Sichuan Maccura Biotechnology, Chengdu, China. These measurements were conducted in the hospital's clinical laboratory at or around the time of enrolment. Prior to the analysis, hepatic markers were log-transformed and standardised into Z scores.

Fecal sample collection and storage

A total of 6973 fecal samples were collected from 4956 women across early (n = 4956), middle (n = 1026), and late (n = 991) pregnancy. Fecal samples for middle and late pregnancy were obtained from a subset of the 4956 women. Fecal samples were collected and stored in ice boxes, either at home or at the hospital, and were promptly transferred to the hospital within 24 h. Participants received detailed instructions on how to collect, temporarily store, and transport the fecal samples. All samples were stored at −40 °C. The frozen fecal samples were periodically transported to the laboratory at the School of Public Health, Huazhong University of Science and Technology every 2–3 months using dry ice and were maintained at −80 °C for long-term storage. All materials used underwent systematic aseptic processing.

16S rRNA gene sequencing

Genomic DNA was isolated using the PF Mag-Bind Stool DNA Kit (Omega Bio-tek, Georgia, USA) following the manufacturer's guidelines. Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) amplification of the V3–V4 region of the bacterial 16S rRNA gene was performed using primers 338F (5′-ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCAG-3′) and 806R (5′-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3′). The PCR reaction mixture comprised 4 μL of 5 × Fast Pfu buffer, 2 μL of 2.5 mM dNTPs, 0.8 μL of each primer (5 μM), 0.4 μL of Fast Pfu polymerase, 10 ng of template DNA, and ddH2O to a total volume of 20 μL. The amplification protocol began with an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 3 min, followed by 27 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, annealing at 55 °C for 30 s, and extension at 72 °C for 45 s, with a final extension at 72 °C for 10 min, and cooling at 4 °C. PCR products were extracted from a 2% agarose gel and purified using the Illumina PE250 platform (Illumina, San Diego, USA) according to standard protocols by Shanghai Majorbio Bio-Pharm Technology Co. Ltd. (Shanghai, China).

Sequencing data were processed using the Quantitative Insights into Microbial Ecology 2 (QIIME2, version 2024.2).16 Paired-end reads were denoised and inferred to amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) using the DADA2 plugin. To reduce the influence of potential contaminants, only ASVs present in more than one sample were retained. We constructed a phylogenetic tree using the ‘align-to-tree-mafft-fasttree’ pipeline and rarefied samples to a sequencing depth of 10,000 reads to calculate phylogenetic diversity metrics, including Faith's phylogenetic diversity (PD), weighted UniFrac, and unweighted UniFrac distances. Taxonomies of ASVs were performed using the Naive Bayes classifier trained on the Silva 138 99% reference database. PICRUSt2 was employed to predict potential microbial metabolic pathways,17 with pathway annotations based on the MetaCyc database. For subsequent association analyses, microbial genera and metabolic pathways with low prevalence (<10%) and low mean abundance (<0.01%) were excluded. The remaining compositional data were subsequently transformed using the centred log-ratio (CLR) method, followed by Z-score standardisation across all samples.

Statistical analyses

Longitudinal profiles of microbiome diversity in ICP and non-ICP participants

We calculated α-diversity indices at the genus level, including Chao1, Shannon, Simpson, and Pielou's evenness. Differences in these indices and Faith's phylogenetic diversity (PD) between the ICP and non-ICP participants at each timepoint were assessed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Linear trends in α-diversity across pregnancy within each group were tested using linear mixed-effects models with random intercepts for participants, adjusting for baseline maternal age, education level, pre-pregnancy body mass index (BMI), parity, smoking status, alcohol intake, and sequencing batch. Temporal changes between groups were assessed by including a group-by-time interaction term in the mixed-effects models, with significance determined by the P value for the interaction term. β-diversity was assessed using distance matrices, including weighted UniFrac, unweighted UniFrac, and genus-level Bray–Curtis distances. These matrices were visualised through principal coordinate analysis (PCoA). Group and time differences were tested using permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) with 999 permutations. The same tests were applied to compare α- and β-diversity between subgroups of ICP, including mild versus severe and early-onset versus late-onset cases. Genus-level diversity calculations and PERMANOVA were performed using the R package vegan, and linear mixed-effects models were implemented using the R packages lme4 and lmerTest.18,19

To evaluate the longitudinal patterns of microbial community dissimilarity, the weighted UniFrac distance matrix was partitioned into groups based on sample characteristics, including within-individual pairs, within-period pairs, within-ICP individual pairs, within-control individual pairs, and within specific–period pairs. Differences in pairwise weighted UniFrac distances among these groups were assessed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test.

Association between microbial genera and ICP

Since only a subset of participants from the initial THSBC of 7281 pregnant women were included in the current analyses due to limitations in data availability, we applied inverse probability weighting to address potential selection bias.20 Specifically, participation probabilities were estimated using logistic regression based on participant characteristics, calibrated with the R package CalibrationCurves,21 and their inverse values were used as weights in subsequent association analyses. To investigate the associations between microbial genera and ICP at each timepoint, weighted logistic regression from the R package survey was utilised,22 adjusting for baseline maternal age, education level, pre-pregnancy BMI, parity, smoking status, alcohol intake, gestational weeks at fecal sample collection, and sequencing batch. To assess microbial genera differences across pregnancy periods, weighted linear mixed-effects models with random intercepts for participants were used, adjusting for the same covariates. These methods were also used to compare microbial profiles between ICP subgroups, including mild versus severe and early-onset versus late-onset cases. P values were adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) method,23 with genera showing PFDR < 0.05 considered differentially abundant. Heterogeneity across pregnancy periods was assessed using Cochran's Q test implemented in the R package metafor.24

To investigate the temporal structure of differential genera identified in early pregnancy, the variance explained by gestational age was quantified as the difference in R2 between two linear mixed-effects models (each with participant-specific random intercepts)—with and without gestational weeks as a fixed effect. We then identified temporal clusters of log odds ratios for ICP-associated genera using the k-means for longitudinal data as implemented in the R package latrend.25 The number of clusters was tested from 1 to 10, and the optimal solution was selected based on local maxima in average silhouette width (ASW), minimum Bayesian information criterion (BIC), and elbow-point criteria for weighted mean absolute error (WMAE) and weighted residual sum of squares (WRSS).

Correlations among differentially abundant genera in early pregnancy were estimated using the CCLasso. CCLasso is specifically designed to infer correlations in high-dimensional compositional data.26 To account for potential selection bias, we modified the cov function to cov.wt within the cclasso function from the R package NetCoMi enabling incorporation of sample weights into the covariance estimation.27 The strongest 5% of correlations were visualised.

Dynamic co-abundance networks of microbial genera in ICP and non-ICP participants

For co-abundance network analyses, we applied the modified CCLasso method to assess correlations between gut microbial genera at three timepoints in both ICP and non-ICP groups. An absolute correlation coefficient (ρ) threshold of 0.4 was used for network visualisation. Node importance was quantified using eigenvector centrality, which identifies central nodes based on their connections to other important nodes in the network. The top 5% of nodes with the highest centrality scores were considered network hubs. Network modules were detected using the Louvain community detection algorithm. To evaluate temporal and group-specific patterns of network connectivity, we calculated threshold-dependent metrics, including module size, number of nodes, number of edges, modularity, clustering coefficient, and positive edge percentage. To assess the robustness of these findings, networks were constructed using absolute ρ thresholds ranging from 0.2 to 0.6. Additionally, threshold-free metrics were computed, including the median, 75th percentile, and 90th percentile of absolute correlation coefficients, as well as NetMoss scores.28 95% confidence intervals for correlation coefficient quantiles were estimated via bootstrap resampling (n = 999), and differences between the ICP and non-ICP groups were compared using paired Wilcoxon rank-sum tests. NetMoss scores were calculated for each node at each timepoint to quantify its importance to network structural transitions between groups. Network construction, visualisation, and topological analyses were performed using the R package NetCoMi, while NetMoss scores were computed using the R package NetMoss2 with default settings.

Mediation analyses for marker genera, hepatic markers and ICP

Stable ICP marker genera were defined as the intersection between early differentially abundant genera and the top 30% of genera with the highest NetMoss scores in the early pregnancy network. To examine associations between marker genera and hepatic markers (ALT, AST, AST/ALT ratio, TBA, and GCA) in early pregnancy, weighted partial Pearson's correlation coefficients were calculated using the R package weights, adjusting for the covariates described above. P values were FDR-adjusted, with PFDR < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

To further understand the role of marker genera and hepatic markers in the association with ICP, we conducted mediation analyses via the R package mediation.29 For significant genus–hepatic marker pairs, two alternative mediation models were tested: (a) marker genera influence ICP through hepatic markers (forward mediation), and (b) hepatic markers influence ICP through marker genera (reverse mediation). P values for mediation proportions were FDR-adjusted, with a positive mediation proportion and PFDR < 0.05 considered statistically significant. Given data types and feasibility of sensitivity analyses, weighted linear regression was used for the mediator model and weighted probit regression for the outcome model, both implemented using the R package survey. Sensitivity analyses were performed using the medsens function (rho.by = 0.01) to assess the robustness of the mediation effects and potential violations of the sequential ignorability assumption.

Functional prediction and association with ICP and hepatic markers

After filtering by prevalence and mean relative abundance, 281 PICRUSt2-predicted microbial MetaCyc pathways were retained for analysis. To minimise redundant testing, we applied a data-driven microbial pathway filtering procedure adapted from previously published methods.30 Pairwise correlation coefficients between pathways were first estimated using the modified CCLasso method, and the pathways were subsequently clustered using Ward's hierarchical clustering, with dissimilarity defined as 1–correlation coefficient. The optimal number of clusters was determined by evaluating solutions from 1 to 200 and selecting the one with the maximal ASW. Within each cluster, the representative pathway was defined as the one with the smallest average dissimilarity to other pathways in the same cluster. Associations between representative pathways in early pregnancy and ICP (as well as ICP subgroups), were assessed using weighted logistic regression. Associations between representative pathways and marker genera or hepatic markers were evaluated using weighted partial Pearson's correlation coefficients. All analyses were adjusted for the covariates described previously. P values were FDR-adjusted, with PFDR < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Associations between ICP, marker genera, and birth outcomes

The Kolmogorov–Smirnov test was used to compare gestational age at delivery between the ICP and control groups. Weighted logistic regression was employed to examine: (a) associations between ICP and birth outcomes, including PTB, IPB, and SPB; and (b) associations of marker genera at each timepoint, as well as cumulative exposure (defined as the mean CLR-transformed abundance across the three timepoints) with birth outcomes. All models were adjusted for the covariates described previously. All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.4.1).

Results

Study overview and characteristics of study participants

Of 4956 women enrolled in early pregnancy (T1, 10.22 ± 2.04 weeks) in the THSBC, 354 women subsequently developed ICP (46 severe ICP and 308 mild ICP) and 4602 participants maintained normal bile acid levels throughout pregnancy. During middle pregnancy (T2, 24.25 ± 1.61 weeks), our follow-up included 1026 participants, comprising 173 women with ICP and 853 who were unaffected. For late pregnancy (T3, 34.19 ± 1.30 weeks), our follow-up included 991 participants, consisting of 172 ICP cases and 819 non-ICP participants (Fig. 1 and Supplemental Tables S1 and S2). A total of 914 women (163 ICP and 751 non-ICP women) had data collected in all the three pregnancy periods. The data source allowed us to comprehensively profile the gut microbiota and capture the dynamic microbial changes in relation to ICP throughout pregnancy.

Study design and analytical workflow for investigating gut microbiota-ICP associations. We comprehensively compared gut microbiota characteristics between women with and without ICP across three timepoints of pregnancy (T1, T2, and T3). Longitudinal analyses examined temporal changes in microbial community composition and identified microbial taxa associated with ICP development throughout pregnancy. We further conducted mediation analyses and functional pathway prediction to elucidate the relationship between identified gut microbiota and ICP. Additionally, we examined relationships with birth outcomes. Key findings were validated in the independent WeBirth cohort. ICP, intrahepatic cholestasis of pregnancy.

Mean (standard deviation, SD) age of the 4956 participants was 26.39 (3.62) years. Mean (SD) pre-pregnancy BMI was 20.92 (2.93) kg/m2. Over half of the participants (57.4%) were nulliparous. In comparison to women without ICP, those with ICP were older at enrolment although the difference was minor (P = 0.035) (Table 1). There were no differences in educational level, parity, and pre-pregnancy BMI between women with and without ICP (P ≥ 0.254).

Longitudinal analyses of microbial diversity patterns in ICP and non-ICP women

We first examined temporal changes in microbial diversity between the ICP and non-ICP groups across three pregnancy periods (T1, T2, and T3). The α-diversity analyses revealed consistently higher Chao1 and Faith's PD indices in the ICP group compared to non-ICP group throughout pregnancy (P < 0.05) (Fig. 2a). Pielou's evenness showed significant differences only at T2 and T3, while Shannon and Simpson indices remained comparable between two groups (Supplemental Fig. S1a). Significant temporal trends across pregnancy were observed in the ICP group for Chao1, Faith's PD, Shannon, and Simpson indices (P-trend < 0.05). Similarly, non-ICP participants exhibited significant temporal trends in multiple α-diversity metrics, indicating dynamic shifts in microbial diversity throughout pregnancy in both groups. However, the two groups followed different trajectories, with non-ICP participants demonstrating more rapid decreases in Chao1 and Faith's PD indices, coupled with increases in Pielou's evenness as pregnancy progressed (P-interaction < 0.05).

The PCoA of weighted UniFrac distances demonstrated distinct microbial community structures from T1 to T3, independent of ICP status (PERMANOVA R2 = 0.016, P < 0.01) (Fig. 2b). Throughout pregnancy, microbial community structures differed significantly between the ICP and non-ICP groups, although the effect size was small (PERMANOVA R2 = 0.002, P < 0.01). In the time-stratified analyses using alternative distance metrics, including unweighted UniFrac and Bray–Curtis distances, significant group differences progressively increased as gestation advanced (Supplemental Fig. S2a–b). To further assess microbiota variations, we partitioned weighted UniFrac distances into multiple comparison groups. Intra-individual temporal variations were significantly lower than inter-individual differences within the same pregnancy period (P < 0.01) (Fig. 2c). Of note, women with ICP exhibited greater intra-individual temporal dissimilarity than participants without (P < 0.05) (Fig. 2d), suggesting reduced microbiota stability throughout pregnancy. Additionally, inter-individual distances within each pregnancy period were significantly reduced at T3 compared to T1 and T2 (P < 0.01) (Fig. 2e), indicating a convergence of maternal microbiota profiles as pregnancy progressed.

Stratification by ICP severity showed that both mild and severe ICP cases exhibited progressive declines in α-diversity indices (Chao1, Faith's PD, Shannon, and Simpson) throughout pregnancy (P-trend < 0.05) (Supplemental Fig. S1b). Notably, the Chao1 index declined more rapidly in women with severe ICP compared to mild cases (P-interaction < 0.05). When stratifying by onset timing, both early- and late-onset ICP groups showed declining α-diversity over time, with no significant difference in rates of decline (P-interaction > 0.05) (Supplemental Fig. S1c). Time-stratified β-diversity analyses revealed distinct microbial community structures between mild and severe ICP cases at T1, with no significant differences observed at T2 and T3. Conversely, women with early- and late-onset ICP exhibited significant compositional differences at T2 and T3, but not at T1 (Supplemental Fig. S2c–d).

Identification and temporal characterisation of ICP-associated microbial signatures

Using logistic regression for differential abundance analyses, we identified 39 genera in early pregnancy significantly associated with ICP occurrence (PFDR < 0.05) (Fig. 3a). Among these, thirteen were consistently associated with onset timing, and four genera exhibited consistent directional associations with disease severity (PFDR < 0.05). Significant and consistent associations were observed for three specific genera (Butyricicoccus, Turicibacter, and Intestinibacter) in relation to multiple clinical dimensions of ICP, including its occurrence, onset timing, and severity. Butyricicoccus was consistently depleted in women with severe and early-onset ICP, whereas Turicibacter and Intestinibacter were enriched.

Our analysis further revealed a distinct co-abundance pattern between ICP-enriched and ICP-depleted genera. ICP-enriched genera showed strong positive correlations with each other, as exemplified by associations between UCG-005 and Christensenellaceae_R-7_group (r = 0.61), and between UCG-005 and UCG-010 (r = 0.53) (Fig. 3b). Similarly, ICP-depleted genera demonstrated positive intra-group correlations, such as between Lachnoclostridium and Flavonifractor (r = 0.38). In contrast, correlations between ICP-enriched and ICP-depleted genera were predominantly negative, including Lachnoclostridium showing negative correlations with Christensenellaceae_R-7_group (r = −0.42), UCG-010 (r = −0.36), and UCG-005 (r = −0.35). These findings suggest intra-group synergy and inter-group antagonism within the ICP-associated microbiota.

To assess the temporal stability of ICP-associated microbial alterations, we first conducted cross-sectional analyses in each pregnancy period. The aforementioned 39 genera were then grouped into five distinct clusters based on their temporal relationship with ICP (Supplemental Fig. S3). Cluster 1 (n = 12 genera) demonstrated consistent negative associations with ICP across all pregnancy periods (Fig. 3c). Clusters 2 and 3 (n = 8 and 9 genera, respectively) showed stronger associations at T1, with attenuated effects in later periods. Clusters 4 and 5 (n = 7 and 3 genera, respectively) exhibited persistent positive associations with ICP, albeit with differential dynamics at T2 and T3. Cross-sectional analyses revealed that four genera remained significantly associated with ICP at T2 and five remained at T3 (P < 0.05). Despite these temporal variations in statistical significance, the overall direction and magnitude of associations remained largely consistent across pregnancy periods (cross-period heterogeneity P > 0.20) (Fig. 3d). Using mixed-effects models incorporating data from all timepoints, we identified persistent positive associations for Slackia, UCG-005, [Eubacterium]_ruminantium_group, Christensenellaceae_R-7_group, UCG-010, Holdemania, Akkermansia and Family_XIII_AD3011_group (P < 0.05). Conversely, Faecalibacterium, Lachnoclostridium, Alloprevotella, Erysipelatoclostridium, and Monoglobus showed consistent negative associations. Notably, gestational age explained a minimal proportion of variance in the abundance of these genera (range: 0.00–0.32%).

The microbial signatures distinguishing ICP from non-ICP pregnancies were subsequently validated in the independent WeBirth cohort from Hangzhou, China.31 The WeBirth cohort specifically enrolled pregnant women who were diagnosed with gestational diabetes mellitus during middle pregnancy (predominantly at 24−28 gestational weeks). In the current analyses, we included 854 women aged 21–44 years with available 16S rRNA sequencing data from middle pregnancy, among whom 21 were diagnosed with ICP. Of the 39 differential genera identified in the THSBC, 29 exhibited concordant association patterns with ICP in the validation cohort (Cochran's Q test for cross-cohort heterogeneity P > 0.20) (Fig. 3d). Given the reduced sample size, we used a conservative threshold of P > 0.20 to indicate no significant heterogeneity. However, despite consistent association patterns being maintained in most genera in the validation cohort, statistical significance was achieved for only one genus: UCG-005, which maintained a positive association with ICP (P < 0.05).

Microbial co-abundance networks revealed distinct interaction patterns in ICP

To investigate microbial interactions in women with and without ICP, we constructed co-abundance networks using the CCLasso method across the three pregnancy periods. All networks exhibited six primary modules and substantial overlapping in hub nodes (Fig. 4a). Among these, four modules (M1, M3, M5, and M6) were compositionally stable across pregnancy periods, showing minimal differences between ICP and non-ICP groups (Fig. 4b). In contrast, two multi-phylum modules (M2 and M4) demonstrated divergent temporal dynamics, characterised by more pronounced fusion during the T1-T2 transition in women with ICP. Network complexity analyses revealed parallel temporal trends in both groups, marked by increased node and edge counts at T2 followed by a reduction at T3 (ICP: T1 = 57 nodes/176 edges, T2 = 59/186, T3 = 53/103; non-ICP: T1 = 57/154, T2 = 63/198, T3 = 52/99) (Fig. 4c). Despite substantial node overlapping between ICP and non-ICP networks, edge overlapping was notably lower than node overlapping, suggesting conservation of taxonomic composition but divergence in microbial interactions. These patterns remained consistent across different correlation thresholds (Supplemental Fig. S4a–b).

The strength of microbial associations demonstrated progressive decline throughout pregnancy in both groups (Fig. 4d). Median absolute correlation coefficients in ICP decreased from 0.066 (T1) to 0.054 (T2) and 0.036 (T3), while those in non-ICP were 0.076, 0.076, and 0.062, respectively. Wilcoxon rank-sum test revealed significantly weaker microbial correlation strengths in women with ICP across pregnancy periods (P < 0.01). This pattern was consistent across different percentiles (Supplemental Fig. S4c). Topological analyses revealed distinct disease-specific patterns, with ICP networks showing marked increases in the modularity and positive edge percentage during T3, while non-ICP networks remained relatively stable (Fig. 4e and Supplemental Fig. S4d). These findings suggest progressive destabilisation of gut microbial ecosystems in ICP, characterised by increasing microbial network instability and reduced inter-module communications as pregnancy progresses.

To identify potential driver taxa for the interactome shifts between groups, we calculated the NetMoss scores for each node. Importantly, we identified consistent driver genera from T1 to T3, including UCG-005, Dialister, Holdemania, Christensenellaceae_R-7_group, and Burkholderia-Caballeronia-Paraburkholderia. These findings suggest that ICP-associated microbial alterations emerge early in pregnancy and persist throughout pregnancy, both in terms of microbial abundance and interactions.

Hepatic markers mediated the association between early pregnancy gut microbiota dysbiosis and ICP development

The integration of NetMoss-derived key taxa with differential abundant microbiota at T1 yielded 13 pivotal marker genera (Fig. 5a). Correlation analyses between these genera and early pregnancy hepatic markers (AST, ALT, AST/ALT ratio, TBA and GCA) identified 11 genera exhibiting significant associations (PFDR < 0.05) (Fig. 5b). Notably, genera positively correlated with ICP, including Christensenellaceae_R-7_group, Family_XIII_AD3011_group, UCG-005, UCG-010, and Coprococcus, also displayed concordant positive correlations with hepatic markers. In contrast, ICP-negatively correlated genera, including Lachnoclostridium and Flavonifractor, demonstrated inverse relationships with these hepatic markers.

To elucidate the complex relationships among microbiota alterations, hepatic markers, and ICP development, we conducted bidirectional mediation analyses. Forward mediation was defined as hepatic markers mediating the effect of microbiota on ICP development, while backward mediation was defined as microbiota mediating the effect of hepatic markers on ICP development (Fig. 5c). Our analyses revealed stronger effects in the forward mediation pathway, where ALT and AST demonstrated substantial mediating effects. For genera positively associated with ICP (UCG-005, Christensenellaceae_R-7_group, and UCG-010), ALT showed mediation proportions of 27.08% (PFDR = 0.036), 20.06% (PFDR = 0.003), and 11.31% (PFDR < 0.001), respectively (Fig. 5d). AST showed a mediation proportion of 16.72% (PFDR = 0.033), 11.96% (PFDR < 0.001), and 7.19% (PFDR < 0.001) for these genera, respectively. For Lachnoclostridium, which was negatively associated with ICP, ALT and AST showed a mediation proportion of 18.36% (PFDR < 0.001) and 10.86% (PFDR = 0.004), respectively. The AST/ALT ratio showed similar mediation effects in these analyses. Moreover, early pregnancy TBA levels also mediated the microbiota-ICP associations, accounting for a 20.36% mediation effect (PFDR <0.001) for Family_XIII_AD3011_group. In contrast, the backward mediation pathway exhibited relatively low mediation proportions (Supplemental Fig. S5a). Sensitivity analyses for potential unmeasured confounding confirmed the robustness of significant forward mediation effects (|ρ| range: 0.09–0.25).

Using PICRUST2-based functional prediction and a data-driven filtering approach, we identified 37 representative MetaCyc pathways (Supplemental Fig. S5b), of which 13 were significantly associated with ICP development (Fig. 5e). The upregulated pathways in the ICP group were predominantly linked to amino acid metabolism and fatty acid oxidation, including arginine, ornithine and proline interconversion, histidine degradation, and fatty acid β-oxidation. Notably, the S-adenosyl-l-methionine (SAM) biosynthesis pathway was downregulated in women with ICP. These pathways demonstrated consistent directional associations with liver enzymes, TBA, and ICP-related genera (Fig. 5f).

Association of ICP-associated microbial taxa with birth outcomes

Our analysis revealed a significant correlation between ICP and gestational age at delivery (P < 0.001), particularly with an increased risk of late PTB (34–37 weeks) (Fig. 6a). Notably, ICP was positively associated with the risk of IPB, but not with SPB (Fig. 6b and Supplementary Table S3). In subsequent detailed analyses of the 13 marker genera in relation to PTB, the cumulative effects analyses across T1 to T3 revealed Faecalitalea was associated with an increased risk of both ICP and PTB (OR = 1.37, 95% CI: 1.11–1.69) (Fig. 6c and Supplemental Fig. S6b). At T1 period, Incertae_Sedis demonstrated positive associations with both ICP and PTB, while Coprococcus exhibited negative associations with both conditions.

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, our study is the largest on the associations between gut microbiota and ICP within a prospective cohort of Chinese pregnant women. This comprehensive longitudinal investigation revealed several crucial findings regarding the role of gut microbiota in ICP pathogenesis. Women with ICP exhibited different temporal changes in microbial diversity compared to non-ICP participants. Distinct microbial signatures in early pregnancy were evident between ICP and non-ICP women, characterised by differential abundance of 39 genera. Longitudinal analyses demonstrated progressive destabilisation of microbial community structure in the women with ICP, manifested by enhanced network perturbation and reduced inter-module communication across pregnancy periods. Network analyses identified key driver genera that orchestrated the transition from a healthy to an ICP-associated microbiota, suggesting a mechanistic basis for ICP onset. Notably, mediation analyses revealed that early pregnancy hepatic markers, particularly ALT and AST, substantially mediated the associations between specific bacterial genera and ICP development. Moreover, certain ICP-related microbes were found to affect PTB. These findings collectively suggest that early pregnancy gut dysbiosis, driven by specific microbiome taxa, may contribute to ICP development through modulation of hepatic function.

Our analyses revealed several genera in early pregnancy were positively associated with ICP, including UCG-005, Family_XIII_AD3011_group, UCG-010 and Christensenellaceae_R-7_group, as well as genera negatively associated with ICP, including Monoglobus, Roseburia, Turicibacter and Lachnoclostridium. Several identified genera maintained significant associations with ICP throughout middle and late pregnancy. Notably, UCG-005 demonstrated consistent associations across cohorts, with validation in the Webirth cohort. These differentially abundant taxa not only demonstrated significant associations with ICP risk, but also exhibited concordant directional correlations with both ICP onset timing and severity. Several Chinese case–control studies have investigated gut microbiota in women with ICP. One study (27 ICP versus 31 controls) reported elevated levels of Blautia and Citrobacter in women with ICP,6 while another cross-sectional study (50 ICP versus 41 controls) identified B. fragilis as a key characteristic of ICP.9 Additionally, a comparison between severe and mild ICP cases (20 severe ICP versus 20 mild ICP versus 20 controls) revealed that Acinetobacter, which contributes to formate metabolism, was significantly enriched in women with severe ICP.11 Our identified microbial taxa differed somewhat from those reported in previous ICP studies, likely because our stool sampling was conducted prior to ICP onset, while all other studies collected samples post-diagnosis.6,9,11,13 Such differences reflected the differential microbial taxa could change before and after ICP onset. Recent studies established direct correlations between UCG-005 abundance and specific bile acid monomer levels in rat models.32 The UCG-005 also exhibited diagnostic value for metabolic associated fatty liver disease (MAFLD) in a case–control study (52 MAFLD children versus 52 controls, aged 5–11 years), primarily through its regulatory effects on bile acid homoeostasis in the gut–liver axis.33 Genus Christensenellaceae_R-7_group exhibited the strongest positive cumulative association with ICP across three periods. A Chinese case–control study (32 MAFLD patients versus 30 controls, aged 32–52 years) revealed its strong correlation with MAFLD-associated metabolomic alterations.34 In a chicken model, bile acid supplementation significantly elevated intestinal abundance of Christensenellaceae_R-7_group.35 These findings indicated that this genus is strongly associated with bile acid metabolism. Moreover, the genus may lead to elevated lipopolysaccharide levels, whose abnormal distribution in maternal and foetal tissues (such as placenta and foetal liver) may accelerate ICP progression.36 We also identified Lachnoclostridium, a potential beneficial microbe for ICP, which exhibited reduced abundance in early pregnancy among ICP participants. Previous studies showed that Lachnoclostridium could enhance gut barrier function and exhibit anti-inflammatory properties.37, 38, 39 Certain Lachnoclostridium species were also reported to metabolise chenodeoxycholic acid into the secondary bile acid ursodeoxycholic acid rather than hepatotoxic lithocholic acid, thereby safeguarding hepatocytes and inhibiting further hepatocyte injury.40

Longitudinal analyses of microbial diversity and interaction networks revealed distinct ecological patterns between ICP and non-ICP groups throughout pregnancy in our work. Notably, alterations in microbial communities were detectable in early pregnancy, substantially preceding the clinical manifestation of ICP. These early differences were characterised by significant disparities in both α- and β-diversity metrics between ICP and non-ICP groups. Moreover, the severity and timing of onset of ICP demonstrated significant associations with early pregnancy microbial diversity patterns, suggesting a crucial role of early pregnancy gut microbiota in ICP pathogenesis and progression. As pregnancy advanced, longitudinal assessments demonstrated substantial temporal evolution in gut microbial ecosystems within both groups, reflected by significant modifications in diversity metrics across pregnancy periods. These dynamic alterations parallel similar patterns observed in other pregnancy complications, including gestational diabetes mellitus and pregnancy-induced hypertensive disorders, indicating a broader implications of gestational microbial dysregulation in maternal disease pathogenesis.41, 42, 43, 44 Network topology analyses uncovered progressive destabilisation of microbial communities in women with ICP, evidenced by increasing modularity and declining correlation coefficients as pregnancy advanced. This pattern revealed dysregulated microbial composition shifts in women with ICP, contrasting with the relatively stable community structure in non-ICP participants. Through the Netmoss score quantification, we identified specific driver genera, including UCG-005, Christensenellaceae_R-7_group, and UCG-010, which consistently exhibited disease-driving characteristics during both the early-to-middle and middle-to-late transitions, indicating their potential mechanistic roles in ICP pathogenesis. The comprehensive network analyses illuminated fundamentally distinct trajectories of microbial community interactions between women with and without ICP. The progressive microbial dyscoordination in women with ICP indicated disrupted ecological networks, potentially driving dysbiosis and subsequent hepatic pathophysiology. These results demonstrate that the dynamic shifts in microbiota and the key driver taxa play a crucial role in the development of pregnancy complications.45,46

Our analyses further revealed that early pregnancy hepatic markers such as ALT and AST partially mediated the association between gut microbiota and ICP risk. Prior studies suggested the role of early pregnancy hepatic markers in ICP prediction. A retrospective analysis in 111 pregnant women demonstrated predictive value of AST, ALT, and AST/platelet ratio index for ICP development,47 while another study in 267 pregnant women revealed early pregnancy ALT/AST ratio as a significant predictor of adverse outcomes and neonatal hyperbilirubinemia.48 ALT and AST are considered as key indicators of liver function, with their levels in early pregnancy reflecting, to some extent, the hepatic functional reserve during pregnancy. This is particularly important in women with ICP, as hepatic function plays a critical role in the enterohepatic circulation of bile acids, which is closely associated with the pathogenesis of ICP.49 The metabolism of bile acids depends on the interplay among gut microbiota, intestinal epithelium, and hepatocytes, collectively forming the enterohepatic circulation.50 Consequently, both hepatocyte function and gut microbiota significantly impact bile acid production and metabolism. Gut microbial alterations are implicated in various hepatic disorders, including alcohol-related liver disease, NAFLD, cirrhosis, and viral hepatitis.51,52 In these conditions, dysbiosis contributes to the deterioration of liver function. Similarly, gut microbiota dysregulation may lead to early hepatocellular injury and disturb the enterohepatic circulation of bile acids, ultimately contributing to ICP development. Moreover, our findings indicated that the metabolic pathways associated with ICP were primarily concentrated in amino acid metabolism and fatty acid oxidation, with the latter being closely related to bile acid metabolism. Fatty acid β-oxidation influences cholesterol synthesis, which serves as a precursor for bile acids synthesis.53 The SAM biosynthesis showed negative correlations with both ICP and related liver enzymes. Given the role of SAM in regulating bile acid transporter expression, insufficient SAM levels may lead to reduced bile acid transport function, potentially contributing to bile acid accumulation.54,55 However, it should be noted that our functional predictions were based solely on 16S sequencing results, thus presenting considerable limitations that warrant further investigation and validation in future studies.

Notably, certain ICP-associated microbial taxa also demonstrated correlations with PTB in our study. Both Faecalitalea and Incertae_Sedis exhibited positive correlations with ICP and PTB, while Coprococcus exhibited negative associations with both ICP and IPB. While studies on Faecalitalea during pregnancy are limited, this genus may potentially influence preterm birth by regulation of gut short-chain fatty acids.56 Genus Incertae_Sedis has been associated with high inflammatory states in renal patients.57 Given that elevated bile acids in ICP can induce systemic inflammation, the inflammatory cascade may represent a mechanistic link through which ICP mediates the effects of these two taxa on PTB.58,59 In pregnant SD rats, acetate supplementation increased the abundance of Coprococcus.60 The genus Coprococcus is recognised as a beneficial microbe capable of producing beneficial SCFAs, thereby improving gut health and systemic inflammatory status. Thus, it may have potential implications for PTB prevention. In future studies with larger sample sizes, we need to further investigate the distinct pathways and mechanisms involved in different types of PTB.

Our study has major strengths such as a large sample size, longitudinal design with repeated microbiota measures, and cross-population validation. However, a few limitations should also be recognised when interpreting our findings. First, the use of 16S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing, instead of shotgun metagenomics sequencing, restricted direct analyses of functional genes alongside microbial structural traits. Second, given the absence of detailed information concerning genetics, diet, and pre/probiotic supplement usage, residual confounding could not be ruled out. Third, as the study population predominantly comprised pregnant women from southwestern China, this may limit the generalisability of our findings to populations with different demographic and geographical characteristics. Furthermore, while our validation analyses yielded some supportive evidence, certain findings showed opposite results. The modest size of the validation cohort inherently limits the statistical power of our validation efforts. These limitations underscore the necessity for large-scale validation studies across diverse populations to further substantiate our findings. Fourth, while our findings demonstrated the association between gut microbiota and ICP, further research is needed on the metabolomic alterations to elucidate the underlying mechanisms across diverse populations.

In conclusion, our study provided evidence on the impact of early pregnancy gut microbiota on the development of ICP. In addition, we identified disturbances in the gut microbiota among pregnant women with ICP throughout pregnancy, which may influence bile acid metabolism and contribute to the progression of ICP. Our findings also demonstrate that hepatic markers in early pregnancy could mediate the association of gut microbiota with ICP. These findings expand our knowledge of the role of gut microbiota in ICP pathogenesis and offer potential targets for microbiota-based interventions.
