Rerandomization for quantile treatment effects Thanks: Corresponding author: yuhaow@tsinghua.edu.cn
Tingxuan Han, Data Science, Tsinghua University, Tsinghua University

Abstract
Although complete randomization is widely regarded as the gold standard for causal inference, covariate imbalance can still arise by chance in finite samples. Rerandomization has emerged as an effective tool to improve covariate balance across treatment groups and enhance the precision of causal effect estimation. While existing work focuses on average treatment effects, quantile treatment effects (QTEs) provide a richer characterization of treatment heterogeneity by capturing distributional shifts in outcomes, which is crucial for policy evaluation and equity-oriented research. In this article, we establish the asymptotic properties of the QTE estimator under rerandomization within a finite-population framework, without imposing any distributional or modeling assumptions on the covariates or outcomes. The estimator exhibits a non-Gaussian asymptotic distribution, represented as a linear combination of Gaussian and truncated Gaussian random variables. To facilitate inference, we propose a conservative variance estimator and construct corresponding confidence interval. Our theoretical analysis demonstrates that rerandomization improves efficiency over complete randomization under mild regularity conditions. Simulation studies further support the theoretical findings and illustrate the practical advantages of rerandomization for QTE estimation.



Keywords:
causal inference, covariate balance, Mahalanobis distance, finite population, randomization inference, heterogeneous treatment effects

Problem set up

2.1
Finite population and QTE estimation

Consider a finite population of
n
units, where each unit
i\in\{1,\ldots\}
has two potential outcomes:
Y_{i}(1)
if assigned to the treatment group, and
Y_{i}(0)
if assigned to the control group.
Each unit is also associated with a covariate vector
\bm{X}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{K_{n}}
, where the dimensionality
K_{n}
may grow with the sample size
n
.
Let
\bm{Y}(1)=(Y_{1}(1),\ldots,Y_{n}(1))^{\top}
and
\bm{Y}(0)=(Y_{1}(0),\ldots,Y_{n}(0))^{\top}
denote the vectors of potential outcomes under treatment and control, respectively.
For
z=0,1
, define the empirical distribution of
\bm{Y}(z)
as

F_{z}(q)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbbm{1}\{Y_{i}(z)\leq q\}.

For
\alpha\in(0,1)
,
the parameter of interest is the
\alpha
-th
quantile treatment effect
(QTE):

\tau_{\alpha}:=q_{1,\alpha}-q_{0,\alpha},

(1)

where
q_{z,\alpha}:=\inf\{q:F_{z}(q)\geq\alpha\}
is the
\alpha
-th quantile of
\bm{Y}(z)
for

z\in\{0,1\}
.
The QTE summarizes distributional treatment effects by comparing the
\alpha
-th quantiles of the potential outcome distributions under treatment and control, thereby revealing heterogeneity that may be obscured by average effects.
This quantity is unobservable and must be estimated using data from a randomized experiment.

For a randomized experiment, denote the treatment assignment as
\bm{Z}=(Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{n})^{\top}\in\{0,1\}^{n}
, where
Z_{i}=1
if unit
i
is assigned to treatment and
Z_{i}=0
otherwise.
Under this assignment, the observed outcome for unit
i
follows the standard potential outcomes representation,

Y_{i}=Z_{i}Y_{i}(1)+(1-Z_{i})Y_{i}(0)
.
Let
r_{1}
and
r_{0}
denote the treatment and control proportions, respectively, with
r_{1}+r_{0}=1
, and corresponding group sizes
n_{1}=nr_{1}
and
n_{0}=nr_{0}
.
Conditional on the assignment vector
\bm{Z}
, the empirical distribution of observed outcomes in group
z\in\{0,1\}
is given by

\hat{F}_{z}(q)=\frac{1}{n_{z}}\sum_{Z_{i}=z}\mathbbm{1}\{Y_{i}\leq q\}.

(2)

The empirical
\alpha
-th quantile in group
z
is then defined as
\hat{q}_{z,\alpha}:=\inf\{q:\hat{F}_{z}(q)\geq\alpha\}
, leading to the natural plug-in estimator of the quantile treatment effect (QTE),

\hat{\tau}_{\alpha}:=\hat{q}_{1,\alpha}-\hat{q}_{0,\alpha}.

(3)

Throughout this paper, we adopt a finite-population perspective and treat the potential outcomes
\{Y_{i}(0),Y_{i}(1)\}_{i=1}^{n}
and covariates
\{\bm{X}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
as fixed.
Consequently, all randomness arises solely from the treatment assignment
\bm{Z}
, which is decided by the experimental design.

2.2
Completely randomized experiments and rerandomization

Under a
completely randomized experiment
(CRE),
n_{1}
units are randomly assigned to the treatment group, and the remaining
n_{0}
units to the control group, so that each assignment
\bm{z}=(z_{1},\ldots,z_{n})^{\top}\in\{0,1\}^{n}
satisfying
\sum_{i=1}^{n}z_{i}=n_{1}
occurs with probability
\binom{n}{n_{1}}^{-1}
.
The average treatment effect (ATE) is commonly estimated by the difference in sample means of the observed outcomes,
\hat{\tau}=n_{1}^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Z_{i}Y_{i}-n_{0}^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(1-Z_{i})Y_{i}
.
Although CRE guarantees covariate balance in expectation, individual randomizations may exhibit chance imbalances, particularly when covariates are high dimensional, which can substantially reduce the precision of the difference-in-means estimator
(
50
)
.

To improve covariate balance,
50
formalized
rerandomization
based on the Mahalanobis distance (ReM), which repeatedly draws treatment assignments until a prespecified balance criterion is satisfied.
Under ReM, covariate imbalance is quantified by the Mahalanobis distance of the covariate difference-in-means vector between the treatment and control groups.
Specifically, the difference in covariate means is defined as

\hat{\bm{\tau}}_{\mathbf{x}}=\frac{1}{n_{1}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Z_{i}\bm{X}_{i}-\frac{1}{n_{0}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(1-Z_{i})\bm{X}_{i},

(4)

and the corresponding Mahalanobis distance is given by

\begin{split}M=\hat{\bm{\tau}}_{\mathbf{x}}^{\top}(\textrm{cov}(\hat{\bm{\tau}}_{\mathbf{x}}))^{-1}\hat{\bm{\tau}}_{\mathbf{x}}=nr_{1}r_{0}\cdot\hat{\bm{\tau}}_{\mathbf{x}}^{\top}\bm{S}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}^{-1}\hat{\bm{\tau}}_{\mathbf{x}},\end{split}

where
\bm{S}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}=(n-1)^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\bm{X}_{i}-\bar{\bm{X}})(\bm{X}_{i}-\bar{\bm{X}})^{\top}
is the finite-population covariance matrix of the covariates and
\bar{\bm{X}}=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bm{X}_{i}
.
A treatment assignment is accepted under rerandomization if and only if

M\leq a_{n}
, where
a_{n}
is a prespecified threshold.
The corresponding theoretical acceptance probability is
p_{n}=\mathbb{P}(\chi^{2}_{K_{n}}\leq a_{n})
, and the realized acceptance probability under rerandomization is
\tilde{p}_{n}=\mathbb{P}(M\leq a_{n})
, where
K_{n}
denotes the dimension of the covariates.
Throughout, we allow the covariate dimension
K_{n}
, the acceptance probability
p_{n}
, and the rerandomization threshold
a_{n}
to vary with the sample size
n
.
Asymptotic theory
(
43
,
57
)
shows that ReM improves covariate balance and enhances the efficiency of the difference-in-means estimator of ATE,
\hat{\tau}
.
Specifically, let
S_{1}^{2}
and
S_{0}^{2}
denote the sample variances of the potential outcomes
Y_{i}(1)
and
Y_{i}(0)
, respectively,
and let
S^{2}_{\tau}
denotes the sample variance of the individual treatment effects
\tau_{i}=Y_{i}(1)-Y_{i}(0)
.
Under CRE, the asymptotic distribution of
\hat{\tau}
is Gaussian with variance
V_{\tau\tau}=n_{1}^{-1}S_{1}^{2}+n_{0}^{-1}S_{0}^{2}-n^{-1}S^{2}_{\tau}
. In contrast, under ReM the asymptotic distribution of
\hat{\tau}
is non-Gaussian and can be expressed as a weighted sum of an independent Gaussian and a non-Gaussian component,

\begin{split}V_{\tau\tau}^{1/2}\left(\sqrt{1-R^{2}_{n}}\cdot\varepsilon_{0}+\sqrt{R^{2}_{n}}\cdot L_{K_{n},a_{n}}\right),\end{split}

where
R^{2}_{n}
is the squared multiple correlation between
\hat{\tau}
and the covariate mean-difference vector
\hat{\bm{\tau}}_{\mathbf{x}}
, and
\varepsilon_{0}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
is independent of

L_{K_{n},a_{n}}:=D_{1}\mid\bm{D}_{K_{n}}^{\top}\bm{D}_{K_{n}}\leq a_{n}
, with
\bm{D}_{K_{n}}
being a
K_{n}
dimensional standard normal random vector.

Intuitively, improved covariate balance under rerandomization should benefit not only the estimation of average treatment effects but also other causal estimands, including the quantile treatment effect (QTE).
In this paper, we develop a comprehensive asymptotic theory for the QTE estimator under ReM and rigorously show that rerandomization continues to improve estimation efficiency in this setting.

3
Non-asymptotic convergence of empirical cumulant estimators

By definition (Eq. (
1
)), the quantile treatment effect (QTE) at level
\alpha\in(0,1)
is the difference between the
\alpha
-th quantiles of the potential outcomes under treatment and control, denoted by
q_{1,\alpha}
and
q_{0,\alpha}
.
Its estimator,
\hat{\tau}_{\alpha}
, is obtained from the corresponding empirical quantiles of the observed outcome distributions,
\hat{F}_{1}(\cdot)
and
\hat{F}_{0}(\cdot)
, as shown in Eq. (
3
).
As a first step toward establishing the asymptotic behavior of
\hat{\tau}_{\alpha}
, we characterize the joint convergence of the empirical distribution functions evaluated at the population quantiles,
(\hat{F}_{1}(q_{1,\alpha}),\hat{F}_{0}(q_{0,\alpha}))
, to their population counterparts
(F_{1}(q_{1,\alpha}),F_{0}(q_{0,\alpha}))
for a fixed
\alpha
as the sample size
n
grows.

For each unit
i=1,\ldots,n
, we define
\bm{u}_{i}:=(r_{0}\mathbbm{1}(Y_{i}(1)\leq q_{1,\alpha}),-r_{1}\mathbbm{1}(Y_{i}(0)\leq q_{0,\alpha}),\bm{X}_{i}^{\top})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{K_{n}+2}
, which incorporates both the potential outcomes and covariates.
Using this construction, the empirical quantities of interest can be compactly expressed as

\left(\begin{matrix}\hat{F}_{1}(q_{1,\alpha})\\
\hat{F}_{0}(q_{0,\alpha})\\
\hat{\bm{\tau}}_{\mathbf{x}}\end{matrix}\right)=\frac{n}{n_{1}n_{0}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Z_{i}\bm{u}_{i}-\frac{n}{n_{0}}\left(\begin{matrix}0\\
-F_{0}(q_{0,\alpha})\\
\bar{\bm{X}}\end{matrix}\right).

In other words, the observed empirical vector is a linear transformation of the sum of a simple random sample of size
n_{1}
drawn from the finite population
\{\bm{u}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
.
To study its sampling properties, let
\bm{q}=(q_{1,\alpha},q_{0,\alpha})^{\top}
and consider the finite-population covariance matrix of
\bm{u}_{i}
,

\bm{S}_{\bm{u}\bm{u}}:=\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\bm{u}_{i}-\bar{\bm{u}})(\bm{u}_{i}-\bar{\bm{u}})^{\top},\ \bar{\bm{u}}=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bm{u}_{i}.

This matrix can be naturally partitioned into blocks corresponding to the potential outcomes and covariates:

\bm{S}_{\bm{u}\bm{u}}=\left(\begin{matrix}\bm{S}_{\bm{q}\bm{q}}&\bm{S}_{\bm{q}\mathbf{x}}\\
\bm{S}_{\mathbf{x}\bm{q}}&\bm{S}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}\end{matrix}\right).

The block for the potential outcomes is given by

\begin{split}\bm{S}_{\bm{q}\bm{q}}&=\textrm{cov}\left(\begin{pmatrix}r_{0}\mathbbm{1}(Y_{i}(1)\leq q_{1,\alpha})\\
-r_{1}\mathbbm{1}(Y_{i}(0)\leq q_{0,\alpha})\end{pmatrix}\right)=\begin{pmatrix}S_{q_{1}q_{1}}&S_{q_{1}q_{0}}\\
S_{q_{1}q_{0}}&S_{q_{0}q_{0}}\end{pmatrix},\end{split}

(5)

where

S_{q_{1}q_{1}}=\frac{nr_{0}^{2}}{n-1}F_{1}(q_{1,\alpha})(1-F_{1}(q_{1,\alpha}))
,

S_{q_{1}q_{0}}=S_{q_{0}q_{1}}=\frac{nr_{0}r_{1}}{n-1}\left(F_{1}(q_{1,\alpha})F_{0}(q_{0,\alpha})-F(q_{1,\alpha},q_{0,\alpha})\right)
,

S_{q_{0}q_{0}}=\frac{nr_{1}^{2}}{n-1}F_{0}(q_{0,\alpha})(1-F_{0}(q_{0,\alpha}))
, with
F(q_{1,\alpha},q_{0,\alpha})=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbbm{1}(Y_{i}(1)\leq q_{1,\alpha},Y_{i}(0)\leq q_{0,\alpha})
.
The covariance between the potential outcomes and covariates is

\begin{split}\bm{S}_{\bm{q}\mathbf{x}}&=\bm{S}_{\mathbf{x}\bm{q}}^{\top}=\textrm{cov}\left(\begin{pmatrix}r_{0}\mathbbm{1}(Y_{i}(1)\leq q_{1,\alpha})\\
-r_{1}\mathbbm{1}(Y_{i}(0)\leq q_{0,\alpha})\end{pmatrix},\bm{X}_{i}\right)=\begin{pmatrix}\bm{S}_{q_{1}\mathbf{x}}\\
\bm{S}_{q_{0}\mathbf{x}}\end{pmatrix}.\end{split}

For convenience, we scale the covariance matrix as
\bm{V}:=(nr_{1}r_{0})^{-1}\bm{S}_{\bm{u}\bm{u}}
and partition it similarly:

\bm{V}=\left(\begin{matrix}\bm{V}_{\bm{q}\bm{q}}&\bm{V}_{\bm{q}\mathbf{x}}\\
\bm{V}_{\mathbf{x}\bm{q}}&\bm{V}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}\end{matrix}\right),\ \bm{V}_{qq}=\begin{pmatrix}V_{q_{1}q_{1}}&V_{q_{1}q_{0}}\\
V_{q_{0}q_{1}}&V_{q_{0}q_{0}}\end{pmatrix},\ \bm{V}_{\bm{q}\mathbf{x}}=\bm{V}_{\mathbf{x}\bm{q}}^{\top}=\begin{pmatrix}\bm{V}_{q_{1}\mathbf{x}}\\
\bm{V}_{q_{0}\mathbf{x}}\end{pmatrix}.

Finally, the multiple squared correlation between the potential-outcome indicators
(r_{0}\mathbbm{1}(Y_{i}(1)\leq q_{1,\alpha}),-r_{1}\mathbbm{1}(Y_{i}(0)\leq q_{0,\alpha}))^{\top}
and the covariates
\bm{X}_{i}
is defined as

\bm{R}_{\bm{q}}^{2}:=\bm{S}_{\bm{q}\bm{q}}^{-1/2}\bm{S}_{\bm{q}\mathbf{x}}\bm{S}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}^{-1}\bm{S}_{\mathbf{x}\bm{q}}\bm{S}_{\bm{q}\bm{q}}^{-1/2}=\bm{V}_{\bm{q}\bm{q}}^{-1/2}\bm{V}_{\bm{q}\mathbf{x}}\bm{V}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}^{-1}\bm{V}_{\mathbf{x}\bm{q}}\bm{V}_{\bm{q}\bm{q}}^{-1/2},

(6)

where
\bm{M}^{-1/2}
denotes the inverse of the square root of a symmetric matrix
\bm{M}
.

Define

\gamma_{n}:=\frac{(K_{n}+2)^{1/4}}{\sqrt{nr_{1}r_{0}}}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|\bm{S}_{\bm{u}\bm{u}}^{-1/2}(\bm{u}_{i}-\bar{\bm{u}})\|_{2}^{3},

which can be interpreted as a normalized third moment of the standardized vectors
\bm{u}_{i}
, capturing the magnitude of potential skewness or “heaviness” in the tails.
For completeness, we set
\gamma_{n}:=\infty
in degenerate cases where either
r_{1}=0
,
r_{0}=0
, or
\bm{S}_{\bm{u}\bm{u}}
is singular.
Next, let
\mathcal{C}_{K_{n}+2}
denote the collection of all measurable convex sets in
\mathbb{R}^{K_{n}+2}
.
To quantify the distance between the finite-sample distribution of the standardized difference-in-means vector and the multivariate standard Gaussian distribution, define the Kolmogorov distance

\Delta_{n}\equiv\sup_{C\in\mathcal{C}_{K_{n}+2}}\left|\mathbb{P}\left(\bm{V}^{-1/2}\left(\begin{matrix}\hat{F}_{1}(q_{1,\alpha})-F_{1}(q_{1,\alpha})\\
\hat{F}_{0}(q_{0,\alpha})-F_{0}(q_{0,\alpha})\\
\hat{\bm{\tau}}_{\mathbf{x}}\end{matrix}\right)\in C\right)-\mathbb{P}\left(\bm{\varepsilon}_{K_{n}+2}\in C\right)\right|,

(7)

where
\bm{\varepsilon}_{K_{n}+2}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\bm{I}_{K_{n}+2})
is a standard Gaussian vector.
Here,
\Delta_{n}
is set to
1
whenever
\gamma_{n}=\infty
.

The following lemma, which is a direct consequence of Theorem 1 in
57
, provides the asymptotic justification for treating the empirical vector
(\hat{F}_{1}(q_{1,\alpha})-F_{1}(q_{1,\alpha}),\hat{F}_{0}(q_{0,\alpha})-F_{0}(q_{0,\alpha}),\hat{\bm{\tau}}_{\mathbf{x}}^{\top})^{\top}
as approximately Gaussian under CRE.

Lemma 1
.

Under a completely randomized experiment, if
\bm{V}
is nonsingular, then the Kolmogorov distance
\Delta_{n}
defined in (
7
) converges to zero if
\gamma_{n}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
.

This lemma provides the foundation for establishing the asymptotic convergence of
(\hat{F}_{1}(q_{1,\alpha})-F_{1}(q_{1,\alpha}),\hat{F}_{0}(q_{0,\alpha})-F_{0}(q_{0,\alpha}))^{\top}
under ReM.
Building on this result, we impose the following assumptions, which were also introduced in
57
to develop the asymptotic theory of ReM for the estimation of the average treatment effect (ATE).

Assumption 1
.

As
n\rightarrow\infty
, the sequence of finite populations satisfies that
\gamma_{n}\rightarrow 0
.

Assumption 2
.

As
n\rightarrow\infty
,

\Delta_{n}/p_{n}\rightarrow 0
, where
p_{n}=\mathbb{P}(\chi^{2}_{K_{n}}\leq a_{n})
is the theoretical acceptance probability of ReM.

Intuitively, Assumption
1
requires that, for sufficiently large
n
, both treatment and control groups contain positive proportions of units, and that the covariance matrix
\bm{V}
is nonsingular.
Consequently, the covariates are not perfectly collinear, and the matrix
\bm{R}^{2}_{\bm{q}}
defined in (
6
) is well-defined, and is strictly smaller than the identity matrix
\bm{I}
in the sense of symmetric matrices. This ensures that the vector
(r_{0}\mathbbm{1}(Y_{i}(1)\leq q_{1,\alpha}),-r_{1}\mathbbm{1}(Y_{i}(0)\leq q_{0,\alpha}))
cannot be fully explained by the covariates, a condition that is likely to hold in most practical applications.
According to
57
, this assumption also restricts the growth rate of the number of covariates
K_{n}
relative to the sample size
n
.
For instance,
\gamma_{n}=o(1)
implies that
K_{n}=o(n^{2/7})
, while if the standardized deviations are uniformly bounded, i.e.,
\|\bm{S}_{\bm{u}\bm{u}}^{-1/2}(\bm{u}_{i}-\bar{\bm{u}})\|_{\infty}\leq C
for some finite constant
C
, then
K_{n}=o(n^{4/7})
suffices to guarantee
\gamma_{n}=o(1)
.
Assumption
2
further ensures that the theoretical acceptance probability
p_{n}
does not decay too rapidly as
n\rightarrow\infty
, allowing the asymptotic results for ReM to hold.

Based on the assumptions above, we can characterize the large-sample distribution of
(\hat{F}_{1}(q_{1,\alpha})-F_{1}(q_{1,\alpha}),\hat{F}_{0}(q_{0,\alpha})-F_{0}(q_{0,\alpha}))^{\top}
under ReM as follows.

Theorem 1
.

Let
\mathcal{C}_{2}
denote the collection of all measurable convex sets in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
Under ReM, and assuming Assumptions
1
and
2
hold,
as
n\rightarrow\infty
,

\begin{split}&\sup_{C\in\mathcal{C}_{2}}\left|\mathbb{P}\left(\bm{V}_{\bm{q}\bm{q}}^{-1/2}\left(\begin{matrix}\hat{F}_{1}(q_{1,\alpha})-F_{1}(q_{1,\alpha})\\
\hat{F}_{0}(q_{0,\alpha})-F_{0}(q_{0,\alpha})\end{matrix}\right)\in C\mid M\leq a_{n}\right)-\mathbb{P}\left((\bm{I}-\bm{R}_{\bm{q}}^{2})^{1/2}\bm{\varepsilon}_{2}+\bm{R}_{\bm{q}}\bm{L}_{K_{n},a_{n}}\in C\right)\right|\\
&=\mathcal{O}\left(\Delta_{n}/p_{n}\right)=o(1),\end{split}

where
\bm{\varepsilon}_{2}
is a 2-dimensional standard Gaussian random vector, independent of

\bm{L}_{K_{n},a_{n}}:=(D_{1},D_{2})^{\top}\mid\bm{D}_{K_{n}}^{\top}\bm{D}_{K_{n}}\leq a_{n}
, with
\bm{D}_{K_{n}}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\bm{I}_{K_{n}})
, and

\bm{R}_{\bm{q}}^{2}
is defined in (
6
).

The proof of Theorem
1
follows a similar strategy as Theorem 3 in
57
.
It shows that under ReM, the asymptotic distribution of
(\hat{F}_{1}(q_{1,\alpha})-F_{1}(q_{1,\alpha}),\hat{F}_{0}(q_{0,\alpha})-F_{0}(q_{0,\alpha}))^{\top}
can be represented as the independent sum of a Gaussian component and a truncated Gaussian component, with the relative contribution of each part determined by the correlation matrix
\bm{R}_{\bm{q}}^{2}
.
In the special case where
a_{n}=\infty
, the truncation disappears and
\bm{L}_{K_{n},a_{n}}
reduces to a standard Gaussian vector.
This immediately yields a corollary for completely randomized experiments (CRE):

\begin{split}&\sup_{C\in\mathcal{C}_{2}}\left|\mathbb{P}\left(\bm{V}_{\bm{q}\bm{q}}^{-1/2}\begin{pmatrix}\hat{F}_{1}(q_{1,\alpha})-F_{1}(q_{1,\alpha})\\
\hat{F}_{0}(q_{0,\alpha})-F_{0}(q_{0,\alpha})\end{pmatrix}\in C\right)-\mathbb{P}\left(\bm{\varepsilon}\in C\right)\right|=\mathcal{O}\left(\Delta_{n}/p_{n}\right)=o(1),\end{split}

where
\bm{\varepsilon}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\bm{I}_{2})
.
This result shows that under CRE, the joint distribution of the empirical quantiles converges to a bivariate normal distribution with covariance
\bm{V}_{\bm{q}\bm{q}}
.

In a similar manner, we can derive the asymptotic distribution of
\hat{F}_{z}(q_{z,\alpha})-F_{z}(q_{z,\alpha})
under ReM for each
z\in\{0,1\}
.
For
z\in\{0,1\}
, define the multiple squared correlation between
(-1)^{1-z}r_{1-z}\mathbbm{1}(Y_{i}(z)\leq q_{z})
and
\bm{X}_{i}
as

R_{q_{z}}^{2}:=\bm{S}_{q_{z}\mathbf{x}}\bm{S}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}^{-1}\bm{S}_{\mathbf{x}q_{z}}/S_{q_{z}q_{z}}=\bm{V}_{q_{z}\mathbf{x}}\bm{V}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}^{-1}\bm{V}_{\mathbf{x}q_{z}}/V_{q_{z}q_{z}}.

(8)

The following theorem gives the asymptotic distribution of

\hat{F}_{z}(q_{z,\alpha})-F_{z}(q_{z,\alpha})
under ReM, and
the proof proceeds analogously to that of Theorem 3 in
57
.

Theorem 2
.

Under ReM, and Assumptions
1
and
2
,
for
z\in\{0,1\}
, as
n\rightarrow\infty
,

\begin{split}&\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\left|\mathbb{P}\left(V_{q_{z}q_{z}}^{-1/2}\left(\hat{F}_{z}(q_{z,\alpha})-F_{z}(q_{z,\alpha})\right)\leq x\mid M\leq a_{n}\right)-\mathbb{P}\left((1-R_{q_{z}}^{2})^{1/2}\varepsilon+R_{q_{z}}L_{K_{n},a_{n}}\leq x\right)\right|\\
&=\mathcal{O}\left(\Delta_{n}/p_{n}\right)=o(1),\end{split}

where
\varepsilon
is a 1-dimensional standard normal random variable, independent of

L_{K_{n},a_{n}}:=D_{1}\mid\bm{D}_{K_{n}}^{\top}\bm{D}_{K_{n}}\leq a_{n}
, with
\bm{D}_{K_{n}}
being a
K_{n}
dimensional standard normal random vector.

4
Asymptotic convergence of QTE estimators

The central objective of this study is to estimate and conduct inference on the quantile treatment effect (QTE),
{\tau}_{\alpha}:={q}_{1,\alpha}-{q}_{0,\alpha}
, which measures the difference between the
\alpha
th quantiles of the potential outcome distributions under treatment and control.
The corresponding estimator is

\hat{\tau}_{\alpha}:=\hat{q}_{1,\alpha}-\hat{q}_{0,\alpha}
.
To study the large-sample behavior of
\hat{\tau}_{\alpha}
, it is therefore essential to first characterize the asymptotic properties of the component quantile estimators
\hat{q}_{z,\alpha}
for
z\in\{0,1\}
.
We begin by introducing a set of assumptions under which a rigorous asymptotic theory can be developed.

Assumption 3
.

Fix any
\alpha\in(0,1)
. For each
z\in\{0,1\}
, there exist a constant
s<0
and two bounded non-negative functions
f_{z,n}(\cdot)
and
r_{z,n}(\cdot)
, depending on
z
and
n
, such that for any
t\in[-2n^{\gamma},2n^{\gamma}]
with some
0<\gamma<1/2
,

|\sqrt{n}(F_{z}(q+t/\sqrt{n})-F_{z}(q))-tf_{z,n}(q)|\leq r_{z,n}(q),

where
r_{z,n}(q_{z,\alpha})=\mathcal{O}(n^{s})
, and
\max\{f_{z,n}(q_{z,\alpha}),1/f_{z,n}(q_{z,\alpha})\}=\mathcal{O}(1)
.

Assumption
3
imposes a local smoothness condition on the distribution of potential outcomes.
The remainder term
r_{z,n}(q_{z,\alpha})
ensures that
f_{z,n}(\cdot)
serves as an effective local density in a shrinking neighborhood of the quantile
q_{z,\alpha}
, acting as an analogue of the derivative of
F_{z}
even when
F_{z}
is not globally smooth.
Further discussion of this assumption is provided in Remark
1
.

Assumption 4
.

For each
z\in\{0,1\}
,
there exists a constant
\delta>0
s.t.
F_{z}(q_{z,\alpha})\leq\alpha+\delta/n
.

Assumption
4
ensures that the distribution of
Y_{i}(z)
places sufficient probability mass in a neighborhood of
q_{z,\alpha}
.
This condition rules out large flat regions or abrupt jumps of
F_{z}
near the target quantile, which would otherwise destabilize the empirical quantile estimator.
Since
F_{z}(q_{z,\alpha})\geq\alpha
by definition, this assumption implies

F_{z}(q_{z,\alpha})\to\alpha
as
n\to\infty
.
Consequently,

S_{q_{z}q_{z}}=\frac{nr_{1-z}^{2}}{n-1}F_{z}(q_{z,\alpha})(1-F_{z}(q_{z,\alpha}))\rightarrow r_{1-z}^{2}\alpha(1-\alpha)>0,

for any
\alpha\in(0,1)
.
Define the rescaled variance

\begin{split}\tilde{V}_{q_{z}q_{z}}:=V_{q_{z}q_{z}}/f_{z,n}^{2}(q_{z,\alpha})=(nr_{1}r_{0})^{-1}S_{q_{z}q_{z}}/f_{z,n}^{2}(q_{z,\alpha}).\end{split}

Under Assumptions
3
and
4
, we have
\liminf_{n}n\cdot{V}_{q_{z}q_{z}}>0
and
\liminf_{n}n\cdot\tilde{V}_{q_{z}q_{z}}>0
,
which guarantees that the relevant variances remain well behaved asymptotically.

Assumption 5
.

As
n\rightarrow\infty
,

-\log p_{n}=\mathcal{O}(n^{m})
for some
0<m<1/2-\gamma
.

Assumption
5
restricts the rate at which the acceptance probability
p_{n}
under rerandomization may decay.
It prevents rerandomization from becoming excessively restrictive as the sample size increases, thereby preserving the validity of the asymptotic approximation.
This assumption is automatically satisfied when
p_{n}
is fixed and does not depend on
n
.

Assumption 6
.

For each
z\in\{0,1\}
, as
n\rightarrow\infty
,

\limsup_{n}R_{q_{z}}^{2}<1
.

By the definition in Eq. (
8
), Assumption
6
ensures that the indicator

\mathbbm{1}(Y_{i}(z)\leq q_{z,\alpha})
cannot be perfectly explained by the covariates.
This condition rules out degeneracy and is expected to hold in most practical applications.

Under the above assumptions, the following theorem characterizes the asymptotic distribution of
\hat{q}_{z,\alpha}
for
z\in\{0,1\}
under rerandomization.

Theorem 3
.

Under ReM and Assumptions
1
-
6
, for
z\in\{0,1\}
and
t\in[-2n^{\gamma},2n^{\gamma}]
,

\begin{split}&\sup_{t\in[-2n^{\gamma},2n^{\gamma}]}\left|\mathbb{P}\left(\sqrt{n}(\hat{q}_{z,\alpha}-q_{z,\alpha})\leq t\mid M\leq a_{n}\right)-\mathbb{P}\left(\sqrt{n}\tilde{V}_{q_{z}q_{z}}^{1/2}\left(\sqrt{1-R^{2}_{q_{z}}}\varepsilon+R_{q_{z}}L_{K_{n},a_{n}}\right)\leq t\right)\right|\\
&=\mathcal{O}(\Delta_{n}/p_{n})+\mathcal{O}(n^{k})=o(1),\end{split}

where
\varepsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
is independent of
L_{K_{n},a_{n}}
, and

k=\max\{s,m/2+\gamma/2-1/4\}<0
.

For a vector
\bm{t}=(t_{1},t_{0})^{\top}
and a random vector
\bm{r}=(r_{1},r_{0})^{\top}
,
we use the shorthand notation
\mathbb{P}(\bm{r}\leq\bm{t})
to denote
\mathbb{P}(r_{1}\leq t_{1},r_{0}\leq t_{0})
.
Moreover, define
\bm{\Lambda}_{\bm{q}}=\text{diag}(f_{1,n}^{-2}(q_{1,\alpha}),f_{0,n}^{-2}(q_{0,\alpha}))
and
\tilde{\bm{V}}_{\bm{q}\bm{q}}^{1/2}=\bm{\Lambda}_{\bm{q}}^{1/2}\bm{V}_{\bm{q}\bm{q}}^{1/2}
.
Building on the marginal asymptotic results of
\hat{q}_{z,\alpha}
in Theorem
3
,
we now extend the analysis to the joint behavior of the quantile estimators
(\hat{q}_{1,\alpha},\hat{q}_{0,\alpha})^{\top}
under rerandomization, as stated in the following theorem.

Theorem 4
.

Under ReM and Assumptions
1
-
6
,
for any
\bm{t}=(t_{1},t_{0})^{\top}\in[-2n^{\gamma},2n^{\gamma}]^{2}
,

\begin{split}&\sup_{\bm{t}\in[-2n^{\gamma},2n^{\gamma}]^{2}}\left|\mathbb{P}\left(\left(\begin{matrix}\sqrt{n}(\hat{q}_{1,\alpha}-q_{1,\alpha})\\
\sqrt{n}(\hat{q}_{0,\alpha}-q_{0,\alpha})\end{matrix}\right)\leq\bm{t}\mid M\leq a_{n}\right)-\mathbb{P}\left(\sqrt{n}\tilde{\bm{V}}_{\bm{q}\bm{q}}^{1/2}((\bm{I}-\bm{R}_{\bm{q}}^{2})^{1/2}\bm{\varepsilon}_{2}+\bm{R}_{\bm{q}}\bm{L}_{K_{n},a_{n}})\leq\bm{t}\right)\right|\\
&=\mathcal{O}(\Delta_{n}/p_{n})+\mathcal{O}(n^{k})=o(1),\end{split}

where
k=\max\{s,m/2+\gamma/2-1/4\}<0
,
\bm{\varepsilon}_{2}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\bm{I}_{2})
is independent of

\bm{L}_{K_{n},a_{n}}:=(D_{1},D_{2})^{\top}\mid\bm{D}_{K_{n}}^{\top}\bm{D}_{K_{n}}\leq a_{n}
,
\bm{D}_{K_{n}}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\bm{I}_{K_{n}})
.

Equipped with the above results, we are now ready to establish the asymptotic distribution of the empirical QTE estimator

\hat{\tau}_{\alpha}:=\hat{q}_{1,\alpha}-\hat{q}_{0,\alpha}
under rerandomization.
To this end, we introduce two additional assumptions.

Assumption 7
.

There exists a constant
k^{\prime}<0
such that

\Delta_{n}/p_{n}=\mathcal{O}(n^{k^{\prime}})
.

Assumption
7
strengthens Assumption
2
by requiring that the Gaussian approximation error
\Delta_{n}
vanishes sufficiently fast relative to the acceptance probability
p_{n}
.
This condition guarantees that conditioning on the rerandomization event does not distort the asymptotic convergence of the QTE estimator.
Under Assumption
1
,
both Assumptions
2
and
7
are automatically satisfied when
p_{n}
is fixed and does not depend on
n
.

Next, let
\bm{c}=(1,-1)^{\top}
and define the scaling matrix

\bm{\Lambda}_{\bm{q}}=\text{diag}(f_{1,n}^{-2}(q_{1,\alpha}),f_{0,n}^{-2}(q_{0,\alpha}))
.
Using this notation, we introduce the rescaled covariance matrix, variance, and multiple squared correlation associated with the QTE estimator:

\begin{split}\tilde{\bm{V}}_{\bm{q}\bm{q}}:=\bm{\Lambda}_{\bm{q}}^{1/2}\bm{V}_{\bm{q}\bm{q}}\bm{\Lambda}_{\bm{q}}^{1/2},\ \tilde{V}_{\bm{q}\bm{q}}:=\bm{c}^{\top}\tilde{\bm{V}}_{\bm{q}\bm{q}}\bm{c},\ \tilde{R}_{\bm{q}}^{2}:=\frac{\bm{c}^{\top}\bm{\Lambda}_{\bm{q}}^{1/2}\bm{V}_{\bm{q}\mathbf{x}}\bm{V}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}^{-1}\bm{V}_{\mathbf{x}\bm{q}}\bm{\Lambda}_{\bm{q}}^{1/2}\bm{c}}{\bm{c}^{\top}\bm{\Lambda}_{\bm{q}}^{1/2}\bm{V}_{\bm{q}\bm{q}}\bm{\Lambda}_{\bm{q}}^{1/2}\bm{c}}.\end{split}

We impose the following regularity condition to ensure that the asymptotic distribution of
\hat{\tau}_{\alpha}
is nondegenerate.

Assumption 8
.

As
n\rightarrow\infty
,
\liminf_{n}n\cdot\tilde{V}_{\bm{q}\bm{q}}>0
, and
\limsup_{n}\tilde{R}_{\bm{q}}^{2}<1
.

A sufficient condition for this assumption to hold is that

\liminf_{n}\lambda_{\min}(n\cdot\bm{V}_{\bm{q}\bm{q}})>0
and

\limsup_{n}\lambda_{\max}(\bm{R}^{2}_{\bm{q}})<1
.
Under these conditions, the vector
(r_{0}\mathbbm{1}(Y_{i}(1)\leq q_{1,\alpha}),-r_{1}\mathbbm{1}(Y_{i}(0)\leq q_{0,\alpha}))
cannot be perfectly predicted by the covariates, and its covariance matrix is nonsingular.
Both conditions are mild and are expected to hold in most empirical applications.

Combining the preceding results with the assumptions above, we establish the asymptotic distribution of the QTE estimator
\hat{\tau}_{\alpha}
, summarized in the following theorem.

Theorem 5
.

Under Assumptions
1
-
8
,
for
t\in[-n^{\gamma},n^{\gamma}]
,
the empirical quantile treatment effect estimator

\hat{\tau}_{\alpha}=\hat{q}_{1,\alpha}-\hat{q}_{0,\alpha}
satisfies

\begin{split}\sup_{t\in[-n^{\gamma},n^{\gamma}]}\left|\mathbb{P}\left(\sqrt{n}\left(\hat{\tau}_{\alpha}-{\tau}_{\alpha}\right)\leq t\mid M\leq a_{n}\right)-\mathbb{P}\left(\sqrt{n}\tilde{V}_{\bm{q}\bm{q}}^{1/2}\left(\sqrt{1-\tilde{R}_{\bm{q}}^{2}}\varepsilon+\tilde{R}_{\bm{q}}L_{K_{n},a_{n}}\right)\leq t\right)\right|=o(1),\end{split}

where
\varepsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
is independent of
L_{K_{n},a_{n}}
.

Theorem
5
shows that the QTE estimator
\hat{\tau}_{\alpha}
is asymptotically distributed as the sum of an independent Gaussian component and a truncated Gaussian component.
The relative contribution of these two components is governed by the rescaled variance
\tilde{V}_{\bm{q}\bm{q}}
and the multiple squared correlation
\tilde{R}_{\bm{q}}^{2}
.
As a direct consequence, the estimator
\hat{\tau}_{\alpha}
is asymptotically unbiased for the quantile treatment effect
\tau_{\alpha}
.
Since complete randomization (CRE) is equivalent to rerandomization with
a_{n}=\infty
, Theorem
5
immediately implies that

\sup_{t\in[-n^{\gamma},n^{\gamma}]}\left|\mathbb{P}\left(\sqrt{n}\left(\hat{\tau}_{\alpha}-{\tau}_{\alpha}\right)\leq t\right)-\mathbb{P}\left(\sqrt{n}\tilde{V}_{\bm{q}\bm{q}}^{1/2}\varepsilon\leq t\right)\right|=o(1),

(9)

that is, under CRE,
\hat{\tau}_{\alpha}
is asymptotically Gaussian with mean zero and variance
\tilde{V}_{\bm{q}\bm{q}}
.

Based on Theorem
5
, we can quantify the efficiency gain of rerandomization (ReM) in estimating the quantile treatment effect.
Let
v_{K_{n},a_{n}}=\textrm{var}(L_{K_{n},a_{n}})
denote the variance of the truncated normal random variable
L_{K_{n},a_{n}}
defined in Theorem
2
.
As shown in
50
, this variance admits the closed-form expression

v_{K_{n},a_{n}}=\mathbb{P}(\chi^{2}_{K_{n}+2}\leq a_{n})/\mathbb{P}(\chi^{2}_{K_{n}}\leq a_{n})
, where
\chi^{2}_{K_{n}}
denotes a chi-square random variable with
K_{n}
degrees of freedom.
The following corollary characterizes the extent to which ReM reduces the asymptotic sampling variance of the QTE estimator relative to complete randomization (CRE), and shows that this reduction is governed by the generalized squared multiple correlation
\tilde{R}_{\bm{q}}^{2}
.

Corollary 1
.

Under Assumptions
1
-
8
,
the percent reduction in the asymptotic sampling variance (PRIASV) of
\hat{\tau}_{\alpha}
, relative to its asymptotic variance under CRE given in Eq. (
9
), is

\begin{split}\text{PRIASV}=1-\frac{\textrm{var}_{a}(\hat{\tau}_{\alpha}\mid M\leq a_{n})}{\textrm{var}_{a}(\hat{\tau}_{\alpha})}=(1-v_{K_{n},a_{n}})\tilde{R}_{\bm{q}}^{2},\end{split}

where
\textrm{var}_{a}
denotes asymptotic variance.

Corollary
1
shows that the precision gain from rerandomization is non-decreasing in
\tilde{R}_{\bm{q}}^{2}
, since
v_{K_{n},a_{n}}<1
whenever
a_{n}<\infty
.
Moreover, the percentage reduction in asymptotic variance is nonincreasing in the acceptance probability
p_{n}
, and attains its maximum value
\tilde{R}_{\bm{q}}^{2}
in the limit as
p_{n}\to 0
.

Remark 1
.

Assumption
3
plays a crucial role in establishing the asymptotic properties of
\hat{\tau}_{\alpha}
.
To illustrate its validity, consider the case where, for each
z\in\{0,1\}
, the potential outcomes
Y_{i}(z)
are independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) with cumulative distribution function (c.d.f.)
F^{z}
and probability density function (p.d.f.)
f^{z}
.
Suppose that
f^{z}
is continuously differentiable and that both
f^{z}
and its derivative
(f^{z})^{\prime}
are bounded.
Furthermore, assume that
f^{z}(t)
is strictly positive for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
.
For a constant
q
, define
U_{i}^{z}(t,q)=\mathbbm{1}\{q<Y_{i}(z)\leq q+t/\sqrt{n}\}
.
Then, the difference in the empirical c.d.f. can be expressed as
F_{z}(q+t/\sqrt{n})-F_{z}(q)=\bar{U}^{z}(t,q)
, where
\bar{U}^{z}(t,q)
denotes the sample mean of
U_{i}^{z}(t,q)
across units
i=1,\dots,n
.
By a Taylor expansion of the c.d.f.
F^{z}
around
q
, for
0<\gamma<1/2
, we have

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}[U_{i}^{z}(t,q)]&=F^{z}(q+t/\sqrt{n})-F^{z}(q)=\frac{t}{\sqrt{n}}f^{z}(q)+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{t^{2}}{n}\right),\\
\textrm{var}(U_{i}^{z}(t,q))&=\mathbb{E}U_{i}^{z}(t,q)-(\mathbb{E}U_{i}^{z}(t,q))^{2}=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{t}{\sqrt{n}}\right).\end{split}

Applying the Central Limit Theorem (CLT) gives

\sqrt{n}(\bar{U}^{z}(t,q)-\mathbb{E}U_{i}^{z}(t,q))=\mathcal{O}_{p}\big(\sqrt{\textrm{var}(U_{i}^{z}(t,q))}\big)
.
Define
f_{z,n}(q)=f^{z}(q)
, and
r_{z,n}(t;q):=|\sqrt{n}\bar{U}^{z}(t,q)-t\cdot f^{z}(q)|
, then we have
r_{z,n}(t;q)=\mathcal{O}_{p}(|t|^{1/2}n^{-1/4})+\mathcal{O}(t^{2}n^{-1/2}).

Let
r_{z,n}(q)=\sup_{t\in[-2n^{\gamma},2n^{\gamma}]}|r_{z,n}(t;q)|
,
then for any
t\in[-2n^{\gamma},2n^{\gamma}]
, we have:
|\sqrt{n}(F_{z}(q+t/\sqrt{n})-F_{z}(q))-tf_{z,n}(q)|\leq r_{z,n}(q)
, where

\begin{split}r_{z,n}(q)=\mathcal{O}_{p}(n^{\gamma/2-1/4})+\mathcal{O}(n^{2\gamma-1/2})=\begin{cases}\mathcal{O}_{p}(n^{2\gamma-1/2}),\text{ if }1/6<\gamma<1/4,\\
\mathcal{O}_{p}(n^{\gamma/2-1/4}),\text{ if }0<\gamma\leq 1/6.\end{cases}\end{split}

Consequently, in this i.i.d. smooth density setting, Assumption
3
holds in probability with
s=2\gamma-1/2
for
1/6<\gamma<1/4
, and
s=\gamma/2-1/4
for
0<\gamma\leq 1/6
.

5
Large-sample inference under ReM

Theorem
5
provides an asymptotic approximation to the distribution of the estimator
\hat{\tau}_{\alpha}
under rerandomization, which in turn enables the construction of large-sample confidence intervals for the quantile treatment effect (QTE).
Specifically, Theorem
5
implies that the asymptotic distribution of
\sqrt{n}(\hat{\tau}_{\alpha}-\tau_{\alpha})
under ReM can be written as

\begin{split}\sqrt{n}\tilde{V}_{\bm{q}\bm{q}}^{1/2}\left(\sqrt{1-\tilde{R}_{\bm{q}}^{2}}\varepsilon+\tilde{R}_{\bm{q}}L_{K_{n},a_{n}}\right)=A_{n}^{1/2}\varepsilon+B_{n}^{1/2}L_{K_{n},a_{n}},\end{split}

where
\varepsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
is independent of
L_{K_{n},a_{n}}
,

A_{n}=n\cdot\tilde{V}_{\bm{q}\bm{q}}(1-\tilde{R}_{\bm{q}}^{2})
,
B_{n}=n\cdot\tilde{V}_{\bm{q}\bm{q}}\tilde{R}_{\bm{q}}^{2}
, and
C_{n}:=A_{n}+B_{n}=n\cdot\tilde{V}_{\bm{q}\bm{q}}
denotes the total asymptotic variance.
For each
z\in\{0,1\}
,
define

S_{q_{z}|\mathbf{x}}^{2}=\bm{S}_{q_{z}\mathbf{x}}\bm{S}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}^{-1}\bm{S}_{\mathbf{x}q_{z}}
.
Then
B_{n}
admits the representation

\begin{split}B_{n}&=\frac{1}{r_{1}r_{0}}\left[\frac{S_{q_{1}|\mathbf{x}}^{2}}{f_{1,n}^{2}(q_{1,\alpha})}+\frac{S_{q_{0}|\mathbf{x}}^{2}}{f_{0,n}^{2}(q_{0,\alpha})}-\frac{2\cdot\bm{S}_{q_{1}\mathbf{x}}\bm{S}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}^{-1}\bm{S}_{\mathbf{x}q_{0}}}{f_{1,n}(q_{1,\alpha})f_{0,n}(q_{0,\alpha})}\right],\end{split}

and
C_{n}
can be expressed as

\begin{split}C_{n}&=\frac{1}{r_{1}r_{0}}\left[\frac{S_{q_{1}q_{1}}}{f_{1,n}^{2}(q_{1,\alpha})}+\frac{S_{q_{0}q_{0}}}{f_{0,n}^{2}(q_{0,\alpha})}-\frac{2\cdot S_{q_{1}q_{0}}}{f_{1,n}(q_{1,\alpha})f_{0,n}(q_{0,\alpha})}\right],\end{split}

where
S_{q_{1}q_{1}}
,
S_{q_{1}q_{0}}
and
S_{q_{0}q_{0}}
are defined in Eq. (
5
).
Unlike
B_{n}
, the term
C_{n}
involves the cross-covariance component
S_{q_{1}q_{0}}
, which depends on the joint distribution

F(q_{1,\alpha},q_{0,\alpha})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbbm{1}\{Y_{i}(1)\leq q_{1,\alpha},Y_{i}(0)\leq q_{0,\alpha}\}
.
Because this quantity involves counterfactual outcomes, it is not identifiable from the observed data.
As a result,
C_{n}
, and hence
A_{n}=C_{n}-B_{n}
, cannot be consistently estimated.
To address this issue, we construct estimable upper bounds for
C_{n}
and
A_{n}
, denoted by
\tilde{C}_{n}
and
\tilde{A}_{n}
, respectively, as formalized in the following proposition.

Proposition 1
.

Define

\begin{split}\tilde{C}_{n}=\frac{1}{r_{1}r_{0}}\left[\frac{S_{q_{1}q_{1}}}{f_{1,n}^{2}(q_{1,\alpha})}+\frac{S_{q_{0}q_{0}}}{f_{0,n}^{2}(q_{0,\alpha})}+\frac{2\min\left\{\frac{r_{1}}{r_{0}}S_{q_{1}q_{1}},\frac{r_{0}}{r_{1}}S_{q_{0}q_{0}}\right\}}{f_{1,n}(q_{1,\alpha})f_{0,n}(q_{0,\alpha})}\right],\end{split}

and

\tilde{A}_{n}=\tilde{C}_{n}-B_{n}=\frac{1}{r_{1}r_{0}}\left[\frac{S_{q_{1}\backslash\mathbf{x}}^{2}}{f_{1,n}^{2}(q_{1,\alpha})}+\frac{S_{q_{0}\backslash\mathbf{x}}^{2}}{f_{0,n}^{2}(q_{0,\alpha})}+\frac{2\left(\min\left\{\frac{r_{1}}{r_{0}}S_{q_{1}q_{1}},\frac{r_{0}}{r_{1}}S_{q_{0}q_{0}}\right\}+\bm{S}_{q_{1}\mathbf{x}}\bm{S}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}^{-1}\bm{S}_{\mathbf{x}q_{0}}\right)}{f_{1,n}(q_{1,\alpha})f_{0,n}(q_{0,\alpha})}\right],

where
S_{q_{1}\backslash\mathbf{x}}^{2}=S_{q_{1}q_{1}}-S_{q_{1}\mid\mathbf{x}}^{2}
, and
S_{q_{0}\backslash\mathbf{x}}^{2}=S_{q_{0}q_{0}}-S_{q_{0}\mid\mathbf{x}}^{2}
.
Then,
C_{n}\leq\tilde{C}_{n}
and
A_{n}\leq\tilde{A}_{n}
.

Proposition
1
provides estimable upper bounds for the unobservable quantities
C_{n}
and
A_{n}
, which in turn allow us to construct an asymptotically conservative estimator of the covariance and a corresponding confidence interval.

Let the sample analogs of the population variances and covariances be

\begin{split}\hat{s}_{q_{1}q_{1}}&=\frac{n_{1}r_{0}^{2}}{n_{1}-1}\big[\hat{F}_{1}(\hat{q}_{1,\alpha})-\hat{F}_{1}^{2}(\hat{q}_{1,\alpha})\big],\ \hat{s}_{q_{0}q_{0}}=\frac{n_{0}r_{1}^{2}}{n_{0}-1}\big[\hat{F}_{0}(\hat{q}_{0,\alpha})-\hat{F}_{0}^{2}(\hat{q}_{0,\alpha})\big],\\
\hat{\bm{s}}_{q_{1}\mathbf{x}}&=\frac{r_{0}}{n_{1}-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Z_{i}(\mathbbm{1}\{Y_{i}(1)\leq\hat{q}_{1,\alpha}\}-\hat{F}_{1}(\hat{q}_{1,\alpha}))(\bm{X}_{i}-\hat{\bar{\bm{X}}}_{1})^{\top},\\
\hat{\bm{s}}_{q_{0}\mathbf{x}}&=\frac{-r_{1}}{n_{0}-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(1-Z_{i})(\mathbbm{1}\{Y_{i}(0)\leq\hat{q}_{0,\alpha}\}-\hat{F}_{0}(\hat{q}_{0,\alpha}))(\bm{X}_{i}-\hat{\bar{\bm{X}}}_{0})^{\top},\\
\hat{\bm{s}}_{q_{1}\mid\mathbf{x}}^{2}&=\hat{\bm{s}}_{q_{1}\mathbf{x}}\bm{S}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}^{-1}\hat{\bm{s}}_{\mathbf{x}q_{1}},\ \hat{\bm{s}}_{q_{0}\mid\mathbf{x}}^{2}=\hat{\bm{s}}_{q_{0}\mathbf{x}}\bm{S}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}^{-1}\hat{\bm{s}}_{\mathbf{x}q_{0}},\end{split}

where
\hat{\bar{\bm{X}}}_{1}=n_{1}^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Z_{i}\bm{X}_{i}
and
\hat{\bar{\bm{X}}}_{0}=n_{0}^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(1-Z_{i})\bm{X}_{i}
are the sample means of covariates in the treatment and control groups, respectively.
Using these quantities, we estimate the upper bound
\tilde{C}_{n}
as

\begin{split}\hat{C}_{n}=\frac{1}{r_{1}r_{0}}\left[\frac{\hat{s}_{q_{1}q_{1}}}{\hat{f}_{1,n}^{2}(\hat{q}_{1,\alpha})}+\frac{\hat{s}_{q_{0}q_{0}}}{\hat{f}_{0,n}^{2}(\hat{q}_{0,\alpha})}+\frac{2\min\left\{\frac{r_{1}}{r_{0}}\hat{s}_{q_{1}q_{1}},\frac{r_{0}}{r_{1}}\hat{s}_{q_{0}q_{0}}\right\}}{\hat{f}_{1,n}(\hat{q}_{1,\alpha})\hat{f}_{0,n}(\hat{q}_{1,\alpha})}\right],\end{split}

and estimate
B_{n}
by

\begin{split}\hat{B}_{n}&=\frac{1}{r_{1}r_{0}}\left[\frac{\hat{\bm{s}}_{q_{1}\mid\mathbf{x}}^{2}}{\hat{f}_{1,n}^{2}(\hat{q}_{1,\alpha})}+\frac{\hat{\bm{s}}_{q_{0}\mid\mathbf{x}}^{2}}{\hat{f}_{0,n}^{2}(\hat{q}_{0,\alpha})}-\frac{2\cdot\hat{\bm{s}}_{q_{1}\mathbf{x}}\bm{S}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}^{-1}\hat{\bm{s}}_{\mathbf{x}q_{0}}}{\hat{f}_{1,n}(\hat{q}_{1,\alpha})\hat{f}_{0,n}(\hat{q}_{1,\alpha})}\right],\end{split}

so that
\hat{A}_{n}=\hat{C}_{n}-\hat{B}_{n}
.
Here, the densities at the empirical quantiles are estimated via kernel density estimation:

\begin{split}\hat{f}_{z,n}(\hat{q}_{z,\alpha})=\frac{1}{n_{z}}\sum_{i:Z_{i}=z}\frac{1}{h_{n}}K\left(\frac{Y_{i}(z)-\hat{q}_{z,\alpha}}{h_{n}}\right),\ z\in\{0,1\},\end{split}

where
K(\cdot)
is the Gaussian kernel and
h_{n}
is the bandwidth.
The bandwidth is chosen such that
\sqrt{n}h_{n}\to\infty
and
n^{\gamma-1/2}/h_{n}=\Theta(n^{v})
for some
v>0
; for instance, when
\gamma>1/6
, a valid choice is
h_{n}=n^{-1/3}
.

Finally, an asymptotically conservative
1-\alpha
confidence interval for the QTE
\tau_{\alpha}
is constructed as

\hat{C}_{\alpha}=[\hat{\tau}_{\alpha}-n^{-1/2}\cdot\nu_{1-\alpha/2,K_{n},a_{n}}(\hat{A}_{n},\hat{B}_{n}),\hat{\tau}_{\alpha}+n^{-1/2}\cdot\nu_{1-\alpha/2,K_{n},a_{n}}(\hat{A}_{n},\hat{B}_{n})],

(10)

where
\nu_{1-\alpha/2,K_{n},a_{n}}(\hat{A}_{n},\hat{B}_{n})
denotes the
(1-\alpha/2)
th quantile of the distribution of
\hat{A}_{n}^{1/2}\varepsilon+\hat{B}_{n}^{1/2}L_{K_{n},a_{n}}
.
To guarantee the asymptotic validity of the confidence interval in (
10
), we impose the following additional regularity condition.

Assumption 9
.

As
n\rightarrow\infty
,

\begin{split}\frac{K_{n}}{\tilde{A}_{n}}\cdot\sqrt{\frac{\max\{\sqrt{n},\log K_{n},-\log p_{n}\}}{n}}\rightarrow 0.\end{split}

This assumption ensures that the covariate dimension
K_{n}
does not grow too rapidly and that the rerandomization acceptance probability
p_{n}
does not decay too quickly relative to the variance bound
\tilde{A}_{n}
.
When both
K_{n}
and
p_{n}
are constant, it reduces to the condition
n^{1/4}\tilde{A}_{n}\to\infty
.
Moreover, under Assumption
8
, we have
\liminf_{n}A_{n}>0
, so the condition is automatically satisfied in this case.

The following theorem establishes the asymptotic conservativeness of the confidence interval defined in (
10
).

Theorem 6
.

Under ReM and Assumptions
1
-
9
, as
n\rightarrow\infty
,

(i)

The estimators
\hat{A}_{n}
and
\hat{B}_{n}
are asymptotically conservative in the sense that

\max\{|\hat{A}_{n}-\tilde{A}_{n}|,|\hat{B}_{n}-B_{n}|\}=o_{p}(\tilde{A}_{n});

(11)

(ii)

For any
\alpha\in(0,1)
, the
1-\alpha
confidence interval
\hat{C}_{\alpha}
in (
10
) is asymptotically conservative, in the sense that

\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{P}(\tau_{\alpha}\in\hat{C}_{\alpha})\geq 1-\alpha
.

6
Simulation analysis

This section presents a series of numerical and real-data-based simulations to evaluate the performance of rerandomization (ReM) relative to complete randomization (CRE) in estimating quantile treatment effects (QTE) across various quantile levels.
While the theoretical results established earlier concern the asymptotic distribution of the QTE estimator as the sample size tends to infinity, these simulations allow us to examine the finite-sample performance of ReM for QTE estimation.
A range of scenarios, including both linear and nonlinear outcome models, are considered to assess how the methods perform under different underlying data-generating mechanisms.

Summary and Discussion

In this paper, we investigate the asymptotic properties of the empirical quantile treatment effect (QTE) estimator under rerandomization (ReM) within a finite-population framework, where potential outcomes and covariates are treated as fixed and no superpopulation distributional assumptions are imposed.
Under mild regularity conditions, including smoothness of the empirical distributions of potential outcomes, appropriate rates for the acceptance probability, and stable limiting behavior of the multiple squared correlations, we show that the asymptotic distribution of the QTE estimator under ReM can be characterized as the sum of an independent Gaussian component and a truncated Gaussian component.
Based on this characterization, we derive the percent reduction in asymptotic sampling variance (PRIASV) under rerandomization and propose an asymptotically conservative confidence interval for the QTE.
Numerical simulations and real-data-based experiments further corroborate our theoretical results, demonstrating the finite-sample advantages of rerandomization over complete randomization in QTE estimation.

In addition to rerandomization, regression adjustment is another widely used strategy for enhancing the precision of treatment effect estimation.
For the average treatment effect,
45
show that combining regression adjustment with rerandomization leads to improved coverage properties and more reliable statistical inference.
This naturally motivates extending regression adjustment to the estimation of quantile treatment effects (QTE).
Recent work has investigated regression-adjusted QTE estimators under the super-population framework, particularly in settings involving covariate-adaptive designs and randomized experiments
(
38
,
10
)
.
However, regression adjustment for QTE, especially its interaction with rerandomization, have not yet been studied within the finite-population framework.
Developing regression-adjusted QTE estimators under rerandomization in the finite-population setting, and establishing their asymptotic distributions and inferential guarantees, therefore constitutes an important and promising direction for future research.

(A.11)

By definition,

\begin{split}&|\hat{f}_{z,n}(\hat{q}_{z,\alpha})-f_{z,n}(q_{z,\alpha})|\\

I:

By definition, for Gaussian kernel
K(u)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-u^{2}/2}
, we have

\begin{split}\frac{1}{n_{z}}\sum_{i:Z_{i}=z}\frac{1}{h_{n}}K\left(\frac{Y_{i}(z)-{q}_{z,\alpha}}{h_{n}}\right)&=\int\frac{1}{h_{n}}K\left(\frac{y-{q}_{z,\alpha}}{h_{n}}\right)d\hat{F}_{z}(y)\\
&=\int\frac{1}{h_{n}}K\left(u\right)d\hat{F}_{z}({q}_{z,\alpha}+h_{n}u)\\
&=\frac{1}{h_{n}}K\left(u\right)\hat{F}_{z}({q}_{z,\alpha}+h_{n}u)|_{-\infty}^{\infty}-\int\frac{1}{h_{n}}\hat{F}_{z}({q}_{z,\alpha}+h_{n}u)K^{\prime}(u)du\\
&=-\int\frac{1}{h_{n}}\hat{F}_{z}({q}_{z,\alpha}+h_{n}u)K^{\prime}(u)du.\end{split}

The normal kernel satisfies that
K^{\prime}(u)=-uK(u)
,

\begin{split}\int-uK^{\prime}(u)du=\int u^{2}K(u)du=1,\ \int K^{\prime}(u)du=0.\end{split}

Hence,

\begin{split}\frac{1}{n_{z}}\sum_{i:Z_{i}=z}\frac{1}{h_{n}}K\left(\frac{Y_{i}(z)-{q}_{z,\alpha}}{h_{n}}\right)&=\int\frac{\hat{F}_{z}({q}_{z,\alpha}+h_{n}u)-\hat{F}_{z}({q}_{z,\alpha})}{h_{n}u}(-uK^{\prime}(u))du.\end{split}

Similarly,

\begin{split}\frac{1}{n_{z}}\sum_{i:Z_{i}=z}\frac{1}{h_{n}}K\left(\frac{Y_{i}(z)-\hat{q}_{z,\alpha}}{h_{n}}\right)&=\int\frac{\hat{F}_{z}(\hat{q}_{z,\alpha}+h_{n}u)-\hat{F}_{z}(\hat{q}_{z,\alpha})}{h_{n}u}(-uK^{\prime}(u))du.\end{split}

Therefore,

\begin{split}&\left|\frac{1}{n_{z}}\sum_{i:Z_{i}=z}\frac{1}{h_{n}}K\left(\frac{Y_{i}(z)-{q}_{z,\alpha}}{h_{n}}\right)-\frac{1}{n_{z}}\sum_{i:Z_{i}=z}\frac{1}{h_{n}}K\left(\frac{Y_{i}(z)-\hat{q}_{z,\alpha}}{h_{n}}\right)\right|\\
\leq&\int\left|\frac{\hat{F}_{z}({q}_{z,\alpha}+h_{n}u)-\hat{F}_{z}({q}_{z,\alpha})}{h_{n}u}-\frac{\hat{F}_{z}(\hat{q}_{z,\alpha}+h_{n}u)-\hat{F}_{z}(\hat{q}_{z,\alpha})}{h_{n}u}\right||uK^{\prime}(u)|du\\
\leq&\int\left|\frac{\hat{F}_{z}({q}_{z,\alpha}+h_{n}u)-\hat{F}_{z}(\hat{q}_{z,\alpha}+h_{n}u)}{h_{n}}\right||K^{\prime}(u)|du+\int\left|\frac{\hat{F}_{z}({q}_{z,\alpha})-\hat{F}_{z}(\hat{q}_{z,\alpha})}{h_{n}}\right||K^{\prime}(u)|du.\end{split}

By Eq.’s (
A.7
) and (
A.8
), under ReM,

under ReM,

, there exists some

,

Similarly, we have

\leq&\exp\left\{-\frac{nr_{z}^{2}t^{2}}{(1+\tau)^{2}s^{2}}\right\}\leq\exp\left\{-M\cdot nh_{n}^{2}t^{2}\right\}.\end{split}

Moreover,

\begin{split}&\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{1}{n_{z}}\sum_{i:Z_{i}=z}\frac{1}{h_{n}}K\left(\frac{Y_{i}(z)-{q}_{z,\alpha}}{h_{n}}\right)-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{h_{n}}K\left(\frac{Y_{i}(z)-{q}_{z,\alpha}}{h_{n}}\right)\leq-t\right)\\

\begin{split}&\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\frac{1}{n_{z}}\sum_{i:Z_{i}=z}\frac{1}{h_{n}}K\left(\frac{Y_{i}(z)-{q}_{z,\alpha}}{h_{n}}\right)-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{h_{n}}K\left(\frac{Y_{i}(z)-{q}_{z,\alpha}}{h_{n}}\right)\right|\geq t\right)\\
\leq&\exp\left\{-\frac{nr_{z}^{2}t^{2}}{(1+\tau)^{2}s^{2}}\right\}\leq\exp\left\{-M\cdot nh_{n}^{2}t^{2}\right\}.\end{split}

According to Assumption

, under ReM, we have

\begin{split}&\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\frac{1}{n_{z}}\sum_{i:Z_{i}=z}\frac{1}{h_{n}}K\left(\frac{Y_{i}(z)-{q}_{z,\alpha}}{h_{n}}\right)-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{h_{n}}K\left(\frac{Y_{i}(z)-{q}_{z,\alpha}}{h_{n}}\right)\right|\geq t\mid M\leq a_{n}\right)\\

III:

By definition,

\begin{split}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{h_{n}}K\left(\frac{Y_{i}(z)-{q}_{z,\alpha}}{h_{n}}\right)&=\int\frac{1}{h_{n}}K\left(\frac{y-{q}_{z,\alpha}}{h_{n}}\right)dF_{z}(y)\\
&=\int\frac{1}{h_{n}}K\left(u\right)dF_{z}({q}_{z,\alpha}+h_{n}u)\\
&=\frac{1}{h_{n}}K\left(u\right)F_{z}({q}_{z,\alpha}+h_{n}u)|_{-\infty}^{\infty}-\int\frac{1}{h_{n}}F_{z}({q}_{z,\alpha}+h_{n}u)K^{\prime}(u)du\\
&=-\int\frac{1}{h_{n}}F_{z}({q}_{z,\alpha}+h_{n}u)K^{\prime}(u)du\\
&=\int\frac{F_{z}({q}_{z,\alpha}+h_{n}u)-F_{z}({q}_{z,\alpha})}{h_{n}u}(-uK^{\prime}(u))du.\end{split}

Since

\begin{split}f_{z,n}(q_{z,\alpha})&=\int f_{z,n}(q_{z,\alpha})(-uK^{\prime}(u))du,\end{split}

and

,
