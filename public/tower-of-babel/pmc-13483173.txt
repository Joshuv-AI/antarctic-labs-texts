A graph-based evaluation framework for smart disaster response systems using IoT design quality metrics
Authors: Iqra Qayyum, Tahir Alyas, Qaiser Abbas, Turki Alghamdi, Nasser Aljohani, Saad Said Alqahtany, Muhammad Shoaib, Aidarus Mohamed Ibrahim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDisaster-response systems have been progressively installed in smart cities through IoT-enabled systems that assist in early warning, situational awareness, and coordination of emergencies. Nevertheless, the majority of existing studies measure such systems by means of data analytics, accuracy of prediction, or anomaly detection by AI, and the quality of the system-level design is not quantified in most cases. Specifically, no standardized framework exists to evaluate the complexity of architecture, resilience, recoverability, and reusability of IoT disaster-response systems before their implementation. The paper presents a graph-based design evaluation model, which represents the IoT disaster-response architectures as layered graphs and suggests a collection of quantitative design quality measures. In contrast to the learning-based approaches, the suggested framework is also algorithm-free and assesses the structure of the system, which can be compared objectively among the various IoT system setups. The framework is validated through a flood-response case study conducted in Pakistan where changes in sensor density, gateway connectivity, and interactions on decision-layers are examined. Evidence indicates that network redundancy is a validated variable that minimizes the complexity of detection and increases system recoverability, whereas sparse connectivity enhances the severity propagation and response latency. The developed framework offers a viable pre-deployment assessment tool, which augments AI-based monitoring systems and that will aid in robustly designed disaster-response systems.Keywords: Internet of Things, Design quality metrics, Smart city, Disaster response frameworkSubject terms: Environmental sciences, Engineering

Introduction

Disasters have the potential to cause widespread destruction and loss of life; therefore, it is essential that disaster response systems be able to respond to these events quickly and effectively. However, the unpredictable and complex nature of disasters can make this challenging. The design and evaluation of disaster response systems involves integrating multiple components, such as sensors, communication networks, and decision-making algorithms1. In recent years, there has been a growing focus on developing smart disaster response systems that leverage advanced technologies, including artificial intelligence, machine learning, and the Internet of Things (IoT), to enhance the effectiveness and efficiency of disaster response. However, despite these efforts, there is a lack of a comprehensive evaluation framework for disaster response systems that can provide a systematic and consistent assessment of the system’s ability to respond effectively to disasters. A disaster management framework encompasses essential stages, including preparedness, response, recovery, mitigation, and adaptation2,3. When studying disaster management using IoT, most prior research has focused on individual system factors, such as sensor accuracy or alert dissemination mechanisms. This study addresses this gap by proposing a comprehensive evaluation framework consisting of nine design quality metrics that assess disaster-response systems at the architectural level. Among these, three metrics are novel contributions: (i) Disaster Sensor Health Monitoring (DSHM), which evaluates sensor reliability during disaster scenarios; (ii) Alert Dissemination Efficiency (ADE), which assesses the effectiveness of alert propagation across communication channels; and (iii) Disaster Component Reusability (DCR), which examines the reuse potential of system components across multiple disaster scenarios. Furthermore, existing metrics such as Disaster Detection Complexity (DDC) and Disaster Severity Identification (DSI) are extended using graph-based formulations to enable system-level analysis beyond single-dimensional indicators. The proposed framework is validated through a simulated flood-response case study based on the 2022 Pakistan floods, demonstrating its applicability in complex multi-region disaster scenarios.Within the framework proposed in the study, a multidimensional set of nine metrics on design quality are introduced, which together assess the disaster detection, severity estimation, disaster-type identification, sensing efficiency, data integrity, sensor-health stability, alert dissemination, component reusability and system recoverability. This breadth enables the evaluation of the lifecycle of disaster-response in a holistic manner instead of considering separate sub systems, a weakness of previous studies.

Although artificial intelligence and machine learning are rapidly adopted in disaster-response systems, the current assessment practices are largely algorithm-focused, and they focus on the accuracy of prediction, the classification performance, or the ability to detect anomalies. These methods provide a little information about the quality of the underlying IoT architecture, especially on complexity, robustness, recoverability, and reusability of components. Therefore, the system designers do not have objective guidelines to determine the resiliency and scales of a disaster-response framework prior to deployment.

Various important performance metrices are employed in this research to determine the quality of design of the IoT-based disaster-response systems. The complexity of Disaster Detection (DDC) indicates the degree of interaction and communication overhead in a system, and the efficiency of Alert Dissemination (ADE) is a measure of the efficiency of alert dissemination among stakeholders. Disaster System Recoverability (DSR) is the capacity of the system to recover its functionality following failures and Fault Tolerance (FT) is the capacity of the system to service incomplete component failures. Early Warning System Accuracy (EWSA) is a measure of how accurate alerts generated by the system are, and Cost Efficiency (CE) is a measure of the tradeoff between the performance of the system and the cost of its operation. The combined metrics give a holistic assessment of the system performance, resilience and the operational efficiency.

In order to fill this gap, this paper presents a graph-based, design-oriented assessment scheme that models IoT disaster-response systems in layered graph forms. Nine quality metrics of quantitative design are suggested to assess complexity of detection, spread of disaster severity, sensing efficiency performance, data integrity, sensor health, alert propagation, recoverability and component reusability. The proposed framework is also not based on any learning models like AI-driven disaster-response models, which rely on available training data and predictive algorithms to provide the optimal design of IoT. This approach involving metrics and structuring is the main innovation of the work.

In addition, by defining the whole smart-disaster-response architecture as an abstract graph schema we are structurally representing the dependencies between sensors, gateways, controllers and response-layer actuators. The graph-based view offers a formal framework to locate the bottlenecks, study the interactions between components, and calculate the quality measures, which are based on metrics. This method enhances interpretability and provides a common mathematical support to assess the performance of SDR in different situations.

System reliability, fault propagation, and risk assessment of engineered systems have been common in the traditional system evaluation methods of Fault Tree Analysis (FTA), Reliability Block Diagrams (RBD), and Failure Mode and Effects Analysis (FMEA). FTA pays attention to determination of possible failure paths based on hierarchical logical structures, whereas RBD models rely on system reliability based on component interactions and dependencies on failures. On the same note, FMEA critically assesses failure modes and their effects on the performance of the system.

Nevertheless, the techniques are mainly aimed at determining the static reliability and fail to explicitly represent dynamic data flow, multi-layer interactions, and real-time patterns of communication that are inherent to the disaster-response system that uses IoT. By comparison, the proposed graph-based frameworks represent the dynamics of structural connectivity and interaction between sensing, networking, decision, and response layers. This allows a more comprehensive analysis of system complexity, timeliness and information propagation.

Thus, the suggested framework is a complement to the traditional methods of reliability since it can be extended to the current setting of IoT-based systems, where the reliability of the individual systems is not the sole aspect of the system performance but also the efficiency of the communications, data integrity, and adaptability in response.

Abstract graph schema representation of a smart disaster response framework

This study employs the disaster response framework proposed by Hong et al.22, which integrates various resources within a smart city Internet of Things network to enhance its resilience to natural disasters. The abstract graph schema representation for this smart disaster response framework utilises graph data structures to model its various components and relationships, as illustrated in Fig. 1. The collaborative smart city disaster response framework addresses heterogeneity and supports an Artificial Intelligence-based decision-making process, making it resilient to on-time disaster response. The framework is based on four major layers (i.e., sensor layer, networking layer, decision layer, and response layer) that are essential for providing evacuation plans during disaster response.

Abstract schema representation for the disaster response framework.

The base layer (named sensor layer) consists of disaster-specific sensors, actuators, and a gateway. These sensor devices monitor real-time environment data and pass it to the IoT disaster-detecting gateway node. On the other hand, actuators perform various actions in disastrous situations. Sensors, devices, and actuators communicate via the gateway. Data coming from sensors is further filtered and formatted by the gateway. When the sensor value approaches/exceeds the threshold value (set by decision-makers), it indicates that disaster will happen and generates alert signals. Above the IoT disaster-detecting gateway (sensor layer), the cloud data collection platform (network layer) logs the sensor’s data. The cloud platform is responsible for extracting meaningful disaster data and then integrating and storing it on the cloud. The data can be updated and retrieved between the platform and the gateway. In addition, this layer keeps examining and updating sensor data as per controller demand (the layer also processes/extracts data using advanced artificial intelligence techniques). Furthermore, decision-makers and experts interact with the decision layers that are above the network layer. In this layer, the AI decision-making controller is dedicated to processing high-level disaster data and making decisions based on the current disaster situation. The system converts sensor data into useful information for precise decision-making in disaster response. The controller addresses disastrous situations through risk area analysis and informs the response layer about potential disaster situations. Furthermore, the decision-making controller helps alert-generating devices (response layer) generate alerts and disseminate them with the aid of actuators.

The controller identifies the area under threat as well. In the response layer, IoT alert-generating devices handle various disaster responses, including alerts, areas, information, and resources. However, this sends information to government bodies to report damage, provides alternative routes to rescue teams, and alerts emergency operation centres to the resources needed. Also, these IoT devices disseminate alert messages to citizens via various channels (i.e., phone, TV, radio, and social media).

The proposed framework, though represented with the help of the flood-response scenario, is based on a graph-based abstraction that can be customized to other disaster types, including earthquakes, wildfires, and landslides. The layered representation (sensor, network, decision, and response) is the same, but the particular elements of each layer may vary depending on the disaster type. An example of this is that the flood sensors could be substituted by seismic sensors, accelerometers and vibration sensors during earthquake conditions and thermal sensors and smoke sensors could be used during wildfire monitoring.

Likewise, there may be variations in communication needs and decision-making timeframes based on disaster types. Ultra-low latency and quick alert dissemination is needed to respond to time-critical events like earthquakes, whereas slower-onset events like floods or landslides may have sensing coverage and data aggregation as priority. Thresholds, weights, and component definitions can be modified to refine the proposed metrics to these domain-specific needs.

The Disaster Severity Identification (DSI) metric quantifies the intensity of a disaster within a specific region based on sensor observations. In the proposed framework, disaster-specific sensors deployed across different areas continuously monitor environmental conditions, and their readings are used to estimate the severity level of the disaster.

Threshold justification and sensitivity considerations

Some of the measures suggested use threshold values to determine the level of quality including low/high severity, acceptable levels of complexity and system recoverability. These thresholds are based on the existing ideas of IoT operational practices, binary-classification cutoffs, and disaster-management severity levels. As an example, the 0.5 value in the Disaster Sensor Health Monitoring (DSHM) and DSR measurement is based on the traditional fault threshold of sensor networks decision. On the same note, the Disaster Severity Identification (DSI) levels (< 0.5, 0.5–0.8, > 0.8) are the typical hydrological severity levels of flood-early-warning systems.

Sensitivity analysis demonstrates that minor changes in threshold boundaries (e.g. changing DSI to 0.85) do not affect the overall trend of the metric, although they do affect the severity labels, and thus maintain the consistency of interpretation. Similarly, changing complexity levels of DDC or SC leads to proportional changes with no change in system ranking.

Each metric output is connected to practical design implications, so high DDC values suggest the necessity of more gateways or load-balancing nodes, low DSHM values imply sensor redundancy or maintenance issues, sub-optimal ADE values imply the presence of communication bottlenecks, and lower DSR values imply the need to implement strategies that can help make the system resilient. This guarantees that the threshold based interpretations can be directly converted into design choices in intelligent disaster-response systems.

Threshold selection and sensitivity analysis

The threshold values used in the proposed evaluation metrics (e.g., 0.5 and 0.8) have been chosen through the analysis of simulated data of IoT disaster-response sensors at different sensor density, communication delay, and noise levels. Statistical analysis of normalized metric distributions showed that the values near 0.5 are always the values which are consistent between low level of confidence and moderate level of confidence of the operational state, and the values which are equal to or greater 0.8 are those which are high severity or high confidence of the system.

Such empirical threshold choice matches with the more recent IoT literature that documents that fixed cut-offs can not operate successfully in heterogeneous settings unless they are based on an analysis that is data-driven or that they are supplemented by adaptive behavior. Based on this, the suggested framework also promotes adaptive thresholding approach, wherein cut-off values are dynamically calculated based on percentile based statistic (e.g. median or upper-quartile values) based on real-time sensor readings.

A sensitivity analysis also confirmed that the relative ranking of alternative IoT system configurations is not influenced (statistically) by moderate threshold variations (± 10–15%), which implies that the suggested framework of evaluation has a high resilience against the plausible threshold perturbation.

It should be mentioned that the suggested measures are intended to be universal and flexible, and the scales of those measures are not to be perceived as absolute universal norms but as comparative ones. This enables the framework to be utilized in various IoT setups and disaster cases. In contrast to the traditional reliability models, FTA, RBD, and FMEA, which aim to study failure analysis and component-wise risk, the suggested metrics offer a system-level analysis of IoT architectures through the interaction complexity, sensing efficiency, and real-time responsiveness. This difference allows the framework to assess the structural resilience and the performance of operations during disaster-response situations.

The further analysis is consistent with the modern IoT disaster-response practice as it is based on real-time sensor surveillance, aggregation of data at the gates, and decision layers that are centralized and common in modern early warning systems. While the proposed metrics have been validated in hypothetical scenarios of the smart flood response case study, this validation remains limited. Among the potential areas for future work, we consider enhancing reliability by benchmarking the proposed metrics against existing solutions in disaster response, such as those presented by Sood et al.5 and Tonmoy et al.16. These comparisons would show more exactly the aftereffect tuning on scalability, responsiveness, or compatibility with QoS (Quality-of-service). Furthermore, we can also utilise real-world empirical data from historical disaster events (such as rainfall intensity, river overflow records, and actual sensor logs) to take a further step in demonstrating the usefulness and robustness of the proposed evaluation Framework in execution.

Although the case study is centered around flood-response, the evaluation framework is not limited to this area. The comparative study in this paper can be further applied to other disaster scenarios through redefinition of sensor configurations, distribution of gateway and response mechanisms. This shows how the proposed framework can be adapted to a variety of disaster-response applications that can be based on IoT.

Validity and generalisability considerations

The given case study of flood-response is grounded on a controllable and representative simulation scenario, in which the sensor density, connectivity of the gateway, and component failure are set to typical IoT-based disaster-response architectures. Although these parameters have been chosen on the basis of those parameters which have been widely reported in the literature, they are not based on a particular real-world deployment.

The main aim of the given study is to show that the suggested graph-based evaluation framework can be applied and have a comparative ability instead of offering absolute performance standards. The metric values/thresholds (e.g., DDC, DSI, DSR) are then used in a relative manner to compare across architectural configurations under similar assumptions.

Even though the findings offer valuable insights into the effect of system design decisions, the overall generalisability of the framework can also be enhanced by means of validation with real-world deployments of IoT or large-scale system simulations. Further research will be aimed at incorporating real sensor data, disaster traces, and simulation environments to test the suggested metrics in the context of realistic operation and various disaster conditions.

As an illustration, IoT-based and coastal-resilience frameworks suggested by Sood et al.5 and Tonmoy et al.16 respectively, both of them do not include structured metrics to quantify complexity, sensing behaviour, component recovery or reusability. In the same fashion, the IDSS framework created by Jung et al.17 provides better decision support without providing a quantifiable evaluation of the system quality.

Graph-based modeling of existing disaster-response frameworks

In order to make visible the non-trivial comparative analysis, the chosen existing IoT-enabled disaster-response models were formally modelled through the proposed abstract graph schema. Each framework had system components that were aligned to graph nodes representing sensing, gateway, decision, and response layers with data flows, control interactions and alert pathways depicted as directed edges. This common graph-based abstraction ensures that every framework is tested on the same structural assumptions, which makes the comparison of them using the presented quality metrics of design objective.

Metric-based differentiation and alignment with known system characteristics

The suggested metrics were subsequently used on the graphical representation of the chosen frameworks so as to achieve quantitative scores in important design dimensions such as; complexity of detection, efficiency of sensing, efficiency of alert dissemination, system recoverability, and fault tolerance. The scores obtained are distinguishable between different frameworks and can be compared to their established architectural features.

Frameworks based on centralized coordination have less sensing complexity but more detection complexity and less fault tolerance, distributed and redundancy-conscious designs have better recoverability and alert timeliness at the expense of increased complexity in their architecture. The varying metric performance and output validate that the suggested framework can diagnose the strengths and weaknesses of the current disaster-response systems, but not deliver generic or homogenous appraisals.

Discussions

The primary aim of this research was to define a quantification system for a smart disaster response framework to determine its design quality. To achieve our design quality goals, we developed a set of metrics for evaluating the framework. We utilised the disaster response framework, with an IoT network as its foundation, for our study. We presented the entire framework as a graph. We defined nine metrics, of which the first is disaster detection complexity metrics to measure software complexity. Second, the disaster severity identification metric to measure the disaster’s nature and level of severity to respond accordingly. Third, the disaster type identifica- tion metric determines the disaster type.

Fourth, sensing complexity metrics defines for measuring disasters and regional complexities. Fifth, the decision data integrity metric is used to measure data accuracy, completeness, and consistency. Sixth, the disaster sensor health monitoring metric is designed to track the health of the deployed sensor. Seventh, the alert dissemination efficiency metric is based on alert spreading performance. Eighth, the disaster component reusability metric indicates the level to which system components can be reused repeatedly. The ninth and final metric is disaster system recoverability, which assesses the stability of all system components and measures the overall system status based on the recovered or failed components. The defined quality metrics are assessed using a smart flood response IoT system case study. Using different scenarios from the case study, we evaluated the proposed metrics. The design quality metrics presented in this study aimed to evaluate the effectiveness and efficiency of the framework in addressing various aspects of disaster response.

The experimental results demonstrate that the proposed model offers a comprehensive and adaptable disaster evaluation paradigm. Previous works have focused on sensor-driven monitoring36, risk-specific assessment37, and federated concepts for decentralised intelligence38, but rarely in a comprehensive model that can also drive resilience. Our work fills this gap by merging 18 design quality measures that assess exposure, communication, resilience and cost-effectiveness. The results presented synthesise the overall strengths and weaknesses observed in the system, summarised as the scalability of sensors, sensor health monitoring capability, and data integrity provided by the IMS technology. This balanced perspective highlights the novelty of our contribution: a multi-metric, holistic evaluation framework that not only diagnoses where smart disaster management systems perform well, but also identifies major weaknesses in cases of stress. Although validation is anchored in simulated disaster scenarios, the metrics designs enable replicable benchmarking in various smart city contexts.

Though the proposed metrics are proven by a representative smart-flood IoT scenario, such a simulated test is mostly a controlled setting of experiment to showcase the metric functionality, interdependencies and interpretability. This kind of validation by simulation is a common initial phase in design-quality testing models, where a real world application may not be possible in the early phases. The empirical testing needs to be done to enable the establishment of generalisability39. With the growing complexity of modern industrial systems, the occurrence of concurrent faults has increased, necessitating intelligent, data-driven diagnostic frameworks beyond traditional sensor-only approaches40,41.

In this work, a concrete future path is proposed that includes the systematic benchmarking of the already developed IoT-based disaster-response frameworks (e.g., developed by Sood et al. and Tonmoy et al.), and an empirical validation on the basis of actual sensor logs, historical hydrological data, and multi-region disaster data. By implementing these measures, it will be possible to conduct comparative assessment among the cities, the types of disasters and deployment settings, thus enhancing the external validity levels and proving the ability to scale in the circumstances of operational settings.

Conclusion

This article presented a comprehensive evaluation methodology for smart disaster response systems. The method is based on using abstract graph schema representations and applying a set of design quality metrics to assess the effectiveness and efficiency of the disaster response system. The metrics presented in this study include complexity, disaster severity identification, Disaster Timeliness Index, disaster sensing complexity, alert dissemination efficiency, sensor health monitoring, disaster recoverability efficiency, and framework component reusability. The proposed evaluation technique offers a valuable tool for designers and policymakers to assess the quality of smart disaster response systems and pinpoint areas for improvement. By incorporating these metrics into the design process, it is possible to ensure that disaster response systems are optimized for effective and efficient eration in the face of disaster events. The design quality metrics presented in this study provide a comprehensive evaluation of smart disaster response systems and their effectiveness in responding to disasters. Using abstract graph schema representations and these metrics can help develop high-quality disaster response systems capable of addressing the complex and unpredictable nature of disaster events.

The suggested aggregation framework is conducive to flexible metric weighting, which enables the adaptation of various disaster-response needs and enhances the real-world applicability. One of the limitations of this study is that it uses a simulated case study with pre-determined parameters. The next-generation of research will be devoted to the validation of the proposed framework by the use of real-world deployments and high-fidelity simulations to increase the external validity and the generalisability. The framework augments current methods of reliability assessment by enhancing the component-level failure analysis to system-level interaction and performance analysis in IoT-based disaster-response settings. In this research proposes a systematic graph-based evaluation framework and develops nine quality metrics for SDRs. Although the framework’s ability has been demonstrated through a theoretical validation example, further validation is still needed. In future work, we will also attempt to benchmark the proposed metrics against other IoT-based disaster response models and further evaluate these metrics using empirical data and deployment contexts. It would lead to higher generalizability, broader applicability and practicability when building robust and high-quality smart disaster response systems.

The suggested framework is flexible across various disaster fields through adjusting the type of sensors, communication plan and decision parameters in the graph model. Its future use will be in other disaster situations like earthquakes, wildfires, and landslides to further confirm its overall applicability.
