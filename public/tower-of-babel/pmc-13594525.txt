Development and Validation of a Nomogram for Predicting Acute Skin Failure in Critically Ill Patients: A Prospective Study
Authors: Hang Wei, Lijun Jiang, Beibei Wei, Wei Zeng, Yuxin Yang, Yahong Xu, Li Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeAcute skin failure is a common skin injury in critically ill patients. This study aimed to develop a nomogram model to assess the risk of acute skin failure in critically ill patients.MethodsThis study enrolled 230 ICU patients from a class III hospital in Nanjing for modeling development from August 2024 to February 2025, while 123 ICU patients were enrolled for external validation from March 2025 to May 2025. Lasso regression and logistic regression were used to analyze the independent prediction factors of acute skin failure in critically ill patients. The nomogram model was constructed with R 4.4.1 software, and the web calculator was developed. The model’s predictive ability was evaluated using the area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve (AUC), calibration curves, and clinical decision curves. Finally, the Shapley additive explanation method (SHAP) was employed to improve the model’s interpretability.ResultsThe results of the study showed that peripheral perfusion index (OR=0.523, p<0.001), skin mottling score (OR=2.239, p=0.007), multiple organ dysfunction syndrome (OR=5.897, p =0.002), lactic acid (OR=1.754, p=0.002) and albumin (OR=0.828, p=0.007) were independent prediction factors for acute skin failure. The area under the curve (AUC) for the modeling set of this model is 0.959 (95% CI: 0.935 ~ 0.983), and for the validation set is 0.930 (95% CI: 0.875 ~ 0.983). The calibration curves and clinical decision curves demonstrate good concordance and the clinical applicability of the model.ConclusionThe nomogram model developed in this study for predicting ASF risk in critically ill patients demonstrates potentially useful predictive performance and assists caregivers in the early identification of high-risk ASF groups.Keywords: critically ill patients, acute skin failure, risk factors, prediction model, nomogram

Introduction

Skin failure (SF) was initially identified by La Puma1 and, similar to other vital organs, occurs when there is a significant perfusion deficit in the skin tissue. Acute skin failure (ASF) is a condition characterized by insufficient perfusion of the skin or subcutaneous tissues resulting from hemodynamic instability or organ dysfunction, manifesting as pear-shaped, butterfly-shaped, or horseshoe-shaped skin lesions that can appear anywhere on the body and progress rapidly.2,3 Critically ill patients are those whose bodily functions are severely impaired due to serious illness or injury, whose vital signs are unstable, who are at potential risk of death, and who require close monitoring and specialized medical care.4 Critically ill patients have rapid changes in their condition, decreased immune function, and often suffer from malnutrition and microcirculation disorders, making them a high-risk group for ASF.5 Relevant studies have reported that the incidence of ASF in critically ill patients ranges from 28.4% to 83.7%,6,7 with an in-hospital mortality rate of 47%.8 Due to the large size of such skin injuries and the depth of the wound up to the whole tissue in severe cases, the treatment and rehabilitation period is long, and the patients will experience intense pain in case of ASF, prolonging their hospitalization, as well as increasing their financial burden and the psychological burden of their caregivers.2,9 The pathophysiological mechanism of acute skin failure is not clear. Current studies believe that hemodynamic disorders, endothelial injury-related microvascular thrombosis, and ischemia-reperfusion injury will interrupt the oxygen and nutrition supply of skin tissue, and cause skin ischemia, hypoxia and irreversible necrosis.10,11 ASF occurs as a result of a confluence of factors,12 and relevant studies5,13,14 have shown that age, vasoactive drugs, organ failure, prolonged mechanical ventilation, and lactate acid are risk factors for ASF. The selection of a suitable risk assessment instrument is essential for the early identification of individuals at risk for ASF and for reducing the effects of controllable risk factors on patients. The Skin Failure Clinical Indicator Scale (SFCIS), created by Hill et al15 in 2020, demonstrated an accuracy of 83.7% for identifying patients with acute skin failure; however, the scale was validated solely in foreign populations.16 Its scientific validity and clinical applicability in Chinese ICU populations require further confirmation, necessitating further confirmation of its scientific validity and clinical applicability within the Chinese ICU population. While studies have been conducted to develop ASF risk prediction models for ICU patients,7,11,17 most of these studies have relied on retrospective data to build their models, which are prone to selection bias and data quality issues, and have not yet been widely validated in the ICU population. In summary, current ASF risk assessment tools have limitations such as incomplete coverage of risk dimensions and insufficient stability in predictive performance. Therefore, there is an urgent need to develop a scientific and feasible decision-making tool based on the clinical characteristics of critically ill patients and ASF risk factors.

Nomogram is a graphical clinical prediction tool based on the results of multifactorial regression analysis that can intuitively predict the probability of occurrence of a specific outcome by integrating key clinical characteristics of a patient.18 In comparison with traditional risk assessment scales, nomogram models provide the advantages of being intuitive, easily comprehensible, and clinically tractable.19 Traditional regression analysis methods are prone to issues such as multicollinearity or overfitting when dealing with high-dimensional data.20 Least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression analysis method is a statistical method combining linear regression and regularization technology.21 It can solve the over-fitting problem in high-dimensional data and improve the generalization ability of the model.21 This study aimed to investigate the risk factors of ASF in critically ill patients, establish a nomogram model for ASF and conduct validation, thereby offering a reference for clinical evaluation of the high-risk ASF population and the formulation of focused intervention strategies.

Materials and Methods

Study Participants

Based on the prospective observational research method, the convenience sampling method was used to select ICU patients in a class III hospital in Nanjing from August 2024 to February 2025 as the research object of the modeling set. The time external validation method was used to select the critically ill patients from March to May 2025 as the research object of the validation set. Inclusion criteria: (1) age ≥18 years; (2) hospitalization duration > 24 hours; (3) Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation (APACHE II) score ≥ 15;22 and (4) informed consent obtained from the patient or family members, with the signing of the informed consent form. Exclusion criteria: (1) patients with previous or current dermatologic diseases resulting in deepening or discoloration of skin color or large tattoos affecting observation; (2) those who refused to accept treatment or left the hospital voluntarily; (3) patients with pre-existing skin injuries at the time of admission; (4) patients who were pregnant; and (5) those with incomplete medical records. This study followed the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki, was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Second Hospital of Nanjing, Affiliated to Nanjing University of Chinese Medicine (Approval number: 2024-LS-ky-035), and all participants or legal guardians gave informed consent.

The sample size was calculated based on the events per variable (EPV) method, which requires a minimum of 10 samples in which the target event occurs for each predictor included in the model.23 Based on the preliminary research, it was predicted that up to 10–15 factors would be included in the prediction model for this study, and the prevalence of ASF in critically ill patients ranges from 28.4% to 83.7%,6,7 which was taken to be 56%, and considering 10% invalid questionnaires and lost visit rate, the sample size of the modeling needed to be 198~297 cases. The sample size of external validation of risk prediction models is 1/4 to 1/2 of the modeling sample size,24 and considering a 10% lost visit rate, the sample size of external validation needs to be 83 to 165 cases. This study ultimately included 230 cases in the modeling set and 123 examples in the validation set.

Data Collection and Quality Control

Data Collection Instruments

The pre-study phase of the study was based on a literature review and semi-structured interviews with healthcare professionals to develop an index of risk factors for ASF in critically ill patients. After two rounds of Delphi expert consultation, the risk factors of ASF in critically ill patients were determined, and a questionnaire was developed. The survey included: (1) sociodemographic characteristics: disease type, age, gender, education level, smoking history, drinking history, hypertension, and diabetes; (2) general health factors of the patients: mean arterial pressure (MAP), consciousness state, edema degree, activity ability, periphery perfusion index (PPI), skin mottling score (SMS), ICU days, skin temperature gradient, Braden score; (3) disease-related factors: organ failure, peripheral arterial disease(PAD), sepsis, shock, co-morbidity degree, APACHE II score; (4) Drug factors: vasoactive drugs, chemotherapeutic drugs, immunosuppressive drugs, sedative-hypnotic drugs, analgesic drugs, hormonal drugs; (5) invasive treatment factors: mechanical ventilation, hemodialysis, and surgical treatment; (6) laboratory parameters: lactic acid, albumin, prealbumin, hemoglobin, creatinine; (7) nursing factors: working years, professional title, critical care specialist nurse, ASF training, ASF familiarity, and ASF nursing experience.

Data Collection Methods

Data were collected by two researchers using the ASF risk factors questionnaire for critically ill patients. Data were collected from the first day of the patient’s admission to the ICU, and the survey was terminated when the patient developed ASF or was discharged, transferred to another department, transferred to another hospital, or died. Patients were categorized into ASF and non-ASF groups based on whether ASF occurred during hospitalization.

Handling of Missing Data

Variables or cases with a missing rate exceeding 20% were directly excluded to avoid systematic bias caused by severe data missingness. For the remaining small amount of missing data, the random forest multiple imputation method (missForest package) is used for processing. This method can effectively capture the complex correlation between variables and retain the sample size to the greatest extent while reducing the deviation.

ASF Judgment Criteria

The diagnostic criteria for ASF were determined based on its definition and clinical manifestations: (1) Pear-shaped, butterfly-shaped, horseshoe-shaped, or irregular skin lesions of red, yellow, purple, or black color on any part of the body;2 (2) Prolonged Capillary Refill Time (CRT): the time required to apply pressure to soft tissues (usually fingertips) and then quickly release it, and observe the time required for the skin to return to its original color is >3s;25,26 (3) The temperature of the hospital room was 18–22°C, the humidity was 50–60%, the patient’s skin was wet and cold and appeared florid, and the body surface temperature was <33.5°C measured with an infrared thermometer;27 (4) Reduced skin elasticity and sensory abilities.28 ASF can be diagnosed by fulfilling item (1) and any one of (2) to (4) of the above assessment indicators.

To improve the accuracy of ASF determination, this study established an ASF judgment team consisting of 8 researchers, including one international wound therapist, two critical care nurse specialists, two critical care physicians, two wound ostomy nurse specialists, all of whom had more than 10 years of experience, and one master’s degree nursing student in the field of wound ostomy. Before the study was conducted, the international wound therapist trained the group members, including the clinical manifestations of ASF, diagnostic criteria, risk factors, assessment indicators, the differentiation between ASF and pressure injury (PI), and other related knowledge, and all of them passed the examination at the end of the training.

Statistical Analysis

The original data were independently verified and cross-checked by two researchers. To ensure data quality and reliability, errors, omissions, duplicates, and outliers in the original data set are corrected. Data processing workflow design is consistent and repeatable Statistical analysis and plotting were conducted using SPSS 25.0 and R 4.4.1 software. Continuous variables that conformed to normal distribution were described using mean ± standard deviation (Mean±SD), and t-tests were used for comparisons between groups; continuous variables that did not conform to normal distribution were described using median (interquartile range)[M (P25, P75)], and nonparametric Mann–Whitney U-tests were used for comparisons between groups. Count data were described using frequencies and percentages [n (%)], and comparisons between groups were made using the χ2-test or Fisher’s exact probability method. To improve the robustness of the model, the variables with statistical significance in univariate analysis (p < 0.05) were preliminarily screened by least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (Lasso) regression analysis. The variance inflation factor (VIF) is used to assess multicollinearity among predictor variables. Variables with a VIF> 5 are considered to have high multicollinearity and should be excluded from the final model.29 Then, the variables screened by Lasso regression were subjected to multifactor logistic regression to screen the independent predictors of ASF, and the “rms” package of R software was applied to construct the predictive model of a nomogram, and the dynamic nomogram of the risk of ASF in critically ill patients was constructed and published by using the “Dyn Nom” package and the “shiny” package. Finally, the complex relationship between the predictor variables and the ASF was further analyzed using the Shapley additive explanation method (SHAP), and these variables were ranked according to the degree of their contribution to the results.

The model’s predictive ability was evaluated by the area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve (AUC), with internal validation performed using Bootstrap resampling 1000 times. The Hosmer-Lemeshow test was employed to evaluate the model’s fit and to plot the calibration curve, while the clinical decision curve (DAC) was utilized to assess the model’s clinical applicability.

They were divided into the non-ASF group (n=182) and the ASF group (n=48) according to the occurrence of ASF, which had a prevalence of 20.87%. There were 123 samples in the validation set, with an average age of (62.19±12.69) years, and the prevalence of ASF was 22.76%. Univariate Analysis of ASF in Critically Ill Patients in the Modeling Set

Univariate analysis showed that there were significant differences between ASF group and non ASF group in age, consciousness state, edema degree, PPI, skin temperature gradient, SMS, Braden score, ICU days, multiple organ dysfunction syndrome(MODS), sepsis, shock, APACHE II score, vasoactive drugs, mechanical ventilation, hemodialysis, lactic acid, albumin, prealbumin, and working years (p<0.05).

LASSO Regression Analysis and Predictive Model Development

The variables with statistical significance in the univariate analysis were taken as independent variables, and whether ASF occurred was taken as the dependent variable. Lasso regression analysis was conducted, and the optimal “λ” value was determined by 10-fold cross-validation. In order to obtain a well-performing and simple ASF prediction model, Lambda.1se was finally determined to be the value of “λ”. When Lambda.1se=0.044, seven predictors were screened: edema degree, PPI, SMS, MODS, APACHE II score, lactic acid, and albumin (as shown in Figures 1a and b). Interpretability Analysis of the ASF Risk Nomogram Prediction Model

The SHAP method is a model interpretation method based on Shapley value in game theory, which can quantify the contribution of each feature to a single prediction result.30 SHAP analysis enables the comprehension of the relative significance of different variables in predicting the risk of ASF in critically ill patients. The order of importance of the SHAP features in this study (as shown in Figure 3a) showed that the PPI had the greatest degree of influence on the predicted outcomes of the model, followed by lactate acid, albumin, SMS, and MODS. The SHAP swarm plot shows the effect of each predictor variable on the results, a SHAP value greater than zero indicates an increased risk of ASF, higher values indicate a greater risk of occurrence, the dots in the plot represent the sample, and each line represents a feature, the further the dots are from the baseline (0 value) suggesting a greater effect on the results, yellow indicates a high value of the feature, and red indicates a low value of the feature. As visualized in Figure 3b, lactic acid, SMS, and MODS had a positive effect on the prediction of ASF in critically ill patients, and PPI and albumin had a negative effect on the prediction of ASF in critically ill patients.

Discussion

Current Status of ASF Occurrence in Critically Ill Patients

The incidence of ASF in critically ill patients in this study was 20.87% ~ 22.76%, which is lower than Zhu et al7 study. It may be related to the differences in the characteristics of the included study subjects, who were all critically ill but whose differences in age and type of disease may have influenced the incidence of ASF. Although this study is based on the consensus of scholars on ASF2,3,25,26,28 to develop its diagnostic criteria and establish the occurrence judgment group, in order to improve the accuracy of ASF judgment. However, since the diagnostic criteria for ASF have not been standardized,12 the diversity of diagnostic criteria across studies is one of the major reasons for the differences in the incidence of ASF. The occurrence of ASF increases the pain of patients and affects their rehabilitation process. Although the incidence of ASF in critically ill patients varies in different studies, it reflects that the occurrence of ASF needs to be highly emphasized by healthcare professionals to a certain extent. Strengthening ASF-related training is an important way to improve the practical ability of nursing staff in skin care.31 Furthermore, refining ASF assessment and management methods, standardizing the evaluation process, fostering multidisciplinary collaboration, and formulating a targeted medical program are essential for ameliorating patients’ skin conditions and decreasing the incidence of ASF.

Risk Factors for ASF in Critically Ill Patients

The peripheral perfusion index (PPI) is the ratio of pulsatile to non-pulsatile or static blood flow in the peripheral circulation, and can be measured using pulse oximetry.32 This study’s results indicated that a lower PPI in critically ill patients correlates with an increased risk of ASF, consistent with findings from prior research.7,17 The monitoring of PPI is simple and noninvasive, and changes in this index can serve as an important basis for reflecting changes in the patient’s tissue perfusion and condition.33 Lima et al34 found that when a patient’s PPI below 1.4, it can suggest the presence of inadequate peripheral perfusion, at which time the body’s compensatory mechanism is dominant,35 sacrificing the perfusion of non-vital organs to prioritize the function of vital organs and the weakening of the blood supply and repair capacity of the skin and subcutaneous tissues, which will significantly increase the risk of ASF in critically ill patients. The SHAP analysis of this study revealed that PPI had the most significant influence on the prediction of ASF risk in critically ill patients. However, currently, ICU nurses lack sufficient knowledge about PPI,36 and nurse managers need to increase training in skin perfusion monitoring techniques and encourage nurses to conduct further research on this topic. Nursing personnel in clinical settings should monitor alterations in patients’ PPI, integrating blood pressure, capillary refill time, and other indicators alongside clinical symptoms to dynamically evaluate patients’ perfusion and skin condition. Once patients have insufficient tissue perfusion, they should actively cooperate with doctors to achieve effective fluid management and condition monitoring, adopt low-perfusion turning methods37 to reduce hemodynamic changes caused by turning, and do a good job of basic skin care to reduce the risk of ASF.

Skin plaques are caused by abnormal and strong contraction of the peripheral small blood vessels, which can reflect abnormal perfusion of the local skin.38 The results of this study indicated that a high skin mottling score (SMS) acts as an independent risk factor for ASF in critically ill patients. The skin mottling score was developed by Ait-Oufella et al39 to score patients according to the degree of mottling in the knee region. According to research by Jing Chenchen et al,28 the skin mottling score combined with lactic acid possesses predictive value for the onset of ASF. Higher skin mottling scores suggest more serious damage to microcirculation, the aggravation of systemic hypoperfusion, and the significant hypoxia of skin tissue, thus accelerating the necrosis of skin cells and increasing the possibility of ASF.40 To promptly identify high-risk patients with ASF, the SMS score should be employed as a routine evaluation index for the skin care of critically ill individuals. When the patient’s SMS was 0~2, attention was paid to strengthening skin monitoring, recording the hourly florid range, skin color, temperature changes, etc., and evaluating the microcirculation status in conjunction with clinical biochemical indexes, so as to detect and deal with the changes in the condition promptly. When the patient’s SMS was 3~5, it suggests that the patient may have severe microcirculatory dysfunction,41 which should strengthen hemodynamic management, maintain the patient’s mean arterial pressure > 65 mmHg,42 form a multi-scientific therapeutic nursing team, optimize the skin management strategy, and actively improve the patient’s skin prognosis.

This study identified multiple organ dysfunction syndrome (MODS) as a significant predictor of acute skin failure in critically sick patients, corroborating findings from prior research.15 Patients with MODS have a rapid progression and poor prognosis,43 and as circulatory disturbances occur in vital organs, compensatory sympathoexcitation, reduced cutaneous perfusion, and skin tissue ischemia and hypoxia accelerate epidermal cell apoptosis.10,35 Moreover, critically ill patients with MODS are mostly in a hypermetabolic state, often accompanied by immune dysfunction and nutritional metabolism imbalance, which tends to weaken the defense function of the skin barrier,10 and various invasive manipulation treatments also increase the risk of skin infections and delay wound healing time, which in turn increases the chance of ASF in critically ill patients. For such patients, ICU nurses should not only pay close attention to the vital signs of patients and do well in fluid management and basic skin care, but also regularly evaluate the peripheral circulation, timely detect adverse circulation, and cooperate with doctors to improve the microcirculation and perfusion of patients. During invasive procedures, the principle of asepsis should be strictly followed, skin care and observation of the operating site should be strengthened, signs of infection should be identified early, resources from critical care medicine, the nutrition department, the rehabilitation department, and wound care should be integrated, individualized treatment and nursing pathways should be developed for patients, and regular consultations should be conducted to adjust treatment plans and summarize clinical nursing experience.

This investigation discovered a strong correlation between lactic acid (Lac) and the incidence of ASF in critically ill patients, corroborating the findings of Wangshujuan et al6 Lactic acid is a product of the anaerobic metabolism of glucose, which can be used as a key indicator of tissue oxygen availability, metabolism, and tissue perfusion and has an important predictive value for disease prognosis.44 Relevant studies have shown45 that increased levels of lactate are indicative of the presence of inadequate tissue perfusion or a state of cellular hypoxia in patients. At this time, the skin’s efficient blood circulation is markedly diminished, the skin sensation is weakened, the self-protection capabilities are compromised, the skin structure is fragile, and the possibility of ASF in patients is increased.6 Lactate as a key indicator that can reflect the function of microcirculation in critically ill patients,45 and dynamic monitoring should be carried out when the patient is first admitted to the room. When the lactate acid exceeds 2 mmol/L,46 ICU nurses should strengthen the monitoring of perfusion indexes, such as PPI, central venous pressure, mean arterial pressure, etc., and actively treat microcirculatory disorders such as sepsis and multiple organ dysfunction syndrome, and control the infection promptly with the use of antibiotics in a reasonable manner. To reduce the incidence of skin injury, ICU nurses should pay attention to the change of skin temperature, meticulously observe the skin for the presence of hard knots, blotches, breakage, etc., and record them in time. Additionally, foam dressings may be applied to areas of thin skin and bony prominences for preventive protection.

The results of this study showed that albumin was significantly correlated with the occurrence of ASF, and the lower the albumin level, the higher the risk of ASF. Albumin is the most abundant circulating protein in the blood and plays an important role in maintaining plasma colloid osmotic pressure and regulating the physiological functions of the circulatory system.47 The decrease of albumin level will lead to a low osmotic pressure of colloid, the infiltration of water from the blood vessel into the tissue interstitium, and the formation of edema, affecting the tension and integrity of the skin.48 Moreover, albumin possesses anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and endothelial integrity maintenance functions,49 and diminished albumin levels directly compromise skin barrier function, hence facilitating the onset of ASF. As an important indicator for assessing nutritional status,50 decreased albumin level suggests that patients may be at risk of malnutrition, which affects skin repair and regeneration processes,51 resulting in decreased wound healing capacity and increasing the difficulty of treating and caring for ASF. Consequently, sustaining normal albumin levels is crucial for facilitating disease recovery and preserving healthy skin conditions in critically ill patients. Healthcare personnel should regularly screen patients for nutritional risks, monitor various nutritional indicators, promptly identify malnourished patients, select appropriate nutritional support modalities according to their conditions, refer to relevant nutritional guidelines, and cooperate with dietitians to formulate individualized nutritional programs for the patients, improve albumin levels, and enhance the body’s resistance and tissue repair ability. For patients with potential skin damage, appropriate skin protectants can be applied to improve the skin’s defense capabilities.

Comparison with Other ASF Risk Assessment Tools

In 2024, the AUC value of the ASF risk prediction model developed by Zhu et al7 was 0.830, which was slightly lower than that reported in this study. The AUC value of the ASF risk prediction model constructed by Wang Minna et al17 is 0.964, which is slightly higher than that of this study. Its prediction model is mainly suitable for patients with septic shock in the ICU, which may have some limitations in patients with a comprehensive ICU. The sample source of this study is more extensive, and it can more comprehensively reflect the skin conditions of patients with different disease severities. Furthermore, since this model was constructed using retrospective data, it is difficult to control for potential confounding factors, and it is prone to selection bias and data quality issues, which may affect the relationship between outcome events and predictive factors. Compared to the two studies mentioned above, this study employed a prospective approach to collect data and conducted an external validation of the ASF risk prediction model, thereby reducing the impact of information bias and demonstrating a certain degree of generalizability.

Scientific Utility Value of the Prediction Model

Scientific evaluation and standardized nursing care are of great significance to realize the risk control of ASF. The ASF risk assessment scale has the advantages of convenient operation and accurate assessment, but its disadvantage is that it can not accurately quantify the probability threshold of ASF in critically ill patients. In comparison, risk prediction models can calculate the specific probability of a patient developing ASF by integrating multiple key predictive factors and applying statistical algorithms, thereby providing a more quantifiable risk reference to support clinical decision-making. This study analyzed relevant clinical data from critically ill patients to identify key risk assessment indicators for ASF onset and constructed a nomogram prediction model to visualize complex regression equations, thereby providing a more intuitive representation of the relationship between patients’ overall condition and their risk of ASF onset. In the analysis process, the key predictive variables in this study were screened using Lasso regression analysis, which can reduce the collinearity problem between variables. To further enhance the model’s interpretability, this study introduced the SHAP explanation framework. At the global level, the SHAP analysis identified the following order of importance for each predictor variable: PPI > lactate > albumin > SMS > MODS. At the individual level, SHAP values quantitatively demonstrate the direction and extent of each factor’s contribution to the prediction results for specific individuals. This strategy, which combines modeling with interpretation, not only ensures the model’s high predictive performance but also provides clinically meaningful explanatory insights, laying a methodological foundation for precision interventions. The visual nomogram model of ASF in critically ill patients was constructed, and the area under the curve (AUC) of internal and external validation was greater than 0.7, indicating that the prediction ability of the model was good. The P-values of the Hosmer-Lemeshow test were all greater than 0.05, and the calibration chart showed that the model had good calibration efficacy in predicting the occurrence probability and actual occurrence probability of ASF in critically ill patients. The clinical decision curves indicated that the nomogram prediction model possesses some clinical utility value, which can provide a reference for healthcare professionals to identify the high-risk group of ASF and develop individualized treatment and care plans. To further enhance the practicality of this model, this study has converted it into a web-based calculator. Clinical nurses can simply enter or select the relevant data based on the patient’s actual condition to obtain the predicted probability of ASF for that patient. This method is more convenient and efficient than using a traditional nomogram, and it can guide nurses in making clinical decisions while improving work efficiency.

Limitations

The ASF nomogram prediction model developed in this study for critically ill patients has good predictive ability and clinical applicability, but there are still several limitations. First, due to limited time and human resources, the study population is derived from a single center with a restricted sample size, and there are geographical limitations, which may affect the generalizability of the model. Future prospective studies with large samples from multiple centers will be conducted to further improve the robustness and generalization of the model. Second, this study provides only a theoretical comparison of existing ASF risk assessment tools and lacks comparative research on their actual effectiveness in clinical practice. In the future, the predictive efficacy of this study’s model and other risk assessment tools will be evaluated and compared, and the ASF nomogram prediction model will be enhanced to optimize its clinical application effect in assessing high-risk groups of severe ASF patients.

Conclusion

This study constructed a visual prediction model of the ASF nomogram for critically ill patients based on five prediction factors: PPI, SMS, MODS, lactic acid, albumin, and formed a web version. SHAP analysis was used to visualize the extent to which the predictor variables contributed to the prediction of the model outcomes, helping healthcare professionals to better understand the predictive mechanism of the ASF model and enhancing their trust in the predictive model. Upon validation, the ASF nomogram prediction model constructed in this study presents favorable differentiation, calibration, and clinical utility, which helps clinical care workers to identify the ASF high-risk group early, optimize clinical decision-making, and formulate timely individualized intervention strategies to enhance patient skin prognosis.
