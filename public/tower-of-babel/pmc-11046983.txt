Breathe easy: encouraging climate-conscious inhalers
Authors: Rachel Blyth, Maya Williams, Charmaine Bvute, Marina Fathalla, Alice Davies
Published: 2024

Introduction

Inhaled therapy is the mainstay of treatment for respiratory diseases such as asthma and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD). However, recent studies have shown how harmful inhalers can be to the environment, accounting for approximately 3% of the entire NHS carbon footprint.1 Improving inhaler sustainability is therefore a priority within the NHS's net-zero ambitions.2

The British Thoracic Society's guidelines for asthma management recommend that inhalers with 'low global-warming potential should be used when they are likely to be equally effective’.3 Despite this, propellant-containing pressurised metered dose (pMDI) devices are more commonly prescribed and yet have a much higher carbon footprint than dry powder inhalers (DPIs).2, 3, 4 Inappropriate disposal of inhalers in household waste is another factor contributing to the carbon footprint.5

There has already been a drive to change practice through initiatives such as 'Greener NHS’ and the NHS net zero target; however, the awareness of sustainable inhaler prescribing still needs to be raised within hospitals. This project aims to reduce the carbon footprint of inhaler prescribing in our hospital by exploring current prescribing practices, identifying possible areas for improvement, patient and staff education, and improving inhaler recycling.

Methods

We audited 60 patients with COPD, collecting data on what inhalers they were admitted with, any new inhaler recommendations made, and if there were lower carbon footprint alternatives in the formulary. Carbon footprint estimates were subsequently calculated and based on PrescQIPP data.6

A staff survey assessed prescribers on the acute assessment unit (AAU) and respiratory ward on inhaler sustainability and recycling knowledge. Another survey was conducted on inpatients who were admitted on inhalers.

Results and discussion

The audit found that 10 patients (25.6%) had new inhalers prescribed (five were initially not on inhaler therapy). The total carbon footprint of all inhalers on admission was 607,463.8 (g CO2e). Of 34 patients with existing inhaler prescriptions, there was an opportunity for a more sustainable inhaler choice for 50%, potentially saving 224,139.4 (g CO2e) – equivalent to flying 1,000 miles.

18 staff and 10 patients responded to the respective surveys. 50% of staff and 80% of patients did not know that some inhalers had a higher carbon footprint than others. Moreover, the majority of staff (66%) and patients (80%) did not know inhalers should be recycled at a pharmacy. 100% of patients surveyed disposed of their inhalers with household waste; however, 90% reported willingness to switch to a lower carbon footprint alternative.

A multidisciplinary team collaborated to arrange teaching sessions to raise awareness of inhaler sustainability for staff and medical students. Educational posters throughout the hospital and recycling reminders on clinic letters were introduced. In addition, we are currently looking into ways to recycle inhalers from the hospital directly.

Conclusion

Patient and staff survey results highlighted the lack of knowledge on sustainable prescribing and recycling. Audits revealed admission to AAU was an opportunity to switch to more sustainable inhalers where clinically appropriate. These results support the need for further education on inhaler sustainability within our hospital which will lead to greener prescribing and more inhaler recycling.
