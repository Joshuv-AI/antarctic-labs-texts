Unveiling the ITGβ1-CLIC1 axis: a new frontier in oral squamous cell carcinoma progression and immune evasion: insights from the tumor-mimetic ECM system
Authors: Yungang He, Renjie Shuai, Jian Wu, Lian Zhou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe progression of oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) is critically influenced by the dynamic interaction between the tumor and immunosuppressive microenvironment. Integrin β1 (ITGβ1, ITGB1), a pivotal mediator of cell-extracellular matrix (ECM) interaction, not only facilitates tumor invasion but may also plays a role in immune evasion, which remains inadequately understood. Emerging evidence indicates that ITGβ1 collaborates with chloride intracellular channel 1 (CLIC1) to regulate both the ECM remodeling and immunological function. This study examines the ITGβ1-CLIC1 axis in OSCC progression and its potential role in shaping the tumor immune landscape.MethodsThe Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) and Tumor Immune Estimation Resource (TIMER) databases were analyzed to evaluate ITGβ1 expression and its potential interaction partners in OSCC, with particular attention to tumor immune infiltration. Clinical tissues and associated data were gathered to assess the expression of ITGβ1 and CLIC1, and the relevance in a clinical context. To investigate the ECM-mediated regulation of ITGβ1 signaling, the tumor-mimetic ECM system was developed to replicate the native architecture of the tumor ECM and its immune-modulatory components. The system facilitated to investigate influences of ECM-ITGβ1 on OSCC cell migration, proliferation, and invasion. Additionally, to elucidate the interaction between ITGβ1 and CLIC1, western blot analysis of CLIC1 expression following ITGβ1 inhibition was conducted.ResultsAnalysis of the GEO database revealed that ITGβ1 is upregulated in OSCC and interacted with CLIC1. Further investigation utilizing TIMER2.0 analysis to examine the correlations between ITGβ1 and CLIC1 and the infiltration of specific immune cells, such as T follicular helper cells, suggesting a potential immune evasion. The expression levels of ITGβ1 and CLIC1 were significantly elevated in OSCC compared to the normal tissues, correlated with a poorer prognosis. In the tumor-mimetic ECM system, ITGβ1 overexpression was associated with enhanced proliferation, migration, and invasion of OSCC cells, indicating its potential role in ECM-regulated tumor behaviors. Subsequent western blot analysis identified a co-expression pattern between ITGβ1 and CLIC1.ConclusionThis study provides evidence supporting the association between the ITGβ1-CLIC1 axis and OSCC progression, and suggests its potential involvement in modulating the tumor immune microenvironment, indicating its promise as a therapeutic target worthy of further investigation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-025-15402-3.

Introduction

Oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) is among the most common malignant cancers worldwide and represents a major threat to public health, and the therapeutic effect and survival rate for patients with OSCC has not shown significant improvement in recent years [1]. The advancement of OSCC is a multifaceted, multi-stage process wherein the extracellular matrix (ECM) assumes a pivotal role [2].Critically, the ECM is not a passive scaffold but a dynamic regulator—a network of structural and signaling molecules that not only provides mechanical support but also actively regulates cancer cell behavior and immune evasion [3]. For example, in breast cancer, ECM stiffness promotes YAP/TAZ-dependent mechano-transduction, fostering tumor aggression and immune suppression [4]. In colorectal cancer, ECM remodeling creates a physical barrier that limits cytotoxic T-cell infiltration, polarizes macrophages toward an M2 phenotype, and upregulates immune checkpoints like PD-L1, thereby facilitating immune escape and therapy resistance [5]. Given the dual role of the ECM in driving OSCC progression and shaping an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment, targeting ECM-immune crosstalk may unveil new therapeutic strategies for OSCC.

However, despite this promise, the recent studies focused mainly on individual ECM components (e.g., collagen crosslinking) rather than their integrated effects on tumor-immune crosstalk [6]. This gap is particularly significant given that ECM-immune interactions are now known to critically influence metastatic spread and therapy resistance. A pivotal driver of these interactions is integrin β1(ITGB1; IGTβ1), the master regulator of ECM sensing, transmitting mechanical and biochemical signals that aid in immune evasion and prepare tumors for spread and treatment resistance [7, 8]. As the most ubiquitously expressed integrin subunit, ITGβ1 pairs with diverse α subunits to form heterodimers that bind ECM ligands (e.g., fibronectin, laminin, collagen), bridging extracellular cues with intracellular signaling [9–11]. In addition to its well-documented tumor-intrinsic functions through the PI3K/Akt and MAPK pathways, as well as he Wnt/β-catenin pathway in cancers [12, 13], ITGβ1 also modulates immune responses. For instance, research by Yu Ri Jung et al. demonstrated that ITGβ1-enriched exosomes promote metastatic niche formation in colorectal cancer, potentially by reprogramming immune cells [14]. Moreover, ITGβ1-mediated FAK/Src activation fosters an immunosuppressive microenvironment by inhibiting cytotoxic T-cell activity through ECM barrier formation; polarizing macrophages toward a pro-tumorigenic M2 phenotype and upregulating immune checkpoints [15]. These findings establish ITGβ1 as a critical intermediary connecting ECM dynamics to immune evasion, offering a promising yet underexplored therapeutic target to disrupt the tumor-immune crosstalk in OSCC. Notably, some clinical evidences have also corroborated ITGβ1 upregulation in OSCC correlates with advanced disease and poor prognosis, thereby offering a foundation for subsequent investigations [16, 17]. However, two key knowledge gaps remain unexplored: [1] Existing models fail to recapitulate the biomechanical complexity of native tumor ECM, restricting studies of ECM-ITGβ1 interactions; [2] The specific mechano-transduction pathways that underlie the functions of ITGβ1 in tumor regulation and the formation of an immunosuppressive microenvironment remain poorly defined. Therefore, addressing these gaps is crucial for developing therapies that simultaneously target ITGβ1-driven tumor invasion and immune suppression in OSCC.

To overcome these limitations, next-generation biomimetic platforms are rising to this challenge. Emerging tumor-mimetic system offers unprecedented opportunities to study ITGβ1’s dual roles in OSCC progression. This tumor-mimetic system employs a decellularization technique designed to remove cellular antigens while preserving the structural integrity of the native ECM [18–20]. Crucially, the tumor-mimetic ECM system maintains the biomechanical properties and immunosuppressive characteristics of tumor matrix, enabling to investigate ITGβ1-mediated ECM remodeling within its physiological context. By enabling controlled modulation of ITGβ1 activity, this platform permits a systematic analysis of its context-dependent effects on both malignant cells and immune populations, effectively addressing previous limitations in the study of ECM-immune interactions [21–23].

Furthermore, emerging studies indicate that the role of ITGβ1 may extend beyond conventional mechanical transduction pathways. For example, the research by Wang H et al. identified novel interactions between ITGβ1 and intracellular chloride channel 1 (CLIC1), offering a new insight for investigating the function of ITGβ1 in OSCC [24]. As a member of the CLIC proteins family, CLIC1 regulates intracellular chloride ion homeostasis and participates in fundamental cellular processes including volume regulation, membrane potential maintenance, and signal transductions, this channel protein exhibits unique dual localization, shuttling between cytoplasmic and membrane compartments to mediate its diverse functions [25]. The potential ITGβ1-CLIC1 interaction gains particular significance from three key observations: (a) CLIC1 is markedly overexpressed in multiple malignancies including glioma and gastrointestinal cancers, where it promotes tumor progression and therapy resistance [26, 27]. (b) Both ITGβ1 and CLIC1 exhibit independent capacities to modulate tumor-associated immune responses [28, 29]; and (c) Spatial analyses demonstrate their coordinated overexpression in tumor stromal compartments across multiple cancer types [30, 31]. These converging lines of evidence position the ITGβ1-CLIC1 axis as a potentially universal mechanism of tumor microenvironment regulation, warranting further investigation in OSCC.

This study employed bioinformatic analyses (GEO and TIMER2.0), clinical validation, and functional assays on the tumor-mimetic ECM system to elucidate the role of the ITGβ1-CLIC1 axis in the progression of OSCC through modulation of both tumor cell behavior and the immune microenvironment. Our findings indicate that ITGβ1 and CLIC1 are co-overexpressed in OSCC, associated with poor prognosis and altered patterns of immune infiltration, and interact functionally to drive tumor progression. This axis is thus identified as a potential therapeutic target for the treatment of OSCC.

Materials and methods

Gene screening and pathway prediction based on GEO datasets

The analysis was performed utilizing four independent datasets sourced from the GEO database (accession numbers: GSE23558, GSE25099, GSE30784, and GSE85446), consisting of a total of 113 samples (85 cancer and 28 normal tissues). Differential gene expression analysis was first performed to identify significantly dysregulated genes across the datasets. Subsequently, Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses were performed to investigate the biological functions and signaling pathways associated with the differentially expressed genes. Furthermore, the protein-protein interaction (PPI) network was constructed to depict the complex interactions among these proteins. The workflow of dataset integration, differential gene screening, functional enrichment, and pathway prediction is summarized in Supp. Figure 1A-B.

Analysis of gene expression variations and immune cell presence

Correlation analysis of 22 types of immune cells for eleven genes ((CLIC1 ITGβ1 ITGA3 ITGA5 ITGA7 ITGA9 ITGAV CD47 AKAP9 SEMA7A TLN1) selected in the Cox regression was performed using the “corrplot” package, and a heatmap was generated. Subsequently, “parallel” and “preprocessCore” packages were utilized to analyze the respective proportions of all samples in the 22 immune cells. Patients were then divided into a high-expression group (group high) and a low-expression group (group low) based on the expression levels of CLIC1 and ITGβ1. Next, “tidyr” packages were used to organize and analyze the data, and “ggsci” and “ggpubr” packages were utilized for visualization. CIBERSORT analyses were performed based on the proportion differences in the 22 immune cells. Finally, the immune cell correlation analysis was verified on the TIMER2.0 website.

Ethics and clinical samples

All research protocols in this study were reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of Chongqing University Cancer Hospital (Ethics Approval Number: CZLS2023198-A) and were conducted in full accordance with the ethical principles stated in the Declaration of Helsinki. This study conducted a retrospective analysis of samples of OSCC and normal tissues, collected from patients treated at the hospital between 2023 and 2025, and informed consent was obtained from all patients prior to the collection and analysis of tissues. Patients with recurrence, a prior history of radiotherapy or chemotherapy, secondary oral cancer, multiple primary malignancies, or other severe systemic diseases were excluded from the study.

The expression of IGTβ1 and CLIC1 in OSCC

Surgically resected tissues were pathologically confirmed OSCC, along with corresponding normal tissues. To accurately quantify the expression levels of IGTβ1 and CLIC1, this study systematically utilized three complementary detection methods: immunohistochemistry (IHC), western blot (WB), and quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR). In the IHC procedure, tissues were subjected to pretreatment steps including dewaxing, hydration, antigen retrieval, and inhibition of endogenous peroxidase activity. Following these steps, the sections were incubated with primary antibodies specific for IGTβ1 (1:500; Wanleibio, Shenyang, China) and CLIC1 (1:500; Wanleibio, Shenyang, China), followed by incubation with the corresponding secondary antibodies. After chromogenic detection using 3,3’-Diaminobenzidine staining and counterstaining with hematoxylin, the expression of target proteins was observed and recorded under optical microscope. In the WB procedure, total protein was extracted from tissues with precision and subsequently quantified. Protein separation was carried out using SDS-PAGE electrophoresis, followed by transfer of the separated proteins onto a PVDF membrane. Critical procedural steps, including membrane blocking, sequential incubation with primary antibodies specific to IGTβ1 (1:1000; Wanleibio, Shenyang, China; Rabbit anti-human) and CLIC1 (1:1000; Wanleibio, Shenyang, China; Rabbit anti-human), subsequent incubation with secondary antibodies (Goat anti-rabbit), and chemiluminescent detection, were systematically performed. Finally, the intensity of the protein bands was quantitatively analyzed using image analysis software to determine the relative levels of protein expression. In the qPCR procedure, total RNA was extracted from tissues, quantified, and efficiently reverse-transcribed into cDNA. PCR amplification was subsequently carried out using gene-specific primers, and the resulting amplicons were analyzed by agarose gel electrophoresis combined with image analysis software to accurately determine the relative mRNA expression levels. The primer sequences for the target genes as well as the internal control (GAPDH) are presented in Table S1.

Clinicopathological information

The clinicopathological parameters collected included age, gender, tumor location, grade, T status, N status, 8th edition stage, and recurrence status, the follow-up period was 2 years. Two pathologists independently assessed the expression levels of IGTβ1 and CLIC1 utilizing the positive cell counting methodology. Five high-power fields (×400 magnification) were randomly selected for analysis, wherein positive cells, defined by a staining intensity of ≥ 1+, were enumerated. Subsequently, the positive rate was calculated as the percentage of positive cells relative to the total number of cells. The mean positive rate across all tissues was used as the threshold to categorize expression groups. Tissues with an expression level less than or equal to the mean were classified as the low-expression group, while those with an expression level greater than the mean were classified as the high-expression group [32, 33].

Tissue decellularization

The fabrication of ECM-mimetic system involves the application of decellularization technology, which consists of a sequence of physical, chemical, and enzymatic processes performed on tissues. The main goal is to remove cellular elements while maintaining the native structure and biological functions of the ECM. First, the tissues were thawed at room temperature and cut into small pieces of approximately 1 cm³. Subsequently, the tissues were rinsed three times with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS; Dowo Bio, Chongqing, China) and treated with 1% Triton X-100 (Solarbio, Beijing, China) for 12 h. Following this, the tissue fragments were washed three times with PBS and subjected to decellularization using 1% sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS; Solarbio, Beijing, China). After decellularized, the tissues were rinsed again three times with PBS. Next, the tissues were immersed in 100 U/mL DNase I (Solarbio, Beijing, China) for 24 h, followed by rinsing with PBS for a minimum of 24 h. Finally, the processed tissues were preserved in a solution containing 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Dowo Bio, Chongqing, China) and stored at 4 °C until further use.

DNA quantification and agarose gel electrophoresis

According to the manufacturer’s instructions (Dowo Bio, Chongqing, China), genomic DNA was extracted from approximately 50 mg of native tissues (n = 3) and cancer-derived decellularized extracellular matrix (CA-dECM, n = 3). The extracted DNA was then quantified using a NanoDrop 1000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, Wilmington, USA). Furthermore, alterations in DNA content before and after the decellularized were analyzed by agarose gel electrophoresis, with DNA bands visualized using the Bio-Rad imaging system (Bio-Rad, California, USA).

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM)

The native and decellularized tissues were fixed by immersion in a 0.1 M sodium phosphate buffer solution (pH 7.4) containing 2.5% glutaraldehyde. Following fixation, the tissues were briefly rinsed with deionized water and then subjected to stepwise dehydration at 4 °C using ethanol solutions of increasing concentrations (70% for 10 min, 95% for 10 min, and 100% for 15 min). After dehydration, the tissues were transferred to acetone and subsequently dried using supercritical carbon dioxide in a critical point drying apparatus (Balzer, Liechtenstein). The dried tissues were mounted on aluminum stubs and coated with a gold-palladium alloy using an ion sputtering device (Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan). Finally, the coated tissues were analyzed and imaged using a SEM (Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan).

Histological analysis

Following fixation and paraffin embedding of the decellularized tissues, sections with a thickness of 6–8 μm were prepared and subjected to histopathological evaluation using hematoxylin-eosin (H&E) and Masson’s trichrome staining (Dowo Bio, Chongqing, China). To assess the distribution of key ECM proteins—including collagen I [34], collagen VI [35], IGTβ1 [36], and fibronectin [37]—within the decellularized matrix, IHC analysis was performed(primary antibodies all were purchased from Wanleibio (Shenyang, China), which were diluted at a ratio of 1:500). All experimental procedures strictly followed the standard IHC protocol; detailed steps were consistent with 2.4.

Preparation of ECM solution

Following the physical disruption of the decellularized matrix, the material underwent enzymatic digestion in a solution containing 0.1 mol/L hydrochloric acid and 10% (w/w) pepsin. This digestion process was carried out in a rotary oscillator operating at 225 rpm for 24 h. Upon completion, the enzymatic reaction was terminated by the addition of sterile PBS, which also adjusted the solution pH to neutrality. The mixture was then centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 5 min to collect the supernatant. The resulting matrix solution was further purified using filtration through a 0.22 μm membrane filter to ensure sterility. Finally, the solution was diluted with sterile PBS to the desired working concentration and stored at −20 °C for use in subsequent experimental procedures.

Cell culture and recellualrized

The human OSCC cell line HSC3 was obtained from the Shanghai Cell Bank, Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China), and cultured in DMEM high-glucose medium (Biological Industries, Israel), supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Biological Industries, Israel) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin antibiotics. Initially, the decellularized matrix was sectioned into 0.5 cm³ fragments and subsequently immersed in 75% ethanol overnight for disinfection purposes. Following sterilization, the matrix was thoroughly rinsed multiple times with PBS to remove residual ethanol, followed by pre-incubation in culture medium for 24 h prior to recellularization. Thereafter, HSC3 cells were seeded onto the matrix and cultured for a period of five days. Upon completion of the culture period, tissues were collected and subjected to SEM analysis to evaluate the biocompatibility of the matrix. For the next experiments, cells were allocated into three distinct groups: the control group (cells cultured in standard DMEM medium), the CA-dECM group (cells cultured in decellularized matrix solution), and the CA-dECM + IGTβ1 inhibitor group (cells cultured in decellularized matrix with the addition of the IGTβ1 inhibitor).

Concentration screening of IGTβ1 inhibitors

To determine the optimal concentration of the IGTβ1 inhibitor, HSC3 cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 5 × 103 cells per well and treated with varying concentrations of the IGTβ1 inhibitor (0µM, 2µM, 4µM, 8µM, 16µM, 32µM, and 64µM). Following 24 h of incubation, cell viability was assessed using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8; Bioss, Beijing, China). The concentration that exhibited minimal cytotoxicity while effectively inhibiting IGTβ1 activity was selected for subsequent experiments.

Cell proliferation assay

The viability of HSC3 cells cultured in CA-dECM solution at a concentration of 0.5 mg/mL was assessed using the CCK-8 assay. The specific procedure was as follows: 1 × 103 HSC3 cells were seeded into each well of a 96-well culture plate. Following a 24-hour incubation period, the original culture medium was replaced with different culture conditions. According to the manufacturer’s instructions, cell proliferation was measured using the CCK-8 kit on days 0, 1, 3, and 5.

Cell migration assay

Approximately 1 × 106 HSC3 cells were seeded into a six-well culture plate and cultured until the confluence reached approximately 80%. A scratch wound was created on the monolayer using a sterile 200-microliter pipette tip. The scratched area was then gently rinsed with PBS to remove detached cell debris. Subsequently, the cells were exposed to different culture conditions: the control group, the CA-dECM group, and the CA-dECM + IGTβ1 inhibitor group. At 0-hour, 24-hour, and 48-hour post-scratching, the wound healing status of each group was captured using a Nikon phase-contrast microscope (Tokyo, Japan). The degree of scratch closure was quantitatively assessed using ImageJ image analysis software by calculating the percentage of the remaining wound area relative to the initial wound area. Each experimental condition was repeated three times to ensure data reliability.

Cell invasion assay

The cellular invasive potential was evaluated utilizing the trans-well migration assay. In this assay, cells were plated at a concentration of 1 × 105 cells per insert in the upper compartment, and the lower compartment was replenished with different culture conditions to act as a chemotactic agent. Following a 24-hour incubation, cells that had successfully traversed the membrane to the lower compartment were subjected to fixation and staining procedures. Subsequently, the cells were enumerated under a microscope. The rate of cellular invasion was quantified by determining the mean number of cells per field of view. Ultimately, statistical analyses were conducted to appraise the significance of differences in invasive capacities among the three distinct cell groups.

Cell apoptosis assay

To assess the apoptotic potential of HSC3 cells cultured in vitro, 2 × 105 cells were harvested and analyzed using an Annexin V-FITC/PI apoptosis detection kit (Solarbio, Beijing, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The percentage of apoptotic cells was subsequently quantified using a Guava EasyCyte Flow Cytometer (Millipore, Massachusetts, USA). Apoptosis levels across different experimental groups were expressed as the percentage of total cells undergoing apoptosis.

Western blot

The total proteins were extracted from the cells in each experimental group, and the expression of IGTβ1 and CLIC1 were subsequently analyzed by WB. Specific primary antibodies were used to detect the target proteins: ITGβ1 (1:1000; Wanleibio, Shenyang, China; Rabbit anti-human) and CLIC1 (1:1000; Wanleibio, Shenyang, China; Rabbit anti-human), followed by incubation with secondary antibody (Goat anti-rabbit). GAPDH (1:1000; Wanleibio, Shenyang, China; Rabbit anti-human) was employed as an internal loading control to normalize and quantify the relative expression levels of the proteins under investigation.

Statistical analyses

Data processing and graphical presentation were conducted using GraphPad Prism 10 software, with results expressed as mean ± standard error of the mean. For comparisons involving more than two experimental groups, one-way analysis of variance was employed. To assess dynamic changes in cell proliferation across multiple time points, a two-way analysis of variance combined with Tukey’s multiple comparisons test was utilized. Error bars in the figures represent mean ± standard error of the mean. The Kaplan-Meier survival curve was employed to examine the association between IGTβ1, CLIC1, and recurrence.

Results

The expression of IGTβ1 in OSCC and its interaction network with CLIC1

Utilizing pertinent gene expression datasets sourced from the GEO database, we performed a comprehensive differential gene expression analysis within the R environment, focusing on identifying statistically significant differences. The analysis revealed several differentially expressed genes, encompassing both upregulated and downregulated genes (Fig. 1A). To ascertain statistical significance, we applied the following criteria: a logarithmic fold change (FC) exceeding 0.5 and a corrected p-value below 0.05 (FC > 1, P < 0.05). The significantly upregulated and downregulated genes were visualized using heatmaps via the “pheatmap (RRID: SCR_016418)” software package (Fig. 1B). To elucidate the functional roles of the differentially expressed genes (DEGs), we performed the GO enrichment analysis on the 16,058 DEGs using the DESeq2 R package, and the target gene, integrin β1, was found to be significantly upregulated in OSCC(Fig. 1C). The KEGG analysis indicated the potential involvement of IGTβ1 in various biological processes and pathological conditions, particularly in critical areas such as cell adhesion, cytoskeletal regulation, carcinogenesis, and infectious diseases (Fig. 1D). GO analysis suggests that IGTβ1 is pivotal in multiple cellular processes and structural functions, especially in cell adhesion, migration, and signal transduction (Fig. 1E). The PPI network illustrated the complex relationships among various proteins. In this network, nodes represent individual proteins, and edges denote different types of interactions. Notably, the proteins CLIC1 and IGTβ1 emerge as central hubs, indicating their significant roles in regulating diverse biological processes (Fig. 1F).

Correlation analysis of ITGβ1 and CLIC1 expression with immune infiltration

To investigate the relationship between ITGβ1, CLIC1, and immune function, the TIMER2.0 database was utilized to assess their influence on the immune microenvironment in OSCC. Heatmap analysis indicated a positive correlation between the expression levels of CLIC1/ITGβ1 and the infiltration of various immune cell types (Fig. 2A). Detailed profiling of immune cell proportions in patients revealed an increased abundance of neutrophils and a decreased proportion of B cells (Fig. 2B). Notably, the reduced infiltration of B cells may be associated with immunosuppressive PD-L1 subsets that inhibit humoral responses through PD-1/PD-L1 signaling pathways. Patients were stratified into high- and low-expression groups based on the median expression levels of CLIC1 and ITGβ1. CIBERSORT analysis demonstrated that the low-expression group for CLIC1 exhibited a higher proportion of T follicular helper (TFH) cells (Fig. 2C). Similarly, the low-expression group for ITGβ1 showed increased TFH cell infiltration (Fig. 2D). TFH cells are distinguished by their high PD-1 expression, which is essential for their role in germinal center responses. Subsequent correlation analysis confirmed a significant positive association between the expression of CLIC1/ITGβ1 and TFH cell infiltration (Fig. 2E-F).

Correction of IGTβ1, CLIC1 expression and prognosis in OSCC

The study analyzed tissues and clinical data from 50 OSCC patients, ultimately including 30 cases after excluding non-compliant ones, Fig. 4A illustrated the screening process of patients. Among these patients, 13 (43.33%) were under the age of 60, and the cohort comprised 17 male patients (56.67%). Tumor localization was as follows: 14 cases (46.67%) in the tongue, 6 cases (20.00%) in the cheek, 3 cases (10.00%) in the gingiva, and 7 cases (23.33%) in other locations. Regarding grade, 14 cases (46.67%) were well-differentiated and 9 cases (30.00%) were moderately differentiated. For T status, 17 cases (56.67%) were classified as T1-T2 stage, and 13 cases (43.33%) as T3-T4 stage. In terms of N status, 18 cases (60.00%) were in stage N0, while 12 cases (40.00%) were in stage N+. Expression levels of ITGβ1 revealed that 14 cases (46.67%) exhibited low expression, whereas 16 cases (53.33%) demonstrated high expression.16 cases (53.33%) exhibited low expression of CLIC1, while 14 cases (46.67%) demonstrated high expression. According to the 8th Edition of tumor staging, 14 cases (46.67%) were classified as stage I-II, and 16 cases (53.33%) were classified as stage III-IV. During the follow-up period, 10 patients (33.33%) experienced relapse (Fig. 4B) (The detailed distribution was presented in Table S2). Statistical analysis indicated that the tumor stage was significantly higher in the ITGβ1 high-expression group compared to the low-expression group (P = 0.0014), and similarly, the CLIC1 high-expression group had a higher tumor stage than the low-expression group (P = 0.012) (Fig. 4C). Survival analysis further revealed that recurrence-free survival rates were significantly lower in the ITGβ1 high-expression group (P = 0.032), the CLIC1 high-expression group (P = 0.046), and among patients classified as stage III-IV (P = 0.037) (Fig. 4D).

Confirmation of decellularization efficacy and biocompatibility

The Fig. 5A illustrated the process for preparing the tumor-mimetic ECM. Following decellularization, the tissue underwent significant modifications; notably, its color shifted from pink to translucent white, and its texture transformed from a solid to a gel-like consistency (Fig. 5B). To more accurately evaluate the efficiency of the decellularized process, quantitative analysis of DNA content and agarose gel electrophoresis were employed to compare and visualize the differences in DNA content between the decellularized and native tissues (Fig. 5C–D). The results of SEM demonstrated that the decellularization process efficiently removed cellular components, resulting in the formation of void-filled gaps and a preserved reticular structure. Following recellularization, SEM imaging clearly revealed the presence and distribution of cells within the ECM scaffold, confirming its favorable biocompatibility (Fig. 5E).

Characterization of decellularized ECM

The H&E and Masson’s trichrome staining demonstrated the absence of nuclear staining in decellularized tissues and revealed a disorganized arrangement of muscle and collagen fibers (Fig. 6A-B). To evaluate the effect of decellularization on the major components, IHC was conducted for collagen type I and VI, IGTβ1 and fibronectin. The results demonstrated that the primary proteins were systematically distributed within native tissues and remained largely unchanged following decellularization (Fig. 6C-F).

Systematic screening of IGTβ1 inhibitor

The effect of concentrations of the IGTβ1 inhibitor on the viability of HSC3 cells was evaluated using the CCK-8 assay. At a concentration of 0nM (control), cell viability was approximately 100%. Upon administration of the inhibitor, viability decreased to around 60% at 2nM and remained relatively stable up to 16nM. Further increases in inhibitor concentration, from 32nM to 1024nM, resulted in a gradual recovery, with cell viability stabilizing at approximately 80%. These findings suggest that the IGTβ1 inhibitor exerts a dose-dependent effect on HSC3 cell viability, characterized by a significant reduction at lower concentrations followed by a plateau at higher concentrations. Therefore, a concentration of 32µM was selected for subsequent experimental procedures (Fig. 7A).

The effect of IGTβ1 on the behavior of HSC3 cells

The CCK-8 assay demonstrated that the proliferative capacity of HSC3 cells was significantly elevated in the CA-dECM group compared to the control group; however, this capacity was substantially reduced in the CA-dECM + IGTβ1 inhibitor group (P < 0.05) (Fig. 7B).The results of the scratch assay revealed a significant enhancement in the migratory capacity of HSC3 cells in the CA-dECM group compared to the control group (P < 0.05). Introduction of the IGTβ1 inhibitor into the CA-dECM environment resulted in a marked reduction in cell migratory ability, falling below the levels observed in the CA-dECM group alone (P < 0.05) (Fig. 7C-D). These results were corroborated by the transwell assay, which indicated a significant increase in the invasive capacity of HSC3 cells within the CA-dECM group relative to the control group. Conversely, the invasive capacity was significantly diminished in the CA-dECM + IGTβ1 inhibitor group compared to the CA-dECM group (P < 0.05) (Fig. 7E-F). Furthermore, no statistically significant difference in apoptosis was detected among the various groups (Fig. 8A–D).

Discussion

This study aimed to systematically explore the role of ITGβ1 in OSCC progression and its potential functional interaction with CLIC1, with a particular focus on their possible impact on the tumor immune microenvironment. Analysis of GEO datasets indicated that ITGβ1 is significantly upregulated in OSCC tissues and may exhibit co-expression patterns with CLIC1. Notably, bioinformatic enrichment analyses suggested that the ITGβ1-CLIC1 axis might be involved in immune-related pathways, including ECM-immune cell interactions, cytokine-cytokine receptor signaling, and pathways related to PD-L1 expression and immune checkpoints. Consistent with these findings, the expression levels of ITGβ1 and CLIC1 were found to be elevated in OSCC tissues compared to normal counterparts, and this elevated expression appeared to be associated with a poorer prognosis. Furthermore, using a tumor-mimetic ECM system, we conducted a series of functional assays to preliminarily assess the effects of ECM and ITGβ1 on OSCC cell behaviors such as proliferation, migration, and invasion. Western blot analysis further suggested a potential synergistic relationship between ITGβ1 and CLIC1. Taken together, these findings suggest that the ITGβ1-CLIC1 axis may play a role in OSCC progression and could potentially influence the tumor microenvironment, implying that therapeutic strategies targeting this axis might offer both anti-tumor and immunomodulatory benefits.

A pivotal advancement in this study is the implementation of the tumor-mimetic ECM system, which overcomes the limitations of conventional 2D culture systems in replicating the tumor-specific ECM microenvironment [38, 39]. Unlike artificial substrates, the decellularized ECM preserves the native architecture, biomechanical properties, and bioactive composition of OSCC stroma while eliminating cellular antigens [40, 41]. The decellularized ECM successfully preserved key structural components (collagen, fibronectin) with minimal residual DNA, while maintaining functional properties that support OSCC cells growth and ECM remodeling. This physiologically relevant model enables investigation of both ITGβ1-mediated mechano-transduction and ECM-immune interactions, providing a valuable platform to study the ITGβ1-CLIC1 axis in OSCC progression.

To our knowledge, this study provides the first systematic exploration of the co-expression pattern of IGTβ1 and CLIC1 in OSCC. To achieve a more comprehensive understanding of their roles in OSCC, it is essential to explore the specific functions of these two proteins. IGTβ1 is a pivotal member of the integrin family, exhibiting widespread expression across various cell types and tissues [42],which functions as a critical transmembrane receptor that facilitates cell adhesion to the ECM by forming heterodimeric complexes with distinct α subunits [43]. These heterodimers specifically recognize and bind to critical ECM components, such as fibronectin, laminin, and collagen [44], thereby facilitating not only mechanical adhesion but also transduction of biochemical signals that influence multiple aspects of tumor biology. In line with previous reports in other malignancies [45–47],our data further support the importance of ITGβ1 dysregulation in OSCC pathogenesis. Similarly, CLIC1 has emerged as an important player in cancer biology. As a member of the chloride channel protein family, CLIC1 participates in maintaining intracellular ion homeostasis and regulating fundamental cellular processes including volume regulation and oxidative stress responses [30, 48]. The observed overexpression of CLIC1 in OSCC aligns with its reported oncogenic roles in gastric cancer, colorectal cancer, hepatocellular carcinoma, and glioma [49–51], where it has been shown to promote malignant behaviors through mechanisms such as cytoskeletal remodeling, epithelial-mesenchymal transition, modulating oxidative stress responses, and contributing to cancer microenvironment remodeling [52, 53]. Our study revealed significant coordination between ITGβ1 and CLIC1 in OSCC, backed by three key lines of evidence. Initially, bioinformatics analyses of large-scale datasets forecasted potential interactions between these two proteins. Subsequently, analysis of clinical specimens, coupled with clinical data, revealed that both proteins are upregulated in OSCC. Finally, molecular co-regulation experiments demonstrated that the inhibition of ITGβ1 results in a concomitant downregulation of CLIC1 expression. Concurrently, bioinformatics analyses indicate significant correlations between the expression levels of ITGB1 and CLIC1 and the infiltration of various immune cell types, including T cells, B cells, and macrophages. These findings suggest that the ITGβ1-CLIC1 axis may contribute to the establishment of an immune-suppressive niche by upregulating immune checkpoint molecules and altering chemokine gradients, thereby hindering the recruitment and activation of immune cells. Furthermore, the observed negative correlation between ITGβ1/CLIC1 expression and tumor purity, alongside their association with follicular helper T cell infiltration, indicates that this interaction contributes to a heterogeneous tumor environment. Additionally, the role of CLIC1 in ion homeostasis may extend to modulating immune cell function within the tumor microenvironment, potentially affecting pH regulation and oxidative stress responses, which could impair cytotoxic T cell function. Collectively, these findings underscore the ITGβ1-CLIC1 axis as a potential therapeutic target for immune-based combination therapies in OSCC, offering new avenues for future research and clinical applications.

While our findings reveal important aspects of the ITGβ1-CLIC1 axis in OSCC, certain limitations warrant consideration and caution in interpreting the results. Firstly, the clinical sample size, though informative, is relatively modest. A larger cohort would enhance the statistical power and robustness of our clinical correlations, particularly for subgroup analyses based on tumor stage, grade, and immune infiltration patterns. Secondly, while research has demonstrated a synergistic interaction between ITGβ1 and CLIC1, the precise mechanisms by which they contribute to tumor progression and immune evasion remain inadequately understood. This is particularly true regarding their interactions with immune cells and the downstream signaling pathways involved, which have been relatively underexplored. To address these gaps, future investigations will employ strategies such as knockdown and overexpression experiments, alongside animal models, to substantiate these findings and thereby enhance the scientific rigor and credibility of our research. Thirdly, although our tumor-mimetic ECM system provides a more physiologically relevant model than traditional 2D cultures, it still lacks the full complexity of a living tumor microenvironment, particularly a functional immune compartment. The absence of immune cells in this system limits our ability to directly observe and validate the proposed immunomodulatory effects of the ITGβ1-CLIC1 axis. Consequently, our inferences regarding immune evasion rely heavily on bioinformatic predictions and require validation in more complex models. Finally, all functional experiments were conducted using a single OSCC cell line (HSC3). The generalizability of these findings across different OSCC cell lines with heterogeneous genetic backgrounds should be confirmed in future work. Addressing these limitations in subsequent studies will be crucial for strengthening the biological insights and translational potential of targeting the ITGβ1-CLIC1 axis in OSCC.

Conclusion

This study provides preliminary evidence indicating a potential association between the ITGβ1-CLIC1 axis and the progression of OSCC. Our experimental findings hint at a possible role of ITGβ1 in promoting tumor cell malignancy through synergistic interactions with CLIC1 within an ECM environment that mimics the tumor microenvironment. Additionally, bioinformatic analyses indicate a possible link between this axis and the modulation of an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment. Collectively, these findings contribute to the understanding of OSCC pathogenesis and point to the ITGβ1-CLIC1 interplay as a candidate for further investigation as a therapeutic target. To advance this field, future studies are warranted to elucidate the precise molecular mechanisms of their crosstalk, develop targeted intervention strategies, and validate the clinical relevance of targeting this axis for innovative therapies aimed at concurrently addressing tumor invasion and immunosuppression.
