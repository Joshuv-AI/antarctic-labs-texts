Invisible pain: Sex/gender differences in pain-related stigma
Authors: Samantha M. Meints, Holly Thomas, Jenna M. Wilson, Simone P. Taubenberger, Robert R. Edwards, Ajay D. Wasan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Objective:Stigma is associated with worse pain, function, and negative affect (i.e., health outcomes). It is unclear whether the well-documented sex/gender disparities in health outcomes extend to stigma and whether relationships between stigma and health outcomes differ by sex/gender. This study examined (1) sex/gender differences in pain, mental health, and opioid stigma (i.e., health-related stigma); (2) the relationships between health-related stigma and health outcomes; and (3) whether sex/gender moderates relationships between health-related stigma and health outcomes.Design:This is a secondary analysis of cross-sectional baseline data from an RCT of multimodal treatment for adults with CLBP and negative affect.Setting:Three academic medical centers across the Midwest and Northeast.Methods:261 adults completed measures of pain, mental health, and opioid medication stigma, pain, function, and negative affect upon enrollment. We compared health-related stigma and health outcomes across cisgender men and women, examined correlations between stigma and health outcomes, and explored sex/gender as a potential moderator of the relationship between stigma and health outcomes.Results:Chronic pain stigma was associated with greater pain, poorer function, and greater (i.e., worse) negative affect, whereas mental health and opioid stigma were primarily associated with greater negative affect. There were no sex/gender differences in stigma or health outcomes nor did sex/gender moderate the relationships between stigma and outcomes.Conclusions:Stigma interventions may improving health outcomes among adults with CLBP and may be similarly important for men and women.

Background

Chronic low back pain (CLBP) is one of the most prevalent and disabling health conditions worldwide. In the United States, approximately 8% of adults experience CLBP, and nearly 75% have some disability as a result (Feldman & Nahin, 2022). The burden of CLBP extends beyond individuals, with estimated social costs, including direct medical expenses and lost productivity, as high as $624.8 billion annually (Dagenais et al., 2008). Despite its widespread impact, CLBP presents with considerable variability in symptom severity and functional interference, making it challenging to treat effectively (Hassan et al., 2023).

The biopsychosocial model of pain offers a lens through which this variability can be understood, highlighting the dynamic interactions between biological, psychological and social factors (Edwards et al., 2016; Fillingim, 2017). One factor that has garnered recent attention is pain-related stigma. Stigma refers to negative social attitudes and responses toward individuals perceived to possess characteristics that deviate from societal norms (in this case, chronic pain)(De Ruddere & Craig, 2016). According to the widely adopted tenets of the biomedical model, pain is expected to result from clear physiological pathology (Bendelow, 2013). However, chronic pain, particularly nonspecific CLBP, often exists in the absence of identifiable pathophysiological findings, creating a context ripe for stigmatizing experiences (Chiarotto & Koes, 2022; van Wilgen & Keizer, 2012). Indeed, there is considerable evidence documenting the pervasiveness of perceived stigma (i.e., an individual’s awareness or belief about negative attitudes held by others about their group) among individuals with chronic pain (Hickling et al., 2024). Patients commonly report not feeling believed by family and friends, and often feel blamed, misled, and dismissed by healthcare providers (De Ruddere & Craig, 2016). Public stigma can result in social disapproval, discrimination, and exclusion, and is associated with poorer physical and psychological well-being (De Ruddere & Craig, 2016). Perceptions of public stigma can also be internalized, a process known as self-stigma, whereby individuals come to believe and apply the negative stereotypes and beliefs held by others to themselves (Waugh et al., 2014). One study found that 38% of individuals with chronic pain experience internalized stigma (Waugh et al., 2014). Self-stigma can lead to isolation and social withdrawal, as well as greater pain intensity, more negative pain thoughts, and lower pain self-efficacy (Boileau et al., 2023; Hickling et al., 2024; Lavefjord et al., 2024; Waugh et al., 2014).

In addition to stigma related to pain itself, individuals with chronic pain may also face stigma associated with mental health conditions and opioid use, both of which are common among individuals with chronic pain (Groenewald et al., 2022; Mullins et al., 2023). There are high rates of comorbidity between CLBP and mental health conditions, including depression and anxiety (Sagheer et al., 2013). Individuals with comorbid chronic pain and mental health conditions report more stigma than those with chronic pain alone (Bean et al., 2022), likely because pain is often invisible and may be misattributed to psychological causes. Similarly, individuals who use opioids to manage chronic pain, even when prescribed, are at risk for stigma, as they may be perceived as drug seeking (Bean et al., 2022). While the relationships of mental health stigma and opioid-related stigma with pain-related outcomes have not been explored in the context of individuals living with chronic pain, broader literature suggests that each is prevalent and associated with adverse outcomes, including worsening symptoms, increased social isolation, and delayed or avoided treatment (Akdağ et al., 2018; Oexle et al., 2018; Yanos et al., 2020).

Because stigma is a social construct, and gender shapes socialization (Stockard, 2006), roles, and expectations, it is possible that experiences of pain-related stigma vary by gender. Hegemonic masculinity leads women who report pain to be judged as overly emotional or taken less seriously which results in women’s pain being minimized thereby amplifying their experience of pain-related stigma (Casale et al., 2021; Holge-Hazelton & Malterud, 2009; Samulowitz et al., 2018). This possibility is further supported by gender disparities in mental health (Jalnapurkar et al., 2018; Salk et al., 2017) and chronic pain prevalence, with women more likely than men to experience depression, anxiety, and chronic pain (Overstreet et al., 2023). However, findings regarding gender differences in medical stigma are mixed. Some studies suggest that women perceive greater stigma related to mental health (Busby Grant et al., 2016) and opioid use (Meyers et al., 2021), but report lower levels of internalized mental health stigma compared to men (Camp et al., 2002). Notably, the literature regarding chronic pain stigma is largely qualitative and shows that there are gendered aspects to the experience of pain stigma. For example, women frequently report that their symptoms are not taken seriously (Asbring & Narvanen, 2002; Glenton, 2003; Monsivais, 2013; Newton et al., 2013; Pryma, 2017; Werner et al., 2004; Werner & Malterud, 2003), often citing negative healthcare interactions. Conversely, other studies find no significant gender differences in stigma related to mental health (Batterham et al., 2013; Griffiths et al., 2008; Pfeiffer & In-Albon, 2023), opioid use (Meyers et al., 2021; Wood & Elliott, 2020), or chronic pain (Looper & Kirmayer, 2004). In sum, the relationship between gender and stigma in individuals with chronic pain remains unclear. Moreover, whether gender influences the relationships between stigma and pain-related outcomes is not well understood. It is possible that the stigmatizing delegitimization and psychologization of women’s pain may result in increased distress which may amplify pain severity via central mechanisms (Casale et al., 2021; Meints et al., 2018; Wilson et al., 2026). Men, on the other hand, are more likely to have their pain attributed to structural causes and receive early interventions resulting in less pain (Leresche, 2011; Seritan & Fishman, 2008).

Therefore, we conducted a secondary analysis of baseline data from a randomized clinical trial of adults with CLBP and negative affect (NA) (i.e., prominent depression and anxiety symptoms). Our aims were to (1) examine whether there were differences in chronic pain, mental health, and opioid use stigma by sex/gender, (2) explore the relationships between stigma and pain intensity and interference, functional outcomes, and negative affect; and (3) examine the extent to which sex/gender influenced the relationships between stigma and pain, function, and negative affect. We hypothesized that (1) cisgender women would experience greater health-related stigma than cisgender men; (2) greater health-related stigma would be associated with greater pain intensity and interference, worse functional outcomes, and greater negative affect; and (3) sex/gender would moderate the relationships between stigma and health outcomes such that the relationships would be stronger among cisgender women.

Methods

The current cross-sectional analysis used baseline data (prior to any intervention) from a randomized comparative effectiveness trial comparing antidepressant optimization, enhanced fear-avoidance rehabilitation, or a combination of the two for the treatment of CLBP with NA. Details of the trial’s methodology have been previously published (Wasan et al., 2023). The study was conducted at three sites across the United States (Brigham and Women’s Hospital in Boston, MA; Mayo Clinic in Rochester, MN; and University of Pittsburgh Medical Center in Pittsburgh, PA). This study was approved by the University of Pittsburgh IRB (2021P0000346) and conducted in accordance with the ethical principles originating in the Declaration of Helsinki, consistent with Good Clinical Practice, and applicable regulatory requirements. All participants provided informed consent prior to engaging in any study procedures. Three-hundred and eight individuals were enrolled and completed baseline assessments. Of these, 240 completed assessments of stigma at baseline and were eligible for inclusion in the current analysis.

Participants

Included individuals: (1) were aged 18–75; (2) had pain for at least 6 months and an average pain score of 3 or greater (out of 10) in the past week; (3) had the low back as the primary site of pain; (4) met Quebec Task Force Classification System categories I-III for low back; (5) had high NA defined as >85th percentile on both depression and anxiety modules of PROMIS; (6) receive medical care at one of the three study sites; (7) had access to the internet and a smartphone; (8) and were able to understand English. Potential participants were excluded if they: (1) had back surgery in the prior 6 months; (2) had psychiatric comorbidity including acute suicidality, history of bipolar disorder, or history of psychosis; (3) were pregnant or intended to become pregnant during the study; (4) intended to add new pain or psychiatric treatments during the first 4 months of the study; or (5) intended to have back surgery in the first 4 months of the study. Participants were recruited by volunteer registries, clinic advertising, electronic bulletin boards, and best practice alerts in the electronic medical records. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants, and the study was approved by the University of Pittsburgh Institutional Review Board as the single-site IRB.

Measures

The Internalized Stigma of Chronic Pain Scale (Waugh et al., 2014) (ISCP) is a 28-item self-report measure of stigma. It includes five domains: Alienation, Stereotype Endorsement, Discrimination Experience, Social Withdrawal, and Stigma Resistance. Each domain score is calculated as a mean for the included items. The ISCP total score is calculated as a mean of all items except those in the stereotype endorsement subscale. Higher scores indicate greater stigma. The reliability for the total ISCP in this sample was excellent (α = 0.90).

The Self-Stigma and Perceived Public Stigma Measures (Kendra et al., 2014) (SSPPS) is a 14-item self-report measure that includes the domains of self-stigma and perceived public stigma about mental health problems. Domain scores are calculated as the mean for the included items with possible scores ranging from 1 to 4, with higher scores indicating greater stigma. Reliability for the self-stigma subscale was acceptable (α = 0.75) and for perceived public stigma was good (α = 0.84).

The modified Brief Opioid Stigma Scale (Yang et al., 2019) (BOSS) is a 12-item self-report measure of stigma related to prescribed opioid medication use. The scale was modified from the original BOSS to clarify prescribed opioid medication use. Domains include awareness of stigma (range 4–20), agreement with stigma (range 5–25), and experience of harm related to stigma (range 3–15). Higher scores indicate greater stigma. Notably, harm of opioid use stigma was assessed only among those who were currently using opioid medications (N = 36). Reliability in this sample was good (α = 0.89).

The Patient Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System-29 (Hays et al., 2018) (PROMIS-29) assesses multiple domains of health and was used to assess pain intensity, pain interference, social function, depression, and anxiety. The PROMIS-29 has been validated for use among adults with CLBP (Khutok et al., 2021). The reliability for PROMIS domains ranged from good to excellent (α = 0.87–0.94).

Participants also self-reported demographic information including sex assigned at birth, gender, age, race, ethnicity, income, education, employment status, relationship status, and prescription opioid use.

Statistical analysis

First, we assessed the data for normality to determine appropriate statistical methods. Due to the small number of gender-diverse participants, we created a combined sex/gender variable with three groups: (1) cisgender women, (2) cisgender men, and (3) gender-diverse individuals. Descriptive statistics, including means (SD) and frequencies (%), are reported separately for each sex/gender group and for the total sample. However, for inferential analyses, gender-diverse individuals were excluded, and comparisons were conducted only between cisgender women and men. To examine sex/gender differences in demographics, pain, and functional outcomes, we used t-tests and Mann-Whitney U tests for continuous variables, depending on distribution, and chi-squared tests for categorical variables. Relationships between stigma and pain and functional outcomes were examined using bivariate correlations. Results of Shapiro-Wilk tests indicated that all variables except perceived public stigma of mental health and depression deviated from normality (p < .05), and thus nonparametric tests were used for relevant analyses. To test whether sex/gender moderated the relationship between stigma and pain or functional outcomes, we used Model 1in the PROCESS macro for SPSS. Given our modest sample size, bootstrap resampling was employed to enhance the robustness of estimates. This approach is advantageous as it does not assume normality and provides more accurate confidence intervals for indirect effects even in smaller samples. We conducted 5000 bootstrap resamples and calculated 95% CIs for moderation effects. Effects were considered significant if the 95% CI did not include zero.

Results

Participants were 261 adults with a mean age of 49 years (SD = 14.5). Most were White (76%), not Hispanic (89%), had less than a college degree (61%), were not partnered (56%), and did not use opioid medications (86%). Approximately half were not employed, and the median household income was $50,000–$74,999.

Regarding sex and gender, the sample included 166 (64%) cisgender women, 89 (34%) cisgender men, 3 (1%) transgender women, 1 (0.4%) transgender man, and 2 (1%) individuals reported their gender as “other” (both assigned female sex at birth).

There were no significant differences in age, race, ethnicity, education, relationship status, employment status, household income, or use of opioid medications between cisgender men and women (see Table 1). There were also no differences in any type of stigma, pain intensity or interference, function, depression, or anxiety between cisgender men and women.

Bivariate correlations between stigma, pain, negative affect, and functional outcomes

Internalized chronic pain stigma was associated with greater pain intensity (ρ = 0.158; p < .05) and interference (ρ = 0.417; p < .01), higher anxiety (ρ = 0.272; p < .01) and depression (ρ = 0.334; p < .01), and poorer physical (ρ = −0.405; p < .01) and social functioning (ρ −0.405; p < .01; see Table 2). A similar pattern was observed among most of the chronic pain stigma subscales. However, alienation due to chronic pain stigma was not associated.

with pain intensity, and stigma resistance was only associated with greater depression (ρ = 0.130; p < .05).

For mental health stigma, self-stigma was associated with greater anxiety (ρ = 0.363; p < .01) and depression (ρ = 0.415; p < .01). Perceived public stigma was also associated with greater anxiety (ρ = 0.189; p < .01) as well as worse social functioning (ρ = −0.170; p < .01), but was not associated with depression (R = 0.125; p < .05). Mental health stigma was not significantly associated with pain intensity, pain interference, or physical function.

Awareness of opioid stigma was only associated with greater anxiety (ρ = 0.231; p < .01) and depression (ρ = 0.137; p < .05), while agreeing with stigmatizing opioid beliefs and harm from opioid stigma was not associated with pain, function, or negative affect (p > .05).

Moderation analyses

Using the PROCESS Macro, we explored the extent to which relationships between stigma variables and pain, function, and negative affect differed between cisgender men and women. Because there were no significant sex/gender differences in demographic, stigma, or pain, function, or negative affect variables, we did not include any covaries in the models below.

Chronic pain stigma

In the first set of models, internalized stigma of chronic pain (total score) was the predictor variable, outcomes included pain intensity and interference, physical and social function, anxiety, and depression, and sex/gender was the moderator. Results indicated no significant interactions between sex/gender and chronic pain stigma for any of these outcomes (Table 3).

Mental health stigma

In the second set of models, the predictors were self-stigma and perceived public stigma of mental health problems (examined in separate models), outcomes included pain intensity and interference, physical and social function, anxiety, and depression, and sex/gender was the moderator. Results indicated no significant interactions between sex/gender and either self-stigma or perceived public stigma of mental health problems in predicting any of the outcomes (see Table 4).

Opioid Medication-related Stigma.

In the third set of models, the predictors were awareness of opioid stigma, agreement with opioid stigma, and harm from opioid stigma (the latter assessed only among participants who were prescribed opioid medications), outcomes included pain intensity and interference, physical and social function, anxiety, and depression, and the moderator was sex/gender. Results revealed no significant interactions between sex/gender and any opioid medication-related stigma in predicting these outcomes (see Table 5).

Discussion

The current study is a secondary analysis of baseline data from a randomized controlled trial of multimodal treatment for CLBP and negative affect. We found small-to-moderate relationships between chronic pain stigma and pain, function, and negative affect. In contrast, mental health stigma and opioid medication-related stigma were primarily associated with negative affect and, to a lesser extent, social function. There were no sex/gender differences in types of stigma, nor did sex/gender moderate the relationships between types of stigma and pain, function, or negative affect. To our knowledge, this is the first study to examine sex/gender differences in health-related stigma and its association with pain and related outcomes, as well as the first to examine the relationships between three domains of health-related stigma that impact individuals with CLBP.

As expected, we found that chronic pain stigma was associated with greater pain intensity and interference, worse physical and social function, and greater anxiety and depression. Consistent with a recent meta-analysis examining relationships between stigma and pain and negative affect, chronic pain stigma was more strongly correlated with pain interference (ρ = 0.417) than pain intensity (ρ = 0.158) (Hickling et al., 2024). Moderate relationships were also observed between pain stigma and both physical and social function. Many individuals with chronic pain report social isolation and poor functioning (Bannon et al., 2021; Franqueiro et al., 2023; Wilson et al., 2022). Based on our findings, it is possible that this is a response to their experience with pain stigma. That is, adults with CLBP may socially withdraw due to their desire to avoid stigmatizing experiences. Thus, behavioral interventions such as cognitive behavioral therapy and resilience-based interventions may be beneficial in improving social functioning (Renner et al., 2014). These interventions address fear-avoidance, and introduce assertive communication and coping skills which may combat and reduce the impact of stigmatizing experiences (Abbas et al., 2023; Lohnberg, 2007; Turk & Meichenbaum, 2017).

While results of the meta-analysis also found that stigma was moderately associated with depression, only two studies examined the relationship between pain stigma and anxiety. Results of these studies were mixed and, when meta-analyzed, resulted in no relationship between stigma and anxiety (Vallabh et al., 2014; Wadley et al., 2023). In our sample of adults with CLBP and high negative affect, pain stigma showed a small-to-moderate relationship with anxiety.

Regarding mental health stigma, we examined relationships between self-stigma (i.e., the internalization of negative stereotypes about a stigmatized condition) and perceived public stigma (i.e., the belief that others in society would judge you negatively for having a condition) with pain, function, and negative affect. We found moderate relationships between self-stigma and both anxiety and depression, but only small relationships between perceived public stigma and anxiety and social function. These findings are consistent with those from the mental health literature that demonstrate greater depressive and anxiety symptoms are associated with greater self-stigma for mental health (Busby Grant et al., 2016). Moreover, while perceiving public stigma about their mental health may be common among people with negative affect (Roeloffs et al., 2003), there is evidence that the internalization of these stigmatizing beliefs more strongly contributes to worse outcomes. According to Beck’s Cognitive Theory of Depression, depression results from a pattern of negative thoughts about oneself, the world, and the future (Beck & Greenberg, 1984). Self-stigma about mental health likely contributes to and reinforces negative self-schemas whereby negative thoughts result in depressive symptoms including avoidance which subsequently reinforce negative thoughts creating a cycle self-stigma and depression (Manos et al., 2009; Shimotsu & Horikawa, 2016). Thus, interventions targeting stigma should be aimed not only on reducing stigmatizing experiences for people with negative affect, but also helping people avoid internalizing these beliefs (Lavefjord et al., 2024). For example, interventions that provide psychoeducation to counteract myths about mental health and cognitive reframing techniques that teach people to identify and combat self-stigmatizing thoughts have been effective in reducing self-stigma (Yanos et al., 2015).

Perhaps the most novel aspect of this study was the examination of stigma about opioid medication use for pain. In our sample, which primarily included individuals not prescribed opioids, simply being aware of opioid-related stigma was associated with greater anxiety and depression. In other words, participants perceived that they could be labeled as “drug seekers” due to their chronic pain even if they were not taking opioid medications. This perception is consistent with broader societal views shaped by opioid pharmacovigilance resulting from the opioid crisis, where individuals with chronic pain are often assumed to be “drug seeking” (Buchman et al., 2016). There have been recent calls for a multilevel health equity approach to reduce opioid stigma, including strategies proposed by the Stanford-Lancet Commission aimed at destigmatizing opioid use and improving systems of care for people with pain (Humphreys et al., 2022).

Surprisingly, we found no sex/gender differences in the experience of stigma or in the relationships between stigma and outcomes. Our results add to the literature examining sex/gender and health-related stigma, supporting studies that found no differences between cisgender men and women (Busby Grant et al., 2016; Camp et al., 2002; Meyers et al., 2021). It is important to consider that previous work on sex/gender and stigma has yielded nuanced findings that depend on factors not considered in the current study (Geary et al., 2014; Goodyear et al., 2018; Wellman et al., 2019). For example, one study found greater stigmatizing attitudes overall toward males, individuals labeled as “drug addicts,” and those who took prescription opioids from a friend. Interaction effects also showed that females labeled with “opioid use disorder” and males labeled as “drug addicts” were rated with higher stigma (Goodyear et al., 2018). It is also possible that participants in this study have shifted from identity related concerns related to their health (e.g., “what does having pain, depression, or using opioid medication say about me as a person?”) to a focus on more pragmatic concerns such as access to care and pain relief. Such a shift could attenuate the effects of gendered stigmatizing experiences. Likewise, other domains of identity (e.g., disability status, socioeconomic status, trauma history) may exert strong effects on stigma and health outcomes than sex/gender thereby overshadowing sex/gender as a moderator. Together, our results along with this body of literature suggests that relationships between sex/gender and health-related stigma are complex and may depend on factors beyond patient sex/gender.

Results of this study should be interpreted in light of several limitations. First, we did not examine the impact of other types of stigmas including sexism, racism, fatphobia, and classism. It is important that future studies examine not only the individual influence of these types of stigma on pain, function, and negative affect, but use an intersectionality lens to understand how people at the intersection of multiple stigmatized identities may differentially be impacted by stigma. Further, while we provided descriptive data for health and stigma variables for participants who identified as gender diverse that suggest there may be differences in patterns of stigma, our sample consisted primarily of cisgender adults with very few gender diverse participants. As such, findings may not generalize to non-cisgender populations or other diverse demographic groups. Future studies should aim to recruit a more diverse sample, especially sexual and gender minorities, in order to better understand how their sex/gender influences their experience of health-related stigma related to chronic pain, mental health, and opioid medication use. Notably, our data is cross-sectional and, as a result, does not help to elucidate causal relationships between stigma and outcome variables. Future studies should examine these relationships longitudinally to better understand the extent to which stigma impacts health outcomes. Finally, while there is a theoretical increased risk for Type I error give the quantity of analyses in this paper, we failed to find significant sex/gender differences in stigma and sex/gender did not moderate relationships between stigma and health outcomes. Our only statistically significant findings (i.e., rejecting the null hypothesis) were in the bivariate correlations between stigma and outcome variables and these were primarily categorized as moderate associations which provides further evidence for our accurately rejecting the null hypothesis for these analyses.

Conclusions

This is the first study to examine three domains of health-related stigma among a sample of adults with CLBP and high negative affect. We found that chronic pain stigma was associated with worse pain, function, and negative affect while mental health and opioid stigma were primarily related to negative affect. Moreover, there were no sex/gender differences in the experience of stigma nor the relationships between stigma and outcomes. Together these results highlight the important role that pain-related stigma plays in the experience of people with CLBP and that it does not differentially impacting cisgender men and women. Future studies should aim to explore whether interventions for addressing stigma are equally beneficial across sex/gender or whether interventions must be tailored by sex/gender to maximize effectiveness.
