The clinical and psychosocial consequences of genomic Multi-Cancer Detection (MCD) tests in underserved populations: a scoping review
Authors: Esther Low, Shritha Gayathri, Alexandra Wicker, Amanda Courtright-Lim, Jessica Austin, Jewel Samadder, Steven Ressler, Jon Tilburt
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionMulti-cancer detection (MCD) tests currently being tested in clinical trials may be a scalable, convenient means to improve cancer screening, yet little is known about the potential clinical and psychosocial consequences of doing so in underserved communities.ObjectiveTo summarize the current literature examining potential clinical and psychosocial outcomes and consequences for MCD testing in underserved populations.MethodsWe searched and then reviewed articles from five databases: PubMed, Embase, Scopus, Web of Science, and Cochrane. For inclusion, studies had to primarily focus on MCD testing within underserved populations. Out of 376 articles identified, 29 met inclusion criteria.ResultsFinancial constraints were the most frequently cited barrier (n = 23). 24 articles mentioned clinical consequences and nine mentioned psychosocial consequences, with the most common being lower rates of cancer screening with proven existing recommended tests (n = 10) and emotional stress, worry, and anxiety (n = 11), respectively. Many expressed concerns about underrepresentation in clinical trials.ConclusionsThe review highlights common potential clinical consequences as well as important factors underserved populations may face if/when MCD testing is implemented in their healthcare. Future prospective studies, experimental and observational, should intentionally include underrepresented patient populations to better understand and address these challenges.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12885-026-16454-9.Keywords: Cancer, Screening, Barriers, Multi-cancer detection, Underserved populations

Introduction

Cancer remains the second leading cause of death worldwide, with its burden projected to increase over the next two decades [1]. More accessible, effective, and scalable screening tools that enable early detection and timely intervention could help reduce this growing burden. However, proven screening tools exist only for a limited number of cancers: breast (via mammography), cervical (Papanicolaou [Pap] and/or HPV testing), lung (low-dose computed tomography [CT]), and colorectal (colonoscopy, fecal occult blood testing, or stool DNA testing). Together, these account for only a fraction of cancers for which early detection may improve outcomes. Additionally, current screening modalities generally target only one cancer at a time, do not always detect cancers in early stages, and may be inconvenient, costly, or difficult to access [2]. These limitations can create substantial barriers for underserved populations already experiencing inequities in cancer prevention and care.

Persistent disparities in cancer outcomes further underscore the need for more accessible screening approaches. Underserved populations experience higher rates of late-stage diagnosis, as well as increased incidence and mortality rates for certain cancers [3]. For example, Black women have a 40% higher breast cancer mortality rate when compared to White women [4], and while rural individuals have lower incidence rates for most cancers, they have an increasingly higher mortality rate than their urban counterparts [5]. These disparities are mirrored in cancer screening: racial and ethnic minority groups have significantly lower colorectal [6] and lung cancer [7] screening rates, and there is a lower screening prevalence of breast, cervical, and colorectal cancers among individuals with a household income of less than $50,000 [8]. Delays in diagnostic evaluation after abnormal screening results are also more common, further contributing to inequities in outcomes [9].

In response to the limitations of conventional screening approaches, advances in molecular diagnostics, computational analytics, and biomarker discovery have enabled the development of minimally invasive cancer detection strategies. Emerging approaches include liquid biopsy platforms, DNA methylation profiling, proteomic and multi-omics assays, artificial intelligence-assisted risk prediction models, and spectroscopic techniques designed to identify cancer-associated biological signatures [10]. Collectively, these innovations reflect a broader shift in cancer screening paradigms, from organ-specific detection toward multi-analyte, system-level strategies intended to extend population-level reach and improve early detection.

Within this evolving landscape, multi-cancer detection (MCD) tests represent a conceptual category of approaches that may utilize a range of biomarker classes, including circulating tumor DNA, DNA methylation patterns, proteins, and other molecular or computational signatures to detect signals from multiple cancer types simultaneously. Although not currently approved by the FDA, some blood-based MCD tests are already clinically available, including Galleri by GRAIL and Cancerguard by Exact Sciences under Laboratory Developed Test (LDT) status. These tests offer broader early detection potential compared to conventional, single-cancer screening techniques [2, 11].

Importantly, MCD tests function as an initial step within a broader diagnostic pathway rather than as standalone diagnostic tools. A positive MCD result indicates the presence of a potential cancer signal but does not itself establish a cancer diagnosis. Diagnostic evaluation following a positive result may therefore require imaging, laboratory testing, endoscopy, biopsy, or other procedures to confirm malignancy and identify the tissue of origin. Access to these downstream diagnostic services may be limited for underserved populations because of financial, geographic, transportation, or healthcare infrastructure barriers. Furthermore, there are currently no standardized guidelines defining the extent or duration of diagnostic workup required after a positive MCD result when no malignancy is identified. As such, concepts such as follow-up care, diagnostic evaluation, and diagnostic resolution remain variably defined across the emerging literature. Consequently, the clinical value of MCD testing depends not only on test performance characteristics (e.g., sensitivity and specificity), but also on access to timely diagnostic evaluation, continuity of follow-up care, and appropriate treatment after diagnosis. These interconnected factors are particularly relevant for populations that already experience structural barriers to healthcare access.

The current evidence base surrounding MCD testing remains underdeveloped. Much of the existing literature is speculative or based on early-phase studies, with little empirical data evaluating real-world clinical or psychosocial outcomes. Early prospective evidence, including the PATHFINDER study [12], suggests that receiving a MCD test result may have measurable psychosocial effects on individuals, including impacts on anxiety, distress, and perceived health uncertainty. However, overall findings remain limited, and many proposed benefits and harms are still theoretical and contingent on how these technologies are implemented in practice.

Despite these uncertainties, MCD tests may hold particular relevance for underserved populations because of their comparative convenience, scalability, and potential to reduce indirect burdens associated with screening, such as transportation challenges, travel time, and time away from work [13]. Still, whether MCD tests will meaningfully reduce disparities in cancer outcomes remains uncertain given persistent structural, cultural, and healthcare access barriers, as well as limited representation of underserved populations in clinical evaluation studies [14, 15].

In this context of rapid technological development and limited empirical evidence, we conducted a scoping review to characterize the existing literature regarding the potential clinical and psychosocial outcomes, both positive and negative, associated with MCD testing in underserved populations. In doing so, we aimed to synthesize anticipated benefits, risks, and barriers, and to situate these findings within the broader context of emerging cancer screening technologies.

Methods

Conducting the search

We used the Joanna Briggs Institute Manual for Evidence Synthesis and PCC (Population, Concept, and Context) framework [16]. The population of interest was underserved individuals, which we defined as demographic or socioeconomic groups historically marginalized, including racial or ethnic minorities, immigrants, rural populations, under- or uninsured individuals, those with incomes below 200% of the federal poverty level, and others with known barriers to healthcare access. The concept was the clinical and psychosocial consequences of MCD testing, and the context was screening-eligible adults. For the purpose of this review, clinical outcomes were defined as downstream effects of MCD testing on healthcare utilization (i.e., use of other screening tools, access to diagnostic evaluation and treatment) and disease-related endpoints (i.e., stage at diagnosis, morbidity, and mortality). Psychosocial outcomes were defined as patient-reported or anticipated psychological, emotional, or social effects associated with testing, such as anxiety, distress, perceived risk, and availability of social support. Here we report our findings in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) [17].

With an institutional research librarian, we developed a set of MeSH-derived keywords to search PubMed, Embase, Scopus, Web of Science, and Cochrane. The search was conducted June 27, 2025, and included records from database inception through that date. The formal search was supplemented with manually identified articles, with detailed strategies provided in Appendix 1. Title and abstract screening, full-text review, and data extraction were conducted between June and July 2025.

Study selection

Studies were included if they: (1) were available in English; (2) had an available full text; (3) focused on MCD or MCED tests, and; (4) specified underserved individuals as defined above as a population of interest (Table 1).

Two independent reviewers screened candidate article titles and abstracts. Articles that seemed to meet the criteria at the abstract phase proceeded to full-text review when both reviewers agreed. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion and consensus between both reviewers. If the article did not meet the inclusion criteria, the criteria not met was also noted.

Data extraction and synthesis

Literature selected for full text review was documented and tracked via an Excel spreadsheet. Then, for data extraction we used a Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap) survey, where the two reviewers simultaneously extracted information from each study. The following elements were recorded for each study: type of literature, location of publication, location of population studied, main findings or key issues, any key theme areas in screening benefits, barrier concerns, clinical consequences, and psychosocial consequences (including relevant subthemes), and whether they proposed any solutions to mitigate potential worsening barriers associated with clinical and psychosocial consequences of MCD testing in underserved populations.

Results

Our search resulted in 376 candidate article titles and abstracts which were screened for inclusion. Of those, forty-five underwent full article review, sixteen of which were excluded, leaving twenty-nine studies for our final analysis (Fig. 1).

Characteristics of twenty-nine included studies

Included literature was published from 2022 to 2025, the majority of which were conducted in either the United States (n = 22; 76%) or the United Kingdom (n = 6; 21%), with one study being based in Japan. Most (n = 26) articles studied a single region, particularly North America (n = 21; 72%) or Europe (n = 5; 17%), with only a few studying global and/or multi-regional outcomes (n = 3; 10%) (Table 2).

Twelve articles (41%) detailed empirical studies, primarily consisting of surveys assessing attitudes toward MCD test usage or existing barriers in currently recommended cancer screenings that may influence uptake. The remaining 17 articles (59%) were commentaries raising concerns about potential challenges in implementing and evaluating MCD tests in underserved populations, while also highlighting projected benefits. Given the limited number of empirical studies, we decided to include commentaries for their valuable hypothesis-generating insights and critical reflections on a wider range of concerns relevant to underserved populations. While some studies identified in the initial search were longitudinal or experimental trials examining psychosocial or clinical outcomes of MCD testing, none focused specifically on underserved populations, and were therefore excluded from the final review. Accordingly, this review synthesizes the most current evidence to outline the clinical and psychosocial consequences and benefits that are expected as these technologies move toward broader use.

Findings - benefits

Across the literature, benefits clustered into accessibility-oriented and system-level domains, with accessibility and related themes (e.g., convenience, non-invasive and culturally acceptable design) discussed alongside broader claims of health equity, scalability, and clinical potential. Health equity potential (n = 18) and convenience (n = 12) appeared most frequently, often linked to expectations that a simplified screening tool would have the potential to improve screening adherence, uptake, and overall cancer burden among underserved populations, thereby leading to a general reduction in screening disparities.

Several articles highlighted unique, yet important, benefits. For instance, Schmeising-Barnes et al. [34] emphasized that blood tests are safe, quick, and familiar, characteristics noted as important facilitators of uptake. Similarly, Alshammari et al. [18] reported that MCD tests may offer a more culturally sensitive alternative to screening procedures that patients often refuse (e.g., Pap smears) because they are simple blood draws. Semenkovich et al. [35] also described the potential of MCD testing to address historical injustices, citing the example of nuclear waste contamination in St. Louis, Missouri, which disproportionately affected underserved families. Collectively, these findings indicate that MCD testing may enhance screening acceptability across underserved populations by combining procedural simplicity with improved cultural acceptability and broader responsiveness to historically rooted structural inequities.

However, these anticipated advantages were largely hypothetical and frequently contingent on assumptions about equitable implementation. For instance, Kessler et al. [29] expressed guarded enthusiasm, noting that while MCD tests hold promise, there is no proof that they will be able to reduce morbidity or mortality. Similarly, several authors cautioned that without conscious efforts to reduce disparities—such as equitable recruitment and representation in clinical trials—the health equity benefits may not be realized.

Findings - clinical consequences

Clinical consequences were not discussed as isolated outcomes but rather as interdependent effects embedded within broader care pathways. Across studies, three recurring and tightly linked domains emerged: (1) potential disruption of existing screening behaviors, (2) uncertainty and gaps in the care pathway following a positive result, and (3) downstream risks of delayed diagnosis and worsened outcomes. Notably, authors frequently described these consequences as cascading effects, where challenges in one domain amplify risks in others.

Disruption of existing screening behaviors

Lower screening rates were the most frequently cited clinical consequence within the literature. Ten articles identified this as an existing disparity in underserved populations and raised concerns that MCD tests may worsen it by deterring individuals from participating in guideline-recommended screenings (e.g., for colorectal, lung, breast, cervical, and prostate cancers). Authors of these studies seemed to agree that while MCD tests can detect multiple cancers from a single blood draw, they vary in accuracy by cancer type and stage, reinforcing the continued importance of traditional screening. However, some authors predicted that patients may mistakenly treat MCD testing as a substitute rather than a complement to standard screening, undermining its net value. Importantly this concern was rarely presented in isolation; most articles linked reduced screening participation to downstream delays in diagnosis and increased reliance on uncertain follow-up pathways.

Care pathway challenges

Most of the twenty-four articles identified post-test diagnostic evaluation as a major concern for underserved populations. If a patient receives a positive MCD test result, questions remain regarding subsequent management. As Loomans-Kropp et al. [3] note, “there is insufficient follow-up for those patients who are test positive but have no diagnostic resolution.” Moreover, the optimal extent and duration of diagnostic evaluation after a positive MCD result, as well as the frequency of repeat testing, remain undefined. Authors agreed that without a clear care pathway, the longitudinal clinical utility of MCD testing may remain questionable.

Another recurring concern across the literature was that many of the structural barriers contributing to disparities in conventional cancer screening among underserved populations would likely persist in the context of MCD testing. Even if an MCD test were free and available to an underserved patient, they may still face difficulties with convenience, transportation, and continuity of care. These issues are further complicated by the risk of false positives. For example, Sempere et al. [36] estimate that MCD tests could yield false positives for approximately 800,000 individuals per year in the U.S. In the PATHFINDER study, 57 of 92 patients with a positive MCD test did not receive a cancer diagnosis within 12 months of follow-up evaluation [3, 43]. Even when an MCD test predicts a likely tissue of origin, initial diagnostic evaluation of the suggested organ site may not identify a malignancy, potentially necessitating additional diagnostic testing across other organ systems to exclude cancer. In the case of a false positive or unresolved findings, patients may therefore undergo substantial and unfruitful service utilization. These concerns were closely tied to broader systemic barriers, suggesting that the clinical utility of MCD testing is inseparable from the strength of downstream diagnostic care infrastructure.

Downstream risks of delayed diagnosis and worsened outcomes

Several articles raised concerns that limitations in both MCD test performance and healthcare access could contribute to delayed cancer diagnosis among underserved populations. A recurring theme was concern about MCD tests’ reduced sensitivity for early-stage cancers. For example, sensitivity for stage I tumors was reported to be less than 40%, with some tests missing up to 85% of stage I cancers [36]. Authors cautioned that if MCD tests are used in isolation, either due to limited access to other screenings or by patient choice, this could lead to more cancers being detected at later stages.

Several studies further suggested that persistent structural inequities may limit the ability of MCD testing to translate into improved cancer outcomes. Even when cancers are identified, underserved populations may continue to experience delays in specialist referral, diagnostic confirmation, or treatment initiation because of healthcare infrastructure limitations [32]. Authors therefore questioned whether broader availability of MCD testing alone would meaningfully reduce cancer mortality without parallel improvements in access to diagnostic evaluation and cancer treatment. Coupled with the possibility of lower participation in established screening programs, these challenges could reduce the reduce the overall population-level benefit of MCD testing and potentially perpetuate existing disparities in cancer outcomes.

Findings - psychosocial consequences

Psychosocial concerns were less frequently studied and were predominantly hypothetical. The literature had a narrow focus on anxiety-related constructs; anticipated harms were closely tied to uncertainty in test results (e.g., false positives, indeterminate findings), while limited attention was given to broader social or structural dimensions.

Emotional stress, worry, anxiety

The most common psychosocial consequence these articles identified was psychological morbidity (e.g., stress, worry, or anxiety; n = 11). In a survey conducted by Crossnohere et al. [22], 56% of participants cited test-related anxiety and 96% cited the inaccuracy of the test as potential concerns. Participants correlated false positive results, lack of evidence, and the need for further screening to anxiety. Similarly, Minasian et al. [31] reported that anxiety was also linked to instances when MCD tests find indolent or untreatable cancers or cause unnecessary procedures. Worry was also connected to fear of diagnosis and cancer treatment burden on family and friends.

False reassurance

Several studies (n = 6) discussed the potential for false reassurance following negative, false negative, or indeterminate MCD test results. Although this concern was frequently discussed in relation to reduced participation in routine cancer screening, these studies also framed false reassurance as a distinct psychosocial outcome characterized by diminished perceived susceptibility to cancer following a negative MCD result. Authors suggested that misplaced confidence in negative findings may influence how individuals interpret future symptoms, assess personal cancer risk, and engage with subsequent healthcare decisions.

Social support

Only one study explored concerns about social support in the context of MCD testing. In this report, underserved populations expressed hypothetical concerns about receiving a positive MCD result, including challenges related to navigating next steps, accessing treatment and resources, and obtaining emotional support [15]. Participants also worried that, if faced with a cancer diagnosis, they might lack the necessary social support to pursue treatment. The anticipation of these challenges may deter underserved populations from undergoing MCD testing.

Overall, the psychosocial literature reflects an early stage of inquiry, with limited empirical data and a strong emphasis on individual-level emotional responses, leaving gaps in understanding community- and system-level effects.

Findings - barriers

Barriers were consistently framed as structural and systemic, often predating MCD testing but likely to shape its implementation and impact. Financial constraints (n = 23) and access to care (n = 16) emerged as dominant and interconnected barriers, suggesting that the appropriateness of MCD testing within underserved populations will depend heavily on broader healthcare infrastructure. Within discussions of financial constraints, lack of insurance and high out-of-pocket costs were cited as two important factors to consider in current cancer screening. For instance, Alshammari et al. [18] outlines how government subsidies covering costs of tests and logistical expenses are crucial for accessibility to MCD tests, especially for low-income populations. Some mentioned that many screening tests are nominally covered but high out of pocket costs continue to prevent usage and long-term effectiveness. Moreover, 29% of primary care providers surveyed by Ueberroth et al. [42] believe that GRAIL’s Galleri MCD test will remain entirely out of pocket for patients. Beyond logistical and financial obstacles, eight studies highlighted underrepresentation in clinical trials as another critical barrier, limiting equitable utility and access of MCD tests across diverse populations.

Taken together, the literature reflects a field in an early, hypothesis-generating stage, where anticipated benefits and harms are closely intertwined and highly dependent on assumptions about implementation. Across domains, a consistent pattern emerges: while MCD testing is framed as a tool to improve access and equity, its real-world impact is likely to hinge on care infrastructure, patient understanding, and continued engagement with guideline-recommended screening. These findings highlight that MCD testing should be understood not as a standalone intervention, but as one embedded within, and constrained by, existing healthcare systems.

Discussion

This scoping review highlights broad concerns regarding the clinical and psychosocial consequences of MCD testing that have arisen in largely cross-sectional, observational and commentary literature. Within the context of an evolving and still immature evidence base, few empirical studies have evaluated these outcomes, and even fewer have focused on populations most susceptible to cancer disparities. To our knowledge, none have studied the real-world consequences of MCD testing in everyday healthcare. Of the 29 articles reviewed, none were experimental or longitudinal studies, and only 12 were peer-reviewed. Despite this limited evidence base and a lack of FDA approval, MCD tests are already marketed to patients, raising concerns about premature clinical adoption without clear evidence of utility or equity for representative samples of adult patients of screening-eligible age.

Most discussions of clinical consequences were hypothetical, including the very real concern about continued participation in guideline-recommended screening, late-stage detection, and a worsening of existing challenges regarding access to treatment. Only limited cross-sectional and commentary data addressed these issues, and no studies provided prospective measurement of clinical outcomes such as service utilization or changes in treatment decisions. Addressing these gaps will require large, prospective, mixed-methods trials to measure the safety, utility, and downstream impact of MCD testing.

Similar to clinical outcomes, psychosocial consequences were mostly discussed as hypothesized concerns rather than studied in patient populations. Concerns included worry and anxiety related to false positives or indeterminate results, limited social support, and misplaced reassurance from false negatives. Future prospective studies might wisely attend to such psychosocial outcomes, including stigma, anxiety, communication barriers, and the role of social support, to understand the real-world implications of testing as this technology is still in the development pipeline.

Perhaps most concerning is that among studies included in this review, only four focused on underserved groups specifically, leaving a paucity of research on the role of MCD testing on psychological and clinical consequences. Excluding from or at least not adequately sampling these groups in MCD research risks exacerbating inequities by creating new evidence voids surrounding the safety, feasibility, or acceptability of this emerging technology in diverse settings, and leaving decision makers in a lurch about their effectiveness in community practice.

Ongoing trials, including Vanguard and NHS-Galleri, may provide critical data on the performance of MCD tests, but underrepresentation of underserved populations remains a concern. To ensure MCD testing is likely to benefit all, future research must pair rigorous clinical evaluation with inclusive study designs that measure both clinical and psychosocial outcomes across real-world settings. These studies should intentionally recruit diverse populations and explicitly measure barriers such as cost, follow-up access, and social support. Only by addressing these questions directly can MCD tests realize their promise as an equitable innovation in cancer screening.

Limitations

This review has several limitations. We restricted our search to English-language articles in five databases that primarily capture U.S.-based literature, which may limit global generalizability. At the same time, the inclusion of studies from diverse international contexts, where structural and policy differences exist, may complicate interpretation of findings. We did not formally assess study quality, limiting our ability to evaluate the reliability and validity of results. Including commentaries allowed us to capture broader perspectives but also introduced subjective, non-empirical viewpoints. It is also possible that terminology differences in defining “underserved” may have influenced our article yield. Finally, the limited number of empirical studies constrained our ability to evaluate disparities in clinical and psychological consequences.

Conclusion

This review clarifies the hypothesized consequences of MCD testing for underserved populations and was conducted with methodological rigor using PRISMA-ScR. Yet the limited literature highlights important considerations about the state of the field and gaps in the current evidence base that may guide the next steps for clinicians, researchers, policymakers, and patients.

To fulfill their potential in improving cancer screening across a broad range of patient populations, MCD tests must be evaluated in clinical trials that include underserved groups, assess both clinical and psychosocial consequences, and determine whether hypothesized concerns manifest in real-world care settings. Future prospective studies, both experimental and observational, should systematically measure these outcomes in varied settings so that all patients have the opportunity to benefit from this promising technology. Ultimately, for MCD tests to positively impact population health, they must be validated in diverse populations with potential barriers and consequences explicitly addressed.
