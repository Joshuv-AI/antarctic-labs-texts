Reversed intestinal malrotation with volvulus in an adult: A case report
Authors: Dumitru Cravcenco, Maxim Crivceanschii, Marinela Secrieru, Maria Cobzac, Feodor Ostopovici
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Congenital intestinal malrotation is a rare developmental anomaly of midgut rotation and fixation, with an estimated incidence of 0.2–0.5% in the adult population. Reversed intestinal rotation represents the rarest variant, accounting for approximately 2–4% of all malrotation cases, and is frequently associated with delayed diagnosis due to nonspecific symptoms. We report the case of a 45-year-old woman with long-standing gastrointestinal symptoms who presented with subocclusive features. Contrast-enhanced multiphasic computed tomography revealed reversed intestinal malrotation complicated by ileocecal volvulus, characterized by abnormal positioning of the bowel, inversion of the superior mesenteric vessels and a typical whirlpool sign. Surgical exploration via midline laparotomy confirmed the imaging findings, including Ladd’s band and increased cecal mobility. A Ladd procedure was performed, consisting of division of fibrous bands, derotation of the bowel, widening of the mesenteric base and appendectomy. The postoperative course was uneventful, with complete resolution of symptoms. This case highlights the essential role of cross-sectional imaging in establishing early diagnosis and guiding timely surgical management to prevent life-threatening complications.

Introduction

Congenital intestinal malrotation is a developmental anomaly caused by abnormal rotation and fixation of the midgut around the superior mesenteric artery during embryogenesis, occurring between the fifth and twelfth weeks of gestation. Although typically diagnosed in infancy, the condition may remain asymptomatic until adulthood, where it is rare, with a reported incidence of 0.2–0.5% [1,2].

Reversed intestinal rotation is the rarest subtype of malrotation, accounting for approximately 2–4% of cases, and is characterized by abnormal anatomical relationships between the duodenum, colon and mesenteric vessels [3,4]. In adults, clinical presentation is often nonspecific, ranging from chronic abdominal symptoms to acute bowel obstruction, frequently resulting in delayed diagnosis [2,5].

Complications such as midgut or ileocecal volvulus may lead to intestinal ischemia and significant morbidity if not promptly recognized [6,7]. Contrast-enhanced multiphasic computed tomography is the imaging modality of choice, as it can demonstrate characteristic features including abnormal orientation of the mesenteric vessels and the whirlpool sign [8,9]. Early diagnosis is essential to enable timely surgical intervention and prevent life-threatening outcomes [6,9].

Case presentation

The patient, a 45-year-old female, has a long clinical history notable for recurrent episodes of bilious vomiting beginning at 3 months of age, including several episodes of feculent vomiting. She was frequently hospitalized during infancy for infections and developed severe malnutrition by one year of age. Throughout her life, symptoms were dominated by persistent vomiting accompanied by progressively worsening abdominal pain. Later, she experienced episodes of acute renal failure, which led to a diagnosis of secondary chronic renal impairment.

In March 2022, she was admitted to the Surgery Department with nausea, postprandial bilious vomiting, and frequent diarrhea of recent onset. Physical examination revealed clean, dry, and warm skin; symmetrical chest wall movement during respiration; respiratory rate of 17/min; pulse of 88 beats/min; and blood pressure of 120/80 mmHg. The tongue was moist. The abdomen was distended and tender to palpation, with peristalsis present and no signs of peritoneal irritation. Stools were diarrheic. Laboratory investigations on admission showed elevated serum urea (9.9 mmol/L; normal range 2.8–8.3) and creatinine (204 μmol/L; normal range 44–115), consistent with acute exacerbation of underlying chronic renal impairment. Mild hyperglycemia (10.0 mmol/L) and mild leukocytosis (WBC 10.8 × 10⁹/L) were also noted. Hemoglobin and hematocrit were at the upper limits of normal (153 g/L and 47.6%, respectively), likely reflecting hemoconcentration. Serum bilirubin and liver aminotransferases were within normal limits.

The CT scan demonstrated a significantly modified anatomy of the midgut, as shown in Fig. 1, Fig. 2. The duodenum crossed from right to left ventrally up to the superior mesenteric artery (SMA), suggesting an intraperitoneal position in all its portions, with the duodenojejunal flexure situated slightly to the left of the midline. The following slightly dilated proximal jejunal loops were found in the left abdomen, involved in volvulus, subsequently the distal jejunal loops located in the right part of the abdomen (Fig. 1B,C and Fig. 2 A,B,C). Some ileal loops were situated in the lower left abdomen (Fig. 1C and Fig. 2C); the ascending colon (with a course from the right part of the abdomen) and the cecum were found in the left part of the abdomen (involved in the intestinal volvulus), thus suggesting a narrow mesenteric pedicle (Fig. 1B,C and Fig. 2B, C). Both the ascending colon and the cecum remained ventral to the mesenteric root, in an intraperitoneal position. The transverse colon passed dorsal to the pedicle of the superior mesenteric artery (SMA) and superior mesenteric vein (SMV) in a retro-arterial position (Fig. 2A), defining this anatomical variation of the midgut as a reversed rotation and extended into the left abdomen to continue as a normal left colic flexure and descending colon. We also observed a reversed relationship of the SMV to the SMA, the vein being situated to the left of the artery (Fig. 1A). The whirlpool sign, produced by the twisted mesenteric vessels and bowel loops around the axis of rotation, was considered pathognomonic for volvulus and represented a key element in establishing the diagnosis (Fig. 2A). Three-dimensional CT angiographic reconstruction further demonstrated a segmental occlusion of the SMA at the level of the twisted mesenteric pedicle, together with dilatation of the porto-mesenteric venous system (Fig. 3A,B), findings that confirmed the vascular compromise suggested by the whirlpool sign and supported the indication for emergency surgical intervention.Fig. 3Three-dimensional volume-rendered CT angiographic reconstruction obtained during the initial preoperative examination. The differential diagnosis of intestinal obstruction in adults includes conditions that may mimic intestinal malrotation. Cecal volvulus typically presents with marked cecal dilatation and ectopic positioning without inversion of the superior mesenteric artery and vein. Sigmoid volvulus is characterized by a markedly distended colonic loop with the classical “coffee bean” sign. Postoperative adhesions and internal hernias (paraduodenal or mesenteric) may also result in bowel obstruction but lack congenital rotational abnormalities.

After establishing the imaging diagnosis, the patient was admitted to the Abdominal Surgery Department, where she was evaluated by the surgical team. Subsequently, surgical intervention was performed via midline laparotomy, which revealed the presence of an intestinal volvulus located in the left flank of the abdominal cavity (left paramedian), a fibrous intestinal band of the Ladd type (a consequence of intestinal malrotation), in the absence of signs of intestinal ischemia at the time of intervention. The "Ladd" surgical procedure was performed, which included: resection of the Ladd fibrous band (which was not visible on CT examination), appendectomy and right oophorectomy, repositioning of the small intestine loop and its fixation to prevent volvulus recurrence.

Postoperatively, the patient was admitted to the intensive care unit for monitoring and supportive care. The postoperative course was uneventful. Follow-up computed tomography demonstrated resolution of the volvulus, with disappearance of the whirlpool sign and restoration of normal bowel configuration (Fig. 4). Discussion

Congenital intestinal malrotation is typically diagnosed in the neonatal period; however, in rare cases it may remain clinically silent until adulthood, when it is often associated with delayed diagnosis and increased morbidity. The reported incidence of intestinal malrotation in adults is low, estimated at 0.2–0.5%, which contributes to its frequent exclusion from the differential diagnosis of chronic or recurrent gastrointestinal symptoms in this population [4,5]. This diagnostic delay is well illustrated by the present case, in which symptoms began in early infancy and persisted for decades before the correct diagnosis was established.

Reversed intestinal rotation represents the rarest variant of intestinal malrotation, accounting for only 2–4% of cases [7]. This anomaly results from an abnormal sequence of midgut rotation and fixation during embryogenesis and is characterized by atypical anatomical relationships between the duodenum, colon and mesenteric vessels. Two anatomical forms have been described: retro-arterial and pre-arterial, depending on the position of the transverse colon relative to the superior mesenteric artery [7,9]. In the present case, imaging findings were consistent with the retro-arterial form, a configuration frequently associated with inadequate fixation and increased mobility of the cecum and ascending colon, predisposing to volvulus [7,10,11].

Adult intestinal malrotation poses a significant diagnostic challenge because clinical manifestations are often nonspecific, ranging from chronic intermittent abdominal pain and vomiting to acute intestinal obstruction. Conventional radiography and clinical examination have limited diagnostic value, making cross-sectional imaging essential for diagnosis. Contrast-enhanced computed tomography is currently regarded as the imaging modality of choice due to its high sensitivity and specificity in detecting malrotation and its complications [12,13]. The presence of abnormal bowel positioning, inversion of the superior mesenteric vessels and the whirlpool sign are highly suggestive of volvulus in the setting of malrotation and were key elements in establishing the diagnosis in this patient [7,12].

Surgical treatment remains the definitive management for symptomatic intestinal malrotation. The Ladd procedure, first described in 1936, is considered the standard surgical approach and aims to relieve obstruction, correct abnormal rotation and reduce the risk of recurrent volvulus [12,14,15]. In the present case, timely surgical intervention resulted in resolution of the volvulus without intestinal ischemia and was followed by a favorable postoperative outcome. This underscores the importance of early recognition and prompt surgical management to prevent life-threatening complications such as bowel ischemia and necrosis.
