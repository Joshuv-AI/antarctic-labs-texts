Electroacupuncture promotes gastrointestinal functional recovery after radical colorectal cancer surgery: a protocol of multicenter randomized controlled trial (CORRECT trial)
Authors: Linxi Sun, Xuqiang Wei, Tienan Feng, Qunhao Gu, Jing Li, Ke Wang, Jia Zhou
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
IntroductionThe incidence of postoperative gastrointestinal dysfunction (POGD) is notably high among patients following colorectal cancer surgery, highlighting the urgency for the prompt development of efficacious preventive and therapeutic approaches. Electroacupuncture (EA) represents an intervention modality that holds promise for the management of POGD. However, the existing empirical evidence substantiating its efficacy remains scarce. The aim of this study is to evaluate the efficacy and safety of EA as a treatment for POGD in patients undergoing colorectal cancer surgery.MethodsThis study is a multicenter, parallel-group, randomized controlled trial, named as CORRECT. CORRECT trial will recruit 300 participants diagnosed with colorectal cancer and about to undergo radical surgery across four sub-centers. The participants will be randomly assigned to one of three groups: the EA group, sham-electroacupuncture group, or control group, with a randomization ratio of 2:2:1. All groups will follow a standardized Enhanced Recovery After Surgery (ERAS) protocol. The EA group will receive EA at acupoints LI4, SJ6, ST36, and ST37, while the SA group will undergo sham-electroacupuncture. The treatments will be administered twice daily from the day of surgery until the fourth day after the operation. The primary endpoint is the time to first flatus, while secondary endpoints encompass time to first defecation, bowel sound emergence, initial water intake, duration of postoperative hospitalization, nausea and vomiting, pain levels, and blinded evaluations. Additional outcomes include medication usage and complication rates, et al.DiscussionThe CORRECT trial aims to provide high-quality evidence for the role of EA in the treatment of POGD following colorectal cancer surgery. It will contribute data towards the integration of acupuncture into ERAS protocols. Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is currently ranked as the third most diagnosed cancer and the second most lethal cancer worldwide. Projections indicate that by 2040, CRC will contribute to 3.2 million new cases and 1.6 million deaths [1, 2]. Radical resection surgery, which is a fundamental component [3, 4], is widely performed. However, abdominal surgery often leads to gastrointestinal dysfunction, resulting in prolonged impairment [5, 6]. Symptoms associated with postoperative gastrointestinal dysfunction (POGD) encompass abdominal distension, pain, nausea, vomiting, weakened or absent intestinal sounds, and absence of anal venting and defecation, potentially leading to other complications [7]. POGD, also known as postoperative ileus (POI), is defined as “inability to tolerate oral feeding during the postoperative period until the recovery of gas and defecation,” and some scholars define POGD as “inability to fully feed via oral feeding due to transient inhibition of gastrointestinal motility in the postoperative period due to non-mechanical reasons” [8]. In 2018, a consensus of the United States Perioperative Quality Initiative Task Force proposed the Intake, Feeling nauseated, Emesis, Exam, and Duration of symptoms scoring system (I-FEED, Fig S1) [9] that is currently the most recommended criterion for diagnosing POGD.

The reported incidence of POGD in CRC radical surgery is notably higher due to specific procedures such as intestinal resection, splenic flexure mobilization, ostomy formation, and open surgical methods, with rates approximately ranging from 17.4 to 34.9% [10–13]. This has significant implications for patients’ postoperative quality of life of patients, as it increases the likelihood of other complications, exacerbates the risk of postoperative mortality, and prolongs hospital stays. A comprehensive meta-analysis conducted on a global scale, encompassing a sample size of 1,860,889 surgical patients [14], has demonstrated that POGD leads to a mean rise in overall hospitalization expenses amounting to 8233 euros or 66.3%, substantially escalating the economic burden on patients and health insurance systems [15].

The onset of POGD is triggered by surgical trauma and intestinal manipulation, which initiate an inflammatory response in the extra-intestinal muscularis propria that involves immune-inflammatory cells, reactive intestinal neuroglia, smooth muscle cells, interstitial cells of Cajal, enteroendocrine cells, epithelial cells, and the luminal microbiome [16]. This neuroinflammatory response is associated with dyskinesia, delayed transit, and constipation. Interestingly, the activation of exogenous neural pathways, for example, through vagus nerve stimulation, has been shown to mitigate gastrointestinal dysfunction and postoperative intestinal obstruction [17]. Prompt recovery of gastrointestinal function is crucial for accelerating postoperative recuperation in CRC patients. Currently, there are various postoperative treatments like avimopam, chewing gum, coffee intake, prokinetic drugs, and nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs [18], yet their clinical efficacy is limited. For instance, prokinetic drugs are debated due to contraindications in fasted patients post upper gastrointestinal surgery. Despite the International Society for Enhanced Recovery After Surgery (ERAS) endorsed several measures [19] to prevent POGD, including less preoperative fasting period, laparoscopic surgery, avoiding abdominal drainage, and restricting intravenous fluids, no clear prevention and treatment guidelines have been established internationally so far [20, 21]. Current pharmacological strategies for POGD prevention are suboptimal.

Considering CRC’s prevalence and the significant impact of POGD on patient outcomes, exploring alternative therapeutic approaches is crucial. Electroacupuncture (EA), a widely accepted and safe therapy, has shown efficacy in enhancing post-CRC surgery gastrointestinal recovery by regulating motility, providing analgesia, and reducing nausea and vomiting [22–25]. Studies have confirmed that acupuncture significantly lessens postoperative abdominal discomfort and bloating, improves intestinal ventilation, and aids in dietary restoration. EA stimulates gastric expansion and emptying via the sympathetic nervous system and decreases colorectal tumor cell count and inflammatory response, potentially through activating the expression of intestinal silent information regulator 1, thereby promoting cell autophagy [26]. Acupuncture has gained widespread acceptance in clinical practice as an efficacious therapeutic option for POGD.

Despite an increasing number of clinical researches on acupuncture for POGD in recent years, meta-analyses [25] reveal considerable heterogeneity among studies. Consequently, there is a pressing need for high-quality studies with adequate effect size designs. In light of this, we are poised to initiate a multicenter, large-sample, sham-acupuncture randomized controlled trials (RCT), aiming to provide a solid clinical foundation for optimizing acupuncture treatment for POGD in CRC patients.

Methods

Study design

This will be a prospective, multicenter, sham-controlled RCT, employing a three-arm parallel group design with an allocation ratio of 2:2:1. Clinical Trial Registry registration was approved by the ClinicalTrials.gov committee on November 2023 with the ClinicalTrials.gov Identifier: NCT06128785.

Each hospital will be required to provide written informed consent in accordance with the latest version of the Helsinki Declaration. Participants and setting

Each center participates in a competitive enrollment process. A total of 300 eligible participants will be randomly assigned to the EA group, SA group, and control group. After meeting the inclusion criteria and signing the informed consent form, a total of 300 Chinese patients undergoing CRC surgery will be sequentially enrolled. Clinical assistants who have received training from the institutional review board will be responsible for patient registration.

Eligibility

Diagnostic criteria

Definitive pathological diagnoses of colorectal cancer will be established [4], utilizing the eighth edition of the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) TNM staging system [29].

Inclusion criteria

(1) Patients who meet the diagnostic criteria for colorectal cancer and require scheduled curative surgery for CRC. (2) Age between 18 and 79 years, regardless of gender. (3) Understanding and willingness to participate in the study, with a signed informed consent form. (4) American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) classification grade I to III.

Exclusion criteria

(1) Patients with mental disorders. (2) Patients who have participated in or are currently participating in other clinical studies within the past 3 months. (3) Patients with severe malnutrition (NRS2002 score ≥ 3). (4) Patients who have undergone total colectomy. (5) Patients who have undergone ileorectal anastomosis for rectal cancer. (6) Patients requiring resection of other organs due to tumor infiltration. (7) Patients with prophylactic stomas. Any patient meeting any of the above criteria will be excluded from the study.

Criteria for exclusion, termination, and dropout

(1) Patients experiencing unbearable pain or severe adverse reactions during acupuncture treatment. (2) Patients whose symptoms worsen or develop other critical illnesses during observation and cannot continue treatment. (3) The investigator identifies serious safety issues. (4) Patients unable to adhere to the treatment for various reasons. (5) Patients who voluntarily withdraw from the study. (6) Loss to follow-up. Terminated cases should be explained, and data analysis will follow the intention-to-treat (ITT) principle.

Recruitment

Participants will be recruited from the 4 centers through hospital posters, WeChat public announcements, and volunteer recommendations. Physicians will first conduct screenings of potential colorectal cancer patients who meet the criteria and determine their eligibility based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Trained research assistants will proactively approach all eligible patients to provide them with detailed information about the study. This will ensure that all patients have an equal opportunity to participate, regardless of their initial level of interest. By doing so, we aim to minimize self-selection bias and enhance the representativeness of our study sample. Written informed consent (refer to S3 File) from individuals willing to participate will be obtained by the research assistants prior to the baseline assessment. Subsequent to this act of authentication, the aforementioned file shall be ensconced within a secure repository, thereby ensuring the confidentiality of the participant's volitional expression.

Randomization, allocation concealment, and blinding

Post-baseline assessment, eligible participants will be randomly allocated to the EA, SA, or ERAS groups in a 2:2:1 ratio. This will be facilitated by the centralized randomization system of the Clinical Research Institute of Medicine School, Shanghai Jiao Tong University, guaranteeing complete allocation concealment. Randomization will be stratified by age (≤ 70 or > 70 years), surgical site (left or right-sided colon), and sub-center. The randomization sequence using the Panther Interactive Web Response System (IWRS) with a permuted block design and block size of 6 will be implemented upon a participant meeting inclusion and exclusion criteria. Clinical Research Institute of Medicine School, Shanghai Jiao Tong University will provide an Electronic Data Capture (EDC) system for research data management, overseen by independent statisticians uninvolved in participant recruitment, assessment, or intervention.

Perioperative care and postoperative opioid

Routine monitoring including electrocardiogram, noninvasive blood pressure measurements, and pulse oximetry every 1–2 h, or more frequently, if necessary, from intraoperative to 24 h postoperatively.

Preparation program: due to the individualization of patients, there may be the need for appropriate intraoperative atropine application to control heart rate, airway secretion, if the critical intraoperative operations such as accompanied by the patient's transient deep breathing, can be a very small dose of atracurium to deal with. If transient respiratory depression occurs in combination with inadequate sedation and analgesia, a small amount of esketamine may be applied.

Postoperative opioid use [30]: a multimodal analgesic program will be implemented, which included intraoperative nerve blocks, local analgesia at the incision site, and analgesic pumps, among other modalities. Patients were given detailed information about the potential benefits and risks of postoperative analgesic pumps and the decision to use self-controlled analgesia in the postoperative period was made in consultation with their healthcare team. The patient-controlled analgesia (PCA) pump formulation was sufentanil 30–60 μg/oliceridine 20 mg + ketorolac tromethamine injection 120 mg/dexmedetomidine 50 μg/oxycodone 20–30 mg in 0.9% saline to 100 ml. Parameters were set: background dose 2 ml/h, PCA 2 ml/dose, and lockout time 15 min. All should be dose-adjusted according to age and renal function.

Intervention

The acupuncturists participating in the study hold a Traditional Chinese Medicine practice license issued by the Ministry of Health of the People’s Republic of China and have more than 3 years of acupuncture experience. Prior to the official commencement of the trial, all acupuncturists will undergo training in standardized operating procedures (refer to S4 File) to thoroughly understand the location of acupoints, as well as the techniques for needle manipulation. During the intervention period, minimal verbal communication will be maintained between the acupuncturists and the participants.

Electroacupuncture group (EA)

Acupoint: bilateral LI4 (Hegu), SJ6 (Zhigou), ST36(Zusanli), and ST37 (Shangjuxu). A map of the acupoints is shown in Fig S2. All acupoints are localized according to the 2021 China National Standard: Nomenclature and location of meridian points (GB/T 12346–2021)(31) (Table 1). The study(32) indicates that Zusanli (ST36) and Shangjuxu (ST37) are frequently used acupoints for treating POGD after colorectal cancer surgery. These two points embody the concept of combined treatment of the internal organs; their synergistic use can regulate the secretion of gastrin, promoting intestinal peristalsis and aiding in the recovery of gastrointestinal function after abdominal surgery. Hegu (LI4) is the source point of the Large Intestine Meridian of Hand Yangming, a key site where the meridian qi passes and converges. It is an important acupoint for the gathering and circulation of qi and blood and a commonly used point in acupuncture anesthesia. It can clear and purge Yangming, calm pain, and alleviate nausea and vomiting. Zhigou (SJ6) is an acupoint of the SanJiao Meridian of Hand Shaoyang, a point where the meridian qi is abundant. It functions in regulating the qi mechanism, reducing adverse qi flow, and facilitating bowel movements. It is an experienced and effective point for treating constipation, recommended by acupuncture experts at Yueyang Hospital. Therefore, we select bilateral LI4, SJ6, ST36, and ST37 as acupuncture points. Sham-electroacupuncture (SA) group

To ensure maximum participant blinding, both the intervention and SA groups will employ adhesive pads. The SA needles, identical in appearance to traditional acupuncture needles but featuring a blunt tip (produced by Suzhou Medical Supplies Co., Ltd., specification 0.25 × 40 mm), will be used. These needles will be inserted and twisted on the adhesive pad, simulating actual acupuncture, yet without penetrating the pad. Furthermore, the SA device will be modified by severing the output wire, maintaining its outward appearance but rendering it incapable of delivering electrical stimulation. This modification ensures that, although the device appears connected, it will not provide any electrical stimulation [34]. Acupoint selection, timing, frequency, duration of needle retention, and overall treatment duration for the sham group will mirror that of the intervention group. Participants will be informed about the possibility of receiving a mild electrical current, which might not be perceptible as a sensation, and no current will be delivered [35]. An example of sham electropuncture is shown in Fig S3.

To prevent interaction among study participants, they will be treated individually. The success of the blinding strategy will be evaluated in two selected hospitals. Participants will be questioned within 5 min post the first and final treatment sessions to ascertain whether they believe they received EA or SA.

Safety measurements

Safety measures will be enforced to minimize potential inadvertent harm from the study. Any unforeseen adverse events (AE) occurring during the intervention period will be closely monitored and reported promptly to a designated research assistant. An in-depth analysis will be undertaken to understand the causal relationship between the EA intervention and any severe adverse events. In the case of serious adverse events, comprehensive reports will be submitted to the overseeing ethics committee, adhering to established ethical guidelines.

Sample size

This study utilizes a comparative design. Previous studies [39, 40] have indicated that the first passage time of flatus post three treatments averages 48 h (SD 21.6) in the electroacupuncture group, 58.9 h (SD 16.8) in the SA group, and 62.4 h (SD 26.4) in the control group. Adopting a bilateral α of 0.01 and a power (1-β) of 0.8, the primary focus is the comparison between the EA and control groups. Using a 2:2:1 ratio for group allocation, sample size calculations performed with PASS15.0 software suggest a requirement of 108 cases each in the EA and SA groups, and 54 in the control group, totaling 270 cases. Given the study’s brief duration, a dropout rate of 10% is anticipated. Therefore, the final sample size was determined to be 300 cases, with 120 in the EA group, 120 in the SA group, and 60 in the control group.

Data management and quality control

Data monitoring committee (DMC) served by the Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine (independent of the sponsor and conflict of interest), ensuring the authenticity, completeness, confidentiality, and traceability of clinical trial data. A case report form (CRF) will be used to collect study-related data. The electronic case report form (eCRF) system records data modifications and requires electronic signatures for data locking after source data verification, data review, and query resolution. The CRIP system is used for data management, with data entry performed using a double-entry method and verified for accuracy. The database adheres to FDA requirements and CDISC standards, ensuring traceability of all system activities. Data verification involves logical checks and prompt responses to inquiries, with the data administrator ensuring all issues are resolved before database locking. The Data Blind Verification Report is completed post-verification. Once verified, the database is locked by the principal researcher, sponsor, statistical analysts, and monitor, with alterations only permitted under specific conditions. The database then undergoes statistical analysis as per the statistical analysis plan, culminating in a statistical analysis report.

Laboratories must strictly follow standard operating procedures (SOPs) and enforce quality control measures. Investigators are subject to a stringent qualification process and must maintain consistent personnel standards. Comprehensive training on the study protocol and SOPs is mandatory for all personnel before initiating a clinical trial. Acupuncturists, with a minimum of 5 years’ experience, undergo standardized training and evaluation prior to performing treatments in the trial. They are solely dedicated to acupuncture, avoiding other treatments such as acupressure, moxibustion, or transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation.

The principal investigator leads a research coordination committee, which includes both the sponsor and principal investigator. Experienced monitors are appointed to oversee the trial. Separate operating and treatment rooms are designated for each patient group to prevent any interaction or communication. A blinded evaluation method is implemented, ensuring that evaluators are unaware of the group assignments.

Laboratory test results should be computer-generated for traceability. The training provided aims to familiarize researchers with the study process and its implementation details. Participant compliance is monitored through treatment counts, with compliance calculated as the ratio of actual treatments received to the total required, expressed as a percentage. An adherence rate of at least 80% is required for successful compliance. Measures to improve compliance include encouraging voluntary participation, obtaining informed consent, and enhancing the medical environment and affordability.

Data analysis

No interim analyses are planned prior to collection of the primary outcomes. The efficacy analysis will be conducted within the full analysis set (FAS), which will include all randomized subjects, adhering to the intention-to-treat (ITT) principle. Sensitivity analysis will be performed on the per-protocol set (PPS) for the primary endpoints to assess the robustness of the study conclusions on the selected population for analysis. The PPS is a subset of the FAS, comprising all randomized subjects without major protocol deviations. The safety analysis set (SS) will consist of actual data from subjects who have received at least one treatment and have safety measure records.

Statistical descriptions will be performed on general information of patients, medical history, treatment history, and other relevant factors. Descriptive statistics will be used for continuous data, including mean, standard deviation, median, minimum, and maximum values. Categorical data will be described using rates or proportions. For normally distributed continuous variables with homogeneity of variance, analysis of variance (ANOVA) with randomization block design will be utilized. Non-normally distributed variables or variables with heterogeneous variances will be compared using the Friedman rank sum test. Baseline comparisons of categorical data, such as gender, ethnicity, medical history, family history, and etiology, between the two groups will be assessed using the chi-square test. All statistical analyses will be conducted using R 4.3.1, with the significance threshold defined as a two-sided P < 0.05.

Subgroup and multivariable analyses

Subgroup analysis will be conducted for different surgical approaches (laparoscopic and open) and age groups (18–64 years and 65–79 years). We will include patients with a history of multiple previous abdominal surgeries or postoperative hyperthermic intraperitoneal chemotherapy (HIPEC). Given the potential variability in postoperative recovery among these patients, we will plan a subgroup analysis to assess any differences in outcomes related to the number of previous surgeries or postoperative HIPEC. Multiple linear regression analysis will be performed for continuous dependent variables. Logistic regression models will be used for analysis of categorical dependent variables, including binary, unordered categorical, and ordered categorical data. All dosage regimens for temporary analgesic pumps will be documented in the CRF. A secondary analysis is anticipated.

Discussion

The CORRECT trial assesses the efficacy of EA in enhancing gastrointestinal recovery following colorectal cancer’s radical resection, and the dynamic information will be updated on the website http://clinic.dev.tiderway.com/. EA may potentially reduce postoperative opioid dosages through synergistic analgesic effects, and it could also be an underlying mechanism responsible for accelerating the recovery of POGD. We shall meticulously document the dosages of postoperative opioids and intend to conduct a secondary analysis on this issue. Needle insertion frequency will be recorded for a dose–response evaluation of acupuncture’s effects. Control group interventions, including sham acupuncture, will serve as comparators to elucidate sham procedures’ placebo effects. Patients in the sham acupuncture group will be informed they are receiving superficial stimulation treatments, deliberately avoiding terms like “sham” or “placebo” to reduce psychological biases. We have referenced a core outcome set (COS) [41] of postoperative ileus outcome measures derived from a Delphi consensus survey [42], along with outcomes used in relevant RCTs over the past 5 years [36]. These encompass postoperative flatus and defecation time, incidence of nausea and vomiting, incisional pain intensity, and complications, among others. Additionally, monitoring of CRP and IL-6 levels will be conducted postoperatively at 1, 12, and 72 h. The outcomes we are observing are sufficiently pertinent to both patients and healthcare professionals and can evaluate the impact of EA on short-term inflammatory markers and postoperative quality of life at 1 month.

Meta-analyses [25, 43] have shown significant improvements in gastrointestinal function recovery with acupuncture over control interventions, including shorter times for recovery of oral food intake, first flatus, and distension, as well as overall surgical recovery. While evidence points to acupuncture’s restorative benefits for gastrointestinal function within ERAS protocols, its integration into global ERAS guidelines is lacking. Previous studies have been limited by size, potential biases, and observation duration, with most interventions applied once daily for 3–6 days. This study’s protocol aims to scrutinize the dose–response relationship of electroacupuncture, potentially informing colorectal cancer postoperative care and hospitalization optimization.

Qi, a cornerstone of traditional Chinese medicine, is often characterized as the vital energy circulating within the body [44]. Post-colorectal cancer surgery, patients typically exhibit severe depletion of qi and blood, with a particular deficiency in positive qi. Qi deficiency impairs gastrointestinal motility, leading to organ dysfunction and weakened spleen and stomach [45, 46]. Traditional Chinese Medicine theory posits that surgical trauma disrupts qi flow, causing imbalances such as gastrointestinal dysfunction [47]. The qi flow throughout the body is of paramount importance for sustaining physiological functions and maintaining the balance of energy within the human system. Additionally, liver qi stagnation in the middle jiao disrupts meridian flow, while blood deficiency deprives organs of nourishment, further exacerbating the loss of qi and blood in a cyclical manner. This results in qi and blood deficiency, stasis, and disorders manifesting as spleen and stomach disharmony and gastrointestinal dysfunction. Furthermore, perioperative fear and anxiety can inhibit liver function and disrupt bile flow, worsening spleen deficiency. Prolonged bed rest and fasting can weaken qi and blood circulation, leading to issues that affect various organs. In essence, the pathogenesis of POGD involves both qi and blood deficiencies and stagnation. Thus, treatment aims to support, replenish deficiencies, and regulate qi. Thus, the treatment’s primary aim is to regulate visceral qi flow and facilitate the smooth functioning of internal organs [48]. The biological mechanisms by which acupuncture influences gastrointestinal function are complex and under study, with current theories suggesting that acupoint stimulation activates a chain of sensory signals through the central nervous system. EA initiates a series of responses through various signaling pathways, including neuroendocrine and neuroimmune systems. These interconnected mechanisms significantly affect gastrointestinal functions by coordinating the nervous, endocrine, and digestive systems’ interactions [49].

A thorough review of clinical literature and expert consultation has identified key acupoints for treating POGD, including LI4, SJ6, ST36, and ST37(50). LI4 (Hegu), situated on the Large Intestine Meridian, is crucial for qi and blood convergence and circulation. Acupuncture at Hegu can elevate qi and promote blood circulation. ST36 (Zusanli), on the Stomach Meridian, is essential for spleen and stomach regulation, harmonizing stomach descent and nourishing primordial qi. It harmonizes the stomach, strengthens the spleen, regulates qi, and descends rebellious qi while resolving phlegm within the organs. ST37 (Shangjuxu) is associated with visceral sensitivity reduction and pain perception in internal organs, resolving stagnation, clearing heat, and relieving dampness, making it a common point for treating large intestine diseases. The combined use of ST36 and ST37 is shown to balance the TLR4/NF-kB and MAPK signaling pathways [51, 52] and the cholinergic anti-inflammatory pathway, providing a comprehensive immune and inflammatory modulation. SJ6 (Zhigou), a point on the Sanjiao meridian, is known for its full meridian qi and its ability to regulate qi and alleviate constipation. Collectively, these four acupoints enhance qi transportation and stimulate intestinal peristalsis, tonifying the spleen and stomach.

EA intervention details such as needle depth, waveform frequency, twisting techniques, and needle retention duration were determined based on prior studies and clinical expertise. Low-frequency continuous wave therapy is favored for its prolonged analgesic effect through the release of met-enkephalin (M-ENK) and β-endorphin (β-EP) [53]. It is observed that the analgesic effect resulting from low-frequency stimulation tends to have a prolonged duration compared to the stimulation duration itself. Conversely, the analgesic effect elicited by high-frequency stimulation diminishes shortly after the cessation of stimulation [54, 55].

The inherent nature of acupuncture complicates the application of a double-blind method. To overcome this, sham acupuncture without skin puncture is used as a control to blind participants. Some studies [56, 57] suggest that sham acupuncture may have therapeutic effects. Thus, an ERAS care group will also serve as a negative control to provide a basis for comparison and minimize bias [58]. A comprehensive approach has been taken for this study, including a larger sample size, a broader range of surgical types, increased intervention frequency, and subgroup analysis for gender, age, and surgical type distinctions. Non-penetrating needling is utilized for the sham acupuncture group to induce a minor, nonspecific effect. In response to the current blinding challenges of acupuncture studies, this protocol will explore non-invasive sham acupuncture methods and techniques, which will be evaluated for their interpretability in relation to efficiency. We anticipate that our research will underpin the standardization of acupuncture interventions, contribute to compelling evidence, and promote management for patients with POGD.

Strengths and limitations

The CORRECT trial adopts a sham and conventional intervention control to discern placebo and actual EA effects in multicenter. While non-puncturing sham acupuncture has a minimal placebo effect, its sole use as a control may have underestimated the efficacy of EA. The study will also identify optimal timings and intervention durations for EA to enhance treatment outcomes. Nevertheless, the protocol has limitations, such as the lack of long-term POGD outcome data and challenges in blinding researchers. Considering ethical constraints, clinical practicality, and the extrapolation of research conclusions findings, we refrain from imposing stringent restrictions on postoperative opioid use, acknowledging the potential for bias. We intend to conduct a post hoc analysis to investigate the influence of postoperative opioid administration on the expedited recovery of gastrointestinal function after colorectal surgery. Additionally, acupuncturist proficiency, despite SOP training, could introduce variability in treatment effectiveness.

Conclusion

EA shows promise in promoting gastrointestinal recovery postoperatively, but the current evidence is not compelling. The CORRECT trial will rigorously verify EA’s efficacy in POGD for colorectal cancer patients with multicenter, large-sample data. We will enhance transparency, repeatability, and high quality in the trial progress to generate more convincing data. The CORRECT trial may reinforce the evidence of EA in reducing POGD post-CRC surgery, which may support the early prevention and effective treatment strategies for POGD.
