Association between the ACTN3 R577X polymorphism and skeletal muscle mass: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Sakura Hattori, Mika Kamiya-Saito, Naoki Kikuchi, Koichi Nakazato
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe ACTN3 R577X polymorphism is one of the genetic factors influencing skeletal muscle mass. Although several studies have reported that the X allele of the ACTN3 R577X polymorphism is associated with reduced muscle mass, others have found no significant relationship between genotype and muscle mass. These inconsistencies may be explained by differences in background characteristics of the study populations, such as sex, age, ethnicity, and measurement site. This systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to clarify the association between the ACTN3 R577X polymorphism and skeletal muscle mass. In addition, we investigated whether the effect of the ACTN3 gene is modified by population background.Main bodyA total of 34 cohorts (9,496 participants) derived from 24 studies were included in the meta-analysis. In the overall analysis under the dominant model for the X allele (RR vs. RX + XX), the ACTN3 genotype showed a significant effect on skeletal muscle mass (SMD [95% CI] = − 0.08 [− 0.15 to − 0.02], p = 0.014), with the RX + XX group exhibiting lower skeletal muscle mass compared with the RR group. In contrast, the recessive model (RR + RX vs. XX) did not yield a statistically significant effect (SMD [95% CI] = − 0.02 [− 0.12 to 0.07], p = 0.616). Under the additive model, a trend toward an effect was observed (per-allele; β [95% CI] = − 0.05 [− 0.10 to 0.01], p = 0.078). Moderate heterogeneity was observed across all models (dominant: I2 = 38.8%; recessive: I2 = 55.8%; additive: I2 = 52.5%). No significant subgroup effects were observed for sex, age, or ethnicity. Meta-regression for measurement site indicated that the association became more pronounced as lower-limb specificity increased, although residual heterogeneity remained.ConclusionThe ACTN3 R577X polymorphism showed a small association with skeletal muscle mass under the X-allele dominant model, suggesting that this association may be detectable even in heterozygous individuals. Although measurement site may modulate this association, none of the examined factors substantially explained the observed heterogeneity when considered individually. The remaining variability may reflect combinations of background characteristics that were not fully captured by single-factor analyses. Further studies accounting for background characteristics are warranted to clarify the genetic contribution of ACTN3 R577X to skeletal muscle mass.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s40101-026-00439-5.Keywords: ACTN3, α-actinin-3, ACTN3 R577X polymorphism, Skeletal muscle mass, Sarcopenia

Introduction

Sarcopenia is recognized not merely as a decline in musculoskeletal function but as a risk factor for various diseases [1]. Skeletal muscle serves multiple critical physiological roles beyond mechanical support, functioning as a glucose metabolic tissue [1, 2], an endocrine organ [3], an energy reservoir [4, 5], and a site for drug metabolism [6–8]. Therefore, maintaining adequate skeletal muscle mass is essential for preventing age-related sarcopenia and frailty, reducing the risk of lifestyle-related diseases, and improving clinical outcomes across diverse medical conditions.

The importance of genetic determinants in individual differences in skeletal muscle mass and sarcopenia has been increasingly recognized in recent years. Twin and family studies have reported that heritability estimates for muscle mass–related traits, including lean body mass, limb circumference, and cross-sectional area, range from approximately 56% to over 80%, depending on adjustment factors [9–13]. Elucidating the mechanisms underlying this diversity in muscle mass may enable personalized risk management and the prevention of sarcopenia. Despite the identification of numerous associated genes [14], the full landscape of genetic factors determining skeletal muscle mass remains unclear.

The ACTN3 rs1815739 polymorphism is one of the genetic factors suggested to be associated with skeletal muscle mass. This variant leads to the replacement of an arginine residue with a premature stop codon at position 577 (R577X), a nonsense allele. ACTN3 knockout (KO) mice, which model the R577X, have demonstrated that deficiency of α-actinin-3, encoded by the ACTN3 gene, reduces skeletal muscle mass [15–18]. Although these animal studies consistently support the association between ACTN3 genotype and muscle mass, findings in humans remain inconsistent. Zempo et al. (2010) reported that RR + RX genotype exhibited greater muscle mass than the XX genotype [19], whereas a large cohort study by Delmonico et al. (2008) found no significant genotype-related differences [20].

These inconsistencies may be attributable not only to limitations in statistical power due to small sample sizes, but also to differences in population characteristics, such as age [21, 22] and sex [21, 23, 24], and in assessment methods, including the measurement site from which skeletal muscle mass was evaluated [25, 26]. In addition, given that the ACTN3 polymorphism has been suggested to have undergone evolutionary selection pressures [15, 27, 28], it is plausible that differences in genetic background across populations may further modify the phenotypic effects of this variant. Thus, to elucidate the physiological significance of the ACTN3 polymorphism, it is necessary to adopt a multifaceted perspective that considers not only the factors previously reported in the literature but also population-specific genetic contexts that may influence its effects.

Accordingly, the present study aimed to systematically evaluate the association between the ACTN3 R577X polymorphism and skeletal muscle mass and to gain insights into factors that may modulate this association by conducting a meta-analysis along with subgroup analyses based on potential effect modifiers.

Method

Eligibility criteria

Only studies that investigated the association between the ACTN3 R577X polymorphism and skeletal muscle mass were considered eligible for inclusion. Specifically, studies were included if they (1) examined the association between the ACTN3 R577X polymorphism and skeletal muscle mass; (2) enrolled human participants, with no restrictions on age, sex, physical activity level, or athletic status; (3) reported quantitative measures of skeletal muscle mass, such as lean body mass (LBM), fat-free mass (FFM), muscle cross-sectional area (CSA), or muscle volume; and (4) were published as full-text articles in the English language.

Studies were excluded if they (1) were review articles, meta-analyses, animal studies, or in vitro experiments; (2) did not provide separate data for the ACTN3 RX genotype in addition to the RR and XX genotypes; (3) lacked sufficient statistical data to calculate effect sizes (i.e., mean and standard deviation of skeletal muscle mass for each ACTN3 R577X genotype); or (4) contained participant data that overlapped with a previously included publication.

Search strategy

The PubMed, Web of Science, and SPORTDiscus databases were searched up to November 2025 using the following search string: (ACTN3 OR actinin3 OR “alpha actinin3”) AND (“muscle mass” OR “lean body mass” OR “muscle volume” OR “cross sectional area” OR “Muscular Atrophy” OR “Sarcopenia”).

Study records

All search results were exported to Rayyan [29] and Microsoft Excel (Microsoft Corp.) for recordkeeping and data management. The Excel spreadsheets were used to document the search records and organize the extracted data. Full-text articles and associated datasets were stored in a shared Dropbox folder, which was accessible to all review authors.

Two authors independently screened the titles and abstracts of all retrieved records to identify potentially eligible studies. The full texts of the remaining articles were subsequently assessed for eligibility. Any disagreements between reviewers were resolved through discussion. In addition, further relevant studies were identified by screening the reference lists of the included articles. Reasons for study exclusion at the full-text stage were systematically documented.

Data were independently extracted by two authors using a standardized extraction form. The following information was collected from each study: PubMed ID, article title, authors, journal name, year of publication, skeletal muscle mass data (mean, standard deviation [SD] or standard error [SE], and sample size for each genotype), participant characteristics (sex, age, country, height, weight), phenotype information, and methodological details. For studies involving training interventions, skeletal muscle mass data at baseline before the intervention were extracted. When necessary, the corresponding study authors were contacted to obtain summary statistics required for the meta-analysis.

Quality assessment

The methodological quality and risk of bias of the included studies were assessed using the Risk of Bias Assessment Tool for Non-randomized Studies, version 2 (RoBANS-2) [30], which is specifically designed to evaluate cross-sectional studies. Each study included in the meta-analysis was independently assessed for risk of bias by two reviewers. The assessment was performed separately for each participant group analyzed in the meta-analysis, and any disagreements were resolved through discussion. An overall risk-of-bias judgment for each study was determined according to the following predefined rules: a study was judged to have a high risk of bias if at least one domain was rated as High; a low risk of bias if all domains were rated as Low or if only one domain was rated as unclear; and an unclear risk of bias in all other cases.

Potential publication bias was evaluated by visually inspecting funnel plots and statistically tested using Egger’s regression test [31].

Statistical analysis

Genotype effects were evaluated under three commonly used genetic models: dominant, recessive, and additive for the X allele. In the dominant model (RR vs RX + XX) and the recessive model (RR + RX vs XX), genotype groups were first collapsed according to the assumed inheritance pattern, and the standardized mean difference (SMD) was then calculated based on the resulting two-group comparison. Hedges’ g was used as the SMD and was calculated from the mean, standard deviation, and sample size of each group to account for potential small-sample bias in individual studies [30]. In the additive model, genotypes were coded as 0 (RR), 1 (RX), and 2 (XX), and the per-allele effect size was calculated as the regression coefficient (β). All effect sizes were oriented so that more negative values indicated lower skeletal muscle mass in association with the X allele.

A random-effects model was used to synthesize the data, as heterogeneity among studies was anticipated. The Knapp-Hartung adjustment was applied to calculate the 95% CI for the pooled effect size, as this method provides a more robust estimate in the presence of heterogeneity [32]. Statistical heterogeneity was evaluated with Cochran’s Q test and quantified using the I2 statistic. Following the recommendations of Higgins et al. (2002), I2 values of 25%, 50%, and 75% were interpreted as representing low, moderate, and high levels of heterogeneity, respectively [33].

Furthermore, to assess the robustness of the pooled results, a leave-one-out sensitivity analysis was performed. This involved iteratively removing one study at a time and recalculating the overall effect size to ensure that the results were not unduly influenced by any single cohort.

To explore potential sources of heterogeneity, we conducted subgroup analyses based on pre-specified study characteristics: sex, age, ethnicity, and measurement site. The age variable was dichotomized into two categories, defined as young (< 60 years old) and old (≥ 60 years old). The ethnicity was determined for each study based on participant information and the country where the experiment was conducted. The statistical significance of differences between subgroups was assessed using Cochran’s Q test, and the degree of heterogeneity was quantified using the I2 statistic [34]. For each subgroup analysis, datasets with missing data for the subgroup-defining variables were excluded on an analysis-specific basis.

To examine whether the effect size under the additive model varied according to measurement site, measurement-site categories were coded as an ordinal moderator reflecting increasing lower-limb specificity. Whole-body, four-limb, and lower-limb categories were assigned values of 0, 1, and 2, respectively. A meta-regression analysis was then performed with this ordinal variable entered as a continuous moderator. The model was fitted using restricted maximum likelihood estimation, and statistical inference for the moderator was based on the Knapp–Hartung adjustment. The proportion of heterogeneity explained by the moderator was expressed as R2*.

All analyses were performed using R (version 4.5.1) with the meta package (version 8.2.0) and metafor package (version 4.8.0). A two-sided p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all analyses.

Result

Study selection

The study selection process is summarized in Fig. 1. Our initial database search yielded 187 potential articles. After removing 57 duplicates, 130 articles were screened. After excluding 81 records based on title and abstract review, we assessed 49 articles at the full-text level. In addition to the 19 studies identified from the primary search, five more were included through a reference search. Ultimately, 24 studies satisfied the eligibility criteria, from which 34 independent datasets were extracted and included in the meta-analysis.

Publication bias and risk-of-bias assessment

The results of the risk-of-bias assessment are summarized as a traffic-light plot in Additional file 1. Overall, 13 analyses were judged to have a high risk of bias, 14 were judged as low risk, and 7 were unclear. The primary domains contributing to a High risk of bias were Confounders (D3), followed by Comparability of the target group (D1), Blinding of assessors (D5), Outcome assessment (D6), and Incomplete outcome data (D7). The risk of bias summary was presented with consideration of study weights from the random-effects model used in the meta-analysis (Additional file 1). Using this weighting approach, studies with a high risk of bias accounted for approximately 25% of the evidence across the dominant, recessive, and additive models.

Funnel plots for all genotype models are shown in Additional file 2. Egger’s test indicated no significant publication bias in any of the genotype models (dominant: p = 0.174, recessive: p = 0.910, additive: p = 0.290).

Overall effect of the genotype

Under the X allele dominant model, a statistically significant association between the ACTN3 genotype and skeletal muscle mass was observed, with the combined RX + XX genotype group exhibiting lower skeletal muscle mass compared with the RR genotype group (Fig. 2; SMD = − 0.08 [95% CI − 0.15 to − 0.02], p = 0.014). In contrast, the pooled analysis under the recessive model did not show a significant association between ACTN3 genotype and skeletal muscle mass (Fig. 3; SMD = − 0.02 [95% CI − 0.12 to 0.06], p = 0.616). Under the additive model, there was a trend toward an association (Fig. 4; β = − 0.05 [95% CI − 0.10 to 0.00], p = 0.078). All model demonstrated moderate heterogeneity (dominant: I2 38.8%; Cochran’s Q p = 0.014, recessive: I2 55.8%; Cochran’s Q p < 0.001, additive: I2 52.5%; Cochran’s Q p < 0.001). Leave-one-out sensitivity analyses indicated that the results were robust, with detailed results presented in Additional file 3.

Because the measurement-site categories could be interpreted as reflecting increasing lower-limb specificity, whole body, four limbs, and lower limbs were coded as an ordinal variable with values of 0, 1, and 2, respectively. To examine whether differences in effect size across measurement sites followed an ordinal trend, we performed a meta-regression analysis using this ordinal variable. The meta-regression analysis showed a negative association between the ordinal measurement-site variable and effect size (Fig. 5; β = − 0.072 [95% CI − 0.140 to − 0.004], p = 0.039), indicating that the X allele was more strongly associated with lower muscle mass as the measurement site became more specific to the lower limbs. However, this linear trend did not explain the overall heterogeneity (R2*, amount of heterogeneity accounted for = 0.00%). Therefore, we visually explored whether the trend across measurement sites coincided with other study-level characteristics, such as ethnicity and age group. Visual inspection of the plot suggested that some datasets involving EAS populations, older participants, and lower-limb measurements showed more negative effect sizes, as indicated by the orange circles (Fig. 5).

Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis investigated the association between the ACTN3 R577X polymorphism and skeletal muscle mass. The overall effect on the dominant model was statistically significant, with the combined RX + XX genotypes exhibiting lower skeletal muscle mass compared with the RR genotype. Because moderate heterogeneity was observed across all genetic models, we examined whether the effects of the ACTN3 polymorphism differed according to population characteristics and assessment methods. As a result, a trend toward a significant subgroup effect was observed among measurement-site categories. Meta-regression further suggested that the association of the X allele with lower skeletal muscle mass became more pronounced with increasing lower-limb specificity.

The findings of a significant association under the dominant model imply that even the heterozygous (RX) state may exhibit partial functional deficiency. Biologically, the ACTN3 R577X polymorphism is a nonsense variant; individuals with the XX genotype lack α-actinin-3, whereas those with the RX genotype express it. Therefore, this variant is predicted to follow a recessive pattern. However, the observed data suggests that the functional impact may not be strictly recessive, indicating an intermediate phenotype for heterozygotes. Supporting this notion, studies using Actn3 knockout mice suggest that the heterozygous genotype is associated with reduced muscle mass, accompanied by a significant reduction in muscle fiber diameter [57]. In humans, several studies have reported that the effects of ACTN3 genotype may be additive. For example, a study comparing 40-m sprint time showed that the association between ACTN3 genotype and sprint performance was consistent with an additive model [58]. In addition, a meta-analysis examining the association between ACTN3 gene polymorphism and flexibility reported a significant association only under the recessive model [59]. In present study, while the dominant model yielded significant results, the additive model showed only a trend. This pattern may indicate that the additive trend was partly driven by the RX genotype showing a phenotype closer to the XX genotype than to the RR genotype.

One possible mechanism through which the ACTN3 R577X polymorphism may influence skeletal muscle mass is an increased vulnerability of muscle fiber structure resulting from reduced expression of α-actinin-3. Loss of α-actinin-3 due to the R577X variant is accompanied by compensatory upregulation of α-actinin-2, thereby preserving the total amount of actinin proteins [60]. However, this compensation appears insufficient to fully maintain structural integrity, potentially leading to reduced mechanical stability of muscle fibers. Consistent with this notion, studies in Actn3 knockout mice have demonstrated greater muscle damage following eccentric contractions [60, 61]. Moreover, even under resting conditions, increased expression of Z-disk–associated proteins and the presence of centrally located nuclei have been observed, suggesting the presence of chronic muscle stress and ongoing remodeling processes [60]. Such persistent structural stress may impair satellite cell regenerative capacity through repeated cycles of muscle damage and regeneration, as has been reported in Duchenne muscular dystrophy, ultimately compromising the maintenance of muscle mass [62]. Therefore, sustained structural fragility resulting from the absence or reduction of α-actinin-3 may promote chronic muscle fiber damage and contribute to decreased skeletal muscle mass via impaired satellite cell–mediated regeneration. In addition, increased expression of Z-disk–related proteins, such as desmin and ZASP, has also been reported in heterozygous mice carrying the RX genotype [57]. This observation is consistent with the present findings, suggesting that the effects of the ACTN3 polymorphism may already be evident in individuals carrying a single X allele.

Because previous studies have suggested that the effects of the ACTN3 R577X polymorphism may differ between sexes [21, 23, 24], we examined the influence of genotype on skeletal muscle mass in male and female participants using subgroup analysis. However, when the available studies were quantitatively synthesized, no clear sex-specific difference in the genotype effect was observed. A recent study using skeletal muscle from Actn3 knockout mice and human muscle biopsy samples reported that androgen receptor protein levels were lower in both Actn3 knockout mice and individuals with the XX genotype, irrespective of sex [63]. In contrast, studies using androgen receptor knockout mice have shown that the reduction in skeletal muscle mass caused by androgen receptor deficiency is evident in males, but not in females [64, 65]. These findings suggest that the effect mediated by reduced androgen receptor signaling may predominantly operate in males. In females, however, skeletal muscle mass may be more strongly influenced by female sex hormones, including estrogen. Notably, α-actinin-2 has been reported to interact not only with androgen receptor activity but also with estrogen receptor activity [66], suggesting that altered estrogen signaling may also contribute to reduced skeletal muscle mass. Therefore, the absence of a detectable sex difference in the present subgroup analysis may reflect distinct but convergent interactions between ACTN3 genotype and sex hormone-related pathways in males and females. Further studies are needed to clarify these interactions, particularly the potential involvement of estrogen signaling.

In the subgroup analysis stratified by measurement site, a trend toward a significant subgroup effect was observed under the additive model. When we further examined whether the effect size varied linearly according to increasing lower-limb specificity, the model showed a significant linear fit. Specifically, the association of the X allele with reduced muscle mass became stronger as the measurement site became more specific to the lower limbs, suggesting that the effect of the X allele may be more pronounced in lower-limb measurements. This site-specific tendency may be related to impaired remodeling following the structural damage described above. Lower-limb muscles are repeatedly exposed to weight-bearing mechanical loads and eccentric contractions during daily activities [67, 68]. Therefore, reduced tolerance to mechanical stress caused by α-actinin-3 deficiency may be more likely to manifest over time as differences in damage repair and remodeling responses in lower-limb muscles. Although muscle mass is one of the major determinants of muscle strength, a meta-analysis examining the effect of ACTN3 R577X on muscle strength did not show the lower-limb-specific effect observed in the present study [69]. One possible explanation for this discrepancy is that variation in the effect on muscle mass according to measurement site may not have been large enough to be detected as a clear site-specific difference in muscle strength, which is a complex phenotype. Muscle strength is determined not only by muscle mass but also by multiple factors, including muscle fiber-type composition, contractile properties, and neural control. ACTN3 R577X, through α-actinin-3 deficiency, has been reported to reduce the proportion of Type IIx fibers, which generate high tension per unit cross-sectional area [24, 45]. Thus, changes in muscle fiber composition and muscle quality may have obscured the influence of site-specific variation in muscle mass when muscle strength was assessed as the outcome.

The linear effect across measurement sites did not explain the overall heterogeneity, and the other subgroup factors also did not show detectable subgroup effects when examined individually. These findings suggest that heterogeneity may reflect unexamined factors or the combined influence of multiple factors. Although the limited number of available datasets prevented us from statistically examining how combinations of multiple factors influence variability in the effect of ACTN3 R577X (Fig. 5), which visualizes the study-level categories, suggests that the X allele may be associated with lower lower-limb muscle mass particularly in older East Asian populations. Further studies including diverse background populations are therefore warranted to clarify the influence of such combined factors. In particular, data on lower-limb muscle mass in young East Asian populations remain especially limited, and additional evidence from this group is needed to determine whether the apparent effect of the X allele on lower muscle mass is specific to older East Asian individuals.

In the present meta-analysis, the overall effect sizes were small, indicating that the contribution of the ACTN3 genotype alone to skeletal muscle mass is limited and unlikely to represent a pathological phenotype associated with a substantially increased risk of sarcopenia. From an alternative perspective, the modestly lower muscle mass associated with the X allele may reflect a potentially rational and adaptive phenotype. Skeletal muscle is a primary site of energy expenditure during physical activity and is considered a metabolically costly tissue [70, 71]. Therefore, a slight reduction in muscle mass that does not impair activities of daily living could theoretically enhance whole-body energy efficiency and confer an advantage for survival. In line with this notion, a study of Spanish centenarians reported a potential contribution of the X allele to longevity [72]. Such an interpretation may provide a plausible evolutionary framework for understanding the signatures of selection at the ACTN3 locus. Energy conservation is a critical factor for survival and reproduction. Previous studies have suggested that the X allele-associated shift toward a more aerobic muscle metabolism improves energetic efficiency and may have been advantageous during human evolution [15]. The modest reduction in muscle mass observed in the present analysis may be interpreted within a similar evolutionary context. Nevertheless, further research is required to clarify the contribution of the X allele to natural selection.

Limitation

The number of available studies was limited, with data obtained from a total of 34 cohorts. We were unable to examine subgroup effects related to athlete status, sport participation, exercise habits, or physical activity levels because sufficient data for these factors could not be obtained. In particular, we did not identify any datasets that reported both skeletal muscle mass and ACTN3 R577X genotype in athletes. Evaluating the potential modifying effects of physical activity levels, athlete status, and training history on the association between ACTN3 R577X and skeletal muscle mass may be important because previous studies have suggested that individuals with the XX genotype may show attenuated training-induced muscle adaptations, potentially through reduced activation of hypertrophy-related pathways such as mTORC1 signaling [73, 74]. In the present study, only baseline skeletal muscle mass was extracted from intervention studies; therefore, genotype-related differences that may emerge after training or in highly trained populations could not be captured.

Furthermore, multiple cohorts were extracted from the same studies. Although we confirmed in the Methods section that there was no overlap of participants, data derived from the same study inevitably share the same study design and data collection methods. Thus, the cohorts cannot be regarded as completely independent, and this may have influenced the pooled estimates. Finally, different measurement methods for skeletal muscle mass were included. The present meta-analysis incorporated data assessed by fat-free mass, muscle volume, and cross-sectional area. To integrate these heterogeneous measures, standardized mean differences were calculated based on the standard deviation of each dataset. Therefore, the results should be interpreted with caution, because the effect size reflects relative differences standardized by variability among studies, rather than absolute differences in skeletal muscle mass.

Conclusion

This meta-analysis suggests that the ACTN3 R577X polymorphism is associated with skeletal muscle mass and that this effect may be detectable even in heterozygous individuals. However, the magnitude of this association was very small. Differences in measurement sites were suggested to modulate the effect of ACTN3 R577X on skeletal muscle mass; however, measurement site alone did not substantially explain the observed heterogeneity. Similarly, none of the other factors examined in the present study, including sex, age, and ethnicity, substantially explained the variability in the effect of ACTN3 R577X when assessed as a single factor. The remaining heterogeneity may reflect the combined influence of multiple background characteristics or factors not examined in the present study. Therefore, future studies should aim to collect data from diverse populations while carefully considering differences in background characteristics.
