Biomechanical evaluation of the gravity varus test in a cadaveric model of posterolateral rotatory instability of the elbow
Authors: Jordan A. Bauer, Rohan Rajan, Rajendra Singh, Martinus Megalla, Zachary Grace, Alexander Maindon, Michael Megafu, Conrad Yu, Andrew E. Caputo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPosterolateral rotatory instability of the elbow is an uncommon sequela of elbow dislocation, most often resulting from incompetence of the lateral collateral ligament complex (LCLC). Diagnosis is challenging because symptoms are subtle and examination findings unreliable, and the pivot shift test is painful and poorly tolerated in awake patients. The gravity varus test is a passive maneuver in which the weight of the forearm generates the varus moment, but the joint motion it produces, and the influence of forearm rotation on that motion, have not been quantified. The purpose of this study was to quantify the kinematic effects of the gravity varus test in cadaveric elbows and to determine whether supination produces greater ulnar external rotation than pronation under conditions of LCLC insufficiency.MethodsTwelve fresh-frozen cadaveric elbows (mean age, 71 years; range, 40-91 years) were mounted with the epicondylar axis perpendicular to the ground. A 3-Space Fastrak electromagnetic tracking system recorded humeral, ulnar, and radial motion. Each specimen was tested through 5 arcs of flexion-extension in full supination and in full pronation. The LCLC was then released to create posterolateral rotatory instability, and the protocol was repeated. Kinematic data were analyzed in 5° increments. Ulnar external rotation was compared between the intact and sectioned states, and between forearm positions, using paired t-tests on the within-specimen differences with Holm–Bonferroni correction across the 29 flexion angles; the 40°-60° arc of flexion was designated the primary comparison on clinical grounds.ResultsAcross the 40°-60° arc, sectioning increased ulnar external rotation in supination by 6.36° (95% confidence interval [CI], 5.72-7.01; P < .001), peaking at 6.74° ± 1.20° at 45° of flexion, while no change occurred in pronation (−0.26°; 95% CI, −1.04 to 0.52; P = .48). In the sectioned state, supination exceeded pronation by 8.65° across the arc (95% CI, 7.11-10.19; P < .001). The increase was significant at every angle in supination and at none between 5° and 115° in pronation. In intact elbows, supination exceeded pronation by 1.92° (95% CI, 1.40-2.45).ConclusionIn this cadaveric model, sectioning of the LCLC increased ulnar external rotation during the gravity varus test with the forearm supinated but not pronated, and the increase was greatest within the 40°-60° arc in which apprehension is reported clinically. These findings support the biomechanical plausibility of the test and confirm that pronation stabilizes the lateral elbow.

Posterolateral rotatory instability (PLRI) of the elbow is characterized by symptomatic subluxation of the ulnohumeral and radiocapitellar joints, most commonly resulting from insufficiency of the lateral ulnar collateral ligament (LUCL) and associated structures within the lateral collateral ligament complex (LCLC).1,8,9 The LCLC, comprising the LUCL, radial collateral ligament, and annular ligament, is essential for resisting varus and rotatory forces and maintaining joint integrity and congruity.3,7 The pathophysiology of PLRI involves disruption of these capsuloligamentous stabilizers, particularly the LUCL, leading to abnormal posterolateral rotatory translation and instability during axial loading and supination.5

Clinically, PLRI presents with pain, mechanical symptoms such as clicking or locking, and functional instability, typically following trauma, iatrogenic injury, or repetitive stress; diagnosis remains challenging due to the subtlety of symptoms and the limitations of imaging modalities, requiring reliance on physical examination techniques that vary in sensitivity and specificity and may require anesthesia for optimal performance.13 The lateral pivot-shift test, first described by O'Driscoll et al14,15, is considered the traditional physical examination technique to evaluate PLRI. The test is performed by supinating the forearm and applying combined axial compression and a valgus moment while the elbow is brought from a flexed position into extension. In this extended position, the radiocapitellar joint subluxes posteriorly and produces a characteristic “clunk” as the joint reduces during flexion, most often observed near 40° of elbow flexion.14,15 However, the pivot-shift test is frequently painful and poorly tolerated in the outpatient setting, often resulting in false-negative results and underdiagnosis of PLRI in clinical practice.14,15 Several modified tests, such as the chair-push-up, tabletop relocation, and posterolateral drawer tests, have been proposed to improve diagnostic accuracy and patient comfort, but these techniques also rely on active patient participation or induce discomfort due to axial or valgus loading of the joint.12,21

The gravity varus test was designed to overcome these limitations by employing passive varus torque generated by gravity, eliminating the need for axial or valgus compression.10 With the shoulder flexed to 90° and internally rotated, the elbow is extended in full supination while unsupported, allowing the weight of the forearm to create varus stress. Apprehension or palpable lateral gapping between 40° and 60° of flexion has been described as suggestive of PLRI.10,16,22 The clinical description of the maneuver rests on a limited evidence base. From a conceptual standpoint, this maneuver is thought to recreate the rotational instability pattern observed during the lateral pivot shift test in a more passive, less painful manner.10

Kwak et al10 examined the LCLC under gravity varus loading in a cadaveric model, releasing the complex sequentially in thirds and measuring ulnohumeral articular contact pressure with intra-articular thin-film sensors. That study established that loss of the anterior two-thirds or more of the complex significantly increases mean contact pressure on the coronoid and shifts load from the lateral to the medial coronoid, and it demonstrated that the LCLC is a principal restraint against gravitational varus torque. Two features of that model define what remains unknown. First, the outcome measure was articular contact pressure rather than joint kinematics, so the rotational displacement that an examiner is presumed to detect during the gravity varus test was not quantified. Second, the forearm was held in pronation throughout testing; the authors identified fixed forearm position as a constraint of their model and explicitly called for further study of the effect of forearm rotation on elbow stability during progressive LCLC disruption.10 That gap is directly relevant to clinical interpretation of the test, because forearm rotation is the element the examiner actively manipulates and because the maneuver is described as being performed in supination. The present study addresses it by measuring ulnar external rotation—a kinematic rather than a pressure outcome—in both full supination and full pronation, across the full flexion arc, before and after release of the LCLC.

Quantifying how ulnar external rotation varies with forearm rotation and with the integrity of the LCLC is a necessary first step toward understanding what the gravity varus test detects. We hypothesized that, after sectioning of the LCLC, ulnar external rotation during the gravity varus test would be significantly greater in full supination than in full pronation, and that this difference would be maximal between 40° and 60° of flexion.

Methods

Specimen characteristics and preparation

Twelve fresh-frozen cadaveric upper extremities were studied (mean age 71 years, range 40-91 years), obtained from 6 male and 6 female donors. Specimens were excluded if there was a history of trauma, deformity, or prior surgery about the elbow, as determined by review of the donor records. Screening radiographs were not obtained, and bone mineral density was not assessed. Specimens were thawed overnight and dissected free of soft tissue proximal to the mid-humerus and distal to the mid-forearm. The humerus was secured proximally in an acrylic mounting frame with the epicondylar axis oriented perpendicular to the ground to replicate the position used in the clinical gravity varus test (Fig. 1). The radius and ulna were left free to move through flexion and extension under the influence of gravity.Figure 1Securing of the humerus to the mounting frame used to reproduce the gravity varus test position, with the epicondylar axis perpendicular to the ground and the forearm unsupported.

Securing of the humerus to the mounting frame used to reproduce the gravity varus test position, with the epicondylar axis perpendicular to the ground and the forearm unsupported.

Surgical exposure and lateral collateral ligament complex transection

A standard Kocher approach to the lateral elbow was performed in all specimens (Fig. 2). A longitudinal skin incision was made centered over the lateral epicondyle. The interval between the anconeus and extensor carpi ulnaris was bluntly developed to expose the capsule and LCLC. Care was taken to preserve the common extensor origin and annular ligament during initial exposure. For baseline (intact) testing, the capsule and ligamentous structures were left undisturbed, and the interval was temporarily closed with layered sutures to restore soft tissue tension.Figure 2Lateral exposure and visualization of the LCLC of the elbow through the Kocher interval. LCLC, lateral collateral ligament complex.

Lateral exposure and visualization of the LCLC of the elbow through the Kocher interval. LCLC, lateral collateral ligament complex.

To create the PLRI condition following baseline measurement, the LCLC—comprising the LUCL and the radial collateral ligament—was sharply released as a single sleeve from its origin on the lateral epicondyle. The annular ligament was left intact, the common extensor origin was preserved, and the lateral joint capsule was left in continuity. No sequential or partial sectioning was performed; every specimen underwent the same complete release, producing a uniform acute injury model. The joint capsule was then closed with interrupted non-absorbable sutures to maintain consistent joint volume and prevent confounding gross instability during subsequent testing.

Instrumentation and testing protocol

A 3-Space Fastrak electromagnetic motion tracking system (Polhemus, Colchester, VT, USA) was used to record 3-dimensional orientation data of the humerus, radius, and ulna. Sensors were affixed rigidly to each bone segment via bicortical 2.4-mm threaded pins and secured with epoxy adhesive to prevent motion artifact (Fig. 3). A 3-Space stylus was used to digitize anatomic landmarks, including the medial and lateral epicondyles, radial head, sigmoid notch, and ulnar styloid (Fig. 4). All data were processed through a custom MATLAB (MathWorks, Natick, MA, USA) program that transformed raw sensor data into clinically relevant kinematic parameters.Figure 3Electromagnetic sensors affixed to the humerus, radius, and ulna via bicortical threaded pins.Figure 4Visual representation of the articular registration of the distal humerus, radial head, and sigmoid notch of the ulna with digitized reference points marked.

Electromagnetic sensors affixed to the humerus, radius, and ulna via bicortical threaded pins.

Visual representation of the articular registration of the distal humerus, radial head, and sigmoid notch of the ulna with digitized reference points marked.

Local coordinate systems were constructed from the digitized landmarks in accordance with the recommendations of the International Society of Biomechanics.26 For the humerus, the medial–lateral axis was defined by the line connecting the medial and lateral epicondyles, with the long axis directed proximally along the humeral shaft and the third axis defined as their cross product. For the ulna, the long axis was defined from the center of the sigmoid notch to the ulnar styloid. Relative motion of the ulna with respect to the humerus was decomposed into flexion–extension, varus–valgus, and axial (internal–external) rotation using an Euler Z-X-Y sequence, with external rotation of the ulna defined as positive.26 Ulnar external rotation relative to the humeral coordinate system was recorded at 5° increments of elbow flexion throughout the arc of motion.

Each specimen underwent 5 cycles of flexion-extension from full flexion to full extension in both full supination and full pronation, simulating the gravity varus test motion. Elbow flexion was performed passively, allowing varus stress under gravity. The first flexion-extension cycle was discarded as preconditioning, and the remaining 4 were averaged to yield a single kinematic curve per specimen for each forearm position and ligamentous condition. No cycles were excluded. The test sequence was recorded for the intact state and repeated following complete transection of the LCLC through the Kocher interval to simulate PLRI. Because the release is destructive, the intact state was necessarily tested before sectioning in every specimen; the implications of this fixed testing order are addressed in the Discussion.

Statistical analysis

Each comparison was formed as a within-specimen paired difference, so that every specimen served as its own control and the pairing between conditions was preserved. At each 5° increment of flexion, the paired difference was tested against zero using a one-sample t-test (df = 11), and 95% confidence intervals were derived from the same t-distribution. To control the family-wise error rate across the 29 flexion angles within each comparison, P values were adjusted using the Holm–Bonferroni step-down procedure, which is uniformly more powerful than the Bonferroni correction and requires no assumption of independence among the tests. Two-sided adjusted P < .05 was considered significant. All values are reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD) with 95% confidence intervals. The supination–pronation difference within the sectioned state was obtained by summing, at each flexion angle, the corresponding difference in the intact state and the change produced by sectioning. Because the per-specimen values underlying the 2 components could not be obtained, the SD of that sum was taken as the sum of the 2 component SDs. This is the largest value the sum can take in the absence of their covariance, and it therefore yields conservative confidence intervals and P values. Analyses were performed using R statistical software (version 4.2.3; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

The 40°-60° arc of elbow flexion was designated the primary comparison. This arc was defined on clinical grounds from the published description of the gravity varus test, in which apprehension is reported between 40° and 60° of flexion, rather than from inspection of the present data.10 The arc-level estimate was obtained by averaging the per-angle values within the arc, with its SD taken as the mean of the per-angle SDs. Because adjacent angles are sampled from the same continuous kinematic curve and are therefore strongly correlated within a specimen, this is the conservative choice and yields a wider interval than treating the angles as independent. Comparisons at each individual 5° increment are reported for completeness and are interpreted as supporting description rather than as independent tests.

No a priori power analysis was performed. Twelve specimens were studied, a sample size consistent with published cadaveric investigations of elbow ligamentous stability.6,7,10,23,24 With 12 specimens and the observed within-specimen SDs, the study was able to detect a paired difference of approximately 1.0° in supination and 1.3° in pronation with 80% power at a 2-sided α of 0.05 before adjustment for multiplicity.

Results

All 12 cadaveric specimens completed testing without structural failure or data loss.

Primary comparison: the 40°-60° arc of flexion

Across the 40°-60° arc of elbow flexion, sectioning of the LCLC increased ulnar external rotation in supination by a mean of 6.36° (95% confidence interval [CI], 5.72°-7.01°; t = 21.6, P < .001). Per-angle mean increases within this arc ranged from 6.00° ± 0.95° at 60°-6.74° ± 1.20° at 45°, and every comparison within the arc remained significant after adjustment (Table I). Over the same arc with the forearm pronated, the mean change was −0.26° (95% CI, −1.04° to 0.52°; t = −0.72, P = .48), with per-angle values ranging from −0.34° ± 1.07° to −0.21° ± 1.29° and no comparison approaching significance (Table II). Ulnar external rotation in intact specimens

In intact elbows, supination produced consistently greater ulnar external rotation than pronation. Averaged across all 5° flexion increments, the mean difference was 1.53°, and across the 40°-60° arc, it was 1.92° (95% CI, 1.40°-2.45°; P < .001). The largest difference at any single flexion angle was 2.35° ± 1.40° (95% CI, 1.46°-3.24°; adjusted P = .002) at 95° of flexion. The difference was significant from 5° through 120° of flexion and not significant beyond 120° (Fig. 5). Effect of lateral collateral ligament complex release across the full flexion arc

Following LCLC release, the mean increase in ulnar external rotation in supination averaged 4.96° across all flexion angles, with a peak of 6.74° ± 1.20° (95% CI, 5.98°-7.50°) at 45° of flexion. The increase was significant at every one of the 29 flexion angles tested after Holm–Bonferroni adjustment, the weakest comparison being at 140° of flexion (2.33° ± 1.87°; 95% CI, 1.14°-3.51°; adjusted P = .001) (Table I, Fig. 6).Figure 6Mean change in ulnar external rotation from the intact to the sectioned (PLRI) state during the gravity varus test, shown separately for full supination and full pronation across the flexion arc. Effect of lateral collateral ligament complex release with the forearm pronated

With the forearm pronated, the picture was quite different. Across the mid-range of flexion, from 5° through 115°, no comparison reached significance, and the mean change across all flexion angles was 0.14°. Small but statistically significant changes were present only at the extremes of the arc: a reduction of 0.65° ± 0.55° at full extension (95% CI, −1.00° to −0.30°; adjusted P = .044) and increases of approximately 1.0° to 1.1° between 120° and 140° of flexion (adjusted P = .001-0.044). The largest single-angle change was 1.30° ± 1.27° at 115° of flexion, which did not remain significant after adjustment (95% CI, 0.50°-2.10°; adjusted P = .103) (Table 2, Fig. 6). None of these changes approaches the magnitude observed in supination.

Supination–pronation comparison in the sectioned state

Within the sectioned state, ulnar external rotation was substantially greater with the forearm supinated than pronated. Across the 40°-60° arc, the difference averaged 8.65° (95% CI, 7.11°-10.19°; t = 12.4, P < .001), with a peak of 8.82° ± 2.67° (95% CI, 7.13°-10.52°; adjusted P < .001) at 45° of flexion. Averaged across the full flexion arc, the difference was 6.19°. It was significant at all 28 flexion angles that could be tested, the weakest comparison being at 130° (2.49° ± 2.73°; adjusted P = .018); the comparison at 140° could not be tested because a standard deviation was not available at that angle (Fig. 7). Discussion

This cadaveric study quantifies the elbow kinematics produced during the gravity varus test before and after sectioning of the LCLC. Sectioning significantly increased ulnar external rotation with the forearm supinated, with no corresponding change in pronation, and the increase was greatest within the 40°-60° arc of flexion. These findings support the biomechanical plausibility of the maneuver: it produces, under controlled conditions, the motion pattern that the test is presumed to detect. They do not address the sensitivity, specificity, or interobserver reliability of the test, patient tolerance, or clinical outcomes, none of which can be determined from a cadaveric model.

These kinematic findings can be mapped onto the elements of the clinical examination. After LCLC sectioning, ulnar external rotation in supination increased by a mean of 6.4° across the 40°-60° arc of flexion—the arc in which apprehension is reported during gravity varus testing and in which lateral ulnohumeral gapping is described as palpable.10,16,22 With the forearm pronated, no increase was observed over the same arc, which corresponds to the clinical observation that patients with PLRI report a sense of security in pronation and apprehension in supination.6,16 The correspondence is, however, inferential. A cadaveric model produces motion but not symptoms; it cannot reproduce apprehension, guarding, or pain, and it cannot establish that the rotation measured here is what an examiner perceives at the bedside. Demonstrating that link requires study in awake patients with surgically or radiographically confirmed LCLC insufficiency.

The observed differences between supination and pronation underscore the critical role of forearm rotation in modulating lateral elbow stability.19,20 The magnitude of that dependence is the most striking feature of the present data. In intact elbows the supination–pronation difference across the 40°-60° arc averaged 1.92°, a value at or below the threshold generally regarded as detectable on clinical examination. After sectioning of the LCLC, the same difference averaged 8.65°, an approximately fourfold increase, and one that falls within the range of motion that has been described as palpable at the lateral joint line. Forearm position is therefore not simply a modifier of the maneuver but the variable that converts a subclinical asymmetry into one an examiner could plausibly appreciate, which is the mechanical argument for performing the test in supination as originally described. One explanation for this position-dependence is that, in supination, the radial head and proximal ulna rotate externally away from the capitellum, effectively “unwinding” the lateral column and increasing strain in the LUCL; in vivo measurements of collateral ligament length during forearm rotation are consistent with this interpretation.19,25 Cadaveric laxity studies likewise demonstrate that forearm position modulates lateral elbow stability.6,20 In contrast, pronation tightens the flexor-pronator mass and compresses the radiocapitellar articulation, providing a secondary dynamic stabilizing effect.6,24 Omori et al19 reported that radiocapitellar congruency is greatest in pronation while ulnohumeral congruency is comparatively unaffected by forearm rotation, which is consistent with the position dependence observed here. We did not measure ligament strain directly, and the “unwinding” mechanism should therefore be regarded as a plausible explanation for the observed kinematics rather than a demonstrated one. This interplay between static and dynamic stabilizers explains the clinical finding that patients with PLRI often report a subjective sense of stability in pronation and apprehension in supination.6,16

The present findings extend the work of Kwak et al10 in 2 respects. That study, which tested the forearm in pronation only, demonstrated that progressive release of the LCLC raises coronoid contact pressure and redistributes load medially under gravity varus. The present data show that the same gravitational loading produces a substantial rotational displacement of the ulna, but only when the forearm is supinated: with the forearm pronated, the mean change in ulnar external rotation across the entire flexion arc was 0.1°. Forearm position is therefore not a passive detail of the maneuver but the variable that determines whether the instability is expressed at all, which supports performing the test in supination as originally described and offers a mechanical explanation for the pronation-based rest and rehabilitation positions recommended after lateral ligament injury. It is also notable that the 2 studies localize the effect to different portions of the flexion arc: Kwak et al10 found contact-pressure differences greatest between 5° and 35° of flexion, whereas the rotational difference reported here peaked at 45°. Contact pressure and joint rotation are distinct outcomes, and the 2 findings are not in conflict, but the discrepancy indicates that the flexion angle at which the lateral elbow is most vulnerable may depend on which consequence of ligament loss is being measured.

These results are consistent with prior cadaveric investigations showing that sequential sectioning of the LUCL induces external rotation and lateral gapping of the ulnohumeral joint during supination and extension.6,17,18,23 O'Driscoll et al16 described a similar pattern during the pivot shift test, wherein the radiocapitellar joint subluxes posterolaterally near 40° of flexion and reduces with a palpable clunk as flexion increases. The present data replicate these kinematics under passive gravitational loading. Previous investigators have reported increases of 5°-8° in external rotation and up to 2 mm of lateral ulnohumeral gapping following complete LCLC release.23 Our peak increase of 6.74° in supination falls within this range, whereas the corresponding change in pronation did not exceed 1.30° at any flexion angle, confirming that gravitational varus torque alone is sufficient to elicit measurable instability when the forearm is supinated.

The gravity varus test differs mechanically from existing maneuvers in that the varus moment is generated by the weight of the forearm rather than by the examiner, so no applied axial compression or valgus load is required. Whether this mechanical difference translates into better patient tolerance, greater diagnostic accuracy, or lower interobserver variability than the lateral pivot shift or the chair push-up and tabletop relocation tests is not established by the present data and would require prospective clinical comparison.2,12 The present findings establish only that the maneuver produces the expected kinematic signature of LCLC insufficiency in a cadaveric model.4,11

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the cadaveric model contains no active muscular stabilization. The flexor-pronator and extensor-supinator groups contribute meaningfully to lateral elbow stability in vivo, and the magnitude of instability reported here is therefore likely to exceed that present in an awake, guarding patient. This limitation is more pronounced in the present model than in that of Kwak et al10, who simulated biceps, brachialis, and triceps loading with pneumatic actuators, whereas our protocol was entirely passive. Second, the mean donor age of 71 years exceeds that of the population in which PLRI is typically encountered, which tends to comprise younger, more active patients following trauma or surgical insult; reduced tissue stiffness in older specimens would bias toward greater measured motion.

Third, our injury model consisted of complete, acute release of the LCLC from its humeral origin in every specimen. This produces a uniform and maximal lesion and therefore almost certainly overestimates the instability present in the majority of clinical cases, which more often involve partial tearing, chronic attenuation, or scarred but incompetent tissue in continuity, sometimes with associated osseous or capsular pathology. The magnitudes reported here should accordingly be regarded as an upper bound rather than as values expected at the bedside, and the ability of the gravity varus test to detect lesser degrees of insufficiency cannot be inferred from these data. A graded sectioning protocol of the kind used by Kwak et al10, in which the complex was divided in thirds, would be required to establish the threshold of injury at which the kinematic change becomes detectable.

Fourth, this study did not quantify lateral ulnohumeral gapping or radiocapitellar translation. Electromagnetic tracking of rigidly fixed sensors resolves segmental rotation reliably but does not provide the joint-surface resolution required to measure millimeter-scale distraction, and we have made no claim about gapping. Because gapping is the finding the examiner is said to palpate during the gravity varus test, its direct measurement by dynamic fluoroscopy or stress ultrasonography is an important next step. Fifth, this model cannot assess pain, apprehension, or patient tolerance, which are the very features that motivate interest in the gravity varus test as an alternative to the lateral pivot shift; the correspondence between the kinematics measured here and the symptoms a patient reports is inferred and not demonstrated.

Sixth, because the release is destructive, the intact condition was necessarily tested before sectioning in every specimen. Any progressive laxity induced by repeated cycling is therefore confounded with the effect of the release and would bias the observed difference upward. A randomized or counterbalanced testing order was not possible because the release is destructive. The first flexion-extension cycle in each condition was discarded as preconditioning, and the remaining 4 averaged, which mitigates but does not eliminate this effect, and intact-state measurements were not repeated at the end of testing, so the magnitude of any residual sequence effect could not be estimated. Seventh, within-specimen repeatability of the kinematic curves was not formally quantified; no intraclass correlation coefficient was calculated, so the reproducibility of the tracking across cycles is not established. Eighth, all testing was performed under standardized mechanical conditions by a single protocol, so this study provides no information about interobserver variability—a limitation of particular relevance given that examiner-dependent variability is a documented weakness of the lateral pivot shift test.12

Future directions

Building upon these results, future investigations should examine how the gravity varus test performs in vivo with dynamic muscle activation and varying degrees of ligament injury. Prospective clinical studies correlating kinematic findings with patient apprehension, imaging, and surgical confirmation of LUCL deficiency would be required to define sensitivity and specificity. Additionally, imaging-based validation using stress ultrasonography or dynamic fluoroscopy could provide real-time visualization of lateral joint gapping during the test. Finally, exploring the gravity varus test's ability to distinguish partial from complete LCLC tears or post-operative laxity could broaden its clinical utility as both a diagnostic and post-operative assessment tool.

Conclusion

In this cadaveric model, sectioning of the LCLC increased ulnar external rotation during the gravity varus test with the forearm in supination but not in pronation, and the increase was greatest between 40° and 60° of elbow flexion—the arc in which apprehension has been described clinically. These findings support the biomechanical plausibility of the gravity varus test as a maneuver that elicits the motion pattern characteristic of PLRI, and they demonstrate that forearm pronation suppresses that motion almost entirely. They do not establish the diagnostic accuracy, interobserver reliability, or patient tolerance of the test, nor its superiority over existing maneuvers. A prospective clinical study in awake patients, with comparison against surgical or imaging confirmation of LCLC insufficiency, is required before the gravity varus test can be recommended in place of established examination techniques.
