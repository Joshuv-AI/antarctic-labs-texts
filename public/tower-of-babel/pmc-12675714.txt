Psychological resilience and clinical depression on bone loss and fracture
Authors: Yejee Lim, Chaiho Jeong, Jinyoung Kim, Jeongmin Lee, Jeonghoon Ha, Mee Kyoung Kim, Hyuk-Sang Kwon, Ki-Ho Song, Sheng-Min Wang, Ki Hyun Baek
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe effects of psychological resilience and depressive symptoms on bone health have not been well-studied. This work aimed to evaluate the association between psychological resilience and depressive symptoms on bone mineral density (BMD) and osteoporotic fracture in community-dwelling adults in Korea. Data from the Chungju Metabolic Disease Cohort were used. Between May 2007 and March 2011, 4029 participants aged 40 years or older underwent a baseline examination, and approximately four years later, they underwent a second examination. BMD was measured, and resilience and depression scales were completed. Participants in the lowest resilience group had lower BMD Z-scores at the femoral neck (0.04 ± 0.53 vs. 0.22 ± 0.950, p < 0.001) and the total hip (0.17 ± 0.98 vs. 0.34 ± 0.92, p < 0.001) compared to those in the highest resilience group. Individuals with clinical depression also had lower BMD Z-scores than those without it. However, resilience status was not associated with the prevalence of osteoporosis at follow-up or incident fracture. In contrast, the odds of osteoporosis were 1.39 (95% CI 1.12–1.71) in women with clinical depression even after adjusting for covariates. In men, clinical depression was not associated with the odds of osteoporosis. Among non-osteoporotic participants, subjects with clinical depression had a 1.40-fold (95% CI 1.07–1.92) higher fracture incidence than those without depression. In this study, low resilience did not appear to negatively affect the prevalence of osteoporosis at follow-up or fracture. Introduction

Osteoporosis is a disease defined by low bone mass, deterioration of the bone microstructure, and compromised bone strength, which results in an increased risk of fracture1. Osteoporotic fractures are one of the most common causes of disability and a major contributor to medical care costs in many regions of the world2. The prevalence of osteoporosis and osteoporotic fracture is increasing throughout the world as the population ages2–4. Common risk factors for osteoporotic fracture that are independent of aging and osteoporosis include smoking, excessive alcohol use, and low body weight5. In addition, decreased physical performance or activity and the propensity to fall have also been recognized as important predictors of fractures6,7.

Psychological resilience is the process and outcome of successfully adapting to difficult or challenging life experiences; it is characterized by mental, emotional, and behavioral flexibility and adjustment to external and internal demands8. In short, psychological resilience is the ability to bounce back from stressful times. Higher psychological resilience can mitigate the effect of chronic health conditions on physical function among older adults9. In addition, it can protect against the limitations in activities of daily living (ADLs) associated with aging10 and improve physical function11. Because decreased physical function is a risk factor for fracture, analyzing the effects of psychological resilience on fracture is important for predicting morbidity and disability in adults. However, to the best of our knowledge, the effects of resilience on fracture have not been studied.

Psychological resilience is also related to depression in individuals with physical disabilities. Specifically, changes in resilience are associated with changes in depression11. Depression is a medical illness accompanied by symptoms of fatigue, sadness, self-limiting attitude, neglect, disability, and guilt12. Depression increases the risk of various chronic diseases and complicates the prognosis of chronic diseases such as stroke, diabetes, and osteoporosis13,14. However, research about the effect of depression itself on bone loss and fracture has not produced consistent results. Furthermore, only a few studies have examined the relationship between depression and bone loss in community-dwelling adults in a large cohort of Asians15.

Thus, our aim in this study was to evaluate the effect of psychological resilience and clinical depression on bone strength and osteoporotic fracture in a large cohort of community-dwelling adults in Korea. We hypothesized that low resilience and clinical depression would increase the risk of osteoporosis and osteoporotic fractures.

Methods

Study population

The Chungju Metabolic Disease Cohort (CMC) study is a community-based study of metabolic diseases, including diabetes, metabolic syndrome, and osteoporosis, in a population aged 40 years or older and living in the rural area around the city of Chungju, Korea. The details of the CMC study have been described elsewhere16. In total, 2294 men and 3713 women were selected by stratified random cluster sampling, and they underwent a baseline examination with dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) between May 2007 and March 2011. Among those subjects, 4063 participants underwent a second examination approximately 4 years after the baseline examination, between April 2011 and December 2015. Because the baseline examinations were conducted over a four-year period and the follow-up examinations were conducted over another four-year period, the follow-up duration varied among participants. Among those participants, 34 individuals did not receive any baseline mental health evaluation. Therefore, 4029 participants were included in this study. The flow chart of the study is illustrated as Fig. 1. The institutional review board of the Catholic University of Korea approved this study (No. KCMC07OT076). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Assessment of resilience and clinical depression

Resilience was assessed using the Connor-Davidson Resilience Scale (CD-RISC), which comprises 25 items, each rated on a five-point Likert scale from strongly disagree to strongly agree. Total scores range from 0 to 100, with higher scores reflecting greater resilience17. Because CD-RISC scores appear to be influenced by the location in which the data are obtained and the nature of the sample, scores have differed among reports18. Therefore, we divided participants into four groups based on the quartiles of CD-RISC score of 60, 75, and 91; those whose CD-RISC score was less than 60 were considered to have the lowest resilience. In adults younger than 65 years, depressive symptoms were measured using the Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (CES-D)19. This 20-item measure asks how often during the past week respondents experienced symptoms associated with depression. Responses are scored on a four-point scale (ranging from rarely or none of the time to most or almost all the time: 0, 1, 2, 3); total scores range from 0 to 60. We considered persons scoring 16 or greater to be at risk for clinical depression20. Subjects aged 65 years or older at the time of the assessment completed the Geriatric Depression Scale short form (sGDS), a 15-item depressive symptoms checklist designed to detect current depression in older persons21. We defined a score greater than 5 on the sGDS as suggesting clinical depression22. Using a cut-off of 6 or more symptoms, the sGDS has a sensitivity of 88% and specificity of 62% compared with a structured clinical interview for depression23. Prior studies have shown that CES-D and sGDS offer comparable diagnostic performance in older adults, supporting the use of these age-appropriate tools across the cohort24–26.

Bone mineral density (BMD) and osteoporotic fracture

The BMD of the femoral neck, total hip, and lumbar spine was measured using DXA (QDR-4500C, Hologic, Waltham, MA, USA). The precise assessment protocol and regions of interest for the DXA examinations have been described elsewhere27. Following the guidelines set by the World Health Organization, osteoporosis was defined as having a T-score of − 2.5 or lower at the lumbar spine (L1–L4), femoral neck, or total hip. In this study, the prevalence of osteoporosis follow-up was analyzed in participants aged 50 years or older to determine whether baseline psychological resilience and depression status were associated with the likelihood of having osteoporosis at the second examination. For comparisons of BMD across mental health subgroups, Z-scores were used to account for age- and sex-related differences in bone mass. The rate of change in BMD is expressed as an annualized percentage of the initial value, i.e., percentage change in BMD per year. Throughout the study period, we evaluated the occurrence of fractures through medical interviews. When a participant reported a diagnosed fracture, their general practitioner conducted an interview to determine the fracture’s type, date, and cause6. Additionally, three physicians centrally reviewed clinical fragility fractures resulting from forces that typically do not lead to fractures28. In this study, fractures resulting from low-energy trauma at any anatomical site except the skull, face, hands, ankles, and feet were classified as clinical osteoporotic fractures29. For vertebral fractures, only clinical vertebral fractures that occurred due to low-energy trauma on a particular date were included.

Other assessments

Weight and height measurements were taken at the time of BMD assessment. Body Mass Index (BMI) was calculated as the ratio of body weight (kg) to height (m2). Demographic information, medical history, and lifestyle behavior data were obtained using standardized questionnaires. Information on participants’ current smoking status (yes/no), current alcohol consumption (yes/no), performing exercise at least once a week (yes/no), and their history of falls in the preceding 12 months was also collected.

Statistical analysis

The participants were divided into four groups according to their resilience status. The descriptive statistics for the continuous variables are reported as the mean and standard deviation, and those for the categorical variables are reported as the number and percentage. To compare the principal characteristics of the study subjects according to their resilience status, Kruskal–Wallis testing and chi-square testing were used to analyze the continuous and categorical variables, respectively. To analyze the associations between mental health and the baseline Z-score and between mental health and the annualized BMD change rate, Kruskal–Wallis tests and Wilcoxon signed-rank test were used. For each statistical analysis, participants with missing values for the variables required in that specific analysis were excluded. The sample sizes after exclusion are indicated in each table. The prevalence of osteoporosis was evaluated in participants aged 50 years or older, as T-score-based diagnostic thresholds are clinically relevant to this age group. The sex-stratified associations between mental health and the prevalence of osteoporosis at follow-up were examined using univariate and multivariate logistic regression models. Univariate and multivariate Cox proportional hazard regressions were used to evaluate the sex-stratified effects of low resilience and clinical depression on the risk of fracture. Because multiple fractures do not contribute to independent observations, this analysis was based on the first clinical fracture per individual. Age, BMI, current drinking, current smoking, regular exercise, medications known to influence bone metabolism, and baseline T-score were adjusted in the multivariable model in regression analyses. Baseline T-score was categorized into osteoporosis (≤ − 2.5), osteopenia (− 2.5 to − 1.0), and normal (> − 1.0) groups and included as a covariate to adjust for baseline bone health status while minimizing multicollinearity with follow-up osteoporosis and fracture outcomes. In women, menopausal status was additionally adjusted for in logistic regression analyses. The significance threshold was set at a two-sided p < 0.05. All analyses were performed in R software (version 4.0.2, R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

Study population and baseline characteristics

The baseline characteristics of the 4029 participants, stratified by resilience quartiles, are presented in Table 1. Participants were categorized into four groups based on the CD-RISC scores: Q1 (highest resilience, n = 993), Q2 (n = 992), Q3 (n = 992), and Q4 (lowest resilience, n = 992). Compared with individuals in the highest resilience group, those in the lowest group were significantly older, more frequently female, shorter in stature, leaner, and less likely to be current smokers or alcohol consumers. They had lower BMD at all measured sites and slightly lower fasting glucose levels than those in the highest resilience group. In addition, they showed a higher tendency to fall in the previous 12 months and were less likely to do regular exercise.

A total of 3911 participants completed either the CES-D or sGDS assessments. Among them, 1086 individuals (27.8%) met the criteria for clinical depression based on established cut-offs for these instruments. Subjects with clinical depression also had different characteristics from those without depression in all variables except fasting glucose level and taking anti-hypertensive medication. Participants with clinical depression had CD-RISC scores that were on average 11 points lower than those without depression (Supplementary Table 1). Additionally, both CES-D and sGDS showed significant inverse correlations with CD-RISC in participants aged ≥ 65 years and < 65 years, respectively (r = − 0.243 and r = − 0.346; both p < 0.001; data not shown).

Association between baseline resilience status and BMD Z-scores and yearly percent change

Compared with those in the highest resilience group, participants in the lowest resilience group had lower mean Z-scores at the femoral neck (0.04 ± 0.53 vs. 0.22 ± 0.95, p < 0.001) and total hip (0.17 ± 0.98 vs. 0.34 ± 0.92, p < 0.001) in the baseline examination. In addition, participants with clinical depression had lower Z-scores than those without clinical depression at all measured sites (Table 2). The participants who were enrolled in the baseline examination completed the follow-up examination after an average of 48.16 ± 17.29 months. The annualized rate of BMD loss at the total hip was higher in men in the lowest resilience group than those in the highest resilience group (− 0.73 ± 1.38% vs. − 0.41 ± 0.82%, p = 0.013). However, the annualized rate of BMD loss at the femoral neck and the lumbar spine in men and at all measured sites in women did not differ significantly according to resilience status. Men with clinical depression had a higher annualized rate of BMD loss than those without clinical depression at the femoral neck (− 0.72 ± 1.48 vs. − 0.52 ± 1.27, p = 0.013) and total hip (− 0.68 ± 1.33 vs. − 0.53 ± 1.09, p = 0.012). However, women with clinical depression had a higher annualized rate of BMD loss than those without clinical depression only at the femoral neck (− 0.97 ± 1.82 vs. − 0.82 ± 2.17, p = 0.024). Spinal BMD in women with clinical depression actually increased annually during the study period (Table 3).

Prevalence of osteoporosis at follow-up according to resilience and clinical depression status

The prevalence of osteoporosis was analyzed in 3852 participants (1434 men and 2418 women) aged 50 years or older. The lowest resilience status and clinical depression were associated with high risks of osteoporosis at the second examination (48.58% and 56.06%, respectively). The odds of osteoporosis at the second examination according to the presence of baseline clinical depression was 1.41 (95% CI 1.15–1.74, p = 0.001) in women when adjusting for age and BMI. This relationship did not change even after adjusting for the status of current drinking, current smoking, exercise, fall history, baseline spine BMD, menopausal status, and use of medications known to influence bone metabolism (OR 1.39 [95% CI 1.12–1.71], p = 0.002) (Table 4). However, low resilience status in both genders and clinical depression in men did not have a higher prevalence of osteoporosis when adjusting for covariates.

Resilience and clinical depression status and the incidence of osteoporotic fractures

During the follow-up period, 319 participants (53 men and 266 women) experienced osteoporotic fractures. Among them, 131, 96, 24, and 15 new clinical vertebral, wrist, hip, and shoulder fractures occurred, respectively. The median time from enrollment to the first osteoporotic fracture occurrence was 42 [range 1–101] months. Participants with clinical depression had higher rates of osteoporotic fractures than those without depression (9.85% vs. 7.07%, respectively, p = 0.003). Women with clinical depression had a 1.32-fold (95% CI 1.02–1.70, p = 0.031) increased risk of osteoporotic fracture compared with those who did not have clinical depression. However, after adjusting for all covariates that could potentially affect fracture incidence, this association was no longer observed. In men, depression was not associated with the incidence of osteoporotic fracture in either the univariable or multivariable analyses. In addition, resilience was not associated with the incidence of osteoporotic fracture in the univariable or multivariable analyses (Table 5).

Because clinical depression was associated with osteoporotic fracture in the univariate analysis, we illustrated the additive effect of clinical depression and BMD on osteoporotic fracture prediction. We divided the subjects into four groups according to the presence of clinical depression and osteoporosis (Supplementary Table 2). We then calculated the relative osteoporotic fracture risk based on a reference group of participants without osteoporosis or clinical depression. Participants who had osteoporosis had a higher incidence of fracture than the reference group, regardless of the presence of clinical depression (with clinical depression: HR 2.70 (95% CI 1.91–3.81); without clinical depression: HR 2.93 (95% CI 2.14–4.02). Among the participants without osteoporosis, the presence of clinical depression resulted in a 1.40-fold (95% CI 1.07–1.92, p = 0.015) higher risk of osteoporotic fracture than found in those without clinical depression (Fig. 2).

To evaluate the joint effects of depression and resilience on fracture risk, participants were categorized into four groups based on depression status (present or absent) and whether their resilience score was above or below the median value (Supplementary Table 3). When analyzing these four groups, we found that among participants without depression, those with low resilience had a slightly higher fracture risk compared to those with high resilience, although this was not statistically significant (HR 1.31, 95% CI [1.00–1.72], p = 0.054). Among participants with depression, both low and high resilience groups showed significantly higher fracture risks compared to the no depression/high resilience group (HR 1.53, 95% CI [1.11–2.10], p = 0.008; and HR 1.65, 95% CI [1.18–2.30], p = 0.003, respectively) (Fig. 3).

Discussion

We aimed to evaluate the effects of low resilience and clinical depression on bone loss. In this longitudinal cohort study, participants in the lowest resilience group had lower BMD Z-scores than participants in the highest resilience group. However, the low resilience status was not associated with an increased prevalence of osteoporosis at follow-up or incident fracture. Participants with clinical depression had a higher rate of BMD loss at the femoral neck than those without depression. In addition, women with clinical depression had approximately 1.39-fold higher osteoporosis prevalence at the follow-up examination than those without depression, even after adjusting for covariates. However, the association between clinical depression and an increased risk of incident fracture diminished after adjusting for covariates.

Psychological resilience has been shown to influence physical function. For example, people who are more resilient engage in more physical activity than those who are less resilient30. In previous studies, patients with a higher level of resilience suffered from fewer independent ADL limitations and had improved physical functioning compared with those with low resilience31,32. In our study, participants with low resilience status were less likely than others to do regular exercise and had lower Z-scores at the femoral neck and total hip. Physical performance and physical activity are important determinants of BMD because mechanical loading is an important contributor to bone density and quality33,34. The reduced activity levels in this group likely contributed to the lower Z-scores observed at these sites.

On the other hand, we found no relationship between resilience status and the rate of bone loss or incident fracture. Only men in the lowest resilience group showed a high annualized rate of BMD loss at the total hip. In previous studies, psychological resilience played a major role in reducing physical disabilities after chronic and acute disease events10. In addition, after hip fracture, psychological resilience showed positive associations with physical function9,35. Because we observed no association between resilience and osteoporosis and fracture in our study population, we assume that psychological resilience cannot prevent the occurrence of fractures.

While resilience may support physical functioning and correlate with healthier behaviors such as increased physical activity, it appears to influence bone mass in a cross-sectional manner rather than exerting a sustained impact on long-term skeletal outcomes. This underscores the need to distinguish baseline psychological traits from longitudinal predictors in musculoskeletal research. To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first attempt to evaluate the association between resilience and fracture incidence; therefore, further longitudinal studies are needed to clarify the influence of psychological resilience on bone loss.

In this study, clinical depression was associated with low Z-scores at all measured sites; women with clinical depression had an especially high prevalence of osteoporosis at follow-up compared with those without depression, even after adjusting for covariates. In accordance with our results, several previous studies have reported a significant association between clinical depression and BMD in women36,37. In contrast, the association was not relevant in men. Inconsistent relationships were reported among the male subjects, with some studies reporting an association between clinical depression and low BMD in elderly men15,38 while others reporting no such association36,39. The sex-specific effect of depression on bone is still not conclusive. Some biological mechanisms have been suggested such as sympatho-vagal activity or hypercortisolism40,41. Further longitudinal studies are needed to clarify the influence of sex in depression on bone loss.

The increased risk of incident osteoporotic fracture related to clinical depression diminished after adjustment for covariates. Previous studies have reported mixed findings about the relationship between depression and fracture risk, with some studies finding a positive association14,42,43 and others finding no relationship44,45. Some studies have suggested that the association between depression and fracture risk is mediated by antidepressant use rather than clinical depression itself46,47. These findings raised the question of whether the effects of depression on fracture risk are modified by bone health status or resilience. Therefore, we first examined the joint effects of osteoporosis and depression on fracture risk. We found that depression was associated with an increased fracture risk in participants without osteoporosis, but not in those with osteoporosis. Then we analyzed the joint effects of depression and resilience. Only participants with depression showed higher fracture risks compared to the no depression/high resilience group, regardless of their resilience level. Given that participants with depression were more likely than others to be older, female, and have lower BMD at baseline, those confounding factors might have affected the increased fracture risk in non-osteoporotic individuals with depression. Nevertheless, it might be wise to take the depressive individuals’ potential increased risk of fracture (compared with those with neither osteoporosis nor depressive symptoms) into clinical consideration.

The strengths of our study lie in our evaluation of the effects of resilience and depression on bone health in a longitudinal, community-dwelling cohort. Many CMC participants were followed for 4 years. Detailed data on their medical histories, lifestyle behaviors, and fracture occurrence were obtained through close interviews. The investigators were trained in the study protocol prior to conducting physical examinations and administering questionnaires. We quantified resilience and depression with the CD-RISC, CES-D, and sGDS. By using comprehensive questionnaires, we were able to adjust for several factors known to influence bone health: weight, exercise, smoking, drinking and regular exercise. Moreover, our analysis of resilience in relation to BMD and fracture risk is unique in the literature.

Our study has several limitations as well. First, recall limitations might have affected our ability to precisely diagnose fracture episodes because participants were asked to provide this information retrospectively. To reduce over-reporting and inaccurate recall, we included only clinical spine fractures as osteoporotic vertebral fractures. Second, the fracture incidence might be underestimated. Because a major osteoporotic fracture deteriorates one’s ability and causes high mortality48, subjects who suffered fractures might have been reluctant to undergo the follow-up examination, especially if disability had occurred. Third, physical function testing and medication histories, including anti-depressive drugs, were not investigated in this research. As mentioned, several studies reported that the effect of depression on bone loss was worsened or mediated by antidepressants43,47. The pure effect of antidepressants needs to be further clarified. Lastly, although the CES-D and sGDS are well-validated screening instruments with acceptable sensitivity and specificity for identifying individuals likely to meet criteria for major depressive disorder or clinically relevant depression19,49, they do not constitute formal diagnoses based on the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders or the International Classification of Diseases. The absence of diagnostic confirmation by a trained mental health professional represents a limitation of the present study.

Conclusion

We found no evidence to support a significant relationship between low resilience and the risk of osteoporosis and fracture. Although the association between clinical depression and fracture diminished after adjusting for covariates, depressive individuals still had higher fracture incidence than those without depression or osteoporosis. Therefore, clinicians could take into consideration that depressive individuals have a potentially increased risk of fracture compared with those with neither osteoporosis nor depressive symptoms.
