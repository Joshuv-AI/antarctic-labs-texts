Triglyceride glucose related indices predict incident breast cancer risk in a population based cohort study
Authors: Qing Xie, Yaolan Zhen, Zhixing Fan, Ying Hu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe association between triglyceride-glucose (TyG)-related indices and breast cancer risk is not fully understood. This study aimed to investigate the role of TyG-related indices in predicting breast cancer risk.MethodsWe analyzed data from 220,396 female participants in the UK Biobank, with a median follow-up of 13.63 years. TyG-related indices including TyG, TyG-body mass index (TyG-BMI), TyG-waist circumference (TyG-WC), TyG-waist to height ratio (TyG-WHtR), TyG-waist to hip ratio (TyG-WHR). Cox proportional hazards models were used to evaluate the association between TyG-related indices and breast cancer risk. Mediation analysis was performed to assess the role of white blood cell (WBC) and C-reactive protein (CRP) in this association. Interaction analysis was conducted to assess if age, menopausal status, and breast cancer screening modified the association between TyG-related indices and breast cancer risk.ResultsThe highest quartiles of TyG, TyG-BMI, TyG-WC, TyG-WHtR, and TyG-WHR were significantly associated with increased breast cancer risk, with hazard ratios (95% CI) of 1.10 (1.04–1.17), 1.28 (1.20–1.35), 1.30 (1.22–1.38), 1.23 (1.16–1.31), and 1.17 (1.10–1.24), respectively. Interaction analysis revealed stronger associations in postmenopausal women, those aged ≥ 60, and individuals who had not undergone breast cancer screening (P for interaction < 0.05). Mediation analysis showed that CRP contributed a larger proportion of the mediation effect than WBC, with CRP mediating 26.8%–54.7% and WBC mediating 14.3%–40.4%.ConclusionTyG-related indices were significantly associated with increased breast cancer risk, highlighting their potential as biomarkers for risk assessment and prevention strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-025-04031-8.

Introduction

Breast cancer remains one of the most prevalent and deadly cancers among women [1], with 2.26 million new diagnoses and 685,000 deaths reported globally in 2020 [2, 3]. Beyond its high mortality, the disease significantly affects long-term quality of life. Epidemiological data indicated a continuous rise in incidence across various regions [4], underscoring its growing public health impact. These patterns emphasize the urgent need to identify modifiable risk factors and improve understanding of its etiology to inform effective prevention efforts.

Breast cancer is increasingly understood as a metabolically influenced disease, with insulin resistance (IR) playing a central role in linking obesity, dyslipidemia, and tumorigenic pathways [5]. The triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index, calculated as the natural logarithm of fasting blood glucose multiplied by triglycerides, is a widely accepted surrogate marker for IR [6]. It showed a strong inverse correlation (ρ = − 0.68) with the hyperinsulinemic-euglycemic clamp, the reference standard for IR assessment [7]. However, due to its complexity, cost, and need for specialized personnel, the clamp method is rarely used in clinical settings [8]. In contrast, the TyG index offers a simple, affordable, and scalable alternative. Recent studies suggested that combining TyG with anthropometric indicators such as BMI or waist circumference (e.g., TyG-BMI, TyG-WC) improves risk stratification for IR-associated cancers [9, 10]. However, evidence regarding the specific link to breast cancer remains inconsistent. While case-control studies in Chinese populations supported a positive association [11, 12], a prospective analysis of European cohorts found no significant link with postmenopausal breast cancer [13]. This discrepancy highlights a critical knowledge gap and underscores the need for further large-scale, prospective studies. Moreover, existing research has largely overlooked the combined effect of insulin resistance and adiposity using integrated TyG-obesity indices.

Understanding the underlying biological mechanisms is key to clarifying the relationship between TyG-related indices and breast cancer. Chronic inflammation is a plausible mediator, as insulin resistance (IR) contributes to adipose tissue inflammation, promoting a tumor-supportive microenvironment [14, 15]. To comprehensively capture this pathway, we focused on two specific and complementary inflammatory biomarkers. C-reactive protein (CRP), a sensitive marker of low-grade inflammation, and white blood cell (WBC) count, an indicator of subclinical immune activation [16]. This approach allows us to assess the consistency of the inflammatory mediation pathway across different biological layers. Although CRP has been linked to obesity-related cancers [17], few studies have simultaneously evaluated the mediating roles of both CRP and WBC across various TyG-obesity indices.

This study investigated the associations between TyG-related indices, as markers of IR, and breast cancer risk. It further explored whether WBC and CRP mediate these associations, offering insights into their utility as potential biomarkers for targeted prevention strategies.

Materials and methods

Study design and population

The UK Biobank is a large, prospective cohort study that enrolled over 500,000 participants aged 40–73 from England, Scotland, and Wales between 2007 and 2010 [18]. At baseline, participants completed detailed touchscreen questionnaires covering sociodemographic, lifestyle, and health information. Ethical approval was obtained from the North West Multi-centre Research Ethics Committee, and all participants provided written informed consent for long-term data linkage. This study was conducted in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines for reporting observational cohort studies.

This analysis included 273,188 women. After excluding individuals lost to follow-up, with pre-existing breast cancer, or missing TyG-related indices, 220,396 participants remained for the primary analysis (analysis 1). For mediation analyses, those with missing WBC or CRP data were further excluded, yielding 214,041 women with complete data (analysis 2), as illustrated in Figure S1.

Ascertainment of breast cancer cases

Breast cancer cases were identified using the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10) code C50. Cancer incidence data were obtained from the National Cancer Registry, which compiles information from cancer centers across the UK. Follow-up began at enrollment and continued until the occurrence of breast cancer, death, or October 31, 2022, whichever came first.

Measurement of TyG-related indices

During the baseline survey of the UK Biobank, random peripheral venous blood samples from participants were collected. At the assessment center, measurements of height (m), weight (kg), waist circumference (WC, cm), hip circumference (HC, cm), triglycerides (TG, mg/dL), and fasting blood glucose (FBG, mg/dL) were taken, and body mass index (BMI, kg/m²), waist-to-height ratio (WHtR), and waist-to-hip ratio (WHR) were calculated. All anthropometric measurements were taken by trained research staff following standardized protocols. TyG-related indices were determined using TG and FBG levels measured after 8–12 h of fasting to ensure data accuracy and comparability. The TyG index was calculated using the formula: TyG = Ln [Fasting Triglycerides (mg/dL) × Fasting Blood Glucose (mg/dL)/2]. TyG-BMI, TyG-WC, TyG-WHtR, and TyG-WHR were calculated by multiplying TyG by BMI, WC, WHtR, and WHR, respectively. Additional information can be found on the UK Biobank website (https://www.ukbiobank.ac.uk).

Evaluation of WBC count and CRP

WBC count (cells/L) were measured using the Coulter LH 750 system (Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA) per the manufacturer’s protocols. (CRP levels were assessed via the Beckman Coulter AU5800 clinical chemistry analyzer. Given the skewed distribution of WBC count and CRP data, log transformation was applied.

Measurement of covariates

Sociodemographic variables, including age, ethnicity, educational attainment, and the Townsend index, were collected via a touchscreen questionnaire. The analysis also incorporated multiple lifestyle factors such as alcohol status, smoking status, dietary health, and physical activity. Additional covariates included family history of breast cancer in first-degree relatives, parity, age at menarche, ever use of oral contraceptives, ever use of hormone replacement therapy, prior breast cancer screening, and menopausal status. Details of these variables’ definitions and measurements are available in prior studies [18, 19].

Statistical analyses

Missing values for both categorical and continuous variables were imputed using the random forest method. Continuous variables were reported as means with standard deviations, while categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages.

TyG-related indices were categorized into quartiles based on the data distribution. Kaplan-Meier curves and log-rank tests were used to evaluate breast cancer risk across these quartiles. To investigate the relationships between TyG-related indices and breast cancer, we employed Cox proportional hazards models. Model 1 adjusted for age, ethnicity, education, and Townsend index. Model 2 additionally adjusted for alcohol status, smoking status, diet health, physical activity based on model (1) Model 3 additionally adjusted for family history of breast cancer, number of live births, age at menarche, ever used oral contraceptive pills, ever used HRT, ever had breast cancer screening, and menopausal status based on model (2) We used restricted cubic spline (RCS) models to analyze dose-response relationships, accounting for confounding factors. Subgroup analyses were conducted based on age, smoking status, alcohol status, diet health, breast cancer screening, and menopausal status, and interactions between TyG-related indices and these factors were tested. Counterfactual mediation analysis was performed using the CMAverse package. The mediating roles of WBC and CRP in the associations between TyG-related indices and breast cancer risk were then estimated [19].

To assess the robustness of our findings, several sensitivity analyses were conducted. First, analyses were repeated using a complete-case dataset without imputation. Next, participants with pre-existing diabetes, cardiovascular disease, hypertension, chronic bronchitis, emphysema, or COPD were excluded to minimize confounding from chronic conditions. Finally, events occurring within the first two years of follow-up were removed to address potential reverse causation.

All analyses were conducted using SAS 9.4 and R 4.3.1, with statistical significance defined as a two-sided p-value < 0.05.

Results

Baseline characteristics

The average follow-up duration was 13.63 years (IQR: 12.82, 14.35). Among the 220,396 participants, 9446 developed breast cancer. Those who developed breast cancer were slightly older (57.20 vs. 56.20 years). They had a higher proportion of White ethnicity (95.90% vs. 94.26%). They were more likely to report a family history of breast cancer (9.96% vs. 6.20%). Postmenopausal status was more common (74.92% vs. 71.18%). A history of HRT use was also more frequent (42.10% vs. 38.03%). Table 1 presented the key baseline characteristics of participants. The incidence rates of breast cancer across different quartiles of TyG-related indices were shown in the Table S1. The incidence rate for TyG index rose from 2.96 to 3.57 per 1000 person - years from Q1 to Q4. Likewise, TyG-BMI increased from 2.77 to 3.66, TyG-WC from 2.69 to 3.66, and TyG-WHtR from 2.80 to 3.62. TyG-WHR ranged from 2.92 in Q1 to 3.64 in Q4.

TyG-related indices with breast cancer

Figure 1 showed Kaplan–Meier curves for cumulative breast cancer risk across TyG-related index quartiles. All indices exhibited significant group differences (log-rank test, P < 0.001). In Model 3, all TyG-related indices were significantly associated with increased breast cancer risk. Compared to the lowest quartile, participants in the highest quartile had elevated risks: TyG (HR = 1.10, 95% CI 1.04–1.17), TyG-BMI (HR = 1.28, 95% CI 1.20–1.35), TyG-WC (HR = 1.30, 95% CI 1.22–1.38), TyG-WHtR (HR = 1.23, 95% CI 1.16–1.31), and TyG-WHR (HR = 1.17, 95% CI 1.10–1.24), all with P for trend < 0.001. These associations remained consistent across Models 1 and 2 (Table 2). As shown in Fig. 2, all indices were significantly linked to breast cancer risk (P < 0.001). TyG-BMI, TyG-WC, and TyG-WHtR demonstrated non-linear associations, whereas TyG and TyG-WHR followed linear trends.

Subgroup analysis

Subgroup analyses revealed significant interactions between TyG-related indices and age, breast cancer screening, and menopausal status (P for interaction < 0.05). For TyG-BMI, TyG-WC, and TyG-WHtR participants aged ≥ 60 exhibited steeper risk gradients across quartiles compared to those aged < 60. Similarly, stronger associations were observed in postmenopausal women (vs. premenopausal) and unscreened individuals (vs. screened) for these indices. No significant interactions were detected with smoking, alcohol status and Diet health status (P for interaction > 0.05) (Table 3).

Sensitivity analysis

Sensitivity analyses supported the robustness of our findings. Results were consistent after excluding participants with major chronic conditions, using complete-case data, and omitting events within the first two years (Tables S2–S4).

Discussion

This study found that higher TyG-related indices were significantly associated with an increased risk of breast cancer. The association was stronger in women aged ≥ 60, postmenopausal women, and those not regularly screened. Mediation analysis showed that WBC and CRP mediated a significant portion of the relationship between TyG indices and breast cancer risk.

Comparison with other studies

To date, several studies have indicated that the TyG index can independently predict an increased risk of various types of cancer, including prostate cancer and breast cancer. A case-control study conducted in China found that the highest quartile of the TyG index was significantly associated with an increased risk of breast cancer compared to the lowest quartile, with an OR (95% CI) of 1.61 (1.19–2.17) [11]. Data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES, 1999–2018) also indicated that for each standard deviation increase in the TyG index, the OR for breast cancer risk in women was 2.25 (95% CI: 1.50–3.37). A recent study reported a non-linear dose-response relationship between the TyG index and breast cancer risk in an Indonesian population [20]. However, research from six European cohorts did not observe an association between the TyG index and postmenopausal breast cancer risk [13], while another study from the UK found an association between the TyG index and breast cancer risk in postmenopausal women [21]. In this study, we found that participants in the highest quartile of the TyG index had a 10% higher risk of breast cancer compared to those in the lowest quartile, with a clear linear association. The observed heterogeneity may be attributed to differences in the definition of study populations and the adjustments made for confounding factors.

Recent studies have shown that combining the TyG index with obesity-related indicators (BMI, WC, WHtR, and WHR) results in stronger associations with cancer risk compared to using TyG alone [9, 10]. The relationship between obesity and breast cancer risk is complex, with obesity having a dual negative impact on breast cancer patients: first, it is associated with a significant increase in breast cancer incidence; and second, it is linked to poorer treatment outcomes for most breast cancer subtypes. However, an exception to this trend has been observed in premenopausal women, where evidence suggests an inverse relationship between obesity and breast cancer risk [22]. In this study, we found that TyG-BMI, TyG-WC, TyG-WHtR, and TyG-WHR were more strongly associated with breast cancer risk compared to TyG alone. Therefore, a comprehensive investigation of the combination of TyG indices and obesity markers in relation to breast cancer risk holds significant public health implications.

Participants were grouped based on age, smoking status, alcohol consumption, diet health, breast cancer screening, and menopausal status for subgroup analyses. The results revealed significant interactions between TyG-related indices and these factors (including age, breast cancer screening, and menopausal status). Specifically, participants aged ≥ 60 exhibited steeper risk gradients across quartiles of TyG-BMI, TyG-WC, and TyG-WHtR compared to those aged < 60. Additionally, stronger associations were observed in postmenopausal women and individuals who had not undergone breast cancer screening. These findings suggested that age and hormonal status may enhance the relationship between TyG-related indices and breast cancer risk by altering metabolic levels. Potential mechanisms underlying these findings may involve several factors. First, with aging, metabolic and endocrine changes may exacerbate obesity, insulin resistance, and inflammation, which could influence TyG index levels [23, 24]. Second, postmenopausal hormonal changes may further affect fat distribution, insulin sensitivity, and inflammation levels, increasing breast cancer risk [25, 26]. Additionally, the absence of breast cancer screening may lead to fewer opportunities for early detection, thus affecting risk assessment related to TyG indices [27].

In this study, the total effects of all TyG indices on breast cancer risk were significant. CRP mediated more of the association than WBC. The relationship between TyG indices and breast cancer is mechanistically rooted in the dual pathways of IR and chronic inflammation. IR and hyperinsulinemia increase bioavailable increasing bioavailable insulin-like growth factor-1 and activate PI3K/AKT and MAPK pathways, promoting tumor growth [28]. The stronger mediation through CRP may be attributed to its well-established role as a robust marker of systemic, low-grade inflammation [29], which is closely linked to adiposity, insulin resistance, and metabolic dysregulation [30]. It is important to note that CRP primarily reflects hepatic acute-phase responses driven by cytokines like IL-6, whereas WBC represents a broader leukocytic response [31, 32]. Neither biomarker directly captures local inflammatory signaling within the breast microenvironment. This body-wide inflammatory state can promote carcinogenesis by activating pro-inflammatory pathways that lead to DNA damage, increased cell proliferation, and suppression of apoptosis [33, 34]. Notably, these pathways exhibit significant crosstalk. as IR could exacerbate adipose tissue inflammation, and inflammatory cytokines can further impair insulin signaling, creating a vicious cycle. While our findings highlight systemic inflammation, the potential interplay with local inflammation within the breast tissue, driven by factors like adipose tissue remodeling, warrants future investigation [35]. Furthermore, beyond the inflammatory pathways examined here, future research should aim to explore other potential mediators in the TyG-cancer pathway. This should include adipokines, such as adiponectin, which suppresses breast cancer growth by reprogramming fatty acid metabolism and inducing lipophagy [36], and leptin, which, in concert with adiponectin deficiency, fuels cancer progression within the obesity-associated inflammatory milieu [37]. These adipokines represent critical metabolic links between insulin resistance and oncogenesis.

Our findings have important implications for breast cancer prevention and risk stratification. The TyG-related indices, particularly the composite indices like TyG-BMI and TyG-WC, are derived from routine, low-cost measurements (blood tests and anthropometry), making them promising tools for practical public health and clinical applications. These indices could potentially be integrated into existing risk assessment models to identify high-risk subgroups, such as postmenopausal women and those aged ≥ 60, who might benefit from more intensive screening (e.g., earlier or more frequent mammography) or targeted lifestyle interventions aimed at improving metabolic health. Looking forward, several key next steps are warranted. First, our findings require external validation in more diverse and representative populations to ensure generalizability beyond the UK Biobank cohort. Second, future research should investigate the joint effects of TyG indices with genetic risk scores and other established risk factors to build more powerful integrated prediction models. Third, exploring the dynamics of these indices over time and their association with specific breast cancer molecular subtypes will provide deeper insights into their etiological and prognostic relevance.

Strengths and limitations

This study’s strengths include its large-scale prospective design and substantial sample size, which allowed for a comprehensive evaluation of the associations between TyG-related indices and breast cancer risk. The use of standardized measurements and adjustment for a wide range of covariates enhances the validity of the findings. Furthermore, consistent results across multiple sensitivity analyses support the robustness of the conclusions. However, several limitations should be noted. First, TyG-related indices were measured only at baseline, limiting assessment of temporal changes. Second, despite adjusting for a wide range of confounders, residual confounding from unmeasured or imperfectly measured factors cannot be ruled out. These include detailed dietary intake patterns, specific pharmacological treatments (e.g., detailed dosages and durations of metformin or statins), and environmental exposures, which might influence both metabolic health and breast cancer risk. Third, the participants of the UK Biobank are not fully representative of the general population, as they are predominantly of White European ancestry, healthier, and from less socioeconomically deprived areas compared to the UK general population (“healthy volunteer” selection bias). This may limit the generalizability of our findings to other ethnic and socioeconomic groups.

Conclusions

This study found that TyG-related indices were significantly associated with increased breast cancer risk. Subgroup analyses revealed significant interactions with age, menopausal status, and breast cancer screening. WBC and CRP played a key role in mediating this relationship. These findings emphasized that TyG-related indices are potential biomarkers for risk assessment and prevention strategies of breast cancer.
