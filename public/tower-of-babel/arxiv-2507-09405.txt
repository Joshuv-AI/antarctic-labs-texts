1.
Introduction

Let
t_{1},t_{2},\dots
be variables, and let
S
be the unique formal power series in these variables satisfying

(1)

S=1+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}t_{n}S^{n}.

Let

. Wildberger and Rubine
[

is divisible by

In this paper we discuss some of the properties of the Geode. In Section

which gives another proof that

1

1

Wildberger and Rubine’s inductive proof may be sketched as follows: Let

be the sum of all terms in

of degree

is

. Then
S_{1}=t_{1}+t_{2}+\cdots
, in agreement with our previous definition of

, and for

on both sides of (

is a sum of products, each of which is divisible by some

with

.

We also introduce a related power series

and

, and we show that

which implies that

has nonnegative coefficients.

In Section
3
we give combinatorial interpretations to the series

,
G
, and

, and to the formulas relating them, in terms of lattice paths. (Wildberger and Rubine worked with polygon dissections rather than lattice paths.) We represent the paths by sequences of integers greater than or equal to

and the weight of

In Section

we look at what happens when we set

In Section

we gave an indirect alternative proof of the lattice path interpretation of the Geode, using one of the standard techniques of lattice path enumeration.

2.
Formulas for the Geode

We first show the existence of a power series
G
in the variables
t_{1},t_{2},t_{3},\dots
satisfying
S=1+GS_{1}
.
(Wildberger and Rubine did not include the variable
t_{1}
as it is not so relevant to polygon dissections.)

Since the ring of formal power series in
t_{1},t_{2},\dots
(over the integers or rationals, for example) is an integral domain, we may work in its field of fractions. Thus we may always divide by a nonzero power series, even though the quotient is not guaranteed to be a power series.
We define
G
to be
(S-1)/S_{1}
, so
S=1+S_{1}G
and we define

to be

Theorem 2.1
.

We have

(2)

\displaystyle S

\displaystyle=\biggl(1-\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}t_{n}S^{n-1}\biggr)^{-1}

(3)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1-HS_{1}},

(4)

G=\biggl(1-\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}t_{n}(1+S+S^{2}+\cdots+S^{n-1})\biggr)^{-1},

and

(5)

H=\biggl(1-\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}t_{n}(S+S^{2}+\cdots+S^{n-1})\biggr)^{-1}.

Proof.

Equation (

From (

\displaystyle G

\displaystyle=(S-1)/S_{1}=1+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}t_{n}\left(\frac{S^{n}-1}{S_{1}}\right)

\displaystyle=1+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}t_{n}\frac{S-1}{S_{1}}(1+S+S^{2}+\cdots+S^{n-1})

Then (

Given (

Equation (

Their conjecture is that

G(0,-f,f,\dots,-f,f)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}k^{n}f^{n},

where there are

and

We have the following corollary of Theorem

Suppose that

3.1.
Paths and factorization

We define a path formally to be a finite sequence of integers. We represent
(s_{1},s_{2},\dots,s_{k})
geometrically as the path in the plane with vertices
(0,s_{1}),(1,s_{1}+s_{2}),\dots,(k,s_{1}+s_{2}+\cdots+s_{k})
. Thus, for example, the path
(1,1,-3,2)
is represented geometrically as in Figure
1
.

Figure 1.
Geometric depiction of the path
(1,1,-3,2)
.

The geometric representations of paths by default start at the origin (so our figures do not show coordinate axes). The entries
s_{i}
in a path
(s_{1},s_{2},\dots,s_{k})
are called the
steps
of the path.
We will often refer to paths geometrically rather than using the formal definition; for example, if we say that a path
(s_{1},\dots,s_{k})
ends on the
x
-axis, we mean that
s_{1}+\cdots+s_{k}=0
.

If
P=(p_{1},\dots,p_{m})
and
Q=(q_{1},\dots,q_{n})
are paths, then their
product

PQ
is the path
(p_{1},\dots,p_{m},q_{1},\dots,q_{n})
. Geometrically,
PQ
is obtained by translating
Q
to start at the endpoint of
P
.

The
reversal
of a path
(s_{1},\dots,s_{k})
is the path
(-s_{k},\dots,-s_{1})
. Geometrically, reversing a path is reflecting it in a vertical axis.
We call a path
nonnegative
if it never goes below the
x
-axis; i.e., every partial sum is nonnegative. (Nonnegative paths are sometimes called
meanders
.) We call a path
positive
if it stays above the
x
axis; i.e., every nonempty partial sum is positive. Note that the empty path is a positive path. We call a path
reverse-nonnegative
if it is the reversal of a nonnegative path and
reverse-positive
if it is the reversal of a positive path. (See Figure
2
.) Then reverse-nonnegative paths are paths whose endpoints are lowest points, and reverse-positive paths are paths in which the endpoint is strictly lower than every other point.

Figure 2.
The nonnegative path
(1,1,-2,1)
and its reversal
(-1,2,-1,-1)
.

The
height
of point
(m,n)
is
n
, so the height of the endpoint of the path
(s_{1},\dots,s_{k})
is
s_{1}+\cdots+s_{k}
.
An
excursion
is a path (possibly empty) that ends at height 0 and never goes below height 0. (See Figure
3
.)) Thus an excursion is a path that is both nonnegative and reverse-nonnegative.

Figure 3.
An excursion.

From here on, we assume that all steps are in the set
\{-1,0,1,2,\dots\}
. We call nonnegative steps
up steps
(so
0
is considered an up step) and we call
-1
the
down step
. It will be convenient in representing factorizations of paths by formulas to denote the up step
n
by
U_{n}
and the down step
-1
by
D
. The restriction on the step set implies that reverse-nonnegative and reverse-positive paths have very simple decompositions, but nonnegative and positive paths, which are closely related to the Geode, are more complicated.

Next, we describe the decompositions for reverse-nonnegative and reverse-positive paths that are helpful in deriving the combinatorial interpretations for
S
,
G
and
H
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
P
be a reverse-nonnegative path that ends at height
-n
.
Then
P
can be factored uniquely as
E_{1}DE_{2}D\cdots DE_{n+1}
where each
E_{i}
is an excursion and
D
is the down step
-1
.

Proof.

We proceed by induction on
n
. If
n=0
then
P
is an excursion. If
n>0
then
P
must have a first down step from height 0 to height
-1
. (This is where we use the assumption that there are no steps less than
-1
.) Cutting
P
before and after this down step shows that
P=E_{1}DQ
where
Q
is a reverse-nonnegative path that, when translated to start at the origin, ends at height
-(n-1)
. We then apply induction to
Q
.
We leave it to the reader to verify that this factorization is unique.
∎

See Figure
4
for an example of the factorization of Lemma
3.1
with
n=2
.

In Lemma
3.1
, and similarly in other factorization statements, it is understood that the converse is also true, in the sense that if
P=E_{1}DE_{2}D\cdots DE_{n+1}
where each
E_{i}
is an excursion, then
P
is a reverse-nonnegative path that ends at height
-n
.

Figure 4.
The factorization of Lemma
3.1
.

We have a variant of Lemma
3.1
that will also be needed later.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
P
be a reverse-positive path that ends at height
-n
.
Then
P
can be factored uniquely as
E_{1}DE_{2}D\cdots E_{n}D
where each
E_{i}
is an excursion and
D
is the down step
-1
.
(
If
n=0
this means that
P
is the empty path.
)

Proof.

The case
n=0
is clear, so suppose that
n>0
.
Since
P
satisfies the hypothesis of Lemma
3.1
, it can be factored uniquely as
E_{1}DE_{2}D\cdots DE_{n+1}
as in
Lemma
3.1
. But since
P
stays above height
-n
until the end, it must end with a down step, so
E_{n+1}
is empty.
∎

3.2.
Generating functions

Let us assign a weight to every step, and assign to each path the product of the weights of its steps. Then the
generating function
(also called
generating series
or
counting series
) for a set of paths is the sum of its weights, where we assume that the sum exists as a formal power series. It is clear that if
A
and
B
are disjoint sets of paths then the generating function for
A\cup B
is the sum of the generating functions of
A
and
B
, and if every element of
AB
has a unique factorization
\alpha\beta
with
\alpha\in A
and
\beta\in B
then the generating function for
AB
is the product of the generating functions of
A
and
B
.

For our applications, we take the weight of each up step
n\geq 0
to be
t_{n+1}
and we take the weight of the down step
-1
to be 1.
(We weight the up step
n
by
t_{n+1}
rather than
t_{n}
to agree with the notation of Wildberger and Rubine
[
14
]
; this also makes the defining equation (
1
) look nicer.)

Theorem 3.3
.

The series
S
is the generating function for excursions.

Proof.

Let
\tilde{S}
be the generating function for excursions. A path counting by
\tilde{S}
is either empty, with weight 1, or starts with an up step. Suppose that such a path
P
starts with the up step
n-1
. Then by Lemma
3.1
,
P
may be factored uniquely as
U_{n-1}E_{1}DE_{2}D\cdots E_{n}

where each
E_{i}
is an excursion.
Thus the sum of the weights of all excursions starting with the up step
n-1
is
t_{n}\tilde{S}^{n}
, so summing on
n
gives

\tilde{S}=1+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}t_{n}{\tilde{S}}^{n}
. Then by (
1
) and the uniqueness of the solution of this equation, we have
\tilde{S}=S
.
∎

Theorem 3.4
.

The Geode
G
is the generating function for nonnegative paths.

Proof.

Let
\tilde{G}
be the generating function for nonnegative paths. We will show that
S-1=\tilde{G}S_{1}
, which implies that
\tilde{G}=G
.

Let
E
be a nonempty excursion. Then
E
contains a last up step (recall that
0
is an up step).
Removing this last up step and the following down steps leaves a nonnegative path. Conversely, we obtain all nonempty excursions
uniquely by starting with a nonnegative path, appending an arbitrary up step, and then appending enough down steps to bring the path down to the
x
-axis. Since down steps have weight 1, we see that
S-1=\tilde{G}S_{1}
.
∎

See Figure
5
for an example of the construction described in this proof.

Figure 5.
An excursion obtained from a nonnegative path.

Note that the proof of Theorem
3.4
depends critically on down steps having weight 1; there does not seem to be such a simple formula for counting nonnegative paths where we keep track of the endpoint.

Theorem 3.5
.

The series
H
is the generating function for positive paths.

Proof.

Let
\tilde{H}
be the generating function for positive paths.
Every nonnegative path may be factored uniquely as a possibly empty excursion followed by a possibly empty positive path. (See Figure
6
for an example.)
Thus
G=S\tilde{H}
so
\tilde{H}=G/S=H
.
∎

Figure 6.
Factorization of a nonnegative path as an excursion followed by a positive path.

3.3.
Free monoids

To give combinatorial interpretations to the formulas of Theorem
2.1
, it is helpful to use the concept of free monoids.
A
monoid
is a set with an associative binary operation and an identity element.
We call an element
x
of a monoid
irreducible
if it is not the identity and cannot be factored as
yz
where neither
y
nor
z
is the identity. If every element of a monoid
M
has a unique factorization into irreducibles then
M
is called a
free monoid
. If
M
is a free monoid, we will call its irreducible elements
primes
. It is clear that the set of all paths (with any fixed set of steps), with the operation of concatenation, is a free monoid, in which the primes are the steps. As a more interesting example, the set of excursions is a free monoid, in which the primes are nonempty excursions that return to the
x
-axis for the first time at the end. These are called
arches
.

Free monoids are useful in working with generating functions for the following reason. Let
M
be a free monoid. Suppose that a weight function is defined on
M
so that the weight of an element of
M
is the product of the weights of its constituent primes. Then if
U
is the sum of the weights of all elements of
M
and
V
is the sum of the weights of all the primes of
M
, then

(8)

U=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}V^{n}=\frac{1}{1-V},

assuming that the sum converges as a formal power series.
All of the formulas of Theorem
2.1
will be seen to be instances of (
8
).

To prove one of these formulas we must first identify the set of paths counted by the left side, prove that it is a free monoid (in these cases it will obviously be a monoid), determine the primes, and find their generating function.

Although the proofs of freeness of the monoids we consider are simple enough that we could use ad hoc arguments, there is a helpful lemma that makes the proofs easier.

Let us say that a submonoid
M
of a free monoid
F
satisfies
Schützenberger’s criterion
if it has the property that for every
p
,
q
, and
r
in
F
, if
p
,
pq
,
qr
, and
r
are in
M
then
q
is in
M
. (In our applications
F
is the free monoid of all paths.)

Then we have the following lemma due to Schützenberger
[
12
, Theorem 1.4]
; see also Gessel and Li
[
7
, Lemma 3]
for a conveniently accessible proof. (We will use only the sufficiency of Schützenberger’s criterion.)

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
M
be a submonoid of a free monoid.
Then
M
is free if and only if
M
satisfies Schützenberger’s criterion.

It is easy to see directly that excursions form a free monoid, in which the primes are arches, but we can also use Schützenberger’s criterion: if
p
and
pq
are excursions then
q
is an excursion, so Schützenberger’s criterion is satisfied for excursions.

We want to show that the set of nonnegative paths and the set of positive paths also form free monoids. If
M
is either of these sets then
M
is clearly a monoid and if
qr\in M
then
q\in M
, so
M
satisfies Schützenberger’s criterion.

Similarly, the set of reverse-nonnegative paths and the set of reverse-positive paths are free monoids. Lemma
3.2
shows that the prime reverse-positive paths are the paths of the form
ED
, where
D
is an excursion. On the other hand, Lemma
3.1
does not describe the prime reverse-nonnegative paths; we leave it to the curious reader to determine what they are.

We are now ready to explain the combinatorial interpretations of the formulas of Theorem
2.1
.
For (
2
) we need to show that the generating function for arches is
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}t_{n+1}S^{n}
. The proof is similar to the proof of Theorem
3.3
. An arch must start with an up step
n
for some
n\geq 0
, followed by a reverse-positive path ending at height
-n
. Then by Lemma
3.2
, it can be factored as
U_{n}E_{1}DE_{2}D\cdots E_{n}D
. This gives the generating function for arches as

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}t_{n+1}S^{n}
.

For (
3
) we need to show that the generating function for arches may also be expressed as
HS_{1}
. The proof is very similar to that of Theorem
3.4
: every arch can be obtained uniquely by starting with a positive path, appending an arbitrary up step, and then appending enough down steps to bring the path down to the
x
-axis.

For (
4
) we need to describe a decomposition for prime nonnegative paths. Let
P
be a prime nonnegative path and suppose that
P
starts with the up step
n
so that
P
may be factored as
U_{n}Q
for some path
Q
.
Then
P
ends at height less than or equal to
n
since if not, the factors
U_{n}
and
Q
would both be nonnegative and nonempty so
P
would not be a prime. Suppose that
P
ends at height
j
, where
0\leq j\leq n
. Then there cannot be any points of
P

at a height less than or equal to
j
other than the starting and ending points, since otherwise cutting
P
at such a point would factor it into two nonempty nonnegative paths. Thus
Q
is a reverse-positive path, and we may apply Lemma
3.2
to
Q
to see that
Q
can be factored uniquely as

E_{1}DE_{2}D\cdots E_{n-j}D
where each
E_{i}
is an excursion. (If
j=n
then
Q
is the empty path.) Thus the generating function for prime nonnegative paths with these values of
n
and
j
is
t_{n+1}S^{n-j}
. Summing over
j
from 0 to
n
gives
t_{n+1}(1+S+S^{2}+\cdots+S^{n})
, so the generating function for prime nonnegative paths is

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}t_{n+1}(1+S+S^{2}+S^{n})
.

For (
5
) we describe a decomposition for prime positive paths. We omit details, as this decomposition is similar to that for prime nonnegative paths. Every prime positive path starting with the up step
n
ends at height
j
for some
j

with
1\leq j\leq n
and can be factored as
U_{n}Q
, where
Q
is reverse-nonnegative path. By Lemma
3.1
,
Q
can be factored as

E_{1}DE_{2}D\cdots DE_{n-j+1}
, where each
E_{i}
is an excursion. Thus the generating function for prime positive paths is

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}t_{n+1}(S+S^{2}+\cdots+S^{n})
.

for an example of the factorization of a prime positive path with

and

. Note that this path is not a prime nonnegative path.

4.
Catalan, Motzkin, Riordan, and Schröder numbers

The identities in Theorem
2.1
and their combinatorial interpretations are of some interest even in the case in which

t_{n}=0
for
n>2
. In this case we have the explicit formulas

(9)

\displaystyle S=\frac{1-t_{1}-\sqrt{(1-t_{1})^{2}-4t_{2}}}{2t_{2}}

(10)

\displaystyle H=\frac{1+t_{1}-\sqrt{(1-t_{1})^{2}-4t_{2}}}{2(t_{1}+t_{2})}

(11)

\displaystyle G=SH=\frac{1-t_{1}-2t_{2}-\sqrt{(1-t_{1})^{2}-4t_{2}}}{2t_{2}(t_{1}+t_{2})}=(H-1)/t_{2}.

The equation
G=(H-1)/t_{2}
is easily explained combinatorially: A nonempty positive path with steps
-1
,
0
, and
1
must consist of an up step
1
followed by a nonnegative path. This equation also implies that the coefficients of
G
are essentially the same as the coefficients of
H
, so we discuss only the coefficients of
H
below.

If we set
t_{1}=0
and
t_{2}=x
then

S=H=\frac{1-\sqrt{1-4x}}{2x},

the generating function for the Catalan numbers.

Our combinatorial interpretation for
S
reduces to the well-known combinatorial interpretation of the Catalan numbers as counting Dyck paths. The combinatorial interpretation to the Catalan numbers given by Theorem
3.4
in this case is number 28 in Stanley’s collection of Catalan number interpretations
[
13
]
. Stanley attributes the result to Emeric Deutsch and gives essentially the same proof as our proof of Theorem
3.4
in this case.

If we set
t_{1}=x
and
t_{2}=x^{2}
in Equations (

S=\frac{1-x-\sqrt{1-2x-3x^{2}}}{2x^{2}},

the generating function for the Motzkin numbers, and

H=\frac{1+x-\sqrt{1-2x-3x^{2}}}{2x(1+x)},

the generating function for Riordan numbers.

If we set
t_{1}=x
and
t_{2}=x

the generating function for the large Schröder numbers,

H=\frac{1+x-\sqrt{1-6x+x^{2}}}{4x},

the generating function for the small Schröder numbers.

Appendix: Another proof of Theorem
3.4

In this section we present another proof of Theorem
3.4
, which states that the Geode
G
is the generating function for nonnegative paths. This proof is less straightforward than the one in Section
3
but is an interesting application of lattice path enumeration techniques. It proceeds by first using an indirect method to count nonnegative paths with a nontrivial weight on down steps, and then carefully setting the weight for down steps to
1
.

We start by recalling a well-known factorization for lattice paths, sometimes called the Wiener-Hopf factorization, which applies to paths with arbitrary steps. We can factor a path into three parts (each possibly empty) by cutting the path at the first lowest point and at the last lowest point.
The first part is a reverse-positive path, the second part is an excursion, and the third part is a positive path.
An example is shown in Figure
8
. This factorization is quite useful in lattice path enumeration. See
[
2
,
3
,
4
,
6
,
9
]
for some applications.

Figure 8.
The Wiener-Hopf factorization of a path

For our problem, we want to combine the second and third parts into a nonnegative path, so we have a factorization of every path into a reverse-positive path followed by a nonnegative path.

The set of all paths is easy to count, and Lemma
3.2
allows us to count reverse-positive paths. However, there is a complication.
There is no problem in assigning down steps the weight 1 when counting nonnegative paths, since for any finite multiset of up steps, there are only finite many nonnegative paths using these up steps, no matter how many down steps they have. But for arbitrary paths, and for reverse-positive paths, there are infinitely many paths with a given multiset of up steps. So to get a generating function identity from the factorization of arbitrary paths into reverse-positive paths and nonnegative paths, we change our definition of the generating function for a set of paths: we keep the weight of
t_{i+1}
for the up step
i\geq 0
, but we change the weight of the down step
-1
from 1 to a new variable
y
. Let
S(y)
be the new generating function for excursions, so
S(1)=S
by Theorem
3.3
. Let
N(y)
be the new generating function for nonnegative paths. Then to prove Theorem
3.4
we will show that
N(1)=G
.

By Lemma
3.2
the new generating function for reverse-positive paths is
\bigl(1-S(y)y\bigr)^{-1}
. The new generating function for all paths is
(1-y-t_{1}-t_{2}-\cdots)^{-1}=(1-y-S_{1})^{-1}
. Then the Wiener-Hopf factorization gives the identity

(12)

\frac{1}{1-y-S_{1}}=\frac{1}{1-S(y)y}N(y).

We would like to determine
N(1)
from (
12
). We cannot set
y=1
in (
12
), since the result would not converge as a formal power series in
t_{1},t_{2},\dots
.
But this problem is easy to fix. We replace (
12
) with the equivalent equation

1-S(y)y=(1-y-S_{1})N(y).

Now there is no problem in setting
y=1
and we find that
1-S=-S_{1}N(1)
, so
N(1)=(S-1)/S_{1}=G
, as desired.
