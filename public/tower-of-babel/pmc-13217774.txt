Women’s experiences with diagnosis and management of vaginal symptoms of menopause: a mixed-methods study
Authors: Fiona G. Li, Deborah J. Street, Rebecca Deans, Richard De Abreu Lourenco, Jason Abbott, Jody Church
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundVaginal symptoms associated with menopause affect around 50% of women. Although the multi-factorial impact of vaginal symptoms has been well explored, experience of management and treatment preferences are poorly understood.MethodsA mixed-methods study was conducted in Australia to explore patient attitudes and experience related to the recognition and management of vaginal symptoms associated with menopause. In part 1, symptomatic post-menopausal women were recruited from hospital clinics and by word-of-mouth. Semi-structured interviews were conducted, transcribed, and coded by two researchers independently using a reflexive thematic analysis guided by interpretative phenomenological approach. In part 2, a quantitative online survey investigating attitudes towards care-seeking and management of vaginal symptoms associated with menopause in symptomatic or previously symptomatic, peri- or post-menopausal women was conducted. This study provides the context for a separate DCE investigating patient treatment preferences for this condition.ResultsData were collected from 7 semi-structured interview participants (February-September 2023) and 578 survey respondents (April-May 2024). We identified considerable variation in experience of vaginal symptoms between interview participants. Core themes were extracted from the semi-structured interviews: recognition and attitudes towards vaginal symptoms, impact of symptoms, healthcare experience, and treatment of vaginal symptoms.Although bothersome vaginal symptoms were reported by 194/349 (56%) respondents, they were less frequently reported as a respondent’s most bothersome symptom compared to other menopausal symptoms, ranked by respondents in their top three most bothersome symptoms by 83/349 (24%). Whilst 320/349 (92%) of symptomatic respondents were aware of treatments for vaginal symptoms associated with menopause, only 182/349 (52%) had tried any treatment, and 159/349 (46%) had presented to a health practitioner for this indication. There was no significant difference in rates of previous treatment or consultation with healthcare practitioner, whether vaginal symptoms were ranked in a respondent’s top three most bothersome symptoms or not.ConclusionsImpact and experience of vaginal symptoms associated with menopause is variable between individuals, necessitating personalised care. Relative bother of vaginal symptoms is not associated with rates of treatment or presentation to a healthcare practitioner for this condition and suggests other factors may influence engagement with care. Understanding patient preferences in management and treatment of vaginal symptoms associated with menopause is essential to guiding clinical practice and health funding in improving delivery of menopause care.

Introduction

Vaginal symptoms associated with menopause, which constitutes a part of the genitourinary syndrome of menopause, affect around 50% of women. [1] Over the last few decades, studies of women’s perception and experience of menopause have established that vaginal symptoms can detrimentally impact various dimensions of an individual’s quality of life. [2–4].

While few advances have been made in available treatments (predominantly non-hormonal and hormonal topical vaginal agents), there remains a limited understanding of patient preferences in the management and treatment of vaginal symptoms associated with menopause. Energy-based treatments have become widely used, but sham-controlled evidence demonstrates limited efficacy. [5] Societal attitudes and persistent stigmas surrounding menopause may present a barrier to symptom recognition and seeking treatment. [6] A better understanding of what is important to women who experience vaginal symptoms is crucial to informing development of health services and patient-centred models of care.

The aim of this study is to explore patient experience in the recognition and management of vaginal symptoms associated with menopause, using a mixed-methods approach. This will also provide the context for a discrete choice experiment, reported separately, that will aim to define patient treatment preferences in this condition.

Methods

This mixed-methods study comprised two parts: (1) qualitative interviews exploring patient preferences in the treatment of vaginal symptoms associated with menopause; and (2) online participant survey exploring previous experiences with management of vaginal symptoms associated with menopause (see Fig. 1). This study was approved by South Eastern Sydney Local Health District Ethics Committee (2022/ETH01152).

All data were collected from interview participants and survey respondents following consent (written or electronic). Data are stored on secure servers complying with University of New South Wales (UNSW) policy and de-identified for analysis and inclusion.

Study development

A literature review was performed on 24 May 2021, including qualitative studies exploring perception, concerns, or preferences in management of vaginal symptoms in post-menopausal women. From this review, [7–18] the recognised factors affecting individual’s experience of vaginal symptoms associated with menopause included: (1) expectations of the menopause experience, (2) patient knowledge of vaginal symptoms and management options; (3) availability of social support networks; (4) accessibility and type of healthcare services, referral process and waiting time (GP, gynaecology clinic, private gynaecologists) and (5) financial and emotional burden. These themes were used to develop questions for the semi-structured interviews (Table 1) and online survey.

Part 1: Qualitative – semi-structured individual participant interviews

Study design

We employed an interpretative phenomenological approach to explore the lived experience of women experiencing vaginal symptoms during menopause. This approach seeks to understand and gain deeper insights into how people make sense of a particular phenomenon (in this case, vaginal symptoms associated with menopause) through their personal experiences, capturing the essence of the condition for these women, including how they experience it physically, emotionally, and socially, and how they interpret its impact on their lives. [19] Semi-structured one-on-one interviews were considered more appropriate than focus groups due to the complexity and sensitivity of the topic.

In addition to exploring patient experiences of this condition, the results were used to provide a qualitative basis for the design of a discrete choice experiment (DCE) exploring treatment preferences for this indication, which will be conducted and reported separately. A qualitative study reporting checklist for DCEs is provided in Appendix A. [20]

Participants

Post-menopausal individuals were eligible if they had experienced vaginal symptoms severe enough to present to a health practitioner. Suitable candidates were recruited from previous research studies (with consent for further contact), word of mouth, and gynaecology clinics in public hospitals. This purposive sampling aligns with phenomenological research, which prioritises depth and richness of data over generalisability. [19] Recruitment continued until data saturation was achieved, determined when no new treatment characteristics emerged for three interviews in a row, for the purpose of informing the design of the DCE.

Procedures

Semi-structured individual interviews were conducted, allowing participants to describe their experiences with symptom management and perceptions of treatment in their own words, while giving the researcher flexibility to probe deeper into emerging themes.

Prior to commencement, participants were consented for participation and audio recording of the interviews which were to remain confidential and de-identified. Interviews were conducted by two interviewers (JC and FGL) in an encrypted online private video call room (Zoom, Zoom Video Communications Inc, San Jose, California, United States). The interview commenced with a broad, open-ended question which introduced the topic inviting the participant to tell their story. Targeted probing questions (see Table 1) were used to gather more information about symptom experience as the interview progressed if it had not arisen organically. These probes focused on the experience of diagnosis of menopause, vaginal symptoms, treatments used and perceptions of treatment efficacy.

All participants were reimbursed with a $50 AUD gift card as a token of appreciation for their time commitment and participation.

Data analysis

Interviews were transcribed and de-identified and coded by two researchers (JC and FGL) independently using reflexive thematic analysis. [21] Concepts emerging from each participant were compared and systematically categorised into themes and collated using a category tree map.

These themes were further refined into specific attributes for application in the DCE. It was important to identify all potential attributes that are important to decision making and to distinguish those that could be influenced by intervention. [22].

The analysing team included clinicians and qualitative researchers with experience in women’s health. The lead researcher’s clinical background in obstetrics and gynaecology informed the study focus and interpretation of findings. Reflexivity was supported through analytic discussions within the research team and ongoing consideration of how the researchers’ perspectives may have shaped coding and theme development.

Part 2: Quantitative online survey

Study design

To further explore women’s experiences in the management of vaginal symptoms associated with menopause, an online survey was conducted to explore experience with health practitioners and treatment options for this condition. These questions were administered online as part of the DCE survey (see Fig. 1) and immediately preceded the DCE choice sets (Part 3 of this study, which will be reported in a separate manuscript).

Participants

Any peri-menopausal and post-menopausal woman, regardless of vaginal symptom experience, was eligible for this survey (as those without experience of vaginal symptoms were included in the DCE (Part 3) of this study), but only data from those with previous or current vaginal symptoms were included in this mixed-methods study. An age limitation of 35 years and above was implemented to reduce the risk of inauthentic responses whilst still capturing a clinically representative population.

Procedures

Survey hosting and recruitment was performed by Pureprofile, a global consumer data and insights organisation with an online membership panel who are financially reimbursed for their participation and insights, based in Sydney (New South Wales, Australia). Respondents were recruited online from the Pureprofile panel and compensated for their time by Pureprofile upon completion of the survey.

Outcomes

The online survey collected demographic and socioeconomic characteristics relevant to the study population; health- and menopause-related characteristics (including medical history, current social factors, and sexual activity); previous health practitioners consulted for vaginal symptoms of menopause; experiences of menopausal symptoms (systemic, current, or previous); and rankings of the three most bothersome symptoms from a predefined list, with an option to specify other symptoms via open text response).

Statistical analysis

Demographics are reported as frequencies (n, %) for categorical variables and means ± standard deviations (SD) for continuous variables. Categorical variables were analysed using a Pearson Chi-square test, or Fisher’s exact test when cell counts were < 5 in any category. Parametric continuous variables were analysed using independent t-tests or one-way ANOVA, as appropriate.

Results

Part 1: Qualitative – semi-structured interviews

Of the 12 post-menopausal women approached for semi-structured interviews, 3 did not respond, 1 was not interested in participating in an interview, and 1 was excluded due to other severe concurrent genitourinary symptoms. Qualitative semi-structured interviews were conducted between February to September 2023 with 7 participants aged 44–72 years, who were 2 to 17 years post-menopause. Mechanism of menopause included spontaneous (n = 4) and iatrogenic following breast cancer (n = 3). Mean interview length was 43 ± 10 min.

Theme 1: Recognition and attitudes towards vaginal symptoms associated with menopause

In the one-on-one interviews, participants described variation in initial experience and recognition of vaginal symptoms, however, the majority reported encountering some form of barrier that contributed to subsequent delays in seeking help or treatment.

Many participants did not recognise vaginal symptoms as a part of menopause, attributing them to other factors including ageing, lifestyle, and side effects of cancer treatment in the oncological survivors (see Table 2, 3.1.1A, including participant quotes supporting each theme). Symptom severity varied in prior expectation and experience of symptoms (see Table 2, 3.1.1B).

For many, there was a reluctance to discuss menopause due to cultural expectations, embarrassment, and negative associations with ageing (see Table 2, 3.1.1 C). The stigma of menopause and ageing was particularly noted by women undergoing premature or iatrogenic menopause at an earlier age. Several participants did not seek help unless prompted by a health practitioner. This was often due to delayed recognition of symptoms as a treatable part of menopause (see Table 2, 3.1.1D).

Theme 2: Impact of symptoms

Participants described varying impacts of vaginal symptoms beyond physical discomfort, with potentially detrimental and far-reaching impacts on intercourse, intimacy, relationship stability, and psychosocial stability. The physical symptoms predominantly described were vaginal dryness and dyspareunia to varying degrees, leading to avoidance or reduced enjoyment of sex.

Some expressed a determination to persist through discomfort, so to minimise the impact on their intimate partners. Respondents perceived further impact of vaginal symptoms on intimate relationships, which in some situations contributed to strain and termination of relationships, and impacted psychosocial security (see Table 2, 3.1.2 A). For many, these physical symptoms can have a wider impact beyond discomfort, influencing body image perception, sexual interest, and sexual activity (see Table 2, 3.1.2B-C).

For most participants, systemic menopause symptoms were perceived to be more severe and difficult to manage. Some women viewed vaginal symptoms as less bothersome compared to other menopausal symptoms, such as cognitive decline, hot flushes, and fatigue (see Table 2, 3.1.2D) but for some, this shifted over time.

Theme 3: The healthcare experience

Where symptoms were bothersome, respondents often felt an embarrassment or reluctance to discuss vaginal symptoms with health practitioners. Many respondents described a self-perceived discomfort, or perception of practitioner discomfort, which was a feeling more frequently present with male health practitioners (see Table 2, 3.1.3 A). This stigma remained for some respondents even when referred to gynaecologists or menopause specialists.

Respondents also described experiences of feeling dismissed by doctors when seeking help for vaginal symptoms (see Table 2, 3.1.3B). Some participants felt that they were seen by doctors who were inadequately educated regarding menopause management, leading to delays in recognition and management of symptoms (see Table 2, 3.1.3 C). Many respondents sought information instead through peers and word-of-mouth (see Table 2, 3.1.3D).

However, respondents expressed a recent shift in practice with practitioners creating more supportive spaces, particularly within dedicated menopause centres and by oncologists. Respondents also sought support groups to share their experiences and exchange information. (see Table 2, 3.1.3E)

Theme 4: Experience of treatments for vaginal symptoms associated with menopause

There was considerable variability in participants’ experiences with treatments for vaginal symptoms.

Administration and efficacy (See Table 2, 3.1.4 A).

Frequent application and short duration of relief provided by topical therapies was more acceptable to some individuals than others. Participants balanced how easy a treatment was to use with how effective it was. Daily or frequent treatments (lubricants, creams, pessaries) were seen as burdensome, especially if they were messy or time-consuming, where others did not find the routine difficult to maintain. Procedural treatments (vaginal laser, vaginal injections, physiotherapy) were perceived as more convenient in the long run, despite upfront effort and cost. Participants preferred treatments that didn’t interfere with daily life or intimacy.

Perceived efficacy varied widely between individuals. Some found hormonal creams or laser therapy life-changing, while others saw little to no improvement. Many participants described a frustrating trial-and-error process, switching between lubricants, hormonal creams, or alternative therapies. Treatment side effects (e.g., irritation, discharge, pain) presented a barrier to compliance.

Hormones (See Table 2, 3.1.4 B).

For many, concerns about hormone-related cancer risk significantly influenced their willingness to use hormonal treatments (topical or systemic oestrogen). Breast cancer survivors were often highly cautious and avoided hormonal treatments unless symptoms were debilitating. Even those without a history of cancer were influenced by family experiences and media coverage of perceived breast cancer secondary to hormonal therapies. Conflicting or uncertain advice from doctors regarding safety contributed to patient hesitancy, particularly in participants with a history of breast cancer.

Cost (See Table 2, 3.1.4 C).

Treatments were often assessed in terms of cost vs. benefit, particularly for ‘expensive’ options like vaginal laser therapy. High out-of-pocket expenses discouraged some individuals from trying newer treatments.

Discussion

This mixed-methods study explores the nuances and variation between individuals’ experiences of vaginal symptoms in the context of the broader menopause experience, to gain an insight into current attitudes towards management. This provides essential context for the development and interpretation of a discrete choice experiment that explores patient preferences in treatment of vaginal symptoms associated with menopause.

Our qualitative study revealed considerable variation in the impact and experience of vaginal symptoms associated with menopause between individuals. For some, the impact of their vaginal symptoms was detrimental and pervasive, leading to emotional strain, relationship breakdown, and loss of familial or financial support. However, in the quantitative study, although 55% of symptomatic respondents reported vaginal symptoms as bothersome, they were ranked lower compared with other menopause symptoms for level of bother. This contributes to previous data suggesting that the level of symptom severity and the multi-factorial impacts on physical comfort and emotional wellbeing, sexual satisfaction and sexuality, self-image, and inter-personal relationships can vary greatly. [8, 12, 15, 17, 18] Interview participants described variability in efficacy and acceptability of treatments for vaginal symptoms. Evidence demonstrates that treatment effectiveness for vaginal symptoms may vary between individuals, including vaginal oestrogen [23] or procedures such as vaginal laser, [24] but acceptability of treatment to individuals is not well understood. Determining the decision-making factors and trade-offs women make in the treatment of vaginal symptoms associated with menopause is a logical progression to facilitate effective change in menopausal symptom management.

In this study, although 92% of symptomatic individuals were aware of treatments for vaginal symptoms, only 52% had tried treatments in the past. The discrepancy between awareness and care-seeking for vaginal symptoms associated with menopause has been identified in previous surveys. For example, a 2009 survey of over 4000 post-menopausal women in Europe and the USA found that 60% of women felt reluctant discussing vaginal symptoms with their health practitioner due to embarrassment, [2] and a 2016 survey from the USA of 1858 women with symptoms of vulvovaginal atrophy similarly reported that 44% of women had discussed their vaginal symptoms with their health practitioner. [3].

As aforementioned, lower symptom severity or bother may contribute to why some women do not consult health practitioners for this indication. In this study, perceived bother of vaginal symptoms was not associated with engagement with treatment or healthcare practitioners, although this study did not explore absolute symptom severity and need for treatment. Interview participants perceived other barriers to care-seeking, including stigma surrounding vaginal symptoms and sexual health, discomfort with practitioners (particularly male), and a lack of sensitivity or recognition of the cause, severity or impact of symptoms by practitioners. These barriers experienced by interview participants align with issues reported in previous qualitative studies. [3, 11, 17, 18] In a 2024 survey of 520 participants with vulvodynia, 27% reported clinicians demonstrating belittling behaviours and 21% reported not being believed, which identified that lack of widespread knowledge, challenging diagnoses, and dismissive behaviours by clinicians that were more likely to lead to patient disengagement or present a barrier to patient care. [25] How much of this gap between awareness and uptake of treatment may be accounted for by these barriers, or by patient preference to self-manage symptoms will be further explored in a subsequent DCE.

Survey respondents in our study perceived that practitioners had an overall strong understanding of management of vaginal symptoms associated with menopause, with the greatest confidence in gynaecologists, followed by primary care practitioners, and allied health or non-medical practitioners. Participants also reported that conflicting messaging around treatment options from health practitioners can be confusing for patients, particularly regarding the safety of topical hormones, which is consistent with previous qualitative studies, [7, 12, 15, 17]. A 2025 RCT of 116 sexually active post-menopausal women comparing proximal and distal vaginal oestrogen with lubricant demonstrated no significant difference between groups in serum oestrogen concentration. [26] This finding is consistent with other studies suggesting low serum oestrogen levels following vaginal oestrogen use [27] and adds to growing evidence to support the safety of vaginal oestrogen in patients with a history of breast cancer. [28] Robust evidence to support interdisciplinary consensus and guidelines, along with education and training to empower health practitioners (especially to improve consistency in primary care, who are the most frequently consulted) is crucial to improving individualised care in management of menopause symptoms.

Furthermore, vaginal symptoms may be more detrimental in certain groups such as oncological survivors. Although prospective studies have previously suggested that vaginal symptoms are not more severe in oncological survivors, [29] women may experience a greater impact in the context of an unexpectedly early menopause and sexual dysfunction. [30], [31] This is complicated by the complexities of a cancer diagnosis and management in a society with ageist tendencies, [7, 11] which aligned with the themes expressed by our interview participants. However, interview participants observed a shift in practice and particularly valued clinician proactivity regarding symptoms, including counselling by oncologists and active educational support groups for oncology patients. Access to multi-disciplinary and culturally sensitive services, along with public health education to destigmatise menopause and support women and their support people (including intimate partners), may help reduce the impact of these symptoms.

Key strengths of this study are the inclusion of a diverse sample, particularly by age, demographic background and of both natural and iatrogenic menopause, in both the interview participants and survey respondents. However, given the recruitment method was through an online consumer panel, there is the potential for selection bias towards individuals with greater health and digital literacy. External validity may also be limited by our Australian context, where a universal healthcare system and hybrid service models subsidise essential medications (e.g., vaginal oestrogen formulations) to improve accessibility. Similarly, results of this study may not be generalisable to other specialty healthcare groups such as oncologists who are uniquely positioned to discuss adverse outcomes of iatrogenic menopause secondary to oncological treatment, an area that was not specifically explored in this study.

While the small qualitative sample size is a limitation, the interview component of this study aimed to inform the design of a DCE for which it achieved data saturation. It also provides the opportunity to correlate information using a mixed-methods approach for a broader and deeper understanding of women’s experiences in the management of vaginal symptoms associated with menopause. Insights from this work will inform the development of a DCE to explore individual preferences and trade-offs (including willingness-to-pay) for management of vaginal symptoms, thereby strengthening the evidence base for treatment options.

Conclusion

Vaginal symptoms associated with menopause are prevalent and may be detrimental, but experience with symptoms, impact and recognition varies greatly between individuals which necessitates personalised care. Understanding patient preference in the management of vaginal symptoms, by determining decision-making factors and trade-offs in the treatment of this condition, is an essential next step to informing patient-centred menopause care.
