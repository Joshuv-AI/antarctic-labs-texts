A modular APEX biosensor enables sensitive and visualized monitoring of viral protease activity
Authors: Biao Li, Bao Dong, Yuehong Chen, Jing Li, Xiaoping Kang, Yuchang Li, Sen Zhang, Jiaqi Xiong, Zhengwei Zhang, Jun Hu, Shuimeng Song, Shenghai Huang, Ye Feng, Tao Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Viral proteases are key targets for the development of broad-spectrum antiviral drugs development. However, screening platform capable of accurately assessing inhibitor activity within physiologically relevant cellular environments remain urgently needed. Traditional methods, such as fluorescent protein assays and Förster resonance energy transfer (FRET), suffer from significant limitations, including susceptibility to non-specific conformational interference by test compounds and an inability to faithfully reflect intracellular inhibitory effects. To address these challenges, we constructed two modular biosensors (TS3AR and C3SIR) based on engineered ascorbate peroxidase (APEX). Their detection mechanism relies on specific cleavage of the substrate recognition sequence by the target protease, which triggers the reassembly of split APEX fragments, restores enzymatic activity, and generates fluorescent signals generated via cascade amplification reaction. Validation using the coronavirus main protease (Mpro) as a model showed that the TS3AR sensor achieved the signal-to-noise ratio up to 1500-fold for enzyme activity detection, while the C3SIR sensor effectively avoided the false positives caused by conformational interference seen in traditional methods and accurately identified high-potency Mpro inhibitors, including enstrelvir, PF-00835231, and nirmatrelvir. Moreover, by replacing the protease recognition sequence, these modular biosensors can be flexibly adapted for activity analysis and drug evaluation of Mpro from various coronaviruses (e.g., SARS-CoV-2, MERS-CoV) as well as other viral proteases (e.g., enterovirus 71, Epstein-Barr virus and hepatitis A virus). Overall, this platform provides a reliable, highly specific intracellular screening tool to accelerate the development of broad-spectrum therapeutics against both emerging and existing viral threats.

Introduction

Proteases are core biological catalysts that mediate specific proteolytic reactions and play pivotal roles exerted throughout the viral life cycle. Proteases encoded by viral genomes—including the main protease (Mpro) (Zhang, et al., 2024) protease of coronaviruses and the protease of human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) (Berlec et al., 2018)—mediate the precise cleavage of viral polyprotein precursors during viral replication within host cells, thereby releasing mature functional viral proteins and driving viral genomic replication as well as structural protein assembly. This proteolytic process constitutes an indispensable step for viral replication and the production of infectious progeny virions. Thus, owing to the essentiality of their biological functions and the high conservation of their active sites across related viruses, viral proteases have been identified as key targets for the development of broad-spectrum antiviral drugs (Ran et al., 2025).

Activity detection technologies with real-time capability, high sensitivity and adaptability to physiological environments are essential for the in-depth investigation of viral protease functions and the identification of targeted inhibitors. To date, biosensors based on fluorescent protein reassembly (e.g., FlipGFP) (Zhang, et al., 2019; Ma et al., 2022) or bioluminescent energy transfer (e.g., FlipNluc) (Woo et al., 2020) have been extensively developed and applied in related research. However, these conventional methods still face distinct bottlenecks in practical applications: fluorescence-based systems are susceptible to autofluorescence interference, which elevates background signals and reduced signal-to-noise ratios, precluding reliable detection of weak enzymatic activity. In contrast, biofluorescence-based systems, despite the relatively high sensitivity they exhibit, cannot provide spatial localization information on protease activity at subcellular structures (Scott et al., 2011). These limitations severely impeded the precise and dynamic dissection of endogenous, low-abundance protease activity in complex cellular environments.

In response to the limitations of current protease activity detection technologies in sensitivity, specificity and dynamic monitoring, we developed a modular and programmable protease activity biosensor in this study based on an engineered ascorbate peroxidase (APEX) platform. APEX is a variant of soybean ascorbate peroxidase obtained through directed evolution (e.g., APEX2/APEX3) (Becker et al., 2023). Compared with traditional horseradish peroxidase (HRP) (Liu et al., 2023), APEX retains high catalytic activity in the reductive intracellular environment of living cells (Takashima et al., 2024), and exhibits better expression stability and lower background signals. Thus, it serves a key tool for live-cell protein labeling, proximity-dependent biotinylation and the detection of dynamic molecular events. On this basis, we adopted a split-protein strategy, dividing the APEX enzyme into two independently expressed structural units without catalytic activity: the N-terminal fragment (AP) and the C-terminal fragment (EX). These two fragments show no enzymatic activity when present alone, while reassembly into a complex with complete peroxidase activity is achieved under the drive of specific molecular interactions (Han et al., 2019). Through the introduction of a conformation-locking mechanism and a protease-dependent reassembly mechanism, the system achieves highly sensitive and low-background detection of protease cleavage events. Specifically, a sequence recognizable by specific proteases is inserted between the AP and EX fragments, with their spontaneous reassembly inhibited via structural constraints. Only after the cleavage by the target protease occurs are the AP and EX fragments released from inhibition, whereupon them to reassemble and restore enzymatic activity, thereby converting protease activity into a detectable signal output. The modular architecture of the system allows flexible and rapid adaptation to proteases from different sources. Conversion of detection targets is achieved simply by replacing the protease recognition sequence, and the system shows good technical potential and application versatility in addressing emerging viral infectious diseases.

A modular biosensor system based on APEX was presented in the present study, with detection sensitivity enhanced remarkably via a cascade signal amplification mechanism. A design incorporating interchangeable protease recognition sequences was adopted by this system, with rapid adaptation to a variety of viral proteases achieved thereby. Two optimized versions of the system were constructed: a signal-to-background ratio of approximately 1500-fold was exhibited by the TS3AR system in the detection of coronaviral Mpro, whereas approximately 3000-fold was achieved by the C3SIR system within a shorter time frame, with superior response timeliness demonstrated thereby. The capacity of the system for the detection of SARS-CoV-2 Mpro was validated, with its application successfully extended to the activity detection of a variety of viral proteases including EV-71 3C protease (Guo et al., 2014), EBV BVRF2 protease and HAV 3C protease (Pena-Francesch et al., 2023). These results confirm its practical potential in the dynamic monitoring of living cells and the high-throughput screening of inhibitors. High sensitivity, robust specificity, stable response and favorable reproducibility are exhibited by this platform. A universal and flexible research tool was provided by the modular design of the platform for the activity analysis of various pathogenic viral proteases and the development of antiviral drugs.

Results

Establishment of highly sensitive TS3AR and C3SIR protease biosensors

The fluorescent protease activity biosensor based on split-ascorbate peroxidase (split-APEX) was developed in the present study, on the basis of the CLAPon protease biosensor reported by Zhou et al. (Zhou, et al., 2022). As illustrated in Fig. 1A, the core of this system resided in the division of the APEX enzyme into the N-terminal “AP” fragment (200 amino acids) and the C-terminal “EX” fragment (50 amino acids). The EX fragment was locked in a cyclic conformation via the E5/K5 heterodimer, with spontaneous binding between the EX and AP fragments abrogated thereby. Only upon the recognition and cleavage of the specific intercalated site by the target protease was the EX fragment released, with subsequent reassembly into a complex with intact peroxidase activity achieved via its combination with the AP fragment. Protease activity detection was thus realized through a substrate chromogenic reaction. A DD destabilization domain was introduced at the N-terminus of the reporter sequence, to attain further background reduction and signal-to-noise ratio enhancement. Compared with the system lacking the DD sequence, significant background signal reduction and signal-to-noise ratio improvement were achieved by this design, as shown in Supplementary Fig. S1A. Further enhancement of the system's application efficacy in antiviral inhibitor screening was achieved via its structural integration and optimization in the present study. As illustrated in Fig. 2A, the coding sequence of the coronavirus main protease (Mpro) and its specific cleavage site were co-constructed into a single plasmid together with the aforementioned optimized APEX reporter module (containing the AP and EX fragments), with co-expression in 293T cells realized thereby. In this design, in the absence of inhibitors, the specific cleavage site was cleaved by Mpro endogenously, with the reassembly of the AP and EX fragments abrogated thereby. In contrast, in the presence of inhibitors, Mpro activity was suppressed and the cleavage reaction was blocked, with the AP and EX fragments undergoing reassembly to restore intact enzymatic activity. The inhibitory effect was thus reflected via fluorescent signals accordingly. This structural integration simplified the transfection procedure and facilitated the implementation of high-throughput screening. The TS3AR and C3SIR systems were separately transfected into HEK293T cells for expression, with corresponding negative control groups established in parallel: coronavirus Mpro was omitted in the TS3AR group, and no inhibitors were added in the C3SIR group. System performance was assessed via fluorescent signal detection, with the results presented in Fig. 1B and 2B, and a significant signal difference exhibited between the experimental groups and the negative control groups. Quantitative analyses (Fig. 1D and 2C) demonstrated that a signal-to-noise ratio of approximately 1500-fold was achieved by the TS3AR system, and approximately 3200-fold by the C3SIR system, with excellent signal-to-noise ratio characteristics exhibited by both systems thereby. To eliminate the potential influence of cell number differences on fluorescent signals, cell number uniformity among all groups was further verified in this study. Post-transfection cell counting results showed that the cell numbers of all groups ranged steadily from 5.2 × 104 to 6.2 × 104. No obvious fluctuations were observed in basal cell quantities among groups, and overall baseline cell density remained stable and controllable. Bright-field microscopic images in Supplementary Fig. S1C and S1D further confirmed similar cell distribution and seeding density in all groups.

The dynamic detection performance of the biosensors was systematically evaluated via a time-lapse imaging system in this study. As shown in Fig. 1G, fluorescent signals of the TS3AR system appeared at 12 h post-transfection and increased gradually with extended culture time, with its signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) peaking at 24 h with an SNR of approximately 1500 (Fig. 1E). However, further extension of culture time caused an increase in background signals in the control group, which in turn led to a decrease in SNR. In contrast, the C3SIR system showed faster dynamic response characteristics: its fluorescent signals were detectable at 4 h post-transfection (Fig. 2F). As further quantified in Fig. 2D, the SNR rose continuously over time and reached a stable plateau at 16 h. Although the SNR of the TS3AR system was generally lower than that of the C3SIR system in temporal response, the TS3AR system exhibited excellent performance in detection sensitivity. As shown in Fig. 1H, stable and distinguishable fluorescent signals were still generated by the system even when the concentration of coronaviral Mpro protein was diluted to 12.5 ng (equivalent to 1/8 of the original concentration). As further illustrated in Fig. 1F, quantitative analysis revealed that the TS3AR system maintained ultrahigh detection sensitivity at very low expression levels of the target protease.

Subsequently, the functionality of the biosensors was validated via Western blot (WB) in HEK293T cells with target protein expression. For the TS3AR system (Fig. 1C), specific bands at the expected molecular weight were detected via WB following co-transfection with TEV, a non-coronaviral protease. In contrast, no corresponding bands were observed at this position upon co-transfection with the coronaviral Mpro, demonstrating that the TS3AR system underwent specific cleavage by Mpro, with no cross-reactivity with non-target proteases detected in the assay. For the C3SIR system (Fig. 2E), the expected bands were detected in experimental groups with Mpro inhibitor supplementation, whereas no such bands were observed in control groups without inhibitor treatment. These results confirmed that autocleavage of the system was mediated by Mpro, with this proteolytic process effectively blocked by the corresponding inhibitors, thereby demonstrating the system's distinct response specificity to Mpro inhibitors.

Validation of the broad-spectrum activity and specificity of the biosensor

A series of experiments were performed with the biosensor, and its specificity and broad-spectrum response to target proteases were systematically evaluated. Responses of the system to viral proteases with different structural features were investigated via the construction of corresponding expression vectors. Expression vectors for the tobacco etch virus protease and a catalytically inactive mutant of the coronavirus main protease (Mpro) were also constructed. Binding specificity between the biosensor and the proteases was examined. Specific fluorescent signals were detected only in the presence of wild-type coronavirus Mpro, as shown in Fig. 3A. No obvious signal response was observed in groups transfected with the tobacco etch virus protease or the catalytically inactive Mpro mutant. Quantitative fluorescence results are presented in Fig. 3B, which further confirmed the high specificity of the biosensor for coronavirus Mpro. The application scope of the system was expanded via the construction of expression vectors for Mpro of human coronaviruses with different genotypes. Phylogenetic analysis and identification revealed that the catalytic domain of coronavirus Mpro was highly conserved across different genotypes (Fig. 3G). Experimental results showed that the sensor detected Mpro from all six major human coronaviruses and generated specific fluorescent signals (Fig. 3C). Quantitative analysis showed that a signal-to-noise ratio of approximately 700-fold was detected even for Mpro from the MERS coronavirus, which exhibited the weakest signal response (Fig. 3D). Specificity of the C3SIR biosensor in the recognition of Mpro inhibitors was further verified via systematic evaluation with mutation experiments. Mutants of the biosensor with a cleavage site deletion (C3SIR-Mpro cs) and a catalytically inactive Mpro C145A mutant [C3SIR-Mpro(C145A)] were constructed separately in the experiments. Groups treated with the coronavirus inhibitor GC376 were set as the experimental group, and groups without inhibitor addition were set as the control group. Signal responses were detected separately in both groups. Specific fluorescent signals were not observed only in the negative control group without Mpro, as shown in Fig. 3E. Effective signal responses were detected in both the Mpro C145A mutant group and the biosensor cleavage site mutant group. Significantly enhanced fluorescent signals were observed in the experimental group treated with GC376. Quantitative analysis results (Fig. 3F) further confirmed that the signal output of the C3SIR biosensor relied on specific cleavage by Mpro. In addition, detection applicability of the system for different coronaviruses was expanded via modular modification of the biosensor. Mpro recognition sequences in the reporter plasmid were replaced with specific recognition sequences of two human coronaviruses (229E and HKU1) (Fig. 3H). Results of validation experiments showed that the optimized biosensor generated stable specific fluorescent signals (Fig. 3I), which indicated that the system had the potential for broad-spectrum coronavirus detection via sequence adaptation.

Validation of cross-enzyme applicability of the biosensor in other viral proteases

Application of the constructed biosensor in detection of activities of these diverse viral proteases or drug validation was verified. Customized biosensors were designed for each virus. Corresponding cleavage sequences of each protease were integrated into these systems. Each protease and its corresponding cleavage sequence are presented in Fig. 4B. Background signals were evaluated when the TS3AR sensor was co-transfected with expression vectors encoding the corresponding proteases. Visible fluorescent signals were detected for all viral reporter genes, as shown in Fig. 4A. Quantitative analysis of reporter signals (Fig. 4C) indicated that fluorescence intensity of all viral reporter genes was significantly increased relative to background levels. The HAV reporter gene showed the highest signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) of approximately 1200 compared with the blank control. Strong SNRs of 700 and 1100 were observed for the EBV and EV-71 reporter genes, respectively. Validation of the EV71 enterovirus 3C protease was performed with the C3SIR sensor-based system. Visible fluorescent signals were generated by the biosensor, as shown in Fig. 4D. Quantitative analysis of these results was presented in Fig. 4E. Drug concentrations were tested in a gradient manner, which met the requirements for drug screening applications. Application of the biosensor in drug activity evaluation

In this study, inhibitory activities of candidate compounds against Mpro were evaluated with the TS3AR and C3SIR biosensors in a coronavirus-expressing cell model, and the experimental workflow is illustrated in Fig. 5A. TS3AR relied on a signal-decrease mechanism and allowed flexible detection of diverse Mpro variants via co-transfection. C3SIR was based on a signal-increase mechanism and adopted a single-plasmid design with simpler operation. Stable and strong inhibitory effects of two well-characterized broad-spectrum Mpro inhibitors, lufotrelvir and GC376, were observed in both sensors, as shown in Fig. 5B and 6A. IC50 values of 2.852 μM (Lufotrelvir) and 2.609 μM (GC376) were determined with TS3AR (Fig. 5C). IC50 values of 1.865 μM and 2.035 μM were obtained with C3SIR (Fig. 6B). These values matched published reports and confirmed the reliability of the experimental system. Notably, specific inhibitory signals induced by Ebselen were not detected in the sensor platform of this study, even though previous FRET-based experiments suggested inhibitory potential of Ebselen against SARS-CoV-2 Mpro (Fig. 5C and 6B). This result aligned with recent studies that classified Ebselen as a non-specific inhibitor of Mpro. Integration of signal-decrease and signal-increase strategies effectively eliminated interference from such non-specific compounds. Screening of Mpro inhibitors and evaluation of inhibition reversal based on the C3SIR biosensor

High-throughput drug screening was performed via the C3SIR biosensor, with the robustness of the system in high-throughput screening evaluated accordingly. Discussion

Viral proteases served critical roles in the viral life cycle. Viral polyprotein precursors are specifically cleaved by such proteases, and functionally mature viral proteins are produced accordingly (Costanzo, 2025). Given the high conservation of this function across a wide range of viruses, viral proteases were established as key targets for the development of broad-spectrum antiviral drugs. Studies of viral protease activity not only enhance understanding of viral replication and infection mechanisms, but also represent an important tool for antiviral drug screening. However, conventional methods used for protease activity detection possessed clear limitations. Luciferase-based biosensors typically required cell lysis and did not support the acquisition of subcellular localization information. In comparison, fluorescence protein-based approaches enabled subcellular localization, but their spatial resolution was limited by the optical diffraction limit, and problems such as low signal-to-noise ratios were commonly encountered (Scott et al., 2011). In response to these limitations, two APEX-based biosensors for protease activity, designated TS3AR and C3SIR, were constructed in this study. The TS3AR system allowed highly sensitive and specific detection of protease activity. The C3SIR system employed a single-plasmid design and was suitable for high-throughput screening of protease inhibitors. Both systems presented multiple key advantages: low background signal, high sensitivity, strong specificity, modular design, and high spatial resolution. The modular architecture of these systems permitted rapid adaptation to proteases derived from different viruses through replacement of the substrate-recognition element. A continuous research platform was thereby established that covered both protease activity detection and inhibitor screening. This platform supported rapid functional characterization of proteases from emerging viruses and could be directly used in high-throughput screening of targeted inhibitors. The interval from viral identification to drug development was thus significantly shortened.

The biosensors were constructed on the basis of a mechanism defined as “sAPEX–protease-specific cleavage-mediated recombination”. These biosensors were developed from the CLAP on system, which was established by Zhou et al. and applied in the detection of protease activity and protein–protein interactions (Zhou, et al., 2022). The simple construction mode with direct fusion of full-length APEX and DD was abandoned in the TS3AR system of this study, and designs based on split APEX were adopted instead (Nicholls et al., 2011). The DD domain served to enhance the structural stability of the E5/K5 dimeric ring and reduced nonspecific background signals by minimizing EX-AP recombination (Leonard et al., 2023). Non-specific spontaneous assembly of the reporter module within cells was effectively suppressed, and background signals were maintained at an extremely low level. A key design choice in our system is the use of a split-APEX (sAPEX) configuration rather than simply fusing DD to full-length APEX. In a full-length APEX+DD design, the protease-cleaved fragments could retain residual activity, limiting the achievable signal-to-background ratio. By contrast, our split-APEX design allowed the two fragments to reassemble into an enzymatically active APEX only after protease cleavage, thereby generating a sharp signal switch from “off” to “on”. Without the DD domain, the biosensor exhibited a signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) of less than 3-fold (Supplementary Fig. S1B). After DD fusion and initial optimization, the SNR was increased to approximately 390-fold. Following further integration of the protease and its cleavage site into the sAPEX system within a single expression cassette, the C3SIR biosensor achieved an SNR of approximately 3200-fold, representing a substantial improvement over both the unoptimized system and conventional dual-plasmid designs. On this basis, the protease and its specific cleavage site were further integrated with the sAPEX system into a single expression cassette within one plasmid, and the C3SIR biosensor was established. This system was designed in contrast to conventional dual-reporter or split-fragment dual-plasmid systems (Berlec et al., 2018). Transfection of the single-plasmid biosensor was simple and convenient, and no imbalance in transfection efficiency among different plasmids occurred. The C3SIR biosensor enabled rapid evaluation of protease inhibitors by monitoring signal changes when protease self-cleavage was blocked. Thus, it provided a promising functional platform supporting high-throughput drug screening.

The APEX-based biosensor established in this study exhibited strong signal amplification capacity. This signal amplification relied on a protease-triggered cascade reaction. Protease cleavage within the biosensor drove the reconstitution of APEX. The peroxidase activity of reconstituted APEX efficiently catalyzed target substrates and generated resorufin, and signal amplification of 39- to 390-fold was achieved (Han et al., 2019). This feature enabled detection of protease activity at low abundance. The present system outperformed fluorescent protein sensors with low signal-to-noise ratios and long expression requirements in both detection speed and spatial resolution. Previous studies reported that effective fluorescence detection with GFP as a reporter protein often required 6–24 h. Strong signals were generated by the APEX biosensor via enzymatic reactions within several hours. In the detection of coronavirus Mpro protease activity, fluorescent signals were detected with the TS3AR biosensor even at a transfected amount as low as 12.5 ng in 48-well plates, and signal readout was accomplished within 12 h after transfection. The C3SIR biosensor enabled fluorescent signal detection as early as 4 h post-transfection. These systems effectively overcame the limitations of conventional fluorescent protein biosensors, including insufficient sensitivity, slow fluorophore maturation, and cell cytotoxicity caused by overexpression (Kleeman et al., 2018). The APEX biosensor constructed in this study catalyzed the conversion of Amplex UltraRed substrate into the fluorescent product resorufin with high efficiency following activation. Fluorescence imaging data obtained in HEK 293T cells showed that this signal was suitable for observation under high-resolution microscopy and permitted in the situ cellular localization of protease activity (Kaewsapsak et al., 2017).

The APEX biosensor constructed in this study catalyzed the conversion of the Amplex UltraRed substrate into the fluorescent product resorufin with high efficiency following activation. Fluorescence imaging data obtained in HEK 293T cells showed that this signal was suitable for observation under high-resolution microscopy and permitted the in situ cellular localization of protease activity. In addition, stable catalytic activity was maintained by this system in the reducing environment of living cells, and in situ localization of activity at the cellular level was achieved (Dong et al., 2025). Universal detection of Mpro from all seven known human-infective coronaviruses was achieved with this system. Compared with reported SARS-CoV-2 detection platforms (Senapati et al., 2025), the present system also showed advantages in viral coverage. Experiments showed that signal-to-noise ratios above 700 were maintained in the detection of these seven coronaviruses, even for the MERS coronavirus, which yields the lowest ratio among these strains. High specificity was also observed. Validation with the catalytically inactive coronavirus C145A mutant and non-coronavirus Mpro proteins confirmed clear discrimination between active Mpro, inactive proteases, and non-target proteases. False positive signals caused by protease inactivation via mutation or interference from other viral proteases were thus avoided. Broad-spectrum utility of this system stemmed from its modular design. Rapid adaptation to diverse viral protease targets is accomplished through substitution of the protease recognition sequence. A universal and expandable technical platform was thereby provided for detection of various viral protease activities. The TS3AR biosensor system was successfully applied to detection of protease activity across diverse viruses and generated favorable signal-to-noise ratios. Following substitution of the corresponding protease recognition sequence, the targeted biosensor yielded signal-to-noise ratios of 1100 and 1200 for EV-71 3C protease and HAV 3C protease (Picornaviridae), respectively, and a stable ratio of approximately 700 for EBV BVRF2 protease (Herpesviridae). These results demonstrated reliable performance of the system in cross-family viral protease detection and rapid inhibitor screening through modular sequence replacement. The EV-71 biosensor constructed on the basis of the C3SIR platform displayed clear dose-dependent responses in inhibitor titration experiments. These data confirm the potential of this system for high-throughput screening of antiviral agents. Effective detection of coronaviruses including 229E and HKU1 was also achieved following substitution of the system with coronavirus Mpro recognition sequence. The modular biosensor maintained a stable core signal amplification mechanism and enabled rapid, accurate detection of diverse viral protease activities solely through recognition sequence substitution. A favorable balance was achieved among broad-spectrum utility, sensitivity, and specificity. A flexible and reliable technical platform was thus provided for research on various viral proteases and development of both specific and broad-spectrum antiviral protease-targeting drugs. Using the APEX-based biosensors, we obtained IC50 values for known Mpro inhibitors (GC376, lufotrelvir, boceprevir, telaprevir, and narlaprevir) that were consistent with literature reports, while non-coronavirus inhibitors showed no detectable activity. Moreover, three in silico-predicted Mpro candidates (rutin, apigenin, and kaempferol) were confirmed by both our system and FRET assays to lack anti-coronaviral activity, suggesting that virtual screening predictions may require experimental validation using sensitive and specific cell-based systems like the one developed here. Collectively, these results demonstrated that our APEX biosensor platform offered reliable, specific, and high-throughput-compatible detection of viral protease inhibitors, effectively avoiding false-positive signals caused by cytotoxicity or non-specific compound interference.

Meanwhile, good anti-interference ability was exhibited by APEX biosensors. No significant changes in fluorescent signals were observed even in the presence of the coronavirus inhibitor Ebselen at concentrations as high as 100 μM. This finding demonstrated the specificity of the system for detecting target inhibitors. It was not affected by protein spatial conformation and better simulated real enzymatic reaction processes in organisms, instead of relying solely on signal output from protein-protein interactions. This system was compared with FRET-based biosensors (Sun et al., 2021), APEX-based sensors operated independently of energy transfer between donor and acceptor fluorescent proteins. False-positive inhibitory signals induced by inhibitor-mediated disturbances of protein conformation were thereby avoided. Signal amplification represented a fundamental design advantage of the biosensor architecture employed herein. In conventional signal-attenuation systems such as FlipGFP, reduced reporter output could arise from either genuine target inhibition or confounding factors including compound-induced cytotoxicity or non-specific protein conformational changes. Such ambiguous signal drops were frequently misinterpreted as inhibitory activity, as documented in previous studies (Ovechkina et al., 2021). By contrast, the APEX-based platform developed in this study operated through signal amplification, wherein enzymatic activity directly generated detectable luminescence. Consequently, compound-induced cytotoxicity or conformational interference that diminishes protein folding efficiency would not generate false-positive signals; instead, such conditions would merely reduce the dynamic range or abolish the signal entirely, rather than mimicking target inhibition. Furthermore, the C3SIR sensor component enabled parallel monitoring of cell viability, allowing effective discrimination between cytotoxic compounds and genuine enzymatic inhibitors during drug assessment. This dual-readout design therefore provided evaluation models that more accurately reflect direct inhibitory effects on enzymatic activity and were more representative of in vivo pharmacological conditions.

The APEX reporter system developed in this study exhibited high sensitivity, high specificity and favorable modularity. Nevertheless, we also acknowledge certain practical limitations. Like most cell-based approaches, the current assay involves relatively more operational steps and higher reagent costs than biochemical high-throughput screening (HTS) formats. Consequently, it might not be directly suitable for ultra-large-scale primary screening. However, we consider this system to be most valuable as a secondary or confirmatory platform—particularly for prioritizing physiologically relevant activity after initial biochemical hits were identified. Furthermore, the signal output of this system was completely dependent on the peroxidase activity of APEX, which was vulnerable to interference caused by abnormal intracellular redox states, such as oxidative stress in immune cells and hepatocytes (Eid et al., 2024). This compromises the stability of quantitative measurements. Additionally, current systems were primarily optimized for soluble proteases in the cytoplasm. Substrate accessibility, cleavage efficiency, and signal response mechanisms remain to be further validated and optimized for membrane-bound or secreted proteases (Elmsaouri et al., 2022). Finally, all performance validations were conducted at the cellular model level. Permeability, long-term stability and applicability of this system in more complex tissue environments, animal models, and prospective clinical samples are regarded as important directions for subsequent exploration. Development of APEX2 variants resistant to interference from oxidation and reduction processes would be pursued in future work (Takashima et al., 2024). Strategies including modification of targeting signal peptides or incorporation of dual-reporter gene calibration could be adopted (Hung et al., 2016; Lobingier et al., 2017). The reliability and broad applicability of this system in viral protease activity detection within physiological and pathological contexts could be further enhanced.

The APEX biosensor showed great potential for further development. In cases of emerging infectious disease viruses, corresponding activity evaluation systems were constructed through incorporation of substrate recognition sequences from viral proteases. Changes in viral infection and proliferation were assessed, and antiviral drugs targeting viral proteases were screened. Support for emergency responses to infectious diseases was thereby provided. Meanwhile, subcellular localization signals can be fused with this system (Battison et al., 2025). Spatially resolved imaging of protease activity was achieved. Further application was enabled for investigations of the dynamic interactions between proteases and their substrates. Biotinyltyramide radicals were produced through APEX-mediated catalys Biotinyl (Kalocsay, 2019). Proteins proximal to enzyme active sites were covalently labeled. High-resolution imaging and spatial proteomic analysis were accomplished simultaneously within single experiments. A multimodal research platform was established for dissection of protease functional networks at the subcellular level. Furthermore, integration of this system with organoid models and in vivo imaging techniques was achieved (Trompet et al., 2024). Protease function and drug efficacy were evaluated in models with higher physiological complexity. Our understanding of protease function is deepened and the development of targeted antiviral drugs is accelerated through these developmental directions.

In summary, the modular split-APEX biosensor developed in this study resolved multiple key bottlenecks in viral protease activity detection and drug screening through its high sensitivity, low background, real-time dynamics and high extensibility. A powerful tool was provided for virological research. A solid technical foundation was established for responses to future emerging infectious disease threats and accelerated discovery of broad-spectrum antiviral agents.

Conclusions

In summary, a split APEX-based fluorescent protease biosensor was developed in this study and engineered into two complementary platforms, TS3AR and C3SIR. Both systems benefited from the mechanistic advantages of the split APEX architecture, where signal output was driven by enzyme reassembly rather than conformational sensing. This key feature eliminated false positives caused by inhibitor-induced protein distortion and provided readouts that better reflected physiological enzyme activity. These split APEX sensors exhibited high sensitivity, low background noise, real-time dynamic monitoring capacity, and excellent scalability, thus addressing key limitations in current methods for viral protease detection and drug screening. These sensors offered a robust and flexible toolkit for basic research and antiviral drug development. In future work, spatially resolved imaging of protease activity can be achieved and in vivo efficacy evaluation can be promoted by incorporation of subcellular localization signals or combination with complex models such as organoids.

Materials and methods

Construction of the coronavirus Mpro biosensor

The coronavirus Mpro protease cleavage-dependent biosensor was constructed using a commercially synthesized pcDNA3.1(+) vector plasmid (Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China). This initial system was adapted from the CLAPon framework and was optimized by introducing a fusion degradation domain (DD) at the N-terminus to improve signal-to-noise ratio (Leonard et al., 2023). Subsequently, a second, application-optimized biosensor was engineered for high-throughput drug screening by integrating the expression cassette for the protease and the APEX reporter module into a single plasmid construct. The Mpro cleavage sequence (VAKLQSGF) utilized in these biosensors was a laboratory-optimized motif (Dong et al., 2025). To validate system specificity, an inactive Mpro mutant (C145A) was generated using the pcDNA3.1(+)-SARS-CoV-2-nsp5 plasmid as a template (Muramatsu et al., 2016) and the Mut Express II Fast Mutagenesis Kit V2 for site-directed mutagenesis. A parallel control biosensor containing the tobacco etch virus (TEV) protease cleavage sequence was (Kapust et al., 2002) also constructed. Furthermore, a tetracycline-inducible coronavirus Mpro expression plasmid was built using the pLVX-TetOne vector to support controlled drug screening experiments (Geng et al., 2023). All gene fragments were human codon-optimized and commercially synthesized by Sangon Biotech (China). The binding specificity between the biosensor and target proteases was assessed. HEK293T cells were co-transfected with the TS3AR biosensor and plasmids encoding either the wild-type coronavirus Mpro, the TEV protease, or the inactive Mpro (C145A) mutant.

Western blot

At 24 h post-transfection, HEK-293T cells were lysed in a buffer containing radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) buffer and phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF), followed by Western blot analysis. The extracted proteins were mixed with loading buffer, denatured by boiling for 15 min, separated by electrophoresis on a 15% Tris-Glycine PAGE gel, and transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes. The membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk for 1 h, followed by overnight incubation at 4 °C with primary antibodies, including anti-HA antibody (100028MM10, BBI Life Sciences, Shanghai, China) and anti-β-actin antibody (TA-09, ZSGB-BIO, Beijing, China). After washing with bovine serum albumin (BSA) solution, the membranes were incubated with the corresponding conjugated secondary antibody (ZB-2305, ZSGB-BIO, Beijing, China). Immunoreactive proteins were detected using SuperSignal West Pico+Chemiluminescent Substrate (34577, ThermoFisher, Waltham, MA, USA).

Validation of TS3AR for compatibility with different coronaviruses

Due to the high conservation of the amino acid sequence of the coronavirus main protease (Mpro), codon-optimized fragments of seven coronavirus Mpro genes (Supplementary Table S1) were cloned into the pcDNA3.1(+) vector. A C-terminal C145A mutation of the Mpro gene was used as a negative control. HEK293T cells were seeded into 96-well plates treated with poly-l-lysine and cultured overnight. TS3AR was co-transfected with each of the seven coronavirus Mpro expression vectors into 293T cells separately. Fluorescent activity was measured 24 h after transfection.

Construction of biosensors for other viral proteases

EBV, HAV and EV-71 proteases, as well as C3SIR (for EV-71, 229E and NL63), were constructed using commercially synthesized pcDNA3.1(+) vector plasmids (Sangon Biotech, China). Sequence information is provided in Supplementary Table S2. Mutations of restriction enzyme sites in the TS3AR biosensors (for EBV, HAV and EV-71) were performed using the Mut Express II Fast Mutagenesis Kit V2. All gene fragments were optimized according to human expression codons and synthesized by Sangon Biotech (China). The C3SIR (EV-71) biosensor was validated with Rupintrivir (TargetMOI). A 10-fold serial dilution was applied, and final concentrations ranged from 100 μM to 1 μM.

Fluorescence signal assay for APEX biosensor validation

The fluorescent signals generated by the APEX-based biosensor were detected following the procedures below. First, the cell culture medium was aspirated and discarded. Cells were gently washed three times with pre-chilled phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), with each wash lasting 1 min. Subsequently, under light-protected conditions, a PBS-based reaction working solution containing 50 μM Amplex UltraRed fluorescent substrate and 6.7 mM hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) was added to each well. The cell plate was then incubated on ice for 25 min under light protection. After the reaction, the reaction solution was completely aspirated to terminate staining. Cells were washed three times with PBS again, 1 min per wash. Then, cells were fixed with pre-chilled 4% (v/v) paraformaldehyde in PBS on ice for 30 min. After fixation, cells were washed three times with PBS, with each wash lasting 5 min, to fully remove the fixative. Finally, samples were placed under a fluorescence microscope, and images of the red fluorescence channel were acquired with an excitation wavelength of 575 nm.

Cell viability assay

Cell viability was determined using the CellTiter-Glo Luminescent Cell Viability Assay Kit (Promega). Experimental procedures were performed in accordance with the manufacturer's instructions. HEK-293T cells (ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA) were seeded into 96-well plates and cultured for 24 h, after which the medium was replaced with drug-containing Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM). Following an additional 24 h culture period, the 96-well plate and CellTiter-Glo reagent were equilibrated at room temperature for 30 min. Subsequently, 100 μl of the reagent was added to each well. The plates were mixed on an orbital shaker for 2 min and then incubated at room temperature for 10 min. A 100 μL aliquot of the solution was transferred to a white 96-well plate. Finally, luciferase activity was measured using the Nano-Glo Luciferase Assay System, with a signal acquisition time set at 1 s.

Feasibility verification of drug screening for candidate Mpro Inhibitors

HEK293T cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 2.5 × 104 cells per well and cultured overnight. Transfections were carried out at 70%–80% cell confluence using Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) following the manufacturer's instructions. The TS3AR reporter plasmid (50 ng) and the SARS-CoV-2 Mpro (pLVX-TetOne, GentleGen Biotech, Suzhou, China) expression plasmid (50 ng) were co-transfected, or 100 ng of C3SIR was transfected into HEK293T cells. The culture medium was replaced 6 h after transfection with medium containing candidate Mpro inhibitors: GC376 (S0475, Selleck Chemicals, Houston, USA), Lufotrelvir (PF-07304814, TargetMol Chemicals Inc., Boston, MA 02481, USA), and Ebselen (TargetMoI, T0825). Each inhibitor was subjected to 10-fold serial dilution with fresh DMEM medium, and final concentrations ranged from 100 μM to 0.01 μM. Cells treated with 0.1% dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO, solvent control; see Supplementary Fig. S3 for DMSO tolerance validation) and 20 μM GC376 (a known Mpro inhibitor) served as the negative control and positive control, respectively. After 24 h of compound exposure, cells were washed twice with pre-chilled PBS and incubated with Amplex UltraRed/H2O2 working solution. Fluorescent signals were recorded using a microplate reader. Inhibition rates were calculated with the following equation: Inhibition rate = 100% × (Sample value − Negative control) ÷ (Positive control − Negative control) Dose–response curves were generated with GraphPad Prism 10.1.2 software, and half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) values were determined via a four-parameter logistic regression model. All experimental operations were repeated three times for reliable data outcomes.

High-throughput screening

In the high-throughput screening (HTS) assays for inhibitors, compound screening was performed using the L6100 Polyphenol Compound Library and L1100 Protease Inhibitor Library, with a total of 1307 compounds included (Supplementary Table S1). Cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 2.5 × 104 cells per well and cultured overnight. One hundred ng of the C3SIR reporter plasmid was transfected using Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific), following the manufacturer's instructions. At 6 h post-transfection, (Hayashi et al., 2025; Shen et al., 2025). the cell culture medium was replaced with medium containing the test compounds at a concentration of 20 μM. Cells treated with fresh medium alone and those treated with medium containing the Mpro inhibitor GC376 (20 μM) were used as the negative and positive controls, respectively. Fluorescent signals were measured at 16 h post-treatment. Compounds with positive signals detected were tested in triplicate under independent conditions, with inter-assay reproducibility evaluated thereby. Cell viability assays were performed for the positive compounds identified; after confirmation of unimpaired cellular activity, IC50 assessments were further conducted in three independent experiments, with mean IC50 values and 95% confidence intervals calculated via dose-response curves. The Z′-factor was used to evaluate the quality and efficiency of the HTS assays. This index was calculated based on replicate data from the control groups, and its values remained consistently above 0.5. This result confirmed that the screening system has good reproducibility and a wide signal window, and is thus suitable for HTS applications.

FRET assay

Tested inhibitors were prepared at appropriate concentrations in DMSO. Ebselen (10 mM stock in DMSO, with a reported IC50 of 0.5–2 μM) was used as the positive control. Assay Reagent was freshly formulated according to the total number of testing samples, consisting of 92 μL Assay Buffer plus 1 μL recombinant 2019-nCoV Mpro per well. Blank control, 100% enzyme activity control, positive control and sample groups were arranged in black 96-well plates. Briefly, blank wells received 93 μL of Assay Buffer, whereas all remaining wells were supplemented with 93 μL pre-mixed Assay Reagent. For positive control wells, 5 μL of Ebselen solution was added; blank and 100% enzyme activity control wells were supplemented with 5 μL of the sample solvent (DMSO); and 5 μL of each individual test compound was dispensed into respective sample wells. Following thorough mixing, 2 μL of the fluorogenic substrate was rapidly added into each well. Plates were incubated at 37 °C in the dark for 5–20 min (the incubation time could be prolonged to 20–30 min when weak fluorescent signals were observed). Fluorescence intensity was measured via a microplate reader at excitation/emission wavelengths of 340 nm/490 nm.

Phylogenetic analysis

To clarify the phylogenetic position of the coronavirus main protease (Mpro), phylogenetic analysis was performed using MEGA7 software. Relevant reference sequences were downloaded from GenBank, and multiple sequence alignment was conducted using Clustal W with all parameters set to default values. Sequence ends were trimmed after alignment. The phylogenetic tree was constructed using the maximum likelihood method, and the best-fit nucleotide substitution model (GTR+G+I) was selected based on ModelTest. The confidence level of each tree branch was evaluated via the bootstrap method with 1000 replicates.

Data processing and statistical analysis

Data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation and derived from at least three independent replicate experiments. Routine data processing was performed using Microsoft Excel. Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism software (Version 10.1.2; GraphPad Software, San Diego, USA). Unless otherwise specified, comparisons of means between groups were performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Tukey's multiple comparison test (∗∗∗P < 0.001, ∗∗∗∗P < 0.0001). To calculate the half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50), inhibition analysis was conducted using the [inhibitor] vs. response-variable slope (four-parameter) function. Signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) was calculated as the ratio of fluorescence intensity in the experimental group (biosensor) to that in the negative control group (biosensor co-transfected with empty vector or inhibitor).
