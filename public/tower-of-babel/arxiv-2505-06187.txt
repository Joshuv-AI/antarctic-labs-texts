Preferential Attachment Trees with Vertex Death: Persistence of the Maximum Degree
Bas Lodewijks

Abstract
. We consider an evolving random discrete tree model called Preferential Attachment with Vertex Death , as introduced by Deijfen [ 13 ] . Initialised with an alive root labelled 1 1 , at each step n ≥ 1 n\geq 1 either a new vertex with label n + 1 n+1 is introduced that attaches to an existing alive vertex selected preferentially according to a function b b , or an alive vertex is selected preferentially according to a function d d and killed . In this article we introduce a generalised concept of persistence for evolving random graph models. Let O n O_{n} be the smallest label among all alive vertices (the oldest alive vertex), and let I n m I_{n}^{m} be the label of the alive vertex with the m th m^{\mathrm{th}} largest degree. We say a persistent m m -hub exists if I n m I_{n}^{m} converges almost surely, we say that persistence occurs when I n 1 / O n I_{n}^{1}/O_{n} is tight, and that lack of persistence occurs when I n 1 / O n I_{n}^{1}/O_{n} tends to infinity. We identify two regimes called the infinite lifetime and finite lifetime regimes. In the infinite lifetime regime, vertices are never killed with positive probability. Here, we provide conditions under which we prove the (non-)existence of persistent m m -hubs for any m ∈ ℕ m\in\mathbb{N} . This expands and generalises recent work of Iyer [ 25 ] which covers the case d ≡ 0 d\equiv 0 and m = 1 m=1 . In the finite lifetime regime, vertices are killed after a finite number of steps almost surely. Here we provide conditions under which we prove the occurrence of persistence , which complements recent work of Heydenreich and the author [ 23 ] , where lack of persistence is studied for preferential attachment with vertex death.



1.
Introduction

Random graph models based on preferential attachment are quite well-understood nowadays due to a large body of research formed over the last three decades (see
[
34
,
35
]
and the references therein for a good overview of the literature). These models have become a staple in random graph theory due to their use in modelling real-world networks. Still, a clear gap between most of these models and networks that form in the real world is the fact that preferential attachment graphs allow for
growth
only. Vertices and edges are sequentially attached to the graph, causing the graph to grow. However, this is not particularly realistic, as nodes and bonds in real-world networks can be
removed
in almost all contexts as well. As an example, people are born but also pass away; friendships are formed but can also be broken; users of social media can befriend or follow others, but also unfriend or unfollow them and even remove their accounts all together.

A limited body of work focusses on (preferential attachment type) models that allow for vertices and/or edges to also be removed from the network. Generally speaking, in such models at each step,

(1)

A new vertex is added to the graph with probability
p_{1}
, which connects to
m
already existing vertices (preferentially or uniformly at random).

(2)

A new edge is added to the graph between existing vertices with probability
p_{2}
, where the existing vertices are selected preferentially and/or uniformly.

(3)

A vertex is selected preferentially or uniformly and is removed with probability
p_{3}
(including the edges incident to it).

(4)

An edge is selected uniformly and is removed with probability
p_{4}
.

Examples include the models studied by Cooper et al.
[
12
]
(with
m\in\mathbb{N}
) with further work of Lindholm and Vallier
[
28
]
and Vallier
[
33
]
, Chung and Lu
[
11
]
(with
m=1
), Deo and Cami
[
15
]
(with
m=1
,
p_{2}=p_{4}=0
, and vertex deletion is done anti-preferentially, i.e. favouring vertices with low degree), and Deijfen and Lindholm
[
14
]
(with
m=1
,
p_{3}=0
). Another model of Britton and Lindholm
[
8
]
, with further work in
[
9
,
27
]
, assigns a random fitness value to each vertex, and edges are added between existing vertices with a probability proportional to the fitness values of the vertices. Work on duplication-divergence type models with edge deletion includes, among others,
[
32
,
22
,
4
,
29
]
.

The work on such models with vertex and/or edge removal is
limited
for a number of reasons. First, it generally only considers the degree distribution of the model, and if further properties are studied, this is under restrictive conditions and assumptions. Second, all models mentioned only study affine preferential attachment or uniform attachment for attaching edges. Third, in most models the choice to add or remove vertices and edges at each step is determined by fixed model parameters (the
p_{i}
) and does not depend on the evolution of the graph itself.

Some recent work partially addresses these concerns. Diaz, Lichev, and the author study a model with uniform attachment and uniform vertex removal (
m
is random,
p_{2}=p_{4}=0
), where the local weak limit, existence of a giant component, and the size and location of the maximum degree are studied
[
17
]
. Work of Bellin, Kammerer et al.
[
5
,
6
,
26
]
studies a tree model of uniform attachment and uniform vertex removal (
m=1
,
p_{2}=p_{4}=0
), but where vertices are not removed but are ‘frozen’ and can then no longer make new connections, and where the choice to freeze vertices need not be random. This allows them to study the model in a ‘critical window’ where they obtain precise results for the height of the tree and its scaling limit.

Finally, Deijfen
[
13
]
studies a Preferential Attachment tree model with Vertex Death (PAVD). Here, death is equivalent to freezing as in the work discussed above but simply carries a different name. In this model, the probability of killing a vertex or adding a vertex, as well as to which alive vertex this new vertex connects itself, is dependent on the state of the tree. In particular, it depends on the in-degrees of the alive vertices in a general way. Deijfen studied the limiting degree distribution of the tree conditionally on survival, and shows with a number of examples how introducing death can yield novel behaviour compared to preferential attachment trees without death.

In this article we focus on the PAVD model introduced and studied by Deijfen. To this end, let us provide a definition of the model. For a tree
T
, we naturally think of its edges as being directed towards the root. We then let
\deg_{T}(v)
denote the in-degree of a vertex
v
in
T
. For a sequence of trees
(T_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, we write
\deg_{n}(v)
rather than
\deg_{T_{n}}(v)
for ease of writing.

Definition 1.1

(Preferential Attachment with Vertex Death)
.

Let
b\colon\mathbb{N}_{0}\to(0,\infty)
and
d\colon\mathbb{N}_{0}\to[0,\infty)
be two sequences. We recursively construct a sequence of trees
(T_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and a sequence of sets of vertices
(\mathcal{A}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
as follows. We initialise
T_{1}
as a single vertex labelled
1
and
\mathcal{A}_{1}=\{1\}
. For
n\geq 1
, conditionally on
T_{n}
and
\mathcal{A}_{n}
, if
\mathcal{A}_{n}\neq\varnothing
, we select a vertex
i
from
\mathcal{A}_{n}
with probability

(1.1)

\frac{b(\deg_{n}(i))+d(\deg_{n}(i))}{\sum_{j\in\mathcal{A}_{n}}b(\deg_{n}(j))+d(\deg_{n}(j))}.

Then, conditionally on
T_{n}
and
i
, we either
kill
vertex
i
with probability

(1.2)

\frac{d(\deg_{n}(i))}{b(\deg_{n}(i))+d(\deg_{n}(i))},

and set
T_{n+1}=T_{n}
and
\mathcal{A}_{n+1}=\mathcal{A}_{n}\setminus\{i\}
, or otherwise construct
T_{n+1}
from
T_{n}
by introducing a new vertex
n+1
which we connect by a directed edge to
i
and set
\mathcal{A}_{n+1}=\mathcal{A}_{n}\cup\{n+1\}
.

If
\mathcal{A}_{n}=\varnothing
, we terminate the recursive construction, set
T_{i}=T_{n}
for all
i>n
, and say that
the tree has died
. When
\mathcal{A}_{n}\neq\varnothing
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
we say that the
tree process survives
.

We see from the definition that the probabilities to both select and kill a vertex depend on the evolution of the tree itself. Furthermore, in our analysis of the model, we do not restrict ourselves to the uniform case (
b
and/or
d
constant) or the affine case (
b(i)=b_{1}i+b_{2}
and/or
d(i)=c_{1}i+c_{2}
). Finally, we recover the standard preferential attachment tree model when
d\equiv 0
.

Persistence of the maximum degree.
The main focus of this article is
persistence of the maximum degree
, which in evolving random graphs is defined as the emergence of a
fixed
vertex that attains the largest degree in the graph for all but finitely many steps. When there is lack of persistence, such a fixed vertex does not exist and the maximum degree changes hands infinitely often (i.e. the maximum degree is attained by different vertices infinitely often). Persistence of the maximum degree is also known as degree centrality and is often leveraged in network archaeology and root-finding algorithms (see e.g.
[
3
,
7
,
10
]
and references therein).

The notion of persistence in evolving random graphs has been studied from different perspectives, starting with the work of Dereich and Mörters
[
16
]
. Later, conditions under which persistence and the lack thereof hold have been weakened by Galashin
[
20
]
, Banerjee and Bhamidi
[
2
]
, and Iyer
[
25
]
. In short (and omitting minor technical assumptions), using the PAVD formulation with
d\equiv 0
, so that there is no vertex death and we recover the classic preferential attachment model, persistence of the maximum degree occurs almost surely if and only if

(1.3)

\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{b(i)^{2}}<\infty.

Heuristically, persistence occurs when high-degree vertices have a sufficiently strong advantage over low-degree vertices when establishing new connections, so that they are most likely to increase their in-degree further. This allows one vertex to attain the largest degree for all but finitely many steps, instead of the largest degree switching between vertices infinitely often. The transition between whether the advantage is strong enough or not lies exactly at the point when the series in (
1.3
) goes from finite to infinite.

When vertices are killed and can no longer make new attachments afterwards, as in the PAVD model, the notion of persistence as stated above is not sufficiently general. In this case, we distinguish between whether vertices can live forever with positive probability, or whether they are killed after a finite (random) number of steps almost surely. In the former case, it may be possible that a vertex attains the
m^{\mathrm{th}}
largest degree for all but finitely many steps. We call such an individual a
persistent
m
-hub
. In the latter case, no individual lives forever, so that in particular no persistent
m
-hub can exist for any
m\in\mathbb{N}
. Here, a related question is whether the
oldest alive individual
and the the
richest alive individual
, that is, the alive individual with the smallest label and the alive individual with the largest degree, respectively, have labels that are ‘close’ or ‘far apart’. More precisely, whether the ratio of their labels is bounded or unbounded. When the ratio is bounded, we say that
persistence
occurs, whereas
lack of persistence
occurs when the ratio diverges to infinity.

In the case of preferential attachment without death all individuals are always alive, so that the oldest alive vertex is always the vertex with label
1
. The ratio of their labels thus equals the label of the maximum degree vertex, which implies that the existence of persistent
1
-hub is essentially equivalent to persistence. This is, however, not the case in models that incorporate vertex death.

Our contribution.
In this article we study the two regimes outlined above. In the
infinite lifetime
regime, in which vertices can live forever with positive probability, we provide conditions, similar to (
1.3
), under which a persistent
m
-hub exists almost surely conditionally on the tree process
(T_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
surviving, for each
m\in\mathbb{N}
, as well as conditions under which persistent hubs do not exist. This analysis generalises and expands recent work of Iyer
[
25
]
, which focusses on the case
d\equiv 0
and
m=1
.

The second regime is the
finite lifetime
regime, in which vertices are killed after a finite number of steps almost surely. Here, we provide conditions, similar to (
1.3
), under which persistence occurs. This builds on recent work by the author and Heydenreich
[
23
]
, in which lack of persistence is studied for the PAVD model in both the infinite and the finite lifetime regime, and extends the methodology developed by Deijfen in
[
13
]
using an embedding of the discrete tree process in a continuous-time branching process known as a Crump-Mode-Jagers branching process. We use a more precise formulation of this embedding compared to Deijfen, which allows us to simplify certain proofs of results in
[
13
]
, but mainly to develop novel and precise results regarding the behaviour of the branching process embedding that were unattainable previously. Furthermore, we extend ideas used by Banerjee and Bhamidi in
[
2
]
to precisely analyse the optimal window in which the vertex with the largest degrees is found in the finite lifetime regime.

Results

Infinite lifetime regime.
We first present our result for the PAVD model when vertices can survive forever with positive probability. To this end, we introduce the following assumptions. Recall

\text{There exists $\lambda>0$ such that }\widehat{\mu}(\lambda)<\infty.

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{b(i)+d(i)}=\infty,

Discussion, Examples, and Related work.
The results presented in this section identify the behaviour of the PAVD model in the ‘infinite lifetime’ and ‘finite lifetime’ with regards to persistence of the maximum degree (or
m^{\mathrm{th}}
-largest degree, in general). In the former regime, we require fairly mild conditions only. In the ‘finite lifetime’ regime, stronger assumptions are required compared to the ‘infinite lifetime’ regime. This is due to the fact that we need very precise asymptotic expansions of the random variables

and

(see Section
3
for more details). In Section
6
we discuss some open problems related to this regime.

3.
Methodology: Embedding in CMJ branching process

To prove the results presented in Section
2
, we make use of a technique where one ‘embeds’ a random discrete process into a Crump–Mode-Jagers (CMJ) continuous-time branching process. The random discrete structure is then equal in distribution to the CMJ branching process, when viewed at certain stopping times, whilst the CMJ branching process in continuous time provides more analytical advantages compared to the discrete process.

General CMJ branching processes.
We first introduce some definitions and notation to facilitate the introduction of the CMJ branching process that relates to the discrete PAVD model. We let
\mathcal{U}_{\infty}
denote the Ulam-Harris tree. That is,

(3.1)

\mathcal{U}_{\infty}\coloneq\{\varnothing\}\cup\bigcup_{k=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{N}^{k}.

For
v=v_{1}v_{2}\cdots v_{k}\in\mathcal{U}_{\infty}
, we view
v
as the
v_{k}^{\mathrm{th}}
child of
v_{1}\cdots v_{k-1}
(where
v_{1}\cdots v_{k-1}
denotes
\varnothing
when
k=1
). In particular, we view
j\in\mathbb{N}
as the
j^{\mathrm{th}}
child of
\varnothing
. We assign to each individual
v\in\mathcal{U}_{\infty}
a sequence of non-negative
inter-birth time
random variables
(X(vi))_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
and an
offspring
random variable
D^{(v)}
, which for each
v\in\mathcal{U}_{\infty}
are i.i.d. copies of some random variables
(X(i))_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
and
D
, respectively. We assume throughout that
X(i)<\infty
for all
i\in\mathbb{N}
, almost surely.

Given the inter-birth times of an individual
v
, we define its
lifetime
as

(3.2)

L^{(v)}\coloneq\sum_{i=1}^{D^{(v)}+1}X(vi).

Furthermore, we can recursively construct all
birth times

\mathcal{B}(v)
by setting
\mathcal{B}(\varnothing)\coloneq 0
and

(3.3)

\mathcal{B}(vj)\coloneq\begin{cases}\mathcal{B}(v)+\sum_{i=1}^{j}X(vi)&\mbox{for }v\in\mathcal{U}_{\infty},j\in\{1,\ldots,D^{(v)}\},\\
\infty&\mbox{for }v\in\mathcal{U}_{\infty},j\in\{D^{(v)}+1,\ldots\}.\end{cases}

Given that
v
is born in finite time, each child
v1,\ldots,vD^{(v)}
is born finite time and children
vj
for
j>D^{(v)}
are never born (as their birth-time is infinite). The individual
v
then dies after
L^{(v)}
time. If
v
is never born (i.e.
\mathcal{B}(v)=\infty
) then it is clear that all descendants of
v
are never born either.

We can then construct a branching process
(\mathrm{BP}(t))_{t\geq 0}
by setting

(3.4)

\mathrm{BP}(t)\coloneq\{u\in\mathcal{U}_{\infty}:\mathcal{B}(u)\leq t\}\qquad\text{for }t\geq 0.

It is clear that individuals
v=v_{1}\cdots v_{m}
with
v_{1},\ldots,v_{m}\in\mathbb{N}
and
m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
are born in the process
\mathrm{BP}
if and only if
v_{i}\leq D^{(v_{1}\cdots v_{i-1})}
for all
i\in[m]
. All other individuals are never born. We further define

(3.5)

\mathcal{A}^{\mathrm{ct}}_{t}\coloneq\{u\in\mathcal{U}_{\infty}:\mathcal{B}(u)\leq t<\mathcal{B}(u)+L^{(u)}\},\qquad\text{ for }t\geq 0,

as the set of alive individuals at time
t
. We can view this as the continuous-time counterpart of
\mathcal{A}_{n}
, as defined in Definition
1.1
. We abuse notation to write

(3.6)

\mathcal{S}=\{\forall t\geq 0:\mathcal{A}_{t}^{\mathrm{ct}}\neq\varnothing\}

as the event that the branching process
\mathrm{BP}
survives.

Continuous-time embedding of PAVD.
We now provide a specific case for the general definition above that we coin the continuous-time preferential attachment with vertex death branching process (CTPAVD), which is directly related to the PAVD model. Recall the random variables
D
,
(E_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
, and
(S_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
from (
2.6
). For each individual
v\in\mathcal{U}_{\infty}
, we let
D^{(v)}
be an i.i.d. copy of
D
, and set
X(vi)=E_{i-1}^{(v)}
, where
E^{(v)}_{i-1}
is an i.i.d. copy of
E_{i-1}
for each
i\in\mathbb{N}
. With
S_{k}^{(v)}\coloneq\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}E^{(v)}_{i}
, we can write for each
v\in\mathcal{U}_{\infty}
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
,

(3.7)

\sum_{i=1}^{k}X(vi)=S_{k}^{(v)}\qquad\text{and, in particular,}\qquad L^{(v)}\overset{\mathrm{d}}{=}S_{D+1}^{(v)}.

Let us introduce for
v\in\mathcal{U}_{\infty}
the characteristics

(3.8)

\chi^{(v)}_{\mathrm{b}}(t)\coloneq\mathbbm{1}_{\{t\geq 0\}},\qquad\text{and}\qquad\chi^{(v)}_{\mathrm{a}}(t)\coloneq\mathbbm{1}_{\{0\leq t<L^{(v)}\}},

and define, for
\square\in\{\mathrm{a},\mathrm{b}\}
,

(3.9)

Z_{\square}(t)\coloneq\sum_{v\in\mathcal{U}_{\infty}}\chi^{(v)}_{\square}(t-\mathcal{B}(v)).

Z_{\mathrm{a}}
and
Z_{\mathrm{b}}
‘count’ the branching process
\mathrm{BP}
according to the characteristics
\chi_{\mathrm{a}}
and
\chi_{\mathrm{b}}
, respectively. Here,
Z_{\mathrm{a}}(t)
denotes the number of alive individuals in
\mathrm{BP}(t)
and
Z_{\mathrm{b}}(t)
denotes the total number of individuals born in
\mathrm{BP}(t)
. We then set

(3.10)

N(t)\coloneq Z^{\mathrm{b}}_{t}+(Z^{\mathrm{b}}_{t}-Z^{\mathrm{a}}_{t})\qquad\text{for }t\geq 0,

as the number of births and deaths that have occurred in the branching process up to time
t
. We let
(\tau_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be stopping times, defined as

(3.11)

\tau_{n}\coloneq\inf\{t\geq 0:N(t)=n\},\qquad n\in\mathbb{N}.

That is,
\tau_{n}
denotes the time at which the
n^{\mathrm{th}}
birth or death event occurs. We then have the following correspondence between the discrete tree process
(T_{n},\mathcal{A}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and the CMJ branching process
(\mathrm{BP}(\tau_{n}),\mathcal{A}_{\tau_{n}}^{\mathrm{ct}})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
viewed at the sequence of stopping times.

Proposition 3.1

(Embedding of PAVD in CTPAVD)
.

Let
(T_{n},\mathcal{A}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be the sequence of trees and sets of alive vertices of a PAVD model and
(\mathrm{BP}(t),\mathcal{A}_{t}^{\mathrm{ct}})_{t\geq 0}
be a CTPAVD model and the set of alive individuals, both constructed with the same birth and death sequences
b
and
d
, respectively. Then,

(3.12)

(T_{n},\mathcal{A}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\overset{\mathrm{d}}{=}(\mathrm{BP}(\tau_{n}),\mathcal{A}^{\mathrm{ct}}_{\tau_{n}})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}.

The proposition follows from the memoryless property of exponential random variables and properties of minima of exponential random variables. The technique of embedding evolving discrete structures was originally pioneered by Athreya and Karlin
[
1
]
and has found fruitful applications in a wide variety of discrete (combinatorial) models, such as Pólya urns, discrete randomly growing trees such as preferential attachment trees and uniform attachment trees (as first observed by Pittel
[
31
]
), evolving simplicial complexes, and many more.

The description of the branching process
\mathrm{BP}
in which we embed the PAVD tree is used by Deijfen
[
13
]
to determine an explicit description for the limiting offspring distribution of alive individuals. The description of the branching process used here is somewhat different (though equivalent). Namely, the explicit construction in terms of the sum of inter-birth times

Persistence in the CTPAVD branching process.

We can view the concepts of persistent
m
-hubs, and persistence and the lack thereof from a continuous-time perspective as well. To this end, we define

(3.13)

\deg_{t}(u)\coloneq\min\big\{\sup\big\{k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}:S_{k}^{(u)}\leq t-\mathcal{B}(u)\big\},D^{(u)}\big\},\qquad u\in\mathcal{U}_{\infty},t\geq 0,

as the number of children
u
has produced by time

. We use the convention that

for all

as the birth-time of the oldest alive individual at time

, which is the continuous-time counterpart of

and for

(3.16)

I_{t}^{(m),\mathrm{ct}}\coloneq\min\big\{\mathcal{B}(v):v\in\mathcal{A}^{\mathrm{ct}}_{t}\setminus\{I_{t}^{(i),\mathrm{ct}}\}_{i\in[m-1]},\deg_{t}(v)\geq\deg_{t}(u)\text{ for all }u\in\mathcal{A}^{\mathrm{ct}}_{t}\setminus\{I_{t}^{(i),\mathrm{ct}}\}_{i\in[m-1]}\big\}.

Here

denotes the birth-time of the oldest alive individual that has the

. We say a branching process

We say that

When

-hub for any

(I^{(1),\mathrm{ct}}_{t}-O^{\mathrm{ct}}_{t})_{t\geq 0}\text{ is a tight sequence of random variables with respect to $\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{S}}$,}

and that lack of persistence occurs when

(3.20)

I^{(1),\mathrm{ct}}_{t}-O^{\mathrm{ct}}_{t}\xrightarrow{\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{S}}}\infty.

As in the discrete setting,

containing a persistent

-hub implies that it contains a persistent elder, and it implies persistence in

. On the other, hand, lack of persistence implies that
(\mathrm{BP}(t))_{t\geq 0}

1
-hub.

Proposition

\{(O_{n},I_{n}):n\in\mathbb{N}\}\overset{\mathrm{d}}{=}\{(N(O^{\mathrm{ct}}_{\tau_{n}}),N(I^{\mathrm{ct}}_{\tau_{n}})):n\in\mathbb{N}\}.

In particular, this relates the definition of a persistent hub and (lack of ) persistence in

to the same concepts for the PAVD discrete tree process

Related work.
The results presented in Theorem

are related to recent work of Iyer on persistent hubs in general CMJ branching processes
[

•

Iyer focusses on general branching processes, where the inter-birth times

are not necessarily exponentially distributed.

•

Iyer focusses the case where the offspring

is infinite for each individual

•

The results of Iyer are restricted to the case

and do not include (

In short, based on proof techniques developed by Iyer, we extend his results to the case

(which is the case when Assumption (

These extensions can be proved for more general CMJ branching processes, where we do not assume the inter-birth times to be exponentially distributed, as well. As the focus of this work lies on the PAVD model, we do not prove this explicitly, but rather provide a discussion on how to extend (part of) the results in Theorem

to general CMJ branching processes can be found Appendix

Proof of Theorem

To translate the results in Theorem

in

Lemma 4.2
.

Recall the Laplace transform
\widehat{\mu}
from (
2.8
) and suppose that Assumption (
F-L
) is satisfied. That is, suppose that there exists
\lambda>0
such that
\widehat{\mu}(\lambda)<\infty
. Then,
|\mathrm{BP}(t)|<\infty
for all
t\geq 0
almost surely.

Proof.

We start by observing that Assumption (
F-L
) is equivalent to there existing
\lambda^{\prime}\geq\lambda
such that
\widehat{\mu}(\lambda^{\prime})<1
. Indeed, since
\widehat{\mu}(\lambda)<\infty
and
\widehat{\mu}(\alpha)
is decreasing in
\alpha
, it follows from the dominated convergence theorem that

(4.2)

\lim_{\alpha\to\infty}\widehat{\mu}(\alpha)=\mu(\{0\})=0\qquad\text{and so there exists $\lambda^{\prime}\in[\lambda,\infty)$ such that }\widehat{\mu}(\lambda^{\prime})<1.

We then prove the result. For any
v=v_{1}\cdots v_{m}\in\mathcal{U}_{\infty}
, with
m\in\mathbb{N}
and
v_{1},\ldots,v_{m}\in\mathbb{N}
,

(4.3)

\mathbb{P}\!\left(\mathcal{B}(v)\leq t\right)=\mathbb{P}\!\left(D^{(v_{1}\cdots v_{\ell-1})}\geq v_{\ell}\text{ for all }\ell\in[m]\text{, and }\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}\sum_{i=0}^{v_{\ell}-1}E^{(v_{1}\cdots v_{\ell-1})}_{i}\leq t\right).

We use the independence of the offspring random variables and the inter-birth times, together with a Chernoff bound with
\lambda^{\prime}
, to bound the right-hand side from above by

(4.4)

\mathrm{e}^{\lambda^{\prime}t}\prod_{\ell=1}^{m}\mathbb{P}\!\left(D\geq v_{\ell}\right)\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\bigg(-\lambda^{\prime}\sum_{i=0}^{v_{\ell}-1}E_{i}\bigg)\right].

By summing over all individuals
v\in\mathcal{U}_{\infty}
(where
\mathbb{P}\!\left(\mathcal{B}(\varnothing)\leq t\right)=1
as
\mathcal{B}(\varnothing)=0
), we obtain

(4.5)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[|\mathrm{BP}(t)|\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{v\in\mathcal{U}_{\infty}}\mathbb{P}\!\left(\mathcal{B}(v)\leq t\right)

\displaystyle\leq 1+\mathrm{e}^{\lambda^{\prime}t}\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\sum_{v_{1}=1}^{\infty}\cdots\sum_{v_{m}=1}^{\infty}\prod_{\ell=1}^{m}\mathbb{P}\!\left(D\geq v_{\ell}\right)\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\bigg(-\lambda^{\prime}\sum_{i=0}^{v_{\ell}-1}E_{i}\bigg)\right].

As the terms in the product depend on distinct summation indices, we can interchange summation and product to yield

(4.6)

\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\Bigg(\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\!\left(D\geq j\right)\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\bigg(-\lambda^{\prime}\sum_{i=0}^{j-1}E_{i}\bigg)\right]\Bigg)^{m}.

By rewriting the inner sum and using that
D
is independent of
(E_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
, we obtain

(4.7)

\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\!\left(D\geq j\right)\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\bigg(-\lambda^{\prime}\sum_{i=0}^{j-1}E_{i}\bigg)\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{j=1}^{D}\exp\bigg(-\lambda^{\prime}\sum_{i=0}^{j-1}E_{i}\bigg)\right]=\widehat{\mu}(\lambda^{\prime}),

where we recall
\widehat{\mu}
from (
2.8
). We finally obtain that
\mathbb{E}\left[|\mathrm{BP}(t)|\right]\leq\mathrm{e}^{\lambda^{\prime}t}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\widehat{\mu}(\lambda^{\prime})^{m}
, which is finite since
\widehat{\mu}(\lambda^{\prime})<1
. Hence,
|\mathrm{BP}(t)|<\infty
almost surely, as desired.
∎

Theorem
2.1
then readily follows.

Recall the stopping times

Lemma 4.3

Let

(X(i)^{\prime})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}

\mathbb{P}\bigg(\exists j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\colon Y+\sum_{i=1}^{k+j}X(i)\leq\sum_{i=k}^{k+j}X(i)^{\prime}\bigg)\leq\bigg(\prod_{i=k}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}\Big[\mathrm{e}^{\lambda(X(i)^{\prime}-X(i))}\Big]\bigg)\mathbb{E}\bigg[\exp\bigg(-\lambda\bigg(Y+\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}X(i)\bigg)\bigg)\bigg].

In short, the proof of Lemma

Lemma 4.4

(Proposition

in
[

Suppose that
(S_{i})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\sum_{i=1}^{n}S_{i}=\infty\qquad\text{and}\qquad\liminf_{n\to\infty}\sum_{i=1}^{n}S_{i}=-\infty,\qquad\text{almost surely.}

The proof of Lemma

Lemma 4.5
.

Let

Remark 4.6

(i)
If we set

and

The product in (

(ii)

\blacktriangleleft

Proof.

To prove the convergence of the series of exponential random variables, we check the conditions of Kolmogorov’s three series theorem (see e.g.
[
19
, Theorem
2.5.4
]
). This theorem states that a random series
\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}X_{i}
of independent random variables
(X_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
converges almost surely if the following conditions holds for some
A>0
, and only if the following conditions hold for any
A>0
:

(4.14)

(i)\ \sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\!\left(|X_{i}|\geq A\right)<\infty\quad(ii)\ \sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[X_{i}\mathbbm{1}_{\{|X_{i}|\leq A\}}\right]<\infty\quad(iii)\ \sum_{i=0}^{\infty}{\rm Var}(X_{i}\mathbbm{1}_{\{|X_{i}|\leq A\}})<\infty.

We notice that for symmetric random variables, the second condition is always satisfied. It thus suffices to focus on conditions
(i)
and
(iii)
, for
X_{i}=E_{i}-E_{i}^{\prime}
.

Let us first prove the ‘if’ direction. We suppose that

. Then,

(4.15)

\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}{\rm Var}(X_{i}\mathbbm{1}_{\{|X_{i}|\leq A\}})=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[X_{i}^{2}\mathbbm{1}_{\{|X_{i}|\leq A\}}\right]\leq\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[X_{i}^{2}\right]=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}2{\rm Var}(E_{i})=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\frac{2}{\lambda_{i}^{2}},

which is finite by assumption. Condition
(iii)
is hence satisfied. Also,

(4.16)

\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\!\left(|X_{i}|\geq A\right)\leq\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\!\left(E_{i}+E_{i}^{\prime}\geq A\right)\leq 2\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\!\left(E_{i}\geq\frac{A}{2}\right)=2\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda_{i}A/2}

By assuming that
\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\lambda_{i}^{-2}<\infty
, it follows that
\lambda_{i}\to\infty
with
i
. Hence, there exists
I\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda_{i}A/2}\leq 1/\lambda_{i}^{2}
, from which Condition
(i)
follows.

For the ‘only if’ direction, we argue by contradiction. We assume that there exists
A>0
such that either Condition
(i)
or

Suppose first that Condition

\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}{\rm Var}(X_{i}\mathbbm{1}_{\{|X_{i}|\leq A\}})=\infty\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}{\rm Var}(X_{i})=\infty.

However, the equality on the right-hand side contradicts the assumption that

We can rewrite this as

0\leq\mathcal{B}(v)-\mathcal{B}(u)+\sum_{i=0}^{n_{\ell}-1}E^{(v)}_{i}-E^{(u)}_{i}\leq E^{(u)}_{n_{\ell}}\qquad\text{for all }\ell\in\mathbb{N}.

By Lemma
4.5
and Remark
4.6
the series
\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}(E^{(v)}_{i}-E^{(u)}_{i})
converges almost surely. Hence, the distribution of the random variable
\mathcal{B}(v)-\mathcal{B}(u)+\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}(E^{(v)}_{i}-E^{(u)}_{i})
does not contain an atom on
\mathbb{R}
by
[
25
, Lemma 3.2]
.
However, for any
\varepsilon>0
, the inequality
E^{(u)}_{n_{\ell}}<\varepsilon
will hold for infinitely many
\ell
almost surely by the Borel-Cantelli lemma, since

(4.59)

\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\!\left(E_{i}\geq\varepsilon\right)\leq\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-(b(i)+d(i))\varepsilon}\leq\sum_{i=0}^{K}\mathrm{e}^{-(b(i)+d(i))\varepsilon}+\sum_{i=K+1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(b(i)+d(i))^{2}}<\infty.

Here, the final sum is finite and
K\in\mathbb{N}
is such that
\mathrm{e}^{-(b(i)+d(i))\varepsilon}\leq(b(i)+d(i))^{-2}
for all
i\geq K
, which is possible since
b(i)+d(i)
tends to infinity, both due to Assumption (
C-V
).

The inequalities in (
4.58
) thus lead to a contradiction, so that any pair of vertices satisfies that their degrees are equal for only a finite amount of time. This directly implies (
4.1
), which concludes the proof.
∎

It remains to prove Proposition
4.9
. We define the following (random) subset of
\mathcal{U}_{\infty}
. For
K\in\mathbb{N}
fixed and
u\in\mathcal{U}_{\infty}
, set

(4.60)

I(u)\coloneq\begin{cases}K&\mbox{if }D^{(u)}=\infty,\\
D^{(u)}&\mbox{if }D^{(u)}<\infty\end{cases}

Then, we define

(4.61)

\mathcal{U}_{K}^{<\infty}=\mathcal{U}_{K}^{<\infty}((D^{(u)})_{u\in\mathcal{U}_{\infty}})\coloneq\{u\in\mathcal{U}_{\infty}:u_{j}\leq I(u_{1}\cdots u_{j-1})\text{ for all }j\in[|u|]\}\cup\{\varnothing\}.

That is, given
(D^{(u)})_{u\in\mathcal{U}_{\infty}}
, one constructs the set
\mathcal{U}_{K}^{<\infty}
iteratively as follows. Initialise
\mathcal{U}^{(0)}\coloneq\{\varnothing\}
and, given
\mathcal{U}^{(i)}
with

, we set

(4.62)

\mathcal{U}^{(i+1)}\coloneq\mathcal{U}^{(i)}\cup\bigcup_{u\in\mathcal{U}^{(i)}}\{u1,\ldots,uI(u)\}.

Note that, if
u\in\mathcal{U}^{(i)}
for some
i
and
D^{(u)}=0
, then we do not add any children
uj
to
\mathcal{U}^{(i+1)}
. This iterative process either never terminates, in which case
\mathcal{U}^{<\infty}_{K}=\cup_{i=0}^{\infty}\mathcal{U}^{(i)}
is infinite, or it terminates at some (random) step

, so that
\mathcal{U}^{<\infty}_{K}=\mathcal{U}^{(M)}
is finite. We observe that, conditionally on survival of the process
(\mathrm{BP}(t))_{t\geq 0}
, the set
\mathcal{U}^{<\infty}_{K}
contains at least one individual
u
such that
D^{(u)}=\infty
, almost surely, when
\mathbb{P}\!\left(D=\infty\right)>0
(which is the case when Assumption (
F-D
) is satisfied). We also define

(4.63)

\mathcal{U}^{<\infty}\coloneq\mathcal{U}_{\infty}^{<\infty}=\{u\in\mathcal{U}_{\infty}\colon u_{j}\leq D^{(u)}\text{ for all }j\in[|u|]\}\cup\{\varnothing\}

as the set of all individuals that are eventually born in
\mathrm{BP}
.

We now prove Proposition
4.8
for
m=2
. The case

follows similarly, but requires fewer steps, whilst the difference for

to be the stopping time at which the branching process contains at least two individuals that have produced (at least)

\mathcal{U}^{<\infty}\subseteq\mathcal{A}_{-}\cup\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\in\mathcal{A}_{-}\\
D^{(u)}=\infty\end{subarray}}\{uv\colon v\in\mathcal{U}_{\infty},v_{1}>K\}\cup\bigcup_{u\in\mathcal{A}_{+}}\{uv\colon v\in\mathcal{U}_{\infty}\}.

Third, suppose that

Using this superset, we then construct a superset for

\displaystyle\cup\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\in\mathcal{A}_{-}\setminus\{V_{2}\}\\

Sets in (

).

\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{S}}

\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{S}}

(4.71)

Let

be the

. We note that

,
\mathcal{A}_{-}
,
\mathcal{A}_{+}

. Take

Case (i)

. Similar to the proof of Proposition

, we can write for any

,

, and

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{S}}\big(uv{}

\displaystyle\text{ catches up to }V_{2}\text{ and }D^{(uv)}=\infty\,\big|\,\mathcal{F}_{T}\big)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{S}}\Bigg({}

\displaystyle D^{(uv)}=\infty,D^{(uv_{1}\cdots v_{\ell-1})}\geq v_{\ell}\text{ for all }\ell\in[m],

\displaystyle\exists j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\colon\mathcal{B}(u)+\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}\sum_{i=0}^{v_{\ell}-1}E^{(uv_{1}\cdots v_{\ell-1})}_{i}+\sum_{i=0}^{K+j}E^{(uv)}_{i}\leq\mathcal{B}(V_{2}K)+\!\!\!\sum_{i=K}^{K+j}E^{(V_{2})}_{i}\,\Bigg|\,\mathcal{F}_{T}\Bigg).

. Furthermore, since we suppose that

\mathcal{B}(uK)\leq T
. We can thus omit

and
\mathcal{B}(V_{2}K)
from the event to obtain the upper bound

(4.73)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{S}}\Bigg({}

\displaystyle D^{(uv)}=\infty,D^{(uv_{1}\cdots v_{\ell-1})}\geq v_{\ell}\text{ for all }\ell\in[m],

\displaystyle\exists j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\colon\sum_{i=K}^{v_{1}-1}E^{(u)}_{i}+\sum_{\ell=2}^{m}\sum_{i=0}^{v_{\ell}-1}E^{(uv_{1}\cdots v_{\ell-1})}_{i}+\sum_{i=0}^{K+j}E^{(uv)}_{i}\leq\sum_{i=K}^{K+j}E^{(V_{2})}_{i}\,\Bigg|\,\mathcal{F}_{T}\Bigg).

We now observe that all random variables in the conditional probability, with the exception of

, are independent of
\mathcal{F}_{T}
. Since we know, however, that
D^{(u)}=\infty
and thus

is satisfied, the probability almost surely equals

(4.74)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{S}}\Bigg({}

\displaystyle D^{(uv)}=\infty,D^{(uv_{1}\cdots v_{\ell-1})}\geq v_{\ell}\text{ for all }\ell\in\{2,\ldots,m\},

\displaystyle\exists j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\colon\sum_{i=K}^{v_{1}-1}E^{(u)}_{i}+\sum_{\ell=2}^{m}\sum_{i=0}^{v_{\ell}-1}E^{(uv_{1}\cdots v_{\ell-1})}_{i}+\sum_{i=0}^{K+j}E^{(uv)}_{i}\leq\sum_{i=K}^{K+j}E^{(V_{2})}_{i}\Bigg).

\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{S}}\left(\mathcal{E}\right)\leq\mathbb{P}\!\left(\mathcal{E}\right)/\mathbb{P}\!\left(\mathcal{S}\right)
for any event
\mathcal{E}
, using the independence of the offspring and inter-birth random variables, and applying Lemma

(where we note that the condition in (

(4.75)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathbb{P}\!\left(D=\infty\right)}{\mathbb{P}\!\left(\mathcal{S}\right)}{}

(4.76)

\frac{1}{\mathbb{P}\!\left(\mathcal{S}\right)}\prod_{i=K}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{e}^{\lambda(E_{i}^{\prime}-E_{i})}\right]\prod_{\ell=1}^{m}\mathbb{P}\!\left(D\geq v_{\ell}\right)\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\bigg(-\lambda\sum_{i=0}^{v_{\ell}-1}E_{i}\bigg)\right].

. By summing over all
uv
with

such that

, we thus obtain

(4.77)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{S}}\Big[{}

\displaystyle|\{uv\colon v\in\mathcal{U}_{\infty},v_{1}>K,uv\text{ catches up to }V_{2}\text{ and }D^{(uv)}=\infty\}|\,\Big|\,\mathcal{F}_{T}\Big]

\displaystyle\leq{}

\displaystyle\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\sum_{v_{1}=K+1}^{\infty}\sum_{v_{2}=1}^{\infty}\cdots\sum_{v_{m}=1}^{\infty}c\prod_{i=K}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{e}^{\lambda(E_{i}^{\prime}-E_{i})}\right]\prod_{\ell=1}^{m}\mathbb{P}\!\left(D\geq v_{\ell}\right)\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\bigg(-\lambda\sum_{i=1}^{v_{\ell}}E_{i}\bigg)\right]

\displaystyle\leq c\prod_{i=K}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{e}^{\lambda(E_{i}^{\prime}-E_{i})}\right]\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\Bigg(\sum_{v=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\!\left(D\geq v\right)\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\bigg(-\lambda\sum_{i=0}^{v-1}E_{i}\bigg)\right]\Bigg)^{m},

where in the last step we interchanged summation and products and extended the range of the sum over

to all

. We observe that the inner sum on the last line equals

, so that by Assumption (

, as follows from (

(4.78)

\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{S}}\left(\{uv\colon v\in\mathcal{U}_{\infty},v_{1}>K,uv\text{ catches up to }V_{2}\text{ and }D^{(uv)}=\infty\}|<1\,\Big|\,\mathcal{F}_{T}\right)=1,\quad\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{S}}\text{-a.s.}

Case (ii)

u=V_{1}
. In this case, let

(4.79)

\mathcal{T}_{T}(V_{1})\coloneq\{V_{1}v\colon v\in\mathcal{U}_{\infty},v_{1}>K,\mathcal{B}(V_{1}v)\leq T\}

be the sub-tree rooted at

and their descendants that are born by time

. It is clear that

, so that it is finite
\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{S}}
-almost surely, and that it is
\mathcal{F}_{T}
measurable. For

, we define

K(w)\coloneq\max\{0\leq k\leq D^{(w)}:wk\in\mathcal{T}_{T}(V_{1})\}

to be the ‘last’ child of

\mathcal{T}_{T}(V_{1})
. We then have the inclusion

(4.81)

\displaystyle\{V_{1}v:{}

\displaystyle v\in\mathcal{U}_{\infty},v_{1}>K,D^{(V_{1}v)}=\infty,V_{1}v\text{ c.u.t.\ }V_{2}\}

\displaystyle\subseteq\mathcal{T}_{T}(V_{1})\cup\bigcup_{w\in\mathcal{T}_{T}(V_{1})}\{wv:v\in\mathcal{U}_{\infty},v_{1}>K(w),D^{(wv)}=\infty,wv\text{ c.u.t.\ }V_{2}\}.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{S}}{}

\displaystyle\Big[|\{V_{1}v:v\in\mathcal{U}_{\infty},v_{1}>K,\mathcal{B}(V_{1}v)>T,D^{(V_{1}v)}=\infty,V_{1}v\text{ c.u.t.\ }V_{2}\}|\,\Big|\,\mathcal{F}_{T}\Big]

\displaystyle|\mathcal{T}_{T}(V_{1})|+\!\!\!\!\sum_{w\in\mathcal{T}_{T}(V_{1})}\!\!\!\!\!\!\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{S}}\left[|\{wv:v\in\mathcal{U}_{\infty},v_{1}>K(w),D^{(wv)}=\infty,wv\text{ c.u.t.\ }V_{2}\}|\,\Big|\,\mathcal{F}_{T}\right].

As already concluded, the first term on the right-hand side is finite

-almost surely. Each expected value in the sum on the right-hand side can be written as

\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\sum_{v_{1}=K(w)+1}^{D^{(w)}}\sum_{v_{2}=1}^{\infty}\cdots\sum_{v_{m}=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{S}}\left(D^{(wv)}=\infty,wv\text{ c.u.t.\ }V_{2}\,\Big|\,\mathcal{F}_{T}\right),

and each such probability equals

(4.84)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{S}}\bigg({}

\displaystyle\exists j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\colon\mathcal{B}(wK(w))+\!\!\!\!\!\sum_{i=K(w)}^{v_{1}-1}\!\!\!\!E^{(w)}_{i}+\sum_{\ell=2}^{m}\sum_{i=0}^{v_{\ell}-1}E^{(wv_{1}\cdots v_{\ell-1})}_{i}+\!\sum_{i=0}^{K+j}\!E^{(wv)}_{i}\!\leq\mathcal{B}(V_{2}K)+\!\sum_{i=K}^{K+j}\!E^{(V_{2})}_{i},

\displaystyle D^{(wv)}=\infty,D^{(wv_{1}\cdots v_{\ell-1})}\geq v_{\ell}\text{ for all }\ell\in[m]\,\bigg|\,\mathcal{F}_{T}\bigg).

We observe that

by the definition of

, so that we obtain the upper bound

(4.85)

), as in (
3.19
). That is, for a mapping
s\mapsto f(s)
to be determined, we show that for any
\varepsilon>0
small and

large, there exist

such that for all
t\geq t^{\prime}
, the events

(5.1)

\displaystyle|O_{s}^{\mathrm{ct}}-f(s)|

\displaystyle<K\quad\text{for all }s\in[t-A,t+A],

and

(5.2)

\displaystyle I_{s}^{\mathrm{ct}}-O_{s}^{\mathrm{ct}}

\displaystyle<K\quad\text{for all }s\in[t-A,t+A],

occur with probability at least
1-\varepsilon
. We first collect several preliminary results in Section

and prove (

) in Section

. We then prove several results on the offspring of ‘young’ and ‘old’ individuals in Section

5.2.
The oldest alive individual

In this sub-section we study the quantity
O_{t}^{\mathrm{ct}}
; the birth-time of the oldest alive individual in
\mathrm{BP}(t)
conditionally on survival. To this end, we first obtain precise asymptotic distributional properties of the lifetime
L=S_{D+1}
of individuals in the branching process, to then prove an asymptotic expansion for
O_{t}^{\mathrm{ct}}
as
t\to\infty
.

5.2.1.
Lifetime distribution

We know from
[
23
, Lemma
6.1
]
that the lifetime
L
of an individual is asymptotically distributed as an exponential random variable with rate
d^{*}
when
d
converges to
d^{*}\leq R
. That is
\mathbb{P}\!\left(L>t\right)=\exp(-(d^{*}+o(1))t)
as
t
tends to infinity. A more precise asymptotic expansion is provided in
[
23
, Lemma
6.3
]
when Assumption (
C-V
) is
not
satisfied. We complement this result by the following lemma, which provides a (different) higher-order expansion when Assumption (
C-V
) is satisfied.

Lemma 5.6

(Third-order asymptotic lifetime distribution)
.

Suppose that
b
and
d
are such that Assumptions (
N-E
), (
F-D
), and (
C-V
) are satisfied, and recall
R
from (
2.14
). Also suppose that
\lim_{i\to\infty}d(i)=d^{*}\in[0,R)
. Recall the function
\mathcal{K}_{\alpha}
from (
2.17
), and suppose that Assumption
\mathcal{K}_{\alpha}
is satisfied. Then,

(5.15)

\sup_{t\geq 0}\big|\log(\mathbb{P}\!\left(L>t\right))+d^{*}t+\mathcal{K}_{\alpha}(t)\big|<\infty.

Proof.

We start by making the following observations. Since
\varphi_{2}
converges and
d
converges, it follows that
\rho_{2}
, as in (
5.3
), converges. Furthermore, as
\varphi_{2}(k)={\rm Var}(S_{k})={\rm Var}(S_{k}-\varphi_{1}(k))
converges and
\varphi_{1}(k)=\mathbb{E}\left[S_{k}\right]
, it follows that
S_{k}-\varphi_{1}(k)
converges almost surely to some random variable
M_{\infty}
by Kolmogorov’s two series theorem
[
19
, Theorem 2.5.6]
.

Lower bound.
Set
k\coloneq\lfloor\varphi_{1}^{-1}(t)\rfloor
. Then, using Lemma
5.1
and the above observations,

(5.16)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\!\left(L>t\right)

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{P}\!\left(D\geq k\right)\mathbb{P}\!\left(S_{k}>t\right)

\displaystyle=\exp(-\rho_{1}(k)+\mathcal{O}(1))\mathbb{P}\!\left(S_{k}>t\right)

\displaystyle\geq\exp(-\rho_{1}(\varphi_{1}^{-1}(t))+\mathcal{O}(1))\mathbb{P}\!\left(S_{k}-\varphi_{1}(k)>0\right)

\displaystyle=\exp(-d^{*}t-\mathcal{K}_{\alpha}(t)+\mathcal{O}(1))(\mathbb{P}\!\left(M_{\infty}>0\right)+o(1)),

which yields the desired lower bound.

Upper bound.
We set

(5.17)

M=M(t)\coloneq\lceil(1-\varepsilon)t\rceil\qquad\text{and}\qquad k_{i}\coloneq\lceil\varphi_{1}^{-1}(t-i)\rceil\quad\text{for }i\in\{0,\ldots,M\}.

Further, we fix
\theta\in(d^{*},R)
and take
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
small enough such that
\theta(1-\varepsilon)>d^{*}
. Then, using Lemma
5.1
and Chernoff bounds,

(5.18)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\!\left(L>t\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\!\left(D\geq k_{0}\right)+\mathbb{P}\!\left(S_{k_{M}}>t\right)+\sum_{i=0}^{M-1}\mathbb{P}\!\left(D\geq k_{i+1}\right)\mathbb{P}\!\left(S_{k_{i}}>t\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathrm{e}^{-\rho_{1}(k_{0})}+\mathrm{e}^{-\theta t}\prod_{i=0}^{k_{M}-1}\frac{b(i)+d(i)}{b(i)+d(i)-\theta}+\mathrm{e}^{-\theta t}\sum_{i=0}^{M-1}\mathrm{e}^{-\rho_{1}(k_{i+1})}\prod_{i=0}^{k_{i}-1}\frac{b(i)+d(i)}{b(i)+d(i)-\theta}.

Since
\varphi_{2}
converges, it follows that for any
i\leq M
,

(5.19)

\prod_{i=0}^{k_{i}-1}\frac{b(i)+d(i)}{b(i)+d(i)-\theta}=\exp(\theta\varphi_{1}(k_{i})+\mathcal{O}(1))=\exp(\theta t-\theta i+\mathcal{O}(1)),

where the
\mathcal{O}(1)
term is independent of
t
and
i
. By the definition of
\alpha
in (
2.16
), we thus arrive at the upper bound

(5.20)

\mathrm{e}^{-d^{*}t-\mathcal{K}_{\alpha}(t)}+\mathrm{e}^{-\theta(1-\varepsilon)t}+\sum_{i=0}^{M-1}\mathrm{e}^{-d^{*}t-\mathcal{K}_{\alpha}(t-(i+1))-(\theta-d^{*})i+\mathcal{O}(1)}.

As
\theta(1-\varepsilon)>d^{*}
by the choice of
\theta
and
\varepsilon
, and
\mathcal{K}_{\alpha}=o(t)
by Lemma
5.5
(c)
, it follows that the second term is negligible compared to the first term. To deal with the sum, we define

(5.21)

\widetilde{\alpha}(k)=\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\frac{|d(i)-d^{*}|}{b(i)+d(i)}\qquad\text{for }k\in\mathbb{N}_{0},\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{K}_{\widetilde{\alpha}}(t)\coloneq\widetilde{\alpha}\big(\varphi_{1}^{-1}(t)\big)\qquad\text{for }t\geq 0,

where we extend the domain of
\widetilde{\alpha}
to
\mathbb{R}_{+}
by linear interpolation. We then distinguish between two cases.

\bm{\mathcal{K}_{\widetilde{\alpha}}}
converges.
In this case, it follows that
\mathcal{K}_{\alpha}
is bounded, as
|\mathcal{K}_{\alpha}(t)|\leq\mathcal{K}_{\widetilde{\alpha}}(t)
for all
t\geq 0
. Hence the term
\mathcal{K}_{\alpha}(t-(i+1))
in the exponential summands in (
5.20
) can be included in the
\mathcal{O}(1)
term for each
i\leq M-1
, to yield

(5.22)

\sum_{i=0}^{M-1}\mathrm{e}^{-d^{*}t-\mathcal{K}_{\alpha}(t-(i+1))-(\theta-d^{*})i+\mathcal{O}(1)}=\sum_{i=0}^{M-1}\mathrm{e}^{-d^{*}t-(\theta-d^{*})i+\mathcal{O}(1)}\leq C\mathrm{e}^{-d^{*}t},

for some constant
C>0
. Combined with the first two terms in (
5.20
) this implies the desired result, since
\mathcal{K}_{\alpha}
is bounded.

\bm{\mathcal{K}_{\widetilde{\alpha}}}
tends to infinity.
In this case, we let
C^{\prime}>2(\theta-d^{*})^{-1}
be a constant and split the sum in (
5.20
) into two parts, namely

(5.23)

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{M-1}\mathrm{e}^{-d^{*}t-\mathcal{K}_{\alpha}(t-(i+1))-(\theta-d^{*})i+\mathcal{O}(1)}={}

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{C^{\prime}\mathcal{K}_{\widetilde{\alpha}}(t)}\mathrm{e}^{-d^{*}t-\mathcal{K}_{\alpha}(t-(i+1))-(\theta-d^{*})i+\mathcal{O}(1)}

\displaystyle+\sum_{i=C^{\prime}\mathcal{K}_{\widetilde{\alpha}}(t)}^{M-1}\mathrm{e}^{-d^{*}t-\mathcal{K}_{\alpha}(t-(i+1))-(\theta-d^{*})i+\mathcal{O}(1)}.

We note that this is possible since
\mathcal{K}_{\widetilde{\alpha}}(t)=o(t)
(this follows in the same way as
\mathcal{K}_{\alpha}(t)=o(t)
in Lemma
5.5
(c)
) and
M\geq(1-\varepsilon)t
. Using the second part of Lemma
5.5
(c)
yields

(5.24)

\mathcal{K}_{\alpha}(t-(i+1))-\mathcal{K}_{\alpha}(t)=o(i)\quad\text{for any }i\leq C^{\prime}\mathcal{K}_{\widetilde{\alpha}}(t).

As a result, since
\theta>d^{*}
, the first sum on the right-hand side of (
5.23
) can be bounded from above for all
t
large by

(5.25)

\mathrm{e}^{-d^{*}t-\mathcal{K}_{\alpha}(t)}\sum_{i=0}^{C^{\prime}\mathcal{K}_{\widetilde{\alpha}}(t)}\mathrm{e}^{-(\theta-d^{*}+o(1))i+\mathcal{O}(1)}\leq C_{2}\mathrm{e}^{-d^{*}t-\mathcal{K}_{\alpha}(t)},

for some constant
C_{2}>0
. For the second sum on the right-hand side of (
5.23
), we bound for each index
i
in the sum,

(5.26)

-\mathcal{K}_{\alpha}(t-(i+1))\leq\sup_{x\in[\varepsilon t,t]}|\mathcal{K}_{\alpha}(x)|\leq\sup_{x\in[\varepsilon t,t]}\mathcal{K}_{\widetilde{\alpha}}(x)=\mathcal{K}_{\widetilde{\alpha}}(t),

where the final step uses that
\mathcal{K}_{\widetilde{\alpha}}
is increasing. As a result, we obtain

(5.27)

\sum_{i=C^{\prime}\mathcal{K}_{\widetilde{\alpha}}(t)}^{M-1}\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\exp(-d^{*}t-\mathcal{K}_{\alpha}(t-(i+1))-(\theta-d^{*})i+\mathcal{O}(1))\leq\exp(-d^{*}t+\mathcal{K}_{\widetilde{\alpha}}(t)-(\theta-d^{*})C^{\prime}\mathcal{K}_{\widetilde{\alpha}}(t)+\mathcal{O}(1)).

By the choice of
C^{\prime}
, the upper bound is at most

(5.28)

\exp(-d^{*}t-\mathcal{K}_{\widetilde{\alpha}}(t)+\mathcal{O}(1))\leq\exp(-d^{*}t-\mathcal{K}_{\alpha}(t)+\mathcal{O}(1)).

Combined with the bound in (
5.25
) for the first sum on the right-hand side of (
5.23
) and the first two terms in (
5.20
), we thus arrive at the desired result and conclude the proof.
∎

5.2.2.
Non-survival of old individuals

We conclude this sub-section by proving asymptotic results for
O_{t}^{\mathrm{ct}}
. In essence, we show that all individuals that are ‘too old’ have all died by time
t
and that there exists ‘young enough’ individuals that survive up to time
t
with probability close to one. This is made precise in the following result.

Proposition 5.7

(The oldest alive individual)
.

Suppose that the sequences
b
and
d
are such that Assumptions (
N-E
), (
F-D
), (
Ma
), and (
C-V
) are satisfied. Recall
R
from (
2.14
) and recall that the event
\mathcal{S}
denotes that the branching process
\mathrm{BP}
survives. Suppose that
\lim_{i\to\infty}d(i)=d^{*}\in[0,R)
and that Assumption
\mathcal{K}_{\alpha}
is satisfied. Then, for any
\varepsilon,A>0
there exist
K,t^{\prime}>0
such that for all
t>t^{\prime}
,

(5.29)

\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{S}}\left(\forall s\in[t-A,t+A]:\Big|O_{s}^{\mathrm{ct}}-\frac{d^{*}}{\lambda^{*}+d^{*}}t-\frac{1}{\lambda^{*}+d^{*}}\mathcal{K}_{\alpha}(r(t))\Big|<K\right)>1-\varepsilon.

Proof.

For
K>0
, we define

(5.30)

\ell_{t}\coloneq\frac{d^{*}}{\lambda^{*}+d^{*}}t+\frac{1}{\lambda^{*}+d^{*}}\mathcal{K}_{\alpha}(r(t))-K,\qquad u_{t}\coloneq\frac{d^{*}}{\lambda^{*}+d^{*}}t+\frac{1}{\lambda^{*}+d^{*}}\mathcal{K}_{\alpha}(r(t))+K.

Then, as
O_{s}^{\mathrm{ct}}
is increasing in
s
,

(5.31)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{S}}{}

\displaystyle\bigg(\forall s\in[t-A,t+A]:\Big|O^{\mathrm{ct}}_{s}-\frac{d^{*}}{\lambda^{*}+d^{*}}t-\frac{1}{\lambda^{*}+d^{*}}\mathcal{K}_{\alpha}(r(t))\Big|<K\bigg)

\displaystyle\geq 1-\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{S}}\left(O^{\mathrm{ct}}_{t-A}\leq\ell_{t}\right)-\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{S}}\left(O^{\mathrm{ct}}_{t+A}\geq u_{t}\right).

By conditioning on the number of individuals born up to time
\ell_{t}
and using a union bound, we bound the first probability on the right-hand side from above by

(5.32)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{S}}\left(O^{\mathrm{ct}}_{t-A}\leq\ell_{t}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{S}}\left(\exists v\in\mathcal{B}(0,\ell_{t}):L^{(v)}>t-A-\mathcal{B}(v)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{M}{\mathbb{P}\!\left(\mathcal{S}\right)}\mathrm{e}^{\lambda^{*}\ell_{t}}\mathbb{P}\!\left(L>t-A-\ell_{t}\right)+\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{S}}\left(\sup_{t\geq 0}|\mathcal{B}(0,t)|\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda^{*}t}>M\right).

The second probability on the right-hand side is at most
\varepsilon/4
when we choose
M=M(\varepsilon)
sufficiently large, as follows from (
5.13
) in Corollary
5.4
. For the first probability, we use Lemma
5.6
and the definition of
\ell_{t}
to further bound this from above by

(5.33)

\displaystyle\exp\big(\lambda^{*}{}

\displaystyle\ell_{t}-d^{*}(t-A-\ell_{t})-\mathcal{K}_{\alpha}(t-A-\ell_{t})+C_{1}\big)

\displaystyle=\exp\Big({}

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{\alpha}(r(t))-\mathcal{K}_{\alpha}\Big(\frac{\lambda^{*}}{\lambda^{*}+d^{*}}t-\frac{1}{\lambda^{*}+d^{*}}\mathcal{K}_{\alpha}(r(t))+K-A\Big)-(\lambda^{*}+d^{*})K+d^{*}A+C_{1}\Big),

where
C_{1}>0
is a sufficiently large constant. We use Lemma
5.5
(c)
to obtain that for any
K,A>0
there exists
t^{\prime}=t^{\prime}(K,A)>0
such that for all
t\geq t^{\prime}
,

(5.34)

\Big|\mathcal{K}_{\alpha}\Big(\frac{\lambda^{*}}{\lambda^{*}+d^{*}}t-\frac{1}{\lambda^{*}+d^{*}}\mathcal{K}_{\alpha}(r(t))+K-A\Big)-\mathcal{K}_{\alpha}\Big(\frac{\lambda^{*}}{\lambda^{*}+d^{*}}t-\frac{1}{\lambda^{*}+d^{*}}\mathcal{K}_{\alpha}(r(t))\Big)\Big|<1.

Then, Assumption
\mathcal{K}_{\alpha}
yields that there exists a constant
C_{2}>0
such that for all
t>0
,

(5.35)

\Big|\mathcal{K}_{\alpha}\Big(\frac{\lambda^{*}}{\lambda^{*}+d^{*}}t-\frac{1}{\lambda^{*}+d^{*}}\mathcal{K}_{\alpha}(r(t))\Big)-\mathcal{K}_{\alpha}(r(t))\Big|\leq C_{2}.

We thus arrive for all
t\geq t^{\prime}
at the upper bound

(5.36)

\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{S}}\left(O^{\mathrm{ct}}_{t-A}\leq\ell_{t}\right)\leq\exp\big(C_{2}+1-(\lambda^{*}+d^{*})K+d^{*}A+C_{1}\big)+\varepsilon/4\leq\varepsilon/2,

where we choose
K
sufficiently large so that the exponential term is at most
\varepsilon/4
in the final step. We note that we can first choose such a
K
and then take
t^{\prime}=t^{\prime}(K,A)
sufficiently large such that (
5.34
) holds.

We then bound the second probability on the right-hand side of (
5.31
). In a similar way as for the first term, with
\widetilde{u}_{t}=u_{t}-K/2
,

(5.37)

\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{S}}\left(O^{\mathrm{ct}}_{t+A}\geq u_{t}\right)\leq\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{S}}\left(\forall v\in\mathcal{B}(\widetilde{u}_{t},u_{t}):L^{(v)}\leq t+A-\widetilde{u}_{t}\right).

For ease of writing, we define the events

(5.38)

\mathcal{E}_{t}\coloneq\{\forall v\in\mathcal{B}(\widetilde{u}_{t},u_{t}):L^{(v)}\leq t+A-\widetilde{u}_{t}\},\qquad\mathcal{D}_{t}\coloneq\{|\mathcal{B}(\widetilde{u}_{t},u_{t})|>m\}\quad\text{for }m\in\mathbb{N},t\geq 0.

We can thus bound

(5.39)

\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{S}}\left(O^{\mathrm{ct}}_{t+A}\geq u_{t}\right)\leq\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{S}}\left(\mathcal{E}_{t}\right)=\mathbb{P}\!\left(\mathcal{S}\right)^{-1}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{S}}\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{E}_{t}}\right]\leq\mathbb{P}\!\left(\mathcal{S}\right)^{-1}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{S}}\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{E}_{t}}\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{D}_{t}}\right]+\mathbb{P}\!\left(\mathcal{S}\right)^{-1}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{S}}\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{D}_{t}^{c}}\right].

The second term on the right-hand side equals the probability of the event
\mathcal{D}_{t}^{c}
under the conditional probability measure
\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{S}}
. For the first term, we condition on two things. First, we condition
\mathcal{F}_{\widetilde{u}_{t}}
, the
\sigma
-algebra generated by the branching process up to time
\widetilde{u}_{t}
. Second, we condition on
|\mathcal{B}(\widetilde{u}_{t},u_{t})|
, the number of individuals born in the branching process in the interval
(\widetilde{u}_{t},u_{t})
. We can thus write

(5.40)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\!\left(\mathcal{S}\right)^{-1}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{S}}\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{E}_{t}}\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{D}_{t}}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\!\left(\mathcal{S}\right)^{-1}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{S}}\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{E}_{t}}\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{D}_{t}}\,|\,\mathcal{F}_{\widetilde{u}_{t}},|\mathcal{B}(\widetilde{u}_{t},u_{t})|\right]\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\!\left(\mathcal{S}\right)^{-1}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{D}_{t}}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{E}_{t}}\,|\,\mathcal{F}_{\widetilde{u}_{t}},|\mathcal{B}(\widetilde{u}_{t},u_{t})|\right]\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\!\left(\mathcal{S}\right)^{-1}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{D}_{t}}\mathbb{P}\!\left(\mathcal{E}_{t}\,|\,\mathcal{F}_{\widetilde{u}_{t}},|\mathcal{B}(\widetilde{u}_{t},u_{t})|\right)\right].

Conditionally on
|\mathcal{B}(\widetilde{u}_{t},u_{t})|
and upon the event
\mathcal{D}_{t}
, we can use the independence of the lifetimes of distinct individuals to bound the terms in the expected value from above by

(5.41)

\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{D}_{t}}\mathbb{P}\!\left(\mathcal{E}_{t}\,|\,\mathcal{F}_{\widetilde{u}_{t}},|\mathcal{B}(\widetilde{u}_{t},u_{t})|\right)\leq\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{D}_{t}}\big(1-\mathbb{P}\!\left(L\geq t+A-\widetilde{u}_{t}\right)\big)^{m}\leq\exp\big(-m\mathbb{P}\!\left(L\geq t+A-\widetilde{u}_{t}\right)\big),

where we have omitted the indicator random variable and used that
1-x\leq\mathrm{e}^{-x}
for
x\in\mathbb{R}
in the last step. Combining all of the above we thus arrive at

(5.42)

\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{S}}\left(O_{t+A}^{\mathrm{ct}}\geq u_{t}\right)\leq\mathbb{P}\!\left(\mathcal{S}\right)^{-1}\exp\big(-m\mathbb{P}\!\left(L\geq t+A-\widetilde{u}_{t}\right)\big)+\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{S}}\left(|\mathcal{B}(\widetilde{u}_{t},u_{t})|\leq m\right).

We choose
m=M^{-1}\exp(\lambda^{*}u_{t})
for a large constant
M>0
to be determined later to obtain the upper bound

(5.43)

\mathbb{P}\!\left(\mathcal{S}\right)^{-1}\exp\big(-M^{-1}\mathbb{P}\!\left(L\geq t+A-\widetilde{u}_{t}\right)\mathrm{e}^{\lambda^{*}u_{t}}\big)+\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{S}}\left(|\mathcal{B}(\widetilde{u}_{t},u_{t})|\leq M^{-1}\mathrm{e}^{\lambda^{*}u_{t}}\right).

We bound the first term from above and take care of the second term later. We use Lemma
5.6
and the definition of
u_{t}
and
\widetilde{u}_{t}
to arrive at the upper bound

(5.44)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\!\left(\mathcal{S}\right)^{-1}\exp\big(-M^{-1}\exp\big(-d^{*}(t{}

\displaystyle+A-\widetilde{u}_{t})-\mathcal{K}_{\alpha}(t+A-\widetilde{u}_{t})-C_{3}+\lambda^{*}u_{t}\big)\big)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{S})^{-1}\exp\Big(-M^{-1}\exp\Big({}

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{\alpha}(r(t))-\mathcal{K}_{\alpha}\Big(\frac{\lambda^{*}}{\lambda^{*}+d^{*}}t-\frac{1}{\lambda^{*}+d^{*}}\mathcal{K}_{\alpha}(r(t))-\frac{K}{2}\Big)

\displaystyle+(d^{*}/2+\lambda^{*})K-d^{*}A-C_{3}\Big)\Big),

where
C_{3}>0
is a large constant. We again use Lemma
5.5
(c)
and Assumption
\mathcal{K}_{\alpha}
to obtain similar bounds as in (
5.34
) and (
5.35
). We thus obtain, for some constants
C_{4}>0
and
t^{\prime}=t^{\prime}(K,A)>0
that for all
t>t^{\prime}
we have have the upper bound

(5.45)

\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{S})^{-1}\exp\Big(-M^{-1}\exp\Big(C_{4}+(d^{*}/2+\lambda^{*})K-d^{*}A-C_{3}\Big)\Big).

Given
A
, we bound
C_{4}-d^{*}A-C_{3}\geq-C_{5}
for some large constant
C_{5}=C_{5}(A,d^{*})
¿0. Then, we choose
K
sufficiently large so that the entire term is at most
\varepsilon/4
. Again, we can first choose such a
K
and then choose
t^{\prime}
.

It remains to bound the final term in (
5.43
) from above by
\varepsilon/4
to obtain the desired result. To this end, we write

(5.46)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{S}}\left(|\mathcal{B}(\widetilde{u}_{t},u_{t})|\leq M^{-1}\mathrm{e}^{\lambda^{*}u_{t}}\right)={}

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{S}}\left(|\mathcal{B}(0,u_{t})|\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda^{*}u_{t}}-|\mathcal{B}(0,\widetilde{u}_{t})|\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda^{*}\widetilde{u}_{t}-\lambda^{*}K/2}\leq M^{-1}\right)

\displaystyle\leq{}

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{S}}\left(|\mathcal{B}(0,u_{t})|\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda^{*}u_{t}}\leq\frac{2}{M}\right)

\displaystyle+\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{S}}\left(|\mathcal{B}(0,\widetilde{u}_{t})|\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda^{*}u_{t}}\geq\frac{1}{M}\mathrm{e}^{\lambda^{*}\frac{K}{2}}\right).

We can bound the first term on the right-hand side from above by

(5.47)

\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{S}}\left(\inf_{t\geq 0}|\mathcal{B}(0,t)|\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda^{*}t}\leq\frac{2}{M}\right),

which can be made smaller than
\varepsilon/6
when taking
M
sufficiently large by (
5.14
) in Corollary
5.4
. Given such a fixed
M
, we then choose
K
sufficiently large so that, by (
5.13
) in Corollary
5.3
,

(5.48)

\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{S}}\left(\sup_{t\geq 0}|\mathcal{B}(0,t)|\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda^{*}t}\geq\frac{1}{M}\mathrm{e}^{\lambda^{*}K/2}\right)<\varepsilon/12.

Combined, we thus obtain

(5.49)

\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{S}}\left(|\mathcal{B}(\widetilde{u}_{t},u_{t})|\leq M^{-1}\mathrm{e}^{\lambda^{*}u_{t}}\right)<\varepsilon/4,

as desired, which concludes the proof.
∎

5.3.
The richest individual

In this sub-section we provide an asymptotic expansion of
I^{\mathrm{ct}}_{t}
, the birth-time of the richest individual in
\mathrm{BP}(t)
. That is, the individual that is alive at time
t
and has produced the largest offspring by time
t
. To this end, we show that individuals that are ‘too young’ produce ‘small’ offspring, whereas there exist ‘old’ individuals that produce large offspring. Combined, these then imply that
I_{t}^{\mathrm{ct}}
is close to
O_{t}^{\mathrm{ct}}
.

5.3.1.
Young individuals have small offspring and no individual has too large offspring

We start by showing that individuals that are alive in an interval of time centred at
t
but born ‘too late’, that is, born too long after the birth-time
O_{t}^{\mathrm{ct}}
of the oldest alive individual (at time
t
), have too small offspring to attain the largest offspring among all alive individuals. Similarly, we show that there does not exist any individual that attains a ‘too large’ offspring by time
\approx t
.

Fix
C_{1},C_{1}^{\prime}>0
and set

(5.50)

\displaystyle k

\displaystyle\coloneq\lceil\varphi_{1}^{-1}\big(\tfrac{\lambda^{*}}{\lambda^{*}+d^{*}}t-\tfrac{1}{\lambda^{*}+d^{*}}\mathcal{K}_{\alpha}(r(t))-C_{1}\big)\rceil,\qquad u_{t}\coloneq\frac{d^{*}}{\lambda^{*}+d^{*}}t+\frac{1}{\lambda^{*}+d^{*}}\mathcal{K}_{\alpha}(r(t)),

\displaystyle k^{\prime}

\displaystyle\coloneq\lceil\varphi_{1}^{-1}\big(\tfrac{\lambda^{*}}{\lambda^{*}+d^{*}}t-\tfrac{1}{\lambda^{*}+d^{*}}\mathcal{K}_{\alpha}(r(t))+C_{1}^{\prime}\big)\rceil,

where we recall the function
r
from Assumption
\mathcal{K}_{\alpha}
. Then, we have the following result.

Lemma 5.8
.

Suppose
b
and
d
satisfy Assumptions (
N-E
), (
F-D
), (
Ma
), and (
C-V
). Recall
R
from (
2.14
) and suppose that
\lim_{i\to\infty}d(i)=d^{*}\in[0,R)
. Finally, suppose that Assumption
\mathcal{K}_{\alpha}
is satisfied. For any
\varepsilon,A,C_{1}>0
there exist
C_{2},t^{\prime}>0
such that for all
t>t^{\prime}
,

(5.51)

\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{S}}\left(\forall s\in[t-A,t+A]\ \forall v\in\mathcal{B}(u_{t}+C_{2},t)\cap\mathcal{A}_{s}^{\mathrm{ct}}:\deg_{s}(v)<k\right)\geq 1-\varepsilon.

Similarly, for any
\varepsilon,A>0
there exists
C_{1}^{\prime},t^{\prime}>0
such that for all
t>t^{\prime}
,

(5.52)

\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{S}}\left(\forall s\in[t-A,t+A]\ \forall v\in\mathcal{A}_{s}^{\mathrm{ct}}:\deg_{s}(v)<k^{\prime}\right)\geq 1-\varepsilon.

The first result states that all individuals born ‘too late’, namely after time
u_{t}+C_{2}
, that survive up to time
s\in[t-A,t+A]
, have a degree smaller than
k
with probability close to
1
. The second result tells us that no individual that survives up to time
s\in[t-A,t+A]
has degree larger than
k^{\prime}
with probability close to one.

Proof.

We bound the probability of the complement of the event of interest from above by
\varepsilon
. That is, we bound

(5.53)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{S}}{}

\displaystyle\big(\exists s\in[t-A,t+A]\ \exists v\in\mathcal{B}(u_{t}+C_{2},t)\cap\mathcal{A}_{s}^{\mathrm{ct}}:\deg_{s}(v)\geq k\big)

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{S}}\left(\exists s\in[t-A,t+A]\ \exists v\in\mathcal{B}(u_{t}+C_{2},t):D^{(v)}\geq k,S_{k}^{(v)}\leq s-\mathcal{B}(v),S^{(v)}_{D^{(v)}+1}>s-\mathcal{B}(v)\right).

We now introduce the event
\{\sup_{t\geq 0}|\mathcal{B}(0,t)|\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda^{*}t}\leq M\}
, for some large but fixed
M>0
to be determined. We then partition the set
\mathcal{B}(u_{t},t)
into sets of individuals born in the intervals
(u_{t}+C_{2}+(j-1),u_{t}+C_{2}+j)
, i.e. into sets
\mathcal{B}(u_{t}+C_{2}+(j-1),u_{t}+C_{2}+j)
, with
j\in[\lceil t-u_{t}-C_{2}\rceil]
and apply a union bound. For individuals
v\in\mathcal{B}(u_{t}+C_{2}+(j-1),u_{t}+C_{2}+j)
, we can bound their birth-time
\mathcal{B}(v)
from below and above by
u_{t}+C_{2}+(j-1)
and
u_{t}+C_{2}+j
, respectively. Similarly we can bound
s
from below and above by
t-A
and
t+A
, respectively. As a result, we bound the above probability from above by

(5.54)

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{\lceil t-u_{t}-C_{2}\rceil}{}

\displaystyle\frac{M}{\mathbb{P}\!\left(\mathcal{S}\right)}\mathrm{e}^{\lambda^{*}(u_{t}+j)}\mathbb{P}\!\left(D\geq k,S_{k}\leq t+A-(u_{t}+C_{2}+(j-1)),S_{D+1}>t-A-(u_{t}+C_{2}+j)\right)

\displaystyle+\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{S}}\Big(\sup_{t\geq 0}|\mathcal{B}(0,t)|\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda^{*}t}\geq M\Big).

We now choose
M
large enough so that, by (
5.13
) in Corollary
5.4
, the probability on the second line is at most
\varepsilon/2
. It thus remains to bound the sum from above by
\varepsilon/2
to arrive at the desired result. For any
j\in[\lceil t-u_{t}\rceil]
, we omit the part of the event that
S_{D+1}>t-A-(u_{t}+C_{2}+j)
, use the independence of
D
and
S_{k}
and use a Chernoff bound with
\theta>0
to arrive at the upper bound

(5.55)

\mathbb{P}\!\left(D\geq k\right)\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{e}^{-\theta S_{k}}\right]\exp\big(\theta(t+A-(u_{t}+C_{2}+(j-1)))\big).

Applying the inequality
\log(1+x)\geq x-x^{2}/2
for
x>0
, we can bound

(5.56)

-\log\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{e}^{-\theta S_{k}}\right]=\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\log\Big(1+\frac{\theta}{b(i)+d(i)}\Big)\geq\theta\varphi_{1}(k)-\frac{\theta^{2}}{2}\varphi_{2}(k).

Since
\varphi_{2}(k)
is increasing and converges to
\varphi_{2}(\infty)\coloneq\lim_{k\to\infty}\varphi_{2}(k)
as
k
tends to infinity by Assumption (
C-V
), and by using Lemma
5.1
, we finally arrive at the upper bound

(5.57)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\!\left(D\geq k\right){}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{e}^{-\theta S_{k}}\right]\exp\big(\theta(t+A-(u_{t}+C_{2}+(j-1)))\big)

\displaystyle\leq\exp{}

\displaystyle\Big(-\rho_{1}(k)-\theta\varphi_{1}(k)+\frac{\theta^{2}}{2}\varphi_{2}(\infty)+\theta(t+A-(u_{t}+C_{2}+(j-1)))\Big).

Note that
\rho_{1}
and
\varphi_{1}
are increasing and that
\rho_{1}(x)=d^{*}\varphi_{1}(x)+\alpha(x)
for
x\geq 0
. Then, also using the definitions of
k
and
u_{t}
in (
5.50
), we obtain the upper bound

(5.58)

\displaystyle\exp\Big({}

\displaystyle-\frac{\lambda^{*}d^{*}}{\lambda^{*}+d^{*}}t+\frac{d^{*}}{\lambda^{*}+d^{*}}\mathcal{K}_{\alpha}(r(t))+d^{*}C_{1}-\mathcal{K}_{\alpha}\big(\tfrac{\lambda^{*}}{\lambda^{*}+d^{*}}t-\tfrac{1}{\lambda^{*}+d^{*}}\mathcal{K}_{\alpha}(r(t))-C_{1}\big)

\displaystyle+\theta\big(C_{1}-C_{2}+A-(j-1)\big)+\frac{\theta^{2}}{2}\varphi_{2}(\infty)\Big).

We apply Lemma
5.5
(c)
and Assumption
\mathcal{K}_{\alpha}
and set
\theta=(C_{2}+(j-1)-(A+C_{1}))/\varphi_{2}(\infty)
(which is positive for all
j\geq 1
when
C_{2}
is large), to bound this for all
t>t^{\prime}
by

(5.59)

\exp\Big(-\lambda^{*}\Big(\frac{d^{*}}{\lambda^{*}+d^{*}}t+\frac{1}{\lambda^{*}+d^{*}}\mathcal{K}_{\alpha}(r(t))\Big)+C+d^{*}C_{1}-\frac{1}{2}\frac{(C_{2}+(j-1)-(A+C_{1}))^{2}}{\varphi_{2}(\infty)}\Big),

where
C>0
and
t^{\prime}
are fixed constants, where
C
is independent of all other constants and parameters, and
t^{\prime}=t^{\prime}(C_{1},d^{*},\lambda^{*})
. By substituting this upper bound in the sum in (
5.54
), we can bound it from above by

(5.60)

\frac{M}{\mathbb{P}\!\left(\mathcal{S}\right)}\sum_{j=1}^{\lceil t-u_{t}-C_{2}\rceil}\exp\Big(\lambda^{*}C_{2}+\lambda^{*}j+C+d^{*}C_{1}-\frac{1}{2}\frac{(C_{2}+(j-1)-(A+C_{1}))^{2}}{\varphi_{2}(\infty)}\Big).

We now choose
C_{2}
large enough so that
\lambda^{*}-(C_{2}-(A+C_{1}))/\varphi_{2}(\infty)<0
. This implies that the summands are exponentially decreasing in
j
and are maximised when
j=1
. This yields, for some constant
c=c(C_{2})>0
, the upper bound

(5.61)

\frac{Mc}{\mathbb{P}\!\left(\mathcal{S}\right)}\exp\Big(\lambda^{*}C_{2}+\lambda^{*}+C+d^{*}C_{1}-\frac{1}{2}\frac{(C_{2}-(A+C_{1}))^{2}}{\varphi_{2}(\infty)}\Big).

The constant
c
is decreasing in
C_{2}
, so that we can make this upper bound smaller than
\varepsilon/2
by again choosing
C_{2}
sufficiently large. Using this in (
5.54
) thus yields the desired result.

For the second result in Lemma
5.8
, an analogous proof can be used (where, at least intuitively, one would take
C_{2}<0
such that
|C_{2}|
is large but fixed and
C_{1}<0
such that
|C_{1}|
is sufficiently large and set
C_{1}^{\prime}=-C_{1}
). We leave the details to the reader.
∎

5.3.2.
There exists an old individual with large offspring

The next step is to show that there exists an ‘old’ individual that survives until time
\approx t
and has a sufficiently large offspring. Combined with Proposition
5.7
and Lemma
5.8
, this implies that the birth-time of the individual with the largest offspring (i.e.
I_{t}^{\mathrm{ct}}
) cannot be ‘too large’ compared to
O_{t}^{\mathrm{ct}}
. We make this precise in the following result.

Lemma 5.9
.

Suppose
b
and
d
satisfy Assumptions (
N-E
), (
F-D
), (
Ma
), and (
C-V
). Recall
R
from (
2.14
) and suppose that
\lim_{i\to\infty}d(i)=d^{*}\in[0,R)
. Finally, suppose that Assumption
\mathcal{K}_{\alpha}
is satisfied. Recall
k=k(C_{1})
and
u_{t}
from (
5.50
). For any
\varepsilon,A>0
there exist
C_{1},t^{\prime}>0
such that for all
t>t^{\prime}
,

(5.62)

\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{S}}\left(\forall s\in[t-A,t+A]\ \exists v\in\mathcal{B}(u_{t},u_{t}+\tfrac{1}{2}C_{1})\cap\mathcal{A}_{s}^{\mathrm{ct}}:\deg_{s}(v)\geq k\right)\geq 1-\varepsilon.

Proof.

We first rewrite the probability as

(5.63)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{S}}{}

\displaystyle\big(\forall s\in[t-A,t+A]\ \exists v\in\mathcal{B}(u_{t},u_{t}+\tfrac{1}{2}C_{1}):D^{(v)}\geq k,S^{(v)}_{k}\leq s-\mathcal{B}(v),S^{(v)}_{D^{(v)}+1}>s-\mathcal{B}(v)\big)

\displaystyle\geq{}

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{S}}\big(\exists v\in\mathcal{B}(u_{t},u_{t}+\tfrac{1}{2}C_{1}):D^{(v)}\geq k,S^{(v)}_{k}\leq t-A-u_{t}-\tfrac{1}{2}C_{1},S^{(v)}_{D^{(v)}+1}>t+A-u_{t}\big).

Here we bound

and
\mathcal{B}(v)
from below and above to obtain the inequality. Similar to the steps in (
5.37
) through (
5.42
), we bound the right-hand side from below for any

by

(5.64)

\displaystyle 1{}

\displaystyle-\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{S}}\left(|\mathcal{B}(u_{t},u_{t}+\tfrac{1}{2}C_{1})|\leq m\right)

\displaystyle-\mathbb{P}\!\left(\mathcal{S}\right)^{-1}\exp\Big(-m\mathbb{P}\!\left(D\geq k,S_{k}\leq t-A-u_{t}-\tfrac{1}{2}C_{1},S_{D+1}>t+A-u_{t}\right)\Big).

We then bound the probability in the exponential term from below. For any
\eta>0
there exists
t^{\prime}>0
such that
d(i)\leq d^{*}+\eta
for all
i\geq k
and all
t>t^{\prime}
(since

tends to infinity with

). Hence, by using Lemma
5.2
,

(5.65)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}({}

\displaystyle D\geq k,S_{k}\leq t-A-u_{t}-\tfrac{1}{2}C_{1},S_{D+1}>t+A-u_{t})

\displaystyle\geq{}

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\!\left(D\geq k\right)\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbbm{1}\{S_{k}\leq t-u_{t}-A-\tfrac{1}{2}C_{1}\}\exp\big(-(d^{*}+\eta)(t-u_{t}-A-\tfrac{1}{2}C_{1}-S_{k})\big)\right]

\displaystyle\times\exp\big(-(d^{*}+\eta)(2A+\tfrac{1}{2}C_{1})\big).

We bound the expected value from below by restricting the range of
S_{k}
in the indicator to
S_{k}\in[t-u_{t}-A-C_{1},t-u_{t}-A-\tfrac{1}{2}C_{1}]
, to obtain

(5.66)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[{}

\displaystyle\mathbbm{1}\{S_{k}\leq t-u_{t}-A-\tfrac{1}{2}C_{1}\}\exp\big(-(d^{*}+\eta)(t-u_{t}-A-\tfrac{1}{2}C_{1}-S_{k})\big)\big]

\displaystyle\geq{}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[\mathbbm{1}\{S_{k}\in[t-u_{t}-A-C_{1},t-u_{t}-A-\tfrac{1}{2}C_{1}]\}\exp\big(-(d^{*}+\eta)(t-u_{t}-A-\tfrac{1}{2}C_{1}-S_{k})\big)\big]

\displaystyle\geq{}

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\!\left(S_{k}\in[t-u_{t}-A-C_{1},t-u_{t}-A-\tfrac{1}{2}C_{1}]\right)\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{1}{2}(d^{*}+\eta)C_{1}}.

We then use that

converges almost surely to some random variable

as

tends to infinity, since

converges by Assumption (

). By the choice of

tends to infinity (since

(5.68)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\!\left(S_{k}\in[t-u_{t}-A-C_{1},t-u_{t}-A-\tfrac{1}{2}C_{1}]\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\!\left(S_{k}-\varphi_{1}(k)\in[-A+o(1),-A+\tfrac{1}{2}C_{1}+o(1)]\right)

We observe that the right-hand side is strictly positive for any choice of

for all

) in (

\mathbb{P}(D\geq k,S_{k}\leq t-A-u_{t},S_{D+1}>t+A-u_{t}+\tfrac{1}{2}C_{1})\geq c_{\infty}(A,C_{1})\mathbb{P}\!\left(D\geq k\right)\mathrm{e}^{-(d^{*}+\eta)(2A+C_{1})}.

Then, we use Lemma

. As in (

in (

) and (

(5.71)

\displaystyle\rho_{1}(k)={}

\displaystyle\rho_{1}\big(\varphi_{1}^{-1}\big(\tfrac{\lambda^{*}}{\lambda^{*}+d^{*}}t-\tfrac{1}{\lambda^{*}+d^{*}}\mathcal{K}_{\alpha}(r(t))-C_{1}\big)\big)+o(1)

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle d^{*}\Big[\frac{\lambda^{*}}{\lambda^{*}+d^{*}}t-\frac{1}{\lambda^{*}+d^{*}}\mathcal{K}_{\alpha}(r(t))-C_{1}\Big]+\mathcal{K}_{\alpha}\big(\tfrac{\lambda^{*}}{\lambda^{*}+d^{*}}t-\tfrac{1}{\lambda^{*}+d^{*}}\mathcal{K}_{\alpha}(r(t))-C_{1}\big)+o(1).

Using Lemma
5.5
(c)
and Assumption
\mathcal{K}_{\alpha}
, we can bound the right-hand side from above for all

6.
Discussion and Open Problems

In this article we have analysed the existence persistent
m
-hubs and the occurrence of persistence for the
preferential attachment tree with vertex death
model. In both the ‘infinite lifetime’ regime and the ‘finite lifetime’ regime, we have seen that the summability condition in (
C-V
) is crucial for the emergence of either persistent hubs or of persistence. Complemented by the study of lack of persistence in
[
23
]
, this provides a good overview of the different regimes and the behaviour therein. Still, there are several further extensions and open problems that we would like to state here.

Characterising the difference between the richest and oldest individual.
In the ‘rich are old’ regime, we have showed that the difference between the birth-time of the richest and oldest individual in
(\mathrm{BP}(t))_{t\geq 0}
, that is,
I^{\mathrm{ct}}_{t}-O^{\mathrm{ct}}_{t}
, is a tight sequence of random variables with respect to the probability measure
\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{S}}
. It remains unclear if more precise results can be proved.

Open Problem 6.1
.

Under the conditions in Theorem
2.6
, characterize the random process
(I^{\mathrm{ct}}_{t}-O^{\mathrm{ct}}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
.

Bounded non-converging death rates.
The results presented in the ‘finite lifetime’ regime do not consider the case when the death rates are bounded but do not converge. This is mainly due to the difficulty to determine the lifetime distribution of individuals in complete generality. As an example, let us consider the case of converging birth rates and alternating convergent death rates. That is, take three sequences
(c_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
,
(d_{1,i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
and
(d_{2,i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
such that
\lim_{i\to\infty}c_{i}=c>0
and
\lim_{i\to\infty}d_{j,i}=d_{j}
for
j\in\{1,2\}
, with
d_{1}\neq d_{2}\geq 0
. We set

(6.1)

d=(d_{1,1},d_{2,1},d_{1,2},d_{2,2},\ldots),\qquad b(i)=c_{i}.

We then claim (without proof) that the lifetime distribution satisfies

(6.2)

\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{t}\log\mathbb{P}\!\left(L>t\right)=-\min\bigg\{c+\frac{d_{1}+d_{2}}{2}-\sqrt{c^{2}+\Big(\frac{d_{1}-d_{2}}{2}\Big)^{2}},R\bigg\}=:-\theta.

This already non-trivial result can be obtained due to the somewhat simple choice of the birth and death rates. More general birth or death rates (e.g.
b
that diverge to infinity) make it much harder to determine the limiting value of
\theta
that characterises the exponential decay of the lifetime distribution.

We expect that in this more general setting, persistence can be shown to occur when
\theta<R
and when Assumption (
C-V
) is satisfied. We leave this as an open problem.

Open Problem 6.2
.

Suppose the sequences
b
and
d
are such that Assumptions (
N-E
) and (
Ma
) are satisfied. Assume that
\theta\coloneq\lim_{t\to\infty}-\frac{1}{t}\log\mathbb{P}\!\left(L>t\right)
exists and recall
R
from (
2.14
). If
\theta<R
and Assumption (
C-V
), does persistence occur in the sense of (
P
)?

Non-tree graphs.
The analysis carried out here considers trees only. It would be interesting to also study evolving graphs with vertex death that are not trees, e.g. when every new vertex connects to
k>1
many alive vertices. The continuous-time embedding into a CMJ branching process is central in the analysis in this paper and naturally restricts us to the setting of trees.

In the case of
affine
preferential attachment graphs without vertex death, a procedure known as
collapsing branching processes
is used to extend the branching process analysis to non-tree graphs as well
[
21
]
. However, this is limited to the affine case only. When including vertex death, this could only have a potential when the death rates are affine as well. Even then, this introduces difficulties due to the fact that now the lifetime of distinct vertices could have dependencies, which further complicates the analysis.

Other modelling choices.
In the (embedding of the) PAVD model the offspring of an individual equals the number of children said individual has produced. Similarly, the rate at which an individual produces its next child or dies depends on the total number of children produced. Instead, one could let the birth and death rates depend on the number of
alive
children only. Either way of defining the model can be motivated from applications. However, analysing this alternative model presents more challenges, since now an individual needs to produce
n
children out of which
n-k
should die to have an offspring of size
k
. The birth and death rates of an individual thus depend the lifetimes of its children. Deijfen concludes from simulations presented in
[
13
]
that the asymptotic behaviour of the degree distribution should not change significantly, and we expect this to be the case for other properties such as persistence as well. Still, it would be interesting to further investigate these models too.

Appendix A
Persistent hubs in general CMJ branching processes

In this appendix we discuss how part of Theorem

can be proved for more general branching processes. To this end, we recall the general definition of CMJ branching processes at the start of Section
3

D
and the inter-birth time random variables
(X(i))_{i\in\mathbb{N}}

Assumption A.1
.

(i)

(X(i))_{i\in\mathbb{N}}

(ii)

(iii)

(iv)

\mathbb{P}\!\left(D=\infty\right)>0

Part
(i)
is natural for many classical branching process models (e.g. the Bienaymé-Galton-Watson branching process, the Yule branching process, and the Bellman-Harris branching process). Part

(iii)

(iv)
states that individual produce infinite offspring with positive probability, which is necessary for the (non-)existence of persistent hubs to be a non-trivial question. It is relatively straightforward to check that the CTPAVD branching process defined in Section
3
that we analyse in Sections
4

5

A.1
.

Let us now discuss the further assumptions used in Section
4

\mathcal{R}
, as in (
2.6
), is now defined as

(A.1)

\mathcal{R}\coloneq\sum_{k=1}^{D}\delta_{\sum_{i=1}^{k}X(i)}.

With

\mathcal{R}
and

its Laplace transform, we have

(A.2)

\widehat{\mu}(\lambda)=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{k=1}^{D}\exp\bigg(-\lambda\sum_{i=1}^{k}X(i)\bigg)\right].

Assumption (
F-L
) then remains identical, though now for the more general

stated here. Similar to the argument in (

), Assumption

(iii)
implies that Assumption (

) is equivalent to the existence of a

) is generalised to

(N-E’)

\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}X(i)=\infty\qquad\text{almost surely},

which implies that no individual can produce infinite offspring in finite time, preventing the branching process from growing infinitely large in finite time with positive probability. In Section
4
we worked with Assumption (
F-D
)
not
being satisfied, which ensured that individuals can produce infinite offspring with positive probability. This is now replaced by Assumption
A.1
(iv)
. Finally, Assumption (
C-V
), in light of Lemma
4.5
(and Remark
4.6
), is now replaced by the more general

(C-V’)

\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}(X(i)-X(i)^{\prime})<\infty\qquad\text{almost surely},

where
(X(i)^{\prime})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
is an i.i.d. copy of
(X(i))_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
.

With these more general definitions and assumptions at hand, we have the following result, which generalises part of Theorem
4.1
.

Theorem A.2
.

Consider the CMJ branching process
(\mathrm{BP}(t))_{t\geq 0}
as defined in Section
3
, where the offspring
D
and inter-birth times
(X(i))_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfy Assumption
A.1
.

(a)

If Assumption (
C-V’
) is not satisfied, then, for any
m\in\mathbb{N}
, the branching process
(\mathrm{BP}(t))_{t\geq 0}
does not contain a persistent
m
-hub
\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{S}}
-almost surely.

(b)

If Assumption (
C-V’
) is satisfied and there exists
\lambda>0
and
K\in\mathbb{N}
such that

(A.3)

\widehat{\mu}(\lambda)<1\qquad\text{and}\qquad\prod_{i=K+1}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{e}^{\lambda(X(i)^{\prime}-X(i))}\right]<\infty.

Then,
\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{S}}
-almost surely
(\mathrm{BP}(t))_{t\geq 0}
contains a persistent
1
-hub.

Remark A.3
.

Theorem
A.2
does not include the existence of persistent
m
-hubs for
m>1
. This is due to the fact that, in the proof of Theorem
4.1
(b), we rely on the memoryless property of the exponential distribution when
m>1
.

Part
(b)
does not require Assumption (
N-E’
), as it is implied by
\widehat{\mu}(\lambda)<1
and Assumption (
C-V’
).
\blacktriangleleft

Iyer
[
25
, Theorem 2.8]
present sufficient conditions under which a persistent
1
-hub, when it exists, is unique, in the sense that there exists a unique individual in the branching process such that it attains the largest offspring for all but a finite amount of time. This result can directly be extended to the more general class of CMJ branching process as well as to persistent
m
-hubs for any
m\in\mathbb{N}
. introduced in this appendix.

Theorem A.4

(Uniqueness of persistent
m
-hubs, Theorem 2.8 in
[
25
]
)
.

Consider the CMJ branching process
(\mathrm{BP}(t))_{t\geq 0}
as defined in Section
3
, where the offspring
D
and inter-birth times
(X(i))_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfy Assumption
A.1
. Fix
m\in\mathbb{N}
and suppose that
(\mathrm{BP}(t))_{t\geq 0}
contains a persistent
m
-hub
\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{S}}
-almost surely. Also suppose one of the following conditions is satisfied.

(1)

We have

(A.4)

\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\!\left(0\leq\sum_{i=1}^{k}(X(i)^{\prime}-X(i))-\sum_{i=1}^{k}X(i)<X(k+1)\right)<\infty.

(2)

Both of the following conditions hold:

(i)

We have
\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\!\left(X(i)\neq X(i)^{\prime}\right)=\infty
, or there exists
i\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\mathbb{P}\!\left(X(i)\neq X(i)^{\prime}\right)=1
.

(ii)

For any
\varepsilon>0
we have
\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\!\left(X(i)>\varepsilon\right)<\infty
.

Then, the persistent
m
-hub is
\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{S}}
-almost surely unique.

Remark A.5
.

We have used Condition (2) to prove the uniqueness of the persistent

-hub in the proof of Theorem
4.1
(b).

Adapting the proof of Theorem

[
25

(c_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}

c_{j}=0

\mathbb{P}\!\left(X(uj)-X(vj)=c_{j}\right)>0\quad\text{for all }j\in\mathbb{N},\text{ and}\quad\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\!\left(X(uj)-X(vj)\neq c_{j}\right)<\infty.

Hence, by the Borel-Cantelli lemma,
X(uj)=X(vj)
for all but finitely many
j
(as
c_{j}=0
for these
j
). As a result, we have that either (
4.26
) or (
4.27
) is satisfied, or that
\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{S}}
-almost surely,

(A.6)

\mathcal{B}(u)-\mathcal{B}(v)+\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}(X(uj)-X(vj))=0\qquad\text{for all }k\geq K_{0}.

\mathcal{B}(uk)\leq\mathcal{B}(vk)
for all
k\geq K_{0}
or
\mathcal{B}(vk)\leq\mathcal{B}(uk)
for all
k\geq K_{0}
, is still valid.

Finally, for Part

of Lemma
4.7
, replacing

with

for
