Introduction

Kidney transplantation is the preferred treatment for end-stage renal disease (ESRD), significantly reducing disease-related mortality by up to 80% in some studies[1]. However, long-term use of immunosuppressive medications in post-transplant patients increases their risk of developing malignancies[2]. Bladder urothelial carcinoma (formerly termed transitional cell carcinoma, TCC) occurs in 1.6–3.3% of kidney transplant recipients, typically emerging 2–12 years after transplantation. Post-transplant bladder tumors tend to be more aggressive, with higher grade, stage, and associated mortality compared to the general population[3–5]. A meta-analysis demonstrated that TCC incidence is higher among transplant recipients than among patients receiving dialysis[1]. In the general population, bladder cancer in adolescents is exceedingly rare[6]. To our knowledge, there are no reported cases of bladder urothelial carcinoma in adolescent patients with failed kidney transplants. This case report has been reported in line with the SCARE checklist[7].

Case presentation

A 17-year-old boy presented to our urology clinic with painless gross hematuria of 2 days’ duration. His medical history was remarkable for focal segmental glomerulosclerosis (FSGS), diagnosed at 1 year of age, which progressed to ESRD by age 3. He initially underwent hemodialysis for 1 year before receiving a living-related kidney transplant at the age of 4. Post-transplant, he was maintained on a long-term immunosuppressive therapy consisting of tacrolimus 2 g in the morning and 1 g in the evening, mycophenolate 360 mg twice daily, and prednisolone 5 mg once daily, and these medications have been continued since the age of 4.

At age 16, the patient developed recurrence of FSGS in the transplanted kidney, which led to progressive graft dysfunction and eventual graft failure. He was subsequently transitioned to regular peritoneal dialysis, while continuing immunosuppressive therapy. He also reported a 1-year history of cigarette smoking, amounting to approximately 1 pack-year.

On presentation, vital signs were stable (blood pressure: 140/90 mm Hg, pulse: 98/min, temperature: 37 °C, respiratory rate: 18/min, oxygen saturation: 98% on room air). The patient was alert, oriented, and in no respiratory distress. Chest examination revealed normal bilateral breath sounds and cardiac examination showed a regular rhythm with normal heart sounds. The abdomen was soft and non-tender, with a peritoneal dialysis catheter in situ. Mild bilateral lower-limb edema was noted, with no clinical signs of deep vein thrombosis.

Laboratory investigations showed anemia (Hb 9.8 g/dL) with normal coagulation profile and elevated serum creatinine consistent with ESRD. Urinalysis confirmed gross hematuria without evidence of infection. Abdominal ultrasound demonstrated an irregular urinary bladder with a posterior rounded soft-tissue mass measuring approximately 1.3 × 1.1 cm, showing internal vascularity, consistent with a bladder polypoid lesion, and the transplanted kidney appeared normal, with no evidence of hydronephrosis or suspicious focal masses. Abdominal and pelvic computed tomography scan without contrast demonstrated large intravesical clots but no upper tract lesions or lymphadenopathy.

The patient underwent diagnostic and therapeutic cystoscopy, which revealed extensive bladder clots and a papillary lesion on the left lateral bladder wall (Fig. 1). Transurethral resection of bladder tumor (TURBT) with clot evacuation was performed. Histopathological analysis revealed non-invasive high-grade papillary urothelial carcinoma. Immunohistochemical staining was diffusely positive for GATA3 and PanCK, confirming urothelial origin. The bladder tumor in this case corresponded to TNM staging of pTa high grade, N0, M0. Two months later, surveillance cystoscopy identified a recurrent papillary lesion adjacent to the original resection site. A second TURBT was performed, and histology once again confirmed high-grade non-invasive urothelial carcinoma (Fig. 2). Polymerase chain reaction testing for BK virus and cytomegalovirus was negative. Following multidisciplinary discussion, the patient was counseled on strict smoking cessation, continued close cystoscopic surveillance, and consideration of discontinuation of immunosuppressive therapy in view of the failed transplant. Adjuvant intravesical Bacillus Calmette–Guérin (BCG) immunotherapy was also discussed as part of ongoing management.

Discussion

Kidney transplantation is a well-established treatment for ESRD. Over recent decades, advances in surgical techniques and the development of more individualized immunosuppressive regimens have significantly improved outcomes. However, the prolonged use of immunosuppressive agents to maintain graft function increases the incidence of de novo malignancies by more than fivefold compared to the general population[8]. The oncogenic potential of immunosuppression is attributed to impaired immune surveillance, viral reactivation, and direct mutagenesis[2].

A meta-analysis published in 2009 reported that renal transplant recipients and patients with ESRD present with bladder cancer at more advanced stages and higher grades than the general population[9]. Specific risk factors in the post-transplant setting include polyomavirus infection, particularly BK virus (relative risk 11.7), and cigarette smoking (relative risk 5.6)[10].

Gross hematuria is the most common presenting symptom of bladder carcinoma in renal transplant patients and should always prompt suspicion of malignancy. Comprehensive evaluation with laboratory testing, radiological imaging, and cystoscopic biopsy is essential for timely diagnosis[11].

In young and adolescent transplant recipients, bladder malignancy is exceedingly rare; however, when it occurs, it is often aggressive and of high grade. Although isolated reports describe urothelial carcinoma in young patients, including cases associated with BK virus infection[11], there are no prior reports of high-grade bladder carcinoma in an adolescent with a failed renal transplant.

The early recurrence observed in this case suggests an underlying biologically aggressive tumor behavior. Early recurrence after TURBT is a recognized surrogate marker of aggressive urothelial biology and has been associated with high proliferative activity, genomic instability, and a propensity for rapid clonal regrowth. In immunosuppressed patients, impaired immune surveillance may further facilitate early tumor recurrence and progression by allowing residual malignant cells to evade host defense mechanisms[12–15]. Therefore, the rapid recurrence in this patient likely reflects a combination of intrinsic tumor aggressiveness and host-related immunological factors rather than technical inadequacy of resection alone.

Although smoking and BK virus infection were excluded, de novo bladder cancer has been reported in chronically immunosuppressed renal transplant recipients. Long-term immunosuppression impairs immune surveillance and increases the risk of urothelial malignancy independent of traditional risk factors[16]. Thus, immunosuppression represents the most plausible etiological factor in this rare presentation.

Our case of a 17-year-old boy who developed high-grade non-invasive urothelial carcinoma highlights important considerations. First, the continuation of immunosuppression in the setting of a failed graft may expose patients to unnecessary oncogenic risk. Second, even mild symptoms such as painless hematuria in this population must be carefully evaluated to exclude malignancy. Finally, smoking cessation is critical in kidney transplant recipients, as smoking synergistically increases the already elevated risk of urinary tract malignancies[17].

In our patient, we emphasized close cystoscopic surveillance, enforced smoking cessation, and considered discontinuation of immunosuppression along with intravesical BCG immunotherapy as part of ongoing management.

This case report is valuable due to its rarity and novelty, documenting high-grade urothelial carcinoma in an adolescent with a failed renal transplant – a scenario scarcely reported in the literature. It provides comprehensive clinical details, including history, laboratory findings, imaging, cystoscopy, histopathology, and tumor recurrence, which enhances its educational value for clinicians. The report also emphasizes modifiable risk factors, such as smoking, and highlights the impact of prolonged immunosuppression, offering practical insights into surveillance and management strategies. However, limitations include its single-patient design, which restricts generalizability, and the absence of long-term follow-up data on oncologic outcomes. Additionally, molecular analyses or genetic studies were not performed, which could have provided further insight into the pathogenesis of post-transplant bladder carcinoma in adolescents.

Conclusion

Although bladder cancer is uncommon in kidney transplant recipients – especially among pediatric and adolescent patients – any episode of painless gross hematuria in the post-transplant period should be regarded as a red flag and prompt a complete urological evaluation for early detection of urothelial carcinoma. We emphasize the importance of regular screening and strict follow-up in this population – as well as the elimination of modifiable risk factors, such as smoking – to reduce the incidence of urinary tract malignancies. In patients with failed renal grafts, discontinuation of unnecessary immunosuppressive therapy should also be carefully considered to minimize long-term oncologic risk.
