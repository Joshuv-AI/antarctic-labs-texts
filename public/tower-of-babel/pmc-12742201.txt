Antibacterial effects and remineralization potential of silver nanoparticles in a novel nanosilver fluoride formulation
Authors: Praphasri Rirattanapong, Chareerut Phruksaniyom, Suthara Asanahsark, Yuwapa Cheevarungnapakul, Thanapha Putthanuparp, Nisarat Ruangsawasdi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSilver-based therapies are effective in preventing caries but often cause undesirable tooth staining. This study aimed to develop a dual-action nanosilver-fluoride (NSF) formulation that arrests caries while minimizing discolouration.MethodsSilver nanoparticles (AgNPs) were synthesized via chemical reduction and characterized using transmission electron microscopy and dynamic light scattering. Various AgNP concentrations (0–150 µg/mL) were evaluated for their ability to inhibit Streptococcus mutans biofilm formation using a colorimetric assay, while bacterial viability within established biofilms was further assessed at AgNP concentrations of 5, 15, 50, 150, and 300 µg/mL through colony-forming unit (CFU) counts and live/dead staining analysed via confocal laser scanning microscopy. Brain Heart Infusion (BHI) medium and 1% NaOCl served as negative and positive controls, respectively. Next, the optimal AgNP concentration was combined with three fluoride concentrations (1,000 ppm, 20,000 ppm, and 40,000 ppm) to assess synergistic antibacterial effects compared with fluoride alone. Although all three AgNP–fluoride combinations inhibited biofilm formation, only those containing 20,000 ppm and 40,000 ppm fluoride exhibited significantly enhanced bactericidal activity. Therefore, two NSF formulations were developed for further analysis: NSFlow (300 µg/mL AgNPs + 20,000 ppm fluoride) and NSFhigh (300 µg/mL AgNPs + 40,000 ppm fluoride). These formulations were compared with silver diamine fluoride (SDF), fluoride varnish (Fvarnish), and untreated controls in terms of: (1) antibacterial activity (biofilm inhibition and bacterial susceptibility within biofilms), (2) fluoride release (measured using an ion-selective electrode; n = 6 per group), and (3) remineralization potential of artificial carious lesions (evaluated via microcomputed tomography; n = 10 per group). All experiments were conducted in triplicate, and statistical analyses were performed accordingly.ResultsThe synthesized AgNPs were spherical with an average diameter of 2.87 ± 0.96 nm and exhibited biofilm inhibition beginning at 2.34 µg/mL. At 300 µg/mL, AgNPs significantly reduced CFU and increased the percentage of dead bacteria compared with BHI, showing results comparable to 1% NaOCl. When 300 µg/mL AgNPs were combined with fluoride, all concentrations (1,000, 20,000, and 40,000 ppm) produced over 80% biofilm inhibition, similar to fluoride alone. However, the AgNP-fluoride combinations demonstrated greater bactericidal activity overall, showing lower CFU and higher percentages of dead bacteria than fluoride alone. Except the 1,000ppm fluoride combination, the percentage of dead bacteria was similar to that observed with fluoride alone. Accordingly, only NSFlow and NSFhigh were selected for further evaluation. Both NSF formulations, along with SDF and Fvarnish, significantly inhibited biofilm formation and decreased bacterial viability compared with the untreated control, with no statistically significant differences among the treatment groups. Fluoride release from NSF and SDF occurred rapidly within the first two days, after which concentrations dropped below the detection limit, whereas the release of Fvarnish was gradual and sustained over 7 days. Microcomputed tomography analysis revealed that only NSFhigh achieved remineralization levels comparable to those observed with SDF and Fvarnish.ConclusionsNSFhigh, combining 300 µg/mL AgNPs with 40,000 ppm fluoride, demonstrated strong antibacterial efficacy and remineralization potential equivalent to established treatments. This formulation shows promise as an effective, stain-reducing topical therapy for caries arrest.Keywords: Silver nanoparticles, AgNPs, Nanosilver fluoride, NSF, Anticaries, Topical fluoride therapy, Caries arrest

Introduction

Dental caries is one of the most common chronic diseases worldwide, affecting approximately 3.5 billion people in 2017 [1]. It is particularly concerning in young children, where it significantly impacts their physical, mental, and social quality [2]. To address this issue, fluoride has long been used for both the prevention and management of caries. It acts primarily through ion exchange, which inhibits demineralization and enhances remineralization [3–5]. Professionally applied topical fluoride provides a minimally invasive approach that is particularly beneficial for high-risk children and those in underserved communities [6, 7].

Among the available topical fluoride therapies, silver diamine fluoride (SDF) has exhibited superior clinical efficacy in halting carious lesion progression [8–11]. However, it presents challenges related to aesthetic concerns and side effects. SDF, which is composed of silver, fluoride, and ammonia, provides both antimicrobial properties and remineralization benefits and has been clinically proven to prevent and arrest caries in both primary and permanent teeth. Clinical guidelines recommend the biannual application of SDF for preschool-aged children [11, 12], with studies showing that it outperforms fluoride varnish (Fvarnish) in arresting cavitated caries. Despite its efficacy, a significant drawback of SDF is its tendency to cause black staining of carious lesions due to the oxidation of silver phosphate, which can negatively impact aesthetics, particularly in paediatric patients. While potassium iodide has been proposed as a method to mitigate staining, its effectiveness remains limited [9, 13, 14]. Additionally, transient adverse effects such as oral discomfort, a metallic taste, and mucosal staining have been reported in some cases [15, 16].

To address the limitations of SDF staining, silver nanoparticles (AgNPs) have emerged as promising alternatives because of their broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity and reduced risk of tooth discolouration. The small size of AgNPs increases their surface contact with bacterial cells, thereby enhancing their antimicrobial efficacy [17]. Compared with other nanoparticles, AgNPs have demonstrated greater bactericidal activity against Streptococcus mutans [17]. The synthesis of AgNPs requires control over particle size, shape, and stability to optimize their antimicrobial properties and minimize side effects. The choice of reducing agent, stabilizer, or solvent also influences their final characteristics. Given these advantages, AgNPs are increasingly incorporated into formulations such as nanosilver fluoride (NSF) to combine antimicrobial activity with the remineralization of fluoride, offering a promising solution for caries prevention and management.

Previous studies have incorporated AgNPs into a new topical fluoride formulation known as NSF [18–22], and the combination has been shown to arrest caries in a dose-dependent manner, providing protection for up to 12 months without causing discolouration [18–20]. While some studies have suggested promising results for NSF [21, 22], the interaction between fluoride ions and silver particles could reduce their individual effectiveness. Specifically, fluoride may aggregate with AgNPs, forming complex particles that diminish the efficacy of both agents, possibly interfering with the antibacterial activity of AgNPs [23]. Furthermore, the optimal particle size and delivery vehicles for NSF have yet to be determined, which limits its full potential for enhanced application and efficiency. Its effectiveness in remineralizing incipient enamel lesions also remains underexplored, with limited comparative studies available.

Given these insights, there is a need to develop a caries-arresting material that not only inhibits S. mutans biofilm formation and strongly promotes bactericidal activity but also promotes remineralization without causing aesthetic drawbacks. In our study, we aimed to synthesize AgNPs with controlled characteristics and enhanced antimicrobial properties for caries management. By adjusting key parameters such as concentration, pH, and reaction time, we sought to produce AgNPs that effectively target S. mutans while minimizing tooth discolouration. This study evaluated the effects of various concentrations of self-prepared AgNPs, their activity in combination with fluoride, and a novel NSF formulation on S. mutans biofilms in vitro and compared NSF with current caries therapies. Biofilm inhibition and susceptibility were assessed via microplate assays, colony-forming unit (CFU) enumeration, and confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM), whereas fluoride release and the depth of remineralization in artificial enamel lesions were measured using a fluoride-selective electrode and microcomputed tomography (micro-CT). Ultimately, this research aims to identify nonstaining, antibacterial agents for the management of paediatric caries.

Materials and methods

AgNP synthesis and characterization

AgNPs were synthesized via chemical reduction in the Pharmacology Department, Faculty of Dentistry, Mahidol University, Thailand. One gram of chitosan from shrimp shells following a previous method [24] was dissolved in 200 mL of 2% (v/v) acetic acid (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany). The solution was stirred overnight and filtered under vacuum. While stirring in an ice bath, a silver nitrate solution (0.012 mol/L) was added and mixed for 30 min. Sodium borohydride (Sigma Aldrich, St. Louis, Missouri, USA) was then added dropwise, maintaining a 1:6 molar ratio of silver nitrate to sodium borohydride. The morphology of the AgNPs was observed via transmission electron microscopy (TEM; JEM-2100, JEOL Ltd., Akishima, Tokyo, Japan). The samples were diluted with 100% ethanol and deposited onto carbon-coated copper grids. Imaging was conducted at magnifications of 40,000× and 500,000× with an accelerating voltage of 200 kV. A dynamic light scattering (DLS) particle size analyser (Zetasizer Nano ZS, Malvern Instruments Ltd., UK) was used to measure the particle size and dispersion. The synthesized AgNPs were stored at −80 °C until further use.

Optimal concentration of AgNPs for antimicrobial activity

To determine the optimal antimicrobial concentration, various AgNP concentrations were tested. For the biofilm inhibition test, ten concentrations (0.293, 0.586, 1.172, 2.344, 4.688, 9.375, 18.75, 37.5, 75, and 150 µg/mL) were prepared via twofold serial dilutions from a stock solution of 300 µg/mL in 1.5% sucrose tryptic soy broth (TSB; BD Difco™, Franklin Lakes, New Jersey, USA). The positive control was only 1.5% sucrose TSB, and the negative control was 1.5% sucrose TSB with S. mutans. For the biofilm susceptibility test, five concentrations (5, 15, 50, 150, and 300 µg/mL) of AgNPs were tested. The positive control was 1% sodium hypochlorite (NaOCl), and the negative control was brain heart infusion (BHI) broth (BD Difco™, Franklin Lakes, New Jersey, USA).

Antimicrobial efficacy of AgNPs formulated with fluoride

AgNPs (300 µg/mL) were combined with sodium fluoride (Fisher Scientific, Pittsburgh, Pennsylvania, USA) at final concentrations of 1,000, 20,000, and 40,000 ppm. For the biofilm inhibition test, 1.5% sucrose TSB was used as the positive control, and 1.5% sucrose TSB with S. mutans served as the negative control. For the biofilm susceptibility test, 1% sodium hypochlorite (NaOCl) and BHI broth were used as positive and negative controls, respectively.

Comparative evaluation of NSF with conventional therapies

The NSF formulation was prepared by mixing 80% AgNPs (300 µg/mL), 20,000 ppm NaF (NSFlow) or 40,000 ppm NaF (NSFhigh), 1% (w/v) carboxymethyl cellulose (CMC; Krungthepchemi, Bangkok, Thailand), and 5% ethanol. The antimicrobial efficacy of NSF was compared with that of SDF (Topamine®, DentaLife, Australia), while the remineralization efficacy was benchmarked with both SDF and fluoride varnish (Fvarnish; Duraphat®, New York, New York, USA). The control consisted of untreated samples.

Bacterial culture

Streptococcus mutans ATCC 35,688 (Manassas, Virginia, USA) was cultured on BHI agar and incubated at 37 °C for 48 h in an incubator (Thermo Scientific™; Waltham, Massachusetts, USA). Three to four colonies were subsequently selected and inoculated aerobically in BHI broth at 37 °C for 24 h. The bacterial suspension was standardized to 1.5 × 10⁸ CFU/mL using the 0.5 McFarland standard and then further diluted for use in biofilm assays.

Biofilm inhibition test

This assay was conducted to assess the preventive effect of test agents on biofilm formation following previously described with minor modifications [25], and performed in triplicate. Flat-bottom 96-well microtiter plates were used. A bacterial suspension (1.5 × 10⁶ CFU/mL in 5% sucrose TSB) was prepared. Each well received 100 µL of test solution followed by 100 µL of the bacterial suspension, and plates were incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. Experiments were performed in triplicate, with wells containing 5% sucrose TSB without bacteria serving as the control for calculations. After incubation, plates were inverted and rinsed five times with tap water to remove planktonic bacteria. The formed biofilms were stained with 200 µL of 0.1% crystal violet (Panreac, Spain) for 30 min at room temperature, washed, dried, and then treated with 30% acetic acid under shaken at 150 RPM for 10 min to extract the stain. Biofilm susceptibility test

To evaluate the bactericidal efficacy within biofilms, colony-forming unit (CFU) counts and live/dead staining were performed in triplicate according to previously established protocols [26, 27]. Biofilms were formed by incubating S. mutans (1.5 × 10⁶ CFU/mL) in 200 µL of 1.5% TSB in 96-well plates at 37 °C for 48 h. After washing, each well was treated with 50 µL of BHI broth mixed with 50 µL of the test agent for 5 min, followed by another wash with BHI broth. The biofilms were further incubated with 1.5% TSB at 37 °C for 24 h before being scraped with sterile pipette tips and transferred into glass tubes with 15 glass beads (ø 2 mm). The suspension was vortexed for 1 min, serially diluted in BHI broth, and plated on BHI agar. The CFUs were counted after 48 h of incubation at 37 °C by a blinded evaluator. The biofilms used for live/dead analysis were grown on 8-chamber glass slides (ibidi®, Grafelfing, Germany). Each chamber was filled with 250 µL of S. mutans suspension at a concentration of 1.5 × 10⁶ CFU/mL in TSB supplemented with 1.5% sucrose and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. Afterward, the biofilms were treated with the test solutions for 5 min as previously described, followed by three additional washes with 0.9% normal saline solution (NSS). The chambers were then replenished with 250 µL of BHI and incubated again at 37 °C for 24 h. To visualize the biofilms, the samples were stained with a BacLight™ bacterial viability kit (0.75 µM SYTO 9 and 3 µM propidium iodide; Thermo Scientific™, Waltham, MA, USA) in the dark for 15 min. The samples were then washed and fixed with 10% neutral-buffered formalin. Fluoride release evaluation

Fluoride release was measured over 7 days in treated human primary teeth. The inclusion criterion was sound, noncarious teeth obtained through natural exfoliation or routine extraction. Teeth exhibiting carious lesions, enamel cracks, or developmental defects were excluded. All the samples were examined under both wet and dry conditions. Six teeth per group were used, as calculated with G*Power software on the basis of our pilot study, with α = 0.05 and power = 95%. The study was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Faculty of Dentistry and Faculty of Pharmacy, Mahidol University (COE.No. MU-DT/PY-IRB 2018/055.1511 & 2019/058.0710). Thirty teeth were randomized into five groups: (1) Fvarnish (2) SDF, (3) NSFlow (300 µg/mL AgNPs + 20,000 ppm fluoride), (4) NSFhigh (300 µg/mL AgNPs + 40,000 ppm fluoride), and (5) untreated. All teeth were cleaned with pumice and cut 1 mm below the cementoenamel junction by a highspeed fissure carbide bur before being coated with nail polish, leaving a 3 × 3 mm² window on the labial surface. Remineralizing agents were similarly applied at 5 µL on the prepared labial surface via a micropipette. After fluoride application and a 3-min drying period, the teeth were immersed in 5 mL of deionized water at 37 °C. Every 24 h, the samples were transferred to fresh containers. Each 3 mL sample was mixed with 0.3 mL TISAB III buffer (Total Ionic Strength Adjustment Buffer III), and the fluoride concentration was measured using a fluoride-selective electrode (Thermo Fisher Scientific) calibrated with standard solutions. Data were recorded in parts per million (ppm) with a detection limit of 0.02 ppm.

Remineralization properties

The remineralization potential of the tested agents was evaluated using human primary anterior teeth. Ethical exemption was granted by the Institutional Review Board of the Faculty of Dentistry and Faculty of Pharmacy, Mahidol University (COE.No. MU-DT/PY-IRB 2018/055.1511 & 2019/058.0710). Sample size determination on the basis of our pilot study and calculation via G*Power software with α = 0.05 and power = 95% resulted in ten samples per group. The teeth (n = 50) were randomly allocated into five experimental groups: the Fvarnish, SDF, NSFlow, NSFhigh and untreated groups. Tooth selection and preparation were performed as described above for the samples used for the fluoride release test. Each sample was coated with nail varnish, leaving a 1 × 1 mm² window on the labial surface for artificial carious lesion formation and agent application. Artificial enamel lesions were induced by immersing each tooth in 3 mL of demineralizing solution consisting of 2.2 mM CaCl₂, 2.2 mM NaH₂PO₄, and 0.05 M acetic acid, adjusted to pH 4.4 with 1 M KOH, at 37 °C for 96 h. The solution was changed every 24 h. Following lesion formation, the teeth were rinsed, dried, and stored in artificial saliva containing 0.65 g/L KCl, 0.058 g/L MgCl₂, 0.165 g/L CaCl₂, 0.804 g/L K₂HPO₄, 0.365 g/L KH₂PO₄, and 2 g/L sodium carboxymethyl cellulose. The samples were stored for no more than four days before use. Baseline mineral density (MD_pre) was assessed via micro-CT. Teeth with lesion depths less than 120 μm or visible surface defects were excluded. Eligible samples were ultrasonically cleaned for one minute and dried with sterile tissue. Each tooth was positioned in utility wax within a silicone jig to enable standardized repositioning on the micro-CT stage. The labial surface was oriented horizontally, and two perpendicular reference lines were marked across the wax and jig to ensure consistent alignment. For all the treatment groups, 5 µL of agent was applied via a micropipette. The agents were allowed to penetrate the lesion for 4 min in the Fvarnish group and for 3 min in the SDF and both NSF groups, in accordance with the product recommendations. The treated surfaces were then wiped with clean tissue and ultrasonically cleaned for one minute. The samples were subjected to a seven-day pH-cycling regimen, with each daily cycle consisting of two three-hour demineralization periods and a two-hour remineralization period between them. Prior to each solution change, the samples were rinsed with 20 mL of deionized water, ultrasonically cleaned for one minute, and dried. The demineralizing solution was identical to that used for lesion formation, but the pH was adjusted to 4.7. The remineralizing solution consisted of 1.5 mM CaCl₂, 0.9 mM NaH₂PO₄, and 0.15 M KCl and was adjusted to pH 7.0 with 1 M KOH. Overnight immersion in the remineralizing solution was maintained throughout the protocol. Following the completion of pH cycling, the mineral density was re-evaluated (MD_post). To determine the mineral density, each tooth was repositioned in its original jig and scanned using a micro-CT system (Skyscan 1173, Bruker, Belgium) under the following settings: 70 kV source voltage, 114 µA current, 0.4° rotation step, and a 0.5 mm aluminum filter. The reconstructed 3D images were processed via NRecon software and further aligned via DataViewer software (Bruker, Billerica, Massachusetts, USA). The image resolution was 9.86 μm/pixel. Calibration was performed using hydroxyapatite phantoms with known mineral densities (0.25 and 0.75 g/cm³). Linear attenuation coefficients derived from phantom scans were used to convert grayscale values to mineral density values via CTAn software. The calibration accuracy was verified prior to sample analysis. In each cross-sectional image, the lesion surface and base were identified. A 1 × 1 mm² region of interest (ROI), corresponding to the treatment window, was selected. Mineral density values were recorded at depths of 0, 40, 80, and 120 μm from the enamel surface. All samples were randomly selected and repeatedly analysed under standardized conditions by a single examiner, and intraexaminer reliability was assessed to ensure consistency of the measurements.

Statistical analysis

All the statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics version 21. Normality was evaluated using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and homogeneity of variance was verified via Levene’s test. Biofilm inhibition and microbial susceptibility were analysed via one-way ANOVA, followed by Tukey’s test for post hoc comparisons. The fluoride release data were analysed via two-way repeated-measures ANOVA, followed by Bonferroni-corrected pairwise comparisons. For mineral density at various depths, two-way ANOVA with the Bonferroni post hoc correction was used. Intraexaminer reliability was assessed via the intraclass correlation coefficient. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

AgNP synthesis and characterization

The synthesized AgNPs were characterized via TEM and DLS analyses, confirming successful nanoparticle formation with desirable morphological and size characteristics. TEM images at a lower magnification revealed numerous well-dispersed, spherical black dots indicative of silver nanoparticles, with a relatively uniform size distribution and minimal aggregation (Fig. 1). Higher magnification images provided a closer view of individual nanoparticles, which appeared nearly spherical with clear edges and slight size variation; some degree of agglomeration was also observed. DLS measurements revealed a sharp peak in the number distribution centred at approximately 2–4 nm (mean size ~ 2.87 ± 0.96 nm), indicating a predominantly monodisperse population. In contrast, the intensity distribution displayed broader peaks between 10 and 100 nm, suggesting the presence of a small fraction of aggregates. The autocorrelation function exhibited smooth decay, with the initial slope and decay profile further supporting the formation of stable, small colloidal nanoparticles.

Optimal concentration of AgNPs for antimicrobial activity

To determine the optimal concentration of AgNPs for inclusion in the NSF formulation, the antimicrobial efficacy of the synthesized AgNPs against S. mutans biofilms was evaluated. A range of AgNP concentrations from 0 to 300 µg/mL was tested for their ability to inhibit biofilm formation. As shown in Fig. 2, concentrations above 2.34 µg/mL significantly reduced biofilm formation compared with the untreated group (0 µg/mL), indicating the minimum effective dose for biofilm inhibition. The bactericidal effects of AgNPs on biofilms determined via CFU count and live/dead analysis revealed a clear dose-dependent antibacterial effect. Compared with the BHI group, the AgNP group presented significantly lower viable bacterial counts and greater proportions of dead S. mutans cells at the highest concentration tested (300 µg/mL). Notably, the bactericidal activity at this concentration was comparable to that of 1% NaOCl. Confocal laser scanning microscopy further confirmed these findings, revealing a predominance of red fluorescence (indicating dead cells) at 300 µg/mL, with the proportion of dead bacteria increasing progressively with increasing AgNP concentration. This dose-dependent effect supports the use of 300 µg/mL as an optimal concentration for effective antimicrobial action.

Antimicrobial efficacy of AgNPs formulated with fluoride

Test agents were developed by combining 300 µg/mL AgNPs with fluoride (AgNPs + F) at three concentrations: 1,000, 20,000, and 40,000 ppm. The effects of these compounds on S. mutans biofilm inhibition and bacterial viability were compared to those of fluoride alone. All AgNPs + F agents, as well as fluoride alone, effectively inhibited biofilm formation (Fig. 3a). However, only the AgNPs + F group demonstrated significant bactericidal activity. CFU analysis revealed significantly lower viable bacterial counts across all AgNPs + F agents than fluoride alone (Fig. 3b). Live/dead confocal imaging further confirmed these results (Fig. 3c). AgNPs with 20,000 and 40,000 ppm fluoride led to predominantly dead cells (red fluorescence), whereas AgNPs with 1,000 ppm fluoride resulted in a mixed population of live and dead bacteria. In contrast, fluoride alone resulted in mostly live cells (green fluorescence), indicating a limited bactericidal effect (Fig. 3d).

Asterisks indicate statistically significant differences (p ≤ 0.05)

Comparative evaluation of NSF with conventional therapies

Two NSF formulations, NSFlow and NSFhigh, were evaluated for their antimicrobial efficacy against S. mutans biofilms in comparison with SDF, a current commercial anticaries agent. The AgNP alone and untreated groups were included as controls. All the tested agents strongly inhibited the biofilm, reducing S. mutans biofilm formation by more than 90% compared with that in the untreated group (Fig. 4a). Similarly, in the biofilm susceptibility tests, silver-based agents (AgNPs, NSFlow, NSFhigh, and SDF) had significantly greater bactericidal activity than did the untreated group (p ≤ 0.001), as indicated by a lower bacterial count (Fig. 4b) and greater percentage of dead bacteria (Fig. 4c). Among them, SDF resulted in the highest percentage of dead bacteria at 67.47 ± 6.16%, followed by NSFlow at 58.94 ± 4.28%, AgNPs at 55.18 ± 5.92%, and NSFhigh at 54.22 ± 5.79%. However, the statistical comparison did not reveal a significant difference between the treatments. Confocal imaging supported these findings: the SDF group presented predominantly red fluorescence, indicating extensive bacterial death. In both the NSF groups and the AgNP groups, red and green fluorescence were mixed, producing an orange to yellow hue that reflected partial bacterial viability (Fig. 4d). In contrast, the untreated group exhibited mostly green fluorescence, indicating that there was no antimicrobial effect.

Fluoride release and remineralization of NSF on enamel lesions

The fluoride release and remineralization efficacy of the two formulated NSFs were evaluated using artificial enamel carious lesions and compared with those of the Fvarnish, SDF, and untreated groups. Over a 7-day application period, Fvarnish exhibited sustained fluoride release, whereas both the NSF formulation and SDF resulted in initial burst release (Fig. 4a). Notably, during the first two days, SDF released the greatest amount of fluoride at 6.55 ± 0.64 ppm, followed by NSFhigh (5.65 ± 0.24 ppm), NSFlow (3.44 ± 0.20 ppm) and then Fvarnish (3.02 ± 0.25 ppm). Microcomputed tomography analysis revealed that in all the NSF-treated groups, SDF and Fvarnish significantly increased the mineral density up to a lesion depth of 120 μm compared with that of the untreated control (Fig. 4b). NSFhigh did not significantly differ from either Fvarnish or SDF at any depth. However, NSFlow exhibited a significantly lower density than did the other groups at the 0 μm depth. Notably, the yield of Fvarnish was greater than that of NSFlow until it reached 80 μm. At 120 μm, no significant differences in mineral density were observed among the treatment groups.

Discussion

The efficacy of AgNPs is highly dependent on their synthesis methods, particle characteristics, and formulation components, all of which influence their antibacterial activity and biocompatibility [21, 28]. Therefore, it is essential to evaluate the properties of our own synthesized AgNPs and NSF to ensure optimal performance and confirm their safety and effectiveness for clinical use in our target population. This study introduces a novel NSF formulation as a dual-action anticaries agent with both antimicrobial and remineralization capabilities. After the AgNPs were synthesized, we identified the optimal concentration required to effectively inhibit S. mutans biofilms, followed by observation of the agents that combine the AgNPs with fluoride. The antimicrobial properties of the AgNPs, both alone and with fluoride, and the two formulated NSFs, NSFlow and NSFhigh, were evaluated via biofilm inhibition and susceptibility assays, with comparisons made against conventional Fvarnish and SDF. The synthesized AgNPs significantly inhibited biofilm formation at concentrations as low as 2.34 µg/mL and exhibited a dose-dependent bactericidal effect, with 300 µg/mL substantially reducing viable bacterial counts in the biofilm. The AgNP + F agent, which combines the optimal AgNP concentration with 20,000 or 40,000 ppm fluoride, effectively inhibited S. mutans biofilms and showed comparable performance to SDF. The remineralizing properties of both NSF formulas revealed that most fluoride was released immediately within two days, similar to SDF but different from Fvarnish, with NSFhigh promoting remineralization comparable to that of SDF and Fvarnish. These findings suggest that NSFhigh is a promising alternative to existing treatments, providing effective biofilm control and remineralization while potentially minimizing the aesthetic drawbacks associated with traditional silver-based agents.

S. mutans biofilm formation is a major contributor to oral diseases, leading to the development of biofilm inhibitors such as AgNPs, whose antimicrobial efficacy is influenced by size and shape, and various methods can be used to produce nanosized particles from silver compounds. In this study, AgNPs were manufactured from spherical, monodisperse silver nitrate with an average size of ~ 2.87 ± 0.96 nm using a chemical reduction method with sodium borohydride as the reducing agent and chitosan as the stabilizer. These particles were small enough to easily penetrate the bacterial membrane [29, 30] and effectively inhibited S. mutans biofilm formation at concentrations as low as 2.34 µg/mL, demonstrating stronger activity than previously reported AgNPs with larger diameters [31]. The efficacy of our AgNPs might be due to their smaller size, which allows more effective interactions with bacterial cell walls and/or membranes, potentially disrupting their permeability [32]. AgNPs also exhibited a dose-dependent bactericidal effect within biofilms, with significant bacterial killing observed at 300 µg/mL, as confirmed by CFU analysis and live/dead assays. Although a previous report by Perez-Diaz reported that a lower AgNP concentration of 100 µg/mL efficiently eradicated S. mutans in the biofilm at a greater than 99.99% killing rate, their application required more than 24 h of exposure, which is different from our study, which determined only a 5 min contact time [33]. These findings suggest that the smaller size of our AgNPs enhances their interaction with bacteria, contributing to their potent antibiofilm activity. Our results support the use of 300 µg/mL AgNPs as an effective antimicrobial agent in anti-caries applications, and this concentration could be used for NSF formulation.

This study investigated the potential of enhancing AgNPs as an anticaries agent by combining fluoride at concentration equivalent to that in SDF, a material well established for its dual remineralization and antibacterial properties. The fluoride-loaded silver nanoparticles were formulated with 300 µg/mL AgNPs combined with fluoride at concentrations of 1,000, 20,000, and 40,000 ppm, reflecting typical levels found in toothpaste, Fvarnish, and SDF, respectively. All AgNPs + F formulations demonstrated significant antibiofilm activity against S. mutans, which aligns with previous findings [34, 35]. Compared with fluoride alone, the combination of AgNPs and fluoride significantly enhanced antibacterial efficacy, as indicated by reduced viable cell counts, increased dead cell staining, and disrupted biofilm architecture, as observed via confocal microscopy. These results suggest that the AgNPs + F formulations may address limitations of conventional fluoride treatments, such as inadequate penetration and limited bactericidal activity in mature biofilms, through the additive or potentially synergistic effects of their components. The observed antimicrobial mechanism is attributed to AgNPs disrupting the bacterial cell wall and/or membranes and interfering with DNA replication [36], fluoride impairing bacterial metabolism and acid tolerance [37], and chitosan contributing through membrane disruption and nutrient limitation [24]. Notably, the AgNPs + F containing 1,000 ppm fluoride seemed to exhibit bacteriostatic activity rather than bactericidal activity, as reflected by higher live cell proportions in the live/dead assay despite low CFU counts. Accordingly, this study supports the use of AgNP + F combined agents, particularly 20,000 or 40,000 ppm fluoride, as a promising alternative to SDF for caries prevention.

The current and emerging applications of AgNPs and NSF underscore their potential as multifunctional agents in dentistry. In paediatric care, NSF has gained attention as a promising topical agent, with studies demonstrating its efficacy in preventing caries. Santos et al. described NSF as a “new silver bullet” for childhood caries, and the annual application of NSF was more effective than placebo in hardening and arresting dental caries in primary teeth [38], producing outcomes comparable to those of SDF but without side effects such as black staining or metallic taste. Our study supports these findings, showing that NSF, especially at relatively high fluoride concentrations, can reduce biofilm formation and eliminate bacteria within it. The percent inhibition from the synthesized AgNPs and both NSF groups, NSFhigh and NSFlow, was comparable to that of SDF, which aligns with prior reports [39]. Both NSF groups also matched SDF in performance, potentially due to the “zombie effect” [39], where the nanosized particles continued to kill bacteria six hours after agent removal. Furthermore, the percentage of dead bacteria, as determined by the fluorescence-positive area, was not significantly different between the AgNP and NSF groups, which indicates that fluoride addition did not affect the antibacterial activity of the AgNPs. Although SDF had the highest proportion of dead bacteria, its dose effectiveness may be lower given its silver concentration of approximately 250,000 µg/mL compared with 300 µg/mL in the synthesized AgNPs. Crucially, unlike SDF, both NSF groups did not cause immediate dark staining of the biofilm, offering a clear aesthetic advantage for paediatric and appearance-conscious patients. Given its clinical promise, further evaluation of NSF’s remineralization efficiency was conducted, as this property is also key to successful caries arrest.

In addition to its antimicrobial properties, NSF demonstrated fluoride release and remineralization comparable to those of the current therapies. In this study, NSFlow immediately released fluoride ions in similar amounts to those released by Fvarnish, which is a well-studied and widely used product. However, only Fvarnish showed sustained fluoride release over 7 days, with initial high release followed by a gradual decline, likely because the natural resin in the formulation enhances adhesion and slows dissolution. SDF and NSFhigh initially released fluoride at a greater rate than did Fvarnish and NSFlow, with SDF resulting in a slightly greater release concentration than NSFhigh. Over time, fluoride release from the SDF and both NSF groups decreased to the detection limit. Despite adding CMC to NSF to improve consistency and adhesion during application, fluoride ions dissolved quickly and were easily washed away, similar to SDF. This burst release delivers a high local fluoride concentration that enhances caries inhibition and limits lesion progression [40], supporting rapid remineralization and antibacterial activity [41, 42]. However, the loss of sustained fluoride presence after two days could weaken the maintenance phase, leaving the treated surface increasingly vulnerable over time, especially in individuals at high caries risk. Thus, while the immediate release from NSF may benefit early caries arrest, future formulations should be optimized for sustained fluoride release to support long-term enamel repair. Notably, NSF did not cause enamel discolouration during the 7-day period, unlike SDF, which caused immediate dark staining. This difference is likely due to the distinct chemical characteristics of NSF, including its lower ammonia content and the neutral, nonionic, and more stable nature of AgNPs, which are less prone to oxidize into black compounds like silver sulfide or silver phosphate [43]. Mineral density analysis after pH cycling showed that the untreated group experienced demineralization up to a depth of 120 μm, whereas all the treatments promoted significant remineralization from 0 μm to 120 μm. Among the treatment groups, NSFlow was less efficient than the other treatments were, while Fvarnish likely promoted better remineralization than did NSFlow until reaching 80 μm. These different results are likely caused by variations in the fluoride concentration, formulation characteristics, and exposure time. NSFlow, which had a lower fluoride content than NSFhigh and SDF (~ 44,800 ppm fluoride) and less durability than fluoride varnish, did not promote remineralization as deeply into lesions as the other groups did. Nevertheless, NSFhigh demonstrated comparable efficiency in promoting remineralization with SDF and Fvarnish agents, revealing therapeutic benefits for early enamel lesion repair and offering better antimicrobial results than fluoride alone without the aesthetic drawbacks of silver phosphate compounds.

In conclusion, our results show that this new NSF formula with 40,000 ppm fluoride could work as a dual-action anticaries treatment, combining strong bacteria-killing power with a good ability to repair early tooth damage. While both silver and fluoride are known to have additive effects on caries control, this NSF formula requires further validation to assess its long-term efficacy and potential adverse effects. Nonetheless, NSF presents a promising alternative to conventional treatments, particularly in clinical situations where both antimicrobial action and aesthetic considerations are crucial.
