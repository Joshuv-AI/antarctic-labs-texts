Can the Severity of Dental Caries Predict Hypertension in Adult Patients?
Authors: Karpal Singh Sohal, Linus Jeremiah, Uchenna Okechi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimDental problems are associated with hypertension and cardiovascular diseases. It is believed that untreated dental caries can lead to systemic physiological changes such as altered blood pressure control. This study aimed to determine the prevalence of hypertension and its association with the severity of dental caries among the adult population in Dar es Salaam, Tanzania.MethodsThis study employed a descriptive cross‐sectional design, conducted in a university dental clinic in Dar es Salaam, Tanzania. It included 1365 adult patients. A questionnaire was used to collect the sociodemographic characteristics of the participants. World Health Organization guidelines were followed to confirm the presence of dental caries. Blood pressure was recorded as the average of the three readings. Data were analyzed using SPSS version 29. Univariate and multivariable logistic regression were used to determine predictors of hypertension. A p‐value less than 0.05 was taken as a cut point to determine significance.ResultsThe male‐to‐female ratio of participants was 1:1.74, and the age range was between 18 and 90 years. Most participants (N = 604; 44.2%) had attained a tertiary level of education. Slightly more than half (N = 749; 54.9%) were married, and most (N = 395; 28.9%) had informal employment. Most patients (N = 592; 43.4%) had highly severe dental caries. About one‐third (N = 457, 33.5%) of the participants were hypertensive. The DMFT scores of hypertensive patients were slightly higher than those without hypertension; however, the association was weakly positive (r = 0.145, p = 0.01). There was no statistically significant relation between hypertension and severity of dental caries (p ≥ 0.05).ConclusionHypertension is prevalent among patients attending a university dental clinic. Dental caries and hypertension share some common risk factors as far as sociodemographic characteristics are considered. The severity of dental caries cannot be used to predict whether a patient is hypertensive or not.Keywords: Dental caries, DMFT, hypertension, risk factors

Introduction

Hypertension, a non‐communicable disease (NCD), refers to a persistently elevated blood pressure of 140/90 mmHg and above, or when the blood pressure is controlled to guideline‐recommended levels by use of antihypertensive drugs [1, 2]. Hypertension is an important public health issue globally, with the estimated number of adults with hypertension having increased from 594 million in 1975 to 1.3 billion in 2015 [3, 4]. Similarly, dental caries or tooth decay is one of the major chronic oral diseases worldwide [5, 6] and is considered an important component of the global disease burden, with a prevalence among adults as high as 100% in most countries [7].

Dental caries is associated with socioeconomic factors, such as age, sex, level of education, and income status, and socio‐behavioral risk factors, like poor dietary habits [8]. Equally, the risk factors for hypertension include non‐modifiable factors like sex and age, and modifiable factors include lifestyle‐related factors such as diet, obesity, low physical activity, cigarette smoking, and excessive alcohol consumption [4, 9].

Associations between dental problems, hypertension, and cardiovascular diseases (CVD) have been previously investigated [3]. It is believed that untreated dental caries can cause persistent discomfort, inflammation, and infection, which can lead to systemic physiological changes such as altered blood pressure control and vascular function [10]. A recent meta‐analysis provided an overview and appraisal of studies on the relationship between the number of missing teeth/tooth and hypertension. It was found that people with more tooth loss exhibited a higher prevalence of hypertension [9]. Haroon et al. [10] found a strong correlation between children's and teens’ primary hypertension and the severity of dental caries.

Studies from Tanzania reported that about 30% of the adults in the community have undiagnosed hypertension [1, 11]. On the other hand, a study assessing the prevalence of dental caries among adults attending a referral hospital in Tanzania found that up to 87.8% of participants had dental caries experience [12]. Such findings indicate that there are high odds that some of the patients attending dental clinics have undiagnosed hypertension.

Despite the importance of hypertension in dental treatment and the fact that a dental professional may be the first health professional to see a patient in his/her life in a Tanzanian setting, only a few dental professionals check their patients’ blood pressure before any dental procedures. This may be due to a lack of blood pressure monitoring machines or ignorance. To raise the suspicion index of the dental professional about the hypertensive status of their patient before initiating any dental treatment, we hypothesized that the severity of dental caries may be a predictor of hypertension, considering their shared risk factors [3]. This survey, therefore, aimed to determine the prevalence of hypertension and its association with the severity of dental caries among the adult population attending the university dental clinic in Dar es Salaam, Tanzania.

Materials and Methods

Study Design, Setting, and Population

This was a hospital‐based cross‐sectional study conducted at the Muhimbili University of Health and Allied Sciences (MUHAS) dental clinic from November 2022 to November 2023. The MUHAS dental clinic is situated in the heart of Dar es Salaam and serves as the leading training center for undergraduate and postgraduate students in dentistry.

All patients who attended the MUHAS dental clinic during the study period were considered the source population, and all randomly selected patients from the source population attending the health facility during the study period were considered the study population. The inclusion criteria were all adult patients (18 years and above) who visited the MUHAS dental clinic. The exclusion criteria included individuals with intellectual disabilities, patients who had severe pain and were thus unable to communicate, and those who presented with oral tumors, jaw fractures, and severe odontogenic space infections.

Sample Size Determination

The sample size was estimated using the population adjustment formula for single‐proportion estimation 9. A minimum sample of 1067 participants was calculated based on a 95% confidence level, a 3% precision, and an expected proportion of 50%. The list of patients who attended the dental clinics on the day of data collection was obtained, and each was assigned a number. A systematic random sampling technique was then used to select the study participants.

Data Collection

A structured questionnaire (SI‐1) composed of questions inquiring about sociodemographic characteristics (age, sex, marital status, level of education, and occupation) and history of hypertension was used to collect information from the patients.

Study variables:

Dependent variable: Hypertension.

Independent variables: Socio‐demographic characteristics (Age, Sex, Educational status, income, marital status), DMFT, and severity of dental caries.

Physical examination: For each patient who visited the clinic, aseptic technique was observed throughout the measurement of blood pressure. The blood pressure was assessed after participants had rested in a seated position for 10 min. Measurements were taken on the upper arm of the non‐dominant side using a digital blood pressure machine (Omron Blood Pressure Monitor, 3 Series). A second and third reading were obtained after an interval of 5 min between measurements. The average of the three readings was recorded as the participant's blood pressure. If the readings between any of the three differed by more than 10 mmHg, a fourth measurement was taken after an additional 5 min. In such cases, the average of all four readings was used as the final blood pressure value.

Each tooth of the individual was examined for dental caries according to the World Health Organization (WHO) recommendations. Dental probes, dental mirrors, disposable tongue depressors, and an overhead dental chair lamp were used to perform the physical examination. Teeth were inspected and probed for the presence of Decay (D), Missing (M), and Filled (F). Clinically, caries was confirmed if a lesion was seen in a pit or fissure, or on the tooth surface, and the presence of detectable softened floor, softened wall, or undermined enamel. When a filled tooth had one or more restorations and one or more decayed areas, it was also included in this category. The tooth was considered missing because of caries if a person had a history of toothache and/or a cavity before extraction. All clinical observations were recorded on the assessment form.

Data Quality Control

Before the main study, pretesting of the questionnaire and clinical forms was carried out on individuals representing 3% of the total sample size. The pretest aimed to evaluate the suitability and reliability of the data collection tools. Two qualified dentists participated in a 2‐day training session focused on conducting interviews and performing dental caries assessments. To ensure data consistency, both interpersonal and intrapersonal reliability were assessed. Cohen's Kappa statistics demonstrated almost perfect intrapersonal agreement [К = 0.83 (95% CI: 0.63–1.03), p < 0.001] and substantial interpersonal agreement [К = 0.80 (95% CI: 0.61–1.00), p < 0.001]. The batteries of the digital blood pressure machine were replaced after every 50 patients.

Data collection was closely supervised, and at the end of each day, all completed questionnaires and clinical examination forms were reviewed for completeness and accuracy.

Data Analysis

The Statistical Package for Social Sciences software (SPSS) for Windows (version 29, Armonk, New York: IBM Corp) was used to code and analyze the data. For descriptive analysis, the standard error of the mean (SEM) and proportion were used.

The patients’ ages were categorized into young adults (< 40 years), middle‐aged (40–59 years), and elderly (≥ 60 years). The level of education was dichotomized into two categories: a low level (no formal education and primary education) and a high level (secondary and tertiary education). Marital status was categorized into two groups: those with partners (married, cohabiting) and those without partners (single, divorced, widowed). Employment status was categorized into stable income (civil servants and formal private employment) and unstable income (informal employment, peasants, students, the unemployed, and retirees). Blood pressure was dichotomized into non‐hypertensive (< 140/90 mmHg) and hypertensive (≥ 140/90 mmHg) as per World Health Organization guidelines of 2021 [13]. Severity of dental caries was categorized as low (DMFT ≤ 1), moderate (DMFT = 2–4), and severe (DMFT ≥ 5).

Results were reported as the mean and standard error of the mean (SEM) for continuous variables, such as age and DMFT. Frequency and percentage were computed using univariate analysis to get summary values. To assess the association between categorical variables, a chi‐square test was done, and to assess the correlation between hypertension (categorical variable) and DMFT (continuous variable), a Point–Biserial correlation test was carried out. Significance was set at p < 0.05. The variables with a p‐value < 0.05 were selected for multivariable logistic regression analysis to determine predictors of hypertension.

Operational Definitions

Decayed tooth (D): This category included carious teeth, filled teeth with recurrent decay, a tooth with only one root left, and temporarily filled tooth surfaces with other surfaces decayed.

Missing tooth (M): This category includes teeth that are missing due to caries, but doesn't include teeth missing for reasons other than caries.

Filled teeth (F): Includes teeth that have one or more permanent restorations with no secondary (recurrent) caries.

The DMFT index is the average number of teeth per person that are decayed (D), missing because of caries (M), or filled (F). It is a quantitative expression of the lifetime caries experience of the permanent teeth.

Written informed consent was obtained before data collection. Consent was ensured when a consent form was signed in a designated area. All information was handled confidentially, and refusal to participate or withdraw from the study did not result in any consequences. All procedures were performed in accordance with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Results

Description of the Study Population

A total of 1365 adult patients participated in this study, with more female participants (N = 867; 63.5%), giving a male‐to‐female ratio of 1:1.74. The ages of participants ranged between 18 and 90 years, with a mean age of 39.26 years (S.E.M. 0.42). A majority (N = 784; 57.4%) were young adults. Most participants (N = 604; 44.2%) had attained a tertiary level of education. Slightly more than half (N = 749; 54.9%) of the participants were married, and most (N = 395; 28.9%) had informal employment (Table 1).

Hypertension Among Patients Visiting a Dental Clinic and Its Associated Factors

Out of the 1365 patients who were enrolled in the study, 164 (12.0%) reported being hypertensive. Upon measuring the blood pressure of the patients, about one‐third (N = 457, 33.5%) had high blood pressure and were classified as hypertensive. The mean systolic blood pressure of the study participants was 129.03 mmHg (SEM = 0.56), ranging between 81.5 and 227.5 mmHg. The mean diastolic blood pressure was 79.5 mmHg (SEM = 0.31), ranging from 40.0 to 141.5 mmHg. Hypertension had a statistically significant association with all sociodemographic characteristics of the patients (p < 0.05) except for the income levels of the patients (Table 3).

Association of Hypertension With DMFT and Severity of Dental Caries

Upon running the correlation test, it was found that hypertensive patients had slightly higher DMFT scores than those without hypertension; however, the association was weakly positive (r = 0.145, p = 0.01).

The proportion of patients with hypertension was 26.5% (N = 84), 31.1% (N = 142), and 39% (N = 231) among those with low, moderate, and highly severe dental caries, respectively. The association between hypertension and the severity of dental caries was statistically significant (p < 0.001).

Predictors of Hypertension Among Patients With Oral Diseases

All factors that had a significant association with hypertension in the bivariate analysis were entered into the multivariable logistic regression model as independent variables for the outcome of dental caries. The study found a significant association between hypertension and the age group, sex, and partner status of the participants. There was a linear relationship between hypertension and age of the patient, whereby compared to young adults, the odds of hypertension were 3.5 times and 9 times higher among middle‐aged and elderly patients, respectively. For sex, females had 36% lower odds of being hypertensive than males, and in the case of the partner's status, patients who had partners were 1.6 times more likely to be hypertensive than those without partners. There was no statistically significant relation between hypertension and either education level or severity of dental caries (Table 4).

Discussion

Dental caries has been associated with the release of inflammatory mediators such as C‐reactive proteins, which can cause endothelial dysfunction and vascular changes. These inflammatory mediators may increase arterial stiffness, impair vasodilation, and ultimately lead to the development of high blood pressure [10]. Hence, this study investigated a potential link between dental caries and hypertension among adults attending the University dental clinic in Dar es Salaam, Tanzania.

In the present study, we found that about one‐third (33.5%) of the adult patients attending treatment at the university dental clinic had high blood pressure and were classified as hypertensive. This finding is comparable to various other studies carried out in Tanzania on the prevalence of hypertension in the community [4, 11, 14, 15]. Contrary to the findings of the current study, a lower prevalence was found in a study done in India, which assessed the prevalence of hypertension in dental patients, and reported that about 20% were hypertensive [2], and a study from Nigeria, which found the prevalence of about 16% [16]. However, a higher prevalence (49%) was found in a study carried out among dental patients in Saudi Arabia [17]. The differences in prevalence of hypertension among the dental patients may be attributed to socio‐cultural factors of the population studied and methodological differences between the studies.

The prevalence of self‐reported hypertension was 12%, whereas objectively detected hypertension reached 33.5%. This substantial discrepancy indicates a large proportion of undiagnosed hypertensive individuals within the population. Such underdiagnosis underscores gaps in awareness, screening, and access to healthcare services, and highlights the need for targeted implementation measures, including strengthened community‐based screening, improved health education, and enhanced integration of blood pressure monitoring into routine dental care.

According to the World Health Organization, NCDs usually have multiple associated factors that can explain their onset, including genetic, physiological, environmental, and behavioral determinants [18]. In the present study, we noted that both dental caries and hypertension had a statistically significant association with all the sociodemographic characteristics of the patients except for the income levels of the patients. Though the only difference was in the sex of the patients, where women tend to have high severity of dental caries, on the other hand, males had a higher preponderance of hypertension. Our findings further cement the fact that dental caries is indeed a non‐communicable disease (NCD), as it shares similar risk factors to other NCDs.

The current survey aimed to assess whether the severity of dental caries was associated with hypertension, thus being a predictive factor in dental settings. The correlation test indicated a weakly positive association between hypertension and DMFT scores, and on univariate analysis, the association between hypertension and the severity of dental caries was statistically significant, similar to findings from another study [3]. However, as Almoznino et al. [3] had cautioned, the results of univariate analysis in the present survey might have been influenced by a large sample size, which enables the detection of even weak statistical associations that may be clinically non‐significant. Considering the confounder of sociodemographic characteristics of the patients on both conditions, multivariate analysis was performed, and the results conclude that the association between hypertension and severity of dental caries was not statistically significant, results similar to findings by Almoznino et al. [3]. Therefore, the severity of dental caries is not a reliable factor to be used to predict the hypertensive status of the patient. However, considering almost every 3rd person was hypertensive, all dental professionals are urged to make necessary efforts to measure the blood pressure of the patients attending their clinics regularly.

Limitation

Despite the study having some notable strengths, like a large sample size and repeat blood pressure measurements to confirm high blood pressure, hence giving approximately true estimates of the prevalence of hypertension among dental patients, it has some limitations worth noting. It was a single‐center based cross‐sectional study; this limits the generalizability of the results to the entire population. Moreover, apart from social demographic factors, we did not assess the role of other factors that could influence the severity of dental caries and hypertension, such as dietary habits, smoking, alcohol consumption, body mass index, exercise, quality of tooth brushing, and oral health habits of the participants. Multicentric longitudinal studies should be considered to further explore the causal relationship between dental caries and hypertension and to confirm or refute the findings observed in this study.

Conclusion

The findings of this study indicate that hypertension is prevalent among patients attending the university dental clinic. Dental caries and hypertension share some of the risk factors as far as sociodemographic characteristics are considered. The severity of dental caries cannot be used to predict whether a patient is hypertensive or not.
