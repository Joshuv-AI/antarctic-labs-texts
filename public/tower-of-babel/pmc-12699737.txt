Longitudinal quality improvement project to increase colorectal cancer screening for an underserved population in a resident physician-led primary care clinic
Authors: Vivek Vardhan Jasti, Sarah N Grebennikov, Mariah Barlow, David Y Lo, Shail M Govani, Miram Chan, Aimee E Willett, Christopher Barlow
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe overall rates of colorectal cancer (CRC) in the US population have been declining steadily over the past 30 years, in large part due to timely cancer screenings. Despite the emergence of new screening modalities, a large portion of the population has not completed CRC screening in accordance with guidelines. Adequate and timely screening can prevent or detect CRC in earlier stages. Various factors have been identified that influence screening completion. Lower screening rates have been associated with the underserved population, especially in the immigrant community and patients with inadequate access to healthcare. In the USA, primary care clinics associated with internal and family medicine residency programmes often serve as catch-net clinics for the community, serving to provide care to those with a higher burden of social determinants of health.ObjectiveWe aimed to increase the rates of CRC screening in a primary care clinic associated with the internal medicine residency programme of a large healthcare system. In October 2021, the clinic had a CRC screening rate of 55.40%.MethodsThis prospective quality improvement study was implemented from July 2022 to January 2025 at the resident-run clinic. Data on CRC screening completion were analysed monthly.InterventionsSix Plan–Do–Study–Act cycles were implemented over the course of the project. These interventions were developed from results of root-cause analyses as well as feedback from patients and providers.ResultsCRC screening rates rose from 55.40% to 65.79% over the period of interest with the implementation of sequential targeted interventions.ConclusionsUnderserved patients are at higher risk of CRC screening incompletion. Multifaceted interventions and multidisciplinary collaboration are essential for increasing CRC screening in this vulnerable population.

WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS

This project includes a comprehensive approach to improving CRC screening in a vulnerable patient population. Six different Plan–Do–Study–Act cycles were conducted that targeted different areas in the barriers to screening completion. We have included a replicable process and have seen an increase in CRC screening test ordering practices and an overall 10% increase in screening rates. Additionally, to our knowledge, this QI effort is the longest and includes the most interventions compared with the existing literature.

Introduction

Problem description

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is one of the leading causes of cancer-related morbidity and mortality in the USA.1 Adequate and timely screening can prevent CRC and reduce cost and burden of disease.1 The underserved population, particularly patients with low socioeconomic status, inadequate access to healthcare, limited English proficiency, recent immigration status or belonging to an ethnic/racial minority, are at the highest risk of suboptimal cancer screening.1 2 This quality improvement (QI) initiative was conducted in a resident-run internal medicine clinic affiliated with a large hospital system in Columbus, Ohio. Prior to the initiation of QI interventions, we identified a CRC screening rate of 55.40% in 2021, well below the national average of 71.6% at that time.3

Available knowledge

Although the overall rate of CRC incidence has decreased in older adults, the rate of CRC in younger adults between the ages of 20–54 has been rising.1 In May 2021, the US Preventive Task Force (USPSTF) recommended lowering the age of CRC screening in average-risk patients from 50 to 45, following a similar recommendation from the American Cancer Society in 2018.4 5 The increase in CRC rates in a younger demographic has resulted in increased interest in prevention and early detection of disease. Numerous QI and research projects, with a range of successful interventions, have been reported in the literature. These interventions vary from staff and patient education, targeted interventions towards patients with limited English proficiency, emphasis on stool-based tests, electronic medical record (EMR) reminders and even mailed stool kits.69 Some projects were based in resident graduate medical education clinics; however, a few projects included both multifaceted interventions and a longitudinal focus with multiple iterations of Plan–Do–Study–Act (PDSA) cycles.

This QI project was the first project aimed at addressing low CRC screening rates at this clinic site. We believe the application of the QI process in an underserved resident clinic using a variety of interventions, a longitudinal focus and having resident physicians (RPs) serve as project leads can encourage the implementation of similar interventions at other residency associated primary care clinics across the country.

Rationale

We hypothesised that encouraging resident leadership of the QI project would increase CRC screening rates through project engagement and the development of sustainable interventions.

Specific aims

The goal for this project was to increase CRC screening rates at the resident-run internal medicine clinic. In accordance with the SMART (Specific, Measurable, Attainable, Relevant, and Timely) goal format, the target screening rate was re-evaluated with subsequent PDSA cycles. At the time of project conception, in October 2021, our original goal was to increase CRC screening to 60% by January 2023. In June 2023, this SMART goal was revised to 65% by January 2024. This goal was further increased in September 2024 to 70% by July 2025. Our final goal was to reach a 15% improvement in CRC screening rates, in the resident clinic, over a 3-year time span. The purpose of this manuscript is to share our methods, challenges encountered, and our impact on CRC screening in hopes of providing a framework for improving CRC screenings in similar primary care clinics.

Methods

This prospective QI project was officially underway as of October 2021, with the first intervention beginning in July 2022. This manuscript includes each of the interventions implemented until October 2024. This manuscript follows the Revised Standards for Quality Improvement Reporting Excellence (SQUIRE 2.0) guidelines.10

Context

The resident-run primary care clinic is composed of 54 internal medicine RPs and 6 attending preceptor physicians. Support staff include multiple medical assistants (MAs), registered nurses, pharmacists, community health and social workers. The internal medicine residency is split into three groups of 18 RPs, who rotate through the clinic on a 2-week basis. Each group is assigned to a patient panel for continuity of care. On average, there are 18 RPs in the clinic at any given time.

This project was selected in 2021 when annual clinic metric review revealed that the resident-run clinic was underperforming in several quality-based patient outcomes. Concurrently, the residency programme leadership was in the process of overhauling QI education for RPs to be more experience-based. RPs created their own QI groups and selected topics based on identified areas of deficiency. RPs were assigned one clinic-based mentor for guidance, and additional mentors were included at the team’s discretion. In 2021, the resident-run clinic had a baseline CRC screening rate that was 15% lower than the national average and about 12% lower than the state average.

The QI team consisted of six RPs during any given academic year. Two RPs were recruited from each level of training (first, second and third year). RPs with an interest in gastroenterology, preventative care or outpatient medicine were preferred. One fourth-year chief resident advised the project during the 2023–2024 academic year. During the 2024–2025 academic year, the project was advised by a fourth-year chief resident and a fourth-year QI fellow. Additional team members included two clinic attending physicians and two local gastroenterology physicians.

As each RP advanced through residency, they took on additional leadership roles within the project. First-year RPs were expected to attend QI core lectures and build a foundation of practical QI knowledge. Second-year RPs were expected to be the primary leaders of the QI team; they directed the team’s efforts, coordinated brainstorming sessions and delegated tasks. Third-year RPs ensured task completion in a timely manner and were responsible for data analysis. All RPs were expected to partake in data collection, root-cause analysis exercises, intervention brainstorming, and work-in-progress presentations to other RPs and stakeholders.

The chief residents and the QI fellow were graduates of the residency programme that served as junior advisors to the RPs. Their hands-on experience with the project as prior RPs allowed for guidance during root-cause analysis brainstorming sessions and understanding of shortcomings from previous interventions. The clinic attending physicians were available for senior mentorship, clinic-specific knowledge and practice limitations, and connection to clinic stakeholders. The gastroenterology physicians provided specialty-specific expertise and specialty-specific connections. Hospital leadership did not directly support the project. The clinic practice manager was aware of interventions and supported the QI team by helping coordinate interventions that involved ancillary clinic staff.

The QI team met every 6–8 weeks with frequent electronic communication between meetings. Meetings were typically in person but with a virtual option in the event of patient care duties. During intervention brainstorming, if multiple ideas were presented, the group held an informal vote to pick a course of action. Often, the alternative intervention that was proposed would be re-considered at future meetings and several were implemented in future PDSAs.

The patients seen in the clinic include several underserved populations. We identified 44% of patients to be on Medicaid or uninsured. 14% of patients are non-English-speaking. The ethnic make-up of our patients includes 52% identifying as Caucasian and 27% identifying as African American or black. As a part of the regular intake process, the clinic also screens for social determinants of health. 26% of patients screened positive for food, financial, and/or transport insecurities.

We gathered baseline data regarding completion rate of CRC screening at the clinic using the physician dashboard of the EMR. These data were gathered in October 2021 and revealed a CRC screening rate of 55.40%. Of note, these data were collected after the USPSTF updated CRC recommendation in May 2021. The hypothesised effect of these recommendations is discussed further under the ‘Limitations’ section.

Prior to the initiation of any interventions, stakeholder analysis was completed. We recruited clinic faculty and our clinic practice manager for administrative support. We used a fishbone diagram to determine potential root causes of the performance gap prior to the implementation of any interventions (online supplemental figure 1a). The fishbone and root-cause analysis were repeated after PDSA 3 (online supplemental figure 1b). A part of the root-cause analysis included patient’s perspectives to identify barriers to CRC screening. This was achieved by an anonymous patient questionnaire to determine the reasons why patients may choose to forgo or delay CRC screening (online supplemental figure 2). A random week was selected to administer this in-person questionnaire prior to PDSA 1. All patients who were overdue for CRC screening, which was presented on their previously scheduled clinic appointment, were offered the questionnaire. 86 patients were eligible to complete the questionnaire, and 61 patients completed it. The primary reasons for forgoing or delaying screening included fear, transportation insecurity, lack of counselling regarding screening options and financial burdens. Unfortunately, the patient survey did not include sociodemographic or clinical characteristics. The inclusion of these data and expansion of the survey to a wider number of patients may reveal at-risk subgroups and highlight areas for more targeted intervention.

During the root-cause analysis, prior to the initiation of PDSA 1, we identified several barriers to screening completion. Table 1 lists several identifiable barriers and corresponding interventions to address these barriers. A more detailed driver diagram was also created (online supplemental figure 3).

Patient and public involvement

Patients participated in the study during the preintervention root-cause analysis. Patient surveys were used to understand the reasons for not completing timely CRC screening. Results from these surveys influenced the direction of several PDSA cycles. Neither patients nor the public were involved in study design, conduct, reporting or dissemination of the results of this study.

Analysis

Microsoft Excel was used to track the total monthly CRC screening rate. These data were extracted from the physician dashboard of the EMR and recorded monthly. The EMR physician dashboard was also used to collect data regarding positive stool tests, and manual chart review was used to assess occurrence of follow-up colonoscopy. Microsoft Excel was also used to construct the statistical control chart. Qualitative data from surveys were used to assist with future PDSA cycle development but not included in data analysis.

PDSA cycle 1 (July 2022–September 2022)

In the preintervention fishbone diagram, we suspected a gap in provider comfort about alternative screening methods. The patient questionnaire had also identified a gap in patient knowledge regarding different types of screening modalities. Therefore, the first PDSA cycle was targeted at both resident and patient education using a multipronged approach.

To address resident education, the QI team created and delivered a brief, 5 min presentation during scheduled resident didactic time. This session was repeated so that all three clinic groups were included in the education process. The presentation reviewed updated USPSTF guidelines for CRC screening, different CRC screening modalities, patient eligibility criteria for stool-based screening, estimated cost per screening modality and clinic-specific processes for ordering tests. A dot phrase was created and shared in the EMR with all clinic providers, which included quick and convenient access to the information provided.

To further support the educational sessions, flyers were placed in resident work areas that compared conventional screening with colonoscopy to stool-based testing, including faecal immunochemical tests (FITs) and faecal DNA testing. The flyer reviewed patient eligibility, frequency of testing, risk and benefits, and practical information on how to order each test, along with price transparency (online supplemental figure 4).

For patient education and awareness, an informational flyer was created, highlighting updated screening guidelines which lowered the age of CRC screening from 50 to 45 years and different CRC screening modalities. These visual aids were placed in areas with high patient traffic (online supplemental figure 5).

Study of intervention 1

For each PDSA cycle, the primary outcome measure was completion of a screening test. This was assessed using an EMR-derived physician dashboard targeted at the clinic department level and remained consistent throughout the entirety of the project. At the end of PDSA cycle 1, the rate of completed CRC screening rose from 56.1% to 57.73%. In retrospect, follow-up surveys could have helped assess the most meaningful portion of this intervention. RPs could have been surveyed to gauge the effectiveness of the educational session and patients could have been surveyed to determine if visual aids were informative and directly or indirectly led to CRC screening.

Although education is lower on the hierarchy of intervention effectiveness, we recognised potential gaps in knowledge and were able to create a low-effort intervention that did produce some quick wins on subsequent data analysis. The sustainability of this intervention is high as the resources provided to both RPs and patients can be reused.

PDSA cycle 2 (October 2022–January 2023)

In response to the feedback received on the patient questionnaire, which indicated some eligible patients were never offered CRC screening by their provider, PDSA cycle 2 was focused on reminding providers that their patients are due for screening. The QI team enlisted the help of three of our clinic nursing colleagues to implement PDSA cycle 2. As part of an existing workflow, clinic nurses review the daily list of scheduled patients for overdue preventative care topics.

This PDSA cycle added to the existing nursing workflow by asking clinic nurses to note if a patient was overdue for CRC screening based on EMR auto-populated best practice reminders. Nursing would then notify each provider via secure EMR-based messaging system about which scheduled patients were due for a CRC screening discussion on a given day.

Study of intervention 2

This intervention only increased CRC screening from 58.27% to 59.86%. Although moderately successful, there were concerns about the sustainability of this intervention. We received informal feedback from clinic nurses and RPs. Nursing staff felt that this intervention was too burdensome on their current workflow. From a provider’s perspective, it was often difficult to address CRC screening on the same day due to time constraints spent on addressing acute issues. In some rare instances, providers were notified of screening eligibility during or after the visit had been completed.

During PDSA 2, a helpful balancing measure would have been measurement of minutes spent reviewing the clinic schedule by our nurses before and during the intervention. We suspect there was a significant increase in nursing minutes spent reviewing the clinic schedule and notifying the appropriate providers. In addition, despite reminders and provider awareness that their patients were overdue for CRC screening, the length of the appointment was a limiting factor.

PDSA cycle 3 (February 2023–April 2023)

Based on the limitations identified in prior PDSA cycles and the barriers to completing colonoscopies in our underserved and uninsured clinic population, the QI team focused on testing modality for PDSA cycle 3. We suspected that targeting ease of test completion and patient accessibility would have a greater effect on our clinic’s total screening rate rather than solely increasing the number of orders through reminders and education. We drew inspiration from the mobile mammogram concept, which physically brought screening opportunities to the patients. As previously noted, our patient population faces numerous barriers to colonoscopy screening. Therefore, this intervention placed greater emphasis on stool-based testing, specifically, FIT. Following the recommendation of the American College of Gastroenterology, we began to stress that “the best screening test is the one that gets completed”.11 The timing of our intervention coincided with March; therefore, we launched the ‘Get FIT Campaign for Colorectal Cancer Awareness Month’

The first phase of this intervention occurred in February 2023 and was focused on identifying patients with outstanding orders for CRC screening (screening colonoscopy or stool-based testing). Identified patients were then divided among the QI team members. Charts were manually reviewed to confirm screening eligibility. If no high-risk features were identified, patients were called to confirm they were appropriate for stool-based testing and offered in-office FIT. Patients were scheduled during a 2-week block in March to pick up FIT and receive education from a nurse or MA on completion. Patients were given written, pictorial and verbal instructions on how to complete the FIT. To help facilitate patient triage at the front desk, a process map was created (online supplemental figure 6). To further celebrate CRC Awareness Month, our group made themed t-shirts and decorated the clinic lobby (online supplemental figure 7).

Study of intervention 3

During PDSA cycle 3, we saw an increase in CRC screening from 59.84% to 60.42%. With this intervention, we also included a process measure and tracked the number of stool-based tests ordered and completed. During the 5-week intervention period, there were a total of 29 stool-based tests that were ordered. Of these, 16 tests were completed during the intervention period, a rate of 3.2 tests completed per week. In comparison, we reviewed data from 37 weeks prior to the intervention and identified 30 completed stool-based tests performed for CRC screening, a rate of 0.81 tests per week.

Although this intervention was well received by patients and our data supported its effectiveness, there were concerns about the sustainability of this intervention. Each member of the CRC QI team was responsible for chart reviewing and calling about 30 patients each, which was thought to be too labour intensive. A balancing measure tracking the amount of time each QI group member spent reviewing patient charts or on the phone with patients would have substantiated concerns for sustainability.

PDSA cycle 4 (August 2023–October 2023)

Feedback from two consecutive PDSA cycles highlighted concerns for sustainability from various interdisciplinary team members. Therefore, for the fourth PDSA cycle the primary focus was sustainability. We hypothesised that electronic reminder messages sent to patients via the EMR would positively impact ordering and completion of stool-based tests while creating minimal provider burden. In September 2023, the EMR provider report function was used to identify 506 overdue patients. A generic message was sent to each patient’s online portal (online supplemental figure 8). Patients were notified of their overdue screening status, a list of potential screening modalities and a website link with more detailed information. For data analysis, charts with new orders for gastroenterology referral were manually reviewed to confirm referrals were solely for CRC screening purposes.

Study of intervention 4

After PDSA cycle 4, there was minimal change in the total CRC screening rates from 60.53% to 60.37%. For our process measure, we tracked patient interaction with the electronic reminder message as well as CRC screening orders. Of the 506 overdue patients identified, only 356 had an active electronic portal and were able to receive the message. Of the 356 patients messaged, under half (140) of the patients read the message. In the 8 weeks prior to the intervention, there were 43 referrals for colonoscopy and 5 stool-based orders. In the 8 weeks following the intervention, there were 34 referrals for colonoscopy and 18 stool-based orders, indicating a significant increase in orders for stool-based tests.

This intervention was very well received. It was the most sustainable intervention thus far. We believe there was a lag time in the data that coincided with the next intervention and believe this PDSA cycle potentially had more impact than the data reveal. Given the sustainability, the CRC QI team decided that this intervention could be repeated on a quarterly basis. The reminder message was only sent to patients during the 2023–2024 academic year before being phased out.

PDSA cycle 5 (November 2023–January 2024)

With the fifth PDSA cycle, we decided to revisit a problem that we identified from our initial root-cause analysis. We suspected a major reason for our clinic’s lower CRC screening rate was related to lack of follow-up on previously ordered tests. PDSA cycles 1 and 2 focused primarily on increasing the number of tests ordered. PDSA cycles 3 and 4 placed extra emphasis on stool-based testing. For PDSA 5, we decided to target the order follow-up process. The CRC QI team was able to enrol one MA in a new process to follow up on incomplete screening tests. This new process was integrated into our MA’s workflow and was intended to be completed on a weekly basis. Patients who had a stool-based screening test ordered were given 60 days to complete their screening. After 60 days had elapsed, patients would receive a call from our MA reminding them about their overdue screening and encouraging them to complete the ordered screening test. This patient list was generated from the EMR database by clinic management and provided to the MA.

Study of intervention 5

We saw an increase in the total CRC screening rate from 60.85% to 61.82% with this intervention. No additional process or balancing measures were recorded for PDSA 5. This was another low-effort, high-impact intervention that had an overall positive impact on our screening rates. We found that by giving patients time to complete screening on their own we were preventing early, unnecessary calls. Calling patients once 60 days had elapsed helped identify patients that would likely not have completed the test. Having a dedicated MA for this task kept the work standardised. However, due to staff turnover the intervention was ultimately discontinued in February 2024. We were able to resume this intervention in July 2024 after training a new staff member to take this role.

PDSA cycle 6 (February 2024–October 2024)

In the sixth PDSA cycle, we continued efforts to encourage stool-based testing based on previous successes for our clinic population. We partnered with a US-based diagnostics company that specialises in faecal DNA testing kits to receive a monthly donation of five free kits for uninsured or Medicaid patients in our clinic for a total of 10 months. To participate in the donation programme, we completed legal documents and mandatory provider and clinic staff education sessions. We were officially approved for faecal DNA kit donation in March 2024 and received our first supply of kits in June 2024. The kit donation also included pamphlets and video in various languages for our non-English-speaking patient population.

In conjunction with donated faecal DNA kits, the CRC QI team did a phone outreach intervention to increase screening during CRC Awareness Month, like PDSA cycle 3. We identified patients with upcoming in-office appointments and outstanding stool-based CRC screening orders. In contrast to PDSA cycle 3, we excluded patients with open-screening colonoscopy orders. We believed excluding these patients would eliminate the need for in-depth chart review or prolonged phone conversation for risk assessment, thereby decreasing provider burden and increasing intervention sustainability.

Study of intervention 6

We saw an increase in our overall CRC screening rate from 62.67% to 64.91%. This intervention was much longer than most of our previous interventions, primarily due to the delayed arrival of faecal DNA kits to the clinic. By the end of PDSA cycle 6, there were 6 completed faecal DNA tests, 6 picked up and incomplete tests, and 13 unused tests.

The first phase of the intervention, the phone outreach, was better received by the CRC QI team members compared with PDSA 3. Each team member was responsible for calling 16 patients each, almost half as many as PDSA 3. The second phase of the intervention, the donated faecal DNA kits, required additional preparatory measures to meet the requirements for donation. One of the clinic attendings, who also served as an advisor for the CRC QI team, completed the necessary paperwork and meetings to secure this donation. Once we were approved for kit donation, there were minimal additional requirements to continue receiving kits. We categorised this intervention as high effort and high impact.

Statistical control chart analysis

The statistical control chart data for the CRC QI project showed an increase in CRC screening completion from 55.40% to 65.79% between October 2021 and January 2025 (figure 1). By January 2025, the median CRC completion rate for the 3-year duration of the project was 60.40%. This median value exceeded the initial SMART goal of 60% that was set in October 2021. From 2021 to 2024, the clinic saw an increase in total enrolled patients from 1868 to 2151 patients.

Statistical control chart depicting monthly colorectal cancer (CRC) screening rates from October 2021 to January 2025. PDSA, Plan–Do–Study–Act.

The overall trend of our statistical control chart data was upwards. From May to July 2023, there was an unexpected rise and fall in the screening rate. We suspect that this trend may have occurred due to new patient enrolment. Several of the interventions may have had an impact beyond the confined PDSA cycle. For example, PDSA 1 included resident education, dot phrases and flyers. These educational materials remained available throughout the year and were passed down to the incoming residency classes. For PDSA 4, a reminder MyChart message was sent every 3–4 months until June 2024. For PDSA 5, the order follow-up process was paused between February 2024 and June 2024, until a new staff member could assume the vacant role but was continued from July 2024 onwards. The intervention with the greatest effect on screening completion was PDSA 6. We observed an increase in screening by 3.12% over 11 months. The length and sustainability of the sixth intervention likely contributed to its success. In comparison, the average intervention length for PDSA 1–5 was 3.2 months. From January 2022 to January 2025, a total of 25 patients tested positive on a stool-based test. Of the 25 patients, 14 completed a colonoscopy. The 11 patients who had yet to complete a colonoscopy had varying reasons for not completing a colonoscopy (online supplemental figure 9).

Discussion

Review of the literature

During this 3-year QI project, we saw a rise in the CRC screening rate from 55.40% to 65.79% after the implementation of six interventions. Each of the interventions selected was chosen after root-cause analyses (online supplemental figure 1a and 1b), QI group discussions and identification of barriers to screening completion, as depicted in table 1. Many of the interventions selected were inspired by similar initiatives in the literature. Education was implemented as a low-effort intervention for the introductory PDSA. A large systematic review and meta-analysis identified that patient-directed education had a small positive association with increased screening but was non-significant.12 This study did not review any trials with provider-focused education. Our initial intervention included both patient and resident education. One study that was conducted in a similar setting, a resident-run clinic in the USA, included patient and provider education but in combination with other interventions to increase CRC screening rates.6 Results from education alone from this study were not evaluated for the improvement of screening rates.6

Baron et al investigated the use of provider reminder systems to increase breast, cervical and colon cancer screening and identified consistently favourable results for a provider reminder system across various forms of cancer screening.13 A provider reminder system was the primary intervention of our second PDSA cycle. However, one limitation that the authors recognised was for a provider reminder system to be effective patients had to present to clinic appointments. As a result, patients with inadequate access or underuse of healthcare would be missed by such interventions.13 One randomised controlled trial found a non-statistically significant difference with a provider reminder system, but a trend towards significance after three or more visits.14 A more recent, larger, systematic review corroborated the findings of Baron et al and found a “consistent positive association” between clinician reminder and CRC screening rates compared with usual care.12

For the fourth PDSA cycle, we relied on reminders to patients within the EMR. Several studies within the literature have found a statistically significant increase in CRC screening with patient notification.81416 However, when studied over a duration of time, the effect of patient reminders was thought to be temporary.8 During our intervention, we repeated the EMR reminder to patients on a quarterly basis to increase lasting efficacy. Most studies identified on systematic review suggested a pooled positive effect of patient reminder for increasing CRC screening.12 Compared with the literature, we observed a less significant increase in screening after the use of EMR reminder messages. As previously mentioned, we suspected that this was related to a lag time in test completion given that the number of ordered stool-based tests was higher. Several interventions reported in the literature had varying intervention lengths, which may influence the perceived effect.

The interventions that appeared to have the greatest benefit during our QI initiative were those that emphasised stool-based testing. As mentioned previously, the American College of Gastroenterology guideline for CRC screening recommends that “the best test is the one that gets completed”.11 Several studies within the literature support the use of stool-based testing for average-risk patients to facilitate timely screening.6 17 18 One study even suggested that offering FIT at the same time as influenza vaccination increased screening uptake.19 This may suggest that the accessibility of stool-based screening alone may increase screening. Outreach to promote stool testing was universally associated with a positive rise in CRC screening according to a systematic review that included 17 RCTs.12

Multiple studies in the literature used a mailed FIT initiative that was strongly associated with an increase in screening.92022 We proposed this idea to clinic attendings and the clinic practice manager, but we were unable to gain funding support for a mailed FIT initiative.

Summary

This project demonstrated a successful approach to CRC screening in an underserved population using multifaceted interventions. Completion of CRC screening is a nuanced process when caring for patients who belong to underserved populations. There is a higher risk of incomplete or inadequate screening in these populations, and physicians are required to have a better understanding of social determinants of health and community resources at their disposal. Unsurprisingly, the literature supports the idea that multicomponent interventions that address barriers at various levels are the most efficacious for improving CRC screening rates.6 12 17 18 23

With this QI project, we also aimed to improve resident education by having the project primarily led by RPs. Each of the six PDSA cycles was developed by RPs with assistance from faculty mentors and was presented by RPs at quarterly ‘work-in progress’ meetings. One of the strengths of this project included gauging patient perspective on barriers to completing adequate screening and attempting to target interventions based on patient survey results.

Any proposed intervention that changes the nature of a practice may face initial resistance. However, adoption of a practice change and sustainment is possible if interventions are efficacious and considered non-burdensome. The interventions with the least effort and highest positive feedback from staff and the QI team came from EMR reminders and the order follow-up process. These interventions allowed for realistic, long-term sustainable processes that extended beyond the intervention periods. Additionally, emphasis on stool-based testing, without ‘cold-calling’ patients, may still positively impact screening rates without requiring a high effort and therefore become a sustainable process.

One of the purposes of this manuscript was to describe our processes and findings for the purpose of replicability. We learnt many lessons from our interventions and adapted our future interventions to address feedback and barriers from prior PDSA cycles. In the setting of a resident-run clinic, this was a slow process with frequent re-orientation, which is discussed further below. Although return may appear low, in similar practice settings, implementation of these interventions may occur more smoothly and quickly with the understanding of which interventions were effective and which were ineffective during our QI initiative. A table depicting the interventions, sustainment considerations and key takeaways is also available (online supplemental figure 10).

Interpretation

The initiation of a QI project to address CRC screening had a clear impact on screening completion in our resident’s clinic. Of the six interventions employed during this longitudinal QI project, the most impactful for this population revolved around emphasising stool-based testing and employing multidisciplinary coordination with clinic MAs and nursing staff. The QI team had good buy-in from clinic management and support for various interventions that involved our non-physician staff members. The environment at our clinic fostered a patient-centred focus and team-based approach that helped achieve our SMART goals. No additional resources or external funding was provided for this project. The interventions implemented were completed with no additional cost to the clinic or patients. Additionally, having taken place in a resident-run clinic meant our PDSA cycles had to be cost and time-effective.

Limitations

One of the major limitations of this project is the setting in which it was conducted. A resident’s clinic, responsible for providing care for the underserved, is often associated with frequent staff turnover and, naturally, RP graduation. During the project, several MAs, three nurses and one attending preceptor left the practice, along with three classes of graduating residents. As a result, re-education and project orientation were frequent. Greater staff retention would have accelerated the rate of CRC screening, as re-education and orientation could be avoided, and team members would become more comfortable in their designated roles. In addition to slowing project momentum, loss of staff strains existing staff dynamics as existing employees take on additional roles until a replacement is hired and new hires may shift interpersonal team dynamics. Turnover also results in institutional memory loss as knowledgeable practitioners in the QI initiative are replaced with novices.24

It is unsurprising that staff turnover hinders QI activities in the clinical setting, and entirely preventing turnover is unrealistic. Particularly within graduate medical education, turnover is expected. So how can we overcome this limitation? A few potential modalities to combat turnover include redundancy training with cross-training of staff, detailed documentation of QI processes, meetings and decisions to preserve institutional knowledge and formal exit processes to ensure adequate transfer of QI knowledge to other staff.24

The project took place in Columbus, Ohio, a midwestern city in the USA with a population of about 1 million people. Smaller cities and rural areas may endure greater struggles in the face of fewer community resources. Our results and methods may not be as applicable to alternative settings.

As previously mentioned, the USPSTF guidelines were updated in May 2021 and recommended reducing the screening age for CRC from 50 to 45. These recommendations likely lowered our baseline completion rate. However, we suspect that our clinic still had significant room for improvement for the completion of CRC screening.

Conclusions

The patients in our residency clinic are experiencing increased value-based care targeting CRC screening completion at no increased cost to the patient or practice. The underserved population is at a high risk for inadequate cancer screening, and the providers should focus on advocating for screening tests that have the highest chance of completion and patient buy-in. Future steps for this QI project include standardising the follow-up process after a positive stool-based test and sustaining the rate of CRC screening completion after free faecal DNA test kits are no longer available.
