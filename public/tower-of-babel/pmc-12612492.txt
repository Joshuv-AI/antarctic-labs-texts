The efficacy and safety of mirvetuximab soravtansine for the treatment of recurrent ovarian cancer: an update systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Qiling Dai, Jiawen Tang, Ting Du, Qin Huang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IntroductionThis meta-analysis aims to evaluate the efficacy and safety of mirvetuximab soravtansine (MIRV) in patients with recurrent ovarian cancer, with particular emphasis on those with platinum-resistant disease.MethodA comprehensive search of PubMed, EMBASE, and the Cochrane Library was conducted through August 31, 2025. Data on progression-free survival (PFS), overall response rate (ORR), overall survival (OS), duration of response (DOR), cancer antigen 125 response, and treatment-related adverse events (TRAEs) were extracted and analyzed using meta-analysis to assess the efficacy and safety of MIRV.ResultTwelve studies involving a total of 1112 patients with recurrent ovarian cancer were included in this meta-analysis. The PFS with MIRV was 6.28 months (95% confidence interval CI 5.70–6.87), and the ORR was 0.37 (95% CI 0.34–0.40). Among platinum-resistant patients treated with a combination of MIRV and bevacizumab, the PFS was 7.66 months (95% CI 6.45–8.87), while the ORR reached 0.42 (95% CI, 0.34–0.50). In addition, MIRV achieved an OS of 16.00 months (95% CI 12.36–19.63) and a DOR of 7.69 months (95% CI 6.80–8.58). The overall incidence of TRAEs was 93% (95% CI 92–95), with grade ≥ 3 adverse events occurring in 34% of patients (95% CI 31–37). The most common TRAEs were blurred vision (incidence 0.45; 95% CI, 0.42–0.48), nausea (0.39; 95% CI 0.37–0.42), and diarrhea (0.37; 95% CI 0.34–0.39).ConclusionMIRV demonstrates significant efficacy and an acceptable safety profile in the treatment of recurrent ovarian cancer. Its use in platinum-resistant ovarian cancer with high FRα expression represents a particularly promising Therapeutic approach. Furthermore, the combination of MIRV and bevacizumab shows substantial potential for improving outcomes in patients with platinum-resistant disease.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-025-03933-x.

Introduction

Ovarian cancer is a highly heterogeneous gynecological malignancy, with more than 90% of cases arising from epithelial cells and a smaller proportion originating from germ cells or sex cord-stromal tissues [1]. Globally, it is the eighth-leading cause of cancer-related mortality among women [2]. The high mortality rate is largely due to delayed diagnosis, as most patients present with advanced-stage disease [3]. Standard treatment typically involves cytoreductive debulking surgery followed by either neoadjuvant or postoperative platinum-based chemotherapy. Although most patients initially respond to platinum-based chemotherapy, approximately 75% experience recurrence, and nearly all eventually develop platinum-resistant ovarian cancer [4]. Among recurrent cases, the median progression-free survival (PFS) is 12–18 months, and the overall 5-year survival rate is 45–50% [5]. Thus, recurrence and platinum resistance remain two of the greatest challenges in ovarian cancer management.

In recent years, targeted therapies and immunotherapies have developed rapidly, with growing interest in their application to ovarian cancer. Ongoing research is particularly focused on identifying suitable molecular targets for treatment. Folate receptor-α (FRα) has been shown to be highly expressed in ovarian cancer tissues, while its expression in nonmalignant ovarian tissues remains minimal [6]. Previous studies demonstrated that folate enhances ovarian cancer cell migration and invasion, effects that are significantly reduced following FRα knockdown [7]. These findings suggest that FRα contributes to the migratory and invasive potential of ovarian cancer cells, highlighting its relevance as a potential therapeutic target.

Research efforts have therefore focused on developing FRα-directed therapeutics, including monoclonal antibodies, antibody–drug conjugates (ADCs), and small-molecule folate conjugates. Mirvetuximab soravtansine (MIRV), the first FRα-targeting ADC, was recently approved by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration for the treatment of platinum-resistant ovarian cancer [8]. Preclinical studies have demonstrated strong antitumor activity of MIRV in ovarian cancer models [9], and clinical trials have further reported encouraging outcomes in patients [10–19]. However, since most of the currently available evidence is derived from single-arm studies, we performed this meta-analysis to comprehensively evaluate the efficacy and safety of MIRV, with particular emphasis on its role in platinum-resistant ovarian cancer.

Methods

This study was conducted and reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [20].

Search strategy

A comprehensive search of PubMed, EMBASE, and the Cochrane Library was performed to identify relevant articles published up to August 31, 2025. The search strategy incorporated the following medical subject headings and keywords: (“mirvetuximab soravtansine” OR “IMGN853” OR “IMGN-853”) AND (“ovarian neoplasms” OR “ovarian cancer” OR “ovarian tumor”).

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria for this meta-analysis were as follows: (1) Population: patients with histologically confirmed epithelial ovarian cancer (EOC), primary peritoneal cancer, or fallopian tube cancer; (2) Intervention: MIRV monotherapy or MIRV in combination with bevacizumab or chemotherapy; (3) Study design: prospective studies, including randomized controlled trials and single-arm studies; (4) Outcomes: studies reporting at least one prespecified clinical endpoint evaluating the efficacy and safety of MIRV. The assessed parameters included PFS, overall response rate (ORR), overall survival (OS), duration of response (DOR), cancer antigen 125 (CA-125) response (defined according to Gynecologic Cancer InterGroup criteria as a ≥ 50% reduction in CA-125 levels from baseline, confirmed and maintained for at least 28 days) [21], and treatment-related adverse events (TRAEs). Tumor response was assessed according to the response evaluation criteria in solid tumors (RECIST, version 1.1) [22]. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) duplicate publications; (2) case series, case reports, book chapters, review articles, letters to the editor, conference abstracts, cross-sectional studies, case–control studies, and cohort studies; (3) studies without access to the full manuscript; (4) non-human studies; and (5) studies published in languages other than English.

Data extraction and quality management

Two researchers independently screened and selected the studies. In cases of disagreement, a third author was consulted to resolve the dispute. Baseline information was extracted from each included study, including the first author, year of publication, participant inclusion criteria, treatment regimen, sample size, median age, median PFS, median OS, ORR, median DOR, and TRAEs. The methodological quality of single-arm studies was assessed using the methodological index for non-randomized studies (MINORS) [23], while the risk of bias in randomized controlled trials (RCTs) was evaluated using the Cochrane risk of bias 2 (ROB2) tool. The assessment results are provided in the Supplementary Table.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis for this meta-analysis was performed using Stata version 16.0 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA). Heterogeneity was assessed using the Q-test and the I2 statistic. When I2 was < 50%, a fixed-effects model was applied to calculate the pooled estimates and corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs); otherwise, a random-effects model was used. Forest plots were generated to visually present the pooled results. Sensitivity analyses were conducted using forest plots. Publication bias was evaluated with Egger’s test; a p-value > 0.05 indicated no significant publication bias, whereas a p-value ≤ 0.05 suggested potential bias.

Results

Literature inclusion

A total of 496 publications were initially identified across the three databases: 114 from PubMed, 331 from EMBASE, and 51 from the Cochrane Library. The detailed process of study identification and screening is shown in Fig. 1. After removing duplicates, 357 articles remained. Based on title, abstract, and full-text screening, 347 articles were excluded. Ultimately, two randomized controlled trials [14, 17] and ten non-randomized studies [10–13, 15, 16, 18, 19, 24, 25] were included in this meta-analysis. Following careful assessment and selection, baseline study characteristics, including first author, year, national clinical trial number, patient demographics (mean age and disease status), interventions, and outcomes, were extracted (Table 1). A total of 1,057 patients with recurrent ovarian cancer treated with MIRV were included. Of these, 948 were platinum-resistant (defined as progression or relapse within six months of completing prior platinum-based therapy), 137 were platinum-sensitive (defined as progression or relapse more than six months after the last dose of the most recent platinum therapy), and 27 had unclear platinum status. With respect to FRα expression, 795 patients had high FRα expression, 72 had medium expression, 40 had low expression, and 205 were unspecified. Regarding treatment regimens, 839 patients received MIRV monotherapy, while 273 received combination therapies, including MIRV with bevacizumab (n = 177), MIRV with pembrolizumab (n = 55), and MIRV Efficacy of MIRV

Across the included studies, PFS for recurrent ovarian cancer ranged from 2.80 to 22.80 months, with a pooled estimate of 6.28 months (95% CI 5.70–6.87; Fig. 2a). In subgroup analyses, the pooled PFS for platinum-resistant patients was 5.74 months (95% CI 5.25–6.23; Fig. 2b). Among platinum-resistant patients with high FRα expression, the PFS was 5.72 months (95% CI 5.14–6.29; Fig. 2d). The PFS associated with MIRV monotherapy was 5.46 months (95% CI 4.91–6.01), whereas higher PFS values were observed with combination regimens: 13.50 months with MIRV plus bevacizumab and carboplatin, 7.66 months with MIRV plus bevacizumab, and 4.20 months with MIRV plus pembrolizumab (Fig. 2c). Sensitive analysis and bias analysis

No studies were excluded in the sensitivity analysis, as none exhibited significant heterogeneity (S1-3). Publication bias was assessed using Egger’s test. As shown in S4-6, the p-values for PFS, ORR, and DOR were 0.462, 0.172, and 0.721, respectively, indicating no evidence of publication bias among the included studies. The two randomized controlled trials were assessed using the ROB2 tool and demonstrated a low risk of bias (Supplementary Table 2). For the remaining ten non-randomized studies, risk of bias was evaluated using the MINORS tool, which similarly indicated a low risk of bias (Supplementary Table 1).

Discussion

In this systematic review and meta-analysis, we pooled data from 1,112 patients across twelve clinical trials to evaluate the efficacy and safety of MIRV in the treatment of ovarian cancer. Since MIRV has only recently been introduced into clinical practice, most of the included studies were non-randomized. Although we provided a detailed assessment of methodological quality and risk of bias for each trial, the overall evidence quality was not high, which may have influenced our results. The pooled PFS with MIRV was 6.28 months (95% CI 5.70–6.87), and the pooled ORR was 0.37 (95% CI 0.34–0.40). In addition, the pooled OS was 16.00 months (95% CI 12.36–19.63), and the pooled DOR was 7.69 months (95% CI 6.80–8.58). These outcomes appear promising, particularly considering that most of the studies focused on platinum-resistant patients.

Surgery combined with platinum-based chemotherapy remains the standard first-line treatment for advanced ovarian cancer [4], with the primary goal of achieving maximal cytoreduction (removal of all visible tumor tissue), often facilitated by the increasing use of neoadjuvant chemotherapy. However, despite achieving a complete response or partial response, approximately 75% of patients experience disease recurrence, and nearly all eventually develop platinum resistance [26]. This highlights the clinical relevance of subgroup analyses based on platinum sensitivity. In our study, although treatment regimens varied, the overall ORR was 63% in platinum-sensitive patients compared with 34% in platinum-resistant patients. This finding suggests that platinum-sensitive patients may derive greater benefit from MIRV therapy; however, additional RCTs are required to validate this observation. Overcoming platinum resistance remains a major therapeutic challenge in ovarian cancer. Notably, in our analysis, the ORR in platinum-resistant patients was 34%, which is comparable to the pooled ORR of 37%. This result is particularly meaningful for patients with limited treatment options, as MIRV may represent a valuable therapeutic alternative. Moreover, several studies have reported that even in the absence of a marked improvement in ORR, MIRV treatment can still induce meaningful tumor shrinkage at target lesions [25].

Among platinum-resistant patients, we further stratified studies based on FRα expression levels and treatment regimens. Patients with medium or high FRα expression demonstrated higher ORRs (0.36, 95%CI 0.24–0.48 and 0.38, 95%CI 0.34–0.43, respectively) compared with those with low FRα expression (0.26, 95%CI 0.11–0.41). These findings suggest that higher FRα expression is associated with improved response rates, although patients with low FRα expression also exhibited meaningful remission rates. In addition, platinum-resistant patients receiving combination therapy achieved higher ORRs, as well as longer PFS and DOR, compared with those treated with MIRV monotherapy. Notably, platinum-sensitive patients, particularly those treated with the triple combination of MIRV, bevacizumab, and carboplatin, achieved superior ORRs and prolonged PFS and OS compared with platinum-resistant patients.

It is important to recognize that the improved outcomes associated with MIRV-based combination therapy in our analysis may, in part, reflect patient selection factors, as these regimens were frequently evaluated in platinum-sensitive populations with inherently better prognoses. This potential confounding factor underscores the need for cautious interpretation, and definitive conclusions regarding the efficacy of combination strategies will require future randomized controlled trials specifically designed for this purpose. With respect to safety, gastrointestinal reactions and ocular events were the most commonly reported TRAEs, while serious TRAEs occurred infrequently.

This meta-analysis has several limitations. First, the included studies ranged from phase I to phase III clinical trials, which limited their comparability. Second, not all studies provided sufficient data; for example, only two reported OS outcomes. Although this review incorporated an RCT published in the New England Journal of Medicine [17], only two RCTs were identified in our search, and one of them also lacked critical data [14]. Therefore, additional randomized controlled trials and real-world studies are needed to confirm the effectiveness of MIRV in ovarian cancer across different platinum sensitivity groups. Third, most patients included in the analyzed studies had received one to three prior lines of systemic therapy, leaving the efficacy of MIRV in patients with primary refractory disease uncertain. Finally, while the majority of evidence focused on platinum-resistant patients, there is insufficient data to evaluate the efficacy and safety of MIRV in platinum-sensitive populations. Future RCTs and large multicenter real-world studies are warranted to further elucidate the role of MIRV in ovarian cancer treatment.

Conclusion

In summary, this meta-analysis demonstrates that MIRV offers promising efficacy and an acceptable safety profile in the management of recurrent ovarian cancer. Moreover, the combination of MIRV with bevacizumab appears to provide additional therapeutic benefit, particularly in patients with platinum-resistant ovarian cancer. Nevertheless, further clinical trials are warranted to clarify its underlying mechanisms and to validate the clinical effectiveness of MIRV across diverse patient populations.
