Graph-Anchored Knowledge Indexing for Retrieval-Augmented Generation
Zhenghao Liu, Mingyan Wu, Xinze Li, Yukun Yan, Shuo Wang, Cheng Yang, Minghe Yu, Zheni Zeng, Maosong Sun

Abstract
Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) has emerged as a dominant paradigm for mitigating hallucinations in Large Language Models (LLMs) by incorporating external knowledge. Nevertheless, effectively integrating and interpreting key evidence scattered across noisy documents remains a critical challenge for existing RAG systems. In this paper, we propose GraphAnchor, a novel Graph-Anchored Knowledge Indexing approach that reconceptualizes graph structures from static knowledge representations into active, evolving knowledge indices. GraphAnchor incrementally updates a graph during iterative retrieval to anchor salient entities and relations, yielding a structured index that guides the LLM in evaluating knowledge sufficiency and formulating subsequent subqueries. The final answer is generated by jointly leveraging all retrieved documents and the final evolved graph. Experiments on four multi-hop question answering benchmarks demonstrate the effectiveness of GraphAnchor, and reveal that GraphAnchor modulates the LLM’s attention to more effectively associate key information distributed in retrieved documents. All code and data are available at https://github.com/NEUIR/GraphAnchor .

Introduction

Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG)
13
has emerged as a promising paradigm for mitigating hallucinations in Large Language Models (LLMs)
2
. For a given query, typical RAG models retrieve relevant documents, which are incorporated as contextual evidence to guide LLMs in generating accurate answers
23
;
25
;
18
. Although RAG models have demonstrated effectiveness across many knowledge-intensive tasks
1
, the retrieved contexts often contain substantial noise
33
, and supporting evidence is frequently distributed across multiple documents
19
. The dispersed nature of key information across retrieved documents poses a challenge for RAG models in effectively integrating and leveraging the retrieved knowledge
12
.

Figure 1:
Illustration of GraphAnchor. The graph is progressively updated for knowledge indexing.

To address this issue, numerous studies have explored structuring retrieved knowledge to better aggregate supporting evidence distributed across multiple passages
19
;
17
. These approaches typically investigate the effectiveness of different representation formats for extracting and representing salient information from retrieved evidence
32
;
19
;
38
. Although these methods enhance the coherence of knowledge representations and help reduce noise in the retrieved contexts, they primarily leverage these structures to filter out irrelevant information and assist LLMs in better utilizing the retrieved knowledge. As a result, they often fail to fully exploit crucial evidence that cannot be captured in these formats and overlook the role of structured data in facilitating deeper interpretation and reasoning over the retrieved knowledge
21
.
As shown in Figure
1
, graphs provide a well-defined structural representation for explicitly modeling entities and their relations. Such graphs can be incrementally updated by introducing newly identified entities, for example, adding “Carbon County” and linking it with “Red Lodge” through the “county seat” relation. This graph updating process progressively interprets the retrieved documents, which motivates us to treat the graph as a knowledge index for RAG systems.

This paper presents GraphAnchor, a Graph-Anchored Knowledge Indexing approach that iteratively updates a graph to enhance the interpretation of retrieved knowledge during the iterative retrieval process. Unlike previous methods
32
;
19
;
38
, GraphAnchor redefines the role of the graph, shifting it from a mere knowledge representation to an active indexing tool. At each retrieval step, GraphAnchor first retrieves documents based on the current query and updates the graph based on these retrieved documents. Then GraphAnchor assesses whether the current knowledge, informed by the graph, is sufficient to answer the query. If the knowledge is lacking, a new query is generated. Once the available knowledge is sufficient, the final response is generated according to the documents retrieved at each step with the final version of the graph.

Our experiments on four multi-hop QA datasets with different backbone LLMs demonstrate the effectiveness of GraphAnchor. Rather than refining knowledge for question answering, GraphAnchor focuses on knowledge anchoring and improves both the retrieval and QA modules within an iterative RAG framework. Specifically, GraphAnchor facilitates the retrieval of more relevant and diverse documents across multiple retrieval steps, and enables LLMs to better interpret and utilize the retrieved knowledge. Further analysis shows that, as the retrieval process progresses, the graph anchors an increasing number of related entities and relations into the index, and subsequently modulates the attention mechanism of LLMs to aggregate distributed evidence from the retrieved documents.

Related Work

Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) models typically retrieve relevant passages and incorporate them as contextual inputs, thereby helping Large Language Models (LLMs) to generate accurate and grounded responses
23
;
13
;
25
;
26
. However, conventional RAG approaches often suffer from insufficient knowledge acquisition, particularly in multi-hop question answering scenarios where reasoning requires the integration of evidence across multiple passages.

To mitigate this limitation, several studies have proposed iterative retrieval strategies that progressively seek and accumulate relevant knowledge to support answer generation
(
28
;
9
;
30
)
. In addition, some methods perform deep retrieval by interacting with external retrieval tools during the reasoning process, enabling more flexible and adaptive information seeking
9
;
16
. Nevertheless, as the number of retrieval steps increases, these RAG systems inevitably introduce more noisy or irrelevant information, which can substantially degrade the quality of the generated outputs
3
.

To improve knowledge utilization in LLMs, prior work has explored reranking retrieved passages
1
or summarizing salient information to filter out noise
29
;
33
. However, these approaches typically process retrieved passages in isolation, with limited modeling of relationships among different documents. As a result, cross-document evidence may be overlooked or insufficiently captured, particularly in settings that require aggregating complementary information across multiple sources
19
.
To better aggregate diverse knowledge from multiple retrieved passages, a growing body of work leverages structured representations, including schemas, tables, and graphs, to explicitly extract key information scattered across these retrieved documents
5
;
17
;
19
;
6
;
38
.
20
further explores multiple reasoning paths over the constructed knowledge graph, identifying conflicting paths as input context, thereby alleviating knowledge conflicts between internal and external information.

Instead of replacing retrieved passages with structured representations, some works
14
;
31
construct graphs to identify related information across multiple passages or chunks. Specifically, they extract key entities and facts from different chunks or passages and connect them via a graph structure. Such graphs enable LLMs to better capture long-range dependencies and multi-hop relationships within long texts or across documents, thereby facilitating more effective localization of relevant evidence. In contrast, GraphAnchor maintains an evolved graph through iterative retrieval, which anchors key concepts for knowledge interpretation and subquery generation.

Methodology

As illustrated in Figure
2
, this section presents the proposed GraphAnchor, which introduces an evolving graph to anchor knowledge within an iterative RAG framework. We first describe the overall architecture of GraphAnchor, highlighting how the evolving graph is integrated to support reasoning, query generation, and question answering (Sec.
3.1
). We then elaborate on the construction and dynamic updating of the graph, which functions as a context-aware knowledge index to enhance the knowledge association and comprehension capabilities of LLMs (Sec.
3.2
).

3.1
Graph-based Knowledge Anchoring for Iterative RAG

Given an initial query
q_{0}
that requires multi-hop retrieval, existing approaches
24
;
28
typically perform iterative retrieval to progressively accumulate evidence for answering the query. Different from these methods, GraphAnchor introduces an evolving knowledge graph
G
as a structured indexing mechanism, which simultaneously supports reasoning, next-query generation and final answer prediction.

At the
t
-th retrieval step, GraphAnchor retrieves a set of relevant documents
D_{t}
conditioned on the previous query
q_{t-1}
:

D_{t}=\text{Retriever}(q_{t-1}),

(1)

where
1\leq t\leq T
and
T
denotes the maximum number of retrieval iterations. The evolving graph is then updated by incorporating the newly retrieved documents
D_{t}
:

G_{t}=\mathcal{M}(q_{0},D_{t},o_{t-1}),

(2)

where
\mathcal{M}
denotes the LLM employed for RAG modeling. The previous output
o_{t-1}
consists of the graph
G_{t-1}
, the intermediate reasoning result
R_{t-1}
, and the query
q_{t-1}
.
Conditioned on the updated graph
G_{t}
, the retrieved documents
D_{t}
, and the previous output
o_{t-1}
, the LLM produces an intermediate reasoning result
R_{t}
along with the next query
q_{t}
:

R_{t},q_{t}=\mathcal{M}(q_{0},D_{t},o_{t-1},G_{t}).

(3)

The reasoning result
R_{t}
contains a reasoning trace enclosed by
<think>
and
</think>
, as well as a knowledge sufficiency judgement enclosed by
<judgement>
and
</judgement>
. The judgement is classified as either
sufficient
or
insufficient
, which determines whether the iterative retrieval process terminates. If the judgement is
insufficient
, the model generates the next query
q_{t}
conditioned on the current reasoning result
R_{t}
.

After completing the iterative retrieval process, all retrieved documents are aggregated to form the complete document set:

\mathcal{D}=D_{1}\cup D_{2}\cup\dots\cup D_{k},

(4)

where
k
denotes the actual number of retrieval iterations. Finally, the LLM generates the answer
\mathcal{Y}
to the original query
q_{0}
based on the aggregated documents
\mathcal{D}
and the final evolving graph
G_{k}
:

\mathcal{Y}=\mathcal{M}(\text{Instruct}_{\text{Gen}},q_{0},\mathcal{D},G_{k}).

(5)

As the number of retrieval iterations increases, the aggregated document set
\mathcal{D}
inevitably accumulates noisy and redundant information. In contrast, the evolving graph
G
is progressively refined at each iteration (see Sec.
3.2
for details), acting as a structured knowledge anchor that preserves salient facts and relational structures. By grounding reasoning on this graph-based representation, the model can more effectively organize and interpret retrieved knowledge, leading to improved reasoning accuracy and more reliable answer generation by LLMs.

3.2
Stepwise Graph Evolution for Context-Aware Knowledge Indexing

In this subsection, we first introduce the definition and representation of the evolving graph
G
, and then present our stepwise graph update mechanism.

Graph Representation.

During graph construction, the LLM is tasked with extracting entities and the relations among them. Accordingly, the graph is defined as
G=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E})
, where
\mathcal{V}=\{v_{1},\dots,v_{n}\}
denotes the set of entities, and
\mathcal{E}=\{t_{1},\dots,t_{m}\}
denotes the set of relations extracted from documents, represented as RDF triples
11
.

Following prior work
15
;
8
, we linearize the graph
G
by verbalizing both its entities and triples:

\displaystyle\text{Entities: }X(v_{1}),X(v_{2}),\dots,X(v_{n});

(6)

\displaystyle\text{Relations: }X(t_{1}),X(t_{2}),\dots,X(t_{m}),

where
X(v_{i})
denotes an entity verbalization function that encodes the entity name together with its associated attributes, and
X(t_{j})
denotes the verbalized RDF triple
t_{j}
, consisting of a head entity, a relation, and a tail entity. The resulting linearized graph representation is wrapped with the special tokens
<graph>
and
</graph>
.

Stepwise Graph Evolution.

During iterative retrieval, GraphAnchor adopts a stepwise graph evolution mechanism to maintain the graph
G
, which functions as a dynamic knowledge index for both model reasoning and subsequent query generation.

As formalized in Eq.
2
, the graph at step
t
is updated based on the previous graph
G_{t-1}
, the reasoning trace
R_{t-1}
, and the generated query
q_{t-1}
:

G_{t}=\mathcal{M}(q_{0},D_{t},\{G_{t-1},R_{t-1},q_{t-1}\}).

(7)

Specifically, when transitioning from
G_{t-1}
to
G_{t}
, the graph is incrementally updated by incorporating the retrieved documents
D_{t}
at step
t
, resulting in a stepwise evolutionary process:

\Delta G_{t-1\rightarrow t}=\text{Index}(D_{t},\{R_{t-1},q_{t-1}\}),

(8)

where
\text{Index}(\cdot)
denotes a document indexing operation conditioned on the prior reasoning trace
R_{t-1}
and the previously issued query
q_{t-1}
. These two signals jointly guide the extraction of salient, query-relevant information from
D_{t}
for graph updating. As a result, the graph progressively integrates query-aware evidence across retrieval steps, allowing key entities and relations to be incrementally identified, highlighted, and anchored within the evolving graph structure.

Experimental Methodology

In this section, we describe the datasets, baselines, evaluation metrics, and implementation details in our experiments. More experimental details are shown in Appendix
A.2
.

Datasets.
Following previous work
24
, we use four multi-hop question answering datasets to evaluate the effectiveness of our method, including MuSiQue
27
, HotpotQA
37
, 2WikiMultihopQA (2WikiMQA)
7
, and Bamboogle
22
.

Baselines.
In our experiments, we compare GraphAnchor with the Vanilla RAG model
23
and four multiple iterations retrieval models, including IRCoT
28
, Iter-RetGen
24
, DeepNote
30
, and Search-R1
9
. For the vanilla RAG model, we follow previous work
23
and feed 5 retrieved documents as context to answer the question. IRCoT and Search-R1 perform reasoning on retrieved knowledge and guide the model to retrieve missing information, so that accumulate more information. Iter-RetGen views the LLMs’ response generation on retrieved knowledge as needed information to retrieve new information. DeepNote dynamically refines the retrieved knowledge into a note for knowledge accumulation.

Evaluation Metrics.
Following
30
, we utilize Token-Level F1 (F1) and Exact Match (EM) as evaluation metrics. F1 score captures the trade-off between content coverage and redundancy, allowing for partial matches between the predicted and reference answers. Exact Match (EM) focuses on whether the model’s output exactly and completely matches the reference answer, providing a stricter measure of precision.

Implementation Details.
We implemented GraphAnchor using Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, Qwen2.5-14B-Instruct
36
, Qwen3-32B
35
, and Llama3.1-8B-Instruct
4
as the backbone models. During retrieval, bge-large-en-v1.5
34
is used to retrieve query-related documents, and the top 5 retrieved documents are fed into the LLM at each retrieval step. The maximum number of retrieval steps was limited to 4. All instruction templates used in GraphAnchor are shown in Appendix
A.2
.

Overall Performance

As shown in Table

The evaluation results demonstrate the effectiveness of GraphAnchor, which achieves more than a 3% improvement over all baseline models. Notably, these gains consistently generalize across different testing scenarios and backbone model scales, indicating strong robustness and generalization ability of GraphAnchor.
Compared with deep retrieval-based methods such as IRCoT, Iter-RetGen, and Search-R1, GraphAnchor achieves approximately 10% improvements. It is worth noting that the graph constructed by GraphAnchor is designed to fully exploit the retrieved knowledge rather than replacing raw evidence with a new representation, thus avoiding the loss of potentially critical clues contained in the original retrieved documents.

5.2
Ablation Study

To better understand the contribution of each core component in GraphAnchor, we conduct ablation studies as shown in Table
2
.

Five ablation variants are compared. First, we evaluate GraphAnchor (QA w/ Docs) and GraphAnchor (QA w/ Graph) to assess the knowledge representation capability by prompting LLMs to answer questions based solely on retrieved documents and the constructed graph, respectively. Then, we implement GraphAnchor w/ Text Index, where the summarization results are used as the index for GraphAnchor, aiming to examine the effectiveness of structural representations for knowledge indexing. The instruction is shown in Appendix
A.2
. Finally, we conduct GraphAnchor w/o Graph and GraphAnchor w/o Iterative Retrieval to analyze the roles of different model components.

When question answering relies only on the constructed graph (GraphAnchor (QA w/ Graph)), the RAG performance drops by approximately 3%. This indicates that the graph alone does not provide more effective knowledge for directly answering queries; instead, it serves as a form of knowledge anchoring that helps LLMs better interpret the retrieved information. Notably, for Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, GraphAnchor (QA w/ Graph) performs worse than GraphAnchor (QA w/ Docs), suggesting that some necessary information may be lost during graph construction and evolution.
Compared with GraphAnchor w/ Text Index, GraphAnchor achieves improvements of over 2%, demonstrating that structural representations, such as graphs, are beneficial for knowledge interaction and understanding during the indexing process.

We further analyze the effects of removing the iterative retrieval and graph modules. The evaluation results show that GraphAnchor w/o Iterative Retrieval suffers a performance drop of more than 10%, underscoring the critical role of iterative retrieval in incorporating sufficient knowledge for multi-hop QA tasks. When the graph is removed from both retrieval and QA stages of GraphAnchor, rather than only from the QA stage (GraphAnchor (QA w/ Docs)), the RAG performance decreases more substantially, by over 6% (about 2% more than the drop observed in GraphAnchor (QA w/ Docs)). This result indicates that the graph structure further facilitates iterative retrieval by enabling the generation of more effective subqueries, which in turn retrieve more relevant knowledge.

Effectiveness of Graphs in GraphAnchor During Iterative Retrieval

This subsection analyzes the effectiveness of the graphs constructed by GraphAnchor in the iterative retrieval process. We conduct experiments on the full HotpotQA dataset using the Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct model to show the role of the graph in knowledge anchoring and retrieval.

Graph Effectiveness in Knowledge Anchoring.

As shown in Figure

First, Figure

Notably, this growth continues to improve after the third retrieval step, while the hit rate of the ground-truth answer reaches a plateau. This observation suggests that the indexing process does not merely incorporate query-related entities and triples, but instead dynamically adjusts the graph structure to enable more effective knowledge indexing.
Then, we evaluate the RAG performance at different retrieval steps using the F1 score in Figure

The index-related attention consistently increases as we utilize the graph constructed through successive retrieval iterations, indicating that the evolving graph progressively anchors key information from the retrieved documents at each step, forming part of the graph-based index maintained by GraphAnchor. Meanwhile, the perplexity of ground-truth answer generation steadily decreases, suggesting that the evolving graph index indeed helps LLMs leverage the retrieved documents more effectively for question answering.
As shown in Figure

The peak intensity along the diagonal indicates that, at each retrieval step, the newly graph-anchored entities receive substantially higher attention once the graph constructed at the corresponding retrieval iteration is incorporated. This observation suggests that the graph functions as a step-by-step indexing intermediate, effectively modulating the attention distribution of LLMs to facilitate more accurate knowledge interpretation and utilization.

6
Conclusion

This paper introduces GraphAnchor, a novel graph-based knowledge anchoring approach that reformulates the graph structure from a refined knowledge representation into an evolutive index for RAG. By dynamically updating the graph at each retrieval step, the proposed graph enables LLMs to anchor a broader set of related entities for knowledge utilization, which offers a new perspective for alleviating knowledge conflicts in LLMs.

Limitations

Although GraphAnchor demonstrates effectiveness in knowledge indexing for RAG systems, the quality of the constructed graph index remains constrained by the capabilities of the underlying LLM. Specifically, the performance of the proposed graph construction and utilization depends on the model’s ability to accurately extract, structure, and organize relevant information from retrieved documents. In addition, the current implementation represents the graph solely through verbalized entities and relations; more expressive and structured graph representation methods may further enhance performance.

Knowledge Utilization Performance of Different Indexing Formats

As shown in Figure
7
, we evaluate how different knowledge indexing formats support RAG models in effectively utilizing external knowledge during answer generation. We compare GraphAnchor with GraphAnchor w/ Text index.
Subsequently, we conduct QA performance experiments by using Chain-of-Thoughts (CoT) generated based on knowledge indexing constructed under different formats.

Knowledge Usage Performance.

As shown in Figure
7(a)
, we design three evaluation scenarios to assess the effectiveness of different indexing formats: Has-Answer, Miss-Answer, and Internal Knowledge.
The Has-Answer scenario includes cases where the retrieved documents contain the golden answer, and evaluates whether the model can accurately identify and leverage answer-relevant evidence from the retrieved context to support final answer generation.
The Miss-Answer scenario focuses on cases in which the retrieved documents do not contain the golden answer, thereby measuring the model’s robustness to retrieval noise and its ability to avoid being misled by irrelevant or spurious information.
Finally, the Internal Knowledge scenario examines how different models resolve potential conflicts between internal parametric knowledge and externally retrieved evidence.

We evaluate GraphAnchor on the MuSiQue, HotpotQA, and 2WikiMHQA datasets and report the averaged results. As shown in the evaluation results, GraphAnchor consistently outperforms GraphAnchor w/ Text index across all datasets and testing scenarios.
In particular, GraphAnchor achieves substantially higher knowledge usage in the Has-Answer scenario compared to GraphAnchor w/ Text index. This result indicates that structured knowledge indexing enables more precise identification and aggregation of answer-supporting evidence, thereby enhancing the model’s ability to exploit retrieved documents effectively.
In the Miss-Answer scenario, both methods exhibit a notable decline in knowledge usage. Nevertheless, GraphAnchor still maintains a clear advantage, suggesting that graph-based indexing is more effective at suppressing irrelevant or noisy information and reducing the risk of being misled by external evidence.
For the Internal Knowledge scenario, both indexing formats achieve comparable performance. This observation highlights a shared benefit of explicit knowledge indexing: by organizing external information into a structured and context-aware anchoring, the model can better mitigate inconsistencies between internal and external knowledge through more selective integration. The slight performance gain of GraphAnchor further suggests that additional structural constraints can contribute to improved alignment.

Reasoning Ability.

To further investigate the contribution of structured indexing to reasoning quality, we conduct an experiment in which CoT are generated based on the constructed knowledge indexing. We compare GraphAnchor with GraphAnchor w/ Text index by using the generated CoT to produce final answers. As illustrated in Figure
7(b)
, GraphAnchor consistently outperforms text-based indexing across all datasets.
This consistent improvement indicates that structured indexing provides a more suitable reasoning substrate for CoT generation. By explicitly modeling entities and their relationships, the model is able to construct more coherent and evidence-grounded intermediate reasoning steps, which in turn more effectively guide final answer generation.

In contrast, Vanilla RAG displays a more diffuse attention pattern across the paragraph, covering substantial amounts of less relevant contextual text. A.7
Case Study

Figure 12:
Case Study.

Figure
12
presents a case study illustrating how iterative graph indexing facilitates complex multi-hop reasoning.

The query asks for a singer who was both written for by Stacy Barthe and sang in church during childhood. During the initialization phase, the retrieved documents identify several candidate entities (Katy Perry, Kelly Rowland, and Rihanna) associated with Stacy Barthe, but fail to confirm the “singing in church” constraint. The model therefore constructs an initial graph to anchor these entities and explicitly identifies the missing information as an unresolved reasoning node. Based on this gap, the model formulates a targeted follow-up query: “Did Katy Perry, Kelly Rowland, or Rihanna sing in church during their childhood?”. In the subsequent graph update phase, the retriever acquires specific evidence verifying Katy Perry’s background. This new evidence is incorporated into the existing graph index by adding the corresponding node and relation, thereby completing the reasoning chain. By explicitly encoding the connection between Katy Perry and church singing, the graph enables the model to transition from insufficient evidence to a complete and well-supported reasoning path, ultimately producing the correct answer. This case study demonstrates that our method effectively maintains a structured representation of intermediate reasoning states while actively resolving missing logical links through iterative retrieval and graph updates.
