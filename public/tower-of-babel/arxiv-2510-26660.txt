Equivalences for the (2-)categories of monoids and unital semigroups
Xavier Mary

Abstract
. We construct a category equivalent to the category 𝐌𝐨𝐧 \mathbf{Mon} of monoids and monoid homomorphisms, based on categories with strict factorization systems. This equivalence is then extended to the category 𝐌𝐨𝐧 𝐬 \mathbf{Mon_{s}} of unital semigroups and semigroup homomorphisms. By introducing suitable natural transformations, we turn these equivalences into 2-equivalences between 2-categories. The 2-category 𝐌𝐨𝐧 𝐬 𝟐 \mathbf{Mon_{s}^{2}} constructed this way proves the good one to study Morita equivalence of monoids.



Introduction

It is standard to consider a monoid
M
as a one-object category (its ‘delooping’
{\mathbf{B}}M
), where elements of the monoid become morphisms of the category. Or, even better, as a pointed and connected small category (where there is exactly one isomorphism class of objects). This viewpoint, in particular, leads to an equivalence between the (
2
-)category
\mathbf{Mon}
of monoids and
\mathbf{ConnectedCat*}
, the (
2
-)category of pointed and connected small categories (see notably the lecture by Baez and Shulman on the subject
[
1
, Section 5.6]
).

In this article, we take the opposite point of view and associate to any monoid a kind of ‘loop space’, which is a small category whose objects are the elements of the monoid, described in Section
1.2
. This construction is due to Costa and Steinberg
[
3
]
in the semigroup case, but is actually a special case of a more general categorical construction when one considers monoids. This category, called the Schützenberger category of the monoid, happens to be equipped canonically with a strict factorization system with additional properties. Such systems are investigated in Section
1.1
. This crucial property allows us to construct a category equivalent to the category
\mathbf{Mon}
of monoids and monoid homomorphisms in Section
2
. This equivalence is then extended to the category
\mathbf{Mon_{s}}
of unital semigroups and semigroup homomorphisms. By introducing suitable natural transformations, we finally extend these equivalences to 2-equivalences between 2-categories. It turns out that the 2-category
\mathbf{Mon_{s}^{2}}
constructed this way has the following fundamental property: adjoint equivalence in this 2-category corresponds precisely to Morita equivalence of monoids. This is the content of Section
3
.

1.1.
Strict Factorization Systems

The following definition and results regarding strict factorization systems are taken from Grandis
[
4
]
.
Let
{\mathcal{C}}
be a small category. A
strict factorization system
(SFS) on
{\mathcal{C}}
is a pair
({\mathcal{E}},\mathcal{M})
of wide subcategories (same class of objects) of
{\mathcal{C}}
such that any morphism
f\in{\mathcal{C}}_{1}
admits a unique decomposition
f=em
with
e\in{\mathcal{E}}_{1}
and
m\in\mathcal{M}_{1}
.
In the following, by a
category with a strict factorization system
(CSFS) we will mean a triple
({\mathcal{C}},{\mathcal{E}},\mathcal{M})
where
({\mathcal{E}},\mathcal{M})
is a SFS on
{\mathcal{C}}
.

For any SFS
({\mathcal{E}},\mathcal{M})
it holds that
[
4
]
:

(1)

{\mathcal{E}}_{1}\cap\mathcal{M}_{1}={\mathcal{C}}_{0}
the set of identities;

(2)

{\mathcal{E}}_{1}
and
\mathcal{M}_{1}
are orthogonal in
{\mathcal{C}}
;

(3)

there is a unique
orthogonal factorization system
(OFS)
({\mathbb{E}},{\mathbb{M}})
spanned by
({\mathcal{E}},\mathcal{M})
, where morphisms of
{\mathbb{E}}
(resp.
{\mathbb{M}}
) are of the form
ei
(resp.
im
), with
i
an iso and
e\in{\mathcal{E}}
(resp.
m\in\mathcal{M}
).

Maps in
{\mathcal{E}}
will be depicted by
\twoheadrightarrow
and maps in
\mathcal{M}
will be depicted by
\rightarrowtail
.
Duality for SFS takes a simple form:
({\mathcal{E}},\mathcal{M})
is a SFS for
{\mathcal{C}}
iff
(\mathcal{M}^{op},{\mathcal{E}}^{op})
is a SFS for
{\mathcal{C}}^{op}
.

The SFS
({\mathcal{E}},\mathcal{M})
is epi-monic (or proper) if all
{\mathcal{E}}-
maps are epi (in
{\mathcal{C}}
) and all
\mathcal{M}
-maps are monics (in
{\mathcal{C}}
).

3.
Natural transformations, 2-categories and Morita equivalence

3.1.
Natural transformations and conjugations

Consider two morphisms
F,G:A\to A^{\prime}
in the category
\mathbf{uc-CTSFS}
(
A=({\mathcal{C}},{\mathcal{E}},\mathcal{M},\zeta)
and
A^{\prime}=({\mathcal{C}}^{\prime},{\mathcal{E}}^{\prime},\mathcal{M}^{\prime},\zeta^{\prime})
). As pointed functors, we can consider pointed natural transformations between them.
A pointed natural transformation
\alpha:F\Rightarrow G
between pointed functors must satisfy:

\textstyle{\;\zeta\;\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{\;\zeta^{\prime}\;\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{\;\zeta^{\prime}\;\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}

\textstyle{\;x\;\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}

\textstyle{\;\alpha(x)\;\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{\;G(x)\;\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{\;\zeta\;}
\textstyle{\;\zeta^{\prime}\;\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{\zeta^{\prime}\;\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{\;\zeta^{\prime}\;}

This implies that
F(x)=\alpha(x)=G(x)
for all
x\in{\mathcal{C}}_{0}
, and since
F
and
G
are entirely defined by their action on the objects,
F=G
, and
\alpha
is the identity.

Corollary 3.1
.

The
2
-category of monoids, monoid homomorphisms and trivial
2
-cells is equivalent to the
2
-category of small categories with unital, thin and complete SFS, pointed functors that respect the SFS as morphisms, and pointed natural transformations as
2
-cells.

As semi-pointed functors, however, the natural transformations will not degenerate. A natural transformation
\alpha:F\Rightarrow G
amounts to a function
\alpha:{\mathcal{C}}_{0}\to{\mathcal{C}}_{0}^{{}^{\prime}}
such that the following diagram commutes:

\textstyle{\;a\;\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{\;F(a)\;\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}

\textstyle{\;G(a)\;\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}

\textstyle{\;F(x)\;\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{\;\alpha(x)\;\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{\;G(x)\;\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}

\textstyle{\;F(b)\;\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{\alpha(b)\;\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{\;G(b)\;}

In particular, this function

defines a (non-pointed) functor that respects the SFS.

We let
\alpha=\alpha(\zeta)\in{\mathcal{C}}_{0}^{{}^{\prime}}
. Let
a=b=\zeta
in this diagram. We obtain

\textstyle{\;\zeta\;\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{\;F(\zeta)\;\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{\;\alpha\;\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{\;G(\zeta)\;\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{\;x\;\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{\;F(x)\;\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{\;\alpha(x)\;\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{\;G(x)\;\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{\;\zeta\;}
\textstyle{\;F(\zeta)\;\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{\alpha\;\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{\;G(\zeta)\;}

Since the functor are semi-pointed, this reads (with the notation
\ast

(\forall x\in{\mathcal{C}}_{0})\;\alpha(x)=F(x)\ast^{\prime}\alpha=\alpha\ast^{\prime}G(x).

This expresses
\alpha

and
G
.
In particular (for
x=\zeta
) it holds that
\alpha=F(\zeta)\ast^{\prime}\alpha=\alpha\ast^{\prime}G(\zeta)
.

Conversely, given an element
\alpha\in{\mathcal{C}}_{0}^{{}^{\prime}}
such that
(\forall x\in{\mathcal{C}}_{0})\;F(x)\ast^{\prime}\alpha=\alpha\ast^{\prime}G(x)
, we can define
\alpha(x)=F(x)\ast^{\prime}\alpha=\alpha\ast^{\prime}G(x)
for all
x\in{\mathcal{C}}_{0}
. As the SFS of
A^{\prime}
is thin and complete, all the maps involved in the diagram defining the natural transformation exist and commute. We consider such elements as the natural 2-cells of a (strict) 2-category
\mathbf{uc-CTSFS}
, with semi-pointed functors as 1-cells.

Using the equivalence between
\mathbf{uc-CTSFS_{s}}
and
\mathbf{Mon_{s}}
, we can thus define natural transformations between semigroup homomorphisms. These natural transformations play a special role in the study of Morita equivalence of monoids, and have been called by M. Rogers conjugations (but his definition is opposite to ours).

Definition 3.2

(opposite to
[
10
, Definition 6.2]
)
.

Let
f,g:M\to M^{\prime}
be semigroup homomorphisms. A

\alpha
from
f
to
g
, denoted
\alpha:f\Rightarrow g
is an element
\alpha\in M^{\prime}
such that

f(1)\alpha=\alpha=\alpha g(1)
and for every

,
f(m)\alpha=\alpha g(m)
.

By construction, the functors
\mathbf{{\mathbb{D}}}
and
\mathbf{\Sigma}
extend to 2-functors on the respective 2-categories, where the image of the conjugation
\alpha:f\Rightarrow g
is the natural transformation
\alpha:F\to G
defined by
\alpha(.)=F(.)\alpha=\alpha G(.)
, and conversely to
\alpha:F\to G
we associate the conjugation
\alpha=\alpha(1)
.

Corollary 3.3
.

The functors
\mathbf{{\mathbb{D}}}
and
\mathbf{\Sigma}
define a strict 2-equivalence between the 2-categories
\mathbf{Mon_{s}}
, with conjugations as
2
-cells, and
\mathbf{uc-CTSFS_{s}}
, with natural transformations as
2
-cells.

3.2.
Morita equivalence of monoids

By
[
10
, Corollary 6.6]
, these 2-cells provides exactly the good notion of Morita equivalence between monoids (in the sense of
[
2
,
8
,
11
]
). However, the triangle identities are not considered, and the proof uses topos theory. We will prove it directly below.

First, we recall the following well known characterization of Morita equivalence by enlargements, due independently to Knauer
[
8
, Theorem]
and Banachewski
[
2
, Proposition 4]
. Let
M,M^{\prime}
be monoids. Then
M
and
M^{\prime}
are Morita equivalent if and only if there is an idempotent
e\in M
such that
M=MeM
and
M^{\prime}\simeq eMe
.

To interpret this result in terms of adjoints in our
2
-category, we need the following lemma, that describes adjunctions in terms of conjugations.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
f:M\to M^{\prime};g
be a pair of semigroup morphisms. Let also
\eta:Id_{M}\Rightarrow g\circ f
and
\varepsilon f\circ g\Rightarrow Id_{M^{\prime}}
be two conjugations.
The triangle identities are satisfied (equiv.
{\mathbb{D}}(f)
and
{\mathbb{D}}(g)
are adjoint functors, equiv.
f
and
g
are adjoint in the 2-category
\mathbf{Mon_{s}}
) if and only if:

(1)

f(1)=f(\eta)\varepsilon
;

(2)

g(1^{\prime})=\eta g(\varepsilon)
.

Proof.

The triangle identities are satisfied if and only if for all
m\in M
,
f(m)
is the central element in the factorization of
f(\eta(m))\CM f(g(f(m)))\CE\varepsilon(f(m))
. Since
\eta:id_{M}\Rightarrow(g\circ f)
is a conjugation, it satisfies
\eta(m)=m\eta=\eta g(f(m))
for all
m\in M
, and dually
\varepsilon(f(m))=f(g(f(m)))\varepsilon=\varepsilon m
. Thus the central element in the factorization is
f(\eta)f(g(f(m)))\varepsilon=f(m){\mathrm{f}}(\eta)\varepsilon
and the first identity reduces to: for all
m\in M
,
f(m)=f(m)f(\eta)\varepsilon
. Dually, the second identity reads for all
m^{\prime}\in M^{\prime}
,
g(m^{\prime})=\eta g(\varepsilon)g(m^{\prime})
. But
f
and
g
are semigroup homomorphisms, so that this is equivalent to
f(1)=f(\eta)\varepsilon
and
g(1^{\prime})=\eta g(\varepsilon)
.
∎

We also need to characterize invertible conjugations.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
f,g:M\to M^{\prime}
be two homomorphisms, and
\alpha:f\Rightarrow g
be a conjugation.Then
\alpha
is invertible if and only if there exists
\beta\in M^{\prime}
such that
\alpha\beta=f(1)
and
\beta\alpha=g(1)
. In this case, the inverse of
\alpha:f\Rightarrow g
is the conjugation
\gamma:g\Rightarrow f
defined by the element
\gamma=\beta\alpha\beta
.

(Contrary to
[
10
]
, we do not assume
a priori
that
\beta
is a conjugation).

Proof.

Consider
F,G:{\mathbb{D}}(M)\to{\mathbb{D}}(M^{\prime})
. Then
\alpha:F\Rightarrow G
is invertible if and only if there exists a conjugation
\beta
such that their composite is the identity transformation.
By composition, this implies that for all
x\in M

f(x)=\alpha g(x)\beta=f(x)\alpha\beta
and in particular
f(1)=f(1)\alpha\beta
. But
f(1)\alpha=\alpha
and we conclude that
f(1)=\alpha\beta
. The second identity is dual.

Conversely, suppose that there exists an element
\beta
satisfying these identities. We prove that
\gamma=\beta\alpha\beta
is a conjugation from
g\to f
.
Let
m\in M
. Then

\displaystyle g(m)\beta

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle g(1.m)\beta

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle g(1)g(m)\beta

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\beta\alpha g(m)\beta

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\beta f(m)\alpha\beta\qquad\text{ (since $\alpha:f\to g$ is a conjugation)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\beta f(m)f(1)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\beta f(m.1)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\beta f(m)

Thus
g(m)\gamma=g(m)\beta\alpha\beta=\beta g(m)g(1)=\beta g(1)g(m)=\gamma g(m)
. As also
g(1)\gamma=\gamma=\gamma f(1)
, we deduce that
\gamma
is a conjugation from
g
to
f
.
Finally, we have that
\alpha\gamma=\alpha\beta\alpha\beta=f(1)\alpha\beta=\alpha\beta=f(1)
, and dually
\gamma\alpha=g(1)
. We deduce that the associated natural transformations are reciprocal. ∎

Observe that, under the assumptions of Lemma
3.5
,
\beta
is an inner inverse of
\alpha
in the monoid
M^{\prime}
. In particular,
\alpha\beta,\beta\alpha
are idempotents and the inverse conjugation
\gamma=\beta\alpha\beta
is a reflexive inverse of
\alpha
. Also, we see that in order to be related by an invertible conjugation, two homomorphisms must necessarily satisfy that
f(1)
and
g(1)
are isomorphic idempotents (where two idempotents

in a monoid
M^{\prime}
are
isomorphic
if there exists
x,y\in M^{\prime}
such that
e=xy
and
f=yx
, equiv.
eM^{\prime}\simeq fM^{\prime}
as right
M^{\prime}
-acts, equiv.
e{\mathcal{D}}f
where
{\mathcal{D}}
denotes Green’s equivalence relation).

Example
3.6
.

Let
S(2)
be the symmetric group on

and

be the transformation semigroup on

and

are semigroup homomorphisms. Consider
\alpha=(1\;2\;3\;3)

\displaystyle f(1\;2)\alpha

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(1\;2\;3\;4)(1\;2\;3\;3)=(1\;2\;3\;3)=(1\;2\;3\;3)(1\;2\;3\;3)=\alpha g(1\;2)

\displaystyle f(2\;1)\alpha

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(2\;1\;3\;4)(1\;2\;3\;3)=(2\;1\;3\;3)=(1\;2\;3\;3)(2\;1\;3\;3)=\alpha g(2\;1)

If we consider the third homomorphism

We are finally in position to consider Morita equivalence.
Let

be a monoid,

be an idempotent such that

, and

. Without loss of generality, we can assume that

and

Conversely, suppose that

and

.
The element
