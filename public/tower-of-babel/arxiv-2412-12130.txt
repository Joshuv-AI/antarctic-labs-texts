On a nonlinear Diophantine equation with powers of three consecutive k k –Lucas Numbers
Herbert Batte, Florian Luca

Abstract
Let ( L n ( k ) ) n ≥ 2 − k (L_{n}^{(k)})_{n\geq 2-k} be the sequence of k k –generalized Lucas numbers for some fixed integer k ≥ 2 k\geq 2 whose first k k terms are 0 , … , 0 , 2 , 1 0,\ldots,0,2,1 and each term afterwards is the sum of the preceding k k terms. In this paper, we completely solve the nonlinear Diophantine equation ( L n + 1 ( k ) ) x + ( L n ( k ) ) x − ( L n − 1 ( k ) ) x = L m ( k ) \left(L_{n+1}^{(k)}\right)^{x}+\left(L_{n}^{(k)}\right)^{x}-\left(L_{n-1}^{(k)}\right)^{x}=L_{m}^{(k)} , in nonnegative integers n n , m m , k k , x x , with k ≥ 2 k\geq 2 .

–generalized Lucas numbers; linear forms in logarithms; Baker–Davenport reduction method, LLL–algorithm.

Introduction

1.1
Background

For any integer
k\geq 2
, the sequence of
k
–generalized Lucas numbers is defined by the recurrence relation:

L_{n}^{(k)}=L_{n-1}^{(k)}+\cdots+L_{n-k}^{(k)},\quad\text{for all}\ n\geq 2,

with the initial terms set as
L_{0}^{(k)}=2
and
L_{1}^{(k)}=1
for all
k\geq 2
, and
L_{2-k}^{(k)}=\cdots=L_{-1}^{(k)}=0
for
k\geq 3
. For
k=2
, this reduces to the classical sequence of Lucas numbers, and the superscript
(k)
is typically omitted.

The study of sequences such as the Fibonacci and Lucas numbers, and their generalizations, has led to the discovery of numerous interesting identities and Diophantine equations. Similar to how various authors have explored identities involving the
k
–Fibonacci numbers, the
k
–Lucas numbers also exhibit a rich structure that can be investigated through the lens of number theory. In
[
8
]
, Gómez et al. studied the Diophantine equation

\displaystyle\left(F_{n+1}^{(k)}\right)^{x}+\left(F_{n}^{(k)}\right)^{x}-\left(F_{n-1}^{(k)}\right)^{x}=F_{m}^{(k)},

(1.1)

in nonnegative integers
n
,
m
,
k
,
x
, with
n\geq 1
and
k\geq 2
, where
F_{r}^{(k)}
is the
r^{\text{th}}
term of the
k
–Fibonacci sequence. They showed that the Diophantine eq. (
1.1
) does not have non–trivial solutions
(k,n,m,x)
with
k\geq 2
,
n\geq 1
,
m\geq 2
and
x\geq 1
.

Motivated by the work in
[
8
]
, we study the same Diophantine equation but with
k
–Lucas numbers. Specifically, we solve the nonlinear Diophantine equation

\displaystyle\left(L_{n+1}^{(k)}\right)^{x}+\left(L_{n}^{(k)}\right)^{x}-\left(L_{n-1}^{(k)}\right)^{x}=L_{m}^{(k)},

(1.2)

in nonnegative integers
n
,
m
,
k
,
x
, with
k\geq 2
. We prove the following result.

1.2
Main Result

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
(L_{n}^{(k)})_{n\geq 2-k}
be the sequence of
k
–generalized Lucas numbers. Then, the only integer solutions
(n,m,k,x)
to Eq. (
1.2
) with
n\geq 0,~m\geq 0,~k\geq 2
and
x\geq 0
(with the convention that
0^{0}:=1
in case
(n,x)=(0,0)
and
k\geq 3
) are

\displaystyle(n,m,k,x)\in\{(n,1,k,0),(1,0,k,1):k\geq 2\}\cup\{(0,2,k,1),(1,3,k,2):k\geq 3\}\cup\{(0,3,2,1),(0,3,2,2)\}.

Methods

2.1
Preliminaries

It is known that

\displaystyle L_{n}^{(k)}=3\cdot 2^{n-2},\qquad\text{for all}\qquad 2\leq n\leq k.

(2.1)

Additionally,
L_{k+1}^{(k)}=3\cdot 2^{k-1}-2
and by induction one proves that

L_{n}^{(k)}<3\cdot 2^{n-2}\qquad{\text{\rm holds for all}}\qquad n\geq k+1.

(2.2)

Next, we revisit some properties of the
k
–generalized Lucas numbers. They form a linearly recurrent sequence of characteristic polynomial

\Psi_{k}(x)=x^{k}-x^{k-1}-\cdots-x-1,

which is irreducible over
\mathbb{Q}[x]
. The polynomial
\Psi_{k}(x)
possesses a unique real root
\alpha(k)>1
and all the other roots are inside the unit circle, see
[
13
]
. The root
\alpha(k):=\alpha
is in the interval

\displaystyle 2(1-2^{-k})<\alpha<2

(2.3)

as noted in
[
15
]
. As in the classical case when
k=2
, it was shown in
[
2
]
that

\displaystyle\alpha^{n-1}\leq L_{n}^{(k)}\leq 2\alpha^{n},\quad\text{holds for all}\quad n\geq 0,~k\geq 2.

(2.4)

Also,
L_{-1}^{(k)}=-1
if
k=2
and
0
otherwise. In particular, combining (
2.4
) and (
1.2
), we have, if
n\geq 1
,

\displaystyle\alpha^{m-1}\leq L_{m}^{(k)}=\left(L_{n+1}^{(k)}\right)^{x}+\left(L_{n}^{(k)}\right)^{x}-\left(L_{n-1}^{(k)}\right)^{x}<2\left(L_{n+1}^{(k)}\right)^{x}\leq\alpha^{(n+3)x+2},

where we have used the fact that
2<\alpha^{2}
, for all
k\geq 2
. The above inequality also holds for
n=0
since in this case

(L_{n+1}^{(k)})^{x}+(L_{n}^{(k)})^{x}-(L_{n-1}^{(k)})^{x}\leq 1+2^{x}+1\leq 2^{x+1}<\alpha^{2x+2}<\alpha^{(n+3)x+2}.

This gives
m<(n+3)x+3
. On the other hand, if
n\geq 1
, and
x\geq 2
, we have

\displaystyle\alpha^{m+2}>2\alpha^{m}\geq L_{m}^{(k)}=\left(L_{n+1}^{(k)}\right)^{x}+\left(L_{n}^{(k)}\right)^{x}-\left(L_{n-1}^{(k)}\right)^{x}>\alpha^{nx}+\alpha^{(n-1)x}-2^{x}\alpha^{(n-1)x}\geq\alpha^{nx-1},

from which we have
m>nx-3
. The above inequality also holds for
x=1
since in this case

\alpha^{m+2}>2\alpha^{m}\geq L_{m}^{(k)}=L_{n+1}^{(k)}+L_{n}^{(k)}-L_{n-1}^{(k)}\geq 2L_{n}^{(k)}>\alpha^{n},\quad{\text{\rm so}}\qquad m>n-3.

Finally, the above inequality holds for
n=0
as well since we assume that
m\geq 0
. Therefore, we have

\displaystyle nx-3<m<(n+3)x+3.

(2.5)

Let
k\geq 2
and define

f_{k}(x):=\dfrac{x-1}{2+(k+1)(x-2)}.

(2.6)

We have

\frac{df_{k}(x)}{dx}=-\frac{(k-1)}{(2+(k+1)(x-2))^{2}}<0\qquad{\text{\rm for~all}}\qquad x>0.

In particular, inequality (
2.3
) implies that

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{2}=f_{k}(2)<f_{k}(\alpha)<f_{k}(2(1-2^{-k}))\leq\dfrac{3}{4},

(2.7)

for all
k\geq 3
. It is easy to check that the above inequality holds for
k=2
as well. Further, it is easy to verify that
|f_{k}(\alpha_{i})|<1
, for all
2\leq i\leq k
, where
\alpha_{i}
are the remaining
roots of
\Psi_{k}(x)
for
i=2,\ldots,k
.

Moreover, it was shown in
[
2
]
that

\displaystyle L_{n}^{(k)}=\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k}(2\alpha_{i}-1)f_{k}(\alpha_{i})\alpha_{i}^{n-1}~~\text{and}~~\left|L_{n}^{(k)}-f_{k}(\alpha)(2\alpha-1)\alpha^{n-1}\right|<\dfrac{3}{2},

(2.8)

for all
k\geq 2
and
n\geq 2-k
. This means that

L_{n}^{(k)}=f_{k}(\alpha)(2\alpha-1)\alpha^{n-1}+e_{k}(n),\qquad{\text{\rm where}}\qquad|e_{k}(n)|<1.5.

(2.9)

The left expression in (
2.8
) is known as the Binet formula for
L_{n}^{(k)}
. Furthermore, the right inequality in (
2.8
) shows that the contribution of the zeros that are inside the unit circle to
L_{n}^{(k)}
is small.

Next, we state the following result which we shall use later in the proof of the main result. It is stated as Lemma 3 in
[
6
]
.

Lemma 2.1

(Lemma 3 in
[
6
]
)
.

Let
k\geq 2
,
c\in(0,1)
and
2\leq n<2^{ck}
. Then

L_{n}^{(k)}=3\cdot 2^{n-2}(1+\zeta^{\prime}_{n}),\quad\text{with}\quad|\zeta^{\prime}_{n}|<\begin{cases}\dfrac{4}{2^{(1-c)k}},&\text{if}\ c\leq 0.693,\\
\dfrac{8.1}{2^{(1-c)k}},&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Next, we prove the following result.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
k\geq 2
,
x
,
n
be positive integers. Then

\left(L_{n}^{(k)}\right)^{x}=f_{k}(\alpha)^{x}(2\alpha-1)^{x}\alpha^{(n-1)x}(1+\eta_{n}),

with

|\eta_{n}|<\dfrac{1.5xe^{1.5x/\alpha^{n-1}}}{\alpha^{n-1}}.

Proof.

By the Binet formula in (
2.9
) and inequalities (
2.7
), it follows that

\left(L_{n}^{(k)}\right)^{x}=f_{k}(\alpha)^{x}(2\alpha-1)^{x}\alpha^{(n-1)x}\left(1+\dfrac{e_{k}(n)}{f_{k}(\alpha)(2\alpha-1)\alpha^{n-1}}\right)^{x}.

So, if we define

\eta_{n}:=\left(1+\dfrac{e_{k}(n)}{f_{k}(\alpha)(2\alpha-1)\alpha^{n-1}}\right)^{x}-1=\sum_{j=1}^{x}\binom{x}{j}\left(\frac{e_{k}(n)}{f_{k}(\alpha)(2\alpha-1)\alpha^{n-1}}\right)^{j};

we get

|\eta_{n}|<\sum_{j=1}^{x}\dfrac{(1.5x/\alpha^{n-1})^{j}}{j!}<\dfrac{1.5x}{\alpha^{n-1}}\sum_{j=1}^{x}\dfrac{(1.5x/\alpha^{n-1})^{j-1}}{(j-1)!}\leq\dfrac{1.5xe^{1.5x/\alpha^{n-1}}}{\alpha^{n-1}},

where we have used the fact
|e_{k}(n)|<1.5
and
(2\alpha-1)f_{k}(\alpha)>1
.
∎

Lastly here, we prove the following.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
x\geq 1
,
k\geq 2
,
i\in\{-1,0,1\}
,
n+i\geq k+2
and
\max\{n+i,16x\}<2^{ck}
for some
c\in(0,0.25)
. Then, the estimate

\left(L_{n+i}^{(k)}\right)^{x}=3^{x}\cdot 2^{(n+i-2)x}\left(1+\xi_{n+i}\right)

holds with

|\xi_{n+i}|<\dfrac{2}{2^{(1-2c)k}}.

Proof.

By Lemma
2.1
with
c\in(0,0.25)
, we have

L_{n+i}^{(k)}=3\cdot 2^{n+i-2}\left(1+\zeta^{\prime}_{n+i}\right),\quad\text{with}\quad|\zeta^{\prime}_{n+i}|<\dfrac{4}{2^{(1-c)k}}.

Hence,

\displaystyle\left(L_{n+i}^{(k)}\right)^{x}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 3^{x}\cdot 2^{(n+i-2)x}\left(1+\zeta_{n+i}^{\prime}\right)^{x}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 3^{x}2^{(n+i-2)x}\exp(x\log(1+\zeta_{n+i}^{\prime}))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 3^{x}2^{(n+i-2)x}\exp(\eta_{n+i}^{\prime})\qquad|\eta_{n+i}^{\prime}|<2x|\zeta_{n+i}^{\prime}|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 3^{x}2^{(n+i-2)x}(1+\xi_{n+i}^{\prime})\qquad|\xi_{n+i}^{\prime}|<2|\eta_{n+i}^{\prime}|<4x|\zeta_{n+i}^{\prime}|<\frac{16x}{2^{(1-c)k}}<\frac{1}{2^{(1-2c)k}}\left(<\frac{1}{2}\right)

The above calculations are justified since both inequalities
|\log(1+y)|<2|y|
and
|\exp y-1|<2|y|
hold for
y\in(-1/2,1/2)
, and the fact that
all the intermediate quantities
\zeta_{n+i}^{\prime},~\eta_{n+i}^{\prime},\xi_{n+i^{\prime}}
are in the range
(-1/2,1/2)
follows from the very last inequality above.
∎

2.2
Linear forms in logarithms

We use Baker–type lower bounds for nonzero linear forms in logarithms of algebraic numbers. There are many such bounds mentioned in the literature but we use one of Matveev from
[
12
]
. Before we can formulate such inequalities, we need the notion of height of an algebraic number recalled below.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
\gamma
be an algebraic number of degree
d
with minimal primitive polynomial over the integers

a_{0}x^{d}+a_{1}x^{d-1}+\cdots+a_{d}=a_{0}\prod_{i=1}^{d}(x-\gamma^{(i)}),

where the leading coefficient
a_{0}
is positive. Then, the logarithmic height of
\gamma
is given by

h(\gamma):=\dfrac{1}{d}\Big(\log a_{0}+\sum_{i=1}^{d}\log\max\{|\gamma^{(i)}|,1\}\Big).

In particular, if
\gamma
is a rational number represented as
\gamma=p/q
with coprime integers
p
and
q\geq 1
, then
h(\gamma)=\log\max\{|p|,q\}
.
The following properties of the logarithmic height function
h(\cdot)
will be used in the rest of the paper without further reference:

\displaystyle h(\gamma_{1}\pm\gamma_{2})

\displaystyle\leq h(\gamma_{1})+h(\gamma_{2})+\log 2;

\displaystyle h(\gamma_{1}\gamma_{2}^{\pm 1})

\displaystyle\leq h(\gamma_{1})+h(\gamma_{2});

\displaystyle h(\gamma^{s})

\displaystyle=|s|h(\gamma)\quad{\text{\rm valid for}}\quad s\in\mathbb{Z}.

In Section 3, equation (12) of
[
1
]
these properties were used to show the following inequality:

\displaystyle h\left(f_{k}(\alpha)\right)<3\log k,~~\text{for all}~~k\geq 2.

(2.10)

A linear form in logarithms is an expression

\Lambda:=b_{1}\log\gamma_{1}+\cdots+b_{t}\log\gamma_{t},

(2.11)

where
\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{t}
are positive real algebraic numbers and
b_{1},\ldots,b_{t}
are integers. We assume,
\Lambda\neq 0
. We need lower bounds
for
|\Lambda|
. We write
{\mathbb{K}}:={\mathbb{Q}}(\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{t})
and
D
for the degree of
{\mathbb{K}}
over
{\mathbb{Q}}
.
We give a direct consequence of Matveev’s inequality from
[
12
]
. That is, we quote it in a form which we shall use.

Theorem 2.1

(Matveev,
[
12
]
)
.

Put
\Gamma:=\gamma_{1}^{b_{1}}\cdots\gamma_{t}^{b_{t}}-1=e^{\Lambda}-1
. Then

\log|\Gamma|>-1.4\cdot 30^{t+3}\cdot t^{4.5}\cdot D^{2}(1+\log D)(1+\log B)A_{1}\cdots A_{t},

where
B\geq\max\{|b_{1}|,\ldots,|b_{t}|\}
and
A_{i}\geq\max\{Dh(\gamma_{i}),|\log\gamma_{i}|,0.16\}
for
i=1,\ldots,t
.

In our application of Matveev’s result (Theorem
2.1
), we need to ensure that the linear forms in logarithms are indeed nonzero. To ensure this, we shall need the following result given as Lemma 2.8 in
[
7
]
.

Lemma 2.4

(Lemma 2.8 in
[
7
]
)
.

Let
N:=N_{\mathbb{K}/\mathbb{Q}},
where
\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{Q}(\alpha)
. Then

(i)

For
n,m\geq 1
and
k\geq 2
,
|N(\alpha)|=1
.

(ii)

N(2\alpha-1)=2^{k+1}-3
and
N(f_{k}(\alpha))=(k-1)^{2}/(2^{k+1}k^{k}-(k+1)^{k+1})
.

(iii)

For
k\geq 2
,
N((2\alpha-1)f_{k}(\alpha))\leq 1
. This inequality is strict for
k\geq 3
(and is an equality for
k=2
).

At some point, we treat cases with
t=2
, that is, linear forms in two logarithms. Let
A_{1},~A_{2}>1
be real numbers such that

\log A_{i}\geq\max\left\{h(\gamma_{i}),\frac{|\log\gamma_{i}|}{D},\frac{1}{D}\right\}\quad{\text{\rm for}}\quad i=1,2.

(2.12)

Put

b^{\prime}:=\frac{|b_{1}|}{D\log A_{2}}+\frac{|b_{2}|}{D\log A_{1}}.

The following result is Corollary 2 in
[
10
]
.

Theorem 2.2

(Laurent et al.,
[
10
]
)
.

In case
t=2
, we put

\Lambda:=b_{1}\log\gamma_{1}-b_{2}\log\gamma_{2},

where
|\gamma_{1}|,|\gamma_{2}|\geq 1
are two multiplicatively independent real algebraic numbers and
b_{1},b_{2}
are positive integers. Then, we have

\log|\Lambda|\geq-24.34D^{4}\left(\max\left\{\log b^{\prime}+0.14,\frac{21}{D},\frac{1}{2}\right\}\right)^{2}\log A_{1}\log A_{2}.

During the calculations, upper bounds on the variables are obtained which are too large, thus there is need to reduce them. To do so, we use some results from
approximation lattices and the so–called LLL–reduction method from
[
11
]
. We explain this in the following subsection.

2.3
Reduced Bases for Lattices and LLL–reduction methods

Let
k
be a positive integer. A subset
\mathcal{L}
of the
k
–dimensional real vector space
{\mathbb{R}^{k}}
is called a lattice if there exists a basis
\{b_{1},b_{2},\ldots,b_{k}\}
of
\mathbb{R}^{k}
such that

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathbb{Z}b_{i}=\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{k}r_{i}b_{i}\mid r_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}\right\}.

We say that
b_{1},b_{2},\ldots,b_{k}
form a basis for
\mathcal{L}
, or that they span
\mathcal{L}
. We
call
k
the rank of
\mathcal{L}
. The determinant
\text{det}(\mathcal{L})
, of
\mathcal{L}
is defined by

\displaystyle\text{det}(\mathcal{L})=|\det(b_{1},b_{2},\ldots,b_{k})|,

with the
b_{i}
’s being written as column vectors. This is a positive real number that does not depend on the choice of the basis (see
[
4
]
, Section 1.2).

Given linearly independent vectors
b_{1},b_{2},\ldots,b_{k}
in
\mathbb{R}^{k}
, we refer back to the Gram–Schmidt orthogonalization technique. This method allows us to inductively define vectors
b^{*}_{i}
(with
1\leq i\leq k
) and real coefficients
\mu_{i,j}
(for
1\leq j\leq i\leq k
). Specifically,

\displaystyle b^{*}_{i}

\displaystyle=b_{i}-\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}\mu_{i,j}b^{*}_{j},~~~\mu_{i,j}=\dfrac{\langle b_{i},b^{*}_{j}\rangle}{\langle b^{*}_{j},b^{*}_{j}\rangle},

where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
denotes the ordinary inner product on
\mathbb{R}^{k}
. Notice that
b^{*}_{i}
is the orthogonal projection of
b_{i}
on the orthogonal complement of the span of
b_{1},\ldots,b_{i-1}
, and that
\mathbb{R}b_{i}
is orthogonal to the span of
b^{*}_{1},\ldots,b^{*}_{i-1}
for
1\leq i\leq k
. It follows that
b^{*}_{1},b^{*}_{2},\ldots,b^{*}_{k}
is an orthogonal basis of
\mathbb{R}^{k}
.

Definition 2.2
.

The basis
b_{1},b_{2},\ldots,b_{n}
for the lattice
\mathcal{L}
is called reduced if

\displaystyle\|\mu_{i,j}\|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2},\quad\text{for}\quad 1\leq j<i\leq n,~~\text{and}

\displaystyle\|b^{*}_{i}+\mu_{i,i-1}b^{*}_{i-1}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{3}{4}\|b^{*}_{i-1}\|^{2},\quad\text{for}\quad 1<i\leq n,

where
\|\cdot\|
denotes the ordinary Euclidean length. The constant
{3}/{4}
above is arbitrarily chosen, and may be replaced by any fixed real number
y
in the interval
{1}/{4}<y<1
(see
[
11
]
, Section 1).

Let
\mathcal{L}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{k}
be a
k-
dimensional lattice with reduced basis
b_{1},\ldots,b_{k}
and denote by
B
the matrix with columns
b_{1},\ldots,b_{k}
.
We define

l\left(\mathcal{L},y\right):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{c}\min_{x\in\mathcal{L}}||x-y||\quad;~~y\not\in\mathcal{L}\\
\min_{0\neq x\in\mathcal{L}}||x||\quad;~~y\in\mathcal{L}\end{array}\right.,

where
||\cdot||
denotes the Euclidean norm on
\mathbb{R}^{k}
. It is well known that, by applying the
LLL–algorithm, it is possible to give in polynomial time a lower bound for
l\left(\mathcal{L},y\right)
, namely a positive constant
c_{1}
such that
l\left(\mathcal{L},y\right)\geq c_{1}
holds (see
[
14
]
, Section V.4).

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
y\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
and
z=B^{-1}y
with
z=(z_{1},\ldots,z_{k})^{T}
. Furthermore,

(i)

if
y\not\in\mathcal{L}
, let
i_{0}
be the largest index such that
z_{i_{0}}\neq 0
and put
\sigma:=\{z_{i_{0}}\}
, where
\{\cdot\}
denotes the distance to the nearest integer.

(ii)

if
y\in\mathcal{L}
, put
\sigma:=1
.

Finally, let

c_{2}:=\max\limits_{1\leq j\leq k}\left\{\dfrac{||b_{1}||^{2}}{||b_{j}^{*}||^{2}}\right\}.

Then,

l\left(\mathcal{L},y\right)^{2}\geq c_{2}^{-1}\sigma^{2}||b_{1}||^{2}:=c_{1}^{2}.

In our application, we are given real numbers
\eta_{0},\eta_{1},\ldots,\eta_{k}
which are linearly independent over
\mathbb{Q}
and two positive constants
c_{3}
and
c_{4}
such that

\displaystyle|\eta_{0}+a_{1}\eta_{1}+\cdots+a_{k}\eta_{k}|\leq c_{3}\exp(-c_{4}H),

(2.13)

where the integers
a_{i}
are bounded as
|a_{i}|\leq A_{i}
with
A_{i}
given upper bounds for
1\leq i\leq k
. We write
A_{0}:=\max\limits_{1\leq i\leq k}\{A_{i}\}
. The basic idea in such a situation, from
[
5
]
, is to approximate the linear form (
2.13
) by an approximation lattice. So, we consider the lattice
\mathcal{L}
generated by the columns of the matrix

\mathcal{A}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&\ldots&0&0\\
0&1&\ldots&0&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots\\
0&0&\ldots&1&0\\
\lfloor C\eta_{1}\rfloor&\lfloor C\eta_{2}\rfloor&\ldots&\lfloor C\eta_{k-1}\rfloor&\lfloor C\eta_{k}\rfloor\end{pmatrix},

where
C
is a large constant usually of the size of about
A_{0}^{k}
. Let us assume that we have an LLL–reduced basis
b_{1},\ldots,b_{k}
of
\mathcal{L}
and that we have a lower bound
l\left(\mathcal{L},y\right)\geq c_{1}
with
y:=(0,0,\ldots,-\lfloor C\eta_{0}\rfloor)
. Note that
c_{1}
can be computed by using the results of Lemma
2.5
. Then, with these notations the following result is Lemma VI.1 in
[
14
]
.

Lemma 2.6

(Lemma VI.1 in
[
14
]
)
.

Let
S:=\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}A_{i}^{2}
and
T:=\dfrac{1+\sum_{i=1}^{k}A_{i}}{2}
. If
c_{1}^{2}\geq T^{2}+S
, then inequality (
2.13
) implies that we either have
a_{1}=a_{2}=\cdots=a_{k-1}=0
and
a_{k}=-\dfrac{\lfloor C\eta_{0}\rfloor}{\lfloor C\eta_{k}\rfloor}
, or

H\leq\dfrac{1}{c_{4}}\left(\log(Cc_{3})-\log\left(\sqrt{c_{1}^{2}-S}-T\right)\right).

Finally, we present an analytic argument which is Lemma 7 in
[
9
]
.

Lemma 2.7

(b)

If

\displaystyle L_{n+1}^{(k)}+L_{n}^{(k)}-L_{n-1}^{(k)}=L_{m}^{(k)}.

,

(n,m,k,x)=(1,0,k,1)

Assume from now on that

. Suppose next that

. Since

For the remaining part of the proof, we assume

,

3.2
The case
k=2
and
n=0,2

Assume
k=2
. In the case
n=0
we get the equation

1+2^{x}-(-1)^{x}=L_{m}.

When
x
is even, we get
L_{m}=2^{x}
and it is known (for example, by Carmichael’s primitive divisor theorem
[
3
]
) that the only powers of
2
in the Lucas sequence are
m=0,~3
for which
L_{0}=2,~L_{3}=2^{2}
and the only one with even exponent
x
is
L_{3}=2^{2}
. We get the solution
(n,m,k,x)=(0,3,2,2)
. When
x
is odd, we get
L_{m}=2^{x}+2
. When
x=1
, we get the solution
m=3
which leads to
(n,m,k,x)=(0,3,2,1)
. When
x=2
, we get
L_{m}=6
, which is false since
6
is not a member of the sequence of Lucas numbers. When
x\geq 3
, we get that
2\|L_{m}
so
m
is even (and multiple of
3
). In particular,
L_{m}=L_{m/2}^{2}\pm 2
. We thus get

L_{m/2}^{2}\pm 2=2^{x}+2,\qquad{\text{\rm so}}\qquad L_{m/2}^{2}\in\{2^{x},2^{x}+4\}.

Since
x
is odd, the equation
L_{m/2}^{2}=2^{x}
is not possible. Thus,
L_{m/2}^{2}=2^{x}+4
leading to
2\|L_{m/2}
and
(L_{m/2}^{2}/2)^{2}=2^{x-2}+1
. This is a particular instance of the Catalan equation and its only solution is
x-2=3
and
L_{m/2}/2=3
, leading to
L_{m/2}=6
, but this is wrong since
6
is not a member of the sequence of Lucas numbers.

Assume
n=2
. In this case we get the equation

4^{x}+3^{x}-1=L_{m}.

The left–hand side is a multiple of
3
, so
m
is even. Considering the values of

, we get that the left–hand side is congruent to

modulo
5
(if

), to
4\pmod{5}
(if
x\equiv 2\pmod{4}
) and to
0\pmod{5}
(if
x\equiv 3\pmod{4}
). Since the Lucas sequence is periodic modulo

with period

and achieves the values

, we get that only the cases
L_{m}\equiv 1,4\pmod{5}
are possible and this implies
m\equiv 1,3\pmod{4}
. This contradicts the fact that
m
is even.
