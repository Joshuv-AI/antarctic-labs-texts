Thermal and economic performance of MPCM-enhanced AAC bricks combined with an auxiliary heater plate in cold and freezing climates: A numerical investigation
Authors: Naif Albelwi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study is the first to numerically evaluate the thermal performance of MPCM-enhanced autoclaved aerated concrete (AAC) bricks combined with an auxiliary heater plate for passive space heating in cold and freezing climates. A two-dimensional finite-volume CFD model with time-dependent outdoor boundary conditions and an apparent-heat-capacity formulation for phase change is developed and implemented in a custom C++ solver. The effects of MPCM volume fraction (φ = 0, 4, 8, 12%) and heater-plate heat flux (q″hp = 0, 50, 100, 150 W/m2) are examined over a 24 h cycle with outdoor temperature varying between −15 and 15 °C. Indoor surface temperature (Tis), indoor heat flux (qis), MPCM melting fraction (MF), and daily power consumption (DPC) are employed as performance indicators. For q″ = 100–150 W/m2, φ = 8–12% reduces peak Tis by about 1–2 °C and peak qis by roughly 15–25%, while raising minimum values and delaying the peak by 1 h. At these heater powers, DPC drops from about 0.31–0.34 kWh/m2 for plain AAC to 0.24–0.28 kWh/m2 (0–30% saving) with MPCM, demonstrating the potential of the proposed AAC–MPCM–heater configuration for peak-load mitigation and energy-efficient envelope design in cold climates. Moreover, over a 40-year service life, the techno-economic analysis shows that 4 vol% MCPCM delivers the highest returns, with NPV = $11,689–$15,970 and IRR = 13.55–17.45% across q’'=0–150 W/m2.

Introduction

Reducing reliance on fossil-fuel energy for maintaining stable indoor temperatures has become increasingly important as greenhouse-gas emissions drive global warming and intensify climate variability1–3. Conventional heating and cooling systems, which depend heavily on fossil fuels, contribute substantially to atmospheric carbon accumulation, further amplifying the very temperature fluctuations they are designed to mitigate4,5. This feedback mechanism increases the long-term energy demand of buildings and accelerates environmental degradation. Therefore, lowering fossil-fuel consumption in the building sector is essential not only for improving energy efficiency but also for mitigating climate impacts and promoting more sustainable indoor thermal management strategies6–8. One promising approach to reducing this dependence on fossil-fuel heating and cooling is the incorporation of advanced building materials capable of regulating temperature passively8–10. Among these, thermally smart bricks enhanced with phase-change materials (PCMs) have attracted significant attention due to their ability to store and release latent heat as outdoor conditions fluctuate11–14. By buffering temperature fluctuations, PCM-integrated bricks help maintain a more stable indoor environment without continuous mechanical intervention, thereby lowering overall energy demand15,16. Such materials represent a practical and scalable strategy for improving building thermal performance while supporting broader efforts to decrease greenhouse-gas emissions and enhance sustainability in the built environment. In recent years, numerous studies have evaluated the thermal performance of thermally smart bricks (TSBs) enhanced with various PCMs under different climate conditions17–21. In one investigation22, adding PCM to lightweight wall systems made a big difference: the peak heat flux was cut by 66.4 %, and the transfer of heat through the wall was pushed back by more than 5 hours. The best phase change temperature range also depended on which way the wall was facing, 20–30 °C for east and south orientations, and 18–28 °C for west and north, showing that PCM can be tuned to improve thermal performance based on wall direction. Meng et al.23,24 shown that improving PCM-based thermal energy storage requires both faster heat transfer and better control of when and where stored heat is released. For spherical encapsulated PCM, increasing copper-foam fin thickness up to 15 mm can achieve 67% faster discharging and ~35% faster charging, while preserving > 97% of the PCM volume and increasing cycle efficiency to ~83%. At the building-envelope scale, a dynamic rotating PCM layer can release heat directly indoors during winter, producing an indoor-directed heat flow on the order of −22 to −80 W m⁻2, although it may reduce thermal release and storage capacity by ~33.8–34.8% compared with a static configuration. Taj et al.25 mixed eutectic organic PCMs into clay bricks and tested them both in the lab and in real buildings to assess thermal behavior, structural performance, and energy savings. They reported clear gains: indoor air temperatures were lowered by about 4–5.5 °C, the indoor temperature swing dropped by 32%, the heat-flow response was delayed by up to 150 minutes, and heat flux through the walls decreased by roughly 25–30% compared with standard bricks. Abdulmunem et al.'s26 introduced a bio-based phase change material (BPCM) derived from stearin of sheep tail-fats (SSTF) for sustainable building-envelope thermal energy storage, evaluated through numerical and experimental melting analyses in a rectangular cavity. The authors showed that natural convection dominates heat transfer during melting because SSTF has relatively low thermal conductivity, leading to non-uniform melting. Adding a small amount of carbon nanotubes (0.03%) slightly increases thermal conductivity and improves performance, accelerating melting by 11% and increasing melt velocity by 8.7%, while enhancing heat transfer and storage rates by 13.7% and 7.5%, respectively. Yousef et al.27 conducted numerical analyses of buildings made with hollow bricks, with and without PCM, for eight Mediterranean locations and for various melting temperatures and building types. Their findings indicate that PCM can lower cooling energy demand by as much as 56% in North-East Mediterranean climates (with an optimal melting point around 26 °C), but yields minimal benefits in the much hotter South-East Mediterranean regions.

In addition to pure passive thermal systems, Active PCM systems (also called latent heat–assisted active thermal storage) combine the latent buffering of PCMs with a supplementary heat source, such as electric heating elements, radiant floor circuits, or embedded heaters28–30. This approach has been explored in PCM-enhanced radiant/underfloor heating, where PCM integration increases thermal storage capacity and can shift or smooth heating demand while maintaining comfort. More recent concepts also integrate electrically charged PCM storage directly into building components (e.g., bricks/plates) to address heating needs in cold climates by actively storing heat during charging periods and releasing it later. In this context, the present work, which couples MPCM-integrated AAC bricks with an auxiliary heater plate, is best positioned as a hybrid active–passive system, where the PCM provides passive thermal regulation while the heater plate enables controlled thermal input when required31,32.

Moreover, to enhance the thermal performance of PCM-enhanced TSBs, the PCM is incorporated in micro- or nano-encapsulated form, which effectively prevents leakage during melting and solidification33–38. Encapsulated PCM bricks have become a prominent component of high-performance building envelopes in both residential and commercial sectors, particularly in climates with large day–night temperature swings and growing cooling loads39–42. Vicente43 experimentally examined masonry walls incorporating PCM macrocapsules and showed that these systems substantially damp indoor temperature oscillations and extend the time lag, confirming their effectiveness for passive temperature regulation and reduced energy use. Abdulhossein et al.44 embedded 5 and 10 PCM capsules into conventional bricks and evaluated the resulting wall performance; the configuration with 10 capsules decreased heat flux by 34%, increased stored thermal energy by 50%, and lowered the interior surface temperature by more than 3 °C relative to a reference wall. Maleki et al.45 formulated a plasterboard containing nano-encapsulated PCM produced via miniemulsion polymerization and comprehensively characterized its thermal behavior. Their findings indicate that the nano-PCM significantly enhances latent heat storage, mitigates indoor temperature swings, and improves thermal comfort compared with standard plasterboards. Hassan et al.46 numerically modeled clay hollow blocks and cement layers incorporating nano-encapsulated PCM using a detailed PDE-based framework that more accurately represents latent heat effects than simplified models. Their simulations reveal that increasing NEPCM content up to 10% delays the peak inner-surface temperature, narrows Tis to 23.3–26.6 °C, and reduces heat flux by 13%, alongside a maximum 16% reduction in Pd, highlighting the strong potential of nano-PCM systems for adaptive, energy-efficient envelopes. Nassr et al.47 used CFD together with a techno-economic analysis to assess bricks containing PCM capsules under varying boundary conditions. They reported that the encapsulated PCM performs most effectively for indoor temperatures of 20–24 °C and outdoor temperatures of 25–31 °C, delivering notable cooling-load reductions, while the economic study predicted long-term savings of $415k–$444k over a 30-year period and an internal rate of return well above the discount rate, making PCM-enhanced bricks a financially attractive green-building solution. Additionally, controlling energy consumption during peak heating and cooling periods is essential to mitigate grid overload and reduce operational costs. Peak demand typically occurs when outdoor temperatures are most extreme. To effectively manage these critical periods, in addition to using TSBs, other supplementary components such as heaters are required. Overall, to address the existing gaps in this field, this study, for the first time, investigates a hybrid active–passive PCM-based thermal management system enhanced with pentadecane as a PCM under cold and freezing climatic conditions. To manage peak energy demand during critical periods, a heater plate with varying power levels is integrated with the brick system. In addition, to enhance the thermal storage capability and ensure leakage prevention, the PCM is encapsulated within polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA) shells. A CFD framework based on the FVM is employed to examine the effects of micro-encapsulated PCM (MPCM) volume fraction and heater plate power on the thermal performance of buildings incorporating TSB components. The thermal behavior of the proposed system is evaluated using key indicators, including indoor surface temperature (Tis) and its temporal variation, indoor heat flux (qis), MPCM melting fraction, and daily power consumption (DPC). Additionally, by conducting a techno-economic analysis using NPV and IRR, we present the economic benefits of this hybrid active–passive thermal regulation structure.

Problem and configuration definition

One of the main challenges in heating and cooling buildings is maintaining and controlling indoor temperatures within an acceptable comfort range, particularly under cold and freezing climatic conditions. The reason for selecting a PCM with a melting temperature of 10 °C is that, although the indoor air temperature is maintained at 18 °C, the interior wall surface temperature in winter can be much lower due to the large indoor–outdoor temperature gradient (e.g., 18 °C indoors versus −15 °C outdoors) and may remain only a few degrees above 0 °C. Therefore, choosing an MCPCM with a melting range around 10 °C targets the actual wall/surface temperature window to reduce heat loss by buffering the wall temperature, rather than aiming to raise the indoor air temperature to 18 °C. For this reason, as shown in Figure 1, a passive heating configuration is proposed that combines MPCM-enhanced autoclaved aerated concrete (AAC) bricks with varying MPCM volume fractions (ϕ = 0, 4, 8, and 12%) and an auxiliary heater plate operating at different heat flux levels (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${q}_{hp}^{"}$$\end{document} = 0, 50, 100, and 150 W/m2) under climatic conditions characterized by Tmin = −15 °C and Tmax = 15 °C. The heater plate functions as an auxiliary element to regulate energy consumption during peak demand periods rather than serving as the primary heating source. The geometric dimensions of the system, length (L), width (W), and height (H), are presented in Fig. 1 and are 600 mm, 200 mm, and 150 mm, respectively. The core component of the passive thermal system is the AAC brick integrated with MPCM material. AAC bricks are lightweight, porous building materials whose properties are governed by their cellular microstructure. They exhibit compressive strengths of approximately 2–6 MPa and an elastic modulus of 1–3 GPa, while offering favorable thermal performance with low thermal conductivity in the range of 0.09–0.20 W/m.K. These combined characteristics make AAC bricks suitable for building applications where thermal insulation and energy efficiency are of primary importance. To enhance the thermal performance of AAC bricks, this study integrates pentadecane PCM, encapsulated within a PMMA shell, into the AAC matrix. Pentadecane is an organic phase change material widely used for thermal energy storage due to its suitable melting temperature near indoor comfort conditions. It offers high latent heat capacity and stable phase transition behavior, enabling effective attenuation of temperature fluctuations in building envelopes. In addition, pentadecane exhibits good thermal reliability and chemical stability over repeated melting–solidification cycles, making it suitable for long-term passive thermal regulation applications. Moreover, the PMMA shell serves as a protective encapsulation component that prevents leakage of the PCM during the melting–solidification process. It provides adequate mechanical integrity and chemical compatibility with cementitious matrices, ensuring stable incorporation within AAC bricks. In addition, PMMA offers good thermal stability and transparency, allowing reliable thermal cycling without degradation of the encapsulated PCM. Fundamental equations and assumptions

The accuracy of any CFD analysis strongly depends on the appropriate selection of governing equations and boundary conditions, as these define the physical processes represented in the numerical model. The chosen conservation laws, constitutive relations, and turbulence or radiation models must reflect the dominant transport mechanisms in the system; otherwise, important phenomena may be neglected or incorrectly captured. Likewise, boundary conditions specify how the computational domain exchanges mass, momentum, and energy with its surroundings, so unrealistic assumptions at inlets, outlets, or walls can significantly distort the predicted flow and thermal fields. In order to strengthen the reliability of the CFD simulations, this study introduces time-varying boundary conditions that more faithfully reproduce the dynamic heat exchange between the indoor and outdoor environments, an aspect that has frequently been simplified or omitted in earlier works. To construct a numerically efficient model while retaining the key thermal response of MPCM-improved AAC bricks, a number of simplifying but physically reasonable assumptions are adopted. The bulk thermophysical properties of the AAC and cementitious matrix, including density (ρ), thermal conductivity (k), and specific heat capacity (Cp), are taken as temperature-independent over the simulated range. This widely used approximation reduces computational effort and has been shown to introduce only minor errors in similar building-energy applications. The MPCM component is assumed to be homogeneously distributed within the AAC, so the composite is treated as an effective continuum rather than resolving its detailed microstructure. Volume changes associated with PCM melting and solidification are neglected because they are small and constrained by the capsule shell, and thermally induced deformation of the AAC is not considered, as mechanical effects are beyond the scope of this work. These assumptions are consistent with common practice in thermal energy-storage modeling and allow the transient heat-transfer problem to be written in terms of effective thermophysical properties for the MPCM-improved AAC. During the phase-change interval, the specific heat of the PCM core, Cp,c, no longer represents purely sensible heat storage; it also embeds the latent heat absorbed or released as the material melts or solidifies. This temperature-dependent behavior is essential for accurately capturing the thermal response of the MPCM, because the effective heat-storage capacity rises sharply near the melting temperature. In this study, Cp,c​ is therefore modeled as a temperature-dependent, or “apparent,” specific heat that combines the baseline sensible heat capacity with a distributed latent-heat term spread over the phase-change temperature range. This approach reproduces the progressive absorption and release of energy as the PCM transitions between solid and liquid states, rather than treating the transformation as an instantaneous event. Simulation approach, mesh study, and validation process

CFD offers a powerful and cost-effective approach for examining heat transfer in advanced building materials when experimental studies are constrained by cost, duration, or uncontrolled conditions. In this work, the thermal response of MPCM-enhanced AAC is analyzed using the conjugate heat transfer solver chtMultiRegion from foam-extend 4.1, a C++ finite-volume solver that enforces energy conservation across the heterogeneous solid domain and explicitly models PCM phase change. The transient temperature field is obtained by discretizing the energy equation over a control-volume mesh of the brick–heater assembly. Time integration uses a first-order explicit Euler scheme with a sufficiently small time step for stability, while the diffusive term is spatially discretized via a second-order central-difference scheme, balancing accuracy and computational cost. These numerical choices provide an efficient, robust framework for capturing time-dependent thermal storage and release in MPCM-based building components under realistic, time-varying conditions.

Mesh-independence verification is a basic quality check in CFD because the spatial discretization of the domain can strongly affect the predicted fields if the grid is too coarse. A properly designed grid study demonstrates that the main output quantities are governed by the underlying physics rather than by numerical artifacts associated with the cell size. This is typically done by refining the mesh in several steps and monitoring the evolution of target variables until further refinement produces only marginal changes, indicating that discretization errors have been reduced to an acceptable level. In the present work, mesh sensitivity was assessed by tracking the maximum indoor surface temperature (Max Tis) for the MPCM-improved AAC brick at ϕ = 12% and heater plate with heat flux of 150 W/m2 (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${q}_{hp}^{"}=150$$\end{document} W/m2). As shown in Fig. 2, five meshes containing approximately 60,000, 80,000, 100,000, 120,000, and 140,000 elements were analyzed. The results indicate that Max Tis becomes practically unchanged once the mesh size reaches about 120,000 elements, and any differences beyond this resolution are negligible. This grid level was therefore adopted for all subsequent simulations as a compromise between computational efficiency and numerical accuracy.Fig. 2Mesh convergence analysis based on the maximum Tis results.

Mesh convergence analysis based on the maximum Tis results.

Validation process is a critical step in CFD because it tests whether the numerical model can reproduce measured thermal behavior under comparable conditions, rather than merely solving the equations in a mathematically consistent way. By comparing simulation outputs with independent reference data, one can assess the suitability of the governing equations, thermophysical properties, and boundary conditions used in the analysis, and thus determine whether the predicted fields are physically credible. In this work, the validity of the numerical framework is examined by comparing the computed indoor heat flux (qis)​ at ϕ=10% and t=8h with the benchmark results reported by Hassan et al.46. The present configuration and the reference case share similar geometrical features and identical external thermal loads, providing a consistent basis for comparison. Although Hassan et al. employed a slightly different PCM-embedding strategy and considered somewhat higher ambient temperatures, the overall heat-transfer mechanisms and boundary interactions remain closely aligned, allowing the dataset to serve as a meaningful validation target. The comparison, performed for outdoor temperatures of 5, 10, 15, 20, and 25 °C, shows good agreement between the predicted and reference heat-flux trends (Fig. 3-a), thereby supporting the robustness and physical reliability of the proposed CFD model. In addition, to strengthen the credibility of our manuscript, we compared the indoor surface temperature predicted by our model with the experimental measurements reported by Thongtha et al.55, who investigated AAC bricks with PCM-contained conical holes. Based on Fig. 3-b, an acceptable agreement is observed.Fig. 3Model validation through comparison of the predicted (a) qis with Hassan et al.'s data as a function of ambient temperature and (b) inside temperature with Thongtha et al.'s work.

Economic assessment of using MPCM-enhanced AAC bricks combined with an auxiliary heater plate

A combined thermal, technical, and economic assessment of MCPCM-integrated thermally smart bricks with an auxiliary heater plate is necessary to evaluate not only how effectively they improve heat regulation, but also whether they are financially viable over the building’s service life. While these bricks can reduce energy consumption by stabilizing indoor temperatures and lowering heating and cooling demands, they typically require higher initial costs for fabrication and installation compared with conventional masonry. Incorporating time-value-of-money metrics, such as net present value (NPV) and internal rate of return (IRR), enables a transparent evaluation of whether the additional upfront investment is justified. This approach supports informed decision-making by balancing sustainability benefits against costs and by identifying the operating and design conditions under which these bricks deliver the strongest economic return. The techno-economic results show a clear dependence of profitability on both the heater-plate heat flux q’' and the MCPCM volume fraction (ϕ). For ϕ=4%, the system consistently delivers the strongest economic performance: NPV increases from $11,689 (q''=0) to $15,970 (q''=150 W/m2), and IRR rises from 13.55% to 17.45%, indicating that higher heater flux improves the lifetime savings enough to outweigh the associated costs. For ϕ=8%, the economics remain positive but weaker; NPV improves relative to q’'=0 (from $9,068 to $11,488 at q’'=100 W/m2) and then slightly drops at q’'=150 W/m2 ($11,046), while IRR stays in a narrow band (6.68–7.92%), suggesting diminishing returns at higher flux for this PCM loading.

The IRR for ϕ=12% is also low and relatively flat (3.97–4.27%) for q’'=0–100 W/m2, consistent with the NPV trend. Conclusion

This study examined the thermal and energy behavior of a building envelope that combines MPCM-enhanced AAC with an auxiliary heater plate, a configuration that has not previously been evaluated for cold and freezing climates. The results clearly indicate that the inclusion of MPCM fundamentally alters the thermal response of AAC bricks, particularly under moderate to high heating loads. For heater powers of 100–150 W/m2, MPCM volume fractions of 8–12% consistently moderated the indoor response by reducing peak Tis by approximately 1–2 °C and lowering peak qis by about 15–25% relative to the plain AAC brick. At the same time, minimum temperature and heat-flux levels were elevated and peak occurrence was delayed by roughly one hour, reflecting enhanced thermal inertia and more effective redistribution of heat over time. Lower MPCM content (φ = 4%) was found to be less effective under strong heating, as rapid PCM saturation led to sharper thermal fluctuations rather than smoothing. Analysis of the MF% further clarified these trends. As heater power increased, the system transitioned from short-duration, partial melting to extended phase-change plateaus, with melting fractions approaching 50–52% for several hours at the highest heater setting. This extended melting–solidification cycle explains the observed peak shaving and prolonged heat release at higher MPCM contents. From an energy perspective, MPCM integration yielded substantial reductions in DPC. At zero heater power, DPC decreased from approximately 0.26 kWh/m2 for plain AAC to about 0.19–0.20 kWh/m2, corresponding to savings of 25–30%. Under active heating at 100–150 W/m2, DPC was reduced from roughly 0.32–0.35 kWh/m2 to 0.24–0.28 kWh/m2, representing a 20–25% reduction. These findings demonstrate that the proposed AAC–MPCM–heater system offers a viable strategy for reducing peak loads and improving energy efficiency in cold-region buildings, with optimal performance achieved at MPCM volume fractions in the range of 8–12%. Additionally, the techno-economic analysis over 40 years shows that ϕ=4% provides the most favorable economics, achieving the highest NPV (up to $15,970) and IRR (up to 17.45%), with performance generally improving as the auxiliary heater flux increases. In contrast, higher MCPCM loadings (ϕ=8% and especially ϕ=12%) yield lower NPVs and IRRs, indicating diminishing economic returns compared with the 4% formulation under the investigated operating conditions.
