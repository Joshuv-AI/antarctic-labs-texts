Cerebrospinal fluid and plasma pharmacokinetics of ceftazidime/avibactam in a neurocritical patient with CRKP intracranial infection and augmented renal clearance
Authors: Si-Min Huang, Qi-Hua Chen, Wei Wang, Yi-Ming Wang, Yi-Wen Wei, Qiang Qu, Hai-Nan Zhang, Jian Qu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIntracranial infections caused by carbapenem-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae (CRKP) pose significant therapeutic challenges, primarily due to the limited penetration of antimicrobial agents across the blood-brain barrier. Ceftazidime/avibactam demonstrates efficacy against multidrug-resistant Gram-negative pathogens. However, its CSF pharmacokinetics and optimal dosing in patients with augmented renal clearance (ARC; CrCl > 130 mL/min/1.73 m²) remain inadequately characterized.MethodsWe utilized validated high-performance liquid chromatography to quantify ceftazidime/avibactam concentrations in paired plasma and CSF samples obtained from a traumatic brain injury patient with CRKP ventriculitis and ARC (CrCl 154.37 mL/min/1.73 m²). The MIC of ceftazidime/avibactam was determined using broth microdilution in accordance with Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute guidelines.ResultsStandard Ceftazidime/avibactam dosing (2.5 g q8h) resulted in subtherapeutic trough concentrations in both plasma (ceftazidime/avibactam: 10.39/0.96 µg/mL) and CSF (ceftazidime/avibactam: 20.4/0.68 µg/mL) against the target pathogen (MIC = 4 mg/L). Dose intensification to 2.5 g q6h administered via 3-h prolonged infusion achieved supra-therapeutic exposures (troughs: plasma 65.60/8.70 µg/mL; CSF 58.90/10.82 µg/mL; CSF/plasma ratio = 0.9 for ceftazidime), which correlated with CSF sterilization and resolution of inflammatory markers.ConclusionThis first-in-human pharmacokinetic evidence in a patient with ARC underscores the inadequacy of conventional ceftazidime/avibactam regimens in this population. Our findings advocate for therapeutic drug monitoring-guided dose optimization incorporating extended infusions to achieve therapeutic targets. These results emphasize the critical need for personalized dosing algorithms in the neurocritical care setting.

Introduction

Post-neurosurgical CNS infections represent life-threatening complications associated with neurosurgical procedures or underlying intracranial pathologies.1 Intracranial infections caused by carbapenem-resistant Enterobacteriaceae (CRE) pose a particular challenge, requiring the selection of antibiotic regimens that overcome antimicrobial resistance while achieving sufficient concentrations at the site of infection. Ceftazidime/avibactam, a novel β-lactamase inhibitor, has demonstrated potent activity against CRE.2 Nevertheless, clinical data regarding CSF penetration and pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic (PK/PD) targets of ceftazidime/avibactam in CNS infections remain limited. This article reports a case of CRKP intracranial infection in a patient with augmented renal clearance (ARC) who received a ceftazidime/avibactam-based regimen. Therapeutic drug monitoring (TDM) was employed to measure steady-state concentrations of ceftazidime/avibactam in plasma and CSF, with subsequent dosage adjustments based on concentration results leading to favorable clinical outcomes. This case provides valuable insights for the individualized treatment with ceftazidime/avibactam in patients with intracranial infection and concomitant ARC.

Methods

Under the standard dosing regimen (2.5 g q8h, 2-h infusion, total daily dose 7.5 g), steady-state drug concentrations were achieved on the third day of administration. Blood and CSF samples were collected 30 min prior to the next dose to determine trough concentrations. Following dose adjustment (2.5 g q6h, 3-h infusion, total daily dose 10 g), the sampling protocol was refined to include samples collected 30 min pre-dose (trough) and 5 min post-completion of the intravenous infusion (peak). Peak concentration monitoring was implemented primarily to mitigate the risk of adverse reactions associated with excessively high drug levels.

Blood and CSF samples were collected and promptly transported to the central laboratory for centrifugation and serum separation. Aliquots were stored in transport tubes at −80°C until analysis. Serum and CSF concentrations of ceftazidime/avibactam were quantified using validated HPLC assays, as previously described.3,4

Bacterial identification, antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST), and renal function assessment (serum creatinine, estimated glomerular filtration rate) were performed according to institutional protocols in the hospital’s clinical laboratories. Susceptibilities testing was confirmed using the VITEKVR2 system (bioMérieux, Marcy-l’Étoile, France). The MICs of ceftazidime/avibactam for clinical isolates were determined using the broth microdilution method with a fixed avibactam concentration of 4 mg/L in combination with 2-fold dilutions of ceftazidime, following CLSI guidelines.

Case presentation

A 48-year-old male with a history of traumatic brain injury (TBI) following a motor vehicle accident 2 months prior was transferred to our institution on December 13, 2023, presenting with prolonged coma, recurrent fever (peak 39.2°C), and worsening respiratory status. Before admission, the patient underwent right frontal-temporal subdural hematoma evacuation, partial resection of right frontal-temporal lobe contusion, cerebrospinal fluid leak repair, intracranial pressure monitoring probe placement, and decompressive craniectomy at an external hospital.

On admission, the patient exhibited high-grade fever (maximum 39.2°C) and evidence of severe pulmonary infection. Initial antimicrobial therapy consisted of linezolid and amikacin, based on previous pathogen data. On December 15, t metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS) of bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) detected Acinetobacter baumannii (sequence count: 67097), Klebsiella pneumoniae (sequence count: 29), and Pseudomonas aeruginosa (sequence count: 155). Consequently, amikacin was discontinued, and regimens were changed to cefoperazone-sulbactam 2 g q8h + intravenous polymyxin B 75 mg q12h + polymyxin B 25 mg q12h via inhalation. On December 18, the patient’s fever persisted (peak 38.8°C). CSF analysis revealed elevated white blood cells with neutrophil predominance, positive Pandy’s test, and decreased glucose and chloride levels, highly suggestive of intracranial infection. Concurrent blood NGS indicated Klebsiella pneumoniae (sequence count 215). The antimicrobial regimen was subsequently modified to ceftazidime/avibactam 2.5 g q8h combined with aztreonam to provide coverage against metallo-β-lactamase-producing Klebsiella pneumoniae. On December 19, CSF NGS confirmed Klebsiella pneumoniae (sequence count: 473), prompting the addition of intrathecal polymyxin B 5 mg daily. By December 31, the patient continued to experience high-grade fever with persistent CSF abnormalities. CSF culture grew carbapenem-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae (CRKP) with the following susceptibility profile: polymyxin B (MIC = 1 mg/L), ceftazidime/avibactam (MIC = 4/4 mg/L), and tigecycline (MIC = 4 mg/L). TDM revealed subtherapeutic plasma and CSF concentrations of ceftazidime/avibactam (trough: 10.39/0.96 µg/mL in plasma, 20.4/0.68 µg/mL in CSF). The patient’s serum creatinine was 51 μmol/L, via Cockcroft-Gault equation, with a calculated creatinine clearance (Cockcroft-Gault equation) of 154.37 mL/(min·1.73m²), consistent with ARC. It is noteworthy that in critically ill patients, estimation formulas may lack reliability, and timed urine collection is often recommended for accurate ARC assessment.

Based on these findings, the ceftazidime/avibactam regimen was intensified to 2.5 g q6h administered as a 3-h prolonged infusion, while continuing polymyxin B (intravenous and inhaled) and intrathecal polymyxin B; aztreonam was discontinued. Following this dose adjustment, the patient’s general condition improved, though he remained comatose. Notably, the peak body temperature decreased, and CSF biochemical parameters showed marked improvement [Figure 1 and Table S1 (available as Supplementary data at JAC-AMR Online)].

Repeat TDM on January 4, 2024, demonstrated substantially increased drug exposures: plasma ceftazidime/avibactam concentrations were 65.60/8.70 µg/mL (trough) and 145.51/20.32 µg/mL (peak), while CSF concentrations reached 58.90/10.82 µg/mL for ceftazidime/avibactam, exceeding the pathogen MIC. A follow-up head CT on January 9, 2024, revealed reduced ventricular dilatation, decreased right subdural hematoma, and resolution of previously noted hemorrhagic foci (Figure S1). Throughout treatment, the patient remained comatose but exhibited no signs of neurotoxicity or other adverse drug reactions. By January 21, the patient remained afebrile for 1 week (temperature 37.0°C) and was subsequently transferred to another facility per family request for continued care.

Discussion

Ceftazidime/avibactam, a β-lactam/β-lactamase inhibitor combination, exhibits potent activity against multidrug-resistant Gram-negative pathogens, including extended-spectrum ESBL-producing organisms, AmpC β-lactamase-expressing strains, KPC, OXA-48-type carbapenemase-positive Enterobacteriaceae, and resistant Pseudomonas aeruginosa isolates.2,5,6 Preclinical studies demonstrate that its in vitro antimicrobial efficacy translates effectively to in vivo infection models.7–10 Notably, experimental meningitis studies in rabbits revealed 38% CSF penetration for both ceftazidime and avibactam, achieving a remarkable 5.66 log10 cfu/mL reduction in CSF bacterial burden within 8 h of administration.7 Predictive PK/PD modeling based on MICs of clinical isolates has validated these therapeutic outcomes in murine models.8–10 Emerging clinical evidence supports the therapeutic potential of ceftazidime/avibactam for CNS infections caused by multidrug-resistant pathogens, though human CSF pharmacokinetic data remain limited.11

Despite extensive characterization of ceftazidime/avibactam’s plasma pharmacokinetics, its CSF penetration dynamics in patients with intracranial infections are not fully elucidated. A recent pharmacometric study reported CSF concentrations exceeding 20 × MIC within 60 min post-infusion during CRKP meningitis treatment.12 Research by Udy et al. highlight that ARC, defined as a CrCl exceeding 130 mL/min/1.73 m², accelerates renal clearance of antibiotics in critically ill patients, potentially leading to subtherapeutic blood concentrations of β-lactam agents.

Both ceftazidime and avibactam are predominantly renally excreted (55%–95% and 80%–90%, respectively), rendering their pharmacokinetics highly susceptible to ARC-driven hyperfiltration.13 In critically ill patients with ARC, enhanced renal elimination reduces drug exposure, potentially compromising the time-dependent bactericidal activity of ceftazidime (T > MIC) and the enzyme-inhibitory efficacy of avibactam.13,14 Peggy S et al. found that, microbiological and clinical cure rates were suboptimal in patients receiving ceftazidime for the treatment of serious infections if the AUC was <250 or T > MIC was <100%.15 Studies demonstrate that standard ceftazidime/avibactam dosing (2.5 g q8h) frequently fails to achieve therapeutic targets in ARC patients. For instance, Our previous case series reported subtherapeutic plasma trough concentrations (0.797 mg/L) in an ARC patient (CrCl = 295.49 mL/min), resulting in T > MIC <100% for CRKP with MIC = 2/4 mg/L.14 Similarly, the present case revealed inadequate trough CSF concentrations of ceftazidime/avibactam at 0.68–0.96 µg/mL under standard dosing (MIC = 4/4 mg/L), necessitating dose intensification to 2.5 g q6h with prolonged infusions to restore therapeutic exposures. These observations align with population PK models identifying CrCl as a key covariate for ceftazidime/avibactam clearance, supporting dose escalation in ARC populations.16

We present novel pharmacokinetic data from a neurocritical patient with CRKP intracranial Infection with augmented renal clearance (ARC; CrCl 154.37 mL/min), where standard ceftazidime/avibactam dosing (2.5 g q8h) failed to achieve therapeutic targets (plasma/CSF troughs: 10.39/0.96 µg/mL and 20.4/0.68 µg/mL versus MIC = 4/4 mg/L, avibactam concentration is insufficient). Our prior pharmacokinetic analyses demonstrate significant interpatient variability in ceftazidime/avibactam steady-state concentrations across renal function strata (ARC versus normal renal function versus renal insufficiency),14 with compelling evidence supporting dose escalation in ARC populations.14

Therapeutic optimization through intensified dosing (2.5 g q6h) with prolonged 3-h infusions achieved supra-therapeutic exposures14(plasma/CSF troughs: 65.60/8.70 µg/mL and 58.90/10.82 µg/mL) exceeding the CRKP MIC by >15-fold. Pharmacokinetic analysis revealed exceptional BBB penetration for ceftazidime following dose adjustment (CSF/plasma ratio: 0.9).17 Both peak and trough concentrations of ceftazidime/avibactam in blood and CSF substantially exceeded the MIC, suggesting maintenance of 100% T > MIC throughout the dosing interval. The observed 90% BBB penetration may be attributable to accelerated systemic drug clearance via ARC coupled with relatively slower CSF drug elimination. However, it is important to note that calculating BBB permeability based solely on single time-point concentration ratios may not be fully representative; comprehensive concentration-time curve analysis is preferable for accurate assessment. ARC necessitates aggressive dosing strategies, such as increased frequency (q6h) or extended infusions (3–4 h), to counteract rapid systemic drug elimination and maintain T > MIC >100%. Even with enhanced dosing, ARC may potentially impair CSF penetration secondary to reduced systemic exposure, as observed in our patient where CSF/plasma ratios improved from approximately 0.07 to 0.9 only after dose optimization.

The patient received combination therapy including polymyxin B alongside ceftazidime/avibactam. While polymyxin B undoubtedly contributed to the treatment outcome, the patient’s clinical symptoms and CSF parameters showed limited improvement prior to ceftazidime/avibactam dose intensification. This indirectly suggests that achieving adequate therapeutic concentrations of ceftazidime/avibactam was pivotal for CSF culture sterilization. These findings underscore the critical importance of ARC-adapted dosing strategies in the management of CNS infections.

Conclusion

In conclusion, ARC presents significant challenges to ceftazidime/avibactam efficacy by reducing systemic drug exposure and compromising PK/PD target attainment. For this high-risk population, proactive strategies, including dose intensification, prolonged infusions, and TDM, should be employed to optimize treatment outcomes. While evidence from individual case reports has inherent limitations, our findings highlight the necessity for further clinical studies to guide the individualized use of ceftazidime/avibactam in specific patient populations, particularly those with ARC and CNS infections.
