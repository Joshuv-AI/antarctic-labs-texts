Synthesis and anti-antileishmanial profile of novel thiazolidine-4-one derivatives and molecular docking and molecular dynamics simulations studies
Authors: Soheila Molaei, Jafar Abbasi Shiran, Arina Esmailzadeh, Soroush Rajabi, Mohammad Reza Rahimi, Yasamin Abedini Zal, Saghi Sepehri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Leishmaniasis is a severely neglected intracellular protozoan disease caused by infection with parasites of the Leishmania genus, affecting over 98 countries globally. Presently, the infection threatens the health of more than 350 million people globally, resulting in an estimated 60,000 deaths yearly from both visceral and cutaneous forms of the parasite. In present research, a novel series of thiazolidine-4-one analogues (7a-g) was synthesized and evaluated against Leishmania major. In vitro assays demonstrated using Leishmania major promastigotes and amastigotes, while cytotoxicity was assessed on the J774.A1 macrophage cell line. All tested compounds exhibited inhibitory effects against promastigotes, with compounds 7a (IC50 = 34.97 μM), 7c (IC50 = 40.79 μM), and 7b (IC50 = 62.59 μM) showing the highest potency. Against the amastigote form, analogues 7a, 7b, and 7d demonstrated notable efficacy, with IC50 values of 354.53 μM, 665.72 μM, and 746.59 μM, respectively. Flow cytometry analysis revealed that these compounds induce multiple stages of apoptosis, including both early and late apoptosis, as well as necrosis, in promastigotes. Furthermore, some derivatives exhibited moderate cytotoxic effects on macrophages. Compounds 7a and 7b demonstrated the highest selectivity indices (SI) of 6.23 and 3.22, respectively. Overall, the tested compounds showed stronger anti-promastigote activity compared to their anti-amastigote effects. In silico analyses indicated that the thiazolidine-4-one derivatives possess satisfactory drug-likeness characteristics and identified probable metabolic sites via major cytochrome P450 enzymes. Finally, molecular docking and molecular dynamics simulations were performed at the active site of Leishmania major pteridine reductase 1 (LmPTR1). They revealed that the most active compounds, 7a and 7c, adopt favorable binding orientations and maintain stable interactions with key residues of LmPTR1, correlating with their enhanced in vitro activity. Additionally, RMSD, RMSF, hydrogen bonds, Rg, SASA, PCA, and energy analysis during molecular dynamics simulations indicated stable binding of two compounds with LmPTR1. Furthermore, the results from docking and molecular dynamics simulations demonstrated that hydrophobic interactions were critical in the binding of these compounds to LmPTR1.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-53762-3.

Introduction

Neglected tropical diseases (NTDs) involve a different class of infectious diseases caused by protozoa, helminths, viruses, bacteria, fungi, and other pathogens. These diseases are mainly endemic to tropical and subtropical regions, often overlapping with regions of widespread poverty; however, instances of NTDs have also been reported in non-endemic and developed regions. These diseases affect more than 1.5 billion people worldwide and are responsible for over 530,000 deaths every year. In response to the significant health problem of NTDs, research efforts have intensified, concentrating on the development of effective monitoring systems and the improvement of existing treatment strategies to enhance control over their transmission1. NTDs remain prevalent and continue to present a considerable threat to global health and human well-being; however, the rate of drug development to address these conditions remains significantly slow2. Leishmaniasis is an infectious disease classified as an NTD, caused by Leishmania protozoans and spread to humans by infected sandfly bites. Approximately 21 Leishmania species have been identified as pathogenic to humans, with an endemic distribution across Africa, Asia, the Americas, and Southern Europe. The parasite exhibits a biphasic life cycle, alternating between the extracellular, mobile promastigote stage—found in the gut of female sandflies of the Lutzomyia genus (in the Americas) or Phlebotomus genus (in Asia and Africa)—and the intracellular, non-mobile amastigote stage, which replicates inside the phagolysosomal compartments of the host’s macrophages3. It is estimated that more than 6 million people worldwide are infected with leishmania parasites. Up to 30% of these cases may eventually develop severe complications, including cardiac, gastrointestinal, neurological, and other potentially life-threatening manifestations, often appearing years after the initial infection4,5. Three primary clinical appearances of leishmaniasis have been identified in humans: mucocutaneous leishmaniasis (MCL), cutaneous leishmaniasis (CL), and visceral leishmaniasis (VL), also referred to as kala-azar. Among these, VL represents the most serious form and is generally fatal if left untreated. The most prevalent form is CL, which causes skin lesions or ulcers that often lead to permanent scarring or, in some cases, the more disfiguring mucocutaneous form. More than one million new cases of CL are estimated to occur each year. Leishmania major (L. major) is a primary pathogen responsible for CL in North Africa, the Middle East, and Asia6. The effective organization of this disease is difficult due to several factors, including inconsistent and insufficient clinical diagnosis, limited therapeutic options, and the emergence of drug resistance. Thus, the application of new diagnostic tools and identification techniques is crucial to understanding leishmaniasis pathogenesis, eventually facilitating the advancement of more accurate diagnostics and successful treatments6. Moreover, the lack of effective vaccines, challenges in vector control, and the emergence of drug resistance have contributed to the increasing occurrence of leishmaniasis. The most widely employed treatments include pentavalent antimonials, amphotericin B, paromomycin, and pentamidine; however, these drugs are associated with significant side effects, require prolonged administration at high doses, and are typically given parenterally. Developing a safe, effective, and affordable drug would significantly improve the management of leishmaniasis and reduce problems related to current chemotherapy routines7.

Nitrogen-containing compounds, including thiazolidinones, account for approximately 20% of all compounds with antiparasitic activity. Thiazolidinone, five-membered heterocyclic ring, is biologically significant and includes a sulfur atom at position 1, a nitrogen atom at position 3, and a carbonyl group at one of the positions—2, 4, or 58. These compounds exhibit considerable pharmacological potential and involve a wide range of drug candidates with diverse activities, including antibiotic, antitumor, anti-inflammatory, antiviral, antimicrobial, antifungal, and antidiabetic effects9. Recently, thiazolidin-2-ones have been investigated as inhibitors of the BRD4 bromodomain, while thiazolidin-5-ones find applications in dye chemistry. The compound 2-thioxo-thiazolidin-4-one, commonly known as rhodanine, serves as a key scaffold in numerous drug-like molecules. Thiazolidine-2,4-dione acts as a potent hypoglycemic agent and is a core component of rosiglitazone, a clinically approved drug for treating type II diabetes. The thiazolidin-4-one moiety, often referred to as a “magic moiety” or “wonder core,” is recognized for its broad spectrum of biological activities, including antimicrobial, antitubercular, antiviral, anti-inflammatory, and antidiabetic effects8,10. Recent studies have primarily focused on investigating the antiparasitic activities of thiazolidinone compounds11.

Synthetic chemistry continually demands the development of more efficient and environmentally friendly methods that maximize mass efficiency, minimize intermediate isolation steps, reduce energy consumption, and decreases waste production. Showing organic reactions in a single vessel (one-pot) represents an effective strategy for high yield and they are favored in green chemistry metrics. One-pot synthesis can be classified into three types: domino or cascade reactions, multicomponent reactions (MCRs), and one-pot stepwise synthesis (OPSS). OPSS involves multiple sequential steps in which reactants, reagents, and catalysts are introduced stepwise, allowing changes in reaction conditions at each stage. Examples of OPSS include solid-phase peptide synthesis, organic synthesis employing solid-supported reagents and scavengers, polymer synthesis, and the preparation of certain biomolecules. Numerous biocatalysis-based OPSS methods have also been documented in the literature12.

Advanced computational approaches are integral to structure-based drug discovery, enabling the efficient identification of bioactive compounds. Structure-based virtual screening and molecular docking are widely used to predict binding modes within the active site of biological targets. To address the limitations of static docking, molecular dynamics (MD) simulations is employed to account for protein–ligand flexibility and complex stability. MM-PBSA/MM-GBSA binding free energy calculations provides quantitative estimates of ligand-target affinities. Additionally, QSAR modeling and in silico ADMET predictions are applied to correlate molecular features with biological activity and to evaluate pharmacokinetic and toxicity profiles13. In the present study, these complementary computational methods were integrated to support compound prioritization and experimental validation.

In this study, a series of derivatives based on the thiazolidine-4-one scaffold were synthesized using a one-pot, two-step, three-component method. These compounds were evaluated against both L. major amastigote and promastigote forms, as well as their cytotoxic effects on macrophage cells. Furthermore, in silico analyses were shown to provide insights into the compounds’ stability and potential for further investigation.

Materials and methods

Synthesis

All chemicals were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (USA) and used as received, without any additional purification. FT-IR spectra for the compounds were recorded using a Perkin-Elmer spectrometer. The 1H- and 13C-NMR spectra (in DMSO-d6) were acquired on a Bruker Ultrashield instrument operating at 400 and 100 MHz, respectively. Elemental analysis for carbon, hydrogen, and nitrogen was carried out with a Heraeus CHN-O-Rapid analyzer.

General procedure for the synthesis of (E)-3-(((E)-N-benzylidene)amino)-2/4-substituted-thiazolidin-4-one (7a-g)

A mixture of the corresponding isothiocyanate 1 (1 mmol) and hydrazine derivative 2 (1 mmol) was stirred at room temperature in absolute ethanol (15 mL) and triethylamine (Et3N), leading to the formation of the intermediate N-alkylhydrazinecarbothioamide 3. Subsequently, substituted benzaldehyde 4 (1 mmol) was added to the reaction mixture along with acetic acid and sodium sulfate (Na2SO4) to yield intermediate 5. In the next step, 2-chloroacetyl chloride 6 and Et3N were added, and the mixture was refluxed for 8–12 h (progress was monitored by TLC). Following completion, the reaction mixture was cooled to room temperature and worked up in an ice bath. The resulting precipitates were filtered and recrystallized from a 1:1 (v/v) ethanol/water mixture to afford the pure target compounds (7a-g). Anti-leishmanial activity

Herein, variety of standardized reagents and culture media were employed to evaluate the compounds’ activity against L. major. L. major promastigote (MRHO/IR/75/ER) was initially cultured in Novy-MacNeal-Nicolle (NNN) medium, which consists of Brain Heart Infusion Blood Agar and Luria Broth (Merck, Germany). Following cultivation was done in Roswell Park Memorial Institute medium (RPMI-1640), enriched with fetal bovine serum (FBS) and a combination of penicillin (100 U/mL) and streptomycin (100 µg/mL) (Gibco, Germany) to promote growth and prevent microbial contamination14. All reagents were stored according to the manufacturers’ recommended conditions to ensure their stability and reliability throughout the experiments. The synthesized thiazolidine-4-one derivatives (7a-g) were first dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO; Sigma-Aldrich) to preparate stock solutions with a concentration of 5 mg/mL. To minimize the risk of DMSO-related cytotoxicity, the final concentration of DMSO in all biological assays was strictly maintained below 1%, a threshold previously shown to be non-toxic to leishmania parasites14,15. Serial dilutions of the compounds were prepared in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), resulting in final concentrations ranging from 4.7 to 1000 µg/mL. These dilutions were used to cultures of macrophages, as well as to the amastigote and promastigote forms of the parasite15. To evaluate the cytotoxic and apoptotic effects of the tested compounds, cell viability was assessed by the MTT assay (Sigma-Aldrich). Apoptosis was performed with a FITC Annexin V Apoptosis Detection Kit (Mab Tag, Germany). Amphotericin B (AmpB; Cipla, 50 mg) and meglumine antimoniate (Glucantime®; Sanofi, 1.5 g/5 mL ampoules) were used as reference drugs for assays involving the promastigote and amastigote forms, respectively16.

Cultivation of L. major

The standard Iranian strain of L. major (MRHO/IR/75/ER) was kindly provided by Dr. Ghaffarifar from the Department of Parasitology, Faculty of Medical Sciences, Tarbiat Modares University, Tehran, Iran. Promastigote forms were initially cultured in NNN medium and then sub-cultured in RPMI-1640 supplemented with 20% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% Penicillin–Streptomycin solution, containing streptomycin. Cultures were maintained at 18–24 °C under standard laboratory conditions to support optimal parasite growth17.

Anti-leishmanial activity

Anti-promastigote effects

MTT assay for promastigote viability

The MTT reduction assay, which relies on the conversion of 2-imino-thiazolidin-4-ones into formazan crystals, was used to evaluate the viability of L. major promastigotes. For the assay, 100 μL of RPMI-1640 medium containing L. major promastigotes at a density of 1 × 106 cells/mL in the logarithmic growth phase was added to each well of a 96-well ELISA plate. The parasites were then treated with different concentrations of the synthesized compounds (4.7–1000 μg/mL). Subsequent steps—including incubation and formazan crystal solubilization—were carried out following established protocols14,18. Following incubation, cell viability was evaluated spectrophotometrically by measuring the absorbance of the formazan product. The percentage of viable promastigotes was determined according to the following equation:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{Viability}}\left( \% \right) = {1}00 \times \left[ {\left( {{\mathrm{Abs}}\_{\mathrm{treated}} - {\mathrm{Abs}}\_{\mathrm{blank}}} \right)/\left( {{\mathrm{Abs}}\_{\mathrm{control}} - {\mathrm{Abs}}\_{\mathrm{blank}}} \right)} \right]$$\end{document}

Abs_treated shows the absorbance measured from compound-treated cells, Abs_control represents absorbance from untreated cells (negative control), and Abs_blank corresponds to wells containing only the culture medium without cells. Live promastigotes suspended in PBS without drug treatment served as the control group, while cell-free wells were used as the blank (CTRL-). AmB at 1 µg/mL was included as the positive control. The IC50 values were determined as the concentrations of each compound that inhibited promastigote viability by 50% in comparison to untreated controls14,15.

Anti-intracellular amastigote activity

To evaluate the compounds’ anti-leishmanial activity against intracellular L. major amastigotes, J774A.1 murine macrophages were seeded at a density of 1 × 105 cells per well in 12-well plates containing sterile round glass coverslips. The plates were incubated at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2 for 24 h to allow macrophage adhesion. Non-adherent cells were then gently removed by washing with sterile PBS. Metacyclic promastigotes (1 × 106 parasites per well, corresponding to a 10:1 parasite-to-macrophage ratio) were added to the adherent macrophages and incubated for 24 h to start infection. Following this, to remove any non-internalized parasites, wells were washed with PBS. Infected cultures were then treated with numerous concentrations of the compounds (4.7 to 1000 µg/mL) and incubated for an additional 72 h under standard conditions (37 °C, 5% CO2, 95% humidity). After incubation, cells were fixed with methanol and discolored with Giemsa. The number of intracellular amastigotes (Leishman bodies) per 100 macrophages was counted under a light microscope. IC50 values were determined by comparing the reduction in amastigote numbers in treated samples relative to untreated controls. All experiments were performed in triplicate to ensure reproducibility. Glucantime® (GLU) at 100 µg/mL was used as the positive control14,15.

Flow cytometry assay for apoptosis detection

The induction of apoptosis in L. major promastigotes after treatment with selected compounds was assessed by the Annexin V-FITC Apoptosis Detection Kit (Mab Tag, Germany). Promastigotes in the logarithmic growth phase were treated with the IC50 concentrations of the most potent compounds. After 24 h incubation, the cells were collected, washed twice with cold sterile PBS, and centrifuged at 1000 × g for 15 min. The cell pellets were gently resuspended in 400 μL of binding buffer, and 5 μL of Annexin V-FITC, along with 5 μL of propidium iodide (PI), were subsequently added. The samples were incubated in the dark at room temperature (25 °C) for 15 min to ensure proper staining. Apoptotic and necrotic cell populations were then analyzed using a CyFlow Space flow cytometer (Sysmex-Partec, USA), with data processed by FlowJo™ software (version 10.5.3, Vancouver, BC). Experiments were performed in triplicate, and the apoptotic and necrotic promastigotes’ percentages were calculated for each compound tested14,18.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were carried out in triplicate, and results were reported as mean ± SD. The IC50 values for Leishmania promastigotes and intracellular amastigotes, as well as the CC50 values for J774A.1 macrophages, were calculated based on the average percentage viability of the respective cell populations. Data analysis, IC50 and CC50 calculations, and graphical presentations were accomplished by GraphPad Prism software (GraphPad Software Inc., San Diego, CA). Changes in promastigote viability over time were analyzed using repeated measures ANOVA in the parasite counting assay. To compare IC50 and CC50 values across different compounds, one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test was used to identify statistically significant differences, with P-values below 0.05 considered significant. The selectivity index (SI) for each compound was calculated as the ratio of CC50 to the IC50 against amastigotes, providing an indicator of compound selectivity and potential therapeutic window in animal models.

Computational studies

In silico pharmacokinetic prediction and physicochemical properties

The pharmacokinetic prediction and drug-likeness properties of the compounds were evaluated using the ADMETLab 3.0 online platform (https://admetlab3.scbdd.com). The assessed parameters included absorption (GI absorption, P-gp substrate), distribution (blood–brain barrier permeability), metabolism (CYP450 enzyme inhibition), carcinogenicity, drug-induced nephrotoxicity, drug-induced neurotoxicity and drug-likeness based on Lipinski’s rule of five and other physicochemical descriptors. All predictions were performed using the default settings of ADMETLab 3.0. The platform was accessed in Jul. 202419.

In silico metabolism of compounds in the presence of cytochrome P450

The potential sites of metabolism for the derivatives were systematically predicted using the SMARTCyp 3.0 online server (https://smartcyp.sund.ku.dk/mol_to_som), which estimates likely cytochrome P450-mediated metabolic hot spots based on molecular structure. The analysis specifically focused on the CYP3A4, CYP2D6, and CYP2C9 isoforms, which are major contributors to drug metabolism in human. Predictions were performed using the default settings of SMARTCyp 3.0. The server was accessed in Sep. 201920.

Molecular docking study

The interaction of the derivatives with the active site of L. major pteridine reductase 1 (LmPTR1) was investigated through molecular docking using AutoDock 4.221,22. The three-dimensional structure of LmPTR1 (PDB ID: 5L42) was downloaded from the Protein Data Bank (PDB). The docking procedure followed the methodology reported in an earlier study23,24. After removing crystallized water molecules and ligands, Kollman charges and polar hydrogens were added to the enzyme structure. The derivatives’ structures were prepared and energy-minimized using the MM + method in HyperChem 8.0 software. Gasteiger charges were then assigned to the compounds. Finally, the pdbqt files for both the enzyme and the compounds, required for docking, were generated using AutoDock Tools. Docking calculations were performed using the Lamarckian Genetic Algorithm implemented in AutoDock 4.2. For each ligand, multiple docking runs were carried out, and the resulting binding poses were clustered based on an RMSD tolerance of 2.0 Å. RMSD values were calculated relative to the lowest-energy conformation. The most populated cluster with the lowest binding energy was selected as the representative binding mode for further analysis.

Molecular dynamics simulations study

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations was carried out using GROMACS version 2022.6, following the protocol described in a previous study25,26. Topology files and force field parameters for the selected compounds were generated using the Acpype tool. Type of box was used cubic and the TIP3P water model was applied to solvate the system, incorporating approximately 11,864 water molecules. To maintain overall charge neutrality, counter-ions were added by replacing water molecules in the simulation box, which contained LmPTR1, the ligand, and water. The Amber99sb force field was applied to describe all bonded and nonbonded interactions. Energy minimization of the protein–ligand complex was performed using steepest descent followed by conjugate gradient algorithms. Following minimization, position restraints were applied during equilibration with NVT and NPT ensembles. During the NVT simulation, the temperature was maintained at 300 K using a coupling constant of 0.1 ps for 500 ps. Once the temperature stabilized, the NPT ensemble was conducted at a constant pressure of 1 bar using the Parrinello-Rahman barostat, with a coupling constant of 5.0 ps, over 1000 ps. The Particle Mesh Ewald method handled long-range electrostatics, the LINCS algorithm maintained covalent bond constraints, and the Lennard–Jones potential was used for van der Waals interactions. Lastly, 150 ns MD simulations was carried out, with equilibration monitored by assessing the stability of energy, system density, temperature, and backbone atom root-mean-square deviations (RMSDs).

Results and discussions

Rationale and design

Thiazolidinones are carbonyl-containing derivatives of thiazolidine, the saturated counterpart of thiazole. Structurally, they consist of a five-membered, non-aromatic heterocyclic ring featuring both sulfur and nitrogen atoms. Compounds with a thiazolidinone core display a wide spectrum of pharmacological effects. Due to the vital role of thiazolidinones in the mechanism of many therapeutic agents, they have gained significant attention from medicinal chemists as a valuable scaffold for designing novel drug candidates. Various thiazolidinone derivatives have demonstrated bioactivities like antibacterial, antifungal, anticancer, antioxidant, antimalarial, and antitubercular effects27. Numerous studies have investigated the anti-leishmanial properties of thiazolidinones. For example, Haroon et al. evaluated these compounds against Leishmania amazonensis and Leishmania infantum. Based on a structure–activity relationship (SAR) analysis of thiazolidin-4-one, the presence of a C5-ketone group in combination with a C4-ethyl acetate moiety significantly enhanced leishmanicidal activity compared to miltefosine (Compound 1; Fig. 1)28. Bhat et al. synthesized some of the quinoline-thiazolidin-4-one hybrids and assessed their inhibitory effects against L. donovani. Among them, Compound 2 exhibited notable anti-leishmanial activity. Additionally, cytotoxicity assessment using the MTT assay on mouse embryonic fibroblast cells indicated that compound 2 had low toxicity (Fig. 1)29. Schadich et al. evaluated a series of thiazolidine-based hybrids against L. major (Compound 3; Fig. 1). The most potent candidates demonstrated low cytotoxicity toward normal human fibroblasts and exhibited a favorable therapeutic index. SAR analysis suggested that the lack of substitution at position 3 of the thiazolidinone ring, along with the lack of bulky groups at the five-membered ring positions, contributed to the enhanced anti-leishmanial activity11. Neri et al. synthesized thiazolidine-2,4-dione derivatives with potential anti-leishmanial activity. Among them, the most active derivative against L. infantum (Compound 4; Fig. 1) had a halogen substituent at the 3-position of the phenyl ring. However, introducing halogens at the 2- or 6-positions resulted in reduced activity. A major limitation of this series was their cytotoxicity, although compound 4 demonstrated a SI greater than one. Additionally, compound 5 (Fig. 1) showed strong binding affinity to LmPTR1. Further analysis revealed that small hydrophobic substituents attached to the meta/para positions are essential for activity against LmPTR1. Whereas lipophilic groups at the ortho-position negatively influenced the compound’s efficacy against L. infantum30. Leite et al. utilized the thiazolidinone ring as a bioisosteric substitute for the pteridine/purine core in the design of PTR1 inhibitors. Among the thiazolidine-2,4-dione derivatives investigated, compound 6 (Fig. 1) was introduced as the most promising lead candidate31. Filho et al. applied the concept of bioisosterism to transform a potent antiparasitic aryl thiosemicarbazone, known for its cruzain-inhibitory activity, into thiazolidine-4-one derivatives. Evaluation of antiparasitic activity and cytotoxicity of compound 7 (Fig. 1) against Trypanosoma cruzi Y-strain trypomastigotes and J774 macrophages showed high potency and selectivity. Notably, a phenyl group at the N3 position of the heterocyclic ring of the compound 7 highlighted this substituent as a key structural determinant. Overall, the study demonstrated that converting thiosemicarbazones into thiazolidine-4-ones preserves antiparasitic efficacy while improving selectivity32.Fig. 1Design plan of thiazolidinone analogues (7a-g) against the leishmanial parasite.

Design plan of thiazolidinone analogues (7a-g) against the leishmanial parasite.

According to previous studies, in this study, a series of novel thiazolidine-4-one derivatives featuring electron-withdrawing groups (EWG), electron-donating groups (EDG), and heteroaromatic rings connected to the NH position of the thiazolidine-4-one scaffold, along with various substitutions at the para/meta positions of the benzylidene ring was synthesized. Evaluating drug safety plays an essential role in developing novel therapeutic compounds, as it determines whether a compound can deliver its intended effects without harming host (mammalian) cells. Structure–activity relationship (SAR) studies

The thiazolidine-4-one derivatives (7a-g) were tested against both the L. major promastigote and amastigote forms. The IC50S of compounds were measured and compared to those of GLU and AmB (Table 1). Among them, compounds 7a-c exhibited the strongest anti-leishmanial activity (Table 1). A closer look at these three compounds shows that they share the same benzylidene moiety but differ in the substituents attached to the N-thiazolidine-4-one ring. Structurally, compound 7b demonstrated weaker anti-promastigote activity compared to 7a and 7c. This may be attributed to the presence of a nitramide group (-NH-NO2) in 7b, which is a strong EWG. This group can reduce the molecule’s electronic stability and hinder its interaction with biological targets. Additionally, the high polarity of the nitro group may limit its ability to cross membranes, and it could also be chemically unstable or metabolically reactive. Compound 7c showed moderate anti-promastigote activity. It contains an imino group (C=N) linked to a pyridine ring, which can be protonated at physiological pH, reducing cellular uptake. The relatively large size and polarity of this structure may further restrict cell penetration or interact with intracellular targets. In contrast, compound 7a features a smaller, less polar methylimino group (= N–CH3), which can increase lipophilicity and membrane permeability, facilitating more efficient cell entry. The absence of acidic or basic groups at this position (due to the methyl group) also may improve chemical stability under physiological conditions, resulting in superior anti-promastigote activity. Based on these observations, compound 7a appears to be more potent than 7b and 7c. In summary, differences in anti-promastigote activity among these compounds can be explained by: (I) Lipophilicity and size—compound 7a’s smaller, more lipophilic methylimino group promotes better membrane penetration; (II) Polarity and stability—compound 7b’s highly polar nitro group may lead to rapid degradation or metabolism; and (III) Electronic characteristics—compound 7c’s pyridine moiety may reduce bioavailability due to protonation and limited target interaction. Regarding activity against the amastigote form, the efficacy ranking is 7a > 7b > 7c. The relatively higher anti-amastigote activity of compound 7b compared to 7c might be due to the nitro group enhancing aqueous solubility or optimizing the compound’s lipophilic-hydrophilic balance, which facilitates better penetration and intracellular accumulation in amastigotes. Conversely, the aromatic and bulkier pyridinyloimino group in 7c may reduce solubility and hinder membrane permeation. Since the promastigote and amastigote forms differ in membrane composition and metabolic pathways, compounds that can efficiently penetrate promastigotes or selectively interact with their enzymes and biological targets tend to show greater effectiveness against this stage36–38. In compound 7c, the pyridinyloimino group may enhance lipophilicity or improve membrane permeability in promastigotes compared to compound 7b, which could facilitate better access to biological targets. On the other hand, the nitro group in compound 7b might reduce permeability in promastigotes or be subject to metabolic inactivation by the parasite.

When comparing the anti-leishmanial activity of compounds 7a, 7e, and 7f, all of which share the thiazolidine-4-one core but differ in their aromatic ring substituents (benzylidene moiety). Compound 7a was the most potent compound. It features two methoxy groups at the meta- and para-positions of the benzylidene ring and showed stronger activity than 7e and 7f (Table 1). Methoxy groups are strong EDGs, which can raise electron density on the benzylidene ring and might enhance interactions with biological targets. Additionally, improving lipophilicity helps the compound penetrate membranes more effectively. This combination of electron donation and increased hydrophobicity likely contributes to its superior biological activity. Compound 7f, with a bromine atom at the para-benzylidene position, showed moderate anti-promastigote activity. Bromine is a moderate EWG by induction but can act slightly as an electron donor through resonance. It can increase lipophilicity, aiding membrane permeability; however, its electron-withdrawing effect might weaken electronic interactions with target molecules. Compound 7e, which contains both methoxy and hydroxyl groups at the meta- and para-positions, was less active against promastigotes compared to 7a and 7f. The hydroxyl group is polar and hydrophilic, which can reduce membrane permeability. As a result, compound 7e is overall more polar and less lipophilic. Moreover, the presence of hydroxy and methoxy groups may form intramolecular hydrogen bonding, potentially causing conformational changes that affect activity.

In summary, the nature and position of substituents on the aromatic benzylidene ring play a crucial role in determining the anti-leishmanial potency of these compounds11,28–32. EDGs such as methoxy significantly improve activity by enhancing both membrane penetration and interactions with molecular targets. In contrast, polar groups such as the hydroxyl group tend to reduce membrane permeability and may interfere with optimal binding, resulting in decreased activity. Interestingly, compound 7e showed higher anti-amastigote activity compared to 7f, which contrasts with the trend observed in promastigotes, where 7f was more active than 7e. This difference might be clarified by the fact that 7e’s hydroxyl and methoxy groups can form stronger hydrogen bonds with biological targets inside amastigotes, aiding penetration and binding to intracellular proteins and enzymes, thus improving its effectiveness. Meanwhile, 7f contains a bromine atom, which is hydrophobic and relatively large, properties that may enhance its membrane penetration or specific interactions in promastigotes. Overall, since amastigote and promastigote forms differ in membrane composition and metabolism, compounds may vary in effectiveness depending on these differences. Additionally, promastigotes may be less sensitive to polar groups or metabolize such compounds faster, while amastigotes could retain and absorb them more efficiently. Comparing compounds 7b, 7d, and 7g, which share the thiazolidin-4-one core but differ in substituents on the benzylidene ring. Compound 7d showed moderate activity. The bromine atom at the para-position in 7d is a moderate EWG by induction and a slight electron donor by resonance, increasing lipophilicity but not as much as methoxy groups. Compound 7b, with two methoxy groups at meta- and para-positions, enhances electron density on the ring, promotes π-π stacking and hydrogen bonding with targets, and improves membrane permeability, making it the most potent among the three. On the other hand, 7g, bearing a hydroxyl group, is highly polar and hydrophilic, which reduces lipophilicity and membrane penetration. The hydroxyl group can also form hydrogen bonds that may interfere with target binding or compound stability. Overall, EDGs and lipophilic moieties, such as a methoxy group, might facilitate membrane penetration and target interaction, while polar groups, such as hydroxyl, may reduce bioavailability and activity. Though all these compounds contain nitro groups, their effects on activity are minor and consistent, with most differences attributed to the aromatic substituents. The anti-leishmanial activities of these compounds are consistent across both promastigote and amastigote stages. Looking at compounds 7d and 7f, both have a common benzylidene group but differ in substituents on the thiazolidin-4-one core. Compound 7d exhibited stronger anti-promastigote and anti-amastigote effects than 7f. This is likely because 7d contains a nitroimino group, a strong EWG that increases the electrophilicity of the active site, enhancing interactions with parasitic enzymes. This group can improve cellular uptake and may disrupt critical parasite enzymes. In contrast, 7f’s methylimino group is an EDG and may not interact as effectively with biological targets; it could also negatively impact compound stability or distribution. Both compounds possess a para-bromine substituent on the benzylidene ring, which enhances lipophilicity and facilitates membrane penetration; however, since this feature is common to both, it does not account for the observed differences in their biological activity. Again, their activities against both parasite forms follow a consistent pattern. From evaluating all tested compounds against both L. major amastigote and promastigote forms, several SARs emerged: (I) Most compounds showed similar activity patterns against both forms, suggesting a shared mechanism or target. (II) Strong EWGs like nitroimino groups or halogenated rings enhance activity, likely by increasing electrophilicity and strengthening interactions with parasite biomolecules. (III) The thiazolidin-4-one ring is a key scaffold to activity, probably through hydrogen bonding or π-π stacking with biological targets. (IV) Side chain differences greatly influence activity; bulky or highly polar groups tend to reduce permeability, especially in intracellular amastigotes. (V) Moderate lipophilicity correlates with better activity, notably against promastigotes, as overly lipophilic compounds may have solubility issues, while very hydrophilic ones struggle to penetrate membranes. (VI) Nitroimino-containing compounds outperform methylimino-substituted ones, highlighting the importance of EWGs for improving cellular uptake and target engagement.

Molecular docking study

Computational methods such as molecular docking serve as powerful tools for exploring interactions between enzymes and small molecules. They also play a crucial role in recognizing key pharmacophoric features, thereby guiding the design of potential therapeutically active compounds39. This section aims to provide a rationale for the observed anti-leishmanial activity by focusing on a specific enzymatic target within the parasite. The enzyme pteridine reductase 1 (LmPTR1) from L. major was investigated as a possible target. PTR1, an NADPH-dependent short-chain oxidoreductase, is crucial for the pterin salvage pathway in the parasite40. Leishmania parasites uniquely possess the ability to salvage pterins from the host, a capability absent in the host itself due to a lack of PTR1 activity and the formation of pterin analogues derived from GTP. Consequently, the PTR1 gene represents a significant therapeutic target for drug development21. This enzyme catalyzes the conversion of biopterin into its reduced forms, H2-biopterin and H4-biopterin, while also participating in the degradation of folate derivatives such as 7,8-dihydrofolate and tetrahydrofolate. Disruption of the PTR1 gene leads to the death of promastigotes during the insect stage of the parasite’s life cycle. However, this fatal effect can be partially reversed by supplementation with reduced pterins—but not folates—highlighting the essential role of H4-biopterin. Current studies propose that H4-biopterin plays a regulatory role in parasite differentiation. PTR1-deficient mutants exhibit significantly reduced levels of H4-biopterin, which triggers premature differentiation into the highly infectious metacyclic promastigote form within the sand fly vector41.

PTR1 shows a much broader range of activity, catalyzing successive reductions of conjugated (folate) and unconjugated (biopterin) pterins. Under physiological conditions, PTR1 is responsible of reduction of 10% of the folic acid required by the cell, but when classical antifolate drugs inhibit.

dihydrofolate reductase-thymidylate synthase (DHFR-TS), PTR1 can be overexpressed, allowing for a significant reduction of the necessary amounts of folates to ensure parasite survival. This compensatory mechanism suggests that treatment of trypanosomatid infections could be achieved through inhibition of both DHFR and PTR1 by a single drug or a combination of molecules that are specific inhibitors of the two targets. This evidence supports the importance of PTR1 for parasitic survival and indicates that it is a promising drug target for the treatment of Leishmania42. Nare, Luba, and Beverly reported that upon DHFR-TS inhibition, PTR1 provides enough folate to guarantee the parasite survival43. Furthermore, this enzyme is not present in mammalian hosts rendering it a promising target for drug development31. In some studies, the PTR1 inhibitory activity of thiazolidines and thiazolidinones was investigated. Linciano et al. reported the synthesis of 2-amino-1,3,4-thiadiazoles and evaluated their anti-leishmanial activity. Also, the most potent TbPTR1 inhibitors were assayed44. Leite et al. showed that thiazolidine-2,4-dione derivatives are micromolar inhibitors of LmPTR1 that display competitive behavior with the enzyme cofactor (NADPH)31. Neri et al. synthesized a series of thiazolidine-2,4-dione derivatives and evaluated against LmPTR130. Given the critical role of the PTR1 enzyme in the life cycle of the Leishmania parasite, as well as the history of previous studies that have shown the modulatory activity of thiazolidinone compounds against related targets, in this study, we investigated the stability and molecular interaction patterns of thiazolidinone derivatives with this key enzyme in leishmania.

The catalytic site of PTR1 comprises a triad or tetrad of essential amino acid residues: tyrosine, asparagine, serine, and lysine residues. Among these, aspartate (such as ASP181) is noted to be particularly stable, similar to serine. ARG17 interacts with the pyrophosphate group of the cofactor, which contributes to the stabilization of the cofactor-enzyme complex. Moreover, PHE113 is crucial for ligand stabilization in the active site through hydrophobic interactions that anchor the substrate or inhibitor45,46. The presence of cofactor NADPH is essential for the binding of inhibitors or substrates to PTR147. PHE113 engages in hydrophobic interactions with the nicotinamide ring of NADPH, playing a key role in facilitating substrate recognition and catalytic activity. High conservation of the catalytic amino acids TYR191, ASP181, LYS198, and TYR194 indicates a consistent catalytic mechanism of PTR1 across multiple leishmania species22,45,46,48.

Molecular dynamics simulations study

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations was carried out to assess the stability and dynamic flexibility of the docking complexes over time in a water-based system. This strategy enables the improvement of docking results by accounting for the dynamic behavior of both the compound and the protein, and for a more accurate evaluation of ligand binding affinities. The MD simulations supported the docking outcomes, confirming the involvement of specific amino acids in key hydrogen and hydrophobic bonding interactions. Compounds 7a and 7c bound to LmPTR1, along with the unbound (apo) form of LmPTR1, were selected for simulation. Each system underwent 150 ns MD simulations. The obtained trajectories were subjected to further analysis using several parameters54.

Principal component analysis (PCA) study

Principal Component Analysis (PCA) is a widely used multivariate statistical technique for investigating the collective motions of biomolecular systems, particularly by capturing correlated fluctuations of Cα atoms during MD simulations. By reducing the dimensionality of the trajectory data, PCA allows the identification of dominant, large-scale motions that govern the overall conformational behavior of proteins and protein–ligand complexes69. In the present study, PCA was applied to the MD trajectories of the 7a-LmPRT1 and 7c-LmPRT1 complexes, as well as the apo form of LmPRT1, to evaluate their conformational sampling and dynamic stability throughout the simulation period. The eigenvalue spectrum revealed that the first few principal components (PCs) accounted for the majority of the total variance in the system, indicating that the essential dynamics of the protein can be effectively described by a limited number of collective motions. This observation suggests that the conformational space explored during the simulations is governed primarily by a small set of dominant structural fluctuations.

Comparative analysis of these projections demonstrates that the 7c-LmPRT1 complex undergoes substantially larger conformational rearrangements, reflecting enhanced structural flexibility. In contrast, the 7a-LmPRT1 complex exhibits the smallest amplitude of motion, indicative of a more rigid and conformationally stable protein–ligand assembly. The Ramachandran plot of complexes

The Ramachandran plot serves as a visual tool to map the φ (phi) and ψ (psi) dihedral angles of amino acid residues in the enzyme’s conformational framework. These angles describe how the amino acids in a polypeptide chain rotate around the bonds connecting them. The plot helps identify allowed and disallowed regions for these angles based on steric hindrance and other structural constraints.

Assessing the stereochemical quality of an enzyme’s structure relies heavily on the Ramachandran plot. The absence or scarcity of points in certain regions signifies conformations that are energetically prohibited due to steric clashes or unfavorable torsional strain. Densely populated areas correspond to energetically favorable conformations of the enzyme backbone. The plot is divided into three main regions: (I) Most preferred area: This region includes the most energetically favorable combinations of ψ (psi) and φ (phi) angles, typically associated with common secondary structures like beta sheets and alpha helices. (II) Acceptable area: Torsion angles in this area are energetically allowed but occur less commonly than those within the preferred conformational zone. (III) Outlier area: This area characterizes energetically unfavorable angle combinations. Points here may indicate potential structural problems, including steric hindrance or distorted bond angles and bond lengths70,71. The Ramachandran plot displayed different shaded regions on the ψ-ϕ plot for the complexes (Fig. 22). Conclusion

In this study, a novel one-pot, two-step, three-component approach was developed to synthesize a series of thiazolidin-4-one analogues with anti-leishmanial activity. Biological evaluation identified compounds 7a and 7c as the most promising derivatives, showing strong activity against L. major and low cytotoxicity toward host macrophages, while 7a and 7b were particularly effective against the amastigote stage. SAR analysis highlighted the importance of electronic effects and appropriate N-substitution in enhancing anti-leishmanial potency. Apoptosis assays, together with in silico ADME predictions, supported a targeted mechanism of action and favorable drug-likeness. Molecular docking and molecular dynamics simulations further confirmed stable interactions of the lead compounds with Lm PTR1. Overall, these findings introduce thiazolidin-4-one derivatives as novel lead scaffolds for anti-leishmanial drug development. Future work will focus on structural optimization, in vivo validation, and mechanistic confirmation to advance these compounds toward therapeutic application.
