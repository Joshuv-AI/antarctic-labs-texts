Makkalai Thedi Maruthuvam: evaluating equity in non-communicable disease care through a doorstep primary health program in Tamil Nadu, India
Authors: Sudharshini Subramaniam, Anavarathan Somasundaram, Cowshik E, Judson Neslin J, Ponnien Selvan KN, Jannath Hameeda Banu, Getrude Banumathi, Suganthi Selvarajan, Sabitha Devi Chandrasekaran, Sharon Cynthia, MTM study group
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNon-Communicable Diseases (NCDs) disproportionately affect marginalized populations, often due to delayed diagnosis and poor continuity of care. “Makkalai Thedi Maruthuvam” (MTM), a state-led initiative in Tamil Nadu, India, delivers home-based screening and care services for NCDs, aiming to overcome access barriers and promote equity. This study evaluates the program’s performance in delivering NCD care across key steps of the care cascade.MethodsA cross-sectional household survey was conducted across all 38 districts in Tamil Nadu, covering 6,856 adults aged ≥ 30 years through multi-stage cluster sampling. Data on screening, diagnosis, treatment, and control of diabetes, hypertension, and cancers were collected using handheld digital tools. Descriptive and inferential statistics were used to assess equity across gender, location, caste, and socioeconomic status.ResultsScreening coverage for diabetes and hypertension was 79.4% and 82.6%, respectively, with over two-thirds of services delivered by MTM field staff. While over 95% of diagnosed patients were on treatment, control rates remained low (9.8% for diabetes; 35.4% for hypertension). Cancer screening coverage was substantially lower, with notable disparities across caste and economic groups. Rural and SC/ST communities had greater program reach, but urban areas and male populations lagged. Approximately 30% of patients shifted from private to public care within one year.ConclusionMTM has significantly improved access to NCD care among marginalized populations through a community-based implementation model. However, challenges remain in ensuring equitable cancer screening, improving urban outreach, and achieving disease control. The study underscores the importance of community health workers and doorstep strategies in advancing health equity through primary care systems.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12875-025-03064-w.Keywords: Non Communicable Diseases, Health equity, Primary healthcare, Doorstep health services, Makkalai Thedi Maruthuvam, Community based healthcare

Introduction

Non-Communicable Diseases (NCDs) such as diabetes mellitus, hypertension, and certain cancers are among the leading causes of morbidity and mortality worldwide. These chronic conditions place a substantial burden on individuals, families, and healthcare systems, particularly in low- and middle-income countries like India. Early detection of NCDs through population-based screening programs has been shown to improve clinical outcomes by enabling timely intervention, reducing disease progression, and preventing complications [1, 2]. Moreover, screening programs facilitate risk identification and allow for the implementation of lifestyle modifications and preventive healthcare strategies.

In India, the burden of NCDs is increasing at an alarming rate, with significant urban–rural and socio-economic disparities in disease detection, treatment, and control. Tamil Nadu, a southern Indian state with relatively strong public health indicators, has initiated several progressive strategies to combat NCDs. In 2021, the Government of Tamil Nadu launched Makkalai Thedi Maruthuvam (MTM), meaning “Healthcare at the Doorstep,” a flagship doorstep health service program. This initiative aims to bring essential NCD-related healthcare services—particularly screening, treatment initiation, and follow-up—directly to beneficiaries’ homes, thereby reducing barriers to access and ensuring continuity of care [1].

The MTM program consists of multiple components designed to deliver doorstep NCD care. Services are provided by a tiered workforce comprising Women Health Volunteers (WHVs), Village Health Nurses (VHNs), Health Inspectors, and Mid-Level Health Providers (MLHPs). The package includes screening for diabetes, hypertension, oral cancer (all adults ≥ 18 years), and cervical and breast cancer (women ≥ 30 years), in addition to medication refills, counseling, physiotherapy, dialysis follow-up, and palliative care. Services are coordinated through Primary Health Centres (PHCs) with referral linkages to higher facilities. Doorstep medication delivery and counseling are emphasized to reduce access barriers, especially for immobile and socioeconomically disadvantaged groups [2].

The MTM program integrates community and institutional-level health services through a workforce comprising Women Health Volunteers (WHVs), Village Health Nurses (VHNs), Health Inspectors, and Mid-Level Health Providers. Services offered include screening for diabetes and hypertension in adults over 18 years, screening for oral cancer in adults, and screening for cervical and breast cancer in women over 30 years. The initiative also includes medication delivery, counseling, and monitoring of selected chronic conditions, especially among vulnerable populations such as the elderly, immobile, or those with end-stage renal disease. These services are coordinated through Primary Health Centres (PHCs) and linked to higher levels of care when needed[1, 2].

Despite the large-scale implementation of MTM across all districts of Tamil Nadu, limited evidence exists on how effectively the program has delivered across the various stages of the NCD care cascade. The care cascade framework involves sequential steps: population-level screening, diagnosis, initiation of treatment, and control of disease parameters. Evaluating this cascade is essential to assess real-world effectiveness, identify drop-offs, and guide strategic improvements in public health interventions [3–5].

Previous studies on NCDs in India, including the National Family Health Survey (NFHS-5)[6] and the Tamil Nadu STEPS Survey [7, 8], have reported prevalence estimates and service coverage patterns. However, few have specifically assessed the continuity of care in an integrated doorstep screening model like MTM. Furthermore, there is a need to understand how this care model performs across different demographic and socio-economic subgroups. Generating such evidence is vital for policymakers aiming to replicate or scale similar community-based healthcare strategies in other settings. The MTM program thus represents a scalable model for advancing health equity through community-based primary care and doorstep service delivery, aligning with global implementation science efforts to reduce access barriers in underserved populations.

In this context, the present study was conducted to evaluate the reach and effectiveness of the NCD services provided under the MTM program in Tamil Nadu. Specifically, the study aims to assess the proportion of the eligible population screened for diabetes and hypertension, the proportion receiving treatment, and the extent of disease control among those diagnosed. Additionally, the study explores screening coverage for cervical and breast cancer among women and examines disparities in service coverage across gender, caste, locality, and economic strata.

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

A state-wide, community-based cross-sectional survey was conducted across all 38 districts of Tamil Nadu to assess the care cascade for selected non-communicable diseases (NCDs) under the Makkalai Thedi Maruthuvam (MTM) program. The survey targeted adults aged ≥ 30 years who had resided at the selected household address for at least six months. The choice of the age threshold was based on the operational definition adopted for program evaluation, considering that the risk and prevalence of NCDs such as diabetes and hypertension increase significantly after the third decade of life. Individuals who did not provide informed consent were excluded.

Sample size calculation

The required sample size was calculated to estimate state-level indicators with 95% confidence and a 5% margin of error (MoE). A design effect of 1.5 was applied to account for clustering in the multistage sampling approach. Using the formula n = [Z2 x p (1-p)/d2] x DEFF, where Z = 1.96 (for 95% CI), p = 0.5 (conservative prevalence), d = 0.05 (MoE), and DEFF = 1.5. The expected baseline level for the key indicators was conservatively estimated at 50%, maximizing sample size. This yielded a sample size of 5760 households, further inflated by 20% to account for non-response. Stratification ensured adequate representation across urban/rural and caste/economic groups.

The total estimated sample was 5,760 households, stratified to ensure adequate representation across caste, gender, and urban–rural categories (eight strata). This household-based design was chosen for operational feasibility and to capture all eligible adults within each selected unit.

Sampling strategy

A multi-stage cluster sampling approach was employed.(Fig. 1) Clusters were defined as villages in rural areas and streets in urban areas, with each cluster comprising 30 households.

In total, 6,120 households were approached to achieve the final sample of 5,760 households. Of these, 244 households were locked during visits and 116 households declined participation. Locked households were revisited once; if unavailable, the subsequent household was included as replacement. This yielded a household-level non-response rate of 6.0%.

Data collection and quality control

Data collection was conducted during February–March 2023. Most districts were supported by government medical colleges, with data collection teams led by Community Medicine faculty designated as nodal officers. Each field team consisted of two trained medical interns (one male, one female), fluent in Tamil. Teams were trained in mapping, household listing, digital data entry, and interview procedures. Daily debriefing and random spot verification were undertaken by supervisors to ensure data quality. Refresher training and data checks were conducted as needed.

Each field team covered approximately 8–10 households per day. Supervisors conducted random spot checks of at least 10% of households surveyed in each district. Daily data uploads were reviewed centrally, and inconsistencies triggered immediate feedback to teams for correction.

A semi-structured, interviewer-administered questionnaire was used, originally developed for this study in Tamil and later translated into English. The tool was pre-tested using Epicollect5 mobile software. Data were collected using Android-based handheld devices and uploaded in real-time.

Data analysis

Data were exported from Epicollect5 and analyzed using SPSS software (version 16). Missing data were minimal (< 5% for most variables). Responses with missing outcome variables (e.g., treatment or control status) were excluded from respective analyses. Descriptive statistics were presented as frequencies, percentages, means, and standard deviations. Chi-square tests were used to evaluate associations between categorical variables. Given the multi-stage sampling design, analysis accounted for stratification and clustering where appropriate.

Informed consent was obtained from all individual participants included in the study.

Results

A total of 6,856 individuals from the selected 5,760 households across all 38 districts of Tamil Nadu participated in the study. The mean age of respondents was 51.9 years (SD ± 13.9), with the largest age group being 30–40 years (26.7%). Females comprised 62.4% of the sample, and the majority (60.6%) were residents of rural areas. Most participants identified as Hindu (88.8%) and belonged to the Backward Class (35.2%), although 24.8% chose not to disclose their caste (Table 1).

Regarding the diabetes care cascade, 79.5% of participants reported having ever been screened for diabetes, and among these, 73.9% had been screened within the past year. Of those screened, 67.6% received screening under the MTM program—primarily through field workers (69.6%) rather than MTM-associated health institutions. Approximately 20.7% of all participants had been diagnosed with diabetes mellitus, with 64.8% of recent diagnoses attributed to MTM efforts. Among those with a diagnosis, 97.3% reported being on treatment, and over half (54.5%) received their medications through MTM. Despite high treatment rates, only 9.8% of diabetics met the criteria for good glycemic control. Approximately 29.3% of diabetic patients reported a shift from private to public sector services within the past year. While MTM services may have contributed to this transition, other contextual factors such as increased availability of medications in government facilities or changing economic circumstances could also have influenced this shift. (Table 2).

Hypertension prevalence was similar, with 22.0% of participants reporting a diagnosis. Among these, 19.5% had been diagnosed in the previous year, and 75.4% of the newly diagnosed cases were identified via the MTM program. Among newly diagnosed hypertensive patients, 75.4% reported being diagnosed under the MTM program. As this was self-reported, attribution to MTM should be interpreted with caution and considered as a major but not exclusive source of diagnosis. Treatment coverage for hypertension was also high (96.2%), and 62.7% of hypertensives received treatment through MTM services. Blood pressure control was achieved in 35.4% of hypertensive individuals, a figure notably higher than glycemic control among diabetics. Additionally, 26.4% of hypertensive patients had transitioned from private to public healthcare providers in the previous year (Table 2).

Screening for cancers was less prevalent. Among women aged 30 years and above, 11.1% reported ever undergoing cervical cancer screening, while 14.2% had been screened for breast cancer. Oral cancer screening was reported by only 3.9% of all participants. Among individuals eligible for other home-based services, 64.3% of those receiving palliative care, 66.6% of those undergoing physiotherapy, and 83.7% of those on dialysis reported receiving MTM-facilitated services, with a considerable proportion of these delivered through field-level staff (Table 2).

Stratified analysis revealed notable disparities in diabetes care (Table 3). A slightly higher proportion of women (80.8%) reported having been screened compared to men (77.2%) (80.8% vs. 77.2%; p < 0.001), though no significant gender differences were observed in treatment or glycemic control. Rural residents had higher screening rates compared to their urban counterparts (80.5% vs. 77.9%; p = 0.01), whereas treatment coverage was marginally higher among urban residents (98.5% vs. 96.3%). Glycemic control was low across all strata but slightly higher in urban areas (11.2% vs. 8.5%).

Discussion

This study evaluated the performance of the “Makkalai Thedi Maruthuvam” (MTM) scheme in achieving universal screening and treatment for non-communicable diseases (NCDs) in Tamil Nadu. Launched in 2021, the scheme has shown notable progress in expanding the coverage of screening services, especially for diabetes and hypertension. In our study, four out of five participants had been screened at least once for diabetes, and nearly three-fourths of them had undergone screening in the past year. Among those screened, two-thirds were evaluated through the MTM scheme, with the majority of these screenings conducted by field functionaries, underscoring the program’s emphasis on doorstep care delivery. Approximately 21% of the study participants had been diagnosed with diabetes, with 16.6% of those cases being identified within the previous year. Notably, about two-thirds of the newly diagnosed diabetics were identified through MTM outreach services.

The observed diabetes prevalence in our study (20.7%) aligns closely with NFHS-5 data [6], which reported diabetes prevalence at 22.1% among men and 20.7% among women in Tamil Nadu. While subgroup analyses were not the primary focus of our study, demographic patterns were comparable to findings from the NFHS-5[6] and the Tamilnadu STEPS survey [7, 8], which estimated diabetes prevalence at 17.6% for Tamil Nadu. Furthermore, the finding that 80.8% of women and 80.5% of rural residents had undergone diabetes screening reflects successful reach among these groups. Similar gender trends were reported by Negi et al.[9] in Kerala (2022), where 86.2% of women and 78.3% of men had been screened in the previous year. The present study also highlights significant variability in screening, treatment, and glycemic control across caste, education, and socioeconomic strata, consistent with Maiti et al.[10] (2023), who demonstrated that awareness and management of diabetes differed based on wealth, education, and social status.

Although 97.3% of diagnosed diabetics in our study were on treatment, glycemic control was achieved in only 9.8% of them. The STEPS survey [7, 8] reported a similar control rate (10.8%) but noted significantly lower treatment coverage (around 50%). Maiti et al.[10] reported an even lower control rate (7%) and treatment rate (21.5%). These findings suggest that while MTM has been effective in initiating treatment, further efforts are needed to ensure therapeutic adherence and glycemic control, particularly through follow-up and lifestyle counseling. Disparities in treatment uptake and control were influenced by education, religion, and caste, reinforcing the need for customized interventions that address structural determinants of health.

In terms of hypertension, the study found that over 80% of participants had been screened, and nearly 21% had a history of hypertension, with one-fifth of those diagnosed in the previous year. Among newly diagnosed cases, three-fourths were identified through MTM. Compared to national datasets, our findings align with NFHS-5[6], which reported hypertension prevalence in Tamil Nadu as 24.8% among women and 30.2% among men, and the STEPS survey [7, 8] which documented an overall prevalence of 33.9% (39.5% in males and 28.9% in females). Control of hypertension was achieved in 35.4% of our participants, which, while low, is significantly better than diabetes control, indicating a more favorable outcome for hypertension management under MTM. This trend mirrors Ross et al.[11] (2020) findings, who reported hypertension prevalence of 27.3% among women and 27.6% among men. These observations are in line with Nambiar et al.[12] (2020), who reported moderate levels of blood sugar (53.1%) and blood pressure (61.2%) screening in New Delhi, underscoring the relative effectiveness of the MTM model in achieving wider screening coverage in a shorter span.

The study also examined cancer screening services under MTM. While 3.8% of participants had ever undergone screening for oral cancer, 11.1% and 14.2% of women had ever been screened for cervical and breast cancers, respectively. These figures are significantly higher than NFHS-5[6] estimates for breast (5.6%) and cervical cancer (9.8%) screening in Tamil Nadu. The STEPS survey [7, 8] also reported lower figures for oral cancer screening (2%) and modest values for cervical (15.6%) and breast (17.6%) cancers. Notably, screening uptake for breast cancer was higher among Scheduled Caste women, those from upper economic strata, and those with higher education, indicating potential inequities that require targeted outreach efforts. Oral cancer screening remains the weakest link and calls for intensified awareness campaigns and follow-up.

Other services under MTM, such as palliative care (64.2% covered), physiotherapy (66.5%), and dialysis (83.7%) were primarily delivered through field staff, which reflects the scheme’s operational strength. A significant proportion of participants (29.3% for diabetes and 26.4% for hypertension) had shifted from private to government care in the last year, highlighting a promising trend towards public health trust and reliance. However, women were better covered than men across services, despite men bearing a higher disease burden. This is likely due to the program’s structure, which is based on household outreach—limiting its reach to working men who are often unavailable during visit hours.

Rural areas showed near-universal coverage, while urban regions lagged in MTM-specific services, likely due to poor field infrastructure and large population clusters. Urban residents may already have access to static public or private services, and future MTM strategies should explore preferences and barriers in urban populations through qualitative studies. The MTM program exemplifies how community-based, primary care interventions can bridge systemic access gaps and promote equitable NCD care. Its doorstep delivery model provides a strong case for implementation science approaches aimed at advancing health equity in low-resource settings. Continuum of care is also a critical challenge—while initial screening is widespread, referral linkages and follow-up treatment continuity remain limited. Strengthening digital referral systems, incentivizing Women Health Volunteers through performance-based honoraria, and prioritizing long-term outcome monitoring are recommended for improving the effectiveness of MTM in delivering NCD care at scale.

The relatively lower coverage in urban areas suggests that the doorstep model may face operational barriers such as population density, high mobility of residents, and reduced availability of household members during daytime visits. Future adaptations could include evening or weekend visits for working populations, integration with existing urban health posts, and the use of digital appointment systems to facilitate follow-up.

Overall, across both diabetes and hypertension cascades, coverage was highest for screening (> 80%) and treatment (> 95%), while diagnosis rates remained modest (~ 20%) and control rates were the weakest points (9.8% for diabetes; 35.4% for hypertension), highlighting critical gaps in long-term disease management. This highlights the need for strengthening follow-up, adherence support, and lifestyle modification counseling to improve control outcomes.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the reliance on self-reported diagnoses and treatment status may have introduced recall bias and misclassification. Second, as a cross-sectional design, causal attribution to MTM cannot be established, and secular trends or concurrent interventions may also have contributed to observed outcomes. Third, sampling weights were not applied; hence, state-level generalizability may be limited. Finally, absence of a comparator group restricts definitive equity assessment.

Conclusion

The Makkalai Thedi Maruthuvam (MTM) program has effectively expanded healthcare access to marginalized populations in Tamil Nadu, notably women, rural residents, and individuals from Scheduled Castes and Tribes. Its doorstep delivery model, facilitated through Women Health Volunteers, has enabled high coverage in the screening and treatment of diabetes and hypertension—marking these services as the most impactful within the program. This community-led approach reflects a successful example of task-sharing and grassroots engagement in advancing universal health coverage. Despite these achievements, gaps persist in the uptake of cancer screening services and in MTM’s reach within urban areas. While the infrastructure and outreach in rural settings have supported widespread adoption, urban implementation remains limited due to resource constraints and lack of tailored strategies. To maximize the program’s impact, future efforts should prioritize strengthening cancer screening services, ensuring continuity of care, and designing urban-specific interventions. These steps are essential to realize the full potential of MTM as an inclusive and sustainable public health model.
