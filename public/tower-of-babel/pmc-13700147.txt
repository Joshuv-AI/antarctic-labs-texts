Chromophobe renal cell carcinoma: addressing the translational gap toward precision oncology for advanced disease
Authors: Xiwen Lei, Rui Dong, Lijuan Yin, Zhenjie Wu, Linhui Wang, Miaoxia He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundChromophobe renal cell carcinoma (chRCC) represents a distinct histological subtype of kidney cancer. While typically characterized by an indolent clinical course, it presents significant management challenges in advanced or late-stage disease.Main body of the abstractThis review presents current evidence regarding chRCC histopathology, emerging biomarkers, and proposed grading systems. We comprehensively analyze the molecular landscape, highlighting TP53 and PTEN mutations alongside characteristic chromosomal losses, while evaluating the role of epigenetic modifications and the immune microenvironment. Background

Chromophobe renal cell carcinoma (chRCC) represents the third most common subtype of renal malignancy. Originating from the intercalated cells of the collecting duct, chRCC exhibits gene expression profiles that mirror those of the distal nephron [1]. Although typically associated with a favorable prognosis—with 5-year recurrence-free survival (RFS) rates exceeding 90% in localized cases [2]—this subtype presents a significant clinical challenge at advanced stages. While metastatic chRCC constitutes only 2% of all metastatic RCC cases, it exhibits a disproportionately high incidence of sarcomatoid differentiation (21%) compared to clear cell (ccRCC, 12.6%) and papillary (pRCC, 10.1%) subtypes [3]. Furthermore, chRCC demonstrates distinct metastatic tropism, characterized by frequent hepatic involvement and a significantly poorer prognosis associated with pulmonary spread, suggesting an aggressive biological trajectory in a distinct subset of patients [3].

The clinical management of chRCC is currently hindered by significant diagnostic and therapeutic challenges, necessitating a deeper understanding of its underlying biology. Morphologically, chRCC shares a common cellular origin with renal oncocytoma (RO); however, while RO is benign, chRCC is a malignant neoplasm [1]. Their histological overlap often leads to diagnostic ambiguity, yet precise differentiation is critical for surgical interventions and surveillance strategies. Therapeutically, chRCC has long been overshadowed by the more prevalent ccRCC. Currently, there are no FDA-approved targeted therapies for chRCC, and its classification as an “immunologically cold” tumor—characterized by sparse immune infiltration—contributes to the suboptimal efficacy of standard immune checkpoint inhibitors [4]. These challenges are further compounded by unresolved pathogenetic questions, such as the nature and functional significance of its cytoplasmic granules.

Bridging the translational gap in precision oncology for chRCC—particularly in locally advanced or metastatic settings—is imperative to overcome current diagnostic and therapeutic bottlenecks. Accordingly, this review synthesizes the contemporary research landscape of chRCC, encompassing pathological diagnosis, molecular pathogenesis, and emerging clinical evidence, aiming to provide actionable insights to overcome these translational barriers.

Pathologic features and biomarkers

Histopathologic features

ChRCC typically presents as a solitary, well-circumscribed, and unencapsulated solid mass [5]. The classical chRCC often exhibits a tan-brown or light-tan homogeneous appearance [5, 6]. Central scar, internal hemorrhage, and calcification can be observed in some cases [6]. The tumor cells display diverse architectural patterns, including nested, solid, broad alveolar, and rare papillary arrangements [5]. The classical chRCC is characterized by a predominance of large, pale cells with translucent, reticular cytoplasm and prominent cell membranes, resembling plant cells. Hyperchromatic, wrinkled (raisinoid) nuclei with perinuclear halos are indicative features. The eosinophilic variant of chRCC is characterized by a predominance of smaller eosinophilic cells with fine oxyphilic granularity, morphologically resembling oncocytoma [5, 6]. Histologically heterogeneous, chRCC encompasses rare morphological variants such as pigmented adenomatoid, multicystic, papillary, neuroendocrine, and small-cell [7]. The histotopathologic features of chRCC are easily confused with RO, ccRCC and pRCC. So, recognition of these diverse morphologies is crucial for accurate diagnosis.

In diagnostically challenging cases, the baseline immunohistochemical (IHC) panel should be evaluated as a collective pattern rather than isolated marker evidence. Specifically, chRCC typically features CK7, CD117, and claudin-7 positivity, concurrent with vimentin and CAⅨ(CA9) negativity [5]. Additionally, Hale colloidal iron staining frequently demonstrates diffuse cytoplasmic reactivity [5]. In patients with advanced disease, aggressive components can manifest as sarcomatoid differentiation, a high-grade dedifferentiation process linked to rapid disease progression. These areas may exhibit reduced or absent CK7/CD117 expression alongside occasional, acquired CAⅨ positivity [8, 9]. Consequently, both conventional and aggressive-appearing areas should be sampled before molecular stratification is attempted.

Current concepts in histologic grading

Grading chRCC remains challenging due to its inherent nuclear atypia, which limits the utility of conventional systems such as the Fuhrman or WHO/ISUP scales. Consequently, the WHO 2022 guidelines explicitly discourage grading chRCC [5]. In response, several chRCC-specific grading schemes have been proposed. Paner et al. introduced a three-tier system based on nuclear crowding and anaplasia, which demonstrated superior prognostic discrimination, particularly among nonsarcomatoid tumors [10]. Avulova et al. proposed a four-tier system that adds coagulative tumor necrosis to further stratify Paner’s Grade 2 tumors, which demonstrated improved prognostic value for cancer-specific survival [11]. Lin et al. validated an adjusted Leibovich risk stratification model—incorporating coagulative necrosis and tumor thrombus—that outperforms the original model in chRCC patients [12]. Ohashi et al. proposed a simplified two-tier system (low vs. high grade) based solely on the presence of sarcomatoid differentiation and/or necrosis, which was validated as an independent prognostic factor in a multi-institutional cohort [13]. Despite these advances, no consensus grading system for chRCC has been established, and the relative prognostic value of nuclear crowding versus necrosis remains an area of active research and debate. Therefore, further research is required to elucidate the pathogenesis of aggressive chRCC.

Challenges and emerging biomarkers in the differential diagnosis of chRCC

The morphological overlap within the spectrum of renal oncocytic tumors—primarily chRCC and RO—necessitates the identification and application of robust ancillary markers. Recent studies have identified several novel diagnostic indicators and modalities to aid the differentiation; RO is typically characterized by a diffuse granular pattern in mitochondrial detection [14], strong HNF-1β expression [15], apical or focal LAMP1 expression [16], and positivity for S100A1, MAPRE3, and ADGRF5 [1, 17]. In contrast, chRCC frequently exhibits moderate granular mitochondrial expression [14], diffuse LAMP1 expression [16], and positivity for GPNMB and CD82, often accompanied by the loss of HNF-1β [1, 15, 17].

Molecular and lineage-specific signatures provide further diagnostic precision. For instance, the loss of SF3B1 (a CYCLOPS gene)—resulting from copy number alterations—distinguishes chRCC from other oncocytic neoplasms (e.g., HOCT and opRCC) where its expression is preserved [18]. Beyond mRNA DSG2 and lncRNA AC026888.1 markers [19], chRCC-specific lineage signatures—including FOXI1, RHCG, and the lncRNA LINC01187—demonstrate high specificity across primary and metastatic sites [20]. Notably, the lncRNA LINC01187 has emerged as a highly consistent nuclear lineage marker for chRCC and related oncocytic tumors [21]. NKX6.1, identified as a chRCC-specific master transcription factor (TF), exhibits exclusive nuclear localization in chRCC and lacks expression in other RCC subtypes [22].

Genomic landscape and molecular pathogenesis

Classic genetic alterations and molecular drivers

ChRCC is characterized by a relatively low exomic mutation rate (0.4/Mb), with TP53 and PTEN identified as the most frequently mutated genes (Fig. 1) [1, 23]. Reduced PTEN expression, validated by proteomics and IHC, serves as a key diagnostic hallmark [24]. The p53/p21/RB and PI3K/AKT/mTOR axes underpin chRCC pathogenesis, with mTOR signaling upregulated relative to its intercalated cell of origin [4, 25]. Early-occurrence MTOR and TP53 mutations—present in 17–23% of cases—frequently correlate with poor clinical outcomes [23–25]. Conversely, the RAS/RAF/MAPK pathway is typically suppressed in this subtype [26].

The hallmark chromosomal alterations of chRCC are defined by a recurrent loss of chromosomes 1, 2, 6, 10, 13, and 17, with the classic variant exhibiting greater chromosomal instability than the eosinophilic subtype [1, 23]. These losses encompass 31% of CYCLOPS genes, representing potential therapeutic vulnerabilities [18]. While classical chRCC is typically hypodiploid, metastatic progression often involves “imbalanced chromosome duplication” (ICD) [27]—a nonrandom polyploidization process that restores ploidy to mitigate fitness defects induced by chromosomal loss. Additionally, the temporal relationship between chromosomal loss and whole-genome duplication (WGD) can be inferred from loss of heterozygosity (LOH) patterns [28]. However, despite providing a biologically coherent rationale for vulnerability mapping, the therapeutic relevance of recurrent chromosomal losses and CYCLOPS gene depletion remains largely hypothesis-generating.

Other drivers include mutations in BAP1, CDKN1A, CSPG4, MAP3K13, WNK2, and telomere maintenance genes (TERT, ATRX, DAXX), the latter of which are linked to metastatic potential [23–25]. Either promoter point mutations or significant genomic rearrangements may drive TERT overexpression [23]. Genomic instability is further evidenced by Kataegis along with the prognostic upregulation of centromere genes CENPA and CENPF [23, 29]. Molecular heterogeneity in chRCC is evidenced by distinct mRNA and miRNA-based subtypes; downregulation of miR-145 may contribute to chRCC pathogenesis by upregulating its target gene, the complex I-associated NDUFA4 [23].

Biological behavior of aggressive chRCC

Aggressive chRCC—defined by sarcomatoid pathology or the development of recurrence or metastasis during follow-up [30]—exhibits complex genomic features. Its genomic alterations extend beyond classic chromosomal losses to encompass increased arm-level alterations, focal amplifications, and an elevated tumor mutational burden (TMB) [8, 31]. TP53 and PTEN mutations are pivotal drivers of aggressive phenotypes and poor survival; specifically, TP53 alterations and LOH are near-universal features of sarcomatoid differentiation [30, 32]. Additional drivers include CDKN2A, associated with a poor prognosis, as well as NCOR1 and RB1 mutations, the latter typically restricted to high-grade components [8, 27, 32, 33]. Furthermore, a 7-gene signature (CCNE2, CDC25A, CDKN2C, CYLD, DUSP6, STEAP1, TRIP13) effectively distinguishes aggressive stage I–III tumors from indolent chRCC [30]. The latter is strictly characterized by the complete absence of recurrence, metastasis, and sarcomatoid features throughout follow-up [30].

The progression from classic to aggressive chRCC is conceptualized as a sequential series of key molecular and cellular events. The acquisition of a TP53 hemizygous mutation drives the transition from classical chRCC to an atypical state characterized by increased mitosis and retention of epithelial markers [27]. The widespread LOH and the absence of characteristic chromosomal losses in sarcomatoid chRCC suggest a late-stage ICD event, which often occurs after the acquisition of mutations in RB1, PTEN, or TSC1 [32]. Functionally, these alterations converge to drive a series of changes, including the downregulation of chRCC-specific lineage genes (e.g., FOXI1, DMRT2), the upregulation of pathways involved in epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), cell proliferation, inflammation, and aberrant mTORC1 activation, as well as the downregulation of metabolic pathways such as the fatty acid metabolism pathway [31]. The principal phenotypic consequence is tumor dedifferentiation, which facilitates tumor metastasis [31]. This dedifferentiated state clusters transcriptomically with sarcomatoid RCC and other subtypes, suggesting a convergent evolutionary trajectory [31].

Epigenetic changes

The chromatin regulatory landscape of chRCC differs from that of ccRCC and pRCC, with distinct H3K27ac and H3K4me2 modification patterns [22]. ChRCC-specific H3K27ac peaks are enriched in pathways governing actin regulation, fatty acid oxidation, and ion transport [22, 33, 34]. Four motifs, such as forkhead-like and TFCP2-like motifs, showed strong enrichment within H3K27ac peaks [22]. Enhanced H3K27ac signals at the FOXI1 and TFCP2L1 loci support an intercalated cell origin [22]. While EPAS1 (HIF2α) serves as the master TF for ccRCC, FOXI1 fulfills this role in chRCC [22]. Notably, 46% of EPAS1-binding sites in chRCC colocalize with subtype-specific H3K27ac peaks, targeting metabolic rather than immune-related pathways in ccRCC [22]. Experimental reprogramming of ccRCC cells (786-O) through FOXI1 overexpression and EPAS1 knockdown induces a chRCC-like transcriptomic shift, including the upregulation of TFCP2L1, NR3C2, and KIT [22].

Consistent with its putative cell of origin, chRCC is predominantly hypomethylated relative to ccRCC [23]. Nevertheless, a subset (approximately 20%) characterized by a hypermethylated profile often harbors TP53 mutations and correlates with advanced clinical stages and poor survival [33]. Hypermethylation of WNT pathway genes, including SFRP1 and DKK1, correlates with decreased survival outcomes [33]. An 8-CpG signature (including cg03816707 | KCNS1 and cg05849324 | NHLRC1) further distinguishes indolent from aggressive chRCC [30]. Of interest, indolent chRCC showed predominant hypomethylation relative to the normal kidney tissue, whereas aggressive chRCC exhibited hypermethylation [30]. Although previous studies have suggested a progression from indolent to aggressive chRCC, recent findings revealed distinct epigenetic profiles, implying that aggressive chRCC may not arise directly from its indolent counterpart [30].

As a pivotal epitranscriptomic modification, N6-methyladenosine (m6A) dynamically orchestrates mRNA metabolism—including splicing, translation, and decay—through a complex interplay of writers, erasers, and readers [35]. The downregulation of m6A regulators—WTAP, FTO, ALKBH5, and YTHDF2—in chRCC is associated with a global reduction in m6A levels and a redistribution of the mark from the coding sequence toward the 3′ UTR [36]. A total of 644 hypermethylated and 1,304 hypomethylated m6A peaks were identified [36]. This altered landscape dysregulates key pathogenic drivers in chRCC development, such as NOTCH1 (characterized by decreased m6A levels and expression) and FGFR1 (exhibiting decreased m6A levels but increased expression) [36]. Additionally, the autophagy-related gene ATG10, which is uniquely downregulated in chRCC via m6A-dependent mechanisms, serves as a prognostic marker linked to cuproptosis [37].

Immune microenvironment

Characterized as an immunologically “cold” tumor, chRCC exhibits substantially lower CD45 + infiltration compared to ccRCC [1, 4, 38]. Immune evasion, estimated in approximately 49% of cases, is primarily driven by chromosome 6 loss (HLA class I LOH), leading to the downregulation of HLA class I genes [4, 25]. Within the chRCC immune microenvironment, myeloid cells are the dominant cell type [38] and among them, phagosome and Toll-like receptor signaling are the most significantly enriched pathways [38]. While monocyte and Th17 signatures correlate with improved survival, a metabolically divergent (MD) subset exhibits an elevated Th2-high signature associated with poor prognosis [33, 38]. Moreover, ATG10 expression correlates with the infiltration of B cells and CD8 + T cells [37]. These immune cells exhibit a strong association with ATG10 and related m6A/cuproptosis genes in chRCC [37]. Compared with other subtypes, chRCC shows reduced CD8 + and CD4 + T-cell infiltration, with CD8 + depletion associated with unfavorable outcomes [4]. Infiltrating CD8 + T cells in chRCC exhibited a bystander phenotype with limited tumor specificity [4]. Compared with ccRCC, these cells demonstrated significantly lower immune checkpoint expression, reduced tumor-specific signatures, enriched viral-specific signatures, and minimal clonal expansion [4]. Additionally, pathway analysis revealed upregulation of IL-15 signaling and downregulation of antigen processing and presentation pathways [4].

However, the prognostic value of immune infiltration remains contentious; while some evidence links high infiltration and MHC/immune-checkpoint expression to superior overall survival (OS) [39]. PANoptosis-related clustering suggests that the highest-infiltration subtypes may paradoxically experience the poorest clinical outcome [40]. Additionally, despite high PD-L1 expression in tumor cells (85%) and tumor-infiltrating mononuclear cells (50%), its correlation with clinico-pathological characteristics remains poorly defined [41].

Mitochondrial defects

Mitochondrial defects are the hallmark of chRCC. In chRCC, an increased mitochondrial copy number correlates with the presence of WGD [25]. Approximately 22.2% of sporadic chRCCs harbor non-silent mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) variants, which may drive tumorigenesis in the absence of nuclear gene alterations [42]. Compared with other RCC subtypes, chRCC exhibits a significantly higher frequency of nonsense and frameshift mutations [33]. These alterations frequently involve respiratory and oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) genes; notably, MT-ND5 within Complex I serves as a mutational hotspot in eosinophilic chRCC [23, 43], which may explain its characteristic mitochondrial abundance [24]. Furthermore, MT-CYB and MT-ND4 undergo positive selection for missense mutations [25]. Despite enriched OXPHOS and electron transport chain (ETC) gene signatures, ETC protein levels are paradoxically reduced, reflecting functional impairment [23, 33, 43]. This defect appears to trigger compensatory mitochondrial biogenesis via PPARGC1A upregulation and likely increases cellular dependence on BCL-xL [23, 44].

Metabolic reprogramming

Unlike the classic Warburg effect, chRCC utilizes distinct bioenergetic strategies [23]. It exhibits impaired glucose metabolism. Specifically, this involves a subtle upregulation of glycolytic enzymes (e.g., ATP-dependent 6-phosphofructokinase (PFKM)) alongside a marked downregulation of all gluconeogenic enzymes, including fructose-1,6-bisphosphatase 1 (FBP1) [45–47]. In ccRCC, FBP1 antagonizes tumor progression by inhibiting hypoxia-inducible factor (HIF) and its downstream glycolytic targets [48]. Conversely, HIF does not regulate glucose metabolism in chRCC [45]. Instead, a positive correlation between HIF1α and SF3B1 mRNA in chRCC suggests a potential link between the SF3B1/HIF1α pathway and fructolysis [18]. However, this correlation should be interpreted with extreme caution, as it remains purely hypothetical without direct experimental evidence. Furthermore, compared with ccRCC, most nuclear-encoded tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle genes and several proteins, including citrate synthase (CS) are upregulated in chRCC [23, 33, 46]. Glycogenolysis is similarly altered, favoring PYGB over PYGL, in contrast to the PYGL-dominant pattern in ccRCC [47]. Besides, the amino sugar, nucleotide sugar, fructose, and mannose pathways were enriched in chRCC [49].

ChRCC is further characterized by dysregulated amino acid metabolism. Despite the downregulation of classical biosynthetic pathways, intracellular amino acid pools remain stable [43, 45]. This homeostasis is maintained through compensatory endocytosis of amino acids, driven by the PLCG2/IP3/Ca2+/PKC signaling axis [45]. Additionally, pathways involving taurine, hypotaurine, glycine, serine, and threonine are significantly enriched [49]. Notably, chRCC accumulates markedly elevated levels of reduced glutathione (GSH) and oxidized glutathione (GSSG) [43, 50], despite a decrease in other intermediates of the glutathione pathway [51]. This aberrant accumulation is sustained by two primary mechanisms. The first mechanism is xCT-dependent cystine uptake. This process renders cells highly vulnerable to ferroptosis upon xCT inhibition or cyst(e)inase-mediated depletion [50]. The second mechanism involves ROS-mediated stress responses triggered by mtDNA depletion [43]. Furthermore, the glutathione intermediate gamma-glutamylthreonine exerts a strong causal effect on chRCC pathogenesis [52].

Finally, chRCC exhibits distinct lipidomic and proteomic profiles. While overall lipid metabolism is suppressed, glycerophospholipid pathways are specifically enriched [46, 49]. Compared with ccRCC, lipid coregulation is less disrupted. This is consistent with AMPK overexpression, which restricts fatty acid synthesis and promotes mitochondrial biogenesis [33, 53]. Concurrently, ferroptosis-associated pathways are upregulated, as evidenced by elevated NUPR1, FTL, and FTH1 levels [4]. In contrast, HSP70 family genes (e.g., HSPA5, HSPA6, and HSPA8) are suppressed [4]. Of particular significance, MD chRCCs are characterized by reduced TCA cycle, ETC, and AMPK pathway genes, alongside upregulated ribose metabolism genes [33]. These tumors typically correlate with advanced stages (III/IV), sarcomatoid dedifferentiation, and poor survival outcomes [33]. Ultimately, this comprehensive metabolic reprogramming likely explains why chRCC, unlike ccRCC, lacks a clinical association with obesity [54].

Clinical evidence and therapeutic outcomes

Clinical outcomes for tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) in chRCC remain inconsistent. While a phase 2 trial reported an encouraging efficacy for sunitinib—demonstrating an objective response rate (ORR) of 40% and a median progression-free survival (PFS) of 12.7 months (Table 1) [55]—subsequent trials yielded modest outcomes. Specifically, the ASPEN trial reported an ORR of 10% while the ESPN trial reported a median PFS of 8.9 months and a median OS of 31.6 months [56, 57]. Retrospective data for sunitinib demonstrated a clinical benefit rate (partial response + stable disease) of 78%, with a median PFS and OS of 10 and 26 months, respectively [58]. Other TKIs have also demonstrated clinical activity. Choueiri et al. reported an ORR of 25% among patients with chRCC treated with sunitinib or sorafenib [59]. The overall median PFS was 10.6 months, with 8.9 months observed for sunitinib compared to 27.5 months for sorafenib [59]. In a retrospective study involving 30 patients with metastatic non-clear cell RCC (including 6 with chromophobe histology), one patient with chRCC achieved partial response (PR) with cabozantinib [60].

Mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) inhibitors are key therapeutic molecules for chRCC, often demonstrating superior clinical outcomes compared to TKIs in some contexts. In the ASPEN trial, everolimus showed a trend toward improved outcomes compared with sunitinib (mPFS, 11.4 vs. 5.5 months; ORR, 33% vs. 10%) [56]; however, the ESPN trial failed to replicate these outcomes [57]. Although evidence suggests that mTOR inhibitors may yield better survival outcomes in chRCC than in ccRCC [4], conflicting data indicate that everolimus fails to improve RFS or OS in these patients [61]. Nevertheless, a potential benefit cannot be entirely excluded, given that the lower bound of the 95% confidence interval (CI) is 0.89 [61]. This variability may stem from molecular heterogeneity. In drug response prediction, elevated TFRC expression has been associated with everolimus resistance [39]. Furthermore, the combination of everolimus and lenvatinib achieved an ORR of 44%, suggesting favorable antitumor activity for this regimen [62].

Immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI)-based therapy is a rapidly evolving frontier in cancer therapeutics. ICIs seem to yield less favorable outcomes in chRCC compared to clear-cell RCC [4]. Efficacy appears largely confined to the sarcomatoid phenotype, where responses are more frequently observed [63, 64]. While dual ICI therapy (nivolumab plus ipilimumab) consistently demonstrates an ORR of 23–27% [63, 65, 66], ICI/TKI combinations yield varied outcomes. Although pembrolizumab monotherapy exhibits modest efficacy (ORR, 9.5%), its combination with lenvatinib achieves a higher ORR of 28% [67, 68]. Atezolizumab-based regimens have demonstrated only modest activity, yielding an ORR of 10% with bevacizumab and 11% with cabozantinib [69, 70]. Nevertheless, the combination of nivolumab and cabozantinib shows negligible efficacy, with a phase II trial reporting no objective responses [71]. Despite the 33% ORR reported by Takemura et al. for combined ICIs and TKIs [66], Zhong et al. found no significant ORR benefit for the combination of PD-1 inhibitors and TKIs over TKI monotherapy [72]. Taken together, while ICIs have expanded the therapeutic arsenal, their definitive benefit in chRCC remains to be elucidated.

Toward a precision oncology framework for chRCC: Integrating multi-omics insights for strategy formulation

Historically, therapeutic strategies for advanced or aggressive chRCC have relied heavily on extrapolations from established ccRCC guidelines. As discussed in the pathologic features section, morphological assessment alone remains insufficient for definitive differential diagnosis, particularly in distinguishing chRCC from other renal oncocytic tumors. The unique biological landscape of chRCC—characterized by widespread chromosomal aneuploidy, mitochondrial dysfunction, metabolic reprogramming, and an “immune-cold” microenvironment—demands a specialized, multi-dimensional precision oncology framework. Integrating histopathological features, emerging biomarkers, metabolic vulnerabilities, and clinical evidence provides the foundation for transitioning from empirical, histology-agnostic treatments to rationally designed, biomarker-driven interventions.

A cornerstone of this framework is the refined stratification of patients enabled by precision diagnostics through a standardized stepwise pathway and decision-tree algorithm (Fig. 2). The first step focuses on definitive diagnosis and differential diagnosis. Integrating tumor histopathological characteristics, a recommended immunohistochemical panel including CK7, CD10, CAIX, and CD117, and classical actionable mutations (e.g., VHL alterations prevalent in clear cell renal cell carcinoma), this step confirms the diagnosis of chRCC and identifies the presence of high-risk aggressive pathological features, such as sarcomatoid differentiation and tumor necrosis. The second step involves prognostic assessment and systematic risk stratification. Combined grading models specific to chRCC and the TNM staging system are applied to categorize patients into low-, intermediate-, or high-risk disease subgroups. The third step is treatment allocation. Based on the stratified risk status and results of genetic and molecular profiling—including mitochondrial and OXPHOS aberrations, ferroptosis susceptibility, and immune-cold tumor phenotypes—an optimal therapeutic strategy is formulated. Available options include mTOR inhibitors, TKIs, ICIs, and immunotherapy with combination regimens adopted when clinically warranted. Additionally, this step identifies patient populations suitable for priority enrollment in clinical trials. The fourth step defines validated clinical trial endpoints for translational evaluation. The core validation metrics include PFS and OS, standardized biomarker calibration, dynamic surveillance of tissue markers and circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA), as well as endpoint assessments for predictive enrichment.

In a word, the clinical management of chRCC is increasingly pivoting toward personalized, kidney-sparing strategies. For specific patients, active surveillance, stereotactic ablative body radiotherapy (SABR), and liver-directed interventions for metastatic disease represent viable alternatives [73–75]. Establishing a standardized precision oncology framework for chRCC and shifting from empiric surgical approaches to integrated multimodal management would serve as a translationally actionable decision-making tool.

Future directions and unresolved challenges in precision oncology for chRCC

After establishing a precision-oncology framework, the next challenge for chRCC is to move beyond descriptive molecular catalogues. The goal is to translate biological insights into testable hypotheses with clear clinical relevance. Because chRCC is rare and usually indolent, precision oncology must identify the subset with aggressive disease. It should distinguish heterogeneity that merely defines chRCC from heterogeneity that affects prognosis, therapeutic vulnerability, or clinical decision-making. Future studies should therefore define “aggressive chRCC” as a biologically and clinically specified state rather than as a retrospective label assigned after progression. This definition should prospectively incorporate dedifferentiation, necrosis, metastatic patterns, temporal disease kinetics, prior treatment exposure, and molecular alterations. The aim should be to determine which features provide value beyond stage and histology, which are reproducible across institutions, and which can be linked to actionable interventions.

A second priority is to set a higher evidentiary threshold for therapeutic actionability. Several candidate vulnerabilities have been reported in chRCC. These include FSP1-mediated ferroptosis resistance [76], BCL-xL/MCL-1-dependent survival signaling [44, 77], and DPP4- or cuproptosis-related metabolic pathways [37, 78]. Additional candidates include TFRC-associated everolimus resistance [39] and KIT-directed antibody-drug conjugates [79]. However, these findings should not be prematurely translated into clinical decision rules. In chRCC, bulk molecular expression, in vitro sensitivity, and retrospective drug-response prediction can generate important hypotheses. Yet none alone establishes a therapeutic dependency. Candidate targets should undergo orthogonal validation using matched primary and metastatic specimens, patient-derived organoids, ex vivo perturbation platforms, and biomarker-defined clinical cohorts. Validation should prioritize target density, lineage specificity, pathway redundancy, pharmacodynamic target engagement, and therapeutic windows in normal tissues sharing lineage-associated markers.

Therapeutic development in chRCC also requires trial designs proportionate to disease rarity and resistant to interpretive bias. Broad non-clear cell RCC trials improve accrual but can obscure histology-specific efficacy signals. They also hinder distinction between true biological sensitivity and small-number fluctuation. Future trials should mandate central pathology review and prespecified chRCC subgroup reporting. They should also annotate sarcomatoid or other dedifferentiated components and standardize prior-line stratification and response assessment. Prospective studies should not rank regimens using unstable subgroup objective response rates. Instead, they should embed translational endpoints that explain why responses occur or fail. These endpoints may include paired pre- and on-treatment biopsies, spatial immune and metabolic profiling, and dynamic pharmacodynamic markers. They should also include correlative analyses of resistant lesions. Negative or neutral studies should therefore be considered biologically informative, not merely clinically disappointing. Such studies can refine target selection and prevent repeated extrapolation of ccRCC paradigms to chRCC.

The immunotherapy question in chRCC should also be reframed. Limited and inconsistent activity of immune checkpoint inhibitors does not prove immunotherapy resistance in chRCC. Instead, conventional chRCC may lack the antigen-presentation, T-cell infiltration, and effector-cell states needed for effective checkpoint blockade. Sarcomatoid or otherwise inflamed tumors may define a distinct immune-responsive state. By contrast, conventional tumors may require rational immune priming, innate immune activation, or microenvironment-matched combination strategies. Future work should prioritize the biological conditions that permit immune responsiveness over empiric escalation of checkpoint blockade. This distinction is essential for trials that test immune conversion, rather than merely document immune failure.

Finally, progress in chRCC will depend as much on infrastructure as on target discovery. Because single-institution cohorts are inherently underpowered, multi-institutional registries, harmonized tissue collection, shared molecular annotation, and prospective rare-tumor trial networks are essential. Data standards should capture treatment response, duration of disease control, metastatic organ tropism, renal functional outcomes, toxicity, and quality of life. For this indolent subtype, short-term radiographic response alone cannot adequately measure clinical benefit. In selected patients, durable disease control with preserved renal function may be equally relevant. Ultimately, precision oncology for chRCC will mature only when each proposed biomarker is supported by evidence across four domains. These include diagnostic reproducibility, mechanistic plausibility, therapeutic tractability, and prospective clinical utility. Without this evidence chain, precision oncology may remain a molecular description of chRCC rather than a clinically transformative strategy.

Conclusions

ChRCC represents a distinct clinico-pathological entity that can no longer be treated as a mere footnote in the broader landscape of RCC. While accurate histopathological diagnosis and the paucity of dedicated therapeutic targets remain challenging, recent insights into its genomic landscape, mitochondrial defects, and metabolic reprogramming offered significant translational potential. Transitioning from generalized treatment paradigms toward a dedicated precision oncology framework will facilitate the validation of predictive biomarkers and the exploitation of chRCC-specific metabolic vulnerabilities. Sustained multi-institutional collaboration and rigorously designed, biomarker-driven clinical trials are essential to translate these mechanistic insights into improved survival outcomes for patients with chRCC.
