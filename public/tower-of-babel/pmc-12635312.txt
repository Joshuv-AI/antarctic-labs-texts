Afferent input differentially regulates establishment and maintenance of synapses in the mammalian retina
Authors: Julie Wallin, Andrew Miller, Raunak Sinha, Mrinalini Hoon
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
How afferent input shapes synaptic connections is fundamental to our understanding of cues that govern assembly of sensory circuits. In the retina, photoreceptors provide afferent visual information to second-order bipolar cells (BCs) that in turn transfer signals to output neurons. BCs have distinct inhibitory synapses at dendrites and axons but the role of afferent input for regulating the composition and function of these synapses remains unknown. We used a photoreceptor degeneration murine transgenic with labeled BCs and combined immunohistochemical assessment of synaptic proteins across timepoints with single-cell electrophysiology and transcriptomics to address how photoreceptor input regulates BC synapses. We find that inhibitory synapses across BCs have distinct dependencies on afferent input, with axonal synapses reacting first to deafferentation even though the dendritic synapses are at the site of deafferentation. Synapses were altered in a BC-type specific manner and deafferentation differentially impacted expression of synaptic proteins vs. RNA transcripts for synaptic genes revealing disrupted synaptic trafficking pathways. Loss of afferent input also prompted production of nonfunctional receptor proteins and led to withdrawal of BC output synapses. Our findings thus reveal susceptible and resilient retinal synapse types upon deafferentation and uncover how afferent input differentially regulates synapses across second-order neurons.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-25009-0.

Introduction

The first step in vision commences in the retina with photoreceptors, broadly divided into rods and cones depending on their operation range1–3. Rod photoreceptors code for visual information in dim-light conditions whereas cone photoreceptors operate at bright-light conditions. This segregation is maintained with dedicated rod bipolar cells (RBCs) and cone bipolar cells (cone BCs) collecting information from rods and cones respectively1–3. BCs apart from being compartmentalized by photoreceptor input can also be categorized as ON or OFF depending on their response profiles1–3. ON BCs respond to light increments with depolarization. In contrast OFF BCs serve as the corresponding parallel channel and respond to light decrements with depolarization1–3. Dendrites of BCs laminate in the outer plexiform layer (OPL) where they establish specialized synaptic connections with glutamatergic photoreceptor terminals. Axon terminals of BCs establish output synaptic connections in the inner plexiform layer (IPL). Axon terminals and dendrites of RBCs and cone BCs have a heterogenous distribution of inhibitory GABAergic synapses4 but the role of photoreceptor input for establishing and maintaining these diverse inhibitory synapses across BC compartments remains unknown. This is of importance given that distinct synapse types could have disparate sensitivities to loss of afferent input and the information about resilient vs. susceptible synapse types would be informative in formulation of therapeutic strategies aimed at re-instating functionality of the retinal circuit experiencing photoreceptor loss. Of note, diseases such as retinitis pigmentosa and macular degeneration which cause photoreceptor death lead to deafferented BCs and can induce structural re-modeling of the remaining circuitry2,5.

In the adult retina, two types of GABAergic synapses are enriched in RBC and ON cone BC terminals defined by the inclusion of specific ionotropic GABA receptors – one GABAA population contains the GABAAa1 receptor type whereas the other is enriched with GABAC receptors4,6. There is also a small fraction of GABAAa3 receptors at these BC terminals in the adult retina7. The BC dendrites contain a different subtype of GABAAa1 receptor synapses with a distinct receptor subunit composition compared to axonal GABAAa1 receptors4. The GABAergic synapses at BC axons are crucial for modulating the release of glutamate from these terminals7,8. RBC and cone BC terminals contain specialized ribbon synapses that provide high-throughput glutamate release9–11 to inner retinal amacrine cell (AC) interneurons and output retinal ganglion cell (GC) postsynaptic processes respectively and the inhibition provided by GABAergic synapses at BC terminals regulates this release and improves the fidelity of the signal that is transmitted through these terminals12–14. As a result, this inhibition tunes the kinetics and response amplitude of visual signals emanating out of the retina13,15. OFF BC terminals contain GABAA but not GABAC synapses4. In this study we harnessed the heterogeneity of inhibitory GABAergic synapses across retinal BCs and combine assays of synaptic protein expression with functional recordings, serial electron microscopy and transcriptomics to address how deafferentation in a murine model of retinitis pigmentosa impacts the different synapse types spread across BC compartments.

Results

Synapses across BC axon terminals are first to be affected by photoreceptor loss

To study the impact of deafferentation on BC inhibitory synapse types, we utilized the homozygous rd1 retinal degeneration line where rods and the majority of cones are lost within 1-month of age16–18. We performed all our immunohistochemical analyses in the mid-central retinal region as this region is devoid of photoreceptors in the rd1 retina at the 1-month timepoint17. At first, we labeled for the GABAA receptor type, GABAAa1 that is typically enriched at adult BC axon terminals7. Figure 1A compares the GABAAa1 expression profile in OPL and IPL of the rd1 retina at the 1-month (top panel) and 2-month (bottom panel) degeneration timepoint. We quantified the receptor pixel density in rd1 compared to control and found selective reduction of GABAAa1 pixel density in the IPL of rd1 retinas at both the 1-month and 2-month timepoints (Fig. 1B-C; IPL thickness was comparable across genotypes (Supplemental Fig. 1)). As the IPL is where BC axon terminals laminate, the GABAAa1 receptors across BC terminals could be reduced soon after photoreceptor loss.

Dendrites of RBCs and ON cone BCs utilize metabotropic mGluR6 receptors to sense the glutamate released from photoreceptors1–3. To determine the GABA receptor profiles specifically across BCs we crossed the rd1 line to the Grm6-tdtomato transgenic, where RBCs and ON cone BCs are fluorescently labeled4,19 as the promoter for mGluR6 drives tdtomato expression in this line. We focused our analyses on the RBCs that are the only BC type that collect information from rods and on the Type 6 (T6) ON cone BC that collect information from the cones and provide the majority of the input to a primary GC output pathway in the murine retina1–3,20. Both these BC types are robustly labeled in the Grm6-tdtomato line allowing individual BC-specific analyses.

Dendrites of RBCs and T6 receive photoreceptor input at the OPL where horizontal cells (HCs) mediate lateral interactions. BC axon terminals laminate in the IPL where AC interneurons provide inhibitory signaling to tune the information flow in the inner retina (Fig. 2A). At the 1-month timepoint, rd1 RBC and T6 axon terminals showed markedly reduced expression of GABAAa1 receptors (Fig. 2B) compared to control. The small GABAAa3 receptor fraction at 1-month RBC and T6 axon terminals appeared comparable across genotypes (Fig. 2C), whereas the GABAC receptor fraction appeared upregulated in the 1-month RBC and T6 rd1 terminals compared to control (Fig. 2D). GABAAa1 receptors on the dendrites of RBC and T6 BCs are distinct from axonal GABAAa1 receptors in not incorporating the g2 (gamma-2) subunit within the receptor pentamer4. The dendritic GABAAa1 receptor fraction in 1-month rd1 RBC and T6 appeared equivalent to control (Fig. 2E). We next quantified the volume of the receptor pixels above background and normalized this to the volume of the BC process (metric of % occupancy more details in Methods and Hoon et al., 201721). At the 1-month timepoint, the % occupancy of GABAAa1 within rd1 RBC and T6 terminals is significantly reduced, GABAC receptor expression within these terminals is significantly upregulated with no change in the axonal GABAAa3 fraction or the BC dendritic GABAAa1 receptor fraction (Fig. 2F). These observations indicate that at BC terminals, GABAAa1 synapses are susceptible to photoreceptor loss, but GABAC receptors might act as the resilient receptor fraction demonstrating a possible compensatory upregulation in synaptic protein expression levels after the BCs have lost afferent input.

Long term deafferentation impairs synapse profiles across both dendritic and axonal compartments of BCs

We next asked if the alterations we observed across 1-month rd1 synapse types were also maintained at a prolonged degeneration timepoint. To this end, we labeled for each GABA receptor fraction at the 2-month rd1 timepoint and estimated expression at RBC and T6 axon terminals and dendrites. Similar to the 1-month timepoint, we found a marked reduction of GABAAa1 at 2-month rd1 RBC and T6 axon terminals (Fig. 3A, quantification of % occupancy in 3E), but no change in the GABAAa3 fraction (Fig. 3B, quantification: 3E). Contrary to the elevated levels of GABAC present at the RBC and T6 axon terminals in 1-month rd1 retina, at the 2-month timepoint the GABAC expression levels in rd1 RBC and T6 axon terminals were comparable to control (Fig. 3C, quantification: 3E). This observation underscores that in a deafferented retina, there is a temporal progression of receptor alterations for certain synapse types and that upregulation of receptor protein levels, which could imply a possible compensatory attempt, occurs at a defined relatively early timepoint soon after photoreceptor degeneration. At the dendritic compartment of 2-month rd1 retinas, we saw BC-type specific effects on GABAAa1 expression. Whereas GABAAa1 levels at RBC dendrites appeared upregulated in 2-month rd1 retinas compared to controls, the GABAAa1 levels at T6 dendrites were downregulated (Fig. 3D, quantification: 3E).

Given that photoreceptor death can alter the morphology of the post-photoreceptor retinal circuit5,22,23, we assessed the morphology of RBC and T6 axons and dendrites at both the 1-month and 2-month rd1 timepoint (Supplemental Fig. 2). Of note our measures of % receptor occupancy however are unaffected by changes in the size or coverage of BC axonal or dendritic compartment as the occupancy measures are levels of receptor proteins normalized to the volume of the BC compartment (axonal or dendritic process). At the 1-month timepoint the axon terminals of both rd1 RBCs and T6s were slightly smaller in their coverage area compared to control (Supplemental Fig. 2A). At the 2-month timepoint however, only the rd1 T6 terminals covered a significantly smaller area compared to control (Supplemental Fig. 2B). In contrast, the dendrites of both RBC and T6 in both 1-month and 2-month rd1 retinas remained comparable to control in their coverage area (Supplemental Fig. 2C-D). The RBC dendrites in 2-month rd1, however, were mispositioned compared to control with rd1 dendritic arbors located at the base of the soma instead of emanating into the OPL from the top of the soma (Supplemental Fig. 2D). We quantified the density of dendritic processes relative to OPL and soma location and observed a shift in the positioning of the 2-month rd1 RBC dendritic processes. Whereas control RBC dendrites were concentrated in the OPL, the 2-month rd1 RBC dendrites were positioned basally with dendritic processes close to the base of the soma (Supplemental Fig. 2E). This dendritic retraction was specific to RBCs alone as 2-month rd1 T6 dendrites did not demonstrate this. Thus, morphological alterations in response to deafferentation can be both compartment-specific and BC type-specific.

Our next question was to ascertain if OFF BC terminals in the rd1 retina also showed a similar reduction of GABAAa1 as rd1 ON BC terminals did. To this end we used the marker, synaptotagmin-2 (Syt2) for labeling the axonal terminals of Type 2 (T2) OFF cone BCs24,25. GABAAa1 expression levels within T2 OFF cone BC terminals remained unchanged in both 1-month and 2-month rd1 retinas (Supplemental Fig. 3) underscoring a BC type-specific alteration of this axonal synapse type after deafferentation. Our findings also reveal that GABAA synapses at T2 OFF BC terminals could perhaps be more resilient compared to ON BC terminals.

Regulation of scaffolding protein expression across deafferented axon terminals can be distinct from postsynaptic receptors

The divergent alterations of GABAAa1 and GABAC receptor expression at rd1 RBC terminals made us question whether the scaffolding protein at these GABAergic synapses was also altered after deafferentation. To determine this, we immunolabeled for LRRTM4, which is the postsynaptic organizer for GABAAa1 and GABAC receptor synapses at adult RBC terminals26. Lack of LRRTM4 leads to downregulation of both GABAAa1 and GABAC receptor expression at RBC axon terminals26 and a reduction in GABAAa1 expression at RBC terminals due to a lack of early molecular cues is accompanied by an attenuation in LRRTM4 expression levels7. After deafferentation however, we found that LRRTM4 was robustly expressed at rd1 RBC terminals at both the 1-month and 2-month timepoints (Fig. 4A). Quantification of the % occupancy of LRRTM4 within rd1 and control RBC terminals showed that LRRTM4 was upregulated at rd1 RBC terminals at both the 1-month and 2-month timepoints (Fig. 4B). These observations reveal that upon deafferentation, postsynaptic organizing proteins can undergo a distinct regulation compared to the receptor proteins equipping the same synapses. If the GABAAa1 synapse at RBC terminals was in a general state of disorganization one would have expected both receptor and organizing proteins to be similarly downregulated. In contrast we observe an upregulation in LRRTM4 levels at rd1 RBC terminals implying that GABA synapses at deafferented RBC terminals are possibly attempting a compensatory upregulation of synaptic elements which persists even several weeks after complete deafferentation. These findings also underscore that the mechanisms that are downregulating GABAAa1 expression in rd1 RBC terminals are probably distinct from those regulating LRRTM4 expression at these synapses.

Ultrastructural organization of deafferented BC terminals highlight withdrawn output synaptic sites

Our previous work has shown that alterations in the level of GABAergic inhibition onto RBC terminals impacts the ultrastructural organization of ribbon output sites across these terminals7,26,27. We thus performed serial block face scanning electron microscopy and reconstructed the RBC terminals and its constituent synapses in 2-month rd1 retinas. We selected the late deafferentation timepoint to capture the ultrastructural organization several weeks after complete photoreceptor loss. Figure 5A shows a 3D reconstruction of a 2-month rd1 RBC terminal with the incorporated ribbon structures (red). Ribbons are specialized sites of glutamate release at RBC axon terminals and typically in a healthy adult retina > 95% of ribbons within a RBC terminal are apposed to two postsynaptic partners forming a ‘dyad’ synaptic configuration2,26,27. We observed several filopodia-like structures emanating out of the 2-month rd1 RBC terminal and several instances of withdrawn globular ribbon profiles (Fig. 5A; Supplemental Fig. 4). We quantified the total number of ribbons within rd1 RBC terminals and compared this number to the total number of ribbons within healthy (control) RBC terminals (Fig. 5B). We did not observe any difference in this synaptic parameter, implying that the total number of ribbons within a RBC terminal is un-affected by deafferentation. We next ascertained the distribution of ribbon sites within the rd1 RBC terminal. Whereas the majority of ribbons in a healthy RBC are typically vertically apposed to two partners in a ‘vertical dyad’ configuration (~ 97% Fig. 5C top graph for Control RBCs) the ribbons within a 2-month rd1 RBC were only observed in this configuration at a frequency of ~ 38% (Fig. 5C bottom graph for 2-month rd1 RBC). Alternatively, a large fraction of ribbons within 2-month rd1 RBC terminals were located at single partner ‘monad’ configurations (~ 23%) or assumed un-anchored ‘floating’ profiles (~ 31%; Fig. 5C). There were also some instances of ribbons within rd1 RBCs in an erroneous 3-partner ‘triad’ profile and some in a dyad configuration where the ribbon lay laterally (designated as ‘horizontal dyads’; Fig. 5C). Thus, deafferentation impairs the localization of ribbon profiles across BC terminals with the reduction in output sites probably serving to balance the reduced dendritic input. Of note cross-compartment regulatory cues do exist for BCs as retrograde signaling from BC axons can control photoreceptor connectivity at the dendritic compartment28.

Functional responses of both GABAA and GABAC synapses across deafferented BC terminals show impairments

Our next question was whether the alterations we observed at the level of synaptic protein expression were also mirrored in the functional responses. To this end we addressed whether the reduction of GABAAa1 and upregulation of GABAC expression that we observed across 1-month rd1 RBC terminals was also reflected in the amount of functional GABAA and GABAC receptors at the surface. We performed single-cell patch-clamp recordings from 1-month rd1 RBCs and recorded the responses to a GABA puff application at the axon terminals of RBCs. We observed a ~ 50% reduction in the GABA response amplitude from 1-month rd1 RBCs compared to control (Fig. 6). Typically, the GABA response at RBC terminals is composed of two components that have near equal magnitudes – a GABAA receptor component and a GABAC receptor component4,7,26. These components can be delineated through pharmacology. Specifically, the GABAC receptor blocker TPMPA uncovers the GABAA-specific component which can in turn be suppressed by the drug GABAzine (Fig. 6A). We found a significant reduction of the total GABA response, the GABAA component and the GABAC component in 1-month rd1 RBCs compared to control (Fig. 6B). The reduction in the GABAA component mirrors the reduced expression of GABAAa1 receptors we observed through our immunohistochemical analyses. The reduction in the functional GABAC component implies that fewer functional GABAC receptors are present on the surface of RBC axon terminals. Because our immunohistochemical analyses showed an increased expression of GABAC receptor subunits at RBC axon terminals in 1-month rd1 retinas these results reveal that the GABAC receptors being inserted at the RBC axonal surface in response to deafferentation are nonfunctional.

Establishment of GABA synapse types at BC terminals is altered due to impaired afferent input

In the rd1 mouse model, photoreceptor loss commences during circuit formation16,17. Given that deafferentation commences during the time of GABAA and GABAC synapse formation at BC axon terminals, we next determined the accruing of these receptor types at rd1 BC terminal synapses during circuit establishment to understand the role of afferent input during circuit formation. We first determined the timepoint when appreciable photoreceptor loss initiates in the rd1 retinas. We labeled the nuclear layers of rd1 retinas and littermate controls that were crossed into the Grm6-tdtomato background across different developmental timepoints and quantified the outer nuclear layer (ONL) thickness (Supplemental Fig. 5). As the ONL contains the photoreceptor cell bodies its thickness is representative of the extent of photoreceptor loss in rd1 retinas. As expected from previous work17 that details postnatal day 30 (P30) as a timepoint when all photoreceptors are lost in mid-central rd1, we also observed no remaining ONL in the rd1 retinas at this timepoint (Supplemental Fig. 5A-B). At P9 when the BC terminals have completed their lamination in the IPL, no change in the ONL thickness in rd1 retinas can be observed compared to control. At P12 however, which is just before eye-opening, the ONL thickness in the rd1 retinas begins to be significantly reduced compared to control. The ONL thickness then continues to decline in the rd1 retinas compared to control as assayed at P14, P16 and P21 (Supplemental Fig. 5). Thus, noticeable loss of photoreceptors in the rd1 retinas commences at P12.

P12 is the developmental timepoint when the GABAAa1 accruing becomes appreciable in RBC and T6 axon terminals. Typically, during development, GABAAa1 expression thereafter increases around P16 (couple of days after eye-opening) which is followed by a reduction in receptor expression levels across BC axon terminals4,7. To determine if the developmental accruing of GABAAa1 at BC terminal synapses is impaired due to the ongoing deafferentation in rd1 retinas, we quantified GABAAa1 expression at RBC and T6 terminals in P12, P16 and P21 rd1 retinas (Fig. 7). We found that GABAAa1 expression levels were reduced in rd1 RBC and T6 axon terminals compared to control at all developmental timepoints (Fig. 7A-C). Interestingly, however, the GABAAa1 expression at rd1 RBC and T6 terminals increased from P12 to P16 followed by a decrease similar to the developmental profile seen in control retinas (Supplemental Fig. 6). This indicates that the general trajectory of GABAAa1 expression at RBC and T6 terminals could be similar in rd1 and control retinas at these developmental stages as some amount of photoreceptor input is maintained in rd1 retinas at these timepoints (Supplemental Fig. 5). Together our observations reveal the role of afferent input for the accruing of receptors during developmental establishment of GABAAa1 synapses at BC terminals.

Our previous work has shown that the GABAAa1 increase at ON BC terminals prior to eye-opening at P12 is paralleled by a concomitant downregulation of GABAAa3 receptors at these developing BC terminals7. We thus determined the expression levels of GABAAa3 at P12 rd1 RBC terminals (Supplemental Fig. 7). We found comparable levels of this receptor type at rd1 and control RBC terminals at P12 revealing that the developmental downregulation of GABAAa3 at BC terminals is maintained in rd1 retinas. We next addressed if the clustering of GABAC receptors at rd1 RBC and T6 axon terminals could be impaired prior to 1-month/P30. To this end we assayed GABAC expression levels at P21 which is a timepoint when GABAC receptor clustering starts increasing in ON BC terminals4,26. We found GABAC receptor clustering in P21 rd1 RBC and T6 terminals to be significantly elevated compared to control (Fig. 7D) indicating an aberrant elevated initiation of receptor clustering at GABAC synapses across rd1 BC terminals. Thus, establishment of GABAAa1 and GABAC synapses across BC terminals with impaired afferent input is altered from an early developmental timepoint.

Transcriptomic profiles of Rd RBCs reveal mechanism underlying perturbed GABAAa1 trafficking to axon terminals

The final question we addressed in terms of the regulation of BC synapses in rd1 retinas was if altered expression of synaptic proteins is paralleled by concordant alterations at the transcript level. To this end, we performed single cell RNA sequencing from 1-month (P30) rd1 retinas vs. littermate control (Fig. 8). We sorted the RBC cluster in each sample through selection for known markers Vsx2, Otx2, Grm6, Isl1, Prkca, Car8, Sebox in accordance with the list detailed for RBCs in Shekhar et al.29. We also identified the rod and cone clusters based on expression of specific markers29. At the P30 time-point, there was no discernable rod cluster from the rd1 sample as expected (Fig. 8A), since rod degeneration is complete by P30 in rd117. Given that cone degeneration follows rod degeneration in the rd1 with a center to peripheral gradient17 the rd1 sample did show a cone cluster at P30, which is representative of the remaining cone fraction at this timepoint primarily from peripheral retina. We confirmed that the RBC cluster was enriched in the RBC-specific markers Amigo2, Bhlhe23, Gpr179, Grm6, Pcp2, Prkca, Vsx2, Isl1, Sebox and Car829,30 and deficient in AC-specific markers Slc32a1, Gad1, Gad2, Igfbp2 and Gjd2 (Supplemental Fig. 8). We next compared the expression level of GABA receptor genes in the 1-month rd1 vs. control RBC cluster. We found comparable expression levels for Gabra1 (gene for GABAAa1), Gabrr1 and Gabrr2 (genes encoding GABAC receptor subunits) and Lrrtm4 in the rd1 RBCs compared to control (Fig. 8B). We also found comparable fraction of cells in the RBC cluster expressing these genes in the rd1 and control samples (Fig. 8B). As GABAA receptor pentamers typically exhibit a 2α-2β-γ subunit stoichiometry31, we next assessed the gene transcripts for the two GABAA receptor subunits that are associated with GABAAa1 receptors at RBC axons – namely GABAAg2 and GABAAb34. Whereas transcripts for Gabrb3 (gene encoding GABAAb3) were comparable in expression levels and fraction of cells expressing Gabrb3 in the rd1 vs. control RBC clusters, the expression level of Gabrg2 (gene encoding GABAAg2) was reduced in rd1 RBCs compared to control (Fig. 8B). There was also a reduction in the fraction of cells in the 1-month rd1 RBC cluster expressing Gabrg2 compared to control (Fig. 8B). As GABAAg2 (gamma-2) is the subunit which is deemed necessary for the synaptic targeting of GABAA receptors32, a reduction in the expression transcript of this subunit could underlie the impaired trafficking of GABAAa1 receptors to RBC terminals in 1-month rd1 retinas. This is also supported by the observation that the gene transcript level of RBC GABAAa1 remains unaffected in 1-month rd1 samples compared to control.

Discussion

The retina has a rich diversity in the synapse types employed for processing distinct aspects of the visual scene2. This diversity is represented in the distinct subtypes of inhibitory GABAergic synapses across the axons and dendrites of RBCs and cone BCs4. Our findings reveal that deafferentation has a disparate impact on BC synapses with some synapse types displaying susceptible vs. resilient modifications. Our data further reveal that in degeneration conditions, protein level alterations are not always mirrored by corresponding transcriptomic changes providing new insights into the mechanisms underlying synaptic alterations in the diseased circuit. In this regard, we uncovered that a reduction at the transcriptional level of the GABAAg2 subunit across 1-month rd1 RBCs could be the mechanism leading to the selective reduction in the expression of GABAAa1 receptors trafficked to RBC axonal terminals but which did not impact protein expression of GABAAa1 receptors at the dendrites of 1-month rd1 RBCs as these assemble without the g2 subunit4. Future studies specifically overexpressing GABAAg2 in rd1 RBCs would be informative to determine if this could rescue the impaired GABAAa1 receptor clustering at rd1 RBC axonal synapses.

Our findings emphasize a synapse-specific impact of deafferentation. Whereas GABAAa1 receptors are downregulated from synaptic sites at both RBC and T6 axon terminals soon after photoreceptor loss, GABAC receptor expression at these terminals is transiently upregulated soon after photoreceptor loss. This also underscores that different synaptic profiles can be observed at later (2-month) degeneration timepoints compared to earlier timepoints. Of note, alterations in the activity impinging onto the RBC can differentially impact GABAAa1 and GABAC inhibitory synapses at RBC terminals. Our previous work has shown that impairing GABA synthesis which reduces the amount of inhibition onto RBC terminals reduces receptor clustering at GABAAa1 but not GABAC synapses33. Further, impairments in GABAAa1 receptor accruing at RBC terminals due to lack of early molecular cues does not impact receptor clustering at GABAC synapses7. Thus, GABAAa1 and GABAC synapses on BC terminals can have distinct and divergent responses to activity alterations as could be encountered in the degenerating rd1 milieu. Strikingly, the T2 OFF BC axon terminals in rd1 retinas did not show altered expression levels of GABAAa1. These findings imply that GABAA synapses across T2 OFF BC terminals might perhaps be more resilient to deafferentation. In this regard it is interesting to note that photoreceptor loss does not impact the ionotropic glutamate receptors at OFF BC dendrites but downregulates expression of mGluR6 at ON BC dendrites34. Future studies across other rd1 OFF BC types are needed to determine if GABAAa1 axonal synapses across all rd1 OFF BCs maintain receptor expression. GABAAa1 receptors at RBC and T6 dendrites which are distinct from the axonal GABAAa1 receptors in lacking the incorporation of a g2-subunit4 show altered expression profiles only at the 2-month timepoint. Thus, inhibitory synapses at BC dendrites react at a much later timepoint to deafferentation compared to axonal synapses even though the dendritic synapses are at the site of deafferentation. Further, the alterations in the dendritic GABAAa1 receptors seem to be BC-type specific: whereas RBC dendritic processes show an upregulation, T6 dendrites downregulate expression of GABAAa1 in 2-month rd1 retinas. These type-specific alterations are reminiscent of the impact of suppressed inhibitory neurotransmission which also leads to BC-type-specific alterations of GABAAa1 at the dendritic compartment4. Thus, BC synapses respond differentially to deafferentation and activity-manipulations with some synapse types being more susceptible to lack of afferent input and/or altered network activity.

Underscoring the BC-type specific response to deafferentation, the midget BCs in primate retina show a reduced number of ribbon profiles at their terminals after photoreceptor loss35 whereas we observed similar number of ribbons across 2-month rd1 mouse RBC terminals compared to control. Furthermore, although the number of ribbons at primate midget BC terminals was reduced after photoreceptor loss the actual configurations of the ribbon relative to postsynaptic partners were largely unchanged35. This is distinct from our observations of altered ribbon configurations across the 2-month rd1 RBC terminals in mouse retina. We observed fewer dyad profiles, more monad profiles, some triad profiles and some dyads with lateral instead of vertical ribbon configurations across the 2-month rd1 RBC terminals. Additionally, we found several instances of floating ribbon profiles in deafferented mouse RBC terminals, but primate midget BC terminals did not show unanchored floating ribbon profiles after photoreceptor loss35. Regarding the mechanisms that drive RBC ribbons to be displaced to erroneous output locations in rd1 RBC terminals, our previous work has shown that manipulations that alter the amount of inhibition received by the RBC terminal can lead to alterations in ribbon localization7,26.

Our combinatorial analyses determined distinct impacts of deafferentation on synaptic protein expression levels compared to mRNA levels for RBC GABAAa1 receptors highlighting that alterations in protein trafficking could be the underlying reason for altered receptor clustering across 1-month rd1 RBC terminals. Furthermore, our single-cell patch-clamp recordings from RBCs divulged a reduction of functional GABAC receptors at 1-month rd1 RBC terminals which reveals that the elevated GABAC receptor expression observed through immunolabeling experiments at this timepoint is probably indicative of nonfunctional GABAC receptors being inserted across rd1 RBC terminals. To this end, it should be noted that our GABAC antibody is a pan-rho subunit antibody, and it could be that the incorporated GABAC receptor pentamer at rd1 RBC terminals involves subunit assemblies that are atypical and functionally compromised compared to receptor configurations at healthy RBC terminal synapses. GABAC receptors are composed of pentamers of rho1-3 subunits which can assemble as homomers or heteromers36,37. Rho1 and rho2 subunits are the most abundantly expressed GABAC subunits in murine retina37. But expression of murine GABAC receptor subunits in heterologous systems has shown that homomers composed of rho2 subunits are unable to form functional receptors38. GABAC receptors with different rho-subunit combinations can also have distinct functional properties38–40. Thus, GABAC receptors in rd1 RBCs could include assemblies with different subunit composition(s) and altered functional profiles compared to receptor configuration(s) in healthy retina. Indeed, disease states can promote alterations in the assembly and function of ionotropic GABA receptors. Channel conductance of GABAA receptors for example is impaired in disease conditions such as epilepsy due to alterations in the subunit(s) constituting the receptor pentamer41. Future studies that assay the pentamer arrangements of receptor subunits in healthy vs. diseased retinal synapses can shed further insights into the alterations imposed on the structural configurations and functional profiles of assembled synaptic receptors during disease progression.

Retinal degenerative diseases are typically associated with altered protein homeostasis within retinal neurons42. Whereas direct stress within the degenerating cell type (in rd1 this would be the photoreceptors) can initiate pathways that locally dysregulate protein turnover in that cell type, it is possible that an imbalanced synaptic input at RBC dendrites imposed by photoreceptor loss in rd1 alters intrinsic protein regulation pathways in rd1 RBCs. The alterations we observe across RNA, protein and functional levels for GABAAa1 and GABAC point to a general dysfunction of the synaptic protein regulatory pathways in rd1 RBCs. Of note altering the amount of inhibition early RBC terminals receive through their GABAAa3 receptors can also lead to trafficking deficits for GABAAa1 receptors and impaired establishment of functional GABAA synapses at RBC terminals7. Sensory deprivation, however, attenuates both the abundance of Gabra1 mRNA transcripts in RBCs and impairs accruing of GABAAa1 receptors at RBC terminal synapses43. Thus, the underlying mechanisms impeding correct assembly of receptors at retinal synapses can be at the transcriptional or protein trafficking level both of which for the RBC can be regulated by network activity and the balance of synaptic inputs impinging onto the RBC.

Functionally the inhibitory synapse impairments we see at rd1 RBC and T6 BC terminals could contribute towards the aberrant oscillatory profiles commonly observed in rd1 inner retinas44,45. At the level of the output GCs, inhibition seems to be modified in response to loss of about 50% of upstream cone photoreceptors. Interestingly this alteration is observed only when cone photoreceptors are ablated and not when retinal stimulation mimics 50% of input activation46. These observations together with our findings of distinct GABAA and GABAC alterations in rod and cone bipolar terminals after photoreceptor loss underscores that loss of photoreceptors and hence afferent input in the retina impacts diverse inner retinal inhibitory circuits in a synapse-specific manner. Given the wide variety of inhibitory circuits and interneuron types in the inner mammalian retina more work needs to be done to parse out changes across different retinal inhibitory synapses in response to deafferentation as this can disparately impact the computational abilities of the degenerating circuit.

Western blot analyses of protein levels from 2-3month rd1 retinas revealed a global upregulation of retinal GABAA and glycine receptors after photoreceptor loss47. Our observations underscore that alterations in synaptic proteins can be synapse-type-specific and thus a cell-type and synapse-specific comparison of protein expression levels coupled with functional analyses is imperative to determine the response of the diverse retinal synaptome to deafferentation. Altogether the alterations at the protein, RNA and functional level need to be understood for the different retinal synaptic subsets across disease conditions as a misalignment across these three domains could be characteristic of disease progression. Furthermore, disparity between these domains needs to be considered when thinking of therapeutic interventions given that the effectiveness of treatment aimed at reinstating a specific synaptic pathway will depend on the level of misalignment across these domains and whether production of atypical synaptic protein types are being accentuated in response to disease.

Methods

Experimental model details

All experiments were conducted following guidelines of the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of the University of Wisconsin-Madison, all experimental protocols were approved by the University of Wisconsin-Madison IACUC, and reporting conforms with the ARRIVE guidelines. To visualize bipolar cells, the rd1 transgenic mice homozygous for the Pde6b mutation16 were obtained from Jackson labs and crossed into the Grm6-tdTomato line4,19. The Grm6-tdTomato line uses the Grm6 promoter for selective fluorescent reporter (tdTomato) expression in ON retinal bipolar cells (RBCs and ON cone BCs). In the rd1 mouse line, mutation in the Pde6b gene of the phototransduction cascade leads to rod photoreceptor degeneration which is followed by cone photoreceptor degeneration2,16,48. Both male and female mice were used for this study. The specific ages used are defined in the study timepoints.

Immunohistochemistry

Mice were deeply anesthetized with isoflurane followed by cervical dislocation. Eyes were harvested after euthanasia, retinas were dissected out in oxygenated artificial cerebral spinal fluid media (ACSF; preparation details in Sinha et al., 20217) and flat mounted onto a gridded filter paper (Millipore HABG01300). Thereafter retinas were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde prepared in ACSF media for 15 min, and rinsed in 0.1 M phosphate buffer solution (PBS). Primary antibody incubation was done in blocking medium containing 5% donkey serum and 0.5% triton in PBS. Antibodies used in this study include: anti-GABAAa1 and GABAAa3 (1:3000, guinea pig, J.M. Fritschy49), anti-LRRTM4 (1:500, rabbit, T. Siddiqui50), anti-GABAC (pan-rho subunits, 1:500, rabbit, R. Enz, H. Wassle and S. Haverkamp51), anti-DsRed (1:1000, rabbit, Clontech) and anti-Synaptotagmin-2 (1:1000, mouse, znp-1 from Zebrafish International Resource Center). Primary antibody incubation (GABAAa3 and GABAC antibodies were co-incubated) was done for 3 days on a shaker at 4 °C. Following primary antibody incubation and washes in PBS, retinas were incubated in anti-isotypic fluorescently conjugated secondaries (1:1000, Alexa Fluor from Invitrogen or Dylight from Jackson Immunoresearch) overnight at 4 °C. This was followed by washes in PBS and mounting on slides using Vectashield mounting medium (Vector labs). For labeling nuclei TO-PRO (catalog T3605, Thermo Fisher Scientific) was used at 1:10000 with the last PSB wash before mounting. For performing retinal immunolabeling in slices, post-fixation the retina was embedded in agarose (low gelling temperature, Sigma) and then sectioned (120 micron) using a Vibratome (Leica, VT1000S). The collected slices were processed through the general steps for immunohistochemistry as outlined above with an overnight primary antibody incubation time.

Confocal imaging and image analyses

Slides were imaged using a Leica SP8 confocal microscope using a 1.4 NA 63X oil objective at a typical resolution of 0.09 − 0.06 micron x-y resolution and 0.3 micron z resolution. Mid-central regions of the retina were imaged for the immunohistochemical analyses and image settings were similar for rd1 and control retinas. Images were thereafter processed using Image J (NIH) and Amira (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Images were imported and analyzed as per methods outlined in Hoon et al., 201721. Briefly, to calculate percent (%) volume occupancy of synaptic signals the bipolar cell process was masked in 3D using the LabelField function in Amira. Thereafter using the Arithmetic function in Amira, the synaptic signal specifically included within the bipolar cell process was isolated. After this the pixels corresponding to the mask volume and to the thresholded synaptic signal were determined and % occupancy was calculated = (volume of thresholded signal/volume of bipolar cell process)*100. A threshold about 2–3 standard deviations above the noise peak was selected for the synaptic signal to exclude background noise across the images (more details in Hoon et al., 201721). To calculate the receptor pixel density, the image planes representing the synaptic layers were thresholded to eliminate background noise and receptor pixels above this threshold were represented relative to the total number of image pixels (i.e., pixel density). To calculate the area of the bipolar cell axon and dendrite, a 3D mask of the process was generated in Amira and thereafter the maximum intensity projection of the mask was imported into Image J. The Hull and Circle plugin was thereafter used to calculate the area of the process. This area is representative of the area of the polygon enveloping the borders outlining the processes. To calculate the z-axis profile of the bipolar cell dendrites, which represents the dendritic pixel density or density of dendritic pixels across the image planes, the dendrite of the bipolar cell was first masked in Amira and exported to Image J. Soma location was noted with the start being depicted as ‘0’. The outer plexiform layer-outer nuclear layer (OPL-ONL) border where bipolar cell dendrites are typically placed was assigned a value of -5 and the start of the bipolar cell axon was assigned a + 5 value. Average signal representing the density of the dendritic processes was thereafter plotted relative to the − 5 to + 5 z-axis location with a symmetric image plane division across the image stack.

Electrophysiology

Single cell patch clamp recordings were performed from rod bipolar cells in a slice preparation. Slices were generated from mid-central regions of the retina after agarose embedding as described previously4,7,26,43. The retinal slices (~ 250 micron) were constantly perfused in warm oxygenated Ames medium (Sigma) and viewed under infrared illumination. Whole-cell voltage clamp recordings were performed for rod bipolar cels with ~ 10 Mohm pipettes containing intracellular solution of (in mM): 105 Cs methanesulfonate, 10 TEA-Cl, 20 HEPES, 10 EGTA, 2 QX-314, 5 Mg-ATP, 0.5 Tris-GTP and 0.1 Alexa hydrazide (~ 275 mOsm, pH ~ 7.3 with CsOH). 200 µM of GABA was dissolved with Alexa hydrazide and applied through a puff pipette (50 ms puff, Picospritzer II, General Valve). The Alexa hydrazide enabled localization of the GABA puff direction and confirmation that the puff covered the rod bipolar cell axon terminal. The cells were held at the excitatory reversal potential. 20 µM GABAzine (Sigma) and 50 µM TPMPA (Tocris (1,2,5,6-Tetrahydropyridin-4-yl) methylphosphinic acid), was used to pharmacologically isolate the GABAA and GABAC response components. Peak amplitude of the GABA puff evoked currents and the GABAA and GABAC components were estimated by calculating the difference of the baseline current (pre-stimulus) and the peak response current averaged across trials.

Serial block face scanning electron microscopy

Retina blocs were processed from the mid-central region and prepared for serial block face scanning electron microscopy as described previously7,26,43,52 after fixation in 4% glutaraldehyde in 0.1 M sodium cacodylate buffer. A Zeiss 3View serial block face scanning electron microscope was used, and images were acquired at a x-y resolution of 5 nm and a z-resolution of 50 nm. The image stack was thereafter viewed and annotated in TrakEM2 (Fiji/Image J). Rod bipolar cell terminals, included ribbon profiles and ribbon localization were identified through their characteristic ultrastructural morphological features as detailed previously7,26,43.

Retinal cell dissociation

Littermate control wildtype and rd1 mouse eyes (8 retinas from 4 animals pooled per genotype) that were crossed into the Grm6-tdtomato background were enucleated following euthanasia at postnatal day 30 and immediately transferred to ice-cold oxygenated (95% O2 / 5% CO2) Ames medium (Sigma). Enucleated eyes were dissected by removing the cornea and lens and separating the retina from the sclera, choroid and pigment epithelium. Isolated retinas were dissociated with papain (20 units/mL, 1mM L-cysteine, 0.5mM EDTA, 0.005% DNase; Worthington Biochemical) in 5mL of oxygenated (95% O2 / 5% CO2) Ames medium for 15 min at 37 °C. The remaining dissociation steps were performed on ice. 1mL of the papain solution was removed and replaced with an albumin-ovomucoid protease inhibitor solution (10 mg ovomucoid inhibitor per mL, 10 mg bovine serum albumin per mL, 0.005% DNase; Worthington Biochemical). Retinal tissue was triturated using wide-bore pipettes (Rainin) and then centrifuged at 200 x g for 20 s at 4 °C. 1 mL of the supernatant containing dissociated retinal cells was collected. The albumin-ovomucoid addition, trituration and collection steps were repeated two more times or until the retinal tissue was completely dissociated. The collected supernatant was then passed through a 40 μm mesh strainer (pluriSelect) and centrifuged at 375 x g for 10 min at 4 °C. Rod photoreceptors were depleted in the WT sample via magnetic microbead separation using a rat anti-mouse CD73 antibody (BD Biosciences), anti-rat IgG MicroBeads (Miltenyi Biotec) and large cell separation columns (Miltenyi Biotec) according to the MiniMacs Separator (Militenyi Biotec) protocol. The unlabeled effluent fraction was collected and enriched for live cells using a magnetic microbead dead cell removal kit (Miltenyi Biotec). Collected cells were resuspended in Ames medium with 4% bovine serum albumin.

Single cell RNA-sequencing and analysis

Dissociated retinal cells were submitted for high throughput single cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) at the University of Wisconsin Biotechnology Center—Gene Expression Center. The cDNA libraries were generated using the 10X Genomics workflow and sequenced on a NovaSeq X Plus Sequencing System. The scRNAseq data were analyzed by the University of Wisconsin Bioinformatics Resource Center. Cell Ranger software was used to perform demultiplexing, alignment, filtering, barcode counting, UMI counting and gene expression estimation for each sample according to 10x Genomics documentation. The gene expression estimates from each sample were aggregated using Cell ranger to compare experimental groups with normalized sequencing-depth and expression data. Further analysis was performed with R Statistical Software (v4.3.0) and Seurat (v5.0.3). Seurat objects were created with thresholds of 3 cells minimum for each gene and between 500 and 10,000 features for each cell. Rod photoreceptor, cone photoreceptor, and rod bipolar cell (RBC) clusters were identified by the expression of known markers (Rods: Rho, Pdc, Nrl, Pde6a; Cones: Arr3, Opn1mw, Opn1sw, Pde6h; RBCs: Vsx2, Otx2, Grm6, Isl1, Prkca, Car8, Sebox) (Shekhar et al., 201629). Non-parametric Wilcoxon rank sum tests were used to identity differentially expressed genes between groups.

Statistics

Statistical details about number of cells and animals for each data set are included in the respective Figure Legends. All morphology and functional data are presented as mean ± SEM (standard error of mean). A two-tailed unpaired T-test was performed to determine significance. * p < 0.05, **p < 0.01 and *** p < 0.001.
