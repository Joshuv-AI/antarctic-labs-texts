Investigation of the Structural Characteristics and Electrical Conductivity of Natural and Synthetic Coal-Derived Graphitic Carbon
Authors: Leibo Ji, Kuo Li, Yongshuo Guo, Yongkang Shang, Qinfu Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Graphitic carbon is highly valuable due to its versatile applications in industry, and coal-derived graphitic carbon represents a significant and abundant resource. Electrical conductivity is a critical parameter for its performance during utilization. In this study, the carbon structural features and electrical conductivity of both naturally graphitized coal series and synthetic series produced by high-temperature treatment were comparatively studied by using X-ray diffraction (XRD), Raman spectroscopy, high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM), etc. Structures of coal-derived graphitic carbon become more ordered with increasing metamorphic degree or pyrolysis temperature. Multiple nanoscale graphitic carbons co-occur within a single sample during graphitization, suggesting that graphitization is not a homogeneous process. The raw anthracite exhibits extremely low electrical conductivity (electrical resistivity is 1.899 × 105 Ω·cm), while electrical conductivity increases quickly as the metamorphic degree increases or when treated with high temperature. However, the electrical conductivity of coal-derived graphite (electrical resistivity is 0.017 Ω·cm for naturally graphitized; 0.141 Ω·cm for pyrolyzed) cannot compare to that of well-crystallized flake graphite (0.004 Ω·cm) and lump graphite (0.008 Ω·cm). The electrical conductivity of naturally graphitized coals is always higher than that of the high-temperature-treated series. Based on the structural and conductivity study of coal-derived graphitic carbon, three factorsgraphitization degree, nanoscale pore structure, and packed characteristics of graphitized particleswere found to affect the electrical conductivity of coal-derived graphitic carbon. This study will contribute to the design and development of conductive graphitic carbon materials from coal.

Introduction

Graphitic carbon is a ubiquitous geomaterial on Earth, and its diverse structures formed due to carbon atoms varying the configuration of their valence orbitals under different geological conditions. Graphitic carbon has also played an important role in cutting-edge technologies, such as electric vehicles, nuclear power, superconducting materials, etc. − In the energy industry, the graphite mineral takes a dominant position as an anode in lithium-ion batteries, where the graphitic carbon layer allows for the efficient intercalation and deintercalation of lithium ions. Activated carbon, also one type of graphitic carbon, is the fundamental material used in supercapacitors as the primary component of electrodes. In the rubber industry, carbon black acts as an irreplaceable reinforcing filler in rubber mixtures for tires and as a pigment. Graphitic carbon with different crystalline sizes forms a broad family of carbon black materials, allowing for different utilization purposes. , However, the traditional graphitic carbon manufacturing process is extremely energy-consuming and air-polluting. Seeking low-cost raw materials and green preparation methods will benefit the continued development of the graphitic carbon industry. ,

Coal is a natural material that consists of abundant graphitic carbon, and the graphitic carbon type varies with origins, metamorphism, and composition of coal. , Thus, coal has the potential as a natural precursor to developing graphitic carbon materials. With increasing rank, aromatic clusters of coal condense and grow, evolving into nanometric scale graphitic crystallites in high-rank coals. Although nanocrystalline graphitic carbon appears from subanthracite stages or even lower, no long-range ordered carbon can be detected in these kinds of coal, evidencing an amorphous carbon structure of coal. Wang et al. observed the nanocarbon structure of coals with rank from bituminous to anthracite by high-resolution transmission electron microscope (HRTEM). The nanographitic carbon is only with the grain size of 1–2 nm in anthracite, whereas graphitic carbon grows up into locally molecularly oriented domains (<10 nm in size) at the meta-anthracite stage, but without long-range ordered carbon appears. Once the coal stage evolves into the semigraphite and graphite stage, abundant graphitic carbon with a size of tens of nanometers shows up, revealing the formation of real coal-derived graphite. The graphitic carbon structure of coal can also be prepared through controlled high-temperature treatment; an overall progressive increasing size of the graphitic carbon lattice fringe has been observed for high-temperature-treated bituminous coal and anthracite. −

The conductivity of graphitic carbon is an important parameter for evaluating its industrial utilization. Zhang et al. proposed a screening method for coal using conductivity as a benchmark, because conductive coal must mainly comprise conductive graphitic nanograins. The electrical conductivity of graphitic carbon mainly originates from its layered graphite structure composed of sp2-hybridized carbon. In ideal graphite, carbon atoms within each graphene layer form a two-dimensional conjugated network via delocalized π electrons, giving rise to metallic-like conductivity within the basal planes, whereas the interlayer transport along the c-axis is much poorer due to weak van der Waals bonding, resulting in a highly anisotropic conduction behavior of graphitic carbon. , When the structure is highly ordered and the crystallite size is large enough in real graphite, the room-temperature electrical conductivity can reach 102–103 S·cm–1, comparable to that of metals. In contrast, in amorphous or poorly graphitized carbons, abundant sp3-hybridized carbon and structural defects interrupt the π-conjugated network, and the conductivity decreases by several orders of magnitude, showing semiconducting or poorly conducting characteristics. The conductivity of coal is strongly dependent on its graphitization degree, crystallite size, and defect density. Akamatu et al. demonstrated that the increasing treated temperature could lead to reducing interlayer spacing of condensed aromatic layers of coal, and the growth of graphite microcrystals accompanied by enhanced conductivity. Seehra et al. reported that extremely high-temperature treatment of coal drives the interlayer spacing of coal-derived graphite toward that of natural graphite and significantly improves conductivity.

Low-rank coal is almost nonconductive, whereas coal-derived graphite is a good electrical conductor. Although a lot of work has been done on the structural features and electrical conductivity of coals and their derived graphite, − however, few studies report on a broad graphitic carbon series ranging from anthracite to real graphite. In this study, the structural features and electrical conductivity of graphitic carbon both from natural and synthetic sources were studied by XRD, Raman spectroscopy, HRTEM, etc. The aim is to uncover how the structural properties of coal-derived graphitic carbon affect its electrical conductivity.

Samples and Methods

Samples

Naturally Graphitized Coals and Well-Crystallized Graphite

Six naturally graphitized coals with metamorphosed degrees from anthracite to coaly graphite were collected underground from Xinhua County of Central Hunan Province, China (Figure a,b). The coal occurrence at the Ceshui Formation of Carboniferous strata was highly altered by the Tianlongshan granite igneous intrusion during the late Indosinian period (Figure b,c). Coals from the five mines show an increased metamorphosed degree as they approach the igneous intrusion. The five mines include Jinzhushan (JZS) mine, Choumu (CM) mine, Shengli (SL) mine, Baichong (BC) mine, and Shihangli (SHL) mine (Figure b,c). Our previous study shows that the JZS sample is anthracite, CM and SL-5 samples are at the meta-anthracite rank level, SL-3 and BC samples are semigraphite samples, and the SHL sample reaches the real graphite level.

Sampling locations of the naturally graphitized coal series. (a) The location of Hunan province and the mining area; (b) the coal basin; (c) sampling mines. Reproduced from ref Copyright [2019] American Chemical Society.

For comparison, one natural coal-derived graphite from the Lutang mining area (LT), South Hunan Province, China, one well-crystallized flake graphite from the Pingdu mining area (PD), Shandong Province, China, and one well-crystallized lump graphite from Sri Lanka (SriL) were also studied. The Lutang coal graphite is known as the most graphitized coal in the world for now. It occurs in the Longtan coal-bearing formation of Permian strata, and the coal is highly altered by the Qitianling granite intrusion during the Yanshanian period. , The PD flake graphite from Shandong Province is the ancient organic carbon of pellite that has undergone a long period of middle- to high-grade regional metamorphism during the late Paleoproterozoic era. The lump graphite from Sri Lanka was formed by the precipitation of oversaturated carbon-bearing hydrothermal fluids within vein structures.

Synthetic Graphitized Coal by High-Temperature Treatment

Anthracite from the above-mentioned Jinzhushan (JZS) colliery in central Hunan Province, China (Figure b), was selected as the starting material. To investigate the structural evolution from anthracite to graphite, a series of high-temperature treatment experiments were conducted on the raw coal. The heat treatment temperatures ranged from 500 to 2900 °C, specifically at 500, 1000, 1300, 1400, 1500, 1700, 2000, 2300, 2600, and 2900 °C. Samples treated between 500 and 1500 °C were processed in a tube furnace under a nitrogen atmosphere, while the graphitization treatments from 1700 to 2900 °C were conducted in a graphitization furnace under an argon atmosphere. A constant heating rate of 10 °C/min was maintained, and samples were held at the target temperature for 3 h before cooling naturally to ambient temperature. The naming rule follows JZS plus temperature value; for instance, JZS-500 stands for JZS anthracite treated at 500 °C.

Methods

X-Ray Diffraction Analysis

X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis was performed on the powdered samples using a Rigaku D/MAX-2500PC diffractometer equipped with a Cu Ka radiation source (λ = 0.154 nm). The instrument was operated at a voltage of 40 kV and a current of 100 mA. Diffraction patterns were recorded over a scanning range of 2.5–80° (2θ) with a scanning speed of 2°/min. Peak characteristics, including position, intensity, and full width at half-maximum (FWHM), were determined using Jade software. The interlayer spacing (d 002) was calculated using Bragg’s equation based on the (002) peak position. The average crystallite sizes, specifically the stacking height (L c) and the lateral size (L a), were calculated using the Scherrer formula: L a = 1.84λ/β100 cos­(φ100) and L c = 0.9λ/β002cos­(φ002), where λ is the wavelength of the X-ray source, and β and φ represent the FWHM (in radians) and the diffraction angle of the corresponding peaks, respectively. The degree of graphitization (DOG) was calculated using the following relationship: DOG = (3.440 – d 002)/(3.440 – 3.354).

Raman Spectroscopy

Raman spectroscopic study was performed using a high-resolution confocal micro-Raman spectrometer (WITec alpha 300R) equipped with a 532 nm excitation laser. The laser beam was focused onto the sample through a 50× long-working-distance objective lens. To prevent thermal damage or photo-oxidation of the sample surface during irradiation, the laser power was strictly maintained at a low level (approximately 0.6 mW). Raman signals were recorded over a spectral range of 800–3500 cm–1, covering both the first-order (800–2000 cm–1) and second-order (2200–3400 cm–1) regions of carbonaceous materials. Prior to the measurements, the system was calibrated using the characteristic peak of a standard silicon wafer at 520.4 cm–1.

For data analysis, the raw spectra were subjected to baseline correction and peak deconvolution using commercial curve-fitting software. The first-order Raman region was typically fitted with five subbands (D, D2, D3, D4, and G) to accurately characterize the structural evolution according to the methods of Sadezky et al. The Gaussian line shape was applied to the D3 band, while the Lorentzian line shape was used for the G, D, D2, and D4 bands. Structural parameters, including the peak position, full width at half-maximum (FWHM), intensity ratio of the D to G band (R 1 = I D/I G), and integrated area ratio of the D to G band (R 2 = A D/A G) were extracted to quantitatively evaluate the degree of structural ordering and graphitization.

SEM Analysis

The surface morphologies and microtextural characteristics of the samples were investigated using a Hitachi SU8020 field-emission scanning electron microscope (SEM). Prior to imaging, the powdered samples were mounted onto an aluminum stub by using conductive carbon tape. To prevent surface charging and enhance the secondary electron signal emission, a thin layer of gold was sputter-coated onto the sample surfaces. Imaging was conducted at varying magnifications to capture carbon features ranging from the micrometer to submicrometer scale. To ensure that the observations represented the bulk sample, a minimum of ten randomly selected fields of view were examined for each sample.

TEM Analysis

Nanometric structural evolution was characterized using an FEI Tecnai G2 F30 transmission electron microscope (TEM) operating at an accelerating voltage of 300 kV. For sample preparation, the materials were hand-ground to a fine powder (<200 mesh) and dispersed in ethanol. The mixture was ultrasonicated for 10–30 min to ensure a uniform suspension and to break up agglomerates. A droplet of the suspension was then deposited onto a holey carbon-coated copper grid and allowed to dry under an infrared lamp. High-resolution TEM (HRTEM) images were acquired at magnifications of 300,000× and 500,000× to directly visualize the lattice fringes and stacking order of the carbon layers. Quantitative analysis of the lattice fringe parameters was subsequently performed using ImageJ image processing software.

Electrical Conductivity Measurements

The electrical conductivity was measured to systematically assess the electrical transport properties across the samples with varying degrees of graphitization. Before the conductivity measurement, each powder sample was mixed with potassium bromide (KBr) at a mass ratio of 1:1 to make a pellet. The sample and KBr mixture were mechanically ground thoroughly to ensure that the sample was dispersed among KBr homogeneously. For each pellet, 400 mg of the mixture was compacted by a cylindrical mold with a diameter of 1 mm. Compaction was performed using a hydraulic powder press, where a uniaxial pressure of 20 MPa was applied and held constant for a dwell time of 3 min. The electrical resistivity was subsequently measured on these consolidated pellets at room temperature using a 4-probe analyzer.

Results and Discussion

XRD Analysis

The XRD patterns of a series of natural graphitic carbon samples are shown in Figure a. The diffraction pattern shapes of the anthracite sample (JZS) and meta-anthracite samples (CM and SL-5) show a broad band at ∼26° (2θ), indicating that these samples have an amorphous carbon structure. A comparison of the structural parameters between the JZS sample and the CM-5 and SL-5 samples shows no significant changes in L c value, which is in the range of 1.5–2.5 nm, whereas the L a value increases from 4.59 to 9.14 nm, and there is no significant variation in the interlayer spacing (d 002 in Table ). The diffraction peaks of semigraphite samples SL-3 and BC become stronger and sharper than those of anthracite, indicating that the arrangement of carbon atoms in semigraphite samples gets ordered. Compared to the anthracite samples, the semigraphite samples (SL-3 and BC) exhibit a notably smaller interlayer spacing d 002, along with a significant increase in the structural parameters L a and L c (Table ). For the highly graphitized coal samples SHL and LT, their diffraction peaks at (10) split into two obvious secondary subpeaks (100) and (101), indicating that these samples developed a three-dimensionally ordered crystalline structure. Raman Spectroscopy Analysis

Raman spectroscopy was applied to unveil the microstructural evolution of carbonaceous materials during a natural metamorphic series and high-temperature treatment. In the first-order Raman region (800–2000 cm–1), all samples except Sri Lanka graphite exhibit two distinct bands centered at ∼1350 cm–1 (D band) and ∼1580 cm–1 (G band), respectively (Figure a). The D band is a defect-induced mode, whereas the G band originates from the in-plane stretching vibration of sp2-hybridized carbon atoms. The second-order Raman spectral bands of carbon materials at 2400–3500 cm–1 are attributed to the double frequency (2D) or combination frequency (D+G) of first-order Raman bands. The raw coal sample (JZS) shows broad D (FWHM = 121 cm–1, Table ) and G bands, indicative of an amorphous carbon structure. When the coal metamorphic level reaches the meta-anthracite stage (samples CM and SL-5), the D band becomes sharper and stronger than the G band. Once the coal metamorphic level reaches to semigraphite stage, a pronounced 2D band shows up at ∼2700 cm–1, indicating long-range ordered carbon structure appears. From meta-anthracite (SL-5) to the most graphitized coal (LT), the relative intensity of the D band shows a continuous decreasing trend from 1.86 to 0.43, suggesting that the carbon structure becomes ordered gradually. The D band of well-crystallized Sri Lanka graphite disappears, and a clear split of the 2D peak appears, suggesting a very well-graphitized carbon order in this sample.

Figure b presents the Raman spectra for the high-temperature-treated series (from JZS-500 to JZS-2900). In the first-order Raman region (800–2000 cm–1), the samples treated at different temperatures exhibit distinct bands centered at ∼1350 cm–1 (D band) and ∼1580 cm–1 (G band). Similar to the raw coal sample (JZS), the low-temperature treated sample (JZS-500) shows broad D (FWHM = 111 cm–1) and G bands, and the relative intensity of the D band becomes stronger than that of the raw JZS anthracite. When the treatment temperature reaches 1000–1500 °C, the D band becomes sharper and stronger than the G band. Specifically, for the JZS-1500 sample, the intensity ratio of the D and G bands (R 1) reaches a maximum of 2.45. This result suggests that the cracking and condensation of the coal macromolecular structure eliminated heteroatoms and generated abundant nanoscale aromatic clusters, consequently increasing the density of edge defects of graphitic carbon in coal. Once the temperature reaches 1700 °C and above, the Raman spectral features show a decrease in the relative intensity of the D band, indicating that the sample has entered the graphitization stage. From JZS-1700 to JZS-2900, the R 1 value decreases from 1.38 to 0.35, and the FWHM of the G band narrows from 57 to 21 cm–1 (Table ). Simultaneously, a pronounced 2D band appears and strengthens, suggesting that long-range ordered graphitic carbon emerges starting from JZS-1700. For the samples treated at 2600 and 2900 °C (JZS-2600 and JZS-2900), the 2D band is not only intensified but also exhibits a clear splitting, suggesting that a well-ordered graphitic carbon structure has formed under treatment at extremely high temperature conditions.

SEM Analysis

Under scanning electron microscopy, the anthracite sample (JZS) primarily exhibits a dense shape of coal particles with uneven surfaces (Figure a). In contrast, the meta-anthracite sample (CM) starts showing distinct layer-like textures within dense coal particles (Figure b), with the layered features becoming progressively clearer from anthracite to semigraphite samples (Figure c,d). In the semigraphite sample SL-3, tiny graphite flakes are observed among the dense coal particles (Figure d), indicating that the tiny flake graphite grows from the layer-like carbon structure of coal. Meanwhile, in the highly graphitized sample SHL, irregularly shaped graphite crystals are clearly visible, distributed chaotically (Figure e), and no dense coal particles are observed. For the most graphitized coal sample from the Lutang mining area, the single-crystal size becomes larger and more round-shaped than that of the SHL sample (Figure f).

The morphological characteristics of the high-temperature-treated anthracite samples observed under scanning electron microscopy are shown in Figure . After high-temperature treatment, the JZS-500 sample still exhibits dense, fractured coal particles with jagged surface textures and irregular geometric contours (Figure a). For the JZS-1000 sample, the layer-like structure of dense coal tends to be formed (Figure b). TEM Analysis

The HRTEM technique was applied to gain nanometric-scale insight into the structural evolution of graphitic carbon from coal to coal-derived graphite. For the JZS anthracite sample, the graphitic lattice fringes, with size of ∼1 nm and 2–3 stacked layers, were randomly distributed in the matrix (Figure a). These condensed aromatic rings are the basic structural units (BSUs) of carbonaceous materials, and the blurred halos in the selected-area electron diffraction (SAED) pattern (Figure a inserted) suggest an amorphous carbon structure of anthracite. When coal was metamorphosed into the meta-anthracite stage (CM and SL-5 samples), the lattice fringe size grows up into microcolumns (Figure b) that were clearly larger than those of anthracite, and the microcolumns aligned along a preferential direction to form local molecularly oriented domains (LMOD) (Figure c). In these domains, the nanometric-sized BSUs tend to coalesce with each other laterally and vertically (the dashed line indicated in Figure c). But, the SAED pattern collected on these LMOD areas is still ring type, evidencing that no long-range ordered carbon occurred. The kinked lamellar textures are observed more and more prevalently in samples SL-3, BC, SHL, and LT (Figure d–f). These lamellae consist of graphene layers with hundreds of nanometers in extension and tens of stacked layers, and the appearance of (112) reflections of the SAED patterns (Figure d) suggests triperiodic order in these graphitic lamellae. The average diameters of graphitic lamellae increase with the degree of graphitization from SL-3 (semigraphite) to LT (the most graphitized coals in the world) samples. For the most graphitized LT sample, the graphitic lamellae grow up into submicron scale (Figure f), and the SAED pattern shows clear scattered points. It should be noted that a few amorphous carbons coexist in the highly graphitized coals.

High-resolution transmission electron microscopy of naturally graphitized coals. (a) JZS; (b) CM; (c) SL; (d) BC; (e) SHL; and (f) LT.

For the high-temperature treated series, the carbon structures of JZS-500 did not change much compared with those of the JZS anthracite sample (Figure a). The lattice fringes grow up into microcolumn in the JZS-1000 sample (Figure b). With the treated temperature increase, the microcolumns grow up into LMODs like those in the natural series (Figures and c). In these LMODs, the graphitic carbon domains are also bonded together by weakly connected fringes laterally or stacked vertically, and a lot of lattice defects occurs along the adjacent microcolumns. As the microcolumns oriented preferentially and grew in size with increasing treatment temperature, the arc-type SAED pattern appeared in sample JZS-1700 (Figure d). This phenomenon has been observed previously in long-range winkled layers. As the treated temperature increased, stiff stacked graphene layers can be seen on the scale of tens of nanometers (Figure e). The SAED collected on the graphitic carbons shows diffraction points, suggesting that real graphite had formed, but the discrete SAED points tended to connect into cycles, showing that the polycrystals occur together. The stiff stacked graphitic carbon formed as walls of prevalent nanoscale pores in the high-temperature treated samples (Figure e), although part of the amorphous carbon may still remain inside the pores. For the anthracite treated with extremely high temperature (sample JZS-2900), the clearly scattered points of SAED patterns were observably (Figure f), showing that relatively large single graphite crystal formed. It should be noted that, even in the JZS-2900 sample, amorphous carbon was still preserved, suggesting a heterogeneous nature of graphitized coals.

The high-temperature treated coal series samples also show an increase in conductivity with increasing treatment temperature (Table , and the red hollow circle in Figure b). For the JZS-500 sample, its electrical conductivity is still maintained at a low level (electrical resistivity is 7253 Ω·cm), although it shows a sharp increase compared to the raw anthracite. The continued increase in treatment temperature does not significantly improve the conductivity of graphitized coal, even for the JZS-2600 and JZS-2900 samples, their electrical conductivity is only comparable to those of meta-anthracite and semigraphite samples of naturally graphitized coals (Table ).

Discussion

High-resolution TEM analysis reveals that raw anthracite consists primarily of short, condensed aromatic fringes (basic structural units, BSUs), and these BSUs are interconnected by a minor amount of aliphatic carbon, heteroatoms, and ligands, as detected by FTIR and chemical composition previously. This structure, featuring small graphitic domains separated by insulating sp3-hybridized carbon, results in very low electrical conductivity. While low pyrolysis temperatures (≤500 °C) induce only slight carbon structural changes in the raw anthracite, significant structural evolution begins around 1000 °C. At this low-temperature pyrolysis stage (500–1000 °C), aliphatic and aromatic hydrocarbons decompose, releasing gases and creating pores observable via SEM. Between 1000 and 1500 °C, the conductive BSUs extend laterally and stack vertically, forming microcolumns and locally molecular oriented domains (LMODs), with the average crystalline size increasing with temperature (Table ). The electrical conductivity of pyrolyzed coal typically increases rapidly with heat treatment up to ∼1000 °C, then plateaus with increased temperature, as reported by Barnakov et al., which is consistent with the data measured in the current study.

With sufficient thermal treatment from 1600 to 2900 °C, graphitic carbon nanostructures become numerous enough to establish, lamellar textures emerge under SEM, and long-range ordered graphitic carbon is clearly observed under HRTEM, although a certain amount of amorphous and turbostratic phases is still preserved. However, the conductivity of graphitized coal, no matter whether naturally or synthetically derived, does not show a significant increase with continued treatment temperature or metamorphosed degree. The measured electrical resistivity value for the coal-derived graphitic carbon was in the range of 0.052–0.493 Ω·cm (Table ), which is close to silicon-based semiconductors. Compared to the electrical conductivity of natural, well-crystallized graphite, 1 order of magnitude was lower for the coal-derived graphitic carbons, suggesting that the graphitization degree plays the main role in controlling the conductivity of graphitic carbon.

The conductivity of coal-derived graphite carbons is not only affected by their degree of graphitization. Naturally graphitized coals often exhibit higher conductivity than their synthetic counterparts (Figure ). For example, the natural amorphous SL-5 sample possesses an electrical conductivity similar to that of the heated sample JZS-2900, which is highly graphitized as indicated from its structural parameters (Table ). Two reasons could explain the electrical conductivity difference between natural coal-derived graphitic carbon and the synthetic series. The first reason is that a lot of nanoscale pores can be observed in the high-temperature-treated coals (Figure e). These results are consistent with the Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) analysis reported by Zhang et al. , The specific surface area (SSA) of naturally graphitized coal is almost 1 order of magnitude smaller than that of synthetic graphitized coals treated at high temperatures. These pore structures (although amorphous carbon may occur inside) will cause electron movement only in the graphitic carbon around pore walls and reduce the number of intercrystallite contact points (Figure ). The second reason is the loosely packed dense particles in natural coal-derived graphitic carbon compared to the synthetic series. This is probably related to the geological compaction process that makes the natural series samples easily crushed into loosely packed powders, whereas the high-temperature series shows tightly packed particles after pyrolysis. The tightly packed features of high-temperature-treated coals are also explained by the fact that they are much more difficult to crush than naturally graphitized coals. For the high-temperature-treated coals, the discrete particles with independent crystalline micelles lead to high contact resistance at the KBr matrix. In contrast, naturally loose-packed graphitized coals form a continuous mass, where micelles are bridged by physical contact at the KBr matrix, reducing boundary scattering and yielding lower resistivity (Figure ).

Three factors affect the electrical conductivity of coal-derived graphitic carbon.

Conclusions

1Anthracite is a type of graphitic carbon with extremely low electrical conductivity; the small graphitic carbon domains of anthracite were separated by insulating sp3-hybridized carbon. Once coal rank reaches the meta-anthracite stage, its electrical resistivity value decreases to <3 Ω·cm. For naturally graphitized coals, metamorphosed degree reaching up to meta-anthracite and above are good electrically conductive graphitic carbon materials.

But, the conductivity of extremely high-temperature-treated coal is only comparable to that of natural meta-anthracite samples.
