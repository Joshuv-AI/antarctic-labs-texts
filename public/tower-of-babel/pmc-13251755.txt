Comparative analysis of expression, immunogenicity, and safety profiles between linear and circular RNA vaccine platforms
Authors: Soo-Yeon Lee, Jisun Lee, Jae-Hun Ahn, Seok-Yong Lee, Eun-Jin Choi, Dahyeon Ha, Ayoung Oh, Sohee Jo, Subin Yoon, Seonghyun Lee, Hee-Jin Bae, Na-Young Lee, Hyewon Youn, Byeong-Cheol Kang, Jae-Hwan Nam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractmRNA vaccines have revolutionized development and continue to advance through clinical and research applications. Circular RNA is emerging as a promising alternative to linear RNA. However, no direct comparison has identified the optimal RNA platform for mRNA-based drug development. This study compares linear and circular RNA platforms in vitro and in vivo, focusing on expression dynamics, immune responses, and safety. In terms of expression efficiency, the circular RNA showed prolonged expression compared to linear RNA. The immunogenicity was evaluated using influenza HA as an antigen. In humoral immune response, linear RNA with modified nucleosides (Lin-m1Ψ) and circular RNA with unmodified nucleosides (Circ-WT) showed higher neutralizing antibody titers than linear RNA with unmodified nucleosides (Lin-WT). Lin-m1Ψ and Lin-WT induced slightly higher cell-mediated immune responses than Circ-WT. In mice, all RNA types showed a decrease in reticulocytes, monocytes, and alkaline phosphatase on day 2 following high-dose intramuscular injection. Furthermore, Lin-WT and Circ-WT showed greater decreases in platelets than Lin-m1Ψ. However, these changes recovered to normal by day 14 post-vaccination in all groups. These findings provide insights into developing RNA-based immunotherapies and selecting suitable RNA platforms for various applications, including preventive and therapeutic vaccines as well as protein-replacement therapies.Keywords: MT: Oligonucleotides:therapies and applications, mRNA platform, circular RNA, linear RNA, immunogenicity, vaccine

Introduction

Messenger RNA (mRNA) vaccines have transitioned from a novel concept to an integral platform in modern vaccine development, recognized for their rapid production capabilities and versatility.1,2 This platform exploits the ability of mRNA to directly encode specific antigens, thereby eliciting a robust immune response while mitigating the risk of genomic integration by confining mRNA delivery to the cytoplasm.3 The modularity of mRNA technology permits rapid reconfiguration in response to emerging pathogens, making it a versatile tool in combating infectious diseases.4 In most current RNA vaccine platforms, the RNA is encapsulated in lipid nanoparticles (LNPs), which protect the RNA from extracellular degradation, facilitate cellular uptake through endocytosis, and promote endosomal escape to enable efficient cytoplasmic delivery and translation. These delivery systems are therefore essential for enhancing the stability, bioavailability, and immunogenicity of RNA-based vaccines.5

Linear RNA platforms have been widely used in vaccine development, particularly in coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) vaccines, due to their ability to drive rapid and transient protein expression.6,7 These platforms are designed with 5′- and 3'- untranslated regions (UTRs), a cap structure, and a poly(A) tail, with the incorporated nucleosides being either unmodified or chemically modified to enhance stability and effectiveness.2 Unmodified linear RNA platform (Lin-WT), while capable of inducing a strong immune response, is highly susceptible to degradation by cellular ribonucleases and can trigger innate immune activation, leading to reduced translational efficiency and increased potential toxicity.3 Moreover, RNA synthesized using unmodified nucleosides is degraded more rapidly than RNA synthesized using N1-methylpseudouridine (m1Ψ) due to the action of TRIM25, a recently identified proton-sensing factor that mediates exogenous RNA surveillance and degradation.8 To address these issues, modified nucleosides have been introduced, which significantly improve antigen expression while mitigating immune-driven degradation and adverse effects.3,4 Consequently, the modified linear RNA platform (Lin-m1Ψ) has been adopted as the standard for commercial RNA vaccines.

Circular RNA platform presents a promising alternative that may be superior to Lin-m1Ψ in certain applications. Unlike a linear RNA platform that relies on cap analogs, unmodified circular RNA platform (Circ-WT) is generated via the permuted intron-exon (PIE) system and forms a closed-loop structure containing an internal ribosome entry site (IRES), offering inherent stability and prolonged protein production.9 Circ-WT is formed through the PIE system, which utilizes the self-splicing principle of group I catalytic introns.10 This system has a structure in which the 3′ and 5' sequences of introns are rearranged to flank exons. This structural characteristic allows transcripts to self-splice without the need for additional enzymes, forming a stable circular structure. Recent studies have demonstrated the use of Circ-WT in various therapeutic applications, including gene therapy and protein replacement therapies, due to its enhanced durability and translational efficiency.9,11 Despite its potential, additional research is needed to fully assess its functional implications and clinical applicability, especially as RNA technology continues to evolve beyond traditional formats.

Therefore, we investigated the potential of Circ-WT as an alternative to both Lin-WT and Lin-m1Ψ. Recent studies have shown that Lin-WT strongly activates innate immune sensors such as RIG-I (retinoic acid-inducible gene I), which triggers cytokine production and increases degradation and toxicity.3 In contrast, the incorporation of modified nucleosides reduces innate immune activation and modulates the pattern of IFN-γ and cytokine production, thereby mitigating these immune-related effects.4,11 To investigate these differences, we performed a head-to-head evaluation of Lin-WT, Lin-m1Ψ, and Circ-WT. Their expression efficiency and immune-stimulatory profiles were characterized through in vitro and in vivo experiments. Understanding the unique characteristics of each RNA platform may help to select the most appropriate option for prevention or treatment.

Results

Comparison of in vitro and in vivo expression profiles of linear and circular RNA platforms

To assess the protein expression efficiency of unmodified linear RNA (Lin-WT), modified linear RNA (Lin-m1Ψ), and unmodified circular RNA (Circ-WT) platforms, we synthesized RNA encoding firefly luciferase (FLuc) through in vitro transcription (IVT) (Figure 1A). We utilized linear and circular RNA constructs with specific schematic designs (Figures S1A and S1B).12,13 The integrity of the RNA was verified via electrophoresis (Figure S1C). To confirm circularization, cDNA was synthesized from the circular RNA produced, and the junction site was amplified by PCR. The PCR product was then sequenced to confirm the circularization of circular RNA (Figure S1D). To evaluate the purity of the three RNA types, we quantified residual double-stranded RNA (dsRNA) levels. Dot blot analysis using the J2 antibody revealed that dsRNA levels were below 6.25 ng in all samples (2.5 μg total RNA loading), representing less than 0.25% of the total RNA input (Figure S1E). To further validate purification efficiency, a dsRNA enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) was performed (Figure S1F). While Lin-WT exhibited relatively higher residual dsRNA prior to purification, and Circ-WT showed signals likely originating from incomplete transcripts or circularization byproducts, cellulose-based purification effectively reduced these levels. Notably, the purified Lin-WT and Circ-WT platforms reached dsRNA levels comparable to those of the modified Lin-m1Ψ platform. These results demonstrate that all RNA preparations were sufficiently purified to minimize nonspecific immune responses associated with dsRNA contaminants.

Subsequently, we measured FLuc expression in HeLa (human cervical cancer), HEK293 (human embryonic kidney cell), and Jurkat (human lymphoma) cell lines transfected with type of RNA (Lin-WT, Lin-m1Ψ, and Circ-WT). The expression levels were normalized to a baseline at 3 h and plotted over time (Figures 1B–1D). In adherent cell lines (HeLa, HEK293), both unmodified and modified linear RNA expressions increased for the initial 6 h, followed by a decline after 24 h. In contrast, circular RNA expression increased consistently across all platforms, peaking at 24 h and decreasing gradually (Figures 1B and 1C). In suspension cells, such as Jurkat cells, both unmodified and modified linear RNA showed increased expression between 6 and 24 h, followed by a decline. In contrast, circular RNA exhibited a sustained increase in FLuc expression immediately post-transfection, maintaining high levels for 3 days before gradually decreasing (Figure 1D). We also evaluated the in vivo expression of the three platforms. Unmodified and modified linear RNA and circular RNA encoding FLuc encapsulated in LNPs were injected into mice intramuscularly or intravenously, and FLuc expression was evaluated (Figure 2A). The size of mRNA-LNPs produced by microfluidic systems was approximately 80 nm, and the encapsulation efficiency was >90% (Figures S2A–S2D). Consistent with the in vitro results, circular RNA exhibited the highest expression levels and showed prolonged expression, lasting approximately 1 day longer than the expression of both unmodified and modified linear RNA. Intramuscular (i.m.) injections led to expression in both the liver and at the injection site, with most expression localized to the injection site (Figures 2B–2D). Following i.v. injections, luminescence imaging showed widespread distribution of FLuc expression, with a significantly stronger intensity in the liver (Figures 2E and 2F). However, this expression showed a stronger but transient luminescent signal in the liver (Figure 2F). Thus, in i.m. injection, circular RNA expression was higher and more prolonged than linear RNA. Furthermore, in i.v. injection, circular RNA showed higher expression than linear RNA but the difference in expression duration was not significant.

To evaluate the potential of three RNA platforms for therapeutic protein delivery, we monitored the concentration of human immunoglobulin G (IgG) in serum (Figure 3A). The linear RNA (Lin-WT and Lin m1ψ) exhibited rapid expression, reaching peak concentrations at 6 h post-injection. Notably, Lin-m1ψ showed slightly higher initial expression levels compared with Lin-WT. In contrast, Circ-WT demonstrated a distinct kinetic characterized by a progressive increase in human IgG levels up to 72 h. This sustained expression resulted in higher cumulative protein levels over the observed period compared with the linear RNA (Figure 3B). These results indicate that while linear RNAs are suitable for rapid induction, the circular platform offers a significant advantage in maintaining stable protein levels, suggesting its potential for antibody or protein replacement therapies.

Effects of linear and circular RNA platform type on the humoral immune response

Type of RNA (Lin-WT, Lin-m1Ψ, and Circ-WT) encoding hemagglutinin (HA) antigen was transfected using lipofectamine and found to be well expressed in vitro (Figure S3A). To investigate the humoral immune response elicited by the type of RNA platform, we encapsulated Lin-WT, Lin-m1Ψ, and Circ-WT encoding the HA antigen from the influenza PR8 virus into LNP and evaluated the antibody response. Balb/c mice were intramuscularly immunized twice at 2-week intervals with 10 μg of each HA-encoding RNA platform or DPBS (Dulbecco's phosphate-buffered saline) (negative control). Two weeks after the second injection, serum samples were collected to assess the antibody response (Figure 4A). The mice showed no significant difference in hemagglutination inhibition (HI) titer (Figure 4B). Interestingly, both Lin-m1Ψ and Circ-WT exhibited superior neutralizing ability compared to Lin-WT in the microneutralization titer (Figure 4C). Endpoint titers of total IgG, IgG1, and IgG2a were measured by ELISA. All RNA platforms effectively induced antibody titers but the RNA platform-dependent efficacy was not significant. (Figures 4D–4F). These results suggest that circular RNA may serve as a viable alternative to linear RNA, which is widely used in commercial vaccines, in terms of neutralizing antibody induction.

Effects of linear and circular RNA platform type on cell-mediated immune response

We analyzed CD8 and CD4 T cell responses to assess the effect of different RNA platforms on cell-mediated immunity. Balb/c mice were immunized twice at 2-week intervals with 10 μg of each HA-encoding RNA platforms via i.m. injection (Figure 5A). Following immunization, splenocytes were isolated and analyzed for interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) secreting cells using the ELISpot assay. All HA-encoding RNA platforms induced IFN-γ secretion. Although Circ-WT showed slightly lower IFN-γ levels than the other platforms, the difference was not statistically significant (Figures 5B and 5C). To further evaluate cytokine responses, CD8 and CD4 T cells secreting cytokines were analyzed using flow cytometry. Lin-WT and Lin-m1Ψ induced similar proportions of IFN-γ, interleukin-2 (IL-2), and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α)-positive CD8 and CD4 T cells (Figures 5D–5I). In contrast, Circ-WT exhibited a distinct immune profile that was not significantly different from that of control. Circ-WT exhibited a lower percentage of IFN-γ secreting CD8 T cells than Lin-WT (Figure 5D). Notably, in contrast to the significant induction of cytokine secreted CD8 T cells by both linear RNAs, Circ-WT elicited a response that was not significantly different from the control, indicating a more controlled immune profile (Figure 5E). The secretion patterns of IFN-γ, IL-2, and TNF-α in CD4+ T cells were also analyzed (Figures 5G–5I). Notably, only Circ-WT elicited a significant increase in IFN-γ secretion, whereas the linear platforms showed no significant difference from the control (Figure 5G). In contrast, a significant elevation in IL-2 secretion was observed only with Lin-WT, while both Lin-m1ψ and Circ-WT did not significantly induce its production (Figure 5H). For TNF-α, although an overall increasing trend was observed across RNA-treated groups, only selected conditions reached statistical significance compared with the DPBS control, indicating a more modest and variable induction pattern among the platforms (Figure 5I).

A broader analysis of CD8 and CD4 T cells co-expressing two cytokines revealed distinct immune activation profiles among the platforms (Figures 5J and 5K). In the CD8 T cell, while all RNA platforms increased the frequency of IFN-γ +/TNF-α+ cells compared with the control, only the linear RNA platforms (Lin-WT and Lin-m1ψ) significantly induced IL-2-co-expressing populations (IFN-γ +/IL-2+ and TNF-α+/IL-2+). In contrast, Circ-WT showed no significant difference from the control in these IL-2-producing polyfunctional groups, reinforcing its more controlled activation profile (Figure 5J). Interestingly, for double-positive CD4 T cells, Circ-WT consistently elicited significant increases across all cytokine combinations, whereas Lin-WT did not reach statistical significance due to higher variability (Figure 5K). These results indicate that overall, Circ-WT shows a slightly lower level of CD8 T cell activation than Lin-WT and Lin-m1Ψ, which should be considered in the development of prophylactic and therapeutic vaccines.

Differential innate immune responses depending on mRNA platform type

To assess the innate immune response induced by three RNA platforms, A549 cells were transfected with 2 μg RNA, and innate immune-related gene expressions including RIG-I, interferon-beta (IFN-β), TNF-α, and interleukin-6 (IL-6) were measured 6 h post-transfection. Lin-WT induced the highest levels of all cytokines, while Circ-WT also induced higher levels of RIG-I and IFN-β than Lin-m1Ψ (Figures S4A–S4E). These results indicate that both Lin-WT and Circ-WT, using unmodified nucleosides, induce higher levels of inflammatory cytokines than Lin-m1Ψ. However, even with wild-type uridine, Circ-WT induced lower inflammatory cytokines compared with Lin-WT. This suggests the need for further investigation into the impact of these inflammatory cytokines on safety.

Following in vitro analysis, in vivo testing was conducted using Balb/c mice inoculated with three RNA platform encapsulated in LNP. Cytokine responses were assessed using serum and lymph nodes at 6 and 24 h post-inoculation (Figure 6A). Lin-WT induced significant increases in IFN-γ, RIG-I, IFN-β, TNF-α, and IL-6 levels at both time points (Figures 6B–6G). Circ-WT also induced the expression of IFN-γ, though at lower levels than those induced by Lin-WT, but comparable to those induced by Lin-m1Ψ (Figures 6B and 6C). RIG-I expression increased significantly in all RNA platforms compared with the control, with no significant differences between the platforms (Figure 6D). Lin-m1Ψ did not significantly alter IFN-β levels at either time point, while Circ-WT had no significant effect on IFN-β expression (Figure 6E). Notably, TNF-α levels induced by Circ-WT were significantly lower than those induced by Lin-WT at 24 h (Figure 6F). Similarly, IL-6 levels induced by both Circ-WT and Lin-m1Ψ were significantly lower than those induced by Lin-WT and comparable to those in control (Figure 6G), indicating reduced immunotoxicity—a key concern for RNA vaccines. These in vivo results show that, as expected, Lin-WT induced the strongest pro-inflammatory cytokines, whereas Circ-WT, despite also containing unmodified nucleosides, induced lower levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines.

Safety assessment across the mRNA platform types

Female CrlOri: CD1 (ICR) mice were intramuscularly immunized twice at 2-week intervals with 100 μg/head of each RNA platform (Figure 7A). Mice were sacrificed at 2 days post-inoculation (dpi) or at 14 dpi following the second dose. The collected samples were analyzed to assess blood counts and tissue health, enabling a comprehensive evaluation of safety and potential toxicity for each RNA platform (Figure 7A).

In the hematological analysis, no significant changes in red blood cell (RBC) counts were observed across any RNA platform at either 2 or 14 dpi (Figure 7B). However, reticulocyte levels were significantly lower at 2 dpi across all RNA platforms but recovered to baseline levels by 14 dpi, with no significant differences among the platforms (Figure 7C). Similarly, monocyte levels decreased significantly at 2 dpi in all mice administered RNA platforms but recovered to baseline levels by 14 dpi, with no significant difference observed across the platforms (Figure 7D). The administration of both Lin-WT and Circ-WT significantly decreased platelet counts at 2 dpi, while Lin-m1Ψ administration did not change significantly. By 14 dpi, platelet levels recovered across all platforms to levels comparable to those in the control (Figure 7E).

In the hepatological analysis, albumin (ALB, a liver-synthesized protein indicating synthetic function) levels were significantly reduced following the administration of all RNA platforms at 2 dpi but by 14 dpi, only mice administered Lin-WT showed significantly lower levels, while those administered Lin-m1Ψ and Circ-WT returned to baseline (Figure 7F). Alkaline phosphatase (ALP) levels, an enzyme marker for bile duct function and hepatobiliary damage, were significantly reduced across all platforms at 2 dpi; by 14 dpi, mice administered Circ-WT exhibited significantly higher ALP levels compared with the control (DPBS), with no significant differences in the mice administered Lin-m1Ψ or Lin-WT (Figure 7G). Alanine aminotransferase (ALT) levels, an enzyme reflecting hepatocyte injury, remained stable, with no significant changes across all RNA platforms at either time point (Figure 7H). In contrast, aspartate aminotransferase (AST) levels, an enzyme indicating liver cell injury and possibly muscle injury, were significantly elevated in both mice administered Lin-m1Ψ and Circ-WT at 2 dpi compared with the control; however, by 14 dpi, no significant differences were noted in any of the platforms (Figure 7I).

Histological analysis revealed changes in tissues from the bone marrow, muscle (injection site), spleen, and lymph nodes at 2 dpi, with corresponding histological scores indicating the severity of the observed changes (Figures 7J–7N). Notably, a significant decrease in erythroid cells was observed in the bone marrow tissue of all mice (Figure 7K). Moreover, moderate inflammatory changes were observed at the injection site (femoral muscle) in all mice administered the RNA platforms (Figure 7L). Spleen tissue showed increased cellularity and megakaryocytes in all mice administered the RNA platforms (Figure 7M). Lymphadenopathy was also observed in all mice within the experimental groups; however, severe inflammation and necrosis were most pronounced in mice administered Lin-WT. Mice administered Lin-m1Ψ and Circ-WT showed significantly fewer inflammatory lesions in the lymph nodes than those administered Lin-WT (Figure 7N). These findings provide a comprehensive overview of the hematological, hepatological, and histopathological effects associated with the different RNA platforms, demonstrating transient changes and platform-specific differences in tissue response.

Discussion

The rapid development and deployment of mRNA vaccines, particularly during the COVID-19 pandemic, have demonstrated their transformative potential in vaccinology.14 As mRNA vaccine platforms continue to evolve, efforts are centered on optimizing their efficacy, stability, and safety.2 This study provides a direct comparative analysis of linear RNA and circular RNA platforms, highlighting their advantages and limitations. Our findings offer valuable insights into key challenges in vaccine and therapeutics development, including sustained antigen expression, immune activation, and safety considerations.

In this study, we demonstrated that Circ-WT outperforms linear RNA platforms in terms of protein expression duration. In both in vitro and in vivo models, Circ-WT exhibited prolonged antigen expression, likely due to its closed-loop structure, which confers resistance to exonuclease degradation, which is a persistent issue with the linear RNA platform.15,16 This extended expression resulted in slightly higher cumulative protein levels over time compared with linear RNA. Our results with human IgG further validate the potential of circular RNA as a promising modality for therapeutic protein expression.

In terms of immune responses as vaccine, both circular and linear RNA platforms elicited comparable humoral immunity, as determined by neutralizing antibody titers. However, immune activation must be carefully balanced with safety considerations. We focused on transcriptional cytokine analysis in the draining lymph nodes to capture the rapid and transient innate immune signatures inherent to the RNA platforms. The selected time points and the use of a low-immunogenic reporter (FLuc) allowed for a sensitive evaluation of platform-specific effects, independent of antigen-driven interference. This strategy effectively identifies early immunological triggering that precedes detectable protein-level changes. Unmodified linear RNA induced higher levels of IFN-β, TNF-α, and IL-6, reflecting stronger innate immune activation.3,17 This aligns with the known RIG-I-mediated recognition of linear RNA, particularly its 5′-triphosphate and double-stranded regions, which activate downstream signaling pathways leading to cytokine production.18,19 Notably, while RIG-I expression increased across all platforms, cytokine induction was lower in circular RNA and modified linear RNA, suggesting that RIG-I activation alone does not fully determine inflammatory response intensity and that secondary structural features and chemical modifications play a critical role.3 These insights enhance our understanding of how RNA structural properties influence immune regulation. Recent studies have emphasized the importance of structural optimization and analytical evaluation in next-generation RNA therapeutics. Computational modeling is increasingly vital for circular RNA platform development. For instance, graph-based structural modeling enables precise prediction of circular RNA functional interactions,20 and integrated informatics tools further optimize therapeutic RNA platforms.21 Consistent with these developments, our results demonstrate that RNA platform type influences both expression dynamics and innate immune activation, highlighting the importance of platform design in next-generation RNA vaccine development. In addition to circular RNA, self-amplifying RNA (saRNA) represents another powerful strategy to enhance the efficiency of RNA platforms.22,23 saRNA encodes a viral replicase, allowing it to replicate autonomously within cells and sustain protein expression at lower doses than conventional mRNA. This property is advantageous for reducing production costs and minimizing dose-dependent adverse effects. While saRNA relies on a viral enzyme system for amplification, circular RNA achieves prolonged stability through its covalently closed structure, resisting exonuclease degradation. Both platforms offer unique advantages in overcoming the transient nature of conventional mRNA, and further research into their respective applications will be essential for clinical translation.

Notably, circular RNA platforms elicited slightly lower CD8 T cell responses than linear RNA vaccines with unmodified and modified nucleosides. This suggests that while circular RNA vaccines effectively support humoral immunity, they may exhibit a reduced ability to elicit the strong cellular immunity essential for vaccines targeting intracellular pathogens and cancers.24,25 This difference may stem from variations in the translation efficiency or antigen presentation pathways of the two platforms.3,26 These findings highlight an important limitation of circular RNA platforms, underscoring the need for optimization strategies to enhance their CD8+ T cell activation. Notably, the extent of immune modulation varies depending on the structural and biochemical properties of each RNA platform. Previous studies on circular RNA-based cancer vaccines have demonstrated enhanced tumor-specific immune responses attributed to prolonged antigen presentation.27 While these findings suggest potential benefits in oncology, our findings indicate that circular RNA platforms induce lower CD8+ T cell responses than linear mRNA platforms, which may limit their effectiveness in eliciting cytotoxic T cell-mediated immunity. Therefore, circular RNA-based cancer vaccines may more effectively induce apoptosis through tumor-associated antigen (TAA) overexpression rather than relying solely on CD8+ T cell activation. These findings emphasize the need for tailored strategies when using circular RNA in immunotherapy.

Safety remains a critical consideration in mRNA vaccine development, particularly in mitigating excessive innate immune activation and inflammatory responses.17 The use of unmodified linear RNA, particularly at high doses, was associated with heightened inflammation, elevated cytokine levels, and increased lymph node necrosis. While cytokine responses are essential for vaccine efficacy, excessive activation may lead to adverse events such as fever or cytokine storms, as evidenced in some mRNA vaccine recipients, particularly following higher doses or repeated administrations.17

Our previous study showed that i.m. injection of a linear RNA vaccine with modified nucleosides significantly reduced erythroid lineage cells in bone marrow tissue and led to a significant decrease in circulating reticulocytes in mice.28 In this study, both bone marrow erythroid lineage cells and circulating reticulocytes were reduced to similar levels across all types of RNA vaccines, suggesting that the type of RNA platform may not influence RNA vaccine-induced bone marrow suppression. Further studies are needed to elucidate the specific mechanisms underlying this phenomenon. Several studies have raised concerns over the potential of severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2 mRNA) vaccines in inducing thrombotic thrombocytopenia; however, the precise mechanism remains to be elucidated.29,30 Notably, platelet counts transiently decreased following administration of both the linear RNA with unmodified nucleosides and the circular RNA, whereas no significant reduction was observed in the linear RNA vaccine with modified nucleosides. These findings strongly support the hypothesis that RNA vaccine-induced thrombotic thrombocytopenia is mediated by the mRNA-LNP component itself. Furthermore, mRNA with unmodified nucleosides may exert a more pronounced effect on platelet levels than mRNA with modified nucleosides.

All types of RNA platforms significantly induced immune responses in lymphoid organs, leading to hypertrophy. Notably, in the draining lymph node (inguinal lymph node), linear RNA with unmodified nucleosides induced the most severe inflammation and necrotic changes than other RNA types. Research has established that exogenous RNA activates the innate immune response by stimulating pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) and that nucleoside modifications mitigate PRR-mediated innate immune activation.31,32 Upon immune stimulation, a substantial amount of oxidative stress, primarily in the form of reactive oxygen species (ROS), is generated, contributing to tissue inflammation and necrosis.33 In particular, the overexpression of IFN-γ and TNF-α promotes both apoptosis and necrosis by activating death signaling pathways.34,35 Additionally, excessive cell proliferation and inflammatory responses in lymph nodes can reduce local blood flow, further exacerbating tissue necrosis.36,37 Consistent with these findings, we found that linear RNA with unmodified nucleosides induced significantly higher expression of RIG-I, IFN-β, TNF-α, IL-6, and IFN-γ in mice compared with other RNA types. These results suggest that linear RNA with unmodified nucleosides can cause tissue damage (especially in draining lymph nodes) through excessive innate immune activation, highlighting the need for careful consideration of this aspect in future mRNA vaccine development.

From an industrial perspective, linear RNA remains the primary focus for large-scale manufacturing, supported by well-established and scalable production pipelines. saRNA can be integrated into these existing frameworks with relatively minor adaptations, although further optimization is required to improve yield and process robustness.38,39 In contrast, the transition to circular RNA presents additional manufacturing challenges. Circularization processes, typically mediated by ribozymes or enzymatic ligation, introduce extra steps in purification and quality control, which are not yet fully optimized for high-volume good manufacturing practice (GMP) production. These differences highlight important considerations for the translational scalability of next-generation RNA platforms.40,41

In summary, this study provides a comprehensive analysis of key challenges in mRNA vaccine development. Unlike previous research focusing on a single RNA format, this study provides the first direct comparative evaluation of circular and linear RNA vaccines, bridging critical knowledge gaps in their relative immunogenicity, safety, and translational potential. As mRNA vaccine technology advances, balancing immune activation and safety is essential for optimizing efficacy. Our findings contribute to advancing precision medicine and guiding the development of mRNA vaccines and therapeutics for complex diseases, including cancer, chronic infections, and autoimmune disorders.

Materials and methods

Plasmid construction

The linear mRNA platform (CUK3-1) was constructed using a DNA template containing a 5ʹ-UTR, a 3ʹ-UTR, and a poly-A tail. Detailed sequence information is available in the patent (KR10-2022-0115033).12 The circular RNA platform was built from constructs used in previous studies.13 The circular RNA platform consists of 3′- and 5'-group 1 introns, exons, and IRES. The DNA template was engineered to include four restriction enzyme sites, resulting in several cloning sites between the untranslated sections for inserting antigens such as FLuc and influenza HA.

Preparation of linear and circular RNA

Linear and circular RNAs were produced as previously described.42,43 For linear RNA production, the template DNA of linear and circular RNA was linearized by incubation at 37°C for 2 h using Not1 restriction enzyme (Enzynomics, Daejeon, Korea). IVT was then performed using the EZ T7 High-Yield In vitro Transcription Kit (Enzynomics, Daejeon, Korea) in a total volume of 50 μL containing 1 μg of linearized DNA template per reaction to generate mRNA from DNA templates. For capping process, SC101 (ST pharm, Gyeonggi-do, South Korea) was incorporated during the transcription process according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The reaction was incubated at 37°C for 4 h. Residual DNA was removed by DNase 1 treatment for 30 min. The resulting linear RNA was precipitated by incubating with lithium chloride at −20°C for 30 min. After centrifugation (18,000× g for 15 min), the supernatant was removed, and the pellets were washed with 70% ethanol (700 μL). The RNA pellets were resuspended in sterile distilled water.

To generate circular RNA, 2 mM GTP was added to the DNase 1-treated reaction and incubated at 55°C for 15 min to promote cyclization. The RNA was then purified using the HiYield RNA UltraPurification kit. The column-purified RNA was heated at 65°C for 3 min and cooled on ice. RNase R treatment (Abm, #E049) was performed at 37°C for 15 min to further enrich the circRNAs. RNase R-treated RNA was purified using column purification. The resulting linear and circular RNAs were subjected to a second purification step of cellulose purification to minimize dsRNA byproduct. The concentration of the synthesized RNA was determined using a NanoDrop-2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Finally, the resuspended RNA was aliquoted and stored at −80°C for subsequent experimental procedures. The final RNAs containing the FLuc, influenza HA and human IgG sequences in the open reading frames were named FLuc, HA, human IgG RNA, respectively.

Detection of residual dsRNA in linear and circular RNA

Residual dsRNA levels were determined using a dot blot analysis and dsRNA ELISA. For the dot blot, total 2.5 μg of RNA per sample was spotted onto positively charged nylon membranes (Sigma-Aldrich, Germany). To enable, quantification, a 142-bp dsRNA standard (Jena Bioscience, Germany) was used as a positive control, prepared via 2-fold serial dilutions ranging from 50 to 3.125 ng. The membrane was blocked for 1 h at room temperature (RT) in Tris-buffered saline (TBS) with 0.1% Tween 20 (0.1% TBS-T) containing 5% (w/v) skim milk powder. After three washes using 0.1% TBS-T, the membranes were incubated overnight at 4°C with a J2 dsRNA-specific mouse monoclonal antibody (Jena bioscience, Germany). Following incubation, the membrane was washed three times with 0.1% TBS-T and incubated for 1 h with a horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (H + L) antibody (Bethyl Laboratories, Inc., CA, USA). After final washing, the signal was developed using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) reagent (Donginbio, Seoul, South Korea) and captured using the ChemiDoc MP Imaging System (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Inc., CA, USA).

Additionally, dsRNA levels were measured using a KRIBIOELISA dsRNA ELISA kit (J2-based) (cat. KBBA56, KRISHGEN BioSystems, India) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. A total of 1 μg of RNA samples per well was used for the assay.

Formulation of mRNA-LNP

LNPs were prepared as previously described.44 Briefly, all lipid components, including cholesterol, 1,2-dimyristoyl-rac-glycero-3-methoxypolyethylene glycol-2000 (DMG-PEG 2000), phospholipids 1,2-distearoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine (DSPC), and ionizable lipids (SM-102), were dissolved in ethanol. The lipid mixtures and RNA were separately dissolved in ethanol and citrate buffers (pH 4.0, 50 mM), respectively, at an N/P ratio of 6. mRNA-LNPs were formulated using Enparticle with a mixing volume ratio of 1:3 (lipid solution:mRNA solution). The resulting mRNA-LNPs were washed twice with 1X DPBS and concentrated using a 1736R centrifuge (LABOGENE Co., Ltd., South Korea) with Amicon Ultra-15 Centrifugal Filters (Merck Millipore, Germany).

Measurement of size and zeta potential for mRNA-LNP

The mRNA-LNP solutions were prepared by 200-fold dilution with DPBS (1X) for size and polydispersity index (PDI) measurements and with deionized water (DIW) for zeta potential measurements. The size, PDI, and zeta potential of the LNPs were measured using a PANalytical Zetasizer Ultra (Malvern, USA) with 12 mm2 polystyrene cuvettes (DTS0012, for size and PDI) and folded capillary zeta cells (DTS1070, for zeta potential).

Ribogreen assay for mRNA-LNP

The encapsulation efficiency of mRNA-LNP was evaluated using fluorescent Ribogreen assays. RNA standards were prepared by serial dilution of the RNA stock solution with 1X TE buffer. Subsequently, mRNA-LNP solutions were diluted 200-fold with 1X TE buffer, allowing the measurement of any remaining free mRNA outside the LNP. To determine the total amount of RNA both inside and outside the LNP, the LNP solutions were diluted 3,200-fold and lysed in TE buffer containing 0.5% Triton X-. Quant-it Ribogreen dye (Invitrogen, USA) was added to all samples in a 96-well plate, and fluorescence intensities were recorded at 500–550 nm using GloMax Explorer microplate reader (Promega, Seoul, Republic of Korea) at an excitation wavelength of 475 nm (λex = 475 nm). FLuc assay

For the transfection of linear and circular RNA, HeLa, HEK293, and Jurkat cells were seeded in 48-well plates at densities of 6 × 104 cells/well, 1 × 105 cells/well, and 2 × 105 cells/well, respectively. After 16 h of incubation, 500 ng of Lin-WT, Lin-m1Ψ, and Circ-WT encoding FLuc were transfected into the cells using Lipofectamine 2000. FLuc expression was measured over time using Promega’s luciferase assay system (Promega, WI, USA), following the manufacturer’s protocol. Luminescence was quantified using the GloMax instrument from Promega Corp.

Western blot analysis

Vero cells were seeded in a 6-well plate at a density of 5 × 105 cells/well. Linear RNA and circular RNA encoding influenza HA were transfected using Lipofectamine 2000. Following incubation at 37°C for 24 h, the cells were washed in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and cell lysates were mixed with SDS loading buffer and heated at 100°C for 5 min. Protein samples were loaded onto a 10% SDS-PAGE gel and electrophoresed in 1X SDS running buffer at 100 V for 90 min at RT. The resulting protein bands were immunoblotted onto a nitrocellulose membrane. The membrane was blocked in PBS containing 5% skim milk and 0.1% Tween 20 for 1 h at RT. Subsequently, the membranes were incubated with anti-HA antibody (diluted 1:1,000) or anti-β-actin antibody (diluted 1:3,000) (Sinobiological) overnight at 4°C. After washing three times, the membrane was incubated with goat anti-mouse HRP conjugated H + L antibody (diluted 1:5,000) for 1 h at RT. Signal detection was performed using ECL solution (Donginbiotech Co., Ltd) and ChemiDoc (Bio-Rad).

Animals

For experimental studies, female 6-week-old mice of different strains were utilized: Balb/c mice for the immune response test, ICR (female, 6-week-old) mice for the toxicity test, and C57BL/6 for human IgG expression experiments. All mice were procured from KOATECH (Pyeongtaek, Gyeonggi-do, South Korea) and acclimatized for 1 week. All mice were housed under specific-pathogen-free conditions at a controlled temperature of 23°C ± 2°C, with a 12 h light/dark cycle. Animal experiments were conducted following the ethical guidelines and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the Catholic University of Korea (approval no: CUK-IACUC-2023-031, SML-BP-IACUC-2023-16) and Seoul National University Hospital (approval no. 20-0264-C1A1).

Immunization

Female Balb/c mice were randomly divided into three groups (n = 5) for linear and circular RNA delivery. Mice were immunized intramuscularly with 10 μg of mRNA-LNP encoding either the HA sequence from influenza strain A/Puerto Rico/8/1934 H1N1 or FLuc. For evaluation of HA-specific immune response, mice received a prime injection followed by a boost injection, with a 2-week interval between injections. To confirm innate immune response, a single i.m. dose of 10 μg of mRNA-LNP encoding FLuc was administered.

BLI of mRNA expression in vivo

To monitor bio-distribution and persistence of RNA expressions, bioluminescence imaging (BLI) was performed after injection of mRNA-LNP encoding the FLuc as a reporter. The mice were anesthetized with a mixture of isoflurane (1.5%) and oxygen during imaging. FLuc mRNA-LNP at a dose of 5 μg FLuc RNA per mouse was injected intramuscularly into the right thigh or i.v. into the lateral tail vein in 10–12-week-old Balb/c nude mice. D-luciferin (Promega, WI, USA) was injected intraperitoneally at a dose of 3 mg per mouse as a luciferase substrate. Bioluminescence images of the FLuc RNA expression were captured sequentially at 5-min intervals, and the maximum signal was used for quantitative analysis. BLI signals were acquired using an IVIS Luminar II (PerkinElmer, MA, USA) and analyzed using Living Imaging software v.4.7.4 (PerkinElmer).

Human IgG ELISA

The systemic concentration of human IgG was quantified by sandwich ELISA after the administration of 20 μg of RNA. Briefly, 96-well plates (SPL Life Sciences, Korea) were coated with 20 ng/well of capture antibody (Thermo Fisher Scientific) overnight at 4°C. Following incubation, the wells were blocked with 100 μL of blocking buffer (1% bovine serum albumin (BSA) in 0.05% phosphate-buffered saline with Tween 20 (PBST)) for 1 h at RT. Human IgG standards (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and samples, diluted in blocking buffer, were then added and incubated for 2 h at 25°C. After three washes with 300 μL of 0.05% PBST, a goat anti-human IgG (H + L) secondary antibody (Thermo Fisher Scientific; 1:5,000 dilution in blocking buffer) was added and incubated for 1 h at RT. The plates were washed again three times, and tetramethylbenzidine (TMB) substrate (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was added for 5 min. The enzymatic reaction was quenched with 2 N H2SO4, and the optical density was measured at 450 nm using a GloMax Explorer microplate reader (Promega, Korea).

ELISA

An ELISA was performed to assess the levels of antigen-specific total IgG, IgG1, and IgG2a in mouse serum. Briefly, a 96-well plate was coated with HA at a concentration of 50 ng per well and incubated overnight at 4°C. Subsequently, the wells were blocked with 100 μL of blocking buffer (1% BSA in PBS) for 1 h at RT. Serial diluted serum samples were added to the wells and incubated for 2 h at RT. The wells were washed three times with 300 μL of PBS-T (PBS containing 0.05% Tween 20). HRP-conjugated anti-mouse total IgG, IgG1 (Bethyl Laboratories, Montgomery, TX, USA), and IgG2a (Bethyl Laboratories, Montgomery, TX, USA) antibodies were diluted 1:5,000 in 1% BSA and added to the wells and incubated at RT for 1 h. Following three washes with PBS-T, TMB was added to the wells. The reaction was stopped by the addition of 2 N H2SO4. Finally, the optical density was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader (GloMax Explorer, Promega, Seoul, Republic of Korea). Endpoint titers were defined as the dilution yielding an optical density (OD) value three times higher than that of the blank control.

HI assay

The HI assay was conducted according to the guidelines provided by the World Health Organization in their “manual for the laboratory diagnosis and virological surveillance of influenza.” Briefly, mouse sera were treated with receptor-destroying enzyme (RDE) (Denka Seiken, Tokyo, Japan) at 37°C overnight, followed by inactivation at 56°C for 30 min. To minimize nonspecific responses, mouse sera were adsorbed onto chicken RBCs. Sera were serially diluted in 25 μL of PBS using V-bottom 96-well microtiter plates and incubated with standardized viral suspensions (4 HA U/25 μL) for 30 min at RT. Subsequently, 50 μL of 2% chicken RBCs were added to each well, and the plates were further incubated for 30 min at RT. Antibody titers, expressed as geometric mean titers, were calculated by determining the reciprocal of the highest serum dilution that completely inhibited agglutination. The lower limit of detection was 1:80, and titers below this threshold were recorded as 1:80 for data analysis.

Microneutralization assay

Serum samples were inactivated for 30 min at 56°C, following which they were 2-fold serially diluted starting from 1:20 in serum-free medium in 96-well cell culture plates. Each dilution was then incubated with the same volume of viral suspension containing 100 TCID50 of influenza PR8 H1N1 at 37°C for 1 h in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2. After incubation, 100 μL of the mixture at each dilution was added to a cell plate containing a Madin-Darby Canine Kidney (MDCK) monolayer. The plates were incubated for 48 h at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2. Following incubation, the supernatant was carefully discarded and 100 μL of a 4% formaldehyde solution (DaeJung Chemicals & Metals, Siheung-si, Gyeonggi-do, Korea) was added for 30 min at RT. The 4% formaldehyde solution was then discarded, and 100 μL of 0.1% crystal violet solution (Sigma-Aldrich, St Louis, MO, USA) was added. After 1 h of staining, the crystal violet solution was discarded, and wells were washed with tap water. The results were observed using a Leica DMi8 microscope (Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany). The highest serum dilution that protected the cell monolayer was considered the neutralization titer.

ELISpot

To quantify cytokine levels in splenocyte culture supernatants, splenocytes were harvested from immunized mice and seeded at a density of 2.5 × 105 cells per well in a 96-well plate. Splenocytes were stimulated with a 5-μg mixture of HA-specific T cell epitope peptides (IYSTVASSL, LYEKVKSQL, DYEELREQL, SFERFEIFPKE, HNTNGVTAACSH, KLKNSYVNKKGK, NAYVSVVTSNYNRRF, and CPKYVRSAKLRM) per well and incubated for 24 h at 37°C. These HA peptides were synthesized by Peptron (Daejeon, Korea). IFN-γ secreting T cells were detected using the ELISpot assay and the mouse IFN-γ ELISpot BASIC kit from Mabtech (Stockholm, Sweden), following the manufacturer’s instructions. Spot development was once clear discrimination between groups was achieved. Spots were enumerated using ELISpot 7.0 iSpot software (Autoimmun Diagnostika GmbH, Strassberg, Germany).

Flow cytometry

For flow cytometry, 1 × 106 splenocytes were seeded in a 96-well round-bottomed plate. Cells were stimulated in the same conditions as used for the ELISpot assay. To assess cytokine secretion by T cells, splenocytes were blocked using CD16/CD32 (eBioscience) for 20 min at 4°C and stained with CD8a (clone 53-6.7, BioLegend, San Diego, CA, USA), CD4 (GK1.5, BioLegend, San Diego, CA, USA) and Fixable Viability Dye eFluor520 (eBioscience) for 30 min at 4°C in the dark. Stained cells were fixed using 4% paraformaldehyde. The stained cells were permeabilized using a Fixation/Permeabilization Solution kit (BD Biosciences) for 1 h at 4°C in the dark, and subsequently stained with IFN-γ (XMG 1.2, BioLegend, San Diego, CA, USA), TNF-α (MP6-XT22, BioLegend, San Diego, CA, USA) and IL-2 (JES6-5H4, BioLegend, San Diego, CA, USA) for 30 min at 4°C in the dark. Cells were analyzed using the CytoFLEX (Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA, USA), and the data were analyzed using CytExpert (Beckman Coulter).

RNA isolation and real-time PCR

Total RNA was isolated from draining lymph nodes using TRIzol reagent. Total RNA (200 ng) served as template for reverse transcription using the cDNA synthesis kit (TOYOBO, code no. FSQ-201) according to the manufacturer’s protocols. Expression levels of RIG-I, IFN-β, TNF-α, and IL-6 in draining lymph nodes were determined by two-step real-time PCR using TB Green Premix Ex Taq II on the QuantStudio5 system. The cycling conditions were as follows: denaturation at 95°C for 30 s, annealing at 52°C for 1 min. Relative mRNA levels were normalized to glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) as a housekeeping gene. The sequence of mouse-specific primers for real-time PCR are listed in Table S1.

Safety assessment

Animal experiment: all animal experimental procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Seoul National University Hospital (approval no. 22–0088). Female CrlOri: CD1 (ICR) mice aged 6 weeks were obtained from Orient Bio Inc. (Seongnam, Gyeonggi-do, South Korea). The animals were housed in an Association for Assessment and Accreditation of Laboratory Animal Care (AAALAC) and good laboratory practice (GLP) test-accredited facility with individually ventilated cages under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions, at a temperature range of 21°C–25°C and 30%–70% humidity. After a 1-week adaptation period, the experiments were conducted when mice were 7 weeks of age. The mice received i.m. injections of three different RNA platforms formulated with LNPs (RNA dose: 100 μg/100 μL per mouse) twice at 2-week intervals. This dose selection follows WHO regulatory guidelines for nonclinical vaccine evaluation, which allow the direct administration of the intended human clinical dose to animals without allometric scaling.45 Using a higher dose ensures maximal exposure to evaluate potential adverse effects, a standard approach in nonclinical mRNA vaccine safety studies.28,46,47,48 The animals were euthanized at either 2 or 14 days post-inoculation (dpi). Complete blood count (CBC), serum biochemistry, and histopathological analyses were conducted.

CBC: Blood was collected from the caudal vena cava under isoflurane anesthesia and immediately transferred into K2 EDTA blood collection tubes (BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA). The following CBC parameters were analyzed using an automated hematology analyzer, ADVIA 2120i (Siemens Diagnostics, Tarrytown, NY, USA): RBC count, platelet count, monocyte count, and reticulocyte count.

Serum biochemistry: blood samples were collected in serum-separating tubes (BD Biosciences) and centrifuged at 2000 RCF for 15 min. The following serum biochemical parameters were analyzed using an automated chemistry analyzer 7070 (Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan): ALB, ALP, aspartate transaminase (AST), and alanine transaminase (ALT).

Histopathological analysis: following necropsy, tissues were fixed in 10% neutral-buffered formalin. Bone tissue was decalcified for 24 h using Calci-clear rapid solution (Cat#: HS-105, National Diagnostics, Charlotte, NC, USA). The samples were processed, embedded in paraffin, sectioned at a thickness of 4 μm, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). Microscopic examination was conducted by experts specializing in laboratory animal pathology to assess histopathological alterations. Severity scores were assigned on a scale of 0–5 based on the following criteria: reduction in erythroid cells (bone marrow), inflammatory cell infiltration and affected area (injection site), increased cellularity and organ hypertrophy (spleen), and inflammation, necrosis, and hypertrophy (lymph node).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using Prism 9 software (GraphPad Software Inc., CA, USA). Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or geometric mean with geometric SD. For comparisons between multiple groups, the normality and lognormality of the data were evaluated before analysis. Ordinary one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple comparison test was used for normally distributed data. If any group failed the normality test, the Kruskal-Wallis test followed by Dunn’s multiple comparison test was employed. For data involving two independent variables, two-way ANOVA by Tukey’s multiple comparison test was performed. Differences were considered statistically significant at ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, ∗∗∗p < 0.001, and ∗∗∗∗p < 0.0001.
