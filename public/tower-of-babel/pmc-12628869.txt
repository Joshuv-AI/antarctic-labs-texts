The association between blood urea nitrogen to albumin ratio and 28-day ICU mortality in sepsis patients: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Meiting Wu, Huijun Sun, Huiying Lv, Aiyong Zhu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSepsis remains a leading cause of mortality in intensive care unit (ICU) patients, with mortality rates up to 30%. While the Blood Urea Nitrogen (BUN) to Albumin ratio has been associated with outcomes in various conditions, the dose–response relationship between this ratio and mortality in sepsis patients remains unclear.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study analyzed 9,783 sepsis patients from the eICU Collaborative Research Database. The relationship between BUN/Albumin ratio and 28-day ICU mortality was examined using multivariate logistic regression and threshold effect analysis. Models were adjusted for demographics, clinical parameters, comorbidities, and laboratory values. Threshold effect analysis was performed using two-piecewise linear regression.ResultsA non-linear relationship was identified between the BUN/Albumin ratio and 28-day ICU mortality, with a threshold at 20.91 mg/g (95% CI 17.19–26.90). Below this threshold, each unit difference in the ratio was associated with higher mortality odds (OR: 1.06, 95% CI 1.04–1.08, P < 0.0001). Above the threshold, the association persisted but is markedly attenuated (OR: 1.01, 95% CI 1.00–1.02). This relationship remained robust after adjusting for potential confounders and in sensitivity analyses.ConclusionsA nonlinear association was observed between the BUN/Albumin ratio and 28-day ICU mortality among patients with sepsis, with an inflection point established at 20.91 mg/g. This readily accessible parameter has the potential to aid in early risk stratification within the ICU setting. Additional research is warranted to validate these findings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40001-025-03434-1.Keywords: Blood urea nitrogen to albumin ratio, Sepsis, EICU, ICU mortality, Association

Introduction

Sepsis represents a life-threatening condition characterized by organ dysfunction resulting from a dysregulated host response to infection [1]. Global statistics indicate that sepsis affects approximately 50 million individuals annually, with mortality rates reaching 30% and causing an estimated 10 million deaths worldwide [2]. Sepsis is no longer viewed solely as an infection‑driven syndrome; it sits at the crossroads of a multisystem continuum in which immune dysregulation, metabolic reprogramming, and endothelial injury mutually amplify one another [3].

Convergent inflammatory–mitochondrial mechanisms span sepsis, cardiovascular disease, MASLD, and gastrointestinal malignancies, providing a systems‑level rationale for their frequent co‑occurrence [4–6]. This perspective implies that a single‑organ insult can rapidly propagate systemic dysfunction and that biomarkers integrating multi‑organ reserve may outperform organ‑specific indices for risk stratification.

Within this continuum, renal, hepatic, and cardiac functions are tightly coupled through oxidative‑stress and glutathione‑metabolism pathways. When infection triggers tumor necrosis factor‑α release, the JNK–ASK1–SAB mitochondrial cascade can be activated, promoting albumin catabolism, increasing urea production, and enhancing capillary leakage [4].

Among these parameters, the blood urea nitrogen to albumin (BUN/Albumin) ratio, which reflects both renal function and nutritional status, has been associated with clinical outcomes in various critical conditions. The blood urea nitrogen to albumin (BUN/albumin) ratio reflects the combined influences of renal perfusion and clearance, hepatic synthetic capacity, nutritional reserve, and endothelial integrity—four pillars that tend to erode concurrently across the sepsis–metabolism–malignancy spectrum.

Previous studies have demonstrated its association with clinical outcomes in community-acquired pneumonia [7], lung cancer [8], chronic heart failure [9], coronary heart disease [10], Escherichia coli bacteremia [11], and gastrointestinal bleeding [12]. A recent study has also explored the relationship between BUN/Albumin ratio and short-term mortality (30-day) in septic patients [13]. However, the dose–response relationship between BUN/Albumin ratio and 28-day ICU mortality in sepsis patients remains to be elucidated.

BUN, as an indicator of kidney function, is associated with a higher risk of mortality when its levels are elevated [14]. In a retrospective cohort study involving 12,713 patients with sepsis, a non-linear association between BUN level and 30-day mortality was identified [15]. Albumin serves as a critical biomarker for assessing nutritional status and plays a substantial role in maintaining physiological homeostasis. Reduced levels of albumin have been associated with adverse clinical outcomes and exhibit a negative correlation with the incidence of sepsis [16]. Therefore, we postulate the existence of a nonlinear relationship between the BUN/Albumin ratio and 28-day mortality following admission to the intensive care unit (ICU) in patients with sepsis.

Understanding the potential non-linear relationship between the BUN/Albumin ratio and mortality is of particular clinical significance. Previous research [15] has demonstrated that identifying non-linear associations and threshold effects of biomarkers can significantly improve risk stratification in sepsis patients. While linear associations can identify general trends, non-linear relationships and threshold effects could provide more precise risk stratification points, enabling clinicians to make more informed decisions about patient management and resource allocation. This is especially crucial in the ICU setting, where early recognition of high-risk patients and timely intervention are essential for improving outcomes. Therefore, we aimed to investigate the dose–response relationship between BUN/Albumin ratio and 28-day ICU mortality in sepsis patients, with a specific focus on potential non-linear associations and threshold effects.

In this retrospective cohort study, we utilized the extensive, multicenter Philips eICU Collaborative Research Database (eICU-CRD) to examine the association between the blood urea nitrogen (BUN)/albumin ratio and 28-day intensive care unit (ICU) mortality in septic patients. We hypothesized that the BUN/albumin ratio functions as a continuum biomarker of multi‑organ reserve loss such that, once it exceeds a critical threshold, 28‑day mortality accelerates nonlinearly. Using this large‑scale database, we aimed to quantify the dose‑response shape of this relationship and identify the inflection point at which the risk of death escalates disproportionately.

Methods

Data source

This retrospective study utilized data from the eICU Collaborative Research Database (eICU-CRD), which includes detailed records of over 200,000 ICU admissions across the US, collected electronically through the Philips Healthcare eICU program from 2014 to 2015 [17]. The eICU-CRD is widely used for conducting observational studies [18–20]. Access to this database requires passing an exam and obtaining certification per the PhysioNet Review Board's data usage agreement. The database is released under HIPAA Safe Harbor. Approval for data access was granted after completing the CITI program “Data or Specimens Only Research”. The study, identified as record ID 62421491, was exempt from MIT's institutional review board approval due to its retrospective nature, absence of direct patient intervention, and secure data handling certified by Privacert. Informed consent was waived for the same reasons. The study adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki and relevant guidelines and regulations.

Study population

All ICU patients diagnosed with sepsis, defined by a suspected or confirmed infection and a SOFA score increase of over 2 points [21] per the APACHE IV dataset [22], were included. Infection was identified using ICD-9 codes from the eICU Collaborative Research Database. Exclusion criteria included: (1) not the first ICU admission, (2) ICU stay under 24 h, (3) under 18 years old, (4) missing ICU outcome, and (5) missing BUN or Albumin data within the first 24 h after ICU admission or system errors.

We restricted analyses to the first ICU admission to maintain independence and a uniform baseline. Readmissions are shaped by prior treatments and evolving complications, introducing time‑dependent confounding and clinical heterogeneity; mixing variable and readmission episodes without explicit modeling may obscure exposure–outcome associations [23].

Variables

The eICU database contains demographic details, bedside monitor readings, ICD-9-CM diagnoses, and routine lab data.

Data from the first 24 h after ICU admission were gathered from the eICU-CRD [1, 22]. Physiological variables like temperature, respiratory rate, heart rate, and mean arterial pressure (MAP) were sourced from the apacheApsVar table [24]. Baseline characteristics, including age, gender, ethnicity, and weight, were obtained from the patient and apachePatientResult tables. Laboratory values like Blood urea nitrogen (BUN), calcium, white blood cells (WBC), platelets, albumin, serum creatinine, serum potassium, and sodium levels were collected from laboratory records [13–15]. Disease severity was assessed using SOFA and APACHE IV scores [22, 24, 25]. For the exposure, we calculated the BUN-to-albumin ratio as BUN divided by albumin, using the earliest available BUN and albumin measurements recorded within the first 24 h after ICU admission. We selected this 24-h window to provide a pragmatic, clinically actionable time point for early risk stratification that is broadly available and comparable across centers in the eICU-CRD. Accordingly, our primary exposure represents early ICU biochemical status rather than time-updated values over the entire ICU course.

Outcomes

In this study, the principal outcome was 28‑day all‑cause mortality after ICU admission. This 28‑day horizon is widely adopted in critical care and sepsis outcomes research [1, 25]. All study variables were obtained within the first 24 h after ICU admission, consistent with established ICU practice that defines baseline status within this initial 24‑h period, as exemplified by APACHE‑IV and related severity scoring systems [22]. To ensure that measurement of study variables preceded outcome follow‑up, ICU stays with a length of stay < 24 h were excluded at cohort entry. Deaths occurring within the first 28-days, including very early deaths such as those occurring on day 1, were included in the endpoint provided that baseline measurements had been captured during the first 24 h after ICU admission.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are shown as means ± SD or medians with IQRs, while categorical data are given as numbers and percentages. The outcome variable stratified baseline patient characteristics. Group comparisons used the chi-square test for categorical variables and the Kruskal–Wallis test for continuous variables.

We conducted univariate analysis to examine potential risks for 28-day ICU mortality and used multiple logistic regression to assess the link between BUN to Albumin ratio and 28-day ICU death. Results are shown as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Both adjusted and unadjusted models were used. Potential confounders were initially identified based on their association with outcomes or effect estimate changes over 10% [26]. These statistically significant variables were then evaluated for their clinical relevance in sepsis outcomes. The final adjustments included the following clinically meaningful covariates: age, sex, ethnicity, admission weight, heart rate, respiratory rate, mechanical ventilation use, SOFA score, COPD, CHF, AMI, diabetes, sepsis infection sites, calcium, serum creatinine, serum potassium, platelet count, white blood cell count, AIDS, lymphoma, metastatic cancer, and immunosuppression. The final regression model included 22 clinically relevant covariates, resulting in an event-to-variable ratio of 44:1. This is well above the commonly recommended threshold of 10 events per variable for logistic regression [27], effectively reducing the risk of overfitting and improving the reliability of parameter estimates. Model discrimination was assessed using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC). Model calibration was evaluated with the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test, where a p > 0.05 indicates acceptable calibration [28]. Multicollinearity was assessed using VIF and tolerance. Sparse binary predictors were evaluated for instability; penalized logistic sensitivity analyses supported robustness of the main findings.

Additionally, to assess the potential threshold (non-linear) effect between the BUN/Albumin ratio and 28-day ICU mortality, we performed a two-piecewise logistic regression analysis. The optimal cut-off (inflection point) was determined by repeatedly fitting the model at every possible BUN/Albumin value and selecting the value that maximized the log-likelihood, as initially indicated by a smoothing plot using a generalized additive model (GAM). We obtained a 95% CI for the inflection point using 1,000 bootstrap replications. Model fit for the segmented specification was compared with the single‑line model using likelihood ratio tests (LRT).

We conducted sensitivity analyses to test the robustness of our results. We use dummy variables for missing covariate values when over 1% of both continuous and categorical data were missing. We examined whether the associations remained consistent across different subgroups, including demographic characteristics, vital signs, clinical parameters (including SOFA score), comorbidities, infection sites, and laboratory values. To assess unmeasured confounding between the BUN/Albumin ratio and 28-day mortality, we calculated E-values [29]. The E-value quantifies the necessary strength of an unmeasured confounder that could potentially nullify the observed association between the BUN/Albumin ratio and 28-day mortality.

A P value below 0.05 was deemed significant for all tests. Statistical analyses were conducted using EmpowerStats (X&Y solutions, Inc., Boston, MA) and R software version 3.6.1. (http://www.r-project.org).

Ethics approval and consent to participate

Data were extracted from the eICU-CRD database (record ID: 62421491) under the HIPAA safe harbor provision, following the PhysioNet review committee's data usage agreement. This retrospective analysis used anonymous data and did not need local ethics committee approval.

Patients in the highest BUN/Albumin ratio quartile were generally older, more often female, and had greater needs for mechanical ventilation. Furthermore, patients in the highest quartile exhibited significantly elevated mortality rates at 28 days compared to those in the first, second, and third quartiles.

Identification of nonlinear relationship

After adjusting for covariates, we observed a nonlinear association between the BUN/Albumin ratio and 28‑day ICU mortality in patients with sepsis (Fig. 2; Table 4). In the linear analysis (Model I), each one‑unit increase in BUN/Albumin ratio was associated with higher mortality odds (OR 1.02, 95% CI 1.02–1.03; P < 0.0001), indicating that patients with higher ratios had incrementally higher odds of mortality compared to those with lower ratios. Each SD difference in the ratio was also associated with higher mortality odds, (OR 1.33 95% CI 1.24–1.44; P < 0.0001). Threshold analysis (Model II) identified an inflection point at 20.91 (95% CI 17.19–26.90). Below this inflection point, the association was steeper (per‑unit OR 1.06, 95% CI 1.04–1.08; P < 0.0001), whereas above it, the slope was attenuated (per‑unit OR 1.01, 95% CI 1.00–1.02; P = 0.059). Consistent with the restricted cubic spline plot, risk increased monotonically without a clear plateau, but with an inflection around BUN/Albumin ratio = 20.91 after which the increase was more gradual and uncertainty widened. The segmented model provided a better fit than the single‑line model (likelihood ratio test P < 0.001).

In additional robustness checks, the turning point for the BUN-to-albumin ratio remained stable at approximately 20.5–20.91 mg/g across alternative specifications (Supplementary Tables S1–S3). Results were consistent when (1) handling missing covariates using missing-indicator (dummy) variables (n = 9,706; turning point ≈20.5 mg/g; LRT P < 0.001), (2) comparing models before and after additional adjustment for immunocompromised conditions (AIDS, lymphoma, metastatic cancer, immunosuppression), with the turning point unchanged at 20.91 mg/g and the piecewise model favored (LRT P < 0.001), and (3) conducting stratified analyses across demographics, severity, comorbidities, and laboratory strata, which showed concordant effect directions (Supplementary Table S4). The E-value for the primary association was 1.31, indicating that only an unmeasured confounder associated with both exposure and outcome by an odds ratio > 1.31 could fully explain the findings, suggesting robustness to modest unmeasured confounding.

VIFs were low (maximum 1.35; minimum tolerance 0.74; Supplementary Table S5), indicating no concerning multicollinearity. For sparse predictors, estimates were consistent between MLE and ridge (e.g., AIDS: MLE OR 2.39 [95% CI 1.10–5.23] vs. ridge OR 2.11; Supplementary Table S6). The penalized model was used to assess coefficient stability rather than for predictive optimization; performance metrics from this sensitivity analysis (AUROC 0.514; Brier 0.0899; Supplementary Table S7) are therefore not directly comparable to the primary model. Additionally, in a sensitivity analysis excluding patients with “Other/Unknown” ethnicity, the association between BUN/Albumin ratio and 28‑day ICU mortality remained robust (see Supplementary Table S8).

Discussion

We retrospectively explored the relationship between the BUN/albumin ratio and 28-day ICU mortality using a large, multi-center eICU-CRD database. In a cohort of 9,783 patients, we found a nonlinear relationship between the BUN/albumin ratio and 28-day ICU mortality. When the BUN/Albumin ratio is below 20.91 mg/g, an increase in the ratio is significantly associated with a higher risk of 28-day ICU mortality (OR 1.06, 95% CI 1.05–1.08, P < 0.0001). Above this threshold, the association persisted but is markedly attenuated. (OR: 1.01, 95% CI 1.00–1.02). Consistent with the restricted cubic spline plot, risk increased monotonically without a clear plateau, with an inflection around 20.91 mg/g and widening uncertainty at higher values.

Previous studies [13, 30, 31] have demonstrated an association between the BUN/Albumin ratio and short-term mortality (28- or 30-day mortality) in sepsis patients. While our findings align with these studies in confirming the relationship between BUN/Albumin ratio and mortality, our methodological approach provides additional insights. Using flexible curve‑fitting and segmented (two‑piecewise) logistic regression, we identified a statistically supported inflection at 20.91 mg/g, below which the association was stronger and above which it was attenuated but persistent. This nuanced, nonlinear pattern suggests that risk rises monotonically yet with a reduced slope at higher BUN/albumin ratios, which may reflect more complex underlying pathophysiology and may inform risk communication and exploratory cut‑point selection.

Several hypotheses might explain this threshold effect in the relationship between BUN/Albumin ratio and mortality.

First, from the perspective of compensatory mechanisms, when the ratio is below 20.91, the body's homeostatic systems likely remain functional, enabling a more dynamic response to changes in BUN and albumin levels. Studies have shown that albumin plays a crucial role in maintaining fluid balance and osmotic pressure [16, 32], while BUN reflects kidney function and metabolic status [14]. Below this threshold, the detrimental effects of impaired renal function and malnutrition may work synergistically to influence mortality risk [33, 34], with the body still maintaining some capacity to compensate.

Second, this threshold might represent a transition point in organ dysfunction patterns. Below the threshold, the relationship primarily reflects the isolated effects of renal dysfunction and nutritional status [35–37], leading to a stronger association with mortality. As the ratio increases beyond 20.91, the emergence of multiple organ dysfunction syndrome might become the dominant factor influencing mortality risk [15, 33, 38, 39], thereby diminishing the relative impact of further increases in the BUN/Albumin ratio. At this stage, other factors or comorbidities may become more influential in determining patient outcomes, leading to the observed plateau in mortality rates.

Third, the threshold could indicate a critical point of metabolic adaptation where the body shifts to alternative pathways for managing nitrogen waste and protein metabolism [40]. This is supported by studies showing that both elevated BUN levels and low albumin levels are associated with poor nutritional status and increased mortality risk [41]. The metabolic shift beyond the threshold might explain the observed plateauing effect, as the body reaches a new, albeit compromised, steady state in its handling of nitrogen waste and protein metabolism [42]. This adaptation, while not optimal, might explain why further increases in the ratio do not substantially impact mortality risk.

These hypotheses are not mutually exclusive and might work in concert to produce the observed non-linear relationship. This synergistic effect is particularly evident in our threshold analysis, where the strength of association between the BUN/Albumin ratio and mortality changes significantly. Below this threshold, we observed a relatively modest increase in mortality risk with rising BUN/Albumin ratios, suggesting a compensatory phase where physiological mechanisms might still maintain homeostasis [43, 44]. However, above this threshold, the relationship becomes more pronounced, with a steeper increase in mortality risk, potentially indicating a decompensation phase where the body's regulatory mechanisms are overwhelmed [38]. This differential pattern might reflect underlying pathophysiological mechanisms where the combination of elevated BUN (indicating increased protein catabolism and potential renal dysfunction) [45] and decreased albumin (suggesting both nutritional status compromise and increased capillary permeability) [46] reaches a critical point that significantly impacts patient outcomes.

As hypothesized in our research objectives, this study advances our understanding of the BUN/albumin ratio in sepsis by revealing its nonlinear relationship with ICU mortality. The identification of a specific threshold at 20.91 mg/g provides a concrete reference point for clinical assessment. This threshold could help clinicians identify patients who may require closer monitoring. Furthermore, the nonlinear pattern suggests potential differences in underlying pathophysiological mechanisms above and below this threshold.

Given that the BUN/albumin ratio is influenced by multiple factors, we applied multivariable adjustment and conducted stratified analyses. Effect directions were consistent across strata, supporting robustness. Sensitivity analyses including missing‑indicator handling, additional adjustment for immunocompromised conditions, and penalized (ridge) models yielded stable estimates and a consistent inflection, thereby reinforcing the stability of our main findings. The sensitivity analysis excluding patients with “Other/Unknown” ethnicity showed results consistent with the main analysis, suggesting that missing ethnicity data did not materially affect the stability of the association between BUN/Albumin ratio and 28‑day ICU mortality (Table S8).

While our observational findings suggest the potential effect of this threshold, we acknowledge that, as an association study, causal relationships cannot be established. The clinical implications of this threshold require careful validation through future interventional studies before any specific management strategies can be recommended. These findings may serve as a theoretical basis for designing such validation studies across different patient populations, particularly in determining whether threshold-based risk stratification could improve patient outcomes.

This study has several notable strengths: (1) A large sample size with blood urea nitrogen and albumin measurements obtained within 24 h of ICU admission, ensuring data reliability; (2) The innovative application of advanced curve-fitting techniques revealed a crucial non-linear relationship and identified a specific inflection point, providing more nuanced insights than traditional linear analyses; (3) The identification of this threshold offers potential guidance for clinical risk stratification and management strategies.

We acknowledge that BUN and albumin measured within 24 h of ICU admission may already reflect early resuscitation and supportive treatments (e.g., fluid administration and albumin infusion) and cannot capture temporal dynamics over the ICU course. Accordingly, our estimates should be interpreted as the association between early ICU biochemical status—summarized by the BUN/albumin ratio—and mortality, rather than time-updated effects across the entire stay. From a bias perspective, early fluids could dilute BUN and albumin infusion could increase serum albumin, both tending to lower the BUN/albumin ratio and potentially bias the association toward the null; thus, our findings may be conservative. Conversely, if catabolic stress and early renal impairment precede full resuscitation, a stronger association may be observed. While the timing and intensity of treatments vary across centers and patients, we attempted to mitigate confounding through extensive covariate adjustment and multiple robustness checks. Notably, the nonlinear association and the turning point remained stable at approximately 20.5–20.91 mg/g across alternative specifications (including missing-indicator handling, additional adjustment for immunocompromised conditions, and subgroup stratifications; see Supplementary Tables S1–S3), suggesting that the observed relationship is unlikely to be entirely driven by treatment-related shifts. Prospective studies with serial measurements and time-dependent modeling are warranted to evaluate whether trajectories or time-updated thresholds improve risk prediction and to delineate treatment-modified effects.

Taken together, because early resuscitation would be expected to generally lower the BUN/albumin ratio and bias estimates toward the null, the persistence of the nonlinear association and a stable turning point around 20.5–20.91 mg/g across multiple alternative specifications supports the interpretation that our findings are unlikely to be entirely driven by early-treatment effects.

However, several limitations of this study should be noted. First, this study is limited to adult sepsis patients using a U.S. multicenter ICU database. Therefore, generalizability is constrained, and applicability to non‑sepsis populations, other healthcare systems, or ICU readmissions should be interpreted with caution. Second, as this is an observational study, there are important methodological limitations to acknowledge: we can only detect associations and cannot prove causation. Although we defined all covariates within the first 24 h after ICU admission and ensured that follow-up for 28‑day mortality started thereafter (ICU length of stay ≥ 24 h), residual issues of temporality and indication bias may persist. Third, the issue of confounding is a major consideration in observational research. We adjusted for a comprehensive set of clinically relevant covariates and conducted multiple robustness checks, including missing‑indicator methods, additional adjustment for immunocompromised conditions, stratified analyses, and penalized (ridge) sensitivity models. Results were stable, with a consistent inflection point (approximately 20.5–20.91 mg/g) and an E‑value of 1.31, which indicates that an unmeasured confounder would need to be associated with both the exposure and the outcome by an odds ratio of at least 1.31, conditional on measured covariates, to fully explain away the observed association. Nonetheless, residual or unmeasured confounding from unknown factors cannot be fully eliminated. Fourth, while we identified a threshold effect of the BUN/albumin ratio at 20.91 mg/g, the biological mechanisms underlying this nonlinear relationship remain unclear. The threshold we observed might be influenced by the specific characteristics of our study population and the timing of measurements. Fifth, BUN and albumin were obtained within 24 h of ICU admission. These measurements reflect early ICU biochemical status rather than time-updated effects over the entire ICU stay, do not capture longitudinal dynamics, and may already be influenced by early resuscitation and supportive treatments (e.g., fluid administration, albumin infusion). Such treatments would generally lower the BUN/albumin ratio and could bias the association toward the null, implying our estimates may be conservative. Consequently, future work should include prospective, multi‑center studies with serial measurements and time‑dependent modeling to validate the nonlinear association and to evaluate whether trajectories or time‑updated thresholds improve risk stratification.

Conclusions

In this large multicenter cohort study, we identified a nonlinear association between the BUN/Albumin ratio and 28-day ICU mortality among sepsis patients, with an inflection point at 20.91 mg/g. Below this inflection point, increases in the BUN/Albumin ratio were strongly correlated with an elevated risk of mortality, whereas above it, the association persisted but is markedly attenuated.

These findings suggest that the BUN/Albumin ratio, which is simple and routinely available, may help clinicians in the early risk stratification and management of sepsis patients in the ICU. Further validation in multi-center and more diverse populations is necessary to confirm the generalizability and clinical significance of these associations..
