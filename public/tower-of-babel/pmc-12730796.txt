Addressing workplace harassment: a multifaceted approach for nursing students in a tertiary hospital in Singapore
Authors: Michele Lim, Manpreet Kaur, Rui Du, Dorin Ramirez Diomano, Caryn Kai Lin Ng, Jian Hao Tan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractWorkplace harassment among healthcare workers, particularly nursing students, has reached concerning levels, often resulting in serious consequences. At an acute tertiary teaching hospital in Singapore, reported cases of harassment among nursing students doubled from four in 2019 to eight in 2021. Delayed reporting, often due to a lack of awareness of a structured reporting system, exacerbates the issue. The Clinical Abuse Preventive Education for Students (CAPES) team was formed in January 2022 to empower nursing students to identify, manage and report abusive situations, with its impact evaluated using Kirkpatrick’s four-level training evaluation framework.MethodsInterventions included the development and dissemination of infographic booklets, the establishment of CAPES Buddy for incident reporting and faculty training in psychological support skills. A total of 811 nursing students commenced training under the CAPES initiative between January 2023 and April 2024. Of these, 542 completed both baseline and follow-up surveys, which measured understanding of workplace harassment and confidence in responding. Additionally, 54 faculty members underwent psychological support training to strengthen their ability to support students in crisis.ResultsFollowing the interventions, nursing students demonstrated a 13.7% increase in their ability to identify harassment and an 18.7% increase in confidence to respond appropriately. Overall, 98% of students demonstrated comprehension of harassment-related issues. These improvements were accompanied by increased reporting and more appropriate triage of harassment cases, with students using CAPES Buddy for complex incidents while clinical instructors managed less severe cases as first-line support. Faculty members also reported enhanced competence in supporting students, contributing to a safer learning environment.ConclusionCAPES strengthened students’ awareness of workplace harassment and improved their confidence in responding and reporting incidents. While most students found the training relevant and beneficial, delayed reporting remains a challenge. Future work should explore factors contributing to reporting delay such as fear of retaliation or lack of trust in reporting systems and evaluate targeted strategies to address them.

Problem

Health professionals, particularly nurses, face a higher-than-average risk of workplace violence, with nurses being most prone to injury.1 Workplace violence is defined as any act or threat of physical violence, harassment, intimidation or disruptive behaviour occurring within the workplace.2 While often perpetrated by patients, violence can also stem from their families and visitors.3 4 Nursing students, due to their inexperience, may be even more vulnerable to such incidents.5

In one tertiary hospital in Singapore, the nursing education department, consisting of over 50 faculty members, oversees the clinical education of approximately 1462 nursing students annually from four academic institutions. The cases of workplace harassment reported among nursing students doubled from four in 2019 to eight in 2021. These incidents were often associated with delays in reporting which amplified the issue.

Nursing students play a critical role in clinical settings, where their education directly influences their professional development.6 The presence of workplace harassment may diminish their confidence, hinder the acquisition of essential clinical skills and reduce engagement in learning, ultimately compromising clinical learning outcomes, professional development and long-term retention in the nursing workforce.7

Background

Workplace harassment among healthcare workers, particularly nursing students, has become a pressing issue. Studies suggest that up to 50% of nursing students experience some form of mistreatment during their clinical rotations, though reporting rates may be lower due to fear of retaliation, under-reporting or normalisation of abusive behaviour. A recent meta-analysis found that 45.37% of nursing students experienced workplace violence, while 53.76% had witnessed it.8 The figures are comparable to and in some cases higher than those reported by qualified nurses. These statistics highlight the vulnerability of students, who lack both authority and confidence to respond effectively.

The consequences of workplace harassment are far-reaching. Emotional responses such as fear, shame, anxiety and loss of self-esteem are common among students who experience physical or verbal abuse.9 10 Verbal aggression can undermine students’ confidence, while sexual violence can lead to trauma and avoidance of specific patients.11 12 A significant number of students reported that workplace violence made them afraid to learn and negatively impacted patient care.13 In some cases, students even considered leaving the nursing profession due to their experiences (19.8%).13 Importantly, workplace harassment has been shown to disrupt learning, with nearly 1 in 5 students considering leaving the nursing profession due to such experiences.13

Despite these challenges, many incidents go unreported. Students often refrain from reporting due to fear of punishments, scepticism that reporting will lead to change or perceiving harassment as a normalised aspect of clinical culture. This highlights the need for transparent, accessible and supportive reporting mechanisms.12 14

Addressing workplace harassment requires a multifaceted approach that includes clear institutional policies, effective reporting systems and systematic training for both students and faculty.15 Prior studies have emphasised the importance of equipping students with practical skills to manage harassment and preparing them for the realities of clinical placements.12 16 17

In response to these challenges, the Clinical Abuse Preventive Education for Students (CAPES) team was established in January 2022. The CAPES team aims to empower nursing students to recognise, report and manage abusive situations, foster psychological safety and equip faculty with the necessary skills to support students facing harassment.

Measurement

To assess the impact of the CAPES initiative, a baseline survey and follow-up surveys were administered between January 2023 and April 2024. A total of 811 nursing students participated in CAPES training during this period, and 542 students completed both baseline and follow-up surveys. The remaining unmatched responses (n=269) resulted from operational constraints, including occasional missed survey distribution and short 2-week postings, and no data imputation was performed. Surveys measured nursing students’ understanding of workplace harassment and their confidence in responding to it. The inclusion criterion was enrolment in a clinical placement at the study site, and no exclusion criteria were applied.

Data collection and frequency

Baseline surveys were administered on the first day of students’ clinical placements, while follow-up surveys were conducted immediately after completion of their placements. Clinical placement ranged from 2 to 10 weeks in duration. Administering the follow-up survey at the end of each placement, regardless of length, ensured consistency by capturing students’ experiences within the same relative learning context. This design allowed for meaningful pre-post comparison and attribution of observed changes to the CAPES initiatives.

Evaluation framework

Kirkpatrick’s four levels of training evaluation was used to assess CAPES outcomes. Student satisfaction and perceived relevance were measured using post-training feedback (Level 1: reaction). Knowledge and awareness were assessed through baseline and follow-up quizzes (Level 2: learning). Confidence and reporting behaviour were evaluated using surveys and CAPES Buddy activation data (Level 3: behaviour). Organisational outcomes, including increased reporting rates and enhanced faculty capacity in psychological support, represented the results level (Level 4: results).

Design

The CAPES initiatives were designed using Knowles’ adult learning principles to enhance engagement, support self-directed learning and ensure application in practice.18 19

Intervention components

Self-concept and self-directed learning

Recognising that adults are independent learners, the CAPES team curated infographic booklets and introduced CAPES Buddy, an online reporting tool, to support self-directed learning. These resources were accessible at students’ own pace, tailoring learning to individual needs. Engagement was reinforced through reminders during pre-clinical orientation and regular check-ins by clinical instructors throughout their clinical placements.

Experience-based learning and problem-centred approaches

Case studies, role-play and group discussions based on real harassment scenarios were integrated into teaching sessions, enabling students to practise critical thinking and problem solving while applying knowledge in realistic contexts. These activities encouraged reflection on real-life clinical encounters, promoted peer learning and were evaluated through reflective debriefs at the end of each session.

Psychological support and faculty training

In addition to empowering students, the CAPES initiatives also focused on equipping teaching faculty with the skills needed to support students facing workplace harassment. 54 faculty members attended Crisis Support for Individual Resilience, an 8-hour course conducted by the Department of Trauma Recovery and Corporate Solutions. The training covered principles of psychological first aid, including recognising signs of stress and crisis reactions, applying the CARE (Communicate with empathy, Awareness of struggles, Risk Assessment, Empower to make changes) framework to provide crisis support, guiding distressed individuals towards professional help and practising self-care. While formal evaluation of this externally conducted course was not available, faculty members who attended the course subsequently facilitated student debriefs and check-ins, integrating psychological support into educational practice and contributing to a safer learning environment.

Strategy for improvement and implementation

Our strategy to address workplace harassment among nursing students was developed through stakeholder consultations and refined across several Plan-Do-Study-Act (PDSA) cycles, using a three-pronged approach of education, reporting and support.

PDSA cycle 1—Education foundations: preclinical Orientation with infographic booklets was introduced to build baseline awareness. Early feedback highlighted the need for a clearer reporting pathway, leading to the creation of CAPES Buddy.

PDSA cycle 2—Strengthening reporting: initial use of CAPES Buddy raised confidentiality concerns. Reporting guidelines were clarified and emphasised to our students during orientations.

PDSA cycle 3—Encouraging disclosure: ongoing hesitancy to share experiences prompted integration of role-play and structured debriefs to normalise conversations about harassment.

PDSA cycle 4—Enhancing support: faculty completed psychological first aid training to strengthen first-line support and promote psychological safety.

Through this iterative process, CAPES evolved from a single-session intervention into a sustainable, multi-pronged initiative that improved students’ awareness, confidence, reporting behaviours and access to support.

For nursing students

Preclinical orientation programme: A 45-min face-to-face session, conducted by CAPES team members to educate nursing students on identifying, preventing, responding to and reporting workplace harassment.

For clinical instructors

Upskilling faculty: to ensure teaching faculty were equipped to support students, we integrated the Crisis Support for Individual Resilience Course into their onboarding. This training ensures that faculty can sensitively and effectively respond to harassment situations, fostering a culture of trust and psychological safety.

Faculty observation: initially, the Preclinical Orientation Programme was delivered only during consolidated postings. To expand our reach, we upskilled the teaching faculty to conduct CAPES briefings on Day 1 of nursing student postings. Observations were followed by co-facilitation and feedback from senior clinical instructors, ensuring consistency in delivery. Faculty effectiveness was assessed through student feedback surveys, which highlighted improved confidence and competence in handling students’ concerns.

For CAPES core team

Ongoing training and rotational support: CAPES team members undergo regular training in post-incident dialogue, psychological first aid, breakaway techniques and relevant courses to strengthen their ability to manage harassment cases and support distressed students. They also remain aligned with institutional staff-protection policies, and violence and aggression management workflows. To ensure timely assistance, 2 of the 7 team members are rostered weekly to respond to CAPES activations, with all cases and follow-up actions logged in a tracking sheet for continuity and shared learning.

Faculty engagement: initially, the CAPES core team were the main contact for harassment incidents, leaving faculty less involved. Buy-in was reinforced through recognition at departmental meetings, sharing positive student feedback and peer mentoring opportunities, with collective recognition when CAPES received awards.

Level 3: behaviour

Behavioural shifts in reporting

Students’ confidence in reporting harassment improved. In 2023, 5 of 7 cases were submitted via CAPES Buddy. In 2024, 2 of 9 cases were submitted through the platform. However, this reflects a mechanism shift rather than reduced reporting. With the hospital-wide zero-tolerance policy and improved faculty training, clinical instructors increasingly provided first-line psychological support, managing straightforward cases directly and escalating complex incidents to CAPES.

These trends reflect greater student confidence and faculty readiness to act, complementing Level 1 and 2 gains, demonstrating a progression from awareness to appropriate action and escalation.

Level 4: organisational impact

The CAPES initiatives contributed to measurable changes in the broader organisational environment.

Enhanced clinical learning environment

Nursing students reported feeling safer and more supported during their clinical placements, which fostered stronger engagement in clinical learning. Students also demonstrated greater ability to manage abusive encounters, contributing to fewer unreported incidents.

Improved workplace culture

A clear cultural shift was observed, with greater recognition of the importance of addressing workplace harassment. Feedback from clinical instructors captured through discussions and faculty check-ins indicated increased confidence in providing first-line psychological support and more open communication with students. Faculty became increasingly proactive in identifying and addressing harassment, fostering a more supportive and respectful training environment.

Together, these outcomes suggest that CAPES not only strengthened students’ knowledge and confidence (Levels 1–3) but also built faculty capacity, contributing to a safer and more responsive clinical learning environment. Long-term evaluation of cultural change and student retention remains an important next step.

In conclusion, the CAPES initiatives improved nursing students’ knowledge, confidence and reporting behaviours, while strengthening faculty capability and fostering a culture of zero tolerance for harassment. These outcomes support the long-term value of embedding CAPES into institutional practice.

Lessons learned

Increased awareness leads to more reporting

Greater awareness of workplace harassment resulted in more student-reported incidents. While we initially anticipated a decline in cases, the rise in reporting reflects increased recognition and confidence in speaking up. This is consistent with the intended outcome of CAPES and a mark of psychological safety rather than worsening conditions.

Empowerment through education

CAPES empowered students to recognise, respond to and report harassment. To sustain this impact, CAPES 2.0 will incorporate bystander intervention strategies, reinforcing shared responsibility among students and healthcare professionals in creating a safe clinical environment.

Need for a tiered teaching approach

Feedback indicated that repeating the same content annually limited learning progression. We identified the need for a tiered model. In year one, students will focus on understanding, identifying harassment and reporting procedures. In year two, we will introduce the 5Ds for bystander intervention, and in year three, students will have the opportunity to role-play real-life scenarios, reinforcing their skills and confidence in applying their knowledge.

Strengthening faculty and peer support networks

Upskilling clinical instructors enhanced students’ access to timely support, increasing the likelihood that incidents were addressed early and reported appropriately. Peer support also emerged as an important facilitator of psychological safety during clinical placements.

Limitations

Increased reporting does not equate to decreased incidence

While the increase in reports may reflect improved recognition and willingness to speak up, it does not necessarily indicate a decrease in the occurrence of harassment itself. We are hopeful that with continued education and sustained organisational support, the number of harassment incidents will decline over time.

Potential bias in reporting

Self-reported data may be affected by under-reporting, over-reporting or social desirability, particularly as surveys were administered by educators. Anonymity and the absence of grading implications helped mitigate these risks.

Limited data on long-term impact

Findings reflect short-term outcomes. The long-term influence of CAPES on reporting behaviours, career experiences and workplace culture remains unknown. Tracking graduates is challenging due to movement between institutions. Future longitudinal evaluation aligned with CAPES 2.0 may help assess sustained impact.

Generalisability

This study was conducted in a single tertiary hospital in Singapore with specific institutional policies, including POHA-related20 protections and a zero-tolerance stance. Adaptation may be required before applying CAPES to other settings.

Conclusion

The CAPES initiative improved students’ knowledge, confidence and reporting behaviours, with rising reports reflecting increased recognition of harassment and trust in the reporting systems. Using Kirkpatrick’s model, gains were observed across awareness, action and faculty engagement. To address ongoing delays in reporting, CAPES 2.0 will incorporate targeted PDSA cycles, including a tiered teaching model introducing the 5Ds bystander intervention and scenario-based application in later years, refinement of the CAPES Buddy user interface to streamline reporting, and scheduled faculty check-ins during clinical placements to prompt earlier disclosure. Future efforts will continue to focus on sustaining cultural change and evaluating long-term outcomes as CAPES evolves.

Sustainability and spread

Institutional sustainability

CAPES is embedded in the nursing curriculum, with faculty continually trained in crisis support and harassment response. Core team members are rostered weekly to support students, and scenarios are updated regularly based on recent incidents. Under CAPES 2.0, the team will conduct role-play sessions with year three students to reinforce skills and confidence in applying the 5Ds of bystander intervention, ensuring sustained engagement.

Reducing harassment incidents

While reporting has increased, the long-term goal is to reduce incidents. CAPES 2.0 will introduce bystander training and stronger leadership engagement to reinforce zero tolerance and institutional accountability. CAPES is also exploring interprofessional education, with potential extension of training to junior doctors, medical students and allied health professionals to strengthen collective responsibility for a safe clinical environment.

Spread to other institutions

CAPES initiative has been presented nationally, generating interest from other hospitals. Two institutions have approached the team for detailed information and suggestions. In some contexts, adaptations are required. For example, hospitals that host students only in year two for short, specialised postings (eg, paediatrics or gynaecology) may require a modified version of CAPES. To facilitate future spread, the team plans to develop a replication toolkit containing teaching materials, scenario banks, reporting workflows and faculty onboarding guidance.

Investment and cost savings

While the project required initial investment in materials, training and resource development, the potential savings associated with reducing incidents of harassment and fostering a safer work environment for nursing students are substantial. CAPES has the potential to reduce absenteeism, attrition and litigation risk, which in turn may generate long-term organisational value. While we did not conduct a formal cost-benefit analysis, these areas represent potential savings that could outweigh initial costs.
