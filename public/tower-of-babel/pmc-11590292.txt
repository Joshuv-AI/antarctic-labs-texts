Application of 3D printing technology in preoperative planning and treatment of proximal humerus fractures: a retrospective study
Authors: Dong Li, Rong Zhou, Chunjian Song, Jianhua Lu, Jun Lu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe application of 3D printing technology in preoperative planning and treatment of these fractures has shown promise in improving surgical efficiency, trauma, and patient outcomes. This study aims to demonstrate the advantages of applying 3D printing technology to the treatment of proximal humeral fractures by comparing cases selected for 3D printing with a control group (conventional surgery group), in order to further promote the application of 3D technology in a broader range of trauma treatments.MethodsThis study conducted the clinical data of 51 patients with proximal humeral fractures. Inclusion criteria encompassed patients diagnosed with Neer II and III type proximal humerus fractures. Among them, 24 patients underwent traditional surgery, while 27 patients underwent surgery with 3D printing technology support. Intraoperative parameters, functional outcomes, radiographic results at 6 months, and pain scores were collected and analyzed.ResultsThe 3D printing group demonstrated significantly reduced total surgery time compared to the traditional surgery group (P < 0.05). Implant placement accuracy was significantly higher in the 3D printing group compared to the traditional surgery group (P < 0.05). Functional outcomes at 6 months favored the 3D printing group, with higher Constant-Murley Score and lower Disability of the Arm, Shoulder, and Hand (DASH) Score compared to the traditional surgery group (P < 0.05). Additionally, the 3D printing group showed a significantly lower prevalence of heterotopic ossification compared to the traditional surgery group (P < 0.05). Preoperative and early postoperative pain scores were significantly lower in the 3D printing group compared to the traditional surgery group (P < 0.05).ConclusionThe integration of 3D printing technology into the preoperative planning and treatment of complex proximal humerus fractures demonstrated significant advantages in surgical efficiency, functional outcomes, radiographic prognosis, and pain management.Keywords: Printing, three-dimensional; Preoperative period; Proximal femoral fractures; Therapy

Introduction

Proximal humeral fractures, including fractures at or above the surgical neck, were common injuries, accounting for approximately 4–5% of all body fractures [1–3]. There were various types of proximal humeral fractures, and regardless of their complexity, there was still no clear consensus on their management, leading to a relatively high disability rate [4, 5]. Traditional surgical methods typically rely on 2D imaging modalities such as plain X-rays and computed tomography (CT) for preoperative planning, which may not fully reflect the complexity of the fracture types [6, 7]. Since its inception in the 1980s, 3D printing technology has matured in various fields and has now extended its applications to various disciplines within the medical field, including neurosurgery, spinal surgery, plastic surgery, otolaryngology, and human anatomy [8, 9]. 3D printing technology offers precise preoperative planning, which can enhance the success rate of open reduction and internal fixation procedures, reduce postoperative complications, and achieve the goals of precision and personalized treatment [10, 11]. For example, Previous studies have indicated that preoperative 64-row spiral CT scans of the ankle joint, using the DICOM format to generate STL format data for 3D printing, and subsequent 1:1 model printing with a 3D printer, resulted in an 87.5% success rate in achieving anatomical reduction and fixation in ankle fractures [12]. During the follow-up period, none of the patients experienced complications such as loss of reduction, internal fixation loosening, or avascular necrosis of the talus. Furthermore, research has also explored the application of 3D printing technology in surgery for degenerative lumbar scoliosis in the elderly. The findings suggest that 3D-printed lumbar spine models and pedicle screw guides can improve the accuracy of pedicle screw placement, reduce surgical time, and decrease intraoperative blood loss and fluoroscopy usage [13].

The use of 3D printing technology in orthopedic surgery has gained significant attention due to its ability to provide patient-specific models and guides that enhance preoperative planning and surgical accuracy [14]. 3D printing has been successfully applied in various orthopedic procedures, including joint replacement surgeries, spinal surgeries, and the management of complex fractures [15]. For individual cases with significant variations in proximal humeral fractures, especially complex fractures, 3D printing offers unique applications that align with the need for personalized treatment [16, 17]. 3D printing technology enhances preoperative planning through the establishment of fracture models, guiding surgical treatment, reducing surgical time, and minimizing operative trauma. Despite these advancements, the impact of 3D printing on clinical outcomes, such as functional recovery and patient-reported outcomes, remains underexplored. This study aims to demonstrate the advantages of applying 3D printing technology to the treatment of proximal humeral fractures by comparing cases selected for 3D printing with a control group, in order to further promote the application of 3D technology in a broader range of trauma treatments.

Materials and methods

Study design and population

This study is a retrospective cohort study. This study analyzed the clinical data of 51 patients with proximal humerus fractures admitted to our hospital from January 2023 to June 2023. Informed consent was waived for this study due to the exclusive use of de-identified patient data, which posed no potential harm or impact on patient care. This research has been approved by the authors’ affiliated institutions. Inclusion criteria: Patients diagnosed with Neer II and III type proximal humerus fractures; time from fracture to treatment < 2 weeks, all cases were fresh closed fractures; patients with normal coagulation function; patients with complete clinical data. Exclusion criteria: Patients with large area infections around the affected shoulder joint; those with difficult-to-repair rotator cuff tears (incurable by surgery); individuals with a history of shoulder joint disease or surgery; those with severe internal medicine complications (such as severe heart failure, active hematological malignancies or severe coagulopathies, severe liver disease), stroke, tumors, and other diseases. According to inclusion and exclusion criteria, among 51 patients with proximal humerus fractures, 24 patients underwent traditional surgery, while 27 patients underwent surgery with 3D printing technology support (Fig. 1).

Preoperative planning and operative technique

In traditional surgery group, patients underwent treatment using the open reduction and internal fixation with locking compression plate technique for proximal humerus fractures. Patients were placed in the supine position and received general anesthesia. Following venous access, sterile draping and disinfection were performed to ensure aseptic conditions for the procedure. An incision was made through the deltoid muscle, the fractured shoulder was exposed, soft tissue dissection was performed, and careful protection of the blood supply to the soft tissue was ensured. After debridement of the fractured bone, reduction was achieved, an appropriately sized locking compression plate was selected, and it was secured with screws. Postoperatively, wound irrigation, drainage, and closure were performed.

The patients in the 3D printing group underwent a process of 3D printing-assisted preoperative planning. Preoperatively, thin-slice CT data of the patient’s affected shoulder was obtained and used for three-dimensional reconstruction in Mimics software (version 22.0, Materialise NV, Leuven, Belgium), the proximal humerus was segmented from the CT images. This involved manually outlining the region of interest (ROI) on each slice of the CT scan. The segmented ROI was then used to generate a 3D surface model of the proximal humerus. This process involved interpolating between the outlined slices to create a smooth 3D surface, create a model of the fractured shoulder and a reduced fracture model. The model generation took approximately 1 h per case. The 3D models were used for surgical simulation on-screen, which involved planning the optimal surgical approach, implant selection, and pre-contouring of plates if necessary. This facilitated preoperative planning for the placement of the plate and screws, aimed at minimizing surgical time and ensuring procedural efficiency. For implant capture, we used virtual implants that were available in the Mimics Innovation Suite library. The virtual implants were placed and adjusted in the 3D model to simulate the surgical procedure. This step took approximately 1 h per case. The 3D printing method employed was additive manufacturing, specifically Fused Deposition Modeling (FDM). This method builds parts by extruding thermoplastic filament layer by layer according to the STL file derived from the patients’ CT scans. The final 3D models were exported as STL files and printed using a FDM 3D printer (Ultimaker S5, Ultimaker B.V., Geldermalsen, Netherlands). The printing process took approximately 4 h per model (Fig. 2). During the surgery, patients were placed in the supine position and received a brachial plexus and axillary block anesthesia. An incision was made through the deltoid muscle, the fractured shoulder was exposed, soft tissue dissection was performed, and careful protection of the blood supply to the soft tissue was ensured. Temporary fixation of the reduced fracture was done using Kirschner wires, and under C-arm fluoroscopy guidance, an appropriately selected plate was placed based on preoperative simulation. Screws of suitable length were percutaneously inserted, ensuring shoulder cuff repair. Subsequently, after confirming satisfactory shoulder joint mobility, fracture reduction, and plate screw positioning, wound irrigation, hemostasis, and closure were performed to conclude the surgery. Data collection and outcome measures

Data collection

General patient data (age, gender, body mass index (BMI), fracture severity, hypertension, diabetes, hyperlipidemia, smoking history, dominant arm, mechanism of injury), intraoperative parameters (total surgery time, blood loss, implant placement accuracy), functional scores (Constant-Murley score, DASH score, range of motion, return to work) and pain scores were obtained from the medical records system and imaging parameters. These data were collected by a single experienced physician.

Constant-murley score

The Constant-Murley score, excluding muscle strength, was used to assess shoulder joint function before and after treatment. The total score was 75 points, with 15 points for pain, and 10 points each for daily activity level, hand positioning, forward flexion, abduction, external rotation, and internal rotation. A higher score indicates better shoulder joint function. The Cronbach’s alpha of Constant-Murley was 0.819 [18].

DASH score

The DASH score was used to evaluate patients’ upper limb function, consisting of Parts A and B. Part A assesses physical function with 23 items, scored 1 to 5 for each item. Part B assesses upper limb pain and discomfort and comprises 7 items, also scored 1 to 5 for each. The DASH score was calculated as (Part A score + Part B score − 30) / 1.2, with a total score of 0-100 points, and scores were inversely correlated with limb function. The internal consistency and the test-retest were Cronbach’s alpha 0.969 and interclass correlation coefficient 0.861, respectively [19].

Pain scoring

The Visual Analog Scale (VAS) was a commonly used pain assessment tool, utilized for evaluating pain intensity, including chronic and postoperative pain. It typically consists of a horizontal line or vertical bar with “no pain” at one end and “worst imaginable pain” at the other. Patients mark a point on the line to indicate their current pain level. Scores range from 0, representing no pain, to 10, indicating the most severe pain that requires immediate intervention. The Cronbach’s alpha of VAS was 0.94 [20].

Radiographic results

Radiological signs of nonunion are defined as: Lack of bridging callus formation across the fracture site. Persistent or increased fracture line visibility. Absence of trabecular bone bridging the fracture gap. Possible shortening or malalignment of the affected segment. The presence of bone callus, the status of the fracture line, and the degree of remodeling were assessed using the RUSH (Radiological Union Score for Humerus) scoring system.

Radiological signs of implant failure are defined as: Displacement or migration of the implant. Loosening of screws or plates, indicated by gaps between the implant and bone. Fracture through the implant material. Evidence of stress shielding leading to bone resorption around the implant. The position and movement of the implant relative to the bone were assessed using the Radiological Evaluation of Implant Stability (REIS).

Radiological signs of heterotopic ossification are defined as: Abnormal bone formation outside the normal skeletal structure, typically near joints. Often appears as cloud-like densities adjacent to the surgical site or joint capsule. May progress from soft tissue calcifications to mature bone over time. Heterotopic ossification was categorized into four grades (I to IV) using the Brooker classification system. Grade I is minimal ossification not crossing joint lines, while Grade IV involves severe ossification that limits joint motion.

Radiological signs of osteonecrosis are defined as: Loss of subchondral bone density. Subchondral cysts or lucencies. Joint space narrowing. Bone necrosis and the extent of involvement were diagnosed using the Steinberg criteria, and grading its severity based on radiographic and clinical findings.

Statistical analysis

The data were analyzed using SPSS 25.0 statistical software (SPSS Inc., Chicago, USA). For categorical data, [n (%)] was used for representation. The chi-square test was applied with the basic formula when the sample size was ≥ 40 and the theoretical frequency T was ≥ 5, with the test statistic represented by χ2. When the sample size was ≥ 40 but the theoretical frequency 1 ≤ T < 5, the chi-square test was adjusted using the correction formula. In cases where the sample size was < 40 or the theoretical frequency T < 1, statistical analysis was conducted using Fisher’s exact probability method. The Shapiro-Wilk test was used to assess the normal distribution of continuous variables. For normally distributed continuous data were expressed as mean ± standard difference (SD). Non-normally distributed data were analyzed using Wilcoxon rank-sum test. P < 0.05 were considered as statistical significance.

Results

Basic characteristics

In the comparative analysis of patient characteristics, the traditional surgery group and 3D printing group exhibited similar baseline demographics (Table 1). The mean age in the traditional surgery group was 58.75 ± 5.32 years, compared to 59.31 ± 4.78 years in the 3D printing group (P > 0.05), showing no significant difference between the groups. Similarly, Gender distribution, body mass index (BMI), fracture severity, prevalence of hypertension, diabetes, hyperlipidemia, smoking history, dominant arm, and mechanism of injury demonstrated no significant differences between the two groups, as evidenced by non-significant P values in all respective comparisons (P > 0.05). Overall, the two groups were well-balanced with regard to basic characteristics.

Intraoperative parameters

In analyzing intraoperative parameters, the 3D printing group exhibited notable advantages over the traditional surgery group (Table 2). The mean total surgery time in the 3D printing group was 122.48 ± 12.75 min, significantly less than the traditional surgery group’s mean of 130.75 ± 15.32 min. For duration from fracture to surgery, blood loss and number of intraoperative imaging procedures, the difference did not reach statistical significance. Notably, the 3D printing group achieved higher implant placement accuracy, significantly surpassing the traditional surgery group. These results indicate that the application of 3D printing technology in preoperative planning and treatment of complex proximal humerus fractures led to significantly reduced surgery time and improved implant placement accuracy.

Functional outcomes

The comparison of functional outcomes at 6 months revealed notable differences favoring the 3D printing group over the traditional surgery group (Fig. 4). The mean Constant-Murley Score in the 3D printing group was significantly higher compared to the traditional surgery group. In addition, DASH score favored the 3D printing group, significantly lower than the traditional surgery group. Although the range of motion displayed a non-significant difference, with the 3D printing group exhibiting a slightly higher compared to the traditional surgery group, the return to work duration was also noticeably reduced in the 3D printing group, indicating an improving trend compared to the traditional surgery group. It suggested that the application of 3D printing technology in preoperative planning and treatment of complex proximal humerus fractures was associated with superior functional outcomes, as evidenced by higher functional scores and potentially quicker return to work.

Radiographic outcomes

The comparison of radiographic outcomes at 6 months revealed notable distinctions between the traditional surgery group and the 3D printing group (Table 3). The incidence of non-union displayed minimal variation, with 8.33% in the traditional surgery group compared to 3.7% in the 3D printing group, indicating no significant difference. However, the presence of heterotopic ossification significantly favored the 3D printing group, with only 11.11% compared to 45.83% in the traditional surgery group. Conversely, the rates of implant failure and osteonecrosis exhibited no statistically significant differences between the two groups, highlighting an advantage of the 3D printing approach in reducing the occurrence of heterotopic ossification, a known complication in the management of complex proximal humerus fractures. These findings suggest that the application of 3D printing technology in preoperative planning and treatment may contribute to improved radiographic outcomes, particularly in reducing the incidence of heterotopic ossification.

Patient pain levels

The comparison of patient pain levels between the traditional surgery group and the 3D printing group revealed significant differences in favor of the 3D printing approach (Table 4). The preoperative pain score, measured using the VAS, was significantly lower in the 3D printing group compared to the traditional surgery group, demonstrating a notable difference. Furthermore, the postoperative 1-week pain scores also favored the 3D printing group, significantly lower than that of the traditional surgery group. While the postoperative 6-week pain score displayed a statistically significant difference, with lower scores in the 3D printing group compared to the traditional surgery group, the postoperative 3-month and 6-month pain scores showed no significant differences between the two groups. These findings indicate that the application of 3D printing technology in preoperative planning and treatment of complex proximal humerus fractures was associated with reduced preoperative pain levels and improved early postoperative pain control.

Discussion

The application of 3D printing technology in preoperative planning and treatment of complex proximal humerus fractures has gained significant attention in the field of orthopedic surgery [21]. In this prospective cohort study, we evaluated surgical efficiency, trauma, and patient outcomes following 3D printing-assisted surgery compared to traditional surgical approaches. Our findings reveal notable advantages associated with the use of 3D printing technology, suggesting its potential in improving surgical outcomes and patient recovery. The findings of this study indicate several key advantages associated with the use of 3D printing technology in the context of complex proximal humerus fractures. One of the significant benefits identified was the improvement in surgical efficiency. The 3D printing group exhibited a significantly reduced total surgery time compared to the traditional surgery group. This reduction in surgical time was a crucial factor in enhancing procedural efficiency and minimizing the overall impact of surgery on patients. Additionally, the 3D printing group demonstrated improved implant placement accuracy, a factor that was vital in ensuring the long-term stability and function of the shoulder joint following surgical intervention. These findings align with previous research highlighting the ability of 3D printing to streamline surgical procedures and optimize implant placement in orthopedic surgeries [22].

Furthermore, the study revealed superior functional outcomes associated with the use of 3D printing technology. Patients in the 3D printing group attained higher Constant-Murley and DASH scores, indicating better shoulder joint function and reduced upper limb disability compared to those in the traditional surgery group. These results were consistent with previous literature suggesting that 3D printing-assisted surgical approaches may lead to improved functional recovery and reduced disability in orthopedic patients [23]. In terms of radiographic outcomes, the study highlighted a notable advantage of 3D printing in reducing the incidence of heterotopic ossification, a common complication in the management of proximal humerus fractures. The lower prevalence of heterotopic ossification in the 3D printing group signifies a potential benefit in mitigating postoperative complications and improving the overall radiographic prognosis for patients undergoing surgical treatment for these fractures. This finding aligns with the growing body of evidence supporting the role of 3D printing technology in optimizing implant fit and alignment [14], which, in turn, may contribute to reducing the occurrence of complications such as heterotopic ossification in orthopedic surgery.

Moreover, the study provided valuable insights into patient pain levels following surgical intervention. The 3D printing group exhibited significantly lower preoperative and early postoperative pain scores, indicating a potential advantage of 3D printing technology in contributing to improved pain management during the early stages of recovery. This finding was consistent with the concept of personalized surgical planning facilitated by 3D printing [15], which allows for tailored approaches to fracture reduction and fixation, potentially minimizing soft tissue trauma and postoperative pain. The integration of 3D printing technology into preoperative planning and treatment of complex proximal humerus fractures offers several benefits attributed to technological advancements, personalized treatment approaches, and enhanced precision in surgical interventions [24]. Technological advancements in 3D printing have revolutionized orthopedic surgery by enabling the creation of patient-specific models, implants, and surgical guides using advanced imaging modalities like CT scans [25]. This allows for precise anatomical models of fracture patterns, empowering surgeons to develop customized surgical plans tailored to the patient’s unique anatomy, optimizing implant fit and alignment [26]. Additionally, 3D printing enables the creation of intricate, patient-specific guides for accurate implant placement, leading to improved surgical precision and reduced operative time [27]. The continuous refinement of 3D printing techniques and materials further enhances its utility in orthopedic procedures, fostering a paradigm shift towards personalized and optimized surgical interventions [28].

Incorporating 3D printing technology into the preoperative planning and treatment of complex proximal humerus fractures not only improves surgical outcomes but also has significant economic implications. A comprehensive cost-benefit analysis is essential to assess the economic feasibility of widespread adoption of this technology. The initial investment for 3D printing technology includes the cost of the 3D printer, software, and materials. These costs can be substantial but are offset by the potential for long-term savings due to improved surgical outcomes and reduced revision surgeries. As demonstrated in this study, 3D printing technology significantly reduces the total surgery time. Shorter operative times translate into fewer resources required in the operating room, including anesthesia, staff, and equipment, leading to direct cost savings. In the traditional surgery group, patients received general anesthesia due to the extensive surgical field and the need for complete immobility. Conversely, in the 3D printing group, a regional anesthesia approach, including a brachial plexus and axillary block, was utilized given the less invasive nature of the procedure and the ability to monitor the patient’s response during surgery. Improved implant placement accuracy and reduced postoperative complications, such as heterotopic ossification, result in fewer revision surgeries and decreased hospital readmissions. The long-term impact of 3D printing technology on the healthcare system depends on its widespread adoption. As the technology becomes more prevalent, economies of scale will likely reduce the costs associated with 3D printing, making it more accessible and affordable.

The implementation of 3D printing technology allows for individualized and patient-specific treatment approaches, addressing the variability in fracture patterns and patient anatomy [29]. Surgeons can simulate fracture reduction and preoperatively plan the optimal positioning of fixation devices tailored to the patient’s unique bone morphology, minimizing soft tissue trauma and promoting a stable reconstruction of the proximal humerus [30]. Furthermore, 3D-printed surgical guides facilitate the precise execution of preoperatively planned strategies, contributing to improved intraoperative efficiency and accuracy [31]. Customizing treatment plans based on individual patient anatomy and fracture characteristics optimizes surgical outcomes and patient recovery. The precision afforded by 3D printing technology plays a pivotal role in improving the outcomes of surgical interventions for complex proximal humerus fractures. The creation of patient-specific anatomical models and surgical guides allows for meticulous preoperative planning, enhancing the precision and accuracy of surgical maneuvers while customizing implants and fixation devices to match the patient’s specific anatomical requirements [32]. This optimized precision contributes to improved functional outcomes, reduced postoperative complications, and enhanced long-term stability of the reconstructed shoulder joint [33]. In summary, the benefits of integrating 3D printing technology into the preoperative planning and treatment of complex proximal humerus fractures encompass technological advancements, personalized treatment approaches, and enhanced precision in surgical interventions, ultimately improving patient outcomes and redefining contemporary standards of fracture management [34].

Implementing 3D printing technology in clinical settings presents several practical challenges that must be addressed to ensure successful integration and optimal utilization. Surgeons must develop the technical skills necessary to interpret 3D models and use them effectively in surgical planning. This involves understanding how to manipulate digital models and visualize the surgical procedure. Adequate infrastructure, including 3D printers, software, and dedicated personnel, is required to produce high-quality 3D models. Hospitals and clinics must invest in the necessary equipment and facilities. Quality assurance protocols must be established to ensure that 3D printed models and guides meet safety and efficacy standards. This includes regular calibration and maintenance of 3D printers. While 3D printing technology offers significant benefits in the preoperative planning and treatment of complex proximal humerus fractures, addressing the learning curve for surgeons, resource requirements, and regulatory considerations is crucial for successful implementation. Collaborative efforts between clinicians, engineers, and regulatory bodies are necessary to overcome these challenges and ensure the widespread adoption of 3D printing technology in orthopedic surgery. Although the advantages of incorporating 3D printing technology into the preoperative planning and treatment of complex proximal humerus fractures were considerable, it was essential to recognise the constraints of this study. A limitation of this study is the relatively limited sample size, which may introduce selection bias and affect the generalisability of the experimental results. For instance, no notable discrepancy was identified between the two cohorts with respect to major complications, such as osteonecrosis in proximal humerus fractures following surgery. Additionally, the study primarily focuses on the potential benefits of 3D printing in surgical interventions. However, further research is required to comprehensively evaluate its long-term impact on patient outcomes, cost-effectiveness, and the extent of its adoption across diverse clinical settings. Moreover, the study does not sufficiently address the potential challenges and barriers associated with the implementation of 3D printing technology in orthopaedic surgery. These include the learning curve for surgeons, resource requirements and regulatory considerations. These limitations highlight the need for continued research to fully understand the implications and practical implementation of 3D printing in the management of proximal humerus fractures.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the findings of this prospective cohort study support the benefits of integrating 3D printing technology into the preoperative planning and treatment of complex proximal humerus fractures. The observed improvements in surgical efficiency, functional outcomes, radiographic prognosis, and pain management underscore the potential of 3D printing to enhance the overall care and outcomes of patients undergoing surgical intervention for these challenging fractures.
