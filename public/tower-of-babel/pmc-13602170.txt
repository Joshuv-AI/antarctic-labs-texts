Mapping sex‐specific associations of adiposity with age‐related cognitive decline and multimodal Alzheimer's pathology
Authors: Jingjing Zhang, Dan Liu, Yueran Yang, Xiaoyu Lou, Hongwei Ren, Wei Tan, Yan Zeng, for the Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative, and the Hubei Memory and Aging Cohort Study
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONThe adiposity–cognition relationship in aging is controversial. This study aimed to assess its sex‐ and age‐specific associations with Alzheimer’s disease (AD) pathology using multimodal biomarkers.METHODSIn two longitudinal cohorts (Hubei Memory and Aging Cohort Study [HMACS], n = 2,349; Alzheimer’s Disease Neuroimaging Initiative [ADNI], n = 1,858), we assessed body fat via Clínica Universidad de Navarra‐Body Adiposity Estimator (CUN‐BAE) and performed sex‐/age‐stratified analyses using multimodal biomarkers (plasma, cerebrospinal fluid [CSF], Positron Emission Computed Tomography [PET]).RESULTSIn adults ≥65 years of age, higher baseline and increasing CUN‐BAE were associated with lower risk of AD‐related mild cognitive impairment (MCI) and dementia, with stratified analyses suggesting distinct patterns by sex and age; however, formal interaction tests were non‐significant. Each 1% increase in ΔCUN‐BAE at ages 70–74 was associated with lower normal cognition (NC)‐to‐MCI transition risk by 20%. In females, higher CUN‐BAE associated with lower phosphorylated tau 217 (p‐tau217) and nonlinearly with amyloid beta (Aβ)42 with an inflection point near 37.6%. In males, increasing adiposity correlated with lower p‐tau217 and neurofilament light chain (NfL). Neuroimaging revealed inverse associations with Aβ deposition in females and with tau deposition in Aβ‐positive males with MCI.DISCUSSIONHigher adiposity was associated with lower incidence of AD‐related decline and less pronounced pathology; stratified exploratory analyses suggested patterns by age and sex, although formal interaction tests were non‐significant. However, these observational findings do not support recommendations of blindly increasing body fat for dementia prevention.

BACKGROUND

The global shift toward an aging population underscores the critical need to promote healthy aging, 1 with preserving cognitive health as a central goal. 2 As the primary risk factor for cognitive decline and Alzheimer's disease (AD), aging makes AD and its precursor, mild cognitive impairment (MCI), major barriers to this objective. 3 Identifying modifiable factors associated with lower risk, therefore, is essential for developing effective intervention strategies.

Body composition is a key modifiable factor linked to cognitive aging. 4 , 5 Although midlife obesity is a well‐established risk factor for dementia, 6 the relationship becomes complex and paradoxical in older age, exemplified by the obesity paradox phenomenon. 7 This complexity may arise from multifactorial interactions, 8 , 9 including critical age dependency, 10 significant sexual dimorphism likely driven by sex‐specific biological pathways, 11 , 12 and potential AD‐pathology specificity. 13

Specifically, the association between adiposity and cognition across the lifespan follows a paradoxical trajectory: midlife obesity consistently elevates late‐life dementia risk, whereas in older adults, higher adiposity has been repeatedly linked to better cognitive performance, lower dementia incidence, and reduced mortality. 7 , 14 Unintentional late‐life weight loss often precedes clinical cognitive decline by several years, suggesting that leanness in advanced age may reflect a prodromal marker rather than a protective state. 15 The association between adiposity and lower cognitive decline risk appears concentrated within specific age windows, particularly among the young‐old elderly, and attenuates in more advanced age, 10 , 16 indicating that age functions not merely as a confounder to be adjusted for, but as a biological effect modifier that necessitates age‐stratified analyses.

Similarly, sex represents a fundamental biological variable that shapes both adipose tissue biology and AD risk. Females face a higher lifetime risk of AD, exhibit faster longitudinal tau accumulation, and carry greater overall pathology burden. 11 , 17 , 18 Epidemiological studies have reported that the association between obesity and cognitive function varies by sex. Some studies identified a stronger association with lower cognitive decline risk in males, whereas others observed sex‐specific associations, 8 , 10 , 12 suggesting that combining data from different sexes may mask genuine biological signals and underscoring the necessity of conducting pre‐designed sex‐stratified analyses.

Current research faces significant limitations, which lie in the reliance on static metrics like body mass index (BMI), 13 inadequate consideration of age and sex differences in analyses, 19 and a fragmented mechanistic understanding lacking a comprehensive evidence chain from fluid‐based biomarkers to neuroimaging. 19 , 20 , 21

Building on the obesity paradox and established sex differences in AD pathophysiology, this study tests the hypothesis that higher adiposity in older adults is associated with reduced AD‐related cognitive decline and pathology through age‐dependent and sexually dimorphic mechanisms. We characterize these mechanisms using a multimodal biomarker framework spanning plasma, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), and neuroimaging in two independent cohorts (Hubei Memory and Aging Cohort Study [HMACS], 2018–2023, mean follow‐up: 2.27 years, n = 2349; Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative [ADNI], 2006–2023, mean follow‐up: 4.23 years, n = 1858). Utilizing data from the HMACS, we estimated body fat percentage using the Clínica Universidad de Navarra‐Body Adiposity Estimator (CUN‐BAE) formula, which is validated against and closely correlated with dual‐energy X‐ray absorptiometry (DEXA) measurements, 22 with a focus on its longitudinal changes. Analyses are rigorously stratified by sex and age to uncover dimorphic patterns and age‐dependent effects. Findings are validated in the ADNI cohort. We systematically integrate multi‐level biomarkers to outline the evidence continuum linking dynamic fat changes to peripheral AD pathology and clinical cognitive status, thereby testing pathological specificity. Through this multidimensional approach, this research aims to provide systematic evidence on the role of dynamic adiposity in cognitive aging and to inform future precision prevention strategies.

METHODS

Study design and population

This investigation utilized data from two prospective studies of AD cohorts. The HMACS (ChiCTR1800019164) enrolled community‐dwelling older adults in Hubei Province, China. Ethical oversight was provided by the academic ethics committee of Wuhan University of Science and Technology (approval number: 201845). All participants provided written informed consent. Between 2018 and 2023, participants underwent standardized clinical assessments conducted by trained clinicians across seven research centers, which included cognitive testing, physical measurements, and biospecimen collection. Follow‐up assessments occurred every 1–2 years using identical protocols. The analysis included 2349 baseline dementia‐free participants with at least one follow‐up assessment from this cohort (Figure S1).

The ADNI served as an independent validation cohort and a multi‑level biomarker association analysis. Data accessed on August 10, 2025, comprised baseline dementia‐free participants who underwent structured cognitive assessments and provided concurrent multimodal neuroimaging data, including amyloid beta–positron emission tomography (Aβ‐PET), tau‐PET, and fluid‐attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR), alongside fluid biomarkers from plasma and CSF. A total of 1858 eligible participants were included for multimodal validation (Figure S1). The ADNI protocol received ethical approval from institutional review boards at all participating institutions, as detailed online. This study adhered to Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guidelines for observational research.

The selection of ADNI as a validation cohort was motivated by its complementarity with HMACS. Although HMACS provides population‐based epidemiological evidence from a Chinese community setting, ADNI offers standardized multimodal biomarker assessments that are not available in HMACS.

Assessment of body fat percentage

Body adiposity was assessed using the CUN‐BAE, a validated equation that estimates body fat percentage based on height, weight, age, and sex. 23 The CUN‐BAE formula was developed and cross‐validated in diverse populations, providing a reliable surrogate for adiposity distribution and correlates with metabolic risk beyond conventional BMI. We selected CUN‐BAE because it (1) requires only basic anthropometric data, making it feasible for large‐scale community‐based studies like HMACS; (2) has been validated against DEXA in diverse populations, including older adults; (3) estimates total body fat percentage rather than weight status alone; and (4) allows for dynamic tracking of adiposity changes over time, which is central to our analytical focus on longitudinal trajectories. It is calculated as follows: CUN−BAE(BF%)=−44.988+(0.503×age)+(10.689×sex)+(3.172×BMI)−(0.026×BMI2)+(0.181×BMI×sex)−(0.02×BMI×age)−(0.005×BMI2×sex)+(0.00021×BMI2×age) where sex is coded as 0 for males and 1 for females, age is in years, and BMI is calculated as weight (kg) divided by height squared (m2). CUN‐BAE is expressed as percentage body fat (percentage points). A 1 percentage‐point increase corresponds to 1 percentage point. We report associations per 1 percentage‐point increase for continuous models and per quartile for categorical descriptive analyses.

To account for sex‐specific adiposity patterns, CUN‐BAE quartiles were derived separately for males and females. This stratification ensured balanced distributions within each sex stratum. In the HMACS cohort, sex‐specific quartile thresholds were defined for males as Q1 (<23.17%), Q2 (23.17 to 25.74%), Q3 (25.75 to 28.61%), Q4 (>28.61%) and for females as Q1 (<35.67%), Q2 (35.67 to 38.33%), Q3 (38.34 to 40.98%), Q4 (>40.98%). CUN‐BAE was analyzed as a continuous variable in all primary models. Sex‐specific quartiles were used for descriptive characterization and cumulative incidence curves, with cut points derived from the baseline distribution. Sensitivity analyses using continuous CUN‐BAE yielded consistent results.

Cognitive assessment and diagnostic adjudication

Comprehensive neuropsychological assessment in HMACS

Cognitive function within the HMACS cohort was evaluated using a standardized neuropsychological battery. The core assessment included the Mini‐Mental State Examination (MMSE) and the Montreal Cognitive Assessment‐Basic (MoCA‐B). This was supplemented by a series of domain‐specific tests: the Auditory Verbal Learning Test (AVLT; assessing memory), Shape Trail Test Parts A and B (STT; assessing executive function and processing speed), the Digit Span Test (DST) in both forward and backward conditions (assessing attention and working memory), the Boston Naming Test (BNT; assessing language), the Animal Fluency Test (AFT; assessing verbal fluency and executive function), and the Clock Drawing Test (CDT; assessing visuospatial ability and executive function). 24 , 25 , 26 , 27 , 28

All cognitive scores were standardized and expressed as z‐scores. Standardization was performed using the baseline cohort distribution (mean = 0, SD = 1), stratified by education level to account for literacy effects. Baseline, rather than full longitudinal distributions were used as the reference to avoid conditioning the metric on a selected follow‐up subset. Education‐stratified z‐scores allow interpretation of effect sizes in SD units relative to the baseline study population, with longitudinal change reflecting deviation from this fixed baseline standard.

Diagnosis of cognitive status in HMACS

Diagnoses of MCI and dementia were established at baseline and all follow‐up visits by a consensus panel comprising neurologists and neuropsychologists specializing in cognitive disorders.

MCI was diagnosed according to Petersen's criteria, which require: (1) cognitive decline reported by the participant, an informant, or the clinician; (2) objective evidence of impairment in one or more cognitive domains; (3) generally preserved independence in functional abilities, with allowance for mild difficulties in complex instrumental activities (as assessed by the Clinical Dementia Rating scale and Activities of Daily Living scales); and (4) the cognitive impairment not meeting criteria for dementia. Education‐adjusted cutoffs on the MoCA‐B were applied to aid in identifying cognitive impairment indicative of MCI: a score of ≤19 for illiterate individuals and those with a primary school education, ≤22 for those with a middle school education, and ≤24 for those with a university education.

Dementia was diagnosed based on the criteria from the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM‐5). The criteria included: (1) evidence of significant cognitive decline from a previous level of performance in one or more cognitive domains; (2) the cognitive deficits interfering with independence in everyday activities; and (3) the deficits not being better explained by delirium or another major mental disorder. The MMSE was used with the following education‐specific cutoffs for dementia screening: ≤17 for illiterate individuals, ≤20 for those with a primary school education, and ≤24 for those with a middle school education or above.

Etiological attribution to AD clinical phenotype in HMACS

For the etiological classification of MCI or dementia, the HMACS protocol mandated an integrated clinical assessment by experienced neurologists to identify cases deemed highly consistent with the clinical phenotype of AD. This classification of probable AD etiology was applied to cases meeting the syndromic criteria for MCI or dementia that also exhibited the following core clinical features: (1) an insidious onset over a period of months to years; (2) a clear history of progressive cognitive decline; and (3) initial and most prominent cognitive deficits that aligned with either (a) an amnestic presentation (characterized by impaired learning and recall of recently learned information, plus deficits in at least one other cognitive domain) or (b) a non‐amnestic presentation (involving prominent language impairment, visuospatial deficits, or impaired reasoning and judgment).

Exclusion criteria for this classification comprised the presence of: significant cerebrovascular disease (e.g., a confirmed stroke, multiple or extensive infarcts, or severe white matter hyperintensities); or core clinical features consistent with dementia with Lewy bodies that were not attributable to AD‐related pathology.

Incident probable AD‐related MCI or dementia in HMACS, analyzed as a combined outcome. Secondary endpoints include: (i) all‐cause MCI and dementia separately; (ii) all‐cause cognitive impairment combining MCI and dementia; (iii) non–AD‐related cognitive outcomes as a negative control; and (iv) ADNI biomarker‐supported outcomes for mechanistic extension.

Cognitive assessment and etiological diagnosis in the ADNI

Participants in the ADNI underwent a structured and standardized neuropsychological evaluation. The assessment battery included the MMSE, MoCA, the Alzheimer's Disease Assessment Scale‐Cognitive Subscale in both its 11‐item (ADAS‐Cog11) and 13‐item (ADAS‐Cog13) versions, and the global Clinical Dementia Rating (CDR). To enable robust cross‐study comparisons, we utilized the empirically validated cognitive composites developed by the Phenotype Harmonization Consortium (PHC). These composites provide co‐calibrated measures across four key cognitive domains: episodic memory (PHC‐MEM), executive function (PHC‐EXF), language (PHC‐LAN), and visuospatial ability (PHC‐VSP). The development of these composites involved advanced psychometric co‐calibration across 14 major longitudinal aging studies, including ADNI, ensuring that scores are on a common, directly comparable metric.

The diagnosis of MCI or dementia consistent with AD etiology within ADNI was based on a biomarker‐supported framework. Cases were classified as MCI or dementia due to AD if they met the respective clinical syndrome criteria (based on comprehensive cognitive testing and CDR assessment) and concurrently exhibited evidence of Alzheimer's pathology, as indicated by a positive Aβ‐PET scan.

It is important to note that HMACS and ADNI used different diagnostic frameworks: HMACS relied on clinical phenotype‐based adjudication without biomarker confirmation, whereas ADNI used biomarker‐supported diagnoses. This difference affects direct comparability of absolute incidence rates between cohorts but does not invalidate cross‐cohort comparison of association directions, which is the primary purpose of our validation approach.

Plasma biomarker quantification in HMACS

In the HMACS cohort, the following six plasma biomarkers were quantified: phosphorylated tau 217 (p‐tau217), p‐tau181, amyloid beta (Aβ)40, Aβ42, neurofilament light chain (NfL), and glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP).

Regarding sample collection and storage, venous blood samples were collected and immediately centrifuged at 2000–3000 rpm for 10 min. Plasma and serum aliquots were subsequently separated and stored at −80°C until assessment. Samples were transported on dry ice to the same third‐party testing company, following standardized protocols for biomarker quantification. The assay platform was the Simoa HD‐X analyzer system. The specific ultrasensitive single‐molecule array (Simoa) assays employed were: GFAP, Aβ40, Aβ42, and NfL using the Neurology 4‐Plex E kit (Cat# 103670; Lot# 503940); p‐tau181 using the Simoa P‐tau181 Advantage V2 kit (Cat# 103714; Lot# 503732); and p‐tau217 using the ALZpath Simoa P‐tau 217 v2 Assay kit (Cat# 104371; Lot# 999048).

Assay‐specific limits of detection were obtained from manufacturer documentation (Neurology 4‐Plex E: http://www.igeneseq.com/uploads/allimg/20251030/1‐25103011244E43.pdf; p‐tau181: https://www.chimera‐biotec.com/fileadmin/public/images/Datasheets/pTau‐181_item_103714_Chimera.pdf; p‐tau217: https://max.book118.com/html/2025/1124/8022112056010013.shtm).

To mitigate batch effects, we applied the ComBat function (sva package) to AD biomarker data. Missing values were imputed using half the minimum positive value observed in non‐missing samples; samples with all biomarkers missing were assigned a very small value (1×10− 6). All biomarker levels underwent log2 transformation to reduce skewness, followed by z‐score normalization for cross‐biomarker comparability.

Technical details of neuroimaging in ADNI

Aβ and tau PET imaging

Aβ and tau PET imaging in the ADNI cohort followed standardized protocols to ensure multisite consistency, 29 , 30 as documented in the ADNI technical procedures (https://adni.loni.usc.edu/) and previously published processing pipelines. For Aβ PET, participants received intravenous injections of either [1 1C]Pittsburgh compound B (PiB; 740 MBq ± 10%), [1 8F]florbetapir (FBP; 370 MBq ± 10%), or [1 8F]florbetaben (FBB; 300 MBq ± 20%), with scans acquired during validated post‐injection time windows (40–60 min for PIB, 50–70 min for florbetapir, 90–110 min for florbetaben) to optimize tracer uptake and signal‐to‐noise ratio. The tau PET utilized [1 8F]flortaucipir (AV‐1451), administered at 370 MBq ± 10% with scanning performed 75–105 min post‐injection to target specific tau aggregates in neurodegenerative regions.

Image preprocessing for both Aβ and tau PET followed a standardized pipeline using Statistical Parametric Mapping (SPM) 12. First, PET images were motion‐corrected and coregistered to corresponding T1‐weighted magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) to align functional and structural data. Structural MRI underwent gradient distortion correction (Gradwarp) and B1 nonuniformity adjustment before segmentation into gray matter, white matter, and CSF. Nonlinear spatial normalization to the Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) template was achieved via DARTEL in SPM12, with transformation parameters applied to PET images to ensure anatomic consistency across participants. A Gaussian smoothing kernel (6 mm full‐width at half‐maximum [FWHM]) was applied to PET data to reduce noise while preserving regional biomarker distributions. Regional standard uptake value ratios (SUVRs) were extracted using the GAAIN and NPDKA anatomic atlases, with the whole cerebellum as the reference region for Aβ‐PET and the inferior cerebellar cortex for tau‐PET. No partial volume correction was applied, consistent with standard ADNI protocols.

Quality control measures were integral to maintaining data integrity. Scanner calibration was performed using phantom‐based protocols to standardize intensity values across sites, and manufacturer‐specific normalization (GE/Philips/Siemens) was applied to account for hardware differences. PET images were visually inspected for motion artifacts, poor co‐registration, or abnormal tracer uptake, with non‐compliant scans excluded from analysis. Quantitative biomarker values were calculated using a cerebellar cortex reference region, a validated method for Aβ and tau quantification in ADNI, enabling robust comparison of pathology across cortical and subcortical regions.

The state and pathological changes of Aβ are determined via PET imaging. Aβ PET data were obtained following ADNI's standardized multisite acquisition protocols. All acquired PET scans were subjected to a rigorous, centralized quality control procedure implemented by the Mayo Clinic, which screened for protocol compliance and imaging artifacts. Scans receiving a quality rating greater than 3 on a 4‐point scale were excluded from analysis. Quantitative analysis was performed using tracer‐specific reference regions, with the whole cerebellum used as the reference for Aβ‐PET tracers. A global Aβ‐PET summary metric was derived by calculating the mean SUVR across a composite of regions of interest, including the prefrontal, orbitofrontal, parietal, temporal, anterior and posterior cingulate, and precuneus cortices. Amyloid positivity was defined using previously validated tracer‐specific thresholds: a composite SUVR ≥1.11 for FBP or ≥ 1.08 for FBB. Centiloid scaling was not applied; instead, tracer‐specific SUVRs were modeled separately in regional analyses.

FLAIR imaging

FLAIR MRI in ADNI was acquired using a three‐dimensional (3D) sequence optimized for white matter hyperintensity (WMH) detection, with consistent parameters across sites: repetition time (TR) = 8000 ms, echo time (TE) = 120 ms, inversion time (TI) = 2200 ms, slice thickness = 1 mm, matrix size = 256 × 256, and field of view (FOV) = 240 mm. This protocol maximizes contrast between WMH (hyperintense on FLAIR) and normal white matter, supporting reliable segmentation of small or diffuse lesions.

Image preprocessing for FLAIR focused on isolating and quantifying WMH volume (WMHVOL). First, images were standardized to a uniform resolution of 256 × 256 × 256 voxels with an isotropic voxel size of 1 × 1 × 1 mm3, followed by non‐uniformity bias field correction and intensity normalization to a grayscale range of 0–255 using the N3 method (nu_correct.mni), which is optimized for FLAIR images. Skull stripping and brain extraction were performed to remove non‐brain tissue and enhance lesion visibility, supplemented by Robert's edge detection to minimize noise at the brain contour. WMH segmentation was implemented using a Gaussian Mixture Model (GMM) with the Expectation–Maximization (EM) algorithm, a validated method for ADNI that combines intensity thresholding with morphological operations to exclude false positives. Segmented WMH masks were then normalized to MNI space using DARTEL transformation parameters from the corresponding T1‐weighted MRI, ensuring alignment with PET and structural data for multimodal analysis.

Quality control for FLAIR included rigorous checks for motion artifacts, image blur, or incorrect skull stripping, with scans failing these criteria excluded. WMHV was quantified as total volume (in mm3, converted to cm3 by dividing by 1000) using FreeSurfer's mri_segstats tool and normalized to intracranial volume (ICV) to account for individual differences in head size. Inter‐rater reliability was assessed for a subset of scans to validate segmentation consistency, and automated quality control (QC) tools were used to flag outliers in WMHV or segmentation quality, ensuring reproducibility of results across the cohort.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were conducted under a pre‐specified hierarchical framework. The primary exposure was continuous CUN‐BAE, expressed as percentage body fat. CUN‐BAE was analyzed as a continuous variable in all primary models. The primary outcome was incident probable AD‐related MCI or dementia in HMACS. The primary stratification variables were sex (male vs female) and age group (≥65 vs <65 years). Formal interaction tests for CUN‐BAE × sex, CUN‐BAE × age, and CUN‐BAE × sex × age were conducted for all primary clinical and biomarker outcomes. All results were non‐significant (Table S1). Given the non‐overlapping sex‐specific distributions of CUN‐BAE and the established age dependency of the obesity paradox, we retained our pre‐specified sex‐ and age‐stratified analytical framework regardless of interaction test significance, as stratification ensures biological interpretability. ADNI served as a validation cohort for biomarker and neuroimaging associations. Primary analyses tested the sex‐ and age‐specific associations of CUN‐BAE with incident probable AD‐related MCI/dementia in HMACS, with validation in ADNI; secondary analyses examined biomarker and neuroimaging correlates; all remaining analyses were exploratory.

Baseline characteristics were summarized by sex and CUN‐BAE body fat quartiles. Continuous variables were described using mean (SD) for normally distributed data or median (IQR) for non‐normally distributed data, based on Shapiro–Wilk normality testing. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies (percentages). Between‐group differences across quartiles were assessed using one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) for normally distributed variables, Kruskal–Wallis test for non‐normally distributed variables, and χ2 or Fisher's exact test for categorical variables. For post hoc pairwise comparisons, one‐way ANOVA followed by Tukey's honestly significant difference (HSD) test was used when homogeneity of variances was met; otherwise, the Kruskal–Wallis test followed by Dunn's test with Bonferroni correction was applied.

For cross‐sectional analyses, generalized linear models (GLMs) were employed to evaluate the linear relationship between CUN‐BAE and cognitive test scores at baseline, stratified by sex and adjusted for age, education, hypertension, and diabetes. Hyperlipidemia was not included as a covariate due to collinearity with CUN‐BAE (Variance Inflation Factor [VIF] >5), given that lipid metabolism is a core function of adipose tissue. The potential interaction between CUN‐BAE and age on the risk of MCI was also visualized using contour plots derived from generalized additive models (GAMs).

For longitudinal analyses, age‐scale Cox proportional hazards models were applied to assess the association between baseline CUN‐BAE and the incidence of all‐cause cognitive outcomes (encompassing both MCI and dementia), with age serving as the underlying time scale. The left truncation point was defined as the baseline age, and the event age was incorporated into the model. The proportional hazards assumption was satisfied by the Schoenfeld residual test and Log–Log survival curves. To investigate the potential linear or nonlinear dose–response relationship between baseline CUN‐BAE and the incidence of cause‐specific cognitive outcomes, restricted cubic splines (RCSs) were incorporated into age‐scaled Cox models. The number of knots for the RCS regression was set to four, selected to minimize the Akaike Information Criterion and thereby ensure optimal model fit. Cumulative incidence curves for probable AD‐related MCI and dementia across baseline CUN‐BAE quartiles were also generated using age‐scale Cox models. We utilized Cox proportional hazards models and Fine–Gray subdistribution hazards models to assess the association between baseline CUN‐BAE quartiles and the risk of cognitive outcomes. Cause‐specific hazard ratios (csHRs) were calculated using age as the time scale with left truncation at baseline age to account for the varying entry times of participants into the risk set. To account for competing risks from other cause cognitive outcomes we used Fine–Gray competing risk models, which estimate subdistribution HRs (sHRs). All models were stratified by sex and adjusted for education level, hypertension, diabetes, and baseline age.

We investigated the association between changes in CUN‐BAE (ΔCUN‐BAE, defined as follow‐up CUN‐BAE minus baseline CUN‐BAE, expressed as an absolute change in percentage points) and cognitive state transitions using multinomial logistic regression models, stratified by baseline cognitive status. Relative risk ratios (RRRs) with 95% Wald confidence intervals (CIs) were estimated to quantify the association between each unit increase in baseline CUN‐BAE or ΔCUN‐BAE and the RR of cognitive transitions compared to the reference category. We used a continuous‐time Markov chain (CTMC) framework to model dynamic cognitive state transitions over time. Baseline cognitive states included normal cognition (NC) and MCI, and dementia was defined as an absorbing state with no further transitions after diagnosis. Transitions were specified as NC to NC, NC to MCI, MCI to NC, MCI to MCI, and MCI to dementia. The model was parameterized by a transition intensity matrix Q. Cumulative transition probabilities over 1–5 years were calculated using the matrix exponential of Q, which converts instantaneous transition intensities to time‐specific cumulative probabilities. Irregular visit intervals were naturally accommodated by the continuous‐time formulation. In CTMC models, ΔCUN‐BAE was a fixed exposure representing total adiposity change over the observation period, and transitions were assumed to occur at the midpoint of the visit. The baseline CUN‐BAE score, age, gender, education level, hypertension, and diabetes were included as covariates in the model. Model fitting was performed using the Broyden‐Fletcher‐Goldfarb‐Shanno (BFGS) optimization algorithm to estimate the maximum likelihood function. Stratified analyses by age subgroups (65–69 years, 70–74 years, ≥75 years) were performed to explore age‐specific effects of ΔCUN‐BAE on cognitive trajectories. We further employed Bayesian generalized linear mixed‐effects models (BGLMMs) to analyze the association between CUN‐BAE dynamics and cognitive trajectories, with a focus on between‐individual and within‐individual effects and their interactions with time. In BGLMMs, CUN‐BAE was a time‐varying predictor with participant‐specific random slopes, enabling separation of between‐ and within‐individual effects. The within‐individual effect captures whether increasing CUN‐BAE over follow‐up is associated with cognitive trajectory, independent of baseline adiposity level. BGLMMs were fitted using Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) sampling via the No‐U‐Turn Sampler (NUTS). BGLMMs used weakly informative normal priors (mean = 0, Standard Deviation [SD] = 10) for fixed effects and half‐Cauchy priors (scale = 2.5) for random effect SDs. Four MCMC chains were run with 2000 warmup iterations and 4000 sampling iterations. Convergence was assessed via R‐hat <1.01 and effective sample size >400 for all parameters. We compared BGLMM results with linear mixed‐effects models (LMERs) to confirm consistency in point estimates, but retained Bayesian results for their superior characterization of uncertainty.

To elucidate the mechanistic pathways linking CUN‐BAE to cognition function, we investigated relationships between CUN‐BAE and AD‐related biomarkers. GLMs assessed baseline linear associations between CUN‐BAE and biomarkers, with 10‐fold cross‐validation confirming the reliability of these links. GAMs explored nonlinear trends in relationships between CUN‐BAE and biomarkers, whereas segmented linear regression identified threshold effects. The effective degrees of freedom (EDF) from GAM indicate the complexity of nonlinear relationships, where an EDF close to 1 suggests a linear relationship, an EDF between 1 and 2 indicates mild nonlinearity, and higher EDF values reflect more complex nonlinear patterns. Principal component analysis (PCA) decomposed the multivariate structure of associations to geometrically validate sex‐specific patterns. BGLMMs tested time‐by‐CUN‐BAE interactions to examine whether CUN‐BAE changes impacted biomarker trajectories. We evaluated associations between CUN‐BAE and neuroimaging features across cognitive states, sexes, and longitudinal changes. Cross‐sectional relationships between CUN‐BAE and regional Aβ/tau deposition were examined using GLMs, stratified by cognitive status and sex. Longitudinal associations between intra‐individual CUN‐BAE changes and corresponding Aβ/tau deposition changes were assessed with GLMMs. CUN‐BAE levels across Braak stages were compared via Kruskal–Wallis test. The interaction of CUN‐BAE changes and time on longitudinal WMH volume changes was investigated using BGLMMs.

To empirically validate the selection of CUN‐BAE as the primary adiposity metric, we performed sensitivity analyses comparing CUN‐BAE with BMI, weight, waist circumference, and waist‐to‐hip ratio (WHR) for associations with incident cognitive outcomes in the HMACS cohort. These analyses used identical model specifications as the primary CUN‐BAE models. These sensitivity analyses additionally adjusted for cerebrovascular disease, cardiovascular disease, social network, nicotine exposure, diet, physical activity, cognitive activity, sleep health, and mental health, further addressing potential residual confounding.

To correct for multiple comparisons across the large number of hypothesis tests, we applied the Benjamini–Hochberg (BH) procedure to control the false discovery rate (FDR) at a threshold of q < 0.05. This correction was applied independently and separately to the set of p‐values within each predefined analysis family: (1) cognitive outcomes (cross‐sectional and longitudinal); (2) plasma biomarkers; (3) CSF biomarkers; and (4) neuroimaging regions. Unless specified as exploratory, all reported p‐values are FDR‐corrected within the relevant analysis family. This approach balances control of false positives with preservation of power for distinct hypothesis domains. All tests were two‐tailed with significance set at p < 0.05. Statistical analyses were conducted using R (version 4.5.2) and Python (version 3.8.20). All analyses report associations, not causal effects. The term “association” is used throughout to describe statistical relationships, and no causal claims are made.

Among the 1858 dementia‐free participants from the ADNI cohort (1014 males and 844 females), sex‐specific associations between adiposity and cognitive function diverged from those observed in the HMACS cohort (Table S3). In males, higher CUN‐BAE was significantly associated with reduced cognitive impairment (ADAS‐Cog11, 0.12 [−0.46 to 0.94] vs 0.05 [−0.59 to 0.75] vs −0.07 [−0.63 to 0.56] vs −0.14 [−0.54 to 0.43]). In females, significant differences in cognitive performance across CUN‐BAE quartiles were observed across multiple domains, including global cognition measured by the MMSE and MoCA, dementia‐specific severity assessed by ADAS‐Cog11, ADAS‐Cog13, and global CDR, as well as memory and language performance (all p < 0.05).

Associations of baseline CUN‐BAE with cognitive decline

In HMACS, baseline CUN‐BAE was positively associated with better cognitive performance, with significant correlations observed in males across multiple tests (MMSE, β = 0.02; MoCA, β = 0.02; BNT, β = 0.04; DST, β = 0.03), and similar associations were observed among participants with baseline NC (Figure 1A, Figure S2, Table S4). Consistent trends were observed in the ADNI cohort, where higher baseline CUN‐BAE was associated with better cognitive performance in females ≥65 years of age (MMSE, β = 0.013; PHC‐MEM, β = 0.016). A tendency for worse cognitive performance with higher CUN‐BAE was noted in younger participants, including males on MoCA (β = −0.053) and females on PHC‐EXF (β = −0.014) (Table S5, Figure S3). Models evaluating the joint effect of CUN‐BAE and age on MCI risk in the HMACS indicated significant interactions in both males and females (Figure S4, S5). Although higher CUN‐BAE was associated with lower risk of age‐related MCI in both sexes, the magnitude of this trend associated with lower risk was more pronounced in females (Figure 1B). Similar but more specific trends are demonstrated in the ADNI cohort (Figure S6–S8).

A similar result was observed for probable AD‐related dementia, with cause‐specific risks decreasing by 39% (Q2, 0.61 [0.38 to 0.99]), 44% (Q3, 0.56 [0.34 to 0.93]), and 45% (Q4, 0.55 [0.34 to 0.89]) across ascending CUN‐BAE quartiles. The third CUN‐BAE quartile group of males was significantly associated with a 69% lower risk of probable AD‐related dementia (0.31 [0.13 to 0.77]). In contrast, no associations were observed between CUN‐BAE and other cause cognitive decline in either sex and non‐significant trends. Comparative associations of alternative adiposity metrics with cognitive outcomes

To empirically validate the selection of CUN‐BAE as the primary exposure, we compared CUN‐BAE with BMI, body weight, waist circumference, and WHR for their associations with cognitive outcomes in the HMACS cohort (Tables S9, S10, Figure S9). In females, CUN‐BAE showed the strongest association (HR, 0.97 [95% CI, 0.94 to 1.00], p = 0.072), with BMI (0.97 [0.94 to 1.01], p = 0.191) and weight (0.99 [0.97 to 1.00], p = 0.076) showing weaker or null trends. WHR and waist circumference were essentially null. In males, CUN‐BAE (0.95 [0.92 to 0.98]) and weight (0.98 [0.96 to 0.99]) performed similarly, but BMI (0.95 [0.91 to 0.99]) and waist circumference (0.98 [0.97 to 0.99]) also showed significant associations. For all‐cause dementia, CUN‐BAE remained significant in both females (0.96 [0.93 to 0.99]) and males (0.93 [0.88 to 0.99]). BMI was null in females (0.97 [0.93 to 1.01]) and marginally significant in males (0.93 [0.86 to 1.01], p = 0.068). Weight was significant in both sexes but with smaller effect sizes.

For AD‐consistent MCI, CUN‐BAE exhibited the strongest and most consistent associations across both sexes (females: csHR, 0.94 [95% CI, 0.90 to 0.98]; males: 0.91 [0.86 to 0.96]), outperforming BMI, body weight, waist circumference, and WHR. Notably, WHR produced highly unstable estimates with extremely wide CIs (females: 0.77 [0.06 to 10.15]; males: 0.20 [0.01 to 3.75]), indicating poor reliability in this population. For non‐AD cognitive outcomes, none of the adiposity metrics showed significant associations (all p’s > 0.10), further supporting the AD‐pathology specificity of the adiposity–cognition link.

GAMs revealed significant joint nonlinear interactions between CUN‐BAE and age on MCI risk (females: joint effect p = 0.004; males: p < 0.001), as well as for BMI (females: p = 0.002; males: p < 0.001), with the lowest MCI risk observed at higher ages and moderate adiposity levels. In contrast, WHR showed no significant interaction with age in females (p = 0.287) and a weaker effect in males (p = 0.03). Waist circumference demonstrated a significant age interaction in females (p = 0.002) but exhibited a non‐monotonic, complex pattern that was difficult to interpret clinically.

After additionally adjusting for cerebrovascular disease, cardiovascular disease, social network status, nicotine exposure, dietary habits, physical activity, cognitive engagement, sleep quality, and mental health, the associations between continuous CUN‐BAE and all‐cause as well as cause‐specific cognitive outcomes remained robust (Tables S9, S10). However, the association between continuous CUN‐BAE and all‐cause MCI risk in females was only marginally significant (HR, 0.97 [95% CI, 0.94 to 1.00], p = 0.072).

Associations of CUN‐BAE changes with cognitive state transitions

Consistent with our prior results, higher baseline CUN‐BAE in the HMACS and increases in CUN‐BAE over follow‐up were associated with a reduced risk of cognitive decline, with distinct patterns by baseline cognitive status (Table 3, Figure 2A). Among participants who were cognitively normal at baseline, an increase in CUN‐BAE over time (∆CUN‐BAE) was associated with a 9% lower risk of cognitive decline per unit increase (0.91 [0.85 to 0.97]). Both baseline level and positive change in CUN‐BAE showed consistent lower risk of cognitive decline in sex‐stratified analyses for females. In males, a higher baseline CUN‐BAE was also associated with a lower risk (0.92 [0.87 to 0.98]), whereas the effect of increasing CUN‐BAE was directionally similar but not statistically significant. Sex‐specific analyses revealed that higher baseline CUN‐BAE was particularly associated with a lower risk of progression to dementia in males (0.86 [0.77 to 0.96]).

BGLMMs revealed consistent associations of individual average CUN‐BAE and within‐individual changes with cognitive performance across sexes. Higher baseline individual average CUN‐BAE was positively linked to better language ability (BNT, male, PM, 0.021 [95% CI 0.005 to 0.037]; female, 0.017 [0.004 to 0.030]) and working memory (DST, male, 0.022 [0.006 to 0.039]; female, 0.013 [0.000 to 0.027]). Results from LMERs were consistent with those from BGLMMs (Table S14, Figure S10). In the ADNI cohort, higher per‐individual average CUN‐BAE was consistently associated with improved cognitive performance (Tables S15, S16). The findings remained robust even when excluding participants who developed incident dementia (Tables S17, S18, Figure S11, S12).

Regulatory effects of CUN‐BAE on multimodal AD biomarkers

Among female participants in the HMACS, CUN‐BAE showed a significant linear negative association with plasma p‐tau217 (β = −0.023) (Figure 3A, Figures S13, S14, Table S19). Segmented linear regression revealed a nonlinear association between CUN‐BAE and plasma Aβ42. Specifically, when CUN‐BAE exceeded 37.62 in females, higher CUN‐BAE levels were significantly associated with elevated plasma Aβ42 (Figure 3B, Figure S15). Ten‐fold cross‐validation demonstrated reliable yet weak associations between CUN‐BAE levels and plasma Aβ and tau burden (Figure 3C). PCA showed that increased CUN‐BAE was accompanied by a negative trend of p‐tau in males, geometrically validating a sex‐specific link between higher adiposity and attenuated tau‐related pathological burden (Figure 3D, Table S20). Results from BGLMMs further indicated that intra‐individual increases in CUN‐BAE in females significantly associated with a lower accumulation rate of Aβ40 (−0.134 [−0.194 to −0.076]), Aβ42 (−0.131 [−0.190 to −0.070]), p‐tau181 (−0.057 [−0.113 to −0.002]), and p‐tau217 (−0.065 [−0.109 to −0.021]) over time (Figure 3E, Figure S16, Table S21).

In the ADNI cohort, these trends were not only validated but also extended. CUN‐BAE showed significant negative associations with plasma‐based p‐tau, p‐tau217/Aβ42, GFAP, and NfL in both sexes, alongside a positive association with CSF‐derived Aβ42 and a negative association with CSF p‐tau (Figure 3F, Figures S17–S20, Tables S22, S23). Significant negative associations between CUN‐BAE and plasma GFAP and NfL were also observed in female participants <65 years of age (Figures S21, S22, Table S24). Similarly, intra‐individual increases in CUN‐BAE in males were significantly associated with reduced accumulation rates of plasma p‐tau217, p‐tau217/Aβ42, and NfL (Figure S23, Table S25). Compared to participants with negative CSF Aβ and tau status, those with positive status had significantly lower CUN‐BAE levels (Figure 3G). Furthermore, each 1 percentage‐point increase in CUN‐BAE was associated with a significant 9% reduction (odds ratio [OR], 0.91 [95% CI, 0.87 to 0.96]) in the odds of being in the A+T+ class in males and a 7% reduction (0.93 [0.89 to 0.97]) in females (Figure 3H, Table S26).

Neuroimaging analyses in the ADNI cohort revealed that among NC and MCI participants, higher baseline CUN‐BAE levels were broadly associated with lower Aβ deposition across cortical and subcortical brain regions, particularly within the default mode network and executive control network, and this association was not influenced by sex. This inverse association was consistently observed across multiple Aβ‐PET tracers and demonstrated stronger effect sizes in females (Figure 4A,B, Table S27). In contrast, higher CUN‐BAE levels correlated with reduced cortical tau deposition, most notably in the medial temporal lobe network, only in males with MCI, especially those who were Aβ positive (Figure 4C,D, Table S28). Longitudinal analyses of regional Aβ and tau deposition indicated that intra‐individual increases in CUN‐BAE were associated with attenuated Aβ deposition in cortical/subcortical regions of females and reduced tau deposition in males (Figure 4E, Tables S29, S30, Figures S24–S27). Men at earlier Braak stages exhibited significantly higher baseline CUN‐BAE levels (p = 0.002; Figure 4F). However, no statistically significant differences in CUN‐BAE were observed between participants with versus without Braak stage progression over 3 years (p > 0.05; Figure 4G). Similarly, intra‐individual changes in CUN‐BAE showed no significant association with the rate of white matter hyperintensity volume progression (Figure 4H, Table S31).

DISCUSSION

This study investigated the associations of body fat percentage and its changes with AD‐related cognitive decline and pathology in non‐dementia older adults using the HMACS cohort, with validation in ADNI. Our core innovation is a multidimensional framework integrating age dynamics, sexual dimorphism, and pathological specificity. Stratified analyses suggested differential association between adiposity and core AD pathologies by sex, although formal interaction tests were non‐significant. Supported by longitudinal evidence, higher and moderately increasing body fat in older adults was associated with lower AD pathology and cognitive decline risk through sexually dimorphic mechanisms. 19 Stratified exploratory analyses suggested tau‐related patterns in males, manifesting as a nonlinear relationship with lower risk and tau burden, and amyloid‐related patterns in females, associated with lower linear risk and Aβ burden. The associations with lower risk of cognitive decline are also age‐dependent, evident in advanced age but not in younger older subgroups. Together, these results provide comprehensive evidence for a potential neuroprotective role of body fat in aging and form a foundation for precision prevention strategies based on sex, age, and dynamic monitoring. It is worth reminding that excessive adiposity is associated with metabolic and cardiovascular risks that must be considered in clinical decision‐making.

Obesity and related metabolic disorders exhibit pronounced sex differences, 31 possibly stemming from the age‐, dose‐, and experience‐dependent neuroregulatory effects of sex hormones on hippocampus. 32 Females have higher lifetime AD risk and greater burden with apolipoprotein E (APOE) ε4. 32 Meta‐analyses indicate faster tau accumulation in females, 17 and female AD patients show greater overall pathology. 18 However, existing studies often pool sexes 19 or focus on single biomarkers, missing divergent pathways. Our findings demonstrate clear sexual dimorphism in the adiposity–cognitive association, providing population evidence that the relationship is modulated by multiple factors. 8 , 9 Specifically, higher baseline body fat was associated with a lower risk of cognitive decline in both sexes but with distinct patterns: in males, it correlated with reduced risk of all‐cause and AD‐related MCI and dementia, with AD dementia risk showing nonlinear decline; in females, a monotonic linear decrease in AD‐related MCI risk. This extends preclinical metabolic sex differences to clinical endpoints via large longitudinal designs 12 and integrates reports of stronger male associations 11 or female‐specific links. 33 These clinical‐level patterns suggest exploratory hypotheses regarding distinct pathways, pending confirmation in studies with significant effect modification.

The adiposity–cognitive association is not static. Midlife obesity is a well‐established dementia risk factor, 6 , 34 but late‐life studies often report inconsistent or inverse findings. Late‐life weight loss correlates with higher mortality and dementia risk, 14 , 35 and longitudinal studies indicate slower cognitive decline in overweight/obese older adults. 16 Pooling broad age ranges may obscure true patterns. 19 Our study identifies a critical age inflection point. 7 In individuals ≥65 years of age, higher body fat is associated with a lower risk of cognitive decline and significantly attenuated age‐related MCI risk escalation, especially in females. Conversely, in younger elderly subgroups, higher body fat trended toward worse cognition. This reversal aligns with life‐course epidemiology 35 and precedes AD cognitive decline. 15 Crucially, longitudinal analysis confirmed that in cognitively normal older adults aged 70–74 years, increasing body fat during follow‐up was directly associated with lower MCI conversion risk. This suggests maintaining or moderately increasing adiposity may represent a positive physiological adaptation at specific elderly stages, challenging universal weight loss recommendations. 36 , 37 Sensitivity analyses confirmed CUN‐BAE as the most consistent and strongest correlate of AD‐related cognitive decline across sexes, versus null/unstable estimates for central adiposity metrics (waist circumference, WHR), empirically validating CUN‐BAE as the ideal indicator for the obesity paradox in cognitive aging.

Multimodal biomarker analysis elucidated underlying pathobiology. The association between body fat and AD pathology follows a male‐tau/female‐Aβ differentiation pattern. In females, higher baseline CUN‐BAE was associated with lower plasma p‐tau217 and a segmented relationship with plasma Aβ42; when CUN‐BAE exceeded 37.62, Aβ42 increased with further adiposity, suggesting an optimal metabolic range. Longitudinally, intra‐individual CUN‐BAE increases were associated with a lower accumulation rate of Aβ40, Aβ42, p‐tau181, and p‐tau217, but not with Aβ42/Aβ40 ratio changes, possibly due to complex Aβ isoform dynamics. 38 , 39 Although reduced p‐tau181/p‐tau217 accumulation rates clearly indicate attenuated tau progression, slower Aβ40/Aβ42 accumulation requires caution: in this longitudinal context, it reflects trajectory stabilization, not cross‐sectional plasma level decreases. Lower plasma Aβ42 classically links to greater amyloid deposition cross‐sectionally, but our dampened trajectory likely reflects altered Aβ homeostasis/clearance, not the favorable attenuation implied by p‐tau reductions. In males, follow‐up CUN‐BAE increases were linked to slowed rises in p‐tau217 and NfL, corroborated in ADNI.

Deeper support from CSF and tau‐PET in ADNI confirmed sex‐ and pathology‐type dependence. In males, increased adiposity was linked primarily to attenuated cerebral tau deposition. In females, it was more strongly associated with reduced cerebral Aβ burden and slower Aβ accumulation. Higher CUN‐BAE linked to lower global Aβ deposition (default mode and executive control network) showed no sex difference in presence but a large female effect size. The pathway associated with lower cognitive decline risk in females focuses more on Aβ cascade, suggesting that adiposity may inhibit Aβ aggregation or enhance clearance. Conversely, the CUN‐BAE association with lower cortical tau deposition (medial temporal lobe) was exclusively observed in Aβ‐positive males with MCI. This suggests that the association of lower risk of cognitive decline in male obesity may delay downstream tau spread given pre‐existing Aβ pathology, explaining why lower‐risk associations in males concentrate on tau markers. This mechanistic divergence corroborates reports that lower testosterone correlates with higher Aβ, 32 and sex modulates baseline CSF and Aβ‐PET biomarkers in females. 40 Collectively, body fat may differentially regulate AD core protein homeostasis via sex‐specific hormonal/metabolic pathways offering mechanistic clues to the obesity paradox and supporting sex‐differentiated prevention strategies.

Although prior studies link CUN‐BAE to metabolic diseases, 41 , 42 , 43 , 44 , 45 this work first systematically reveals its unique role and association with lower risk in resisting AD‐related cognitive decline. Multi‐level validation reinforced robustness, including consistent trends in ADNI, sensitivity analyses after excluding dementia progressors, and concordance across models. CUN‐BAE may serve as a novel potential marker for AD‐related cognitive risk. Moreover, focusing on dynamic fat changes overcomes the limitations of static metrics. 42 Its significant association with cognition offers a low‐cost screening tool for primary care. Higher adiposity's metabolic and vascular risks are well established and must be weighed against potential neurocognitive associations. Our findings do not contradict these risks, but highlight a complex, trade‐off relationship in late life. Weight management decisions should consider overall health, comorbidities, and individual risk profiles, not cognition alone.

Strengths include a validated body fat estimator (CUN‐BAE), 22 , 23 prospective design with rigorous sex/age stratification, and validation in an independent cohort. 46 Limitations: First, observational design precludes causal inference, although we strengthened temporality via prospective design, fat changes preceding outcomes, BGLMM/Markov models and sensitivity analyses. The absence of a consistent association with a lower risk in non‐AD cognitive impairment supports AD‐specific processes over reverse causality or frailty. Second, residual confounding from unmeasured factors (diet, physical activity, early‐life weight) cannot be fully ruled out, but the sex‐ and pathology‐specific patterns suggest biological specificity. Third, estimated body fat precludes fat distribution distinction, yet even this accessible metric captures strong AD‐correlated signals, highlighting practical utility. Fourth, mechanistic molecular pathways remain unexplored; we propose the male‐tau/female‐Aβ hypothesis for future research. Fifth, cohort differences in design, ethnicity, and socioeconomic context preclude direct effect‐size comparisons, although consistent directional associations support robustness and highlight the need for broader validation. We distinguish replicated epidemiological findings from exploratory mechanistic analyses only testable in ADNI. Sixth, the short HMACS follow‐up (mean 2.27 years) limits exclusion of early incident outcomes; longer follow‐up will enable stricter temporal analyses. Seventh, survivorship bias may select metabolically resilient high‐adiposity individuals, while those with premature mortality are underrepresented, so findings may reflect long‐term survivors rather than the general population. Eighth, lacking mortality data precludes assessing CUN‐BAE's association with death or conducting competing risk analyses, preventing evaluation of net clinical benefit against known metabolic and cardiovascular risks. Finally, these results should not be interpreted as endorsing increased body fat for dementia prevention. Our value lies in cautioning against universal weight loss, proposing body fat as a low‐cost screening tool for AD risk subgroups, and emphasizing personalized management considering sex, age, and pathology. Any intervention requires clinical guidance and benefit–risk assessment. In summary, this observational study cannot establish causality; alternative explanations (reverse causation, survival bias, unmeasured confounding, competing risks) remain. These associations are descriptive patterns requiring mechanistic and interventional validation, not causal evidence.

In conclusion, this study suggests that in older adults, higher and moderately increasing body fat percentage was associated with lower risks of AD‐related cognitive decline and less pronounced pathology, with sexual dimorphism, age dependency, and pathological specificity. However, current evidence does not support altering body fat specifically for dementia prevention. These findings argue against universal weight loss for the elderly and highlight personalized, dynamic body composition management. Future research should explore sex‐ and age‐specific adipose–brain mechanisms to inform targeted interventions.
