Weierstrass representations of discrete constant mean curvature surfaces in isotropic space
Joseph Cho, Masaya Hara

Abstract
. In this paper, we obtain Weierstrass representations for discrete constant mean curvature surfaces in isotropic 3 3 -space, and use this to construct examples with discrete closed-form parametrizations.



2020 Mathematics Subject Classification
Primary: 53A70, Secondary: 53A35, 53B30

1.
Introduction

Weierstrass representations have been fundemental in obtaining a deeper understanding of various classes of surfaces that admit them, as they provide a powerful method to analyze and construct surfaces of high differential geometric interest.
This is exemplified by the rich study of surface classes that admit the representations, including

•

minimal surfaces in Euclidean space
[
31
]
,

•

maximal surfaces and timelike minimal surfaces in Minkowski space
[
17
,
18
]
, and

•

constant mean curvature
1
(cmc-
1
) surfaces in hyperbolic space
[
5
]
.

The representations themselves have also been revisited from various differential geometric viewpoints, such as spin geometry
[
19
]
, singularity theory
[
30
]
, or transformation theory
[
29
,
13
,
23
]
.
Non-zero constant mean curvature (cmc) surfaces in Euclidean space or Minkowski space also admit Weierstrass-type representations
[
11
,
4
]
; however, these methods are different from the aforementioned representations as they utilize the theory of loop groups.

Zero mean curvature surfaces in isotropic space also admit Weierstrass representations, as first found by Strubecker
[
28
]
, and these representations have been recently rediscovered from various geometric viewpoints, for example,
[
10
,
27
,
26
,
8
]
.
However, the case of isotropic space stands out from other space forms as the class of cmc surfaces in isotropic space also admit Weierstrass representations
[
8
]
that is similar to the zero mean curvature case: any cmc-
H
surface
Y
in isotropic space can locally be represented as

Y=\Re\int(\bar{h}+g,1,-\mathbbm{i})\omega

(1.1)

where
h
is a holomorphic function,
g
a meromorphic function, and
\omega
a holomorphic
1
-form such that
g^{2}\omega
is holomorphic while
\dif{h}=H\omega
.

With the growth of discrete differential geometry, a field that seeks to study discrete geometric structures with integrability as its central tenet, various works have found the discrete counterparts of Weierstrass representations, such as

•

discrete minimal surfaces in Euclidean space
[
2
]
,

•

discrete maximal surfaces in Minkowski space
[
32
]
,

•

discrete cmc-
1
surfaces in hyperbolic space
[
14
]
, or

•

discrete zero mean curvature surfaces in isotropic space
[
22
]
.

The common key ingredient in recovering these discrete counterparts
is the characterization of smooth Weierstrass representations in terms of
transformation theory
of isothermic surfaces, those surfaces that admit conformal curvature line coordinates away from umbilics.
Building on these characterizations, many aforementioned works on discrete Weierstrass representations use the theory of discrete isothermic surfaces
[
2
]
and its transformation theory
[
12
,
13
]
to obtain the results.

In this paper, our aim is to examine the case of cmc surfaces in isotropic space, and obtain a discrete analogue of Weierstrass representations (
1.1
) for these surfaces.
However, the Weierstrass representation of smooth cmc surfaces lack a known characterization in terms of transformation theory.
Therefore, we choose an alternate characterization that is unique to cmc surfaces in isotropic space
[
27
]
: namely, any cmc surface in isotropic space can be constructed from a zero mean curvature surface and a sphere.
By reinterpreting the Weierstrass representation (
1.1
) in terms of this characterization, we will obtain a discrete analogue of the Weierstrass representation, and check that the resulting discrete surface indeed has constant mean curvature given in terms of
mixed areas

[
24
,
22
]
.

To achieve this goal, we structure our paper as follows.

Section
2
is preparatory in nature, introducing smooth and discrete surface theory in isotropic
3
-space.
In detail, after introducing isotropic space in Section
2.1
, Sections
2.2
and
2.3
concern smooth surface theory, culminating in the important reinterpretation of Weierstrass representation for cmc surfaces in Example
2.5
.
Then we switch our attention to discrete surface theory in Sections
2.4
and
2.5
, reviewing the concept of circular nets and discrete isothermic surfaces in the context of isotropic space
[
25
]
.
In doing so, we also closely examine the concept of
discrete lightlike Gauss maps
of circular nets, as it allows us to consider mean curvatures for discrete surfaces via mixed areas.

Our main result is contained in Section
3
: we will first use the characterization of cmc surfaces in terms of a zero mean curvature surface and a sphere, and create a discrete isothermic surface with identical property in Section
3.2
.
Then in Section
3.3
, we will construct a discrete lightlike Gauss map for the discrete isothermic surface, and use this to check that the surface indeed has constant mean curvature in Section
3.4
, culminating in the desired Weierstrass representation for discrete cmc surfaces in Theorem
3.8
.
After discussing the existence of parallel discrete cmc surfaces in Theorem
3.9
, we put our representation to test by constructing concrete examples in Section
3.5
, including discrete Delaunay-type surfaces in isotropic space.
In particular, we obtain
explicit closed-form parametrizations
of all our examples, a relatively rare result in discrete differential geometry.

2.
Preliminaries

We first introduce isotropic
3
-space and the surface theory within, both smooth and discrete.

2.1.
Laguerre geometric model of isotropic
3
-space

In this paper, we will use the Laguerre geometric model of isotropic
3
-space, which we briefly review here.
(For a more detailed account, we refer the readers to
[
8
, Section 2.2]
.)

Let
\mathbb{R}^{3,1}
denote the Minkowski
4
-space equipped with the bilinear form
(\cdot,\cdot)
of signature
(-+++)
, and for any
\tilde{S}\in\mathbb{R}^{3,1}
, let

\mathcal{L}_{\tilde{S}}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\{X\in\mathbb{R}^{3,1}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}(X-\tilde{S},X-\tilde{S})=0\}

be the affine lightcone of
\mathbb{R}^{3,1}
centered around
\tilde{S}
.
For brevity, we will write the lightcone
\mathcal{L}_{0}
of
\mathbb{R}^{3,1}
as
\mathcal{L}
.
Choosing
\mathfrak{p},\tilde{\mathfrak{p}}\in\mathcal{L}
such that
(\mathfrak{p},\tilde{\mathfrak{p}})=1
, we have

\langle\mathfrak{p},\tilde{\mathfrak{p}}\rangle^{\perp}\cong\mathbb{R}^{2},

allowing us to split
\mathbb{R}^{3,1}
as

\mathbb{R}^{3,1}=\mathbb{R}^{2}\oplus_{\perp}(\langle\mathfrak{p}\rangle\oplus\langle\tilde{\mathfrak{p}}\rangle).

Thus, we may define projections
\pi_{\mathfrak{p}}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\mathbb{R}^{3,1}\to\langle\mathfrak{p}\rangle
and
\pi_{\tilde{\mathfrak{p}}}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\mathbb{R}^{3,1}\to\langle\tilde{\mathfrak{p}}\rangle
via

\pi_{\mathfrak{p}}(X)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=(X,\tilde{\mathfrak{p}})\mathfrak{p}\quad\text{and}\quad\pi_{\tilde{\mathfrak{p}}}(X)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=(X,\mathfrak{p})\tilde{\mathfrak{p}},

which in turn allows us to define orthoprojection
\pi_{2}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\mathbb{R}^{3,1}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
via

\pi_{2}(X)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=X-\pi_{\mathfrak{p}}(X)-\pi_{\tilde{\mathfrak{p}}}(X).

Under the splitting, isotropic
3
-space
\mathbb{I}^{3}
is given by the kernel of
\pi_{\tilde{\mathfrak{p}}}
, that is, it is the subspace

\mathbb{I}^{3}=\{X\in\mathbb{R}^{3,1}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}(X,\mathfrak{p})=0\}=\mathbb{R}^{2}\oplus\langle\mathfrak{p}\rangle.

Therefore, any
X\in\mathbb{I}^{3}
can be written as

X=\pi_{2}(X)+\pi_{\mathfrak{p}}(X)=\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}x+\chi\mathfrak{p}

for some
\chi\in\mathbb{R}
.
To make use of explicit coordinates for isotropic space, we normalize

\mathfrak{p}=(1,0,0,1)^{t}\quad\text{and}\quad\tilde{\mathfrak{p}}=\frac{1}{2}(-1,0,0,1)^{t}

so that
\mathbb{I}^{3}
admits a coordinate chart
\psi\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\{(\mathbf{l},\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\}\to\mathbb{I}^{3}
via

\psi((\mathbf{l},\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}))=(\mathbf{l},\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},\mathbf{l})^{t}.

Then the induced metric on isotropic space reads

\dif{\mathbf{x}}^{2}+\dif{\mathbf{y}}^{2}.

(2.1)

We will refer to both
\{(\mathbf{l},\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},\mathbf{l})^{t}\}
and
\{(\mathbf{l},\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\}
as isotropic
3
-space, and call
\mathbf{l}
–direction “vertical”.

Then
(spacelike) spheres

S
in isotropic
3
-space are given by the affine lightcones via

S=\mathbb{I}^{3}\cap\mathcal{L}_{\tilde{S}}.

Elliptic circles
in isotropic
3
-space are then given by the intersection between spheres and spacelike
2
-planes, so that it is in the shape of a Euclidean ellipse (such that its orthoprojection to
\mathbb{R}^{2}
is a circle), while
parabolic circles
are given by the intersection between spheres and vertical
2
-planes, so that it is in the shape of a Euclidean parabola (with vertical axis).

2.2.
Smooth surface theory in isotropic space

For some simply-connected domain
D\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
, let
X\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}D\to\mathbb{I}^{3}
be a spacelike immersion.
Viewing
X
as a codimension two surface in
\mathbb{R}^{3,1}
, every fiber of the normal bundle is a vector space of induced signature
(1,1)
having
\mathfrak{p}
as a constant null section.
Thus, we may find a unique null section
N\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}D\to\mathcal{L}
of the normal bundle such that

(\dif{X},N)=0\quad\text{and}\quad(N,\mathfrak{p})=1.

This is the
lightlike Gauss map
of
X
.

Under this setting, the first and second fundamental forms of
X
are given by

\dif{s}^{2}=(\dif{X},\dif{X})\quad\text{and}\quad\mathrm{II}=-(\dif{X},\dif{N}),

and the definitions of
(extrinsic) Gauss curvature
K
and
mean curvature
H
follow.
In particular, the mean curvature
H
of a surface
X
can be given explicitly in terms of the vertical coordinate as follows (see, for example,
[
28
, Equation (5.2)]
,
[
25
, Equation (9)]
, or
[
8
, Lemma 3.4]
):

Fact 2.1
.

Let
X\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}D\to\mathbb{I}^{3}
be a spacelike immersion parametrized by coordinates
(u,v)\in D
via

X(u,v)=(\mathbf{l}(u,v),\mathbf{x}(u,v),\mathbf{y}(u,v)).

Then the mean curvature
H
of
X
is given by

H=\frac{1}{2}\Delta_{X}\mathbf{l}

where
\Delta_{X}
denotes the Laplacian with respect to the induced metric on
X
.

Remark 2.2
.

We note here that since the induced metric of
X
and
x\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\pi_{2}(X)
are identical by (
2.1
), the mean curvature
H
of
X
can also be found via

H=\frac{1}{2}\Delta_{x}\mathbf{l}.

Example 2.3
.

As an example, let us find the mean curvature of a sphere.
Let
S\in\mathbb{R}^{3,1}
be given by

\tilde{S}=c+r\tilde{\mathfrak{p}}=(c_{0}-\tfrac{r}{2},c_{1},c_{2},c_{0}+\tfrac{r}{2})^{t}

where
c=\pi_{2}(S)+\pi_{\mathfrak{p}}(S)
and
r\tilde{\mathfrak{p}}=\pi_{\tilde{\mathfrak{p}}}(S)
, so that
S=\mathcal{L}_{\tilde{S}}\cap\mathbb{I}^{3}
is a sphere in isotropic space with center
c\in\mathbb{I}^{3}
and radius
r\in\mathbb{R}
.
Then one can calculate directly that
X=(\mathbf{l},\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},\mathbf{l})^{t}\in S
if and only if

\mathbf{l}=\frac{1}{2r}((\mathbf{x}-c_{1})^{2}+(\mathbf{y}-c_{2})^{2})+c_{0}.

Therefore, we can view sphere
\tilde{S}
with radius
r
as the graph of a function

f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=\frac{1}{2r}((\mathbf{x}-c_{1})^{2}+(\mathbf{y}-c_{2})^{2})+c_{0},

and calculate that the mean curvature
H
of the sphere must satisfy

H=\frac{1}{2}(f_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}+f_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}})=\frac{1}{r}.

Example 2.4

(Weierstrass representations of zero mean curvature surfaces)
.

If the mean curvature
H
of a surface
X
identically vanishes, then the surface is called a
zero mean curvature surface
, and must locally be the graph of a harmonic function.
Thus, zero mean curvature surfaces in isotropic
3
-space also admit Weierstrass representation
[
28
, Equation (8.31)]
: any zero mean curvature surface
X
can locally be represented as

X=\Re\int(g,1,-\mathbbm{i})\omega

where
g
is a meromorphic function and
\omega
is a holomorphic
1
-form such that
g^{2}\omega
is holomorphic.
Here,
\mathbbm{i}
denotes the complex imaginary unit.

2.3.
Sum of surfaces as graphs

For
\iota=1,2
, let
X_{\iota}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}D\to\mathbb{I}^{3}
be spacelike immersions so that
\pi_{2}(X_{1})=\pi_{2}(X_{2})=x
, while
\pi_{\mathfrak{p}}(X_{\iota})=\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\mathbf{l}_{\iota}\mathfrak{p}
so that

X_{1}=x+\mathbf{l}_{1}\mathfrak{p}\quad\text{and}\quad X_{2}=x+\mathbf{l}_{2}\mathfrak{p}.

Then the respective mean curvatures satisfy

H_{\iota}=\frac{1}{2}\Delta_{x}\mathbf{l}_{\iota}.

Now let
X\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}D\to\mathbb{I}^{3}
be the surface obtained by adding
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
as graphs, denoted by

X=X_{1}+_{\mathrm{gr}}X_{2},

that is,

X=x+(\mathbf{l}_{1}+\mathbf{l}_{2})\mathfrak{p}=\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}x+\mathbf{l}\mathfrak{p}.

Then the mean curvature
H
of
X
must be

H=\frac{1}{2}\Delta_{x}\mathbf{l}=\frac{1}{2}\Delta_{x}(\mathbf{l}_{1}+\mathbf{l}_{2})=H_{1}+H_{2}.

(2.2)

Thus if a surface
X
is obtained as a sum of two surfaces
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
viewed as graphs of functions, then the mean curvature
H
of the original surface
X
must be obtained by adding the mean curvatures
H_{1}
and
H_{2}
of the surfaces
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
, respectively.

Example 2.5

(Weierstrass representation of non-zero constant mean curvature (cmc) surface)
.

In
[
8
, Theorem 3.10]
, a Weierstrass representation for cmc-
H
surfaces were given: any cmc-
H
surface
Y
can locally be obtained via

Y=\Re\int(\bar{h}+g,1,-\mathbbm{i})\omega

for some holomorphic
h
, meromorphic
g
, and holomorphic
1
-form
\omega
such that

(1)

g^{2}\omega
is holomorphic, and

(2)

\dif{h}=H\omega
.

Let

S\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\Re\int(\bar{h},1,-\mathbbm{i})\omega\quad\text{and}\quad X\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\Re\int(g,1,-\mathbbm{i})\omega

so that

Y=S+_{\mathrm{gr}}X.

Then by Example
2.4
we see that
X
is a zero mean curvature surface, while
[
8
, § 3.5]
tells us that
S
is a sphere with radius
\tfrac{1}{H}
, so that it has mean curvature
H
by Example
2.3
.
Thus (
2.2
) tells us that
Y
indeed is a cmc-
H
surface.

In summary, Weierstrass representation for cmc-
H
surfaces tells us that any cmc-
H
surface
Y
can locally be obtained as

Y=S+_{\mathrm{gr}}X

(2.3)

where
S
is a sphere with mean curvature
H
, while
X
is a zero mean curvature surface. (See also
[
27
, Section 7]
.)

We shall use this characterization of cmc-
H
surfaces in isotropic space to obtain a discrete analogue of the Weierstrass representation.

2.4.
Circular nets

Now we shift our focus to discrete surfaces in isotropic
3
-space.
Let us assume throughout that
\Sigma\subset\mathbb{Z}^{2}
is a discrete simply-connected domain in the sense of
[
6
, Definition 2.15]
with
(m,n)\in\Sigma
.
We will often refer to an edge
((m,n),(m+1,n))
or
((m,n),(m,n+1))
as edge
(ij)
, or a face
((m,n),(m+1,n),(m+1,n+1),(m,n+1))
as an
elementary quadrilateral
(ijk\ell)
.

For a discrete function
\mathfrak{f}
defined on
\Sigma
, we will often write for any
i\in\Sigma
,

\mathfrak{f}_{i}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\mathfrak{f}(i).

Then the discrete
1
-form
\dif{\mathfrak{f}}
is given via

\dif{\mathfrak{f}}_{ij}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\mathfrak{f}_{i}-\mathfrak{f}_{j}

on any oriented edge
(ij)
.
We can then use

\mathfrak{f}_{ij}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\frac{1}{2}(\mathfrak{f}_{i}+\mathfrak{f}_{j}),

to state the Leibniz rule for discrete functions:

Fact 2.6

(
[
7
, Equation (2.2)]
)
.

Let
\mathfrak{f},\mathfrak{g}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\Sigma\to V
where
V
is a vector space equipped with a symmetric bilinear product
\odot
.
Then we have

\dif{(\mathfrak{f}\odot\mathfrak{g})}_{ij}=\dif{\mathfrak{f}}_{ij}\odot\mathfrak{g}_{ij}+\mathfrak{f}_{ij}\odot\dif{\mathfrak{g}}_{ij}.

We will focus on the particular case of
circular nets
:

Definition 2.7

(
[
25
, Theorem 1]
)
.

A discrete surface
X\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\Sigma\to\mathbb{I}^{3}
is called a
circular net
, if on any elementary quadrilateral
(ijk\ell)
, we have
X_{i}
,
X_{j}
,
X_{k}
, and
X_{\ell}
lies on an elliptic circle.
Equivalently,
X
satisfies the conditions

(1)

X_{i}
,
X_{j}
,
X_{k}
, and
X_{\ell}
are coplanar, and

(2)

for
x=\pi_{2}(X)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\Sigma\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
,
x_{i}
,
x_{j}
,
x_{k}
, and
x_{\ell}
are concircular

on any elementary quadrilateral
(ijk\ell)
.

To see how curvatures are defined on circular nets, we will discretize the notion of lightlike Gauss maps, and introduce

discrete lightlike Gauss maps
.
For this, let
X\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\Sigma\to\mathbb{I}^{3}
be a circular net, and define

P\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\{X\in\mathbb{R}^{3,1}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}(X,\mathfrak{p})=1\}.

Now, let
N\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\Sigma\to\mathcal{L}\cap P
be determined by the condition

\dif{X}_{ij}\parallel\dif{N}_{ij}\neq 0

(2.4)

on any edge
(ij)
generically.

We must first check that (
2.4
) gives rise to a well-defined
N
, only depending on the choice of
N
at a single vertex.

Lemma 2.8

(cf.
[
9
, Lemma 4.1.2]
)
.

The propogation of
N
via (
2.4
) is consistent.

Proof.

To see this, first fix any edge
(ij)
, and let
\alpha
be some real-valued positive function defined on unoriented edges so that

N_{i}-N_{j}=\dif{N}_{ij}=-\alpha_{ij}\dif{X}_{ij}.

To find
\alpha_{ij}
, we use

N_{j}=\alpha_{ij}\dif{X}_{ij}+N_{i},

to calculate

0=(N_{j},N_{j})=\alpha_{ij}^{2}(\dif{X}_{ij},\dif{X}_{ij})+2\alpha_{ij}(\dif{X}_{ij},N_{i}),

and conclude that

\alpha_{ij}=-\frac{2(\dif{X}_{ij},N_{i})}{(\dif{X}_{ij},\dif{X}_{ij})}

since
\alpha_{ij}\neq 0
.
Therefore, on any edge
(ij)
,
N_{j}
is uniquely determined from
\dif{X}_{ij}
and
N_{i}
via

N_{j}=-\frac{2(\dif{X}_{ij},N_{i})}{(\dif{X}_{ij},\dif{X}_{ij})}\dif{X}_{ij}+N_{i}.

(2.5)

Now, by applying appropriate rotations and translations, assume without loss of generality that
X_{i}
,
X_{j}
,
X_{k}
, and
X_{\ell}
lie on a unit circle in
\mathbb{R}^{2}\cong\langle\mathfrak{p},\tilde{\mathfrak{p}}\rangle^{\perp}
, that is, there is some
\theta_{\iota}\in\mathbb{R}
for
\iota\in\{i,j,k,\ell\}
such that,

X_{\iota}=(0,\cos\theta_{\iota},\sin\theta_{\iota},0)^{t}.

Defining

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{\iota}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=(0,-\sin\theta_{\iota},\cos\theta_{\iota},0)^{t}

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{\iota}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=(0,-\cos\theta_{\iota},-\sin\theta_{\iota},0)^{t},

and taking
\{\mathcal{T}_{\iota},\mathcal{N}_{\iota},\mathfrak{p},\tilde{\mathfrak{p}}\}
as a basis of
\mathbb{R}^{3,1}
at
X_{\iota}
, we see that
N_{\iota}\in\mathcal{L}\cap P
implies

N_{\iota}=A_{\iota}\mathcal{T}_{\iota}+B_{\iota}\mathcal{N}_{\iota}-\tfrac{1}{2}(A_{\iota}^{2}+B_{\iota}^{2})\mathfrak{p}+\tilde{\mathfrak{p}}

(2.6)

for some
A_{\iota},B_{\iota}\in\mathbb{R}
.
Under this setting, we use (
2.5
) to calculate
N_{j}
from
N_{i}
and find that

N_{j}=-A_{i}\mathcal{T}_{j}+B_{i}\mathcal{N}_{j}-\tfrac{1}{2}(A_{i}^{2}+B_{i}^{2})\mathfrak{p}+\tilde{\mathfrak{p}}.

(2.7)

Thus, for any initial choice of
N
at a single vertex, say
i\in\Sigma
, we can now calculate
N
around an elementary quadrilateral
(ijk\ell)
using the propogation formula (
2.7
) to see that

\displaystyle N_{i}

\displaystyle=A_{i}\mathcal{T}_{i}+B_{i}\mathcal{N}_{i}-\tfrac{1}{2}(A_{i}^{2}+B_{i}^{2})\mathfrak{p}+\tilde{\mathfrak{p}}

\displaystyle N_{j}

\displaystyle=-A_{i}\mathcal{T}_{j}+B_{i}\mathcal{N}_{j}-\tfrac{1}{2}(A_{i}^{2}+B_{i}^{2})\mathfrak{p}+\tilde{\mathfrak{p}}

\displaystyle N_{k}

\displaystyle=A_{i}\mathcal{T}_{k}+B_{i}\mathcal{N}_{k}-\tfrac{1}{2}(A_{i}^{2}+B_{i}^{2})\mathfrak{p}+\tilde{\mathfrak{p}}

\displaystyle N_{\ell}

\displaystyle=-A_{i}\mathcal{T}_{\ell}+B_{i}\mathcal{N}_{\ell}-\tfrac{1}{2}(A_{i}^{2}+B_{i}^{2})\mathfrak{p}+\tilde{\mathfrak{p}}.

Finally, denoting the propogation of
N
along the edge
(\ell i)
from
N_{\ell}
as
\tilde{N}_{i}
, we find that

\tilde{N}_{i}=A_{i}\mathcal{T}_{i}+B_{i}\mathcal{N}_{i}-\tfrac{1}{2}(A_{i}^{2}+B_{i}^{2})\mathfrak{p}+\tilde{\mathfrak{p}}=N_{i}

so that the propogation is consistent.

Remark 2.9
.

Since
X
and
N
are edge-parallel, or

,
N

[

Remark 2.10
.

Note that the expression for
N

\nu\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=N-\tilde{\mathfrak{p}}

\mathbb{I}^{3}

Now that lightlike Gauss map
N
of a circular net

Definition 2.11

(
[
22
, p. 822]
(cf.
[
6
, Lemma 2.12]
))
.

Let
X,\tilde{X}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\Sigma\to\mathbb{R}^{3,1}
be edge-parallel circular nets.
The
\wedge^{2}\mathbb{R}^{3,1}
–valued
mixed area
of
X
and
\tilde{X}
, denoted by
A(X,\tilde{X})
, is given by

A(X,\tilde{X})_{ijk\ell}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\frac{1}{4}((X_{i}-X_{k})\wedge(\tilde{X}_{j}-\tilde{X}_{\ell})-(X_{j}-X_{\ell})\wedge(\tilde{X}_{i}-\tilde{X}_{k})).

Remark 2.12
.

It follows directly that the notion of mixed area is bilinear on the space of pairwise edge-parallel circular nets.

Using the notion mixed areas, mean curvature of a circular net
X
is given via the lightlike Gauss map
N
:

Definition 2.13

(
[
22
, Definition 3.2]
)
.

Let
X\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\Sigma\to\mathbb{I}^{3}
be a circular net with lightlike Gauss map
N
.
The mean curvature
H
of
X
over an elementary quadrilateral
(ijk\ell)
is given by the relation

A(X,N)_{ijk\ell}+H_{ijk\ell}A(X,X)_{ijk\ell}=0.

Remark 2.14
.

Since for any diagonal
(ik)
over an elementary quadrilateral
(ijk\ell)
, we have

N_{i}-N_{k}=\nu_{i}-\nu_{k}

where
\nu=N-\tilde{\mathfrak{p}}
is the corresponding Gauss map, we can rewrite the mean curvature as in
[
22
, Examples 3.3(ii)]
:

A(X,\nu)_{ijk\ell}+H_{ijk\ell}A(X,X)_{ijk\ell}=0.

(2.8)

Now that the mixed areas in the expression for the mean curvature (
2.8
) only involves circular nets in
\mathbb{I}^{3}
, we can use (
2.1
) to further simplify the expression as in
[
25
, Equation (17)]
in terms of
x\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\pi_{2}(X)
and
n\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\pi_{2}(\nu)
:

A(x,n)_{ijk\ell}+H_{ijk\ell}A(x,x)_{ijk\ell}=0.

(2.9)

2.5.
Discrete isothermic surfaces

Smooth isothermic surfaces are characterized as surfaces that admit conformal curvature line coordinates away from umbilics; as circular nets are discretizations of curvature line parametrized surfaces
[
21
]
, discrete isothermic surfaces are defined as a subclass of circular nets using the notion of discrete holomorphic functions in
\mathbb{C}
:

Definition 2.15

(
[
2
, Definition 8]
)
.

A discrete map
x\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\Sigma\to\mathbb{C}\cong\mathbb{R}^{2}
is called a
discrete holomorphic function
if on every elementary quadrilateral, we have

\mathrm{cr}(x_{i},x_{j},x_{k},x_{\ell})\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\frac{x_{i}-x_{j}}{x_{j}-x_{k}}\frac{x_{k}-x_{\ell}}{x_{\ell}-x_{i}}=\frac{m_{i\ell}}{m_{ij}}

for some real non-vanishing discrete function
m
defined on unoriented edges satisfying the
edge-labeling
property, that is,

m_{ij}=m_{\ell k}\quad\text{and}\quad m_{i\ell}=m_{jk}.

The discrete function
m
is called a
cross-ratios factorizing function
of
x
, and is determined up to multiplication by a constant.

Definition 2.16

(
[
25
, Theorem 3]
)
.

A circular net
X\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\Sigma\to\mathbb{I}^{3}
is called a
discrete isothermic surface
if
x\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\pi_{2}(X)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\Sigma\to\mathbb{R}^{2}\cong\mathbb{C}
is a discrete holomorphic function.

These notions can be combined to give the Weierstrass representation for discrete zero mean curvature surfaces:

Fact 2.17

(
[
22
, Theorem 4.1]
(cf.
[
25
, § 4]
))
.

Let
g\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\Sigma\to\mathbb{C}
be a discrete holomorphic function.
If
X\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\Sigma\to\mathbb{I}^{3}
is given recursively via

\dif{X}_{ij}=\frac{1}{m_{ij}}\Re\left((g_{ij},1,-\mathbbm{i})\frac{1}{\dif{g}_{ij}}\right),

then
X
is a discrete isothermic zero mean curvature surface.

3.
Discrete cmc surfaces in isotropic space

In this section, we will use the characterization of smooth cmc-
H
surfaces in isotropic
3
-space obtained in Example
2.5
to obtain a Weierstrass representation for discrete cmc surfaces in isotropic space, and show that these discrete surfaces indeed have constant mean curvature by choosing a suitable lightlike Gauss map.

3.1.
Adding discrete surfaces as graphs

As the characterization of smooth cmc-
H
surfaces obtained in Example
2.5
depend on the notion of adding two surfaces as graphs, we will first define a similar notion for discrete surfaces:

Definition 3.1
.

Let
X_{1},X_{2}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\Sigma\to\mathbb{I}^{3}
be discrete surfaces such that

\pi_{2}(X_{1})=\pi_{2}(X_{2})=x\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\Sigma\to\mathbb{R}^{2}

so that there exists functions
\mathbf{l}_{1},\mathbf{l}_{2}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\Sigma\to\mathbb{R}
with

X_{1}=x+\mathbf{l}_{1}\mathfrak{p}\quad\text{and}\quad X_{2}=x+\mathbf{l}_{2}\mathfrak{p}.

Then the discrete surface
X\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\Sigma\to\mathbb{I}^{3}
obtained via

X\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=x+(\mathbf{l}_{1}+\mathbf{l}_{2})\mathfrak{p}

is called a
sum of
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
as graphs
, denoted as

X=X_{1}+_{\mathrm{gr}}X_{2}.

The definition of circular nets in Definition
2.7
and discrete isothermic surfaces in Definition
2.16
immediately yields the following:

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
X_{1},X_{2}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\Sigma\to\mathbb{I}^{3}
be circular nets with
\pi_{2}(X_{1})=\pi_{2}(X_{2})
.
If
X=X_{1}+_{\mathrm{gr}}X_{2}
, then
X
is also a circular net.
Furthermore, if
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
are discrete isothermic surfaces, then
X
is also a discrete isothermic surface.

3.2.
Construction of the discrete surface

We will construct a discrete surface
Y\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\Sigma\to\mathbb{I}^{3}
from a discrete isothermic zero mean curvature surface
X\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\Sigma\to\mathbb{I}^{3}
following the smooth characterization in (
2.3
), that is,

Y\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=X+_{\mathrm{gr}}S

for some mapping
S
into a sphere in isotropic space.

To start, let us fix some discrete isothermic zero mean curvature surface
X\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\Sigma\to\mathbb{I}^{3}
arising from a discrete holomorphic function
g\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\Sigma\to\mathbb{C}
with cross-ratios factorizing function
m
via Weierstrass representation in Fact
2.17
, that is,

\dif{X}_{ij}=\frac{1}{m_{ij}}\Re\left((g_{ij},1,-\mathbbm{i})\frac{1}{\dif{g}_{ij}}\right).

(3.1)

Now let
S\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\Sigma\to\mathbb{I}^{3}
be a map into a sphere of non-zero mean curvature
H\neq 0
.
We may assume without loss of generality that
S
is centered at the origin, so that

S\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\mathbf{l}=\frac{H}{2}(\mathbf{x}^{2}+\mathbf{y}^{2}).

To add
X
and
S
as graphs we require that
\pi_{2}(S)=\pi_{2}(X)=\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}x
, allowing us to write

S=x+\frac{H}{2}(x,x)\mathfrak{p}.

In fact, so obtained
S
is a circular net:

Lemma 3.3
.

S
is a circular net.

Proof.

For any elementary quadrilateral
(ijk\ell)
, we will show that
S_{i}
,
S_{j}
,
S_{k}
, and
S_{\ell}
are coplanar.
As we know that
x_{i}
,
x_{j}
,
x_{k}
, and
x_{\ell}
are concircular since
X
is discrete isothermic, we can deduce that
S_{i}
,
S_{j}
,
S_{k}
, and
S_{\ell}
must lie on the intersection of the sphere

\mathbf{l}=\tfrac{H}{2}(\mathbf{x}^{2}+\mathbf{y}^{2})

(3.2)

and the circular cylinder

(\mathbf{x}-x_{1})^{2}+(\mathbf{y}-x_{2})^{2}=r^{2}

(3.3)

for some real constants
x_{1}
,
x_{2}
, and
r>0
.

From (
3.3
), we get

\mathbf{x}^{2}+\mathbf{y}^{2}=2x_{1}\mathbf{x}-x_{1}^{2}+2x_{2}\mathbf{y}-x_{2}^{2}+r^{2},

and substituting into (
3.2
), we have

\mathbf{l}=Hx_{1}\mathbf{x}+Hx_{2}\mathbf{y}+\tfrac{H}{2}(r^{2}-x_{1}^{2}-x_{2}^{2}).

Thus, the intersection of a sphere and a circular cylinder must lie on a plane.
∎

Now we wish to find an explicit parametrization of
S
in terms of
g
.
To achieve this, first note that since
g
is a discrete holomorphic function, it is a discrete isothermic surface; thus
g
admits a Christoffel dual
h\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\Sigma\to\mathbb{C}
(see
[
2
, Theorem 6]
or
[
1
, Equation (1.5)]
), that is,
h
is well-defined recursively via

\dif{h}_{ij}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\frac{H}{m_{ij}\dif{g}_{ij}}.

(3.4)

Such
h
is also a discrete holomorphic function with cross-ratios factorizing function
\tilde{m}=\frac{1}{m}
.

Remark 3.4
.

Since
h
is a Christoffel dual of
g
in the complex plane, we have that
h
is a Christoffel dual of
\bar{g}
when viewed as maps into
\mathbb{R}^{2}
(see
[
15
, § 5.2.2]
).
Thus, by
[
24
, Equation (8)]
,
[
3
, Theorem 4.42]
, or
[
6
, Theorem 4.11]
, we have

A(h,\bar{g})\equiv 0.

Using
h
, we can now rewrite
\dif{X}_{ij}
in (
3.1
) as

\dif{X}_{ij}=\frac{1}{m_{ij}}\Re\left((g_{ij},1,-\mathbbm{i})\frac{1}{\dif{g}_{ij}}\right)=\frac{1}{H}\Re\left((g_{ij},1,-\mathbbm{i})\dif{h}_{ij}\right),

(3.5)

which immediately yields

\dif{x}_{ij}=\frac{1}{H}\Re\left((0,1,-\mathbbm{i})\dif{h}_{ij}\right).

Therefore, without loss of generality, we may assume that

x=\frac{1}{H}\Re((0,1,-\mathbbm{i})h)=\frac{1}{H}(0,\Re h,\Im h).

(3.6)

This allows us to write
S
explicitly since

(x,x)=\frac{1}{H^{2}}((\Re h)^{2}+(\Im h)^{2})=\frac{|h|^{2}}{H^{2}}

and thus

S=x+\tfrac{H}{2}(x,x)\mathfrak{p}=\left(\tfrac{1}{2H}|h|^{2},\tfrac{1}{H}\Re h,\tfrac{1}{H}\Im h\right)=\tfrac{1}{H}\Re\left(\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\bar{h},1,-\mathbbm{i}\right)h\right).

Using the fact that

\displaystyle\Re(\bar{h}_{ij}\dif{h}_{ij})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\Re(|h_{i}|^{2}-\bar{h}_{i}h_{j}+h_{i}\bar{h}_{j}-|h_{j}|^{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\Re(|h_{i}|^{2}+2\mathbbm{i}\Im(h_{i}\bar{h}_{j})-|h_{j}|^{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\Re(|h_{i}|^{2}-|h_{j}|^{2}),

(3.7)

we can verify

\displaystyle\dif{S}_{ij}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{H}\Re\left(\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\bar{h}_{i},1,-\mathbbm{i}\right)h_{i}\right)-\Re\left(\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\bar{h}_{j},1,-\mathbbm{i}\right)h_{j}\right)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{H}\Re\left(\left(\tfrac{1}{2}|h_{i}|^{2},h_{i},-\mathbbm{i}h_{i}\right)-\left(\tfrac{1}{2}|h_{j}|^{2},h_{j},-\mathbbm{i}h_{j}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{H}\Re\left(\bar{h}_{ij}\dif{h}_{ij},\dif{h}_{ij},-\mathbbm{i}\dif{h}_{ij}\right)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{H}\Re\left(\left(\bar{h}_{ij},1,-\mathbbm{i}\right)\dif{h}_{ij}\right).

(3.8)

Therefore, if
Y\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=X+_{\mathrm{gr}}S
, then (
3.5
) and (
3.8
) implies

\dif{Y}_{ij}=\frac{1}{H}\Re\left((\bar{h}_{ij}+g_{ij},1,-\mathbbm{i})\dif{h}_{ij}\right).

Finally, Proposition
3.2
and Lemma
3.3
allows us to conclude
Y
is a discrete isothermic surface.
Summarizing:

Proposition 3.5
.

For a discrete holomorphic function
g\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\Sigma\to\mathbb{C}
with cross-ratios factorizing function
m
, a discrete isothermic surface
Y\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\Sigma\to\mathbb{I}^{3}
can be defined recursively via

\dif{Y}_{ij}=\frac{1}{H}\Re\left((\bar{h}_{ij}+g_{ij},1,-\mathbbm{i})\dif{h}_{ij}\right)

(3.9)

where

\dif{h}_{ij}=\frac{H}{m_{ij}\dif{g}_{ij}}.

3.3.
Construction of the lightlike Gauss map

We will now construct the lightlike Gauss map
N\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\Sigma\to\mathcal{L}\cap P
of the discrete isothermic surface
Y\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\Sigma\to\mathbb{I}^{3}
obtained via (
3.9
).

Defining
\varphi\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\bar{h}+g
, if we take

N\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=-\frac{1}{2}(1+|\varphi|^{2},2\Re\varphi,-2\Im\varphi,-1+|\varphi|^{2})^{t},

then we can directly verify that

(N,N)=0\quad\text{and}\quad(N,\mathfrak{p})=1.

We will use
\nu=N-\tilde{\mathfrak{p}}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\Sigma\to\mathbb{I}^{3}
given by

\nu=-\frac{1}{2}(|\varphi|^{2},2\Re\varphi,-2\Im\varphi)

(3.10)

to show the following:

Lemma 3.6
.

For a real function
\beta
defined on unoriented edges of
\Sigma
given by

\beta_{ij}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=-m_{ij}|{\dif{g}_{ij}}|^{2}-H,

we have

\dif{N}_{ij}=\dif{\nu}_{ij}=\beta_{ij}\dif{Y}_{ij}.

Proof.

Using a similar relation to (
3.7
) for
\varphi
, we may write

\displaystyle\dif{\nu}_{ij}

\displaystyle=\left(-\tfrac{1}{2}(|\varphi_{i}|^{2}-|\varphi_{j}|^{2}),-\Re\dif{\varphi}_{ij},\Im\dif{\varphi}_{ij}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(-\Re(\varphi_{ij}\dif{\bar{\varphi}}_{ij}),-\Re\dif{\bar{\varphi}}_{ij},\Re(\mathbbm{i}\dif{\bar{\varphi}}_{ij})\right)

\displaystyle=\Re\left((-\varphi_{ij},-1,\mathbbm{i})\dif{\bar{\varphi}}_{ij}\right).

(3.11)

Therefore, using the definition of
\dif{h}_{ij}
(
3.4
)

\displaystyle\dif{\nu}_{ij}-\beta_{ij}\dif{Y}_{ij}

\displaystyle=-\Re\left((\varphi_{ij},1,-\mathbbm{i})\left(\dif{h}_{ij}+\dif{\bar{g}_{ij}}+\tfrac{\beta_{ij}}{H}\dif{h}_{ij}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=-\Re\left((\varphi_{ij},1,-\mathbbm{i})\left(\tfrac{H+\beta_{ij}}{H}\tfrac{H}{m_{ij}\dif{g}_{ij}}+\dif{\bar{g}_{ij}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=-\Re\left((\varphi_{ij},1,-\mathbbm{i})(-{\dif{\bar{g}}_{ij}}+\dif{\bar{g}_{ij}})\right)

\displaystyle=0,

giving us the desired conclusion.
∎

Lemma
3.6
tells us that
N
is a lightlike Gauss map of
Y
, allowing us to summarize as follows:

Proposition 3.7
.

The discrete isothermic surface
Y\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\Sigma\to\mathbb{I}^{3}
given by

\dif{Y}_{ij}=\frac{1}{H}\Re\left((\bar{h}_{ij}+g_{ij},1,-\mathbbm{i})\dif{h}_{ij}\right)

(3.12)

with

\dif{h}_{ij}=\frac{H}{m_{ij}\dif{g}_{ij}}

admits a lightlike Gauss map
N\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\Sigma\to\mathcal{L}\cap P
of the form

N=-\frac{1}{2}(1+|\varphi|^{2},2\Re\varphi,-2\Im\varphi,-1+|\varphi|^{2})^{t}

(3.13)

where

\varphi\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\bar{h}+g.

3.4.
Weierstrass representation for discrete cmc surface

Finally, we will use the lightlike Gauss map
N
in (
3.13
) to show that
Y
obtained via (
3.12
) has constant mean curvature
H
.
In particular, we will make use of the expression (
2.9
): thus let us define
x\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\pi_{2}(X)
and
n\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\pi_{2}(\nu)
.
Viewing the discrete complex functions
g
,
h
,
\varphi
, and their conjugates as maps into
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, the expression for
x
(
3.6
) tells us that

x=\frac{1}{H}(0,\Re h,\Im h)=\frac{1}{H}h

while the expression for
\nu
(
3.10
) allows us to see that

n=-(0,\Re\varphi,-\Im\varphi)=-\bar{\varphi}=-h-\bar{g}.

Using these, we can calculate that

A(x,n)_{ijk\ell}+HA(x,x)_{ijk\ell}=A(x,n+Hx)=A\left(\tfrac{1}{H}h,-\bar{g}\right)=-\tfrac{1}{H}A(h,\bar{g}),

which vanishes by Remark
3.4
.
Hence, we have
H_{ijk\ell}\equiv H
, that is,
Y
has constant mean curvature
H
, allowing us to conclude that (
3.12
) gives a Weierstrass representation for discrete isothermic cmc surfaces:

Theorem 3.8

(Weierstrass representation for discrete cmc surfaces)
.

Let
g\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\Sigma\to\mathbb{C}
be a discrete holomorphic function with cross-ratios factorizing function
m
, and let
h
be a discrete holomorphic function defined on any edge
(ij)
via

\dif{h}_{ij}=\frac{H}{m_{ij}\dif{g}_{ij}}=\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}H\omega_{ij}

(3.14)

for some real non-zero constant
H
.
Then the discrete
1
-form

\dif{Y}_{ij}=\Re\left((\bar{h}_{ij}+g_{ij},1,-\mathbbm{i})\,\omega_{ij}\right)

(3.15)

on any edge
(ij)
defines a discrete isothermic constant mean curvature
H
surface
Y\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\Sigma\to\mathbb{I}^{3}
with lightlike Gauss map
N\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\Sigma\to\mathcal{L}\cap P
given by

N=-\frac{1}{2}(1+|\varphi|^{2},2\Re\varphi,-2\Im\varphi,-1+|\varphi|^{2})^{t}

(3.16)

for

\varphi=\bar{h}+g.

In this case, we say that
(h,g)
are the Weierstrass data of
Y
.

As in the smooth case, discrete cmc surfaces in Euclidean space admit a parallel cmc surface
[
12
, p. 23]
.
We will now show the isotropic analogue of such result using the Weierstrass representation:

Theorem 3.9
.

Let
Y\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\Sigma\to\mathbb{I}^{3}
be a discrete isothermic cmc-
H
surface with Gauss map
\nu
given by the Weierstrass data
(h,g)
.
Then
Y^{P}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\Sigma\to\mathbb{I}^{3}
defined by

Y^{P}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=Y+\frac{1}{H}\nu

(3.17)

is a parallel cmc-
(-H)
surface with Weierstrass data

(h^{P},g^{P})=(\bar{g},\bar{h}).

(3.18)

Proof.

From the definition of
Y^{P}
(
3.17
), it is easy to see that
Y^{P}
is edge-parallel to
Y
.
Let us define discrete holomorphic functions
g^{P}
and
h^{P}
via (
3.18
).
Now we calculate using (
3.12
) and (
3.11
) that

\displaystyle\dif{Y}^{P}_{ij}

\displaystyle=\dif{Y}_{ij}+\frac{1}{H}\dif{\nu}_{ij}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{H}\Re\left((\varphi_{ij},1,-\mathbbm{i})\dif{h}_{ij}\right)-\frac{1}{H}\Re\left((\varphi_{ij},1,-\mathbbm{i})\dif{\bar{\varphi}}_{ij}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{H}\Re\left((\varphi_{ij},1,-\mathbbm{i})(\dif{h}_{ij}-\dif{\bar{\varphi}}_{ij})\right)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{H}\Re\left((g_{ij}+\bar{h}_{ij},1,-\mathbbm{i})\dif{\bar{g}}_{ij})\right)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{H}\Re\left((\bar{h}^{P}_{ij}+g^{P}_{ij},1,-\mathbbm{i})\dif{h}^{P}_{ij})\right).

Therefore, the Weierstrass representation for discrete cmc surfaces allows us to conclude that
Y^{P}
is a cmc-
(-H)
surface with Weierstrass data
(\bar{g},\bar{h})
.
∎

and
