Partial replacement of corn and soybean meal with yeast culture improves growth performance and intestinal health and is associated with cecal microbiota modulation in broilers
Authors: Yu Zhang, Jin-Xin Guo, Lan Chen, Dessalegn Lamesgen, Jing-Qi Huang, Mahmoud Mohamed Khalil, Wen-Pan Wang, Lv-Hui Sun, Zhang-Chao Deng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Yeast culture (YC) has demonstrated beneficial effects on animal growth and intestinal health as a functional additive; however, its potential as a partial substitute for conventional corn and soybean meal (SBM) in broiler diets remains unclear. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the effects of partially replacing corn and soybean meal with YC on growth performance, antioxidant capacity, intestinal health, and gut microbiota in broilers. A total of 900 healthy one-day-old Cobb broilers were randomly divided into three treatment groups with 15 replicates of 20 birds/pen. The broilers were fed either a basal diet (CON), a basal diet with 2% YC replacing 1% corn and 1% SBM (2% YC), or a basal diet with 3% YC replacing 1.5% corn and 1.5% SBM (3% YC) for 35 days. The results showed that, compared with the CON, 3% YC treatment significantly increased (p < 0.05) the average daily gain and average daily feed intake, as well as reduced (p < 0.05) the feed-to-gain ratio during the various experimental periods. Meanwhile, 3% YC increased (p < 0.05) the activities of SOD and GPX in serum, villus height, and the expression of ZO-1, Claudin-2, and IL-10 proteins in jejunum. Further cecal microbiota analysis showed that 3% YC enriched (p < 0.05) the abundances of Butyricicoccus, and Kineothrix genera. Furthermore, 3% YC treatment increased (p < 0.05) the cecal butyric acid concentration and also showed a trend toward increased (p = 0.07) butyric acid production in an in vitro fermentation trial. In conclusion, the improvement in broiler growth performance with 3% YC substitution may be associated with increased antioxidant capacity, jejunal morphology and barrier function, and modulation of cecal microbiota. These findings suggest that 3% YC could serve as a promising partial substitute for corn and SBM in broiler diets.

Introduction

The broiler industry plays an important role in ensuring the global supply of animal protein and promoting food security (Nassar et al., 2026). With the rapid development of global intensive poultry production, the demand for feed ingredients has increased sharply. Corn and soybean meal (SBM), as conventional energy and protein sources in poultry diets, have long faced severe challenges such as resource shortages, extreme price volatility, and the growing “feed-food competition” between livestock and humans (Cao et al., 2024; Srikanthithasan et al., 2024). Therefore, developing strategies to reduce and substitute corn and SBM in diets without affecting growth performance is of great significance for alleviating the feed resource crisis and supporting long-term food security.

Yeast culture (YC), a fermentation product containing yeast metabolites and growth substrates (Zhang et al., 2022), is a rich source of nutrients and bioactive compounds (Ahiwe et al., 2020). Previous studies reported that YC can improve intestinal morphology, barrier integrity, and immune function, as well as enhance growth performance in broilers (Kim et al 2022). These beneficial effects are primarily mediated through improved nutrient digestibility and stabilization of gut microbiota (Kyoung et al., 2023), as well as through immunomodulatory and antioxidant activities attributed to bioactive components such as β-glucans and mannans (Fadl et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2022; Zhao et al., 2022). Moreover, YC not only serves as both a nutrient source and a functional modulator, but also improves host health by promoting the proliferation of beneficial bacteria and enhancing the production of short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) (Hou et al., 2025; Mei et al., 2025), which are one of crucial bridges connecting the gut microbiota and host metabolic health (Deng et al., 2025a; Fu et al., 2026).

Although most studies have focused on YC as a functional additive, its application as a partial substitute for conventional corn and SBM in the broiler diets remains insufficiently explored. We hypothesized that YC partially replacing corn and SBM in diets could remodel the cecal microbial structure and increase SCFA production, thereby improving the antioxidant, intestinal health, and growth performance in broilers. Therefore, this study aims to evaluate the effects of partially replacing corn and SBM with YC on growth performance, antioxidant capacity, intestinal health, and gut microbiota in broilers.

Materials and methods

Experimental design and sample collection

The animal protocol was approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Huazhong Agricultural University (HZAUCH-2026-0026), China. A total of 900 healthy one-day-old Cobb broiler chickens (54 ± 0.14 g) were randomly assigned to three dietary treatment groups with 15 replicates of 20 birds per replicate. The broilers from the three groups were fed a basal diet (CON), CON supplemented with 2% YC replacing 1% corn and 1% SBM (2% YC), and CON supplemented with 3% YC replacing 1.5% corn and 1.5% SBM (3% YC). The composition and nutrient levels of each group diets were presented in Table 1. The nutritional composition of YC included crude protein (26.17%), moisture (9.93%), ash (7.17%), and mannan (1.83%). The birds were provided with feed and water ad libitum. Feed intake and mortality rates were recorded throughout the trial period, and all birds were weighed on days 1, 21, and 35 on a cage basis to calculate the initial body weight (IBW), final body weight (FBW), average daily gain (ADG), average daily feed intake (ADFI), and feed-to-gain ratio (F/G). The F/G was calculated based on an individual basis and mortality correction was performed. On day 35, 10 broilers from each group were randomly selected, fasted for 8 hours, weighed, euthanized, and then slaughtered. Serum biochemical and antioxidant status

The serum was prepared by centrifugation at 4 °C at 3000 × g for 15 min and stored at -20 °C. The serum activities of aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), total protein (TP), albumin (ALB), alkaline phosphatase (ALP), and total bilirubin (TBIL) were measured by an automatic biochemistry analyzer (Beckman Synchron CX4 PRO) as previously described (Deng et al., 2024). Serum malondialdehyde (MDA) content, as well as the activities of glutathione peroxidase (GPX), superoxide dismutase (SOD), and catalase (CAT), were measured using commercial assay kits (Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Nanjing, Jiangsu, China) (Huang et al., 2026).

Intestinal morphology

Samples of the duodenum, jejunum, and ileum were examined microscopically after being fixed in 10% buffered formalin, embedded in paraffin, sectioned at 5 µm, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin. The sections were then stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and examined under a light microscope for observation and image acquisition. Villus height (VH), crypt depth (CD), and the villus height-to-crypt depth ratio (VH:CD) were measured and calculated using Prism 9.

Real‑time qPCR analysis

Quantitative PCR (qPCR) analysis was performed as previously described (Shi et al., 2025). Briefly, total RNA was extracted from jejunal tissues using Trizol reagent (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Primers were designed with Primer Express 3.0 software (Applied Biosystems) and are presented in the Supplementary Table S1. The relative mRNA expression levels of target genes was normalized to β-actin using the 2^–ΔΔCt method (Luo et al., 2026).

Western blot analysis

Western blot analysis of jejunal samples was performed as previously described (Yang et al., 2024; Niu et al., 2025). Specific primary antibodies were used for each target protein (Supplementary Table S2). Protein concentrations were determined using a commercial assay kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, China). All protein levels were normalized to the housekeeping protein β-actin.

Cecal 16S rRNA gene sequencing and microbiota analysis

Microbial analysis was performed using cecal digesta samples, which were sequenced by Majorbio Bio-Pharm Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Microbial DNA was extracted according to the previously described protocol (Deng et al., 2025b). Briefly, 10 replicates were randomly selected from three groups, and each sample was amplified with the V3-V4 primers. All amplicons were sequenced using an Illumina Nova6000 platform (2 × 250 bp paired-end), yielding an average of 5G high-quality reads per sample. Raw sequences were quality filtered, denoised, and de-chimerized to avoid sequencing inaccuracy using the EasyAmplicon which is a user-centric guide to perform amplicon sequencing data analysis (Yousuf et al., 2024). To account for uneven sequencing depths across samples, the ASV table was rarefied to 13,200 reads per sample, and data were normalized using the DESeq2 variance-stabilizing transformation (VST) method. Taxonomic classification of amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) was performed against the SILVA 138.2 database at 99% sequence identity. Alpha diversity (chao1 index and shannon index), beta diversity (Bray-Curtis distance), and taxonomic composition were calculated using OmicStudio tools (https://www.omicstudio.cn/tool). Linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe) was applied to identify differentially abundant bacterial taxa, with a linear discriminant analysis (LDA) score threshold of > 3 (Song et al., 2026). PICRUSt2 analysis was used to predict functions of microbial communities, and the differentially enriched metabolic pathways were analyzed using STAMP (v. 2.1.3). Statistical significance of beta diversity among groups was tested using PERMANOVA (999 permutations) via the adonis function in the R vegan package, with treatment group as the fixed factor. Significant differences in other parameters among groups were assessed using the Kruskal-Wallis H test (non-parametric), and Post-hoc pairwise comparisons were conducted using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. The false-discovery rate (FDR) adjusted P values were corrected using the Benjamini-Hochberg method, and an adjusted P-value < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Determination of SCFAs contents

The SCFAs were determined by gas chromatography (Li et al., 2025a) as previously described. Briefly, 0.4 g of the cecal sample was mixed with 1 mL of chromatographic-grade methanol and vortexed for 30 s, followed by centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 10 min at 4°C. The supernatant was then diluted with 25% metaphosphoric acid (v:v,1:5), filtered through a 0.22-µm membrane, and subsequently analyzed using a gas chromatography system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) for SCFAs quantification.

In vitro fermentation experiment

Cecal contents were collected from broiler chickens and immediately used as inoculum for in vitro fermentation. Briefly, 0.1 g of cecal contents was suspended in 900 μL of anaerobic dilution solution (ADS) and vortexed thoroughly (Olson et al., 2025). The obtained 1 mL suspension was then transferred into 299 mL ADS, resulting in a 3000-fold dilution of the cecal inoculum. Subsequently, 20 mL of the diluted inoculum was dispensed into fermentation bottles. Dietary supplementation of 3%YC increased butyric acid concentration, while 2% YC treatment did not alter the concentrations of SCFAs in the cecum, when compared to the CON group (Fig. 6A-E). Thus, two substrate treatments were prepared: (1) CON, containing 0.2 g of basal diet, and (2) 3% YC, in which 3% yeast culture replaced 1.5% corn and 1.5% soybean meal in the basal diet. The fermentation bottles were incubated under anaerobic conditions at 39°C. Fermentation liquid samples were collected at 48 h and 96 h for the determination of SCFAs.

Statistical analysis

Experimental data were organized using Excel and statistically analyzed with SPSS 22.0 software. The experimental unit was the pen (n = 15) for growth performance variables (ADG, ADFI, and F/G), whereas the individual bird (n=10) was considered the experimental unit for serum biochemical parameters, intestinal morphology, and cecal microbiota measurements. A one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed for each response variable, with dietary treatment (CON, 2% YC, and 3% YC) as a fixed effect. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and homogeneity of variances was checked using Levene’s test. When significant main effects were detected, Tukey’s honestly significant difference (HSD) test was used for post hoc multiple comparisons. When assumptions were violated, data were log-transformed or analyzed using the Kruskal–Wallis non-parametric test. Statistical results were expressed as the mean ± standard error (SEM), with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Cecal microbiota community

To determine the composition of the cecal microbiota, we performed high-throughput 16S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing of cecal chyme collected at the end of the 35-day feeding trial (Fig. 4). Compared to the CON group, YC treatment had no affect (p > 0.05) on chao1 and shannon index (Fig. 4A, B). Meanwhile, principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) showed a significant separation (R = 0.1498, p = 0.016) of cecal microbial communities among three groups (Fig. 4C). Notably, compared with the CON group, YC treatment altered a distinct shift in microbial community structure, with the 3% YC group showing a more pronounced separation than the 2% YC group. Further taxonomic composition analysis showed that the microbial community was dominated by Firmicutes and Bacteroidota phylum, while Faecalibacterium was the most abundant genus (Fig. 4D, E). To identify which bacterial taxa were distinct among the three groups, LEfSe analysis was performed, and the results exhibited that a total of 7 and 11 bacterial taxa were enriched in the 2% and 3% YC group when compared to the CON group, respectively (Fig. 5A, B). Specifically, the abundances of Sporobacter and Massiliimalia were significantly higher in the 2% YC group; the abundances of Kineothrix, Butyricicoccus, and Bifidobacterium significantly increased in the 3% YC group; while the abundance of Mogibacterium was reduced after YC treatment (p < 0.05; Fig. 5C-H). Discussion

Unlike the majority of previous studies that evaluated YC as a functional feed additive in broiler diets (Hoque et al., 2021; Zhao et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2022), our research was specifically designed to address the pressing industry challenge of feed-food competition by evaluating YC as a partial substitute for conventional corn and SBM. In this study, replacing corn and SBM with 2% or 3% YC resulted in only a marginal numerical decrease in the calculated crude protein (CP) and metabolizable energy (ME) during the same period compared to the basal diet (Table 1). Given that the variations in ME and CP were marginal, it is unlikely that they were the primary drivers of the observed performance improvements. The improved performance, enhanced intestinal morphology, and modulated cecal microbiota suggest that the benefits were predominantly attributable to the YC bioactivity. Therefore, the novelty of this research does not lie in discovering a completely new biological mechanism of YC, but rather in providing robust experimental-scale evidence (900 broilers, 15 replicates per treatment) that YC can effectively serve as an alternative feed ingredient to partially replace corn and SBM. This suggests a viable strategy for alleviating the feed resource crisis in commercial poultry production.

The current study shows that 3% YC replacing corn and SBM in diet improved the growth performance of broilers, as reflected by higher ADG and ADFI, as well as lower F/G value during the various experimental periods. The increased feed intake may be partially attributed to the improved palatability of the diet and the presence of yeast-derived nutrients which are known to stimulate appetite (Zhao et al., 2022). Concurrently, the improved feed efficiency is likely associated with the YC promotes intestinal villus development and optimizes the absorptive capacity of the gut (Kyoung et al., 2023). Thus, YC promotes broiler growth might involve a dual mechanism of stimulating feed intake and enhancing nutrient conversion efficiency.

Antioxidant enzymes play an essential role in protecting cells from oxidative damage by scavenging excessive reactive oxygen species (Wang et al., 2019; Yang et al., 2026). YC contains abundant bioactive components, such as β-glucans and mannans, whose supplementation reduces oxidative stress and promotes growth in broilers (Wang et al., 2021; Li et al., 2025b). Consistent with these findings, our study also revealed that replacing corn and SBM with YC in the diet enhanced the antioxidant capacity of broilers, as evidenced by increased serum activities of SOD and GPX. Moreover, serum biochemistry parameters have been generally recognized as biomarkers for overall health status of animals (Öner et al., 2008). Our study found that dietary YC supplementation had no negative effect on the activities of ALT, AST, and ALP, as well as concentrations of TP, ALB, TBIL, GLU, TG, TC, CREA, and BUN in serum. No adverse changes were detected in the measured serum biochemical indices and organ indices under the conditions of this study, suggesting that YC may serve as a potential strategy to reduce dietary corn and SBM inclusion without compromising broiler health.

Intestinal health plays a central role in determining nutrient utilization efficiency and overall growth performance in broilers (Li et al., 2026). Its morphological structure and barrier function directly determine nutrient utilization efficiency and overall health status (Alagbe et al., 2026). 3% YC replacing corn and SBM significantly increased jejunal VH and the VH/CD ratio in this experiment. Similarly, YC supplementation increased VH and VH/CD in the jejunum of geese and laying hens (Zhang et al., 2023; Qiu et al., 2024). Consistent with previous findings in geese and laying hens, YC improved intestinal development across poultry species. These results support that YC could improve the intestinal morphology, thereby increasing the effective surface area available for nutrient absorption. Furthermore, improvements in intestinal morphology are often accompanied by modulation of epithelial barrier integrity (Yang et al., 2025). Tight junction proteins regulate paracellular permeability and maintain mucosal barrier homeostasis (Horowitz et al., 2023; Sandra et al., 2024). In this study, YC significantly upregulated the protein level of ZO-1 in the jejunum.The upregulation of tight junction proteins restricts the trans-epithelial translocation of harmful luminal antigens, subsequently reducing inflammatory stimuli (Haas et al., 2022; Kuo et al., 2022). This is further supported by our finding that the protein level of the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10 was increased in the jejunum (Jacob and Pescatore, 2017; Kim et al., 2022). Reduced inflammatory stimulation may lower intestinal energy expenditure for immune responses, allowing more nutrients to be utilized for growth. Moreover, YC treatment upregulated the mRNA expression of host defense peptides (LEAP-2 and Cathelicidin-3), suggesting that YC treatment could enhance intestinal defense against pathogens in broilers. Taken together, these results further suggest that YC replacing corn and SBM may enhance broiler intestinal health status by promoting intestinal barrier integrity and immune defense, both of which are vital for their overall well-being.

Cecal microbiota is crucial for intestinal health by promoting gut development, enhancing immunity, and defending against pathogens in broiler chickens (Yadav and Jha 2019). The improvement of intestinal health of broilers by YC treatment is related to the promotion of beneficial bacteria in cecum to produce SCFAs (Hou et al., 2025; Mei et al., 2025). SCFAs, particularly butyric acid, are key microbial metabolites that contribute to intestinal epithelial integrity and immune homeostasis (Cong et al; 2022). In this study, 16S rRNA sequencing revealed that YC markedly altered the cecal microbial composition, specifically enriching Butyricicoccus and Kineothrix, which are well-recognized butyric acid-producing genera (Liu et al., 2024; Ncho et al., 2025; Zhao et al., 2025). To investigate the metabolic consequences of these microbial shifts, we further evaluated cecal SCFA concentrations. As expected, YC significantly increased the cecal butyric acid concentration in broilers. This metabolic change is strongly corroborated by our in vitro fermentation results, where YC tended to increase butyric acid concentrations at 96 h. Butyric acid is known to enhance the expression of epithelial tight junction proteins and exert potent anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties (Liu et al., 2021; de-Cara et al., 2023). Therefore, the enriched butyric acid-producing bacteria and the subsequent accumulation of butyric acid may contribute to the observed enhancement of the intestinal barrier function and the reduction of inflammatory burden.

Conclusion

In summary, dietary 3% YC replacing corn and SBM could improve growth performance, antioxidant capacity, and intestinal health status of broilers. These beneficial effects were associated with modulation of the cecal microbiota, characterized by enrichment butyric acid-producing bacteria and increase butyric acid production. Overall, these findings suggest that the dietary 3% YC replacing corn and SBM might be a feasible nutritional strategy in broiler production.
