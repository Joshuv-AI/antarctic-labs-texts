Allograft to bone-tunnel integration in a canine anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction model: a comparison study of allograft preparation methods
Authors: Xiaoke Shang, Jianzhong Hu, Jin Qu, Peng Wen, Jian Li, Qi Li, Jun Zheng
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The procedure of anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) allograft preparation can be divided into fresh-frozen method (FF-allograft) or freeze-dried method (FD-allograft). This study aims to biomechanically and histologically compare the graft to bone tunnel integration between the two allografts. In-vitro results indicated that FF-allograft and FD-allograft showed excellent biocompatibility and biomechanics, while FD-allograft showed a denser collagen fiber arrangement than FF-allograft and autograft. Then, in-vivo preformation of the FF-allograft, FD-allograft, and autograft on bone tunnel integration was evaluated via a canine ACL reconstruction model. In-vivo results indicated that no signs of infection or osteoarthritis were shown in the femur-graft-tibia complexes, but more vascularity and synovitis formed around the implanted FF-allograft. Micro-computed tomography showed that peri-graft bone in the FF-allograft group was significantly increased and remodeled compared with the FD-allograft group; Histologically, the FF-allograft group exhibited similar graft-bone tunnel healing to the FD-allograft group. Tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase (TRAP) staining showed significantly more osteoclasts presented in the FD-allograft group compared to the FF-allograft group. Meanwhile, a significantly higher failure load was shown in the FF-allograft group when compared with the FD-allograft group (P < 0.05). In conclusion, the FF-allograft integrated more firmly into the bone tunnel than the FD-allograft when used in ACL reconstruction.

Introduction

Anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) injury is a common sports injury in knee [1]. Arthroscopy-assisted reconstruction is the most frequently performed surgery for patients with ACL injury. During ACL reconstruction, autograft is routinely used [2], while allografts are gaining increasing popularity in recent years. In the United States, about 20% of patients with ACL injury are reconstructed with allografts [3]. Allografts used for ACL reconstruction have many notable advantages, including elimination of donor-site morbidity, a shorter operation time, no graft size limitation, and greater suitability for ACL revision surgery [4–7]. However, the most concerning problems associated with allograft application are the storage procedures, which directly determine the immunogenicity and the mechanical properties of allografts.

To solve these dilemmas, researchers and clinical surgeons performed various studies to improve the procedures of allograft preparation. The procedures of allograft preparation include sterilization and storage. Firstly, for the aim of minimizing the risk of transmitting blood-borne diseases, like hepatitis, bacterial or fungal infection, and HIV, aggressive donor, and graft screening together with graft sterilization were commonly performed. These strategies have made transmission of blood-borne diseases rarely reported [8, 9]. Meanwhile, the methods for allograft sterilization include ethylene oxide and gamma irradiation. Among these sterilization techniques, the toxicity of ethylene oxide or its by-products limited its application, thus gamma irradiation is employed more widely [10–12]. Considering that ‘‘high-dose gamma radiation’’ compromises graft structural integrity and even results in high failure rates after ACL reconstruction, lower gamma irradiation doses, typically from 10 to 18 kGy [5, 10], were normally employed to sterilize the allograft tendons. The methods for the screening of donor blood-borne diseases and graft sterilization have been well-optimized. Therefore, the improvement of preparing allografts should mainly focus on the storage methods.

In the past decades, several studies used freeze-dried tendons or fascia lata as ACL substitutes and acquired satisfactory biomechanical and histological results [13, 14]. The use of fresh-frozen rather than freeze-dried tissue has also been reported in the literature. S P Arnoczky et al. published their results of fresh-frozen patellar tendon allograft for ACL reconstructions in the dog model with good histological results [15]. K Shino et al. published favorable clinical results in 31 patients who had undergone an ACL reconstruction with fresh-frozen patellar tendon allograft [16]. Thus, depending on the storage method, the ACL allografts can be classified into fresh-frozen (FF) allografts or freeze-dried (FD) allografts [12, 17, 18]. In the FF procedure, allografts normally experience gradual freezing and then deep freezing, which is the most frequent and popular method for allograft storage [19]. As for the FD procedure, the graft is procured under sterile conditions and then lyophilized for storage [20]. Before transplantation, rehydration of freeze-dried allografts requires a minimum of 30 min. Both storage methods have their advantages and disadvantages. In comparison with the FF procedure, the FD procedure is more beneficial for the decrease of allogenic substances, thus reducing the immunogenicity of allografts [19, 21–23]. This reduced immunogenicity of allograft may be good for the bony integration of allograft after ACL reconstruction. In addition, the allograft prepared by FD procedure can be easily stored at room temperature for 3–5 years, while FF-allografts need cryopreservation, which is expensive and inconvenient. On the other hand, the FD procedure has some disadvantages, such as decreasing the stiffness of the allograft [24], which may impact the mechanical performance of the allograft in vivo. Even though both FF-allograft and FD-allograft have been used as a graft in ACL reconstruction, there are no controlled studies that investigated the effectiveness of FF-allograft versus FD-allograft as an ACL substitute.

This study aimed to biomechanically and histologically compare graft integration in the bone tunnel at postoperative month 2 or 6 using 3 types of ACL substitutes: FF-allograft, FD-allograft, and autograft (superficial digital flexor tendon) as controls. The hypothesis of this study was that FF-allograft may be a better ACL substitute compared to the FD-allograft.

Methods

Ethical statement

The model of ACL reconstruction was performed in skeletally mature, male beagles. Animals were procured from a licensed dog farm. The protocols described below were reviewed and approved by the animal experiment ethics committee of our institution (2020-KY-125).

Allograft preparation and in-vitro evaluation

Under sterile conditions, the superficial digital flexor tendons (SDFTs) were harvested from the hind legs of adult beagles and used for other experiments. These experiments did not involve the musculoskeletal system. After the harvested SDFTs were rinsed with phosphate buffer solution (PBS) containing antimycotic antibiotic, part of them were sterilized with gamma irradiation, and then put into sterilized 50-mL centrifuge tubes, and then slowly frozen using controlled rate freezing of 1 to 3 °C per minute and maintained at a temperature of − 80 °C [3]. After being stored at − 80 °C for 8 weeks, the FF-allografts were prepared. The other SDFTs were freeze-dried for 7 days to a water content of less than 1%, then stored at room temperature for 8 weeks [25]. After being sterilized with gamma irradiation, the FD-allografts were prepared.

According to literature [3, 26], the FF-allograft, the FD-allograft, or the fresh SDFTs (n = 4) were cut into 2 × 2 mm2 pieces and rehydrated with ﻿a complete culture medium (CAXMX-90011, Cyagen Biosciences, CHN) containing antimycotic antibiotic for 2 h. After that, the pieces of FF-allograft, FD-allograft, or fresh SDFTs were individually placed in the well center of 96-well cell-culturing plates (Corning), and then canine BMSCs (CAXMX-01001, Cyagen Biosciences, CHN) were seeded into the 96-well cell-culturing plates with a density of 2.0 × 103 cells/well.

At 1, 3, 5, 7, 9, and 11 days after coculturing, BMSCs proliferation on the FF-allograft, FD-allograft, or SDFT was quantified by Cell Counting Kit-8 assay (70-CCK8100, MultiSciences, China) (n = 4). In addition, to evaluate the cytotoxicity of FF-allograft, FD-allograft, and SDFTs on BMSCs, cell viability was evaluated with a Live/Dead Assay kit (40747ES76, Yeasen, Shanghai, China) at post-seeding 3-day (n = 4). The green-stained (live cells) and red-stained cells (dead cells) were imaged by a fluorescence microscope with an excitation wavelength of 488/594 nm. Cell viability was calculated as follows: (live cells/total cells) × 100%.

After the FF-allograft, FD-allograft, and fresh SDFTs were lyophilized in a vacuum freeze dryer (FD8-5 T; SIM), their surface microstructure was imaged with scanning electron microscopy (SEM; S-3400 N, Hitachi). Before the mechanical test, the FF-allografts were thawed completely at room temperature, while the FD-allograft was rehydrated in physiologic saline. The fresh SDFTs (n = 6) harvested from the hind legs of adult beagles were immediately used for the mechanical test, and the acquired mechanical parameters were used as a control. The two free ends of the graft were sutured with a 3–0 silk braided non-absorbable suture (Ethicon) to enhance the frictional force of the tendon (Fig. 1A). Mechanical testing of the graft was performed using an Instron tension system. The sutured ends of the graft wrapped tightly with sandpaper were securely mounted onto a metallic clamp (Fig. 1B). After the graft was preloaded with 20 N for 5 min and then loaded with a speed of 5 mm/s to failure. In-vivo study design

A total of 66 male beagle dogs weighing approximately 9.7 to 12.3 kg were included in the study and randomly divided into autograft group, FF-allograft group, and FD-allograft group (n = 22/group). The dogs in the autograft group underwent ACL reconstruction in the right hind leg with autologous SDFT harvested from the right hind leg, the dogs in the FF-allograft group underwent ACL reconstruction in the right hind leg with FF-allograft, while the dogs in the FD-allograft group underwent ACL reconstruction in right hind leg with FD-allograft. The dogs were sacrificed at postoperative month 2 or 6 to harvest the right knees, and then carefully dissect femur-graft-tibia complexes. Five knees from each group at each time point were used for micro-CT and then histological evaluations, while the other six specimens were evaluated via biomechanical testing (Fig. 1C). The number of dogs required for micro-CT, histological evaluations, and biomechanical testing was determined by power analysis.

ACL reconstruction model

ACL reconstruction surgeries were performed with the dog under anesthesia induced by intravenous injections with pentobarbital (25 mg/kg). After anesthesia, the dog was fixed on the operation table in the supine position allowing the knee joint free motion. The right hind leg of the beagle was shaved and then sterilized three times with 2.5% iodophors. As shown in Fig. 1D, in the dogs allocated to the autograft group, SDFT was harvested through a longitudinal incision made on the posteromedial side of the distal right hind legs, and then prepared with the Krakow stitch technique on each end of the graft using 3–0 silk braided non-absorbable suture. A 6-cm-long medial parapatellar incision was made to dislocate the patella laterally, and the ACL was excised from its tibial and femoral insertions. Two bone tunnels of 4 mm in diameter were respectively drilled through the femur and tibia, which enter the knee joint at the anatomic origin and the insertion of the native ACL. The length of each tunnel was measured. With the help of a 0.8-mm-diameter guide wire, the prepared autograft was pulled through the bone tunnels of the femur and tibia. The graft was fixed within the femur tunnel by an interference screw (3.5 mm × 10 mm). Then, with the knee flexed to 45 °C, the graft was stretched and fixed within the tibia tunnel by another interference screw. After the joint was irrigated, the retinacular incision of the patella as well as the wound were closed in layers with 3–0 silk braided non-absorbable sutures. In the dogs allocated to the FF-allograft group or the FD-allograft group, the ACL in the right hind leg was reconstructed with the FF-allograft or the FD-allograft, respectively. Postoperatively, the dogs were allowed to move freely in the cages. Intramuscular prophylactic antibiotic injections (penicillin G) were given every day for 3 days after the operation, while wound cleaning was performed once a day for 14 days. All of the surgical procedures were performed by 2 authors (Q.L. and J.Z.) who were experienced in clinical and experimental ACL reconstructions.

Specimen harvest and gross evaluation

The knee appearance of dogs was noted after the operation, and then the knee cavity was opened to assess synovial fluid at months 2 and 6 after surgery. In addition, the implanted graft was assessed for hypertrophy, vascularity, scar tissue formation, and signs of inflammatory reactions.

Evaluation of peri-graft bone remodeling

Micro-CT: Five selected specimens used for micro-CT were fixed in 4% neutral buffered formalin and then washed with PBS solution. Micro-CT (Quantum GX micro-CT, PerkinElmer) was used to scan those specimens. The scanning region covered the whole length of the bone tunnel in the tibia and femur. The regions of interest (ROIs) of the peri-graft bone were selected from the bone tunnel closing to the intra-articular part, which is a cylinder with a diameter of 4.0 mm and a height of 150 sections in a tibial tunnel or femoral tunnel subregion (Fig. 2A). Bone morphological parameters of these ROIs were measured by one author (X.S.), who was blinded to the animal groups, including bone volume to total volume ratio (BV/TV), trabecular thickness (Tb.Th), and trabecular number (Tb.N). Tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase (TRAP) staining: After micro-CT evaluation, these specimens were decalcified with 10% ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid for 3 to 4 months and then embedded in paraffin. The samples were sectioned parallel to the longitudinal axis of the bone tunnel with 5-μm thickness, and then deparaffinized and rehydrated. Subsequently, the sections were incubated in an acetate-tartaric acid buffer for 20 min at room temperature. After incubation, 0.5 mg/mL of naphthol AS-MX phosphate (N5000, Sigma) and 1.1 mg/ml of fast red TR salt (F6760, Sigma) were added to the buffer solution. The slides were incubated for 90 min at room temperature to develop bright red TRAP localization. The TRAP-positive cells indicated the osteoclasts in the peri-graft bone. The TRAP-stained slices were captured to count the number of positive cells.

Evaluation of graft-bone integration by histological assessment

The above-mentioned sections were stained with Hematoxylin & eosin (H&E), Safranin O/fast green (SO/FG), and Masson's trichrome (MT), and then captured using a light microscope (Eclipse CI, Nikon, Japan). Evaluation of tensile properties of femur-graft-tibia complexes

Before tensile testing, the specimen was thawed overnight at 4 °C. During testing, the specimens were kept moist with saline. All soft tissue except for the ACL graft was dissected, leaving only the graft secured to the femur (proximally) and tibia (distally). With the help of custom-designed grips, each specimen was mounted on an Instron tension system (Instron 5967; USA). The knee was positioned at an approximate flexion angle of 30°, in which the graft was parallel to the testing axis. After the femur-graft-tibia complex was preloaded three times with 5N, it was loaded to failure at a rate of 5 mm/min. The load-deformation curve was recorded, from which the Failure load (N) and stiffness (N/mm) of these specimens were acquired. The failure mode was noted as midsubstance failure, femoral pullout, or tibial pullout.

Statistical analysis

The sample size of animal experiments is based on previous studies and power analysis. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS Version 25.0 software (IBM), and statistical significance was set at P < 0.05. All quantitative data were expressed as mean and standard deviation and differences between the 3 groups were evaluated by one-way analysis of variance and with post hoc test. The histological scores among the AT, FF, and FD groups were performed using the Kruskal–Wallis test. The Fisher exact test was used to test for differences in the failure mode of the femur-graft-tibia complex during biomechanical testing.

Microstructure of FF-Allograft and FD-Allograft

Using SEM, the collagen fibers of the FD-allograft or the FF-allograft were arranged in a regular and parallel manner similar to the fresh tendon, but the collagen fibers of the FD-allograft presented a denser microstructure compared with the FF-allograft or the fresh SDFT (Fig. 3C).

Tensile properties of FF-Allograft and FD-Allograft

As shown in Fig. 3D, the failure load (N) of the FF-allografts, the FD-allografts, or fresh SDFTs were 1344.19 ± 234.57, 1293.85 ± 188.50, 1409.52 ± 210.32, respectively. The stiffness (N/mm) of the FF-allograft, the FD-allograft, or fresh SDFTs was 326.59 ± 29.92, 319.70 ± 29.63, or 341.38 ± 27.18, respectively. There was no significant difference in failure load among the three groups, while the FD-allograft showed a lower value in failure load and stiffness compared with the FF-allograft and the fresh SDFT.

Gross observations

The knee passive motion range of these dogs was not influenced after surgery. No animals died during or after surgery. No postoperative infection happened to these animals. The surgical wounds of these animals diversely healed from postoperative days 14 to 16. The knee joint of part dogs swelled obviously and filled with bloody synovial fluid on postoperative day 5, and gradually subsided on postoperative day 10. At postoperative mouth 2 (Fig. 4B), after dissecting the femur-graft-tibia complex, we found that moderate synovial fluid located in the knee cavity presented pale yellow, and the articular cartilage showed no sign of degradation. Graft-bone integration

A distinct graft-bone integration was evident in the three groups, which exhibited time-dependent improvements in peri-graft bone formation, fibrocartilage regeneration, and collagen fiber reconnection from 2 to 6 months.

In postoperative month 2 (Fig. 8A): Compared with the FF-allograft group and FD-allograft group, the peri-graft bone in the autograft group exhibited intense bone formation characterized by thicker trabecular bone. Since metachromasia in safranin O/fast green staining showed fibrocartilage regeneration, the metachromasia evaluation results indicated that the fibrocartilage area and the proteoglycan deposition at the graft-bone healing site in the autograft group were more obvious than those of the FF-allograft group and FD-allograft group. As for the collagen fiber reconnection, the implanted FF-allograft and FD-allograft near the bone tunnel were filled with large newly formed bloods and substantial fibroblasts, while few newly formed bloods and fibroblasts could be found in the implanted autograft graft. More specially, the newly formed bloods and fibroblasts in the FD-allograft group are more obvious than these of the FF-allograft group. These results indicated that the reconnection of collagen fibers in the autograft group was the best, the FF-allograft group was the second, and the FD-allograft group was the worst. Discussion

In the past, allografts have been mainly used in revision ACL surgery. In recent years, the use of allograft has risen exponentially in primary ACL reconstruction with the attraction of absent donor site morbidity, reduced surgical time, and reliable graft size. Structurally, allografts available for primary ACL reconstruction can be classified into bone-tendon-bone allografts and soft-tissue allografts. In this study, the Achilles tendon from the canine was used to prepare an allograft, indicating our study belongs to an experimental study of a soft-tissue allograft. Using a canine model, our results indicated that the integration of implanted FF-allograft to the bone tunnel was significantly improved when compared with the FD-allograft, but still did not reach the integration quality of autograft. This integration difference was characterized by delayed peri-graft bone formation, immature fibrocartilage regeneration, and limited collagen fiber reconnection. This study provides a meaningful reference for the storage of soft-tissue allografts used in the clinic.

The origin of the graft, storage, and sterilization methods are variable, thus various types of allografts can be provided for ACL reconstruction. In this study, the Achilles tendon was selected as the origin tissue for preparing allograft. Currently, some clinical reports have illustrated the usage of Achilles tendon-derived allograft for primary ACL reconstruction [29, 30]. As for allograft preservation, it can be stored using an FF method or FD method. The allografts stored using the FD method contain fewer allogenic substances compared with the allografts stored using the FF method, thus showing lower immunogenic, which may result in limited immunologic responses as an ACL substitute [19, 21–23]. This lower immunogenic property of allografts may be beneficial for graft integration to the bone tunnel. Additionally, the FD method was more convenient for storage, because these FD-allografts can be kept at room temperature, while FF-allografts should be stored in a deep-freezing environment, which needs an expensive refrigeration facility. Strangely, the FD-allografts did not apply widely in clinical ACL reconstruction. The reason for this phenomenon may be that some researchers believe that FD treatment will impact the mechanical properties of allografts [12, 24]. In our study, the mechanical tests indeed determined that rehydrated FD-allograft has a little lower value of failure load and stiffness than FF-allograft and autograft, but no significant differences were found among them.

As for the sterilization method of allograft, the tendon tissue can be sterilized with ethylene-oxide or gamma radiation [18, 20, 23]. Both ethylene-oxide and gamma radiation have some inevitable disadvantages. The allograft sterilized using ethylene-oxide has a high risk of causing synovitis, while gamma radiation led to the decrease of biomechanical strength of the allograft [18, 23, 31]. In consideration of the inevitable residues of ethylene oxide, gamma irradiation was more widely used [10, 11]. To minimize the negative influence of gamma radiation on graft structure, these tendon-derived allografts received sterilization with low-dose gamma radiation. Previous studies have reported that irradiated allografts have a higher potential to fail than non-irradiated allografts [5, 32]. Interestingly, no graft failure was observed during our study. Gamma radiation decreases the strength of tissue by inducing the formation of free radicals in water-containing parts. Logically speaking, FD-allografts have less water load, and their mechanical strength may not be influenced by gamma radiation as much as FF-allografts. On the contrary, our mechanical test showed that the FD-allografts showed inferior mechanical properties to the FF-allograft, indicating that the freeze-drying process has a greater impact on the mechanical properties of tendon tissue than the fresh-frozen process.

Previous studies indicated that the transplanted allograft showed slower incorporation and remodeling than autograft [33, 34]. Immunogenicity properties of transplanted allografts are supposed to be the main factors affecting allograft incorporation and remodeling by stimulating host inflammatory response around grafts [12]. FF-allografts are the most immunogenic, and FD-allografts are less immunogenic [35, 36]. Based on this literature, owing to their minimal inflammatory potential, FD-allografts are more likely to be better grafts than FF-allografts for ACL reconstruction. As determined by the TRAP staining, the FF-allograft group showed significantly more osteoclasts than the FD-allograft group and autograft at postoperative month 2, indicating that FF-allografts are the most immunogenic. Interestingly, our in-vivo results indicated that the FD-allograft did not show better graft-bone incorporation than the FD-allograft at postoperative month 2. The reasons for this contradictory phenomenon may be that the better graft-bone incorporation in the FD-allograft group induced by the low immunogenicity properties might have been partly offset by the dense collagen fibers caused by the freeze-drying process. According to the literature, both autograft and allograft after ACL reconstruction undergo a process of peripheral synovialization, central ischemic necrosis, revascularization, cellular proliferation, graft repopulation, and eventual ligament engraftment [37–39]. The dense collagen fibers of FD-allograft were not convenient for endogenous cell migration, thus delaying the ligament engraftment of the transplanted graft. As time goes by, the regenerated ligament will replace both autograft and allograft. Thus, at month 6, the FD-allograft showed similar integration to the bony tunnel with the FF-allograft, as characterized by well-developed peri-graft bone, and mature fibrocartilage regeneration, thus displaying similar biomechanical properties among the three groups.

Nevertheless, there are several limitations to our study. Firstly, the ACL reconstruction of humans is performed under an arthroscope that minimally invasive surgery, while the surgical protocol of canine ACL reconstruction in this study is invasive. In addition, canine knee biomechanics were different from humans, thus it is not suitable to directly translate our results into human ACL reconstruction. Secondly, this study determined that the FF-allograft incorporated with the bone tunnel more firmly than the FD-allograft, as shown by the better peri-graft bone formation and mature fibrocartilage regeneration in the early phase of post-operation. However, the mechanism of why FF-allograft is better than FD-allograft for graft-bone integration is not clear and should be further investigated. Thirdly, before mechanical testing and in-vivo application, the FF-allografts were stored at -80 degrees for 8 weeks, while the FD-allografts were sterilized using gamma radiation, freeze-dried, and then stored at room temperature for 8 weeks. In clinical applications, the storage time of these allografts varies. Next step, a study should be designed to investigate the influence of the storage time on the in-vivo performance of the allografts. Despite these limitations, our conclusion was rigidly confirmed by micro-CT, histological, and biomechanical evaluations, and the findings of this study may offer some meaningful foundations for guiding the preparation of soft-tissue allografts.

Conclusion

In summary, our results showed that the osteointegration of FF-allograft is superior in the canine ACL reconstruction model compared to the FD-allograft at the early phase of post-operation. This study provides a meaningful reference for the preparation of soft-tissue allografts in the clinic.
