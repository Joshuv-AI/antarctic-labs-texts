Mechanical behaviour evaluation of scrab tire based rubberized lightweight and normal structural concrete and response at elevated temperatures
Authors: Mohammed Ibrahim Majeed Al-Hadeethi, Ali Serdar Ecemiş
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn recent years, the exponential increase in the world population had led to sharp increase in discarded scrap tires, creating a serious ecological problem. In addition, environmental and social damage that occur during the extraction of aggregate is one of the controversial issues of land use. The use of rubber manufactured from scrap tires in concrete production instead of natural aggregate has been considered as one of the solutions. This study investigates the comparative effects of the use of scrap tire rubber on the compressive - flexural strength, toughness of structural both normal weight and lightweight concrete produced with pumice; a lightweight aggregate on which limited research has been conducted; and also the response at elevated temperatures comparatively. The target compressive strength of 28-days cube concrete samples was determined as 30 MPa. 6 different series of rubberized lightweight and normal concrete mixtures were produced by replacing the natural aggregate. 2 types of rubber (crumb and fiber form, as fine and coarse size) with 3 different mixes and with 4 ratios of rubber content, 5%, 10%, 15% and 20% by weight, were used in the study. After the target compressive strength is achieved 72 cubes (15 × 15 × 15 cm) and 72 beams were tested. For the effect of temperature 10 × 10 × 10 cm cube specimens exposed to high temperatures (200 °C, 400 °C and 600 °C) were also tested. As a result with the use of scrap tire rubber, both bending and compressive strengths of concrete decreased. In addition to this with the increase of scrap tire rubber content, the decrease in compressive and flexural strength of rubberized lightweight concrete was less than that of rubberized normal concrete in all series. At elevated temperatures, the results show that the weight loss for all scrap tire ratios was very low (NC 0.45%, LC 1.2%) at temperatures up to 200 degrees for both normal concrete and lightweight concrete. In normal concrete, mass loss increased with increasing temperature and scrap tire ratio at 400 and 600 degrees, reaching an average of 3.00% and 9.00% respectively. Especially at 600 degrees mass loss tends to increase more rapidly with increasing temperature and tire ratio. In lightweight concrete, mass loss is more sensitive to temperature and tire ratio. The average mass loss was 7.21% at 400 degrees and 12.6% at 600 degrees, respectively. Considering the RCSI results, an increase or a slight change in strength was observed between 22 and 200 degrees, more in normal concrete. At 400 and 600 degrees, there was a decrease in strength in both concrete types and for all series. These values were determined as 0.49 ~ 0.74, 0.76 ~ 0.85 at 400 degrees and 0.31 ~ 0.50 and 0.50 ~ 0.57 at 600 degrees for lightweight concrete and normal concrete, respectively. The shape of scrap tire was effective on the strength reduction and this was the highest in the series using coarse and fine crumb scrap tire rubber. In addition, the compressive strength formula for both types of concrete depending on the waste tire ratio and temperature change was determined by multiple linear regression analysis and proposed.Keywords: Scrap tire rubber, Rubberized lightweight concrete, Rubberized normal weight concrete, Pumice, Flexural strength, Compressive strength, Structural concrete, Toughness, Elevated temperature, Mass lossSubject terms: Engineering, Environmental sciences, Materials science

Introduction

Global warming is one of the biggest and most hazardous issues that humanity has been dealing with recently. It is a result of environmental pollution brought on by human activity in many different sectors, and it is getting worse as the world’s population grows. One of those sectors is construction, which necessitates the consumption of natural raw resources, and another sector is transportation, which necessitates the creation of tires and their eventual disposal. An estimated 1.55 billion tires are consumed annually in the European Union and United States alone1. Since scrap rubber takes over 50 years to decompose, the disposal methods of burning or landfilling tires pose considerable risks to human health, ecosystems, and economic stability through contamination of air, water, and soil environments2–4.

Regular storage of scrap tires.

The most common building material utilized in the construction sector is concrete. Reducing the production cost of concrete, increasing its strength and durability, and making concrete structures more environmentally friendly is an important task of this production5. One of the key ingredients in concrete production is aggregate, which is often referred to as filler considering the volume and weight of concrete. Strength, gradation, absorption, moisture content, specific gravity, form, and texture are some of the physical and mechanical attributes of aggregates that determines concrete’s strength and durability1. The extraction of aggregates is one of the controversial ways to utilize land. This debate is mostly brought on by the environmental and social harm that aggregate extraction causes6. In addition to this, cement’s carbon footprint and production costs also cause environmental and financial problems. Recycling is the best solution to deal with this problem as it reduces CO2 emissions and waste accumulation while also reducing the environmental impact of aggregate and cement production and use7. Giving high strength, ductility and strain-controlling qualities, used tires can be replaced by some traditional concrete components5. Tire wastes are able to be used in concrete and mortar as binders or to replace fine and coarse aggregates. Any engineering cement composite with crumb rubber in it minimizes CO2 emissions, making it more environmentally friendly thus the use of scrap tires in concrete production methods has been one of the research areas in the field of civil engineering in recent years8,9.

The results of previous studies have shown that recycled rubber from scrap tires can have detrimental effects on the mechanical properties of concrete, including natural aggregate replacement, compressive strength, flexural strength, splitting tensile strength, and elastic modulus. However, it is suggested for compressive strength that when the replacement of scrap rubber with natural aggregate was low (<%20), the lowering in excessive strength can be avoided. The decrease in flexural strength, tensile strength, and elastic modulus of rubberized concrete were lower than the decrease in compressive strength10. Additionally, the use of recycled tire rubber in place of natural aggregate improved dynamic properties like impact resistance, ductility, and fatigue resistance as well as toughness and density when compared to conventional concrete5. The addition of used tires to concrete transformed the concrete’s fracture mode from brittle to ductile11. Also previous studies revealed that the size, proportions, and surface texture of the rubber particles have a significant impact on the compressive strength of rubberized concrete mixtures12,13. The compressive strength of concrete mixtures incorporating tire chips and crumb rubber aggregates is lower than that of normal Portland cement concrete12. It has been stated that there is a decrease of approximately 85% in compressive strength when coarse crumb rubber chips are used instead of coarse aggregate. However, when fine rubber was used in place of all of the fine aggregate, a 65% loss in compressive strength was seen. The compressive strength of concrete is decreased more by the addition of coarse rubber chips than by the addition of fine crumb rubber13,14. Rubberized concrete often has a lower compressive strength than normal concrete5,15. Concrete containing rubber components of between 5% and 50% of natural aggregates with diameters ranging from 0.075 mm to 6 mm showed a 4%–70% reduction in strength16. The percentage of size, shape, mechanical qualities, and rubber aggregate substitution levels were cited as the causes of the general decline in rubberized concrete strength17. The compressive strength of rubberized concrete is reduced proportionately to the size of the rubber particles5. Compacted concrete that was replaced with 100% piece rubber exhibited a loss in compressive strength of up to 90%18. As the proportion of rubber content in the aggregate increases, the compressive strength steadily declines. Comparing reinforced concrete with a maximum 20% rubber content to ordinary concrete with no rubber, the compressive strength is reduced by 50%. Lack of adhesion between cement and rubber particles is one of the reasons for the decrease in compressive strength. When a material is subjected to compressive stress, these defects represent mechanical weak points that result in cracks. The majority of studies on the use of waste rubber in concrete production show that adding 5% crumb rubber to cement has no negative effects and does not weaken the concrete1.

Rubber based aggregates have also effect on tensile strength of concrete. Tensile stresses form along the surface of the rubber particle and the bound cement paste, causes rubberized concrete to crack prematurely. Such stresses are caused by the softness of rubber particles in concrete. In rubberized concrete, a very wide and porous weak interfacial transition zone is observed because of the hydrophobic structure of the rubber aggregate repels the cement paste5.

As the amount of rubber by weight increases, the flexural strength of the rubberized concrete mixture diminishes gradually. Flexural strength of rubberized concrete has a similar tendency to decline as compressive and splitting tensile strength19. When rubber is used instead of 20% sand in concrete, the flexural tensile strength is reduced by 25–27%18. Rubberized concrete’s early-stage flexural strength is not much lower than normal concrete having up to 30% rubber and a low water-cement ratio. Unlike normal concrete, rubberized concrete does not deteriorate suddenly under bending effects, which is a positive contribution made by rubber. Thus, under bending strain, rubberized concrete does not display brittle fracture and endures some deformation but does not rupture completely. This phenomenon is observed with two forms of rubber reinforcement: chipped rubber and milled rubber. The researchers observed that the chipped rubber could be easily removed from the mixture after the concrete samples were broken because of the chipped rubber’s weak bonding with the cement during the aggregation phase. This is regarded as a key factor in the gradual loss of compressive and flexural strength. In comparison to a drop in compressive strength, the weak link between the rubber and cement paste generates a steeper decrease in flexural strength5.

Although most studies have focused on the mechanical properties of Rubberised Concrete (RuC) at room temperature, many structures are exposed to fire hazards during their service life. High temperature affects the mechanical properties of RuC. Gupta et al.20 investigated the effect of 6 different temperature levels and 3 different heat exposure times on concrete produced using waste fiber rubber and found that there was a decrease in strength and an increase in mass loss with increasing temperature. Gulemine et al.21 investigated the physical and mechanical properties of recycled crumb rubber mortar at 4 different temperature levels. As a result of the research, it was revealed that the mixtures containing more than 3% rubber were more sensitive to heat, the residual effects of heat up to 300 degrees were less, while the effects of heat above 300 degrees were stronger. Another study revealed that the addition of rubber pieces reduces mass loss and increases relative compressive strength. When a high proportion of rubber was added, a more porous structure was formed due to the melting of rubber under high temperature and a decrease in strength occurred22,23. However, adding rubber improved some properties of concrete such as fatigue life, impact resistance24,25, and it was also reported that rubber pieces improved the fire resistance of normal concrete26. Zaleska et al.27 reported that the use of scrap tire-rubber aggregate causes a decrease in the unit weight of the prepared rubberized concrete, a worsening of mechanical parameters and a significant decrease in its thermal conductivity.

Although there are numerous studies investigating the effects of the use of recycled scrap tire rubber as a substitute for fine and/or coarse aggregate in normal weight concrete’s properties in the fresh and hardened stages, there is no or very few research on the effect of using scrap tire rubber in lightweight concretes produced using pumice type natural lightweight aggregates. Turkey is one of the most important pumice producer countries as shown in Table 1. In addition, there is no previous study comparing the effect of using scrap tires on structural normal weight concrete and structural lightweight concrete both ambient and elevated temperatures. In this study, the effect of using scrap rubber on the compressive strength, flexural strength, and flexural toughness of structural normal concrete and structural lightweight concrete produced using pumice-type natural lightweight aggregate, which is not found in the literature, was investigated comparatively at ambient and high temperatures. Also compressive strength, mass loss and surface spalling of both RuC types are investigated at elevated temperatures (200–400–600 °C). Therefore, the target compressive strength of 28-days-old samples was determined as 30 MPa (cube). 6 different series of rubberized lightweight and normal concrete mixtures were produced by changing the natural aggregate of the structural lightweight and normal concrete. 2 types of rubber (crumb and fiber form, with two sizes as fine and coarse size) with 3 different mixes and with 4 ratios, 5%, 10%, 15% and 20% by weight, were used in the study. Totally (78 + 117) cubes and 78 beams were tested at the Necmettin Erbakan University laboratory.

In the research, the method of replacing natural aggregates with rubber aggregates by weight was followed. Coarse and fine natural aggregate was replaced by 5%, 10%, 15% and 20% weight of rubber aggregate in the shape of crumbs and/or fibers (and each shape has two volumes as fine and coarse). 3 series of rubberized lightweight concrete mix RuLC and 3 series of rubberized normal concrete mix RuNC were prepared. The mixtures prepared are given below, respectively:

1 st Serie RuLC (1 S-RuLC) and 1 st Serie RuNC (1 S-RuNC): Four mixes were made for each of these two series by replacing 5%, 10%, 15% and 20% of the coarse natural aggregate and the fine natural aggregate with coarse crumb rubber aggregate and fine crumb rubber aggregate respectively.

1 st Serie RuLC (1 S-RuLC) and 1 st Serie RuNC (1 S-RuNC): Four mixes were made for each of these two series by replacing 5%, 10%, 15% and 20% of the coarse natural aggregate and the fine natural aggregate with coarse crumb rubber aggregate and fine crumb rubber aggregate respectively.

2nd Serie RuLC (2 S-RuLC) and 2nd Serie RuNC (2 S-RuNC): Four mixes were made for each of these two series by replacing 5%, 10%, 15%, and 20% of the coarse natural aggregate and the fine natural aggregate with coarse fiber rubber aggregate and fine fiber rubber aggregate respectively.

2nd Serie RuLC (2 S-RuLC) and 2nd Serie RuNC (2 S-RuNC): Four mixes were made for each of these two series by replacing 5%, 10%, 15%, and 20% of the coarse natural aggregate and the fine natural aggregate with coarse fiber rubber aggregate and fine fiber rubber aggregate respectively.

3rd Serie RuLC (3 S-RuLC) and 3rd Serie RuNC (3 S-RuNC): Four mixtures were made for each of these two series by replacing 5%, 10%, 15%, and 20% of the coarse natural aggregate and the fine natural aggregate with crumb and fiber shaped coarse rubber aggregate and crumb and fiber shaped fine rubber aggregate with equal amounts of each shape 2.5%, 5%, 7.5% and 10% respectively.

3rd Serie RuLC (3 S-RuLC) and 3rd Serie RuNC (3 S-RuNC): Four mixtures were made for each of these two series by replacing 5%, 10%, 15%, and 20% of the coarse natural aggregate and the fine natural aggregate with crumb and fiber shaped coarse rubber aggregate and crumb and fiber shaped fine rubber aggregate with equal amounts of each shape 2.5%, 5%, 7.5% and 10% respectively.

A total of 26 mixtures which consist of 2 reference concrete mixtures (ReLC and ReNC) and 24 rubberized concrete mixtures with 6 series (3 RuLC series and 3 RuNC series) were prepared for testing. Three cubes of 15 × 15 × 15 cm for compressive strength, 10 × 10 × 10 cubes for thermal tests and three beams of 40 × 10 × 10 cm for flexural strength tests for each mixture were produced in accordance with TS EN 12390-242. These samples were cured by immersion in a treatment pool of water at constant temperature 20 °C. The required age of the samples to run the tests is 28 days, the samples were removed from the treatment pool 24 h before the test date to allow the samples to dry at room temperature37. Thermal test procedure

The heat loading rate was determined as 5 °C/min. Similar heat loading procedures have been used by many researchers in the literature20,21,23,42,43. Target temperatures were determined as 3 stages, 200–400 and 600 °C. Similarly, the target temperatures were determined as a result of literature research21,23,43,44. At the beginning of the heating process, the ambient temperature is 16 ± 2 degrees. After reaching the target temperature, the test specimens were kept at constant temperature for 60 min to ensure that the heat was fully dissipated inside. After the 60 min were completed, the oven door was opened and the specimens were allowed to cool naturally until the ambient temperature dropped to 22 °C ± 2. Experimental results and discussions

The decrease in compressive strength of both rubberized concrete types with the increase in rubber content is due to two reasons:

Properties of rubber aggregate: The compressive strength of concrete depends on the physical and mechanical properties of the materials used during concrete production. Rubber has low mechanical resistance and density compared to natural aggregates. Also, rubber, which is a flexible and elastic material, has the ability to be compressed, which causes the rubber granules to act as voids in the concrete structure. In addition, due to the nonpolar nature of the rubber particles, the rubber particles become hydrophobic, which causes the water to move away from the rubber particles and the air to approach, creating air pores. With the increase of rubber particles, these air pores also increase. Poor adhesion between the surfaces of cement paste and rubber granules is another reason. All of these reasons weaken the concrete structure and thus the compressive strength of both types of rubber concrete decreases with the increase in rubber content.

Toughness index

Toughness quantifies a material’s ability to absorb energy before fracture. Rubber particles can dissipate energy through microcracking, effectively absorbing the applied load and delaying the onset of major cracks. This energy dissipation mechanism is attributed to the elevated bending deformation and fiber-like nature of rubber. As cracks initiate, rubber particles act as bridging agents, effectively restraining crack opening and limiting their propagation. This “toughening mechanism” leads to increased energy absorption capacity and improved resistance to fracture47–49. Traditional concrete typically exhibits a brittle failure mode, characterized by a sudden and catastrophic failure after reaching its ultimate load. However, the incorporation of rubber can shift traditional concrete’s brittle failure behavior towards a more ductile response, resulting in a gradual decrease in load-carrying capacity after the initial crack formation. This ductile post-cracking behavior is attributed to the rubber’s ability to deform and absorb energy, preventing sudden failure and allowing the structure to withstand further loading even after cracking occurs49,50. The high plastic energy capacity and superior ductility of crumb rubber aggregate particles enable them to function as small, dispersed shock absorbers in the mixture, resulting in a tougher and more ductile mixture with a higher impact energy absorption capacity51.

The toughness values for RuLC and RuNC concrete types were obtained by calculating the area under the load-displacement curves. For this purpose, the area under the maximum load in the load - displacement curves and the area under the curves at the moment when the maximum load drops to 85% of the maximum load (post-peak region) were calculated. Figure 28 shows the calculated areas under the curves.

Evaluation of toughness index.

Toughness index was calculated by Eq. (1)52–54 and the results are presented in Fig. 29.

In concretes produced using scrap tires, the toughness values increased in both RuLC and RuNC compared to ReLC and ReNC concrete. The maximum increase was obtained when the scrap tire ratio was 15% and above. In RuLC, the maximum increase was obtained at 1 S-20% with a value of 2.20. In normal concrete, the increase in toughness values was more significant in all series with the increase in rubber ratio in general. Especially in the 3rd serie, the increase is almost linear. No such significant change was observed in lightweight concrete. Maximum increase was obtained at 2 S-15%, 2 S-20% and 3 S-20%. In both concrete types and series, the effect of 5% tire ratio on the toughness values is negligible.

Studies have shown that the inclusion of rubber in concrete increases its toughness and rupture stress by increasing the plastic energy demand in the post-elastic range10,55. However, it has been stated that as the rubber concentration approaches 25% of the total aggregate volume, the toughness index will reach its maximum level, and after this value, the toughness index will decrease due to a drop in strength52. Also SEM (Scanning Electron Microscopy) studies have demonstrated the ability of rubber particles to bridge cracks in the cement matrix. This bridging effect, observed even in fully formed cracks, contributes to better control of crack width and better stress redistribution. While rubber can cause microcracks due to stress concentrations around the particles, it also contributes to improving energy dissipation mechanisms within the composite by acting as a crack arresting agent49.

The utilization of fiber-formed tires has been demonstrated to be more efficacious than other tire forms, due to the increased length of the fibers, which facilitates the efficient transfer of the applied load through the interface friction force56,57. It has been suggested that replacing high-strength, high-stiff aggregate with a material possessing high elasticity, such as rubber, results in a decrease in compressive strength but an increase in toughness58. Since lightweight aggregate has lower strength and stiffness than normal aggregate, the increase in toughness was also less.

Conclusions and recommendations

In this study, the effect of using scrap tire rubber in different shapes and proportions in concrete production on the compressive strength, flexural strength, toughness index and the effect of high temperatures on these strengths, mass loss, surface changes of both structural lightweight and normal (C30 cube) concrete were comparatively investigated. The results obtained in the research can be summarized as follows:

In both types of RuLC and RuNC, there was a decrease in compressive and flexural strength with the increase of rubber content, but the amount of decrease in RuNC is greater.

In this research, the mass method was used to replace natural aggregates with rubber aggregates. There is a big difference in specific gravity between normal weight natural aggregate, light weight natural aggregate and rubber aggregate. The use of the mass method in replacing aggregates with this difference in specific gravity results in an increased difference in volume of replaced rubber aggregates with respect to the volume of replaced natural aggregates in both concrete types, where this difference is greater in rubberized normal weight concrete than in rubberized lightweight concrete. For this reason, it is recommended to conduct another study in which the aggregates are changed using the volumetric method to avoid the previously mentioned difference in volume. By this way, the use of mass and volumetric substitution methods of aggregate can be compared in terms of the properties of both concrete types.

Further research examining additional properties of rubberized structural lightweight concrete produced with pumice aggregate, such as durability, segregation, dynamic properties, water absorption, bond strength and shear behavior, could bring innovative approaches to applications in the construction field. If the results provide adequate performance, the use of such concrete in building components such as ceilings can reduce dead loads in buildings.
