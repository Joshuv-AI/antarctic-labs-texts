Learning DNF through Generalized Fourier Representations
Mohsen Heidari, Roni Khardon

Abstract
The Fourier representation for the uniform distribution over the Boolean cube has found numerous applications in algorithms and complexity analysis. Notably, in learning theory, learnability of Disjunctive Normal Form (DNF) under uniform as well as product distributions has been established through such representations. This paper makes five main contributions. First, it introduces a generalized Fourier expansion that can be used with any distribution D D through the representation of the distribution as a Bayesian network (BN). Second, it shows that the main algorithmic tools for learning with the Fourier representation, that use membership queries to approximate functions by recovering their heavy Fourier coefficients, can be used with slight modifications with the generalized expansion. These results hold for any distribution. Third, it analyzes the L 1 L_{1} spectral norm of conjunctions under the new expansion, showing that it is bounded for a class of distributions which can be represented by difference bounded tree BN, where a parent node in the BN representation can change the conditional expectation of a child node by at most α < 0.5 \alpha<0.5 . Lower bounds are presented to show that such constraints are necessary. The fourth contribution uses these results to show the learnability of DNF with membership queries under difference bounded tree BN. The final contribution is to develop an algorithm for learning difference-bounded tree BN distributions, thus extending the DNF learnability result to cases where the distribution is not known in advance.

The key to our analysis is incorporating the representation of the distribution

Bayesian Network.

A BN is specified by a directed acyclic graph and a set of conditional probability tables (or functions).
Let
G=(V,E)
be the graph where nodes correspond to the individual random variables.
Then the joint probability distribution can be written as a product of individual probability distributions of each node conditioned on its parent variables:

\displaystyle P(X_{1},\cdots,X_{n})=\prod_{v\in V}P(X_{v}|X_{\operatorname{pa}(v)}),

where
\operatorname{pa}(v)
is the set of the parents of node
v
in
G
, and where in this paper the variables are binary, i.e.,
X_{i}\in\{0,1\}
.

For some of the results, we will need to restrict the class of distributions
D
. For any node
v
, let
\mu_{v,x_{\operatorname{pa}(v)}}
and
\sigma_{v,x_{\operatorname{pa}(v)}}
denote the conditional expectation and standard deviation of
X_{v}
given its parents’ realization

, respectively. Note that the values
\{\mu_{v,x_{\operatorname{pa}(v)}}\}
are exactly the parameters that specify the BN representation of
D
.

Definition 2
.

A distribution
D
is

bounded for

if for all
v\in V
and any assignments

we have
c\leq P(X_{v}=x|x_{\operatorname{pa}(v)}=x^{\prime})\leq 1-c
.
Moreover,

is
\alpha
-difference-bounded if for all
v
and for any two assignments

to
\operatorname{pa}(v)
,
we have

\absolutevalue{\mu_{v, x_{\pa(v)}} - \mu_{v, y_{\pa(v)}}}\leq\alpha
and
\absolutevalue{\sigma_{v, x_{\pa(v)}} - \sigma_{v, y_{\pa(v)}}}\leq\alpha
.

Based on this definition, a distribution is a

, if it can be expressed as a BN where each node has at most a single parent and the conditional probability tables in the specification are
c
-bounded and
\alpha
-difference bounded. The difference boundedness implies a limitation on the influence of the parent nodes on the first two moments of a child node.

Our results generalize previous works that established the learnability of functions using the Fourier representation under uniform and bounded product distributions

;

;

;

;

Fourier Expansion on the Boolean Cube with Product Distributions.

We briefly discuss prior constructions of the Fourier basis; see
ODonnell2014
;
Wolf2008
for a review.
In the following,
given any distribution
D
, define the induced inner product between any pair of functions
f,g:\{0,1\}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}

by

\langle f,g\rangle=\mathbb{E}_{D}[f(\mathbf{X})g(\mathbf{X})]
.
The expansion relies on a set of basis functions defined as
\psi_{i}(\mathbf{x})=(-1)^{x_{i}}=1-2x_{i}

where
x_{i}\in\{0,1\}
and
i\in[n]
. The orthonormal Fourier basis for the uniform Boolean Fourier is defined as
\psi_{S}(\mathbf{x})=\prod_{i\in S}\psi_{x_{i}}(\mathbf{x})
, for all subsets
S\subseteq[n]
and
\mathbf{x}\in\{0,1\}^{n}
.
Any function
f
on the Boolean cube admits the following decomposition

f(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{\mathcal{S}\subseteq[n]}\hat{f}_{\mathcal{S}}~\psi_{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbf{x}),\quad\forall\mathbf{x}\in\{0,1\}^{n},

where
\hat{f}_{\mathcal{S}}
are called the Fourier coefficients of
f
and are calculated as

\hat{f}_{\mathcal{S}}=\langle f,\psi_{\mathcal{S}}\rangle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim\mbox{Uniform}}[f(\mathbf{X})\psi_{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbf{X})]=\frac{1}{2^{n}}\sum_{\mathbf{x}}f(\mathbf{x})\psi_{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbf{x}).

This expansion relies on the restriction that the input variables are uniformly distributed over the Boolean cube.
The orthonormal basis for product distributions, generalizing this, is given by
\psi_{i}(\mathbf{x})=\frac{\mu_{i}-x_{i}}{\sigma_{i}}
, where
\mu_{i}
and
\sigma_{i}
are the expectation and the standard deviation of
X_{i}
.
Therefore, for product distributions the Fourier coefficients of
f
are calculated as
\hat{f}_{\mathcal{S}}=\mathbb{E}_{D}[f(\mathbf{X})\psi_{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbf{X})]
. This formulation reduces to the one for the uniform distribution, where
\mu_{i}=0.5
and
\sigma_{i}=\sqrt{\mu_{i}(1-\mu_{i})}=0.5
.
In our development below for the general case, we use the negation of these basis functions in order to simplify the presentation. However, our basis can be seen as a direct extension of the product case.

The above expansion for non-uniform product distributions is not technically a Fourier transform as
\psi_{\mathcal{S}}
are not group characters and therefore some properties are not satisfied (see related discussion by
ODonnell2014
).
However, certain nice properties of the Fourier transform are satisfied such as the Plancherel formula. Therefore, we use the term
Fourier expansion
to distinguish it from the Fourier transform.

There are other forms of orthogonal decomposition including the Hoeffding-Sobel decomposition
(
HoeffdingDecomp
;
Sobol1993
;
Chastaing2012
)
and its generalization
(
Chastaing2012
)
. However, such decompositions are basis-free, making their learning implications unclear. Moreover, extensions of the Fourier expansion to non-product distributions have been studied
HeidariICML2021
;
Heidari2022
. When the distribution is not known, such works rely on an empirical orthogonalization process that is applied on the above Fourier expansion. Although these Fourier expansions are suitable for machine learning problems such as feature selection, they may not be applicable to prove upper bounds on learning of Boolean functions, especially conjunctions. The reason is that the basis functions are unknown prior to the given samples. In our work, we generalize the Fourier expansion to non-product distributions described by
BN
.

4
Extending KM to Arbitrary Distributions

In this section, we show that
the KM algorithm
KM1993
can be generalized to recover the large coefficients of a function
f
,
for any
distribution
D
, given a BN representation for it and the corresponding basis.
The algorithm is based on a recursive procedure that identifies whether a subset of coefficients includes any large coefficients and in this way identifies all the large coefficients.
The main new development in our work is given in the following lemma, which shows how the construction of the main tool, and its proof of correctness can be adapted to the new basis.

To introduce the generalization, we extend the notation to specify sets using binary strings. We represent a set
\mathcal{S}\subseteq[n]
with a binary vector
\gamma\in\{0,1\}^{n}
where
\gamma_{i}=1
if
i\in\mathcal{S}
, and
\gamma_{i}=0
otherwise. With this notation strings can refer to sets in a 1-1 manner in a natural way.
Moreover, the Fourier expansion is written as

\displaystyle f(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{\gamma\in\{0,1\}^{n}}\hat{f}_{\gamma}\phi_{\gamma}(\mathbf{x}).

Let
\alpha\in\{0,1\}^{k}
and
\beta\in\{0,1\}^{n-k}
. We use
\beta\alpha
to denote the concatenation of the two strings which is of length
n
. Moreover, to avoid confusion, our strings, serving as subscripts or arguments to our function
f
, basis functions or coefficients, will always be of length
n
. To achieve this we pad a string
\alpha\in\{0,1\}^{k}
on the left with
\bar{0}=0^{n-k}
to get
\overline{0\alpha}=\bar{0}\alpha
where we use the overline to emphasize the concatenation. Similarly, we pad
\beta\in\{0,1\}^{n-k}
on the right with
\bar{0}=0^{k}
to get
\overline{\beta 0}=\beta\bar{0}
. When using this notation the length of
\bar{0}
should be clear from the context.

Let

4.1
Extended KM Algorithm

The KM algorithm uses the facts above to find the heavy Fourier coefficients of
f
under the uniform distribution
KM1993
.
The Extended KM Algorithm
(see Algorithm
1
)
is a slight elaboration that controls the ordering of variables in the construction of
\alpha

and modifies the form of sampling in estimating
\mathbb{E}_{U}[g_{\alpha}^{2}(U)]
.

For a distribution
D
described by a BN, Lemma
3
implies that if we can evaluate

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{U}[g_{\alpha}^{2}(U)]=\mathbb{E}_{U}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{Y}_{1}|U,\mathbf{Y}_{2}|U}[f(U\mathbf{Y}_{1})f(U\mathbf{Y}_{2})\phi_{\overline{0\alpha}}({U\mathbf{Y}_{1}})\phi_{\overline{0\alpha}}({U\mathbf{Y}_{2}})],

we can recursively find all the large coefficients of
f
and that the number of such coefficients is not too large.
In addition, once the coefficient set is chosen, the algorithm has to estimate the coefficients which are themselves expectation
\hat{f}_{\mathcal{S}}=\mathbb{E}_{D}[f(\mathbf{X})\phi_{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbf{X})]
.
In both of these expressions, the basis functions are bounded by
(1/\sigma)^{|\mathcal{S}|}

where
\sigma=\sqrt{\mu(1-\mu)}
is the minimal standard deviation.
However, since this is exponential in
|\mathcal{S}|
, we need a more refined argument than in the case of the uniform distribution.
In previous work on product distributions,
Jackson1997
relied on the fact that
|\mathcal{S}|
is logarithmic to obtain a polynomial bound.

Kalai2009
developed an alternative form for the estimates that still allows for the use of Hoeffding’s bound even for large
|\mathcal{S}|
, but their construction relies on independence and is not easily applicable for the general case.

We therefore use a different route, using Chebyshev’s bound for the estimation.
Toward that we introduce three random variables
Z_{1},Z_{2},Z_{3}
so that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[Z_{1}]

\displaystyle=\hat{f}_{\mathcal{S}}=\mathbb{E}_{D}[f(\mathbf{X})\phi_{S}(\mathbf{X})],

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[Z_{2}|u]

\displaystyle=g_{\alpha}(u)=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{Y}|u}[f(u\mathbf{Y})\phi_{\overline{0\alpha}}({u\mathbf{Y}})],

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[Z_{3}|u]

\displaystyle=g^{2}_{\alpha}(u)=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{Y}_{1}|u,\mathbf{Y}_{2}|u}[f(u\mathbf{Y}_{1})f(u\mathbf{Y}_{2})\phi_{\overline{0\alpha}}({u\mathbf{Y}_{1}})\phi_{\overline{0\alpha}}({u\mathbf{Y}_{2}})],

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[Z_{3}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{U}[g^{2}_{\alpha}(U)]=\mathbb{E}_{U,\mathbf{Y}_{1}|u,\mathbf{Y}_{2}|u}[f(U\mathbf{Y}_{1})f(U\mathbf{Y}_{2})\phi_{\overline{0\alpha}}({U\mathbf{Y}_{1}})\phi_{\overline{0\alpha}}({U\mathbf{Y}_{2}})],

where we have identified the arguments of the expectations with
Z_{1},Z_{2}|u,Z_{3}|u,Z_{3}
respectively.
We have the following observation.

Observation 1
.

(1)
\mathsf{var}(Z_{1})\leq 1
,
(2)
\mathsf{var}(Z_{3})\leq\frac{5}{4}
.

Proof.

Recall that
|f(\cdot)|\leq 1
.
We have
\mathsf{var}(Z_{1})\leq\mathbb{E}[Z_{1}^{2}]\leq\mathbb{E}[\phi_{S}(X)^{2}]=1
which proves (1).
We next show that
\mathsf{var}(Z_{2}|u)\leq 1
.
This follows from

\mathsf{var}(Z_{2}|u)\leq\mathbb{E}[Z_{2}^{2}|u]\leq\mathbb{E}[\phi_{\overline{0\alpha}}({u\mathbf{Y}})^{2}]=1

where the last equality follows because, conditioned on
u
, the function
\phi_{\overline{0\alpha}}({u\mathbf{Y}})
is a normalized basis function in the reduced
k
-dimensional space.
To see this observe that conditioning on a root variable in a BN produces another BN with one less variable and the same conditional distributions (and basis functions) for other variables and since
u
is an ancestor set, we can condition on all its variables in this manner.
The bound for
Z_{2}
also implies
g^{2}_{\alpha}(u)=\mathbb{E}[Z_{2}|u]^{2}\leq\mathbb{E}[Z_{2}^{2}|u]\leq 1
, where we used the Jensen’s inequality.

Next, using a similar reasoning we
show that
\mathsf{var}(Z_{3}|u)\leq 1
.
This follows from

\mathsf{var}(Z_{3}|u)\leq\mathbb{E}[Z_{3}^{2}|u]\leq

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{Y}_{1}|u}[\phi_{\overline{0\alpha}}({u\mathbf{Y}_{1}})^{2}]

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{Y}_{2}|u}[\phi_{\overline{0\alpha}}({u\mathbf{Y}_{2}})^{2}]=1
.

Finally, using the law of total variance we get:

\mathsf{var}(Z_{3})=\mathbb{E}_{U}[\mathsf{var}(Z_{3}|U)]+\mathsf{var}_{U}(\mathbb{E}[Z_{3}|U])\leq 1+\mathsf{var}_{U}(g^{2}_{\alpha}(U))\leq\frac{5}{4},

where in the last step we used
0\leq g^{2}_{\alpha}(u)\leq 1
and Popoviciu’s inequality to bound the variance of
g^{2}_{\alpha}(U)
.
∎

The estimation procedure samples
m_{1}
values for
U
, and conditioned on each
u^{i}
samples a pair of values,
\mathbf{Y}^{i}_{1},\mathbf{Y}^{i}_{2}
, independently.
We then calculate
G_{\alpha}
as follows:

\displaystyle Z^{i}_{3}

\displaystyle=f(u^{i}\mathbf{Y}^{i}_{1})f(u^{i}\mathbf{Y}^{i}_{2})\phi_{\overline{0\alpha}}({u^{i}\mathbf{Y}^{i}_{1}})\phi_{\overline{0\alpha}}({u^{i}\mathbf{Y}^{i}_{2}}),

\displaystyle G_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m_{1}}\sum_{i}Z_{3}^{i}.

(6)

Note that
this differs from prior work where sampling is done in two stages, first estimating
Z_{3}|u
and then estimating
G_{\alpha}
and where Hoeffding’s bound is applicable. Since we use Chebyshev’s bound in the analysis the direct approach yields a tighter bound.

What is crucial for our case is that this only requires forward sampling in the BN:
u
is sampled sequentially from the roots of the BN and
Y_{1},Y_{2}
are sampled conditional on
u
. That is, the process can be performed in so called ancestral sampling
KollerFriedman2009

where variables are sampled in their ordering from the BN and

p(X_{i}|X_{\operatorname{pa}(i)})
.
Since all variables are binary,
sampling can be done in polynomial time in the size of the CPT and hence polynomial in the representation size of the BN.
Hence the algorithm is the same as the original KM algorithm, but it specifically constrains the sampling order over variables using the BN in this process. We have therefore argued that:

Proposition 1
.

The sampling required by the extended KM algorithm can be done in time polynomial in the size of the BN specification (number of nodes and size of CPTs).

With this in place the analysis follows similarly to the original one, where sample bounds are inverse polynomial rather than logarithmic in
1/\delta
.
First, since
\mathsf{var}(Z_{3})\leq\tfrac{5}{4}

by Chebyshev inequality with probability at least
1-\delta

P(|G_{\alpha}-\mathbb{E}_{U}[g_{\alpha}^{2}(U)]|>\frac{\theta^{2}}{4})\leq\tfrac{20}{m_{1}\theta^{4}}

Lemma 4

For

with probability at least

Therefore, with probability at least
1-\delta

.
On the other hand if
\mathbb{E}_{U}[g_{\alpha}^{2}(U)]<\frac{1}{4}\theta^{2}
then
G_{\alpha}<\theta^{2}/2
.
As in
KM1993

the algorithm can then recurse if

. With that, the algorithm outputs
all
\alpha

and no

Therefore, the number of

Next note that, using Chebyshev’s inequality,
with

We have so far established that each individual estimate of

In total we have

Scaling

The run time has an additional poly

factor to generate samples and

We have therefore established the following theorem.

(cf. Theorem 3.10 of

5

Z_{i-1}=X_{i-1}
and the expectation equals

\displaystyle A_{i}(0)A_{i-1}+\mathbb{E}_{X_{i-1}}\quantity[X_{i-1}^2| X_{i-2}](A_{i}(1)-A_{i}(0))

\displaystyle=A_{i}(0)A_{i-1}+A_{i-1}(A_{i}(1)-A_{i}(0))

\displaystyle=A_{i}(1)A_{i-1}.

Case 2.2:
Suppose
i-1\in\mathcal{T}_{0}\backslash\mathcal{S}
. Then
Z_{i-1}=1-X_{i-1}
and
the expectation equals

\displaystyle A_{i}(0)A_{i-1}+\mathbb{E}_{X_{i-1}}\quantity[X_{i-1}(1-X_{i-1})| X_{i-2}](A_{i}(1)-A_{i}(0))

\displaystyle=A_{i}(0)A_{i-1}.

Case 3:
Suppose
i-1\in\mathcal{S}\backslash\mathcal{T}
. Then
Z_{i-1}=\Phi_{i-1}
which zeros out the first expectation.
Using Lemma
5
the second term equals

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{X_{i-1}}\quantity[\Phi_{i-1}X_{i-1}| X_{i-2}](A_{i}(1)-A_{i}(0))

\displaystyle=\sigma_{i-1,X_{i-2}}(A_{i}(1)-A_{i}(0))=A_{i-1}(A_{i}(1)-A_{i}(0)).

Case 4.1:
Suppose
i-1\in\mathcal{S}\cap\mathcal{T}_{1}
. Then
Z_{i-1}=\Phi_{i-1}X_{i-1}
and
using
Lemma
5

the expectation equals

\displaystyle A_{i}(0)A_{i-1}+\mathbb{E}_{X_{i-1}}\quantity[\Phi_{i-1}X_{i-1}^2| X_{i-2}](A_{i}(1)-A_{i}(0))

\displaystyle=A_{i}(0)A_{i-1}+A_{i-1}(A_{i}(1)-A_{i}(0))

\displaystyle=A_{i}(1)A_{i-1}.

Case 4.2:
Suppose
i-1\in\mathcal{S}\cap\mathcal{T}_{0}
. Then
Z_{i-1}=\Phi_{i-1}(1-X_{i-1})
and the expectation equals

\displaystyle A_{i}(0)A_{i-1}+\mathbb{E}_{X_{i-1}}\quantity[\Phi_{i-1}(1-X_{i-1})X_{i-1}| X_{i-2}](A_{i}(1)-A_{i}(0))

\displaystyle=A_{i}(0)A_{i-1}.

Putting together all the cases, we have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{X_{i-1}}\quantity[Z_{i-1} A_{i}| X_{i-2}]=\begin{cases}A_{i}(0)+A_{i-1}(A_{i}(1)-A_{i}(0))&if~i-1\notin\mathcal{T}\cup\mathcal{S}\\
A_{i}(1)A_{i-1}&if~i-1\in\mathcal{T}_{1}\backslash\mathcal{S}\\
A_{i}(0)A_{i-1}&if~i-1\in\mathcal{T}_{0}\backslash\mathcal{S}\\
A_{i-1}(A_{i}(1)-A_{i}(0))&if~i-1\in\mathcal{S}\backslash\mathcal{T}\\
A_{i}(1)A_{i-1}&if~i-1\in\mathcal{S}\cap\mathcal{T}_{1}\\
A_{i}(0)A_{i-1}&if~i-1\in\mathcal{S}\cap\mathcal{T}_{0}.\end{cases}

∎

The previous lemma allows us to recurse over all the iterative expectations to compute
\hat{f}_{\mathcal{S}}
.
The additive term
c_{i}
yields hierarchically structured expressions with a constant term and a linear term.
However,
note that
c_{i}
will be canceled if the next expectation
j<i
where
Z_{j}\not=1
is
j\in\mathcal{S}\setminus\mathcal{T}
.
That is
\mathbb{E}[\Phi_{j}(c_{i}+b_{i}A_{i-1})]=\mathbb{E}[\Phi_{j}b_{i}A_{i-1}]

because
\mathbb{E}[\Phi_{j}]=0
.
This is crucial in understanding the structure of
\hat{f}_{\mathcal{S}}
.

5.2
The Fourier Coefficients of Conjunctions

We first present the following example to illustrate the combinatorial structure of the Fourier coefficients.

Example 1
.

Consider a chain with
n=10
variables and the monotone conjunctions
f=X_{2}X_{6}X_{10}
and
g=X_{1}X_{6}X_{10}
. We calculate
{\hat{f}}_{S}
and
{\hat{g}}_{S}
for
S=\{3,6,8\}
.
To facilitate the presentation, for variable
i
we denote
D_{i,\mu}=\mu_{i,1}-\mu_{i,0}
and
D_{i,\sigma}=\sigma_{i,1}-\sigma_{i,0}
.
We implicitly use the facts
\mathbb{E}[X_{i}]=\mu_{i,X_{i-1}}
,
\mathbb{E}[\phi_{i}]=0
,
\mathbb{E}[\phi_{i}X_{i}]=\sigma_{i,X_{i-1}}
,

\mu_{i,0}+D_{i,\mu}=\mu_{i,1}
,
\sigma_{i,0}+D_{i,\sigma}=\sigma_{i,1}
and

(a+bX_{i})X_{i}=(a+b)X_{i}
as derived above.
Starting with
f
we have:

\displaystyle{\hat{f}}_{S}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[X_{2}\phi_{3}\phi_{6}X_{6}\phi_{8}X_{10}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[X_{2}\phi_{3}\phi_{6}X_{6}\phi_{8}(\mu_{10,0}+D_{10,\mu}X_{9})]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[X_{2}\phi_{3}\phi_{6}X_{6}\phi_{8}(\mu_{10,0}+D_{10,\mu}\mu_{9,0}+D_{10,\mu}D_{9,\mu}X_{8})]

\displaystyle=(D_{10,\mu}D_{9,\mu})\mathbb{E}[X_{2}\phi_{3}\phi_{6}X_{6}\phi_{8}X_{8}]

\displaystyle=(D_{10,\mu}D_{9,\mu})\mathbb{E}[X_{2}\phi_{3}\phi_{6}X_{6}(\sigma_{8,0}+D_{8,\sigma}X_{7})]

\displaystyle=(D_{10,\mu}D_{9,\mu})\mathbb{E}[X_{2}\phi_{3}\phi_{6}X_{6}(\sigma_{8,0}+D_{8,\sigma}\mu_{7,0}+D_{8,\sigma}D_{7,\mu}X_{6})]

\displaystyle=(D_{10,\mu}D_{9,\mu})\mathbb{E}[X_{2}\phi_{3}\phi_{6}(\sigma_{8,0}+D_{8,\sigma}\mu_{7,0}+D_{8,\sigma}D_{7,\mu})X_{6}]

\displaystyle=[(D_{10,\mu}D_{9,\mu})(\sigma_{8,0}+D_{8,\sigma}\mu_{7,1})]\mathbb{E}[X_{2}\phi_{3}\phi_{6}X_{6}]

\displaystyle=[(D_{10,\mu}D_{9,\mu})(\sigma_{8,0}+D_{8,\sigma}\mu_{7,1})]\mathbb{E}[X_{2}\phi_{3}(\sigma_{6,0}+D_{6,\sigma}X_{5})]

\displaystyle=[(D_{10,\mu}D_{9,\mu})(\sigma_{8,0}+D_{8,\sigma}\mu_{7,1})]\mathbb{E}[X_{2}\phi_{3}(\sigma_{6,0}+D_{6,\sigma}\mu_{5,0}+D_{6,\sigma}D_{5,\mu}X_{4})]

\displaystyle=([D_{10,\mu}D_{9,\mu})(\sigma_{8,0}+D_{8,\sigma}\mu_{7,1})](D_{6,\sigma}D_{5,\mu})\mathbb{E}[X_{2}\phi_{3}X_{4})]

\displaystyle=[(D_{10,\mu}D_{9,\mu})(\sigma_{8,0}+D_{8,\sigma}\mu_{7,1})](D_{6,\sigma}D_{5,\mu})\mathbb{E}[X_{2}\phi_{3}(\mu_{4,0}+D_{4,\mu}X_{3}))]

\displaystyle=[(D_{10,\mu}D_{9,\mu})(\sigma_{8,0}+D_{8,\sigma}\mu_{7,1})](D_{6,\sigma}D_{5,\mu}D_{4,\mu})\mathbb{E}[X_{2}\phi_{3}X_{3}]

\displaystyle=[(D_{10,\mu}D_{9,\mu})(\sigma_{8,0}+D_{8,\sigma}\mu_{7,1})](D_{6,\sigma}D_{5,\mu}D_{4,\mu})\mathbb{E}[X_{2}(\sigma_{3,0}+D_{3,\sigma}X_{2})]

\displaystyle=[(D_{10,\mu}D_{9,\mu})(\sigma_{8,0}+D_{8,\sigma}\mu_{7,1})][(D_{6,\sigma}D_{5,\mu}D_{4,\mu})(\sigma_{3,1})]\mathbb{E}[X_{2}]

\displaystyle=[(D_{10,\mu}D_{9,\mu})(\sigma_{8,0}+D_{8,\sigma}\mu_{7,1})][(D_{6,\sigma}D_{5,\mu}D_{4,\mu})(\sigma_{3,1})]\mathbb{E}[(\mu_{2,0}+D_{2,\mu}X_{1})]

\displaystyle=[(D_{10,\mu}D_{9,\mu})(\sigma_{8,0}+D_{8,\sigma}\mu_{7,1})][(D_{6,\sigma}D_{5,\mu}D_{4,\mu})(\sigma_{3,1})][()(\mu_{2,0}+D_{2,\mu}\mu_{1})].

In these equations we identified 3 segments in
[\ldots]
where each segment includes a
D
component and a hierarchical component.
The segments
[7-10],[3-6],[1-2]
are split at indices in the conjunction
f
and the split into
D
component corresponds to the (least)
S
index in the corresponding region.
For example, the least
S
index in
[7-10]
is 8 and this segment splits into a
D
portion
[9,10]
and hierarchical portion
[7,8]
.
In this example the segment
[1-2]
does not have a
S
element and correspondingly no
D
component, which we emphasize with the empty parenthesis.

Turning to the function
g
, all the steps up to the expectation over
X_{2}
are the same (replacing
X_{2}
with
X_{1}
in the conjunction). Continuing from there we have

\displaystyle{\hat{g}}_{S}

\displaystyle=[(D_{10,\mu}D_{9,\mu})(\sigma_{8,0}+D_{8,\sigma}\mu_{7,1})](D_{6,\sigma}D_{5,\mu}D_{4,\mu})\mathbb{E}[X_{1}(\sigma_{3,0}+D_{3,\sigma}X_{2})]

\displaystyle=[(D_{10,\mu}D_{9,\mu})(\sigma_{8,0}+D_{8,\sigma}\mu_{7,1})](D_{6,\sigma}D_{5,\mu}D_{4,\mu})\mathbb{E}[X_{1}(\sigma_{3,0}+D_{3,\sigma}\mu_{2,0}+D_{3,\sigma}D_{2,\mu}X_{1})]

\displaystyle=[(D_{10,\mu}D_{9,\mu})(\sigma_{8,0}+D_{8,\sigma}\mu_{7,1})][(D_{6,\sigma}D_{5,\mu}D_{4,\mu})(\sigma_{3,0}+D_{3,\sigma}\mu_{2,1})]\mathbb{E}[X_{1}]

\displaystyle=[(D_{10,\mu}D_{9,\mu})(\sigma_{8,0}+D_{8,\sigma}\mu_{7,1})][(D_{6,\sigma}D_{5,\mu}D_{4,\mu})(\sigma_{3,0}+D_{3,\sigma}\mu_{2,1})][()(\mu_{1})].

We see that the same structure arises when
i=1
is in the conjunction. In this case the last
D
component is always empty and the hierarchical component is just
\mu_{1}
.

Our proofs below show that this type of structure
where each segment is split into a

D
component and a hierarchical component
always arises and therefore leads to a bounded L1 norm.

Consider an auxiliary node
0
at the top of the chain BN that is not connected to any other nodes. Therefore,
\mathbb{E}[X_{1}|X_{0}=0]=\mathbb{E}[X_{1}|X_{0}=1]
; implying that
A_{1}(1)=A_{1}(0)
; hence
A_{1}=A_{1}(0)
.

Definition 6
.

For any
i\in[n]
define
D_{i}:=A_{i}(1)-A_{i}(0)
. Given any pair of subsets
\mathcal{S}
and
\mathcal{T}
, let
a_{1}<\cdots<a_{m}
denote the the ordered elements of
\mathcal{S}\cup\mathcal{T}
. For
i\in[m]
and any
r\in[a_{i-1},a_{i}]
define

\displaystyle D^{\prime}_{r,a_{i}}

\displaystyle=\prod_{r\leq\ell\leq a_{i}}D_{\ell},

\displaystyle A^{\prime}_{r,a_{i}}(0)=\sum_{r\leq\ell\leq a_{i}}D^{\prime}_{(\ell+1,a_{i})}A_{\ell}(0),

(10)

and by convention
D^{\prime}_{r,a_{i}}=1
for any
r>a_{i}
. Let
A^{\prime}_{r,a_{i}}(1)=A^{\prime}_{r,a_{i}}(0)+D^{\prime}_{r,a_{i}}
and define the random variable

A^{\prime}_{r,a_{i}}:=A^{\prime}_{r,a_{i}}(0)+X_{r-1}(A^{\prime}_{r,a_{i}}(1)-A^{\prime}_{r,a_{i}}(0))=A^{\prime}_{r,a_{i}}(0)+X_{r-1}D^{\prime}_{r,a_{i}}
.
Note that when
r>a_{i}
then
A^{\prime}_{r,a_{i}}(0)=0
and
A^{\prime}_{r,a_{i}}(1)=D^{\prime}_{r,a_{i}}=1
.

As a special case of the above definition
D^{\prime}_{a_{i},a_{i}}=D_{a_{i}}
and

A^{\prime}_{a_{i},a_{i}}=A_{a_{i}}
.
We highlight that
A^{\prime},A^{\prime}(1),A^{\prime}(0)
and
D^{\prime}
satisfy the same relations as
A,A(1),A(0)
and
D
.
Moreover, observe that

Lemma 8
.

A^{\prime}_{(r,a_{i})}=A^{\prime}_{(r+1,a_{i})}(0)+A_{r}D^{\prime}_{(r+1,a_{i})}.

Proof.

For the left term note that

A^{\prime}_{r,a_{i}}=A^{\prime}_{r,a_{i}}(0)+X_{r-1}D^{\prime}_{r,a_{i}}=A^{\prime}_{r+1,a_{i}}(0)+A_{r}(0)D^{\prime}_{r+1,a_{i}}+X_{r-1}D^{\prime}_{r,a_{i}}
.
On the other hand expanding
A_{r}
in the right term we have

A_{r}D^{\prime}_{(r+1,a_{i})}=A_{r}(0)D^{\prime}_{r+1,a_{i}}+X_{r-1}D_{r}D^{\prime}_{r+1,a_{i}}=A_{r}(0)D^{\prime}_{r+1,a_{i}}+X_{r-1}D^{\prime}_{r,a_{i}}.

∎

As in the example, we split the chain into segments and analyze the expectation calculating
\hat{f}_{\mathcal{S}}
over segments.
The next two lemmas prepare the ground by analyzing individual segments with boundaries at
T
nodes.

Lemma 9

(Single
\mathcal{S}\cup\mathcal{T}
segment)
.

Consider a chain of nodes
X_{0}\rightarrow\cdots\rightarrow X_{n+1}
that form a segment in the sense that
\mathcal{S},\mathcal{T}\subseteq\{1\}
. Then,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}}\quantity[Z_1 A'_{(n+1,n+1)}(X_n)| X_{0}]=\begin{cases}A^{\prime}_{(1,n+1)}(X_{0})&~if~1\notin\mathcal{T}\cup\mathcal{S}\\
D^{\prime}_{(2,n+1)}A_{1}(X_{0})&~if~1\in\mathcal{S}\backslash\mathcal{T}\\
A^{\prime}_{(2,n+1)}(1)A_{1}(X_{0})&~if~1\in\mathcal{T}_{1}\\
A^{\prime}_{(2,n+1)}(0)A_{1}(X_{0})&~if~1\in\mathcal{T}_{0}.\end{cases}

Proof.

Assume
n>1
as the lemma follows from Lemma
7
when
n=1
. By the law of total expectation, the expectation in the lemma iterates as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{X_{1}}\quantity[Z_1 \EE_{X_2}\qty[ \cdots\EE_{X_{n}}\qty[A'_{(n+1,n+1)} \Big| X_{n-1}]. \cdots\Big| X_1] \Big| X_0].

By an inductive argument we prove that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{X_{2}}\quantity[ \cdots\EE_{X_{n}}\qty[A'_{(n+1,n+1)} \Big| X_{n-1}]. \cdots\Big| X_1]=A^{\prime}_{(2,n+1)}.

(11)

Observe that
A^{\prime}_{(n+1,n+1)}(X_{n})=A_{n+1}(X_{n})
. From Lemma
7
, as
n\notin\mathcal{S}\cup\mathcal{T}
, the innermost expectation equals

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{X_{n}}\quantity[ A'_{(n+1,n+1)}| X_{n-1}]=D_{n+1}A_{n}+A_{n+1}(0)=A^{\prime}_{(n,n+1)},

where the last equality follows from Lemma
8
.

Assuming that
\mathbb{E}_{X_{m},\cdots,X_{n}}\quantity[A'_{(n+1,n+1)} \Big| X_{m-1}]=A^{\prime}_{(m,n+1)}
holds for a fixed
2<m\leq n
, for
m-1
we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{X_{m-1},\cdots,X_{n}}\quantity[A'_{(n+1,n+1)} \Big| X_{m-1}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{X_{m-1}}\quantity[ A'_{(m,n+1)}|X_{m-2}]

\displaystyle=A^{\prime}_{(m,n+1)}(0)+D^{\prime}_{(m,n+1)}\mathbb{E}_{X_{m-1}}\quantity[ X_{m-1}|X_{m-2}]

\displaystyle=A^{\prime}_{(m,n+1)}(0)+D^{\prime}_{(m,n+1)}A_{m-1}=A^{\prime}_{(m-1,n+1)},

where the last step holds because
m-1\not\in\mathcal{S}\cup\mathcal{T}
and
\mathbb{E}[X_{m-1}|X_{m-2}]=\mu_{m-1,X_{m-2}}=A_{m-1}
.
Therefore, with this inductive argument, we proved (
11
).
Hence it remains to compute the outermost expectation. From Lemma
6
and
7
, have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{X_{1}}\quantity[Z_1 A'_{(2,n+1)}\Big| X_0]

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{=}}A^{\prime}_{(2,n+1)}(0)\mathbb{E}_{X_{1}}\quantity[Z_1\big| X_0]+D^{\prime}_{(2,n+1)}\mathbb{E}_{X_{1}}\quantity[Z_1 X_{1}|X_{0}]

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(b)}}{{=}}A^{\prime}_{(2,n+1)}(0)(\mathbf{1}_{\{1\notin\mathcal{T}\cup\mathcal{S}\}}+A_{1}\mathbf{1}_{\{1\in\mathcal{T}\}})+D^{\prime}_{(2,n+1)}A_{1}\mathbf{1}_{\{1\notin\mathcal{T}_{0}\}},

(12)

where (a) follows from Definition
6
.
For (b) note that when
1\in\mathcal{T}_{0}
we have
\mathbb{E}_{X_{1}}\quantity[Z_1 X_{1}|X_{0}]=0
because
X_{1}(1-X_{1})=0
.
We then have
\mathbb{E}_{X_{1}}\quantity[Z_1 X_{1}|X_{0}]=A_{1}\mathbf{1}_{\{1\notin\mathcal{T}_{0}\}}
that holds from the definition of
A_{1}
and

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{X_{1}}\quantity[Z_1 X_{1}|X_{0}]=\mu_{1,X_{0}}\mathbf{1}_{\{1\notin\mathcal{T}\cup\mathcal{S}\}}+\mu_{1,X_{0}}\mathbf{1}_{\{1\in\mathcal{T}_{1}\setminus\mathcal{S}\}}+\sigma_{1,X_{0}}\mathbf{1}_{\{1\in\mathcal{S}\setminus\mathcal{T}\}}+\sigma_{1,X_{0}}\mathbf{1}_{\{1\in\mathcal{S}\cap\mathcal{T}_{1}\}}.

Separating (
12
) for the different memberships of
1
gives the desired expression.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{X_{1}}\quantity[Z_1 A'_{(2,n+1)}\Big| X_0]=\begin{cases}D^{\prime}_{(2,n+1)}A_{1}+A^{\prime}_{(2,n+1)}(0)=A^{\prime}_{(1,n+1)}&if~1\notin\mathcal{T}\cup\mathcal{S}\\
A^{\prime}_{(2,n+1)}(0)A_{1}+D^{\prime}_{(2,n+1)}A_{1}=A^{\prime}_{(2,n+1)}(1)A_{1}&if~1\in\mathcal{T}_{1}\backslash\mathcal{S}\\
A^{\prime}_{(2,n+1)}(0)A_{1}&if~1\in\mathcal{T}_{0}\backslash\mathcal{S}\\
D^{\prime}_{(2,n+1)}A_{1}&if~1\in\mathcal{S}\backslash\mathcal{T}\\
A^{\prime}_{(2,n+1)}(0)A_{1}+D^{\prime}_{(2,n+1)}A_{1}=A^{\prime}_{(2,n+1)}(1)A_{1}&if~1\in\mathcal{S}\cap\mathcal{T}_{1}\\
A^{\prime}_{(2,n+1)}(0)A_{1}&if~1\in\mathcal{S}\cap\mathcal{T}_{0}.\end{cases}

∎

Remark 1
.

The lemma implies an alternative definition of
A^{\prime}
that is insightful. Consider
A^{\prime}_{(r,a_{i})}
as in Definition
6
and an empty segment
[r,a_{i})
, then Lemma
9
(with
1\leftarrow r,n\leftarrow a_{i}-1
) implies that

\displaystyle A^{\prime}_{(r,a_{i})}(X_{r-1})=\mathbb{E}_{X_{r},\ldots,X_{a_{i}-1}}\quantity[A_{a_i} | X_{r-1}].

(13)

Lemma 10

(Single
\mathcal{T}
-segment, multiple
\mathcal{S}
-segments)
.

Consider a chain
X_{0}\rightarrow\cdots\rightarrow X_{n+1}
that potentially has multiple
\mathcal{S}
nodes inside a
\mathcal{T}
segment,
where

\mathcal{T}\subseteq\{1,n\}
and

\mathcal{S}\subseteq[n]
.
If
\mathcal{S}\setminus\mathcal{T}
is not empty, define
s_{\circ}:=\min\mathcal{S}\setminus\mathcal{T}
. Let
\Gamma=\mathbb{E}\quantity[ \prod_{1\leq j\leq n} Z_j A'_{(n+1,n+1)}\Big| X_{0}]
. Then,

\displaystyle\Gamma=\begin{cases}(a):~if~\mathcal{T}=\emptyset~\text{then}&D^{\prime}_{(s_{\circ}+1,n+1)}A^{\prime}_{(1,s_{\circ})}(X_{0}),\\
(b):~if~\mathcal{T}=\{1\}~\text{then}&D^{\prime}_{(s_{\circ}+1,n+1)}A^{\prime}_{(2,s_{\circ})}(y_{1})A_{1}(X_{0}),\\
(c):~if~\mathcal{T}=\{n\}~\text{then}&A_{n+1}(y_{n})D^{\prime}_{(s_{\circ}+1,n)}A^{\prime}_{(1,s_{\circ})}(X_{0}),\\
(d):~if~\mathcal{T}=\{1,n\}~\text{then}&A_{n+1}(y_{n})D^{\prime}_{(s_{\circ}+1,n)}A^{\prime}_{(2,s_{\circ})}(y_{1})A_{1}(X_{0}).\\
\end{cases}

where
y_{n}=\mathbf{1}_{(n\in\mathcal{T}_{1})}
, and
y_{1}=\mathbf{1}_{(1\in\mathcal{T}_{1})}
. If
\mathcal{S}\setminus\mathcal{T}
is empty, then

\displaystyle\Gamma=\begin{cases}(e):~if~\mathcal{T}=\emptyset~\text{then}&A^{\prime}_{(1,n+1)}(X_{0}),\\
(f):~if~\mathcal{T}=\{1\}~\text{then}&A^{\prime}_{(2,n+1)}(y_{1})A_{1}(X_{0}),\\
(g):~if~\mathcal{T}=\{n\}~\text{then}&A_{n+1}(y_{n})A^{\prime}_{(1,n)}(X_{0}),\\
(h):~if~\mathcal{T}=\{1,n\}~\text{then}&A_{n+1}(y_{n})A^{\prime}_{(2,n)}(y_{1})A_{1}(X_{0}).\\
\end{cases}

Proof.

The proof follows by the law of iterative expectations and by breaking the chain into segments and applying Lemma
9
on each segment. Let
\Gamma
be the expectation of interest as in the lemma’s statement. If
\mathcal{S}\setminus\mathcal{T}
is not empty, let
s_{\circ}=a_{1}<\cdots<a_{k}
be the ordered elements of
\mathcal{S}\setminus\mathcal{T}
with
k=|\mathcal{S}\setminus\mathcal{T}|
. We consider four cases depending on
\mathcal{T}
elements.

Case 1 (
\mathcal{T}=\emptyset
):
In this case, the segments are
[1,a_{1})
,
[a_{i},a_{i+1})
for
i=1,\ldots,k-1
, and
[a_{k},n+1)
. Starting from the tail segment
[a_{k},n+1)
, from Lemma
9
, since
a_{k}\in\mathcal{S}\setminus\mathcal{T}
the contribution is

\displaystyle\Gamma_{[a_{k},n+1)}=\mathbb{E}_{X_{a_{k}},\ldots,X_{n}}\quantity[ Z_{a_k} A'_{(n+1,n+1)}(X_n)| X_{a_k-1}]=D^{\prime}_{(a_{k}+1,n+1)}A_{a_{k}}(X_{a_{k}-1}).

Note that
A_{a_{k}}=A^{\prime}_{(a_{k},a_{k})}
, implying that the input to the next segment is an
A^{\prime}
term. From this point for any
\mathcal{S}
-segment
[a_{i},a_{i+1})
, the contribution is

\displaystyle\Gamma_{[a_{i},a_{i+1})}=\mathbb{E}_{X_{a_{i}},\ldots,X_{a_{i+1}-1}}\quantity[ Z_{a_i} A'_{(a_{i+1}, a_{i+1})}| X_{a_i-1}]=D^{\prime}_{(a_{i}+1,a_{i+1})}A^{\prime}_{(a_{i},a_{i})},

(14)

where we used the fact that
A^{\prime}_{(a_{i},a_{i})}=A_{a_{i}}
. Continue this argument until the head segment, if
s_{\circ}=1
, the head segment is
[a_{1},a_{2})
that has been covered and contributing
A^{\prime}_{(1,1)}
. If
s_{\circ}>1
, then the head segment is
[1,s_{\circ})
. The input to this segment is
A^{\prime}_{(a_{1},a_{1})}
. Again from Lemma
9
, as
1\notin\mathcal{S}\cup\mathcal{T}
, the contribution is

\displaystyle\Gamma_{[1,s_{\circ})}=\mathbb{E}_{X_{1},\ldots,X_{s_{\circ}-1}}\quantity[ Z_1 A'_{(s_{\circ}, s_{\circ})}| X_{0}]=A^{\prime}_{(1,s_{\circ})}(X_{0}).

(15)

Notice that the notation
A^{\prime}_{(1,s_{\circ})}
in (
15
) is consistent with the case where
s_{\circ}=1
. Lastly, multiplying all the contributions gives the desired expression:

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\text{case~1}}=D^{\prime}_{(a_{k}+1,n+1)}\prod_{i=1}^{k-1}D^{\prime}_{(a_{i}+1,a_{i+1})}A^{\prime}_{(1,s_{\circ})}(X_{0})=D^{\prime}_{(s_{\circ}+1,n+1)}A^{\prime}_{(1,s_{\circ})}(X_{0}),

(16)

where the last equality holds as products of consecutive
D^{\prime}
terms gives another
D^{\prime}
with the total interval.
Now consider
\mathcal{S}\setminus\mathcal{T}=\emptyset
. Since
\mathcal{T}
is empty then so is
\mathcal{S}
. Hence, we have an empty chain. From Lemma
9
, the contribution is
A^{\prime}_{(1,n+1)}(X_{0})
.

Case 2 (
\mathcal{T}=\{1\}
):

We first assume
\mathcal{S}\setminus\mathcal{T}
is not empty.
There are one
\mathcal{T}
node and potentially multiple
\mathcal{S}
nodes in the chain. The segments are
[1,a_{1})
,
[a_{i},a_{i+1})
for

, and
[a_{k},n+1)

. Otherwise, from Lemma
9
, the contribution is

\displaystyle\Gamma_{[1,s_{\circ})}=\mathbb{E}_{X_{1},\ldots,X_{s_{\circ}-1}}\quantity[ Z_1 A'_{(s_{\circ}, s_{\circ})}| X_{0}]=A^{\prime}_{(2,s_{\circ})}(y_{1})A_{1}(X_{0}),

(17)

where
y_{1}=\mathbf{1}_{(1\in\mathcal{T}_{1})}
. Note that the above equation reduces to

when

and hence it is consistent with the empty segment case. This is because, when
a_{1}=1
, then
s_{\circ}=1
and
A^{\prime}_{(2,s_{\circ})}=1
.
Therefore, multiplying all the contributions gives the desired expression:

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\text{case~2}}=D^{\prime}_{(s_{\circ}+1,n+1)}A^{\prime}_{(2,s_{\circ})}(y_{1})A_{1}(X_{0}).

(18)

If
\mathcal{S}\setminus\mathcal{T}
is empty, then we have a single
\mathcal{T}
segment
[1,n+1]
. From Lemma
9
the contribution is
A^{\prime}_{(2,n+1)}(y_{1})A_{1}(X_{0})
.

Case 3 (
\mathcal{T}=\{n\}

\mathcal{S}\setminus\mathcal{T}

[1,a_{1})
,

, and
[a_{k},n)
and

. Starting from the tail segment, from Lemma

,

\displaystyle\Gamma_{[n,n+1)}=\mathbb{E}_{X_{n}}\quantity[ Z_n A'_{(n+1,n+1)}(X_n)| X_{n-1}]=b_{n+1}A_{n}=A_{n+1}(y_{n})A^{\prime}_{(n,n)}(X_{n-1}),

where
y_{n}=\mathbf{1}_{(n\in\mathcal{T}_{1})}
. The
\mathcal{S}
-segments in between contribute
\Gamma_{[a_{i},a_{i+1})}
as in (
14
). As for the last segment
[1,s_{\circ})
, if
s_{\circ}=1
, this segment is empty and hence produces
A^{\prime}_{(1,1)}(X_{0})
. Otherwise from Lemma
9
, as

, the last contribution is

. As a result, the overall contribution is

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\text{case~3}}=A_{n+1}(y_{n})D^{\prime}_{(s_{\circ}+1,n)}A^{\prime}_{(1,s_{\circ})}(X_{0}).

(19)

If
\mathcal{S}\setminus\mathcal{T}
is empty, then we have two segments: an empty segment

\mathcal{T}
(or

) segment

per Lemma
9
.

Case 4 (
\mathcal{T}=\{1,n\}
):
Assuming that
\mathcal{S}\setminus\mathcal{T}
is not empty, the segments are
[1,a_{1})
,
[a_{i},a_{i+1})
for

, and
[a_{k},n)
and
[n,n+1)
. Starting from the tail segment, from Lemma

,

\displaystyle\Gamma_{[n,n+1)}=\mathbb{E}_{X_{n}}\quantity[ Z_n A'_{(n+1,n+1)}(X_n)| X_{n-1}]=b_{n+1}A_{n}=A_{n+1}(y_{n})A^{\prime}_{(n,n)}(X_{n-1}),

The next segment is a

\displaystyle\Gamma_{[a_{k},n)}=\mathbb{E}_{X_{a_{k}}\ldots,X_{n-1}}\quantity[ Z_{a_k} A'_{(n,n)}(X_{n-1})| X_{a_k-1}]=D^{\prime}_{(a_{k}+1,n)}A^{\prime}_{(a_{k},a_{k})}.

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\text{case~4}}

\displaystyle=A_{n+1}(y_{n})D^{\prime}_{(a_{k}+1,n)}\prod_{i=1}^{k-1}D^{\prime}_{(a_{i}+1,a_{i+1})}A^{\prime}_{(2,s_{\circ})}(y_{1})A_{1}(X_{0})

\displaystyle=A_{n+1}(y_{n})D^{\prime}_{(s_{\circ}+1,n)}A^{\prime}_{(2,s_{\circ})}(y_{1})A_{1}(X_{0}).

If

The lemma implies that

.

Lemma 11

(Fourier coefficients of conjunctions)
.

Consider a conjunction
f
with
\mathcal{T}
being the set of literals. Let
\mathcal{T}_{0}\subset\mathcal{T}
be the negated literals and
\mathcal{T}_{1}=\mathcal{T}\backslash\mathcal{T}_{0}
be the set of literals without negation. Suppose
\mathcal{S}
is any subset of
[n]
. If
\max\mathcal{S}>\max\mathcal{T}
, then
\hat{f}_{\mathcal{S}}=0
; otherwise

\displaystyle\hat{f}_{\mathcal{S}}=\prod_{a_{j}\in\mathcal{T}_{0}\cup\{0\}}A^{\prime}_{(a_{j}+1,a_{j+1})}(0)\prod_{a_{k}\in\mathcal{T}_{1}}A^{\prime}_{(a_{k}+1,a_{k+1})}(1)\prod_{a_{l}\in\mathcal{S}\backslash\mathcal{T}}D^{\prime}_{(a_{l}+1,a_{l+1})},

where
0<a_{1}<a_{2}<\cdots
are the ordered elements of
\mathcal{S}\cup\mathcal{T}\cup\{0\}
.

Proof.

The main idea is to break the chain into the
\mathcal{T}
segments with boundaries at
\mathcal{T}
nodes. As we show the contribution of each segment is independent of the rest of the chain and we can analyze each one separately using Lemma
10
.

We proceed by adding an auxiliary node
X_{0}
as a parent of
X_{1}
, i.e., the chain is,
X_{0}\rightarrow X_{1}\rightarrow\cdots\rightarrow X_{n}
but we assign
p(X_{1}|X_{0})=p(X_{1})
.
This allows us to unify the presentation noting that conditioning on
X_{0}
has no effect on the probability of other variables.
The Fourier coefficient is calculated as the following iterative expectation

\displaystyle\hat{f}_{\mathcal{S}}=\mathbb{E}_{X_{1}}\quantity[Z_1 \cdots\EE_{X_{n}}\qty[Z_{n} \Big| X_{n-1}] \cdots\Big| X_0],

(21)

where
Z_{i}
depends on the
i
th node and is defined as in (
7
).

Let
t^{\ast}=\max(\mathcal{T})
and
s^{\ast}=\max(\mathcal{S})
be the largest index in each of the two sets. If
s^{\ast}>t^{\ast}
then
\hat{f}_{\mathcal{S}}=0
.
This holds,
because, due to the order of the nodes in the chain and the law of total expectation:

\displaystyle\hat{f}_{\mathcal{S}}=\mathbb{E}_{X_{1}}\quantity[Z_1 \cdots\EE_{X_{\smax}}\qty[Z_{\smax} \Big| X_{\smax-1}] \cdots\Big| X_0].

Since the only term dependent on
X_{s^{\ast}}
is
\Phi_{s^{\ast}}
, then the inner most expectation equals

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{X_{s^{\ast}}}\quantity[Z_{\smax} \Big| X_{\smax-1}]=\mathbb{E}_{X_{s^{\ast}}}\quantity[\Phi_{\smax} \Big| X_{\smax-1}]=0,

where we used Lemma
1
implying that
\hat{f}_{\mathcal{S}}=0
.

Suppose
s^{\ast}\leq t^{\ast}
, and let
t_{1}<\cdots,<t_{d}=t^{\ast}
be the ordered elements of
\mathcal{T}
with
d=|\mathcal{T}|
.
These nodes create
d-1
,
\mathcal{T}
-segments
[t_{i-1},t_{i})
, a head segment
[1,t_{1})
and a tail segment
[t^{\ast},n]
.
Let
t_{0}:=0
,

\mathcal{S}_{i}=\mathcal{S}\cap[t_{i-1},t_{i})
for any
i\in[d]

and note that since
\mathcal{S}\subseteq[n]
, we have
\mathcal{S}_{1}\subseteq[1,t_{1})
.
If
\mathcal{S}_{i}\setminus\mathcal{T}
is not empty, define
h_{i}:=\min\mathcal{S}_{i}\setminus\mathcal{T}
; otherwise set
h_{i}=t_{i}
.
For any
i\leq j
, let
\mathbf{Z}_{i}^{j}=\prod_{i\leq l\leq j}Z_{l}
.
Then,
\hat{f}_{\mathcal{S}}
breaks into expectations over each segment:
