Development of an exploratory mismatch negativity-based prognostic nomogram for consciousness recovery in patients with prolonged disorders of consciousness: a derivation study
Authors: Yucong Zou, Xiao Lv, Weiming Lin, Jing Zhou, Wenshi Chen, Juan Wang, Dan Li, Fubing Zha, Yulong Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundProlonged disorders of consciousness (pDOC) pose significant clinical and societal challenges. Evaluating the prognosis of patients with pDOC is of a great concern for clinicians. This derivation study aimed to establish a nomogram based on mismatch negativity (MMN) to predict the recovery of consciousness in patients with pDOC.MethodsThis is a single-center retrospective derivation study. From September 2021 to June 2023, demographic and clinical information and MMN results of patients with pDOC were collected. The prognosis of patients who were admitted to the hospital for 6 months was assessed using the Glasgow Outcome Scale, categorized as “unfavorable prognosis” and “favorable prognosis.” In this study, Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regression analyses were used to select the most relevant predictors and create the nomogram. Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC), calibration curves and Decision Curves Analysis (DCA) in the nomogram explained the predictive efficacy and clinical utility of the patients.Results101 patients with pDOC were included, with 57 and 44 having unfavorable and favorable prognose. Univariate and LASSO regression analysis indicated that the MMN amplitude at Fz, Coma Recovery Scale-Revised scores, disease duration, and multiple intensive care unit admissions were the independent factors that were used to develop the exploration nomogram. ROC curves, calibration curves, and DCA showed good predictive power (area under the ROC curve: 0.821), with DCA suggested potential net benefit within clinically relevant decision-probability ranges.ConclusionIn this study, an exploratory nomogram developed based on MMN predicted the 6-month outcome of patients with pDOC. This exploratory, internally validated model may support prognostic assessment.Keywords: Prolonged disorders of consciousness, Mismatch negativity, Prognosis, Nomogram, Exploratory

Introduction

With advances in critical care, more patients with brain injuries survive and progress to disorders of consciousness (DOC) [1]. Generally, patients with DOC duration of more than 28 days are defined as having prolonged DOC (pDOC) [2]. This usually comprises a vegetative state or unresponsive wakefulness syndrome (VS/UWS) and a minimally conscious state (MCS) [2, 3]. The recovery of consciousness in patients with pDOC has become a concern for physicians and patients’ families. pDOC not only presents substantial clinical challenges but also imposes a significant economic burden on families and society, directly impacting subsequent medical decisions [4–6]. Therefore, assessing the prognosis of patients with pDOC is essential.

Behavioral scales, neuroimaging, and neurophysiological examinations are currently used to evaluate patients with pDOC [6]. Coma Recovery Scale-Revised (CRS-R) is commonly used in clinical practice due to its ease of implementation [7]. Specifically, the CRS-R primarily involves observing patients’ behavioral responses, with higher scores indicating a better prognosis [2, 8]. However, this method is relatively subjective, resulting in a misdiagnosis rate of approximately 40%, potentially affecting prognosis accuracy [8]. Neuroimaging techniques such as functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) and positron emission tomography (PET) show more advantages compared to behavioral scales [6, 9]. fMRI provides high-resolution structural and functional imaging, reflecting the activation levels of various brain networks [10]. Stronger functional connectivity (FC) typically correlates with a more favorable prognosis [9, 11]. PET imaging assesses cerebral metabolic activity, offering valuable prognostic insights [9, 10]. However, neuroimaging techniques are less suitable for bedside use and present challenges regarding result accessibility [8]. Neurophysiological examinations are advantageous due to their portability, bedside applicability, and high temporal resolution [10, 12]. Among these, event-related potentials (ERP) are regarded as a reliable method for investigating cognitive function and predicting coma outcomes, as they reflect the brain’s capacity to process information [13, 14].

Mismatch negativity (MMN) is one of the ERP components that reflects relatively automatic response to an occasional mismatched deviant stimulus [15, 16]. Specifically, MMN is a negative wave that appears between 100 and 250 ms in the EEG after stimulus onset [17]. Its amplitude maybe a valuable indicator of the state of consciousness in patients with pDOC because it does not require voluntary attention [13, 18]. Previous studies have indicated that MMN has the potential to predict subsequent recovery in acute coma patients [14]. However, there is currently a lack of studies exploring the prognostic value of MMN in patients with pDOC.

In recent years, nomograms have gained popularity for predicting patient prognosis [19]. These visual graphical tools represent the probability of event occurrence for individual patients [20]. Therefore, we aimed to conduct an exploratory derivation to develop an MMN-based nomogram for estimating consciousness-recovery probability in patients with pDOC.

Methods

Patients

This is a retrospective derivation study. We collected data on patients with pDOC admitted to the Rehabilitation Department of Shenzhen Second People’s Hospital between September 2021 and June 2023. Data included demographic information, clinical information, and MMN results. The inclusion criteria were: (1) age ≥ 18 years, (2) duration of DOC >28 days, (3) clinical diagnosis of VS/UWS or MCS according to the standard criteria, and (4) no use of muscle relaxants or sedatives in the 24 h prior to the MMN examination. The exclusion criteria were (1) pre-existing neurological diseases, (2) hearing impairment (N100 component not detected within 100 ms of MMN latency) [13], (3) missing clinical information.

A total of 101 patients with pDOC were included in this study, as illustrated in Fig. 1. The acquisition and analysis of data garnered approval from the Ethics Committee of Shenzhen Second People’s Hospital (20221019001-FS01). The study follows the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. This retrospective analysis of demographic and clinical information and MMN was exempted from the need for informed consent.

Clinical assessment

Baseline and clinical information

We collected relevant potential prognostic factors for analysis, as these variables are commonly considered to be associated with outcomes in patients with pDOC. Potential predictors including demographic and clinical information. To minimize data-driven selection, candidate predictors and their classified forms were pre-specified a priori. Besides, the data were collected and checked by three doctors. All data was collected from the electronic medical records system and recorded using electronic spreadsheets.

The demographic information included data on age, sex, etiology, brain region of injury, and disease duration. Etiology was categorized as “Cerebral hemorrhage,” “Cerebral infarction,” “Traumatic brain injury,” and “Other”. The brain region of injury was classified as “Cerebral cortex,” “Thalamus or corpus callosum,” “Brainstem,” “Cerebellum,” and “Other”. Disease duration was defined as the time from the onset of the DOC to the time of the MMN examination We reported the disease duration as median with interquartile range (IQR) and the “>3 months” and “≤ 3 months” indicator was categorized. The clinical information collected included medical history, intensive care unit (ICU) admission history, use of indwelling catheters, complications, and rehabilitation plans. Medical history items comprised smoking, alcohol consumption, complications (including lung infection, urinary tract infection, and cranial infection), pupillary reflexes, and light reflexes were coded as “no” and “yes.” The use of indwelling catheter and tracheal tube was coded similarly. Information on ICU admissions included whether the patients were hospitalized in the ICU and whether they experienced multiple ICU admissions defined as admission in the ICU ≥ 2 times.

All included patients received conventional rehabilitative wakefulness treatment during hospitalization. In addition, many patients received wakefulness drugs after exclusion of contraindications. Previous studies have suggested that the use of arousal-promoting drugs may influence the prognosis of patients with pDOC [2, 21]. Therefore, the variable “Wake-promoting drugs” was coded as “no” and “yes.” Wake-promoting drugs commonly administered during hospitalization included amantadine, baclofen, zolpidem, midazolam, and ziconotide [2, 21].

The CRS-R

The CRS-R consists of six subscales, with a total score ranging from 0 to 23 [22, 23]. In this study, the state of consciousness in patients with pDOC was diagnosed as MCS or VS/UWS using the CRS-R. To minimize bias due to fluctuations in patients’ consciousness, the CRS-R was evaluated by two experienced physicians on five to seven repeated occasions over 1 week. The highest CRS-R score was used for prognosis.

MMN data acquisition and paradigm

The MMN examination was conducted during the week of the patient’s admission to the rehabilitation medicine department, and the examination was placed in a noiseless environment. MMN data were collected using a BrainAmp MR plus 64 EEG analyzer (Brainproduct, Munich, Germany) set according to the international 10–10 system [24]. Skin impedance during the acquisition was maintained below 5 kΩ with a sampling interval of 1 ms and resolution at 0.1 µV. The reference electrodes for all channels were TP9 and TP10 in the bilateral mastoid processes, and the ground electrode was placed at Fpz. The electrical signals were digitally amplified and continuously digitized at a sampling rate of 1000 Hz and the bandwidth ranged from 10 to 1000 Hz. Given that some patients underwent unilateral or bilateral decompressive craniectomy, we recorded the wave amplitudes of three brain regions at the midline: Fz, Cz, and Pz.

The MMN component was evoked using the oddball auditory paradigm, which consists of two types of auditory stimuli with different frequencies (standard and deviant stimuli). The paradigm was presented continuously and pseudo-randomly, where the standard stimulus consisted of 800 pure sounds of 50 ms duration and 1000 Hz frequency, and the deviant stimulus consisted of 200 pure sounds of 50 ms duration and 2000 Hz frequency. Auditory stimuli were delivered through noise-cancelling headphones with the volume set to 70 dB. The entire oddball paradigm lasted for 10 min, and the paradigm is shown in Fig. 2.

Schematic of the Oddball paradigm

MMN analysis

The MMN was analyzed by a credentialed neurophysiologist using BrainVision Analyzer 2.2 (Brainproduct, Munich, Germany). First, the collected raw data were processed by infinite impulse response (IIR) filtering, the band-pass filtering frequency was set to 0.1–40 Hz, and a notch filter of 48–52 Hz was used to eliminate the interference of alternating current [25]. Second, artifacts such as electromyogram (EMG), electrooculogram (EOG), and electrocardiogram (ECG) were identified and eliminated using independent component analysis [26]. Third, raw data inspection was conducted on the EEG signals to eliminate interference due to device malfunctions or abnormal patient movements, and data with an amplitude exceeding 120 µV were automatically rejected [27]. Raw data with excessive artifacts (>50%) were excluded due to the inability to perform effective segmentation. Fourth, the preprocessed data were segmented to obtain the standard (S6) and deviant stimuli (S7), and baseline corrections and averages were performed to obtain evoked waveforms [28]. Finally, the S7 waveform was subtracted from the S6 waveform to obtain MMN components [28].

Outcome assessment

The prognosis of patients with pDOC was monitored by a physician via telephone 6 months after discharge from the hospital. Prognosis was evaluated using the Glasgow Outcome Scale (GOS), which is widely used to evaluate the recovery of consciousness or quality of survival after brain injury [29]. The GOS scores ranged from 1 to 5 (1: Dead; 2: Vegetative state; 3: Severe disability; 4: Moderate disability; and 5: Good recovery).

In this study, a GOS score of 1–2 was classified into the unfavorable prognosis group and 3–5 into the favorable prognosis group.

Statistical analysis

This nomogram was exploratory and focused on model development and internal validation. EmpowerStats software (www.empowerstats.com, X&Y Solutions, Inc., Boston, MA, USA) and R 3.4.3 (http://www.R-project.org, The R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) were used for data analysis. Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation when the data followed a normal distribution and as median with IQR when the data had a skewed distribution. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. All statistical hypothesis tests were two-tailed, and statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

There was no missing data for model variables in the final analysis dataset. The statistical analysis steps were as follows: (1) We first conducted baseline comparisons of the demographic and clinical characteristics between the favorable prognosis and unfavorable prognosis groups. Differences between the two groups were examined using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, two-sample t-test, and χ2 test. We also evaluated reliability of the CRS-R total score using an intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) with 95% confidence intervals. (2) Univariate logistic regression was used to select statistically significant variables associated with the prognostic outcomes. Subsequently, least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression was used to screen for predictor variables from the indicators with P < 0.1 in the univariate analysis and pre-specified variables considered prognostically relevant by other studies. (3) A nomogram model was developed for variables with non-zero coefficients in the LASSO regression. The linear predictor of nomogram is calculated as the weighted sum of the predictor variables, where each variable is multiplied by its corresponding regression coefficient derived from LASSO regression. The total scores were obtained from the values of each index and plotted on the total point scale, which is used to assign a corresponding value for the predicted consciousness recovery rate of the nomogram (bottom). To characterize the balance of sample information relative to model size, we reported the events-per-variable (EPV) ratio, defined as the number of outcome events divided by the number of estimated regression parameters. The lower EPV indicates greater risk of overfitting and less stable estimates. (4) Internal validation of the nomogram with 1000 bootstrap resampling and the area under the curve (AUC) of the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve were used to reflect the predictive efficiency of the nomogram. The optimism-corrected AUC from bootstrap validation was also reported. The clinical utility of the nomogram was assessed using curve analysis (DCA). DCA is a methodology for evaluating alternative models’ clinical utility, applied to nomograms by quantifying net benefits at different thresholds. Curves for the treat-all-patients nomogram strategy (representing maximum clinical costs) and the treat-none nomogram strategy (indicating no clinical benefit) served as reference benchmarks.

Results

Baseline characteristics

Table 1 shows the baseline characteristics of the 101 patients included in the study, among whom 57 had no recovery of consciousness and 44 had recovery of consciousness. During the follow-up period, eight patients were reported to have died, resulting in a mortality rate of 7.92%. The mean age of the included patients was 55.38 ± 15.72 years, with 69 males and 32 females. The disease duration was 60.00 (30.00–108.00) days in unfavorable prognosis group, and 45.00 (36.50–64.00) days in favorable prognosis group. Regarding etiology, 52.48% were diagnosed with cerebral hemorrhage, 12.87% had cerebral infarction, 17.82% had traumatic brain injury, and the remaining patients were classified as having other conditions. Lesion location did not differ significantly between outcome groups. Regarding MMN, the MMN amplitude at Fz (FzMMNA), the MMN amplitude at Cz (CzMMNA), and the MMN amplitude at Pz (PzMMNA) were significantly higher in the unfavorable prognosis group than in the favorable prognosis group. However, no significant differences were observed between groups in MMN latencies at Fz (FzMMNL), the MMN latencies at Cz (CzMMNL), or the MMN latencies at Pz (PzMMNL). In addition, Inter-rater reliability for the CRS-R total score was ICC (2,1) = 0.994 (95% CI, 0.991–0.996).

Construction of nomogram

As shown in Fig. 5, an exploratory four-predictor nomogram for estimating 6-month consciousness recovery in patients with pDOC was developed: disease duration, multiple ICU admissions, CRS-R score, and FzMMNA (OR = 0.1577, 95%CI: 0.0214–0.7120; OR = 0.0139, 95%CI: 0.0000- 0.6677; OR = 1.3609, 95%CI: 1.1631–1.6798; OR = 1.3576, 95%CI: 1.0851–1.8980). The higher total scores on the nomogram corresponded to a greater probability of recovery. Specifically, patients with a disease duration of less than 3 months, no history of multiple ICU admissions, higher CRS-R scores, and higher FzMMNA amplitudes were more likely to achieve favorable outcomes. Validation of the nomogram

As shown in Fig. 6, internal validation of the nomogram using 1000 bootstrap resampling showed high consistency between the observed and predicted values as indicated by the calibration curves. The final model included four parameters with 44 outcome events, yielding EPV = 11. Additionally, Fig. 7 illustrates the evaluation of the model’s discrimination using the ROC curve, revealing a discrimination was AUC = 0.831 (95% CI = 0.750–0.912) and optimism-corrected AUC = 0.821 (95% CI = 0.7377–0.9035), and no external validation was performed in this dataset..The formula for the final model from bootstrap was as follows: −2.66947 −1.84703× (disease duration = more than 3 months) + 0.30569 × FzMMNA − 4.27537×(multiple ICU admissions = yes) + 0.30812× CRS-R scores.

The DCA was used to evaluate the clinical utility of the nomogram (Fig. 8). For example, if one patient has an estimated 50% probability of a favorable prognosis, the nomogram indicates a net treatment benefit of 0.4, indicating additional advantages of our model.

Discussion

The number of patients with pDOC has progressively increased in recent years, and the accurate prediction of prognosis can effectively help physicians. In this study, we developed an exploratory nomogram based on MMN and other clinical variables to estimate the 6-month probability of consciousness recovery in hospitalized pDOC patients. The model incorporates four predictors: FzMMNA, CRS-R score, disease duration, and multiple ICU admissions. This exploratory, internally validated model provides an estimate of 6-month recovery and requires prospective external validation prior to clinical use.

The MMN reflects the pre-attentive processing of different auditory stimuli in the cerebral cortex and indicates residual brain function [13, 28, 30, 31]. Previous studies have suggested that the MMN amplitude is maximal in the Fz brain region, and the nomogram in this study also showed that FzMMNA was an independent factor in the prognosis of patients with pDOC [14]. Our study findings indicated that higher FzMMNA levels were associated with a better prognosis, suggesting increased levels of alertness in patients with pDOC. Fischer et al. also found a tendency for the MMN amplitude to gradually increase during recovery in coma patients [32]. A plausible explanation is that MMN amplitude reflects neural processes modulating the auditory discrimination [33]. In addition, Zhou et al. indicated a positive relationship between FzMMNA and prognosis in patients with acute coma [28]. Although differences in disease duration across studies may introduce variability, these findings provide objective support for the prognostic relevance of FzMMNA observed in our study.

It is worth noting that although the potential of MMN amplitude as a neurophysiological biomarker for predicting consciousness recovery, its prognostic value should be interpreted with caution. MMN amplitude is influenced by disease severity and age, potentially affecting observed associations. In patients with pDOC, variations in the level of consciousness may affect MMN amplitude. Wang et al. reported a significant correlation between MMN amplitude and CRS-R scores, suggesting that more severely impaired patients tend to exhibit lower MMN amplitudes [18]. Aging is also associated with declines in central auditory processing, leading to reduced MMN amplitudes in older adults [34, 35]. The association may be explained by age-related variation in cortical function relevant to MMN generation [36]. In addition, the sensitivity and specificity of MMN in prognostic contexts warrant careful consideration [37, 38]. Several studies have indicated that while MMN tends to show high specificity, its sensitivity in predicting consciousness recovery in DOC patients is relatively low [38–40]. This may be attributable to MMN’s reliance on intact auditory cortical and sensory-memory processes, which confers high specificity for preserved neurocognitive function but low sensitivity when these processes are transiently suppressed in severe brain injury [37, 38, 41]. Given these limitations, relying solely on MMN amplitude for prognostication is insufficient. A more accurate and reliable approach may involve integrating MMN results with clinical assessments and other relevant disease-related variables in a multifactorial framework.

Additionally, the CRS-R score reflects patients’ behavioral responses and state of consciousness [22]. Compared with previous studies that used the GCS, the nomogram using the CRS-R as a predictor provided a detailed and accurate explanation of the behavioral responses of patients with pDOC [6, 8]. This study found that higher CRS-R scores correlated with better outcomes in patients with pDOC. Portaccio et al. indicated that improved consciousness at hospital discharge was associated with higher CRS-R scores at admission [42]. Considering that CRS-R may be affected by fluctuations in consciousness or subjectivity, our model combined it with MMN quantitative waveforms, which was associated with higher apparent predictive performance.

Moreover, disease duration and multiple ICU admissions were negatively associated with outcomes in patients with pDOC. Specifically, patients with a disease duration of less than 3 months showed a higher probability of recovery after 6 months. Previous studies have indicated that for patients with pDOC, the first 3 months are crucial for the restoration of consciousness [43]. Lee et al. reported that patients with prolonged and extensive pDOC had a lower probability of neurobehavioral recovery, which may be related to the temporal dynamics and cumulative effects of impaired neurobehavioral function [44]. Consistent with the early recovery of motor function in stroke patients, recovery of consciousness may primarily occur in the first few months [44, 45]. Regarding ICU admissions, our study found that the patients with multiple ICU admissions had worse outcomes. In our study of 101 patients with pDOC, a total of 15 patients were repeatedly admitted to the ICU due to worsening conditions. The primary reasons included pneumonia leading to septic shock, recurrent stroke, and acute myocardial infarction. Although ICU care may improve short-term survival and reduce acute complications such as cachexia in DOC patients, multiple ICU admissions typically reflect greater disease severity and are associated with a higher likelihood of prolonged DOC [46, 47].

Previous studies have indicated that age and comorbidities are important factors influencing the prognosis of patients with DOC [48–50]. In elderly pDOC patients, reduced metabolic function and decreased neuroplasticity may negatively affect recovery [48, 49]. Additionally, comorbid conditions such as lung infections and intracranial infections may exacerbate systemic and neuroinflammatory responses, further impairing the likelihood of recovery [48, 50]. However, in our study, neither age nor comorbidities were included in the final nomogram. Although these variables were considered during both univariate and multivariate regression analyses, they did not show statistically significant associations with recovery outcomes. This may be due to a relatively narrow age distribution in our sample or limited statistical power due to sample size constraints. Despite their exclusion from the model, clinicians should still consider these factors during prognostic evaluation to enhance the comprehensiveness and clinical applicability of outcome predictions.

To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first to develop an exploratory nomogram incorporating MMN to predict outcomes in patients with pDOC. This study has several insightful clinical strengths. First, our nomogram provides a clearer reflection of the probability of regaining consciousness after six months in patients with pDOC. Second, compared to conventional clinical predictors, we combined the MMN and CRS-R yielded a finer-grained view of consciousness and contributed to prognostic estimation in this derivation cohort. Third, other factors of the model such as “disease duration,” and “multiple ICU admissions,” are easily accessible and usable in clinical practice. Furthermore, we further avoided data-driven overfitting by pre-specifying candidate predictors. The optimism-corrected AUC and the EPV are reported to quantify validated performance, supporting the stability of the derivation nomogram.

Finally, this study has some limitations. First, this is an exploratory, single-center, retrospective derivation study with internal validation only. As a retrospective study, no pre-registered statistical analysis plan was implemented, which we acknowledge as a limitation. Given its exploratory status, the nomogram requires prospective, protocol-driven external validation before any clinical application. Second, due to the limited sample size, we were unable to perform external validation or establish a cross-validation set to assess the nomogram’s predictive performance and clinical utility. In future research, we intend to increase the sample size to further validate the nomogram’s predictive accuracy and clinical applicability. Third, factors mentioned in previous studies (e.g., age, comorbidities) may be related to the prognosis of patients with pDOC were not included in the nomogram. Furthermore, although lesion location was assessed in logistic regression, it was not retained in the nomogram. Quantitative measures of lesion burden/volume and multimodal imaging biomarkers (e.g., blood-oxygen-level-dependent-fMRI indices and functional connectivity) were not collected in this cohort and are being prospectively acquired in our ongoing studies for inclusion at external validation. To further validate our findings, we have initiated a series of pre-registered, multi-center, prospective studies. These ongoing works employ harmonized MMN protocols and systematically acquire advanced neuroimaging biomarkers to enable robust external validation, model refinement, and the incorporation of detailed lesion metrics.

Conclusion

In this study, an exploratory nomogram was developed to predict the prognosis of patients with pDOC based on the MMN. We found that patients with higher FzMMNA levels, higher CRS-R scores, disease duration of less than three months, and without multiple ICU admissions showed higher probability of achieving consciousness recovery after 6 months. This nomogram may serve as a useful tool for visualizing the probability of recovery, thereby supporting clinical decision-making and individualized patient management. This exploratory nomogram offers visual estimates of recovery that may inform patient–clinician communication, but it is not intended for clinical decision-making until prospectively validated.
