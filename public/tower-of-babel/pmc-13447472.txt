Accuracy of iliac crest reference pin placement in total hip arthroplasty: a CT-based analysis of risk factors for cortical perforation
Authors: Yasutaka Masada, Tomonori Tetsunaga, Kazuki Yamada, Tomohiro Inoue, Ryuichiro Okuda, Tomoko Tetsunaga, Yuki Okazaki, Toshifumi Ozaki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionAccurate iliac crest reference pin placement is essential for safe navigation-assisted total hip arthroplasty (THA). Although complications are uncommon, inaccurate placement may cause neurovascular injury. This study aimed to evaluate pin insertion accuracy using postoperative computed tomography (CT), identify risk factors for cortical perforation, and determine whether CT-detected cortical perforation was associated with postoperative acetabular cup positioning accuracy.Materials and methodsWe retrospectively analyzed 200 primary THAs performed using portable or CT-based navigation between December 2022 and July 2025. Pin position was assessed on CT 1 week postoperatively and classified as complete intraosseous insertion or cortical perforation. Risk factors were examined using univariable and multivariable logistic regression analyses. Perforation direction was evaluated using Firth’s penalized logistic regression. Postoperative cup positioning accuracy was assessed using CT-based radiographic inclination (RI), radiographic anteversion (RA), and absolute errors from target angles of 40° for RI and 15° for RA.ResultsCortical perforation occurred in 70 of 200 hips (35.0%): 32 medial and 38 lateral. On a pin-by-pin basis, 113 of 400 pins (28.3%) demonstrated perforation. In multivariable analysis, smaller ASIS width, lateral decubitus positioning, and 4-mm pin use independently predicted cortical perforation. Medial perforation was independently associated with lateral decubitus positioning, 4-mm pin use, and smaller iliac wing angle in Firth’s model. RI, RA, absolute RI error, and absolute RA error did not differ significantly between the intraosseous and cortical perforation groups. All hips in both groups were within 10° of the target angles for both RI and RA. No dislocation or revision surgery occurred in either group.ConclusionsCT-detected cortical perforation of iliac crest reference pins was common but was rarely accompanied by clinically apparent complications and was not associated with inferior postoperative cup positioning accuracy. Perforation risk was influenced by insertion position, platform-associated instrumentation, and pelvic morphology. These findings should be interpreted primarily as a technical accuracy endpoint rather than as evidence of clinically proven harm. The clinical significance of CT-detected cortical perforation remains uncertain, and particular attention should be paid to minimizing medial cortical breach.

Introduction

Total hip arthroplasty (THA) relieves pain and improves function in patients with end-stage hip disease and is regarded as one of the most successful procedures in modern orthopaedics [10]. However, its success depends on precise implant positioning [14], as malalignment can lead to polyethylene wear, impingement, instability, and ultimately, component failure [2].

Navigation systems are widely used to improve the accuracy of implant placement. Portable and computed tomography (CT)-based navigation provide excellent accuracy and clinical outcomes in THA [16, 22], and robot-assisted THA demonstrates accurate component positioning [1, 11, 17]. These navigation- and robot-assisted systems generally require the insertion of reference pins into the iliac crest (Fig. 1). Minor complications related to pin insertion include short-term postoperative pain and discomfort [9], whereas rare but serious events, such as pelvic fractures, vascular injuries, and lateral femoral cutaneous nerve (LFCN) palsy, have also been reported [8, 15, 20]. Because critical neurovascular and visceral structures lie medial to the ilium, avoiding excessive medial extraosseous protrusion is essential to minimize the risk of severe injury [15]. At the same time, adequate pin purchase is required for reliable navigation, and limited transcortical or bicortical engagement may enhance fixation stability. Thus, the optimal balance between secure fixation and avoidance of hazardous overpenetration remains incompletely defined. Despite the widespread use of navigation, quantitative evidence defining the patient-, technique-, and device-level factors that drive iliac crest pin placement accuracy and cortical perforation risk remains limited. Heterogeneity in patient habitus and pelvic morphology, surgical positioning (supine vs. lateral decubitus), and device selection (pin diameter and platform type) obscures their relative contributions to perforation.

This study addresses this gap by combining (i) early postoperative CT to objectively classify the pin position (intraosseous vs. medial/lateral cortical perforation) with (ii) a comprehensive analysis across three domains (patient, technique, and device) in a large single-center cohort. We aimed to (1) evaluate the accuracy of iliac crest reference pin insertion using 1-week postoperative CT, (2) identify patient-, technique-, and device-related factors associated with cortical perforation, (3) examine pin-related complications, and (4) additionally assess whether CT-detected cortical perforation was associated with postoperative acetabular cup positioning accuracy. We hypothesized that greater soft tissue thickness, a narrower iliac crest, use of larger diameter pins, and lateral decubitus positioning would individually be associated with a higher perforation risk. In addition, we postulated that surgeon experience and the severity of hip deformity, as reflected by the Crowe classification, might also influence the risk of cortical perforation by affecting technical execution and local pelvic anatomy.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

This retrospective study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of our institution (IRB number: K2511-022). All procedures adhered to the ethical standards outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki (1964) and its subsequent amendments as well as applicable institutional and national guidelines. Given the retrospective design, the requirement for informed consent was waived. This study is reported in accordance with the STROBE guidelines. We screened 211 consecutive patients who underwent primary THA between December 2022 and July 2025. After the exclusion of patients with a history of pelvic osteotomy (n = 1), pelvic fracture (n = 2), and those who underwent primary THA without navigation (n = 29), 179 patients (200 hips) were included in the final analysis. All included procedures were performed using portable or CT-based navigation systems that required iliac crest reference pin insertion (Fig. 2). All recorded variables including demographic, surgical, and radiographic measurements were complete in all cases. No values were missing from this dataset.

Devices and surgical procedure

Three navigation systems were used in this study: the Naviswiss system (Naviswiss AG, Brugg, Switzerland), Stryker CT-based navigation (Stryker, Kalamazoo, MI, USA), and Ortoma Treatment Solution (OTS™) (Ortoma AB, Gothenburg, Sweden). The Naviswiss system is a portable navigation platform, whereas Stryker CT-based navigation and OTS are CT-based navigation systems. The choice of navigation platform was determined preoperatively according to the degree of hip deformity and the surgical plan. Pin diameter was dependent on the navigation platform: 3-mm pins were used with the Naviswiss system and Stryker CT-based navigation, whereas 4-mm pins were used with OTS. Because pin diameter was determined by the navigation platform, it may partly reflect platform-related characteristics rather than an independent technical choice. Six surgeons performed pin insertion. Two of these surgeons had extensive experience in THA (> 500 cases each), whereas the remaining four had performed fewer than 500 THAs. The surgeons were therefore categorized into two groups according to THA experience: experienced and less experienced. In cases using the Naviswiss system, pin insertion and registration were inherently performed in the supine position. Accordingly, the procedure was performed either in the supine position or after turning the patient using the flip technique [5, 21]. In contrast, Stryker CT-based navigation and OTS did not require pin insertion and registration in the supine position; therefore, when surgery was performed in the lateral decubitus position, the pins were inserted after the patient had been placed laterally. Before pin insertion, iliac wing morphology was confirmed on axial CT images. The anterior superior iliac spine (ASIS) was palpated, and the first pin was inserted 20–30 mm posterior to the ASIS, while the second was inserted posterior and parallel to the first. A total of 400 reference pins were used, with two pins placed in each of the 200 procedures. For insertion of the second pin, the choice between a parallel guide and freehand insertion was left to the discretion of the surgeon. The pins were inserted using a power driver in drill mode. The target insertion depth was defined as complete intraosseous placement of the threaded portion of the pin.

Evaluations

The primary outcome was pin insertion status on CT obtained 1 week postoperatively, classified on a case-by-case basis as either complete intraosseous insertion or cortical perforation (medial or lateral) (Fig. 3). Reference pins were removed intraoperatively at the end of the procedure. Therefore, postoperative CT evaluation was performed based on the osseous pin tract (pinhole), and cortical perforation was defined as a breach in the inner or outer cortex of the pin tract. Pin-by-pin data were presented descriptively, and inferential analyses were conducted on a case-by-case basis to avoid within-subject correlation. All postoperative CT scans were performed using a standardized protocol with 2 mm contiguous axial slices. To reduce metal-related artifacts from the hip prostheses, a dedicated metal artifact reduction technique (MAR) was employed as part of the routine imaging protocol. This approach enhances the delineation of the iliac crest and pin trajectory for reliable measurement. The CT images were reviewed independently by two experienced orthopedic surgeons who were blinded to the clinical data and surgical details, including pin diameter and patient position. Any disagreements were resolved by consensus. A cortical perforation was defined as any portion of the threaded tip crossing the inner or outer cortex. Because cortical perforation in either reference pin may pose a clinical safety concern, the perforation status was defined on a case-by-case basis (i.e., perforation was recorded if either pin breached the cortex). For all 400 pins, we analyzed the specific number and distribution of perforations, categorizing them into cases in which only the first pin, only the second pin, or both pins demonstrated cortical perforation. Axial and coronal CT images were reviewed to determine the insertion status and to measure the subcutaneous fat thickness at the ASIS (ASIS-SFT) (Fig. 4a), ASIS width (ASIS-W) (Fig. 4b), and iliac wing angle (IWA) [18]. The IWA was defined on axial CT as the angle between (1) a line from the ASIS to the most dependent midpoint of the buttock in contact with the CT table and (2) the transverse axis of the sacrum (Fig. 4c). A smaller IWA indicated a more upright iliac wing. The insertion status was primarily assessed using axial images; however, coronal images were also evaluated in cases in which determination was difficult using axial views alone. The following variables were evaluated as potential correlates of cortical perforation: age, body mass index (BMI), Crowe’s classification [6] in patients with osteoarthritis, surgeon experience (experienced or less-experienced), navigation platform, pin diameter (3 vs. 4 mm), patient position during insertion (supine vs. lateral decubitus), ASIS-SFT, ASIS-W, and IWA as an index of iliac wing morphology. Medical records were reviewed for infection, pin loosening, fracture, lateral femoral cutaneous nerve palsy, and neurovascular or visceral events.

Postoperative cup position was assessed using a 3D-template system (ZedHip, LEXI, Tokyo, Japan) on CT images obtained 1 week postoperatively. Radiographic inclination (RI) and radiographic anteversion (RA) were measured with reference to the functional pelvic plane. The target cup angles were set at 40° for RI and 15° for RA. Absolute errors from the target angles were calculated as |postoperative RI − 40°| and |postoperative RA − 15°|. The mean values, standard deviations, ranges, and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated for RI, RA, and their absolute errors. The proportions of hips within 5° and 10° of the target angles were also evaluated.

Complication and symptom assessment

Postoperative complications related to iliac crest reference pin placement were assessed through retrospective review of the medical records. Symptoms such as pin site pain, numbness or paresthesia, and sensory disturbances in the lateral femoral cutaneous nerve (LFCN) were documented at each postoperative clinic visit. We assessed symptom status approximately 1 month after surgery based solely on medical chart documentation, as no prospective telephone follow-up or standardized patient questionnaire was administered. Persistent or recurrent symptoms reported at later visits were also documented; however, systematic long-term follow-up beyond routine clinical care was not performed. In addition, postoperative THA-related adverse events, including dislocation and revision surgery, were reviewed through the medical records during the available follow-up period. The median follow-up was 23 months (IQR, 9–41 months).

Statistical analysis

The cases were classified as intraosseous insertions or cortical perforations. The age, BMI, ASIS-SFT, ASIS-W, and IWA were compared using Student’s t-test, while Crowe’s classification, surgeon experience, navigation platform, pin diameter, and patient position during insertion were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test in univariate analyses. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Navigation platform was evaluated descriptively and in univariate analyses. However, because platform was strongly associated with pin diameter and patient position in this cohort, it was not included in the multivariable models to avoid multicollinearity. Instead, pin diameter was used as the device-related variable. Covariates for the multivariable logistic regression model were specified a priori based on clinical relevance and potential confounding. BMI was included regardless of its univariate significance. For the primary analysis (intraosseous vs. perforation), the multivariable model included BMI, surgeon experience, ASIS-SFT, ASIS-W, pin diameter, and patient position during insertion. For the perforation subtype analysis (medial vs. lateral), we used multivariable logistic regression that included BMI, surgeon experience, IWA, pin diameter, and patient position. Because near-complete separation was present, estimates were obtained using Firth’s penalized logistic regression. For the analysis of cup positioning accuracy, RI, RA, absolute RI error from the target angle of 40°, and absolute RA error from the target angle of 15° were compared between the intraosseous and cortical perforation groups using Student’s t-test. The proportions of hips within 5° and 10° of the target angles were compared using the chi-square test, as appropriate. 95% CIs were calculated for the mean values of RI, RA, and their absolute errors. All analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics, version 29 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Firth’s penalized logistic regression was performed using the STATS_FIRTHLOG extension command in IBM SPSS Statistics, with IBM SPSS Statistics Essentials for R and the R package “logistf.” Interobserver agreement was assessed before a consensus was reached. Cohen’s kappa (κ) was calculated for the binary outcome (cortical perforation vs. intraosseous insertion), and weighted kappa (κw) was calculated for perforation direction (medial vs. lateral). For continuous CT measurements (ASIS-SFT, ASIS-W, and IWA), interobserver reliability was evaluated using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC; two-way random-effects model, absolute agreement).

Results

Patient characteristics

In total, 200 primary THAs were included. The cohort was predominantly female, and osteoarthritis of the hip was the most common indication; osteonecrosis of the femoral head and rheumatoid arthritis were less frequent, and other etiologies were rare (Table 1). Age, BMI, and CT-derived morphological parameters (ASIS-SFT, ASIS-W, and IWA) had wide distributions. Patient positioning during pin insertion was evenly split between the supine and lateral decubitus positions (Table 1). Regarding navigation platform, 80 cases (40%) were performed using the Naviswiss system, 36 (18%) using Stryker CT-based navigation, and 84 (42%) using OTS (Table 1).

Overall insertion status

On CT performed 1 week after surgery (n = 200), complete intraosseous placement of the threaded tip in both reference pins was confirmed in 130 cases (65.0%). The remaining 70 cases (35.0%) were classified as perforated on a case-by-case basis (i.e., if either pin demonstrated cortical perforation). Perforations occurred in 32 medial (16.0% of the cohort) and 38 lateral cases (19.0% of the cohort). No cases had mixed-direction perforations. On a pin-by-pin basis (400 pins in total), 113 pins (28.3%) showed cortical perforation. Perforation involved only the first pin in 12 cases (12 pins), the second pin in 15 cases (15 pins), and both pins in 43 cases (86 pins).

Intraosseous vs. perforation

Perforation cases showed greater ASIS-SFT (p = 0.010), smaller ASIS-W (p < 0.001), more frequent use of a 4-mm pin (p < 0.001), and more frequent lateral decubitus insertion (p < 0.001) than intraosseous cases in the univariate analyses. No significant associations were observed for age, BMI, or IWA (p = 0.16, 0.13, and 0.26, respectively) (Table 2). Navigation platform was also significantly associated with cortical perforation in the univariate analysis (p < 0.001), with perforation observed in 15 of 80 Naviswiss cases (18.8%), 14 of 36 Stryker cases (38.9%), and 41 of 84 OTS cases (48.8%) (Table 2). In the multivariable logistic regression analysis, a smaller ASIS-W (OR: 0.72 per 1-mm increase; 95% CI: 0.63–0.83; p < 0.001), lateral decubitus positioning (OR: 2.87; 95% CI: 1.20–6.88; p = 0.018), and use of a 4-mm pin (OR: 2.26; 95% CI: 1.04–4.91; p = 0.039) remained independent predictors of cortical perforation (Table 3). Although ASIS-SFT demonstrated a positive trend toward higher perforation risk (OR: 1.03 per 1-mm increase; 95% CI: 0.99–1.06), this association did not reach statistical significance after adjustment (p = 0.099). BMI and surgeon experience were not independently associated with the risk of perforation. Surgeon experience was strongly associated with patient positioning during pin insertion. Experienced surgeons predominantly performed pin insertions in the lateral decubitus position (84/103, 81.6%), whereas less-experienced surgeons more frequently inserted pins in the supine position (81/97, 83.5%). This association was highly significant (p < 0.001), indicating a strong dependency between surgeon experience and the insertion position.

Medial vs. lateral perforation

In the univariate analysis, surgeon experience differed between the medial and lateral perforation groups (p = 0.022; Table 4), but it was not independently associated with perforation direction in the Firth penalized model (p = 0.082; Table 5). The IWA was significantly smaller in medial perforations than in lateral perforations (128.9 ± 6.8° vs. 134.4 ± 5.2°, p < 0.001). In addition, medial perforations were associated with more frequent use of 4-mm pins (27/32 vs. 15/38, p < 0.001) and a markedly higher prevalence of lateral decubitus insertion (31/32 vs. 17/38, p < 0.001) (Table 4). Navigation platform also differed significantly between medial and lateral perforation cases (p < 0.001): medial perforation occurred in 1 of 15 Naviswiss perforation cases (6.7%), 4 of 14 Stryker perforation cases (28.6%), and 27 of 41 OTS perforation cases (65.9%) (Table 4).

Because lateral decubitus positioning was observed in nearly all medial perforation cases, resulting in near-complete separation of the outcome, conventional maximum-likelihood logistic regression yielded unstable estimates with inflated odds ratios and wide confidence intervals. Therefore, to obtain more reliable and bias-reduced estimates, multivariable analysis was performed using Firth’s penalized logistic regression (Table 5). In this penalized model, lateral-decubitus positioning was strongly associated with medial perforation (OR: 19.81; 95% CI: 2.62–573.65), as was the use of a 4-mm pin (OR: 10.62; 95% CI: 2.43–61.9). A smaller IWA was also an independent predictor, with each 1-degree increase in IWA associated with reduced odds of medial perforation (OR: 0.81 per 1-degree increase; 95% CI: 0.71–0.92). BMI (OR: 0.92 per 1-unit increase; 95% CI: 0.80–1.06) and surgeon experience (OR: 0.20; 95% CI: 0.018–1.21) were not independently associated with perforation direction.

Cup positioning accuracy according to perforation status

Cup positioning accuracy was compared between the intraosseous and cortical perforation groups (Table 6). RI was 40.8° ± 2.1° (range, 37.0°–45.8°) in the intraosseous group and 41.0° ± 2.4° (range, 37.0°–48.5°) in the cortical perforation group. RA was 14.8° ± 2.2° (range, 9.0°–18.7°) and 15.2° ± 2.4° (range, 11.8°–24.3°), respectively. No significant differences were observed between the groups in RI or RA. The absolute RI error from the target angle of 40° was 2.0° ± 1.2° (95% CI, 1.8°–2.2°; range, 0°–5.8°) in the intraosseous group and 2.0° ± 1.6° (95% CI, 1.6°–2.4°; range, 0°–8.5°) in the cortical perforation group. The absolute RA error from the target angle of 15° was 1.9° ± 1.1° (95% CI, 1.7°–2.1°; range, 0°–6.0°) and 1.9° ± 1.4° (95% CI, 1.6°–2.2°; range, 0°–9.3°), respectively. Student’s t-tests showed no significant differences in absolute RI error or absolute RA error between the groups.

Complications

One case of intraoperative pin loosening occurred and navigation could not be used thereafter. No infections, fractures, LFCN palsies, or neurovascular or visceral events were identified. No dislocation or revision surgery occurred in either the intraosseous or cortical perforation group during the follow-up period.

Measurement reliability

Interobserver agreement was κ = 0.85 for perforation status and κw = 0.96 for perforation direction. The interobserver reliabilities of the ASIS-SFT, ASIS-W, and IWA measurements were ICC = 0.92, 0.93, and 0.86, respectively.

Discussion

The key findings of this study were as follows: (1) CT-detected cortical perforation occurred in 35% of cases; (2) lateral decubitus insertion and platform-determined 4 mm pin use were the strongest risk factors for cortical perforation; (3) medial perforation was closely associated with unfavorable pelvic morphology, specifically a smaller IWA; and (4) CT-detected cortical perforation was not associated with inferior postoperative acetabular cup positioning accuracy; no dislocation or revision surgery was observed in either group during the available follow-up. By analyzing postoperative CT images, we assessed the technical accuracy of pin placement and identified risk factors across three domains: patient, technique, and device. Because the navigation platform was closely linked to procedural conditions in our cohort, these device-related findings should be interpreted in the context of platform-associated differences in pin diameter and insertion workflow.

Importantly, the relationship between surgeon experience and perforation risk should be interpreted in the context of the surgical setup. In our cohort, experienced surgeons more frequently performed pin insertions in the lateral decubitus position, whereas less-experienced surgeons more commonly used the supine position. This marked imbalance indicates a strong dependency between surgeon experience and insertion position, suggesting that the higher perforation rate observed among experienced surgeons in the univariate analysis may reflect the technical difficulty of lateral decubitus insertion rather than a lack of surgical skill. Accordingly, the apparent effect of insertion position should be interpreted with caution, because surgeon-related and workflow-related factors could not be fully separated in this retrospective cohort. Nevertheless, this finding suggests that even extensive experience may not fully overcome the difficulty of accurately perceiving pelvic orientation in the lateral decubitus position. Although difficulty in assessing pelvic orientation in the lateral decubitus position is well recognized, to the best of our knowledge this is the first CT-based study to quantify its impact on iliac crest reference pin tract cortical breach, to describe direction-specific breach patterns (medial vs lateral), and to identify independent, potentially modifiable predictors through multivariable risk modeling, including pelvic morphology and platform-associated technical conditions.

In response to the 35% CT-detected perforation rate, we identified smaller ASIS-W, lateral decubitus insertion, and 4-mm pin use as independent predictors. Specifically, a 5-mm decrease in ASIS-W was associated with an approximately five-fold increase in the odds of perforation, highlighting that subtle variations in iliac wing morphology can substantially affect the technical safety of pin placement. In contrast, soft tissue thickness (ASIS-SFT) was not a significant factor after adjustment, suggesting that osseous morphology and insertion conditions may be more influential than soft tissue thickness.

Among the perforation cases, medial perforations were strongly associated with lateral decubitus insertion and 4-mm pins and occurred more frequently in patients with a smaller IWA. Using Firth’s penalized logistic regression, we confirmed the independent contribution of the IWA, along with lateral decubitus positioning and pin diameter. These findings suggest that the combination of lateral decubitus positioning and an “upright” iliac wing morphology substantially narrows the safe osseous corridor, thereby increasing vulnerability to medial cortical perforation. Because medial and lateral perforations are unlikely to have equivalent clinical implications, this distinction is important when interpreting the present results. In particular, medial cortical breach is likely to be of greater preventive concern because of the proximity of critical neurovascular and visceral structures.

With the increasing adoption of navigation and robotic assistance in THA, the accuracy of reference pin placement has become an important technical component of the procedure [1, 4, 22]. Although pinless systems have been reported [3, 12], many contemporary platforms still require rigid pin fixation. Critical neurovascular and visceral structures, including the ascending branch of the deep circumflex iliac artery, lateral femoral cutaneous nerve (LFCN), and bowel, are located medial to the ilium [7]. Injuries to these structures are hazardous because they are often not visible within the operative field. Complications such as those following external fixation through the high pelvic route [13], LFCN palsy in robot-assisted THA [8], and retroperitoneal hematoma due to deep circumflex iliac artery injury have been documented [15]. Recommended preventive measures include placing the pin sufficiently posterior to the ASIS and selecting a trajectory that keeps the distal portion of the pin within the iliac wing while avoiding deep medial protrusion [7, 15].

Despite the relatively high rate of CT-detected cortical perforation, clinically apparent complications were rare in this cohort, with only one case of intraoperative pin loosening and no cases of infection, fracture, LFCN palsy, or neurovascular or visceral injury. Therefore, the present findings should be interpreted primarily as reflecting technical pin placement accuracy rather than directly proven clinical harm. Importantly, the high rate of CT-detected cortical perforation should not be interpreted as evidence that cortical perforation itself is clinically harmful. Rather, this endpoint identifies deviation from the intended intraosseous trajectory and may serve as a surrogate marker of potentially unsafe pin placement.

In addition, our CT definition of “cortical perforation” was intentionally sensitive and classified any breach of the inner or outer cortical wall along the postoperative pin tract. Accordingly, some CT-defined breaches may represent minor cortical violations—or even intended transcortical/bicortical engagement—rather than clinically relevant overpenetration. In such situations, cortical breach may not necessarily compromise fixation; indeed, increased purchase could theoretically enhance pin stability and navigational reliability. Conversely, excessive extraosseous protrusion, particularly medially, may still increase the risk of injury to adjacent neurovascular/visceral structures [15] and may contribute to pin-related pain [9]. Because the protrusion distance was not quantified and intraoperative pin stability or navigation performance (e.g., insertion torque, micromotion/loosening, tracker displacement, or registration drift) were not systematically assessed, the functional consequences of CT-detected breach—whether detrimental or beneficial—remain uncertain. Accordingly, CT-detected cortical perforation should be regarded primarily as a technical accuracy endpoint, while particular attention should be paid to minimizing medial breaches. The present study demonstrates the frequency and risk factors of CT-detected cortical perforation, but it does not establish a direct causal relationship between cortical perforation and clinically meaningful adverse outcomes.

To clarify the clinical relevance of CT-detected cortical perforation, we evaluated cup positioning accuracy according to perforation status. RI, RA, and the absolute errors from the target angles were comparable between the intraosseous and cortical perforation groups. Furthermore, the proportions of hips within 5° and 10° of the target angles were high in both groups, and all hips were within 10° of the target angles for both RI and RA. No dislocation or revision surgery occurred in either group during the available follow-up. Therefore, although cortical perforation represents a technical deviation in pin trajectory, it did not appear to compromise postoperative cup orientation as assessed by CT. However, these findings do not establish the clinical significance of CT-detected cortical perforation, because clinically meaningful adverse events were exceedingly rare and the study was not powered to evaluate rare complications.

The difficulty associated with lateral decubitus insertion likely stems from substantial variation in pelvic tilt and rotation, which makes it challenging to reliably reproduce the functional pelvic plane through external inspection [19]. Furthermore, a smaller IWA indicates a more upright iliac wing, which narrows the medial bony corridor from the customary entry point. Collectively, these factors provide a plausible explanation for why lateral decubitus insertion predisposed patients to medial perforation in the present cohort.

Therefore, preoperative CT assessment of ASIS-W and IWA may be useful for identifying patients at increased risk of inaccurate pin placement, particularly when lateral decubitus insertion is anticipated. Based on our results, adoption of the “flip technique” (inserting pins in the supine position before turning the patient into a lateral position) may represent a practical strategy to improve placement accuracy and potentially reduce the risk of medial cortical breach [5, 21]. However, the present study did not directly assess intraoperative navigation stability, registration drift, tracker micromotion, or pin grip strength. Therefore, these procedural implications should be interpreted cautiously.

This study had several limitations. First, the retrospective, single-center design may have limited its generalizability. Second, the unit of analysis was the operated hip (200 hips from 179 patients); thus, a subset of patients had bilateral THAs. Although we used a case-based definition to avoid within-case correlations between the two reference pins, observations from the same patient may not have been fully independent between sides. This residual intra-patient correlation could have led to underestimation of standard errors and may have affected the precision of the estimated associations. Moreover, because the primary analysis was case-based, pin-specific technical differences may not have been fully captured. Third, the technical parameters for pin insertion were not standardized. Fourth, bone quality was not evaluated in this study. Factors such as cortical thickness, osteoporosis, bone mineral density measured by dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry, and skeletal morphology, including the canal flare index, may influence pin purchase, fixation stability, and the risk of cortical perforation. Because these variables were not available in the present retrospective dataset, their effects could not be assessed. Fifth, the choice of navigation platform was not randomized but determined by preoperative clinical considerations (e.g., deformity severity). In addition, platform use was closely associated with procedural requirements: Naviswiss required supine pin insertion and registration, whereas Stryker and OTS did not; moreover, Naviswiss and Stryker used 3-mm pins, whereas OTS used 4-mm pins. Because pin diameter and related instrumentation differed between platforms, the observed association between pin diameter and cortical breach may partly reflect platform-specific characteristics and case selection rather than a causal effect of pin size itself. Accordingly, our findings regarding pin diameter should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating, and future studies with standardized instrumentation and/or randomized platform allocation are warranted. Sixth, the follow-up period was relatively short, with a median of 23 months. Although clinically apparent complications such as LFCN palsy, pin-site pain, infection, fracture, and neurovascular or visceral events would likely have been identified during routine follow-up, longer-term outcomes, including chronic pin-site pain, persistent sensory disturbance, delayed neuropathic symptoms, and other patient-reported symptoms, were not systematically evaluated. In addition, symptom assessment was based on retrospective chart review rather than a standardized questionnaire or prospective patient-reported outcome measure; therefore, minor or transient symptoms may have been underestimated. Seventh, the severity of cortical perforation (e.g., protrusion distance beyond the cortex) was not quantified; therefore, we could not determine how the magnitude of breach relates to clinical symptoms or complications. Although we evaluated cup positioning accuracy according to perforation status, this analysis was limited to postoperative cup orientation and did not directly assess intraoperative navigation stability, registration drift, tracker micromotion, functional outcomes, or patient-reported outcomes. In addition, because no dislocation or revision surgery occurred in either group, the relationship between cortical perforation and rare adverse clinical events could not be statistically evaluated. Because this retrospective dataset was not designed or powered to derive validated cutoff values, post hoc threshold analyses were not performed to avoid overinterpretation. Finally, no a priori sample size calculation was performed because of the retrospective design, and the subgroup analyses, particularly those for medial and lateral perforation, may have been underpowered despite the use of penalized regression methods. Although serious adverse events are rare, short-term pin site pain and discomfort are frequently reported [9]. Future prospective studies should evaluate the effects of CT-detected cortical perforation on patient-reported outcomes and clinically relevant surgical outcomes. The strength of this study lies in its objective evaluation of a large cohort of 200 THAs.

Conclusions

Cortical perforation of iliac crest reference pins occurred in 35% of primary THAs. Lateral decubitus insertion, 4-mm pin use, and unfavorable pelvic morphology were major contributors, with lateral decubitus positioning and 4-mm pin use independently predicting medial perforation. Because pin diameter was determined by the navigation platform in this cohort, the effect of pin size should be interpreted with caution. CT-detected cortical perforation was not associated with inferior postoperative acetabular cup positioning accuracy, and no dislocation or revision surgery occurred in either group during the available follow-up period. However, because clinically meaningful adverse events were not observed, CT-detected cortical perforation should be interpreted primarily as a technical accuracy endpoint rather than as a proven predictor of clinical complications. Careful preoperative CT assessment and supine pin insertion using the flip technique may help reduce the risk of cortical perforation during iliac crest pin placement.
