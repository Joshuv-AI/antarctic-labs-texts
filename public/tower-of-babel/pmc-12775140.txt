Impact of conflict-induced stress on the length of craniofacial growth
Authors: Thabet Asbi, Doron Haim, Isabelle Meinster, Liat Barness-Hadar, Jamil A Shibli, Ofer Sarne, Nir Shpack, Diva Lugassy
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn this study, we investigated the effect of chronic environmental stress, due to long-term residence in a conflict zone, on the lengths of the anterior and posterior cranial base, maxilla, and mandible in healthy children and adolescents. A retrospective cohort of 158 Israeli children (mean age: 13.42 ± 1.41 years) was divided into two groups: children from Gaza envelope (n = 82), with chronic exposure to security threats, and their counterparts from central Israel (n = 76), with low exposure. Cephalometric measurements were obtained from lateral radiographs using standardized protocols. The children’s skeletal and dental maturation stages were assessed via cervical vertebral maturation and Demirjian’s tooth development index. Compared with children in the central Israel, Gaza envelope children presented significantly shorter lengths of both the anterior (mean difference: 3.37 mm, p = 0.008) and posterior cranial bases (mean difference: 2.92 mm, p = 0.005). No significant differences were found in mandibular body length, ramus height, effective mandibular or maxillary length, or the maxillomandibular differential. Correlation analyses revealed weakened associations among craniofacial measurements in the conflict-exposed group, suggesting environmental disruption of coordinated craniofacial growth. Notably, female children in the Gaza envelope group were more affected, with significantly smaller measurements across most craniofacial parameters. Chronic exposure to conflict-related stress is associated with reduced cranial base growth in children, particularly in females. Introduction

Craniofacial development is governed by a tightly coordinated interplay of genetic, hormonal, and environmental factors. A key element in this process is the cranial base, a central structure formed through endochondral ossification, in which cartilage is gradually replaced by bone via chondrocyte proliferation1. This mechanism, which the cranial base shares with long bones of the limbs2, is modulated by systemic endocrine signals, most notably growth hormone and insulin-like growth factor 1 (IGF-1), and is thus particularly susceptible to systemic biological disruptions3–5. In contrast, the maxilla and mandible develop through intramembranous ossification, involving direct bone formation within mesenchymal tissue, rendering these structures less reliant on hormonal pathways and possibly more resistant to systemic environmental stressors6.

The cranial base grows through cartilaginous synchondroses, which serve as active growth centers analogous to epiphyseal plates in long bones2. These synchondroses govern the anteroposterior elongation of the cranial base and are essential in shaping the overall craniofacial architecture. The anterior cranial base influences the positioning of the maxilla through brain expansion, whereas the posterior cranial base plays a critical role in the articulation and positioning of the mandible1. Therefore, perturbations in cranial base development can have cascading consequences for midfacial projection, occlusion, and facial harmony7,8.

While genetic factors account for a significant proportion (50–80%) of the variability in skeletal maturation9,10, environmental influences, including nutrition, physical activity, and psychosocial stress, can significantly modulate developmental timing11,12. Chronic stress, in particular, has emerged as a key extrinsic factor that is capable of disrupting endocrine regulation13–15.

Stress triggers the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenocortical (HPA) axis, which serves as a central regulatory pathway linking the nervous and endocrine systems. Upon perceiving external stressors, the hypothalamus secretes corticotropin-releasing hormone (CRH), which in turn stimulates the anterior pituitary to release adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH). ACTH promotes the secretion of glucocorticoids, primarily cortisol, from the adrenal cortex. Cortisol subsequently exerts a negative feedback effect, inhibiting further activation of the HPA axis to maintain homeostasis16. As a key effector of the stress response, cortisol modulates immune suppression and enhances cardiovascular tone, thereby facilitating physiological adaptation to stress16.

Empirical evidence suggests that persistent and severe stress during childhood, stemming from adverse conditions such as poverty, economic hardship, or inadequate parental care, can alter the sensitivity and reactivity of the HPA axis. Such dysregulation has been associated with a spectrum of developmental disturbances, including impaired emotional regulation, behavioral and social difficulties, cognitive deficits, memory impairments, and reduced attentional capacity17.

Through activation of the hypothalamic‒pituitary‒adrenal (HPA) axis, stress increases the secretion of cortisol, growth hormone, and estrogen while suppressing IGF-1 expression. This hormonal milieu may lead to accelerated skeletal maturation and premature epiphyseal closure, with potential consequences for growth and, ultimately, stature18,19.

These effects are especially relevant for children who are exposed to prolonged conflict-related stress20,21. Israeli children residing in the Gaza envelope, a region that is subject to frequent rocket attacks and continuous security threats, experience a chronic stress load, which has been associated with elevated rates of anxiety and posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD) and disrupted physiological regulation22,23. In a previous study using the cervical vertebral maturation (CVM) method, children in this region demonstrated significantly earlier skeletal maturation than their age-matched peers in nonconflict zones24.

Although accelerated skeletal maturation has been widely linked to reduced adult height25,26, the implications for craniofacial morphology, particularly the length of the cranial base and jaws, remain poorly explored. The biological analogy between the cranial base and long bones suggests potential vulnerability to stress-induced endocrine changes. However, the differential ossification mechanism of the maxilla and mandible may render them less responsive to systemic hormonal fluctuations6.

Several studies have explored craniofacial morphology in syndromic populations exhibiting disturbances in normal growth regulation6,27. For example, Turner syndrome and neurofibromatosis are associated with short stature and reduced cranial base length. However, little is known about whether similar skeletal adaptations occur in healthy children who are exposed to chronic psychosocial stress. Moreover, the effects of sex-specific hormonal responses on these developmental trajectories have received limited attention.

Previous studies have shown that Israeli children living in the Gaza envelope experience chronic exposure to conflict-related stress, which has been linked to elevated rates of anxiety, posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD), and dysregulated physiological functioning22,23. Building on this evidence, our study aimed to examine whether such prolonged psychosocial stress also affects craniofacial development. Specifically, we investigated whether children chronically exposed to environmental threat exhibit reduced cranial base growth, structures formed by endochondral ossification that are highly sensitive to hormonal disruption, compared with peers from low-stress regions, while expecting less impact on the maxilla and mandible, which develop through intramembranous ossification.

Statistics

A post hoc sensitivity power analysis was conducted using G*Power to estimate the minimum detectable effect size given our sample sizes (Gaza Envelope group: n = 82; Central Israel group: n = 76), α = 0.05, and 80% power. The analysis indicated that the study was sufficiently powered to detect a medium effect size (Cohen’s d ≈ 0.44) for between-group comparisons using the Mann–Whitney U test.

The assumptions of normality of the dependent variables (skeletal and dental maturation) were assessed using the Kolmogorov‒Smirnov test. Mann‒Whitney tests were used for comparisons between the population from the Gaza envelope and the population from central Israel in relation to craniofacial length. Mann‒Whitney tests were also used to explore differences between the sexes in each population. Kendall’s tau nonparametric correlation tests were performed to test for correlations among skeletal maturation, tooth maturation and craniofacial structure length. Two linear regression models were constructed to evaluate the effects and explanatory power of various factors on anterior and posterior cranial base length. All analyses were performed using SPSS (Version 29.0,IBM Corp.,Armonk, NY, USA). Significant differences were considered at p < 0.05.

Discussion

This study is the first to investigate the effects of chronic conflict-related stress on the linear growth of craniofacial structures, specifically the anterior and posterior cranial base, maxilla, and mandible, in children and adolescents who are otherwise healthy. Our findings support the hypothesis that prolonged psychosocial stress disrupts cranial base development, particularly that of the anterior cranial base, in children exposed to war-related trauma in the Gaza envelope. Children in this high-stress environment presented significantly shorter cranial base lengths, by approximately 3 mm, than their peers in low-stress areas did. These differences were more pronounced in female children, suggesting sex-dependent sensitivity to environmental adversity.

Cranial base development is governed by endochondral ossification32. In this developmental process, the chondrocranium functions as a structural scaffold upon which the osseous cranial base is formed. The residual cartilaginous regions between cranial base bones—known as synchondroses—serve as active growth centers analogous to the epiphyseal plates found in long bones33. During the growth phase, these cartilaginous zones undergo endochondral ossification, wherein cartilage is progressively replaced by bone through chondrocyte proliferation, a mechanism that is critically regulated by systemic hormonal signals, particularly those mediated by the HPA axis18. Upon completion of the pubertal growth spurt, longitudinal growth of the cranial base ceases as the synchondroses undergo complete ossification. Premature closure of these growth centers—due to hormonal or environmental perturbations—can result in a reduction in cranial base length33.

In individuals under chronic stress, increased cortisol levels and disrupted IGF-1 expression may impair chondrocyte proliferation and lead to premature fusion of growth synchondroses, limiting the growth potential of endochondral structures such as the cranial base and long bones1,2,18. This finding is supported by evidence from previous studies indicating accelerated skeletal maturation under stress20,21 and aligns with developmental models suggesting that stress accelerates bone maturation but may restrict longitudinal growth25,26. Premature ossification of the cranial base may significantly influence both the timing and efficacy of orthodontic interventions, particularly those involving dentofacial orthopedic appliances designed to modulate craniofacial growth through the application of mechanical forces. A reduced cranial base length is often associated with a concomitant shortening of the maxilla, thereby exacerbating the severity of skeletal Class III malocclusion characterized by maxillary deficiency. In such cases, orthopedic approaches aimed at maxillary protraction may prove ineffective, ultimately necessitating orthognathic surgical correction34.

Our results revealed that the anterior cranial base is more affected than the posterior cranial base is, which is consistent with previous reports highlighting its greater sensitivity to environmental modulation1. The anterior cranial base, through its connection with the midface and maxilla, plays a pivotal role in determining facial projection, sagittal jaw positioning, and the overall harmony of facial aesthetics7,8. A shorter anterior cranial base may lead to a more retrusive midface and flatter profile, which could have both aesthetic and functional consequences, particularly in orthodontic patients, where the direction and projection of facial growth are central to treatment planning8.

Interestingly, no significant differences in mandibular or maxillary length were found between the groups. This may be explained by their development via intramembranous ossification, a process that is less reliant on hormonal regulation and therefore more resilient to systemic endocrine disruption6. These findings reinforce the hypothesis that craniofacial structures formed through endochondral ossification are more vulnerable to environmental stressors, whereas intramembranous structures are relatively protected. It is important to recognize that the maxilla and mandible follow distinct temporal patterns of growth35. The maxilla generally attains its peak growth earlier in adolescence, whereas the mandible exhibits its most substantial acceleration later in the pubertal period35. Given the mean age of our cohort (approximately 13 years), it is likely that we captured the majority of maxillary growth, while the mandibular growth spurt had not yet fully commenced. As a result, any mandibular differences between groups, if present, may not have been detected.

The sex-specific analysis revealed that nearly all craniofacial parameters were significantly more affected in female children in the Gaza envelope group. This may reflect both biological and psychosocial differences: females tend to enter puberty earlier than males35,36 and may exhibit stronger neuroendocrine and emotional responses to chronic stress37. As such, the compounded effect of early hormonal shifts and psychological sensitivity may result in amplified skeletal alterations in girls. Previous studies have concluded that women exhibit higher rates of psychological disorders compared to men, a disparity largely attributed to the modulatory effects of sex hormones38,39. In particular, elevated estrogen levels, especially during periods of stress are thought to influence emotional reactivity by buffering the HPA axis and amplifying neuroendocrine responses40.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this was a retrospective study, and while the two cohorts were matched for socioeconomic status and age, we did not have data from direct psychometric or biochemical assessments of stress. Second, radiographic assessments, although standardized and reliable, reflect static images of dynamic growth processes. Third, the sample was drawn from one health network in Israel and may not fully represent all regional or ethnic variations. Fourth, residential allocation was based on address at the time of examination, and lifetime residential histories were unavailable. While unlikely, some participants may have moved between regions during childhood, potentially leading to misclassification of conflict exposure. Finally, the findings, while compelling, require validation in longitudinal or multicenter studies incorporating endocrine, genetic, and psychological variables.

Conclusion

This study provides compelling evidence that chronic exposure to conflict-related stress during childhood is associated with reduced cranial base length, particularly that of the anterior cranial base, and is not associated with the lengths of intramembranous structures such as the maxilla and mandible. These findings highlight the biological vulnerability of endochondral growth sites to psychosocial stress and suggest a potential disruption of coordinated craniofacial development.

Children living under chronic environmental threat, especially female children, may exhibit craniofacial growth patterns that predispose them to midfacial retrusion and altered skeletal proportions. These findings have important implications for early orthodontic assessment, growth prediction, and treatment timing, particularly in conflict-affected populations.
