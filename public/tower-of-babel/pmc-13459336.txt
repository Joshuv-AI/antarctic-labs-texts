Key components for responding to burn mass casualty incidents: a comprehensive systematic literature review
Authors: Mohammad Mahdi Doust Mohammadi, Saeed Fallah, Mohammad Reza Khajehaminian, Hojjat Farahmandnia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEffective management of burn mass casualty incidents (BMCIs) requires coordinated systems that include early stabilization, definitive burn care, surge capacity, and rehabilitation, supported by adequate infrastructure and workforce capacity. Although different countries have national guidelines, the full synthesis of the components of the health system needed by BMCIs response remains limited. This study aimed to systematically identify and classify the key components of the response to BMCIs.MethodA systematic literature review was conducted to identify and synthesize the key components required for effective response to BMCIs. A systematic review was conducted in accordance with PRISMA guidelines and registered in PROSPERO (CRD42024625737). This research was conducted through a systematic review and searches of the databases PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and Google Scholar. A Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) was used for systematic search, and a Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool (MMAT) was used for quality assessment of final extracted articles.ResultsFrom an initial set of 7,975 articles, 27 studies were included for analysis. The key components for responding to BMCIs were analyzed in 5 categories and 36 subcategories. The primary themes and related main categories included: (1) Preparedness and Prevention; (2) Command, Control, Coordination, and Communication; (3) Prehospital and Transport Management; (4) Hospital and Surge Capacity Management; and (5) Clinical and Supportive Care.ConclusionThis review provides a comprehensive, evidence-informed framework outlining essential health system components for effective BMCIs response. The identified domains may guide the development of national and regional response models, strengthen disaster preparedness, and enhance health system resilience to BMCIs scenarios.Keywords: Burns, Mass casualty incidents, BMCI, Burn disaster, Health system

Introduction

Manmade and natural disasters are different incidents that can cause burn mass casualty incidents (BMCIs) [1]. The American Burn Association (ABA) estimates that a large percentage of casualties in traumatic incidents would require burn care [2]. Burn injuries require a comprehensive treatment plan that includes initial injury care, stabilization, wound care, and rehabilitation. Literature suggested that treatment should be based on severity of the burn, with transfer to specialized burn centers for severe burns. The importance of infrastructure and qualification of staff was also referred to as important factors for effective burn care [3]. BMCIs are infrequent but challenging events in which the abrupt influx of casualties exceeds the capacity of local burn care owing to the lack of burn specialists and burn beds in most countries. The differential distribution of specialized centers makes response more difficult [4].

In particular, low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) are poorly equipped to care for a large volume of burn injuries. There are few resources available for planning particularly on large scale burn casualties [5, 6]. The guideline recommends that each country should develop disaster planning framework according to its needs. These guidelines outline basic concepts for medical professionals and government authorities involved in preparedness and response [7]. The importance of preparing for BMCIs has led the International Society for Burn Injuries (ISBI) to publish guidelines for the management of BMCIs. These guidelines promote the development of a disaster planning system tailored to the needs of the country [8]. There is insufficient awareness of BMCIs globally resulting in inadequate funding and preparation for burn disaster management. 2. This lack of planning can create gaps in response when BMCI events do occur. Handling BMCIs requires good incident planning and well-funded, high-quality burn centers capable of dealing with these situations [9].

Despite the national and international guidelines, there is still a lack of unified evidence-based synthesis of the key health system components for effective BMCI response [10]. Current recommendations often concentrate on clinical management or discrete operational elements rather than the full scope of preparedness, coordination, surge capacity, and care delivery across the continuum [11]. Such segmented understanding impairs the development of coherent, scalable and resilient response systems especially in resource-limited settings. To fill this gap, we undertook a systematic review of the literature to identify, analyze and categorize the key components needed for an effective health system response to BMCIs. The resulting framework is designed to inform policy, enhance disaster preparedness, and assist in developing context-specific BMCI response strategies worldwide.

Methods

This systematic literature review aims to identify the key components of responding to BMCIs. This review was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines for systematic and comprehensive evaluation of the current literature [12]. The protocol of this systematic review has been officially registered on PROSPERO (registration number CRD42024625737), in order to ensure transparency and in accordance with predefined methodologies in the course of the research.

Search strategy

This study was carried out to systematically review published research on components of response in BMCIs. We conducted a thorough search of English international databases, including Web of Science, PubMed, Scopus, Ovid and Embase. The scope of the literature search was from January 2000 to April 2025. The main search terms consisted of “Burn Unit”, “Unit Burn”, “Burn Center”, “Center Burn”, “thermal injury,” “thermal injuries”, “casualty incident”, “Mass casualty incidents”, “Mass Incident”, “Mass Casualty”, “Mass Casualty Incident”, “Mass Casualty”, “Casualties Mass”, “disaster”, and “catastrophe”. Synonyms for the keywords were extracted using Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) from the National Library of Medicine. Using the logical operators OR and AND, a combination of keywords was utilized and inputted into the search field of the designated databases.

Articles and documents selection

The eligibility screening procedure consisted of the independent assessment of titles and abstracts by two reviewers, identified as MD and HF. In cases where discrepancies were identified among reviewers, the variations were acknowledged, and a final decision was reached by a third reviewer, MK, through consensus. Abstracts or titles identified as potentially valuable by the reviewers led to the retrieval of full-text articles, which were subsequently subjected to detailed examination to determine their eligibility. The systematic and collaborative approach ensured a comprehensive assessment and identification of relevant studies for the review.

Database search

The initial database search retrieved a total of 7,975 records from the included electronic databases. Reference management software and manual verification were used to identify and remove 3,016 duplicated records. Titles and abstracts of 4,959 records were screened. A total of 4924 records were excluded according to the eligibility criteria during the title and abstract screening phase. This yielded 35 potentially relevant records for full text assessment. Eight of the 35 articles were later excluded for the following reasons: It was not a survey of burn victims but a report of burn cases (n = 1); The discussion focused on burn treatment, neglecting the challenges posed by mass casualties in disaster scenarios (n = 4), The epidemiology of burns has been discussed, but there has been no discussion about the correct response and what actions should be taken in the event of a burn (n = 3). Finally, 27 articles met all eligibility criteria and were included in this systematic review, with a detailed breakdown of these articles shown in Fig. 1.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The main inclusion criteria for this study were articles or documents that specifically mentioned the BMCIs key components for response. Qualitative and quantitative original articles published from 1 January 2000 to 1 April 2025 were included for analysis. The exclusion criteria included studies published in editorial format and research that did not specifically focus on burn models. Further exclusions included systematic reviews, proceedings of conferences, correspondence, opinion pieces, and technical documents.

Quality assessment

The quality of included studies was assessed using the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool (MMAT), a validated instrument for critically appraising qualitative, quantitative, and mixed methods research. The MMAT’s design-specific criteria are structured into three sections: qualitative, quantitative (randomized controlled, nonrandomized, and descriptive), and mixed methods. Each criterion is rated as ‘yes,’ ‘no,’ ‘partial,’ or ‘cannot tell. The MMAT was used to assess the methodological rigor and overall quality of the included literature, not to exclude studies. MD and HF independently assessed the studies using the MMAT, then convened to compare scores and resolve any discrepancies through discussion to reach a consensus, as shown in Table 1. The final scores reflected methodological quality, ranging from 0% (low quality) to 100% (high quality), with all studies included regardless of quality [13]. No study was excluded based on methodological quality because the objective was to identify components rather than estimate intervention effects.

Thematic analysis

This systematic review identified key components for response to BMCIs, which were grouped into five main categories and 36 subcategories. Main categories are: preparedness and prevention (8 sub categories), command, control, coordination and communication (8 sub categories), prehospital and transport management (6 sub categories), hospital and surge capacity management (5 sub categories) and clinical and supportive care (9 sub categories) (Fig. 3).

Framework illustrating main components

Main category 1: Preparedness and prevention

The reviewed literature highlighted the importance of preparedness and prevention for BMCIs because response measures are based on beforehand planning. Studies indicated that response efforts can be hampered by limitations in planning, facilities, personnel, or training. Often described as intimately related to effective mass casualty incident (MCI) response, particularly for complex incidents such as BMCIs, several key elements were discussed concerning preparedness and prevention.

Subcategory 1.1: Planning and policy frameworks

The reviewed literature emphasized burn incident preparedness due to the potential for large numbers of casualties, complex clinical needs, and significant system disruption [14]. Preparedness was particularly described as important for specialized processes such as burn triage, fluid resuscitation, and acute management, which rely on predefined protocols and trained personnel. Organizations such as the ABA have developed guidelines and programs for responding to BMCIs that align with core clinical and operational principles and have been incorporated into disaster planning frameworks in some settings to support readiness and response capacity [15, 16].

In BMCI planning, one must specifically address the limited availability of specialized burn-care resources and the unique clinical trajectory of burn patients. Planning frameworks should incorporate regional burn center networks, predefined burn center referral criteria, burn specialty team activation, surge expansion of burn intensive care and operating room capacity, and coordination of specialized resources such as burn dressings, blood products, and rehabilitation services [17]. Because patients with major burns require prolonged multidisciplinary care extending from initial resuscitation to reconstruction and rehabilitation, BMCI policies must also support sustained regional coordination beyond the immediate disaster response. These burn-specific planning requirements distinguish BMCI preparedness from conventional mass casualty planning [18].

Subcategory 1.2: Triage system development and protocols

The literature reviewed has defined burn triage as the rapid assessment of injury severity and estimation of total body surface area (TBSA) involvement by using standardized protocols and guidelines [19–21]. Structured triage systems have been described as being able to prioritize patients, allocate resources and provide the correct level of care at the right time and have been linked to improved patient outcomes [8].

Burn triage differs from generic MCI triage systems that are primarily based on physiologic status and injury severity at the time of evaluation. Burn triage incorporates burn-specific factors such as TBSA, burn depth, inhalation injury, patient age, comorbidities, and anticipated consumption of specialized burn-care resources. These factors are important because burn patients often require long hospital stays, intensive care, multiple surgical procedures and specialized wound management [22]. Thus, burn-care resources are scarce and burn triage protocols must balance individual survival probability with the availability of these resources, making burn triage fundamentally different from conventional MCI triage approaches [23].

Subcategory 1.3: Surge capacity and resource planning

The burn surge plan details policies and procedures for system activation, field triage, emergency department (ED) assessment and treatment, and burn center coordination [24]. Findings emphasized the optimization of care to critically burned patients with minimal changes to current Emergency Medical Services (EMS) authority protocols so as to preserve operational continuity. Supporting appendices cover important clinical domains such as fluid resuscitation, inhalation injury management, nutritional support and standardized wound care documentation [18].

Surge capacity is an important consideration in all MCIs, but especially problematic in BMCIs. Surge capacity in BMCIs is the expansion of very specialized but inherently limited resources, such as burn center beds, burn ICU capacity, operating rooms for excision and grafting procedures, wound-care supplies, skin substitutes, and personnel trained in burn surgery and critical care [25]. Additionally, patients with severe burns often require prolonged hospitalization, multiple surgical procedures, intensive fluid resuscitation, and lengthy rehabilitation, resulting in sustained resource utilization that frequently exceeds the needs of most other mass casualty incident (MCI) patient populations [19].

Subcategory 1.4: Supply and logistics preparedness

The reviewed literature identified preplanned strategies for expanding critical care services and maintaining updated inventories of essential supplies as key components of surge capacity in burn centers during BMCIs [26]. Despite these measures, current burn care training and stockpiles remain insufficient for large-scale incidents [27].

The reviewed literature highlighted the importance of timely availability and replenishment of specialized burn-care resources, including burn dressings, topical antimicrobial agents, skin substitutes, fluid resuscitation products, ventilators for patients with inhalation injury, and advanced wound care equipment [28]. Severe burns require prolonged treatment and substantial healthcare resources, creating unique logistical challenges. Accordingly, the reviewed literature describes logistics preparedness for BMCIs as extending beyond routine emergency supply management, reflecting the sustained need for specialized burn-care resources, regional resource-sharing agreements, and supply chain contingency planning [3].

Subcategory 1.5: Training and education

According to a guideline, burn team personnel should undergo specialized training in burn care with an emphasis on BMCIs, including both clinical skills and coordination competencies [16]. The reviewed literature described training programs as being accessible, cost-effective, and adaptable to regional contexts, with support from experienced professionals through mentorship and faculty development [15]. Several studies described blended learning approaches incorporating simulations and workshops and highlighted the importance of tailoring training programs to available resources and local needs to improve preparedness across different care settings [29].

Training and preparedness are essential in MCI, but in BMCI education includes the development of special competencies in burn assessment, TBSA estimation, burn-specific triage, fluid resuscitation, recognition of inhalation injury, wound management, escharotomy, and decision-making regarding burn-center referral. Training must also prepare health care professionals to function in settings with limited burn-care resources where ethical triage decisions and prioritization of specialized treatment are critical [30]. The simulation exercise should also incorporate burn-specific challenges not normally seen in other MCI scenarios, such as mass burn triage, saturation of the burn center, prolonged patient care needs, and coordination between regional burn networks [7, 15].

Subcategory 1.6: Exercises, drills and evaluation

The reviewed literature described stepwise assessment of BMCI response models in controlled or simulated environments as a method to assess performance, identify strengths and gaps, and detect operational challenges within programs [21]. The BMCI management personnel were also reported to have conducted regular exercises and drills to improve preparedness and support a coordinated response during real incidents [8].

The importance of training and preparedness exercises for enhancing readiness and ensuring a swift response in MCIs is magnified in BMCIs. BMCI drills should evaluate competencies specific to burns such as burn triage, TBSA estimation, fluid resuscitation, and inhalation injury management, as well as surge capacity, resource allocation, interfacility transfer, and coordination across burn centers. The clinical and operational challenges specific to BMCI drills are not the same as those encountered in typical disaster preparedness exercises [2].

Subcategory 1.7: Coordination, integration and governance

Responsible organization and with external stakeholders who can provide resources or expertise [15]. Such collaboration was reported to support an integrated management approach, and was associated with the better efficiency and effectiveness of response [26]. A unified strategy was specified as cooperation between all stakeholders working towards common goals with efficient use of resources, exchange of information and organized management, which helped to lessen the impact of disasters [31].

BMCI governance necessitates close coordination among burn centers, regional burn networks, EMS, trauma centers, and disaster management authorities to deal with the shortage of specialized burn care resources. Effective governance should provide for real-time assessment of burn-bed availability, coordination of inter-facility transfers, sharing of specialised personnel and equipment, and equitable distribution of patients across regional burn-care systems [32]. The management of severe burn patients necessitates long-term intensive care, multiple surgical interventions, and specialized rehabilitation services, thus requiring BMCI coordination beyond the initial response phase and continuous regional and interorganizational collaboration throughout the continuum of care [26].

Subcategory 1.8: Special considerations and innovation

Management of BMCI was found to pose different challenges to those encountered in other MCIs scenarios, suggesting the need for burn-specific response strategies [33]. Ethical considerations in burn triage were consistently identified as an important component of BMCI response, particularly regarding the equitable allocation of limited medical resources under high-pressure conditions [34]. Several aspects of burn care, including wound care, fluid resuscitation, and triage of critical care, were described as needing approaches that differ from those employed in other mass casualty situations or that exceed conventional disaster response protocols. Resource management was also identified as an important aspect of BMCI response, including allocation and use of personnel, supplies and equipment under resource-constrained conditions. Resource management in BMCIs is inherently complex, demanding careful planning and efficient utilization of personnel, supplies, and equipment to optimize patient outcome [14]. The reviewed literature also highlighted the importance of expertise and age-specific management strategies in the care of pediatric burn patients due to the unique physiologic and psychologic aspects of this population [35].

BMCIs are distinct from most other MCIs in that they involve prolonged resource utilization, few specialized burn-care services, difficult ethical triage decisions and require very specialized clinical interventions [30]. Innovation in BMCI management thus extends beyond traditional disaster response strategies to include the development of burn-specific triage algorithms, telemedicine-assisted burn consultation, regional burn-bed tracking systems, crisis standards of care for scarce resource allocation, and novel approaches to surge expansion. Pediatric burn disasters pose unique issues for age-specific fluid resuscitation, burn size estimation, airway management, and long-term psychosocial recovery, requiring specific protocols not typically employed for other MCI populations. These unique clinical, ethical, and operational issues underscore the need for BMCI-centered planning and innovation [18].

Main category 2: Command, control, coordination and communication

Command, coordination and communication were key components of BMCI management identified in reviewed literature. “A well-defined organizational structure with reliable communication systems between and among responding agencies to facilitate coordinated teamwork and operational efficiency. Effective coordination was also reported to facilitate timely triage, treatment and patient transport and appropriate distribution of casualties among the available burn centres was associated with more efficient use of resources. Together, these coordinated processes were described as contributing to improved response performance and were associated with reduced mortality, fewer complications and improved patient outcomes, highlighting the role of a structured, team-based approach in BMCI management.

Subcategory 2.1: Command and incident leadership

The literature reviewed reported the presence of a well-established Incident Command System with clearly defined roles which is triggered during BMCI responses as per pre-established arrangements [26]. The included studies all described effective communication between command structures, triage sites, receiving hospitals and burn centres as an important element of BMCI management. The presence of burn centers in regional disaster plans and early notification to leadership and functional communication between centers was found to support coordinated decision-making and patient distribution [15, 36].

As with other MCIs, effective leadership in BMCIs requires prompt decision making, efficient allocation of resources, and coordinated communication among response agencies. However, because definitive burn care facilities are limited, BMCI incident leadership requires the direct integration of specialized burn centers into the command structure. Burn-bed availability, burn severity stratified by TBSA, inhalation injury, and burn depth in real-time must be factored into command decisions, as well as availability of specialized resources such as burn ICUs and surgical teams [37]. In addition, BMCI command systems should be able to enable the regional reallocation of patients across burn networks, rather than relying only on local hospital capacity, so that centralized coordination of burn-specific resources becomes a hallmark of BMCI leadership [28].

Subcategory 2.2: Specialized teams and roles

In the literature reviewed, Burn Specialty Teams (BSTs) were described as specialized extensions of Disaster Medical Assistance Teams (DMATs) that provide burn-specific expertise and enable resource sharing to improve burn care capacity during disasters [33]. BSTs were observed to enhance local and regional healthcare systems, have rapid deployment capability (within 12 h), and assist with secondary triage, patient stabilization, and transfer coordination during burn catastrophes throughout the included studies [5, 38].

Unlike conventional MCI response teams, BST provide experience in the management of severe burn injuries. Their roles include specialist triage of burns, TBSA and burn depth evaluation, management of inhalation injuries, fluid resuscitation recommendations, wound-care consultation, escharotomy decision-making, and coordination of referrals to specialized burn centers [10]. In addition to direct clinical support, BSTs enable regional resource coordination through assistance with burn-bed allocation, interfacility transfers, and deployment of specialized burn-care professionals. These specific functions differentiate BSTs from classical disaster medical teams and are an important aspect of BMCI response systems [11].

Subcategory 2.3: Coordination of patient flow and resources

The literature reviewed described rapid deployment of experienced burn clinicians and logisticians (within 6–12 h) to assist with definitive triage, clinical assessment and coordinated distribution of patients across healthcare facilities. The teams were also reported to offer technical consultation, resource assessment, liaison with emergency operation centers and decision support for referral coordination and resource allocation during BMCI response [16, 27].

Effective coordination of patient flow, like with other MCIs, depends on rapid triage, appropriate distribution of patients and efficient use of available healthcare resources. However, patient flow coordination in BMCIs also has to consider the availability of specialized burn-care resources, which is limited, and the requirement to match patients with the appropriate levels of burn expertise [36]. Disposition decisions are based not only on severity of injury but also on burn-specific factors such as TBSA, depth of burn, inhalation injury, anticipated surgical needs and burn center capacity. This specialized approach requires real-time tracking of burn-bed availability, operating room capacity, intensive care resources, and interfacility transfer capacity throughout regional burn networks to prevent overload of individual burn centers and assure timely access to definitive burn care [39].

Subcategory 2.4: Communication systems

The literature reviewed described the hospital incident control center (ICC) to be an important organizational element to coordinate internal units (e.g. emergency department, burn team, operating rooms, ICU and hospital administration) and external stakeholders (e.g. emergency authorities, burn centers, ambulance services, and surrounding hospitals). Hospital incident control centers were reported to assist in surge assessment, capacity evaluation, resource allocation, and timely dissemination of clinical information during BMCI response [27, 36, 40].

As with other MCIs, effective communication is important for the timely sharing of information, coordinated decision making, and efficient use of resources. However, communication in BMCIs must also allow for the continuous exchange of burn-specific clinical and operational information between burn centers, EMS, and regional disaster networks [11]. This includes real-time reporting of burn bed availability, burn ICU capacity, operating room readiness, specialized burn personnel and patient specific factors such as TBSA, burn depth, and inhalation injury. This type of specialized communication allows for timely consultation with a burn center, decision making regarding interfacility transfer, and regional coordination of scarce burn-care resources, thus preventing overload of individual burn centers and helping to ensure appropriate definitive care [41].

Subcategory 2.5: Transport and evacuation coordination

The literature reviewed defined EMS as the first transport of burn victims to appropriate health care facilities. Several studies have also reported that limited burn-specific expertise among prehospital providers may impact the quality of early burn care [21, 25]. The reviewed literature described interfacility transfer, particularly at long distances, as often being aeromedical evacuation supported by specialized teams and advanced clinical capabilities [40].

BMCI transport and evacuation Decisions on where to transport should be based on clinical criteria related to burns and not just proximity. The decision to transport directly or secondarily transfer to definitive burn care facilities is based on factors such as total body surface area (TBSA), burn depth, inhalation injury, associated trauma, and availability of specialized burn-center resources. Furthermore, long transport times may necessitate advanced burn-specific interventions, including airway management, ongoing fluid resuscitation, temperature management to prevent hypothermia, and continuous monitoring by experienced burn critical care personnel [6, 42]. Coordinated aeromedical evacuation and regional burn-center distribution are integral parts of BMCI transport strategies to optimize patient outcomes while avoiding overload of individual burn centers [17].

Subcategory 2.6: Surge activation and phased response

The reviewed literature indicated that surge activation included pre-defined protocols for scalable and coordinated medical and logistical responses during BMCIs. As incident magnitude increased and local resources were overwhelmed, adaptive management was facilitated by a phased response approach [20, 24].

BMCI’s phased response must specifically address progressive expansion of specialized burn-care capacity as demand outstrips available resources. This includes the staged activation of burn centers; expansion of burn intensive care unit (ICU) and operating room capacity; mobilization of the BST; redistribution of patients through regional burn networks; and implementation of crisis standards of care when specialized burn resources become critically limited [10, 11]. It is also important to note that the BMCI surge activation should take into account the continuing need for resources over a course of days to weeks and not just the initial surge of casualties. Patients with severe burns will require prolonged critical care, multiple operations and prolonged rehabilitation. These burn-specific operational requirements differentiate BMCI phased response from conventional MCI surge strategies [43].

Subcategory 2.7: Monitoring, data and quality assurance

The reviewed literature described comprehensive monitoring systems as monitoring key indicators across the continuum of care, from triage to rehabilitation. Accurate data were reported that support situational awareness, resource allocation, and performance evaluation, and integrated quality assurance mechanisms were described as promoting protocol adherence and improved outcomes in complex BMCI settings [35, 44].

BMCI monitoring and quality assurance should include burn-specific clinical and operational indicators such as TBSA, burn depth, inhalation injury, timing and adequacy of fluid resuscitation, wound progression, infection, grafting requirements, burn ICU utilization, and long-term functional outcomes [34]. Ongoing monitoring of burn center capacity, bed occupancy, operating room availability, and interfacility transfer activity is also critical to optimize regional resource allocation. These burn-specific quality indicators allow for assessment of adherence to specialized burn-care protocols and encourage continuous improvement of BMCI preparedness and response [6].

Subcategory 2.8: Public and media communication

The literature reviewed identified effective public and media communications during BMCIs as important to manage risk perception, disseminate accurate information, and support coordinated response efforts. Communication strategies were described to provide up-to-date information, safety information and information on services available to affected individuals [4].

BMCI communication should provide burn-specific information to assist in clinical management and public safety. This includes advice on immediate burn first aid (e.g. cooling burns with running water and avoiding harmful traditional remedies), identification of inhalation injury symptoms requiring urgent medical attention, and clear referral pathways to specialized burn centers [5, 31]. Transparent updates on burn centre capacity, patient reunification processes and expected need for prolonged treatment and rehabilitation should also be part of public communication. Accurate burn-specific messaging can help to decrease misinformation, prevent inappropriate first-aid practices, and promote equitable utilization of scarce specialized burn-care resources during large-scale burn events [19].

Main category 3: Prehospital and transport management

BMCI response was described in the literature reviewed as coordinated and systematic prehospital management. The main components identified in the included studies were rapid triage, immediate stabilization, and timely transport of burn victims to appropriate health care facilities. It was said that effective transport management involved planning of the allocation of resources and distribution of patients according to hospital capacity and expertise in burn care. These approaches were reported to support timely transfer of patients and were associated with improved coordination and patient outcomes.

Subcategory 3.1: Scene triage and casualty prioritization

The literature reviewed described accurate burn triage as involving trained first responders and emergency personnel using standardized, burn-specific triage guidelines within major incident frameworks. Delayed airway and respiratory compromise were consistently reported to receive particular attention during the triage process [14, 38]. Advanced training programs like Advanced Trauma Life Support (ATLS) and Emergency Management of Severe Burns (EMSB) were associated with better assessment accuracy, less over- and under-triage, and better prioritization of care [31, 41].

BMCI prioritization is based on burn-specific clinical parameters including TBSA, depth of burn, inhalation injury, burn location (e.g., face, hands, perineum, and major joints), age of patient, and associated comorbidities. These factors affect not only the urgency of treatment, but also the expected requirement for specialized burn-center care and limited resources including burn ICU beds and surgical capacity [41]. BMCI scene triage also needs repeated reassessment to identify patients who may deteriorate clinically despite appearing initially stable. This is important because airway compromise and burn shock may develop after initial assessment. The burn-specific nuances distinguish BMCI triage from conventional MCI casualty prioritization [26].

Subcategory 3.2: Immediate first aid and stabilization

The literature reviewed indicated that initial on-scene management included rapid cooling of burn injuries, preferably within 20 min, followed by a structured assessment during prolonged transport. Early basic airway management, infection prevention and the avoidance of practices such as applying ointments or poultices to burn wounds were also frequently mentioned as part of initial prehospital care when indicated [43].

In BMCIs, however, immediate stabilization requires burn-specific interventions that directly affect tissue preservation and clinical outcomes, unlike generic MCI first aid. Prompt cooling of the burn wound, prevention of hypothermia, early recognition of inhalation injury, protection of the damaged skin barrier and timely initiation of fluid resuscitation for extensive burns are all essential components of burn care [31].These are specific to burn injuries and should be initiated at the scene to reduce progression of the burn, minimize complications and improve survival before definitive management at a burn center [43].

Subcategory 3.3: Airway, breathing and circulation (critical prehospital care)

The reviewed literature identified prioritizing airway, breathing and circulation (ABC) as a core component of BMCI management. This approach was described as involving systematic airway protection, ventilatory support and circulatory stabilization to maintain organ perfusion and reduce the risk of shock across the included studies [11, 25]. The reviewed literature found that effective ABC management is a key part of the BMCI response, especially in resource-limited settings with high patient numbers [45].

Prehospital care for BMCIs must address burn-specific pathophysiological challenges including progressive airway edema secondary to inhalation injury, carbon monoxide and cyanide poisoning, extensive plasma loss leading to burn shock and a high risk of hypothermia due to disruption of the skin barrier [35]. Hence, early airway assessment, timely endotracheal intubation when indicated, burn specific fluid resuscitation according to established protocols, oxygen administration for suspected inhalation injury, and active temperature management are key components of prehospital ABC care. These burns-specific interventions distinguish BMCI prehospital management from traditional MCI resuscitation strategies [1].

Subcategory 3.4: Transport decision-making and prioritization

The reviewed literature highlights efficient transport decision-making as a major element in the management of BMCI. Included studies described transport systems as triaging patients based on burn severity, inhalation injury, associated trauma and urgency of intervention [24, 27, 40]. Literature reviewed indicated predefined protocols with objective acuity criteria helped facilitate consistent decision-making and equitable allocation of resources during complex incidents [35].

In making transport decisions, BMCI must balance the individual needs of patients with the limited availability of specialized burn-care resources. Besides injury severity, transport prioritization is driven by burn-specific factors such as TBSA, burn depth, inhalation injury, anatomical location of burns, anticipated need for burn surgery, and burn-center admission criteria [37]. Real-time burn center capacity, availability of burn ICU beds, and need of secondary interfacility transfer or aeromedical evacuation should also be taken into account when making transport decisions. This resource-sensitive approach ensures that patients requiring specialized burn care are referred to the most appropriate facilities, while maximizing regional burn-care capacity in the event of large-scale burn incidents [22].

Subcategory 3.5: Transport resources and logistics

The literature reviewed found that the availability and coordinated use of transport resources, including ground and aeromedical systems, are important components of timely patient transfer from the incident scene to appropriate care facilities [8, 35]. Coordinated resource management and logistical planning were associated with reduced morbidity and mortality during BMCI response [27].

Critical burn patients transported by BMCI resources must be transferred to a burn center capable of providing burn specific clinical care during transport. This includes access to aeromedical evacuation for long-distance transfers, teams trained in burn critical care, equipment for advanced airway management and mechanical ventilation, ongoing burn-specific fluid resuscitation, temperature regulation to prevent hypothermia, and continuous physiologic monitoring [10, 23]. Moreover, logistic planning needs to take into account the capacity of regional burn centers, the availability of specialized transport, and interfacility transfer networks to make the best use of limited burn care resources. Such specialized logistical requirements set BMCI transport systems apart from conventional MCI transport operations [6].

Subcategory 3.6: Documentation and tracking

The extensive documentation and patient tracking systems reviewed were important components of BMCI response because of the high volume and complexity of injuries. Clinical status and treatment progression were accurately documented to facilitate communication, continuity of care and resource management and to provide data for performance evaluation and quality improvement [21].

The documentation in the BMCI must include burn-specific clinical information needed for continued management and interfacility transfer. Important data elements include TBSA, depth of burn, anatomical burn location, presence of inhalation injury, fluid resuscitation, wound-care interventions, and decisions regarding referral to a burn center. Patient tracking systems should also track interfacility transfers, utilization of burn-center beds, surgical scheduling, and long-term rehabilitation needs, as burn patients often require prolonged multidisciplinary care [46]. These burn-care-specific documentation and tracking requirements enable continuity of specialized care, regional resource utilization, and quality improvement across the entire burn-care continuum [15].

Main category 4: Hospital and surge capacity management

Hospital management and surge capacity were major elements of BMCI response as demonstrated in the reviewed literature. Hospital management was identified as allocation of resources, staffing and management of bed capacity, and surge capacity strategies were reported to allow expansion of care beyond normal operational limits. Rapid expansion of beds, mobilization of personnel and procurement of essential equipment were key measures across included studies. Coordinated planning was used to enable timely delivery of care during BMCIs.

Subcategory 4.1: Hospital triage and patient prioritization

Hospital-based triage and prioritization are essential for timely intervention to patients with the most severe injuries to maximize survival and clinical outcomes. The burn severity is rapidly assessed and life threatening conditions are identified and sorted according to urgency in order to guide the sequencing of treatment, taking into account the burn extent, depth, inhalation injury and comorbidities [30].

BMCI hospital prioritization encompasses burn-specific prognostic factors and the availability of resources for burn-specialized care. Decisions are made regarding TBSA, burn depth, inhalation injury, age, associated trauma, comorbidities, and the potential benefit from specialized interventions such as burn intensive care, early excision and grafting, and advanced wound management [47]. Burn-center capacity is often limited during large-scale burn incidents, and hospital triage must also weigh individual patient needs against resource stewardship to optimize overall survival. This combination of burn-specific clinical assessment and resource-sensitive prioritization is what differentiates BMCI hospital triage from conventional MCI hospital triage [14].

Subcategory 4.2: Burn severity assessment tools and criteria

Standardized tools for assessing burn severity are essential for triage accuracy, treatment planning, and resource allocation in BMCIs. Evaluation generally involves estimation of TBSA, burn depth and associated injuries [35]. Consistent estimates of TBSA in both pediatric and adult populations can be derived from methods such as the Lund–Browder chart and Rule of Nines, and when combined with expert judgment, lead to more reliable clinical decision-making [39].

BMCI assessment relies on burn-specific tools to measure the extent of injury and the anticipated resource needs. Assessment of TBSA, burn depth, inhalation injury, anatomical location of burns, patient age and associated comorbidities provides critical information for predicting survival, estimating fluid resuscitation needs, determining eligibility for burn-center referral and prioritizing surgical interventions [27]. Burn-specific scoring systems and standardized assessment tools therefore inform clinical decision-making and also regional allocation of scarce burn-care resources during mass casualty events. Such specific assessment criteria differentiate BMCI management from the usual severity assessment of MCI, which generally focuses on physiological derangement rather than the unique pathophysiology and resource implications of burn injuries [5].

Subcategory 4.3: Hospital surge capacity planning and expansion

Surge capacity planning described in the literature reviewed included anticipation of increased demand for personnel, equipment and specialized burn care resources during BMCIs [36]. Surge expansion strategies across the included studies included repurposing clinical areas, setting up temporary treatment spaces, and reallocating resources, alongside logistical measures such as supply chain management and patient transfer coordination [40].

The BMCI surge capacity should include expansion specifically of the limited number of highly specialized burn-care services. This includes augmenting the capacity of the burn ICU, availability of operating rooms for early excision and skin grafting, dedicated wound-care facilities, and access to burn-trained multidisciplinary teams, including burn surgeons, intensivists, specialized nurses, rehabilitation professionals, and mental health providers [47]. Strategies for hospital expansion must also ensure an adequate supply of burn dressings, topical antimicrobial agents, skin substitutes, blood products, and resources for fluid resuscitation. This should be considered in BMCI surge planning, as major burns often require prolonged hospitalizations, multiple surgeries and long rehabilitation periods, and as such, a sustained use of specialized resources over the span of weeks, not just the initial wave of patients. These burn-specific requirements differentiate BMCI hospital surge planning from standard MCI surge planning [48].

Subcategory 4.4: Staffing and workforce management

The literature reviewed emphasizes the need for a competent and well-trained workforce, including clinical, support and administrative staff, for the provision of complex burn care. Workforce planning often included methods for rapid mobilization, role assignment, fatigue mitigation, and staff well-being to sustain quality of care during prolonged response periods [41].

The BMCI staff includes specialists in burn surgery, burn critical care, wound care, anesthesia, respiratory therapy, rehabilitation, nutrition, infection prevention, and psychosocial support. Because of the need for prolonged hospitalization, multiple surgical procedures, intensive nursing care and multidisciplinary rehabilitation for patients with major burns, workforce management must be able to sustain staffing capacity, rather than short-term surge alone [20]. Cross-training, deployment of Burn Specialty Teams, regional sharing of burn-care professionals, and just-in-time training for non-burn clinicians are also key strategies to augment limited burn expertise during large-scale burn incidents. These burn specific workforce needs distinguish BMCI staffing from traditional MCI personnel management [47].

Subcategory 4.5: Patient flow, monitoring and safety

Patient flow, clinical monitoring, and safety were identified as the key components in BMCI management by the literature review. The effective triage was reported to help prioritize patients and refer the burn victims to the appropriate level of care. Continuous monitoring of vital signs, fluid balance and wound status allowed early recognition and management of complications [42]. The included studies described maintaining a safe care environment (e.g., infection prevention and control, hazardous materials management, and adequate resource availability) to support both patient and staff safety. In general, these measures were associated with improved care coordination and patient outcomes in BMCIs [27].

The patient flow and monitoring in BMCIs need to be adjusted to the prolonged and resource demanding course of burn care. Burn specific monitoring includes serial evaluation of burn wound progression, adequacy of fluid resuscitation, signs of inhalation injury, infection and sepsis surveillance, graft viability following surgical intervention, nutritional status and prevention of complications such as compartment syndrome and hypothermia [49]. Patient flow should also facilitate repeated movement between ICUs, operating rooms, wound-care facilities and rehabilitation services with limited treatment delays and maintaining infection prevention measures. BMCI hospital management differs from standard MCI care pathways regarding burn-specific monitoring and patient flow requirements, which are critical to maximizing outcomes in severely burned patients [10].

Main category 5: Clinical and supportive care

The reviewed literature identified clinical and supportive care strategies as key components of the management of BMCI, including interventions from initial stabilization through definitive treatment and long-term rehabilitation. The included studies described coordinated clinical care as supportive of burn management and associated with reduced morbidity and mortality. Supportive care measures such as pain management, nutritional support and psychosocial interventions were also reported to aid in the patient’s recovery, comfort and well-being, as well as offering support for caregivers over the course of care.

Subcategory 5.1: Airway and inhalation management

The reviewed literature identified airway management was recognized as a crucial component of BMCI care in the literature reviewed, especially for patients with inhalation injuries that can induce airway edema, bronchospasm, or acute respiratory distress. Rapid assessment and timely interventions including advanced airway support and mechanical ventilation when indicated were reported to support the management of inhalation injuries across the included studies and were associated with improved patient outcomes [26, 43].

Thermal and smoke inhalation injuries leading to progressive airway edema, carbon monoxide poisoning, cyanide toxicity, and delayed respiratory compromise must be specifically considered in the airway management of the BMCI. The management of burns includes early recognition of inhalation injury, low threshold for prophylactic endotracheal intubation in patients at risk of airway obstruction, administration of high concentration oxygen, and advanced ventilatory support [42]. Airway collapse may continue for hours following the initial insult and thus, ongoing reassessment and close monitoring are essential throughout the entire prehospital and hospital course of care. BMCI management differs from traditional MCI respiratory management due to these burn-specific airway problems [30].

Subcategory 5.2: Fluid resuscitation and hemodynamic management

The capillary permeability is increased in burn injury, leading to fluid shift and circulatory compromise. In the reviewed literature, fluid resuscitation, hemodynamic monitoring and optimization of tissue perfusion were described as core components of burn management. These interventions were reported to support organ perfusion, and were associated with improved organ function and less complications [43, 45].

BMCI resuscitation must address the severe capillary leak syndrome and burn shock that occur after extensive thermal injury. Fluid therapy is guided by burn-specific resuscitation protocols based on TBSA, patient weight, urine output and physiological response rather than standard trauma resuscitation principles [5]. Continuous reassessment is essential to prevent under-resuscitation that can lead to organ hypoperfusion and over-resuscitation (“fluid creep”) that can lead to pulmonary edema, extremity or abdominal compartment syndrome and worsening tissue edema. These burn-specific pathophysiologic considerations render fluid resuscitation a defining component of BMCI clinical management, and differentiate it from conventional MCI circulatory support [11].

Subcategory 5.3: Wound and surgical management

The reviewed literature showed that adequate wound care and timely surgical intervention were key components in the management of BMCI. Wound care strategies across the included studies included wound assessment, debridement, infection prevention and reconstructive procedures. These interventions were described as delivered by coordinated surgical teams under standardized clinical management protocols [16, 50].

BMCI care requires specialized management of burn wounds along the continuum of care. Clinically, management involves serial assessment of the wound, early excision of devitalized tissue, skin grafting or application of skin substitutes, meticulous prevention of infection and staged reconstructive procedures to restore function and reduce long-term disability [22]. Burn surgery is resource-intensive and often requires multiple operative sessions; therefore, surgical prioritization should be based on a balance of burn severity, patient prognosis, and availability of operating rooms, burn surgeons, and specialized wound-care resources [27]. These burn specific surgical and wound management requirements distinguish BMCI care from standard MCI surgical practice [47].

Subcategory 5.4: Infection prevention and control

Burn patients are highly susceptible to infection. The reviewed literature identified infection prevention and control as a key component of BMCI management. Across the included studies, infection prevention measures, including aseptic wound care, hygiene protocols, and appropriate antimicrobial strategies, were described as supporting infection control and were associated with reduced morbidity, mortality, and transmission of antimicrobial-resistant organisms during large-scale incidents [39].

BMCI infection prevention must address the profound loss of the skin barrier, extensive open wounds, immune dysregulation, and the prolonged hospitalization characteristic of severe burn injuries [47]. Burn-specific infection control measures include meticulous wound dressing techniques, early wound excision and closure when feasible, surveillance for burn wound infection and sepsis, isolation precautions when indicated, antimicrobial stewardship, and rigorous environmental decontamination within burn care units [1, 35]. Because infection remains a leading cause of morbidity and mortality in patients with major burns, maintaining specialized infection prevention practices is a defining component of BMCI management and distinguishes burn care from conventional MCI infection control strategies [7].

Subcategory 5.5: Pain management and palliative care

Pain management was consistently identified as an important aspect of care in the acute treatment and rehabilitation phases across the included studies. The incorporation of palliative care principles was reported to contribute to comprehensive management by addressing physical, psychological, emotional and spiritual needs of patients and their families during severe BMCIs [11].

BMCI care, unlike generic MCI pain management, must consider the long-term, repetitive and often severe pain associated with extensive burn injuries, frequent dressing changes, wound debridement, surgical excision and grafting, and rehabilitation procedures. Therefore, burn-specific pain management requires multimodal analgesia in combination with procedural sedation and continuous reassessment throughout the continuum of care [6]. Palliative care also plays a unique role in patients with extensive and non-survivable burns or when specialized resources are limited, such as symptom relief, maintaining dignity, communication with families, and ethical decision making for goals of care. Such burn-specific clinical and ethical considerations distinguish pain management and palliative care in BMCIs from usual MCI practice [33].

Subcategory 5.6: Nutrition and rehabilitation

The importance of nutritional support and rehabilitation as part of burn care post BMCI was identified from the literature reviewed. Nutritional support was reported to contribute to wound healing and metabolic recovery after burn injury. It was also observed that post-discharge programs and structured rehabilitation services assisted in functional and long-term recovery [31].

Nutritional management in BMCIs must take into account the profound hypermetabolic and hypercatabolic response induced by major burn injuries. Early enteral nutrition, tailored caloric and protein supplementation, and ongoing metabolic monitoring are critical elements in maintaining lean body mass, facilitating wound healing, minimizing infectious complications, and aiding recovery. Similarly, rehabilitation in BMCIs is not only about physical recovery but involves early, multidisciplinary interventions” including physiotherapy, occupational therapy, splinting, scar management, contracture prevention, reconstructive follow-up, and psychosocial rehabilitation” to maximize long-term functional outcomes and quality of life. Nutrition and rehabilitation are therefore defining components of burn-specific care, and differ from traditional MCI recovery pathways, as burn survivors often suffer from prolonged physical, psychological and functional impairments [25, 26, 36].

Subcategory 5.7: Psychological and psychosocial support

BMCIs can have serious psychological effects on patients, their families, and first responders. In the literature reviewed, psychosocial services and mental health support were identified as important components of BMCI care. The included studies described these interventions as supporting coping, resilience and psychological recovery at the individual and community levels [21].

Psychological support in BMCIs must address the unique emotional and social consequences of severe burn injury, such as permanent disfigurement, altered body image, chronic pain, prolonged hospitalization, repeated surgical interventions, and long-term functional impairment. Burn survivors are at risk for anxiety, depression, post-traumatic stress disorder and social isolation, and need early and ongoing psychological intervention throughout the continuum of care [11]. BMCI management also includes family-centered support, counseling on rehabilitation and reintegration, and psychosocial support for burn care teams that are exposed to emotionally demanding situations. These burn-specific psychological and psychosocial challenges differentiate BMCI care from standard MCI mental health support [21].

Subcategory 5.8: Staff support and wellbeing

The high intensity of the BMCI response and the physical and emotional toll on healthcare personnel are significant. The literature reviewed highlighted strategies to support staff well-being as important components for maintaining workforce effectiveness and reducing the risk for burnout, including psychological support, workload management and practical assistance [31].

Hence, BMCI workforce wellbeing programs need to address the ongoing physical and psychological burden associated with prolonged burn care. Burn clinicians are always faced with extensive injuries, painful wound care procedures, high mortality in the critically burned patient population, and long-term relationships with patients and families through recovery. These challenges increase the risk of compassion fatigue, moral distress, secondary traumatic stress and burnout [17]. Effective support of BMCI staff should therefore include structured psychological support, adequate rotation, planned rest periods, peer support and debriefing, access to mental health services and continuous education to improve resilience during prolonged response operations. Specialized burn personnel are a limited and irreplaceable resource, and their wellbeing must be protected to ensure the continuity and quality of burn care during a BMCI response [45].

Subcategory 5.9: Comprehensive clinical protocols

Standardized clinical protocols were identified as a critical component of BMCI management in the literature reviewed. Across the included studies, these protocols were described as providing an integrated framework for burn triage, assessment, stabilization, definitive treatment and long-term care. Standardized protocols were also described to enable coordinated decision making and consistent care delivery across the continuum of BMCI response [33].

The literature reviewed showed that full BMCI protocols contain burn-specific components throughout the continuum of care, including burn triage, TBSA assessment, fluid resuscitation, management of inhalation injury, escharotomy when needed, wound care, infection prevention, surgical excision and grafting, nutritional support, rehabilitation, and palliative care. These protocols include burn center referral criteria, crisis standards of care, and regional resource allocation strategies to guide clinical decision-making when specialized burn resources are overwhelmed [50]. The use of these specialized clinical pathways differentiates BMCI protocols from traditional MCI guidelines and assures standardized, evidence-based management of the patient with major burn injuries during the disaster response and recovery [27].

Discussion

This study identified five core components essential to effective BMCI response: preparedness and prevention; command, control, coordination and communication; prehospital and transport management; hospital and surge capacity management; and clinical and supportive care. These domains together capture the unique features of burn disasters which are fundamentally different from other MCIs in terms of complexity of injuries, long treatment requirements and high resource intensity.

The MMAT scores of this study suggest that, although several of the included studies were of lower methodological quality and mainly consisted of after-action reports, expert opinion documents, and descriptive reports, these sources provided valuable operational insights that are difficult to capture through conventional empirical research in the context of BMCIs. There was relatively robust evidence in the domains of clinical management, coordination and communication, and capacity and resource management, supported by multiple moderate- to high-quality studies and operational reports. On the other hand, domains like preparedness and health system support were more dependent on lower-level evidence such as expert consensus, organizational guidelines and lessons learned from real-life incidents. Therefore, although these findings are important for informing BMCI preparedness and response, recommendations in these areas should be interpreted with caution and require further validation through higher quality empirical research.

Preparedness and prevention

Good coordination and efficient operations are needed to respond to BMCIs at all levels, be it the local burn unit or the national office. As proposed by Leclerc et al. [15], national BMCI preparedness plans should be provided with clear and broad directions to guide patients to the most appropriate treatment facilities according to their clinical needs. Referral guidelines have been reported in the literature as an important part of timely and accurate routing of burn patients to the appropriate facilities. In the reviewed literature, secondary triage systems were described as pertaining to the transfer of patients requiring specialized burn center care. BMCI plans reportedly include setting up organizational structures and resources to implement secondary transfers to facilitate the safe and efficient movement of patients needing advanced care to specialized facilities for timely treatment.

Jeng et al. [50] pointed out the absence of globally applicable guidelines for handling burn disasters and considered the establishment of such guidelines an essential measure to enhance preparedness worldwide. In the reviewed literature, triage and transport decisions in which directing patients to specialized facilities (e.g., trauma centers for severe injuries, burn centers for extensive burn injuries, and local hospitals for less critical injuries that could be managed with available resources) was prioritized were consistently reported [38, 50]. However, according to the literature, there is a lack of detailed guidance on how to refer and distribute patients in specific contexts during large-scale emergencies, which can affect coordination and effective use of resources. The literature reviewed suggests the need for recommendations and standardized frameworks that are applicable globally to strengthen preparedness and improve response capacity to burn disasters worldwide.

BMCIs are distinguished by the need for highly specialized care pathways and referral systems. Severe burn injuries differ from many trauma-related incidents in that stabilization and short-term interventions may be sufficient. Severe burn injuries require early transfer to specialized burn centers, prolonged critical care, multiple surgical procedures and long-term rehabilitation. The literature reviewed identified clearly defined referral algorithms, secondary triage systems, and interfacility transfer coordination mechanisms specific to the needs of burn care as key components of preparedness planning. Also, the literature indicates a lack of globally standardized guidance, emphasizing the need for flexible international frameworks, especially for resource-limited settings where burn disasters disproportionately burden.

Command, control, coordination and communication

Proper management of MCIs requires clear definition of roles and responsibilities and structured protocols for coordination and communication between fire departments, civil protection units, EMS, police, military forces and non-governmental organizations [38]. Persistent inter-center coordination gaps hinder the implementation of standardized protocols and operational consistency. Therefore, the optimization of patient distribution is based on predefined processes which are supported by robust communication systems, real-time data sharing to make informed decisions on dispatch and resource allocation, and accountability by defining roles explicitly [15].

Operational effectiveness also relies on effective on-scene management, smooth inter-team coordination and scalable response systems in proportion to the incident magnitude. In the studies reported herein, command structures in major burn disasters were frequently integrated with existing incident management systems to ensure continuity, clarity of roles, and coordinated deployment of resources under pressure [31, 50]. The authors propose that the inclusion of dedicated burn expertise within these larger command structures is needed to ensure clinical decision-making by appropriately designated leaders at each level of operation.

In BMCIs, comprehensive and specialized burn care training for all burn team members is essential, particularly in light of the unique clinical and logistical challenges involved [16]. In addition to clinical competence the reviewed literature supports formal training for leadership personnel in coordination strategies and emergency management principles to strengthen operational command capabilities [2, 40]. The included studies also highlighted the importance of surge capacity in enhancing the response to BMCIs [14, 26]. Furthermore, multidisciplinary collaboration strong team cohesion and reliable communication channels were consistently identified as key elements that facilitate an effective accountable and integrated response system.

Prehospital and transport management

Prehospital and transport management is the organized delivery of medical care prior to hospital admission, including initial assessment, stabilization and safe transport of patients requiring emergent care [41]. This area aims to improve early clinical decision-making and continuity of care during transport, which is critical to improve survival and long-term outcomes [19]. The simultaneous presentation of a high number of patients with complex burn injuries in the setting of BMCIs additionally emphasizes the critical importance of effective prehospital and transport management [27, 44].

The reviewed literature indicates that triage criteria for burn injuries early airway management and fluid resuscitation differ from those used in general trauma care [11, 22]. Burn trauma is characterized by complex pathophysiological changes frequently involving inhalation injury substantial fluid shifts and multisystem involvement while the operational demands of BMCIs further complicate prehospital decision-making [41]. Across the included studies structured protocol-driven and regularly exercised prehospital systems were consistently described as facilitating more coordinated and effective responses [9, 45]. Similarly efficient resource allocation effective interagency communication and adherence to established clinical and operational protocols were commonly identified as important elements of BMCI management [25]. The reviewed studies further suggest that inadequate coordination across these domains may delay definitive care and lead to inappropriate patient distribution [20, 25].

Hospital and surge capacity management

Surge capacity is an effective “force multiplier” that increases the resilience of the health system and allows local facilities to continue providing care during large-scale emergencies. Secondary transfer is a key strategy to support surge capacity; patients are redistributed to burn centers with available capacity in order to balance workload and maintain standards of care [27, 39]. Burn patients need prolonged hospital stay, intensive monitoring, specialized equipment and multidisciplinary care teams, which makes the hospital surge capacity challenges even more acute in BMCIs. BMCIs are different from other incidents because even small numbers of severe burn patients can quickly deplete available resources, whereas patient turnover might be faster in other incidents.

This approach allows for regional load sharing and improves system capacity, but it can be vulnerable to delays, especially when natural disasters or major crises disrupt transportation and communication networks. As a result, the literature reviewed identified flexible transfer mechanisms and contingency strategies as key elements of surge planning to mitigate these constraints.

Clinical and supportive care

An early burn wound management is an integral part of damage control surgery in the immediate post-BMCI period. Initial treatment includes careful cleaning, decontamination and dressing of the wound to reduce the risk of infection and to stabilize the injured tissues [45]. When clinically indicated, especially in circumferential or near circumferential burns, prompt recognition and performance of escharotomy is essential. Along with progressive tissue oedema, the inelastic eschar can produce a tourniquet-like effect that impairs vascular perfusion and, in truncal burns, may restrict chest wall expansion, leading to respiratory compromise [25]. Hence, early surgical intervention is mandatory in such cases to prevent limb ischemia and ventilatory failure.

Burn care is a long process, resource intensive and often requires prolonged stay in ICU and subsequent admission for rehabilitation beyond the acute phase [33, 43]. Many patients will require serial staged surgical procedures including debridement, skin grafting, wound closure, and reconstructive procedures. Quality burn care requires constant investment in specialized personnel, infrastructure and multidisciplinary collaboration.

The significant physical and psychological toll of severe burns means that extensive psychosocial support is crucial, not just for patients navigating trauma and long-term recovery, but also for healthcare professionals who are subjected to recurrent high-stress, emotionally taxing environments. Such an integrated approach is crucial to ensure quality of care and recovery in BMCI settings. In summary, the findings highlight that BMCIs require a unique, systems approach that is different from other MCIs. Improving surge capacity, especially in LMICs, standardizing international guidelines and strengthening preparedness are critical priorities to improve outcomes in future burn disasters.

Transferability of the findings to low- and middle-income country settings

The identified components provide a holistic framework for BMCI readiness and response, but their implementation should be considered in the context of local health system capacity. The majority of studies included were from high-income countries with well-established burn networks, specialized burn centers, advanced prehospital systems and greater workforce and resource availability. Consequently, some components such as interfacility patient redistribution, specialized burn surge capacity, advanced communication platforms, and multidisciplinary rehabilitation services may not be readily transferable to LMICs, where infrastructure, specialist workforce, transport systems, and critical care resources are often limited. The general principles identified in this review, including coordinated incident command, standardised triage, effective communication, workforce training, resource prioritisation and flexible surge planning are however generally applicable in a wide range of settings. In resource-limited settings, these components will likely require contextual adaptation through simplified clinical protocols, task-sharing, phased capacity expansion, strengthened referral networks and greater reliance on regional coordination and community-based resources. Future research should examine the implementation and effectiveness of these components in LMICs to create context-specific BMCI preparedness and response strategies that account for differences in health system capacity and resource availability.

Limitations of the study

This systematic review critically appraises and synthesizes the available literature on components for responding to BMCIs to identify the key elements emphasized across relevant studies. However, there are several limitations to be mentioned. Firstly, the number of included studies is relatively small, which may affect the robustness and comprehensiveness of the findings. Secondly, restricting to English-language publications may have introduced a selection bias, although this was done to capture the majority of relevant research in the field. In addition, there is a possibility of inclusion bias as many studies were excluded at the title and abstract screening stage and this may have affected the generalizability of the results. Thirdly, is the variation in methodological quality of the included studies. Finally, the inconsistency in the study of BMCIs across regions and the fact that most studies were conducted in higher income countries limits the generalizability of the findings to LMICs and warrants cautious interpretation of the findings.

Conclusion

BMCIs have distinct challenges that differentiate them from general MCIs, such as complex injuries, prolonged critical care, and high resource requirements. An effective response relies on five core domains: preparedness and prevention; command, control, coordination and communication; prehospital and transport management; hospital and surge capacity management; and clinical and supportive care. Early, burn-specific triage, rapid referral to specialized centers and standardized clinical protocols are essential to optimize outcomes. BMCIs require integrated, scalable and multidisciplinary approaches across all phases of care, including long-term rehabilitation and psychosocial support. The literature reveals gaps in standardized guidelines and strategies specific to contexts particularly for low- and middle-income settings. To reduce morbidity and mortality in future burn disasters, it is critical to enhance preparedness, surge capacity, and burn-specific expertise within disaster frameworks. By defining these key components, health care providers may be better able to understand what is needed for a successful response to burns during large-scale disasters. The identified components can then be used to inform the development and creation of comprehensive response plans as well as the underlying principles that guide those plans. In so doing, they may contribute to improving the delivery of services during BMCIs and the overall resilience of the health care system and the community it serves. Dealing with these factors will allow the development of more robust and effective strategies to deal with the complex challenges presented by such incidents and ultimately lead to better outcomes for burn casualties and a more prepared and resilient society.
