Early classification of functional connectomes in Parkinson’s disease: a comparison of machine learning classifiers using multi-scale topological features
Authors: Leandro Donisi, Rosa De Micco, Maria Agnese Pirozzi, Mattia Siciliano, Federica Franza, Noemi Pisani, Bukhtawar Zamir, Mario Cirillo, Alessandro Tessitore, Fabrizio Esposito
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundParkinson’s disease (PD) affects brain networks across multiple anatomical scales, but it is unclear how correctly machine learning (ML) would classify the (altered) topology of functional connectomes in single PD cases at an early clinical stage. Leveraging network graph theory (NGT) for multi-scale topological feature extraction, here we determined (i) which features (across scales, lobes and types) are relatively more important, and (ii) which current state-of-the-art ML classifiers provide better performances, when discriminating newly diagnosed, drug-naive PD patients vs. healthy control (HC) subjects in a single-center MRI study.MethodsResting-state functional MRI was consecutively performed in 112 drug-naïve PD patients and 17 HC subjects. Following standard (automated) preprocessing, the Brainnetome atlas (210/36 cortical/subcortical areas) was applied to reconstruct individual functional connectomes. NGT features were extracted at global, lobar/cortical and lobar/subcortical scales. Feature importance was assessed as the information gain (IG) criterion. The synthetic minority oversampling technique (SMOTE) was used to augment HC datasets. Nine different ML classifiers were trained on discriminating PD vs. HC (balanced) classes and validated via repeated stratified cross-validation.ResultsAmong global NGT features, characteristic path length (IG = 20.3%) was more important than global (IG ~ 14%) and average local (IG ~ 14%) efficiency. Basal ganglia (IG = 18.5%), parietal cortex (IG = 17.7%) and thalamus (IG = 15.6%) cumulatively contributed the most informative NGT features. The “extra-tree classifier” achieved the best performances (F1-score = 95.8 ± 3.8%; AUCROC = 99.3 ± 1.8%).ConclusionsBoth global and local NGT features are important for the topological classification of functional connectomes in drug-naïve PD patients. Combining multi-scale functional connectomics with ML may contribute to the design of health information technologies (for decision-making) and for the definition of early, accurate and interpretable, non-invasive neuroimaging PD biomarkers.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12911-025-03303-1.Keywords: Functional connectome, Feature importance, Machine learning, Multi-scale features, Network graph theory, Neuroimaging, Parkinson’s disease

Background

Parkinson’s disease (PD) is a chronic neurodegenerative disorder, affecting the topology of brain circuits across multiple functional scales [1, 2]. In PD, the progressive loss of dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra pars compacta leads to a progressive widespread dysfunction via cortico-striatal-thalamo-cortical (looping) pathways [3, 4] and even if motor symptoms (e.g., tremor, rigidity and bradykinesia) represent the clinical hallmark for diagnosis, mounting evidence indicates that PD may affect a broader set of brain (sub)networks, already from the prodromal stage. Indeed, pathological changes may begin years before the overt manifestation of motor signs [3–5], and numerous studies support the view of PD as a multi-system disorder with early involvement of non-motor brain regions. Consistently with this notion, non-motor symptoms, including cognitive impairment [6], neuropsychiatric disturbances (e.g., anxiety and depression [7]), autonomic dysfunctions (e.g., constipation and orthostatic hypotension [6]) and sleep disorders (REM sleep behavior disorder [8]) may often precede the emergence of classical motor symptoms by several years [5, 6, 9–11], potentially escaping (or delaying) the focused attention of the clinicians.

Machine Learning (ML) algorithms detect patterns in clinical imaging data that are not apparent to clinicians and may therefore support early diagnosis of diseases, when potential disease-modifying treatments could be more effective. Among neuroimaging modalities, resting-state functional MRI (rs-fMRI) has been extensively used for probing brain functional (re)organization in clinical cohorts [12], but also, more recently, in combination with ML, to provide useful interpretable features for the classification of single patient cases, including PD and some of its more common variants or subtypes [13–21]. Particularly, since functional neuronal alterations are often detectable before irreversible degeneration (i.e., brain atrophy) [5], and can be themselves reversible due to spontaneous (or drug-induced) neural plasticity effects [22, 23], newly diagnosed drug-naïve PD patients represent an ideal cohort for investigating whether (and how) these would be detected at an early stage of the disease (i.e., stage 1 or 2) without the confounding effects of dopaminergic therapy [24].

Regional alterations in the spontaneous synchronized fluctuations of blood-oxygen-level-dependent (BOLD) signals can be effectively detected with rs-fMRI in clinical cohorts [12]. However, the highly spatially distributed structure, and consequently the fine graininess, of individual spatio-temporal component or descriptive rs-fMRI maps usually makes them far too noisy and variable for reliable classifications of single PD cases, often requiring additional modalities and/or ad hoc, data-driven or other creative strategies, for robust feature extraction (and selection), before ML classification [18, 25]. In PD patients, functional alterations may occur at different spatial scales, e.g., within (or between) large-scale (cortical) networks [26–28] or within small-scale (cortical or subcortical) networks and regions [29–31]. On the other hand, while cortical signal sources span considerably larger spatial scales (mainly due to the extensive surface area of the cortical lobes), causing BOLD signals to synchronize at a more global anatomical scale, subcortical signal sources are more compact and confined, causing BOLD signals to synchronize over smaller, more localized, anatomical scales.

To address multiple functional scales of the brain, individual rs-fMRI time-series are also used to reconstruct the (so-called) functional connectome, i.e., a high-dimensional, single-network (whole-brain) matrix representation of all temporal dependencies (e.g., linear correlations) among all regional BOLD signals from a given brain parcellation [32]. To characterize the topology of the network, the functional connectome is conveniently abstracted to a weighted graph structure, whereby the brain regions provide the locations for the nodes (independently of the original extension) and measures of their temporal dependency (or functional connectivity) provide the weights for the edges, thus enabling the estimation of several metrics via network graph theory (NGT) [33, 34]. Particularly, applying NGT to individual functional connectomes enables the extraction of global metrics (e.g., global efficiency, characteristic path length, transitivity, assortativity), quantifying the overall integrative capacity of the whole brain network, as well as local (nodal) metrics (e.g., degree, clustering coefficient, betweenness centrality), measuring the specific role of single regions, across different scales (e.g., cortical and subcortical), under different, clinically relevant (e.g., normal, disease- or drug-modified) conditions for the studied patient [33, 35–37]. A previous study on a large sample of drug-naïve PD patients (n = 147) [38] specifically illustrated how the reorganization of the functional connectome may occur (early) in drug-naïve PD patients, showing that (i) NGT metrics may capture significant differences in PD patients vs. healthy control (HC) subjects in both global and lobar features, and (ii), among lobar features, both cortical and subcortical areas features may contribute to predict clinical progression at 2-year follow-up.

ML offers many powerful solutions for identifying high-dimensional, multivariate patterns at an individual level [39]. Particularly, supervised ML classifiers, such as support vector machines, random forests, or gradient boosting, can be trained to discriminate PD vs. HC cases starting from relatively large sets of meaningful features in relatively small samples [20, 40], albeit there is yet no consensus about the best strategy of feature extraction and selection. Moreover, no previous study assessed the performances of typical ML classifiers trained only with NGT features, covering multiple anatomical scales of the (rs-fMRI derived) functional connectome, without a priori (hypothesis- or data-driven) “out-of-the-box” selection of the patterns, nor the relative importance of global (vs. lobar) and cortical (vs. subcortical) NGT features within the whole set, was previously investigated, suggesting an overall ranking of the many different types of NGT features, with respect to the problem of separating a class of drug-naïve PD patients from a class of HC subjects. Therefore, the goal of this study was to fill this knowledge gap, assessing the discriminative power of multi-scale topological features in combination with different types of state-of-the-art ML classifiers.

Methods

Starting from consecutively acquired rs-fMRI data sets on the same MRI scanner, in a new independent sample of drug-naïve PD patients (n = 112) and HC subjects (n = 17), we aimed at (i) extracting multi-scale NGT features from each individual functional connectome without prior (hypothesis- or data-driven) “out-of-the-box” selection (either at an image or feature level), and (ii) internally validating nine different supervised ML classifiers (with default hyperparameter settings from currently available software tools) trained for the optimal discrimination between two balanced classes of PD (real subjects) and HC (augmented subjects). In the presented pipeline, the features encompassed three anatomical scales of the functional connectome: (i) global, including summary and average NGT metrics describing the whole-brain network topology; (ii) lobar-cortical, based on the average values of NGT metrics within major cortical subdivisions (lobes), including sensorimotor, fronto-insular, occipital, parietal, and temporal lobes; and (iii) lobar-subcortical, based on the average values of NGT metrics within the basal ganglia and the thalamus. In addition to the comparative analysis of major binary ML classifiers, a model-free (information theoretic) feature importance analysis was performed to anticipate the relatively more important NGT features, across (and within) scales of the functional connectome, as well as the most important brain areas (as lobes), in terms of the expected impact on the topological classification of the functional connectome.

Study population

The study sample was extracted from an ongoing project enrolling, on a consecutive basis, early drug-naïve PD patients at the time of clinical diagnosis of PD, according to the Movement Disorder Society Clinical Diagnostic Criteria for Parkinson’s Disease [41]. For the present study, 112 PD patients were selected based on the following inclusion criteria: (1) diagnosis of idiopathic PD at baseline; (2) modified Hoehn and Yahr (mH&Y) stage ≤ 2.5; (3) no current or previous exposure to anti-Parkinsonian medications or any centrally acting treatments at baseline. Exclusion criteria included: (1) disease onset before the age of 40; (2) any prior treatment with dopaminergic, anticholinergic, antidepressant, or other centrally acting drugs, to avoid potential confounding effects on functional connectomics; (3) any other clinically significant medical condition.

A group of 17 HC subjects, matched for age (t = -0.41, degrees of freedom = 127, p = 0.68) and gender (χ² = 0.06, degrees of freedom = 1, p = 0.81), with no personal or family history of PD or parkinsonism, was also included in the analysis. MRI data acquisition

All MRI scans were acquired on a 3 Tesla (3T) GE Discovery MR750 scanner (GE Healthcare, Milwaukee, WI, USA) using a 32-channel parallel receive-only head-neck coil.

The MRI protocol of this study included structural three-dimensional T1-weighted (3D-T1w) and two-dimensional T2* gradient echo echoplanar imaging (2D-GRE-EPI) functional (rs-fMRI) sequences. High-resolution anatomical images were obtained using an inversion recovery-prepared fast spoiled gradient recalled (IR-FSPGR) sequence with the following parameters: repetition time (TR) = 3.9 ms, echo time (TE) = 3.0 ms, inversion time (TI) = 650 ms, flip angle (FA) = 9°, slice thickness = 1 mm with no gap, matrix size = 256 × 256, field of view (FOV) = 256 mm, in-plane resolution = 1.0 × 1.0 mm2, duration = 6.49 min. Rs-fMRI was performed with the following parameters: TR = 1500 ms, TE = 19 ms, FA = 90°, 64 axial slices covering the whole brain in an interleaved descending order with anterior-to-posterior phase encoding direction, slice thickness = 3 mm (with no gap), matrix size = 128 × 96 (reconstructed to 128 × 128), FOV = 256 mm, in-plane resolution = 2.0 × 2.0 mm2, number of volumes (time-points) = 320, acceleration factor (parallel imaging via ASSET) = 4 and total readout time = 10.764 ms, effective echo spacing = 0.0848 ms (total duration = 8 min).

MRI data processing

Preprocessing of 3D-T1w images was performed using the fMRIPrep toolbox (version 23.2.0, https://fmriprep.org/en/stable/) [42], which included bias field correction and skull-stripping. Tissue segmentation into grey matter (GM), white matter (WM), and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) was then performed in the native anatomical space using the Statistical Parametric Mapping toolbox (SPM12, https://www.fil.ion.ucl.ac.uk/spm/software/).

Preprocessing of rs-fMRI data was performed using the pipeline implemented in fMRIPrep toolbox that included BOLD reference image generation, head motion estimation (yielding 6 rigid-body parameters: 3 translations and 3 rotations per volume) [43], slice timing correction to the middle of each TR [44], and susceptibility distortion correction based on field inhomogeneity maps acquired during the same scanning session [45]. In addition, nuisance regressors were estimated on a per-volume basis, including the 24 Friston’s parameters derived from the motion estimates [46], framewise displacement outliers (motion spikes) [47], and global signals extracted from WM and CSF. These confounds were regressed out using a general linear model [48]. Finally, the rs-fMRI time series were band-pass filtered within the frequency range 0.01–0.1 Hz, compatible with the acquisition TR [49].

Following the preprocessing of structural and functional MRI data, brain parcellation was performed using the Brainnetome atlas [50], which provides a fine-grained, structural and functional connectivity-informed subdivision of the cortex and subcortical structures into 246 regions (210 cortical and 36 subcortical). The atlas was first registered to the anatomical (3D-T1w) space using the Advanced Normalization Tools (ANTs, https://github.com/ANTsX/ANTs) software package, in order to apply the subject-specific GM mask to the cortical parcels. The 3D-T1w image and the transformed atlas were then warped to each subject’s native rs-fMRI space. For each subject, a functional connectivity matrix was computed by calculating Pearson’s correlation coefficients between the mean BOLD time series extracted from each Brainnetome region. In these matrices, nodes represent the individual regions defined by the Brainnetome atlas, while edges correspond to the strength of functional connectivity (i.e., the degree of temporal correlation) between pairs of regions.

NGT feature extraction

From the functional connectivity matrices, NGT metrics were extracted using the Brain Connectivity Toolbox (https://sites.google.com/site/bctnet/) [33] in MATLAB R2024b (The MathWorks, Inc.). Prior to graph construction, correlation coefficients were transformed using Fisher’s r-to-z transformation to stabilize variance and the sampling distribution under the assumption of normality. To ensure inter-subject comparability, the matrices were subjected to proportional thresholding, retaining the top 10% of the strongest-weighted connections, as recommended to control for inter-individual variability in graph density [51–53]. Four global NGT metrics were computed, selected to represent the main topological properties of the functional connectome: characteristic path length (CPL) and global efficiency (GE) as measures of integration; transitivity (TRA) as a measure of segregation; and assortativity (ASS) as an index of network resilience [33, 35, 54]. In addition, the following node-specific NGT metrics were computed: betweenness centrality (BC), clustering coefficient (CC), degree centrality (DC), eccentricity (ECC), local efficiency (LE), modularity (MOD), and node strength (NS).

The nodal metrics were then averaged across all network nodes to derive global summary description of these topological properties on the whole functional connectome, so as to characterize integration (ECC, LE), segregation (CC, LE), hubness (DC, BC), functional specialization (MOD), and information load distribution (NS) [33, 35]. In the following, feature names are prefixed with “mean” to denote averaged values. For a lobar-level characterization, nodal metrics were aggregated according to the subdivision of the cerebral cortex provided by the Brainnetome Atlas parcellation for both hemispheres [50] and previous literature adopting lobar-based analyses of functional brain networks [38, 55–57]. The regions were grouped into the following five anatomical macro-areas (hereinafter referred to as lobes):

Dataset augmentation

To possibly reinforce the conclusions of the present study, two additional (alternative) approaches for dataset augmentation were also considered for our comparisons. First, adaptive synthetic (ADASYN) oversampling [63] was applied to augment the HC class of Dataset 1 as this technique enables a more adaptive generation of synthetic samples within regions of the feature space that are in principle harder for a classifier to learn. Second, a generative adversarial network (GAN) was trained, using the architecture proposed in the original implementation [64], by replacing the convolutional and image reshaping layers with fully connected layers to map latent noise vectors to the feature space corresponding to Dataset 1. In addition, a robust approach based on the Mahalanobis distance with Ledoit–Wolf covariance estimation [65, 66] was used to reveal the presence of possible outliers in the minority class of Dataset 1.

Machine learning algorithms

Dataset 1 was used to select the features from the largest set before training each ML classifier to discriminate between PD patients and HC subjects.

First, the variance inflation factor (VIF) was estimated to address feature multicollinearity, which might cause the instability of ML algorithms [67]. For each feature, the corresponding VIF was calculated as 1 / (1 - R²), R² resulting from the multiple linear regression model using that feature as the dependent variable and all other features as predictors, to measure how much the variance of an independent variable is influenced by its correlation with the other independent variables. A high VIF value for a feature indicates strong linear dependence on the remaining features, suggesting redundancy and a potential need for elimination. Because the reciprocal of VIF provides an estimate for the tolerance of the model to the multicollineariy, in this study, a threshold of 10 [68], corresponding to a 10% tolerance, was used to identify features with high multicollinearity. If the maximum VIF among all features exceeded the predefined threshold, the feature with the highest VIF was removed. This process was repeated until all remaining features had a VIF below the threshold of 10.

Second, considering that some ML algorithms are more sensitive to the scale of the input features [69], the min-max normalization was applied by scaling each feature to a fixed range, where the largest occurring data point corresponds to the maximum value and the smallest one to the minimum. Feature importance

For each dataset, the most informative NGT metrics and brain areas (both cortical lobes and subcortical areas) were identified with respect to the expected importance for the classification of drug-naïve PD patients (vs. HC subjects).

The feature importance analysis was carried out to determine which NGT metrics were most important to characterize PD, and which brain areas (both cortical and subcortical) would be mainly affected or altered by the PD condition, in its contribution to the functional connectome. A feature importance method based on the Information Gain (IG) criterion was applied. For a given feature, the IG index is estimated from the mutual information between the feature values and the corresponding (known) class values and therefore measures the reduction in the uncertainty of its values caused by the knowledge of the corresponding class. By definition, this quantity can be estimated from the difference between the absolute uncertainty (unconditional entropy) and the same uncertainty when the class is known (conditional entropy), i.e., the reduction in the uncertainty after accounting how much information that particular feature contributes toward explaining the variability in the data [71]. Discussion

Training an ML classifier to identify drug-naïve PD patients is important for several reasons, especially in the context of precision medicine, and for possible early interventions. In this study, nine different ML classifiers, using NGT features from (rs-fMRI-derived) functional connectomes, were trained to discriminate early drug-naïve PD patients vs. HC subjects. A multi-scale feature extraction strategy was adopted to best capture the important topological properties of the functional connectome across different anatomical scales, including global (whole brain), lobar/cortical (large) and lobar/subcortical (small) scales, without relying on the mean group differences between patients and controls. An information-theoretic analysis based on the IG metric was performed to determine which NGT features (per scale, type and brain area) of the functional connectome were most informative for the characterization of the drug-naïve PD functional connectome.

The IG values were preliminary evaluated for the full dataset of multi-scale NGT features (dataset 1) to check whether (and how many of) all three functional scales (global, lobar/cortical and lobar/subcortical) would be potentially more relevant to a correct assignment of each functional connectome to the PD (vs. HC) class. According to the results, all three scales were included, respectively, among the first three (one feature per scale) and the first ten (two global, four lobar/cortical and four lobar/subcortical), most informative features, indicating that an optimal early classification of the (drug-naïve) PD functional connectome would rely on all anatomical scales.

The classification results on dataset 1 indicated that tree-based ML classifiers (DT, ETC, RF, XGB) consistently outperformed other types of ML classifiers. Among them, DT showed the lowest performance (F1-score of 90.0 ± 7.3%), while ETC achieved the best performance (F1-score of 95.8 ± 3.8%), ensuring a highly accurate discrimination between drug-naïve PD and HC cases. Therefore, the findings of this study reinforce the advantage of using ensemble tree-based classifiers in neuroimaging applications [72–74]. Particularly, the high F1-score reflects how tree-based ML classifiers are inherently robust to multicollinearity and intrinsic dependency between the features (here implied by the multi-scale and integrative nature of the NGT metrics), a property that nicely adds up to the known capability of tree-based ML solutions of better capturing non-linear interactions by not relying on strong distributional or independence assumptions. Moreover, these classifiers seem to efficiently handle high-dimensional data without the need for extensive feature selection, making them particularly well-suited for the classification of functional connectomes [39, 75–77].

When comparing the performances on the restricted datasets (Dataset 2, Dataset 3), similar results were obtained, i.e., tree-based ML classifiers consistently outperformed the other types of ML classifiers. However, when only lobar features were used (Dataset 3) the performances were even higher confirming that lobar features are overall more important for (and contribute more significantly to) the classification in comparison to when the same model is trained on Dataset 1 that combines global (Dataset 2) and lobar (Dataset 3) features. This clearly suggests that lower-ranked global features might have introduced some noise, slightly confounding the discrimination power of the models, but also indicates that the inclusion of higher-ranked (more important) global features still guarantees very high performances with the multi-scale feature set of Dataset 1.

In contrast, the other ML classifiers showed notably lower performances, with MLP performing the worst overall (F1-score 62.3 ± 29.6%). This performance likely reflects that MLP classifiers typically require larger sample sizes to work with a large set of features (high dimensionality), unless an extensive hyperparameter optimization is included in the iterations [78, 79], which would also call for even larger sets of data observations. Notably, to facilitate comparisons (and replications by others), all classifiers were here trained by strictly keeping all default settings (i.e., no hyperparameter optimization). Indeed, hyperparameter optimization requires nested cross-validation and smaller splits, which makes the results increasingly more dependent on the data and less dependent on features (which were, instead, more central to the focus of this study). As a consequence, even the performances of SVM, KNN and LR algorithms, despite being commonly used for the classification of neuroimaging features [80, 81], were notably worse than those achieved by tree-based algorithms. Among these, KNN was the lower-performing model (F1-score: 70.6 ± 13.3%), while SVM (F1-score 75.3 ± 9.8%) and LR (F1-score 73.7 ± 10.6%) reached slightly higher performances. KNN, indeed, exhibits limited flexibility in handling high-dimensional, interdependent feature spaces, such as those defined by multi-scale NGT features, due to its sensitivity to the curse of dimensionality and lack of capacity to handle feature dependencies [67]. SVM, equipped with radial basis function, on the contrary, is more suitable for high-dimensional data and small sample sizes [15], and this may justify the slightly better performances, whereas LR, which is based on a linear model in the sparse version [82], showed worse results. Finally, the NB classifier, which assumes conditional independence among features, showed seriously limited performance (F1-score: 77.8 ± 10.4%). Although multicollinearity was preliminary reduced through VIF analysis during feature preprocessing, the inherent interdependence among NGT features in connectomic data likely violates the conditional independence assumption required by NB [83]. This may explain its suboptimal performance, underscoring the general unsuitability of probabilistic models like NB in contexts where complex topological dependencies are present.

By separating global (dataset 2) and local (dataset 3) features, the feature importance analysis based on IG values provided even more valuable insights into the type and location of the functional connectome alterations occurring in drug-naïve PD patients. Among global NGT metrics, CPL emerged as the most informative global feature (IG = 20.3%), followed by GE and mean LE. These three metrics (orderly) reflect (i) the altered range of node-to-node communications, (ii) the altered balance between node integration and segregation and (iii) the widespread occurrence of local unbalancing of the connections, all three aspects contributing most to the delineation of an aberrant pattern of re-organization of the functional connectome secondary to a system-level brain disorder. The observed alterations in these features are consistent with previous evidence of disrupted large-scale network organization and reduced functional integration in PD [65–67]. Among the global NGT metrics, while TRA and CC also showed relatively higher IG values, suggesting a global alteration in the localized connectivity and general capacity for specialized information processing, ASS, BC, and NS contributed relatively less, possibly reflecting less disease-specific variations of these NGT metrics at the global level. Notably, MOD emerged as the least informative global feature (IG = 2.4%), suggesting that the general capacity for creating “communities” within the functional connectome is relatively preserved in the earlier stages of PD, but, as further discussed below, the discriminative value of MOD was relatively higher in some specific brain areas that normally operate at a smaller anatomical scale, such as the thalamus and the basal ganglia, highlighting the importance of assessing topological properties, concurrently and comparatively, at all three different operating scales of the functional connectome (global, cortical, subcortical).

Indeed, when considering all lobar (cortical and subcortical) features, the basal ganglia exhibited the highest cumulative IG (18.5%), tightly followed by the parietal cortex (17.7%) and the thalamus (15.6%), indicating that both subcortical and associative parietal areas should provide far more characteristic topological features for the classification of functional connectomes of drug-naïve PD patients. This is consistent with the known central involvement of the cortico-striato-thalamo-cortical looping circuits in PD pathophysiology [3, 4], as well as with the typically earlier dysfunction in attentional and integrative cortical systems [27, 84]. Nonetheless, more sensory cortical domains, such as occipital (IG = 15.5%) and temporal (IG = 15.0%) lobes, may also provide relevant features. Indeed, while their IG values were slightly lower than the parietal lobe, their contribution should not be neglected and may reflect early alterations in visual and auditory/language processing networks, which are, indeed, increasingly recognized as being affected in the early stages of PD [85]. On the other hand, these findings complement previous reports of early functional reorganization of the connectome along visual and auditory pathways in drug-naïve PD patients [27]. Conversely, and partly surprisingly, sensorimotor (10.4%) and fronto-insular (7.4%) areas showed lower overall IG values, suggesting that NGT features from these nodes may carry relatively less topological information about the ongoing (aberrant) reorganization of the individual functional connectome. Particularly, the relatively lower importance of the cortical sensorimotor features, compared to, e.g., the basal ganglia or the parietal cortex, may suggest that the PD pathology does not prominently change the topology of the functional connectome at the cortical nodes of the sensorimotor areas. On the other hand, even if sensorimotor areas most often exhibit reduced levels of (resting-state) functional connectivity (compared to HC), previous studies in drug-naïve PD patients have also shown how the functional connectivity of these areas may quickly return back to approximately normal levels after the first levodopa intake [22] (suggesting a preservation of the intrinsic connections) or may sometimes appear substantially preserved (such as, e.g., in patients with increased vulnerability and associated abnormal functional hyperconnectivity in other areas) [26]. Thus, altogether, these results underscore the spatial heterogeneity of functional connectome alterations in drug-naïve PD patients and highlight the importance of both subcortical and higher-order associative cortical lobes to be involved in the training of ML classifiers.

The analysis of individual NGT features across all brain areas also revealed a spatially heterogeneous pattern of feature type relevance. In the thalamus, the highest discriminative features were MOD (5.5%) and DC (5.3%), followed by NS (3.1%) and ECC (1.3%). Conversely, LE and BC had minimal contributions in this region. For the basal ganglia, ECC (4.9%), MOD (4%), and LE (3.6%) dominated the feature importance profile, suggesting altered nodal centrality and network segregation occurring at this (smaller) anatomical scale. However, this also highlights that, in contrast to its limited relevance at the global level, MOD emerged as one of the most informative features within subcortical features for characterizing the functional connectome of drug-naïve PD patients.

In the parietal cortex, CC (5.3%), LE (3.8%), and DC (3.8%) were the top contributors, indicating the importance of local connectivity and integration. In the occipital lobe, ECC (3.6%), BC (2.9%), and DC (2.9%) provided the strongest contributions, highlighting the role of both central and peripheral topological features in visual network (re-)organization. Instead, the temporal lobe showed a more distributed pattern, with ECC (3.5%), LE (3.5%), and BC (2.3%) being the most informative NGT features, possibly reflecting early topological alterations associated with either difficulties or impairments in language [86] and/or some possible apathy manifestations [87]. In the sensorimotor cortex, NS (2.2%), MOD (2.0%), and DC (1.9%) dominated, suggesting a different type, albeit less prominent (as previously discussed), of early topological alteration of the functional connectome. Finally, in the fronto-insular areas, ECC (2.7%), CC (1.7%), and MOD (1.6%) contributed most, but the overall feature relevance was the lowest among all the brain areas studied, suggesting a limited local involvement of these areas in the functional connectome alteration, at this stage of the disease course.

Overall, the observed pattern of feature importance across brain lobes and topological metrics indicates that different brain systems contribute in a topologically distinct manner to the early functional alterations of the PD connectome, which might explain the heterogeneity of the many effects observed in previous connectivity studies, including (aberrant or compensatory) hyperconnectivity effects (plasticity), nodal degeneration and impaired integrative processing [27, 36, 38, 88–92]. Notably, no single NGT metric emerged as generally dominant across all brain areas, emphasizing the importance of a comprehensive multi-scale feature set for capturing the complexity of topological alterations in the PD functional connectome, especially at an early stage of the disease.

The main limitation of this study lies in the small control group size and the resulting severe imbalance between PD and HC classes which warrants extreme caution in the interpretation of the results. This issue was nevertheless partially addressed via a previously validated data balancing technique, such as SMOTE, thereby ensuring a proper (and principled) augmentation of the HC class instances while keeping real data for PD features. Moreover, even when replacing the SMOTE technique with a different data augmentation approach, such as ADASYN or GAN, similar performances (and conclusions) could be reached. In this way, we could show how the use of multi-scale NGT features for the training of ML classifiers allowed reaching very high performances, albeit it remains important to emphasize that the core mechanism of any data augmentation techniques does not generate any new authentic biological information. Consequently, any of the models trained here might still have been overfitted to the highly correlated, interpolated patterns inherent in the synthetic data, rather than learning the true and diverse biological characteristics of the minority class. Thereby, while some of the models demonstrated excellent performances on the validation set, and there was no evidence for the presence of outliers among the observations of the minority class, its generalization capabilities (on new, unseen real-world samples) remain to be addressed, particularly because any incidental artifacts within the studied sample might have been disproportionately amplified through the generation process, potentially interfering with the ability of the model to learn clean, generalizable features.

Future studies are particularly needed to generalize the presented results beyond the technical settings used here for the estimation of the functional connectome, determining the values obtained for the extracted features. The longitudinal extension of the present study should preliminarily require a test-retest assessment of the same computed metrics on the same subjects across repeated MRI sessions (on the same scanner) to determine how much the variability in the measurements affects the accuracy of the classification and (prospectively) the quality of the predictions. Moreover, while we used here a univariate assessment of feature importance (via the Information Gain), thereby ensuring that the obtained value for a given feature is not affected by those obtained for the other features in the same input set, especially in the presence of strong correlation between features, the differences between these values may also be not sufficiently stable, and it will be therefore essential to verify whether the observed ranking of the features will be preserved. More in general, a fully objective feature selection process, such as the one illustrated here, despite the evidence for meaningful results in a single-center study, does not guarantee that (i) this specific set of features is more biologically meaningful and/or (ii) the accuracy of the classification or the quality of the prediction is better or worse than any other possible set of features.

Concerning the choice of using cortical lobes, rather than the intrinsic mapping of the individual parcels to the canonical large-scale rs-fMRI networks, as the main criterion for grouping regional features across scales, in line with [38], we considered that the mapping of subcortical regions within the cortical networks may not be as spatially stable as for the cortical regions and therefore we decided to keep a purely anatomical division for grouping features across the whole connectome for both cortical and subcortical regional features. In a previous work [38], this turned out to be an effective approach to highlight the effect of the PD pathology on the functional connectome. In the future, as different criteria for grouping regional features have been proposed, unifying cortical and subcortical regions within the same whole-brain partition, e.g., resulting in extended large-scale intrinsic networks, other schemes of division might prove equally (or more) effective to capture the multi-scale information in the functional connectome.

Moreover, while the purpose of this study was to compare different ML classifiers, rather than deploying the ultimate solution, the actual generalizability of the trained ML classifiers should be possibly tested using new datasets, from different (multi-center) cohorts of drug-naïve PD patients. Anyway, larger training samples will be needed to evaluate the impact of more stratified classifications, e.g., based on genotypic and phenotypic information, albeit the impact of using alternative atlases and parcellations, especially to address the differences in the native resolution of the data, may not be negligible in multi-center data sets. Finally, different data normalization strategies, and more advanced feature importance approaches, within the more specific context of explainable ML, may provide additional information on the performance of ML classifiers operating on multi-scale NGT features from the functional connectome.

Conclusions

In conclusion, the findings presented in this study support the potential of functional connectomics and ML as powerful tools to support clinical diagnosis in early PD patients, highlighting the complementary importance of both global and local NGT features for capturing the topological alterations in the PD-associated functional connectome.
