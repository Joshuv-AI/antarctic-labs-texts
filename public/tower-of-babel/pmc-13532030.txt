CD86‐Positive Mature Tertiary Lymphoid Structures Are Associated With CD8 + T‐Cell Infiltration and Favorable Clinical Outcomes in Pancreatic Ductal Adenocarcinoma
Authors: Takaomi Seki, Kenichiro Araki, Takehiko Yokobori, Hayato Ikota, Norio Kubo, Takamichi Igarashi, Mariko Tsukagoshi, Norihiro Ishii, Yasuo Hosouchi, Hiroshi Saeki, Ken Shirabe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimsAlthough tertiary lymphoid structures (TLSs) contribute to antitumor immunity, the significance of CD86 expression within mature TLSs in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) remains unclear. This study evaluated CD86‐positive mature TLSs as a marker of an immunologically active tumor microenvironment.MethodsWe analyzed 128 treatment‐naive patients with PDAC who underwent curative resection. TLSs were evaluated by hematoxylin and eosin staining and immunohistochemistry for CD4, CD8, CD20, CD21, CD23, and CD86. TLS maturation, CD86 expression, TLS density and diameter, intratumoral T‐cell infiltration, and clinical outcomes were assessed. Multiplex immunofluorescence for CD8 and Granzyme B was performed in selected cases.ResultsTLSs were identified in 124 patients. Among TLS‐positive cases, 13, 21, and 90 were classified as immature, intermediate, and mature TLSs, respectively. Mature TLSs were associated with increased intratumoral CD8+ T‐cell infiltration compared with TLS‐absent cases (p = 0.027), whereas CD4+ T‐cell infiltration did not differ significantly. CD86‐positive mature TLSs were associated with higher intratumoral CD8+ T‐cell infiltration (p = 0.002), greater TLS density, and larger TLS diameter than CD86‐negative mature TLSs. Multiplex immunofluorescence confirmed that most CD86‐positive cells co‐expressed CD20. Exploratory CD8/Granzyme B analysis suggested a higher proportion of Granzyme B‐expressing CD8+ T cells in CD86‐positive mature TLSs. Patients with CD86‐positive mature TLSs showed favorable clinical outcomes.ConclusionCD86‐positive mature TLSs may represent a pathological feature associated with an immunologically active tumor microenvironment in PDAC, as reflected by increased intratumoral CD8+ T‐cell infiltration, a higher proportion of Granzyme B‐expressing CD8+ T cells in the exploratory analysis, and favorable clinical outcomes.Keywords: antigen‐presenting B cells, cluster of differentiation 86, pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, prognostic biomarkers, tertiary lymphoid structures

Introduction

Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) remains a highly lethal malignancy, with a reported 5‐year survival rate of approximately 10% [1]. This poor prognosis is partly attributable to the tumor immune microenvironment, which is characterized by a highly immunosuppressive and “immune‐cold” phenotype [2]. Although immune checkpoint inhibitors have recently attracted considerable attention and have demonstrated clinical efficacy across various solid tumors, their therapeutic benefit in PDAC remains limited [3]. Therefore, the identification of novel biomarkers capable of accurately assessing prognosis and the tumor immune microenvironment in patients with refractory PDAC is urgently needed.

Tertiary lymphoid structures (TLSs) are ectopic lymphoid aggregates that develop within chronically inflamed tissues or tumor‐associated microenvironments and function similarly to secondary lymphoid organs by supporting antigen presentation, T‐cell activation, and the induction of antitumor immune responses. Accordingly, the presence of TLSs has been recognized as a favorable prognostic factor in various malignancies [4].

TLSs are characterized by a distinct spatial organization consisting of CD20+ B‐cell–rich germinal center regions surrounded by CD3+ T‐cell zones. The degree of germinal center maturation—assessed using markers such as CD21, CD23, and Ki‐67—as well as TLS localization (intratumoral or peritumoral), density, and size, has been reported to be a critical determinant of TLS functionality and immunological relevance [5, 6, 7, 8, 9]. Multiple clinical studies have demonstrated that the presence of TLSs is associated with a favorable prognosis in pancreatic cancer [10, 11, 12, 13, 14].

CD86, which is the focus of this study, is widely used as a marker for macrophages and dendritic cells. However, recent studies have demonstrated that CD86‐positive B cells within TLSs exhibit antigen‐presenting capacity and contribute to T‐cell activation and the maintenance of antitumor immune responses within the tumor microenvironment [15]. In particular, a comprehensive analysis across 10 solid tumor types revealed that the presence of TLSs—especially those containing CD86‐positive B cells within germinal centers—was associated with increased infiltration of tumor‐specific cytotoxic T lymphocytes and improved patient prognosis. Notably, PDAC was not included in that analysis, and the relationships between CD86‐positive TLSs, TLS maturation, cytotoxic T‐cell infiltration (a key component of tumor immunity), clinicopathological features, and prognosis in PDAC remain unclear.

The present study was conducted to clarify the clinical significance of TLSs with CD86‐positive germinal centers in primary PDAC as a biomarker reflecting the tumor immune microenvironment. TLS maturation was evaluated in 128 resected PDAC cases without preoperative treatment using immunohistochemical (IHC) staining for CD4, CD8, CD20, CD21, CD23, and CD86, and the associations of TLS maturation with clinicopathological characteristics, immunological parameters, and patient outcomes were analyzed.

Methods

Patients and Study Design

Between 2007 and 2022, a total of 128 patients with PDAC who underwent radical surgical resection at Gunma University Hospital or Saiseikai Maebashi Hospital were included in this study. None of the patients received neoadjuvant therapy. Patients with insufficient clinicopathological data or unavailable tissue specimens for pathological analyses were excluded.

For pathological evaluation, tissue sections prepared from the largest cross‐sectional area of the tumor were used, and serial sections were analyzed. Relevant clinicopathological data were collected retrospectively from medical and operative records. Pathological staging was performed in accordance with the 8th edition of the Tumor–Node–Metastasis classification of the International Union Against Cancer.

This study was conducted in accordance with the institutional guidelines of both participating hospitals and the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. The study protocol was approved by the institutional review board (approval no. HS2022‐271). Patient consent was obtained using an opt‐out approach, and details of the study were publicly disclosed on the official websites of Gunma University Hospital and Saiseikai Maebashi Hospital.

Immunohistochemistry

IHC staining was performed on formalin‐fixed, paraffin‐embedded (FFPE) PDAC tissue sections (4‐μm thick) as previously described [5, 6]. Identical procedures were used for deparaffinization, blocking, signal detection, and counterstaining. Briefly, antigen retrieval was performed using Immunosaver (Nisshin EM, Tokyo, Japan) at 98°C for 45 min, except for CD86 and Ki‐67, for which citrate buffer (pH 6.0) with autoclave treatment at 120°C for 20 min was used. Primary antibodies were applied at the following dilutions: anti‐CD8 (1:400; Abcam; ab4055), anti‐CD4 (1:500; Abcam; ab133616), anti‐CD20 (ready to use; Ventana; 760‐2531), anti‐CD86 (1:400; Cell Signaling Technology; #91882S), anti‐CD21 (1:100; Leica; NCL‐L‐CD21‐2G9), anti‐CD23 (1:1000; Cell Signaling Technology; #87960S), and anti‐Ki‐67 (1:500; Cell Signaling Technology; #9027S). Negative controls were prepared by omission of the primary antibody.

Evaluation of TLS

TLSs were defined using a modified version of previously described criteria as aggregates of B and T cells located within a 1‐mm‐wide region centered on the tumor margin, encompassing both the intratumoral and extratumoral sides [5, 8, 9]. Consecutive stained sections were digitized using PidPort (Medmain Inc., Fukuoka, Japan). Tumor margin length was measured on whole‐slide images, and TLS density was calculated as the number of TLSs per unit tumor margin length. The maximum TLS diameter was also measured.

TLS maturation was classified according to previously described criteria based on CD21 and CD23 expression within the follicular/germinal center–like region [13]. Although these structures have been referred to as early TLSs, primary follicle‐like TLSs, and secondary follicle‐like TLSs in previous studies, we defined them as immature, intermediate, and mature TLSs to reflect their degree of maturation. Immature TLSs were defined as CD8+CD20+CD21−CD23− aggregates, intermediate TLSs as CD8+CD20+CD21+CD23− structures, and mature TLSs as CD8+CD20+CD21+CD23+ structures. Marker positivity was assessed qualitatively; weak but specific staining was considered positive when localized to the appropriate cellular or follicular/germinal center–like regions. For each case, TLS maturation status was determined based on the most advanced TLS observed in the peritumoral area. Mature TLSs showing CD86 positivity within the germinal center–like region were defined as CD86‐positive mature TLSs.

TLS maturation and CD86 status were independently assessed by two blinded evaluators, including a pathologist. Discrepant cases were jointly re‐reviewed, and final classifications were reached by consensus. Interobserver agreement was assessed using concordance rates and Cohen's kappa coefficient. Agreement for TLS presence was assessed in all 128 cases, whereas agreement for mature TLSs and CD86‐positive mature TLSs was assessed in consensus‐defined TLS‐positive and mature TLS‐positive cases, respectively.

Evaluation of Immunohistochemistry

Quantification of CD8+ and CD4+ T cells was performed using a modified version of a previously described method [16]. For each specimen, three regions with the highest density of CD8+ or CD4+ lymphocytes (“hotspots”) were selected separately for each marker and imaged at 200× magnification using a BZ‐X700 digital microscope (Keyence, Osaka, Japan). The number of positive cells in each field was quantified automatically using Hybrid Cell Count software (BZ‐H3C; Keyence). The mean number of positive cells per mm [2] across the three fields was calculated for each marker and used for subsequent analyses. Assessment of Ki‐67 expression was conducted as previously described [5]. Briefly, 100 tumor cells were evaluated per microscopic field, and nuclear Ki‐67 positivity was counted. A total of 500 tumor cells were examined per case, and the mean Ki‐67 positivity across five fields was defined as the Ki‐67 labeling index.

Multiplex Immunofluorescence Staining

Multiplex immunofluorescence staining was performed on FFPE tissue sections using the Opal 3‐Plex IHC Kit (Akoya/PerkinElmer). Following deparaffinization and antigen retrieval in AR6 buffer, sequential staining cycles were conducted according to the target markers.

For the evaluation of CD86, CD20, and CD4 expression, three sequential staining cycles were performed. Each cycle consisted of blocking, incubation with primary antibodies against CD86 (1:50; Cell Signaling Technology; #91882), CD20 (ready to use, diluted 1:1; Ventana; 760‐2531), or CD4 (1:500; Abcam; ab133616), application of a horseradish peroxidase‐conjugated polymer secondary antibody, and signal amplification using Opal 690 for CD86, Opal 520 for CD20, and Opal 570 for CD4. Antibody stripping between cycles was performed by microwave treatment in AR6 buffer. Nuclear staining was not performed separately; instead, slides were mounted using ProLong Gold Antifade Mountant with DAPI (Thermo Fisher Scientific), which served as the nuclear counterstain. Fluorescence images were acquired using a BZ‐X700 microscope (Keyence, Osaka, Japan).

In a separate experiment, multiplex immunofluorescence staining for CD8 and Granzyme B was performed to assess Granzyme B expression in CD8+ T cells. The proportion of Granzyme B‐expressing CD8+ T cells was defined as the number of Granzyme B+CD8+ double‐positive cells divided by the total number of CD8+ T cells. After deparaffinization and antigen retrieval, sections were incubated with primary antibodies against CD8 (1:400; Abcam; ab4055) and Granzyme B (ready to use; CMC; 760–4283). Signals were amplified using Opal 570 for CD8 and Opal 480 (coumarin blue) for Granzyme B. Sections were counterstained with hematoxylin for 3 s for bright‐field visualization.

This analysis included five randomly selected cases from each of the CD86‐negative and CD86‐positive mature TLS groups. For each case, the proportion of Granzyme B‐expressing CD8+ T cells was calculated in five intratumoral CD8+ T‐cell hotspot fields, and the mean value across the five fields was used as the representative value for that case. Images were acquired using a BZ‐X1000 fluorescence microscope (Keyence, Osaka, Japan).

Statistical Analysis

Associations between categorical variables were analyzed using the chi‐square test. Differences in continuous variables between two groups were assessed using the Student's t‐test or the Mann–Whitney U test, as appropriate. Comparisons among three or more groups were performed using one‐way analysis of variance followed by Tukey's post hoc test. Survival curves were generated using the Kaplan–Meier method and compared using the log‐rank test. Univariate and multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression analyses were performed to identify prognostic factors for overall survival (OS). Variables for the Cox regression analyses were selected based on established clinicopathological prognostic factors in PDAC and were modeled with reference to variables used in previous reports [17]. The multivariate model included age, sex, preoperative CA19‐9 level, pathological T stage, pathological N status, resection margin status, adjuvant chemotherapy, and CD86‐positive mature TLS status. Age was dichotomized at 65 years based on previous reports, and the preoperative CA19‐9 level was dichotomized at 37 U/mL according to the institutional upper limit of normal. All statistical analyses were performed using JMP Pro 18 (SAS Institute) and GraphPad Prism 10 (Dotmatics), and a p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Distribution and Maturation of TLSs in Resected Pancreatic Ductal Adenocarcinoma Specimens

To clarify the distribution of TLSs in PDAC specimens, the TLS evaluation area was defined as a 1‐mm‐wide region centered on the tumor margin and encompassing both the intratumoral and extratumoral sides (Figure 1A,B). The histological features and maturation stages of TLSs were assessed by IHC staining for CD20, a B‐cell marker; CD21, a follicular dendritic cell marker; CD23, a germinal center B‐cell marker; and CD8, a T‐cell marker. TLSs were identified as organized lymphoid structures composed of aggregated immune cells (Figure 1C).

For TLS presence and mature TLS assessment, the concordance rates between the two evaluators were 89.8% and 88.7%, respectively, with corresponding Cohen's κ values of 0.35 and 0.73 (Table S1). Discordant cases were re‐reviewed, and the final classification was determined by consensus between the two evaluators.

The median maximum TLS diameter was 0.53 mm (range, 0.10–1.16 mm), and the median TLS density was 0.17 counts/mm (range, 0.02–1.39 counts/mm).

Among the 128 resected PDAC specimens, TLSs were absent in four cases (3.1%), which were classified as the TLS‐absent group. Among the remaining 124 TLS‐positive cases, 13 cases (10.2%) were classified as having immature TLSs, 21 (16.4%) as having intermediate TLSs, and 90 (70.3%) as having mature TLSs (Figure 1D).

Association Between TLS Maturation, Intratumoral CD8 + and CD4 + T‐Cell Infiltration, and Clinical Outcomes

To evaluate the impact of TLS maturation on the tumor immune microenvironment, intratumoral CD8+ T‐cell infiltration was compared among groups stratified by TLS maturation status. Compared with the TLS‐negative group, the mature TLS group showed significantly higher numbers of intratumoral CD8+ T cells (p = 0.027; Figure 2A,B).

Because B‐cell‐mediated antigen presentation is closely related to CD4+ T‐cell activation, we also assessed intratumoral CD4+ T‐cell infiltration according to TLS maturation status. However, no significant differences in intratumoral CD4+ T‐cell infiltration were observed between the TLS‐negative group and the groups stratified by TLS maturation status.

In survival analyses, patients with mature TLSs showed a trend toward improved OS compared with those without mature TLSs (p = 0.058) and had significantly longer recurrence‐free survival (RFS) (p = 0.029; Figure 2C,D). In addition, patients with TLS absence or immature TLSs showed poorer clinical outcomes, whereas those with intermediate or mature TLSs showed relatively favorable outcomes (Figure S1 A,B).

Characteristics and Significance of CD86‐Positive TLSs in Pancreatic Ductal Adenocarcinoma

CD86 is a costimulatory molecule expressed on antigen‐presenting cells, including B cells. IHC analysis revealed that CD86‐positive cells were rarely observed within the germinal centers of immature and intermediate TLSs. In contrast, among cases with mature TLSs, 51 of 90 cases showed no CD86‐positive cells within the germinal center, whereas 39 cases exhibited clear localization of CD86‐positive cells within the germinal center (Figures 1D and 3A). The concordance rate was 83.3%, with a Cohen's κ value of 0.65 (Table S1). These findings suggest that antigen‐presenting cell populations may be enriched within the germinal center‐like regions of mature TLSs.

The CD86‐positive mature TLS group showed no significant difference in intratumoral CD4+ T‐cell infiltration compared with the CD86‐negative mature TLS group. In contrast, intratumoral CD8+ T‐cell infiltration was significantly higher in the CD86‐positive mature TLS group than in the CD86‐negative mature TLS group (p = 0.002; Figure 3B).

Prognostic Value of CD86‐Positive Mature TLSs in Pancreatic Ductal Adenocarcinoma

In survival analyses, the CD86‐positive mature TLS group showed significantly better OS and RFS than the mature TLS‐absent group (p = 0.010 and p = 0.014, respectively; Figure 3D). The CD86‐positive mature TLS group also showed a trend toward more favorable OS and RFS than the CD86‐negative mature TLS group.

Furthermore, Cox proportional hazards regression analysis showed that the absence of CD86‐positive mature TLSs was significantly associated with poor OS in univariate analysis (hazard ratio: 1.79, 95% CI: 1.11–2.87, p = 0.017; Table 2). However, in multivariate analysis, the absence of CD86‐positive mature TLSs was not identified as an independent prognostic factor for poor OS.

Multiplex Immunofluorescence Analysis of CD86‐Positive B Cells and CD4 + T Cells in CD86‐Positive Mature TLSs

Because CD86 is expressed not only on B cells but also on dendritic cells and macrophages, multiplex immunofluorescence staining for CD86, CD20, and CD4 was performed to confirm the cellular origin of CD86‐positive cells. In addition, because B‐cell‐mediated antigen presentation is closely associated with CD4+ T‐cell activation, the spatial relationship between CD86‐positive B cells and CD4+ T cells was also evaluated.

Multiplex immunofluorescence showed that most CD86‐positive cells within germinal center‐like regions co‐expressed CD20, indicating that these cells were predominantly B cells. CD4+ T cells were observed around CD86‐positive TLSs, and some were located near germinal center‐like regions. However, direct functional interactions between CD86‐positive B cells and CD4+ T cells could not be demonstrated in this study. These findings support the presence of CD86‐positive B cells within mature TLSs, while the functional significance of their spatial relationship with CD4+ T cells should be interpreted cautiously (Figure 4).

Association Between CD86‐Positive Mature TLSs and Granzyme B Expression in CD8 + T Cells

Because the CD86‐positive mature TLS group showed increased intratumoral CD8+ T‐cell infiltration, multiplex immunofluorescence staining for CD8 and Granzyme B was performed to evaluate Granzyme B expression in CD8+ T cells. For this analysis, five cases were randomly selected from each of the CD86‐negative mature TLS and CD86‐positive mature TLS groups (Figure 5A).

Multiplex immunofluorescence staining for CD8 and Granzyme B to evaluate Granzyme B expression in CD8+ T cells. (A) Representative CD8, Granzyme B, bright‐field, and merged images from cases with CD86‐negative mature TLSs and CD86‐positive mature TLSs (scale bar = 20 μm). (B) Bar graph comparing the proportion of Granzyme B+CD8+ T cells between five cases with CD86‐negative mature TLSs and five cases with CD86‐positive mature TLSs.

In each case, five intratumoral hotspot fields with abundant CD8+ T‐cell infiltration were identified, and the proportion of cells co‐expressing CD8 and Granzyme B was evaluated. The CD86‐positive mature TLS group showed a significantly higher proportion of CD8+ T cells co‐expressing Granzyme B among total CD8+ T cells than the CD86‐negative mature TLS group (p = 0.012; Figure 5B). These exploratory findings suggest that CD86‐positive mature TLSs are associated with increased Granzyme B expression among intratumoral CD8+ T cells.

Discussion

In this study, we investigated the relationship among TLS maturation, tumor immune responses, and clinical outcomes in PDAC. TLSs containing CD86‐positive germinal center B cells were predominantly observed in mature TLSs, and patients with CD86‐positive mature TLSs showed increased TLS density and larger TLS size. Moreover, CD86‐positive mature TLSs were associated with increased intratumoral CD8+ T‐cell infiltration and a higher proportion of Granzyme B‐expressing CD8+ T cells among total CD8+ T cells. Multiplex immunofluorescence further confirmed that most CD86‐positive cells within germinal center–like areas co‐expressed CD20, indicating a B‐cell origin, and that some CD4+ T cells were located close to these regions. These findings suggest that CD86‐positive mature TLSs may represent a pathological feature associated with an immunologically active tumor microenvironment, characterized by increased CD8+ T‐cell infiltration and a higher proportion of Granzyme B‐expressing CD8+ T cells in the exploratory analysis. However, this study did not directly demonstrate functional interactions among CD86‐positive B cells, CD4+ T cells, and CD8+ T cells. Clinically, CD86‐positive mature TLSs were associated with favorable OS and RFS, and their absence was significantly associated with poorer OS in univariate analysis.

In TLS research, the reproducibility of histopathological TLS assessment is important. However, the reported frequency of TLS positivity varies widely, ranging from 46% to 100%, which may partly be due to the lack of a standardized TLS definition, particularly regarding size criteria [10, 11, 12, 14]. In the present study, many discrepant cases were related to whether small lymphoid aggregates should be classified as TLSs.

Although the concordance rate for TLS presence was high, Cohen's κ value was relatively low. This should be interpreted with caution, as it may reflect a prevalence effect caused by the predominance of TLS‐positive cases and the small number of TLS‐negative cases.

In contrast, relatively good agreement was observed for TLS maturation and CD86 positivity. Discrepancies may have been caused by overlooked TLSs in cases with numerous TLSs or by weak CD86 positivity within germinal centers. In the future, objective assessment using digital pathology image analysis or AI may help improve the reproducibility of TLS evaluation.

The presence of mature TLSs containing CD86‐positive B cells was found to be significantly associated with increased intratumoral CD8+ T‐cell infiltration, suggesting that CD86‐positive mature TLSs may reflect an immunologically active tumor microenvironment rather than passive lymphoid aggregates. These findings suggest that CD86‐positive mature TLSs may be associated with localized antitumor immune activation. Accumulating evidence indicates that B cells exert immunological functions beyond antibody production, including regulation of T‐cell responses through antigen presentation, and contribute to immune response formation within the tumor microenvironment [18]. Although B‐cell–mediated antigen presentation is not restricted to organized lymphoid structures, it has been observed in both inflammatory tissues and tumor microenvironments independent of TLS formation [15, 19, 20]. The organized architecture of TLSs is thought to provide a distinct functional advantage by facilitating close spatial interactions between B cells and T cells.

CD86 is a prototypical costimulatory molecule that promotes T‐cell activation through interaction with CD28 and is considered a marker of activated antigen‐presenting B cells [21]. Previous studies have demonstrated that CD86‐positive B cells accumulate within tumor‐associated TLSs and have been reported to promote CD4+ T‐cell activation through major histocompatibility complex Class II‐dependent tumor antigen presentation and CD86‐mediated costimulatory signaling, thereby supporting the maintenance of CD8+ T‐cell responses [15, 18]. In addition, activated B cells within TLSs have been reported to facilitate T‐cell recruitment, Th1 polarization, and sustained cytotoxic T‐cell activity through chemokine production and other immunoregulatory mechanisms based on their antigen‐presenting capacity and expression of costimulatory molecules [15, 18]. Collectively, previous studies [15, 18], suggest that CD86‐positive B cells may contribute to, or serve as markers of, localized immune activation; however, the present study cannot distinguish between these possibilities. Importantly, the present findings suggest that CD86‐positive mature TLSs may represent pathological features associated with localized immune activation within the otherwise immunologically “cold” PDAC microenvironment.

In pancreatic cancer, TLS presence has been reported as a predictive marker of favorable responses to both chemotherapy and immune checkpoint inhibitor therapy [22, 23]. T cells residing within TLSs in PDAC have been shown to co‐express multiple immune checkpoint receptors, including programmed cell death protein 1 and T‐cell immunoreceptor with immunoglobulin and immunoreceptor tyrosine‐based inhibitory motif domains, along with their respective ligands, programmed death‐ligand 1 and CD155 [24]. These observations suggest that TLSs are not merely passive lymphoid aggregates but rather function as immunological hubs in which immune activation and immune suppression are simultaneously regulated. Taken together, these characteristics support the rationale for combinatorial immune checkpoint blockade targeting TLS‐localized T‐cell populations as a potentially effective therapeutic strategy for PDAC, a malignancy characterized by profound immunosuppression.

To overcome the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment of PDAC, therapeutic strategies aimed at promoting TLS formation and maturation may be beneficial. TLS development has been shown to be closely associated with the formation of HEVs and the expression of lymphoid chemokines such as C‐X‐C motif chemokine ligand 13 [25, 26]. HEVs formed at the periphery of TLSs regulate intratumoral trafficking of lymphocytes, and induction of HEV‐like tumor vasculature itself may represent a promising strategy to promote TLS formation. Recent reviews, largely based on preclinical evidence, have suggested that combination therapy with immune checkpoint inhibitors and anti‐angiogenic agents can reprogram tumor vasculature toward an HEV phenotype, thereby enhancing TLS formation and antitumor immunity [27, 28]. Furthermore, in pancreatic cancer models, intratumoral administration of C‐X‐C motif chemokine ligand 13 and C‐C motif chemokine ligand 21 in combination with gemcitabine has been shown to promote robust TLS formation and induce tumor regression not achievable with chemotherapy alone [29]. Importantly, the present findings suggest that TLS maturation, density, size, and CD86 positivity may collectively characterize TLSs associated with an immunologically active tumor microenvironment. However, the current data cannot determine whether CD86 expression has an independent biological role or instead reflects larger and more highly mature TLSs.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, this was a retrospective, two‐institution study with a limited sample size, and selection bias cannot be excluded. Validation in larger, multi‐institutional cohorts is warranted. Second, although whole‐slide imaging was employed, analyses were based on selected FFPE sections, which may not fully capture tumor heterogeneity. Third, although multiplex immunofluorescence showed that some CD4+ T cells were located near CD86‐positive mature TLSs, this spatial proximity does not establish a functional interaction between CD86‐positive B cells and CD4+ T cells. Moreover, intratumoral CD4+ T‐cell infiltration was not significantly associated with TLS maturation or CD86‐positive mature TLS status. Therefore, the present data do not provide direct evidence for a mechanistic cascade involving CD86‐positive B cells, CD4+ T cells, and CD8+ T‐cell activation. In addition, while exploratory multiplex immunofluorescence for CD8 and Granzyme B suggested an increased proportion of Granzyme B‐expressing CD8+ T cells in tumors with CD86‐positive mature TLSs, other cytotoxic markers (e.g., perforin) and exhaustion markers (e.g., PD‐1, TIGIT, and LAG‐3) were not evaluated. Therefore, the functional state of CD8+ T cells could not be comprehensively assessed in this study. Furthermore, the mechanisms by which CD86‐positive B cells mediate antigen presentation and are associated with cytotoxic T‐cell responses were not directly examined. Further mechanistic studies using in vitro and in vivo models are required. In the future, assessment of TLS maturation may contribute to optimizing therapeutic strategies and predicting immunotherapy efficacy in PDAC. In addition, CD86‐positive mature TLSs showed greater TLS density and larger TLS diameter than CD86‐negative mature TLSs. Therefore, the present study cannot determine whether CD86 expression itself has an independent biological role or whether it simply serves as a pathological marker of larger and more highly mature TLSs. CD86 positivity should therefore be interpreted as a pathological feature associated with an immunologically active tumor microenvironment rather than as evidence that these TLSs are functionally active.

Finally, although TLS maturation was classified using previously reported IHC criteria based on CD8, CD20, CD21, CD23, and CD86 expression and showed acceptable interobserver agreement, discrepancies were observed in identifying immature TLSs, particularly in distinguishing small lymphoid aggregates from TLSs. In addition, evaluation of CD86 status required careful assessment, especially in cases with numerous or small TLSs. Therefore, standardized criteria, thorough review of all TLSs within each section, and independent or repeated evaluations may improve the reproducibility of TLS maturation assessment and CD86‐positive mature TLS assessment.

Conclusions

CD86‐positive mature TLSs were associated with increased TLS density and size, increased intratumoral CD8+ T‐cell infiltration, and favorable clinical outcomes in PDAC. Exploratory multiplex immunofluorescence analysis suggested a higher proportion of Granzyme B‐expressing CD8+ T cells in tumors with CD86‐positive mature TLSs. These findings indicate that CD86‐positive mature TLSs may serve as a pathological feature associated with an immunologically active tumor microenvironment, although further mechanistic studies are required to clarify their functional role.
