Private Information Retrieval over Graphs
Gennian Ge, Hao Wang, Zixiang Xu, Yijun Zhang

Abstract
The problem of Private Information Retrieval (PIR) in graph-based replication systems has received significant attention in recent years. A systematic study was conducted by Sadeh, Gu, and Tamo, where each file is replicated across two servers and the storage topology is modeled by a graph. The PIR capacity of a graph G G , denoted by 𝒞 ⁡ ( G ) \mathcal{C}(G) , is defined as the supremum of retrieval rates achievable by schemes that preserve user privacy, with the rate measured as the ratio between the file size and the total number of bits downloaded. This paper makes the following key contributions. • The complete graph K N K_{N} has emerged as a central benchmark in the study of PIR over graphs. The asymptotic gap between the upper and lower bounds for 𝒞 ⁡ ( K N ) \mathcal{C}(K_{N}) was previously 2 and was only recently reduced to 5 3 \frac{5}{3} by Kong, Meel, Maranzatto, Tamo, and Ulukus. We shrink this gap to 3 4 ​ ( e − 2 ) ≈ 1.0444 \frac{3}{4(e-2)}\approx 1.0444 , bringing it close to resolution. More precisely, (1) Sadeh, Gu, and Tamo proved that 𝒞 ⁡ ( K N ) ≤ 2 N + 1 \mathcal{C}(K_{N})\leq\frac{2}{N+1} and conjectured this bound to be tight. We refute this conjecture by establishing the strictly stronger bound 𝒞 ⁡ ( K N ) ≤ 1 e − 2 ⋅ 1 N ≈ 1.3922 N . \mathcal{C}(K_{N})\leq\frac{1}{e-2}\cdot\frac{1}{N}\approx\frac{1.3922}{N}. Our proof combines graph-theoretic insights with information-theoretic analysis. Within the same framework, we also improve the upper bound for the balanced complete bipartite graph 𝒞 ⁡ ( K N / 2 , N / 2 ) \mathcal{C}(K_{N/2,N/2}) from 2 N \frac{2}{N} to 1.5415 N \frac{1.5415}{N} asymptotically. (2) The first general lower bound on 𝒞 ⁡ ( K N ) \mathcal{C}(K_{N}) , due to Sadeh, Gu, and Tamo, was 2 N − 1 2 N − 1 − 1 ⋅ 1 N \frac{2^{N-1}}{2^{N-1}-1}\cdot\frac{1}{N} , which was recently sharpened to 6 5 − 2 3 − N ⋅ 1 N \frac{6}{5-2^{3-N}}\cdot\frac{1}{N} by Kong, Meel, Maranzatto, Tamo, and Ulukus. We provide explicit, systematic constructions that further improve this bound, proving 𝒞 ⁡ ( K N ) ≥ ( 4 3 − o ⁡ ( 1 ) ) ⋅ 1 N , \mathcal{C}(K_{N})\ \geq\Bigl(\frac{4}{3}-o(1)\Bigr)\cdot\frac{1}{N}, which in particular implies 𝒞 ⁡ ( G ) ≥ ( 4 3 − o ⁡ ( 1 ) ) ⋅ 1 | G | \mathcal{C}(G)\geq\Bigl(\frac{4}{3}-o(1)\Bigr)\cdot\frac{1}{|G|} for every graph G G . • We establish a conceptual bridge between deterministic and probabilistic PIR schemes on graphs. This connection has significant implications for reducing the required subpacketization in practical implementations and is of independent interest. We also design a general probabilistic PIR scheme that performs particularly well on sparse graphs.



1
Introduction

1.1
Background

Private Information Retrieval (PIR), introduced by Chor, Goldreich, Kushilevitz, and Sudan
[
3
]
, enables a user to retrieve a specific file from a public (replicated) database without revealing its identity (
privacy requirement
) while guaranteeing successful reconstruction (
reliability requirement
). In the information-theoretic framework established by Sun and Jafar
[
16
]
, servers are assumed non-colluding and file length can be arbitrarily large, rendering the upload (query) cost negligible so that the download (answer) cost dominates. Under this model, the efficiency of a PIR scheme is measured by the
PIR rate
, which is the number of desired message bits retrieved per bit of download, and the supremum over all schemes is the
PIR capacity
, which is determined exactly in the fully replicated setting
[
16
]
. Capacity-achieving designs typically fall into two complementary paradigms: (i)
deterministic
coding schemes inspired by blind interference alignment
[
16
]
, and (ii)
probabilistic
schemes whose randomized queries keep every candidate message equiprobable at each server
[
11
,
21
]
.

A crucial systems consideration is
subpacketization
, the segmentation of each file into multiple equal-sized parts during retrieval
[
12
]
. Excessive subpacketization can impose unrealistic constraints on file sizes, metadata overheads, and update complexity, limiting deployability even when rates are information-theoretically optimal. The importance of subpacketization extends beyond PIR, most notably to
coded caching
, where reducing subpacketization is a central practical challenge and has driven extensive research
[
26
,
27
,
25
,
13
]
. Thus, both capacity and subpacketization must be addressed for implementable protocols.

While coding for storage is powerful and widely studied, large-scale systems still heavily rely on
replication
for simplicity, ease of updates, and high availability, despite lower storage efficiency and fault tolerance compared to coded storage
[
14
,
15
,
29
]
. At the same time, fully replicating every file across all servers becomes increasingly impractical at modern scales. This tension motivates PIR under
partial replication
, naturally modeled by (hyper)graphs: vertices represent servers and (hyper)edges represent files, each stored on the incident servers. When each file is stored on exactly
r
servers, we obtain an
r
-replication system; in particular,
2
-replication corresponds to simple graphs
[
9
]
. Within this framework, graph topology directly affects privacy structure and retrieval efficiency.

Building on this viewpoint, a growing body of work has analyzed graph-based PIR. For
2
-replication (simple graphs), Raviv, Tamo and Yaakobi
[
9
]
initiated the study under colluding servers and showed how topology shapes achievable schemes. Capacity bounds for regular graphs and exact capacity for cycles were obtained in
[
2
]
. Upper bounds and improved constructions for several canonical topologies, such as complete graphs, stars, and complete bipartite graphs were developed in
[
10
]
, while the exact capacity of the
4
-file star was determined in
[
28
]
. More recently, Kong, Meel, Maranzatto, Tamo, and Ulukus
[
6
]
extended this line to
multigraph-based
PIR with finite uniform multiplicity, in which each edge of the underlying simple graph is replaced by parallel edges, enabling richer replication patterns and new design possibilities.

Parallel to these advances, several orthogonal generalizations have been pursued: collusion-resistant PIR and its fundamental limits
[
18
,
17
,
4
]
, coded PIR with storage-retrieval tradeoffs via MDS coding
[
1
,
7
,
20
,
30
,
31
,
32
]
, and symmetric PIR that enforces mutual privacy so the user learns nothing beyond the requested file
[
19
,
24
]
. Within this framework, Meel and Ulukus
[
8
]
further investigated graph-based symmetric PIR and established fundamental results in this setting. We refer to
[
22
,
23
]
for comprehensive surveys.

Despite substantial progress, the capacity of general (hyper)graph-based PIR systems remains largely unresolved even without collusion; this is true already for basic
2
-replication models. Among graph families,
complete graphs
play a pivotal role: as observed in
[
10
]
and formalized in our work, any PIR scheme over the complete graph
K_{N}
can be converted into a PIR scheme over
any

N
-vertex simple graph (see
Lemma

2.2
). Consequently, tightening the capacity bounds for
K_{N}
is a key step toward understanding broader classes of graphs. In this paper, we significantly sharpen these bounds for complete (and balanced complete bipartite) graphs, and we build a conceptual bridge between deterministic and probabilistic graph-based PIR that enables
very small subpacketization
, especially on sparse topologies.

1.2
Problem statement and notations

For a positive integer
a
, let
[a]:=\{1,2,\ldots,a\}
. For integers
a<b
, the discrete interval is denoted by
[a,b]:=\{a,a+1,\ldots,b\}
. Given a family
\{A_{i}\}_{i\in I}
of objects indexed by a set
I
, and a subset
J\subseteq I
, we denote the corresponding subcollection by
A_{J}:=\{A_{i}:i\in J\}
. For discrete random variables
A
and
B
,
H(A)
denotes the entropy of
A
, and
I(A;B)
the mutual information between
A
and
B
. Furthermore, for a collection of random variables
\mathcal{A}=\{A_{1},A_{2},\dots,A_{n}\}
, we use the shorthand
H(\mathcal{A})=H(A_{1},A_{2},\dots,A_{n})
. The notations
H(B\mid\mathcal{A})
and
I(\mathcal{A};B)
are defined analogously.

In this work, we study the PIR problem in the setting of graph-based storage systems. Specifically, we mainly consider a
2-replication system
modeled by a simple undirected graph, where each file is stored on exactly two servers, and any pair of servers shares at most one file. We further assume that servers are
non-colluding
, meaning that they do not exchange information with one another.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a simple undirected graph with
|V|=N
vertices and
|E|=K
edges. The vertices
V=\{S_{1},S_{2},\dots,S_{N}\}
represent servers, and each edge
e=\{i,j\}\in E
corresponds to a unique file
W_{\{i,j\}}
stored on both servers
S_{i}
and
S_{j}
. For notational simplicity, we denote
W_{\{i,j\}}
simply as
W_{i,j}
(or equivalently,
W_{j,i}
).

Let
\mathcal{W}=\{W_{e}:e\in E\}
denote the set of total files in the system. For each server
S_{i}
, we define the set of files stored on it as

\mathcal{W}_{S_{i}}:=\{W_{i,j}:(i,j)\in E\}.

As standard, we assume all files are of equal size. This model is widely applicable, as it captures a key aspect of many distributed storage systems: their architecture is fundamentally defined by an underlying graph structure that governs file placement.

In a PIR scheme, each file is typically divided into several equal-sized sub-files. For instance, a file
W
may be partitioned as
W=(W_{1},W_{2},W_{3})
. To ensure privacy, the user privately and uniformly selects a random permutation
\sigma
of the index set
[3]
, and defines the permuted sequence
(w_{1},w_{2},w_{3}):=(W_{\sigma(1)},W_{\sigma(2)},W_{\sigma(3)})
. Thus, recovering the original file
W
is equivalent to retrieving all permuted sub-files
w_{1},w_{2},w_{3}
.

We assume that all files are equally subpacketized into
L
sub-files, where
L
is referred to as the
subpacketization level
of the PIR scheme. This parameter
L
serves as a key performance metric. For each file, the user privately generates a permutation of
[L]
to shuffle its sub-file indices; let
\mathcal{G}
denote the collection of these permutations. Each sub-file is treated as a single symbol (a dit) in a finite field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
. Consequently, the entropy of each file is
L
dits, i.e.,
H(W)=L
.

As we have mentioned above, excessive subpacketization might hinder practical deployment, as the number of sub-files could exceed the actual file size. Therefore, among schemes with equal retrieval rates, lower subpacketization is generally preferred for better efficiency and applicability.

In the PIR problem, a user privately generates
\theta\in E(G)
and wishes to retrieve
W_{\theta}
(i.e., retrieve all permuted sub-files of
W_{\theta}
) while keeping
\theta
hidden from each server. To achieve this privacy goal, various scheme design methodologies have been developed. Broadly speaking, there exist two primary approaches in the literature: the deterministic approach, as used in
[
16
]
, and the probabilistic approach, as in
[
11
,
21
]
.

In the deterministic approach, once
\theta
and
\mathcal{G}
are fixed, the queries to be sent to the servers are completely determined. That is, the user constructs queries
Q_{1}^{[\theta,\mathcal{G}]},Q_{2}^{[\theta,\mathcal{G}]},\dots,Q_{N}^{[\theta,\mathcal{G}]}
, where
Q_{i}^{[\theta,\mathcal{G}]}
is sent to server
S_{i}
. To ensure privacy, the core idea is to enforce
message symmetry
in the queries, so that all files are treated uniformly in the view of each server.

In contrast, the probabilistic approach incorporates additional private randomness. Specifically, it relies on a set of universal queries that, from the perspective of any individual server, are used for all possible requested files with equal probability. The user first constructs a universal query set based on
\theta
and
\mathcal{G}
, and then privately samples a random variable
R
. The queries sent to each server are selected from the universal query set according to the value of
R
. That is, the queries
Q_{1}^{[\theta,\mathcal{G},R]},Q_{2}^{[\theta,\mathcal{G},R]},\dots,Q_{N}^{[\theta,\mathcal{G},R]}
are constructed based on
\theta
,
\mathcal{G}
, and
R
. Privacy is ensured by enforcing
query symmetry
, meaning that the distribution of the query sent to each server is independent of the requested file index.

Throughout the paper, when the dependence on
\theta
,
\mathcal{G}
, and possibly
R
, is clear from context, we omit these superscripts and simply write
Q_{i}
to refer to
Q_{i}^{[\theta,\mathcal{G}]}
or
Q_{i}^{[\theta,\mathcal{G},R]}
. Let
\mathcal{Q}:=\{Q_{i}:i\in[N]\}
denote the set of all queries sent to the servers. We assume that the requested messages in each
Q_{i}
consist of linear combinations of sub-files from the files stored on server
S_{i}
, which is a standard assumption satisfied by all known PIR schemes.

Since the user has no prior knowledge about the contents of the files, the queries must be statistically independent of the file data, which can be formally expressed as:

I(\mathcal{W};\mathcal{Q})=0.

Upon receiving query
Q_{i}
, server
S_{i}
returns an answer
A_{i}
, which is deterministically computed as a function of
Q_{i}
and the set of files it stores, denoted by
\mathcal{W}_{S_{i}}
. Hence, for every
i\in[N]
, the response satisfies:

H(A_{i}\mid Q_{i},\mathcal{W}_{S_{i}})=0.

(1)

Let
\Pi
be a PIR scheme on a graph-based storage system with
N
servers and
K
files, each of entropy
L
. The scheme defines, for every desired file index
\theta\in[K]
, a random query distribution and a deterministic answering mechanism for each server. A PIR scheme must satisfy two fundamental requirements:
reliability
and
privacy
, defined as follows.

Reliability:

The user must be able to recover the requested file
W_{\theta}
with zero probability of error using the answers
A_{1},A_{2},\dots,A_{N}
received from the servers and the queries
\mathcal{Q}
. Formally,

H(W_{\theta}\mid A_{[N]},\mathcal{Q})=0,

(2)

where
A_{[N]}:=\{A_{1},A_{2},\dots,A_{N}\}
.

Privacy:

No individual server should gain any information about the identity of the requested file. Formally, this means that for every server
i\in[N]
and every requested file
\theta\in E(G)
, the joint distribution of the query
Q_{i}^{[\theta]}
, the answer
A_{i}^{[\theta]}
, and the set of files
\mathcal{W}_{S_{i}}
stored on server
S_{i}
must be independent of
\theta
. That is,

(Q_{i}^{[\theta]},A_{i}^{[\theta]},\mathcal{W}_{S_{i}})\sim(Q_{i}^{[1]},A_{i}^{[1]},\mathcal{W}_{S_{i}}),

(3)

where
X\sim Y
indicates that the random variables
X
and
Y
are identically distributed.

An equivalent formulation in terms of mutual information is given by

I(\theta;Q_{i},A_{i},\mathcal{W}_{S_{i}})=0,

which, using the relationship in (
1
), simplifies to the conditional entropy condition

H(\theta\mid Q_{i},\mathcal{W}_{S_{i}})=H(\theta).

(4)

The central performance measure of a PIR scheme is its
retrieval rate
, which represents the ratio of the total amount of information downloaded from the servers.

Definition 1.1

(Retrieval rate of a PIR scheme)
.

The
retrieval rate
of a PIR scheme
\Pi
, denoted by
R(\Pi)
, is defined as the ratio between the retrieved file size in bits, and the total number of bits downloaded as answers, i.e.,

R(\Pi):=\frac{L}{\sum_{i=1}^{N}H(A_{i})},

where
A_{i}
is the answer sent by server
S_{i}
under scheme
\Pi
. This quantity measures the ratio of the desired information retrieved to the total amount of information downloaded from the servers.

It is standard in the PIR literature to assume that the file size can be made arbitrarily large. Under this assumption, the upload cost (that is, the cost of sending queries) becomes negligible compared to the download cost (that is, the cost of receiving answers). As a result, the above definition coincides with alternative definitions that condition on the queries:

R(\Pi):=\frac{L}{\sum_{i=1}^{N}H(A_{i}\mid Q_{i})}.

We now define the
capacity
of a graph-based PIR system.

Definition 1.2

(PIR capacity of a graph)
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a simple undirected graph with
N=|V|
servers and
K=|E|
files, where each file is stored on the two servers corresponding to its endpoints. The
PIR capacity
of
G
, denoted by
\mathcal{C}(G)
, is defined as

\mathcal{C}(G):=\sup_{\Pi}R(\Pi),

where the supremum is taken over all valid PIR schemes
\Pi
on
G
, and
R(\Pi)
denotes the retrieval rate achieved by scheme
\Pi
. A PIR scheme is said to be
capacity-achieving
or
optimal
if it achieves this supremum.

Then the main theoretical problems in this field can be stated as follows.

Question 1.3
.

For given graph
G
, determine
\mathcal{C}(G)
.

While this is a central question from a theoretical perspective, a practically motivated problem might be more important.

Question 1.4
.

For given graph
G
, construct PIR schemes over
G
that achieve large capacity and operate with small subpacketization.

1.3
Related work and known results

The study of PIR in graph-based replication systems has garnered growing interest in recent years. This model was first introduced by Raviv, Tamo, and Yaakobi
[
9
]
, and further explored in works such as
[
2
,
5
]
. A more systematic treatment was later given by Sadeh, Gu, and Tamo
[
10
]
, who focused specifically on
1.3
.

The
complete graph
on
N
vertices, denoted
K_{N}
, is the undirected graph in which every pair of distinct vertices is connected by exactly one edge. Among all graph classes, the complete graph
K_{N}
plays a particularly central role in the study of PIR. Any PIR scheme designed for
K_{N}
can be naturally restricted to its subgraphs, making
K_{N}
a universal benchmark. Moreover, its high degree of symmetry renders it both theoretically appealing and technically tractable.

The graph
K_{N}
contains
\binom{N}{2}
edges, each corresponding to a distinct file in the PIR setting. In
[
10
,
2
]
, the authors established the following general bounds on its PIR capacity.

Theorem 1.5

(
[
10
,
2
]
)
.

The PIR capacity
\mathcal{C}(K_{N})
satisfies the following bounds:

(1)

For any integer
N\geq 3
,
\frac{2^{N-1}}{2^{N-1}-1}\cdot\frac{1}{N}\leq\mathcal{C}(K_{N})\leq\frac{2}{N+1}.

(2)

\mathcal{C}(K_{3})=\frac{1}{2}\ \textup{and\ }\mathcal{C}(K_{4})\geq\frac{3}{10}.

Beyond the complete graphs, Sadeh, Gu, and Tamo
[
10
]
investigated a variety of graph families, including stars, cycles, wheel graphs, and complete bipartite graphs, deriving refined capacity bounds through a blend of combinatorial constructions and information-theoretic techniques. A key general upper bound they established asserts that for any graph
G
,

\mathcal{C}(G)\leq\min\left\{\frac{\Delta(G)}{|E(G)|},\,\frac{1}{\nu(G)}\right\},

where
\Delta(G)
denotes the maximum degree of
G
, and
\nu(G)
its matching number.

Very recently, Kong, Meel, Maranzatto, Tamo and Ulukus
[
6
]
made significant progress in characterizing the PIR capacity
\mathcal{C}(G)
for several fundamental graph topologies, including paths, complete bipartite graphs, and complete graphs. Of particular importance is their construction of an efficient PIR scheme over complete graphs
K_{N}
, which achieves a strictly higher retrieval rate compared to prior approaches in
[
10
,
2
]
.

Theorem 1.6

(
[
6
]
)
.

For any integer
N\geq 3
, the PIR capacity satisfies

\mathcal{C}(K_{N})\geq\frac{6}{5-2^{3-N}}\cdot\frac{1}{N}.

For a general graph
G
, applying the PIR scheme designed for the complete graph to
G
yields a lower bound on the retrieval rate achievable for any arbitrary simple graph. Therefore,
Theorem

1.6
also serves as a lower bound for
\mathcal{C}(G)
for any graph
G
.

Another particularly interesting class of graphs is the family of complete bipartite graphs. Let
K_{M,N}
denote the complete bipartite graph with two sets of vertices of sizes
M
and
N
. The best-known bounds on their PIR capacity are given below.

Theorem 1.7

(
[
6
,
10
]
)
.

For any integers

, the PIR capacity satisfies

\max\left\{\frac{6}{5-2^{3-(M+N)}}\cdot\frac{1}{M+N},\frac{1}{2M\sqrt{N}+M}\right\}\leq\mathcal{C}(K_{M,N})\leq\min\left\{\frac{1}{M},\frac{1}{\sqrt{2MN}-\frac{M}{2}}\right\}.

Since parts of our results also apply to PIR schemes over multigraphs, we briefly introduce some relevant background and results. Let
G=(V,E)
be a simple graph with
|E|=K^{\prime}
. We define the
r
-multigraph extension
of
G

PIR schemes over

Definition 1.8

(Symmetric retrieval property
[
6
]
)
.

A graph-based PIR scheme is said to satisfy the

(SRP) if, for every possible file index

retrieved from each of the two servers storing it is equal. For instance, if
\theta=k

W_{k}
is replicated across servers
S_{1}
and
S_{2}

(
[
6
]
)

Let
\Pi

. Then there exists a scheme
\Pi^{(r)}
for the corresponding
r

that achieves the following lower bound on capacity:

\mathcal{C}(G^{(r)})\geq R(\Pi)\cdot\left(\frac{1}{2-\frac{1}{2^{r-1}}}\right).

1.4
Our main contributions

The aforementioned results form a foundation for the study of graph-based PIR schemes. Among them, the complete graph
K_{N}
stands out due to its universality and high degree of symmetry. Any PIR scheme designed for
K_{N}
can be naturally adapted to arbitrary simple graphs with
N
vertices, making the task of determining tight bounds for
\mathcal{C}(K_{N})
a central challenge in the field.

In
[
10
]
, it was conjectured that the general upper bound
\frac{2}{N+1}
might be tight for the complete graph
K_{N}
, and that this rate could potentially be achieved via a more sophisticated PIR scheme. In this work, we disprove this conjecture by establishing a strictly improved upper bound valid for all
N\geq 4
. Our main result is as follows.

Theorem 1.10
.

For any integer
N\geq 3
,

\mathcal{C}(K_{N})\leq\frac{1}{\sum_{i=2}^{N}\frac{1}{i!}}\cdot\frac{1}{N}.

This new upper bound exactly characterizes
\mathcal{C}(K_{3})
and strictly improves on the previously conjectured bound
\frac{2}{N+1}
for all
N\geq 4
. In particular, it asymptotically approaches

\frac{1}{e-2}\cdot\frac{1}{N}\approx\frac{1.3922}{N},

which is a significant tightening compared to the former
\frac{2}{N+1}
bound. This result reveals a new structural limitation of PIR schemes over complete graphs, providing new insights into how symmetry and full connectivity constrain the capacity. Moreover, our techniques extend naturally to yield improved upper bounds for the PIR capacity of some other symmetry graphs, especially complete bipartite graphs.

Theorem 1.11
.

For any even integer
N\geq 4
,

\mathcal{C}\left(K_{\frac{N}{2},\frac{N}{2}}\right)\leq\frac{1}{\sum_{i=1}^{N/2}\frac{1}{i!\cdot 2^{i}}}\cdot\frac{1}{N}.

Note that
Theorem

1.11
represents a substantial improvement over the previously known bound in
Theorem

1.7
for the capacity of
K_{\frac{N}{2},\frac{N}{2}}
. Prior work established an upper bound

\mathcal{C}\left(K_{\frac{N}{2},\frac{N}{2}}\right)\leq\frac{2}{N}.

In contrast, our bound is strictly tighter for all even
N\geq 4
since

\sum_{i=1}^{N/2}\frac{1}{i!\cdot 2^{i}}<e^{1/2}-1\approx 0.6487,

implying an upper bound around
\frac{1.5415}{N}
when
N
goes to infinity.

On the other hand, for any
N\geq 3
, we construct an explicit PIR scheme over
K_{N}
. Furthermore, when
N
is sufficiently large, this construction yields an improved lower bound on
\mathcal{C}(K_{N})
, as described below.

Theorem 1.12
.

For any integer
N\geq 3
,

\mathcal{C}(K_{N})\geq\left(\frac{4}{3}-\epsilon_{N}\right)\cdot\frac{1}{N},

where
\epsilon_{N}
tends to zero as
N

\frac{4}{3}

In general, we construct a probabilistic PIR scheme that applies to arbitrary (multi-)graphs. The proposed scheme is simple, universally applicable, and requires only subpacketization 1. Compared to existing constructions, it achieves a higher retrieval rate for certain classes of graphs, particularly when the underlying graph is relatively sparse.

For any (multi-)graph

Upper bounds for PIR capacity

2.1
General framework to upper bound
\mathcal{C}(G)

In this section, we establish a general framework for upper bounding
\mathcal{C}(G)
in the case of several symmetric graph families. We begin with introducing a fundamental lemma in
[
10
]
that formalizes the privacy constraint: the query sent to any individual server must be statistically independent of the identity of the desired file.

Lemma 2.1

(
[
10
]
)
.

Let
G
be a graph with
N
vertices, and let
\Lambda\subseteq E(G)
be a subset of its edges. Define
\mathcal{W}_{\Lambda}:=\{W_{e}\mid e\in\Lambda\}
. Then, for any PIR scheme over
G
, for any edge
\theta\in E(G)
and any server index
i\in[N]
, the following holds:

H(A_{i}\mid\mathcal{W}_{\Lambda},Q_{i},\theta)=H(A_{i}\mid\mathcal{W}_{\Lambda},Q_{i}).

(6)

When designing a PIR scheme over a graph, the structure of the graph plays a pivotal role. The following lemma, which will be used throughout the paper, formalizes this dependency. While the core idea was previously discussed in
[
10
]
, a complete formal proof was not provided; for completeness, we include it here.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
be two graphs such that
G_{1}
is a subgraph of
G_{2}
. Then, any PIR scheme over
G_{2}
can be adapted to construct a valid PIR scheme over
G_{1}
.

Proof of
Lemma

2.2
.

For convenience and without loss of generality, we assume that
G_{1}
is a spanning subgraph of
G_{2}
. In fact, if this is not the case, we can extend
V(G_{1})
by adding isolated vertices to match the larger one, as isolated vertices do not affect the entropy of the server responses. Let us first fix a PIR scheme over
G_{2}
. Let
S_{1},\dots,S_{N}
denote the
N
vertices of both
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
. For each edge
e\in E(G_{j})
, let
W_{e}^{j}
denote the corresponding file in graph
G_{j}
, and let
\mathcal{W}_{S_{i}}^{j}
denote the set of files stored on server
S_{i}
in graph
G_{j}
, for
j=1,2
. Let
\mathcal{Q}^{j}=\{Q_{i}^{j}\mid i\in[N]\}
denote the set of all queries sent to the servers in graph
G_{j}
, and let
A_{i}^{j}
be the corresponding answer from server
S_{i}
, for
j=1,2
.

For any
\theta\in E(G_{2})
chosen by the user, it follows from
Lemma

2.1
that each query
Q_{i}^{2}
is independent of
\theta
, which implies

H(\theta\mid Q_{i}^{2},\mathcal{W}_{S_{i}}^{2})=H(\theta).

(7)

Moreover, by the reliability condition (
2
) for graph
G_{2}
, we have

H(W_{\theta}\mid A_{[N]}^{2},\mathcal{Q}^{2})=0.

(8)

We now construct a PIR scheme for graph
G_{1}
by leveraging the existing scheme designed for
G_{2}
. For any
\theta\in E(G_{1})
, the queries in the PIR scheme consist of summations that are linear combinations of sub-files from the files stored at each server. Specifically, we define
Q_{i}^{1}
as the query obtained from
Q_{i}^{2}
by removing all sub-files belonging to files in
\mathcal{W}_{S_{i}}^{2}\setminus\mathcal{W}_{S_{i}}^{1}
. In other words,
Q_{i}^{1}
is the restriction of
Q_{i}^{2}
to the file set
\mathcal{W}_{S_{i}}^{1}
.

Under this construction, the following identities hold:

H(Q_{i}^{1}\mid Q_{i}^{2})=0,

(9)

H(A_{i}^{2}\mid A_{i}^{1},\mathcal{W}_{S_{i}}^{2}\setminus\mathcal{W}_{S_{i}}^{1},Q_{i}^{2})=0,

(10)

H(A_{i}^{1}\mid A_{i}^{2},\mathcal{W}_{S_{i}}^{2}\setminus\mathcal{W}_{S_{i}}^{1},Q_{i}^{2})=0.

(11)

Equation (
9
) follows from the fact that
Q_{i}^{1}
is entirely determined by
Q_{i}^{2}
. Equations (
10
) and (
11
) follow from the observation that the difference between
A_{i}^{1}
and
A_{i}^{2}
consists solely of linear combinations of sub-files of files in
\mathcal{W}_{S_{i}}^{2}\setminus\mathcal{W}_{S_{i}}^{1}
, which are themselves fully determined by
\mathcal{W}_{S_{i}}^{2}\setminus\mathcal{W}_{S_{i}}^{1}
and
Q_{i}^{2}
.

We now verify that the derived PIR scheme over
G_{1}
satisfies both the reliability and privacy requirements.

Reliability:

For any
\theta\in E(G_{1})
, we analyze the entropy of the desired file conditioned on the available information:

\displaystyle H(W_{\theta}\mid A_{[N]}^{1},\mathcal{Q}^{1})

\displaystyle=H(W_{\theta}\mid A_{[N]}^{1},\mathcal{Q}^{1})+H(\mathcal{Q}^{2}\mid A_{[N]}^{1},\mathcal{Q}^{1})-H(\mathcal{Q}^{2}\mid A_{[N]}^{1},\mathcal{Q}^{1})

\displaystyle=H(W_{\theta}\mid A_{[N]}^{1},\mathcal{Q}^{1})+H(\mathcal{Q}^{2}\mid W_{\theta},A_{[N]}^{1},\mathcal{Q}^{1})-H(\mathcal{Q}^{2}\mid A_{[N]}^{1},\mathcal{Q}^{1})

(12)

\displaystyle=H(W_{\theta},\mathcal{Q}^{2}\mid A_{[N]}^{1},\mathcal{Q}^{1})-H(\mathcal{Q}^{2}\mid A_{[N]}^{1},\mathcal{Q}^{1})

\displaystyle=H(W_{\theta}\mid A_{[N]}^{1},\mathcal{Q}^{1},\mathcal{Q}^{2})

\displaystyle=H(W_{\theta}\mid A_{[N]}^{1},\mathcal{W}^{2}\setminus\mathcal{W}^{1},\mathcal{Q}^{2})

(13)

\displaystyle=H(W_{\theta}\mid A_{[N]}^{1},A_{[N]}^{2},\mathcal{W}^{2}\setminus\mathcal{W}^{1},\mathcal{Q}^{2})

(14)

\displaystyle\leq H(W_{\theta}\mid A_{[N]}^{2},\mathcal{Q}^{2}),

where (
12
) follows from the fact that the queries are generated independently of the specific file contents and server responses; (
13
) follows from (
9
) and the independence of
\mathcal{W}^{2}\setminus\mathcal{W}^{1}
from
W_{\theta}
,
A_{[N]}^{1}
,
\mathcal{Q}^{1}
, and
\mathcal{Q}^{2}
; and (
14
) follows from (
10
). Therefore, applying (
8
), we obtain

H(W_{\theta}\mid A_{[N]}^{1},\mathcal{Q}^{1})\leq H(W_{\theta}\mid A_{[N]}^{2},\mathcal{Q}^{2})=0.

(15)

This confirms that the user can retrieve the desired file in the derived PIR scheme over
G_{1}
, completing the proof of reliability.

Privacy:

We now show that the queries and stored data reveal no information about the identity of
\theta
:

\displaystyle H(\theta)

\displaystyle\geq H(\theta\mid Q_{i}^{1},\mathcal{W}_{S_{i}}^{1})

\displaystyle\geq H(\theta\mid Q_{i}^{1},Q_{i}^{2},\mathcal{W}_{S_{i}}^{2})

\displaystyle=H(\theta\mid Q_{i}^{2},\mathcal{W}_{S_{i}}^{2})

(16)

\displaystyle=H(\theta),

(17)

where (
16
) follows from (
9
), and (
17
) follows from the privacy condition (
7
).
Therefore,

H(\theta\mid Q_{i}^{1},\mathcal{W}_{S_{i}}^{1})=H(\theta),

which confirms that the scheme preserves the user’s privacy. This finishes the proof.

∎

We continue to use the notation established in the proof of
Lemma

2.2
. One of the most important consequences of
Lemma

2.2
is the following result, which establishes a direct relationship between the capacities of
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
when
G_{1}
is a subgraph of
G_{2}
.

Corollary 2.3
.

Let
G_{1}\subseteq G_{2}
be two graphs and let
A_{i}^{2}
denote the response of server
S_{i}
in a PIR scheme over
G_{2}
with subpacketization
L
. Then, for any
\theta\in E(G_{1})
, we have

\sum_{i\in V(G_{2})}H\left(A_{i}^{2}\mid\mathcal{W}^{2}\setminus\mathcal{W}^{1},\mathcal{Q}^{2}\right)\geq\frac{L}{\mathcal{C}(G_{1})}.

(18)

As a consequence, it follows that
\mathcal{C}(G_{1})\geq\mathcal{C}(G_{2})
.

Proof of
2.3
.

Without loss of generality, we assume that
V(G_{1})=V(G_{2})=S_{[N]}
. For any
i\in[N]
, let
A_{i}^{1}
denote the response of server
S_{i}
in the PIR scheme over
G_{1}
, which is derived from the PIR scheme over
G_{2}
as described in
Lemma

2.2
. Let
\mathcal{Q}^{1}
denote the corresponding set of queries in graph
G_{1}
. Then, by the definition of capacity, we have

\sum_{i\in[N]}H(A_{i}^{1}\mid Q_{i}^{1})\geq\frac{L}{\mathcal{C}(G_{1})}.

(19)

Moreover, the following claim plays a crucial role.

Claim 2.4
.

For any
i\in[N]
and any
\theta\in E(G_{1})
, we have

H(A_{i}^{2}\mid\mathcal{W}^{2}\setminus\mathcal{W}^{1},\mathcal{Q}^{2})=H(A_{i}^{1}\mid Q_{i}^{1}).

(20)

Proof of claim.

Note that

\displaystyle H(A_{i}^{2}|\mathcal{W}^{2}\setminus\mathcal{W}^{1},\mathcal{Q}^{2})

\displaystyle=H(A_{i}^{2}|\mathcal{W}_{S_{i}}^{2}\setminus\mathcal{W}_{S_{i}}^{1},Q_{i}^{2})

\displaystyle=H(A_{i}^{2}|\mathcal{W}_{S_{i}}^{2}\setminus\mathcal{W}_{S_{i}}^{1},Q_{i}^{2})-H(A_{i}^{2}|A_{i}^{1},\mathcal{W}_{S_{i}}^{2}\setminus\mathcal{W}_{S_{i}}^{1},Q_{i}^{2})

(21)

\displaystyle=I(A_{i}^{1};A_{i}^{2}|\mathcal{W}_{S_{i}}^{2}\setminus\mathcal{W}_{S_{i}}^{1},Q_{i}^{2})

\displaystyle=H(A_{i}^{1}|\mathcal{W}_{S_{i}}^{2}\setminus\mathcal{W}_{S_{i}}^{1},Q_{i}^{2})-H(A_{i}^{1}|A_{i}^{2},\mathcal{W}_{S_{i}}^{2}\setminus\mathcal{W}_{S_{i}}^{1},Q_{i}^{2})

\displaystyle=H(A_{i}^{1}|\mathcal{W}_{S_{i}}^{2}\setminus\mathcal{W}_{S_{i}}^{1},Q_{i}^{2})

(22)

\displaystyle=H(A_{i}^{1}|\mathcal{W}_{S_{i}}^{2}\setminus\mathcal{W}_{S_{i}}^{1},Q_{i}^{1},Q_{i}^{2})

(23)

\displaystyle=H(A_{i}^{1}|Q_{i}^{1},Q_{i}^{2})

(24)

\displaystyle=H(A_{i}^{1}|Q_{i}^{1})

(25)

where (
21
) follows from (
10
); (
22
) follows from (
11
); (
23
) follows from (
9
); (
24
) follows from the fact that
A_{i}^{1}
is independent of
\mathcal{W}_{S_{i}}^{2}\setminus\mathcal{W}_{S_{i}}^{1}
, since these files are not stored on server
S_{i}
in the graph
G_{1}
; and (
25
) follows from the fact that
A_{i}^{1}
depends only on
Q_{i}^{1}
and
\mathcal{W}_{S_{i}}^{1}
, so knowledge of
Q_{i}^{2}
provides no additional information about
A_{i}^{1}
.

∎

Therefore, by
2.4
, we obtain

\sum_{i\in[N]}H(A_{i}^{2}\mid Q_{i}^{2})\geq\sum_{i\in[N]}H(A_{i}^{2}\mid\mathcal{W}^{2}\setminus\mathcal{W}^{1},\mathcal{Q}^{2})=\sum_{i\in[N]}H(A_{i}^{1}\mid Q_{i}^{1})\geq\frac{L}{\mathcal{C}(G_{1})}.

Moreover, by the definition of capacity, we have

\frac{L}{\mathcal{C}(G_{2})}=\inf\left\{\sum_{i\in[N]}H(A_{i}^{2}\mid Q_{i}^{2})\right\}\geq\frac{L}{\mathcal{C}(G_{1})},

(26)

which implies that
\mathcal{C}(G_{1})\geq\mathcal{C}(G_{2})
. This finishes the proof of
2.3
.

∎

With the above preparations in place, we now develop a framework for estimating the information entropy of the server responses. For convenience, for any
1\leq k\leq N-2
, we set
\mathcal{W}_{[0]}:=\varnothing
and define

\mathcal{W}_{[k]}:=\bigcup_{\ell=1}^{k}\{W_{e}\mid\ell\in e\}=\bigcup_{\ell=1}^{k}\mathcal{W}_{S_{\ell}}.

Lemma 2.5
.

For any PIR scheme over an arbitrary graph
G
, the following inequality holds:

\sum_{i=1}^{N}H(A_{i}\mid\mathcal{Q})\geq L+H(A_{1}\mid\mathcal{W}_{S_{N}},\mathcal{Q})+\sum_{i=2}^{N-1}H(A_{i}\mid\mathcal{W}_{[i-1]},\mathcal{Q}).

(27)

Proof of
Lemma

2.5
.

First, we observe that

\displaystyle L

\displaystyle=H(W_{1,N})

\displaystyle=H(W_{1,N}|\mathcal{Q},\theta=(1,N))

(28)

\displaystyle=H(W_{1,N}|\mathcal{Q},\theta=(1,N))-H(W_{1,N}|A_{[N]},\mathcal{Q},\theta=(1,N))

(29)

\displaystyle=I(W_{1,N};A_{[N]}|\mathcal{Q},\theta=(1,N))

\displaystyle=H(A_{[N]}|\mathcal{Q},\theta=(1,N))-H(A_{[N]}|W_{1,N},\mathcal{Q},\theta=(1,N))

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{N}H(A_{i}|\mathcal{Q},\theta=(1,N))-H(A_{[N]}|W_{1,N},\mathcal{Q},\theta=(1,N))

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}H(A_{i}|\mathcal{Q})-H(A_{[N]}|W_{1,N},\mathcal{Q},\theta=(1,N)),

(30)

where (
28
) follows since the queries
\mathcal{Q}
and the identity of the desired file are independent of the specific file content; (
29
) follows from the reliability requirement of the PIR scheme; and (
30
) follows from
Lemma

2.1
.

Rearranging the above inequality yields

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}H(A_{i}|\mathcal{Q})

\displaystyle\geq L+H(A_{[N]}|W_{1,N},\mathcal{Q},\theta=(1,N))

\displaystyle=L+\sum_{i=1}^{N}H(A_{i}|A_{[i-1]},W_{1,N},\mathcal{Q},\theta=(1,N))

(31)

\displaystyle=L+H(A_{1}|W_{1,N},\mathcal{Q},\theta=(1,N))+\sum_{i=2}^{N}H(A_{i}|A_{[i-1]},W_{1,N},\mathcal{Q},\theta=(1,N))

\displaystyle\geq L+H(A_{1}|\mathcal{W}_{S_{N}},\mathcal{Q},\theta=(1,N))+\sum_{i=2}^{N}H(A_{i}|A_{[i-1]},\mathcal{W}_{[i-1]},\mathcal{Q},\theta=(1,N))

(32)

\displaystyle=L+H(A_{1}|\mathcal{W}_{S_{N}},\mathcal{Q},\theta=(1,N))+\sum_{i=2}^{N}H(A_{i}|\mathcal{W}_{[i-1]},\mathcal{Q},\theta=(1,N))

(33)

\displaystyle\geq L+H(A_{1}|\mathcal{W}_{S_{N}},\mathcal{Q},\theta=(1,N))+\sum_{i=2}^{N-1}H(A_{i}|\mathcal{W}_{[i-1]},\mathcal{Q},\theta=(1,N))

\displaystyle=L+H(A_{1}|\mathcal{W}_{S_{N}},\mathcal{Q})+\sum_{i=2}^{N-1}H(A_{i}|\mathcal{W}_{[i-1]},\mathcal{Q}),

(34)

where (
31
) follows from the chain rule of joint entropy function; (
33
) follows from
A_{[i-1]}
can be totally determined by
\mathcal{W}_{[i-1]}
and
\mathcal{Q}
for any
i\geq 2
; and (
34
) follows from
Lemma

2.1
. This finishes the proof.
∎

2.2
Complete graphs: Proof of
Theorem

1.10

In this section, we derive an upper bound on the PIR capacity of the complete graph
K_{N}
, leveraging
Lemma

2.5
. To that end, we begin by recalling a key structural property of
K_{N}
that will play an essential role in our analysis. A graph
G
is said to be
vertex-transitive
if its automorphism group
\operatorname{Aut}(G)
acts transitively on the vertex set. That is, for any pair of vertices
u,v\in V(G)
, there exists an automorphism
f\in\operatorname{Aut}(G)
such that
f(v)=u
. Before presenting the formal proof of
Theorem

1.10
, we recall the following two important lemmas from
[
10
]
.

Lemma 2.6

(Lemma 3,
[
10
]
)
.

Let
i,j\in[N]
be two distinct servers that share the file
W_{i,j}\in\mathcal{W}
, then

L\leq H(A_{i}|\mathcal{W}\setminus\{W_{i,j}\},\mathcal{Q})+H(A_{j}|\mathcal{W}\setminus\{W_{i,j}\},\mathcal{Q}).

Lemma 2.7

(Lemma 11,
[
10
]
)
.

In a vertex-transitive graph-based replication system, for any achievable rate
R
, and for any
i\neq j\in[N]
, there exists a PIR scheme with rate
R
such that

H(A_{i}\mid\mathcal{Q})=H(A_{j}\mid\mathcal{Q}),

and

H(A_{i}\mid\mathcal{W}\setminus\{W_{i,j}\},\mathcal{Q})\geq\frac{L}{2}.

Since the complete graph
K_{N}
is vertex-transitive, the conclusion of
Lemma

2.7
applies to PIR schemes constructed over
K_{N}
. Furthermore, owing to the high degree of symmetry in
K_{N}
, we establish the following stronger result tailored specifically to the complete graph. The proof of
Lemma

2.8
follows a similar line of reasoning as that of
Lemma

2.7
. The proof of the following lemma is deferred to
Appendix

A
.

Lemma 2.8
.

Let
N\geq 3
be an integer. For any achievable rate
R
and any
1\leq k\leq N-2
, there exists a PIR scheme over
K_{N}
with rate
R
such that

H(A_{i}\mid\mathcal{W}_{[k]},\mathcal{Q})=H(A_{j}\mid\mathcal{W}_{[k]},\mathcal{Q}),

(35)

for any
k<i\neq j\leq N
; and

H(A_{i}\mid\mathcal{W}_{S_{N}},\mathcal{Q})=H(A_{j}\mid\mathcal{W}_{S_{N}},\mathcal{Q}),

(36)

for any
i\neq j<N
.

With the above preparations in place, we now formally prove
Theorem

1.10
. The proof proceeds by induction on
N
. For the base case
N=3
, it was shown in
[
2
]
that
\mathcal{C}(K_{3})=\frac{1}{2}
. For the induction step, assume that for every
n\leq N-1
, we have

\mathcal{C}(K_{n})\leq\frac{1}{\left(\sum_{i=2}^{n}\frac{1}{i!}\right)\cdot n}.

Now, suppose
R
is an achievable rate for
K_{N}
. Then there exists a PIR scheme
\Pi_{N}
with rate
R
that satisfies the equations in
Lemma

2.8
.

For any
2\leq i\leq N-2
, let
G_{i}=K_{N-i+1}
be the complete graph whose vertex set consists of
S_{i},S_{i+1},\dots,S_{N}
. Then, the set of files corresponding to the edges in
E(K_{N})\setminus E(G_{i})
is precisely the collection
\mathcal{W}_{[i-1]}
. Therefore, for any
2\leq i\leq N-2
, we have

\displaystyle(N-i+1)\cdot H(A_{i}\mid\mathcal{W}_{[i-1]},\mathcal{Q})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=i}^{N}H(A_{j}\mid\mathcal{W}_{[i-1]},\mathcal{Q})

(37)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{L}{\mathcal{C}(G_{i})}

(38)

\displaystyle=\frac{L}{\mathcal{C}(K_{N-i+1})}

\displaystyle\geq\left(\sum_{j=2}^{N-i+1}\frac{1}{j!}\right)\cdot(N-i+1)\cdot L,

(39)

where (
37
) follows from (
35
); (
38
) follows from
2.3
; and (
39
) follows from the inductive hypothesis. Thus, for
2\leq i\leq N-2
, we have

H(A_{i}\mid\mathcal{W}_{[i-1]},\mathcal{Q})\geq\left(\sum_{j=2}^{N-i+1}\frac{1}{j!}\right)\cdot L.

(40)

Similarly, we obtain

H(A_{1}\mid\mathcal{W}_{S_{N}},\mathcal{Q})\geq\left(\sum_{j=2}^{N-1}\frac{1}{j!}\right)\cdot L.

(41)

Combining (
35
) for
k=N-2
and
Lemma

2.6
, we have

H(A_{N-1}|\mathcal{W}_{[N-2]},\mathcal{Q})=H(A_{N-1}|\mathcal{W}\setminus\{W_{N-1,N}\},\mathcal{Q})\geq\frac{L}{2}.

(42)

Combining (
34
), (
40
), (
41
), and (
42
), we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}H(A_{i}\mid\mathcal{Q})

\displaystyle\geq L+H(A_{1}\mid\mathcal{W}_{S_{N}},\mathcal{Q})+\sum_{i=2}^{N-2}H(A_{i}\mid\mathcal{W}_{[i-1]},\mathcal{Q})+H(A_{N-1}\mid\mathcal{W}_{[N-2]},\mathcal{Q})

\displaystyle\geq L+\left(\sum_{j=2}^{N-1}\frac{1}{j!}\right)\cdot L+\sum_{i=2}^{N-2}\left(\sum_{j=2}^{N-i+1}\frac{1}{j!}\right)\cdot L+\frac{L}{2}

\displaystyle=\left(1+\sum_{j=2}^{N-1}\frac{N-j+1}{j!}\right)\cdot L

(43)

\displaystyle=\left(1+\left(\sum_{j=2}^{N-1}\frac{1}{j!}\right)\cdot(N+1)-\sum_{j=2}^{N-2}\frac{1}{(j-1)!}\right)\cdot L

\displaystyle=\left(\left(\sum_{j=2}^{N-1}\frac{1}{j!}\right)\cdot N+\frac{1}{(N-1)!}\right)\cdot L

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{j=2}^{N}\frac{1}{j!}\right)\cdot NL,

where (
43
) follows by counting how many times each term
\frac{1}{j!}
appears in the summation.
Thus, the rate of the scheme
\Pi_{N}
satisfies

R(\Pi_{N})=\frac{L}{\sum_{i=1}^{N}H(A_{i}\mid\mathcal{Q})}\leq\frac{1}{\left(\sum_{i=2}^{N}\frac{1}{i!}\right)\cdot N},

which completes the proof of
Theorem

1.10
.

2.3
Complete bipartite graphs: Proof of
Theorem

1.11

In this section, we derive an upper bound on the PIR capacity of the complete bipartite graph
K_{\frac{N}{2},\frac{N}{2}}
with parts
U
and
V
, where
|U|=|V|=\frac{N}{2}
. We consider a PIR scheme operating over this bipartition, with servers
S_{1},S_{3},\dots,S_{N-1}
assigned to
U

We aim to prove the bound for

.

For any

\displaystyle(N-2k)\cdot H(A_{2k+1}\mid\mathcal{W}_{[2k]},\mathcal{Q})

\displaystyle\geq\frac{L}{\mathcal{C}(G_{k})}

and

(52)

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}H(A_{i}\mid\mathcal{Q})

3.3
General PIR scheme over
K_{N}

3.3.1
Overview of the scheme

We now present a recursive construction of a PIR scheme over the complete graph
K_{N}
, applicable for any
N\geq 3
. In this construction, the answer of each server consists of carefully structured summations selected from
k
-sums, where
1\leq k\leq N-1
. The scheme is built iteratively over
N-1
steps, indexed by
k=1
to
N-1
.

At each step
k
, we focus exclusively on the
(k\!-\!1)
-sums and
k
-sums appearing in the server responses
A_{[N]}
. These summations are strategically combined to recover portions of sub-files of the desired file. The key design principle is to define the summations used in step
k
based on the set of unused
(k\!-\!1)
-sums remaining after steps
1
through
k\!-\!1
, which we refer to as the
side information
after step
k\!-\!1
.

Throughout the construction, we aim to utilize the available side information in each step as efficiently as possible, ensuring that the scheme fully leverages the combinatorial structure of summations across steps.

Our construction can be viewed as a recursive generalization of the PIR scheme introduced in
[
16
]
, which serves as a foundational component of our approach. The full construction procedure is described in
Section

3.3.3
. In
Section

3.3.4
, we rigorously establish the feasibility of the proposed scheme by verifying both reliability and privacy. Subsequently,
Section

3.3.5
analyzes the resulting PIR rate, highlighting the structural complexity and key challenges involved.

However, a direct implementation of this recursive scheme leads to prohibitively large subpacketization. To address this issue, we propose a probabilistic variant of the scheme in
Section

4.1
, which significantly reduces the subpacketization to 1.

Inspired by the methodology used to derive our upper bound, we conjecture that this recursive framework, arguably the core innovation of this work, can be extended to construct efficient PIR schemes for certain broader classes of graphs.

In this subsection, we focus on constructing a PIR scheme

As discussed earlier, the construction proceeds in

,

Definition 3.5

(Residual multiplicity)
.

For distinct

denote the file shared by servers
S_{i}
and
S_{j_{\ell}}

of a

-sum of type

from server

is equal to

r

k
-sums of this type, from
S_{i}
, present as side information.

According to
3.5
, it holds that, for any
k
, the residual multiplicity of
k
-sum with some label must be no more than the multiplicity of
k
-sum. Recall that for any
k
, the recovery patterns
\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\zeta
each represents a class of symmetric, structured, specific recovery patterns. We emphasize that when the user consumes side information, all realizations of these recovery patterns are utilized uniformly. This uniform usage will be illustrated in detail using Step 2 as a representative example. Therefore, in our scheme, the residual multiplicity
of a
k
-sum depends only on its label and is uniform across all servers. For instance, on server
S_{1}
, the 2-sums

and

share the same label

The following proposition provides a complete specification of our PIR scheme construction over the complete graph
K_{N}
.

Proposition 3.6
.

Let
\{x_{k}\}_{k=1}^{N-1}
,
\{y_{k}\}_{k=2}^{N-1}
, and

(61)

If

Our verification proceeds in three parts. First, we show that the sequences

\displaystyle z_{k}

\displaystyle=x_{k}-\frac{k-1}{2}x_{k-1}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{N-k+1}{2}x_{k-1}+z_{k-1}\right)-\frac{k-1}{2}x_{k-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{N-2k+2}{2}x_{k-1}+z_{k-1}

Lemma 3.12
.

The scheme

Proof of

.

We verify those properties as follows.

Privacy:

The privacy argument follows directly from
[

, Lemma 3]
. Specifically, it suffices to show that, for any

, the distribution of the query
\mathcal{Q}_{i}
sent to server

is independent of the index
\theta
of the desired file. We break this argument into two parts:

•

Structural independence:
Ignoring the subscripts of the requested sub-files, the structure of the query to each server depends solely on the pre-determined multiplicity sequence
\{x_{k}\}_{k=1}^{N-1}
. As this sequence is independent of
\theta
, the overall query structure is also independent of
\theta
.

•

Subscript independence:
For each single server, the subscripts of the referred sub-files of some specific file
W_{i,j}
only need to be pairwise distinct. And the specific subscripts are determined using random permutations
\sigma_{i,j}
, which are independently and uniformly chosen and do not depend on
\theta
. Therefore, the joint distribution of subscripts of sub-files of
W_{i,j}
referred in the query is independent of the identity of the desired file and only determined by the permutation
\sigma_{i,j}
.

To elaborate, for any server
S_{i}
, since the selection of subscripts of sub-files of distinct files is independent of each other, the joint distribution of the whole subscripts is only dependent on the permutations and is independent of the desired file. Moreover, the query consists of
x_{k}M
portions of
k
-sums of type
W_{i,j_{1}}+\cdots+W_{i,j_{k}}
, for any distinct
j_{1},\dots,j_{k}\in[N]\setminus\{i\}
. This amount is also independent of the desired file. Hence, the joint distribution of the entire query to
S_{i}
is independent of
\theta
, establishing privacy.

Reliability:

The reliability of the scheme follows by construction. The queries set
\mathcal{Q}
consists of
L
valid recovery patterns and some side information, where each pattern allows the recovery of a distinct sub-file of the desired file
A
. As shown in (
72
), the subpacketization
L
equals the total number of such recovery patterns. Since each pattern recovers a different sub-file and the entire file
A
is partitioned into
L
sub-files, the user can correctly reconstruct
A
. This confirms the reliability of the scheme.
∎

3.3.5
Estimation of the rate

In this subsection, we analyze the rate of the PIR scheme
\Pi_{N}
constructed above.

Lemma 3.13
.

The rate of the PIR scheme
\Pi_{N}
over
K_{N}
is given by

R(\Pi_{N})=\frac{2\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}\binom{N-2}{k-1}x_{k}}{N\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}\binom{N-1}{k}x_{k}}\geq\left(1-\epsilon_{N}\right)\frac{4}{3N},

(87)

where
\epsilon_{N}\to 0
as
N\to\infty
.

Proof of
Lemma

3.13
.

Recall from (
72
) that the subpacketization of our scheme is

L=2\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}\binom{N-2}{k-1}x_{k},

and that each server sends

\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}\binom{N-1}{k}x_{k}

messages in response to the user’s query. Since the total download is
N
times this quantity, the rate is given by

R(\Pi_{N})=\frac{L}{N\cdot\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}\binom{N-1}{k}x_{k}}=\frac{2\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}\binom{N-2}{k-1}x_{k}}{N\cdot\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}\binom{N-1}{k}x_{k}}.

Define
A_{k}:=\binom{N-2}{k-1}x_{k}
and
B_{k}:=\binom{N-1}{k}x_{k}
, so that

R(\Pi_{N})=\frac{2\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}A_{k}}{N\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}B_{k}}.

We now estimate the behavior of the ratio. First, by (
61
) and (
62
), we analyze the growth rate of the sequence
\{A_{k}\}
as follows.

From the recurrence relations, we have for
k\leq\left\lfloor\frac{N}{2}\right\rfloor+1
,

\frac{x_{k}}{x_{k-1}}\geq\frac{N-k+1}{2},

which implies that

\frac{A_{k}}{A_{k-1}}=\frac{\binom{N-2}{k-1}x_{k}}{\binom{N-2}{k-2}x_{k-1}}\geq\frac{(N-k)(N-k+1)}{2(k-1)}>1.

For
k\geq\left\lfloor\frac{N}{2}\right\rfloor+2
,

\frac{x_{k}}{x_{k-1}}=\frac{k-1}{2k-N},

which implies that

\frac{A_{k}}{A_{k-1}}=\frac{\binom{N-2}{k-1}x_{k}}{\binom{N-2}{k-2}x_{k-1}}=\frac{N-k}{2k-N}.

(88)

By analyzing this expression, we find that
\{A_{k}\}
increases for
k\leq\lfloor\frac{2N}{3}\rfloor
and decreases afterward. In particular, if we assume for simplicity that
\frac{2N}{3}\in\mathbb{Z}
, then
A_{k}
attains its maximum at
k=\frac{2N}{3}-1
or
k=\frac{2N}{3}
. To lower bound the rate, we estimate the total contribution of the dominant terms near the peak of
A_{k}
. Let
\epsilon>0
be an arbitrarily small constant. Then, we claim:

Claim 3.14
.

For any
\epsilon>0
,

\sum_{k\in[1,(2/3-\epsilon)N]\cup[(2/3+\epsilon)N,N-1]}A_{k}\leq(N-1)\cdot e^{-\frac{3}{2}\epsilon^{2}N}\cdot A_{2N/3}.

Proof of claim.

Assume
\epsilon N\in\mathbb{Z}
. For any
k\in[1,(2/3-\epsilon)N]\cup[(2/3+\epsilon)N,N-1]
, we have

A_{k}\leq\max\left\{A_{(2/3-\epsilon)N},\,A_{(2/3+\epsilon)N}\right\}.

Let
R:=A_{2N/3}
. We estimate
A_{(2/3-\epsilon)N}
as follows:

\displaystyle A_{(2/3-\epsilon)N}

\displaystyle=R\cdot\prod_{t=0}^{\epsilon N-1}\frac{A_{2N/3-t-1}}{A_{2N/3-t}}

\displaystyle=R\cdot\prod_{t=0}^{\epsilon N-1}\frac{1-6t/N}{1+3t/N}

(89)

\displaystyle\leq R\cdot\prod_{t=0}^{\epsilon N-1}\left(1-\frac{6t}{N}\right),

where (
89
) follows from (
88
). Taking logarithms:

\ln A_{(2/3-\epsilon)N}\leq\ln R-\sum_{t=0}^{\epsilon N-1}\frac{6t}{N}=\ln R-3\epsilon(\epsilon N-1)\leq\ln R-3\epsilon^{2}N.

Thus, we have

A_{(2/3-\epsilon)N}\leq R\cdot e^{-3\epsilon^{2}N}.

Similarly,

A_{(2/3+\epsilon)N}\leq R\cdot e^{-\frac{3}{2}\epsilon^{2}N}.

Therefore,

\sum_{k\in[1,(2/3-\epsilon)N]\cup[(2/3+\epsilon)N,N-1]}A_{k}\leq(N-1)\cdot R\cdot e^{-c\epsilon^{2}N}

for any
c\leq\min\left\{\frac{3}{2},\,3\right\}
, which completes the proof.
∎

3.14
means that when
N\rightarrow\infty
, then

\sum_{k\in[1,(2/3-\epsilon)N]\cup[(2/3+\epsilon)N,N-1]}A_{k}=o(A_{2N/3}).

A similar argument shows that when
N\rightarrow\infty
, we have

\sum_{k\in[1,(2/3-\epsilon)N]\cup[(2/3+\epsilon)N,N-1]}B_{k}=o(B_{2N/3}).

Moreover, for all
k\in\left[(2/3-\epsilon)N,(2/3+\epsilon)N\right]
, we have

\frac{A_{k}}{B_{k}}=\frac{k}{N-1}\geq\frac{2}{3}-\epsilon.

Therefore, we can estimate

\displaystyle R(\Pi_{N})

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{N}\cdot\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}A_{k}}{\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}B_{k}}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{N}\cdot\frac{\sum_{k=(2/3-\epsilon)N}^{(2/3+\epsilon)N}A_{k}+o(A_{2N/3})}{\sum_{k=(2/3-\epsilon)N}^{(2/3+\epsilon)N}B_{k}+o(B_{2N/3})}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{2}{N}\cdot\left(\frac{(\frac{2}{3}-\epsilon)\sum B_{k}}{\sum B_{k}+o(B_{2N/3})}-o(1)\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{2}{3}-\epsilon-o(1)\right)\cdot\frac{2}{N}.

Since
\epsilon>0
is arbitrary, we conclude that

R(\Pi_{N})\geq(1-o(1))\cdot\frac{4}{3N},

as
N\to\infty
, completing the proof.
∎

4
Probabilistic PIR schemes

In this section, we present several results concerning

PIR schemes. In

4.1

into a probabilistic PIR scheme with subpacketization reduced to

4.1
Generating probabilistic PIR schemes from the deterministic one

We then prove

1.14
by establishing a transformation from any deterministic PIR scheme to a corresponding probabilistic PIR scheme. The proof can be mainly divided into two parts:

•

First, we show that for any deterministic PIR scheme satisfying the independence property, its queries can be decomposed into a collection of recovery patterns, possibly accompanied by a small amount of residual side information.

•

Second, we demonstrate that any deterministic PIR scheme admitting such a decomposition can be naturally converted into a probabilistic PIR scheme with subpacketization equal to
1
.

4.1.1
Generating recovery patterns

In this subsection, we focus on the procedure for extracting recovery patterns from a given deterministic PIR scheme. The key idea relies on a structural property that is commonly satisfied by many existing PIR schemes, which we formalize in the following definition.

Definition 4.1

(Independence Property)
.

Let
G
be a (multi-)graph with
N
vertices. Let
\Pi
be a deterministic PIR scheme over
G

For every

For every

(3)

For every requested file

\mathcal{Q}
.

(4)

For every
\theta\in E(G)
, each sub-file of the desired file

can be recovered using at most one requested summation from each server.

In fact, all existing graph-based PIR schemes satisfy the independence property. Consequently, provided the condition

Next, we formally verify the correctness and feasibility of the algorithm.

Lemma 4.4
.

If a deterministic PIR scheme
\Pi
satisfies the independence property, then for each
j\in[L]
, the output
\mathcal{P}^{j}
generated by Algorithm
1
is a valid recovery pattern for the desired file
W_{\theta}
.

Proof of
Lemma

4.4
.

To verify that
\mathcal{P}^{j}
is a recovery pattern, we need to show the following:

•

\mathcal{P}^{j}
includes at most one summation from each query set
\mathcal{Q}_{i}
for all
i\in[N]
;

•

The sum of all summations in
\mathcal{P}^{j}
recovers the sub-file
a_{j}
.

For the first statement, by property (3) of the independence property in
4.1
, there exists exactly one summation in
\mathcal{Q}
, denoted
\omega_{j}
, such that
a_{j}\in\mathrm{supp}(\omega_{j})
. Therefore, due to the reliability of
\Pi
, the user must be able to recover
a_{j}
using
\omega_{j}
together with some additional summations. Since sub-files behave like distinct free variables, recovering a target sub-file requires canceling out all other interfering sub-files. To cancel any non-desired sub-file that appears in
\omega_{j}
, another summation containing that sub-file must exist.

By the structure of the replication system and property (2) of
4.1
, each non-desired sub-file appears in at most two summations across all queries (because each file is stored on exactly two servers). Hence, for any sub-file involved in
\omega_{j}\setminus\{a_{j}\}
, there exists at most one additional summation that includes it. Thus, to cancel all interfering sub-files in
\omega_{j}
, the algorithm iteratively adds other summations that share these sub-files, updating the support set
\mathcal{S}^{j}
in the process. If the user can indeed recover
a_{j}
, then all these added summations are necessary for the recovery, and so all the summations in set
\mathcal{P}^{j}
must be included in the required recovery pattern. By property (4) in
4.1
, this implies that each
\mathcal{P}^{j}
includes at most one summation from each
\mathcal{Q}_{i}
.

We now verify the second statement: the sum of all summations in
\mathcal{P}^{j}
equals
a_{j}
. Suppose not. Then there must be some sub-file in
\mathcal{S}^{j}\setminus\{a_{j}\}
that is not canceled out in the total sum of summations in
\mathcal{P}^{j}
. However, by construction (line 5 of Algorithm
1
), there is no other summation in
\mathcal{Q}\setminus\mathcal{P}^{j}
whose support intersects
\mathcal{S}^{j}
. This would contradict the assumption that
\Pi
is a reliable scheme. Hence, the summations in
\mathcal{P}^{j}
must sum to
a_{j}
.
∎

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
\Pi
be a deterministic PIR scheme satisfying the independence property. Then, the query set
\mathcal{Q}
of
\Pi
can be partitioned into the recovery patterns
\mathcal{P}^{1},\mathcal{P}^{2},\dots,\mathcal{P}^{L}
and the remaining side information
\mathcal{D}
.

Proof of
Lemma

4.5
.

Let
\mathcal{P}^{1},\dots,\mathcal{P}^{L}
and their corresponding support sets
\mathcal{S}^{1},\dots,\mathcal{S}^{L}
be the outputs of Algorithm
1
. We need to show that the recovery patterns
\mathcal{P}^{1},\dots,\mathcal{P}^{L}
are pairwise disjoint.

Assume, for the sake of contradiction, that there exists a summation
\omega\in\mathcal{P}^{1}\cap\mathcal{P}^{2}
. Then, since
\omega
appears in both patterns, we must have
\mathrm{supp}(\omega)\subseteq\mathcal{S}^{1}\cap\mathcal{S}^{2}
, implying
\mathcal{S}^{1}\cap\mathcal{S}^{2}\neq\varnothing
. We now establish the following claim.

Claim 4.6
.

\mathcal{P}^{1}=\mathcal{P}^{2}
.

Proof of claim.

We show that
\mathcal{P}^{1}\subseteq\mathcal{P}^{2}
; the reverse inclusion follows symmetrically.

By the construction in Algorithm
1
(lines 5–8), the pattern
\mathcal{P}^{1}
is built by successively including all summations whose supports intersect with the growing support set, starting from
\mathcal{S}^{1}=\{a_{1}\}
. Thus, there exists a chain of summations
\omega_{1},\omega_{2},\dots,\omega_{m}=\omega\in\mathcal{P}^{1}
, such that:

•

a_{1}\in\mathrm{supp}(\omega_{1})
,

•

\mathrm{supp}(\omega_{i})\cap\mathrm{supp}(\omega_{i+1})\neq\varnothing
for all
i\in[m-1]
.

Since
\omega=\omega_{m}\in\mathcal{P}^{2}
, and Algorithm
1
applies the same support-propagation rule, it follows inductively that all previous
\omega_{i}
must also be included in
\mathcal{P}^{2}
. In particular,
\omega_{1}\in\mathcal{P}^{2}
, so
a_{1}\in\mathcal{S}^{2}
. As a result, by the construction of the algorithm, all of
\mathcal{P}^{1}
must be contained in
\mathcal{P}^{2}
. Hence,
\mathcal{P}^{1}\subseteq\mathcal{P}^{2}
. This completes the proof.
∎

However,
4.6
leads to a contradiction. Since both
a_{1}\in\mathcal{S}^{1}
and
a_{2}\in\mathcal{S}^{2}
, and
\mathcal{P}^{1}=\mathcal{P}^{2}
, it follows that a single recovery pattern
\mathcal{P}^{1}
contains two distinct sub-files of the desired file. This contradicts Property (3) of the independence property in
4.1
, which requires that each sub-file of the desired file appears in exactly one summation in
\mathcal{Q}
. As a result,
a_{1}
cannot be recovered as the sum of all summations in
\mathcal{P}^{1}
, leading to a contradiction.

Therefore, the recovery patterns
\mathcal{P}^{1},\dots,\mathcal{P}^{L}
must be pairwise disjoint. It follows that the query set
\mathcal{Q}
can be partitioned as

\mathcal{Q}=\left(\bigsqcup_{j=1}^{L}\mathcal{P}^{j}\right)\sqcup\mathcal{D},

where
\mathcal{D}:=\mathcal{Q}\setminus\left(\bigcup_{j=1}^{L}\mathcal{P}^{j}\right)
consists of side information that is not involved in the recovery of the desired sub-files. This finishes the proof of
Lemma

4.5
.
∎

4.1.2
Relation between deterministic PIR schemes and probabilistic PIR schemes

Recall from
3.3
that a recovery pattern is defined as a collection containing at most one summation from each query set
\mathcal{Q}_{i}
. For a recovery pattern
\mathcal{P}
, if the request to server
S_{i}
is represented by a (possibly empty) set
q_{i}
, then we write
\mathcal{P}=(q_{1},q_{2},\dots,q_{N})
, or more compactly,
\mathcal{P}=(q_{i})_{i\in[N]}
. For instance, in
Table

5
, there exists a recovery pattern
\mathcal{P}
where the user requests
\{a_{2}+b_{2}\}
from
S_{1}
, nothing from
S_{2}
, and
\{b_{2}\}
from
S_{3}
. This pattern is denoted by
\mathcal{P}=(\{a_{2}+b_{2}\},\varnothing,\{b_{2}\})
.

To formally describe how recovery patterns cover or partition the query sets in a PIR scheme, we introduce the following definitions of union and intersection for recovery patterns.

Definition 4.7
.

Let
\Pi
be a deterministic PIR scheme with query sets
\mathcal{Q}=\{\mathcal{Q}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{Q}_{N}\}
. Suppose
\{\mathcal{P}^{j}=(q_{i}^{j})_{i\in[N]}\}_{j\in\Lambda}
is a family of recovery patterns indexed by a set
\Lambda
. The
union
of these recovery patterns is defined as

\bigcup_{j\in\Lambda}\mathcal{P}^{j}:=\left(\bigcup_{j\in\Lambda}q_{i}^{j}\right)_{i\in[N]}.

That is, for each server
S_{i}
, the union of requests consists of all summations requested from
S_{i}
across the entire family of recovery patterns.

Similarly, the
intersection
of recovery patterns is defined as

\bigcap_{j\in\Lambda}\mathcal{P}^{j}:=\left(\bigcap_{j\in\Lambda}q_{i}^{j}\right)_{i\in[N]}.

We say that the query set
\mathcal{Q}=(\mathcal{Q}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{Q}_{N})
is
partitioned
by the recovery patterns
\{\mathcal{P}^{j}\}_{j\in\Lambda}
if, for every
i\in[N]
, the query set
\mathcal{Q}_{i}
is the disjoint union of the corresponding requests:

\mathcal{Q}_{i}=\bigsqcup_{j\in\Lambda}q_{i}^{j}.

As an example, the queries in the PIR scheme shown in
Table

5
can be partitioned into six disjoint recovery patterns.

The following lemma formalizes the connection between deterministic and probabilistic PIR schemes. Specifically, we consider a deterministic PIR scheme with subpacketization level
L
, where the queries are partitioned into
L
recovery patterns—each enabling recovery of a distinct sub-file of the desired file using at most one summation per server.

Lemma 4.8
.

Let
\Pi
be a deterministic PIR scheme over a graph
G
with subpacketization
L
and rate
R
, where every summation in the queries involves sub-files from distinct files. Suppose that, for each server
S_{i}
, the entropy
H(A_{i})
of its response equals the number of summations in
A_{i}
. If the query set
\mathcal{Q}
across all servers can be partitioned into
L

\Pi^{\prime}
over
G
with subpacketization
1
and the same rate
R
.

Proof of
Lemma

4.8
.

Assume the
L

\mathcal{P}^{j}=(q_{i}^{j})_{i\in[N]}
for
j\in[L]
. For any file
W
, let
(W)_{s}
denote its
s

i
and

corresponds to a summation of
k(i,j)

k

•

If
k(i,j)=0

.

•

\Pi^{\prime}
with subpacketization
1
. The user selects an index

\mathcal{P}^{j}
, with the following simplification:

•

k(i,j)=0

S_{i}
.

•

If
k(i,j)\geq 1
, the user requests
W_{i,t_{1}}+\cdots+W_{i,t_{k}}
from
S_{i}
.

\mathcal{P}^{j}
. Let

denote the responses from

Claim 4.9
.

\Pi^{\prime}
is a valid probabilistic PIR scheme.

Proof of claim.

We verify both correctness and privacy.

Correctness.

Each recovery pattern
\mathcal{P}^{j}
in
\Pi
recovers one sub-file of the desired file. In
\Pi^{\prime}
, the user retrieves the corresponding full-file summation. Since the summation structure is preserved, the user can recover the desired file by applying the randomly chosen recovery pattern.

Privacy.

For any server
S_{i}
, the response
A_{i}^{\prime}
is independent of the desired file index
\theta
. Suppose
A_{i}^{\prime}=W_{i,t_{1}}+\cdots+W_{i,t_{k}}
. Let
N(W_{i,t_{1}}+\cdots+W_{i,t_{k}})
be the number of recovery patterns
\mathcal{P}^{j}
in which
S_{i}
returns a
k
-sum of type
W_{i,t_{1}}+\cdots+W_{i,t_{k}}
in the deterministic PIR scheme
\Pi
. Then,

\Pr(A_{i}^{\prime}=W_{i,t_{1}}+\cdots+W_{i,t_{k}})=\frac{1}{L}\cdot N(W_{i,t_{1}}+\cdots+W_{i,t_{k}}).

Let
M(W_{i,t_{1}}+\cdots+W_{i,t_{k}})
be the number of times this
k
-sum appears in
A_{i}
of scheme
\Pi
. Since the recovery patterns partition the queries,

N(W_{i,t_{1}}+\cdots+W_{i,t_{k}})=M(W_{i,t_{1}}+\cdots+W_{i,t_{k}}).

Thus,

\Pr(A_{i}^{\prime}=W_{i,t_{1}}+\cdots+W_{i,t_{k}})=\frac{1}{L}\cdot M(W_{i,t_{1}}+\cdots+W_{i,t_{k}}),

which is independent of

Now consider the case

. This occurs precisely when

in the chosen recovery pattern

. Since

contains

is not queried is

, yielding

\Pr(A_{i}^{\prime}=0)=\frac{L-H(A_{i})}{L},

(92)

which is also independent of
\theta
.
∎

Rate.

Finally, we show that

has the same rate as

\displaystyle R(\Pi^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sum_{i=1}^{N}H(A_{i}^{\prime})}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sum_{i=1}^{N}(1-\Pr(A_{i}^{\prime}=0))}

(93)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(1-\frac{L-H(A_{i})}{L}\right)}

(94)

\displaystyle=\frac{L}{\sum_{i=1}^{N}H(A_{i})}=R(\Pi),

where (

94
) uses (
92

∎

Lemma 4.10
.

Let
\Pi
be a deterministic PIR scheme over a graph

with subpacketization
L
and rate

, such that every summation in the queries is composed of sub-files from distinct files. Moreover, assume that the entropy

of the answer from each server

.

. Denote the

for

. As in

: for each

. To preserve privacy, we modify the construction as follows.

\mathcal{Q}_{i}^{j}
denote the query sent to server

in round

. Note that for some

in the original scheme. Specifically, suppose the side information for

includes a

Probabilistic PIR schemes over general graphs

4.2.1
Capacities for probabilistic PIR schemes: Proof of
Theorem

1.15

We now present a probabilistic PIR scheme that achieves the retrieval rate stated in
Theorem

1.15
. This scheme applies to any (multi-)graph, requires at most one summation from each server, and is implemented through a simple randomized strategy. Moreover, we identify scenarios in which it provides improved performance over existing constructions.

Consider a (multi-)graph-based replication system with
N
servers
S_{1},S_{2},\dots,S_{N}
, and
K
files
W_{1},W_{2},\dots,W_{K}
. Let
G=(V,E)
be an undirected graph representing this replication system, where
|V|=N
and
|E|=K
. Each edge in
G
corresponds to a file, so we label the edges as
E=\{e_{1},e_{2},\dots,e_{K}\}
, where
e_{k}
corresponds to file
W_{k}
. For a vertex (server)
S_{i}\in V
, with
i\in[N]
, define
N_{i}
as the set of indices of all edges incident to
S_{i}
. The degree of server
S_{i}
is then
d(i)=|N_{i}|
.

Suppose the user wishes to retrieve file
W_{\theta}
for some
\theta\in[K]
, which is stored on servers
S_{\theta_{1}}
and
S_{\theta_{2}}
, where
\theta_{1},\theta_{2}\in[N]
. Without loss of generality, we assume
\theta_{1}<\theta_{2}
.

We begin by outlining the core strategy of our PIR scheme. The user generates two independent binary vectors of length
K
:

\bm{\mu}=(\mu_{1},\mu_{2},\dots,\mu_{K})\quad\text{and}\quad\bm{\lambda}=(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\dots,\lambda_{K}),

where each entry is drawn independently and uniformly at random from
\{0,1\}
. Intuitively, the vector
\bm{\mu}
specifies which files participate in the queries, while
\bm{\lambda}
determines the sign (positive or negative) assigned to each file within the queries.

For each
i\in[N]
and each
j\in[K]
, if file
W_{j}
is stored on server
S_{i}
(i.e.,
j\in N_{i}
), we define a sign value
\epsilon_{i,j}\in\{-1,+1\}
based on the edge
e_{j}
and a uniformly random bit
\lambda_{j}\in\{0,1\}
associated with file
W_{j}
.

Fix any
j\in[K]
, and suppose file
W_{j}
is stored on servers
S_{i_{1}}
and
S_{i_{2}}
, where
i_{1}<i_{2}
. We define

\epsilon_{i_{1},j}=(-1)^{\lambda_{j}},\quad\text{and}\quad\epsilon_{i_{2},j}=(-1)^{\lambda_{j}+1}.

This ensures that the contributions from the two servers cancel out:

\sum_{i\in e_{j}}\epsilon_{i,j}=\epsilon_{i_{1},j}+\epsilon_{i_{2},j}=0.

(95)

Thus, the values
\epsilon_{i,j}
are now fully defined for all pairs
(i,j)
with
j\in N_{i}
, including the case
j=\theta
. Since each
\lambda_{j}
is independently and uniformly chosen from
\{0,1\}
, the signs
\epsilon_{i,j}
are also independent across different
j
. In particular, for any fixed server
i
, the collection
\{\epsilon_{i,j}:j\in N_{i}\}
consists of mutually independent random variables, each uniformly distributed over
\{-1,+1\}
.

Based on the vectors
\bm{\mu}
,
\bm{\lambda}
, and the sign values
\epsilon_{i,j}
, the user generates the queries according to the following strategy:

•

For each server
S_{i}
with
i\in[N]\setminus\{\theta_{1},\theta_{2}\}
, the user sends the linear combination:

\sum_{j\in N_{i}}\mu_{j}\epsilon_{i,j}\cdot W_{j}.

•

For server
S_{\theta_{1}}
, the user sends:

\mu_{\theta}\epsilon_{\theta_{1},\theta}\cdot W_{\theta}+\sum_{j\in N_{\theta_{1}}\setminus\{\theta\}}\mu_{j}\epsilon_{\theta_{1},j}\cdot W_{j}.

•

For server
S_{\theta_{2}}
, the user sends:

(1-\mu_{\theta})\epsilon_{\theta_{2},\theta}\cdot W_{\theta}+\sum_{j\in N_{\theta_{2}}\setminus\{\theta\}}\mu_{j}\epsilon_{\theta_{2},j}\cdot W_{j}.

In this construction, the value
\mu_{j}
determines whether a non-desired file
W_{j}
is included in the queries. If
\mu_{j}=1
, then
W_{j}
appears in the queries to both of the servers storing it, but with opposite signs due to the definition of
\epsilon_{i,j}
, ensuring cancellation in the overall response. For the desired file
W_{\theta}
, only one of the two servers holding it includes it in its response. The bit
\mu_{\theta}
determines which server it is: if
\mu_{\theta}=1
, then
W_{\theta}
appears only in the query to
S_{\theta_{1}}
; otherwise, it appears only in the query to
S_{\theta_{2}}
.

Next, we verify that the constructed queries form a valid PIR scheme and compute the corresponding retrieval rate.

Reliability:

Consider the sum of all server responses:

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in[N]}A_{i}

\displaystyle=\mu_{\theta}\epsilon_{\theta_{1},\theta}\cdot W_{\theta}+\sum_{j\in N_{\theta_{1}}\setminus\{\theta\}}\mu_{j}\epsilon_{\theta_{1},j}\cdot W_{j}+(1-\mu_{\theta})\epsilon_{\theta_{2},\theta}\cdot W_{\theta}+\sum_{j\in N_{\theta_{2}}\setminus\{\theta\}}\mu_{j}\epsilon_{\theta_{2},j}\cdot W_{j}

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{i\in[N]\setminus\{\theta_{1},\theta_{2}\}}\sum_{j\in N_{i}}\mu_{j}\epsilon_{i,j}\cdot W_{j}

\displaystyle=\mu_{\theta}\epsilon_{\theta_{1},\theta}\cdot W_{\theta}+(1-\mu_{\theta})\epsilon_{\theta_{2},\theta}\cdot W_{\theta}+\sum_{j\in[K]\setminus\{\theta\}}\sum_{i\in e_{j}}\mu_{j}\epsilon_{i,j}\cdot W_{j}

(96)

\displaystyle=(1-2\mu_{\theta})\epsilon_{\theta_{2},\theta}\cdot W_{\theta}+\mu_{\theta}(\epsilon_{\theta_{1},\theta}+\epsilon_{\theta_{2},\theta})\cdot W_{\theta}+\sum_{j\in[K]\setminus\{\theta\}}\mu_{j}W_{j}\sum_{i\in e_{j}}\epsilon_{i,j}

\displaystyle=(1-2\mu_{\theta})\epsilon_{\theta_{2},\theta}\cdot W_{\theta},

(97)

where (

Since both

and

Privacy:

Fix some
i\in[N]
. We need to show that the distribution of the response

is independent of the choice of the desired file
\theta\in[K]
. Without loss of generality, suppose there exists a subset

\hat{\epsilon}_{i,j}\in\{-1,+1\}

First, consider the case where
\theta\notin N_{i}

\Pr\left(A_{i}=\sum_{j\in\Lambda}\hat{\epsilon}_{i,j}W_{j}\right)

\displaystyle\Pr\left((\mu_{j}=0\text{ for all }j\in N_{i}\setminus\Lambda)\penalty\ \wedge\penalty\ (\mu_{j}=1\text{ for all }j\in\Lambda)\penalty\ \wedge\penalty\ (\epsilon_{i,j}=\hat{\epsilon}_{i,j}\text{ for all }j\in\Lambda)\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{|N_{i}\setminus\Lambda|}\cdot\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{|\Lambda|}\cdot\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{|\Lambda|}

(99)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{d(i)+|\Lambda|}},

where (

\mu_{j}

Now consider the case

Rate:

For any

\displaystyle\Pr(A_{i}=0)

\displaystyle=\Pr(\mu_{j}=0\text{ for all }j\in N_{i})

\displaystyle=\prod_{j\in N_{i}}\Pr(\mu_{j}=0)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{d(i)}},

where

in

For

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sum_{i\in[N]}H(A_{i})}

\displaystyle=\frac{L}{\sum_{i\in[N]}\Pr(A_{i}\neq 0)\cdot 1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sum_{i\in[N]}\left(1-\frac{1}{2^{d(i)}}\right)}.

Compared to the scheme derived from the complete-graph-based construction, our scheme achieves better performance when the average degree of the graph is below 2. While the complete-graph-based approach may yield a higher retrieval rate when the average degree exceeds 2, this construction offers a practical advantage by avoiding the need to solve auxiliary derived schemes. This trade-off between rate and implementation complexity may make our approach more favorable in many practical scenarios.

This confirms that, for general multi-graphs, the probabilistic scheme presented in this section is strictly superior.

5
Concluding Remarks

In this work, we investigated the PIR capacity of graph-based replication systems with non-colluding servers, focusing particularly on complete graphs. We introduced a novel approach to derive improved upper bounds that exploit the symmetry of the underlying graph structure, yielding results that are substantially tighter than existing bounds. From an algorithmic perspective, we developed an explicit recursive construction of PIR schemes that provides enhanced lower bounds on the capacity. Consequently, we reduced the asymptotic gap between the upper and lower bounds for the PIR capacity of complete graphs to approximately 1.0444, thereby making significant progress toward resolving this fundamental problem.

Beyond these concrete advances, our results reveal deeper structural insights into how graph topology constrains information flow in PIR protocols. Notably, our techniques extend naturally to other highly symmetric graphs such as complete bipartite graphs and certain multigraphs. Moreover, the connection we established between deterministic and probabilistic PIR schemes offers new perspectives for designing low-subpacketization protocols, which are crucial for practical deployment. Despite these developments, many fundamental questions remain open:

•

Exact capacity of small graphs.
Can the remaining gap between upper and lower bounds for
\mathcal{C}(K_{4})
be closed? Determining the precise capacity of small graphs remains a challenging but potentially tractable problem.

•

Beyond complete graphs.
Can the recursive construction framework developed for
K_{N}
be generalized to yield bounds for other graph classes such as trees, expanders, or bounded-degree graphs?

•

Tight bounds for multigraphs.
The probabilistic PIR scheme we propose for the multigraph
K_{N}^{(r)}
is relatively simple and likely far from optimal. Is it possible to establish tighter upper or lower bounds on
\mathcal{C}(K_{N}^{(r)})
, especially as
r
increases?

•

Beyond 2-replication.
Our current model assumes each file is replicated on exactly two servers. Can our techniques be extended to hypergraph-based systems, where each file may be stored on more than two servers?

•

Graph density vs PIR capacity.
In
2.3
, we showed that
\mathcal{C}(G_{1})\geq\mathcal{C}(G_{2})
whenever
G_{1}\subseteq G_{2}
. This raises a broader question: is there a general monotonic relationship between the density or degree distribution of a graph and its PIR capacity?

We believe that the methods introduced in this paper, from entropy-based upper bounds to structure-aware PIR constructions, lay the groundwork for further progress in understanding PIR over structured storage systems. In particular, exploring connections with extremal graph theory, information inequalities, and coding-theoretic tools may yield deeper insights into the combinatorial limits of private information retrieval.

Appendix C
Proof of
Lemma

3.15

We first focus on deriving a closed-form expression for
x_{k}
when
1\leq k\leq\left\lfloor\frac{N}{2}\right\rfloor+1
. Combining the recursive relations for
x_{k}
and
z_{k}
given in (
61
), we obtain:

x_{k+2}=\frac{N-k+1}{2}x_{k+1}-\frac{k}{2}x_{k}.

(106)

Fix an integer
N\geq 3
. Define a new sequence
\{g_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
satisfying the same recurrence:

g_{k+2}=\frac{N-k+1}{2}g_{k+1}-\frac{k}{2}g_{k},\quad\text{with }g_{0}=0,\,g_{1}=1.

Then, by uniqueness of solutions to the recurrence, we have
x_{k}=g_{k}
for all
k\leq\left\lfloor\frac{N}{2}\right\rfloor+1
.

Let
G_{N}(t):=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}g_{k}t^{k}
denote the generating function of the sequence
\{g_{k}\}
. We now derive a differential equation for
G_{N}(t)
. The recurrence implies:

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}g_{k+2}t^{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{G_{N}(t)-t}{t^{2}},

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{N-k+1}{2}g_{k+1}t^{k}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}G_{N}^{\prime}(t)+\frac{N+2}{2}\cdot\frac{G_{N}(t)}{t},

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{kg_{k}}{2}t^{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}tG_{N}^{\prime}(t).

Substituting these into the recurrence yields the following first-order linear differential equation:

(t^{2}+t^{3})G_{N}^{\prime}(t)+(2-(N+2)t)G_{N}(t)-2t=0.

(107)

We first solve the associated homogeneous equation:

(t^{2}+t^{3})G_{N}^{\prime}(t)+(2-(N+2)t)G_{N}(t)=0.

(108)

Rewriting this as a separable ODE gives:

\frac{dG_{N}(t)}{G_{N}(t)}=\frac{(N+2)t-2}{t^{2}(1+t)}\,dt.

Integrating both sides, we find that the general solution to (
108
) is:

G_{N}(t)=C\left(\frac{t}{1+t}\right)^{N+4}e^{\frac{2}{t}},

for some constant
C
.

To solve the original inhomogeneous equation (
107
), we apply the method of variation of parameters. We consider a solution of the form

G_{N}(t)=C(t)\left(\frac{t}{1+t}\right)^{N+4}e^{\frac{2}{t}}.

(109)

Substituting (
109
) into (
107
) and simplifying yields the following differential equation for
C(t)
:

C^{\prime}(t)=\frac{2}{t^{2}}\left(\frac{1+t}{t}\right)^{N+3}e^{-\frac{2}{t}}.

(110)

For each integer
n\geq 0
, define

J_{n}(t):=\int\frac{1}{t^{2}}\left(\frac{1+t}{t}\right)^{n}e^{-\frac{2}{t}}\,dt.

Then the general solution to (
107
) is

G_{N}(t)=\left(2J_{N+3}(t)+C_{0}\right)\left(\frac{t}{1+t}\right)^{N+4}e^{\frac{2}{t}},

for some constant
C_{0}
.

We now compute
J_{n}(t)
. For
n\geq 1
, we have:

\displaystyle 2J_{n}(t)

\displaystyle=\int\frac{2}{t^{2}}\left(\frac{1+t}{t}\right)^{n}e^{-\frac{2}{t}}\,dt

\displaystyle=\int\left(\frac{1+t}{t}\right)^{n}\,d\left(e^{-\frac{2}{t}}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1+t}{t}\right)^{n}e^{-\frac{2}{t}}+n\int\frac{1}{t^{2}}\left(\frac{1+t}{t}\right)^{n-1}e^{-\frac{2}{t}}\,dt

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1+t}{t}\right)^{n}e^{-\frac{2}{t}}+nJ_{n-1}(t).

In particular,

2J_{0}(t)=\int\frac{2}{t^{2}}e^{-\frac{2}{t}}\,dt=e^{-\frac{2}{t}}+C.

By induction, we obtain:

J_{n}(t)=\sum_{i=0}^{n}\frac{n!}{2^{n-i+1}i!}\left(\frac{1+t}{t}\right)^{i}e^{-\frac{2}{t}}+C,

for some constant
C
. Hence,

C(t)=\sum_{i=0}^{N+3}\frac{(N+3)!}{2^{N-i+3}i!}\left(\frac{1+t}{t}\right)^{i}e^{-\frac{2}{t}}+C.

(111)

Substituting into (
109
) and
G_{N}(0)=0
, we obtain

G_{N}(t)=\sum_{i=0}^{N+3}\frac{(N+3)!}{2^{N-i+3}i!}\left(\frac{t}{1+t}\right)^{N+4-i}.

(112)

To extract
g_{k}
, we compute
G_{N}^{(k)}(0)
. Let
f_{i}(t):=\left(\frac{t}{1+t}\right)^{i}
. Then:

f_{i}^{(k)}(0)=\begin{cases}0,&\text{if }k<i,\\
(-1)^{k-i}k!\binom{k-1}{i-1},&\text{if }k\geq i.\end{cases}

Hence,

\displaystyle k!g_{k}

\displaystyle=G_{N}^{(k)}(0)=\sum_{i=0}^{N+3}\frac{(N+3)!}{2^{N-i+3}i!}f_{N-i+4}^{(k)}(0)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{N+3}\frac{(N+3)!}{2^{N-i+3}i!}(-1)^{k-N+i-4}k!\binom{k-1}{N-i+3}.

Therefore, for
k\leq\left\lfloor\frac{N}{2}\right\rfloor+1
, we have:

\displaystyle x_{k}=g_{k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=N-k+4}^{N+3}\frac{(N+3)!}{2^{N-i+3}i!}(-1)^{k-N+i-4}\binom{k-1}{N-i+3}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\frac{(N+3)!}{2^{j}(N-j+3)!}(-1)^{k-j-1}\binom{k-1}{j}.

For
k_{0}:=\left\lfloor\frac{N}{2}\right\rfloor+2
, we have:

\displaystyle x_{k_{0}}

\displaystyle=\frac{k_{0}-1}{2k_{0}-N}(x_{k_{0}-1}+z_{k_{0}-1})

(113)

\displaystyle=\frac{k_{0}-1}{2k_{0}-N}\left(2x_{k_{0}-1}-\frac{k_{0}-2}{2}x_{k_{0}-2}\right).

(114)

Substituting the closed forms of
x_{k_{0}-1}
and
x_{k_{0}-2}
, we obtain:

\displaystyle x_{k_{0}}=\frac{k_{0}-1}{2k_{0}-N}\cdot\sum_{j=0}^{k_{0}-2}\left((-1)^{k_{0}-j-2}\cdot\frac{(N+3)!}{2^{j}(N-j+3)!}\cdot\frac{k_{0}-j+2}{2}\cdot\binom{k_{0}-2}{j}\right).

For
k>k_{0}
, by iterating the recurrence in (
62
), we get:

\displaystyle x_{k}

\displaystyle=x_{k_{0}}\prod_{i=k_{0}+1}^{k}\frac{i-1}{2i-N}

\displaystyle=\left(\prod_{i=k_{0}}^{k}\frac{i-1}{2i-N}\right)\cdot\sum_{j=0}^{k_{0}-2}\left((-1)^{k_{0}-j-2}\cdot\frac{(N+3)!}{2^{j}(N-j+3)!}\cdot\frac{k_{0}-j+2}{2}\cdot\binom{k_{0}-2}{j}\right).

This completes the proof of
Lemma

3.15
.
