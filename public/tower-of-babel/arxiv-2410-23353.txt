On Computational Complexity of Unitary and State Design Properties
Yoshifumi Nakata, Yuki Takeuchi, Martin Kliesch, Andrew Darmawan

Abstract
We investigate unitary and state t t -designs from a computational complexity perspective. First, we address the problems of computing frame potentials that characterize (approximate) t t -designs. We present a quantum algorithm for computing frame potentials and establish the following: (1) exact computation can be achieved by a single query to a # ​ 𝖯 {\#{\mathsf{P}}} -oracle and is # ​ 𝖯 {\#{\mathsf{P}}} -hard; (2) for state vectors, deciding whether the frame potential is larger than or smaller than certain values is 𝖡𝖰𝖯 {\mathsf{BQP}} -complete, provided the promise gap between the two values is inverse-polynomial in the number of qubits; and (3) for both state vectors and unitaries, this promise problem is 𝖯𝖯 {\mathsf{PP}} -complete if the promise gap is exponentially small. Second, we address the promise problem of deciding whether or not a given set is a good approximation to a design. Given a certain promise gap that could be constant, we show that this problem is 𝖯𝖯 {\mathsf{PP}} -hard, highlighting the inherent computational difficulty of determining properties of unitary and state designs. We further identify implications of our results, including variational methods for constructing designs, diagnosing quantum chaos, and exploring emergent designs in Hamiltonian systems.

In this paper, we formulate these questions as computational problems and initiate a computational complexity theoretic analysis of unitary and state design properties.

II.1
Notation

We use the Schatten
p
-norms for linear operators, which are defined by
\|X\|_{p}\coloneqq(\mathrm{Tr}\bigl[|X|^{p}\bigr])^{1/p}
(
p\in[1,\infty]
) with
|X|=\sqrt{X^{\dagger}X}
.
We particularly use the trace (
p=1
), the Hilbert-Schmidt (
p=2
), and the spectral (
p=\infty
) norms. They satisfy

\|O\|_{\infty}\leq\|O\|_{2}\leq\|O\|_{1}\\
\leq\sqrt{\textrm{rank}(O)}\|O\|_{2}\leq\textrm{rank}(O)\|O\|_{\infty}.

(1)

For superoperators, we use the diamond norm
\|{\,\cdot\,}\|_{\diamond}
, which is defined by
\|{\mathcal{T}}\|_{\diamond}=\max_{n\geq 1}\|{\mathcal{T}}\otimes{\rm id}_{n}\|_{1\rightarrow 1}
, where
{\rm id}_{n}
is the identity map on the
n
-dimensional Hilbert space, and
\|{\mathcal{E}}\|_{1\rightarrow 1}=\max_{X}\|{\mathcal{E}}(X)\|_{1}/\|X\|_{1}
with the maximization over all non-zero operators
X
.

Throughout the paper, we consider probability measures over multisets of state vectors or unitaries. An average is written as
\mathbb{E}_{|\psi\rangle\sim\mu}[{\,\cdot\,}]
for the probability measure
\mu
of state vectors, and as
\mathbb{E}_{U\sim\nu}[{\,\cdot\,}]
for the probability measure
\nu
of unitaries.
When a finite multiset of state vectors or unitaries is given, the uniform distribution over the multiset is naturally defined. Hence, with a slight abuse of notation, we use the same symbol for the multiset and the uniform distribution over the multiset. For instance, for a finite multiset of state vectors
\textsf{S}=\{|\psi_{j}\rangle\}_{j=1,\dots,K}
, we express its average such as
\mathbb{E}_{|\psi\rangle\sim\textsf{S}}[{\,\cdot\,}]=1/K\sum_{j=1}^{K}({\,\cdot\,})
. Hereafter, we refer to a multiset as a set unless it is necessary to clearly distinguish them.

An important probability measure on the unitary group
\mathbb{U}(d)
is the (normalized)
Haar
measure
H
. A unitary
U
drawn uniformly at random with respect to the Haar measure
H
is called a Haar random unitary and is conventionally denoted by
U\sim\textsf{H}
.

By applying a Haar random unitary to a fixed state vector
|\varphi_{0}\rangle
, a unitarily invariant probability measure on the set of all state vectors can be defined, which we call a
Haar random state
. Due to the unitarily invariant property of a Haar random unitary, a Haar random state is independent of the choice of the fixed vector
|\varphi_{0}\rangle
. Similarly to a Haar random unitary, we use the notation
|\varphi\rangle\sim\textsf{H}
for a Haar random state.

II.2
Unitary and state designs

We provide a brief overview of definitions and important properties of state and unitary designs.

II.2.1
State designs

In the original definition from design theory
[
56
,
57
]
, a state
t
-design (
t\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
) is defined as the uniform distribution over a finite set (not a multiset) of state vectors that has the same
t
-th moment as a Haar random state.
However, in quantum information, it is common to define a design as the uniform distribution over a finite multiset, which more closely aligns with the concept of a
weighted

t
-design in design theory. We follow the convention in quantum information and adopt the following definition of a state design.

Definition 1

(
\delta
-approximate state
t
-design)
.

When the uniform probability measure over a finite multiset

d

\Bigl\|\mathbb{E}_{|\psi\rangle\sim\textsf{S}}\bigl[|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|^{\otimes t}\bigr]-\mathbb{E}_{|\psi\rangle\sim\textsf{H}}\bigl[|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|^{\otimes t}\bigr]\Bigr\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{\delta}{d_{t}},

(2)

it is called a
\delta
-approximate state
t
-design. Here,
d_{t}=\binom{d+t-1}{t}
is the dimension of the symmetric subspace in
(\mathbb{C}^{d})^{\otimes t}
.
When
\delta=0
, it is called an
exact
state
t
-design.

The expectation of the second term in
Eq.

2
can be explicitly spelt out using Schur-Weyl duality. Denoting by
\Pi_{\rm sym}
the projection onto the symmetric subspace in
(\mathbb{C}^{d})^{\otimes t}
, we have

\mathbb{E}_{|\psi\rangle\sim\textsf{H}}\bigl[|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|^{\otimes t}\bigr]=\frac{\Pi_{\rm sym}}{d_{t}}.

(3)

In
Definition

1
, we measure the approximation precision by using the spectral norm following one of the original proposals
[
77
]
. In recent years, the trace norm has also been used instead, to define a
\delta
-approximate state
t
-design by

\Bigl\|\mathbb{E}_{|\psi\rangle\sim\textsf{S}}\bigl[|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|^{\otimes t}\bigr]-\mathbb{E}_{|\psi\rangle\sim\textsf{H}}\bigl[|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|^{\otimes t}\bigr]\Bigr\|_{1}\leq\delta.

(4)

This definition is weaker than
Definition

1
in the sense that
Eq.

2
implies
Eq.

4
, which is due to

1

(\mathbb{C}^{d})^{\otimes t}
. Hence, we use

1

A useful quantity that characterizes a state design is a
state frame potential
.

Definition 2

(State frame potential)
.

Let
\mu
be a probability measure on a multiset of state vectors. The state frame potential of degree

\displaystyle F_{t}(\mu)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\Bigl\|\mathbb{E}_{|\psi\rangle\sim\mu}\bigl[|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|^{\otimes t}\bigr]\Bigr\|_{2}^{2}

(5)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{|\psi\rangle,|\phi\rangle\sim\mu}\Bigl[\bigl|\langle\psi|\phi\rangle\bigr|^{2t}\Bigr].

(6)

Important properties of the state frame potential are summarized in

1

These properties are well-known, but for completeness, we provide a proof in

Note that the assumption

Let

be such that

1.

F_{t}(\textsf{H})=1/d_{t}

2.

For any probability measure

F_{t}(\mu)\in\bigl[1/d_{t},\,1\bigr],

(7)

where equality of the lower bound holds if and only if it is an exact state

F_{t}(\textsf{S})\in\bigl[\max\bigl\{1/d_{t},\,1/K\bigr\},\,1\bigr].

(8)

3.

If a finite set

\delta
-approximate state

F_{t}(\textsf{S})\leq\frac{1}{d_{t}}+\frac{\delta^{2}}{d_{t}}.

(9)

4.

If a finite set

(10)

then

\delta
-approximate state

-design.

5.

For a finite set

\delta
-approximate state

(11)

From the property
2
in
Proposition

1
, one observes that the frame potential is indeed a potential in the sense that a state

This is extended to approximate state designs by the properties

However, if the frame potential

\frac{1}{d_{t}}+\frac{\delta^{2}}{d_{t}^{2}}<F_{t}(\textsf{S})<\frac{1}{d_{t}}+\frac{\delta^{2}}{d_{t}},

we cannot conclude whether or not the set is a

The last property

II.2.2
Unitary designs

To define a
\delta
-approximate unitary
t
-design, it is convenient to use the
moment operator of degree
t
of a probability measure of unitaries.
So, let
\mu
be a probability measure on the unitary group
\mathbb{U}(d)
. The moment operator is given by

M^{(t)}_{\mu}\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{U\sim\mu}[U^{\otimes t}\otimes\bar{U}^{\otimes t}],

(13)

where
\bar{{\,\cdot\,}}
denotes the complex conjugate in a fixed orthonormal basis.

Based on the moment operator, a unitary
t
-design can be defined as follows.

Definition 3

(
\delta
-approximate unitary
t
-design)
.

When the uniform probability measure over a finite multiset
\textsf{U}=\{U_{j}\}_{j=1,\dots,K}
satisfies

\bigl\|M^{(t)}_{\textsf{U}}-M^{(t)}_{\textsf{H}}\bigr\|_{1}\leq\delta,

(14)

it is called a
\delta
-approximate unitary
t
-design
.
When
\delta=0
, it is called an
exact
unitary
t
-design.

Similarly to state designs, Schur-Weyl duality allows us to identify the expectation in
Eq.

14
over the Haar measure. Let
\pi_{t}:U\in\mathbb{U}(d)\mapsto U^{\otimes t}\otimes\bar{U}^{\otimes t}
be a representation of the unitary group
\mathbb{U}(d)
. Then, taking the expectation yields a projection onto trivial irreducible representations of
\pi_{t}
.

Approximate unitary designs can also be defined in terms of different norms to measure the approximation. One of the standard definitions is based on the diamond norm of superoperators. For a given probability measure
\mu
on the unitary group
\mathbb{U}(d)
, let
{\mathcal{G}}^{(t)}_{\mu}
be the CPTP map given by

{\mathcal{G}}^{(t)}_{\mu}(X)=\mathbb{E}_{U\sim\mu}\bigl[U^{\otimes t}XU^{\dagger\otimes t}\bigr],

(15)

where

is an operator on a

-dimensional Hilbert space. A

-approximate unitary

-design in terms of the diamond norm is the uniform probability measure over a finite multiset

that satisfies

\bigl\|{\mathcal{G}}^{(t)}_{\textsf{U}}-{\mathcal{G}}^{(t)}_{\textsf{H}}\bigr\|_{\diamond}\leq\delta.

(16)

While these two definitions are not exactly the same, using either one generally does not yield significant differences in applications of unitary designs. Indeed, if
U

\delta
-approximate unitary

-design in one of the two definitions, then

-approximate unitary

d^{-2t}\bigl\|M^{(t)}_{\textsf{U}}-M^{(t)}_{\textsf{H}}\bigr\|_{1}\leq\bigl\|{\mathcal{G}}^{(t)}_{\textsf{U}}-{\mathcal{G}}^{(t)}_{\textsf{H}}\bigr\|_{\diamond}\\
\leq d^{t}\bigl\|M^{(t)}_{\textsf{U}}-M^{(t)}_{\textsf{H}}\bigr\|_{1}.

(17)

For the derivation, see the comment after
Proposition

2
.
As most circuit constructions of
\delta
-approximate unitary

-designs on
n

{\mathsf{poly}}(n,t,\log 1/\delta)

,

,
63
,
64
,
65
,
66
,

68

It is also important that one can, in general, amplify the approximation accuracy simply by repeating the unitaries. That is, for any
\delta
-approximate unitary

-design
\mathsf{U}=\{U_{j}\}_{j}
in terms of the moment operators (

II.3
Complexity classes

The standard idea for characterizing the difficulty of a computational problem is to identify which complexity class the problem belongs to and to show that it is among the most difficult problems in that class.
We briefly review the complexity classes
{\#{\mathsf{P}}}
,
{\mathsf{PP}}
,
{\mathsf{PQP}}
,
{\mathsf{BQP}}
, and
{\mathsf{FP}}^{\#{\mathsf{P}}}
which are important for this work.

The class
{\#{\mathsf{P}}}
is a set of all problems of counting the number of accepting paths of a non-deterministic polynomial-time Turing machine.
By definition, the output of a
{\#{\mathsf{P}}}
-problem is a non-negative integer.

We take the common view in quantum information theory that the classes
{\mathsf{PP}}
,
{\mathsf{PQP}}
, and
{\mathsf{BQP}}
are
promise problems

[
78
,
79
]
.
For such a problem the answer is either true or false but has to be given (correctly) only for certain problem instances, which are specified by a promise. That is, each problem comes along with a potential promise and only has to be solved when it is fulfilled.

The class
{\mathsf{PP}}
is the set of promise problems for each of which some classical algorithm with polynomially bounded runtime
(more formally, a probabilistic polynomial-time Turing machine)
can output their correct answer with probability strictly larger than
1/2
.
Since the probability may be exponentially close to
1/2
,
{\mathsf{PP}}
contains the problems that are unlikely to be solvable by classical algorithms in polynomial time.
The class
{\mathsf{PQP}}
is a quantum analogue of
{\mathsf{PP}}
, in which the probabilistic polynomial time Turing machine is replaced with a polynomial-time quantum algorithm.
The class
{\mathsf{BQP}}
is defined similarly to
{\mathsf{PQP}}
but with a higher success probability of
\geq 2/3
.

Obviously,
{\mathsf{BQP}}\subseteq{\mathsf{PQP}}
and
{\mathsf{PP}}\subseteq{\mathsf{PQP}}
. It is also known that
{\mathsf{PQP}}\subseteq{\mathsf{PP}}

[
79
]
. Hence,
{\mathsf{BQP}}\subseteq{\mathsf{PQP}}={\mathsf{PP}}
.
It is expected that
{\mathsf{BQP}}\neq{\mathsf{PQP}}
, i.e.,
{\mathsf{PQP}}={\mathsf{PP}}
is expected to contain problems that are intractable even for scalable universal quantum computers.

We will rely on the concepts of hard and complete problems.
Informally, a problem is
hard
for some class if it is at least as difficult as the most difficult problem of that class in the sense that a solver for that problem can be used to solve any other problem of the class with some “small overhead”.
Technically, hardness is always defined with respect to a specific type of reduction, which depends on the class in question.
If a problem is hard for some class and, at the same time, a member of that class, then it is
complete
for that class.

Particularly important complete problems in this work are
{\mathsf{\#SAT}}
and
MAJ-SAT
, which are
{\#{\mathsf{P}}}
-complete and
{\mathsf{PP}}
-complete problems, respectively.

Definition 5

(
{\mathsf{\#SAT}}
)
.

Given the input as a classically efficiently computable function
f:\{0,1\}^{N}\rightarrow\{0,1\}
specified by a Boolean circuit, output the number of
x\in\{0,1\}^{N}
satisfying
f(x)=1
.

Note that this problem
{\mathsf{\#SAT}}
is equivalent to calculating the number of satisfying assignments of a Boolean formula, which is the origin of its name.

Definition 6

(
MAJ-SAT

[
80
]
)
.

Let
f:\{0,1\}^{m}\rightarrow\{0,1\}
be a Boolean function that is computable in classical polynomial time, and let
s(f)
be
\sum_{x\in\{0,1\}^{m}}f(x)
.
Decide whether
0\leq s(f)<2^{m-1}
or
2^{m-1}\leq s(f)\leq 2^{m}
.

Theorem 1

(See, e.g.,
[
81
]
)
.

{\mathsf{\#SAT}}
is
{\#{\mathsf{P}}}
-complete, and
MAJ-SAT
is
{\mathsf{PP}}
-complete.

Finally,
{\mathsf{FP}}^{\#{\mathsf{P}}}
is the class of functions that can be computed by a deterministic algorithm in polynomial time with additional query access to a solver for
{\mathsf{\#SAT}}
.
That is, once a part of the algorithm has specified an instance of
{\mathsf{\#SAT}}
, its solution can be obtained in a single computation step.

III.1
Data format for sets of states and unitaries

When we consider computational problems related to unitary or state designs, it is natural to suppose that the input of the problem is a set of unitaries acting on
\mathbb{C}^{d}
or states in
\mathbb{C}^{d}
.
If a set consists of
{\mathsf{poly}}(d)
elements, such problems may be tractable in polynomial time with respect to
d
. However, in a quantum setting, it is standard to start with quantum circuits on
n=\log_{2}d
qubits consisting of
{\mathsf{poly}}(n)
gates and to ask about the efficiency in terms of
n
. Throughout the paper, we focus on such situations.

When concerned with designs on
n
qubits, we need to deal with the set consisting of
\Omega(\exp(n))
elements. In fact, the property
5
in
Proposition

1
and the property
23
in
Proposition

2
imply that a set
S
of
n
-qubit state vectors with cardinality
|\textsf{S}|<K_{\rm st}(2^{n},t,\delta)
is not a
\delta
-approximate state
t
-design, and that a set
U
of
n
-qubit unitaries with cardinality

|\textsf{U}|<K_{\rm uni}(2^{n},t,\delta)

is not a
\delta
-approximate unitary
t
-design. Note that both
K_{\rm st}(2^{n},t,\delta)
and
K_{\rm uni}(2^{n},t,\delta)
are exponentially large in
n
.

To efficiently handle a set with an exponentially large cardinality, the set should be well structured. We formalize this by introducing
a set of unitaries with a computationally efficient description
.

Definition 7

(Computationally efficient descriptions)
.

A multiset
\mathsf{U}=\{U_{j}\}_{j=1}^{K}\subseteq\mathbb{U}(2^{n})
of
n
-qubit unitaries, where
K\in{\mathcal{O}}(2^{{\mathsf{poly}}(n)})
, is said to have a
computationally efficient description
, if it is specified by a partial function
f
defined on
\{0,1\}^{m}
with
m\coloneqq\lceil\log_{2}K\rceil
, which is computable in classical polynomial time, that outputs a classical description
f(j)
(
j\in\{0,1\}^{m}
) of a
{\mathsf{poly}}(n)
-sized quantum circuit of
U_{j}
.

Trivial instances of a set of unitaries with a computationally efficient description are a set of
n
-qubit Pauli operators and that of
n
-qubit Clifford unitaries.
The former can be specified by
2n
bits
x\in\{0,1\}^{2n}
, and the description
f(x)
specifies a tensor product of single-qubit Pauli unitaries, which is a quantum circuit with depth
1
.
The latter can be similarly specified by
{\mathcal{O}}({\mathsf{poly}}(n))
bits with a suitable efficiently computable function
f
that identifies a Clifford circuit of circuit size
{\mathcal{O}}(n^{2}/\log(n))
, which is sufficient to represent any Clifford unitary
[
82
]
.

A set of of
n
-qubit unitaries appearing in
1-dimensional local random quantum circuit (1D LRC)
with
{\mathcal{O}}({\mathsf{poly}}(n))
depth is also an important instance of a set with a computationally efficient description. A 1D LRC repeats two layers of two-qubit unitaries organized in a brickwork fashion.
In the first layer,
2
-qubit gates, each randomly and independently drawn from a fixed gate set
{\mathcal{G}}
, are applied to odd-even qubits in parallel.
Similarly, in the second layer, random and independent
2
-qubit gates from
{\mathcal{G}}
are applied to even-odd qubits.
To specify each quantum circuit generated in this way, we need
{\mathcal{O}}(\{\mathrm{\#\ of\ gates}\}\log_{2}|{\mathcal{G}}|)
bits, where
|{\mathcal{G}}|
is the cardinality of the gate set
{\mathcal{G}}
.
The number of gates is
{\mathcal{O}}({\mathsf{poly}}(n))
whenever the depth is
{\mathcal{O}}({\mathsf{poly}}(n))
.
In a standard case,
|{\mathcal{G}}|
is constant, but even if
|{\mathcal{G}}|\in{\mathcal{O}}({\mathsf{exp}}(n))
, each quantum circuit generated in this way can be specified by
{\mathsf{poly}}(n)
bits (in the latter case, we need to assume that each gate in
{\mathcal{G}}
has a constant-size description).
This is also the case for local random quantum circuits on any graph in which the graph describes the two qubits to which a
2
-qubit gate is applied.

Other important sets of unitaries are those generated by Hamiltonian dynamics. When a Hamiltonian
H
satisfies certain conditions, it is known that the corresponding time evolution
e^{-itH}
can be simulated efficiently in terms of the time
t
by quantum circuits with small error (see e.g.
[
83
]
).
Hence, by sampling discrete times
t_{j}\in[0,{\mathsf{poly}}(n)]
(
j=1\dots,K
with
K\in\Theta(2^{{\mathsf{poly}}(n)})
) in an efficiently computable manner, we can define a set
\{e^{-iHt_{j}}\}_{j=1}^{K}
of unitaries with computationally efficient descriptions.
This type of set of unitaries may be of particular interest in numerical or experimental approaches to the emergent designs in quantum many-body systems
[
38
,
51
,
72
,
73
,
74
,
75
,
76
]
. Note that, in numerical or experimental settings, continuous degrees of freedom typically must be discretized and, hence, it is natural to consider discrete choices of time. Instead of discrete times, one may also consider a set of Hamiltonians
\{H_{j}\}_{j=1}^{K}
and may define
\{e^{-iH_{j}t}\}_{j=1}^{K}
at a fixed time
t\in{\mathcal{O}}({\mathsf{poly}}(n))
. This set also has a computationally efficient description as far as the Hamiltonian dynamics can be simulated efficiently by quantum circuits.

We also introduce a
set of state vectors with a computationally efficient description
. It is a set of state vectors generated by applying a set of unitaries with a computationally efficient description to a fixed initial state vector that can be prepared in a quantum polynomial time, such as a computational-basis state.

III.2
Quantum algorithm for computing frame potentials

Propositions

2

This motivates us to first provide quantum algorithms for estimating the unitary and state frame potentials.
The idea behind the quantum algorithm comes from the fact that the frame potentials are defined by a sum of inner products between state vectors or unitaries. By ‘encoding’ all the inner products into a single quantum state, we can compute the frame potential.

III.4
Promise problems on frame potentials

From the results in the previous section, it follows that an efficient general algorithm, whether classical or quantum, for exactly computing frame potentials, is unlikely to exist. It is then natural to consider approximate computation. One approach to address this question is to formulate it as a promise problem.

We introduce the state frame potential problem
\operatorname{{\mathsf{SFP}}}(t,\epsilon)
as follows.

Problem 3

(
\operatorname{{\mathsf{SFP}}}(t,\epsilon)
)
.

Input:

•

a set
\textsf{S}_{n}
of

(degree)

•

\alpha>\beta\geq\max\{1/d_{t},\,1/K_{n}\}

From the definition of

An algorithm for solving

In

In terms of the computational complexity, this implies the following.

For any constant

Combining these two, we conclude that, for any constant

III.6
Implications of our results

Our approach is based on computational complexity theory. Due to its broad applicability, our results have immediate implications for the topics related to unitary or state designs. Below, we outline three such implications along with a brief overview of the literature.

III.6.1
Variational approach to constructing designs

Generating
\delta
-approximate unitary
t
-designs is of fundamental importance. In
[
60
]
, the first efficient circuit construction has been proposed, which uses
O(tn^{2}(tn+\log 1/\delta))
gates if
t\in{\mathcal{O}}(n/\log n)
and
t\leq 2^{n/50}

[
84
]
.
Later, the number of gates has been improved to
{\mathcal{O}}(t^{4+o(1)}n(tn+\log 1/\delta))
for any
t
by local random circuits
[
64
]
and to
(t+1)n^{2}+n\log 1/\delta+{\mathcal{O}}(n)
for
t\in o(\sqrt{n})
by phase-Hadamard-cocktail random circuits
[
62
]
, where the term ‘phase-Hadamard-cocktail’ has been proposed in
[
85
]
.
Recently, structured random circuits with
{\mathcal{O}}(tn(\log n+\log 1/\delta))
gates have been shown to suffice
[
70
,
69
]
. Due to the substantial importance of designs and related concepts, more and more circuit constructions have been proposed
[
65
,
66
,
67
,
68
,
69
]
.

These results have been obtained first by proposing specific quantum circuits, such as local random circuits or phase-Hadamard-cocktail random circuits, and then by analysing their design properties.

An alternative approach is a variational method, explored in
[
71
]
to construct a design with small cardinality.
The variational method is based on a variational ansatz set
A
with computationally efficient descriptions and an update protocol, such as gradient descent, and may proceed as follows:

1.

Initialize an ansatz set
A
.

2.

Compute the frame potential of
A
and its derivative.

3.

Update the ansatz set
A
to reduce its frame potential.

4.

Repeat steps 2 and 3 until the frame potential approaches its minimum sufficiently closely.

Such a variational method performs well in small systems
[
71
]
. Moreover,
Theorem

7
indicates that, for state vectors, this method may work well on quantum computers until the frame potential approaches its minimum with an accuracy of
\Omega(1/{\mathsf{poly}}(n))
.
However, achieving higher accuracy for state vectors, such as
{\mathcal{O}}(2^{-{\mathsf{poly}}(n)})
, and dealing with a set of unitaries requires careful consideration.
In fact,
Theorems

9
,
10
,
11
and
12
immediately imply the following.

Corollary 1
.

If the ansatz set
A
can represent arbitrary sets with computationally efficient description, there is no variational method that outputs an approximate
t
-design in quantum and classical polynomial-time, assuming that
{\mathsf{PP}}\neq{\mathsf{BQP}}
and
{\mathsf{PP}}\neq{\mathsf{BPP}}
, respectively.

Our results, therefore, highlight that it is essential to restrict the representability of the ansatz set
A
for the variational approach to scale up to a large system.

III.6.2
Computational complexity of chaos via OTOCs

The unitary frame potential is closely related to OTOCs, which are widely used as diagnostics for quantum chaotic dynamics in many-body systems.
A
2t
-point OTOC is defined for an
n
-qubit state
\rho
, an
n
-qubit unitary
U
, and
2t

n
-qubit observables
\{O_{j}\}_{j=1,\dots,2t}
as follows:

{\rm OTOC}_{2t}(\rho,U,\{O_{j}\}_{j})\\
={\rm Tr}\Bigl[\Bigl(O_{2t}O_{2t-1}(U)\dots O_{2}O_{1}(U)\Bigr)\rho\Bigr],

(35)

where
O_{j}(U)=U^{\dagger}O_{j}U
. Note that the unitary is applied only to operators with odd indexes.
The OTOCs are important quantities for characterizing chaotic behaviour in quantum many-body systems, where
\rho
is a thermal state of a given Hamiltonian
H
,
U=e^{-iHT}
with
T
being time, and observables are commonly chosen to be single-qubit Pauli operators.
Under these settings, the OTOCs typically decrease as time
T
increases, and the speed of the decrease characterizes quantum chaotic dynamics in terms of the Lyapunov exponent
[
36
]
.

It has been shown in Ref.
[
37
]
that the OTOCs are closely related to the frame potential. To explain this, let us set
\rho
to be the completely mixed state
\pi
, corresponding to the infinite temperature, and let us take the average of the OTOCs over a given set
\textsf{U}_{n}
of
n
-qubit unitaries. By summing each observable
O_{j}
over an operator basis
\textsf{B}_{n}
, such as a set of all
n
-qubit Pauli operators, we can define an average
2t
-point OTOC at infinite temperature as

{\rm OTOC}_{2t}(\textsf{U})\\
=\frac{1}{2^{4nt}}\sum_{\{O_{j}\}_{j}\in\textsf{B}_{n}^{2t}}\mathbb{E}_{U\sim\textsf{U}_{n}}\bigl|{\rm OTOC}_{2t}(\pi,U,\{O_{j}\}_{j})\bigr|^{2}.

(36)

This quantity is shown to be equivalent to the unitary frame potential of
\textsf{U}_{n}
of degree
t
, namely,

{\rm OTOC}_{2t}(\textsf{U}_{n})=\frac{1}{2^{2(t+1)n}}F_{t}(\textsf{U}_{n}).

(37)

From
Eqs.

37
,
5
and
6
, it directly follows that computing the average
2t
-point OTOCs at infinite temperature is hard for any constant
t\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
.

Corollary 2
.

For any constant
t\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
, computing
{\rm OTOC}_{2t}(\textsf{U}_{n})
for a set
\textsf{U}_{n}
of unitaries with a computationally efficient description is in
{\mathsf{FP}}^{\#{\mathsf{P}}}
and
{\#{\mathsf{P}}}
-hard.

This indicates that verifying quantum chaotic dynamics via OTOCs is, in general, computationally hard in large systems, which characterizes the complex nature of chaotic dynamics from the computational complexity perspective.

III.6.3

The concepts of unitary and state designs have also been applied to investigate higher-order properties of thermalization and ergodicity in quantum many-body systems.

Let

be a Hamiltonian on a composite system

It is conjectured, with supporting numerical evidence, that if the Hamiltonian

[

By leveraging the connection between state designs, thermalization, and ergodicity, this insight has been extensively applied to the study of complex quantum many-body systems

The conjecture has been analytically addressed in a few solvable models in the thermodynamic limit

While general numerical simulations of a projected state ensemble are difficult, as they have to compute the measurement probability distributions, there may exist a class of Hamiltonians for which this is also computationally tractable. Even in such cases, our results highlight the importance of developing numerical methods that account for the specific dynamics governed by the Hamiltonian

Corollary 3

Let

III.7
Proof sketch

Below, we provide a brief overview of our proof methods. We first focus on the inclusion results and then explain the methods that we use for proving the hardness results.

III.7.1
Methods for the inclusion results

Our strategy to obtain the inclusion results is to begin with quantum algorithms, which are not necessarily efficient, and to dequantize them.
The inclusion results for
\operatorname{{\mathsf{compSFP}}}
and
\operatorname{{\mathsf{compUFP}}}
are obtained by dequantizing the quantum algorithm based on
Lemma

1
, which is achieved by a similar technique in Ref.
[
86
]
. For
\operatorname{{\mathsf{SFP}}}
and
\operatorname{{\mathsf{UFP}}}
, we propose variants of the quantum algorithm. In this case, the dequantization step follows from the equivalence that
{\mathsf{PQP}}={\mathsf{PP}}

[
79
]
.

From these inclusion results of the computational problems related to frame potentials, the inclusion results of
\operatorname{{\mathsf{StDes}}}
and
\operatorname{{\mathsf{UniDes}}}
can be immediately obtained as frame potentials characterize designs.

III.7.2
Methods for the hardness results

Proving hardness results is less straightforward. To establish those results, we employ a technique that we call “hide-a-hard-instance-in-a-set.” This consists of three steps. First, we choose a computationally hard instance relevant to the desired proof. Second, we embed the hard instance in state vectors or unitaries and, finally, we hide them in a set of state vectors or unitaries with known properties.

The specific hard instance depends on the statement to be shown. For the
{\#{\mathsf{P}}}
-hardness of
\operatorname{{\mathsf{compSFP}}}
and
\operatorname{{\mathsf{compUFP}}}
, we use a
{\#{\mathsf{P}}}
-complete problem
{\mathsf{\#SAT}}
. For the
{\mathsf{PP}}
-hardness of
\operatorname{{\mathsf{SFP}}}
and
\operatorname{{\mathsf{UFP}}}
with suitable parameters, a
{\mathsf{PP}}
-complete problem
MAJ-SAT
is used. For the
{\mathsf{BQP}}
-hardness of
\operatorname{{\mathsf{SFP}}}(t,\Omega(1/{\mathsf{poly}}(n)))
, we use a quantum circuit that solves a
{\mathsf{BQP}}
-complete problem.

These problems are embedded in a pair of state vectors or unitaries with a computationally efficient description. This embedding is such that the solution of the problems is given by the inner product of the pair.
The pairs are then “hidden” in a set of state vectors or unitaries that we know the details.
In the case of the computational problems related to frame potentials, this is enough for obtaining the hardness result: as the frame potential is a function of inner products, the frame potential of the resulting set should contain the information about the solution of the hard instance.
That is, if one can evaluate the frame potential of the resulting set, one can use the same method to obtain the solution of the hard instance. This provides a polynomial-time reduction from a complete problem to the corresponding computational problem.

This “hide-a-hard-instance-in-a-set” technique is also useful for proving the hardness results of
\operatorname{{\mathsf{StDes}}}
and
\operatorname{{\mathsf{UniDes}}}
, in which the main concern is to evaluate the approximation accuracy of a given set to a design.
In the proofs, we similarly use the
MAJ-SAT
problem and embed it into a pair of state vectors or unitaries. We then hide the pair in an approximate design. Although these steps are similar to those in the proof of the problems related to frame potentials, the proofs are more intricate due to the following two challenges.

First, unlike the frame potential, it is not immediately obvious if the approximation accuracy of the set is directly related to the inner product. Note that it is the inner product of the hidden pair that contains the solution of
MAJ-SAT
.
Through careful evaluations of matrix norms, we explicitly compute the approximation accuracy of the resulting set and show that it is given as a function of the inner product of the hidden pair. This evaluation is tight, which is also crucial as the tightness is the key factor to guarantee that any Boolean function can be embedded into
\operatorname{{\mathsf{StDes}}}
and
\operatorname{{\mathsf{UniDes}}}
while keeping their exclusive promise.

The second difficulty is that, in general, hiding even a single pair of state vectors or unitaries in approximate designs significantly degrades their approximation accuracy.
This imposes a substantial restriction on the parameter
\delta
in
\operatorname{{\mathsf{StDes}}}(t,t^{\prime},\delta,\delta^{\prime})
and
\operatorname{{\mathsf{UniDes}}}(t,t^{\prime},\delta,\delta^{\prime})
, preventing us from dealing with accurate designs in the problems. That is, we cannot choose, e.g,
\delta\in{\mathcal{O}}(2^{-{\mathsf{poly}}(n)})
.
We circumvent this limitation by using the multiplicity degree of freedom of approximate designs. Specifically, we use the fact that the multiplicity of approximate designs can be arbitrarily increased without changing the approximation accuracy. By hiding hard instances into a
sufficiently large
set, we can control the restriction on the approximation of the set after hiding and can remove the restriction on
\delta
.

We prove

Proof

From

F_{t}(\textsf{S}_{n})=\mathrm{Tr}\bigl[(\varrho^{A}_{\rm st})^{2}\bigr]

\langle\varrho_{\rm st}|^{\otimes 2}(\mathbb{F}^{AA^{\prime}}\otimes I^{BB^{\prime}})|\varrho_{\rm st}\rangle^{\otimes 2}

We show this by using a technique similar to the one used in Ref.

We first map the state
|\varrho_{\rm st}\rangle^{\otimes 2}
to the one that is generated by a quantum circuit consisting only of the Hadamard and Toffoli gates. To this end, let
\sum_{\alpha}(x_{\alpha}+iy_{\alpha})|\alpha\rangle

(x_{\alpha},y_{\alpha}\in\mathbb{R})
be an expansion of

in the computational basis. Using this notation and

39
, the frame potential is given as

\displaystyle F_{t}(\textsf{S}_{n})

\displaystyle=\langle\varrho_{\rm st}|^{\otimes 2}(\mathbb{F}^{AA^{\prime}}\otimes I^{BB^{\prime}})|\varrho_{\rm st}\rangle^{\otimes 2}

(40)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha,\beta}(x_{\beta}-iy_{\beta})(x_{\alpha}+iy_{\alpha})h(\alpha,\beta)

(41)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha,\beta}(x_{\alpha}x_{\beta}+y_{\alpha}y_{\beta})h(\alpha,\beta),

(42)

where
h(\alpha,\beta)=\langle\beta|(\mathbb{F}\otimes I)|\alpha\rangle
. In the last line, we used the fact that
h(\alpha,\beta)=h(\beta,\alpha)
, which is either
0
or
1
.

We now introduce one more ancillary qubit and define
|\bar{\varrho}^{\otimes 2}_{\rm st}\rangle
by

|\bar{\varrho}^{\otimes 2}_{\rm st}\rangle=\sum_{\alpha}|\alpha\rangle\otimes(x_{\alpha}|0\rangle+y_{\alpha}|1\rangle).

(43)

This is a state on
2(\kappa_{n}+nt)+1
qubits, where
\kappa_{n}=\log K_{n}
, and
K_{n}
is the cardinality of
\textsf{S}_{n}
.
When
|\varrho_{\rm st}\rangle^{\otimes 2}
can be efficiently generated by a quantum circuit, which is the case in our problem, it is known that
|\bar{\varrho}^{\otimes 2}_{\rm st}\rangle
can also be efficiently generated from the computational zero state only by using the Hadamard and Toffoli gates
1
1

1

Strictly speaking, the state generated by the quantum circuit composed only of the Hadamard and Toffoli gates is exponentially-close to the original state. We ignore this approximation error as it can be made small arbitrarily within the order of
\Theta(1/\exp(n))
.
.
Furthermore, it holds that

\displaystyle\langle\bar{\varrho}^{\otimes 2}_{\rm st}|(\mathbb{F}^{AA^{\prime}}\otimes I^{BB^{\prime}}\otimes I)|\bar{\varrho}^{\otimes 2}_{\rm st}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha,\beta}(x_{\alpha}x_{\beta}+y_{\alpha}y_{\beta})h(\alpha,\beta),

(44)

which is equal to the frame potential
F_{t}(\textsf{S}_{n})
due to
Eq.

42
.
Hence, the state frame potential can be obtained as an expectation value by the state
|\bar{\varrho}_{\rm st}^{\otimes 2}\rangle
that is generated by the quantum circuit consisting only of the Hadamard and Toffoli gates.
Below, we denote by
M
the total number of gates in the quantum circuit generating
|\bar{\varrho}_{\rm st}^{\otimes 2}\rangle
and by
h
the number of Hadamard gates in the circuit. The number of Toffoli gates is
M-h
.

Let
\tilde{H}
be a ‘rescaled’ Hadamard gate
\sqrt{2}H
, where
H
is the Hadamard gate. Denoting by
G_{m}
the unitary on
(2(\kappa_{n}+nt)+1)
qubits corresponding to a single
\tilde{H}
or a Toffoli gate in the quantum circuit, we can rewrite
|\bar{\varrho}_{\rm st}^{\otimes 2}\rangle
as follows:

|\bar{\varrho}_{\rm st}^{\otimes 2}\rangle=2^{-h/2}G_{M}G_{M-1}\dots G_{1}|0\rangle^{\otimes 2(\kappa_{n}+nt)+1}.

(45)

By substituting the identity
I=\sum_{\alpha}|\alpha\rangle\langle\alpha|
after each
G_{m}
, where
|\alpha\rangle
are the computational basis states, we obtain

|\bar{\varrho}_{\rm st}^{\otimes 2}\rangle=2^{-h/2}\sum_{\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{M}}\prod_{m=1}^{M}g_{m}(\alpha_{m},\alpha_{m-1})|\alpha_{M}\rangle,

(46)

where
g_{m}(\alpha_{m},\alpha_{m-1})=\langle\alpha_{m}|G_{m}|\alpha_{m-1}\rangle
, and
|\alpha_{0}\rangle=|0\rangle^{\otimes 2(\kappa_{n}+nt)+1}
.
Since
|\bar{\varrho}_{\rm st}^{\otimes 2}\rangle
is a state on
2(\kappa_{n}+nt)+1
qubits,
\alpha_{m}\in\{0,1\}^{2(\kappa_{n}+nt)+1}
.
Below, we use the notation
g(\vec{\alpha})=\prod_{m=1}^{M}g_{m}(\alpha_{m},\alpha_{m-1})
for
\vec{\alpha}=(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{M})
. With this notation, we have

|\bar{\varrho}_{\rm st}^{\otimes 2}\rangle=2^{-h/2}\sum_{\vec{\alpha}}g(\vec{\alpha})|\alpha_{M}\rangle,

(47)

and

F_{t}(\textsf{S}_{n})=2^{-h}\sum_{\vec{\alpha},\vec{\beta}}g(\vec{\alpha},\vec{\beta})\bar{h}(\alpha_{M},\beta_{M}),

(48)

where
g(\vec{\alpha},\vec{\beta})=g(\vec{\alpha})g(\vec{\beta})
, and
\bar{h}(\alpha_{M},\beta_{M})=\langle\beta_{M}|\bigl(\mathbb{F}^{AA^{\prime}}\otimes I^{BB^{\prime}}\otimes I\bigr)|\alpha_{M}\rangle\in\{0,1\}
.

Since
G_{m}
is a unitary corresponding to either single
\tilde{H}
or a single Toffoli gate,

is computable in classical polynomial time. This further implies that
g(\vec{\alpha})
is also computable in classical polynomial time, and so is
g(\vec{\alpha},\vec{\beta})
.
Clearly,
\bar{h}(\alpha_{M},\beta_{M})
is also computable in classical polynomial time.

We now define a Boolean function
f:\{0,1\}^{L+1}\rightarrow\{0,1\}
, where
L=2M(2(\kappa_{n}+nt)+1)
, by

f(\vec{\alpha},\vec{\beta},b)=\begin{cases}1&\text{if $g(\vec{\alpha},\vec{\beta})\bar{h}(\alpha_{M},\beta_{M})\geq b$,}\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

(49)

As both
g(\vec{\alpha},\vec{\beta})
and
\bar{h}(\alpha_{M},\beta_{M})
are computable in classical polynomial time, so is
f(\vec{\alpha},\vec{\beta},b)
.

We claim that

F_{t}(\textsf{S}_{n})=2^{-h}\bigl(s(f)-2^{L}),

(50)

where
s(f)=\bigl|\{(\vec{\alpha},\vec{\beta},b)\in\{0,1\}^{L+1}:f(\vec{\alpha},\vec{\beta},b)=1\}\bigr|
.
This claim implies that the state frame potential can be computed by counting the number
s(f)
of satisfying instances of an efficiently computable Boolean function
f
. As counting
s(f)
is in
{\#{\mathsf{P}}}
, one can compute the state frame potential by a single access to a
{\#{\mathsf{P}}}
oracle.

To show
Eq.

50
, let
s_{\pm}
and
s_{0}
be the number of
(\vec{\alpha},\vec{\beta})
such that

g(\vec{\alpha},\vec{\beta})\bar{h}(\alpha_{M},\beta_{M})=\pm 1,0,

(51)

respectively. As the left-hand side is either
\pm 1
or
0
for any
\vec{\alpha}
and
\vec{\beta}
,
s_{-}+s_{0}+s_{+}=2^{L}
.
It also follows from
Eq.

48
that
F_{t}(\textsf{S}_{n})=2^{-h}(s_{+}-s_{-})
.

By definition, the number of
(\vec{\alpha},\vec{\beta})
such that
f(\vec{\alpha},\vec{\beta},0)=1
and the number of those satisfying
f(\vec{\alpha},\vec{\beta},1)=1
are
s_{+}+s_{0}
and
s_{+}
, respectively. This implies that

. As
s_{-}+s_{0}+s_{+}=2^{L}

For a Boolean function
f

\textsf{S}_{n}=\{|f\rangle,|p\rangle\}\cup\{|\psi_{j}\rangle\}_{j=1}^{K_{n}-2}

\displaystyle|f\rangle=2^{-(n-2)/2}\sum_{x\in\{0,1\}^{n-2}}|x\rangle|f(x)\rangle|0\rangle,

(52)

\displaystyle|p\rangle=|+\rangle^{\otimes n-2}|1\rangle|0\rangle,

(53)

\displaystyle|\psi_{j}\rangle=|\varphi_{j}\rangle|1\rangle.

(54)

Here,
|+\rangle=(|0\rangle+|1\rangle)/\sqrt{2}
, and
\textsf{S}_{\varphi}\coloneqq\{|\varphi_{j}\rangle\}_{j=1}^{K_{n}-2}
is any set of
(n-1)
-qubit state vectors with a computationally efficient description such that

can be computed in classical polynomial time.

The set
\textsf{S}_{n}
constructed in this way also has a computationally efficient description. In fact, one can obtain the description of
\textsf{S}_{n}
by adding one bit to the computationally efficient description of
\textsf{S}_{\varphi}

|f\rangle
or

\textsf{S}_{\varphi}

(55)

-complete problem,

, to
\operatorname{{\mathsf{compSFP}}}
.

\hfill\qed

4
, we have used a set
\textsf{S}_{\varphi}
of
(n-1)
-qubit state vectors with a computationally efficient description, for which we can compute the state frame potential in polynomial time. For completeness, we below provide three instances of such a set.

The first one works when

A more non-trivial choice of

(56)

Let
K\coloneqq K_{n}-2
and assume that

The frame potential

can be computed as

\displaystyle F_{t}(\textsf{S}_{\varphi})

\displaystyle=\cfrac{1}{K^{2}}\sum_{j,k=1}^{K}\left|\langle\varphi_{j}|\varphi_{k}\rangle\right|^{2t}

(57)

\displaystyle=\cfrac{1}{K^{2}}\sum_{j,k=1}^{K}\cos^{2t}{\left(\cfrac{k-j}{K}\pi\right)}

(58)

\displaystyle=\cfrac{1}{K^{2}}\sum_{j=1-K}^{K-1}(K-|j|)\cos^{2t}{\left(\cfrac{j}{K}\pi\right)}

(59)

\displaystyle=\cfrac{2}{K^{2}}\sum_{j=0}^{K-1}(K-j)\cos^{2t}{\left(\cfrac{j}{K}\pi\right)}-\cfrac{1}{K}

\displaystyle 2\sum_{j=0}^{K-1}(K-j)\cos^{2t}{\left(\cfrac{j}{K}\pi\right)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{K-1}(K-j)\cos^{2t}{\left(\cfrac{j}{K}\pi\right)}+\sum_{j=0}^{K}j\cos^{2t}{\left(\cfrac{K-j}{K}\pi\right)}

(62)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{K-1}(K-j)\cos^{2t}{\left(\cfrac{j}{K}\pi\right)}+\sum_{j=0}^{K-1}j\cos^{2t}{\left(\cfrac{j}{K}\pi\right)}+K

(63)

\displaystyle=K\left[1+\sum_{j=0}^{K-1}\cos^{2t}{\left(\cfrac{j}{K}\pi\right)}\right].

(64)

Moreover, we can further simplify

by using Theorem 2.1 in Ref.
[
88
]

\displaystyle F_{t}(\textsf{S}_{\varphi})

\displaystyle=2^{1-2t}\binom{2t-1}{t-1}.

(65)

For any constant
t
,

can be computed in classical constant time.
Note that, by using this set, we can vary the cardinality
K_{n}
without changing the frame potential
F_{t}(\textsf{S}_{\varphi})
.

We may also generalize

|\varphi_{j}\rangle=\left(\cfrac{|0\rangle+e^{2ij\pi/(K_{n}-2)}|1\rangle}{\sqrt{2}}\right)^{\otimes m}\otimes|0^{n-m-1}\rangle

(66)

for any natural number

2^{1-2mt}\binom{2mt-1}{mt-1}.

(67)

This value can be dependent on the number

of qubits if we choose

.

From

3
and

\operatorname{{\mathsf{compSFP}}}\in{\mathsf{FP}}^{{\#{\mathsf{P}}}}
and is
{\#{\mathsf{P}}}
-hard.
Although there is a gap between the result about the inclusion and the hardness, this is merely due to the fact that the answer to
\operatorname{{\mathsf{compSFP}}}
is a real number, while the answer to
{\#{\mathsf{P}}}
problems is a non-negative integer.
One way to close the gap is to restrict a set of state vectors and consider a “rescaled” frame potential.
A possible example is a set
\textsf{S}_{{\mathcal{X}}}
of state vectors on
n
qubits in the form of

|\psi_{j}\rangle=\chi^{-1/2}\sum_{x\in{\mathcal{X}}}(-1)^{a_{jx}}|x\rangle,

where

with cardinality

, and

.
We call this type of state a

. The subset phase states and their relations to a state design have been studied in a general context from the viewpoint of entanglement and thermalization phenomena
[
89
]
.
In recent years, special types of subset phase states have also been of particular interest in quantum cryptography because the state reduces to a phase-random state
[
54
,
90
,
91
]
when
\chi=2^{n}
and each
a_{jx}
is randomly chosen, and to a random subset state
[
92
,
93
]
when

is randomly chosen and
a_{jx}=0
. Both are known to be useful as quantum cryptographic primitives.

For the subset phase states, we can show that computing a rescaled state frame potential is in
{\#{\mathsf{P}}}
.

Theorem 17
.

Let
\textsf{S}_{{\mathcal{X}}}
be a set of
n
-qubit subset phase states with
a_{jx}
being efficiently computable in classical polynomial time, and
K_{n}:=|\textsf{S}_{{\mathcal{X}}}|
be its cardinality. For any constant
t\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
, computing

\frac{\chi^{2t}K_{n}^{2}}{2}\Bigl(1-F_{t}\bigl(\textsf{S}_{{\mathcal{X}}}\bigr)\Bigr)

(69)

is in
{\#{\mathsf{P}}}
. This is the case even if
K_{n}\geq K_{\rm st}(2^{n},t,0)
.

Proof

(
Theorem

17
)
.

The frame potential of degree
t
for
\textsf{S}_{{\mathcal{X}}}
is given by

\displaystyle F_{t}\bigl(\textsf{S}_{{\mathcal{X}}}\bigr)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\chi^{2t}K_{n}^{2}}\sum_{j,k=1}^{K_{n}}\Bigl|\sum_{x\in{\mathcal{X}}}(-1)^{a_{jx}+a_{kx}}\Bigr|^{2t}

(70)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\chi^{2t}K_{n}^{2}}\sum_{j,k=1}^{K_{n}}\sum_{\vec{x}\in{\mathcal{X}}^{2t}}(-1)^{|a_{j\vec{x}}+a_{k\vec{x}}|},

(71)

where
a_{j\vec{x}}=(a_{jx_{1}},\dots,a_{jx_{2t}})
, and
|a_{j\vec{x}}+a_{k\vec{x}}|:=\sum_{m=1}^{2t}(a_{jx_{m}}+a_{kx_{m}})
with all the addition taken as XOR.

For simplicity, we introduce a
(K_{n}\times\chi)
matrix
A=(a_{jx})_{jx}
and define a Boolean function
f_{A}(j,k,\vec{x})
from
\{0,1\}^{2(\log K_{n}+t\log\chi)}
to
\{0,1\}
such as

f_{A}(j,k,\vec{x})=|a_{j\vec{x}}+a_{k\vec{x}}|.

(72)

As we have assumed that
a_{jx}
are efficiently computable in classical polynomial time,
f_{A}(j,k,\vec{x})
is also computable in classical polynomial time. Using
s(f_{A})=\sum_{j,k,\vec{x}}f_{A}(j,k,\vec{x})
, which is the number of inputs satisfying
f_{A}(j,k,\vec{x})=1
, it follows that

\displaystyle F_{t}\bigl(\textsf{S}_{{\mathcal{X}}}\bigr)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\chi^{2t}K_{n}^{2}}\sum_{j,k=1}^{K_{n}}\sum_{\vec{x}\in{\mathcal{X}}^{2t}}(-1)^{f_{A}(j,k,\vec{x})}

(73)

\displaystyle=\frac{\chi^{2t}K_{n}^{2}-2s(f_{A})}{\chi^{2t}K_{n}^{2}},

(74)

which further implies that

s(f_{A})=\frac{\chi^{2t}K_{n}^{2}}{2}\Bigl(1-F_{t}\bigl(\textsf{S}_{{\mathcal{X}}}\bigr)\Bigr).

(75)

Thus, computing
Eq.

69
is in
{\#{\mathsf{P}}}
.

)
.

We denote the length of a bit string
x
by
|x|
.

Let
L=(L_{\rm yes},L_{\rm no})\subseteq\{0,1\}^{\ast}
be a
{\mathsf{BQP}}
-complete problem.
By definition, for
x\in L
, there exists a unitary
U_{x}
corresponding to a polynomial-sized quantum circuit on
m\in{\mathsf{poly}}(|x|)
many qubits such that the probability

q_{1}(x)\coloneqq\langle 0^{m}|U_{x}^{\dagger}(|1\rangle\langle 1|\otimes I^{\otimes m-1})U_{x}|0^{m}\rangle

(80)

of measuring the first qubit in state
|1\rangle
satisfies

1.

if
x\in L_{\rm yes}
, then
q_{1}(x)\geq\cfrac{2}{3}
,

2.

if
x\in L_{\rm no}
, then
q_{1}(x)\leq\cfrac{1}{3}
.

We assume
m\geq 2
without loss of generality.
We define
V_{x}\coloneqq(U_{x}^{\dagger}\otimes H)CZ_{1,m+1}(U_{x}\otimes H)
with
CZ_{1,m+1}
and
H
being the controlled-
Z
gate applied on the first and
(m+1)
th qubits and the Hadamard gate, respectively.
As shown in Ref.
[
94
]
,
q_{1}(x)=|\langle 0^{m}1|V_{x}|0^{m+1}\rangle|
holds.

We consider the set
\{|v_{j}\rangle,|a_{j}\rangle\}_{j=1}^{K}
of state vectors, where

\displaystyle|v_{j}\rangle

\displaystyle\coloneqq

\displaystyle V_{x}|0^{m+1}\rangle\otimes\cfrac{|0\rangle+e^{2ij\pi/K}|1\rangle}{\sqrt{2}},

(81)

\displaystyle|a_{j}\rangle

\displaystyle\coloneqq

\displaystyle|0^{m}\rangle\otimes|1\rangle\otimes\cfrac{|0\rangle+e^{2ij\pi/K}|1\rangle}{\sqrt{2}},

(82)

and
K
is set to be larger than
t
.
Since
\{U_{x}\}_{x}
is a uniform family of polynomial-sized quantum circuits, this is a multiset of state vectors with a computationally efficient description.
For simplicity, let
|\varphi_{j}\rangle\coloneqq(|0\rangle+e^{2ij\pi/K}|1\rangle)/\sqrt{2}
for all
1\leq j\leq K
.

The state frame potential of degree
t
for the above set is

\displaystyle\cfrac{1}{4K^{2}}\sum_{j,k=1}^{K}\left(|\langle v_{j}|v_{k}\rangle|^{2t}+|\langle a_{j}|a_{k}\rangle|^{2t}+2|\langle a_{j}|v_{k}\rangle|^{2t}\right)\ \ \ \

(83)

\displaystyle=\cfrac{1+{q_{1}(x)}^{2t}}{2}\left(\cfrac{1}{K^{2}}\sum_{j,k=1}^{K}|\langle\varphi_{j}|\varphi_{k}\rangle|^{2t}\right)

(84)

\displaystyle=\cfrac{1+{q_{1}(x)}^{2t}}{4^{t}}\binom{2t-1}{t-1},

(85)

where we have used
Eq.

65
to derive the last equation.
From
Eq.

85
, we can solve the
{\mathsf{BQP}}
-complete problem by deciding whether the frame potential is at least

\displaystyle\cfrac{\binom{2t-1}{t-1}}{4^{t}}+\cfrac{\binom{2t-1}{t-1}}{9^{t}}

(86)

or at most

\displaystyle\cfrac{\binom{2t-1}{t-1}}{4^{t}}+\cfrac{\binom{2t-1}{t-1}}{36^{t}}.

(87)

Therefore, the promise gap
\epsilon
is

\displaystyle\left(\cfrac{1}{9^{t}}-\cfrac{1}{36^{t}}\right)\binom{2t-1}{t-1},

(88)

and hence, it is constant.

\hfill\qed

V.2
Complexity of
\operatorname{{\mathsf{SFP}}}
with a promise gap
{\mathcal{O}}(1/{\mathsf{exp}}(n))

See
9

Our proof of
Theorem

9
is based on the complexity class
{\mathsf{PQP}}
, i.e., is a quantum analogue of
{\mathsf{PP}}
.

Proof

(
Theorem

9
)
.

Consider the following algorithm:

1.

With probability
p=2/(2+\alpha+\beta)
, proceed to the next step.
With the remaining probability, output “reject.”

2.

Choose
i
and
j
from
\{1,2,\ldots,K_{n}\}
uniformly at random.

3.

Perform the swap test for
|\psi_{i}\rangle^{\otimes t}
and
|\psi_{j}\rangle^{\otimes t}
, where
|\psi_{i}\rangle,|\psi_{j}\rangle\in\textsf{S}_{n}
.
If the outcome is
0
(
1
), output “accept” (“reject”).

Note that the second step, choosing
i
and
j
, can be done in
{\mathsf{poly}}(n)
time since we consider a set with a computationally efficient description, the cardinality of which is
K_{n}\in{\mathcal{O}}(2^{{\mathsf{poly}}(n)})
.

The acceptance probability of this algorithm is

\cfrac{p}{K_{n}^{2}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{K_{n}}\frac{1+|\langle\psi_{i}|\psi_{j}\rangle|^{2t}}{2}=p\cfrac{1+F_{t}(\textsf{S}_{n})}{2}.

(89)

Therefore, the acceptance probability is either

\geq p\cfrac{1+\alpha}{2}=\cfrac{1}{2}+\cfrac{\alpha-\beta}{2(2+\alpha+\beta)}

(90)

or

\leq p\cfrac{1+\beta}{2}=\cfrac{1}{2}-\cfrac{\alpha-\beta}{2(2+\alpha+\beta)}.

(91)

This means that
\operatorname{{\mathsf{SFP}}}(t,\epsilon)
is in
{\mathsf{PQP}}
if
\alpha-\beta>\epsilon\in\Omega\bigl(2^{-{\mathsf{poly}}(n)}\bigr)
. The proof is completed by the fact that
{\mathsf{PQP}}={\mathsf{PP}}
.
\hfill\qed

This proof also provides an alternative proof of
Theorem

7
, stating that
\operatorname{{\mathsf{SFP}}}(t,\epsilon)\in{\mathsf{BQP}}
when
\epsilon\in\Omega(1/{\rm poly}(n))
.

We next provide the proof of the hardness of
\operatorname{{\mathsf{SFP}}}
for sufficiently small promise gaps.

See
10

To show
Theorem

10
, we provide a polynomial-time reduction from a
{\mathsf{PP}}
-complete problem,
MAJ-SAT
, to
\operatorname{{\mathsf{SFP}}}
.
Below, we prove
Theorem

10
by explicitly embedding the quantity
s(f)
in
MAJ-SAT
to the state frame potential
F_{t}(\textsf{S}_{n})
.

Proof

(
Theorem

10
)
.

Let
f:\{0,1\}^{n-2}\rightarrow\{0,1\}
be a Boolean function that is computable in classical polynomial time. Consider the same set
\textsf{S}_{n}
defined by
Eqs.

52
,
53
and
54
, which has a computationally efficient description.
As in
Eq.

55
, the state frame potential
F_{t}
of degree
t
for
\textsf{S}_{n}
is given by

F_{t}(\textsf{S}_{n})=\biggl(1-\frac{2}{K_{n}}\biggr)^{2}F_{t}\bigl(\textsf{S}_{\varphi}\bigr)+\frac{2}{K_{n}^{2}}\biggl[1+\biggl(\frac{s(f)}{2^{n-2}}\biggr)^{2t}\biggr].

(92)

We set
\alpha
and
\beta
as

\displaystyle\alpha

\displaystyle\coloneqq\biggl(1-\frac{2}{K_{n}}\biggr)^{2}F_{t}\bigl(\textsf{S}_{\varphi}\bigr)+\frac{2}{K_{n}^{2}}\biggl[1+2^{-2t}\biggr],

(93)

\displaystyle\beta

\displaystyle\coloneqq\biggl(1-\frac{2}{K_{n}}\biggr)^{2}F_{t}\bigl(\textsf{S}_{\varphi}\bigr)+\frac{2}{K_{n}^{2}}\biggl[1+\Bigl(\frac{2^{n-3}-1}{2^{n-2}}\Bigr)^{2t}\biggr].

(94)

It suffices for solving
MAJ-SAT
to determine either
F_{t}(\textsf{S}_{n})\geq\alpha
or
\leq\beta
. As
MAJ-SAT
is
{\mathsf{PP}}
-complete (
Theorem

1
),
\operatorname{{\mathsf{SFP}}}(t,\alpha-\beta)
is
{\mathsf{PP}}
-hard.

The promise gap, i.e.,
\alpha-\beta
, is evaluated as

\displaystyle\alpha-\beta

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{2t-1}K_{n}^{2}}\left[1-\left(1-\frac{1}{2^{n-3}}\right)^{2t}\right]

(95)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{t}{2^{2t-1}K_{n}^{2}(2^{n-4}+t)},

(96)

where we have used
(1-\epsilon)^{m}\leq(1+m\epsilon)^{-1}
.
\hfill\qed

V.3
Complexity of
\operatorname{{\mathsf{UFP}}}

In a similar way to
\operatorname{{\mathsf{SFP}}}
, we can clarify the computational complexity class of
\operatorname{{\mathsf{UFP}}}
.

See
11

Proof

(
Theorem

11
)
.

Let
\alpha^{\prime}\coloneqq\alpha/2^{2tn}
and
\beta^{\prime}\coloneqq\beta/2^{2tn}
.
We run the following algorithm:

1.

With probability
p=2/(2+\alpha^{\prime}+\beta^{\prime})
, proceed to the next step.
With the remaining probability, output “reject.”

2.

Select
i
and
j
from
\{1,2,\ldots,K_{n}\}
uniformly at random.

3.

Let
|\Phi\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2^{n}}}\sum_{k=0}^{2^{n}-1}|kk\rangle
be a maximally entangled state. Perform the swap test for

|\Psi_{i}\rangle\coloneqq\bigl(\left(I^{\otimes n}\otimes U_{i}\right)|\Phi\rangle\bigr)^{\otimes t}

(97)

and

|\Psi_{j}\rangle\coloneqq\bigl(\left(I^{\otimes n}\otimes U_{j}\right)|\Phi\rangle\bigr)^{\otimes t},

(98)

where
U_{i},U_{j}\in\textsf{U}_{n}
.
If the outcome is
0
(
1
), output “accept” (“reject”).

The acceptance probability of this algorithm is

\frac{p}{K_{n}^{2}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{K_{n}}\cfrac{1+|\langle\Psi_{i}|\Psi_{j}\rangle|^{2}}{2}=p\cfrac{1+F_{t}(\textsf{U}_{n})/2^{2tn}}{2},

(99)

which is either

\geq p\cfrac{1+\alpha^{\prime}}{2}=\cfrac{1}{2}+\cfrac{\alpha^{\prime}-\beta^{\prime}}{2(2+\alpha^{\prime}+\beta^{\prime})}

(100)

or

\leq p\cfrac{1+\beta^{\prime}}{2}=\cfrac{1}{2}-\cfrac{\alpha^{\prime}-\beta^{\prime}}{2(2+\alpha^{\prime}+\beta^{\prime})}.

(101)

This implies that
\operatorname{{\mathsf{UFP}}}(t,\epsilon)
is in
{\mathsf{PQP}}
. The fact that
{\mathsf{PQP}}={\mathsf{PP}}
completes the proof.

\hfill\qed

Unlike the case of
\operatorname{{\mathsf{SFP}}}(t,\epsilon)
, this proof does not imply that
\operatorname{{\mathsf{UFP}}}(t,\epsilon)
is in
{\mathsf{BQP}}
even if
\epsilon\in\Omega(1/{\rm poly}(n))
. This is due to the factor
2^{-2tn}
in
\alpha^{\prime}
and
\beta^{\prime}
.

See
12

Proof

(
Theorem

12
)
.

We provide a polynomial-time reduction from a
{\mathsf{PP}}
-complete problem,
MAJ-SAT
, to
\operatorname{{\mathsf{UFP}}}(t,\epsilon)
.
Let
f:\{0,1\}^{n-2}\rightarrow\{0,1\}
be a Boolean function that is computable in classical polynomial time. Consider the same set
\textsf{U}_{n}
defined by
Eqs.

76
,
77
and
78
, of unitaries on
n
qubits, which has a computationally efficient description.
As in
Eq.

79
, the unitary frame potential for
\textsf{U}_{n}
is

F_{t}(\textsf{U}_{n})=2^{2t}\biggl(1-\frac{2}{K_{n}}\biggr)^{2}F_{t}(\textsf{V}_{n-1})\\
+\frac{2}{K_{n}^{2}}\bigl[2^{2nt}+g_{t}\bigl(s(f)\bigr)\bigr],

(102)

where
g_{t}(x)=\bigl(2^{n}-8+2^{-2n+7}x^{2}\bigr)^{2t}
.

We set
\alpha
and
\beta
such as

\displaystyle\alpha\coloneqq 2^{2t}\biggl(1-\frac{2}{K_{n}}\biggr)^{2}F_{t}(\textsf{V}_{n-1})+\frac{2}{K_{n}^{2}}\bigl[2^{2nt}+g_{t}\bigl(2^{n-3}\bigr)\bigr],

(103)

\displaystyle\beta\coloneqq 2^{2t}\biggl(1-\frac{2}{K_{n}}\biggr)^{2}F_{t}(\textsf{V}_{n-1})+\frac{2}{K_{n}^{2}}\bigl[2^{2nt}+g_{t}\bigl(2^{n-3}-1\bigr)\bigr].

(104)

If one can determine whether
F_{t}(\textsf{U}_{n})\geq\alpha
or
\leq\beta

M<2^{n-3}
or

(105)

This concludes the proof.

VI.1
Complexity of
\operatorname{{\mathsf{StDes}}}

The fact that
\operatorname{{\mathsf{StDes}}}
is in
{\mathsf{PP}}
if
\delta^{\prime}
and
\delta
satisfy a certain condition directly follows from
Theorem

9
, i.e., from the statement that
\operatorname{{\mathsf{SFP}}}(t,\epsilon)\in{\mathsf{PP}}
if
\epsilon
is not too small.

See
13

Proof

(
Theorem

13
)
.

Due to the promise of the problem, it is guaranteed that a given set
\textsf{S}_{n}
is either a
\delta
-approximate or not a
\delta^{\prime}
-approximate state
t
-design. If the former is the case,
F_{t}(\textsf{S}_{n})\leq 1/d_{t}+\delta^{2}/d_{t}
, and, if the latter is the case,
F_{t}(\textsf{S}_{n})>1/d_{t}+\delta^{\prime 2}/d_{t}^{2}
. Since
(1/d_{t}+\delta^{\prime 2}/d_{t}^{2})-(1/d_{t}+\delta^{2}/d_{t})\in\Omega\bigl(2^{-{\mathsf{poly}}(n)}\bigr)
by assumption, checking which is the case is in
{\mathsf{PP}}
due to
Theorem

9
. Hence,
\operatorname{{\mathsf{StDes}}}(t,t,\delta,\delta^{\prime})\in{\mathsf{PP}}
.
\hfill\qed

We next show the hardness of
\operatorname{{\mathsf{StDes}}}
. The following is the restatement of
Theorem

14
.

See
14

Proof

(
Theorem

14
)
.

In the proof, we use the notation
d=2^{n}
for simplicity.
Let
f:\{0,1\}^{n-1}\rightarrow\{0,1\}
be a Boolean function that is computable in classical polynomial time, and let
|f\rangle
and
|p\rangle
be
n
-qubit state vectors given by, respectively,

\displaystyle|f\rangle=\sqrt{\frac{2}{d}}\sum_{x\in\{0,1\}^{n-1}}|x\rangle|f(x)\rangle,

(106)

\displaystyle|p\rangle=|+\rangle^{\otimes n-1}|1\rangle.

(107)

Let
\delta_{0}
be such that
\delta_{0}\in o(\delta/d)
, and
\textsf{D}_{t^{\prime}}
be a set of
n
-qubit state vectors with a computationally efficient description that forms an exact state
1
-design and a
\delta_{0}
-approximate state
t^{\prime}
-design. Such designs exist as
\delta\in\Omega(2^{-{\mathsf{poly}}(n)})
. For instance, it can be generated by first applying Pauli operators uniformly at random to a fixed state and then applying a local random circuit with
{\mathsf{poly}}(n)
depth. The first application of Pauli operators guarantees that the generated states form an exact state
1
-design.
We denote by
D
the cardinality of
\textsf{D}_{t^{\prime}}
.

We define a multiset
\textsf{S}_{n}(f)
of state vectors by

\textsf{S}_{n}(f)=\{|f\rangle,|p\rangle\}^{\oplus m}\cup\textsf{D}_{t^{\prime}},

(108)

where
\oplus m
implies the multiplicity
m\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
.
A key quantity in the proof is
D/m
, which we will later choose appropriately depending on
\delta
. Here, we note that
D/m
can be an arbitrary positive rational number because we can choose arbitrarily large positive integers
m
and
D
. Note that this is the case for
D
since, given a
\delta_{0}
-approximate state
t^{\prime}
-design with some cardinality, one can increase its cardinality by changing the multiplicity of each state vector. The resulting multiset is a
\delta_{0}
-approximate state
t^{\prime}
-design with cardinality that is an integer multiple of the original one.

For
\tau\in\{1,t,t^{\prime}\}
, the set
\textsf{S}_{n}(f)
is a
\delta_{\tau}(f)
-approximate state
\tau
-design, where

\delta_{\tau}(f)\coloneqq d_{\tau}\left\|\Delta^{(\tau)}_{\textsf{S}_{n}(f)}\right\|_{\infty}

(109)

with

\Delta^{(\tau)}_{\textsf{S}_{n}(f)}\coloneqq\frac{\Pi_{\textrm{sym}}^{(\tau)}}{d_{\tau}}-\mathbb{E}_{|\varphi\rangle\sim\textsf{S}_{n}(f)}\bigl[|\varphi\rangle\langle\varphi|^{\otimes\tau}\bigr].

(110)

Here,
\Pi_{\textrm{sym}}^{(\tau)}
is the projection onto the symmetric subspace
{\mathcal{H}}^{(\tau)}_{\rm sym}
,
d_{\tau}=\mathrm{Tr}\bigl[\Pi_{\textrm{sym}}^{(\tau)}\bigr]
, and

\mathbb{E}_{|\varphi\rangle\sim\textsf{S}_{n}(f)}\bigl[|\varphi\rangle\langle\varphi|^{\otimes\tau}\bigr]\coloneqq\\
\frac{1}{2m+D}\Bigl[m\bigl(|f\rangle\langle f|^{\otimes\tau}+|p\rangle\langle p|^{\otimes\tau}\bigr)+D\,\mathbb{E}_{|\varphi\rangle\sim\textsf{D}_{t^{\prime}}}\bigl[|\varphi\rangle\langle\varphi|^{\otimes\tau}\bigr]\Bigr].

(111)

Below, we compute
\delta_{\tau}(f)
.

We begin by checking the eigenvalues of
|f\rangle\langle f|^{\otimes\tau}+|p\rangle\langle p|^{\otimes\tau}
. By expanding
|f\rangle^{\otimes\tau}
as
\alpha_{\tau}|p\rangle^{\otimes\tau}+\sqrt{1-\alpha_{\tau}^{2}}|q\rangle
, where

\alpha_{\tau}=\langle p|f\rangle^{\tau}=\left(\frac{2s(f)}{d}\right)^{\tau},

(112)

with
s(f)=\sum_{x\in\{0,1\}^{n-1}}f(x)
, and
|q\rangle
orthogonal to
|p\rangle^{\otimes\tau}
, we have

|f\rangle\langle f|^{\otimes\tau}+|p\rangle\langle p|^{\otimes\tau}=\begin{pmatrix}1+\alpha_{\tau}^{2}&\alpha_{\tau}\sqrt{1-\alpha_{\tau}^{2}}\\
\alpha_{\tau}\sqrt{1-\alpha_{\tau}^{2}}&1-\alpha_{\tau}^{2}\end{pmatrix},

(113)

where the matrix representation is in terms of
\{|p\rangle^{\otimes\tau},|q\rangle\}
.
Hence, the eigenvalues are
1\pm\alpha_{\tau}
. Below, we denote the corresponding eigenvectors by
|\alpha_{\tau,\pm}\rangle
.

When
\tau=1
, we can use the fact that
\textsf{D}_{t^{\prime}}
is an exact state
1
-design. That is,

\mathbb{E}_{|\varphi\rangle\sim\textsf{D}_{t^{\prime}}}\bigl[|\varphi\rangle\langle\varphi|\bigr]=\frac{I}{d}.

(114)

Also,
\Pi_{\textrm{sym}}^{(1)}=I
and
d_{1}=d
. Hence, it follows that

\displaystyle\delta_{1}(f)

\displaystyle=\frac{m}{2m+D}\left\|2I-d\left(|f\rangle\langle f|+|p\rangle\langle p|\right)\right\|_{\infty}

(115)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{D/m+2}\left(d+2s(f)-2\right),

(116)

where we have used that the eigenvalues of
|f\rangle\langle f|+|p\rangle\langle p|
are
1\pm\alpha_{1}
, which is
1\pm 2s(f)/d
.

When
\tau>1
, we derive an upper and a lower bound on
\delta_{\tau}(f)
.
Using the eigenvectors
|\alpha_{\tau,\pm}\rangle
, a lower bound on
\delta_{\tau}(f)
is given as

d_{\tau}\max\Bigl\{\bigl|\langle\alpha_{\tau,+}|\Delta^{(\tau)}_{\textsf{S}_{n}(f)}|\alpha_{\tau,+}\rangle\bigr|,\bigl|\langle\alpha_{\tau,-}|\Delta^{(\tau)}_{\textsf{S}_{n}(f)}|\alpha_{\tau,-}\rangle\bigr|\Bigr\}\\
\leq\delta_{\tau}(f),

(117)

which turns out to be

d_{\tau}|\langle\alpha_{\tau,+}|\Delta^{(\tau)}_{\textsf{S}_{n}(f)}|\alpha_{\tau,+}\rangle|\leq\delta_{\tau}(f).

(118)

As
|\alpha_{\tau,+}\rangle\in{\mathcal{H}}^{(\tau)}_{\rm sym}
, we have

\langle\alpha_{\tau,+}|\Delta^{(\tau)}_{\textsf{S}_{n}(f)}|\alpha_{\tau,+}\rangle=\frac{1}{d_{\tau}}-\frac{1}{2m+D}\biggl(m(1+\alpha_{\tau})+\\
D\langle\alpha_{\tau,+}|\mathbb{E}_{|\varphi\rangle\sim\textsf{D}_{t^{\prime}}}\bigl[|\varphi\rangle\langle\varphi|^{\otimes\tau}\bigr]|\alpha_{\tau,+}\rangle\biggr).

(119)

Hence,

\delta_{\tau}(f)\geq\biggl|1-\frac{d_{\tau}}{2m+D}\biggl(m(1+\alpha_{\tau})+\\
D\langle\alpha_{\tau,+}|\mathbb{E}_{|\varphi\rangle\sim\textsf{D}_{t^{\prime}}}\bigl[|\varphi\rangle\langle\varphi|^{\otimes\tau}\bigr]|\alpha_{\tau,+}\rangle\biggr)\biggr|.

(120)

Moreover, as
\tau\in\{1,t,t^{\prime}\}
and
t\leq t^{\prime}
,
\textsf{D}_{t^{\prime}}
is a
\delta_{0}
-approximate state
\tau
-design. This implies that

\frac{1-\delta_{0}}{d_{\tau}}\leq\langle\alpha_{\tau,+}|\mathbb{E}_{|\varphi\rangle\sim\textsf{D}_{t^{\prime}}}\bigl[|\varphi\rangle\langle\varphi|^{\otimes\tau}\bigr]|\alpha_{\tau,+}\rangle\leq\frac{1+\delta_{0}}{d_{\tau}}.

(121)

From
Eqs.

120
and
121
, we obtain a lower bound on
\delta_{\tau}(f)
, which reads as

\frac{1}{D/m+2}\left[(1+\alpha_{\tau})d_{\tau}-2-\frac{D}{m}\delta_{0}\right]\leq\delta_{\tau}(f).

(122)

Remember that
\alpha_{\tau}=(2s(f)/d)^{\tau}
.
For the later convenience, we introduce a real-valued function
\textrm{LB}_{\tau}(q,x)
as

\textrm{LB}_{\tau}(q,x)\coloneqq\frac{1}{q+2}\left\{\left[1+\Bigl(\frac{2x}{d}\Bigr)^{\tau}\right]d_{\tau}-2-q\delta_{0}\right\}.

(123)

Using this notation, we can rephrase the lower bound as
\textrm{LB}_{\tau}\bigl(D/m,s(f)\bigr)\leq\delta_{\tau}(f)
.

It is easy to obtain an upper bound on
\delta_{\tau}(f)
. From the triangle inequality, we obtain

\delta_{\tau}(f)\leq d_{\tau}\Bigl\|\frac{\Pi_{\textrm{sym}}^{(\tau)}}{d_{\tau}}-\frac{D}{2m+D}\mathbb{E}_{|\varphi\rangle\sim\textsf{D}_{t^{\prime}}}\bigl[|\varphi\rangle\langle\varphi|^{\otimes\tau}\bigr]\Bigr\|_{\infty}\\
+\frac{md_{\tau}}{2m+D}\Bigl\||f\rangle\langle f|^{\otimes\tau}+|p\rangle\langle p|^{\otimes\tau}\Bigr\|_{\infty}.

(124)

As
\textsf{D}_{t^{\prime}}
is a
\delta_{0}
-approximate state
\tau
-design, it holds that

\Bigl\|\frac{\Pi_{\textrm{sym}}^{(\tau)}}{d_{\tau}}-\frac{D}{2m+D}\mathbb{E}_{|\varphi\rangle\sim\textsf{D}_{t^{\prime}}}\bigl[|\varphi\rangle\langle\varphi|^{\otimes\tau}\bigr]\Bigr\|_{\infty}\\
\leq\Bigl(1-\frac{D}{2m+D}\Bigr)\frac{1}{d_{\tau}}+\frac{D}{2m+D}\frac{\delta_{0}}{d_{\tau}}.

(125)

We also have

\Bigl\||f\rangle\langle f|^{\otimes\tau}+|p\rangle\langle p|^{\otimes\tau}\Bigr\|_{\infty}=1+\alpha_{\tau},

(126)

from the diagonalization of
|f\rangle\langle f|^{\otimes\tau}+|p\rangle\langle p|^{\otimes\tau}
. Hence, we obtain

\delta_{\tau}(f)\leq\frac{1}{D/m+2}\left[(1+\alpha_{\tau})d_{\tau}+2+\frac{D}{m}\delta_{0}\right].

(127)

Similarly to the lower bound, we introduce a real-valued function
\textrm{UB}_{\tau}(q,x)
as

\textrm{UB}_{\tau}(q,x)\coloneqq\frac{1}{q+2}\left\{\left[1+\Bigl(\frac{2x}{d}\Bigr)^{\tau}\right]d_{\tau}+2+q\delta_{0}\right\},

(128)

and write the upper bound as
\delta_{\tau}(f)\leq\textrm{UB}_{\tau}\bigl(D/m,s(f)\bigr)
.

To summarize so far, we have obtained

\delta_{1}(f)=\frac{d+2s(f)-2}{D/m+2},

(129)

and for
\tau>1
, a lower and an upper bound on
\delta_{\tau}(f)
:

\textrm{LB}_{\tau}\left(\frac{D}{m},s(f)\right)\leq\delta_{\tau}(f)\leq\textrm{UB}_{\tau}\left(\frac{D}{m},s(f)\right).

(130)

Importantly, for any fixed
q\geq 0
, both
\textrm{LB}_{\tau}(q,x)
and
\textrm{LB}_{\tau}(q,x)
are monotonically increasing in
x
when
x\geq 0
.

We now choose
D/m
.
For our purpose, we specifically use
D/m
that approximately satisfies the following: if
t=1

\frac{d+2s_{0}-2}{D/m+2}=\delta,

(131)

with
s_{0}=d/4-2/3
, and if
t>1
,

\textrm{LB}_{t}\left(\frac{D}{m},\frac{d}{4}-\frac{2}{3}\right)=\delta.

(132)

As
\textrm{LB}_{t}
is defined as
Eq.

123
, the latter equation is explicitly given by

\frac{1}{D/m+2}\biggl[\Bigl\{1+\Bigl(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{4}{3d}\Bigr)^{t}\Bigr\}d_{t}-2\biggr]-\frac{D/m}{D/m+2}\delta_{0}=\delta.

(133)

As
D/m
can be an arbitrary positive rational number by choosing the appropriate cardinality
D
of
\textsf{D}_{t^{\prime}}
and multiplicity
m
of
\{|f\rangle,|p\rangle\}
in
\textsf{S}_{n}(f)
, the solutions of these equations can be approximated by
D/m
with arbitrary precision.

With this choice of
D/m
, we set
\delta^{\prime}
as follows. If
t^{\prime}=1
,

\delta^{\prime}=\frac{d+2s_{1}-2}{D/m+2},

(134)

with
s_{1}=d/4-1/3
, and if
t^{\prime}>1
,

\displaystyle\delta^{\prime}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\textrm{UB}_{t^{\prime}}\left(\frac{D}{m},\frac{d}{4}-\frac{1}{3}\right)

(135)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{D/m+2}\biggl[\Bigl\{1+\Bigl(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{2}{3d}\Bigr)^{t^{\prime}}\Bigr\}d_{t^{\prime}}+2\biggr]+\frac{D/m}{D/m+2}\delta_{0}.

(136)

From simple calculations and using
\delta_{0}\in o(\delta/d)
, it follows that

\delta^{\prime}\in\Theta(d^{t^{\prime}-t}\delta).

(137)

Clearly,
\delta^{\prime}-\delta\in\Theta(\delta^{\prime})=\Theta(d^{t^{\prime}-t}\delta)
for
t<t^{\prime}
. When
t=t^{\prime}
, it is also straightforward to see that
\delta^{\prime}-\delta\in\Theta(\delta/d)
.

Below, we show that
MAJ-SAT
can be solved by a single use of the algorithm
{\mathcal{A}}
for solving
\operatorname{{\mathsf{StDes}}}(t,t^{\prime},\delta,\delta^{\prime})
. By definition,
the algorithm
{\mathcal{A}}
can determine whether
\textsf{S}_{n}(f)
satisfies

\delta_{t^{\prime}}(f)>\delta^{\prime},\ \ \textrm{or}\ \ \delta_{t}(f)\leq\delta,

(138)

under the exclusive assumption that only one of the two holds.

We begin by checking that, for any Boolean function
f:\{0,1\}^{n-1}\rightarrow\{0,1\}
, the corresponding set
\textsf{S}_{n}(f)
must exclusively satisfy one of the two conditions in
Eq.

138
.
We check this condition separately for three cases. The first case is
t=t^{\prime}=1
, the second case is
t=1<t^{\prime}
, and the last case is
1<t\leq t^{\prime}
.

In the first case, in which
t=t^{\prime}=1
, we observe from
Eqs.

129
,
131
and
134
that

\displaystyle\delta_{1}(f)\leq\delta

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow s(f)\leq s_{0}=\frac{d}{4}-\frac{2}{3},

(139)

\displaystyle\delta_{1}(f)>\delta^{\prime}

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\frac{d}{4}-\frac{1}{3}=s_{1}<s(f).

(140)

As
s(f)
is a non-negative integer, these cases are equivalent to

\displaystyle\delta_{1}(f)\leq\delta

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow s(f)\leq\frac{d}{4}-1,

(141)

\displaystyle\delta_{1}(f)>\delta^{\prime}

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\frac{d}{4}\leq s(f).

(142)

It is clear that all Boolean functions must exclusively satisfy one of the two.

In the second case, in which
t=1<t^{\prime}
, as shown above,
\delta_{1}(f)\leq\delta
is equivalent to
s(f)\leq d/4-1
.
On the other hand, if
\delta_{t^{\prime}}(f)>\delta^{\prime}
, it follows from
Eqs.

135
and
130
that

\textrm{UB}_{t^{\prime}}\left(\frac{D}{m},\frac{d}{4}-\frac{1}{3}\right)=\delta^{\prime}<\delta_{t^{\prime}}(f)\leq\textrm{UB}_{t^{\prime}}\left(\frac{D}{m},s(f)\right).

(143)

As
\textrm{UB}_{t^{\prime}}(q,x)
is monotonically increasing in
x
for any fixed
q\geq 0
, this implies that
s(f)>d/4-1/3
. As
s(f)
is a non-negative integer, we conclude that
\delta_{t^{\prime}}(f)>\delta^{\prime}
implies that
s(f)\geq d/4
.
We also show that, if
s(f)\geq d/4
, then
\delta_{t^{\prime}}(f)>\delta^{\prime}
.
That is,
\delta_{t^{\prime}}(f)>\delta^{\prime}
is equivalent to
s(f)\geq d/4
.
We show the contraposition of this statement. To this end, it is crucial to note that

\textrm{UB}_{t^{\prime}}\left(\frac{D}{m},\frac{d}{4}-\frac{1}{3}\right)<\textrm{LB}_{t^{\prime}}\left(\frac{D}{m},\frac{d}{4}\right),

(144)

which is obtained by the direct calculation using
1<t^{\prime}
, and the fact that
\textsf{D}_{t^{\prime}}
is a
\delta_{0}
-approximate state
t^{\prime}
-design with
\delta_{0}\in o(\delta/d)
. As
\delta^{\prime}
for
t^{\prime}>1
is defined as
Eq.

135
, this implies that

\delta^{\prime}<\textrm{LB}_{t^{\prime}}\left(\frac{D}{m},\frac{d}{4}\right).

(145)

Thus, if
\delta_{t^{\prime}}(f)\leq\delta^{\prime}
, we obtain, by using
Eq.

130
,

\textrm{LB}_{t^{\prime}}\left(\frac{D}{m},s(f)\right)\leq\delta_{t^{\prime}}(f)\leq\delta^{\prime}<\textrm{LB}_{t^{\prime}}\left(\frac{D}{m},\frac{d}{4}\right).

(146)

Using the monotonicity of
\textrm{LB}_{t^{\prime}}(q,x)
, this reduces to
s(f)<d/4
. That is,
\delta_{t^{\prime}}(f)\leq\delta^{\prime}
implies that
s(f)<d/4
. Taking the contraposition of this, we obtain that
s(f)\geq d/4
implies that
\delta_{t^{\prime}}(f)>\delta^{\prime}
.

All together, in the case of
t=1<t^{\prime}
, we have

\displaystyle\delta_{1}(f)\leq\delta

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow s(f)\leq\frac{d}{4}-1,

(147)

\displaystyle\delta_{t^{\prime}}(f)>\delta^{\prime}

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\frac{d}{4}\leq s(f).

(148)

Clearly, all Boolean functions must exclusively satisfy one of the two.

Finally, when
1<t\leq t^{\prime}
, we begin by rephrasing
\delta_{t}(f)\leq\delta
in terms of
s(f)
.
If
\delta_{t}(f)\leq\delta
, we have from
Eqs.

130
and
132

\textrm{LB}_{t}\left(\frac{D}{m},s(f)\right)\leq\delta_{t}(f)\leq\delta=\textrm{LB}_{t}\left(\frac{D}{m},\frac{d}{4}-\frac{2}{3}\right).

(149)

As
\textrm{LB}_{t}(q,x)
is monotonically increasing in
x
for any fixed

, together with the fact that
s(f)
is a non-negative integer, this implies that
s(f)\leq d/4-1
.
We can also show that

implies that
\delta_{t}(f)\leq\delta
. To this end, we consider the contraposition. The crucial observation is

\displaystyle\textrm{UB}_{t}\left(\frac{D}{m},\frac{d}{4}-1\right)\leq\textrm{LB}_{t}\left(\frac{D}{m},\frac{d}{4}-\frac{2}{3}\right),

(150)

which can be shown using
1<t
and the fact that
\textsf{D}_{t^{\prime}}
is a
\delta_{0}
-approximate state
t
-design with
\delta_{0}\in o(\delta/d)
. Note that
t\leq t^{\prime}
.
From
Eq.

132
, this implies that

\displaystyle\textrm{UB}_{t}\left(\frac{D}{m},\frac{d}{4}-1\right)\leq\delta.

(151)

Hence, if
\delta_{t}(f)>\delta
, then

\displaystyle\textrm{UB}_{t}\left(\frac{D}{m},\frac{d}{4}-1\right)\leq\delta<\delta_{t}(f)\leq\textrm{UB}_{t}\left(\frac{D}{m},s(f)\right),

(152)

where we have used
Eq.

130
. Using the monotonicity of
\textrm{UB}_{t}(q,x)
, this further implies that
d/4-1<s(f)
. By taking the contraposition, we obtain that
d/4-1\geq s(f)
implies that
\delta_{t}(f)\leq\delta
.

Therefore, when
1<t\leq t^{\prime}
, we have

\displaystyle\delta_{t}(f)\leq\delta

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow s(f)\leq\frac{d}{4}-1,

(153)

\displaystyle\delta_{t^{\prime}}(f)>\delta^{\prime}

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\frac{d}{4}\leq s(f).

(154)

Note that the latter follows from
Eq.

148
.
Thus, also in the case of
1<t\leq t^{\prime}
, all Boolean functions must exclusively satisfy one of the two conditions in
Eq.

138
.

We finally show that the algorithm
{\mathcal{A}}
for solving
\operatorname{{\mathsf{StDes}}}(t,t^{\prime},\delta,\delta^{\prime})
can be used to solve
MAJ-SAT
.
We simply apply the algorithm
{\mathcal{A}}
to the set
\textsf{S}_{n}(f)
constructed from a Boolean function
f
. As we have shown,
\textsf{S}_{n}(f)
satisfies the exclusive promise of
\operatorname{{\mathsf{StDes}}}(t,t^{\prime},\delta,\delta^{\prime})
and, hence, the algorithm
{\mathcal{A}}
can determine which of the following two is the case:

\delta_{t^{\prime}}(f)>\delta^{\prime},\ \ \textrm{or}\ \ \delta_{t}(f)\leq\delta.

(155)

From
Eqs.

141
,
142
,
147
,
148
,
153
and
154
, it is clear that, for any

, the algorithm
{\mathcal{A}}
can decide

s(f)\geq\frac{d}{4}=2^{n-2},\ \ \textrm{or}\ \ s(f)\leq\frac{d}{4}-1=2^{n-2}-1.

(156)

Hence, one can efficiently solve
MAJ-SAT
with one query to
{\mathcal{A}}
.

Below is a restatement of
Theorem

16
for clarity.

See

Proof

(
Theorem

16
)
.

The proof is almost in parallel with that of
\operatorname{{\mathsf{StDes}}}
, though we make use of unitaries instead of state vectors.
Let
f:\{0,1\}^{n-1}\rightarrow\{0,1\}
be a Boolean function that is computable in classical polynomial time, and let
|f\rangle
and
|p\rangle
be
n
-qubit state vectors given by, respectively,

\displaystyle|f\rangle=\sqrt{\frac{2}{d}}\sum_{x\in\{0,1\}^{n-1}}|x\rangle|f(x)\rangle,

(157)

\displaystyle|p\rangle=|+\rangle^{\otimes n-1}|1\rangle.

(158)

Using these states, we define unitaries as

V_{f}=I-2|f\rangle\langle f|,\ \ \text{and}\ \ V_{p}=I-2|p\rangle\langle p|.

(159)

Let
\delta_{0}
be such that
\delta_{0}\in O(\delta^{2}/d^{2t})
, and let
\textsf{D}_{t^{\prime}}
be a
\delta_{0}
-approximate unitary
t^{\prime}
-design of
n
qubits with a computationally efficient description, which can be generated by, e.g., local random circuits with
{\mathsf{poly}}(n)
depth.
We denote by
D
the cardinality of
\textsf{D}_{t^{\prime}}
.

We define a multiset
\textsf{U}_{n}(f)
of unitaries by

\textsf{U}_{n}(f)=\{V_{f},V_{p}\}^{\oplus m}\cup\textsf{D}_{t^{\prime}},

(160)

where
\oplus m
implies the multiplicity
m\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
.
Later, we determine
D
and
m
depending on
\delta
such that
D/m
is appropriately set for our purpose. Note that
D/m
can be an arbitrary positive rational number.

Let
\tau\in\{t,t^{\prime}\}
. The set
\textsf{U}_{n}(f)
is a
\delta_{\tau}(f)
-approximate unitary
\tau
-design, where

\delta_{\tau}(f)\coloneqq\left\|M^{(\tau)}_{\textsf{U}_{n}(f)}-M^{(\tau)}_{\textsf{H}}\right\|_{1}.

(161)

Here,
M^{(\tau)}_{\mu}=\mathbb{E}_{U\sim\mu}[U^{\otimes\tau}\otimes\bar{U}^{\otimes\tau}]
is the moment operator.
Below, we compute a lower and an upper bound on
\delta_{\tau}(f)
.

The moment operator of the Haar measure is a projector onto the irreducible representation of
\pi_{\tau}:U\mapsto U^{\otimes\tau}\otimes\bar{U}^{\otimes\tau}
. Denoting the projector by
\Pi_{\textrm{irrep}}^{(\tau)}
, the moment operator of the uniform measure on
\textsf{U}_{n}(f)
is in the form of

\displaystyle M^{(\tau)}_{\textsf{U}_{n}(f)}

\displaystyle=\Pi_{\textrm{irrep}}^{(\tau)}+\Pi_{\textrm{irrep}}^{(\tau)\perp}M^{(\tau)}_{\textsf{U}_{n}(f)}\Pi_{\textrm{irrep}}^{(\tau)\perp},

(162)

where
\Pi_{\textrm{irrep}}^{(\tau)\perp}=I-\Pi_{\textrm{irrep}}^{(\tau)}
. Note that we have used the irreducibility.
Hence,

\delta_{\tau}(f)=\left\|\Pi_{\textrm{irrep}}^{(\tau)\perp}M^{(\tau)}_{\textsf{U}_{n}(f)}\Pi_{\textrm{irrep}}^{(\tau)\perp}\right\|_{1},

(163)

with

\displaystyle M^{(\tau)}_{\textsf{U}_{n}(f)}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{U\sim\textsf{U}_{n}(f)}\left[\pi_{\tau}(U)\right]

(164)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{D+2m}\left[m\left(\pi_{\tau}(V_{f})+\pi_{\tau}(V_{p})\right)+DM_{\textsf{D}_{t^{\prime}}}^{(\tau)}\right].

(165)

From the triangle inequality, we have that

\frac{1}{D+2m}\left|mA-DB\right|\\
\leq\delta_{\tau}(f)\leq\frac{1}{D+2m}\left(mA+DB\right),

(166)

where

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle=\left\|\Pi_{\textrm{irrep}}^{(\tau)\perp}\left(\pi_{\tau}(V_{f})+\pi_{\tau}(V_{p})\right)\Pi_{\textrm{irrep}}^{(\tau)\perp}\right\|_{1}

(167)

\displaystyle B

\displaystyle=\left\|\Pi_{\textrm{irrep}}^{(\tau)\perp}M_{\textsf{D}_{t^{\prime}}}^{(\tau)}\Pi_{\textrm{irrep}}^{(\tau)\perp}\right\|_{1}\leq\delta_{0}.

(168)

Note that the upper bound on
B
follows from the fact that
\textsf{D}_{t^{\prime}}
is a
\delta_{0}
-approximate
\tau
-design as
\tau\in\{t,t^{\prime}\}
and
t\leq t^{\prime}
.

To compute
A
, we use the expansion that

\pi_{\tau}(V_{f})+\pi_{\tau}(V_{p})=\\
2\Pi_{\textrm{irrep}}^{(\tau)}+\Pi_{\textrm{irrep}}^{(\tau)\perp}\left(\pi_{\tau}(V_{f})+\pi_{\tau}(V_{p})\right)\Pi_{\textrm{irrep}}^{(\tau)\perp},

(169)

and the fact that the first and the remaining term in the right-hand side have orthogonal support. Hence, using
\tau!=\mathrm{Tr}\bigl[\Pi_{\textrm{irrep}}^{(\tau)}\bigr]
, we obtain

\left\|\pi_{\tau}(V_{f})+\pi_{\tau}(V_{p})\right\|_{1}=\\
\left\|\Pi_{\textrm{irrep}}^{(\tau)\perp}\left(\pi_{\tau}(V_{f})+\pi_{\tau}(V_{p})\right)\Pi_{\textrm{irrep}}^{(\tau)\perp}\right\|_{1}+2\tau!.

(170)

In
Appendix

C
, we show that

\left\|\pi_{\tau}(V_{f})+\pi_{\tau}(V_{p})\right\|_{1}=\Lambda_{\tau}\left(s(f)\right).

(171)

Here,
\Lambda_{\tau}(x)
is defined for
x\in[0,d/2]
as

\Lambda_{\tau}(x)\coloneqq\\
2\sum_{s=0}^{2\tau}\binom{2\tau}{s}(d-2)^{2\tau-s}\sum_{r=0}^{s}\binom{s}{r}\left|T_{|2r-s|}\left(\frac{2x}{d}\right)\right|,

(172)

and
T_{m}(z)=\cos m\theta
, where
z=\cos\theta
, are the Chebyshev polynomials of the first kind.
Hence,

A=\Lambda_{\tau}\left(s(f)\right)-2\tau!.

(173)

Substituting
A
and
B
into
Eq.

166
, we obtain

\frac{1}{D/m+2}\left(\Lambda_{\tau}\left(s(f)\right)-2\tau!-\frac{D}{m}\delta_{0}\right)\leq\\
\delta_{\tau}(f)\leq\frac{1}{D/m+2}\left(\Lambda_{\tau}\left(s(f)\right)-2\tau!+\frac{D}{m}\delta_{0}\right).

(174)

In the lower bound, we have assumed that
\delta_{0}
is sufficiently small. We will check this condition later.

For later convenience, we introduce functions
\textrm{UB}_{\tau}(q,x)
and
\textrm{LB}_{\tau}(q,x)
by

\displaystyle\textrm{UB}_{\tau}(q,x)=\frac{1}{q+2}\left(\Lambda_{\tau}\left(x\right)-2\tau!+q\delta_{0}\right)

(175)

\displaystyle\textrm{LB}_{\tau}(q,x)=\frac{1}{q+2}\left(\Lambda_{\tau}\left(x\right)-2\tau!-q\delta_{0}\right)

(176)

using which we have

\textrm{LB}_{\tau}\left(\frac{D}{m},s(f)\right)\leq\delta_{\tau}(f)\leq\textrm{UB}_{\tau}\left(\frac{D}{m},s(f)\right).

(177)

Importantly, for any fixed
q\geq 0
, these functions are monotonically increasing as
x
increases for sufficiently large
d
. Indeed, the leading order expansion of
\Lambda_{\tau}(x)
is given by

\Lambda_{\tau}(x)=2d^{2\tau}-8\tau d^{2\tau-1}\left(1-\frac{2x}{d}\right)+{\mathcal{O}}\left(d^{2\tau-2}\right),

(178)

as shown in
Appendix

Thus, if
d
is sufficiently large,
\Lambda_{\tau}(x)
is monotonically increasing in
x
, and so are
\textrm{UB}_{\tau}(q,x)
and
\textrm{LB}_{\tau}(q,x)
for a fixed
q\geq 0
.

We now choose
D
and
m
such that
D/m
satisfies

\textrm{LB}_{t}\left(\frac{D}{m},\frac{d}{4}-\frac{2}{3}\right)=\delta.

(179)

From the order estimation of
\Lambda_{t}(x)
and the facts that
\delta_{0}\in{\mathcal{O}}(\delta^{2}/d^{2t})
and
\delta\in o(d^{2(t-1)})
, which implies that
\delta\in o(d^{2t})
, we have
D/m\in\Theta(d^{2t}/\delta)
. We also check the assumption on
\delta_{0}
in

174
, which is that
\delta_{0}

\textrm{LB}_{\tau}\left(D/m,s(f)\right)\geq 0

We also set
\delta^{\prime}

\delta^{\prime}=\textrm{UB}_{t^{\prime}}\left(\frac{D}{m},\frac{d}{4}-\frac{1}{3}\right)\in\Theta(d^{2(t^{\prime}-t)}\delta).

(180)

Clearly,
\delta^{\prime}-\delta\in\Theta(d^{2(t^{\prime}-t)}\delta)
for
t<t^{\prime}
.
When
t=t^{\prime}
, we need more careful analysis based on

for
t=t^{\prime}
.

With this choice of
D/m
, we below show that an algorithm
{\mathcal{A}}
to solve
\operatorname{{\mathsf{UniDes}}}(t,t^{\prime},\delta,\delta^{\prime})
can be used to solve

.
By definition, the algorithm can determine

\delta_{t^{\prime}}(f)>\delta^{\prime},\ \ \textrm{or}\ \ \delta_{t}(f)\leq\delta,

(181)

under the exclusive promise that only one of the two holds.

Let us first check if
\textsf{U}_{n}(f)
constructed from any Boolean function

To this end, we show that

\displaystyle\delta_{t^{\prime}}(f)>\delta^{\prime}

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow s(f)\geq\frac{d}{4},

(182)

\displaystyle\delta_{t}(f)\leq\delta

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow s(f)\leq\frac{d}{4}-1.

(183)

From these two, it is clear that all Boolean functions must exclusively satisfy one of the two conditions
\delta_{t}(f)\leq\delta
or
\delta_{t^{\prime}}(f)>\delta^{\prime}

To show

182

\delta_{t^{\prime}}(f)>\delta^{\prime}

\textrm{UB}_{t^{\prime}}\left(\frac{D}{m},\frac{d}{4}-\frac{1}{3}\right)=\delta^{\prime}<\delta_{t^{\prime}}(f)\leq\textrm{UB}_{t^{\prime}}\left(\frac{D}{m},s(f)\right).

On the other hand, if

,

. To show this, we use the fact that, for sufficiently large

which is shown in

. Hence, if
\delta_{t^{\prime}}(f)\leq\delta^{\prime}
, it follows from

177
and

that

\textrm{LB}_{t^{\prime}}\left(\frac{D}{m},s(f)\right)\leq\delta_{t^{\prime}}(f)\leq\delta^{\prime}\\
=\textrm{UB}_{t^{\prime}}\left(\frac{D}{m},\frac{d}{4}-\frac{1}{3}\right)<\textrm{LB}_{t^{\prime}}\left(\frac{D}{m},\frac{d}{4}\right).

(186)

From the monotonicity of
\textrm{LB}_{t^{\prime}}(q,x)

s(f)<d/4
. Taking the contraposition, we obtain that, if

.

To show

, if
\delta_{t}(f)\leq\delta
, we have from

\textrm{LB}_{t}\left(\frac{D}{m},s(f)\right)\leq\delta_{t}(f)\leq\delta=\textrm{LB}_{t}\left(\frac{D}{m},\frac{d}{4}-\frac{2}{3}\right).

Using the monotonicity of
\textrm{LB}_{t}(q,x)
in

and the fact that

is a non-negative integer, this implies that
s(f)\leq d/4-1
.
To show that

A key observation is

\textrm{UB}_{t}\left(\frac{D}{m},\frac{d}{4}-1\right)<\textrm{LB}_{t}\left(\frac{D}{m},\frac{d}{4}-\frac{2}{3}\right),

(188)

shown in
Appendix

using the assumption that
\delta\in o(d^{2(t^{\prime}-1)})
.
Hence, if
\delta_{t}(f)>\delta
, we have from

that

\textrm{UB}_{t}\left(\frac{D}{m},\frac{d}{4}-1\right)<\textrm{LB}_{t}\left(\frac{D}{m},\frac{d}{4}-\frac{2}{3}\right)=\delta\\
<\delta_{t}(f)\leq\textrm{UB}_{t}\left(\frac{D}{m},s(f)\right).

(189)

From the monotonicity, we have
d/4-1<s(f)
. By taking the contraposition, we obtain that
s(f)\leq d/4-1
implies that
\delta_{t}(f)\leq\delta
.

We finally show how the algorithm
{\mathcal{A}}
for solving
\operatorname{{\mathsf{UniDes}}}(t,t^{\prime},\delta,\delta^{\prime})
can be used to solve
MAJ-SAT
. As
\textsf{U}_{n}(f)
satisfies the exclusive promise of
\operatorname{{\mathsf{UniDes}}}(t,t^{\prime},\delta,\delta^{\prime})
for any Boolean function
f
, the algorithm is capable of determining whether
\textsf{U}_{n}(f)
satisfies

\delta_{t^{\prime}}(f)>\delta^{\prime},\ \ \textrm{or}\ \ \delta_{t}(f)\leq\delta.

(190)

From
Eqs.

182
and
183
, this implies that
\mathcal{A}
can determine

s(f)\geq\frac{d}{4}=2^{n-2},\ \ \textrm{or}\ \ s(f)\leq\frac{d}{4}-1=2^{n-2}-1.

(191)

Hence, one can efficiently solve
MAJ-SAT
with one query to
{\mathcal{A}}
.

\hfill\qed

VII
Conclusions and discussion

In this paper, we have initiated computational complexity-theoretic analyses about the problems related to unitary and state designs and have paved the way to understanding randomness in quantum systems from the complexity perspective.
We have begun with an explicit quantum algorithm for computing the state and unitary frame potentials and then clarified the complexity classes of exactly computing the frame potentials,
\operatorname{{\mathsf{compSFP}}}(t)
and
\operatorname{{\mathsf{compUFP}}}(t)
, which have been shown to be
{\#{\mathsf{P}}}
-hard and to be achievable by a single query to a
{\#{\mathsf{P}}}
-oracle.

We have then introduced promise problems,
\operatorname{{\mathsf{SFP}}}(t,\epsilon)
and
\operatorname{{\mathsf{UFP}}}(t,\epsilon)
, to decide whether the frame potential is larger than or smaller than certain values with a promise gap
\epsilon
. These problems are important to address the complexity of approximately computing the frame potentials.
For
\operatorname{{\mathsf{SFP}}}(t,\epsilon)
, we have proven that the problem is
{\mathsf{BQP}}
-complete for
\epsilon\in\Theta(1/{\mathsf{poly}}(n))
, but is
{\mathsf{PP}}
-complete for
\epsilon\in\Theta(2^{-{\mathsf{poly}}(n)})
. The latter has been shown to be the case for
\operatorname{{\mathsf{UFP}}}(t,\epsilon)
.

We have finally addressed promise problems that are directly related to state and unitary designs: whether a given set is a
\delta
-approximate
t
-design or not a
\delta^{\prime}
-approximate
t^{\prime}
-design, under the promise that only one of the two is the case. We have shown that these problems,
\operatorname{{\mathsf{StDes}}}(t,t^{\prime},\delta,\delta^{\prime})
and
\operatorname{{\mathsf{UniDes}}}(t,t^{\prime},\delta,\delta^{\prime})
, are in
{\mathsf{PP}}
if
t=t^{\prime}
and the gap between
\delta
and
\delta^{\prime}
is not too small. We have also shown the hardness result. Specifically, these problems are
{\mathsf{PP}}
-hard if the gap
is
\delta^{\prime}-\delta\in\Theta(2^{c(t^{\prime}-t)n}\delta)
for
t<t^{\prime}
and
\delta^{\prime}-\delta\in\Theta(2^{-cn}\delta)
for
t=t^{\prime}
, where
c=1
for
\operatorname{{\mathsf{StDes}}}(t,t^{\prime},\delta,\delta^{\prime})
and
c=2
for
\operatorname{{\mathsf{UniDes}}}(t,t^{\prime},\delta,\delta^{\prime})
.
We would like to emphasize that these results imply the computational hardness of deciding if a given set is an exponentially-accurate approximation to a
t
-design or a constant-away approximation to a
(t+1)
-design that is not an accurate
t
-design, highlighting the computationally hard nature of checking design properties.

These results have far-reaching implications, owing to the broad applicability of complexity theory and the universal significance of designs. Specifically, we have demonstrated their relevance to variational constructions of designs, the characterization of quantum chaos via the average OTOC, and the emergence of designs in Hamiltonian systems. Notably, our results imply that, for algorithms addressing these problems to scale efficiently to large systems, it is crucial to tailor them to leverage the specific structure of the underlying problems.

While we have thoroughly investigated the computational complexity classes of the introduced problems, the complexity in some parameter regions, such as
\operatorname{{\mathsf{UFP}}}(t,\epsilon)
for
\epsilon\in\Omega(1/{\mathsf{poly}}(n))
, remains open. Investigating these cases is an important future work.
While it is natural to expect that
\operatorname{{\mathsf{UFP}}}(t,\epsilon)
with inverse-polynomial or constant
\epsilon
is computationally easy, showing this is not straightforward, at least in our approach.
The difficulty is intrinsic in our approach, where we start with a quantum algorithm to compute the purity of a quantum state that encodes all the information of the unitary frame potential.
This approach inevitably introduces the prefactor
2^{-2nt}
between the unitary frame potential and the purity. Therefore, for estimating the frame potential with constant approximation in our approach, it is necessary to evaluate the purity with exponential accuracy, which is, in general, computationally intractable. This is, however, the difficulty specific to our approach, and it is open if
\operatorname{{\mathsf{UFP}}}(t,\epsilon)
with inverse-polynomial or constant
\epsilon
is computationally feasible. Developing a new approach toward this is an interesting open problem.

Regarding
\operatorname{{\mathsf{StDes}}}(t,t^{\prime},\delta,\delta^{\prime})
and
\operatorname{{\mathsf{UniDes}}}(t,t^{\prime},\delta,\delta^{\prime})
, it is of great interest to further investigate the problems when
t=t^{\prime}
. In this case, we have succeeded in proving the hardness only with small gap
\delta^{\prime}-\delta
. To this end, however, our methods, i.e., to hide a hard instance in a set, may not work.
It may also be interesting to consider the computational problems related to other definitions of unitary designs, such as the one based on the diamond norm. As different definitions do not lead to significant differences in applications, this is not practically important, but may still be of theoretical interest. In this case, the key is to tightly compute the approximation accuracy as we have done for proving the
{\mathsf{PP}}
-hardness of
\operatorname{{\mathsf{UniDes}}}
in terms of the moment operators.

Our results, showing the computationally hard nature of checking design properties, indicate that there may exist
pseudo

t
-designs, namely, a set of state vectors or unitaries that is not a
t
-design but indistinguishable from
t
-designs in polynomial time. This direction may be of interest in regard to the recent progress in computationally-secure quantum cryptography based on pseudorandom states or unitaries.
We expect that our results, that good
t
-design cannot be efficiently distinguished from bad
(t+1)
-design that fails to be a good
t
-design, could offer an interesting starting point toward this direction.

From a more practical viewpoint, it will also be important to consider restricted sets of state vectors or unitaries, for which the problems that we have introduced in this work become solvable in classical or quantum polynomial time.
This direction is of significant importance for developing the practical variational ansatz for constructing designs or algorithms for studying emergent designs in Hamiltonian systems.
