Multi-omics integration identifies PON2 as a prognostic biomarker and mediator of tumor microenvironment remodeling in gallbladder cancer
Authors: Jian Yang, Lingbo Hu, Chao Yang, Haoyang Bei, Yulian Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGallbladder cancer (GBC) is an aggressive biliary tract malignancy with limited therapeutic options and poor prognosis. Tumor microenvironment (TME) remodeling contributes substantially to GBC progression; however, the molecular drivers linking malignant epithelial programs to microenvironmental alterations remain poorly defined. This study aimed to characterize the cellular ecosystem of GBC and identify clinically relevant molecular targets associated with tumor progression and TME remodeling.MethodsSingle-cell RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) data from 10 GBC tissues and 4 matched adjacent normal tissues were analyzed to define the cellular landscape of GBC. Unsupervised clustering, cell-type annotation, and copy number variation analysis were used to characterize TME composition and identify malignant epithelial populations. Integrated analyses of single-cell transcriptomic, bulk RNA-seq, and tumor proteomic datasets were performed to screen candidate prognostic biomarkers. The clinical relevance of the candidate gene was evaluated by survival analysis and validated in clinical GBC specimens using immunohistochemistry and quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction. Functional assays were conducted in GBC cell lines to assess its effects on tumor cell proliferation and migration. Cell-cell communication analysis was further used to explore potential interactions between malignant epithelial cells and non-malignant microenvironmental components.ResultsSingle-cell profiling revealed substantial cellular heterogeneity within the GBC microenvironment and identified malignant epithelial clones with distinct transcriptional programs. Multi-omics integration identified paraoxonase 2 (PON2) as a potential prognostic biomarker in GBC. Elevated PON2 expression was validated in clinical GBC specimens and was associated with worse overall survival. In vitro knockdown of PON2 significantly suppressed the proliferation and migration of GBC cells. Further single-cell analysis showed that the PON2-high malignant epithelial state was accompanied by remodeling of endothelial and stromal subpopulations. Cell-cell communication analysis suggested that PON2-high malignant epithelial cells exhibited enhanced interactions with endothelial cells, fibroblasts, and pericytes through specific ligand-receptor networks, indicating a potential role of PON2 in shaping a pro-tumorigenic microenvironment.ConclusionsThis multi-omics study identifies PON2 as a clinically relevant biomarker associated with GBC progression and TME remodeling. PON2 may serve as a prognostic indicator and potential therapeutic target, providing new insights into TME-directed translational strategies for GBC.Keywords: Gallbladder cancer (GBC), paraoxonase 2 (PON2), tumor microenvironment (TME), single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq), tumor progression

Key findings

• Paraoxonase 2 (PON2) is enriched in malignant epithelial cells of gallbladder cancer (GBC) and is associated with unfavorable prognosis, tumor cell proliferation/migration, and remodeling of endothelial and stromal niches.

What is known and what is new?

• Tumor microenvironment (TME) remodeling contributes to GBC progression, but molecular drivers connecting malignant epithelial programs with microenvironmental changes remain incompletely defined.

• This multi-omics analysis identifies PON2 as a clinically relevant biomarker and a potential epithelial driver linked to endothelial and stromal reprogramming.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• PON2 may help prognostic stratification in GBC and provides a candidate target for future mechanistic and TME-directed therapeutic validation.

Introduction

Gallbladder cancer (GBC) is the most common malignancy of the biliary tract, characterized by rapid progression, early metastasis, and a poor prognosis (1-3). Due to the lack of specific early symptoms and reliable diagnostic biomarkers, most patients are diagnosed at advanced stages, making them ineligible for curative surgical resection (4-6). Furthermore, GBC frequently exhibits resistance to conventional chemotherapy and radiotherapy (7-9). This treatment resistance is increasingly attributed to the complex tumor microenvironment (TME) of GBC (10-13), which facilitates tumor survival, immune evasion, and metastasis (14-17). Therefore, characterizing the cellular heterogeneity of the GBC microenvironment and identifying specific molecular drivers are required to develop novel diagnostic and therapeutic strategies (18-20).

Recent applications of single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) have advanced our understanding of intra-tumoral heterogeneity and intercellular crosstalk within the TME across various solid tumors (21-25). However, the specific evolutionary trajectories of malignant epithelial cells and their interactions with stromal and endothelial compartments in GBC remain to be fully elucidated (26-28). Moreover, while transcriptomic profiling provides valuable high-resolution insights, identifying clinical biomarkers often necessitates the integration of multi-omics approaches to bridge the gap between gene expression and protein translation (29-31).

In this study, we aimed to characterize the cellular landscape of GBC and identify oncogenic drivers mediating tumor progression and TME remodeling. By integrating scRNA-seq mapping with tumor proteomics and bulk RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) cohorts, we identified paraoxonase 2 (PON2) as a potential pathogenic target and prognostic biomarker (32-34). Through in vitro assays and intercellular communication analyses, we observed that PON2 is associated not only with the proliferation and migration of malignant epithelial cells but also with stromal and endothelial remodeling (35). These findings provide a multi-omics framework for understanding GBC pathogenesis and suggest that PON2 may serve as a viable therapeutic target. We present this article in accordance with the MDAR reporting checklist (available at https://tcr.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tcr-2026-1620/rc).

Methods

Data collection and processing

The scRNA-seq and bulk RNA-seq data for GBC utilized in this study were originally generated in the study titled “Multi-omic analysis of gallbladder cancer identifies distinct tumor microenvironments associated with disease progression”. The scRNA-seq data were accessed from the CNGB Sequence Archive (CNSA) under accession number CNP0004660, and the Genome Sequence Archive for Human (GSA-Human) under accession number HRA009841. The pre-processed scRNA-seq datasets were obtained via Zenodo (https://zenodo.org/records/15400138). Corresponding bulk RNA-seq data were retrieved from GSA-Human under accession number HRA011357. The GRCh38 assembly (GCF_000001405.26) was used as the reference genome for all mapping procedures. In total, 14 samples, comprising 10 GBC tissues and 4 matched adjacent normal tissues, were included in our analyses. Data processing, quality control, and downstream scRNA-seq analyses were performed using the Seurat R package (version 4.3.2). To ensure the retention of high-quality cells, strict filtering criteria were applied: cells expressing fewer than 300 or more than 7,000 genes were excluded; genes expressed in fewer than 3 cells were removed; and cells with a mitochondrial RNA fraction exceeding 20% were filtered out. Following quality control, a total of 147,859 cells were retained for the single-cell transcriptome analysis.

For the bulk transcriptomic cohort, differential gene expression (DEG) analysis was conducted using the limma R package (version 4.1.3). When integrating data or comparing cohorts, the “normalizeBetweenArrays” function from the limma package was applied to eliminate potential batch effects. The significance thresholds for defining DEGs were set at |log2(fold change)| >1 and P value <0.05.

Ethics approval

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Taizhou Hospital of Zhejiang Province Affiliated to Wenzhou Medical University (approval No. K20260731). The requirement for written informed consent was waived by the Ethics Committee due to the retrospective nature of the study. This study did not involve animal experiments or animal-derived in vivo research.

Definition of PON2-high and PON2-low groups

For all analyses requiring stratification by PON2 expression, the high- and low-expression groups were defined using the median PON2 expression value as the cut-off within the corresponding dataset. For survival analysis and clinicopathological comparison in Table 1, patients were divided according to the median normalized bulk RNA-seq expression value of PON2. For single-cell analyses, malignant epithelial cells were divided into PON2-high and PON2-low groups according to the median normalized single-cell expression of PON2 within the malignant epithelial compartment. The same median-based criterion was applied consistently to all downstream stratified analyses involving PON2 expression.

Data clustering and dimensionality reduction

Clustering analysis and Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP)-based dimensionality reduction of the GBC scRNA-seq data were performed using the Seurat R package. Following data normalization, the “RunPCA” function was employed to perform principal component analysis (PCA) on the top 2,000 highly variable genes. Based on the cumulative standard deviation of the principal components, the top 9 principal components were selected for downstream clustering. Cell-cell distances were calculated using the “FindNeighbors” function with default parameters, and a K-nearest neighbor (KNN) graph was constructed based on the overlap between cell neighbors. Subsequently, the “FindClusters” function with a resolution of 0.2 was applied to partition the single-cell populations. Finally, the “RunUMAP” function was utilized to perform non-linear dimensionality reduction and visualize the clustering results in a two-dimensional space. To assign biological annotations to each cluster, canonical cell identity markers were curated from established cell marker databases and relevant published literature.

Copy number variation (CNV) inference and pseudotime trajectory analysis

To distinguish malignant epithelial clones from non-malignant epithelial cells, large-scale chromosomal alterations were inferred from the scRNA-seq data using the inferCNV R package. Immune cells or normal epithelial cells were utilized as the non-malignant reference background to calculate the CNV burden (CNV scores) for each epithelial subpopulation. Furthermore, the Monocle3 R package was employed to conduct pseudotime trajectory analysis, enabling the projection of cellular states onto a two-dimensional continuum to map the dynamic transcriptomic evolution of epithelial cells during tumorigenesis.

Cell-cell communication network analysis

To decode the complex intercellular crosstalk networks between the malignant epithelial compartment and distinct subsets of endothelial and stromal cells within the TME, the CellChat R package was utilized. Based on the expression of verified ligand-receptor (L-R) gene pairs, we quantified the global interaction strength and interaction frequencies. Comparative network analyses were further performed to delineate the specific L-R pairs mediating the hyperactive TME crosstalk stratified by PON2 expression status (PON2_high vs. PON2_low).

Bulk RNA-seq data, proteomics datasets, and corresponding clinical follow-up information for GBC were retrieved from public databases. Survival curves were constructed using the Kaplan-Meier method and compared via the log-rank test. Hazard ratios (HRs) were calculated using univariate and multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression models to evaluate independent prognostic factors. The diagnostic predictive efficacy of PON2 was assessed utilizing receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves. Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway functional enrichment analyses, alongside Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA), were performed using the clusterProfiler R package.

Cell culture and transfection

Human GBC cell lines GBC-SD and NOZ were obtained from the Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China). These two cell lines were selected because they are well-established human GBC models that are commonly used for in vitro studies of GBC biology and showed stable growth and transfection efficiency in our preliminary experiments. Using two independent GBC cell lines also allowed us to evaluate whether the effects of PON2 knockdown were reproducible across different cellular backgrounds. Cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) (Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin, and maintained in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO2. For targeted knockdown of PON2, cells were transfected with PON2-specific small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) and then collected for protein analysis and functional experiments. The siRNA for PON2 was purchased from GenePharma (Shanghai, China), and the sequences of siPON2 were as follows: sense, 5'-CCGUUGUGAUAGAUAGUTATT-3'; antisense, 5'-UGUCUAUCATUCACCUCGGTT-3'. Transfections were performed using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Cells transfected with scrambled sequences served as the negative control (NC). Knockdown efficacy was validated by quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) and Western blotting.

qRT-PCR

Total RNA was extracted from clinical tissues and cultured GBC cells using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen). Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized using a reverse transcription kit. qRT-qPCR was subsequently performed on a real-time PCR detection system using SYBR Green Master Mix (Takara, Kyoto, Japan). The specific primer sequences used for PON2 were as follows: forward, 5'-CCAGAAGCTCTTCGTGTATGACC-3', and reverse, 5'-GAACTTCCTTGGAGAACAGACCC-3'. The relative mRNA expression levels of PON2 were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt (ΔCt = ΔCttarget – ΔCtACTB, ΔΔCt = ΔCt – ΔCtcontrol) method, with GAPDH serving as the endogenous control. All in vitro experiments were independently repeated three times. For qRT-PCR assays, technical replicates were included within each independent biological replicate where appropriate.

Western blotting analysis

Total cellular or tissue proteins were extracted using RIPA lysis buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) supplemented with a protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China). Protein concentrations were determined utilizing a BCA protein assay kit (Keygen Biotech, China). Equal amounts of total protein extracts were separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and electro-transferred onto PVDF membranes (Bio-Rad, California, USA). Following blocking with 5% non-fat milk, the membranes were incubated with primary antibodies against PON2 (1:1,000, Proteintech, China) and the internal control (GAPDH) (1:1,000, Proteintech, China) overnight at 4 ℃. The membranes were then incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:3,000). Protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) detection system, and quantitative densitometric analysis was performed utilizing ImageJ software. All in vitro experiments were independently repeated three times. For Western blotting assays, technical replicates were included within each independent biological replicate where appropriate.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

Paraffin-embedded sections of GBC and matched adjacent normal tissues were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to heat-induced antigen retrieval. Following the quenching of endogenous peroxidase activity with a blocking solution, the sections were incubated with a PON2-specific primary antibody overnight at 4 ℃. Subsequently, the slides were incubated with an HRP-conjugated secondary antibody, visualized using 3,3'-diaminobenzidine (DAB) substrate, and counterstained with hematoxylin. The positive expression of PON2 was observed and independently evaluated under a light microscope.

Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) proliferation assay

The proliferative capacity of GBC cells was evaluated using the CCK-8 assay. Exponentially growing NC and PON2-knockdown (siPON2) cells were seeded into 96-well plates at an optimized density. At specific time points (0, 24, 48, 72, and 96 h), 10 μL of CCK-8 reagent (Vazyme) was added to each well, followed by a 2-hour incubation at 37 ℃ in the dark. Cellular viability was determined by measuring the optical density (OD) absorbance at 450 nm using a microplate reader. All in vitro experiments were independently repeated three times. For CCK-8 assays, technical replicates were included within each independent biological replicate where appropriate.

GBC cells were seeded into 6-well plates and grown to >90% confluence. A uniform linear scratch (wound) was created across the cellular monolayer using a sterile 200 μL pipette tip. After gently washing with PBS to remove detached cells, the cells were further cultured in a serum-free medium. Microscopic images of the wounds were captured at 0 h and after 24 h. The wound closure area was quantitatively measured using ImageJ software to assess the migratory capacity of the cells. All in vitro experiments were independently repeated three times. For the wound healing migration assay, technical replicates were included within each independent biological replicate where appropriate.

Statistical analysis

All bioinformatics visualizations and statistical analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.3.2) and GraphPad Prism (version 10.6.1). Quantitative continuous data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical significance between two groups was analyzed using the unpaired Student’s t-test or the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, as appropriate. Comparisons among multiple groups were determined using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) or the Kruskal-Wallis test, followed by post hoc pairwise comparisons when applicable. Differences in cell-type proportions were evaluated after aggregating cells at the indicated tissue or PON2-expression group level and were interpreted cautiously because the number of biological samples was limited. A two-tailed P value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant (*P<0.05, **P<0.01, ***P<0.001).

Results

Single-cell transcriptomic profiling delineates the cellular landscape of GBC

To characterize the TME of GBC, we performed scRNA-seq on 10 GBC tissues and 4 matched adjacent normal tissues. After quality control and batch-effect correction, a total of 147,859 cells were retained for downstream analysis (Figure 1A). Unsupervised clustering identified nine major cell lineages, including epithelial cells, T/natural killer (NK) cells, B cells, plasma cells, monocytes/macrophages, neutrophils, endothelial cells, mesenchymal cells, and mast cells (Figure 1B,1C). These populations were annotated according to canonical lineage markers, and the accuracy of cell-type annotation was supported by representative markers across major clusters (Figure 1D,1E). Epithelial cells were annotated using keratin markers such as KRT7, KRT18 and KRT19; T-cell annotation was supported by CD3D, CD8A, and CD4 expression. Aggregated comparison of cellular proportions revealed marked differences in TME composition between GBC tissues and matched adjacent normal tissues (Figure 1F). The sample-level distribution of major cell lineages further showed inter-patient heterogeneity within immune and stromal compartments (Figure 1G). Collectively, these data provide a comprehensive single-cell atlas of the GBC microenvironment and establish a foundation for investigating cell-type-specific programs associated with tumor progression.

Transcriptional heterogeneity and functional divergence of the epithelial compartment

Given the central role of epithelial cells in GBC initiation and progression, we next re-clustered the epithelial compartment to explore its transcriptional heterogeneity. Unsupervised sub-clustering identified five transcriptionally distinct epithelial subpopulations, designated clusters 0–4 (Figure 2A,2B). These epithelial subsets exhibited specific marker gene signatures. Cluster 0 showed high expression of OLFM4, CEACAM5, and CTSE; cluster 1 was characterized by LGALS1, MT1E, and BST2; cluster 2 was marked by ALDOB, FABP1, and LMO4; cluster 3 exhibited increased expression of PIGR, FXYD2, and SPP1; and cluster 4 was distinguished by SPINK4, MUC2, and REP15 (Figure 2C). These canonical marker genes were used to define transcriptional states rather than to infer direct regulatory relationships among subgroups. Although several markers showed cluster-restricted or mutually exclusive expression patterns, direct positive or negative regulation among these genes cannot be concluded from single-cell expression data alone. To investigate the biological features of these epithelial subsets, we performed pathway enrichment and gene set enrichment analyses. The results revealed distinct functional programs across epithelial clusters (Figure 2D,2E). Cluster 0 was enriched in pathways related to adenosine triphosphate (ATP) synthesis and mitochondrial electron transport, suggesting increased metabolic activity. Cluster 1 was associated with antigen processing and presentation, indicating a potential immunomodulatory phenotype. Clusters 2, 3, and 4 were enriched in pathways related to fatty acid metabolism, protein secretion, and microtubule bundle formation, respectively, suggesting functional compartmentalization within the epithelial compartment. Aggregated comparison showed notable compositional differences between tumor and adjacent normal tissues, with cluster 3 being predominantly enriched in adjacent normal tissues (Figure 2F). The sample-level distribution further showed heterogeneity across individual patients (Figure 2G). To distinguish malignant epithelial cells from epithelial populations with low CNV burden, we inferred large-scale chromosomal CNVs from scRNA-seq data. CNV analysis revealed different degrees of genomic instability across epithelial subsets. In particular, cluster 0 displayed a higher CNV score, supporting its malignant identity, whereas cluster 3 exhibited a lower CNV burden (Figure 2H,2I). Statistical comparison of CNV scores across epithelial clusters was performed using the Kruskal-Wallis test followed by post hoc pairwise comparison; the highest CNV burden was observed in cluster 0. Pseudotime trajectory analysis was then performed to infer potential epithelial state transitions during GBC progression. Cluster 0 was positioned toward the terminal end of the trajectory, further suggesting that this subset represents an advanced malignant epithelial state (Figure 2J).

Multi-omics integration identifies and validates PON2 as a clinically relevant biomarker in GBC

To identify potential molecular drivers of GBC, we integrated tumor proteomics, bulk RNA-seq, and gene expression profiles derived from malignant epithelial cells identified by scRNA-seq. This multi-omics strategy identified 184 significantly upregulated genes shared across the three independent datasets (Figure 3A). We then evaluated these overlapping genes for prognostic relevance using HR-based survival analysis. Among the top candidate genes, PON2 emerged as a clinically relevant biomarker with significant prognostic potential (Figure 3B). ROC curve analysis was performed to assess the diagnostic performance of PON2 in distinguishing GBC tissues from non-tumor tissues. PON2 yielded an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.704, suggesting moderate diagnostic value (Figure 3C). To validate these computational findings, we examined PON2 expression in clinical tissue specimens. IHC and Western blotting demonstrated that PON2 protein expression was increased in GBC tissues compared with matched adjacent normal tissues (Figure 3D,3E). Consistently, qRT-PCR confirmed transcriptional upregulation of PON2 in GBC tissues (Figure 3F). Kaplan-Meier survival analysis was performed using bulk RNA-seq expression data, and patients were divided into high- and low-PON2 groups according to the median normalized PON2 RNA expression level. Patients with high PON2 RNA expression had significantly worse overall survival than those with low PON2 expression (Figure 3G). In addition, analysis of clinicopathological characteristics indicated that high PON2 expression, defined using the same median cut-off, was associated with more advanced tumor-node-metastasis (TNM) stage (Table 1). Collectively, these results suggest that PON2 is upregulated in GBC and may serve as a clinically relevant biomarker associated with disease progression and poor prognosis.

Single-cell localization and in vitro validation support an oncogenic role of PON2 in GBC

To determine the cellular distribution of PON2 within the GBC microenvironment, we evaluated PON2 expression across the annotated cell lineages. UMAP visualization and violin plot analysis showed that PON2 expression was predominantly enriched in malignant epithelial cells compared with immune and stromal populations (Figure 4A,4B), suggesting that PON2 is mainly associated with the malignant epithelial compartment in GBC. To further explore the transcriptional programs associated with PON2 expression, malignant epithelial cells were stratified into PON2-high and PON2-low subsets according to the median normalized PON2 expression value within the malignant epithelial compartment. Differential expression analysis revealed a distinct gene expression profile in PON2-high malignant epithelial cells (Figure 4C). Subsequent GO and KEGG enrichment analyses showed that genes upregulated in the PON2-high subset were enriched in biological processes related to cell proliferation and epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) (Figure 4D,4E), suggesting that PON2-high malignant cells may exhibit a more aggressive phenotype. We next performed in vitro experiments to validate the functional role of PON2 in GBC cells. PON2-knockdown models were established in GBC-SD and NOZ cells, and knockdown efficiency was confirmed by Western blotting and quantitative densitometric analysis (Figure 4F,4G). CCK-8 assays demonstrated that PON2 depletion significantly reduced the proliferative capacity of both GBC cell lines (Figure 4H,4I). Moreover, wound-healing assays showed that PON2 knockdown markedly impaired GBC cell migration (Figure 4J,4K). These results indicate that PON2 contributes to the proliferative and migratory abilities of GBC cells, supporting its potential oncogenic role in GBC progression.

PON2 expression is associated with TME remodeling and enhanced epithelial-endothelial communication

To investigate whether PON2 expression is associated with remodeling of the GBC microenvironment, we analyzed global cellular composition according to PON2 expression status in malignant epithelial cells. Comparative compositional analysis and observed-to-expected ratio assessment revealed notable differences in the distribution of immune and stromal populations between the PON2-high and PON2-low groups (Figure 5A-5C). These findings suggest that PON2 expression in malignant epithelial cells is associated with distinct TME states and inter-patient heterogeneity. Given the observed differences in stromal composition, we first focused on endothelial cells and performed sub-clustering to resolve endothelial heterogeneity. Seven transcriptionally distinct endothelial cell subtypes were identified, including MKI67_ECs, PDPN_ECs, ESM1_ECs, FBLN5_ECs, SPP1_ECs, MADCAM1_ECs, and VCAM1_ECs (Figure 5D). These subtypes were defined by specific marker gene expression patterns (Figure 5E). Although the endothelial subclusters were detected across all samples (Figure 5F), their relative proportions differed between tumor and adjacent normal tissues. In particular, MKI67_ECs were predominantly enriched in tumor tissues, suggesting increased proliferative activity within the tumor-associated endothelial compartment (Figure 5G). We further assessed endothelial subset distribution according to PON2 expression status. Although all seven endothelial subtypes were present in both the PON2-high and PON2-low groups (Figure 5H), their relative abundances differed. Notably, MADCAM1_ECs were more abundant in the PON2-high group, whereas PDPN_ECs and ESM1_ECs were relatively enriched in the PON2-low group (Figure 5I,5J). These findings indicate that a PON2-high malignant epithelial state is associated with remodeling of the endothelial niche. To explore potential communication between malignant epithelial cells and endothelial populations, we performed cell-cell interaction analysis. The overall interaction strength between PON2-high malignant epithelial cells and endothelial subpopulations was higher than that observed for PON2-low malignant epithelial cells (Figure 5K). Specifically, PON2-high malignant epithelial cells exhibited enhanced outgoing communication networks toward multiple endothelial subpopulations, including MKI67_ECs, ESM1_ECs, SPP1_ECs, FBLN5_ECs, VCAM1_ECs, and MADCAM1_ECs. These findings suggest that the PON2-high malignant epithelial state is associated with a more active endothelial niche characterized by enhanced epithelial-endothelial communication, which may contribute to vascular remodeling and tumor progression in GBC. Further analysis of L-R pairs suggested that PON2-high malignant epithelial cells may communicate with endothelial subsets through VEGF family signaling and extracellular matrix-receptor interaction pathways (Figure 5L). Taken together, these results suggest that elevated PON2 expression is associated with endothelial niche remodeling and enhanced epithelial-endothelial crosstalk, which may contribute to a pro-angiogenic and pro-metastatic microenvironment in GBC.

High-resolution mapping reveals PON2-associated stromal remodeling and rewired epithelial-stromal crosstalk

To further characterize stromal heterogeneity within the GBC microenvironment, we performed unsupervised sub-clustering of mesenchymal and stromal cells. This analysis identified ten transcriptionally distinct stromal subpopulations, including complement factor D-positive cancer-associated fibroblasts (CFD_CAFs), BMP5_CAFs, CLEC3B_CAFs, membrane metalloendopeptidase-positive cancer-associated fibroblasts (MME_CAFs), ISG15_CAFs, MYH11_vSMCs, CCL21_Pericytes, RGS5_Pericytes, Schwann_cells, and Mesothelial_cells (Figure 6A). The identity of each subset was supported by canonical marker genes showing subset-specific expression and detection patterns (Figure 6B). Although all stromal subpopulations were detected across samples, their relative proportions varied considerably (Figure 6C). CFD_CAFs were the predominant stromal subset in matched adjacent normal tissues, whereas MME_CAFs were enriched in tumor tissues (Figure 6D). We next compared stromal composition between the PON2-high and PON2-low groups. Although all stromal subsets were present in both groups, their relative abundances differed substantially (Figure 6E). The PON2-high microenvironment was characterized by a distinct stromal composition, with CFD_CAFs and ISG15_CAFs representing major stromal populations. In particular, ISG15_CAFs were markedly enriched in the PON2-high group (Figure 6F,6G). Given that ISG15-positive fibroblast populations have been implicated in cytokine production, EMT induction, and angiogenic signaling, their enrichment suggests that PON2-high malignant epithelial cells may be associated with a more pro-tumorigenic stromal niche. Because tumor-stroma interactions are critical for GBC progression, we next assessed whether PON2 expression was associated with altered epithelial-stromal communication. Global cell-cell communication analysis revealed that PON2-high malignant epithelial cells exhibited enhanced predicted outgoing interactions with stromal populations compared with PON2-low malignant epithelial cells (Figure 6H). Increased interaction strength was observed across multiple stromal subsets, with prominent enhancement involving CLEC3B_CAFs, ISG15_CAFs, MYH11_vSMCs, and RGS5_Pericytes. These findings indicate that the PON2-high malignant epithelial state is associated with reinforced epithelial-stromal communication and stromal niche remodeling, suggesting a potential role of PON2 in shaping a tumor-supportive microenvironment in GBC. Detailed L-R analysis further revealed that PON2-high malignant epithelial cells expressed ligands such as LAMB3 and LGALS9, which may mediate enhanced communication with fibroblast and pericyte subpopulations (Figure 6I). Collectively, these findings indicate that PON2 expression is associated with stromal niche remodeling and strengthened epithelial-stromal crosstalk in GBC. This PON2-associated communication network may contribute to the formation of a permissive microenvironment that supports tumor progression.

Discussion

In this study, we integrated single-cell transcriptomics, tumor proteomics, and bulk RNA-seq datasets to systematically characterize the cellular ecosystem and molecular landscape of GBC. Through this multi-omics strategy, we identified PON2 as a clinically relevant biomarker associated with GBC progression and poor prognosis. In addition to its tumor-intrinsic role in regulating cancer cell proliferation and migration, our findings suggest that PON2 expression is closely associated with remodeling of endothelial and stromal compartments, contributing to a more interactive and potentially tumor-supportive TME. A major strength of this study is the single-cell resolution analysis of the epithelial compartment in GBC. By identifying five transcriptionally distinct epithelial subpopulations and integrating inferred CNV patterns, we distinguished malignant epithelial clones from epithelial populations with lower inferred CNV burden. The functional differences among these epithelial subsets, including metabolic activation, antigen presentation, fatty acid metabolism, protein secretion, and cytoskeletal organization, highlight the substantial intratumoral heterogeneity of GBC. Such transcriptional heterogeneity may contribute to diverse cellular states during tumor progression. Therefore, identifying key molecular regulators associated with malignant epithelial states may provide new opportunities for biomarker development and therapeutic intervention in this aggressive biliary tract malignancy.

PON2 has been implicated in cancer biology because of its roles in oxidative stress regulation, mitochondrial function, and cell survival (36-38). Recent studies have suggested that PON2 may represent a potential cancer biomarker and therapeutic target (36,39). Consistent with previous observations in other malignancies, our study demonstrates that PON2 is upregulated in GBC and associated with aggressive tumor characteristics. Importantly, our findings extend previous knowledge by integrating single-cell and multi-omics analyses to define the cellular context in which PON2 is expressed and functionally associated with GBC progression.

Beyond its tumor-intrinsic role, our single-cell analysis revealed that PON2-high malignant epithelial cells were associated with distinct TME remodeling patterns. Specifically, PON2-high tumors exhibited altered endothelial and stromal cell compositions and enhanced predicted epithelial-endothelial and epithelial-stromal communication networks. Cell-cell communication analysis suggested increased interactions involving endothelial subpopulations, fibroblast subsets, and pericytes, indicating that PON2 expression may be associated with a more tumor-supportive microenvironment. However, these L-R analyses represent predicted communication patterns and require experimental validation in future studies.

From a translational perspective, PON2 may have potential value as a prognostic biomarker for patient stratification. If validated in larger multicenter cohorts, PON2 expression may help identify patients with aggressive disease who require closer surveillance or intensified therapeutic strategies. In addition, the association between PON2 expression and TME remodeling raises the possibility that PON2-high tumors may benefit from combination approaches targeting both tumor-intrinsic pathways and microenvironmental programs, such as angiogenic signaling or extracellular matrix-associated pathways. Nevertheless, therapeutic strategies targeting PON2 remain exploratory and require further preclinical investigation.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, although PON2 knockdown experiments demonstrated functional effects on GBC cell proliferation and migration, the downstream molecular mechanisms remain incompletely understood. Second, the relationship between PON2 expression and TME remodeling was mainly inferred from single-cell transcriptomic and L-R analyses; direct experimental validation of these interactions is required. Third, our clinical validation cohort was retrospective, and larger prospective multicenter cohorts are needed to confirm the prognostic value of PON2. In addition, external validation using dedicated GBC cohorts remains necessary because currently available public cancer databases provide limited independent GBC datasets. Finally, in vivo studies using orthotopic or stroma-rich GBC models will be required to further evaluate the biological and therapeutic relevance of PON2.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this multi-omics study identifies PON2 as a clinically relevant biomarker associated with GBC progression and TME remodeling. By integrating single-cell transcriptomics, bulk RNA-seq, tumor proteomics, clinical validation, and functional assays, we demonstrate that PON2 is enriched in malignant epithelial cells, correlates with poor prognosis, and contributes to GBC cell proliferation and migration. PON2-high malignant epithelial states are also associated with altered endothelial and stromal composition and enhanced intercellular communication networks. These findings suggest that PON2 may serve as a prognostic biomarker and potential therapeutic target, while further mechanistic and in vivo studies are required to validate its translational potential.
