Fasting serum annexin A2 is associated with peripheral insulin sensitivity and may enhance glucose uptake via Src‐dependent signaling
Authors: Yuichi Ito, Tsuyoshi Okura, Mari Anno, Sonoko Kitao, Satomi Endo, Yoshinori Ichihara, Tatsuya Sawano, Junichiro Miake, Etsuko Ueta, Masahiko Kato, Shin‐ichi Taniguchi, Kazuhiro Yamamoto, Takeshi Imamura
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimSubcellular annexin A2 (ANXA2) is known to regulate membrane dynamics and GLUT4 trafficking in adipocytes; however, the role of serum ANXA2 in glucose metabolism remains unclear. We aimed to investigate the association between fasting serum ANXA2 and insulin sensitivity in humans and to examine the role of extracellular ANXA2 in glucose uptake in cultured skeletal myocytes.MethodsWe conducted a clinical study in 15 non‐diabetic Japanese adults who underwent hyperinsulinemic–euglycemic glucose clamp studies to assess peripheral insulin sensitivity. Fasting serum ANXA2 levels were measured and correlated with metabolic parameters. Cultured C2C12 myotubes were treated with recombinant human ANXA2 and the effects of ANXA2 on glucose uptake and insulin signaling mediators were evaluated.ResultsFasting serum ANXA2 showed a significant positive correlation with mean glucose infusion rate (mean GIR; r = 0.76, P = 0.00095). In C2C12 myotubes, both ANXA2 and insulin significantly increased glucose uptake compared with controls. ANXA2 enhanced IRS‐1 and Akt phosphorylation independently of insulin, and this effect was suppressed by the Src‐kinase inhibitor PP2.ConclusionsIn conclusion, these results indicated that fasting serum ANXA2 is associated with peripheral insulin sensitivity in humans, and that exogenous ANXA2 may enhance glucose uptake in association with IRS‐1/Akt signaling and Src‐dependent pathways. Together, these findings support further evaluation of serum ANXA2 as a potential indicator reflecting peripheral insulin sensitivity.Keywords: Annexin A2, glucose uptake, insulin sensitivity

INTRODUCTION

Type 2 diabetes mellitus is characterized by insulin resistance and impaired insulin secretion. Although numerous molecules have been proposed as regulators of insulin resistance, the elucidation of clinical indices of insulin sensitivity is still insufficient. Annexin A2 (ANXA2) is a calcium‐ and phospholipid‐binding protein involved in membrane trafficking and cellular signaling. We previously demonstrated that ANXA2 expression is regulated by thiazolidinediones and that ANXA2 participates in insulin‐stimulated GLUT4 translocation in adipocytes 1 . In addition, ANXA2 exerts anti‐inflammatory and anti‐atherogenic effects in vascular cells 2 , 3 , 4 , underscoring its importance across several metabolically relevant tissues.

ANXA2 also functions in skeletal muscle. Earlier studies showed that ANXA2 interacts with dysferlin to facilitate sarcolemmal membrane repair, a process essential for maintaining muscle integrity during mechanical stress 5 . Because membrane repair and cytoskeletal remodeling influence vesicle trafficking and metabolic adaptation, these structural roles raise the possibility that ANXA2 contributes to glucose handling in muscle cells.

ANXA2 is further implicated in intracellular signaling pathways relevant to metabolism. In non‐muscle cells, including esophageal squamous cell carcinoma cells, ANXA2 has been reported to activate the Akt/mTOR pathway through the mitotic kinase TTK 6 , and its phosphorylation and localization are regulated by Src family kinases 7 , 8 . Src‐dependent signaling intersects with pathways governing insulin‐independent glucose uptake, suggesting that ANXA2 may modulate skeletal muscle glucose metabolism. However, whether extracellular ANXA2 influences glucose uptake and insulin‐related signaling in skeletal muscle cells remains unknown.

Peripheral insulin sensitivity is most accurately assessed by the hyperinsulinemic–euglycemic clamp (HEC), which primarily reflects skeletal muscle insulin responsiveness 9 , 10 . In contrast, the homeostasis model assessment of insulin resistance (HOMA‐IR) primarily reflects hepatic insulin resistance and is less specific to skeletal muscle 11 . However, whether circulating ANXA2 levels are associated with insulin sensitivity measured by these methods remains unknown.

Based on these considerations, we hypothesized that ANXA2 promotes glucose uptake in skeletal muscle and contributes to systemic insulin sensitivity. To test this hypothesis, we examined the association between fasting serum ANXA2 levels and insulin sensitivity in non‐diabetic individuals using the glucose clamp, and we investigated whether exogenous ANXA2 directly stimulates glucose uptake and intracellular signaling in C2C12 myotubes.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Participants

Japanese adults without diabetes were recruited at Tottori University Hospital between 2013 and 2023 through study advertisement posters and voluntary participation. The predefined inclusion criteria were as follows: age 20–75 years at the time of informed consent, provision of written informed consent, and absence of diabetes, defined as HbA1c <6.0% and fasting plasma glucose <110 mg/dL. These criteria were confirmed based on medical history and routine health checkup data or screening blood tests when necessary. Participants with cancer, pancreatic disease, chronic viral hepatitis, liver cirrhosis, renal dysfunction (serum creatinine >114.92 μmol/L [1.3 mg/dL]), or treatment with hyperglycemic medications such as corticosteroids were excluded. A total of 15 participants (9 men and 6 women) met the selection criteria and were included in the study.

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Medicine, Tottori University (approval number G161). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. The cohort was originally established to investigate novel factors associated with peripheral insulin sensitivity using HEC studies. Based on accumulating experimental evidence, including our previous finding that ANXA2 participates in insulin‐stimulated GLUT4 translocation in adipocytes 1 , ANXA2 was recognized as a promising candidate molecule involved in insulin action. Subsequent participant recruitment and sample collection were conducted to allow evaluation of circulating ANXA2 as one of the candidate molecules.

Hyperinsulinemic–euglycemic clamp analysis

A HEC was performed in the morning after an overnight fast. To assess peripheral insulin sensitivity, a HEC was performed using an artificial pancreas (STG 55; Nikkiso, Japan), as previously described 12 , 13 . Insulin was infused at a rate of 100 mU/m2/min, and plasma glucose levels were maintained at 5.2 mmol/L (95 mg/dL). A steady‐state insulin concentration of 1,200 pmol/L (200 μU/mL) was achieved. The mean glucose infusion rate (mean GIR) was calculated as the average glucose infusion rate during the final 30 min of the 120‐min clamp procedure. Glucose tracers were not used in the present study; therefore, hepatic glucose production (HGP) was not directly assessed. However, under the present clamp conditions, the contribution of HGP is expected to be minimal 14 . Accordingly, the steady‐state mean GIR was used as an index of peripheral insulin sensitivity.

Body composition

The whole‐body muscle and fat masses were measured on the same day as the HEC using an Inbody 770 body composition analyzer (InBody Japan, Tokyo, Japan).

Assessment of insulin sensitivity

Homeostatic model assessment of insulin resistance (HOMA‐IR) was calculated as [fasting plasma glucose (mmol/L)] × [fasting plasma insulin (μU/mL)]/22.5 15 .

HOMA of β‐cell function (HOMA‐β) was calculated as {20 × [fasting plasma insulin (μU/mL)]}/{[fasting plasma glucose (mmol/L)] − 3.5} 15 .

Fasting insulin concentrations were originally measured in pmol/L and converted to μU/mL (1 μU/mL = 6 pmol/L) for the calculation of HOMA indices.

Measurement of ANXA2

Fasting serum levels of ANXA2 were measured using a commercially available human ANXA2 ELISA kit (Cloud‐Clone Corp., Wuhan, China; Cat. No. SEB944Hu) in accordance with the manufacturer's instructions.

Differentiation of C2C12 skeletal myocytes

Mouse C2C12 myoblasts were obtained from the RIKEN Cell Bank (Tsukuba, Japan) and cultured in high‐glucose (4.5 g/L) Dulbecco's Modified Eagle's Medium (DMEM; Gibco, Billings, MT, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Hyclone, GE Healthcare, Chicago, IL, USA) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin solution (Gibco, USA) at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2.

When the cells reached 100% confluence, the medium was replaced with MEMα containing 2% horse serum (Gibco, USA) to induce differentiation. After 6 days, the medium was switched to MEMα containing 5% horse serum.

Differentiated myotubes were then serum‐starved for 12 h prior to stimulation. Some cells were treated with insulin (3 nmol/L; Sigma, St. Louis, MO, USA) or recombinant human ANXA2 (Cloud‐Clone Corp., Wuhan, China; Cat. No. RPB944Hu01; 1 μg/mL in 0.1% BSA) for 30 min before subsequent assays.

Glucose uptake assay

Glucose uptake was measured as previously described 16 . In brief, C2C12 cells were seeded into 12‐well plates and cultured. Three hours before the assay, the cells were washed once and incubated with 0.5 mL/well glucose‐free medium at 37°C. The cells were then incubated with assay buffer (Hank's buffer containing 5 mmol/L HEPES, 0.1% BSA, pH 7.8) and subsequently treated either with negative control (NC) or ANXA2 (1 μg/mL) for 30 min. This treatment duration was selected based on preliminary time‐course experiments evaluating acute signaling responses. Cells were then treated with or without insulin (3 nmol/L) for 10 min at 37°C. After treatment, 2‐[3H]‐deoxyglucose (0.01 mmol/L) was added for 4 min at 35°C. Cells were washed four times with ice‐cold PBS, lysed, and counted. Lysates were transferred to scintillation vials, and [3H] radioactivity was measured by liquid scintillation counting. Values were normalized to cell number. The experiments were performed with 2–3 technical replicates per condition and repeated independently four times.

Fluorescence microscopy

To evaluate ANXA2 internalization, recombinant human ANXA2 was labeled with Alexa Fluor 594 using the Alexa Fluor™ 594 Microscale Protein Labeling Kit (Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA; Cat. No. A30008) according to the manufacturer's instructions, as described in previous studies using NHS ester–based fluorescent protein labeling techniques 17 .

Differentiated C2C12 myotubes grown on 12‐well plates were treated on day 6 with fluorescently labeled ANXA2 at 5 or 20 μg/mL, or with BSA at 20 μg/mL NC, for 14 h in serum‐free medium. This incubation period was selected to evaluate ANXA2 internalization and intracellular accumulation and to allow sufficient time for endocytic processes.

After incubation, cells were washed three times with PBS and imaged using a BZ‐X700 fluorescence microscope (Keyence, Osaka, Japan) with identical acquisition settings across all groups.

For fluorescence quantification, images were analyzed using ImageJ (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA). Background fluorescence was subtracted using a constant rolling‐ball radius. In each well, five non‐overlapping regions of interest (technical replicates) were randomly selected and measured, and the mean value was calculated to obtain one fluorescence intensity value per well. Data from independent wells were treated as biological replicates. Group differences were analyzed using one‐way anova followed by Dunnett's post hoc test. Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05.

Western blotting

To assess insulin signaling pathways, Western blotting was conducted after treatment with ANXA2 (1 μg/mL) and/or the Src‐kinase inhibitor PP2 (10 μM, Funakoshi, Japan). C2C12 cells were lysed using RIPA buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktails on ice. After centrifugation at 15,000 g for 10 min at 4°C, the supernatants were collected. Proteins were denatured in Laemmli sample buffer at 95°C for 5 min, separated by SDS‐PAGE, and transferred to PVDF membranes. Membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk in TBS‐T for 1 h at room temperature, then incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies against phospho‐IRS‐1 (Tyr612, CST, USA, #2386, 1:1,000), total IRS‐1 (CST, USA, #2382, 1:1,000), phospho‐Akt (Ser473, CST, USA, #9271, 1:1,000), total Akt (CST, USA, #9272, 1:1,000), and α‐tubulin (CST, USA, #2125, 1:1,000). After washing, membranes were incubated with HRP‐conjugated secondary antibodies (anti‐rabbit IgG, CST, USA, #7074, 1:2,500; anti‐mouse IgG, CST, USA, #7076, 1:2,500) for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence substrate and imaged with a ChemiDoc MP system (Bio‐Rad, USA).

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Areas under the curves were calculated using the trapezoidal method. The relationships between clinical parameters and ANXA2 values were evaluated using Pearson's correlation analysis. ImageJ (NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA) was used to quantify band intensities in the Western blot analysis. For comparisons of glucose uptake, fluorescence intensity, and Western blotting data across multiple groups, one‐way anova followed by Dunnett's post hoc test was applied. All statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism version 8.4.1 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA), and EZR was used for additional analyses of clinical parameters 18 . Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05.

Correlation between fasting serum ANXA2 levels and metabolic parameters

Correlation analysis demonstrated a significant positive association between fasting serum ANXA2 levels and the mean glucose infusion rate (mean GIR) (r = 0.76, P = 0.00095), indicating that higher ANXA2 levels are linked to higher peripheral insulin sensitivity (Figure 1a). A post hoc power analysis using G*Power version 3.1.9.7 yielded a statistical power of 0.95 for the observed correlation. In contrast, ANXA2 levels showed a weak, non‐significant negative correlation with HOMA‐IR (r = −0.20, P = 0.46) (Figure 1b). No significant associations were observed between ANXA2 levels and HOMA‐β (r = −0.41, P = 0.13) (Figure 1c) or the C‐peptide index (CPI) (r = −0.33, P = 0.24) (Figure 1d). Taken together, these findings suggest that ANXA2 is associated with peripheral insulin sensitivity, but not with indicators of β‐cell function or insulin secretion.

Regarding musculoskeletal and anthropometric parameters (Figure 2), ANXA2 levels exhibited a weak and non‐significant positive correlation with the skeletal muscle mass index (SMI) (r = 0.28, P = 0.32) (Figure 2c). ANXA2 levels were not significantly associated with waist circumference (r = −0.16, P = 0.58) (Figure 2d). Consistent with these results, no significant correlations were found between ANXA2 levels and total skeletal muscle mass (r = 0.046, P = 0.87) (Figure 2a) or total body fat mass (r = −0.30, P = 0.28) (Figure 2b).

Overall, these findings suggest that circulating ANXA2 levels may be associated with peripheral insulin sensitivity independently of body composition. Although insulin sensitivity is generally influenced by adiposity and waist circumference, ANXA2 showed a significant association with mean GIR under hyperinsulinemic conditions but no association with anthropometric or body composition measures. This pattern indicates that ANXA2 may capture an aspect of insulin action that is not explained by obesity‐related factors.

Effect of ANXA2 on glucose uptake in C2C12 myotubes

Cultured C2C12 myotubes were treated with ANXA2 or insulin, and glucose uptake was measured using [3H]‐2‐deoxy‐D‐glucose. Compared to the NC, both ANXA2 and insulin significantly increased glucose uptake, as shown in Figure 3 (one‐way anova followed by Dunnett's post hoc test, P < 0.05).

The mechanisms of exogenous ANXA2‐induced glucose uptake

Treatment with ANXA2 (1 μg/mL, 30 min) significantly increased IRS‐1 phosphorylation at Tyr612 compared with NC (P = 0.0198, Figure 5). This effect was attenuated by co‐treatment with the Src‐kinase inhibitor PP2, and IRS‐1 phosphorylation was significantly lower in the ANXA2 + PP2 group than in the ANXA2‐treated group alone (P = 0.0294). These findings suggest that ANXA2‐induced IRS‐1 phosphorylation is associated with Src‐kinase‐dependent signaling, although the effect was weaker than that induced by insulin stimulation.

Similarly, ANXA2 significantly enhanced Akt phosphorylation at Ser473 compared with NC (P = 0.0235, Figure 6). This effect was also attenuated by PP2 co‐treatment, and Akt phosphorylation was significantly lower in the ANXA2 + PP2 group than in the ANXA2‐treated group alone (P = 0.0122). These findings suggest that ANXA2‐induced Akt activation occurs, at least in part, through Src‐dependent signaling pathways.

Interestingly, treatment with PP2 significantly inhibited basal phosphorylation levels of both IRS‐1 and Akt (Figures 5 and 6), suggesting that Src‐kinase activity contributes to basal signaling in skeletal myocytes. Collectively, these findings suggest that exogenous ANXA2 may enhance glucose uptake in skeletal myocytes through Src‐dependent IRS‐1/Akt signaling independently of direct insulin receptor stimulation (Figure 7).

DISCUSSION

Annexin A2 (ANXA2) is a member of the annexin family of calcium‐dependent phospholipid‐binding proteins, playing a role in the regulation of cellular growth and in signal transduction pathways 19 . It has been known that subcellular ANXA2 can function with caveolin‐1 in receptor signaling at caveolae, a membrane microdomain, and that caveolin‐1 is involved in insulin receptor signaling 20 , 21 , 22 . In fact, we have previously reported that ANXA2 is one of the mediators of insulin receptor signaling in cultured adipocytes, and that both the mRNA and protein expression of ANXA2 were induced by thiazolidinedione, a peroxisomal proliferator‐activated receptor (PPAR)‐gamma ligand, which leads to insulin sensitization 1 . In addition, recent studies revealed that extracellular ANXA2 in blood circulation functions as a biomarker of cancer development 23 . Therefore, in this study, we hypothesized that serum ANXA2 may have a role in insulin sensitivity.

In the present study, fasting serum ANXA2 was associated with peripheral insulin sensitivity assessed by the HEC in non‐diabetic adults. In contrast, serum ANXA2 was not associated with HOMA‐IR, indices of β‐cell function, or body composition parameters. These findings suggest that fasting serum ANXA2 may be more closely associated with peripheral (muscle) insulin sensitivity than with hepatic insulin action. Under hyperinsulinemic conditions, the steady‐state mean GIR during the final 30 min of the clamp is considered to predominantly reflect peripheral glucose uptake, particularly in skeletal muscle.

Since subcellular ANXA2 can function as a mediator of insulin signaling to stimulate glucose uptake (1), we examined whether extracellular ANXA2 can affect glucose transport into cultured myocytes. Interestingly, we found that exogenous ANXA2 alone increased glucose uptake into C2C12 myotubes, although its effects were weaker than those induced by insulin stimulation (Figure 3). In addition, we found that exogenous ANXA2 was internalized into cells in a time‐ and dose‐dependent manner (Figure 4), possibly through the ANXA2 receptor‐mediated internalization. Although previous studies have reported the involvement of ANXA2 receptors in apoptosis in certain cancer cells, ANXA2 itself has not been shown to directly mediate receptor‐dependent apoptosis 24 . At least under the conditions used in the present study, no apparent cell death was observed (Figure 4). Furthermore, exogenous ANXA2 increased IRS‐1 and Akt phosphorylation in C2C12 skeletal myocytes, and these effects were attenuated by Src‐kinase inhibition (Figures 5 and 6).

Previous studies have reported that intracellular ANXA2 is localized at caveolae, functioning together with caveolin‐1 20 , and that ANXA2 interaction with caveolin‐1 and Src‐kinase has been described in other cell types 25 , 26 , suggesting a possible association between ANXA2 and Src‐dependent signaling. In addition, recent studies have suggested that ANXA2 may also be involved in Akt/mTOR‐related signaling pathways in other cell types and disease models 27 . In fact, it has been reported that Src kinase can phosphorylate IRS‐1 in the extracellular matrix signaling 28 , and these findings are partially consistent with our findings (Figures 5 and 6). These results suggest that exogenous ANXA2 is internalized into skeletal myocytes and may promote Src‐dependent phosphorylation of IRS‐1 and downstream insulin signaling mediators, even in the absence of insulin stimulation. The receptor(s) responsible for extracellular ANXA2 signaling in skeletal muscle cells and the associated upstream signaling mechanism remain unclear. Therefore, the present study could not directly investigate the receptor‐mediated signaling cascade linking extracellular ANXA2 to Src‐dependent IRS‐1/Akt signaling.

Further studies are warranted to identify the responsible receptor(s) and clarify the underlying molecular mechanisms.

Skeletal muscle is a major contributor to basal metabolic rate (BMR) 29 , and reduced BMR has been associated with diabetes risk 30 . Although the mechanisms regulating nutrient uptake into skeletal muscle under fasting conditions remain incompletely understood, the present findings raise the possibility that extracellular ANXA2 may contribute to glucose uptake in skeletal muscle in the absence of exogenous insulin stimulation.

The mechanisms regulating ANXA2 secretion into blood circulation remain unclear. Previous studies have suggested that insulin signaling may be involved in ANXA2 secretion 31 . Further investigations are required to clarify the regulatory mechanisms that govern ANXA2 secretion for a better understanding of glucose homeostasis in muscle under fasting states and the relationship with insulin resistance. In conclusion, these results indicated that fasting serum ANXA2 is associated with peripheral insulin sensitivity in humans, and that exogenous ANXA2 may enhance glucose uptake in association with IRS‐1/Akt signaling and Src‐dependent pathways. Together, these findings support further evaluation of serum ANXA2 as a potential indicator reflecting peripheral insulin sensitivity.

LIMITATIONS

The present study has several limitations. First, the sample size was relatively small and included only Japanese individuals without diabetes, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. The relatively small sample size reflects the invasive and labor‐intensive nature of the HEC procedure, which limited the number of participants who could be enrolled. Because no previous studies have examined the association between circulating ANXA2 levels and clamp‐derived insulin sensitivity, it was difficult to estimate the expected effect size and determine the sample size a priori. Nevertheless, a significant association between serum ANXA2 and mean GIR was observed despite the limited sample size. Second, because glucose tracers were not used in the present study, HGP could not be directly assessed. Therefore, the steady‐state mean GIR during the final 30 min of the clamp should be interpreted as a surrogate index primarily reflecting peripheral glucose uptake under the hyperinsulinemic conditions used in the present study. Future studies using glucose tracers to directly assess HGP are warranted. Third, the receptor(s) and detailed mechanisms underlying the effects of extracellular ANXA2 were not investigated in this study. Further studies with larger cohorts and more detailed mechanistic analyses will be necessary to clarify the clinical and biological significance of extracellular ANXA2.
