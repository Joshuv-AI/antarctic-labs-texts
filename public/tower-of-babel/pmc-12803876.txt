Common Peroneal Nerve Entrapment Neuropathy Involving a Vein―Case Report
Authors: Taisuke YOSHINAGA, Kyongsong KIM, Toyohiko ISU, Juntaro MATSUMOTO, Yasuhiro ITO, Masanori ISOBE, Hiroshi ABE
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Compression neuropathy of the common peroneal nerve commonly occurs around the fibular head, typically caused by fibrous bands or muscle fascia. We report a unique case of a 54-year-old woman with common peroneal nerve entrapment caused by a vein. She suffered from a 6-year history of numbness and weakness in the left lower leg. Conservative treatments were ineffective, and we performed surgical treatment. Surgical neurolysis revealed a vein penetrating and compressing the common peroneal nerve; the common peroneal nerve was decompressed by coagulation and transection. The patient's symptoms resolved after the surgery. Surgeons should be aware of venous structures as potential causes of common peroneal nerve entrapment.

Introduction

Entrapment neuropathy of the common peroneal nerve (CPN) is most common entrapment neuropathies affecting the lower extremities, and typically occurs around the fibular head.1) The patients with CPN entrapment (CPN-E) suffer from sensory disturbances and numbness extending from the lateral aspect of the lower leg to the dorsum of the foot and motor weakness of mainly the tibialis anterior and extensor hallucis longus. Also, intermittent claudication induced by dynamic factors has been reported.1-6) Initial treatment typically involves pharmacological management with non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) and medications for neuropathic pain. For patients with foot drop, physical therapy with ankle-foot orthoses may be employed. In cases refractory to conservative treatment, surgical intervention is considered and has been reported to result in symptom improvement.

As the entrapment site of the CPN, the fibrous band deep to the peroneus longus,7,8) fibrous band surface of the deep head of the peroneus longus,8) peroneal muscle fascia,7,9) confluence of the origin of the soleus and peroneus,7,8) the intermuscular septum9) and the entrance of the fibular tunnel1) has been reported. Additionally, as a dynamic factor, there have been reports suggesting that repetitive knee flexion and extension caused by well-developed leg muscles in athletes can lead to neuropathy.7) It has also been reported that CPN-E is affected by the contraction of the soleus and peroneus longus muscles that occur during ankle movement while walking.3-5) Although there were reports of peroneal nerve palsy caused by deep vein thrombosis,10,11) peroneal nerve entrapment due to a vein is rare. Here, we report that the vein directly compressed the CPN, leading to neuropathy.

Case Report

This 54-year-old woman, who had no history of earlier trauma, accidents, or spinal surgery, suffered from a 6-year history of left lateral lower leg numbness (numerical rating scale [NRS]: 8). Her left anterior tibial muscle was some week (manual muscle testing 4) and Tinel's sign of the CPN around the left fibular head was positive. Her numbness worsened after walking 300 meters (intermittent claudication). There were no changes in symptoms related to standing or sitting positions. Lumbar magnetic resonance imaging did not reveal L5 nerve entrapment and spinal canal stenosis. In electrophysiological findings, the nerve conduction velocity was 44.1 m/s, and the compound muscle action potential (CMAP) amplitude of the left CPN was significantly reduced to 250 μV compared to the right side (2.2 mV). The numbness was in the area of common peroneal neuropathy, but not in the area of the lateral sural cutaneous nerve, and we determined it to be CPN damage near the fibular head. We diagnosed her symptoms as common to peroneal nerve entrapment neuropathy. The patient had been taking oral medication for 3 years, such as mirogabalin, acetaminophen, and NSAIDs, but as there was no improvement in symptoms, surgical CPN neurolysis was performed.

Under local anesthesia, with the patient in the right lateral position, a 4 cm skin incision was made from the dorsal side of the fibular head between the long fibular muscle and the soleus muscle (Fig. 1A). The CPN was detected at the level of the fibular head. Nerve decompression was performed along the CPN. On the entry of the fibular tunnel formed by the peroneus longus muscle, a vein runs between the peroneus longus and the soleus muscle. This vein passed through the CPN and strangulated the nerve (Fig. 1B). There were no findings of varix or venous malformation. For the nerve decompression, the vein was coagulated and transected, and adequate nerve decompression was achieved, with an impression on the nerve observed after vein removal (Fig. 1C).

Postoperatively, her clinical course was uneventful, and her symptoms improved. At the final follow-up period, 8 months after surgery, her numbness improved (NRS 0), and the left anterior tibial muscle was normal.

Discussion

There have been several previous reports of peripheral neuropathy related to veins.12-16) Kelley et al.12) reported two cases in which a developed venous vascular bundle ran shallowly and perpendicular to the nerve at the proximal part of the medial epicondyle, compressing the ulnar nerve and contributing to cubital tunnel syndrome. They speculated that the anatomical structure of the veins of the upper limbs is irregular compared to arteries,17) and this may have been a factor in the symptoms.12) González Porto et al.13) reported a case of carpal tunnel syndrome caused by venous malformation, and Pai et al.16) reported a rare case of Guyon's canal syndrome caused by a venous malformation. As with CPN-E neuropathy, there were reports of deep vein thrombosis being the cause;10,11) however, to the best of our knowledge, there was no previous report of a vein directly compressing the nerve, leading to neuropathy, as in our case.

In previous reports, CPN-E is affected by the dynamic factor of the peroneus longus and soleus muscle contraction, which leads to intermittent claudication.3-5) In our case, the vein ran between the peroneus longus and the soleus muscles and passed through the nerve and strangulating the CPN. The patient suffered from intermittent claudication due to CPN neuropathy, and these findings suggested that the dynamic factor, due to not only muscle contraction but also venous strangulation worsening according to muscle contraction, may affect the CPN neuropathy.

The diagnosis was based on clinical symptoms, the presence of Tinel's sign, and a reduction in CMAP observed during electrophysiological studies. However, according to the report by Morimoto et al.,2) negative findings in electrophysiological studies were observed in 13 out of 22 cases (59.1%), indicating the need for caution when interpreting the results. There have been reports of paralysis of the anterior tibial muscle caused by CPN palsy, which showed significant improvement after surgery,1,7,18-20) similar to the present case. One report19) suggested that favorable outcomes can be achieved when surgery is performed within 12 months of onset. However, in the present case, surgery was performed 6 years after onset, and a favorable outcome was still achieved.

To our knowledge, there have been no previous reports in which symptoms caused by venous compression of the nerve persisted for a clearly documented period of several years, as observed in the present case. Differences in postoperative symptom improvement between arterial and venous compression have been previously reported. In one report,21) arterial compression of a nerve resulted in delayed recovery, which required approximately 6 months. Although there is only one report22) of venous aneurysms, improvement of paralysis has been observed as early as 6 weeks postoperatively. Venous compression, unlike the pulsatile compression caused by an artery, is thought to exert continuous pressure on the nerve.22) In the present case, despite the time elapsed since onset, early postoperative symptom improvement was achieved because the nerve compression was venous in nature.

It has also been noted2) that differentiation from lumbar spinal stenosis should be considered, as the condition resembles L5 nerve root impairment. The patient at our hospital experienced improvement in leg numbness and paralysis following surgical treatment. During surgery for CPN neuropathy, careful intraoperative observation is required, as veins may cause nerve compression.

Conclusion

We report the case of CPN neuropathy attributable to a vein. Surgeons should consider the possibility of venous compression during decompression surgery for CPN neuropathy.
