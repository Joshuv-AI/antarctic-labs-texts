Poster 28. Sex-Specific Implications in Extensor Autograft Selection from an ACL Reconstruction Return-to-Sport Database
Authors: Richard Ma, Chelsea Harrison, Lindsey Colbert, David Echelmeyer, Hongjie Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjective:Patient outcomes following anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction (ACLR) are influenced by a complex relationship of patient demographic, surgical, and postoperative rehabilitation factors. Extensor mechanism-based autografts (e.g., bone-patellar-tendon bone autograft; BPTB, and quadriceps tendon autograft; QT) are popular graft choices for ACLR. There is limited understanding on the rehabilitation impact of type of extensor autograft used in ACLR and patient sex. The objective of this study was to evaluate sex-specific differences around a return to sport assessment in patients following BPTB or QT ACLR.Methods:An IRB approved registry was used for this study. Patients who underwent ACLR with either BPTB or QT autograft and completed a return to sport test (RTS) at a single academic institution were included (male=76 patients; female=92 patients). Demographics including age, prior knee injuries, concomitant injuries, and time from ACLR to RTS evaluation were recorded. In cases where patients completed multiple RTS evaluations, duplicates were removed and the latest RTS assessment was used for analysis. The RTS evaluation included PROMs (e.g., visual analog score, VAS; Single Assessment Numeric Evaluation, SANE; and ACL-Return to Sport after Injury, ACL-RSI), isokinetic strength assessments (e.g., knee strength tests at 90°/sec, 180°/sec, 300°/sec; Biodex Medical Systems), dynamic movement quality screening (e.g., Y-Balance test), and functional performance tests (e.g., single-leg hop, cross-hop, triple-hop, timed-6-meter hop tests). The results of isokinetic strength tests, Y-balance, and hop tests were compared to the contralateral uninvolved limb and expressed as a limb symmetry index (LSI (%), = (Affected Limb Score / Unaffected Limb Score) * 100). The hamstring-to-quadriceps (H:Q) ratio was expressed as a fraction of the hamstring isokinetic strength over quadriceps isokinetic strength at a given angular speed multiplied by 100 (%). Patient's age, VAS, SANE, ACL-RSI, LSI, and H:Q ratios were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Two-sample t-test was used to compare means between groups. Fischer's exact test was used to compare proportions (e.g., % of sex, concomitant meniscus repair, prior injury to ACLR knee, proportion of patients achieving >=90% LSI or >=66% H:Q ratio) between groups. Significance was set as p<0.05.Results:The patient demographic is detailed in Table 1. The male group was older compared to the BPTB group (p=0.02). There were no differences between the groups' proportion of concomitant meniscus repairs, prior knee injury history, and time from surgery to RTS evaluation. There were no significant male and female differences in PROMs for VAS, SANE, and ACL-RSI after BPTB or QT ACLR. In terms of isokinetic strength, there were no significant sex differences after BPTB or QT ACLR. Male BPTB patients were able to achieve greater single-leg hop LSI than females (p=0.049), but both sexes achieved similar cross-hop, triple hop, and timed-6-meter hop LSI. Both sexes also achieved similar Y-balance LSI. There were also no significant differences between proportion of male and female patients achieving >= 90% LSI on isokinetic strength tests or hop tests. When we analyzed the male and female ACLR populations, we found functional differences with graft selection between BPTB or QT autografts. Female patients who had BPTB ACLR had significantly lower H:Q ratios for all three angular speeds when compared to female QT ACLR (Figure 1). Female patients who had BPTB ACLR also had lower LSI for their left composite reach when compared to those with QT ACLR (female BPTB - 92.4 ± 8.84% vs. female QT - 97.01 ± 10.59%, p=0.045). There were, however, no significant differences in proportion of female patients achieving >=90% LSI or >=66% on H:Q ratios after BPTB or QT ACLR. For male patients, we found male BPTB patients had lower H:Q ratio at 300°/sec (male BPTP - 60.49 ± 22.78% vs. male QT - 74.30 ± 27.47%, p=0.047). Male BPTB patients were also less likely to achieve >=90% in H:H LSI at 300°/sec (p=0.03) and >=66% H:Q ratio at 300°/sec (p=0.01) when compared to male QT patients. Despite having lower isokinetic strength values, male BPTB patients had higher single-leg hop LSI (male BPTB - 98.17 ± 7.84%, male QT - 91.57± 12.5%, p=0.03) and were able to achieve >=90% LSI in greater proportion (male BPTB - 93.33% vs. male QT 63.64%, p=0.04).Conclusions:Our study demonstrated that males and females achieved similar PROM and functional outcomes after ACLR using extensor-based autografts. Most of the significant differences seen were between extensor graft selection (e.g., BPTB vs. QT) within the female or male populations. In our study, females and males who had BPTB autografts had more posterior-chain dysfunction compared to QT autograft counterparts after ACLR. This is evident by the lower H:Q ratios seen in both sexes and males less likely to achieve >=90% H:H LSI and >=66% H:Q ratio at higher angular velocities. These sex-specific trends may be influenced by underlying anatomical, hormonal, or biomechanical differences and point to the value of tailoring surgical decision-making not only to individual anatomy and sport demands, but also to sex-related rehabilitation outcomes. These results reinforce the need for personalized graft selection strategies that consider both sex and functional outcomes most relevant to the patient's return-to-sport goals.
