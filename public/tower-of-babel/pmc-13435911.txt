Randomised controlled trial assessing the efficacy of balance acceptance and commitment therapy (ACT) for post-COVID syndrome: a study protocol
Authors: Nicole Carvalho, Michail Kalfas, Caroline Jolley, Gerrard F Rafferty, Oliver Pearson, Richard Emsley, Alan J Metcalfe, Barbara Barrett, Debbie Brewin, Lily Felton, Tasbiha Khan, Nicholas Hart, Emma L Duncan, Timothy Nicholson, Oliver C Witard, Rachael A Evans, Carole Beckwith, Trudie Chalder

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionPost-COVID syndrome (PCS) is an ongoing legacy of the SARS-CoV-2 pandemic. Defined as symptoms persisting beyond 12 weeks following SARS-CoV-2 infection unexplained by an alternative diagnosis, many individuals continue to experience symptoms for months post-infection. Fatigue, breathlessness, cognitive difficulties and sleep disturbances are all commonly reported, frequently impairing daily functioning and quality of life. Acceptance and commitment therapy (ACT), which enhances psychological flexibility and supports values-based behaviour change, has shown effectiveness across long-term health conditions. This protocol describes the evaluation of Balance-ACT as a novel, adapted ACT-based intervention to address the complex physical and psychological needs of people with PCS.Methods and analysisA randomised controlled trial (RCT), co-developed with people with PCS, will evaluate the efficacy and cost-effectiveness of Balance-ACT for PCS. A total of 196 adults with PCS will be recruited from specialist long COVID services within secondary care across NHS organisations in England and randomised 1:1 to Balance-ACT or treatment as usual (TAU). Balance-ACT consists of 10 therapist-delivered sessions delivered over 13 weeks. TAU comprises brief educational videos and a self-help leaflet for COVID-19 recovery. Outcomes will be assessed at 7, 14 and 20 weeks post-randomisation. The primary outcome is health-related quality of life, measured using the SF-36 Physical Component Score at 14 weeks post randomisation. Secondary outcomes include physical health-related quality of life at 20 weeks, perceived fatigue, clinical global improvement, sleep disturbance, cognitive functioning, depressive symptoms, anxiety symptoms, post-exertional malaise, breathlessness, muscle strength, muscle fatigue and heart-rate variability. Mediation analyses will examine whether the intervention leads to changes in psychological flexibility, fear-avoidance beliefs, damage beliefs, embarrassment-avoidance, symptom focusing, muscle strength and physical activity and whether these changes are associated with physical health outcomes. We will also conduct a nested qualitative study to explore participants' views of the intervention. Cost-effectiveness will be assessed by comparing health and social care service costs and quality of life outcomes between Balance-ACT and TAU at 20 weeks.Ethics and disseminationEthical approval has been granted by the NHS Camberwell St Giles Research Ethics Committee (reference 23/LO/0941). The study results will be disseminated in peer-reviewed journals and presented at international conferences.Trial registration numberNCT06231238.Keywords: Fatigue, Randomized Controlled Trial, Post-Acute COVID-19 Syndrome, Quality of Life

Introduction

Post-COVID syndrome (PCS), also known as ‘long COVID’, emerged as a public health concern following SARS-CoV-2 infection.1 The National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) defines PCS as signs and symptoms that develop during or after an infection consistent with COVID-19, which continue for more than 12 weeks, without an alternative explanation.2 The latest self-reported (and uncontrolled) estimates from the Office for National Statistics (March 2024) indicated that 2 million people in the UK were experiencing ongoing symptoms of PCS, 75% of whom report that symptoms adversely affected their day-to-day activities.

The most common reported symptoms of PCS are fatigue, breathlessness, cognitive impairment and sleep disturbances, which all substantially impair daily functioning, employment and health-related quality of life.3–5 These symptoms are associated with increased healthcare visits, including frequent medical appointments and hospital attendance.6 Although PCS imposes a burden on individuals and healthcare services, there are currently few established treatments for the condition, leaving many patients feeling unsupported and isolated.7

Recent research by Fernandez-de-las-Peñas et al8 underscores the complex and multifactorial nature of PCS, highlighting substantial variability in symptom presentation and the influence of pre-existing health conditions on symptom persistence and severity. Identified risk factors for the development of PCS include female sex, greater comorbidity and higher levels of psychological distress.9–11 Conversely, the severity of COVID-19 infection may not predict the development of PCS, and individuals with initially mild infections also appear susceptible to persistent symptoms.12 This clinical uncertainty complicates diagnosis and treatment planning, reinforcing the need for flexible interventions that address both the physical and psychological dimensions of the condition.13

Guidelines from NICE recommend a multidisciplinary rehabilitation approach to PCS, recognising the need for holistic, person-centred interventions.2 Emerging evidence is beginning to identify potentially beneficial interventions.2 A recent systematic review14 reported moderate certainty that cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT) and combined physical and mental health (MH) rehabilitation programmes are likely to improve symptoms of PCS. Despite these promising findings, the evidence base remains limited, and further rigorous evaluation of accessible interventions is warranted. A therapeutic model that may be particularly relevant in this context is acceptance and commitment therapy (ACT), a behavioural intervention that aims to increase psychological flexibility by helping individuals accept difficult thoughts and feelings while engaging in actions aligned with their personal values. ACT has demonstrated effectiveness in improving functioning and well-being in people with long-term health conditions.15 Previous work demonstrates that ACT supports individuals to accept ongoing symptoms that may not be controllable while engaging in meaningful, values-based behaviour change.16

We co-developed an intervention using ACT integrated with the principles of homeostasis, the self-regulating process by which individuals maintain psychological and physiological stability in response to internal and external changes,17 called Balance-ACT.1,8 As disruptions in homeostatic processes may contribute to PCS symptoms,19–22 the intervention is aligned with the principles of ACT (mindfulness strategies and values-based goal setting) alongside helping individuals to establish balance, regulating behaviours such as sleep and physical activity and reducing stress. Improving sleep and reducing stress may indirectly support homeostasis, given their influence on multiple physiological systems.23–27 Mindfulness has also been shown to improve sleep, self-reported fatigue and cognitive function, which are frequently impaired in PCS.28 29

To ensure acceptability, Balance-ACT was co-developed with patients and healthcare professionals through qualitative interviews18 30 and a patient and public involvement (PPI) advisory group. This paper presents a study protocol for Balance-ACT as a novel ACT-based intervention that is specifically tailored to address the complex needs of people with PCS.

Trial objectives

The Balance-ACT trial will assess the efficacy and cost-effectiveness of Balance-ACT (see ‘Main research question’).

Main research question

What is the efficacy and cost-effectiveness of Balance-ACT as a novel psychological intervention versus treatment as usual (TAU, ie, videos and self-help leaflet for COVID-19) in people with PCS.

Research objectives

Primary objective

To investigate the impact of Balance-ACT on self-reported physical health-related quality of life, assessed using the Physical Component Summary (PCS) of the 36-item Short Form Health Survey (SF-36) compared with TAU in people with PCS, at 14 weeks post randomisation.

Secondary objectives

To investigate the impact of Balance-ACT self-reported outcomes (physical health-related quality of life (measured at week 20), perceived fatigue, clinical global improvement, sleep disturbance, cognitive functioning, anxiety symptoms, depressive symptoms, post-exertional malaise and breathlessness) and physical measurements (muscle strength, muscle fatiguability and heart-rate variability) and cost-effectiveness, compared with TAU in people with PCS at 14 weeks post randomisation.

Methods

Study status

Recruitment for this trial began on 04 June 2024. The estimated date for primary completion is 30 May 2026, and the final study completion is expected by 01 August 2026.

Trial design

A single-blind randomised controlled trial (RCT).

Method

196 participants with a diagnosis of PCS will be recruited from specialist long COVID services within secondary care across seven London National Health Service (NHS) Trusts. Participants will be individually randomised with a 1:1 allocation ratio to Balance-ACT or TAU. Follow-up assessments will be conducted at 7, 14 and 20 weeks post randomisation.

Setting

This is a single-centre trial conducted at King’s College London (KCL). Participants are given the option of receiving the intervention either face-to-face at KCL or via Teams, according to their preference, and this will be recorded.

Withdrawal criteria

Participants will be withdrawn from the trial if there are any concerns regarding their continued willingness or ability to provide informed consent. Participants may withdraw from the study at any point of the trial without stating a reason. Participants wishing to withdraw can inform either a member of the research team, comprising the research assistants and trial coordinator, or their Balance-ACT therapist. This information will be communicated to the trial coordinator, who will discuss the available withdrawal options with the participant and complete the relevant withdrawal documentation. Participants may choose to (1) withdraw from all aspects of the study and treatment; (2) discontinue treatment but continue participating in research follow-up assessments; (3) continue treatment while withdrawing from the research follow-up assessments or (4) where appropriate, continue contributing data to the primary outcome measure only. The trial coordinator will ensure that the relevant members of the research team are informed of the participant’s decision. Participant withdrawal forms will be used to document withdrawals and, where possible, the reason for withdrawal.

Planned intervention: Balance-ACT

Balance-ACT is a structured psychological therapy, with flexible/individualised elements, based on ACT, specifically designed to support individuals experiencing PCS. Rooted in ACT principles, Balance-ACT supports individuals in accepting what they cannot change while committing themselves to the things they value and can change (ie, meeting their goals in life and doing these alongside symptoms and distress within reason).

Structure and delivery

Balance-ACT consists of 10 sessions, delivered over a 13-week period. Sessions will be of 60 min duration and conducted weekly. The intervention will be delivered face-to-face or remotely (ie, via Microsoft Teams or telephone), allowing accessibility for participants experiencing fatigue and mobility challenges associated with PCS. Participants in the TAU group will receive a self-help leaflet for COVID-19 recovery, along with a document containing links to 10 brief educational videos supporting recovery after COVID-19.

Patient and public involvement

The intervention has been co-developed with individuals with lived experience of PCS through qualitative interviews18 30 and a PPI advisory group. These insights have shaped the session structure, language and delivery format to ensure feasibility and relevance. In addition, a PPI member with lived experience of PCS is involved throughout the study and attends regular study meetings, including the Trial Steering Committee and Trial Management Group. Their contributions have informed the development of participant-facing materials, recruitment approaches, choice of outcome measures and dissemination planning, and they continue to provide input throughout the study to ensure that the research remains sensitive to the needs and experiences of people living with PCS.

Therapy training

Training was delivered via interactive workshops over 5 days to three therapists (one occupational therapist and two physiotherapists), all with experience of working with people with physical persistent symptoms or PCS. Therapists were informed of the trial protocol which included information on how to deal with any protocol violations and confidential storage of audio/video recordings. Throughout the trial, all records of the therapy sessions will be kept in accordance with the clinical service in which participants are recruited from and in accordance with professional guidelines.

Therapy supervision

All therapists will receive monthly group supervision and ad hoc 1:1 supervision to ensure adherence to the intervention. During supervision, therapists will have the opportunity to discuss clinical issues that are problematic or challenging. In addition, the supervision will check that the manual is largely being followed and ensure that the quality of therapy is sustained.

Treatment fidelity

All therapy sessions will be audio/video recorded for treatment fidelity during the trial. A proportion of recordings will be analysed by two independent clinicians, trained in ACT, once the trial has been completed. As there is no universally applicable ACT fidelity measurement, and the most widely used measurement31 has not been comprehensively validated, fidelity to the therapy will be assessed using the adapted ACT fidelity measure (ACT-FM)32 in conjunction with a bespoke Balance-ACT-specific rating scale. The bespoke Balance-ACT-specific rating scale includes three intervention-specific items: (1) therapist-delivered therapy consistent with the Balance-ACT intervention logic model (addressing the regulation of sleep, activity, stress and emotions); (2) therapist-supported routines, long-term habit formation and behaviour change in line with Balance-ACT targets; and (3) the intervention delivered aimed to restore balance physiologically and psychologically. Each item will be rated on a 5-point scale (0–4), where 0 indicates the behaviour did not occur despite being relevant or necessary for the session; 1 indicates the therapist rarely enacted the behaviour; 2 indicates the therapist sometimes enacted the behaviour; 3 indicates the therapist consistently enacted the behaviour and 4 indicates the therapist consistently enacted the behaviour with a high degree of skill. The bespoke rating scale was developed by the chief investigator (CI), who is an ACT therapist, as well as the therapist supervisor and trainer.

Intervention adherence

The therapists will record how many Balance-ACT sessions out of 10 the participant attended, session modality (face-to-face, video link or telephone) and whether any adverse events occurred. At the end of therapy, the therapist will also score how well the participant adhered to therapy on a 4-point Likert rating scale: participant change, participant adherence to in-between sessions work and the degree to which the participant accepted the model of therapy.

If participants do not attend a session, the therapist will contact the participant by telephone to ascertain the problem regarding attendance and will discuss options regarding how to proceed. This process will be followed up by an email should the participant not respond.

Recruitment

Participants will primarily be recruited from specialist long COVID services within secondary care across NHS organisations in England, including King’s College Hospital NHS Foundation Trust, South London and Maudsley NHS Foundation Trust, Guy’s and St Thomas’ NHS Foundation Trust, Bromley Healthcare, Central and North West London NHS Foundation Trust, University College London Hospitals NHS Foundation Trust and Whittington Health NHS Trust.

Study procedure

Interested participants will be asked to sign an agreement to be contacted by the research team. The research assistants will then be responsible for screening patients to check trial eligibility. Eligible patients will be provided with verbal and written information on the trial. To formally enrol, patients will be required to complete a signed informed consent form. The participant informed consent form is available in online supplemental file 1. Informed consent will be obtained by appropriately trained members of the research team. Once consent is obtained, they will be asked to complete the baseline assessment, which consists of psychological and physical components. Participants will then be randomised to one of two arms: Balance-ACT or TAU (an information leaflet for COVID-19 and a link to 10 videos available online, developed by the South East London Long COVID Programme, Guy’s and St Thomas’ NHS Foundation Trust).

Randomisation

Following verbal and written consent, participants will be randomised in a 1:1 ratio to one of the two trial arms (Balance-ACT or control group (TAU)) within 48 hours of completing the baseline assessment. The randomisation will be conducted by the unblinded study coordinator using the King’s Clinical Trial Unit (KCTU) randomisation system. The randomisation system will be managed by the KCTU and will be hosted on a server within KCL. The system will employ block randomisation with randomly varying block sizes. The details needed for randomisation (month and year of birth, initials and unique participant identity number) will be held in a dedicated database. Following randomisation, the unblinded study coordinator will inform participants of their allocation group by telephone and within one working day. Following allocation to the treatment group, the study coordinator will inform the therapists who will contact the participant to arrange the first session.

Proposed sample size

A total sample size of 196 participants (98 per arm) will be recruited to allow for 15% attrition. This sample provides 80% power to detect a minimally clinically important effect size (Cohen’s d) of 0.4 at a two-sided 5% significance level. The target effect size is informed by existing literature, including a trial of CBT for long COVID reporting an effect size of 0.34 on the SF-36 physical functioning (PF) subscale.33 The anticipated attrition rate was informed by clinical experience within participating specialist PCS services, where patients were actively seeking treatment for persistent symptoms. They were considered likely to remain engaged with both the intervention and study follow-up procedures. To minimise the impact of missing data, all reasonable efforts will be made to retain participants and to ensure completion of at least the primary outcome measure in instances where participants discontinue further involvement in the study.

Data management

Patient data will be pseudo-anonymised, and participants will be given a unique participant number that will be used for identification purposes. Identifiable information (eg, name, contact details) will be filed securely in a separate password-protected document on the KCL OneDrive. Data will be stored in line with the General Data Protection Regulation (UK GDPR) and the Data Protection Act and archived according to Sponsor requirements. A web-based electronic data capture (EDC) system will be designed (MACRO). The EDC will be created in collaboration with the trial statistician and the CI and will be maintained by the KCTU for the duration of the project. The EDC will be hosted on a dedicated server within KCL. Data entry and management will be undertaken by the research assistants, and random checks will be undertaken by the study coordinator. The CI will be the custodian for the trial dataset.

Blinding

All research assistants assigned to collect outcome data will be blinded to participants’ allocated arm. The CI (TC) will also be blinded to allocation. The senior statistician will be blinded until review of the statistical report; the trial statistician was initially blinded, then became unblinded from preparing Data Monitoring and Ethics Committee and Trial Steering Committee (DMEC/TSC) reports from May 2025. The study coordinator and the Balance-ACT therapists are the only other members of the study team who will be unblinded, to ensure the participants randomised to the intervention receive the intervention in a timely fashion.

Data collection and follow-ups

Outcome measurements will be collected at baseline (pre-randomisation) and at 7, 14 and 20 weeks post randomisation. A ±21 day window around each scheduled follow-up assessment will be permitted for data collection. Participants will be asked to complete self-report measures in writing, electronically via Qualtrics, or in special circumstances, over the telephone. If participants decline to complete the full follow-up assessment but have not revoked their consent to be followed up, they will be asked to fill in only the primary outcome questionnaires. Participants who do not return their questionnaires will be contacted by the Research Assistants via telephone/email to remind them to complete and return their questionnaires.

Primary outcome measure

The primary outcome will be assessed using the PCS of the 36-item Short Form Health Survey (SF-36) at week 14. The study will administer the RAND 36-Item Health Survey,35 which contains the same items as the SF-36.36 Scoring will follow the SF-36 manual to compute the PCS. The SF-36 is a widely used questionnaire designed to assess health-related quality of life.37 It consists of 36 items that measure eight different domains of health: PF, role physical (RP), bodily pain (BP), general health (GH), vitality (VT), social functioning (SF), role emotional (RE) and MH. These eight domains can be further summarised into two distinct concepts: a physical dimension, represented by the PCS, and a mental dimension, represented by the Mental Component Summary (MCS).

Statistical analysis

A statistical analysis plan will be developed by the statisticians and agreed with the CI, senior statistician and DMEC/TSC before database lock. The analysis will be reported in line with the CONSORT guidelines with the 2018 extension for reporting social and psychological intervention trials.66 Descriptive statistics will be used to summarise assessments of recruitment, drop-out and completeness of therapy.

The analysis will follow intention-to-treat principles whereby participants are included in the treatment group to which they were randomised. Hypothesis tests will be two-sided and estimated treatment effect will be accompanied by a 95% CI. For all analyses, a significance level of 5% will be used. Methods for handling missing data will be prespecified in the ‘Statistical analysis plan.

The primary analysis will be a mixed effects linear regression of the PCS of the SF-36. Fixed effects will be recruitment source, baseline assessment for the outcome under investigation, random allocation, time and time*random allocation interactions. Participants will be included as random intercepts. For continuous secondary outcomes the same approach will be followed, and for binary secondary outcomes logistic mixed models will be used. The minimum number of therapy sessions for the per protocol analysis will be 5.

Moderation analysis

Moderation analyses will involve adding interaction terms between random allocation, time and the respective moderators. The treatment effect difference between levels of the moderator will be interpreted as the difference in estimated treatment effects between participants with baseline moderator values of a+1 and a. This will be reported in a secondary paper, separate from the primary trial paper. Additional exploratory analyses may also be conducted to investigate other potential moderators of treatment response where clinically or theoretically justified.

Participants are offered the option of receiving the intervention either face-to-face or via Microsoft Teams. Although this factor was not included in the original analysis plan, an exploratory moderator analysis will be conducted, in a secondary paper, to assess whether treatment effects vary according to mode of intervention delivery.

Thematic analysis

The qualitative interviews will be transcribed verbatim and analysed inductively using reflexive thematic analysis as outlined by Braun and Clarke68 69 following their six-phase approach. Consistent with Braun and Clark’s later work,69 data analysis will be an active and reflexive process, with themes developed through interpretative engagement with the dataset rather than emerging passively.

Economic evaluation

A health economics analysis plan will be developed by the economists and agreed with the grant holders (CJ, ELD, NH, GFR, TN, OCW) before database lock. The analysis aims to compare service use and costs between Balance-ACT versus TAU and assess the cost-effectiveness of Balance-ACT in relation to TAU. Service use data will be collected using the AD-SUS and combined with unit costs to calculate total health and social care costs. The cost of the Balance-ACT intervention will be estimated using standard costing procedures. Quality-adjusted life-years (QALYs) will be derived from EQ-5D-5L data using the NICE recommended approach. Cost-effectiveness will be evaluated by combining costs and outcomes, with results presented as incremental cost-effectiveness ratios and cost-effectiveness acceptability curves.

Procedures for recording and reporting serious adverse events

Adverse events (AEs) will be assessed at 7, 14 and 20 weeks after randomisation. All serious adverse events (SAEs) and serious adverse reactions (SARs) will be reported immediately to the CI, sponsor and DMEC/TSC. The reporting of SAEs will be based on the following criteria: (1) death of a participant, (2) life-threatening event, (3) hospitalisation or prolonged hospitalisation, (4) persistent or significant disability or incapacity, (5) significant psychological distress, (6) the disclosure of illegal activity, (7) breaches of security and/or data breaches and (8) safeguarding concerns. Given the psychological nature of the intervention, anticipated harms are considered low risk; however, potential adverse effects may include temporary emotional distress or worsening of symptoms during participation. Harms may be identified through participant self-report, therapist observation or communication with the research team during study procedures and intervention sessions.

The study researchers will discuss any concerns about participants’ well-being with the CI (TC) who will decide on the appropriate course of action. If there are any safety concerns, TC or the respiratory physicians (NH, CJ) will be consulted, and the participant’s healthcare professional (GP) will be contacted. If a participant states that they are actively suicidal and have plans to harm themselves, they will be assessed by a member of the team. A plan will be agreed to ensure the safety of the participant. All procedures relating to risk management will be conducted in line with the policies and procedures of the relevant participating NHS Trust from which the participant is recruited. Any distress experienced by participants will be formally assessed during the in-person study assessments, at baseline (W0), mid-treatment (W7), post-intervention (W14) and follow-up (W20). Spontaneous AEs reported during therapy sessions will also be recorded.

Discussion

PCS presents an ongoing public health challenge, with many affected individuals experiencing persistent symptoms that impair daily functioning, employment and healthcare utilisation. Despite this substantial burden, effective treatments remain limited, highlighting the urgent need for evidence-based interventions and rigorous studies assessing the effectiveness of available care.

This paper outlines the study protocol for a RCT evaluating the efficacy and cost-effectiveness of a novel psychological intervention for PCS. By targeting the behavioural and physiological mechanisms underlying PCS symptoms, Balance-ACT aims to enhance quality of life and overall functioning. Importantly, the intervention is not designed to address all underlying causes of PCS; rather, it seeks to support individuals in developing adaptive coping strategies and engaging in meaningful activities to facilitate recovery. If effective, this intervention could not only improve patient outcomes but also yield long-term economic benefits for the NHS.

Strengths and limitations of the study

The study has several strengths, including its randomised controlled design and the use of a theory-driven intervention targeting both behavioural and physiological processes associated with PCS. The inclusion of a health economic evaluation will also enable assessment of the potential cost-effectiveness of the intervention within NHS services.

Several limitations should also be considered. Participant retention and intervention adherence may be challenging given the fluctuating and heterogenous nature of PCS symptoms. Follow-up completion and intervention adherence will be recorded and summarised, and sensitivity analyses (including a per protocol analysis) will be conducted to explore the impact of intervention adherence. In addition, the study relies primarily on self-reported outcome measures, which may introduce reporting bias. Variability in symptom severity and presentation across participants may also influence treatment response and interpretation of findings. Strategies such as flexible participation (e.g. remote follow-up options and scheduling flexibility) and tailored intervention delivery have been incorporated to mitigate these challenges; however, these factors should be considered when interpreting the study outcomes.

As participants and therapists cannot be blinded to treatment allocation, there is a potential for expectancy bias, particularly for self-reported outcomes such as the SF-36 Physical Component Score, which is a self-reported measure of health-related quality of life. Importantly, participant expectations may influence outcomes in either direction, as individuals may hold positive, neutral, or negative beliefs regarding the likely effectiveness of the intervention. Similarly, participants allocated to TAU may have either positive or negative expectations regarding their recovery. To minimise the risk of bias, outcome assessors and the CI remain blinded to treatment allocation. The research team will also maintain a position of equipoise throughout recruitment, informing participants that there is genuine uncertainty regarding the relative effectiveness of the study intervention. To facilitate interpretation of the results, both subjective and objective outcome measures will be reported, and associations between these measures will be explored. Examining the extent to which self-reported improvements correspond with objective indicators of functioning will provide additional insight into the robustness of the observed treatment effects.

A further limitation relates to the inability to distinguish the specific effects of the Balance-ACT intervention from the non-specific effects associated with regular therapist contact. Participants allocated to the intervention receive up to ten therapist-delivered sessions over 13 weeks, whereas those in the TAU group receive substantially less contact with healthcare professionals. Consequently, any observed benefits may reflect, in part, the effects of therapeutic attention, support, validation and ongoing engagement rather than the specific therapeutic components of Balance-ACT itself. This is an inherent limitation of pragmatic trials comparing a complex intervention with usual care. Findings will therefore be interpreted with caution, and any observed treatment effect will be considered as representing the combined impact of the intervention and the additional therapist contact. Further research may benefit from the inclusion of an attention-matched comparator to better isolate the specific effects of the intervention.

If effective, the findings could inform future clinical guidelines and support the development of tailored interventions for PCS. Further research will be needed to explore the long-term sustainability of intervention effects and explore potential integration with other treatment approaches, including pharmacological options.
