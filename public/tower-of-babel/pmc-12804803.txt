Deep learning based real-time prediction of depth of penetration during activated tungsten inert gas welding of 10 mm thick 316LN stainless steel
Authors: N. Chandrasekhar, M. Vasudevan, C. R. Das
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
A deep learning-based approach has been developed for real-time weld pool semantic segmentation and predicting the depth of penetration (DOP) during activated tungsten inert gas (A-TIG) welding of 10 mm thick 316LN stainless steel plate. A custom U-Net framework, with VGG19 as the encoder, was used for semantic segmentation of weld pool images in real-time. Model development involved three main steps: semantic segmentation of the weld pool using a convolutional neural network (CNN), surface profile measurement and DOP prediction using a back-propagation neural network (BPNN). The CNN model exhibited excellent accuracy on the segmented images. Key features were extracted from the segmented images and along with the welding current were used as inputs to the BPNN while the measured DOP values were used as output to train the BPNN. All the three steps were combined to predict the depth of penetration in real-time. The model’s execution time was found as 110 ms. Replacing the encoder with EfficientNet-B0 reduced the execution time to 70 ms. A Python-based graphical user interface (GUI) was developed to monitor the depth of penetration as a function of weld distance in real-time. Excellent agreement was achieved between the predicted and measured values.

Introduction

In recent years, TIG welding has become essential in the fabrication of stainless steels, providing precise heat input control to minimize distortion and prevent carbide precipitation1. The process finds wider application for large component fabrication in power generation, chemical and pharmaceutical industries. The Activated Tungsten Inert Gas (A-TIG) welding process is a variant of the Gas Tungsten Arc Welding (GTAW) process that enhances weld penetration and quality, particularly for high-alloy materials such as stainless steel, titanium, and nickel alloys. The key feature of A-TIG is the application of an activating flux on the weld surface, which reduces molten pool surface tension, increases fluidity, and enables deeper penetration without requiring higher current. The flux also concentrates heat at the weld zone, improving heat transfer and reducing the size of the heat-affected zone (HAZ). As a result, A-TIG produces greater penetration depth and aspect ratio compared to conventional TIG, making it suitable for precise and high-productivity welding applications2,3. The current research focuses on optimizing flux composition, application methods, automation, and process control to further improve weld quality and efficiency. Advanced flux formulations aim to minimize oxidation, enhance corrosion resistance, and reduce post-weld cleaning. The process performance is influenced by factors such as flux type, welding parameters (current, speed, arc length), and workpiece chemistry. Studies have shown that optimized fluxes improve weld pool behavior, material dilution, slag reactions, and overall penetration depth, contributing to higher process stability and versatility4–6. Moreover, weld quality is primarily determined by welding current, speed, shielding gas flow and arc gap, which directly affect weld penetration depth and bead shape. Skillful control of these variables is critical for achieving strong and defect-free welds7. On the other hand, quality welds increase the weld strength and durability. In particular, penetration depth is crucial for ensuring a strong bond between the base material and the weld metal, directly impacting the joint’s strength and integrity8. Insufficient penetration can weaken the weld, while excessive penetration can cause burn-through. Hence, achieving a consistent penetration depth is essential for meeting industry standards and ensuring the long-term reliability of welded components9. Recent advancements in real-time monitoring and control of welding processes have addressed several challenges by integrating deep learning techniques and vision systems. Deep learning (DL) has emerged as a powerful alternative to traditional machine learning (ML) methods such as support vector machines, decision trees, and random forests. Unlike these approaches, which rely on manual feature extraction and domain expertise, DL models such as convolutional and recurrent neural networks can automatically learn hierarchical features directly from raw data. This enables accurate modeling of complex, nonlinear, and high-dimensional relationships while reducing pre-processing requirements. With advances in GPU computing and network optimization, DL offers superior accuracy, adaptability, and real-time performance for monitoring, prediction, and control in complex industrial processes such as welding10. This approach enhances weld quality and increases productivity by dynamically adjusting welding parameters based on sensor feedback, enabling immediate corrective action and anticipatory tuning11. Moreover, Baek et al.12 extensively utilize the vision system for real-time weld penetration depth estimation, offering an accurate, non-contact approach to quality assessment, thereby addressing these challenges. In addition, other sensors, including CMOS cameras, infrared cameras, laser sensors and acoustic sensors, are also employed to monitor and control penetration depth, ensuring consistent and quality welds13. Among the key technologies, Kang et al.14 highlighted the CMOS cameras as a key technology. These cameras provide adequate resolution and contrast imaging, allowing precise weld depth measurement even in challenging lighting conditions. Similarly, HDR cameras are designed to handle extreme light variations in dynamic welding environments, capturing bright plasma arcs and dark molten pools. With a dynamic range of 100 to 120 dB, these cameras provide clear, high-quality images even in challenging lighting conditions. The High Dynamic Range (HDR) cameras are specifically designed to handle the extreme light variations present in dynamic welding environments, effectively capturing both bright plasma arcs and dark molten pools. With a dynamic range of 100–120 dB, these cameras can produce clear, high-quality images even under challenging illumination conditions. However, conventional HDR imaging relies on multi-exposure acquisition, which introduces processing delays and makes it unsuitable for real-time welding analysis. Moreover, fluctuations in the molten pool can cause artifacts such as ghosting, reducing the accuracy of weld pool boundary detection15. To overcome these limitations, a Cavitar CMOS weld camera equipped with a laser light source and a 600–700 nm optical band-pass filter was employed. This filter isolates the weld pool’s emission and suppresses arc glare, providing high contrast in a single frame and enabling faster, more accurate boundary detection than computational approaches like exposure adjustment or post-processing. Mehta et al.16 focused on adaptive welding, addressing the challenge of deviations between nominal and actual manufacturing conditions. They emphasized the need for automated and robotic systems to sense the welding process, extract relevant sensor data, predict process responses to adjustments and optimize welding parameters. The research also highlighted the potential of artificial intelligence, particularly deep learning, to automate the learning process and adapt welding systems by using experimental data while also focusing on human-robot collaboration for further adaptation. Nomura et al.17 applied a machine learning (ML) model to molten pool extraction in TIG welding, achieving precise, real-time predictions of penetration depth. Also, the weld penetration assessment was developed by automating welding data analysis using ML, thereby allowing for accurate prediction of penetration depth. ML models are beneficial as they adapt to varying welding conditions, enabling real-time monitoring and improving quality control.

Additionally, Saeed et al.18 proposed a robust method for estimating penetration depth in GTAW using CNNs trained on weld pool images. Their research explores how various elements within the weld pool affect prediction outcomes and refines the CNN architecture by optimizing hyperparameters. Moreover, in Metal Inert Gas (MIG) welding, the presence of spatter and fume emissions presents substantial challenges for image-based weld pool analysis. Spatter particles introduce optical noise and false visual features in captured images, necessitating the development of specialized image-processing algorithms to effectively remove these artifacts. Additionally, frequent short-circuiting and spray transfer events lead to rapid fluctuations in arc intensity, while the bright arc plasma and molten droplet transfer obscure the weld pool region. Collectively, these factors complicate the semantic segmentation of weld pool images. In contrast, the Wolfram Inert Gas (WIG) welding process provides a more stable and cleaner arc environment, making it better suited for deep learning–based image segmentation and real-time monitoring applications19. In addition to these methods, Wang et al.20 developed a data-driven model to estimate the backside width of the weld pool, which is a key indicator of penetration in GTAW. Using computer vision to capture 3D weld pool features, they determined weld pool width, trailing length and surface height. These factors were found to be key in predicting backside width. As a result, they demonstrated its potential for closed-loop control of penetration during welding. Cai et al.8 present a deep learning method for predicting penetration depth by analyzing topside weld pool images. This approach employs semantic segmentation with ResNet to extract weld pool shapes, which are then input into a BPNN regression model for accurate depth prediction. Yu et al.21 investigated the monitoring of weld pool boundaries during welding processes to enhance tracking and data collection. They propose a U-Net-based deep learning model designed for accurate image segmentation, even with limited training data. The model is trained to detect weld pool boundaries under different welding currents and speeds, showing strong performance and reliability for real-time monitoring. For instance, Xu et al.22 introduced a polar coordinate model to geometrically characterize the weld pool and identify its parameters in real-time. Due to the challenges posed by non-linear optimization, a neural network was employed to assist in parameter identification. The system employed pulsed laser illumination to capture clear images of the weld pool. In contrast, an image processing algorithm was used to extract the pool’s boundary, leading to a real-time system for monitoring weld penetration. Zhang et al.23 developed a vision-based system to measure the 3D specular surface of the weld pool in gas tungsten arc welding, which mimics a skilled welder’s ability to observe the process. By projecting a dot-matrix laser onto the weld pool and capturing the reflected pattern on a distant plane, the system analyzes the deformation of the pattern, allowing the surface to be reconstructed at a speed of 3.04 milliseconds per frame. The method was validated through simulations and physical tests and confirmed to be accurate and robust, thereby paving the way for more intelligent welding process monitoring.

Although real-time monitoring of welding processes has advanced considerably, real-time prediction of weld penetration depth using embedded hardware remains challenging and requires further development. The primary objective of this study is to develop a system for real-time monitoring and prediction of depth of penetration during TIG welding that will ensure consistent depth of penetration (DOP) if feedback control is enabled. Among process parameters, welding current has the greatest influence on DOP, making it the most suitable variable for feedback control. While welding speed and gas flow rate affect mechanical properties such as tensile strength and hardness24, their impact on DOP is relatively minor. Therefore, this study focuses on maintaining consistent penetration depth by dynamically adjusting the welding current, ensuring process stability under varying conditions. Excluding welding speed and gas flow rate simplifies the control system without compromising DOP regulation or process efficiency. To achieve this, a deep learning based approach is proposed for real-time DOP prediction using embedded hardware during TIG welding of 10 mm thick plates. The approach involves developing a CNN model for weld pool image segmentation, extracting features from the segmented images, and constructing a BPNN model for DOP prediction. These components will be integrated for real-time inference on an NVIDIA Jetson Nano platform. Additionally, a Python-based GUI will be developed to display the predicted DOP in real-time as a function of weld distance.

Materials and methods

Welding experiments were carried out to generate a dataset of weld pool images that would be used to train the CNN model, which is being proposed for monitoring and predicting the weld penetration depth in real-time. To accomplish this, a series of bead-on-plate A-TIG welding experiments were performed on 316LN stainless steel plate with the size of 300 × 250 × 10 mm. The chemical composition of the material and welding parameters are shown in Table 1. The experiments were conducted on a 6-axis articulated robotic TIG welding system which is shown in Fig. 1 and the welding parameters are shown in Table 2. The welding current was varied from 90 to 300 A to achieve different weld penetration depths. The welding length was constant at 100 mm to ensure consistency across all welding trials. The travel speed was kept constant at 60 mm per minute, with an arc gap of 1 mm. A 2% thoriated tungsten electrode with a tip angle of 30° was used to ensure arc stability. Pure argon was employed as the shielding gas to minimize contamination while flowing at a rate of 10 L per minute. The welding current was varied from 90 to 300 A to achieve different penetration depths. After completing the welds, the weldments were cut transversely in the welding direction and the samples were extracted. These samples were then polished and electrochemically etched using a 10% oxalic acid solution to enable precise measurement of the DOP. The macro cross-sections of the weld samples are shown in Fig. 2. The measured penetration depths for each welding current are reported in Table 3. These measurements will enable the correlation of welding parameters with penetration depth, thereby assisting in developing the CNN model. A Cavitar CMOS welding camera with an inbuilt laser light source was mounted on the robotic welding torch and used to capture the real-time weld pool images. This arrangement will maintain the relative motion of the welding speed. The process of capturing real-time images is explained in the following “Image acquisition using embedded hardware” section.

Image acquisition using embedded hardware

A custom adjustable fixture mounted the welding camera to the welding torch, ensuring consistent focal distance and proper alignment for real-time image capture. The camera was precisely adjusted to enhance the field of view and image sharpness, with a focal distance of 220 mm and an angle of 27° for optimal image quality. The camera was configured to capture images at a resolution of 550 × 550 × 3 pixels and a 12-bit depth, recording at a rate of 30 frames per second (fps). Additionally, the camera features an integrated laser light source, emitting at 645 nm with a power of 5 mW. The camera details are shown in Table 4. During welding, the laser was directed at the molten pool to reduce the intense brightness of the plasma arc. This improved the clarity of the captured images and made it easier to distinguish the edges of the molten pool. The portable industrial Jetson Nano embedded hardware involved in capturing the real-time welding images and specifications is shown in Table 5. It is designed to capture images and execute the deep learning models and it contains a 128 core GPU and a CPU. The GPU improves real-time image processing execution and the CPU manages the overall system performance to ensure seamless operation. Instead of relying solely on a CPU, the embedded hardware leverages the GPU to increase deep learning and image processing performance. The welding camera is connected through a gigabit Ethernet communication port and the display unit, keyboard and mouse are integrated with the respective ports. Preprocessing of the image

The image preprocessing techniques were used to prepare the dataset for training the CNN model, including resizing, histogram equalization and normalization. The weld images were initially captured at a resolution of 550 × 550 × 3 to provide a detailed view of the weld pool. To meet the CNN model’s input requirements, the images were resized and the channel was converted to 224 × 224 × 3 using bi-cubic interpolation. This technique improves image quality by considering neighboring pixels, reducing artifacts and preserving crucial image information for precise scaling. Further, histogram equalization was used to improve image contrast, highlighting important features, while normalization adjusted pixel values to a consistent range, helping to avoid gradient-related problems during the training process. Applying these preprocessing steps optimized the model’s feature extraction, leading to fast learning and better overall performance. This approach also contributed to faster convergence and improved the model’s generalization ability. Python 3.9 was used to implement the model and to fully achieve optimal performance and compatibility.

Dataset preparation

From the 12 bead-on-plate A-TIG welding experiments, a dataset was generated for training the CNN model. Each welding trial involves a 100 mm weld at a travel speed of 60 mm per minute. The welding process was recorded at 30 fps to generate 3000 images per welding trial as reported by Jiao et al.25. The dataset was separated into two parts. 80% of the data was designated for training and 20% for testing. Details of the dataset are shown in Table 6 and additional experiments were performed, as discussed in the next section, to validate the model.

U-Net framework

The U-Net-based CNN model is most suitable for semantic segmentation tasks due to its specialized architecture. This architecture allows for the precise and efficient extraction of spatial features from images. The encoder-decoder framework, complemented by skip connections, preserves fine details and captures broader contextual information, making it highly effective for applications like weld pool segmentation. U-Net’s capability to perform pixel-level segmentation ensures that crucial features are clearly defined and it excels in real-time welding applications, providing both speed and accuracy.

VGG19 architecture

In this task, VGG19 was employed for semantic segmentation. The VGG19 architecture is effective for semantic segmentation due to its deep structure. It consists of 16 convolutional layers and 3 fully connected layers. For segmentation tasks, the fully connected layers are typically substituted with convolutional layers, which allows for dense predictions at the pixel level. The networks consist of 3 × 3 convolution filters, which help to capture the fine details across various hierarchical levels, which is most significant for segmenting the weld pool.

EfficientNet-B0 architecture

EfficientNet-B0 is a lightweight model that performs well without requiring much computing power and uses a method that scales its depth, width and resolution in a balanced way to maintain both efficiency and accuracy. It includes special building blocks that reduce computation. At the same time, added layers, shortcut connections and the Swish activation function help the model learn detailed features and train faster26. It also uses global average pooling to lower the number of parameters and shrink the model’s size, making it especially useful for real-time welding applications in limited-resource settings9.

Results and discussion

Weld pool semantic segmentation using VGG19 architecture as an encoder

In semantic segmentation, pixels are grouped into categories, enabling precise classification of visual features. For evaluating weld penetration depth, it’s important to segment the molten pool accurately. This helps in predicting the depth of the weld. Thus, semantic segmentation was chosen for the task. Each weld pool image had to be paired with labeled images for proper training. The labels were generated manually using ImageJ software by outlining the weld pool region for each class. In the proposed CNN model, a custom U-Net-based architecture was designed for weld pool segmentation. The model follows a U-Net structure, consisting of an encoder-decoder framework, with VGG19 as the encoder. The deep, 19 layer architecture of VGG19 contributes to extracting high-level features from the weld pool images, enabling the encoder to capture abstract representations. These extracted features are then passed through the network bottleneck and decoded into a segmentation mask, with skip connections ensuring the preservation of spatial details. This design greatly improved the accuracy of the weld pool segmentation. The decoder is responsible for reconstructing the segmented weld image to its original dimensions, while also incorporating fine-grained details from the encoder via skip connections. Figure 6 provides a schematic representation of the segmentation pipeline, mapping the transformation from the raw weld image to the segmented weld pool image. To assess the segmentation performance, the Intersection over Union (IoU) metric was used, providing a reliable measure of the overlay between the predicted and actual segmentation masks. Despite the strengths of VGG19’s deep architecture, its large number of parameters could lead to over-fitting. To mitigate this, the model included dropout and batch normalization techniques, which helped prevent over-fitting and improved the model’s generalization capabilities. The hyperparameters of the model are presented in Table 7.

Further, several data augmentation techniques were applied to enhance generalization and reduce over-fitting. These techniques included rotating images, shifting width and height, adjusting contrast and using fill-mode, which fills any uncovered regions created by transformations with suitable pixel values. These augmentation methods were designed to simulate the real-world variations typically seen in welding environments and make the model more robust to such changes. Additionally, a learning rate scheduler was employed during training to dynamically adjust the learning rate based on the model’s performance. If the loss remained unchanged for five consecutive epochs, the specified factor reduced the learning rate. This adaptive strategy prevented the model from becoming stuck in local minima and promoted stable convergence by adjusting the learning rate only when necessary.

Image feature extraction

The dataset for training the BPNN was generated from extracted weld image features using the segmented weld pool image. It was used to predict the DOP. These features include weld pool width, length, area and centroid, calculated in pixels. The significance of these parameters is crucial for assessing weld DOP, an essential factor influencing the weld integrity and its quality. The weld pool width reflects the extent of the molten pool’s spread along the plate, with a consistent width indicating stable welding conditions. However, variations in width may lead to inconsistent arc length or welding speed, which may influence the DOP. The weld pool length represents the molten material’s movement along the plate, which influences fusion between the base and molten material. A longer weld pool length may cause overheating, while a shorter length could indicate insufficient heat input, hindering proper fusion. The weld pool area, which measures the size of the molten pool, is directly linked to heat input distribution and plays a significant role in determining the DOP. Finally, the weld pool centroid is essential for ensuring the weld bead’s symmetry and stability. Any shift in the centroid may signal instability in the arc or misalignment of the torch, disrupting heat distribution and potentially affecting penetration. These four features were extracted from the segmented weld pool image and used as inputs to the BPNN along with welding current since it is a critical parameter influencing the depth of penetration. The BPNN is trained to predict penetration depth based on these features. The weld image, ground truth, segmented weld pool image and segment overlay are shown in Fig. 9a–d. Back-propagation neural network

Back-propagation is a pivotal algorithm to train neural networks by adjusting weights and biases to minimize the error between predicted and actual outputs. It involves a forward pass where input data is processed, followed by a backward pass that computes gradients of the loss function concerning the network’s parameters using the chain rule. These gradients are then used to update the weights through an optimization method like gradient descent and this process is repeated until the model converges to a solution that generalizes well to new data. However, the BPNN model was set up with 5 input features, a single hidden layer containing 12 neurons and 1 output, as detailed parameters are shown in Table 8.

Performance evaluation by predicting the accuracy and losses of network

The schematic diagram of the BPNN with input, output and hidden layers and neurons is shown in Fig. 10 for predicting the DOP. To avoid over-fitting, a 30.2% dropout rate was used during training to randomly deactivate some neurons in each iteration, ensuring the model does not depend too much on specific neurons. Furthermore, L2 regularization with a strength of 0.00395 was applied to penalize large weights, improving generalization and stopping the model from fitting to noise. Additionally, a learning rate of 0.0005 was selected for slow convergence, allowing the model to train gradually. The plots of accuracy and loss as a function of epochs are shown in Figs. 11 and 12 respectively. Training continues for 800 epochs, giving the model enough time to learn. Since the patience parameter is set to 50, the training stops early if the testing performance does not improve for 50 consecutive epochs. This helps to avoid unnecessary computation and over-fitting. These settings are intended to keep the model balanced, preventing both under-fitting and over-fitting, while focusing on generalizing well and achieving optimal performance over time. With a learning rate of 0.0005, both training and testing metrics improved gradually. Specifically, the training MSE ranged from 0.0514 to 0.0673, while the testing MSE varied from 0.0565 to 0.0733, showing that the model generalized well. The R² values for both training and testing remained high, around 0.989 and 0.987, which shows the model effectively learned the relationships in the data. Dropout reduced over-fitting by randomly turning off neurons and L2 regularization prevented over-fitting by limiting large weights. Moreover, training over 800 epochs, with early stopping at epoch 339, allowed the model to learn efficiently and avoid unnecessary computation. The model achieved high accuracy and low error on both training and testing sets, proving the effectiveness of the selected hyperparameters like learning rate, dropout rate, L2 regularization and early stopping. As a result, these settings allowed the model to learn from the data and perform well on new and unseen data.

Semantic segmentation of the weld pool using U-Net framework with EfficientNet-B0 as an encoder

Semantic segmentation starts with a weld image input, typically sized at 224 × 224 × 3. Then, it is passed through an encoder based on EfficientNet-B0. EfficientNet-B0 scales well, using a combination of depth-wise separable convolutions and point-wise convolutions in its mobile inverted bottleneck convolution MBConv blocks to reduce computational time and achieve high performance. These convolutional layers extract features by progressively reducing the spatial size while increasing the depth of the feature maps. EfficientNet-B0 uses an initial convolutional layer to capture weld image boundaries, followed by MBConv blocks to process low-level and high-level features efficiently. Skip connections in these blocks enable the model to retain important spatial information at multiple resolutions. During the encoding phase, the weld image size progressively reduces as it passes through multiple convolutions and pooling layers. The size reduction allows the model to focus on high-level, global features, while the increased depth of feature maps enables the capture of more detailed weld image features. This hierarchical feature extraction is significant for segmenting the weld pool boundaries. The feature maps are gradually up-sampled to their original dimensions in the decoder phase. The decoder uses skip connections from the encoder to bring in finer details that were lost during down sampling, allowing the precision of the weld pool segmentation to be enhanced. This process is essential for pixel-level accuracy in weld pool boundary segmentation tasks. The decoder up-sampling methodology restores spatial resolution, allowing the model to generate a high-quality segmentation mask. The final step uses a 1 × 1 convolution to produce a segmentation mask of size 224 × 224 × 1, where each pixel corresponds to a specific region, such as the weld pool area. Throughout the network, batch normalization and the ReLU activation function help stabilize training, add non-linearity and make the model more robust. The U-Net framework, with EfficientNet-B0 as the encoder, effectively handles fine details and large-scale features, making it particularly suitable for the semantic segmentation of weld images. Summary and conclusions

Deep learning approach has been successfully developed for monitoring and predicting the depth of penetration during A-TIG welding of 10 mm thick 316LN stainless steel. The developed deep learning model operates in three stages: first, it isolates the weld pool through semantic segmentation using a convolutional neural network (CNN); second, it extracts image features from the segmented images; and third, it predicts the depth of penetration (DOP) using a back-propagation neural network (BPNN). A U-Net architecture with a VGG19 encoder was employed for weld pool segmentation, achieving accuracies of 0.9764 on the training set and 0.9720 on the test set. Additionally, weld pool features such as width, length, area, and centroid were extracted, along with welding current, which was also treated as an input parameter to train the BPNN, with DOP as the target output. The BPNN achieved a training accuracy of 0.989 and a testing accuracy of 0.987, with an execution time of 110 milliseconds.

To further reduce execution time, EfficientNet-B0 was adopted as the encoder in the U-Net framework, resulting in a segmentation accuracy of 0.9718. Following feature extraction and BPNN training, the model achieved an overall accuracy of 0.982. The entire system was deployed on an NVIDIA Jetson Nano. Validation experiments were carried out with constant current and varying current. Comparison of predicted and measured depth of penetration during validation experiments exhibited a RMS of 0.1168 mm and R² score of 0.9979 confirming good accuracy. Execution time was further reduced to 70 milliseconds, and a Python-based graphical user interface was developed to enable real-time DOP prediction and visualization as a function of welding distance. The developed approach would find applications during remote and in-situ welding of reactor components.
