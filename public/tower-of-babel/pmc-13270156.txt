Gentle-Sketch: a high-performance and compact invertible sketch for top-K estimation
Authors: Yao Xin, Chuan Chen, Shufan Cao, Wenhao Jia, Yuqiao Luo, Lingfeng Qu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Top-k estimation has been a significant research focus in network data stream processing, owing to its broad range of applications. However, the rapid detection of top-k frequent flows in massive network traffic poses considerable challenges, primarily due to the stringent requirements for high-speed packet processing and constraints in resource availability. Invertible sketches, as summary data structures, enable the recovery of top-k flows with limited memory usage and bounded error guarantees. To the best of our knowledge, most existing invertible sketch algorithms suffer from high memory access overhead, which adversely affects their performance in top-k estimation. This article proposes Gentle-Sketch, a high-performance and compact invertible sketch that supports top-k estimation using small and statically allocated memory. Designed around the inherent characteristics of data streams, Gentle-Sketch adopts an adaptive structure that organizes multiple buckets in a specific manner. The entry size per bucket is tailored to match the stream distribution. Gentle-Sketch hashes each flow to multiple buckets and flexibly relocates overflowing flows, improving memory utilization. This mechanism preserves elephant flows and incorporates more mice flows without throughput loss. Extensive experimental results demonstrate that Gentle-Sketch achieves invertibility while maintaining high accuracy and throughput in top-k estimation compared to existing sketch algorithms. In particular, compared to the state-of-the-art Double-Anonymous Sketch, Gentle-Sketch improves estimation precision by over 20% and increases throughput by more than a factor of two.

Introduction

Network measurement plays a central role in computer networks, such as quality of service1,2, anomaly detection3–6, performance diagnosis7, traffic behavior analysis8 and network telemetry9,10. In addition, as a distinct class of streaming data, network-traffic measurement techniques have been widely adopted in domains such as databases11, data mining4,12–15 and information security16. Top-k estimation refers to selecting k flows with the largest number of frequencies in network traffic, and providing frequency estimation. Normally, top-k estimation is widely considered one of the most basic part of network measurements since various applications are centered on it15,17–19. For example, in data center networks, identifying top-k largest flows allows operators to perform timely load balancing and congestion avoidance; in cyber-security, detecting the top-k most frequent suspicious connections can help reveal ongoing attacks like DDoS20–22 or scanning activities; and in video streaming services, recognizing the top-k popular content can optimize caching strategies and improve user experience. Furthermore, modern network infrastructures are increasingly burdened by massive and complex multimedia data streams. For instance, advanced downstream applications such as visual-textual fusion for underwater image enhancement23 and mutual guidance networks for remote sensing visual question answering24 heavily rely on the efficient transmission and processing of large-scale visual data. In these contexts, efficiently identifying top-k flows is essential for network operators to prioritize bandwidth allocation and ensure the real-time performance of such data-intensive image tasks.

In those applications mentioned above, the network stream is generated at a high rate, long term and only one-pass. Although using large-scale storage to record complete flow information is theoretically feasible, it is often impractical due to excessive economic and computational costs. Thus, the ordinary large-memory scheme is not affordable or outweighs its benefits. Consequently, many probabilistic algorithms that store data temporarily in limited memory are subsequently invented25. Sampling-based techniques26 select a statistically representative subset of the original traffic and subsequently infer global traffic characteristics from this subset. These approaches impose modest computational and storage demands and are compatible with limited hardware resources. Nevertheless, the resulting traffic profile is inherently incomplete and exhibits low accuracy. Consequently, sampling is now considered inadequate for high-speed network measurement27. Some variants of Bloom filter28,29 can be used to estimate the frequency of flow in a data stream, yet they are not purpose-built for this task. In contrast, sketch-based structures have obtained wide acceptance and interest to address the task of top-k estimation due to their efficiency in terms of both time and space, although they admit small, bounded errors30.

For top-k estimation, classical sketches31–33 have been proven effective; however, they are non-invertible. Although such sketches allow querying whether a specific flow belongs to the top-k, they do not support the direct recovery of all top-k flows from the sketch structure alone. Instead, exhaustive queries over all possible flows are required to identify the top-k flows34. Such a brute-force approach is computationally expensive for an extremely large flow key space (e.g., the size is \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$2^{104}$$\end{document} for 5-tuple flows). This fundamental limitation motivates the use of invertible sketches, which not only provide provable error bounds akin to classical sketches but also enable direct and efficient decoding of all top-k flows without requiring exhaustive queries, thereby significantly reducing computational overhead and rendering large-scale stream monitoring practically viable.

Existing top-k invertible sketch algorithms generally follow two basic strategies35: Min-Heap-based and Save-all-potentiality. Min-Heap-based methods employ a Min-Heap to track the top-k flows. Each new flow is inserted into the sketch, and if necessary, the flow with the smallest frequency in the heap is replaced. However, frequent heap operations incur substantial overhead, resulting in slow performance and inefficient memory usage. Alternatively, Save-All-Potential methods avoid external data structures by storing <flow ID, frequency> pairs (flow ID usually refers to source/destination address pairs or 5-tuples headers) directly within the sketch. Although this eliminates heap-related overhead, it introduces other limitations. Since each bucket must accommodate these full identifiers–which typically require 4–16 bytes each–the bit-width per bucket increases significantly, leading to elevated memory consumption. In memory-limited scenarios, this reduces the number of storable flows and impairs estimation accuracy. Moreover, retrieving top-k flows requires traversing and sorting all recorded flows, which introduces additional computational cost. In summary, both strategies exhibit notable limitations in terms of performance and accuracy when operating under tight memory constraints.

This article proposes Gentle-Sketch, a high-performance and compact invertible sketch for efficient top-k flow estimation. The structure consists of m buckets, each containing B entries with monotonically increasing bit-widths to adapt to real-world traffic skewness. Within each bucket, the largest entries store <flow ID, frequency> pairs for elephant flows, while the rest keep <fingerprint, frequency> pairs for mice flows, achieving high memory efficiency and O(1) bucket access. To handle overflows, Gentle-Sketch first attempts to relocate a flow to a larger entry within the same bucket in one memory access. If this fails, an eviction mechanism inspired by Ark Filter36 reassigns flows across buckets, maintaining separation between elephant flows (high-frequency flows) and mice flows (low-frequency flows) while maximizing memory utilization. This design ensures high throughput and accurate top-k estimation.

Through these key mechanisms, Gentle-Sketch attains high accuracy in identifying top-k flows and directly recovering their identifiers. Related work

Sketch-based methods represent a popular and efficient approach for top-k estimation in network measurement, particularly in resource-constrained streaming scenarios, and have attracted significant research attention in recent years. Sketch is a hash-based data structure that aggregates key-value pairs by mapping each key through a hash function to a single counter, thereby achieving substantial space reduction. The value stored in the counter constitutes an approximate measurement of the true value. It provides different theoretical trade-offs across memory usage, performance, and accuracy. As previously discussed, there are generally two basic strategies of the top-k invertible sketch algorithms.

Min-heap-based

This approaches always use a standard sketch structure for frequency estimation alongside an additional Min-Heap structure for the invertible detection of top-k flows.

Count-Min-Heap is an augmented Count-Min Sketch 33 that uses a heap to track all candidate top-k flows and their estimated frequency. For each arriving flow, we insert it into CM sketch, and then update the Min-Heap by its estimated value. However, a downside of this process is that CM stores distinct flows in the same space, which can cause a mice flow may be misclassified as an elephant flow, thereby reducing estimation accuracy.

HeavyKeeper 37 employs an exponential decay mechanism and a compact hash table to maintain elephant flows. When an incoming flow is not present in the corresponding bucket, its frequency is decayed probabilistically–with the decay rate decreasing exponentially as the stored flow’s frequency increases. If a counter decays to 0, the entry is replaced. This approach effectively evicts mice flows while retaining elephant flows, though it may under-estimate true frequencies due to decay. Moreover, its fixed bucket sizes limit adaptability to real traffic distributions, leaving room for improvement in accuracy–especially for large k and large-scale datasets. The accompanying Min-Heap requires 360\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times k$$\end{document} bits, and the continuous updates to both the sketch and the heap impede processing throughput.

Cuckoo Counter 25 reduces hash collisions via cuckoo hashing with an eviction threshold. It uses two arrays of w buckets, each subdivided into B monotonically increasing entries that store a fingerprint and a variable-width counter. A Min-Heap of k entries tracks top-k flows. Candidate buckets are located using \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$h_1(e) = \text {hash}(e)$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$h_2(e) = h_1(e) \oplus \text {hash}(e.\text {fp})$$\end{document}, enabling bidirectional lookup. Nevertheless, it still relies on a Min-Heap, which introduces substantial memory overhead, becomes a throughput bottleneck, and complicates the overall design–limiting its practicality under tight memory constraints.

Save-all-potentiality

This approach stores all potential frequent flows along with their full identifiers in the data structure.

Space-Saving 38 is a classic representative of this category. It maintains a fixed-size structure called Stream-Summary. When a new flow arrives whose identifier is not currently stored, it replaces the flow with the smallest estimated frequency, denoted as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$n_{min}$$\end{document}, and assigns the new flow an initial frequency of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$n_{min}$$\end{document}+1. A major drawback of this strategy is that it systematically overestimates flow frequencies, particularly for mice flows, due to the inherited counts.

WavingSketch 17 organizes multiple cells within each bucket, each storing a full flow ID, a frequency counter, and a flag, thereby enabling invertible mappings for recovering frequent items. However, this design requires scanning all entries across all buckets to identify top flows, incurring significant computational overhead. More critically, storing complete flow identifiers in every cell leads to substantial memory consumption.

Double-Anonymous Sketch 30 was introduced to achieve top-k-fairness in identifying global top-k flows across multiple disjoint data streams. It decouples top-k selection from frequency estimation via a two-component structure, ensuring unbiased and stream-size-independent frequency estimates for local top-k flows–essential for fair and accurate global aggregation. The approach incorporates optimizations such as hot panning and early freezing to improve accuracy and throughput. Nonetheless, DAS is primarily designed for distributed settings and is less suitable for single-node scenarios, such as the focus of this paper. Furthermore, its two-layer architecture introduces higher time complexity, resulting in lower throughput compared to even Min-Heap-based structures.

Bubble Sketch 35 is a recently proposed single-layer sketch for high-performance top-k estimation. It uses a ladder-like bucket structure with entries of progressively increasing bit-width. The largest entries store <flow ID, frequency> pairs pairs for hot items, while smaller ones store <fingerprint, frequency> pairs for cold items. The algorithm employs real-time bubble sorting within buckets to maintain frequency order and a threshold relocation mechanism to resolve conflicts by moving candidate top-k flows to alternate buckets. By avoiding external structures such as a Min-Heap, it significantly improves throughput. Despite its advantages, Bubble Sketch has certain limitations: First, the use of real-time bubble sorting within buckets introduces non-negligible computational overhead, particularly under high-throughput conditions or with large bucket sizes, which can become a throughput bottleneck. Second, the conflict resolution mechanism allows only a one-time relocation per flow into a hot entry. Although efficient in stable traffic, this restricts adaptability in highly dynamic environments: a potential top-k flow initially assigned to a cold entry due to previous low frequency cannot be re-promoted even if its frequency later surpasses that of hot entries. This reduces accuracy in tracking shifting distributions and adversely affects top-k estimation precision under dynamic stream conditions.

Summary and motivation

In summary, although various solutions address top-k estimation, both fundamental strategies exhibit notable limitations. Min-Heap-based methods suffer from the computational and memory overhead of maintaining an external heap, while Save-All-Potentiality methods consume excessive memory by storing full identifiers in every bucket, which severely impairs estimation accuracy under tight memory constraints. Consequently, few methods can successfully achieve invertibility while maintaining high speed, high accuracy, and high precision. This structural gap directly motivates the design of Gentle-Sketch, which eliminates the Min-Heap to maximize throughput while employing an adaptive, multi-sized bucket structure to achieve superior memory utilization for both elephant and mice flows.

The framework of Gentle-Sketch

In this section, we comprehensively detail the architecture and operational mechanics of Gentle-Sketch. To efficiently process high-speed data streams under strict resource constraints, an invertible sketch must excel in three primary operations: the Insert() function for dynamically accommodating new packet arrivals, the Query() function for retrieving accurate frequency estimates of specific flows, and the Detection() function for directly recovering all top-k flows without requiring exhaustive space traversals. Before delving into the step-by-step algorithms of these operations, we first establish the foundational data structure and the key system invariants that mathematically guarantee our sketch’s high throughput and structural stability.

Mapping mechanisms to the top-k problem

Before delving into the operational details of the data structure, it is crucial to understand how Gentle-Sketch’s underlying mechanisms specifically address the fundamental challenges of top-k estimation. The core problem in top-k measurement is accurately distinguishing a small number of heavy hitters (elephant flows) from an overwhelming volume of transient noise (mice flows) under strictly bounded memory constraints.

To directly tackle this, Gentle-Sketch introduces two targeted mechanisms: Adaptive Capacity Allocation Instead of uniformly allocating large memory entries (which wastes space on mice flows) or relying on an external heap (which bottlenecks throughput), our ladder-like bucket structure dynamically assigns large bit-widths exclusively to established elephant flows, confining mice flows to compact fingerprint tags.Dynamic Eviction for Heavy-Tailed Traffic To handle the severe hash collisions characteristic of Zipf-distributed network traffic, our cross-array eviction mechanism ensures that transient bursts of mice flows cannot overwrite established top-k candidates, thereby preserving high estimation precision even under adversarial network conditions.

Data structure

Fig. 1The data structure of Gentle-Sketch Sketch.

The data structure of Gentle-Sketch Sketch.

The Gentle-Sketch data structure, illustrated in Fig. 1, comprises two arrays, Remainder Array and Quotient Array. Each array has m buckets, and each bucket contains B entries of varying sizes, labeled \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\{entry_1, entry_2, \ldots , entry_B\}$$\end{document}. In the Gentle-Sketch, an entry serves as the fundamental storage unit, while a bucket represents the basic unit for a single access operation. The bit width of each entry increases in a stepwise manner, creating a ladder-like distribution pattern. Specifically, the two largest entries, namely \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$entry_B$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$entry_{B-1}$$\end{document}, are designed to store elephant flows (candidate top-k flows) with a unique ID and a large counter for the <flow ID, frequency> pair. Conversely, the smaller entries are allocated for mice flows and contain a tag which records the part information of the fingerprint (either the remainder or the quotient as specified later), along with a smaller counter for the <tag, frequency> pair. To be specific, we store the quotient in the Remainder Array and the remainder in the Quotient Array. Although the width of the ID and tag remains constant across all entries, the width of the counter varies according to the storage requirements. This design takes advantage of the skewed nature of data streams, thereby optimizing storage efficiency by accommodating as many conflicting flows as possible within the given space constraints (Fig. 2).

Building upon the ladder-like bucket structure inspired by Bubble Sketch, Gentle-Sketch introduces several key innovations to overcome its limitations. In contrast to Bubble Sketch, which relies on real-time bubble sorting within buckets to maintain frequency order and permits only one-time relocation per flow, Gentle-Sketch employs an special eviction mechanism that supports dynamic flow migration across entries and even between arrays. This allows a flow initially stored in a small entry to be promoted to a larger one if its frequency increases, enhancing adaptability in highly dynamic streams. Furthermore, Gentle-Sketch eliminates the need for intra-bucket sorting, reducing computational overhead and improving throughput, especially under high skewness. These features collectively enable Gentle-Sketch to achieve higher precision and better scalability than Bubble Sketch.

In the Gentle-Sketch, an element e is successfully represented if its corresponding remainder R is stored in the bucket indexed by the quotient Q, or if the quotient Q is stored in the bucket indexed by the remainder R. The process of determining the indices of the two candidate buckets for any element e is detailed as follows: First, we calculate the fingerprint FP of element e using a hash function \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$h(e): FP = h(e)\%[m(m-1)]$$\end{document}. The fingerprint FP is then divided into a quotient Q and a remainder R based on the filter length m: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Q = FP/m$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R = FP\%m$$\end{document}. These values Q and R serve as the indices for the two candidate buckets in the sketch. Specifically, the remainder R is used to index a bucket in the Remainder Array, where we can find the corresponding entry. This entry contains the quotient Q stored in the tag field or can be calculated using the stored ID. Conversely, the quotient Q is used to index a bucket in the Quotient Array, where the remainder R can be found.

It is worth noting that the fingerprint bit width in Gentle-Sketch is not a free hyperparameter. To guarantee a deterministic bijection for our quotient-remainder indexing mechanism without inducing out-of-bounds memory accesses, the fingerprint FP is strictly bounded by the modulo operation to the maximum capacity of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$m(m-1)$$\end{document}. Consequently, its required bit width is mathematically locked to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\lceil \log _2(m(m-1)) \rceil$$\end{document}. This design fundamentally couples the fingerprint size to the filter length m, ensuring structural integrity and robust collision resolution across both arrays without requiring independent parameter tuning.

This bidirectional indexing mechanism allows for efficient location of candidate buckets in both arrays, thereby facilitating rapid insertion and query operations. This design leverages the properties of the fingerprint to directly index the candidate buckets without additional hash calculations, significantly enhancing the efficiency of the sketch.

Algorithm and operations

Below we present the implementation processes for the three operations of Gentle-Sketch: insertion, query and detection, which form the basis of top-k estimation tasks.

Insert: For the sake of brevity, we denote the entry at the j-th position of the i-th bucket in the Remainder Array as RA[i][j], and the entry at the j-th position of the i-th bucket in the Quotient Array as QA[i][j]. Initially, all entries in both arrays are set to 0. When inserting a packet p that belongs to flow e, we first compute the fingerprint FP of element e using the hash function: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$FP=h(e)\%[m(m-1)]$$\end{document}. Subsequently, we calculate the quotient Q and remainder R from FP as follows: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Q = FP/m$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R = FP\%m$$\end{document}. These values Q and R serve as the indices to locate the two candidate buckets: RA[R] and QA[Q]. Then we examine all entries RA[R][j] and QA[Q][j] \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(1\le j \le B)$$\end{document} within these two buckets.

If the flow e already exists, the counter of the corresponding entry is incremented by 1. If e is a new flow, the algorithm checks for available entries in either RA[R] or QA[Q]. Should an empty entry be found, the packet is inserted into that entry and its counter is initialized to 1. In cases where both buckets are fully occupied, a flow e’ is selected from either RA[R][1] or QA[Q][1] that has the smaller counter value; this counter is then decremented by 1. If the counter reaches 0, the new flow e is inserted into that entry. The algorithm only modifies an \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$entry_1$$\end{document} from RA[R][1] or QA[Q][1] when all buckets mapped to flow e are full, thereby ensuring that \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$entry_1$$\end{document} consistently tracks the mice flow within the network stream.

When the counter of an entry reaches its maximum capacity–for instance, upon overflow in \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$entry_1$$\end{document}–a scan is initiated across the larger entries within the same bucket. If an entry with a larger size and a counter value lower than that of the overflowed entry is found, the two entries are swapped to improve space efficiency. If no such entry exists within the current bucket, the algorithm proceeds to inspect the corresponding bucket in the alternate array. Should an available entry of larger size be present in the alternate bucket, the flow e is inserted therein. Otherwise, the search continues for an entry with both a larger size and a counter value smaller than that of the overflowed entry. Once identified, the flow e’ currently occupying that entry is evicted. The evicted flow e’ is then reassigned to a bucket in the other array based on the quotient or remainder derived from its fingerprint and the filter length.

If the target bucket is full, an entry is selected whose counter value is smaller than that of e and which has a larger size. The flow e” in that entry is subsequently evicted, and e’ is inserted in its place. This eviction process repeats iteratively until all flows are successfully inserted or until the number of ejections reaches a predefined maximum threshold, denoted as maxloop. Once maxloop is reached, a final check is performed: if the counter value of the last evicted flow e”’ exceeds that of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$entry_1.cnt$$\end{document} in the current bucket, the tag of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$entry_1.cnt$$\end{document} is updated to the quotient Q or remainder R of e”’, while retaining the original counter value of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$entry_1.cnt$$\end{document}. Otherwise, e”’ is discarded to prevent further overflow and ensure system stability. The complete insertion procedure is outlined in Algorithm 1- 2.

Query: When querying for a flow e, the fingerprint FP of element e is first computed using the function: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$FP=h(e)\%[m(m-1)]$$\end{document}. Subsequently, the quotient Q and remainder R are derived from FP as follows: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Q = FP/m$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R = FP\%m$$\end{document}. Next, we match the ID or quotient Q of e with the ID or tag stored in RA[R][j], and match the ID or remainder R of e with the ID or tag stored in QA[Q][j] \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(1\le j \le B)$$\end{document}. If a match is found, the counter value of the corresponding entry is returned. If at least one empty entry is detected, a value of 0 is returned. Otherwise, the lower counter value between RA[R][1].counter and QA[Q][1].counter is returned. The algorithm of Query is given in Algorithm 3.

Detection: The method for detecting the top-k frequent flows is straightforward. The algorithm traverses all flows stored in RA[R][j] and QA[Q][j] for \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(B-1 \le j \le B)$$\end{document}, sorts these flows in descending order based on their counter values, and finally returns the k flows with the highest frequencies. The complete detection procedure is outlined in Algorithm 4.

Bounded-eviction analysis

The entanglement of intra-bucket promotion and cross-array eviction is strategically designed to maximize space utilization. To theoretically guarantee high throughput, Gentle-Sketch draws inspiration from bounded-eviction frameworks like Ark Filter 36. In such frameworks, the time complexity of an insertion is strictly bounded to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$O(b\times max)$$\end{document}, where b is the number of slots per bucket and max is the relocation limit. Similarly, Gentle-Sketch enforces a strict upper bound on the recursive ejection chain through the maxloop parameter. Let B represent the number of entries per bucket and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$L_{max}$$\end{document} denote the predefined maxloop threshold. In the worst-case scenario, an insertion triggers the maximum number of cross-array evictions. During each step of the eviction process, the algorithm scans at most B entries within the target bucket to identify a suitable candidate for swapping or eviction.

Therefore, the worst-case time complexity for a single insertion, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$T_{worst}$$\end{document}, can be formally bounded as:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} T_{worst}=\sum _{i=1}^{L_{max}}O(B)=O(B\times L_{max}) \end{aligned}$$\end{document}Since both B and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$L_{max}$$\end{document} are statically configured constants, the worst-case insertion time is strictly bounded to O(1).

Furthermore, we can analyze the amortized cost by considering the probability distribution of the eviction chain lengths. By enforcing the Monotonic Promotion invariant, a victim flow can only displace an entry with a strictly smaller counter value, creating a directional eviction path that naturally resists long cyclic loops. Let P(i) be the probability that exactly i evictions occur during an insertion. The amortized time complexity, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$T_{amortized}$$\end{document}, is the expected cost over a sequence of operations:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} T_{amortized}=\sum _{i=0}^{L_{max}}P(i)\cdot O(i\times B) \end{aligned}$$\end{document}Because the probability P(i) decreases exponentially as the number of evictions i increases, the expected number of memory accesses remains extremely low. Thus, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$T_{amortized}\approx O(1)$$\end{document}. This bounded-eviction guarantee inherently prevents cyclical eviction collapse, theoretically validating the empirical observations of stable and high-speed processing under heavy network skew.Fig. 3An example of Gentle-Sketch.

Performance evaluation

Experiment setup

Platform

The evaluation is performed on a server equipped with a 24-core processor (Intel(R) Core(TM) Ultra 9 285K CPU @ 3.70GHz) and 64 GB of DRAM, running Ubuntu 20.04. All algorithms are implemented in C++ and compiled using GCC 9.4.0 with the -O3 optimization flag to maximize performance. For cryptographic uniformity and to minimize collision variance across all sketch structures, we adopted the 64-bit BobHash3 algorithm as our standard hash function.

Dataset

The experiments are carried out on four different datasets, comprising two real-world datasets and two synthetic datasets, as detailed below.

CAIDA: This dataset includes network traffic traces from the Equinix-Chicago monitor 41. It contains 109K flows, 2.49M packets. The maximum flow size is 17K.

Real-Life Transactional Datasets: We acquire the RealLife Transactional dataset called WebDocs from Cuckoo Counter25. This dataset is built from a spidered collection of web html documents. More details about the dataset are in42. Each sub-dataset has a size of 102MB. The frequency of each flow ranges from 1 to 5349.

Synthetic Datasets: We generate synthetic traces using the Zipf tool43. The skewness of the traces include 1.0 and 1.5. Each dataset contains 3M packets, with distinct flow counts of 98K and 21K respectively.

Top-k estimation: We evaluated the GS algorithm against WavingSketch17, Double-Anonymous Sketch30, Cuckoo Counter25, HeavyKeeper37, Unbiased Space-Saving18, Space-Saving38 and Bubble Sketch35. The memory budget and the parameter k are configured manually. The memory size dictates the number of buckets allocated to each data structure, whereas k specifies the number of top flows to be returned. Every algorithm reports the k largest estimated flows. In all experiments, we set k=1000.

Ablation study: the effect of the eviction mechanism (Maxloop)

Gentle-Sketch introduces a critical parameter, maxloop, that bounds the maximum number of evictions. Because our data structure is tightly integrated, we conduct an ablation study on our core eviction mechanism by systematically varying this parameter. Setting maxloop = 1 effectively restricts the algorithm to a baseline state with minimal cross-bucket flow migration, whereas higher values enable the full dynamic eviction module. To determine an appropriate setting for subsequent experiments, we systematically vary maxloop and observe its impact on top-k precision, AAE, ARE and insertion throughput. All tuning runs are conducted with a fixed memory budget of 16 KB. The resulting trends are depicted in Fig. 4.

We conduct experiments on both the CAIDA and Real-Life datasets; the observed trends are qualitatively identical. Insertion throughput decreases monotonically as maxloop increases. AAE and ARE decline while maxloop rises from 1 to 8, but exhibit a slight rebound beyond this point. Precision improves monotonically up to maxloop = 8 and then marginally deteriorates. Consequently, we select maxloop = 8 as the optimal value for top-k estimation. During insertion, a larger maxloop grants potential elephant flows stored in small entries a higher probability of migrating to larger entries before overflow. Nevertheless, the increased frequency of evictions degrades insertion throughput. Moreover, an excessively large maxloop may cause an elephant flow to be perturbed by a evicted flow while migrating from a small to a large entry. This effect is bounded: it does not misclassify an elephant flow as a mice flow, yet it marginally elevates estimation error. We further evaluated the impact of maxloop across varying memory allocations on both datasets. The results are presented in Figs. 5 and 6. Across all configurations, maxloop = 8 yields the highest precision and the lowest AAE and ARE, while throughput remains statistically indistinguishable from other settings. These observations corroborate the selection of maxloop = 8 as the optimal parameter. Ultimately, this ablation analysis demonstrates that enabling the dynamic cross-bucket eviction module (as opposed to restricting it at maxloop = 1) is essential for maximizing precision and minimizing estimation error, cleanly illustrating the value of this specific design choice.

Experiments on top-k estimation

In this part, we illustrate the performance of our Gentle-Sketch applied to finding top-k flows. The main metrics are insert throughput, precision, AAE and ARE. We compare our Gentle-Sketch (GS) with related Bubble Sketch (BS), WavingSketch (WS), Double-Anonymous Sketch (DAS), Cuckoo Counter (CC), HeavyKeeper (HK), Unbiased Space-Saving (USS), and Space-Saving (SS). We take GS’s maxloop = 8. Figures 8, 9, 10 and 11 illustrate the performance of sketch algorithms across different datasets in top-k estimation, with k set at 1000 to identify the 1000 most frequent flows in each dataset. It is important to note that USS, CC and HK cannot operate with less than 64KB of memory due to their Min-Heap-based storage mechanism.

Throughput

As illustrated in Fig. 8, GS consistently achieves the highest throughput across all memory configurations and datasets. By eliminating the need for a Min-Heap-based structure, GS significantly reduces the number of operations per insertion, leading to a more efficient update process. On the CAIDA, RealLife, and Zipf-1.0 datasets, GS attains an average throughput that is approximately twice that of CC. Notably, on the Zipf-1.5 dataset–characterized by high skewness–GS outperforms CC by a factor of four. Although BS demonstrates competitive throughput, closely approaching GS in low-skewness environments (CAIDA, RealLife, and Zipf-1.0), GS exhibits a substantial advantage under high skewness, surpassing BS by an average of nearly 20 Mips on the Zipf-1.5 dataset. These results underscore the superior efficiency and scalability of GS across diverse data distributions.

Precision

As shown in Fig. 9, GS exhibits exceptional accuracy in the top-k estimation task, consistently achieving higher precision than all competing algorithms across diverse datasets. Notably, under tight memory constraints, the precision gap between GS and the second-best performer, BS, reaches up to 0.15, highlighting its substantial advantage in high-precision scenarios. These results demonstrate the robustness and reliability of GS under limited resource conditions, further validating its superior design for accurate top-k flow identification.

AAE

Figure 10 compares the AAE performance of various algorithms. GS shows differentiated behavior across datasets: it achieves the best AAE on the highly skewed Zipf-1.5 dataset, significantly outperforming BS and other baselines, which demonstrates its strong stability and robustness in heavily skewed streaming environments. However, on the CAIDA, RealLife, and Zipf-1.0 datasets, BS consistently obtains lower AAE values than GS, indicating its superior capability in controlling absolute error under these conditions. These outcomes highlight the influence of data distribution characteristics on algorithm performance and suggest that GS is particularly well-suited for scenarios with high skewness.

ARE

As illustrated in Fig. 11, GS exhibits equally remarkable performance in ARE. It achieves the lowest ARE on the Zipf-1.5 dataset, reaffirming its ability to maintain low relative error under high skewness–a crucial feature for accurate top-k estimation. In contrast, BS delivers superior ARE results on datasets with moderate skewness or real-world characteristics, such as CAIDA, RealLife, and Zipf-1.0, reflecting its higher estimation reliability in general settings. Although GS does not exceed BS in ARE on most datasets, its leading performance under high skewness emphasizes the unique algorithmic advantages and practical utility of GS in specific high-skew applications.

Conclusion

Top-k estimation in data streams is crucial for many real-world applications. To address this task effectively, we propose Gentle-Sketch (GS), a high-performance and compact invertible sketch algorithm. In summary, GS incorporates three key ideas to achieve its design objectives: 1) Memory Efficiency: The entry size in each bucket is carefully calibrated to accommodate both mice flows and elephant flows separately, improving memory utilization and efficiently handling skewed data distributions. 2) High Throughput: Each bucket serves as a hash unit, supporting O(1) access time and linear internal traversal. This structure ensures high update throughput. Furthermore, by eliminating external data structures such as a Min-Heap, GS significantly enhances processing speed. 3) High Accuracy and Precision: Upon overflow, GS first attempts to relocate the flow to a larger entry within the same bucket. If unavailable, it employs a lightweight and efficient method to evict a flow from a larger entry in an alternative bucket. This mechanism ensures that both elephant and mice flows are assigned to suitably sized entries, enabling accurate storage and retrieval of top-k frequent flows. Experimental results demonstrate that GS outperforms state-of-the-art methods, achieving high accuracy and throughput even under constrained memory conditions for top-k estimation. For future work, we plan to implement and deploy Gentle-Sketch on programmable hardware data planes, such as FPGAs and P4-enabled switches, to evaluate its line-rate processing capabilities in real-world network environments. Furthermore, extending the current single-node architecture to support distributed, network-wide top-k estimation across multiple measurement nodes represents another promising research direction.
