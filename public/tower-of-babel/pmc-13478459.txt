Exploring provider attitudes towards digital therapeutics for diabetes and depression
Authors: Odia Kane, Kadija Ferryman, Nancy Kass
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDigital therapeutics (DTxs) are AI-enabled FDA-regulated health apps prescribed to patients for a variety of conditions. DTxs use several methods including gamification, health education, and personalized insights to deliver targeted health behavior modification for users to self-manage a treatment or therapy, and or make necessary lifestyle changes. We investigate primary care provider attitudes (n = 224) towards adding DTxs to treatment plans for adult patients with diabetes or depression using experimental vignettes to examine differences in referral rates for a DTx versus in-person self-management program for patients with diabetes or depression of different ethnic and racial identities (Black, Latine, or White), ages (25 or 68), and sexes (male or female). In the findings of this pilot, we did not detect a difference in judgments of effectiveness of DTxs compared to in-person programs. When adjusting for all other variables, we found a statistically significant difference in belief that a digital therapeutic would be more effective than a self-management program for patients with diabetes compared to patients with depression (p < 0.05). In addition, providers overwhelmingly opted to let the patient decide the intervention, often emphasizing shared or patient-centered/driven care decision-making. Overall, these results suggest that, when informed that an in-person program and DTxs are equally effective, providers may consider them equally for their patients.Subject terms: Diseases, Health care, Medical research

Introduction

Digital therapeutic (DTx) interventions are designed to influence patient behavior using combinations of gamification, cognitive behavioral therapy lessons, chatbots, and other virtual techniques. DTxs are marketed as novel digital health tools that use artificial intelligence and machine learning (AI/ML) technology for more customized, personalized, and precise self-management for a variety of medical conditions. Most DTxs complete an FDA regulatory review process before pursuing a direct-to-consumer or, the more common, prescription-required path to market1. For FDA clearance, moderate to high risk DTxs are evaluated by FDA’s Center for Devices and Radiological Health (CDRH) for safety and effectiveness.

A report by IQVIA calculated that over one-third of disease-specific health apps are for diabetes and mental illness management and treatment2. Diabetes is a chronic condition that can occur due to an autoimmune reaction (Type 1), physiological dysregulation (Type 2), or pregnancy (gestational), that hinders the body’s ability to appropriately regulate blood sugar though the decreased production of or decreased sensitivity to insulin. In general, all types of diabetes require extensive daily monitoring to be well controlled. About 1 in 10 adults in the United States have diabetes and its prevalence has been steadily increasing. An economic analysis estimated cost savings of up to $178 per month for patients with Type 2 Diabetes that use digital therapeutics combined with treatment as usual within a three-year time frame3. Studies have shown that DTxs for patients with Type 1 and 2 diabetes report effective results in diabetes management and improved clinical outcomes (e.g. lowered A1c levels)4–8. For example, WellDoc, a DTx and FDA cleared Class II medical device for adults with diabetes and other chronic conditions uses AI to synthesize data across health apps (e.g. smart continuous glucose monitors) to generate “health reports.” Health reports summarize user submitted information such as medication use, sleep, vital readings, notes, weight, condition symptoms, dietary habits, and/or physical activity that patients and providers can in their respective WellDoc accounts9.

By 2021, approximately 37% of DTxs are focused on psychiatric conditions2 (IQVIA Institute, 2021). Episodic depression diagnosis rates in the United States have increased steadily over the past five years, with the highest prevalence among young adults (age 18–25), women, and white Americans10. Empirical meta-analyses on randomized controlled trials of mental health apps found statistically significant reduction in depressive symptoms and stress in app users11,12. Between 2020 and 2022, patient utilization of digital mental health services has increased 8-fold13 (Weiner, 2022). In clinical trials, deprexis®, a DTx that uses cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT)-inspired techniques and recorded modules to improve user emotional regulation, reported participants in the treatment group were significantly more likely to experience a 50% or greater reduction in depression symptoms compared to control groups14. As mental health professionals consider ways to increase the pool of available workers, digital therapeutics are viable and clinically supported interventions that can be used, in addition to traditional interventions, for an unmet population need.

While some patients may find DTxs on their own, many may be introduced to them by a provider. In either case, a provider with strong preferences for or against the use of AI-enabled digital health tools also can impact whether and how a digital health solution is presented to patients or used in practice15. Providers may consider AI-based tools riskier (e.g. less scientifically validated or more erroneous) than other digital or non-digital options and may believe that this additional risk outweighs the potential benefits16,17. For instance, an AI tool may miss subtilties in a user’s tone or speech that may be caught and addressed by a human provider in a talk therapy session. Some studies show that physicians have expressed an increased interest in using digital therapeutics because they are tools that can both support patients and collect data that may assist clinical decision making18. However, the choice whether to deploy a digital therapeutic can also be influenced by the provider’s attitudes towards the patient. It has been well documented that medical providers can harbor implicit biases towards patients of different racial, gender, and age groups in healthcare settings19. There are also documented differences in clinical decision-making behavior across different clinician demographics (e.g. gendered differences)20.

These concerns about preferences are compounded by issues of data privacy and security, as well as increasing reports of algorithmic bias against patients from communities of color and medically neglected backgrounds21. Many algorithms are trained on datasets that over-represent white male patients or use race-corrected calculations which can create disparate outcomes for women and BIPOC (Black, Indigenous, and People of Color) patients, particularly BIPOC women22,23.

Currently, the FDA does not require applicants to disclose the demographics of the participants or data in the clinical studies of medical devices which includes those for DTxs.

While providers have acknowledged many benefits of digital therapies, they may be hesitant to suggest them as an option for older patients, patients with severe symptoms, or for patients who providers believe may best benefit from in-person treatment18. Providers have also expressed concerns about DTxs leading to issues of inequity in terms of patients who lack consistent internet access, have low digital literacy, or do not speak English as a primary language24. There is also the challenge of access. Because several DTxs require a prescription or referral for a patient to access, a provider’s willingness to recommend digital therapies is a crucial gateway for patients. As more DTxs are developed and disseminated, concerns about their ethical use, long-term efficacy, integration, and best practices must be addressed.

Descriptive analysis

We conducted several descriptive analyses to measure experience using general health tools generally, attitudes towards prescribing a DTx and perceptions of DTx effectiveness compared to the in-person self-management program. The majority of respondents in the sample reported having some prior experience using digital health tools with electronic health records (88%), conducting telehealth appointments (84%), or recommending wearable digital health tools (62%), health apps (60%), digital therapeutics (5%), or other digital health tools (4%). Only 4% of respondents reported having not recommended or used digital or online health tools to patients. Regarding comfort with prescribing DTxs in a future context, 34% of respondents were neither comfortable nor uncomfortable (Fig. 1.1). About 30% reported feeling discomfort (somewhat or extremely uncomfortable) compared to 36% who reported feeling comfortable prescribing DTxs. Across professions, physician assistants reported higher discomfort (40%) compared to physicians (28%) and nurse practitioners (24%) (Fig. 1).

Comfort prescribing DTxs by profession.

When asked to compare the effectiveness of the DTx vs. in-person intervention, most clinicians believed the DTx and the in-person self-management program were equally effective (54%). More clinicians believed that the self-management program was definitely (8%) or probably (23%) more effective than the DTx (3% and 12%, respectively). These comparisons between the DTx vs. in-person intervention vary slightly by the number of years the providers have practiced medicine (Fig. 2). Only 10% of providers with 6–10 years of experience responded that the DTx would be a better option (probably or definitely better) to the in-person program compared to 20% of providers with over 11 years of experience. Very early career providers with 1–5 years of experience had higher rates of preference towards the in-person intervention with over 30% indicating that the self-management program was more effective than the DTx.

Comparison of DTxs versus self-management intervention by years in practice.

Of the choice actions the respondents were presented with, the vast majority (92%) chose to present the patient with both intervention options to let the patient decide. Of the remaining responses, 5% of participants chose to write a referral to the self-management program and 2% chose to prescribe the patient the DTx. Only one respondent chose to present the patient with neither option and monitor the patient further.

Open-ended responses

The open-ended text responses were analyzed and organized into five categories: clinical philosophy, patient characteristics, intervention features, provider preference and evidence. Of the sample, 205 respondents (92%) provided an eligible response. Of the open-ended responses, clinical philosophy was the most typical theme of comments. This was referred to by just over half (53%) of responses (Table 4). This included comments like “I believe in a patient centered approach where you provide patients with options and then let them choose” and “[patient] autonomy should dictate this decision.” The next most common themes were about the patient’s characteristics (32%) and the intervention’s benefits (11%). Those responses included quotes like “patients prefer a customized approach, especially as the younger generation prefers the remote option” and “[i]n person is likely to create a lasting impression and accountability to continue,” respectively. Citing supporting or the lack of evidence (2%) and provider preference (2%) were the least frequent response type. These responses included comments like “you told me there’s strong data for similar efficacy of both approaches, so seems reasonable to offer both” and “I feel like in person training will help the patient with the necessary skills before using AI techniques.”

Discussion

In this pilot study, we found that providers’ perceptions of the effectiveness of DTxs when compared to an in-person self-management program were consistent across patient age, race, and gender. A key finding was a statistically significant difference in provider judgment in DTx effectiveness to treat depression. Respondents who viewed the vignette of the patient with depression were over 60% less likely to consider the DTx as an effective intervention compared to an in-person self-management program (p < 0.05). These findings suggest that providers may be less confident in the effectiveness of DTx for patients with psychiatric conditions while being more permissive of their use for patients with diabetes. Since most FDA-cleared DTxs for diabetes and depression require a prescription, should a provider feel under-informed or have low trust in the DTx option, they may discourage the patient away from a demonstrated clinically effective digital intervention. Despite these differences in judgement of effectiveness for patients with depression versus those with diabetes, when informed that an in-person program and DTx were equally effective, providers overwhelmingly (92%) opted to let the patient decide the intervention—emphasizing shared or patient-centered healthcare decision-making.

The literature on provider attitudes supports these results, finding that generally, providers may see the potential value of mobile health apps (DTxs and similar tools), and do not have a strong bias against mobile health apps, but may be hesitant to prescribe—particularly without the input of the patient. In a related study by Kong et al. on provider attitudes towards mobile health apps, the authors found that even though approximately half (51%) of providers were open to discussing mobile health apps with patients, only 18% were willing to recommend apps to patients25.

Providers’ apprehension about prescribing or recommending health apps may be attributed to the quantity and quality of evidence on the effectiveness of these tools, lack of guidelines from trusted sources, and lack of understanding of the tool’s capabilities18,25,26. However, this is likely to change as more providers and patients use DTxs and other health apps to manage care. There are also ethical concerns about patients with psychiatric conditions using DTxs because apps often use methods that activate the rewards system of the brain for subtle behavior modification to achieve stated health benefits, but the use of those techniques and similar mechanisms may affect or conflict with overall therapeutic goals27. The use of self-management or behavior modification health apps for depression (as opposed to apps that teaching specific coping skills such as meditation) is still relatively new, whereas there is extensive literature on increasing consumer uptake of specialized medical devices and health apps for diabetes management, which may make DTxs appealing to patients and providers28–32.

There may also be concerns of an app overlooking or triggering more serious symptoms (e.g. suicidal ideation) for patients with depression. Given these reasons, it is likely that the providers in this study selected the option to present most patients with both intervention options to engage in shared decision making with the patient before deciding which choice would be best. Physicians tend to be more supportive of shared decision making when they do not feel strongly about a treatment option, feel patients are able to participate, and perceive multiple treatment options33. However, a majority of our sample identified as women (79%) and literature supports the supposition that female practitioners are also more likely to engage in shared decision-making and favor the incorporation of digital therapeutics34,35. These findings are important for DTx integration in primary care because provider comfort level with new technology in medical treatment can directly impact patient access and understanding.

Among studies exploring differences in provider DTx prescriptions for patients in real world settings, there have been recorded differences in which patient groups are prescribed DTxs. A combined retrospective cohort and survey study by Ridout et al. of patients and providers on the use of DTx for mental health conditions found that patients who were prescribed a DTx were more likely to be younger, Asian or Hispanic, female36. While our results do not detect these biases in provider self-reported attitudes, there is reason to believe that provider behavior may change in practice.

Vignettes are helpful methods for experimentation, establishing research questions and unveiling ways of thinking that may not otherwise be possible to explore37. Hence, it is expected that vignette responses may reflect attitudes in ideal settings or may reflect the respondent’s more conscious decision-making whereas biases may be more implicit19,38. There may also be practical factors that influence which patients are more likely to be prescribed and to use DTxs. For instance, patients and providers who are early adopters or champions of digital health apps may be more likely to use a DTx compared to those who are not.

There were several limitations in this study. First, there is the limitation of sample representativeness. The sample skews more towards white and women providers which may not fully capture the beliefs of men, non-binary, and providers of color. Due to respondent self-selection in the study, the findings of this study may overrepresent providers with positive attitudes towards or more experience with digital health tools since recruitment materials told potential respondents the purpose of the study. Relatedly, half of the same included participants from organizations based in Maryland which may have also skewed results. Due to the use of vignettes, the results may be subject to hypothetical bias, where responses would reflect more favorable attitudes in a hypothetical scenario compared to a real-world condition as well as satisficing, which occurs when respondents process vignette contents less effectively than in natural conditions39–42. The vignette used in this study made explicit mention that the interventions were of “equal effectiveness” which may have influenced the providers’ ability to express underlying skepticism about the DTx option for patients with diabetes and encouraged more favorability towards shared decision-making, overall.

Similarly, respondents may display a social desirability bias where they provide responses that are more socially desirable as opposed to answers that reflect their true opinions which may, in turn, mask implicit biases as well43. However, vignettes can be helpful tools in capturing the perspectives of healthcare providers on growing issue areas and several measures were taken in the development of this study’s vignettes to include details that were reflective of real-world conditions44. A pervasive challenge and limitation with online survey research is the screening out of bots and other bad actors45,46. To mitigate this concern, recruitment sites were instructed to send the recruitment materials to closed email lists and to use other private means of connecting with members. There were three instances when a survey link was posted publicly or was accessible to populations who were not part of the target sample; those custom links were closed and all responses associated with that organization were removed from the final sample. This procedure successfully screened out hundreds of fraudulent responses.

Conclusion

This pilot found no difference in judgments of effectiveness of DTxs compared to in-person programs. Similarly, it found no difference in judgments of effectiveness of DTxs for patients depending on their race, sex, or age. These results suggest that, when informed that an in-person program and DTxs are equally effective, providers may consider them equally for their patients. In addition, providers overwhelmingly opted to let the patient decide the intervention, often emphasizing shared or patient-centered/driven care decision-making even if they thought a specific intervention would be better for that patient. The findings of this study highlight user perspectives that would be relevant when developing institutional guidance for DTx integration in healthcare. Better understanding of provider attitudes towards emerging technologies can support critical implementation efforts for medical institutions seeking to incorporate more digital health technologies into practice. For regulatory agencies such as the FDA, this project helps to identify provider concerns that may inform opportunities and gaps in DTx regulation.

Methods

Survey design and validation

The survey consisted of three sections: respondent demographics (8 questions), a randomized vignette (3 questions), and experience with digital therapeutics (2 questions) for a total of 13 questions, taking an average of 5 min to complete.

Due to the novelty of digital therapeutics, the research team created an original survey to fulfill the study’s objective. Following the demographic questions, each respondent (a licensed medical provider) read a vignette that detailed a typical follow-up appointment with a hypothetical adult patient with diabetes or depression who mentions a condition-related struggle and desire for additional support to improve symptom management. The vignette explained that the hospital does not have any internal services for the patient and then described two similarly effective self-management interventions, an in-person local program and a digital therapeutic.

The respondent was then asked which of the intervention options, an in-person self-management program or digital therapeutic, they believed would be most effective for the patient and which (if any) intervention they would recommend in the scenario. After these questions, the respondents were asked to explain their choice in an open-ended response textbox. In each vignette, the patient’s medical condition (diabetes or depression), race and ethnicity (Black, Latine, or White), age (25 or 68), and sex (male or female) was varied and randomly distributed. For example, one respondent might have read a vignette of a 68-year-old Latine woman with diabetes, and the next respondent might have read a vignette of a 25-year-old Black man with depression. A total of 24 unique vignettes were randomly disseminated in the survey.

The study materials were reviewed in advance by a total of 10 providers who tested the survey flow, functionality, accessibility, and validity of the vignettes and study questions. The first draft of the survey was piloted with four physicians and one nurse practitioner. Feedback from the pilot was used to establish the survey structure and determine the time to completion. Pilot participants suggested edits to the vignette to allow more realistic elements of the clinical encounter such as providing information on internal hospital resources and more insight on the provider’s prior history with the patient. After the first set of edits, five additional primary care physicians were asked to independently review the second version of the survey. They recommended adding a hypothetical study that validated the digital therapeutic and in-person self-management program as similarly effective and clinically useful interventions for patients with diabetes or depression. The final version of the survey received unanimous approval before distribution to recruitment sites and targets (Appendix 1 A).

Recruitment

For this study, we used a multimodal recruitment approach. We first started with a convenience sample of primary care providers in a local health system, local professional organizations of primary care providers, and members of statewide Maryland primary care professional organizations. In addition, we sought to recruit a national sample where recruitment methods included outreach to: (1) nationwide professional organizations for primary care providers, (2) direct outreach at nationwide convenings and conferences for primary care practitioners throughout the United States, (3) purposive sampling of organizations for providers from underrepresented backgrounds in medicine, and (4) direct outreach to statewide organizations for physicians, physician assistants, and nurse practitioners in all 50 states, and (5) national outreach through the American Medical Association (AMA)-approved licensed vendor, RediData. Respondents were eligible if they were adults (age 18+), a licensed physician, physician assistant, or nurse practitioner in the United States, able to prescribe medications, and had experience treating patients with diabetes and depression. The survey was disseminated from January 31 to October 31, 2024, using survey distribution software Qualtrics. Respondents received a custom weblink primarily through electronic mail (email). Emails were sent through mailing lists of professional organizations, directly from a member of the research team, and or from a source at the recruitment site. A minority of the respondents came across the survey through a closed members-only newsletter.

Data analysis

Statistical calculations were performed using STATA Version 17 (Stata Corp, 2021). Raw data was exported from Qualtrics and compiled into Microsoft Excel spreadsheets before the final analysis in STATA. We performed descriptive statistics on multiple-choice questions regarding the provider’s judgment of effectiveness of DTxs for the vignette patient and choice action in the scenario. Using logistic regression, we tested the effect of the hypothetical patient’s characteristics (age, gender, and race) on the provider’s judgment of effectiveness and on their choice to prescribe a DTx. To perform the regression, respondent judgments about the effectiveness of DTxs were dichotomized as DTx favorable (“probably the DTx” or “definitely the DTx”) versus all other responses (“both are likely equally effective,” “probably the in-person self-management program,” and “definitely the self-management program”). The respondent’s choice action was also dichotomized as “prescribe the patient to the DTx” versus all other responses (“refer the patient to the self-management program,” “tell the patient about both options and let them decide,” and “bring up neither option and monitor the patient further”). For these analyses, we aggregated the responses based on the primary variable of interest (age, sex, and race) of the hypothetical patients presented in the vignette (e.g. all respondents to all vignettes of a female patient versus all respondents to all vignettes of the male patient, etc.). Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

The open-ended responses were reviewed and organized into description categories. Responses were considered complete and fit for analysis if they included at least two words and were reasonably meaningful. For example, a two-word response that read “I think” was excluded whereas a response “younger patient” was included in the open-ended response analysis. This study was approved by the Johns Hopkins Bloomberg School of Public Health Institutional Review Board (IRB00025249). Given the experimental element of the vignettes and low risk, the JHU IRB approved an informed consent exemption. All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations, and in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.
