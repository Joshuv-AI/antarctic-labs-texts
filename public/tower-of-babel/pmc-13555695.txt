Unveiling the Structure‐Performance Relationship in Ruthenium‐Based Anode Catalysts for PEM Water Electrolyzers
Authors: Zeeshan Ahmad, Shahid Zaman, Samaneh Shahgaldi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTRuthenium (Ru) based electrocatalysts are considered cost‐effective alternatives to expensive iridium (Ir)‐based anode catalysts in proton exchange membrane water electrolyzers (PEMWEs), due to their lower cost and high catalytic activity. However, these catalysts suffer from poor stability mainly due to metal dissolution, overoxidation, and lattice oxygen activation. Therefore, a detailed understanding of structure‐performance relationships in Ru‐based catalysts is crucial to develop robust catalysts for PEMWEs. Herein, a comprehensive discussion on geometric and electronic structural parameters reveals a direct correlation with catalyst performance. A detailed analysis of dynamic intrinsic structural transformation related to Ru–O bonding under different reaction mechanisms provides understanding of Ru catalyst degradation. Similarly, recent advances in Ru‐based catalysts, including Ru single atoms, Ru alloys with noble and non‐noble metals, heteroatom doping strategies, and strong metal support interactions, demonstrate a significant impact of structural and compositional tuning on catalytic performance. These modifications influence active‐site dynamics, Ru–O covalency, and surface regeneration, which play crucial roles in enhancing catalytic performance. More importantly, single‐cell performance combined with reaction mechanistic insights helps to identify the key stabilizing factors to develop robust catalysts for long‐term durability in PEMWEs. Finally, future perspectives are outlined to design durable and cost‐effective Ru‐based catalysts for PEMWEs.Keywords: catalyst degradation, oxygen evolution catalyst, reaction mechanism, Ru–O covalency, structural reconstruction

Introduction

The rising concerns regarding the global energy crisis, climate change, and environmental challenges demand an urgent transition from fossil fuels to alternative sustainable energy sources to achieve carbon neutrality [1, 2, 3, 4]. Hydrogen (H2) is a green and sustainable energy source with high energy density (142 MJ kg−1) and zero carbon emissions [5]. H2 production through water electrolysis applying renewable energy sources (e.g., solar or wind energy) plays a crucial role in the global energy transition, offering a sustainable alternative to H2 derived from fossil‐based methods such as methane reforming and coal combustion [6]. The global H2 production demand is expected to surpass 500 million tons, with market estimates of water electrolyzer technologies exceeding $1 trillion by 2050 [7, 8].

Water electrolysis technology comprises four main categories based on electrolytes, including alkaline water electrolyzers (AWEs), anion exchange membrane (AEM) electrolyzers, proton exchange membrane water electrolyzers (PEMWEs), and solid oxide electrolyzers (SOEs) [9, 10, 11, 12, 13]. Among them, AWEs with KOH electrolyte represent the most mature and widely commercialized technology. They offer advantages such as low capital cost and use of non‐noble metal catalysts. However, AWEs suffer from several limitations, including higher ohmic losses, lower hydrogen purity, limited current density, and relatively slow reaction kinetics [14]. SOEs, on the other hand, use solid oxide or ceramic electrolytes and operate at high temperatures, which can accelerate the degradation of key electrolyzer components and reduce system lifetime [15]. AEMWEs use an anion membrane as an electrolyte, favor low‐cost catalysts, and operate in harsh alkaline conditions but possess low achievable current density and insufficient chemical stability [16, 17].

Compared to other electrolyzer technologies, PEMWEs are an emerging technology, offering high energy efficiency, compact system design, highly pure H2, rapid dynamic response, and operating at relatively higher current density. These features make PEMWEs promising for decarbonizing energy sectors and supporting global carbon neutrality. However, the primary obstacles to PEMWE are high cost and limited availability of Ir‐based anode catalysts, which makes the overall cost of H2 production significantly higher. The overall electrochemical water electrolysis involves cathodic hydrogen evolution reaction (HER) and anodic oxygen evolution reaction (OER). In HER, hydrogen is produced at the cathode via two‐electron transfer with a thermodynamic potential of 0 V. In contrast, OER proceeds through a four‐electron transfer mechanism and requires a potential of 1.23 V versus reversible hydrogen electrode (RHE). In acidic media, OER is hindered by sluggish kinetics and requires a high overpotential due to breaking of the strong covalent O–H bond of H2O and formation of an O–O bond simultaneously, acting as a critical step for water electrolysis [18, 19]. Thus, OER at the anode is considered the main factor to determine the efficiency of PEMWEs. Currently, iridium oxide (IrOx)‐based catalysts are the only commercial benchmark anodic electrocatalyst that favors the operational conditions of practical PEMWE technology. Nevertheless, the limited availability of Ir metal (annual production <10 tons) and high cost (∼Ir, US$151.91 g−1) are significant barriers for large‐scale hydrogen production via PEMWEs [20, 21, 22]. Thus, it is crucial to design cost‐effective and high‐performance non‐iridium‐based anode electrocatalysts for wider development of PEMWEs.

Ruthenium oxide (RuO2) has evolved as an efficient alternative anode catalyst due to its significantly lower cost (∼US$13.50 g−1 vs. ∼Ir, US$151.91 g−1) and superior intrinsic activity comparable to that of IrOx [23]. Furthermore, its unprecedented physicochemical properties such as optimal binding energy and dynamic Ru–O covalency enhance the overall performance [24]. However, Ru‐based catalysts are susceptible to dissolution under high OER potential, particularly at the current densities above 1–2 A cm−2, which greatly influence their long‐term stability. This poor stability under harsh PEMWE conditions is attributed to different phenomena that occur on the catalyst surface, such as overoxidation, surface rearrangement, ion intercalation, lattice oxygen (OL) participation, and interface evolution. The overoxidation at high anodic potentials initiates multiple, interrelated degradation pathways [25, 26]. Under acidic OER conditions, the Ru centers are driven to higher oxidation states at potentials (>1.4 V vs SHE), leading to destabilization of the oxide framework due to formation of volatile RuO4 or soluble RuO5 2 − species. These changes in the oxide network result in surface reconstruction that alters the coordination environment and activity of the active sites (Figure 1) [27, 28]. Similarly, the proton and electrolyte ion intercalation into the RuOx lattice induces structural strain and facilitates lattice distortion. The OL involvement in the OER mechanism further exacerbates instability by promoting oxygen vacancy (Vo) formation and non‐reversible structural disorder [29, 30]. Meanwhile, the continuous evolution of the catalyst‐electrolyte interface, including changes in surface composition and morphology, accelerates dissolution processes and contributes to the progressive loss of catalytic activity and long‐term durability.

Schematic illustration of major degradation pathways for Ru‐based catalysts, including (a) dissolution due to overoxidation under high voltage conditions, (b) Vo generation because of lattice oxygen participation that leads to (c) catalyst structure collapse and degradation.

Various strategies have been developed to address the limited stability challenges of Ru‐based catalysts. These comprise nanocatalyst design to stabilize Ru oxidation states through electron‐donating non‐noble metals, nanoparticles (NPs) integration into corrosion‐resistant supports, and single‐atom catalysts (SACs) development [31]. Strong interactions between Ru NPs and suitable supports modulate the electronic structure and local charge distribution, which improve both activity and durability [32]. At the atomic level, engineering, particularly Ru‐based SACs, has gained significant attention, where isolated reaction sites maximize the Ru metal utilization and serve as active centers that significantly enhance mass activity [33]. Moreover, synergistic interactions between Ru and non‐noble metals, either through alloying or spatial arrangements, provide stability via sacrificial phenomena that protects the Ru leaching, thereby substantially improving stability [34]. Numerous research works have highlighted these strategies, such as Ru single‐atom dispersion at cation sites within a lamellar (MnO2) structure that activates the lattice oxygen redox mechanism for improved performance [35]. Similarly, Ru NPs incorporation into conductive, corrosion‐resistant Ti4O7 supports demonstrated excellent stability due to strong metal‐support interactions [36]. Thus, alloying Ru with transition metals evidenced significant changes in electronic structure that inhibit Ru overoxidation via strong interfacial interaction [37, 38, 39]. Despite these significant research advancements, structure‐performance correlation and fundamental degradation pathways of Ru‐based anode catalysts in PEMWEs remain insufficiently explored. Therefore, comprehensive and systematic studies are essential to understand the degradation mechanisms of Ru catalyst layers in PEMWEs and to develop effective strategies for overcoming their inherent stability limitations.

Among most of the recent reviews, they discussed the general strategies of Ru‐based OER catalysts and performance in half‐cell studies, with limited discussion on performance and degradation in PEMWEs [40, 41, 42, 43]. Specifically, these studies lack a comprehensive analysis of structure‐performance relationships, which is a primary factor that decides the efficiency of Ru‐based catalysts [44, 45]. Particularly, the OER reaction mechanism has a strong influence on the activity and stability of Ru‐based catalysts. For instance, adsorbate evolution mechanism (AEM) provides superior stability by preserving the metal‐oxygen lattice, whereas the lattice oxygen mechanism (LOM) significantly enhances activity but triggers rapid degradation through Vo formation and subsequent Ru dissolution [46, 47]. Additionally, these reviews also lack discussion on the correlation between OER mechanisms on particular Ru catalyst structures, which is linked to catalyst degradation in PEMWEs. Therefore, it is crucial to provide a broad discussion on the structure‐performance relationships of Ru‐based catalysts, specifically detailing how AEM‐ and LOM‐based OER mechanisms govern the activity‐stability trade‐off and subsequent degradation under PEMWE operating conditions (Figure 2). This review addresses the existing gaps by providing comprehensive discussions of Ru‐based anode catalysts for PEMWEs. Discussion on how dynamic structural transformations and Ru–O bonding changes lead to catalyst degradation under diverse reaction pathways will provide a detailed understanding of the degradation process. By examining recent advances in catalyst design with a significant focus on how active‐site dynamics and surface regeneration in single‐atom and alloyed systems impact performance. This review focuses on the correlation between catalyst structure and single‐cell performance to establish key stabilizing principles and a roadmap for developing durable, cost‐effective Ru‐based anodes for PEMWEs.

OER Mechanism and Degradation

Understanding OER mechanisms is crucial for developing efficient and durable electrocatalysts because the structural changes under dynamic PEMWE conditions lead to catalyst degradation. Recent advances in operando techniques and computational modeling have elucidated different multi‐electron reaction pathways. Currently, three main OER mechanisms are widely identified, including AEM, LOM, and OPM. Catalyst structure is greatly influenced by these OER mechanisms due to distinct electronic structures of different electrocatalysts, thereby impacting performance and stability. A major challenge for Ru‐based catalysts is their limited stability in PEMWEs, highlighting the need to investigate their deactivation mechanisms. This section provides an overview of different OER mechanisms and their impact on the structure‐performance relationship of the catalysts.

Adsorbate Evolution Mechanism (AEM)

AEM is considered a conventional OER mechanism in which oxygen is generated from water in the electrolyte and correlates with the relationship between structure and performance of electrocatalysts. It is the metal redox chemistry in which metal bands act as catalytically active centers where adsorption and desorption of various reaction intermediates occur [48]. AEM contains four successive proton‐electron transfer steps, each characterized by adsorption of multiple O‐containing intermediates (e.g. OH*, O*, and OOH*) on metal active centers (Figure 3a) [49]. Rossmeisl et al. first proposed the oxygen adsorption energy ΔG O* as a descriptor for OER performance and displayed that the binding energy of O‐containing intermediates follows linear scaling relationships on the surface of an electrocatalyst [50]. This linear correlation between *OH and *OOH intermediates with adsorption energies (ΔGOOH‐ΔGOH≈3.2 ± 0.2 eV) decreases intrinsic activity and requires a minimum theoretical overpotential of ∼370 mV for OER to proceed through AEM. Thus, a linear scaling relationship involving adsorption free energies of *OH and *OOH intermediates limits the theoretical overpotential [51]. Moreover, modulating Ru–O covalency in RuO2 can bypass scaling limitations via shifting O (2p) and Ru (4d) orbitals below the Fermi level (EF). This results in suppressing OL involvement and vacancy formation, thereby enhancing adsorption sites. However, the vacancy formation yet again leads to destabilization of the catalyst [52]. A study reported where Cr is doped into RuO2 demonstrated a transition from LOM to AEM due to Cr incorporation [53]. Cr doping in the RuO2 lattice weakens the Ru–O covalency, which effectively restricts OL involvement and prevents the subsequent Ru dissolution by switching the LOM into AEM. According to theoretical calculations, the potential determining step (PDS), typically the *O to *OOH transition, shows a 0.07 eV lower energy barrier on the Cr0.33Ru0.67O2 surface compared to RuO2 and indicates higher catalytic activity (Figure 3b). The synthesized catalyst displayed efficient OER performance that achieved 1.5 Acm−2 current density at 1.63 V cell voltage and operated stably in PEMWEs at 300 mA cm−2 for over 2300 h with minimal degradation (Figure 3c).

Lattice Oxygen Mechanism (LOM)

In contrast, LOM has gained significant attention due to its potential to overcome the scaling limitations of AEM and is considered a promising alternative pathway for improved OER activity [55]. LOM is based on oxygen redox chemistry in which OL around the EF directly facilitates O–O bond coupling (Figure 3d) [49]. LOM also eliminates the linear scaling limitation by suppressing the formation of undesirable intermediate species, thus offering superior OER activity [56, 57]. However, this direct O–O coupling releases O2, which produce an Vo in the catalyst lattice that leads to quick degradation due to structural collapse of the oxide network [58]. Moreover, LOM activation is triggered by high Ru–O covalency, which tunes the electronic structure through precise hybridization between Ru 4d and O 2p orbitals, thereby influencing the OER mechanism. Upon oxidation of metal catalysts, the valence state shifts the O 2p band towards EF, which promotes OL activation [59]. As a result, electron shifts between adsorbed intermediates and O 2p orbitals instead of Ru 4d orbitals, which changes the redox center from metal cation to OL (Figure 3e). Thus, Ru–O is the central point that allows OL to actively involve in OER, which facilitates direct O–O coupling and lower the reaction energy barrier. Thus, high‐valence metal oxides are crucial for initiating the LOM pathway [60, 61].

A recent study reported La doped into RuO2@TM as a composite anode catalyst for PEMWEs [54]. This work highlights the activation of OL through La doping into Ru‐O covalency. La incorporation effectively modified intermolecular interactions, which reduces the activation energy barrier via altering the d‐band center between O 2p and Ru 4d orbitals. La‐doped RuO2 as an anodic catalyst achieved a current density of 1 A cm−2 at 1.815 V and demonstrated stable operation for 120 h at 60°C (Figure 3f). Catalysts that follow LOM pathways demonstrate higher OER activity compared to those that proceed through the AEM pathway. This improvement is related to direct participation of OL that enables more facile O–O bond formation and a lower kinetic barrier of the rate‐determining step compared to surface‐limited pathways like AEM [62]. For instance, a novel approach is proposed to enhance the OL participation by incorporating rare‐earth d0 metal ions (Y, Ce, Gd, and La) in RuO2 [63]. These ions facilitate Vo regeneration in LOM due to their oxophilic nature and improved performance. Therefore, the optimized catalyst shows high performance in PEMWE, delivering a current density of 3 A cm−2 at 1.81 V. However, lattice OL in LOM can induce structural degradation that leads to compromise in catalyst stability [30]. Thus, achieving a balance between activity and stability requires careful modulation of AEM and LOM pathways to boost the performance and stability of PEMWEs.

Oxide Path Mechanism (OPM)

Beyond AEM and LOM, the OPM has drawn significant interest in terms of its balanced OER stability and performance [64, 65]. Unlike traditional pathways, OPM contains only *OH and *O intermediates, which avoid the formation of Vo, eliminates OL participation, and break the scaling limitation of AEM, offering a balance between performance and stability (Figure 3g) [49, 66]. Thus, in OPM, O2 is produced through direct *O‐*O radical coupling, which requires precise geometric orientations and synergistic interaction between adjacent metal active sites with an optimal metal‐metal distance [67]. Thus, this mechanism requires two adjacent active sites with an appropriate geometric arrangement and optimal metal‐metal distance to enable effective *O‐*O coupling. A recent work demonstrated the OPM mechanism where Ru atom arrays were deposited on α‐MnO2 through a one‐step cation exchange method. In α‐MnO2, Ru atoms are periodically arranged by substituting Mn sites to form small, ordered, and uniform coordination environments. This periodic arrangement reduces the bonding distance of Ru‐Ru in Ru/MnO2 (2.9 Å vs. 3.1 Å in RuO2), regulates Ru–O bonding strength, and enables *O‐*O coupling (Figure 3h) [49]. Operando studies confirmed the absence of *OOH intermediates, which suggested Ru/MnO2 followed a direct O–O coupling pathway with a lower energy barrier and resulted in enhanced stability compared to the conventional AEM route. Consequently, catalyst displayed a lower overpotential of 161 mV at 10 mA cm− 2 and was stable over 200 h (Figure 3i). Structure‐Performance Correlation

The development of efficient Ru‐based catalysts for PEMWEs requires a broad understanding of structure‐performance correlation. Such understanding is essential for identifying key geometric, electronic (physicochemical), and energetic parameters that influence OER activity, stability, and reaction pathways. Various parameters such as adsorption free energies, Ru–O covalency, d‐band centers, coordination environment, and bond length provide useful information on how structural features influence catalytic performance. Geometric Parameters/Descriptors

Geometric descriptors represent a class of structural parameters that describe the atomic‐scale configuration of Ru active sites, including local coordination environment, lattice configuration, and spatial arrangement of atoms within the catalyst layer. These descriptors are considered critical parameters for Ru‐based catalysts since they influence both intrinsic activity and stability of Ru surface sites in acidic OER conditions. By correlating coordination environment to adsorption energies and reaction kinetics, geometric descriptors provide a link between atomic‐scale configuration and electrocatalytic performance. Recent research studies have demonstrated that changes in crystal structure, lattice distortion, surface strain, and defects in Ru‐based catalysts can effectively adjust the reaction kinetics by optimizing adsorption energies of O‐intermediates [72, 73, 74, 75]. Furthermore, DFT and operando studies have shown that local coordination environment and structure distortion are responsible for catalytic performance compared to bulk composition, which indicates the significance of nanoscale geometric control in Ru‐based catalysts [76, 77].

Ru–O Bond Length

The Ru–O bond length is a geometric parameter that shows variations in the local structural environment, such as compression, distortion, or elongation in the bond lengths of Ru‐based catalysts. Rutile RuO2 has a uniform Ru–O bond in RuO6 octahedra with P42/mnm space group [78]. However, recent studies show that surface regeneration, lattice strain, and heteroatom doping alter bond lengths, which influence Ru–O bonding strength. For instance, a study reported the intercalation of Li into the RuO2 lattice, which induces structural distortion by lattice strain to improve both activity and stability [79]. Li donates its electrons to Ru, which decreases the valence state and forms a stable Li–O–Ru local structure. This suppresses OL participation and Ru dissolution that improves RuO2 stability. Furthermore, Li occupies octahedral interstitial sites coordinated by six neighboring oxygen atoms, leading to lattice expansion rather than substitution of Ru cations (Figure 4a). Ex situ XRD analysis confirms the lattice expansion, showing that LixRuO2 retains the characteristic diffraction pattern of pristine RuO2. However, slight peaks shift towards lower angles indicate lattice expansion that is induced by lithium intercalation (Figure 4b). Fourier‐transformed Ru K‐edge extended X‐ray absorption fine structure (FT‐EXAFS) was used to investigate atomic‐level structural changes such as Ru–O bond length and Ru–Ru connectivity. FT‐EXAFS shows similar spectral features of RuO2 and LixRuO2, although a gradual decrease in peak intensity and slight shifts in peak position are observed with increasing lithium content (Figure 4c). The first and second coordination shells correspond to Ru–O and Ru–Ru scattering paths, respectively. EXAFS fitting reveals a slight decrease in the Ru–O coordination number with increasing Li concentration, indicating lattice distortion and strain induced by lithium intercalation. In addition, the Ru–O bond length increases with higher Li content, suggesting weakened Ru–O interactions, which may suppress OL oxidation during the OER and improve stability.

Another study reported that introduction of acid‐resistant silicon into the RuO2 lattice effectively stabilizes Ru sites while suppressing OL oxidation [80]. Due to its small ionic radius, Si+4 (0.26 Å) compared to Ru+4 (0.62 Å), Si occupies interstitial sites within the RuO2 lattice, thereby enhancing structural stability and improving durability under acidic OER conditions (Figure 4d). FT‐EXAFS confirms that the main scattering peak in Si‐RuO2‐0.1 slightly shifts from 1.54 to 1.52 Å compared to Com‐RuO2, indicating that Si insertion modifies the local Ru–O bonding environment. Thus, Si insertion increases Ru–O bond length from 1.96 Å to 1.98 Å reduces Ru valence state and Ru–O bond strength, suppressing Ru dissolution and improving stability (Figure 4e). In addition, a dynamic support strategy using LiCoO2 (RuO2/LiCoO2) has been proposed, wherein the evolution of the LiCoO2 support modulates charge redistribution in RuO2, thereby enhancing its catalytic performance. Li insertion into Co3O4 leads to the formation of O3‐type LiCoO2, which shows excellent structural stability and conductivity (Figure 4f). In situ characterization under OER conditions shows that interlayer extraction of Li+ ions promotes charge redistribution and activates electron transfer within the CO–O framework. This dynamic Li+ extraction, coupled with strong interfacial Ru–O interactions, contributes to the structural stabilization of RuO2 by suppressing OL participation (Figure 4g). Moreover, as the potential increases from OCV to 1.7 V, Ru–O bond length shows a significant decrease from 1.93 to 1.81 Å, indicating strong metal‐oxygen interaction under OER conditions. In RuO2/LiCoO2, the coordination number of RuO2 changes slightly, which shows a stable coordination environment. In contrast, coordination numbers are reduced in pristine RuO2, leading to the formation of oxygen defects and destabilizing the crystal structure (Figure 4h). Strong Ru–O bond strength favors the LOM pathway and generates oxygen vacancies in OER, which induces surface structural degradation. Thus, RuO2/LiCoO2, as an anode, demonstrates stable operation for 2000 h at 1 A cm−2 in PEMWE and achieves a current density of 1 A cm−2 at a cell voltage of 1.68 V.

Ru–O bond length is an important geometric structural parameter that corelate the structure with catalyst performance. Several studies have shown that contraction in Ru–O bond length is often associated with OER kinetics due to strong overlap and activate OL, whereas bond length elongation leads to structure stabilization and improve performance. However, excessive bond elongation can collapse lattice structure and accelerate Ru dissolution. Therefore, several studies such as doping, alloying, support interaction have been proposed to optimize the bond length by correlating strain effect and catalytic activity in Ru based catalysts.

Coordination Environment

Coordination environment plays a vital role in determining the activity and stability of Ru‐based catalysts by regulating geometric symmetry and optimizing Ru–O coordination number. As in rutile RuO2, Ru atoms form a stable RuO6 octahedral coordination, which are essential for structural stability but restrict configurational flexibility for adsorbate interaction. Recent studies have shown that catalyst design strategies including doping, alloying, and strong support interactions can introduce defects in coordination environment, generate exposed Ru surface atoms, or induce amorphous RuOx phases containing unsaturated coordination (RuCUS) sites (e.g., RuO5 or RuO4) [84]. These structural features improve OER activity by optimizing adsorption and conversion of reaction intermediates. A study reported that rutile RuO2 has (1 0 0) surface, which demonstrates higher activity than the thermodynamically stable (1 1 0) plane (Figure 4i). This improvement is associated with RuCUS sites, which have a higher density on (1 0 0) surface (∼7.2 nm−2) compared to the (1 1 0) surface (∼5.1 nm−2) [82]. Furthermore, in situ Raman spectroscopy analyze potential‐induced surface regeneration. A study reported that tantalum single‐atom‐doped RuO2 (Ta‐RuO2) was analyzed using Raman spectroscopy over a potential range of 1.1–1.8 V versus. RHE in 0.5 M H2SO4 (Figure 4j)[84]. The vibrational modes of RuO2 shows characteristic Eg, A1g, and B2g peaks, while Raman band at 935.0 cm−1 is related to symmetric stretching vibration of ClO4 −. Ru–O related peaks show minor shifts with increasing potential which determine the robust nature of Ru–O bonds and inhibits surface regeneration of Ru active sites during OER conditions. The peak intensity for *OOH intermediates at 964.3 cm−1 increases with increasing potential which suggests that Ta‐RuO2 follows AEM pathway and high OER activity.

Operando analyses have shown that under anodic potential, Ru coordination environment undergoes dynamic surface rearrangements [85]. As a result, highly reactive, RuCUS surface species are generated that act as transient active sites for O–O bond formation. However, the uncoordinated centers are thermodynamically less stable and lead to Ru dissolution that further highlights the activity‐stability tradeoff [86]. Thus, recent design approaches such as interface optimization through metal support interactions, doping, alloying, mainly focus on regulation of coordination environments of Ru‐based catalyst to provide a balance between activity and stability.

Electronic Parameters/Descriptors

Electronic parameters arise from geometric structural variations that directly influence the electronic structure of Ru active sites. For instance, Ru–O covalency is a core governing factor that determine adsorption energies of oxygenated intermediates and optimize metal‐oxygen interaction by shifting d‐band center position. Similarly, O 2p energy band centre, metal‒oxygen covalency, and orbital occupancy correlates structure‐performance relationship by regulating adsorption energies of O‐containing intermediates (*OH, *O, *OOH), and reaction pathways (AEM vs. LOM).

d‐Band Center

The d‐band center is an important electronic structural parameter that is used to determine the position of electronic states and energy distribution between Ru 4d and O 2p band centers near the EF. This theory was proposed by Hammer and Nørskov, which correlates the electronic structure of transition metals with their catalytic performance [87]. In Ru‐based catalysts, the adsorption strength of O‐containing intermediates and the occupancy of d‐electron states during OER conditions are predicted by using d‐band theory. When an adsorbate binds to a transition metal surface, it will initially interact with s‐band that leads to broadening and downward energy shift of adsorbate electronic states due to hybridization (Figure 5a) [87, 88, 89]. This interaction delocalizes adsorbate orbitals and stabilizes them energetically. As the coupling increases with the metal d‐band, it splits into filled bonding and partially filled antibonding orbitals [90]. The extent of antibonding orbital occupancy is mainly determined by relative position to the EF. A higher d‐band center is closer to the EF shifts antibonding states to higher energy. As a result, orbital occupancy decreases and thus leads to strong adsorbate‐metal bonding [91]. In contrast, antibonding orbitals with lower energy leads to weaker surface‐adsorbate bonding. The d‐band electronic structure can be tune by alloying, heteroatom doping, support interaction and strain effects which influence stability‐activity tradeoff of Ru active sites. For instance, Cr doping in RuO2 modifies the electronic structure of catalyst by shifting d‐band center to lower energy relative to EF [92]. This downward shift decreases the energy of antibonding states which further enhance the adsorption strength by weakening the interaction between Ru active sites and adsorbed *OOH intermediates (Figure 5b,c). Similarly, it also suppresses the overoxidation and Ru dissolution in OER condition, improving activity and stability.

O 2p Band Center

In transition metal oxides such as RuO2, the O 2p band center serves as an important electronic descriptor that describe the position of O 2p band relative to EF in terms of energy. The d‐orbital degeneracy of transition metal cations is lifted in an electrostatic field generated by surrounding oxygen ions which significantly alter their electronic structure. In an octahedral crystal field, five d‐orbitals split into doublet eg (higher energy) orbitals and triplet t2g (lower energy) orbitals (Figure 5d) [89]. The eg orbitals strongly interact with O 2p orbitals which form σ‐bonding and σ*‐antibonding orbitals. While O 2p orbitals show weak overlap with t2g orbitals and leads to the formation of π‐bonding and π*‐antibonding orbitals. Considering the translational symmetry of unit cell, the molecular orbitals emerge into energy bands, called metal d‐bands and O 2p bands. The electronic properties of catalysts can be regulated by the bonding states which arise from the hybridization between metal d‐band and O 2p band.

In comparison to d‐band center which mainly focus on metal‐adsorbate interaction, O 2p band center provides more details about the OL participation in redox reactions. Near the EF, O 2p band has high energy which increases the interaction between O 2p and Ru 4d orbitals [93]. This strong interaction further activates the OL and promotes LOM, which often leads structural instability. Whereas O 2p band possess lower energy when it is far from EF, decreases O 2p and Ru 4d coupling, inhibits OL involvement and proceeds through AEM which provide structural stability. Recent research studies have shown that electronic structural modification can be efficiently tune the O 2p band center through doping, alloying, and support interactions that regulate metal‐oxygen redox behavior and optimize activity‐stability trade‐offs. For instance, RuO2 lattice doped with interstitial Si has been demonstrated downward energy shift of O 2p band center relative to EF [80]. This downshift suppresses OL oxidation and reduces Ru dissolution which provide stability in OER conditions. Consequently, O 2p band center act as an electronic descriptor that correlate the electronic structure with reaction mechanisms and provide a balance between OL participation and structural stability for Ru based catalysts in PEMWEs.

Metal‐Oxygen Covalency

Metal‐oxygen covalency provides deep understanding about extent of electron sharing and orbitals overlap between transition metal d‐orbitals and O 2p orbitals. It represents an important class of electronic descriptors that correlate the activity‐stability trade‐off through optimizing charge‐transfer kinetics, adsorption energies of O‐intermediates and OL participation in reaction mechanisms [94, 95]. Energy difference between metal d‐orbitals and O 2p orbitals is determine the extent of metal‐oxygen covalency. A narrow energy band gap between Ru 4d and O 2p orbitals indicate strong Ru–O covalency with high covalent character (Figure 5e). During redox process, this strong covalent Ru–O bonds activate OL, offer more active metal sites to accept electrons from O 2p band and favor LOM. However, OL participation leaves behind oxygen vacancies which lead to structural degradation and compromises long term durability in PEMWEs. In contrast, weak covalency is related to wide energy gap between Ru 4d and O 2p orbitals leading to upshift Ru 4d and downshift O 2p bands with respect to EF. Weak Ru–O covalent bond prevents OL involvement, provide structural stability and promote AEM to achieve stable acidic OER (Figure 5f). Thus, ideal covalency is essential to balance activity with stability in PEMWEs.

Recent studies have shown that Ru–O covalency can optimize the electronic structure by doping, alloying, interfacial support interactions that regulate the orbital hybridizations and improve structure‐performance relationships [52, 96, 97]. For instance, Sb doping in RuO2 polarizes the metal‐oxygen redox process which enables effective utilization of Ru redox center through electron distribution between Ru 4d and O 2p orbitals and stabilizes OL oxidation. Similarly, weak Ru–O covalency limits electron transfer from OL to Ru center which promote a reversible Ru redox cycle and inhibits uncontrolled OL oxidation which offer a stable, corrosion‐resistive electronic structure (Figure 5g) [93].

Catalyst Design Strategies

Several synthesis approaches are used to prepare Ru‐based catalysts such as Ru SACs, Ru NPs, RuO2, and Ru nanocatalysts with various electronic structures which improve catalytic performance [86] These modifications could limit excessive OL participation and prevent Ru over‐oxidation, although a partial OL involvement still occurs. Therefore, by such strategies, the catalysts could achieve higher activity, improved stability, and greater potential for industrial PEMWE applications [100, 101]. This section provides a detailed analysis of different strategies to stabilize Ru‐based electrocatalysts in PEMWEs. These strategies include, (1) designing Ru‐based SACs which allows maximum metal atom utilization through optimized active site synergy. (2) Ru alloying with noble and non‐noble metals which comprises the combination of two or more transition metals in definite proportion to modify the coordination environments of active sites and reduces noble metal loadings. (3) Electronic modulation via doping includes incorporation of metal or non‐metals dopants to tune the electronic structure and stabilize the active sites. And finally, (4) fabrication of supported catalyst that enhances the electron transfer Ru and catalyst support further optimizing the Ru surface structure and improves stability in acidic OER. Ru Single Atom Catalysts

SACs serve as a bridge between heterogeneous and homogeneous catalysis and conform to benefits of both while diminishing their respective limitations [102]. SACs provide robust interfacial response, optimized reaction pathway, peculiar electronic and geometrical structures, excellent selectivity, and larger atomic site exposure [103, 104]. The distinct electronic and geometric characteristics of single atoms provide unique catalytic behaviors such as altering electron transfer pathways and robust durability due to embedding into support materials (Figure 6) [105]. Ru SACs could be particularly crucial for acidic OER because the isolated active sites can suppress bulk Ru overoxidation and limit OL participation, which are the primary causes of catalyst degradation. Also, Ru atoms embedded into conductive and stable catalyst support stabilizes the active centers, enhances electron transfer, and ensures efficient O–O bond formation at low overpotentials. This precise atomic control improves catalytic activity and long‐term durability, making Ru SACs highly promising for industrial PEMWE applications

Schematic illustration of Ru based single atom catalysts demonstrating the prominent characteristics of Ru SACs such as maximum exposure of active sites, robust stability, strong metal support interaction and efficient charge transfer between catalyst and support.

Having a relatively lower cost, Ru based SACs offer high OER catalytic performance due to their exceptional atom utilization, high mass activity, and quantum size impacts. The strong interactions between Ru atoms and their supports modify the electronic structure and charge distribution at catalytic active sites that improve structure‐activity and selectivity relationship [41]. Moreover, Ru SACs effectively anchor active sites and tune their coordination environment to inhibit degradation and promote reaction pathways which enhance catalyst stability [106]. The maximum dispersion provides more exposure to active sites which is favorable to attain high catalytic activity [107]. In addition, Ru SACs significantly strengthen atom utilization and improve atom economy [108]. However, SACs can suffer from site migration, dissolution under high anodic potentials, support corrosion, and limited durability at industrial current densities, which will constrain their long‐term performance. Thus, rational design of Ru SACs on corrosion resistant support materials is crucial to control the performance and durability under harsh PEMWEs operating conditions.

A cation exchange method used to fabricate Ru/MnO2 catalyst through uniform dispersion of Ru single atoms (SAs) on a stable lamellar MnO2 support demonstrated [35]. This strategy inserted Ru–O sites within the MnO2 lattice that reduced the interatomic distances and enhanced catalytic performance by modulating Ru 4d and O 2p orbital energies. Jahn‐Teller distortion in MnO2 is suppressed by electron transfer from Mn to Ru‐sites through O‐bridge and provides structural stability. In addition, MnO2 lattice allows rapid Vo regeneration that prevents catalyst degradation. Aberration‐corrected high annular dark‐field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF‐STEM) confirmed the Ru SAs dispersion in Ru/MnO2, where the bright spots representing that Ru atoms occupied Mn sites within the ε‐MnO2 lattice and verified the isolation of atomic Ru SA (Figure 7a) [121]. The introduction of Ru SAs shortened the Ru–O bond length (1.77 Å) in Ru/MnO2 compared to the pure RuO2 bond length (1.91 Å), indicated stronger Ru–O interactions that promote the LOM (Figure 7b). Moreover, the band structures of pure RuO2 and Ru/MnO2 further confirmed the orbital interactions between Ru (4d) and O (2p) orbitals. As illustrated in (Figure 7c), Ru 4d band lies above the O 2p band in pure RuO2, indicating a significant orbital gap between Ru and O. In contrast, for Ru/MnO2, the Ru 4d band shifts downward while the O 2p band moves upward, narrowing orbital gap. This modulation of Ru–O covalency facilitates stronger Ru–O interactions, promotes O–O coupling, and activates LOM mechanism. Finally, they evaluated the electrocatalytic performance of Ru/MnO2 (0.1 mgRu cm− 2) as an anode with a Nafion 115 membrane and achieved 1 A cm− 2 current density at a low cell voltage of 1.71 V, outperforming commercial RuO2 by 110 mV. Furthermore, Ru/MnO2 demonstrated excellent durability for 500 h at 0.5A cm− 2 at a low degradation rate of 0.17 mV h− 1 compared to commercial RuO2 which is degraded rapidly after only 80 h. Authors attributed the extended stability of Ru/MnO2 is due to the effective Vo regeneration which strengthens the Ru–O bonding and minimizes the Ru dissolution (Figure 7d).

A recent work on atomically dispersed Ru1‐xPtxCu4‐x SACs with PtCu/Ptskin core‐shell structures demonstrate a high OER performance due to optimized lattice features as per volcano relationship between activity and lattice constant [121]. The volcano plot of OER overpotential h as a function of ΔGO—ΔGOH and ΔGOH on the Ru1‐xPtxCu4‐x (1 1 1) SACs shows optimized OER performance attributed to the tunable Ru‐adsorbate interaction (Figure 7e). The relatively weaker binding increased the dehydrogenation barrier, while strong binding hindered *OOH formation and subsequent O2 evolution. Increasing the Pt‐to‐Cu ratio gradually shifted the Ru1 density of states toward the EF due to reduced surface strain, enhancing oxygen intermediate binding and improving both activity and stability (Figure 7f). Similarly, another work reported Ru‐Co3O4 catalyst via a local compression strain strategy by embedding Ru SAs in Co3O4 lattice [122] HAADF‐STEM confirmed the atomic level dispersion of Ru in Co3O4 matrix, while structural analysis revealed a reduced CO–O bond length 1.90 Å in Ru‐Co3O4 compared to 1.92 Å in pristine Co3O4, suggesting a lattice strain due to Ru SAs incorporation (Figure 7g). This strain effect effectively suppresses Ru and Co dissolution that inhibits structural degradation and improves stability. The electrochemical half‐cell evaluations in 0.1 M HClO4 demonstrated outstanding durability, with the 4.49 wt. % Ru‐Co3O4 catalyst maintaining stable performance for over 400 h at current densities of 10 and 30 mA cm−2 (Figure 7h). These results demonstrate that embedding Ru single atoms into the Co3O4 lattice via local compression strain effectively enhances structural stability and durability, highlighting a promising strategy for designing long‐lasting acidic OER catalysts.

A similar strategy is utilized to disperse Ru SAs on a protonated iridium oxide catalyst (H3.8Ir1‐xRuxO4), also exhibited a remarkable acidic OER performance and durability [109]. In situ characterization and 18O isotopic differential electrochemical mass spectrometry (DEMS) shows that Ru SA modulates the electronic structure of Ir via electron donation. The localized oxygen vacancies generated during the Ru‐Ir electronic interaction coordinatively stabilize Ir active sites by preventing overoxidation and dissolution. DEMS detected two different oxygen signals for 34O2/32O2 and 36O2 in H3.8Ir1‐xRuxO4 which implicates partial OL involvement in the OER and shows minor LOM contribution. This behavior is attributed to the formation of Ru–O bonds that facilitate the redox activity of OL, inducing Vo generation. However, 34O2 accounts for <1% of the 32O2 signal, confirming that the reaction is predominantly governed by the AEM pathway. The synergistic interaction between Ru SAs and Vo modulates the Ir‐O hybridization, improves *OOH adsorption and enhances OER performance. The effect of doped and undoped Ru on catalytic performance is highlighted in free energy diagram where both the catalysts follow the AEM mechanism. DFT calculations also showed that Ru doping increases *OOH adsorption and decreases energy barrier for its formation to 0.466 eV on Ru‐H3.8IrO4‐Ov, compared to 0.478 eV on HxIrO4‐Ov, confirming improved OER kinetics. Furthermore, they have demonstrated the practical applications of H3.8Ir1‐xRuxO4 by employing as an anode catalyst in PEMWE. At 80°C, the cell exhibited voltages of 1.78 V at 1 A cm− 2 and 2.11 V at 2 A cm− 2, shows the excellent intrinsic OER performance. Most importantly, this catalyst also displayed stable operation for 1280 h at 2.0 A cm− 2 with a degradation rate of 39 µV h− 1 demonstrating the excellent stability under high current density operation. Thus, it is obvious that Ru SACs can efficiently modify the electronic structure and provide strong interaction between Ru SAs and support that boosts reaction kinetics and durability under PEMWEs conditions.

From mechanistic point of view, the distinct electronic and geometric structure of Ru SACs can direct OER mechanisms through competing LOM and AEM. The isolated Ru sites downshift O 2p band relative to EF which provides a wide energy band gap between Ru 4d and O 2p orbitals. As a result, Ru–O covalency decreases and suppress OL participation which stabilizes adsorbed O‐intermediates and favor AEM. Whereas the strong electronic interfacial support interaction with Ru SACs increases Ru–O covalency by narrowing the band gap between Ru 4d and O 2p orbitals. This strong covalency activates OL and promotes LOM. The formation of uncontrolled oxygen vacancies in Ru SACs is often prevented due to uniform atomic dispersion of active sites and strong metal‐support interactions. However, the excessive activation of OL can promote LOM and accelerate Ru dissolution. Consequently, SACs provide a balance between AEM and LOM, where AEM dominates and stabilizes O‐intermediates while minor LOM contribution optimize kinetics without affecting catalyst structure. Therefore, distinct coordination and electronic environment of Ru SACs regulate the Ru–O covalency and OL participation which directly influence catalysts performance by balancing AEM/LOM contributions.

SACs mostly suffer from limited stability due to dissolution, migration, or support corrosion. However, anchoring Ru single atoms within the host lattice through structural engineering strengthens the Ru‐support interaction, which improves catalyst durability. Among various supports, Ir‐based systems show superior performance, but their high‐cost limits scalability, highlighting the need for more affordable alternatives. Future research should focus on optimizing the coordination environment and electronic structure of Ru SACs on cost effective corrosion resistant supports such as metal oxides, nitride and oxynitride. A deeper understanding of the interaction between Ru atoms, supports, and reaction intermediates will be key to advancing catalyst design and facilitating industrial‐scale water electrolysis for hydrogen production.

Ru Alloying

Alloying Ru with other transition metals is an effective strategy to diminish the restriction of isolated metal atoms, reduces noble metal loading and overall catalyst cost [123, 124]. Alloying introduces numerous metallic active sites that effectively tailor surface physicochemical properties and induces synergistic effect such as electron redistribution and lattice strain. These effects arise from atomic orbital mixing and electron redistribution influencing d‐band centers, electronic/geometric coordination of metal sites, and improving catalytic performance [125, 126, 127]. Though Ru displays high intrinsic activity, it suffers from limited stability due to insufficient structural robustness in harsh acidic conditions. Thus, alloying Ru with other metal significantly improves adsorption of key O‐intermediates (*OH, *O, *OOH), optimize coordination environment and electronic arrangements. Furthermore, it can hinder excessive Ru oxidation, which enhances activity and stability [124, 128, 129].

A bimetallic alloy of RuIr nanosheets over nitrogen doped carbon (RuIr‐NC) with coral‐like structures formed by anisotropically grown 2D nanosheets exhibited excellent OER performance and durability [130]. This unique coral structure consists of 3 nm thick sheets and a significantly low Ir content of only 6 at.% Ir. The excellent performance is attributed to the Ir (0001) hcp lattice plane with low surface energy due to synergistic effects of alloying, unique geometry, and facet exposure in RuIr‐NC compared to conventional spherical NPs (Figure 8a–c). Another study demonstrated the Ru alloy with cadmium and IrOx (CdRu2IrOx nanoframes) synthesized via cation exchange followed by thermal treatment shows diverse bonding patterns such as Ru‐Ir, Ru‐Ru, Ru–O, and Ir‐O through a synergy induced structural distortion [131]. A twisted octahedral [RuO6] structure with edge/face sharing formed by this distortion lowers the energy barrier of RDS in acidic OER (Figure 8d). DFT studies also show that distortion lowers the energy barrier of RDS, thus increasing catalytic performance. Ru in CdRu2IrOx is oxidized to a higher valence state Ru+5, which serves as the active intermediate species for high activity. Under an applied potential of 1.8 V, further oxidation of Ru+5 to volatile RuO4 is hindered which indicates an improved resistance to Ru dissolution in acidic conditions and increases stability in PEMWEs (Figure 8e).

Ru alloying could also be done with non‐precious metals (i.e. transition metals) to improve performance and lower cost simultaneously [132, 133]. The lattice strain and charge redistribution due to certain transition metals as an effective strain inducer which significantly alters the electronic structure and interface nature of Ru catalytic surface. Under acidic OER conditions, these metals readily undergo surface reconstruction, resulting in modification to their electronic configuration, atomic arrangement, and lattice strain. An immiscible RuCd alloy NPs synthesized via pulsed‐laser ablation in liquid, which efficiently bypasses the thermodynamic constraints of poor adsorption energy of Cd towards OER intermediates [134]. Cd alloyed with Ru effectively balanced the strong Ru adsorption energy and enhanced structural stability of Ru‐Cd alloys (Figure 8f). The HR‐TEM analysis shows that RuCd‐0.5 nanoparticle has 0.234 nm lattice spacing and attributed to Ru (1 0 0) plane. Extended X‐ray absorption fine structure (EXAFS) analysis of RuCd‐0.5 before and after OER demonstrated that a decrease in Ru‐Ru coordination and an increase in Ru–O bond interactions, indicating oxygen incorporation into the Ru lattice. DFT calculation showed that Cd incorporation significantly reduces the adsorption free energies for O‐intermediates related to pure RuO2, which reduces OER energy barrier. The coordinatively unsaturated sites of Cd‐RuO2 display the lowest energy barriers, attributed to weaken O‐adsorption strength with surface oxidation further contributing to superior OER performance. RuCd‐0.5 demonstrated displayed a lower cell voltage (1.5 V at 50 mA cm− 2) compared to commercial RuO2 (1.9 V) and maintaining stable performance without noticeable voltage decay over 50 h, (Figure 8g).

A recent work demonstrated rapid Joule heating method to fabricate RuCo alloy NPs, attaining exceptional performance and durability for acidic OER. Using these well‐defined alloys as model catalysts, they systematically investigated dissolution and surface reconstruction processes through combined electrochemical and experimental characterizations, providing critical insights into performance regulation [135]. Furthermore, RuFe@CF as an anode catalyst in PEMWE exhibited voltages of 1.898 V at 1 A cm− 2 and sustained a stable voltage of ∼1.6 V for 250 h at 200 mA cm− 2 (Figure 9c).

High Entropy Alloys

High‐entropy alloys (HEAs) are generally formed as randomly mixed solid solutions by combining five or more elements in near‐equimolar atomic ratios, typically ranging from 5% to 35% for each element. The random distribution of multiple elements permits tunable electronic structures and allows optimization of adsorption energies of reaction intermediates. These materials have attained significant consideration in electrocatalysis because of their stabilizing and synergistic effects, which enable them with unique physicochemical properties and enhanced structural stability [136, 137]. The lattice distortion arises from atomic size mismatches among constituent elements can increase diffusion energy barriers and suppress component leaching under harsh conditions. Moreover, the multicomponent interactions in HEAs such as ligand and strain effect can tailor the d‐band center and electronic properties of active sites, further enhances catalytic performance and stability [138, 139]. Integrating HEA strategies into Ru‐based electrocatalysts offers a promising pathway to improve durability and performance while reducing Ru dependence. Recently, a nano HEA RuMnFeMoCo@CF electrocatalyst fabricated through rapid‐Joule heating method evidenced superior anodic performance [113]. This multicomponent design strategy overcomes the poor stability and activity of RuO2 in acidic OER. Whereas the incorporation of non‐precious transition metals enhances performance and reduces noble metal usage, enabling cost‐effective catalysis. Microstructural and DFT investigation confirmed that integrating corrosion‐resistant elements (Ru, Mo, Co) improves stability under acidic conditions.

Additionally, Mn, Fe, and Co reduce OER energy barrier, thereby accelerating kinetics and lowering overpotential (Figure 9d). These results underscore the synergistic effect of multi‐element integration in HEAs, which effectively addresses the trade‐off between activity and stability. The practical potential of RuMnFeMoCo@CF for PEMWE was evaluated using a 2 × 2 cm2 MEA with an anode catalyst loading of 3 mg cm− 2 (0.6 mg cm− 2 active material; 0.16 mg cm− 2 Ru) at 60°C was performed at 500 mA cm− 2 for 150 h with negligible loss in performance. Furthermore, even at 1 A cm− 2 a stable operation had persisted for over 150 h with low voltage degradation rate (∼0.12 mV h− 1), highlighting excellent stability in PEMWE overpotential (Figure 9e). Alloying significantly enhances OER activity and stability of Ru‐based electrocatalysts by introducing multiple metal coordination, structure modulation, and dynamic surface regeneration, particularly under industrial current densities. Atomic level mixing of Ru lattice with other transition metals can modify the electronic structure and regulate adsorption energies of reaction intermediates through charge redistribution, lattice strain and structural stabilization. In Ru alloying, secondary metals influence Ru–O covalency by modifying d‐band center and electron redistribution at Ru–O interface, which can shift reaction mechanism between LOM and AEM. When an electron donating metals introduce to Ru lattice, Ru–O covalency decreases that prevents OL activation and favors AEM pathway. In contrast, geometric or strain effect creates lattice mismatch which modify Ru–O bond length and active sites environment. As a result, strain shift O 2p band near the EF due to strong d‐p orbitals overlap which induces OL oxidation and promotes LOM. Moreover, alloying can stabilize Ru oxidation states, inhibits the formation of unstable RuO4 species and leads to improve catalytic performance. Thus, Ru alloying strategy regulates a balanced AEM‐LOM system, where OL involvement accelerates kinetics while stabilized Ru–O bond maintains catalytic structure and offers stability. Beyond static coordination effects, dynamic structural reconstruction during OER is crucial for stabilizing the catalytic interface. The self‐optimized surface structure further protects active sites from overoxidation in acidic media through enhancing the intrinsic activity of Ru‐based electrocatalysts. Such structural reconstruction underscores the importance of designing Ru‐based alloys with controllable evolution pathways to attain high activity and durability. However, major challenges persist, including the high cost of precious metals, structure instability, and limited strength to complex environments during long‐term processes. Thus, advancing active corrosion‐resistant core shell structures and in situ characterization to monitor dynamic evolution, and reducing synthesis costs are crucial for industrial PEMWE applications.

Doping

Doping is a widely implemented approach to enhance electrocatalytic performance and stability. The introduction of foreign atoms into RuO2 can tailor the electronic structure, surface chemistry, and corrosion resistance which improve performance and stability of the electrocatalyst [140, 141]. When highly electronegative elements dope with Ru, they modify its intrinsic electronic structure by shifting the d‐band center relative to the EF. As a result, the interaction of Ru with O‐containing intermediates (*O, *OH, *OOH) is weakened, thereby lowering the OER energy barrier. Heteroatom doping further modulates Ru–O‐M charge transfer by introducing extra electrons or holes, enhancing surface conductivity and accelerating OER kinetics [142, 143]. Foreign atoms incorporation can optimize the RuO2 surface structure by introducing new active site or tailoring the chemical states of O‐species (Vo, OL) to facilitates OER pathway. Moreover, the addition of acid‐resistant metals enhances durability by mitigating Ru dissolution under acidic conditions [17]. This section categorizes heteroatoms doping discussion and their applications in PEMWEs.

Single Metal Doping

Various foreign metals including p‐block elements (In, [144] Sn, [145]), d‐block elements such as 3d (Cr, [146] Mn, [147] Co, [148]), 4d (Nb, [149] etc.), 5d (W, [150] Ta [73]), and lanthanides (La, [151] Ce, [152]), have been incorporated into RuO2 lattice to partially substitute Ru atoms. Single‐atom doping can tune the coordination geometry and electronic structure of Ru active sites. However, structure of SACs is reasonably simple which leads to certain limitations such as limited control of atomic sites. Introducing short range ordered SAs provides a more effective strategy for tuning the coordination structures and improving performance [153]. These short range SAs further enables the synergistic effect which efficiently modulate the reactant and O‐intermediates compared to isolated SAs. Accordingly, a study reported the successful synthesis of short‐range ordered Ta doped RuO2 (Ta‐RuO2) with asymmetric formation of Ru–O‐Ta (‐O‐Ta) active units that improved acidic OER performance [83]. Ta doping serves as an effective electronic modifier which induces tensile strain to tailor the d band center of Ru. It weakens Ru–O covalency, which optimizes the O‐intermediates adsorption and improves acidic OER performance. Furthermore, electron transfer from adjacent Ta through oxygen bridges, combined with the high corrosion resistance of Ta‐O bonds, suppresses Ru overoxidation and improves stability (Figure 10a). The Ta‐RuO2 catalyst demonstrated outstanding performance in half cell test, requiring only 1.431 V vs RHE to achieve 10 mA cm− 2 and maintaining exceptional stability over 280 h of continuous operation (Figure 10b).

Another study reported the development of RuSbOx catalyst featuring a composite structure with a single‐atom Sb‐doped RuO2 exhibiting remarkable stability in PEMWE [115]. The incorporation of acid‐resistant Sb SA into RuO2 elongates Ru–O bonds, thereby suppressing the LOM pathway and preventing Run+ (n >4) dissolution. Single‐atom Sb doping rebuilds the interfacial water network on catalyst surface, which decreases the overall acidity by accelerating deprotonation process and enhancing OER stability and performance. HAADF‐STEM analysis showed Sb atoms as bright spots with higher Z‐contrast, are uniformly dispersed as single atoms on Ru NPs and RuO2 lattice regions (Figure 10c). A 5 cm2 PEMWE using RuSbOx as both anode and cathode catalysts with a Nafion 115 membrane exhibited outstanding performance, requiring only 1.72 and 1.95 V to reach 1.0 and 2.0 A cm− 2, outperforming Com‐RuO2 || Pt/C. At 2.2 V, it delivered 2.87 A cm− 2 with an H2 production rate of 6.00 L h−1 and demonstrated stable operation for 200 h at 200 mA cm− 2 (Figure 10d).

Multi‐Metals Doping

Beyond single‐metal doping, incorporating multiple metals into RuO2 offers a strategy to synergistically modulate its electronic structure and active sites, which significantly increases electrocatalytic performance [154, 155]. Ru overoxidation during OER can be thermodynamically suppressed by tailoring RuO2 electronic structure to increase the formation energy of oxygen vacancies Vo above the H2O/O2 redox potential. Therefore, elemental co‐doping is a good approach to widen the gap between O 2p band centers and the EF. Thus, this modulation improves the energy barrier for OL oxidation and inhibits Vo formation [60, 156, 157]. For instance, a recent study showed the fabrication of W and Er co‐doped RuO2 (W0.2Er0.1Ru0.7O2 −δ) nanosheets via a hydrothermal method [116]. DFT studies show that W and Er co‐dopants in the RuO2 lattice alter the electronic structure and suppress Ru dissolution by reducing the Ru–O covalency. Thus, in W0.2Er0.1Ru0.7O2 −δ, direct O–O coupling on O 2p states above the EF becomes thermodynamically unfavorable, increases ΔG for OH* formation, and restricts Vo generation. This downshift of O 2p band in W0.2Er0.1Ru0.7O2 −δ inhibits soluble RuO4 species generation compared to pure RuO2 (Figure 11a). As a result, W0.2Er0.1Ru0.7O2 −δ functioned as an anodic electrocatalyst and maintained stable operation for 120 h at a high current density of 100 mA cm− 2 in acidic PEM conditions (Figure 11b).

Recently, a study reported the successful synthesis of bimetallic Mo, Rh dopants in RuO2 (Mo,Rh‐RuO2) nanocatalyst through a facile molten salt method [117]. Mo,Rh‐RuO2 catalysts significantly overcome the activity‐stability tradeoff of conventional RuO2, which facilitates durable PEMWEs at ampere‐level current densities. Mo doping withdraws electrons from Ru and increases the proportion of hypervalent OER‐active Ru species. While Ru–O covalency is strengthened by Rh doping, which stabilizes the hypervalent Ru OER‐active sites. Notably, the synergistic effect enables acidic OER via dominant AEM and minor LOM pathways. DFT calculations further indicated that AEM is the thermodynamically more promising pathway for improved acidic OER performance (Figure 11c). A PEMWE device using Mo, Rh‐RuO2 as the anode and Pt/C as the cathode displayed outstanding performance, requiring only 1.58, 1.73, and 1.87 V cell voltage to deliver 1.0, 2.0, and 3.0 A cm− 2 at 80°C, respectively. Unlike undoped RuO2, which showed rapid voltage rise and poor durability, the Mo,Rh‐RuO2 catalyst demonstrated remarkable stability with an exceptionally low degradation rate of 0.03 mV h− 1 over 2000 h at 1.0 A cm− 2 (Figure 11d‐e). Bimetallic co‐doping offers a promising strategy to optimize the electronic structure and dynamically regulate Ru‐based electrocatalysts via the synergistic effects of dual metals. This approach enables precise control of Ru electron density by enhancing electron localization and balancing dynamic charge. As a result, multi‐metal doping demonstrated excellent OER performance and durability in acidic OER over monometal doping.

Introduction of foreign metal into RuO2 lattice regulates the electronic and geometric structures that effectively tune the catalytic properties of Ru‐based catalysts. Heteroatoms dopants redistribute the electronic density and induce lattice distortion which tailors the Ru–O covalency and impact the adsorption energies of O‐intermediates (*OH, *O, *OOH). Dopants with electron‐withdrawing ability extract electrons from Ru–O lattice, redistribute the electron density at Ru sites, which shifts the d‐band center away from EF and reduces Ru–O covalency. This electronic effect prevents the formation of Vo, reduces the energy barrier of *OOH intermediates, and favors the AEM pathway. Conversely, electron‐donating metals induce lattice distortion, which can shift the O 2p band near EF. As a result, Ru–O covalency increases by activating OL and proceeds through LOM. Therefore, doping is an effective strategy to regulate OER mechanisms due to controlled orbital hybridization, charge redistribution, and providing a transition between LOM‐AEM pathways.

Support‐Catalyst Interaction in Ru‐Based Electrocatalysts

OER performance is directly affected by the catalytic interfaces. Catalyst supports provide a base for NPs dispersion and play a vital role in governing their charge and electron transfer properties. Using appropriate supports significantly lowers noble metal usage and maximizes active site exposure [158]. Strong metal‐support interactions (SMSI) can effectively stabilize coordination interfaces and electron transfer that improve catalytic performance and durability [159, 160]. The synergy in supported catalysts tailors the coordination environment and optimizes electron transport at active sites [161]. Thus, for industrial applications, it is essential that these supports sustain structural stability at high current densities and suppress dissolution.

Ru Single Atoms on Support Materials

Single‐atom catalysts consist of atomically dispersed metal atoms that serve as active sites and have recently emerged as a promising class of materials. The atomically dispersed Ru SAs on appropriate support ensure high atom utilization and tunable coordination environments that efficiently enhance OER performance. The choice of support critically influences the stability, electronic structure, and catalytic behavior of Ru single atoms, dictating their activity and long‐term durability under acidic OER conditions. For instance, Ru SA anchored on self‐supported, vertically aligned mesoporous (Ru‐N4O1‐VMC/CP) on carbon and Ti felt via the pyrolysis method for durable acidic OER [118]. Vertically grown polydopamine films on conductive supports and coordinates with Ru3+, which, upon pyrolysis, yield Ru‐N5 single‐atom sites embedded within PDA‐derived VMC. This self‐supported VMC anchors Ru sites against detachment and facilitates efficient mass transport. The electronic structure was tuned from Ru‐N5 to Ru‐N4O1, further enhancing OER performance. HAADF‐STEM analysis confirmed the uniform dispersion of Ru SA on support (Figure 12a). DFT calculations suggested that converting Ru‐N to Ru–O induces charge redistribution, optimizing the electronic structure of Ru active sites and enhancing intrinsic adsorption during acidic OER via the AEM pathway. The Ru‐N4O1 configuration exhibits a significantly lower Gibbs free energy barrier (2.124 eV) for *OOH formation compared to Ru‐N5 (2.868 eV), indicating accelerated reaction kinetics and improved catalytic activity. Furthermore, the reduced energy requirement for *OOH to O2 conversion highlights the beneficial role of oxygen coordination in promoting deprotonation and overall OER performance (Figure 12b). In a PEM dynamic system, Ru‐N4O1‐VMC/TF was employed as an anode and Pt/C as the cathode, a low cell voltage of 1.791 V was attained at 1 A cm− 2, demonstrating operational stability for over 720 h (Figure 12c).

The symmetric arrangement of Ru sites forms an atom array that favors the OPM OER pathway. STEM analysis revealed the formation of Ru atomic chains and ordered atom arrays (Figure 12d). Ru Nanoparticles Anchored on Metal Substrate

Anchoring Ru‐based NPs on appropriate metal supports can enhance the geometric OER performance through tailoring Ru mass loading effectively. SMSI stabilizes the active sites of NPs, prevents detachment under harsh acidic conditions, and optimizes water dissociation kinetics for improved catalytic performance [83]. Moreover, the conductive support promotes efficient electron and charge transport between the catalyst, electrolyte, and electrode [162]. A study has highlighted the interaction between Ru NPs with various titanium oxides (TiO, Ti4O7, TiO2) supports via a non‐stoichiometric approach in which Vo are periodically arranged [36]. Among them, Ti4O7 demonstrated better structural strength than TiO, higher conductivity compared to TiO2, and suitable MSI, promoting Ru/Ti4O7 to function efficiently for OER. Its higher work function enriches Ru with electrons, mitigating Ru dissolution during OER, while the minimal work function difference (ΔΦ = 0.30 eV) between Ti4O7and Ru reduces the interfacial Schottky barrier and facilitates charge transfer. Notably, MSI in Ru/Ti4O7 induces electron enhancement at Ru sites, which suppresses the LOM and promotes deprotonation via stable Ti–O–Ru units improving OER performance and durability (Figure 13a). Moreover, Ru/Ti4O7 exhibited lower cell voltage and was stable for 200 h at 200 mA cm−2 (Figure 13b).

Another study proposed a pluralistic electronic structure optimization strategy to improve Ru stability through in situ development of MnxRu1‐xO2 on MnO2‐coated carbon fibers (MnxRu1‐xO2/MnO2/CFs) [119] A facile in situ electrodeposition followed by ion‐exchange strategy was employed to fabricate MnxRu1‐xO2/MnO2/CFs (Figure 13c). In the MnxRu1‐xO2 solid solution, Mn dopants act as electron acceptors due to their higher electronegativity and activate Ru sites. Supported catalysts can stabilize the Ru–O coordination environment and influence the OER mechanisms by optimizing interfacial charge transfer between support and catalyst nanoparticles. SMSI effectively anchors Ru active sites, which inhibits OL participation. Moreover, SMSI induces electron redistribution to shift the d‐band center with respect to EF and modifies Ru–O bond strength, which further tunes the adsorption energies of (*OH, *O, *OOH) intermediates. Conductive support stabilizes lower oxidation states of Ru and resists overoxidation, thereby leading to promote through AEM pathway. However, oxygen‐deficient support may induce strong orbital hybridization, providing high Ru–O covalency, which can partially activate OL and trigger LOM contributions. As a result, supported catalysts can be promoted by a transition between AEM‐LOM pathways depending on the electronic interaction of support and catalyst.

Conclusion and Perspectives

The limited stability of Ru‐based catalyst layers in PEMWEs remains a major challenge for large‐scale applications. The primary reason is the severe overoxidation of Ru that leads to dissolution via the formation of unstable and soluble RuO4 species. Nevertheless, Ru‐based electrocatalysts are still highly attractive alternatives for Ir‐based catalysts in PEMWEs. Thus, activity‐stability tradeoffs are indispensable for Ru‐based catalysts to achieve comparable performance in PEMWEs. Extensive efforts have aimed to balance activity and stability, including the design of stable Ru single‐atom catalysts, alloying with non‐noble metals, electronic structure modulation through doping, and strong interactions with various supports (Figure 14). Despite significant progress, major challenges remain, such as large‐scale application of SACs, structural instability of alloys due to surface oxidation, lattice mismatch in heterostructures, and limited control over heteroatom doping and vacancy formation. Moreover, OL participation and surface exchange kinetics critically influence long‐term durability under PEMWE conditions. Considering these issues, future research for industrial‐scale PEMWE applications should focus on addressing the following key challenges.

Future perspectives for high performance and durable Ru catalysts in PEMWEs.

Ru Catalysts Development

Among different Ru‐based catalysts, single‐atom catalysis represents a unique and highly promising strategy for developing cost‐effective, efficient, and durable electrocatalysts. Compared with conventional nanocatalysts, single‐atom catalysts offer maximized atomic utilization, enhanced mass activity, and distinct quantum size effects, leading to superior catalytic performance. The distinct electronic and geometric arrangements make them suitable candidates for increasing the active sites density and improving atom economy by maximizing the atomic exposure thereby offering enhanced mass activity. The isolated Ru active sites anchored on suitable supports regulate the coordination environment and optimize reaction pathways. Moreover, strong metal‐support interaction stabilizes Ru active sites, controls lattice oxygen participation, inhibits dissolution, and improves structure‐activity and selectivity relationships. However, under PEMWE conditions, sustaining the long‐term durability of Ru single‐atom catalysts remains a major challenge. Because their overall metal loading is very low compared to conventional catalysts, electrochemical dissolution and atom migration could lead to degradation due to rapid loss of active sites. This highlights the need for further development of robust coordination environments and strong support‐metal interactions.

Alloying with non‐noble metals and heterometal doping are effective strategies for the structure regulation of Ru‐based catalysts, which reduces noble metal usage and shield Ru active sites by dynamic surface regeneration. The synergistic electronic and geometric arrangements further optimize and stabilize the oxidation states of Ru. Introduction of heterometal tunes the d‐band center, modulates intermediate adsorption, induces lattice strain and charge redistribution, creating more unsaturated active sites and reducing reaction barriers. Furthermore, heteroatom doping regulates Ru–O ‐M charge transfer, improves kinetics and conductivity, optimizes surface reconstruction of Ru through tailoring the Voand lattice oxygen participation, and protects Ru dissolution in harsh acidic conditions. Nonetheless, atomically dispersed Ru atoms may be susceptible to agglomeration and dissolution, whereas Ru SACs alloys suffer from phase dissociation and non‐noble metal leaching. Stability is affected by the metastable structure of Ru catalysts since surface regeneration may disturb the atomic configurations in acidic OER conditions. In addition, some conventional carbon or oxide‐based support materials are also prone to corrosion that may reduce metal‐support interactions and increase Ru dissolution. Therefore, a comprehensive understanding and optimization of reaction conditions, precise control over chemical composition, and the introduction of corrosion‐resistant support materials are essential for practical PEMWE applications.

Beyond atomic‐level electronic modulation, structural engineering of Ru‐based catalysts and catalyst layers is also critical for translating intrinsic activity into practical PEMWE performance. Under high‐current‐density operation, rapid oxygen evolution can lead to bubble accumulation, catalyst‐layer flooding, limited water access, and increased local mass‐transport resistance. Therefore, large pore supports, hierarchical porous architectures, and porosity of RuO2 catalysts could be considered. Properly engineered pore size, pore connectivity, and catalyst‐layer porosity can facilitate oxygen bubble release, improve water transport to active Ru sites, and enhance catalyst utilization. In addition, porous and interconnected catalyst layers may reduce local concentration gradients and mechanical stress caused by gas evolution, thereby contributing to improved durability. Thus, the future Ru‐based anode design should integrate atomic‐level stabilization with catalyst‐layer and pore‐structure engineering to meet industrial PEMWE requirements.

Dynamic Structural Analysis and In Situ Characterization

Structural collapse of Ru catalysts is the predominant reason for their poor stability due to OL participation in OER. Thus, it is crucial to modify the electronic structure to facilitate the surface reconstruction under PEMWEs conditions. A modified electronic structure of a typical Ru‐based catalyst provides more robust OER due to changes in the surface electronic properties. A typical Ru‐based catalyst prefers LOM predominantly for OER due to strong interaction of OL with the reactants. However, modified electronic surface balances the surface energy and changes the reaction mechanism partially or completely. In other words, the modified surface of Ru catalyst stabilizes the doped species through surface regeneration, variable metal oxidation states, orbital hybridization, and electron perturbation, which greatly influence electrocatalytic performance. The controlled structural regeneration is important for balancing activity and stability and provides a promising approach to safeguard active sites in Ru‐based catalysts. Furthermore, under operating conditions, it is challenging to establish a clear structure‐performance relationship due to the complex dynamic structural modifications. Although a performance‐stability tradeoff is often observed in AEM, LOM, and OPM, it is still demanding to intrinsically correlate the governing factors that influence performance and stability. Therefore, it is important to qualitatively evaluate the origin of structure‐performance‐stability relationships in Ru‐based OER catalysts.

Conventional DFT alone cannot thoroughly elucidate dynamic bonding and electron transfer mechanisms at different applied potentials. Therefore, advanced in situ characterization techniques (DEMS, Raman, XRD, and XPS), when linked with DFT analysis, can offer useful mechanistic insights. These techniques can analyze the structure‐performance relationship by identifying reaction intermediates precisely, explaining dissolution mechanisms and reaction kinetics. In‐situ characterization also provides a clear insight into the actual coordination environment and reaction mechanism, thereby advancing the development and optimization of Ru‐based electrocatalysts in PEMWEs.

Tuning Ru–O Covalency and Reaction Mechanism

A particular OER reaction mechanism depends on the M–O bond strength in OER catalysts. For instance, in IrOx‐based catalysts, the OER primarily proceeds via the AEM pathway, which is attributed to a relatively weaker Ir‐O bond arising from its more ionic character. However, in Ru‐based catalysts, LOM is the primary OER mechanism due to a much stronger Ru–O bond formation because of higher covalency arising from stronger Ru 4d‐ O 2p hybridization, which promotes OL participation and favors the LOM pathway during OER. Covalency substantially impacts the stability of Ru‐based electrocatalysts in PEMWEs. Strong Ru–O covalency triggers the OL, favoring LOM that lowers the adsorption energy barrier of O‐intermediates and effectively promotes electron transfer, thereby improving catalyst performance. However, participation of OL generates O‐vacancies, induces structural dissolution via Ru overoxidation, and sacrifices long‐term stability in PEMWEs. Whereas weak Ru–O covalency favors AEM that control OL participation and improve stability but they mainly suffer from linear scaling relation due to the formation of unfavorable adsorption intermediates, which decrease intrinsic catalytic activity. Therefore, it gives us space to tune the M–O bond and push a particular reaction mechanism to our desired one on the catalyst surface. For example, M–O bonding could be optimized by altering the atomic distance between oxygen atoms and active metal through Ru single‐atoms, TM doping, and reconstruction approaches. These strategies alter the electron density around the metal atom, and interaction between O atoms and active metals changes the energy barrier for a particular reaction mechanism to proceed.

An optimized Ru–O covalency can push the mechanism from pure LOM to a balanced AEM‐LOM and retain Ru‐level activity while approaching Ir‐like stability. Additionally, moderate Ru–O covalency also promotes OPM, which avoids Vo generation, bypasses OL involvement, and eliminates linear scaling limitation, leading to better performance and stability. However, OPM requires a precise tuning of Ru–O covalency, control surface regeneration, active site accessibility, and optimization of kinetics at high current densities. Thus, an optimized Ru–O covalency is crucial for balancing performance and stability. It is recommended to rationally develop Ru‐based electrocatalysts towards PEMWEs that focus on the correlation of Ru–O covalency with respect to AEM, LOM, and OPM. Moreover, it is important to establish quantitative studies to investigate the adsorption energies of reaction intermediates, Vo formation, and Ru overoxidation coupled with operando techniques and DFT calculations to understand the effect of Ru–O covalency.

Additionally, the catalyst support not only acts as a static carrier for Ru active sites, but it can engage with catalyst NPs under dynamic reaction conditions. For example, a particular phase of catalyst support can guide the OER mechanism, such as α‐MnO2 supported Ru shows that Ru substitutes the Mn sites which changes the Ru arrangements leading to lower Ru‐Ru bond distance and results in switching the LOM to OPM. Thus, lattice phases of support provided a dynamic platform for the Ru active sites to divert the corresponding reaction mechanism. Under dynamic operating conditions of PEMWE, mechanistic studies can be validated using operando 18O labeling isotope to ensure the structure regeneration and various oxidation states of Ru. Hence, Ru–O covalency is a comprehensive criterion to correlate the electronic arrangements, reaction pathways, activity‐stability tradeoff, and contributing towards the advancement of industrial Ru based electrocatalysts for PEMWEs.

Gap Between Half‐Cell and Single Cell Testing

Most of the literature use half‐cell (three electrode setup) to evaluate catalyst performance, where test conditions differ significantly from a practical PEMWEs setup. Both the half cell and single cell testing are equally important to evaluate the catalyst performance and durability. While instrumental, both techniques possess inherent limitations that preclude a comprehensive evaluation of industrial‐grade catalysts. Half‐cell studies, for example, are primarily restricted to assessing the intrinsic catalytic activity governed by reaction kinetics; however, they fail to replicate the complex mass transport and ohmic environments‐such as local protonic resistance which significantly influence the MEA performance. Moreover, the stability evaluation of same catalyst can significantly differ between single cell and half‐cell operating conditions such as operating voltage which plays a crucial role in determining the different aspects of durability of a particular catalyst. Thus, it is impossible to predict the practical applications of a catalyst until it is tested under realistic conditions of PEMWE that can resemble the industrial conditions.

Therefore, it is important to distinguish between intrinsic catalyst stability measured in half‐cell configurations and MEA durability measured under PEMWE single‐cell operation. Half‐cell tests mainly provide information on catalyst‐level degradation, such as Ru dissolution, Ru over‐oxidation, Vo evolution, and changes in Ru–O covalency. In contrast, MEA durability is affected not only by intrinsic catalyst stability but also by CL microstructure, ionomer degradation, porosity, interfacial contact resistance, water/gas transport, and local mass‐transport limitations. Therefore, excellent half‐cell activity or stability does not necessarily guarantee comparable durability in PEMWE devices. Future Ru‐based catalyst studies should combine intrinsic electrochemical evaluation with MEA‐level validation to establish a more reliable link between catalyst design and practical operating durability. A complete evaluation will also help to develop next generation catalyst for better analysis of catalyst scalability, energy utilization efficiency, overall system cost and safety measurements.

Large‐Scale Fabrication and Applications

The practical application of certain technology is impossible until it can be produced in bulk quantities or at least at gram scale. Particularly, the fine‐tuned catalyst with delicate morphologies is very unlikely to produce on gram scale due to significantly refined preparation methods and conditions. In Ru based catalysis, production is carried out at milligrams scale in laboratories, and it is challenging to produce these catalysts at large scale for PEMWEs applications. Yet, the development and utilization of Ru anode catalysts on industrial scale is crucial to overcome the scarcity and high cost of Ir.

Similarly, the application of these newly developed catalysts in practical PEMWEs is yet another crucial aspect as mostly these catalysts are premature to be executed in realistic operating conditions of PEMWEs. Recently, various high‐performance catalysts have been reported in the literature Table (3). However, there is a significant gap in current Ru based performance and Department of Energy (DOE) technical targets of PEMWEs stack and systems (total PGM loading: 0.5 mg cm−2 2026 target, 0.125 mg cm−2 ultimate target; performance: 3 A cm−2 at 1.8 V 2026 and 3 A cm−2 at 1.6 V ultimate; lifetime: 80,000 h target; degradation rate: 2 mV kh−1) 20,000 h needed for industrial use. For example, one Ru‐based catalyst showed stable operation for 1,300 h at 1 A cm− 2 with a cell voltage of only 1.5 V, using a Nafion 115 membrane. This is significant performance for a typical Ru based catalyst considering their fast degradation. However, 1,300 h is still much less than the 20,000 h needed for industrial use. Most of Ru‐based catalysts demonstrate stability for over 100 h at ampere‐level current densities, only few sustain performance beyond the 200‐hours, which is significantly lower than DOE targets. This gap becomes more sever when Ru loading is reduced under <0.1 mgRu cm−2 because ultra‐thin CLs suffer from poor electrical percolation weak catalyst‐ionomer contact, nonuniform current distribution, and accelerated local degradation. To bridge the existing gap, next‐generation anodes must maintain structural and kinetic integrity at high loads (>1‐3 A cm− 2) for tens of thousands of hours. Achieving this requires catalyst architectures that suppress OL participation and minimize metal dissolution. Furthermore, utilizing advanced PEMWE diagnostic techniques is essential to probe the evolving relationship between electrode microstructure, charge transfer kinetics, and mass transport resistance. To meet industrial PEMWE requirements, Ru‐based anodes require atomic and electrode‐level optimization. The key strategies include tuning Ru–O covalency, stabilizing Ru with dopants/supports and suppressing excessive lattice oxygen participation. Similarly, optimizing catalyst‐layer structure, ionomer content, porosity, and PTL/CL contact for high‐current operation are crucial to meet the technical target. Additionally, standardized testing, including polarization, EIS/HFR, durability at ≥1‐3 A cm−2, accelerated stress tests, and post‐mortem/operando diagnostics, is essential to identify catalyst dissolution, membrane degradation, interfacial resistance, and mass‐transport losses over long term testing.

In summary, the rational design of Ru SACs integrated with advanced operando characterization techniques with robust theoretical calculations and device level operation provides an effective strategy to understand structure‐performance relationships and mechanisms. Catalyst development and testing under standardized practical PEMWEs operating conditions could accelerate the development of cost‐effective, highly active, and stable Ru‐based electrocatalysts for PEMWEs.
