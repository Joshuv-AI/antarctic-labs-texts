Nonlinear inverse association between hemoglobin-to-red cell distribution width ratio and short- and long-term mortality in patients with cardiac arrest: A retrospective cohort study based on the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care IV database
Authors: Li Zhang, Xiangxin Liao, Jia Huang, Zhaohui Zhang, Shuhan Cai, Lu Li, Zhiyong Peng, Gaosheng Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveCardiac arrest is a life-threatening condition associated with high mortality. This study aimed to investigate the association between the hemoglobin-to-red cell distribution width ratio (HRR) measured within 24 h of admission and 28-, 90-, and 180-day all-cause mortality in patients with cardiac arrest and evaluate its potential early prognostic value.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study included patients with cardiac arrest admitted to the intensive care unit, identified from the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care IV database. The exposure was HRR measured within 24 h of admission, and the outcomes were 28-, 90-, and 180-day all-cause mortality. Multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression, restricted cubic spline, and threshold effect analyses were performed to evaluate the association between HRR and mortality after adjustment for potential confounders.ResultsIn total, 1409 patients were included in the final analysis and stratified into quartiles according to HRR levels. Patients in the lowest HRR quartile had significantly higher 28-, 90-, and 180-day mortality than those in the highest quartile (all p < 0.001). These associations remained significant after adjustment for potential confounders. Subgroup analyses indicated that renal disease status significantly modified the association between HRR and mortality. Restricted cubic spline analysis demonstrated a significant nonlinear inverse association between HRR and both short- and long-term mortality p < 0.001). However, receiver operating characteristic curve comparisons demonstrated that red cell distribution width had higher area under the curve values than HRR at all follow-up time points, and the incremental prognostic value of HRR beyond single red cell distribution width alone could not be confirmed in the current analysis.ConclusionLower HRRs were significantly associated with higher risks of 28-, 90-, and 180-day all-cause mortality in patients with cardiac arrest. HRR may serve as a readily available alternative composite hematological biomarker with potential auxiliary clinical value.

Introduction

Cardiac arrest (CA) is a life-threatening medical emergency characterized by the sudden cessation of cardiac mechanical activity, resulting in systemic ischemia and hypoxia. Despite advances in cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) and defibrillation, achieving return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC) remains challenging. Moreover, even among patients who achieve ROSC, subsequent outcomes remain poor, with persistently high mortality and substantial medical and economic burdens on patients, their families, and society.1,2 Therefore, identifying clinical indicators for accessing and predicting survival outcomes in patients with CA is essential for accurate risk stratification, optimal allocation of healthcare resources, and improved patient outcomes.

Currently, prognostic assessment in patients with CA primarily relies on neurological function scores, specific biomarkers, and imaging examinations.3–6 However, the clinical utility of these approaches may be limited by factors such as timing of assessment, the need for complex laboratory testing, or high costs, which may restrict their application in early assessment and dynamic monitoring in the intensive care unit (ICU). Therefore, identifying a simple, noninvasive, and reproducible indicator that can comprehensively reflect patients’ physiological reserve remains an important priority in clinical research.

The hemoglobin-to-red cell distribution width ratio (HRR) is a novel, simple, and cost-effective hematological indicator. Hemoglobin (Hb) reflects the oxygen-carrying capacity of the blood and nutritional status, whereas red cell distribution width (RDW) reflects variability in red blood cell size and is closely associated with systemic inflammation, oxidative stress, and tissue hypoperfusion.7–10 Previous studies have demonstrated that HRR is a useful prognostic marker in various critical illnesses, including sepsis, acute myocardial infarction, and heart failure, with lower HRRs associated with poorer clinical outcomes.11–13 Recent evidence has also suggested that HRR is clinically relevant in inflammatory cardiovascular diseases. One study has shown that HRR may serve as a valuable biomarker for assessing disease severity and prognosis in patients with myocarditis. 14 Compared with conventional indicators, including troponin and C-reactive protein (CRP), HRR may provide additional prognostic information. Another study has indicated that HRR may be a useful biomarker for assessing disease severity and informing therapeutic decision making in patients with acute pericarditis. However, the association between HRR and both short- and long-term mortality, particularly mortality beyond 28 days, in patients with CA remains insufficiently investigated. 15

Therefore, this retrospective cohort study aimed to investigate the association between HRR and 28-, 90-, and 180-day all-cause mortality in patients with CA, using data from the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care IV (MIMIC-IV) database. We hypothesized that lower HRRs are independently associated with an increased risk of mortality. It may serve as a novel and simple clinical reference for early preliminary risk stratification of patients with CA and provide a theoretical basis for future intervention studies involving these patients.

Materials and Methods

Data source

This retrospective cohort study utilized data extracted from the MIMIC-IV database and was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki of 1975, as revised in 2024. The reporting of this study conforms to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines. 16 The database includes critically ill patients admitted to the ICU or the emergency department of Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center in Boston between 2008 and 2022. All patient details have been deidentified to ensure patient confidentiality. A designated investigator successfully completed the Collaborative Institutional Training Initiative program to obtain database access privileges, and the study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center (Record ID: 66989051).

Study participants and data selection

This study enrolled adult patients admitted to the ICU with ROSC following CA. 17 We included patients from the MIMIC-IV v2.2 database who were aged >18 years, admitted to the ICU for the first time during their initial hospitalization, had an ICU stay >24 h, and were diagnosed with CA at hospital admission according to the International Classification of Diseases Ninth and Tenth Revision codes. Patients were excluded if they met any of the following exclusion criteria: (a) had a non-first ICU admission; (b) were aged <18 years; (c) had missing data on Hb level and RDW; (d) had an ICU stay <24 h; and (e) had multiple hospital admissions. The patient selection methodology is presented in Figure 1.

The extracted data included the following: (a) demographic information, including age (years), weight (kg), sex (male or female), and race (White, Black, or Others); (b) vital signs, including heart rate (beats/min (bpm)); mean blood pressure (MBP; mmHg); respiration rate (RR; breaths/min); body temperature (T; °C); oxygen saturation (SpO2; %); and scores such as the simplified acute physiology score II (SAPS II), Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score and, Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI); (c) comorbid diseases, including myocardial infarction, congestive heart failure, peripheral vascular disease, cerebrovascular disease, dementia, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, rheumatic disease, peptic ulcer disease, liver disease, diabetes, paraplegia, renal disease, malignant cancer, and metastatic solid tumor; (d) laboratory test results obtained within 24 h of ICU admission, including white blood cell (WBC; ×109/L) count; Hb (g/L), platelet (PLT; ×109/L) count, phosphate (mmol/L), alanine aminotransferase (ALT; U/L), aspartate aminotransferase (AST; U/L), total bilirubin (TBIL; µmol/L), creatinine (Cr; µmol/L), blood urea nitrogen (BUN; µmol/L), glucose (Glu; mg/dL), sodium (Na+;mmol/L), potassium (K+; mmol/L), chloride (Cl−; mmol/L), calcium (Ca2+; mmol/L), base excess (BE; mmol/L), and lactate (Lac; mmol/L) levels; hematocrit (HCT; %); RDW (%); potential of hydrogen (pH); partial pressure of carbon dioxide (pCO2; mmHg); partial pressure of oxygen in arterial blood to fraction of inspired oxygen ratio (pO2/FiO2; mmHg); prothrombin time (PT; s); and partial thromboplastin time (PTT; s); (e) treatments involving the use of vasoactive drugs, renal replacement therapy (RRT), and mechanical ventilation; and (e) outcomes, including the hospital length of stay (LOS); ICU LOS; and 28-, 90-, and 180-day mortality. All laboratory data and vital signs analyzed in this study were derived from the first measurements obtained within 24 h after ICU admission. HRR was calculated by dividing the Hb level (g/L) by RDW (%). Participants were categorized into four groups based on HRR quartiles. To explore the association between different HRRs and 28-, 90-, and 180-day all-cause mortality, enrolled patients were categorized into four groups according to the HRR quartiles within the study cohort (with the 25th, 50th, and 75th percentiles used as cutoff points).

Statistical analyses

Continuous variables were reported as mean ± SD for normally distributed data and as median (interquartile ranges) for non-normally distributed data, whereas categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. Between-group differences were assessed using the unpaired Student's t-test (for normally distributed continuous variables), Mann–Whitney U test (for skewed continuous variables), and chi-squared test (for categorical variables).

Univariable and multivariable Cox regression analyses as well as restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis were performed to evaluate the relationship between elevated HRR and 28-, 90-, and 180-day all-cause mortality in the ICU. Kaplan–Meier curves with log-rank tests were used to compare cumulative survival rates between high and low HRR groups. Six hierarchical regression models were established, adjusting for potential confounders identified from existing literature and clinical experience. Model 1 was unadjusted. Model 2 was adjusted for age, sex, and race. Model 3 was further adjusted for CCI, myocardial infarction, congestive heart failure, peripheral vascular disease, cerebrovascular disease, dementia, chronic pulmonary disease, rheumatic disease, peptic ulcer disease, liver disease, diabetes, paraplegia, renal disease, malignant cancer, and metastatic solid tumor, based on Model 2. Model 4 was further adjusted for heart rate, MBP, RR, T, and SpO2, based on Model 3. Model 5 was further adjusted for SAPS II; SOFA score; WBC count; PLT; HCT; ALT, AST, TBIL, Cr, phosphate, BUN, Glu, Na+, K+, Cl−, Ca2+, BA, and Lac levels; pH; pCO2; pO2/FiO2; PT; and PTT, based on Model 4. Model 6 was further adjusted for vasoactive drugs use, RRT, mechanical ventilation, and ICU LOS, based on Model 5. Outcomes were expressed as hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

Subgroup analyses were conducted, stratified by age, sex, race, liver disease, diabetes, renal disease, vasoactive drugs use, RRT, and mechanical ventilation. All statistical analyses were performed using Stata version 17.0 and the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) software (IBM SPSS version 26.0), with a two-tailed p-value <0.05 defined as statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics

As shown in Table 1, in total, 1409 patients were ultimately enrolled in the study, with a mean age of 66.4 ± 16.3 years; 61.2% of the participants were male (n = 862). The 28-, 90-, 180-day all-cause mortality rates were 47.3% (667/1409), 53.7% (756/1409), and 56.2% (792/1409), respectively. The mean HRR was 0.7 (0.7 ± 0.2). Based on all-cause mortality outcomes at 28, 90, and 180 days, participants were stratified into survival and non-survival groups at the corresponding time points, respectively. HRRs in the non-survival groups were significantly lower than those in the survival groups (p < 0.001) (Supplementary Table 1).

Furthermore, the enrolled patients were divided into four groups based on HRR quartiles: (a) Q1 (HRR < 0.514); (b) Q2 (HRR: 0.514–0.662); (c) Q3 (HRR: 0.663–0.860); and (d) Q4 (HRR ≥ 0.860). Patients in the lowest HRR groups (Q1) were older and predominantly male, with higher SOFA score and SAPS II; elevated levels of CCI, Lac, TBIL, Cr, and BUN; and prolonged PTs and PTTs. They also had a higher prevalence of comorbidities, including congestive heart failure, peripheral vascular disease, liver disease, diabetes, renal disease, malignant cancer, and metastatic solid tumor. Additionally, the use of vasoactive drugs and RRT was more common among these patients. As HRR increased, phosphate, TBIL, Cr, BUN, and Lac levels; PT; and PTT demonstrated a declining trend. Notably, all-cause mortality across all evaluated time points exhibited a statistically significant decreasing trend across HRR groups; the 28-day mortality was 58% in Q1, 50.7% in Q2, 41.1% in Q3, and 39.7% in Q4 (p < 0.001). The 90-day mortality was 67.3% in Q1, 55.8% in Q2, 48.2% in Q3, and 43.3% in Q4 (p < 0.001). Furthermore, the 180-day mortality was 70.2% in Q1, 60.1% in Q2, 50.4% in Q3, and 44.2% in Q4 (p < 0.001). These findings consistently demonstrated a robust inverse association between higher HRRs and reduced mortality risk (Table 1).

Univariate Cox regression analysis

For each unit increase in HRR, the risk of 28-, 90-, 180-day mortality was reduced by 58.19% (HR = 0.4181, 95% CI: 0.2933–0.596, p < 0.001), 63.69% (HR = 0.3631, 95% CI: 0.2596–0.5079, p < 0.001), and 66.5% (HR = 0.335, 95% CI: 0.2411–0.4654, p < 0.001), respectively. When patients were dichotomized into two equal groups based on HRR (Q1 and Q2), the HRs for Q2 (with Q1 as the reference) at 28, 90, 180 days were 0.699 (95% CI: 0.5995–0.8149, p < 0.001), 0.6776 (95% CI: 0.5865–0.7828, p < 0.001), and 0.6538 (95% CI: 0.5677–0.753, p < 0.001), respectively. Further stratification into tertiles (Q1, Q2, and Q3) revealed a graded decrease in the mortality risk at 28 days (Q2 vs. Q1: HR = 0.7591 (95% CI: 0.6343–0.9085, p < 0.005) and Q3 vs. Q1: HR = 0.6345 (95% CI: 0.526–0.7655, p < 0.001)), 90 days (Q2 vs. Q1: HR = 0.724 (95% CI: 0.6119–0.8567, p < 0.001); and Q3 vs. Q1: HR = 0.5857 (95% CI: 0.4907–0.6991, p < 0.001)), and 180 days (Q2 vs. Q1: HR = 0.734 (95% CI: 0.6234–0.8642, p < 0.001); and Q3 vs. Q1: HR = 0.5564 (95% CI: 0.4673–0.6626, p < 0.001)). In addition, when patients were stratified into quartiles (Q1–Q4) based on HRRs, the risks of 28-, 90-, and 180-day mortality decreased significantly, particularly in Q3 and Q4. Detailed information is presented in Table 2. Our results indicate a significant dose-dependent association between lower HRRs and higher short- and long-term mortality risk.

Multivariate Cox regression analysis

The effect of HRR on 28-, 90-, and 180-day all-cause mortality were assessed using the Cox proportional hazard model. When HRR was treated as a continuous variable, it was positively associated with 28-day mortality. When HRR was treated as a continuous variable, in the unadjusted Model 1, each unit increase in HRR was associated with a 58% decrease in the risk of 28-day mortality (HR = 0.42, 95% CI: 0.29–0.6, p < 0.001) (Table 3). In Model 2 (adjusted for age, sex, and race), Model 3 (adjusted for variables in Model 2 + CCI, myocardial infarct, congestive heart failure, peripheral vascular disease, cerebrovascular disease, dementia, chronic pulmonary disease, rheumatic disease, peptic ulcer disease, liver disease, diabetes, paraplegia, renal disease, malignant cancer, and metastatic solid tumor), Model 4 (adjusted for variables in Model 3 + heart rate, MBP, RR, T, and SpO2), Model 5 (adjusted for variables in Model 4 + SAPS II; SOFA score; WBC count; PLT; HCT; BE, ALT, AST, TBIL, Cr, phosphate, BUN, Glu, Na+, K+, Cl−, Ca2+, and Lac levels; pH; pCO2; pO2/FiO2; PT; and PTT), and Model 6 (adjusted for variables in Model 5 + vasoactive drugs use, RRT, mechanical ventilation, and ICU LOS), the results showed statistically significant changes. When the patients were divided into equal groups based on HRR (Q1 and Q2), with the Q1 group used as the reference group, the results remained significant in different models. To further explore the effect of HRR, patients were divided into three or four groups based on HRR (Q1, Q2, and Q3), with the Q1 group serving as the reference. The results revealed that higher HRRs were associated with a more significant reduction in the mortality risk. Furthermore, the trends across adjusted Models 2, 4, 5, and 6 were consistency with that observed in the unadjusted Model 1, demonstrating a clear dose–response relationship that validates the predictive value of HRR for 28-day prognosis in patients with CA. An increase in HRR was closely associated with a reduction in the 90- and 180-day mortality, as presented in Supplementary Tables 2 and 3. However, according to receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve comparisons, RDW exhibited higher area under the curve (AUC) values than HRR at all follow-up time points, and the incremental prognostic value of HRR beyond single RDW could not be confirmed in the current analysis (Supplementary Figure 2).

In contrast, no significant interactions were observed for age, sex, race, liver disease, diabetes, vasoactive drugs use, RRT, and mechanical ventilation (Table 4). The additive interaction between HRR and 90- and 180-day mortality yielded similar results (Supplementary Table 4 and Table 5).

Nonlinear relationship analysis

Following covariate adjustment in Model 6, RCS analysis demonstrated a nonlinear relationship between HRR and 28-day mortality (Figure 3), 90-day mortality (Figure S1), and 180-day mortality (Figure S2). As shown in Table 5, the corresponding inflection points were identified as 0.372, 0.370, and 0.376, respectively. For values below the inflection point, the effect estimates and 95% CIs were all 0 (0–0, p < 0.05). In contrast, above the inflection point, HRR exhibited a strong inverse association with mortality, with HRs of 0.081 (95% CI: 0.035–0.186, p < 0.001) for 28-day mortality, 0.089 (95% CI: 0.041–0.194, p < 0.001) for 90-day mortality, and 0.093 (95% CI: 0.043–0.202, p < 0.001) for 180-day mortality.

Discussion

This study leveraged the large-scale, high-quality MIMIC-IV database to evaluate mortality at multiple follow-up time points and investigate the association between HRR and mortality in patients with CA. 18 In total, 1409 patients were included in the final analysis. The principal finding was a significant nonlinear inverse association between HRR and both short- and long-term mortality in patients with CA. At each evaluated time point, patients in the lowest HRR quartile had significantly higher mortality rates than those in the highest quartile. This association remained significant after adjustment for potential confounders. Subgroup analysis further indicated that the association between HRR and mortality differed according to renal disease status, with a significant interaction observed. These findings suggest that HRR has potential value as a prognostic indicator of both short- and long-term outcomes in patients with CA and can contribute to clinical risk stratification.

CA is defined as the sudden cessation of cardiac mechanical activity, leading to the interruption of systemic circulation and subsequent oxygen deprivation of vital organs. 19 It therefore constitutes a life-threatening medical emergency. 20 The abrupt loss of cardiac output triggers a cascade of pathophysiological processes, including systemic ischemia–reperfusion injury, mitochondrial dysfunction, excessive production of reactive oxygen species, and dysregulated inflammatory responses, all of which may further impair organ function and adversely affect clinical outcomes. 21 As a major global clinical challenge, CA is associated with a high disease burden and poor prognosis, imposing substantial pressure on healthcare systems worldwide.1,22 Despite advances in resuscitation techniques and post-resuscitation care over recent decades, overall survival and long-term functional outcomes after CA remain unsatisfactory, underscoring the need to further elucidate its pathophysiological mechanisms and prognostic determinants and develop targeted interventions. 23

Previous studies have primarily focused on the associations of individual hematological indicators, such as Hb level and RDW, with prognosis in patients with CA.24–27 Urben et al. evaluated the mean and peak RDW values at admission and reported that RDW independently predicted adverse neurological outcomes at discharge; however, the results were limited to neurological functional outcomes. 26 Another study examined changes in RDW during ICU hospitalization, using 30- and 360-day all-cause mortality as outcomes, and reported that both elevated RDW at admission and increases in RDW during hospitalization were independently associated with a higher risk of mortality in patients with CA. 27 In the present study, HRR was evaluated as a composite hematological indicator to investigate its association with short- and long-term mortality at 28, 90, and 180 days in patients with CA. Our findings demonstrated a nonlinear inverse association between HRR and mortality, suggesting that HRR serves as a simple composite marker for prognostic assessment in this population. Existing studies have shown that low Hb levels, which may reflect anemia, are associated with reduced oxygen-carrying capacity and may exacerbate ischemic and hypoxic injury to the myocardium and brain after CA, thereby contributing to an increased risk of death.28,29 Elevated RDW reflects greater heterogeneity in red blood cell size, which may arise from altered erythropoiesis or increased red blood cell destruction and has been associated with systemic inflammation, oxidative stress, malnutrition, and adverse outcomes in critically ill patients. By integrating Hb and RDW data, HRR may provide a more comprehensive representation of the balance between the oxygen-carrying capacity and red blood cell heterogeneity than either parameter alone. The observed inverse association between HRR and mortality may therefore reflect the combined prognostic implications of these two hematological characteristics. Notably, RDW consistently showed higher AUC values than HRR at all evaluated time points, suggesting that the prognostic performance of HRR is driven largely by its RDW component rather than by the ratio itself. Notably, ROC comparison results across all evaluated time points demonstrated that RDW consistently outperformed HRR in terms of AUC values, which indicates that HRR cannot be regarded as a superior biomarker compared with isolated RDW, and the incremental prognostic value of HRR beyond its single component RDW cannot be confirmed based on current data. Despite the lower discriminative AUC of HRR as a composite index combining Hb and RDW, HRR can jointly reflect oxygen transport function, erythrocyte heterogeneity, and multiple pathological disturbances after CA, whereas RDW alone only describes red blood cell size variation, without providing any information regarding the oxygen-carrying capacity. HRR is a low-cost index that can be calculated using routine blood test data, and it delivers multidimensional physiological data that RDW alone cannot provide, enabling comprehensive evaluation of patients’ physiological reserves.

Furthermore, to the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to systematically evaluate the prognostic value of HRR in critically ill patients with CA. Previous studies have demonstrated significant associations between HRR and adverse outcomes in cardiovascular conditions, including myocardial infarction, heart failure, and hypertension.30,31 However, evidence regarding the association between HRR and both short- and long-term mortality in patients with CA, a condition characterized by systemic ischemia–reperfusion injury and marked autonomic dysfunction, remains limited. Using large-scale real-world data, our study demonstrated that lower HRRs were independently associated with higher 28-, 90-, and 180-day all-cause mortality in patients with CA. We further identified a nonlinear dose–response association and found that comorbid renal disease may modify the association between HRR and mortality. These findings extend the potential prognostic application of HRR among critically ill patients with CA and support its potential value as a simple and readily available hematological marker for risk assessment in this population.

In addition, our findings may have potential implications for risk stratification in patients with CA. First, HRR can be readily derived from routine laboratory measurements obtained within 24 h of admission and may help identify patients at increased risk of mortality at an early stage. Our nonlinear analysis suggested that mortality risk varied markedly across HRR quartiles, potentially reflecting differences in underlying physiological reserve. 32 Patients with low HRR may therefore require closer clinical attention and more comprehensive assessment. However, whether HRR-guided monitoring or management improves clinical outcomes requires further prospective investigation. Second, subgroup analysis identified a significant interaction between renal disease and the association of HRR with mortality. Patients with renal disease frequently exhibit electrolyte disturbances, altered drug metabolism, and autonomic dysfunction, which may partly contribute to this observed heterogeneity. 33 These findings suggest that renal disease status should be considered when interpreting the prognostic significance of HRR in patients with CA. Further studies are needed to determine whether incorporating comorbidity-specific information can improve individualized risk stratification.

Certain study limitations should be acknowledged. First, this was a retrospective analysis based on the MIMIC-IV database, which lacked several clinically important variables, including arrest setting (in-hospital or out-of-hospital), initial cardiac rhythm, CPR duration, time to ROSC, use of targeted temperature management, and severity of post-CA neurological injury. These unmeasured variables may have partially mediated or confounded the observed nonlinear inverse correlation between the admission HRR and all-cause mortality, serving as major sources of residual confounding and potentially biasing the true association between HRR and mortality risk. Future prospective studies should collect data on these clinically relevant variables to minimize residual confounding and further validate our findings. In addition, detailed information regarding the etiology of CA, resuscitation-related parameters, and subsequent treatment strategies was unavailable, which may also have affected the results. Second, HRR was assessed using a single measurement obtained within 24 h of admission, precluding evaluation of its dynamic changes during hospitalization and their association with prognosis. Moreover, although ROC curves were used to compare the prognostic performance of Hb, RDW, and HRR, this study did not determine whether HRR provided incremental prognostic value beyond RDW. Future prospective studies with adequate data are warranted to address this issue. Third, no external validation cohort was included. Therefore, the prognostic value of HRR requires further validation in multicenter, large-scale, prospective cohorts before its clinical application. Notably, a single-center retrospective study conducted in China among 122 patients resuscitated after out-of-hospital CA has also reported that HRR was independently associated with in-hospital prognosis and showed favorable prognostic performance in their cohort. 34 These findings are generally consistent with those of the present study and provide additional support for the potential prognostic relevance of HRR in patients with CA. Finally, the study period extended from 2008 to 2022; therefore, the specific hematology analyzer used for each complete blood count measurement could not be identified. Therefore, minor inter-instrument variability in RDW–coefficient of variation (CV) measurements cannot be completely excluded, despite the consistency observed in time-stratified sensitivity analyses.

Conclusion

HRR within 24 h of admission demonstrated a significant, nonlinear inverse association with 28-, 90-, and 180-day mortality in patients with CA. HRR may serve as a potential alternative composite hematological marker for preliminary assessment of short-term and long-term prognosis in this patient population, providing a reference for clinical risk stratification and individualized management. Its incremental predictive value beyond single RDW and generalizability across different medical centers require further prospective multicenter verification.
