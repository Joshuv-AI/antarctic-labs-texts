A cross-sectional study on the associations between serum metal exposure and thyroid hormones in chinese pregnant women
Authors: Ying Li, Tianyu Shi, Xinnan Cui, Tianshu Zhang, Xingwang Ding, Limei Chen, Jinqi Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Prenatal exposure to environmental metals has been increasingly recognized as a crucial factor influencing maternal thyroid function, which plays a vital role in regulating metabolism, maintaining pregnancy, and supporting fetal neurodevelopment. However, the effects of emerging or less-studied metals, such as titanium (Ti), selenium (Se), and strontium (Sr), on thyroid hormone homeostasis during pregnancy remain poorly understood. In this cohort of 495 pregnant women, 12 of 19 metals showed detection frequencies above 90%. After adjusting for major confounders, Ti exposure was negatively associated with Total triiodothyronine (FT4), while Se and Sr showed consistent inverse associations with Total triiodothyronine (TT3) and Free triiodothyronine (FT3), indicating potential disruption of thyroid hormone synthesis and conversion. Mixture analyses using WQS and BKMR further identified key metals contributing to altered thyroid hormone levels and suggested heterogeneous directions of association within the metal mixture. These findings reveal previously unrecognized associations between Ti, Se, Sr, and maternal thyroid hormone alterations, providing new insights into the endocrine effects of environmental metal exposure. This study expands the understanding of how both essential and non-essential metals influence maternal thyroid function, emphasizing the need for stricter environmental monitoring and preventive strategies to protect maternal endocrine health and optimize fetal developmental outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-51478-y.

Introduction

Pregnancy is a critical window during which maternal endocrine homeostasis exerts a pivotal impact on fetal growth and neurodevelopment1. Among the endocrine regulators, thyroid hormones (THs), particularly thyroxine (T4) and triiodothyronine (T3), are essential for maintaining maternal metabolism and ensuring proper fetal brain maturation2. Maternal thyroid function that varies slightly during gestation has been correlated with adverse outcomes like miscarriage, preterm birth, and impaired neurocognitive development in offspring.3,4. Recent evidence suggests that exposure to environmental trace metals may interfere with the synthesis, metabolism, and transport of thyroid hormones, thereby disrupting maternal thyroid homeostasis5–7.

In recent years, environmental exposure to metals has become a growing global concern, particularly in vulnerable populations such as pregnant women. Due to rapid industrialization, urbanization, and increased use of metal containing products, metals such as plumbum (Pb), cadmium (Cd), mercury (Hg), cuprum (Cu), zinc (Zn), and selenium (Se) are now widely detected in air, water, food, and household dust8. Pregnant women are especially susceptible to these exposures, as physiological changes during gestation, such as increased gastrointestinal absorption and altered renal excretion, which can enhance metal accumulation and transplacental transfer9. Studies from various countries have reported measurable levels of both essential and non-essential metals in maternal blood, urine, and cord blood, suggesting ubiquitous exposure even in the general population10.

Previous epidemiological and toxicological studies have revealed that several trace elements, including Cu and Zn, are indispensable for maintaining thyroid peroxidase activity and antioxidant balance11. However, excessive or deficient exposure can disturb the homeostasis of thyroid hormone production12. Titanium (Ti) and strontium (St) have been reported to influence thyroid cellular signaling and oxidative stress responses, thereby inducing functional alterations of the thyroid gland13. Moreover, Se, a critical component of glutathione peroxidase and deiodinase enzymes, is crucial for converting thyroxine (T4) to triiodothyronine (T3)14. Insufficient Se levels during pregnancy have been associated with maternal hypothyroidism, impaired fetal neurodevelopment, and adverse pregnancy outcomes15.

Abnormal maternal thyroid hormone levels during pregnancy can impair maternal metabolism and adversely affect offspring, including neurodevelopment and immune function. Even slight hormonal fluctuations in early gestation have been linked to preterm birth, miscarriage, and low birth weight. Therefore, examining the relationship between metal exposure and thyroid hormone imbalance is crucial for maternal and fetal health. This study aims to assess maternal metal exposure during pregnancy and its relationship with thyroid hormone levels, identifying key metals associated with hormonal abnormalities. The findings may inform risk assessment, early warning, and preventive strategies to reduce metal related thyroid dysfunction in pregnancy.

Material and methods

Study participants and serum collection

This cross-sectional study was conducted using the baseline survey data from Wuxi People’s Hospital, affiliated to Nanjing Medical University. This study was designed as a cross-sectional study, aiming to assess the association between environmental metal exposure during pregnancy and the thyroid function of the mothers. Pregnant women were consecutively recruited at the time of routine antenatal registration at Wuxi People’s Hospital, when they first established prenatal care. Pregnant women were enrolled according to the following criteria: (Ⅰ) at least three years of consecutive residence prior to enrollment in the Wuxi area; (Ⅱ) singleton pregnancy; (Ⅲ) no history of thyroid disorders or thyroid surgery, no family history of thyroid dysfunction, and no current usage of medications known to affect thyroid function; (Ⅳ) intention to receive prenatal care and deliver at the hospital. Pregnant women with multiple pregnancies (twins or more) and those undergoing assisted reproduction are excluded. The specific process is shown in Figure 1. Serum samples were collected at enrollment. Between September 2019 and April 2021, 636 women were initially recruited, among whom 119 with missing key covariate information and 11 with insufficient serum samples were excluded. The inclusion/exclusion criteria and questionnaire content for this cohort have been described previously16. Ultimately, a total of 495 participants were included in the subsequent study. maternal venous blood samples were collected in the second trimester using standard venipuncture techniques. Serum was separated by centrifugation within 2 hours of collection, aliquoted, and stored at –80°C until analysis. The questionnaire gathered basic demographic data (age, height, weight, education level, monthly household income) as well as behavioral characteristics such as smoking and alcohol consumption before or during pregnancy. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. At enrollment, fasting peripheral venous blood samples were collected from each participant. Serum was separated, aliquoted, and stored at −80 °C until analysis. Metal concentrations and thyroid hormone levels were measured using paired serum samples obtained from the same blood draw for each individual participant, ensuring a one-to-one correspondence between exposure and outcome measurements. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Jiangnan University Affiliated Children’s Hospital. All procedures involving human subjects were conducted in accordance with the Helsinki Declaration. Metal levels measurement in pregnant women’s serum

In maternal serum samples collected at enrollment, metal concentrations were determined17. Nineteen elements were analyzed, including Ti, V, Cr, Mn, Co, Ni, Cu, Zn, Sr, As, Se, Mo, Ag, Cd, Sn, Sb, Ba, Hg, Pb. Serum samples were digested with 0.05% (v/v) Triton X 100 and 0.1% (v/v) nitric acid, then spiked with a mixed internal standard containing 10 mg/L of Tb, In, Bi, Y, and Sc. Quantification was performed using an iCAP Q ICP-MS instrument (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Bremen, Germany).

To ensure analytical reliability, blanks containing only diluent and internal standards were processed in parallel. A certified reference material (Seronorm™ Trace Elements Serum L-1) was analyzed after every 20 samples to verify accuracy. Additionally, pooled serum quality controls (10% of total specimens) and blanks (one per 10 samples) were included throughout the run. The relative standard deviations (RSDs) for quality control and pooled samples ranged from 2.1% to 11.1%. Limits of detection (LODs) were calculated as three times the standard deviation of 10 replicate blank measurements. Values below the limit of detection (LOD) were substituted with LOD/√2.

Determination of maternal thyroid hormone levels

FT4, TT3, FT3, TT4 and TSH were detected during the second trimesters by electro chemiluminescent microparticle immunoassays using the Architect system (Roche GmbH, Mannheim, Germany). The reference intervals for the second trimester were 0.39~5.22 mIU/L for TSH, 9.81~17.26 pmol/L for FT4, 3.60~5.41 pmol/L for FT3, 0.92~2.79 nmol/L for TT3 and 65~181 nmol/L for TT418.

Weighted Quantile Sum (WQS) regression

When evaluating the impact of metal mixtures on thyroid hormone levels, we employed the WQS regression method, which is specifically designed to identify the main influencing factors in the mixture by assigning data-driven weights to each exposure component. This method can detect exposure factors that are more strongly associated with the results, while also alleviating the problem of collinearity among related exposure factors. During the analysis, the metal concentrations were standardized, and a weight index was constructed to represent the cumulative effect of the mixture. The model, like the multivariate regression model, adjusted for key confounding factors and reported changes in thyroid hormones based on a four quartile change in the metal exposure levels in the mixture.

Bayesian Kernel Machine Regression (BKMR)

The Bayesian Kernel Machine Regression (BKMR) method was employed to simulate the complex, nonlinear and interactive effects of metal exposure on thyroid hormone levels. Unlike parametric models, BKMR does not impose strict functional assumptions, making it highly suitable for evaluating exposure response relationships that may be non-monotonic or involve interactions in environmental mixtures. In this analysis, BKMR was used to examine the overall mixed effect and the individual contribution of each metal element, while adjusting for the same confounding factors as in multivariate regression models.

Statistical analysis

Demographic characteristics and the distribution of serum metals and thyroid hormones (THs) in the study population were summarized using descriptive statistics. Subsequent analyses focused on the five metals with the highest median concentrations. To approximate normal distributions, maternal serum metal concentrations were log transformed, and thyroid hormone levels were natural log transformed. Spearman’s correlation was used to assess the associations between metal exposures and TH levels. Multivariable linear regression models were then applied to examine the relationships between maternal serum metal concentrations and thyroid hormone levels, with adjustments for potential confounders. Covariates were selected a priori based on previous literature and included maternal age, BMI, education level, monthly household income, exposure to passive smoking before and during pregnancy, and parity. To account for multiple comparisons, the false discovery rate (FDR) was controlled using the Benjamini Hochberg procedure, and FDR adjusted P values (q values) were reported where appropriate. All analyses were conducted using R (version 4.3.1). A two side P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Associations between maternal metals and thyroid hormones

Single-metal models were employed for assessing the associations between prenatal metal exposure and thyroid hormone levels. (Figure 2A). Initial analyses identified five metals (Zn, Cu, Se, Sr, Ti) showing significant correlations with thyroid parameters. Maternal characteristics, including age, height, weight, education level, and household income, are known to influence both metal exposure and thyroid hormone levels during pregnancy19–21,22. After adjusting for maternal age, body mass index, education, household income, passive smoking exposure, and parity, several metal-thyroid associations remained significant (Figure 2B-C). Specifically, Cu and Sr exhibited positive associations with TSH (β = 1.29, 95% CI: 0.28, 2.30 and β = 1.56, 95% CI: 0.93, 2.20, respectively) (Figure 2D). For TT4, only copper maintained a positive association (β = 0.3, 95% CI: 0.12, 0.48) (Figure 2E). Se and Sr showed negative associations with TT3 (β = -0.37, 95% CI: -0.54, -0.19 and β = -0.16, 95% CI: -0.31, -0.02, respectively) (Figure 2F). The effects of mixed exposure to perfluorinated compounds on thyroid hormones during pregnancy

WQS results demonstrate that the metal mixture as a whole was significantly associated with multiple thyroid hormones. Specifically, the WQS index showed a positive association with TSH (β = 0.49, 95% CI: 0.39~0.60, P < 0.001), TT4 (β = 0.04, 95% CI: 0.01~0.06, P = 0.002), and FT4 (β = 0.15, 95% CI: 0.11~0.18, P < 0.001). In contrast, a negative association was observed between the mixture and TSH (β = -0.18, 95% CI: -0.35~-0.01, P = 0.04), while no statistically significant associations were found for TT3 or FT3 in the mixture framework (Figure 3A). Importantly, BKMR analyses further validated these findings, yielding consistent overall mixture thyroid hormone associations and showing no strong evidence of pronounced nonlinear exposure response relationships or high order interactions among metals (Figure 3B, Supplementary Table 1 and Figure S1). Discussion

The present study investigated the correlations between individual metals exposure and maternal thyroid hormone profiles during pregnancy. After adjusting for major maternal and socioeconomic confounders, several metals including zinc, copper, selenium, strontium, and titanium showed significant correlations with thyroid parameters. Specifically, copper and strontium were positively associated with TSH, while copper also exhibited a positive relationship with TT4. Selenium and strontium were inversely associated with TT3 and FT3, whereas zinc and strontium showed positive correlations with FT4, and titanium was negatively associated. These findings indicate that both essential and nonessential metals may differentially influence thyroid homeostasis in pregnant women.

Our observations are partly consistent with previous studies suggesting that trace metals can modulate thyroid hormone synthesis and regulation through redox imbalance, enzymatic inhibition, or receptor interference23–25. Cu and Zn are essential cofactors in thyroid peroxidase and antioxidant systems, yet excessive exposure can induce oxidative stress, leading to compensatory elevations in TSH or altered thyroxine levels26. The positive associations of copper with TSH and TT4 observed in this study may therefore reflect adaptive responses to oxidative injury or subclinical thyroid dysfunction. Similarly, the consistent correlations of strontium with TSH and FT4 suggest that strontium may interfere with calcium dependent thyroid signaling or iodide transport, thereby influencing hormone biosynthesis27.

Se, a key component of deiodinase and antioxidant enzymes, is critical for converting T4 to active T3 and protecting thyroid tissue from oxidative damage.14. The inverse associations between selenium and TT3/FT3 concentrations may indicate impaired deiodinase activity or a disrupted feedback mechanism resulting from cumulative oxidative stress28. In contrast, the negative correlation between titanium and FT4 implies that nonessential metals may act as endocrine disruptors through mitochondrial dysfunction or interference with thyroid receptor pathways29. Collectively, these results suggest that prenatal metal exposure can perturb multiple levels of thyroid regulation, from hormone synthesis to receptor signaling.

Alterations in maternal thyroid hormones during pregnancy have important implications for both maternal and fetal outcomes. Even mild thyroid dysfunction such as elevated TSH or reduced FT4 has been linked to impaired placental function, fetal growth restriction, and neurodevelopmental deficits in offspring30–32. The endocrine sensitivity of pregnancy makes this period particularly vulnerable to environmental insults. Therefore, the associations identified in this study highlight the potential for prenatal metal exposure to induce both immediate hormonal imbalances and long-term developmental risks.

The underlying mechanisms likely involve a combination of oxidative stress, reduced selenoprotein activity, interference with iodine uptake, and modulation of thyroid peroxidase expression33,34. Essential and trace metals are closely involved in thyroid hormone synthesis, metabolism, and redox homeostasis. Zinc contributes to the structural integrity of Zn finger transcription factors and supports deiodinase activity; disturbances in zinc status can impair T4 toT3 conversion and enhance oxidative stress35. Cu participates in redox reactions within thyroid tissue, and excess copper may promote reactive oxygen species generation and interfere with thyroid peroxidase dependent hormone synthesis36. Se is a key component of deiodinases and glutathione peroxidases, making it central to thyroid hormone activation, inactivation, and protection against oxidative damage37. Interestingly, strontium showed positive associations with both TSH and FT4, which are typically inversely related. This unexpected pattern may reflect complex effects on multiple thyroid pathways or residual confounding from co-occurring metals. Sr, due to its chemical similarity to calcium, may influence thyroid cellular signaling and iodine uptake, although evidence is still emerging38. Collectively, imbalances of these metals can disrupt micronutrient interactions, oxidative stress pathways, and hormone metabolism, providing biologically plausible mechanisms for the associations observed in our study. Furthermore, metals may influence the hypothalamic pituitary thyroid (HPT) axis through epigenetic regulation or hormonal receptor interactions, amplifying systemic endocrine disruption39,40. Although we applied mixture modeling approaches, including WQS regression and BKMR, to account for correlated metal exposures, single-metal models were also used and remain limited in their ability to fully capture the complexity of real-world co-exposure patterns. Therefore, findings from single-metal analyses should be interpreted cautiously and in conjunction with results from mixture models. All associations reported were statistically significant, but the effect sizes were modest; nonetheless, even small changes in maternal thyroid hormone levels may be relevant at the population level, given the sensitivity of fetal neurodevelopment. We also acknowledge limitations, including single-time-point measurements, the second-trimester sampling, and lack of data on iodine status, thyroid medication use, thyroid autoantibodies, dietary intake, and supplement use, which may influence maternal metal levels and thyroid function. Future studies with longitudinal measurements and more comprehensive covariate data are needed to confirm and extend these findings.

Several methodological considerations should be acknowledged regarding the handling of missing smoking status data. In the present study, approximately 12–13% of participants had missing information on smoking status, and we treated these cases as a separate category in the regression models. While this approach allowed us to retain the full sample and account for missingness without case deletion, we recognize that a “missing” category lacks direct real-world interpretability and may introduce potential bias in coefficient estimation, as noted in prior methodological literature. The underlying assumption—that missingness is non-differential with respect to the exposure and outcome—may not be fully verifiable. Therefore, the results involving smoking status should be interpreted with caution. Future studies with similar data structures are encouraged to adopt more rigorous missing data handling techniques, such as multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE), which can provide less biased estimates under the missing-at-random assumption. Alternatively, sensitivity analyses using complete-case approaches or inverse probability weighting could further strengthen the validity of findings.

From a public health perspective, these findings underscore the need to monitor maternal exposure to environmental metals and implement preventive measures, including nutritional optimization and strengthened environmental regulation. Integrating mixture exposure assessments with molecular and epigenetic biomarkers will further clarify causal pathways and support effective strategies for maternal and fetal health protection. In summary, this study provides new evidence that prenatal exposure to specific metals can disrupt maternal thyroid hormone homeostasis through oxidative, enzymatic, and signaling mechanisms. Although derived from single-exposure models, the results emphasize the importance of advancing toward mixture based research frameworks to better evaluate cumulative risks and inform targeted public health interventions.

Conclusions

In conclusion, prenatal exposure to titanium, selenium, and strontium may disturb maternal thyroid hormone balance, suggesting possible interference with hormone synthesis or conversion. These results emphasize the need for monitoring both essential and non-essential metals to protect maternal thyroid function and promote healthy fetal development.
