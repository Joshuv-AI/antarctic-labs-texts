Sequence-Structure–Property Relationships in Short-Chain Polyesters: How Primary Structure Governs Macroscopic Performance
Authors: Vera Bocharova, Isaiah T. Dishner, Jared I. Bowman, Jeffrey C. Foster
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
While polymer properties are fundamentally linked to their nanostructure, the influence of monomer sequence remains less understood than stereochemical factors like tacticity. This study examines how sequence distribution affects the thermal behavior and morphology of homo- and copolyesters, specifically comparing polymers derived from constitutionally identical monomers but with varying degrees of sequence regularity depending on monomer structure or polymerization selectivity. Our findings show that increasing sequence defects progressively diminish thermal stability, crystallinity, melting temperatures, and morphological order. As new materials become more compositionally complex, this work underscores the importance of sequence control in the design of advanced polymers for emerging applications.

Introduction

Controlling polymer nanostructure is of both fundamental and technological importance. − Factors such as tacticity, regiochemistry, and sequence contribute to material thermomechanical, − conductive, barrier, , and spintronic properties, providing a means beyond molecular weight to finely tune the performance of polymeric materials for their desired use. The impact of microstructure is exemplified by polypropylene, a commodity thermoplastic, which can be prepared as a fully amorphous or highly semicrystalline material simply by controlling its side chain tacticity, enabling the use of a single material type for a broad range of applications from structural materials to electrical insulators to hot-melt adhesives.

While a variety of commercial polymeric materials rely on tacticity control to achieve desirable performance, less effort has been directed to the synthesis and study of polymers with precisely defined primary sequences. Here, we define sequence broadly to include both regiochemical regularity and comonomer distribution. Sequence-property effects are notable in biological macromolecules but have been underappreciated in synthetic polymers predominately due to synthetic limitations. Still, seminal works by the Wagener and Mecking groups have demonstrated that the impacts of simple polymer sequence manipulation on bulk material properties are profound. Wagener and co-workers exploited functional polyethylenes (PEs) prepared by acyclic diene metathesis (ADMET) polymerization to compare the effects of regular or random placement of alkyl, halide, carboxylic acid, sulfonic acid, or dipeptide groups, among many other motifs, on polymer properties. Materials with regular spacings between side chains generally exhibited sharper melting transitions, more uniform and well-ordered crystal structures, and higher degrees of crystallinity relative to random analogues. The Mecking group also used ADMET to prepare long-chain aliphatic polyesters, wherein the distance between the ester motifs was varied through copolymerization of ester-containing and unfunctionalized diene comonomers. Increasingly longer spacings provided consistently higher melting temperatures and melting enthalpies, unraveling valuable insight into the design of intrinsically degradable polyethylene mimetics. Building on these studies, Winey, Meyer, Segalman, and others have further reinforced the critical relationships between polymer sequence and properties and have motivated the development of increasingly sophisticated synthetic methodology to access precisely sequenced copolymers. ,−

This study investigates sequence-property relationships in short-chain aliphatic polyesters, focusing on how regiochemical defects impact bulk properties. In the laboratory context, these materials are often synthesized via ADMET polymerization or ring-opening metathesis polymerization (ROMP) due to monomer accessibility and mild reaction conditions. , Polymerization of asymmetric diene or cyclic alkene monomers containing internal ester linkages results in regiochemical defects, specifically head-to-head and tail-to-tail connections. ,− This inconsistency compromises chain structure beyond the frame of the repeating unit, effectively generating diol, diacid, and hydroxyacid subunits of differing sizes and configurations and ultimately eroding sequence regularity. Previous research has largely bypassed this limitation by using symmetrical diene monomers (containing two ester groups), − leaving the effects of nonregular microstructures from asymmetric monomers poorly understood.

To address this gap, we evaluated how regiochemical purity (i.e., sequence) affects the thermal properties of short chain aliphatic polyesters synthesized by ADMET polymerization. Polyesters prepared from constitutionally identical diene monomers exhibited starkly divergent thermal profiles and morphologies based on their susceptibility to regiochemical defects. We further demonstrated synthetic methodologies to produce highly sequence-defined analogous materials by leveraging the unique selectivity of acrylate-alkene cross metathesis, yielding high-precision polyesters with enhanced crystallinity and thermal stability. Building on these principles, we synthesized regular and random terpolymers to explore polymer sequence-property relationships in systems resembling next generation degradable materials. Sequence control enhanced crystallinity in the terpolymer system, opening new pathways to enhance the mechanical performance, solvent resistance, barrier properties, or other targets in copolymers with desirable compositions but poor sequence fidelity. Our data further underpin the critical role of polymer sequence in material performance and provide a robust synthetic framework for the creation and study of sequence-defined polymeric materials.

Results and Discussion

Homopolymer Synthesis and Structural Characterization

To study sequence-property effects in a simple polyester model system, two asymmetric diene monomers, each containing a single ester functional group, were synthesized using alkenyl carboxylic acid and alcohol building blocks (M1 and M2, Scheme ). These asymmetric monomers possessed identical chemical formulas, differing only in the relative position of their internal ester motifs. In contrast, monomer M3 was designed to present acrylate functionality. Acrylates exhibit unique behavior in olefin metathesis reactions, resisting homodimerization but undergoing selective cross-metathesis with electron rich alkenes to produce internal α,β-unsaturated esters characterized by exclusively E alkene configuration. Previous studies have used acrylate-containing monomers in ADMET (co)­polymerizations to synthesize alternating copolymers. − Here, we exploit alternating ADMET (ALTMET) polymerization to enforce strict heat-to-tail regiochemistry to enable robust sequence-property comparisons.

Synthesis of Unsaturated Polyesters by ADMET or ALTMET Polymerization Using Monomers M1-M3 and Catalysts HGII or GI

With monomers M1-M3 in hand, ADMET polymerizations of M1 were carried out using either HGII or GI in 1,2,4-trichlorobenzene (TCB) at 60 °C for 6 h to produce polymers P1–1 and P1–2, respectively (Scheme ). P2–1 and P2–2 were prepared from M2 in analogous fashion. Vacuum was applied during these polymerizations to remove the condensing ethylene gas. ALTMET polymerization of M3 was conducted using HGII in refluxing CH2Cl2 under Ar atmosphere for 6 h (Scheme ). We note that ethylene gas removal was not critical to achieve high molecular weights (MWs) for this latter polymerization due to the irreversibility of acrylate/alkene cross-metathesis. , Following precipitations in CH3OH, the resulting polymers were characterized by nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy (1H, 13C, and COSY) and size-exclusion chromatography (SEC). Their thermal behaviors were determined using differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) and thermogravimetric analysis (TGA). Spectroscopic characterization of polyester P1–1 revealed the presence of multiple regiochemical defects, exemplified by the broadness of its 1H NMR spectral resonances (Figure C) and the diversity of signals visible in the alkenyl region of its 13C NMR spectrum (Figure D). These defects arose from a combination of head-to-head (H/H) and tail-to-tail (T/T) polymerization and olefin migration promoted by the highly active catalyst HGII. , These complex spectra indicated that P1–1 comprised a variety of repeating units differing in methylene lengths and ester orientations spanned by differently configured alkene linkages. ADMET polymerization of the same monomer by G1 to produce P1–2 resulted in less defects due to suppressed olefin migration, as indicated by the increased resolution of its 1H NMR spectrum and the salient reduction in the complexity of its 13C NMR spectrum. Individual signals corresponding to H/H, H/T, and T/T regioisomers could be identified and assigned for this polymer. Qualitatively, the 13C NMR spectrum for P1–2 indicated a statistical distribution of H/H, H/T, and T/T units as would be expected for the approximately equivalent reactivity of the various olefin groups in M1 and M2. ADMET polymerizations of M2 with HGII or GI produced polymers P2–1 and P2–2, respectively, that were also characterized by regiochemical defects. As with the samples prepared from M1, the spectral complexities, and thus the complexities of their polymer sequences, were reduced when using GI compared with HGII. Similar to P1–2, P2–2 was estimated to contain a statistical distribution of the three possible regioisomers.

In stark contrast, the ALTMET polymerization of M3 produced highly regio- and stereoregular P3 (>97% of alternating diads, >99% E configured alkenyl groups via comparison of Ha and Hb integrals to all other alkenyl resonances). The clean resonances in both its 1H and 13C NMR spectra were assigned according to Figure A. A small population of H/H defects (<3 mol %) could also be detected in these spectra at ca. 150 and 120 ppm that likely formed through olefin homodimerization (see Figure S21). Importantly, no evidence of olefin migration was observed, and the ensemble polymer sequence could be readily quantified. We also note that the regiochemical complexities of P1–1 and P1–2 were not immediately apparent upon inspection of their 1H NMR spectra and that such defects are often overlooked in related ADMET polymer systems.

A comparison of the molecular weight (MW) distributions of the various copolymers as measured by SEC is shown in Figure A (distribution averages are provided in Table S1). P1–1 and P2–1 possessed the lowest number-average MW, M n, values of the sample series and P3 the highest. The reduced M n’s for P1–1 and P2–1 were likely attributed to the loss of terminal functionality and the coincident formation of nonvolatile byproducts by olefin migration. Calculated dispersity values, Đ, were ≤2.11 for all synthesized polymers but did not appear to trend with regiochemical purity.

Thermal and Morphological Characterization

Regiochemical purity profoundly influenced the bulk thermal properties of the synthesized polyesters. Thermal stability generally trended with increased regularity (Figure B and Table S1), with P3 clearly outperforming P1–1 despite their similar chemical constitutions. Polymers P1–1 and P1–2, which possessed the greatest dispersity of repeat unit sequences, were characterized as viscous liquids with glass transition temperatures, T g, well below room temperature (Figure C and Table S1). P1–2 showed similar thermal behavior to P1–1 despite its enhanced regularity. P2–1 was also amorphous with a low T g, while P2–2 was semicrystalline. For these comparable samples, contrary to classical expectations, P2–1despite its lower molecular weightshows a higher T g and remains amorphous, while P2–2 is semicrystalline, indicating that the enhanced regiochemical purity of P2–2 rather than molecular weight resulted in sufficient uniformity to enable chain packing and crystallization. P3, which possessed the highest uniformity in terms of both regio- and stereochemistry, was semicrystalline with a T m near room temperature.

Having established how sequence affected unsaturated polyesters, we shifted our focus to saturated systems. Toward this end, polymers P1–1 and P3, representing the two extremes of regiochemical purity, were hydrogenated with tosyl hydrazide under typical conditions to produce P1–1-H and P3–H, respectively (Figure A). Complete reduction of alkenyl functionality was confirmed by 1H NMR spectroscopic analysis of the precipitated polymers (Figures S24 and S27). Consistent with its saturated precursor, P1–1-H was characterized by a broad variety of regiochemical defects, most evident in its 13C NMR spectrum (Figure B). Resonances from 20–30 ppm indicated excessive methylene environments, suggesting notable deviation from the desired repeat unit. By comparison, the 13C NMR spectrum of P3 was cleanly resolved and each methylene resonance could be readily assigned.

Hydrogenation accentuated the differences in the polyesters’ thermal behavior. The onset of thermal decomposition, T d, of P1–1-H was minimally changed after hydrogenation (from 220 °C for P1–1 to 255 °C for P1–1-H), while the T d of P3 increased dramatically (from 241 °C for P3 to 360 °C for P3–H, Figure C and Table S1). This remarkable difference in thermal stability (>100 °C) was attributed to microstructural differences alone. Short methylene segments between ester motifs in P1–1-H, produced by olefin migration, were likely more susceptible to decomposition by thermal cyclization, facilitating the formation of small (5–7 membered) cyclic ester or anhydride thermolysis products. DSC analysis of these samples demonstrated that P3–H exhibited a higher melting temperature (72 °C) compared to P1–1-H (53 °C), consistent with its greater structural regularity and more stable crystalline lattice. At the same time, the enthalpy of melting (ΔH m) of P1–1-H (125 J/g) was slightly higher (∼14%) than that of P3–H (108 J/g) (Figure D), likely reflecting differences in local packing and lamellar organization rather than overall regularity. Accordingly, despite its higher regularity and T m, P3–H may not have developed fully mature lamellae under the processing conditions used, resulting in a slightly lower melting enthalpy.

X-ray scattering under vacuum showed layered structures with scattering vector q 1L1 = 0.041 Å–1 (period = 153 Å) for P1–1-H, and a broad peak with q 1L1 = 0.039 Å–1 (period = 161.1 Å) for P3–H (Figure E). The presence of a strong second order reflection peak for P1–1-H at q 2L1 = 2q 1L1 confirmed a well-defined lamellar structure with long-range periodic order. This assignment was further supported by atomic force microscopy (AFM), where phase imaging revealed a layered morphology (Figure F). The assignment of a precise morphology for P3–H was challenging from the X-ray data alone. Further details were obtained from AFM, where phase images for this sample indicated the coexistence of several lamellar morphologies. One morphology resembled the lamellar structure observed in P1–1-H, which was consistent with the position of q 1L1 peak with the lamellar period = 161.1 Å obtained from SAXS. Another morphology corresponded to thinner, more defined lamellar fibers (Figure G), exhibiting a smaller periodicity that was associated with a peak concealed within the broad low-q peak in the SAXS data with the tentative position outlined as q 1L2 on Figure E. It is important to note that, although the higher-order lamellar reflection was present, it was not as intense as in case of P1–1-H. Based on AFM results, we do not interpret the low intensity of this higher-order reflection to indicate a lack of long-range order; rather, it suggested a structural crosstalk within the system, leading to complex scattering behavior. The crystallographic state of both polyesters’ morphologies exhibited similar peak shapes and positions as demonstrated by WAXS (Figure S1). The intense peaks corresponded to the [110] and [200] planes, consistent with the orthorhombic solid-state structure typical of HDPE and other polyesters. , The dimensions of the crystallographic unit cell were slightly smaller for P1–1-H, as the crystallographic peaks were consistently shifted to higher-q compared with P3–H. Both P3–H and P1–1-H exhibited Bragg reflections at q = 0.57 Å–1 (d-spacing = 11 Å) (Figure E), indicative of a chain-folded structure. We hypothesized that this peak corresponded to the periodicity of CO motifs within polymer crystalline domains. The strong intensity of this peak in P3–H compared to its weak presence in P1–1-H suggested a higher degree of uniformity and periodicity in the crystalline phase of the more precisely sequenced material.

Copolymer Synthesis and Characterization

While ALTMET polymerization of M3 provided superior sequence control over traditional diene polymerization, regiochemically pure P3–H could alternatively be synthesized via ring-opening polymerization (ROP) or condensation polymerization. − However, these methods offer minimal microstructural control in more complex (i.e., ternary) systems. For example, poly­(butylene adipate terephthalate) (PBAT), as synthesized by condensation polymerization, is a random copolymer characterized by low crystallinity and limited morphological order due to the statistical arrangement of its constituent butanediol, adipic acid, and terephthalic acid units (Scheme , top). Although alternating PBAT is theoretically possible using a triad plus monad approach, for example via condensation of bis­(4-hydroxybutyl) terephthalate with adipic acid, sequence scrambling from transesterification typically occurs under the high-temperature conditions of melt polymerization. Indeed, installing polyfunctionality into novel materials through copolymerization often comes at the expense of periodicity and thus thermomechanical performance.

Synthesis of Semiaromatic Polyester Copolymers via Condensation Polymerization (Top) to Make PBAT or by ALTMET Polymerization (Bottom) Followed by Hydrogenation to Make P4–H

To investigate sequence-property effects in a more complex copolymer system, we designed a new set of monomers to approximate a terpolymerization of terephthalic acid, hexanediol, and 9-hydroxynonanoic acid and afford a semiaromatic polyester analogous to PBAT. Our approach encompassed the ALTMET copolymerization of diene M4 and diacrylate M5 to produce fully alternating P4 (Scheme , bottom), followed by hydrogenation with tosyl hydrazide to yield PBAT analogue P4–H. While the ester orientations and methylene spacer lengths of P4–H did not exactly match those of PBAT, concessions were made in monomer design to facilitate access to high MW polymer. We note that attempts to incorporate other dialkyl terephthalate monomers with shorter methylene spacers were met with limited success due to the inhibitory effects of Ru-carbonyl chelation. For comparison, we also synthesized random copolymer PBAT via traditional melt condensation as shown in Scheme (top).

1H NMR spectroscopic analyses of PBAT and P4–H showed nearly equimolar incorporation of their constituent comonomers (Table S1, Figures S30, and S33). As shown in Figure A, the 13C NMR spectrum of PBAT showed the expected carbonyl, aromatic, and aliphatic resonances. Upon closer inspection, methylene e (Figure A) exhibited three unique chemical environments that were assigned to the internal methylene carbons of butanediol segments in H/H, H/T, and T/T configurations (with a qualitatively slight excess of H/T isomers). This observation confirmed the statistical sequence structure of PBAT and implied a qualitatively equal composition of terephthalate-butanediol and adipate-butanediol units. In stark contrast, P4–H was characterized by high sequence fidelity (>99% alternating diads), confirming the desired periodic sequence of aromatic and alkyl units shown in Scheme . Importantly, sequence scrambling by transesterification was not observed to occur due to the relatively low temperature conditions of ALTMET polymerization and the absence of nucleophilic alcohol functionality. ALTMET polymerization emerges here as a potent toolkit, paving the way for the synthesis of next generation, sequence-specific macromolecular architectures.

Thermal analysis of PBAT by DSC showed a T g at −36 °C and a broad melting transition with a peak at ca. 139 °C (ΔH m = ∼36 J g–1) (Figure C). This broad melting endotherm reflected PBAT’s stochastic comonomer distribution, which caused formation of crystallites of varying size and stability due to heterogeneous crystallization. By contrast, DSC analysis of P4–H showed a T g at −31 °C and two sharp melting endotherms at 52 and 66 °C (Figure E). While P4–H’s combined ΔH m of 41 J g–1 suggested similar overall crystallinity for both P4–H and PBAT, the narrowed range of the melting endotherm observed for P4–H suggested that this material contained a greater uniformity of lamellae size with fewer overall crystalline defects.

The SAXS data of PBAT (Figure D) demonstrated lamellar organization, as evidenced by the presence of a primary scattering peak (q 1) at q = 0.06 Å–1. However, the absence of higher-order scattering peaks suggested that the lamellae were not well-defined nor extended over appreciable distances. WAXS of the PBAT sample showed crystalline reflections with intense peaks at q = 1.15, 1.21, 1.42, 1.60, and 1.74 Å–1. These peaks could not be fully described by the triclinic unit cell reported for poly­(ethylene terephthalate) (PET). While the peaks at 1.21, 1.60, and 1.74 Å–1 could tentatively be related to the main reflections of a triclinic-like cell, the additional peaks at 1.15 and 1.42 Å–1 could not be assigned. These peaks may arise from several possible features (e.g., lattice distortions, minor defects, local variations in chain packing, or the presence of a polymorphic crystalline phase) which made definitive assignment challenging. Regardless, the presence of these structural imperfections contributed to the broadened melting endotherm observed in DSC. By comparison, X-ray scattering of P4–H (Figure F) showed a combination of crystalline and amorphous peaks in the high-q region of the spectra resembling the triclinic crystalline structure found in PET. As before, AFM was used to further assign morphology (Figure S2). The AFM phase image showed lamellar structures organized into spherulites. Characteristic dimensions were derived via assignment of peaks in the middle and low-q areas of the X-ray spectrum. The first low-q peak at q s(pherulite) = 0.047 A–1 (13.4 nm) represented the average distance between lamellae in spherulites. The second low q-peak at q 1lamellar = 0.2 A–1 (3.1 nm), combined with pronounced second and third order reflections at q 2 = 0.4 A–1 and q 3 = 0.6 A–1, confirmed a well-defined lamellar structure. Compared to PBAT, the morphology and crystalline structure of P4–H was substantially more ordered, demonstrating that polymer primary structure directly impacts emergent structural features.

Conclusions

In summary, this work provides a direct correlation between macromolecular sequence purity and the macroscopic thermal and morphological behavior of unsaturated and saturated polyester systems. Our comparative analysis of constitutionally identical homopolymers and random or sequenced terpolymers revealed that monomer sequence distribution was as critical a determinant of material properties as the more widely studied stereochemical effects. Specifically, we have demonstrated that even minor sequence defects significantly suppressed crystallinity, depressed melting temperatures, and compromised morphological order.

These findings underpin the current paradigm shift from simple composition-based design toward a more rigorous sequence-controlled synthesis approach, investigating for the first time the impacts of structural precision on performance for polymer systems synthesized using constitutionally identical building blocks. As the demand for architecturally advanced materials grows, particularly in high-performance and precision applications, the ability to manipulate primary sequence will be indispensable. Future research should prioritize the development of high-fidelity synthetic methodologies that minimize sequence defects to fully realize the performance potential of next-generation polymeric materials.
