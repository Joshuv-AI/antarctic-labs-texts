Pulsation of quantum walk between two arbitrary graphs with weakly connected bridge
Taisuke Hosaka, Etsuo Segawa

Abstract




Abstract

We consider the Grover walk on a finite graph composed of two arbitrary simple graphs connected by one edge, referred to as a bridge.
The parameter
\epsilon>0
assigned at the bridge represents the strength of connectivity: if
\epsilon=0
, then the graph is completely separated. We show that for sufficiently small values of
\epsilon
, a phenomenon called pulsation occurs. The pulsation is characterized by the periodic transfer of the quantum walker between the two graphs. An asymptotic expression with respect to small
\epsilon
for the probability of finding the walker on either of the two graphs is derived. This expression reveals that the pulsation depends solely on the number of edges in each graph, regardless of their structure. In addition, we obtain that the quantum walker is transferred periodically between the two graphs, with a period of order
O(\epsilon^{-1/2})
. Furthermore, when the number of edges of two graphs is equal, the quantum walker is almost completely transferred.

Introduction

Quantum walks play key roles in the quantum computing like quantum algorithms
[
5
,
3
,
10
]
, quantum simulation
[
21
]
, and quantum cryptography
[
25
]
.
Such effectiveness of application of quantum walks is based on distinctive properties compared to classical random walks, such as localization
[
15
,
14
,
17
]
, ballistic spreading
[
18
]
and periodicity
[
22
,
19
,
12
]
.
One of the main topics in the study of quantum walks is quantum search algorithms
[
23
]
and perfect state transfer
[
8
]
. Spatial search algorithms based on quantum walks aim to locate the marked vertex with high probability, providing quadratic speed-up over classical search algorithms on some graphs
[
1
,
4
]
. Perfect state transfer refers to the phenomenon in which a quantum walker is transferred from one position to another with probability one
[
9
,
24
,
26
]
. Characterizing graphs that allow perfect state transfer remains an important research direction. Also, without being obsessed with perfectly transferring the quantum walker, some studies have shown that a sufficient amount of quantum walkers transfer to one position, such as the antipodal point or self-loop
[
20
]
.

Both of these topics share a common goal: concentrating the quantum walker at a specific positions. Motivated by this, we investigate a related phenomenon called pulsation, where the quantum walker repeatedly transfers between two regions of the graph
[
13
]
. This behavior may be seen as a generalization of both spatial search algorithms and perfect state transfer. In this work, we aim to understand when pulsation occurs and to clarify the key factors that cause it. We hope that this pulsation can be added as one of the distinctive properties of quantum walks.

Our study treats the Grover walk on the finite graph constructed by two graphs connected by an edge, referred to as
a bridge
.
We put a weight parametrized by
\epsilon>0
on a bridge, while all edges except a bridge are put weight
1
. The parameter
\epsilon
is considered as the strength of connectivity. If
\epsilon=1
, standard Grover walk on the graphs is reproduced. While if
\epsilon=0
, effectively the bridge disappears, and the graph is decomposed into the three disconnected parts: the two subgraphs and the bridge edge itself.
This means that the smaller the value of
\epsilon
, the weaker the connectivity between two graphs in our model. Then, intuitively, if
\epsilon
is close to 0, it would be expected that the quantum walker would have difficulty transferring between the two graphs and would remain trapped on one side of the subgraphs. However, we observe a counterintuitive phenomenon: for sufficiently small
\epsilon
, the quantum walker goes back and forth between subgraphs via
a weakly bridge
, that is, the pulsation occurs in these settings.

We obtain an asymptotic behavior of the finding probability of the quantum walker in either graph with respect to small
\epsilon
, presented in Theorem
4.1
(see Section 4). Theorem
4.1
implies that the behavior of the quantum walker determined solely by the number of edges in each graph and is independent of their structure. Theorem
4.2
gives the periodicity of the behavior of the quantum walk of order
O(\epsilon^{-1/2})
.
This work provides a new feature of quantum walks called pulsation and we hope that it contributes to a deeper understanding of quantum transport phenomena.

This paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we proposed the settings of the graphs and our quantum walk model. Section
3
shows some numerical simulations. In Section
4
, we give our main results. In Section
5
, we address the proof of the main theorem. Section
6
summarizes our results and discusses future work.

2
Setting the model

Let
H_{j}=(V_{j},A_{j})\,(j=1,2)
be a simple connected graph. Here,
V_{j}
and
A_{j}
are a set of vertices and symmetric arcs, respectively. The origin and terminal vertex of an arc
a
are indicated by
o(a)
and
t(a)
. The inverse arcs of an arc
a
are denoted by
\bar{a}
. Note that
t(a)=o(\bar{a})
and
o(a)=t(\bar{a})
hold.
For a fixed vertex
\xi_{j}\in V_{j}
, we define
\mathbb{e}_{*}=\{(\xi_{1},\xi_{2}),(\xi_{2},\xi_{1})\}
. It is called
the bridge
between
H_{1}
and
H_{2}
.
We set a graph
G=(V,A)
where
V=V_{1}\cup V_{2},A=A_{1}\cup A_{2}\cup\mathbb{e_{*}}
. Let
\delta V=\{\xi_{1},\xi_{2}\}\subset V
be the boundary vertex set.
The weight function
w:{A}\rightarrow[0,1]
is defined by

\displaystyle w(a)=\begin{cases}1&:a\notin\mathbb{e}_{*},\\
\epsilon&:a\in\mathbb{e}_{*}.\end{cases}

(2.1)

We set a function
p_{\epsilon}:{A}\rightarrow[0,1]
as
p_{\epsilon}(a)=w(a)/m(o(a))
,
where
m(x)=\sum_{a:\,o(a)=x}w(a)
.
We should remark that
p_{\epsilon}(a)
is regarded as the probability of a random walker from a vertex
o(a)
to
t(a)
depending on the weight.
Combining with a definition of
p_{\epsilon}
and Eq.(
2.1
), it follows that

\displaystyle p_{\epsilon}(a)=\begin{cases}1/\deg(o(a))&:o(a)\notin\delta V,\\
1/(\deg(o(a))+\epsilon)&:o(a)\in\delta V,a\notin\mathbb{e}_{*},\\
\epsilon/(\deg(o(a))+\epsilon)&:a\in\mathbb{e}_{*}.\end{cases}

The boundary matrix
d_{\epsilon}:\mathbb{C}^{A}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{V}
is defined by

\displaystyle(d_{\epsilon})_{x,a}=\begin{cases}\sqrt{p_{\epsilon}(\bar{a})}&:t(a)=x,\\
0&:otherwise.\end{cases}

for any
a\in A,x\in V
.
The shift matrix
S:\mathbb{C}^{A}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{A}
is defined by

\displaystyle(S)_{b,a}=\delta_{a,\bar{b}}.

Here,
\delta_{x,y}
is the Kronecker delta.
The time evolution matrix
U(\epsilon):\mathbb{C}^{A}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{A}
is

\displaystyle U(\epsilon)=S(2d_{\epsilon}^{*}d_{\epsilon}-I)

Remark 2.1
.

If
\epsilon=0
,
U(\epsilon)
acts on each of the three divided graphs
H_{1}
,
H_{2}
and
\{\mathbb{e}_{*}\}
. While if
\epsilon=1
,
U(\epsilon)
is regarded as Grover walks on
G
.

This remark implies that the smaller
\epsilon
is, the weaker the connection of
\mathbb{e}_{*}
.
In this paper, we focus on the case with sufficiently small
\epsilon
.
Let us set the initial state
\ket{\psi_{0}}
as the uniform superposition on
H_{1}
, that is,

\displaystyle\ket{\psi_{0}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{|A_{1}|}}\sum_{a\in A_{1}}\ket{a}.

(2.2)

Let
\mu_{t}(H_{j})
be the probability of the existence of a quantum walker on
H_{j}\,(j=0,1,2)
at time step
t
, denoted by

\displaystyle\mu_{t}(H_{j})=\sum_{a\in A_{j}}\left|\bra{a}U(\epsilon)^{t}\ket{\psi_{0}}\right|^{2}.

Here, we set
H_{0}=(\delta V,\{\mathbb{e}_{*}\})
.
We should remark that it follows that

\displaystyle\mu_{t}(H_{0})=1-\mu_{t}(H_{1})-\mu_{t}(H_{2}).

Thus, our main purpose is to estimate the asymptotic expression of
\mu_{t}(H_{1})
and
\mu_{t}(H_{2})
with respect to the parameter
\epsilon\ll 1
, which is the strength of the connectivity between
H_{1}
and
H_{2}
.

4
Main Theorem

This section shows the main theorems mentioned in the previous section.

Theorem 4.1
.

For sufficiently small
\epsilon
, it is following that

\displaystyle\mu_{t}(H_{1})=\left(\frac{|A_{1}|+|A_{2}|\cos{(t\theta(\epsilon))}}{|A_{1}|+|A_{2}|}\right)^{2}+O(\epsilon),

\displaystyle\mu_{t}(H_{2})=\left(\frac{\sqrt{|A_{1}||A_{2}|}}{|A_{1}|+|A_{2}|}(1-\cos(t\theta(\epsilon)))\right)^{2}+O(\epsilon).

where
\theta(\epsilon)
is the declination of the eigenvalue of
U(\epsilon)
that has the largest real part except
1
given by

\displaystyle\cos\theta(\epsilon)=1-\left(\frac{1}{|A_{1}|}+\frac{1}{|A_{2}|}\right)\epsilon+O(\epsilon^{2}).

Here,
Theorem
4.1
implies that the pulsation depends only on the number of arcs. Thus, it can be considered that this phenomenon is a universal property of quantum walks on finite graphs.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
\tau(\epsilon)
be the time step when
\mu_{t}(H_{2})
is maximized for the first time.
For sufficiently small
\epsilon
, it is following that

\displaystyle\tau(\epsilon)=\left\lfloor\frac{\pi}{\sqrt{2}}\sqrt{R_{\rm eff}(|A_{1}|,|A_{2}|)}\times\epsilon^{-1/2}\right\rfloor,

where
R_{\rm{eff}}(|A_{1}|,||A_{2}|)
is the effective resistance of an electric circuit consisting of two resistors of resistance values
|A_{1}|
and
|A_{2}|
connected in parallel, that is,
R_{\rm eff}^{-1}(|A_{1}|,|A_{2}|)=1/|A_{1}|+1/|A_{2}|
.

Theorem
4.2
implies that
\tau(\epsilon)
is order
\epsilon^{-1/2}
. In addition, it is interesting that
R_{\rm{eff}}(|A_{1}|,||A_{2}|)
, used in electric circuits, has been found. However, the relationship is not clearly clarified.
Focusing on Theorem
4.1
, we get the condition when
\mu_{t}(H_{2})
obtains the maximum value.

Corollary 4.1
.

When
|A_{1}|=|A_{2}|
and
t=\tau(\epsilon)
,
\mu_{t}(H_{2})
gets the maximum value
1+O(\epsilon)
.
Especially in the case
|A_{1}|=|A_{2}|
, it follows that

\displaystyle\mu_{t}(H_{1})

\displaystyle=\cos^{4}\left(\frac{t\theta(\epsilon)}{2}\right)+O(\epsilon),

\displaystyle\mu_{t}(H_{2})

\displaystyle=\sin^{4}\left(\frac{t\theta(\epsilon)}{2}\right)+O(\epsilon).

Corollary
4.1
shows that almost all quantum walker transfers from
H_{1}
to
H_{2}
when the number of arcs is the same on both graphs.
Figure
3
shows the case
K_{6}
and
C_{15}
. Here,
C_{n}
is the cycle graph with
n
vertices. The structure of the graphs and the number of vertices are quite different; however, the number of arcs is equal. Therefore, almost all quantum walker come back and forth between the two graphs.

and

with

, respectively.

5
Proof of Main Theorem

In this section, we show a proof of Theorem
4.1
and Theorem
4.2
. First, we consider the spectral analysis of random walk with respect to
p_{\epsilon}
. Let
W(\epsilon)
be a transition probability matrix of
G
depending on the weight
w
, that is,

\displaystyle(W(\epsilon))_{x,y}=\begin{cases}1/\deg(x)&:x,y\notin\delta V,x\sim y,\\
1/(\deg(x)+\epsilon)&:x\in\delta V,y\notin\delta V,x\sim y,\\
\epsilon/(\deg(x)+\epsilon)&:x,y\in\delta V,\\
0&:otherwise,\end{cases}

where
x\sim y
means that the vertices
x
and
y
are adjacency.
Because of the property of the transition matrix, we immediately see

\displaystyle\ket{\boldsymbol{1}_{V}}\in\mathrm{Ker}(W(\epsilon)-I)

(5.3)

where
\ket{\boldsymbol{1}_{V}}
is the all-one vector with respect to the set of vertex
V
.
By an expansion with respect to
\epsilon
,
W(\epsilon)
is denoted by

\displaystyle W(\epsilon)=W^{(0)}+\epsilon W^{(1)}+O(\epsilon^{2}),

where

\displaystyle(W^{(0)})_{x,y}=\begin{cases}1/\deg(x)&:x\sim y,(x,y)\notin\mathbb{e}_{*},\\
0&:otherwise,\end{cases}

\displaystyle(W^{(1)})_{x,y}=\begin{cases}-1/\deg(x)^{2}&:x\in\delta V,y\notin\delta V,x\sim y,\\
1/\deg(x)&:(x,y)\in\mathbb{e}_{*},\\
0&:otherwise.\end{cases}

For simplicity, we deal with
W(\epsilon)
expressed as a block matrix consisting of
H_{1}
and
H_{2}
components.
Then,
W^{(0)}
is given by

\displaystyle W^{(0)}=\begin{pmatrix}{\huge{W_{1}}}&{\huge{O}}\\
{\huge{O}}&{\huge{W_{2}}}\end{pmatrix}.

We should remark that
W_{j}
is a transition probability matrix of simple random walk on
H_{j}
.
From the property of
W_{j}
, we immediately get

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\ket{\boldsymbol{1}_{V_{1}}}\\
\boldsymbol{0}\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{0}\\
\ket{\boldsymbol{1}_{V_{2}}}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathrm{Ker}(W^{(0)}-I).

In other words,
W^{(0)}
has eigenvalue 1 with multiplicity 2.
Then, the eigenvalue
1
splits into two eigenvalues for small
\epsilon
.
Therefore, we should use a method called reduction process
[
16
]
to get the perturbed eigenvalue of
W(\epsilon)
.
We should remark that one eigenvalue of
W(\epsilon)
is
1
from Eq. (
5.3
) and another one eigenvalue of
W(\epsilon)
is expressed as

\displaystyle\lambda(\epsilon)=1+\epsilon\lambda^{(1)}+O(\epsilon^{2}).

In this case, we should consider the following matrix

\displaystyle\tilde{W}(\epsilon)=\frac{1}{\epsilon}(W(\epsilon)-I)\Pi,

where
\Pi
is the projection of
W^{(0)}
corresponding to eigenvalue 1.
Since
1
is semi-simple eigenvalue of
W^{(0)}
, the matrix
\tilde{W}(\epsilon)
can be expressed as

\displaystyle\tilde{W}(\epsilon)=\tilde{W}^{(1)}+O(\epsilon),

where
\tilde{W}^{(1)}=\Pi W^{(1)}\Pi
[(2.20) in Ch. ii, Sect. 2.2
[
16
]
].
From a property of
W_{j}
, we have

\displaystyle\Pi=\begin{bmatrix}\Pi_{1}&{\huge O}\\
{\huge O}&\Pi_{2}\end{bmatrix},

where

\displaystyle\Pi_{j}=\ket{\boldsymbol{1}_{V_{j}}}\bra{\pi_{j}},

and
\ket{\pi_{j}}
is the reversible measure of
W_{j}
written as

\displaystyle\pi_{j}(x)=\braket{\pi_{j}|x}=\frac{\deg(x)}{|A_{j}|}

for
x\in V_{j}
.
By direct calculation,
\tilde{W}^{(1)}
is denoted by

\displaystyle\tilde{W}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}-\displaystyle\frac{1}{|A_{1}|}\Pi_{1}&\displaystyle\frac{1}{|A_{1}|}\ket{\boldsymbol{1}_{V_{1}}}\bra{\pi_{2}}\\
\displaystyle\frac{1}{|A_{2}|}\ket{\boldsymbol{1}_{V_{2}}}\bra{\pi_{1}}&-\displaystyle\frac{1}{|A_{2}|}\Pi_{2}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}&\displaystyle\frac{1}{|A_{1}|}\ket{\boldsymbol{1}_{V_{1}}}\\
&-\displaystyle\frac{1}{|A_{2}|}\ket{\boldsymbol{1}_{V_{2}}}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}-\bra{\pi_{1}}&\bra{\pi_{2}}\end{bmatrix}.

From
[
16
]
and above formula. the coefficient
\lambda^{(1)}
can be obtained by

\displaystyle\lambda^{(1)}\in\mathrm{Spec}\left(\tilde{W}^{(1)}|_{\mathrm{Ran(\Pi)}}\right)\setminus\{0\}=\left\{-\frac{1}{|A_{1}|}-\frac{1}{|A_{2}|}\right\}.

The eigenvector of
\lambda^{(1)}
is calculated by

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}&-|A_{2}|\,\ket{\boldsymbol{1}_{V_{1}}}\\
&|A_{1}|\,\ket{\boldsymbol{1}_{V_{2}}}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathrm{Ker}(\tilde{W}^{(1)}-\lambda^{(1)}I).

Here, we should remark that if the eigenprojections of
W(\epsilon)
and
\tilde{W}^{(1)}
is defined
P(\epsilon)
and
P
, then it follows that

\displaystyle P(\epsilon)=P+O(\epsilon).

Therefore, we get following lemma.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
W(\epsilon)
be a transition matrix depending on the weight
w
. Then, it follows that

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\ket{\boldsymbol{1}_{V_{1}}}\\
\ket{\boldsymbol{1}_{V_{2}}}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathrm{Ker}(W(\epsilon)-I),\quad\begin{bmatrix}&-|A_{2}|\,\ket{\boldsymbol{1}_{V_{1}}}\\
&|A_{1}|\,\ket{\boldsymbol{1}_{V_{2}}}\end{bmatrix}+O(\epsilon)\in\mathrm{Ker}(W(\epsilon)-\cos(\theta(\epsilon))I),

where

\displaystyle\cos(\theta(\epsilon))=1-\left(\frac{1}{|A_{1}|}+\frac{1}{|A_{2}|}\right)\epsilon+O(\epsilon^{2}).

Next, we show the proposition connecting the eigenvalue and the eigenvector between
W(\epsilon)
and
U(\epsilon)
.
Let
\pi_{G}\in\mathbb{C}^{V}
be the reversible measure of
W^{(0)}
, that is,

\displaystyle\pi_{G}(x)=\frac{\deg(x)}{|A_{1}|+|A_{2}|}.

We define the diagonal matrix
D^{1/2}:\mathbb{C}^{V}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{V}
by

\displaystyle(D^{1/2})_{x,y}=\begin{cases}\sqrt{\pi_{G}(x)}&:x=y,\\
0&:otherwise.\end{cases}

Then, we give the following proposition.

Proposition 5.1

(
[
11
]
)
.

Let us set
f\in\mathrm{Ker}(\cos\theta(\epsilon)-W(\epsilon))
. Then, the corresponding eigenvector
\psi_{\pm\theta}\in\mathrm{Ker}(e^{\pm i\theta(\epsilon)}-U(\epsilon))
is given by

\displaystyle\psi_{\pm\theta}(a)=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{2(|A_{1}|+|A_{2}|)}|\sin\theta|}(f(t(a))-e^{\pm i\theta}f(o(a)))+O(\epsilon)&:\cos\theta\neq\pm 1,\\
f(t(a))&:\cos\theta=\pm 1,\end{cases}

with
||D^{1/2}f||=1
.

Proof.

Let us set
f\in\mathrm{Ker}(\cos\theta(\epsilon)-W(\epsilon)),\;g\in\mathrm{Ker}(\cos\theta(\epsilon)-W_{sym})
with
||g||=1
. Here,
W_{sym}=d_{\epsilon}Sd_{\epsilon}^{*}
.
We should remark that
W_{sym}=D^{1/2}WD^{-1/2}
. Then, we immediately see

, that is,

\displaystyle g(x)=\sqrt{\pi_{G}(x)}f(x)

(5.4)

for
x\in V
.
By spectral mapping theorem of quantum walk
[
11
]
, we see

\displaystyle\psi_{\pm\theta}(a)=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}|\sin\theta|}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{\deg(t(a))}}g(t(a))-\frac{e^{\pm i\theta}}{\sqrt{\deg(o(a))}}g(o(a))\right)+O(\epsilon)&:\cos\theta\neq\pm 1,\\
\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{\deg(t(a))}}f(t(a))&:\cos\theta=\pm 1.\end{cases}

Combining Eq. (
5.4
) and above equation, for
\cos\theta\neq 1
case, it is obtained by

\displaystyle\psi_{\pm\theta}(a)

\displaystyle=\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}|\sin\theta|}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{\deg(t(a))}}\sqrt{\pi_{G}(t(a))}f(t(a))-\frac{e^{\pm i\theta}}{\sqrt{\deg(o(a))}}\sqrt{\pi_{G}(o(a))}f(o(a))\right)+O(\epsilon)

\displaystyle=\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{2(|A_{1}|+|A_{2}|)}|\sin\theta|}\left(f(t(a))-e^{\pm i\theta}f(o(a))\right)+O(\epsilon)

with
||g||=||D^{1/2}f||=1
. Similarly, we see the
\cos\theta=1
case.
Hence, we get the desired conclusion.
∎

Combining with Lemma
5.1
and Proposition
5.1
, we immediately get the following lemma.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
U(\epsilon)
be the time evolution matrix. Then it follows that

\displaystyle\{1,e^{\pm i\theta(\epsilon)}\}\subset\mathrm{Spec}(U(\epsilon)).

Corresponding eigenvectors
\ket{\psi_{1}}\in\mathrm{Ker}(I-U(\epsilon))
and
\ket{\psi_{\pm\theta}}\in\mathrm{Ker}(e^{\pm i\theta(\epsilon)}-U(\epsilon))
are given by

\displaystyle\psi_{1}(a)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{|A_{1}|+|A_{2}|}},

\displaystyle\psi_{\pm\theta}(a)=\frac{1-e^{\pm i\theta(\epsilon)}}{\sqrt{2|A_{1}||A_{2}|(|A_{1}|+|A_{2}|)}\,|\sin(\theta(\epsilon))|}\times\begin{cases}-|A_{2}|&:t(a)\in V_{1},\\
|A_{1}|&:t(a)\in V_{2},\end{cases}

respectively.

Since
U(\epsilon)
is the unitary, we have

\displaystyle\mu_{t}(H_{1})

\displaystyle=\sum_{a\in A_{1}}|\bra{a}U(\epsilon)^{t}\ket{\psi_{0}}|^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{a\in A_{1}}\left|\sum_{\mu\in\mathrm{Spec}(U(\epsilon))}\mu^{t}\braket{a|\psi_{\mu}}\braket{\psi_{\mu}|\psi_{0}}\right|^{2}.

We focus on the overlap between the eigenvectors and the initial state. Then we get the following lemma.

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
\ket{\psi_{1}}
and
\ket{\psi_{\pm\theta}}
be eigenvectors of
U(\epsilon)
corresponding to eigenvalues 1 and
e^{\pm i\theta(\epsilon)}
, respectively.
Let
\ket{\psi_{0}}
be an initial state given by Eq. (
2.2
). Then we have

\displaystyle|\braket{\psi_{1}|\psi_{0}}|

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{|A_{1}|}{|A_{1}|+|A_{2}|}},

\displaystyle|\braket{\psi_{\pm\theta}|\psi_{0}}|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\sqrt{\frac{|A_{2}|}{|A_{1}|+|A_{2}|}}+O(\epsilon).

Proof.

By directly, we get the first formula. Combining Eq. (
2.2
) with Lemma
5.2
, we see

\displaystyle|\braket{\psi_{\pm\theta}|\psi_{0}}|=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\sqrt{\frac{|A_{2}|}{|A_{1}|+|A_{2}|}}\times\left|\frac{1-e^{i\theta(\epsilon)}}{\sin\theta(\epsilon)}\right|.

Thus, we have

\displaystyle\left|\frac{1-e^{\pm i\theta(\epsilon)}}{\sin\theta(\epsilon)}\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\frac{e^{\pm i\theta(\epsilon)/2}(e^{\pm i\theta(\epsilon)/2}-e^{\mp i\theta(\epsilon)/2})}{\sin\theta(\epsilon)}\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\frac{2i\sin(\theta(\epsilon)/2)}{\sin\theta(\epsilon)}\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\frac{1}{\cos(\theta(\epsilon)/2)}\right|

\displaystyle=1+O(\epsilon).

Therefore, we get the desired conclusion.
∎

From Lemma
5.3
,
\mu_{t}(H_{1})
is asymptotically described as

\displaystyle\mu_{t}(H_{1})

\displaystyle=\sum_{a\in A_{1}}\left|\braket{a|\psi_{1}}\braket{\psi_{1}|\psi_{0}}+e^{it\theta(\epsilon)}\braket{a|\psi_{\theta}}\braket{\psi_{\theta}|\psi_{0}}+e^{-it\theta(\epsilon)}\braket{a|\psi_{-\theta}}\braket{\psi_{-\theta}|\psi_{0}}\right|^{2}+O(\epsilon).

For any
a\in A_{1}

6
Summary and discussion

This paper investigates a phenomenon called pulsation, inspired by quantum search algorithms and perfect state transfer. We considered the Grover walk on a graph formed by connecting two arbitrary graphs via a single edge, referred to as a bridge. A parameter
\epsilon>0
, representing the strength of connectivity, is assigned to the bridge.
We showed that for sufficiently small
\epsilon
, pulsation occurred in this setting. That is, the quantum walker periodically transfers between the two graphs. We derived an asymptotic expression for the probability of finding the walker on either graph. These results revealed that the behavior of pulsation is determined not by the structure of the graphs, but solely by the number of arcs in each.
Furthermore, we demonstrated that the quantum walker goes back and forth between the two graphs, with a period of order
O(\epsilon^{-1/2})
.
In this study, we focused on the case of a composed graph consisting of two graphs connected by one edge.

We guess that since
a weakly bridge
might be regarded as the potential barrier for small
\epsilon
, this phenomenon may be interpreted as a model that handles the phenomenon known as a kind of the tunneling effect
[
6
,
7
]
in quantum mechanics using quantum walks.

This model may be considered as an analogy of quantum batteries
[
2
]
. One of the objectives of those studies is to extract the energy from the batteries efficiently by using unitary operators. Whereas, this paper showed that the energy (quantum walker) was extracted from
H_{1}
to
H_{2}
by
U(\epsilon)
. We conceive that the results of this paper may be one of the directions of quantum batteries and quantum walks on finite graphs.

A natural direction for future work is to investigate whether similar behavior occurs on graphs composed of more than three graphs or connected by more than two edges. In addition, the effective resistance of electric circuits appeared in Theorem
4.2
. Clarifying the connection between the electric circuit and this paper is another future work.
