Macroscopic quantum teleportation with ensembles of qubits
Manish Chaudhary, Zhiyuan Lin, Shuang Li, Mohan Zhang, Yuping Mao, Valentin Ivannikov, Tim Byrnes

Abstract
We develop methods for performing quantum teleportation of the total spin variables of an unknown state, using quantum nondemolition measurements, spin projection measurements, and classical communication. While theoretically teleportation of high-dimensional states can be attained with the assumption of generalized Bell measurements, this is typically experimentally non-trivial to implement. We introduce two protocols and show that, on average, the teleportation succeeds in teleporting the spin variables of a spin coherent state with average zero angular error in the ideal case, beating classical strategies based on quantum state estimation. In a single run of the teleportation, there is an angular error at the level of ∼ 0.1 \sim 0.1 radians for large ensembles. A potential physical implementation for the scheme is with atomic ensembles and quantum nondemolition measurements performed with light. We analyze the decoherence of the protocols and find that the protocol is robust even in the limit of large ensemble sizes.

The simplest version of the teleportation protocol involves teleporting a two-level system, i.e. a qubit. Extending this to higher dimensional systems has always been a topic of fascination among physicists, but has thus far remained experimentally challenging. The largest discrete system that has been teleported, at the time of writing is a qutrit (3-level) photonic system

Conditional classical unitary operations then complete the teleportation process. The problem with this is that generating the high-dimensional entangled state and/or realizing the entangled basis measurement are not easily performed. Furthermore, there is the risk that high-dimensional systems suffer decoherence at an accelerated rate in comparison to qubits, such that any quantum effects are removed

Atomic ensembles constitute a natural system that is suitable for realizing quantum operations on high-dimensional states, due to the precise controllability and low decoherence rates

In this paper, we introduce two protocols to teleport the spin variables of an unknown state on a qubit ensemble.
In constructing our protocol, we restrict ourselves to only experimentally viable operations, namely QND measurements
Aristizabal-Zuluaga et al. 2021
;
Chaudhary et al. 2022

1

The two variants of the protocols (called I and II), give similar results, but differ in their details and the correction protocol. In Protocol I, one QND measurement is performed, followed by local spin measurements on the two ensembles. In Protocol II, two QND measurements are made, in the
z
, then in the
x

We find that on average, the spin readout gives remarkably
perfect
transfer of Alice’s original spin variables for a spin coherent state. We limit the teleportation to spin variables, as opposed to the full quantum state, due to the restricted operations that are available in the context of qubit ensembles. We however note that the task of teleporting spin averages is important in the context of spinor quantum computing

;
Byrnes and Ilo-Okeke 2021

We make a brief contrast of our teleportation protocols to past works.
In the context of atomic ensembles, there have been two approaches to perform teleportation, one being continuous variable teleportation
Krauter et al. 2013
;
Sherson et al. 2006
and the other using collective excitations
Bao et al. 2012
. For continuous variables teleportation, the approach is to use the Holstein-Primakoff (HP) approximation to effectively work within the phase space of position and momentum
Braunstein and Van Loock 2005
. This works in a small region of Hilbert space, examining small deviations from a polarized spin direction. For example, working with an ensemble polarized in the
S^{x}
direction, under the HP approximation one is constrained to Bloch sphere angles in the region of
\theta=\pi/2\pm O(1/\sqrt{N})
and
\phi=0\pm O(1/\sqrt{N})
. In contrast, our teleportation is designed to work for arbitrary
\theta,\phi
. Collective excitations also work in a similar way, by polarizing the spins in an ensemble, then looking at discrete excitations of this. Our protocol differs from these scheme in that the aim is to teleport states with an arbitrary polarization on the Bloch sphere. Previously, we proposed two “beyond-HP” schemes, for teleportation of the equatorial
Pyrkov and Byrnes 2014a
and full Bloch sphere coordinates
Pyrkov and Byrnes 2014b
. These were both however based on
S^{z}S^{z}
interactions between ensembles, which are still yet to be experimentally realized for remote ensembles in a controllable way. Therefore, to our knowledge, there is still no practically realizable scheme for beyond-HP teleportation.

II
Physical system and required operations

Here we introduce the necessary formalism to capture the physical system in question and the required operations to carry out the quantum teleportation protocol.

II.1
Qubit ensembles

The physical system we consider are ensembles consisting of
N
non-interacting qubits. Quantum information is stored on the collective spin operators in terms of the sum of individual spin operators

\displaystyle S^{\eta}_{l}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sigma^{x}_{l,n}

(1)

where
\eta\in\{x,y,z\}
and
\sigma^{\eta}_{l,n}
is a Pauli operator in the
\eta
direction for the
n^{\text{th}}
atom in the
l^{\text{th}}
ensemble. We consider the number of atoms
N
in each ensemble to be equal for simplicity. To keep the discussion as general as possible, we do not further specify the physical system, beyond that the decoherence to be at a sufficiently low level such that the quantum operations in the protocol can be performed coherently. Qubit ensembles can be physically implemented by neutral atomic gas ensembles, for example. More details on the experimental implementation is deferred to Sec.
VII
. The commutation relation for the spin operators are

\displaystyle[S^{i}_{l},S^{j}_{m}]=2i\epsilon_{ijk}S^{k}\delta_{lm},

(2)

where
\epsilon_{ijk}
is the Levi-Civita symbol.

The spin operators (
1
) are symmetric under particle interchange. This is a important limitation of working with such ensembles, that the underlying particles are not addressable, but each ensemble is individually controllable. It then follows that if the initial state is symmetric under particle interchange and only symmetric operators are used to manipulate the state, the state remains in the symmetric subspace
Byrnes and Ilo-Okeke 2021
. We may then equivalently work using Schwinger boson operators

\displaystyle S^{x}_{l}

\displaystyle=a_{l}^{\dagger}b_{l}+b_{l}^{\dagger}a_{l}

\displaystyle S^{y}_{l}

\displaystyle=-ia_{l}^{\dagger}b_{l}+ib_{l}^{\dagger}a_{l}

\displaystyle S^{z}_{l}

\displaystyle=a_{l}^{\dagger}a_{l}-b_{l}^{\dagger}b_{l}.

(3)

where the bosonic annihilation operator for the qubit states are
a_{l},b_{l}
respectively. Working in the completely symmetric subspace reduces the Hilbert space dimension of each ensemble from
2^{N}
to
N+1
. For convenience we shall use the bosonic formulation (
II.1
) henceforth.

A spin coherent state for
N
uncorrelated qubits in an ensemble is defined as

\displaystyle|\theta,\phi\rangle\rangle_{l}=\frac{(\cos\frac{\theta}{2}e^{-i\phi/2}a_{l}^{\dagger}+\sin\frac{\theta}{2}e^{i\phi/2}b_{l}^{\dagger})^{N}}{\sqrt{N!}}|\text{vac}\rangle

(4)

where
\theta,\phi
are the angles on the Bloch sphere, and
|\text{vac}\rangle
is the vacuum state containing no atoms. The subscript
l
on states refers to the ensemble number throughout this paper.

The number (or Dicke) states are defined as

\displaystyle|k\rangle_{l}=\frac{(a_{l}^{\dagger})^{k}(b_{l}^{\dagger})^{N-k}}{\sqrt{k!(N-k)!}}|\text{vac}\rangle.

(5)

The number states are eigenstates of the
S^{z}
operator defined as

\displaystyle S^{z}_{l}|k\rangle_{l}=(2k-N)|k\rangle_{l}.

(6)

II.2
Ensemble operations

Here we list the operations that we assume are available on the qubit ensembles.

We assume that each of the ensembles can be manipulated individually with total spin rotations

\displaystyle U_{l}(\vec{n},\varphi)=e^{-i\vec{n}\cdot\vec{S}_{l}\varphi/2}

(7)

where
\vec{n}
is a three dimensional unit vector that specifies the axis of the rotation,
\vec{S}_{l}=(S^{x}_{l},S^{y}_{l},S^{z}_{l})
, and
\varphi
is the angle of rotation.

In addition, measurements in the number basis are assumed to be possible, corresponding to the measurement operator

\displaystyle\Pi_{k}^{z,l}=|k\rangle_{l}\langle k|_{l},

(8)

where the outcome is
k
and the
z
denotes a measurement in the
S^{z}
eigenbasis. The superscript
l
indicates which ensemble the projector acts on. Measurements in the
x
basis correspond to

\displaystyle\Pi_{k}^{x,l}=|k\rangle_{l}^{(x)}\langle k|_{l}^{(x)}.

(9)

where the number states in the
x
basis are defined as

\displaystyle|k\rangle^{(x)}_{l}=e^{-iS^{y}_{l}\pi/4}|k\rangle_{l}.

(10)

These are projection operators and satisfy

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{N}\Pi_{k}^{z,l}=\sum_{k=0}^{N}\Pi_{k}^{x,l}=I_{1}

(11)

where
I_{1}
is the identity matrix for a single ensemble with dimension
N+1
.

To entangle the ensembles, we assume that QND measurements can be performed on two ensembles. Under particular conditions (see Appendix
A
), this can be written as a projection operator

\displaystyle P_{\Delta}^{z}=\sum_{k=\max(0,-\Delta)}^{\min(N,N-\Delta)}|k,k+\Delta\rangle\langle k,k+\Delta|,

(12)

where the
z
refers to the basis that the number states are in.

We may also perform QND measurements in the
x
basis according to

\displaystyle P_{\Delta}^{x}=e^{-i(S^{x}_{1}+S^{x}_{2})\pi/4}P_{\Delta}^{z}e^{i(S^{x}_{1}+S^{x}_{2})\pi/4},

(13)

where the exponential factors are single ensemble rotations (
7
). Through this paper, the QND measurements always act on ensembles 1 and 2, hence we omit the ensemble labels on the left hand side of (
13
). The projection operators satisfy

\displaystyle\sum_{\Delta=-N}^{N}P_{\Delta}^{z}=\sum_{\Delta=-N}^{N}P_{\Delta}^{x}=I_{2},

(14)

where
I_{2}
is the identity matrix of two ensembles with dimension
(N+1)^{2}
.

II.3
Maximally entangled state preparation

One of the key resources that is required in the quantum teleportation is shared entanglement between Alice and Bob. For this we prepare a maximally entangled state between two ensembles shared by Alice and Bob (see Fig.
1
). We define the maximally entangled state as
Kitzinger et al. 2020
;
Byrnes 2024

\displaystyle|\Phi\rangle\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N!\sqrt{N+1}}(a_{1}^{\dagger}a_{2}^{\dagger}+b_{1}^{\dagger}b_{2}^{\dagger})^{N}|\text{vac}\rangle

(15)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N+1}}\sum_{k=0}^{N}|k\rangle\otimes|k\rangle.

(16)

In Ref.
Chaudhary et al. 2023
, a deterministic method of preparing this state was introduced. The method uses only the QND measurements (
12
), (
13
), and conditional unitary rotations (
7
). We summarize how this is performed in Appendix
B
.

III
Teleportation protocols

In this section we introduce two teleportation protocols (called Protocol I and II) for transmitting the total spin information of an unknown ensemble. Here, we simply give a concrete description of the protocols. The theoretical analysis, and the rationale of the steps will be performed in Sec.
IV
.

III.1
Protocol I

Protocol I involves performing one QND measurement in the
z
-basis, followed by local number measurements of Alice’s ensembles in the
x
-basis (see Fig.
1
). The specific steps are as follows.

1.

Alice prepares her initial state on ensemble 1 and shares a maximally entangled state with Bob:

\displaystyle|\Psi_{0}\rangle

\displaystyle=|\psi_{0}\rangle_{1}\otimes|\Phi\rangle\rangle_{23}.

(17)

2.

A QND measurement in the
z
-basis is performed on ensembles 1 and 2, which yields the state
P_{\Delta}^{z}|\Psi_{0}\rangle
.

3.

A local measurement in the number state basis is made on ensembles 1 and 2. This produces the state
\Pi_{k_{1}}^{x,1}\Pi_{k_{2}}^{x,2}P_{\Delta}^{z}|\Psi_{0}\rangle
.

4.

Alice transmits the outcomes of the measurement
(\Delta,k_{1},k_{2})
to Bob.

5.

Bob measures the expectation value of
\vec{S}_{3}
and performs a correction according to

\displaystyle S^{x}_{\text{tel},I}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{H(k_{1}-\frac{N}{2})+H(k_{2}-\frac{N}{2})}\langle S^{x}_{3}\rangle

\displaystyle S^{y}_{\text{tel},I}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{H(k_{1}-\frac{N}{2})+H(k_{2}-\frac{N}{2})}\langle S^{y}_{3}\rangle

\displaystyle S^{z}_{\text{tel},I}

\displaystyle=\langle S^{z}_{3}\rangle-2\Delta,

(18)

where
H(x)
is the Heaviside step function with
H(x)=1
for
x\geq 0
and
H(x)=0
otherwise. The Bloch sphere angles can be found by

\displaystyle\theta_{\text{tel},I}=

\displaystyle\cos^{-1}\left(\frac{S^{z}_{\text{tel},I}}{N}\right)

(19)

\displaystyle\phi_{\text{tel},I}=

\displaystyle\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{S^{y}_{\text{tel},I}}{S^{x}_{\text{tel},I}}\right)

\displaystyle+\pi(H(k_{1}-\frac{N}{2})+H(k_{2}-\frac{N}{2})).

(20)

III.2
Protocol II

Protocol II involves performing two QND measurements in the
z
-basis and
x
-basis in that order (see Fig.
1
). The specific steps are as follows.

1.

Alice prepares her initial state on ensemble 1 and shares a maximally entangled state with Bob:

\displaystyle|\Psi_{0}\rangle

\displaystyle=|\psi_{0}\rangle_{1}\otimes|\Phi\rangle\rangle_{23}.

(21)

2.

A QND measurement in the
z
-basis is performed on ensembles 1 and 2, which yields the state
P_{\Delta_{1}}^{z}|\Psi_{0}\rangle
.

3.

A QND measurement in the
x
-basis is performed on ensembles 1 and 2, which yields the state
P_{\Delta_{2}}^{x}P_{\Delta_{1}}^{z}|\Psi_{0}\rangle
.

4.

Alice transmits the outcomes of the measurement
(\Delta_{1},\Delta_{2})
to Bob.

5.

Bob measures the expectation value of
\vec{S}_{3}
and performs a correction according to

\displaystyle S^{x}_{\text{tel},II}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{H(|\Delta_{2}|-\Delta_{+})}\langle S^{x}_{3}\rangle

\displaystyle S^{y}_{\text{tel},II}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{H(|\Delta_{2}|-\Delta_{+})}\langle S^{y}_{3}\rangle

\displaystyle S^{z}_{\text{tel},II}

\displaystyle=\langle S^{z}_{3}\rangle-2\Delta,

(22)

where

\displaystyle\Delta_{+}=\frac{1}{2}\left(-N-1+\sqrt{3N^{2}+6N+1}\right).

(23)

The Bloch sphere angles can be found by

\displaystyle\theta_{\text{tel},II}=

\displaystyle\cos^{-1}\left(\frac{S^{z}_{\text{tel},II}}{N}\right)

(24)

\displaystyle\phi_{\text{tel},II}=

\displaystyle\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{S^{y}_{\text{tel},II}}{S^{x}_{\text{tel},II}}\right)+\pi H\Big(|\Delta_{2}|-\Delta_{+}\Big).

(25)

We point out that the correction operations that are performed in (
18
) and (
22
) both only require the measurement outcomes
k_{1},k_{2},\Delta
for Protocol I and
\Delta_{1},\Delta_{2}
for Protocol II. As such, the protocols can be executed without Alice or Bob’s knowledge of the state
|\Psi_{0}\rangle
.

IV.1
Protocol I

We now analyze Protocol I by giving the expressions for the quantum state.

The unnormalized state after step 3 of the protocol evaluates to

\displaystyle|\Psi_{I}\rangle=\Pi_{k_{1}}^{x,1}\Pi_{k_{2}}^{x,2}P_{\Delta}^{z}|\Psi_{0}\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N+1}}|k_{1}\rangle^{(x)}_{1}|k_{2}\rangle^{(x)}_{2}

\displaystyle\otimes\sum_{k=\max(0,-\Delta)}^{\min(N,N-\Delta)}\psi_{k}^{(0)}R_{k_{1},k}R_{k_{2},k+\Delta}|k+\Delta\rangle_{3},

(26)

where
\psi_{k}^{(0)}=\langle k|\psi_{0}\rangle
and we defined the matrix elements

\displaystyle R_{k^{\prime},k}=\langle k^{\prime}|^{(x)}|k\rangle.

(27)

The matrix elements can be evaluated using the expression in Appendix
C
.
After the
x
-basis projections, we can see that the ensemble 3 is disentangled from the other two ensembles.

The probability of obtaining this measurement outcome is

\displaystyle p_{I}(k_{1},k_{2},\Delta)

\displaystyle=\langle\Psi_{I}|\Psi_{I}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N+1}\sum_{k=\max(0,-\Delta)}^{\min(N,N-\Delta)}|\psi_{k}^{(0)}R_{k_{1},k}R_{k_{2},k+\Delta}|^{2}

(28)

The factors of
R
and the offset
\Delta
of the number state in (
26
) give a modification of the initial state
|\psi_{0}\rangle
originally on ensemble 1. To better understand the nature of these corrections, we make an approximation for the
R
matrix elements using the expression (
99
)

\displaystyle R_{k^{\prime},k}\approx A_{k^{\prime},k}(e^{i\phi_{k^{\prime}}(2k-N)/2}+(-1)^{n_{k^{\prime}}}e^{-i\phi_{k^{\prime}}(2k-N)/2})

(29)

where we defined

\displaystyle\phi_{k^{\prime}}=\cos^{-1}\Big(\frac{2k^{\prime}}{N}-1\Big).

(30)

and

\displaystyle n_{k}=\frac{N}{2}-\left|k-\frac{N}{2}\right|.

(31)

and the function
A_{k^{\prime},k}
is defined in (
98
).

Substituting the approximation (
29
) into (
26
), we obtain

\displaystyle|\Psi_{I}\rangle\approx\sum_{k=\max(0,-\Delta)}^{\min(N,N-\Delta)}A_{k_{1},k}A_{k_{2},k+\Delta}\psi_{k}^{(0)}

\displaystyle\times(e^{i\phi_{k_{1}}(2k-N)/2}+(-1)^{n_{k_{1}}}e^{-i\phi_{k_{1}}(2k-N)/2})

\displaystyle\times(e^{i\phi_{k_{2}}(2k+2\Delta-N)/2}+(-1)^{n_{k_{2}}}e^{-i\phi_{k_{2}}(2k+2\Delta-N)/2})|k+\Delta\rangle_{3},

(32)

where we have dropped the states on ensembles 1 and 2 and overall constant factors which do not affect the quantum state on ensemble 3.

The amplitude function
A_{k^{\prime},k}
is a slowly varying function of
k
which only affects the wavefunction if
\psi_{k}^{(0)}
is broad in the
k
variable. For teleporting states such as spin coherent states,
\psi_{k}^{(0)}
is strongly peaked which makes the dominant effect in the phase factors in (
29

\displaystyle|\Psi_{I}\rangle

\displaystyle\approx\frac{1}{2}\Big[e^{-i\phi_{k_{1}}\Delta}e^{i(\phi_{k_{1}}+\phi_{k_{2}})S^{z}/2}

\displaystyle+(-1)^{n_{k_{1}}}e^{i\phi_{k_{1}}\Delta}e^{-i(\phi_{k_{1}}-\phi_{k_{2}})S^{z}/2}

\displaystyle+(-1)^{n_{k_{2}}}e^{-i\phi_{k_{1}}\Delta}e^{i(\phi_{k_{1}}-\phi_{k_{2}})S^{z}/2}

\displaystyle+(-1)^{n_{k_{1}}+n_{k_{2}}}e^{i\phi_{k_{1}}\Delta}e^{-i(\phi_{k_{1}}+\phi_{k_{2}})S^{z}/2}\Big]T_{\Delta}|\psi_{0}\rangle.

(33)

where we introduced the shift operator

\displaystyle T_{\Delta}=\sum_{k=\max(0,-\Delta)}^{\min(N,N-\Delta)}|k+\Delta\rangle\langle k|.

(34)

To verify the approximate expression (
33
), we plot the Q-function of the wavefunction (
26
) which is defined as

\displaystyle Q(\theta,\phi)=|\langle\Psi_{I}|\theta,\phi\rangle\rangle_{3}|^{2},

(35)

where the spin coherent state is on ensemble 3. In Figure

we show the Q-function for two initial states
|\psi_{0}\rangle
. One of the initial states is chosen as a spin coherent state, and the other is a squeezed spin coherent state, both centered at

\pm(\phi_{k_{1}}\pm^{\prime}\phi_{k_{2}})
.

Taking the expectation value of the spin operators in the
x
and
y
direction with respect to the state (

\displaystyle\langle S^{x}\rangle

\displaystyle\approx\frac{1}{2}(\cos(\phi_{k_{1}}+\phi_{k_{2}})+\cos(\phi_{k_{1}}-\phi_{k_{2}}))\langle S^{x}\rangle_{0}

(36)

\displaystyle\langle S^{y}\rangle

\displaystyle\approx\frac{1}{2}(\cos(\phi_{k_{1}}+\phi_{k_{2}})+\cos(\phi_{k_{1}}-\phi_{k_{2}}))\langle S^{y}\rangle_{0}

(37)

where
\langle\vec{S}\rangle_{0}
is the expectation value with respect to the initial state
|\psi_{0}\rangle
and we use the rotation identities

\displaystyle e^{-i\phi/2}S^{x}e^{i\phi/2}

\displaystyle=\cos\phi S^{x}+\sin\phi S^{y}

\displaystyle e^{-i\phi/2}S^{y}e^{i\phi/2}

\displaystyle=\cos\phi S^{y}-\sin\phi S^{y}.

(38)

In obtaining (
37
) we assume that the four duplicates of the state that are rotated around the
z
axis in (
33
) are well-separated as in the case Fig.
2
. In this situation, the cross terms can be neglected leading to (
37
). Substituting (
30
) into (
37
) and simplifying we obtain

\displaystyle\langle S^{x}\rangle

\displaystyle\approx\frac{(2k_{1}-N)(2k_{2}-N)}{N^{2}}\langle S^{x}\rangle_{0}

(39)

\displaystyle\langle S^{y}\rangle

\displaystyle\approx\frac{(2k_{1}-N)(2k_{2}-N)}{N^{2}}\langle S^{y}\rangle_{0}.

(40)

For the
S^{z}
expectation values the phase factors in (
33
) do not affect the result. The only factor that affects the result is
T_{\Delta}
and we obtain

\displaystyle\langle S^{z}\rangle

\displaystyle\approx\langle S^{z}\rangle_{0}+2\Delta,

(41)

where the approximation is that the limits of the sum in (
34
) can be extended to the full range, which is valid as long as

\displaystyle\langle T_{\Delta}^{\dagger}T_{\Delta}\rangle_{0}=\sum_{k=\max(0,-\Delta)}^{\min(N,N-\Delta)}|\psi_{k}^{(0)}|^{2}\approx 1.

(42)

This occurs as long as the wavefunction does not have significant elements in the truncated parts of the sum. Again, the cases we shall mainly examine are states such as spin coherent states, where the distribution tends to be peaked in
k
, such that the approximation holds.

We may now understand the origins for the corrections in Step 5 of the protocol in Sec.
III.1
. For the polar angle
\theta_{\text{tel}}
, by subtracting off the constant
2\Delta
from the expectation value of
S^{z}
, we obtain the original expectation value of the state
|\psi_{0}\rangle
. For the azimuthal angle
\phi_{\text{tel}}
, dividing (
40
) by (
39
) removes the factors associated with the measurement outcomes
k_{1},k_{2}
. To correctly recover
\phi
in the correct quadrant,
\pi
is added to the principal value of
\tan^{-1}
if the sign of
(2k_{1}-N)(2k_{2}-N)
is negative.

IV.2
Protocol II

We now analyze Protocol II in a similar way. The wavefunction after Step 3 of the protocol is

\displaystyle|\Psi_{II}\rangle=

\displaystyle P_{\Delta_{2}}^{x}P_{\Delta_{1}}^{z}|\psi_{0}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{N+1}}\sum_{k=\max(0,-\Delta_{1})}^{\min(N,N-\Delta_{1})}\sum_{k^{\prime}=\max(0,-\Delta_{2})}^{\min(N,N-\Delta_{2})}

\displaystyle\times\psi_{k}^{(0)}R_{k^{\prime},k}R_{k^{\prime}+\Delta_{2},k+\Delta_{1}}|k^{\prime},k^{\prime}+\Delta_{2}\rangle^{(x)}_{12}|k+\Delta_{1}\rangle_{3}.

(43)

The probability of obtaining the measurement outcome is

\displaystyle p_{II}(\Delta_{1},\Delta_{2})=

\displaystyle\langle\Psi_{II}|\Psi_{II}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N+1}\sum_{k=\max(0,-\Delta_{1})}^{\min(N,N-\Delta_{1})}\sum_{k^{\prime}=\max(0,-\Delta_{2})}^{\min(N,N-\Delta_{2})}

\displaystyle\times|\psi_{k}^{(0)}R_{k^{\prime},k}R_{k^{\prime}+\Delta_{2},k+\Delta_{1}}|^{2}.

(44)

Using the same methods to approximate the
R
matrix elements as in Protocol I, we obtain the equivalent expression for the wavefunction in Protocol II

\displaystyle|\Psi_{II}\rangle\approx\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k^{\prime}=\max(0,-\Delta_{2})}^{\min(N,N-\Delta_{2})}|k^{\prime},k^{\prime}+\Delta_{2}\rangle^{(x)}_{12}

\displaystyle\otimes\Big[e^{-i\phi_{k^{\prime}}\Delta}e^{i(\phi_{k^{\prime}}+\phi_{k^{\prime}+\Delta_{2}})S^{z}/2}

\displaystyle+(-1)^{n_{k^{\prime}}}e^{i\phi_{k^{\prime}}\Delta_{1}}e^{-i(\phi_{k^{\prime}}-\phi_{k^{\prime}+\Delta_{2}})S^{z}/2}

\displaystyle+(-1)^{n_{k^{\prime}+\Delta_{2}}}e^{-i\phi_{k^{\prime}}\Delta_{1}}e^{i(\phi_{k^{\prime}}-\phi_{k^{\prime}+\Delta_{2}})S^{z}/2}

\displaystyle+(-1)^{n_{k^{\prime}}+n_{k^{\prime}+\Delta_{2}}}e^{i\phi_{k^{\prime}}\Delta_{1}}e^{-i(\phi_{k^{\prime}}+\phi_{k^{\prime}+\Delta_{2}})S^{z}/2}\Big]T_{\Delta_{1}}|\psi_{0}\rangle_{3}.

(45)

The main difference to the equivalent wavefunction (
33
) for Protocol I is that there is entanglement between ensemble 3 and the remaining ensembles (see Fig.
1
). This occurs because QND measurements only partially decouple the states, when performed as a sequence in the
z
and
x
bases. It was shown in Ref.
Chaudhary et al. 2022
that a long sequence of such measurements eventually results in the convergence towards an entangled state. With two measurements, there is some decoupling, but some remnant entanglement still remains.

Evaluating the
S^{x},S^{y}
expectation values with respect to the state (
45
) we obtain

\displaystyle\langle S^{x}_{3}\rangle\approx c(\Delta_{2})\langle S^{x}\rangle_{0}

(46)

\displaystyle\langle S^{y}_{3}\rangle\approx c(\Delta_{2})\langle S^{y}\rangle_{0}

(47)

where

\displaystyle c(\Delta)=\sum_{k^{\prime}=\max(0,-\Delta)}^{\min(N,N-\Delta)}\frac{(2k^{\prime}-N)(2k^{\prime}+2\Delta-N)}{N^{2}}

(48)

is an averaged version of the coefficients of the spin expectation values in (
39
) and (
40
) originating from the additional sum in (
45
). Evaluating the sum, and factorizing we obtain

\displaystyle c(\Delta)=\frac{2}{3}(|\Delta|-\Delta_{+})(|\Delta|-\Delta_{-})(|\Delta|-N-1),

(49)

where

\displaystyle\Delta_{\pm}=\frac{1}{2}\left(-N-1\pm\sqrt{3N^{2}+6N+1}\right).

(50)

The function
c(\Delta_{2})
gives negative values in the range
\Delta_{+}<|\Delta_{2}|<N+1
and is positive otherwise. This property of the coefficients gives the correction operation (
25
).

The
\theta_{\text{tel}}
correction (

\displaystyle\langle S^{z}_{3}\rangle\approx\langle S^{z}\rangle_{0}+2\Delta_{1},

(51)

which gives the correction operation (

VI
Comparison to classical bounds and decoherence

Our teleportation protocols only achieve sending the total spin expectation values of the state
|\psi_{0}\rangle
and not the state itself. The reason for this is visible in Fig.
2
— the state that is transmitted involves a four-fold duplicate of the original state. This raises the question of exactly how quantum the protocol is. Is there a classical strategy that could match or outperform the scheme? These questions are important also from the perspective of proving that a non-trivial quantum operation has been performed. Typically, experimental demonstrations of quantum teleportation are accompanied by classical bounds in the fidelity to show that a non-classical operation has been performed.

A purely classical strategy involves Alice measuring the spin expectation value
\langle\vec{S}\rangle_{0}
using only one copy of the state
|\psi_{0}\rangle
. She then classically transmits her best estimate of the spin expectation value to Bob. For spin coherent states, we may use the quantum state estimation bounds derived by Massar and Popescu, where the optimal fidelity of estimating
N
copies of a qubit is
F=(N+1)/(N+2)
. Converting this to a qubit trace distance
Fuchs and Van De Graaf 1999
defined as in (
55
), we have
Pyrkov and Byrnes 2014b

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{\text{QSE}}\geq\sqrt{1-F}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N+2}},

(62)

which represents the lowest error that can be attained using the classical strategy. Comparing this to the average error of Protocols I and II given as (
56
) and (
60
), we find that in the ideal case

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{\text{tel}}<\varepsilon_{\text{QSE}}

(63)

for both Protocols I and II, showing that the teleportation exceeds the performance of a purely classical strategy. As shown in the next section, decoherence will increase the average error, which may lift the teleportation error above the classical bound.

VII
Decoherence

An important consideration in the context of macroscopic teleportation is decoherence. Typically macroscopic quantum states (e.g. Schrodinger cat states) decohere extremely quickly, rendering them impossible to realize them experimentally
Zurek 2003
. In the case of atomic ensembles, the primary forms of decoherence that take place are in the form of atomic loss and dephasing
Byrnes et al. 2015
.
The most sensitive part of the protocol to decoherence is the preparation of the maximally entangled state (
16
) and also the final states (
26
) and (
43
) which involve Schrodinger cat-like states. Such states involve macroscopic superpositions and are potentially susceptible to decoherence that scale unfavorably with
N
. Therefore, on first glance it may appear that our protocol cannot work effectively when decoherence is included. In this section, we show that in fact our protocol is rather robust under decoherence.

To understand the effect of decoherence, we apply dephasing according to

\displaystyle\frac{d\rho}{dt}=-\frac{\gamma}{2}\sum_{l=1}^{3}\Big[(S^{x}_{l})^{2}\rho-S^{x}_{l}\rho S^{x}_{l}+\rho(S^{x}_{l})^{2}\Big],

(64)

which is applied to the initial state (
17
) and also after the measurements are made (after Steps 1 and 3 in both Protocols I and II). We then evaluate the expectation values and evaluate the average error according to (
55
). We apply dephasing in the
x
direction because in Step 2 of the protocol we apply a QND projection in the
z
direction. Dephasing in the
z
direction commutes with dephasing and gives a more trivial dependence. The details of the calculation are deferred to Appendix
F
.

Figure
7
(a)(b) shows the dependence of the average error (
55
) with
N
for various initial states and decoherence rates. We see that in all cases the average error has no dependence on
N
. There is very little difference between the Protocol I and II cases. The reason for the independence with
N
may be understood as follows.
After the measurement steps in the teleportation protocols, states such as shown in Fig.
1
are generated, which are Schrodinger cat-like states. These are typically highly susceptible to decoherence in terms of the states themselves, but spin expectations are generally unaffected. For example, consider the Schrodinger cat state

\displaystyle|\xi\rangle\approx\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|\theta,\phi\rangle\rangle+|\theta^{\prime},\phi^{\prime}\rangle\rangle)

(65)

where we took the coordinates
(\theta,\phi)
and
(\theta^{\prime},\phi^{\prime})
sufficiently distant such that
\langle\langle\theta^{\prime},\phi^{\prime}|\theta,\phi\rangle\rangle\approx 0
. Under decoherence such as state very quickly collapses to a incoherent mixture

\displaystyle\rho\approx\frac{1}{2}(|\theta,\phi\rangle\rangle\langle\langle\theta,\phi|+|\theta^{\prime},\phi^{\prime}\rangle\rangle\langle\langle\theta^{\prime},\phi^{\prime}|).

(66)

Evaluating spin expectation values, the only difference between the cat state and the incoherent mixture is the presence of cross terms

\displaystyle\langle\xi|\vec{S}|\xi\rangle-\text{Tr}(\rho\vec{S})=\text{Re}(\langle\langle\theta,\phi|\vec{S}|\theta^{\prime},\phi^{\prime}\rangle\rangle).

(67)

Since the spin operators only cause single number state transitions
|k\rangle\rightarrow|k\pm 1\rangle
, for well separated cat states, the difference (
67
) is exponentially suppressed
Byrnes and Ilo-Okeke 2021
. In deriving the expectation values in Sec.
IV
we have approximated that such cross-terms are negligible. In the presence of decoherence, neglecting such terms become exact. For these reasons, the spin expectation values of the teleportation should be unaffected by the decoherence of the cat states generated after the measurement steps of the teleportation protocol.

In our calculations we also apply decoherence to the initial state (
17
). From the results in Fig.
7
(a)(b), we conclude that there is no sensitivity with
N
, and only has the overall effect of dampening the spin expectation values of the teleported state in the
y
and
z
directions. The stability of the maximally entangled state under dephasing was examined in works such as Ref.
Byrnes 2013
;
Gao et al. 2022
;
Gao et al. 2023
;
Li et al. 2022

and was found to be more robust than states such as Schrodinger cat states, since they do not directly involve macroscopically different superpositions. Since the technique to prepare it (Ref.
Chaudhary et al. 2023
) is a measurement based method, we expect it to be more robust as the projections act to project the state into the desired spin sector. We therefore expect that as long as the teleportation protocols are executed within a suitable decoherence window, these can be performed with the high fidelity value.

In Fig.
7
(c)(d) we also show the
\theta_{0}
dependence of the average error for various
\phi_{0}
values at a fixed
N
. We see that the largest effect of the decoherence occurs for the states at the poles of the Bloch sphere. This is not surprising as we apply dephasing in the
x
direction, and spin coherent states polarized in the
z
direction are most susceptible. As the state approaches the
x
direction, the state becomes less affected, since the dephasing only creates a global phase. There is again very little difference between the effect on Protocol I and II, since both effectively teleport the initial state.

In summary, we expect our teleportation protocols are highly robust in the presence of decoherence. The primary reason for this is that the teleported variables are spin expectation values, rather than the full quantum states themselves. We expect similar results for other types of decoherence. For example, for atomic loss, the total spin operator merely results in an amplitude damping of the total spin.

VIII
Experimental implementation

We now briefly describe the experimental requirements necessary for realizing our teleportation protocols. Our protocols have been designed with experimental methods that have been demonstrated in the past, hence we believe that our approach should be experimentally accessible.

The qubit ensembles can be realized with neutral gas ensembles, trapped in glass cells, or with multiple optical/magnetic traps such as with an atom chip. Each atom has two populated internal states. A common choice for the internal states are hyperfine ground states, such as the
F=1,m_{F}=-1
and
F=2,m_{F}=1
states in the case of
87
Rb
Pezzè et al. 2018
. Either hot or cold atomic ensembles or BECs may be used, due to the equivalence of the total spin operators in the symmetric subspace
Byrnes and Ilo-Okeke 2021
. Three ensembles are required to perform the quantum teleportation, involving either multiple atomic cells or with an atom chip with multiple traps. For the preparation of the initial state, coherent microwave frequency radiation can prepare spin coherent states, as is routinely performed with atomic ensembles
Pezzè et al. 2018
.

Both Protocol I and II rely on using the QND measurements as described in Appendix
A
. QND measurements have already been proven to be an effective method for generating entanglement between atomic ensembles
Kuzmich et al. 2000
;
Julsgaard et al. 2001
;
Hammerer et al. 2010
. The Mach-Zehnder configuration as shown in Fig.
8
is an equivalent way of performing the same task, which measures the relative
S^{z}_{1}-S^{z}_{2}
eigenvalue
Aristizabal-Zuluaga et al. 2021
. The details of such measurements in a POVM framework is given in Refs.
Ilo-Okeke et al. 2023
;
Chaudhary et al. 2022
. Numerous works have shown that the type of entanglement that is generated is robust against various decoherence sources such as photon loss and spontaneous emission
Ilo-Okeke and Byrnes 2024
;
Gao et al. 2022
;
Gao et al. 2023
. The main challenges will be to precisely realize the projection operator (
12
), which ideally gives a readout at the single atom level. The outcomes
\Delta
corresponds to the eigenvalues of
(S^{z}_{1}-S^{z}_{2})/2
which is deduced from the photon number outcomes. A sufficiently large photon amplitude, combined with an accurate estimation of the photon intensity should give a good estimate on
\Delta
, but to our knowledge this has not been experimentally realized. With an accurate QND measurement, the maximally entangled state can be deterministically prepared as discussed in Sec.
II.3
.

Finally, in order to read out the state of the spins on ensemble 3, and also to perform the projective measurements in Protocol I, the single ensemble projective measurement (
8
) is required. This is routinely performed in atomic ensemble experiments, for example using absorption imaging
Böhi et al. 2009
;
Riedel et al. 2010
. A precise readout is required for both a precise estimation of the spin
\langle\vec{S}\rangle
and to perform the correction operations in Protocol I. Readout at near-single atom resolution has been demonstrated in Refs.
Bücker et al. 2009
;
Hume et al. 2013
;
Bergschneider et al. 2018
;
Alberti et al. 2016
.

While we have emphasized the readout at the single atom level above, we note that our protocols are
not
in fact extremely sensitive to readout error, both in the final spin readout nor the correction operations. For example, the spin readout
\langle S^{z}\rangle
is evaluated by counting the difference between the
a
and
b
qubit states, and errors in counting these only affect the Bloch sphere angles
\theta_{\text{tel}},\phi_{\text{tel}}
unless they are of magnitude
\sim N
. Similarly, the correction operations such as
H(k_{1,2}-N/2)
and
H(|\Delta_{2}|-\Delta_{+})
are only significantly affected when the errors in the readouts
k_{1,2}
and
\Delta_{2}
are at the
\sim N
level. This is rather different, for example, to if the correction operations involved the parity of
k_{1,2}
and
\Delta_{2}
, which are very sensitive to the readout error.

IX
Summary and Conclusions

We have introduced two teleportation protocols to transmit an unknown spin expectation values using entanglement and classical communication based on qubit ensembles. One of the main features of the protocol is that it does not use any operations requiring microscopic control of the qubits within each ensemble. The only operations that are required consist of collective spin rotations and measurements of each ensemble, and entangling QND measurements between two ensembles. An arbitrary spin pointing in any direction of the Bloch sphere can be teleported, hence the protocol goes beyond the conventional Holstein-Primakoff approximation for performing continuous variable teleportation. We have introduced two versions of the teleportation scheme (Protocols I and II) since they have a similar performance albeit with small differences in the general behavior. Which protocol is chosen depends entirely on which is more convenient for the implementation.

In terms of the performance, we point out that the protocols are not perfectly deterministic in the sense that every measurement outcome yields a spin expectation that is precisely in the same spin direction as the initial state. However, when averaged over measurements (including the classically communicated correction), the averaged spin remarkably gives
perfect
transmission, for the case of a spin coherent state. While we are able to perform analytical calculations of the resulting state, this has involved approximations, notably of the matrix elements
\langle k|k^{\prime}\rangle^{(x)}
. In general such factors deviate the state from a simple state such as a spin coherent state (see for e.g. Eq. (
26
)) hence it is not obvious how the spin expectations are so perfectly preserved. This may point to an alternative way of analyzing the resulting wavefunctions without resorting to the approximations which we have implemented. We note that we mainly considered initial states that are spin coherent states, and the protocol could be used for other types of states. For other states we expect that the perfect transmission may be adversely affected since the way they are formulated in Sec.
III
, only angular variables are transmitted, and some states may not be on the surface of the Bloch sphere as is implicitly assumed here.

While our protocol is not perfectly deterministic, they are quasi-deterministic in the sense that the standard deviation of the states distributed around the Bloch sphere is rather tightly distributed. The distribution only occurs in the
\theta
direction, and in the
\phi
direction there is no variance since points with errors occur with a phase difference of
\pi
. To derive the correction steps in Protocol I and II, we have used the analytical approximations, and this is successful in picking up a majority of the states. But as can be seen from Figs.
3
and
4
some outcomes are not corrected perfectly. Hence there is still scope for improvement to use the correction protocol in order to better approximate a deterministic scheme.

We have tested the robustness of our protocol under decoherence and found that it is rather robust. For the dephasing model that we considered, there is no dependence on the ensemble size
N
. This is important since in teleporting macroscopic states, one potential point of failure is that coherence rapidly degrades for large systems, such as in Schrodinger cat states. Our protocol has been designed with this in mind, where more robust states such as spin coherent states and the maximally entangled state
|\Phi\rangle\rangle
are used. We have analyzed the effect of decoherence in previous works
Byrnes 2013
;
Gao et al. 2022
;
Gao et al. 2023
;
Chaudhary et al. 2021
;
Chaudhary et al. 2023
and know that such states are generally rather robust. Teleporting the total spin variables (rather than the full state) is also a key factor that allows for the protocol to be successful in the presence of decoherence, since spin operators tend to only decohere at the same rate as that for the corresponding qubit variables. Our protocol is however not extremely sensitive to readout errors since only spin averages and correction operations in two broad regions of the measurement outcome space are required. We therefore expect that our protocols should be experimentally accessible.

Appendix A
Projection operators for QND measurements

Here we summarize the theory of QND measurements in the positive operator valued measure (POVM) formalism
Aristizabal-Zuluaga et al. 2021
;
Chaudhary et al. 2022
;
Ilo-Okeke et al. 2023
.

The basic setup for a QND measurement is shown in Fig.
8
. Coherent light is used to perform an indirect measurement of the atomic ensembles arranged in a Mach-Zehnder configuration. The interaction between photons and atoms is governed by the Hamiltonian
Kuzmich et al. 2000
,

\displaystyle H=\kappa(S^{z}_{1}-S^{z}_{2}+2N)J^{z},

(68)

where
\kappa
is the coupling constant and
J^{z}=e^{\dagger}e-f^{\dagger}f
is the Stokes operator for the two optical modes
e,f
that enter into each arm of the interferometer. After interacting with the atoms, the photonic modes are interfered with a beam splitter, giving rise to new modes
c,d
and the photons are detected by the detectors with counts
n_{c},n_{d}
respectively.

The QND measurement between two ensembles can be described in terms of a Positive Operator Valued Measure (POVM) as
Chaudhary et al. 2022

\displaystyle M_{n_{c}n_{d}}(\tau)=\sum_{k_{1},k_{2}=0}C_{n_{c},n_{d}}[(k_{1}-k_{2}+N)\tau]|k_{1},k_{2}\rangle\langle k_{1},k_{2}|,

(69)

where
|k_{1},k_{2}\rangle=|k_{1}\rangle\otimes|k_{2}\rangle
and
the modulating function is defined as

\displaystyle C_{n_{c},n_{d}}(\chi)=\frac{\alpha^{n_{c}+n_{d}}e^{-|\alpha|^{2}/2}}{\sqrt{n_{c}!n_{d}!}}\cos^{n_{c}}(\chi)\sin^{n_{d}}(\chi),

(70)

and
\tau=\kappa t
is the interaction time. The resulting state after the measurement is

\displaystyle M_{n_{c}n_{d}}(\tau)|\psi\rangle=\sum_{k_{1},k_{2}}\psi_{k_{1}k_{2}}C_{n_{c},n_{d}}[(k_{1}-k_{2}+N)\tau]|k_{1},k_{2}\rangle.

(71)

where
\psi_{k_{1},k_{2}}=\langle k_{1},k_{2}|\psi\rangle
.
Here we see that the initial wave function is modulated by an extra factor of
C_{n_{c},n_{d}}[(k_{1}-k_{2}+N)\tau]
.

For large photon number
|\alpha|^{2}\gg 1
and short interaction times
\tau\sim 1/N
, the modulating function
C_{n_{c},n_{d}}
takes a Gaussian form
Aristizabal-Zuluaga et al. 2021
and is sharply peaked at

\displaystyle\sin^{2}[(k_{1}-k_{2}+N)\tau]=\frac{n_{d}}{n_{c}+n_{d}}.

(72)

This is the mechanism that results in the measurement-induced generation of entanglement since it enforces the argument of the modulating function to be a constant value, i.e.
(k_{1}-k_{2})\tau=\text{const}
. This is equivalent to a measurement of the operator
S^{z}_{1}-S^{z}_{2}
. For smaller photon counts the modulating function becomes broader and corresponds to a weak measurement.

Taking the interaction time
\tau=\pi/4N
and assuming
|\alpha\tau|^{2}\gg 1
, as defined in Ref.
Chaudhary et al. 2022
, we may then approximate the POVM (
69
) as a projection operator (
12
) according to

\displaystyle M_{n_{c}n_{d}}(\tau=\frac{\pi}{4N})\approx P_{\Delta}^{z},

(73)

where the projections
\Delta=k_{1}-k_{2}
and photonic measurements
n_{c},n_{d}
are related according to (
72
).

Figure 8:
Entanglement generating QND measurements between qubit ensembles.
Coherent light
|\alpha\rangle
interacts with ensembles via the QND Hamiltonian interaction (
68
) arranged in a Mach-Zehnder configuration. After the second beam splitter
B_{2}
, the photon modes are detected yielding counts
n_{c},n_{d}
. This entangles ensemble 1 with ensemble 2.

Appendix B
Maximally entangled state preparation

The protocol involves repeated measurements of QND projection operator (
12
) and (
13
) on two ensembles. The method takes advantage of the fact that the state (
16
) is a simultaneous eigenstate of the projection operator with probability 1

\displaystyle P_{0}^{z}|\Phi\rangle\rangle=P_{0}^{x}|\Phi\rangle\rangle=|\Phi\rangle\rangle.

(74)

This means that once the state (
16
) is obtained, the QND measurements no longer affect the state, and results in a fixed point of the repeated measurements. This is however only true for the measurement outcome
\Delta=0
in both the
x
and
z
basis. Since quantum measurements are intrinsically random, we must account for the fact that
\Delta\neq 0
may be obtained. To overcome this an adaptive measurement sequence is performed:

\displaystyle T_{\vec{\Delta}}^{z}=\prod_{j=1}^{L}U_{\Delta_{j}}P_{\Delta_{j}}^{z}

(75)

where
\vec{\Delta}=(\Delta_{1},\Delta_{2},\dots,\Delta_{L})
is the measurement sequence where the last measurement value is
\Delta_{L}=0
. A suitable adaptive unitary is

\displaystyle U_{\Delta}=e^{iS^{y}\pi\Delta/2N}\otimes I.

(76)

Then by applying such a sequence in both the
z
and
x
basis, an arbitrary state is driven towards the maximally entangled state

\displaystyle\prod_{r=1}^{M}(T_{\vec{\Delta}^{x}_{r}}^{x}T_{\vec{\Delta}^{z}_{r}}^{z})|\psi_{0}\rangle\rightarrow|\Phi\rangle\rangle,

(77)

where
T_{\vec{\Delta}}^{x}=e^{-i(S^{x}_{1}+S^{x}_{2})\pi/4}T_{\vec{\Delta}}^{z}e^{i(S^{x}_{1}+S^{x}_{2})\pi/4}
.

In Ref.
Chaudhary et al. 2023
a slightly different QND measurement was used but the above sequence (
12
) and (
13
) is equally effective at producing the maximally entangled state.

In this paper, we primarily consider the case where Alice and Bob are not separated by large distances. In principle, generating entanglement separated by large distances is not a fundamental obstacle to the above scheme as it relies only on QND measurements and local spin rotations. QND measurements are performed using bright coherent light
Aristizabal-Zuluaga et al. 2021
, which is not as sensitive as alternative entanglement generation approaches relying on single photon transmission, for example. Using a polarization encoding of the modes, long-distance interferometry should be possible
Ilo-Okeke et al. 2023
, realizing long-distance entanglement. For other types of entanglement, other approaches have also been developed using more conventional quantum repeater methods
Pyrkov et al. 2024
.

Appendix F
Decoherence

We use the formulation following Ref.
Pyrkov and Byrnes 2014b
to apply the Lindblad dephasing master equation (
64
) using a stochastic rotation, such that starting from an initial state
\rho=\rho_{0}
, evolves as

\displaystyle\rho(t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\gamma t}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\xi\exp\left(-\frac{\xi^{2}}{2\gamma t}\right)e^{i\xi S^{x}}\rho_{0}e^{-i\xi S^{x}}.

(100)

This has the physical interpretation of a mixture of random phase rotations.

We first apply the dephasing to the initial state
\rho_{0}=|\Psi_{0}\rangle\langle\Psi_{0}|
, where the state
|\Psi_{0}\rangle
is (
17
). Applying the random phases to all three ensembles, we have

\displaystyle|\Psi_{0}(\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\xi_{3})\rangle

\displaystyle=e^{i\xi_{1}S^{x}_{1}}e^{i\xi_{2}S^{x}_{2}}e^{i\xi_{3}S^{x}_{3}}|\Psi_{0}\rangle

\displaystyle=|\psi_{0}(\xi_{1})\rangle\otimes e^{i(\xi_{2}+\xi_{3})S^{x}_{3}}|\Psi\rangle\rangle,

(101)

where we defined a rotated version of the initial state
|\psi_{0}(\xi_{1})\rangle=e^{i\xi_{1}S^{x}_{1}}|\psi_{0}\rangle
and use the basis invariant property of the maximally entangled state
Kitzinger et al. 2020
.

For Protocol I, we then apply the measurements as given in (
26
). Since the measurements are only applied on ensembles 1 and 2, we may easily write

\displaystyle|\Psi_{I}(\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\xi_{3})\rangle=\Pi_{k_{1}}^{x,1}\Pi_{k_{2}}^{x,2}P_{\Delta}^{z}|\Psi_{0}(\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\xi_{3})\rangle

\displaystyle=e^{i(\xi_{2}+\xi_{3})S^{x}_{3}}|\Psi_{I}(\xi_{1})\rangle,

(102)

where
\psi_{k}^{(0)}(\xi_{1})=\langle k|\psi_{0}(\xi_{1})\rangle
and

\displaystyle|\Psi_{I}(\xi_{1})\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N+1}}

\displaystyle\times\sum_{k=\max(0,-\Delta)}^{\min(N,N-\Delta)}\psi_{k}^{(0)}(\xi_{1})R_{k_{1},k}R_{k_{2},k+\Delta}|k+\Delta\rangle_{3}

(103)

is the state after Step 3 of Protocol I with a rotated initial state.

We then apply decoherence again to (

) to all three ensembles with the random phases
\xi_{1}^{\prime},\xi_{2}^{\prime},\xi_{3}^{\prime}
. At this point ensembles 1 and 2 are disentangled and hence the random phases have no effect, giving the final state

\displaystyle|\Psi_{I}(\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\xi_{3},\xi_{3}^{\prime})\rangle=e^{i(\xi_{2}+\xi_{3}+\xi_{3}^{\prime})S^{x}_{3}}|\Psi_{I}(\xi_{1})\rangle,

(104)

\displaystyle\rho_{I}=\frac{1}{(2\pi\gamma t)^{2}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\xi_{1}d\xi_{2}d\xi_{3}d\xi_{3}^{\prime}e^{-\frac{\xi^{2}_{1}+\xi^{2}_{2}+\xi^{2}_{3}+(\xi_{3}^{\prime})^{2}}{2\gamma t}}
