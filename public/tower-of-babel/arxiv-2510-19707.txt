Open Neighborhood Ideals of Well Totally Dominated Trees are Cohen-Macaulay
Jounglag Lim, James Gossell, Keri Ann Sather-Wagstaff, Devin Adams, Vi Anh Nguyen, Suzanna Castro-Tarabulsi, Aayahna Herbert, Yifan Qian, Matthew Schaller, Zoe Zhou, Yuyang Zhuo

Abstract
. We introduce and investigate the open neighborhood ideal 𝒩 ⁡ ( G ) \mathcal{N}(G) of a finite simple graph G G . We describe the minimal primary decomposition of 𝒩 ⁡ ( G ) \mathcal{N}(G) in terms of the minimal total dominating sets (TD-sets) of G G . Then we prove that the open neighborhood ideal of a tree is Cohen-Macaulay if and only if the tree is unmixed (well totally dominated) and calculate the Cohen-Macaulay type. We also give a descriptive characterization of all unmixed trees which takes polynomial time to verify.



1.
Introduction

Commutative algebra and combinatorics have a rich history of intersections.
In this paper, we focus on connections between commutative algebra and graph theory.
This approach begins with work of Villarreal
[
18
]
who introduced and investigated the
edge ideal

\mathcal{I}(G)
of a finite, simple graph
G
, which is the ideal generated by the edges of
G
in an appropriate polynomial ring. He described the minimal primary decomposition of
\mathcal{I}(G)
in terms of the minimal vertex covers of
G
and proved that the edge ideal of a tree is Cohen-Macaulay if and only if the tree is unmixed (well covered). He also gave explicit descriptive and constructive characterizations of all unmixed trees.
Much subsequent research has expanded our understanding of the relations between the algebraic properties of
\mathcal{I}(G)
and the graph-theoretic properties of
G
; see, e.g.,
[
17
,
19
,
9
,
11
,
7
]
.

Variations on this algebraic construction relate to different properties of the graph.
For instance, Conca and De Negri
[
6
]
introduced the
r
-path ideal
\mathcal{I}_{r}(G)
which is generated by the paths of length
r
in
G
and is connected to
r
-path covers;
the edge ideal is the special case
r=1
, i.e.,
\mathcal{I}_{1}(G)=\mathcal{I}(G)
.
Campos, et al.
[
5
]
characterized the Cohen-Macaulay
r
-path ideals of trees.
Sharifan and Moradi
[
15
]
introduced the closed neighborhood ideal of
G
which is generated by the closed neighborhoods in
G
and is connected to domination sets.
Honeycutt and Sather-Wagstaff
[
12
]
characterized the trees whose closed neighborhood ideals are Cohen-Macaulay as those that are unmixed (well dominated tree).

This paper investigates a similar construction based on open neighborhoods: the
open neighborhood ideal

\mathcal{N}(G)
of
G
is the ideal generated by the open neighborhoods of
G
.
We define this formally in Definition
2.2.4
below.
Section
2
contains some foundational information.
It includes Theorem
2.2.6
which describes the minimal primary decomposition of
\mathcal{N}(G)
in terms of the minimal total dominating sets (TD-sets) of
G
.

Section
3
consists of graph-theoretic tools, our descriptive characterization of unmixed trees in Theorem
3.4.9
, and the constructive characterization of unmixed balanced trees in Theorem
3.5.6
.
Then in Section
4
, we show that the open neighborhood ideals of unmixed trees are Cohen-Macaulay; see Corollary
4.2.16
which follows from the fact that the corresponding Stanley-Reisner simplicial complex is shellable, proved in Theorem
4.2.15
.
The current paper ends with Section
5
where we use our results to compute the Cohen-Macaulay type of open neighborhood ideal of unmixed trees in Theorem
5.2.7
.

Assumptions
.

Throughout, we assume that
G
is a finite, simple graph,
\Bbbk
is a field, and
A
is a nonzero commutative ring with unity.
The vertex set and the edge set of
G
are
V(G)
and
E(G)
, respectively.
If the graph we consider is clear in context, then we write
V:=V(G)
and

Since

Let

be the polynomial ring over

We set

2.
Total Dominating Sets and Open Neighborhood Ideals

This section contains some graph-theoretical, foundational information and Theorem
2.2.6
which describes the minimal primary decomposition of
\mathcal{N}(G)
in terms of the minimal total dominating sets (TD-sets) of
G
.

2.1.
Foundations

Definition 2.1.1
.

For
v\in V(G)
, the
open neighborhood of
v
in
G
is the set of vertices

N_{G}(v):=\left\{u\in V\mid uv\in E\right\}.

For any subset
S\subseteq V(G)
, we define

N_{G}(S):=\bigcup_{v\in S}N_{G}(v).

If the graph
G
is clear in the context, we set
N(v):=N_{G}(v)
and
N(S):=N_{G}(S)
.
For
U\subseteq V
, define the
monomial of
U
in
A[V]
by

X_{U}:=\prod_{v\in U}v
.

Definition 2.1.2
.

Fix
S\subseteq V
.
A set
D\subseteq V
is an
S
-totally dominating set
of
G
(
S
-TD-set
of
G
) if
N(D)\supseteq S
. An
S
-TD-set
D
of
G

is
minimal
if there is no proper subset
D^{\prime}
of
D
such that
N_{G}(D^{\prime})\supseteq S
, i.e., if
D
does not properly contain another
S
-TD-set of
G
.
We say that
G
is
S
-
unmixed
if every minimal
S
-TD-set of
G
has the same size.
The
S
-open neighborhood ideal of
G
is the following ideal in
R=A[V]

\mathcal{N}_{S}(G):=\left\langle X_{N(v)}\mid v\in S\right\rangle.

Example 2.1.3
.

Consider the path
P_{5}
with 5 edges and set
R=A[V]
and
S=\left\{v_{2},v_{4},v_{6}\right\}
.

Then the
S
-open neighborhood ideal of
P_{5}
is

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{S}(P_{5})=\left\langle X_{N(v_{2})},X_{N(v_{4})},X_{N(v_{6})}\right\rangle=\left\langle v_{1}v_{3},v_{3}v_{5},v_{5}\right\rangle=\left\langle v_{1}v_{3},v_{5}\right\rangle

which decomposes irredundantly as

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{S}(P_{5})

\displaystyle=\left\langle v_{1}v_{3},v_{5}\right\rangle=\left\langle v_{1},v_{5}\right\rangle\cap\left\langle v_{3},v_{5}\right\rangle.

It is straightforward to see that the sets
\left\{v_{1},v_{5}\right\}
and
\left\{v_{3},v_{5}\right\}
corresponding to the ideals in this decomposition are exactly the minimal
S
-TD-sets of
P_{5}
.
In particular,
P_{5}
is
S
-unmixed.

The following fact is used in the proof of Theorem
2.2.2
which gives a general decomposition of
\mathcal{N}_{S}(G)
.

Fact 2.1.4
.

Let
S\subseteq V
. The following statements are straightforward to verify.

(1)

For any
V^{\prime}\subseteq V^{\prime\prime}\subseteq V
, if
V^{\prime}
is an
S
-TD-set of
G
, then
V^{\prime\prime}
is an
S
-TD-set of
G
.

(2)

Every
S
-TD-set of
G
contains a minimal
S
-TD-set of
G
.

2.2.
Decompositions

Lemma 2.2.1
.

Let
S,V^{\prime}\subseteq V
and
R=A[V]
.
Then
V^{\prime}
is an
S
-TD-set of
G
if and only if
\mathcal{N}_{S}(G)\subseteq\left\langle V^{\prime}\right\rangle
.

Proof.

For the forward implication, assume that
V^{\prime}
is an
S
-TD-set.
Consider a generator
X_{N(s)}\in\mathcal{N}_{S}(G)
with
s\in S
.
Since
V^{\prime}
is an
S
-TD-set, there exists some vertex
v\in V^{\prime}
such that
v\in N(s)
.
Hence we get
v|X_{N(s)}
, so
X_{N(s)}\in(v)R\subseteq\left\langle V^{\prime}\right\rangle
.
Thus
\mathcal{N}_{S}(G)\subseteq\left\langle V^{\prime}\right\rangle
.

For the converse implication, assume that
\mathcal{N}_{S}(G)\subseteq\left\langle V^{\prime}\right\rangle
.
Let
u\in S
.
Then
X_{N(u)}\in\mathcal{N}_{S}(G)\subseteq\left\langle V^{\prime}\right\rangle
.
Hence there exists some
v\in V^{\prime}
such that
v|X_{N(u)}
.
By definition of
X_{N(u)}
, we must have
v\in N(u)
.
Thus
u\in N(v)\subseteq N(V^{\prime})
.
Since
u
is an arbitrary vertex in
S
, we get
S\subseteq N(V^{\prime})
, so
V^{\prime}
is an
S
-TD-set.
∎

The following theorem describes the decomposition of
S
-open neighborhood ideals using
S
-totally dominating sets.

Theorem 2.2.2
.

Let
S\subseteq V
, and
R=A[V]
.
The
S
-open neighborhood ideal has the following decomposition

\mathcal{N}_{S}(G)=\bigcap_{D}\left\langle D\right\rangle=\bigcap_{D\ min}\left\langle D\right\rangle

where the first intersection is taken over all
S
-TD-sets of
G
, and the second intersection is taken over all minimal
S
-TD-sets of G.
Moreover, the second decomposition is irredundant.

Proof.

For any
A,B\subseteq V
, we have
\left\langle A\right\rangle\subseteq\left\langle B\right\rangle
if and only if
A\subseteq B
.
Hence the second intersection is irredundant.
Now let
V^{\prime}\subseteq V
be an
S
-TD-set which is not minimal.
Then
V^{\prime}
contains a minimal
S
-TD-set by Fact
2.1.4
.
So, we have
\bigcap_{D}\left\langle D\right\rangle=\bigcap_{D\neq V^{\prime}}\left\langle D\right\rangle
.
Since
V
is finite, by repeating the same argument finitely many times, we can conclude that
\bigcap_{D}\left\langle D\right\rangle=\bigcap_{D\ min}\left\langle D\right\rangle
.

By Lemma
2.2.1
, we get
\mathcal{N}_{S}(G)\subseteq\bigcap_{D}\left\langle D\right\rangle
.
For the containment
\mathcal{N}_{S}(G)\supseteq\bigcap_{D}\left\langle D\right\rangle
, since
\mathcal{N}_{S}(G)
is a square-free monomial, there are subsets
D_{1},\dots,D_{k}\subseteq V
such that
\mathcal{N}_{S}(G)=\bigcap_{i=1}^{k}\left\langle D_{i}\right\rangle
.
For any index
j\in[k]
, we have
\mathcal{N}_{S}(G)\subseteq\left\langle D_{j}\right\rangle
, which implies that
D_{j}
is an
S
-TD-set by Lemma
2.2.1
.
Thus we get
\mathcal{N}_{S}(G)=\bigcap_{i=1}^{k}\left\langle D_{i}\right\rangle\supseteq\bigcap_{D}\left\langle D\right\rangle
.
∎

Now we consider a specific case when
S=V
, the entire vertex set.

Definition 2.2.3
.

A subset
D\subseteq V(G)
is a
total dominating set (TD-set) of
G
if
N(D)=V(G)
.
We say
D
is a
minimal TD-set
if no proper subset of
D
is a TD-set.
We say
G
is
unmixed
if every minimal TD-set of
G
has the same size.

The minimal TD-set problem on graphs is a well studied subject in graph theory
[
10
]
, and finding the size of the smallest minimal TD-set of a graph turns out to be an NP-complete problem
[
13
]
.
Also, finding and characterising unmixed graphs (also called
well totally dominated graphs
) is an interesting problem
[
8
,
1
]
.
In this paper, we focus on the unmixedness property of trees to establish our goal.

Definition 2.2.4
.

The
open neighborhood ideal of
G
in
R=A[V]
is the ideal generated by the monomials of the open neighborhoods of the vertices of
G

\mathcal{N}(G):=\mathcal{N}_{V}(G)=\left\langle X_{N_{G}(v)}\mid v\in V\right\rangle.

Example 2.2.5
.

Let
T
be the following tree with vertex set
V=\left\{v_{1},v_{2},\dots,v_{8}\right\}
.

Then
R=A[V]=A[v_{1},\dots,v_{8}]
, and we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}(T)=\left\langle v_{4},v_{5},v_{1}v_{2}v_{6},v_{3}v_{7},v_{4}v_{8},v_{5}v_{8},v_{6}v_{5}\right\rangle=\left\langle v_{4},v_{5},v_{1}v_{2}v_{6},v_{3}v_{7},v_{6}v_{5}\right\rangle.

We get the following decomposition theorem of open-neighborhood ideals as a corollary of Theorem
2.2.2
. When
A
is a field, this gives the (minimal) primary decomposition of
\mathcal{N}(G)
.

Theorem 2.2.6
.

The open neighborhood ideal of
G
in
R=A[V]
has the following decomposition

\mathcal{N}(G)=\bigcap_{V^{\prime}}\left\langle V^{\prime}\right\rangle=\bigcap_{V^{\prime}\ min}\left\langle V^{\prime}\right\rangle

where the first intersection is taken over all TD-sets of
G
, and the second intersection is taken over all minimal TD-sets of
G
.
Moreover, the second decomposition is irredundant.

Example 2.2.7
.

Consider the tree
T
and its open neighborhood ideal from Example
2.2.5
.
One can show that the following sets are the minimal TD-sets of
T
:

\displaystyle\left\{v_{4},v_{5},v_{6},v_{7}\right\}

\displaystyle\left\{v_{1},v_{4},v_{5},v_{7}\right\}

\displaystyle\left\{v_{2},v_{4},v_{5},v_{7}\right\}

\displaystyle\left\{v_{4},v_{5},v_{6},v_{3}\right\}.

Thus

is unmixed, and by Theorem
2.2.6
, we get

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}(T)

\displaystyle=\left\langle v_{4},v_{5},v_{1}v_{2}v_{6},v_{3}v_{7},v_{6}v_{5}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle v_{4},v_{5},v_{6},v_{7}\right\rangle\cap\left\langle v_{1},v_{4},v_{5},v_{7}\right\rangle\cap\left\langle v_{2},v_{4},v_{5},v_{7}\right\rangle\cap\left\langle v_{4},v_{5},v_{6},v_{3}\right\rangle.

On the other hand it is straightforward to show that the path
P_{4}
\ell_{1}
s_{1}
u
s_{2}
\ell_{2}

is mixed because

\mathcal{N}(P_{4})=\left\langle s_{1},\ell_{1}u,s_{1}s_{2},u\ell_{2},s_{2}\right\rangle=\left\langle s_{1},s_{2},\ell_{1},\ell_{2}\right\rangle\cap\left\langle s_{1},s_{2},u\right\rangle.

3.
Characterizations of unmixed trees

Our goal in this section is to give two characterizations of unmixed trees.
The first one is descriptive, allowing one to detect whether a given tree
T
is unmixed; see Theorem
3.4.9
, which is the main result of Subsection
3.4
.
Essentially,
T
has two noteworthy “interior” subgraphs,
T_{\mathcal{R}}
and
T_{\mathcal{B}}
, which are induced subforests that arise from a red-blue coloring of
T
, and such that
T
is unmixed if and only if
T_{\mathcal{R}}
and
T_{\mathcal{B}}
are unmixed; see Corollary
3.4.8
.

The connected components of
T_{\mathcal{R}}
and
T_{\mathcal{B}}
have a property that we call “balanced.”
The general properties of balanced trees are the focus of Subsection
3.2
, and their unmixedness is the topic of Subsection
3.3
, the main result of which is our descriptive characterization of them in Theorem
3.3.12
.

The second characterization of unmixed trees is constructive, allowing us to build unmixed trees.
It is the main result of Subsection
3.5
; see Theorem
3.5.6
.

3.1.
Domination selector

The goal of this subsection is to define the notion of a
domination selector
of
G
, which is a useful tool for demonstrating the minimality of a given set of vertices.
The main result of this subsection is Lemma
3.1.11
, which we use subsequently in several key places.
We begin with some fundamental graph-theoretic terminology.

Notation 3.1.1
.

Recall that a forest is a finite disjoint union of trees.
Let
x,y\in V
.
A
path
from
x
to
y
is a sequence of distinct vertices
(v_{0},\dots,v_{d})
where
d\in\mathbb{N}
,
v_{0}=x
,
v_{d}=y
, and
v_{i}v_{i+1}\in E
for all
i=0,\dots,d-1
.
The
length
of a path
(v_{0},\dots,v_{d})
is
d
also written as
d=:|(v_{0},\dots,v_{d})|
, and the
distance
from
x
to
y
is the length of the shortest path from
x
to
y
, denoted
\text{dist}(x,y)
.
In case the given graph is a tree (or a forest), denote the (shortest) path from
x
to
y
by
\mbox{path}(x,y)
.
If a vertex
z\in V
equals to one of the
v_{j}
in
\mbox{path}(x,y)
, we write
z\in\mbox{path}(x,y)
.

Definition 3.1.2
.

A vertex
v\in V
is
isolated
if
\deg(v)=0
, it is a
leaf
if
\deg(v)=1
, and it is a
support vertex
if it is adjacent to a leaf.
Every vertex adjacent to a support vertex is called a
supported vertex
.

Definition 3.1.3
.

Let
G
be a graph with at least one leaf.
The
height
of a vertex
v\in V
is given by

\mbox{height}(v):=\min\left\{\text{dist}(v,\ell)\mid\ell\mbox{ is a leaf in }G\right\}.

If
v
is an isolated vertex, then we set
\mbox{height}(v)=0
as a convention.
We denote

V_{k}=V_{k}(G):=\{v\in V\ |\ \mbox{height}(v)=k\}

and the
height
of
G
is the integer

\mbox{height}(G):=\max\left\{k\in\mathbb{N}\mid V_{k}\neq\emptyset\right\}.

For instance, every leaf of a graph has height 0, and every non-leaf support vertex has height 1.

Lemma 3.1.4
.

Let
G
be a graph with a support vertex
s
.
Then for any minimal TD-set
D
, we have
|D\cap N(s)\cap V_{0}(G)|\leq 1
.

Proof.

Assume not.
Then there are two vertices
x,y\in D\cap N(s)\cap V_{0}(G)
.
Then
N(D\setminus\{x\})=V(G)
as
s\in N(y)
, which contradicts the minimality of
D
.
∎

Lemma
3.1.5
is the first application of Lemma
3.1.4
.
It states that given a graph
G
, adding a leaf to a support vertex or deleting a leaf adjacent to a support vertex with more than one leaf adjacent does not change the mixedness of
G
.

Lemma 3.1.5
.

Let
G
be a connected graph with at least one leaf.
Let
G^{\prime}
be a graph obtained from
G
by attaching any number of leaves to its suppport vertices.
Then
G
is unmixed if and only if
G^{\prime}
is unmixed.

Proof.

Let
s\in V(G)
be a support vertex adjacent to a leaf
\ell\in V(G)
.
Let
H
be a graph with
V(H)=V(G)\cup\left\{\ell^{\prime}\right\}
and
E(H)=E(G)\cup\left\{s\ell^{\prime}\right\}
.
It suffices to prove the claim for
G
and
H
.

Let
S\subseteq V(G)
be a minimal TD-set of
G
.
Since
N_{G}(S)=V(G)\ni\ell
, we have
s\in S
as
s
is the only vertex adjacent to
\ell
.
Now consider
S\subseteq H
.
Since
s\in S
, we have
N_{H}(S)=V(H)
.
To show that
S
is a minimal TD-set of
H
, assume by way of contradiction that there is a proper subset
S^{\prime}\subsetneq S
with
N_{H}(S^{\prime})=V(H)
.
Then the set
S^{\prime}\subseteq V(G)
has
N_{G}(S^{\prime})=V(G)
, contradicting the minimality of
S
in
G
.
Hence
S
is a minimal TD-set of
H
.

Next, let
S\subseteq V(H)
be a minimal TD-set of
H
.
We show that there is a minimal TD-set of
G
of the same size as
S
.
If
\ell^{\prime}\not\in S
, then
S\subseteq V(G)
is also a minimal TD-set of
G
by the preceding paragraph.
So suppose that
\ell^{\prime}\in S
.
Then by Lemma
3.1.4
,
\ell^{\prime}
is the only leaf in
S
adjacent to
s
.
By the minimality of
S
, we have
N_{H}(S\setminus\left\{\ell^{\prime}\right\})=V(H)\setminus\left\{s\right\}
.
Hence the set
S^{\prime}:=(S\setminus\left\{\ell^{\prime}\right\})\cup\left\{\ell\right\}
is a minimal TD-set of
H
.
Since
S^{\prime}\subseteq V(G)
, the set
S^{\prime}\subseteq V(G)
has
N_{G}(S^{\prime})=V(G)
.
To show that
S^{\prime}
is minimal in
G
, asumme by way of contradiction that there is a proper subset
S^{\prime\prime}\subsetneq S^{\prime}
with
N_{G}(S^{\prime\prime})=V(G)
.
Then by the previous argument,
S^{\prime\prime}
is a minimal TD-set of
H
contained in
S^{\prime}
, contradicting the minimality of
S^{\prime}
in
H
.
Thus
S^{\prime}
is a minimal TD-set of
G
with
|S^{\prime}|=|S|
.

So far, we have shown that any minimal TD-set of
G
is a minimal TD-set of
H
, and for any minimal TD-set of
H
, there is a minimal TD-set of
G
of the same size.
This implies that
G
is unmixed if and only if
H
is unmixed.
∎

We next extend Definitions
2.1.2
and
2.2.3
to any subset of vertices.

Definition 3.1.6
.

A subset
D\subseteq V(G)
is
minimal
(with respect to open neighborhoods) if there is no proper subset
D^{\prime}\subsetneq D
, such that
N(D^{\prime})=N(D)
.

From now on, a set being minimal will always refer to Definition
3.1.6
.
The following lemma gives an equivalent definition of a minimal set.

Lemma 3.1.7
.

Let
D\subseteq V
. Then
D
is minimal if and only if for every vertex
v\in D
, there is a vertex
u\in N(D)
such that
N(u)\cap D=\{v\}
.

Proof.

First, suppose that
D
is minimal.
By way of contradiction, assume that there exists a vertex
c\in D
such that for all
x\in N(c)
we have
(N(x)\cap D)\setminus\{c\}\neq\emptyset
.
We claim that
N(D\setminus\left\{c\right\})=N(D)
, which contradicts the minimality of
D
.
The containment
N(D\setminus\left\{c\right\})\subseteq N(D)
follows from Definition
2.1.1
.
For the other containment, let
v\in N(D)
.
Then there exists
u\in D
such that
vu\in E
.
If
u\neq c
, then
u\in D\setminus\left\{c\right\}
, hence
v\in N(D\setminus\left\{c\right\})
.
So suppose that
u=c
.
Then we have
(N(v)\cap D)\setminus\left\{c\right\}\neq\emptyset
.
So, there exists
c^{\prime}\in D
with
vc^{\prime}\in E
and
c^{\prime}\neq c
.
Thus
v\in N(D\setminus\left\{c\right\})
.

Now suppose that for every vertex
v\in D
, there is a vertex
u\in N(D)
such that
N(u)\cap D=\{v\}
.
Assume for a contradiction that
D
is not minimal.
Hence there is
c\in D
such that
N(D\setminus\{c\})=N(D)
.
By hypothesis, there is some vertex
y\in N(D)
such that
N(y)\cap D=\{c\}
.
Hence

Lemma 3.1.11

Let

3.2.
Balanced trees, RD-, and BD-sets

In this subsection, we define balanced trees and two types of
S
-TD-sets which we then use to give our first characterization of unmixed trees in the main result of this subsection, Corollary
3.2.11
.

Assumptions
.

In this subsection, we assume that
T=(V,E)
is a tree with a 2-coloring
\chi:V\to\left\{\mathcal{R},\mathcal{B}\right\}
where
\mathcal{R}
and
\mathcal{B}
stands for red and blue color, respectively.

Definition 3.2.1
.

The tree
T
is
balanced
if no two vertices of the same height are adjacent.

Proposition
3.2.4
gives an equivalent definition of balanced trees.
We next give a lemma used in its proof.

Lemma 3.2.2
.

Let
T
be a balanced tree, and let
u,v\in V
be distinct vertices that are adjacent to each other.
Then
\mbox{height}(u)-\mbox{height}(v)=\pm 1
.

Proof.

Set
\mbox{height}(u)=h
and
\mbox{height}(v)=h^{\prime}
.
Since
T
is a balanced tree, we must have
h\neq h^{\prime}
as
uv\in E
.
Without loss of generality, assume that
h>h^{\prime}
.
We claim that
h^{\prime}=h-1
.
By way of contradiction, suppose that
h^{\prime}<h-1
.
Then there exists a leaf
v_{0}\in V_{0}
so that
\mbox{path}(v_{0},v)=(v_{0},\dots,v_{h^{\prime}})
with
v_{h^{\prime}}=v
.
Now consider
\mbox{path}(v_{0},u)=(v_{0},\dots,v_{h^{\prime}},u)
; the equality holds by the uniqueness of paths in trees.
Since we have

\displaystyle|\mbox{path}(v_{0},u)|=h^{\prime}+1<h

\mbox{height}(u)
is less than
h
, a contradiction.
∎

Corollary 3.2.3
.

Let
T
be a balanced tree and let
u,v\in V_{h}
for some
0\leq h\leq\mbox{height}(T)
.
Then
\text{dist}(u,v)
is even.

Proof.

If
u=v
, then
\text{dist}(u,v)=0
which is even.
So assume that
u\neq v
.
By way of contradiction, assume that
\text{dist}(u,v)=2k+1
for some
k\in\mathbb{N}
.
Then by inductively applying Lemma
3.2.2
on
k
, one can show that the parity of
\mbox{height}(u)
and
\mbox{height}(v)
are different, hence
\mbox{height}(u)\neq\mbox{height}(v)
, a contradiction.
∎

Proposition 3.2.4
.

The following conditions on a tree
T
are equivalent:

(1)

T
is balanced.

(2)

Any two vertices of the same height have the same color under
\chi
.

(3)

Every leaf has the same color.

Proof.

(1\Rightarrow 2)
Assume
T
is balanced, and let
u,v\in V_{h}
for some
0\leq h\leq\mbox{height}(T)
.
Then by Corollary
3.2.3
,
\text{dist}(u,v)
is even, hence
\chi(u)=\chi(v)
.

(2\Rightarrow 3)
Since all leaves have height 0, they have the same color.

(3\hskip-4.2679pt\Rightarrow\hskip-2.84526pt1)
Assume that every leaf in
T
has the same color.
Let
u,v\in V_{h}
for some
0\leq h\leq\mbox{height}(T)
.
If
h=0
, then
u
and
v
are leaves, so they have the same color by assumption, implying they are not adjacent.
If
h>0
, then there are leaves
u_{0},v_{0}\in V_{0}
so that
\mbox{path}(u_{0},u)=(u_{0},\dots,u_{h})
and
\mbox{path}(v_{0},v)=(v_{0},\dots,v_{h})
where

and
v_{h}=v
.
Since
u_{0}
and
v_{0}
have the same color,
u
and
v
must have the same color, implying that

.
∎

This contradicts (2).
Now suppose that
|V_{2}|<|V_{1}|
.
Then by (2) and the pigeonhole principle, there exists a vertex
c\in V_{2}
that is adjacent to at least two support vertices, contradicting (1).
Therefore, we get
|V_{2}|=|V_{1}|
.

The hypotheses (1)–(2) and the condition
|V_{2}|=|V_{1}|
implies that the set of edges

E(V_{1},V_{2}):=\left\{uv\in E\mid u\in V_{1},v\in V_{2}\right\}

forms a bijection (perfect matching) between
V_{1}
and
V_{2}
.
Hence for each
v\in V_{2}
, let
s(v)
be the unique support vertex adjacent to
v
; i.e.,
s(v)v\in E(V_{1},V_{2})
.

To show that
T
is unmixed, we use Corollary
3.2.11
.
First, we show that every minimal BD-set of
T
has the same size.
More specifically, we show that
V_{1}
is the only minimal BD-set of
T
.
Let
D
be a minimal BD-set of
T
.
Then
N(D)=V_{0}\cup V_{2}
.
Since
V_{0}\subset N(D)
and the support vertices are the only vertices that are adjacent to the leaves, we have
V_{1}\subseteq D
.
But, we also have
N(V_{1})=V_{0}\cap V_{2}
.
By mininmality of
D
, we have
V_{1}=D
.
Thus
V_{1}
is a minimal BD-set contained in any BD-set, showing that
V_{1}
is the only minimal BD-set in
T
.

Next, we show that every minimal RD-set has the same size.
First, notice that the set
V_{2}
is a minimal RD-set because it dominates
V_{1}\cup V_{3}
and each
s\in V_{1}
is dominated by a unique
x\in V_{2}
.
Let
D
be a minimal RD-set.
We show that
|D|=|V_{2}|
.
Since there are no vertices
c\in D\subseteq V_{0}\cup V_{2}
with
|N(c)\cap V_{1}|\geq 2
, we have
|D|\geq|V_{1}|
.
Next, we show that
|D|\leq|V_{1}|
.
Let
s\in V_{1}
.
Then there are vertices
l\in V_{0}
and
u\in V_{2}
such that
N(s)=\left\{l,u\right\}
by the assumptions on
T
.
Since
N(l)=\left\{s\right\}\subseteq N(u)
, the minimality of
D
implies that
|D\cap N(s)|\leq 1
.
Since
D\subseteq V_{0}\cup V_{2}=\bigcup_{t\in V_{1}}N(t)
where the union is disjoint, we get

|D|=\left|\bigcup_{t\in V_{1}}(D\cap N(t))\right|=\sum_{t\in V_{1}}|D\cap N(t)|\leq\sum_{t\in V_{1}}1=|V_{1}|\ .

This gives
|D|=|V_{1}|
hence every minimal RD-set of
T
has the same size.
∎

Now we present our descriptive characterization of unmixed balanced trees.

Theorem 3.3.12
.

Let

be a balanced tree.
Then

is unmixed if and only if

(1)

\mbox{height}(T)\leq 3
,

(2)

for all

,
|N(v)\cap V_{1}|=1
, and

(3)

for all

,
|N(v)\cap V_{2}|\leq 1

Proof.

(
\Rightarrow
) Suppose that

is unmixed.
Then Theorem
3.3.7
gives (1), Theorem
3.3.4
and Lemma
3.3.6
give (2), and Theorem
3.3.10
and gives (3).

(
\Leftarrow
) Suppose that (1), (2), and (3) hold.
By (2), we have
\mbox{height}(T)\neq 2
as height 2 vertices cannot be leaves.
Hence by (1), we have
\mbox{height}(T)=0,1
, or

.
If
\mbox{height}(T)=0

is a graph with a single vertex, which is vacuously unmixed.
If

is a graph with a single support vertex

with at least 2 leaves attached (such graphs are called star graphs, see Figure

If

is unmixed.
∎

Theorem
3.3.12
and the proof of Theorem

show that if

is an unmixed balanced tree with

Lemma 3.4.5

3.5.
Constructing unmixed balanced trees

The main result of this subsection, Theorem
3.5.6
, gives a constructive characterization of unmixed balanced trees.

Assumptions
.

Set
\mathbb{N}_{0}:=\mathbb{N}\cup\left\{0\right\}
.
For
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}

[n]:=\left\{1,2,\dots,n\right\}
and
[n]_{0}:=\left\{0,1,\dots,n\right\}

For every
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}

P_{n}
to be the path graph with
V(P_{n})=[n]_{0}
and
E(P_{n})=\left\{(0,1),(1,2),\dots,(n-1,n)\right\}
.

We apply the following operation to balanced trees below.

Definition 3.5.1
.

For two graphs
G_{1}=(V^{\prime},E^{\prime})
and
G_{2}=(V^{\prime\prime},E^{\prime\prime})
with vertices
v^{\prime}\in V^{\prime}
and
v^{\prime\prime}\in V^{\prime\prime}
, we set
e:=v^{\prime}v^{\prime\prime}
and define the
edge join
of
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
by
e
to be the graph

G_{1}+_{e}G_{2}:=(V^{\prime}\cup V^{\prime\prime},E^{\prime}\cup E^{\prime\prime}\cup\left\{v^{\prime}v^{\prime\prime}\right\}).

We define an operation
\mathcal{O}
as follows:
Let
T=(V,E)
be a tree, and let
v\in\bigcup_{i=1}^{3}V_{i}
.

(1)

If
\mbox{height}(v)=1
, then

\mathcal{O}(T,v):=T+_{(v,0)}P_{0}.

(2)

If
\mbox{height}(v)=2
, then

\mathcal{O}(T,v):=T+_{(v,3)}P_{3}.

(3)

If
\mbox{height}(v)=3
, then

\mathcal{O}(T,v):=T+_{(v,2)}P_{2}.

In other words, the operator
\mathcal{O}
adds a “whisker” of length 1, 4, or 3 to

, depending on
\mbox{height}(v)
.
In particular
\mathcal{O}(T,v)
is a tree, so for
v_{1}\in\bigcup_{i=1}^{3}V_{i}
after appropriate relabeling so that
V(\mathcal{O}(T,v_{1}))\cap\mathbb{N}_{0}=\emptyset
, let
v_{2}\in\bigcup_{i=1}^{3}V_{i}(\mathcal{O}(T,v_{1}))
, and set

\mathcal{O}(T,(v_{1},v_{2})):=\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{O}(T,v_{1}),v_{2}).

Inductively, given
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
v_{j}\in\bigcup_{i=1}^{3}V_{i}\left(\mathcal{O}(T,(v_{1},\dots,v_{j-1}))\right)
for
j\in[n]
, we set

\mathcal{O}(T,(v_{1},\dots,v_{n})):=\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{O}(T,v_{1},\dots,v_{n-1}),v_{n}).

T
balanced and

Our goal in this section is to prove that the Stanley-Reisner complex

4.1.

In this subsection, we define the stable complexes of graphs and the even-stable complexes of balanced trees; see Definitions

4.1.7
.
We then show that the stable complexes of trees are the join of the even-stable complexes of its interior graphs; see Theorem
4.1.9
.
To start, we review some basic notions of simplicial complexes.

Let
V
be a finite set.
We say
\Delta\subseteq 2^{V}
(where
2^{V}
denotes the power set of
V
) is a
simplicial complex
if
F\in\Delta
and
G\subset F
implies
G\in\Delta
.
Elements in
\Delta
are called
faces
, and the maximal elements (under inclusion) in
\Delta
are called
facets
.
We denote the set of all facets of
\Delta
by
\mathcal{F}(\Delta)
.
If
\mathcal{F}(\Delta)=\left\{F_{1},\dots,F_{m}\right\}
, then we also write
\Delta=\left\langle F_{1},\dots,F_{m}\right\rangle
.
In this case, we say
\Delta
is
generated
by
F_{1},\dots,F_{m}
.
The
dimension
of a face
F\in\Delta
is
\dim(F):=|F|-1
, and the
dimension
of
\Delta
is
\dim(\Delta):=\max\left\{\dim(F)\mid F\in\Delta\right\}
.
We say
\Delta
is
pure
if every facet in
\Delta
has the same dimension.

Definition 4.1.1
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a graph.
A set
V^{\prime}\subseteq V
is
stable
if
V\setminus V^{\prime}
is a TD-set.
We say
V^{\prime}
is
maximal
if
V\setminus V^{\prime}
is minimal.
The
stable complex
of
G
is the simplicial complex on
V
generated by the maximal stable sets of
G
:

\mathcal{S}(G):=\left\langle F\subseteq V\mid F\text{ is a maximal stable set}\right\rangle=\left\langle F\subseteq V\mid V\setminus F\text{ is a minimal TD-set}\right\rangle.

Stable sets and their properties appear in the transversal theory of hypergraphs; see
[
2
]
for more details.

Let
\Delta
be a simplicial complex on
V=\left\{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\right\}
.
The
Stanley-Reisner ideal
associated with
\Delta
is the square-free monomial ideal in
R:=\Bbbk[V]

I_{\Delta}:=\left\langle X_{F}\mid F\not\in\Delta\right\rangle.

On the other hand, let
I\subseteq R
be a square-free monomial ideal.
Then the
Stanley-Reisner (simplicial) complex
associated to
I
is the simplicial complex on
V

\Delta_{I}:=\left\{F\subseteq V\mid X_{F}\not\in I\right\}

where we assume the product of elements in the empty set to be
1\in R
.
The following fact is a basic property of stable complexes.
Its proof is a routine application of Theorem
2.2.6
.

Fact 4.1.2
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a graph.
Then
I_{\mathcal{S}(G)}=\mathcal{N}(G)
and
\Delta_{\mathcal{N}(G)}=\mathcal{S}(G)
.

The following definition and fact are standard results in the theory of simplicial complexes.

Definition 4.1.3
.

Let
\Delta^{\prime}
and
\Delta^{\prime\prime}
be simplicial complexes on
V^{\prime}
and
V^{\prime\prime}
, respectively, and assume that
V^{\prime}\cap V^{\prime\prime}=\emptyset
.
The
join
of
\Delta^{\prime}
and
\Delta^{\prime\prime}
is the simplicial complex on
V^{\prime}\cup V^{\prime\prime}
:

\Delta^{\prime}*\Delta^{\prime\prime}:=\left\{F^{\prime}\cup F^{\prime\prime}\mid F^{\prime}\in\Delta^{\prime},F^{\prime\prime}\in\Delta^{\prime\prime}\right\}.

Fact 4.1.4

(
[
3
]
)
.

Let
\Delta^{\prime}
and
\Delta^{\prime\prime}
be simplicial complexes as in Definition
4.1.3
.
Then
\Delta^{\prime}*\Delta^{\prime\prime}
is shellable if and only if
\Delta^{\prime}
and
\Delta^{\prime\prime}
are shellable.

Hence, by Fact
4.1.4
, if the stable complex
\mathcal{S}(T)
of an unmixed tree
T
can be expressed as the join of two simplicial complexes,
\Delta^{\prime}*\Delta^{\prime\prime}
, we only need to show that
\Delta^{\prime}
and
\Delta^{\prime\prime}
are shellable to prove that
\mathcal{S}(T)
is shellable.

Definition 4.1.5
.

Let
T=(V,E)
be a balanced tree.
We denote the set of
even height
vertices in
T
by

V_{even}(T):=\left\{v\in V\mid\mbox{height}(v)\mbox{ is even}\right\}.

Similarly, we denote the set of
odd height
vertices in
T
by

V_{odd}(T):=\left\{v\in V\mid\mbox{height}(v)\mbox{ is odd}\right\}.

For
D\subseteq V
, we say
D
is an
odd-TD-set
if
D
is an
V_{odd}
-TD-set in
T
; see Definition
2.1.2
.

The following fact is a consequence of Definition
4.1.5
and the properties of the interior graphs.

Fact 4.1.6
.

Let
T=(V,E)
be a tree and let
T_{\mathcal{B}}
and
T_{\mathcal{R}}
be its interior graphs.
Then

V_{even}(T_{\mathcal{B}})=V_{\mathcal{B}}(T_{\mathcal{B}})\mbox{ and }V_{odd}(T_{\mathcal{B}})=V_{\mathcal{R}}(T_{\mathcal{B}}).

Similarly, we have

V_{even}(T_{\mathcal{R}})=V_{\mathcal{R}}(T_{\mathcal{R}})\mbox{ and }V_{odd}(T_{\mathcal{R}})=V_{\mathcal{B}}(T_{\mathcal{R}}).

Hence odd-TD-sets in
T_{\mathcal{B}}
and
T_{\mathcal{R}}
correspond to the RD-sets and BD-sets (from Subsection
3.2
) in
T_{\mathcal{R}}
and
T_{\mathcal{B}}
, respectively.

Definition 4.1.7
.

Let
T=(V,E)
be a balanced tree.
The
even-stable complex
of
T
is the simplicial complex on
V_{even}(T)
:

\mathcal{S}_{even}(T):=\left\langle V_{even}(T)\setminus D\mid D\text{ is a minimal odd-TD-set}\right\rangle.

The following lemma is used to prove the main theorem of this subsection.

Lemma 4.1.8
.

Let
T
be a tree with a 2-coloring
\chi
. Then
V(T)
can be partitioned as

V(T)=V_{even}(T_{\mathcal{B}})\cup V_{even}(T_{\mathcal{R}})\cup V_{1}(T).

Proof.

We will show that
V(T)
is partitioned by
V_{even}(T_{\mathcal{B}})
,
V_{even}(T_{\mathcal{R}})
, and
V_{1}(T)
.
The containment
V(T)\supseteq V_{even}(T_{\mathcal{B}})\cup V_{even}(T_{\mathcal{R}})\cup V_{1}(T)
is clear by definition, so we only need to show the other containment.
Let
v\in V(T)
.
By symmetry, assume that
v
is blue.
We consider two cases:
v\in V(T_{\mathcal{B}})
or
v\not\in V(T_{\mathcal{B}})
.
First, suppose that
v\in V(T_{\mathcal{B}})
.
Then by Corollary
3.2.6
and the construction of
V_{\mathcal{B}}
(every leaf is blue color),
v\in V_{even}(T_{\mathcal{B}})
.
Next, suppose that
v\not\in V(T_{\mathcal{B}})
.
Then by the construction of
T_{\mathcal{B}}
,
v
must be a blue support vertex in
T
since the blue vertices in
T
that are not in
T_{\mathcal{B}}
are the blue support vertices of
T
.
Thus
v\in V_{1}(T)
.
Thus we get
V(T)\subseteq V_{even}(T_{\mathcal{B}})\cup V_{even}(T_{\mathcal{R}})\cup V_{1}(T)
.

Next, we show that the sets
V_{even}(T_{\mathcal{B}})
,
V_{even}(T_{\mathcal{R}})
, and
V_{1}(T)
are pairwise disjoint.
By Corollary
3.2.6
, vertices in
V_{even}(T_{\mathcal{B}})
are blue and vertices in
V_{even}(T_{\mathcal{R}})
are red, giving us
V_{even}(T_{\mathcal{B}})\cap V_{even}(T_{\mathcal{R}})=\emptyset
.
By symmetry, it suffices to show that
V_{1}(T)\cap V_{even}(T_{\mathcal{B}})=\emptyset
.
Let
v\in V_{1}(T)
.
If
\chi(v)=\mathcal{B}
, then
v\not\in V(T_{\mathcal{B}})
by the construction of
T_{\mathcal{B}}
, and thus
v\not\in V_{even}(T_{\mathcal{B}})
.
If
\chi(v)=\mathcal{R}
, then
v\not\in V_{even}(T_{\mathcal{B}})
since vertices in
V_{even}(T_{\mathcal{B}})
are blue.
∎

Theorem 4.1.9
.

Let
T=(V,E)
be a tree.
Then

\mathcal{S}(T)=\mathcal{S}_{even}(T_{\mathcal{B}})*\mathcal{S}_{even}(T_{\mathcal{R}})
.

Proof.

We will only show that
\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{S}(T))\subseteq\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{S}_{even}(T_{\mathcal{B}})*\mathcal{S}_{even}(T_{\mathcal{R}}))
(the reverse inclusion is proved similarly).
Let
F\in\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{S}(T))
be a facet.
Then there exists a minimal TD-set
D\subseteq V(T)
such that
F=V(T)\setminus D
.
By Fact
3.2.9
, there exist an RD-set
D^{\prime}\subseteq V_{\mathcal{B}}(T)
and a BD-set
D^{\prime\prime}\subseteq V_{\mathcal{R}}(T)
in
T
such that
D=D^{\prime}\cup D^{\prime\prime}
.
Then by Lemma
3.4.5
and Fact
4.1.6
, there are minimal odd-TD-sets
D_{1}\subseteq V_{even}(T_{\mathcal{B}})
in
T_{\mathcal{B}}
and
D_{2}\subseteq V_{even}(T_{\mathcal{R}})
in
T_{\mathcal{R}}
such that

D^{\prime}=V_{1,\mathcal{B}}\cup D_{1}\text{ and }D^{\prime\prime}=V_{1,\mathcal{R}}\cup D_{2}

where
V_{1,C}=V_{1}(T)\cap V_{C}(T)
for all
C\in\left\{\mathcal{R},\mathcal{B}\right\}
.
Also, we have
V_{1}(T)=V_{1,\mathcal{B}}\cup V_{1,\mathcal{R}}
.
Thus

\displaystyle F

\displaystyle=V(T)\setminus D

\displaystyle=V(T)\setminus(D^{\prime}\cup D^{\prime\prime})

\displaystyle=V(T)\setminus\left((V_{1,\mathcal{B}}\cup D_{1})\cup(V_{1,\mathcal{R}}\cup D_{2})\right)

(Lemma
4.1.8
)

\displaystyle=\left(V_{even}(T_{\mathcal{B}})\cup V_{even}(T_{\mathcal{R}})\right)\setminus(D_{1}\cup D_{2})

\displaystyle=(V_{even}(T_{\mathcal{B}})\setminus D_{1})\cup(V_{even}(T_{\mathcal{R}})\setminus D_{2}).

By Definition
4.1.7
, we have
V_{even}(T_{\mathcal{B}})\setminus D_{1}\in\mathcal{S}_{even}(T_{\mathcal{B}})
and
V_{even}(T_{\mathcal{R}})\setminus D_{2}\in\mathcal{S}_{even}(T_{\mathcal{R}})
.
∎

4.2.
Shellability of even stable complexes

In this subsection, we will prove that the even-stable complexes of unmixed balanced trees are shellable; see Theorem
4.2.12
.

Definition 4.2.1

(
[
4
, Definition 5.1.11]
)
.

Let
\Delta
be a pure simplicial complex.
An ordering
F_{1},\dots,F_{m}
of facets of
\Delta
is a
shelling
if it satisfies one of the following equivalent conditions:

(i)

For all
i
such that
1<i\leq m
, the simplicial complex
\left\langle F_{1},\dots,F_{i-1}\right\rangle\cap\left\langle F_{i}\right\rangle
is pure of dimension
\dim(\Delta)-1
.

(ii)

for all
i,j
such that
1\leq i<j\leq m
, there exist a vertex
v\in F_{j}\setminus F_{i}
and an index
k\in[j-1]
satisfying
F_{j}\setminus F_{k}=\left\{v\right\}
.

We say
\Delta
is
shellable
if
\Delta
has a shelling order.

The following equivalent definition of shellability can be proven using Definition
4.2.1
.

Fact 4.2.2
.

Let
\Delta
be a pure simplicial complex. Then
\Delta
is shellable if and only if there is an ordering of facets
F_{1},\dots,F_{m}
such that for all
1\leq i<j\leq m
, if
|F_{i}\cap F_{j}|<\dim(\Delta)
, then there exists
1\leq k<j
such that
F_{i}\cap F_{j}\subseteq F_{k}\cap F_{j}
and
|F_{k}\cap F_{j}|=\dim(\Delta)
.

Throughout, we will use Fact
4.2.2
to show that a simplicial complex is shellable.
We also use the following well-known result about shellable simplicial complexes; see
[
14
,
20
]
.

Fact 4.2.3
.

Let
|V|=n
and
1\leq k\leq n
.
Then the simplicial complex
\left\langle S\subseteq V\mid|S|=k\right\rangle
is shellable.

We first consider balanced trees of heights 0 and 1.

Lemma 4.2.4
.

Let
T
be a balanced tree with
\mbox{height}(T)\leq 1
.
Then
\mathcal{S}_{even}(T)
is shellable.

Proof.

The proof of Theorem
3.3.12
implies that
T
is unmixed, and hence
\mathcal{S}_{even}(T)
is pure.
If
\mbox{height}(T)=0
, then
T
is a graph with a single vertex, say
v
.
Hence
\emptyset
is its only TD-set by assumption, so
\mathcal{S}_{even}(T)=\left\langle\left\{v\right\}\right\rangle
, which is shellable.
Suppose that
\mbox{height}(T)=1
.
Then
T
is a tree with a unique support vertex
s
and leaves
\left\{l_{1},\dots,l_{k}\right\}
for some
k>1
.
In particular, every minimal odd-TD-set of
T
is of the form
\left\{l_{i}\right\}
.
Thus we get

\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{S}_{even}(T))=\left\{V_{0}\setminus\left\{l_{i}\right\}\mid 1\leq i\leq k\right\}=\left\{V^{\prime}\subseteq V_{0}\mid|V^{\prime}|=k-1\right\}.

By the last equality above,
\mathcal{S}_{even}(T)
is shellable by Fact
4.2.3
.
∎

Next, we consider unmixed balanced trees of height 3.
We use the following convention for the labeling of vertices in unmixed balanced trees of height 3.

Assumptions
.

Let

,

Let

Corollary 4.2.7
.

Let

Then every minimal TD-set of

and a minimal odd TD-set of

Definition 4.2.8
.

Let
T
be an unmixed balanced tree of height 3.
Define

P_{T}:=\left\{(a_{1},\dots,a_{p})\mid\text{for all }i\in[p],1\leq a_{i}\leq k_{i}\right\}

and

U_{T}:=\left\{V_{even}\setminus\left\{u_{1,a_{1}},\dots,u_{p,a_{p}}\right\}\mid 1\leq a_{i}\leq k_{i}\right\}.

Define a bijection
\nu_{T}:U_{T}\to P_{T}
by

\nu_{T}(V_{even}\setminus\left\{u_{1,a_{1}},\dots,u_{p,a_{p}}\right\})=(a_{1},\dots,a_{p}).

For
(a_{1},\dots,a_{p})\in P_{T}
, define

\ell(a_{1},\dots,a_{p})=|\left\{i\mid a_{i}=1,1\leq i\leq p\right\}|.

Define a total order
<_{P}
on
P_{T}
as follows:
for
\alpha,\beta\in P_{T}
,
\alpha<_{P}\beta

if and only if

(1)

\ell(\alpha)>\ell(\beta)
, or

(2)

\ell(\alpha)=\ell(\beta)
and
\alpha<_{lex}\beta

is the lexicographic order;
\alpha<_{lex}\beta
if and only if
a_{1}<b_{1}
or there is an index

such that
a_{j}=b_{j}
for all

and

.

on

as follows: for all

Lemma 4.2.10
.

Let
T
be an unmixed balanced tree of height 3.
Let
F_{1},F_{2}\in\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{S}_{even}(T))
with
\nu_{T}(F_{1})=(a_{1},\dots,a_{p})
and
\nu_{T}(F_{2})=(b_{1},\dots,b_{p})
.
Then

|F_{1}\cap F_{2}|=\dim(\mathcal{S}_{even}(T))+1-|\left\{i\mid a_{i}\neq b_{i},1\leq i\leq p\right\}|.

Proof.

Set
I_{0}:=\left\{i\mid a_{i}=b_{i},1\leq i\leq p\right\}
and
I_{1}:=\left\{i\mid a_{i}\neq b_{i},1\leq i\leq p\right\}
.
Also, write
D_{i}=V_{even}\setminus F_{i}
for
i=1,2
.
Then by definition of
\nu_{T}
, we get

D_{1}=\left\{u_{1,a_{1}},\dots,u_{p,a_{p}}\right\}\ \ \ \text{ and }D_{2}=\left\{u_{1,b_{1}},\dots,u_{p,b_{p}}\right\}.

Thus
|D_{1}\cap D_{2}|=|I_{0}|
, and
|D_{2}|-|I_{0}|=|I_{1}|
.
Hence we get

\displaystyle|F_{1}\cap F_{2}|

\displaystyle=|(V_{even}\setminus D_{1})\cap(V_{even}\setminus D_{2})|

\displaystyle=|V_{even}\setminus(D_{1}\cup D_{2})|

(
D_{1}\cup D_{2}\subseteq V_{even}
)

\displaystyle=|V_{even}|-|D_{1}\cup D_{2}|

\displaystyle=|V_{even}|-(|D_{1}|+|D_{2}|-|D_{1}\cap D_{2}|)

\displaystyle=|V_{even}|-|D_{1}|-|D_{2}|+|I_{0}|

(
|F_{1}|=|V_{even}|-|D_{1}|
)

\displaystyle=|F_{1}|-|D_{2}|+|I_{0}|

(
|D_{2}|-|I_{0}|=|I_{1}|
)

\displaystyle=|F_{1}|-|I_{1}|

\displaystyle=\dim(\mathcal{S}_{even}(T))+1-|\left\{i\mid a_{i}\neq b_{i},1\leq i\leq p\right\}|.

∎

In particular, for two facets
F_{1},F_{2}\in\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{S}_{even}(T))
,
|F_{1}\cap F_{2}|=\dim(\mathcal{S}_{even}(T))
if and only if
\nu_{T}(F_{1})
and
\nu_{T}(F_{2})
differ by exactly one entry.

Lemma 4.2.11
.

Let
T
be an unmixed balanced tree of height 3, and let

D=\left\{u_{1,a_{1}},\dots,u_{p,a_{p}}\right\}\subseteq V

be a minimal odd-TD-set in
T
.
Suppose that there exists some index
i
such that
a_{i}\neq 1
.
Then for any
c\in[k_{i}]
, the set

D^{\prime}=(D\setminus\left\{u_{i,a_{i}}\right\})\cup\left\{u_{i,c}\right\}

is also a minimal odd-TD-set.

Proof.

First, suppose that
c\neq 1
.
Then
u_{i,c}
is a leaf adjacent to
s_{i}
.
So,
D^{\prime}
is obtained by replacing the leaf
u_{i,a_{i}}
by another leaf
u_{i,c}
that is adjacent to the same support vertex
s_{i}
.
Thus
D^{\prime}
is also a minimal odd-TD-set.
Next, suppose that
c=1
.
Since
D
is a minimal odd-TD-set, we get
N(s_{i})\cap D=\left\{u_{i,a_{i}}\right\}
by Lemma
4.2.6
.
Hence we have
N(D\setminus\left\{u_{i,a_{i}}\right\})=V_{odd}\setminus\left\{s_{i}\right\}
.
Since
N(u_{i,1})\supseteq\left\{s_{i}\right\}
, we get

N(D^{\prime})=N(D\setminus\left\{u_{i,a_{i}}\right\})\cup N(u_{i,1})=V_{odd}.

To show that
D^{\prime}
is minimal, consider the function
\mathcal{D^{\prime}}:D^{\prime}\to N(D^{\prime})
given by

\mathcal{D^{\prime}}(v)=\begin{cases}s_{j}&v=u_{j,a_{j}}\text{ where }j\neq i\\
s_{i}&v=u_{i,1}\end{cases}.

Then Lemma
4.2.6
implies that
\mathcal{D^{\prime}}
is a domination selector, therefore
D^{\prime}
is minimal.
∎

We prove the main result of this subsection.

Theorem 4.2.12
.

Let
T
be an unmixed balanced tree of height 3.
Then the total order
<_{T}
on
U_{T}
restricted to
\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{S}_{even}(T))
is a shelling of
\mathcal{S}_{even}(T)
.

Proof.

Since
\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{S}_{even}(T))\subseteq U_{T}
by Lemma
4.2.6
,
<_{T}
is a total order on
\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{S}_{even}(T))
.
Now index the facets in
\mathcal{S}_{even}(T)
under
<_{T}
and write

\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{S}_{even}(T))=\left\{F_{1},F_{2},\dots,F_{m}\right\}.

To show that
<_{T}
is a shelling, suppose that there exist indicies
i,j
with
1\leq i<j\leq m
such that
|F_{i}\cap F_{j}|<\dim(\mathcal{S}_{even}(T))
.
We need to find a facet

Corollary 4.2.14

Remark 4.2.17
.

Let
T
be a tree.
The
suspension
of
T
is a tree
\Sigma T
obtained by adding a leaf to each vertex of
T
.
It is well known that the edge ideal of
\Sigma T
,
\mathcal{I}(\Sigma T)
, is Cohen-Macaulay
[
18
]
.
A
complete edge subdivision
of a graph

is a graph
\mathcal{E}(G)
obtained by inserting a new vertex on each edge in

.
It is not hard to see that

is either a

or a height 3 unmixed balanced tree.
Moreover, we have

Hence every Cohen-Macaulay edge ideal of trees arises as an odd-open neighborhood ideal of an unmixed balanced tree.

5.
Cohen-Macaulay Type of unmixed trees

In this section, we give a graph theoretic way to compute the Cohen-Macaulay type of open neighborhood ideal of unmixed trees.

5.1.
Cohen-Macaulay type of balanced trees

In this subsection, we compute the type of
\mathcal{N}_{V_{\text{odd}}}(T)
where
T
is an unmixed balanced tree.

Assumptions
.

In this subsection, we assume that
T=(V,E)
is an unmixed balanced tree, and set
R=\Bbbk[V_{\text{even}}]
and
(V_{\text{even}})R=:\mathfrak{X}
, unless otherwise stated.
Also, we write
\mathcal{N}_{\text{odd}}(T):=\mathcal{N}_{V_{\text{odd}}}(T)
.

To compute
\mbox{type}(R/\mathcal{N}_{\text{odd}}(T))
, we first identify a maximal
(R/\mathcal{N}_{\text{odd}}(T))
-regular sequence.
First, we consider unmixed balanced trees of height 0 and 1.

Lemma 5.1.1
.

Suppose that
\mbox{height}(T)\leq 1
.
Let
Z\subseteq R
be defined as followings:

(1)

If
\mbox{height}(T)=0
, set
V=\left\{v\right\}
and
Z:=\left\{v\right\}
;

(2)

If
\mbox{height}(T)=1
, set
V_{1}=\left\{s\right\}
,
V_{0}=\left\{\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{n_{0}}\right\}
with

Then
Z
is a maximal
R/\mathcal{N}_{\text{odd}}(T)
-regular sequence in
\mathfrak{X}
.

Proof.

(1) If
\mbox{height}(T)=0
, then
T
is a graph with a single vertex, and we have
R=\Bbbk[v]
and
\mathcal{N}_{\text{odd}}(T)=0
.
Thus
R/\mathcal{N}_{\text{odd}}(T)\cong R=\Bbbk[v]
and
\left\{v\right\}=Z
is a maximum regular sequence for
R
.

(2) Now suppose that
\mbox{height}(T)=1
.
We have
R=\Bbbk[V_{\text{even}}]=\Bbbk[\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{n_{0}}]
and
\mathcal{N}_{\text{odd}}(T)=\left\langle X_{N(s)}\right\rangle=\left\langle\ell_{1}\cdots\ell_{n_{0}}\right\rangle
.
Since the m-irreducible decomposition of
\mathcal{N}_{\text{odd}}(T)
is

\mathcal{N}_{\text{odd}}(T)=\left\langle\ell_{1}\cdots\ell_{n_{0}}\right\rangle=\bigcap_{i=1}^{n_{0}}\left\langle\ell_{i}\right\rangle,

we have
\dim(R/\mathcal{N}_{\text{odd}}(T))=n_{0}-1
.
Then we have

\displaystyle\frac{R}{\mathcal{N}_{\text{odd}}(T)+\left\langle Z\right\rangle}=\frac{\Bbbk[\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{n_{0}}]}{\left\langle\ell_{1}\cdots\ell_{n_{0}}\right\rangle+\left\langle\left\{\ell_{1}-\ell_{j}\mid 2\leq j\leq n_{0}\right\}\right\rangle}\cong\frac{\Bbbk[\ell_{1}]}{\left\langle\ell_{1}^{n_{0}}\right\rangle}.

Since the ideal
\left\langle\ell_{1}^{n_{0}}\right\rangle
is an m-irreducible decomposition, we get that
\dim(\Bbbk[\ell_{1}]/\left\langle\ell_{1}^{n_{0}}\right\rangle)=1-1=0
.
Since
R/\mathcal{N}_{\text{odd}}(T)
is Cohen-Macaulay and
|Z|=n_{0}-1
,
the elements in
Z
form a maximal
(R/\mathcal{N}_{\text{odd}}(T))
-regular sequence.
∎

if and only if

for some
D_{\mathcal{B}}\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{B}}
and
D_{\mathcal{R}}\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{R}}
; note that the sets
D_{\mathcal{B}}
,
D_{\mathcal{R}}
, and
V_{1}(T)
are pairwise disjoint by Lemma
4.1.8
since
D_{\mathcal{B}}\subseteq V_{\text{even}}(T_{\mathcal{B}})
and
D_{\mathcal{R}}\subseteq V_{\text{even}}(T_{\mathcal{R}})
.
Fix
D\in\mathcal{D}
,
D_{\mathcal{B}}\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{B}}
, and
D_{\mathcal{R}}\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{R}}
such that
D=D_{\mathcal{B}}\cup D_{\mathcal{R}}\cup V_{1}(T)
.
Then we have

\displaystyle\dim\left(\frac{R}{\mathcal{N}(T)}\right)

\displaystyle=|V(T)|-|D|

(Lemma
4.1.8
)

\displaystyle=|V_{\text{even}}(T_{\mathcal{B}})\cup V_{\text{even}}(T_{\mathcal{R}})\cup V_{1}(T)|-|D|

\displaystyle=|V_{\text{even}}(T_{\mathcal{B}})\cup V_{\text{even}}(T_{\mathcal{R}})\cup V_{1}(T)|-|D_{\mathcal{B}}\cup D_{\mathcal{R}}\cup V_{1}(T)|

\displaystyle=|V_{\text{even}}(T_{\mathcal{B}})|+|V_{\text{even}}(T_{\mathcal{R}})|-|D_{\mathcal{B}}|-|D_{\mathcal{R}}|

\displaystyle=\dim\left(\frac{R_{\mathcal{B}}}{\mathcal{N}_{\text{odd}}(T_{\mathcal{B}})}\right)+\dim\left(\frac{R_{\mathcal{R}}}{\mathcal{N}_{\text{odd}}(T_{\mathcal{R}})}\right)

(Corollary
5.2.4
)

\displaystyle=0pt\left(\frac{R_{\mathcal{B}}}{\mathcal{N}_{\text{odd}}(T_{\mathcal{B}})}\right)+0pt\left(\frac{R_{\mathcal{R}}}{\mathcal{N}_{\text{odd}}(T_{\mathcal{R}})}\right)

\displaystyle=|Z_{\mathcal{B}}|+|Z_{\mathcal{R}}|

\displaystyle=|Z|.

Thus
Z
is a maximal
R/\mathcal{N}(T)
-regular sequence.
Writing
V_{\mathcal{B}}:=V_{\text{even}}(T_{\mathcal{B}})
and
V_{\mathcal{R}}:=V_{\text{even}}(T_{\mathcal{R}})
, we get

\displaystyle\mbox{type}\left(R/\mathcal{N}(T)\right)\hskip-78.24507pt

\displaystyle=\mbox{type}\left(\frac{R}{\mathcal{N}(T)+\left\langle Z\right\rangle}\right)

\displaystyle=\mbox{type}\left(\frac{\Bbbk[V_{\mathcal{B}}]}{\mathcal{N}_{\text{odd}}(T_{\mathcal{B}})+\left\langle Z_{\mathcal{B}}\right\rangle}\otimes_{\Bbbk}\frac{\Bbbk[V_{\mathcal{R}}]}{\mathcal{N}_{\text{odd}}(T_{\mathcal{R}})+\left\langle Z_{\mathcal{R}}\right\rangle}\right)

\displaystyle=\dim_{\Bbbk}\left(\mbox{Ext}_{\Bbbk[V_{\mathcal{B}}\cup V_{\mathcal{R}}]}^{0}\left(\Bbbk,\frac{\Bbbk[V_{\mathcal{B}}]}{\mathcal{N}_{\text{odd}}(T_{\mathcal{B}})}\otimes_{\Bbbk}\frac{\Bbbk[V_{\mathcal{R}}]}{\mathcal{N}_{\text{odd}}(T_{\mathcal{R}})}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\dim_{\Bbbk}\left(\mbox{Ext}_{\Bbbk[V_{\mathcal{B}}]}^{0}\left(\Bbbk,\frac{\Bbbk[V_{\mathcal{B}}]}{\mathcal{N}_{\text{odd}}(T_{\mathcal{B}})+\left\langle Z_{\mathcal{B}}\right\rangle}\right)\otimes_{\Bbbk}\ \mbox{Ext}_{\Bbbk[V_{\mathcal{R}}]}^{0}\left(\Bbbk,\frac{\Bbbk[V_{\mathcal{R}}]}{\mathcal{N}_{\text{odd}}(T_{\mathcal{R}})+\left\langle Z_{\mathcal{R}}\right\rangle}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\dim_{\Bbbk}\left(\mbox{Ext}_{\Bbbk[V_{\mathcal{B}}]}^{0}\left(\Bbbk,\frac{\Bbbk[V_{\mathcal{B}}]}{\mathcal{N}_{\text{odd}}(T_{\mathcal{B}})+\left\langle Z_{\mathcal{B}}\right\rangle}\right)\right)\cdot\dim_{\Bbbk}\left(\mbox{Ext}_{\Bbbk[V_{\mathcal{R}}]}^{0}\left(\Bbbk,\frac{\Bbbk[V_{\mathcal{R}}]}{\mathcal{N}_{\text{odd}}(T_{\mathcal{R}})+\left\langle Z_{\mathcal{R}}\right\rangle}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\mbox{type}\left(\frac{\Bbbk[V_{\mathcal{B}}]}{\mathcal{N}_{\text{odd}}(T_{\mathcal{B}})+\left\langle Z_{\mathcal{B}}\right\rangle}\right)\cdot\mbox{type}\left(\frac{\Bbbk[V_{\mathcal{R}}]}{\mathcal{N}_{\text{odd}}(T_{\mathcal{R}})+\left\langle Z_{\mathcal{R}}\right\rangle}\right)

\displaystyle=\mbox{type}(R/\mathcal{N}_{\text{odd}}(T_{\mathcal{B}}))\cdot\mbox{type}(R/\mathcal{N}_{\text{odd}}(T_{\mathcal{R}}))

(Theorem
5.2.5
)

\displaystyle=m_{\mathcal{B}}\cdot m_{\mathcal{R}}

as desired.
∎
