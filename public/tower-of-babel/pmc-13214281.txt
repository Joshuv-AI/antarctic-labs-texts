Comparison of mechanical versus bioprosthetic valve replacement in mitral stenosis surgery
Authors: Jinfeng Ma, Qingming Xue
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMitral stenosis is a common valvular heart disease, and the choice of valve material during surgical treatment significantly impacts patient prognosis. Currently, data on mid-term outcomes following valve replacement in Chinese patients with mitral stenosis are limited. This study aimed to compare the clinical efficacy and mid-term prognosis between mechanical and bioprosthetic valves in mitral stenosis surgery.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed 200 patients who underwent mitral valve replacement at our cardiac surgery department between January 2015 and December 2020. Patients were divided into mechanical valve group (n = 80) and bioprosthetic valve group (n = 120) based on the valve material used. Preoperative clinical data, surgical parameters, and postoperative follow-up data were recorded, including postoperative complications, valve-related events, survival rates, and quality of life scores. The follow-up period ranged from 1 to 6 years.ResultsComparing baseline characteristics between the two groups, the mean age in the bioprosthetic valve group was significantly higher than in the mechanical valve group (65.7 ± 8.3 years vs. 52.4 ± 9.6 years, P < 0.001). There was no significant difference in surgical mortality between the two groups (mechanical valve group 2.5% vs. bioprosthetic valve group 3.3%, P = 0.721). During the five-year follow-up period, the mechanical valve group had a higher incidence of bleeding complications than the bioprosthetic valve group (8.8% vs. 3.3%, P = 0.099), while the bioprosthetic valve group had a higher incidence of SVD than the mechanical valve group (7.5% vs. 1.3%, P = 0.049). The five-year survival rate was 92.5% in the mechanical valve group and 87.5% in the bioprosthetic valve group (P = 0.134).ConclusionFor patients with mitral stenosis, mechanical valves offer better durability but require lifelong anticoagulation therapy; bioprosthetic valves have fewer bleeding complications but carry the risk of valve deterioration. The choice of valve material should be individualized based on factors such as patient age, anticoagulation contraindications, and life expectancy. Introduction

Mitral stenosis is a common valvular heart disease characterized by reduced mitral valve orifice area, with an adult prevalence of approximately 0.02%-0.2% globally, where rheumatic heart disease remains the predominant etiology in developing countries [1–3]. When the mitral valve area decreases to less than 1.5 cm², patients may experience symptoms such as dyspnea, fatigue, and palpitations, severely affecting quality of life [4–6]. Treatment strategies include medical therapy, percutaneous transmitral commissurotomy (PTMC), and surgical intervention. For patients with contraindications to PTMC, such as severe calcification, significant regurgitation, or left atrial thrombus, mitral valve replacement is the primary treatment modality [7–12]. Currently, the commonly used valve materials in clinical practice include mechanical valves and bioprosthetic valves. Mechanical valves have a longer lifespan but require lifelong anticoagulation therapy with associated bleeding risks; bioprosthetic valves have lower anticoagulation requirements but inferior durability, particularly prone to valve deterioration in younger patients [13–17]. Existing studies are predominantly derived from Western populations, and data on mid-term outcomes following valve replacement in Chinese patients with mitral stenosis are relatively limited. Therefore, this study aimed to systematically evaluate the clinical efficacy and mid-term prognosis differences between mechanical and bioprosthetic valves through retrospective cohort analysis, focusing on postoperative complication rates, reoperation rates, survival rates, and quality of life indicators, to enrich the evidence-based medicine evidence in this field.

Methods

Patient selection

This study retrospectively enrolled patients who underwent mitral valve replacement at our cardiac surgery department between January 2015 and December 2020. Rigorous selection criteria were established to ensure the representativeness of study subjects and validity of data. Importantly, patients who underwent mitral valve repair were excluded; only those receiving mechanical or bioprosthetic valve replacement were included. Inclusion criteria were: (1) echocardiographically diagnosed mitral stenosis (mitral valve area ≤ 1.5 cm²), ensuring diagnostic clarity; (2) patients undergoing isolated mitral valve replacement or combined with other cardiac procedures, covering common clinical practices; (3) complete clinical and follow-up data, ensuring data quality. To minimize confounding factors, comprehensive exclusion criteria were established: (1) history of previous cardiac valve surgery, to avoid the influence of reoperation factors; (2) emergency surgery, as outcomes may be affected by various urgent factors; (3) concurrent infective endocarditis, as its unique pathophysiological process may interfere with study results; (4) severe hepatic or renal insufficiency, which may affect postoperative recovery and drug metabolism; (5) life expectancy < 1 year, precluding mid-term follow-up assessment; (6) incomplete follow-up data, affecting accuracy of result interpretation. Although 342 patients initially met the inclusion criteria, only 200 patients with complete clinical and follow-up data were included in the final analysis.

Grouping strategy

Regarding grouping strategy, patients were divided into mechanical valve group (n = 80) and bioprosthetic valve group (n = 120) based on the valve material used during surgery. Valve type selection followed clinical practice principles, primarily based on patient age, anticoagulation contraindications, lifestyle, patient preferences, and surgeon experience. According to domestic and international guidelines, we generally prefer mechanical valves for patients under 65 years to achieve long-term durability; bioprosthetic valves are preferred for patients over 75 years to reduce anticoagulation-related risks; for “gray zone” patients aged 65–75 years, decisions are made based on individual factors such as comorbidities, life expectancy, and lifestyle habits. Additionally, for patients with anticoagulation contraindications (such as bleeding tendency, need for frequent surgical treatment, poor anticoagulation compliance), bioprosthetic valves are preferred regardless of age. To ensure comparability of baseline characteristics between the two groups, we recorded and analyzed in detail the demographic characteristics, underlying diseases, cardiac function status, and other factors of patients.

Surgical procedure

Surgical procedures were performed according to standardized protocols to minimize the impact of surgeon and technical variations on outcomes. All patients underwent surgery via median sternotomy under general anesthesia, with establishment of cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) and moderate hypothermia (28–32 °C) protection strategy. After cardiac arrest, the mitral valve was exposed through a left atrial approach, the extent and characteristics of valve pathology were carefully evaluated, then the diseased valve and its associated structures were excised, preserving partial chordae tendineae when possible to maintain left ventricular geometry and function. According to the predetermined valve type, appropriately sized mechanical or bioprosthetic valves were implanted [18, 19]. In this study, the mechanical valves primarily used were St. Jude Medical bileaflet valves and CarboMedics bileaflet valves, both internationally widely used with excellent hemodynamic performance; bioprosthetic valves mainly included Carpentier-Edwards bovine pericardial valves and Medtronic Mosaic porcine valves, both with good durability and biocompatibility. Valve implantation employed interrupted or continuous suturing techniques to ensure valve stability without paravalvular leak. After valve suturing was completed, thorough de-airing was performed, cardiac rhythm was gradually restored, CPB was weaned, and the chest was closed. Postoperative anticoagulation regimens were established based on valve type: mechanical valve group patients received lifelong warfarin anticoagulation therapy with target international normalized ratio (INR) of 2.5–3.5, monitored through regular outpatient follow-up with dose adjustment as needed; bioprosthetic valve group patients received 3-month warfarin anticoagulation therapy (target INR 2.0–3.0), then switched to aspirin or discontinued anticoagulation based on specific circumstances such as concurrent atrial fibrillation (AF).

Data collection

To comprehensively evaluate the clinical efficacy and mid-term prognosis of both valve types, we established a systematic follow-up data collection system including preoperative, intraoperative, and postoperative indicators. Preoperative data comprehensively collected patient baseline characteristics including age, sex, body mass index (BMI), New York Heart Association (NYHA) functional classification, AF, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, cerebrovascular disease history, renal insufficiency, and other underlying disease information; preoperative echocardiographic parameters were also recorded, including mitral valve area, left atrial diameter, left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), etc., providing baseline references for evaluating surgical efficacy. Intraoperative data detailed surgical time, CPB time, aortic cross-clamp time, valve model and size, and concomitant surgical procedures, for analyzing the impact of surgical technical factors on prognosis. Early postoperative (within 30 days) follow-up indicators included mortality, reoperation rate, bleeding complications, thromboembolic events, arrhythmias, and cardiac function recovery, assessing the immediate efficacy and safety of surgery. Mid-term follow-up was conducted through outpatient visits, telephone follow-up, or home visits, with follow-up duration of 1–6 years (median follow-up 3.8 years), recording all-cause mortality, cardiovascular-related mortality, valve-related complications (including bleeding events, thromboembolic events, structural valve deterioration (SVD), valve thrombosis, paravalvular leak, etc.), reoperation rate, cardiac function status, and quality of life scores, comprehensively evaluating the mid-term clinical efficacy of both valve types. All mid-term outcomes were analyzed using time-to-event methods. Kaplan-Meier curves and log-rank tests were used for unadjusted comparisons, and multivariable Cox proportional hazards models were used to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). For reoperation, the Fine-Gray subdistribution hazard model was employed to account for competing risks, as death may preclude the occurrence of the event. Quality of life assessment used the Short Form-36 (SF-36) health survey questionnaire and NYHA functional classification, providing objective quantitative indicators for patients’ subjective experiences [20–22]. Valve deterioration and reintervention were defined according to the Valve Academic Research Consortium-3 (VARC-3) consensus document [23]. SVD was limited to morphological and/or hemodynamic deterioration confirmed by imaging evidence (such as increased leaflet thickening, calcification, or tear) and echocardiographic hemodynamic changes (mean gradient increase ≥ 10 mmHg or new moderate-to-severe regurgitation). Non-structural dysfunction (such as paravalvular leak, patient-prosthesis mismatch) was not classified as SVD. Valve-related reoperation was defined as any repeat surgical or transcatheter intervention on the implanted valve due to confirmed SVD, thrombosis, or endocarditis.

Statistical analysis

For statistical analysis, we used SPSS 25.0 software for comprehensive and systematic data processing. First, normality tests were performed on all data to determine appropriate statistical methods [23, 24]. All statistical tests were two-sided, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Additionally, to reduce the impact of selection bias, we also performed propensity score matching analysis, comparing prognostic differences between the two groups after matching for age, sex, and underlying diseases, enhancing the reliability of study results. Variables with missing values < 5% were imputed using multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE) with five imputations, and pooled HRs were reported according to Rubin’s rules. For variables with missing values > 5%, complete case analysis was performed as sensitivity analysis to assess robustness. All imputation and pooling procedures were executed using the mice package in R.

Results

Baseline characteristics comparison

The two groups showed some differences in baseline demographic and clinical characteristics. The mean age in the bioprosthetic valve group was significantly higher than in the mechanical valve group (65.7 ± 8.3 years vs. 52.4 ± 9.6 years, P < 0.001). The proportion of females was higher in the bioprosthetic valve group (58.3% vs. 48.8%, P = 0.183), but the difference was not statistically significant. Regarding comorbidities, compared with the mechanical valve group, the bioprosthetic valve group had higher rates of hypertension (48.3% vs. 36.3%, P = 0.089), diabetes mellitus (23.3% vs. 13.8%, P = 0.084), and coronary heart disease (19.2% vs. 10.0%, P = 0.073), but differences did not reach statistical significance. Preoperative AF was more common in the bioprosthetic valve group (42.5% vs. 33.8%, P = 0.205), but the difference did not reach statistical significance. There were no statistically significant differences between the two groups in BMI, smoking history, cerebrovascular disease history, and renal insufficiency (all P > 0.05). Preoperative echocardiography showed no significant differences between the two groups in mitral valve area (1.0 ± 0.3 vs. 1.1 ± 0.3 cm², P = 0.365) and LVEF (56.8 ± 7.9% vs. 57.4 ± 8.0%, P = 0.596), but the left atrial diameter was slightly larger in the bioprosthetic valve group (50.5 ± 6.3 vs. 48.8 ± 5.9 mm, P = 0.058) (Table 1).

Surgical parameters analysis

Surgical parameters showed no significant difference in operative time between the mechanical valve group and bioprosthetic valve group (204.5 ± 45.6 vs. 197.3 ± 42.1 min, P = 0.249). The CPB time was slightly longer in the mechanical valve group than in the bioprosthetic valve group (105.3 ± 28.7 vs. 97.8 ± 25.4 min, P = 0.053), and the aortic cross-clamp time was also slightly longer (78.6 ± 21.3 vs. 73.4 ± 19.8 min, P = 0.082), but differences did not reach statistical significance. The two groups showed differences in concomitant cardiac surgery rates, with the bioprosthetic valve group having a higher rate of concomitant coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) (15.0% vs. 8.8%, P = 0.173), while the mechanical valve group had a higher rate of concomitant tricuspid valve surgery (23.8% vs. 18.3%, P = 0.340), but differences were not statistically significant. Regarding valve size, there was no significant difference between the two groups, with mean implanted valve diameter of 27.4 ± 1.8 mm in the mechanical valve group and 27.6 ± 1.9 mm in the bioprosthetic valve group (P = 0.457) (Table 2).

Early clinical outcomes comparison

Early clinical outcomes within 30 days postoperatively showed no significant difference in surgical mortality between the two groups (mechanical valve group 2.5% vs. bioprosthetic valve group 3.3%, P = 0.721). Major causes of death included low cardiac output syndrome, multiple organ failure, and severe infection. Among early complications, the mechanical valve group had a slightly higher rate of bleeding requiring reoperation (2.5% vs. 1.7%, P = 0.678), but the difference was not statistically significant. The bioprosthetic valve group had a higher incidence of acute kidney injury (AKI) (8.3% vs. 5.0%, P = 0.357), while respiratory complications were similar between the two groups (mechanical valve group 7.5% vs. bioprosthetic valve group 7.5%, P = 1.000). There was no significant difference in postoperative hospital stay between the two groups (mechanical valve group 12.4 ± 4.3 days vs. bioprosthetic valve group 12.7 ± 4.4 days, P = 0.629). Immediate postoperative echocardiographic evaluation showed good valve function in both groups without significant paravalvular leak or valve dysfunction (Table 3).

Mid-term prognosis comparison

During mid-term follow-up with a median follow-up time of 3.8 years, the five-year all-cause mortality was 7.5% (6/80) in the mechanical valve group and 12.5% (15/120) in the bioprosthetic valve group, with no statistically significant difference between the two groups (P = 0.259). Cardiovascular mortality was 5.0% (4/80) in the mechanical valve group and 8.3% (10/120) in the bioprosthetic valve group (P = 0.352). Regarding valve-related complications, the mechanical valve group had a higher incidence of bleeding events than the bioprosthetic valve group (8.8% vs. 3.3%, P = 0.099), but the difference did not reach statistical significance. Conversely, the bioprosthetic valve group had a higher incidence of SVD than the mechanical valve group (7.5% vs. 1.3%, P = 0.049). There was no significant difference in thromboembolic events between the two groups (3.8% vs. 2.5%, P = 0.601). The reoperation rate was higher in the bioprosthetic valve group than in the mechanical valve group (5.8% vs. 1.3%, P = 0.099), but the difference did not reach statistical significance. The proportional hazards assumption was assessed for all Cox models using Schoenfeld residuals and global goodness-of-fit tests. No significant violations were observed for most models (all global test P > 0.05), except for marginal deviation in the SVD model (global P = 0.034) and borderline results for the bleeding model (global P = 0.056). The deviations were minor and do not materially affect model interpretation. According to VARC-3 definitions [23], all reoperations were classified as valve-related, and valve deterioration events were limited to confirmed SVD. Non-structural dysfunction was not included (Table 4, S1, S2).

Cumulative incidence of reoperation with all-cause death as competing event

In the competing risk analysis with all-cause death as a competing event, the Fine-Gray subdistribution hazard model showed that the cumulative incidence of reoperation was lower in the mechanical valve group compared to the bioprosthetic valve group (subdistribution HR = 0.22, 95% CI 0.03–1.79, P = 0.158). Due to the small number of events (1 in the mechanical valve group, 7 in the bioprosthetic valve group), the confidence interval is wide, and this result should be interpreted with caution. After adjusting for age, sex, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, preoperative AF, and LVEF, mechanical valves were associated with lower reoperation risk but did not reach statistical significance. The cumulative incidence curves showed that the reoperation risk gradually increased over time in the bioprosthetic valve group, while the mechanical valve group maintained a relatively stable low incidence throughout the follow-up period (Fig. 1).

Sensitivity analysis

To address potential selection bias, additional causal inference methods were performed. After 1:1 nearest-neighbor propensity score matching (caliper = 0.2 standard deviation [SD]), baseline covariates were well balanced between groups (all standardized mean difference [SMD] < 0.1). Consistent results were observed using inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) and overlap weighting, with all weighted HRs remaining similar in magnitude and direction. In the overlap weighting model, mechanical valve implantation was associated with reduced risk of SVD (HR = 0.14 [95% CI 0.02–1.03], P = 0.054), but the confidence interval crossed 1, and results should be interpreted with caution. The corresponding E-value was 13.9, indicating that unmeasured confounders would need to have an association risk ratio of ≥ 13.9 with both valve type and SVD to fully explain this observed association. Covariate balance improved significantly after propensity score methods (Fig. 2). Panel A shows the SMD of key baseline variables before and after 1:1 nearest-neighbor matching, with all post-matching SMD values < 0.1. Panel B shows that both IPTW and overlap weighting achieved excellent covariate balance (all SMD < 0.1). These findings confirm that the weighted and matched populations are well balanced, supporting the robustness of subsequent Cox analyses. The consistent results across all adjustment strategies further suggest that the observed protective association between mechanical valve implantation and SVD is unlikely to be driven by residual confounding. Missing data were minimal (< 5% for all covariates except LVEF), and multiple imputation produced results consistent with complete case analysis (Table S4), supporting the robustness of our findings. Multiple imputation using MICE produced HR estimates highly consistent with complete case analysis for all endpoints, indicating that missing data are unlikely to have materially biased the results (Tables S3, S4).

Quality of life and cardiac function assessment

Postoperative quality of life assessment using the SF-36 health survey questionnaire showed significant improvement in both groups after surgery. At one-year follow-up, both groups showed significant improvement in all eight dimensions (physical functioning, role-physical, bodily pain, general health, vitality, social functioning, role-emotional, and mental health) (all P < 0.001), but there were no statistically significant differences between groups (P > 0.05). At five-year follow-up, the mechanical valve group had slightly higher scores in physical functioning and vitality than the bioprosthetic valve group (P = 0.047 and P = 0.039). However, the differences between groups (physical functioning: 3.1 points; vitality: 3.5 points) only slightly exceeded the minimal clinically important difference (MCID) threshold for SF-36 (typically 3–5 points), and the clinical significance may be limited. Regarding cardiac function assessment, preoperative NYHA functional classification was similar in both groups, predominantly class II-III. At one-year follow-up, cardiac function improved significantly in both groups, with 92.5% of patients in the mechanical valve group and 90.0% in the bioprosthetic valve group in class I-II (P = 0.530). At five-year follow-up, the mechanical valve group maintained better cardiac function with 88.8% in class I-II, while the bioprosthetic valve group decreased to 82.5% (P = 0.225), but the difference did not reach statistical significance (Table 5).

Risk factor analysis for valve selection

Using Cox proportional hazards regression models, we analyzed independent risk factors affecting mid-term prognosis. Multivariable analysis showed that advanced age (HR = 1.64, 95% CI: 1.28–2.10, P < 0.001), preoperative AF (HR = 1.46, 95% CI: 1.12–1.89, P = 0.005), diabetes mellitus (HR = 1.52, 95% CI: 1.15–2.01, P = 0.003), and reduced preoperative LVEF (HR = 1.37, 95% CI: 1.06–1.78, P = 0.018) were independent risk factors for all-cause mortality. For valve-related complications, mechanical valve (HR = 2.14, 95% CI: 1.45–3.16, P < 0.001) was an independent risk factor for bleeding events, while bioprosthetic valve (HR = 3.86, 95% CI: 2.13–7.02, P < 0.001), age < 60 years (HR = 2.41, 95% CI: 1.67–3.48, P < 0.001), and valve size > 29 mm (HR = 1.82, 95% CI: 1.24–2.66, P = 0.002) were independent risk factors for SVD. Additionally, poor anticoagulation compliance (HR = 2.08, 95% CI: 1.42–3.05, P < 0.001) was an independent predictor of thromboembolic events (Fig. 3).

Subgroup analysis results

As an exploratory analysis, we performed subgroup analysis by age. Among patients under 60 years old, there were 38 individuals in the mechanical valve group and 22 individuals in the bioprosthetic valve group, selected from the total cohorts of n = 80 and n = 120, respectively. The two subgroups showed no significant difference in 1-year survival rate (94.7% vs. 90.9%, P = 0.562), but the bioprosthetic valve group had a higher SVD rate than the mechanical valve group (4/22, 18.2% vs. 0/38, 0.0%, P = 0.006), and a higher 5-year reoperation rate (3/22, 13.6% vs. 0/38, 0.0%, P = 0.019). Due to the small subgroup sample size (60 total) and limited events (4 cases of SVD and 3 reoperations all occurred in the bioprosthetic valve group), these results should be considered hypothesis-generating in nature (Fig. 4).

Discussion

Based on the results of this comprehensive retrospective study comparing mechanical and bioprosthetic valves in mitral stenosis surgery, several important considerations emerge for clinical practice. Our study provides valuable insights into the mid-term outcomes of these two valve types in a Chinese patient population.

Mitral stenosis continues to pose significant challenges in cardiovascular medicine, particularly in developing countries where rheumatic heart disease remains prevalent. The global burden of this disease is substantial, with estimated adult prevalence of 0.02–0.2%, disproportionately affecting women [24–26]. In China, as in many other developing regions, rheumatic heart disease remains the predominant etiology, although the landscape is gradually shifting with increasing prevalence of degenerative calcification as the population ages [27, 28].

The decision between mechanical and bioprosthetic valves represents a critical juncture in the management of mitral stenosis requiring surgical intervention. Our study confirms the established paradigm that mechanical valves offer superior durability but require lifelong anticoagulation, while bioprosthetic valves provide freedom from lifelong anticoagulation at the cost of potential structural deterioration over time. However, our findings also highlight several nuanced considerations for different patient subgroups.

Age remains a primary determinant of valve selection, as reflected in international guidelines from the American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association (ACC/AHA) and European Society of Cardiology (ESC), which generally recommend mechanical valves for patients under 65 years and bioprosthetic valves for those over 65 years. Our results support this approach, particularly with the exploratory subgroup analysis showing a concerning SVD rate (18.2%) in younger patients (< 60 years) who received bioprosthetic valves. However, due to the small subgroup sample size (38 in the mechanical valve group, 22 in the bioprosthetic valve group) and limited events, this conclusion needs to be verified in larger-scale prospective studies. This finding aligns with previous studies demonstrating accelerated deterioration in younger patients, possibly related to more robust immune responses and calcium metabolism in this population.

However, the traditional 65-year age threshold may need reconsideration given increasing life expectancy and improvements in valve technology. Our data suggest that even in the “gray zone” of 60–70 years, mechanical valves maintain excellent durability with acceptable bleeding risk when anticoagulation is properly managed. This challenges the recent clinical practice trends toward increasingly liberal use of bioprosthetic valves in younger patients, particularly in Western countries.

For elderly patients (> 70 years), the bioprosthetic valve offers advantages of fewer bleeding complications and acceptable durability within expected lifespan. This supports current practice of preferring bioprosthetic valves in this age group, where the risk of anticoagulation-related complications outweighs the benefits of mechanical valve durability.

Beyond age, our study identified several independent risk factors for adverse outcomes that should influence the valve selection process. Preoperative AF, diabetes mellitus, and reduced LVEF emerged as significant predictors of mortality, emphasizing the importance of comprehensive patient assessment. Notably, poor anticoagulation compliance strongly predicted thromboembolic events, highlighting a critical consideration in the Chinese context where regular monitoring may be challenging in rural areas with limited healthcare accessibility.

The etiology of mitral stenosis appears to influence outcomes, with our study showing lower rates of structural deterioration in bioprosthetic valves used for degenerative lesions compared to rheumatic lesions [29]. This observation, while requiring further study, suggests that the inflammatory nature of rheumatic disease may accelerate bioprosthetic valve deterioration, potentially necessitating more conservative use of these valves in rheumatic heart disease patients.

Regarding quality of life outcomes, while both valve types achieved significant postoperative improvement, the mechanical valve group showed slightly higher scores in physical functioning and vitality at 5-year follow-up than the bioprosthetic valve group. However, the differences between groups (3.1 points and 3.5 points, respectively) only slightly exceeded the MCID threshold for SF-36 (typically 3–5 points), and the clinical significance may be limited. Furthermore, this study did not fully assess the quality of life burden associated with lifelong anticoagulation therapy for mechanical valve patients, including frequent INR monitoring, dietary restrictions, concerns about bleeding events, and drug interactions. These factors may have important impacts on patients’ daily lives, and future studies should more comprehensively assess anticoagulation-related quality of life burden.

This study provides a real-world comparison of mechanical and bioprosthetic valve outcomes in a relatively young cohort (mean age 59 years), which approaches the lower bound of low-risk patients who might be considered for next-generation transcatheter valve therapies. Based on these findings, future studies could prospectively evaluate early transcatheter mitral valve replacement (TMVR) versus surgical bioprosthetic valve implantation in low-to-intermediate risk populations. Additionally, our institution has initiated a first-in-human trial of the TRIA™ polymeric mitral valve, with plans to recruit 60 patients over the next two years. The current results will serve as an important historical control for these ongoing studies. Recent preclinical evidence on polymeric valve durability and hemocompatibility further supports the potential for polymer-based materials to overcome structural deterioration seen in traditional tissue valves [30]. Taken together, these directions highlight the transition toward personalized, technology-driven valve therapy that integrates novel materials, transcatheter approaches, and artificial intelligence-assisted decision-making for optimal mid-term and long-term outcomes.

Limitations

This study has several important limitations. First, as a single-center retrospective study, valve type selection was non-random and mainly driven by patient age, resulting in significant confounding by indication. Although we employed multiple statistical methods (propensity score matching, IPTW, overlap weighting) to control for selection bias, these methods can only adjust for measured confounders and cannot completely eliminate residual confounding. Second, the sample size of 200 patients is relatively small, resulting in a low number of events for some endpoints (e.g., reoperation events: 1 in the mechanical valve group, 7 in the bioprosthetic valve group), wide confidence intervals, and a high risk of Type II error, which may have failed to detect truly existing differences. Third, the 1–6 year follow-up period (median 3.8 years) can only reflect mid-term outcomes and is insufficient to fully assess long-term prognosis, which is particularly important for younger patients requiring lifelong valve use. Fourth, our single-center experience may not fully represent the diversity of Chinese and international clinical practice. Finally, the subgroup analyses in this study should be considered exploratory, and their conclusions need to be verified in larger-scale multicenter prospective studies.

Conclusion

In summary, our study reinforces the importance of individualized decision-making in valve selection for mitral stenosis, requiring careful consideration of patient age, comorbidities, anticoagulation contraindications, and lifestyle factors. While age remains the primary determinant, with mechanical valves generally preferred for younger patients and bioprosthetic valves for older patients, decisions must be tailored to each patient’s unique circumstances. This study provides important mid-term follow-up data for the Chinese population following valve replacement, but the conclusions need to be further verified in larger-scale prospective studies.
