Effect of adding proprioceptive neuromuscular facilitation to conventional physiotherapy on scapular balance and shoulder proprioception in adolescents with idiopathic scoliosis: a randomised controlled trial protocol
Authors: Yanzi Xu, Mengying Feng, Ruidi Wu, Yu Wang, Zhaoyi Yang, Ziyu Wang, Zhenghong Kang, Lingfeng Xie, Hua Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionAdolescent idiopathic scoliosis (AIS) involves three-dimensional structural spinal changes, frequently accompanied by scapular dyskinesis (SD) and proprioceptive dysfunction. Although physiotherapeutic scoliosis-specific exercises effectively correct spinal alignment, their efficacy in addressing SD and associated sensorimotor deficits remains underexplored. This randomised controlled trial (RCT) will evaluate whether integrating upper limb proprioceptive neuromuscular facilitation (PNF) with conventional physical therapy yields synergistic improvements in scapular kinematics and proprioceptive function in AIS.Methods and analysisThis single-centre RCT will be conducted at the Department of Rehabilitation Medicine, Tongji Hospital, Tongji Medical College, Huazhong University of Science and Technology, Wuhan, China. 48 adolescents with AIS will be randomly assigned to either a control group (n=24) receiving conventional physical therapy or an experimental group (n=24) receiving combined conventional therapy and upper limb PNF interventions. Both groups will follow a structured 60 min rehabilitation programme, administered five sessions per week for 8 weeks (including two outpatient sessions and three home training sessions per week). Follow-up assessments will be conducted at 12 weeks postintervention to evaluate the maintenance of therapeutic effects. Primary outcomes will include the scapular balance angle and the active relocation test for shoulder proprioception. Secondary outcomes will comprise the scapular index, the lateral scapular slide test, normalised surface electromyography (root mean square, %muscle activation degree) of the upper trapezius, lower trapezius, serratus anterior, posterior deltoid and infraspinatus during standardised tasks, posture analysis, Cobb angle, angle of trunk rotation and the Simplified Chinese version of the Scoliosis Research Society-22 questionnaire for health-related quality of life. Data will be analysed using Analysis of Covariance and linear mixed-effects models, adjusted for baseline values, following the intention-to-treat principle.Ethics and disseminationThe study protocol has been approved by the Ethics Committee of Tongji Hospital, Tongji Medical College, Huazhong University of Science and Technology (Approval No. TJ-IEB2025-018) and has been registered on ClinicalTrials.gov. The findings will be disseminated through peer-reviewed journal publications, conference presentations and media releases.Trial registration numberChiCTR2500099252.

Introduction

Adolescent idiopathic scoliosis (AIS) involves complex three-dimensional structural spinal changes characterised by rotational displacement in the axial (horizontal) plane, lateral curvature in the coronal plane and sagittal plane abnormalities such as hyperlordosis or kyphosis.1 Predominantly affecting adolescents aged 10–18 years, AIS accounts for approximately 90% of idiopathic scoliosis cases,2 with an incidence approximately 1.46-fold higher in females than in males.3 AIS is widely regarded as a multifactorial disorder influenced by genetic predisposition,4 neurological dysfunction5 and hormonal factors,6 ultimately resulting in abnormal bone formation.7 In addition to structural changes, AIS compromises trunk biomechanics, reduces pulmonary function, limits physical activity and negatively impacts psychological well-being.8

Current evidence suggests AIS often coincides with scapular postural deviations and impaired movement coordination.9 Scapular Dyskinesis (SD), a clinically significant complication of AIS, is characterised by static or dynamic malpositioning of the scapula.10 The abnormal spinal structure in AIS induces compensatory changes in the scapular girdle.11 Specifically, the vertebral body rotates towards the convex side of the curve, with its anterior aspect projecting into the convexity while the spinous process deviates towards the concavity. This vertebral rotation and thoracic cage deformation lead to scapular asymmetry: on the convex side, the scapula is elevated, internally rotated in the transverse plane and anteriorly tilted in the sagittal plane, whereas on the concave side, the scapula is relatively lower, retracted and posteriorly tilted. This static imbalance contributes to dynamic dysfunction during upper limb abduction, where the convex-side scapula demonstrates insufficient upward rotation, reduced posterior tilt and compensatory excessive anterior tilt.12 13 These characteristic scapular changes are most evident in thoracic AIS, while thoracolumbar or lumbar curves exert less direct influence on scapular alignment, and double-curve patterns may present variable scapular positions depending on the dominant curve. These asymmetries disrupt the upper limb kinetic chain and alter thoracic-scapular mechanics.14 15 Furthermore, they disrupt scapulohumeral rhythm, narrow the subacromial space and significantly increase the risk of subacromial impingement.12 Importantly, preoperative ipsilateral shoulder elevation has been identified as a predictive factor for postoperative SD in surgically treated AIS patients.16

In AIS, dysfunction in neuromuscular control of the scapular girdle is closely linked to altered proprioception. Effective neuromuscular control relies on integrated sensorimotor feedback loops; disordered proprioceptive input impairs muscle coordination and dynamic joint stability,17 compromising functional performance and elevating injury risk. Numerous studies have documented proprioceptive deficits across multiple joints in AIS patients—including the cervical spine,18 elbow,19 knee20 and ankle21—evidenced by elevated repositioning errors, impaired motion detection thresholds and abnormal somatosensory-evoked potentials. Nevertheless, the causal relationship between AIS and proprioceptive dysfunction remains incompletely understood. Scapular girdle involvement has been reported in AIS, with existing literature focusing on scapular structural asymmetry and periscapular soft-tissue adaptations.22 23 However, the specific impact of these changes on shoulder proprioception remains largely unexplored. Surface electromyography (sEMG) studies confirm that scapular depressors exhibit significantly delayed activation latency (specifically the rhomboid muscles) and altered activation patterns,24 indicating deterioration in their coordination and motor control. We hypothesise that these neuromuscular disturbances, coupled with significant proprioceptive deviation, may directly impair upper limb movement accuracy; this disorder may reinforce maladaptive postural compensation patterns via erroneous sensory feedback, creating a detrimental cycle.

Contemporary rehabilitation protocols for AIS typically combine physical therapy, occupational therapy (where indicated) and orthotic management.25 In patients with mild to moderate AIS, physical therapy constitutes a cornerstone of rehabilitation, incorporating core stabilisation, physiotherapeutic scoliosis-specific exercises (PSSE), therapeutic exercise and occasionally traction. PSSE is the SOSORT()-endorsed first-line non-surgical approach,26 27 applying the three-dimensional self-correction principle through individualised plans. It functions by redistributing asymmetric spinal loading and normalising pathological biomechanics to mitigate curve progression.28 While PSSE has demonstrated efficacy in reducing scoliosis progression, modifying Cobb angle and improving trunk mobility, its effectiveness in managing SD is not well established. In this study, conventional physical therapy, as the comparison intervention, refers to a standard rehabilitation approach that addresses general aspects of scoliosis through core stabilisation, postural management and spinal exercises, but without a specific focus on SD.

Proprioceptive neuromuscular facilitation (PNF), a neurophysiological rehabilitation approach, demonstrates clinical efficacy in shoulder rehabilitation by improving proprioceptive acuity, dynamic stability and scapulothoracic kinematics.29 30 A key mechanism of PNF is its capacity to optimise neuromuscular control using proprioceptively enriched techniques. This includes using spiral-diagonal movement patterns to promote the recruitment of target motor units and applying combined resistance-stretch stimuli to directly reset muscle spindle afferent signals.31 Preliminary research confirms that pelvic PNF patterns in AIS modulate neuromuscular activation and improve three-dimensional postural deviations.32 33 However, the application of PNF specifically for treating SD in individuals with AIS remains largely unexplored.

This randomised controlled trial (RCT) will investigate the efficacy of a combined intervention of conventional physiotherapy and upper limb PNF compared with conventional physiotherapy alone in the management of adolescents with AIS. The primary outcomes will be the scapular balance angle (SBA), reflecting scapular alignment, and the active relocation test (ART), reflecting shoulder joint position sense, both of which will assess proprioceptive dysfunction and postural control in the torso and shoulder. Secondary outcomes will include the scapular index (SI), the lateral scapular slide test, sEMG, posture parameters, Cobb angle, angle of trunk rotation (ATR), and health-related quality of life assessed with the simplified Chinese version of the Scoliosis Research Society-22 (SC-SRS-22) questionnaire.

Methods/design

Study design

This single-centre, prospective, parallel-group RCT will be conducted in the Department of Rehabilitation Medicine at Tongji Hospital, Huazhong University of Science and Technology, adhering to the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki (ethics approval No. TJ-IRB202502141) and implementing the Standard Protocol Items: Recommendations for Interventional Trials guidelines27 and the 2024 AIS Rehabilitation Guideline.25 Eligible participants meeting AIS diagnostic criteria will be consecutively recruited from outpatient rehabilitation clinics. Recruitment will be ongoing throughout the study period. Each recruited participant will then receive the assigned intervention for a duration of 8 weeks. The study coordinator will invite eligible participants to join the study during these visits, providing them with detailed information about the study, answering questions and ensuring informed consent before enrolment (online supplemental material 2). Sample size calculation

The SBA will be selected as the primary outcome for sample size estimation. According to a previous study,36 to calculate an appropriate effect size for the between-group comparison, we will use the post-intervention group means from a preliminary dataset (experimental group: M=6.00, SD=2.45; control group: M=8.38, SD=2.50; n=8 per group) and will compute Cohen’s d as the standardised mean difference between groups:

SDpooled=n1-1SD122+n2-1SD22n1+n2, d=M1-M2SDpooled

Substituting these values will yield SDpooled≈2.48 and d≈0.96. Using this between-group effect size in G*Power 3.1 for a two-tailed independent-samples t-test with α=0.05 and power (1−β) =0.80, the required sample size will be calculated to be 19 participants per group (total n=38). Accounting for an anticipated 20% dropout rate, the final recruitment target will be set at 48 participants (24 per group).

This calculation explicitly will refer to postintervention between-group differences and the associated independent-samples t-test. We acknowledge that the actual enrolled sample may be smaller than this target, which may limit the statistical power to detect smaller differences.

Randomisation and blinding

This assessor-blinded, parallel-group RCT will use computer-generated block randomisation for the allocation of eligible participants to either the experimental or control group following baseline assessments. Randomly distributed block sizes of 4 and 6 will be used to ensure balanced group allocation while maintaining randomisation integrity. An independent, blinded statistician will generate and conceal randomisation codes via sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes accessible only to the treating therapists. Both the statistician responsible for randomisation and the statistician performing the final data analyses will remain blinded to group allocation throughout the study. Standardised assessor training established inter-rater reliability >0.90 (Intraclass Correlation Coefficient, ICC) for all outcome measures prior to trial commencement. Blinded assessors will conduct all evaluations throughout the data collection and statistical analysis phases, with blinding integrity systematically verified through post-trial questionnaires.

Intervention

Participants with AIS will be randomised into two treatment arms. The control group will receive evidence-based conventional physical therapy, while the experimental group will undergo a combined regimen of conventional physical therapy and PNF training. Both groups will follow a structured rehabilitation schedule comprising 60 min sessions, delivered five times weekly (one session/day), with two supervised clinic sessions per week and three home-based sessions per week, over an 8-week intervention period. After the 8-week intervention period, participants will undergo a 12th-week follow-up evaluation to assess the sustained effects of the intervention on scapular alignment, shoulder function and other outcome measures. Therapy protocols will be standardised across cohorts to ensure uniformity in dosage and therapeutic exposure.

Conventional physical therapy

Conventional physical therapy is based on the Schroth, Lyon and Scientific Exercise Approach to Scoliosis (SEAS) methods3739 and includes daily posture management, core stability training, scoliosis-specific exercises and spinal manual release. Patients will first be assisted in identifying improper postures using mirrors and instructed on how to adjust the positions of the pelvis and scapular girdle to a neutral alignment. Standing and sitting postures will then be designed based on the three-dimensional correction principle, taking into account the direction of the primary spinal curve. Core training will begin with the activation of deep stabilising muscle groups to enhance neuromuscular control via abdominal breathing and low-intensity isometric contractions, such as hip bridges, dead bugs and Superman exercises. Individualised training protocols will subsequently be developed based on comprehensive assessments. These protocols will incorporate active three-dimensional correction strategies in various positions (lateral, sitting and standing) to improve the flexibility of the muscles and soft tissues on the convex side, alleviate excessive tension and facilitate functional activation and strength training of the concave-side musculature—thereby restoring overall spinal and muscular balance. Additionally, active control exercises for the pelvis and scapula will be integrated to optimise their dynamic stability and mechanical linkage with the spine. Rotational breathing techniques will be employed to enhance thoracic symmetry. In specific corrective positions, subjects will perform deep inhalations to expand the concave side of the thorax, thereby promoting rotational realignment and symmetry restoration of the convex thoracic vertebrae. Furthermore, spinal manual release techniques will be applied to improve intervertebral joint mobility, reduce soft tissue tension and alleviate pain, thus augmenting the overall effectiveness of the three-dimensional spinal correction training. The programme will involve a prescribed regimen of 10 cycles per set, with 3 sets per day for each patient. The exercises will be tailored based on the individual’s specific needs, with adjustments to be made based on progress and tolerance. This dosing protocol will be consistently followed throughout the 8-week intervention period, ensuring that each patient will receive a standardised amount of exercise to target the necessary neuromuscular and postural improvements. Upper limb PNF training

The experimental group will receive a 60 min daily intervention comprising 40 min of conventional physical therapy40 41 supplemented with 20 min of PNF upper limb pattern training using elastic bands. Resistance levels will be individualised based on baseline functional capacity, defined as the maximum resistance at which the participant will be able to complete the prescribed movements with correct form, without pain and without compensatory movements. Training progression will be achieved by increasing band resistance once participants could comfortably complete the exercise protocol. In the control group, conventional physical therapy will include active scapula exercises aimed at improving dynamic stability and the mechanical linkage between the scapula and spine. These exercises will focus on controlled scapular movements such as scapular retraction, protraction, elevation and depression, which will be performed in various postures to optimise scapular stability. In the experimental group (PNF), scapular control techniques will be incorporated into the intervention using PNF to enhance neuromuscular activation through diagonal movement patterns. PNF exercises will include resistance, proprioceptive cues and functional movement patterns designed to improve dynamic shoulder stability, proprioception and motor control. The focus of the PNF intervention will be to restore multidimensional neuromuscular activation and improve functional movement patterns to address SD more effectively.

Compliance supervision

To foster interdisciplinary collaboration, comprehensive pretreatment educational sessions will be administered to participants and their guardians, emphasising the biomechanical risks of untreated scoliosis progression, potential long-term health implications and evidence-based rationale for therapeutic intervention. During the home-based training phase, guardians will be instructed to directly supervise exercise protocols and will systematically document daily training metrics, including adherence rates, movement accuracy and subjective feedback. A licensed physical therapist will conduct weekly reviews of submitted records, providing personalised feedback to optimise technique and offering motivational reinforcement through progress-tracking visualisations. If participant compliance declines, the research team will promptly communicate with the parents to remind and encourage them to provide additional support and supervision, helping participants overcome difficulties to complete the training. Overall compliance will be summarised as the percentage of prescribed home and supervised sessions actually completed over the 8-week intervention (adherence rate=completed sessions/prescribed sessions×100%). In addition, the accuracy of exercise execution will be rated by the supervising therapist based on weekly video/record review (3-point scale: accurate, partially accurate, inaccurate). These two indicators (session adherence and movement accuracy) will be combined to provide a quantitative description of participant compliance.

Assessment

Prior to experimental commencement, a standardised baseline data collection protocol will be implemented to ensure methodological rigour. This will encompass anthropometric parameters (age, height and body mass index) and clinical history documentation, including pre-existing musculoskeletal conditions or prior spinal interventions. These data will help us account for individual differences in physical characteristics and health backgrounds, ensuring a more accurate evaluation of how the exercise programme affects each participant. By understanding these foundational factors, we will be able to better distinguish the specific impacts of the intervention from other variables that might influence the results.

Outcome measurements

Primary outcomes

Scapular Balance Angle

During the assessment, participants will be instructed to remove upper body garments and stand with their torso exposed, arms relaxed at their sides. The examiner, positioned posteriorly, will mark the following anatomical landmarks using circular adhesive markers: the inferior angles of both scapulae, the spinous processes of the seventh cervical vertebra (C7), ninth thoracic vertebra (T9) and tenth thoracic vertebra (T10). The SBA will be calculated as the absolute difference between the angles formed by (1) the line connecting the inferior scapular angles and (2) the line linking C7, T9 and T10.42 A SBA exceeding 7° will indicate SD.43

Active Relocation Test

Participants will stand 100 cm from a wall-mounted target grid. A laser pointer will be affixed to the lateral forearm, and an electrogoniometer will be secured to the humeral lateral epicondyle.44 45 Participants will actively elevate their shoulders to predefined angles (low: 55°±10°, medium: 90°±10°, high: 125°±10°) with the elbow extended, forearm neutrally rotated and thumb directed upward. At each target angle, participants will be given a single 2–3 s familiarisation trial to memorise the position. Participants will then don an occlusive mask and attempt to replicate the memorised angles. The examiner will record the laser’s deviation from the target centre (cm) and will calculate the angular error (difference between target and achieved angles). Three trials per direction will be conducted, with 30 s rests between directional changes. Absolute error (AE) will be derived as the mean deviation across trials:AE=1n∑i=1nXi-T, where Xi is the reproduced angle in trial i, T is the target angle, and n=3 trials.

Secondary outcomes

Scapular Index

The examiner will mark the suprasternal fossa, coracoid processes, spinous process of the third thoracic vertebra (T3) and lateral angles of the scapulae with adhesive markers. The SI will be defined as the ratio of the distance from the suprasternal fossa to the coracoid process relative to the distance from the T3 spinous process to the scapular lateral angle.46 A ratio below 1.0 will signify abnormal scapular positioning.

Lateral Scapular Slide Test

This test will evaluate scapular symmetry across three positions as follows47:

Relaxed stance: Arms resting naturally at the sides.

Relaxed stance: Arms resting naturally at the sides.

Hands-on-iliac stance: Participants will place their hands on both iliac crests.

Hands-on-iliac stance: Participants will place their hands on both iliac crests.

Abducted-internal rotation stance: Shoulders will be abducted to 90° with internal rotation (thumbs pointing downward).

Abducted-internal rotation stance: Shoulders will be abducted to 90° with internal rotation (thumbs pointing downward).

The distance from the inferior scapular angle to the nearest thoracic spinous process will be measured in each posture. Asymmetry exceeding 1.5 cm between sides or across positions will be classified as abnormal.

Surface electromyography

This study will use the real-time, non-invasive Ultium sEMG system (Noraxon USA, Scottsdale, Arizona, USA) for electromyographic signal acquisition, with a sampling rate of 2000 Hz.

Prior to electrode placement, the skin overlying the target muscles—upper trapezius (UT), lower trapezius (LT), serratus anterior (SA), posterior deltoid (PD), and infraspinatus (IS)—will be cleaned with alcohol and abraded to reduce impedance. Electrode–skin impedance will be tested using the Ultium system’s quality check, and values below 5 kΩ will be considered acceptable to ensure high-quality signal acquisition. Disposable Ag/AgCl surface electrodes (20 mm inter-electrode distance) will be positioned in accordance with SENIAM guidelines,48 and wireless sensors will be secured to ensure stable signal transmission. Electrodes will be placed bilaterally on the UT, LT, SA, PD and IS, and EMG signals will be recorded simultaneously from both the convex and concave sides for comparison. Electrode placement positions and maximum voluntary isometric contraction (MVIC) test manoeuvres for the girdle muscles will be detailed in figure 4 and table 2.49 MVIC will be assessed for each muscle: Participants will perform three 10 s isometric contractions against manual resistance applied by the examiner, with 30 s rest intervals between trials. Following a 5 min recovery period, sEMG data will be recorded during a standardised upper limb functional task. Participants will adopt a quadruped kneeling position with feet crossed behind the body, hands shoulder-width apart and palms flat on the floor. The movement will consist of two phases: (1) inhalation while slowly lowering the torso via elbow flexion until the chest nears the floor and (2) exhalation while forcefully extending the elbows to return to the starting position.

Posture analysis

The Global Postural System will be used to collect and analyse the data.50 Participants will stand barefoot on a standardised footprint platform with arms relaxed. Adhesive markers (8 mm diameter) will be placed on anatomical landmarks: spinous processes of C7 and T9, inferior scapular angles and anterior tips of the coracoid processes. After three preparatory breaths, static posture will be captured in four views (anterior, posterior, left lateral and right lateral). Key parameters analysed will include: Forward Head Angle, Scapular Inclination and Scapular Protraction Angle. Cobb angle

A portable ultrasound scoliosis evaluation system (SCN201, Scolioscan) will be employed to measure Cobb angles.51 After applying ultrasound gel to the spinal area, participants will maintain an upright stance while a high-frequency linear probe (12 MHz, 75 mm in length) will be swept longitudinally along the spinal midline. The system will not directly visualise vertebral endplates; instead, it will identify posterior vertebral landmarks, primarily laminae, through acoustic reflections. These landmarks will serve as the primary basis for reconstructing the spinal curvature in the coronal plane, from which the ultrasound coronal angle will be derived. A three-dimensional spinal profile will be reconstructed from these posterior landmarks and surface topography data, and the Cobb angle will be indirectly estimated from this reconstructed alignment, rather than directly measured as in radiography. Three consecutive measurements will be averaged to mitigate intrarater variability.52

Angle of trunk rotation

Trunk rotation will be assessed using a gravity-assisted scoliometer.53 54 Participants will stand with feet together, palms opposed, and trunk flexed 90° at the hips. The examiner will align the device’s base groove with the C7 spinous process and will guide it smoothly caudally to L5. The maximum ATR, reflected by the integrated inclinometer’s displacement, will be recorded. Triplicate measurements will be averaged for final analysis.

SC-SRS-22 Questionnaire

The SC-SRS-22,55 a culturally adapted 22-item tool, will be used to assess health-related quality of life across five domains: pain, function, self-image, mental health and treatment satisfaction. Each item will use a 5-point Likert scale (1=worst, 5=best). Domain scores will be converted to 100-point scales for comparative analysis, with higher scores indicating better quality of life.56

Adverse events

Mild discomfort or transient muscle soreness, resolving spontaneously within 24–48 hours will be classified as an expected physiological response. Participants will receive guidance on conservative management strategies (eg, gentle stretching and localised heat therapy) and be monitored until symptom resolution. Preintervention and postintervention health screenings, including vital signs and musculoskeletal assessments, will be conducted to exclude contraindications. All procedures will adhere to institutional safety protocols, with emergency response plans reviewed prior to trial initiation.

Serious adverse events (SAEs), defined as unanticipated incidents requiring medical intervention (eg, acute injury, syncope), will trigger immediate trial suspension. SAEs will be documented using standardised case report forms, including participant demographics, event chronology and potential causative factors. All incidents will be reported to the Institutional Ethics Committee within 24 hours for independent review, and follow-up actions will be aligned with Good Clinical Practice guidelines.

Data management

The researchers will ensure accurate, complete, clear and timely data entry of the clinical trial data into Excel software based on the original observation records of the participants. Any discrepancies identified during entry will be documented in a discrepancy tracking system and resolved through consensus review. Following data entry, an independent auditor will perform source-data verification against case report forms to ensure accuracy. All participants’ personal information will be anonymised using unique identification codes, with encrypted files storing identifiable data separately from analysis results. Access to the database will be restricted to authorised personnel via role-based permissions, and backups will be performed weekly to prevent data loss. On completion of the analyses, deidentified datasets will be deposited in an institutional data repository and made available on reasonable request, in accordance with ethical approval and participant consent.

Statistical analysis

All analyses will be performed using SPSS Statistics V.26.0, with significance thresholds set at α=0.05 (two-tailed). Continuous variables will be assessed for normality via Shapiro-Wilk tests and reported as mean±SD (parametric) or median (IQR) (non-parametric). Between-group differences will be evaluated using independent t-tests (normal distribution) or Mann-Whitney U tests (non-normal distribution). Intragroup comparisons will employ paired t-tests or Wilcoxon signed-rank tests, as appropriate. To minimise the number of comparisons and control the risk of type I error, linear mixed-effects models or mixed-model analysis of variances will also be considered as a complementary approach, with pairwise comparisons conducted when indicated. Categorical data will be expressed as counts (%) and analysed via χ² or Fisher’s exact tests. Effect sizes (Cohen’s d, Cramér’s V) will be calculated to quantify clinical relevance.

Ethics and dissemination

This study has been approved by the Ethics Committee of Tongji Hospital Affiliated to Tongji Medical College of Huazhong University of Science and Technology. And informed consent will be obtained from all participants (or their guardians, if applicable) prior to data collection. Presentation of study results will include peer-reviewed publications, conference reports and media releases.

Discussion

This RCT will investigate the synergistic effects of integrating PNF with PSSE, which draw on principles of Schroth, Lyon and SEAS methods, on three-dimensional spinal realignment, SD and proprioceptive function in adolescents with mild-to-moderate AIS. The mechanistic rationale underpinning this approach will encompass three interrelated hypotheses: (a) PNF-mediated neuromuscular modulation may optimise proprioceptive processing via diagonal movement patterns, potentially enhancing dynamic scapular control by regulating force-couple balance between synergistic stabilisers (eg, SA-LT coactivation vs UT dominance)57; (b) PSSE, through techniques, such as rotational breathing and three-dimensional self-correction, may restore scapulothoracic kinematics not only by normalising thoracic kypholordotic parameters but also by reducing abnormal thoracic rotation, which directly influences scapular alignment and motion and (c) The combined intervention is hypothesised to induce proprioceptive recalibration through sensorimotor adaptation, thereby disrupting the pathological cycle of spinal malalignment → SD → proprioceptive impairment.

Existing research suggests that PNF techniques can stimulate proprioceptors via specific movement patterns,58 enhancing CNS-mediated regulation of scapular stabilisers, while Schroth exercises are proposed to facilitate three-dimensional spinal correction through rotational breathing and postural re-education.59 The therapeutic synergy will be examined through three interdependent mechanisms: (a) Neuromuscular re-education: Schroth therapy is proposed to achieve spinal correction through respiratory modulation and postural adjustments, primarily activating paraspinal core musculature via neuromuscular facilitation.60 AIS patients are expected to exhibit scapulothoracic neuromuscular dysfunction characterised by diminished stabiliser preactivation, impaired neuromuscular synchronisation and reduced contractile efficacy.24 Previous EMG studies have demonstrated that adolescents with AIS show delayed anticipatory activation of scapular stabilisers such as the SA and LT (onset latencies ~40–60 ms longer than controls),24 asymmetric reductions in normalised RMS amplitudes of stabilisers during shoulder abduction (~20%–25% lower on the concave side),61 and decreased median frequency values reflecting reduced fatigue resistance62 in adolescents with AIS, which may justify a focus on neuromuscular re-education. To address these impairments, PNF’s resistance-relaxation-active movement sequence is expected to enhance periscapular force-couple coordination (eg, SA-LT synergy vs UT overactivity). While such effects have been reported primarily in cerebral palsy populations,63 they may also apply to AIS, underscoring the importance of testing PNF in this group. (b) Proprioceptive Enhancement: Repetitive PNF patterns may facilitate signal integration along spinal-thalamocortical pathways using spiral-diagonal biomechanics, promoting sensorimotor cortex synchronisation.64 65 These effects have been demonstrated primarily in healthy adults, where PNF tasks were associated with increased alpha power in the dorsolateral prefrontal and superior parietal cortices47 and EEG modulation in the alpha band.48 A systematic review concluded that PNF improves joint position sense across both healthy and musculoskeletal populations.66 Although these findings have not yet been confirmed in AIS, they provide a rationale for hypothesising proprioceptive benefits in this population. (c) Scapulospinal kinematic integration: Schroth rotational breathing is designed to direct airflow towards the thoracic concavity, expanding ribs and inducing posterior rotation to correct spinal rotation and lateral curvature.38 This may realign muscular tension vectors (rhomboids, middle/LT), reducing compensatory scapular anterior tilt/winging. Such impairments and proprioceptive deficits have been observed in older adults with hyperkyphosis.67 While these observations do not come from AIS populations, they support the rationale for testing whether combined PNF and PSSE could optimise scapulothoracic kinematics in AIS.

Contemporary AIS rehabilitation paradigms tend to prioritise spinal realignment but often overlook concomitant SD and impaired proprioceptive function. Our integrated therapeutic strategy will aim to bridge this clinical gap through a multidimensional framework concurrently targeting structural malalignment and neuromuscular dysfunction. Critically, preoperative scapular malalignment has been identified as an independent predictor of postoperative shoulder imbalance,68 suggesting that early intervention might mitigate surgical indications and reduce iatrogenic morbidity. By harmonising spinal biomechanics with sensorimotor control, the proposed intervention is expected to optimise functional mobility and quality of life, embodying the SOSORT-endorsed principle of structural-functional equilibrium.

Therefore, this study will aim to provide preliminary insights into non-surgical intervention expansion, preoperative risk management and function-oriented rehabilitation frameworks for AIS rehabilitation. It will also acknowledge several strengths and limitations. Strengths include its randomised controlled design, integration of PSSE with a PNF-based intervention, and multidimensional assessment of scapular alignment, proprioception and neuromuscular function. However, certain limitations should be noted. First, as both groups will receive active rehabilitation interventions, the estimated between-group effect size used in the sample size calculation may be overoptimistic, and this exploratory study may be underpowered to detect smaller but clinically meaningful differences. Nevertheless, the findings will provide essential preliminary data to guide more precise sample size estimation for future large-scale trials. Second, although the 8-week intervention period will offer valuable preliminary evidence, SOSORT guidelines recommend that exercise-based trials ideally span at least 12 months—preferably until skeletal maturity—to capture sustained treatment effects. These limitations will be considered when interpreting the findings, which will represent an essential first step towards larger and longer-term clinical trials.
