Mitigating Mismatch within Reference-based Preference Optimization
Suqin Yuan, Xingrui Yu, Jiyang Zheng, Lei Feng Dadong Wang Ivor Tsang Tongliang Liu

Abstract
Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) has become the de facto standard for offline preference alignment of large language models, but its reliance on a reference policy introduces a critical tension. DPO weighs each update relative to a reference, which stabilizes the training by regularizing the updates within a trusted region. This reliance becomes problematic for pessimistic pairs, where the reference model prefers the rejected response. For these pairs, DPO prematurely attenuates the gradient as soon as the policy margin ( Δ θ \Delta_{\theta} ) merely beats the reference margin ( Δ ref \Delta_{\mathrm{ref}} ) even if the policy is still wrong ( Δ θ < 0 \Delta_{\theta}<0 ). We name this failure premature satisfaction , which is a concrete form of the training–inference mismatch . Reference-free objectives remove this mismatch by optimizing the absolute margin, but at the cost of discarding the stabilizing signal of the reference. We mitigate this tension with Hybrid-DPO (HyPO) , a drop-in modification to DPO that applies reference conditionally : HyPO behaves exactly like DPO when the reference is optimistic or neutral, and it treats the reference as neutral when it is pessimistic by replacing Δ θ − Δ ref \Delta_{\theta}-\Delta_{\mathrm{ref}} with Δ θ − max ⁡ { 0 , Δ ref } \Delta_{\theta}-\max\{0,\Delta_{\mathrm{ref}}\} . This one-line change strictly strengthens per-example learning signals on pessimistic pairs while preserving DPO’s objective form and computational cost. By conditionally debiasing the pessimistic reference signal, HyPO mitigates premature satisfaction ; empirically, across preference alignment, HyPO improves inference-aligned metrics and achieves higher pairwise win rates. Our results provide evidence that direct preference alignment could be enhanced by conditionally debiasing the reference signal, rather than discarding it.

Introduction

Aligning Large Language Models (LLMs) with human preferences and values is paramount for developing safe, helpful, and reliable AI systems
(
2
)
. The pioneering Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) paradigm
(
9
)
achieved this by first training a reward model on human preference pairs
(
80
;
58
;
43
;
5
;
54
;
5
)
, and then using reinforcement learning
(
54
)
to optimize the LLM for reward maximization, usually with a KL-divergence penalty to regularize deviation from a reference policy
(
43
)
. Despite the widespread success, RLHF’s multi-stage process suffers from complexity, instability, and high computational cost
(
56
;
20
)
. Addressing these challenges, Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)
(
49
)
emerged as a compelling alternative, offering a significant simplification by analytically deriving a direct policy loss. This loss allows for direct optimization on pairwise preference data
(x,y_{w},y_{l})
, bypassing explicit reward modeling. Its mechanism implicitly enforces KL regularization by comparing the policy to a reference model (
\pi_{\text{ref}}
), making the loss dependent on the log-likelihood margin
\Delta_{\theta}-\Delta_{\text{ref}}
. DPO has gained widespread adoption due to its stability, efficiency, and strong empirical performance
(
49
;
18
;
78
;
73
;
50
)
.

While DPO is simple and effective, its reliance on a reference model introduces a tension that has led to two seemingly opposing lines of work. One argues that the reference should be removed due to a
training–inference mismatch

(
6
;
40
)
: DPO optimizes a reference-relative margin (e.g.,
\Delta_{\theta}-\Delta_{\mathrm{ref}}
), whereas inference depends on
absolute
log-likelihoods with no reference. Empirically, after DPO alignment, the agreement between the implicit-reward ordering and the likelihood ordering is only
\sim
50%, motivating reference-free objectives that directly optimize the inference metric and often outperform DPO
(
40
)
. The other line takes the opposite view: the reference can be beneficial when strengthened or made adaptive. Updating
\pi_{\text{ref}}
alongside the policy helps mitigate over-optimization
(
21
;
22
)
, and employing a stronger or better-matched reference improves stability and final performance, sometimes surpassing reference-free methods
(
38
;
3
;
44
)
. This leaves a practical dilemma: removing
\pi_{\text{ref}}
eliminates metric mismatch and can enhance performance, whereas retaining
\pi_{\text{ref}}
improves training stability and can also enhance performance.

To mitigate the tension between reference-based and reference-free direct alignment, we propose a unifying principle: the proximal stability offered by the reference model should be applied conditionally. We argue that the reference provides a constructive signal for stable learning only when it aligns with the preference data. When it is pessimistic (
\Delta_{\mathrm{ref}}<0
), its stabilizing pull becomes a misleading bias, making the learning objective unnecessarily difficult and causing the gradient to attenuate even when the absolute ordering (
\Delta_{\theta}
) is still incorrect. We term this effect
premature satisfaction
. Prior approaches either accept this bias (standard DPO;
49
) or discard potentially useful reference signal altogether (reference-free methods;
73
;
40
).

To realize this principle, we introduce
Hybrid-DPO
(
HyPO
), a minimalist and efficient modification to the DPO objective.
HyPO
behaves identically to DPO when the reference is helpful (optimistic or neutral) but treats a pessimistic reference as neutral (
\Delta_{\mathrm{ref}}=0
). This is achieved with a minor change to the DPO loss: the log-likelihood margin
\Delta_{\theta}-\Delta_{\mathrm{ref}}
becomes
\Delta_{\theta}-\max\{0,\Delta_{\mathrm{ref}}\}
. As illustrated in Figure
1
, standard DPO (a) prematurely attenuates the learning signal on pessimistic examples; typical reference-free method (b) discard the reference signal entirely; our proposed
HyPO
(c) uses the stabilizing reference where it helps and nullifies it where it is detrimental, thereby continuing to push the policy toward the correct absolute ordering.

We conduct a comprehensive empirical evaluation on preference datasets, aligning both base and instruction-tuned variants of two widely-used LLM family
(
28
;
1
)
. Our proposed method,
HyPO
, demonstrates superior performance on general instruction-following benchmarks like AlpacaEval 2.0
(
33
)
and Arena-Hard-v0.1
(
32
)
. Notably, these results translate to a 41.2% average relative improvement over the original DPO. Further analyses and ablations corroborate the robustness of our approach, showing that
HyPO
maintains its advantage when scaling to larger models and transferring to different datasets, all while preserving performance on a wide array of downstream tasks. These findings empirically validate our central thesis: conditionally debiasing the pessimistic reference signal, rather than discarding it entirely, provides a more robust and effective path toward stable, high-performing direct preference alignment.

Related Work

We situate
HyPO
within the off-policy branch of RLHF: methods trained on fixed human preference pairs without on-policy rollouts. Accordingly, we do not compare to on-policy RLHF
(
9
;
55
)
, and instead focus on recent direct preference alignment (DPO-style) objectives and extensions, underscoring
HyPO
’s distinct features.

Manipulating the Reference Signal.

DPO’s reliance on
\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
has inspired a spectrum of solutions. One direction focuses on refining the reference: performance is sensitive to reference choice and strength
(
38
;
3
)
, leading to Trust-Region DPO (TR-DPO)
(
21
)
, which dynamically updates
\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
during training to keep it aligned with the policy. RainbowPO
(
77
)
mixes the standard reference with an idealized constant-margin policy. Pre-DPO
(
44
)
further refines the reference model’s role as an implicit sample reweighter. At the other extreme, reference-free methods like RRHF
(
73
)
, SLiC-HF
(
78
)
, CPO
(
66
)
, ORPO
(
24
)
, and SimPO
(
40
)
eliminate the reference to directly optimize absolute log-likelihoods, addressing the
training–inference mismatch
at the cost of removing a stabilizing prior. The core problem of reference mismatch has also been identified in other modalities; for example, MaPO
(
57
)
removes the reference to improve multimodal alignment.
HyPO
offers a principled middle ground: it keeps the DPO-style logistic loss, but clips the reference margin, replacing
\Delta_{\mathrm{ref}}
with
\max\{0,\Delta_{\mathrm{ref}}\}
. This removes pessimistic anchoring while preserving proximality when the reference is non-pessimistic.

Explicit Reweighting and Robust Optimization.

A parallel line of work makes the reference-induced sample weighting explicit to improve training dynamics and robustness. Several distributionally robust DPO variants (e.g., rDPO
(
8
)
, Dr.DPO
(
64
)
, WDPO
(
67
)
) reweight examples to minimize the impact of label noise
(
70
;
71
;
72
;
69
)
or worst-case distributional shifts. Others use data-driven signals; for instance,
\beta
-DPO
(
65
)
dynamically adjusts the temperature
\beta
based on data quality, while WPO
(
79
)
reweights pairs to better simulate on-policy optimization from off-policy data. Some methods operate at a finer granularity, such as TDPO
(
75
)
and TIS-DPO
(
36
)
, which apply importance sampling at the token level. While these methods all leverage reweighting, their criteria are typically noise, data quality, or on-policy correction. In contrast,
HyPO
gates the reference contribution by the reference model’s own pessimism: when
\Delta_{\mathrm{ref}}\geq 0
it behaves exactly like DPO, and when
\Delta_{\mathrm{ref}}<0
it reduces to an absolute margin update on those pairs.

Alternative Objective Formulations.

Beyond manipulating the reference or loss weights, several methods alter the structure of the DPO objective.
f
-DPO generalizes DPO beyond reverse-KL to broader
f
-divergences
(
61
)
. R-DPO diagnoses and mitigates length biases within the DPO objective
(
45
)
, and AlphaDPO adaptively sets a data-dependent reward margin
(
63
)
.
Techniques such as DPO-Shift
(
68
)
and BDPO
(
7
)
modify the loss function to bound or rebalance the influence of the rejected response, while FocalPO
(
37
)
adds a modulating factor to focus on correctly ranked ones. Anchored methods like APO
(
18
)
retain the DPO form but add explicit constraints to control the absolute likelihood drift of chosen or rejected responses.

HyPO
differs in where it acts: it preserves DPO’s gradient form (same logistic weighting and
\beta
) but modifies the argument of the loss to
\Delta_{\theta}-\tilde{\Delta}{\mathrm{ref}}
with
\tilde{\Delta}{\mathrm{ref}}=\max\{0,\Delta_{\mathrm{ref}}\}
, thereby reallocating gradient mass on pessimistic pairs without introducing an extra multiplicative weight.

3
Mitigating Mismatch in Direct Preference Alignment

To fully appreciate the design trade-offs in modern alignment methods, we briefly recall the KL-regularized RLHF principle
(
9
;
80
)
that underpins them. We begin by considering a distribution of prompts
\mathcal{D}_{p}
from which prompts
x
are sampled, and let
\pi_{\theta}
be the trainable policy initialized from a supervised policy
\pi_{\text{SFT}}
. In KL-regularized policy optimization (e.g., PPO;
54
), a canonical KL-regularized objective used in practice is:

\max_{\pi_{\theta}}\;\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D}_{p},\,y\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)}\big[r_{\phi}(x,y)\big]\;-\;\tau\,\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D}_{p}}\!\big[D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)\;\|\;\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)\big)\big],

(1)

where
r_{\phi}
is a reward model trained from human preferences,
\pi_{\text{ref}}
is a fixed reference (typically
\pi_{\text{SFT}}
), and
\tau>0
controls the regularization strength.
For each
x
,
\pi_{\theta}^{\star}
denotes the optimal policy, the per-context optimum of equation
1
has the Gibbs form
(
46
;
3
)
:

\pi_{\theta}^{\star}(y\mid x)=\frac{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\,\exp\!\big(r_{\phi}(x,y)/\tau\big)}{\sum_{y^{\prime}}\pi_{\text{ref}}(y^{\prime}\mid x)\,\exp\!\big(r_{\phi}(x,y^{\prime})/\tau\big)},

(2)

which exposes the role of the KL term: it anchors
\pi_{\theta}
near
\pi_{\text{ref}}
, making updates stable and discouraging distributional drift that could exploit reward misspecification
(
53
;
54
)
.

Direct Preference Optimization
(DPO;
49
) circumvents explicit reward modeling and reinforcement learning by reformulating the objective for an offline setting, directly optimizing pairwise preferences
(x,y^{+},y^{-})
where
y^{+}\succ y^{-}
under a Bradley–Terry likelihood
(
5
)
. We define the policy and reference margins as:

\displaystyle\Delta_{\theta}(x,y^{+},y^{-})

\displaystyle=\log\pi_{\theta}(y^{+}\mid x)-\log\pi_{\theta}(y^{-}\mid x),

(3)

\displaystyle\Delta_{\mathrm{ref}}(x,y^{+},y^{-})

\displaystyle=\log\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y^{+}\mid x)-\log\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y^{-}\mid x).

DPO minimizes the logistic loss:

\mathcal{L}_{{\text{DPO}}}=\mathbb{E}_{(x,y^{+},y^{-})}\!\left[-\log\sigma\!\Big(\beta\big(\Delta_{\theta}-\Delta_{\mathrm{ref}}\big)\Big)\right]=\mathbb{E}_{(x,y^{+},y^{-})}\!\left[\log\!\Big(1+\exp\!\big(-\beta(\Delta_{\theta}-\Delta_{\mathrm{ref}})\big)\Big)\right],

(4)

where
\beta=1/\tau
. Under the Bradley–Terry model, the first-order optimality condition shares the Gibbs structure of KL-regularized policy optimization
(
49
)
and satisfies:

\log\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)-\log\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)=\beta\,r^{\star}(x,y)-\log Z(x).

(5)

This follows by taking logarithms of the KL-regularized optimum in Eq.
2
:

\pi^{\star}(y\mid x)\ \propto\ \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)\,\exp\!\big(r^{\star}(x,y)/\beta\big),

(6)

where
r^{\star}
is a preference-consistent reward and
Z(x)
the partition function. This makes explicit how DPO preserves proximity to
\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
without explicit RL.
Differentiating Eq.
4
with respect to the policy margin
\Delta_{\theta}
yields the per-example gradient-weight form:

\displaystyle\nabla_{\theta}\ell_{\mathrm{DPO}}=-\beta\,\underbrace{\sigma\!\big(-\beta(\Delta_{\theta}-\Delta_{\mathrm{ref}})\big)}_{w_{\mathrm{DPO}}\in(0,1)}\,\nabla_{\theta}\Delta_{\theta}.

(7)

Although the introduction of the reference policy anchors the policy
\pi_{\theta}
near
\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
via the relative margin
(\Delta_{\theta}-\Delta_{\mathrm{ref}})
serving as the gradient weight
(
44
)
in Eq.
7
, thereby providing stability and suppressing distributional drift, it simultaneously introduces a
training–inference metric mismatch

(
6
;
40
)
. We refer to this side effect as
premature satisfaction
.

Premature Satisfaction.

Let the scaled relative margin be:

z=\beta\big(\Delta_{\theta}-\Delta_{\mathrm{ref}}\big),\qquad w_{\mathrm{DPO}}=\sigma(-z)=\frac{1}{1+e^{z}},\quad z\geq 0\ \Rightarrow\ w_{\mathrm{DPO}}\leq e^{-z}.

(8)

Hence, from Eq.
7
we obtain:

\bigl\|\nabla_{\theta}\ell_{\mathrm{DPO}}\bigr\|=\beta\,\sigma(-z)\,\bigl\|\nabla_{\theta}\Delta_{\theta}\bigr\|\ \leq\ \beta\,e^{-z}\,\bigl\|\nabla_{\theta}\Delta_{\theta}\bigr\|.

When the reference is pessimistic on a pair (
\Delta_{\mathrm{ref}}<0
), even if the current policy is still wrong in absolute terms (
\Delta_{\theta}<0
), as soon as the relative margin becomes positive (
\Delta_{\theta}-\Delta_{\mathrm{ref}}>0
), the gradient shrinks exponentially, even though the policy remains wrong in absolute terms (
\Delta_{\theta}<0
).

For example, take
\beta=1
,
\Delta_{\mathrm{ref}}=-3
, and
\Delta_{\theta}=-1
. Then
\Delta_{\theta}-\Delta_{\mathrm{ref}}=2
, we have

w_{\mathrm{DPO}}=\sigma(-2)\approx 0.119,\bigl\|\nabla_{\theta}\ell_{\mathrm{DPO}}\bigr\|\approx 0.119\,\bigl\|\nabla_{\theta}\Delta_{\theta}\bigr\|.

As illustrated by the lower-left region of the heatmap in Figure
1
(a) (i.e.,
\Delta_{\theta}-\Delta_{\mathrm{ref}}>0
), the weight
w_{\mathrm{DPO}}
decays rapidly; when
\Delta_{\mathrm{ref}}<0
, this low-weight band still covers many points with
\Delta_{\theta}<0
.

Premature satisfaction
reveals the tension between stability (proximality) and mismatch: while DPO suppresses drift, it may also systematically attenuate the training signal of examples that disagree with
\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
yet remain substantially wrong.

Reference-free Alignment.
A natural reaction is to remove the reference to obtain an unbiased signal based on the absolute correctness of
\Delta_{\theta}
, aligned with inference-time likelihoods. Reference-free objectives (e.g., SimPO;
40
) optimize the absolute margin:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{abs}}=\mathbb{E}_{(x,y^{+},y^{-})}\Big[\;\ell_{\text{abs}}(\Delta_{\theta})\;\Big],\qquad\ell_{\text{abs}}(\Delta_{\theta})=\log\!\big(1+\exp(-\beta\,\Delta_{\theta})\big),

(9)

with gradient:

\frac{\partial\ell_{\text{abs}}}{\partial\Delta_{\theta}}=-\beta\,\sigma(-\beta\Delta_{\theta}),\qquad\nabla_{\theta}\ell_{\text{abs}}=-\beta\,\sigma(-\beta\Delta_{\theta})\,\nabla_{\theta}\Delta_{\theta}.

(10)

Compared to Eq.
7
, attenuation now depends only on the policy’s own performance: pairs with
\Delta_{\theta}<0
inevitably receive large gradients until
\Delta_{\theta}
crosses zero, directly aligning the training signal with the inference-time objective (increasing
\log\pi_{\theta}(y^{+}\mid x)
and decreasing
\log\pi_{\theta}(y^{-}\mid x)
). However, Eq.
9
discards the very mechanism that implemented proximality in Eq.
4
and Eq.
5
. Although recent work suggests that RLHF
(
10
;
42
)
does not typically induce substantial parameter drift, removing this proximality nonetheless markedly reduces the robustness
(
44
;
38
)
of reference-free alignment.

Direct Preference Optimization with Better Reference.

Another approach argues that we should improve reference model to enhance DPO framework. This idea motivates using a stronger reference policy within the DPO framework
(
38
;
3
;
44
)
. The premise is that a more accurate reference margin,
\Delta_{\mathrm{ref}}
, makes the relative margin
(\Delta_{\theta}-\Delta_{\mathrm{ref}})
a better learning signal, which should improve optimization.

While often effective, these methods only modify the reference policy
\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
and leave the core DPO loss unchanged. Consequently, the phenomenon we term
premature satisfaction
persists whenever the reference is pessimistic on a pair (i.e.,
\Delta_{\mathrm{ref}}<0
). To quantify this, we compute the sequence-level likelihood margin in Eq. equation
3
under three Llama-3-8B–based references (Base, SFT, and a SimPO-aligned model) on the UltraFeedback training split
(
13
)
, and report (i) the density of
\Delta_{\mathrm{ref}}
and (ii) summary statistics (full settings and results in Appendix
A
). Stronger references are indeed pessimistic less often and shift the distribution of
\Delta_{\mathrm{ref}}
to the right; yet a substantial fraction, approximately
45\%
, of pairs remains in the pessimistic region even for the SimPO-aligned reference, which is designed to mitigate such mismatch. This imposes a practical ceiling on the “better reference” strategy: it improves stability and overall performance, but the
training–inference mismatch
persists wherever
\Delta_{\mathrm{ref}}<0
.

3.1
Hybrid-DPO

In this section, we introduce
Hybrid-DPO
(
HyPO
), an approach designed to mitigate the
premature satisfaction
problem while preserving the stability of original DPO
(
49
)
. The stability benefit of DPO, inherited from KL-regularized RLHF (Eq.
6
), relies on the assumption that the reference policy
\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
provides a constructive learning signal. We argue that this KL-induced proximality should be
conditional
: When the reference is optimistic or neutral (
\Delta_{\mathrm{ref}}\geq 0
), it provides a valid anchor for stable, relative improvement, as the DPO objective effectively preserves proximity to the reference (Eq.
5
). However, when the reference is pessimistic (
\Delta_{\mathrm{ref}}<0
), it acts as a misleading prior that causes
premature satisfaction
; as analyzed in our discussion of the DPO gradient, the weight
w_{\mathrm{DPO}}
in Eq.
7
attenuates even when the absolute margin
\Delta_{\theta}
remains negative.
HyPO
realizes this principle by clipping the reference margin: it preserves DPO’s relative objective (relative margin,
(\Delta_{\theta}-\Delta_{\mathrm{ref}})
) for reliable pairs but nullifies the reference’s influence for pessimistic ones, compelling the model to optimize the absolute objective (absolute margin,
\Delta_{\theta}
) instead.

Objective.

Formally, for a given threshold
\gamma\geq 0
(with
\gamma=0
by default), we modify the DPO objective by replacing the reference margin
\Delta_{\mathrm{ref}}
with its clipped, non-negative counterpart:

\widetilde{\Delta}_{\mathrm{ref}}=\max\{\Delta_{\mathrm{ref}},\gamma\},\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{HyPO}}=\mathbb{E}\left[\log\left(1+\exp\big(-\beta(\Delta_{\theta}-\widetilde{\Delta}_{\mathrm{ref}})\big)\right)\right].

(11)

The gradient of the per-example loss retains the DPO structure, weighted by the modified margin:

\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{HyPO}}=-\beta\cdot\underbrace{\sigma\big(-\beta(\Delta_{\theta}-\widetilde{\Delta}_{\mathrm{ref}})\big)}_{w_{\mathrm{HyPO}}}\cdot\nabla_{\theta}\Delta_{\theta}.

(12)

By construction,
HyPO
coincides with DPO on
non-pessimistic
samples (
\Delta_{\mathrm{ref}}\!\geq\!\gamma
), i.e., its per-example weight in Eq.
12
equals that of DPO in Eq.
7
(
w_{\mathrm{HyPO}}\!=\!w_{\mathrm{DPO}}
), thereby preserving proximal stability where the reference is reliable. On pessimistic samples (
\Delta_{\mathrm{ref}}\!<\!\gamma
), clipping nullifies the misleading anchor and restores an absolute–margin–driven update, pointwise dominating the reference-free weight
w_{\mathrm{abs}}:=\sigma(-\beta\Delta_{\theta})
induced by Eq.
10
(
w_{\mathrm{HyPO}}\!\geq\!w_{\mathrm{abs}}
), thus preventing premature attenuation. An intuitive visualization is shown in Figure
1
(c).

For a globally smooth objective, we can replace the hard maximum with a softplus function
(
17
)
. The smoothed reference margin is defined as:

\widetilde{\Delta}_{\mathrm{ref}}=\gamma+\frac{1}{\alpha}\log\!\big(1+\exp(\alpha(\Delta_{\mathrm{ref}}-\gamma))\big),\quad\alpha>0,

(13)

where
\alpha
controls the smoothness (
\alpha\to\infty
recovers Eq.
11
). This simple, smoothed formulation allows
HyPO
to be implemented as a plug-in modification to the standard DPO loss.

Effectiveness of
HyPO
.
To quantify how effectively the
HyPO
objective mitigates the

compared to DPO
(

, we track two key metrics on the evaluation set that are closely aligned with inference-time performance. The first, the

\operatorname{Agree}@t:=\Pr[\Delta_{\theta}^{(t)}>0]
(with
\Delta_{\theta}
the absolute policy margin as in Eq.

), it measures the fraction of evaluation pairs whose absolute likelihood ordering matches the desired preference, directly mirroring the inference-time decision rule.
Further, we use a more targeted

, which tracks the average absolute margin,
\mathbb{E}[\Delta_{\theta}\mid\Delta_{\mathrm{ref}}<0]
, on the subset where the (fixed) reference policy is pessimistic. This metric isolates the challenging cases that trigger

in DPO and precisely measures
HyPO
’s ability to improve learning on them. Further details on metric definitions are provided in Appendix

(
33
)
. For models
i
(row) and

(column), the entry
W_{i,j}:=100\times\Pr[\text{model }i\succ\text{model }j]

4.1
.

. In this run, the final agreement rate increases from

for DPO to

for

A key advantage of
HyPO
is that it keeps a DPO-like form and is therefore compatible with enhancements for the DPO. In our practical implementation, we leverage this compatibility in two ways. First, we adopt a better reference
(
38
;
44
)
by using a pre-aligned, same-family model instead of the SFT default. Second, we apply a uniform home advantage margin
h
to impose stronger learning pressure, leading to the final objective:
\Delta_{\theta}\;-\;\max\{\Delta_{\mathrm{ref}},\,\gamma\}\;-\;h.

We do not use length normalization
(
45
)
due to its hyperparameter sensitivity.

5
Conclusion

In this paper, we introduced
Hybrid-DPO (HyPO)
to address
premature satisfaction
, a critical failure in Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) where a pessimistic reference model down-weights learning signals.
HyPO
’s simple conditional mechanism, clipping the reference margin at zero, corrects this bias while retaining DPO’s inherent stability. Empirically,
HyPO
consistently and significantly outperforms both various DPO and strong reference-free baselines across a range of models and benchmarks, proving robust and scalable without an additional “alignment tax”. Our work provides evidence that direct preference alignment could be enhanced by conditionally debiasing the reference signal, rather than discarding it, thereby achieving a more effective and stable compromise.

6
Limitations and Future Work

Our approach aims to
mitigate
rather than fully resolve the training-inference mismatch. By design,
HyPO
retains the reference-based behavior on optimistic pairs to preserve training stability and leverage better reference models, meaning the training–inference mismatch persists for this subset. Furthermore, our method assumes general label reliability; in cases of severe label noise where the reference model correctly disagrees with a wrong label,
HyPO
enforces a strong learning signal and may arguably amplify noise compared to DPO. Finally, while we prioritized a minimalist, parameter-free clipping mechanism, more sophisticated functional forms, such as adaptive clipping thresholds or learned gating mechanisms, remain promising directions for future research.

Appendix B
Analysis of DPO with SFT Loss

In this section, we explore a natural baseline: adding a supervised fine-tuning (SFT) loss on the chosen response to the standard DPO objective. This acts as a regularizer to ensure the model maintains high likelihood for preferred responses. The
DPO + SFT-on-chosen
objective is defined as:

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO + SFT-on-chosen}}=\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}+\lambda\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{SFT}}(y_{w}),}

(14)

where
\mathcal{L}_{\text{SFT}}(y_{w})=-\log\pi_{\theta}(y_{w}\mid x)
.

B.1
Hyperparameter Search

To ensure a fair comparison, we conducted a hyperparameter sweep for the coefficient
\lambda
over the range
\{0.01,0.03,0.1,0.3,0.5\}

We established
\lambda=0.5

\lambda
should not exceed the point where SFT dominates the preference optimization.
Empirically, we found that the optimal performance on AlpacaEval 2.0 was achieved at
\lambda=0.03
. We use this best-performing configuration for the definitive comparison below.

Appendix E
Evaluation Metric Definitions

To provide a fine-grained analysis of model performance during training, as shown in Figure
3
, we track two key inference-aligned metrics on a held-out evaluation set of preference pairs
(x,y_{w},y_{l})
, where
y_{w}\succ y_{l}
. These metrics are designed to directly quantify how effectively our proposed method,
HyPO
, mitigates the training-inference mismatch inherent in DPO. Both metrics are computed using the policy and reference log-likelihood margins,
\Delta_{\theta}
and
\Delta_{\mathrm{ref}}
, as formally defined in the main text in Equation
3
. We denote the policy margin at a given training checkpoint
t
as
\Delta_{\theta}^{(t)}
.

Absolute Agreement Rate.

The Absolute Agreement Rate provides a global measure of how well the policy’s log-likelihoods align with the ground-truth preferences. It is defined as the probability that the policy assigns a higher log-likelihood to the winning response
y_{w}
than the losing response
y_{l}
:

\operatorname{Agree}@t:=\Pr_{(x,y_{w},y_{l})}\left[\Delta_{\theta}^{(t)}(x,y_{w},y_{l})>0\right].

At inference time, a model’s performance depends on its
absolute
log-likelihoods, as the reference model is not used. This metric therefore directly reflects the desired inference-time behavior. A higher agreement rate signifies that the policy is more accurately ranking responses in absolute terms, indicating a successful reduction of the training-inference mismatch.

Absolute Margin on the Pessimistic Subset.

This metric is a targeted diagnostic designed to measure performance on the specific subset of data where DPO is most prone to failure. We first define the
pessimistic subset
as all evaluation pairs where the reference model incorrectly prefers the losing response:

\mathcal{D}_{\text{pessimistic}}:=\{(x,y_{w},y_{l})\mid\Delta_{\mathrm{ref}}(x,y_{w},y_{l})<0\}.

The metric is then the average policy margin,
\Delta_{\theta}
, computed exclusively over this subset:

\text{Margin}_{\text{pessimistic}}@t:=\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_{w},y_{l})\in\mathcal{D}_{\text{pessimistic}}}\left[\Delta_{\theta}^{(t)}(x,y_{w},y_{l})\right].

This metric directly probes the phenomenon of
premature satisfaction
. For pairs in
\mathcal{D}_{\text{pessimistic}}
, DPO’s learning signal weakens as soon as the policy margin
\Delta_{\theta}
surpasses the negative reference margin
\Delta_{\mathrm{ref}}
, even if
\Delta_{\theta}
itself is still negative. A strong and increasing positive value for this metric demonstrates that the policy is successfully applying corrective pressure and overcoming the misleading signal from the pessimistic reference, a core goal of
HyPO
.

Appendix F

\alpha

In Eq.
13
we introduced a smooth variant of the clipped reference margin using a softplus transformation,
with smoothness parameter
\alpha
:

\alpha
yield a smoother transition, while
\alpha\to\infty
recovers the hard

\max\{\Delta_{\mathrm{ref}},\gamma\}
