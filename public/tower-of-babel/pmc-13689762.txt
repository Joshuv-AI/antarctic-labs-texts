Overview of the Case of Presumed Fatal Bronchospasm from Adenosine Administered for Assessment of Fractional Flow Reserve (FFR)

History of Presentation

A 79-year-old man was referred for coronary angiography to assess exertional chest pain, consistent with Canadian Cardiovascular Society grade II-III angina. The chest pain was associated with shortness of breath and was alleviated by glyceryl trinitrate. In view of his comorbidities, he was referred directly for invasive coronary angiography. While awaiting the procedure, he developed worsening angina occurring at rest, and his procedure was expedited accordingly.

Take-Home Messages

•Although extremely rare, adenosine carries a risk of fatal bronchospasm, and alternative hyperemic agents or nonhyperemic physiological indices should be used preferentially in patients with known obstructive airway disease.

Physical Examination

Formal physical examination is not routinely performed before elective coronary angiography, though the patient was comfortable without distress or wheeze. The resting vital signs were a heart rate of 75 beats/min, blood pressure of 170/93 mm Hg, and peripheral oxygen saturation of 97% on room air.

Past Medical History

He had a prior coronary angiogram 10 years previously demonstrating significant disease in the first diagonal and obtuse marginal arteries with mild disease elsewhere. His medical history was notable for type 2 diabetes mellitus, obesity (body mass index of 41 kg/m2), hypertension, hyperlipidemia, atrial flutter, and a diagnosis of asthma and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease for which he used regular bronchodilators, including salbutamol, budesonide, and formoterol.

Differential Diagnosis

The primary differential diagnosis was obstructive coronary artery disease given multiple cardiovascular risk factors and evidence of branch vessel disease on previous angiography. Other differential diagnoses included pulmonary embolism and gastroesophageal reflux disease.

Investigations

Laboratory tests performed 2 days before the angiogram demonstrated normal hemoglobin, electrolytes, serum creatinine, and white blood cell count. The resting electrocardiogram showed normal sinus rhythm. Transthoracic echocardiography performed 2 years prior demonstrated moderate right ventricular systolic impairment as evidenced by a reduced fractional area change. Pulmonary artery pressure could not be estimated because of an incomplete tricuspid regurgitation jet envelope. Left ventricular systolic function was preserved (left ventricular ejection fraction of 55%-60%). There were no significant valvular lesions. Spirometry performed 6 years prior demonstrated a forced expiratory volume in 1 second of 3.5 L (66% of predicted), a forced vital capacity of 4.8 L (68% of predicted), and a reduced diffusing capacity for carbon monoxide (71%), consistent with airflow limitation and impaired gas transfer.

Management

The patient underwent urgent outpatient coronary angiography, which demonstrated the pre-existing lesions in the obtuse marginal and diagonal arteries. In addition, a moderate-to-severe lesion was identified in the proximal left anterior descending artery (Figure 1). Given uncertainty regarding its hemodynamic significance, pressure wire assessment was performed to determine whether revascularization was indicated.

Coronary Angiography

Cranial projection of coronary angiography demonstrating a moderate-to-severe stenosis in the proximal left anterior descending artery.

After systemic heparinization, an Abbott PressureWire X wire was advanced to the distal left anterior descending artery. The resting full-cycle ratio was 0.91, which was above the accepted threshold of ≤0.89 for hemodynamic significance. However, given the well-recognized discordance between nonhyperemic pressure indices and fractional flow reserve (FFR) in approximately 20% of cases, particularly for focal lesions,1 assessment of FFR was performed for confirmation. This “hybrid strategy” of physiological assessment is supported by a recent American Heart Association scientific statement.2 Intravenous adenosine was administered for FFR evaluation, and this resulted in significant atrioventricular node block (Figure 2), necessitating cessation of the infusion. Shortly afterward, the patient reported difficulty breathing and developed audible wheezing, progressive hypoxemia, and central cyanosis.

The patient progressed to cardiac arrest. Intravenous adrenaline was administered in accordance with Advanced Cardiovascular Life Support guidelines, and cardiopulmonary resuscitation was initiated. Despite nebulized salbutamol, oxygen saturation remained between 50% and 60% throughout, with a nadir of 42%, and breath sounds were markedly diminished on auscultation. Following urgent endotracheal intubation with suxamethonium and rocuronium, the patient was found to be extremely difficult to ventilate, requiring airway pressures of 30-40 cm H2O to achieve tidal volumes of only 100-150 mL. A temporary transvenous pacing wire was inserted via the right femoral vein, achieving electrical but not mechanical capture. Resuscitation efforts were ceased after approximately 25 minutes.

Because of the rapid clinical deterioration and cardiac arrest, check angiography was not performed, as there was no indication of an acute ischemic event such as ST-segment changes or chest pain.

Outcome and Follow-Up

The patient died on the procedure table. The cause of death was attributed to presumed fatal adenosine-associated bronchospasm. An autopsy was not performed; however, the combination of a plausible biologic mechanism, the temporal relationship between adenosine administration and clinical deterioration, and the clinical findings described above provide reasonable grounds for this attribution.

Discussion

Intracoronary physiological assessment is recommended for intermediate coronary lesions to guide revascularization decisions, with supporting evidence from multiple randomized trials incorporated into current clinical guidelines. FFR has traditionally been the gold standard technique, requiring administration of a coronary vasodilating agent to induce maximal hyperemia. The most commonly used agent is adenosine, although alternatives including regadenoson and sodium nitroprusside have also been described.

Adenosine exerts its physiological effects via 4 receptor subtypes (A1, A2A, A2B, and A3), with the highest affinity for the A1 and A2A receptors.3 Induction of hyperemia is primarily mediated through A2A receptor activation, leading to coronary vasodilation.3,4 Clinical indications for adenosine use include acute treatment of supraventricular tachycardias, stress perfusion cardiac magnetic resonance imaging, assessment of accessory conduction pathways, and FFR measurement.

Commonly reported side effects of adenosine include chest pain, dyspnea, and arrhythmias.5 Given its very short half-life, these effects are typically transient and self-limiting. Acute bronchospasm is a recognized side effect, attributed to activation of bronchial A2B receptors causing mast cell degranulation and bronchoconstriction, although this finding relates principally to inhaled adenosine, whereas intravenous adenosine in patients with mild to moderate asthma has not been shown to reliably provoke objective bronchospasm.3,6

Adenosine-based FFR is a safe procedure in the vast majority of cases, and a recent systematic review and meta-analysis reported an excellent overall safety profile. Although there were 2 deaths identified, these were attributable to wire-related coronary artery injury, and bronchospasm was reported in 0.2% of this series.7 Further, in a large retrospective series of 9,440 FFR procedures, bronchospasm was reported in 5 cases, with no deaths but 1 case of respiratory arrest in a patient with underlying chronic obstructive pulmonary disease.8

To our knowledge, this represents the first report of presumed fatal adenosine-associated bronchospasm during FFR testing in the published literature. The patient’s comorbidities likely contributed to the adverse clinical outcome. Intravenous adenosine is dosed at 140 μg/kg/min based on total body weight, resulting in larger absolute doses in patients with obesity. Furthermore, morbid obesity significantly reduces functional residual capacity. In the setting of reduced right ventricular systolic function, we propose that bronchospasm-induced dynamic hyperinflation, which resulted in acutely elevated pulmonary vascular resistance, precipitated acute cor pulmonale and hypoxemia, which would further stimulate endogenous adenosine release from the ischemic right ventricle, leading to a vicious cycle perpetuating bronchospasm and atrioventricular node block. Furthermore, suxamethonium may have contributed to bronchospasm via histamine release. This sequence of events could have culminated in electromechanical dissociation and pulseless electrical activity.

Aminophylline, a competitive, nonselective adenosine receptor antagonist, has been used successfully to reverse adenosine-induced bronchospasm.8,9 Intravenous aminophylline 50-250 mg can be administered by slow intravenous injection or infusion. In the present case, aminophylline was not available in the procedure room, and it was not known to the treating team that this was an effective reversal agent. Following this event, aminophylline has since been made readily available in our cardiac catheterization laboratories. Given that adenosine is used across multiple clinical settings, aminophylline should be readily available wherever adenosine is used—ideally in a prefilled syringe for rapid deployment.

Adenosine is listed under the Food and Drug Administration warnings for use in patients with bronchoconstriction or asthma. In our experience, and as reflected in the published literature, adenosine is widely used in patients with obstructive airway disease without serious adverse effects in the majority of cases. However, this case has led to a change in our practice. In patients at elevated risk—including those with asthma, chronic airway disease, or regular bronchodilator use—alternative approaches to coronary physiological assessment should be considered. Nonhyperemic pressure ratios do not require administration of a vasodilating agent and therefore eliminate the risks associated with adenosine. Noninvasive stress testing represents an additional adenosine-free alternative. Finally, alternative vasodilating agents can be used.

This case serves as an important reminder that clinicians must be aware of the potentially fatal consequences of adenosine use. Patients who are prone to bronchospasm should be informed of the specific risks associated with adenosine—including the possibility of severe bronchospasm and the extremely rare possibility of death—as part of the informed consent process.

Conclusions

Adenosine used for FFR measurement or in other clinical contexts carries a rare but potentially fatal risk of severe bronchospasm. This appears to be the first reported case of presumed fatal adenosine-associated bronchospasm during FFR testing. Extra caution is warranted in patients with pre-existing airway disease, and alternatives to adenosine should be considered in high-risk individuals. Aminophylline is an effective reversal agent and should be immediately available in all settings where adenosine is used.
