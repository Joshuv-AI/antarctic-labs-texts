Bridging the Gap: How Endothelial-Adipocyte Cx43 Mediated Gap Junctions Could Revolutionize Adiposity Regulation
Authors: Teresa Vezza, Víctor M Víctor
Published: 2024

A Perspective on “Endothelial-Adipocyte Cx43 Mediated Gap Junctions Can Regulate Adiposity”

The current global obesity epidemic represents a major health crisis, contributing significantly to a rise in metabolic disorders, cardiovascular diseases, and general public health concerns.1 Adipose tissue, which is vital for energy storage and metabolic regulation, consists of more than just adipocytes; it includes a diverse array of cell types, many of which are integrated within a complex capillary network.2 These capillaries, predominantly composed of endothelial cells (ECs) and pericytes, are essential for the proper functioning of adipose tissue. Among them, capillary adipose endothelial cells (CaECs) play a pivotal role in the onset and progression of endothelial dysfunction linked to obesity.2

Capillaries differ significantly from arteries and veins, operating in a low-flow, low-pressure environment essential for the efficient exchange of gases and nutrients between blood and tissues. In adipose tissue, capillaries play a critical role in the uptake and transport of serum lipids to adipocytes, which is crucial for the correct storage of lipids and the prevention of lipotoxicity and oxidative stress. CaECs are particularly well suited to manage this lipid uptake due to their specific location and function within the capillary network. This specialization enables CaECs to play a central role in maintaining lipid homeostasis and overall metabolic health.

To perform these tasks effectively, CaECs must express a unique set of proteins that facilitate the efficient uptake and transport of lipids to nearby adipocytes. While the exact mechanisms behind the development of this specialized protein profile in CaECs remain largely unexplored, it is thought that their distinct environment—characterized by partial pericyte coverage and exposure to the interstitial space—plays an important role. The direct interaction between CaECs and adipocytes is likely to underlie their ability to manage lipid transport and storage efficiently.3

Gap junctions, composed of connexin proteins, are vital for cell-to-cell communication, allowing the movement of ions, metabolites, and signaling molecules between cells. Among these proteins, connexin 43 (Cx43) has been particularly well studied and is known to play a critical role in various physiological processes.4 In the vascular system, Cx43-mediated gap junctions connect endothelial cells with smooth muscle cells, significantly influencing vasodilation and overall vascular function.4

In the context of CaECs, Cx43 gap junctions are believed to be pivotal in mediating communication between endothelial cells and adipocytes. These junctions facilitate the transfer of metabolic and signaling molecules, which can influence adipocyte differentiation, lipid metabolism, and the overall homeostasis of adipose tissue.4 This intercellular communication is vital for maintaining metabolic health and preventing conditions such as lipotoxicity and oxidative stress. Understanding the specific mechanisms and effects of Cx43-mediated gap junctions in CaECs could lead to new therapeutic approaches to obesity and related metabolic disorders.

In a recent issue of Function, Melissa A. Luse and colleagues5 have highlighted a novel mechanism by which CaECs regulate epididymal white adipose tissue (eWAT) and serum lipids through direct contact with adipocytes. This interaction is mediated by the gap junction protein Cx43, with specific phosphorylation at serine 368 playing a key role in controlling intercellular communication. Lipid treatments and high-fat diets (HFDs) particularly enhance phosphorylation at this site, leading to increased channel closure and reduced communication between endothelial cells and adipocytes. This undermined interaction is thought to contribute to the endothelial dysfunction associated with obesity.

Furthermore, single-cell RNA sequencing data reveal that key proteins involved in fatty acid handling, such as fatty acid binding protein 4 (FABP4) and glycosylphosphatidylinositol anchored high-density lipoprotein-binding protein 1 (GPIHBP1),6,7 are upregulated in capillaries in response to HFD. This upregulation, which occurs particularly in CaECs, underscores both their critical role in managing high lipid concentrations for adipocyte storage and their significant impact on lipid metabolism and insulin sensitivity. The direct contact between CaECs and adipocytes, along with increased lipid levels, would seem to prime CaECs to upregulate fatty acid machinery in response to metabolic stress.

For the first time, Melissa A. Luse et al.5 have characterized and described the heterocellular contact between CaECs and adipocytes. This interaction, which resembles the contact between arterial endothelial cells and smooth muscle cells observed in resistance arteries, suggests a regulatory function in adipose tissue similar to that of myoendothelial junctions (MEJs) in systemic blood pressure regulation.8 The aforementioned study shows that this heterocellular contact primarily occurs between endothelial cells and adipocytes, positioning CaECs as a crucial indicator of nutrient status, especially during metabolic stress.

Experimental evidence obtained in a co-culture transwell model further demonstrates that Fabp4 expression is enhanced by direct contact between human adipose microvascular endothelial cells (HAMECs) and adipocytes. FABP4, which binds long-chain fatty acids and other lipophilic molecules, is integral to the intracellular transport of fatty acids. Its expression is closely associated with obesity, insulin resistance, and inflammation, making it a significant focus of metabolic and cardiovascular research. The study in question also found that lipid treatment of HAMECs in direct contact with adipocytes significantly induced Fabp4 expression in adipocytes, highlighting the importance of physical coupling for effective lipid uptake and storage.5

Melissa A. Luse and coworkers also reported that phosphorylation of Cx43 at serine 368 is associated with reduced communication between CaECs and adipocytes, potentially contributing to both endothelial and adipocyte dysfunction in obesity. This link is supported by genetic models; for example, mice lacking EC Cx43 experienced worse metabolic outcomes than those with an enhanced channel opening when both groups underwent an obesogenic diet. Notably, the latter group was protected from the metabolic dysfunction induced by the HFD in their counterparts, indicating that EC Cx43 plays a regulatory role in eWAT adiposity through heterocellular communication.5

Evidence suggests that EC Cx43 communication is crucial under conditions of metabolic stress. Mice lacking EC Cx43 and on an HFD exhibit increased body mass, eWAT mass, circulating cholesterol and triglycerides, which confirms the importance of Cx43-mediated communication in maintaining lipid homeostasis. Future research should explore potential interactions between ECs and other tissues, such as the liver, skeletal muscle, and brown adipose tissue, to better understand systemic lipid regulation.

In conclusion, understanding the role of Cx43-mediated gap junctions in CaECs provides valuable insights for developing therapeutic strategies to target obesity and related metabolic disorders. It would be of interest if future studies focus on manipulating Cx43 communication pathways to create targeted interventions for weight management and metabolic health.
