Designing genetically stable multicopy gene constructs with the ChimeraUGEM web server
Authors: Moritz Burghardt, Alon Diament, Tamir Tuller
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractHigh expression of heterologous proteins is often achieved by integrating multiple copies of a gene into a host. However, such multicopy systems are prone to genetic instability due to homologous recombination between identical sequences. We present the multisequence ChimeraMap (MScMap), an algorithm for designing multiple synonymous coding sequences that minimizes recombination risk while maintaining high expression. MScMap extends the ChimeraMap framework by selecting diverse nucleotide blocks from a host genome to encode the target protein, balancing host adaptation and sequence dissimilarity. We introduce heuristics for block selection and concatenation to reduce long common substrings, a known driver of recombination. Our method outperforms a multi-objective evolutionary algorithm in both genetic stability and predicted expression across a wide range of human proteins while being significantly faster. We also show that MScMap can also be used to reduce sequence repeats within a single coding sequence. A web tool for single and multicopy coding sequence optimization is available online.

Introduction

In many biotechnological applications, the aim is to maximize the expression levels of target proteins, and various approaches have been developed to optimize transcriptional and translational rates. One common strategy is integrating multiple copies instead of a single copy of a target gene into a host organism’s genome. While this has increased yields in several host organisms, this relationship is not always linear and sometimes additional copies lead to a decrease in expression levels (Fig. 1A).

One of the challenges for these multicopy systems is their genetic instability, which can lead to variations in the copy number of a target gene over time. Since the production of the target protein presents a metabolic burden, selective pressure exists for decreasing copy number, causing a decline of the copy number in the host population over time [4]. One possible deletion mechanism is loop-out recombination (Fig. 1B), where recombination between repetitive sequences, such as different copies of the target gene, causes a loss of genetic material [5]. Multiple factors are known to predict the likelihood of recombination between genetic repeats. Various studies have reported a strong increase in recombination rate between two sequences with their homology length. For homologies up to a few hundred base pairs, Shen and Huang [6] and Khasanov et al.[7] report a linear relationship beyond a minimum homology length of 20–40 base pairs required for recombination in Escherichia coli and Bacillus subtilis. For homologies of thousands of base pairs, Michel and Ehrlich [8] and Fujitani et al.[9] find a super-linear relation. Therefore, to decrease the likelihood of intercopy recombination, copies of the target protein should have different coding sequences to avoid LCSs. Secondly, repetitive sequences are more likely to interact and recombine if they are physically close to each other. Chédin et al., Lovett et al., and Bi and Liu [10–12] have found recombination frequency to decrease exponentially with distance, where distance is measured as the number of base pairs separating the repeats. For example, an intervening sequence of 1 kb caused a drop of 99% in deletion frequencies in B. subtilis [10]. Therefore, recombination events are most likely between physically proximate copies or repetitive regions within a single coding sequence [13].

Apart from genetic stability, target genes should be adapted well to the host organism to achieve high expression. Depending on the host, different factors have been found to increase expression levels, including codon optimization [14], messenger RNA folding, avoidance/selection of different regulatory patterns [15], and the optimization of ribosomal traffic [16]. Many tools have been developed to optimize one [17] or multiple [18, 19] of these objectives for a single sequence, but few tools exist for the optimization of multiple sequences for multicopy systems. Terai et al.[20] presents an evolutionary algorithm (EA) to generate multiple coding sequences that are optimized regarding codon usage, common substring lengths, and hamming distance between gene copies. While these sequences will be less likely to recombine, their expression will be sub-optimal since they do not capture expression factors other than codon usage. Additionally, finding apt sequences with an EA is computationally expensive given the vast search space and the complex objective function [20].

This study proposes a method to design multiple coding sequences for the same protein based on the Chimera algorithms [21, 22]. The designed sets of sequences adhere to the gene expression rules of the host beyond codon usage, while avoiding large LCS between copies, for improved genetic stability (Fig. 1C). We provide a user-friendly webtool that generates one or multiple synonymous optimized sequences.

Materials and methods

The MScMap algorithm extends the Chimera approach to multiple sequences

The ChimeraMap (cMap) algorithm designs heterologous coding sequences by composing the target gene from codon blocks found in host genes [22]. This method can be applied to any sequenced host genome without needing additional information beyond gene annotations. To encode the target protein sequence, the cMap algorithm searches for the longest common prefix between the amino acid sequence of the target protein and the host proteome. It then copies the most common nucleotide sequence that encodes this amino acid prefix in the host genome and uses it to encode the block in the target protein. The search is then repeated for the suffix starting from the end of the previous block until the entire target protein is encoded.

The multi-sequence ChimeraMap algorithm (MScMap) extends the cMap algorithm by creating multiple nucleotide sequences that encode the target protein (Fig. 2/Algorithm 1). The algorithm shares most logic with the single-sequence version: target gene copies are iteratively composed of nucleotide blocks found in the reference genome that encode amino acid blocks in the target protein. In the single-sequence version, the engineered sequence is constructed using the most common nucleotide sequence in the reference genome that encodes the amino acid blocks. To achieve high differentiation within the engineered gene set, the multi-sequence version uses multiple, different, nucleotide blocks that encode the longest amino acid block and appear in the reference genome. The user can choose via the block_select parameter whether to use all nucleotide blocks for a given amino acid block or only the n_seqs most frequent ones, where n_seqs is the number of sequences to generate. If the number of engineered genes is larger than the number of nucleotide sequences to choose from, they are over-sampled.

Example of the MScMap algorithm for n_seqs = 4, min_blocks = 2. (A) Block selection for an example protein and three reference genes. (B) Concatenation of the selected blocks. Sequence blocks are colored according to their reference gene of origin.

Over-sampling of long nucleotide blocks can result in LCSs between the copies, increasing the risk of recombination. To address this issue, instead of only considering the longest amino acid blocks shared between the target protein and the host genome, shorter amino acid blocks can be used if encoded by a larger number of different nucleotide sequences, increasing differentiation between the engineered sequences. At every position, the largest amino acid block is found and iteratively shortened by one amino acid until the number of unique nucleotide blocks lies above a configurable parameter min_blocks or the block length reaches one. This parameter controls the weighting between the two objectives: a high min_blocks will increase differentiation between copies but reduce adaptation to the host genome and vice-versa. The block is not shortened if the shorter amino acid block is encoded by fewer unique nucleotide blocks than the previous, longer amino acid block.

Algorithm 1: multi-sequence ChimeraMap (MScMap). The LongestPrefix routine is the same as that defined in [21].

The engineered gene copies are obtained by concatenation of the selected nucleotide blocks. We use the following heuristic, which aims to avoid long homologies: sequences are composed by alternating between two strategies, one where blocks are assigned in “chunks,” and one where we cycle through blocks one by one. Since identical blocks in the first chunk-based approach will be adjacent in the array of sequences and distal from each other in the cyclic approach, this reduces the chance that identical blocks are repeatedly appended to the same sequences.

The cARS algorithm estimates the expression of a sequence

The Chimera average repetitive substring (cARS) algorithm measures the adaptiveness of a gene to a host genome [22]. It accomplishes this by identifying the longest substring that originates from each position in the sequence and also appears in a set of reference sequences, typically consisting of highly expressed genes from the host. The model then calculates the average substring length across all positions. This measure assumes that long repetitive substrings in the reference sequences have been selected and are optimal for the organism’s gene expression machinery and therefore are likely to induce high expression. The cARS model can predict expression of endogenous and heterologous genes, often outperforming CAI [21, 23]. To estimate the adaptiveness of multiple copies, we calculate their average cARS.

Genetic stability is estimated from the length of common substrings

Since homologous recombination strongly depends on long repetitive sequences, we use the longest common substring (LCS) as a measure of the genetic stability of the engineered gene sets, as defined in [20]. We calculate LCS by finding for each position of each gene the longest substring that appears at least twice in the gene set (this can be in the same gene).

An evolutionary algorithm is used as a benchmark

An EA that generates multiple copies of a gene, as introduced in [20], is implemented as a benchmark. The algorithm generates an initial population of N = 10 of sets of sequences where all sequences are equal to the (single-sequence) cMap solution for the target protein. Offspring populations are generated by introducing synonymous mutations, i.e. the change of one codon to a random synonymous one, ensuring that sequences always encode the target protein. Synonymous mutations occur with probability pm = 0.05, which was found optimal in [20]. Individuals from the parent and offspring population are selected for the next generation according to two objectives: the LCS and mean cARS of the respective gene set. The algorithm terminates after G = 100 generations. We used the NSGA-II algorithm, which prioritizes individuals that occupy sparsely populated regions in the objective space and thus creates a diverse set of solutions [24] and implemented it in the Pymoo multi-objective-optimization framework [25].

To compare this benchmark against MScMap, we used two established performance indicators for multi-objective optimization problems: the coverage between two sets or C-value, defined as the fraction of solutions in one set that is dominated by a solution in the other set and the hypervolume (Fig. 1C), which quantifies the size of the dominated space [26]. Hypervolumes were calculated relative to the reference point cARS = 10, LCS = 100.

Data sources

All genomes used were reference genomes from NCBI. Human protein sequences were downloaded from UniProt (Proteome ID UP000005640, single representative protein per gene dataset) [27]. To select highly expressed reference genes in the ChimeraUGEM webtool, protein abundances from PaxDb are used [28].

Results

The MScMap algorithm designs sets of highly expressed, genetically stable copies

The algorithm was applied to design sequence sets for two recombinant proteins of interest, human insulin and growth factor (Fig. 3A and B). High min_blocks values require copies to be composed of a larger number of different blocks and thereby lower the LCS, but as shorter nucleotide blocks from the host genome are used, these copy sets are associated with lower cARS, and vice-versa. Our results show that the min_blocks parameter is an effective way to tradeoff between the two objectives. Comparing the results for different set sizes, we observe that for a given cARS, larger sets have higher LCS. This can be expected as for large sets, nucleotide blocks are oversampled more often and some of the copies become more similar to each other. Additionally, we verified the effectiveness of our block concatenation heuristic (“alternating”) by comparing it to three benchmarks (Fig. 3C): one where sequences are composed by randomly choosing from all available nucleotide blocks and ones where only chunk-based or cyclic concatenation is used (see Algorithm 1). We find that alternating between the chunk-based and cyclic strategies significantly lowers LCS. Since for all concatenation heuristics sequences are composed of nucleotide blocks from the reference genome of the same length, they have almost identical cARS values.

MScMap outperforms generic optimization approaches

We compared the performance of MScMap against the performance of an EA that optimizes sets of synonymous coding sequences according to the two objectives. Figure 4A shows an example for human insulin, where all solutions generated by the MScMap algorithm dominate the EA population, i.e. achieve higher mean cARS for a given LCS.

(A) Comparison of objectives for sets of five sequences encoding human Insulin generated by the MScMap algorithm using min_blocks = 1, 2, 3, or 5 and an EA. (B–D) distribution of performance and runtime of solutions generated by MScMap and the EA for 100 randomly selected human proteins. For all results, P. pastoris reference genes were used.

To evaluate the performance of MScMap and the EA over many genes, we generated multicopy gene sets for 100 additional, randomly selected human proteins and calculated several performance metrics (Fig. 4B–D). We find that the size of the dominated objective space (hypervolume) is greater for MScMap compared to the EA for 95 of the 100 genes, even though we generated fewer solutions per gene for MScMap [7] compared to the EA [13]. Additionally, we evaluated how often each algorithm generated dominated solutions, i.e. solutions that had higher LCS than any other solution with equal or higher cARS or lower cARS than any other solution with equal or lower LCS. For each set of solutions for a gene generated by MScMap, we evaluated the fraction that was dominated by any of the solutions generated by the EA for the same human gene and vice versa (C-value). Only for three genes did the EA solution dominate any of the MScMap solutions. On the other hand, MScMap solutions dominated at least one EA solution for all genes. This demonstrates MScMap is not only able to generate strong solutions but also rarely generates “weak” solutions which can be easily dominated by the EA.

Additionally, the median runtime of the MScMap algorithm for the results shown above is >100× faster than the EA on the same hardware. We adjusted for the fact that the EA generates multiple solutions simultaneously, whereas MScMap generates them one-by-one, by calculating runtime per generated solution. As noted in [20], the most time-consuming step for the EA is evaluation of the objective function, particularly the LCS.

Our algorithm can improve the genetic stability of repeat proteins

We found that our extension of the ChimeraMap algorithm can also be used to reduce repeats within single coding sequences, likely benefitting their genetic stability. This is particularly relevant for proteins that are repetitive in their amino acid sequence, e.g. fibrous proteins like elastin or spiker-silks, which are of interest for recombinant expression [13, 29].

Since the default cMap algorithm (block_select = most_freq, min_blocks = 1) always uses the same nucleotide block for each occurrence of a repetitive amino acid motif, the optimized nucleotide sequence can contain long repeats (Table 1). By utilizing different nucleotide blocks to encode repetitive amino acid motifs (block_select = all), our extension of the ChimeraMap algorithm lowers the length of these repeats. Repeats can be shortened further by shortening the individual amino acid blocks by choosing a higher min_blocks value. Note that as in the case for multiple sequences, there is a trade-off with expression estimators like cARS—for example, for fibroin, the min_blocks = 1 variant has cARS of 12.7, whereas the min_blocks = 10 variant has cARS of 11.8.

All chimera algorithms are available as a webtool

We have developed a webtool (Fig. 5), available at https://www.cs.tau.ac.il/~tamirtul/CChimeraUGEM/MScMap.html, to make both the established [21, 22] and the multicopy Chimera algorithms available without requiring software download/installation by the user. The user specifies one or multiple target genes and reference genes. For convenience, we provide reference genomes for several model species. For these reference genes, the user can additionally specify whether to only use a configurable number of the most highly expressed genes, where expression levels are taken from PaxDb [28]. Results are provided as FASTA file format (for coding sequences optimized by ChimeraMap) or CSV files (for ChimeraARS scores).

Discussion

We introduced the MScMap algorithm, an algorithm that constructs multiple highly expressed and genetically stable coding sequences for heterologous expression. While previous tools exist for designing one [30] or multiple [20, 31] coding sequences for genetic stability, they all optimize indices based on codon usage frequencies. MScMap is based on the Chimera approach and can therefore capture longer gene expression codes. LCSs are avoided through two heuristics, one for selecting amino acid blocks that are encoded by multiple synonymous nucleotide blocks from the reference genome and one for concatenating these nucleotide blocks.

We show that our problem-specific heuristics outperform a generic optimization approach, an EA, across a wide range of target proteins while significantly reducing runtime. As shown in [20], the solutions generated by the EA can be improved by increasing the number of generations or population size. Therefore, it is likely that the performance gap between the MScMap and the EA can be somewhat narrowed, however, at additional computational cost.

Because codon choices are finite, MScMap cannot guarantee an upper bound for LCS. This poses a challenge for large copy numbers or target proteins that are highly repetitive in their amino acid sequence, as shown in Table 1. That said, we find that for the target proteins we tested, MScMap produced variant sets with LCS below typical thresholds required for homologous recombination (Figs 3A and B, and 4A).

Apart from the sequence optimization for multicopy systems, our algorithm can be useful for single-copy systems by generating multiple variants for a target gene that all have high cARS score. This can be helpful since most biological models, including cARS for gene expression, are only noisy estimates and therefore, finding highly expressed sequences in practice may require generating and experimentally testing multiple computationally optimized variants. Additionally, we show that MScMap improves the genetic stability of single sequences compared to the existing Chimera implementation by making use of all available nucleotide blocks and thus avoiding long repeats in the coding sequence.

We expect that genetic stability can be further improved by combining MScMap with orthogonal approaches, such as coupling the expression of the heterologous constructs to essential host genes [32], avoiding mutational hotspots within the host genome [33] or avoiding repeats outside of the coding regions by designing non-repetitive promoters and UTRs [34]. While most experimental studies focus on a single strategy, it will be interesting to measure their relative and combined impact. Additionally, to our knowledge, there exist no systematic characterization of genetic stability and gene expression for single loci vs multi loci integration, where integration at multiple loci may improve stability due to the larger distance between copies.
