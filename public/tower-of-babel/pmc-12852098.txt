IL-33 expands plasma cells, disrupts germinal centers, and increases autoantibody production in mice
Authors: Eva Conde, Seblewongel Asrat, Andrea Vecchione, Kaitlyn Gayvert, Paulina Pedraza, Carley Tasker, Sharon Huang, Dmitry Yarilin, Dylan Birchard, Li-Hong Ben, Wei Keat Lim, Andrew J. Murphy, Matthew A. Sleeman, Andre Limnander, Jamie M. Orengo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IL-33 is an inflammatory cytokine contributing to asthma, Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease (COPD), and autoimmune diseases. Although recent studies suggest that IL-33 can induce the generation of autoantibodies, the role of IL-33 on B cell maturation and tolerance is poorly understood. Here, by inducing systemic overexpression of IL-33 in mice, we show that this cytokine induces the IL-5 and CD4 T cell-dependent accumulation of plasmablasts and plasma cells of all isotypes in the spleen, and leads to an increased antibody production. IL-33 also disrupts splenic architecture and elevates autoantibody production, indicating a break in peripheral tolerance. Consistently, elevated levels of IL-33 exacerbate autoantibody production, kidney damage, and decrease survival in a mouse model of lupus. Additionally, intranasal delivery of IL-33 in mice or exposure to house dust mite extract (HDM) increases autoantibodies in the lung. Notably, blocking IL-33 reduced the autoantibodies generated during HDM exposure, indicating that HDM-induced autoantibody production is IL-33 dependent. Thus, our findings implicate IL-33 in the break of peripheral B cell tolerance, opening new therapeutic avenues for the treatment of infection, COPD and autoimmune conditions.

Introduction

Interleukin-33 (IL-33) is an inflammatory cytokine of the IL-1 family1. It is primarily released by structural cells, including fibroblasts, epithelial cells, and endothelial cells in response to tissue damage and can activate inflammatory pathways in both the innate and adaptive systems2. IL-33 binds to its cognate receptor ST2 in multiple cell types, amplifying both type 1 (IFNγ, IL-12) and type 2 inflammation (IL-4, IL-13, IL-5)3. IL-33 affects the activation and function of various cell types, including mast cells, T cells, eosinophils, basophils and ILCs, and IL-33/ST2 signaling plays an important role in several inflammatory diseases. IL-33 upregulation in inflamed tissue further amplifies Type 1/Type 17 (T1/T17) and/or type 2 (T2) inflammation, influenced by the local cytokine milieu, and the increased pool of IL-33-responsive cells, resulting in pathologic inflammation. In addition, high and sustained levels of IL-33 remained increased, even after the triggering agent was removed4.

Early clinical data, as well as genetic data in asthma and COPD, suggest a potential role of IL-33 in respiratory diseases5,6. There is also emerging evidence that IL-33 plays a role in autoimmunity, although the importance of this cytokine in driving disease is less understood. In addition, autoantibodies have been detected in respiratory diseases, such as COPD, but their relevance and etiology remain unclear. Autoimmune diseases are characterized by an aberrant immune response, with B cells and T cells reacting to normal constituents of the host. A variety of mechanisms have been proposed to trigger inappropriate recognition of self-antigens, including environmental and genetic factors7. In addition, certain cytokines have been linked to the induction or progression of autoimmune diseases8,9. Increased levels of IL-33 and soluble ST2 (sST2) have been described in patients with different autoimmune conditions, including rheumatoid arthritis, systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), multiple sclerosis, inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) and myasthenia gravis10. In mice, recombinant IL-33 induced an increase of splenic plasma cells, serum IgA, and IgE1 and recent evidence suggests that IL-33 could induce the production of autoantibodies11. However, the mechanisms underlying autoantibody production and the role of IL-33 on B cells or plasma cells are not understood.

In this study, we show that IL-33 induces the differentiation and accumulation of PBs/PCs in the spleen and mesenteric lymph nodes, leading to increases in circulating antibodies of all isotypes. IL-33 disrupts splenic architecture and germinal centers (GC), reduces the quality of the antibodies produced in response to immunization, leads to the generation of autoantibodies and accelerates pathology in a mouse model of SLE. Administration of intranasal IL-33 also induces autoantibodies in the lung, as does exposure to intranasal house dust mite (HDM). Importantly, the administration of an IL-33 blocking antibody decreases HDM-induced autoantibodies. Collectively, our data demonstrates that elevated IL-33 levels impact antibody responses and promote a break of peripheral B cell tolerance. Therefore, tight regulation of IL-33 may be required to enable appropriate adaptive responses to infection and to prevent autoantibody-mediated pathologies.

Results

IL-33, but not other type 2 cytokines, induces expansion of PB/PC

To determine the impact of different cytokines on B cells in secondary lymphoid organs, we used hydrodynamic gene delivery (HDD) to overexpress selected cytokines in vivo (Fig. 1a). HDD is a non-viral method that allows in vivo systemic overexpression of proteins by hepatocytes via intracellular delivery of macromolecules (DNA, RNA, proteins) in rodents12. We confirmed that the liver was the main producer by tracking IL-33 expression after HDD from the different tissues (Supplementary Fig. 1a). Using this method, IL-4, IL-5, IL-13, IL-25, TSLP, IL-9 and IL-33 were overexpressed in mice and detected in serum at day 7 (Supplementary Fig. 1a). In addition, IL-33 overexpression also increased soluble ST2 in serum (Supplementary Fig. 1a). Since HDD primarily induces gene expression in the liver, we first evaluated the impact of IL-33-HDD on the liver. We observed an increase of tissue ILC2, eosinophils and to a lesser extent, B cells and CD4 + T cells, but not a significant increase of NKT cells in the liver of mice that received IL-33 (Supplementary Fig. 1a). These data suggest that the inflammation observed in the liver is related to the effect of IL-33 in the tissue, rather than a general effect induced by HDD. Overexpression of IL-33, but not IL-4, IL-5, IL-13, IL-9, IL-25 or TSLP led to a robust increase in PB/PCs in spleen (9x fold increase) (Fig. 1b and Supplementary Fig. 1e). All PB/PC populations were expanded in the spleen irrespective of the isotype expressed in mice receiving IL-33 by HDD (IgG1, IgE, IgM, IgG2a and IgA), with IgG1 and IgM being the most abundantly expanded isotypes (Fig. 1c). The expansion of PB/PCs in spleen was also accompanied by an increase in antibody levels (Fig. 1d and Supplementary Fig. 1f). Interestingly, TSLP also induced a minor expansion of plasma cells, some of which were IgE + , but no significant impact was observed on other PCs (Fig. 1b and Supplementary Fig. 1g, h). In addition, we confirmed that overexpression of IL-4 was not sufficient to trigger an induction of IgE and IgG1 PB/PC expansion in the spleen (Supplementary Fig. 1h). Expression of the IL-33 receptor ST2 (IL1RL1) was very low on the surface of splenic CD19 + B cells at baseline, consistent with what has been previously reported13. In addition, ST2 expression has not been detected in RNA sequencing data sets (data assembled by the ImmGen consortium)14 and was not increased after IL-33 overexpression (Fig. 1f), but a marked increase was observed on CD4 + T cells in the spleen. These results suggest that IL-33 drives PB/PC expansion potentially through ST2 + T cells, but not by directly activating B2 cells through the ST2 + receptor15.

IL-33-induced PC expansion is reduced by CD4 T cell depletion

To further understand the role of T cells in IL-33 induced PB/PC expansion, we depleted either CD4+ or CD8+ T cells in the IL-33 overexpression model (Fig. 2a and Supplementary Fig. 2a). Depletion of CD4+ T cells led to a significant 50% reduction of IL-33 driven PB/PCs expansion, while CD8 + T cell depletion did not (Fig. 2b). Furthermore, CD4 + T cell depletion, specifically reduced IgG1 and IgE PB/PCs in the spleen (Fig. 2c, d), suggesting that IL-33 HDD induces a T-dependent expansion of splenic IgG1 and IgE PCs. In contrast, expansion of IgM PB/PCs (Fig. 2e) and IgM and IgG2a serum levels (Supplementary Fig. 2b) were not impacted by CD4 + T cell depletion, suggesting that IL-33 HDD can also drive a separate, T-independent response. Interestingly, we observed CD40L upregulation on CD4+ T cells upon overexpression of IL-33 (Fig. 2f) and found higher expression of CD40L on ST2+ than in ST2- CD4 + T cells (Fig. 2g). Of note, CD69 expression on CD4+ T cells, a marker of T cell activation16, was increased after IL-33 overexpression (Fig. 2h). In an effort to characterize the mechanism that drives IgG1 and IgE PB/PC expansion downstream of CD4+ T cells, we interrogated which cytokines are induced by IL-33 HDD and noticed that IL-4 and IL-13 were significantly upregulated after IL-33 HDD (Supplementary Fig. 3a). To determine the contribution of IL-4 and IL-13 in IL-33 HDD induced PB/PC expansion, we overexpressed IL-33 in IL-4Rα KO mice for 7 days and quantified PBs/PCs in spleen (Fig. 2i). Consistent with what we reported in WT mice after CD4+ T cell depletion, IL-4Rα KO mice had a significant reduction in IgG1 and IgE PB/PC expansion but still showed significant expansion of IgM and IgG2a PBs/PCs. Taken together, these findings demonstrate that IL-33 can directly activate ST2+ CD4+ T cells, which in turn upregulate CD40L, leading to B cell class switching and PB/PC differentiation to IgG1 and IgE. IL-5 from IL-33 overexpression expands IgM and IgG2a PB/PCs

Notably, IL-33 overexpression also increased IL-5 levels in the serum (Fig. 3a and Supplementary Fig. 3a). IL-5 has previously been shown to drive differentiation of mouse B cells to plasma cells17 and plays a key role in IgM production18. We also noted that IL-5 overexpression led to a moderate increase of PB/PCs in the spleen, albeit the induction was much less robust than what we observed upon IL-33 overexpression (Fig. 1a). We further compared the increase of serum IgM and IgG2a in mice receiving IL-33 or IL-5 HDD and noted a consistent and significant increase in serum with both cytokines (Fig. 3b). In mice receiving IL-33 HDD, we measured intracellular IL-5 from ST2+ cells. To probe if the increase of IgM and IgG2a triggered by IL-33 HDD was dependent on IL-5, we neutralized IL-5 using an anti-IL-5 antibody in mice receiving IL-33 HDD (Fig. 3c). We confirmed the efficacy of the anti-IL-5 by measuring blood eosinophils (Supplementary Fig. 3c). We observed that IgM, but not IgG1 or IgE PB/PCs in the spleen were significantly blocked by the IL-5 antibody treatment compared to isotype control (Fig. 3d). Circulating IgM and IgG2a increases (but not IgG1 nor IgE) were also blocked with the anti-IL-5 treatment (Fig. 3e). Consistent with this result, IL-5R expression was uniquely detected on the surface of IgM, but not IgG1 or IgE PCs following IL-33 HDD (Fig. 3f). Together these data suggests that in response to overexpression of IL-33 (and in the absence of immunization), PB/PC expansion is driven through 2 separate mechanisms; a CD4+ T cell dependent mechanism that expands IgG1 and IgE PCs and a T cell-independent, IL-5 driven mechanism that expands IgM and IgG2a PB/PCs (Supplementary Fig. 3d).

We then determined if combining CD4+ T cell depletion with IL-5 blockade can prevent all PB/PC expansion, by overexpressing IL-33 and treating mice with a combination of anti-CD4, anti-IL-5 or a combination of the two antibodies (Fig. 3g). Strikingly, we found that the IL-33 induced expansion of PB/PCs of all isotypes in the spleen was only prevented when treating with the two antibodies combined, leading to a reduction in all Ig isotypes in serum (Fig. 3h).

IL-33 overexpression suppresses germinal centers in the spleen

Plasma cells can arise from different B cell subsets with different functional consequences. To understand which B cell subset contributes to the IL-33-induced PB/PCs expansion, we profiled splenic B cells 7 days after overexpressing IL-33 and observed a decrease in total splenic B cells, including naïve B2 cells, B1 cells and marginal zone (MZ) B cells (Fig. 4a). This suggests that IL-33 can promote differentiation of pre-existing B cells into PB/PCs and does not induce proliferation of B cells. To evaluate the overall impact of IL-33 overexpression in the spleen, we performed unbiased global transcriptome analysis following IL-33 HDD and analyzed cell type enrichment. Consistent with our previous observation, IL-33 HDD led to a general depletion of genes associated with B cell populations and an increase of genes associated with plasma cells (Fig. 4c). Interestingly, cell enrichment analysis revealed that not all B cell subsets were equally impacted by IL-33 HDD. CD11c + /CD21+ and Tbet + /CD21+ ratios were increased and uniquely observed in IL-33 HDD (Fig. 4d and Supplementary Fig. 4b), suggesting an increase of age-associated B cells (ABC) and subsequently a change in the phenotype of the remaining B cells towards an alternative memory B cell lineage. Overexpression of IL-33 also impacted T cells, promoting genes associated with Th1 and Th2 cell differentiation. (Fig. 4c).

Histological analysis of the spleen showed that IL-33 overexpression induced a disorganization of the spleen white pulp with poorly discernible regions (Supplementary Fig. 4c). Other cytokines, such as IL-25 did not trigger that effect (Supplementary Fig. 4d). To further examine GC organization, we used immunofluorescence to stain for B cells (CD19 +), T cells (CD3 +), naïve B cells (CD19+IgD +) and GC cells (Bcl6 +) (Fig. 4e–g) and observed that IL-33 overexpression decreased the percentage of follicular area (white pulp) (Fig. 4f) along with decreased total B cells and T cells in the spleen (Supplementary Fig. 4e). The remaining follicles were small (Fig. 4e, g), appear to have lost GC structures and notably had decreased Bcl6+ cells within the follicles (T cells and B cells) (Fig. 4g and Supplementary Fig. 4f). Furthermore, HDD IL-33 significantly decreased lymphotoxin alpha levels, a cytokine that has been shown to be critical for the lymph node structural development19 (Supplementary Fig. 4g). When analyzing the spatial distribution of ST2 in the follicles, we confirmed an increased expression of the ST2 receptor on CD3 + T cells (but not on CD19 + B cells) in the follicles after IL-33 overexpression (Fig. 4h).

As expected, control mice immunized with NP-KLH showed an increased frequency of GC B cells in the draining lymph nodes (Fig. 5b) which was blocked when IL-33 HDD was performed seven days after NP-KLH immunization (Fig. 5b). Overexpression of IL-33 did not alter NP+ memory or naïve B cells in the draining lymph node suggesting that despite the disruption in GC, the presence of antigen was sufficient to drive similar numbers of NP+ B cells relative to control mice (Fig. 5b). We then overexpressed IL-33 prior to NP-KLH immunization (Model 2, Fig. 5c) and observed that GC B cells in draining lymph nodes were generated at a lower level compared to control mice, suggesting that overexpression of IL-33 does not completely block the GC formation in the draining lymph node (Fig. 5d) or the spleen (Supplementary Fig. 5a), but rather drives splenic disorganization. As previously observed in the draining lymph node in model 1, neither NP+ memory B cells nor naïve B cells were impacted by the overexpression of IL-33 in model 2 (Fig. 5d). However, in the spleen, and similarly to what we observed in non-immunized mice (Fig. 4a), memory and MZ B cells were decreased (Supplementary Fig. 5b) suggesting that in addition to the impact on NP specific B cells, the overexpression of IL-33 could drive the differentiation of non-NP-KLH specific B cells into plasma cells and therefore reducing the existing B cell pool. Interestingly, CD5 + Cd1d + B cells were also decreased, which have been reported to have regulatory functions21(Supplementary Fig. 5c). We analyzed the T cell compartment in this model 2 and found that IL-33 overexpression leads to important changes in the spleen of NP-KLH vaccinated mice (Fig. 5e) by decreasing splenic CD4 + T cell, but not CD8 + T cells. In addition, the number of Tfh and Tfr cells was significantly reduced, contributing to the GC disruption.

Next, we quantified PB/PCs and compared the effect of IL-33 overexpression prior to NP-KLH vaccination (Model 2) and found that both NP+ and NP- plasma cells were increased in the draining lymph nodes and the spleen (Fig. 5f and Supplementary Fig. 5d). We analyzed the quantity and the quality of the antibodies produced after immunization. The relative affinity of the antibody pool can be evaluated by measuring the ratio of NP2/NP27 titers. Coating with BSA-NP2 (low valency capture) only captures high-affinity antibodies, while BSA-NP27 will bind both high and low-affinity antibodies15. Remarkably, we observed that overexpression of IL-33 increased both NP2 (high affinity) and NP27 (total) specific IgM titers (Fig. 5g). In contrast, NP2 (high affinity) specific IgG1 titers were decreased as well as the ratio NP2/NP27 IgG1, suggesting that the NP specific IgG1 antibodies generated in the mice that received IL-33 HDD were of lower affinity compared to control immunized mice. These results suggest that the disruption of the GC structures induced by IL-33 overexpression impacts the quality of the antibody response to immunization, increasing the abundance of antibodies produced but decreasing their relative affinity towards the antigen.

IL-33 breaks tolerance and accelerates disease in NZB/W F1 mice

Our data show that IL-33 overexpression disrupts GCs while inducing an antibody response, suggesting that this response could be predominantly extrafollicular. Extrafollicular responses are associated with autoantibody production in systemic autoimmunity, such as in patients with SLE22, and GCs are important structures to enforce self-tolerance in the periphery23,24. We thus hypothesized that elevated levels of IL-33 might contribute to loss of tolerance in mice. Interestingly, autoantibodies to double stranded DNA (dsDNA) (Fig. 6a) and αvβ6 (Supplementary Fig. 6a), autoantibodies which have been reported in patients with SLE and ulcerative colitis, respectively, were significantly increased in WT mice 7 days after IL-33 overexpression. dsDNA autoantibodies were partially blocked by anti-IL-5 treatment (Fig. 6a). Depletion of CD4+ T cells also led to a partial reduction, albeit less pronounced than IL-5 blockade, while the combination of anti-IL-5 and anti-CD4 treatment completely blocked dsDNA autoantibody production. We then investigated the impact of IL-33 HDD in a model of autoimmunity. NZB/W F1 mice develop spontaneous autoimmunity with features that resemble SLE, including autoantibody production, immune complex deposition in the kidney leading to proteinuria, glomerulonephritis and premature death25 (Fig. 6c). NZB/W F1 mice that received IL-33 HDD showed a lower probability of survival compared to mice that received the EV control, suggesting that IL-33 accelerates pathology in the model (Fig. 6d). Four weeks after the HDD, we also observed an increase in PB/PC numbers in the spleen of NZB/W F1 mice that received IL-33 HDD, similar to the effect in WT mice (Fig. 6e). Of note, IL-33 overexpression altered splenic B cell subsets of NZB/W F1 mice, accumulating age-associated B cells and decreasing marginal zone B cells (Fig. 5f). We also analyzed the impact of IL-33 overexpression in the antibody levels in serum and reported that NZB/W F1 mice have higher baseline levels of anti-dsDNA compared to WT (Fig. 6g), but irrespective of genetic background, IL-33 overexpression led to an increase in dsDNA levels in serum. This effect was initially driven by a significant increase of IgM (observed at week 3), followed by a trend towards increased anti-dsDNA IgG1 (Fig. 6h). Finally, we measured the albumin to creatine ratio (ACR) in the urine of these mice four weeks after HDD. NZB/W F1 mice receiving IL-33 HDD had a significant increase of ACR, with the average above 300, which indicates an ongoing severe proteinuria (Fig. 6i). Of note, the ACR was also measured in WT mice receiving HDD IL-33 or EV. Although, WT mice receiving IL-33 HDD had a trend towards an increase in the ACR ratio, the increase was not as consistent as it was observed in NZB/W mice (Supplementary Fig. 6b). Interestingly, WT mice that had the highest ACR ratio also corresponded to the WT mice with the higher anti-dsDNA levels (Supplementary Fig. 6c). Consequently, these results show that IL-33 overexpression increases splenic PB/PCs and autoantibodies, induces kidney pathology in genetically predisposed mice and decreases their probability of survival. These findings suggest that in the context of spontaneous autoimmunity, IL-33 exacerbates disease and increases autoantibody production.

Interestingly, PBs and PCs were expanded in the lung after challenging mice with IL-33 for 4 weeks (Fig. 7c), which included expansion of IgM, IgA and IgG1 plasma cells (Fig. 7d). Consistent with this increase in lung PBs and PCs, we also observed an increase of IgM, IgG1 and IgA antibodies in the lung lysate (Fig. 7e). Notably, lung IgG2a was not increased in this model highlighting the difference between a systemic and a mucosal IL-33 challenge. We measured autoantibodies in the lung lysate by assessing anti-dsDNA titers and confirmed that IL-33 challenge in the lung also breaks peripheral tolerance, leading to an increase in autoantibodies in the tissue. Of note, we did not observe an increase in the number of germinal center B cells in the lung after 4 weeks of intranasal IL-33 administration (Supplementary Fig. 7a), suggesting that the expansion of PB/PCs happens independently of inducible organized structures in the lung, such as iBALT.

In addition, we characterized the T cell response in this model by harvesting mice challenged with saline or IL-33 for 4 days (Supplementary Fig. 7b). We observed an increase of GATA3 + Th2 and Tbet + Th1 cells in the lung (Supplementary Fig. 7d), consistent with the results from the spleen after systemic IL-33 expression. An increase of CD69 + T cells suggested an increase of T cell activation already after one week of IL-33 exposure (Supplementary Fig. 7e). We further analyzed ST2 + T cells to characterize which T cells were specifically responding to IL-33 and observed that 50% of ST2 + T cells were GATA3 + Th2 cells and 20% were Tbet+ Th1 cells (Supplementary Fig. 7 f), suggesting a critical role of these cells in this model. Upon IL-33 challenge, ILC2 were also increased in the lung (Supplementary Fig. 7g) as well as IL-5 (Supplementary Fig. 7h).

Finally, to assess the role of IL-33 and IL-5 in antibody production in a well-established mouse model of severe chronic airway inflammation4, mice were chronically exposed to house dust mite (HDM) for up to 15 weeks (Fig. 7g). First, we examined the role of T cells in the development of the IgM and IgG response (Supplementary Fig. 7i). To block the T-B cell interaction, mice were treated prophylactically with anti-CD40L for 4 weeks. As anticipated, treatment with anti-CD40L significantly reduced IgG plasma cells, confirming the T cell dependency of IgG production in this model. In contrast, IgM production remained unaffected, indicating that IgM production occurs independently of T cells. Next, we investigated the specific role of IL-5 in antibody production. Prophylactic treatment with an anti-IL-5 blocked antigen-specific IgM response, while sparing the IgG response. These results highlight the role of IL-5 in driving the T-independent response in a relevant model of lung inflammation. Finally, we evaluated the impact of blocking IL-33 on autoantibody production in this model. Starting at week 4, mice were treated with either an IL-33 blocking antibody or an isotype control (Fig. 7g). We measured anti-nuclear autoantibodies and observed an increase after 15 weeks of HDM challenge. Interestingly, blocking IL-33 decreased the levels of autoantibodies, with values similar to those observed in saline control mice (Fig. 7i). The decrease of autoantibodies was accompanied by a decrease of ST2 + T cells (Supplementary Fig. 7j). These results confirmed the physiological relevance of our findings, demonstrating the impact of IL-33 on autoantibody production.

Discussion

IL-33 is a proinflammatory cytokine released by damaged structural cells, activating immune cells of both the innate and adaptive systems2,26. Although the role of IL-33 has been studied in the context of airway inflammation, with early POC clinical data reported in asthma and COPD with the IL-33 blocking antibody itepekimab or tozorakimab5,6,27, little is known about the role of IL-33 on B cell lineages and the humoral response. In this study, we describe an underappreciated role of IL-33 in driving the expansion of PB/PCs and increasing antibody production. We show that the impact of IL-33 on the B cell/PC compartment is not direct, but rather driven by T cells (for IgG1 and IgE) and a T cell-independent source of IL-5 (for IgM and IgG2a), potentially ILC2s or mast cells, which are known to express ST2 and produce high amounts of IL-528,29. Surprisingly, we observed a decrease in B cells, likely reflecting the enhancement of differentiation into PB/PCs, and the absence of B cell proliferation. Concomitant with this plasma cell expansion, we observed disrupted GC organization and function. Consistent with this, overexpression of IL-33 significantly reduced GC B cells and Tfh cells during a T-dependent immunization (NP-KLH model), resulting in compromised GC function and production of low-quality antibodies. Interestingly, IL-33 can drive a break of tolerance and autoantibody production in WT mice and in a mouse model of SLE. In WT mice, dsDNA and αvβ6 -specific autoantibodies were observed following IL-33 overexpression, while in the NZB/W F1 mice, IL-33 accelerated disease, increasing age-associated B cells, plasma cells, and autoantibodies, ultimately promoting kidney dysfunction and decreasing the probability of survival. Interestingly, kidney pathology triggered by high and sustained levels of IL-33 was more consistent in NZB/W F1 than in WT, highlighting the role of IL-33 in accelerating autoimmunity. Finally, autoantibodies in the lung were increased by intranasal exposure to IL-33 or HDM, and the HDM-induced autoantibodies were decreased by an IL-33 blocking antibody. Taken together, these results suggest that a sustained increase of IL-33 disrupts GCs, leading to a break in peripheral tolerance that can be detrimental in mouse models of autoimmunity and lung inflammation.

Following activation in secondary lymphoid organs, B cells make the ultimate fate decision to enter GCs and undergo refinement of their BCR or remain outside of GCs to induce a rapid extrafollicular response. GCs are critical structures that organize and improve the quality of an antibody response by facilitating somatic hypermutation, affinity maturation and class switching23. This process also serves as a tolerance checkpoint, as it can promote mutation of otherwise autoreactive BCRs away from autoreactivity and towards pathogen/immunogen reactivity24. In contrast, an extrafollicular B cell response, which may or may not involve disorganized T cell help, is crucial for controlling infection by rapid production of low affinity antibodies in response to an antigen30. Importantly, long-lived plasma cells, which maintain long-term serological memory to vaccines and prior infections, are thought to be primarily derived from GC reactions31,32.

The fate decision to participate either in extrafollicular or GC response is a dynamic process that is tightly regulated by many cytokines33. IL-21 has been shown to induce GC responses during physiological conditions34 while IL-12 suppresses Tfh cell differentiation, blocking GC formation33. In addition, IL-6 has also been shown to stimulate Tfh cells, hence indirectly promoting GCs. It has also been reported that IL-33 increases BAFF, leading to autoantibody production11. However, the exact contribution of how IL-33 regulates extrafollicular, or GC responses, has not yet been fully characterized.

Interestingly, our data highlights a role of IL-33 in regulating B cell fate and disrupting GCs while expanding PBc/PCs that produce low-quality antibodies to an immunogen. The role of IL-33 on B cell fate could have important implications at the different stages of antigen exposure or infection. The presence of IL-33 early during infection could help the expansion of low- affinity antibodies, allowing time for a productive antigen-dependent response to develop. However, our work demonstrates that sustained and unrestrained IL-33 production can disrupt GCs and hinder an effective adaptive B cell response. Similar disrupted GC structures, as those observed with IL-33 overexpression in mice, have been reported following infections, such as in severe COVID-19, malaria or Toxoplasma gondii infection35–38. Sustained IL-33 levels have also been documented in severe infections39. Although not direct evidence, it is compelling to speculate that IL-33 could contribute to the loss of GCs observed in such severe diseases.

The mechanism by which IL-33 leads to disruption of GC is not yet clear. Our data show that IL-33 promotes Th1 and Th2 differentiation at the expense of Tfh, suggesting that this could be a potential mechanism by which IL-33 disrupts GC. A similar mechanism has been suggested in the loss of GCs post malaria infection37. ST2 receptor is also expressed on a variety of myeloid cells, and IL-33 could also be impacting dendritic cells or macrophages, important cell types supporting splenic architecture38,40.

Unlike GC reactions, extrafollicular responses lack certain checkpoints to prevent autoreactivity, and as such, are more prone to generate autoantibodies24. Expansion of extrafollicular response along with loss of GC is a characteristic feature of autoimmune diseases such as SLE22. These features are consistent in mouse models of lupus as well as in patients with lupus. Autoreactive B cell activation outside GCs has been demonstrated in the MRL/lpr mouse background (with CD95 deficiency)41. Previous work has shown that patients with SLE are characterized by a distinct differentiation of autoreactive naïve B cells into PC precursors with hyper-responsiveness to innate stimuli, with prominence of extrafollicular B cell activation and presence of double negative B cells (or DN2 cells)42. Acute SLE flares are characterized by the expansion of DN2 cells, contributing to a major increase of circulating PB/PCs and an increase in autoantibodies22,42. These findings suggest that an extrafollicular B cell response takes place during SLE flares with an increase of autoantibodies, and without the need of GC-dependent somatic hypermutations. Interestingly, all these characteristic features were observed in our studies in mice that received IL-33, suggesting a link between IL-33 and autoimmunity.

Consistent with this observation, IL-33 has been linked to many autoimmune diseases, including inflammatory bowel disease43, rheumatoid arthritis44–46 and SLE47,48. IL-33 and sST2 increased serum levels have also been directly linked to SLE pathology and severity47,49,50. In addition, NETs decorated with IL-33 have been observed in infiltrated inflamed tissues from patients with SLE, inducing IFN-α levels and correlating with disease severity50. In patients with rheumatoid arthritis, IL-33 levels were shown to correlate with autoantibody levels46. Finally, in MRL/Lpr mice, a lupus-prone mouse model, anti-IL-33 treatment significantly reduced mortality and lessened serum anti-dsDNA levels and circulating immune complexes49. In our studies, we expanded on these findings and revealed a role for IL-33 in triggering and exacerbating disease. Autoimmune diseases are characterized by periodic flares that exacerbate pathologies and, in some cases, can be triggered by an infection51. Our data in NZB/W F1 mice raise the possibility that IL-33 release in response to an infection can drive PB/PC expansion, leading to increased autoantibody production during flares.

Importantly, clinical data with the IL-33 blocker itepekimab support a role for IL-33 in COPD pathogenesis5,6, and one intriguing feature of COPD is the concomitant production of various autoantibodies, the relevance of which is still debated52,53. Our data suggest that elevated levels of IL-33 in the lung can give rise to an increase in autoantibodies, potentially contributing to the disease. In addition, in our mouse model of chronic lung inflammation, autoantibodies were detected and blocked by an IL-33 blocking antibody. Therefore, it will also be intriguing to define the relevance of our findings linking IL-33 with B-cell tolerance in patients with COPD.

Although these findings point to a role of IL-33 in the pathogenesis of autoimmune disease, there are limitations. We acknowledge that levels of IL-33 achieved with our HDD overexpression system are high and are not usually found in mild diseases. However, increases in IL-33 to levels approaching those observed in our model have been reported in severe infections or RA54. In addition, in the HDM-induced chronic airway inflammation mouse model, IL-33 has been shown to significantly increase over time, and more importantly, the levels persist in the absence of additional insult4, which suggests that persistent IL-33 production is a characteristic feature of many diseases. Blocking IL-33 was effective at reducing autoantibodies in this model, thus demonstrating that IL-33 plays a role in regulating B cell fate and autoantibody production in this context. Future work is needed to address the relevance of blocking IL-33 in autoimmune settings such as SLE, and more importantly, to define which subset of patients could benefit from such treatment.

Overall, this study highlights that IL-33 is a potent cytokine that promotes a low-affinity antibody response. We show that when unrestrained, IL-33 could be detrimental by disrupting GCs, decreasing antigen-specific antibody affinity, and increasing autoantibody production, and this antibody response can exacerbate autoimmune disease. Reversing this process with IL-33 blockade could represent a new therapeutic approach that could skew the B cell response and prevent an increase of autoantibodies. Taken together, these data demonstrate that IL-33 impacts antibody responses by multiple mechanisms with potential implications in infection, COPD and autoimmunity.

Methods

In vivo Study design

Hydrodynamic DNA delivery (HDD) was performed, as previously described55. Briefly, pRG977 plasmid coding for mouse cytokines or empty vector was diluted in sterile saline to a volume of approximately 10% of body weight and injected into the tail veins of female mice over 6 to 10 s. The plasmid carrier was pRG97756. Cytokine sequences were added or leave empty as a control (EV control). Different plasmids were used (IL-4, IL-5, IL-13, IL-9, TSLP, IL-33 and IL-25) at different doses ranging from 5ug to 25ug. To study the contribution of T cells and IL-5, mice were treated prophylactically, twice a week with 25 mg/kg of Rat IgG2b anti-mouse CD4 (Bioxcell, clone GK1.5), Rat IgG2b anti-mouse CD8a (Bioxcell, clone 2.43), Rat IgG1 anti-mouse IL-5 (Bioxcell, clone TRK5), isotype control rat IgG2b (Bioxcell, clone LTF-2) or isotype control rat IgG1 (Bioxcell, clone TNP6A7). To understand the effect of HDD IL-33 in immunization, mice were subcutaneously immunized with NP-KLH (Biosearch Technology) + aluminum adjuvant (Thermo Scientific) (50ug in 200uL total volume). For survival experiments in NZB/W F1 mice, mice were bled every two weeks to analyze antibody levels. For lung models, two models were used, either challenging with IL-33 (1) or HDM (2). (1) Mice were challenged 3 times per week during 4 weeks with either saline as a control or IL-33 (1 μg/20μL). (2) Mice were challenged with HDM or saline as a control 3 times per week for 15 weeks. Starting at week 4, mice were therapeutically treated with an IL-33 blocking antibody or an isotype control. To identify the T cell contribution in the model, HDM-challenged mice were prophylactically treated twice a week with 25 mg/Kg of anti-mouse CD40L (Bioxcell, clone MR-1).

Mice

All procedures were conducted in compliance with protocols approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Regeneron Pharmaceuticals. Female C57BL/6 WT mice were produced and maintained in a specific pathogen-free facility at Regeneron Pharmaceuticals. NZB/W F1 mice were purchased from Charles River (mice were redirected from the Jackson Laboratory, strain Code 100008). IL-4R KO and IL-33 humanized mice were generated on a hybrid 129S6/C57BL/6 background. Mice were 15–20 weeks at the beginning of the experiment, except experiments in Fig. 7c–h, where mice were 6 months old. Experimental and control animals used in the experiments were co-housed. At the end of the experiments, mice were euthanized by CO2 followed by cervical dislocation.

Tissue processing and flow cytometry analysis

Spleens and lymph nodes were collected and mashed on 12-well, 70 μm filter plates (Corning Costar) in complete RPMI 1640 media (cRPMI) to generate single-cell suspensions. Peyer’s patches (PP) were dissociated by incubating in a 12-well, 70 μm filter plate (Corning Costar) in dissociation buffer (HBSS, 10% FBS, 5 mM EDTA, 15 mM HEPES) during 15 min at 37 °C. PP were transfer to cRPMI and mashed. Right inferior lung lobe was harvested in liberase TH 0.7 U/mL, DNase 20ug/mL and HBSS with Ca and Mg. Lung was chop into small pieces and incubated at 37 °C for 20 min. Tissue was transferred into gentleMACS C tubes (Miltenyi), and tubes were attached upside down on a gentleMACS dissociator (Miltenyi). RBC lysis was performed on single-cell suspensions for spleen and lung. Colon was harvested, washed, and cleaned. Tissue was incubated in dissociation solution containing HBSS, FBS, HEPES and EDTA during 40 min at 37 °C to remove intestinal epithelial cells. After, tissue was transferred into the enzymatic digestion solution containing collagenase D, DNAseI and Dispase and incubated during 45 min at 37 °C. Remaining tissue was then mashed and filtered through a 70 μm cell strainer to recover cells from the lamina propia. Cells were labeled with LIVE/DEAD Blue solution (ThermoFisher Scientific) for 10 min in the dark at room temperature (RT). Cells were then blocked using Fc Block (Tongo Biosciences) for 10 min at 4 °C, followed by incubation with extracellular antibody mix for 30 min at 4 °C in Brilliant Stain Buffer (BD Biosciences). The antibodies used for staining are included in Supplementary Table 1. For intracellular staining, samples were fixed and permeabilized (BD Cytofix/Cytoperm and BD Perm/Wash buffer) and resuspended with the antibody mix. Samples were acquired on BD Symphony A5 (BD Bioscience). Data was analyzed using OMIQ software (Dotmatics).

RNA-seq data generation and analysis

The protocols for RNA extraction and sequencing library preparation were similar to those described earlier57. Strand-specific RNA-seq libraries were prepared from 500 ng RNA using the KAPA stranded mRNA-Seq Kit (KAPA Biosystems). The libraries were amplified by twelve-cycle PCR. Sequencing was performed on Illumina HiSeq®2000 (Illumina) by a multiplexed single read run with 33 cycles. The resulting FASTQ files were analyzed via FastQC to ensure sufficient data quality. After RNA extraction and sequencing library preparation, reads were mapped to the human genome (National Center for Biotechnology Information GRCh37) using Array Studio software (Omicsoft, Cary, NC). Enrichment of cell type signatures was assessed using the xCell package in R.

Histology analysis

Spleens were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 24 h at RT and preserved in 70% ethanol. Longitudinal sections were paraffin-embedded and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) for assessment of the red and white pulp structure. Multiplex immunofluorescence staining was performed by the Preclinical Histology Core in Regeneron on 4 μm sections of formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded spleens. Antibodies used were BCL6 (0.8 μg/mL, Cell Signaling Technologies 5650S), IgD (2 μg/mL, Bio-Rad MCA4693), CD19 (0.05 μg/mL, Cell Signaling Technologies 90176), and CD3 (0.6 μg.mL Dako A0452). ST2 RNA probes (Molecular Instruments) were used to detect ST2 RNA by Hybridization Chain Reaction (HCR, Molecular Instruments). All IHC/ISH staining was performed on Leica Bond Rx auto-stainers (Leica Biosystems) using the Bond Refine polymer-HRP detection system with TSA-amplification. TSA-conjugated 543 (Biotium), 594 (Biotium), 647 (Invitrogen) fluorophores, and Biotin-TSA with Streptavidin-750(Invitrogen) fluorophores were used for visualization by fluorescence microscopy, and slides were counterstained with DAPI.

Total mouse isotyping

Concentrations mouse IgG1, IgG2a, IgG3, IgA was determined by using mouse isotyping MSD kit from Mesoscale. Briefly, plates are coated with SULFO-TAG labeled detection antibodies (anti-IgA, IgG1, IgG2a, IgG2b, IgG3, IgM). 25 μL of calibrator or sample are added to each well. Serum samples were diluted at 1:20,000, and protein lysates were diluted at 1:100 in 1% BSA/PBS. After incubation of 25 μl of a mix of detection antibody, plates are washed and 150 μL of Read Buffer T is added. Plates are analyzed using the QuickPlex SQ120 plate reader (Meso Scale Discovery, Rockville, Md). Total mouse IgE was quantified using the OptEIA kit (BD Biosciences) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, plates were coated with an anti-IgE coating antibody. After blocking for 3 h, calibrator and samples were added and incubates O/N at 4 °C. Serum samples were diluted at 1:50, and lung samples were diluted at 1:2 in BD assay diluent (PBS 10% FBS). After washing, a mix of detection antibody and Streptavidin-HRP agent was added. Before revealing the ELISA, plates were washed 7 times to avoid a high background. Plates were read using a SpectraMax M5 spectrophotometer.

ELISA anti NP2 and NP27 antibody levels

Anti-NP2 and NP27 specific IgM and IgG1 were measured by ELISA. Plates were coated overnight at 4 °C with 4 μg/mL of NP2-BSA or NP27-BSA (Bioresearch Technologies). After blocking with 1% BSA during 2 h at RT, serum samples were added, and a 3-fold serial dilution was performed starting at 1:10,000. After 2 h incubation at RT, bound antibodies were detected either with an anti-mouse IgG1-HRP (Invitrogen A10551) at 1/5000 or with an anti-mouse IgM-HRP (Invitrogen 04-06820) at 1/10,000. Plates were revealed using TMB substrate, and the reaction was stopped with 1 M H2SO4. To measure the relative affinity, the ratio of the titer NP2/NP8 was calculated.

ELISA anti-dsDNA and derp1 antibody levels

Total anti-dsDNA Ig were measured using the kit from Alpha Diagnostic (# 5110), following the manufacturer instructions. Anti-dsDNA or derp1 specific IgM and IgG1 were measured by ELISA. Plates were coated overnight at 4 C with 4 microg/mL of dsDNA or 2 microg/mL of derp1. After blocking with 1% BSA during 2 h at RT, serum samples were added, and a 3-fold serial dilution was performed starting at 1:50. After 2 h incubation at RT, bound antibodies were detected either with an anti-mouse IgG1-HRP (Invitrogen A10551) at 1/5000 or with an anti-mouse IgM-HRP (Invitrogen 04-06820) at 1/10000. Plates were revealed using TMB substrate, and the reaction was stopped with 1 M H2SO4.

ELISA Anti-nuclear autoantibody

Total anti-mouse anti-nuclear antibodies (ANA) were quantified using the Mouse ANA ELISA kit (antibodies.com, A87504) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, lung samples were added at 1:40 in the sample dilution buffer. Standards and samples were added to pre-coated wells and incubated for 90 min at 37 °C. After washing, biotin label antigen was added and incubated at 37 °C for 60min, followed by an HRP streptavidin conjugate incubation. Plates were revealed and read using a SpectraMax M5 spectrophotometer.

ELISA Mouse soluble ST2/IL-33R

Soluble ST2 was quantified using Mouse ST2/IL-33R Elisa Kit- Quantikine (Biotechne, MST200), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, standards and samples were added to pre-coated wells and incubated. Serum samples were diluted at 1:10 in calibrator diluent. After washing, the anti-mouse ST2 antibody was incubated, followed by substrate development. The reaction was stopped, and absorbance was read using a SpectraMax M5 spectrophotometer.

Cytokine analysis

Cytokine levels were measured with a customized mouse Procartaplex panel (Thermo Fisher) using Luminex xMAP technology. The experiment was conducted following the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, magnetic beads were added to the plate. After washing the beads, 25 μl of universal assay buffer was added, followed by standards and samples. Serum samples were diluted 1:4, and lung samples were added neat. After overnight incubation at 4 C, plates were washed followed by detection antibody mixture incubation during 45 min and streptavidin-PE incubation during 30 min. Plates were washed, and 120 μL of reading buffer was added. After 5 min incubation, the plates were read using the Luminex FlexMap3D reader.

Albumin to creatine ratio in urine

Metabolic cages were set up at 4 weeks after HDD to collect urine overnight. Albumin was measured using Albuwell M (Mouse Albumin ELISA Kit, Ethos Biosciences) and following the manufacturer's instructions. Creatinine levels are measured in urine using the ADVIA® Chemistry XPT blood chemistry analyzer (Siemens).

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed with GraphPad Prism software. Normality of data was evaluated by using the Shapiro-Wilk test. For multiple comparisons, normal data with non-statistically different SDs (assessed by using the Brown-Forsythe test) were interpreted by using 1-way ANOVA, followed by the Tukey post hoc test for multiple comparisons. Non-normal data or data with significantly different SDs were interpreted by using the Kruskal-Wallis test, followed by the Dunn post hoc test for multiple comparisons. When only comparing two groups, the Mann-Whitney U test (for not normally distributed samples) or the Unpaired Student’s t test (for normally distributed samples) were conducted. Differences were considered statistically significant at a P value of less than 0.05.
