A stereotactic laser interstitial thermal therapy strategy to flank dense calcifications within epileptogenic lesions: illustrative case
Authors: Nicole M. De La Peña, Bryan J. Neth, Brian N. Lundstrom, Timothy J. Kaufmann, Kai J. Miller
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUNDLaser interstitial thermal therapy is a minimally invasive surgical technique commonly used for treating epileptogenic foci in patients with drug-resistant epilepsy. Densely calcified lesions pose a challenge because they prevent the placement of a laser cannula within them and act as a heat shield. Laser interstitial thermal therapy (LITT) is an increasingly utilized minimally invasive surgical technique that has multiple clinical indications including treating MRI-identified lesions or brain regions that are determined to be epileptogenic foci. This procedure involves utilizing stereotaxy to precisely position laser filaments within such lesions in order to deliver thermal energy and induce ablation of targeted tissue, resulting in lesionectomy or disconnection of the foci from surrounding structures.1,2 Proton resonance frequency shift MR thermography monitoring during the procedure allows for careful delivery of thermal energy to the target while monitoring for potential damage to surrounding normal brain parenchyma, including highly eloquent structures. This technique has been described for several epileptogenic lesion types, including tuberous sclerosis, hypothalamic hamartoma, mesial temporal sclerosis, cortical dysplasia, and periventricular nodular hyperplasia.3

Polymorphous low-grade neuroepithelial tumor of the young (PLNTY) is a newly classified entity since the 2021 update of the WHO Classification of Tumours of the Central Nervous System.4 This is a low-grade primary brain tumor that typically affects individuals younger than 20 years of age, with a range reported from 4 to 57 years.5 These tumors commonly occur in the temporal lobe and have a strong epileptogenic propensity.5 A majority of these tumors contain calcifications, with either a prominent central calcification or peripheral lamellar calcifications.5,6 Given that these are low-grade (“benign”) tumors that can be relatively deep seated, they can be prime targets for LITT ablation to avoid potential neurological deficits or morbidity that can occur with more invasive open resection. However, lesions that contain or are composed of dense calcifications pose a significant challenge in terms of positioning the laser cannula, as the increased density may prevent their full penetration by the laser cannula or probe. Therefore, one must avoid directly targeting the heavily calcified area but still ensure that the thermal ablation zone or zones encompass the entire lesion. Additionally, calcifications can act as heat shields, preventing adequate thermal ablation of all parts of the lesion. Here, we present a surgical technique of flanking a densely calcified lesion with multiple introduced laser cannulas, illustrated by a case of a patient with a presumed medial occipitotemporal PLNTY.

Illustrative Case

Preoperative Evaluation

A 22-year-old right-handed male with a medical history significant for autism spectrum disorder, attention deficit hyperactivity disorder, anxiety, and episodes of possible psychosis presented for evaluation of a lesion in the right medial occipitotemporal gyrus in the setting of intractable epilepsy. He initially presented at the age of 12 years after undergoing imaging in the setting of long-standing behavioral concerns, where brain MRI demonstrated a T2 hypointense, minimally enhancing lesion in the right medial occipitotemporal gyrus that was radiographically consistent with a PLNTY (Fig. 1A and B). This lesion was noted to be densely calcified on CT imaging (Fig. 1C). He was followed with serial imaging over time, which showed overall stability of the size and appearance of the mass, although there was noted increasing size of the central calcification. Beginning at the age of 20 years, he developed daily episodes of gastrointestinal upset, heartburn, and a rising sensation with associated cognitive dysfunction that lasted for 3–4 minutes, concerning for focal seizures with preserved consciousness. These episodes rarely progressed to brain fog and decreased responsiveness with associated impaired consciousness. He underwent EEG recording in the epilepsy monitoring unit, which recorded seizures arising from the right centro-parieto-occipital head regions, as well as interictal right frontotemporal sharp waves. These findings were consistent with the lesion likely serving as the epileptogenic focus. He was started on lacosamide with dose escalation over time given continued seizure events, but other medications were not trialed as there were concerns for potential mood-related side effects. Therefore, surgical treatment of the presumed PLNTY was discussed. The patient’s case was discussed at a multidisciplinary conference involving the neurology, neurosurgery, and neuroradiology teams. In the setting of a well-demarcated lesion with concordant electrophysiology and clinical presentation that indicated the PLNTY was likely to be the epileptogenic focus, the team conferred that upfront treatment of the lesion would be the optimal approach as opposed to pursuing further workup (such as stereotactic EEG). Given the patient’s psychiatric history, a minimally invasive approach was preferred to decrease potential postoperative exacerbation of his psychiatric symptoms. Additionally, with the deep-seated nature of the lesion in the medial temporal lobe, open resection posed a higher risk of damage to the surrounding parenchyma and postoperative neurological deficits as opposed to a minimally invasive ablative procedure. Therefore, LITT ablation was chosen as the treatment modality. Biopsy of the lesion in conjunction with LITT was considered but ultimately not chosen. First, tissue diagnosis was not essential for therapy considerations. Second, a biopsy could potentially introduce air and blood into the ablation site, which could lead to distorted anatomy, such that the accuracy of subsequent placement of the laser cannula might be affected, in addition to causing additional heat sinks leading to suboptimal spread of heat during the ablation. The patient underwent preoperative neuropsychiatric testing and was found to have a mild relative weakness in visuospatial construction.

Surgical Technique

Following induction of general endotracheal anesthesia, a stereotactic Leksell head frame (Elekta) was placed, after which a CT venogram was obtained and subsequently spatially registered with the preoperative MR image. Given its dense calcification, a laser cannula would not be able to be positioned within the center of the lesion. Therefore, two trajectories were planned that flanked the centrally calcified portion of the lesion at its anterior and posterior aspects (labeled A and P, respectively, in Fig. 2). The patient was positioned supine with a bump underneath his right shoulder and his head turned toward the left, exposing the right lateral region. The stereotactic MRI and CT were coregistered to the Leksell frame for stereotactic alignment, and the frame was aligned to the anterior approach first. A drill was advanced directly through the scalp and skull, and the dura was cauterized and opened. A Medtronic Visualase bolt was screwed into the skull. A 1.65-mm-diameter 15-W maximum Visualase laser cannula was advanced to target, the inner stylet was removed, and a 10-mm laser filament was placed within the cannula. The same steps were performed for the posterior trajectory. The patient was then brought into a 3T MRI scanner. Thermometry was set up in two planes and registered to the T2-weighted images. High and low temperature limits were set to avoid cannula overheating and for protection of vulnerable structures. Ablations were then performed sequentially with both lasers, beginning with the anterior trajectory. Following low-energy test laser heatings to evaluate heat spread, the anterior laser fiber was activated to 9.0 W for 30 seconds and 136 seconds for treatment. Based on the heat spread of the posterior laser fiber after low-energy test laser heatings, the cannula was advanced 2–3 mm. The posterior laser fiber was then activated to 9.0 W for 143 seconds and 141 seconds for treatment. Following ablation, the laser filaments and bolts were removed, and the insertion site puncture wounds in the scalp were closed with suture. Outcome

The thermal ablation zones, as depicted by Visualase estimated cell damage maps calculated through MR thermography, covered the entirety of the lesion when summing the anterior (Fig. 3A) and posterior (Fig. 3B) ablations. A shadow effect from the heavy calcification blocking heat spread to the other side of the lesion was seen during each ablation. Postprocedure MRI demonstrated overlapping peripherally enhancing ablation zones within the right medial occipitotemporal gyrus, which subsumed the entirety of the calcified mass (Fig. 4A). The patient tolerated the procedure well and was discharged on postoperative day 1 without neurological or visual field deficits. However, over the next week postoperatively, he noted worsening of his psychiatric symptoms, including increased severity and frequency of intrusive thoughts, magical thinking, and auditory hallucinations. These symptoms improved within 1 month after the procedure. He was seen for neuropsychiatric evaluation postoperatively, where he subjectively did not note any episodes of cognitive dysfunction and on formal evaluation did not have a decline in memory, concentration, or perceptual deficits compared to his preoperative baseline. Follow-up imaging at 2.5 months demonstrated a mildly contracted peripherally enhancing ablation zone that continued to fully encompass the lesion (Fig. 4B). On the last follow-up at 14 months, he continued to remain clinically and electrographically seizure free and was able to decrease his lacosamide dose. Imaging demonstrated decreased volume of the lesion and continued contraction of the ablation zone (Fig. 4C). Discussion

Observations

A paucity of literature exists regarding the treatment of patients with drug-resistant epilepsy secondary to PLNTY with LITT; additionally, there has not been significant discussion of utilizing LITT in the treatment of lesions containing dense calcifications.3 Here, we present a technical consideration for flanking densely calcified lesions with multiple laser filaments in order to encompass the entire lesion within the thermal ablation zones. This technique allows for adequate coverage of the lesion while avoiding inadvertent thermal damage to the surrounding normal brain parenchyma and critical structures.

There are several considerations for LITT ablation of lesions containing calcifications, such as is common in PLNTY. Stereotactic positioning of the cannula within the lesion may not be feasible depending on the extent of calcification. However, eccentric laser filament positioning risks not encompassing the entire lesion in the thermal ablation zone. This is particularly relevant when considering that calcifications have different absorption coefficients, reflecting the extent of photon absorption during the ablation, compared to surrounding lesional tissue and brain parenchyma, which can impact the targeted thermal energy delivery during ablation.3 Typically, the absorption coefficient of pathological tissue is higher than normal brain tissue, which leads to a preferential ablation of the pathological tissue surrounding the laser filament, unless it is highly perfused.3 In a case series of 12 patients with subependymal giant cell astrocytomas that were treated with LITT ablation, it was noted that progression was seen only in 2 cases in which there was intratumoral calcification present.7 This finding brought up the question of a potential heat shielding mechanism from intralesional calcification that leads to lower thermal conduction across the calcification and could potentially decrease or block the thermal ablation of the adjacent lesional tissue. Indeed, we saw a thermal shielding effect from the calcification on MR thermography, with a noted shadow effect around the calcified portion that appeared to persist throughout the ablation. On the anterior ablation, the shadow appeared to be on the posterior side of the calcification (and the opposite during the posterior ablation). It has been documented that CSF spaces and blood vessels that are adjacent to the lesional tissue may introduce heat sinks during the ablation, where the flow of fluid through these spaces, as well as the lower thermal conduction of these tissues, leads to a dissipation of heat.3,8,9 Calcifications may also act as a heat sink or shield, in which tissue beyond or behind the calcification in relation to the laser fiber is shielded from the heat spread. This is likely distinct in mechanism from the flow-induced dissipation that occurs with CSF spaces and blood vessels.

By positioning laser filaments on more than one side of the calcification, there may be an increased effectiveness of thermal ablation and tissue coagulation and necrosis of the shielded, noncalcified portions of the lesion, increasing the likelihood of seizure control postprocedure. During the present study’s ablation, there were overlapping ablation zones from the anterior and posterior laser filaments, again increasing the likelihood of complete lesional tissue necrosis.

Lessons

Utilizing stereotaxy to position multiple laser filaments to flank densely calcified lesions that are epileptogenic foci is a strategy to effectively encompass all of the lesion within the ablation zones, overcoming the heat shielding effect that occurs secondary to the calcification during ablation and ensuring adequate lesional tissue necrosis, as demonstrated in this case. This technique can be utilized in cases in which positioning a laser cannula directly within a portion of the calcified lesion is not viable.

There are several limitations to this technique. Care must be taken with lesions that are adjacent to eloquent brain areas, given the potential for damage to surrounding structures with cannulas placed eccentrically in the lesion as opposed to directly within the center of the lesion itself. In the current report, there was minimal anterior and posterior extension of the ablation zones outside of the targeted lesion that was closely attended to during the procedure with observed response on thermography. Calcifications can distort MR thermography maps during the ablation, inducing susceptibility artifacts, which can make thermography difficult to monitor.3 Recognizing areas of the field on thermography that cannot be reliably monitored is critical, particularly for determining the extent and aggressiveness of ablation in certain regions. Lastly, studies have cited an increased rate of complications during procedures with an increasing number of placed probes, particularly with malposition of the cannulas, as brain shift can occur after placement of the first cannula. In one study investigating complications associated with LITT at a single institution, the rate of complications when a single probe was introduced was around 10.8% of cases, which increased to 54.5% of cases with two passed probes.10 However, a different study described the use of two to four laser fibers during corpus callosotomy in a total of 10 patients without any reported perioperative complications.11 MR images obtained after laser filament placement can help guide if any malposition has occurred prior to ablation.
