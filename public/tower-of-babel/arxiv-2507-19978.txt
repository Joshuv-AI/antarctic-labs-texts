Extreme value theory for singular subspace estimation in the matrix denoising model
Junhyung Chang, Joshua Cape

Abstract
This paper studies fine-grained singular subspace estimation in the matrix denoising model where a deterministic low-rank signal matrix is additively perturbed by a stochastic matrix of Gaussian noise. We establish that the maximum Euclidean row norm (i.e., the two-to-infinity norm) of the aligned difference between the leading sample and population singular vectors approaches the Gumbel distribution in the large-matrix limit, under suitable signal-to-noise conditions and after appropriate centering and scaling. We apply our novel asymptotic distributional theory to test hypotheses of low-rank signal structure encoded in the leading singular vectors and their corresponding principal subspace. We provide de-biased estimators for the corresponding nuisance signal singular values and show that our proposed plug-in test statistic has desirable properties. Notably, compared to using the Frobenius norm subspace distance, our test statistic based on the two-to-infinity norm has higher power to detect structured alternatives that differ from the null in only a few matrix entries or rows. Our main results are obtained by a novel synthesis of and technical analysis involving entrywise matrix perturbation analysis, extreme value theory, saddle point approximation methods, and random matrix theory. Our contributions complement the existing literature for matrix denoising focused on minimaxity, mean squared error analysis, unitarily invariant distances between subspaces, component-wise asymptotic distributional theory, and row-wise uniform error bounds. Numerical simulations illustrate our main results and demonstrate the robustness properties of our testing procedure to non-Gaussian noise distributions.



Introduction

This paper considers noisy observable data matrices
\widehat{\mathbf{M}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
of the form

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbf{M}}\coloneqq\mathbf{M}+\mathbf{E}

(1)

where
\mathbf{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
is an unobserved deterministic signal matrix with
r\coloneqq\operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{M})\ll\min\{n,m\}
, and where
\mathbf{E}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
is an unobserved stochastic noise matrix having mean-zero Gaussian entries.
Our goal is to infer properties of the signal matrix
\mathbf{M}
whose singular value decomposition (SVD) is written as
\mathbf{USV}^{\mathsf{T}}
.
Here,
\mathbf{U}
and
\mathbf{V}
denote matrices whose orthonormal columns are the leading left and right singular vectors of
\mathbf{M}
, respectively, while the diagonal matrix
\mathbf{S}=\operatorname{diag}(s_{1},\dots,s_{r})
contains the non-zero singular values of
\mathbf{M}
, where
s_{1}\geq\dots\geq s_{r}>0
.
We write the corresponding top-
r
SVD of
\widehat{\mathbf{M}}
as
\widehat{\mathbf{U}}\widehat{\mathbf{S}}\widehat{\mathbf{V}}^{\mathsf{T}}
provided it is well defined.

This paper focuses on
\mathbf{U}
and
\widehat{\mathbf{U}}
, though the results and machinery herein can be similarly adapted to
\mathbf{V}
and
\widehat{\mathbf{V}}
.
In particular, this paper studies fine-grained estimation of the left singular vectors,
\mathbf{U}
, using the observable noisy counterpart
\widehat{\mathbf{U}}
.
To do so, we consider the difference
\widehat{\mathbf{U}}\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{U}}-\mathbf{U}
, where
\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{U}}
denotes an
r\times r
orthogonal matrix that accounts for the rotational ambiguity inherent in subspace estimation.
Concretely, we take
\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{U}}\coloneqq\operatorname{{sgn}}(\widehat{\mathbf{U}}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{U})
, where the sign of a square matrix,
\operatorname{{sgn}}(\cdot)
, is defined as the orthogonal factor of its polar decomposition, which minimizes the Frobenius norm of
\widehat{\mathbf{U}}\mathbf{O}-\mathbf{U}
over
r\times r
orthogonal matrices
\mathbf{O}

(
Schönemann, 1966
)
.

Instead of considering the more classical Frobenius norm or spectral norm, both of which are insufficient for fine-grained analysis, this paper undertakes a detailed and novel investigation of

\|\widehat{\mathbf{U}}\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{U}}-\mathbf{U}\|_{2,\infty}

(2)

where
\|\cdot\|_{2,\infty}
denotes the two-to-infinity norm of a matrix
(
Cape et al., 2019b
;
Chen et al., 2021
)
.
In particular, the two-to-infinity norm, also known as the maximum Euclidean row norm, is given in terms of the
\ell_{2}
and
\ell_{\infty}
vector norms by

\|\mathbf{A}\|_{2,\infty}\coloneqq\sup_{\|\mathbf{x}\|_{2}=1}\|\mathbf{Ax}\|_{\infty}\equiv\max_{1\leq i\leq n}\|\mathbf{A}_{i,\bm{\cdot}}\|_{2}

(3)

where
\mathbf{A}_{i,\bm{\cdot}}
denotes the
i
-th row vector of the matrix
\mathbf{A}
.
This norm has received considerable attention within statistics and data science in recent years, as detailed in
Section

1.2
.
Although numerous two-to-infinity norm bounds and row-specific estimation results have been obtained in the literature on matrix denoising and related problems, such as principal component analysis, factor analysis, matrix completion, and random graph inference, no prior works develop the distributional properties of the two-to-infinity norm or its rigorous use for statistical hypothesis testing.
Given the inherent extremal nature of the two-to-infinity norm, our analysis necessitates novel technical analysis encompassing spectral methods, function approximation, and extreme value theory.

Contributions

The main purpose of this paper is to establish the asymptotic distributional properties of
\|\widehat{\mathbf{U}}\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{U}}-\mathbf{U}\|_{2,\infty}
and to subsequently develop downstream inferential applications.
To do so, we employ a combination of row-wise eigenvector perturbation analysis, saddle point density approximation methods, and extreme value theory but in a non-traditional high-dimensional setting involving a maximum of the form
\max_{1\leq i\leq n}X_{i,n,m}
, where each random variable
X_{i,n,m}
changes with
n
and
m\equiv m(n)
as
n,m\to\infty
.

As will be explained later, our distributional theory enables methodology for addressing hypothesis testing problems that involve signal subspace structure, loosely speaking of the form

\operatorname{H}_{0}:\mathbf{U}=\mathbf{U}_{0}

versus

\operatorname{H}_{\operatorname{A}}:\mathbf{U}=\mathbf{U}_{1}
.
Our maximum-type test statistic involving
\|\widehat{\mathbf{U}}\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{U}}-\mathbf{U}\|_{2,\infty}
is sensitive to alternatives
\mathbf{U}_{1}
that differ from
\mathbf{U}_{0}
in only a few entries or rows, unlike summation-type test statistics utilizing the Frobenius norm of differences between orthogonal projection matrices.

A key technical contribution of this paper is that we permit the smallest non-zero signal singular value
s_{r}
to have general multiplicity
1\leq\ell\leq r
.
Our analysis characterizes the influence of
\ell
on the extreme value asymptotics for subspace estimation using the two-to-infinity norm.

Our main contributions are summarized as follows.

1.

We establish novel extreme value distributional theory for fine-grained singular subspace estimation in the Gaussian matrix denoising model by proving that
\|\widehat{\mathbf{U}}\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{U}}-\mathbf{U}\|_{2,\infty}
converges in distribution to a standard Gumbel random variable in the large-matrix limit after suitable centering and scaling.
See
Propositions

3
and
4
.

2.

We derive a non-asymptotic Kolmogorov–Smirnov distance error bound between the cumulative distribution function of our two-to-infinity norm-based test statistic and that of the limiting Gumbel distribution.
See
Theorem

5
.

3.

Viewing singular values as nuisance parameters in the subspace estimation framework, we derive de-biased estimators of the signal singular values to establish the convergence in distribution of a data-driven plug-in test statistic.
See
Propositions

6
and
7
.

4.

Our theoretical developments enable a consistent testing procedure for investigating singular subspace structure in the signal matrix for which we provide a power phase transition analysis under simple alternatives.
See
Theorem

9
.

5.

Our extreme value-type test has higher power to detect structured alternatives that differ from the null in only a few matrix entries or rows compared to existing asymptotically Gaussian-type test statistics based on the Frobenius norm and differences between orthogonal projections.
See
Section

4.2.1
.

6.

Simulation studies empirically demonstrate the robustness of our testing framework to departures from stated modeling assumptions.
See
Section

4.3
and the Supplement.

Related work

The problem of
matrix denoising
commonly refers to estimating an unobserved deterministic low-rank signal matrix from its observed noisy counterpart, often modeled as in
Equation

1
.
Historically, interest in estimating the low-rank signal matrix has led to the development of
singular value shrinkage
methods, with the goal of transforming sample singular values to correct for the inflation or bias caused by the noise matrix.
Notable examples include low-rank thresholding
(
Cai et al., 2010
;
Gavish and Donoho, 2014
;
Donoho and Gavish, 2014
;
Chatterjee, 2015

;
Gavish and Donoho, 2017
;
Donoho et al., 2018
;
Gavish et al., 2023
;
Su and Wu, 2025
)
.
More recently, considerable attention has been given to the related problem of
subspace estimation
which pertains to estimating the principal singular subspaces of the signal matrix.
The study of subspace estimation originally focused on error bounds under the more traditional Frobenius or spectral norm
(
Davis and Kahan, 1970
;
Wedin, 1972
;
Cai and Zhang, 2018
;
Ding, 2020
;
Cai et al., 2021
;
O’Rourke et al., 2023
)
and has advanced to a more delicate understanding of row-wise or even entrywise error analysis, via fine-grained eigenvector perturbation theory
(
Fan et al., 2018
;
Cape et al., 2019a
;
Cape et al., 2019b
;
Abbe et al., 2020
;
Damle and Sun, 2020
;
Agterberg et al., 2021
)
.

Beyond the derivation of error bounds, the study of asymptotic distributional theory and the goal of developing inference procedures for principal subspace estimation have both emerged as highly active research topics
(
Cape et al., 2019a
;
Xia, 2021
;
Bao et al., 2021
;
Xia and Yuan, 2021
;
Cheng et al., 2021
;
Agterberg et al., 2021
;
Fan et al., 2022a
;
Fan et al., 2022b
;

;

;
Tang et al., 2017a
;
Tang and Priebe, 2018
;
Li and Li, 2018
;
Du and Tang, 2023
)
.
At a high level, the study of asymptotic distributional theory for subspace estimation in the matrix denoising model can be divided into two categories.
On the one hand, there is distributional theory for quantities related to the subspace spanned by a subset of singular vectors or eigenvectors, such as the norm of the difference of orthogonal projection matrices

(
Xia, 2021
;
Bao et al., 2021
;
Tang et al., 2017b
;
Li and Li, 2018
)
.
On the other hand, there is distributional theory pertaining to individual rows or entries of (concatenated matrices of) singular vectors or eigenvectors
(
Cape et al., 2019a
;
Xie, 2024
;
Fan et al., 2022b
;
Yan et al., 2024
;
Athreya et al., 2016
;
Tang et al., 2017a
;
Tang and Priebe, 2018
;
Agterberg et al., 2021
;
Rubin-Delanchy et al., 2022
;
Du and Tang, 2023
)
.
At the intersection of these two categories lies the distributional theory of linear forms of singular vectors or eigenvectors, enabling the study of individual components as well as linear combinations thereof
(
Fan et al., 2022a
;
Cheng et al., 2021
;
Xia and Yuan, 2021
;
Agterberg, 2024
)
.
Both categories pertain to this paper and are thus discussed in more detail below.

Within the first category, several existing works study the distributional theory of singular subspace estimation in (typically unitarily invariant) matrix norms
(
Xia, 2021
;
Bao et al., 2021
;
Tang et al., 2017b
;
Li and Li, 2018
)
.

Xia, 2021
proved a non-asymptotic normal approximation for a statistic involving the Frobenius norm distance between subspaces via a representation formula for differences of orthogonal projections under i.i.d. Gaussian noise.
There, the result does not restrict the matrix aspect ratio
n/m
nor does it require a singular value gap condition other than for
s_{r}
.

Bao et al., 2021
showed that a statistic also related to the Frobenius norm projection distance is asymptotically normal under i.i.d. Gaussian noise.
There, the signal singular values are assumed to be distinct with a gap condition, and the matrix aspect ratio
n/m
is assumed to converge to a constant.
Other related works include
(
Tang et al., 2017a
;
Li and Li, 2018
)
which developed two-sample testing methodology based on the limiting distribution of Procrustes-type subspace distances in the Frobenius norm.

In the second category, which focuses on the asymptotic normality of individual rows or entries of matrices of singular vectors or eigenvectors,
Cape et al., 2019a
established row-wise normality of the Procrustes type subspace difference
\widehat{\mathbf{U}}\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{U}}-\mathbf{U}
.

Xie, 2024
further developed non-asymptotic Berry–Esseen-type bounds for the rows of the Procrustes-type subspace differences
\widehat{\mathbf{U}}\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{U}}-\mathbf{U}
in the weak signal regime.

Agterberg et al., 2021
further established the limiting distribution of rows and entries under heteroskedastic and possibly dependent noise.
In the related setting of latent space network analysis,
Athreya et al., 2016
;
Tang and Priebe, 2018
;
Rubin-Delanchy et al., 2022
derived the limiting distribution of individual rows or entries of spectral graph embeddings involving the adjacency matrix and graph Laplacian.

Fan et al., 2022b
;
Du and Tang, 2023
used the limiting distribution of individual rows of the adjacency or Laplacian embedding to conduct node-wise latent position network hypothesis tests.

As for the distributional theory of linear forms of singular vectors or eigenvectors, at the intersection of the aforementioned two categories,

Cheng et al., 2021
showed the asymptotic normality of inner products between a fixed vector and an individual observed eigenvector under asymmetry, heteroskedastic noise, and small eigengaps.

Agterberg, 2024
established a Berry–Esseen-type bound for a de-biased inner product between a fixed vector and an individual observed eigenvector under a small-eigengap condition in the context of both matrix denoising and principal component analysis.

Fan et al., 2022a
derived the asymptotic normality of linear and bilinear forms involving individual signal eigenvectors and observed eigenvectors of symmetric matrices.

Xia and Yuan, 2021
extended the result of
Xia, 2021
to enable inference for linear forms in the trace regression setting.

A closely related topic is the distributional theory of eigenvectors of sample covariance matrices under the spiked covariance model
(
Yan et al., 2024
;
Bao et al., 2020
;
Koltchinskii and Lounici, 2016
;
Koltchinskii et al., 2020
;
Bloemendal et al., 2014
)
.
Notably,
Yan et al., 2024
established a non-asymptotic Gaussian approximation for the rows of the principal subspace matrix, as well as individual entries in the covariance matrix estimator under heteroskedastic noise and missing data.
En route, the authors also prove a second-order performance guarantee for matrix denoising using the output of their estimation algorithm.

Finally, we note that the Gumbel distribution (the limiting distribution of our test statistic) has appeared elsewhere in the literature for extreme value-based testing procedures though for different problem settings
(
Donoho and Jin, 2004
;
Hu et al., 2021
;
Fan et al., 2022c
)
.
Briefly,
Donoho and Jin, 2004
developed a higher criticism test statistic for the multiple testing problem, where the test statistic is obtained by taking the maximum over an empirical process related to the fraction of tests that are significant at a given level.

Hu et al., 2021
proposed a goodness-of-fit test for the stochastic blockmodel by examining the maximum entry-wise deviation of the centered and rescaled adjacency matrix of a network.

Fan et al., 2022c
considered testing whether a group of network nodes share similar membership profiles by apply the test in
Fan et al., 2022b
to pairs of nodes in a group and then computing the maximum thereof.

Preliminaries

Model assumptions

This paper considers the matrix denoising model in
Equation

1
under the following specifications.

Assumption 1

(Row-wise Gaussian noise)
.

Let
\ell
denote the multiplicity of the smallest non-zero signal singular value
s_{r}
.
The
n\times m
noise matrix
\mathbf{E}
has independent and identically distributed Gaussian rows satisfying
\mathbf{E}_{i,\bm{\cdot}}\sim{\mathcal{N}}_{m}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{D})
as follows.

•

If
\ell=1
, then assume
\mathbf{D}=\operatorname{diag}(\sigma_{1}^{2},\dots,\sigma_{m}^{2})
, where there exist positive constants
c_{\sigma}
and
\sigma^{2}
such that
0<c_{\sigma}\leq\sigma_{m}^{2}\leq\dots\leq\sigma_{1}^{2}\leq\sigma^{2}<\infty
.

•

If
\ell\geq 2
, then assume
\mathbf{D}=\sigma^{2}\mathbf{I}_{m}
, where
0<\sigma^{2}<\infty
is a constant.

Assumption 2

(Matrix aspect ratio)
.

The matrix dimensions
n
and
m\equiv m(n)
satisfy
n/m\simeq 1
as
n\to\infty
.

Assumption 3

(Signal singular vectors)
.

The delocalization parameter

\mu\coloneqq\max\left\{\frac{n}{r}\|\mathbf{U}\|_{2,\infty}^{2},\frac{m}{r}\|\mathbf{V}\|_{2,\infty}^{2}\right\}\geq 1

satisfies
\mu\simeq 1
.

Assumption 4

(Signal singular values)
.

The smallest non-zero signal singular value
s_{r}
satisfies
\kappa\sigma\sqrt{rn}\log n\ll s_{r}
and has fixed multiplicity
\ell
, where necessarily
\ell\in[\![r]\!]
. Further,
r\coloneqq\operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{M})
is fixed and
\kappa\equiv\kappa(\mathbf{M})
is bounded as
n\to\infty
.

1
posits Gaussian noise which is by far the most common and widely studied setting considered in the matrix denoising literature.
The non-increasing ordering of the noise parameters
(\sigma_{i})_{i\in[\![m]\!]}
holds without loss of generality and is made for notational convenience, since it can always be achieved by applying an appropriate permutation.
Here, the influence of the multiplicity
\ell
on the permissible structure of the covariance matrix
\mathbf{D}
reflects a trade-off between flexibility and specificity when modeling the signal and noise.

2
posits that the matrix dimensions grow at a comparable rate.
This is another standard condition in the literature and is used here to enable studying the concentration properties of linear and quadratic forms involving
\mathbf{E}
and
\mathbf{E}^{\mathsf{T}}
.

3
prevents localization of mass or energy in the individual entries and rows of the matrices of left and right singular vectors.
Assumptions of this type are ubiquitous in the modern literature on entrywise and row-wise matrix perturbation analysis, extending well beyond the matrix denoising problem studied in this paper.
Here, delocalization enables the establishment of uniform row-wise error bounds (see
Lemma

1
), an important tool and precursor to our subsequent asymptotic distributional theory and methodology for hypothesis testing.

4
posits that the smallest non-zero signal singular value diverges sufficiently quickly (namely, slightly faster than the rate
\sigma\sqrt{n}
) and can be interpreted as a form of population-level signal strength.
The assumptions on the rank and condition number of the signal matrix further control the complexity of the model. Assumptions of this type, too, are pervasive in the literature on spectral methods.

All of the above assumptions are standard and well-motivated by the existing literature; see
Section

1.2
for a detailed though incomplete list of references.
Nevertheless, these assumptions will subsequently be revisited and discussed in more detail throughout this paper.

Row-wise perturbation analysis and first-order approximation

The row-specific concentration and distributional properties of
{\widehat{\mathbf{U}}\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{U}}-\mathbf{U}}
are prerequisite for obtaining the limiting distribution of
\|\widehat{\mathbf{U}}\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{U}}-\mathbf{U}\|_{2,\infty}

To date, the asymptotic normality of individual rows of
\widehat{\mathbf{U}}\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{U}}-\mathbf{U}
is a well-studied phenomenon and based on the fact that
\widehat{\mathbf{U}}\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{U}}-\mathbf{U}
is well-approximated by a linear transformation of the noise matrix
\mathbf{E}

(
Cape et al., 2019a
;
Xie, 2024
;
Yan et al., 2024
;
Tang and Priebe, 2018
;
Rubin-Delanchy et al., 2022
;
Agterberg and Cape, 2023
)
.
This topic has received considerable attention, yet existing off-the-shelf bounds are not sufficiently refined or user-friendly for our purposes.
To achieve our stated goals, we require the following lemma which establishes uniform row-wise error bounds for approximating the leading left singular vectors.

Lemma 1

(First-order approximation of subspace differences)
.

Under
1
,
2
,
3
and
4
, the bounds

\displaystyle\|\widehat{\mathbf{U}}\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{U}}-\mathbf{U}\|_{2,\infty}

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{\sigma\sqrt{r\log n}}{s_{r}}\left(1+\frac{\sigma\sqrt{n}}{s_{r}}\frac{\kappa\sqrt{\mu}}{\sqrt{\log n}}\right)

(4)

\displaystyle\|\widehat{\mathbf{U}}\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{U}}-\mathbf{U}-\mathbf{EVS}^{-1}\|_{2,\infty}\lesssim\frac{\sigma\sqrt{r\log n}}{s_{r}}\left(\frac{\sigma\sqrt{n}}{s_{r}}+\frac{\sigma\sqrt{n}}{s_{r}}\frac{\kappa\sqrt{\mu}}{\sqrt{\log n}}+\sqrt{\frac{\mu r}{n}}\right)

(6)

Here,
\mathbf{E}_{i,\bm{\cdot}}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{VS}^{-1}\sim{\mathcal{N}}_{r}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{S}^{-1}\mathbf{V}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{V}\mathbf{S}^{-1})
for each
i
, so it follows that
X_{i}^{2}
is a sum of
r
(possibly non-identical and non-independent) squared zero-mean Gaussian random variables.
Though admitting non-trivial structure, the moment generating function of quadratic forms of Gaussian vectors such as
X_{i}^{2}
is understood
(
Mathai and Provost, 1992
)
and is used heavily in the proofs of our main results via the following technical lemma.
The proof of
Lemma

2
is provided in
Appendix

C
.

Lemma 2

(Moment generating function of
X_{i}^{2}
)
.

Define the matrix
\mathbf{P}\coloneqq 2\mathbf{S}^{-2}\mathbf{V}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{DV}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times r}
.
The following properties hold.

1.

The matrix
\mathbf{P}
has eigenvalues
\lambda_{1}\geq\lambda_{2}\geq\dots\geq\lambda_{r}>0
where
\lambda_{1}\in[2\sigma_{m}^{2}/s_{r}^{2},2r\sigma_{1}^{2}/s_{r}^{2}]
. In particular, if
\mathbf{D}=\sigma^{2}\mathbf{I}_{m}
, then

\displaystyle(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\dots,\lambda_{r})=\left(\frac{2\sigma^{2}}{s_{r}^{2}},\frac{2\sigma^{2}}{s_{r-1}^{2}},\dots,\frac{2\sigma^{2}}{s_{1}^{2}}\right).

2.

Under
1
, for all
i\in[\![n]\!]
, the moment generating function of
X_{i}^{2}
is given by

\displaystyle M_{X_{i}^{2}}(t)=\det(\mathbf{I}_{r}-t\mathbf{P})^{-1/2}=\prod_{j=1}^{r}(1-\lambda_{j}t)^{-1/2},\quad\text{for~}t<1/\lambda_{1}.

Notably, under
1
, it holds that
\lambda_{1}=\cdots=\lambda_{\ell}
.
In addition, the values
(\lambda_{i})_{i\in[\![r]\!]}
are functions of the (diverging) signal singular values
(s_{i})_{i\in[\![r]\!]}
which change with
n
and
m
.
Put differently, each random variable
X_{i}
in
\max_{i\in[\![n]\!]}X_{i}
(not just the maximum itself) changes with
n
and
m
, in stark contrast to traditional settings in extreme value theory.

To avoid possible degeneracy or boundary-type situations in
Section

3
when
r>1
and
\ell<r
, we impose an additional mild assumption on the separation between
\lambda_{1}
, the largest eigenvalue of
\mathbf{P}
, and the next-largest distinct eigenvalue.

Assumption 5

(Gap between
\lambda_{\ell}
and
\lambda_{\ell+1}
)
.

If
r>1
and
\ell<r
, assume there exist constants
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
and
c_{\lambda}\in(0,1)
such that
\lambda_{\ell+1}/\lambda_{\ell}<c_{\lambda}
holds for all
n\geq n_{0}
.

5
prevents
\lambda_{\ell}/\lambda_{\ell+1}\to 1
as
n\to\infty
. This mild assumption holds for many typical choices of
\mathbf{M}
and
\mathbf{D}
via
(s_{i})_{i\in[\![r]\!]}
and
(\sigma_{i})_{i\in[\![m]\!]}
.
For example, if
\mathbf{D}=\sigma^{2}\mathbf{I}_{m}
, then
5
holds for any signal matrix
\mathbf{M}
satisfying
s_{r}/s_{r-\ell}<\sqrt{c_{\lambda}}
.

We pause here to emphasize that
\lambda_{1}=\lambda_{\ell}
holds no matter the value of
\ell
.
Thus, for simplicity of presentation and to avoid potential confusion regarding the downstream role of
\ell
in what follows, we use
\lambda_{1}
rather than
\lambda_{\ell}
.

Main results: distributional theory and hypothesis testing

Extreme value analysis for two-to-infinity norm subspace estimation

For ease of notation, in this section we let
X
denote an independent and identically distributed copy of
X_{1}
in
Equation

6
.
In order to obtain normalizing sequences in
n
for
X
, this section identifies a random variable (more precisely, a sequence of random variables indexed by
n
) that is
tail equivalent
to
X

(
Resnick, 2007
, Section 1.5)
, namely a random variable whose distribution shares upper-tail properties with
X
and whose normalizing sequences are comparatively more straightforward to compute.

Definition 1

(Tail equivalence)
.

Two cumulative distribution functions
S
and
T
are said to be tail equivalent if they have the same right endpoint
x_{0}\coloneqq\sup\{x:S(x)<1\}=\sup\{x:T(x)<1\}
and satisfy the relationship

\displaystyle\lim_{x\to x_{0}}\frac{1-S(x)}{1-T(x)}=A\in(0,\infty).

With this concept in mind, let
H
denote a random variable following the generalized gamma distribution with scale parameter
\sqrt{\lambda_{1}}
and shape parameters
\ell
and
2
, i.e.,
H\sim\operatorname{GG}(\sqrt{\lambda_{1}},\ell,2)
.
In particular,
H
has probability density function
f_{H}(x)=\frac{2}{\lambda_{1}^{\ell/2}\Gamma(\ell/2)}x^{\ell-1}\exp(-x^{2}/\lambda_{1})
for
x\geq 0
.
For reference, this is equivalent to a
\sqrt{\lambda_{1}/2}
-scaled chi distribution with
\ell
degrees of freedom, corresponding to the square root of a sum of
\ell
squared i.i.d. Gaussian random variables with mean zero and variance
\lambda_{1}/2
.
Important special cases of
H
are
\ell=1
, which reduces to the half-normal distribution with scale parameter
\sqrt{\lambda_{1}/2}
, and
\ell=2
, which reduces to the Rayleigh distribution with scale parameter
\sqrt{\lambda_{1}/2}
.

Proposition

3
establishes the tail equivalence of
X
and
H
, a key technical finding in this paper.
The proof of
Proposition

3
is provided in
Section

A.1
and makes crucial use of saddle point density approximation
(
Daniels, 1954
)
, discussed in detail in
Section

A.1.1
.

Proposition 3

(Tail equivalence of
X
and
H
)
.

Under
1
,
4
and
5
, for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
, it holds that

\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{1-F_{H}(x)}{1-F_{X}(x)}=A_{{\mathbf{s}},n}\coloneqq\begin{cases}1&\text{if }\;r=1\text{ or }\;\ell=r,\\
\prod_{j=\ell+1}^{r}\left(1-\frac{\lambda_{j}}{\lambda_{1}}\right)^{1/2}&\text{if }\;r>1\text{ and }\;\ell<r.\end{cases}

Necessarily,
A_{{\mathbf{s}},n}\in(0,1]
.

In words,
Proposition

3
establishes that the right tail of the random variable
X
, which is equal in distribution to the Euclidean norm of the Gaussian random vector
\mathbf{E}_{1,\bm{\cdot}}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{V}\mathbf{S}^{-1}
, is governed by the eigenvalue parameters
(\lambda_{i})_{i\in[\![r]\!]}
, which intricately depend on the interplay between spectral properties of the signal and noise model quantities.
Here, the term
A_{{\mathbf{s}},n}
quantifies the discrepancy between the upper tail of
X
and of the reference
H
.
This non-negative term is bounded away from zero due to
5
, though more tightly spaced values of
(\lambda_{i})_{i\in[\![r]\!]}
lead to smaller values of
A_{{\mathbf{s}},n}
, indicating a heavier tail of
X
relative to the generalized gamma tail of
H
.

In order to establish
Theorem

4
, one of our main results, the remaining key ingredient is to apply a fundamental result in extreme value theory
(
Resnick, 2007
, Proposition 1.19)
which establishes a correspondence between normalizing sequences for tail equivalent random variables.
This enables obtaining normalizing sequences for
X
using (easier to compute) normalizing sequences for
H
.
Finally, using
Lemma

1
, we obtain the limiting distribution of
\|\widehat{\mathbf{U}}\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{U}}-\mathbf{U}\|_{2,\infty}
in
Theorem

4
which is proved
Section

A.2
.

Theorem 4

(Extreme value asymptotics for singular subspace estimation)
.

Let
{G}
denote a standard Gumbel random variable with cumulative distribution function
F_{{G}}(x)=\exp(-\exp(-x))
.
Under
1
,
2
,
3
,
4
and
5
, it holds that

\displaystyle T_{{\mathbf{s}},n}\coloneqq a_{n}^{-1}\left(\|\widehat{\mathbf{U}}\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{U}}-\mathbf{U}\|_{2,\infty}-b_{n}\right)+\log(A_{{\mathbf{s}},n})\rightsquigarrow{G}

as
n\to\infty
, where
(a_{n})_{n\geq 1}
and
(b_{n})_{n\geq 1}
are normalizing sequences given by

\displaystyle a_{n}=\frac{\sqrt{\lambda_{1}}}{2\sqrt{\log n}},\quad b_{n}=\sqrt{\lambda_{1}\log n}+\frac{\sqrt{\lambda_{1}}(\ell-2)\log\log n}{4\sqrt{\log n}}-\frac{\sqrt{\lambda_{1}}\log\Gamma(\ell/2)}{2\sqrt{\log n}}

and where
\Gamma(\cdot)
denotes the gamma function.

In
Theorem

4
,
\log(A_{{\mathbf{s}},n})\in(-\infty,0]
is a necessary bias term that simultaneously reflects properties of the top
r
signal singular values and of the upper tail comparison between
X
and
H
discussed above.
Further, note that the normalizing sequences
(a_{n})_{n\geq 1}
and
(b_{n})_{n\geq 1}
themselves depend on the signal singular values.

Remark 1

(Scaling property of the generalized gamma distribution)
.

An important subtlety is that the distribution of
H
implicitly depends on the number of rows
n
via the shape parameter
\sqrt{\lambda_{1}}
which is a function of the non-zero singular values of
\mathbf{M}
(see
Lemma

2
).
For instance, if
\mathbf{D}=\sigma^{2}\mathbf{I}_{m}
, then
\lambda_{1}=2\sigma^{2}/s_{r}^{2}
and so
\sqrt{\lambda_{1}}=o(1)
since
s_{r}\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
by
4
.
Nevertheless, boundary-type behavior or degeneracy are avoided here by the scaling property of the generalized gamma distribution.
In particular, for any
\rho>0
it holds that
H_{\rho}\overset{\operatorname{d}}{=}\rho H_{1}
, where
H_{\rho}\sim\operatorname{GG}(\rho,\ell,2)
.
Consequently, given
\alpha_{1,n}
and
\beta_{1,n}
,

\displaystyle\alpha_{1,n}^{-1}\left(\max_{i\in[\![n]\!]}H_{1,i}-\beta_{1,n}\right)\overset{\operatorname{d}}{=}(\rho\alpha_{1,n})^{-1}\left(\max_{i\in[\![n]\!]}H_{\rho,i}-\rho\beta_{1,n}\right)

(7)

where
H_{\rho,1},\dots,H_{\rho,n}
denote i.i.d. copies of
H_{\rho}
.
Thus, if
(\alpha_{1,n})_{n\geq 1}
and
(\beta_{1,n})_{n\geq 1}
are real-valued sequences such that the left-hand side of
Equation

7
converges in distribution to an extreme value distribution as
n\to\infty
, then
(\rho\alpha_{1,n})_{n\geq 1}
and
(\rho\beta_{1,n})_{n\geq 1}
are corresponding normalizing sequences for
H_{\rho}
such that the right-hand side of
Equation

7
converges in distribution to the same extreme value distribution, irrespective of whether
\rho
changes with
n
.
We make crucial use of this property which serves as a bridge between our high-dimensional model setting and classical techniques in extreme value theory.

Below,
Theorem

5
bounds the Kolmogorov–Smirnov distance between the cumulative distribution function of the statistic
T_{{\mathbf{s}},n}
and that of the standard Gumbel distribution, yielding a non-asymptotic distributional guarantee that strengthens
Theorem

4
.
The proof of
Theorem

5
is provided in
Section

A.3
.

Theorem 5

(Extreme value convergence rate for singular subspace estimation)
.

Let
F_{n}
denote the cumulative distribution function of
T_{{\mathbf{s}},n}
in
Theorem

4
.
Under
1
,
2
,
3
,
4
and
5
, there exists
n_{*}\in\mathbb{N}
and a constant
C_{*}>0
such that if
n\geq n_{*}
, then

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}|F_{n}(x)-F_{{G}}(x)|\leq C_{*}\left\{\frac{\kappa\sigma\sqrt{rn}\log n}{s_{r}}\left(\frac{1}{\kappa}+\frac{\sqrt{\mu}}{\sqrt{\log n}}\right)+\frac{\ell\log\log n}{\log n}\right\}

with probability at least
1-O(n^{-5})
.

The first term on the right-hand side of the bound in
Theorem

5
arises from the first-order approximation error established in
Lemma

1
using perturbation analysis and does not involve
\ell
, the multiplicity of
s_{r}
.
In contrast, the second term reflects the intrinsic error rate due to extreme value convergence per the study of
Equation

6
and does involve
\ell
.
Although both terms vanish asymptotically as
n\to\infty
, the overall error rate depends on the magnitude of
s_{r}
.
Namely, if
n^{1/2}\log n\ll s_{r}\lesssim n^{1/2}\log^{2}n/\log\log n
, which can be interpreted as corresponding to a weak signal strength regime, then the first term due to the perturbation analysis dominates the overall error.
Conversely, if
s_{r}\gg n^{1/2}\log^{2}n/\log\log n
, which can be interpreted as corresponding to a moderate or strong signal strength regime (encompassing the vast majority of permitted
s_{r}
values), then the second term due to the underlying extreme value convergence dominates the overall error.

Plug-in extreme value test statistic using de-biased sample singular values

In what follows, we pursue hypothesis testing and inference for
\mathbf{U}
, and this leads us to treat the unknown population signal singular values as nuisance parameters.
This section obtains shrinkage-type de-biased estimators
(\widetilde{s}_{i})_{i\in[\![r]\!]}
for the signal singular values using the leading sample singular values of
\widehat{\mathbf{M}}
, thus yielding a data-driven version of
Theorem

4
that begets statistical hypothesis testing.
To enable explicit calculations, and as is often done in the existing literature, we consider some additional simplifying model assumptions as described below.

Assumption 6

(Model simplification for de-biased plug-in estimation)
.

The following items hold.

(i)

(Entry-wise Gaussian noise) The entries of the noise matrix
\mathbf{E}
are i.i.d.
{\mathcal{N}}(0,\sigma^{2})
for some constant
0<\sigma^{2}<\infty
.

(ii)

(Limiting matrix aspect ratio) The matrix dimensions
n
and
m\equiv m(n)
satisfy
\min\{n/m,m/n\}\to c\in(0,1]
as
n\to\infty
.

(iii)

(Well-separated signal singular values) There exist finite constants
n_{0}\geq 1
and
c_{s}>1
such that
\min_{i\in[\![r-1]\!]}\{s_{i}/s_{i+1}\}\geq c_{s}
for all
n\geq n_{0}
.

Item

(i)
strengthens
1
by positing that the entries of
\mathbf{E}
are independent and identically distributed
{\mathcal{N}}(0,\sigma^{2})
random variables to enable computing the almost sure limits of
(\widehat{s}_{i})_{i\in[\![r]\!]}
using the techniques in
Benaych-Georges and Nadakuditi, 2012
.

Item

(ii)
strengthens
2
so that the matrices
\widehat{\mathbf{M}}
,
\mathbf{M}
, and
\mathbf{E}
have a specified limiting aspect ratio, to facilitate direct calculations.

Item

(iii)
, together with
4
, is a singular value gap condition between the non-zero signal singular values, ensuring that the singular value gaps grow at a rate comparable to the singular values themselves.
This separation ensures that the fluctuations of
(\widehat{s}_{i})_{i\in[\![r]\!]}
around their deterministic locations are of order
n^{-1/2}
, which is needed for a theoretical analysis of the de-biasing procedure of
(\widehat{s}_{i})_{i\in[\![r]\!]}
.

Proposition

6
establishes de-biased singular value estimators for our model setting and is proved in
Section

A.4.2
.

Proposition 6

(De-biased singular value estimators)
.

Invoke
1
,
4
and
6
.
For each
i\in[\![r]\!]
, define the estimator

\widetilde{s}_{i}\coloneqq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\widehat{s}_{i}^{2}-(1+c)\sigma^{2}N+\sqrt{[\widehat{s}_{i}^{2}-(1+c)\sigma^{2}N]^{2}-4c\sigma^{4}N^{2}}\right)^{1/2}

where
N\coloneqq\max\{n,m\}
.
If
n
is sufficiently large, then with probability at least
1-O(n^{-6})
the estimators
(\widetilde{s}_{i})_{i\in[\![r]\!]}
satisfy

\frac{\widetilde{s}_{i}}{s_{i}}=1+O\left(\frac{\sigma^{4}n^{2}}{s_{i}^{4}}+\frac{\sigma\log^{1/2}n}{s_{i}}\right)

for each
i\in[\![r]\!]
.

Proposition

6
is the key ingredient for establishing
Theorem

7
which is a plug-in version of
Theorem

4
that accounts for the signal singular values.

Theorem

7
is proved in
Section

A.5
.

Theorem 7

(Data-driven plug-in version of
Theorem

4
)
.

Let
(\widetilde{\lambda}_{j})_{j\in[\![r]\!]}
denote the eigenvalues of
\sigma^{2}\widetilde{\mathbf{S}}^{-2}
where
\widetilde{\mathbf{S}}=\operatorname{diag}(\widetilde{s}_{1},\dots,\widetilde{s}_{r})
per

Define

\displaystyle\widetilde{T}_{\tilde{\mathbf{s}},n}\coloneqq\widetilde{a}_{n}^{-1}\left(\|\widehat{\mathbf{U}}\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{U}}-\mathbf{U}\|_{2,\infty}-\widetilde{b}_{n}\right)+\log(\widetilde{A}_{\tilde{\mathbf{s}},n})

where

\widetilde{a}_{n}=\dfrac{{\widetilde{\lambda}_{1}}^{1/2}}{2\sqrt{\log n}}

and

\widetilde{b}_{n}=\widetilde{\lambda}_{1}^{1/2}\sqrt{\log n}-\dfrac{{\widetilde{\lambda}_{1}}^{1/2}\log\log n}{4\sqrt{\log n}}-\dfrac{{\widetilde{\lambda}_{1}}^{1/2}\log\Gamma(1/2)}{2\sqrt{\log n}}

and

\displaystyle\widetilde{A}_{\tilde{\mathbf{s}},n}\coloneqq\begin{cases}1&\text{if~}r=1,\\
\prod_{j=2}^{r}\left(1-\frac{\widetilde{\lambda}_{j}}{\widetilde{\lambda}_{1}}\right)^{1/2}&\text{if~}r>1.\end{cases}

Under
1
,
3
,
4
,
5
and

, it holds that

as

.

Hypothesis testing for singular subspaces

The asymptotic distributional theory for singular subspace estimation in
Sections

3.1
and
3.2
enables testing for singular subspace structure. Conceptually, we are interested in testing between null and alternative hypotheses of the form

\displaystyle\operatorname{H}_{0}:\mathbf{U}=\mathbf{U}_{0}\quad\text{versus}\quad\operatorname{H}_{\operatorname{A}}:\mathbf{U}=\mathbf{U}_{1}.

(8)

More precisely, we consider sequences of hypotheses indexed by
n

\operatorname{H}_{0}^{(n)}:\mathbf{U}=\mathbf{U}_{0,n}
versus
\operatorname{H}_{\operatorname{A}}^{(n)}:\mathbf{U}=\mathbf{U}_{1,n}

to denote

To accommodate

\Theta\equiv\Theta({r,n,C_{\mu}})\coloneqq\left\{\mathbf{U}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r}:\mathbf{U}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{U}=\mathbf{I}_{r},\sqrt{r/n}\leq\|\mathbf{U}\|_{2,\infty}\leq\sqrt{C_{\mu}r/n}\right\}

is a constant.

4
and

imply asymptotic Type I error control for the hypothesis testing problem posed in

.

(Asymptotic Type I error probability)
.

Under

,

,

,

and

\E_{\operatorname{H}_{0}}[{\phi}_{\alpha,n}(\widehat{\mathbf{U}})]\to\alpha

as

.
Similarly, under
1
,
3
,
4
,
5
and
6
, the test (sequence)
\widetilde{\phi}_{\alpha,n}(\widehat{\mathbf{U}})=\mathds{1}{\{\widetilde{T}_{\tilde{\mathbf{s}},n}\geq F_{G}^{-1}({1-\alpha})\}}

.

Next, for ease of presentation, given two matrices
\mathbf{A}
and

of the same dimensions, let
\mathbf{R}(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B})\coloneqq\operatorname{{sgn}}(\mathbf{A}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{B})

,

Test statistic distribution under the null hypothesis

This section empirically investigates the finite-sample properties of
Theorems

and

.
Here, we consider
\mathbf{M}
having

.
The left and right matrices of singular vectors,
\mathbf{U}

Discussion

This paper establishes novel extreme value distributional theory for
\ell_{2,\infty}
singular subspace estimation and inference in the matrix denoising model with Gaussian noise.
In particular, we characterize properties of the two-to-infinity norm for estimation and testing when considering low-rank approximations of the original data.
Our results develop upon and bring together previously separate tools from the study of spectral methods, function approximation, and extreme value theory.

The proposed test statistic, involving the maximum row-wise Euclidean norm difference of
\widehat{\mathbf{U}}\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{U}}-\mathbf{U}
, results in high power for detecting alternative hypotheses that differ from the null in only a few rows.
In particular, our approach overcomes limitations facing existing approaches which typically either
(i) test for aggregate difference in subspace structure but are unable to identify small row-wise differences between hypotheses or
(ii) test for differences between one or several rows in singular subspace matrices but ignore aggregate subspace structure.

We conclude by mentioning possible extensions and opportunities for future work.

•

Two-sample testing.
The results of this paper could in principle be extended beyond the matrix denoising setting to accommodate two-sample testing problems
(
Tang et al., 2017a
;
Tang et al., 2017b
;
Li and Li, 2018
)
.
In particular, suppose one observes

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbf{M}}_{1}=\mathbf{M}_{1}+\mathbf{E}_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m_{1}}\quad\text{and}\quad\widehat{\mathbf{M}}_{2}=\mathbf{M}_{2}+\mathbf{E}_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m_{2}}.

Here,
\mathbf{M}_{1}=\mathbf{U}_{1}\mathbf{S}_{1}\mathbf{V}_{1}^{\mathsf{T}}
and
\mathbf{M}_{2}=\mathbf{U}_{2}\mathbf{S}_{2}\mathbf{V}_{2}^{\mathsf{T}}
are the rank-
r
singular value decompositions, and
\mathbf{E}_{1}
and
\mathbf{E}_{2}
are two independent and identically distributed noise matrices, all satisfying
1
,
2
,
3
and
4
.
In this setting, one may consider testing whether the row-wise structure of
\mathbf{M}_{1}
and
\mathbf{M}_{2}
are the same, namely, the hypotheses

\displaystyle\operatorname{H}_{0}:\mathbf{U}_{1}=\mathbf{U}_{2}\qquad\text{versus}\qquad\operatorname{H}_{\operatorname{A}}:\mathbf{U}_{1}\neq\mathbf{U}_{2}.

Here, an analogue of the test statistic
T_{{\mathbf{s}},n}
or
\widetilde{T}_{\tilde{\mathbf{s}},n}
would rely on the asymptotic distributional theory of
\|\widehat{\mathbf{U}}_{1}\mathbf{R}(\widehat{\mathbf{U}}_{1},\widehat{\mathbf{U}}_{2})-\widehat{\mathbf{U}}_{2}\|_{2,\infty}
.
Row-wise first-order theory for singular vectors
(
Cape et al., 2019a
;
Yan et al., 2024
)
establishes that, under
1
,
2
,
3
and
4
, the rows of the two independent sample singular vector matrices
\widehat{\mathbf{U}}_{1}
and
\widehat{\mathbf{U}}_{2}
are jointly approximately Gaussian.
This observation suggests that the asymptotic distribution of their row-wise maximum deviation may be derived using arguments similar to those employed in the proof of
Theorem

4
.

•

Plug-in inference under heteroskedastic noise.

Theorem

4
assumes that the rows of
\mathbf{E}
are i.i.d. Gaussian with a diagonal covariance matrix
\mathbf{D}
, while the plug-in version in
Theorem

7
further requires
\mathbf{D}=\sigma^{2}\mathbf{I}_{m}
due to the de-biasing procedure of the singular values of
\widehat{\mathbf{M}}
in
Proposition

6
.
This stricter requirement can be relaxed at the expense of more tedious derivations and presentation.
Given a generic, diagonal row-wise noise covariance matrix
\mathbf{D}
that satisfies
1
, the key technical idea is to consider the
whitened
matrices

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbf{M}}^{(w)}=\mathbf{M}^{(w)}+\mathbf{E}^{(w)},~~\widehat{\mathbf{M}}^{(w)}\coloneqq\widehat{\mathbf{M}}\mathbf{D}^{-1/2},~~\mathbf{M}^{(w)}\coloneqq\mathbf{M}\mathbf{D}^{-1/2},~~\widehat{\mathbf{E}}^{(w)}\coloneqq\widehat{\mathbf{E}}\mathbf{D}^{-1/2},

so that
\mathbf{E}^{(w)}
has i.i.d. entries.
This approach is especially effective in the current left-subspace inference setting, as under
1
, the right multiplication of
\mathbf{D}^{-1/2}
does not affect the column space of the original matrix.
Notably, writing the singular value decomposition of
\mathbf{SVD}^{-1/2}
as
\mathbf{Q\Sigma T}^{\mathsf{T}}
, we have
\mathbf{M}^{(w)}=\mathbf{USV}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{D}^{-1/2}=\mathbf{UQ}\mathbf{\Sigma T}^{\mathsf{T}}

.
In particular, the left singular subspace matrix of
\mathbf{M}^{(w)}
is
\mathbf{U}^{(w)}\coloneqq\mathbf{UQ}
, which is simply an orthogonal transformation of
\mathbf{U}
.
Furthermore, due to Wedin’s
\sin\Theta
theorem
(
Wedin, 1972
)
, the top-
r
left singular subspace matrix of
\widehat{\mathbf{M}}^{(w)}=\widehat{\mathbf{U}}\widehat{\mathbf{S}}\widehat{\mathbf{V}}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{D}^{-1/2}+\widehat{\mathbf{U}}_{\perp}\widehat{\mathbf{S}}_{\perp}\widehat{\mathbf{V}}_{\perp}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{D}^{-1/2}
, denoted by
\widehat{\mathbf{U}}^{(w)}
, is close to that of
\widehat{\mathbf{U}}\widehat{\mathbf{S}}\widehat{\mathbf{V}}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{D}^{-1/2}
, which is denoted by
\widehat{\mathbf{U}}\widehat{\mathbf{Q}}
, where the SVD of
\widehat{\mathbf{S}}\widehat{\mathbf{V}}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{D}^{-1/2}
is denoted as
\widehat{\mathbf{Q}}\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}\widehat{\mathbf{T}}^{\mathsf{T}}
.
Hence, under appropriate regularity conditions, a limit theorem derived by applying
Theorem

7
to the whitened matrices can likely be extended to yield a corresponding limit theorem for
\|\widehat{\mathbf{U}}\mathbf{R}(\widehat{\mathbf{U}},\mathbf{U})-\mathbf{U}\|_{2,\infty}
.
A rigorous perturbation-theoretic justification of this approximation is nontrivial and thus deferred to future work.
We note that similar approaches for whitening the rows or columns of a heteroskedastic noise matrix have been studied in the context of matrix denoising
(
Gavish et al., 2023
)
as well as principal component analysis
(
Hong et al., 2018
;
Hong et al., 2021
;
Hong et al., 2023
)
.

•

Non-Gaussian noise.
The empirical examples in
Section

4.3
suggest that our asymptotic theory may approximately hold under weaker distributional requirements on the noise matrix,
\mathbf{E}
, as long as the entries of the
i
-th row of
\mathbf{EVS}^{-1}
, namely
\mathbf{E}_{i,\bm{\cdot}}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{VS}^{-1}=(s_{j}^{-1}\mathbf{E}_{i,\bm{\cdot}}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{V}_{\bm{\cdot},j})_{j\in[\![r]\!]}
, each satisfy a Lindeberg condition and are asymptotically Gaussian.
In this case, proving extreme value convergence would foreseeably require an additional step of asymptotically approximating the moment generating function of
\|\mathbf{E}_{i,\bm{\cdot}}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{VS}^{-1}\|_{2}
, as its exact form given in
Lemma

2
is no longer available.
The focus of the present paper is the traditional matrix denoising model with Gaussian noise, so the topic of non-Gaussian noise is deferred to future work.

A.1.1
Deriving the saddle point density approximation

The density in question is that of the random variable

f_{X}(x)=2xf_{X^{2}}(x^{2}),\qquad x\geq 0.

By

M_{X^{2}}(t)=\prod_{j=1}^{r}(1-\lambda_{j}t)^{-{1}/{2}}

\ell(t)\coloneqq tx-K_{X^{2}}(t)
has a unique saddle point on

, which serves as the basis for the ensuing saddle point density approximation. The proof of
Lemma

10
is provided in
Section

D.1
.

Lemma 10
.

For
x>0

\ell(t)\coloneqq tx-K_{X^{2}}(t)
has a unique real-valued saddle point
{\widehat{t}_{x}}<1/\lambda_{1}

where

as

. Moreover, if

{\widehat{t}_{x}}=\frac{1}{\lambda_{1}}-\frac{1}{2x}

The condition

eventually holds since

11
is used throughout the subsequent derivations.

The
saddle point approximation

(
Daniels, 1954

of the density

\displaystyle\widetilde{f}_{X^{2}}(x)\coloneqq\frac{\exp\left(K_{X^{2}}({\widehat{t}_{x}})-{\widehat{t}_{x}}\right)}{\sqrt{K_{X^{2}}^{\prime\prime}({\widehat{t}_{x}})}},

is defined by

\epsilon(x)\coloneqq\frac{f_{X^{2}}(x)-\widetilde{f}_{X^{2}}(x)}{\widetilde{f}_{X^{2}}(x)}.

In order to facilitate the analysis of the relative error at
x
, where
x>\E[X^{2}]
is considered to be a fixed value for now, define a random variable
X^{2}[\widehat{t}_{x}]
whose probability density function is the exponentially tilted density of
{X^{2}}
by
\widehat{t}_{x}
, namely

\displaystyle f_{X^{2}[\widehat{t}_{x}]}(y)\coloneqq\exp\left(\widehat{t}_{x}y-K_{X^{2}}(\widehat{t}_{x})\right)f_{X^{2}}(y),

where the support of this density is

.

is a proper density function since it is non-negative and satisfies

for any

A.1.4
Saddle point density approximations: a brief overview

Saddle point approximations are often employed to approximate densities and interval probabilities involving a sum of random variables
(
Kolassa, 2006
;
Butler, 2007
)
.
Daniels, 1954
showed that for a sample of
N
independent and identically distributed random variables
Z_{1},\dots,Z_{N}
with a non-degenerate MGF, and a continuous and integrable characteristic function, the relative error of the saddle point density approximation of
f_{\overline{Z}_{N}}(x)
, the density of the sample mean
\overline{Z}_{N}
, satisfies

1+\epsilon(x)=f_{W_{x}}(w_{x}(x))=c_{0}+O(N^{-1}),

for all
x
and some constant
c_{0}
. That is, the relative error is uniformly controlled for all
x
as
N
grows, meaning that with a large enough sample size, the re-scaled saddle point density approximation
c_{0}\widetilde{f}_{\overline{Z}_{N}}(x)
is accurate even for large values of
x
where
{f}_{\overline{Z}_{N}}(x)
is very small. This control over relative error is a distinguishing feature of saddle point density approximations compared to Edgeworth expansions, where only the absolute error is controlled over the domain of the density function. The approach of
Daniels, 1954
relies on approximating the inverse Fourier transform of the characteristic function to recover the corresponding density function, utilizing the method of steepest descent for approximating integrals of complex-valued functions. Although the derivation of the saddle point approximation in
Equation

15
avoids the inverse Fourier transform entirely, the presence of the saddle point
\widehat{t}
in the saddle point approximation is a result of choosing the steepest descent contour that passes through the saddle point
\widehat{t}
.

The random variable of interest,
X^{2}
, is typically not a sum of i.i.d. random variables, yet
Lemma

12
shows that
1+\epsilon(x)
, equivalently
f_{W_{x}}(w_{x}(x))
, converges to a constant
c_{0}
as
x\to\infty
, which, by
Equation

17
, results in
c_{0}\widetilde{f}_{X^{2}}(x)\to{f}_{X^{2}}(x)
as
x\to\infty
. In this case, the saddle point density approximation is called
tail exact

(
Barndorff-Nielsen and Klüppelberg, 1999
)
. In general, tail exactness does not guarantee convergence of the approximation to the true density for small, fixed values of
x
near the mean of
X^{2}
. Nevertheless, a tail exact approximation is sufficient for our purposes, as the concept of tail equivalence per
Definition

1
requires
x\to\infty
, which is the regime where the approximation is exact.

The following lemma from extreme value theory enables deriving the normalizing sequences of a distribution via those of a tail equivalent distribution. This tool is useful when it is more straightforward to compute the normalizing sequences of the tail equivalent distribution than to compute those of the original distribution.

A.6.1
Technical lemmas

We first introduce and prove supporting lemmas which will be used to prove
Theorem

9
. First,
Lemma

18
shows that the map
\operatorname{{sgn}}:\mathbb{R}^{n\times r}\times\mathbb{R}^{n\times r}\to\mathbb{R}^{r\times r}
is Lipschitz in its arguments. More specifically, holding one argument fixed, the spectral norm difference of the sign functions when the other argument changes is bounded above by the spectral norm difference of the changed arguments.

Lemma 18

(Bounding orthogonal alignment matrix difference)
.

Under
1
,
2
,
3
,
4
and
7
,

\displaystyle\|\widehat{\mathbf{U}}\{\mathbf{R}(\widehat{\mathbf{U}},\mathbf{U}_{0})-\mathbf{R}(\widehat{\mathbf{U}},\mathbf{U}_{1})\}\|_{2,\infty}\lesssim\left(\frac{\mu r}{n}\right)^{1/2}\|\mathbf{U}_{0}-\mathbf{U}_{1}\|_{2},

with probability exceeding
1-O(n^{-6})
.

Proof.

By definition, the matrices
\mathbf{R}(\widehat{\mathbf{U}},\mathbf{U}_{0})
and
\mathbf{R}(\widehat{\mathbf{U}},\mathbf{U}_{1})
are the orthogonal factors of the polar decompositions of the matrices
\widehat{\mathbf{U}}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{U}_{0}
and
\widehat{\mathbf{U}}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{U}_{1}
, respectively. By properties of the two-to-infinity norm along with perturbation results for the polar factorization of matrices
(
Bhatia, 1994
, Corollary 4.1)
, it holds that

\displaystyle\|\widehat{\mathbf{U}}\{\mathbf{R}(\widehat{\mathbf{U}},\mathbf{U}_{0})-\mathbf{R}(\widehat{\mathbf{U}},\mathbf{U}_{1})\}\|_{2,\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\|\widehat{\mathbf{U}}\|_{2,\infty}\|\mathbf{R}(\widehat{\mathbf{U}},\mathbf{U}_{0})-\mathbf{R}(\widehat{\mathbf{U}},\mathbf{U}_{1})\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\|\widehat{\mathbf{U}}\|_{2,\infty}}{s_{r}(\widehat{\mathbf{U}}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{U}_{1})}\|\mathbf{U}_{0}-\mathbf{U}_{1}\|_{2}

(62)

\displaystyle\lesssim\left(\frac{\mu r}{n}\right)^{1/2}\|\mathbf{U}_{0}-\mathbf{U}_{1}\|_{2},

(63)

where the first two inequalities hold with probability one, and the last asymptotic relation holds with probability exceeding
1-O(n^{-6})
. In more detail,
Equation

62
uses
(
Bhatia, 1994
, Corollary 4.1)
along with the submultiplicativity of the spectral norm.
Equation

63
results from the following two properties. First, by
Lemmas

1
,
1
,
2
,
3
and
4
, the numerator of the fraction in
Equation

62
satisfies

\displaystyle\|\widehat{\mathbf{U}}\|_{2,\infty}=\|\widehat{\mathbf{U}}\mathbf{R}(\widehat{\mathbf{U}},\mathbf{U}_{1})\|_{2,\infty}

\displaystyle=\|\mathbf{U}_{1}+(\widehat{\mathbf{U}}\mathbf{R}(\widehat{\mathbf{U}},\mathbf{U}_{1})-\mathbf{U}_{1})\|_{2,\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\|\mathbf{U}_{1}\|_{2,\infty}+\|\widehat{\mathbf{U}}\mathbf{R}(\widehat{\mathbf{U}},\mathbf{U}_{1})-\mathbf{U}_{1}\|_{2,\infty}\lesssim\left(\frac{\mu r}{n}\right)^{1/2}

(64)

with probability exceeding
1-O(n^{-6})
.
On the other hand, by Wedin’s
\sin\Theta
theorem and
1
and
4
, it holds that

\displaystyle\sqrt{1-s_{r}(\widehat{\mathbf{U}}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{U}_{1})^{2}}=\|\sin\Theta(\widehat{\mathbf{U}},\mathbf{U}_{1})\|_{2}=o(1)

with probability exceeding
1-O(n^{-6})
.
Hence, the denominator of the fraction in
Equation

62
satisfies
s_{r}(\mathbf{U}_{1}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{U}_{0})\to 1
with probability exceeding
1-O(n^{-6})
.
∎

Next,
Lemma

19
asserts that the two-to-infinity norm difference of a pair of hypothesis matrices
\mathbf{U}_{1}
and
\mathbf{U}_{0}
misaligned in a specific manner must be of asymptotic order greater than or equal to the properly aligned hypothesis discrepancy
d_{n}
.

Lemma 19

(Misaligned hypotheses)
.

Under
1
,
2
,
3
,
4
and
7
,

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{U}_{1}\mathbf{R}(\mathbf{U}_{1},\widehat{\mathbf{U}})\mathbf{R}(\widehat{\mathbf{U}},\mathbf{U}_{0})-\mathbf{U}_{0}\|_{2,\infty}\gtrsim(\mu r)^{-1/2}d_{n},

with probability exceeding
1-O(n^{-6})
.

Proof.

The proof consists of two parts, depending on the delocalization of
\mathbf{U}_{1}-\mathbf{U}_{0}
.

(i)
(Delocalized case). Suppose that
\|\mathbf{U}_{1}-\mathbf{U}_{0}\|_{2,\infty}\lesssim\sqrt{\mu r/n}\|\mathbf{U}_{1}-\mathbf{U}_{0}\|_{{\operatorname{F}}}
. Then, defining the two-to-infinity norm optimal Procrustes problem solution
\mathbf{R}_{2,\infty}\coloneqq\argmin_{\mathbf{R}\in{O}_{r}}\|\mathbf{U}_{1}\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{U}_{0}\|_{2,\infty}
, where
O_{r}
denotes the group of

orthogonal matrices, it follows that

\displaystyle d_{n}=\|\mathbf{U}_{1}-\mathbf{U}_{0}\|_{2,\infty}\lesssim\sqrt{\frac{\mu r}{n}}\|\mathbf{U}_{1}-\mathbf{U}_{0}\|_{{\operatorname{F}}}\leq\sqrt{\frac{\mu r}{n}}\|\mathbf{U}_{1}\mathbf{R}_{2,\infty}-\mathbf{U}_{0}\|_{{\operatorname{F}}}\leq\sqrt{\mu r}\|\mathbf{U}_{1}\mathbf{R}_{2,\infty}-\mathbf{U}_{0}\|_{2,\infty},

where we have used the fact that
\mathbf{I}_{r}
is the Frobenius norm optimal Procrustes problem solution which is a consequence of

. This result implies that

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{U}_{1}\mathbf{R}(\mathbf{U}_{1},\widehat{\mathbf{U}})\mathbf{R}(\widehat{\mathbf{U}},\mathbf{U}_{0})-\mathbf{U}_{0}\|_{2,\infty}\geq\|\mathbf{U}_{1}\mathbf{R}_{2,\infty}-\mathbf{U}_{0}\|_{2,\infty}\gtrsim({\mu r})^{-1/2}d_{n}.

(65)

(ii)
(Localized case). Suppose that
\|\mathbf{U}_{1}-\mathbf{U}_{0}\|_{2,\infty}\gg\sqrt{\mu r/n}\|\mathbf{U}_{1}-\mathbf{U}_{0}\|_{{\operatorname{F}}}
. By the reverse triangle inequality, and the orthogonal invariance of the spectral norm, it holds with probability one that

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{U}_{1}\mathbf{R}(\mathbf{U}_{1},\widehat{\mathbf{U}})\mathbf{R}(\widehat{\mathbf{U}},\mathbf{U}_{0})-\mathbf{U}_{0}\|_{2,\infty}\geq\|\mathbf{U}_{1}-\mathbf{U}_{0}\|_{2,\infty}-\|\mathbf{U}_{1}(\mathbf{R}(\mathbf{U}_{1},\widehat{\mathbf{U}})\mathbf{R}(\widehat{\mathbf{U}},\mathbf{U}_{0})-\mathbf{I}_{r})\|_{2,\infty}.

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{U}_{1}\{\mathbf{R}(\mathbf{U}_{1},\widehat{\mathbf{U}})\mathbf{R}(\widehat{\mathbf{U}},\mathbf{U}_{0})-\mathbf{I}_{r}\}\|_{2,\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\|\mathbf{U}_{1}\|_{2,\infty}\|\mathbf{R}(\widehat{\mathbf{U}},\mathbf{U}_{0})-\mathbf{R}(\widehat{\mathbf{U}},\mathbf{U}_{1})\|_{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\left(\frac{\mu r}{n}\right)^{1/2}\|\mathbf{U}_{1}-\mathbf{U}_{0}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{\mu r}{n}\right)^{1/2}\|\mathbf{U}_{1}-\mathbf{U}_{0}\|_{{\operatorname{F}}}

\displaystyle\ll\|\mathbf{U}_{1}-\mathbf{U}_{0}\|_{2,\infty},

where the last line is by hypothesis. Thus, plugging back into
Equation

66
yields that for a sufficiently large

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{U}_{1}\mathbf{R}(\mathbf{U}_{1},\widehat{\mathbf{U}})\mathbf{R}(\widehat{\mathbf{U}},\mathbf{U}_{0})-\mathbf{U}_{0}\|_{2,\infty}\geq 2d_{n}

(67)

holds with probability exceeding
1-O(n^{-6})

Remark 3
.

The proof of

is divided into two cases due to the intricacies of the

geometry, where the value of

\displaystyle\widetilde{T}_{\tilde{\mathbf{s}},n}

Proof.

Lemma 26

(

Let
(f_{k})_{k\geq 1}
be a sequence of functions which converges uniformly to a function
f
on a set

. Then,

\displaystyle\lim_{k\to\infty}f_{k}(y_{k})=f(y)

for every sequence
\{y_{k}\}\subset E
such that

, and
y\in E
.

In addition,
Lemma

27
states that the Arzelà–Ascoli theorem
(
Rudin, 1976
, Theorem 7.25)
together with weak convergence implies uniform convergence of a sequence of probability density functions.

Lemma 27

(A sufficient condition for uniform convergence)
.

Let
(f_{k})_{k\geq 1}
be a uniformly bounded and uniformly equicontinuous sequence of probability density functions on a compact set
K
, whose corresponding probability measures
(\mu_{k})_{k\geq 1}
converge weakly to a probability measure
\mu(\mathrm{d}x)\coloneqq f(x)\mathrm{d}x
on
K
. Then,
(f_{k})_{k\geq 1}
converges uniformly to
f
on
K
.

Proof.

By assumption, an arbitrary subsequence
(f_{k_{j}})_{j\geq 1}
is uniformly bounded and uniformly equicontinuous. Hence, by the Arzelà–Ascoli theorem, it has a subsequence
(f_{k_{j_{i}}})_{i\geq 1}
that converges uniformly to
g\in C^{1}(K)
. By Scheffé’s lemma
(
Scheffé, 1947
)
, uniform convergence implies that the measures
(\mu_{k_{j_{i}}}(\mathrm{d}x))_{i\geq 1}
converge weakly to
g(x)\mathrm{d}x
. Due to the uniqueness of weak limits,
f(x)=g(x)
for all
x\in K
. Since the choice of the subsequence was arbitrary, it follows that every subsequence of
(f_{k})_{k\geq 1}
has subsequence that converges uniformly to
f
.

Suppose that
(f_{k})_{k\geq 1}
does not converge uniformly to
f
on
K
. Then, a subsequence
(f_{k_{i}})_{i\geq 1}
can be constructed so that for a finite
\varepsilon>0
,

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in K}|f_{k_{i}}(x)-f(x)|\geq\varepsilon

for all
i\in\mathbb{N}
.
However,
(f_{k_{i}})_{i\geq 1}
must contain a subsequence that converges uniformly to
f
, which is a contradiction. Therefore,
(f_{k})_{k\geq 1}
converges uniformly to
f
on
K
.
∎

Since
W_{k}\rightsquigarrow W

converge weakly to the measure

. Thus,

Lemma

28
states that over a closed interval
[p_{1},p_{2}]\subset\mathbb{R}_{>0}
, the density functions of a family of gamma distributed random variables with shape parameter

are uniformly bounded, Lipschitz, and furthermore, share the same Lipschitz constant over all functions in the family. This is a tool for establishing uniform boundedness and uniform equicontinuity of
(f_{W_{k}})_{k\geq 1}

Lemma 28

(Bounded and Lipschitz family of gamma densities on a closed interval)
.

Let
g\sim\operatorname{Gamma}(1/2,\theta)
where
\theta>0
. For any interval
[p_{1},p_{2}]
such that
0<p_{1}<p_{2}<\infty
, there exist finite positive constants
C_{1}(p_{1})
and
C_{1}(p_{1})
only depending on the left end point of the interval such that

\displaystyle f_{g}(x;\theta)\leq C_{1}(p_{1})\qquad\text{and}\qquad|f^{\prime}_{g}(x;\theta)|\leq C_{2}(p_{1}),

for all
\theta>0
and
x\in[p_{1},p_{2}]

Proof.

Since

\displaystyle f_{g}(x;\theta)=\frac{1}{\Gamma(1/2)\theta^{1/2}}x^{-1/2}\exp(-x/\theta),

(125)

applying the chain rule yields

\displaystyle f^{\prime}_{g}(x;\theta)=-\frac{f_{g}(x;\theta)}{2x}-\frac{f_{g}(x;\theta)}{\theta}.

(126)

Differentiating
f_{g}(x;\theta)
and
f_{g}(x;\theta)/\theta
over
\theta>0
, respectively, and solving for zero and checking the second derivative yields that
f_{g}(x;\theta)
and
f_{g}(x;\theta)/\theta
are maximized at
\theta=2x
and
\theta=2x/3
respectively. Hence, plugging in the maximizers into
Equations

125
and
126
and using the fact that
x\geq p_{1}
yields

\displaystyle f_{g}(x;\theta)\leq\frac{\exp(-1/2)}{\Gamma(1/2)\sqrt{2}x}\leq\frac{\exp(-1/2)}{\Gamma(1/2)\sqrt{2}p_{1}}\eqqcolon C_{1}(p_{1})

and

\displaystyle|f^{\prime}_{g}(x;\theta)|\leq\left|-\frac{f_{g}(x;\theta)}{2x}\right|+\left|\frac{f_{g}(x;\theta)}{\theta}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\exp(-1/2)}{\Gamma(1/2)2^{3/2}x^{2}}+\frac{3^{3/2}\exp(-3/2)}{\Gamma(1/2)2^{3/2}x^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\exp(-1/2)+3^{3/2}\exp(-3/2)}{\Gamma(1/2)2^{3/2}p_{1}^{2}}

\displaystyle\eqqcolon C_{2}(p_{1}),

for all
x\in[p_{1},p_{2}]
and
\theta>0
.
This completes the proof of
Lemma

28
.

\widehat{a}_{n}
, the function

A(x)\coloneqq\frac{\overline{F}_{H_{1}}(x)}{f_{H_{1}}(x)}=\frac{1}{2x}(1+r(x))

has bounded slope on a domain bounded away from zero. Since both
\widetilde{b}_{n}
and
b_{n,*}
are growing at rate
\log n
,
A(\widehat{b}_{n})=\widehat{a}_{n}
and
A(b_{n,*})=a_{n,*}
, it holds that for
n
large enough,

\frac{|\widehat{a}_{n}-a_{n,*}|}{|\widehat{b}_{n}-b_{n,*}|}\leq 1,

which implies that

\delta_{a}\coloneqq\widehat{a}_{n}-a_{n,*}=O\left(\frac{\log\log n}{(\log n)^{3/2}}\right),

as
n\to\infty
. In conclusion, by Slutsky’s lemma,

\displaystyle\widehat{a}_{n}^{-1}\left(\max_{i\in[\![n]\!]}H_{1,i}-\widehat{b}_{n}\right)=a_{n,*}^{-1}\left(\max_{i\in[\![n]\!]}H_{1,i}-{b}_{n,*}\right)\left(\frac{1}{1+\frac{\delta_{a}}{a_{n,*}}}\right)+\frac{\delta_{b}}{\widehat{a}_{n}}\rightsquigarrow{G},

since
{\delta_{a}}/{a_{n,*}}=O(\log\log n/\log n)
and
{\delta_{b}}/{\widehat{a}_{n}}=O(\log\log n/\log n)
.
By the same reasoning, keeping only the largest order term in
\widehat{a}_{n}
and discarding the
o(\widehat{a}_{n})
terms in
\widehat{b}_{n}
does not affect the convergence in distribution. Thus, scaling both sequences by
\sqrt{\lambda_{1}}
yields the final sequences

\displaystyle a_{n}=\frac{\sqrt{\lambda_{1}}}{2\sqrt{\log n}},\qquad b_{n}=\sqrt{\lambda_{1}\log n}+\frac{\sqrt{\lambda_{1}}(\ell-2)\log\log n}{4\sqrt{\log n}}-\frac{\sqrt{\lambda_{1}}\log\Gamma(\ell/2)}{2\sqrt{\log n}}.

(139)

Lastly, using the fact that

\displaystyle\overline{F}_{H}(x)={\mathbb{P}}(H>x)={\mathbb{P}}\left(\frac{H}{\sqrt{\lambda_{1}}}>\frac{x}{\sqrt{\lambda_{1}}}\right)=\overline{F}_{H_{1}}\left(\frac{x}{\sqrt{\lambda_{1}}}\right),

Equation

138
directly yields

\displaystyle n\overline{F}_{H}({b}_{n})=n\overline{F}_{H_{1,\ell}}(\widehat{b}_{n})=1+O\left(\frac{\log\log n}{\log n}\right),

(140)

which completes the proof of
Lemma

15
.
∎

Balanced two-block stochastic blockmodel

In this section, the behavior of the statistic

7
and
8
