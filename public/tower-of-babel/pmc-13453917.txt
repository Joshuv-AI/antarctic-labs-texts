Embracing complexity in transfusion research
Authors: S F Fustolo‐Gunnink
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractComplexity science investigates how complex systems behave and how we interact with them. Its implementation in clinical transfusion research is limited, even though human cells, organ systems, bodies, hospitals and blood supply systems are all examples of complex systems. Complexity science can help us to better understand the systems we study using novel and existing research methods. It also highlights that there is always unpredictability in complex systems, challenging us to both accept and cope with this uncertainty. This review provides a brief introduction of complex systems, explores why many transfusion‐related research questions should be viewed through the lens of complexity and discusses how we can apply this perspective to transfusion research.Keywords: complex systems, complexity science, evidence to practice translation, transfusion medicine, uncertainty

Complexity science is relatively new but already indispensable. It is important to understand complex systems because they are everywhere. Your brain is a complex system and so is your immune system and every cell in your body [1].

CARS AND HURRICANES

We all investigate systems in one way or another. How we conceptualize these systems determines the questions we ask and the methods we use. Sometimes, we assume systems are predictable and fixable. For example, we expect our car mechanic to know exactly what to do when our car breaks down and confidently drive it as soon as it is repaired. In contrast, sometimes we assume systems are not 100% predictable. For example, despite round‐the‐clock analyses, hurricane evacuation zones always account for a margin of error, and people just outside evacuation zones are acutely aware that their situation may change at the last minute. All systems lie somewhere on this continuum of relatively simple and predictable to very complex and less predictable systems. More importantly, it is not only the system that creates complexity, but also the questions we ask of this system. If we ask ‘are winters colder than summers’, we can quite confidently show simple statistics for an answer. But if we ask ‘what will be the exact temperature in Kuala Lumpur, on 23 June, 2026’, then suddenly we need to consider a host of variables and venture into the complexity of our weather system. If we try to answer this question only with simple statistics, it would be like trying to identify a person on a photograph with extremely low resolution.

UPSIDE DOWN ASSUMPTIONS

In medical research, including transfusion medicine, we tend to treat systems and research questions equally, using the same research methods irrespective of how complex the problem is. We tend to assume that systems are predictable, given sufficient data, and that we can fully understand them with our current research methods. This may not always be true (Figure 1). What if we were to turn this assumption around and assume the opposite? For example, that we will never be able to 100% prevent transfusion‐related antibody development because the body will always find a workaround around our interventions. Would this change the way we investigate antibody development? Or the way we monitor for it, or cope with it in our blood supply systems? This obviously is a theoretical thought experiment, but we perform a similar thought experiment when we assume that ‘once we have enough data, we will be able to fully understand antibody formation and eliminate this problem’. That is a similarly unfounded but important assumption that drives our work and our decision making.

WHAT IS A COMPLEX SYSTEM?

Complexity science is a relatively new interdisciplinary scientific discipline that focuses on the study of complex systems across different domains [1]. It is widely established in many disciplines outside transfusion medicine, illustrated by the presence of complexity science research centres across the globe, scientific journals specifically focusing on complexity and newly started complexity science Master's programmes. In contrast, the uptake of a complex systems perspective in medicine is limited, despite its potential and repeated calls to action [3, 4, 5, 6].

What do complexity scientists mean when they talk about a complex system? They distinguish between complicated and complex systems. Complicated systems may be intricate and have many components, but they have clear boundaries and obvious cause‐and‐effect relationships, and the system is predictable. For instance, a car. Complex systems, in contrast, have certain characteristics that make them, to some extent, unpredictable. They are open, dynamic systems with many interactions and feedback loops but without central control, which can adapt and change their own structure in response to their environment. For instance, hurricanes, but also red blood cells (RBCs), economies and bee colonies. Table 1 provides an overview of typical complex system characteristics. The first four characteristics are required for a system to qualify as complex; the remaining six are system‐wide behaviours that can occur because of these characteristics.

Self‐organization or emergence (Table 1, no. 5) requires further explanation. Emergence means that a system recreates order and organization within itself, without any central control but solely through interactions between its parts and with its environment. For example, economies change their structure when growth rates or political stability changes. Human brains change their structure when we do something repeatedly (‘neurons that fire together, wire together’). Tumour architecture changes depending on nutrient supply and interactions with other cells. Emergence is a surprising phenomenon, because we cannot anticipate what will happen by looking at the behaviour of individual components of the system. For example, blood transfusions can cause severe adverse events such as transfusion‐related acute lung injury (TRALI) due to a complex interplay between multiple factors including the underlying clinical conditions, factors in the transfusion product such as donor HLA and/or human neutrophil antigen antibodies or biological response modifiers and other factors such as increased frailty related to surgery. As a result, TRALI remains highly unpredictable and occurs in some, but not all patients [7, 8].

It is important to understand that the unpredictability of complex systems is, in a sense, a sign of a ‘healthy’ complex system. Unpredictability happens because the system is dynamic and adaptive, which makes these types of systems extremely robust and resilient. But it also makes them notoriously difficult to investigate, and the effects of interventions are difficult to predict. Another important characteristic of complexity is that it is unlikely that there is a single disease pathway that we can target to ‘shift’ the system completely because that would make the system highly vulnerable. There are collateral pathways, there is redundancy and the system's ability to adapt. In short, golden bullets are the exception, unintended off‐target effects are the norm.

WHEN OR WHY DO WE NEED A COMPLEX SYSTEMS APPROACH?

As stated previously, all systems lie somewhere on the spectrum between simple and complex. When is a complexity science approach needed? There is no simple answer to this question; there is no such thing as a complexity test. But there is little debate in the field of biology on the fact that cells, organs and the human body are all complex systems. When we encounter a system that meets the criteria in Table 1 and challenges our ability to understand, predict or modify it, it should make us wonder whether we should be studying it through the lens and using methods of complexity science. Clinical transfusion research—where we study the effects of transfusions in unique individual patients with comorbidities and cotreatments—often meets these requirements.

In many patient populations, clinical benefit of transfusions has not been proven conclusively. For example, a 2025 clinical practice guideline for platelet transfusions, which included 21 randomized controlled trials (RCTs) in different patient populations, concluded that ‘there was no consistent evidence across RCTs to support benefit of platelets impacting clinical outcomes’ [9]. Similar conclusions were reached in a 2023 RBC clinical practice guideline including 52 RCTs and a 2025 Cochrane systematic review on blood transfusion strategies for major bleeding in trauma, which included 18 RCTs [10, 11]. A 2025 Cochrane systematic review on RBC transfusions including 61 RCTs in adults and 8 in children showed no difference between liberal and restrictive transfusion strategies for most patient groups except for gastrointestinal bleeding and critically ill patients with brain injury [12]. Recent guidelines for critically ill patients identified 15 PICOs for which recommendations could not be made due to lack of data [13, 14]. And in several instances transfusions may have caused harm [15, 16, 17]. In short, only a handful of studies showed a clear transfusion effect on clinical outcomes in specific patient populations [12, 18, 19, 20]. Even immediate transfusion effects, such as increments or vital signs changes, vary significantly between patients despite seemingly similar baseline characteristics [21, 22, 23, 24].

What do these findings mean? Are transfusions often ineffective? Or are we not able to identify and measure their effects? From a complex systems perspective, this variability and unpredictability is not surprising since transfusions dynamically interact with many different (patho)physiological processes. RBCs alone have at least 22 different biological functions beyond gas transport [25]. Similarly, the vascular endothelium is described as ‘a highly dynamic and interactive systemically disseminated organ’, and platelets are intricate cells known to contain over 5000 different proteins many of which are released upon activation from their granules, and are not a homogeneous population themselves as there are known distinct platelet subpopulations within individuals which may differentially contribute to pathophysiological processes [26, 27, 28, 29, 30]. No two human bodies are identical, and this applies to both patients and donors. Therefore, individual patient transfusion effects may depend on small but relevant and very dynamic differences between and within patients, their treatments and donor and product characteristics. Given that we are limited in our ability to collect and integrate data on all these different levels and timescales, and considering the methodological issues to appropriately analyse transfusion effects on clinical outcomes, it is to be expected that transfusion effects, even at a group level, remain difficult to identify [31].

So how can complexity science help us?

COMPLEX SYSTEMS APPROACHES

System mapping

Complexity science helps us to identify components and characteristics of complex systems that we should address in research. A first step to identify these is by visualizing complex systems using systems mapping approaches, such as causal loop diagrams [32, 33, 34]. This will result in very large models that may not immediately help in clinical decision making but can form a starting point to help us to, for instance, identify factors that we overlook in research, the presence of feedback loops which could destabilize a system or biological pathways that could cause off‐target effects we could monitor for during clinical studies [35]. For example, most clinical studies in critically ill patients focus on one transfusion product at a time, even though patients often receive multiple types of products, the effects of which may interact [36, 37, 38]. A recent study illustrates this by showing that RBCs affect haemostasis via platelet‐ and non‐platelet‐mediated pathways [39]. Additionally, transfusions can interact with other interventions such as mechanical ventilation [40] or pharmaceutical drugs, as Nemkov et al. showed that 65% of 1366 Food and Drug Administration‐approved drugs affected RBC metabolism in blood donors [41]. A recent study showed that caffeine, one of the most common interventions in neonatal medicine, affects transfusion outcomes as well [42, 43]. This suggests that co‐treatments may affect transfused RBCs, and vice versa, and therefore should be included in systems maps that model transfusion effects, as well as monitored and analysed more thoroughly in RCTs [44].

Ideally, these conceptual models are then converted into system dynamic models and quantified, so that we can use them to simulate the effects of interventions. This has not yet been performed in transfusion research; so to illustrate this, Figure 2 shows an example model developed for Alzheimer's disease, which was subsequently quantified to simulate the effects of different interventions [45, 46]. If we could develop models in which we attempt to simulate the effects of different transfusion strategies, while taking into account dynamic changes in patient characteristics and interactions and feedback loops between many biological pathways, this could potentially be a major leap forward. Although unavoidably imperfect, this approach could serve as a good starting point for the preparation of RCTs.

Measures of complexity

The different characteristics of complex systems, such as feedback loops and non‐linearity (Table 1), cannot be measured using just one method but require a combination of so‐called measures of complexity [47, 48]. For example, many statistical models commonly used in clinical research, such as linear or logistic regression models, assume linearity (Table 1, no. 6). They assume that a change in the exposure will result in a proportional change in the outcome, even though we know many biological processes are non‐linear [49]. For example, body temperature is not linearly related to heart rate. Within a small range, heart rate is fairly constant, but above and below this range, heart rate changes drastically. Theoretically, when we use a linear regression model to assess an association, we could conclude that there is no association when there is, in fact, a non‐linear association. For example, we were unable to identify a linear association between platelet count and bleeding risk in preterm infants [50]. But this does not exclude the possibility that there is a threshold below which bleeding risk does increase significantly (although this threshold is most likely dynamic and will vary between infants). Unless we explicitly explore this, we will overlook it [51]. In addition, even if all associations in a complex system are linear, because they interact and adapt, the overall way the system responds to changes would no longer be linear. In other words, a complex system can amplify or dampen effects, so that minute differences between individual patients could lead to significantly different clinical outcomes, and vice versa. Another example is robustness (Table 1, no. 7). This is not commonly assessed in medical research because it focuses on system‐wide behaviour and requires methods that are less familiar. For instance, we can measure robustness by looking at how long it takes for a system to ‘bounce back’ from a disturbance. The assumption is that a complex system can resist disturbances effectively until it reaches a tipping point, after which the system irreversibly changes into a different state. As the system approaches this tipping point, the time to bounce back after disturbances will increase. If we could somehow measure this dynamic change, this information could warn us that a system is reaching the limits of its robustness [52]. An example is the use of time to heart rate recovery after exercise as a commonly used indicator of cardiac fitness. More generally speaking, alongside investigating patterns, which we can identify in RCTs and observational cohort studies, we need to focus explicitly on mechanisms and dynamic changes, for which we need other study designs [48]. One example is improving our bleeding prediction models from only estimating risk at baseline to estimating adjusted risks over time, and incorporate the changing clinical status of patients [53].

Qualitative research has an important role in complex systems research. Lenet et al. showed that knowledge, beliefs about consequences, social/professional role and identity, social influences, environmental context/resources, behavioural regulation, nature of the behaviours as well as memory, attention and decision processes are important factors that influence intraoperative RBC transfusion decision making [54, 55]. An interesting example was the introduction of small‐volume laboratory assays in a Dutch neonatal intensive care unit, with the aim to reduce iatrogenic blood loss. Unexpectedly, the intervention initially increased the number of tests, most likely because clinicians justified extra tests because they were drawing less blood (unpublished data). These unforeseen factors are also relevant in the context of RCTs, where they could influence protocol adherence or adjustment of co‐treatments [44]. Qualitative studies are essential to explore these types of hypotheses and, importantly, to explore patient perspectives and preferences [56].

Another implication of a complex systems perspective is that although RCTs are a valuable tool to assess treatment effects, complexity makes generalization of RCT results more difficult. If the treatment effect depends on a cluster of interdependent factors that we do not fully understand, how can we justify our assumption that the RCT results will replicate elsewhere? And how do we expect the transfusion effects to vary given that there is known practice variation for many other interventions with which transfusions may interact? This is why most RCTs need to be supported by different methods including thorough mechanistic studies and studies to explore variability in the clinical context to support implementation in clinical practice [57, 58, 59, 60]. To illustrate this, consider the findings of the PlaNeT‐2/MATISSE trial, which compared liberal versus restrictive platelet transfusion thresholds for preterm infants and found improved outcomes in infants treated restrictively [16, 61, 62]. To date, the mechanism for this effect remains elusive. Importantly, the trial has not resulted in improved clinical outcomes in most (post‐)implementation studies—although limited in sample size—which would have been expected given the trial results. This suggests that whatever adverse effect mechanism was present in the trial population, it may not have translated to all other settings. Fortunately, implementation studies also show no increase in adverse events, supporting the continued use of restrictive thresholds [63, 64, 65].

More extensive overviews of complexity methods and approaches in healthcare are provided elsewhere [47, 48]. Some of these methods are highly computational and sophisticated, and perhaps difficult to understand and therefore give the impression of a black box. However, difficult questions about complex systems may require difficult models. The alternative, that is, trying to answer difficult questions with simple methods, will almost invariably result in research waste and may even be harmful.

Research waste

Research waste is a pervasive problem in medical research, with some estimates suggesting over 85% of research is actually wasted [66]. Complexity science can help us to identify and avoid research waste and improve the way we prioritize research. As stated previously, trying to answer complex questions using simple methods is inefficient, may lead to false conclusions and as such may even cause harm. A perfectly executed clinical study that insufficiently takes complexity into account can be considered research waste. Instead, we should focus our efforts and resources on studies that can provide insights which are valid in complexity. And we should invest resources in managing uncertainty, not only in attempts to reduce it. Sometimes this may require accepting the fact that a question cannot be answered with the data and the methods we have available.

HUMILITY

While complexity science raises the bar for scientific methods, it simultaneously drives scientific humility, because it makes us aware of all the system components we are not looking at and all the system characteristics that we are not (yet) addressing in our research. In addition, ‘not all that matters can be measured’. There are types of knowledge, for example, more intuitive aspects of clinical expertise, that cannot be fully captured in numerical data. We should be wise to leave space for these kinds of hard‐to‐measure types of valid knowledge. So ultimately, we need to accept that there will exist, always, everywhere, despite artificial intelligence and big data, a level of unpredictability in complex systems [67]. This means we need adaptive management strategies [68]. Acceptance of complexity and uncertainty will pave the way for more realistic diagnostic and therapeutic expectations. Health cannot be manufactured, nor can treatment effects be guaranteed. Finally, our research papers would benefit from explicit discussions on the complexity of the systems we investigate, and a focus on the remaining questions and loose ends, rather than wrapping our data into a coherent but overly neat package.

CONCLUSION

To make scientific progress, to better understand the systems we research and to improve the way we translate evidence into practice, we need to consider the systems we look at as complex systems and accept the implications of that view: the need to innovate our research methods and the need to stay humble about what we think we know. This is not an overnight change, but starts with small steps, and asking ourselves questions, such as ‘Where have I set the boundaries of the problem I am looking at and how could change occur across these boundaries, or where did I assume linearity and could I be wrong?’ But also ‘given that my problem is complex, how certain can I be of my results, and how does this change the new research questions I ask and the way I design my studies?’ What complexity mostly requires is so‐called negative capability, or the ‘ability to resist the urge to conclude’ [69]. It asks us to stay in the uncomfortable uncertainty, to learn from it, and to expect the unexpected. This requires slow science, persistence and curiosity, and it encourages us to take the time to answer the questions that really matter.
