Remodeling gut microbiota and enhancing SCFA production in mice: mechanisms of RS2 and arabinoxylan in ameliorating PCOS phenotypes
Authors: Yun Liang, Songmao Li, Guishan Chen, Qingxia Huang, Xiaoping Yang, Xitao Li, Lichun Lu, Yangyang Hong, Kangyi Lin, Fu Chen, Guoshu Yin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPolycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) is linked to hyperandrogenism and gut microbiota dysbiosis. Dietary fibers (DFs), such as resistant starch type 2 (RS2) and arabinoxylan (AX), modulate the gut microbiota and produce short-chain fatty acid (SCFA), potentially ameliorating PCOS symptoms. This study investigated the therapeutic potential of RS2 and AX in a mouse model of letrozole-induced PCOS and explored the underlying mechanisms involving the gut microbiota-SCFA-liver signaling pathways.MethodsLetrozole-induced PCOS mice were divided into six groups: control (C), RS2 (R), AX (A), PCOS model (M), RS2-treated PCOS (RM), and AX-treated PCOS (AM) groups. The interventions lasted 35 days. Body weight, glucose tolerance, insulin resistance, ovarian morphology, estrous cycles, the gut microbiota (16 S rRNA sequencing), serum SCFA levels, fecal bile acids, and hepatic proteomics (DIA-MS) were analyzed.ResultsRS2 and AX reduced body weight, improved insulin sensitivity (lower HOMA-IR, p < 0.05), restored regular estrous cycles, and mitigated ovarian cystic follicles in PCOS mice. Gut microbiota analysis revealed an increased prevalence of Ligilactobacillus in the RM and AM groups (p < 0.05), which was correlated with elevated SCFA (acetic acid, butyrate) levels. Hepatic proteomics revealed increased expression of AMPK signaling proteins (Prkag2 and Pck1) in treated PCOS mice (p < 0.05), which was linked to the SCFA-mediated activation of GPRs (GPR41/43). AX also modulated pathways associated with nonalcoholic fatty liver disease. Bile acid metabolism showed no significant intervention-related changes.ConclusionRS2 and AX alleviated PCOS phenotypes by enriching Ligilactobacillus, increasing SCFA production, and activating hepatic AMPK signaling. These findings highlight gut microbiota modulation and SCFA-driven metabolic pathways as therapeutic targets for PCOS. This study provides mechanistic insights into dietary fiber interventions for endocrine disorders.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12902-025-02107-8.Keywords: Polycystic ovary syndrome, Dietary fibers, Gut microbiota, Short-chain fatty acids, Insulin resistance

Introduction

Polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) is the most common endocrine disorder among women of childbearing age, and its prevalence ranges from 10% to 13% according to the latest guidelines [1]. PCOS usually manifests as hirsutism, irregular menstruation, and infertility [1]. Numerous complications gradually appear as the disease progresses, such as type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), obesity, cardiovascular disease (CVD), and depression [1]. These morbidities further jeopardize the physical and mental health of patients. The pathological mechanisms of PCOS have not yet been determined, but some studies have reported that hyperandrogenism is the key factor, and some studies have shown that the gut microbiota is also an essential factor [2]. Adjusting the composition and components of the gut microbiota may be an effective way to alleviate the symptoms and complications of PCOS.

There was no consensus regarding the definition of dietary fibers (DFs), which is known to be an important nutrient, until the CODEX Alimentarius issued an international definition of DFs in 2009. The CODEX Alimentarius definition of DFs includes all nondigestible carbohydrate polymers with a degree of polymerization of 3 or more as dietary fiber with the proviso that they show health benefits [3]. Additionally, DFs, regardless of the definition, is recognized as a group of carbohydrate polymers and oligomers that escape digestion in the small intestine and pass into the large bowel, where it is partially or completely fermented by the colonic microflora, with the main product of this reaction being SCFA [3–5]. SCFA, which consist mainly of acetate, propionate and butyrate, have been reported to be a vital factor in regulating the gut microbiota of individuals with PCOS [6]. A global burden of disease study and several prospective studies have shown that DFs plays an important role in health maintenance, and compared with the lowest ones, the highest DFs consumers presented a 15–30% decrease in all-cause and cardiovascular-related mortality, as well as in the incidence of coronary heart disease, stroke, T2DM and colorectal cancer [7]. Some studies also revealed a correlation between DFs and PCOS and emphasized that adequate DFs might play an important role in the development of PCOS [8, 9].

Moreover, some studies have shown that some kinds of DFs can affect the gut microbiota to treat metabolic disorders, which provides novel ideas regarding the treatment of endocrine diseases such as T2DM and obesity [10, 11]. For example, resistant starch (RS), which is considered a type of dietary fiber, is a homopolysaccharide of glucose that can be fermented by gut microbes in the distal intestine and produce SCFA. RS can be classified into five types (RS1, RS2, RS3, RS4, and RS5), among which RS2 (high-amylose corn starch), defined as native starch granules that resist digestion due to the presence of B-type crystallinity, was used in our research [12]. Huating Li et al. reported that resistant starch intake improves glucose tolerance and insulin sensitivity and alleviates symptoms of obesity by reshaping the gut microbiota, which provides the possibility for the alleviation and treatment of PCOS [11].

Additionally, arabinoxylan (AX), a kind of dietary fiber, is the major nonstarch polysaccharide contained in various types of grains [13]. Previous studies have illustrated the effect of arabinoxylan on regulating the gut microbiota and metabolites in diabetic rats [14]; AX also utilizes intestinal fermentation to produce large amounts of SCFA, similar to RS, and may be associated with the hypoglycemic and hypolipidemic effects of these acids [14].

Overall, RS2 and AX both improve insulin resistance and lower serum lipids [11, 14]. Additionally, they both have the potential to treat endocrine diseases by regulating the gut microbiota and metabolites in the host. However, the roles of RS2 and AX in alleviating and treating PCOS remain unclear. If they do play a role, the underlying mechanism still needs to be elucidated. Accordingly, our study aimed to investigate the alterations in the gut microbiota, profiles of bacteria-derived metabolites and related signaling pathway proteins of the liver caused by RS2 and AX in a mouse model of letrozole-induced PCOS. These findings could contribute to understanding the mechanism of RS2 and AX in PCOS and pave the way for exploring optimal strategies to alleviate and treat PCOS.

Methods

Animals and diet treatments

Our study involved 4-week-old healthy female C57BL/6 mice (purchased from GemPharmatech Co., Ltd.) raised at the Laboratory Animal Centre of Shantou University Medical College. The initial body weight of mice was similar in all the groups (15-18g). Mice were maintained on a 12-h light-dark cycle with ad libitum access to standard chow and water. Mice were allowed to adapt to laboratory conditions for 1 week. During the entire experimental process, all the mice were fed regular feed. All procedures in mice were approved by the Animal Welfare and Ethics Committee of Shantou University Medical College (Protocol number STUM2023-477).

Experimental design

Mice were randomly divided into 6 groups, namely, the C, R, A, M, RM and AM groups (n = 6/group, total of 36 mice), and there was no difference in body weight among the groups. The group C, R and A are all healthy mice orally administered with 1% CMC solutions (carboxymethyl cellulose, AbMole BioScience, 5 mL/kg), RS2 (Ingredion, 0.4 g/kg/day), and AX (0.2 g/kg/day) respectively every day lasting 35 days. To establish the PCOS models and PCOS + Intervention models, The group M, RM and AM are orally administered with letrozole (Sigma, 2.8 mg/kg/day), letrozole + RS2 (Ingredion, 0.4 g/kg/day), and letrozole + AX (0.2 g/kg/day) respectively every day lasting 35 days (Fig. 1a).

Letrozole (Sigma, 2.8 mg/kg/day) suspension was dissolved in 1% CMC [15]. RS2 was dissolved in 0.9% normal saline. AX was dissolved in 0.9% normal saline. All the mice were given oral gavage intervention mentioned above every day and weighed weekly throughout the entire experiment.

Confirmation of PCOS

Vaginal citology smears and estrous cycle assessment

Started on the 21st day of the study, we selected 5 mice to assess the estrous cycle and lasted for 15 continuous days. Vaginal smears were collected simultaneously (from 9 to 10 a.m.), and the estrous cycle was determined on the basis of the preponderant epithelial cell types observed through an optical microscope. During proestrus, vaginal smears are composed mainly of nucleated epithelial cells. In estrus, cornified epithelial cells are predominant. For metestrus, both cornified and nucleated epithelial cells as well as polymorphonuclear leukocytes could be observed. In diestrus, polymorphonuclear leukocytes were the main cell type found. On the basis of these preponderant cell type patterns, the specific stage of the estrous cycle can be accurately identified [16].

Ovary morphology assessment

Prior to collecting ovaries, mice were anesthetized via intraperitoneal injection of 1% sodium pentobarbital solution (45 mg/kg body weight) to maintain unconsciousness. Following this procedure, anesthetized mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation. The collected ovaries were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and embedded in paraffin. The samples were subsequently processed into 4-micron sections on glass slides, stained with hematoxylin and eosin, and examined for changes in the pathological structure of the ovary using an optical microscope (400x magnification). The number of cystic follicles (CFs) was counted.

Hormone analyses

In this study, we strictly followed the instructions of the assay kit to evaluate the levels of insulin and testosterone in mouse serum. For the determination of insulin, a 5-µL serum sample was added to a specific mouse ELISA kit (with a detectable range of 0.025-6.9 ng/mL, ALPO). For the determination of testosterone, a 10-µL serum sample was used with the corresponding mouse ELISA kit (detectable range of 0.1-25 ng/mL, ALPO). Each serum sample was measured in duplicate to ensure data reliability. The homeostatic model assessment of insulin resistance index (HOMA-IR) is calculating using the following formula. HOMA-IR = (Fasting Insulin × Fasting Blood Glucose) / 22.5. The unit of fasting insulin is usually µU/mL or mU/L. The unit of fasting blood glucose is usually mmol/L.

Intraperitoneal glucose tolerance test (IPGTT)

Following 5 weeks of treatment, mice were fasted for 12 h while having access to water. An IPGTT was carried out. Blood glucose levels were measured by using a handheld glucometer (One Touch Ultra2, LifeScan, Inc.). Tail vein blood glucose was measured before (time 0) then IP injection of 50% glucose solution (China Otsuka Pharmaceutical Co. Ltd., 2 g/kg BW) was administered and at 30, 60 and 120 min postinjection blood glucose was measured again. The IPGTT curve was subsequently plotted, and the area under the curve (AUC) was calculated.

Collection of tissue and fecal samples

Prior to orbital sinus bleeding and tissue harvesting, mice were anesthetized via intraperitoneal injection of 1% sodium pentobarbital solution (45 mg/kg body weight) to maintain unconsciousness. Following this procedure, anesthetized mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation. After standing for 2 h, the blood was centrifuged to obtain the serum, that was stored at -20 °C. Before the conclusion of the experimental protocol, liver and fecal samples were collected and immediately stored at -80 °C.

Sequencing and data analysis of 16S rRNA

Sequencing of 16S rRNA

Total microbial DNA was extracted from 35 fecal samples by using the E.Z.N.A.® Soil DNA Kit (Omega Biotek, Norcross, GA, U.S.). The V1-V9 regions of the bacterial 16 S rRNA genes were amplified by PCR with the primers 27 F-1492R. Each sample was subjected to triplicate PCRs. The amplicons were subsequently extracted from the 2% agarose gel, and quantitatively measured by a Qubit® 3.0. After the construction and purification of the SMRTbell libraries, all amplicon sequencing was carried out by Shanghai Biozeron Biotechnology Co. Ltd. (Shanghai, China) through the PacBio Sequel IIe platform.

Data analysis of 16S rRNA

PacBio raw reads were processed using the SMRT Link Analysis software version 9.0 to obtain demultiplexed circular consensus sequence (CCS) reads. Sequence analysis was performed by the UPARSE software (version 7.1), and chimeric sequences were identified and removed using UCHIME. Sequences with ≥ 97% similarity were assigned to the same OTU. The RDP classifier (version 2.2) was employed to carry out species classification and annotation for each sequence by alignment with the Silva 16 S rRNA database (v138), with a confidence threshold of 0.7 [17]. The UCLUST algorithm was employed to obtain the species classification information corresponding to each OTU. Subsequently, the correlation analysis regarding the differences in sample composition and community structure was carried out.

Measurement of the serum SCFA

We used an ultrahigh-performance liquid chromatography coupled with tandem mass spectrometry (UHPLC-MS/MS) system (Vanquish™ Flex UHPLC-TSQ Altis™, Thermo Scientific Corp., Germany) to analyze the SCFAs in serum. The chromatographic column used was a Waters ACQUITY UPLC BEH C18 column (2.1 × 100 mm, 1.7 μm), which was maintained at 40 °C. The mobile phase was as follows: solvent A, 10 mM ammonium acetate in water; and solvent B, 50% acetonitrile-isopropanol. Mass Spectroscopy Conditions: Electrospray ionization source (ESI), negative ionization mode. Scanning was performed via multiple reaction monitoring (MRM). Quantitative and qualitative analyses of acetic acid, propionic acid, isobutyric acid, butyric acid, 2-Methylbutyrate, isovaleric acid, valeric acid, 2-Methylvalerate, 3-Methylvalerate, 4-Methylvaleric acid and hexanoic acid were performed using SCFA standards were obtained from ZZ Standards Co., LTD. (Shanghai, China).

Measurement of fecal bile acid

As mentioned previously, we used the UHPLC-MS/MS system to analyze the levels of bile acids in feces. The analysis procedure and setup for bile acids were performed as described above. Quantitative and qualitative analyses of taurohyodeoxycholic acid sodium salt, taurohyocholic acid sodium salt, 7-Ketolithocholic acid, 23-Norcholic acid, taurodeoxycholic acid sodium salt, hyodeoxycholic acid, 23-Nordeoxycholic acid, isolithocholic acid, 12-ketolithocholic acid, allolithocholic acid, iithocholic acid 3-sulfate sodium salt, deoxycholic acid, glycolithocholic acid, taurochenodeoxycholic acid sodium salt, chenodeoxycholic acid, cholic acid, tauroursodeoxycholic acid Dihydrate, chenodeoxycholic acid-3-beta-D-glucuronide, iithocholic acid, 3beta -Ursodeoxycholic acid, glycocholic acid hydrate, taurolithocholic acid sodium salt, ursodeoxycholic acid, 3-dehydrocholic acid, hyocholic acid, alpha-Muricholic acid, beta-Muricholic acid, taurocholic acid, tauro-alpha-Muricholic acid sodium salt and glycodeoxycholic acid were performed using bile acid standards were obtained from ZZ Standards Co., LTD. (Shanghai, China).

Astral data-independent acquisition (DIA) quantitative proteomics

Total protein was extracted from 20 milligrams of liver tissue from each sample. Subsequently, the Bradford protein assay kit was employed to quantitatively determine the protein concentration of the extracted samples. Each sample solution with different dilution factors was measured in triplicate. Subsequently, the protein concentrations of the samples were determined based on the standard curve. Following confirmation of the protein samples by electrophoresis, trypsin digestion was performed. Formic acid was added to the digested sample to modify the pH to below 3, and the mixture was subsequently centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 5 min. The supernatant was carefully transferred to a C18 desalting column and washed with washing buffer 3 times. Then, elution buffer was added to each sample, and the eluents were collected and freeze-dried for subsequent detection. Liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS), with a particular emphasis on the data-independent acquisition (DIA) mode, was employed for the analysis of protein samples. Mobile phase A, which contained 99.9% water and 0.1% formic acid, and mobile phase B, which consisted of 80% acetonitrile and 0.1% formic acid. Data-independent acquisition mode was adopted for mass spectrometry, with a full first-stage mass spectrometry scanning range of m/z 380–980. The resulting data were recorded in raw format for subsequent analysis.

Using the UniProt protein database, the raw files were searched and analyzed using the library search software DIA-NN. DIA-NN software (Direct DIA) was used to perform spectral deconvolution of the raw data and conduct database searching against the species library [18]. A t test was used to conduct a statistical analysis of the protein quantification results. Proteins with significant quantitative differences between the experimental group and the control group (p < 0.05, |log₂FC| >0.263 (FC >1.2 or FC < 0.83 [fold change, FC])) were identified as DEPs.

Data analysis

The IPGTT and body weight curve data are presented as the means ± SEMs. The data in the bar chart are presented as the means along with the maximum and minimum values. Data analysis and graphing were performed via GraphPad Prism 8 software, R-4.2.3, and the NovoSource Cloud Platform. A two-tailed unpaired Student’s t test, and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Turkey’s multiple comparisons test were performed to assess the differences between and among groups. The normality of the data was verified by the Shapiro-Wilk test, and the homogeneity of variance was tested by the Bartlett test (based on the group means). If the data did not satisfy the requirements of normality or homogeneity of variance, the Wilcoxon rank-sum test or the Kruskal-Wallis test was used instead. A p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Lefse differential analysis identifies taxa with significantly different abundances among groups using the non-parametric factorial KW sum-rank test (significance threshold: p < 0.05). Linear Discriminant Analysis (LDA) is used to estimate the contribution of differences in the abundance of each species (that passed the KW test) to inter-group differences (LDA score >2.0). The functional analyses of Gene Ontology (GO) and InterPro (IPR) data were carried out via interproScan software on the nonredundant protein database (which includes Pfam, PRINTS, ProDom, SMART, ProSite, and PANTHER) [19]. The Clusters of Orthologous Groups (COG) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) databases were subsequently used to analyze the protein families and pathway categories of the identified proteins. DEPs were used for volcano plot analysis, clustered heatmap analysis, and pathway enrichment analyses of GO, IPR, and KEGG (significance threshold: p value < 0.05).

Results

RS2 and AX interventions alleviate the polycystic phenotype, including obesity, hyperinsulinemia, disrupted estrous cycles, polycystic ovaries in the PCOS-like mice

The study included adaptation for 1 week and an intervention period of 5 weeks. Mice were randomly allocated into six groups: (1) C, (2) R, (3) A, (4) M, (5) RM and (6) AM (Fig. 1a). A total of 36 four-week-old healthy female C57BL/6J mice were included in the study. During the intervention period, the body weights of the letrozole-treated mice tended to be greater than those of the other five groups (Fig. 1b). The initial body weight was similar in all the groups (15-18 g) (Supplement. Figure 1a); however, after 5 weeks of intervention, the body weights of both the AM group and the RM group decreased compared with those of the M group (Supplement. Figure 1b). These results revealed that RS2 and AX may lead to weight loss in PCOS mice.

Furthermore, compared with the other five groups, the letrozole-treated mice tended to have greater glucose intolerance according to the results of the Intraperitoneal glucose tolerance test (IPGTT) (Fig. 1c). The AUC of the IPGTT revealed that, compared with the PCOS-like mice, the RS2 and AX interventions decreased the serum blood glucose level (Fig. 1d), and the mean blood glucose level showed the same trend in the RM and AM (Fig. 1e). At the 60th minutes of the IPGTT, it showed that the blood glucose of the RM and AM group both lower than the M group, especially the AM group (Fig. 1f). We also detected the serum fasting insulin level via ELISA. RS2 and AX intervention significantly reduced the serum fasting insulin level in the letrozole-treated mice (Fig. 1g). Additionally, the homeostatic model assessment of insulin resistance index (HOMA-IR), an important indicator of insulin resistance, of the AM group and RM group was also significantly lower than that of the M group (Fig. 1h).

In addition, the serum testosterone level was detected by ELISA as well. The serum testosterone level of the RM, AM group both exhibited a slight downward trend compared to the M group, but it isn’t a siginificant difference. We think it’s largely due to insufficient intervention cycle time. This result demonstrated that RS2 and AX may have the potential to alleviate hyperandrogenism but we still need more experiments on extending the intervention period to prove it (Supplement. Figure 1c). These results demonstrated that RS2 and AX play important roles in alleviating hyperglycemia, hyperinsulinemia and insulin resistance in PCOS-like mice.

To investigate the changes in RS2 and AX between the ovaries of the letrozole mice and healthy mice, we observed estrous cycles for the last 15 days of the experiment. The results showed that the estrous cycles of the C, A, and R groups remained normal, whereas the estrous cycles of the M groups were disrupted. However, this phenomenon was reversed in the AM and RM groups. These findings confirmed that RS2 and AX exerted beneficial effects by reversing the disruption of the estrous cycle to normal in letrozole-treated mice (Fig. 2a and b).

Ovarian morphological changes, such as multiple cyst-like follicles, thinner granulosa cell layers and fewer graafian follicles are characteristic of PCOS [1, 2, 20]. Compared with the C group, the M group presented more multiple cyst-like follicles with scant granulosa layers and no graafian follicles. The RS2 and AX treatment led to fewer cyst-like follicles, more graafian follicles and thicker granulosa cell layers than the M group (Fig. 2c-e and Supplement Fig. 1d). The primary ovaries and secondary ovaries reduced in the M group compared with other groups (Fig. 2f-g). Therefore, treatment with RS2 and AX exerts beneficial effects by attenuating pathological changes in ovaries and turning polycystic ovaries into healthy ovaries in PCOS-like mice.

RS2 and AX intervention induced gut microbiota, especially Ligilactobacillus, to colonize and grow in the intestines of PCOS-like mice

The gut microbiota is increasingly recognized as a mature endocrine organ through which dietary fiber, including RS2 and AX, can be fermented into SFCAs to influence the endocrine function of the body [11, 14]. Therefore, we analyzed the gut microbiota of feces in all experimental mice to explore in depth the mechanism by which RS2 and AX mitigate the polycystic phenotype. Fecal samples from all the groups were sequenced to investigate the changes in the gut microbiota during the interventions. The alpha diversity of the gut flora was assessed by the Chao 1, ACE, Shannon, Simpson and Pielou_J indices. The microbial community diversity differed among the six groups, especially between the M group and the AM or RM group (Fig. 3a-e). These results indicate that both RS2 and AX change the composition and characteristics of the gut flora in PCOS-like mice.

The species-level taxonomic profiles derived from the top 30 species ranked by their average abundance across samples revealed that Ligilactobacillus murinus, Bacteroides acidifaciens, Duncaniella freteri, Akkermansia muciniphila, Duncaniella muris, Lactobacillus johnsonii, Mucispirillum schaedleri, Limosilactobacillus reuteri, Muribaculum sp002492595 and Bifidobacterium globosum were the most commonly detected species in the samples, among which Akkermansia muciniphila and Ligilactobacillus murinus both exhibited obvious difference between groups (Supplement. Figure 2a). The other species were observed in significantly lower numbers in the majority of the samples (Fig. 3g). Among these differentially abundant microbes, the Ligilactobacillus_murinus, Lactobacillus_johnsonii and Akkermansia_muciniphila were top-ranking in species level by LDA score in the LEfSe analysis. This result indicated that these three kinds of gut microbiota are the biomarkers with the most statistically significant difference between groups (Fig. 3f and Supplement. Figure 2b).

Furthermore, we identified species with different abundances between groups via the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. The abundance of Ligilactobacillus and Lactobacillus significantly changed when RS2 or AX was used to treat PCOS-like mice at the species level (Fig. 3h). We observed that the abundance of Ligilactobacillus murinus (p = 0.0259) significantly increased in the RM group compared with the letrozole group at the species level (Fig. 3i). Additionally, we found that the abundances of Ligilactobacillus murinus (p = 0.0021), Limosilactobacillus reuteri_E (p = 0.0259) and Ligilactobacillus animalis (p = 0.0086) was greater in the A group than in the C group (Fig. 3j). A comparison of the abundance of Ligilactobacillus between the AM group and the M group revealed significant increases in Lactobacillus paragasseri (p = 0.0411), Ligilactobacillus animalis (p = 0.0411) and Lactobacillus gasseri (p = 0.0086) (Fig. 3k). This finding indicated that RS2 and AX intervention both can reshape compositional structure of gut microbiota and inducing some floras especially Ligilactobacillus, an important probiotic of the human body, to colonize and proliferate in the intestine of heathy and PCOS-like mice.

Additionally, detected a significant change in the abundance of Akkermansia muciniphila between healthy mice and PCOS-like mice. Compared with healthy mice, the gut microbiome of PCOS-like mice showed a significantly increased abundance of Akkermansia muciniphila (p = 0.0259) (Fig. 3l). This increase is primarily attributed to a protective upregulation of this bacterium in the body.

We also analyzed the functional annotation of the relative abundance of sequences from fecal samples of all mice using FAPROTAX. As showed in Supplement. Figure 2c and d, we found the abundance mainly concentrated on fermentation and chemoheterotrophy and it illustrated that the healthy group and RS2, AX intervention all aroused relative function changes (Supplement. Figure 2c and d). To perform prediction of microbial communities, we used PICRUSt (PhylogeneticInvestigation of Communities by Reconstruction of Unobserved States) to analyze relative abundance of functional genes at different KEGG levels. The pathway related to metabolism function, led by carbohydrate metabolism, accounts for a large proportion in all KEGG pathway (Supplement. Figure 2e). The heatmap exhibited that the abundance has significant difference among six groups. The abundance of pathway, including lipid metabolism, carbohydrate metabolism among others, showed low levels in the M group. On the other hand, when RS2 or AX are administered, the abundance of these functional pathways was significantly increased (Supplement. Figure 3a, c,d). In the pathway, led by glycolysis/gluconeogenesis, the RS2 and AX intervention also showed significant changes compared to the M group (Supplement. Figure 3b, e,f).

RS2 and AX May induce Ligilactobacillus to produce more SCFA, like acetic acid, to achieve weight loss and ameliorate insulin resistance in PCOS-like mice

To investigate the machnism behind the benificial effects of RS2 and AX on PCOS, we analyzed the bile acid content of all the mice via targeted metabolomics and the correlation between the gut microbiota and bile acid by conducting Pearson’s correlation analysis (Fig. 4l). The results revealed that the levels of primary bile acids, including chenodeoxycholic acid (CDCA), Cholic acid (CA), glycocholic acid hydrate (GCA), Taurocholic acid (TCA), Taurohyocholic acid sodium salt (THCA), tauro-alpha-muricholic acid sodium salt (T-α-MCA) and Beta-Muricholic acid (β-MCA), and secondary bile acids, including lithocholic acid 3-sulfate sodium salt (LCA-S), 3-dehydrocholic acid (3_DHCA), Taurodeoxycholic acid sodium salt (TDCA), Taurolithocholic acid sodium salt (TLCA) and Tauroursodeoxycholic acid Dihydrate (TUDCA), were significantly greater in the C group than in the M group, which indicated that PCOS could affect the bile acid metabolism of the host (Supplement Fig. 4a, b). However, when RS2 and AX were used in the PCOS-like mice, only CDCA and LCA-S have significant change, which was much lower than in the C group (Fig. 4i, j). This finding revealed that DFs such as RS2 and AX might have beneficial effects on PCOS but not mainly through the regulation of bile acid metabolism.

Discussion

PCOS is a multifaceted endocrine disorder characterized by metabolic dysfunction [21, 22], hyperandrogenism [23], and reproductive abnormalities. While its etiology remains incompletely understood, emerging evidence implicates gut microbiota dysbiosis as a critical contributor to its pathogenesis [24, 25]. This study demonstrated that the DFs such as RS2 and AX effectively alleviate PCOS-like phenotypes in letrozole-induced mice by modulating the gut microbiota composition, enhancing SCFA production, and activating hepatic AMPK signaling (Fig. 6). These findings not only validate the therapeutic potential of DFs in PCOS but also reveal a novel “microbiota-SCFA-liver axis” that links gut microbial metabolism to systemic metabolic and reproductive improvements.

The mechanism for the alleviation of PCOS phenotypes by RS2 and AX via the gut microbiota-SCFA-liver-AMPK pathway. Schematic illustration of the hypothesized pathway: In PCOS-like mouse models, interventions with Resistant Starch (RS2) and Arabinoxylan (AX) promote the colonization of beneficial gut microbiota, particularly genera such as Ligilactobacillus. These bacteria ferment the RS2 and AX to produce key SCFAs, including acetic acid. The SCFAs then activate their cognate receptors (GPR41, GPR43, GPR109a) in liver and subsequently the downstream AMPK signaling pathway. This cascade ultimately contributes to the amelioration of core PCOS phenotypes, including the alleviation of hyperinsulinemia, hyperglycemia, polycystic ovarian morphology, and restore normal estrous cycles

Dietary fiber interventions ameliorate PCOS phenotypes

Our data revealed that the RS2 and AX interventions significantly improved the hallmark features of PCOS in mice. Treated animals exhibited reduced body weight gain, enhanced glucose tolerance, and restored estrous cyclicity, which aligns with clinical observations that high-fiber diets are correlated with lower BMI and improved insulin sensitivity in patients with overweight and NAFLD [11, 26]. Notably, ovarian histology revealed a marked reduction in cystic follicles in the RS2/AX-treated groups, suggesting that DFs may directly or indirectly mitigate ovarian dysfunction. These improvements are likely multifactorial and involve both metabolic and hormonal regulation. For example, insulin resistance exacerbates hyperandrogenemia in individuals with PCOS by stimulating ovarian theca cell androgen production [27]. In addition, RS2 and AX can alleviate ovarian pathology by indirectly suppressing androgen overproduction through enhanced insulin sensitivity [11, 12, 14].

Gut microbiota remodeling: A key mediator of fiber benefits

The gut microbiota has emerged as an important player in PCOS pathophysiology. Previous studies reported reduced microbial diversity and depletion of beneficial taxa (e.g., Lactobacillus, Bifidobacterium and Prevotella) in PCOS patients [25, 28–30]. Here, RS2 and AX strongly enriched Ligilactobacillus in PCOS mice. Ligilactobacillus, a genus within the Lactobacillaceae family, is known for its anti-inflammatory properties and ability to increase gut barrier integrity [19]. Its expansion in treated mice may counteract letrozole-induced dysbiosis, which is characterized by elevated Akkermansia muciniphila, a mucin-degrading bacterium linked to intestinal permeability and metabolic endotoxemia. Notably, while A. muciniphila is often associated with metabolic health, its overgrowth in PCOS mice might reflect compensatory mechanisms to mitigate inflammation or insulin resistance, warranting further investigation.

In studies investigating gut microbiota alterations in endocrine diseases, the abundance of Lactobacillus has been consistently reported to be significantly decreased in PCOS model mice [31, 32]. This aligns with our experimental results, which showed a marked reduction in Lactobacillus abundance in PCOS mice, while intervention with RS2 and AX significantly increased its abundance. This consistency suggests that decreased Lactobacillus abundance is a key characteristic of microbial dysbiosis in PCOS, and restoring its levels is one of the critical points for alleviating the condition.

This study observed that AX and RS2 interventions significantly enriched Lactobacillus in PCOS mice, which differs from previous reports where resistant starch increased Ruminococcus abundance in a PCOS rat model [33] and arabinoxylan elevated Bifidobacteria abundance in obese mice [34]. This discrepancy in microbial response patterns may stem from differences in animal models, PCOS induction methods, and the specific sources of dietary fibers used across studies. These factors collectively shape a unique initial microbial “background” thereby determining the specific response to the interventions.

Despite the differences in the key responding bacterial genera, the underlying beneficial functional outcomes are likely consistent. The Lactobacillus enriched in our study is a primary producer of lactate in the gut. The produced lactate can serve as a critical metabolic intermediate and be further converted into butyrate by butyrate-producing bacteria (such as Anaerobutyricum spp.) via a “cross-feeding” mechanism. Therefore, the enrichment of Lactobacillus may represent an alternative yet equally effective microbial pathway for improving insulin sensitivity and alleviating chronic inflammation within the context of our model. This highlights that in microbiome research, focusing on the functional output of the microbial community (e.g., short-chain fatty acid levels) is more important than merely tracking taxonomic changes.

The specificity of Ligilactobacillus enrichment by RS2 and AX highlights the fiber-dependent nature of microbial modulation. RS2 resists digestion in the small intestine and undergoes fermentation in the colon, selectively promoting bacteria equipped with amylolytic enzymes [35]. Similarly, AX perform the role of a substrate for xylanolytic species such as Ligilactobacillus, which metabolize AX into SCFA. This substrate specificity underscores the importance of fiber type in the design of microbiota-targeted therapies for PCOS.

SCFA: metabolic messengers linking the gut and the liver

These findings further demonstrated that RS2 and AX could promote the proliferation of Ligilactobacillus including Ligilactobacillus murinus and Limosilactobacillus reuteri_E leading to increased production of SCFAs, such as acetic acid and butyric acid. SCFA, which are derived from the intestinal microbial fermentation of indigestible foods, have important metabolic functions and play crucial roles in intestinal health. Acetic acid, one of the most common SCFA, affects lipid metabolism and has functions that work against obesity and obesity-linked type 2 diabetes [36]. Acetic acid has also been shown to ameliorate insulin resistance and hypoglycemic effects [37, 38]. Butyric acid, one of the most abundant SCFA in the gut, has the potential to reduce weight by enhancing energy expenditure and fat oxidation [39]. Additionally, butyric acid contributes to mucus barrier re-establishment and a reduction in mucosal damage [40]. These functions of acetic acid and butyric acid may explain why PCOS-like mice lose weight when RS2 or AX intervention occurs. Moreover, butyrate has been widely reported to have a beneficial role in the protection of glucose homeostasis, as well as butyrate can promote the secretion of GLP-1, a type of gut-derived hormone that can induce glucose-dependent insulin secretion and decrease postprandial glucagon levels [41, 42]. In addition, butyrate increases insulin-positive islets and serum C-peptide levels through activating the expression of the pre-proinsulincoding genes Insulin1 and Insulin2 genes in the pancreas to improve glucose metabolism [43]. These findings provide a rational explanation for why PCOS-like mice could improve insulin sensitivity and ameliorate glucose intolerance. Overall, RS2 and AX may induce Ligilactobacillus, a type of gut microbial taxa that can produce more SCFA, such as acetic acid and butyrate—a key mediator of fat oxidation and glucose homeostasis—can colonize the intestine, contributing to weight loss and improvement insulin resistance in PCOS-like mice. Ligilactobacillus may provide novel insights into the formulation of microbiome-based strategies toward the amelioration and treatment of PCOS.

The AMPK signaling pathway involves a heterotrimeric complex composed of a catalytic α-subunit and two regulatory subunits, β and γ, and is the key modulator of glucose metabolism and lipid metabolism [44]. SCFAs activate the AMPK pathway through regulating fatty acid synthesis and β-oxidation via the gut-liver axis [5]. SCFAs such as acetate, butyrate, and propionate ameliorate liver lipid accumulation by activating GPR43, GPR41, and GPR109a, thereby stimulating the hepatic GPR-AMPK signaling pathway [45, 46]. GPR43 activation can suppress insulin-mediated fat accumulation and improve insulin sensitivity in the liver and muscle [47]. It has been reported that GPR41 regulates body energy expenditure and contribute to metabolic homeostasis through activation of the sympathetic nervous system at the ganglion level [48]. Butyrate may activate the AMPK signaling pathway through the induction of AMP levels in cells from increased consumption of ATP [39]. As we mentioned previously, the levels of SCFA, including acetate, butyrate and propionate, significantly increased when the M group was treated with RS2 or AX. The hepatic GPR-AMPK signaling pathway links SCFA with a polycystic phenotype, such as hyperinsulinemia and obesity, due to insulin resistance [39]. However, the absence of direct SCFA administration in our animal experiments is a limitation of the present work, and the proposed SCFA-liver axis will require further extensive research to fully validate its mechanisms and functional significance.

Clinical implications and translational potential

This study has translational relevance, as its findings are consistent with dietary guidelines for PCOS management. Current recommendations emphasize low-glycemic-index diets rich in fiber to enhance metabolic health [49]. Our findings support the inclusion of RS2 (e.g., green bananas, legumes) and AX (e.g., whole grains, bran) as functional components of such diets. Moreover, the enrichment of Ligilactobacillus suggests that probiotic formulations containing these strains could synergize with DFs to increase SCFA production.

However, translating murine findings to humans requires caution. Letrozole-induced PCOS models replicate hyperandrogenism and ovarian cysts but may not fully capture the hormonal complexity of human PCOS. Future clinical trials should assess fiber interventions in PCOS patients stratified by phenotype (e.g., lean vs. obese) and microbiome composition. Multiomics approaches (metagenomics, metabolomics) could identify biomarkers predictive of therapeutic response, thus enabling personalized nutrition strategies.

Several limitations of this study warrant consideration. First, the letrozole-induced PCOS model recapitulates key phenotypes like hyperandrogenism and ovarian cysts, inherent physiological differences between murine and human reproductive systems suggest caution in direct translation. Second, the 16S rRNA sequencing provided taxonomic insights into microbiota changes but could not establish causal relationships between specific bacteria and metabolic improvements. Similarly, proteomic analyses identified promising AMPK pathway associations but would benefit from future orthogonal validation.

Conclusion

In summary, this study establishes RS2 and AX as effective dietary modulators of PCOS through gut microbiota remodeling, SCFAs production, and hepatic AMPK activation. By promoting Ligilactobacillus enrichment and enhancing acetic acid signaling, these fibers mitigate insulin resistance, ovarian dysfunction, and metabolic disturbances (Fig. 6). These findings not only advance our understanding of PCOS pathophysiology but also provide a scientific rationale for microbiota-targeted dietary therapies. Future research should focus on clinical translation and on optimizing fiber-based strategies to address the heterogeneous manifestations of PCOS and its associated comorbidities.
