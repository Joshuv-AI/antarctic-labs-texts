Abstract
This paper addresses the challenge of solving Constrained Markov Decision Processes (CMDPs) with d > 1 d>1 constraints when the transition dynamics are unknown, but samples can be drawn from a generative model. We propose a model-based algorithm for infinite horizon CMDPs with multiple constraints in the tabular setting, aiming to derive and prove sample complexity bounds for learning near-optimal policies. Our approach tackles both the relaxed and strict feasibility settings, where relaxed feasibility allows some constraint violations, and strict feasibility requires adherence to all constraints. The main contributions include the development of the algorithm and the derivation of sample complexity bounds for both settings. 1
Introduction

Reinforcement Learning (RL) is a dynamic area within machine learning that focuses on agents learning which actions to take in order to maximize their cumulative reward. This field has seen substantial success in diverse domains such as robotics, autonomous driving, healthcare, and game playing
(
Sutton and Barto, 2018
;
Silver et al., 2016
)
. The standard framework for RL problems is the Markov Decision Process (MDP), where an agent makes decisions by considering the current state, selecting actions, and transitioning to new states according to certain probabilities, all while accumulating rewards.

However, many real-world problems impose constraints on the agent’s behavior. The prevalent framework used to model such cases is Constrained Markov Decision Processes (CMDPs)
(
Altman, 1999
)
. CMDPs extend MDPs by introducing the concept of a cost. The constraints then restrict the expected costs of admissible policies. These constraints can be used to reflect a range of potential limitations like: unsafe regions in robot navigation
(
Feyzabadi and Carpin, 2015
)
, budget allocation in finance
(
Xiao et al., 2019
)
, or safety in autonomous systems
(
Garcıa and Fernández, 2015
)
. These constraints are crucial for ensuring feasible and safe operation in practical applications.

Traditional approaches to solving CMDPs rely on knowledge of the transition dynamics, which is often unrealistic in complex environments. However, it is often the case that we instead have access to a generative model. By querying such a model with a given state-action pair, we can sample the next state. This allows us to follow a model-based approach to solving this problem by constructing an empirical model of the transition dynamics. Such generative oracles are practical in many applications, where simulations or real-world data collection can provide the necessary samples required to approximate the environment’s behavior without requiring explicit knowledge of the underlying dynamics.

In this work, we focus on solving CMDPs in scenarios where the transition dynamics are a priori unknown, but a generative model is available. Our aim is to derive and prove sample complexity bounds for learning near-optimal policies under both relaxed and strict feasibility settings. The relaxed feasibility setting allows for some constraint violations, whereas the strict feasibility setting requires policies to strictly adhere to all constraints, which is critical in high-stakes applications such as healthcare or autonomous driving where constraint violations can be disastrous.

The main contributions of this research are as follows:

•

We develop an algorithm for solving infinite horizon CMDPs with multiple constraints in the tabular setting, when the transition dynamics are unknown but we have access to a generative model, addressing both relaxed and strict feasibility settings.

•

We derive sample complexity bounds for our algorithm, providing theoretical guarantees on the number of samples needed to achieve near-optimal policies.

•

Our analysis covers both the relaxed and strict feasibility settings, showing how the sample complexity differs between the two and underlining the challenges of achieving the latter.

2
Related Work

CMDPs can be modeled utilizing a model-free or a model-based approach. Under the model-free setting,
(
Ding et al., 2020
)
proposed a primal-dual natural policy gradient algorithm (NPG-PD) operating and providing non-asymptotic convergence guarantees in both tabular and general settings.
(
Xu et al., 2021
)
proposed the constraint-rectified policy optimization (CRPO) algorithm which adopts a primal update and provides global optimality guarantee. Assuming zero constraint violations,
(
Wei et al., 2021
)
proposed the Triple-Q algorithm while
(
Xu et al., 2021
)
introduced a conservative stochastic primal-dual algorithm (CSPDA) and provided the corresponding sample complexity bounds.

Regarding the model-based approach, the CMDP is solved using a model-based algorithm after the transition matrix is provided or estimated with sufficient accuracy. Under this framework
(
Efroni et al., 2020
)
proposed different algorithms that leverage the primal, dual or primal-dual formulation of the CDMP and achieve sublinear objective and constrained regret.
(
Brantley et al., 2020
)
proposed CONRL algorithm which relies on the principle of optimism under uncertainty operating under convex-concave and knapsack settings.
(
Ding et al., 2021
)
proposed the OPDOP algorithm considering linear transition kernels while they extended their results to the tabular setting as well.

Closer to our approach is the line or research that studies the asymptotic convergence of their algorithm under the assumption of the unknown model
(
Tessler et al., 2018
;
Paternain et al., 2019
)
. These studies use the Lagrangian method to show zero duality gap asymptotically.
(
Vaswani et al., 2022
)
analyzed theoretically the algorithm proposed by
(
Paternain et al., 2019
)
for both the relaxed and the strict feasibility case, considering access to a generative model and a single constraint providing matching complexity bounds
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(\frac{|\mathcal{S}||\mathcal{A}|\log(1/\delta)}{(1-\gamma)^{3}\epsilon^{2}}\right)
to the unconstrained case for the former case.
(
HasanzadeZonuzy et al., 2021
)
also analyzed the relaxed feasibility case and proposed GM-CRL, an extended linear programming algorithm that achieves
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(\frac{\gamma^{2}|\mathcal{S}|^{2}|\mathcal{A}|\log(d/\delta)}{(1-\gamma)^{3}\epsilon^{2}}\right)
sample complexity. Finally,
Bai et al. (2022)
examined the strict feasibility case and proposed a conservative stochastic primal-dual algorithm (CSPDA) which achieves
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(\frac{d|\mathcal{S}||\mathcal{A}|}{(1-\gamma)^{6}\epsilon^{2}\zeta^{2}}\right)
sample complexity.

3
Problem Formulation

3.1
CMDP Definition

We consider an infinite-horizon discounted constrained Markov decision process (CMDP) with
d>1
constraints defined by the tuple
M^{d}=\left\langle\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},\mathcal{P},r,\{c\}_{i=1}^{d},\{b\}_{i=1}^{d},\rho,\gamma\right\rangle
. Here,
\mathcal{S}
represents the state space,
\mathcal{A}
represents the action space,
\mathcal{P}\colon\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\to\Delta_{\mathcal{S}}
is the state transition matrix,
\rho\in\Delta_{\mathcal{S}}
is the initial distribution of the states and
\gamma\in[0,1)
is the discount factor. The reward function is denoted by
r\colon\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\to[0,1]
, and the constraint value functions are given by
c_{i}\colon\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\to[0,1]
, with
b_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
representing the required minimum feasibility values for each constraint
i
, where
i=1,\dots,d
. The value function of a reward objective
l
for a given stationary policy
\pi\colon\mathcal{S}\rightarrow\Delta_{\mathcal{A}}
is defined as
V_{l}^{\pi}(\rho)\coloneq\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{s_{0},a_{0},\dots}\left[\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{t}l(s_{t},a_{t})\,\mid\,s_{0}\sim\rho,a_{t}\sim\pi(\cdot|s_{t}),s_{t+1}\sim\mathcal{P}(\cdot|s_{t},a_{t})\right]
. Also, for each state-action pair
(s,a)
and policy
\pi
, the action-value function of a reward objective
l
is defined as
Q_{l}\colon\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\to\mathbb{R}
and satisfies:
V_{l}^{\pi}(s)=\langle\pi(\cdot|s),Q_{l}^{\pi}(s,\cdot)\rangle
. The goal of the agent in a CMDP is to find a policy that maximizes the value function associated with the reward function
r
, while ensuring that the policy does not violate any of the constraints. Formally, this objective is expressed as:

\max_{\pi}\,V_{r}^{\pi}(\rho)\quad\mathrm{s.t.}\quad V_{c_{i}}^{\pi}(\rho)\geq b_{i},\;\;\forall i\in[d]

(1)

3.2
Relaxed & Strict Feasibility Settings

When attempting to solve CMDPs under unknown transition dynamics, we usually distinguish between the two settings: relaxed feasibility and strict feasibility. These settings define how strictly the constraints must be adhered to in the optimization process.

In the
relaxed feasibility
setting, small violations in the reward and constraint value functions are allowed. Formally, we require a policy
\hat{\pi}
such that for some
\epsilon>0
:

V_{r}^{\hat{\pi}}(\rho)\geq V_{r}^{*}(\rho)-\epsilon,\quad V_{c_{i}}^{\hat{\pi}}(\rho)\geq b_{i}-\epsilon\quad\forall i\in[d]

(2)

In the
strict feasibility
setting, the constraints must be strictly adhered to, with only small violations allowed in the reward function. For some
\epsilon>0
, the requirement is:

V_{r}^{\hat{\pi}}(\rho)\geq V_{r}^{*}(\rho)-\epsilon,\quad V_{c_{i}}^{\hat{\pi}}(\rho)\geq b_{i}\hskip 26.17651pt\forall i\in[d]

(3)

3.3
Sample Complexity

In our setting, we consider the transition matrix
\mathcal{P}
to be unknown and in need of estimation. To that end, the agent has access to a generative model which allows for sampling the next state
s_{t+1}
given any state-action pair
(s_{t},a_{t})
. This means that for any queried state
s_{t}\in\mathcal{S}
and action
a_{t}\in\mathcal{A}
, the generative model provides a sample from the transition probability distribution
\mathcal{P}(\cdot|s_{t},a_{t})
. By repeatedly querying this model, the agent can collect sufficient samples to construct an empirical approximation of the CMDP, which it then uses to learn a near-optimal policy.

The primary objective is to construct an algorithm that solves the problems described in
Eq.

2
and
Eq.

3
, and determine the minimum number of samples
N
required to ensure that for a given
\delta>0
, the constraints will be satisfied with probability
1-\mathcal{O}(\delta)
. We aim to establish bounds on
N
that guarantee these constraints are met, thereby quantifying the sample complexity needed to achieve near-optimal performance under these feasibility constraints.

4
Methodology

Following the approach of
(
Vaswani et al., 2022
)
which we extend for multiple constraints, we construct an empirical CMDP
\hat{M}^{d}=\left\langle\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},\hat{\mathcal{P}},r_{p},\{c\}_{i=1}^{d},\{b_{i}^{\prime}\}_{i=1}^{d},\rho,\gamma\right\rangle
. The primary difference between the actual CMDP
M^{d}
and the empirical CMDP
\hat{M}^{d}
is the use of the empirical transition matrix
\hat{\mathcal{P}}
, which is estimated by drawing samples from the generative model. Additionally, we introduce a perturbed reward function
r_{p}(s,a)\coloneq r(s,a)+\xi(s,a)
, where
\xi(s,a)\sim\mathcal{U}[0,\omega]
, and modify the feasibility constraint
b^{\prime}
to distinguish between relaxed (
b^{\prime}<b
) and strict (
b^{\prime}=b
) feasibility settings. We specify
\omega,b_{i}^{\prime}
and other parameters later when we instantiate our algorithm.

We denote by
\hat{V}_{l}^{\pi}(\rho)
the value function of an objective
l
when following policy
\pi
, when using the empirical transition matrix
\hat{\mathcal{P}}
. The optimization objective for this empirical CMDP
\hat{M}^{d}
is:

\hat{\pi}^{*}\in\left\{\argmax_{\pi}\,\hat{V}_{r_{p}}^{\pi}(\rho)\quad\mathrm{s.t.}\quad\hat{V}_{c_{i}}^{\pi}(\rho)\,\geq\,b_{i}^{\prime},\;\;\forall i\in[d]\right\}

(4)

4.1
Saddle-point Formulation

We can rewrite the empirical CMDP optimization problem in
Eq.

4
as an equivalent saddle-point problem:

\max_{\pi}\,\min_{\lambda_{i}\geq 0}\,\left[\hat{V}_{r_{p}}^{\pi}(\rho)+\sum_{i=1}^{d}\lambda_{i}\left(\hat{V}_{c_{i}}^{\pi}(\rho)-b_{i}^{\prime}\right)\right]\,\Leftrightarrow\,\max_{\pi}\,\min_{\lambda_{i}\geq 0}\,\left[\hat{V}_{r_{p}}^{\pi}(\rho)+\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\top}\left(\hat{\mathbf{V}}_{\mathbf{c}}^{\pi}(\rho)-\mathbf{b}^{\prime}\right)\right]

(5)

The solution to this saddle point problem is
(\hat{\pi}^{*},\,\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{*})
, where
\hat{\pi}^{*}
is the optimal empirical policy for and
\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{*}=[\lambda_{1}^{*},\dots,\lambda_{d}^{*}]
is the vector containing the optimal Lagrange multipliers.

It was proven in
(
Paternain et al., 2019
)
that
this formulation exhibits zero duality gap
. That is, if we consider the reward function of the unconstrained MDP
\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\lambda},\pi)\coloneq\hat{V}_{r_{p}}^{\pi}(\rho)+\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\top}\left(\hat{\mathbf{V}}_{\mathbf{c}}^{\pi}(\rho)-\mathbf{b}^{\prime}\right)
, then

\max_{\pi}\min_{\lambda_{i}\geq 0}\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\lambda},\pi)\,=\,\min_{\lambda_{i}\geq 0}\max_{\pi}\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\lambda},\pi)

(6)

4.2
Solving the saddle-point problem

As in
(
Vaswani et al., 2022
)
, we solve the above saddle-point problem iteratively, by alternatively updating the policy (primal variables) and the Lagrange multipliers (dual variables). If
T
is the total number of iterations of the primal-dual algorithm, we define
\hat{\pi}_{t}
and
\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{t}
to be the primal and dual iterates for
t\in[T]:={1,\dots,T}
. The primal update at iteration
t
is given as:

\hat{\pi}_{t}=\argmax_{\pi}\left[\hat{V}_{r_{p}}^{\pi}(\rho)+\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\top}\hat{\mathbf{V}}_{\mathbf{c}}^{\pi}(\rho)\right]\coloneq\argmax_{\pi}\hat{V}_{r_{p}+\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\top}\mathbf{c}}^{\pi}(\rho)

(7)

The dual update at iteration
t
is given as:

\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{t+1}=\mathcal{R}_{\Lambda}\left[\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{U}}\left[\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{t}-\eta\left(\hat{\mathbf{V}}_{\mathbf{c}}^{\pi}(\rho)-\mathbf{b}^{\prime}\right)\right]\right]

(8)

where
\eta
is the step-size for the gradient descent update,
\mathcal{U}\coloneq[0,U]^{d}
and
\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{U}}(\mathbf{x})
projects each component of
\mathbf{x}
in
[0,U]
, and
\mathcal{R}_{\Lambda}(\mathbf{x})
rounds each component of
\mathbf{x}
to the closest element in the
\epsilon_{1}
-net
\Lambda=\{0,\epsilon_{1},2\epsilon_{1},\dots,U\}
. We also assume that
U>\left\|\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{*}\right\|_{\infty}
is an upper bound for the largest optimal Lagrange multiplier.

4.3
Our approach

To solve this problem, we begin by estimating the empirical transition matrix
\hat{\mathcal{P}}
by querying the next state
s^{\prime}
for every state-action pair
(s,a)

N
(to be specified later) times in total. That is, we set
\hat{\mathcal{P}}(s^{\prime}|s,a)\coloneq\frac{N(s^{\prime}|s,a)}{N}
, where
N(s^{\prime}|s,a)
is the number of samples that have transitions from
(s,a)
to
s^{\prime}
. We then set
\omega,U,\epsilon_{1},\eta,\mathbf{b}^{\prime}
depending on the feasibility setting (relaxed vs strict), and apply the updates in
Eq.

7
and
Eq.

8
for
T
iterations. As we will see in the next sections, by setting these parameters accordingly, we can guarantee that our algorithm will solve
Eq.

2
and
Eq.

3
with
N
total queries and with probabilities
1-3\delta
and
1-4\delta
respectively.

The introduction of a perturbed reward function
r_{p}
in the empirical CMDP, the magnitude of the perturbation
\omega
, the discretization of the Lagrange multipliers using the
\epsilon_{1}
-net
\Lambda
, and the upper-bound on the optimal Lagrange multipliers
U
are all technicalities that help us prove sample complexity bounds for
N
. While the interested reader can find more details in the proofs in the appendices, we will briefly outline the purpose of these technicalities below so that the motivation behind the theorems can be understood with more ease.

•

The
perturbed reward function

r_{p}
is introduced to ensure a separation (or gap, see
Definition

1
) in the empirical
\hat{Q}
-values). This separation is then used to apply Lemma 5 of
(
Vaswani et al., 2022
)
, which is then used to prove concentration bounds between the true and empirical CMDPs for the data-dependent policies
\hat{\pi}_{t}
. That is, we will later require to bound
\|V_{r_{p}}^{\hat{\pi}_{t}}(\rho)-\hat{V}_{r_{p}}^{\hat{\pi}_{t}}(\rho)\|_{\infty}
.

•

The
magnitude of the uniform perturbation

\omega
has to be
\leq 1
, and will be chosen such that it simplifies reward suboptimality terms.

•

The
\epsilon_{1}
-net
\Lambda
is used to ensure that there will be a finite number of unconstrained MDPs, as each unconstrained MDP is uniquely defined by the Lagrange multipliers
\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{t}
(assuming other variables are fixed). This discretization of
\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{t}
will later allow us
to show separation for all possible unconstrained MDPs, by taking a union bound over their finite population.

•

The
upper-bound

U
on
\left\|\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{*}\right\|_{\infty}
helps in the discretization of
\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{t}
and allows us to instantiate our algorithm.

Strict Feasibility Bounds

In the strict feasibility setting, no constraint violations are allowed. Thus, we have to ensure that
V_{c_{i}}^{\bar{\pi}_{T}}(\rho)\geq b_{i},\,\forall i\in[d]

9
Discussion

By expanding the work of
Vaswani et al. (2022)
, we were to able to achieve a natural
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(\frac{d|\mathcal{S}||\mathcal{A}|\log(1/\delta)}{(1-\gamma)^{3}\epsilon^{2}}\right)
sample complexity bound for the relaxed feasibility setting. On the other hand, the bound for the strict feasibility setting
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(\frac{d^{3}|\mathcal{S}||\mathcal{A}|\log(1/\delta)}{(1-\gamma)^{5}\epsilon^{2}{\zeta_{\mathbf{c}}^{*}}^{2}}\right)
is bottlenecked by the cubic term
d^{3}
(note that both bounds recover the original sample complexities for
d=1
). The work of
Bai et al. (2022)
, suggests that this dependence might be sub-optimal. The additional square dependence (compared to the relaxed setting) arises from the fact that the union-bound has to cover all possible relaxed MDPs, and due to the nature of our discretization there’s an exponential number of them. While this term is inside a logarithm (see
Theorem

2
), it later gets squared in
Appendix

D
. This introduces a multiplicative
d^{2}
term, which together with the multiplicative
d
term from the relaxed-feasibility analysis, yields the cubic term.

10
Conclusion

In this work we provided sample complexity bounds for CMDP with multiple constraints, generalizing the results of
Vaswani et al. (2022)
. We adopted the model-based primal-dual algorithm proposed in
Paternain et al. (2019)
which solves the CMDP by iteratively solving an unconstrained MDP problem and updating its primal and dual variables and analyzed both the relaxed and the strict settings. For the former we obtained a
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(\frac{d|\mathcal{S}||\mathcal{A}|\log(1/\delta)}{(1-\gamma)^{3}\epsilon^{2}}\right)
sample complexity. For the latter much more difficult case, which does not allow any constraint violation we obtained a
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(\frac{d^{3}|\mathcal{S}||\mathcal{A}|\log(1/\delta)}{(1-\gamma)^{5}\epsilon^{2}{\zeta_{\mathbf{c}}^{*}}^{2}}\right)
sample complexity under the assumption that the term
\zeta_{\mathbf{c}}^{*}
can be efficiently estimated. In the future, we aim to quantify the difficulty of estimating the term
\zeta_{\mathbf{c}}^{*}
and to try to obtain better bounds for the relaxed feasibility case that have logarithmic dependence in the number of constraints, as in
(
HasanzadeZonuzy et al., 2021
)
.

Theorem 1

(Guarantees for the primal-dual algorithm)
.

For a target error
\epsilon_{\mathrm{opt}}>0
and the primal-dual updates in Eq.
7
- Eq.
8
with
U>\left\|\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{*}\right\|_{\infty}
,
T=\frac{4U^{2}d^{2}}{\epsilon_{\mathrm{opt}}^{2}(1-\gamma)^{2}}\left[1+\frac{1}{(U-\left\|\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{*}\right\|_{\infty})^{2}}\right]
,
\eta=\frac{U(1-\gamma)}{\sqrt{T}}
, and
\epsilon_{1}=\frac{\epsilon_{\mathrm{opt}}^{2}(1-\gamma)^{2}(U-\left\|\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{*}\right\|_{\infty})}{6dU}
, the mixture policy
\bar{\pi}_{T}\coloneq\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\hat{\pi}_{t}
satisfies

\hat{V}_{r_{p}}^{\bar{\pi}_{T}}(\rho)\geq\hat{V}_{r_{p}}^{\hat{\pi}^{*}}(\rho)-\epsilon_{\mathrm{opt}}\quad\text{and}\quad\hat{V}_{c_{i}}^{\bar{\pi}_{T}}(\rho)\geq b_{i}^{\prime}-\epsilon_{\mathrm{opt}}\;\forall i\in[d]

where
\hat{V}_{r_{p}}^{\bar{\pi}_{T}}(\rho)\,=\,\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\hat{V}_{r_{p}}^{\hat{\pi}_{t}}(\rho)
is the value function of the mixture policy
\bar{\pi}_{T}

Proof.

We begin by defining the dual regret with respect to any vector of Lagrange multipliers
\boldsymbol{\lambda}\in\mathcal{U}
as:

R^{d}(\boldsymbol{\lambda},T)=\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{t}-\boldsymbol{\lambda})^{\top}(\hat{\mathbf{V}}_{\mathbf{c}}^{\hat{\pi}_{t}}(\rho)-\mathbf{b}^{\prime})=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}(\lambda_{t,i}-\lambda_{i})(\hat{V}_{c_{i}}^{\hat{\pi}_{t}}(\rho)-b_{i}^{\prime})

(13)

To bound Eq.
13
we begin by fixing an arbitrary
\lambda_{i}\in[0,U]
and tracking the change of
\left|\lambda_{t,i}-\lambda_{i}\right|
. Defining
\lambda_{t+1,i}^{\prime}\coloneq\mathbb{P}_{[0,U]}\left[\lambda_{t,i}-\eta(\hat{V}_{c_{i}}^{\hat{\pi}_{t}}(\rho)-b_{i}^{\prime})\right]

\displaystyle|\lambda_{t+1,i}-\lambda_{i}|\;=

\displaystyle\;\left|\mathcal{R}_{\Lambda}[\lambda_{t+1,i}^{\prime}]-\lambda_{i}\right|\;=\;\left|\mathcal{R}_{\Lambda}[\lambda_{t+1,i}^{\prime}]-\lambda_{t+1,i}^{\prime}+\lambda_{t+1,i}^{\prime}-\lambda_{i}\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\;\left|\mathcal{R}_{\Lambda}[\lambda_{t+1,i}^{\prime}]-\lambda_{t+1,i}^{\prime}\right|+\left|\lambda_{t+1,i}^{\prime}-\lambda_{i}\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\;\epsilon_{1}+\left|\lambda_{t+1,i}^{\prime}-\lambda_{i}\right|

where in the last inequality we used that fact that
\left|\lambda_{i}-\mathcal{R}_{\Lambda}[\lambda_{i}]\right|\,\leq\,\epsilon_{1}
for all
\lambda_{i}\in[0,U]
due to the epsilon net. Squaring both sides we get,

\displaystyle\left|\lambda_{t+1,i}-\lambda_{i}\right|^{2}\;\leq

\displaystyle\;\epsilon_{1}^{2}+\left|\lambda_{t+1,i}^{\prime}-\lambda_{i}\right|^{2}+2\epsilon_{1}\left|\lambda_{t+1,i}^{\prime}-\lambda_{i}\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\;\epsilon_{1}^{2}+2\epsilon_{1}U+\left|\lambda_{t+1,i}^{\prime}-\lambda_{i}\right|^{2}

(since
\lambda_{i},\lambda_{t+1,i}^{\prime}\in[0,U])
)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\;\epsilon_{1}^{2}+2\epsilon_{1}U+\left|\lambda_{t,i}-\eta\left(\hat{V}_{c_{i}}^{\hat{\pi}_{t}}(\rho)-b_{i}^{\prime}\right)-\lambda_{i}\right|^{2}

(since projections are non expansive)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\;\epsilon_{1}^{2}+2\epsilon_{1}U+\left|\lambda_{t,i}-\lambda_{i}\right|^{2}-2\eta\left(\lambda_{t,i}-\lambda_{i}\right)\left(\hat{V}_{c_{i}}^{\hat{\pi}_{t}}(\rho)-b_{i}^{\prime}\right)+\eta^{2}(\hat{V}_{c_{i}}^{\hat{\pi}_{t}}(\rho)-b_{i}^{\prime})^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\;\epsilon_{1}^{2}+2\epsilon_{1}U+\left|\lambda_{t,i}-\lambda_{i}\right|^{2}-2\eta\left(\lambda_{t,i}-\lambda_{i}\right)\left(\hat{V}_{c_{i}}^{\hat{\pi}_{t}}(\rho)-b_{i}^{\prime}\right)+\frac{\eta^{2}}{(1-\gamma)^{2}}

where in the last inequality we used the fact that
0\leq\hat{V}_{c_{i}}^{\hat{\pi}_{t}}(\rho),b_{i}^{\prime}\leq\frac{1}{1-\gamma}
. Rearranging and dividing by
2\eta
we get

\left(\lambda_{t,i}-\lambda_{i}\right)\left(\hat{V}_{c_{i}}^{\hat{\pi}_{t}}(\rho)-b_{i}^{\prime}\right)\;\leq\;\frac{\epsilon_{1}^{2}+2\epsilon_{1}U}{2\eta}+\frac{|\lambda_{t,i}-\lambda_{i}|^{2}-|\lambda_{t+1,i}-\lambda_{i}|^{2}}{2\eta}+\frac{\eta}{2(1-\gamma)^{2}}

Summing from
t=0
to
T-1

\displaystyle\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\left(\lambda_{t,i}-\lambda_{i}\right)\left(\hat{V}_{c_{i}}^{\hat{\pi}_{t}}(\rho)-b_{i}^{\prime}\right)\;\leq

\displaystyle\;T\frac{\epsilon_{1}^{2}+2\epsilon_{1}U}{2\eta}+\frac{1}{2\eta}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\bigg[|\lambda_{t,i}-\lambda_{i}|^{2}-|\lambda_{t+1,i}-\lambda_{i}|^{2}\bigg]+\frac{\eta T}{2(1-\gamma)^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\;T\frac{\epsilon_{1}^{2}+2\epsilon_{1}U}{2\eta}+\frac{U^{2}}{2\eta}+\frac{\eta T}{2(1-\gamma)^{2}}

where for the last inequality we used telescoping summation, bounded the
|\lambda_{0,i}-\lambda_{i}|
term by
U
and dropped the negative term
-|\lambda_{T,i}-\lambda_{i}|
.

Setting
\eta=\frac{U(1-\gamma)}{\sqrt{T}}
:

\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}(\lambda_{t,i}-\lambda_{i})(\hat{V}_{c_{i}}^{\hat{\pi}_{t}}(\rho)-b_{i}^{\prime})\;\leq\;T^{3/2}\frac{\epsilon_{1}^{2}+2\epsilon_{1}U}{2U(1-\gamma)}+\frac{U\sqrt{T}}{1-\gamma}

Summing over all Lagrange multipliers
i=1
to
d

\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}(\lambda_{t,i}-\lambda_{i})(\hat{V}_{c_{i}}^{\hat{\pi}_{t}}(\rho)-b_{i}^{\prime})\;\leq\;d\left[T^{3/2}\frac{\epsilon_{1}^{2}+2\epsilon_{1}U}{2U(1-\gamma)}+\frac{U\sqrt{T}}{1-\gamma}\right]

Therefore we get the following upper-bound for the dual regret:

R^{d}(\boldsymbol{\lambda},T)\;\leq\;d\left[T^{3/2}\frac{\epsilon_{1}^{2}+2\epsilon_{1}U}{2U(1-\gamma)}+\frac{U\sqrt{T}}{1-\gamma}\right]

(14)

7
for any policy
\pi

\hat{V}_{r_{p}}^{\hat{\pi}_{t}}(\rho)+\boldsymbol{\lambda}\top\hat{\mathbf{V}}_{\mathbf{c}}^{\hat{\pi}_{t}}(\rho)\;\geq\;\hat{V}_{r_{p}}^{\pi}(\rho)+\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\top}\hat{\mathbf{V}}_{\mathbf{c}}^{\pi}(\rho)

\hat{\pi}^{*}

\hat{V}_{c_{i}}^{\hat{\pi}^{*}}(\rho)\geq b_{i}^{\prime},\;\forall i\in[d]

\hat{V}_{r_{p}}^{\pi^{*}}(\rho)-\hat{V}_{r_{p}}^{\hat{\pi}_{t}}(\rho)\;\leq\;\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{t}[\hat{\mathbf{V}}_{\mathbf{c}}^{\hat{\pi}_{t}}(\rho)-\mathbf{b}^{\prime}]

13

Lemma 6

(Decomposing the suboptimality)

Lemma 7

(Bounding the dual variables)
.

Let
\tilde{\pi}
be a feasible policy. Define the margin as
\zeta_{\mathbf{c}}^{\tilde{\pi}}\coloneq\min_{i\in[d]}\{V_{c_{i}}^{\tilde{\pi}}(\rho)-b_{i}\}\geq 0

\tilde{\pi}
is strictly feasible (i.e.

), and

.

4

, we can switch the min and the max to get:

\displaystyle\hskip 49.79231pt\;=\;\max_{\pi}\hat{V}_{r_{p}}^{\pi}(\rho)+{\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{*}}^{\top}\left[\hat{\mathbf{V}}_{\mathbf{c}}^{\pi}(\rho)-\mathbf{b}^{\prime}\right]

(since the above term is a max over all policies)

\displaystyle\hskip 49.79231pt\;=\;\hat{V}_{r_{p}}^{\tilde{\pi}}(\rho)+\sum_{i=1}^{d}\lambda_{i}^{*}\left[\left(\hat{V}_{c_{i}}^{\tilde{\pi}}(\rho)-b_{i}\right)+\left(b_{i}-b_{i}^{\prime}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\hskip 49.79231pt\;\geq\;\hat{V}_{r_{p}}^{\tilde{\pi}}(\rho)+\sum_{i=1}^{d}\lambda_{i}^{*}\left[\hat{V}_{c_{i}}^{\tilde{\pi}}(\rho)-V_{c_{i}}^{\tilde{\pi}}(\rho)+\zeta_{\mathbf{c}}^{\tilde{\pi}}+(b_{i}-b_{i}^{\prime})\right]

where the last inequality follows from the definition of

, i.e.

.

1) For the first case, we continue from
Eq.

39
:

\displaystyle\hskip 19.91692pt\hat{V}_{r_{p}}^{\hat{\pi}^{*}}(\rho)\;\geq\;\hat{V}_{r_{p}}^{\tilde{\pi}}(\rho)+\sum_{i=1}^{d}\lambda_{i}^{*}\left[\hat{V}_{c_{i}}^{\tilde{\pi}}(\rho)-V_{c_{i}}^{\tilde{\pi}}(\rho)+\zeta_{\mathbf{c}}^{\tilde{\pi}}+(b_{i}-b_{i}^{\prime})\right]

Lemma 9
