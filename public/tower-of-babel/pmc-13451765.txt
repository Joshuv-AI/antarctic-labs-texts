Epileptic seizures temporally associated with recombinant human growth hormone therapy in children: A case series of three patients
Authors: Shigeru Nagaki, Minobu Shichiji, Hirokazu Oguni, Makiko Osawa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe report three pediatric patients who developed epileptic seizures while receiving recombinant human growth hormone therapy. Case 1 was a 12-year-old girl with a history of febrile seizures and epilepsy whose seizures recurred after discontinuation of antiepileptic medication during ongoing recombinant human growth hormone therapy. Case 2 was an 11-year-old boy born extremely preterm who developed afebrile generalized tonic seizures during recombinant human growth hormone therapy but subsequently remained seizure-free without anticonvulsant treatment. Case 3 was a 6-year-old boy with developmental delay and a history of febrile seizures who was diagnosed with Noonan syndrome. He developed febrile status epilepticus following influenza infection several months after initiation of recombinant human growth hormone therapy and subsequently developed refractory epilepsy with progressive cerebral atrophy. All patients had pre-existing neurological or genetic risk factors for epilepsy. A direct causal relationship between recombinant human growth hormone therapy and seizure occurrence was not established. These cases suggest that seizures occurring during recombinant human growth hormone therapy were more likely related to underlying risk factors than to recombinant human growth hormone therapy itself. Careful neurological follow-up is recommended in children receiving growth hormone therapy, particularly those with established risk factors for epilepsy.Keywords: febrile seizure, epilepsy, Noonan syndrome, short stature, recombinant human growth hormone, adverse events

Introduction

Growth hormone (GH) exerts a wide range of biological effects beyond promoting skeletal and soft-tissue growth. 1 GH crosses the blood–brain barrier and binds to GH receptors in the choroid plexus, hypothalamus, hippocampus, and cerebral cortex, suggesting roles in cognitive development, memory, motivation, and behavior.2,3 Reported adverse events during GH therapy include headache, upper respiratory tract infection, scoliosis, seizures, epilepsy, and neoplasms. 4 According to annual reports from the Kabi Pharmacia International Growth Study (KIGS), the incidence of epilepsy reported as an adverse event during GH therapy is 0.18%. 4 However, the relationship between recombinant human GH (rhGH) therapy and seizures remains unclear. Here, we present a case series of three pediatric patients who developed epileptic seizures during rhGH therapy and discuss the possible relationship between rhGH therapy and seizure susceptibility.

Case series

Case 1

A 12-year-old girl had no history of perinatal abnormalities but did have a family history of febrile seizures, and old brother had epilepsy. She experienced three febrile seizures before the age of 4 years. At the age of 5 and 6 years, she experienced unprovoked generalized tonic seizures and was referred to our hospital. Electroencephalography (EEG) demonstrated a normal background activity with generalized spike-and-wave discharges during sleep, consistent with interictal epileptiform abnormalities (Figure 1). Brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) results were normal. Valproic acid (VPA) therapy was initiated at 6 years and 4 months of age. At 10 years and 8 months, her height was −2.5 standard deviations (SD), and GH stimulation testing revealed GH deficiency. rhGH therapy was initiated at 10 years and 11 months of age. She remained seizure-free, and VPA was gradually tapered and discontinued at 11 years and 9 months of age while GH therapy was continued. Four months after discontinuation of VPA, generalized tonic seizures recurred, leading to the reintroduction of VPA. Furthermore, the plasma concentrations of VPA were maintained at 50–60 μg/mL (therapeutic range: 50–100 μg/mL). She is currently receiving both VPA and rhGH, and her seizures remain well controlled (Table 1).

Case 2

A 11-year-old boy was born prematurely at 25 gestational weeks in a breech presentation, with a birth weight of 831 g and length of 34 cm. He required immediate intubation and oxygen therapy, surfactant, and phototherapy. At 1 year of age, he experienced four febrile seizures and was subsequently diagnosed with moderate intellectual disability intelligece quotient (IQ) 53 at age 4 years and 6 months). At 6 years and 1 month, his height was −3.7 SD. GH stimulation testing yielded a normal response, and he was diagnosed with idiopathic short stature. However, the mean GH concentration during 4 h of sleep was low (0.84 ng/mL; reference >5 ng/mL), and rhGH therapy was initiated at 6 years and 4 months of age. He experienced two additional febrile seizures between 6 and 7 years of age. Between 9 and 10 years of age, he experienced three unprovoked generalized tonic seizures and was diagnosed with epilepsy. EEG during sleep demonstrated normal background activity without epileptiform abnormalities. MRI results were normal. He continued rhGH therapy without anticonvulsant treatment and has remained seizure-free since 11 years of age (Table 1).

Case 3

A 6-year-old boy received phototherapy for neonatal hyperbilirubinemia. Hypotonia and ventricular septal defect were noted, and his developmental quotient at 2 years of age was 54. He was diagnosed with Noonan syndrome based on clinical features. At 4 years of age, his height was −3.6 SD, and rhGH therapy was initiated at 4 years and 10 months. At 5 years and 1 month, he developed influenza followed by febrile status epilepticus with bilateral tonic seizures, right-sided dominance lasting 60 min. GH therapy was discontinued. After recovery, right hemiplegia persisted. Two months later, he experienced another episode of status epilepticus (body temperature 37.5 °C) lasting 40 min. EEG demonstrated background slowing with frequent generalized epileptiform discharges, consisting of irregular spike-and-wave discharges, predominantly in the right hemisphere (Figure 2). Brain MRI demonstrated mild cerebral atrophy after the first episode and moderate atrophy following the second episode (Figure 3). Despite anticonvulsant therapy, he developed refractory epilepsy (Table 1).

Discussion

Principal findings

The three pediatric patients described in this report experienced generalized seizures during rhGH therapy. Importantly, however, all patients had pre-existing neurological or genetic conditions associated with an increased risk of epilepsy. Case 1 had a family history of febrile seizures and epilepsy, as well as a personal history of febrile seizures prior to the onset of epilepsy, suggesting an intrinsically elevated susceptibility to seizure disorders. Case 2 was born extremely preterm and had multiple recognized neurological risk factors, including perinatal complications, intellectual disability, and persistent febrile seizures beyond early childhood. Case 3 had Noonan syndrome, developmental delay, and prolonged febrile status epilepticus associated with influenza infection. Although influenza-associated encephalopathy was considered, the clinical course, neuroimaging findings, and recurrence of seizures suggested that underlying neurological vulnerability played a more substantial role.

Collectively, these observations suggest that pre-existing neurological vulnerability likely played a greater role in epileptogenesis than rhGH therapy itself. While the temporal association between rhGH treatment and seizure occurrence warrants attention, the present cases do not provide evidence supporting a direct causal relationship.

Comparison with existing literature

Previous studies have shown that the risk of epilepsy after febrile seizures is approximately 2.0%–7.5%, increasing to 10%–20% in patients with complex febrile seizures.5,6 Additional established risk factors include a family history of epilepsy, pre-existing neurological abnormalities, developmental delay, and prolonged seizures. 5 Furthermore, epilepsy has been reported in approximately 9% of patients with Noonan syndrome, 7 and the risk following febrile status epilepticus may reach 30%. 8 In the present series, all three patients had substantial baseline risk factors for epilepsy independent of rhGH treatment. Our findings are also consistent with previous reports describing seizure occurrence in children receiving rhGH therapy who had pre-existing neurological vulnerabilities. Zhou et al. reported a patient with congenital hypothyroidism and complex febrile seizures who developed epilepsy during rhGH therapy. 9 Similar to our cases, the patient described by Zhou et al. had recognized risk factors for epilepsy before treatment initiation. Epidemiological evidence does not currently support an increased overall risk of epilepsy associated with rhGH therapy. The prevalence of childhood epilepsy in the general population is estimated to be approximately 0.7%–1.0%, 10 whereas data from the KIGS registry reported an incidence of epilepsy during rhGH therapy of 0.18%. 4 Although direct comparisons between studies should be interpreted cautiously because of differences in study populations and methodologies, these findings suggest that rhGH therapy is unlikely to substantially increase epilepsy risk at the population level.

Mechanistic considerations

The biological mechanisms linking rhGH therapy and seizure susceptibility remain uncertain. Experimental studies have suggested that GH may influence neuronal excitability through modulation of excitatory neurotransmission. GH has been shown to enhance both α-amino-3-hydroxy-5-metthyl-4-isoxazolepropionic acid receptor and N-methyl-D-aspartate receptor-mediated excitatory synaptic transmission in the hippocampal CA1 region without significantly affecting gamma-aminobutyric acid-mediated inhibitory transmission. 11 Animal studies have yielded conflicting results regarding the role of GH in epileptogenesis. In some experimental models, intracerebral GH administration accelerated kindling progression, 11 whereas suppression of endogenous GH delayed epileptogenesis, 12 suggesting potential proconvulsant effects. Conversely, other studies have demonstrated anticonvulsant and neuroprotective properties of GH in chemically induced status epilepticus models. 13 These apparently contradictory findings indicate that the effects of GH on neuronal excitability may be complex and context dependent. Nevertheless, the clinical relevance of these experimental observations remains unclear. The doses, routes of administration, and experimental conditions used in animal studies differ substantially from those encountered in routine pediatric rhGH therapy. Furthermore, the neurological backgrounds of experimental models may not adequately reflect those of children receiving clinical treatment. Therefore, caution is warranted when extrapolating mechanistic findings directly to clinical practice.

Clinical implications

The present case series provides clinically relevant information regarding seizure occurrence during rhGH therapy in children with pre-existing neurological risk factors. Notably, the clinical courses differed considerably among the three patients. In one patient, rhGH therapy was safely continued without anticonvulsant treatment after seizure remission (Case 2). In another, seizures recurred following withdrawal of antiepileptic medication despite continued rhGH therapy, suggesting that epilepsy control was more closely related to antiepileptic treatment than to GH exposure itself (Case 1). In the third patient, refractory epilepsy developed after febrile status epilepticus in the setting of a viral illness and underlying Noonan syndrome (Case 3).

These heterogeneous clinical outcomes further support the interpretation that rhGH therapy was unlikely to represent the primary cause of epilepsy in these patients. However, clinicians should remain aware that seizures may occur during treatment, particularly in children with known neurological vulnerabilities such as a history of febrile seizures, developmental delay, perinatal brain injury, or genetic syndromes associated with epilepsy. Careful clinical follow-up and appropriate neurological assessment may therefore be warranted in selected high-risk patients receiving rhGH therapy.

Limitations

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, this report describes only three patients, precluding definitive conclusions regarding causality between rhGH therapy and seizure occurrence. Second, the observational nature of the study limits the ability to control for potential confounding factors, particularly the substantial neurological comorbidities present in all cases. Third, because epilepsy may develop independently in children with these established risk factors, the temporal association between rhGH therapy and seizure onset does not necessarily imply causation. In addition, publication bias and selective reporting of unusual clinical events may overemphasize rare associations observed during treatment. Accordingly, the findings of this study should be interpreted cautiously.

Conclusion

All three patients described in this report had pre-existing neurological or genetic risk factors for epilepsy, and the findings do not support a direct causal relationship between rhGH therapy and seizure occurrence. Nevertheless, seizures may occur during treatment in neurologically vulnerable children. Clinicians should, therefore, consider careful neurological monitoring in patients receiving rhGH therapy, particularly those with a history of febrile seizures, perinatal complications, intellectual disability, or genetic syndromes such as Noonan syndrome.

The present study is limited by its small sample size and observational design, which preclude definitive conclusions regarding causality. Future prospective studies and large-scale registry analyses incorporating detailed individual patient data are needed to clarify the incidence, mechanisms, and clinical significance of seizure events during rhGH therapy.
