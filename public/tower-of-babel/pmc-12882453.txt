Electrocardiographic abnormalities among children with sickle cell anaemia at steady state and crises attending federal teaching Hospital, Owerri, South East Nigeria
Authors: Kawa Alaoma, Nwolisa Emeka, Josephat M Chinawa
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCardiovascular complications of sickle cell anaemia contribute significantly to the mortality associated with the disease. Some of these complications are obvious on the electrocardiogram.ObjectivesThe aim of this study was to determine the electrocardiographic findings of pediatric sickle cell anemia patients in crises and steady states seen at the Federal Teaching Hospital, Owerri, and to compare them with those of healthy genotype AA controls.MethodsOne hundred thirty-five children aged 2–17 years were consecutively recruited between the 1st of February and the 4th of October, 2023; forty-five children each at steady state and crisis and forty-five Hb AA controls were included. All the patients underwent electrocardiography, and a full blood count was performed.ResultsECG abnormalities were recorded in 33/45 (73.3%) of those in crisis, 22/45 (48.9%) in the steady state, and 17/45 (37.8%) in the control. The most common ECG abnormality recorded for the SCA patients was left ventricular hypertrophy (LVH), characterized by tall RV6, and this was observed more often in those with vaso-occlusive crises (10/45 (55.6%) and 7/45 (38.9%) in hyperhemolytic crises, 1/45 (5.6%) in sequestration crises and only 2/45 (6.7%) in the control, with a p value of 0.001.The mean± SD haemoglobin level was 7.7± 1.5 for the steady state, 6.8 ± 1.6 for the crisis state and 11.7 ±1.1 for the control state. p = 0.001. LVH was observed in 42.3% of those with severe anaemia and in 32.3% of those with moderate anaemia. p=0.388. LVH was observed in 18/45 (40.0%) of the crisis cases and in 13/45 (28.9%) of the steady state cases. p= 0.375.ConclusionThe most common ECG abnormality seen in children with SCA attending Federal University Teaching Hospital Owerri is left ventricular hypertrophy seen in 36.9% of the subjects while left atrial enlargement was the most common ECG finding seen in 32% of the control with left ventricular hypertrophy, bradycardia and Tachycardia statistically significant. The level of anaemia is not statistically related to the ECG findings in SCA. Left Ventricular hypertrophy is more common in SCA age group 14-17yr and there is no gender predilection for the ECG findings of SCA. This may help guide decisions on the need or otherwise of routine ECGs in children with sickle cell anaemia, especially those in crises.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12887-025-06413-x.Keywords: ECG, Sickle cell anaemia, Steady state, Crises, Children

Introduction

Sickle cell anaemia is an inherited genetic haematologic disease characterized by two abnormal haemoglobin S genes, which results in a change in the normal oval shape of the red cell to an abnormal, rigid and sickled shape [1].

The prevalence of SCA in Nigeria is between 0.4% and 3%, affecting about 20 per thousand new‑borns [2–5]. Nigeria has the highest incidence of sickle cell disease in the world, with an estimated 150,000 babies born yearly with sickle cell anaemia [2, 4].

Cardiovascular complications of sickle cell anaemia increase as patients live longer; these include right ventricular hypertrophy arising from pulmonary hypertension, dysrhythmia and sudden death [6, 7]. The commonly reported ECG abnormalities are nonspecific. First-degree atrioventricular block, right ventricular enlargement, conduction defects, and repolarization changes are the most frequent ECG findings [8]. However, several authors, including Ikegwonu et al. [9] in Abakaliki, Nigeria; Adeniyi et al. [10] in Ile-Ife, Nigeria; and Shrivastava [11] in India, reported the most frequent ECG abnormalities to be left ventricular hypertrophy, T-wave changes, heart blocks, and prolonged QTc [10–12].

Pulmonary hypertension (PH) is one of the major vasculopathic complications of SCD. Hemolytic anaemia is an important mechanism for pulmonary vasculopathy [12]. In their study of SCA patients in Enugu, south-eastern Nigeria, Chinawa et al. [13]. reported that 49% of the children had an increased left ventricular internal diameter; however, left ventricular dysfunction was present in only 15.7% of them. Evidence of pulmonary hypertension on the ECG may be seen as signs of right ventricular hypertrophy or right atrial enlargement. ECG findings included right axis deviation, P-pulmonale, right bundle branch block, and an R/S ratio > 1 in lead V1 [14]. The higher the pulmonary artery pressure is, the more sensitive than the ECG [14]. Faro et al. [15], in a study of 109 SCA patients in Brazil between the ages of 7 and 25 years, reported that 37.6% of them had left ventricular hypertrophy, whereas Ikegwonu et al. [6] reported a similar value of 39.6% after studying the ECGs of 164 SCA patients between the ages of 2 and 17 years in south-eastern Nigeria. Yau et al. [8], in a study performed among SCA patients > 16 years of age in Kano, North-western Nigeria, reported that 64% of them had right ventricular hypertrophy according to their ECGs. In addition, Chinawa et al. [1], in a study performed in Enugu, Nigeria, among SCA patients between the ages of 3 and 17 years, estimated the prevalence of right ventricular systolic dysfunction among children with SCA to be 8%, which was also similar to the 8.1% prevalence reported by Adegoke et al. [16] in Osun, south-western Nigeria. The ECG changes associated with right ventricular hypertrophy include large R waves in leads V1 and V2, an rSR’ pattern in V1–V2, secondary ST-T changes in V1–V3 and right axis deviation [17].

ECG changes can occur in 50% of SCA patients before they reach twenty years of age [18–22]. Therefore, early screening of children with ECGs, a non-invasive test to identify these complications, refers to echocardiography when necessary and institutes appropriate management, and follow-up is imperative [23, 24].

The work was therefore aimed at determining the electrocardiographic findings of paediatric sickle cell anaemia patients aged 2 years to 17 years seen at the Federal Medical Centre, Owerri. In addition, this study aimed to determine and compare the electrocardiographic changes (e.g., rhythm, PR interval, QRS morphology, T wave changes, ST changes, and LV and biventricular hypertrophy) in sickle cell anaemic patients with those in matched subjects with a normal Hb genotype AA and to determine the correlation, if any, between Hb concentration and ECG findings in sickle cell anaemic patients. It also determined the difference, if any, in the ECG changes in steady-state SCA patients and those in crisis.

Methods

Study area

The study was conducted at the Children’s Outpatient Clinic and Emergency Paediatric Unit of the Federal Medical Centre, Owerri.

Study design

This was a comparative cross-sectional study.

Study population

The study population consisted of children with sickle cell anaemia who were in a steady state and those who were in crises aged 2–17 years, as seen at the FMC (Now FTH), Owerri. The two-year lower limit was chosen because a previous study reported no ECG abnormalities in children less than 2 years old [9].

The control population included children who were apparently healthy and had a normal haemoglobin (HbAA) genotype. Children between the ages of 2 years and 17 years with haemoglobin SS who were in the steady state were defined as having no history of an acute painful episode that required treatment in the hospital for at least four consecutive weeks after a previous painful crisis and no history of blood transfusion during the previous 4 months; children with SCA in crisis were defined as having several acute conditions, such as vaso-occlusive crisis, aplastic crisis, splenic sequestration crisis, hyperhemolytic crisis, hepatic crisis, dactylitis, and acute chest syndrome. No participant was recruited twice during the course of the study. Parents or caregivers of children who provided written informed consent, and children who gave assent were included in the study, whereas children with known or suspected (from clinical assessment) congenital or acquired heart diseases; children with diarrheal disease who may have electrolyte derangements that may affect the ECG reading; children on medications that affect rhythm abnormalities, e.g., azithromycin or chloroquine; and children whose parents refused to provide signed informed consent were excluded from the study.

Controls

Apparently healthy children with a normal haemoglobin (Hb AA) genotype were matched for age and sex.

Therefore, forty-five in crisis and forty-five in stable state and another forty-five age- and gender-matched non-SCA children were recruited for the study, resulting in one hundred thirty-five children in total.

Physical examination and anthropometry

A thorough physical examination was performed by the researcher, and the findings were recorded. Each child’s height, weight, temperature and blood pressure were measured and recorded. BMI was calculated via the following formula: weight in kilograms divided by the square of height in meters (Wt(kg))/Ht(meters2) [26].

The severity of sickle cell disease

The severity of sickle cell disease was determined by Adegoke’s scoring system [27]. The scores were interpreted as < 8- mild disease; 8-17- moderate disease; and > 17-severe disease. A resting period of no less than five minutes was given to calm each child before an ECG was performed. This was to ensure that the values obtained were truly representative of their resting state.

Based on WHO definition, mild anaemia is defined as Haemoglobin concentration between 10 and 10.9 g/dl, moderate anaemia is defined as Haemoglobin concentration between 7.9 and 9 g/dl while severe anaemia is defined as Haemoglobin concentration of less than 7 g/dl [28].

Sample collection

After the clinical evaluation of the stable subjects, 2 ml of venous blood samples were collected aseptically by the researcher from the study participants to assess the packed cell volume.

A resting period of not less than 5 min was given to calm each child before an ECG was performed. This ensures that the values obtained are truly representative of their resting state. ECG recordings in stable and critically ill subjects were carried out by the researcher.

The haematocrit of each child was checked in the hospital central laboratory by a laboratory scientist who was properly briefed about the study via a micro-haematocrit centrifuge (Haematospin 1400) by Hawksley England, and the PCV after 5 min of spinning was read on the micro-haematocrit reader by the same company.

The hemoglobin genotype of any subject whose genotype was not known was also determined in the central hospital laboratory via electrophoresis by a laboratory scientist via the alkaline cellulose acetate method.

Electrocardiography

The ECG was recorded with a portable commercially available Cardiofax model ECG-960 M 6-Channel ECG machine.

Data analysis

The data were assessed via the Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS) version 26. Means of numerical variables (e.g., age, weight, height, body mass index and haemoglobin concentration) were compared via analysis of variance (ANOVA), whereas chi square tests and Fisher’s exact tests were used to compare categorical variables where appropriate. A P value of < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

Discussion

This study revealed that ECG abnormalities occurred in 48.9% of the patients in the steady state, 73.3% in the crisis state and 37.8% in the control state. This prevalence seen in steady state is similar to the prevalence of 59% reported by Ikegwonu et al. [9] in Abakaliki, southeastern Nigeria, but was less than the 90% prevalence reported by Shrivastava [11] in India. This difference may have occurred because Shrivastava [11] classified sinus arrhythmia, a normal finding in the age group used, as an abnormal ECG finding.

The most common ECG abnormality reported in our study was increased RV6 (evidence of left ventricular hypertrophy), especially in those with VOC and hyperhemolytic crises. This finding revealed that left ventricular hypertrophy is very common in children with SCA. Several authors, such as Ikegwonu et al. [9] in Abakaliki, Nigeria; Adeniyi et al. [10] in Ile-Ife, Nigeria; and Shrivastava [11] in India, also reported LVH as the most common ECG abnormality in the patients which they studied. The prevalence of LVH in our study is, however, less than the 71% prevalence of LVH in SCA reported by Adegoke et al. [16] in Osun, Southwest Nigeria. This may have been a result of the different criteria used to define LVH. These changes in ECG could be explained by chronic anaemia, increased cardiac output with a resulting increase in stroke volume and left ventricular dilation with attendant left ventricular hypertrophy [7]. The prolonged QTc and PR intervals observed in the current study were corroborated by other studies [10, 11, 28–30].

However, among the controls, the most common ECG abnormality was left atrial enlargement (LAE), which was observed in 17.8% of them. This revealed that there was a high prevalence of left atrial enlargement among non-SCA participants. This finding contrasts with reports by Sokunbi et al. [24] and Odike et al. [29], who reported that LVH was the most common abnormality in controls. We could not hazard the exact reason for the LAE that was observed in the control group. The other ECG findings detected in our study were sinus bradycardia, prolonged PR interval, right axis deviation, right ventricular hypertrophy, prolonged QTc, and ST changes, which were also reported by Shrivastava et al. [11] and Odike et al. [29] and other authors [31–33].

Left ventricular hypertrophy was more common in 42.3% of the children with severe anaemia in our study, which is in agreement with reports by Ikegwonu et al. [9] in Abakaliki, Nigeria; Bode-Thomas et al. [32] in Ilorin, Nigeria; and Animasahun et al. [33] in Lagos, Nigeria, where they reported more cardiac abnormalities in SCA children with severe anaemia. Additionally, those with severe anaemia had more ECG abnormalities than those with mild and moderate anaemia. This is also corroborated in other studies [33–36].

The current study showed that left atrial enlargement was 2.6 times more likely to occur in SCA patients in crisis than in those in the steady state. In addition, left ventricular hypertrophy was 1.6 times more likely to occur in crisis patients than in those in the steady state. Similarly, Anah et al. [30] reported that the risk of a prolonged QTc interval was greater during VOC (68.4%) than in the steady state (21.1%) in their study in Calabar, South‒South Nigeria.

From this report, it is evident that SCA children in crisis have more electrocardiographic abnormalities than those in the steady state, and this difference could be because SCA children in crisis are more anaemic and have greater peripheral resistance, higher stroke volume, and higher cardiac output, which predisposes them to more cardiac complications [7].

None of the patients studied had severe disease according to the severity scoring system [27]. The lack of patients with severe disease and the majority of patients with mild disease could be explained by the fact that SCA haplotypes (86.9%) in Nigeria are SCA Benin haplotypes, which are associated with lower frequencies of hospitalization, blood transfusions, and painful episodes than other haplotypes [37–40].

There was a significant relationship between age and the presence of abnormal ECG findings, as 66.7% of the patients in the older age group (14–17 years) had LVH. This revealed that LVH was more common as SCA patients aged. The increase in LVH with age in SCA anaemia could be due to age-related ischaemic changes, LV dilatation, an attempt at healing, and compensatory LV hypertrophy that occurs as they grow with repeated sickling and crises as ageing occurs [7, 41].

Limitations

A prospective study of ECG abnormalities in SCA children in crisis from the point of hospital admission to discharge will add more value to the study. Besides, all the results are qualitative variables, and no quantitative data or values were obtained.

Conclusion

The most common ECG abnormality seen in children with SCA attending Federal University Teaching Hospital Owerri is left ventricular hypertrophy seen in 36.9% of the subjects while left atrial enlargement was the most common ECG finding seen in 32% of the control with left ventricular hypertrophy, bradycardia and Tachycardia statistically significant. The level of anaemia is not statistically related to the ECG findings in SCA. Left Ventricular hypertrophy is more common in SCA age group 14–17 year and there is no gender predilection for the ECG findings of SCA. This may help guide decisions on the need or otherwise of routine ECGs in children with sickle cell anaemia, especially those in crises.

Recommendation

Given the high prevalence of ECG abnormalities in children with sickle cell anaemia, routine ECG screening is recommended.
