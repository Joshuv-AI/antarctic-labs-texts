On Lattice Diameter Segments and A Discrete Borsuk Partition Problem
Anouk E. Brose, Jesús A. De Loera, Gyivan Lopez-Campos, Antonio J. Torres

Abstract
The lattice diameter of a bounded set S ⊂ ℝ d S\subset\mathbb{R}^{d} measures the maximal number of lattice points in a segment whose endpoints are lattice points in S S . Such a segment is called a lattice diameter segment of S S . This simple invariant yields interesting applications and challenges. We describe a polynomial-time algorithm that computes lattice diameter segments of lattice polygons and show that computing lattice diameters of semi-algebraic sets in dimensions three and higher is 𝖭𝖯 \NP -hard. We prove that the function that counts lattice diameter segments in dilations of a lattice polygon is eventually a quasi-polynomial in the dilation factor. We also study the number of directions that lattice diameter segments can have. Finally, we prove a Borsuk-type theorem on the number of parts needed to partition a set of lattice points such that each part has strictly smaller lattice diameter .

Introduction

We study a fundamental geometric invariant of compact sets, called the
lattice diameter
, with a variety of applications in discrete geometry, integer optimization, and the geometry of numbers. We are not the first to study lattice diameters.
This notion was introduced by Corzatt and
Stolarsky
[
Cor74
]
,
studied
by Alarcon
[
AI87
]
and later by Bárány and Füredi
[
BF01
]
. Here are the key definitions of this paper:

Definition 1.1
.

For a compact set
S\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, define

\ldiam(S):=\max_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in S\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d}}|\conv(\{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\})\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d}|-1

as the
lattice diameter
of
S
. If
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{y}
maximize
\ldiam(S)
, we call
\conv(\{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\})
a
lattice diameter segment
. Similarly,
\aff(\{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\})
is a
lattice diameter line
, and finally,
\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}
or any of its non-zero multiples is called a
lattice diameter direction
.

Our paper investigates properties of lattice diameter segments and algorithms to compute them.

Note that the lattice diameter of
S
and
\conv(S)
can differ when
S
is non-convex. When
S
is a convex body, then a lattice diameter line is an affine lattice line
L
such that
|L\cap S\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d}|
is maximal among all affine lattice lines. In this case the lattice diameter of
S
coincides with the lattice diameter of
\conv(\{S\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d}\})
, a lattice polytope, thus we focus on studying lattice diameters of lattice polytopes. The Euclidean diameters and lattice diameters of lattice polytopes need not correlate. For fixed lattice diameter, the Euclidean diameter can be arbitrarily large (
Figure

1(a)
). Moreover, Euclidean diameter lines always pass through vertices
[
GK92
, Lemma 1.7]
, while lattice diameter lines
may not (
Figure

1(b)
).

Lattice lines and lattice diameter lines have several interesting connections. For example, in discrete tomography Gardner and Gritzmann have studied the reconstruction of lattice point sets from “
X
-rays”, i.e., the collection of line sums over parallel lattice lines
[
GG97
]
. Algorithmic connections to combinatorial optimization also exist through the notion of
lattice width
, which is used in algorithms for integer programming. The lattice width has been shown to be bounded by the lattice diameter
[
BF01
]
. Motivated by questions in the geometry of numbers, the volumes of sections and projections of convex bodies have been studied for many years; see the surveys
[
GKZ23
,
NT23
]
. Analogous questions for the number of lattice points have only been studied more recently
[
GGZ05
,
FH22
,
FH24
]
. Algorithmically, it is a challenge to determine a section of a convex body with maximal volume, but algorithms exist for convex polytopes
[
BDLM25
]
. However, computing a section of a lattice polytope with the most lattice points is still an open problem. In the plane, this problem reduces to finding a lattice diameter segment, which we solve here.

Furthermore, classifications of convex lattice polytopes have been studied with respect to different invariants. This is of particular interest in algebraic geometry, where reflexive polytopes correspond to specific toric Fano varieties that have ties to mirror symmetry in physics
[
Bat99
,
Kas06
]
. Arnold, Bárány, Pach, Vershik, and others
[
Arn80
,
BP92
,
BV92
]
have classified lattice polytopes by their volume while Averkov, Lagarias, Nill, Weismantel, Ziegler, and others
[
LZ91
,
AWW11
,
NZ11
]
have continued the classification, based on the number of interior lattice points. We stress that classifying lattice polytopes by their lattice diameter is also possible. A simple result by S. Rabinowitz
[
Rab89
]
shows that if
\ldiam(P)<m
, then
|P\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d}|\leq m^{d}
.
Also in more recent work, Dillon and Arun
[
AD25
]
asymptotically bound the number of vertices that a lattice polytope with fixed lattice
diameter can have.

Another theme of our paper is Ehrhart theory. In its simplest version, for a
d
-dimensional lattice polytope
P
, and
k\in\mathbb{N}
, the Ehrhart counting function,
\Ehr_{P}(k)=|kP\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d}|
, agrees with a polynomial in
k
of degree
d
. This foundational result extends to rational, multivariate, and weighted versions, using generating functions and featuring reciprocity theorems
[
BR15
]
. Here we count not individual lattice points, but subsets of them.
It can be shown that there is an Ehrhart theorem for counting lattice lines with fixed direction in a rational polytope, that pass through a lattice point of the rational polytope
[
Bro
]
.
In this paper we study the counting function of lattice diameter lines (or segments) of a lattice polygon.

A final motivation for our work is the famous Borsuk’s partition problem. In 1933, Borsuk posed the following question in
[
Bor33
]
:

Can every bounded set
S\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be partitioned into
d+1
sets with
smaller diameter?

Here
diameter
refers to the Euclidean diameter, i.e.,
\diam(S):=\max_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in S}\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|_{2}
where
\|\cdot\|_{2}
is the Euclidean norm.
The answer to Borsuk’s question is positive for
d=2
, see
[
Bor33
]
, and for
d=3
, see
[
Per47
]
,
[
Egg55
]
and
[
Grü57
]
. In higher dimensions it was believed to be true until Kahn and Kalai
[
KK93
]
constructed a counterexample for
d=1325
and for each
d>2014
. Today, there are known counterexamples in dimensions
64
and higher, see
[
JB14
]
, but the problem is still open for
4\leq d\leq 63
.
More generally, the problem of partitioning a set into sets with smaller diameter is studied for specific classes of bounded sets. In this paper, we study this problem for sets of lattice points with respect to the lattice diameter instead of the Euclidean diameter. We refer to this challenge as the
discrete Borsuk partition problem
.

Our Contributions

In
Section

2.1
we show that lattice diameter lines of lattice polygons can be computed efficiently:

Theorem 2.4.

Let
P\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a lattice polygon. There exists a polynomial-time algorithm that computes a lattice diameter line of
P
in every direction in which such a line exists. In particular, the lattice diameter of
P
can be computed in polynomial time.

In contrast, we show the following hardness result in
Section

2.2
:

Theorem 2.7.

Let
d\geq 3
and let
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a
d
-dimensional bounded semi-algebraic set.
Computing
a lattice diameter of
K
is an
\NP
-hard problem.

In
Section

3
we generalize Ehrhart theory to counting the set of lattice diameter lines. While counting the number of lattice lines in a fixed direction that intersect
P
in a lattice point is not hard
[
Bro
]
, counting those that are lattice diameter lines is much more difficult. Surprisingly, we have an Ehrhart-type result.

Definition 1.2
.

For a lattice polytope
P
, let
\LD_{P}(k)
denote the number of lattice diameter lines of the dilated polytope
kP
.

Theorem 3.6.
Let
P\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a lattice polygon. There exists a positive integer
q
such that
\LD_{P}:\mathbb{N}_{\geq q}\to\mathbb{N}
agrees with a quasi-polynomial function of degree one whose period divides
q
.

In
Section

4
we begin by generalizing two results by Alarcon
[
AI87
]
to higher dimensions. The first result bounds the total number of lattice diameter directions:

Theorem 4.3.

Let
P\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a lattice
d
-polytope with
\ldiam(P)=1
. Then
P
has at most
\binom{2^{d}}{2}
lattice diameter directions and this bound is best possible.

The second generalization is about a local property of lattice diameter segments. In fact, we prove it more generally for bounded sets. This result is a key ingredient to prove the discrete version of Borsuk’s partition problem.

Lemma 4.6.

Let
S\subset\mathbb{Z}^{d}
be a bounded set and let
\mathbf{x}\in S
. Then, the number of lattice diameter segments of
S
that have
\mathbf{x}
as an endpoint is bounded by
2^{d}-1
, and this bound is best possible.

To study the discrete version of Borsuk’s partition problem, we define the following notion:

Definition 1.3
.

Let
S\subset\mathbb{Z}^{d}
be a bounded set. Define the
lattice Borsuk number
of
S
,
\beta_{\mathbb{Z}}(S)
, as the minimal size of a partition of
S
where each part has a smaller lattice diameter than
\ldiam(S)
.

Finally, the following theorem answers the discrete version of Borsuk’s partition problem:

Theorem 4.8.

Let
S\subset\mathbb{Z}^{d}
be a bounded set. Then,
\beta_{\mathbb{Z}}(S)\leq 2^{d}
and this bound is best possible.

In particular,
Theorem

4.8
says that
\beta_{\mathbb{Z}}(P\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d})\leq 2^{d}
for
d
-dimensional polytopes
P
. Note that we are not partitioning
P
into smaller polytopes, i.e., the parts of
P\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d}
are not necessarily the lattice points of a polytope. The bound
2^{d}
is also best possible for polytopes; it is attained for the lattice points of a
d
-dimensional cube.

Notation

We use basic notions and notation as can be found in the textbooks
[

Zie12
]

A

\spn(S),\,\aff(S)
, and

for the linear, affine, and convex hull of a set

The segment between two points

. A

and a

is a line that passes through at least two lattice points. We say that a line has direction

if it can be written as

. We say
\mathbf{u}

\mathbf{u}
. Furthermore, from now on a

means a lattice diameter direction. The inner product we use is the standard inner product on

, denoted
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
. A hyperplane and its closed half-spaces are denoted
H(\mathbf{a},\alpha):=\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\,\mathrm{:}\,\langle\mathbf{a},\mathbf{x}\rangle=\alpha\}

, we write

which means the intrinsic volume of

. Furthermore, a vector

is called primitive if

. For a positive integer

.
In

[
BM08
]
. A graph is a simple undirected graph

, where

is a finite set and

. In this context,
V
or

2.1
A polynomial-time algorithm to compute lattice diameter lines in the plane

Let
P
be a lattice polygon. We present an algorithm that runs in polynomial time in the input size of
P
and computes a lattice diameter line of
P
for every direction in which one exists.
The algorithm relies on the following three claims:

•

For every diameter direction
\mathbf{u}
, there exists a lattice diameter line in direction
\mathbf{u}
passing through a vertex
\mathbf{v}
and an edge
e
such that some translate of
\aff(e)
is tangent to
P
at
\mathbf{v}
. In this case, we say that
\mathbf{v}
is an
opposite vertex
to the edge
e
.

•

For every pair
(e,\mathbf{v})
, where
e
is an edge and
\mathbf{v}
is an opposite vertex to
e
, one can compute in polynomial time a
local lattice diameter line
at
\mathbf{v}
of the triangle
\conv\{e,\mathbf{v}\}
, i.e., a line through
\mathbf{v}
that intersects
\conv(\{e,\mathbf{v}\})
in the most lattice points.

•

At most three lattice diameter lines pass through any fixed vertex. This was proven by Alarcon
[

, Lemma 2.4]
(and it also follows from our

4.6

The algorithm is as follows:

\nvol(L\cap P):=\frac{\vol(L\cap P)}{\|\mathbf{u}\|}

Lemma 2.1

Let
P

be a line that intersects

. Then

\displaystyle\left\lfloor\nvol(L\cap P)\right\rfloor\leq|L\cap P\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d}|\leq\left\lfloor\nvol(L\cap P)\right\rfloor+1

and the upper bound is attained if
L
contains a lattice point on the boundary of
P
.

Proof.

Parametrize the line as
L=\{\mathbf{x}_{0}+t\mathbf{u}:t\in\mathbb{R}\}
, where
\mathbf{x}_{0}
is an arbitrary lattice point on
L
and
\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
is a primitive vector in the direction of
L
. Then, the intersection
L\cap P
is a segment with endpoints
\mathbf{a}=\mathbf{x}_{0}+t_{1}\mathbf{u}
and
\mathbf{b}=\mathbf{x}_{0}+t_{2}\mathbf{u}
, for some real numbers
t_{1}\leq t_{2}
.
The lattice points in
L\cap P
are precisely those points of the form
\mathbf{x}_{0}+k\mathbf{u}
with
k\in\mathbb{Z}\cap[t_{1},t_{2}]
. Moreover,

\nvol(L\cap P)=\frac{\|\mathbf{b}-\mathbf{a}\|}{\|\mathbf{u}\|}=\frac{\|(t_{2}-t_{1})\mathbf{u}\|}{\|\mathbf{u}\|}=|t_{2}-t_{1}|=t_{2}-t_{1}.

If
t_{1}
is not an integer, then
|L\cap P\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d}|=\lfloor t_{2}\rfloor-\lceil t_{1}\rceil+1=\lfloor t_{2}\rfloor-\lfloor t_{1}\rfloor

and the claim follows from the observation

\lfloor t_{2}-t_{1}\rfloor\leq\lfloor t_{2}\rfloor-\lfloor t_{1}\rfloor\leq\lfloor t_{2}-t_{1}\rfloor+1
. If at least one of
t_{1}
or

is an integer, then

|L\cap P\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d}|=\lfloor t_{2}\rfloor-\lceil t_{1}\rceil+1=\lfloor t_{2}-t_{1}\rfloor+1=\lfloor\nvol(L\cap P)\rfloor+1
.
∎

Proof.

Lemma 2.3

Let

\mathbf{v}

\mathbf{w}_{1},\mathbf{w}_{2}\in T\cap\mathbb{Z}^{2}\setminus\{\mathbf{v}\}

If
\langle\mathbf{a},\mathbf{w}_{1}\rangle=\langle\mathbf{a},\mathbf{w}_{2}\rangle

\mathbf{w}_{1}

\frac{\operatorname{vol}(L_{1}\cap T)}{\|\mathbf{w}_{1}-\mathbf{v}\|}=\frac{\operatorname{vol}(L_{2}\cap T)}{\|\mathbf{w}_{2}-\mathbf{v}\|}.

Applying

On the other hand, if
\langle\mathbf{a},\mathbf{w}_{1}\rangle<\langle\mathbf{a},\mathbf{w}_{2}\rangle

\mathbf{w}^{\prime}\in[\mathbf{v},\mathbf{w}_{2}]
such that
\langle\mathbf{a},\mathbf{w}^{\prime}\rangle=\langle\mathbf{a},\mathbf{w}_{1}\rangle
. Again, by proportionality of lines

\frac{\operatorname{vol}(L_{1}\cap T)}{\|\mathbf{w}_{1}-\mathbf{v}\|}=\frac{\operatorname{vol}(L_{1}\cap T)}{\|\mathbf{w}^{\prime}-\mathbf{v}\|}\geq\frac{\operatorname{vol}(L_{2}\cap T)}{\|\mathbf{w}_{2}-\mathbf{v}\|}.

Applying
Lemma

2.1
it follows that
|L_{1}\cap T\cap\mathbb{Z}^{2}|\geq|L_{2}\cap T\cap\mathbb{Z}^{2}|
.
∎

Using the lemmas above, we prove that the algorithm
Computing Lattice Diameters
is correct and runs in polynomial time in
n
and
\log_{2}(c)
, where
n
is the number of vertices of
P
and
c
is the largest absolute value of a coordinate of a vertex.

Theorem 2.4
.

Let
P
be a lattice polygon. There exists a polynomial-time algorithm that computes a lattice diameter line of
P
for every direction in which a lattice diameter line exists. In particular, the lattice diameter of
P
can be computed in polynomial time.

Proof.

Correctness of the algorithm:
Let
\mathbf{u}
be a diameter direction of
P
. By
Lemma

2.2
, there exists a lattice diameter line
L
in direction
\mathbf{u}
, that passes through an edge
e
and an opposite vertex
\mathbf{v}
to that edge. Thus
L
is a local lattice diameter line of
T=\conv(\{e,\mathbf{v}\})
at
\mathbf{v}
. Let
m=\min\{3,|T\cap\mathbb{Z}^{2}\setminus\{\mathbf{v}\}|\}
, and let
L_{i}
for
i\in[m]
be the lattice lines computed in the second
for loop
. By Alarcon
[
AI87
, Lemma 2.4]
at most three lattice diameter lines pass through
\mathbf{v}
, thus
L
must be contained in
\{L_{i}\,\mathrm{:}\,i\in[m]\}
.

Complexity of the algorithm:
We can assume that the input data is a list of vertices, edges, and incidence relations between the vertices and edges, since in dimension two this data can be computed from the vertices of
P
in polynomial time. Let
n
denote the number of vertices of
P
.

1.

first for loop:
This loop is repeated
n
times, since
P
has
n
edges. Finding an opposite vertex
\mathbf{v}
to an edge
e
is linear optimization, which can be done in polynomial time in the input
[
Sch86
]
. Further, checking if
\mathbf{v}
lies on an edge
[\mathbf{v},\mathbf{v}^{\prime}]
that is parallel to
e
, in which case
e
has two opposite vertices, can also be done in
O(1)
. Each edge contributes at most two triangles, so throughout the whole algorithm
|\mathcal{T}|\leq 2n
.

2.

second for loop:
This for loop is repeated
|\mathcal{T}|
times. For one iteration:
Computing
|T\cap\mathbb{Z}^{2}|
can be computed in polynomial time, for example, by Pick’s Theorem
[
Pic99
]
. Let
\mathbf{a}
be an outward normal vector of
T
with
\min_{\mathbf{x}\in T}\langle\mathbf{a},\mathbf{x}\rangle=\langle\mathbf{a},\mathbf{v}\rangle
.
A lattice point
\mathbf{w}_{1}\in T\cap\mathbb{Z}^{2}\setminus\{\mathbf{v}\}
minimizes the functional
\langle\mathbf{a},\cdot\rangle
if and only if it minimizes this functional over the lattice points of the rational polygon

T^{\prime}:=T\cap H^{-}(\mathbf{a},\langle\mathbf{a},\mathbf{v}\rangle+1).

The other
\mathbf{w}_{i}
for
i\in[m]
can be computed similarly, with an additional check if
\mathbf{w}_{i}\in T\cap H(\mathbf{a},\langle\mathbf{a},\mathbf{w}_{i-1}\rangle)
. Since integer linear programming can be solved in polynomial time in dimension two
[
EL03
]
, the minimizers
\mathbf{w}_{i}
can be computed in polynomial time in the input size of
P
.

3.

The number of lattice points on a segment
L\cap T
, where
L=\aff(\{\mathbf{v},\mathbf{w}\})
, is given by

|L\cap T\cap\mathbb{Z}^{2}|=\left\lfloor\frac{\operatorname{vol}(L\cap T)}{\|\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{v}\|}\right\rfloor+1.

|\mathcal{L}|
is bounded above by
3\cdot(2n)

and finding maximizers of
|L\cap T\cap\mathbb{Z}^{2}|
is linear in
|\mathcal{L}|
.

∎

Remark 2.5
.

extends to simplices in any dimension. Let

be a simplex where

.

2.2
Hardness of computing lattice diameters in dimensions three and higher

A key ingredient in our algorithm to compute lattice diameters of lattice polygons is the existence of lattice diameter lines that pass through vertices. This fails in higher dimensions, as the following example shows.

Example 2.6
.

For
\mathbf{v}_{1}=(-1,0),\,\mathbf{v}_{2}=(0,-1),\,\mathbf{v}_{3}=(1,1)
, let

P=\conv(\{(-m,\mathbf{v}_{1}),(-m,\mathbf{v}_{2}),(-m+1,\mathbf{v}_{3}),(m,-\mathbf{v}_{1}),(m,-\mathbf{v}_{2}),(m-1,-\mathbf{v}_{3})\}),

where
m\geq 2
. The unique lattice diameter segment of
P
is the segment on the first-coordinate axis, from values
-(m-1)
to
m-1
. It does not pass through a vertex of
P
, not even through a lattice point on the boundary of
P
.
Figure

3
depicts this polytope for
m=3
with its lattice diameter segment in orange.

:

We show that in a general setting computing lattice diameters becomes
\NP
-hard.

Theorem 2.7
.

Let
d\geq 3
and let
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a
d
-dimensional bounded semi-algebraic set.
Computing
the lattice diameter of
K
is
\NP
-hard.

Proof.

For integers
a,b,c>0
let
f(x,y):=(x^{2}-a-by)^{2}
and
R(a,b,c):=\{(x,y)\,\mathrm{:}\,1\leq x\leq c-1,\frac{1-a}{b}\leq y\leq\frac{(c-1)^{2}-a}{b}\}
.
It was shown in
[
DLHKW06
, Lemma 2.1]
that deciding whether the minimum of
f
over
R\cap\mathbb{Z}^{2}
is zero, is
\NP
-hard. This is a polynomial-time reduction from the
\NP
-complete problem AN1 in
[
GJ79
, p. 249]
. We may assume that
c>\max\{2,b\}
(which can be checked in constant time), otherwise
R\cap\mathbb{Z}^{2}=\emptyset
. Under these assumptions
R
is also two-dimensional.

We construct a three-dimensional semi-algebraic set
K
with description length polynomial in
a,b,c
, such that determining the lattice diameter of
K
corresponds to determining the minimum value of
f
on
R\cap\mathbb{Z}^{2}
.

Construction of
K
:
Let
\mathbf{p}=(1,\lceil\frac{1-a}{b}\rceil)\in R\cap\mathbb{Z}^{2}
,
Z:=f(\mathbf{p})+\max\{\lfloor\frac{c^{2}-2c}{b}\rfloor+1,c-1\}
, and define
K:=\{(x,y,z)\,\mathrm{:}\,(x,y)\in R,\ f(x,y)\leq z\leq Z\}.

K
is bounded above by
R\times\{Z\}
and below by the graph of
f
.
Figure

4
is a sketch of this.

R\times\{Z\}
R
K

Figure 4
:
The construction given in the proof of
Theorem

2.7
.

The choice of
Z
ensures that a small region around the segment
S_{\mathbf{p}}:=[(\mathbf{p},f(\mathbf{p})),(\mathbf{p},Z)]
is contained in the interior of
K
, thus
\dim K=3
.
Furthermore,
S_{\mathbf{p}}
contains at least
\max\{\lfloor\frac{c^{2}-2c}{b}\rfloor+1,c-1\}+1
lattice points. Any segment not in direction
(0,0,1)
projects to a segment in the
x
-axis, or
y
-axis, but the number of lattice points in the projection of
K
to these axes is bounded by
c-1
, and
\lfloor\frac{c^{2}-2c}{b}\rfloor+1
, respectively. Thus a lattice diameter line of
K
must have direction
(0,0,1)
. Then, for a lattice diameter line
L
of
K

\displaystyle|L\cap K\cap\mathbb{Z}^{3}|=Z-\min_{(x,y)\in R\cap\mathbb{Z}^{2}}f(x,y)+1.

So
|L\cap K\cap\mathbb{Z}^{3}|=Z-1
if and only if
\min_{(x,y)\in R\cap\mathbb{Z}^{2}}f(x,y)=0
, which means that determining the lattice diameter of
K
is
\NP
-hard.

For
d\geq 3
consider
K_{d}:=[0,1]^{d-3}\times K
, which, as a Cartesian product of semi-algebraic sets, is a semi-algebraic set. Analogously, a lattice diameter line of
K_{d}
has direction
(0,\ldots,0,1)
, and determining whether the lattice diameter is
Z-1
is
\NP
-hard, since it determines whether the minimum of
f
over
R\cap\mathbb{Z}^{2}
is zero.

Lastly, note that
K_{d}
for
d\geq 3
is defined by a single quartic polynomial, a constant inequality, which are both polynomial in
a,b,c
, and
2^{d-3}
linear inequalities in
0
and
1
(for
[0,1]^{d-3}
). Hence the description length of
K
and

are polynomial in the encoding length of
a,b
and
c
.
∎

Remark 2.8
.

The construction of
K
is such that the lattice diameter is guaranteed to have direction
(0,\ldots,0,1)
. Thus we proved that computing a lattice diameter of a bounded semi-algebraic set, even in a fixed direction, is
\NP
-hard.

Furthermore, we conjecture that this computation stays hard, even for lattice polytopes:

Conjecture 1
.

Let

and let

be a lattice

-polytope. Computing a lattice diameter of

is an
\NP
-hard problem.

Ehrhart theory studies the number of lattice points in dilations of a given lattice (or rational) polytope. A fundamental result by Ehrhart states that this counting function agrees with a polynomial function for lattice polytopes, and a quasi-polynomial function for rational polytopes. For details, see
[

Lemma 3.2
.

Let

be a lattice polygon and let

. Then, a diameter direction of

Proof.

Let
\mathbf{u}
be a diameter direction of

and let

be a lattice diameter line with direction
\mathbf{u}
that passes through a vertex of
kP
. (That is,
L
is a line that passes through the corresponding vertex of
P
). Let
L^{\prime}
be a lattice line that passes through a vertex of
P
. Then,
kL^{\prime}
also passes through a vertex of
kP
and by
Lemma

2.1

\displaystyle\lfloor\nvol(kL\cap kP)\rfloor+1=|kL\cap kP\cap\mathbb{Z}^{2}|\geq|kL^{\prime}\cap kP\cap\mathbb{Z}^{2}|=\lfloor\nvol(kL^{\prime}\cap kP)\rfloor+1.

(1)

The normalized volume is a homogeneous function in

, so
(

and

\displaystyle|L\cap P\cap\mathbb{Z}^{2}|=\lfloor\nvol(L\cap P)\rfloor+1\geq\lfloor\nvol(L^{\prime}\cap P)\rfloor+1=|L^{\prime}\cap P\cap\mathbb{Z}^{2}|.

Since for every diameter direction of

is a lattice diameter line of
P
. In particular,
\mathbf{u}
is a diameter direction of
P
.
∎

The following lemma shows that under dilation of the polygon only certain lattice diameter lines remain, specifically those for which

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
P
be a lattice polygon. There exists a positive integer
q
such that for all
k\geq q
, and all lattice diameter lines
kL,kL^{\prime}
of
kP
,
\nvol(L\cap P)=\nvol(L^{\prime}\cap P)
.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{R}
be the set of rational lines whose direction is a diameter direction of
P
.
By
Lemma

2.2
we can choose a lattice diameter line
L\in\mathcal{R}
such that
\nvol(L\cap P)\geq\nvol(L^{\prime}\cap P)
for all rational lines
L^{\prime}\in\mathcal{R}
. Let
\nvol(L\cap P)=\frac{p}{q}
for some
p,q\in\mathbb{N}
and note that
\frac{p}{q}\geq 1
.

Let
kL^{\prime}
be a lattice diameter line of
kP
. By
Lemma

3.2
its direction is a diameter direction of
P
, so
L^{\prime}\in\mathcal{R}
. It suffices to show
\nvol(L\cap P)\leq\nvol(L^{\prime}\cap P)
, so that we obtain equality. Towards a contradiction, suppose
\nvol(L\cap P)>\nvol(L^{\prime}\cap P)
. The normalized volume is a homogeneous function in
k
, so

\displaystyle\nvol(kL^{\prime}\cap kP)<\nvol(kL\cap kP)=p+\frac{(k-q)p}{q}.

Together with
Lemma

2.1
and using
\frac{(k-q)p}{q}\geq 1
, this yields

\displaystyle|kL^{\prime}\cap kP\cap\mathbb{Z}^{2}|\leq\lfloor\nvol(kL^{\prime}\cap kP)\rfloor+1<p+\left\lfloor\frac{(k-q)p}{q}\right\rfloor+1=|kL\cap kP\cap\mathbb{Z}^{2}|,

a contradiction to
kL^{\prime}
being a lattice diameter line of
kP
.
∎

Next we observe that parallel lattice diameter lines, for which the segment
\vol(L\cap P)
is maximal, occur between two parallel edges of
P
.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
P
be a lattice polygon and let
\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}\setminus\{0\}
. Define

\mathcal{L}=\{L\,\mathrm{:}\,\text{ L is a $\mathbf{u}$-lattice line and }\vol(L\cap P)\geq\vol(L^{\prime}\cap P)\text{ for all $\mathbf{u}$-lattice lines $L^{\prime}$}\}.

If
|\mathcal{L}|>1
, then there exist parallel edges
e_{1},e_{2}
of
P
such that
L\subseteq\conv(e_{1}\cup e_{2})
for all
L\in\mathcal{L}
.

Proof.

Let
\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}\setminus\{0\}
be orthogonal to
\mathbf{u}
and let
\mathbf{v}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{n}
be the vertices of
P
, enumerated such that
\langle\mathbf{a},\mathbf{v}_{i}\rangle\leq\langle\mathbf{a},\mathbf{v}_{i+1}\rangle
for all
i\in[n-1]
. Define the
parallel chambers

C_{i}:=P\cap H^{+}(\mathbf{a},\langle\mathbf{a},\mathbf{v}_{i}\rangle)\cap H^{-}(\mathbf{a},\langle\mathbf{a},\mathbf{v}_{i+1}\rangle)

for all
i\in[n-1]
for which
\langle\mathbf{a},\mathbf{v}_{i}\rangle<\langle\mathbf{a},\mathbf{v}_{i+1}\rangle
, so
C_{i}
is full-dimensional. A chamber
C_{i}
is bounded by
H(\mathbf{a},\langle\mathbf{a},\mathbf{v}_{i}\rangle),\,H(\mathbf{a},\langle\mathbf{a},\mathbf{v}_{i+1}\rangle)
, and two edges
e_{i1},e_{i2}
of
P
. If the edges
e_{i1}
and
e_{i2}
are parallel, then all segments
C_{i}\cap H(\mathbf{a},b_{i})
for
b_{i}\in[\langle\mathbf{a},\mathbf{v}_{i}\rangle,\langle\mathbf{a},\mathbf{v}_{i+1}\rangle]
have the same length. Otherwise, there is a unique segment of the form
C_{i}\cap H(\mathbf{a},b_{i})
where
b_{i}\in[\langle\mathbf{a},\mathbf{v}_{i}\rangle,\langle\mathbf{a},\mathbf{v}_{i+1}\rangle]
, which is the longest among all segments in this chamber. Note that
P\cap H(\mathbf{a},b_{i})=C_{i}\cap H(\mathbf{a},b_{i})
, so if in the setting of this lemma
|\mathcal{L}|>1
, then all
L\cap P
for
L\in\mathcal{L}
have the same length, and thus are contained in a chamber bounded by parallel edges.
∎

Finally, we count the number of
\mathbf{u}
-lattice diameter lines in dilations of a rational parallelogram. For
\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}\setminus\{0\}
, define

as the number of
\mathbf{u}
-lattice diameter lines (or segments) of
kP
.

Proposition 3.5
.

Let
R
be a rational parallelogram with parallel edges in direction
\mathbf{u}
that each contain an integral vertex. Then there exists a positive integer
q
such that
\LD_{R,u}:\mathbb{N}_{\geq q}\to\mathbb{N}
agrees with a quasi-polynomial function of degree one whose period divides
q
.

Proof.

After applying a unimodular transformation, we may assume that
(0,0)
is a vertex of
R
and that
\mathbf{u}=(1,0)
. Let the other two parallel edges, adjacent to those in direction
\mathbf{u}
, have direction
(a,q)
for some
a,q\in\mathbb{Z},q>0
. Then, one of the edges in direction
\mathbf{u}
has the form
[(0,0),(\frac{p}{q},0)]
and without loss of generality

. This edge contains a
\mathbf{u}
-lattice diameter segment. Furthermore, the other edge emanating from the origin has the form
[(0,0),\lambda(a,q)]
for some

and again we can assume

. That is,
\lambda(a,q)=(\frac{aw}{q},w)
.

Let

be a dilation factor. The lattice width of

in direction
(0,1)
is
kw
.
For
j\in\{0,\ldots,kw\}
, define the lines on height
j
as
L_{j}=(0,j)+\spn(\{(1,0)\})
and denote the corresponding segments by
S_{j}(k)=L_{j}\cap kR
. When

we just write
S_{j}
. Let
\mathcal{L}(k)=\{S_{j}(k)\,\mathrm{:}\,j\in\{0,\ldots,kw\}\}
, that is, all
\mathbf{u}
-lattice segments that intersect
kR
. For a segment

, let
S_{{\mathbb{Z}}}:=\conv(\{S\cap\mathbb{Z}^{2}\})
. So
S_{j}(k)
contains a

-lattice diameter segment if and only if

\mathbf{u}

Claim 1:

\nvol(S_{j}(k))-\nvol(S_{j}(k)_{\mathbb{Z}})<1
if and only if
S_{j}(k)_{\mathbb{Z}}
is a
\mathbf{u}
-lattice diameter segment.

It suffices to show this for

since

is arbitrary. All

have the same length, and

is a lattice diameter segment since it contains a vertex. By

\displaystyle|S_{0}\cap\mathbb{Z}^{2}|=\lfloor\nvol(S_{0})\rfloor+1=\lfloor\nvol(S_{j})\rfloor+1\geq|S_{j}\cap\mathbb{Z}^{2}|=\nvol({S_{j}}_{\mathbb{Z}})+1.

Thus
|S_{0}\cap\mathbb{Z}^{2}|=|S_{j}\cap\mathbb{Z}^{2}|

\lfloor\nvol(S_{j})\rfloor+1=\nvol({S_{j}}_{\mathbb{Z}})+1
which holds if and only if
\nvol(S_{j})-\nvol(S_{j{\mathbb{Z}}})<1
. This proves

.

Claim 2:

\mathbf{u}
-lattice diameter segments

are determined by
j\pmod{q}
.

Let
\chi_{k}:\{0,\ldots,kw\}\to\{0,1\}
be the indicator function with
\chi_{k}(j)=1
if and only if
L_{j}
is a
\mathbf{u}
-lattice diameter line of
kR
. Let
j,j^{\prime}\in\{0,\ldots,kw\}
with

for some

. Then
S_{j^{\prime}}(k)=S_{j}(k)+(ta,tq)
, and
S_{j^{\prime}}(k)_{\mathbb{Z}}=S_{j}(k)_{\mathbb{Z}}+(ta,tq)
. Thus

\displaystyle\nvol(S_{j}(k))-\nvol(S_{j}(k)_{\mathbb{Z}})=\nvol(S_{j^{\prime}}(k))-\nvol(S_{j^{\prime}}(k)_{\mathbb{Z}})

and by
Claim 1

S_{j}(k)_{\mathbb{Z}}
is a
\mathbf{u}
-lattice diameter segment of
kR
if and only if
S_{j^{\prime}}(k)_{\mathbb{Z}}
is.

Claim 3:

\mathbf{u}
-lattice diameter segments
S_{j}(k)_{\mathbb{Z}}
are determined by
k\pmod{q}
.

Let
j\in\{0,\ldots,kw\}
and
i\in\{0,\ldots,q-1\}
. If
S_{j}(k)=[\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}]
, then
S_{j}(i)=[\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}+((k-i)\frac{p}{q},0)]

the right endpoints of

and

, so

\displaystyle\nvol(S_{j}(k))-\nvol(S_{j}(k)_{\mathbb{Z}})=\nvol(S_{j}(i))-\nvol(S_{j}(i)_{\mathbb{Z}}).

By
Claim 1
it follows that
S_{j}(k)_{\mathbb{Z}}
is a
\mathbf{u}
-lattice diameter segment of
kR
if and only if
S_{j}(i)_{\mathbb{Z}}
is a
\mathbf{u}
-lattice diameter segment of

.

The counting function:
Note that
q
and
w
are values that only depend on
R
. Let
i\in\{0,\ldots,q-1\}
, and let

for some
\tilde{k}\in\mathbb{N}
. Define a

as the set of lines

for those

for which

. The number of

\displaystyle\blocks_{i}(k)=\left\lfloor\frac{kw+1}{q}\right\rfloor=\tilde{k}w+\left\lfloor\frac{iw+1}{q}\right\rfloor=\frac{wk}{q}-\left(\frac{iw}{q}-\left\lfloor\frac{iw+1}{q}\right\rfloor\right),

which is a linear function in
k
. Next we count the number of
\mathbf{u}
-lattice diameter lines in

, this only depends on the congruence class

. Take

\displaystyle n_{i}:=\sum_{j=0}^{q-1}\chi_{q+i}(j)=\#\{L_{j}:j=0,\ldots,q-1,\,L_{j}\text{ is a $\mathbf{u}$-lattice diameter line of }(q+i)P\}.

Define

as the number of lattice lines intersecting
kR

q

\rem_{i}=iw+1\bmod q

4
Diameter directions and the discrete Borsuk partition problem

We begin with
Theorem

4.3
which bounds the number of diameter directions of a lattice polytope. Thereafter, we expand our study of lattice diameters to bounded sets
S\subset\mathbb{Z}^{d}
. Note that if
S=\conv(S)\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, then
\ldiam(S)=\ldiam(\conv(S))
, where
\conv(S)
is a lattice polytope, but our results hold for any discrete set of lattice points
S
. In
Lemma

4.6
, we prove an upper bound on the number of lattice diameter segments that end at a given point in
S
. Using this, we prove
Theorem

4.8
, the discrete Borsuk partition problem. A lemma which will be of much use in this section, from
[
Rab89
]
, is the following:

Lemma 4.1

(Rabinowitz, 1989)
.

Let
S\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{d}
be a bounded set and let
m\in\mathbb{N}
satisfy
|S|>m^{d}
. Then
\conv(S)
has at least
m+1
collinear points. In other words, if
\ldiam(S)<m
, then
|S|\leq m^{d}
.

4.1
Diameter directions of a lattice polytope

Alarcon
[
AI87
, Theorem 4.1]
showed that the number of diameter directions of a lattice polygon
P
is at most six if
\ldiam(P)=1
and at most four if
\ldiam(P)>1
; see
Figure

8
for an example.

Figure 8
:
Lattice diameter lines colored by their diameter direction.

We show an analogous statement to the first part, when
\ldiam(P)=1
, in higher dimensions. Our computational experiments suggest that an even smaller bound holds when
\ldiam(P)>1
, but this remains an open problem.

Define the direction
\dir(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
between two points
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
as
\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}
or any of its non-zero multiples. For
X\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
(usually discrete) define the set of its directions as

\displaystyle\dir(X):=\left\{\dir(\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2}):\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2}\in X,\ \mathbf{x}_{1}\neq\mathbf{x}_{2}\right\}.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
X,Y\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
be sets contained in parallel hyperplanes
H(\mathbf{a},\alpha)
and
H(\mathbf{a},\beta)
, respectively. Suppose that
\dir(X)\cap\dir(Y)=\emptyset
. Then all
\dir(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
where
\mathbf{x}\in X
and
\mathbf{y}\in Y
are
pairwise distinct
.

Proof.

Suppose there exist two distinct pairs
(\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{y}_{1}),(\mathbf{x}_{2},\mathbf{y}_{2})\in X\times Y
with
\dir(\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{y}_{1})=\dir(\mathbf{x}_{2},\mathbf{y}_{2})
, that is,
\mathbf{x}_{1}-\mathbf{y}_{1}=\lambda(\mathbf{x}_{2}-\mathbf{y}_{2})
for some non-zero
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
. Then, since
\mathbf{x}_{i}\in X\subseteq H(\mathbf{a},\alpha)
and
\mathbf{y}_{i}\in Y\subseteq H(\mathbf{a},\beta)
we have

\displaystyle 0\neq\alpha-\beta=\langle a,\mathbf{x}_{1}-\mathbf{y}_{1}\rangle=\langle a,\lambda(\mathbf{x}_{2}-\mathbf{y}_{2})\rangle=\lambda\langle\mathbf{a},\mathbf{x}_{2}-\mathbf{y}_{2}\rangle=\lambda(\alpha-\beta)

and thus
\lambda=1
. It follows that
\mathbf{x}_{1}-\mathbf{x}_{2}=\mathbf{y}_{1}-\mathbf{y}_{2}\in\dir(X)\cap\dir(Y)
, a contradiction.
∎

Theorem 4.3
.

Let
P
be a lattice
d
-polytope, with
\ldiam(P)=1
. Then
P
has at most
\binom{2^{d}}{2}
diameter directions and this bound is best possible.

Proof.

Let
d\in\mathbb{N}
. For
d=1
the claim is clear, so assume
d\geq 2
. Since
\ldiam(P)<2
, by
4.1

|P\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d}|\leq 2^{d}
. A lattice diameter line is determined by two lattice points in
P
, so the number of possible diameter directions is bounded above by
\binom{2^{d}}{2}
.

Next, we give a construction of a lattice
d
-polytope attaining this bound. For
t\in\mathbb{N}
and
x\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 3}
, let

\displaystyle\mathbf{v}_{1}=(1,t),\ \mathbf{v}_{2}=(x,tx+1),\ \mathbf{v}_{3}=(-x-1,-tx-t-1)

and let
T_{t,x}=\conv(\{\mathbf{v}_{1},\mathbf{v}_{2},\mathbf{v}_{3}\})
.
Then
(0,0)=\frac{1}{3}\mathbf{v}_{1}+\frac{1}{3}\mathbf{v}_{2}+\frac{1}{3}\mathbf{v}_{3}\in T_{t,x}
and since
T_{t,x}
has area
\frac{3}{2}
, Pick’s Theorem
[
Pic99
]
implies that
T_{t,x}
contains no lattice points besides
(0,0)
. Moreover, no three points in
T_{t,x}
are collinear, thus
\ldiam(T_{t,x})=1
. The slopes of the six diameter lines in
T_{t,x}
are

\displaystyle t,\ t+\dfrac{1}{2x+1},\ t+\dfrac{1}{x+2},\ t+\dfrac{1}{x+1},\ t+\dfrac{1}{x},\text{ and }\ t+\dfrac{1}{x-1},

which are all distinct and contained in
[t,t+\frac{1}{2}]
.

Induction Claim:
For
k\in\{2,\ldots,d\}
there exist
2^{d-k}
lattice
k
-polytopes
P_{1},\ldots,P_{2^{d-k}}
such that
|P_{i}\cap\mathbb{Z}^{k}|=2^{k}
,
P_{i}
has
\binom{2^{k}}{2}
diameter directions, and
\dir(P_{i})\cap\dir(P_{j})=\emptyset
for all
i,j\in[2^{d-k}],\ i\neq j
.

Base Case:
For
k=2
consider
\{T_{i,3}\,\mathrm{:}\,i=1,\ldots,2^{d-2}\}
. As we showed above
T_{i,3}
contains
4=2^{2}
lattice points,
\ldiam(T_{i,3})=2
and all lines through two different lattice points have a different diameter direction, thus
T_{i,3}
has
\binom{4}{2}=6
diameter directions. Furthermore
\dir(T_{i,3})\subseteq[i,i+1/2]
, thus the diameter directions of all
T_{i,3}
for
i=1,\ldots,2^{d-2}
are distinct.

Induction Hypothesis:
For
k\in\{2,\ldots,d-1\}
let
P_{1},\ldots,P_{2^{d-k}}
be lattice
k
-polytopes such that
|P_{i}\cap\mathbb{Z}^{k}|=2^{k}
,
P_{i}
has
\binom{2^{k}}{2}
diameter directions, and
\dir(P_{i})\cap\dir(P_{j})=\emptyset
for all
i,j\in[2^{d-k}],\ i\neq j
. In particular, any two pairs of lattice points in
P_{i}
define lattice diameter lines
with distinct slopes.

Induction Step:
Let
P_{i}
for
i\in[2^{d-k}]
be as given in the induction hypothesis. Let
\underline{P_{i}}:=P_{2i-1}\times\{0\}
and
\overline{P_{2i}}:=P_{2i}\times\{1\}
and construct polytopes

\displaystyle Q_{i}:=\conv\left(\left\{\underline{P_{i}},\overline{P_{2i}}\right\}\right)\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{k+1}.

Since
\underline{P_{i}}
and
\overline{P_{2i}}
are contained in parallel hyperplanes

\displaystyle|Q_{i}\cap\mathbb{Z}^{k+1}|=|\underline{P_{i}}\cap\mathbb{Z}^{k+1}|+|\overline{P_{2i}}\cap\mathbb{Z}^{k+1}|=2^{k+1},

and
\ldiam(Q_{i})=2
. By
Lemma

4.2
all
\dir(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
for any
\mathbf{x}\in\underline{P_{i}}
and
\mathbf{y}\in\overline{P_{2i}}
are distinct so any two points in
Q_{i}
define different lattice diameter lines with distinct diameter directions, i.e.,
Q_{i}
has
\binom{2^{k+1}}{2}
diameter directions. Furthermore, again by
Lemma

4.2
, any two diameter directions in
Q_{i}
and
Q_{j}
are distinct.
This proves the induction claim.
∎

4.2
The discrete Borsuk partition problem

In this section we prove the discrete version of Borsuk’s partition problem for lattice diameters. More generally, we study lattice diameters of bounded sets
S\subset\mathbb{Z}^{d}
.
Notice that the function
f:\mathbb{Z}^{d}\times\mathbb{Z}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
defined by

f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=\nvol([\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}])=|[\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}]\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d}|-1

is a semi-metric, i.e., it is positive-definite and symmetric, so
(\mathbb{Z}^{d},f)
is a semi-metric space. Furthermore,
f
satisfies
f(\lambda\mathbf{x},\lambda\mathbf{y})=|\lambda|\cdot f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
for all
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
and all
\lambda\in\mathbb{Z}
. From this viewpoint,
Theorem

4.8
is closely related to the well-known Boltyansky-Gohberg conjecture
[
BG85
]
which states that any bounded set
S
in a
d
-dimensional normed space can be partitioned into at most
2^{d}
subsets, each having strictly smaller diameter.

Recall that for a bounded set
S
the
lattice Borsuk number
of
S
, denoted
\beta_{\mathbb{Z}}(S)
, is the smallest number of subsets into which
S
can be partitioned, such that each subset has strictly smaller lattice diameter than
\diam_{\mathbb{Z}}(S)
. In
Figure

10
we show a Borsuk partition of a set
S=P\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, where
P
is a lattice polygon, into parts
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
. Necessarily
\ldiam(S_{i})<\ldiam(P)
for
i=1,2
, but
note that
\ldiam(\conv(S_{1}))=\ldiam(P)
.

P
S_{2}
S_{1}

Figure 10
:
A Borsuk partition
P\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d}=S_{1}\sqcup S_{2}
where
\ldiam(S_{1})<\ldiam(\conv(S_{1}))=\ldiam(P)
.

Define the
lattice Borsuk graph
of
S
, denoted
\BG_{\mathbb{Z}}(S)
, as the graph with vertex set
S
and edges
\{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\}\subseteq S
if and only if
f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=\diam_{\mathbb{Z}}(S)
.

Remark 4.5
.

For a bounded set
S\subset\mathbb{Z}^{d}
,
\beta_{\mathbb{Z}}(S)=\chi(\BG_{\mathbb{Z}}(S))
, where
\chi
is the
chromatic number
of
\BG_{\mathbb{Z}}(S)
, i.e., the minimum number of colors needed to color
S
so that no two adjacent vertices in
S
have the same color.

To find a good bound on the lattice Borsuk number it will be useful to bound
\Delta(\BG_{\mathbb{Z}}(S))
, the maximum degree of the lattice Borsuk graph. This is precisely what the next lemma does.

Lemma 4.6
.

Let
S\subset\mathbb{Z}^{d}
be a bounded set and let
\mathbf{x}\in S
. Then, the number of lattice diameter segments in
S
that have
\mathbf{x}
as an endpoint is at most
2^{d}-1
. Therefore,
\Delta(\BG_{\mathbb{Z}}(S))\leq 2^{d}-1
.

Proof.

If
|S|<2
, then there is nothing to show, so suppose
|S|\geq 2
. After an appropriate translation, we may assume that
\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{0}
. Define

S_{\mathbf{0}}:=\{\mathbf{s}\in S:f(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{s})=\operatorname{diam}_{\mathbb{Z}}(S)\}

and consider the corresponding set of vectors

S^{\prime}_{\mathbf{0}}:=\left\{\frac{\mathbf{s}}{\operatorname{diam}_{\mathbb{Z}}(S)}:\mathbf{s}\in S_{\mathbf{0}}\right\}\cup\{\mathbf{0}\}.

Suppose, for contradiction, that
|S_{\mathbf{0}}|>2^{d}-1
, then
|S^{\prime}_{\mathbf{0}}|>2^{d}
, and by
4.1
, we have
\operatorname{diam}_{\mathbb{Z}}(S^{\prime}_{\mathbf{0}})\geq 2
. Let
\mathbf{y}^{\prime},\mathbf{z}^{\prime}\in S^{\prime}_{\mathbf{0}}
such that
f(\mathbf{y}^{\prime},\mathbf{z}^{\prime})\geq 2
. Since all non-zero vectors in
S^{\prime}_{\mathbf{0}}
are primitive, neither
\mathbf{y}^{\prime}
nor
\mathbf{z}^{\prime}
is
\mathbf{0}
. So for
\mathbf{y}=\ldiam(S)\cdot\mathbf{y}^{\prime}
and
\mathbf{z}=\ldiam(S)\cdot\mathbf{z}^{\prime}
we get

f(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{z})=\ldiam(S)\cdot f(\mathbf{y}^{\prime},\mathbf{z}^{\prime})\geq\ldiam(S)\cdot 2,

which is a contradiction. Thus
|S_{\mathbf{0}}|\leq 2^{d}-1
, and
\Delta(\BG_{\mathbb{Z}}(S))\leq 2^{d}-1
follows from
Remark

4.5
.
∎

Now we state Brooks’ Theorem, a graph theoretic result, which relates the maximum degree of a graph to its chromatic number. For the lattice Borsuk graph this translates to a bound on
\beta_{\mathbb{Z}}(S)
. Brooks’ Theorem has many published proofs, see for example
[
BM08
, Theorem 8.4]
.

Theorem 4.7

(Brooks, 1941)
.

If
G
is a connected simple graph, then
\chi(G)\leq\Delta(G)+1
, with equality if and only if
G
is an odd cycle or a complete graph.

Finally, we present a solution to the discrete version of Borsuk’s partition problem.

Theorem 4.8
.

Let
S\subset\mathbb{Z}^{d}
be a bounded set. Then
\beta_{\mathbb{Z}}(S)\leq 2^{d}
and this bound is best possible.

Proof.

By
Remark

4.5
,
Lemma

4.6
and Brooks’ Theorem applied to each component of
\BG_{\mathbb{Z}}(S)
we get

\beta_{\mathbb{Z}}(S)=\chi(\BG_{\mathbb{Z}}(S))\leq\Delta(\BG_{\mathbb{Z}}(S))+1\leq 2^{d}.

The set
S=\{0,1\}^{d}
is an example where this bound is attained.
∎

Notice that
\beta_{\mathbb{Z}}(S)=2^{d}
if and only if there exists an
\mathbf{x}\in S
that is the endpoint of
2^{d}-1
lattice diameter segments, and
\BG_{\mathbb{Z}}(S)
is isomorphic to the complete graph on
2^{d}
vertices. Thus we conjecture the following:

Conjecture 3
.

Let
S\subset\mathbb{Z}^{d}
be a bounded set. Then
\beta_{\mathbb{Z}}(S)=2^{d}
if and only if
\conv(S)
is unimodularly equivalent to a
d
-cube
[0,m]^{d}
for any
m\in\mathbb{N}
.

In dimension two this conjecture is true and can be proven using a characterization of lattice polygons which have four diameter directions, see
[
BF01
, Theorem 2(iii)]
.
