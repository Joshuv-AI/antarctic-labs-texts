Polling on a circle with non-uniform batch arrivals
Tim Engels, Ivo Adan, Onno Boxma, Jacques Resing

Abstract
In this paper, we analyze a polling system on a circle with. Random batches of customers arrive at a circle, where each customer, independently, obtains a location according to a general distribution. A single server cyclically travels over the circle to serve all customers. We analyze the experienced delay of batches for two service policies: globally gated and exhaustive. The Laplace-Stieltjes transform of the experienced delay is found under the former policy. For the latter policy, we propose a mean-value analysis, resulting in an algorithmic approach for the evaluation of the mean experienced delay. Light- and heavy-traffic limits are derived exactly for the system performance.



1
Introduction

Polling systems are queuing models in which the servers attend to multiple queues. In standard polling models, a single server cyclically visits the queues and serves the customers in these queues. The server typically incurs a so-called switch-over time to go from one queue to another. Ample research is devoted to the analysis and optimisation of polling systems, highlighted by the extensive literature reviews of
Takagi (2001)
and

Borst and Boxma (2018)
. The popularity of polling systems lies within their wide range of applications that come from instances where several customers or customer types compete for the service provided by one or more servers.
Examples include computer communication systems
Grillo (1990)
;
Kavitha and Altman (2012)
, traffic systems
Webster (1958)
and warehouse logistics
Gong and de Koster (2008)
. In this paper we analyse the average performance of continuous polling system on a circle with batch arrivals and non-uniform arrival locations.

The analysis is motivated by the following application of warehouse logistics, but can also be applied to other examples, like in ferry-assisted wireless networks
(
Kavitha and Altman, 2012
)
. Milkrun systems are order picking systems where one or more pickers continuously walk through the entire warehouse, picking certain items they encounter
Gong and de Koster (2008)
;
van der Gaast et al. (2019)
. Unlike traditional picking systems, the picker can respond to real time information and does not change her route based on the requested products. By doing so, milkrun systems achieve higher responsiveness, which improves efficiency when the warehouse receives many small orders.

Since the picker in a milkrun system walks a fixed route, she visits the product locations cyclically, similar to polling models. More specifically, one can see the picker as a server and each item location as a queue. A requested item can thus be seen as a customer and an order as a batch of customers. The arrival location distribution represents the storage policy: where high- and low-demand items are located. Hence, a milkrun system can be modelled as a single-server polling system with batch arrivals and general arrival locations. Many warehouses store a large variety of products, and thus it is natural to approximate these systems by a continuous counterpart, where item locations are no longer discrete, but continuous. In case of uniform arrival locations,
Engels et al. (2023)
show that this approximation yields accurate results, even for a relatively small number of different products.

Continuous polling models are an extension of the standard (discrete) polling model. Instead of visiting a fixed number of queues, the server attends to the needs of customers located on a circle. First introduced by
Fuhrmann and Cooper (1985)
, these models have gained considerable attention. Notable mentions include the analyses in
Coffman and Gilbert (1986)
and
Kroese and Schmidt (1992)
. The analysis of continuous polling systems can be quite involved, yet often also results in explicit expressions for the mean performance metrics, see for instance
(
Engels et al., 2023
)
. Going beyond the mean, however, tends to considerably complicate the analysis. One can think of these continuous polling models as limiting cases of their discrete counterpart, where one takes the number of queues to infinity, as proven by
Eliazar (2003)
;
Eliazar (2005)
. Consequently, the continuous model is proven to be an appropriate approximation of its discrete counterpart, especially when the application concerns many queues.

Batch arrivals in polling systems (or related models) have been studied in several papers.
Boxma and Groenendijk (1987)
derive a pseudo-conservation law for a class of queueing models with batch arrivals and vacations, including polling models.
Levy and Sidi (1991)
introduce a framework in which batches of customers arrive, with, possibly, correlated arrival locations. The authors provide an algorithmic approach to find the mean waiting time of a customer.
van der Mei (2002)
extends the research further and finds an exact expression for the waiting time.
van der Gaast et al. (2017)
, instead, focus on the sojourn time of a batch, rather than that of a customer. Using a mean-value approach, the authors derive the mean batch sojourn time in the polling system. In a recent paper,
Engels et al. (2023)
combine the analysis of continuous polling models with batch arrivals, under the assumption that customers arrive uniformly on the circle, and derive analytical expressions for the mean performance of the system.

In the current paper, we relax the assumption about the arrival locations of customers. Instead of assuming uniform arrival locations, we allow customers to be located on the circle according to a predetermined distribution, reflecting the storage policy in the warehousing application. Unlike
Kroese and Schmidt (1993)
, we do not assume that these locations are relative to the server. The current paper is devoted to the analysis of (i) the long-run behaviour of the sojourn time of a batch, that is the time between the arrival of the batch and the service completion of the last customer in that batch and (ii) the time to delivery of a batch of customers, that is the time between the arrival of the batch and the delivery of the batch at a central point. We consider two picking policies: globally gated and exhaustive. The main contributions of this paper are three-fold. (i) We derive exact expressions for the distribution of the batch sojourn time and time to delivery under the globally gated picking policy. (ii) Using a mean-value approach, we derive an iterative algorithm for evaluating the mean performance of the system under the exhaustive service policy and (iii) we find explicit expressions under light- and heavy-traffic limits for both service policies. Using these results, we investigate the system performance under several storage policies. Remarkably, we show that the storage policy only barely affects the average performance of milkrun systems.

This paper is organised as follows. We start with a formal definition of the polling model in Section
2
. Afterwards, we analyse the system under the globally gated policy (Section
3
), resulting in exact expressions for the Laplace-Stieltjes transform and mean of both batch sojourn time and time to delivery. In Section
4
we propose a mean-value analysis of the continuous polling model under exhaustive service. The obtained results are used to find the expected batch sojourn time and time to delivery in Sections
5
and
6
respectively. Section
7
is devoted to the analysis of light- and heavy-traffic behaviour of the polling model. Section
8
contains several numerical results, allowing a numerical comparison of globally gated and exhaustive service and of several storage policies. In that section we also discuss the application of our results to the milkrun system. Here, we will see that the storage policy plays no real role in the performance of the system. Appendices
A
and
B
concern the proofs of Sections
5
and
6
. In appendix
C
, we describe and analyse an algorithm that are used for the numerical results.

2
Model description

Consider a single-server polling system in which batches of customers arrive at a circle (with circumference 1) according to a Poisson process with intensity
\lambda
. The sizes of subsequent customer batches,
K_{1},K_{2},...
, are assumed to be independent and identically distributed with probabilities
p_{k}=\mathbb{P}(K_{1}=k),\,k\geq 1
. We write
\tilde{K}(\cdot)
for the probability generating function of the generic batch size
K
.
The locations on the circle are parametrised to
[0,1)
and denote the clockwise distance from an arbitrary starting point, referred to as the depot, to said location. Throughout this paper, we denote
S
as the server’s location.

Each customer in a batch is independently assigned a location according to a known continuous distribution with density
\pi(x)
,
0\leq x\leq 1
. As customer locations are continuous, no queues will form on the circle. Throughout this paper, we further assume that
\pi(x)<\infty
for all
0\leq x\leq 1
.

A single server is located on the circle and travels at a fixed speed in a clockwise direction, taking a time
\alpha
to traverse the entire circle. The server stops moving whenever she serves a customer. The service times of consecutive customers are denoted as
B_{1},B_{2},...
and are assumed to be independent and identically distributed. We write
B
for the generic service time, with Laplace-Stieltjes transform (LST)
\phi_{B}(\cdot)
. A cycle (of the server) is defined to be the period between two subsequent depot crossings, and its length is denoted by
C
. We let
S^{B}
denote the batch-sojourn time and
D
denote the time to delivery of a batch.

Which customers the server serves in a cycle depends on the discipline we consider. Under the globally gated discipline, the server only serves those customers that were already present in the system at the start of the cycle. Under the exhaustive discipline, the server serves all customers she encounters.

The stability of related systems is studied in several papers. In exhaustive and globally gated polling systems, with a fixed number of queues, the systems are stable under
\rho:=\lambda\mathbb{E}[K]\mathbb{E}[B]<1
, cf.
Boxma and Groenendijk (1987)
;
van der Gaast et al. (2017)
. The continuous variant, discussed in
Kroese and Schmidt (1992)
;
Kroese and Schmidt (1993)
also is known to be stable for
\rho<1
. The proofs of those results can be extended to the current model.

For an alternative proof that
\rho<1
is a sufficient stability condition in the systems under consideration in the present paper, one could use a limiting argument from
Eliazar (2005)
. By generalising this work to allow for batch arrivals, one can prove that this continuous polling model is the limiting case of a discrete polling model, where one takes the number of queues to infinity. This also immediately shows that the long-run average cycle-length satisfies:
\mathbb{E}[C]=\alpha/(1-\rho)
and that the distribution of the server’s location satisfies:

\displaystyle\lim_{\mathrm{d}y\downarrow 0}\frac{\mathbb{P}(S\in[y,y+\mathrm{d}y])}{\mathrm{d}y}=\big[\rho\pi(y)+1-\rho\big].

(1)

3
Globally Gated

In this section, we focus on the globally gated service discipline and derive the LST of both the time to delivery and the batch sojourn time. For this, we start with an initial analysis of the cycle time, and derive expressions for its LST, as well as expressions for the LST of the residual cycle time and age of the cycle time. In turn, these are used for the derivation of the LSTs of the performance measures
S^{B}
and
D
.

Remark that the length of the
n
-th cycle,
C_{n}
, is given by the travel time
\alpha
plus the service time of all customers that arrived during the previous cycle,
C_{n-1}
.
Denote the density of
C_{n}
by
f_{C_{n}}(\cdot)
and its LST by
\phi_{C_{n}}(\cdot)
. Let
A(t)
denote the number of arrival instances that occur in a time interval of length
t
and let
B_{i,j}
denote the service time of the
j
-th customer in the
i
-th batch that arrived during this time. Then the LST of
C_{n}
satisfies:

\displaystyle\phi_{C_{n}}(\omega):=\mathbb{E}\big[\exp(-\omega C_{n})\big]

\displaystyle=\int_{t=0}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}\big[\exp(-\omega C_{n})|C_{n-1}=t\big]f_{C_{n-1}}(t)\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\int_{t=0}^{\infty}\exp(-\omega\alpha)\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(-\omega\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{A(t)}\sum_{j=1}^{K_{i}}B_{i,j}\right)\right]f_{C_{n-1}}(t)\mathrm{d}t.

Since the batches arrive according to a Poisson process, we have:

\displaystyle\phi_{C_{n}}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\int_{t=0}^{\infty}\exp(-\omega\alpha)\exp\left(-\lambda t\left[1-\tilde{K}\big(\phi_{B}(\omega)\big)\right]\right)f_{C_{n-1}}(t)\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\exp(-\omega\alpha)\phi_{C_{n-1}}\left(\lambda\left[1-\tilde{K}\big(\phi_{B}(\omega)\big)\right]\right).

(2)

Taking the limit for
n\to\infty
on both sides now gives a functional equation for the LST
\phi_{C}(\cdot)
of the cycle time. Similar results, in the case of discrete polling models with the globally gated service discipline, can be found in
Boxma et al. (1992)
. It now follows, by repeated application of (
2
), that the LST of the cycle time can be written as an infinite product. Let
\delta_{0}(\omega)=\omega
,
\delta_{1}(\omega)=\lambda\big[1-\tilde{K}\big(\phi_{B}(\omega)\big)\big]
and
\delta_{i+1}=\delta_{1}\big(\delta_{i}(\omega)\big),i\geq 1
. Then:

\displaystyle\phi_{C}(\omega)=\prod_{i=0}^{\infty}\exp\big(-\alpha\delta_{i}(\omega)\big)=\exp\left(-\alpha\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\delta_{i}(\omega)\right).

(3)

Observe that
\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\delta_{i}(\omega)<\infty
because
\rho<1
. Using that
\tilde{K}(x)>1+\mathbb{E}[K](x-1)
and
\phi_{B}(x)\geq 1-\mathbb{E}[B]x
, we indeed have that
|\delta_{i}(\omega)|\leq\lambda\mathbb{E}[K]|1-\phi_{B}(\delta_{i-1}(\omega))|\leq\rho|\delta_{i-1}(\omega)|
.

The LST of the joint distribution of the age of the cycle time,
C_{P}
, and residual cycle time
C_{R}
, that an arbitrary arriving batch encounters, satisfies the following (cf. Section 3.1 of
Boxma et al. (2009)
):

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\exp(-\omega_{P}C_{P}-\omega_{R}C_{R})\right]=\frac{1}{\mathbb{E}[C]}\frac{1}{\omega_{P}-\omega_{R}}\big[\phi_{C}(\omega_{R})-\phi_{C}(\omega_{P})\big].

(4)

This will form an integral part for the derivation of the batch sojourn time and time to delivery. Call
C^{*}:=C_{P}+C_{R}
, the length of the cycle during which an arbitrary batch of customers arrives. Note that the above relation gives:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\exp(-\omega C^{*})\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\exp(-\omega C_{P}-\omega C_{R})\right]

(5)

\displaystyle=\lim_{\omega_{P}\to\omega}\frac{1}{\mathbb{E}[C]}\frac{1}{\omega_{P}-\omega}\big[\phi_{C}(\omega)-\phi_{C}(\omega_{P})\big]=-\frac{1}{\mathbb{E}[C]}\phi_{C}^{\prime}(\omega).

(6)

It now follows that
\mathbb{E}[C^{*}]=\mathbb{E}[C^{2}]/\mathbb{E}[C]
and
\mathbb{E}[C_{P}]=\mathbb{E}[C_{R}]=\mathbb{E}[C^{2}]/(2\mathbb{E}[C])
, where
\mathbb{E}[C^{2}]
can be found by differentiating (
2
) twice and solving for
\mathbb{E}[C^{2}]
:

\mathbb{E}[C^{2}]=\frac{1}{1-\rho^{2}}\cdot\Big\{\alpha^{2}+2\rho\alpha\mathbb{E}[C]+\lambda\mathbb{E}[K]\mathbb{E}[B^{2}]\mathbb{E}[C]+\lambda\mathbb{E}[B]^{2}\mathbb{E}[K(K-1)]\mathbb{E}[C]\Big\}.

Remark that
C,C^{*},C_{R}
and
C_{P}
are all independent of the arrival location density
\pi
.

We can now use these expressions for the LSTs of
C^{*},C_{R},C_{P}
to derive the distribution of both the time to delivery and batch sojourn time. In both derivations, we use that the customers have to wait for the residual cycle time, plus extra work that has arrived/will arrive during the current cycle.

Time to delivery

The time to delivery of a batch consists of the residual cycle time, the travel time of a cycle and the service times of
all
customers who arrive(d) during the same cycle as the tagged batch. Using this observation, the derivation of the LST of the time to delivery immediately follows:

Theorem 1
.

The time to delivery,
D
, in the polling model under the globally gated service discipline has the following Laplace-Stieltjes Transform:

\displaystyle\phi_{D}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\tilde{K}\big(\phi_{B}(\omega)\big)\exp(-\omega\alpha)\frac{1}{\omega\mathbb{E}[C]}

(7)

\displaystyle\cdot\Big[\phi_{C}\Big(\lambda-\lambda\tilde{K}\big(\phi_{B}(\omega)\big)\Big)-\phi_{C}\Big(\omega+\lambda-\lambda\tilde{K}\big(\phi_{B}(\omega)\big)\Big)\Big].

Proof.

The time to delivery of a tagged batch is composed of the following elements: (i) the residual cycle time,
C_{R}
, (ii) the travel time of an entire cycle,
\alpha
, (iii) the service time of the customers in the tagged batch, say
K_{0}
customers arrive in this batch, and (iv) the service time of all other customers who arrived during
C^{*}
. A total of
A(C^{*})
arrivals occur in
C^{*}
, and each of these arrivals contains a batch of customers, say of size
K_{j}
for the
j
-th arriving batch. Let
B_{0,j}
denote the service time of the
j
-th customer in the tagged batch and
B_{i,j}
the service time of the
j
-th customer in the
i
-th batch that also arrived during
C^{*}
. Then, with
\overset{d}{=}

denoting equality in distribution,

D\overset{d}{=}C_{R}+\sum_{j=1}^{K_{0}}B_{0,j}+\alpha+\sum_{i=1}^{A(C^{*})}\sum_{j=1}^{K_{i}}B_{i,j}.

(8)

Let
f_{C_{P},C_{R}}(\cdot,\cdot)
denote the joint density of
C_{P}
and
C_{R}
and condition over the size of the past and residual cycle time. Applying (
4
) then gives:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\exp(-\omega D)\right]

\displaystyle=\tilde{K}\big(\phi_{B}(\omega)\big)\exp(-\omega\alpha)\cdot

\displaystyle\quad\quad\bigg\{\int_{t=0}^{\infty}\int_{s=0}^{\infty}\exp\left(-\omega t-(s+t)\Big[\lambda-\lambda\tilde{K}\big(\phi_{B}(\omega)\big)\Big]\right)f_{C_{P},C_{R}}(s,t)\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{d}t\bigg\}

\displaystyle=\tilde{K}\big(\phi_{B}(\omega)\big)\exp(-\omega\alpha)\frac{1}{\omega\mathbb{E}[C]}

\displaystyle\;\;\;\cdot\Big[\phi_{C}\Big(\lambda-\lambda\tilde{K}\big(\phi_{B}(\omega)\big)\Big)-\phi_{C}\Big(\omega+\lambda-\lambda\tilde{K}\big(\phi_{B}(\omega)\big)\Big)\Big].\qed

Corollary 1
.

The mean time to delivery under the globally gated discipline is given by:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[D]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[B]\mathbb{E}[K]+\alpha+\mathbb{E}[C_{R}]+\lambda\mathbb{E}[B]\mathbb{E}[K]\mathbb{E}[C^{*}]

(9)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[B]\mathbb{E}[K]+\alpha+(1+2\rho)\mathbb{E}[C_{R}].

Proof.

This is a direct consequence of (
8
). Alternatively, one can derive this by evaluating the derivative of (
7
) at
\omega=0
.
∎

Remark 1
.

The arrival location distribution,
\pi
does not affect the distribution of the time to delivery in the polling system under the globally gated policy. This is because the arrival locations do not affect the cycle times, and therefore does not affect the time to delivery.

Batch sojourn time

The approach to the batch sojourn time is similar, where we now only have to consider customers that arrive ahead (in location) of the last customer in the batch. Therefore, we condition over the arrival location of the furthest customer (measured in the clockwise distance from the depot) in the tagged batch. Let
\Pi(x)
denote the cumulative distribution function of the arrival locations and
X^{B}
the arrival location of the furthest customer in the tagged batch, then:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\exp(-\omega S^{B})\right]=\int_{x=0}^{1}\sum_{k}p_{k}k\pi(x)\Pi(x)^{k-1}\mathbb{E}\left[\exp(-\omega S^{B})|K=k,X^{B}=x\right]\mathrm{d}x.

The conditional distribution of the batch sojourn time is now quite similar to the distribution of the time to delivery. Let
X_{i,j}
denote the arrival location of the
j
-th customer in the
i
-th batch arriving in the same cycle as the tagged customer batch. Further, let
K_{i}
again denote the size of the
i
-th batch arriving in the same cycle as the tagged batch, of which the
j
-th customer has a service time
B_{i,j}
. Then we can write for the conditional batch sojourn time:

S^{B}\Big|\{K_{0},X^{B}\}\overset{d}{=}C_{R}+\sum_{j=1}^{K_{0}}B_{0,j}+\alpha X^{B}+\sum_{i=1}^{A(C^{*})}\sum_{j=1}^{K_{i}}B_{i,j}\mathbbm{1}\big\{X_{i,j}\leq X^{B}\big\}.

(10)

Using a similar approach to the time to delivery now shows:

Theorem 2
.

The batch sojourn time,
S^{B}
, in the polling model under the globally gated service discipline has the following Laplace-Stieltjes Transform:

\displaystyle\phi_{S^{B}}(\omega)=\frac{\phi_{B}(\omega)}{\omega\mathbb{E}[C]}\int_{x=0}^{1}\bigg\{

\displaystyle\pi(x)\tilde{K}^{\prime}\Big(\Pi(x)\phi_{B}(\omega)\Big)\exp(-\omega\alpha x)

(11)

\displaystyle\cdot\Big[\phi_{C}\Big(\lambda-\lambda\tilde{K}\big(1-\Pi(x)+\Pi(x)\phi_{B}(\omega)\big)\Big)

\displaystyle-\phi_{C}\Big(\omega+\lambda-\lambda\tilde{K}\big(1-\Pi(x)+\Pi(x)\phi_{B}(\omega)\big)\Big)\Big]\bigg\}\mathrm{d}x.

Proof.

First remark that the distribution of the number of customers in a batch, arriving in front of
X^{B}=x
, has the following probability generating function:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[z^{\sum_{j=1}^{K_{i}}\mathbbm{1}\big\{X_{i,j}\leq x\big\}}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}p_{k}\mathbb{E}\left[z^{\mathbbm{1}\big\{X\leq x\big\}}\right]^{k}

\displaystyle=\tilde{K}\Big(1-\Pi(x)+\Pi(x)z\Big).

Using this, and following the steps of the derivation for the time to delivery, we conclude:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[\exp(-\omega S^{B})|K=k,X^{B}=x\Big]

\displaystyle=\phi_{B}(\omega)^{k}\exp(-\omega\alpha x)\frac{1}{\omega\mathbb{E}[C]}

\displaystyle\quad\cdot\Big[\phi_{C}\Big(\lambda-\lambda\tilde{K}\big(1-\Pi(x)+\Pi(x)\phi_{B}(\omega)\big)\Big)

(12)

\displaystyle\quad\quad-\phi_{C}\Big(\omega+\lambda-\lambda\tilde{K}\big(1-\Pi(x)+\Pi(x)\phi_{B}(\omega)\big)\Big)\Big].

Deconditioning with respect to
X^{B}
and
K
, using
\mathbb{P}(X^{B}\leq x)=\Pi(x)^{k}
, now results in the final expression.
∎

Corollary 2
.

The mean batch sojourn time under the globally gated discipline is given by:

\mathbb{E}[S^{B}]=\mathbb{E}[B]\mathbb{E}[K]+\mathbb{E}[C_{R}]+\alpha-\alpha\int_{x=0}^{1}\tilde{K}(\Pi(x))\mathrm{d}x+\rho\mathbb{E}[C^{*}]\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{K}{K+1}\right].

(13)

Proof.

This result follows from (
10
), where we use that
A(C^{*}),K_{i}
and
B_{i,j}
are independent from
X_{i,j}
and
X^{B}
. The expected distance to the furthest customer in a batch, from the depot, is given by:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[X^{B}]=\int_{x=0}^{1}x\cdot\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}p_{k}\cdot k\cdot\pi(x)\Pi(x)^{k-1}\mathrm{d}x=\int_{x=0}^{1}x\pi(x)\tilde{K}^{\prime}\left(\Pi(x)\right)\mathrm{d}x.

By partial integration, we find that the expected travel time to the furthest customer equals:

\displaystyle\alpha\mathbb{E}[X^{B}]=\alpha-\alpha\int_{x=0}^{1}\tilde{K}(\Pi(x))\mathrm{d}x.

We further note that:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(X_{i,j}\leq X^{B})

\displaystyle=\int_{x=0}^{1}\sum_{k}kp_{k}\pi(x)\Pi(x)^{k}\mathrm{d}x=\int_{x=0}^{1}\pi(x)\Pi(x)\tilde{K}^{\prime}\big(\Pi(x)\big)\mathrm{d}x.

The proof now follows from the substitution of
u=\Pi(x)
in the integral, combined with the observation that:

\displaystyle\int_{u=0}^{1}u\tilde{K}^{\prime}(u)\mathrm{d}u=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}p_{k}\int_{u=0}^{1}ku^{k}\mathrm{d}u=\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{K}{K+1}\right].

One can also prove the corollary by evaluating the derivative of the LST in (
11
) at
\omega=0
.
∎

Remark 2
.

The expected batch sojourn time is solely affected by the arrival location distribution through the expected travel time to the furthest customer in a batch. All other terms remain unaffected. One can minimize the expected batch sojourn time by locating all customers just behind the depot.

Remark 3
.

The difference
\mathbb{E}[D]-\mathbb{E}[S^{B}]
consists of the extra travel distance from the furthest customer in a batch to the depot, and the service of all extra customers that the server encounters during this travelling. Corollaries
1
and
2
show that this is in expectation given by:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[D]-\mathbb{E}[S^{B}]

\displaystyle=\alpha\int_{x=0}^{1}\tilde{K}(\Pi(x))\mathrm{d}x+\rho\mathbb{E}[C^{*}]\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{K+1}\right].

This difference is thus (relatively) large when either
\pi
has most of its mass at the beginning of the circle, or the sizes of customer batches are small. These cases coincide with instances where the batch sojourn time is small.

4
Exhaustive

The analysis of the system under the exhaustive service discipline is more complex on both a conceptual and a technical level. Since the server now serves any customers she encounters, we cannot apply similar methods as in the previous section. Instead of deriving the LST of the performance measures, we now consider their average behaviour. We extend the work in
Engels et al. (2023)
, where only uniform arrival locations are considered, and derive a mean-value analysis of the system with the exhaustive service discipline.

In the current paper, the mean-value approach focuses on the average “spread” of customers on the circle. We do this by analysing
L(A)
, the number of waiting customers with locations within a set
A
. In particular, we focus on this variable conditional on the server’s location. We define the following:

\displaystyle f(x,y)

\displaystyle:=\lim_{\delta\to 0}\frac{\mathbb{E}\big[L\big([x,x+\delta]\big)\big|S=y\big]}{\delta}.

(14)

Intuitively,
f(x,y)\mathrm{d}x
represents the average number of waiting customers in the interval
[x,x+\mathrm{d}x]
when the server is at location
y
(where
x,y
denote distances from the depot). The function,
f(x,y)
, can also be seen as a measure of the
average density
of customers at a certain location.

In the derivations in this section, it will prove to be useful to extend the definition of integration over the circle in the following way for
a,b\in[0,1]
:

\displaystyle{\int_{x=a}^{b}}^{*}f(x)\mathrm{d}x:=\begin{cases}\int_{x=a}^{b}f(x)\mathrm{d}x&\text{if: }a\leq b\\
\int_{x=a}^{1}f(x)\mathrm{d}x+\int_{x=0}^{b}f(x)\mathrm{d}x&\text{if: }a>b.\end{cases}

(15)

Specifically, this definition can be used to define the clockwise distance between two locations:

\displaystyle d(a,b):={\int_{x=a}^{b}}^{*}\mathrm{d}x=\begin{cases}b-a&\text{if: }a\leq b\\
1-a+b&\text{if: }a>b.\end{cases}

The remainder of this section is built up as follows. In Section
4.1
we derive the expected total number of waiting customers in the polling system. Afterwards, we turn our attention to the average spread of customers, dictated by
f
, and prove that this function adheres to an integral equation, see Section
4.2
. In Section
4.3
we find a partial solution to the integral equation, and in Section
4.4
we prove that the entire solution can be found by means of successive substitution.

4.1
Expected number of waiting customers

We start with an analysis of the average number of waiting customers in the system,
\mathbb{E}[L]
, i.e. the expected number of customers whose service has not yet started. We couple the current system to a similar system that serves the customers in a First-Come-First-Serve (FCFS) manner. This system no longer assigns a location to the customer. Instead, the server keeps track of all locations she has to visit. Whenever the server reaches one of these locations, she starts serving the longest waiting customer from the entire system.

Remark that both the service times and travel times between consecutive service starts are the same in the polling system and the FCFS variant, only the order of service is altered. As a direct consequence, the numbers of customers in the systems are equal. Analysing the First-Come-First-Server model arguably is easier, as the waiting time of a customer in this model does not depend on future arrivals.

Lemma 3
.

The average number of waiting customers in the polling model under the exhaustive service discipline is given by:

\mathbb{E}[L]=\frac{\lambda\mathbb{E}[K]}{2(1-\rho)}\Big(\alpha+\rho\frac{\mathbb{E}[B^{2}]}{\mathbb{E}[B]}+\frac{\mathbb{E}[B]\mathbb{E}[K(K-1)]}{\mathbb{E}[K]}\Big).

(16)

Proof.

We consider the related FCFS system, and denote the variables related to this system with a superscript
FCFS
. Remark that the waiting time of a tagged customer under the FCFS model is given by the sum of: (i) the travel time,
T
, to the tagged customer; (ii) the residual service time of the customer in service, if any, at the arrival instant of the tagged customer; (iii) the service time of all waiting customers present at the time of arrival and (iv) the service time of all customers arriving in the same batch, who are served before the tagged customer. Hence:

\mathbb{E}\left[W^{\rm FCFS}\right]=\mathbb{E}[T]+\rho\frac{\mathbb{E}[B^{2}]}{2\mathbb{E}[B]}+\mathbb{E}\left[L^{\rm FCFS}\right]\mathbb{E}[B]+\frac{\mathbb{E}[B]\mathbb{E}[K(K-1)]}{2\mathbb{E}[K]}.

One can now apply Little’s law,
\mathbb{E}[L^{\rm FCFS}]=\lambda\mathbb{E}[K]\mathbb{E}[W^{\rm FCFS}]
to find a first expression for the mean number of waiting customers in the system. It remains to find the average travel time
\mathbb{E}[T]
to a waiting customer.

The travel time under the FCFS service policy is hard to analyse, as this depends on the state of the system at the arrival instant, as well as future arrivals. Instead, we remark that the
average
travel time to a tagged customer is the same for the FCFS model and polling model. This is due to the fact that the sum of the travel times to all customers is the same. We can therefore focus on the original system to derive
\mathbb{E}[T]
. Recall (
1
), stating that the server is within a small interval
[x,x+\mathrm{d}x]
with probability
[\rho\pi(x)+1-\rho]\mathrm{d}x
. Therefore, we have:

\mathbb{E}[T]=\alpha\int_{x=0}^{1}\big[\rho\pi(x)+1-\rho]\mathbb{E}[d(x,X_{1})]\mathrm{d}x=\alpha\rho\mathbb{E}[d(X_{2},X_{1})]+\alpha(1-\rho)\mathbb{E}[d(U,X_{1})],

where
X_{1},X_{2}
denote the locations of two arbitrary customers and
U
denotes a Uniform
[0,1]
random variable. As the average distance from a uniform point on the circle to any point equals
1/2
, also
\mathbb{E}[d(U,X_{1})]=1/2
by the independence of
X_{1}
and
U
. Due to the symmetry, we further have
\mathbb{E}[d(X_{1},X_{2})]=\mathbb{E}[d(X_{2},X_{1})]
. Combined with the fact that
d(X_{1},X_{2})+d(X_{2},X_{1})=1
, we find:
\mathbb{E}[d(X_{2},X_{1})]=1/2
. The proof is now finished by substituting
\mathbb{E}[T]=\alpha/2
in the expression for the waiting time.
∎

Remark 4
.

The mean number of waiting customers in the system is not affected by the arrival location distribution. Using Eliazar’s limit argument
(
Eliazar, 2005
)
, this is a consequence of the pseudo-conservation law for discrete polling models, cf. Equation (3.21) of
Boxma (1989)
, stating that the weighted (by load of queue) sum of expected waiting times is indifferent to the arrival location distribution,
\lambda_{i}/(\sum_{j}\lambda_{j})
.

4.2
Integral equation for spread of customers

We now turn to
f(x,y)
, see (
14
), describing the average spread of customers on the circle. This characteristic is essential to the mean-value analysis of polling models on a circle and can be used to derive expressions for both the mean batch sojourn time and mean time to delivery. In this section, we prove that
f(x,y)
satisfies is the unique solution to a given integral equation.

Consider the class of functions,
C_{\pi}
, consisting of all functions
f:[0,1]^{2}\to[0,\infty)
such that
f(x,y)=O(\pi(x))
. In this section we prove that:

Proposition 4
.

The function
f(x,y)
is the
unique
solution, in
C_{\pi}
, to the integral equation:

\displaystyle\big[\rho\pi(y)+1-\rho\big]f(x,y)

\displaystyle=\rho{\int_{u=x}^{y}}^{*}\big[\rho\pi(u)+1-\rho]\cdot\big(\pi(y)f(x,u)+\pi(x)f(y,u)\big)\mathrm{d}u

(17)

\displaystyle\quad+\lambda\mathbb{E}[K]\pi(x)\begin{aligned} \bigg\{&\alpha{\int_{u=x}^{y}}^{*}\big[\rho\pi(u)+(1-\rho)\big]\mathrm{d}u+\frac{\rho\mathbb{E}[B^{2}]}{2\mathbb{E}[B]}\pi(y)\\
&+\mathbb{E}[B]\pi(y)\frac{\mathbb{E}[K(K-1)]}{\mathbb{E}[K]}{\int_{u=x}^{y}}^{*}\big[\rho\pi(u)+1-\rho\big]\mathrm{d}u\bigg\}.\end{aligned}

Remark 5
.

f(x,y)
should be such that the expected number of waiting customers in the system is equal to
\mathbb{E}[L]
. It can indeed be proven that any solution, in
C_{\pi}
, to the fixed point equation immediately satisfies this condition, see Lemma
20
.

The proof consists of three main elements: (i)
f(x,y)
satisfies (
17
), (ii)
f(x,y)
is an element of
C_{\pi}
and (iii) the integral equation has a unique solution in
C_{\pi}
.

The proof of the first part follows a mean-value based approach. We start from a variant of Little’s law, where we relate the number of customers in the system (at given locations) to the waiting time of a customer (arriving at a given location). We then derive an expression for the average waiting time of such a customer, which again involves the location of the other customers in the system. Ultimately, this results in the integral equation (
17
).

The variant of Little’s law is related to a similar observation as
Winands et al. (2006, Equation (6))
. The idea is that each customer at a location
x
needs to have arrived after the last visit to that location.

Lemma 5
.

Let
W_{y}
denote the waiting time of a customer arriving at location
y
. Then the function
f(\cdot,\cdot)
satisfies the following for all
x,y\in[0,1]
:

\displaystyle{\int_{u=x}^{y}}^{*}\big[\rho\pi(u)+1-\rho]f(x,u)\mathrm{d}u=\lambda\mathbb{E}[K]\pi(x){\int_{u=x}^{y}}^{*}\big[\rho\pi(u)+1-\rho\big]\mathbb{E}[W_{y}|S=u]\mathrm{d}u.

(18)

Proof.

We use an intuitive argument to prove this statement. A similar argument, for Little’s law, can be found in
Tijms (2003, page 51)
.

Consider the case in which each customer at location
x
gets a reward of
1
dollar per time unit she has to wait while the server is in
(x,y)
. Then there would be two ways of paying this out. First, each time unit we can pay each customer at location
x
one dollar if the server is in
(x,y)
. Given that the server is at
u\in(x,y)
, this amounts to an average of
f(x,u)
per time unit the server is at
u
. Using that the server is in
[u,u+\mathrm{d}u]
with probability
[\rho\pi(u)+1-\rho)]\mathrm{d}u
, then shows that per time unit we pay on average:

{\int_{u=x}^{y}}^{*}\big[\rho\pi(u)+1-\rho]f(x,u)\mathrm{d}u.

The second method entails paying each customer at location
x
the moment the server leaves
(x,y)
, i.e. when the server reaches
y
. The amount each customer receives is then given by the time they waited at
x
while the server was in
(x,y)
. Remark that we only pay customers who arrive during the period that the server is in
(x,y)
. The total reward these customers obtain is the residual time of this period at their arrival instant, which is equivalent to the waiting time
W_{y}
of an auxiliary customer arriving at location
y
. With probability
[\rho\pi(u)+1-\rho]\mathrm{d}u
a customer arriving at location
x
sees the server in a small interval
[u,u+\mathrm{d}u]
, obtaining a reward of
W_{y}|S=u
. On average, a total of
\lambda\mathbb{E}[K]\pi(x)
customers arrive at
x
per time unit, therefore on average we pay the following amount per time unit:

\lambda\mathbb{E}[K]\pi(x)\bigg\{{\int_{u=x}^{y}}^{*}\big[\rho\pi(u)+1-\rho\big]\mathbb{E}[W_{y}|S=u]\mathrm{d}u\bigg\}.\qed

Remark 6
.

An alternative argument for Lemma
5
is the following. Each customer at
x
needs to have arrived since the last visit of the server to location
x
. Given that the server is currently in
(x,y]
, the time since the last visit to
x
is given by the age of the period in which the server travels from
x
to
y
. The expected age of this period is equal to the expected residual time of this period, which in turn is equal to the waiting time of an auxiliary customer at location
y
(given that the server is in
(x,y]
).

It is now left to find an expression for
\mathbb{E}[W_{y}|S=u]
. For this, we consider the state of the system at the arrival instant of the customer, and link future arrivals to this current state of the system. Inspired by
Resing (1993)
, we construct a branching process with immigration adhering to the following rules:

•

A customer (a) is the offspring of customer (b) when (a) arrives during the service of (b).

•

A customer (a) is called an immigrant when she arrives during a travel period of the server.

This branching process may include customers who will not be served before the tagged customer at location
y
, and therefore do not add to the experienced waiting time of the customer at
y
. Because of this, we trim the branching process to only include customers who will be served before the tagged customer, see Figure
1

(
Engels et al., 2023
)
.

Using this branching process construction, we can define the “effect” that customers of these two types have on the waiting time of the tagged customer. Formally we define these as:

Definition 1
.

The total service time of customers in a branching process with an ancestor at location
x
and tagged customer at location
y
is denoted as
S(x,y)
. We call this the extra waiting time for a (tagged) customer at location
y
generated by the service of a customer at location
x
.

Definition 2
.

The travel time plus total service time of customers in the branching processes, trimmed after location
y
, of all immigrants that arrive during the travelling of the server from location
x
to
y
is defined as
T(x,y)
. We call this the extra waiting time generated by the travelling of the server from
x
to
y
.

Intuitively, these definitions represent the effect that the service of a customer resp. travelling of the server has on the waiting time of the tagged customer. E.g., without a customer at location
x
, the waiting time of a tagged customer at location
y
would have been
S(x,y)
lower.

The first moment of
S(x,y)
can be found by conditioning on the arrivals that occur during the service of the customer. These arrivals again generate a new time
S(X,y)
, where
X
is their arrival location. This gives a recursive formula, that can be solved analytically. Similarly, the first moment of
T(x,y)
can be found by conditioning on the arrivals that occur during the travelling of the server. The proof heavily relies on the memoryless property of exponential inter-arrival times.

Lemma 6
.

The extra waiting time for a tagged customer at location
y
generated by a customer at location
x
,
S(x,y)
, has expectation:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[S(x,y)]=\mathbb{E}[B]\exp\Big(\rho{\int_{\xi=x}^{y}}^{*}\pi(\xi)\mathrm{d}\xi\Big).

(19)

The expected extra waiting time generated by the travelling of the server from
x
to a tagged customer at location
y
,
\mathbb{E}[T(x,y)]
, is given by:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[T(x,y)]={\int_{u=x}^{y}}^{*}\alpha\exp\Big(\rho{\int_{\xi=u}^{y}}^{*}\pi(\xi)\mathrm{d}\xi\Big)\mathrm{d}u.

(20)

Proof.

During the service of a customer a total of
\rho=\lambda\mathbb{E}[K]\mathbb{E}[B]
customers are expected to arrive. Only those customers that arrive between
x
and
y
contribute to the waiting time of the tagged customer at location
y
. Note that a customer arriving at location
u
again generates an extra waiting time of
S(u,y)
, hence:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[S(x,y)]=\mathbb{E}[B]+\rho{\int_{u=x}^{y}}^{*}\pi(u)\mathbb{E}[S(u,y)]\mathrm{d}u.

Taking the derivative with respect to
x
on both sides gives a first-order differential equation:

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}\mathbb{E}[S(x,y)]=-\rho\pi(x)\mathbb{E}[S(x,y)].

The proposed expression solves this differential equation with the additional condition that
\lim_{x\uparrow y}\mathbb{E}[S(x,y)]=\mathbb{E}[B]
.

The extra waiting time generated by the travelling of the server can now be derived by noting that during the travelling over a distance
\mathrm{d}u
a total of
\lambda\mathbb{E}[K]\alpha\mathrm{d}u
customers are expected to arrive. Again, we only consider customers who arrive in between the server and the tagged customer:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[T(x,y)]

\displaystyle=\lambda\mathbb{E}[K]\alpha{\int_{u=x}^{y}}^{*}{\int_{z=u}^{y}}^{*}\pi(z)\mathbb{E}[S(z,y)]\mathrm{d}z\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle=\alpha d(x,y)+\alpha{\int_{u=x}^{y}}^{*}\bigg(\exp\Big(\rho{\int_{\xi=u}^{y}}^{*}\pi(\xi)\mathrm{d}\xi\Big)-1\bigg)\mathrm{d}u.\qed

Remark 7
.

At the arrival instant of a customer, she might encounter a residual service time,
B^{R}
. The total expected extra waiting time generated by this residual service time,
\mathbb{E}[S^{R}(x,y)]
, satisfies relation (
19
) with
\mathbb{E}[B]
replaced by
\mathbb{E}[B^{R}]=\mathbb{E}[B^{2}]/(2\mathbb{E}[B])
.

With this, we can find an expression for
\mathbb{E}[W_{y}|S=u]
. In combination with Lemma
5
, this results in a fixed point equation for the average spread of customers
f(x,y)
. The proof of Proposition
4
is then finished by proving that this fixed point equation maintains a unique solution.

Proof of Proposition
4
.

We prove this statement in three parts. First we prove that
f(x,y)
satisfies (
17
), then prove that
f\in C_{\pi}
and afterwards prove uniqueness of a solution.

Part (i):
f(x,y)
satisfies (
17
).

We start from (
18
) and use that the waiting time of a customer at location
y
, given that the server is in location
u
, consists of the following four elements: (i) the extra waiting time generated by the travelling of the server from
u
to
y
,
T(u,y)
; (ii) the extra waiting time generated by the residual service time of the customer, possibly in service at the arrival instant. With probability
\rho\pi(u)/(\rho\pi(u)+1-\rho)
the server is working, in which case the extra generated waiting time equals
S^{R}(u,y)
; (iii) the extra waiting time generated by all customers arriving in the same batch that will be served before the tagged customer. Recall that a customer at location
z
generates an extra waiting time of
S(z,y)
; (iv) the work generated by all customers present on the circle in front of
y
at the time of arrival. In expectation, the waiting time of a customer at location
y
, given the server is at
u
, therefore is given by:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[W_{y}|S=u]

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\mathbb{E}\big[T(u,y)\big]}_{(i)}+\underbrace{\frac{\rho\pi(u)}{\rho\pi(u)+1-\rho}\mathbb{E}[S^{R}(u,y)]}_{(ii)}

(21)

\displaystyle+\underbrace{\frac{\mathbb{E}[K(K-1)]}{\mathbb{E}[K]}{\int_{z=u}^{y}}^{*}\pi(z)\mathbb{E}[S(z,y)]\mathrm{d}z}_{(iii)}+\underbrace{{\int_{z=u}^{y}}^{*}\mathbb{E}[S(z,y)]f(z,u)\mathrm{d}z}_{(iv)}.

We now return to (
18
), where we take the derivative w.r.t.

\displaystyle\big[\rho\pi(y)+1-\rho]f(x,y)=\lambda\mathbb{E}[K]\pi(x)\bigg\{

\displaystyle\big[\rho\pi(y)+1-\rho\big]\mathbb{E}[W_{y}|S=y]

(22)

\displaystyle+{\int_{u=x}^{y}}^{*}\big[\rho\pi(u)+1-\rho\big]\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}y}\mathbb{E}[W_{y}|S=u]\mathrm{d}u\bigg\}.

The expectation

\mathbb{E}[W_{y}|S=y]=\frac{\rho\pi(y)}{\rho\pi(y)+1-\rho}\frac{\mathbb{E}[B^{2}]}{2\mathbb{E}[B]}+\frac{1-\rho}{\rho\pi(y)+1-\rho}\cdot 0.

For the second part of (

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}y}\mathbb{E}[W_{y}|S=u]

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\rho\pi(y)\mathbb{E}[T(u,y)]+\alpha}_{(i)}+\underbrace{\rho\pi(y)\frac{\rho\pi(u)}{\rho\pi(u)+1-\rho}\mathbb{E}[S^{R}(u,y)]}_{(ii)}

\displaystyle\quad+\underbrace{\rho\pi(y)\frac{\mathbb{E}[K(K-1)]}{\mathbb{E}[K]}{\int_{z=u}^{y}}^{*}\pi(z)\mathbb{E}[S(z,y)]\mathrm{d}z+\frac{\mathbb{E}[K(K-1)]}{\mathbb{E}[K]}\pi(y)\mathbb{E}[B]}_{(iii)}

Therefore (

\displaystyle\big[\rho\pi(y)+1-\rho]f(x,y)

\displaystyle=\lambda\mathbb{E}[K]\pi(x)\bigg\{\rho\pi(y)\frac{\mathbb{E}[B^{2}]}{2\mathbb{E}[B]}+\rho\pi(y){\int_{u=x}^{y}}^{*}\big[\rho\pi(u)+1-\rho\big]\mathbb{E}[W_{y}|S=u]\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle=\rho\pi(y)\lambda\mathbb{E}[K]\pi(x){\int_{u=x}^{y}}^{*}\big[\rho\pi(u)+1-\rho\big]\mathbb{E}[W_{y}|S=u]\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle\quad+\rho\pi(x){\int_{u=x}^{y}}^{*}\big[\rho\pi(u)+1-\rho\big]f(y,u)\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle\quad+\lambda\mathbb{E}[K]\pi(x)\bigg\{\pi(y)\frac{\rho\mathbb{E}[B^{2}]}{2\mathbb{E}[B]}+\alpha{\int_{u=x}^{y}}^{*}\big[\rho\pi(u)+1-\rho\big]\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle\hskip 85.35826pt+\mathbb{E}[B]\pi(y)\frac{\mathbb{E}[K(K-1)]}{\mathbb{E}[K]}{\int_{u=x}^{y}}^{*}\big[\rho\pi(u)+1-\rho\big]\mathrm{d}u\bigg\}.

The proof that the function

adheres to the integral equation is now concluded by applying the initial equality (

Part (ii):
f
is an element of
C_{\pi}
.

We need to prove that

is indeed bounded by

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[L\big([x,x+\mathrm{d}x]\big)\big]=\int_{u=0}^{1}[\rho\pi(u)+1-\rho]f(x,u)\mathrm{d}u\mathrm{d}x+O(\mathrm{d}x^{2}),

times the expected cycle length (starting from

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[L\big([x,x+\mathrm{d}x]\big)\big]=\int_{u=0}^{1}[\rho\pi(u)+1-\rho]f(x,u)\mathrm{d}u\mathrm{d}x\leq c_{1}\pi(x)\mathrm{d}x.

\displaystyle[\rho\pi(y)+1-\rho]f(x,y)

\displaystyle|g_{0}(x,y)-g_{1}(x,y)|

\displaystyle\leq 4\rho^{3}\pi(y)||g_{0}-g_{1}||_{\infty}{\int_{u=x}^{y}}^{*}\pi(u){\int_{v=x}^{u}}^{*}\pi(v)\mathrm{d}v\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle\quad+4\rho^{3}\pi(y)||g_{0}-g_{1}||_{\infty}{\int_{u=x}^{y}}^{*}\pi(u){\int_{v=y}^{u}}^{*}\pi(v)\mathrm{d}v\mathrm{d}u,

and interchanging the order of the first integrals shows:

\displaystyle|g_{0}(x,y)-g_{1}(x,y)|

\displaystyle\leq 4\rho^{3}\pi(y)||g_{0}-g_{1}||_{\infty}{\int_{v=x}^{y}}^{*}\pi(v){\int_{u=v}^{y}}^{*}\pi(u)\mathrm{d}u\mathrm{d}v

\displaystyle\quad+4\rho^{3}\pi(y)||g_{0}-g_{1}||_{\infty}{\int_{u=x}^{y}}^{*}\pi(u){\int_{v=y}^{u}}^{*}\pi(v)\mathrm{d}v\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle=4\rho^{3}\pi(y)||g_{0}-g_{1}||_{\infty}{\int_{u=x}^{y}}^{*}\pi(u)\mathrm{d}u.

We can now substitute this inequality into (
26
), and repeat this process, to eventually find for any
n>0
:

\displaystyle|g_{0}(x,y)-g_{1}(x,y)|

\displaystyle\leq 4\rho^{n}\pi(y)||g_{0}-g_{1}||_{\infty}{\int_{u=x}^{y}}^{*}\pi(u)\mathrm{d}u.

As we consider bounded solutions
g
, we know that
||g_{0}-g_{1}||_{\infty}
is bounded, and therefore by taking
n\to\infty
the above relation implies
|g_{0}(x,y)-g_{1}(x,y)|=0
, as
\pi(y)<\infty
. Hence, (
25
) attains at most one solution.

Case (ii):
\pi(x)
not strictly positive.
For the case where
\pi(x)
is not strictly positive, we can not directly use the same transformation as before, as it is not defined for
x,y
where
\pi(x)\pi(y)=0
. Note, however, that as
f(x,y)\in C_{\pi}
we have
f(x,y)\leq c\cdot\pi(x)
. Therefore
f(x,y)=0
for
\pi(x)=0
. Turning to (
17
), for
\pi(y)=0
we see that the first integral of (
17
) disappears as both
\pi(y)
and
f(y,u)
are
0
. Therefore, taking the factor
(1-\rho)
to the right-hand side shows:

f(x,y)=\frac{a(x,y)}{1-\rho}\quad\text{if: }\pi(y)=0.

With this, we can now rewrite the integral equation in (
17
) to exclude points at which
\pi(x)\pi(y)=0
:

\displaystyle\big[\rho\pi(y)+1-\rho\big]f(x,y)

\displaystyle=\rho{\int_{u=x}^{y}}^{*}\mathbbm{1}\{\pi(u)>0\}\big[\rho\pi(u)+1-\rho\big]\big[\pi(y)f(x,u)+\pi(x)f(y,u)\big]\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle\quad+\rho{\int_{u=x}^{y}}^{*}\mathbbm{1}\{\pi(u)=0\}\big[\pi(y)a(x,u)+\pi(x)a(y,u)\big]\mathrm{d}u+a(x,y).

To prove that this has a unique solution for
f(x,y)
with
\pi(x)\pi(y)>0
follows the same steps as in the case with
\pi(x)>0
.
∎

4.3
Partial solution

The integral equation, (
17
), consists of two components: a homogeneous part involving
f
(line 1 of (
17
)), and inhomogeneous terms that do not depend on
f
(lines 2 and 3 of (
17
)). The sum of the solutions to the integral equation with each inhomogeneous term alone, also is equal to the solution to the full integral equation. We therefore can also write
f=f_{\alpha}+f_{B^{R}}+f_{K}
. Where
f_{K}
, for instance, is the solution to:

\displaystyle\big[\rho\pi(y)+1-\rho\big]f_{K}(x,y)=

\displaystyle\rho{\int_{u=x}^{y}}^{*}\big[\rho\pi(u)+1-\rho]\cdot\big(\pi(y)f_{K}(x,u)+\pi(x)f_{K}(y,u)\big)\mathrm{d}u

(29)

\displaystyle+\rho\frac{\mathbb{E}[K(K-1)]}{\mathbb{E}[K]}\pi(x)\pi(y){\int_{u=x}^{y}}^{*}\big[\rho\pi(u)+(1-\rho)\big]\mathrm{d}u.

In this section, we find analytical expressions for
f_{\alpha}
and
f_{B^{R}}
. Consequently, we solve the integral equation for the system without batch arrivals.

Proposition 7
.

The solution to the integral equation is given by:

\displaystyle f(x,y)

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda\mathbb{E}[K]\pi(x)\alpha}{1-\rho}{\int_{u=x}^{y}}^{*}\big[\rho\pi(u)+1-\rho]\mathrm{d}u+\frac{\rho\pi(y)}{\rho\pi(y)+1-\rho}\frac{\lambda\mathbb{E}[K]\pi(x)\mathbb{E}[B^{2}]}{2\mathbb{E}[B]}

(30)

\displaystyle+\frac{\rho\pi(y)}{\rho\pi(y)+1-\rho}\frac{\lambda\mathbb{E}[K]\pi(x)\rho\mathbb{E}[B^{2}]}{(1-\rho)\mathbb{E}[B]}{\int_{u=x}^{y}}^{*}\pi(u)\mathrm{d}u+f_{K}(x,y),

where
f_{K}(\cdot)
is the solution to (
29
).

Proof.

Using the splitting of
f
into the different functions, we propose:

\displaystyle f(x,y)

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\frac{\lambda\mathbb{E}[K]\pi(x)\alpha}{1-\rho}{\int_{u=x}^{y}}^{*}\big[\rho\pi(u)+1-\rho]\mathrm{d}u}_{f_{\alpha}}

\displaystyle\quad+\underbrace{\frac{\rho\pi(y)}{\rho\pi(y)+1-\rho}\frac{\lambda\mathbb{E}[K]\pi(x)\mathbb{E}[B^{2}]}{2\mathbb{E}[B]}+\frac{\rho\pi(y)}{\rho\pi(y)+1-\rho}\frac{\lambda\mathbb{E}[K]\pi(x)\rho\mathbb{E}[B^{2}]}{(1-\rho)\mathbb{E}[B]}{\int_{u=x}^{y}}^{*}\pi(u)\mathrm{d}u}_{f_{B^{R}}}+f_{K}(x,y).

We first prove that
f_{\alpha}
solves the integral equation with as inhomogeneous term only the term dependent on
\alpha
. We substitute the solution into the right-hand side of the integral equation:

\displaystyle\rho{\int_{u=x}^{y}}^{*}\big[

\displaystyle\rho\pi(u)+1-\rho\big]\big[\pi(y)f_{\alpha}(x,u)+\pi(x)f_{\alpha}(y,u)]\mathrm{d}u+\lambda\mathbb{E}[K]\pi(x)\alpha{\int_{u=x}^{y}}^{*}\big[\rho\pi(u)+(1-\rho)\big]\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lambda\mathbb{E}[K]\frac{\rho\alpha}{1-\rho}\pi(x)\pi(y){\int_{u=x}^{y}}^{*}\big[\rho\pi(u)+1-\rho\big]{\int_{z=x}^{u}}^{*}\big[\rho\pi(z)+1-\rho\big]\mathrm{d}z\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle\quad+\lambda\mathbb{E}[K]\frac{\rho\alpha}{1-\rho}\pi(x)\pi(y){\int_{u=x}^{y}}^{*}\big[\rho\pi(u)+1-\rho\big]{\int_{z=y}^{u}}^{*}\big[\rho\pi(z)+1-\rho\big]\mathrm{d}z\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle\quad+\lambda\mathbb{E}[K]\pi(x)\alpha{\int_{u=x}^{y}}^{*}\big[\rho\pi(u)+1-\rho\big]\mathrm{d}u.

Note that the following equivalence holds:

\displaystyle{\int_{u=x}^{y}}^{*}

\displaystyle\big[\rho\pi(u)+1-\rho\big]\Big\{{\int_{z=x}^{u}}^{*}\big[\rho\pi(z)+1-\rho\big]\mathrm{d}z+{\int_{z=y}^{u}}^{*}\big[\rho\pi(z)+1-\rho\big]\mathrm{d}z\Big\}\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle={\int_{z=x}^{y}}^{*}\big[\rho\pi(z)+1-\rho\big]{\int_{u=z}^{y}}^{*}\big[\rho\pi(u)+1-\rho\big]\mathrm{d}u\mathrm{d}z

\displaystyle\quad+{\int_{u=x}^{y}}^{*}\big[\rho\pi(u)+1-\rho\big]{\int_{z=y}^{u}}^{*}\big[\rho\pi(z)+1-\rho\big]\mathrm{d}z\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle={\int_{z=x}^{y}}^{*}\big[\rho\pi(z)+1-\rho\big]\mathrm{d}z,

where the last step follows from the fact that the sum of the two inner integrals adds up to the integral over the entire circle which equals 1. Therefore we have:

\displaystyle\rho{\int_{u=x}^{y}}^{*}\big[

\displaystyle\rho\pi(u)+1-\rho\big]\big[\pi(y)f_{\alpha}(x,u)+\pi(x)f_{\alpha}(y,u)]\mathrm{d}u+\lambda\mathbb{E}[K]\pi(x)\alpha{\int_{u=x}^{y}}^{*}\big[\rho\pi(u)+(1-\rho)\big]\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle=\lambda\mathbb{E}[K]\frac{\rho\alpha}{1-\rho}\pi(x)\pi(y){\int_{u=x}^{y}}^{*}\big[\rho\pi(u)+1-\rho\big]\mathrm{d}u+\lambda\mathbb{E}[K]\pi(x)\alpha{\int_{u=x}^{y}}^{*}\big[\rho\pi(u)+1-\rho\big]\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle=\lambda\mathbb{E}[K]\pi(x)\frac{\alpha[\rho\pi(y)+1-\rho]}{1-\rho}{\int_{u=x}^{y}}^{*}\big[\rho\pi(u)+1-\rho\big]\mathrm{d}u.

Hence, the first part of the solution solves the integral equation with the first inhomogeneous term.

We now turn to the integral equation with only the second inhomogeneous term. We prove that
f_{B^{R}}
solves this integral equation. Again we first focus on the right-hand side and substitute the proposed solution:

\displaystyle\rho{\int_{u=x}^{y}}^{*}\big[

\displaystyle\rho\pi(u)+1-\rho\big]\big[\pi(y)f_{B^{R}}(x,u)+\pi(x)f_{B^{R}}(y,u)]\mathrm{d}u+\lambda\mathbb{E}[K]\frac{\rho\mathbb{E}[B^{2}]}{2\mathbb{E}[B]}\pi(x)\pi(y)

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda\mathbb{E}[K]\rho^{3}\mathbb{E}[B^{2}]}{(1-\rho)\mathbb{E}[B]}\pi(x)\pi(y){\int_{u=x}^{y}}^{*}\pi(u){\int_{z=x}^{u}}^{*}\pi(z)\mathrm{d}z\mathrm{d}u+\frac{\lambda\mathbb{E}[K]\rho^{2}\mathbb{E}[B^{2}]}{2\mathbb{E}[B]}\pi(x)\pi(y){\int_{u=x}^{y}}^{*}\pi(u)\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{\lambda\mathbb{E}[K]\rho^{3}\mathbb{E}[B^{2}]}{(1-\rho)\mathbb{E}[B]}\pi(x)\pi(y){\int_{u=x}^{y}}^{*}\pi(u){\int_{z=u}^{y}}^{*}\pi(z)\mathrm{d}z\mathrm{d}u+\frac{\lambda\mathbb{E}[K]\rho^{2}\mathbb{E}[B^{2}]}{2\mathbb{E}[B]}\pi(x)\pi(y){\int_{u=x}^{y}}^{*}\pi(u)\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle\quad+\lambda\mathbb{E}[K]\frac{\rho\mathbb{E}[B^{2}]}{2\mathbb{E}[B]}\pi(x)\pi(y).

Here we can apply the following equivalence relation,. Following a similar reasoning as for the
f_{\alpha}
case, we have:

\displaystyle{\int_{u=x}^{y}}^{*}\pi(u)\Big\{

\displaystyle{\int_{z=x}^{u}}^{*}\pi(z)\mathrm{d}z+{\int_{z=y}^{u}}^{*}\pi(z)\mathrm{d}z\Big\}\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle={\int_{z=x}^{y}}^{*}\pi(z){\int_{u=z}^{y}}^{*}\pi(u)\mathrm{d}u\mathrm{d}z+{\int_{u=x}^{y}}^{*}\pi(u){\int_{z=y}^{u}}^{*}\pi(z)\mathrm{d}z\mathrm{d}u={\int_{u=x}^{y}}^{*}\pi(u)\mathrm{d}u.

Applying this to the previous step, we see the following:

\displaystyle\rho{\int_{u=x}^{y}}^{*}\big[

\displaystyle\rho\pi(u)+1-\rho\big]\big[\pi(y)f_{B^{R}}(x,u)+\pi(x)f_{B^{R}}(y,u)]\mathrm{d}u+\lambda\mathbb{E}[K]\frac{\rho\mathbb{E}[B^{2}]}{2\mathbb{E}[B]}\pi(x)\pi(y)

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda\mathbb{E}[K]\rho^{2}\mathbb{E}[B^{2}]}{(1-\rho)\mathbb{E}[B]}\pi(x)\pi(y){\int_{u=x}^{y}}^{*}\pi(u)\mathrm{d}u+\lambda\mathbb{E}[K]\frac{\rho\mathbb{E}[B^{2}]}{2\mathbb{E}[B]}\pi(x)\pi(y).

Note that this is equal to the left-hand side of the integral equation. Hence, we have solved the integral equation with only the inhomogenous term dependent on
\mathbb{E}[B^{2}]/E[B]
.

The proof now follows from the fact that the sum of the solutions solves the complete integral equation.
∎

Remark 8
.

Under
K\equiv 1
it is evident that
f_{K}(x,y)=0
, and therefore an analytical solution to
f(x,y)
is found.

Example 1

(
\pi(x)=1
)
.

In this case the system simplifies to the case with uniform arrivals. The resulting solution for
f(x,y)
, under
K\equiv 1
, is given by:

\displaystyle f(x,y)

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda\alpha}{1-\rho}d(x,y)+\frac{\rho\lambda\mathbb{E}[B^{2}]}{2\mathbb{E}[B]}+\frac{\rho\lambda\mathbb{E}[B^{2}]}{(1-\rho)\mathbb{E}[B]}d(x,y).

Example 2

(
\pi(x)=nx^{n-1},\,n\geq 1
)
.

Under this case, we find that (
30
), with
K\equiv 1
, reduces to:

\displaystyle f(x,y)

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda\alpha nx^{n-1}}{1-\rho}\Big[(1-\rho)d(x,y)+\rho\big(y^{n}-x^{n}+\mathbbm{1}\{x>y\}\big)\Big]

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{\rho ny^{n-1}}{\rho ny^{n-1}+1-\rho}\frac{nx^{n-1}\lambda\mathbb{E}[B^{2}]}{2\mathbb{E}[B]}+\frac{\rho ny^{n-1}}{\rho ny^{n-1}+1-\rho}\frac{nx^{n-1}\lambda\mathbb{E}[B^{2}]}{(1-\rho)\mathbb{E}[B]}\big(y^{n}-x^{n}+\mathbbm{1}\{x>y\}\big).

For
n=1
this agrees with Example 1 as
d(x,y)=y-x+\mathbbm{1}\{x>y\}
. Additionally, one can verify that this agrees with Proposition 4.6 in
Engels et al. (2023)
.

4.4
Solving the integral equation

Thus far, we have constructed an integral equation for
f(x,y)
and have solved it partly analytically. We have not been able to find an analytical solution to the integral equation (unless
\pi(x)\equiv 1
, the uniform arrival case treated in
Engels et al. (2023)
or
K\equiv 1
). We therefore propose an algorithm to determine the solution to (
17
), and in particular (
29
).

Building on the proof of uniqueness, cf. the third part of the proof of Proposition
18
, we know it is sufficient to instead solve the following simpler integral equation:

g(x,y)=\rho\pi(y){\int_{u=x}^{y}}^{*}\big[g(x,u)+g(y,u)\big]\mathrm{d}u+b(x,y),

(31)

with:

b(x,y)=\frac{\rho\mathbb{E}[K(K-1)]\pi(y)}{\mathbb{E}[K]}{\int_{u=x}^{y}}^{*}\big[\rho\pi(u)+1-\rho\big]\mathrm{d}u.

Note that we have chosen a slightly different representation for the inhomogeneous function (
b(x,y)
) than in the proof of Proposition
4
. The theory in this section will hold for any non-negative function
b(x,y)
. The function
f(x,y)
now follows from the following transformation of
g
:

\displaystyle f(x,y)=\frac{\pi(x)g(x,y)}{\rho\pi(y)+1-\rho}.

Solving the integral equation for
g
can be done by means of successive substitution
(
Saleh Almezel, 2014
, Section 8.3)
. In this algorithm, one starts with an initial guess,
g_{0}
, which is then updated according to the right-hand side of the integral equation in each step. Once the difference between two subsequent guesses is small enough, the iteration is stopped. Formally: let
\delta>0
be the stopping criterion, then the algorithm follows from the following steps:

•

Find
g_{1}
using:

g_{1}(x,y)=\rho\pi(y){\int_{u=x}^{y}}^{*}\big[g_{0}(x,u)+g_{0}(y,u)\big]\mathrm{d}u+b(x,y),

set
n=1
.

•

While
||g_{n}-g_{n-1}||_{\infty}>\delta
do:

–

Find

g_{n+1}=\rho\pi(y){\int_{u=x}^{y}}^{*}\big[g_{n}(x,u)+g_{n}(y,u)\big]\mathrm{d}u+b(x,y),

set
n=n+1
.

•

Set final result:

f_{n}(x,y)=\frac{\pi(x)g_{n}(x,y)}{\rho\pi(y)+1-\rho}.

We use the notation
f^{*},g^{*}
to denote the solutions to their respective fixed point equations. The aim of this section is to prove that the iteration converges and to find the error in the approximation of this function. It can be proven that the algorithm converges, provided that the initial choice for
g
is bounded. Additionally, the error in the final iteration is given in the following Lemma.

Lemma 8
.

Let
\delta>0
an arbitrary stopping criterion. Then the iteration converges, say at iteration
n
, and maintains the following error:

\displaystyle\big|g_{n}(x,y)-g^{*}(x,y)\big|\leq\frac{4\rho\delta\pi(y)}{1-\rho}.

Proof.

This proof is based on the same observation as the uniqueness proof in Propostion
4
. First we consider the convergence. Remark the following:

\displaystyle|g_{i}(x,y)-g_{i-1}(x,y)|

\displaystyle\leq\rho\pi(y)\left|{\int_{u=x}^{y}}^{*}\big[g_{i-1}(x,u)-g_{i-2}(x,u)+g_{i-1}(y,u)-g_{i-2}(y,u)\big]\mathrm{d}u\right|

\displaystyle\leq\rho\pi(y){\int_{u=x}^{y}}^{*}\big[\big|g_{i-1}(x,u)-g_{i-2}(x,u)\big|+\big|g_{i-1}(y,u)-g_{i-2}(y,u)\big|\big]\mathrm{d}u

(32)

\displaystyle\leq 2\rho\pi(y)||g_{i-1}-g_{i-2}||_{\infty}.

(33)

This holds for general
i>1
, therefore we can substitute (
33
) for
i-1
and
(x,u),\,(y,u)
into (
32
):

\displaystyle|g_{i}(x,y)-g_{i-1}(x,y)|\leq 4\rho^{2}\pi(y)||g_{i-2}-g_{i-3}||_{\infty}{\int_{u=x}^{y}}^{*}\pi(u)\mathrm{d}u.

Again, we can substitute this inequality, for
i-2
and
(x,u),\,(y,u)
into (
33
), this gives:

\displaystyle|g_{i}(x,y)-g_{i-1}(x,y)|\leq 4\rho^{3}\pi(y)||g_{i-3}-g_{i-4}||_{\infty}{\int_{u=x}^{y}}^{*}\pi(u)\bigg\{{\int_{v=x}^{u}}^{*}\pi(v)\mathrm{d}v+{\int_{\nu=y}^{u}}^{*}\pi(v)\mathrm{d}v\bigg\}\mathrm{d}u.

Interchanging the order of integration of the first integrals reveals:

\displaystyle|g_{i}(x,y)-g_{i-1}(x,y)|

\displaystyle\leq 4\rho^{3}\pi(y)||g_{i-3}-g_{i-4}||_{\infty}\bigg\{{\int_{v=x}^{y}}^{*}\pi(v){\int_{u=v}^{y}}^{*}\pi(u)\mathrm{d}u\mathrm{d}v+{\int_{u=x}^{y}}^{*}\pi(u){\int_{v=y}^{u}}^{*}\pi(v)\mathrm{d}v\mathrm{d}u\bigg\}

\displaystyle=4\rho^{3}\pi(y)||g_{i-3}-g_{i-4}||_{\infty}{\int_{u=x}^{y}}^{*}\pi(u)\mathrm{d}u.

By repeated application of this, we see that in the
i
-th iteration we have:

\displaystyle|g_{i}(x,y)-g_{i-1}(x,y)|

\displaystyle\leq 4\rho^{i-1}\pi(y)||g_{1}-g_{0}||_{\infty}{\int_{u=x}^{y}}^{*}\pi(u)\mathrm{d}u,

and hence for
i
large enough this will be bounded by
\delta
as
\rho<1
, i.e. the algorithm converges.

For the error bound, we can use this result. Note namely, that the above derivation also implies that for any
i\geq n
:

\displaystyle|g_{i}(x,y)-g_{i-1}(x,y)|

\displaystyle\leq 4\rho^{i-n}\pi(y)||g_{n}-g_{n-1}||_{\infty}{\int_{u=x}^{y}}^{*}\pi(u)\mathrm{d}u.

Secondly, we can rewrite the
i
-th iterate as:

\displaystyle g_{i}(x,y)=g_{0}(x,y)+\sum_{m=1}^{i}\big[g_{m}(x,y)-g_{m-1}(x,y)\big],

with the limit
g^{*}(x,y)=\lim_{i\to\infty}g_{i}(x,y)
. Combining these two observations shows that:

\displaystyle\big|g^{*}(x,y)-g_{n}(x,y)\big|

\displaystyle\leq\bigg|\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\big[g_{m}(x,y)-g_{m-1}(x,y)\big]-\sum_{m=1}^{n}\big[g_{m}(x,y)-g_{m-1}(x,y)\big]\bigg|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{m=n+1}^{\infty}\big|g_{m}(x,y)-g_{m-1}(x,y)\big|\leq\sum_{m=n+1}^{\infty}4\rho^{m-n}\pi(y)||g_{n}-g_{n-1}||_{\infty}{\int_{u=x}^{y}}^{*}\pi(u)\mathrm{d}u.

The final bound now follows from the stopping criterion
||g_{n}-g_{n-1}||_{\infty}\leq\delta
and the trivial bound of
1
for the integral over the density
\pi
.
∎

Corollary 3
.

The error in the final approximation of
f
is bounded as follows:

\displaystyle\big|f_{n}(x,y)-f^{*}(x,y)\big|\leq\frac{\pi(x)\pi(y)4\rho\delta}{(1-\rho)^{2}}.

Proof.

This statement is a direct consequence of the relation:

\displaystyle f(x,y)=\frac{\pi(x)g(x,y)}{\rho\pi(y)+1-\rho},

and thus:

|f_{n}(x,y)-f^{*}(x,y)|\leq\frac{\pi(x)}{1-\rho}|g_{n}(x,y)-g^{*}(x,y)|.\qed

In many applications, analytically determining
g_{n+1}
is (computationally) involved. In Appendix
C
we therefore propose an iterative procedure using left Riemann sums for the approximation of the integrals. In the appendix we further derive bounds on the resulting error from this approximative procedure.

5
Batch sojourn time

In this section, we determine the mean batch sojourn time
\mathbb{E}[S^{B}]
, using the derived expression for
f
. We first condition on the server’s location, batch size and location of the furthest customer:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[S^{B}]=\int_{u=0}^{1}\big[\rho\pi(u)+1-\rho\big]\int_{x=0}^{1}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}p_{k}k\pi(x)

\displaystyle\left({\int_{v=u}^{x}}^{*}\pi(v)\mathrm{d}v\right)^{k-1}

(34)

\displaystyle\cdot\mathbb{E}\big[S^{B}\big|X^{B}=x,K=k,S=u\big]\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}u.

The conditional batch sojourn time is built up from the service time of the furthest customer
\mathbb{E}[B]
plus the following 4 elements: (i) the extra waiting time for the customer at location
x
, generated by the travelling of the server from location
u
,
T(u,x)
; (ii) the extra waiting time generated by the customer possibly in service at the arrival instant. With probability
\rho\pi(u)/(\rho\pi(u)+1-\rho)
the server is working at location
u
, in which case the extra waiting time generated is
S^{R}(u,x)
; (iii) all
k-1
customers who arrive in the same batch generate an extra waiting time. A customer arriving at location
y
generates an extra waiting time of
S(y,x)
, and (iv) the extra waiting time generated by customers already present on the circle. This yields:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[S^{B}\big|

\displaystyle X^{B}=x,K=k,S=u\big]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[B]+\mathbb{E}[T(u,x)]+\frac{\rho\pi(u)}{\rho\pi(u)+1-\rho}\mathbb{E}[S^{R}(u,x)]

(35)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{k-1}{{\int_{y=u}^{x}}^{*}\pi(y)\mathrm{d}y}{\int_{y=u}^{x}}^{*}\pi(y)\mathbb{E}[S(y,x)]\mathrm{d}y+{\int_{y=u}^{x}}^{*}f(y,u)\mathbb{E}[S(y,x)]\mathrm{d}y.

Using this as a basis, we can find an expression for
\mathbb{E}[S^{B}]
, see Theorem
9
. The detailed proof of this statement is given in Appendix
A
.

Theorem 9
.

The mean batch sojourn time,
\mathbb{E}[S^{B}]
, is given by:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[S^{B}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[B]+\frac{\alpha}{1-\rho}-\frac{\alpha}{1-\rho}\int_{u=0}^{1}\big[\rho\pi(u)+1-\rho\big]{\int_{x=0}^{1}}^{*}\big[\rho\pi(x)+1-\rho\big]\tilde{K}\left({\int_{v=u}^{x}}^{*}\pi(v)\mathrm{d}v\right)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}u

(36)

\displaystyle+\frac{\rho(1+\rho)\mathbb{E}[B^{2}]}{2(1-\rho)\mathbb{E}[B]}-\frac{\rho^{2}\mathbb{E}[B^{2}]}{\mathbb{E}[B](1-\rho)}\int_{x=0}^{1}\tilde{K}(x)\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{\lambda}\Big(\exp(\rho)-1\Big)-\mathbb{E}[B]\exp(\rho)+\rho\mathbb{E}[B]\int_{x=0}^{1}\tilde{K}\left(x\right)\exp\left(\rho x\right)\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle+\int_{u=0}^{1}\big[\rho\pi(u)+1-\rho\big]\int_{x=0}^{1}\pi(x)\tilde{K}^{\prime}\left({\int_{v=u}^{x}}^{*}\pi(v)\mathrm{d}v\right){\int_{y=u}^{x}}^{*}f_{K}(y,u)\mathbb{E}[B]\exp\bigg(\rho{\int_{\xi=y}^{x}}^{*}\pi(\xi)\mathrm{d}\xi\bigg)\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}u,

7
Limiting Behaviour

In this section we focus on the limiting behaviour of the continuous polling model. In particular, we consider the light-traffic case of
\lambda\downarrow 0
and the heavy-traffic limit when
\lambda\uparrow 1/(\mathbb{E}[B]\mathbb{E}[K])
(which we interchangeably write as
\rho\uparrow 1
). This limiting behaviour allows for a better understanding of the system behaviour when pushed to either extremes and improves our understanding of (i) how the globally gated and exhaustive policy compare and (ii) the effect of the arrival location distribution of the system performance.

7.1
Globally Gated

The average limiting behaviour of the system under the globally gated policy follows almost directly from the results of Corollary
1
and
2
.

Light traffic

Under light traffic, it is readily verified that:

\displaystyle\lim_{\lambda\downarrow 0}\mathbb{E}[C]=\alpha,\hskip 28.45274pt\lim_{\lambda\downarrow 0}\mathbb{E}[C^{2}]=\alpha^{2},

hence, recalling the results of Section
3
:

\displaystyle\lim_{\lambda\downarrow 0}\mathbb{E}[S^{B}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[B]\mathbb{E}[K]+\frac{3}{2}\alpha-\alpha\int_{x=0}^{1}\tilde{K}(\Pi(x))\mathrm{d}x,

\displaystyle\lim_{\lambda\downarrow 0}\mathbb{E}[D]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[B]\mathbb{E}[K]+\frac{3}{2}\alpha.

Heavy traffic

For the heavy-traffic limit we scale the moments of the cycle time accordingly, and use the following:

\displaystyle\lim_{\rho\uparrow 1}(1-\rho)\mathbb{E}[C]

\displaystyle=\alpha

\displaystyle\lim_{\rho\uparrow 1}(1-\rho)^{2}\mathbb{E}[C^{2}]

\displaystyle=\lim_{\rho\uparrow 1}\frac{1}{1+\rho}\bigg\{(1-\rho)\alpha+2\rho\alpha^{2}+\rho\frac{\mathbb{E}[B^{2}]}{\mathbb{E}[B]}\alpha+\rho\frac{\mathbb{E}[B]\mathbb{E}[K(K-1)]}{\mathbb{E}[K]}\alpha\bigg\}

\displaystyle=\alpha^{2}+\frac{\mathbb{E}[B^{2}]}{2\mathbb{E}[B]}\alpha+\frac{\mathbb{E}[B]\mathbb{E}[K(K-1)]}{2\mathbb{E}[K]}\alpha.

And thus
C^{*}
satisfies:

\displaystyle\lim_{\rho\uparrow 1}(1-\rho)\mathbb{E}[C^{*}]=\lim_{\rho\uparrow 1}\frac{(1-\rho)^{2}\mathbb{E}[C^{2}]}{(1-\rho)\mathbb{E}[C]}=\alpha+\frac{\mathbb{E}[B^{2}]}{2\mathbb{E}[B]}+\frac{\mathbb{E}[B]\mathbb{E}[K(K-1)]}{2\mathbb{E}[K]}.

Consequently:

\displaystyle\lim_{\rho\uparrow 1}(1-\rho)\mathbb{E}[S^{B}]

\displaystyle=\bigg\{\alpha+\frac{\mathbb{E}[B^{2}]}{2\mathbb{E}[B]}+\frac{\mathbb{E}[B]\mathbb{E}[K(K-1)]}{2\mathbb{E}[K]}\bigg\}\cdot\bigg\{\frac{1}{2}+\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{K}{K+1}\right]\bigg\};

\displaystyle\lim_{\rho\uparrow 1}(1-\rho)\mathbb{E}[D]

\displaystyle=\frac{3}{2}\bigg\{\alpha+\frac{\mathbb{E}[B^{2}]}{2\mathbb{E}[B]}+\frac{\mathbb{E}[B]\mathbb{E}[K(K-1)]}{2\mathbb{E}[K]}\bigg\}.

Note that under heavy traffic we have that
\mathbb{E}[S^{B}]
converges to
\mathbb{E}[D]
as
K\to\infty
, regardless of
\pi(\cdot)
(this was not true for the case of arbitrary or light traffic). That is because the contribution of the travel time to the furthest customer in a batch disappears in the heavy-traffic limit.

7.2
Exhaustive

The average spread of customers on the circle, dictated by
f(\cdot)
, is a crucial element in the analysis of the continuous polling model. In the previous sections, we have derived an integral equation for
f(\cdot)
and solved it partly analytically and partly algorithmically. However, the found expression lacks intuition. To develop a better understanding of
f(\cdot)
and in turn,
\mathbb{E}[S^{B}]
, we also consider the light-traffic and heavy-traffic behaviour.

Light traffic

In light traffic,
\lambda\downarrow 0
, the system is mostly empty as
\mathbb{E}[L]=O(\lambda)
(cf. Lemma
3
). Consequently, an arriving batch will most likely arrive to an empty system. Moreover, the expected amount of work arriving during a service, or travelling of the server, is
O(\lambda)
. This implies that
\mathbb{E}[S(x,y)]=\mathbb{E}[B]+O(\lambda)
and
\mathbb{E}[T(x,y)]=d(x,y)+O(\lambda)
. Viz., the average waiting time of a customer is not affected by any future arrivals under light traffic.

In combination with Lemma
5
this allows for the derivation of the light-traffic behaviour.

Proposition 11
.

The continuous polling model under light traffic exhibits the following behaviour:

\displaystyle\lim_{\lambda\downarrow 0}\frac{f(x,y)}{\lambda\mathbb{E}[K]}=\alpha\pi(x)d(x,y)+\mathbb{E}[B]\frac{\mathbb{E}[K(K-1)]}{\mathbb{E}[K]}\pi(x)\pi(y)d(x,y).

(38)

Proof.

We start from Lemma
5
and focus on the right-hand side. As
\mathbb{E}[L]=O(\lambda)
, we infer that the expectation
\mathbb{E}[W_{y}|S=u]
consists of the following factors: the travel time and the service time of the customers arriving in the batch. All other contributions are of the order
O(\lambda^{2})
. Hence:

\displaystyle{\int_{u=x}^{y}}^{*}\big[1+O(\lambda)\big]f(x,u)\mathrm{d}u=\lambda\mathbb{E}[K]\pi(x){\int_{u=x}^{y}}^{*}\bigg\{\alpha d(u,y)+\frac{\mathbb{E}[K(K-1)]}{\mathbb{E}[K]}\int_{z=u}^{y}\pi(z)\mathbb{E}[B]\mathrm{d}z+O(\lambda^{2})\bigg\}\mathrm{d}u.

We now take the derivative w.r.t.
y
on both sides, applying that
\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}y}d(u,y)=1
and that
\lim_{u\uparrow y}d(u,y)=0
. This gives:

\displaystyle\big[1+O(\lambda)\big]f(x,y)=\lambda\mathbb{E}[K]\pi(x){\int_{u=x}^{y}}^{*}\bigg\{\alpha+\frac{\mathbb{E}[B]\mathbb{E}[K(K-1)]}{\mathbb{E}[K]}\pi(y)\bigg\}\mathrm{d}u+O(\lambda^{2}).

The proof is concluded by dividing both sides by
\lambda\mathbb{E}[K]
and taking
\lambda\downarrow 0
.
∎

Remark 10
.

By Proposition
7
it follows that:

\lim_{\lambda\downarrow 0}\frac{f_{K}(x,y)}{\lambda\mathbb{E}[K]}=\mathbb{E}[B]\frac{\mathbb{E}[K(K-1)]}{\mathbb{E}[K]}\pi(x)\pi(y)d(x,y).

Example 3

(Uniform arrival locations)
.

In the case of uniform arrivals (
38
) reduces to:

\lim_{\lambda\downarrow 0}\frac{f(x,y)}{\lambda\mathbb{E}[K]}=\alpha d(x,y)+\mathbb{E}[B]\frac{\mathbb{E}[K(K-1)]}{\mathbb{E}[K]}d(x,y).

Remark that this is also follows from Proposition 4.6 in
Engels et al. (2023)
.

Intuitively, (
38
) can be explained as follows. Recall that all customers at location
x
need to have arrived since the server’s last visit to this location. During this time, the server has travelled a total distance
d(x,y)
. Under light traffic, no customers are served during this time and hence travelling this distance takes a time
\alpha d(x,y)
. Therefore, on average
\lambda\mathbb{E}[K]\pi(x)\cdot\alpha d(x,y)\mathrm{d}x
customers arrived during this time at location
[x,x+\mathrm{d}x)
.

The second term comes from the event that a customer is in service at the time of arrival. This customer arrived alongside other customers in the batch. These so-called siblings might still be in the system at the time of arrival. Observe that, in light traffic, the server is working with probability
\rho\pi(y)/(1-\rho+\rho\pi(y))\approx\rho\pi(y)
, given that the server is at location
y
. On average, the customer in service arrived with a total of
\mathbb{E}[K(K-1)]/\mathbb{E}[K]
other customers in the batch, a fraction
\pi(x)\mathrm{d}x
of which arrived in
[x,x+\mathrm{d}x)
. These customers would only still be in the system if they are being served after the customer at location
y
, which would only happen when the batch arrived while the server was in
(x,y]
. The probability that this happened is
d(x,y)
in light traffic.

Proposition
11
shows that the contribution of
f_{K}
to both the average batch sojourn time (cf. Theorem
9
) and mean time to delivery (cf. Theorem
10
) disappears. This allows for the immediate derivation of the light-traffic behaviour, see Theorem
12
.

Theorem 12
.

The light-traffic behaviour of the mean batch sojourn time satisfies:

\displaystyle\lim_{\lambda\downarrow 0}\mathbb{E}[S^{B}]=\mathbb{E}[K]\mathbb{E}[B]+\alpha-\alpha\int_{u=0}^{1}\int_{x=0}^{1}\tilde{K}\left({\int_{\nu=u}^{x}}^{*}\pi(\nu)\mathrm{d}\nu\right)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}u.

(39)

The light-traffic behaviour of the expected time to delivery satisfies:

\displaystyle\lim_{\lambda\downarrow 0}\mathbb{E}[D]=\mathbb{E}[B]\mathbb{E}[K]+\frac{3\alpha}{2}-\alpha\cdot\int_{u=0}^{1}\tilde{K}\left(\int_{x=u}^{1}\pi(x)\mathrm{d}x\right)\mathrm{d}u.

(40)

Proof.

The first statement follows from the observation that the batch sojourn time, under light traffic, is given by the service time of the batch plus the travel time to the furthest customer in the batch. The latter can be found by conditioning on the server’s location
u
and the location,
x
, of the furthest customer from this point. Here, we use that the server’s location under light traffic is uniformly distributed. Hence:

\displaystyle\lim_{\lambda\downarrow 0}\mathbb{E}[S^{B}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[K]\mathbb{E}[B]+\int_{u=0}^{1}\int_{x=0}^{1}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}kp_{k}\cdot\pi(x)\cdot\left({\int_{\nu=u}^{x}}^{*}\pi(\nu)\mathrm{d}\nu\right)^{k-1}\cdot\alpha d(u,x)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[K]\mathbb{E}[B]+\int_{u=0}^{1}{\int_{x=u^{+}}^{u^{-}}}^{*}\pi(x)\tilde{K}^{\prime}\left({\int_{\nu=u}^{x}}^{*}\pi(\nu)\mathrm{d}\nu\right)\cdot\alpha d(u,x)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}u.

Partial integration of the integral, using
\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}d(u,x)=1
, now gives the light-traffic result of the mean batch sojourn time.

For the time to delivery, we argue that the only factors remaining under light traffic are that of the travel time and service time of the batch itself. The expected travel distance now consists of the expected residual distance to the depot, plus possibly another entire cycle. The extra cycle is only required when the furthest customer in a batch arrives between the depot and the server. As the server is idle with probability
1
in the light-traffic limit, the server attains a uniform location at the arrival instant of a batch. Consequently, we have:

\displaystyle\lim_{\lambda\downarrow 0}\mathbb{E}[D]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[B]\mathbb{E}[K]+\frac{\alpha}{2}+\alpha\cdot\int_{u=0}^{1}\bigg\{1-\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\int_{x=u}^{1}\pi(x)\mathrm{d}x\right)^{K}\right]\bigg\}\mathrm{d}u.

The proof is completed by using the definition of
\tilde{K}
.
∎

Remark that the light-traffic result for
\mathbb{E}[D]
is equal to the light-traffic result for the mean batch sojourn time under the globally gated policy. Since the time to delivery is greater than the batch sojourn time by definition, it immediately follows that the exhaustive service policy is preferred under light traffic.

Intuitively, this makes sense, as the server aimlessly walks around the circle under light traffic waiting for a new batch to arrive. As soon as this happens, the server must first walk to the depot under the globally gated policy, before she can start serving customers.

Remark 11
.

These results allow for the comparison of the light-traffic behaviour under the globally gated (denoted by subscript
GG
) and exhaustive policy (denoted by subscript
EX
):

\displaystyle\lim_{\lambda\downarrow 0}\Big\{\mathbb{E}[S^{B}_{\rm GG}]-\mathbb{E}[S^{B}_{\rm EX}]\Big\}

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha}{2}+\alpha\int_{u=0}^{1}\int_{x=0}^{1}\bigg\{\tilde{K}\left({\int_{\nu=u}^{x}}^{*}\pi(\nu)\mathrm{d}\nu\right)-\tilde{K}\left({\int_{\nu=0}^{x}}^{*}\pi(\nu)\mathrm{d}\nu\right)\bigg\}\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}u,

\displaystyle\lim_{\lambda\downarrow 0}\Big\{\mathbb{E}[D_{\rm GG}]-\mathbb{E}[D_{\rm EX}]\Big\}

\displaystyle=\alpha\cdot\int_{u=0}^{1}\tilde{K}\left(\int_{x=u}^{1}\pi(x)\mathrm{d}x\right)\mathrm{d}u.

Remark 12

We now give a comparison of the heavy-traffic behaviour under the globally gated (denoted by subscript

\displaystyle\lim_{\rho\to 1}(1-\rho)\Big(\mathbb{E}[S^{B}_{\rm GG}]-\mathbb{E}[S^{B}_{\rm EX}]\Big)=\frac{\alpha}{2}+\bigg[\frac{\mathbb{E}[B^{2}]}{2\mathbb{E}[B]}+\frac{\mathbb{E}[B]\mathbb{E}[K(K-1)]}{2\mathbb{E}[K]}\bigg]\cdot\left(\frac{1}{2}-\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{K}{K+1}\right]\right).

Note that the second term is negative whenever

is relatively large, the exhaustive service policy is preferred. However, when the service times are large and

For the time to delivery we have:

\displaystyle\lim_{\rho\to 1}(1-\rho)\Big(\mathbb{E}[D_{\rm GG}]

\displaystyle-\mathbb{E}[D_{\rm EX}]\Big)

\displaystyle=\alpha\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{K+1}\right]+\bigg[\frac{\mathbb{E}[B^{2}]}{2\mathbb{E}[B]}+\frac{\mathbb{E}[B]\mathbb{E}[K(K-1)]}{2\mathbb{E}[K]}\bigg]\cdot\left(\frac{1}{4}-\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{K}{K+1}\right]\right).

Note that the second term is always negative, therefore for
\alpha

The results in Sections

8.1
Warehouse application

We now turn to the warehouse logistic application: milkrun systems. In this system, a picker continuously walks through the entire warehouse picking certain (requested) items that she encounters. Under the globally gated picking policy, the picker receives a new pick list each time she passes the depot. This pick list contains all requested items that have not yet been picked at that time. Under the exhaustive policy, the picker instead picks any requested item she encounters.

The polling representation of this system is as follows: the picker is represented by the server, while the requested items are represented by customers. An order can thus be seen as a batch of customers, immediately highlighting the importance of both the batch sojourn time and time to delivery, as these primarily dictate the performance of a warehouse. The arrival location distribution is determined by the demand of the related products. This particular property is often influenced by the storage policy of the warehouse. Several well-known policies exist. In most cases, storage policies dictate that fast movers (high demand items) need to be located at easier to reach locations, see for instance the detailed analysis in
Dijkstra and Roodbergen (2017)
. However, thus far, the research on the effect of storage policies on the performance of mikrun systems has been limited.

Some of the best known storage policies include random storage and class-based storage. Under the former, the items are randomly located throughout the warehouse, while under class-based storage, items are grouped into different classes (often three classes). Each class is assigned a section of the warehouse in which the corresponding items are stored at random locations (i.e. uniformly at random). Previous research has shown that class-based storage policies can improve the performance of the warehouse, while limiting the practical overhead (e.g. space requirement) involved in applying this policy
(
Hausman et al., 1976
;
Jarvis and McDowell, 1991
)
.

\lambda
per hour. The size of the orders follows a Poisson distribution with an average size of
15

5

600

50/30/20

20/30/50

50/30/20\%

20/30/50

15

We compare the random storage policy, the class-based storage policy with fast movers at the beginning of the route, middle of the route and end of the route. In Figures

Conclusion

In this paper, we have analysed continuous polling models with batch arrivals with a focus on the batch sojourn time and time to delivery. Under the globally gated policy, the current paper finds exact results for the Laplace-Stieltjes transform of both the batch sojourn time and time to delivery, resulting in exact expressions for their mean. The system under exhaustive service is analysed using a mean-value analysis, resulting in an integral equation for the average spread of customers on the circle relative to the server’s location. We find an exact solution to this integral equation when customers arrive alone, and provide an algorithm for the solution for the case with batch arrivals. In turn, exact expressions for the mean batch sojourn time and mean time to delivery are found, albeit still dependent on the output from the algorithm. Moreover, exact light- and heavy-traffic results are given for the average system performance under both the globally gated and the exhaustive service policy. The results in this paper are used to investigate the effect of several model parameters on the performance. Most notably, it is shown that the arrival location distribution of the customers does not have a large influence on the average performance. This is especially true for the case of milkrun order picking systems.

In this paper we made several assumptions, like the symmetry of service times, fixed walking times and independent arrival locations. Extending the results to cases with these assumptions loosened is of practical relevance. For example, in warehousing systems, the products locations in an order often exhibit dependence. Being able to control this dependence, by means of a storage policy, can prove to be useful for the performance of the warehouse. It seems possible to extend the approach in the present paper in that direction. However, this would require a very careful revisit of the model definitions and proofs. It would also be relevant and interesting to extend the analysis to multi-server systems and/or more advanced service policies.

Appendix C
Numerical algorithm for solving the integral equation

We now consider a numerical approach to the algorithm discussed in Section
4.4
. We propose a numerical variant of this algorithm, using left Riemann sums as an approximation for the integration in each iteration step. The interval
[0,1)
is split into
N
equally sized intervals. We then calculate
g_{n+1}
as follows for all
i,j\in\{0,1,...,N-1\}
:

g_{n+1}(i/N,j/N)=\rho\pi(j/N)\sum_{m=i}^{j-1}\Big[g_{n}(i/N,m/N)+g_{n}(j/N,m/N)\Big],

where we use the following convention for the sum:

\displaystyle\sum_{m=i}^{j}a_{m}=\begin{cases}a_{i}+a_{i+1}+...+a_{j}&\text{if: }i\leq j\\
a_{i}+a_{i+1}+...+a_{N-1}+a_{0}+a_{1}+a_{2}+...+a_{j}&\text{if: }i>j.\end{cases}

The approximation of
g
can be extended to non-grid arguments by e.g. interpolation.

Clearly, this routine incorporates approximations errors in each iteration.
For this algorithm to still (i) converge and (ii) give good approximations of
f^{*}
, it is important that these errors do not propagate. This is the case under a mild regularity condition, stating that the Riemann approximation of the integral over
\pi
should be close to
1
.

First we prove (i), following similar steps to the proof of convergence in Lemma
15
.

Lemma 15
.

Assume that the left-Riemann sum approximation of the integral over
\pi
satisfies:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{m=0}^{N-1}\pi(m/N)\leq\frac{1}{\rho},

then the algorithm converges for any arbitrary stopping level
\delta>0
.

Proof.

The proof of the convergence is quite similar to the convergence proof of the analytical procedure, cf. Lemma
15
. Let
g_{n-1},g_{n}
be two arbitrary subsequent iterations of the algorithm, then we have that:

\displaystyle\big|g_{n}(i/N,

\displaystyle j/N)-g_{n-1}(i/N,j/N)\big|

(65)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\rho}{N}\pi(j/N)\sum_{m=i}^{j-1}\big|g_{n-1}(i/N,m/N)-g_{n-2}(i/N,m/N)\big|

\displaystyle+\frac{\rho}{N}\pi(j/N)\sum_{m=i}^{j-1}\big|g_{n-1}(j/N,m/N)-g_{n-2}(j/N,m/N)\big|,

where we can bound the summations by using the supremum norm over the differences:

\displaystyle\big|g_{n}(i/N,j/N)-g_{n-1}(i/N,j/N)\big|

\displaystyle\leq 2\rho\pi(j/N)||g_{n-1}-g_{n-2}||_{\infty}.

(66)

One can now use (
66
) for
g_{n-1}(i/N,m/N)-g_{n-2}(i/N,m/N)
and
g_{n-1}(j/N,m/N)-g_{n-2}(j/N,m/N)
in (
65
). This gives:

\displaystyle\big|g_{n}(i/N,j/N)-g_{n-1}(i/N,j/N)\big|

(67)

\displaystyle\leq{4\rho^{2}}\pi(j/N)||g_{n-2}-g_{n-3}||_{\infty}\cdot\frac{1}{N}\sum_{m=i}^{j-1}\pi(m/N).

We now substitute (
67
) into (
65
), resulting in:

\displaystyle\big|g_{n}(i/N,

\displaystyle j/N)-g_{n-1}(i/N,j/N)\big|

(68)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{4\rho^{3}}{N^{2}}\pi(j/N)||g_{n-3}-g_{n-4}||_{\infty}

\displaystyle\cdot\bigg[\sum_{m=i}^{j-1}\pi(m/N)\sum_{l=i}^{m-1}\pi(l/N)+\sum_{m=i}^{j-1}\pi(m/N)\sum_{l=j}^{m-1}\pi(l/N)\bigg].

This can be further simplified by interchanging the order of summation, noting that:

\displaystyle\sum_{m=i}^{j-1}\pi(m/N)

\displaystyle\sum_{l=i}^{m-1}\pi(l/N)+\sum_{m=i}^{j-1}\pi(m/N)\sum_{l=j}^{m-1}\pi(l/N)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=i}^{j-1}\pi(m/N)\sum_{l=i}^{m}\pi(l/N)+\sum_{m=i}^{j-1}\pi(m/N)\sum_{l=j}^{m-1}\pi(l/N)-\sum_{m=i}^{j-1}\pi(m/N)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{l=i}^{j-1}\pi(l/N)\sum_{m=l}^{j-1}\pi(m/N)+\sum_{m=i}^{j-1}\pi(m/N)\sum_{l=j}^{m-1}\pi(l/N).

Note that the inner sums together add up to the sum over all
\pi(m/N)
values from
m=0
to
N-1
. Therefore:

\displaystyle\sum_{m=i}^{j-1}\pi(m/N)

\displaystyle\sum_{l=i}^{m-1}\pi(l/N)+\sum_{m=i}^{j-1}\pi(m/N)\sum_{l=j}^{m-1}\pi(l/N)=\sum_{m=0}^{N-1}\pi(m/N)\cdot\sum_{l=i}^{j-1}\pi(l/N).

(69)

Turning back to (
68

\displaystyle\big|g_{n}(i/N,

\displaystyle j/N)-g_{n-1}(i/N,j/N)\big|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{4\rho^{3}}{N}\pi(j/N)\sum_{m=0}^{N-1}\pi(m/N)||g_{n-3}-g_{n-4}||_{\infty}\cdot\frac{1}{N}\sum_{l=i}^{j-1}\pi(l/N).

We can repeat these manipulations until we obtain the following for

\displaystyle\big|g_{n}(i/N,

\displaystyle j/N)-g_{n-1}(i/N,j/N)\big|

\displaystyle\leq 4\rho\pi(j/N)\bigg(\rho\cdot\frac{1}{N}\sum_{m=0}^{N-1}\pi(m/N)\bigg)^{n-2}||g_{1}-g_{0}||_{\infty}.

(70)

Due to the regularity condition, we have
\rho/N\cdot\sum_{m=0}^{N-1}\pi(m/N)<1
. Thus, for

sufficiently large,
\big|g_{n}(i/N,j/N)-g_{n-1}(i/N,j/N)\big|\leq\delta
.
∎

Remark 14
.

The regularity condition for this Proposition might appear more strict than it in reality is. Many examples exist in which the condition is not met, however one can overcome this issue by shifting the arrival location distribution by a small amount (
<1/N
) and solve the integral equation under this altered arrival location distribution. One shifts the result by the same amount in the other direction again. Doing so, one can improve the Riemann approximation of
\pi
.

Appendix D

In this appendix, we provide a miscellaneous result that is not directly applicable to the problem at hand, yet it reveals an interesting feature of the integral equation.

Lemma 20
.

Any function
f
satisfying the integral equation (
17
) has the following property:

\displaystyle\int_{u=0}^{1}\int_{x=0}^{1}[

\displaystyle\rho\pi(u)+1-\rho]f(x,u)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda\mathbb{E}[K]}{2(1-\rho)}\Big(\alpha+\lambda\mathbb{E}[K]\mathbb{E}[B^{2}]+\mathbb{E}[B]\cdot\frac{\mathbb{E}[K(K-1)]}{\mathbb{E}[K]}\Big).

Proof.

We first derive some properties of the fixed terms of the integral equation. Let
X_{1},X_{2}
denote two independent random variables with density
\pi(\cdot)
and
U_{1},U_{2}
two independent uniform random variables on
[0,1]
. Additionally, we write

\displaystyle\int_{y=0}^{1}\int_{x=0}^{1}
