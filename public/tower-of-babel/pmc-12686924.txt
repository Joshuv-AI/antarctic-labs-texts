Dataset of social psychology in Japan during COVID-19: A 30-wave panel (January 2020–March 2024)
Authors: Asako Miura, Mei Yamagata
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis data article describes a 30-wave longitudinal online survey of adults in Japan that tracked psychological and social responses to COVID-19 from January 2020 to March 2024. Wave 1 recruited 1248 participants via a nationwide crowdsourcing platform; invitations to wave t + 1 were sent only to those who responded at wave t. To mitigate attrition, an additional cohort of 600 participants was added at Wave 13 under the same procedures. Data collection moved from 2-week to 1-month intervals early on to a regular bimonthly schedule from May 2020. Surveys were self-administered in Japanese. Repeated measures cover interest in COVID-19, risk perception (dread, unknown), infection-prevention behaviors, attitudes toward public-health policies, intergroup attitudes, perceived social disruption, and psychological distress, with time-specific modules added as policy and epidemiological contexts evolved; wave-by-wave availability and exact wording are documented in the codebook and questionnaires (the codebook provides English translations of all items). The dataset is intended for reuse in within-person panel analyses (e.g., fixed-effects, growth-curve, cross-lagged), event-aligned designs around policy milestones, and heterogeneity analyses by age, region, and cohort (baseline vs top-up), and supports replication and pedagogical applications.Keywords: Risk perception, Preventive behavior, Policy support, Intergroup attitudes, Psychological distress, Public trust, Compliance, Longitudinal survey

Background

This dataset was compiled to document how people in Japan perceived and adapted to COVID-19 across the pandemic. Thirty waves (January 2020–March 2024) repeatedly measured risk perception (dread, unknown), infection-prevention behaviors, attitudes toward public-health policies and foreigners/ingroup, perceived social disruption, and psychological distress, with time-specific modules aligned to key policy and epidemiological shifts while preserving continuity of core constructs.

Although several studies have analyzed subsets of these data—early waves on pathogen-avoidance and exclusionary attitudes [2], longitudinal correlates of mask-wearing and hand disinfection [3], political messaging effects through Wave 25 [4], and retrospective bias in recalled perceptions [5]—substantial analytic scope remains. Untapped opportunities include (i) later waves and full-period models of trend breaks; (ii) event-study or interrupted time series (ITS) designs around policy milestones; (iii) dynamic panel or cross-lagged models testing feedback between risk perception and behavior; (iv) measurement invariance/differential item functioning (DIF) checks and harmonized scales across waves; (v) heterogeneity by age, region, socioeconomic status (SES), and compliance profiles, including latent class growth; (vi) attrition-aware inference (e.g., inverse probability weighting (IPW), selection models); and (vii) linkage with external indicators (e.g., case counts, vaccination, mobility, policy indices) for context-dependent effects.

The survey’s measures comprise both established multi-item scales and short indices/single items. All items of K6 psychological distress (6 items; 5-point frequency) [6], Perceived Vulnerability to Disease (PVD; perceived infectability, germ aversion) [7], and Posttraumatic Growth Inventory—Expanded Short Form, Japanese version (PTGI-X-SF-J) [8] were used; Purity Orientation–Pollution Avoidance (POPA) [9] was included selectively(subset of items). Established constructs were otherwise used selectively —e.g., the Purity facet from Moral Foundations [10] and the canonical risk-perception dimensions (dread, unknown)—while many additional items were customized for the Japanese COVID-19 context. To limit respondent burden and discourage panel attrition across 30 waves, numerous domains were intentionally measured with single items or very short indices. Composite scores are not pre-computed in the public release; users may form means/sums as appropriate. Only PVD includes reverse-keyed items; no reverse coding is applied in the released files, and such items are labeled “reverse-keyed item” in the codebook. The dataset spans 30 waves (January 2020–March 2024). Core constructs were repeatedly measured (risk perception—dread/unknown; infection-prevention behaviors; policy attitudes; intergroup attitudes; perceived social disruption; psychological distress), and time-specific modules were added in response to policy/epidemiological changes. Early waves were fielded at 2-week to 1-month intervals, switching to a bimonthly cycle from May 2020. The initial cohort was N = 1248; +600 participants were added at Wave 13 to mitigate attrition.

Questions were administered to all participants unless indicated as time-specific modules; wave-by-wave availability is documented in the codebook. Questionnaires are in the original Japanese; the codebook provides English translations of all items to support international reuse.

Notes on response options and missingness. Several single-choice knowledge items include an explicit “Don’t know” option (e.g., Scientific knowledge S1-S5 coded as 3; Knowledge of COVID-19 reclassification items coded as 9). In analysis, “Don’t know” responses are typically treated as missing; item nonresponse arising from routing is also left missing. Numeric free-entry items may contain extreme values; no systematic outlier removal was performed. Released files contain unprocessed item-level responses; we performed no reverse-coding, re-scaling, imputation, or computation of composite/scale scores. Reverse-keyed items, where present in the PVD scale, are flagged in the codebook and in Table S1; apply reversals before constructing composite scores.

Experimental Design, Materials and Methods

Study design and timeline. We conducted a longitudinal online panel survey in Japan across 30 waves (January 2020–March 2024) to track psychological and social responses to COVID-19. Early waves were fielded at 2-week to 1-month intervals; from May 2020 the cadence shifted to bimonthly. Wave-specific field periods and sample sizes are listed in Table 1, including the interval (days) since the prior wave.

Sampling and recruitment. Participants were adults residing in Japan recruited from a nationwide online research panel via a crowdsourcing platform. Wave 1 invited N = 1248 participants. To mitigate attrition, a top-up cohort of +600 was added at Wave 13, under the same eligibility and procedures. Invitations to wave t + 1 were sent only to participants of wave t (non-participants were not recontacted for the next wave). The panel is non-probability and aimed for broad coverage rather than strict representativeness; consequently, urban and middle-aged residents are somewhat over-represented.

Administration and language. Surveys were self-administered online in Japanese. Invitations were delivered via the panel platform, with participants accessing a secure web questionnaire. Each field period typically remained open for 2–3 days (see Table 1). The questionnaires are provided in the original Japanese; the codebook supplies English translations of all items to support international reuse.

Measures. Core constructs were measured repeatedly: interest in COVID-19; risk perception (two dimensions: dread and unknown); infection-prevention behaviors (e.g., mask wearing, hand hygiene); attitudes toward public-health policies; intergroup attitudes (e.g., impressions of foreigners); perceived social disruption; and psychological distress. In addition, time-specific modules were introduced to reflect policy/epidemiological changes (e.g., vaccination-related perceptions, border/travel restrictions, reclassification of COVID-19). Items used Likert-type scales (e.g., 1–5 or 1–7, depending on item); exact wording, anchors, and wave coverage are documented in the codebook. Response options and missingness. Some single-choice knowledge items included an explicit “Don’t know” option; during analysis these responses are typically treated as missing. Routing produced legitimate skips; these are left missing. Numeric free-entry items may contain extreme values; no systematic outlier removal was applied. Minimal harmonization was undertaken to maintain cross-wave consistency; details appear in the codebook.

Data processing and files. Data are released as an anonymized long-format CSV (all waves combined) and 30 per-wave CSVs, accompanied by a comprehensive codebook (XLSX) and the full questionnaires (QSF/DOCX). The codebook includes variable names/definitions, response formats, and wave availability, and notes any item adjustments across waves.

Limitations

This panel is non-probability and tends to overrepresent urban, middle-aged participants while underrepresenting younger/older and rural residents; generalizability to the national population is limited. Selective attrition across 30 waves may bias longitudinal estimates toward persistent participants, and the Wave-13 top-up cohort (+600) may differ systematically from the original cohort despite identical procedures (comparability checks are advisable). Panel conditioning is possible.

Compared with national census benchmarks for adults in Japan (2020), men and older adults are underrepresented, whereas middle-aged participants are overrepresented in our panel. For example, Wave 1 shows male 34.0 % and mean age 37.0 (SD 9.5); Wave 13 (after the top-up) shows male 38.9 % and mean age 40.2 (9.9) (see Table 2). A compact comparison with census benchmarks for gender, broad age bands, and region is provided in Table S2 to aid downstream weighting or sensitivity checks.

Measures are largely repeated, but some wording/availability changed over time to reflect policy and epidemiological contexts. Users should assess measurement invariance and document any construct discontinuities when pooling waves. Items were self-reported, so social desirability and recall biases may affect estimates; brief or adapted scales may trade depth for feasibility.

Missingness arises from routing, explicit “Don’t know” options (treated as missing in typical analyses), and nonresponse; we retained missing data rather than imputing broadly. Open-ended numerics can include extreme values; no systematic outlier removal was applied. Data processing prioritized cross-wave consistency with minimal recoding; analysts should screen for anomalies and consider sensitivity analyses.

Finally, the field cadence shifted (early 2-week to 1-month waves, then bimonthly), and many exogenous policy events coincided with data collection. Causal claims are outside the scope of these data; when modeling temporal associations, prefer ITS, fixed-effects, growth-curve, or attrition-aware approaches (e.g., IPW, selection models) and report robustness to alternative specifications.
