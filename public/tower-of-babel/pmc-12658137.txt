Effects of β-casein A1/A2 milk and β- and κ-casein genotypes on the performance, body composition, and fecal score of dairy calves
Authors: R. Kappes, V. Schneider, I. J. Giambra, I. Medugorac, D. A. Knob, D. Seichter, G. Lühken, A. Thaler Neto, A. M. Scholz
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The perceived health benefits of β-casein (β-CN) A2 milk for humans have driven increased selection for homozygous β-CN A2 dairy cattle. However, little is known about the effects of feeding milk with different β-CN variants to calves, or about the influence of β-CN genotypes on calf health and performance. We aimed to evaluated the performance, daily fecal score, and body composition of calves that carried β-CN A1A1, A1A2, or A2A2 genotypes and different κ-CN genotypes, fed homozygous β-CN A1 or A2 milk. Every day, during the first 14 days of life, calves were offered 7.5 L/day of either homozygous β-CN A1 or A2 milk (27 females and 25 males per group), and total energy-corrected milk intake was recorded for each calf. On Day 15, the body composition of all 104 calves was assessed using magnetic resonance imaging and dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry. No significant differences were observed in performance, daily fecal scores, or body composition between calves fed β-CN A1 milk and those fed β-CN A2 milk. Compared with β-CN A2A2 calves, calves with the β-CN A1A1 and A1A2 genotypes presented greater final body weights, average daily gains, and feed efficiencies and fewer days with diarrhea. These findings suggest that the exclusive selection of β-CN A2A2 animals should be reconsidered because of the potential impact of this genotype on calf health and growth performance.

Introduction

Milk proteins are the main functional components of milk with substantial nutritional importance and unique physicochemical properties1. Casein (CN) is the primary protein in milk, accounting for approximately 82% of the total protein content; CN proteins are subdivided into four main groups: α-s1, α-s2, β, and κ1,2. The proportions of α-s1-CN, β-CN, κ-CN, and α-s2-CN are 38.1%, 35.7%, 12.8%, and 10.2%, respectively, of the total CN content1,2. β-CN has 15 variants (A1, A2, A3, A4, B, C, D, E, F, H1, H2, I, G, J, K, and L), with A2 and A1 being the most common variants3,4. Similarly, κ-CN has 11 variants (A, B, C, E, F1, F2, G1, G2, H, I, and J), with A and B being the most prevalent variants5.

Many countries have included the B κ-CN allele in animal selection programs because this allele is associated with favorable cheese properties and protein content6–8. For β-CN, the growing interest in so-called “A2 milk”, driven by its purported significance in human health and commercial potential, has led to increased selection of bulls and cows with homozygous β-CN A2 alleles9–11. Studies have previously reported higher milk and protein yields in β-CN A2A2 cows6,12, whereas other studies reported similar milk, fat, and protein yields among cows with the β-CN A1A1, A1A2, and A2A2 genotypes9,10,13. Greater survival rates through the first and second lactation periods were observed in A1A2 cows than in A1A1 cows and did not differ from those in A2A2 cows10. β-CN A1A2 and A2A2 animals reportedly have superior estimated breeding values for production traits, whereas A1A1 animals were superior in terms of fertility, health, and survival11. However, to our knowledge, no study has assessed the performance and health of calves considering the β-CN and κ-CN genotypes. Only two studies have evaluated the effects of feeding homozygous β-CN A1 and A2 milk on calf performance and fecal score14,15. During the first 21 days of life, calves fed homozygous β-CN A1 milk presented greater daily milk intake and feed efficiency and a tendency toward greater average daily gain (ADG) than those fed homozygous β-CN A2 milk14. A lower fecal score and diarrhea prevalence were also observed in calves fed β-CN A1 milk; mainly related to the greater β-casomorphin-7 (BCM7) plasma concentrations14, a peptide with antidiarrheal effects16,17. In a previous study, no differences in growth performance, body composition (visceral adipose tissue [VAT], fat and lean body mass), or days with diarrhea were found between calves that drank β-CN A1 or A2 milk in the first two weeks of life15. The present study is the first to consider both the β-CN milk type and the β-CN calf genotype, either separately or in combination, to better understand their effects on calf performance and health. Therefore, we aimed to compare the effects of feeding homozygous β-CN A1 or A2 milk to calves with the β-CN A1A1, A1A2, or A2A2 genotype and different κ-CN genotypes on body composition, growth performance, fecal score, days with diarrhea, and plasma BCM7 levels during the first two weeks of life.

Materials and methods

All procedures were approved by the “Animal Ethics Committee of the Government of Upper Bavaria” under protocol number ROB-55.2–2532.2. Vet_03-22-20. All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations and are reported in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines18.

Animals

The animal experiment was conducted at the Livestock Center Oberschleissheim, Veterinary Faculty of the Ludwig-Maximilians-University of Munich, from September 2022 to July 2023. In total, we evaluated 104 newborn calves from the German Holstein (GH), German Simmental (GS), and Crossbreed GH x GS (CR) breed groups, all born at the same facility.

Experimental design

This study was an experimental study completely randomized according to the birth order of the calves, with each calf considered an experimental unit. GPower software19 was used for sample size calculation. For a medium Cohen’s effect size20 based on a degrees of freedom numerator of n = 2, a power (1-beta) of 0.8 and an alpha error of 0.05, the sample size was determined to be n = 100. We assumed that the special effect (milk type) would explain 10% of the error variance (100%), resulting in a Cohen’s effect size of f = 0.316.

We tested two feeding groups as follows: (A1) β-CN A1A1 milk diet and (A2) β-CN A2A2 milk diet. The calves were alternately assigned to one of two feeding groups according to their birth order and were not intentionally balanced by sex, breed, or β-CN or κ-CN genotype. Two consecutive calves were assigned to one feeding group, and the next two consecutive calves born were assigned to the other feeding group. The number and distribution of calves in each group according to sex, breed, and β-CN genotype are detailed in Table (1). Each group was composed of 52 calves, 27 females and 25 males. Owing to the randomized selection according to birth order, the number of animals varies across different factors (breed, β-CN genotype, and κ-CN genotype).

Calf management and evaluations

Immediately after birth, the calves were weighed in an electronic scale to determine the birth body weight, received umbilical cord and navel care, were identified with a double set of ear tags, and were housed in disinfected individual boxes with straw bedding. All calves received high-quality colostrum (IgG > 50 mg/ml - measured with a colostrometer) without consideration for the β-CN type ad libitum three times within the first 24 h to meet at least 10% of their body weight. The colostrum offered was from the calf’s own dam; however, when its quality (IgG < 50 mg/mL) or the quantity was insufficient, we offered colostrum from our deep-freeze reserve. All colostrum consumed was measured (offered – leftover). After 48 h, we collected a blood sample to estimate the serum total protein (STP, g/L) concentration with a digital refractometer. All calves had adequate passive immune transfer (STP > 60 g/L), considering a cutoff of 55 g/L21. On the second day of life, after three colostrum feedings, calves were housed in a double-igloo system, paired with another calf from the same feeding group to prevent social isolation stress. The igloos were placed under a roof on a concrete floor with natural ventilation, arranged alternately between treatments and spaced 1 m apart. The calves had ad libitum access to water and hay but no access to calf starter. Each calf was offered 7.5 L of either β-CN A1 or A2 milk per day divided into three feedings at 06:00 (3 L), 11:30 (1.5 L), and 17:30 (3 L). Leftover milk was recorded to calculate both individual daily intake and total milk consumption. Before the beginning of the experiment, all cows in the herd were genotyped for β-CN. The milk offered to the calves was sourced from known homozygous β-CN A1 or A2 cows and was separated using the Lely M4USE system. The milk composition for each selected cow was determined daily using the Lely A3 or A3 Next robotic milking system (Lely Deutschland, Waldstetten, Germany). Owing to differences in milk composition and the inability to balance the nutrient content of the two diets, we calculated the total energy-corrected milk intake (TECMI) using the following equation: (0.327 × MI) + (12.95 × F × MI/100) + (7.65 × P × MI/100)22, where MI = milk intake (kg), F = fat percentage, and P = protein percentage.

The fecal score was recorded daily after the second feeding, by the same researcher. The scoring was performed based on an established methodology23, where a fecal score of 1 = normal, 2 = soft, 3 = runny, and 4 = watery/diarrhea. Fecal scores of 3 and 4 were considered to indicate diarrhea. Finally, we calculated the average fecal score per animal throughout the study. Calves with a fecal score of 4 and a rectal body temperature > 39.5 °C were offered an electrolyte solution and received additional treatment with 140 mg/mL paromomycin sulfate oral solution (Parofor 140, Huvepharma NV) for 3 days.

On the 15th day of life, the calves were deprived of the first feeding and weighed to determine the final body weight, ADG, and feed efficiency (g/L) based on the TECMI. After being weighed, the calves were lightly sedated with 2% xylazine (0.4 mg/kg i.m.) for induction and 10% ketamine (1–2 mg/kg i.v.) for sedation maintenance; while the calves were sedated, their body composition was determined using open magnetic resonance image (MRI) and dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA), and the calves were dehorned.

Image evaluation

All procedures for image evaluation and image analysis were described in detail previously15. Briefly, we used an open low-field MRI system (Siemens Magnetom C!) with a magnetic field strength of 0.35 Tesla (Fig. 1a). MRI analysis was used to assess the VAT; the space between the origin of the last rib and the sacral tuberosity, which was the region of interest, was used as an anatomical reference (Fig. 1b). The calves were placed in a prone position, with their forelimbs and hind limbs extended. The coil was placed in the belly region, and two scanning sequences were performed to encompass the entire region of interest. For image analysis, we used Synedra Personal software (version 16.0.0.3, Synedra Information Technologies GmbH, Innsbruck, Austria) to select the images in sequence that covered the entire region of interest and FDA-approved Able 3D-Doctor software (Release 4.0; Able Software Corp., Lexington, MA) to manually define the fat depots (white mass, Fig. 2b). After all fat depots in all the images were marked (Fig. 2a), a 3D model was created (Fig. 2c), and the total VAT volume in cubic centimeters was calculated using the slice number, slice thickness, and slice distance. After MRI, a GE Lunar iDXA scanner was used for the whole-body scan (Fig. 3a). We used the standard software mode of the scanner, with a radiation dose of 3 µGy and duration of 7.45 min, to assess the VAT (individually adjusting for the same region of interest as for MRI; Fig. 3b), body fat and mass (g), bone mineral content (BMC, g), and bone mineral density (BMD, g/cm²). The calves were placed in a prone position with the forelimbs flexed and the hind limbs extended. After we finished the scan, the calves were dehorned. The study concluded on the 15th day of life, as German regulations mandate that calves must be dehorned by this age, which requires sedation. The same sedation procedure was utilized to carry out the evaluations. Additionally, larger calves would not fit in the MRI equipment, reinforcing the need to complete assessments by this age.

BCM7 and genotype analysis

The calves were sedated approximately 13–14 h after the last feeding, and a blood sample was collected from the jugular vein into EDTA-coated tubes and used to determine the concentration of BCM7. The blood sample was centrifuged at 2000 × g at 4 °C for 25 min. Blood plasma was separated and stored in a − 80 °C freezer until subsequent analyses. The plasma levels of BCM7 were measured using a commercial ELISA Kit (Creative Diagnostics, Shirley, NY, USA) and a Bio-Rad iMark 1.04.02 microplate reader (Bio-Rad, Neuberg, Germany) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions (User’s Manual, Bovine Beta-casomorphin-7, ELISA Kit, DEIA-XYZ57, Version 10 − 09/22, Creative Diagnostics). A second EDTA blood sample was collected and used to genotype the calves for β-CN (CSN2 281099-UMD3.1.1) and κ-CN (CSN3 281728-UMD3.1.1) according to “BovineMine v1.6” (http://bovinemine-v16.rnet.missouri.edu/bovinemine/; see also24. Genomic DNA was extracted from the blood samples via automated separation of superparamagnetic particles (sbeadex™ blood DNA purification kit, LGC Biosearch Technologies and KingFisher™ Flex 96 Purification System, Thermofisher Scientific, Inc.) at the analytical laboratory GeneControl GmbH (Poing, Germany). A customized 50k bovine SNP chip (Bovine Infinium® iSelect-96/XT, Illumina Inc., San Diego, CA) was used for SNP genotyping according to the manufacturer’s protocol. GenomeStudio software version 2020.2.0.5 (Illumina Inc., San Diego, CA) was used for single-nucleotide polymorphism calling of the investigated β-CN and κ-CN variants.

We used the genome-wide SNP genotypes and the method implemented in the R program “snpReady” (https://cran.r-project.org/web/packages/snpReady/vignettes/snpReady-vignette.html) to estimate the unbiased additive genetic relationship (UAR) between all genotyped animals according to previously described procedures25,26. The relationship of the animals to themselves was equal to 1 plus the inbreeding coefficient. Therefore, the diagonal elements of the UAR matrix were used to estimate the relative inbreeding of each animal.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed with SAS 9.4 software (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, North Carolina, USA). The normality of the data distribution was tested via the Kolmogorov‒Smirnov test. ANOVA was performed using the MIXED procedure (The MIXED Procedure, SAS Institute Inc. 2015. SAS/STAT® 14.1 User’s Guide. Cary, NC, USA). Owing to the low frequencies of the κ-CN genotypes AE (n = 3), BE (n = 1), and EE (n = 1), we grouped all genotypes containing the E allele together as the XE κ-CN genotype. Because BCM7 levels and fecal scores did not follow a normal distribution, these variables were log-transformed and are presented as LogBCM7 and LogFS, respectively. Different mixed models were applied for the various traits. For the inbreeding coefficient, the model included milk type, sex, breed, β-CN genotype, κ-CN genotype, and milk type × β-CN genotype as fixed effects. For LogBCM7 and body composition traits, the model included milk type, sex, breed, β-CN genotype, κ-CN genotype, milk type × β-CN genotype, season, and milk type × season as fixed effects and final body weight as a covariate. For colostrum intake, ADG, total milk intake, TECMI, feed efficiency, LogFS, and days with diarrhea, the final model included milk type, sex, breed, β-CN genotype, κ-CN genotype, milk type × β-CN genotype, season, and milk type × season as fixed effects and birth weight as a covariate. In all the models, the igloo group was included as a random effect. The significance level was set at p < 0.05, with a tendency considered at p ≤ 0.10.

Milk type

The milk offered to the calves contained, on average, 3.88 ± 0.07% fat and 3.54 ± 0.02% protein for A1, whereas A2 milk contained 3.87 ± 0.07% fat and 3.55 ± 0.02% protein, not differing between the feeding groups (p = 0.869 and p = 0.686, for fat and protein; respectively). The effects of β-CN milk type on colostrum intake, performance, days with diarrhea, and body composition are presented in Table (2). The colostrum intake was similar between the calves fed A1 and A2 milk (4.21 ± 0.24 vs. 4.58 ± 0.52 L, respectively; p = 0.238). The calves fed A1 milk tended to be heavier at the end of the evaluation period than those fed A2 milk (52.2 ± 0.48 vs. 51.2 ± 0.51 kg, respectively; p = 0.075). Consequently, calves fed A1 milk also tended to have a greater ADG than those fed A2 milk (715 ± 31.8 vs. 645 ± 33.7 g, respectively; p = 0.088). Additionally, feed efficiency tended to be greater in calves fed A1 milk than in those fed A2 milk (97.0 ± 3.7 vs. 88.6 ± 3.9 g/L, respectively; p = 0.079). Furthermore, calves fed A1 milk tended to have fewer days with diarrhea than those fed A2 milk (1.9 ± 0.2 vs. 2.6 ± 0.2 days, respectively; p = 0.093). No significant differences in LogBCM7 concentrations (p = 0.320) or for any body composition trait (p ≥ 0.325) were detected between the two groups.

β-CN and κ-CN genotypes

The effects of the β-CN genotype on performance, days with diarrhea, and body composition are presented in Table (2). In total, 15 calves carried the A1A1 β-CN genotype, 44 carried the A1A2 genotype, and 45 carried the A2A2. Colostrum intake was similar between calves carrying the A1A1 (4.42 ± 0.34 L), A1A2 (4.33 ± 0.29 L), and A2A2 (4.44 ± 0.28 L; p = 0.913). Calves carrying the A1A1 (52.5 ± 0.6 kg) and A1A2 (51.9 ± 0.5 kg) β-CN genotypes were heavier at the end of the evaluation period (15 days) than those carrying the A2A2 genotype (50.7 ± 0.5 kg; p = 0.045). Compared with A2A2 calves (618 ± 37.2 g), A1A1 calves had a greater ADG, gaining more than 100 g/day (719 ± 44.6 g); the difference between A1A1 calves and A1A2 calves (702 ± 37.7 g; p = 0.040) was not significant. Compared with A2A2 calves (85.8 ± 4.3 g), those carrying the A1A1 (96.2 ± 5.2 g) and A1A2 (96.4 ± 4.4 g) β-CN genotypes presented greater feed efficiency, gaining more than 10 g of body weight per liter of milk ingested (p = 0.035). Compared with A2A2 calves (2.8 ± 0.3 days), those with the A1A1 β-CN genotype had nearly one fewer day of diarrhea (1.9 ± 0.3 days), with no significant difference between A1A1 and A1A2 calves (2.0 ± 0.3 days; p = 0.008). The inbreeding coefficients (p = 0.378) and all body composition traits (VAT, fat mass, lean mass, BMC, and BMD; p ≥ 0.188) were similar among the three β-CN genotypes.

The effects of the κ-CN genotype are presented in Table (3). In total, 41 calves carried the AA κ-CN genotype, 44 carried the AB genotype, 14 carried the BB genotype, and 5 carried the XE genotype. No significant differences were detected between calves with different κ-CN genotypes for any of the evaluated variables (p > 0.404; Table 3) – with exception of the colostrum intake (p = 0.032). BB calves consumed significantly more colostrum (+ 1.33 L) than did AB calves.

Seasonal effects

The effects of season are presented in Table (4). Fall-born calves (112.4 ± 45.6 L) had greater TECMI than those born in winter and spring (102 ± 2.2, and 101.4 ± 2.2 L, respectively; p = 0.008). Calves had greater ADGs during fall and spring than during winter (712 ± 45, 707 ± 31, and 620 ± 31 g, respectively; p = 0.049). Spring-born calves were more efficient at converting ingested milk into body weight (100.6 ± 3.6 g/L) than winter-born calves were (87.7 ± 3.7 g/L) but not significantly different from fall-born calves (90.1 ± 5.3 g/L; p = 0.015). Calves born during fall and winter had higher log-transformed fecal scores (LogFS; 3.65 ± 0.08 and 3.63 ± 0.06, respectively; p = 0.044) and a greater number of days with diarrhea (2.4 ± 0.4 and 2.7 ± 0.3 days, respectively; p = 0.033) than calves born in spring (LogFS = 3.46 ± 0.06; days with diarrhea = 1.7 ± 0.3 days). LogBCM7 levels in calves were greater in spring than in winter and fall (2.86 ± 0.1 vs. 2.30 ± 0.1 and 1.35 ± 0.2, respectively; p < 0.001). Calves had greater BMC in spring (1,726 ± 21.5 g) compared to fall (1,626 ± 31.7 g), with no significant difference from winter (1,691 ± 22.1 g; p = 0.018). Additionally, fall-born calves had more VAT than spring- and winter-born calves (426.5 ± 25.9, 317.1 ± 17.6, and 270.4 ± 18.1 cm³, respectively; p < 0.001).

Discussion

In this study, we evaluated the effects of feeding homozygous β-CN A1 and A2 milk, as well as the influence of the β-CN genotype, on calf performance, body composition, incidence of diarrhea, and fecal score. In general, our findings provide evidence that the β-CN genotype affects calf health, as indicated by a lower number of days with diarrhea in A1A1 and A1A2 calves. This improved health status was reflected in better performance outcomes, including greater feeding efficiency and ADG, which resulted in higher body weight at the end of the evaluation period. Similarly, feeding A1 milk tended to reduce the number of days with diarrhea, further enhancing calf performance. These results challenge the current emphasis on selecting homozygous β-CN A2 animals to meet market demands, suggesting that broader consideration of animal performance and welfare is warranted.

The tendency toward greater final body weights in calves fed A1 milk is consistent with the findings of another study14, which also reported a tendency (p = 0.07) for calves fed A1 milk to be heavier at 21 days of age than calves fed A2 milk. In the same study, the authors reported an ADG of 750 g for calves fed A1 milk and 640 g for those fed A2 milk (p = 0.07)14, similar to our findings. The tendency toward greater ADG in calves fed A1 milk may reflect differences in feed efficiency, which tended to be greater for calves fed A1 milk in our study. Calves fed A1 milk required 9.2 L of milk to gain 1 kg of body weight, whereas those fed A2 milk required 10.5 L14, indicating better nutrient utilization in the A1 group. This improved feed efficiency may be partially attributed to the lower incidence of diarrhea. Diarrhea has been previously reported to have a negative effect on ADG because diarrhea leads to increased dehydration and nutrient loss through feces27,28. In our study, calves fed A1 milk tended to have fewer days with diarrhea. Another study also reported significantly lower fecal scores in calves fed A1 milk during the first 21 days of life (1.97 ± 0.18 vs. 2.56 ± 0.17, respectively; p < 0.001)14. The authors also reported that the prevalence of diarrhea (fecal score = 4) was greater in calves fed A2 milk than in those fed A1 milk (10% vs. 6%, respectively)14.

The effect of the type of β-CN milk on the fecal score is attributed primarily to the BCM7 peptide, which is released during digestion, mainly from the A1 variant20. Studies in humans have revealed less consistent stool and other gastrointestinal issues in individuals consuming A1 milk, which was associated with BCM7 plasma concentrations29,30. In calves, the effects of BCM7 appear to be the opposite of those in humans. The only study that evaluated this relationship revealed that calves fed A1 milk had plasma BCM7 levels (55.82 ng/mL) nearly five times greater than those fed A2 milk did (12.73 ng/mL), yet calves in the A1 group presented lower mean fecal scores and a reduced prevalence of diarrhea14. β-casomorphins are believed to have an antidiarrheal effect, stimulating electrolyte and water absorption while prolonging gastrointestinal transit time via opioid-mediated effects on subepithelial receptors16,17. In our study, the similar LogBCM7 concentrations between the groups may be attributed to the timing of blood collection after the last feeding. BCM7 concentrations likely declined over the 13–14 h that elapsed between milk feeding and blood sampling due to metabolism by dipeptidyl-peptidase IV (DPP-IV) and urinary excretion31. Although BCM7 release occurs between 30 min and 6 h after ingestion31, no studies have measured the in vivo half-life of BCMs in blood16.

The similar body composition between the two feeding groups can be explained by the short evaluation period (15 days), which may not have been sufficient to detect changes in body composition. However, extending the evaluation period was not feasible because of the limited spatial capacity of the MRI equipment.

The better performance (final body weight, ADG, and feed efficiency) of calves carrying the β-CN A1A1 and A1A2 genotypes compared with those carrying the A2A2 genotypes can be at least partially attributed to the effects of diarrhea, as previously discussed. However, a potential failure in the transfer of passive immunity may have contributed to the greater prevalence of diarrhea observed in β-CN A2A2 calves. Although all calves had similar colostrum intake and STP concentrations > 60 g/L, STP serves only as an indirect estimate of passive immunity transfer21. It is also possible that a higher inbreeding coefficient among A2A2 calves influenced the occurrence of diarrhea, as presented by a study in which inbred beef calves (inbreeding coefficient of 0.20) had a higher incidence of diarrhea (41%) compared to outbred (inbreeding coefficient not presented) calves (28%)32. However, in our study, the inbreeding coefficient was similar among calves of all three β-CN genotypes. Therefore, the fecal score and diarrhea frequency were not influenced by the inbreeding coefficient, suggesting that other genotype-related factors may be involved. We suggest that the β-CN genotype may be associated with immunological traits or may confer greater robustness and adaptability to challenging environments, thereby enhancing more resistance to disease occurrence for animals carrying β-CN A1 allele. This is partially supported by a previous study that reported greater estimated breeding values for health and survival in β-CN A1A1 cows compared to A1A2 and A2A2 cows11. However, the authors attributed these findings to lower inbreeding depression in A1A1 than A2A2 cows (Median FROH for the whole genome of all cows 7.5 vs. 7.94, respectively; p < 2.2e-16)11. Conversely, another study found that A1A2 cows had greater survival rate during the first and second lactation compared to A1A1 cows, with no significant difference from A2A2 cows10. This is just an attempt to explain possible mechanisms by which the β-CN genotype can influence calf health. However, no definitive explanation has been found regarding the role of β-CN genotype in calf diarrhea and overall health, and further research is need to clarify these associations.

The effects of different κ-CN genotypes were evaluated to determine a possible influence on calf performance. However, no initial hypothesis regarding such an effect affecting calf performance was proposed. Although the colostrum intake differed significantly between AB and BB κ-CN calf genotypes, no significant differences were observed for any of the following performance traits (including birth weight) among the different κ-CN genotypes. Only Polozyuk et al. (2021)33 found that the amount of immunoglobulins in colostrum (mg/l) in κ-CN BB cows was higher compared to the AA genotype and had a greater amount of immunoglobulins by 29.5 mg/l, respectively. In the same study, calves born to cows with the AA κ-CN genotype had lower live weights during the first 30 days of life, 6.34 and 4.78 kg lower than calves from BB and AB cows, respectively, although ADG did not differ significantly. Conversely, Henderson and Marshall (1996)34 observed similar birth weights among the three κ-CN genotypes in crossbred beef calves. However, while ADG and weaning weight were similar between calves from AA and AB cows, calves from BB cows gained less weight overall and were 21 lb lighter at weaning compared with calves from AA cows. Overall, few studies have investigated the effects of κ-CN genotype on colostrum composition or calf performance. Although κ-CN may have some influence on calf development, our results did not reveal any significant impact. Therefore, we suggest that the primary role of κ-CN lies in modulating milk composition, particularly protein content, casein fraction distribution, and technological properties6–8 rather than directly affecting calf performance. These results contrasts with the calf β-CN genotype, which showed an effect on calf performance in terms of growth rate and feed efficiency, although the underlying mechanisms remain unclear.

Total milk intake did not differ among calves born in fall, spring, or winter. However, fall-born calves had greater TECMI than winter- and spring-born calves due to higher milk solids content rather than intake volume. Cold stress increases energy demands for thermoregulation, reducing energy available for growth35, which likely contributed to the lower ADG in winter-born calves. Indeed, calves raised at − 4 °C show 32% higher maintenance energy requirements and reduced daily weight gain compared to those at 10°C36. In our study, the average temperature in winter was 3.2 °C while spring and fall were 14.8 and 10 °C, respectively. In winter, the average temperature was lower than the critical minimum temperature for calves, which varies from 9.5 to 13.4 °C in the first 15 days of age37, negatively affecting the ADG.

The lower occurrence of diarrhea during spring may be associated with the greater LogBCM7 levels than those in winter and fall, as this peptide exhibits antidiarrheal properties16,17. However, whether season directly influences BCM7 concentrations and the potential underlying mechanisms remain unclear. Fall-born calves were expected to have greater BCM7 concentrations, given their greater TECMI. However, no significant interaction was observed between milk type and season for TECMI or LogBCM7, suggesting that other factors may be involved.

The greater amount of VAT in fall-born calves than in those born in spring and winter is likely related to greater TECMI during fall. This response is likely due to the increased availability of ingested energy for storage as fat reserves. A linear increase in body fat percentage in Holstein × Gyr calves at 64 days of age was reported as the amount of milk received was increased, which was directly associated with increased energy intake38. Similarly, in another study, an increase in body fat percentage was observed as the amount of milk replacement powder increased from 1.25% to 2.25% of body weight on a dry matter basis39.

This study has several limitations that should be considered. The evaluation period was short due to the limited spatial capacity of the MRI equipment and was therefore insufficient to detect differences in VAT. Although significant differences in calf performance and diarrhea occurrence were observed among calves with different β-CN genotypes, we did not monitor whether these effects of genotype persisted through the weaning period or influenced adult productive life. Additionally, the study was conducted in a single herd, which limits the generalizability of the β-CN genotype findings. A longer evaluation period and studies conducted in multiple herds are necessary to draw definitive conclusions about the effects of either feeding β-CN milk or the β-CN genotype on calf performance and diarrhea occurrence. Despite these limitations, this study is the first to provide valuable insights into β-CN genotype-based selection regarding calf performance and diarrhea occurrence.

Conclusion

Currently, the selection of bulls and cows for homozygous β-CN A2 milk is becoming more common in several countries because of the growing niche market for so-called “A2 milk,” which is considered “healthier” milk. With this trend, more farmers will be compelled to produce A2A2 milk to meet this new demand. However, before shifting mass directional selection toward the β-CN A2A2 genotype, it is essential to understand the possible effects of this genotype on animal health. The evidence from previous studies and this study prompts an important question: was the genetic alteration from the β-CN A2 allele to the A1 allele part of an evolutionary process to improve calves’ innate resistance to diarrhea-causing factors? The effects of the β-CN genotype and β-CN milk type on calf diarrhea are still not fully understood. We encourage future studies to evaluate these relationships considering the full pre- and postweaning periods and productive life, including measuring immune, hormonal, and metabolic parameters and other health indicators.
