Unveiling Covert Toxicity in Multimodal Data via Toxicity Association Graphs: A Graph-Based Metric and Interpretable Detection Framework
Guanzong Wu, Zihao Zhu, Siwei Lyu, Baoyuan Wu

Abstract
Detecting toxicity in multimodal data remains a significant challenge, as harmful meanings often lurk beneath seemingly benign individual modalities — only emerging when modalities are combined and semantic associations are activated. To address this, we propose a novel detection framework based on Toxicity Association Graphs (TAGs) , which systematically model semantic associations between innocuous entities and latent toxic implications. Leveraging TAGs, we introduce the first quantifiable metric for hidden toxicity, the Multimodal Toxicity Covertness (MTC) , which measures the degree of concealment in toxic multimodal expressions. By integrating our detection framework with the MTC metric, our approach enables precise identification of covert toxicity while preserving full interpretability of the decision-making process, significantly enhancing transparency in multimodal toxicity detection. To validate our method, we construct the Covert Toxic Dataset , the first benchmark specifically designed to capture high-covertness toxic multimodal instances. This dataset encodes nuanced cross-modal associations and serves as a rigorous testbed for evaluating both the proposed metric and detection framework. Extensive experiments demonstrate that our approach outperforms existing methods across both low- and high-covertness toxicity regimes, while delivering clear, interpretable, and auditable detection outcomes. Together, our contributions advance the state of the art in explainable multimodal toxicity detection and lay the foundation for future context-aware and interpretable approaches. 3.1
Task Description

Multimodal toxic content detection aims to identify harmful signals that arise from the interplay between visual and textual modalities. Given an image-text pair
(\bm{x},\bm{t})\in(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{T})
, our goal is to predict two complementary outputs:

•

Toxicity label

y\in\{0,1\}
, indicating whether the input pair conveys harmful content (
y=1
for toxic,
y=0
for non-toxic);

•

Toxicity covertness score

c\in[0,1]
, which quantifies the degree of concealment in the toxic message — higher values of
c
reflect greater subtlety or evasiveness in the expression of toxicity.

We formalize this task as a mapping function
h
:

h:(\bm{x},\bm{t})\rightarrow(y,c),

(1)

where both
y
and
c
are inferred through reasoning over the semantic relationships among entities and concepts present in the multimodal input
(\bm{x},\bm{t})
.
Importantly,
c
is
not
a confidence score for the predicted label
y
; rather, it reflects the perceptual or interpretive difficulty in detecting toxicity. The interplay between
y
and
c
will be further discussed in subsequent sections.

3.2
Toxicity Association Graphs

Toxicity detection often hinges on identifying semantic associations between entities, where the underlying harmful meaning may originate in either the visual or textual modality or emerge from their interaction. To systematically model these associations, we introduce the concept of
Toxicity Association Graphs
(TAGs).

Definition 1

(Toxicity Association Graphs)
.

Given an image-text pair
(\bm{x},\bm{t})
, a toxicity association graph is a tuple of three graphs:

G^{\bm{x},\bm{t}}=(T^{\bm{x}},T^{\bm{t}},B^{\bm{x},\bm{t}}),

(2)

where:

•

Visual association tree

T^{\bm{x}}=(V^{\bm{x}},E^{\bm{x}},P^{\bm{x}})
models the semantic reasoning process associated with the image
\bm{x}
. The node set
V^{\bm{x}}
represents semantic concepts or entities, the edge set
E^{\bm{x}}
encodes the associative links between these nodes, and
P^{\bm{x}}
denotes the transition probabilities along the edges.
The graph adopts a tree-like structure to represent the hierarchical semantic relationships among concepts, where each child node serves as a semantic expansion or specialization of its parent node.

•

Textual association tree

T^{\bm{t}}=(V^{\bm{t}},E^{\bm{t}},P^{\bm{t}})
constructs transition pathways rooted in the text
\bm{t}
. The definitions of
V^{\bm{t}}
,
E^{\bm{t}}
and
P^{\bm{t}}
are analogous to the visual tree.

•

Cross-modality bi-partite graph

B^{\bm{x},\bm{t}}=(V^{\bm{x}}\cup V^{\bm{t}},V^{\bm{x}}\otimes V^{\bm{t}},P^{\bm{x}},P^{\bm{t}})
captures cross-modality semantic associations. Its edge set includes edges connecting all pairs of visual and textual nodes, as
V^{\bm{x}}\otimes V^{\bm{t}}=\{(v^{\bm{x}},v^{\bm{t}})~|~\forall v^{\bm{x}}\in V^{\bm{x}},~\forall v^{\bm{t}}\in V^{\bm{t}}\}
.

TAG provides the structural foundation for reasoning over semantic associations to detect and quantify covert toxicity. By modeling probabilistic transitions among interconnected entities, TAG systematically evaluates multimodal reasoning pathways, revealing how subtle cross-modal interactions convey or amplify hidden toxic intent.

Construction Procedure of
G^{\bm{x},\bm{t}}
.

The key components of
G^{\bm{x},\bm{t}}
are the association trees
T^{\bm{x}}
and
T^{\bm{t}}
. Since
T^{\bm{x}}
and
T^{\bm{t}}
follow the same construction process, we only detail the step-by-step construction for
T^{\bm{x}}
:

•

Step 1: Determining the root node.

The primary entity or concept from the image
\bm{x}
are extracted as the root node
v_{0}^{\bm{x}}
. For example, the previous example of "white powder" and "phone number" yields the root node
white flour
. If multiple entities or concepts are extracted (
e.g
.
, “bag” or “grain texture”), multiple isolated root nodes can be created.

•

Step 2: Determining children nodes and edges.

For any node
v_{l,i}^{\bm{x}}
in layer
l
, we identify up to
k_{max}
(a hyperparameter) closely associated entities or concepts as child nodes
ch_{v_{l,i}^{\bm{x}}}
. These associations include semantic similarity, co-occurrence, appearance similarity, or cultural references (
e.g
.
, linking
white flour
to
cocaine
). Correspondingly, we obtain several edges,
i.e
.
,
e_{l,i,j}^{\bm{x}}:v_{l,i}^{\bm{x}}\rightarrow v_{j}^{\bm{x}}
, with
v_{j}^{\bm{x}}\in ch_{v_{l,i}^{\bm{x}}}
.

•

Step 3: Assigning transition probabilities.

Compute the transition probability of each edge
e_{l,i,j}^{\bm{x}}
,
i.e
.
,
p_{e_{l,i,j}^{\bm{x}}}=p(v_{j}|v_{l,i}^{\bm{x}})
, with the constraint
\sum_{v_{j}^{\bm{x}}\in ch_{v_{l,i}^{\bm{x}}}}p(v_{j}^{\bm{x}}|v_{l,i}^{\bm{x}})=1
. These probabilities quantify the strength of the associations between nodes.

•

Step 4: Stopping conditions.

If
l<l_{max}
(a hyperparameter), proceed to Step 2 for all nodes in layer
l
. Otherwise, terminate and return
T^{\bm{x}}
.

Implementation and Complexity Details.

To ensure tractability and prevent over-expanded associations, we limit the tree depth (
l_{max}
) and the number of child nodes per parent (
k_{max}
). Steps 1–3 leverage pretrained multimodal large language models (MLLMs) with tailored prompts to identify associations efficiently. For space considerations, full implementation and complexity analysis details are provided in
Supplementary Materials
.

3.3
TAG-assisted Covert Toxicity Detection

Built upon Toxicity Association Graphs (TAGs), we propose
Tag-Assisted Covert Toxicity Detection (TA-CTD)
, a novel framework designed to identify covert toxic content in multimodal data. The approach comprises four key steps:

•

Step 1: TAG construction.

For a given multimodal pair
(\bm{x},\bm{t})
, we construct the toxicity association graph
G^{\bm{x},\bm{t}}
using the methodology outlined earlier.

•

Step 2: Toxicity detection.

Given an oracle set of toxic pairs
\mathcal{S}
(user-defined, obtained from a knowledge base, or derived from the implicit knowledge encoded in MLLMs), we systematically check whether any pair in the candidate set
B^{\bm{x},\bm{t}}
matches
\mathcal{S}
. The search proceeds hierarchically from the root to the leaf layer:

–

If a match is found, the process terminates, and
y=1
is assigned.

–

If no match exists after exhaustive checking,
y=0
is assigned.

The procedure inherently identifies the shallowest toxic pair in
B^{\bm{x},\bm{t}}
.

•

Step 3: Multimodal Toxicity Covertness (MTC) score calculation.

For
y=1
, the covertness score
c
is derived as follows:

1.

Compute the joint transition probability for the detected toxic pair
(v_{i}^{\bm{x}},v_{j}^{\bm{t}})
:

\hat{p}_{ij}=p(v_{0}^{\bm{x}}\rightarrow v_{i}^{\bm{x}})\times p(v_{0}^{\bm{t}}\rightarrow v_{j}^{\bm{t}})\in(0,1),

(3)

where:

–

p(v_{0}^{\bm{x}}\rightarrow v_{i}^{\bm{x}})
is the cumulative transition probability from the root
v_{0}^{\bm{x}}
to
v_{i}^{\bm{x}}
in the visual tree
T^{\bm{x}}
.

–

p(v_{0}^{\bm{t}}\rightarrow v_{j}^{\bm{t}})
is analogously defined for the textual tree
T^{\bm{t}}
.

2.

The MTC score is then:

c=1-\hat{p}_{ij}\in(0,1).

(4)

Higher values of
c
indicate greater covertness, reflecting the complexity of the reasoning pathway required to detect toxicity. If
y=0
,
c
is set to
1
.

•

Step 4: Explanation generation.

Using the detected
(y,c)
and the semantic path to
(v_{i}^{\bm{x}},v_{j}^{\bm{t}})
in
G^{\bm{x},\bm{t}}
, a large language model (LLM) generates a natural language explanation. This elucidates the semantic associations underlying the toxicity detection.

Interpretation.

The MTC score
c
captures the complexity of associations required to connect
(\bm{x},\bm{t})
to toxic concepts:

•

When
y=1,c=0
, the input pair
(\bm{x},\bm{t})
is determined as toxic (overt),
i.e
.
, no need for association.

•

When
y=1,0<c<1
, a toxic pair can be associated with
(\bm{x},\bm{t})
through limited associations. Smaller
c
indicates lower covertness (simpler paths), and larger values reflect higher complexity.

•

When
y=0,c=1
, no toxic pairs are linked to
(\bm{x},\bm{t})
within limited associations, signifying maximum covertness.

Advantages.

The TA-CTD approach using
G^{\bm{x},\bm{t}}
provides several advantages over vanilla detection methods that directly rely on a single moderation model:

•

Structured reasoning:
TAG organizes complex multimodal associations into a graph structure, enabling deeper, more targeted semantic reasoning that effectively uncovers hidden toxicity signals.

•

Interpretability and transparency:
The reasoning paths extracted from
G^{\bm{x},\bm{t}}
offer clear explanations for toxicity detection decisions, addressing an important challenge in content moderation systems, where explainable outputs are critical for decision trustworthiness.

•

Enhanced effectiveness:
By combining
G^{\bm{x},\bm{t}}
with a pre-trained moderation model, the approach achieves better performance in detecting subtle, covert toxic implications that traditional methods might overlook.

The Covert Toxic Dataset

4.1
Dataset Construction

Generating high-covertness toxicity is inherently challenging, as it requires not only deep domain expertise but also an understanding of nuanced, context-dependent lexical and visual cues. To overcome this difficulty, we introduce a multi-agent data generation pipeline (Figure
3
) that harnesses the extensive knowledge embedded in large-scale foundation models and the collaborative reasoning of autonomous agents. Built upon GPT-4.1 and GPT-Image-1, the proposed framework follows a generate–evaluate–feedback paradigm, in which specialized agents iteratively refine outputs through self-assessment and contextual critique, ultimately yielding diverse, realistic, and high-fidelity samples of covert toxicity.

Architect Agent.

Serving as the creative director and workflow initiator, this agent transforms abstract user prompts, such as a specified toxicity category or a brief scenario description, into concrete, initial toxic scene concepts. It employs an integrated internet search tool to collect relevant contextual material, followed by structured brainstorming and conceptual synthesis. The resulting output is a well-defined, low-covertness toxic scene description, which serves as the initial input for the subsequent Eraser Agent in the multi-agent generation pipeline.

Eraser Agent.

The primary function of this agent is to enhance the covertness of the toxic scene by systematically removing or replacing explicit toxic cues (
e.g
.
, knives, drugs) with semantically neutral or contextually plausible substitutes (
e.g
.
, specialized tools, flour). This refinement is guided by both the agent’s internal knowledge base and iterative feedback from the Gatekeeper Agent. After modification, the Eraser Agent decomposes the revised scene into two components: a text prompt for image synthesis and its corresponding descriptive caption. It then employs a Text-to-Image (T2I) model to generate the visual counterpart. The resulting candidate image–text pair, along with an erasure reason memo documenting the applied transformations, is subsequently submitted to the Judge Agents for evaluation.

Judge Agents.

This component comprises a panel of diverse evaluators tasked with critically examining each candidate pair from complementary perspectives to ensure balanced and reliable assessment. The panel operates under three distinct evaluation protocols:

•

Blind assessment
— judges view only the image–text pair to assess whether toxicity is perceptible, ensuring that its covertness remains within an optimal range;

•

Reason-informed assessment
— judges are provided with both the image–text pair and the erasure reason memo to evaluate the logical consistency and explainability of the modifications; and

•

Forced reasoning assessment
— judges are informed that the pair contains toxicity but receive no contextual cues, compelling them to infer the underlying harmful meaning and thus testing the implicit discoverability of toxicity.

Collectively, the Judge Agents enable a comprehensive and multi-faceted evaluation to provide detailed judgments on the detectability, coherence, and interpretability of covert toxicity within the generated samples.

Gatekeeper Agent.

Acting as the final decision-maker in the pipeline, this agent consolidates and interprets the feedback collected from the Judge Agents, integrating assessments across the three evaluation protocols to reach a definitive verdict on the candidate pair’s quality. When the Gatekeeper determines that a pair exhibits high-covertness toxicity with appropriate subtlety and coherence, it is approved as a finalized data sample. Conversely, if the candidate fails to meet the desired standards, the Gatekeeper generates targeted, constructive feedback derived from the judges’ evaluations. This feedback is then used to calibrate subsequent iterations, refining the balance between overt and imperceptible toxicity and guiding the system toward producing data that is both realistic and precisely controlled in its level of concealment.

Human Verification.

All candidate pairs approved by the agent framework undergo a final human validation stage to ensure reliability and ethical integrity. Only samples that receive unanimous or clearly substantiated confirmation from annotators are incorporated into the final dataset, ensuring that all retained instances reflect genuine, contextually grounded covert toxicity aligned with the dataset’s design objectives.

5.1
Experimental Settings

Benchmark Datasets.

Beyond our core Covert Toxic Dataset, we evaluated generalization capabilities using: Hateful Memes
17
, Multimodal Implicit Toxicity Dataset
8
and VLSBench
15
as our benchmark datasets, which include both overt and covert toxic dataset.

Baseline Detectors.

Since our TA-CTD method is an input-centric approach that augments the information provided to the detector. Our goal is to show that by structuring external knowledge into an explicit reasoning graph, we can significantly enhance an MLLM’s ability to detect covert toxicity. Thus, we compared two detection methods. For the TA-CTD setting, the detectors receive the TAGs as input with parameters
l_{max}=4~\text{and}~k_{max}=6
. For the Vanilla setting, the input is the original image-text pair directly. We evaluated a suite of multimodal large language models as detectors on our Covert Toxic Dataset. The closed-source models included Claude Sonnet 4
2
, Gemini 2.5 Pro
12
and GPT-4o
30
. The open-source models comprised Qwen 2.5-VL (7b)
3
, Llama 3.2 Vision (11b)
22
and Gemma 3 (12b)
43
. For subsequent experiments on other datasets and ablation studies, we employed Gemma 3 (12b) as the foundational model. Detailed model initialization procedures and hyperparameter configurations are provided in Appendix.

Evaluation Metrics.

The correct prediction means the predicted value
y\in\{0,1\}
for toxicity aligns with the ground truth of the data. Given the characteristics of high-covertness scenarios, we require more meticulous analysis of seemingly normal image-text pairs to ensure that highly covert toxicity is not overlooked. To balance the trade-off between identifying high-covertness toxicity and preserving toxicity-free instances, we select Accuracy and the
F_{2}
-score as our core evaluation metrics. The
F_{2}
-score is defined as

F_{2}=\frac{5\times\text{Precision}\cdot\text{Recall}}{4\times\text{Precision}+\text{Recall}}.

Complexity Analysis

In this section, we analyze the computational complexity of our proposed framework. Our analysis is grounded in the practical implementation where the theoretical "oracle toxic set" is approximated by inference calls to a MLLM. We define
l_{max}
as the maximum graph depth,
k_{max}
as the maximum node capacity of each layer, and
C_{model}
as the cost of a single MLLM inference call, which we treat as the dominant computational unit.

TAG Construction.

The framework begins by constructing two independent TAGs from image and text. The construction of a single TAG involves an initial MLLM call for root nodes followed by
l_{max}-1
layers of expansion, with each layer requiring approximately
k_{max}
MLLM calls. Therefore, the complexity for building one TAG is
O(l_{max}\cdot k_{max}\cdot C_{model})
. Since two TAGs are constructed, the total complexity for this stage remains
O(l_{max}\cdot k_{max}\cdot C_{model})
.

TA-CTD.

The detection process is sequential and features an early-exit mechanism, leading to variable complexity that depends on the detection scenario.

•

Best-Case Complexity
: For content with explicit or shallow toxicity, the toxic association between the two root nodes (representing the initial information from the image and text) is identified in the first check. This requires only a single MLLM call after the TAGs are built. In this scenario, the complexity is dominated by the initial TAG construction, resulting in
O(l_{max}\cdot k_{max}\cdot C_{model})
.

•

Worst-Case Complexity
: This occurs for benign content where no toxic association is found. The algorithm must exhaust all possibilities. After the initial root node check fails, it proceeds to check every possible pair of nodes. The number of nodes in each TAG is approximately
N=l_{max}\cdot k_{max}
. The number of pairwise checks is therefore
N\times N=(l_{max}\cdot k_{max})^{2}
. The complexity of this stage is
O(l_{max}^{2}\cdot k_{max}^{2}\cdot C_{model})
. Thus, the worst-case complexity is
O(l_{max}^{2}\cdot k_{max}^{2}\cdot C_{model})
.

This quadratic worst-case complexity is a critical trade-off of our method. It enables a deep, exhaustive search for covert associations. It is worth noting that in practice, the powerful long-context processing capabilities of MLLMs can be leveraged to batch multiple node pairs for a single inference call. This significantly improves operational efficiency by amortizing the fixed overhead of a single inference and reducing the total number of calls.

MTC Computation.

The Multimodal Toxicity Covertness (MTC) score is computed after a toxic association between a pair of nodes has been successfully identified by the TA-CTD process. The calculation is designed to be efficient by leveraging the results from the detection phase. Specifically, the MTC score primarily involves arithmetic operations on previously gathered data without requiring additional MLLM calls, its intrinsic complexity is negligible, i.e.,
O(1)
. The total cost of obtaining the MTC score is therefore equivalent to the cost of the toxicity detection process that led to the positive finding.

Detailed Specification of Association Types and Generation Mechanisms

To systematically construct the Toxicity Association Graph (TAG), we designed a structured framework for association generation. This framework ensures that the graph construction process is methodical, interpretable, and focused on uncovering deep semantic connections. It is founded on three core pillars: the
Single-Step Association Principle
, a comprehensive set of
Association Types
, and a
Heuristic Framework for Association Generation
designed to stimulate adversarial thinking.

The Single-Step Association Principle.

The construction of a TAG adheres to a core Single-Step Association Principle. This principle mandates that any association extending from a parent node to a child node must be the result of a direct and unmediated semantic or conceptual leap. In other words, the edge connecting two nodes represents a complete step of inference, and the justification for this inference should not itself be instantiated as an intermediate node in the graph. For instance, a direct association from
‘flour‘
to an
‘illicit substance‘
is valid because the underlying reasoning—that both can appear as white powders—constitutes a single conceptual connection based on physical similarity. Conversely, inserting an intermediate node for
‘white powder‘
would violate this principle, as this concept is the justification for the association, not a discrete step in the reasoning chain. This principle ensures that every path in the TAG represents a clear and concise chain of reasoning, effectively preventing semantic ambiguity and controlling the graph’s complexity. Critically, each associative leap is a context-aware process. The model’s association is grounded in the initial root nodes from two modalities and the immediate parent node if one exists, which together provide the background knowledge for the next association. The objective is not to arbitrarily force an association towards a hazardous outcome. Instead, the framework guides the model to generate the most probable and logically sound connection within the given context, using the defined association types as a scaffold for plausible reasoning.

Association Types

We categorize all possible single-step associations into the following nine fundamental types. This classification not only provides a structured guide for the model’s association process but also significantly enhances the interpretability of the TAG paths.

•

Categorical Association.
This association is based on the class, hierarchy, or taxonomy to which a concept belongs, often reflecting an "is-a" relationship. For example, a
‘crowbar‘
, while broadly classified as a
‘tool‘
, can be contextually associated with its potential function as a
‘burglary tool‘
in a risk-aware scenario.

•

Functional Association.
This association is based on the use, function, or intended purpose of a concept. In a typical safety context, while the conventional function of
‘fertilizer‘
is
‘promoting plant growth‘
, its chemical composition allows it to be associated with its potential hazardous use as a
‘component in explosives‘
.

•

Spatial Association.
This association is based on the typical geographical location, venue, or physical proximity of concepts. For instance,
‘matches‘
are often found in a
‘kitchen drawer‘
, a spatial co-location that could lead to a further association with the risk of being
‘accessible to children‘
.

•

Temporal Association.
This association is based on chronological order, the sequence of events, or specific timeframes. For example, the act of
‘drinking alcohol‘
may temporally precede the act of
‘driving a car‘
, thereby forming an event sequence that leads to the risk of a
‘traffic accident‘
.

•

Causal Association.
This association is based on the direct cause-and-effect relationship between concepts. For instance,
‘faulty wiring‘
could be the cause of a
‘short circuit‘
, which in turn could lead to the consequence of a
‘house fire‘
.

•

Emotional Association.
This association is based on the common emotional responses or psychological states that a concept can elicit. For example, prolonged
‘social isolation‘
can be linked to
‘depression‘
, which, in a safety assessment, might be considered a risk factor leading to
‘self-harm‘
.

•

Similarity Association.
This association is established based on shared attributes between concepts, such as physical properties (e.g., shape, color) or abstract characteristics. A direct example is how a
‘toy gun‘
might pose a safety risk due to its high visual similarity to a
‘real gun‘
.

•

Contrast Association.
This association is based on the opposition, contrast, or complementary relationship between concepts. In the security domain, a discussion about
‘security‘
measures naturally invokes an association with its opposite,
‘vulnerability‘
.

•

Cultural Association.
This association is based on societal symbols, idioms, slang, or conventions within a specific cultural context. For example, a
‘red rose‘
culturally symbolizes
‘love‘
, but in a negative context, this symbolism could be distorted and linked to suspicious behaviors such as
‘emotional blackmail‘
.

Heuristic Framework for Association Generation

To guide the model to think like a security expert and proactively uncover deeply concealed or disguised risks, we introduce a heuristic framework. This framework does not introduce new association types but rather serves as a set of "cognitive lenses" that direct the model on how to creatively apply the foundational association types in specific risk scenarios.

•

Physical & Chemical Risk Steganography.
This heuristic focuses on uncovering risks concealed by physical form or functional purpose. For example, using the lens of Form Generalization (applying Similarity Association), the model can identify the morphological similarity between a seemingly harmless
‘decorative crystal‘
and
‘crystalline drugs‘
.

•

Biological Risk Steganography.
This heuristic is concerned with risks disguised by species or pseudo-scientific terminology. Through the lens of Bio-feature Generalization (applying Similarity Association), the model can recognize that a plant labeled as an
‘ornamental seedling‘
actually exhibits the characteristic leaf shape of a
‘narcotic plant seedling‘
.

•

Semantic & Coded Language Steganography.
This heuristic focuses on identifying metaphors, slang, or codes used to obscure true intent. For instance, in a specific context, the seemingly professional term
‘lab reagent‘
might be interpreted as a coded phrase for an
‘illicit chemical precursor‘
through
Cultural Association
.
