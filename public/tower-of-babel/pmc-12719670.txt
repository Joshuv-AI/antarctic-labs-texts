Layered plaque in patients with chronic coronary syndrome
Authors: Takafumi Mito, Daisuke Kinoshita, Yoichiro Otaki, Jun Goto, Seisei Ra, Hiroe Ono, Taku Shikama, Shingo Tachibana, Shigehiko Kato, Tetsu Watanabe, Ik-Kyung Jang, Masafumi Watanabe
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPlaque disruption exposes thrombogenic substrates and triggers local thrombosis. When a fresh thrombus forms, endogenous antithrombotic mechanisms activate. The balance between these two pro- and antithrombotic mechanisms determines the outcome of plaque disruption. When thrombogenic stimuli outweigh the antithrombotic mechanisms, an occlusive thrombus forms, leading to acute coronary syndromes. When antithrombotic mechanisms dominate, a non-occlusive thrombus forms, contributing to the rapid progression of atherosclerosis. A layered or healed plaque can be identified using optical coherence tomography (OCT).PurposeThe study aimed to investigate the association between atherothrombotic biomarkers and layered plaque at culprit lesions.MethodsWe analyzed 119 lesions in patients with chronic coronary syndrome who underwent OCT prior to percutaneous coronary intervention. Four groups of biomarkers were studied: antithrombotic, atherogenic, prothrombotic, and inflammatory. The layer index, defined as the mean layer arc multiplied by the layer length, was assessed using OCT.ResultsPatients were divided into tertiles based on each of the four groups. Among the four groups, only the antithrombotic biomarkers were significantly associated with the layer index (low vs. moderate vs. high: 0 vs. 517 vs. 719, p for trend = 0.037). This positive correlation remained significant after adjusting for confounders. In contrast, no association was found between the layer index and atherogenic, prothrombotic, or inflammatory biomarkers.ConclusionsThe antithrombotic mechanism appears to play a key role in layered plaque formation.Keywords: Layered plaque, Fibrinolysis, Optical coherence tomography, Thrombogenicity, Chronic coronary syndrome

Introduction

The primary mechanism of plaque progression involves repetitive disruption by rupture, erosion, or calcified nodule, followed by subsequent healing. Most plaque disruptions remain silent, resulting in new layer formation after thrombus organization, which contributes to atherosclerosis progression. Once necrotic core components are exposed to blood, they trigger platelet activation and thrombus formation. Inflammatory and smooth muscle cells subsequently promote thrombus organization and fibrotic healing [1]. Repetitive cycles of subclinical thrombus and subsequent repair lead to plaque progression [2]. Evidence of prior plaque disruption has been observed in over 70 % of the cases in an autopsy study and in 25–70 % of patients with coronary artery disease (CAD). The outcome of plaque disruption depends on multiple factors: local and systemic thrombogenicity, endogenous antithrombotic mechanisms, and lumen size. Local flow dynamics, including turbulence and vascular inflammation, are related to these factors. Previous studies have reported that increased thrombotic activity is linked to adverse cardiovascular outcomes, including higher cardiovascular mortality, even among patients with chronic coronary syndrome (CCS) [3]. Another study found that patients with CCS have denser fibrin clot structures, reduced clot permeability, and decreased susceptibility to fibrinolysis, which correlate with worse clinical outcomes [4]. Additionally, reduced plasminogen activation correlates with CAD severity [5], whereas elevated fibrinolytic activity helps preserve vessel patency in atherosclerotic arteries [6]. Plasmin, a major fibrinolytic protease formed by conversion of plasminogen, digests fibrin and promotes vascular repair [7]. Impaired fibrinolysis leads to a dense fibrin network and prolonged clot lysis time, thereby increasing the risk of acute coronary syndrome (ACS) onset [8], [9]. Thus, thrombogenicity is considered a key factor in ACS onset and plaque progression in CCS. However, the precise mechanisms of underlying layered plaque formation remain unclear. Circulating markers of thrombosis and fibrinolysis mirror local intraplaque processes [10], and the dynamic interrelationship among circulating biomarkers provides insight into plaque progression [11]. Since atherosclerosis is a complex process involving thrombogenicity, inflammation, and atherogenic stimuli, we aimed to investigate how these mechanisms collectively influence plaque progression and the formation of layered plaque.

Methods

Study population

This retrospective cross-sectional study included 161 consecutive patients with CCS who underwent optical coherence tomography (OCT) imaging prior to percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) at our institution between January 2018 and May 2024. Patients with in-stent restenosis (n = 16), ACS (n = 21), or suboptimal image quality (n = 5) were excluded. Finally, 119 de novo coronary artery lesions from 119 patients were included (Supplemental Fig. 1). All participants provided written informed consent prior to the start of the study.

Factor analysis

A comprehensive panel of biomarkers was assessed using exploratory factor analysis to evaluate complex interrelationships among biomarkers. DeFilippis et al. conducted a factor analysis to identify distinct clusters of atherothrombotic biomarkers and subsequently evaluated associations between the derived factors and the incidence of atherosclerotic cardiovascular events [12]. In this study, 21 biomarkers related to plaque morphology were included in the factor analysis (Supplemental Table 1). All biomarker values were log-transformed using log(analyte + 1) normalization. To determine the number of factors, a scree plot was generated, revealing an elbow point at five. Based on this, four factors were extracted (Supplemental Fig. 2). Maximum-likelihood exploratory factor analysis with varimax rotation was performed. Eight biomarkers with communalities >0.4 were retained: plasminogen, antithrombin III (AT3), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), malondialdehyde-modified LDL (MDA-LDL), D-dimer, fibrin degradation products (FDP), fibrinogen, and high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP). Their distribution is summarized in Supplemental Table 2, and interrelationships are illustrated in Supplemental Fig. 3. Factor loadings (Supplemental Fig. 4) revealed four distinct factors: Factor 1 (“antithrombotic”)–plasminogen and AT3; Factor 2 (“atherogenic”)–LDL-C and MDA-LDL; Factor 3 (“prothrombotic”)–D-dimer and FDP; Factor 4 (“inflammatory”)–fibrinogen and hs-CRP.

OCT image acquisition and analysis

All OCT datasets were analyzed offline by three independent investigators blinded to clinical information, using a dedicated review workstation (LightLab Imaging Inc.). Both qualitative and quantitative assessments of plaque morphology were performed as previously described [13]. A layered plaque was defined as a lesion showing one or more distinct layers with differing optical densities, clearly demarcated from the underlying plaque structure. The length of the layered component was measured longitudinally. The layer index, representing the volumetric extent of the layered structure, was calculated as the mean layer arc multiplied by the layer length [14]. Thin-cap fibroatheroma (TCFA) was defined as a plaque with a maximal lipid arc >90° and thinnest fibrous cap thickness <65 μm [13]. Macrophage accumulation was defined as the presence of highly backscattering focal granular regions [13]. The degree of macrophage accumulation was graded as follows: grade 0, no macrophage; grade 1, localized macrophage accumulation; grade 2, clustered accumulation <1 quadrant; grade 3, clustered accumulation ≥1 quadrant but <3 quadrants; and grade 4, clustered accumulation ≥3 quadrants. Grading was performed every 1 mm along the entire target plaque, and the summation of 0–4 grades was calculated [15]. Lipid was defined as a signal-poor region with a diffuse border, and the degree of lipid arc was measured at 1 mm intervals [13]. Lipid length was obtained on the longitudinal view, and the lipid index was calculated as the product of the mean lipid arc and lipid length [16]. Lipid-rich plaque was defined as a plaque with a maximal lipid arc greater than 90° [13]. Microvessels were defined as the presence of signal-poor structures with vesicular or tubular shapes [13]. Cholesterol crystals were identified as thin and linear regions of high signal intensity with high backscattering within a plaque [13]. Calcification was defined as a signal-poor or heterogeneous region with a sharply delineated border [13].

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables with a normal distribution were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), while the median (interquartile range [25th–75th percentile]) was used for non-normally distributed variables. Normally distributed variables were compared using the Student t-test, whereas non-normally distributed variables were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical data were expressed as absolute frequencies and percentages and compared using the chi-square test or Fisher's exact test, as appropriate. Tukey's honest significant difference test was used for multiple comparisons. The p for trend was calculated using the Jonckheere-Terpstra test for continuous variables and the Cochran-Armitage test for categorical variables. Multivariable analyses employed generalized linear models with identity links for continuous dependent variables. Confounders were selected based on p < 0.10 in univariable analysis, along with prespecified confounders including age, sex, diabetes mellitus, chronic kidney disease, and smoking status (current or not). Since the four factors made by the factor analysis were exploratory, Bonferroni's correction was applied; thus, a p-value < 0.0125 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using R software version 4.4.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

Baseline characteristics

Baseline characteristics are summarized in Table 1. The median age was 73 years, and 20.2 % of patients were women. The prevalence of hypertension, dyslipidemia, and diabetes mellitus was 75.6 %, 66.4 %, and 17.1 %, respectively. Patients undergoing hemodialysis accounted for 11.8 % of the cohort. The median LDL-C level was 79 mg/dL, and the median hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) was 6.2 %. Among the 119 patients, 89.9 % were receiving statin therapy, whereas only 5.9 % were prescribed direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs).

Four factors and OCT features

We investigated associations between four factors, including antithrombotic, atherogenic, prothrombotic, and inflammatory factors, with plaque morphology. Patients were divided into tertiles based on each factor. Only the antithrombotic factor showed a significant association with layered plaque (Fig. 1). Similarly, higher levels of the antithrombotic factor were associated with the layer index (Fig. 2). Other factors showed no associations with layered plaque or the layer index. Other OCT findings are summarized in Table 2, and Supplemental Table 3–5. No associations were observed between the antithrombotic factor and other quantitative OCT features, including calcium index, macrophage grade, and lipid index. Regarding other factors, lipid-rich plaque and microvessels were more prevalent among patients with higher atherogenic factor levels, and macrophage grade was elevated in this group (Supplemental Table 3). Neither prothrombotic nor inflammatory factors were associated with OCT features (Supplemental Tables 4 and 5).

Multivariable analyses

Multivariable analyses were conducted to identify which factors were associated with the layer index, calcium index, macrophage grade, and lipid index among the four groups. In univariable analysis, only the antithrombotic factor was positively correlated with the layer index (Table 3), while no significant correlations were observed for other variables. The association remained significant after adjustment for potential confounders. In contrast, no variables demonstrated a significant association with the calcium index in either univariable or multivariable analyses. Only the prothrombotic factor was associated with the calcium index in the univariable analysis; however, this association was not significant in the multivariable model (Supplemental Table 6). Regarding inflammatory and lipid-related plaque characteristics, the atherogenic factor showed significant positive correlations with lipid index (Supplemental Tables 7 and 8). These associations remained significant after adjustment for confounders. Sensitivity analyses were conducted in patients without atrial fibrillation or undergoing hemodialysis. The association between the antithrombotic factor and layered plaque formation was consistent (Supplemental Tables 9 and 10).

Discussion

The current study demonstrated: 1) among antithrombotic, atherogenic, prothrombotic, and inflammatory biomarkers, only the antithrombotic biomarkers were associated with layered plaque at target lesions; 2) the atherogenic factor was associated with lipid accumulation and macrophage infiltration, while the associations of prothrombotic and inflammatory biomarkers with plaque morphology were weak.

Plaque healing and layered plaque

The clinical significance of layered plaques remains incompletely understood. The presence of layered plaques on OCT is associated with long-term clinical stability [17], [18], [19], whereas other studies have demonstrated that layered plaque is associated with unplanned revascularization after PCI [20,21]. Notably, these studies consistently demonstrated that layered plaques were not associated with increased risk for ACS. Notably, these studies consistently have demonstrated that layered plaques are not associated with an increased risk of ACS. Araki et al. also showed that layered plaques can be a predictor of rapid plaque progression, but the majority of patients with plaque progression were asymptomatic [22]. Most plaque disruptions are silent and do not result in symptomatic coronary occlusion, particularly when the plaque burden or thrombus volume is small [23]. Plaque burden is a well-established marker of overall atherosclerotic disease severity and an independent predictor of future cardiovascular death or ACS [24]. The progression of atherosclerosis and increased plaque vulnerability underlie the key pathology of plaque disruption, especially plaque rupture and otherwise asymptomatic thrombus formation, followed by the healing [25]. Although the prevalence of layered plaque in patients with CCS is comparable between men and women, more interrupted layered plaques are observed in men, suggesting that plaque rupture, rather than plaque erosion, is the predominant mechanism underlying layered plaque in men compared with women [26]. The balance between thrombotic stimuli and fibrinolytic mechanisms is also important for the development of occlusive coronary thrombosis after plaque disruption [12,17]. In the presence of robust antithrombotic conditions, a plaque disruption may not lead to occlusive thrombosis and can undergo healing without clinical manifestation. During this process, the thrombus is organized and subsequently replaced by smooth muscle cells and collagen fibers, leading to the formation of layered plaques [27]. Adequate plaque healing shifts patients with CAD to a more stable phenotype, with the creation of a new layer but with increased plaque burden [28]. Layered plaque formation has been shown to be more strongly associated with greater plaque volume in CCS than in ACS, indicating the role of antithrombotic mechanisms on plaque progression [14]. However, evidence regarding the impact of antithrombotic mechanisms on plaque morphology remains limited. The current study demonstrated that patients with higher levels of antithrombotic biomarkers exhibited more layered plaques than those with higher levels of atherogenic, prothrombotic, or inflammatory factors, suggesting that stronger antithrombotic mechanisms may mitigate extensive thrombus formation or recurrent coronary thrombosis while contributing to luminal narrowing.

Importantly, plaque healing differs from plaque stabilization; the former contributes to stepwise plaque progression, whereas the latter involves gradual progression through lipid accumulation or calcium deposits [17]. Infiltration of inflammatory cells into plaques is considered an important step in both plaque progression and vulnerability that leads to ACS [29,30]. In line with these findings, the current study found an association only of the atherogenic factor with lipid accumulation and macrophage infiltration.

Layered plaque and anti-thrombotic factor

Comprehensive biomarker analysis helped to elucidate the role of atherothrombotic biomarkers, considering the complex interrelationships among these factors. The current study demonstrated that the prothrombotic factor was not significantly associated with plaque morphology, whereas the antithrombotic factor was involved in the formation of layered plaque. These findings are consistent with prior studies suggesting that, under conditions of adequate fibrinolytic capacity, subclinical thrombi can be resolved and remodeled into layered plaque, thereby avoiding the development of ACS [18]. The serum levels of plasminogen and AT-3 are reportedly lower in patients with ACS than in those with CCS [31,32]. In particular, plasminogen levels are stable throughout the 3-month follow-up period [31]. Thus, the finding that higher levels of the antithrombotic factor were associated with a higher layer index indicates the preventive role of the antithrombotic mechanism in developing occlusive thrombosis.

The observation that layered plaque formation is linked to antithrombotic mechanisms rather than classic atherogenic processes suggests that subclinical thrombotic events and healing responses contribute significantly to plaque burden. This underscores the importance of longitudinal imaging assessments to track plaque changes over time. The COMPASS trial demonstrated that the combination of low-dose rivaroxaban with aspirin significantly reduced cardiovascular mortality in patients with advanced atherosclerotic disease, particularly those with CCS [33], [34]. The effects of antithrombotic intervention, such as low-dose rivaroxaban, on the attenuation of plaque progression remain to be fully elucidated and warrant further investigation.

Limitation

This was a cross-sectional, retrospective, single-center study and included only the Japanese population. The generalizability of these results to populations with different ethnic backgrounds may be limited. It is important to note that the associations identified in this study are correlative in nature and do not imply causality; therefore, the findings should be interpreted with appropriate caution. Second, the number of factors retained depends on criteria derived from scree plots, which can introduce subjectivity. Different extraction methods (e.g., principal component analysis vs. maximum likelihood) may yield varying results. Third, factor analysis assumes that underlying relationships between variables are linear, which may not always hold, especially in complex biological systems such as atherosclerosis and thrombosis. Fourth, while variables are based on shared variance in the factor analysis, they do not account for causal relationships or external influences that may shape plaque formation and thrombogenicity. Fifth, the plasminogen level used in this study was expressed as activity (%) rather than absolute concentration, which may affect comparison with previous reports. Finally, since this study did not include follow-up data, it was not possible to evaluate the prognostic impact of antithrombotic factors. These limitations highlight directions for future research.

Conclusion

Anti-thrombotic factors may be more important for layered plaque formation than atherogenic, prothrombotic, or inflammatory factors. Emphasizing antithrombotic rather than prothrombotic or inflammatory factors as key modulators of layered plaque formation has been underexplored. These findings suggest that plaque healing is not passive but is shaped by antithrombotic mechanisms, which may inform novel preventive strategies for CAD.
