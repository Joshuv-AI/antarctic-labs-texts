Which types of research and knowledge translation are most likely to lead to public health-informed policy responses to unhealthy commodities? A UK-based cross-sectional survey and focus group-based study
Authors: Alice Fabbri, Britta Katharina Matthes, Katherine E Smith
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionProducts of unhealthy commodity industries (UCIs) are major drivers of non-communicable diseases. The role of evidence in supporting regulation of these products remains underexplored. This study examined how different types of research and knowledge translation activities influenced policy change across unhealthy commodities.MethodsA study was conducted combining an online survey and three focus groups. Participants included researchers, civil servants, and advocates working on tobacco, alcohol and unhealthy food. An inductive coding framework was developed. Themes were subsequently interpreted using the policy cycle to examine research influence across stages of policymaking.Results27 survey and 15 focus group participants contributed to the study. Participants described the need for multiple, complementary forms of evidence to advance policy change, conceptualised as an ‘evidence jigsaw’. Different evidence types were perceived as particularly influential at different policy stages (eg, epidemiological evidence for agenda setting, modelling and international experience for policy formulation, lived experience and public opinion for policy adoption and monitoring and evaluation post-adoption to prevent policy rollback). Evidence on UCIs was identified as distinctive in cutting across all policy stages and was perceived as more developed and influential in tobacco control than in alcohol or unhealthy food policy. Effective knowledge translation was facilitated by collaboration between researchers and advocates and timely engagement with policymakers, while misalignment between research production and policy windows posed key challenges.ConclusionsEffective regulation of unhealthy commodities relies on assembling a range of evidence types aligned with policy needs, timing and context, alongside active knowledge translation. The experience of tobacco control illustrates how a more complete ‘evidence jigsaw’, including evidence on industry conduct, can support sustained policy action. Greater cross-sectoral learning and dialogue among public health actors might help accelerate progress in other sectors.Keywords: Public Health, Qualitative Research, Cross-Sectional Studies

Introduction

Non-communicable diseases (NCDs) represent a major public health challenge.1 The products of the so-called unhealthy commodities industries (UCIs), including tobacco, alcohol and ultra-processed foods, are important drivers of this health burden through their contribution to preventable morbidity and premature mortality.2 Addressing the health harms associated with these products has therefore increasingly become a central focus of public health policy in many countries. Reflecting this, the WHO identifies population-level regulatory measures targeting tobacco, alcohol and unhealthy diets as among the most cost-effective ‘Best Buys’ for NCD prevention.3

In the UK, several policies have been introduced in recent years to reduce sales or consumption of unhealthy commodities. Key examples include standardised packaging for factory-made cigarettes and roll-your-own tobacco in 2017, the Soft Drinks Industry Levy in 2018 and Minimum Unit Pricing for alcohol in Scotland in 2018. A substantial body of research has analysed the development, implementation and impacts of these policies, as well as industry opposition and policymaking processes.4–16

The regulation of unhealthy commodities is widely recognised as politically challenging. Through corporate political activities, well-resourced industries seek to shape policy environments by promoting favourable evidence, undermining unfavourable research, lobbying decision-makers and using litigation to avoid, weaken or delay regulation.17 Yet, existing research has paid relatively little attention to how different types of evidence support and facilitate regulation of unhealthy commodities and how diverse forms of evidence are interpreted, valued and used by public health actors within policymaking processes.

The present study aimed to identify which types of research and knowledge translation are most likely to lead to public health informed policy responses to unhealthy commodities. Knowledge translation refers to the processes through which research evidence is shared, exchanged and applied to inform policy and practice, with approaches typically emphasising collaborative partnerships, sustained engagement and exchange between researchers, policymakers and other stakeholders.18 We explored which types of evidence and knowledge translation activities were perceived to be most influential in advancing public health-oriented policy change, drawing on the perspectives of individuals working in UK public health policy, research and advocacy across alcohol, tobacco and unhealthy food/obesity. The study also examined similarities and differences in experiences and perceptions across these policy domains, with the objective of identifying facilitators and challenges. By generating insights across policy domains, this research aims to support cross-sector learning from past policy successes and challenges, inform future regulatory efforts, and contribute to reducing the health harms associated with unhealthy commodities.

Materials and methods

We designed a study which combined an online survey with focus group discussions. The study was informed by a pragmatic orientation, and the qualitative component adopted a qualitative descriptive approach.19 This approach was appropriate given the study’s aim of generating practically useful insights into participants’ perspectives on the role of research and knowledge exchange in policy change.

Sampling and data collection

We used a two-stage sampling approach. We first conducted an online survey using a purposive sampling strategy, inviting members and partners of a UK public health research consortium to participate. The consortium was considered an appropriate network through which to sample individuals working across the three topics of interest (alcohol, tobacco and unhealthy food) because it included experienced researchers, civil servants and advocates working on these issues in a UK setting. All individual members and partners of the consortium were contacted via email and invited to fill the survey. The email contained an OnlineSurveys link to the participant information sheet, consent form and questionnaire. The survey, developed through a series of authors’ meetings, featured a series of open-text questions that aimed to explore participants’ experience and thoughts on the role of research and knowledge exchange activities in achieving policy changes that have impacted on population health.

The second stage of data collection was focus group discussions which were informed by the survey results. Each focus group centred on a different commodity (alcohol, tobacco or unhealthy food) and each involved researchers, civil servants and advocates. For this second phase, we used purposive sampling. We broadened our sample base and recruited also some participants who were not members or partners of the above-mentioned research consortium. Informed by the survey results, we invited individuals who had expertise and professional experience on the topic within the UK public health and policy context, trying to ensure a mix of roles and perspectives relevant to each commodity. Potential participants were approached via email and informed consent was obtained prior to the focus group discussion.

The topic guides for the focus group discussions were informed by the survey results and developed in a series of author meetings. They covered participants’ experiences and perceptions of the role of evidence and knowledge exchange activities in the context of recent policy changes and were tailored to each commodity. The focus group discussions took place online and were facilitated by one of the authors, with a second one taking notes. They lasted between 81 and 85 min. Focus group recordings were transcribed verbatim. The survey questions and interview guide are available in online supplemental file 1.

Data analysis

The qualitative data were analysed in two stages. First, we conducted an inductive thematic analysis to identify themes and patterns across the survey responses and focus group transcripts. To develop an initial coding framework, two authors independently reviewed both the survey responses and focus group transcripts and identified recurring themes across the dataset. Following discussion, an initial coding framework was agreed. The lead author then coded the transcripts and proposed refinements to the framework. A second author reviewed the coding, and the authors met to discuss and agree on the final coding framework. The lead author subsequently refined the coding accordingly. Second, we undertook a deductive framework-based analysis in which the identified themes were mapped onto the stages of the policy cycle. This step was undertaken because participants frequently referred to different phases of policymaking, and the policy cycle therefore provided a useful framework for organising and interpreting the findings. Specifically, themes were mapped against the stages of the policy cycle: problem recognition and agenda setting (the initial stage where issues need to be defined as policy problems and their severity needs to be established), policy formulation and adoption (where objectives are defined, different alternatives are considered, and then proposals are transformed into government programmes), implementation (where a policy is put into practice) and evaluation (where policies are assessed against their intended objectives).20–22 Although the policy cycle has been criticised as an overly simplistic representation of the policymaking process,23 we used it as an analytical tool to structure the findings and enhance their practical relevance. We shared the draft manuscript with focus group participants and asked for comments, which were considered when finalising the manuscript. Seven participants responded.

Patient and public involvement

Patients were not involved in the design, conduct, analysis or reporting of this research. This was because the study aimed to explore perceptions of evidence use and knowledge translation among researchers, advocates and policymakers working on unhealthy commodity regulation, rather than the perspectives of people with lived experience. Public perspectives on evidence and policy responses to unhealthy commodities have been explored separately, within the wider research programme, via citizens’ juries.24

Researcher characteristics and reflexivity

The research team comprised researchers with backgrounds in public health, health policy and social sciences, with experience in research on commercial determinants of health. This informed the study focus and may have shaped interpretations, including a critical perspective on industry influence in policy-making. Two authors had not previously conducted research on the specific policy cases discussed and were not UK nationals, which provided a degree of analytic distance from the national policy context and may have limited case-specific preconceptions. To mitigate potential bias, the study used multiple data sources (survey and focus groups), involved researchers with different disciplinary perspectives in developing data collection tools and analysis, and incorporated iterative team discussions of emerging themes. Draft findings were shared with focus group participants, and their feedback was considered in finalising the analysis. All members of the research team had pre-existing professional relationships with some participants through their work in public health research. All participants were informed that participation was voluntary and that they could decline to participate.

Evidence jigsaw

We found that a particular combination of evidence was needed to move unhealthy commodity regulation forward. Following others, we call this combination an ‘evidence jigsaw’25 26 to emphasise that policymaking requires different pieces of evidence to come together over time to drive change—see figure 1. While the ‘evidence jigsaw’ contains many of the same ‘stages’ as the policy cycle, there are two key differences. First, the ‘jigsaw’ metaphor does not necessarily suggest these ‘stages’ occur in a sequential order. Second, the evidence relating to UCIs’ positions and activities potentially informs every stage which is why figure 1 positions this as a context to the rest of the evidence ‘jigsaw’, rather than a singular piece (as we discuss further below).

The ‘evidence jigsaw’ supporting public health progress in tackling the harms caused by unhealthy commodities.

Below we describe the different evidence types and, reflecting participant accounts, we map these to the policy cycle.

‘Building blocks’: demonstrating the impact of unhealthy commodities (Problem definition and agenda setting)

Evidence on the negative impacts of unhealthy commodities was consistently identified as crucial in bringing public problems to the attention of policymakers. Examples mentioned included epidemiological studies on the health harms of unhealthy products and other statistics concerning the morbidity, mortality and economic losses associated with these products. Participants described this type of evidence as fundamental to the initial stage of the policy cycle—the ‘agenda setting’ phase—as it provides concrete data that policymakers need to understand the magnitude and impact of the problem, helping to make the case for action. For example, one participant reported that analysis of the negative impacts of sugar was foundational in the run-up to the soft drinks industry levy: ‘the key building blocks of the case in terms of evidence, were epidemiological studies showing the adverse effects of sugar, particularly on instance of type 2 diabetes’ (Food Focus Group).

Another related concept that emerged during the focus groups was the importance of ‘problem definition’. How problems are defined or framed, how their nature is described, and how people think about them can affect the urgency and ways in which they will be tackled.27 For example, in the alcohol and tobacco focus groups, participants mentioned the importance of framing the problem in ways that emphasised the ‘harms to others’, such as secondhand smoke (for tobacco) and violence and anti-social behaviour (for alcohol). This way of framing the problem was deemed to be harder for industry to oppose. For example, a participant with experience of tobacco control stated: ‘I think what’s powerful for smoke free is that harm is to others and to sort of innocent standers by, which I think it makes it harder for industry to oppose. I think the tobacco control community was really united and had very clear framing of this issue that they had thought about and consistently used’ (Tobacco Focus Group). Similarly, a participant focusing on alcohol said that, ‘by taking the kind of harm to others approach it felt like the policy was able to land better” (Alcohol Focus Group).

In the case of tobacco, participants pointed out that evidence on the tobacco industry’s unethical behaviours was strategically used to frame the industry as part of the problem that needed to be addressed. For example: ‘We were able to show in the course of the debate [leading to smoke free legislation] that the tobacco industry was not a trustworthy stakeholder, nor interested in health, it was interested in profit. And that kind of knowledge I think went quite deep and was really important”(Tobacco Focus Group).

‘Building a case’: evidence on potential policy solutions, their likely impact and feasibility (Policy formulation)

As well as getting topics on the policy agenda, there also needs to be evidence on what viable policy solutions exist and how these might play out. During policy formulation, evidence can be used to identify and advocate for potential policy options and to assess whether they are likely effective and feasible before taking real-world action. In this regard, evidence on the impact that the policy had already had in different jurisdictions was mentioned as especially useful in demonstrating both viability and impacts. For example: “having evidence of success or even implemented policies from other countries can be really powerful” (Food Focus Group).

Modelling studies were also mentioned as important for assessing the likely impact of policy options, especially where these were innovative options that had not been implemented elsewhere. For example, in the case of Minimum Unit Pricing for alcohol, modelling was described as “absolutely essential to building a case” and as “unique in really giving a sort of hard quotable figure around how many lives will be saved” (Alcohol Focus Group).

According to some participants, evidence on UCIs and their tactics also played an important role in informing policies, especially in tobacco control. For example, one participant stated that: ‘Work on TI [tobacco industry] pricing led to changes to UK tobacco tax policy (and similar proposed changes elsewhere). Work on TI [tobacco industry] and profits has underpinned proposal for polluter pays approaches in UK’ (Survey).

‘Bringing in the voices of people’: lived experiences (Policy adoption)

The adoption of a policy option is determined by several factors. According to some participants, lived experience narratives were a powerful way of supporting policy adoption. They suggested that the experiences of people affected by unhealthy commodities, particularly young people and those most adversely affected, could be especially influential. By humanising complex issues, personal stories can motivate support for policy change and are difficult for industry groups to dismiss. For example, talking about alcohol, a participant stated: ‘So …bringing in the voices of children and young people, bringing in the voices of people in recovery, … that’s something that’s actually quite difficult for the alcohol industry to counter. […] So those are kinda two, two areas where I think that… less sort of formal academicy type evidence is… really helpful and useful [because] it feels a bit more grounded and real world for …decision makers’ (Alcohol Focus Group).

Votes for change: evidence of public support and lived experience (Policy adoption)

In addition, evidence of broader public support for a policy was considered another important piece of the evidence jigsaw supporting policy adoption. Participants suggested that opinion polls or surveys showing that a substantial proportion of the electorate is supportive of a policy can be a powerful motivator for decision-makers, as the following extract attests: ‘And obviously we did the YouGov survey as well, which helped to support that, the narrative around public support and public opinions around smoke free spaces. So that was a really important piece of evidence that was used to, to introduce smoke free spaces’ (Tobacco Focus Group).

‘In the spirit of the law’ (Policy implementation)

Participants mentioned challenges related to policy implementation, particularly the gap between formal adoption and implementation ‘in the spirit of the law’. A key challenge identified was the adaptive (‘work arounds’) response of commercial actors when restrictions are introduced. For example, one participant mentioned how promotional practices in supermarkets have been modified to maintain product visibility after the introduction of place-based promotion restrictions. Since we live in a complex adaptive system where regulatory interventions prompt responses from industry, evidence on how companies have responded to similar policies in other jurisdiction could support effective implementation planning by helping to prevent loopholes.

Preventing policy termination: monitoring and evaluation (Policy evaluation)

Once a policy is in place, participants emphasised the importance of monitoring and evaluating its impacts. This evidence can demonstrate whether a policy is achieving its intended objectives, inform decisions about its continuation, adaptation or expansion, and help protect it from being weakened or repealed. Participants also suggested that evidence of policy success can be used strategically to counter industry-led challenges and misleading narratives. One participant called for more research on this: ‘What we find important in terms of future policies is being able to counter the industry arguments with examples of when that industry argument has been used before and not came to fruition. So, for example, with SDIL [Soft Drinks Industry Levy], bars in Scotland threw their hands up in horror and said this will result in hundreds of job losses. And we know for a fact that it didn’t result in the hundreds of job losses and the CEO [Chief Executive Officer] of bars said very recently, ‘well it would actually now be a disaster if … it was overturned and they did away with the levy’. … But sometimes we don’t capture that well enough in research …, that shift or the fact that that was the argument that was used and … that wasn’t the impact on industry’ (Food Focus Group).

Similarities and differences across unhealthy commodities

Looking across alcohol, tobacco and food, there were clear similarities in how people conceived of evidence being successfully used to inform public health policy progress. In all cases, there was a sense of different types of evidence being required to support distinct policy phases—in short, an ‘evidence jigsaw’ (figure 1) was required to achieve progress. Evidence of the ways UCIs can interfere with evidence and policy-making seemed potentially important across several policy stages, although with variation by industry.

Research on the tobacco industry was frequently identified as having been pivotal in informing policy progress, whereas several participants stated that there is still a particular dearth of similar research focused on the alcohol and food industries. For example: ‘I think we also need to remember that… I think the evidence is further behind. You know the evidence that… of the harms of tobacco came earlier, evidence that industry was hiding those harms came earlier. … That’s really only coming out now on alcohol and food, how harmful they are and the way that industry has hidden it.’ (Tobacco Focus Group).

Some participants pointed to the fact that UCIs are not a homogeneous group and present different policy challenges. Unlike tobacco, the food and alcohol industries at present have a seat at the policy table, and attitudes among policymakers about conflicts of interest with these industries are more variable. For example: ‘Because food is conceptualised differently from the other commodities as being essential rather than a pleasure/a sin, it presents a different ethical, and therefore policy, challenge’ (Survey).

Some participants also stressed the need for more evidence showing commonalities of tactics across UCIs and exposing the inherent conflicts of interest that arise when these industries are involved in policy making. A participant stated: ‘I think research exposing lobbying tactics by the alcohol industry would be really helpful. We really need to get to a place where the alcohol industry is seen similarly to the tobacco industry, ie that it is recognised that there should be no engagement with it on policy making. Unfortunately, we seem to be a long way off that. Any research showing how alcohol industry uses the same/similar tactics as tobacco though would be helpful’ (Survey). However, a participant in the tobacco focus group suggested that the issue was more one of traction, reflecting: ‘I also think there’s some really interesting questions about why industry malfeasance has had traction in tobacco in ways in which it hasn’t in other industries’, noting that: ‘the different ways in which those different forms of … evidence of industry malfeasance play out is, is something that I think we kind of need to unpack a wee [little] bit more’ (Tobacco Focus Group).

Knowledge translation: activities, facilitators and barriers

Beyond the generation of evidence, participants identified several knowledge translation activities that they perceived as facilitating evidence use and supporting policy change. Although these activities were often context-specific, several common approaches emerged across the three policy domains. These included sustained engagement with policymakers (eg, presentations to parliamentary committees and submissions to consultations), collaboration between researchers and advocacy organisations, and strategic use of the media to raise public awareness and build support for policy action. Participants also emphasised the importance of developing trusted relationships between researchers, policymakers and advocates. Such relationships enabled researchers to better understand policy needs, anticipate policy windows, and communicate relevant evidence in a timely way. Finally, several participants highlighted co-production with policy and advocacy actors as a means of ensuring that research addressed policy-relevant questions and was more readily translated into action.

One of the core challenges in the evidence-policy interface noted by participants was the frequent misalignment between the timeframes for policymaking and evidence production; this means that research is often conducted or published after a policy window has closed. For example: ‘I think doing the right work during the acute policy window can play a role but is sometimes not done or done afterwards or not used. … I also think monitoring or rapid response work can be key—this is … hard to plan and time consuming to do but can play a role e.g. in identifying new front groups, or new practices or industry undermining legislation etc. and can also be used to inform more in-depth research’ (Survey).

Discussion

This study suggests that public health policy change is more likely to be achieved when a complementary portfolio of evidence is available, with different forms of evidence becoming particularly influential at different stages of the policy process; an ‘evidence jigsaw’. While public health hierarchies of evidence remain valuable for assessing intervention effectiveness, our findings suggest they provide only a partial guide to the broader evidence required to influence policy. Effective public health policymaking appears to depend on combining different types of data generated through a range of methodological approaches, each making a distinct contribution to understanding the problem, identifying feasible solutions, supporting implementation and sustaining policy change.

Although participants generally suggested that the forms of evidence required to support different stages of policy were distinct and served different functions, a notable exception was evidence on UCIs, which participants described as relevant across the policy process rather than to a single stage. This was most evident in tobacco control but was also seen as having considerable potential to strengthen alcohol and unhealthy food policy. Industry actors use a range of strategies to shape, delay, weaken and circumvent public health policies.17 In this sense, industry-focused evidence appeared to operate as a cross-cutting component of the broader ‘evidence jigsaw’.

At each stage, participants highlighted the importance of evidence that could counter industry narratives and actions. For example, research on UCI practices may serve different functions at different stages of the policy process. Research on corporate conduct can help build the case for policies that address industry behaviours, such as marketing or pricing strategies. During policy development, evidence on corporate political activities can help anticipate and counter attempts to influence or delay legislation. Following implementation, research examining industry responses to regulation can identify how policies are circumvented in practice, informing legislative refinements and strengthening the design of future policies in other jurisdictions. Finally exposing industries’ tactics could help de-normalise their behaviours and change the policy environment because, as one participant noted: “Sunlight is a great disinfectant.”

It is important to note that evidence relating to UCIs was perceived to be more established in tobacco control. This likely reflects the longer history of tobacco control regulation,28 the influence of the World Health Organisation Framework Convention on Tobacco Control,29 and the extensive availability of internal industry documents, released following litigations that have been analysed by researchers providing unique insights into corporate strategies.30 By contrast, while internal documents from other industries have also become available, the scope and accessibility of such evidence remain more limited: for example, the UCSF Industry Documents Library contains nearly 20 million tobacco industry documents compared with just over 170 000 food industry documents, constraining opportunities for comparable analysis and potentially shaping how evidence is used across sectors.31

Finally, while the ‘evidence jigsaw’ identifies the complementary forms of evidence required across the policy process, complementary knowledge translation activities appeared crucial. Rather than representing another piece of the jigsaw, participants described knowledge translation as the processes through which different forms of evidence are brought together, communicated and mobilised to influence policy. In this sense, knowledge translation enables the strategic assembly of ‘evidence jigsaws’ in support of public health policy change.

The study has several limitations. First, it involved a relatively small sample and focused primarily on the UK context, which may limit the transferability of the findings to other policy settings. Second, although we sought to include researchers, policymakers and advocates, researchers were more strongly represented in the survey, and some participant groups in the focus groups were represented by only one individual. This may partly reflect the limited availability of policymakers to participate in research. Consequently, the findings are likely to reflect academic perspectives on evidence use and knowledge translation more strongly than those of policymakers. Third, although participants consistently identified lived experience as an important form of evidence for policy change, this study did not explore the perspectives of people with lived experience directly because its focus was on the perceptions of policy actors. Public perspectives on evidence and policy responses to unhealthy commodities have been examined separately within the wider research programme using citizens’ juries.24 Future research could usefully compare these perspectives to better understand how different actors conceptualise the role of evidence in shaping policy. Finally, because participants focused much more extensively on evidence than on knowledge translation activities, the findings relating to knowledge translation are necessarily less developed. Future research could examine knowledge translation processes in greater depth in relation to this policy area and could explore how well these insights align with established knowledge translation frameworks.

Despite these limitations, the findings have clear implications for those seeking to promote evidence-informed regulation of unhealthy commodities. They suggest that policy progress is more likely when multiple forms of evidence are assembled strategically to meet the needs of different stages of the policy process, rather than when evidence generation is pursued in isolation. The experience of tobacco control illustrates how a more complete ‘evidence jigsaw’—including evidence on industry conducts—can support sustained policy action. This raises important questions about whether research on UCI practices functions as a crucial accelerant for policy change, and why such evidence appears to gain traction in some sectors more than others. Understanding the conditions under which industry-focused research reshapes policy norms may be an important priority for future research and for efforts to strengthen regulation of unhealthy commodities beyond tobacco.

Finally, while the ‘evidence jigsaw’ emerged as a useful way of understanding how different forms of evidence contribute to policy change, there is the potential for selective assembly of evidence. Because each piece of the jigsaw may draw on different types and bodies of evidence rather than a single comprehensive synthesis—there is scope for actors to selectively emphasise studies that align with their views or objectives. In policy domains characterised by scientific uncertainty, this may contribute to the emergence of competing ‘evidence narratives’.

Conclusions

This study examined how different forms of evidence are perceived to support policy change aimed at reducing the harms associated with alcohol, tobacco and unhealthy food. Participants consistently described the need for multiple, complementary forms of evidence—an ‘evidence jigsaw’—that together support different stages of the policy process. One form of evidence, however, emerged as distinctive in its reach and function: research on the activities and strategies of UCIs. Unlike other evidence types, which tended to align more clearly with specific policy stages, evidence on UCIs was described as cutting across the entire policy process.

Our findings suggest that tobacco control has benefited from a more developed ‘evidence jigsaw’ than alcohol and unhealthy food policy, alongside effective knowledge translation characterised by strong relationships between researchers, advocates and policymakers. This highlights opportunities for cross-sector learning about the combinations of evidence and the knowledge translation activities needed to mobilise them that are most likely to support and sustain public health policy change.
