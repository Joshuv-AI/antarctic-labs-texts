Factors influencing the impact of financial toxicity on the quality of life of breast cancer patients after mastectomies
Authors: Xiaoyan Ma, Xinrong Liu, Jie Ren, Min Wang, Li Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study aims to explore the relationship between financial toxicity (FT), fear of cancer recurrence (FCR), quality of life (QOL), and illness perception (IP) in breast cancer patients undergoing chemotherapy after a mastectomy, to provide new ideas and directions for improving their quality of life.MethodsA total of 305 breast cancer patients who received chemotherapy after a mastectomy were included in this study using the convenience sampling method. FT, FCR, IP, and QOL were cross-sectionally investigated using the General Information Questionnaire, Comprehensive Score for Financial Toxicity, Simplified Fear of Disease Progression Questionnaire, Functional Assessment of Cancer Therapy-General, and Brief Illness Perception Questionnaire. Data were analyzed using SPSS 26.0.ResultsPlace of residence, employment status, household income per month, personal monthly income, age, literacy, and healthcare payment method, radiotherapy and surgical approach were found to affect the QOL of patients. QOL and FT scores were significantly correlated. FCR could mediate the effect of FT on QOL, and IP played a moderating role in the relationship between FT and QOL.ConclusionsThe relationships between FT, FCR, IP, and QOL aligned with the findings of the stress response model. FT directly affects QOL and also further affects it via FCR, while IP modulates the effect of FT on the QOL, offering new insights for clinical staff tasked with choosing measures to improve the quality of life of patients.Keywords: Breast cancer, Financial toxicity, Fear of cancer recurrence, Illness perception, Quality of life, Mediation model

Background

According to the latest data released by the WHO in 2024, 2.3 million females worldwide were diagnosed with breast cancer by 2022, with 670,000 deaths attributable to the disease. In the United States, breast cancer surpassed lung cancer as the most frequently diagnosed cancer among females. Breast cancer accounts for 15% of all cancer-related deaths, and was the second leading cause of mortality [1]. In China, the incidence rate of breast cancer among females reached 15.6%, second only to lung cancer. Although the global incidence rate of breast cancer has continued to rise in the past 20 years, the survival rate has improved significantly due to advancements in treatment modalities [2]. This positive shift results from the development and improvement of these treatments. Currently, common treatment modalities for breast cancer include surgery (such as mastectomy), chemotherapy, radiotherapy, targeted therapy, immunotherapy, and endocrine therapy. Among these, adjuvant chemotherapy following mastectomy is the most commonly used method, and could effectively reduce the risk of metastasis and cancer cell dissemination. This results in improved prognostic outcomes, with a 5-year survival rate extending up to 82.0% [3]. This surgical-based treatment approach is more suitable for early-stage patients [4]. However, despite its efficacy in preventing metastasis and enhancing patient survival rates, postoperative adjuvant chemotherapy imposes a tremendous financial burden on patients during disease treatment and recovery, which ultimately leads to financial toxicity [5–7].

Financial toxicity (FT) refers to the negative impact of the high costs associated with the treatment of cancer patients, encompassing both objective and subjective financial difficulties. The objective hardship of financial toxicity mainly includes direct expenses, such as medical costs, and indirect expenses, such as lost income and transportation costs incurred during treatment [8]. The subjective distress of financial toxicity includes emotional and mental health issues, such as anxiety and depression. These challenges can reduce treatment adherence, increase the fear of cancer recurrence (FCR), and thus affect the patient’s QOL [9–11]. FCR is defined as the concern regarding cancer recurrence or progression, and is one of the key factors leading to a significant decline in patients’ QOL [12, 13]. Notably, breast cancer patients report higher levels of financial toxicity and elevated scores associated with the FCR [14–16]. Despite the continuous development of breast cancer treatment technologies, tumor recurrence and metastasis remain major challenges in modern medicine. Even breast cancer patients with small tumor diameters and negative lymph nodes have a recurrence risk of approximately 10% [17]. Therefore, addressing the FCR has positive implications for all breast cancer patients.

Increased social and psychological stress, along with financial toxicity, have contributed to a more pronounced negative illness perception (IP) in breast cancer patients, severely affecting their QOL [18–22]. The concept of IP was defined by Howard Leventhal et al. in 1980 as the knowledge and beliefs that individuals hold about symptoms, pain, illness, medical conditions, and health threats. Research indicates that IP analyses can improve psychological states and health outcomes, thereby affecting patients’ QOL [23].

According to the theory on psychological stress and coping developed by Lazarus and Folkman, the impact of a stressful event on an individual depends on two important psychological processes: cognitive evaluation and coping [24]. Cognitive appraisal encompasses primary appraisal, secondary appraisal, and reappraisal, each of which can lead to distinct coping strategies in patients. In this study, treatment costs are considered as the stressor, with FT and FCR representing the negative outcomes of primary and secondary cognitive appraisals, respectively. IP is viewed as the reappraisal process, while variations in the QOL are considered as consequences of different coping strategies. Based on this framework, the following hypotheses were proposed: (1) FCR mediates the relationship between FT and QOL; (2) Negative IP significantly moderates the impact of FT on QOL. This study aims to investigate the current status of FT, FCR, IP, and QOL in patients undergoing adjuvant chemotherapy after a mastectomy for breast cancer and verify our hypotheses.

Methods

Participants

A convenient sampling method was employed in this study. A total of 305 patients undergoing chemotherapy after a mastectomy were recruited at the Department of General Surgery and Oncology at Qingdao Municipal Hospital from August 2023 to March 2024. Patients were included based on the following enrollment criteria: (1) confirmed primary breast cancer through clinical and pathological examination; (2) underwent chemotherapy after mastectomies; (3) aged 18 to 80 years; (4) were conscious, aware of their condition, and were willing to participate in the study. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) presence of other malignant tumors or important organ lesions; (2) current or past history of mental illnesses; (3) cognitive dysfunction affecting communication; (4) participation in psychological counseling, psychotherapy, or other scientific studies; (5) serious complications after surgery; (6) recurrence and metastasis after surgery.

Measures

A cross-sectional study was designed using the General Information Questionnaire, Comprehensive Score for Financial Toxicity (COST), simplified Fear of Disease Progression Questionnaire (FoP-Q-SF), Functional Assessment of Cancer Therapy-General (FACT-G), and Brief Illness Perception Questionnaire (BIPQ).

General information questionnaire

A survey questionnaire was designed through an extensive literature review based on the research objectives and content. The main survey included 16 items, i.e., age, place of residence, job situation, family monthly income, family history of cancer, personal income, education level, marital status, medical payment method, clinical stage, disease duration (time since diagnosis), chemotherapy cycle, and number of hospitalizations in the past year, occurrence of other chronic diseases, radiotherapy and surgical approach.

Comprehensive score for financial toxicity

The comprehensive score for financial toxicity (COST) was developed by Souza et al. at the University of Chicago in 2014, and its Chinese version was translated and revised by Huihui et al., preserving 11 original items. The scale included two dimensions: positive wealth status and negative psychological reactions. A lower score indicates a greater degree of FT. The scale indicated good reliability, with a Cronbach’s α coefficient of 0.928. Scores ≤ 26 indicated no impact on QOL (level 0), scores from 14 to 25 indicated a mild impact (level 1), scores from 1 to 13 indicated a moderate impact (level 2), and a score of 0 indicated a high impact (level 3). A score at level 1 or higher was indicative of FT.

Fear of disease progression questionnaire

Herschbach et al. developed the Fear of disease progression questionnaire (FoP-Q), which was subsequently simplified by the German scholar Mehner. Wu Qiyun et al. translated the scale into Chinese and categorized it into two dimensions: the physical health and social family aspects. The Cronbach’s α coefficient of the scale was 0.884, indicating good reliability and validity.

Functional assessment of cancer therapy-general

Functional assessment of cancer therapy-general (FACT-G) data was compiled by Cella et al. at Northwestern University in 1993, and the Chinese version was introduced and revised by Wan Chonghua et al. The scale has been used widely to evaluate the QOL of cancer patients during treatment. It includes 27 items and the following 4 dimensions: physical, social, emotional, and functional well-being. Higher scores indicate a better QOL. The Cronbach’s α coefficient was 0.953, indicating good reliability and validity.

Brief illness perception questionnaire

The brief illness perception questionnaire (BIPQ) was developed by Broadbent et al. and translated and adapted by Mei Yaqi. It has been used to evaluate the level of IP in breast cancer patients. The Chinese version of the questionnaire consists of 9 items, mainly evaluating the cognitive representation of the disease (5 items), emotional representation (2 items), understanding of the disease (1 item), and evaluation of the cause of the disease (1 item) [25, 26]. The open-ended question related to the causal item of IP was not analyzed in this study. Instead, only the cognitive (5 items), emotional (2 items), and understanding (1 item) dimensions, comprising a total of 8 items, were included in the analysis. The Cronbach’s α coefficient of the scale was 0.785, indicating good reliability and validity.

Collection process and description of clinical data

This study was conducted after obtaining ethical approval (ID#2023-KY-070) and written informed consent from all participants. To minimize subjective bias, all investigators received standardized training and passed a qualifying assessment prior to data collection. Subjects were strictly screened based on predetermined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Data were collected via one-on-one structured interviews conducted in private hospital rooms. Initial quality control was performed on-site immediately after each questionnaire was completed. All collected questionnaires underwent logical validation to exclude invalid responses. Data were then independently entered by two researchers using a double-entry system to ensure accuracy. Consistency checks and outlier verification were subsequently performed to guarantee the reliability and cleanliness of the database. According to Kendall’s rough estimation approach, the sample size should be at least 5 to 10 times the number of variables. In this study, a total of 27 variables were included, comprising items from the general information questionnaire (e.g. age, place of residence, working status, monthly family income per capita, family history of cancer, personal income, educational level, marital status, medical payment method, clinical stage, disease duration, chemotherapy cycles, number of hospitalizations in the past year, concurrent chronic diseases, radiotherapy history and surgical approach), as well as two dimensions of the Score for FT, two dimensions of the Fear of Disease Progression Questionnaire, three dimensions of the Brief Illness Perception Questionnaire, and four dimensions of the Functional Assessment of Cancer Therapy-General. Considering a 10% loss to follow-up rate, the sample size could be calculated as follows: Sample size = (number of variables*10)/(1–10%) = 300 cases. A total of 314 questionnaires were distributed in this study, and 305 valid questionnaires were retrieved. Three questionnaires were considered invalid as patients stopped filling out the questionnaires because of discomfort during the process. Four questionnaires were considered invalid as answers were inconsistent with the actual situation, and another two questionnaires were considered invalid as patients stopped filling out the questionnaires because of undergoing treatment during the process. The validity rate of the questionnaires was 97.1%.

Statistical analysis

The Excel database was imported into SPSS 26.0 software, and statistical analysis was performed. The statistical significance level was set at α = 0.05.

(1) Descriptive analysis of current status: In this study, we used descriptive analysis by frequency and percentage for the general data of patients treated with chemotherapy after a mastectomy.

Descriptive analysis was performed by determining mean ± standard deviation/median values and quartiles for scores of FT, FCR, and QOL.

(2) Comparison between groups: Independent sample t-tests and one-way ANOVA with population variables were used to compare FT and QOL scores between groups.

(3) Influence factor analysis: Multiple linear regression analysis was performed to identify the factors influencing the QOL in breast cancer patients who underwent mastectomies and chemotherapy.

(4) Correlation analysis: Pearson correlation analysis was used to analyze the correlation between FT, FCR, and QOL.

(5) Test of the moderating effect of the mediator: The causal pathways of FT, FCR, IP, and QOL were analyzed using the Process plugin in SPSS 26.0 statistical software. The mediating and moderating effects of FT, FCR, IP, and QOL were verified using the Process model 5. The significance of the mediating and moderating variables was tested using the Bootstrap method.

Results

Basic information description

All the included participants were female, ranging in age from 20 to 79 years, with an average age of 52.27 ± 13.16 years. Most participants, totaling 215 individuals (70.5%), resided in urban areas. Most participants, i.e., 103 individuals, worked full-time (33.8%), while 91 individuals (29.8%) were retired. The monthly per capita income of the family mainly ranged between 1000 and 3000 yuan and 3000–5000 yuan for 114 (37.4%) and 93 (30.5%) participants, respectively. The personal monthly income was mainly in the range of 3000–5000 yuan and 1000–3000 yuan, accounting for 108 (35.4%) and 97 (31.8%) participants, respectively. Most participants had no family history of cancer, totaling 252 individuals (82.6%). In terms of educational level, most had received a junior high school or high school/technical secondary school education, accounting for 92 (30.2%) and 76 (24.9%) participants, respectively. Most participants were married, accounting for 268 individuals (87.9%). Regarding the medical payment method, the majority of participants were covered by employee medical insurance, totaling 174 individuals (57%); 106 (34.8%) were covered by urban and rural resident medical insurance, and 25 (8.2%) were self-paying. Most participants had stage II disease, totaling 161 individuals (52.8%), and the disease course was mainly 7–11 months, totaling 169 individuals (55.4%). The chemotherapy cycle was mainly in the 2nd-4th cycle, totaling 196 individuals (64.3%). The number of hospitalizations in the past year was mainly between 6 and 9, totaling 197 individuals (64.6%). The majority did not have other concurrent chronic diseases, totaling 244 individuals (80%), and did not receive radiotherapy, totaling 278 individuals (91.1%). Among them, 56.7% of the patients underwent modified radical mastectomy for treatment. Table 1 provides a summary of other general information.

Discussion

In this study, 72.13% of the 305 breast cancer patients receiving post-mastectomy chemotherapy exhibited FT, a finding consistent with previous research on breast cancer patients [9]. This indicates that FT remains a significant factor affecting the QOL for breast cancer patients receiving post-radical chemotherapy. QOL, a crucial indicator of patient QOL, was assessed in this study and found to be at moderate levels, aligning with findings from other studies [27]. It is particularly important to monitor and implement effective measures to improve the QOL for breast cancer patients receiving post-radical chemotherapy. Furthermore, the single-factor analysis indicates that surgical approaches are significantly associated with the QOL. However, when FT and FCR are included in the multivariate model, the independent influence of surgical approaches significantly weaken and no longer have statistical significance. This finding may be related to the sample characteristic of a relatively high proportion of early-stage cancer patients in this study cohort. Therefore, this study does not elaborate on this factor in detail. While prior research has reported on FT, notably its impact on QOL, few studies have explored the specific pathogenic mechanisms linking FT and QOL [28–31]. Building on this foundation, our study used the mediation moderation model to reveal that FCR mediates the relationship between FT and QOL, while IP significantly moderates its impact [24, 32].

The mediating effect of FCR

This study reveals that FT not only directly impacts QOL but also intensifies their FCR [33]. Notably, patients experiencing more severe FT demonstrate heightened recurrence anxiety—a finding consistent with results obtained in previous studies [11]. The exorbitant treatment costs may prevent patients from completing recommended maintenance therapy, follow-up examinations, or preventive treatments. This “forced compromise” creates constant anxiety about potential treatment interruptions or downgrades due to financial constraints, which amplifies FCR. Moreover, the treatment burden often leads to work disability, drastic income loss, or depleted savings, forcing patients to struggle with daily basic living expenses [34]. This persistent survival pressure significantly weakens psychological resilience, magnifying concerns about cancer recurrence and amplifying overwhelming anxiety [35]. Consequently, patients face greater difficulty managing this emotional challenge, increasing vulnerability to helplessness and despair. Additionally, the fear of financial insufficiency for subsequent treatments after recurrence and uncertainty about continued therapy further exacerbate recurrence anxiety [36]. The heavy economic burden of cancer treatment, combined with inevitable feelings of self-blame, pain, and confusion, perpetuates persistent fears of cancer recurrence.

Furthermore, this study reveals that the fear of relapse not only manifests as financial toxicity but also significantly impacts QOL. Specifically, patients experiencing more severe relapse fears demonstrate markedly poorer QOL. Excessive worry about relapse may trigger generalized anxiety, panic attacks, or depressive symptoms. This recurring anxiety disrupts daily emotional states, ultimately compromising mental health [37]. Such psychological damage can lead to sleep difficulties and weakened immune function, negatively affecting physical health. It may also result in excessive dependence on family relationships, strained intimate connections, and diminished caregiving capacity, ultimately causing emotional disorders and reduced quality of life [38]. Moreover, the heavy psychological burden may hinder patients’ ability to return to work, perform previous duties competently, or maintain relationships with former colleagues and friends, leading to social dysfunction, disrupted life rhythms, and further deterioration of QOL [39, 40]. Consequently, this fear of relapse continuously disrupts daily routines, severely impacting the QOL of patients.

Therefore, this study proposes a FT-FCR-QOL model, where fear of cancer recurrence acts as a mediating variable. Patients with high FCR often experience constant fantasies about cancer recurrence when facing significant financial pressure, leading to heightened feelings of helplessness and survival anxiety [41]. This may drive patients to adopt excessive medical interventions, over-treatment, or other measures that increase financial burdens to alleviate their FCR, thereby exacerbating financial toxicity and reinforcing FCR. This vicious cycle prompts patients to resort to more passive coping strategies, resulting in a sharp decline in QOL. Conversely, patients with low FCR demonstrate less anxiety and confusion about cancer recurrence when confronting substantial financial pressure, enabling them to adopt more proactive approaches to manage treatment-related challenges. This helps mitigate the negative impacts of FT and improves both QOL. Therefore, our findings suggest that timely psychological interventions may be beneficial for patients with high FT, potentially offering positive guidance to address their FCR. When facing equivalent financial burdens, improved management of recurrence fear significantly reduces post-cancer depression risks and substantially enhances QOL. In this context, FCR serves as a mediating variable that alters patients’ cognitive evaluation and emotional responses to economic stress, thereby improving the overall impact of FT on QOL. It plays a crucial role in the FT-QOL relationship.

Regulatory effects of IP

Another noteworthy finding of this study is that patients with similar FT scores exhibit varying levels of QOL. Further investigation revealed that IP serves as a moderating variable in the pathway where FT impacts QOL. Under identical FT conditions, patients with lower IP demonstrated better QOL regulation compared to those with higher perception. Previous extensive research indicates that breast cancer patients predominantly hold negative perceptions of their condition [42]. The stronger the negative IP, the more pronounced the impact of FT on QOL becomes, leading to poorer living standards [43], demonstrating that negative IP acts as an obstacle to improving QOL. Patients with lower IP exhibit greater awareness of treatment benefits. Through multi-channel guidance and education programs to alleviate economic stress-induced FT, patients develop more open-minded attitudes and proactive behaviors, thereby enhancing self-confidence and improving self-care capabilities, significantly reducing the negative impact on daily life. As patients’ self-care abilities strengthen, accompanying family members often provide supervision and support for medical visits, resulting in fewer instances of improper medical practices or neglect during treatment. This alleviates FT and further improves the overall QOL. In contrast, when patients are dominated by negative IP, they tend to develop more maladaptive coping strategies. This leads to diminished self-management capabilities, amplified disease-related anxieties, and fears, increased medical expenses, heavier financial burdens, and even treatment complacency, ultimately adversely affecting disease progression and QOL. The intensity of the impact of FT on QOL can be moderated through patients’ perceived disease severity. Notably, those with low illness perception demonstrate greater resilience against the adverse effects of financial toxicity. However, patients with high illness perceptions often exhibit heightened emotional sensitivity, showing increased awareness of disease-related changes and more pronounced negative perceptions. Consequently, breast cancer patients with elevated illness perception frequently struggle to rationally assess the impact of cancer, magnifying perceived health threats and resulting in heightened feelings of helplessness and anxiety [44]. Even when FT remains mild, patients may develop significant negative psychological states, such as pessimistic views toward themselves, the world, and the future, leading to generalized anxiety, impaired social functioning, and increased susceptibility to depressive tendencies and immune system decline [45]. Furthermore, excessive illness perception makes patients vulnerable to misinformation from media or social influences, causing doubts about treatment efficacy and resulting in erroneous judgments. Patients with heightened illness perception tend to associate every aspect of daily life with their cancer, interpreting others’ distance or intimacy, complaints or praise as indicators of their condition’s progression. This leads to psychological states of greater confusion and distress, making normal social interactions difficult and severely compromising QOL. Furthermore, such patients tend to overestimate their contributions to treatment, believing their significant efforts did not yield the expected positive outcomes. This cognitive distortion intensifies dissatisfaction with cancer treatment plans, undermines confidence in therapeutic value, reduces treatment adherence, and increases rates of excessive medicalization or treatment abandonment, thereby further diminishing QOL [46]. Interventions targeting highly sensitive patients with heightened IP often struggle to alter their negative mental states, resulting in limited effectiveness. Therefore, in patients with low IP, more positive guidance could be provided, and it could play a stronger role in regulating the impact of FT on QOL.

Clinical treatment recommendations

Based on the pathways identified in this study, when improving the QOL of patients, addressing psychological health concerns could be an important component of care [47]. Preliminary evidence suggests that improved clinician-patient communication, particularly through the use of concrete data and cost transparency, may contribute to reduced ambiguity and potentially alleviate some psychological distress, including FCR [48]. However, the magnitude and mechanisms of this effect require further empirical validation.

This helps patients accurately identify early warning signs of recurrence, reduces unnecessary anxiety caused by cancer treatment or financial burdens, enhances their sense of control over their condition, and bridges the information gap between doctors and patients. Hence, it alleviates psychological stress and diminishes FCR. Future intervention studies could explore whether encouraging patients to participate in treatment decisions helps to mitigate FCR, developing scientific monitoring plans based on individual conditions and specific psychological fears, incorporating precision medicine principles [49]. Interventional approaches, including mindfulness-based therapy and acceptance and commitment therapy (ACT), may be implemented by doctors to manage cancer-related anxiety and FCR; however, their efficacy in this context and general applicability require further validation through robust clinical trials [50]. Furthermore, clinical staff may tailor interventions according to patients’ perceived disease severity to mitigate the impact of FT on QOL. They can also guide patients toward positive lifestyle changes to enhance their QOL. Patients can confide their concerns about their illness to family members or social support providers. These listeners offer emotional and practical support, strengthening the patient’s family and social bonds. By actively discussing treatment options, care strategies, and seeking professional assistance, caregivers can significantly alleviate patients’ negative emotions, ensuring high-quality life during treatment [19]. Additionally, when working with patients experiencing heightened IP, clinicians should first guide cognitive restructuring to enhance emotional regulation before implementing positive interventions. Furthermore, clinicians should address the diverse needs of breast cancer patients by identifying high-risk groups with financial toxicity and fear of cancer recurrence, providing targeted interventions accordingly.

The limitations of this study include its single-center design, which introduces selection bias in the sample due to insufficient multicenter research and limited sample size. It is particularly important to note that since surgical treatment is mostly applied to patients with early-stage breast cancer, the sample selection in this study mainly focused on early-stage patients [4]. Therefore, future investigations should adopt large-scale, multi-center studies with expanded participant enrollment to enhance sample representativeness and applicability. While our assessment of FT, FCR, IP, and QOL was objective and standardized, the absence of longitudinal studies, clinical application-related experiments on IP, and comparative evaluations across other cancers or conditions limits the clinical applicability of these findings. These gaps warrant further exploration. Therefore, evaluating FT, FCR, IP, and QOL across multiple cancers holds significant research value, potentially providing actionable insights to improve QOL for breast cancer patients.

Conclusions

Previous studies have reported on the interactive relationships between FT and QOL; FT and FCR; and FCR and QOL. However, there has been a lack of exploration of the associations between FT, FCR, IP, and QOL. This study further investigated the associations between FT, FCR, IP, and QOL, and found that FCR plays a mediating role on the impact of FT on QOL, and IP plays a moderating role on the impact of FT on QOL. Our findings indicate that FT in post-mastectomy chemotherapy patients affects QOL through the mediating role of FCR, while IP moderates this relationship. Our model validation provides empirical evidence showing that interventions targeting both economic and psychological dimensions may effectively improve the QOL in this population.

These findings highlight the need for clinical initiatives involving dual-focused strategies addressing the financial burden and cognitive-emotional adjustment to optimize survivorship care for breast cancer patients receiving adjuvant chemotherapy. Finally, it is important to note that the mediating and moderating effects identified here are based on cross-sectional data. The generalizability of these findings, particularly to patients with advanced disease, may be limited by the single-center design of this study and its cohort, which predominantly comprised early-stage patients. Consequently, future multicenter studies focusing on advanced populations are warranted to validate the efficacy of this approach. Furthermore, while this study suggests potential targets for intervention (e.g., reducing FCR through counseling, modifying negative IP through cognitive restructuring), the efficacy of these approaches must be rigorously tested in future prospective interventional studies.
