Abstract

PDE constrained optimal control problems require regularisation to
ensure well-posedness, introducing small perturbations that make the
solutions challenging to approximate accurately. We propose a finite
element approach that couples both regularisation and discretisation
adaptivity, varying both the regularisation parameter and mesh-size
locally based on rigorous a posteriori error estimates aiming to
dynamically balance induced regularisation and discretisation errors,
offering a robust and efficient method for solving these problems. We
demonstrate the efficacy of our analysis with several numerical
experiments.

Keywords:

PDE-constrained optimal control,
Adaptive regularisation,
Finite element methods,
A posteriori error estimates

Introduction

Optimal control problems constrained by partial differential equations
(PDEs) are fundamental in various scientific and engineering
applications. These problems play an important role in areas such as
fluid dynamics
[
13
]
, heat
conduction
[
15
]
, structural
optimisation
[
18
]
, crystal
growth
[
9
]
, and medical treatment planning, such
as radiotherapy
[
11
]
. Solving these
problems efficiently and accurately is essential for making
advancements in these domains.

Traditionally, the mathematical framework for PDE-constrained optimal
control problems, particularly those involving linear and nonlinear
elliptic or parabolic PDEs, has been
well-established
[
5
,
27
,
12
]
. These
formulations typically lead to first-order optimality conditions,
where adjoint states or Lagrange multipliers are introduced to derive
the necessary conditions for optimality. Such formulations have paved
the way for numerous numerical methods, providing a robust theoretical
foundation for solving these problems.

Common numerical approaches for solving these problems involve
discretising the continuous optimal control problem using finite
element methods (FEM)
[
2
,
7
]
, spectral
methods
[
28
]
, or wavelet
methods
[
19
]
. Among these, FEM is widely favoured for
its flexibility in handling complex geometries and boundary
conditions, making it well-suited for problems involving non-smooth
solutions or intricate domains. However, accurately solving such
PDE-constrained control problems often comes with high computational
cost, particularly when dealing with large-scale or high-dimensional
systems.

A significant aspect of PDE-constrained optimisation is the choice of
the regularisation, which is typically introduced to ensure
well-posedness and stability of the problem. However, choosing an
appropriate regularisation parameter can be challenging, especially
when dealing with non-smooth or complex solutions. While much of the
existing work has focused on a priori analysis of FEMs
[
22
]
,
less attention has been given to adaptive strategies that adjust the
regularisation parameter dynamically based on the solution’s behaviour,
particularly in the context of a posteriori error analysis.

Adaptive regularisation strategies have been widely studied in the
context of inverse problems
[
30
,
17
]
, where balancing
regularisation and data fidelity is important. Given the close
relationship between inverse problems and PDE-constrained optimal
control problems
[
10
]
, techniques from adaptive inverse problem
regularisation provide a valuable perspective for designing robust
numerical methods in control applications.

This paper presents a novel approach that addresses this gap by
introducing an adaptive strategy for regularising PDE-constrained
optimal control problems. We propose a method that leverages a
posteriori error estimates to adaptively vary the regularisation
parameter across the computational domain. This allows the
regularisation to be varied elementwise, balancing the approximation
error and regularisation inconsistency. This potentially enhances
solution accuracy whilst also reducing computational overhead by
focusing computational effort where it is most needed, enabling more
efficient handling of non-smooth solutions, boundary layers and
complex geometries.

The novelty of our approach lies in the dynamic adjustment of both the
regularisation parameter and the mesh size. This is guided by rigorous
error estimators derived from a posteriori estimates, extending
traditional adaptive finite element
methods
[
31
,
1
]
. Our
method ensures that the regularisation and discretisation errors are
balanced, leading to more efficient and accurate solutions for
nonlinear optimisation problems.

The remainder of this paper is organised as follows:
In section
2
, we introduce the linear quadratic optimal control
problem that forms the basis of this work. We derive the optimality
conditions and prove well-posedness, showing the equivalence between
solving the optimal control problem and a fourth-order singularly
perturbed equation. In section
3
, we describe the finite element
discretisation of the problem. In section
4
, we
derive global upper and local lower error bounds, which are then used
to develop an adaptive numerical scheme. Finally, in section
5
, we
demonstrate the algorithm’s convergence and present numerical
results that showcase the effectiveness of our approach.

2

Optimal Control Problem

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be an open bounded Lipschitz domain with polyhedral boundary
\partial\Omega
.
For
D\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
, where
n=1,2,3
, we denote the
standard Lebesgue spaces by
\operatorname{L}^{p}(D)
for
1\leq p\leq\infty
and Hilbert spaces
\operatorname{H}^{m}(D)
for
0\leq m\leq\infty
.
The
\operatorname{L}^{2}
inner product over

D
is denoted
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{D}
.
Additionally, when
D=\Omega
, we omit the
subscript and write
\|\cdot\|
and
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
to
represent the
\operatorname{L}^{2}(\Omega)
-norm and inner product respectively.
We let
\operatorname{H}^{1}_{0}(D)
be
functions in
\operatorname{H}^{1}(D)
with vanishing trace.

As a model problem, we consider a linear quadratic optimal control
problem (OCP) posed on
\Omega
. Let
u\in\operatorname{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
represent the state variable and
f\in\operatorname{L}^{2}(\Omega)
the control variable. We seek to find the optimal
state-control pair
(u,f)\in\operatorname{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\times\operatorname{L}^{2}(\Omega)
such that

(u,f)=\arg\min_{\!\left({v,g}\right)\in\operatorname{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\times\operatorname{L}^{2}(\Omega)}\left(\frac{1}{2}\|v-d\|^{2}+\frac{\alpha}{2}\|g\|^{2}\right),

(1)

subject to the constraint

a(v,\varphi):=\langle\nabla v,\nabla\varphi\rangle=\langle g,\varphi\rangle,\qquad\forall\varphi\in\operatorname{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega),

(2)

where
d\in\operatorname{L}^{2}(\Omega)
is the desired state and
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
is the regularisation
parameter, typically with
\alpha\ll 1
.

2.1

Optimality Conditions

Since this is a linear quadratic control problem, the optimal solution
can be obtained by solving the first-order optimality conditions,
commonly referred to as the Karush–Kuhn–Tucker (KKT) conditions

[
4
]
. For the problem described above, these conditions
are given weakly by: Find

\!\left({u,f,z}\right)\in\operatorname{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\times\operatorname{L}^{2}(\Omega)\times\operatorname{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega)

such that

\displaystyle\langle\nabla u,\nabla\varphi\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle f,\varphi\rangle,

\displaystyle\forall\varphi\in\operatorname{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega),

(3)

\displaystyle\langle\nabla z,\nabla\psi\rangle+\langle u,\psi\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle d,\psi\rangle,

\displaystyle\forall\psi\in\operatorname{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega),

(4)

\displaystyle\alpha\langle f,\tau\rangle-\langle z,\tau\rangle

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\forall\tau\in\operatorname{L}^{2}(\Omega),

(5)

where
z
is a Lagrange multiplier, referred to as the adjoint
variable. It can be shown that the solution
(u,f,z)
to this problem
coincides with the optimal state and control given
by (
1
)–(
2
).

Remark

(Equivalence of OCP to a singularly perturbed equation)

The optimal control problem presented in (
1
)
and (
2
) is equivalent to solving the singularly
perturbed biharmonic equation

\alpha\Delta^{2}u+u=d\quad\text{in }\Omega,

with the boundary conditions

u=\Delta u=0\quad\text{on }\partial\Omega.

2.2

Primal-Dual Formulation

It is convenient to reformulate equations
(
3
)–(
5
)
for the state variable
u
and the adjoint variable
z
. We seek
u,z\in\operatorname{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
such that

\displaystyle\langle\alpha\nabla u,\nabla\varphi\rangle-\langle z,\varphi\rangle

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\forall\varphi\in\operatorname{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega),

(6)

\displaystyle\langle\nabla z,\nabla\psi\rangle+\langle u,\psi\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle d,\psi\rangle,

\displaystyle\forall\psi\in\operatorname{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega).

(7)

Let
X:=\operatorname{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\times\operatorname{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
. We define the
bilinear form

\displaystyle b((u,z),(\varphi,\psi)):=\langle\alpha\nabla u,\nabla\varphi\rangle-\langle z,\varphi\rangle+\langle\nabla z,\nabla\psi\rangle+\langle u,\psi\rangle,

(8)

which allows us to reformulate (
6
)–(
7
) into the
following problem: Find
(u,z)\in X
such that

\displaystyle b((u,z),(\varphi,\psi))=\langle d,\psi\rangle=:L((\varphi,\psi)),\quad\forall(\varphi,\psi)\in X.

(9)

The energy norm associated with
b
is given by

\displaystyle\|(u,z)\|_{X}:=\left(\alpha\|\nabla u\|^{2}+\|\nabla z\|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

(10)

Theorem 2.1

(Well-posedness)

Let
d\in\operatorname{L}^{2}(\Omega)
be given. Then, there exists a unique
pair
(u,z)\in X
that solves (
9
).

Proof.

We show that the bilinear form defined in (
8
) is coercive
and bounded. To begin, note

\displaystyle b((u,z),(u,z))

\displaystyle=\alpha\|\nabla u\|^{2}+\|\nabla z\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\|(u,z)\|_{X}^{2},

hence the problem is coercive. For boundedness apply the
Cauchy-Schwarz inequality to (
8
) and use the Poincaré
inequality to see

\displaystyle b((u,z),(\varphi,\psi))

\displaystyle\leq C_{B}\|(u,z)\|_{X}\|(\varphi,\psi)\|_{X},

where

C_{B}=\left(1+C_{p}^{2}\max\left(1,\alpha^{-1}\right)\right),

and
C_{p}
is the Poincaré constant.

Since the bilinear form (
8
) is both coercive and bounded,
the Lax-Milgram theorem guarantees the existence of a unique solution to
(
9
).
∎

3

Finite Element Discretisation

Let
\mathcal{T}
be a conforming, shape-regular partition of
\Omega
into simplices (or quadrilaterals/hexahedra)
T
. The diameter of an element
T
is denoted by
h_{T}
, and
h=\max\{h_{T}:T\in\mathcal{T}\}
. We let
\mathcal{E}
denote the set of

(n-1)
-dimensional facets (edges in 2D, faces in 3D)
associated with the partition
\mathcal{T}
and let
h_{E}
denote the size of a facet
E
.

For each element
T\in\mathcal{T}
, we define the local finite element space
\mathcal{R}_{k}(T)
as

\mathcal{R}_{k}(T)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\mathbb{P}_{k}(T)&\textrm{if }T\textrm{ is a simplex},\\
\mathbb{Q}_{k}(T)&\textrm{if }T\textrm{ is an affine quadrilateral/hexahedron},\end{array}\right.

where
\mathbb{P}_{k}(T)
denotes the polynomials of total degree
k
on
T
, and
\mathbb{Q}_{k}(T)
denotes the polynomials of degree less than or equal to
k
in each variable, mapped from a reference element through an affine mapping.

Now, for
k\geq 1
, we define the global finite element space as

V_{h}:=\{v_{h}\in C^{0}(\Omega):v_{h}|_{T}\in\mathcal{R}_{k}(T)\;\forall\,T\in\mathcal{T}\}\cap\operatorname{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega).

We denote the patch of an element
T
by
\widetilde{T}
, where

\widetilde{T}:=\{S\in\mathcal{T}:\overline{T}\cap\overline{S}\neq\emptyset\}.

We also denote the pair of elements sharing a facet
E
by
\widehat{T}:=T^{+}\cup T^{-}
. We define the jump operator of a vector quantity
\boldsymbol{v}
over a facet by

[\![\boldsymbol{v}]\!]=\boldsymbol{v}\rvert_{T^{+}}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}_{T^{+}}+\boldsymbol{v}\rvert_{T^{-}}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}_{T^{-}}.

Finally, we define the space
X_{h}=(V_{h},V_{h})\subset X
.

We adopt an “optimise then discretise” approach and approximate
(
9
) over the finite element space. Additionally, we
replace the constant parameter
\alpha
with a piecewise function,
allowing its value to vary elementwise. This is the heart of our
adaptive regularisation strategy. Let
(u_{h},z_{h})\in X_{h}
be the
finite element approximation of
(u,z)
. We introduce the elementwise
discontinuous function
\alpha_{h}
, which we take to be piecewise constant
for exposition, where
\alpha_{h}|_{T}\in\mathbb{R}
and
\alpha\leq\alpha_{h}|_{T}\,\leq 1
for each element
T
. We define the bilinear form

\displaystyle b_{h}((u_{h},z_{h}),(\varphi_{h},\psi_{h})):=\langle\alpha_{h}\nabla u_{h},\nabla\varphi_{h}\rangle-\langle z_{h},\varphi_{h}\rangle+\langle\nabla z_{h},\nabla\psi_{h}\rangle+\langle u_{h},\psi_{h}\rangle.

(11)

We then seek a pair
(u_{h},z_{h})\in X_{h}
such that

\displaystyle b_{h}((u_{h},z_{h}),(\varphi_{h},\psi_{h}))=L((\varphi_{h},\psi_{h})),\quad\forall(\varphi_{h},\psi_{h})\in X_{h}.

(12)

The numerical scheme naturally induces an energy norm,

\displaystyle\left\|(u_{h},z_{h})\right\|_{X_{h}}:=\left(\left\|\alpha_{h}^{\frac{1}{2}}\nabla u_{h}\right\|^{2}+\left\|\nabla z_{h}\right\|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

(13)

Lemma 3.1

(Well-posedness of the FE approximation)

Let
d\in\operatorname{L}^{2}(\Omega)
be given data. Then, there exists a unique pair

(u_{h},z_{h})\in X_{h}
that solves (
12
).

Proof.

The proof follows the same steps as in Theorem
2.1
.
The bilinear form
b_{h}
(
11
) is coercive and bounded with respect to the norm
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{X_{h}}
defined in (
13
). The boundedness constant
C_{B}
is the same as on the continuum case,
noting that
\alpha_{h}\geq\alpha>0
.
∎

Theorem 3.2

(Galerkin Orthogonality)

Let
(u,z)
solve (
9
) and
(u_{h},z_{h})
solve (
12
). Then,

\displaystyle b\big((u-u_{h},z-z_{h}),(\varphi_{h},\psi_{h})\big)+\langle(\alpha-\alpha_{h})\nabla u_{h},\nabla\varphi_{h}\rangle=0,\quad\forall(\varphi_{h},\psi_{h})\in X_{h}.

(14)

Proof.

Since
X_{h}\subset X
, we have

b((u,z),(\varphi_{h},\psi_{h}))=L((\varphi_{h},\psi_{h})),\quad\forall(\varphi_{h},\psi_{h})\in X_{h}.

Subtracting (
12
) from this expression, we obtain

\displaystyle b((u,z),(\varphi_{h},\psi_{h}))-b_{h}((u_{h},z_{h}),(\varphi_{h},\psi_{h}))=0,\quad\forall(\varphi_{h},\psi_{h})\in X_{h}.

Noting that

b_{h}((u_{h},z_{h}),(\varphi_{h},\psi_{h}))=b((u_{h},z_{h}),(\varphi_{h},\psi_{h}))-\langle(\alpha-\alpha_{h})\nabla u_{h},\nabla\varphi_{h}\rangle

concludes the proof.
∎

Let the error between the continuous solution and the finite element
approximation be denoted by
\boldsymbol{e}:=(e_{u},e_{z})=(u-u_{h},z-z_{h})
.

Lemma 3.3

(Strang’s Lemma)

Let
(u,z)\in X
solve (
9
), and let
(u_{h},z_{h})
be
the finite element approximation solving (
12
). Then,
there exists a constant
C>0
such that

\displaystyle\norm{\vec{e}}_{X_{h}}\leq C\left(\inf_{(v_{h},w_{h})\in X_{h}}\|(u-v_{h},z-w_{h})\|_{X_{h}}+\norm{\alpha_h^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\alpha_h - \alpha)\nabla u}\right).

(15)

Proof.

To begin, note that for any
(v_{h},w_{h})\in X_{h}
, we have

\displaystyle\left\|(u_{h}-v_{h},z_{h}-w_{h})\right\|_{X_{h}}^{2}

\displaystyle=b_{h}\left((u_{h}-v_{h},z_{h}-w_{h}),(u_{h}-v_{h},z_{h}-w_{h})\right)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&=b_{h}\left((u_{h}-u,z_{h}-z),(u_{h}-v_{h},z_{h}-w_{h})\right)\\
&\quad+b_{h}\left((u-v_{h},z-w_{h}),(u_{h}-v_{h},z_{h}-w_{h})\right).\end{split}

Using the definitions of the bilinear forms (
8
) and (
11
), we obtain

\displaystyle\begin{split}\left\|(u_{h}-v_{h},z_{h}-w_{h})\right\|_{X_{h}}^{2}=&\langle(\alpha_{h}-\alpha)\nabla u,\nabla(u_{h}-v_{h})\rangle\\
&+b_{h}\left((u-v_{h},z-w_{h}),(u_{h}-v_{h},z_{h}-w_{h})\right).\end{split}

(16)

Applying the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality to the first term, we get

\displaystyle\langle(\alpha_{h}-\alpha)\nabla u,\nabla(u_{h}-v_{h})\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\alpha_{h}^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\alpha_{h}-\alpha)\nabla u,\alpha_{h}^{\frac{1}{2}}\nabla(u_{h}-v_{h})\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\alpha_{h}^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\alpha_{h}-\alpha)\nabla u\right\|\left\|\alpha_{h}^{\frac{1}{2}}\nabla(u_{h}-v_{h})\right\|.

By definition of
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{X_{h}}
(
13
)

\langle(\alpha_{h}-\alpha)\nabla u,\nabla(u_{h}-v_{h})\rangle\leq\left\|\alpha_{h}^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\alpha_{h}-\alpha)\nabla u\right\|\left\|(u_{h}-v_{h},z_{h}-w_{h})\right\|_{X_{h}}.

Using boundedness of
b_{h}
from Lemma
3.1
, we obtain from (
16
)

\displaystyle\left\|(u_{h}-v_{h},z_{h}-w_{h})\right\|_{X_{h}}\leq C_{B}\left\|(u-v_{h},z-w_{h})\right\|_{X_{h}}+\left\|\alpha_{h}^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\alpha_{h}-\alpha)\nabla u\right\|.

(17)

Making use of the triangle inequality

\displaystyle\left\|u-u_{h},z-z_{h}\right\|_{X_{h}}\leq\left\|u-v_{h},z-w_{h}\right\|_{X_{h}}+\left\|u_{h}-v_{h},z_{h}-w_{h}\right\|_{X_{h}}

(18)

and taking the infimum concludes the proof.
∎

Remark

(A priori coupling)

Lemma
3.3
suggests an appropriate a priori coupling between
\alpha_{h}
and the mesh size
h
. Indeed, for
u,z\in H^{r+1}(\Omega)
,

\inf_{(v_{h},w_{h})\in X_{h}}\|(u-v_{h},z-w_{h})\|_{X_{h}}\leq Ch^{\min(k,r)}\left(\left\|u\right\|_{H^{r+1}(\Omega)}+\left\|z\right\|_{H^{r+1}(\Omega)}\right),

for a mesh-size-independent constant
C
.
The error in the inconsistency term is controlled by

\norm{\alpha_h^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\alpha_h - \alpha)\nabla u}\leq\left\|\alpha_{h}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right\|_{\infty}\left\|\nabla u\right\|\left\|\alpha_{h}-\alpha\right\|.

Thus, in an a priori setting, one would ideally choose
\alpha_{h}
such that

\left\|\alpha_{h}-\alpha\right\|\sim h^{\min(k,r)}

to balance the approximation and inconsistency terms in Strang’s Lemma. For piecewise-linear basis functions
k=1
and
r\geq k
this implies the following a priori relationship between
\alpha_{h}
and
h

\left\|\alpha_{h}-\alpha\right\|\sim h,

to ensure consistent asymptotic behaviour.

Remark

(Target Approximation)

It is relevant to consider how well the discrete
state
u_{h}
approximates the desired target
d
. Such estimates have been considered in
[
22
,
21
]
for optimal control problems using energy (meaning
H^{-1}
-norm) regularisation where
\alpha_{h}|_{T}\sim h_{T}^{2}
.
As shown in
[
25
, Theorem 4.1]
, for an optimal control problem using an
\operatorname{L}^{2}
regularisation norm, regularisation error estimates of the
form

\left\|u-d\right\|\leq c\,\alpha^{\frac{s}{4}}\left\|d\right\|_{\operatorname{H}^{s}(\Omega)},\quad s\in[0,1],

can be derived when extra regularity is imposed on the target
d
. These estimates indicate that if
\alpha
is chosen too large, the target may never
be well resolved. For the problem under consideration in this work, assuming
d\in\operatorname{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
, one can build on the results above to show

\left\|u_{h}-d\right\|\leq\!\left({C_{1}(\alpha)h^{\min(k,r)}+C_{2}\alpha^{\frac{s}{4}}}\right)\left\|d\right\|_{\operatorname{H}^{s}(\Omega)},

although this estimate may be sharpened using appropriate duality arguments.

4

Residual Error Estimates

In this section, we show an a posteriori upper and lower bound on the
error. The section is primarily focussed on the proof of the following
two theorems:

Theorem 4.1

(Upper bound)

Let
(u_{h},z_{h})\in X_{h}
be the finite element solution to
(
12
). Let the residual and jump estimators be defined
by

\displaystyle\begin{split}R_{z}^{2}&:=h_{T}^{2}\norm{d + \Delta\fem{z} - \fem{u}}_{T}^{2}\ \ \qquad J_{z}^{2}:=h_{E}\norm{\jump{\fem{z}}}_{E}^{2},\\
R_{u}^{2}&:=h_{T}^{2}\alpha^{-1}\norm{\alpha\Delta\fem{u} + \fem{z}}_{T}^{2}\qquad J_{u}^{2}:=h_{E}\alpha^{-1}\norm{\alpha\jump{\fem{u}}}_{E}^{2}\end{split}

Let the regularisation and discretisation estimators respectively
be defined by

\displaystyle\eta_{\alpha,T}^{2}

\displaystyle:=\norm{\alpha^{-\frac{1}{2}} (\alpha_h - \alpha) \nabla\fem{u} }_{T}^{2},

(19)

\displaystyle\eta_{h,T}^{2}

\displaystyle:=R_{z}^{2}+R_{u}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\left(\sum_{E\in\mathcal{E}_{T}}J_{u}^{2}+J_{z}^{2}\right)

(20)

and define the global estimators as

\displaystyle\eta_{\alpha}^{2}:=\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}}\eta_{\alpha,T}^{2}\qquad\eta_{h}^{2}:=\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}}\eta_{h,T}^{2}.

Then, there exists a constant
\widetilde{C}
, independent of
h
,

\alpha
, and
\alpha_{h}
, such that

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{e}\|_{X}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\widetilde{C}\left(\eta_{\alpha}^{2}+\eta_{h}^{2}\right).

(21)

Remark

(A posteriori inconsistency)

Compare the structure of the a posteriori inconsistency term

\eta_{\alpha,T}
with the a priori version in Lemma

3.3
. The key idea behind the a posteriori methodology
we describe is to refine both
\alpha_{h}
and
h
in such a way that
the inconsistent regularisation is balanced with the approximation
error.

Theorem 4.2

(Lower bound)

Using the notation from Theorem
4.1
, let
d_{h}
be an
arbitrary function in
V_{h}
. We assume
u\in\operatorname{H}^{2}(\Omega)
and
z\in\operatorname{H}^{2}(\Omega)
.
Then, the following local lower error
bounds hold:

\displaystyle R_{z}^{2}+R_{u}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq c_{1}h_{T}^{2}\norm{d-d_h}_{T}^{2}+c_{2}\!\left({\alpha\left\|\nabla e_{u}\right\|_{T}^{2}+\left\|\nabla e_{z}\right\|_{T}^{2}}\right)+c_{3}h_{T}^{2}\alpha^{-1}\!\left({\alpha\norm{e_u}_{T}^{2}+\norm{e_z}_{T}^{2}}\right),

(22)

\displaystyle J_{u}^{2}+J_{z}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq c_{5}h_{E}^{2}\left\|d-d_{h}\right\|_{\widehat{T}}^{2}+c_{4}\!\left({\alpha\left\|\nabla e_{u}\right\|_{\widehat{T}}^{2}+\left\|\nabla e_{z}\right\|_{\widehat{T}}^{2}}\right)+c_{5}h_{E}^{2}\alpha^{-1}\!\left({\alpha\left\|e_{u}\right\|_{T}^{2}+\left\|e_{z}\right\|_{\widehat{T}}^{2}}\right),

(23)

\displaystyle\eta_{\alpha,T}

\displaystyle\leq\!\left({\frac{1}{\alpha}-1}\right)\left\|\alpha^{\frac{1}{2}}\nabla e_{u}\right\|_{T}+\alpha^{-\frac{1}{2}}\left\|\alpha_{h}^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\alpha_{h}-\alpha)\nabla u\right\|_{T}.

(24)

where
c_{1},\dots,c_{5}\in\mathbb{R}
are strictly positive constants independent of
h,\alpha,\alpha_{h}
.

Remark

(A priori inconsistency vs a posteriori)

Notice that the a posteriori inconsistency term

\eta_{\alpha,T}
is bounded above by its a priori analogue. This is inline with a posteriori studies for inconsistent methods, see for example
[
8
]
.

The proof of Theorem
4.1
and
4.2
requires the
following technical lemmata:

Lemma 4.3

(Scott-Zhang Quasi-Interpolant
[
29
, c.f. Theorem 4.1]
)

We denote the Scott-Zhang quasi-interpolant by
I_{h}:\operatorname{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\to V_{h}
. For all
T\in\mathcal{T}
and
E\in\mathcal{E}
, there exist
constants
C_{q},C_{r}>0
such that the following error estimates
hold:

\displaystyle\norm{v - I_h v}_{T}

\displaystyle\leq C_{q}h_{T}\norm{\nabla v }_{\widetilde{T}},

(25)

\displaystyle\norm{v - I_h v}_{E}

\displaystyle\leq C_{r}h_{E}^{\frac{1}{2}}\norm{\nabla v }_{\widehat{T}},

(26)

5

Numerical Results

We draw inspiration from adaptive finite element methods

[
6
]
to develop our numerical scheme. These
methods typically employ local a posteriori error estimators to
determine which elements require refinement. The process follows an
adaptive loop

\textsc{solve }\rightarrow\textsc{ estimate }\rightarrow\textsc{ mark }\rightarrow\textsc{ refine}.

First, the variational problem is solved using a finite element method
on a coarse mesh. Second, error estimators are evaluated on
each element. Third, the elements with the largest error estimators
are marked for refinement, based on a marking strategy. Common marking
strategies include Dörfler marking, maximum strategy, and
equidistribution strategy
[
26
]
. Finally, the
marked elements are refined using a bisection method. This loop is
repeated until the desired resolution is achieved.

We apply a similar scheme for adaptively selecting the regularisation
parameter. Starting with a relatively large initial regularisation
parameter,
\alpha_{h}^{0}\,\,\sim\mathcal{O}(1)\,
, we compute the estimator
\eta_{\alpha,T}

(
19
) for each
T\in\mathcal{T}
. The
regularisation parameter
\alpha_{h}
is then reduced for each element,
relative to the size of the estimator.
In places where the estimator is large,
\alpha_{h}
is reduced the most.
This loop continues until a desired error tolerance is achieved.

Algorithm
1
outlines the proposed
adaptive regularisation numerical scheme in conjunction with adaptive
mesh refinement.
This algorithm balances the regularisation and discretisation errors.
Two error tolerances
\texttt{tol}_{\alpha}\text{ and }\texttt{tol}_{h}
are provided for the regularisation error and discretisation error respectively. The adaptive method is executed until both estimators are below their error tolerances. Once the error tolerance for one of the quantities is reached, we stop the refinement process for this quantity and continue refining the other until its convergence criteria is satisfied.

We have chosen to use maximum strategy to mark the elements for adaptive mesh refinement. We exclude any elements where the inconsistency
error
\eta_{\alpha}
is larger than the discretisation error
\eta_{h}
.
As will be demonstrated in
subsection 5.1
Figure
3(c)
, the inconsistency error dominates over the discretisation error. Removing these elements from mesh refinement avoids refining the mesh
unnecessarily in places where the error could have been decreased by reducing
\alpha_{h}
.

As this is an optimal control problem, we are naturally interested in finding the control variable.
The finite element approximation of the control variable,
f_{h}
, can be reconstructed in a post-processing step after the adaptive scheme has concluded.
We choose to find
f_{h}
in the space of piecewise-linear polynomials, however, other choices of finite element spaces could be used so long as
f_{h}\in\operatorname{L}^{2}(\Omega)
.
We introduce the global finite element space

W_{h}:=\{w_{h}\in C^{0}(\Omega):w_{h}|_{T}\in\mathcal{R}_{1}(T)\;\forall\,T\in\mathcal{T}\}.

We discretise (
5
) and seek
f_{h}\in W_{h}
such that

\langle\alpha_{h}f_{h}-z_{h},\tau_{h}\rangle=0,\,\,\,\forall\tau_{h}\in W_{h}.

It is clear from this equation that the control is given by the
\alpha_{h}
-weighted
\operatorname{L}^{2}
-projection of
z_{h}/\alpha_{h}
into the space
W_{h}
, or equally, an interpolation operator could be chosen.

6

Conclusions

In this work, we propose a novel approach to regularising
PDE-constrained optimal control problems. These problems typically
require a small regularisation parameter, making them equivalent to
solving a singularly perturbed problem. Our method is based on an
inconsistent approximation of the singular perturbation, allowing
for a stable numerical approximation. Specifically, we replace the
small parameter with one that varies spatially and can be chosen
adaptively. We derive rigorous upper and lower a posteriori error
bounds for a finite element scheme based on this inconsistent
formulation and demonstrate the efficacy of the method through
numerical examples, including smooth targets with local support,
boundary layers and discontinuous targets. Our results highlight how
this approach balances regularisation and discretisation errors.

In this work, we restrict our analysis to a basic optimal
control problem: regularisation using the
\operatorname{L}^{2}
-norm of the
control, with the PDE constraint given by the Poisson equation. A
natural extension would be to consider weaker regularisation norms,
such as energy regularisation, as in

[
22
,
21
]
. Additionally,
investigating the case where the regularisation parameter varies
spatially,
\alpha=\alpha(x)
, is of particular interest. In this
setting, higher-order polynomial approximations of
\alpha_{h}
could
improve the convergence of the inconsistency term, motivated by
Lemma
3.3
.

Another avenue for future work is to
explore coupling the choice of
\alpha_{h}
to the mesh estimator

\eta_{h}
. While we opted to keep them independent to clarify their
individual roles, an alternative approach could involve adapting

\alpha_{h}
alongside mesh coarsening, analogous to techniques used
in evolutionary problems
[
20
, cf.]
.
