Role of sub-barrier dynamics in coherent and incoherent radiation during tunneling ionization
Michael Klaiber, Karen Z. Hatsagortsyan, Christoph H. Keitel

Abstract
Radiation during strong-field tunneling ionization is investigated. The spontaneous as well as the coherent component of the radiation are calculated and the role of the under-the-barrier dynamics is analyzed. The competition of the contribution of different mechanisms in different spectral regions is examined. Transition-like radiation (Brunel radiation) is dominated at low-frequencies, Thomson scattering – at the laser frequencies, and radiative recombination via the three-step process – at high-order harmonics. The under-the-barrier dynamics is shown to enhance the Brunel radiation for near-zero-frequencies in asymmetric laser pulses for spontaneous as well as for the coherent radiation.



I
Introduction

It is well known that tunneling ionization is accompanied by high-order harmonic generation (HHG) via the three-step model
Corkum 1993
, when the recombination of the ionized electron wave packet to the bound state at the recollision after the excursion in the laser field and gaining energy results in the emission of a high energy photon
Ferray et al. 1988
;
Lewenstein et al. 1994
;
Agostini and DiMauro 2004
. At the recollision, radiation may emerge also due to electron scattering, rather than recombination, yielding free-free HHG
Kohler et al. 2010
.
In the macroscopic picture, the ionization of a gas target results in the creation of an electron current after the interaction with a laser pulse, which can be a source of low frequency THz radiation, with the emitted frequency determined by the laser pulse envelope
Kim et al. 2008
;
Kim 2009
;
Babushkin et al. 2010a
;
Babushkin et al. 2010b
. The same mechanism can work also in the case of a single atom producing THz radiation in asymmetric laser pulses, e.g. in a two-color laser field
Babushkin et al. 2017
;
Babushkin et al. 2022
.

In contrast to the coherent radiation discussed above, emission of spontaneous low-order harmonic radiation during the bound-continuum transition with the origin similar to Brunel radiation
Brunel 1987
;
Brunel 1990
has been recently shown in Ref.
Milošević 2023
, with the typical frequency determined by the Keldysh time
Keldysh 1964
. Finally, harmonic radiation at tunneling ionization arises also from Thomson scattering of the laser wave off the ionized free electron wave packet, with higher harmonics than the fundamental one suppressed in the nonrelativistic regime.

Coherent radiation should be distinguished from the incoherent one of the same origin. For instance, the spontaneous recombination of a high-energy electron to an atomic bound state results in a photon emission of a random phase, which is an incoherent process
Sundaram and Milonni 1990
. However, when the recombining electron is released from the bound state and recombines into the same bound state driven by a laser field, the HHG is coherent with the phase determined by the coherent superposition of the ground state wave function with the ionization continuum wave packet. Similarly, the coherent part of radiation due to Compton scattering of a strong laser wave from an ionized electron wave packet
Krekora et al. 2002
;
Chowdhury et al. 2005
is phase-matched in forward direction, while the incoherent one has a broad angular distribution
Peatross et al. 2008
;
Corson and Peatross 2011
;
Tarbox et al. 2015
;
Ware et al. 2016
;
Pan and Gover 2019
. For the incoherent process, in contrast to the coherent one, the final state of the electron does not exist initially. The coherent radiation intensity scales as
\sim{\cal N}^{2}
with the number of emitters
{\cal N}
, due to the phase matching of the radiation, while for the incoherent radiation interference is suppressed and the total intensity scaling is
\sim{\cal N}

Marcuse 1971
. The coherent radiation can be calculated using the quantum mechanical expectation value of the current density within the classical radiation theory
Peatross et al. 2008
.

In this paper we investigate the role of the under-the-barrier dynamics for the spontaneous and coherent Brunel radiation during tunneling ionization. We analyze how the different mechanisms of the radiation during the strong field ionization are scaled in different spectral regions of emission and the feasibility of their observation in a coherent or incoherent form.

The harmonics emitted from a gas target is the result of a multi-atom contribution, and therefore, are dominated by the coherent radiation. For the observation of the incoherent radiation either a single atom experiment should be carried out (when each time a single atom existed in the interaction region), or the emission should be measured at a stray direction far from the phase-matching one
Ware et al. 2016
.

The structure of the paper is the following. In Sec.
II
the coherent radiation during tunneling ionization is calculated using the strong field approximation (SFA). Spontaneous emission at the tunneling ionization is discussed in Sec.
III
. We give estimations of radiation intensities in Sec.
IV
. Our conclusion is given in Sec.
V
.

We calculate coherent radiation emitted during tunneling ionization of a laser-driven hydrogenlike atom for the nonrelativistic regime. The dynamics is described by the Schrödinger equation
i\partial_{t}=H
with Hamiltonian

\displaystyle H=\mathbf{p}^{2}/2+V(r)+\mathbf{r}\cdot\mathbf{E}(t)+\mathbf{r}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{E}}(t),

(1)

where
V(\mathbf{r})=2\pi/\kappa\delta(\mathbf{r})(1+\partial_{r})
is the regularized atomic zero-range potential,
\mathbf{E}(t)
the laser electric field,
\hat{\mathbf{E}}(\mathbf{r},t)=\sum_{\mathbf{k}}\sqrt{\frac{2\pi\omega}{\cal{V}}}\hat{\mathbf{e}}\left(a_{\mathbf{k}}e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{r}-i\omega t}+a^{+}_{\mathbf{k}}e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{r}-i\omega t}\right)

is the field of the emitted photons in second quantization, with the creation and annihilation operators
a^{+}_{\mathbf{k}}
and
a_{\mathbf{k}}
, the emitted photon frequency
\omega=c|\mathbf{k}|
, the polarization unit vector
\hat{\mathbf{e}}
, the speed of light
c
, and the quantization volume
\cal{V}
. Atomic units are used throughout. In the nonrelativistic regime the dipole approximation for the emission is applied, neglecting the spatial dependence of the emission field
\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{r}\ll 1
.

With the electron wave function
\Psi
as a solution of the Schrödinger equation in the atomic potential and the laser electric field, the amplitude of the coherent photon emission process in the first-order perturbation will read

\displaystyle m_{\mathbf{k}}=-i\int ds\langle\Psi(s),1_{\mathbf{k}}|\mathbf{r}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{E}}(s)|\Psi(s),0_{\mathbf{k}}\rangle,

(2)

yielding the differential probability for the coherent photon emission:

\displaystyle\frac{dw}{d^{3}\mathbf{k}}=\left|m_{\mathbf{k}}\right|^{2}\frac{\cal{V}}{(2\pi)^{3}}.

(3)

Describing the interaction of the electron with the photon field in the velocity gauge:

\displaystyle m_{\mathbf{k}}=-i\int ds\langle\Psi(s),1_{\mathbf{k}}|\hat{\mathbf{A}}(s)\cdot\hat{\mathbf{p}}|\Psi(s),0_{\mathbf{k}}\rangle,

(4)

where
\hat{\mathbf{A}}=-\hat{\mathbf{E}}(t)/\omega
is the vector potential operator for the emitted photon.

After projecting on the photon initial and final states, and integrating over the emission direction, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{dw}{d\omega}=\frac{2\alpha\omega}{3\pi c^{2}}\left|\int ds\langle\Psi(s)\left|\hat{\mathbf{p}}\right|\Psi(s)\rangle\exp(i\omega s)\right|^{2}

(5)

with the fine-structure constant
\alpha
. The spectral intensity of Eq. (
5
) can be viewed as classical radiation of the quantum current
\mathbf{j}(\mathbf{r},t)=\Psi(\mathbf{r},t)^{*}\hat{\mathbf{p}}\Psi(\mathbf{r},t)
.
In this way the coherent part of the radiation is derived
Marcuse 1971
.

We describe the electron state
\Psi
in Eq. (
5
) during tunneling ionization via the strong-field approximation (SFA)
Becker et al. 2002
:

\displaystyle\Psi=\psi^{(0)}+\psi^{(1)}+\psi^{(2)},

(6)

where the zeroth-order term
\psi^{(0)}
coincides with the unperturbed bound state
\psi^{(a)}
in the atomic potential. In the first-order SFA

\displaystyle\psi^{(1)}=i\int^{s}dt\int d\mathbf{p}\psi^{f}_{\mathbf{p}}\langle\psi^{f}_{\mathbf{p}}(t)|\mathbf{r}\cdot\mathbf{E}(t)|\psi^{a}(t)\rangle

(7)

with the unperturbed continuum state
\psi^{f}
in the laser field, Volkov state
Wolkow 1935
,
and in the second-order SFA
Becker et al. 2002
:

\displaystyle\psi^{(2)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle i\int^{s}dr\int^{r}dt\int d\mathbf{p}\int d\mathbf{q}\psi^{f}_{\mathbf{p}}

(8)

\displaystyle\times\langle\psi^{f}_{\mathbf{p}}(r)|V|\psi^{f}_{\mathbf{q}}(r)\rangle\langle\psi^{f}_{\mathbf{q}}(t)|\mathbf{r}\cdot\mathbf{E}(t)|\psi^{a}(t)\rangle.

The wave function
\psi^{(1)}
describes the electron dynamics in the continuum, including the under-the-barrier dynamics, and the motion in the laser field after leaving the barrier. In Eq. (
5
) we can identify different probability currents
j(s)=j_{0,1}(s)+j_{1,0}(s)+j_{1,1}(s)
,
where

\displaystyle j_{0,1}(s)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle\psi^{(0)}(s)\left|\hat{p}_{x}\right|\psi^{(1)}(s)\rangle,

\displaystyle j_{1,0}(s)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle\psi^{(1)}(s)\left|\hat{p}_{x}\right|\psi^{(0)}(s)\rangle,

(9)

\displaystyle j_{1,1}(s)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle\psi^{(1)}(s)\left|\hat{p}_{x}\right|\psi^{(1)}(s)\rangle.

The second-order terms
j_{0,2}
,
j_{2,0}
describe the contribution to the radiation due to the under-the-barrier recollisions, which are usually small in the net radiation, see Sec.
III
. The radiation via the combination of
j_{01}
and
j_{10}
emerges due to the recombination to the bound state and, therefore, describes three-step HHG, including below-threshold harmonics. The radiation via
j_{11}
term includes the effect of the electron transition to the continuum and the continuum dynamics, which will be the main point of our study.

For the physical interpretation, we introduce an heuristic model similar to Babushkin
Babushkin et al. 2017
, to simulate the radiation only due to the continuum dynamics excluding the under-the-barrier contribution. In this model we evaluate the ionized electron probability current in the continuum, consisting of the tunnel ionized electron density via Amosov-Delone-Krainov (ADK) theory
Ammosov et al. 1986
;
Perelomov et al. 1966
multiplied by the laser driven velocity:

\displaystyle\mathbf{j}_{B}(s)=\int^{s}dt[\mathbf{A}(s)-\mathbf{A}(t)]\,\frac{\kappa^{2}}{2}\frac{E_{0}}{E_{a}}\exp\left[-\frac{2E_{a}}{3|\mathbf{E}(t)|}\right],

(10)

with the atomic field
E_{a}=\kappa^{3}
,
\kappa=\sqrt{2I_{p}}
, and the atomic ionization energy
I_{p}
, and calculate the corresponding spectrum via Eq. (
5
).

We have carried out the calculation of the coherent radiation spectra in the case of a symmetric and asymmetric few-cycle laser pulses defined by their displacement

\displaystyle{\bm{\alpha}}(t)=-\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{x}\frac{E_{0}}{\omega_{0}^{2}}\left[\cos(\omega_{0}t)+\mathscr{A}\sin(2\omega_{0}t)\right]\cos(\omega_{0}t/N)^{4},

(11)

where
\alpha^{\prime\prime}(t)=A^{\prime}(t)=-E(t)
,
E_{0}
and
\omega_{0}
are the laser field amplitude and frequency, respectively,
N=6
, and
{\cal A}
is the asymmetry parameter of the laser pulse,
{\cal A}=0
for the symmetric monochromatic pulse, and
{\cal A}=0.1
for the asymmetric two-color one. In the calculation of the different currents
j_{ij}
, the time integrations are performed numerically, while all other integrals are evaluated analytically in the applied 3D zero-range potential case, using
the corresponding ionization matrix element

\displaystyle\int d^{3}\mathbf{r}\psi_{\mathbf{p}}(\mathbf{r})^{*}x\psi^{a}(\mathbf{r})=-\frac{2i\sqrt{\kappa}p_{x}}{\pi\left(\kappa^{2}+p^{2}\right)^{2}},

(12)

where
\psi_{\mathbf{p}}
is a plane wave with momentum
\mathbf{p}
.
Further, boundary terms in the temporal Fourier transformation of the currents are treated as follows

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(s)ds=\frac{f(t_{i})^{2}}{f^{\prime}(t_{i})}+\int^{t_{f}}_{t_{i}}dsf(s)-\frac{f(t_{f})^{2}}{f^{\prime}(t_{f})}.

(13)

In the asymmetric pulse [Fig.

While

shows the near-zero-frequency spike in the spectrum, it however includes additionally the under-the-barrier effect, which enhances the emission in most of the spectral region (at

\tau\sim 200

III
Spontaneous radiation during tunneling ionization

The spontaneous radiation during tunneling ionization is calculated as a photon emission during the transition between the state of the tunneling electron
\Psi(t)
and the continuum state in the laser field:

\displaystyle dw=\left|m(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{p})\right|^{2}\frac{d^{3}\mathbf{k}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\frac{d^{3}\mathbf{p}}{(2\pi)^{3}},

(17)

where the emission amplitude for the photon momentum
\mathbf{k}
, and the electron final momentum
\mathbf{p}
reads

\displaystyle m(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{p})=\int dt\langle\psi_{\mathbf{p}}^{f}(t),1|\hat{\mathbf{p}}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{A}}(t)|\Psi(t),0\rangle,

(18)

with the SFA wave function of the tunneling electron
\Psi(t)
. Here
{\cal V}=1
is assumed.
Integrating over the final electron momenta we obtain:

\displaystyle\frac{dw}{d\omega}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{4\pi\omega^{2}}{c^{3}}\int d^{3}\mathbf{p}\left|\int dt\langle\psi_{\mathbf{p}}^{f}(t),1|\hat{\mathbf{p}}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{A}}(t)|\Psi(t),0\rangle\right|^{2}.

(19)

In the first-order SFA
\Psi=\psi^{(0)}(t)
and we obtain the photon emission probability which coincides with the result of Ref.
Milošević 2023
in velocity gauge.
In the second-order SFA calculation, the electron initial state during the photon emission process is
\Psi(t)=\psi^{(0)}(t)+\psi^{(1)}(t)
, which includes the continuum dynamics in the tunneling ionization process via
\psi^{(1)}(t)
. Comparing the first-order probability with the second-order one, we learn about the role of the continuum dynamics for the spontaneous radiation. We define the zero-order amplitude as

\displaystyle m_{0}(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{p})=\int dt\langle\psi_{\mathbf{p}}^{f}(t),1|\hat{\mathbf{p}}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{A}}(t)|\psi^{(0)}(t),0\rangle,

(20)

and the first-order one

\displaystyle m_{1}(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{p})=\int dt\langle\psi_{\mathbf{p}}^{f}(t),1|\hat{\mathbf{p}}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{A}}(t)|\psi^{(1)}(t),0\rangle,

(21)

as well as the total amplitude

\displaystyle m(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{p})=m_{0}(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{p})+m_{1}(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{p}).

(22)

We have carried out the calculation of the incoherent radiation spectra in the case of a symmetric and asymmetric few-cycle laser pulses defined above with
E_{0}=0.1
,
\omega_{0}=0.05
, and
N=2
.

Here again, in the calculation of the amplitudes
m_{i}
the time integrations are performed numerically. All other inner integrals are calculated analytically. Especially,
m_{1}
has the structure of a nested integral and is transformed via two partial integrations in the following way

\displaystyle m_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int ds\left[\mathbf{p}+\mathbf{A}(s)\right]\exp(i\omega s)\int^{s}dt\tilde{m}_{1}(t)

(23)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\int ds\left[\mathbf{p}+\mathbf{A}(s)\right]\tilde{m}_{1}(s)\int^{s}dt\exp(i\omega t)

\displaystyle+\int ds\mathbf{E}(s)\int^{s}dt\tilde{m}_{1}(t)\int^{s}dt\exp(i\omega t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\int ds\left[\mathbf{p}+\mathbf{A}(s)\right]\tilde{m}_{1}(s)\int^{s}dt\exp(i\omega t)

\displaystyle-\int ds\tilde{m}_{1}(s)\int^{s}dr\mathbf{E}(r)\int^{r}dt\exp(i\omega t).

All inner time integrals are analytical and the outer numerical integration one-dimensional over the full time-space.
The final momentum integration is also performed numerically. Further, boundary terms of the time integrals are treated like

\displaystyle\int dsf(s)=\int^{t_{f}+2i\gamma/\omega}_{t_{i}+2i\gamma/\omega}dsf(s).

(24)

The spectra for the spontaneous radiation are shown in Fig.

. The spectrum via
m_{0}(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{p})
is similar to the calculation of Ref.
Milošević 2023
, with the spectral width
\Delta\omega\sim 1/\tau_{K}
, determined by the Keldysh time
\tau_{K}=\kappa/E_{0}
. Up to
\omega<6
eV it is much smaller with respect to
m_{1}(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{p})
, which includes the continuum motion. In the near-zero-frequency region,
m_{1}(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{p})
has a spike similar to that in the coherent radiation. At larger frequencies
\omega>6
eV,
m_{0}(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{p})
and
m_{1}(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{p})
compensate each other. Thus, the amplitude
m_{0}(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{p})
does not describe the observable spectrum neither at low frequencies
\omega\rightarrow 0

IV
Estimation of radiation intensities

Let us estimate the relation between the coherent and incoherent Brunel radiation. We use the dipole radiation power
Landau and Lifshitz 1975
:

\displaystyle I=\frac{2\ddot{d}^{2}}{3c^{3}}.

(25)

Estimating the current from Eq. (
10
):

\displaystyle\dot{d}_{\rm coh}\sim(E_{0}/\omega_{0})w_{i}T,

(26)

where
w_{i}\sim(\kappa^{2}/2)(E_{a}/E_{0})\exp[-2E_{a}/(3E_{0})]
is the laser ionization rate,
T=2\pi/\omega_{0}
is the laser period. The probability of the coherent Brunel photon emission per laser period with the energy
\omega
is

\displaystyle w_{\rm coh}\sim\frac{I_{\rm coh}T}{\omega}\sim\frac{\alpha E_{0}^{2}}{c^{2}\omega}\left(\frac{\omega}{\omega_{0}}\right)^{2}w_{i}^{2}T^{3}\sim\alpha a_{0}^{2}\gamma^{-1}(w_{i}T)^{2},

(27)

where
a_{0}=E_{0}/c\omega_{0}
is the relativistic invariant field parameter.

Now let us estimate the probability of the spontaneous Brunel radiation again via the dipole radiation theory. For spontaneous radiation, we estimate the dipole moment derivative as

\displaystyle\dot{d}_{\rm sp}\sim\omega_{c}\ell_{c},

(28)

with the characteristic frequency
\omega_{c}\sim 1/\tau_{K}
, and
\ell_{c}\sim\kappa\tau_{K}
, with the Keldysh time
\tau_{K}=\kappa/E_{0}
, and the atomic velocity
\kappa
. Then, the spontaneous radiation power is

\displaystyle I_{\rm sp}\sim\frac{\alpha\omega_{c}^{4}\ell^{2}}{c^{2}}w_{i}T,

(29)

where we multiplied by the ionization probability because the dipole moment
d_{\rm sp}
is created during tunneling. Then, the probability of the spontaneous photon emission during tunneling ionization:

\displaystyle w_{\rm sp}\sim\frac{I_{\rm sp}T}{\omega_{c}}w_{i}T\sim\frac{\alpha\omega_{c}^{3}\ell^{2}}{c^{2}}w_{i}T^{2}\sim\alpha\frac{\kappa^{2}}{c^{2}}\gamma^{-1}w_{i}T,

(30)

with the Keldysh parameter
\gamma=\omega_{0}\tau_{K}
.

Thus, the ratio of the probabilities is

\displaystyle\frac{w_{\rm sp}}{w_{\rm coh}}\sim\frac{\kappa^{2}}{c^{2}}\frac{1}{a_{0}^{2}w_{i}T}

(31)

For
E_{0}=0.1
(the laser intensity
3.5\times 10^{14}
W/cm
2
),
E_{0}/E_{a}\sim 0.1
, and
a_{0}\sim 1.5\times 10^{-2}
,
\frac{I_{p}}{\omega_{0}}\sim 10
,
e^{-\frac{2E_{a}}{3E_{0}}}\sim 1.3\times 10^{-3}
,
w_{i}T\sim 10^{-2}
, we obtain
w_{\rm sp}/w_{\rm coh}\sim 10^{2}
, which is roughly in accordance with the spectra of Figs.
1
and
4
.

We may estimate also the spontaneous radiation per electron:
w_{\rm sp}^{(e)}\sim w_{\rm sp}/w_{i}T\sim(\alpha/\gamma)(\kappa/c)^{2}\sim 10^{-6}
, which indicates complications for the electron and photon coincidence measurement. The Brunel radiation scales as
1/\gamma
with the Keldysh parameter.

When the Brunel radiation is observed with a gas target, the coherent Brunel radiation will be dominating over the spontaneous one, if the target density is large enough:
{\cal N}=n{\cal V}>w_{\rm sp}/w_{\rm coh}
, with the particle number density
n
, the focal volume
{\cal V}\sim(\pi w^{2}/\lambda)(\pi w^{2})
, and the laser beam waist size
w
. With
w\sim 3\lambda
,
\lambda\sim 1\,\mu
, this will take place at a gas density
n>10^{11}
cm
-3
.

V
Conclusion

We have investigated the coherent and incoherent radiation during tunneling ionization. The coherent radiation in the near-zero-frequency (
\omega\sim 0.2
eV) domain is dominated by the continuum dynamics via the
j_{11}
term in the current. We have shown that due to the under-the-barrier motion the radiation can be enhanced. The nonadiabaticity increases the effect of the under-the-barrier dynamics at large Keldysh parameters, however generally decreasing the radiation yield.

The spontaneous radiation is not affected by the asymmetry of the driving laser pulse. The characteristic width of the spontaneous radiation is determined by the Keldysh time
\Delta\omega\sim 1/\tau_{K}
. There are two contributions into the spontaneous radiation: due to the direct transition during tunneling (with the amplitude
m_{0}
) investigated in Ref
Milošević 2023
, and due to additional motion in the continuum (with the amplitude
m_{1}
). At larger frequencies
\omega\gtrsim 1/\tau_{K}
,
m_{0}
and
m_{1}
