Network pharmacology and Mendelian randomization analysis to unravel the mechanism of Qingwen Baidu Decoction in sepsis treatment
Authors: Weina Wang, Yushu Ruan, Xiaobing Wu, Lili Li, Na Liu, Yuanyuan Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The aim of this study was to evaluate the potential components and targets of Qingwen Baidu Decoction (QBD) for sepsis by integrating a comprehensive strategy of network pharmacology and Mendelian randomization (MR). Systematically search the Traditional Chinese Medicine Systems Pharmacology, HERB, and Stitch databases for potential active ingredients and therapeutic targets of QBD. GeneCards, and DisGeNET databases were used to find the target of sepsis. We analyze the active ingredients and disease targets of drugs through network pharmacology, combined with pathway enrichment, protein–protein interaction and “Drug-component-target” network analysis to explore molecular mechanisms. MR was performed to assess the causal relationship between key targets and sepsis risk. The eQTLGen Consortium (sample sizes: 31,684) provided data on cis-expression quantitative trait loci (exposure). The summary data on sepsis (outcome) from genome-wide association studies were gathered from the IEU OpenGWAS project (sample sizes: 486,484). We built a target interaction network between the probable active ingredient targets of QBD and the therapeutic targets of sepsis. We identified 216 active ingredients in QBD, corresponding to 626 drug targets and 2575 sepsis-related targets, with 282 overlapping targets. Key active ingredients include quercetin, kaempferol, luteolin, wogonin, and naringenin. Protein–protein interaction network analysis revealed 17 hub targets. Gene ontology analysis demonstrated significant involvement in biological processes such as inflammatory response, while Kyoto encyclopedia of genes and genomes pathway analysis highlighted pathways including PI3K-Akt, TNF, HIF-1, and interleukin-17 signaling. MR analysis suggested a potential genetic association between STAT1 and increased sepsis risk (OR = 1.51, P = .024). STAT1 was predicted as a potential candidate target linking QBD to sepsis based on integrated network pharmacology and MR, but this finding requires experimental validation and does not establish therapeutic causality.

1. Introduction

Sepsis, a life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated host response to infection, remains a leading cause of global mortality, responsible for nearly 20% of all deaths worldwide.[1–3] Despite advancements in critical care, mortality rates persist stubbornly high, reflecting the limitation of current single-target therapies against its complex pathophysiology, which involves concurrent excessive inflammation and immunosuppression.[2,4]

Traditional Chinese medicine (TCM), with its holistic “multi-target, multi-pathway” philosophy, offers a promising complementary strategy. Qingwen Baidu Decoction (QBD), a classic TCM formula for treating febrile epidemics, has demonstrated broad anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory activities in modern studies and shows potential in sepsis management.[5–8] However, its precise molecular mechanisms remain unclear, hindering its wider scientific acceptance.

To bridge this gap, this study employs an integrated bioinformatics approach. We first utilize network pharmacology to systematically predict the active compounds of QBD, their potential protein targets, and the interaction network with sepsis-related genes, thereby constructing a comprehensive “herb-compound-target-disease” framework. Key signaling pathways will be identified through functional enrichment analysis. To strengthen the causal inference beyond correlation, we then apply Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis. Using genetic variants as instrumental variables from large-scale genome-wide association study (GWAS) data, we will assess the causal relationships between the core targets prioritized from the network and sepsis risk.

2. Methods

2.1. Screening of action targets of QBD

The active ingredients and potential targets of the ten constituent herbs of QBD (Zhimu, Gancao, Huanglian, Huangqin, Zhizi, Chishao, Mudanpi, Lianqiao, Xuanshen, and Jiegeng) were retrieved from the Traditional Chinese Medicine Systems Pharmacology Database (TCMSP, https://www.tcmsp-e.com) using the screening criteria of oral bioavailability ≥30% and drug-likeness ≥0.18.[9] The obtained target protein names were then converted to standard gene names via the UniProt database (https://www.uniprot.org). For the remaining herbs (Zhuye, Xijiao, and Shengdihuang) candidate compounds were identified from the HERB database (http://herb.ac.cn).[10] Their structures were acquired from PubChem using the corresponding PubChem IDs.[11] All candidate compounds underwent a two-step pharmacokinetic screening: first, a preliminary filter based on Lipinski’s Rule of 5 (molecular weight ≤ 500, miLogP ≤ 5, number of hydrogen bond donors ≤ 5, number of hydrogen bond acceptors ≤ 10); second, a refined evaluation using the SwissADME platform, where compounds meeting at least 3 of the established bioavailability rules (Lipinski, Ghose, Veber, Egan, and Muegge) were retained. The potential protein targets of these final active compounds were predicted using the SwissTargetPrediction database,[12] with all targets having a probability score >0 being collected. As the primary component of Shigao is calcium sulfate dihydrate (CaSO4·2H2O), its potential interacting targets were predicted using the STITCH database (http://stitch.embl.de).

2.2. Screening of sepsis targets

Disease-associated genes were retrieved by searching the keyword “Sepsis” in the GeneCards database (http://www.genecards.org)[13] and the DisGeNET database (http://www.disgenet.org).[14] In the GeneCards database, the median of the relevance scores was applied as the screening criterion. The target lists from both databases were combined, and duplicates were removed to obtain the final sepsis-associated targets.

2.3. Gene ontology (GO) and Kyoto encyclopedia of genes and genomes (KEGG) analysis of potential targets

Enrichment analysis, including GO and KEGG pathway analysis, was performed on the intersecting targets using R software with the Bioconductor package. The results were filtered with a significance threshold of P ≤ .05 and q ≤ 0.05. The top 10 terms from each of the 3 GO categories (biological process; cellular component, CC; molecular function) and the top 30 KEGG pathways were selected for visualization.

2.4. Protein–protein interaction (PPI) network and core targets

The intersecting targets were submitted to the STRING database (https://cn.string-db.org/) for PPI analysis.[15] The analysis was performed under the “Multiple proteins” mode with the organism set to Homo sapiens. An interaction score of ≥0.9 (highest confidence) was set as the threshold, and disconnected nodes were hidden from the network, while all other parameters remained default. The resulting interaction data was downloaded as a TSV file and imported into Cytoscape software (version 3.7.1) to construct the PPI network.[16] Topological analysis of the network was conducted using the cytoNCA tool, through which 6 centrality measures were calculated for each node: betweenness, closeness, degree, eigenvector, local average connectivity, and network centrality. Targets whose values for all 6 parameters exceeded the respective median were retained. This screening process was performed iteratively for 2 consecutive rounds, ultimately identifying the most topologically central targets within the PPI network derived from the intersecting gene set.

2.5. “QBD-active ingredients-targets-Sepsis” network

The network of interactions between the active components of QBD and the disease target genes was constructed in Cytoscape by integrating data on the herbal constituents, their active compounds, and the identified disease targets.

2.6. MR analysis

In accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology using MR (STROBE-MR) guidelines, a two-sample MR analysis was performed to investigate the causal relationships between the 17 core genes identified from PPI screening and sepsis. GWAS data for the 17 core genes were obtained from the eQTLGen database, while GWAS data for sepsis were sourced from the IEU Open GWAS database. Instrumental variables (IVs) were selected according to the following criteria: single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) with a genome-wide significance level (P < 5 × 10−8) were included; to ensure independence, linkage disequilibrium clumping was applied (window size: 10 Mb, linkage disequilibrium threshold: R2 < 0.001) using the European population as a reference; only IVs with strong instrument strength (F-statistic > 10) were retained[17]; and SNPs associated with the outcome (P < 5 × 10−8) were excluded. The remaining SNPs after these steps were used as final IVs. Causal estimates were primarily derived using the inverse-variance weighted or Wald ratio methods, supplemented by the weighted median and MR-Egger regression approaches. Sensitivity analyses were conducted to assess pleiotropy and heterogeneity: horizontal pleiotropy was evaluated using the MR-Egger intercept test (P > .05 indicating no significant pleiotropy), and heterogeneity was assessed using Cochran Q statistic (P > .05 indicating no significant heterogeneity).[18–20] The MR pleiotropy residual sum and outlier method was also applied to detect and remove outlier SNPs. All analyses were performed using the TwoSampleMR package (version 0.5.6) in R (version 4.2.3). A causal association was considered statistically significant if P < .05.

3. Results

3.1. Active ingredients and targets of QBD

A total of 216 active ingredients of QBD were identified and screened through the TCMSP, HERB, PubChem, SwissADME, and STITCH databases. Using the TCMSP and SwissTargetPrediction databases, 4743 potential targets of these active ingredients were predicted. After removing duplicates, 626 unique targets were obtained.

3.2. Sepsis targets and intersection targets with QBD

A total of 3648 sepsis-related targets were retrieved from the GeneCards database. After screening using the median Relevance score of 0.7747, 1824 targets remained. An additional 1453 targets were obtained from the DisGeNET database. Merging and deduplicating these lists yielded 2575 unique sepsis-related targets. The intersection between the active ingredient targets of QBD and these sepsis-related targets was then identified, resulting in 282 common targets (Fig. 1).

3.3. GO and KEGG enrichment analysis

GO enrichment analysis yielded 3105 biological process terms, primarily involving response to lipopolysaccharide, positive regulation of cytokine production, and regulation of inflammatory response; 126 CC terms, mainly including membrane raft, membrane microdomain, and vesicle lumen; and 255 molecular function terms, predominantly related to nuclear receptor activity, ligand-activated transcription factor activity, and cytokine receptor binding. The top 10 significantly enriched terms are detailed in Figure 2. KEGG enrichment analysis identified 192 pathways. Key pathways closely associated with sepsis included the PI3K-Akt, TNF, HIF-1, and interleukin-17 (IL-17) signaling pathways. The top 30 enriched pathways are presented in Figure 3.

GO analysis of intersection targets. GO = gene ontology.

KEGG analysis of intersection targets. KEGG = Kyoto encyclopedia of genes and genomes.

3.4. PPI network and core targets

The PPI network was constructed using the STRING database, resulting in an interaction network comprising 238 nodes and 938 edges, representing protein–protein interactions. Within Cytoscape, the network underwent 2 consecutive rounds of screening based on the median values of 6 topological parameters: betweenness, closeness, degree, eigenvector, local average connectivity, and network centrality. This process ultimately identified 17 core targets (Fig. 4).

The PPI network of Qingwen Baidu Decoction and sepsis targets. PPI = protein–protein interaction.

3.5. Network topology diagram of “QBD-active ingredients-targets-Sepsis”

The 282 common targets were imported into Cytoscape to construct a “Herbs-Components-Targets-Sepsis” network diagram (Fig. 4), which comprised 494 nodes and 2002 edges. Based on topological analysis of the network, herbal components with relatively high degree values were screened, including Gancao (85), Xijiao (43), and Shengdihuang (41). Active compounds with high degree values included quercetin (103), kaempferol (41), luteolin (41), wogonin (31), and naringenin (26). These compounds with high connectivity may represent potential active ingredients of QBD for the treatment of sepsis(Fig. 5).

The network of the QBD–compounds–targets–sepsis. QBD = Qingwen Baidu Decoction.

3.6. MR results

Inverse-variance weighted analysis indicated that genetically predicted STAT1 (OR = 1.51, 95% CI = 1.02–2.22, P = .024) was associated with an increased risk of sepsis.

No evidence of pleiotropy or heterogeneity was detected, and sensitivity analysis confirmed the robustness of these findings (Fig. 6).

4. Discussion

QBD, as a classic TCM formula, holds a significant position in the treatment of infectious diseases. This study is the first to integrate network pharmacology and MR to systematically elucidate the potential molecular mechanisms through which this formula acts against sepsis, revealing its characteristic “multi-component, multi-target, multi-pathway” mode of action.

In this study, 216 active ingredients and 626 potential targets of QBD were screened using databases such as TCMSP and HERB. A total of 2575 sepsis-related targets were identified, yielding 282 intersection targets. Through PPI network and MR analysis, STAT1 was ultimately confirmed as a core target with a significant causal association with sepsis risk. Multiple studies have indicated that STAT1 increases the risk of sepsis. Li et al demonstrated that endothelial cell-derived CCL7 promotes macrophage polarization and exacerbates sepsis-induced acute lung injury via CCR1-mediated STAT1 succinylation.[21] Zhu et al demonstrated that p-STAT1 and IRF1 protein levels were significantly elevated, while GPX4 levels were significantly decreased in sepsis patients compared to healthy controls. These expression changes were consistent with those observed in in vivo and in vitro sepsis models, confirming a close association between the STAT1/IRF1/GPX4 axis and clinical sepsis.[22] Collectively, these findings underscore the critical role of STAT1 in sepsis pathogenesis and validate its potential as a key therapeutic target for QBD. These experimental findings suggest that STAT1 plays a critical role in sepsis pathogenesis. However, our study only provides computational predictions (network pharmacology) and genetic association data (MR) linking STAT1 to sepsis. We do not provide direct evidence that QBD or its active compounds modulate STAT1. Therefore, STAT1 should be considered a prioritized candidate target for future experimental studies testing whether QBD actually engages this target.

QBD exerts its therapeutic effects on sepsis by modulating multiple key signaling pathways. KEGG enrichment analysis indicates that pathways such as PI3K-Akt, HIF-1, and IL-17 are collectively involved in its mechanism of action. The PI3K/Akt pathway plays a pivotal role in sepsis pathogenesis. Activation of this pathway in sepsis models has been shown to reverse detrimental changes, including the upregulation of pro-inflammatory cytokines and the downregulation of antiapoptotic proteins, thereby exerting significant anti-inflammatory and antiapoptotic effects.[23] HIF-1α is a critical regulator in sepsis, governing the host’s adaptive response to hypoxia and influencing the release of inflammatory mediators, as well as the balance between pro-inflammatory and immune-tolerant states.[23] Its activation also modulates crucial pathophysiological processes like mitochondrial function and apoptosis. Concurrently, IL-17, a key pro-inflammatory cytokine, is central to the inflammatory response and immune regulation. Signal transduction is the linchpin of IL-17’s function, mediating its role in both maintaining homeostasis and driving the pathogenesis and progression of sepsis.[24,25]

QBD likely ameliorates sepsis by simultaneously modulating these interconnected pathways: it activates the PI3K/Akt pathway to suppress inflammation and apoptosis, while also potentially regulating the HIF-1α-mediated hypoxic response and the IL-17-driven inflammatory cascade. This multi-targeted approach highlights the complex and synergistic mechanisms underlying QBD’s therapeutic efficacy.

A primary limitation of this study is that the identified interactions, including those involving STAT1, remain database-derived rather than biologically validated. Because our target identification relies heavily on resources like TCMSP, HERB, and SwissTargetPrediction, the findings inherently include predicted and indirect associations. This introduces unavoidable uncertainty, potential database bias, and a risk of false positives. Without experimental validation, we cannot confirm that QBD directly modulates these targets in a biological system. Inherent gaps in these databases may also mean certain active ingredients were missed, and the effects of drug metabolites were not captured. Additionally, while MR suggests a genetic causal link for STAT1, the specific mechanisms by which QBD might regulate this target require verification through cellular or animal experiments. Finally, the reliance on European-population GWAS data may limit the generalizability of these findings to other ethnic groups. Moreover, the clinical relevance and translational applicability of our findings remain unclear. This study did not evaluate the pharmacokinetics or bioavailability of QBD’s active compounds, nor did it determine whether therapeutic concentrations can be achieved in vivo. Without such pharmacological characterization and direct clinical evidence in human sepsis, any potential therapeutic implications derived from our computational predictions are speculative and require further preclinical and clinical investigation.

In conclusion, this hypothesis-generating study integrates network pharmacology and MR to prioritize candidate targets and pathways of QBD in sepsis. Our computational analysis suggests that QBD may exert its effects through modulating key sepsis-related pathways, such as PI3K-Akt, TNF, HIF-1, and IL-17 signaling, with STAT1 emerging as a prioritized causal target from these networks. However, these findings are entirely derived from predictive databases and genetic association data, which cannot establish therapeutic mechanisms or clinical efficacy. Therefore, this study provides a prioritized set of hypotheses and a theoretical foundation for future experimental research, including in vivo and in vitro validation, rather than immediate clinical application.
