Socioeconomic determinants of practical adaptive capacity and food security under climate stress in Western Iran
Authors: Mohammad Reza Pakravan-Charvadeh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Climate change poses significant risks to smallholder farmers’ livelihoods and food security, particularly in vulnerable agricultural regions. This study examines how multidimensional adaptive capacity is associated with household food insecurity among small-scale farmers and determines the socio-economic factors associated with adaptation strategies in western Iran. Using survey data from 296 farming households, a composite multidimensional adaptation indicator was developed, and structural equation modeling was applied to analyze relationships among adaptation dimensions and food security outcomes. The results show that higher adaptive capacity is associated with lower levels of household food insecurity. Among the adaptation dimensions, institutional and knowledge-based strategies and livelihood diversification exhibit the strongest associations with food security, while other strategies contribute to a lesser extent. In addition, key socioeconomic factors, including education, asset ownership, and access to information, are positively associated with adaptation intensity, while reliance on external credit is negatively associated. These findings highlight the importance of considering adaptation as a multidimensional process and provide empirical evidence to support targeted interventions aimed at strengthening farmers’ resilience under climate stress.

Introduction

Climate change has emerged as one of the most pressing global challenges of the twenty-first century, exerting profound impacts on ecosystems, livelihoods, and food production systems1,2. Its effects are particularly severe in the agricultural sector, where shifting temperature regimes, erratic rainfall, and the growing frequency of extreme weather events threaten the stability of food supply chains and rural livelihoods3; Rashidi et al.4,5. Agriculture remains the backbone of most developing economies, and millions of smallholder farmers depend directly on it for their sustenance and income6. Due to the severe issue of climate change, smallholders are often resource-constrained when it comes to adapting to it (Jabbar, Liu, et al., 2023). Without effective adaptation strategies, agricultural productivity in vulnerable regions may decline by up to 30% by 2050, intensifying the risks of hunger and poverty Rashidi et al.4.

In response to these challenges, the global community has placed increasing emphasis on the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), particularly SDG 2 (Zero Hunger) and SDG 137,8; United Nations9. These goals highlight the urgent need to enhance food security while strengthening resilience and adaptive capacity to climate-related hazards United Nations9. Achieving these targets requires not only technological innovation and mitigation strategies but also a deeper understanding of how farmers adapt to changing climatic conditions within their socioeconomic and institutional contexts10–12.

Adaptation plays a central role in reducing the adverse impacts of climate variability11. It encompasses a wide range of actions that collectively enhance resilience and sustainability (Rashidi et al.4. Yet, despite growing recognition of its importance, empirical evidence on the multidimensional nature of adaptation remains fragmented and context-specific. A critical gap in the literature lies in the lack of multidimensional approaches to assess the intensity and diversity of adaptation. Many studies employ binary or unidimensional indicators that fail to account for the complexity of farmers’ adaptive behavior, and there are few studies that focus on multidimensional adaptive capacity13; Waseem & Rana, 2025). Moreover, socioeconomic and institutional determinants are often overlooked or examined independently rather than within an integrated analytical framework. This limits our understanding of how these factors jointly shape adaptation decisions and their subsequent influence on food security outcomes. Another shortcoming of the existing research is the weak linkage between adaptation and food security outcomes. While it is widely assumed that adaptive practices enhance food availability and stability, empirical validation of this relationship remains limited (Rashidi et al.4, particularly in developing regions where both adaptation and food insecurity are most pronounced. On the other hand, although the literature on climate adaptation has expanded substantially in recent years, the majority of previous studies have concentrated narrowly on the application or adoption of adaptation strategies14–18, rather than the broader enabling conditions that determine their effectiveness. In reality, successful adaptation requires more than the mere implementation of specific practices such as crop diversification, irrigation, or soil conservation (Rashidi et al.4. As a result, much of the existing evidence captures what farmers do to adapt, but not whether they possess the capacity, opportunity, or institutional backing to sustain those actions over time1,4.

The present study contributes to closing these gaps by developing a multidimensional indicator to measure farmers’ adaptation strategies against climate change and by linking it directly to food security outcomes. This research integrates the availability and accessibility of essential resources and inputs, the knowledge and awareness required to select and apply strategies appropriately, and the advisory and institutional support necessary to facilitate effective implementation. These dimensions were systematically incorporated into the construction of the multidimensional indicator to better reflect the holistic nature of adaptive capacity. Also, the study addresses key gaps in climate adaptation literature by moving beyond simple adoption counts, providing a fully integrated framework linking adaptation to food security, and offering one of the first statistically validated multidimensional adaptation indicators in a Global South context. In addition, the study contributes to the literature by empirically examining both direct and indirect relationships among adaptation constructs and food insecurity outcomes using SEM and multidimensional index construction approaches in a climate-vulnerable agricultural context. By embedding these enabling conditions within the analytical framework, the study provides more accurate and meaningful estimates of the factors influencing adaptation intensity against climate change and their implications for food security. This approach enables a comprehensive understanding of how socioeconomic and demographic characteristics and resource endowments interact to shape farmers’ adaptive responses. By addressing both the intensity and breadth of adaptation, the analysis delivers deeper insights into the mechanisms that sustain resilience and food security at the household level. Theoretical and conceptual frameworks

The present study is guided by an Integrated Adaptive Capacity and Food Security Framework (IACFSF), which synthesizes multiple theoretical foundations to explain the multidimensional nature of farmers’ climate adaptation and its relationship with household food security. This integrative framework draws from the Sustainable Livelihoods Framework (SLF), Adaptive Capacity Theory (ACT), and Food and Nutrition Technical Assistance (FANTA). Collectively, these perspectives provide a comprehensive explanation of how socioeconomic, environmental, institutional, and informational determinants interact to shape farmers’ adaptive responses and food security outcomes under climate stress. The SLF posits that households rely on some key forms of capital to build and sustain their livelihoods19,20. These assets determine the capacity to cope with shocks such as climate variability. In this study, the SLF underpins the multidimensional indicator by linking resource endowments (land, water, income, education, institutional access) to adaptive capacity. The framework justifies why farmers with greater access to natural and institutional capital are more capable of adopting sustainable and diversified adaptation strategies that enhance food security. Building on the ACT, the framework conceptualizes adaptation as a dynamic behavioral process reflecting the ability of individuals and systems to anticipate, respond to, and recover from climate-related shocks21,22. Adaptive capacity is operationalized through six multidimensional constructs, including agronomic and crop management strategies (ACMS), soil and land management strategies (SLMS), water resource management strategies (WRMS), livestock-oriented strategies (LSOS), economic and livelihood diversification strategies (ELDS), and institutional and knowledge management strategies (IKMS) (Rashidi et al.4. This theory justifies examining adaptation as an integrated capacity rather than isolated actions, emphasizing learning, innovation, and flexibility as the core of resilience. This theoretical lens supports the ACMS and SLMS constructs, emphasizing ecological balance, soil fertility, and integrated pest management as mechanisms to sustain productivity and reduce vulnerability. It also justifies the inclusion of water conservation practices, irrigation management and optimization, and equitable water distribution as key components of adaptive capacity that safeguard food production under increasing water stress. In this framework, the ELDS and LSOS dimensions capture households’ pursuit of off-farm employment, asset accumulation, and livestock production as means of reducing vulnerability and maintaining food access during environmental shocks. Finally, the FANTA Project is a USAID-funded initiative designed to improve food security and nutrition policies by developing standardized measurement indicators like the HFIAS23. The IACFSF therefore integrates these theoretical strands into a cohesive explanatory model. Agroecological, land management, and natural capital principles enhance the environmental resilience of production systems, and livelihood diversification and water management buffer economic and ecological risks, while institutional and informational mechanisms act as enabling drivers across all constructs.

To clarify how variables are operationalized within the study, the IACFSF systematically links seven distinct theoretical perspectives to specific adaptation constructs. The SLF is applied to explain how households manage assets and income to buffer economic shocks, while ACT is utilized to frame the role of information and institutional support as enabling factors. Collectively, these interconnected pathways strengthen adaptive capacity and contribute to household food security, aligning with SDGs 1, 2, and 13, which advocate for poverty reduction, zero hunger, and climate resilience. Materials and methods

Study location

This study was conducted in Khorramabad, the administrative center of Lorestan Province in western Iran. The study area was selected due to its high vulnerability to climate variability, strong dependence on smallholder agriculture, and diversity of agroecological conditions, which together provide a suitable context for examining multidimensional adaptation and food security. According to the most recent data released by the Statistical Center of Iran in 2020, Khorramabad hosts a population of approximately 373,000 inhabitants. The city lies in a mountainous basin, positioned at an elevation of about 1,148 m above sea level, and is geographically framed by several neighboring urban areas: Selseleh to the north, Borujerd toward the northeast, Dorud in the east, Aligudarz to the southeast, Andimeshk to the west, and Dowreh-Poldokhtar in the southwest (see Fig. 2). Located at the coordinates 33.48° N latitude and 48.35° E longitude, Khorramabad occupies a strategic position along the Zagros Mountain range, giving it both climatic diversity and agricultural significance within the province.

Agriculture in the region is primarily rain-fed and highly sensitive to fluctuations in temperature and precipitation, making farming households particularly exposed to climate-related risks. In recent years, the area has experienced increasing climate variability, including irregular rainfall patterns, rising temperatures, and more frequent drought events24. These environmental shifts have placed considerable pressure on the local agricultural systems, reducing crop productivity and water availability, and consequently motivating a number of farming households to consider migration as a coping or adaptive measure24. According to the Köppen–Geiger climate classification, Khorramabad features a hot-summer Mediterranean climate (Csa), characterized by warm, dry summers and mild, wet winters. The city stands at an altitude of approximately 1,148 m above sea level, receiving an average of 511 mm of precipitation annually, with a mean yearly temperature of about 17.2 °C25. The presence of heterogeneous farming systems, including crop production, livestock activities, and mixed livelihood strategies, further makes the area appropriate for analyzing multiple dimensions of adaptation, such as agronomic, water management, and livelihood diversification strategies. In addition, variation in access to institutional support and resources across households allows for a meaningful assessment of socioeconomic and institutional determinants within the analytical framework.

Participant, sampling technique, and recruitment

The study used a two-phase sampling design combining stratified random selection of villages with purposive and snowball sampling of households. The target population was rural farming households involved in crop or livestock production. Using G*Power with an assumed small–moderate effect size, 5% significance level, 90% power, and 32 predictors, the minimum required sample was calculated as 268 and adjusted to 296 after accounting for design effects. Ultimately, 296 valid household surveys were obtained. In the first stage, the study area (Khorramabad County) was first stratified into five agroecological zones. Villages were then selected randomly from these strata using Probability Proportional to Size (PPS). This ensured a representative spatial distribution of the sample. In the second stage, we used purposive snowball sampling to identify eligible farming households through key informants and respondent referrals (extension agents and village leaders), an approach suitable for remote areas and for capturing variation in climate adaptation practices. This approach was used to access dispersed farming populations and capture variation in adaptation practices, particularly in areas where formal household lists were unavailable or outdated. Due to the integrated use of purposive and snowball techniques, it was not possible to determine the exact proportion of households derived from each method. However, both approaches were applied within each randomly selected village to ensure diversity in farm size, livelihood strategies, and socioeconomic characteristics. Several measures were taken to mitigate potential selection bias. These included stratification across agroecological zones, inclusion of multiple villages within each stratum, and deliberate efforts to capture heterogeneity among respondents. Despite these efforts, the use of non-probability sampling at the household level may limit the statistical representativeness of the sample. Non-response was reduced through repeated visits, with replacements identified through local networks. Although snowballing introduces some representativeness limitations, the hybrid design balanced statistical rigor with practical field conditions and ensured access to knowledgeable farmers. Enumerators received extensive training, a 10% subsample of interviews was back-checked, and written informed consent was obtained. The final dataset of 296 households satisfied power requirements and provided a reliable foundation for econometric analysis of climate adaptation and food security determinants. All methods were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations, including the ethical standards of the institutional research committee and the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Assessment instrument and questionnaire

Data were collected using a structured questionnaire developed to capture comprehensive information on farmers’ climate adaptation practices, household food security, and socioeconomic characteristics. The instrument was designed after an extensive review of prior adaptation and food security studies and was refined through expert consultation with agricultural extension officers and rural development specialists. It consisted of three primary components: (1) adaptation strategy practices, (2) a food security assessment tool, and (3) socioeconomic and demographic characteristics. The questionnaire was administered through face-to-face interviews using a computer-assisted survey tool to ensure data accuracy and completeness. A pilot test was conducted in a village within the Khorramabad region that was not included in the final sample. The purpose of the pilot test was to verify the face validity and cultural relevance of the questionnaire items, assess the internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha) of the scales, and estimate the average time required for face-to-face interviews. Based on feedback from 30 households in this pilot phase, minor linguistic adjustments were made to improve clarity before the full-scale survey was launched.

Multidimensional adaptation indicator

The adaptation practices section captured the multidimensional nature of farmers’ responses to climate variability and was structured around six constructs: ACMS, SLMS, WRMS, LSOS, ELDS, and IKMS. The construction of the index is grounded in latent variable theory, which conceptualizes adaptive capacity as an unobserved construct manifested through different but interrelated adaptation strategies. Each construct consists of several practices, including ACMS with 11 practices, SLMS with four practices, WRMS with five practices, LSOS with four practices, ELDS with seven practices, and IKMS with five practices. For each adaptation practice, respondents were asked to respond to five key dimensions, knowledge, application, availability, accessibility, and advisory support, using Likert-type scales. Some strategies of this study were replaced with local strategies based on the current situation of Khorramabad city26–30; Rashidi et al.4. The five dimensions of each practice included the following:

The knowledge dimension assessed the respondent’s awareness and understanding of the strategy (1 = no knowledge, 5 = very high knowledge).The application dimension measured the extent to which the strategy had been practiced in the past three agricultural seasons (1 = never applied, 5 = always applied).The availability dimension captured whether the inputs or resources required for the strategy were locally available (1 = not available at all, 5 = fully available).The accessibility dimension reflected the farmer’s ability to obtain or afford the necessary inputs and services (1 = not accessible, 5 = highly accessible).The advisory support dimension measured the degree of technical guidance, extension contact, or peer advice received regarding that strategy (1 = no support, 5 = strong advisory support).

The use of Likert scaling enabled the measurement of both the intensity and perceived feasibility of adaptation practices, allowing for the construction of weighted composite indices that represent multidimensional adaptation capacity. This structure provided a more refined understanding of the behavioral, structural, and institutional enablers and constraints that shape adaptation behavior among small-scale farmers. To synthesize these five dimensions into a unified measure, a hierarchical factor analysis approach was employed. First, Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA) was utilized to determine the relative importance (weights) of each dimension for specific adaptation practices, which were then aggregated into item-level composite scores. Second, a second-level factor analysis was performed to aggregate these item scores into construct-specific indices for the six adaptation constructs. Finally, a higher-order factor analysis integrated these six construct scores to create the final MAI. This weighted approach ensured that the MAI accurately captures the overall intensity of adaptation strategies adopted by each household. To assess the robustness of the index construction, alternative specifications were examined. These included equal-weight aggregation of dimensions and simplified averaging methods. The results remained qualitatively consistent across these specifications, indicating that the findings are not sensitive to the specific weighting approach adopted. While the index construction involves multiple steps, this approach allows for a more accurate representation of the multidimensional and latent nature of adaptive capacity. Detailed computational procedures are provided in Appendix A to ensure transparency and reproducibility. Construct validity was verified through the Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) test and Bartlett’s Test of Sphericity before each factor extraction, ensuring sampling adequacy (KMO > 0.70). Internal consistency was confirmed using Cronbach’s alpha (α ≥ 0.70). Factor loadings greater than 0.50 were considered significant and retained for computation.

Food security assessment

Household food insecurity was assessed using the HFIAS developed by the Food and Nutrition Technical Assistance (FANTA) Project. This HFIAS has been recently validated in Iran31,32. The HFIAS consists of nine standardized questions that capture the occurrence and frequency of food insecurity experiences over the previous four weeks (Rashidi et al.4. Each question addresses a progressively severe condition of food access, ranging from anxiety about food supply to experiences of hunger. Responses were recorded using standard frequency categories (never, rarely, sometimes, often) and coded according to established HFIAS guidelines. Households were categorized into standard food insecurity levels, including mildly, moderately, or severely food insecure33. Table 1 shows the structure of HFIAS questions. Data collection was conducted during autumn, corresponding to the post-harvest season.

Structural equation model (SEM)

To evaluate the complex relationships between the latent constructs of adaptive capacity and food security and to test the hypotheses derived from the Integrated Adaptive Capacity and Food Security Framework (IACFSF), this study employed Structural Equation Modeling (SEM). SEM was chosen as the primary analytical technique because it allows for the simultaneous estimation of multiple interrelated dependence relationships and accounts for measurement error within the latent variables34. Specifically, Partial Least Squares SEM (PLS-SEM) was performed using Smart-PLS 3 software. The analysis followed a two-step analytical approach. First, the measurement model was assessed to ensure the validity and reliability of the constructs11. This involved examining internal consistency reliability using Cronbach’s alpha and composite reliability (CR) and convergent validity using the average variance extracted (AVE)35. Discriminant validity was verified using the Fornell-Larcker criterion to confirm that distinct constructs were measuring separate phenomena36. Once the measurement model was confirmed to be robust, the structural model was evaluated to test the hypothesized causal pathways37. The significance of the path coefficients was determined using a bootstrapping procedure with 5000 subsamples, which generates standard errors and t-statistics to estimate the precision of the estimates.

Statistical technique

The Tobit regression model, also known as the censored regression model and developed by James Tobin (1958) [41], was employed to estimate the determinants of multiple adaptation strategies’ indices (ACMS, SLMS, WRMS, LSOS, ELDS, IKMS, and MULTI). This model is appropriate when the dependent variable is censored at a specific limit (e.g., zero), meaning that some observations fall below or above a threshold [42]. In this context, standard OLS may generate biased and inconsistent estimates because it does not account for the censored distribution of the dependent variable [43]. The Tobit model jointly estimates the probability of being censored and the intensity of adaptation among uncensored observations, making it appropriate for modeling adaptation indices with mass points at the boundary [44]. The model was estimated using maximum likelihood estimation (MLE). The suitability of the Tobit model was assessed by comparing it with OLS and by examining the extent of censoring in the dependent variables. The adaptation indices contained a non-negligible number of left-censored observations at zero, ranging from 26 observations for the overall MAI to 53 observations for LSOS. Results

Descriptive analysis

Table A2 presents summary statistics for the demographic, socioeconomic, institutional, and asset-related variables included in the analysis. The average age of household heads and mothers is in the late forties. The mean household size is approximately four members. Continuous variables such as income, housing characteristics, and landholding size exhibit substantial variation, as reflected in their standard deviations. Most households are male-headed and married. Literacy rates among household heads and mothers are relatively high. The majority of respondents report agriculture as their main occupation. Access to financial and institutional variables shows variation across the sample. A smaller proportion of the households report adequate access to credit funds, savings, or formal institutional support. Most households report home ownership, while fewer indicate participation in training activities or the use of social media. Asset-related variables, including landholding size, livestock ownership, and equipment ownership, also vary across households. These variables correspond to the key explanatory factors used in the regression and structural models. Tobit regression model

The Tobit regression results presented in Table 10 summarize the determinants of farmers’ climate adaptation strategies across six multidimensional constructs as well as the MAI. Among demographic characteristics, sex of the household head showed a significant and positive relationship with adaptation in most constructs (ACMS, WRMS, ELDS, and MULTI). The age of the mother exhibited a consistent positive association with adaptation across several constructs. Educational variables also played a vital role: mother’s literacy was significantly and positively associated with adaptation intensity in ACMS, WRMS, LSOS, and IKMS. Conversely, higher education among children had a negative association in some models (notably IKMS and MULTI). Economic variables demonstrated mixed associations. Main occupation in agriculture had a strong and positive influence across all models (p < 0.05), confirming that households engaged primarily in farming are more likely to adopt adaptation strategies. In contrast, access to credit funds showed a significant negative relationship across all constructs. Institutional support was significant and negative in some models (SLMS and IKMS), while homeownership emerged as a positive determinant in nearly all adaptation constructs. Within housing and asset-related variables, home area and horticultural or cultivation area showed positive associations, underscoring the role of landholding size in facilitating resource-based adaptation strategies. Sprayer ownership was also positively associated with adaptation (notably in ACMS, ELDS, and MULTI). Social and informational factors were particularly important: social media use was highly significant and positive across all constructs (p < 0.01). In contrast, participation in nutrition courses did not exhibit a statistically significant association with adaptation.

The diagnostic tests confirmed the robustness and validity of the Tobit estimations. The F-statistics were significant at the 1% level across all models, confirming overall model significance. Likelihood ratio χ² tests also supported the superiority of the Tobit model over OLS. In addition to likelihood-ratio comparisons, the relative suitability of the Tobit specification was evaluated using Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), and pseudo-R2 statistics. Across all adaptation models, Tobit specifications consistently exhibited lower AIC and BIC values than corresponding OLS models, indicating improved fit for the censored adaptation indices. To assess potential overfitting, sensitivity analyses were conducted using reduced-variable specifications and stepwise screening procedures. The estimated coefficient signs and significance levels remained substantively stable across alternative specifications, supporting the robustness of the main findings. Furthermore, all VIF values remained below conservative thresholds (1.85–1.96), suggesting no serious multicollinearity among explanatory variables. Breusch–Pagan and White’s tests suggested no evidence of heteroskedasticity, while the Jarque–Bera and Shapiro–Wilk tests confirmed the normality of residuals. The RESET test further validated the functional form of the models, supporting their correct specification.

Discussion

This study provides comprehensive insights into how small-scale farmers adapt to climate change within a multidimensional framework that links adaptation intensity to food security outcomes. The analysis reveals that climate adaptation is not a uniform process but a multidimensional construct encompassing agronomic, soil, water, livelihood, livestock, and institutional strategies. Each of these dimensions contributes uniquely to overall adaptive capacity and, consequently, to the stability of household food systems. This integrative approach enhances understanding beyond conventional unidimensional frameworks, which often overlook the complex interrelations among resource access, socioeconomic constraints, and institutional mechanisms [45]. Some studies confirmed that resilience capacity serves to improve food security status and decrease the proneness of households to suffering from food insecurity in the presence of shocks [46]. Most of the hypothesized relationships were supported, although several pathways were weak or statistically non-significant.

Practical adaptive capacity and food security

The results demonstrate that institutional and knowledge-based adaptation dimensions exerted the most significant association with food security, underscoring the critical role of access to extension services, advisory networks, and knowledge dissemination in shaping adaptive behavior. These findings reinforce the central tenets of the KTIDT [47], which posits that the spread of innovation is contingent upon effective information exchange, social learning, and local capacity development. Farmers with greater access to institutional support were more likely to integrate complex adaptation strategies, confirming the pivotal enabling role of institutional capital in enhancing adaptive responses [11,48].

Economic and livelihood diversification strategies exhibit a strong inverse association with food insecurity, indicating that households engaging in diversified income and livelihood activities experience greater food stability. This finding reinforces the LDT [49], which posits that diversification buffers income fluctuations and mitigates agricultural risk. In rural agrarian systems, diversified households can rely on multiple revenue streams to smooth consumption during climate shocks [50]. This result aligns with evidence from sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia, where diversification has consistently reduced vulnerability to crop failure and food shortage [51,52]. By broadening income sources, ELDS is associated with higher purchasing power and reduces dependence on single-season harvests, translating into higher resilience against market and climatic variability. Thus, economic diversification functions as a key pathway through which adaptive capacity strengthens household food security ([11]; Rashidi et al., [4]).

Livestock-oriented strategies also show a negative and statistically significant association with food insecurity, suggesting that households maintaining diversified livestock systems benefit from improved nutritional access and economic flexibility. Livestock represents a crucial component of natural and financial capital under the SLF, serving both as an income source and as a safety net during climatic shocks. Households with greater livestock holdings are better able to withstand production losses, as animals can be sold or used for home consumption during periods of food shortage [53]. Empirical evidence from arid and semi-arid regions of Africa shows that mixed crop–livestock systems enhance adaptive efficiency by recycling nutrients, improving soil fertility, and stabilizing household caloric intake [54,55]. In this study, the significant role of LSOS underscores that adaptation is not merely about adopting new crops or practices but about maintaining integrated production systems that contribute directly to both income and dietary adequacy.

Soil and land management strategies demonstrate a similarly an inverse and significant association with food insecurity, emphasizing that conservation-oriented practices play a central role in sustaining agricultural productivity under climate pressure. These findings are theoretically grounded in the SLMT and AT theories, both of which view healthy soil systems as foundational for long-term resilience. SLMS enhances water retention, nutrient recycling, and overall soil fertility, reducing yield variability and ensuring consistent household food supplies [56,57]. The positive contribution of SLMS to food security aligns with prior research indicating that farmers who invest in soil conservation and organic matter management not only secure higher yields but also maintain greater adaptive flexibility during climatic extremes ([58]; G. A. Kumar et al., [59]. These outcomes highlight that environmental stewardship and food security are mutually reinforcing dimensions of adaptive capacity [11]. The interdependence between SLMS and other constructs, particularly water management and livelihood diversification, underscores that ecological and economic adaptation pathways are mutually reinforcing rather than substitutive.

When comparing these constructs, a clear pattern emerges: economic diversification primarily strengthens the income stability and purchasing power dimensions of food security, livestock-oriented strategies enhance nutritional adequacy and asset liquidity, and soil and land management strategies secure the production and environmental sustainability dimensions. Their collective significance indicates that climate adaptation and food security are functionally intertwined, economic, ecological, and productive resilience must coexist for households to attain sustained food access and utilization [6,60]. The study’s results therefore validate the integrated approach underpinning the multidimensional adaptation framework, where livelihood diversification, resource management, and ecological stewardship act as synergistic components of climate resilience.

The multidimensional indicator developed in this study allowed for a more nuanced understanding of adaptation by capturing not only the presence of adaptation strategies but also their depth and interconnectivity. Traditional single-dimensional measures tend to overlook critical aspects such as access, availability, and advisory support. The normalization approach and weighting scheme used in this study offered a balanced representation of adaptation constructs, revealing that households often exhibit heterogeneity across adaptation dimensions. These results underscore the necessity of holistic assessment frameworks that recognize adaptation as a process encompassing behavioral, institutional, and ecological transformations [61].

Practical adaptive capacity and socio-economic determinants

Human and household characteristics emerge as crucial drivers of adaptive engagement. Male-headed households consistently exhibit higher adaptation scores across ACMS, ELDS, and MAI, reflecting gender-linked access to productive resources and decision autonomy (Rashidi et al., [4]). The mother’s education level shows a robust positive association across most constructs, particularly ACMS, WRMS, and IKMS, indicating that maternal literacy is associated with household knowledge assimilation and strategy implementation. Conversely, higher education among children has a weak or negative association in the IKMS model, suggesting that educated youth may pursue nonfarm employment, reducing direct engagement in adaptation activities. The age of the mother also contributes positively to several adaptation dimensions, underscoring experiential learning in coping with environmental variability (Rashidi et al., [4]).

Economic and occupational characteristics show that households whose main occupation is agriculture report higher adaptation scores in constructs related to land and resource management (ACMS, IKMS). Farmers who depend primarily on agriculture as their main source of livelihood face direct exposure to climatic variability and production risks. This exposure creates a stronger incentive to adopt and maintain adaptive practices that sustain productivity, soil fertility, and income stability. In contrast, households that rely more heavily on non-farm or diversified income sources may not perceive the same level of urgency or necessity to invest in agricultural adaptation. Some studies contended that farmers with non-farm participation were less vulnerable to poverty and consumed more food (Jabbar, Wei, et al., 2023). Also, the opportunity cost of inaction is much higher for those whose livelihood depends entirely on farming. Non-farm households can compensate for poor yields through other income sources, but farm-dependent households must rely on adaptive agricultural and institutional practices to ensure survival. Therefore, their adaptation responses are typically more intense, diversified, and structured, are associated with higher scores in constructs such as ACMS and IKMS. Household income is not statistically significant, implying that resource endowments and knowledge pathways, rather than income alone, shape adaptation intensity. In contrast, access to credit funds repeatedly exhibits a negative and significant association across nearly all constructs (WRMS, LSOS, ELDS, IKMS, and MAI). Although Kandel et al., [62] contend that credit enables farmers to invest in agriculture to improve food security, The negative association between access to credit and several adaptation dimensions in this study should be interpreted cautiously and within the socioeconomic context of the study area. Although access to financial resources is often expected to facilitate adaptation, rural credit in many developing-country settings may also reflect financial vulnerability and reactive borrowing behavior. In the study region, households frequently rely on informal or short-term borrowing to smooth consumption, repay previous debts, or cope with production losses during climatic shocks rather than to finance long-term adaptation investments (Rashidi et al., 2021; Rashidi et al., [4]; [6]). Consequently, households with greater credit dependence may simultaneously exhibit lower adaptive capacity and higher economic stress. Meanwhile, home ownership demonstrates a strong and consistent positive association, suggesting that tenure security encourages investment in land improvement and other sustainable practices [63].

Institutional and informational factors play a pivotal enabling role. Social media use emerges as a uniformly positive and significant determinant across nearly all constructs, highlighting its role as a vehicle for knowledge exchange, peer learning, and information dissemination [64,65]. Such findings reflect the growing importance of digital communication platforms in bridging knowledge gaps in rural areas, especially in the dissemination of timely climate information and sustainable farming techniques [40]. Similarly, institutional support is positive in sign though less consistently significant, reflecting its partial but important role in linking farmers to advisory services and programs. These findings underscore that adaptation depends not only on available technologies but also on the social infrastructure that supports information flows ([66]; Rashidi et al., [4]).

Land and resource variables such as home area, horticultural area, and sprayer ownership are positively associated with adaptation, indicating that physical and productive assets enhance farmers’ capacity to implement land and crop management measures. Larger home or land areas signal greater wealth and flexibility to experiment with adaptive options [6]. The home area is significantly associated with sustainable land management dimension, for instance, while economic and livelihood diversification and agronomic strategies respond strongly to resource variables and basic equipment ownership such as sprayers. These findings reinforce the linkage between physical capital and adaptive intensity [11,67,68]. The findings related to land and housing characteristics reveal that land tenure security and home ownership correlate positively with adaptive capacity. Secure property rights and stable living conditions encourage long-term investments in soil conservation, agroforestry, and water management practices [68]. When farmers perceive ownership and control over land resources, they are more likely to commit to soil fertility management and climate-resilient land use practices.

When comparing across constructs, a clear hierarchy of association emerges. Constructs directly related to land and resource management (ACMS, SLMS, WRMS) are primarily driven by physical assets, education, and occupation. Livelihood and institutional constructs (ELDS, IKMS) depend more on social, informational, and institutional factors, emphasizing human and relational capital. Notably, access to credit maintains a consistent negative coefficient across all constructs, while social media use, home ownership, and agricultural occupation maintain positive associations, demonstrating their foundational role in adaptive capacity. The composite MAI reflects these aggregate dynamics: households with greater educational capital, land ownership, professional farming engagement, and information connectivity exhibit higher adaptation scores, whereas those relying heavily on external credit show weaker adaptation intensity. Collectively, these results affirm that adaptation is multidimensional and cumulative, requiring concurrent access to assets, information, and institutional support to sustain resilience and food security.

Discussion of unsupported hypothesis

Hypothesis 9 (H9), which proposed that ELDS would positively associate with SLMS, was not supported by the data. This result contradicts the assumption that off-farm income necessarily provides the capital required to invest in sustainable land practices. The lack of a significant association suggests that in the study context, livelihood diversification may function primarily as a distress-coping mechanism to meet immediate household needs rather than as a strategy for capital accumulation. Consequently, households may prioritize allocating their limited labor resources toward securing off-farm income at the expense of labor-intensive soil management activities. Additionally, this finding implies that the barriers to adopting sustainable land management practices may be more technical or knowledge-based than purely financial; therefore, increased economic capital alone, without complementary advisory support or technical training, is insufficient to drive the adoption of these specific strategies.

Strengths, limitations, and directions for future research

Strengths of the study

This research offers several significant methodological and conceptual contributions. First, it develops MAI that integrates five dimensions, knowledge, application, availability, accessibility, and advisory support, across six adaptation constructs. This provides a more holistic assessment of adaptive capacity compared with conventional adoption-based or binary indicators. Second, the study employs an analytical strategy, combining exploratory and confirmatory factor analysis, Tobit regression, and structural equation modeling, which allows both the measurement of latent constructs and the identification of direct and indirect pathways linking adaptation to food security. Third, the study is among the few in developing-country contexts to explicitly link enabling conditions (institutional access, knowledge systems, livelihood resources) with food security outcomes, producing policy-relevant insights for resilience-building. Finally, this study provides empirical evidence on the multidimensional nature of climate adaptation and its association with household food security among smallholder farmers in western Iran. The findings contribute to the growing literature examining adaptation as a multidimensional and institutionally embedded process rather than a simple adoption outcome.

Limitations

Despite its strengths, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the use of a hybrid stratified–purposive–snowball sampling approach, while necessary due to the absence of comprehensive and up-to-date sampling frames in the rural study area, introduces potential selection bias. Because the recruitment of subsequent respondents relied on referrals from initial participants, there is a risk that the sample may not be fully representative of the wider farming population, as individuals within the same social networks often share similar characteristics. Consequently, while the sample size ensures sufficient statistical power for structural equation modeling, caution is advised when generalizing the findings to the broader population of small-scale farmers in Iran. Second, the cross-sectional design restricts the study’s ability to infer causal relationships or capture dynamic changes in adaptation behavior over time. Longitudinal data could provide stronger evidence of temporal adaptation pathways. Third, the study focuses on one region of western Iran; therefore, the results may not be generalizable to areas with different agroecological, institutional, or socioeconomic conditions. Additionally, unobserved factors such as farmers’ risk preferences, climate shock histories, psychological determinants, or market exposure were not included in the model but may associated with both adaptation intensity and food security. Although the SEM framework allows examination of theoretically structured relationships among latent constructs, the estimated paths should be interpreted as statistical associations rather than evidence of causal directionality. The possibility of endogeneity cannot be fully excluded. Some explanatory variables may be jointly determined with adaptation behavior or food security status, while omitted contextual variables, such as local governance quality, market integration, climatic heterogeneity, or social network structures, may simultaneously influence multiple constructs within the model. Although robustness checks and alternative specifications produced substantively similar results, residual endogeneity concerns may remain. the multidimensional adaptation indicator (MAI) is subject to potential measurement uncertainty. While the index construction followed latent variable and composite indicator approaches commonly used in adaptation research, the weighting structure derived from factor loadings may still be sensitive to sample composition and construct specification. Alternative weighting procedures generated broadly similar patterns; however, measurement error associated with latent constructs and indicator aggregation cannot be completely eliminated. Also, the relatively large number of predictors and structural pathways increases the possibility of model complexity, overfitting, and spurious statistical significance. Although multicollinearity diagnostics indicated acceptable VIF values and sensitivity analyses supported coefficient stability across alternative model specifications, the inclusion of multiple correlated socioeconomic and institutional variables may still introduce specification uncertainty. Accordingly, the findings should be interpreted as context-specific associative patterns rather than universally generalizable or causal relationships. Another key limitation of this study relates to the measurement of food security using the HFIAS, which is based on a four-week recall period. While this approach captures recent household experiences, it may be influenced by short-term fluctuations in food access and consumption. In agricultural contexts such as the study area, food security conditions often vary across seasons, particularly between post-harvest and lean periods. Therefore, the reported prevalence of food insecurity reflects conditions during the survey period rather than long-term or annual food security status. Finally, the use of self-reported recall data introduces the possibility of recall bias, as respondents may underreport or overreport food insecurity experiences. Although the standardized structure of the HFIAS is designed to minimize such errors, this limitation cannot be fully eliminated. These factors should be considered when interpreting the results, and future research would benefit from longitudinal or panel data that capture seasonal dynamics and reduce reliance on recall-based measures.

Suggestions for future research

Future research would benefit from methodological approaches that strengthen causal inference and reduce specification uncertainty. Longitudinal and panel-data studies could help capture the temporal dynamics of adaptation and food security while addressing reverse associations between adaptation capacity and household welfare outcomes. Similarly, quasi-experimental and instrumental-variable approaches may provide stronger identification strategies for evaluating the direction and magnitude of adaptation–food security relationships under climate stress. Future studies could also compare alternative modeling approaches, including generalized structural equation models, fractional response models, multilevel frameworks, and Bayesian estimation techniques, to assess the robustness of adaptation indicators and structural relationships across specifications. Additional robustness analyses examining alternative weighting procedures, latent construct configurations, and variable selection strategies would further strengthen multidimensional adaptation measurement. Also, future research could incorporate objective environmental and climatic data, including drought indices, satellite-based vegetation data, rainfall anomalies to triangulate self-reported adaptation behavior. Additionally, expanding the analytical scope to include behavioral determinants such as risk perception, trust in institutions, climate knowledge, or social norms may improve understanding of adoption barriers and decision-making processes. Comparative studies across multiple provinces or countries could help test the external validity of the multidimensional adaptation indicator and reveal context-specific versus universal drivers of adaptation. Future models may also examine market-related and value-chain factors, which play critical roles in shaping access to inputs, technology uptake, and income diversification opportunities. Finally, integrating systems-based or agent-based simulations could help predict how different adaptation pathways influence long-term resilience under various climate scenarios.

Policy and practical recommendations

The findings of this study reveal that effective climate resilience requires moving beyond generic recommendations to targeted, actionable interventions that address the specific determinants of the MAI and food security. Given that ELDS demonstrated the strongest inverse association with food insecurity, policies must actively support off-farm income generation through specific actions such as establishing rural enterprise funds that provide low-interest microloans for non-agricultural startups. This should be complemented by the creation of vocational training hubs in rural districts to build skills in high-demand sectors, thereby reducing reliance on climate-sensitive rain-fed agriculture alone. Furthermore, since the study identified IKMS as critical drivers, extension services should transition from top-down methods to a champion farmer model where successful adopters of resilient practices are incentivized to host demonstration plots and train neighboring farmers. To address the inverse association between reliance on external credit and adaptive performance, financial policies must shift from standard production loans to climate-adaptation grants and weather-indexed insurance products that offer grace periods aligned with harvest cycles, removing the immediate pressure of repayment during climate shocks. Additionally, to leverage the significant association of SLMS, the government should implement green input voucher schemes that subsidize the cost of organic fertilizers and conservation equipment, or tie access to general subsidies to the adoption of specific conservation practices. Finally, addressing the gap in advisory support requires investment in mobile-based advisory services (m-Agri) to deliver localized weather forecasts and market data via SMS, ensuring that even remote farmers have the timely information necessary to make adaptive decisions. By implementing these specific, evidence-based measures, stakeholders can effectively enhance the adaptive capacity and food security of small-scale farming households.

Linking local adaptation to regional, national, and global climate policy

The findings demonstrate that farm-level adaptation is not an isolated behavioral response but part of a broader multilevel governance system. The relationships between institutional access, knowledge management, and livelihood diversification indicate that local adaptive practices are deeply embedded within policy environments that shape access to resources, information, and markets. When aggregated across communities, these localized adaptive strategies contribute to regional food security and ecological sustainability, directly aligning with national priorities articulated in frameworks such as the National Adaptation Plan (NAP), Nationally Determined Contributions (NDCs), and the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs 1, 2, and 13).

Strengthening vertical policy integration, linking grassroots adaptation initiatives to regional and national decision-making, can enhance policy coherence and ensure that bottom-up innovations inform top-down planning. This can be achieved through targeted extension systems, inclusive financing instruments, participatory land-use planning, and decentralized governance that empowers local institutions.

At the global level, local adaptation efforts form the building blocks of collective resilience under the Paris Agreement’s Global Goal on Adaptation (GGA) and the UNFCCC Global Stocktake process. Empirical insights from smallholder systems, such as those presented in this study, provide the evidence base needed to operationalize these global commitments, bridging local realities with international adaptation targets. Moreover, aligning national adaptation reporting with global frameworks, through metrics that capture multidimensional adaptive capacity and food security linkages, can strengthen transparency, comparability, and accountability in global adaptation tracking. Integrating local innovations into regional and global knowledge-sharing platforms can also foster cross-country learning and the scaling up of contextually effective adaptation models, thereby reinforcing the global agenda for climate-resilient and food-secure development pathways.

Conclusion

This study examined the association between multidimensional adaptive capacity and household food insecurity among smallholder farmers in western Iran using an integrated analytical framework combining multidimensional adaptation measurement, SEM, and Tobit regression analysis. The findings indicate that several adaptation dimensions, particularly institutional and knowledge management strategies, livelihood diversification, and soil and land management practices, are associated with lower levels of household food insecurity. At the same time, socioeconomic factors such as education, landholding size, asset ownership, and access to information were positively associated with adaptation intensity across multiple domains. The results further suggest that adaptation operates as an interconnected and multidimensional process shaped by institutional access, livelihood resources, and behavioral practices rather than by isolated adaptation measures alone. However, these relationships should be interpreted cautiously given the cross-sectional nature of the data, the hybrid sampling design, and the potential for measurement and specification uncertainty within multidimensional adaptation indices. Rather than providing definitive evidence of causal pathways, the findings offer context-specific empirical insights into how different adaptation dimensions are associated with household food insecurity within climate-vulnerable farming systems. The study therefore contributes to the growing literature emphasizing the importance of integrated and multidimensional approaches to understanding adaptation and food security under climate stress. Overall, the findings suggest that policies aimed at improving institutional access, information availability, livelihood diversification opportunities, and resource accessibility may support farmers’ adaptive capacity and potentially contribute to more stable household food conditions. Nevertheless, future longitudinal and comparative research is needed to clarify the causal and dynamic relationships between adaptive capacity and food security across different environmental and institutional contexts.

Step 4 Statistical validity and reliability

Construct validity was verified through the Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) test and Bartlett’s Test of Sphericity before each factor extraction, ensuring sampling adequacy (KMO>0.70).
