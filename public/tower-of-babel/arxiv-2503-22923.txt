Nested Stochastic Gradient Descent for Sinkhorn Distance-Regularized Distributionally Robust Optimization
Yufeng Yang, Yi Zhou, Zhaosong Lu

Abstract
Distributionally robust optimization (DRO) is a powerful technique to train robust models against data distribution shift. This paper aims to solve regularized nonconvex DRO problems, where the uncertainty set is modeled by a so-called generalized Sinkhorn distance and the loss function is nonconvex and possibly unbounded. Such a distance allows to model uncertainty of distributions with different probability supports and divergence functions. For this class of regularized DRO problems, we derive a novel dual formulation taking the form of nested stochastic programming, where the dual variable depends on the data sample. To solve the dual problem, we provide theoretical evidence to design a nested stochastic gradient descent (SGD) algorithm, which leverages stochastic approximation to estimate the nested stochastic gradients. We study the convergence rate of nested SGD and establish polynomial iteration and sample complexities that are independent of the data size and parameter dimension, indicating its potential for solving large-scale DRO problems. We conduct numerical experiments to demonstrate the efficiency and robustness of the proposed algorithm.



1
Introduction

In classic machine learning, the primary goal is to achieve good predictive performance in the test set after training the model on a designated training set. The training problem is typically formulated as an empirical risk minimization (ERM) problem.
However, empirical risk minimization assumes that the training set and the test set follow the same underlying data distribution, which is often unrealistic and may result in poor test performance when data distribution shift exists.

Data distribution shift is prevalent in real-world scenarios. It can be caused by many factors such as sampling bias, presence of anomalies, data merging and change of measurements, etc. To tackle this challenge, distributionally robust optimization (DRO)
(
Herbert 1957
)
was proposed, which formulates the objective function as a min-max problem. DRO aims to learn a robust model by minimizing the expected risk over the worst-case data distribution within a predefined ambiguity set. This formulation offers a principled framework to learn the optimal resilient solution in the face of distribution uncertainty.

One key factor in DRO is the selection of an appropriate divergence measure for modeling the ambiguity set. Specifically, the divergence measure should not only be computationally tractable but also yield a solution that avoids excessive conservatism. In the existing literature, various divergence-based ambiguity sets have been studied. In
(
Pflug & Wozabal 2007
;
Wozabal 2012
;
Shafieezadeh Abadeh et al. 2015
;
Esfahani & Kuhn 2018
;
Gao & Kleywegt 2023
;
Luo & Mehrotra 2019
)
, the authors focused on reformulating the expressions of loss functions under the worst-case distributions into a tractable form and exploring possible algorithms to tackle DRO problems under Wasserstein-type ambiguity sets. For more information, we refer the readers to
(
Kuhn et al. 2019
)
for a comprehensive survey on Wasserstein DRO. In
(
Hu & Hong 2013
;
Bayraksan & Love 2015
;
Levy et al. 2020
;
Duchi & Namkoong 2020
)
, the authors analyzed alternative expressions of loss functions under the worst-case distribution and developed algorithms to solve DRO problems under Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence-based and
f
-divergence-based ambiguity sets. However, the aforementioned divergence measures have certain limitations. For example, it is known that DRO with Wasserstein distance requires high computational complexity
(
Pele & Werman 2009
;
Ba et al. 2010
)
. Also, both KL and
f
-divergence are not symmetric when assessing distributions. Furthermore, these two divergence measures require that the distributions share the same probability support, a strong condition that may fail to capture extreme distributions at certain points. See the Examples
3.1
and
3.2
for some concrete application examples.

The Sinkhorn distance, first introduced in
(
Cuturi 2013
)
, was designed to address the aforementioned limitations. Sinkhorn distance is symmetric and allows distributions from the same sample space to have different probability support. Furthermore, Sinkhorn distance is a convex function with respect to distributions, ensuring computation tractability and efficiency for large-scale problems. In
(
Wang et al. 2023
)
, constrained Sinkhorn DRO was initially investigated. Specifically, they derived the equivalent formulation of constrained Sinkhorn DRO problem, which can be solved by the stochastic mirror descent algorithm. However, the convergence analysis conducted in their work assumed that the loss function is convex and bounded, which may not hold in practical modern machine learning applications.

In this work, we develop and study dual formulations of Sinkhorn-distance regularized DRO problems (see formulation (
1
)). The problem becomes nested contextual stochastic programming. Unlike traditional stochastic programming, our formulation makes dual decision variable dependent on data sample following distribution
\mathbb{P}
. Previous works
(
Hu et al. 2023
;
Hu et al. 2024
)
treat such problem as contextual bi-level optimization problem and utilize multi-level monte-carlo (MLMC) methods to estimate objective’s hyper-gradient. However, we notice that such framework encounters significant limitations in our derived formulation (
3
). To elaborate, their contextual stochastic bi-level programming requires hessian information and inner loop to estimate inner objective’s minimizer using epoch SGD
(
Hazan & Kale 2011
)
. The computing of hessian and MLMC significantly increase algorithmic complexity for our DRO problem especially when model parameters are high dimension. Additionally, the convergence analysis for solving contextual bi-level programming is established on strong-convexity of inner function, which is impractical for most scenarios. To tackle this challenge,
we found the special relationship between outer problem’s gradient and inner problem’s gradient. (see Lemma
4.3
and Theorem
4.1
).
This enables us to use sample-average approximation (SAA) to estimate gradients, solve dual problem efficiently via Nested SGD algorithm (See Algorithm
2
) and establish convergence analysis without additional assumptions including bounded, convex loss assumed in
(
Wang et al. 2023
)
and strong convexity of inner function used in
(
Hu et al. 2023
)
. Finally, we train several models including logistic regression and neural network over large-scale dataset, and conduct experiments to compare test performance of Sinkhorn DRO formulation with other baseline methods over perturbated test dataset. Our results demonstrate Sinkhorn DRO formulation (
3
) and proposed nested algorithm
2
successfully improve machine learning models’ robustness against distribution shift.

1.1
Summary of Contributions

•

We introduce a generalized Sinkhorn distance based on the class of
f
-divergence measures. This generalized notion not only retains the advantages of the original Sinkhorn distance but also allows to use a broader range of divergences to model the ambiguity set.

•

We derive an equivalent dual formulation of the regularized Sinkhorn DRO problem with strong duality guarantee. The dual problem takes the novel form of contextual nested stochastic programming, where the variable for inner stochastic programming is dependent on the data sample.

•

To solve the contextual nested stochastic problem with nonconvex and unbounded loss, we design a practical Nested-SGD algorithm with convergence guarantee under relaxed assumptions. Our Nested-SGD is specifically tailored for solving large-scale regularized Sinkhorn DRO problems. Experiments over large-scale dataset demonstrate our proposed formulation and algorithm improve various machine learning models’ robustness against distribution shift.

3
Regularized Nonconvex DRO with Generalized Sinkhorn Distance

In this section, we first introduce a class of regularized nonconvex distributionally-robust optimization (DRO) problems, where the data distribution uncertainty is quantified by generalized Sinkhorn distance. We then study its dual formulation.

3.1
Problem Formulation

In distributionally-robust optimization (DRO), the goal is to learn a model which achieves good and robust performance when the underlying data distribution is uncertain. Specifically, consider a machine learning problem with the nonconvex loss function denoted by
\ell(x;\xi)
, where
x\in\mathbf{R}^{d}
denotes the collection of model parameters and
\xi
corresponds to a data sample that follows an underlying data distribution
\mathbb{Q}
. Then, with a regularization parameter
\lambda>0
, we study the following regularized DRO problem, which is a popular formulation in robust machine learning
(
Chen & Paschalidis 2018b
;
Gao et al. 2022
;
Sagawa et al. 2019
)
.

\displaystyle\min_{x\in\mathbf{R}^{d}}\sup_{\mathbb{Q}}\Bigl\{\mathbb{E}_{\xi\sim\mathbb{Q}}\bigr[\ell(x;\xi)\bigr]-\lambda W_{\epsilon}(\mathbb{P},\mathbb{Q})\Bigl\},

(1)

where
W_{\epsilon}(\mathbb{P},\mathbb{Q})
denotes a certain function (with parameter
\epsilon>0
) that measures the
distance between a nominal data distribution
\mathbb{P}
and the underlying data distribution
\mathbb{Q}
. In particular, the operation
\min_{x}\sup_{\mathbb{Q}}
aims to optimize the model
x
under the worst-case data distribution
\mathbb{Q}
to enhance model robustness against the distribution shift from the nominal distribution
\mathbb{P}
. In the existing literature, many studies have considered KL-divergence
(
Hu & Hong 2013
;
Shapiro et al. 2023
)
,
f
-divergence
(
Levy et al. 2020
;
Duchi & Namkoong 2020
;
Jin et al. 2021
;
Namkoong & Duchi 2016
)
and Wasserstein distance
(
Shafieezadeh Abadeh et al. 2015
;
Esfahani & Kuhn 2018
;
Gao & Kleywegt 2023
;
Luo & Mehrotra 2019
)
to quantify the above distribution shift. However, both the KL-divergence and the
f
-divergence require
\mathbb{Q}
and
\mathbb{P}
to have the same probability support, which is restricted and undesired for many machine learning applications. The following two examples illustrate the limitations of
f
-divergence.

Example 3.1
.

In robust Markov Decision Process (MDP)
(
Wang et al. 2022
)
, denote the underlying environment’s transition kernel as
\mathbb{P}
. Then, robust reinforcement learning aims to optimize the following robust state value function over the policy
\pi
.

\displaystyle V^{\pi}(s):=\inf_{\mathbb{Q}:D_{f}(\mathbb{Q}|\mathbb{P})\leq\rho}\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{t}r_{t}\mid\pi,s_{0}=s\Big],

where
\gamma
is a discount factor,
s
corresponds to the state and
r_{t}
represents the reward obtained after the
t
-th state transition. Here, the robust value function
V^{\pi}(s)
considers the worst-case environment transition kernel
\mathbb{Q}
over the uncertainty set defined by the

f
-divergence, i.e.,
\{\mathbb{Q}:D_{f}(\mathbb{Q}|\mathbb{P})\leq\rho\}
. In this setting, if the nominal transition kernel
\mathbb{P}
cannot visit a certain crucial state
s
, then neither can the transition kernels
\mathbb{Q}
from the uncertainty set visit that state
s
. This indicates that
f
-divergence does not handle “unknown” uncertainties (e.g., states that never visited by
\mathbb{P}
).

Example 3.2
.

Consider the following
f
-divergence-regularized DRO problem.

\displaystyle\min_{x\in\mathbf{R}^{d}}\sup_{\mathbb{Q}}\Big\{\mathbb{E}_{\xi\sim\mathbb{Q}}\big[\ell(x;\xi)\big]-\lambda D_{f}(\mathbb{Q}|\mathbb{P})\Big\},

where
the distribution shift on data is characterized by the
f
-divergence. Suppose we want to train a face detection model. If the nominal data distribution
\mathbb{P}
only covers face images collected from the majority group and excludes the minority groups, then the
f
-divergence DRO cannot yield a robust model over all the groups.

On the other hand, the classic Wasserstein distance does not require the distributions
\mathbb{P},\mathbb{Q}
to have the same probability support. However, it is known that Wasserstein distance suffers from computational intractability for high-dimension data
(
Pele & Werman 2009
;
Ba et al. 2010
)
, and hence is not suitable for large-scale problems in machine learning.

To tackle these challenges, inspired by the Wasserstein distance and the discrete Sinkhorn distance
(
Cuturi 2013
)
, we consider the following generalized Sinkhorn distance to quantify the data distribution shift. Throughout the paper, we consider a sample space
\Omega
associated with
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{F}
. Furthermore, for distributions
\mathbb{Q}
,
\mathbb{P}
over a measurable subset of
\mathcal{F}
, we assume they are absolutely continuous with regard to some reference measures
\nu
and
\mu
, i.e.,
\mathbb{Q}\ll\nu
,
\mathbb{P}\ll\mu
.

Definition 3.3

(Generalized Sinkhorn Distance)
.

Consider probability distributions
\mathbb{Q}
and
\mathbb{P}
over
\bigr(\Omega,\mathcal{F}\bigr)
and let
\nu
and
\mu
be reference measures satisfying
\mathbb{Q}\ll\nu
,
\mathbb{P}\ll\mu
.
Denote
\Gamma(\mathbb{P},\mathbb{Q})
as the set of joint distributions that have marginal distributions
\mathbb{P},\mathbb{Q}
. For a fixed regularization parameter
\epsilon>0
and a cost metric
c:\Omega\times\Omega\to\mathbf{R}
, the generalized Sinkhorn distance is defined as

\displaystyle W_{\epsilon}(\mathbb{P},\mathbb{Q})=\inf_{\gamma\in\Gamma(\mathbb{P},\mathbb{Q})}\Bigl\{\mathbb{E}_{(\zeta,\xi)\sim\gamma}\bigr[c(\zeta,\xi)\bigr]+\epsilon D_{f}(\gamma|\mathbb{P}\otimes\nu)\Bigl\},

where
D_{f}
corresponds to the
f
-divergence, that is,

D_{f}(\gamma|\mathbb{P}\otimes\nu)=\int f\big(\frac{\mathrm{d}\gamma(\zeta,\xi)}{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}(\zeta)\mathrm{d}\nu(\xi)}\big)\mathrm{d}\nu(\xi)\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}(\zeta),

where the function
f:[0,+\infty)\rightarrow[-\infty,+\infty\bigr]
is convex and satisfies
f(1)=0
and
f(0)=\lim_{t\rightarrow 0+}f(t)
, and
\frac{\mathrm{d}\gamma(\zeta,\xi)}{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}(\zeta)\mathrm{d}\nu(\xi)}
represents the density ratio of
\gamma
with respect to
\mathbb{P}\otimes\nu
.

Remark 3.4
.

The absolute continuity condition
\mathbb{Q}\ll\nu
,
\mathbb{P}\ll\mu
is crucial to guarantee that the generalized Sinkhorn distance is well-defined. Typical choices of the reference measure
\nu
include the Lebesgue measure or the Gaussian measure.
In addition, when
\mathbb{Q}\ll\nu
,
D_{f}(\gamma|\mathbb{P}\otimes\nu)
and
D_{f}(\gamma|\mathbb{P}\otimes\mathbb{Q})
are equivalent up to a constant, which does not affect the optimal solution in the regularized setting. Thus, we consider the former term for simplicity.

The proposed generalized Sinkhorn distance is regularized by the
f
-divergence , which generalizes the KL-divergence regularization adopted in the definition of the standard Sinkhorn distance
(
Wang et al. 2023
)
. This generalized Sinkhorn distance still allows the distributions
\mathbb{P}
and
\mathbb{Q}
to have different probability support. Also, by adding
f
-divergence regularization, it preserves the joint convexity with respect to the probability distributions and thus guarantees the uniqueness of the optimal solution, which helps reduce the computation complexity. Moreover, the generalized Sinkhorn distance provides more flexibility to model data distribution uncertainty compared to other divergence-based measures
(
Levy et al. 2020
;
Jin et al. 2021
)
.

3.2
Dual Formulation

With generalized Sinkhorn distance, the regularized DRO problem (
3.3
) can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\min_{x\in\mathbf{R}^{d}}\sup_{\mathbb{Q}}\Big\{\mathbb{E}_{\xi\sim\mathbb{Q}}\bigr[\ell(x;\xi)\bigr]

\displaystyle-\inf_{\gamma\in\Gamma(\mathbb{P},\mathbb{Q})}\bigr\{\mathbb{E}_{(\zeta,\xi)\sim\gamma}\bigr[\lambda c(\xi,\zeta)\bigr]+\lambda\epsilon D_{f}(\gamma|\mathbb{P}\otimes\nu)\bigr\}\Big\}.

(2)

The primal Sinkhorn distance regularized DRO problem in (
2
) is hard to solve, since it is challenging to obtain an analytical form of the worst-case distribution
\mathbb{Q}
. However, the generalized Sinkhorn distance involves special structures that can transform the primal regularized DRO problem (
1
) in into a simpler dual form. The following theorem deduces an equivalent dual formulation.

Theorem 3.1

(Dual formulation)
.

The DRO problem in (
2
) has the following equivalent dual formulation.

\displaystyle\min_{x\in\mathbf{R^{d}}}\mathbb{E}_{\zeta\sim\mathbb{P}}[\Psi_{\zeta}(x)],~\text{where}~

\displaystyle\Psi_{\zeta}(x)=\min_{\eta\in\mathbf{R}}\mathbb{E}_{\xi\sim\nu}\big[\lambda\epsilon f^{*}\big(\frac{\ell(x;\xi)-\lambda c(\zeta,\xi)-\eta}{\lambda\epsilon}\big)\big]+\eta,

(3)

and
f^{*}
denotes the conjugate function of
f
.

Remark 3.5

(Technical Novelty)
.

Proving primal problem (
2
) is equivalent to dual problem (
3
) is crucial. To elaborate, we first decompose the joint distribution as
\gamma(\zeta,\xi)=\gamma_{\zeta}(\xi)\mathbb{P}(\zeta)
, where
\gamma_{\zeta}
corresponds to the conditional distribution over
\xi
. Then, by the principle of interchangeability (Lemma
A.1
in appendix), we are able to swap the order between
\mathbb{E}_{\zeta\sim\mathbb{P}}
and

\sup_{\gamma_{\zeta}}
without changing the optimal value, which yields

\displaystyle\min_{x\in\mathbf{R}^{d}}\mathbb{E}_{\zeta\sim\mathbb{P}}\Big[

\displaystyle\Psi_{\zeta}(x)=\sup_{\gamma(\xi|\zeta)}\big\{\mathbb{E}_{\xi\sim\gamma(\cdot|\zeta)}\bigr[\ell(x;\xi)-\lambda c(\zeta,\xi)\bigr]

\displaystyle-\lambda\epsilon D_{f}\big(\gamma(\xi|\zeta)|\nu(\xi)\big)\big\}\Big].

Then, by utilizing techniques of data processing inequality, we show that
\Psi_{\zeta}(x)
is equivalent as following auxiliary function

\displaystyle\widetilde{\Psi}_{\zeta}(x)=\sup_{{\mu}_{\gamma|\zeta}}\!\Big\{\!\mathbb{E}_{{\mu}_{\gamma|\zeta}}\bigr[\ell(x;\xi)\!-\!\lambda c(\zeta,\xi)\bigr]-\lambda\epsilon D_{f}({{\mu}_{\gamma|\zeta}}|{\mu}_{\nu})\!\Big\}.

where
\sup_{{\mu}_{\gamma|\zeta}}
corresponds to the supremum over all possible distributions
{\mu}_{\gamma|\zeta}
induced by
\gamma(\xi|\zeta)
.
Last, by utilizing inverse c.d.f sampling on
\widetilde{\Psi}_{\zeta}(x)
introduced from
(
Duchi & Namkoong 2020
)
, we are able to derive equivalent formulation
\Psi_{\zeta}(x)=\min_{\eta\in\mathbf{R}}\int_{0}^{1}\sup_{r\in\mathbf{R}_{+}}\big[rF^{-1}(u)-\eta(r-1)-\lambda\epsilon f(r)\big]\mathrm{d}u,
which gives the desired result.

We notice that in the appendix of
(
Wang et al. 2023
)
, the authors also presented an equivalent formulation for the regularized Sinknorn DRO problem with
D_{f}
being the KL divergence. Specifically, they showed the following equivalent formulation of the problem.

\displaystyle\min_{x\in\mathbf{R}^{d},\lambda>0}\!\Big\{\!

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\zeta\sim\mathbb{P}}\Big[\lambda\epsilon\log\big(\mathbb{E}_{\xi\sim\nu}\big[\exp(\frac{\ell(x;\xi)\!-\!\lambda c(\zeta,\xi)}{\lambda\epsilon})\big]\big)\!\Big]\!\Big\}.

The above dual formulation involves compositional structure that makes the problem challenging to solve. To elaborate, for the variable
\lambda
,
(
Wang et al. 2023
)
utilizes bisection search to optimize it. For the variable
x
, they utilize multi-level monte-carlo (MLMC) method to control the estimation bias and variance of the objective’s gradient. If the loss function is used to train large models such as neural network, bisection search and MLMC estimation will require performing a significant number of forward and backward passes at each iteration.
As a comparison, in our dual formulation (
3
) derived using Lagrange duality, if we choose the same KL divergence, i.e.,
f(t)=t\log(t)
, the resulting dual formulation becomes

\displaystyle\min_{x\in\mathbf{R}^{d}}\mathbb{E}_{\zeta\sim\mathbb{P}}

\displaystyle\Big[\min_{\eta\in\mathbf{R}}\mathbb{E}_{\xi\sim\nu}\big[

\displaystyle\lambda\epsilon\exp\big(\frac{\ell(x;\xi)-\lambda c(\zeta,\xi)-\eta}{\lambda\epsilon}\big)-\lambda\epsilon\big]+\eta\Big].

One can see that our dual formulation involves nested minimization structure, where the inner minimization is with respect to the dual variable. As we show later in Lemma
4.3
and Theorem
4.1
, we can break our proposed formulation into a nested form, in which both the inner and outer objectives’ gradients can be efficiently computed via sample-average approximation with controllable bias and variance.

4
Solving the Dual Problem via Nested SGD

To solve minimization with nested structure, such as the dual Sinkhorn formulation (
3
), previous work
(
Hu et al. 2023
)
studied this type of problems under the framework of contextual bi-level optimization, but their proposed algorithm needs to estimate the inner-minimizer, utilize second-order information to compute hyper-gradient at each iteration, and strong convexity assumption of inner function to guarantee convergence. These bottlenecks aggregate the algorithmic complexity and the applicability for solving formulation (
3
) in large-scale settings.

In this section, we aim to relax additional requirements in
(
Hu et al. 2023
)
by exploring the special relationship between the inner and outer functions’ gradients of formulation (
3
). Recall that the dual formulation (
3
) consists of two stochastic optimization problems.
Throughout, we denote the objective function of the inner problem in (
3
) as

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\zeta}(x,\eta)=\mathbb{E}_{\xi\sim\nu}\big[\lambda\epsilon f^{*}\big(\frac{\ell(x;\xi)-\lambda c(\zeta,\xi)-\eta}{\lambda\epsilon}\big)\big]+\eta.

For simplicity, we denote
\eta_{x}^{*}(\zeta)=\arg\min_{\eta}L_{\zeta}(x,\eta)
to highlight its dependence on the fixed parameter
x
and data sample
\zeta
. We also denote
\mathbb{E}_{\zeta}\bigr[\Psi_{\zeta}(x)\bigr]
as the objective function of the outer problem.

To analyze the problem structure, we adopt the following standard assumptions on the loss functions.

Assumption 4.1
.

The functions in (
3
) satisfy:

•

For every
\xi
,
\ell(\cdot;\xi)
is
G
-Lipschitz continuous.
i.e.,

\big\|\ell(x;\xi)-\ell(y;\xi)\big\|\leq G\big\|x-y\big\|
.

•

For every
\xi
,
\ell(\cdot;\xi)
is continuously differentiable and
L
-smooth. i.e.,
\big\|\nabla\ell(x;\xi)-\nabla\ell(y;\xi)\big\|\leq L\big\|x-y\big\|
.

•

The conjugate function
f^{*}
is continuously differentiable and
M
-smooth.

•

The objective function
\mathbb{E}_{\zeta\sim\mathbb{P}}\bigr[\Psi_{\zeta}(\cdot)\bigr]
is bounded below.

Remark 4.2
.

Note that the loss function
\ell(x;\xi)
is generally nonconvex. Regarding the smooth assumption on
f^{*}
in the third item, some typical examples of
f
-divergence include the
\chi^{2}
-divergence and the KL-divergence. Specifically, their corresponding conjugate functions are given by
f^{*}(y)=-1+\frac{1}{4}(y+2)^{2}_{+}
and
f^{*}(y)=\exp(y-1)
, respectively.

Since the dual problem takes a nested form, we need an efficient way to compute the gradient of the objective function.
The following lemma, proved by
(
Jin et al. 2021
)
, provides an analytical formula for computing the exact gradient.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let Assumption
4.1
hold and consider any fixed
x
and
\zeta
. Then, the function
\Psi_{\zeta}(x)
is differentiable and satisfies
\nabla\Psi_{\zeta}(x)=\nabla_{1}\mathcal{L}_{\zeta}(x,\eta_{x}^{*}(\zeta))
, where
\eta_{x}^{*}(\zeta)\in\arg\min_{\eta}\mathcal{L}_{\zeta}(x,\eta)
.

This lemma shows that we can directly compute the outer problem’s gradient
\nabla\mathbb{E}_{\zeta}[\Psi_{\zeta}(x)]
given the exact minimizer
\eta_{x}^{*}(\zeta)
of the inner problem. In particular, there is no need to compute second-order information such as
\nabla_{x}\eta^{*}_{x}(\zeta)
, as required by the more general contextual Bi-level optimization framework
(
Hu et al. 2023
)
. Hence, in the following subsection, we are further motivated to develop an efficient estimation of the minimizer
\eta_{x}^{*}(\zeta)
via SGD.

4.1
Inexact Estimation of Inner Minimizer via SGD

Recall that the inner problem of the dual problem (
3
) takes the following form.

\displaystyle\min_{\eta\in\mathbf{R}}

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\zeta}(x,\eta)=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\xi\sim\nu}\big[\lambda\epsilon f^{*}\big(\frac{\ell(x;\xi)-\lambda c(\zeta,\xi)-\eta}{\lambda\epsilon}\big)+\eta\big],

(4)

which is a stochastic programming problem and hence one can apply SGD to solve for an approximation of the minimizer
\eta_{x}^{*}(\zeta)
. Specifically,
for fixed
x
and sample

When the stochastic estimator
v^{\tilde{B}}(\eta)
is used to approximate the full gradient

\nabla_{2}\mathcal{L}_{\zeta}(x,\eta^{*}_{x}(\zeta))
, it introduces inexactness to the gradient estimation as well as the convergence of the algorithm. Specifically, the approximation error composes two parts: one corresponds to the stochastic approximation error between
v^{\tilde{B}}(\eta)
and
\nabla_{2}\mathcal{L}_{\zeta}(x,\eta)
, and the other one corresponds to the inexactness error between
\nabla_{2}\mathcal{L}_{\zeta}(x,\eta)
and
\nabla_{2}\mathcal{L}_{\zeta}(x,\eta_{x}^{*}(\zeta))
.
To quantify such approximation errors, we adopt the following assumption on the stochastic approximation error between
v^{\tilde{B}}(\eta)
and
\nabla_{2}\mathcal{L}_{\zeta}(x,\eta)
.

Assumption 4.4

(Sub-Gaussian Noise)
.

For any
x
,
\zeta
and
\eta
, the stochastic approximation error
\Lambda=v^{\tilde{B}}(\eta)-\nabla_{2}\mathcal{L}_{\zeta}(x,\eta)
follows sub-Gaussian distribution with variance
R_{2}/\tilde{B}
for some
R_{2},\tilde{B}>0
, i.e.,

\mathbb{E}[\exp\big(s\Lambda\big)]\leq\exp(\frac{s^{2}R_{2}}{2\tilde{B}}),\forall s\in\mathbf{R}.

By adjusting the batch size
\tilde{B}
, assumption
4.4
guarantees high probability convergence for output of algorithm
1
. The following theorem summarizes this result and further provides practical criteria to check the optimality of the solution by leveraging the gradient structure relationship between
\nabla\Psi_{\zeta}(x)

and
\nabla_{2}\mathcal{L}_{\zeta}(x,\eta)
.

Theorem 4.1

(Approximation Error and Optimality)
.

Let Assumptions
4.1
and
4.4
hold
and choose batch size
\tilde{B}=\Theta(\log(\frac{2}{\omega})R_{2}\varepsilon^{-2})
for some
\varepsilon>0
. Then, with probability at least
1-\omega
, the stochastic approximation error of
v^{\tilde{B}}(\eta)
can be bounded as

\displaystyle\big|\Lambda\big|=\big|v^{\tilde{B}}(\eta)-\nabla_{2}\mathcal{L}_{\zeta}(x,\eta)\big|\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{4}.

(6)

Furthermore, suppose there exists a stochastic algorithm that solves (
4
) and produces a sequence
\{\eta^{d}\}_{d}
with convergence guarantee

\mathbb{E}_{d}\big|\nabla_{2}\mathcal{L}_{\zeta}(x,\eta^{d})\big|\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{2}
, and set

\tilde{d}=\arg\min_{d}\big|v^{\tilde{B}}(\eta^{d})\big|
.
Then, with probability at least
1-\omega
, the full gradient
\nabla\Psi(x)
and
\nabla_{2}\mathcal{L}_{\zeta}(x,\eta^{\tilde{d}})
satisfy

\displaystyle\bigl|\nabla_{2}\mathcal{L}_{\zeta}(x,\eta^{\tilde{d}})\bigl|\leq\varepsilon\quad\text{and}

\displaystyle\bigl\|\nabla\Psi_{\zeta}(x)\bigl\|\leq G\varepsilon+\bigl\|\nabla_{1}\mathcal{L}_{\zeta}(x,\eta^{\tilde{d}})\bigl\|,\forall\zeta\sim\mathbb{P}.

(7)

Remark 4.5
.

The novelty of our proof lies in the usage of the monotonicity property of
(f^{*})^{\prime}
, which enables us to move
\mathbb{E}_{\xi\sim\nu}
into the norm
\big\|\cdot\big\|
. Thanks to special structure of formulation (
3
), such operation swap doesn’t change its value since each inner problem
\min_{\eta}\mathcal{L}_{\zeta}(x,\eta)
depends on a fixed
\zeta
. In the proof, we also utilize the convexity of the conjugate function
f^{*}(\frac{\ell(x;\xi)-c(\zeta;\xi)-\eta}{\lambda\epsilon})
in
\eta
, albeit the loss function
\ell(x;\xi)
is generally non-convex.

Discussion.

We note that Theorem
4.1
requires to have an algorithm capable of generating a sequence of
\eta
that satisfies
\mathbb{E}_{d}|\nabla_{2}\mathcal{L}_{\zeta}(x,\eta^{{d}})|<\frac{\varepsilon}{2}
. This condition can be achieved by the standard SGD Algorithm
1
under Assumption
4.4
.
Moreover, the gradient estimator
v^{\tilde{B}}(\cdot)
is a scalar variable, which allows us to efficiently execute Algorithm
1
for each sub-sampled
\zeta
in practice.

Inequalities (
7
) reveal the gradient relationship between
\nabla_{1}\mathcal{L}_{\zeta}(x,\eta^{\tilde{d}})
and
\nabla_{2}\mathcal{L}_{\zeta}(x,\eta^{\tilde{d}})
under the presence
\eta^{\tilde{d}}
, which is an inexact minimizer of inner problem (
4
). It suggests that, by controlling
\eta^{\tilde{d}}
to be
\varepsilon/2
-stationary point, i.e.,
\big|\nabla_{2}\mathcal{L}_{\zeta}(x,\eta^{\tilde{d}})\big|\leq\varepsilon/2
, we can guarantee that for every
\zeta
,
\nabla_{1}\mathcal{L}_{\zeta}(x,\eta^{d})
approximates
\nabla\Psi(x)
with error up to
G\varepsilon
with high probability. Thus, (
7
) motivates us to design a nested-type algorithm (see the next subsection), where the inner algorithm first obtains an inexact minimizer
\eta^{\tilde{d}}
for every
\mathcal{L}_{\zeta}(x,\eta)
, and then the outer algorithm utilizes SGD to optimize
\min_{x}\mathbb{E}_{\zeta\sim\mathbb{P}}\big[\mathcal{L}_{\zeta}(x,\eta)\big]
.
In particular, (
7
) also indicates that
\nabla_{1}\mathbb{E}_{\zeta}[\mathcal{L}_{\zeta}(x,\eta^{\tilde{d}})]
approximates
\nabla\mathbb{E}_{\zeta}[\Psi_{\zeta}(x)]
with controllable error up to
G\epsilon
.
To see this, taking expectation over
\zeta
in (
7
), we obtain that

\displaystyle\big\|\nabla_{1}\mathbb{E}_{\zeta\sim\mathbb{P}}

\displaystyle\big[\mathcal{L}_{\zeta}(x,\eta^{\tilde{d}})\big]-\nabla\mathbb{E}_{\zeta\sim\mathbb{P}}\big[\Psi_{\zeta}(x)\big]\big\|

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{\zeta\sim\mathbb{P}}\big\|\nabla_{1}\mathcal{L}_{\zeta}(x,\eta^{\tilde{d}})-\nabla\Psi_{\zeta}(x)\big\|\leq G\varepsilon,

(8)

which further implies

\displaystyle\big\|\nabla\mathbb{E}_{\zeta\sim\mathbb{P}}\big[\Psi_{\zeta}(x)\big]\big\|\leq G\varepsilon+\big\|\nabla_{1}\mathbb{E}_{\zeta\sim\mathbb{P}}\big[\mathcal{L}_{\zeta}(x,\eta)\big]\big\|.

(9)

4.2

Based on Lemma
4.3
and Theorem

, we propose the Nested SGD algorithm presented in Algorithm

. To elaborate, at each iteration,
the algorithm first samples one
\zeta

\big\{\xi\big\}_{B}

\eta^{*}_{x}(\zeta)
. It then evaluates the mini-batch gradient estimator of
\nabla\mathbb{E}_{\zeta}\big[\Psi(x_{t})\big]
at
(x_{t},\eta^{\tilde{d}})

\displaystyle\hat{g}_{t}^{B}=\frac{1}{B}\sum_{i=1}^{B}(f^{*})^{\prime}\big(\frac{\ell(x;\xi_{i})-c(\zeta,\xi_{i})-\eta^{\tilde{d}}(\zeta)}{\lambda\epsilon}\big)\nabla\ell(x_{t};\xi_{i}).

(10)

After that, Algorithm

5
Convergence Analysis of Nested SGD

To analyze the convergence of Nested SGD, we adopt the following bounded variance assumptions on the loss function
\ell
and the cost metric
c
. Here, for any function
\rho(.):\mathbf{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbf{R}
and random variable
\varpi\in\mathbf{R}^{d}
, we define the variance as

Var_{\varpi}(\rho(\varpi))=\mathbb{E}\bigr[\rho(\varpi)-\mathbb{E}\bigr[\rho(\varpi)\bigr]\bigr]^{2}
.

Assumption 5.1
.

There exists
\sigma,\delta>0
such that:

•

For every
x
, the variance of
\ell(x;\cdot)
over
\xi
is bounded by
\sigma^{2}
, i.e.,
Var_{\xi}\big(\ell(x;\xi)\big)\leq\sigma^{2}
.

•

For every
\zeta
, the variance of
c(\zeta,\cdot)
over
\xi
is bounded by
\delta^{2}
, i.e.,
Var_{\xi}\big(c(\zeta,\xi)\big)\leq\delta^{2}
.

•

For every
\xi
, the variance of
c(\cdot,\xi)
over
\zeta
is bounded by
\delta^{2}
, i.e.,
Var_{\zeta}\big(c(\zeta,\xi)\big)\leq\delta^{2}
.

We first analyze the convergence rate of the inner Algorithm
1
. Based on Assumptions
4.1
and
5.1
, we can establish the following expected smoothness property.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let Assumption
4.1
hold and
denote
\mathcal{L}_{\zeta,\xi}(x,\eta)=\lambda\epsilon\mathbb{E}_{\xi\sim\nu}\bigr[f^{*}(\frac{\ell(x;\xi)-c(\zeta,\xi)-\eta}{\lambda\epsilon})\bigr]+\eta
. Then, for any
\eta
and
\eta^{\prime}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\xi\sim\nu}\big\|\nabla_{2}\mathcal{L}_{\zeta,\xi}(x,\eta)-\nabla_{2}\mathcal{L}_{\zeta,\xi}(x,\eta^{\prime})\big\|^{2}\leq(K^{\prime})^{2}\big\|\eta-\eta^{\prime}\big\|^{2},

where
K^{\prime}=M(\lambda\epsilon)^{-1}
.

We bound the second moment of
\nabla_{2}\mathcal{L}_{\zeta}(x,\eta)
as follows.

Lemma 5.3
.

Let Assumption
5.1
hold. Then, the second moment of
\nabla_{2}\mathcal{L}_{\zeta,\xi}(x,\eta)
is bounded by

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\left\|\nabla_{2}\mathcal{L}_{\zeta,\xi}(x,\eta)\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2M^{2}(\lambda\epsilon)^{-2}(\sigma^{2}+\lambda\delta^{2})

\displaystyle\quad+\left\|\nabla_{2}\mathcal{L}_{\zeta}(x,\eta)\right\|^{2}.

(11)

Based on Lemmas
5.2
and
5.3
and the standard SGD analysis
(
Ghadimi & Lan 2013
;
Arjevani et al. 2022
)
, we obtain the following convergence result of Algorithm
1
.

Corollary 5.4
.

Let Assumption
4.4
hold and denote
\widehat{\Delta}=\max_{\zeta}\{L_{\zeta}(x_{t},\eta^{0})-\Psi_{\zeta}(x_{t})\}
. Apply Algorithm
1
to solve the inner problem (
4
) with constant learning rate
\alpha_{d}=\min\big\{\frac{1}{K^{\prime}},\sqrt{\frac{2\widehat{\Delta}}{K^{\prime}(R_{2}/\tilde{B})D}}\big\}
and batch size
\tilde{B}=\Theta(\log(\frac{2}{\omega})R_{2}\varepsilon^{-2}
).
Then, with probability at least
1-\omega
,

\mathbb{E}_{d}\big|\nabla_{2}\mathcal{L}_{\zeta}(x,\eta^{d})\big|^{2}\leq\mathcal{O}\big(\sqrt{\frac{\widehat{\Delta}K^{\prime}R_{2}}{D\tilde{B}}}\big)+\mathcal{O}\big(\frac{\hat{\Delta}K^{\prime}}{D}\big).

(12)

In particular, it takes
\mathcal{O}\big(\log^{-1}(\frac{2}{\omega})\widehat{\Delta}K^{\prime}\varepsilon^{-2}\big)
number of iterations to obtain an
\frac{\varepsilon}{2}
-stationary point, and the stochastic gradient oracle complexity is
\mathcal{O}(\widehat{\Delta}K^{\prime}R_{2}/\tilde{B}\varepsilon^{-4})
.

Next, we analyze the convergence of Algorithm
2
. We first study the smoothness property of the objective function
\mathbb{E}_{\zeta\sim\mathbb{P}}\bigr[\Psi_{\zeta}(x)\bigr]
. We note that the bi-variate function
\mathbb{E}_{\zeta\sim\mathbb{P}}\bigr[\mathcal{L}_{\zeta}(x,\eta)\bigr]
has been shown to satisfy a generalized-smooth condition
(
Zhang et al. 2020
;
Chen et al. 2023
)
. However, if
\eta
is chosen to be the minimizer
\eta_{x}^{*}(\zeta)
, we show that the objective function satisfies the following directional smoothness property
(
Mishkin et al. 2024

Lemma 5.5

(Directional Smoothness)
.

Let Assumption
4.1
hold. For any
x
and
x^{\prime}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\zeta}\big\|\nabla\Psi_{\zeta}(x)-\nabla_{1}\mathcal{L}_{\zeta}(x^{\prime},\eta_{x}^{*}(\zeta))\big\|^{2}\leq K^{2}\big\|x-x^{\prime}\big\|^{2},

\displaystyle\text{ where }K=G^{2}(\lambda\epsilon)^{-1}M+L.

(13)

Recall that
\eta^{\tilde{d}}_{x}(\zeta)
is inexact estimation of
\eta_{x}^{*}(\zeta)
obtained from algorithm
1

\hat{g}_{t}^{B}

Lemma 5.6
.

The second moment of the mini-batch gradient estimator
\hat{g}_{t}^{B}
in (

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\zeta\sim\mathbb{P},\xi_{B}\sim\nu}\big\|\hat{g}_{t}^{B}\big\|^{2}

7
Conclusion

In this paper, we investigate generalized Sinkhorn distance-regularized distributionally robust optimization. By deriving a new dual formulation with strong duality guarantee, we show that the resulting Sinkhorn DRO problem has nested stochastic programming structure, which enables us to design a Nested SGD algorithm with convergence guarantee under mild assumptions. Numerical studies demonstrate that our Sinkhorn DRO formulation is applicable to large-scale problems and can attain stronger robustness against distribution shifts than
f
-divergence regularized DRO.
