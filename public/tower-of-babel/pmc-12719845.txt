Albuminuria is associated with worse outcomes in non‐diabetics hospitalized with acute heart failure
Authors: Yoshimitsu Takaoka, Yu Horiuchi, Michael Strader, Patrick Murray, Nicholas Wettersten
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsChronic kidney disease (CKD) increases the risk of morbidity and mortality in patients with heart failure (HF). Guidelines recommend assessing CKD using the urinary albumin‐to‐creatinine ratio (UACR), as albuminuria is strongly associated with the risk of incident HF and outcomes in chronic HF; however, its prognostic role in acute HF (AHF) remains unclear. We evaluated if albuminuria was associated with adverse outcomes in individuals hospitalized with AHF in Renal Optimization Strategies Evaluation‐Acute Heart Failure (ROSE‐AHF).MethodsThere were 339 participants with baseline UACR, 248 with UACR at Day 7 and 237 with both measurements for assessing change in UACR. Associations of baseline, Day 7 and change in UACR with all‐cause mortality at 6 months and the composite of first HF hospitalization or cardiovascular mortality at 2 months were assessed with Cox proportional hazards models. We assessed for effect modification by treatment arm and diabetes status.ResultsThe mean age was 70 years, 73.2% were male, 55.8% had diabetes mellitus, the mean estimated glomerular filtration rate was 45 mL/min/1.73 m2 and mean UACR was 208 mg/g. There were 65 deaths and 82 HF hospitalizations or cardiovascular deaths. Baseline UACR was not associated with either outcome; however, there was a significant interaction by diabetes status for all‐cause mortality (P = 0.022) such that baseline UACR was associated with the risk of death in non‐diabetics [hazard ratio (HR) 2.58, 95% CI 1.00, 6.66, P = 0.050 third tertile versus first tertile], but not diabetics. Neither Day 7 UACR nor the change in UACR were associated with outcomes.ConclusionsAlbuminuria is largely not associated with the risk of death, cardiovascular death, or HF hospitalization in AHF, except admission UACR is associated with the risk of death in non‐diabetics.Keywords: acute heart failure, albuminuria, diabetes status, non‐diabetics, all‐cause mortality

Introduction

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) affects almost 50% of individuals with heart failure (HF) and increases the risk of death and HF hospitalization as it progresses. 1 , 2 Studies have traditionally assessed CKD by measures of glomerular filtration function using serum creatinine or the estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR); however, the Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) guidelines also recommend measuring albuminuria, as the urinary albumin‐to‐creatinine ratio (UACR), to assess for CKD. 3 Albuminuria reflects glomerular injury including vascular endothelial injury, tubular injury and activation of neurohormonal systems in cardiovascular disease, and even levels of UACR below traditional cut‐offs have been associated with higher risk of cardiovascular events. 4 , 5 In HF, albuminuria is primarily attributed to glomerular and tubular injury caused by congestion, though this may not be the same in individuals with diabetes. 4 , 6 In diabetes, glomerular injury and microvascular damage resulting from hyperglycaemia are the principal causes; thus, the underlying mechanisms of albuminuria may differ in individuals with HF with and without diabetes. 4 , 7 The presence and severity of albuminuria are strongly associated with the risk of developing HF and worse outcomes in individuals with chronic HF; however, the relationship between albuminuria and the prognosis of acute HF (AHF) is still unclear. 5 , 8

The ROSE‐AHF (Renal Optimization Strategies Evaluation‐Acute Heart Failure) trial was a multicentre study that found neither low‐dose dopamine nor low‐dose nesiritide enhanced decongestion or improved kidney function in patients with AHF and kidney dysfunction. 9 We evaluated if the presence of albuminuria, systematically measured in ROSE‐AHF and its change during hospitalization were associated with adverse outcomes in AHF. We hypothesized that more severe and persistent albuminuria would be associated with a higher risk of death and HF readmission.

Methods

Analytic cohort

The rationale and methodology of the ROSE‐AHF trial have been previously outlined. 7 Briefly, ROSE‐AHF enrolled 360 patients hospitalized with AHF and kidney dysfunction, defined as an eGFR of 15 to 60 mL/min/1.73 m2, to determine if low‐dose dopamine or low‐dose nesiritide improved diuresis and preserved kidney function compared with placebo. The complete list of inclusion and exclusion criteria from ROSE‐AHF is provided in Table S1. From these 360 participants, 21 participants were missing baseline UACR, resulting in an analytic cohort of 339 participants with at least baseline UACR measurements available for this analysis. The study protocol was approved by the institutional review boards (IRBs) at all participating sites, with written informed consent obtained from all patients prior to randomization. This post‐hoc analysis was approved by the San Diego Veterans Affairs IRB.

UACR measurements

Five spot urine samples were collected in ROSE‐AHF: baseline, 24 h, 48 h, 72 h and Day 7. We used the Day 7 value as a surrogate for discharge UACR. The change in UACR was defined as the difference between baseline UACR and UACR measured on Day 7. Beyond the 339 participants with baseline UACR measurements, 248 had measurements at Day 7 and 237 participants had UACR measurements at both Day 7 and baseline for calculating change in UACR.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was all‐cause mortality at 6 months. The secondary outcome was a composite outcome of first HF hospitalization or cardiovascular mortality within 2 months.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics are presented as percentages for categorical variables and means with standard deviations or medians with interquartile ranges for continuous variables. Participant characteristics were compared between tertiles of UACR using analysis of variance (ANOVA), Kruskal–Wallis test, the χ 2 test or the Fisher's exact test as appropriate. UACR measurements at admission and Day 7 demonstrated a right‐skewed distribution and thus were log base 2‐transformed to obtain a more normal distribution and such that associations can be interpreted as representing a ‘per two‐fold’ higher in UACR. The difference in UACR between Day 7 and admission demonstrated a fat‐tailed distribution pattern; thus, tertiles of the change were utilized for analysis of change.

We used a mixed linear effects model to evaluate the temporal changes in UACR over the first 7 days of hospitalization. A prior study reported a correlation between UACR and markers of congestion in outpatients with chronic HF 6 ; thus, we evaluated the correlation between baseline log2 UACR and admission log2 N‐terminal pro‐brain natriuretic peptide (NT‐proBNP), individual signs and symptoms of congestion and a composite congestion score using univariate linear regression. The composite congestion score was calculated by summing the individual scores for jugular venous pressure (JVP), rales, orthopnoea and peripheral oedema using a scale similar to prior studies which assessed signs and symptoms of congestion at admission (Table S2). 11

We evaluated the association of baseline, Day 7 UACR, and change in UACR with outcomes using Cox proportional hazards models. For both baseline and Day 7 UACR, we evaluated the unadjusted association of UACR with outcomes. Next, for baseline UACR, we adjusted for age, sex, admission blood urea nitrogen (BUN), admission eGFR, sodium, admission systolic blood pressure (SBP) and diabetes status. These variables were selected because they are risk factors frequently associated with adverse outcomes in HF. 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 For Day 7 UACR and change in UACR, we only adjusted for age, sex and diabetes status. We chose not to include the parameters from admission because they were not proximate to the time of biomarker measurement. Baseline and Day 7 UACR were analysed as continuous variables and by tertiles to assess their functional forms, while change in UACR was evaluated only by tertiles. Because individuals with diabetes may have albuminuria related to diabetes rather than resulting from AHF, we tested for effect modification by diabetes status in all analyses by either testing the interaction between continuous UACR × diabetes status, when feasible, or by performing analysis in subgroups of individuals with and without diabetes. Because exposure to nesiritide or dopamine may influence albuminuria, the interaction between UACR × treatment arm was evaluated for Day 7 UACR and change in UACR.

All analyses performed with R version 4.4.1. A two‐tailed value of P < 0.05 was considered significant for all analyses.

Results

Baseline characteristics

Baseline characteristics by tertiles of UACR are presented in Table 1 . Individuals with higher admission UACR had significantly higher admission SBP and diastolic blood pressure (DBP). There was no difference in the prescription of beta‐blockers or inhibitors of the renin–angiotensin system (RAS); however, there was significantly less mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist (MRA) use in individuals with higher UACR. Individuals with higher UACR levels were more likely to have hypertension, while diabetes status was not different between tertiles of UACR. Kidney dysfunction at admission, reflected by higher serum creatinine and lower eGFR, was more frequent with higher UACR tertiles. When baseline characteristics were assessed in individuals with and without diabetes, findings were similar in diabetics except individuals with higher UACR were significantly younger than those with low UACR. Conversely, SBP, DBP, serum creatinine and eGFR were no longer significantly different across tertiles of UACR in non‐diabetics (Tables S3 and S4). The temporal trends in UACR are presented in Figure 1. As time progressed, UACR decreased by an average of 10.4 mg/g each day (P < 0.001). The daily decrease in UACR did not differ in individuals with and without diabetes (P interaction = 0.992).

Association of UACR with measures of congestion

Higher NT‐proBNP at admission was associated with higher levels of UACR, with each doubling of NT‐proBNP associated with 37% higher UACR. None of the signs and symptoms of congestion nor the composite congestion score was associated with UACR (Table 2). Findings were similar when evaluated in individuals with and without diabetes (Tables S5 and S6).

Association of UACR and all‐cause mortality

During a median follow‐up of 177 days, 65 participants died. Baseline UACR was not associated with the risk of all‐cause mortality; however, there was a significant interaction by diabetes status (P interaction = 0.022) such that UACR was associated with mortality in non‐diabetics but not in individuals with diabetes (Table 3). Compared with the first tertile of UACR, non‐diabetics in the third tertile of UACR had 2.58 times higher risk of death; and each doubling of UACR was associated with 17% higher risk of death when evaluated, though this continuous association did not reach statistical significance (Figure 2, Table 3).

UACR and composite cardiovascular outcome

During a median of 59 days of follow‐up, 82 participants experienced the composite cardiovascular outcome. Baseline and Day 7 UACR were not associated with risk for the composite outcome, and no significant interaction by diabetes status was found (baseline UACR, P interaction = 0.108; Day 7 UACR, P interaction = 0.608) or treatment arm (Dopamine: P interaction = 0.777; nesiritide: P interaction = 0.883; Figure 3, Table 3). Discussion

In this study, evaluating the prognostic utility of albuminuria in AHF, we found albuminuria was not significantly associated with all‐cause mortality, HF hospitalization or cardiovascular mortality. However, the association of albuminuria at hospital admission with mortality was dependent on diabetes status, as baseline albuminuria in non‐diabetic patients was significantly associated with all‐cause mortality. These findings suggest further evaluation of UACR as a prognostic marker is warranted, especially in individuals without diabetes.

Multiple studies have shown a strong association between albuminuria and HF outcomes. Albuminuria has been shown to be a potent predictor of risk for developing HF in community‐based cohorts, with studies showing a 1.7 to 2.7 fold greater risk of developing HF. 17 , 18 , 19 In individuals with chronic HF, studies have also found that higher urinary albumin excretion is associated with worse cardiovascular outcomes and greater mortality risk. Previous studies in patients with chronic HF have demonstrated that, compared with normoalbuminuria, mortality risk is 1.18 to 1.80 times higher in patients with microalbuminuria and 1.33 to 2.48 times higher in those with macroalbuminuria. 8 , 20 , 21 Furthermore, UACR has been identified as an independent prognostic factor for cardiovascular or HF‐related hospitalization and cardiovascular mortality. 8 , 20

Fewer studies have evaluated the association of albuminuria with outcomes in AHF. A prior study found that albuminuria at admission was associated with a 1.28‐fold higher risk of all‐cause mortality, heart transplantation or left ventricular assist device implantation compared with those without albuminuria. 22 Another study found that higher UACR at admission was associated with greater risk of HF hospitalization within 1 year. 23 Our findings differ from these prior two studies as we largely did not find an association between albuminuria and outcomes in ROSE‐AHF. Potential reasons for these differences include ROSE‐AHF enrolling individuals exclusively with kidney dysfunction at admission, differences in HF severity between cohorts, lengths of follow‐up, sample sizes, and differences in the confounders adjusted for in models. While we largely did not find an association between UACR and outcomes, we did find diabetes status was an important effect modifier for the association between albuminuria and all‐cause mortality.

In AHF, albuminuria may result from acute changes in glomerular pressure, pathologic processes involved in cardiorenal syndrome, or progressive kidney dysfunction. 24 However, diabetes itself can induce albuminuria from end‐organ damage in the kidney that leads to loss of the glomerular basement membrane, mobilization of inflammatory cells and renal tubular dysfunction. 7 Thus, the presence and causes of albuminuria in AHF may have distinct implications in diabetics versus non‐diabetics. 25 , 26 , 27 Despite the importance of diabetes as a potential confounder for interpreting albuminuria in HF, only two prior studies have evaluated if the association between albuminuria and outcomes differs in diabetic and non‐diabetic patients with HF. These studies reported conflicting results with one study finding no difference based on diabetes status and the other reporting no association between albuminuria and outcomes in non‐diabetics after adjusting for confounders. 8 , 21 Importantly, both of these studies were in outpatients with chronic HF that likely do not have the same acute haemodynamic changes that occur in AHF nor the acute pathologic changes from cardiorenal syndrome. Thus, our study advances current literature by suggesting there are distinct prognostic implications for albuminuria in AHF based on diabetes status.

As noted, a potential cause of albuminuria in AHF is the acute haemodynamic effects of congestion on the kidneys. 6 , 28 While we did find an association between NT‐proBNP and albuminuria at admission, we did not find associations with other signs and symptoms of congestion. Though NT‐proBNP levels correlate with congestion status, they also reflect neurohormonal activation and HF severity, which may explain the association between NT‐proBNP and albuminuria. 29 , 30 , 31 Alternatively, higher NT‐proBNP levels may be correlating with progressive kidney dysfunction as levels increase with progressive declines in eGFR. 32 Thus, our findings suggest that the drivers of albuminuria in AHF are not solely related to congestion but also pathologic processes involved in cardiorenal syndrome like neurohormonal activation, inflammation and oxidative stress or from progressive declines in kidney function. 24

Our findings have important implications for future research. With the increasing focus on cardiovascular–kidney–metabolic health, it is important to recognize biomarkers that perform differently in diabetics and non‐diabetics. 33 Our findings exemplify this given the distinct association between albuminuria and mortality in diabetics and non‐diabetics. As novel therapies emerge to treat albuminuria, such as non‐steroidal MRA, sodium glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors, and glucagon‐like peptide 1 receptor agonists, the implications of changes in albuminuria with medication in diabetics and non‐diabetics warrant further evaluation. Our study suggests these agents may have a distinct effect as individuals in the highest tertile of albuminuria had the lowest prescription rates of MRAs. However, these findings are only hypothesis generating and require further research.

Our study has multiple strengths, including a patient population enrolled from multiple sites, systematically collected biologic specimens, and adjudicated outcomes. Our study also has important limitations. As a post‐hoc analysis and cross‐sectional study, our findings are only hypothesis‐generating and require further evaluation in other cohorts and prospectively. The study population was relatively small, and follow‐up was limited to 6 months for mortality and 2 months for cardiovascular outcomes; thus, we may have been underpowered to detect associations with longer follow‐up and more events. Changes in medical therapies by the treating physicians, other than the investigational therapies, and changes in other clinical parameters such as blood pressure or laboratory values during the study period may have introduced confounding factors that were not accounted for. While urine creatinine is often used to control for urine dilution, diuretics may further influence this relationship and indexing urine albumin to urine creatinine may be an imperfect surrogate and not fully account for the influence of diuretics on albuminuria. Because ROSE‐AHF only enrolled individuals with AHF and kidney dysfunction, our findings are not generalizable to other AHF populations. Given that the ROSE‐AHF trial was conducted over a decade ago, its findings should be interpreted cautiously in the context of contemporary AHF practice. Lastly, a substantial number of participants lacked UACR measurements at Day 7, limiting our power to detect associations with Day 7 values or changes in UACR between enrolment and Day 7.

In conclusion, we found that the presence of albuminuria in AHF is largely not associated with all‐cause mortality or cardiovascular outcomes. However, the association between albuminuria and mortality may depend on diabetes status, with greater albuminuria associated with greater risk of mortality in non‐diabetics but not diabetics. Further research is needed to determine if albuminuria's association with outcomes in AHF differs by diabetes status.
