Endogenous Estrogen Exposure and All-Cause Mortality Among Women with Overweight or Obesity According to Metabolic Health Status: Insights from an 18-Year Prospective Cohort Study
Authors: Sara Sadeghi, Maryam Barzin, Maryam Mahdavi, Majid Valizadeh, Fahimeh Ramezani Tehrani, Maryam Farahmand, Fereidoun Azizi, Farhad Hosseinpanah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDespite extensive research on estrogen and its implications, there remains a gap in understanding the relationship between the duration of endogenous estrogen exposure (EEE) and all-cause mortality among women with overweight or obesity according to their metabolic health status. Therefore, this study examined the association between EEE duration and all-cause mortality over an 18-year follow-up period, separately among women with metabolically healthy overweight/obesity (MHO) and those with metabolically unhealthy overweight/obesity (MUO).MethodsIn this large population-based cohort, 1162 post-menarcheal women aged ≥20 years with a body mass index (BMI) ≥25 kg/m2 from the Tehran Lipid and Glucose Study were included. Participants were classified into MHO and MUO phenotypes, and each phenotype was further divided into tertiles of EEE. Cox proportional hazards models were then applied to estimate incidence rates and hazard ratios (HRs) for all-cause mortality across EEE tertiles.ResultsDuring the follow-up, 62 deaths occurred (2.2 per 1000 person-years; 95% CI: 1.7–2.8), including 20 in the MHO group and 42 in the MUO group. Among individuals with MHO, incidence rates per 1000 person-years (95% CI) were 0.94 (0.42–2.11), 1.50 (0.81–2.79), and 0.60 (0.22–1.60) across the first, second, and third EEE tertiles, respectively. Corresponding rates for MUO were 6.46 (4.02–10.40), 3.42 (1.78–6.58), and 6.05 (3.71–9.88). After adjusting for confounders, the highest EEE tertile in MHO was associated with a significantly lower hazard of all-cause mortality (HR: 0.19; 95% CI: 0.05–0.76).ConclusionLonger EEE durations appear to reduce the risk of all-cause mortality among women with overweight/obesity who are metabolically healthy.

Introduction

Estrogen exerts a broad range of physiological effects that extend well beyond the reproductive system, influencing multiple organ systems and contributing to the regulation and pathophysiology of various conditions, including metabolic disorders, obesity, type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), cardiovascular disease (CVD), musculoskeletal disorders, hepatic dysfunction, and neurodegenerative diseases.1 Notably, estrogen demonstrates both protective and deleterious effects, as fluctuations in exposure have been linked to differential risks of certain cancers and variations in both all-cause and cause-specific mortality.2,3 The concept of endogenous estrogen exposure (EEE) encompasses the cumulative lifetime exposure of a woman to estrogen, which is modulated by reproductive and hormonal factors such as age at menarche and menopause, parity, duration of lactation, and use of oral contraceptives (OC).4

Obesity is a major and increasingly prevalent public health concern worldwide, with a particularly substantial burden among women. In Iran, Shirvani et al reported a consistently higher prevalence of obesity among women than men, increasing from 22.62% in 2006 to 40.17% in 2019 and projected to reach 52.84% by 2040.5 Beyond its increasing prevalence, the classification of obesity as a disease has been subject to ongoing debate, partly owing to the complexity of defining disease and the substantial heterogeneity in its phenotypic and metabolic manifestations. In this context, individuals with overweight or obesity are often characterized according to their metabolic status as having metabolically healthy overweight/obesity (MHO) or metabolically unhealthy overweight/obesity (MUO).6,7 MHO generally refers to individuals with overweight/obesity who exhibit a relatively favorable metabolic profile, characterized by preserved insulin sensitivity, normotension, normolipidemia, low-grade inflammation, and relatively preserved hormonal, hepatic, and immune function.8 In contrast, MUO is defined by a similar excess body weight phenotype but distinguished by the presence of metabolic abnormalities. Although several studies have reported lower risks of T2DM, CVD, and all-cause mortality among individuals with MHO compared with those with MUO,9,10 accumulating evidence suggests that MHO may not represent a stable or entirely benign state. Rather, individuals with MHO may progress over time to a metabolically unhealthy phenotype and remain susceptible to obesity-related metabolic complications.11–13

The increasing prevalence of obesity, together with the substantial metabolic heterogeneity among women with overweight or obesity, raises the question of whether lifetime EEE is differentially associated with long-term health outcomes according to metabolic phenotype. However, evidence regarding the association between EEE duration and mortality among women with MHO and MUO remains limited. In this context, the Tehran Lipid and Glucose Study (TLGS), a well-characterized prospective cohort with long-term follow-up and detailed reproductive and metabolic data, provides an appropriate setting to investigate this association in Iranian women. Accordingly, the present study aimed to investigate the association between EEE duration and all-cause mortality over an 18-year follow-up period, separately among women with MHO and MUO.

Methods

Study Design and Participants

Study participants were recruited from the TLGS. TLGS is an ongoing, population-based, prospective cohort study initiated in 1999. It is a representative sample of urban Tehranian residents designed to determine the prevalence and incidence of non-communicable disease risk factors. The details of TLGS are available elsewhere.14 In brief, the TLGS comprises an initial cross-sectional assessment of non-communicable diseases and risk factors (Phase I, 1999–2001) with prospective triennial follow-ups updating demographic, biochemical, clinical, anthropometric, and reproductive data across subsequent phases: II (2002–2005), III (2006–2008), IV (2009–2011), V (2012–2015), VI (2016–2019), and VII (2020–2023).

The present study included all post-menarcheal women, either reproductive or menopausal (natural or surgical), aged ≥20 years, with no history of hormone replacement therapy (HRT), who completed relevant assessments during the first (1999–2001) or second (2002–2005) phases of the TLGS (n = 7196). Participants with a body mass index (BMI) <25 kg/m2 (n = 2245), insufficient data to calculate EEE (n = 3332), active cancer (n = 9), or no follow-up evaluations (n = 4) were excluded. Ultimately, 1606 women were eligible and followed until death from any cause or the end of the study period, whichever occurred first (Figure 1). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment, and the study protocol was approved by the Research Institute for Endocrine Sciences’ Ethics Committee (approval number: IR.SBMU.ENDOCRINE.REC.1403.036). Measurements

Data on demographic and lifestyle characteristics, non-communicable disease risk factors, and individual, familial, and reproductive medical histories were collected through direct interviews conducted by trained personnel using a standardized 110-item questionnaire.15,16 Anthropometric measurements were obtained following standardized protocols: weight and height were measured in light clothing and without shoes using calibrated equipment, and body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight (kg) divided by height squared (m2). Waist circumference (WC) was measured midway between the lower rib margin and the iliac crest, at the level of the umbilicus, to the nearest 0.1 cm at the end of gentle expiration. Blood pressure (BP) was measured twice on the right arm in a seated position after 15 minutes of rest, with a 5-minute interval between measurements; the mean value was used for analysis. Blood samples were collected between 7:00 and 9:00 a.m. after 12–14 hours of overnight fasting, and analyses were performed on the same day. Additional details on protocols, laboratory procedures, and assay methods quality control have been reported elsewhere.15

Physical activity was assessed using the lipid research clinic (LRC) questionnaire17 during Phase I of the TLGS. However, due to the subjective and qualitative nature of the LRC, it was subsequently replaced by Modifiable Activity Questionnaire (MAQ)18 and a Persian translated form of that is used to assess physical activity level in the TLGS participants.15 MAQ assesses physical activity across household, occupational, and leisure domains and estimates total activity in metabolic equivalent task (MET) minutes per week for subsequent follow-up phases. Because the LRC did not capture activity duration, participants in Phase I were classified as physically active if they reported engaging in intense physical activity at least three days per week. For those assessed in Phase II and later, physical activity was defined as achieving more than 600 MET-minutes/week.

Definitions

A WC ≥90 cm was considered the cutoff predictive of CVD and all-cause mortality.19 T2DM was defined as fasting plasma glucose (FPG) ≥126 mg/dL, 2-h post 75 g oral plasma glucose (2hPG) load ≥200 mg/dL, or current use of glucose-lowering medication.20 Current smokers were defined as individuals who reported using any form of tobacco, including cigarettes, pipes, or water pipes, at the time of the interview.21 Premature coronary artery disease (CAD) was defined as the first clinical manifestation of CAD occurring before the age of 60 years.22 All-cause mortality was defined as death from any cause during the follow-up period. In cases of death, data were obtained by an authorized local physician from hospital records or death certificates and subsequently verified by the TLGS outcome committee.

The MHO phenotype was defined as a BMI ≥25 kg/m2 accompanied by none or only one of the following four metabolic syndrome (MetS) components, excluding WC:23,24 1) Triglyceride (TG) ≥150 mg/dl or use of lipid-lowering medication; 2) high-density lipoprotein-cholesterol (HDL-C) <40 mg/dl in men or <50 mg/dl in women, or use of lipid-lowering medication; 3) systolic blood pressure (SBP) ≥135 mmHg, diastolic blood pressure (DBP) ≥85 mmHg, or use of antihypertensive medication; and 4) FPG ≥100 mg/dl or use of glucose-lowering medication. The MUO phenotype was defined as BMI ≥25 kg/m2 plus the presence of two or more of the above MetS components.

Age at menarche was defined as the age at onset of first menstrual bleeding. Natural menopause was defined, in accordance with the World Health Organization (WHO) criteria,25 as the permanent cessation of menstruation for 12 consecutive months not attributable to any pathological or physiological cause. The time point one year before the completion of the 12-month amenorrhea period was considered the date of menopause. For women with surgical menopause, the date of surgery was regarded as the end of estrogen exposure. EEE was defined as the duration between age at menarche and age at menopause, incident mortality, or end of follow-up, whichever occurred first. To determine the estrogen-dominant (follicular) period, the cumulative durations of pregnancies (40 weeks per live birth and 20 weeks per abortion), progesterone-dominant OC use, breastfeeding (months per child), and luteal phases of menstrual cycles (2 weeks per cycle) were subtracted from this interval.21

Statistics

Normally distributed continuous variables were presented as mean (SD), while skewed continuous variables were expressed as median (interquartile range). Categorical variables were summarized as frequency (percentage). Differences in baseline characteristics across tertiles of EEE duration, defined according to the duration of EEE, were assessed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), the Kruskal–Wallis test, or the Chi-square test for normally distributed, skewed, and categorical variables, respectively. No post hoc pairwise analyses were performed, as the objective was to assess overall differences across EEE tertiles rather than to make specific pairwise comparisons between individual tertiles. Comparisons between included and excluded participants within the MUO phenotype were conducted using independent-sample t tests, Mann–Whitney U-tests, or Chi-square tests, as appropriate. Cox proportional hazards regression models were applied to examine the association between EEE tertiles and all-cause mortality. To assess potential unmeasured confounding, E-values were calculated to estimate the minimum strength of association that an unmeasured confounder would need to have with both exposure and outcome to fully explain the observed association.26 All statistical analyses were conducted using Stata version 14.0 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX, USA), and two-tailed p-values <0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Based on the exclusion criterion of incomplete information regarding the EEE course, 2183 of the 2627 eligible individuals with the MUO phenotype were excluded, resulting in a final analytical sample of 444 participants. Comparative assessment of baseline characteristics between included and excluded MUO subjects indicated that, although the included group exhibited clinically meaningful higher prevalence of HTN and low physical activity, the excluded group demonstrated greater mean WC as well as a higher prevalence of elevated WC and abdominal obesity (Supplementary Table 1).

Hazard ratios (HRs) for incident all-cause mortality across tertiles of EEE were calculated separately for the MHO and MUO phenotypes (Table 3). Among participants with MHO, the incidence rates per 1000 person-year (95% CI) were 0.94 (0.42–2.11), 1.50 (0.81–2.79), and 0.60 (0.22–1.60) across the first, second, and third tertiles, respectively. As a sensitivity analysis, we computed the E-value to determine how strongly an unmeasured confounder would need to be associated with both EEE and all-cause mortality to fully account for the observed association in the third EEE tertile of the MHO phenotype. The calculated E-value was 9.57, indicating that only an unmeasured confounder with a very large effect could explain away the observed relationship. This high E-value provides reassurance that the association is unlikely to be solely attributable to unmeasured confounding.

Discussion

Findings from this longitudinal, community-based cohort study with 18 years of follow-up indicate that, within the context of metabolic health, prolonged exposure to endogenous estrogen is independently associated with a lower risk of all-cause mortality. Additionally, the results reaffirm the prognostic distinction between obesity phenotypes: compared with the MHO phenotype, MUO exhibit markedly higher risks and incidence rates of all-cause mortality.

Pre-menopausal women exhibit a lower risk of CVD compared with same-aged men and post-menopausal women, a difference largely attributed to the protective effects of endogenous estrogen.27–29 EEE was first introduced as a quantitative measure in 20024 and has since been applied in numerous epidemiologic investigations. Findings from the TLGS, including 3656 women with a mean age of 45.7 years and a median follow-up of 14.2 years, demonstrated that shorter EEE durations were associated with increased CVD risk.21 Similarly, in another TLGS investigation of 3045 women (mean age: 43.1 years) followed over 15 years, shorter EEE durations were identified as a risk factor for chronic kidney disease.30

However, evidence regarding the relationship between EEE and all-cause mortality remains inconsistent. A study of 4076 post-menopausal women in the Netherlands (median age: 69.1 years; median follow-up: 16.6 years) reported that longer durations of estrogen exposure were associated with higher all-cause mortality.31 In contrast, data from 11,669 post-menopausal women in the Swedish Twin Registry (mean age: 63.5 years; maximum follow-up: 19 years) indicated that longer reproductive lifespan was associated with reduced all-cause mortality.32 We hypothesized that these discrepancies may be attributable to differences in metabolic health status. Accordingly, we examined the association between EEE and all-cause mortality across two distinct overweight/obesity phenotypes, MHO and MUO. Our findings indicate that, within the metabolically healthy context of the MHO phenotype, longer EEE durations were associated with a lower risk of all-cause mortality. In contrast, among women with MUO, this pattern was not observed to the same extent, possibly due to the higher burden of cardiometabolic risk factors in this group, highlighting differences in the observed associations between EEE duration and all-cause mortality across the two metabolic phenotypes.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first large, population-based cohort study with 18 years of follow-up to examine the relationship between EEE and all-cause mortality stratified by metabolic health status in women with overweight or obesity. The inclusion criteria enabled the evaluation of both pre-menopausal and post-menopausal women aged ≥20 years, and the availability of relevant clinical and biochemical variables allowed for comprehensive multivariable adjustment in the Cox models. Furthermore, the calculation of the E-value provided a rigorous sensitivity analysis to assess the robustness of the observed associations against potential unmeasured confounding. Despite these strengths, the study has several limitations. First, recall bias may have occurred, as key reproductive variables including age at menarche and menopause, breastfeeding duration, and hormonal contraceptive use, were assessed through self-report questionnaires. Second, the application of a single value to approximate the follicular phase, along with the exclusion of the progesterone-dominant phase of the menstrual cycle and pregnancy from EEE calculations, may introduce misclassification, particularly given the limited understanding of progesterone’s cardiovascular effects. Third, although TLGS benefits from a large sample size, a considerable proportion of MUO participants were excluded because of insufficient reproductive history data. Comparison of included and excluded MUO women showed only minor differences, namely, lower WC and higher prevalence of HTN among those retained, suggesting that substantial selection bias may be less likely, although it cannot be completely excluded. Therefore, the possibility that exclusion related to the availability of reproductive history data may have affected the representativeness of the final analytical sample should be acknowledged. Finally, socioeconomic and nutritional factors, which may influence both metabolic health and mortality, were not available and therefore could not be incorporated into the analyses.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this 18-year follow-up cohort study suggest that longer EEE duration is associated with a lower risk of all-cause mortality among women with MHO, whereas this association was not observed among those with MUO. These findings add to the growing evidence on the potential long-term health implications of reproductive estrogen exposure by highlighting the importance of considering metabolic health when evaluating the relationship between EEE and mortality. Further prospective studies in diverse populations are warranted to replicate these findings and to clarify the mechanisms underlying the differential associations of EEE with mortality across metabolic phenotypes. Future research should also investigate whether these associations vary according to age, specific causes of mortality, and other reproductive and cardiometabolic characteristics, as well as the potential modifying roles of nutritional factors and socioeconomic status, which may influence both metabolic health and long-term mortality risk.
