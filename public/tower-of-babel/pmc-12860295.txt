Ruptured spinal dural arteriovenous fistula with subdural hematoma: A case report
Authors: Lauren Elizabeth Williamson, Brian Fabian Saway, Robert John Weil, Alejandro Marin Spiotta, Roberta Kay Sefcik
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background: Spinal dural arteriovenous fistulas (SDAVFs) are the most common spinal vascular malformations and can lead to significant neurological deficits if left untreated. Although typically associated with chronic myelopathy, rupture of SDAVFs is a rare yet severe complication that can result in acute spinal cord injury secondary to intradural hematoma expansion.Case Description: A 53-year-old male presented with fluctuating neck and back pain, unsteady gait, right leg numbness, and urinary retention, eventually attributed to a ruptured thoracic SDAVF resulting in a T3-T4 subdural hematoma. The original magnetic resonance imaging demonstrated a subdural collection with severe spinal cord compression, without any evidence of flow-voids indicative of a vascular malformation; the emergent spinal angiogram was negative for any vascular abnormalities. An emergent T2-3 laminectomy was performed for hematoma evacuation and fistula ligation (i.e., after identification of the fistula intraoperatively). Postoperatively, the patient gradually improved.Conclusion: Early recognition and emergent surgical management of spinal subdural hematomas due to SDAVF ruptures are critical. Adequate preoperative preparation for open-vascular management of these lesions may be warranted despite negative preoperative vascular imaging.

INTRODUCTION

Spinal dural arteriovenous fistulas (SDAVFs) are the predominant type of spinal vascular malformations (50–85% of cases). [4] They typically present with progressive myelopathy due to venous congestion, impaired spinal cord perfusion, and subsequent tissue hypoxia.[10,12] Symptoms may develop gradually, fluctuate, or worsen in a stepwise fashion, often leading to misdiagnosis and delayed treatment; untreated lesions may result in irreversible paralysis.[4,10] Magnetic resonance (MR) is the imaging modality of choice for diagnosing SDAVFs and typically reveals T2-WI consistent with spinal cord edema and perimedullary flow voids suggestive of abnormal vasculature.[4] Definitive diagnosis/precise localization of these fistulas requires digital subtraction angiography (the gold standard).[4,12] Here, a 53-year-old male presented with the rare rupture of a thoracic SDAVF, resulting in a spinal subdural hematoma with severe cord compression.

CASE PRESENTATION

A 53-year-old male with a history of atrial fibrillation on rivaroxaban, heart failure, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, and prune-belly syndrome (PBS: characterized by deficient/absent abdominal musculature and genitourinary abnormalities) presented with a 4-day history of upper neck and back pain that progressed to mid/low back pain and sensory loss over the right anterior thigh/knee. Within the past 1–2 days, he additionally developed urinary hesitancy. His neurologic examination revealed right lower extremity/perineal numbness and urinary retention.

The thoracic computed tomography revealed a hyperattenuating dorsal spinal canal lesion from T1-4, asymmetric to the right, with severe cord compression at T2-3; a hematoma was suspected. Thoracic MR imaging with and without contrast demonstrated a spinal subdural hematoma extending from T3-4 [Figures 1a and b]. The patient emergently underwent a spinal angiogram, but it was negative for a vascular malformation. He was then emergently taken for surgical decompression and hematoma evacuation.

(b) Axial T2-weighted image at the T3 level demonstrating an intradural lesion eccentric to the right side (white arrow), displacing the cord to the left. No serpiginous intradural flow voids are appreciated. (c) Intraoperative photograph demonstrating incised and expanded dura with exposure of the underlying subdural hematoma. (d) Spinal dural arteriovenous fistula traversing the dura adjacent to the exiting T2 nerve root.

Surgery

Following a T2-3 laminectomy, intraoperative ultrasound confirmed the presence of a subdural hematoma compressing the nonpulsatile cord. After evacuating the subdural hematoma, further exploration revealed an arterialized vein adjacent to the right T2 nerve root, adherent to clot and membranes, consistent with an SDAVF [Figures 1c and d]. The nerve root was mobilized away from the fistula with microdissectors; the fistula was temporarily clipped and sensory and motor evoked potentials were monitored. With no change in evoked potentials for over 13 min, the fistula was then ligated with bipolar cautery and microscissors. Intraoperative ultrasound confirmed complete evacuation of the hematoma. Postoperatively, the patient regained normal strength and sensation but had mild proprioceptive deficits in the right lower extremity affecting his gait, which gradually improved with inpatient rehabilitation, but he continued to experience urinary retention.

DISCUSSION

The clinical manifestations of SDAVFs reflect this progressive spinal cord dysfunction, often presenting initially with nonspecific neurologic symptoms that can complicate timely diagnosis.[4,12] In a retrospective review of 27 patients with SDAVFs, Rosenblum et al.[6] reported that the most common initial symptoms were paresis and sensory disturbances, while bowel and bladder dysfunction, back pain, and impotence were more frequently observed at the time of diagnosis.[6] In addition, Jellema et al. found gait disturbances as the initial presentation in 34% of their cohort of 80 patients.[3]

Spinal subdural hematomas secondary to AVF rupture remain exceedingly rare, with only a handful of cases described in the literature.[2,7] The pathophysiology underlying SDAVF-related hemorrhage is not fully understood but may involve venous hypertension causing rupture of fragile perimedullary veins or secondary disruption of vessel walls due to increased shear stress and hypoxia.[10]

The negative preoperative angiogram in this case represents a rare but significant diagnostic challenge. While spinal angiography is considered the gold standard for detecting spinal vascular malformations, its sensitivity may be compromised in the setting of mass effect.[9] In our patient, the subdural hematoma likely obscured the fistula, rendering it angiographically occult and delaying diagnosis until surgical exploration. A similar phenomenon was described by Han et al.,[1] in which spinal angiography failed to reveal a symptomatic thoracic SDAVF; however, T2-weighted MRI demonstrated a low-intensity signal attributed to degrading blood products within the spinal canal.[1] In our case, the patient’s history of PBS, a condition characterized by genitourinary anomalies and deficient abdominal musculature, added a unique dimension to the clinical scenario.[1]

Role of surgery

Following confirmation of an SDAVF, prompt intervention is critical to prevent further neurologic deterioration [Table 1]. The primary therapeutic goal is permanent occlusion of the arterialized draining vein at the site of the fistulous connection, thereby eliminating venous congestion.[4,8] This can be achieved by either open surgical ligation or endovascular embolization. Surgical ligation offers direct visualization and disconnection of the fistula, with high success rates of 85–95% reported in retrospective series and 98% reported in a meta-analysis.[8] In contrast, endovascular embolization is less invasive but may be limited if the fistulous point involves critical vessels such as the artery of Adamkiewicz.[11] Importantly, incomplete embolization may lead to recanalization and recurrence, necessitating subsequent surgical intervention.[11]

CONCLUSION

This case highlights that SDAVFs may present with negative angiographic findings due to spinal subdural hematoma compression, requiring high clinical suspicion and readiness for exploratory surgery.
