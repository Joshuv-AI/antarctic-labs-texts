Development and temporal validation of nomograms and machine learning models for predicting overall and cancer-specific survival in primary distal cholangiocarcinoma: a SEER-based study
Authors: Wentao Shang, Xibin Duan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDistal cholangiocarcinoma (dCCA) differs from intrahepatic and perihilar cholangiocarcinoma in anatomical origin, surgical management, and molecular profile, yet most existing prognostic tools either pool all biliary tract cancers together or rely on anatomical Tumor Node Metastasis (TNM) staging alone. A dCCA-specific model integrating clinical, treatment, and tumor-burden variables is therefore needed. We aimed to develop and evaluate nomograms and machine learning models for predicting overall survival (OS) and cancer-specific survival (CSS) in patients with primary dCCA.MethodsWe identified 1,515 patients with primary dCCA diagnosed between 2010 and 2017 in the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) database. Patients diagnosed in 2010–2015 were randomly divided into a training cohort (70%, n=773) and an internal testing cohort (30%, n=331); patients diagnosed in 2016–2017 (n=411) formed a temporal validation cohort with no calendar-year overlap. Independent predictors were selected by univariable and multivariable Cox regression for OS and by Fine-Gray subdistribution hazard modeling for CSS, and were used to construct nomograms predicting 1-, 3-, and 5-year survival. Four machine learning survival models—decision tree, random survival forest, gradient boosting survival analysis, and an XGBoost-based accelerated failure time model (XGBAFT)—were trained for OS prediction and interpreted with SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP). Performance was assessed by Harrell’s concordance index (C-index), time-dependent area under the curve (AUC), calibration plots, and decision curve analysis, and benchmarked against the eighth-edition American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) TNM staging system.ResultsThe development cohort was predominantly male (53.2%) and aged over 65 years (61.6%); 28.9% underwent radical resection of the primary site and 47.6% received chemotherapy, with a median observed survival of 8 months. For OS, the nomogram achieved a C-index of 0.754 and 1-, 3-, and 5-year AUCs of 0.854, 0.868, and 0.871 in the internal testing cohort, and a C-index of 0.742 with AUCs of 0.830, 0.836, and 0.843 in the temporal validation cohort. For CSS, the corresponding C-indices were 0.726 and 0.710, with AUCs of 0.813/0.797/0.760 and 0.792/0.712/0.702. Both nomograms outperformed the eighth-edition TNM system (OS C-index, 0.585; CSS C-index, 0.635) and showed good calibration and net clinical benefit. Among the machine learning models, XGBAFT achieved the highest discrimination (AUCs, 0.861/0.834/0.831 internally and 0.807/0.803/0.789 temporally). SHAP identified chemotherapy, primary-site radical surgery, regional lymph node surgery as the most influential predictors. The nomogram stratified patients into low-, intermediate-, and high-risk groups with significantly divergent survival curves (P<0.001).ConclusionsWithin the SEER population, our dCCA-specific nomograms and the XGBAFT model provided accurate, well-calibrated, and interpretable predictions of OS and CSS, outperformed eighth-edition TNM staging, and supported effective risk stratification. Because both cohorts were drawn from SEER, these results constitute internal and temporal validation; prospective multicenter studies in geographically and ethnically diverse cohorts are required before clinical implementation.

Highlight box

Key findings

• We developed and temporally validated distal cholangiocarcinoma (dCCA)-specific nomograms and four machine learning survival models for 1-, 3-, and 5-year overall and cancer-specific survival in 1,515 Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) patients with primary dCCA.

• Both the Cox nomograms and the XGBoost-based accelerated failure time model (XGBAFT) achieved good discrimination and calibration and substantially outperformed the eighth-edition American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) Tumor Node Metastasis (TNM) staging system.

• SHAP analysis identified chemotherapy, primary-site radical surgery, and regional lymph node surgery as the dominant prognostic factors, and the nomogram-derived risk strata showed clearly divergent survival curves across development, internal testing, and temporal validation cohorts.

What is known and what is new?

• It is known that dCCA is an anatomically, surgically, and molecularly distinct subtype of cholangiocarcinoma, and that the eighth-edition AJCC TNM system provides only coarse prognostic stratification within this entity.

• This study adds a dCCA-specific, transparent prognostic framework that integrates clinical, socioeconomic, tumor-burden, and treatment variables; benchmarks a Cox nomogram against four representative survival learners on identical inputs; uses SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) to make the best-performing model interpretable; and confirms stable performance in a calendar-year hold-out cohort.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• Within the SEER population, the proposed nomograms and XGBAFT model can support individualized prognostic discussion, risk-stratified surveillance, and shared decision-making about adjuvant therapy in dCCA.

• Before routine clinical use, the models require prospective multicenter external validation in geographically and ethnically diverse cohorts and ideally augmentation with molecular markers and detailed treatment variables not captured in SEER.

Introduction

Background

Cholangiocarcinoma (CCA) is the second most common primary hepatic malignancy, accounting for ~15% of primary liver tumors and 3% of gastrointestinal cancers (1,2). It is anatomically classified into intrahepatic CCA (iCCA), perihilar CCA (pCCA), and distal CCA (dCCA) subtypes, which differ substantially in surgical management, molecular landscape, and clinical outcome (3-5). dCCA, arising between the cystic duct insertion and the ampulla of Vater, accounts for approximately 30% of all CCA cases (6,7). Patients typically present with painless obstructive jaundice and nonspecific symptoms that overlap with other periampullary neoplasms, often delaying diagnosis until locally advanced disease (8,9). Pancreaticoduodenectomy with regional lymphadenectomy remains the only potentially curative treatment, and margin-negative (R0) resection is the strongest modifiable determinant of long-term survival (10-14). Even after optimal surgery, nearly half of patients recur within the first postoperative year, reported 5-year overall survival (OS) varies widely (16–66%), and lymph node metastasis, positive margins, high grade, and perineural invasion are repeatedly identified as adverse prognostic factors (15-17).

Rationale and knowledge gap

Although several prognostic tools have been developed for biliary tract cancers, most either pool all CCA subtypes or focus on iCCA or pCCA, leaving dCCA underrepresented. SEER-based nomograms have been built for iCCA using American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC)-derived variables, while perihilar-specific models incorporating Bismuth-Corlette type and vascular invasion cannot be transferred to distal disease (18-22). Existing dCCA-specific studies have generally been limited by small samples, reliance on Tumor Node Metastasis (TNM) stage alone, and a lack of external or temporal validation (23).

Three lines of evidence justify treating dCCA as a distinct entity. Surgically, it arises from the intrapancreatic bile duct and is managed by pancreaticoduodenectomy rather than hepatectomy or hilar resection, yielding a different perioperative risk and recurrence profile. Biologically, dCCA carries a higher prevalence of KRAS, TP53, SMAD4, and ARID1A alterations and a near-absence of the IDH1/2 and FGFR2 aberrations characteristic of iCCA (24) . Prognostically, dCCA shows a distinct pattern of regional lymph node spread, and the eighth edition of the AJCC TNM system provides only coarse stratification within this subtype while incorporating a limited set of clinical variables. A dCCA-specific framework is therefore expected to outperform tools derived from mixed biliary cohorts (25,26).

Cox proportional-hazards regression remains the standard survival modeling tool but assumes log-linear effects and proportional hazards—assumptions frequently violated in surgical oncology cohorts (27). Machine learning methods can relax these assumptions and capture higher-order interactions, but algorithms differ markedly in their inductive biases and none can be assumed optimal a priori. We therefore selected four learners spanning the principal methodological families of survival analysis: a survival decision tree (DT), as a transparent nonparametric baseline; random survival forest (RSF), which reduces variance through bagging and resists overfitting in moderately sized clinical datasets; gradient boosting survival analysis (GBSA), which stage-wise refines the cumulative hazard and typically outperforms bagging when interactions are subtle; and an XGBoost-based accelerated failure time model (XGBAFT), which models survival time directly rather than the hazard and is attractive when the proportional-hazards assumption is in doubt (28). Benchmarking these four—a single tree, a bagged ensemble, a boosted hazard ensemble, and a boosted AFT model—against a Cox nomogram allows a clinically meaningful assessment of incremental discrimination and the interpretability–performance trade-off.

Objective

In this study, we used the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) database to develop nomograms for OS and cancer-specific survival (CSS) in patients with primary dCCA, with internal testing and a calendar-year hold-out cohort for temporal validation. We additionally trained and compared the four machine learning models for OS prediction, applied SHAP analysis to interpret feature contributions, and derived a nomogram-based risk stratification scheme. Our aim was to provide a transparent, dCCA-specific prognostic framework that can support individualized prognostic assessment within the SEER population and serve as a basis for subsequent external multicenter validation. We present this article in accordance with the TRIPOD reporting checklist (available at https://tcr.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tcr-2026-0780/rc).

Methods

Study design and cohort construction

This was a retrospective prognostic model development and validation study conducted in accordance with the TRIPOD reporting framework. Data were extracted from the SEER program of the U.S. National Cancer Institute, which captures cancer incidence and survival information for approximately 34.6% of the U.S. population. We used SEER/*Stat v8.4.2 to query all incident cases of primary dCCA diagnosed between 1 January 2010 and 31 December 2017, with survival follow-up updated through 2022. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. Because SEER data are de-identified and publicly available, the study was exempt from institutional review board approval and from individual informed consent; all investigators completed the SEER Research Data Agreement before data extraction.

Eligible cases were identified by ICD-O-3 histology codes 8140, 8160, 8260, 8480, and 8490 in combination with ICD-O-3 primary site code C24.0 (extrahepatic bile duct), restricted to tumors anatomically classified as distal by CS Site-Specific Factor 25 (value =10; coding scheme valid for 2004–2017). We excluded patients who (I) had missing or unknown values for survival time, vital status, or cause of death; (II) were not coded as primary dCCA; or (III) were younger than 18 years at diagnosis. After applying these criteria, 1,515 patients with primary dCCA were included in the analytical cohort. A detailed case-selection flow diagram is shown in Figure 1.

To minimize selection bias relative to the broader U.S. dCCA population, no further restrictions were imposed on race, sex, treatment received, or tumor stage. Although SEER is geographically representative of the U.S. population, the cohort may still underrepresent patients managed in non-SEER registries or outside the United States; this is addressed in the Limitations.

Development, internal testing, and temporal validation cohorts

To preserve the temporal logic of model evaluation, the cohort was partitioned by calendar year of diagnosis before any modeling was performed. Patients diagnosed between 2010 and 2015 (n=1,104) constituted the development cohort and were further split at random into a training set (70%, n=773) for model fitting and an internal testing set (30%, n=331) for unbiased estimation of in-sample performance. Patients diagnosed in 2016–2017 (n=411) were held out as a temporal validation cohort and were not used at any stage of variable selection, model fitting, or hyperparameter tuning. There was therefore no overlap in calendar year between the development and temporal validation cohorts.

Predictors and outcomes

Candidate predictors were defined a priori on the basis of clinical plausibility and prior literature, and included age at diagnosis, sex, race, marital status, urban/rural residence, household income, tumor size, T stage, N stage, M stage, radical surgery of the primary site, radiotherapy, chemotherapy, regional lymph node surgery, surgery for distant metastasis, and the lymph node ratio (LNR, defined as the number of positive regional nodes divided by the number of nodes examined). LNR was trichotomized as low (<0.1), med (0.1–0.3), and high (>0.3), with high as the reference group. Household income was trichotomized as low (<$80,000) and high (≥$80,000), with high as the reference group.

Disease stage was assigned according to the AJCC eighth edition. For cases in which the eighth-edition stage was not directly available in SEER, stage was reconstructed from the sixth- or seventh-edition TNM classification together with documented tumor characteristics (including tumor size and extent).

The two primary outcomes were OS, defined as the interval from diagnosis to death from any cause or last follow-up, and CSS, defined as the interval from diagnosis to death directly attributable to dCCA, with deaths from unrelated causes treated as a competing event. Both outcomes were modeled at 1-, 3-, and 5-year horizons.

Cases with missing outcome data were excluded. For categorical variables with a high proportion of missingness (e.g., T stage), missing values were retained as an explicit "Unknown" category to preserve sample size. For categorical variables with only a small proportion of missing values, the corresponding cases were removed. The exact missingness proportions for each variable are detailed in Table 1.

Sample size and events per variable

The analytical sample size was determined by the number of eligible incident dCCA cases recorded in SEER during the study period, rather than by a prespecified power calculation. To assess whether this sample was adequate for stable model fitting, we evaluated the events-per-variable (EPV) ratio in the training cohort, following the widely used recommendation of at least 10 events per candidate predictor for Cox regression. The OS nomogram included 11 predictors retained after multivariable selection; with more than 700 deaths observed in the 773-patient training cohort, the EPV exceeded 60, well above the conventional threshold. The CSS model included 8 retained predictors and was fitted to a comparable number of cancer-specific events, again yielding an EPV well above 10 under the Fine-Gray framework. Performance in the internal testing cohort (n=331) and the temporal validation cohort (n=411) provided additional safeguards against overfitting, with the latter offering a more conservative estimate of out-of-period performance.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were summarized as median (range) and categorical variables as counts (percentages). Between-group comparisons of baseline characteristics were performed with the Mann-Whitney U test for continuous variables and the χ² or Fisher exact test for categorical variables, as appropriate.

Variable selection

Candidate predictors were screened in the training cohort by univariable Cox proportional hazards regression for OS. Variables with P<0.05 were carried forward into a multivariable Cox model, from which independent prognostic factors were identified by backward stepwise selection based on the Akaike information criterion (AIC). For CSS, the same screening strategy was applied within a Fine-Gray subdistribution hazard framework, in which death from causes unrelated to dCCA was treated as a competing event. The proportional hazards assumption was assessed by inspection of scaled Schoenfeld residuals, and multicollinearity among retained predictors was evaluated by the variance inflation factor (VIF), with VIF <5 considered acceptable.

Nomogram construction

Two nomograms were constructed from the final multivariable Cox model (OS) and Fine-Gray model (CSS) to predict 1-, 3-, and 5-year survival probabilities. Total points and corresponding survival probabilities were derived directly from the regression coefficients of the retained predictors.

Machine learning models. Four survival learners—DT, RSF, GBSA, and an XGBAFT—were trained on the same training cohort and predictors as the OS nomogram, so that model comparisons reflected algorithmic differences rather than differences in inputs. Hyperparameters were tuned by 5-fold cross-validation within the training cohort using a grid search, with the integrated Brier score as the tuning objective; the internal testing and temporal validation cohorts were not used during tuning. Categorical predictors were one-hot encoded, and continuous predictors were retained on their original scale. To improve the transparency of the best-performing learner, SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) values were computed to quantify both the global importance and the directional effect of each predictor on individual predictions.

Performance evaluation

Discrimination was assessed by Harrell’s concordance index (C-index) and by the time-dependent area under the curve (AUC) at 1, 3, and 5 years. Calibration was evaluated graphically by comparing predicted and observed survival probabilities at each time horizon using bootstrap-resampled calibration curves (1,000 resamples). Clinical utility was examined by decision curve analysis (DCA), which quantifies net benefit across a range of threshold probabilities. The same metrics were applied uniformly to the Cox nomogram, the four machine learning models, and the eighth-edition AJCC TNM staging system to enable a direct head-to-head comparison. We did not prespecify a single AUC threshold for “clinical utility”; instead, performance was interpreted using the conventional guideposts that AUC values above 0.70 indicate acceptable discrimination, above 0.80 good discrimination, and above 0.90 excellent discrimination, in combination with calibration and DCA findings. Predicted survival probabilities (for the nomograms) and predicted survival times (for the XGBAFT model) were obtained for both the internal testing cohort and the temporal validation cohort by applying the final models fitted in the training cohort to each patient’s predictor values. No model updating or recalibration was performed during validation.

Risk stratification

Patients were stratified into low-, intermediate-, and high-risk groups on the basis of the linear predictor of each nomogram, using cut-points selected in the training cohort to balance group size and prognostic separation. Survival curves between strata were compared by the Kaplan-Meier method and the log-rank test.

All analyses were performed in R version 4.5.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) using the survival, rms, cmprsk, timeROC, randomForestSRC, gbm, xgboost, and survex/SurvSHAP packages, and in SPSS version 27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) for descriptive analyses. All tests were two-sided, and P<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

The development cohort was predominantly male (53.2%; n=587) and over 65 years of age (61.6%; n=680), with a median observed survival time of 8 months (range, 1–149 months). The median follow-up, estimated by the reverse Kaplan-Meier method, was 114 months [95% confidence interval (CI): 108–132], indicating sufficient observation time to support long-term (5-year) survival prediction. Radical resection of the primary site was performed in 28.9% (n=319) of patients, and 47.6% (n=526) received chemotherapy. In the training cohort (n=773), there were 724 deaths and 654 cancer-specific deaths; in the internal testing cohort (n=331), there were 314 deaths and 280 cancer-specific deaths; and in the temporal validation cohort (n=411), there were 369 deaths and 318 cancer-specific deaths.

In the development cohort, univariable Cox regression identified age, marital status, urban/rural residence, household income, tumor size, T stage, M stage, primary-site radical surgery, regional lymph node surgery, chemotherapy, and LNR as significant predictors of OS (all P<0.05), and age, urban/rural residence, tumor size, T stage, M stage, primary-site radical surgery, regional lymph node surgery, and chemotherapy as significant predictors of CSS (all P<0.05) (Table 3). Collinearity diagnostics confirmed that all retained predictors had VIF values well below the threshold of 5 (mean VIF, 1.6), indicating no meaningful multicollinearity. After multivariable selection, 11 variables were retained in the final Cox model for OS and 8 in the Fine-Gray model for CSS.

Model performance and comparison with TNM staging

Discrimination of the nomogram versus the eighth-edition TNM staging system

In the internal testing cohort, the OS nomogram achieved a C-index of 0.754 and time-dependent AUCs of 0.854, 0.868, and 0.871 at 1, 3, and 5 years, respectively. For CSS, the C-index was 0.726, with corresponding AUCs of 0.813, 0.797, and 0.760. The eighth-edition AJCC TNM staging system performed considerably worse on the same cohort, yielding a C-index of 0.585 and 1-, 3-, and 5-year AUCs of 0.645, 0.635, and 0.669 for OS, and a C-index of 0.635 with AUCs of 0.634, 0.592, and 0.572 for CSS (Figure 2). The nomograms therefore showed a substantial and consistent gain in discrimination over anatomical staging across all evaluated time horizons and for both endpoints (Table 4).

Calibration and clinical utility

Calibration plots showed close agreement between nomogram-predicted and observed 1-, 3-, and 5-year survival probabilities for both OS and CSS, with calibration curves lying near the 45° reference line and no systematic over- or under-estimation across the risk spectrum. DCA demonstrated that the nomograms provided a higher net benefit than either the eighth-edition TNM staging system or the “treat-all” and “treat-none” reference strategies across a clinically relevant range of threshold probabilities, supporting their potential utility for individualized decision-making.

Temporal validation (2016–2017 cohort)

In the temporal validation cohort, the performance of both nomograms remained stable, with only modest attenuation relative to the internal testing cohort. The OS nomogram achieved a C-index of 0.742 and 1-, 3-, and 5-year AUCs of 0.830, 0.836, and 0.843, respectively, while the CSS nomogram yielded a C-index of 0.710, with corresponding AUCs of 0.792, 0.712, and 0.702. Calibration plots in the temporal validation cohort followed a pattern similar to that observed in the internal testing cohort. Because the validation sample was drawn from the same SEER registry, these results constitute temporal rather than external validation and should be interpreted accordingly.

Machine learning models

Among the four machine learning survival models trained on the same predictors as the OS nomogram, XGBAFT achieved the highest discrimination, followed by GBSA, RSF, and the DT. In the internal testing cohort, XGBAFT yielded 1-, 3-, and 5-year AUCs of 0.861, 0.834, and 0.831, respectively; in the temporal validation cohort, the corresponding AUCs were 0.807, 0.803, and 0.789 (Figure 3). The performance gap between XGBAFT and the Cox nomogram was modest at each time point, suggesting that the nomogram already captures most of the predictable signal available in the SEER variables. SHAP analysis of XGBAFT identified chemotherapy, primary-site radical surgery, regional lymph node surgery as the three most influential predictors of OS, with regional lymph node surgery and age contributing additional, time-varying effects—findings consistent with the variables retained by the Cox nomogram.

Nomogram construction

Nomograms for 1-, 3-, and 5-year OS and CSS were constructed from the final multivariable Cox model and Fine-Gray model, respectively (Figure 4). The OS nomogram incorporated 11 independent predictors—age, marital status, urban/rural residence, household income, tumor size, T stage, M stage, primary-site radical surgery, regional lymph node surgery, chemotherapy, and LNR—whereas the CSS nomogram retained the 8 predictors that remained significant under the competing-risk framework.

To translate the nomograms into a clinically interpretable tool, we stratified patients into low-, intermediate-, and high-risk groups on the basis of the linear predictor (total nomogram score) derived from the OS and CSS models, respectively. Cut-points were selected in the training cohort to balance group size and prognostic separation, and were then applied unchanged to the internal testing and temporal validation cohorts to avoid optimistic bias.

In the training cohort, the OS nomogram stratified patients into three groups with markedly divergent survival curves (log-rank P<0.001), with median OS decreasing monotonically from the low- to the high-risk group. The same cut-points reproduced the three-tier separation in the internal testing cohort (log-rank P<0.001) and in the temporal validation cohort (log-rank P<0.001), with no crossing of Kaplan-Meier curves at any time horizon (Figure 4). An analogous pattern was observed for CSS under the Fine-Gray framework, with cumulative incidence functions of cancer-specific death increasing stepwise across risk strata in all three cohorts.

Model interpretation and visualization

To improve the transparency of the best-performing machine learning model, SHAP values were computed for the XGBAFT model trained on the OS endpoint. SHAP decomposes each individual prediction into additive contributions from each predictor, allowing both global feature importance and the direction of each variable’s effect on predicted survival time to be quantified.

The mean absolute SHAP value identified chemotherapy, primary-site radical surgery, and regional lymph node surgery as the three most influential predictors of OS, followed by M stage, age at diagnosis, T stage, and LNR (Figure 5). This ranking closely mirrored the variables retained by the multivariable Cox model, supporting the consistency of the prognostic signal across modeling frameworks. The SHAP summary plot showed that receipt of chemotherapy, primary-site radical surgery, and regional lymph node surgery were each associated with positive SHAP values—indicating longer predicted survival time under the XGBAFT formulation—whereas distant metastasis (M1), higher T stage, larger tumor size, higher LNR, and older age contributed negative SHAP values, indicating shorter predicted survival (Figure 6). These directional effects are biologically and clinically coherent and align with the hazard-ratio directions observed in the Cox nomogram.

Discussion

Key findings

In this study, we developed and internally validated dCCA-specific nomograms for OS and CSS in a SEER cohort of 1,515 patients, and further evaluated the models in a temporal hold-out cohort of patients diagnosed in 2016–2017. The OS nomogram incorporated age, marital status, urban/rural residence, household income, tumor size, T stage, M stage, primary-site radical surgery, regional lymph node surgery, chemotherapy, and the LNR, whereas the CSS model retained the subset of these covariates that remained significant under a competing-risk framework. Both nomograms showed good discrimination and calibration and outperformed the eighth-edition AJCC TNM staging system across all evaluated time horizons. Among the four machine learning survival models, XGBAFT achieved the highest discrimination, and SHAP analysis identified chemotherapy, primary-site radical surgery, regional lymph node surgery as the most influential predictors of OS. Although no universally accepted AUC threshold for clinical utility exists, models with AUC values consistently above 0.80 are generally regarded as having strong discriminative ability; the OS models in our study met this benchmark in both the internal testing set (AUC, 0.854–0.871) and the temporal validation set (AUC, 0.830–0.843), supporting their potential value for individualized prognostic assessment within the SEER population. CSS discrimination at 5 years attenuated more than OS in the temporal cohort (AUC 0.760 → 0.702), likely reflecting fewer 5-year events in the 2016–2017 subset (median follow-up shorter than the development cohort) and the inherent difficulty of cause-of-death classification at long horizons.

Strengths and limitations

The principal strengths of this study are its focus on primary dCCA as an entity distinct from intrahepatic and perihilar disease; the prespecified, calendar-year-based partitioning of development, internal testing, and temporal validation cohorts; the head-to-head benchmarking of a Cox nomogram against four representative survival learners on identical inputs; and the use of SHAP to make the best-performing machine learning model interpretable.

Several limitations should nonetheless be acknowledged. First, although patients diagnosed in 2016–2017 were held out for temporal validation and did not overlap in calendar year with the development cohort, both samples were drawn from the same SEER registry. Our analysis therefore constitutes internal and temporal validation rather than true external validation, and the generalizability of the models to non-SEER populations—particularly Asian and European cohorts with different epidemiology, treatment patterns, and surgical practices—remains to be established; we have accordingly tempered our conclusions regarding clinical applicability. Second, SEER does not record several clinically important variables, including comorbidity burden, tumor differentiation grade, margin status at resection, chemotherapy regimen and dose, biliary drainage, and treatment intent (curative vs. palliative); the absence of these covariates may limit residual-confounding control and predictor completeness. Third, missing categorical data were retained as an "Unknown" category to preserve sample size, which may introduce bias compared with multiple imputation; sensitivity analyses using alternative missing-data strategies were beyond the scope of this study. Finally, although we followed the spirit of the TRIPOD reporting framework, we acknowledge that some items—such as full reporting of the final model equation and detailed missing-data handling—are best supported by supplementary materials that should accompany any future external validation effort.

Comparison with similar research

To date, very few prognostic models have been specifically dedicated to dCCA. A pioneering study by Zhao et al. (23) constructed the first SEER-based nomogram for dCCA, utilizing SEER data spanning 1973 to 2015, a period over which biliary surgical and systemic therapies evolved substantially. They reported a moderate predictive accuracy, with a concordance index (C-index) of 0.71 and area under the curve (AUC) values of 0.76–0.78 for 1- and 3-year OS in the validation cohort. While their work established a valuable baseline for the field, our models achieved C-indices of 0.754 for OS and 0.726 for CSS in the internal testing cohort, and 0.742 and 0.710, respectively, in the temporal validation cohort. The OS nomogram further yielded 1-, 3-, and 5-year AUCs of 0.854, 0.868, and 0.871 in the internal testing cohort, and 0.830, 0.836, and 0.843 in the temporal validation cohort—representing a meaningful improvement over the AUC range reported by Zhao et al. at all evaluated time points, although direct cross-study comparison is inherently limited by differences in cohort era, eligibility criteria, and variable definitions.

This gain in predictive performance is at least in part attributable to our broader and more contemporary feature set. Whereas the model of Zhao et al. centered on core anatomical and staging variables (N/M stage, tumor size) with only limited treatment indicators (surgery) and a single sociodemographic covariate (marital status), our model additionally integrates: (I) a granular tumor-burden metric—LNR; (II) more explicit treatment modalities (primary-site radical surgery and chemotherapy); and (III) key socioeconomic determinants (urban/rural residence and household income) that are known to shape access to care and long-term outcomes. Multivariable selection confirmed that many of these variables remained independently associated with survival, suggesting that they capture clinically meaningful patient-level heterogeneity that extends beyond anatomical TNM descriptors.

Beyond feature enrichment, our study also extends—rather than merely replicates—the methodological framework for dCCA prognostication. Zhao et al. provided a valuable SEER-based foundation by modeling OS with a Cox proportional-hazards nomogram; however, in a dCCA population characterized by advanced age and competing comorbidities, OS alone cannot disentangle cancer-specific mortality from non-cancer death. To address this limitation, we additionally applied a Fine-Gray subdistribution hazard model to estimate CSS while explicitly accounting for non-cancer mortality as a competing event.

Methodologically, we also moved beyond a single-model paradigm. Although the Cox-based nomogram retains clear bedside interpretability, its additive formulation may not fully capture nonlinear effects or interactions among clinical predictors. By benchmarking the nomogram against advanced machine learning survival models—most notably XGBAFT—and applying SHAP analysis, we were able to characterize complex, nonlinear contributions (e.g., chemotherapy, M stage, and radical surgery) without sacrificing interpretability. This dual approach—a transparent traditional nomogram combined with an explainable machine learning comparator—achieved higher discrimination than the 8th edition AJCC TNM staging system alone and improved upon the performance envelope reported for earlier dCCA-specific tools, offering a more granular and individualized framework that is amenable to future external validation in geographically and ethnically diverse cohorts.

Explanations of findings

Several of our findings are consistent with the established biology and clinical management of dCCA. Older age was associated with shorter OS, in line with prior reports identifying age at diagnosis as a dominant prognostic determinant across gastrointestinal malignancies (29,30). Being married, urban residence, and higher household income were each associated with improved survival, an observation echoed by a growing body of socioeconomic research in digestive cancers (31-33). These associations most plausibly reflect earlier diagnosis, better access to multidisciplinary care, higher treatment adherence, and the psychosocial and financial support associated with marriage, although unmeasured biological and comorbidity-related pathways cannot be entirely excluded.

Larger tumor size and higher T stage were both associated with worse OS, consistent with previous studies (34-36). Neuroinvasion is usually considered a form of local invasion and is also a recognized prognostic indicator for dCCA (37-39). Distant metastasis remained the strongest adverse prognostic factor and was among the top contributors in the SHAP analysis, in agreement with earlier SEER-based reports (23). Patients who underwent radical resection of the primary tumor or formal regional lymphadenectomy had markedly better survival than those managed nonoperatively (26,40), reinforcing the central role of pancreaticoduodenectomy as the principal curative-intent operation for resectable dCCA. Although SEER does not capture perioperative morbidity, the most frequent complications after pancreaticoduodenectomy—pancreatic fistula, delayed gastric emptying, and bile leakage—remain important determinants of short-term outcome and merit careful surgical planning (41-43). LNR also emerged as an independent prognostic factor, with higher values predicting worse OS. This finding aligns with prior evidence that LNR may complement absolute positive-node counts, particularly when the number of examined nodes falls below recommended thresholds (44-46).

Chemotherapy was identified as an independent favorable prognostic factor in our cohort, paralleling previous reports (47).The BILCAP trial established adjuvant capecitabine as the current standard of care for resected biliary tract cancer, including dCCA, demonstrating a clinically meaningful OS benefit (48). In contrast, gemcitabine-based adjuvant regimens, including gemcitabine-oxaliplatin (GEMOX), have not shown a consistent survival advantage over observation in randomized trials (49), although the evidence base for adjuvant gemcitabine–cisplatin and for neoadjuvant strategies continues to evolve. Because SEER records only whether chemotherapy was administered—without information on regimen, sequencing, or dose intensity—our findings should be interpreted as supporting the prognostic relevance of receiving systemic therapy in general, rather than endorsing any specific regimen.

Implications and actions needed

Within the SEER population, the proposed nomograms and the XGBAFT model offer a transparent and interpretable framework for individualized prognostic discussion and for risk-stratified surveillance and adjuvant therapy decision-making in dCCA. The complementary use of a Cox nomogram and a SHAP-explained XGBAFT model may be particularly useful in settings where both bedside transparency and granular individual-level risk explanation are valued.

Future work should focus on prospective multicenter external validation in geographically and ethnically distinct cohorts; incorporation of molecular markers (e.g., KRAS, TP53, IDH1, FGFR2) and detailed treatment variables (chemotherapy regimen, dose intensity, margin status, biliary drainage); sensitivity analyses using multiple imputation under a missing-at-random assumption; and head-to-head comparison with emerging deep-learning survival frameworks. Only after such validation should these models be considered for routine clinical use.

Conclusions

We developed and temporally validated dCCA-specific nomograms and machine learning models for predicting 1-, 3-, and 5-year OS and CSS within the SEER population. Both the Cox nomograms and the XGBAFT model showed good discrimination, calibration, and net clinical benefit, and substantially outperformed the eighth-edition AJCC TNM staging system; SHAP analysis identified chemotherapy, M stage, and primary-site radical surgery as the dominant prognostic factors. The nomogram-derived risk strata provide a transparent and interpretable framework that may inform individualized prognostic discussion and clinical decision-making in dCCA. Prospective external validation in geographically and ethnically diverse cohorts is required before these models can be recommended for routine clinical use.
