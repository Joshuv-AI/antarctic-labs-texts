Convex Constrained Controller Synthesis for Evolution Equations
Lauren Conger, Antoine P. Leeman, Franca Hoffmann

Abstract
We propose a convex controller synthesis framework for a large class of constrained linear systems, including those described by (deterministic and stochastic) partial differential equations and integral equations, commonly used in fluid dynamics, thermo-mechanical systems, quantum control, or transportation networks. Most existing control techniques rely on a (finite-dimensional) discrete description of the system, via ordinary differential equations. Here, we work instead with more general (infinite-dimensional) Hilbert spaces. This enables the discretization to be applied after the optimization (optimize-then-discretize). Using output-feedback System Level Synthesis (SLS), we formulate the controller synthesis as a convex optimization problem. Structural constraints like sensor and communication delays, and locality constraints, are incorporated while preserving convexity, allowing parallel implementation and extending key SLS properties to infinite dimensions. The proposed approach and its benefits are demonstrated on a linear Boltzmann equation.

Contributions:
We propose a convex (output-feedback) controller parametrization for deterministic or stochastic evolution equations in infinite-dimensional spaces, incorporating convex structural constraints such as locality, sensor delay, and communication delays.

Problem Setup

2.1
Notation

Let
\langle\cdot,\,\cdot\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}
denote the inner product in a real Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
and its topological dual
\mathcal{H}^{*}:=\{f:\mathcal{H}\to\mathbb{R}\text{ continuous, linear functions}\}
. For a linear bounded operator
A:\mathscr{D}(A)\subseteq\mathcal{H}_{1}\to\mathcal{H}_{2}
between two Hilbert spaces with domain
\mathscr{D}(A)
and range
\mathscr{R}(A)
, its dual operator
A^{*}:\mathscr{D}(A^{*})\subseteq\mathcal{H}_{2}^{*}\to\mathcal{H}_{1}^{*}
maps
f(\cdot)\in\mathcal{H}_{2}^{*}
to the function
g(\cdot)=f(A\cdot)\in\mathcal{H}_{1}^{*}
. The Riesz map allows us to identify
A^{*}
with the adjoint
A^{\prime}:\mathscr{D}(A^{\prime})\subseteq\mathcal{H}_{2}\to\mathcal{H}_{1}
of
A
, which is defined via the relation
\langle x_{2},\,Ax_{1}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{2}}=\langle A^{\prime}x_{2},\,x_{1}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{1}}
for all
x_{1}\in\mathscr{D}(A)\subseteq\mathcal{H}_{1},x_{2}\in\mathscr{D}(A^{\prime})\subseteq\mathcal{H}_{2}
. Thanks to the Riesz map,
\mathcal{H}
is isometrically isomorphic to
\mathcal{H}^{*}
. From now on, we identify
\mathcal{H}^{*}
with
\mathcal{H}
and
A^{\prime}
with
A^{*}
. We remark that the operator domain is part of the definition of the operator itself, and therefore
A
and
\mathscr{D}(A)
are always stated together.

Throughout, we will use that the state
x^{t}\in\mathcal{X}
, input
u^{t}\in\mathcal{U}
, output
y^{t}\in\mathcal{Y}
, disturbance
w_{x}^{t}\in\mathcal{X}
, and observation noise
w_{y}^{t}\in\mathcal{Y}
at time
t
are in Hilbert spaces.
For a Hilbert space
\mathcal{X}
and discrete times
t\in\{0,\dots,T\}
, we use the script
\mathscr{X}\coloneqq\mathcal{X}^{\otimes(T+1)}
to denote the
T+1
dimensional vector of elements
x^{0},\dots,x^{T}\in\mathcal{X}
. This is again a Hilbert space when endowed with the inner product
\langle x,\,\bar{x}\rangle_{\mathscr{X}}:=\sum_{t=0}^{T}\langle x^{t},\,\bar{x}^{t}\rangle_{\mathcal{X}}
. In the context of
PDEs
, a very useful Hilbert space is the Sobolev space
H^{k}(\Omega)
with
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
denoting the class of functions whose weak derivatives exist up to order
k
and are in
L^{2}(\Omega):=\{f:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}\,s.t.\,\int_{\Omega}|f(z)|^{2}\mathrm{d}z<\infty\}
. Finally, we denote by
\delta_{z_{0}}(\cdot)
the Dirac delta at
z_{0}
.

2.2
Strong and Weak Forms of the Dynamics

Consider the discrete-time dynamics

\displaystyle x^{t+1}

\displaystyle=A^{*}x^{t}+B^{*}u^{t}+w_{x}^{t}\,,

(1a)

\displaystyle y^{t}

\displaystyle=C^{*}x^{t}+w_{y}^{t}\,.

(1b)

with operators

\displaystyle\text{Dynamics }\quad A^{*}:\mathscr{D}(A^{*})\to\mathcal{X}\,,\quad

\displaystyle\mathscr{D}(A^{*})\subseteq\mathcal{X}\,,

\displaystyle\text{Control }\quad B^{*}:\mathscr{D}(B^{*})\to\mathcal{X}\,,\quad

\displaystyle\mathscr{D}(B^{*})\subseteq\mathcal{U}\,,

\displaystyle\text{Observation }\quad C^{*}:\mathscr{D}(C^{*})\to\mathcal{Y}\,,\quad

\displaystyle\mathscr{D}(C^{*})\subseteq\mathcal{X}\,.

The Hilbert spaces
\mathcal{X},\mathcal{U},\mathcal{Y}
may be finite- or infinite-dimensional
1
1

1

An alternative is to use general dual spaces, going beyond Hilbert spaces.
.
In infinite dimensions, a large class of
PDEs
and integral equations can be expressed as (
1
) for suitable choices of operators
A^{*},B^{*}
and
C^{*}
.
If
\{x^{t}\}_{t=0}^{T}=\{x^{t}(\cdot)\}_{t=0}^{T}
and
\{w_{x}^{t}\}_{t=0}^{T}=\{w_{x}^{t}(\cdot)\}_{t=0}^{T}
are functions over some space
\Omega
, Equation (
1a
) is said to hold

, i.e.
x^{t+1}(z)=(A^{*}x^{t})(z)+(B^{*}u^{t})(z)+w_{x}^{t}(z)
pointwise for all

. However, this notion of solution to (
1
) may be too restrictive in many application settings. For instance, one may want to consider cases where (
1a
) is the time-distretization of an evolution equation

\partial_{t}x(t,z)=L^{*}x(t,z)+B^{*}u(t,z)+w_{x}(t,z)\,,

(2)

for some differential operator
L^{*}:\mathscr{D}(L^{*})\subseteq\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{X}
(for Forward Euler discretization with time step
\Delta t
, one would recover (
1a
) by setting
A^{*}=\Id+\Delta tL^{*}
). If
(L^{*}x)(z):=v\cdot\nabla_{z}x(z)
is the advection operator for some fixed velocity field
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
B^{*}=0
,
w_{x}=0
, then a step function at the origin,
x^{0}(z):=\text{sign}(z)+1
, is propagated through the dynamics and remains a step function at all times. This solution, which is physically relevant, does not solve (
2
) strongly since a step function is not differentiable in the classical sense. Or, consider for instance dynamics
L^{*}
such that the solution
x(t,z)
develops shocks or discontinuities over time, loosing differentiability. To give a sense to ’solving (
1
)’ in these cases, we use a more general notion of solution:
(x^{t},u^{t})_{t=0}^{T}
solves (
1
)
weakly
if
for all
f\in\mathscr{D}(A)\cap\mathscr{D}(B)\subseteq\mathcal{X}
and
g\in\mathscr{D}(C)\subseteq\mathcal{Y}
,

\displaystyle\langle x^{t+1},\,f\rangle_{\mathcal{X}}

\displaystyle=\langle x^{t},\,Af\rangle_{\mathcal{X}}+\langle u^{t},\,Bf\rangle_{\mathcal{U}}+\langle w^{t}_{x},\,f\rangle_{\mathcal{X}}\,,

(3a)

\displaystyle\langle y^{t},\,g\rangle_{\mathcal{Y}}

\displaystyle=\langle x^{t},\,Cg\rangle_{\mathcal{X}}+\langle w^{t}_{y},\,g\rangle_{\mathcal{Y}}.

(3b)

Here,
f,g
are considered test functions, and applying differential operators to test functions instead of the solution itself allows for less regular solutions, see Example
2
.
We will use the weak notion of solution throughout this paper, and all pointwise statements are thus to be understood in the almost everywhere sense.

Example 1
.

In finite dimensions, the weak and strong forms are equivalent, so our framework reduces to the standard
SLS
setting. When
\mathcal{X}=\mathbb{R}^{n_{x}}
,
\mathcal{U}=\mathbb{R}^{n_{u}}
, and
\mathcal{Y}=\mathbb{R}^{n_{y}}
, the weak form of the dynamics (
3
) reduces to the standard (strong form) finite-dimensional control setting, since
\mathcal{X}
,
\mathcal{U}
and
\mathcal{Y}
are Hilbert spaces when equipped with the
2
-norm. Observe that the domains of
A^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{x}\times n_{x}}
,
B^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{x}\times n_{u}}
, and
C^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{y}\times n_{x}}
are the entire spaces
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{U}
, which is not necessarily true in the infinite dimensional setting.

Example 2
.

Consider
\mathcal{X}=\mathcal{U}=L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
, and the dynamics

\displaystyle x^{t+1}(z)=\partial_{z}x^{t}(z)+\int b(z-z^{\prime})u^{t}(z^{\prime})\mathrm{d}z^{\prime}\,,

for
z\in\mathbb{R}
,
b\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
with
b(-z)=b(z)
.
This corresponds to the strong form of the dynamics (
1a
) with
A^{*}=\partial_{z}
the differentiation operator and
B^{*}
the convolution operator. Here,

\mathscr{D}(A^{*})=H^{1}(\mathbb{R}):=\{f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})\,s.t.\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\partial_{z}f(z)|^{2}\mathrm{d}z<\infty\}
and
B^{*}u^{t}=b\ast u^{t}
with
\mathscr{D}(B^{*})=\mathcal{U}
. The weak form (
3a
) of the dynamics is

\displaystyle\langle x^{t+1},\,f\rangle_{\mathcal{X}}

\displaystyle=-\langle x^{t},\,\partial_{z}f\rangle_{\mathcal{X}}+\langle u^{t},\,b\ast f\rangle_{\mathcal{U}}\,,

for all
f\in\mathscr{D}(A)=H^{1}(\mathbb{R})
, where
A=-\partial_{z}
and
Bf=b\ast f
. Explicitly writing the
L^{2}
inner products, the above means

\displaystyle\int x^{t+1}(z)f(z)\mathrm{d}z=-\int x^{t}(z)\partial_{z}f(z)\mathrm{d}z+\iint u^{t}(z)b(z-z^{\prime})f(z^{\prime})\mathrm{d}z\mathrm{d}z^{\prime}\,,\quad\forall\ f\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R})\,.

The strong form requires
x^{t}\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R})
to be differentiable, whereas for the weak form
x^{t}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
is sufficient, shifting regularity requirements to the test functions
f\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R})
instead. In this way the weak form gives sense to a non-differentiable function
x^{t}
solving a differential equation.

2.3
System Level Synthesis

Finite-dimensional
SLS
can be used to optimize a linear controller while convexly enforcing locality, communication delay and speed constraints
[
2
]
,
which is not possible when optimizing over
the (linear) controller gains
directly.
In particular, locality constraints allow for large-scale problems to be solved in parallel, with controllers using only local information, leading to
a scalable controller implementation.
We leverage
SLS
to generalize the convex controller synthesis to infinite dimensional settings, including deterministic and stochastic
PDE
dynamics and integral equations.

In the weak sense the control input
u^{t}
is given via a family of operators
K_{x}^{t,\tau}:\mathscr{D}(K_{x}^{t,\tau})\subseteq\mathcal{U}\to\mathcal{X}
and
K_{y}^{t,\tau}:\mathscr{D}(K_{y}^{t,\tau})\subseteq\mathcal{U}\to\mathcal{Y}

for
t,\tau\in\{0,\dots,T\}
such that for all test functions
h\in\cap_{\tau=0}^{t}\mathscr{D}(K_{x,y}^{t,t-\tau})\subseteq\mathcal{U}
,

\displaystyle\langle u^{t},\,h\rangle_{\mathcal{U}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\tau=0}^{t}\langle x^{\tau},\,K_{x}^{t,t-\tau}h\rangle_{\mathcal{X}}\quad\text{for state feedback,}

\displaystyle\langle u^{t},\,h\rangle_{\mathcal{U}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\tau=0}^{t}\langle y^{\tau},\,K_{y}^{t,t-\tau}h\rangle_{\mathcal{Y}}\quad\text{for output feedback}\,.

We will present results for both state feedback (SF) and output feedback (OF) settings. The controller is
u^{t}=\sum_{\tau=0}^{t}(K_{x}^{t,t-\tau})^{*}x^{\tau}
in the SF setting, and
u^{t}=\sum_{\tau=0}(K_{y}^{t,t-\tau})^{*}y^{\tau}
in the OF setting.

Remark 1
.

Taking operator adjoints means switching the spaces for domains and ranges, so generalizing
SLS
to operators in Hilbert spaces switches the conventional source and target spaces from existing
SLS
theory. For example, if
B:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{U}
then
B^{*}:\mathcal{U}\to\mathcal{X}
. For details, see
[
21
]
.

3.3
Optimization and Structural Constraints

Classical control problems aim to construct controllers that minimize some cost, usually in terms of the state
x
and input
u
. In the
SLS
framework, it was shown in
[
2
, Section 2.2.2]
that optimizing over a weighted Frobenius norm of (finite-dimensional)
\theta_{x}
and
\theta_{u}
solves the linear quadratic regulator (LQR) problem. Other common objectives such as
\mathcal{H}_{\infty}
and
\mathcal{L}_{1}

can also be posed in terms of closed-loop maps (see, e.g.,
[
8
,
2
,
10
]
for
SLS
-based robustness guarantees). In the same vein, we propose optimizing over (infinite-dimensional)
\theta_{x}
and
\theta_{u}
instead of the state and input. In the state feedback setting, the (infinite-dimensional) convex optimization problem given a cost
J
is

\displaystyle\min_{\theta_{x},\theta_{u}}J(\theta_{x},\theta_{u})\quad\text{such that }\eqref{root_eq:SLP_state_feedback}\text{ holds,}

and in the output feedback setting,

\displaystyle\min_{\theta_{xx},\theta_{ux},\theta_{uy},\theta_{xy}}J(\theta_{xx},\theta_{ux},\theta_{uy},\theta_{xy})\quad\text{such that }\eqref{root_eq:SLP_output_feedback}\text{ holds.}

Let
\theta
be the set of closed-loop maps, with
\theta=(\theta_{x},\theta_{u})
in the state feedback setting and
\theta=(\theta_{xx},\theta_{ux},\theta_{uy},\theta_{xy})
in the output feedback setting.
A key feature of the original finite-dimensional
SLS
formulation
[
2
]
is its ability to impose structural constraints such as communication delay, sensor delay and locality constraints by constraining the support of
\theta
, while maintaining the convexity of the optimization problem.
This proposed infinite-dimensional formulation has the same properties. When restricting to a constraint set
S
, the optimization problem then becomes

\displaystyle\min_{\theta\in S}J(\theta)\quad\text{s.t. }\eqref{root_eq:SLP_state_feedback}\text{ or }\eqref{root_eq:SLP_output_feedback}\text{ holds.}

The following example shows how to construct such constraint sets
S
.

Example 3

(Integral Dynamics with Constraints)
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a compact set.
Consider the system with full state measurements

x^{t}\in L^{2}(\Omega)
,
u^{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{u}}
, parameterized by
a\in L^{2}(\Omega)
and
b:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{n_{u}}
,

\displaystyle A^{*}x^{t}(\cdot)

\displaystyle=(a\ast x^{t})(\cdot)\in L^{2}(\Omega)\,,\quad\forall\,x^{t}\in L^{2}(\Omega)\,,

\displaystyle B^{*}u^{t}

\displaystyle=\sum_{l=1}^{n_{u}}b(l,z)u^{t}_{l}\,\in L^{2}(\Omega)\,,\quad\forall\,u^{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{u}}\,.

This means (
3
) is a simplified scattering equation, also known as linear Boltzmann, and is used to model phenomena like bacterial movement
[
19
, Sec 5.6]
. The functions
a
and
b
have compact support; for
a
, this specifies that
\mathcal{A}
is local, i.e., the state at
z
affects only nearby states at the next time step. For
b
, the control affects states around the location of each actuator, where the location of the
i^{th}
of
n_{u}
actuators is located at
\hat{z}^{(i)}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Assume
\supp a(\cdot)=\{z\in\Omega\penalty\ |\penalty\ \lVert z\rVert_{2}\leq r\}
and
\supp b(l,\cdot)=\{z\in\Omega\ |\ \lVert z-\hat{z}^{(l)}\rVert_{2}\leq r\}
,
where
r
is sufficiently small.
For this problem set-up, we optimize over causal operators
\theta^{*}=(\theta_{x}^{*},\theta_{u}^{*})
that can be expressed via kernels: they are upper block-triangular as in (
7
), and for
f\in L^{2}(\Omega)
and
z\in\Omega
,

\displaystyle((\theta_{x}^{t,\tau})^{*}f)(z)=\int_{\Omega}\vartheta_{x}^{t,\tau}(\tilde{z},z)f(\tilde{z})\mathrm{d}\tilde{z}\,,

\displaystyle((\theta_{u}^{t,\tau})^{*}f)_{l}=\int\vartheta_{u}^{t,\tau}(\tilde{z},\hat{z}^{(l)})f(\tilde{z})\mathrm{d}\tilde{z}\,,

with kernels
\vartheta_{x}^{t,\tau}(\tilde{z},z)\in L^{2}(\Omega\times\Omega)
,
\vartheta_{u}^{t,\tau}(\tilde{z},z)\in L^{2}(\Omega,C(\Omega))
.
To impose locality constraints on
\theta_{x}^{*}
and
\theta_{u}^{*}
, we fix the constraint set
S
to operators with restricted support of their corresponding kernels:
\supp\vartheta_{x}^{t,\tau}=\supp\vartheta_{u}^{t,\tau}=\left\{\tilde{z},z\in\Omega\ \big|\ \lVert\tilde{z}-z\rVert_{2}\leq r\right\}
.

Note that in the general linear operator setting, the system is often not controllable due to under-actuation. This makes it more difficult to ensure that the dynamics and the support constraints are feasible, i.e., there exists some
\theta^{*}
that satisfies both. An interesting question for future work is rigorously defining notions of controllability on subspaces.

3.4
Parallel Solving for Integral Transforms

Consider the state space
\mathcal{X}=L^{2}(\Omega)
for some
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and consider
CLMs
with the following integral transform structure: for
f\in\mathcal{X}
,

\displaystyle(\theta_{x}^{t,\tau})^{*}f=\int_{\Omega}\vartheta_{x}^{t,\tau}(\tilde{z},\cdot)f(\tilde{z})\mathrm{d}\tilde{z}\,,

\displaystyle(\theta_{u}^{t,\tau})^{*}f=T_{u}\circ\int_{\Omega}\vartheta_{u}^{t,\tau}(\tilde{z},\cdot)f(\tilde{z})\mathrm{d}\tilde{z}\,,

where
T_{u}:\mathcal{V}\to\mathcal{U}
for a suitable functional space
\mathcal{V}
over
\Omega
(not necessarily a Hilbert space), and kernels
\vartheta_{x}^{t,\tau}\in L^{2}(\Omega\times\Omega)
,
\vartheta_{u}^{t,\tau}\in L^{2}(\Omega,\mathcal{V})
.
We call
\mathcal{M}
the set of all such admissible causal kernels
\vartheta=(\vartheta_{x}^{t,\tau},\vartheta_{u}^{t,\tau})
, and define for fixed
\tilde{z}\in\Omega
the set

\mathcal{M}_{1}:=\{\vartheta\,|\,\vartheta_{x}^{t,\tau}(\tilde{z},\cdot)\in\mathcal{X},\vartheta_{u}^{t,\tau}(\tilde{z},\cdot)\in\mathcal{V}\}

and for fixed
z\in\Omega
the set
\mathcal{M}_{2}:=\{\vartheta\,|\,\vartheta_{x}^{t,\tau}(\cdot,z),\vartheta_{u}^{t,\tau}(\cdot,z)\in\mathcal{X}\}
.
Optimizing over
CLMs
is equivalent to optimizing over kernels
\vartheta\in\mathcal{M}
.
When there exists a feasible set
S
of locality constraints, it is possible to solve this
optimization problem in parallel.

Consider cost functions
J_{i}:\mathcal{M}_{i}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
and
J:\mathcal{M}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
for
i=\{1,2\}
.
Denote the constraints by
P
, where
P
collects the controller parametrization (
10
) or (
13
), and the structural constraints
S
.

Definition 1

(Disturbance-Parallelizable)
.

J
and
P
are disturbance-parallelizable if there exists an increasing function
\varphi_{1}
and norm
\|\cdot\|_{1}
such that
J(\vartheta)=\varphi_{1}(\|\psi_{1}\|_{1})
for
\psi_{1}(\tilde{z}):=J_{1}(\vartheta(\tilde{z},\cdot))
, and

\displaystyle\overline{\vartheta}=\argmin_{\vartheta(\tilde{z},\cdot)\in\mathcal{M}_{1}}J_{1}(\vartheta(\tilde{z},\cdot))\quad\text{s.t. }P(\vartheta(\tilde{z},\cdot))=0\quad\forall\tilde{z}\,,

results in the same minimizer as the full problem, that is,

\displaystyle\overline{\vartheta}=\argmin_{\vartheta\in\mathcal{M}}J(\vartheta)\quad\text{s.t. }P(\vartheta)=0\,.

Definition 2

(
xuy
-Parallelizable)
.

J
and
P
are
xuy
-parallelizable
if there exists an increasing function
\varphi_{2}
and norm
\|\cdot\|_{2}
such that
J(\vartheta)=\varphi_{2}(\|\psi_{2}\|_{2})
for
\psi_{2}(z):=J_{2}(\vartheta(z,\cdot))
, and

\displaystyle\overline{\vartheta}=\argmin_{\vartheta(\cdot,z)\in\mathcal{M}_{2}}J_{2}(\vartheta(\cdot,z))\quad\text{s.t. }P(\vartheta(\cdot,z))=0\quad\forall z\,,

results in the same minimizer as the full problem, that is,

\displaystyle\overline{\vartheta}=\argmin_{\vartheta\in\mathcal{M}}J(\vartheta)\quad\text{s.t. }P(\vartheta)=0\,.

Disturbance-parallelizable is analogous to column-separable in finite dimensional
SLS
;
xuy
-parallelizable is analogous to row-separable in finite dimensional
SLS
.

Remark 2
.

If the system is parallelizable or separable into disturbance- and
xuy
-parallelizable components,
results from
[
6
]
, and finite-dimensional
SLS

[
2
,
1
]
suggest Alternating Direction Method of Multipliers (ADMM) as a potential distributed solver implementation.

We will use a disturbance-parallelizable objective and constraint in the following section and illustrate results with parallel computation.

From (

\displaystyle\vartheta_{x}^{t+1,\tau+1}(\tilde{z},z)

\displaystyle=(A^{*}\vartheta_{x}^{t,\tau}(\tilde{z},\cdot))(z)+(B^{*}T_{u}\circ\vartheta_{u}^{t,\tau}(\tilde{z},\cdot))(z)=\int a(z-z^{\prime})\vartheta_{x}^{t,\tau}(\tilde{z},z^{\prime})\mathrm{d}z^{\prime}+\sum_{l=1}^{n_{u}}b(l,z)\vartheta_{u}^{t,\tau}(\tilde{z},\hat{z}^{(l)})\,,

Conclusion

We proposed a new convex output-feedback controller synthesis framework for infinite-dimensional
evolution equations, based on
SLS
. The framework allows for convex structural constraints, such as sensor delay, communication delays, and locality constraints. Due to its similarities with finite-dimensional
SLS
, we expect many
SLS
properties to translate to this infinite-dimensional setting, e.g., efficient distributed solving, robustness guarantees, or robust constraint satisfaction.
