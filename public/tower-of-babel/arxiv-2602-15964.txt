Computing Approximate Pareto Frontiers for Submodular Utility and Cost Tradeoffs DOI: XXXXXXX.XXXXXXX Conference: ACM SIGKDD; Aug 2026; Jeju, Korea Note: Preprint version.
Karan Vombatkere, Evimaria Terzi

Abstract
. In many data-mining applications, including recommender systems, influence maximization, and team formation, the goal is to pick a subset of elements (e.g., items, nodes in a network, experts to perform a task) to maximize a monotone submodular utility function while simultaneously minimizing a cost function. Classical formulations model this tradeoff via cardinality or knapsack constraints, or by combining utility and cost into a single weighted objective. However, such approaches require committing to a specific tradeoff in advance and return only a single solution, offering limited insight into the space of viable utility–cost tradeoffs. In this paper, we depart from the single-solution paradigm and examine the problem of computing representative sets of high-quality solutions that expose different tradeoffs between submodular utility and cost. For this, we introduce ( α 1 , α 2 ) (\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}) -approximate Pareto frontiers that provably approximate the achievable tradeoffs between submodular utility and cost. Specifically, we formalize the Pareto– ⟨ f , c ⟩ \langle f,c\rangle problem and develop efficient algorithms for multiple instantiations arising from different combinations of submodular utility f f and cost functions c c . Our results offer a principled and practical framework for understanding and exploiting utility–cost tradeoffs in submodular optimization. Experiments on datasets from diverse application domains demonstrate that our algorithms efficiently compute approximate Pareto frontiers in practice.

Introduction

Submodular optimization appears in many data-mining applications, including

recommender systems

(

;
35
;
19
)
,

influence maximization

(
20
;
7
)
, and

(

;

;
38
;
39
)
.
In these settings, the goal is to select a set of entities (e.g., items, nodes in a social network, or experts in team formation)
that maximizes a monotone submodular utility function while minimizing an associated cost.
The specific instantiations of both the utility and cost functions are application-dependent; for example, in influence maximization,
the objective is to maximize the spread of influence while minimizing the cost of seeding nodes, whereas in team formation the goal
is to cover required skills while minimizing coordination costs.
Traditionally, such problems have been studied under cardinality or matroid constraints,
for which strong approximation guarantees are known
(
28
;
22
;
34
;
21
;
5
;
24
)
.

More recent work has considered multi-objective formulations that combine submodular utility and cost into a single weighted objective
(
16
;
19
;
30
;
40
;
38
;
39
)
.
While this approach is flexible, it requires selecting weights
a priori
and typically produces only a single solution.
Moreover, the resulting objective may take both positive and negative values, for which standard approximation guarantees no longer apply,
and small changes in the weights can lead to qualitatively different solutions.

A common characteristic of the above approaches is their focus on computing a
single
solution.
In many real-world decision-making scenarios, this single-solution paradigm is limiting.
Practitioners are often interested in understanding the broader tradeoff between utility and cost:
budgets may be negotiable, different stakeholders may prefer different operating points, and small increases in cost may yield
disproportionately large gains in utility.
Returning a single solution obscures these tradeoffs and makes it difficult to reason about alternative solutions.

In this paper, we depart from the single-solution paradigm and study utility–cost tradeoffs in submodular maximization through the lens of Pareto optimization.
We formalize the
Pareto–
\langle f,c\rangle
problem, with the goal of computing a
polynomial-size
,
representative
set of solutions that approximately captures the Pareto frontier between submodular utility
f
and cost
c
. Inspired by the work of
31
, we introduce
(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})
-approximate Pareto frontiers tailored to submodular maximization and develop efficient algorithms for finding them.
Our algorithms work for arbitrary monotone submodular utility functions and a broad class of cost functions, including cardinality, knapsack, and graph-based costs, modeling problems that appear in recommender systems, influence maximization, and team formation.
We also demonstrate the practical effectiveness of our methods through experiments on diverse real-world datasets, showing that they efficiently compute high-quality approximate Pareto frontiers.

Related Work

Submodular Maximization with Constraints.

Maximizing a monotone submodular function under constraints is a central problem
in combinatorial optimization, with well-established guarantees.
Under a cardinality constraint, the greedy algorithm achieves the optimal

(1-1/e)
approximation
(
28
;
22
)
.
This guarantee extends to knapsack and matroid constraints via techniques such as partial enumeration and continuous relaxations

(
34
;
21
;
5
;
24
)
.
Subsequent work has focused on improving efficiency and robustness,
leading to fast greedy variants, lazy evaluations, and guess-free algorithms that retain strong guarantees
(
3
;
12
;
11
)
.
These methods are widely used in data-mining applications where costs correspond
to budgets or capacities, including influence maximization

(
20
;
7
)
, sensor placement

(
23
)
, and recommendation systems

(
19
)
.
A parallel line of work considers submodular maximization under richer structural constraints, particularly tree or graph-based costs.
For example, work in team formation considers expert
communication costs
(
25
;
1
;
2
;
13
)
.
In all cases, the goal is to compute a single solution that optimizes the objective under constraints.

Combined Objective Treatment.

An alternative to constrained submodular maximization is to combine utility and
cost into a single objective, typically via weighted sums or regularization
(
16
;
27
)
.

This paradigm has been widely adopted in recommendation systems that incorporate cost or diversity
penalties directly into the objective
(
19
)
, and team
formation, where hiring costs, workload balance, or social compatibility are
modeled as part of a unified objective

(
29
;
30
;
40
;
38
;
39
)
.
Such formulations admit efficient algorithms, but they require
fixing tradeoff weights
a priori
and they return a
single solution
,
obscuring the underlying utility–cost tradeoffs and limit the ability to reason about alternative solutions.

Our work departs from the single-solution paradigm and formalizes the
submodular utility–cost tradeoff by approximating the Pareto frontier across
cost models.

Approximate Pareto Frontiers.

Approximate Pareto frontiers originate in the work of Papadimitriou and
Yannakakis
(
31
)
, who showed that any
multi-objective optimization problem admits a polynomial-size

\varepsilon
-approximate Pareto set.
Subsequent work developed methods for constructing succinct tradeoff
curves in combinatorial optimization
(
36
;
8
)
.
These results are largely existential and typically assume access to oracles or near-exact solvers.

Algorithmic developments in this area have primarily focused on linear or
problem-specific objectives and rely on structural assumptions that do not
extend to general submodular functions.
Representative applications include bicriteria shortest paths

(
32
;
14
)
, network design

(
23
)
, and hydropower dam placement

(
41
;
4
)
.
In team formation, skyline and two-phase methods balance coordination quality and
hiring costs
(
43
;
17
;
18
)
, but under complete task-coverage constraints and often without
approximation guarantees.

Additional work explores Pareto tradeoffs in specific domains such as fairness
or quality in clustering and recommendation systems.
For example, Pareto formulations have been used to balance fairness and
clustering quality
(
15
)
and to preserve diversity and quality
in group recommendation
(
42
)
.
Learning-based methods aim to approximate Pareto sets empirically, but lack
theoretical guarantees and robustness across cost models

(
26
;
6
)
.

Within submodular optimization, Pareto formulations have received limited
attention. Fazzone et al.
(
10
)
study the Submodular
Maximization with Fair Representation (SMFR) problem, which jointly maximizes a
primary submodular utility function and multiple representativeness objectives
under knapsack or matroid constraints.
Although minimizing cost is mathematically equivalent to maximizing the
negative cost function, exactly because of this,
the approximation guarantees of their framework cannot be directly applied to our setting.
Soma and Yoshida
(
33
)
study representative selection for
multi-objective submodular maximization via regret-ratio minimization, producing
a finite set of solutions corresponding to different non-negative linear
combinations of the objectives.
While this framework captures tradeoffs among multiple submodular functions, it doesn’t provide approximation guarantees for individual objectives, and does not yield explicit guarantees on the utility–cost Pareto frontier.

Our work complements this literature by establishing provable approximate Pareto guarantees for monotone submodular objectives under multiple cost models, and by demonstrating their practical effectiveness.

3.
Preliminaries

Let
\mathcal{V}=\{1,\dots,n\}
denote a finite ground set of items of cardinality
n
.
A
solution
is any subset
S\subseteq\mathcal{V}
.
We evaluate each solution
S
using two criteria: the utility function
f(S)
,
which we seek to maximize, and a cost function
c(S)
, which we seek to minimize.
We consider the following utility and cost functions.

Submodular Utilities.

Throughout the paper, we assume that our utility function
f:2^{\mathcal{V}}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
is a non-negative,
monotone, and submodular function.
Monotonicity implies that

\displaystyle f(S)\leq f(T)\quad\forall S\subseteq T\subseteq\mathcal{V}.

Submodularity implies that for all
S\subseteq T\subseteq\mathcal{V}
and
u\in\mathcal{V}\setminus T
,

f
satisfies the diminishing-returns property:

\displaystyle f(T\cup\{u\})-f(T)\leq f(S\cup\{u\})-f(S).

Cost Functions.

Each solution
S\subseteq\mathcal{V}
is associated with a non-negative cost

c:2^{\mathcal{V}}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
.
The cost function encodes structural or feasibility constraints and may take
different forms. Motivated by work in recommender systems
(
19
)
, influence maximization
(
20
)
and team formation
(
25
;
30
)
, we focus here on three cost variants.

Cardinality
cost.

Maximizing a submodular function subject to a cardinality constraint has been well-studied
(
22
)
. To constrain the number of selected items to be at most
k
, we define the cost function as follows:

c_{k}(S)=|S|.

This cost balances the
size
of a solution with its utility.

Linear
cost.

Similar to prior work in budgeted submodular maximization and team
formation
(
30
;
21
;
11
)
, each item
i\in\mathcal{V}
is assigned a non-negative weight
w_{i}
, and the total cost is additive:

c_{\ell}(S)=\sum_{i\in S}w_{i}.

This is a linear (knapsack-like) cost on the items in
S
.

Diameter
cost.

We assume an underlying weighted graph
G=(\mathcal{V},E)
, where edge weights encode pairwise coordination costs
(
2
;
25
;
38
)
.
Let
d(i,j)\geq 0
denote the shortest-path distance between items

i
and
j
in
G
. We then define the diameter cost of solution
S
:

c_{d}(S)=\max_{i,j\in S}d(i,j).

This cost captures the worst-case coordination difficulty within the selected
set and induces a non-linear, non-additive constraint.

Greedy Primitive.

Given a monotone submodular function
f
and a linear cost function
c
defined
over a ground set
\mathcal{V}
, the
Greedy
procedure constructs a solution
incrementally. It repeatedly adds the feasible element with
maximum cost-scaled marginal gain

\Delta_{f}(e\mid S)\;=\;\frac{f(S\cup\{e\})-f(S)}{c(\{e\})}.

The notion of feasibility depends on the objective being optimized.
We consider two complementary stopping conditions.
In the
cost-constrained
case, the procedure grows the solution until its
total cost reaches a prescribed budget
B
.
In the
utility-target
case, the procedure continues until the solution
achieves a target utility value
K
.
By instantiating the greedy primitive with either a cost threshold
B
or a
utility target
K
, we capture both optimization regimes within a single
algorithmic template.

Often, the greedy procedure is initialized with a set of

seeds
, where each seed is a feasible subset
S_{0}\subseteq\mathcal{V}
of
size at most
\tau
;
\tau
controls the tradeoff between running time and
approximation quality.
Enumerating all such seeds mitigates sensitivity to poor initializations, a
standard technique in submodular maximization
(
34
;
11
)
.

Pareto Frontiers.

Pareto points
are a standard concept in multi-objective optimization. In our case, we have two objectives, where one is maximized (
f(\cdot)
) and the other is minimized (
c(\cdot)
).
We describe each problem with the pair
\langle f,c\rangle
of its objectives. We also describe a solution
S
by the
2
-dimensional point of the values for these objectives, i.e. ,
(f(S),c(S))
.

Definition 0 (Pareto Point).

A
Pareto point
is defined as a solution
S\subseteq\mathcal{V}
such that there
exists
no

other solution
S^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{V}
that is better in
both
objectives, i.e. there is no other solution
S^{\prime}
that satisfies:

(1)

\displaystyle f(S^{\prime})\geq f(S)\quad\text{and}\quad c(S^{\prime})\leq c(S).

In other words, a Pareto point is a feasible solution whose vector of the various objectives (in our case two) is not dominated by the vector of another solution.
The set of all Pareto points
define the
Pareto Frontier
of the problem and, intuitively, they describe the tradeoffs that different solutions attain.

Note that the Pareto frontier of a multi-objective problem (such as
\langle f,c\rangle
) can have exponential size.
This resulted in a large body of work on finding
approximate Pareto frontiers
.
We discuss approximate Pareto frontiers and provide formal definitions related to our problem in the next section.

4.
The
Pareto–
\langle f,c\rangle
Problem

We motivate our definition by considering the Pareto frontier of the bi-objective problem

\langle f,\texttt{Cardinality}\rangle
,
where the goal is to maximize a submodular function and minimize the
Cardinality
cost function.
Note that since
c
takes integer values in
\{1,\ldots,n\}
, the Pareto frontier is polynomial in size. However, in order to find it, we need to solve the following problem for different values of
k
:

\displaystyle\max_{S\subseteq\mathcal{V}}f(S)\text{ such that }|S|\leq k.

However, this problem is submodular maximization under cardinality constraints and it is
NP
-hard
(
28
;
37
)
. Therefore, despite the fact that the Pareto frontier is polynomial in size, we cannot find the points in the frontier in polynomial time unless
P=NP
.

4.1.
Problem Definition

For problems like the one above, we need to define
approximate Pareto frontiers
.
While on a high level, the definition of approximate Pareto frontiers we give is similar to the one proposed by
Papadimitriou and Yannakakis
(
31
)
, the motivation behind this definition is different; in their case, the motivation was the size of the frontiers. In our case, the computational complexity of the underlying computational problem forces us to consider approximate Pareto frontiers regardless of the size of the optimal Pareto frontiers.
We propose the following definition of approximate Pareto frontiers:

Definition 0 (
(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})
-approximate Pareto frontier).

Assume a bi-objective optimization problem
\langle f,c\rangle

and let

\mathcal{P}^{\ast}
be its optimal Pareto frontier for a given input.
For
\alpha_{1}\in(0,1]
and
\alpha_{2}\geq 1
, we say that a set of solutions
\mathcal{P}_{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}}
is an
(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})
–approximate Pareto frontier for
\langle f,c\rangle
such that for every solution
S^{\ast}\in\mathcal{P}^{\ast}
there exists a
S\in\mathcal{P}_{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}}
such that

\displaystyle f(S)\geq\alpha_{1}f(S^{\ast})\quad\text{and}\quad c(S)\leq\alpha_{2}c(S^{\ast}).

The notion of an
(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})
–approximate Pareto frontier
provides a multiplicative approximation of the Pareto frontier. The closer the values of
\alpha_{1}
and
\alpha_{2}
are to
1
, the better the approximation of the optimal Pareto frontier.

The main question is whether there exist values of
\alpha_{1}
and
\alpha_{2}
such that, for any instance
\langle f,c\rangle
, the corresponding
(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})
-approximate Pareto frontier is both polynomially bounded in size and efficiently computable.
We formalize this in the following definition.

Problem 1 (Pareto–
\langle f,c\rangle
).

Consider a ground set
\mathcal{V}
of
n
items, a non-negative monotone submodular function
f:2^{\mathcal{V}}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
to be maximized, and a non-negative cost function
c:2^{\mathcal{V}}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
to be minimized.
The goal of
Pareto–
\langle f,c\rangle
is to find a polynomial-size set of solutions
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}\subseteq 2^{\mathcal{V}}
that forms an
(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})
-approximate Pareto frontier for the
\langle f,c\rangle
problem.

The
Pareto–
\langle f,c\rangle
problem takes different instantiations depending on the
submodular function
f
and the cost function
c
we consider. Throughout, we will assume that
f
is a monotone submodular function and that
c
is any of the three cost functions defined in Section
3
, i.e.,
Cardinality
,
Linear
and
Diameter
.

4.2.
Real-World Instantiations

We map the
Pareto–
\langle f,c\rangle
problem to real-world applications in team formation
(
1
;
2
;
25
;
29
;
40
;
38
)
, influence maximization
(
20
;
7
)
and recommender systems
(
35
;
19
)
.
We provide formal definitions of
V,f
, and
c
, for each case.

Team Formation.

The Pareto frontier formulation captures trade-offs between task coverage and team costs in team formation.
Let
V
be a set of experts, and let
U
be a universe of skills.
Each expert
i\in V
possesses a subset of skills
S_{i}\subseteq U
.
Given a task
T\subseteq U
, which requires a certain set of skills, the
coverage
of a set of experts
Q\subseteq V
is a monotone, submodular function defined as:

(2)

\displaystyle f(Q)=\big|\;\big(\cup_{i\in Q}S_{i}\big)\cap T\;\big|.

To encode the linear cost function, we associate a
hiring
cost
w_{i}
with each expert. To encode the diameter cost function we use a coordination graph,
G=(V,E)
with edge weights
d(i,j)
encoding pairwise communication costs between the experts.

Recommender Systems.

Following Kazemi et al.
(
19
)
, we define the ground set
V
to be restaurants and balance recommendation quality against the size or distance between recommended restaurants.
Each item
i\in V
is associated with a feature representation, which induces a distance function
\kappa(i,j)
between items. From this distance, a similarity matrix
M
is defined as
M(i,j)=e^{-\kappa(i,j)}
.
Given a recommendation set
Q\subseteq V
, the utility function is:

(3)

\displaystyle f(Q)=\sum_{i\in V}\max_{j\in Q}M(i,j).

This is a monotone, submodular function, and measures how well the selected items
represent the full item set.

We represent the linear cost function
c(Q)=\sum_{i\in Q}w_{i}
,
where
w_{i}
denotes the Euclidean distance of restaurant
i
from the city center.
The diameter cost follows from the graph
G=(V,E)
with nodes characterized by their geographical coordinates and edge weights
d(i,j)
corresponding to the geographical distances.

Influence Maximization.

This formulation captures the trade-off between influence spread and
resource expenditure in viral marketing and social network intervention tasks.
Consider a social network
G=(V,E)
and an information propagation model
P
. The goal of influence maximization is to pick a set of seed nodes
Q\subseteq V
such that the spread of influence (e.g., an idea or product) to other nodes in the social network is maximized.

Let
f
denote the expected number of nodes influenced using an Independent Cascade or Linear Threshold model for
P
.
Given a seed set
Q\subseteq V
, it is known that
f(Q)
is monotone, submodular
(
20
)
.
For linear costs, we associate a cost
w_{i}
with each node
i\in V
, modeling the operational cost of targeting that individual (e.g., incentives or advertising spend), yielding

c(Q)=\sum_{i\in Q}w_{i}
.
To model coordination constraints among seeds, we define a graph

G=(V,E)
where edge weights
d(i,j)
encode communication or geographical
distances between users, and instantiate the diameter cost as

c(Q)=\max_{i,j\in Q}d(i,j)
, encouraging well-coordinated seed sets.

5.
Computing Approximate Pareto Frontiers

Papadimitriou and Yannakakis
(
31
)
showed that polynomial-size
approximate Pareto frontiers
can be computed via discretization of the objective space. Their framework relies on
the ability to solve constrained single-objective subproblems.
In our setting, this does not hold: constrained maximization of
f
(resp. minimization of
c
) subject to a constraint on
c
(resp.
f
) is
NP
-hard.
We get around this computational obstacle, and present algorithms with approximation guarantees for different instantiations of
Pareto–
\langle f,c\rangle
; proofs are deferred to Appendix
A
.

5.1.
Algorithms for
Pareto–
\langle f,\texttt{Linear}\rangle

Let us start by considering two special cases of
Pareto–
\langle f,\texttt{Linear}\rangle
in order to gain some intuition for the general algorithm.

The
Pareto–
\langle f,\texttt{Cardinality}\rangle
Problem
. This is a variant of

Pareto–
\langle f,\texttt{Linear}\rangle
, where the items have uniform cost i.e., for each
i\in V
,
w_{i}=1
; note that the

, and thus the Pareto frontier has size at most

; i.e., polynomial in the size of the input.

We find an approximate Pareto frontier for this problem using the
C-Greedy

2
).
The algorithm exploits the discreteness of the cardinality cost, and constructs a set of cost thresholds

\mathcal{B}=\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
; for each threshold
B\in\mathcal{B}
,
invokes the greedy procedure
{\texttt{Greedy}}(f,c_{k},B,0)
to approximately solve

\max_{S\subseteq\mathcal{V}}\;f(S)\quad\text{s.t.}\quad|S|\leq B.

Each threshold yields a candidate solution that approximates the optimal utility
achievable at the corresponding cardinality level.

The
FC-Greedy
algorithm.

The special cases discussed above illustrate how discreteness in either the
cost or the objective can be exploited to construct approximate Pareto
frontiers.
For general
Pareto–
\langle f,\texttt{Linear}\rangle

submodular utility function
f
nor the linear cost function

have a polynomially-bounded range.
As a result, the Pareto frontier may be exponentially large, and the techniques
used for
C-Greedy
and
F-Greedy
no longer apply directly.

To address this setting, we
discretize the cost and utility axes
by picking a polynomial number of utility and cost values (which define a grid).
We then solve the corresponding constrained-optimization problems at each grid point.
This yields two complementary approximate Pareto sets: one obtained by applying

C-Greedy
on the cost, and one obtained by applying
F-Greedy
on the utility.

Discussion.

The
FC-Greedy
algorithm provides a general and theoretically grounded approach
for approximating Pareto frontiers in the general

Pareto–
\langle f,\texttt{Linear}\rangle
setting when logarithmic grids

\mathcal{K}_{\varepsilon}
and
\mathcal{B}_{\varepsilon}
are used.
However, the need to solve one constrained subproblem per grid point makes the
approach polynomial but often impractical on large instances.
This motivates the development of a faster algorithm that approximates the Pareto
frontier using a grid-free approach and fewer greedy runs.

The
ParetoGreedy
algorithm.

We introduce an efficient alternative that directly enumerates candidate
tradeoffs using a small number of greedy runs.
Rather than solving separate subproblems for each budget level,

ParetoGreedy
explicitly collects these intermediate solutions (prefixes) and
applies Pareto pruning.

Inspired by prior work on submodular maximization with knapsack
constraints

enumerates all feasible seed sets

For each seed

up to a maximum budget

B

records all intermediate solutions encountered.
All recorded solutions are then Pareto-pruned to obtain an approximate Pareto
frontier.

5.2.
Algorithm for
Pareto–
\langle f,\texttt{Diameter}\rangle

In the
Pareto–
\langle f,\texttt{Diameter}\rangle
problem, the cost of a
solution is defined by its diameter in a weighted graph that defines a distance metric between
its nodes.
Although the diameter is not a linear function, it admits a strong
geometric characterization: any set of diameter at most
B
is contained in a
metric ball of radius
B
centered at some vertex.
This property allows us to enumerate all cost levels implicitly,
without an explicit discretization.

C-Greedy-Diameter
(Alg.
6
)
extends
C-Greedy
to the diameter setting by
replacing numeric cost thresholds with metric balls.
For each vertex
v\in V
, the algorithm orders all vertices by increasing
distance to
v
and considers the resulting nested family of balls

B(v,r)=\{u\in\mathcal{V}:d(v,u)\leq r\},

as
r
ranges over all distinct distances.
Each such ball induces a feasible solution with diameter at most
2r
.
All candidate solutions obtained in this manner are evaluated and subsequently
Pareto-pruned to produce the approximate Pareto frontier.

7.
Conclusion

We examined utility–cost tradeoffs in submodular maximization through the lens of approximate Pareto frontiers. We formalized the general
Pareto–
\langle f,c\rangle
problem and analyzed it across multiple classes of submodular objectives and cost functions arising in recommender systems, influence maximization, and team formation. We developed algorithms with provable guarantees alongside efficient practical methods, and demonstrated empirically that they compute high-quality approximate Pareto frontiers on real-world datasets. Together, our theoretical results and experiments establish a principled and practical framework for understanding utility–cost tradeoffs in submodular optimization.

Appendix A
Proofs of Lemmas

A.1.
Proof of Lemma
1

Lemma 0.

Given cardinality thresholds
\mathcal{B}=\{1,\ldots,n\}
and
\tau\geq 0
, the
C-Greedy
algorithm returns an
\left(1-\frac{1}{e},1\right)
–approximate Pareto frontier
\mathcal{P}
of size
\mathcal{O}(n)
for the
Pareto–
\langle f,\texttt{Cardinality}\rangle
problem.

Proof.

For each cardinality threshold
B\in\mathcal{B}=\{1,\ldots,n\}
, the algorithm
invokes the greedy procedure to approximately solve

\max_{S\subseteq\mathcal{V}}f(S)\quad\text{s.t.}\quad|S|\leq B.

By the classical result of Nemhauser et al.
(
28
)
,
greedy returns a solution
S_{B}
satisfying

f(S_{B})\;\geq\;\left(1-\tfrac{1}{e}\right)\cdot\mathit{OPT}(B),

where
\mathit{OPT}(B)
denotes the maximum value achievable by any set of size at most

B
.
Since
|S_{B}|\leq B
, the cost of
S_{B}
matches that of the corresponding Pareto
point.
Thus, for every Pareto-optimal solution
S^{\ast}
with
|S^{\ast}|=B
, there exists
a solution
S_{B}
such that

f(S_{B})\geq(1-1/e)\,f(S^{\ast})
and
|S_{B}|\leq|S^{\ast}|
.
Collecting these solutions for all
B\in\mathcal{B}
and applying Pareto
pruning yields a
(1-1/e,1)
–approximate Pareto frontier.
The frontier contains at most one candidate per cardinality level, and therefore
has size
\mathcal{O}(n)
.
∎

A.2.
Proof of Lemma
2

Lemma 0.

Assuming
f
takes polynomially many distinct values and letting
\mathcal{K}

denote this value set, and
\tau\geq 0
, the
F-Greedy
algorithm
returns an
(1,\log n)
–approximate Pareto frontier
\mathcal{P}
of polynomial
size for the
Pareto–
\langle f,\texttt{Linear}\rangle
problem.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{K}
denote the polynomial-size set of attainable values of
f
.
For each target value
K\in\mathcal{K}
, the
F-Greedy
algorithm invokes the
greedy procedure to approximately solve the submodular cover problem

\min_{S\subseteq\mathcal{V}}c(S)\quad\text{s.t.}\quad f(S)\geq K.

Let
\mathit{OPT}(K)
denote the minimum cost of any solution achieving utility at least

K
.
By the standard approximation guarantee of the greedy algorithm for weighted set
cover
(
37
)
, the greedy solution
S_{K}
satisfies

c(S_{K})\;\leq\;O(\log n)\cdot\mathit{OPT}(K),\qquad f(S_{K})\;\geq\;K.

Thus, for every Pareto-optimal solution
S^{\ast}
with
f(S^{\ast})=K
, there exists
a solution
S_{K}
such that

f(S_{K})\geq f(S^{\ast})\quad\text{and}\quad c(S_{K})\leq O(\log n)\,c(S^{\ast}).

Collecting these solutions for all
K\in\mathcal{K}
and applying Pareto pruning
yields a
(1,\log n)
–approximate Pareto frontier.
Since
|\mathcal{K}|
is polynomial in
n
, the resulting frontier has polynomial
size.
∎

For the
\mathcal{K}_{\varepsilon}

\mathcal{B}_{\varepsilon}
constructed as above, and
\tau=2

\left((1-\tfrac{1}{e})(1-\varepsilon),\;(1+\varepsilon)\,O(\log n)\right)

\mathcal{K}_{\varepsilon}

\mathcal{B}_{\varepsilon}

A.4.
Proof of Lemma
4

Lemma 0.

ParetoGreedy
returns a

\bigl(1-(1-\beta)e^{-\gamma},\,1\bigr)
–approximate Pareto frontier
\mathcal{P}

for
Pareto–
\langle f,\texttt{Linear}\rangle
.
Specifically, for every Pareto-optimal solution
S^{\ast}
,
\mathcal{P}
contains a
solution
S
such that

f(S)\;\geq\;\bigl(1-(1-\beta)e^{-\gamma}\bigr)f(S^{\ast}),\qquad c_{\ell}(S)\;\leq\;c_{\ell}(S^{\ast}),

where
\beta\in(0,1]
is the maximum fraction of
f(S^{\ast})
captured by a seed

S_{0}\subseteq S^{\ast}
, and
\gamma\in(0,1]
is the fraction of the remaining
budget
c_{\ell}(S^{\ast})-c_{\ell}(S_{0})
attained by
S
.

Proof.

Fix an arbitrary Pareto-optimal solution
S^{\ast}
with cost
c_{\ell}(S^{\ast})
.
Since
ParetoGreedy
enumerates all feasible seed sets
S_{0}\subseteq\mathcal{V}

of size at most
\tau
, there exists a seed

S_{0}\subseteq S^{\ast}
such that

f(S_{0})\geq\beta f(S^{\ast})
, where

\beta\in(0,1]
denotes the maximum fraction of
f(S^{\ast})
captured by any
seed contained in
S^{\ast}
.

Starting from
S_{0}
,
ParetoGreedy
runs the greedy procedure under
budget
c_{\ell}(S^{\ast})
and records all intermediate prefixes.
Let
S_{q}
denote the prefix such that

c_{\ell}(S_{q})\;\geq\;\gamma\bigl(c_{\ell}(S^{\ast})-c_{\ell}(S_{0})\bigr),

where
\gamma\in(0,1]
is the fraction of the remaining budget attained by the
prefix.
By the standard analysis of greedy submodular maximization with a knapsack
constraint,
S_{q}
satisfies

f(S_{q})\;\geq\;f(S_{0})+\bigl(1-e^{-\gamma}\bigr)\bigl(f(S^{\ast})-f(S_{0})\bigr).

Using
f(S_{0})\geq\beta f(S^{\ast})
, we obtain

f(S_{q})\;\geq\;\bigl(1-(1-\beta)e^{-\gamma}\bigr)\,f(S^{\ast}).

Since
ParetoGreedy
records all greedy prefixes and applies Pareto pruning,
it considers a solution
S_{q}
with

c_{\ell}(S_{q})\leq c_{\ell}(S^{\ast})
and retains a non-dominated solution

S\in\mathcal{P}
satisfying

f(S)\;\geq\;\bigl(1-(1-\beta)e^{-\gamma}\bigr)f(S^{\ast}),\qquad c_{\ell}(S)\;\leq\;c_{\ell}(S^{\ast}).

As the choice of
S^{\ast}
was arbitrary, the returned set
\mathcal{P}
forms a

\bigl(1-(1-\beta)e^{-\gamma},\,1\bigr)
–approximate Pareto frontier for

Pareto–
\langle f,\texttt{Linear}\rangle
.

A.5.
Proof of Lemma
5

Lemma 0.

The
C-Greedy-Diameter
algorithm returns a
(1,\,2)
–approximate Pareto frontier of size
\mathcal{O}(n^{2})
for
Pareto–
\langle f,\texttt{Diameter}\rangle
.

Proof.

Let
S^{\ast}
be an arbitrary Pareto-optimal solution for

Pareto–
\langle f,\texttt{Diameter}\rangle
with diameter
c_{d}(S^{\ast})=D^{\ast}
.
Fix any element
v\in S^{\ast}
.
By definition of diameter, every
u\in S^{\ast}
satisfies

d(v,u)\leq D^{\ast}
, and hence

S^{\ast}\subseteq B(v,D^{\ast}).

Since
d(\cdot,\cdot)
is a metric, for any
u,w\in B(v,D^{\ast})
,
the triangle inequality implies

d(u,w)\leq 2D^{\ast}
, so any subset of
B(v,D^{\ast})
has diameter at most
2D^{\ast}
.

The
C-Greedy-Diameter
algorithm enumerates all centers
v\in\mathcal{V}

and considers the nested balls
B(v,r)
for all distinct distances
r
.
In particular, it considers the ball
B(v,D^{\ast})
and the corresponding set

S=B(v,D^{\ast})
.
Since
f
is monotone,
f(S)\geq f(S^{\ast})
, and

c_{d}(S)\leq 2D^{\ast}
.
After Pareto pruning, the output set
\mathcal{P}
therefore contains a solution

S
such that

f(S)\geq f(S^{\ast})\quad\text{and}\quad c_{d}(S)\leq 2\,c_{d}(S^{\ast}).

Since each center
v\in\mathcal{V}
induces at most
n
distinct ball radii
(corresponding to pairwise distances
d(v,u)
for
u\in\mathcal{V}
), the total
number of candidate solutions considered by
C-Greedy-Diameter
is
O(n^{2})
.
∎

Appendix B
Additional Experimental Details

B.1.
Dataset Descriptions and Preprocessing

We evaluate our algorithms on real-world datasets drawn from three representative
application domains in which submodular optimization naturally arises:
team formation (
Freelancer
,
Bibsonomy
,
IMDB-1
,
IMDB-2
),
recommender systems (
Yelp-PHX
,
Yelp-LV
),
and influence maximization (
NetPHY
,
NetHEPT
).
These domains capture a broad range of practical settings in data mining where
the objective is to select a subset of entities—such as experts, items, or
network nodes—that maximizes a monotone submodular utility function while
minimizing an associated cost

(
9
;
35
;
19
;
20
;
7
;
30
;
40
;
38
;
39
)
.

For each domain, we apply application-specific preprocessing to construct the
ground set of items
\mathcal{V}
, define the submodular utility function
f
, and
instantiate the cost function
c
as described in
Section
4.2
.
This includes filtering and sampling entities, constructing feature
representations or graphs as needed, and normalizing costs to comparable scales.
To ensure robustness, we evaluate all methods on multiple random samples drawn
from each dataset and report aggregated results.

We provide detailed descriptions of each dataset, along with all preprocessing
steps and parameter choices, in the remainder of this section.
To support full reproducibility, we make the processed datasets and the
complete preprocessing code used in our experiments available online
2
2

2

https://anonymous.4open.science/r/Pareto-Teams-01B8
.

Team Formation.

We evaluate team formation on four datasets:
Freelancer
,
Bibsonomy
,
IMDB-1
,
and
IMDB-2
.
In all cases, items correspond to experts, each expert is associated with a set
of skills, and tasks specify required skills.
Given a task, the utility function
f(S)
measures the coverage of
task-required skills by the selected expert set
S
, as defined in
Section
4.2
.
A collaboration graph over experts induces pairwise coordination distances,
which are used to define diameter costs.

Freelancer.

The Freelancer dataset consists of real job postings and freelancers collected
from
freelancer.com
.
The raw data contains
993
jobs (tasks) requiring discrete skills and
1{,}212

freelancers (experts) with associated skill sets.
We restrict attention to freelancers possessing at least two skills and randomly
sample a subset of
|\widehat{\mathcal{V}}|=50
experts and a fixed skill
vocabulary, as summarized in Table
1
.
Tasks are formed by randomly sampling from the job set.
The coordination cost graph is defined as a complete graph over experts, where
the distance between two experts is given by the Jaccard distance between their
skill sets.
By construction, these distances lie in
[0,1]
, with smaller values indicating
greater skill overlap.

IMDB.

The IMDB datasets are derived from the International Movie Database, which
contains information about movies, TV shows, and documentaries, including their
associated actors, directors, release years, and genres.
We simulate a team-formation setting in which movie genres correspond to skills,
directors act as experts, and actors define tasks.
The skill set of a director or actor is defined as the union of genres of the
movies they have participated in.
For example, a director such as Christopher Nolan is associated with skills
{drama, action, history, biography, sci-fi, thriller}, while an actor such as
Emma Stone is associated with skills {crime, comedy, sci-fi, animation, romance,
adventure}.

We construct two instances,
IMDB-1
and
IMDB-2
, by selecting all movies
released after fixed year thresholds and retaining directors and actors
associated with at least two genres.
We additionally enforce that each director shares at least one actor with at
least one other director, ensuring connectivity in the collaboration graph.
From the resulting pool, we randomly sample
|\widehat{\mathcal{V}}|=200
and

|\widehat{\mathcal{V}}|=400
directors, respectively, and sample a fixed set of

300
actors to form the task sets (Table
1
).

We construct a social graph over directors, where an edge exists between two
directors if they have collaborated with at least two common actors.
The coordination cost of an edge is defined as
e^{-fD}
, where
D
is the number
of shared actors and
f=1/10
, yielding edge weights in
(0,1)
with smaller
values indicating stronger collaboration ties.

BibSonomy.

The BibSonomy dataset is obtained from a social bookmarking and publication
sharing system.
Each publication is associated with user-generated tags; we remove stopwords and
retain the
75
most frequent tags as skills.
We focus on prolific authors with at least
12
publications as experts, and
less prolific authors as tasks.
An author’s skill set is defined as the union of tags across their publications.

We construct instances by filtering publications by year and retaining authors
who share at least one co-authored paper with another author.
From this pool, we randomly sample
|\widehat{\mathcal{V}}|=250
experts to form
the
Bibsonomy
dataset used in our experiments (Table
1
).
A collaboration graph is defined using co-authorship: two authors are connected
if they have written at least one paper together.
Edge weights are defined as
e^{-fD}
, where
D
is the number of distinct
co-authored papers and
f=1/10
.

Cost Models.

Across all team-formation datasets, we consider three cost functions.
The cardinality cost bounds the number of selected experts.
For the linear (knapsack) cost, we assign each expert a hiring cost drawn
uniformly at random from the range
[5,100]
.
For the diameter cost, we use the maximum shortest-path distance among selected
experts in the corresponding collaboration graph.

Recommender Systems.

For restaurant summarization, we use data from the Yelp Academic Dataset
3
3

3

https://www.yelp.com/dataset
,
restricting attention to all restaurant businesses located in the Las Vegas and
Phoenix metropolitan areas, which we denote by
Yelp-LV
and
Yelp-PHX
,
respectively.
After filtering by business category and location, the resulting ground sets
contain
1{,}849
restaurants for
Yelp-PHX
and
3{,}203
restaurants for

Yelp-LV
.

Each restaurant
i\in V
is represented by a feature vector capturing
categorical and numerical attributes (e.g., cuisine categories, price range,
ratings, and amenities), following standard preprocessing pipelines used in prior
work.
These features induce a distance function
\kappa(i,j)
between restaurants,
from which we define a similarity matrix

M(i,j)=e^{-\kappa(i,j)}
.
Given a recommendation set
Q\subseteq V
, we use the facility-location utility
function

f(Q)=\sum_{i\in V}\max_{j\in Q}M(i,j),

which is monotone and submodular and measures how well the selected restaurants
represent the full set.
To evaluate performance, we sample random subsets of restaurants of fixed size
(Table
1
).

We consider three cost models.
The
cardinality
cost bounds the number of selected restaurants.
For the
linear (knapsack)
cost, we define

c(Q)=\sum_{i\in Q}w_{i},

where
w_{i}
is the Euclidean distance between restaurant
i
and a fixed city
center.
Distances are computed from latitude and longitude coordinates using

scipy.spatial.distance
.
The city center location is fixed per metropolitan area and shared across all
experiments.

For the
diameter
cost, we define a graph
G=(V,E)
whose nodes correspond
to restaurants and whose edge weights
d(i,j)
are given by the Euclidean
distance between their geographic coordinates.
The diameter cost of a recommendation set
Q
is then

c(Q)=\max_{i,j\in Q}d(i,j),

which encourages geographically compact and well-distributed recommendation
sets.

Influence Maximization.

We evaluate influence maximization on two academic collaboration networks:

NetHEPT
and
NetPHY
.
Following the experimental setup of
(
20
;
29
)
,
we use the High Energy Physics–Theory collaboration network from the arXiv
e-print repository.
Nodes correspond to authors and edges represent co-authorship relations.

Following prior work, we extract a large connected component from the full
collaboration graphs.
For
NetHEPT
, this yields a subgraph with

|\widehat{\mathcal{V}}|=1{,}673
nodes and
3{,}863
edges, while for
NetPHY
the
largest connected component contains
|\widehat{\mathcal{V}}|=912
nodes and

8{,}748
edges, which we use as the experimental ground sets.

Influence spread is modeled using the Independent Cascade (IC) model.
Edge multiplicities are treated as weights, and, as in prior work, we assign a
uniform activation probability
p=0.01
to each edge.
The utility function
f(S)
is the expected number of activated nodes starting
from seed set
S
, estimated via Monte Carlo simulation with a fixed number of
runs (Table
1
).
We note that similar qualitative results are observed under the Linear Threshold
model.

For cost modeling, the cardinality constraint bounds the number of seed nodes.
For linear (knapsack) costs, we associate a cost
w_{i}
with each node proportional
to its degree, reflecting the increased effort or expense required to target
highly connected individuals.

To define diameter costs, we construct a weighted graph where edge weights depend
on structural similarity.
Specifically, for an edge
(u,v)
, we define its weight as

w(u,v)=\exp(-\alpha\cdot|\Gamma(u)\cap\Gamma(v)|),

where
\Gamma(u)
denotes the neighborhood of
u
and
\alpha>0
is a fixed
scaling parameter.
Pairwise distances are then defined as shortest-path distances in this weighted
graph.
Unreachable node pairs are assigned a large penalty value.
The diameter cost of a seed set
S
is defined as the maximum pairwise distance
among nodes in
S
, encouraging well-coordinated and structurally cohesive seed
sets.
