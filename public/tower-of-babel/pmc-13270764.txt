Prenatal non-infectious mild stressor modulates early-life lung development in a time-specific manner
Authors: Ying Dong, Stefan Hadzic, Annika Leidner, Manuela Marega, Stefano Rivetti, Harald Ehrhardt, Saverio Bellusci
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMaternal exposure to various insults during pregnancy can influence early-life organ development. Whether this applies to seemingly mild stressors remains unclearly defined. Better understanding of the nature and strength of prenatal exposure factor to trigger fetal programming effect in the lung may help to optimize perinatal-neonatal health care.MethodsPregnant wild type C57BL/6J mice were randomized at 15.5 day post coitum (dpc) or 18.5 dpc to intraperitoneal (i.p.) injection of sterile physiological saline. Mice undergoing a natural process of pregnancy without any intervention served as blank controls. Newborn mice were euthanized at postnatal day 1 for lung harvests. Lung tissues were subjected to Weigert staining to visualize elastic fiber distribution and quantify total parenchymal cells. Additionally, qPCR, immunofluorescence staining and western blot were performed to assess the expression of signature markers of the lung epithelium and other compartments.ResultsNo adverse perinatal outcomes occurred except a non-significant reduction in maternal weight gain after i.p. injection of sterile physiological saline. Pups demonstrated similar birth weights across groups. The total number of lung parenchymal cells increased in exposed pups compared with blank controls, with no significant difference in elastin fiber distribution. Enhanced macrophage infiltration with predominant type 2 cytokine responses was observed in newborn mice lungs following maternal i.p. injection of saline. There was an elevated surfactant protein C (Sftpc) expression in alveolar type 2 (AT2) cells in the 15.5 dpc exposure group compared to blank controls. No sex-dependent difference was demonstrated in Sftpc expression. Correlational analysis of signature gene expression indicated crosstalk between AT2 cells and other lung cellular compartments. The expression of stem cell antigen (Sca1) in lung mesenchymal progenitor cells demonstrated a time-related response without sex differentiation upon prenatal exposure to maternal i.p. injection of sterile physiological saline.ConclusionsResults of this preclinical study in newborn mice contribute to current understanding of fetal programming by showing that maternal exposure to even non-infectious mild stressors before birth is able to modulate early-life lung development. This underscores an intensified sensibility of unborn fetus to seemingly harmless low-magnitude maternal stressors.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12931-026-03719-x.Keywords: Alveolar type 2 cells, Fetal programming, Mesenchymal progenitor cell, Preterm, Pregnancy, Pulmonary macrophage, Surfactant protein C, Stressor

Introduction

The antenatal period constitutes a critical developmental window for the health and disease of the offspring [1]. Emerging evidence indicates that maternal exposure to various factors during pregnancy, including but not limited to infection, substance abuse, diet and air pollution, may influence early-life organ development and have lasting impacts on health outcomes beyond infancy [2–6]. Detailed understanding of fetal programming can contribute to improved maternal and neonatal care with profound implications for public health [7].

Due to ethical concerns, animal exposure models are indispensable to elaborate fetal programming. Not only accumulative maternal exposure to strong biochemical insults [3, 5, 6], but also mild stressors during pregnancy are capable of influencing fetal organ development at cellular and molecular levels [8, 9]. Of note, certain prenatal factors are shown to induce disruptive, protective or no effects in fetal organs, depending on the exposure time at early-, mid- or late-gestation [10, 11]. Moreover, both clinical and preclinical data imply that male and female fetuses may be differently affected by maternal exposure factors [12, 13], suggesting that fetal programming can be orchestrated in a sex-specific manner.

Nowadays, preterm births constitute approximately 10% of live-born neonates worldwide, with temporally rising incidences of extremely preterm infants (EPIs) in many industrialized countries [14]. Preterm birth-related complications, especially lung epithelial immaturity marked by surfactant deficiency, represent a pivotal determinant of EPIs’ survival postnatally [14, 15]. Indeed, proper lung epithelial development is regulated by a sophisticated crosstalk between epithelium and other lung cellular compartments, including mesenchyme, vasculature and immune system [16–18]. Experimental mice represent rational models for investigating early-life human lung development and diseases, due to structural similarities between mice lungs at birth and those of EPIs at the late canalicular/early saccular stage [19, 20]. Until now, a limited variety of perinatal factors, predominantly lipopolysaccharides (LPS) and hyperoxia, have been investigated in mice exposure models focusing on early-life lung morbidities [16, 21–25]. In this study, maternal exposure to seemingly mild non-infectious stimuli, namely intraperitoneal (i.p.) injection of sterile physiological saline, was found to induce considerable pulmonary changes in newborn mice. This will expand our current knowledge on the nature and strength of prenatal exposure factors to program fetal lung development, and may provide preclinical evidence to support clinical studies aimed at optimizing respiratory management.

Materials and methods

Experiment model

Ethical approval was obtained by the Federal Authorities for Animal Research of the Regierungspraesidium Giessen, Germany (G76/2021, 1066_GP) in accordance with the German Animal Welfare Law and the European Legislation for the Protection of Animals used for Scientific Purposes (2010/63/EU). This study is a secondary analysis of blank and sham controls in previous mice experiments of prenatal LPS-induced lung injury [25]. Wild type C57BL/6J mice (Charles River Lab, Sulzfeld, Germany) were fed ad libitum and kept under a light-dark cycle of 12 h in a specific pathogen-free (SPF) animal facility of Justus-Liebig University (JLU), Giessen. Female mice at 8 to 16 weeks of age were mated with C57BL/6J males for one night and checked next day at 6 a.m. for vaginal plug, which indicates 0.5 days post coitum (dpc). Pregnancy was confirmed if there is a weight increase of ≥ 2 g at 12.5 dpc as compared with that at 0.5 dpc. Being randomly assigned at 15.5 or 18.5 dpc, pregnant mice were injected intraperitoneally (i.p.) with 100 µl sterile physiological saline using a 26-G cannula (BD PrecisionGlide Needle, Becton Dickinson, Heidelberg, Germany). After i.p. injection, daily health monitoring including weight control was carried out based on standardized score sheets of the ethical approval (G76/2021, 1066_GP). Mice undergoing a natural process of pregnancy without any intervention served as blank controls. Compared to human lung development, 15.5 dpc corresponds to the pseudoglandular stage and 18.5 dpc to the saccular stage of mice [19]. These two time points were selected to mimic maternal exposure in the mid- and late-pregnancy, respectively.

Lung harvest and sex genotyping of newborn mice

At postnatal day 1 (P1), pups were euthanized with a lethal dose (600 mg/kg) of i.p. pentobarbital (Narcoren, Boehringer Ingelheim, Germany). Then, the right ventricle of heart was perfused with cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) at 20 cm H2O as previously described [26], with modified perfusion time of 5 min to prevent tissue damage of P1 lungs [25]. In the following, right lung lobes were resected for mRNA and protein analysis, and the left lobe for histochemical assessment. The gender of newborn mice was determined using the male-specific gene Sry locus of genomic DNA isolated from tail biopsies [27]. Amplicons were run on a 1.5% (w/v) agarose gel, and visualized by ethidium bromide staining.

Weigert staining of lung tissues

The left lobe was perfused intratracheally with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) as previously described [26], and then fixed in 4% PFA for 24 h at 4 °C. Next, tissues were serially dehydrated in ethanol and embedded in paraffin (EG 1150 C, Leica, Wetzlar, Germany). Slices were cut at 3 μm, and those from the midplane of the left lobe were stained using Resorcinol-Fuchsin solution (X877.3, Carl Roth, Karlsruhe, Germany) to visualize elastic fibers. To enhance the contrast, nuclei of lung parenchymal cells were counterstained with 1% methyl green (C. I. 42590, Carl Roth) [25]. Images were captured using a bright field microscope (Olympus, Hamburg, Germany) at 40× magnification, and the number of parenchymal cell nuclei was counted in 5 fields of view per tissue section and 3 random tissue sections per animal using Duo Staining protocol of Qwin V3 software (Leica, Wetzlar, Germany) [28, 29]. In this protocol, elastic fibers (dark purple) and the nuclei of lung parenchymal cells (light brown/blue) are differentiated based on their color thresholds.

RNA extraction and RT-qPCR

RNA was extracted from two lobes of the right lung by using RNeasy Plus Micro Kit (74034, Qiagen, Hamburg, Germany). After quantification under NanoDrop™ Spectrophotometer 2000/2000c (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Rockford, USA), RNA was reverse transcribed (QuantiTect Reverse Transcription Kit, 205313, Qiagen) to cDNA at a final concentration of 5 ng/µl. SYBR Green Master Mix (11733-038, Thermo Fisher Scientific) was used for real time (RT)-qPCR (Light Cycler 480 system, Roche, Mannheim, Germany). Mouse primers used in this experiment were listed in Table 1, and samples were run in duplicates using hypoxanthine phosphoribosyltransferase (Hprt) as the house-keeping gene. Values of cycle threshold (CT) were obtained with dCT being calculated: dCT = CT (reference gene) – CT (gene of interest).

Immunofluorescence staining

Lung sections were deparaffinized and blocked with 6% bovine serum albumin (BSA) (001-000-162, Jackson ImmunoResearch, Cambrigeshire, UK) with 0.4% Triton X-100 (X100, Sigma Aldrich) in PBS at room temperature (RT) for 1 h. In the next, primary and secondary antibodies (Table 2) were sequentially incubated at 4 °C overnight and at RT for 1 h, respectively. For macrophage marker F4/80, an additional step of antigen unmasking via proteinase K (1122470, 20 mg/ml, Qiagen) for 10 min at RT was performed before the incubation with primary antibody. After being washed for 3 times after the incubation with secondary antibodies, slides were stained with nucleic counterstains 4,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) (62248, Thermo Fisher Scientific), and fluorescence images were acquired via Leica DM5500 B fluorescence microscope (Leica, Wetzlar, Germany). The same setting (e.g. brightness and exposure) were applied to all slides from different exposure groups. The quantification of F4/80 immunofluorescent signals was carried out in 5 fields of view per lung section and 3 random sections per animal using open-source Image J (Fiji) software.

Protein extraction and western blot

The remaining right lung lobes were homogenized with 1x Halt™ protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (78429, Thermo Fisher Scientific). After measuring the concentration via BCA protein assay (23225, Thermo Fisher Scientific), samples in prepared loading buffer containing 1x RIPA (9806, Cell Signaling Technology), 2x laemmli buffer (1610737, Bio-Rad) and β-mercaptoethanol (M6250, Sigma Aldrich) were added onto 10% Mini-PROTEAN® TGX™ Precast Gels (4561034, Bio-Rad) and run at 100 V for 1 h. Next, distributed protein samples were transferred to a 0.2 μm polyvinylidene fluoride membrane (1704156, Bio-Rad) by semi-dry electroblotting for 15 min (25 V, 2.5 A) using Transblot Turbo System (1704150, Bio-Rad). The membrane was then blocked with 6% skimmed milk (68514-61-4, Carl Roth) in Tris-buffered saline with Tween 20 (TBST) at RT on a shaker for 1 h, followed by incubation with the primary antibody (Table 2) overnight at 4 °C. After washing with 1x TBST the membrane was incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-labelled secondary antibody at RT for 1 h. The protein bands were detected by ECL western blotting reagents (RPN2235, Amersham, Germany), and chemiluminescent signals were quantified by Image Lab Software (Bio-Rad).

Statistical analyses

Data from independent experiments were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Differences among groups were analyzed by non-parametric Kruskall-Wallis test and Dunn’s post-hoc test for multiple comparisons. The number of biological samples for each group is depicted in figure legends. For correlation analysis, non-parametric Spearmann coefficient r value was computed for the target gene expression versus others, with r > 0.5 or < -0.5 being defined as relevant correlation. For time- and sex-dependent analysis, two-way ANOVA followed by Bonferroni’s multiple comparisons test was performed. All statistical analyses were carried out by using Prism® 6 software (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA), with statistical significance defined as p value < 0.05.

Results

Neonatal and maternal outcome

Maternal exposure to i.p. injection of sterile physiological saline at either 15.5 or 18.5 dpc has caused no adverse perinatal outcomes, such as preterm deliveries or stillbirths. Newborn mice demonstrated no significant weight differences across groups (Fig. 1A, p = 0.212). Nonetheless, there was a slight stagnation in the weight gain of dams around the time point of i.p. injection at 15.5 dpc (Fig. 1B). Similar data were not available for exposure group at 18.5 dpc, as term delivery mostly occurs at 19.5 dpc in our animal facility. Compared to blank controls showing 10.9% of increase in the median weight between 14.5 and 16.5 dpc, 15.5 dpc exposure group had a corresponding value of 4.8% (p = 0.363 vs. blank controls).

Cellular composition and elastin fiber distribution in newborn mice lungs

As displayed in Fig. 2A, there is an abundance of lung parenchyma dispersed with air-filled spaces in newborn mice lungs at P1. The count of total lung parenchymal cells increased in exposed pups versus blank controls, with statistical significance being observed for 18.5 dpc exposure group (Fig. 2B). Signs of beginning alveolarization are demonstrated by the formation of ridges at air-sac walls. Elastin provides essential scaffolds for alveolar development in early-life [30]. Developing alveoli are richly encompassed by elastin fibers extending to the tip of septal ridges (Fig. 2A). Maternal i.p. saline exposure, regardless of the timing, seems to have no significant effect on elastin distribution in newborn mice lungs (Fig. 2A).

Prenatal stimuli at mid-gestation promoted lung AT2 maturation

Compared to blank controls, maternal i.p. injection of physiological saline at 15.5 dpc significantly promoted the gene expression of pulmonary surfactant protein C (Sftpc) (Fig. 3A). No significant change of Sftpc expression in the 18.5 dpc exposure group was observed (Fig. 3A). Sex-specific data yielded no difference in Sftpc gene expression between male and female newborn mice under the same exposure time point (Supplemental Fig. 1). Of note, a significant reduction in Sftpc gene expression was observed for males in 18.5 dpc compared to 15.5 dpc exposure group, but no such change was demonstrated for female pups (Supplemental Fig. 1). Immunofluorescence staining of intracellular prosurfactant protein C (proSPC) specifically identifies alveolar type 2 (AT2) cells (Fig. 3B), which demonstrated similar time-dependent signal patterns as the gene expression of Sftpc (Fig. 3B).

Correlational cues on crosstalk between AT2 cells and other lung compartments

Signature markers of each lung compartment were chosen based on previous transcriptomic or single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) studies with particular focus on prenatal or early postnatal life period [21, 26, 31, 32] (Fig. 4). Coefficient values showed a strong correlation in gene expression between AT2 marker Sftpc and mesenchymal progenitor marker stem cell antigen 1 (Sca1) (r > 0.75), followed by M2 macrophage marker resistin like alpha (Retnla also Fizz1) (r = 0.58) and AT1 signature marker HOP homeobox (Hopx) (r = 0.54) (Fig. 4A). This indicates an interaction between AT2 cells and other cellular compartments to facilitate surfactant synthesis.

Signature genes of vascular compartment, including platelet endothelial cell adhesion molecule 1 (Pecam1), vascular endothelial growth factor A (Vegfa) and collagen type 1 alpha 1 (Col1a1), seem not to be closely correlated with Sftpc expression (Fig. 4A). There is significant reduction of Pecam1 and Vefga expression upon maternal i.p. of saline at 18.5 dpc compared to 15.5 dpc exposure group and blank controls, respectively (Fig. 4B).

Prenatal non-infectious stimuli drive pulmonary macrophage type 2 cytokine responses

With reference to previous relevant studies, several representative type 1 pro-inflammatory and type 2 anti-inflammatory cytokines were selected [22, 33]. At the mRNA level, gene expression of type 1 cytokines was not significantly stimulated by maternal i.p. of sterile saline, except Ccl2 (Fig. 5A). In contrast, type 2 cytokines including macrophage M2 phenotype marker Fizz1 were generally highly upregulated as compared to blank controls, pointing towards a shift in favor of type 2 cytokine responses (Fig. 5A). All type 1 and 2 cytokines were not differentially expressed between male and female mice (Supplemental Fig. 2).

Immunofluorescence labelling demonstrates that macrophages expressing the mature marker F4/80 were found to be abundantly present in newborn mice lungs at P1, especially near the junction of air sacs (Fig. 5B). Notably, maternal i.p. injection of sterile saline enhanced pulmonary infiltration of macrophages in offspring lungs at P1 (Fig. 5B), with a time-dependent pattern similar as cytokine gene expression profiles. Quantification of F4/80 positive cells out of total counted cells showed statistical significance between the 18.5 dpc exposure group and blank controls (Fig. 5B).

Sca1-expressing mesenchymal progenitor cells are associated with Sftpc expression

Based on our previous scRNA-seq data, Sca1 and glioma-associated oncogene 1 (Gli1) belong to the upstream progenitor markers of lung resident mesenchymal cells (rMCs) [16, 26, 31]. Gene expression of Sca1 was upregulated upon maternal i.p. injection of saline at 15.5 dpc compared to blank controls (Fig. 6A). For both genders, Sca1 expression was significantly decreased in 18.5 dpc versus 15.5 dpc exposure group, with no difference between genders at the same time point (Supplemental Fig. 3). At the protein level, a significant reduction in SCA1 expression was demonstrated in 15.5 dpc exposure group as compared to blank controls (Fig. 6B). The discrepancy between the transcriptional and translational expression of SCA1 may, on the one hand, be explained by a limited sample size. On the other hand, intrinsic temporal differences between mRNA transcription and protein synthesis cannot be fully excluded. Furthermore, SCA1 is known to be involved in lipid rafts [34]. Our previous study demonstrated that, compared with blank controls, maternal i.p. injection of saline significantly reduced perilipin 2 (PLIN2), which regulates lipid storage and metabolism [25]. Therefore, the detected protein level of SCA1 may also be influenced. Sftpc gene expression was more strongly associated with Sca1 than with the other mesenchymal progenitor marker Gli1 (Fig. 4A).

Discussion

In contrast to previous experimental studies investigating potent perinatal insults, we found unexpectedly that one single maternal i.p. injection of sterile physiological saline was able to induce significant changes in the cellular composition and gene expression of the newborn mice lungs. The strength of this prenatal exposure factor is mild as reflected by favorable maternal and neonatal outcome with generally unaffected weight gain of dams after i.p. injection. A dominant pulmonary type 2 anti-inflammatory cytokine response, in turn, corroborated a non-infectious character of this prenatal exposure factor. Our findings also emphasize the potential modulating effect of sham controls compared with non-interventional blank controls in animal experiments.

Pregnancy can be stressed by various factors. Stressors such as physical restraints, social isolation, pain, noise and transportation, has been explored in preclinical animal studies showing significant impact of maternal stress on developing fetal organs [8–10]. Although the underlying pathomechanisms are yet to be elucidated, it has been postulated that physiological, metabolic, immunologic and hormonal pathways may be implicated in stress-mediated fetal organ programming [10–13]. Weight decrease belongs to the most common signs of stress in preclinical studies, and has been used as a sensitive biological variable to evaluate animal stress responses [8, 10]. Our study demonstrated non-significant reduction in maternal weight gain after i.p. injection of sterile physiological saline, suggesting a mild level of stress. Some prenatal stressors, particularly LPS, have been demonstrated in preclinical mice studies to regulate surfactant production, showing either stimulatory or inhibitory effects [35–38]. In this study, prenatal non-infectious stimuli in the form of i.p. injection of sterile physiological saline induced surfactant synthesis, suggesting a potential maturity-promoting effect on the lung.

Lung organogenesis is mediated by intricate interaction between cells in different compartments [16–18]. Consistent with relevant literature [18], our study pointed towards a potential correlation between pulmonary macrophages and lung AT2 epithelial cell development. Furthermore, we showed that even non-infectious mild stressors during pregnancy are capable of inducing macrophage recruitment to the newborn mice lung. In normal conditions, pulmonary macrophages seem to exhibit predominantly M1 pro-inflammatory state at birth and transit to M2 anti-inflammatory state later in life [39, 40]. Contrarily, pulmonary macrophages tend to sustain the M1 pro-inflammatory profile in the context of evolving lung diseases [22, 41]. Results of this study suggest that prenatal non-infectious stressor may exert beneficial effects on the developing lung by shifting the cytokine response of pulmonary macrophages to M2 status. Newborn mice lungs at birth are characterized by beginning alveolarization and a large proportion of mesenchymal compartment, which harbors progenitor cells for the development of lung epithelium [42]. So far, the heterogeneity and dynamics of rMCs during lung homeostasis and diseases have not been clearly defined [42]. Our previous scRNA-seq data indicated that Sca1-expressing rMCs may play a supportive role in AT2 cell proliferation and functional maturation [16, 21], whereas Gli1-positive subpopulation seems to be less relevant for epithelial development [43].

There are heterogeneous data regarding sex-specific pattern in pulmonary gene expression following exposure factors in preclinical mouse models. While some didn’t show difference between genders [22, 44, 45], others reported differential modulatory effects of exposure factors on the lungs of male and female mice [16, 46]. Due to a sophisticatedly regulated temporal pattern of lung organogenesis [42], it is understandable that the fetal programming effect may also follow a time-specific manner. An interesting finding of this study was the opposite regulatory trend of AT1 signature gene expression compared with that of AT2 cells from mid- to late gestation. Based on our previous data revealing lineage-flexibility between AT1 and AT2 progenitors during late pseudoglandular stage (14.5–16.5 dpc in mice) [32], this leads to speculation of potential cross-contribution of AT1 towards AT2 cells upon exposure to non-infectious mild stressor at 15.5 dpc. However, to testify this hypothesis requires lineage-tracing methods, which was beyond the scope of this study due to predefined regulations of ethical approval.

This preclinical study has limitations despite its clinical relevance. First, the end point of experiments was set on P1 without longitudinal analysis. However, our focus was set on early-life respiratory morbidity after delivery, and evidence shows that pulmonary status in the early postnatal period can predict long-term respiratory outcomes [47]. Second, maternal i.p. injection of sterile physiological saline is postulated to be a mild antenatal stressor mainly based on indirect stress indicators (e.g. maternal and neonatal weight). To more accurately define the characteristics of prenatal exposure factors, metabolic, hormonal and physiological variables of pregnant mice should be analyzed in further exposure models. It should be noted that data of this study are based on secondary analyses of blank and sham controls in previous mice experiments exploring prenatal LPS-modulated neonatal lung development [25]. As such, mechanistic analyses in this study were largely restricted to signature markers in each lung compartment. We also acknowledge the limited sample size for the analysis of signature marker expression at both transcriptional and translational levels. Nonetheless, the aim of this exploratory study is to gain a preview of potential cellular and molecular changes in early-life lung development following antenatal stressors of a non-infectious mild nature, which have been seldom investigated so far. In future preclinical studies, extensive transcriptomic profiling and subsequently lineage tracing with transgenic mice are intended for detailed mechanistic understanding. Last but not the least, the extrapolation of preclinical data to clinical context should be cautious, given interspecies differences between mice and humans.

Conclusions

Taken together, results of this study add to current understanding of fetal programming that maternal exposure to even non-infectious mild stressors before birth can modulate early-life lung development. The end effect may be promoted lung AT2 development with enhanced surfactant production, especially when this kind of prenatal exposure occurs in mid- other than in late-gestation.
