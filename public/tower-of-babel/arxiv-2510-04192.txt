Cooperative Flexibility Exchange: Fair and Comfort-Aware Decentralized Resource Allocation
Rabiya Khalid, Evangelos PournarasSchool of Computer Science, University of Leeds, Leeds, United Kingdom, Email: {r.khalid, e.pournaras}@leeds.ac.uk

Abstract
The growing electricity demand and increased use of smart appliances are placing new pressures on power grids, making efficient energy management more important than ever. The existing energy management systems often prioritize system efficiency (balanced energy demand and supply) at the expense of user comfort. This paper addresses this gap by proposing a novel decentralized multi-agent coordination-based demand-side management system. The proposed system enables individual agents to coordinate for demand-side energy optimization while improving the user comfort and maintaining the system efficiency. A key innovation of this work is the introduction of a slot exchange mechanism, where agents first receive optimized appliance-level energy consumption schedules and then coordinate with each other to adjust these schedules through slot exchanges. This approach improves user comfort even when agents show non-altruistic behaviour, and it scales well with large populations. The system also promotes fairness by balancing satisfaction levels across users. For performance evaluation, a real-world dataset is used, and the results demonstrate that the proposed slot exchange mechanism increases user comfort and fairness without raising system inefficiency cost, making it a practical and scalable solution for future smart grids.

I
Introduction

In smart grids, demand-side management (DSM) plays a crucial role in balancing electricity demand with supply, especially given the rising integration of renewable energy sources and the dynamic nature of user behaviour. Residential DSM remains an ongoing challenge, despite extensive research and the development of numerous optimization techniques in recent years. The balance between grid performance and user comfort has received relatively little attention in existing studies
[
1
]
. While much of the literature focuses on shaping user demand to smoothen load profiles and prevent peak consumption, the impact on user comfort is often overlooked or treated as a trade-off. Yet, user comfort is a crucial factor influencing user participation and satisfaction in DSM programs. User comfort is defined as the degree to which an individual’s energy consumption pattern aligns with their personal lifestyle preferences, for example, appliance usage schedules. Enhancing comfort means minimizing the disruption to these preferences while still contributing to overall grid objectives (balanced demand and supply of electricity).

One promising approach to address this challenge is the use of multi-agent systems (MASs), which provide a way to model the decentralized and dynamic nature of residential energy management. In a MAS framework, each user or household can be represented by an autonomous agent capable of making local decisions that align with individual comfort preferences while still responding to system-level objectives. Unlike centralized methods, which may not fully capture the diversity of users’ needs, MASs enable agents to coordinate and communicate, share information, make independent decisions and negotiate adjustments to their energy usage in ways that benefit both the grid (balanced demand and supply) and the community (reduced monetary costs). Agents play an important role in decentralized decision-making and control by collecting and analyzing data, taking the right decision and optimizing the overall performance of the system
[
2
,
3
]
. An agent-based decentralized and coordinated decision-making can improve the system flexibility, scalability, and adaptability to changing conditions.

The benefits of agent coordination have been demonstrated in various domains. For example, in traffic systems

These examples show how local decisions, when aligned through communication and cooperation, can achieve system-wide goals more effectively than isolated actions. Coordination between agents allows multiple agents to work together towards a common goal

. Without coordination, the agents may act independently and potentially interfere with or undermine each other’s efforts. Coordination can help to optimize the overall performance and efficiency of the system, as well as increase its robustness and adaptability to changing conditions. In the literature, multi-agents-based DSM systems are proposed where agents coordinate to optimize energy consumption and reduce costs using decentralized coordination techniques

While agent-based DSM systems have shown considerable potential in optimizing energy consumption patterns and reducing system-level costs, they often neglect two aspects that are fundamental for the long-term sustainability and user acceptance of such programs: comfort and fairness. In most approaches, user comfort is treated merely as a secondary constraint or as a trade-off against system efficiency, rather than being explicitly optimized. To compensate, programs frequently rely on monetary incentives or dynamic pricing to encourage participation. However, financial rewards alone may not ensure lasting engagement, particularly when households experience repeated disruptions to their daily routines—for example, through forced rescheduling of appliance usage or inconvenient load shifting. When such incentives come at the expense of comfort, or are perceived as unfairly distributed, users may become dissatisfied, reduce their willingness to cooperate, or even withdraw from DSM programs altogether. This underscores the need for DSM strategies that go beyond economic incentives and embed comfort as core design principles to secure long-term participation and trust.
Figure 1
illustrates the trade-off between user discomfort cost (x-axis) and system inefficiency cost (y-axis). The curve represents the set of feasible solutions. For example, at point A, both discomfort and inefficiency costs are relatively high. Moving towards point B reduces the discomfort cost but increases the inefficiency cost, highlighting the inherent conflict between the two objectives. Hence, finding an alternative point C, where the inefficiency cost remains unchanged while the discomfort cost is reduced, is a challenge that will be addressed in this paper. In this way, the efficiency of the system is preserved, while user comfort is simultaneously improved.
Furthermore, the issue of fairness in distributed DSM solutions remains largely underexplored
[
14
]
. In this context, unfairness can be defined as the disparity in comfort levels across agents. When agents adjust their local demand to plyport system-level goals, some households may consistently shoulder a disproportionate share of the inconvenience
[
15
]
. Such unequal distribution of comfort loss undermines trust in the system and discourages cooperative behaviour, especially when households differ in their flexibility or willingness to adjust schedules. Therefore, there is a pressing need for DSM mechanisms that explicitly balance system performance with user comfort, while also ensuring that the burden of demand adjustments is distributed equitably among all participants.
Another challenge is the varying degree of altruism or selfishness among agents
[
16
]
. In a practical residential DSM context, each household prioritizes its own comfort and convenience over collective benefits. When agents act selfish, they may refuse to adjust their schedules, potentially jeopardizing the balance between demand and supply. Conversely, overly altruistic behavior can lead to unnecessary comfort sacrifices. Understanding how different levels of agent altruism affect overall system performance, user comfort, and fairness is therefore vital for designing robust and practical DSM mechanisms. To address these gaps, this paper proposes a novel multi-agent-based coordination mechanism that enhances user comfort and fairness while maintaining the fundamental balance between electricity demand and supply. The optimization process is structured in two stages. First, agents collectively coordinate to reach an optimized solution that ensures grid stability while considering their individual comfort preferences: formulation of the problem as a multi-objective optimization that balances local and global goals. Then, in a second stage, agents engage in individual negotiations to further improve their comfort through schedule adjustments, without compromising the system-wide stability already achieved. In the proposed framework, each household is modelled as an autonomous agent with its own comfort preferences, operational flexibility, and degree of altruistic behaviour. Agents negotiate and coordinate with each other to mutually adjust their schedules to better align with their individual comfort requirements while collectively supporting grid stability. The main contributions of this research are as follows.

•

The design of a novel comfort-aware decentralized agent-based DSM mechanism that improves user satisfaction without compromising grid stability.

•

The development of an innovative collaborative slot exchange protocol that enables flexible and mutually beneficial energy schedule adjustments among agents.

•

A comprehensive study of the impact of agent altruism and proposed slot exchange protocol on comfort and fairness in the system.

•

The demonstration of the approach in realistic smart grid scenarios, validating its effectiveness and practical applicability.

•

The release of open-source code and dataset to facilitate reproducibility and support further research in decentralized, comfort-aware DSM.

This work advances the state of the art in DSM research towards more user-centric, equitable, and resilient solutions, paving the way for next-generation smart grids that align technological efficiency with human comfort and social acceptance. Beyond academic contributions, the findings are directly relevant for energy communities, which can adopt the proposed mechanism to foster cooperative and fair energy sharing; for system operators, who can leverage the approach to maintain grid stability while improving user participation; and for policy makers, who can use the insights to design incentive structures and regulatory frameworks that balance efficiency with fairness and consumer well-being.

II-B

Optimizing user comfort

Recent advancements in smart home technologies have emphasized the need for energy-efficient solutions that balance user comfort with minimal energy consumption. Researchers have explored various optimization techniques to achieve this equilibrium, considering factors such as thermal comfort, visual comfort, and indoor air quality (IAQ). Thermal comfort refers to the satisfaction of occupants with the surrounding temperature and humidity conditions, while visual comfort relates to the adequacy and quality of lighting for comfortable and efficient task performance. IAQ reflects the cleanliness and freshness of indoor air, directly influencing both health and overall comfort.

An energy optimization system is proposed for smart homes that maximizes user comfort while minimizing energy consumption
[
26
]
. The system employs Kalman filtering to refine sensor data and compares Genetic Algorithm (GA) and Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO) for optimization. Results indicate that GA outperforms PSO, reducing energy consumption by 31.42% while improving the comfort index by 10%. This work highlights the importance of accurate sensor data and efficient optimization in long-term energy management. Another approach applies a modified Bat Algorithm (BA) with exponential inertia weight to optimize temperature, illumination, and IAQ
[
27
]
. User comfort in multi-occupant environments presents additional challenges due to varying individual preferences. A negotiation-based model for visual comfort leverages soft computing algorithms to accommodate different user profiles and activities
[
28
]
. This study dynamically adjusts lighting based on external and artificial sources, enhancing energy efficiency. Meanwhile, research on thermal comfort employs multi-objective optimization to balance energy costs with occupant satisfaction
[
29
]
. The study examines air speed, humidity, and temperature across varying climates, demonstrating annual energy savings of up to 9.1 kWh/m² while maintaining acceptable comfort levels. These findings underscore the potential of tailored optimization frameworks in reducing operational costs without compromising user well-being.

Advanced AI-driven approaches further enhance smart home energy management. One study integrates Computational Fluid Dynamics (CFD) simulations with a Back-Propagation Neural Network (BPNN) for rapid IAQ and thermal comfort predictions
[
30
]
. When predictions fall short, an Adaptive Multi-Objective PSO-Grey Wolf Optimization (AMOPSO-GWO) algorithm adjusts environmental controls, achieving a 35% energy reduction alongside improved air quality and comfort. This hybrid AI-CFD approach exemplifies the potential of data-driven optimization in real-time smart home systems. Emerging energy solutions, such as green hydrogen, are also being explored for sustainable smart home management. Research on the Enhanced Northern Goshawk Optimization (ENGO) algorithm overcomes limitations of its predecessor by improving exploitation and avoiding local optima
[
31
]
. The study highlights the tradeoff between electricity costs and user comfort, demonstrating the effectiveness of ENGO in reducing peak demand and expenses while maintaining satisfactory comfort levels. The integration of hydrogen-based energy storage with advanced optimization techniques presents a promising direction for future smart grid applications.

The reviewed studies underscore the critical tradeoff between energy efficiency and user comfort in smart home environments, highlighting that comfort is shaped by multiple factors such as thermal conditions, lighting, and air quality. Advanced optimization techniques, including GA, PSO, BA, and hybrid AI models, have proven effective in enhancing comfort by fine-tuning temperature, illumination, and IAQ. However, these methods also reveal a fundamental dilemma: reducing energy costs often necessitates compromises in comfort, particularly regarding delays in using appliances. Despite ongoing technological advances that improve comfort optimization, the inherent tension between energy savings and user convenience persists as a significant hurdle.

Achieving truly sustainable and occupant-friendly smart home ecosystems requires a holistic approach, one that integrates intelligent scheduling and user-centric flexibility. While some DSM strategies address thermal and visual comfort by maintaining acceptable temperature or lighting levels, these efforts typically focus solely on HVAC and lighting systems
[
32
]
. Moreover, cost reductions in electricity consumption frequently come at the expense of user comfort

Nevertheless, when comfort sacrifices become too frequent or incentives are perceived as unfairly distributed, engagement may still decline over time. Multi-objective optimization techniques have been employed to balance comfort, cost savings, and peak-to-average load ratio minimization; however, these approaches often depend on fixed weightings that may not fully capture dynamic user preferences
[
38
]
. In contrast, the cooperative multi-agent algorithm proposed in this paper enables agents to collaborate and coordinate their energy consumption strategies, striving to maximize average comfort levels across the community while maintaining a balanced energy demand and supply. Problem formulation

III-A

Plan generation and representation

Assume that each energy user in the system is assisted by an agent
a
, with a total of
n
agents in the network, such that
A=\{1,2,3,\ldots,n\}

\forall a\in A
. Initially, each user specifies their preferred energy consumption pattern along with a flexibility value. The flexibility value indicates how much deviation from the initial preference is acceptable to reduce the inefficiency cost, for example, by flattening peaks in electricity demand at specific intervals.

This deviation directly impacts the user’s satisfaction and is thus treated as the discomfort in the system. The flexibility value is used to generate a finite set of possible energy consumption plans for each agent. Let
P_{a}=\{\rho_{a,1},\rho_{a,2},\rho_{a,3},\ldots,\rho_{a,k}\}
denote the set of feasible plans for agent
a
, where
k
is the total number of plans generated and
\rho_{a,i}\in P
. Each plan
\rho_{a,i}
is represented as a vector of size
d
, where
d
corresponds to the number of time slots in a day (e.g., if each slot represents one hour, then
d=24
).

Each plan represents a unique pattern of energy consumption over time but results in the same total daily energy use; only the distribution of consumption across time slots differs. Every plan
\rho_{a,i}
has an associated discomfort value that quantifies the deviation from the user’s preferred pattern. For example, if an agent selects a plan that exactly matches the preferred plan (i.e., with zero deviation), the discomfort value is zero, yielding maximum comfort (comfort = 1). In contrast, if the plan deviates by
\pm 1
unit in any time slot, the discomfort value falls between 0 and 1, indicating reduced comfort.

During the energy optimization phase, each agent can select only one plan at a time from its feasible set, referred to as the selected plan
\rho_{a,s}
. The selection of plans across all agents determines the aggregate demand profile and directly influences both inefficiency cost and individual discomfort.

III-B

Discomfort cost

An agent’s preference for each plan is determined by the level of discomfort associated with it. Plans that closely resemble the agent’s preferred energy consumption pattern are assigned higher preference and correspondingly lower discomfort cost
D
. The discomfort cost of the selected plan
\rho_{a,s}
for an agent
a
is determined by

D_{a,s}=f_{D}(\rho_{a,s}).

(1)

III-C

Inefficiency cost

The selected plans of all agents are aggregated to form a global plan
g=\sum_{a=1}^{n}\rho_{a,s}
. The global plan consists of selected plans of all agents, which is the total energy demand of all agents in a community over time. The cost associated with the global plan is represented as

I=f_{I}(\sum_{a=1}^{n}\rho_{a,s}).

(2)

It is also called global cost, i.e., difference between demand and supply of energy.
A global response with lower cost is preferred over the one that has higher cost.

III-D

Unfairness

In a multi-agent energy management system, it is not only important to minimize the inefficiency cost and individual discomfort, but also to ensure fairness among agents. When agents coordinate in an altruistic manner, there is a risk that some agents may consistently sacrifice their comfort or pay higher costs for the benefit of the overall system. This results in an unfair distribution of burden among agents, which should be minimized to maintain trust and participation. The unfairness metric quantifies the disparity among agents by measuring the deviation of each agent’s individual cost from the overall average cost. It is defined as the standard deviation of all agents’ discomfort costs:

U=\sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{a\in A}(D_{a}-\overline{D})^{2}},

(3)

where
U
represents the system-wide unfairness,
D_{a}
is the discomfort cost for agent
a
and
\overline{D}
is the average discomfort cost across all agents. A lower value of
U
indicates a fairer distribution of costs among agents, whereas a higher value implies greater inequality. The goal is to minimize this unfairness metric while achieving an optimal trade-off between inefficiency cost and individual comfort.

III-E

Behavioral modeling of agents for demand-supply balance

In a multi-agent network, an agent’s plan choice is influenced by the selections made by other agents. In such a system, getting the optimal solution means solving an NP-hard optimization problem
[
39
]
. The agents select a plan
\rho_{a,s}
and minimize the discomfort cost while considering the impact of their choice on inefficiency cost. While selecting a plan, an agent chooses the plan that has maximum comfort value. When all agents show similar behavior (selfish behavior), the inefficiency cost increases as energy demand deviates from the available supply. This problem can be solved when agents show altruistic behavior, i.e., select a plan that has low local and inefficiency cost. The choice of agents for being altruistic or selfish can be measured by the
\beta
value. The
\beta
value of an agent varies between 1 and 0, where 1 means selfish behaviour of an agent, and 0 represents altruistic behavior. It implies that there is a trade off between minimizing the discomfort cost and inefficiency cost values that needs to be balanced to get an optimal solution and
\beta
value is used by each agent to assign weight to the discomfort cost. Hence, at the plan selection phase following equation is used
[
40
]
:

\rho_{a,s}=arg\overset{k}{\underset{s=1}{\min}}\Big((1-\beta)\times f_{I}(g)+\beta\times f_{D}(\rho_{a,s})\Big)

(4)

Equation
4
shows that during the agent’s plan selection phase, information about other agents’ selected plans is required, which is not possible without coordination among agents. Making informed decisions is crucial for balancing energy demand and supply because when agents act independently and shift their load to off-peak hours to reduce costs, new peaks in energy demand can emerge.

III-F

Coordinated plan adjustment and discomfort minimization

When agents act altruistically to minimize the inefficiency cost, they do not necessarily select the plan that minimizes their own discomfort. This creates a challenging trade-off because an agent’s preferred plan for maximum comfort may conflict with the system-wide goal of balancing energy demand and supply. Therefore, agents must coordinate and exchange parts of their schedules to improve individual comfort while respecting overall system constraints.

After the plan selection phase, each agent
a\in A
has:

•

A
selected plan

\rho_{a,s}=\{s^{a}_{1},s^{a}_{2},\dots,s^{a}_{d}\}
chosen during plan selection to help minimize inefficiency cost.

•

A
preferred plan

\rho_{a,p}=\{p^{a}_{1},p^{a}_{2},\dots,p^{a}_{d}\}
which yields the lowest possible individual discomfort.

Here,
s^{a}_{i}
denotes the value of the
i
-th slot in the selected plan, while
p^{a}_{i}
represents the value of the
i
-th slot in the preferred plan, where
i
corresponds to any slot in the plan set. There are a total of
d
time slots in each plan of an agent. The value of slot
d
is the energy consumption value.

An agent compares each slot of its selected plan to its preferred plan. If
s^{a}_{i}\neq p^{a}_{i}
, it can request an exchange with another agent who has the desired value. For example, suppose agent
a_{1}
wants to exchange a slot with agent
a_{2}
. The exchange must satisfy the following constraints:

\sum_{a\in A}\sum_{i=1}^{d}s^{a}_{i}=E.

(5)

s^{a_{1}}_{i}+s^{a_{2}}_{j}=s^{a_{1}^{\prime}}_{i}+s^{a_{2}^{\prime}}_{j}.

(6)

Equation
5
, ensures that the total energy consumption
E
in the system remains constant, maintaining balance with the available supply. Whereas, In equation
6
,
s^{a_{1}^{\prime}}_{i}
and
s^{a_{2}^{\prime}}_{j}
are the slot values after exchange. This constraint guarantees that the energy values for the involved slots remain balanced before and after the exchange for both agents.

The goal of coordination is to minimize the average discomfort for all agents:

\min D^{\text{cost}}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{n\times d}\sum_{a\in A}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\Big(s^{a}_{i}-p^{a}_{i}\Big)^{2}}.

(7)

Through such coordinated slot exchanges, agents can approach their preferred comfort levels without violating global energy constraints.

IV
Proposed solution

The system model is illustrated in
Figure 2
. As discussed in the previous section, each energy user has a preferred plan for energy consumption, which is initially defined by the agent on behalf of the user. Next, a plan generator
[
41
]
produces k alternative plans for each agent. The number of generated plans reflects the level of flexibility a user is willing to offer for load management. In this system, 10 alternative plans are generated per agent. To select an optimized solution for energy consumption, the agents employ the Iterative Economic Planning and Optimized Selections (I-EPOS) algorithm. It is suitable for decentralized, privacy-preserving energy optimization. Unlike many distributed algorithms that require extensive data sharing
[
42
,
43
,
44
,
45
]
, I-EPOS ensures that agents do not disclose their complete electricity consumption patterns; instead, only aggregated information is exchanged, thereby minimizing the risk of sensitive data exposure. Furthermore, its tree-based coordination structure reduces communication overhead, making the algorithm both efficient and scalable for large populations of agents. These properties make I-EPOS an effective foundation for balancing local comfort with system-wide objectives in real-world demand-side management scenarios. Using I-EPOS, each agent chooses a plan that balances local comfort and global objectives while coordinating with other agents to reduce overall demand peaks. However, since the initial plans are generated independently by each agent and the optimizer selects plans solely from this finite set, it is possible that the final selection may result in non-optimal comfort levels for some agents, even though the inefficiency cost is minimized. This introduces a potential sub-optimality at the local level: some agents may experience unnecessary discomfort simply due to the combinatorial nature of the optimization space. To address this limitation, the proposed system incorporates a slot exchange mechanism. This mechanism allows agents to negotiate the exchange of specific time slots within their selected plans to reduce their individual discomfort levels, while ensuring that the overall inefficiency cost remains unchanged. In practice, if an agent
a_{1}
has a non-preferred slot in its selected plan, it may request an exchange with agent
a_{2}
that holds the preferred slot of
a_{1}
. If the requested slot is not the preferred slot of agent
a_{2}
, the exchange can take place. Otherwise, the request is declined. This additional coordination step enables the system to further refine the solution space beyond the initial I-EPOS output, improving fairness and individual comfort without violating system constraints or increasing total energy demand. A detailed discussion of the I-EPOS algorithm and the slot exchange mechanism is provided in the following subsections.

IV-A

I-EPOS

I-EPOS is a decentralized, multi-agent optimization mechanism designed to manage large-scale coordination problems, such as energy consumption scheduling in smart cities
[
41
,
46
]
, vehicle route planning
[
47
]
, electric vehicle charging control
[
48
,
49
]
, etc. I-EPOS operates by structuring agents into a tree topology, allowing them to perform fully decentralized and self-organized decision-making. The system functions through iterative collective learning, where agents alternate between bottom-up and top-down phases during optimization. In the bottom-up phase, agents make decisions based on their local preferences and the aggregated preferences of agents below them in the tree. In the top-down phase, agents adjust their choices based on the decisions propagated from agents higher in the tree, enabling them to balance discomfort with system inefficiency. This structure minimizes communication costs and ensures efficient coordination of decisions, as each agent processes inputs in a way that guarantees contributions from the same agents are not added more than once. I-EPOS is particularly well-suited for energy consumption optimization because of its ability to solve decentralized optimization problems in a privacy-preserving and scalable manner
[
41
]
. In this paper, I-EPOS is used to optimize the energy consumption patterns of agents because of its proven efficacy in decentralized environments where multiple agents achieve collective optimization without having to communicate directly with every other agent. Its ability to reduce peak energy demands while preserving user privacy, as well as its low communication overhead due to the tree topology, makes it an ideal choice for balancing local and inefficiency costs in complex energy systems. Additionally, the iterative learning process of I-EPOS allows for progressive improvement of solutions, making it highly effective for real-time optimization scenarios.

IV-B

Slot exchange method

To implement the slot exchange process, a blackboard agent is introduced that acts as an information-sharing facilitator among agents. After all agents select their plans using the I-EPOS algorithm, they share the information of selected plans with the blackboard agent. In the next step, each agent compares its selected plan,
\rho_{a,s}
, with its preferred plan,
\rho_{a,p}
. Here, a slot refers to a discrete time interval within the scheduling horizon (as discussed in Section
III-F
). If an agent identifies a mismatch between the time slot assigned in its selected plan and the one in its preferred plan, it may seek to exchange that slot with another agent. Instead of each agent directly sending multiple messages to all other agents, which can create excessive communication overhead and increase the risk of conflicting exchange attempts, agents query the blackboard agent to learn which other agent currently holds the slot value they desire. The blackboard agent provides the necessary information to help agents identify potential exchange partners. All decisions regarding whether to initiate or accept a slot exchange are controlled by the individual agents as they communicate in a peer-to-peer manner. Multi-Agent Energy Optimization with Slot Exchange

When an agent wishes to exchange a slot, it submits a request specifying the time slot index and the preferred energy value it wants to achieve. The blackboard agent runs a simple matching algorithm: it scans the relevant slot values held by other agents and identifies which agent’s slot matches the requested preferred value. The blackboard agent then shares this information with the requesting agent, which can decide whether to send an exchange proposal. Once an exchange partner is identified, the agents communicate via the blackboard agent to confirm and update their plans. To prevent conflicts, both agents are temporarily marked as unavailable for other exchanges during this transaction. After the slot exchange is finalized, their availability status is reset, allowing them to participate in further exchanges if needed.

This approach ensures an efficient and fair slot exchange mechanism while avoiding the drawbacks of direct peer-to-peer negotiation. It reduces redundant communication, prevents coordination conflicts, and protects sensitive preference information. Moreover, since the blackboard agent only provides information and does not dictate the final exchange decisions, the system remains decentralized and respects the autonomy of all agents. An agent can still refuse a slot exchange request if the requested slot is its own preferred slot, maintaining local comfort priorities. Moreover, queuing is not implemented in this process. In the case of a successful slot exchange, the values of both participating agents are updated, meaning that any agent waiting in a queue would now be attempting to match against outdated values. As a result, the queued request would no longer be valid. Conversely, in the case of an unsuccessful exchange, the request-denying agent retains its preferred slot because keeping queued requests is meaningless.

Simulation environment

For each agent, k=10 feasible energy plans are generated. To evaluate the proposed multi-agent coordination mechanism, a series of simulations is conducted using this dataset. V-B

Effect of behavioral parameter
\beta
and slot exchange

As described in the previous section, the agents first use the I-EPOS algorithm to select locally optimal plans from their available options. After this initial decentralized optimization, agents further improve their comfort by engaging in a slot exchange mechanism that enables them to swap individual time slots while maintaining the overall system efficiency.

In I-EPOS, agents are organized hierarchically in a binary tree with nine levels (L1 to L9). Due to the nature of the binary structure, the majority of agents are located at the lowest level (L9). The system uses the weighting factor
\beta
to adjust the balance between selfish (local) and cooperative (global) objectives. Higher
\beta
values encourage agents to prioritize their individual comfort, while lower values promote collective behavior and better inefficiency cost optimization.

Agents nearer the root (L1) are shown with darker hues and characteristic markers, whereas lower-level agents (L9) are represented with progressively lighter tones and distinct marker shapes.

When
\beta

This leaves greater room for agents to improve their comfort through slot exchange, resulting in the highest overall comfort gain and the widest participation across all levels. In this scenario, slot exchanges occur at all levels, from L1 to L9, leading to a more equitable distribution of comfort. As

This reduces the need and opportunity for slot exchange because agents already receive their desired slots (playing little or no role in reducing inefficiency cost). \beta

The slot exchange mechanism enables agents to collaboratively fine-tune their schedules after the primary I-EPOS phase, improving comfort without increasing the inefficiency cost. The mechanism is most effective when agents are willing to prioritize system-wide goals (low
\beta

Comfort gains equal to zero indicate agents who did not participate in the slot exchange phase because their initial plans already matched their preferences, underscoring how agent behavior shifts with the trade-off between local and global objectives. VI
Conclusion and Future work

This paper presents a decentralized energy management approach that integrates a slot exchange mechanism to enhance individual comfort without compromising system-level efficiency. The results show that while agents act more selfishly, achieving their preferred slots and reducing the need for further optimization, the proposed slot exchange mechanism remains crucial. It consistently improves overall comfort, even in less cooperative settings, by enabling agents to engage in mutually beneficial slot exchanges. Notably, the mechanism achieves this without increasing the inefficiency cost, making it an effective tool for sustainable energy management.

Moreover, the results highlight that fairness in comfort distribution improves through the slot exchange process, especially when initial allocations are suboptimal. As population size increases, the system exhibits higher comfort gains due to more opportunities for matching agents with complementary needs. However, the relationship is not strictly linear, as some newly added agents may not always find suitable exchange partners, underscoring the dynamic nature of decentralized coordination.

The key takeaway is that the proposed slot exchange mechanism enhances comfort in a scalable, adaptive, and cost-neutral manner. It provides a robust foundation for real-world applications in utility-led DSM programs and energy community platforms. By embedding this mechanism into smart grid operations, energy providers and communities can optimize scheduling decisions, reduce user dissatisfaction, and promote fairer outcomes across diverse population sizes and behavioral profiles.

In future work, we plan to utilize appliance-level datasets to achieve finer-grained energy optimization.. Additionally, instead of assigning a fixed behavioral parameter (
\beta
) to all agents, we will implement a learning-based coordination approach. Agents will dynamically adapt their behavior by learning from their neighbors, allowing them to intelligently balance personal comfort with system-wide efficiency over time.
