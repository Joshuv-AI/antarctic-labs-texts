Social isolation and lung function in middle-aged and older adults: identifying modifiable pathways across two populations
Authors: Yihui Du, Xiaoqing Ni, Zhaoyang Zhao, Chengmin Yuan, Xiaojiang Ran, Xiaonan Cui, Haiyan Ma, Lilu Ding
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLimited evidence exists on the relationship between social isolation and lung function, with few studies exploring underlying mechanisms. This study examined the association between social isolation and lung function decline, and the mediating roles of smoking, income, physical activity, and BMI in U.S. and Chinese populations.MethodsThis study utilized data from adults aged ≥ 45 years in NHANES (2007–2012, cross-sectional) and CHARLS (2011 and 2015, longitudinal). Lung function metrics from NHANES included Forced Vital Capacity (FVC), Forced Expiratory Volume in 1 s (FEV1), and Peak Expiratory Flow (PEF), while CHARLS provided PEF data. Social isolation was assessed using marital status, living alone, and social participation in both studies, with NHANES including daily life difficulties and CHARLS incorporating infrequent child contact. Weighted linear regression models were used to assess associations. A parallel mediation model was constructed using structural equation modeling, and statistical analysis was performed using the “lavaan” package in R version 4.3.2. Standardized coefficients with p-values and mediation proportions are presented.ResultsA total of 3,575 individuals from NHANES (mean age 63.49 ± 8.27) and 3,752 from CHARLS (mean age 61.00 ± 9.23) were included. Social isolation was significantly associated with lower FVC, FEV1, and PEF in both datasets. In NHANES, mediation analysis revealed significant total indirect effects for: FVC (-0.0628, p < 0.001), 67.0% mediated by smoking, income, activity, and BMI; FEV1 (-0.0666, p < 0.001), 79.3% mediated by the same factors; and PEF (-0.0643, p < 0.001), 49.1% mediated by smoking, income, and activity. Across all three lung function metrics, income contributed the largest proportion of the mediated effect, accounting for 35.32% of FVC, 37.26% of FEV1, and 26.28% of PEF. In CHARLS, only BMI had a significant indirect effect on PEF (-0.0088, p = 0.004), mediating 6.37% of the association.ConclusionsSocial isolation is associated with poorer lung function in both U.S. and Chinese populations aged ≥ 45 years, with distinct mediating pathways. In the U.S., income, smoking, and physical activity play significant roles, while in China, BMI is the primary mediator. Introduction

Impaired lung function remains a pressing global public health issue [1]. Recent estimates show that 10.3% of adults aged more than 30 years worldwide have spirometry-defined COPD, with standardized prevalence slightly higher in low- and middle-income countries like China (10.3%) compared to high-income countries like the US (10.1%) [2]. Poor lung function not only indicates respiratory diseases but also predicts cardiovascular problems, reduced quality of life, and higher mortality risk [3, 4]. Since lung function decline often begins years before symptoms appear, identifying modifiable risk factors is crucial for early intervention [5].

Social isolation has emerged as an important factor affecting lung health. About 30% of older adults in the Americas experience social isolation, with lower but still significant rates in Asia-Pacific regions (22%) [6]. Research shows socially isolated people tend to have faster lung function decline, even after accounting for smoking [7]. This may occur through chronic stress-induced dysregulation of the HPA axis and systemic inflammation [8–10]. However, we still don’t fully understand how social isolation connects to lung function through various behavioral and socioeconomic factors.

The scientific literature has well-characterized several independent predictors of lung function decline, including tobacco use, physical inactivity, elevated BMI, and socioeconomic disadvantage [11–21]. Smoking demonstrates a clear dose-dependent relationship with FEV1 decline [11–13], while sedentary behavior and obesity contribute to restrictive ventilatory patterns through distinct physiological mechanisms [14–18]. Socioeconomic disparities further compound these risks through differential environmental exposures and healthcare access [19–21]. Crucially, these factors rarely operate in isolation; social isolation exhibits consistent associations with each mediator, potentially creating synergistic effects on lung health. Isolated individuals demonstrate higher smoking rates, likely due to stress coping mechanisms and reduced cessation support [22, 23], while also facing greater barriers to physical activity and healthy weight maintenance [24]. The socioeconomic consequences of isolation—including limited educational attainment and employment opportunities [25]—may further exacerbate these risks through material deprivation and reduced health literacy [26, 27].

This study proposes a multiple mediation model to elucidate how these interconnected factors collectively explain the association between social isolation and lung function decline (Fig. 1, Hypothetical Model). By simultaneously examining smoking, income, physical activity, and BMI as potential mediators, our research aims to provide a comprehensive understanding of these pathways in the US and Chinese populations. The findings will advance theoretical frameworks linking social determinants to physiological outcomes while informing targeted interventions to preserve lung function in diverse settings.

Materials and methods

Data source and study population

We utilized the data of two nationally representative surveys: the cross-sectional NHANES from the U.S. and the longitudinal CHARLS from China. NHANES [28], conducted by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, employs a complex, stratified sampling design to represent the non-institutionalized U.S. population and collects health and nutrition data biennially. We analyzed NHANES data from three consecutive two-year cycles (2007–2012) due to the availability of social isolation and lung function data. CHARLS [29] is an ongoing national survey that provides comprehensive data on aging for middle-aged and older populations in China, which included participants from 450 villages and urban communities across 28 provinces. In the CHARLS database, we used data from the 2011 baseline and the 2015 follow-up for a longitudinal analysis. Ethics approval for NHANES was granted by the National Center for Health Statistics, with written informed consent obtained from all participants. Similarly, CHARLS received ethics approval from the Biomedical Ethics Review Committee of Peking University (IRB00001052–11015), and informed consent was required from all interviewees.

For this study (cross-sectional design for NHANES and cohort design for CHARLS), we focused exclusively on adults aged ≥ 45 years to ensure population comparability. Participants were excluded if they aged < 45 years, or lacked lung function measurements, data on social isolation or mediators. Additionally, individuals with low quality of lung function data (grades C/D/F) were excluded. A detailed flowchart of participant selection for NHANES and CHARLS is provided in Fig. 2.

Lung function parameters assessment

Lung function was assessed using three key spirometry measurements: Forced Vital Capacity (FVC, ml), Forced Expiratory Volume in 1 s (FEV1, ml), and Peak Expiratory Flow (PEF, ml/s). FVC represents the total volume of air a person can forcibly exhale after a maximal inhalation, reflecting overall lung capacity. FEV1 measures the volume of air exhaled during the first second of a forced exhalation, indicating the rate of airflow and the presence of airway obstruction. PEF is the maximum flow rate achieved during a forced exhalation, primarily reflecting the patency of large airways. These measurements are essential for evaluating respiratory health and detecting pulmonary abnormalities [30]. The ratio of FEV1 to FVC (FEV1/FVC) was also calculated, with values < 70% indicating irreversible airflow limitation.

In NHANES, the three measurements were assessed using an Ohio 822/827 dry-rolling seal spirometer. Tests were conducted in accordance with the American Thoracic Society (ATS) guidelines [31], with participants standing and repeating the spirometry until acceptable and repeatable results were obtained, and detailed methods have been described elsewhere [32]. The FVC and FEV1 readings were graded from A to F based on the ATS criteria. To ensure the accuracy of measurement data, only data with quality grades A and B were included in the analysis [33].

In CHARLS, only PEF was collected in 2015. Trained technicians measured PEF with a peak flow meter (Everpure Medical Plastic Co, Shanghai, China) and a disposable mouthpiece. Participants stood, took a deep breath, and blew into the mouthpiece as hard and fast as possible. This process was repeated three times, and the highest value was recorded.

Social isolation

In the NHANES (2007–2012), we utilized a social isolation index developed by Saif et al. [34], which was derived from established, validated social isolation measures encompassing multiple domains [35–37]. The index generates a score from 0 to 4 (higher scores indicate greater isolation) based on four components: marital status (1 point for widowed, divorced, separated, or never married), living alone (1 point for household size of one), and two items assessed the functional limitations related to social participation from the Physical Functioning Questionnaire on difficulties in “going out to things like shopping, movies or sporting events” and “participating in social activities [visiting friends, attending clubs or meetings or going to parties]” (1 point for each if there is “some difficulty”, “much difficulty”, or “unable to do”).

In the CHARLS 2011 baseline survey, social isolation was measured using an index based on social networks and engagement according to previous research [38]. This index also scores from 0 to 4, with one point assigned for each of the following: living alone, being currently unmarried (separated, divorced, widowed, or never married), contacting children in person or by phone/email less than once a week, and participating in social activities less than once a month. Social activities included interacting with friends, playing games (Mahjong, chess, or cards, or going to community club), attending sport, social, or other kind of club, taking part in a community-related organization, volunteering, and taking part in educational or training activities. Although this index has not undergone formal validation in Chinese population, studies have demonstrated its association with adverse health outcomes [38–40].

In this study, we categorized participants as non-isolated (score < 2) and isolated (score ≥ 2), following a classification method derived from a study published in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, which defines social isolation as scoring within the top quintile [41]. In our datasets, a score of ≥ 2 corresponded to approximately 20–25% of the population in both NHANES and CHARLS, aligning with this quintile-based criterion.

Mediating variables

Lifetime smoking index

Following the method outlined by Leffondré et al. [42] and Wootton et al. [43], we combined the smoking measures into a lifetime smoking index, an aggregate measure incorporating the most relevant aspects of smoking history—smoking status, intensity, duration, and time since cessation. This index was previously demonstrated to provide a good fit to the data while maintaining a parsimonious representation of lifetime smoking history, in contrast to multivariable modeling of separate effects of several dimensions of smoking behavior [43].

Income

In the NHANES dataset, income status was assessed using the Poverty Income Ratio (PIR), a metric calculated by dividing total family income by the corresponding poverty threshold. A higher PIR indicates better economic conditions. In the CHARLS dataset, annual income, encompassing earnings from work, pensions, and subsidies, was used. Respondent and spousal income was categorized into four groups: no income, < 10,000 yuan, 10,000–30,000 yuan, and > 30,000 yuan.

Physical activity

Physical activity data in NHANES were collected using the Global Physical Activity Questionnaire, developed by the World Health Organization (WHO), which assesses physical activity across different domains, including leisure-time, occupation, and transportation [28, 44]. Participants were classified based on whether they met the American physical activity guidelines: sedentary (0 min/week), somewhat active (>0 min but < 75 min/week of vigorous or < 150 min/week of moderate activity), and active (≥ 75 min/week of vigorous or ≥ 150 min/week of moderate activity) [45]. However, among participants in the CHARLS 2011 dataset, 76% of individuals lacked physical activity information, leading to the exclusion of this variable from the CHARLS data analysis.

BMI

Height was measured using a stadiometer, and weight was measured using a digital scale following standard procedures. BMI was calculated using weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared.

Other covariates

Based on prior research, other covariates included: sex (male, female), age (years), race and ethnicity (for NHANES: Mexican American, other Hispanic, non-Hispanic White, non-Hispanic Black, others; CHARLS: no data available), education level (< high school, high school, >high school), alcohol consumption (non-drinker, drinker), presence of depressive symptoms (no, yes), health insurance status (non-insured, insured), presence of respiratory diseases (no, yes), and medication use (no, yes). For alcohol consumption, in NHANES, drinkers were defined as individuals who reported consuming at least 12 alcoholic drinks within the past year, with a standard drink being 12 oz. of beer, 5 oz. of wine, or 1.5 oz. of liquor; in CHARLS, drinkers were defined as those who responded “yes” to the question, “Did you drink any alcoholic beverages, such as beer, wine, or liquor in the past year?” and indicated they drank more than monthly in the last year. Depressive symptoms were assessed using the PHQ-9 questionnaire in NHANES and the 10-item short form of the CESD10 in CHARLS, with a score ≥ 10 indicating the presence of depressive symptoms in both datasets. Medication use was defined as affirmative response to using prescribed medications (excluding vitamins/minerals) in the past month in NHANES, current use of Western or Chinese traditional medicines in CHARLS.

Statistical analysis

The sample analysis utilised combined cycle weights in NHANES and individual weight in CHARLS considering the complexity of the sampling approach for both studies. Continuous variables were presented as weighted means ± standard deviation, and categorical variables as weighed percentages. Differences in baseline characteristics between socially isolated and non-isolated participants were assessed using χ2 tests or independent sample t-tests. Three weighted linear regression models (unadjusted, partially adjusted, and fully adjusted) were built to evaluate the relationship between social isolation and lung function measurements. All analyses were conducted using SPSS 26.0. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05. Multiple imputation was performed for participants with missing covariate(s) in CHARLS. NHANES provided complete information on covariates thus no imputation was conducted.

To investigate the mediating role of lifetime smoking index, income, physical activity, and BMI in the relationship between social isolation and lung function, we conducted a parallel mediation analysis using structural equation modeling (SEM). We constructed an SEM where social isolation was the independent variable, lung function (FVC, FEV1, PEF) was the dependent variable, and the four mediators were specified as parallel pathways within a single model. As depicted in Fig. 1, the independent variable X (social isolation) had a direct effect (c’) on the dependent variable Y (FVC, FEV1, or PEF) and indirect effects on Y through four mediators: M1 (smoking), M2 (income), M3 (physical activity), and M4 (BMI). In Fig. 1, a1 represents the effect of social isolation on smoking, b1 represents the effect of smoking on FVC/FEV1/PEF, and the specific indirect effect of social isolation on FVC/FEV1/PEF through smoking is estimated as a1*b1, and so forth for the other mediators. The total indirect effect of social isolation on FVC/FEV1/PEF through the four mediators is the sum of the four specific indirect effects. In Fig. 1, c represents the total effect (direct effect + indirect effects) of social isolation on FVC/FEV1/PEF, which was calculated using the formula c = c’ + a1*b1 + a2*b2 + a3*b3 + a4*b4. We included sex, age (years), race and ethnicity, education level, presence of depressive symptoms, health insurance status, presence of respiratory diseases, and alcohol consumption behavior as covariates in the model to control for potential confounding effects. We constructed the model using the “lavaan” package in R 4.3.2 and tested the parallel mediation model using the bootstrap method. All results in the model are reported as standardized coefficients. The mediation proportion of each path was calculated by dividing the specific path’s effect by the total effect.

Sensitivity analysis with complete-case analysis CHARLS dataset was conducted to evaluate the impact of missing covariates data.

Results

Characteristics of the participants

A total of 3,575 individuals from NHANES and 3,752 from CHARLS were included. Of these, 24.2% and 23.1%, respectively, reported experiencing social isolation. In NHANES, socially isolated participants were more likely to be female, of other Hispanic ethnicities, less educated, smokers, have a higher BMI, lower income, lower physical activity levels, exhibit depressive symptoms, and be uninsured. Conversely, in CHARLS, socially isolated individuals were more likely to be female, older, less educated, non-smokers, have a lower BMI, lower income, exhibit depressive symptoms, consume less alcohol, be uninsured, and have medication use. Lung function parameters (FVC, FEV1, and PEF in NHANES; PEF in CHARLS) were significantly lower among socially isolated participants compared to their non-isolated counterparts (Table 1). The characteristics of included versus excluded participants are presented in Table S1. Overall, the two groups demonstrated comparable profiles, with two exceptions observed in NHANES: included participants were older and showed higher prevalence of medication use.

Associations between social isolation and lung function

The association between social isolation and lung function with univariable and multivariable linear regression analyses were shown in Table 2. In NHANES, social isolation was significantly associated with reduced FVC, FEV1, and PEF in both unadjusted and minimally adjusted models. However, in fully adjusted models, the associations with FVC and FEV1 were no longer significant, while the negative association with PEF remained robust across all models. No significant association was observed between social isolation and the FEV1/FVC ratio in any model. In CHARLS, where only PEF was assessed, social isolation was consistently associated with lower PEF across all models, including the fully adjusted model.

FVC

Social isolation had a significant total effect on FVC (c = − 0.0937, P = 0.009). All four mediators—PIR (β=−0.0331, 35.32%), physical activity (β=−0.0078, 8.32%), BMI (β=−0.0147, 15.68%), and smoking (β=−0.0071, 7.58%)—exhibited significant indirect effects. Collectively, they mediated 67.0% of the total effect. Notably, all pathways demonstrated negative associations, suggesting social isolation reduces FVC through multiple modifiable risk factors.

FEV1

Social isolation had a significant total effect on FEV₁ (c = − 0.0840, P = 0.003). Four mediators—smoking (β=−0.0204, 24.28%), PIR (β=−0.0313, 37.26%), physical activity (β=−0.0069, 8.21%), and BMI ((β=−0.0080, 9.52%) —accounted for 79.3% of the total effect.

PEF

In NHANES, social isolation was significantly associated with PEF decline (c = − 0.1309, P < 0.001), with PIR (β=−0.0344, 26.28%), smoking (β=−0.0221, 16.88%), and physical activity (β=−0.0078, 5.96%) mediating 49.1% of the total effect. BMI was not a significant mediator in this population. However, in the CHARLS dataset, BMI emerged as a significant mediator (β=−0.0088, 6.37%), and this observation persisted in the sensitivity analysis using complete-case data from the CHARLS (c = − 0.0106, 8.7%) (Figure S1).

Discussion

This study investigates the association between social isolation and lung function using data from two populations: NHANES (U.S.) and CHARLS (China). Our findings consistently demonstrate that social isolation is linked to poorer lung function, although the mediating pathways differ between populations. In NHANES, social isolation correlated with lower FVC, FEV1, and PEF, though the associations with FVC and FEV1 were attenuated after full adjustment. In CHARLS, social isolation was strongly associated with reduced PEF, highlighting the robustness of this relationship across different contexts. Mediation analysis revealed that socioeconomic and behavioral factors—namely income, smoking, physical activity, and BMI—partially explained these associations, offering new insights into the mechanisms at play.

Parallel mediation analysis revealed that income (PIR in NHANES), smoking, physical activity, and BMI partially mediated the association between social isolation and lung function in NHANES. Specifically, these factors collectively accounted for 67% of the effect on FVC, 79% on FEV1, and 49% on PEF. Among the mediators, PIR contributed the largest proportion of the mediation effect for all three lung function parameters in US population. Individuals experiencing social isolation often receive less social support, including financial assistance, and face limited employment opportunities, which may exacerbate their economic burden [26]. This burden not only intensifies the degree of social isolation but may also further impair lung function, creating a vicious cycle that harms overall health [20]. These findings support the role of socioeconomic and behavioral factors in linking social isolation to lung function. The remaining unexplained variance suggests potential direct effects of social isolation on respiratory physiology through stress-related pathways, such as systemic inflammation or HPA axis dysregulation [8–10].

Variations in mediator effects across lung function parameters offer further insight. In the U.S. population, income and physical activity exhibited comparable mediating effects on FVC and FEV1, while smoking and BMI demonstrated distinct influences. Specifically, smoking exerted a stronger mediating effect on FEV1 than FVC, reflecting its impact on airway obstruction and alveolar function [13]. Conversely, BMI predominantly mediated FVC, consistent with its restrictive effects on lung capacity [17].

The consistent association between social isolation and PEF across both NHANES and CHARLS aligns with prior research [7], yet the distinct mediating pathways highlight the influence of cultural and societal contexts. In NHANES, smoking, income, and physical activity mediated the relationship, whereas in CHARLS, only BMI was a significant mediator. This contrast may reflect differences in smoking culture, body weight norms, and socioeconomic disparities. In NHANES, socially isolated individuals exhibited higher smoking rates and BMI, reinforcing these factors as mediators. Conversely, in CHARLS, isolated individuals had lower smoking prevalence and BMI, with BMI emerging as the primary mediator. Studies suggest that social isolation in Western populations is more likely to lead to smoking than in Asian populations [46]. This may be attributed to China’s smoking culture, where smoking is more prevalent, and sometimes perceived as a social activity, potentially leading to higher smoking rates among those with larger social networks [47, 48]. In CHARLS, cultural emphasis on thinness and potential stigma associated with overweight/obesity may lead to lower BMI among socially isolated individuals [49], possibly through reduced food intake [50]. Furthermore, lower BMI in China may reflect inadequate nutrition or frailty, contributing to reduced PEF [51, 52]. This contrasts with the U.S., where higher BMI is prevalent, and social isolation manifests in reduced physical activity or increased smoking, diluting the direct mediating effect of BMI on PEF. The stronger mediating effect of income in the U.S. compared to China may reflect greater income inequality [53, 54], which is further exacerbated among socially isolated individuals in the U.S.

These cross-cultural differences underscore the necessity for tailored public health strategies. In the U.S., interventions should prioritize smoking cessation campaigns and address income-related health disparities. This could involve allocating increased financial resources to provide free smoking cessation services, including nicotine replacement therapy and psychological counseling, for socially isolated individuals [55]. Additionally, policies should integrate socioeconomic factors into healthcare resource allocation and expand Medicaid coverage to mitigate the health consequences of income inequality [56]. In China, interventions should focus on promoting healthy weight perception among socially isolated individuals. However, given that the direct effect of social isolation on PEF significantly outweighs the indirect effect mediated by BMI, policies directly addressing social isolation are likely to yield greater health benefits.

This study benefits from the use of two large, nationally representative cohorts, enabling robust cross-cultural comparisons and the identification of distinct mediating pathways. The utilization of multiple lung function parameters further enhances the depth of the analysis, providing a comprehensive understanding of social isolation’s influence on respiratory health. However, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional analysis of NHANES precludes causal inferences, raising the possibility of reverse causation, where poor lung function may lead to social isolation due to mobility limitations. While longitudinal data from CHARLS validated PEF findings, this remains a consideration. Second, differences in social isolation measurement between NHANES and CHARLS, along with the absence of race and ethnicity, and physical activity data in CHARLS, may limit the comparability of results and the scope of analysis for the Chinese cohort. Third, reliance on self-reported lifestyle factors, such as smoking, income, and physical activity, introduces the potential for bias. For example, social desirability bias may lead to underreporting of tobacco use, which could attenuate the observed associations with lung function. Fourth, the observed partial mediation suggests that other unmeasured factors, such as environmental influences (e.g., air pollution), may contribute to the association between social isolation and lung function. Fifth, despite largely comparable measurement dimensions, minor discrepancies in social isolation assessment between the two populations necessitate cautious interpretation of cross-cultural comparisons. Sixth, the parallel mediation model employed could not assess potential interaction effects among mediators, such as the interplay between reduced physical activity and elevated BMI. Last, selection bias may affect NHANES results as included participants were older and used more medications than excluded individuals, potentially influencing the observed associations.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates a consistent association between social isolation and poorer lung function among adults aged ≥ 45 years across U.S. and Chinese populations, albeit through distinct mediating pathways. In the U.S., socioeconomic and behavioral factors, particularly income, smoking, and physical activity, play significant roles, while in China, BMI emerges as the primary mediator. These cross-cultural differences highlight the importance of considering societal and cultural contexts when addressing the impact of social isolation on respiratory health. Tailored public health interventions, focusing on smoking cessation and income disparities in the U.S., and promoting healthy weight perception and directly addressing social isolation in China, are essential to mitigate the adverse effects of social isolation on lung function.
