Maternal eating disorders and respiratory outcomes in childhood: findings from the EU Child Cohort Network
Authors: Maja Popovic, Milena Maule, Chiara Moccia, Elena Isaevska, Demetris Avraam, Tim Cadman, Ahmed Elhakeem, Veit Grote, Kathrin Guerlich, Sido Haakma, Jennifer Ruth Harris, Pauline W Jansen, Johanna Lucia Thorbjørnsrud Nader, Angela Pinot de Moira, Katrine Strandberg-Larsen, Morris Swertz, Marieke Welten, Tiffany Yang, Vincent Jaddoe, Liesbeth Duijts, Lorenzo Richiardi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWhile maternal depression and anxiety have been linked to adverse childhood respiratory outcomes, the role of eating disorders (EDs) remains less understood. This study examined associations between maternal EDs and offspring respiratory outcomes, considering ED subtypes, exposure windows and comorbid depression/anxiety.MethodsData from 131 495 mother–child pairs across seven cohorts from the EU Child Cohort Network were analysed. Primary analyses assessed associations between maternal pre-pregnancy EDs and preschool wheezing and school-age asthma. Secondary analyses explored associations in women without comorbid depression/anxiety, specific ED subtypes (anorexia nervosa, bulimia nervosa), exposure periods (pregnancy, post-pregnancy) and — within two cohorts — school-age lung function. Logistic regression models were fitted for each cohort, and results pooled using random-effects meta-analysis.ResultsMaternal pre-pregnancy ED prevalence ranged from 0.8% (health records) to 17.0% (self-reported lifetime EDs). Preschool wheezing prevalence ranged from 20.7% to 49.6%, and school-age asthma from 2.1% to 17.3%. Pre-pregnancy EDs were associated with preschool wheezing (OR: 1.25, 95% CI: 1.06 to 1.47, I2: 74%) and school-age asthma (OR: 1.26, 95% CI: 1.10 to 1.46, I2: 9%). These estimates were slightly attenuated but remained directionally consistent with the main analyses after exclusion of mothers with depression/anxiety. There was evidence of a weak positive association with lung function. Associations across ED subtypes were largely consistent with the pre-pregnancy any ED estimate, while no clear pattern emerged by timing of exposure.ConclusionsMaternal EDs are associated with a higher risk of wheezing and asthma in children, independently of comorbid depression/anxiety. These findings highlight the need to understand mechanisms and long-term respiratory consequences of maternal EDs to inform interventions for improving offspring respiratory health.Keywords: Asthma, Asthma Epidemiology, Child, Paediatric asthma

Background

Maternal perinatal mental disorders have been reported to increase the risk of childhood wheezing, asthma and reduced lung function,1 2 with some studies suggesting no evidence of residual confounding by unmeasured genetic and environmental factors.3 4 To date, research on the effects of maternal mental health on offspring respiratory outcomes has focused predominantly on depression, anxiety and broadly defined stress, with limited evidence on less frequent conditions like eating disorders (EDs).5 A study conducted within the Italian NINFEA (Nascita e INFanzia: gli Effetti dell'Ambiente) birth cohort found that children born to mothers with EDs were at an increased risk of developing infant wheezing, with particularly strong associations when EDs were manifested during pregnancy.5 To our knowledge, these findings were not replicated in independent populations and no studies have investigated other clinically relevant respiratory outcomes, or the persistence of this association beyond infancy.

EDs are most prevalent among adolescent and early adulthood women, with anorexia nervosa (AN), bulimia nervosa (BN) and binge eating disorders (BED) being the most common subtypes. These conditions are often characterised by overlapping symptoms and long-lasting physical, behavioural and psychological consequences.6 Depression and anxiety are also common among patients with EDs, potentially sharing at least some aetiological factors.7 Consequences in children of mothers with EDs have been consistently reported in cognitive, social, emotional, behavioural and eating domains,8 while the evidence is less consistent for offspring physical health outcomes.5 9

The low prevalence of EDs10 makes analysis of individual cohorts challenging due to low statistical power to detect effects. To overcome this, we used the EU Child Cohort Network (EUCCN),11 which offers harmonised measures across multiple cohorts, ensures flexibility in statistical analysis compared with meta-analyses of published studies, and reduces the risk of bias from selective reporting in published studies. The aim of the study was to examine the associations of maternal EDs with childhood wheezing and asthma using a multi-cohort analysis. In a subset of the cohorts, where relevant data were available, we also explored the role of maternal psychiatric comorbidities, tested for differential effects of ED subtypes, timing of the exposure, and the associations of maternal EDs with childhood lung function.

Methods

Study design

The EUCCN11 is a collaboration of pregnancy and birth cohorts, including more than 250 000 children, that established a FAIR (findable, accessible, interoperable and reusable) data resource with harmonised variables necessary to perform multi-cohort analyses.11 12 Cohorts with data on maternal EDs (any exposure timing and any ED subtype) and childhood wheezing and/or asthma (0–14 years of age) were eligible for inclusion. One of the cohorts (GECKO - Groningen Expert Center for Kids with Obesity Drenthe cohort) with available exposure and outcome data but having fewer than three asthma/wheezing cases among the exposed/unexposed to EDs was excluded from the analysis (figure 1). The following seven cohorts were included in the study: Avon Longitudinal Study of Parents and Children (ALSPAC, UK),13 Born in Bradford (BiB, UK; 18% of the original cohort - Mechanisms of the Development of Allergy (MeDALL) substudy),14 15 European Childhood Obesity Project Trial (CHOP, Belgium, Germany, Italy, Poland and Spain),16 Danish National Birth Cohort (DNBC, Denmark),17 Generation R (Gen R, The Netherlands),18 Norwegian Mother, Father and Child Cohort Study (MoBa, Norway)19 and Nascita e INFanzia: gli Effetti dell'Ambiente (NINFEA, Italy).20 A description of the participating cohorts is provided in online supplemental Methods S1 and Table S1.

Participants provided written informed consent, and ethical approval was granted by local ethics committees (online supplemental Table S2).

Maternal EDs

The main exposure of interest was maternal pre-pregnancy history of any ED (AN, BN, BED, EDs not otherwise specified), defined according to the ED categories available within each cohort (online supplemental Table S3). Where data permitted, additional harmonisation was performed for pre-pregnancy AN and BN separately, EDs in pregnancy, and EDs active after the index pregnancy (0–4 years after delivery).

Six of seven participating cohorts assessed maternal EDs by questionnaires (self-reported disorder: ALSPAC, CHOP, DNBC, GenR; self-reported doctor diagnosis: NINFEA; and self-reported criteria of the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fourth Edition: MoBa), while one cohort (BiB) ascertained the ED diagnoses through linkage with general practitioner (GP) health records. Self-reported information was collected prospectively during pregnancy or shortly after delivery, referring to lifetime or pregnancy-active disorders. In the cohort with GP linkage, lifetime ED diagnoses were searched from the earliest available records in the GP system. Post-pregnancy EDs were assessed within 4 years after delivery. We defined EDs during and after pregnancy according to the period they were reported to occur, irrespective of the actual initial time of onset. Detailed cohort-specific information is provided in online supplemental Table S3.

Childhood respiratory outcomes

As primary outcomes, we analysed preschool wheezing (any wheezing episode 0–4 years) and current school-age asthma (median 7.5 years, IQR: 7.5–7.5 years).

Information on wheezing and asthma was obtained from parental questionnaires in all cohorts. Wheezing was defined according to the International Study of Asthma and Allergies in Childhood (ISAAC) questions.21 Current school-age asthma was defined according to the MeDALL criteria,22 where a child is classified as having current asthma if at least two of the following three criteria are satisfied: (1) ever doctor diagnosis of asthma, (2) use of asthma medication in the past 12 months and (3) wheezing in the past 12 months.

As secondary outcomes, we analysed ever diagnosis of asthma, defined as asthma diagnosed at the age of ≥5 years and school-age lung function (available in ALSPAC: median 8 years, IQR: 8–8 years and GenR: median 9 years, IQR: 9–9 years). In both cohorts, forced expiratory volume in 1 second (FEV1), forced vital capacity (FVC) and FEV1/FVC were measured by spirometry according to the American Thoracic Society and European Respiratory Society guidelines,23 and converted to Z-scores for age, height, sex and ethnicity based on the external Global Lung Initiative 2012 reference values.24

Details on respiratory outcomes available in each cohort are provided in online supplemental Table S4.

Covariates

Potential confounding factors were selected a priori and included: maternal age at delivery (years), maternal education (classified according to International Standard Classification of Education 97/2011 into low/medium vs high), the predicted equivalised total disposable household income at baseline,25 maternal ethnicity (Western or Non-Western/Mixed), maternal history of asthma and maternal mental health comorbidities (eg, depression, anxiety). All of these factors were adjusted for in the primary analyses, with the exception of maternal mental health comorbidities, whose influence was examined in sensitivity analyses restricted to mothers without these conditions. In contrast, maternal behavioural and lifestyle factors (eg, body mass index, smoking, breastfeeding) and pregnancy and perinatal characteristics (eg, weight gain, birth weight) were not adjusted for in any of the main or secondary analyses, as these variables are more likely to act as mediators on the causal pathway linking maternal EDs to offspring respiratory outcomes. Details on confounding variables for each cohort are provided in online supplemental Table S5.

Statistical analyses

The analyses were performed remotely on pseudonymised individual participant data (IPD), stored on the cohorts’ local data servers, using the open-source software DataSHIELD,26 27 with DataSHIELD-specific libraries: dsBase version 6.1.0 (https://github.com/datashield) and Statistical Software R version 4.1.1.28

We employed a complete-case approach, restricted the analyses to singletons, and included a cohort only if at least three outcome cases among exposed/unexposed were available for analysis. Associations between maternal EDs and childhood wheezing and asthma were estimated using logistic regression, while the associations with lung function were evaluated through linear regression models. Random-effects meta-analysis was applied if at least three cohorts were available for the specific analysis. In the analyses of wheezing and asthma, we fitted an unadjusted model, and a model adjusted for the maximum set of confounding variables available within each cohort: maternal age at delivery, maternal education and household income in all cohorts, maternal asthma in six cohorts (ALSPAC, BiB, DNBC, GenR, MoBa, NINFEA) and maternal ethnicity in four cohorts (ALSPAC, BiB, CHOP, GenR). To allow comparisons, unadjusted and adjusted models were performed on the same study population (participants with data on exposure, outcome and all confounders). Lung function analyses were adjusted for the same confounders and additionally for child sex and age at lung function measurement.

Heterogeneity between cohorts was evaluated using I2 with the corresponding 95% CIs, the χ2-based Cochran’s Q statistic and 95% prediction intervals (PIs) for the overall effect. To assess the robustness of meta-analytic estimates, we performed ‘leave-one-out’ analysis and formally assessed the impact of each study on the overall estimate using influence diagnostics, including DFBETAS and Cook’s distance.

The secondary analyses included: (1) analysis of maternal pre-pregnancy AN and BN as separate exposures, (2) analysis of exposure periods (pregnancy and post-pregnancy EDs) and (3) analyses of pre-pregnancy EDs in women without pre-pregnancy diagnosis of depression and/or anxiety. These analyses were performed for the two primary outcomes and with identical confounder adjustment as in the main analysis.

Results

Study population

A total of 189 263 children from seven European birth cohorts had data on at least one exposure and one outcome, with the cohort-specific sample sizes ranging from 605 children in CHOP to 90 007 in MoBa (figure 1 and online supplemental Table S1). The descriptive statistics of maternal baseline characteristics are shown in table 1. The complete case sample size for various main and secondary analyses varied between 2254 (single cohort analysis of post-pregnancy EDs with preschool wheezing in BiB) to 131 495 mother–child pairs (meta-analysis of pre-pregnancy ED and preschool wheezing in six cohorts (online supplemental Table S6). Excluded observations more often represented mothers who were younger (except in NINFEA), had lower educational attainment, were of non-Western ethnic background (except in BiB) and had lower household income. This pattern was very similar across the cohorts (online supplemental Table S7).

Figure 2 shows the prevalence of maternal EDs in the participating cohorts by exposure time windows, while online supplemental Figure S1 shows the prevalence of ED subtypes. The overall pre-pregnancy ED prevalence ranged from 0.8% in BiB (GP health records) to 17.0% in CHOP (self-reported problems with AN, BN, BED and any other ED in the past). Despite the high overall ED prevalence in CHOP, the prevalence of maternal AN and BN was similar to the AN and BN prevalence in other cohorts (approximately 2% each) (online supplemental Figure S1). This suggests that the high lifetime prevalence in CHOP is mostly driven by BED, other specified feeding or eating disorders, or milder and undiagnosed cases.

The prevalence of wheezing in the first 4 years of life varied from 20.7% in BiB to 49.6% in GenR (table 1). There were also large variations across cohorts in the prevalence of childhood asthma, with the lowest prevalence in NINFEA (2.1%) and the highest in ALSPAC (17.3%), both assessed at the age of 7 years.

Associations between maternal pre-pregnancy EDs and childhood respiratory outcomes

Overall, maternal pre-pregnancy EDs were associated with an increased odds of preschool wheezing (OR: 1.25, 95% CI: 1.06 to 1.47; figure 3), but with substantial heterogeneity across cohorts (I2: 74%, 95% CI: 0 to 97%, Q-statistic=11.8, p-value=0.04) and a wide 95% prediction interval (95% PI: 0.89 to 1.74). Current school-age asthma showed a similar positive association (OR: 1.26, 95% CI: 1.10 to 1.46), with minimal heterogeneity (I2: 9%, 95% CI: 0 to 90%, Q-statistic=2.9, p-value=0.58) and a narrower corresponding prediction interval (95% PI: 1.06 to 1.51), reflecting more consistent effects across cohorts. Although the estimates for wheezing showed greater heterogeneity across cohorts, they were qualitatively consistent with the asthma findings, with the odds ratio (OR) greater than one in five of the six cohorts. There were only marginal differences between the results of unadjusted and adjusted models (online supplemental Figure S2). Leave-one-out analysis confirmed the robustness of the pooled estimates, with largely consistent estimates across models (online supplemental Table S8).

Secondary analyses

Table 2 shows the results of the secondary analyses that are based on a smaller number of cohorts and mother–child dyads. Preschool wheezing was associated with pre-pregnancy BN, while no evidence of an association was found with pre-pregnancy AN. The direction and the magnitude of the associations with childhood asthma were similar for AN and BN. This consistency was evident in the overall meta-analysis estimate (OR: 1.34, 95% CI: 1.06 to 1.69, I2: 6%, 95% PI: 1.02 to 1.76 for AN and OR: 1.28, 95% CI: 1.04 to 1.57, I2: 0%, 95% PI: 1.04 to 1.57 for BN) as well as across individual cohorts.

The meta-analytical and single cohort’s power for analyses of exposure periods was largely reduced due to the rarity of EDs manifesting during or after pregnancy and limited data availability (pregnancy: DNBC, GenR, MoBa and NINFEA; post-pregnancy: MoBa and BiB). As expected, a high proportion of mothers with pregnancy EDs also reported EDs before pregnancy: 49% in MoBa, 91% in NINFEA and 100% in DNBC and GenR. In MoBa, where data are available across all three exposure periods, about 25% of mothers with post-pregnancy EDs had EDs either before or during pregnancy. EDs during pregnancy were associated with preschool wheezing (meta-analysis of DNBC, GenR, MoBa and NINFEA), with the estimate comparable to the pre-pregnancy period. In MoBa, the association with preschool wheezing was similar across the three periods. The estimate for EDs during pregnancy and childhood asthma, based on only three cohorts (DNBC, GenR and MoBa), shows wider CIs and no clear association (OR: 1.00, 95% CI: 0.77 to 1.45). In MoBa, with analyses across all three exposure periods, the strongest association with asthma was observed for post-pregnancy EDs (OR: 1.41, 95% CI: 1.16 to 1.73 compared with 0.99; 0.82 to 1.21 for pregnancy and 1.16; 0.96 to 1.41 for pre-pregnancy period).

The associations of maternal pre-pregnancy EDs with childhood respiratory outcomes were slightly attenuated but remained directionally consistent with the main analyses after exclusion of women with depression/anxiety (table 2). Although BiB was excluded from this analysis due to a low number of events, overall estimates without BiB (online supplemental Table S8) were very similar to the main analysis (figure 3), indicating that its exclusion had little impact on the secondary analysis findings.

Maternal pre-pregnancy EDs were associated with ever diagnosis of asthma (OR: 1.18, 95% CI: 1.04 to 1.33; table 3). Across individual cohorts, the estimates for current asthma, ever asthma and preschool wheezing were generally consistent, except in ALSPAC, where positive association with pre-pregnancy EDs was found for wheezing and ever asthma diagnosis, but not for current school-age asthma.

Discussion

This analysis of seven European prospective pregnancy and birth cohorts found robust associations of maternal pre-pregnancy EDs with preschool wheezing and school-age asthma. The observed associations were not fully explained by maternal comorbid depression/anxiety. Similar associations with childhood asthma were found for AN and BN, while preschool wheezing was associated with BN only. Although associations differed slightly across exposure periods, no distinct window of susceptibility was identified. The analyses on lung function add some caution in the interpretation of the results, as potential bias mechanisms, such as maternal over-reporting of child respiratory outcomes or attrition in follow-up, might have influenced these findings.

Our study found little evidence that the associations with asthma and wheezing differ according to the timing of EDs – whether before, during or after pregnancy. Notably, a consistent pattern of overlap between EDs across the perinatal periods was observed, though it was less pronounced in MoBa, likely due to differences in assessment. The substantial proportion of mothers with EDs spanning multiple periods suggests that the observed associations may be influenced less by the specific timing of exposure and more by the chronicity or severity of the underlying disorder. However, only one cohort covered all three exposure time windows, limiting our ability to fully assess differences across these periods. We also found minimal differences between AN and BN in their association with school-age asthma. This suggests the role of potentially common psychopathological traits and shared environmental factors rather than of the particularities of single disorders.

The mechanisms underlying the associations between maternal mental health and childhood respiratory outcomes remain unclear. Mental health conditions and associated stress may activate the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis, disrupting the in utero lung development and maturation of the offspring’s immune system,29 30 and increasing susceptibility to immune-system driven conditions, including asthma. Several studies suggest that the impact of these disorders on childhood respiratory outcomes is not limited to pregnancy but may also extend beyond it.3 31 Mental health disorders, and EDs in particular, are characterised by altered body mass index and gestational weight gain and dysfunctional eating behaviours,32 tobacco smoking33 and shorter breastfeeding duration.5 33 Children born to mothers with EDs are at an increased risk of foetal growth restriction, prematurity, Caesarean delivery and low birth weight.32 33 These are also well-known risk factors for respiratory morbidity, suggesting multiple possible mediating pathways in the link between maternal EDs and childhood respiratory outcomes.

In addition, research has shown that both mental health disorders and asthma involve dysregulation in immune response and inflammatory pathways, suggesting a common genetic basis that may contribute to both conditions.34 Since up to 70% of individuals with EDs suffer from one or more comorbid psychiatric disorders,10 focusing solely on a single mental health disorder without considering psychiatric comorbidities represents a limitation of many studies. In our study, the comorbid depression/anxiety among women with EDs ranged from 11% in DNBC to 75% in BiB (online supplemental Figure S4), and our findings suggest that even after restricting to the population of women without these two mental health comorbidities, the associations of maternal EDs with childhood respiratory outcomes persist.

In interpreting the positive associations observed between maternal EDs and childhood lung function, it is important to consider that while the association with lung function was consistent between the two cohorts, the associations with asthma and wheezing differed: they were null or inverse in GenR, but positive in ALSPAC, suggesting possible cohort-specific biases. We hypothesised two potential sources of bias: (1) over-reporting of child asthma by mothers with EDs and (2) sample attrition (online supplemental Figure S3). The hypothesis of over-reporting is supported by robust evidence linking maternal mental health and ratings of their child health problems.35 36 This bias likely extends beyond self-reported data, as women with mental health disorders also have more healthcare-seeking behaviour, increasing the probability of diagnosis and treatment for their children.37 Objective health measures, such as spirometry-based lung function assessments, are thus critical in disentangling potential over-reporting/over-diagnosis bias from true biological associations. Given the inconsistencies between the two cohorts and the strength of the observed associations, we believe that attrition bias may have had a greater impact on the lung function analyses than reporting bias. The role of chance and the role of potential cohort-specific sources of bias complicate the generalisability of this sensitivity analysis to other cohorts lacking child lung function data. Therefore, while the results on the association between EDs and asthma should be interpreted cautiously, their validity is not necessarily undermined by the findings on lung function.

The main strength of this study is the use of a large dataset containing individual participant data harmonised under a common protocol. While not all potential confounding variables were available in every cohort, we were able to adjust for the maximum number of confounders available in each. Homogeneous assessment of the respiratory outcomes, with most of the cohorts using standardised or adapted ISAAC-based questionnaires, the possibility to investigate different EDs and to assess the role of comorbid depression/anxiety are also strengths of the study.

Despite the common harmonisation protocol, we found considerable variation between cohorts in the prevalence of maternal EDs and childhood respiratory outcomes. Differences in childhood wheezing and asthma across European countries have already been noted38 and are possibly explained by country-specific diagnostic guidelines, varying adherence to the guidelines, parental interpretation of “wheezing”, healthcare access, sociocultural factors and environmental exposures. The variation in ED prevalence between European countries may be explained by different data sources and measurements among cohorts (self-reported vs health-registry data, doctor diagnosis vs diagnostic criteria, any EDs vs AN or BN), as well as by body image stereotypes shaped by social and cultural context. While these factors may explain quantitative inconsistencies in the findings between cohorts, heterogeneity between the study estimates for asthma outcomes was small, and the greater heterogeneity in wheezing estimates likely reflects differences in effect size magnitude rather than inconsistency in the direction of the association.

All but one cohort (BiB) included in the study ascertained EDs by questionnaires/interviews, defining them as either self-reported disorder, self-reported doctor-diagnosed disorder, or by using standardised diagnostic criteria. The validity of self-reported EDs has been shown in previous studies reporting similarities in most of the characteristics, except for the disease severity and psychiatric comorbidities, between pregnant women with self-reported EDs and women with hospital-diagnosed EDs,39 and excellent sensitivity and specificity of self-report for lifetime AN and BN against the Composite International Diagnostic Interview.40 As EDs commonly remain undiagnosed, it is likely that self-reported information is more exhaustive in capturing the EDs compared with the register-based studies.

Our main and subgroup analyses were not always based on the same sample size and the number of cohorts varied according to the data availability. Although this may make some of the findings less comparable, the direction and the magnitude of the associations were relatively stable in all the analyses. We harmonised maternal AN and BN separately, but the data on BED and other ED subtypes, including ED not otherwise specified, were available in few cohorts only and were therefore not included in the analyses on ED subtypes. Although prior studies suggest stronger effects of maternal than paternal mental health, limited paternal data in our cohorts prevented further exploration of its potential role. Finally, the use of complete case analysis may have introduced selection bias, as notable differences existed between included and excluded participants. While multiple imputation is often recommended to address missing data, it was not supported within the DataSHIELD infrastructure at the time of analysis.

In summary, maternal pre-pregnancy EDs are associated with an increased risk of preschool wheezing and school-age asthma, and this association was not entirely explained by comorbid maternal depression and/or anxiety. The findings were relatively consistent across cohorts, supporting the robustness of the observed associations. This study revealed minimal to no evidence suggesting a differential effect based on ED subtypes or exposure time windows.
