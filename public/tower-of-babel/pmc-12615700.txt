Changes in emergency and primary care use after adding virtual physicians to HealthLink BC’s 8-1-1 program
Authors: S. Cressman, K. Stewart, F. X. Scheuermeyer, L. Hedden, R. Duncan, L. Riches, R. B. Abu-Laban, D. Raff, J. Assali, H. Novak Lauscher, K. Halani, B. Wong, S. Sundhu, K. Ho
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Health human resource constraints in Canada have left millions of patients without timely access to primary care (PC), leading many to attend emergency departments (ED) to see a doctor. We evaluated changes in service use and costs resulting from the addition of virtual physicians to HealthLink BC’s 8-1-1 program. Visits to ED and PC within 30 days and healthcare costs were measured with time series data from 445,630 telehealth users. The virtual physician intervention was associated with 16.14 (p = 0.001, 95% CI = 25.95–6.33) fewer ED visits and 106.14 (p < 0.001, 95% CI = 74.78–137.49) more PC visits in 30 days per 1000 patients. Patients above age 15, without a known chronic illness or existing relationship with a PC provider, had higher PC follow-up and patient-paid medical travel costs; healthcare system costs were neutral over a year. Long-term health outcomes from increased PC follow-up and variability in the amount of medical travel costs paid by patients will be important economic parameters to consider in Canadian health policy evaluations.

Introduction

Most Canadian jurisdictions have 8-1-1 telephone services that provide rapid access to health information and nursing advice. At the start of the COVID-19 pandemic in April 2020, HealthLink BC’s (HLBC) 8-1-1 program introduced physician services that are delivered by telephone and videoconferencing to support patients in deciding their follow-up care plans when nurses would have otherwise recommended callers seek urgent, in-person, physician services or visit the Emergency Department (ED). The original goals of the program were to reduce the risk of spreading COVID-19 in the ED while preserving ED capacity for those who needed timely access to emergency care. The program involves virtual nurse-physician teams working together to identify patients with high-risk health concerns who need to go to the ED, redirecting most patients to self-manage at home and/or coordinate with primary care (PC) for the appropriate follow-up1. Since its inception, the HealthLink BC Emergency i-Doctor in-Assistance (HEiDi) program has provided over 200,000 encounters to more than 2.5 million callers. The program has redirected approximately 60% of 8-1-1 patients seen to receive longitudinal primary care (PC) or informed, home-based treatment, while nearly 40% are appropriately directed to the ED2. Previous studies by our group have shown that most patients who use the HEiDi service go on to receive the care recommended by the virtual physicians3; however, adherence with follow-up guidance is partial due to differences in accessing PC as a result of health human resource constraints in British Columbia and other factors such as symptom resolution, prohibitive travel costs and cultural safety concerns in receiving follow-up care4,5. There is much interest in gaining an understanding of the downstream impact of virtual care for its potential to integrate with the surrounding in-person “eco-systems” of care6–9.

The dual ability of HEiDi to direct patients with urgent health concerns to ED and safely re-direct most calls to community-based care has led to hypotheses about potentially favorable economic impact of the program within BC’s existing PC services, a combination of full-service family practice, walk-in clinics and urgent primary care centres. Indeed, if HEiDi can safely and appropriately redirect most calls away from crowded EDs, then a range of positive health resource impacts related to reduced ED visits could result (e.g., avoidance of duplicate tests, unnecessary prescriptions, and reduction of medical errors)10. However, if HEiDi does not reduce ED visits and substantially increases PC visits or introduces negative health outcomes, then the program would have an adverse economic impact as others have observed11. Given the complex and interdependent range of possible downstream results, economic analyses of programs like HEiDi warrant careful attention to rigorous methods that appropriately consider causality in inferring relationships between the intervention and its desired outcomes. Our purpose, therefore, was to establish foundational evidence about the HEiDi intervention’s impact on post-encounter ED and PC service use among 8-1-1 callers.

Results

Demographic overview

The 8-1-1 program received a total of 445,630 calls over the observation period that were included in the study: 102,769 (23.1%) were in the intervention group; 312,023 (70.0%), in the low-urgency control; 30,838 (6.9%) were in the high-urgency overnight control (Table 1). The distribution of patients in the 0–14 age group was higher compared with the provincial population (14.3%)12, and there were fewer adults above age 65 (<16.0% in the study cohort vs. 20.3% in the BC population). In addition, the 8-1-1 cohort had a higher proportion of female patients (61.2%); a lower proportion who lived in rural areas (12.9% in the 8-1-1 demographic versus 17.9% overall in BC) and a lower proportion of people with at least one chronic disease13. Difference in differences analysis

When compared with the low-urgency control group via the DID method, the HEiDi intervention was associated with a significant decrease in ED visits (−16.1, p = 0.001, 95% CI = −26.0, −6.3) and increase in PC visits (106.1, p < 0.001, 95% CI = 74.8, 137.5) at 30 days per 1000 (Fig. 2). Hospital admissions and healthcare costs also increased initially, however, at 365 days, no differences in costs or hospitalization rates were observed (p’s > 0.33). Patient-paid costs for attending medical appointments increased by $88.51 (95% CI = $36.92–$140.11) and CO2-equivalent emissions increased by 2.6 kg (p < 0.001, 95% CI = 1.9–3.3) at 365-day post-encounter.Fig. 2Difference in Difference effects for the HEiDi intervention versus the low-urgency control group.

Difference in Difference effects for the HEiDi intervention versus the low-urgency control group.

When the intervention versus low-urgency DID results were stratified by each demographic variable, diverging ED and PC outcomes were observed among patients aged 0–14 versus patients above 15. The 0–14 and 65+ year age groups also had strong patterns of diverging secondary outcomes and the diverging pattern was sustained across all timepoints for the primary outcomes (Fig. 3). Healthcare costs (both health system and patient paid) were higher among pediatric age patients due to high volumes of follow-up service use with PC (age <15) and for patients (age 15–64) the patient and family costs were higher due to increased follow-up with primary care and specialist physicians.Fig. 3Difference-in-difference effects for intervention versus low-urgency control, stratified by age group.

Difference-in-difference effects for intervention versus low-urgency control, stratified by age group.

Additional significant DID interactions were found for other demographic characteristics, including sex (males had fewer ED visits at one-day post-HEiDi encounter compared to females), and patients who have a regular relationship with a family doctor (i.e., attached to a regular primary care provider) had significantly higher PC visits within 7 days compared to patients who are unattached. Patients who called 8-1-1 without chronic conditions had higher ED visits and PC visits after 30 days compared to callers with chronic conditions (Supplementary Fig. S6).

The results from the sensitivity analyses supported the main findings. When compared to the overnight control group, the DID effects reflected the same trends as seen with the low-urgency comparisons, and propensity-matched analysis replicated the main findings; however, the number of callers in the high-urgency overnight control group and propensity-matched analysis was too low to detect significant differences (Supplementary Fig. S8).

Discussion

This is the first study to report on changes in service use and costs attributed to a virtual care coordination program. We found that the implementation of the program was associated with a reduction in ED visits and an increase in PC follow-up among 8-1-1 callers. We observed a pattern among patient groups with relatively higher needs for health information; patients of pediatric age, patients with no-known history of a chronic disease or those without an existing relationship with a regular PC provider had significantly higher PC visits compared with their counterparts, collectively suggesting that the program increases PC follow-up, particularly among patients with a greater need for health information. We observed net neutral health system costs over a year of follow-up from the healthcare system’s perspective, while patient-paid costs increased due to travel for greater follow-up visits with PC and specialists.

Our findings add to mounting evidence that rapid access to virtual care programs may have an important role in preventing unnecessary ED and hospital use; implementation of telemedicine in the US’ Medicare system, for example, has been associated with 2.7% fewer (non-COVID) ED visits, but increased health system costs overall6. Understanding the reasons for cost differences between different program designs and surrounding care ecosystems will be important to the development of new virtual care policy. Our study highlighted the need to assess heterogeneity among the group of patients with greater need for health information (i.e. unattached, pediatric age, and no known history of a chronic disease) which compliments the conclusions of others on differences in virtual care outcomes among pediatric, elderly, rural populations, populations with high healthcare needs and intersecting factors such as gender and immigration status4,14–16. Others have found that post-encounter ED reductions are associated with virtual care providers themselves, noting better outcomes among providers who are female and live in urban areas8. A recent study of a pandemic-responsive virtual ED program in Ontario suggests that some virtual care programs may increase ED visits if they are not fully integrated17. These studies occur against a backdrop of widespread PC provider shortages that underscore the importance of supporting patients in seeking longitudinal PC when it is more appropriate than receiving episodic care in crowded EDs.

Our findings are limited to patients who call BC’s 8-1-1 service, a population with relative social advantages, including a younger age, mainly urban dwellings and an underlying readiness to adapt to virtual care. The results should be interpreted with an understanding of ongoing changes to service use over time and complexity embedded in the ITS method, including unmeasured confounders such as limitations in the data available, partial reliance of triage decisions on nurse and physicians’ personal decision making, labile trust in the medical system, or changes in service use due to fear of contagion. Small sample sizes limited the ability to draw definitive conclusions from the overnight controls and propensity matching. We did not observe any differences in all-cause mortality rates, but ongoing evaluation of long-term health outcomes will be important to monitor in program evaluations. Our cost analysis is also limited to the data sources and our assumptions; if the fees for physician services are higher, health system costs would increase. If the cost of ED use is higher—for example, due to spillover costs associated with ED overcrowding18,19—health system costs would decrease due to the introduction of HEiDi. Finally, by focussing on the costs patients and their families pay for medical travel and without considering their available income, we did not address financial hardship related to accessing medical care.

Continuous data collection and analysis are important for assessing the effectiveness and efficiency of remote triage systems20. Our study shows that virtual physician services can reduce ED visits and increase PC follow-up at net neutral costs to the healthcare system. The findings imply that long-term health outcomes associated with the increases in PC follow-up and patient-paid medical travel costs are important economic considerations to include in forthcoming evaluations of virtual care programs. If increases in PC use are not accompanied by improvements in outcomes usually associated with effective primary care—for example, increased cancer screening rates or prescriptions of preventative metformin and statins—then a call for adaptive policy would be warranted to support coordination of episodic virtual care with longitudinal PC that can achieve these outcomes.

Methods

Study design and setting

We conducted a retrospective observational study on routinely collected health data, comparing outcomes among 8-1-1 callers one year before and two years after the implementation of HEiDi. First, we compared pre-/post-implementation outcomes with an interrupted time series (ITS) study. Second, because the primary outcomes were anticipated to be influenced by externalities (e.g., fear of contracting COVID-19 in the ED, absence of trust in the healthcare system), we used a difference-in-difference (DID) method to compare outcomes among 8-1-1 callers who interacted with a HEiDi physician with those who did not. The DID method observes changes in outcomes that may be attributable to HEiDi by comparing outcomes from control groups to estimate the unobservable counterfactual that would be expected in the absence of the HEiDi intervention21. The study considered the perspective of both the health system and patients and their families who incur time and travel costs to receive care. Patients were involved as research partners in developing the economic analysis plan, co-analysis of the results, and knowledge mobilization. The study was approved by the University of British Columbia’s (UBC) BREB (H22-02603).

British Columbia’s 8-1-1 and HEiDi service

Callers to BC’s 8-1-1 service first speak with a navigator who initiates the call record, enters basic information, and directs most callers to speak with a registered nurse. Nurses are available 24/7/365 to assess callers’ needs based on their chief complaint and assign them one of seven discharge dispositions according to the Healthwise triage protocol22 and a nurses’ own clinical judgment (Fig. 4). HEiDi physicians are available for referrals from nurses for callers who are within the nurse’s high-urgency, non-emergent (yellow and orange) categories, and they provide consultations from 9 a.m. to 11 p.m. daily, with callers’ consent. The criteria for assigning a patient to a high-urgency category have remained consistent over the past decade. Physicians and nurses involved in the program are reimbursed per shift, regardless of the number of consultations they provide.Fig. 4Call flow and disposition categories for the 8-1-1 and HEiDi programs.

Call flow and disposition categories for the 8-1-1 and HEiDi programs.

Data linkages and groupings

All 8-1-1 calls from April 1, 2019, to March 31, 2022, with a valid Personal Health Number (PHN) and BC residency status were considered for inclusion. Calls between April 1–5, 2020, were excluded to prevent overlap with the post-HEiDi period, which began offering physician consults on April 6, 2020. Calls not completed (left open or canceled), disposed to 9-1-1 or “go to ED now”, and callers with a registered date of death before the index call were all excluded. The term “caller” refers to the patient who is receiving the care/advice, even if a representative contacts 8-1-1 on their behalf. The post-intervention group included any patient seen by a HEiDi physician (April 6, 2020, through March 31, 2022); and the pre-intervention group included patients with a high-urgency disposition seen during HEiDi service hours (Fig. 4, yellow and orange boxes) in the year prior to program implementation (from April 1, 2019, through March 31, 2020). Two control groups were defined: (1) a low-urgency daytime control including all 8-1-1 calls with low-urgency dispositions (Fig. 4, green boxes) made during HEiDi hours; and (2) a high-urgency overnight control including all nurse-only 8-1-1 calls with high-urgency dispositions outside of the hours of HEiDi’s operation. Data for patients with multiple 8-1-1 calls during the study period were indexed to their first call. All calls were included regardless of patient outcomes after testing for any differences in all-cause mortality rates among pre/post and control groups.

Electronic medical records from 8-1-1 nurse and HEiDi physician encounters were linked with datasets from the BC Ministry of Health’s Health Data Platform BC (HDPBC), including: Medical Service Plan (MSP) claims data23,24; National Ambulatory Care Record System (NACRS) for hospital and community-based ambulatory care25; Discharge Abstract Database (DAD) with information on hospital discharges26; PharmaNet claims data for prescription drugs and payment-sharing between drug insurers and patients27; the Client Roster with demographic information about age, sex, PC attachment, and geography28; Vital Events for all deaths registered in BC29; the Chronic Disease Registry including 25 chronic disease categories30; and the Canadian Index of Multiple Deprivation (CIMD), a national index to measure socioeconomic status31. We applied the method developed from the UBC Center for Health Services and Policy Research to identify ED visits among callers living in rural areas that do not report NACRS data32.

Outcomes

We defined primary outcomes as any post-encounter ED or PC visit; primary outcomes were evaluated at 30 days to allow time for patients to follow-up with PC providers (typically >1 week) and to draw comparison with other studies33,34. Any MSP Family Medicine claim, virtual or in-person, was assumed to be associated with a PC physician visit. Hospital admissions, health system-paid costs, patient-paid healthcare costs, and CO2-equivalent kilograms arising from patient travel to medical appointments were secondary outcomes. Outcomes were observed over the 1-, 7-, 30- 90-, 180-, and 365-day post-encounter follow-up periods. Service use outcomes were reported per 1000 patients, and heterogeneity was assessed via stratification by baseline demographic characteristics. Data to calculate costs paid by the healthcare system were derived from resource utilization rates from program and administrative records and multiplied by their respective unit costs (Supplementary Table S1) over the specified post-encounter observation period.

Cost analysis

The costs of physician services included any billings for patients recorded in MSP, for family practice, family psychotherapy, medical specialists, surgical, imaging, and laboratory specialists. We referred to billings reported for prescription drug costs (PharmaCare covered and patient-paid portions of drug costs) directly from PharmaNet files. Costs for hospital stays were calculated by each admission’s resource intensity weight in the DAD and multiplied by the yearly provincial cost of a standard hospital stay (e.g., $8,103 CAD in 2021) available from the Canadian Institute for Health Information (CIHI)35. For ED costs, we combined the MSP physician renumeration cost and CIHI’s estimated cost of an average ED visit, which is inclusive of all labs, imaging, administration, and human resources36. The unit costs for each visit were estimated using an average daily ED visit cost of $504, accounting for the ED physician fees ($138) plus facility costs ($366) combined per visit. Costs attributed to ED use were calculated separately for visits that resulted in a hospital admission within 24 h and standalone ED visits.

Costs paid by patients and their families included any productivity costs due to foregone paid or unpaid work, informal caregiving time costs, assuming one caregiver attends appointments with all adults above age 65 and pediatric patients below age 15 and 50% of all adult patients. Time for productivity losses and informal caregiving were valued at the current national average wage ($30.54). We used the human capital method of valuation, accounting for paid and unpaid work among all individuals aged 15–64 (calculated hourly with reference to the average wage in British Columbia at the time of analysis). Out-of-pocket costs were attributed to costs incurred at the time of receiving care, inclusive of region-specific travel costs (e.g., fuel, meals, accommodations, parking, or data usage costs for virtual visits). Costs were calculated by age group, region, and service type (ED CTAS I-III, ED CTAS IV-V, or PC) using a geospatial method developed previously to determine travel distance and time required to attend in-person visits from each Community Health Service Area (CHSA) in BC and aggregated to the Health Authority level37. This method outputs per-visit unit costs based on service type, health authority, and age group. Patient costs for two or more appointments on the same day were aggregated to a single appointment to ensure time and travel were only counted. These costs were then summed with the amount of prescription claims that patients paid out-of-pocket, which were based on PharmaNet records among the study cohort (summarized in Supplementary Table S2). All costs were inflated to the year of analysis (2024) using the average rate of change in the Canadian consumer price index between 2019 and 202338.

Interrupted time series and difference in differences analysis

We applied an interrupted time series (ITS) method observing the level and rate of change of post-encounter outcomes among 8-1-1 callers before and after 2020. For each combination of outcome and follow-up (days since encounter), autoregressive integrated moving average models (ARIMA) were fit to each outcome, with predictors of time (months), intervention (binary, i.e., HEiDi implementation) as a step/level, and time since intervention (months) as a ramp/trend predictor. Demographic covariates were aggregated by calendar month (categorical predictors were entered as proportions with n−1 categories), and a pandemic-by-intervention interaction covariate was included (other potential pandemic interactions were excluded due to collinearity), which corresponded to the COVID-19 pandemic state of emergency in the province (March 2020 through June 2021)39. Each model’s AIC and parameters were extracted, and residuals were plotted, visually inspected, and tested with the Ljung-Box test.

For the DID analysis, a linear regression model was fit to each combination of outcome, follow-up days, and comparison (HEiDi vs. each nurse-only control separately). Binary terms for time (pre-/post-HEiDi) and intervention (nurse-only vs. HEiDi) were included, and the DID estimator was the interaction between time and intervention. In each model, we adjusted for all variables used in the matching, except for the fiscal year. The predictor matrix’s collinearity was examined before running the DID models, and moderate collinearity was observed between age and age group (VIF > 5), so age was removed as a covariate from all analyses. Parallel trends were observed in the pre-HEiDi timeframe, thereby validating the application of the DID method.

Statistical analysis

Demographic variables were descriptively summarized by timeframe and intervention group using means (standard deviations) or counts (percentages). Outcomes were reported over a one-year follow-up period from the index encounter. Missing demographic variables were derived with multiple imputation with chained equations, and the imputed data were used in the main analysis. For the ITS, monthly aggregated outcomes for the intervention group were analyzed using autoregressive integrated moving average (ARIMA) models, controlling for baseline demographic characteristics and the period encompassing the COVID-19 pandemic. For the DID analysis, a linear regression model was fit for each combination of outcome, follow-up days, and comparison (intervention vs. each nurse-control separately), and the DID estimator was the interaction between time and intervention. Sensitivity analyses were carried out to examine the influence of missing data and the effect of baseline differences between the intervention and control groups by drawing comparisons with a high-urgency overnight control and propensity score matching for differences between the high-urgency intervention and low-urgency control groups. The ITS and DID analyses were repeated using propensity score matching for a hypothesized confounder to a patient's likelihood to be assigned to one of the treatment groups before and after the implementation of HEiDi. We sought to retain as many patients as possible from the Intervention group in the matching; first, the pre-/post-HEiDi intervention group was matched on the following variables: age [continuous], age group, sex, health authority, rurality, PC attachment, chronic disease presence, any CIMD quintiles, and one-year history of ED visits, and PC visits prior to their indexed call. All propensity score models used the nearest neighbor method and a 1:1 ratio of treated-to-control units, except when matching to the overnight group, where multiple intervention callers were retained for each overnight caller. For all matches, adjusted standardized mean differences for all variables were inspected after matching; if any absolute differences were >0.1, then the matching method parameters were adjusted and iterated until a satisfactory match was made. A p-value of 0.05 or less was considered statistically significant; two-sided tests of standardized mean differences between comparison groups referenced a 0.1 threshold as recommended for large populations40. All data were extracted using Microsoft SQL Server Management Studio (version 19) and analyzed using R version 4.3.1.
