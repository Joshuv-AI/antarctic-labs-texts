Anti-GLDN antibody–associated CIDP (nodopathy): transient IVIg response and B-cell depletion remission
Authors: Yujun Wu, Liyan Gu, Jieyi Liu, Wenbo Yang, Dongqing Zhu, Jie Wei, Xiangjun Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe classification of chronic inflammatory demyelinating polyneuropathy (CIDP) is evolving with the discovery of autoantibodies against nodal and paranodal proteins, leading to the recognition of “autoimmune nodopathies.” While anti-neurofascin-155 (NF155) and anti-contactin-1 (CNTN1) antibodies are well-characterized, the phenotype of anti-gliomedin (GLDN) antibodies remains poorly defined. We present a comprehensive case to delineate its clinical and serological profile.Case presentationWe report a detailed longitudinal case of a patient with anti-GLDN antibody–positive CIDP, including clinical presentation, electrophysiological and imaging studies, serological testing, treatment response, and follow-up.ResultsA 48-year-old woman presented with an 11-month history of relapsing-remitting, symmetric sensorimotor polyneuropathy, triggered by immune-activating events. Electrodiagnostic studies confirmed a demyelinating polyneuropathy meeting definitive European Academy of Neurology/Peripheral Nerve Society (EAN/PNS) criteria. Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis showed marked albuminocytological dissociation (protein 1631 mg/L). Serology was positive for anti-GLDN IgG (cell-based assay titer 1:32). The patient exhibited an excellent but transient response to intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIg), leading to multiple relapses. Subsequent B-cell targeted therapy with rituximab resulted in sustained clinical stabilization, effective B-cell depletion, and negative conversion of anti-GLDN IgG antibody.ConclusionsThis case suggests that anti-GLDN antibody–associated CIDP may be associated with a recognizable phenotype within the autoimmune nodopathy spectrum. Potential key features include a relapsing course following immune stimulation, markedly elevated cerebrospinal fluid protein, a unique pattern of robust but unsustained response to intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIg), and a favorable response to B-cell depletion therapy as evidenced by clinical remission and seroconversion.

Introduction

The landscape of chronic inflammatory demyelinating polyneuropathy (CIDP) has been refined by the identification of autoantibodies targeting proteins at the node of Ranvier and paranode, such as neurofascin-155 (NF155) and contactin-1 (CNTN1), defining a distinct subgroup termed “autoimmune nodopathy” [1]. These conditions are characterized by specific clinical phenotypes, variable responses to intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIg), and notable efficacy of B-cell depleting therapies like rituximab [2].

Among these targets, gliomedin (GLDN) is an essential glial cell adhesion molecule crucial for the clustering of neurofascin-186 (NF186) and voltage-gated sodium channels at the nodal gap [3]. Anti-GLDN antibodies have been detected in subsets of patients with Guillain-Barré syndrome (GBS) and chronic inflammatory demyelinating polyneuropathy (CIDP). However, current research on the association between GLDN antibodies and CIDP is still in its nascent stage. Internationally, dedicated case reports on “anti-GLDN antibody-positive CIDP” are extremely rare, and most relevant information is only scattered in cohort studies or comprehensive analyses [4]. Consequently, detailed clinical reports correlating anti-GLDN seropositivity with a comprehensive clinical course, treatment response, and serological changes are lacking. This case report aims to fill this critical gap by providing a comprehensive longitudinal description of this rare entity.

Case report

Patient identification and clinical course

A 48-year-old woman was admitted to our hospital due to an 11-month history of limb weakness with a half-month relapse period. She had no notable previous medical history or family history of genetic neurological disorders.

In October 2024, two weeks after an eyelid cosmetic procedure, the patient first presented with progressive weakness in the lower limbs, resulting in impaired walking and stair climbing, accompanied by distal weakness of the upper limbs. She was diagnosed with Guillain-Barré syndrome at a local hospital and achieved complete symptom remission after treatment with intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIg) at a total dose of 2 g/kg (0.4 g/kg daily for 5 consecutive days).

In January 2025, her lower limb weakness relapsed shortly after lip and eyebrow tattooing. A second cycle of IVIg yielded marked clinical improvement, but the effect was transient.

In April 2025, the patient experienced a third relapse following a dental extraction and received a third course of IVIg treatment, after which she was referred to our medical center.

During the disease course, three cycles of IVIg were administered in total. Treatment response was defined as an improvement of ≥ 1 grade in muscle strength according to the Medical Research Council (MRC) scale, and relapse was defined as the reappearance of limb weakness. The patient responded favorably to each IVIg cycle but experienced relapse within 4–6 weeks. Maintenance IVIg was discontinued upon initiation of rituximab therapy, which aimed to achieve sustained B-cell depletion.

Clinical presentation, investigations, and diagnosis

Neurological examination on admission revealed symmetric, distally predominant muscle weakness (lower limb muscle strength MRC grade 4), universal areflexia, and impaired sensation.

Electrodiagnostic studies revealed features of a demyelinating polyneuropathy: significantly reduced motor nerve conduction velocities, prolonged distal latencies, and partial conduction blocks in multiple nerves (Table 1), meeting the definite EAN/PNS 2021 electrodiagnostic criteria for CIDP [1].

Cerebrospinal Fluid (CSF) analysis

Showed significant albuminocytological dissociation with a markedly elevated protein level of 1631 mg/L and a normal white blood cell count.

Imaging

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the brachial and lumbosacral plexuses showed symmetric thickening and swelling of the nerve roots (Fig. 1).

Immunological studies

T-B cell subset analysis revealed a significantly elevated percentage of total CD19 + B cells (23.13%). Serum total IgG4 level was within normal limits (0.03–2.10 g/L, chemiluminescence assay), indicating no elevated IgG4 subclass response. IgG subclass analysis of anti-GLDN antibodies was not performed due to technical constraints. Anti-GLDN IgG antibodies were confirmed positive in serum by a cell-based assay (CBA) using HEK293 cells transfected with full-length human gliomedin (GLDN) plasmid (EUROIMMUN AG, Lubeck, Germany) (Fig. 2). Patient serum (starting dilution 1:10, serial two-fold dilutions) was applied to fixed transfected cells; binding was detected with CF™ 594-conjugated anti-human IgG (H + L) secondary antibody (1:100, Jackson ImmunoResearch). A serum titer of > = 1:10 was considered positive. Assay reproducibility was confirmed by duplicate testing. The clinical and diagnostic significance of this titer requires further validation in larger patient cohorts. Antibodies against NF155, CNTN1, and Caspr1 were negative.

Diagnostic challenges

The diagnosis posed several challenges. Testing for anti-GLDN antibodies requires specialized cell-based assays (CBA) available only at reference laboratories, typically resulting in diagnostic delays. The clinical presentation closely mimics typical CIDP or GBS, often leading to initial misdiagnosis and inappropriate first-line treatment. High testing costs and limited clinician awareness further hindered early diagnosis. Additionally, the diagnostic significance of the anti-GLDN IgG titer (1:32) detected in this patient remains to be established; no validated clinical cutoff or reference values are currently available.

Based on the patient’s clinical manifestations, neurological signs, and ancillary test results, we make a presumptive diagnosis of CIDP associated with anti-GLDN antibody positivity.

Given the relapsing disease course triggered by immune challenges, the transient and ultimately insufficient response to IVIg, and evidence of B-cell dysregulation (CD19 + B-cells at 23.13%), the underlying pathology was considered B-cell driven. Consequently, the therapeutic strategy was shifted to B-cell depletion. IVIg was discontinued at the time rituximab was initiated. The patient received rituximab induction therapy (100 mg on day 1, followed by 500 mg on day 2), with a subsequent dose of 500 mg after six months [5]. Disease severity and treatment response were assessed clinically using the modified Rankin Scale (mRS) and MRC sum score at each visit.

Following rituximab treatment, the patient’s clinical condition stabilized significantly: at the 6-month follow-up, she achieved clinical remission. Repeat T- and B-cell subset analysis confirmed effective B-cell depletion, with total CD19 + B-cells reduced to 1.61%. Follow-up nerve conduction studies further indicated disease stabilization, with no significant worsening of conduction velocities, distal latencies, or pre-existing conduction blocks observed—consistent with a lack of electrophysiological progression. Most notably, serological follow-up conducted in November 2025 and confirmed seroconversion of anti-GLDN antibodies to negative. The patient is now scheduled for rituximab infusions every 6–9 months as maintenance therapy and remains clinically stable. Both IVIg and rituximab were well-tolerated, with no adverse events reported; treatment adherence was also verified during regular clinical follow-ups. Reflecting these positive outcomes, the patient expressed profound relief and satisfaction with the treatment results (Table 2).

Discussion

Given the scarcity of detailed reports on anti-GLDN antibody–positive CIDP, our case with comprehensive longitudinal and serological follow-up may help characterize a phenotype associated with anti-GLDN antibodies within the autoimmune nodopathy spectrum and provide valuable complementary evidence for this emerging field.

The patient’s presentation aligns with the concept of autoimmune nodopathies, where a relapsing-remitting course triggered by immune-activating events is a hallmark [4]. However, a critical observation was the good but transient response to IVIg. This stands in marked contrast to patients seropositive for anti-NF155, anti-NF186, anti-CNTN1, or anti-Caspr1 antibodies (Table 3), who typically show poor or unresponsive to intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIg) but exhibit marked and sustained clinical improvement with rituximab [2, 6–8]. This discrepancy suggests that the mechanism of anti-GLDN antibodies may not fully parallel the strictly IgG4-dominated, complement-independent pathway described for anti-NF155 IgG4 [9]. The robust initial response to IVIg could imply a different Fc-dependent mechanism or a shorter half-life of the autoantibody-producing plasmablasts [10], which are susceptible to IVIg but can be effectively depleted by rituximab.

Although direct evidence from anti-GLDN antibody–positive CIDP cases remains limited, the pathogenic mechanisms can be inferred from studies on paranodal antibodies in general [14, 15] and the known function of GLDN [3, 16]. These mechanisms primarily involve three interconnected levels, which also provide the theoretical basis for the therapeutic choice in this case.(1) Direct Disruption of Axo-Glial Adhesion: GLDN, expressed on Schwann cell microvilli, binds to neurofascin-186 (NF186) on the axolemma, which is crucial for stabilizing the paranodal structure. Anti-GLDN antibodies directly interfere with this interaction, leading to paranodal dissection, disruption of ion channel clusters, and impairment of saltatory conduction, constituting the initial step in neurological dysfunction [3, 17].(2) Complement Activation and Inflammatory Amplification: In experimental models of paranodal antibody-mediated neuropathy, antibody-antigen complexes have been shown to activate the complement cascade, which may cause direct injury to Schwann cells and myelin [14, 18]. Direct evidence in human anti-GLDN CIDP is currently lacking; these mechanisms are inferred from animal models and require further investigation. Concurrently, this process can recruit macrophages and potentially activate T cells, triggering local inflammation and exacerbating tissue damage [15].(3) Central Role of B Cells and Targeted Therapy: The sustained production of the aforementioned pathogenic antibodies relies on CD20 + B cells. Rituximab, as an anti-CD20 monoclonal antibody, effectively depletes these B cells, thereby terminating the production of pathogenic antibodies at the source. The clinical remission and seroconversion observed in this case following rituximab treatment strongly corroborate the central role of B cells and humoral immunity in this disease.

Clinical and therapeutic implications

The favorable response to rituximab, including sustained remission and seroconversion, further indicates that B-cell-related humoral immunity may contribute to the pathogenesis of anti-GLDN antibody-associated CIDP. These observations should, however, be interpreted cautiously in the context of a single-patient study design. Table 1 provides a cohort comparison, highlighting the unique features of our case against other known nodopathies. For patients with this antibody profile, characterized by IVIg dependence and relapse, early initiation of B-cell depletion therapy may be a superior strategy to break this cycle and achieve long-term remission.

Limitations

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the 12-month follow-up period after rituximab treatment is relatively short, which limits the assessment of long-term outcomes and relapse risk beyond this interval. Second, formal disability scales such as INCAT ODS or adjusted INCAT were not systematically used during follow-up, and clinical evaluation was mainly based on the mRS and MRC sum score. Third, longitudinal serological monitoring, including anti-GLDN IgG titers and CD19 + B-cell percentages, was only performed at baseline and 6 months, rendering the detailed kinetic profile of antibody reduction unclear between these time points. Fourth, the IgG subclass of anti-GLDN antibodies was not determined, and whether IgG4 predominance, a common feature in most autoimmune nodopathies, is also present in this entity remains to be further explored. Finally, as this is a single case report, the generalizability of our findings regarding optimal treatment strategy, long-term prognosis, and disease phenotype needs to be validated in larger prospective cohorts.

Conclusion

This case demonstrates that anti-GLDN antibody positivity is associated with a recognizable clinical phenotype within the autoimmune nodopathy spectrum. Key characteristics include a relapsing course post-immune challenge, very high CSF protein, a unique pattern of a strong but unsustained response to IVIg, and a robust response to B-cell depletion evidenced by both clinical remission and seroconversion. The presence of this antibody profile may suggest underlying B-cell dysregulation and guide therapeutic strategy. Early initiation of rituximab appears to be a rational strategy for achieving long-term remission. Further cohort studies are needed to fully elucidate the pathophysiology, particularly the role of IgG subclasses, and to optimize the long-term management of this specific antibody-associated neuropathy.
