The reliability of a ball-tipped probe for detection of breached versus non-breached pedicle drill tracts in the thoracolumbar and lumbar spine of a canine large breed cadaveric model
Authors: Ronan A Mullins, Cristina Ortega, Jason Bleedorn, Marie Pauline Maurin, Seamus Hoey, Jorge Espinel Ruperez, Karl H Kraus, Scott J Hetzel, Julien Guevar
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSpinal stabilization in dogs is challenging, and improper implant placement can result in significant morbidity. Objectives were to evaluate reliability of a ball-tipped probe for detection of breached versus non-breached pedicle drill tracts in a canine large breed cadaveric model. CT scans obtained from T6 to sacrum. Fifty-six pedicles (T7-L7) were randomized to have 1 of 5 modified Zdichavsky drill tracts (non-breached, partial/full medial breach, partial/full lateral breach). All tracts were created using 3D-printed guides. Each pedicle was probed separately by 2 investigators in randomized fashion on 2 occasions, 2 weeks apart. Post-probing CT grading of tracts was performed by one investigator, blinded to probing results. Specificity, sensitivity, positive (PPV) and negative (NPV) predictive values and times to assign grade were compared between surgeons.ResultsCT identified 43 non-breached tracts, 7 medial and 5 lateral breaches. One tract was excluded because of guide misplacement. Specificity was 90.1%. PPV for detection of medial and lateral breaches was 80.0% and 57.1%. Sensitivity for medial (grade 2a/2b) and lateral (grade 3a/3b) breaches was 100.0% and 80.0%. NPV was 98.8%. Overall median time to assign grade with probe was 37.5–59.5 s. Both surgeons had very good intraobserver agreement (k,0.823 and 0.812). Interobserver agreement for round 1 was good (k,0.674) and good-to-very good for round 2 (k,0.800).ConclusionsUse of a ball-tipped probe is associated with excellent specificity, excellent sensitivity to detect medial breaches, and good sensitivity to detect lateral breaches. This technique may be useful to confirm integrity of pedicle screw/pin tracts in dogs.Keywords: Spinal, Vertebra, Dog, Canine, Modified Zdichavsky, Ball-tipped probe

Background

The use of pins or screws in combination with polymethylmethacrylate or titanium rods offers a strong and versatile method of spinal stabilization in dogs [1]. Several techniques have been described to guide insertion of pins or screws into the canine thoracolumbar and lumbar spine including use of 3D-printed patient-specific drill guides (3DPGs), fluoroscopy, intraosseous endoscopy, a free-hand technique based on preoperatively calculated safe corridors, and a modification of the free-hand technique known as the pedicle-probing technique [2–10]. While some of these techniques such as use of 3D-printed patient-specific drill guides are associated with a very low breach rate, the hardware and software required to produce these guides are expensive and not universally available, and expertise is required to design and produce the guides [11]. Freehand placement of pins or screws in the canine spine remains commonplace in veterinary clinical practice [1]. Implant misplacement can have serious consequences for the patient including injury to the contents of the vertebral canal associated with medial breach and reduced implant pull-out strength and potential fixation failure associated with lateral breach [1]. In both human and canine spine surgery, investigators have described use of a flexible ball probe to “palpate” the walls of the pilot hole for tactile feedback to detect pedicle wall violation prior to implant placement [4, 12–14]. The ability to detect pedicle breach prior to definitive implant placement has clear advantages, including decreased patient morbidity, a lower rate of return to the operating room, and decreased duration of hospitalization. If found to be reliable, clinical applications of this technique in veterinary spine surgery would include any conditions that require vertebral stabilization, including spinal fractures, degenerative lumbosacral stenosis, vertebral deformities, and spinal arachnoid diverticulum. However, 2 human cadaveric studies [12, 13] and 1 in vivo study [15] found surprisingly low rates (40.0–85.0%) of detection of vertebral canal breach among experienced or fellowship trained spine surgeons with probing of pedicle drill tracts using a ball-tipped probe. Conversely, the ability of such surgeons to detect the presence of a non-breached pedicle tract was greater (81–93%).12,13 To the authors’ knowledge, this has not been investigated in veterinary spine surgery. Therefore, the objective of this study was to evaluate the reliability of using a ball-tipped probe for the detection of non-breached (grade 1 modified Zdichavsky) pedicle drill tracts and those with medial or lateral violations (grade 2 or 3 modified Zdichavsky) in the thoracolumbar and lumbar spine of a canine large breed cadaveric model. The modified Zdichavsky classification is a 5-point grading system for grading of pedicle screw placement, with grade 1 corresponding to an optimally placed pin tract fully contained within the pedicle (thoracic spine) or vertebral body (lumbar spine), grade 2a denoting partial penetration of the medial pedicle wall, grade 2b corresponding to full penetration of the medial pedicle wall (whole of screw diameter within canal), and grades 3a and 3b denoting partial and full penetration of the lateral pedicle (thoracic spine) or vertebral body (lumbar spine) wall, respectively (Fig. 1) [4]. The study hypothesis was that the use of a ball-tipped probe would be associated with a high (> 90%) rate of accuracy for the detection of non-breached drill tracts and those with medial or lateral violations.

Results

Two greyhound cadavers were included, 1 male and 1 female. Bodyweights included 31.5 kg and 24.5 kg. One drill tract (T7R, cadaver 2, intended grade 1) was excluded because it was drilled with the guide incorrectly positioned. Therefore, 55 drill tracts were finally included in the study. Postoperative CT identified 43 grade 1 modified Zdichavsky drill tracts, 7 grade 2a/b (medial breach) drill tracts, and 5 grade 3a/b (lateral breach) drill tracts (Table 1).

Feasibility

Probing of drill tracts was successfully performed at each of the 55 pedicles. No complications (e.g., breakage of the probe) related to the use of the ball-tipped probe were recognized. Subjectively, there was no observed difference in the ability to pass the probe in the thoracic or lumbar vertebral column or for longer versus shorter drill tracts.

Accuracy

The number of correctly identified non-breached and medially and laterally breached drill tracts in rounds 1 and 2 for both surgeons is presented in Table 1. Surgeons correctly identified 90.1% of intact drill tracts (Fig. 2), 100.0% of medial breaches and 80.0% of lateral breaches (Fig. 3). Overall specificity was 90.1%. Overall PPV for detection of medial and lateral breaches was 80.0% and 57.1%, respectively. Overall sensitivity for detection of medial (grade 2a/2b) and lateral (grade 3a/3b) breaches was 100.0% and 80.0%, respectively (Table 2). Overall NPV was 98.8%. Intraobserver agreement (k [95% CI]) for surgeon 1 was 0.823 (0.658–0.989) and 0.812 (0.655–0.970) for surgeon 2. Interobserver agreement (k [95% CI]) for surgeons 1 and 2 in round 1 was 0.674 (0.475–0.874) and 0.800 (0.637–0.964) in round 2.

Tactile features of probing of pedicle tracts based on the modified zdichavsky classification

Grade 1 modified Zdichavsky drill tracts were characterized by the presence of a continuous column of hard cancellous bone on their medial and lateral aspects. Grade 2 drill tracts were characterized by a disruption in this column of bone on their medial aspect into which the tip of the probe could become lodged. Grade 3 tracts were subjectively the most difficult to identify and were characterized by the presence of a short tract and somewhat of a disparity in the length of their medial and lateral aspects in the case of a grade 3a breach.

Time to assign a modified Zdichavsky grade using the ball-tipped probe

Median (range) time (of averaged times of rounds 1 and 2) to assign a grade using the ball-tipped probe from T7-L7 was 59.5 (17.5–239.5) seconds for surgeon 1 and 37.5 (9.5–137.5) seconds for surgeon 2 (p = 0.00016) (Table 3). Median (range) time to assign a grade in the thoracic spine was 68.5 (35.0–239.5) seconds for surgeon 1 and 40.5 (16.0–137.5) seconds for surgeon 2 (p = 0.00278). Median (range) time to assign a grade in the lumbar spine was 43.3 (17.5–163.5) seconds for surgeon 1 and 35.5 (9.5–124.0) seconds for surgeon 2 (p = 0.00328) (Table 3).

Discussion

The main findings of this study are that the use of a ball-tipped probe was associated with excellent specificity (i.e., the likelihood that a grade 1 non-breached pedicle drill tract was correctly identified), excellent sensitivity (i.e., the likelihood that a breach was correctly identified) for detection of grade 2 (medial) breaches (100.0%) and good sensitivity for detection of grade 3 (lateral) breaches (80.0%). Therefore, we only partly accept our study hypothesis.

The specificity for detection of a grade 1 modified Zdichavsky drill tract in our study was just over 90%, with an approximately 10% false positive rate. The majority of incorrectly classified grade 1 tracts were assigned as grade 3, which most likely occurred because of the difficulty in distinguishing a short grade 1 non-breached tract from a similarly short grade 3 breach (e.g., T11L, cadaver 2, Fig. 4). This was made particularly difficult in our study because surgeons preforming the probing of drill tracts had no knowledge of the trajectory of any drill tracts prior to or during surgery, which made it impossible to compare observed versus expected pedicle tract lengths. This is a limitation of our study because in a clinical case, in addition to use of a ball-tipped probe, surgeons would measure the length of the drill tract and compare it with the expected length on CT. Other factors such as the location of the pilot hole and drilling trajectory would also be taken into consideration to make the most informed decision regarding the safety and quality (bone purchase) of the drill tract.

Human cadaveric studies have demonstrated the use of a ball-tipped probe to be associated with very variable specificity for detection of non-breached pedicle drill tracts, ranging from 68–98% [12–14]. One study found no obvious effect of surgeon experience on specificity, ranging from 88–98% [13]; whereas, in another study by Sedory and colleagues [12], mean specificity among 5 fellowship trained spine surgeons was 81%, with a range of 68–96%. In our study, surgeon 1 had some prior experience with probing of drill tracts in orthopedic- and neurosurgery and was found to have slightly higher specificity than surgeon 2 in both rounds, however, this was not statistically significant. In fact, the overall agreement between surgeons in our study was found to be good (k, 0.674) in round 1 and good-to-very good in round 2 (k, 0.800). Both individual surgeons were also found to be very consistent with their grading, with intraobserver kappa coefficients ranging from 0.812 to 0.823.

We hypothesized that the use of a ball-tipped probe would be associated with a high sensitivity for detection of medial and lateral breaches. Our results demonstrated this technique to have a higher sensitivity for detection of medial breaches (100.0%) than for lateral breaches (80.0%). Despite this very high rate of detection of medial breaches, it should be noted that numerous grade 2b modified Zdichavsky tracts were incorrectly classified as grade 2a (Fig. 5). In a clinical case, the difference between placement of an implant in a grade 2a drill tract compared with a grade 2b tract is likely to be very significant and therefore surgeons should be cautious with overconfidence in the diagnosis of a grade 2a breach with this technique. The sensitivity to detect a lateral breach in our study was less impressive (80.0%) and occurred because of incorrect classification of grade 3 tracts as grade 1 or grade 2 (2 each). In one of these tracts, a grade 3a breach was incorrectly classified by surgeon 2 as a grade 2a in round 1 and 2b in round 2, however, the contralateral drill tract was found on CT to have entered the medial aspect of this tract, giving the impression of a medial breach (Fig. 6C). Two human cadaveric studies found probing of pedicle drill tracts to have an unacceptably low rate of detection of vertebral canal breach [12, 13]. In one study [12], mean sensitivity for detection of a medial breach among 5 fellowship-trained spine surgeons was 68.0%, with a range of 40.0–85.0%. The mean sensitivity to detect the presence of a lateral breach was only slightly higher at 74.0%, with a range of 65.0–84.0%.12 In another study [13], mean sensitivity for detection of a medial breach among 4 surgeons at various levels of training ranging from a fellowship-trained spine surgeon to an orthopedic intern was 33.0%, with a range of 13.0–58.0%. A significant effect of the observer’s experience on the rate of detection of any breach (medial/lateral/anterior) was also identified in that study [13]. Similar to the study by Sedory and colleagues [12], the mean sensitivity to detect the presence of a lateral breach was marginally better at 53.3%, with a range of 30.0–70.0%. In an in vivo clinical study [15], investigators assessed the accuracy of manual palpation for the detection of medial and lateral pedicle breaches during thoracic spine surgery in living patients. Only 4 of 21 (19.0%) medial breaches and 7 of 128 (5.5%) lateral breaches measuring at least 2 mm were detected [15]. This lower rate of detection of lateral breaches is in agreement with the findings of our study. To our knowledge, no data exists in the veterinary literature regarding the sensitivity or specificity of the use of the ball-tipped probe technique and therefore our results are not possible to compare with others.

The NPV in our study was consistently high meaning that there were few false negatives and therefore surgeons can have a high degree of confidence that if they suspect a non-breached pedicle tract it is likely to be the case. Only one pedicle in our study, T9R of cadaver 1, a grade 3b tract (Fig. 6), was incorrectly assigned grade 1, however, this was a particularly challenging case because the drill tract traversed the transverse process of the vertebra and continued through the underlying rib, giving the impression of a single long tract. The overall PPV in our study was only fair for the presence of medial breaches (80.0%) and poor for the presence of lateral breaches (57.1%). The former occurred because of incorrect classification of several grade 1 drill tracts as grade 2 breaches (false positives, Fig. 4), the cause for which is unclear. The poor PPV for the presence of lateral breach is again related to incorrect classification of several grade 1 drill tracts as grade 3 breaches, most likely because of the difficulty in differentiating a short grade 1 non-breached tract from a similarly short grade 3 breach (e.g., T11L, cadaver 2, Fig. 4).

In our study, surgeons were blinded to the specific number of pedicle breaches to prevent biasing of the results because of surgeons unintentionally trying to account for a known number of breaches within both cadavers. Similarly, neither surgeon created the initial pilot holes because tactile feedback with this may have highlighted the number of breaches to account for. Areas of cadavers outside of a small area centered on the pilot hole was draped off with surgical drapes in an effort to prevent the surgeon from potentially remembering the status of a pedicle drill tract (based on its anatomical features) in round 1, biasing grading in round 2. Furthermore, the decision regarding whether there was a pedicle breach was based on palpation of the walls of the drill tract with the probe and not the position of the pilot hole or the angle of the probe within the hole. Surgeons also compared the length of the tract on its medial and lateral aspects to help distinguish between grade 1 tracts and grade 3 breaches. Typically, with a grade 1 drill tract, the length of the medial and lateral aspects of the tract will be very similar. With grade 3b breaches, the length of the medial and lateral aspects of the tracts will again be approximately the same but they would typically be shorter than a grade 1 tract for any given vertebra. Conversely, with grade 3a breaches, there may be a disparity in the length of the medial and lateral aspects of the tract because, with the lateral aspect of the drill tract exiting the vertebra in a more dorsal location and the medial aspect extending more ventral (Fig. 6).

The average time taken to perform probing of the pedicle drill tracts with a ball-tipped probe in our study was just under 1 min. A statistically significant difference in the time taken to assign a modified Zdichavsky grade was identified between surgeons in our study in the thoracic spine, lumbar spine, and overall. However, this difference is unlikely to have any clinically significant effect on surgical time in a clinical case.

Previously published human cadaveric studies investigating the accuracy of probing to detect pedicle wall violations described creation of iatrogenic breaches using a freehand drilling technique [12, 13]. In our study, we chose to create drill tracts with the use of 3DPGs in an effort to produce both partial and full medial and lateral breaches. A small discrepancy between the planned and observed number of breaches was observed in our study and probably relates to lack of experience of the resident in training with use of such guides and is a limitation of our study.

Both cadavers included in this study were used in a separate study [10] by the same investigators that evaluated the feasibility and accuracy of intraosseous endoscopy for inspection of pedicle drill tracts in the canine thoracolumbar and lumbar spine. In line with the principles of the 3Rs (reduction, replacement and refinement) [16], we used the same cadavers and pedicle drill tracts for both studies. However, the methodology of both studies was very carefully considered a priori to prevent one study influencing the other. For instance, surgeons performing endoscopic inspection of pedicle tracts in the first study [10] stood behind a non-transparent screen during grading of pedicle tracts, meaning that they could not see the vertebra being scoped (i.e., blinded), and gave instruction to another investigator who passed the scope in a standardized fashion [10]. The order in which pedicle tracts were scoped/probed was randomized within and between studies. Surgeons performing the passage of the ball-tipped probe in the study herein were only able to visualize an area of 3 × 3 cm2 centered on the pilot hole to prevent the surgeon from remembering/recognizing the status of a pedicle drill tract based on its anatomic features. Surgeons performing endoscopic inspection in the previous study [10] or probing with a ball-tipped probe in the study herein had no knowledge of the number or location of intentionally created breaches, and the actual number of breaches created by 3DPGs was not known to any investigator until CT of both cadavers was performed after completion of both studies.

We recognize a number of important limitations in this study. This was a cadaveric study that included only a single large breed and therefore feasibility cannot be confirmed for smaller breeds. It is possible that our results would not be reproduced in a smaller breed due to the smaller size and reduced maneuverability of the probe within the pedicle drill tracts. The overall number of breaches in our study was kept relatively low (approximately 20%) to make them more difficult to find. However, because of this, the final number of specific grades of medial and lateral breaches was also relatively low. Our principal aim was to assess the reliability of using a ball-tipped probe for the detection of breached versus nonbreached pedicle drill tracts. No previous studies have investigated the accuracy of this technique in small animals and therefore the optimal number of drill tracts required to assess accuracy was unknown. 95% confidence intervals, which are directly linked to sample size, have been provided so that it is possible to determine the range of values the true accuracy may have. However, had there been a greater number of overall drill tracts and individual breaches it is possible that a difference in sensitivity, specificity, PPV and NPV would have been identified between surgeons and that is a limitation of our study. Finally, both cadavers included in this study were used in a previous study that investigated the feasibility of intraosseous endoscopy for inspection of thoracolumbar and lumbar pedicle drill tracts and it is possible that previous passage of a nanoscope through pedicle drill tracts could have damaged or altered the feel of the cancellous bone.

Conclusions

Use of a ball-tipped probe is associated with excellent specificity, excellent sensitivity for detection of medial breaches, and good sensitivity to detect lateral breaches. It is likely that the sensitivity to detect lateral breaches would be improved with greater experience with this technique. Use of a ball-tipped probe to palpate the walls of pedicle drill tracts may be a useful time-efficient adjunct technique during pedicle screw/pin placement in dogs and potentially reduce/avoid the deleterious consequences associated with implant misplacement in the canine thoracolumbar and lumbar spine. Clinical applications for this technique include any conditions that may require vertebral stabilization, including spinal fractures, degenerative lumbosacral stenosis, spinal arachnoid diverticulum, and deformity correction. Further studies are required to investigate this technique in small and medium breeds and to evaluate the accuracy of this technique in clinical cases.

Methods

Sample population

Two skeletally mature greyhound cadavers euthanized for reasons unrelated to the study and donated with owner consent to the primary author’s institution for teaching and research purposes as part of a body donation programme were included. Ethical approval for the study was obtained from University College Dublin animal research ethics committee (AREC-E-23-07-Mullins). Cadavers were stored at -20 degrees Celsius and defrosted 48 h prior to computed tomography (CT). Both of these cadavers and associated drill tracts had been used in a separate study by the same authors that investigated the feasibility of intraosseous endoscopy for inspection of thoracolumbar and lumbar pedicle drill tracts [10].

Preoperative computed tomography

A 16-slice helical CT scanner (SOMATOM Scope, Siemens, Germany) was used in this study. Transverse sections of 0.75-mm slice thickness were obtained from T6 to sacrum. Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine (DICOM) images were exported into medical image viewing software (Horosproject.org; Annapolis, Maryland). After image acquisition, cadavers were refrozen until 3DPGs were designed and printed.

Design and printing of 3DPGs

DICOM images were imported into 3D planning software (Mimics v21, 3-Matic v15, Materialise, Belgium) and virtual models of 3DPGs designed by a board-certified neurologist (J.G.). All guides were bilateral guides and designed as previously described, with a 2.5-mm internal diameter that matched the pilot hole for a 3.2/2.4-mm positive profile pin (Interface pin, IMEX, Longview, TX, USA). Guides were printed by a board-certified small animal surgeon (J.B.) using biocompatible resin using a stereolithography (SLA) printer (Surgical Guide resin, Form 3B, Somerville, MA, USA) with 25-µM XY resolution (horizontal resolution) and 100-µM (0.1 mm) layer height (vertical resolution). 3DPGs were designed to create 2 drill tracts per vertebra, one left and one right, from T7 through L7, equating to a total of 28 drill tracts per cadaver (56 drill tracts in total). Forty-four drill tracts were intended to be grade 1 modified Zdichavsky (no breach), 3 were intended to create a deliberate grade 2a breach (partial medial violation), a further 3 to create a grade 2b breach (full medial violation), another 3 to create a grade 3a breach (partial lateral violation), and a final 3 to create a grade 3b breach (full lateral violation) (Fig. 1). Surgeons performing the probing of drill tracts were blinded to the number and location of deliberate pedicle breaches.

Creation of drill tracts

Cadavers were re-thawed at room temperature for 48 h prior to their use. All drill tracts were created using 3DPGs by a first-year resident-in-training (C.O.). Cadavers were placed in sternal recumbency with the fore limbs extended cranially and the pelvic limbs flexed on either side of the body. A routine dorsal approach to the thoracolumbar and lumbar spine was performed bilaterally from T6 to L7. Epaxial musculature was reflected bilaterally, without disruption of supraspinous or interspinous ligaments. Soft tissues were meticulously removed over areas of bone to ensure complete and precise contact of 3DPGs. Guides were held firmly in position by hand and a 2.5-mm drill bit was used to create a pilot hole through the guide tube.

Assessment of pedicle wall integrity

Probing of each pedicle drill tract was performed independently by 2 board-certified small animal surgeons (R.A.M., M.P.M.) on 2 separate occasions 2 weeks apart. This yielded a total of 224 discrete data points, 112 per surgeon. The probe was passed as many times as necessary to make a decision regarding the integrity of the drill tract. The order in which the pedicles were probed was randomized a priori. Cadavers were positioned on a dissection table in a simulated surgical environment, with regions of cadavers outside of an area of ~ 3 × 3 cm2 centered on the pilot hole draped off with surgical drapes (Fig. 7). For each pedicle drill tract, surgeons were only told whether it was a thoracic or lumbar vertebra being probed. This was done to prevent the surgeon from remembering the status of a pedicle drill tract in round 1 and potentially biasing the grading in round 2. A commercially available ball-tipped probe (Invetra Pedicle Probe, Neuromed, Huddersfield, UK) (Fig. 7) was used to palpate the medial and lateral walls of each drill tract and surgeons made a decision regarding the integrity (grade 1, grade 2a or 2b medial breach, or grade 3a or 3b lateral breach) of the drill tract according to the modified Zdichavsky classification (Fig. 1) [4, 5]. Both surgeons were familiar with this grading classification prior to commencement of the study. The decision of breach versus no breach was based on the feel of the probe as it was brushed against the medial and lateral aspects of the drill tract, the length of the tract and comparison of the length of its medial and lateral aspects, and not the position of the pilot hole or the angle of the probe within the hole. The time taken to determine the grade was recorded and defined as the time in seconds from insertion of the ball-tipped probe into the drill tract until final removal and the recording of the grade in a spreadsheet.

Postoperative CT

After all data were collected, repeat CT examinations were obtained from T6-sacrum using previously described protocol and assessed in Horos. Using MPR, dorsal and transverse plane axes were aligned with each pin tract trajectory and graded on transverse plane images. Grading was performed once by a board-certified neurologist (J.G.) using a modified Zdichavsky classification (Fig. 1) [4, 5].

Statistical analysis

Normality of continuous data was assessed using the Shapiro Wilk test. Continuous data are summarized as median (range). The number of correctly identified breached and non-breached drill tracts was calculated. Specificity (defined as the likelihood that a grade 1 pedicle drill tract was correctly identified), sensitivity (defined as the likelihood that a medial (grade 2a/b) or lateral (grade 3a/b) breach/violation was correctly identified), positive (PPV) and negative (NPV) predictive values were evaluated and round 1 data compared between surgeons using the Fisher’s exact test. PPV was defined as the likelihood that a breach identified at a certain location was truly a breach on postoperative CT, whereas NPV was defined as the likelihood that a grade 1 modified Zdichavsky tract identified at a certain location truly corresponded to a non-breached tract on postoperative CT. Cohen kappa coefficients were used to assess inter- and intraobserver agreement. The medians of the average times from both rounds of surgeons 1 and 2 to assign a modified Zdichavsky grade was compared between surgeons for the thoracic spine (T7-T13), lumbar spine (L1-L7), and overall (T7-L7) using the Wilcoxon Signed Rank test. This averaging of times from round 1 and 2 was performed to avoid artificially increasing the sample size because of repeated measurements, erroneously increasing the power of the hypothesis test, decreasing the p-value and the width of the 95% CI. For all comparisons, p < 0.05 was considered significant. All statistical tests were performed using SPSS Statistics version 27 (IBM, New York, USA).
