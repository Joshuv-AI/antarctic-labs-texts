A parametric survival model with bayesian structural equation based on multi-omics integration
Authors: Jiadong Chu, Yu Wang, Na Sun, Qiang Han, Ziqing Sun, Mengtong Sun, Yuheng Yuan, Qida He, Yueping Shen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMulti-omics integration may provide additional information about the development of tumors and improve the performance of predictive models. The key challenge lies in integrating several omics sources, especially to capture their biological relationships. Previous studies proposed a structural equation model framework to combine two data platforms for predicting survival; however, several limitations remain.ResultsIn this study, we introduce an extended Bayesian survival model combined with a structural equation model for adaptation to broader applications. The No U-turn Sampling (NUTS) algorithm was utilized to efficiently sample the posterior distribution of model parameters. Through a series of simulation studies, our model showed excellent goodness-of-fit and predictive performance. To validate the efficiency of our model, we utilized a gastric cancer dataset with three omics types (mRNA, microRNA, and methylation) obtained from The Cancer Genome Atlas. After bioinformatic processing, we included six mRNA, microRNA, and methylation loci datasets into the framework and discovered that our model exhibited greater predictive performance compared to non-integrated and Integrative Bayesian Analysis of Genomics (iBAG) models.ConclusionsIn conclusion, our extended Bayesian structural equation model for multi-omics survival analysis provides a robust framework that significantly enhances predictive accuracy by effectively capturing complex biological relationships across diverse omics data sources, demonstrating clear advantages over both non-integrated approaches and existing integrative methods like iBAG.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12859-025-06338-3.Keywords: Bayesian framework, Multi-omics, Structural equation model, Cancer, Survival prediction

Introduction

Cancer is the primary cause of disease-related deaths worldwide [1]. Due to poor prognosis, an increasing number of researchers have proposed different predictive models for guiding treatment. Incorporating molecular profiles into computational modeling will considerably improve model prediction accuracy and precision [2, 3]. The development of high-throughput technology enabled the efficient generation of omics data sources, which are readily available from various public databases. Compared to single omics, a multi-omics approach may provide more detailed information about tumor development and progression [4]. Therefore, integrating multi-dimensional data into modeling has gradually become a strategy of interest in cancer prediction research.

However, one major challenge is the lack of suitable statistical approaches for multi-omics data integration. Ritchie et al. [5] systematically discussed two potential methods, i.e., multistage integration strategy and meta-dimensional analysis. The former integrates data from multiple stages, such as using the expression quantitative trait loci (eQTL) to find single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) associated with specific phenotypes [6]. The latter simultaneously combines all data to identify a complex multi-variable structure. Furthermore, from a biological perspective, there are associations among different omics data, such as the similar activity of methylation and microRNA (miRNA) in regulating gene expression.

Based on biological relationships among omics data, integrating genomic data into a statistical model is the most straightforward approach. However, the exact biological relationship may be difficult to confirm, and the setting of latent variables would contribute to better depicting the association between two-omics data. Hence, the structural equation model (SEM), which is commonly used to describe the potential complex relationships of multiple variables (e.g., observed or latent variables), is a potentially suitable method for data integration. SEM has been widely employed in psychology [7], economics [8], and medicine [9]. Traditional SEM utilizes the maximum likelihood approach to perform parameter estimation based on covariance structure analysis. The accuracy of estimates depends on the asymptotic property; thus, using a small-sample size may be unreliable [10]. Subsequently, the SEM based on the Bayesian theory framework (BSEM) is more reliable in case of small-sample evaluations [11, 12]. BSEM utilizes the Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) algorithm to evaluate the posterior distribution of unknown parameters without relying on the assumption of large samples. Moreover, in small-sample contexts, the choice of appropriate prior distribution is particularly important, as it can have a stronger influence on the posterior distribution. This allows Bayesian methods to effectively incorporate prior knowledge, overcoming the limitations imposed by small sample sizes and improving the reliability of the results. Consequently, better small-sample performance is achieved by imposing a suitable prior distribution [13].

In survival modeling, a few studies have been conducted regarding the extension of SEM or BSEM, including the combination of SEM with discrete or continuous survival time [14–19]. Wong et al. [20] first introduced the SEM framework to model The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) molecular data for survival prediction with censoring. However, their model only could consider a single gene at a time. Recently, Maity et al. [21] proposed a BSEM framework involving multi-omics integration for survival prediction. One class of functional genes, the circadian genes, was the focus of their model. Specifically, a two-omics integrated model with a SEM framework was directly established based on an accelerated failure time (AFT) model. However, this method has some limitations. First, the integrative assumption of two omics, , may be inapplicable when additional omics data are included. Specifically, the inclusion of more omics will be a bit complex, thus forming a regression equation involving more unknown parameters. Second, since a log-normal distribution was assumed, other common survival distributions (e.g., Weibull distribution) should also be investigated. Third, prior sensitivity analyses must also be further performed.

In this study, we extended the Bayesian SEM for survival prediction by integrating multi-omics data. Our study was motivated by the work of Maity et al. [21] and aimed to extend this model for adaptation to wider scenarios. Furthermore, it is worth noting that our model’s sole purpose is to capture biological relationships between multi-omics data, like what Wong et al. and Maity et al. proposed. Thus, our formulation of the model is similar in spirit to theirs but focuses on a set of genes that are statistically significant. The remaining part of the article is organized as follows. The Methodology section presents the Bayesian SEM integrating multi-omics data. The Simulation section presents the main simulation results to illustrate the performance of our model. The Real Data Analysis section applies a real gastric cancer dataset from TCGA to fit the proposed model. Finally, the Discussion section provides a discussion of the results in terms of the aim of the study and possible future research directions.

Algorithm implementation

In the Bayesian framework, the inference of the target posterior distribution can be achieved using the MCMC algorithm. Several MCMC methods have been proposed for posterior estimation. The Metropolis-Hastings (MH) method achieves each iteration jump by controlling the acceptance rate [30]. In the high-dimensional case, Gibbs has a greater advantage in terms of speed compared to the MH algorithm based on conditional probability [31]. However, when the posterior distribution is much more complicated, the above approaches both waste a lot of time in useless calculations. A better method has been proposed, namely the Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (HMC) algorithm [32, 33]. Compared to traditional Gibbs, HMC does not rely on sampling from the conditional posterior distribution and reaches convergence status with fewer iterations [34, 35]. However, one concern is the need to pre-set the number of steps in its simulated paths, which determines the length of a single path among all paths in a random direction. Neither too few nor too long steps would lead to appropriate samples for approximating the target distribution, and the latter may lead to a U-turn. Although the length of the steps can be manually adjusted, modification is difficult when the distribution is complex. However, an improved algorithm called No U-turn sampling (NUTS) can adaptively discover a fine number of steps and efficiently explore the target distribution [36]. In this study, Bayesian inference was performed using the NUTS algorithm, implemented via the Rstan package, which is a Stan-based probabilistic programming language for the R environment. The brms package, which is a convenient and efficient tool for Bayesian modeling, was utilized for model construction and verification [37, 38]. The details of NUTS algorithms used in Stan have been reported by Hoffman et al. [36]. See Supplementary file for all R codes.

Sensitivity analysis

In the simulation scenarios above, we assumed an IG distribution on the variance, which is common in the SEM framework. Several prior distributions are available for testing the variance, including the half-Cauchy and Student’s t-distribution [41, 42]. For a full perspective, we conducted a sensitivity analysis to explore the impact of different priors on model estimation and performance. Under the conditions of 50% censor rate, we compared our prior with the others, specifically IG with small hyperparameters (), half-Cauchy, and Student’s t-distribution. The results revealed that our model is not sensitive to different prior settings, although the ‘weak’ information prior we used may be slightly superior. Real data analysis

Model analysis using TCGA stomach adenocarcinoma (TCGA-STAD) datasets

In this section, we employed our integrated model to evaluate the gastric cancer dataset obtained from TCGA [45], which collects multi-omics data for 31 cancer types [46]. Gastric cancer is one of the most common tumors and the third most important cause of cancer-related deaths [47]. Due to cancer heterogeneity, the construction of an accurate predictive model based on omics data is necessary. We downloaded the corresponding clinical survival and genomic data for gastric cancer, specifically on mRNA, miRNA, and methylation. These datasets were downloaded from UCSC Xena [48], which is linked to several large cancer research projects, including TCGA. The DESeq2 package was used to normalize the gene expression profiles [49].

Our study focuses on a few statistically significant genes and their effects on the patients’ survival. To achieve this, differential expressions, univariate Cox, and LASSO regression analyses were subsequently performed. For the univariate Cox regression, p-values were adjusted for multiple testing using Bonferroni adjustments. We obtained six each of the mRNAs, miRNAs, and methylated sites to fit our model (see the Supplementary Materials for more details), which are found to be mainly involved in several metabolic pathways. Age, sex, and stage were regarded as clinical covariates. As the Q-Q plot (Figure S9) did not fully satisfy the assumption of a log-normal distribution, we established our integrated model in line with Weibull distribution and reached convergence after 4000 iterations. All R hat values were below 1.1, and Tail ESS values exceeded 400, indicating satisfactory convergence. Additionally, our model required 569.16 s to reach convergence.

Table 7 presents the estimation results for the different omics data. Interestingly, the results (and ) indicated that methylation and miRNA omics may play a negative role in the expression profile. Furthermore, the suggested that latent mRNA has a negative direct effect on survival time. Notably, the LOOIC (1052.1) and WAIC (1050.3) results of our integrated model were higher than those of the others (Table 8). The MSE of the uncensored cases for the integrated model was 0.7258, showing a better prediction accuracy than the non-integrated and iBAG models. Furthermore, the C-index of the integrated model was 0.7627, which is slightly superior to the other models.

Discussion

In this study, we constructed a generalized Bayesian SEM with multi-omics data integration for improved survival prediction. Our study demonstrated that our proposed SEM model predicts survival better and fits the data better than non-integrated methods and iBAG model. The most important highlight of our proposed model is to capture biological relationships of different omics layers when integrating multi-omics data. Our model focuses only on a few statistically significant genes.

The biological associations among different kinds of omics data must be considered when performing the integration process. For instance, gene expression can either be positively or negatively regulated via methylation and by miRNAs [50, 51]. Furthermore, the accurate relationship among specific genomic data is difficult to confirm, and thus, the application of latent variables in SEM may assist in identifying multi-omics platforms for subtly depicting biological associations. Additionally, a unified integrative framework would reduce model error and improve the prediction accuracy compared to a multistage integrative framework. Notably, the scale of omics data may have a great impact on estimation because of the different genomics measurement methods. Thus, normalizing the omics scale was necessary to fit our model.

Our model with the SEM framework achieved a better fit to the data and showed improved prediction power. Compared to the previous model, we extended our model to accommodate three or more omics by reforming the structural equation. Different survival distributions have been tested for adaptation to varied scenarios when the assumption of log-normal distribution is violated. In addition, instead of Gibbs, the NUTS algorithm was applied to execute the iteration process, thereby obtaining the same accurate estimates within a few finite iterations. Furthermore, a different method of model identification was applied by fixing the first loadings of latent variables to handle the identification issue instead of fixing the variances of latent variables, which could be directly implemented in a Bayesian setting [39]. The simulation study evaluated the impact of sample size, dimensionality, and censoring rates on model performance. The model showed robustness with a sample size of 200 and up to 10 features per omics layer, with minimal impact on parameter estimation. However, computational time increased with larger sample sizes and higher dimensionality. The model also maintained strong performance under censoring rates as high as 80%. Based on these results, dimensionality is recommended to be limited to 10 features per omics layer for efficiency, and censoring rates are advised to remain below 80% to balance performance and computational feasibility.

The selection of appropriate priors for variance was also investigated using sensitivity analysis. Here, the IG prior was adopted, and the hyperparameters were empirically pre-set. Another way to set the value of the hyperparameters is to impose another prior (i.e., hyper prior) and sample from the posterior, which forms the hierarchical structure. Generally, these hyperparameters are directly specified, although theoretically infinite layers might exist in the full Bayesian view. Additionally, comparison of the estimation bias using different priors revealed that our model showed slight superiority, although all bias deviations were small. Notably, the performance of different priors varied across conditions, corroborating the results of a previous study [52]. Therefore, the choice of different priors or hyperparameters must be carefully applied in practice. Additionally, another sensitivity analysis was conducted across three simulated scenarios: nonlinearity, high collinearity, and low collinearity. Results showed the model’s robustness in parameter estimation and prediction accuracy, consistent with the main conclusions. Nonetheless, there is room for improvement. Future work could focus on developing nonparametric extensions to better capture nonlinear relationships and designing more suitable priors to address collinearity [43].

The computational time was also analyzed for both simulation study and real data applications. In the simulation study, time increased with larger sample sizes and higher censoring rate. The log-normal model was slightly faster than the Weibull model, but both were computationally feasible. For real data, the Weibull model took 569.16 s to achieve convergence. While acceptable for practical use, the computational demands of Bayesian sampling increase with model complexity, highlighting the need for faster Bayesian algorithms in future work.

However, several limitations in our study must be addressed in future research. First, the SEM framework assumed of linear regression, implying relative independence within the omics data. In reality, the correlations between or within genomic data are very complicated. Nonlinear relationships among omics data should also be considered [53, 54]. We could add these traits to relax the restrictions in the brms package for further investigation.

Second, the number of different omics data for integration should not be too high, which may lead to non-convergence or unstable estimation, when the sample size is limited. Thus, statistical strategies for reducing the dimension scale, such as the principal components analysis (PCA) and LASSO regression analysis, must be utilized in practice. This approach is similar to a two-phase modeling process [5] that may be used to combine the dimension-reduction process with model fitting. Actually, the measurement equations in the SEM framework can be regarded as part of the exploratory factor analysis (EFA) when involving reducing dimension. For example, previous studies have explored the combination of SEM and LASSO (or Bayesian LASSO) to simultaneously achieve estimation and model selection [55–57]. Thus, survival model fitting with shrinkage priors embedded in the SEM framework may be an interesting topic for future research.

Finally, our model was mainly based on a full parametric survival regression, specifically the AFT model. This required additional assumptions, such as the distribution hypothesis of survival time, compared to semiparametric regression. Therefore, extending the semiparametric survival model (i.e., the Cox proportional hazard model) is necessary for broader application.

Conclusions

In summary, the utilization of latent variables to integrate multi-omics data and to extend a previous model resulted in the construction of our improved model. The SEM framework was used to understand the relationship between different omics platforms in predicting survival. As our model was based on a fully parametric AFT, different survival distributions were tested. Furthermore, our proposed model exhibited a better performance than the other non-integrated and iBAG models.
