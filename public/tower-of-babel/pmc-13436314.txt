White matter 18F-florbetapir retention predicts cognitive decline and treatment response in Alzheimer’s disease
Authors: Huamei Lin, Ying Zhang, Ruiqing Ni, Min Wang, Jianwei Men, Jingjie Ge, Jiaying Lu, Jing Wang, Huiwei Zhang, Yunhao Yang, Zhengwei Zhang, Yuhua Zhu, Hongguang Zhao, Tzu-Chen Yen, Junlong Wang, Kuangyu Shi, Axel Rominger, Yihui Guan, Min Fang, Jintai Yu, Jiehui Jiang, Chuantao Zuo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeWhite matter (WM) signal on ¹⁸F-florbetapir positron emission tomography (PET) is often regarded as nonspecific, yet its biological significance remains unclear. This study aimed to characterize the trajectory, clinical significance, and biomarker correlates of normal-appearing white matter (NAWM) ¹⁸F-florbetapir retention across the Alzheimer’s disease (AD) continuum.MethodsWe analyzed NAWM ¹⁸F-florbetapir retention in 672 participants across the AD continuum from the Alzheimer’s Disease Neuroimaging Initiative and two Chinese cohorts. Longitudinal PET, plasma, and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) biomarkers, as well as lecanemab treatment effects, were evaluated.ResultsNAWM retention followed a distinct trajectory from cortical amyloid, increasing during preclinical stages and plateauing in symptomatic phases. Elevated NAWM ¹⁸F-florbetapir retention independently predicted cognitive decline, correlated with plasma p-tau217 and CSF p-tau/Aβ42 ratio, and showed significant reductions following lecanemab therapy. Combined assessment of cortical and NAWM PET improved diagnostic accuracy for amyloid positivity to 92%. NAWM retention also correlated strongly with plasma and CSF biomarkers in preclinical stages, and mediation analyses indicated that these fluid markers partly explained NAWM–cognition associations.ConclusionNAWM ¹⁸F-florbetapir retention represents a biologically valid and dynamic biomarker of AD progression, with potential utility for early detection, prognostication, and therapeutic monitoring.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13195-026-02098-1.

Introduction

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder affecting an estimated 57 million people globally, with profound social and economic consequences for patients, families, and healthcare systems [1]. The disease is neuropathologically defined by cortical amyloid-β (Aβ) plaque accumulation, currently recognized as a core biomarker in the 2024 National Institute on Aging—Alzheimer’s Association (NIA-AA) research framework [2]. While the traditional cortical-centric understanding of AD has guided diagnostic criteria and therapeutic development for decades [3], treatments targeting cortical Aβ have produced only modest clinical benefits – suggesting that additional pathological mechanisms may contribute to disease progression [4, 5]. This therapeutic gap has intensified interest in understanding pathophysiology beyond cortical involvement.

Positron emission tomography (PET) with Aβ-specific tracers has transformed our ability to visualize and quantify amyloid pathology in vivo. In this regard, 18F-florbetapir (FBP) – a widely used tracer that binds to fibrillar Aβ deposits – can provide critical diagnostic support and permits longitudinal monitoring of disease progression and treatment response. Although FBP PET imaging studies have traditionally concentrated on cortical signal, substantial tracer retention also occurs in white matter (WM) regions. While this finding has been traditionally dismissed as a nonspecific binding or technical artifact [4, 6], accumulating evidence suggests this interpretation may warrant reconsideration [7]. Accordingly, multiple lines of evidence support meaningful WM pathology in AD. For example, post mortem studies have demonstrated significant levels of soluble Aβ40 and Aβ42 in WM independent of cortical plaque density [8]. Furthermore, baseline WM hyperintensities (WMHs) on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) have been shown to predict subsequent cortical Aβ deposition in longitudinal studies [9]. In clinical AD cohorts, WM pathology has been associated with cortical thinning [10], glucose hypometabolism [10, 11], tau deposition [12, 13], and cognitive decline [14–16]. These findings collectively indicate that WM pathology may represent a fundamental aspect of AD pathophysiology rather than merely a secondary consequence of vascular injury [4, 17]. A particularly intriguing observation from recent PET studies is the paradoxical finding that WMHs are characterized by reduced Aβ tracer retention compared with normal-appearing white matter (NAWM), defined as WM regions that appear normal on conventional MRI(e.g., T2-FLAIR) without visible hyperintense lesions, yet may harbor subtle microstructural abnormalities [4, 5, 18, 19]. These results suggest that tracer signal in NAWM may reflect early pathological processes – potentially including myelin disruption and axonal dysfunction – before overt tissue damage manifests [14, 20]. Despite this provocative hypothesis, the temporal dynamics of NAWM FBP retention across disease stages, its relationship to cognitive trajectories, responsiveness to anti-Aβ immunotherapy, and correlation with CSF and plasma biomarkers in AD patients have not been systematically investigated.

To address these limitations, we designed a multi-cohort study using data from the Alzheimer’s Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI; n = 546) and two Chinese cohorts (n = 126) to test three working hypotheses: first, that NAWM FBP retention may follow stage-specific patterns distinct from cortical accumulation; second, that baseline retention can predict cognitive decline and response to anti-Aβ treatment; and third, that NAWM retention associations with traditional CSF and plasma AD biomarkers.

Materials and methods

Participants

This study included a total of 672 participants pooled from three independent sources: 546 individuals from the ADNI database and 126 from two Chinese clinical centers (Huashan Hospital, Fudan University, Shanghai; First Hospital of Jilin University, Changchun). From ADNI, we enrolled participants who had undergone concurrent Aβ PET, T1-weighted, and T2-weighted fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (T2-FLAIR) MRI. A flowchart of the ADNI cohort selection is provided in Supplementary Fig. 1; full inclusion and exclusion criteria are available at http://adni.loni.usc.edu/methods/documents/.

Within the Huashan cohort, 12 participants with cognitive impairment received lecanemab treatment (lecanemab treatment group) and completed both baseline and follow-up Aβ PET scans at a median treatment duration of one year. For comparison, 18 cognitively impaired participants from the same center who did not receive lecanemab were included as a control group (control group) and similarly underwent Aβ PET imaging at baseline and one-year follow-up. Detailed diagnostic criteria and comprehensive inclusion/exclusion criteria for all Chinese cohorts are provided in Supplementary Method 1 [2]. Patients in the lecanemab treatment group received lecanemab (LEQEMBI®), a humanized IgG1 monoclonal antibody targeting aggregated soluble protofibrillar Aβ. The complete dosing regimen, formulation details (including concentration and vehicle), and administration procedures followed the standard clinical protocol and are described in Supplementary Method 2.

All procedures conformed to the tenets of the Helsinki Declaration and the study protocol was approved by the Huashan Hospital, Shanghai (reference: KY2021555; Huashan cohort), and the First Hospital of Jilin University, Changchun (reference: 24k225-001; JLU-FH cohort). All participants or legal guardians provided written informed consent.

Imaging acquisition and processing

All participants underwent FBP PET and 3T structural MRI (T1-weighted and T2-FLAIR). For the ADNI cohort, PET data were acquired using scanners qualified by the ADNI PET Core under standardized multicenter protocols, including phantom calibration and ongoing quality control procedures to ensure cross-site and longitudinal consistency.

Across ADNI phases, acquisition parameters were harmonized, including an injected dose of ~ 370 MBq, a 50-minute uptake period, and a 20-minute dynamic scan (4 × 5-minute frames), with image reconstruction using OSEM and attenuation correction according to ADNI specifications.

The Chinese cohorts were scanned using the same PET/CT platform (Siemens Biograph mCT), with acquisition and reconstruction protocols harmonized to match ADNI procedures, thereby minimizing inter-site variability.

All PET images were processed using a unified pipeline, including co-registration to individual MRI, spatial normalization to MNI space, RBV-based partial volume correction, and spatial smoothing. Standardized uptake value ratios (SUVRs) were calculated using the whole cerebellum as the reference region. Key analyses were also performed within individual cohorts to confirm the consistency of findings across datasets.

Specifically, T1-weighted images were segmented into gray matter (GM), WM, and CSF compartments (SPM12), with spatial normalization to MNI space via DARTEL. WMHs were segmented from T2-FLAIR using the Lesion Segmentation Toolbox, and subsequent analyses of NAWM excluded WMH voxels.

PET preprocessing included co-registration to the individual T1-weighted MRI, correction for partial volume effects [21], normalization to MNI space, and spatial smoothing. Partial volume correction was conducted using the region-based voxel-wise (RBV) algorithm implemented in the PETPVE toolbox. This approach integrates subject-specific tissue probability maps (GM, WM, and CSF) with spatially matched regional uptake estimates to model and mitigate voxel-level spill-in and spill-out arising from limited PET spatial resolution. By combining anatomical segmentations with regional activity constraints, the RBV procedure yields quantitatively enhanced PET signals that more closely reflect the underlying tracer distribution. Amyloid positivity was defined using the ADNI-derived cutoff of 1.11 SUVR, corresponding to approximately 20 Centiloids and validated in a multisite study [22], which has been shown to be robust across variations in cortical composite definitions. Global cortical (CTX) and NAWM SUVRs were extracted from six bilateral meta-regions (frontal, parietal, temporal, occipital, cingulate, limbic) defined by the Desikan–Killiany atlas. The comprehensive list of constituent sub-regions for each meta-ROI is provided in the Supplementary Method 3. Critically, all NAWM ROIs were meticulously defined after the exclusion of WMH voxels.

For clinical reads, three blinded nuclear medicine physicians independently rated Aβ PET scans, classifying them as positive or negative according to established assessment protocols.

Cerebrospinal fluid and plasma biomarkers

CSF biomarkers (Aβ42, p-tau, t-tau) were quantified using Elecsys electrochemiluminescence immunoassays (Roche, Basel, Switzerland; intra- and interassay coefficients of variation < 5%). Plasma biomarker quantification included Aβ42, Aβ40, p-tau181, p-tau217, glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP), and neurofilament light chain (NfL) via the Simoa HD-1 platform (Quanterix, Billerica, MA, USA) [23, 24]. Plasma Aβ42, Aβ40, p-tau181, and p-tau217 concentrations were quantified in pg/mL, whereas GFAP and NfL levels were expressed in ng/mL, in accordance with ADNI conventions based on observed concentration ranges. The Aβ42/40 ratio was expressed as a unitless metric. Sample processing followed ADNI and local biobank standard operating procedures under centralized quality control. Comprehensive methodological details for the ADNI cohort are accessible at http://adni.loni.usc.edu/methods/.

Clinical evaluations

We assessed cognitive function using Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) and Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) scales in all cohorts. Multidisciplinary expert panels established clinical diagnoses by reviewing neuropsychological testing, functional assessments, and neuroimaging findings. ADNI-specific protocols are detailed at http://adni.loni.usc.edu/methods/.

Immunohistochemistry for Aβ detection in postmortem human brain tissue

Post mortem temporal lobe tissue was obtained from the Netherlands Brain Bank, including nine neuropathologically confirmed AD cases and four cognitively normal controls (Supplementary Table 1), with informed consent and ethical approval. Formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded sections were prepared and processed with standard antigen retrieval, permeabilization, and blocking procedures. Sections were incubated with a monoclonal anti-Aβ antibody (4G8), followed by biotinylated secondary antibody and signal amplification using an avidin–biotin–peroxidase system with DAB as chromogen. Slides were dehydrated, mounted, and digitized at 20× magnification under standardized conditions [25]. Quantification of Aβ plaques in gray and white matter was performed using QuPath and ImageJ with consistent thresholds [26]. Among analyzed cases, Aβ immunohistochemistry data were available for 4 controls and 9 AD cases in gray matter, and for 3 controls and 7 AD cases in white matter, with paired GM and WM data available in the majority of AD cases.

Statistical analysis

Group differences in CTX and NAWM SUVRs were assessed using ANOVA with Bonferroni correction, and longitudinal changes with paired t-tests. Longitudinal changes (ΔSUVR, follow-up − baseline) were further assessed using linear models within an ANCOVA framework, with baseline SUVR included as a covariate to account for potential confounding effects of baseline amyloid burden. Diagnostic performance of CTX, NAWM, and combined SUVRs was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and area under the ROC curves (AUCs), with thresholds derived from ADNI and validated in the Chinese cohorts. Associations between imaging markers, cognition, and fluid biomarkers were examined with Spearman correlations, and mediation analyses tested whether fluid biomarkers explained SUVR–cognition relationships (5,000 bootstraps, adjusted for age and sex).

Prognostic value was evaluated using Cox regression models, defining conversion as cognitive decline below MMSE or MoCA cut-offs (≤ 26) [27], with risk groups stratified by predicted conversion rates. Kaplan–Meier survival curves and log-rank tests compared conversion risk, and hazard ratios quantified biomarker effects. SUVR–cognition trajectories were modeled using non-linear regression. All analyses were performed in R (v4.1.0), with two-tailed p < 0.05 considered significant.

Demographics of participants

The analysis included 672 participants from three cohorts: 546 from the ADNI and 126 from two Chinese clinical centers (Huashan Hospital, Shanghai, and First Hospital of Jilin University, Changchun). Among the ADNI participants, 205 had longitudinal imaging data available (median follow-up: 5 years). Based on amyloid status (SUVR ≥ 1.11, whole cerebellum reference) [28] and clinical diagnosis, ADNI participants were categorized into four groups: Aβ-negative cognitively normal (CN−, n = 106), Aβ-positive CN (CN+, n = 183), Aβ-positive mild cognitive impairment (MCI+, n = 161), and Aβ-positive Alzheimer’s disease dementia (AD+, n = 96). The Chinese cohorts contributed 126 participants: 112 from Huashan Hospital (Wuzhong Road Branch; 10 CN−, 7 CN+, 43 MCI+, 52 AD+), 7 from Huashan Hospital (Hongqiao Branch; 2 MCI+, 5 AD+), and 7 from the First Hospital of Jilin University (3 MCI+, 4 AD+). Difference in white matter and cortical amyloid pathology along the Alzheimer’s disease continuum

Two representative CN+ cases with identical cortical Aβ SUVRs (1.14) exhibited divergent outcomes: the case with higher NAWM retention (ΔSUVR = 0.14) showed greater cognitive decline over 24 months (MMSE/MoCA Δ = − 2), whereas the other remained stable (Δ = 0) (Fig. 2A, B).

Post mortem temporal lobe analysis confirmed prominent Aβ deposition in both GM and WM of AD cases, absent in controls (Fig. 2C; Supplementary Fig. 2). Quantitative comparisons showed higher plaque burden in AD versus CN in GM (p = 0.0028) and WM (p = 0.0167). A positive correlation between WM and GM plaque burden was observed (r = 0.78, p = 0.0105) (Fig. 2D ~ F); however, given the small sample size (n = 7 AD, n = 3 controls) and the heterogeneous distribution of WM values, this relationship should be interpreted with caution and considered preliminary.

Across the AD continuum (CN−, CN+, MCI+, AD+), both CTX Aβ SUVR and NAWM FBP retention differed significantly (all p < 0.0001), except between CN + and MCI + for CTX (p < 0.001) and for NAWM (ns) (Fig. 2G, H). Regional analysis revealed distinct patterns: CTX Aβ SUVR increased progressively in frontal, parietal, temporal, and cingulate regions (all p < 0.001), with smaller changes in occipital and limbic areas. NAWM retention was elevated in frontal, parietal, temporal, and occipital regions from CN − to MCI+, but temporal and occipital NAWM showed no further increase between CN + and MCI+. Notably, NAWM in the cingulate and limbic regions continued to rise across CN+, MCI+, and AD+ (Supplementary Fig. 3). Analyses in deep white matter regions distant from cortex (centrum semiovale, cerebellar white matter, pons) showed persistently higher FBP retention in Aβ-positive versus Aβ-negative individuals (all p < 0.0001), suggesting that NAWM signal cannot be fully explained by cortical spill-over (Supplementary Fig. 4).

Longitudinal imaging reveals progressive CTX Aβ accumulation and NAWM FBP retention across the AD spectrum

To characterize the temporal evolution of Aβ accumulation, FBP PET analyses were performed in four representative cases across the AD spectrum (CN−, CN+, MCI+, AD+). The CN− case demonstrated negligible longitudinal changes in both cortical Aβ SUVR (baseline: 0.98; follow-up: 0.99) and NAWM FBP retention (baseline: 0.92; follow-up: 0.97) (Fig. 3A1, A2). Conversely, the CN+ case exhibited substantial progression in cortical Aβ SUVR (1.21 to 1.28) and NAWM FBP retention (1.55 to 1.60) during the observation period (Fig. 3B1, B2). The MCI+ case likewise showed progressive increases in cortical Aβ SUVR (1.21 to 1.34) and NAWM FBP retention (1.46 to 1.60) (Fig. 3C1, C2). The AD+ case displayed continued amyloid accumulation in both CTX (1.30 to 1.37) and NAWM region (1.43 to 1.54) (Fig. 3D1, D2), notwithstanding elevated baseline values.

Longitudinal intergroup analyses demonstrated that among CN participants (CN−: n = 50, and CN+: n = 84), both CTX Aβ SUVR and NAWM FBP retention increased significantly from baseline to follow-up (CTX: p < 0.001; NAWM: p < 0.01) (Fig. 3E). Among cognitively impaired participants (MCI+: n = 53, and AD+: n = 18), comparable longitudinal progression was observed for CTX Aβ SUVR (p < 0.001) and NAWM FBP retention (p < 0.01) (Fig. 3F).

Regional analyses demonstrated consistent longitudinal progression patterns. Among cognitively unimpaired participants, follow-up CTX Aβ SUVR values exceeded baseline measurements in the frontal lobe (p < 0.0001), parietal lobe (p < 0.001), temporal lobe (p < 0.001), occipital lobe (p < 0.001), and cingulate gyrus (p < 0.01), whereas the limbic system showed no significant change (Supplementary Fig. 5A1 ~ A6). Parallel increases in NAWM FBP retention were observed in frontal (p < 0.001), parietal, temporal, and occipital areas (all p < 0.01), while cingulate gyrus and limbic system NAWM remained stable (Supplementary Fig. 5B1 ~ B6).

Among cognitively impaired participants, CTX Aβ SUVR increased significantly in all evaluated cortical regions – with the exception of limbic structures (all p < 0.001; Supplementary Fig. 6A1 ~ A6). NAWM FBP retention demonstrated significant increases in frontal, parietal, temporal, and occipital regions (all p < 0.01), without significant alterations in the cingulate gyrus or limbic regions (Supplementary Fig. 6B1 ~ B6).

To examine the associations between global Aβ burden and cognitive function, non-linear regression curves were fitted across the AD continuum. CTX Aβ SUVR demonstrated a triphasic pattern: initial stability in CN− individuals (MMSE > 27), subsequent progressive accumulation (MMSE ≤ 27), and ultimate plateau formation (MMSE ≤ 25), consistent with a saturation phenomenon (Fig. 3G; r = 0.504, p < 0.0001). An analogous trajectory was observed for NAWM FBP retention, characterized by stability among CN− participants (MMSE > 28), progressive accumulation (MMSE ≤ 28), and plateau at advanced cognitive impairment (MMSE ≤ 26) (Fig. 3H; r = 0.367, p < 0.0001).

To further assess whether these associations were influenced by potential confounders, we performed partial correlation analyses in the ADNI cohort adjusting for age and APOE ε4 status. After adjustment, global and regional NAWM FBP retention remained significantly associated with both MMSE and MoCA scores across all regions (all p < 0.0001). The strength of these associations was largely preserved (r ranging from − 0.25 to − 0.43), indicating that the relationship between NAWM retention and cognitive impairment is robust and not attributable to these confounding factors. Detailed results are provided in Supplementary Table 5.

An increased global CTX Aβ SUVR and NAWM FBP retention demonstrated robust associations with accelerated progression (NAWM: p < 0.0001, Fig. 5A; CTX: p < 0.0001, Fig. 5B). Among plasma biomarkers, elevated phosphorylated-tau 217 (p-tau217) concentrations emerged as a significant predictor of clinical deterioration over time (p = 0.0002, Fig. 5C). In addition, an elevated p-tau/Aβ42 ratio in the CSF demonstrated a significant association with accelerated disease conversion (p = 0.0016, Fig. 5D).

A comprehensive evaluation of an expanded biomarker panel yielded concordant findings. Accordingly, carriage of the APOE ε4 allele was associated with an increased risk of progression (p < 0.0001, Supplementary Fig. 7A). Reduced plasma Aβ42/40 ratios predicted increased risk of progression (p = 0.0002, Supplementary Fig. 7B). In CSF, elevated p-tau (p = 0.0016, Supplementary Fig. 7D), total tau (t-tau) (p = 0.0016, Supplementary Fig. 7E), and reduced Aβ42 concentrations (p = 0.0014, Supplementary Fig. 7F) similarly demonstrated significant associations with accelerated conversion.

Analysis of demographic variables revealed that female sex (p < 0.0001, Supplementary Fig. 7C) and advanced age (p = 0.0421, Supplementary Fig. 7G) were associated with increased risk of clinical progression. Conversely, plasma p-tau181 (p = 0.1227, Supplementary Fig. 7H), Aβ40 (p = 0.1660, Supplementary Fig. 7I), Aβ42 (p = 0.6831, Supplementary Fig. 7J), GFAP (p = 0.1056, Supplementary Fig. 7K), and NfL (p = 0.1409, Supplementary Fig. 7L) failed to demonstrate statistically significant differences in survival curves between HR and LR groups.

These findings were substantiated through HR analyses (Fig. 5E), which confirmed that CTX Aβ SUVR and NAWM FBP retention, CSF biomarkers, plasma p-tau217, plasma Aβ42/40 ratio, age, and APOE ε4 served as robust predictors of clinical progression (all p < 0.05), whereas plasma NfL, GFAP, Aβ42, Aβ40, and p-tau181 lacked significant predictive value (all p > 0.05). In additional multivariable analyses, inclusion of global NAWM FBP retention did not materially change risk stratification beyond cortical Aβ and fluid biomarkers, likely reflecting shared variance between these measures.

When MoCA served as the outcome measure, the CSF p-tau/Aβ42 ratio remained a significant mediator (β = -2.3569, 95% CI: -4.4905 to -0.4014; Fig. 6A4).

Discussion

While prior studies have suggested that FBP retention in white matter reflects myelin integrity and is associated with AD progression [4], our study provides critical advancements in both scope and implication. Challenging the conventional view that WM FBP uptake represents nonspecific background signal, we demonstrate for the first time that FBP retention in NAWM constitutes a distinct, clinically meaningful PET biomarker across the AD continuum. Specifically, this NAWM signal exhibits a unique temporal progression, responds to anti-amyloid therapy, independently predicts cognitive decline, and is associated strongly with established AD fluid biomarkers. We also identified a striking divergence between the regional dynamics of NAWM FBP retention from those of cortical Aβ plaque accumulation. While both pathologies increased along the continuum from amyloid-negative cognitively unimpaired subjects to amyloid-positive patients with AD, their trajectories differed fundamentally. On the one hand, cortical Aβ deposition progressed steadily across most brain regions through all disease stages. On the other hand, NAWM FBP retention appeared to increase in the frontal, parietal, temporal, and occipital regions during early disease phases (i.e., during the transition from the amyloid-negative cognitively unimpaired status to the CN+/MCI+) – followed by a plateauing pattern during symptomatic stages. This progression was even more restricted in the cingulate gyrus and limbic system, where it halted entirely after the CN+ stage.

To provide supportive histopathological context for our in vivo NAWM FBP findings, we analyzed post-mortem temporal lobe tissue from AD and CN individuals. Immunohistochemistry confirmed substantial Aβ plaque signal in both GM and adjacent subcortical WM in AD cases, with a significant correlation between WM and GM plaque burden. We acknowledge that these WM samples were obtained from regions proximate to the cortex. Thus, while this analysis confirms the presence of Aβ pathology in WM and its correlation with cortical pathology, it cannot definitively establish that the in vivo FBP signal in deeper NAWM regions reflects intrinsic WM amyloid per se, nor fully disentangle contributions from partial volume effects and regional differences in white matter composition. In addition, the WM plaque burden data show considerable inter-individual variability, with some AD cases overlapping with control levels and the observed correlation potentially influenced by a limited number of high-value cases. Therefore, this relationship should be interpreted cautiously and requires validation in larger post-mortem cohorts. Nonetheless, these post-mortem observations provide convergent evidence that the in vivo NAWM FBP signal is, at least in part, biologically meaningful and related to underlying Aβ pathology, rather than solely attributable to nonspecific binding. Importantly, this interpretation is further supported by multiple complementary strategies in our in vivo analyses, including rigorous partial volume correction (RBV algorithm) and the exclusion of WMH regions, which together minimize confounding from gray matter spill-in and overt vascular or demyelinating pathology. In addition, the observed biological associations with fluid biomarkers and the distinct temporal trajectory of NAWM FBP retention further support a specific contribution of amyloid-related processes.

A legitimate concern in interpreting NAWM FBP retention is the potential contribution of nonspecific tracer binding and myelin-related signals. In vitro studies using PiB have suggested nonsaturable binding in white matter, raising the possibility that WM retention may partly reflect nonspecific interactions [29]. However, several lines of evidence indicate that NAWM FBP retention in our study is biologically meaningful. First, post-mortem immunohistochemistry demonstrated clear Aβ plaque deposition in WM, with a correlation between WM and cortical plaque burden (Fig. 2C–F), suggesting that part of the in vivo signal reflects genuine Aβ pathology. Second, we applied rigorous PVC and defined NAWM after excluding WMH, minimizing spill-in and confounding from overt tissue damage. Importantly, the persistence of group differences in deep white matter regions (e.g., centrum semiovale, cerebellar white matter, and pons) further supports that the NAWM signal is not solely driven by cortical spill-in effects. Third, NAWM FBP retention showed robust associations with core AD fluid biomarkers and predicted cognitive decline independently of cortical Aβ. Nevertheless, emerging evidence suggests that NAWM FBP retention may also reflect myelin-related processes. Lower FBP retention has been associated with reduced myelin integrity and increased neuroaxonal injury markers [4], while experimental studies indicate that myelin dysfunction may facilitate Aβ deposition [6]. Taken together, NAWM FBP retention likely represents a composite signal integrating both amyloid-related pathology and myelin alterations. Future multimodal studies combining Aβ PET with myelin-sensitive MRI and neuroinflammatory imaging will be essential to disentangle these contributions.

The observed dynamic of an early increase followed by stabilization suggests that NAWM FBP retention can mirror an initial pathological cascade, likely reflecting incipient WM injury – such as myelin or axonal damage [4, 6, 20, 30–34]– that precedes widespread cortical Aβ plaque formation. This hypothesis is substantiated by prior histopathological reports of Aβ accumulation in perivascular spaces [35] and WM tracts [32], which may indicate impaired clearance or early neuroinflammation [36, 37]. A potential paradox arises if FBP retention in white matter is interpreted as reflecting myelin integrity, which is known to decline with AD progression. However, our findings suggest that NAWM FBP retention represents a composite signal. In early disease stages, processes that increase tracer retention – including amyloid deposition in white matter, alterations at the myelin – axon interface, and neuroinflammatory changes – may outweigh the opposing effects of demyelination. Consistent with this interpretation, prior studies have shown reduced FBP retention in demyelinated lesions (e.g., WMH), whereas NAWM retains higher signal. We therefore propose a dynamic, potentially biphasic trajectory, in which NAWM FBP retention increases in early stages due to Aβ- and inflammation-related processes, followed by a possible decline at later stages as demyelination and tissue loss predominate. In our cohort, we observed the early increase and plateau but not a decline, likely reflecting the disease stages represented in our sample. This framework may help reconcile previously conflicting interpretations of white matter Aβ PET signals.

In longitudinal analyses, both CTX Aβ and NAWM FBP signals were found to intensify in cognitively unimpaired and impaired participants, confirming a parallel evolution of gray and WM pathology [32, 38, 39]. A key finding of this study is that NAWM FBP retention provides prognostic information beyond conventional cortical measures. Among CN+ individuals with identical global CTX Aβ loads, a higher NAWM FBP retention was associated with substantially faster decline [39–41]. Survival analyses confirmed that elevated baseline NAWM FBP retention was a potent predictor of clinical progression, with hazard ratios comparable to those of established CSF biomarkers [42] and plasma p-tau217 [43, 44]. In multivariable analyses, the effect of NAWM FBP retention was attenuated after accounting for cortical Aβ and fluid biomarkers, likely reflecting shared underlying pathophysiology [4, 20, 30, 37, 42, 45].

The clinical relevance of this biomarker is further underscored by its responsiveness to therapy [46, 47]. In the patients who had been treated with lecanemab in our study, we observed significant and concordant reductions in both CTX Aβ SUVR and NAWM FBP signals. This parallel response confirms that NAWM FBP retention is not a static epiphenomenon but a dynamic signal linked to Aβ pathobiology [48], supporting its potential utility as a pharmacodynamic marker [46, 49]. However, the sample size of the treated group (n = 12) is modest, these findings should be interpreted as preliminary. Larger longitudinal cohorts, particularly at preclinical stages, will be required to validate the reproducibility and generalizability of NAWM FBP retention as a treatment-responsive biomarker.

Mechanistically, our results position NAWM FBP retention as an early event in the AD molecular cascade, likely reflecting pathology at the axon-myelin interface [7]. This interpretation is supported by recent proteomic and imaging studies revealing that Aβ can accumulate as fibrillar deposits within the innermost myelin layers and periaxonal space, even in the absence of overt demyelination. Such accumulations may disrupt axo-myelin signaling and ion channel function, leading to the axonal injury evidenced by paranodal expansion and spheroid formation in post mortem AD brains and transgenic mouse models [34, 50–53].

Consistent with these findings, we found that global NAWM FBP retention was associated significantly with established fluid biomarkers of AD pathology – including plasma p-tau217, GFAP, Aβ42/40, and CSF Aβ42, p-tau, and p-tau/Aβ42 ratios – in both the CN + and MCI+ groups. Although some correlation coefficients were modest in magnitude (e.g., r = 0.30 ~ 0.50), such magnitudes are expected when comparing PET measures with soluble biomarkers from different biological compartments, particularly in early disease stages. Importantly, the independent prognostic value and significant mediation effects observed in our analyses support the clinical relevance of these associations. Notably, these correlations were robust within the preclinical (CN + and MCI+) cohort. Furthermore, mediation analyses showed that the associations of both CTX Aβ SUVR and NAWM FBP retention with cognitive decline (MMSE and MoCA scores) were significantly mediated by fluid biomarkers (i.e., plasma p-tau217, CSF Aβ42, CSF p-tau/Aβ42 ratio) – confirming their functional interdependence [54–57]. Collectively, these data biologically anchor NAWM Aβ tracer retention within the core pathophysiological framework of AD, linking it to processes like impaired glymphatic clearance [58] and early vascular dysfunction [59]. It is also plausible that the interaction of Aβ with WM integrity may create a feedback loop that precipitates amyloid propagation and cognitive vulnerability [34, 60–63].

Our results fundamentally challenge the conventional view that WM alterations represent secondary or nonspecific consequences of AD pathology. Instead, our data establish WM changes as primary drivers of disease initiation and progression. The discovery of Aβ fibrils beneath intact myelin sheaths, accompanied by profound proteomic dysregulation at the axon-myelin interface, provides evidence that subcellular amyloid accumulation may directly undermine axonal integrity [7]. Notably, this process appears to trigger tau-mediated pathological cascades, establishing a detrimental positive feedback cycle that accelerates neurodegeneration [34]. These mechanistic insights validate NAWM FBP retention as both an early sentinel marker and an independent predictor of disease trajectory, while demanding a fundamental reconceptualization of WM’s role in AD pathogenesis.

The clinical translation of these findings offers immediate diagnostic advantages. When integrated with conventional cortical PET analysis, NAWM FBP retention assessment provides superior diagnostic discrimination [62, 64, 65], with particular value in detecting early-stage pathology. Accordingly, the analysis of the ADNI cohort revealed that combining global CTX Aβ SUVR with NAWM FBP retention significantly improved the classification of cognitively unimpaired individuals with early amyloid pathology – a capability that neither marker achieved independently. This synergistic benefit proved most pronounced when cortical Aβ levels approached diagnostic decision thresholds [66], precisely where clinical uncertainty is greatest.

In the current study, real-world validation through multicenter visual assessment across a Chinese cohort confirmed that the combined evaluation of CTX Aβ SUVR and NAWM FBP retention achieved diagnostic accuracies reaching 92%, consistently outperforming single-parameter approaches. In this scenario, the established practice of dismissing WM signal on Aβ PET as technical artifact or employing it merely as a reference standard [67, 68] fundamentally undervalues its biological significance. Conversely, based on the available data [4, 69] and our current results, we believe that NAWM FBP retention should serve as an essential component of the AD biomarker armamentarium, particularly for preclinical detection and therapeutic monitoring applications [70].

Despite rigorous partial volume correction and cross-site harmonization, residual confounding from scanner heterogeneity or registration artifacts cannot be fully excluded. NAWM FBP retention likely reflects multiple processes beyond amyloid deposition, including neuroinflammation, gliosis, and vascular dysfunction, which may influence tracer kinetics. Multimodal studies integrating Aβ PET with neuroinflammation and WM microstructural imaging will be needed to disentangle these contributions [71]. Furthermore, our finding that NAWM FBP retention increases with disease progression—whether due to genuine white matter pathology, partial volume effects, or both—has important implications for the use of white matter as a reference region in longitudinal SUVR analyses. As white matter signal may not remain stable over time, this could introduce bias in reference-normalized measurements and should be carefully considered in study design and interpretation. Finally, although responsiveness to lecanemab is encouraging, larger longitudinal treatment cohorts, particularly at preclinical stages, are required to validate its predictive and monitoring value.

Conclusion

In conclusion, NAWM FBP retention represents a promising biomarker across the AD spectrum, distinguished by its early onset, prognostic value beyond cortical Aβ, associations with fluid biomarkers, responsiveness to anti-Aβ therapy, and improved diagnostic accuracy when combined with cortical Aβ, particularly in early disease. By capturing early WM pathology, it offers potential for enhancing early detection, risk stratification, and therapeutic monitoring. Future studies should clarify underlying mechanisms and integrate this marker into multimodal diagnostic frameworks.
