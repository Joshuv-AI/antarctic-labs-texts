Hypersensitivity reaction risk when restarting cenobamate after discontinuation: Insights from a prolonged hospitalized cohort
Authors: Marina Feldman, Andreas Alexopoulos, Juan Bulacio, Dileep Nair, Vineet Punia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo evaluate the risk of hypersensitivity reactions, including dermatological rashes, when cenobamate (CNB) is restarted at maintenance doses after temporary discontinuation.MethodsWe leveraged the natural experiment of long antiseizure medication discontinuation during intracranial EEG evaluation and conducted a retrospective cohort study of these patients at a tertiary epilepsy center who were taking CNB at admission. Clinical data, including duration of interruption, restart dose, and occurrence of dermatologic or hypersensitivity adverse events, were extracted from electronic health records. Patients were followed during hospitalization and for 2 weeks postdischarge.ResultsThirty‐two patients (53% female; median age 29 years) were included. CNB was withheld for a median of 7 days (IQR 5–8) and restarted at a median dose of 200 mg/day (IQR 193.75–250), including 25% restarted at ≥300 mg/day. Four patients had interruptions ≥10 days. Fourteen patients (44%) had a history of rash. No hypersensitivity reactions attributable to CNB, including rash, were observed during hospitalization or follow‐up.SignificanceThese findings provide the first evidence that short‐term CNB interruptions may not require retitration in previously tolerant patients. This has direct implications for managing nonadherence in routine clinical care. Prospective studies are needed to confirm safety across larger cohorts and longer interruptions.Plain Language SummaryMany people with epilepsy miss doses of their seizure medicines, but it is unclear whether cenobamate needs to be slowly restarted after a short break. INTRODUCTION

Antiseizure medication (ASM) nonadherence is common in epilepsy. It is observed in 20%–50% of people with epilepsy (PWE), frequently leading to ASM interruptions. 1 Simulation modeling studies demonstrate that optimal remedial strategies after a missed or delayed ASM dose depend on the duration of interruption, dosing interval, and drug half‐life. 2 Multiple ASM doses are missed in around one‐third of patients. 3 Restarting ASMs after several missed doses introduces a clinical dilemma, especially in aromatic ASMs associated with hypersensitivity reactions. Their rapid titration increases the risk for hypersensitivity reactions, including skin rashes. 4 Therefore, after brief nonadherence, should clinicians resume the maintenance dose or retitrate at a lower dose to reduce the risk of hypersensitivity reactions? The lamotrigine FDA label provides the only explicit guidance for restart scenarios. It recommends that initial dosing recommendations be followed if discontinuation exceeds 5 half‐lives, given that 97% of the drug is washed out of the body. 5 This recommendation is based on pharmacokinetic principles rather than empirical safety data. The medical literature provides no data on dermatologic risk after reinitiating previously tolerated ASMs following short‐term discontinuation.

Cenobamate (CNB) has emerged as one of the most effective new ASMs, with widespread clinical use. 6 , 7 , 8 During early clinical development, a fast titration schedule was associated with hypersensitivity reactions, including a case of DRESS. 9 These events informed the current slow titration protocol, associated with a very low incidence of rash. 10 , 11 FDA prescribing information for CNB does not provide explicit guidance on resuming therapy after missed doses. The established relationship between titration rate and hypersensitivity risk raises a high level of concern when restarting CNB at a maintenance dose after several missed doses. However, this risk remains untested. Such a question cannot be answered ethically through prospective dosing experiments.

Epilepsy monitoring unit (EMU) admissions, during which ASMs are often temporarily discontinued, provide a natural environment for studying this issue. Scalp EEG evaluations typically last a mean of 4.5 days and a median of 5 days. 12 , 13 CNB's long half‐life (50–60 h) limits scalp EMU usefulness as an experimental model. In contrast, intracranial EEG evaluations are substantially longer, allowing for more complete CNB washout before reinitiation. Therefore, they provide a unique opportunity to examine whether restarting CNB at the maintenance dose after temporary discontinuation increases rash risk. This study leverages that clinical setting to address a critical gap in literature of high clinical relevance. We evaluated the dermatologic safety of CNB reinitiation at a maintenance dose in patients undergoing intracranial EEG evaluation.

METHODS

Following institutional review board approval, a retrospective chart review was conducted of all patients who underwent intracranial EEG evaluation at the Cleveland Clinic Epilepsy Center from January 1, 2020 to October 31, 2025. Among them, we identified those taking CNB at the time of admission.

An extensive electronic health record (EHR) review was conducted for patients who met the above criteria. Details about ASMs, including historical use, at the time of hospital admission and discharge, were extracted. The duration and timing of CNB discontinuation and restart during admission, along with its dosing at various timepoints, were extracted. Details on the history of hypersensitivity reactions and rashes, including the inciting causes and the development of any such adverse effects during hospital admission, were extracted. Additionally, we monitored for 2 weeks postdischarge adverse events by reviewing all telephone and electronic communications, emergency department (ED) visits, and hospital readmissions.

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the patient and clinical characteristics of the study cohort. As appropriate, means and medians, alongside standard deviations and interquartile ranges, respectively, were used to summarize continuous variables. Proportions and percentages summarize categorical variables.

ASM use

The median number of total ASMs tried, including those at admission, was 6 (IQR 4–8). At the time of admission, patients were taking a median of 3 ASMs (IQR: 2–3), including CNB. The top three most common concomitant ASMs were lacosamide (n = 9), brivaracetam (n = 9), and zonisamide (n = 8).

CNB use

The median CNB dose at admission was 200 mg daily (IQR: 200–262.5). The median duration of EMU admission was 16 days (IQR: 12–20). During this evaluation, patients did not receive CNB for a median of 7 days (IQR: 5–8), including 4 patients who were off it for ≥10 days. The median restarting dose was 200 mg daily (IQR: 193.75–250). Eight (25%) patients were restarted on a CNB dose different than the admission dose. It included six patients restarted at a lower dose (Patients #5, 7, 11, 26, 27, 30). The CNB dose was reduced by a median of 100 mg daily (IQR 81.25–100). Rash history

Fourteen (44%) patients had a prior history of rash. These included nine (28%) patients with prior rashes attributable to antibiotics only, two (6%) to ASMs only, one (3%) to both antibiotics and ASMs, and two (6%) to topical adhesives or tapes (Table 1). One patient had a history of rash with CNB and with antibiotics (Patient #31). They developed a rash during the EMU evaluation, which was not due to CNB restart (see below section).

Dermatological and other AEs on CNB restart

Two patients developed rash during the hospital admission. One of them (Patient #7) was on home ASMs, including CNB, when an erythematous eruption developed a day after receiving cefazolin. It was the patient's first time receiving a cephalosporin or penicillin‐class antibiotic. The rash affected the entire body but spared the face. There was no facial edema or mucosal symptoms. Cefazolin was switched to clindamycin. Dermatologists saw the patient. The rash was clinically diagnosed and confirmed by biopsy as acute generalized exanthematous pustulosis (AGEP) attributable to cefazolin. The other patient (Patient #31) was off CNB when they reported a transient, erythematous, pruritic rash that improved overnight with diphenhydramine. A change of antibiotics from cefazolin to clindamycin was recommended, but the patient declined as the symptoms had already improved.

During the 2‐week postdischarge period, none of the patients experienced a breakthrough seizure or rash. Two patients had clinical concerns during this period. One patient reported dizziness during a telephonic encounter, which resolved spontaneously (Patient #3). The second patient visited the ED due to emesis and fever, diagnosed as an influenza A infection (Patient #29).

DISCUSSION

Our study found that restarting CNB at maintenance doses after temporary discontinuation can be safe. Patients were off CNB for a median of 7 days and restarted at a median dose of 200 mg daily, the FDA‐recommended maintenance dose. None of the 32 patients developed a hypersensitivity reaction, including rash, which could be attributed to CNB. To our knowledge, this is the first systematic investigation of restarting any ASM at maintenance doses after temporary discontinuation. It addresses an important gap in epilepsy care.

The “start‐low, go‐slow” titration strategy for initiating CNB is mandated to mitigate the risk of severe idiosyncratic hypersensitivity reactions. Similar reductions in rash rates with slower titration are seen with lamotrigine, carbamazepine, and phenytoin. 14 , 15 The lamotrigine FDA label recommends restarting at lower doses after interruptions longer than five half‐lives, based on pharmacokinetic reasoning. However, no empirical data demonstrate the clinical relevance or safety of that approach. No comparable guidance exists for carbamazepine, phenytoin, or other aromatic ASMs. 4 Against this backdrop, our findings are striking. Patients who previously tolerated CNB could restart at a median dose of 200 mg daily without hypersensitivity. It suggests a fundamental difference between initial ASM exposure and re‐exposure after brief discontinuation.

Optimal remedial strategies after missed ASM doses depend largely on drug half‐life. 2 ASMs with longer half‐lives and extended‐release formulations offer greater pharmacokinetic “forgiveness” by reducing peak‐to‐trough variability. 16 CNB's terminal half‐life of 50–60 h may explain the observed safety in our study. 17 After a median 7 day interruption, patients were off CNB for 2.8–3.4 half‐lives. Using FDA's lamotrigine guidance, five CNB half‐lives correspond to roughly 10–12.5 days. The median interruption in our cohort was well below that threshold, suggesting that residual drug exposure may have been sufficient to reduce hypersensitivity risk on restart. Notably, none of the four patients off CNB for ≥10 days developed a hypersensitivity reaction. CNB also exhibits nonlinear pharmacokinetics, with disproportionate increases in plasma levels at doses ≥300 mg daily. Although the median restarting dose in our cohort was 200 mg, eight (25%) patients restarted at ≥300 mg daily dose. None developed hypersensitivity. Additionally, restarting CNB at maintenance dose did not lead to rashes despite their previous occurrence in almost half (44%) of our cohort. While we only evaluated patients undergoing SEEG, our findings may be relevant to scalp EEG presurgical evaluation, which sometimes can be quite prolonged as well. Additionally, many centers start ASM taper before EMU evaluation, a practice reported in as almost three‐quarters of patients in some case series, which can start as early as 1–4 weeks before admission. 18 , 19

Although conducted in the EMU, the clinical relevance of our findings extends to routine outpatient care, particularly to the pervasive problem of ASM nonadherence. Poor adherence is reported in 25%–80% of PWE and is implicated in up to 45% of breakthrough seizures. 20 Missed doses commonly result from forgetfulness, adverse effects, logistical barriers, or refill restrictions, which may be particularly relevant for Schedule V controlled substances such as CNB. 21 Our findings suggest that patients who miss several consecutive days of CNB may be able to safely resume their maintenance dose without retitration, especially provided the interruption remains below approximately five half‐lives. This has practical implications for patient counseling and clinical decision‐making. Retitration starting at 12.5 mg daily dose would prolong the return to therapeutic dosing over several weeks, increasing the risk of breakthrough seizures and potentially discouraging adherence.

Previous studies examining ASM withdrawal in EMU settings have focused almost exclusively on seizure‐related complications, such as seizure clusters or status epilepticus, rather than dermatologic safety upon medication restart. As a result, we lack evidence‐based guidance for managing short‐term ASM interruptions, despite their frequency in both inpatient and outpatient settings. Our findings begin to address this gap for CNB and may inform future investigations of other ASMs with comparable pharmacokinetic characteristics and adverse risk profile.

Several limitations, including a single center, retrospective cohort design, warrant consideration. Our study cohort was predominantly White, which may limit the generalizability of its findings. Our findings cannot be extrapolated to longer discontinuation periods, especially after CNB's five half‐lives, during which retitration may be necessary as residual drug levels decline to negligible concentrations. No hypersensitivity events were observed in our 32‐patient cohort, but our modest sample size does not exclude a clinically meaningful risk. Using a one‐sided 95% confidence bound (rule of three), the upper limit of the true incidence is approximately 3/32 (~9%). 22 Thus, while our findings are consistent with prior reports (approximately 1.4% incidence in early CNB trials), larger cohorts are required to characterize the risk more precisely. 9 In addition, systematic monitoring for subclinical markers of hypersensitivity, such as eosinophilia or liver enzyme elevations, was not performed.

CONCLUSION

In summary, our study provides the first evidence that restarting CNB at previously tolerated maintenance doses after a short‐term interruption, approximately 1 week, appears safe and is not associated with dermatologic adverse effects. These findings have important implications for managing medication in the setting of nonadherence and suggest that, under appropriate circumstances, retitration may not be necessary after brief discontinuation. Larger, prospective studies with longer interruptions and systematic safety monitoring are needed to refine restart recommendations and to extend this work to other ASMs with known hypersensitivity risks.
