Spatial transcriptomics reveals atorvastatin modulates the tumor microenvironment to suppress breast cancer liver metastatic outgrowth
Authors: Neha Atale, Jie Chen, Brian R. Isett, Riyue Bao, Ernest M Meyer, Alan Wells
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Metastatic breast cancer remains incurable, underscoring the need for novel therapeutic strategies. Epidemiological and preclinical evidence suggests statins suppress metastatic outgrowth, however the underlying mechanisms remain unclear. We employed high-resolution spatial transcriptomics (ST) to explore the cellular composition and differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in the metastatic microenvironment following atorvastatin treatment. Formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded breast cancer liver metastases from control and atorvastatin-treated mice were analyzed by Nanostring CosMx Spatial Molecular Imager (SMI)/Bruker using a 973-gene panel, profiling 212 and 207 tumor-adjacent field of views (FOVs) (TAFs) respectively, which identified eight cell clusters, while non-tumor FOVs (NTFs) were sparse. Preferential depletion of LRRC15⁺ myofibroblastic cancer associated fibroblasts (CAFs), CXCL8⁺ inflammatory CAFs, and C1QC⁺/SPP1⁺ tumor associated macrophages (TAMs) subsets were revealed by harmony-assisted cell annotation and Seurat label transfer, which further separated CAFs and TAMs into discrete functional subpopulations. Immunohistochemistry (IHC) was also performed to validate cell types in both the tissues. Wilcoxon-based DEG analysis identified metastasis-associated gene expression changes between groups. Atorvastatin treatment reduced CAFs, Hepatic stellate cells (HSCs) (significantly), and macrophages (trending) in TAFs, with no changes in NTFs. These findings were supported by IHC (n = 4), which confirmed prominent reductions in these cell types while hepatocyte and endothelial markers remained unchanged. DEG analysis revealed coordinated downregulation of metastasis and extracellular matrix- associated transcripts in atorvastatin-treated TAFs. Our findings suggest that atorvastatin alters the cellular milieu in the disseminated microenvironment towards one in which pro-tumorigenic CAF and HSC populations are reduced, to create a microenvironmental niche favorable for maintaining metastatic dormancy and preventing relapse.

Introduction

Breast cancer relapses remain a major challenge as metastatic recurrences frequently acquire therapeutic resistance, ultimately driving disease lethality [1,2] with lung and liver being the most prominent sites for metastasis and associated with rapid mortality.

The metastatic cycle begins when the tumor cells undergo epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) and leave primary site and reach distant organs through the blood stream where they undergo a transient mesenchymal-to-epithelial reverting transition (MErT) and become dormant [3]. This allows them to invade immune response and treatments of primary cancer. They can then randomly become metastatic after years and decades of remaining dormant [[4], [5], [6], [7], [8], [9]]. While the exact mechanisms of this transition to metastatic state remain unclear, ex vivo models have shown that EGF and inflammatory chemokines and cytokines such as IL-8/CXCL8 and CXCL10, can activate dormant cells [[10], [11], [12], [13]].

New evidence indicates interaction between dormant tumor cells and liver host cells in activating MErT [[14], [15], [16]]. Tumor-adjacent areas that can be used to model the tumor microenvironment which acts as the first interaction boundary between micrometastatic tumors and host cells. An analysis of this region would provide insights into the cellular mechanisms and identify the treatment interventions. Recent research has shown that statins might be one of these therapeutic agents that could help for prevention of development of liver metastasis.

Statins, a well-established therapy for hypercholesterolemia and used to reduce cardiovascular risk, also show other effects through the inhibition of 3‑hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA reductase (HMGCR) [17,18]. Atorvastatin, a lipophilic statin with high hepatic uptake, has shown efficacy in limiting metastatic outgrowth in preclinical models, including aggressive ER-negative and TNBC subtypes [[19], [20], [21]]. Furthermore, evidence from in vivo, in vitro, and liver micro-physiological systems (MPS) demonstrates that statins can retard metastatic outgrowth in the liver. However, how atorvastatin affects the interaction between metastatic cells and the hepatic stroma to enable dormancy is unknown. In this paper, we study the role of atorvastatin in the tumor microenvironment remodeling to inhibit the progression of metastasis.

We utilized spatial transcriptomics (ST), that allows the quantification of gene expression while preserving tissue architecture. NanoString CosMx SMI/Bruker platform was used to map the expression of 973 RNA targets across 419 fields of views (FOVs) in FFPE xenograft tissues. By analyzing both tumor-adjacent regions that model the host-tumor boundary and distal non-tumor regions, we evaluated how atorvastatin reshapes the liver micrometastatic niche and attenuates ECM-driven signaling to restrict metastatic outgrowth. Using Harmony-integrated reference mapping and Seurat label transfer, we generated a high-confidence spatial atlas of the metastatic liver niche and identified distinct CAF and TAM subtypes. Atorvastatin selectively reduced activated LRRC15⁺ myofibroblastic CAFs, CXCL8⁺ inflammatory CAFs, hepatic stellate cells, and macrophages, accompanied by coordinated suppression of extracellular matrix-associated genes in tumor adjacent FOVs. Our findings suggest that atorvastatin inhibits breast cancer liver metastasis by reprogramming stromal cell populations and remodeling the metastatic microenvironment.

Materials and methods

Mice and drug treatments

Six female NOD-SCID gamma (NSG) mice per group, purchased from Jackson labs and maintained on standard diet and ad lib water were used. At 8 weeks of age, mice underwent intrasplenic injection of 5 × 10⁴ RFP-labeled MDA-MB-231 cells (50 μL saline) to establish experimental liver metastasis. Seven days later, mice were randomized to receive daily intraperitoneal injections of atorvastatin (10 mg/kg) or vehicle control (2% DMSO). On day 28, mice were euthanized by CO₂ asphyxiation according to AVMA guidelines, and tissues were collected for downstream analyses. Details of the metastatic dormancy and outgrowth model have been reported previously [20]. All experiments were performed under the approved IACUC protocols at the Pittsburgh VA Healthsystem.

Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) staining

Liver tissues were fixed with 10% buffered formalin (Fisher Chemicals) at room temperature for 48 h and then transferred to 70% ethanol for dehydration and paraffin embedding (FFPE) in blocks. The FFPE blocks are cut to 4 μm sections for tissue staining. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining were conducted by Neuro-Histology Research Laboratory at University of Pittsburgh.

Preparation of samples for Cos Mx SMI analysis

Formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) liver tissue sections from both control and atorvastatin-treated mice were processed for spatial transcriptomic analysis using the Nanostring CosMx SMI, following the general workflow as per Bruker RNA assay slide preparation manual [[22], [23], [24]]. Preparation of samples, imaging, and cell segmentation were carried out based on established CosMx protocols. The 973-gene CosMx RNA panel was primarily selected and optimized to profile the host mouse hepatic microenvironment and stromal crosstalk at the tumor boundary; target probes were non-overlapping with human-specific transcripts, focusing our dataset on host stromal dynamics rather than tumor-intrinsic gene expression.

Clustering and cell annotation

To explore the difference between tumor-adjacent region and non-tumor region, we first created new Seurat objects with gene counts and meta data of cells from selected FOVs of the filtered Seurat objects, then integration was performed to remove potential batch effect using method of RPCA Integration. We then performed clustering with resolution of 0.5, and cluster annotations were conducted based on marker genes. The cell annotations were also performed against reference datasets obtained from Mouse StSt [25,26] and scRNATMA [27] to validate annotations on our samples.

Volcano plot and heatmap construction

Differential gene expression analysis was performed using Seurat v5.3.0. Volcano plots were generated with the EnhancedVolcano package, and heatmaps were created using the ComplexHeatmap package. Differential expression was visualized by plotting the log2 fold change (log2FC) against the −log10 adjusted p-value. Genes with an adjusted p-value < 0.05 and |log2FC| > 0.5 were considered significantly differentially expressed. Upregulated and downregulated genes meeting these criteria were highlighted, while non-significant genes were shown separately.

Immunohistochemistry

Formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded tissue sections were deparaffinized in xylene, rehydrated through graded ethanol, and subjected to antigen retrieval in citrate/EDTA buffer at 95°C for 10 min. Endogenous peroxidase activity was quenched with hydrogen peroxide, followed by blocking with 20% goat serum for 1 h. Sections were incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies targeting α- smooth muscle actin (1:100, Cell Signaling, #19245), CD86 (1:100, Cell Signaling, #19589), CD163 (1:100, Abcam, #182422), Vimentin (1:100, Cell Signaling, #5741), Albumin (1:100, Proteintech, 16475-1-AP), CD31 (1:100, Cell Signaling, #77699 1:100), CD34 (1:100, Cell Signaling #26233), SPP-1 (Cell Signaling, #88742), diluted in 10% goat serum containing phosphate-buffered saline. Slides were incubated with Goat-antiRabbit IgG secondary antibody (1:500, Invitrogen, H-3401) for 1 hour at room temperature. After several washes, slides were incubated for 30 min with ABC solution (Vectastain Elite ABC kit, PK-6100) and were stained with DAB (3,3′-Diaminobenzidine) chromogen + substrate (Vector Labs, DAB substrate kit SK-4100) following washing and counterstained with Mayer’s hematoxylin (Vector Labs, H-3401) for 80 s, and rinsed with tap water, dehydrated, cleared in xylene and mounted in Toluene (Fisher Chemical, SP15-100, U.S.A.). Images were captured by Nikon ECLIPSE NI-E1 microscope (Melville, N.Y., USA) in Center for Biologic Imaging, University of Pittsburgh, Pittsburgh, PA, USA. Randomly distributed three microscopic fields for each section were analyzed by QuPath software. The positively stained area within each section was quantified and normalized to the area and presented as % positive area.

Statistical analysis

A Welch t-test was used to examine the significance between means, and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. To identify DEGs between control and atorvastatin treated groups, a Wilcoxon rank-sum test was performed. IHC images were analyzed in QuPath ver 0.6.0 to identify the percentage of positive cells. The mean ± standard deviation was used to express the results.

Atorvastatin modulates spatial distribution of stromal cell populations in liver metastasis

It has been shown previously that atorvastatin decreases breast cancer activation and limits outgrowth in the metastatic micro-environment via breast cancer cell intrinsic inhibition of signaling from small GTPases [20,28], and this could alter cell-cell signaling to hepatic stromal cells. We used Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) analysis in Seurat to cluster the different cell type populations to examine changes in the tumor microenvironment on the treatment with atorvastatin in liver metastasis. After quality control and batch correction (Suppl. Fig. 1), a total of 113,498 cells were included in subsequent analyses, comprising 48,960 cells for control and 64,538 for atorvastatin treated tissue.

Cells were manually annotated into eight known cell populations using the following canonical markers: Cancer associated fibroblasts (Col1a1, Col1a2, Col3a1, Acta2), Hepatic stellate cells (Des, Lrat, Pdgfra, Vim, Vin), M1-like Macrophages (Cd86) and M2-like macrophages (Cd163), Hepatocytes (Alb, Hnf4a, Serpin1a, Ttr), Endothelial cells (Cdh5, Eng, Lyve-1, Pecam-1, vwf), Portal Fibroblasts (Cd34 and Dpt), and Cholangiocytes (Cd24a, Krt7, Sox9, Spp-1) (Fig. 2A). The dot plot shows the expression of different marker genes across the different cell clusters, and higher expression clusters were selected for determining the specific cell type. We observed CAFs were predominantly found in cluster 6, macrophages in cluster 7, 17 and 14, HSCs in cluster 2, hepatocytes in cluster 0, 1, 9, 12 and 15, endothelial cells in cluster 3, Portal Fibroblasts in cluster 11 and cholangiocytes in cluster 13 and 16 (Suppl. Fig. 2). Further, we used Harmony [29] to combine our data with two publicly accessible liver scRNA-seq reference datasets in order to provide high-confidence cell-type identification of the CosMx spatial transcriptomic dataset. Hepatocytes, endothelial cells, cholangiocytes, hepatic stellate cells, Kupffer cells, several macrophage subsets, and various CAF subtypes were all resolved by the integrated reference atlas, exhibiting strong clustering and validation for downstream cell-type annotation (Suppl. Fig. 3). We compared the marker-based annotations (ann.v4) with Seurat label transfer predictions in order to evaluate the accuracy of cell-type annotation and found >70% of annotations matched with reference atlas data for each of endothelial, cholangiocytes, hepatocytes and macrophages. Although the primary liver cell groups were identified by manual annotations, the label transfer method further separated these into transcriptionally unique subpopulations, especially in the CAF and TAM compartments (Suppl. Fig. 4). Atorvastatin treated samples show significant reduction of fibroblast populations (Suppl. Fig. 5), such as mCAF LRRC15+ (6.93% of total cells in control vs 3.58% in treated), iCAF CXCL8+ (4.45% vs 2.91%), ssCAF PI16+ (1.66% to 1.0%) and ssCAF CXCL14+ (0.99% to 0.42%). Some CAF subtypes, however, also showed insignificant increase such as CD74+ apCAF (1.87% in control to 2.15% in treated). Other CAF subtypes such as mCAF COL4A1+ and iCAF ISG15+ which are generally rarely detected in liver samples have not changed in overall abundance. Similar reduction of cell proportion with atorvastatin treatment were seen in other cell types such as monocytes and monocytes-derived cells (3.74% in control vs 2.67% in treated) and macrophage subtypes such as SPP1+ macrophages (0.91% vs 0.61%), C1QC+ myeloid macrophages (1.41% vs 1.02%). By suppressing pro-fibrotic and pro-inflammatory cell states while largely maintaining antigen-presenting CAFs (CD74+ apCAF), atorvastatin may alter the tumor microenvironment and create a less favorable niche for metastatic progression, according to the selective depletion of these stromal and myeloid populations.

Atorvastatin reduces total non-parenchymal cells (CAFs, HSCs and Macrophages) in the tumor microenvironment

The liver contains parenchymal, made up of hepatocytes and cholangiocytes and non-parenchymal cells (NPCs) mainly composed of myeloid cells, fibroblasts, and endothelial cells. In addition to the changes in proportions of the cell compositions, we also analyzed the total number of cells in each subtype to understand how atorvastatin might change the micro-environment composition (Fig. 3A). We observed a reduction of CAFs (2720 in control vs 1842 in atorvastatin), HSCs (5009 vs 4134), M1-like Macrophages (148 vs 116) and M2-like Macrophages (302 vs 176) in TAFs. An increase in total cell numbers was monitored in Hepatocytes (22737 in control vs 36457 in treated), Endothelial cells (5358 vs 7292), FPT (735 vs 741) and Cholangiocytes (737 vs 913). Fig. 3A also shows the proportion of different cell types in control and treated samples within the tumor-adjacent FOVs and non-tumor FOVs. Statistically significant decrease was observed in proportion of CAF and HSC cells in TAFs and non-significant decrease was observed in M1 and M2-like macrophages. As compared to control, CAFs decreased to 0.4 times and HSCs to half in treated TAFs. The NTFs did not show any change on atorvastatin treatment confirming that changes are due to tumor-microenvironmental crosstalk. Atorvastatin treated samples however showed the same proportions as the control non-tumor regions, suggesting that the treatment with atorvastatin was able to arrest this key change in tumor microenvironment. In line with our spatial results, the IHC analysis showed that atorvastatin-treated tissues had significantly less α-SMA⁺ CAFs and vimentin⁺ HSCs than controls (n = 4) (Fig. 3E). There were slight decreases in both CD86⁺ M1-like and CD163⁺ M2-like macrophages, but these variations did not reach statistical significance. Atorvastatin may reduce the pro-tumorigenic immune milieu, as seen by the decline in macrophages, which are known to stimulate tumor growth, angiogenesis, and metastasis. On the other hand, CD31⁺ endothelial cells and albumin⁺ hepatocytes did not vary between groups. Atorvastatin treatment did not result in significant changes in the abundance of CD34⁺ portal fibroblasts or SPP1⁺ cholangiocytes. All these results suggest that atorvastatin modifies the metastatic environment into a less favorable condition for metastatic progression by specifically targeting non-parenchymal stromal and myeloid populations while maintaining the main parenchymal and vascular compartments.

Atorvastatin regulates EMT- and ECM- associated, inflammatory signaling, and stress response related gene expressions in various cell types in TAFs in liver

Within HSCs, 11 genes Alb, Ttr, Serpina1, Apoa1, Gc, Ifitm3, Arg1, Ifitm2, Itgav, Cxcl1, and Il15ra were upregulated, while 6 genes—Clec4f, Azgp1, Ms4a4b, Wnt7b, Col1a1, and Fabp4 were downregulated in atorvastatin-treated tumor-adjacent FOVs. In breast cancer, Apoa1 has a tumor-suppressive function and contributes to apoptosis, which prevents cancer cells from proliferating [31]. Since clec4f and ms4a4b are linked to connections between pro-tumorigenic macrophages and stellate cells, their inhibition may reduce crosstalk that promotes metastatic growth.

In both M1-like and M2- like Macrophages, Mt1, Mt2, Lcn2—were downregulated by atorvastatin in tumor-adjacent FOVs, showing reduced inflammatory activation and enhanced stress tolerance. Several critical genes are significantly downregulated in M1-like macrophages in the atorvastatin-treated group, including Ccl11, Cd59a, Il4ra, and Ifngr2, which are associated with inflammatory signaling, stress response etc. Concurrent downregulation of activation- and cytoskeleton-related genes (Acta2, S100a8, Fn1, and Ctgf) in M2-like macrophages suggests reduced macrophage activation, migratory capacity, and tissue remodeling functions

In endothelial cells, we observed downregulation of 13 genes, Clu, Mt1, Col8a1, Spp-1, Krt8, Krt18, Col1a1, Vim, Crp, Acta2, Ccnd1, Bmp1, Egfr, and Hmgb2 in tumor-adjacent FOVs. This broadly reduced expression of ECM, cytoskeletal, angiogenic, and proliferative markers indicates significant atorvastatin-mediated vascular reprogramming. Finally, in portal fibroblasts, vwf, Apoc1, and Alb were upregulated, which might refer to reprogramming of tumor cells towards immune evasion and angiogenic subtype of portal fibroblasts. whereas 12 genes—S100a6, Vim, Pfn1, Col4a1, Sparc, Col4a2, Sat1, Eif5A, Cd34, Cxcr4, Col15a1, and Cd276 were downregulated in tumor-adjacent FOVs from atorvastatin-treated tissues. Hierarchical clustering was performed using the top 50 DEGs to illustrate the magnitude and direction of expression changes across all samples (Fig. 4B). These findings highlight the broad impact of atorvastatin on multiple stromal components of the TME, leading to attenuation of ECM remodeling, EMT programs, macrophage polarization, and pro-metastatic signaling pathways.

Discussion

Using a model of spontaneous metastasis in which MDA-MB-231 breast cancer cells secondarily colonize the liver, we administered atorvastatin during the outgrowth phase and subsequently analyzed liver metastases with high-resolution spatial profiling. Our findings reveal that atorvastatin greatly lowers the abundance of CAFs, HSCs and macrophages in tumor-adjacent regions in the metastatic ecosystem. We chose to investigate this mechanism in mice, as we could control the timing of the statin and examine micrometastases that are postulated to be affected by the statins. The available human specimens of liver mBC are in a late stage after outgrowth and thus not indicative of the early effects that are the focus herein.

Our results indicate a significant decrease in CAFs. They can influence tumor cell phenotypes through paracrine signaling and also exhibited elevated expression of canonical collagen genes (Col1a1, Col3a1, and Col4a1) involved in ECM remodeling [32]. α-SMA, as a myofibroblastic CAF phenotype that supports a pro-metastatic microenvironment in breast cancer, was markedly reduced in TAFs following atorvastatin treatment, as indicated by IHC [33]. Higher Vim expressions were observed in the tumor and TAFs in control, whereas its expression was reduced in atorvastatin treated tissue. DEGs in different cell types further revealed downregulation of critical genes prone to EMT progression and ECM remodeling by atorvastatin in TAFs. In CAFs, we observed decreased expression of Mgp, supporting atorvastatin’s ability to attenuate stem-like, invasive tumor cell phenotypes [34]. Protein IL-32 derived from CAFs binds to integrin β3 at the surface of breast cancer cells, thereby activating the downstream p38 MAPK pathway to enhance fibronectin, N-cadherin, and vimentin expression and strengthens cancer cell invasion and metastasis [35,36]. In our previous reports, we found statins reduce the availability of prenyl groups and decrease prenylation of small GTPases (e.g., Ras, Rho, Rac) involved in proliferation, survival, and EMT/metastatic phenotypes [20,21,28]. Our data also suggest that atorvastatin might disrupt CAF-mediated microenvironmental cues that support metastatic emergence and outgrowth and suppress stromal cues required for colonization.

Activated fibroblast subsets were selectively reduced by atorvastatin, with CD74+ apCAFs remaining mostly intact and LRRC15+ mCAFs exhibiting the largest drop, followed by CXCL8+ iCAFs. Myofibroblastic CAFs are the main providers of matricellular molecules and structural ECM proteins that improve tissue stiffness, integrin signaling, and growth factor availability—all of which encourage metastatic colonization. Similarly, iCAFs release chemokines and cytokines that attract immunosuppressive myeloid cells and create a long-term inflammatory environment that promotes metastatic outgrowth. Atorvastatin altered the myeloid compartment by decreasing monocytes, monocyte-derived cells, and several macrophage subsets, such as C1QC+ and SPP1+ TAMs. These macrophage populations have been associated with angiogenesis, matrix remodeling, immunosuppression, and the establishment of metastatic niches.

HSCs reside in the Space of Disse and constitute a significant portion (5–10%) of the total non-parenchymal cell population in the liver. In the healthy liver, HSCs adopt a quiescent phenotype, characterized by their non-proliferative state and store vitamin A within cytoplasmic lipid droplets [37]. Previous studies have shown that activated HSCs elevated breast cancer cell growth and proliferation in vitro and stimulated the emergence of dormant disseminated breast cancer cells by secreting soluble factors, including Il-8 and Mcp-1. These factors have been shown to promote the proliferation of breast cancer cells even under serum-starvation conditions possibly via ERK pathway activation [12,38], which suggest that a lower number of HSCs corresponds to reduced emergence, indicating the low proportion and/or non-activated HSC population by atorvastatin may limit metastatic outgrowth.

According to our previous study, M1-like macrophages may assist in metastatic breast cancer cells maintain dormant features, while M2-like macrophages may aid in tumor growth [8]. In the current study, both M1-like (Cd86⁺) and M2-like (Cd163⁺) subsets declined following atorvastatin treatment, as indicated by IHC quantification. Hepatocytes, endothelial cells, portal fibroblasts and cholangiocytes remained more stable, aligning with the preservation of native liver architecture in both the control and atorvastatin treated tumor-adjacent and non-tumor regions. These findings imply that atorvastatin modifies the metastatic microenvironment by specifically reducing stromal and myeloid elements that promote tumor growth. Immunohistology analyses also staining of Alb and Cd31 confirm that hepatocyte and endothelial integrity is maintained, suggesting that atorvastatin-mediated changes are localized to stromal compartments rather than reflecting parenchyma. Atorvastatin showed decrease in Cd34 and SPP-1 positive cells, which is not aligned with our spatial data. This discrepancy may be because we quantified gene expression across the entire tissue rather than focusing solely on tumor-adjacent regions. Importantly, the concordance between ST and histological validation strengthens the conclusion that atorvastatin exerts its anti-metastatic effects by disrupting the multicellular crosstalk sustaining a growth-permissive metastatic niche.

Atorvastatin treatment also showed significant changes in ECM composition in tumor-adjacent FOVs. DEGs of critical genes such as Il15ra have been observed to potentiate metastasis in ccRCC by activation of NF-κΒ/ZEB1 signaling pathway [39] and its marked reduction by atorvastatin further supports suppression of pro-metastatic inflammatory pathways. MMP-14, associated with mBC risk, when downregulated selectively inhibit lung metastasis in TNBC models while leaving primary tumor growth unaffected [40]. The matrix protein Sparc exhibits a complex and somewhat paradoxical expression profile, while it is highly expressed in the stroma and neoplastic cells of various malignancies, including glioma, breast cancer, and melanoma, and linked to tumor suppression [41]. Our findings indicate that atorvastatin-mediated downregulation of Sparc contributes to the prevention of metastatic outgrowth. Atorvastatin treatment markedly reduced several key ECM-associated proteins, including Col1a1, Fabp4, and Cspg4. As a major fibrillar collagen, Col1a1 is extensively documented to promote breast cancer metastasis, and its elevated expression correlates with poor survival outcomes, particularly in ER-positive tumors [42]. The downregulation of Fabp4 in HSCs is shown its established role in driving tumor proliferation via ATPB ubiquitination/NF-κB–IL1α axis. Similarly, the reduction of Cspg4, a transmembrane proteoglycan widely expressed in aggressive tumor types, underscores a broader attenuation of ECM-driven tumor–stromal signaling [43].

Metallothioneins (MTs) are also found to be essential for the development, spread, and resistance to drugs for tumors [44] and supports tumor progression in later stages [45]. Clu, for instance, regulates stemness and supports metastasis by promoting EMT, its silencing has been shown to induce a MErT and restore E-cadherin expression, mirroring the anti-invasive effects we observe with statin therapy [46]. In conclusion, our data strongly support the notion that atorvastatin provides a cell-type-specific and spatially restricted therapeutic strategy by comprehensively targeting the pro-metastatic stromal environment within the tumor-adjacent microenvironment, without affecting the non-tumor parenchyma.

Limitation and conclusion

This study has several limitations. First, because an immunocompromised NSG mouse model (lacking mature B, T, and NK cells, with defective innate myeloid signaling) was required to establish human MDA-MB-231 xenografts, our analysis focused specifically on local non-parenchymal stromal and innate microenvironmental remodeling. Potential atorvastatin effects on adaptive immune responses could not be evaluated in this system. Second, the sample size for initial spatial profiling was limited, though key cellular and transcriptomic changes were independently validated by IHC in additional specimens. Lastly, because spatial transcriptomic profiling was performed on a single biological sample per treatment group, robust neighborhood and spatial interaction analyses across biological replicates were not feasible. Additionally, target panel design prioritized the mouse host microenvironment over human tumor-intrinsic transcriptomic profiling, limiting direct evaluation of cancer cell-intrinsic responses. Still, despite these limitations, robust changes, validated by IHC in a statistically evaluable set of experiments, do allow for conclusions.

Overall, our study provides a compelling mechanistic rationale for exploring statins as adjunctive agents in metastasis-targeted therapeutic strategies, while simultaneously highlighting the power of spatial transcriptomics to comprehensively dissect the complex, localized responses to therapeutic intervention within the tumor microenvironment.
