Molecular evidence of Bartonella spp. in wild felids from Brazil
Authors: Clara Morato Dias, Daniel Antônio Braga Lee, João Vitor dos Santos Alves da Silva, Laura Berger Nunes, Joares Adenilson May Júnior, Vinícius Baggio-Souza, Felipe Bortolotto Peters, Marina Ochoa Favarini, Ana Paula Neuschrank Albano, Suelen Dias Segui, Silvia Resende Terra, Flávia Pereira Tirelli, Jorge Cherem, Hugo Borghesan Morzele, Marcos Adriano Tortato, Adriano Rodrigues Lagos, Felipe Viana Manzano, Tiago Chagas de Oliveira Tourinho, Rosangela Zacarias Machado, João Fabio Soares, Marcos Rogério André
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAmong Felidae, domestic cats play a role as reservoirs for Bartonella henselae, Bartonella clarridgeiae, and Bartonella koehlerae. Despite the well-known relationship between Bartonella spp. and domestic cats, to date, there is still a paucity of reports on Bartonella spp. in wild felids. The aim of this study was to molecularly detect and characterize Bartonella spp. in blood and tissue samples from wild felids sampled in Brazil. Among 291 DNA samples (211 blood, 77 spleens and three livers), 3.09% (9/291) were positive in the Quantitative Real-Time PCR (qPCR) assay for Bartonella spp. based on the nuoG gene: 1/44 Leopardus pardalis, 3/61 Leopardus geoffroyi, 1/66 Panthera onca, 1/3 Herpailurus yagouaroundi, and 3/32 Leopardus wiedii. In the phylogenetic analyses, while the gltA sequence detected in L. wiedii grouped with Bartonella henselae, the gltA and ftsZ sequences detected in L. geoffroyi clustered within the same subclade as Bartonella sp. sequences previously identified in wild rodents and associated fleas. Introduction

The genus Bartonella comprises fastidious, facultative intracellular, Gram-negative α-proteobacteria that parasitize erythrocytes and endothelial cells of several mammalian species (Kosoy et al. 2012). These bacteria usually exhibit high host specificity, often establishing long-lasting asymptomatic bacteremia in their natural mammalian reservoirs. In humans, incidental infections can result in a range of clinical manifestations, most commonly cat-scratch disease (CSD), while other, less frequent manifestations include bacillary angiomatosis and endocarditis (Kosoy et al. 2012).

Among felid species, domestic cats play a role as reservoirs for Bartonella henselae, Bartonella clarridgeiae, and Bartonella koehlerae (Kosoy et al. 2012). Wild felids can also harbor Bartonella species found in domestic cats, such as B. henselae and B. koehlerae, as well as distinct Bartonella lineages (Molia et al. 2004, 2016). However, the real role of wild felids as reservoirs for these agents remains a topic of ongoing debate (Obiegala et al. 2024). Different research groups have molecularly detected Bartonella spp. in wild felids from Japan (Tateno et al. 2013), Germany (Obiegala et al. 2024), South Africa, Kenya, Tanzania, Namibia (Molia et al. 2004), the United States of America (Boulouis et al. 2020), Mexico (López-Pérez et al. 2017), and Brazil (Guimarães et al. 2010; Filoni et al. 2012; Souza et al. 2021). In Brazil, while B. henselae DNA was detected in Leopardus geoffroyi, L. tigrinus, L. wiedii, L. pardalis, Panthera onca, and Herpailurus yagouaroundi, B. koehlerae was detected only in L. wiedii (Guimarães et al. 2010; Filoni et al. 2012; Souza et al. 2021).

Out of eleven wild felid species recognized in Brazil, eight can be found in the state of Rio Grande do Sul (RS), southern Brazil (Souza et al. 2021). Despite the country’s ecological diversity and rich wild feline fauna, few studies have explored the molecular diversity of Bartonella spp. in these hosts. Therefore, this study aimed to molecularly detect and characterize Bartonella spp. in blood, liver, and spleen samples from wild felids across the North, Northeast, Central-West, Southeast, and South regions of Brazil, contributing to a better understanding of their circulation and potential zoonotic risks.

Materials and methods

Sampling and studied area

Between 2017 and 2026, a total of 211 blood, 77 spleen and three liver samples were collected from wild felids across nine Brazilian states, covering the North, Northeast, Central-West, Southeast, and South regions of the country. Sampling was conducted in 51 municipalities, with the sampling area shown in Fig. 1 and the detailed distribution of samples by animal species provided in Supplementary Material 3.

Map generated by QGIS 2.18.2.8, showing the states of Brazil and the location of cities by state where wild felids were sampled in the present study in different colors

The taxonomic identification of the wild felids followed the guidelines of the Taxonomy Committee of the Brazilian Society of Mammalogy (CT-SBMz) (Abreu et al. 2025). Felids were captured using different methods according to their body size. While H. yagouaroundi, L. wiedii, L. pardalis and L. geoffroyi were captured using Tomahawk live traps, Panthera onca individuals were captured using foot snares (de Araujo et al. 2021). Capture devices were placed in areas with previous reports of sightings, camera traps, and evidence of scat and/or footprints of wild felines. The traps were available in the field for the period of 7–15 days and were monitored at least once a day for capture occasions. After capture, the animals were restrained with a combination of ketamine (approximately 10 mg/kg) and xylazine (approximately 1 mg/kg) intramuscularly (Souza et al. 2021). Approximately 2 mL of blood was collected from each animal into EDTA-tubes (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, Missouri, USA) by jugular and/or cephalic vein puncture under aseptic conditions. Immediately after collection, the blood samples were kept refrigerated in reusable ice packs and subsequently transported to a field base, where they were stored at − 20 °C until further molecular analyses.

DNA extraction

DNA was extracted from 291 wild felids’ biological samples (211 blood, 77 spleen, and three liver samples) using a commercial extraction kit PureLink™ Genomic DNA Kit (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, California, USA), following the manufacturer’s instructions. After extraction, DNA concentration was quantified by spectrophotometry using a NanoDrop™ (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, Massachusetts, USA). The DNA concentrations of the samples were standardized to approximately 100 ng/µL. The DNA samples were stored at − 20 °C until further molecular analyses.

Molecular screening and characterization of Bartonella spp

DNA samples were subjected to a qPCR to detect and quantify the number of copies of a fragment of the Bartonella nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide dehydrogenase subunit gamma (nuoG) gene per microliter (André et al. 2015). DNA samples were evaluated in duplicates. When differences in Cq values were greater than 0.5, the samples were retested in triplicates. In the construction of the standard curve of each reaction, serial dilutions were performed at different concentrations (1.0 × 107 to 1.0 × 100 copies) of a plasmid encoding an 83 bp fragment of the nuoG gene of B. henselae (pIDTSMART; Integrated DNA Technologies, Coralville, Iowa, United States), which was also used as positive controls. The number of plasmid copies was determined by the formula (XG/µL DNA/ [Plasmid Length (BP) × 660]) × 6. 22 × 1023 × plasmid copies/µL. The amplification efficiency (E) was calculated according to the slope of the standard curve using the formula E = 10− 1/slope (Bustin et al. 2009). UltraPure™ DNase/RNase-Free Distilled Water (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) was used as a negative control for each reaction performed. The qPCR assays were carried out in a C1000 Touch™ Thermal Cycler with CFX96™ Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA).

Samples positive in the qPCR were subjected to PCR assays targeting eigth molecular markers, selected based on previously published protocols widely used for the molecular characterization and phylogenetic analysis of Bartonella spp., namely gltA (citrate synthase) (Norman et al. 1995), ftsZ (cell division protein FtsZ) (Paziewska et al. 2011), groEL (60 kDa chaperonin GroEL) (Paziewska et al. 2011), nuoG (NADH dehydrogenase I subunit G) (Colborn et al. 2010), pap31 (31-kDa heme-binding protein) (Maggi and Breitschwerdt 2005a), rpoB (DNA-directed RNA polymerase subunit beta) (Renesto et al. 2001), ribC (riboflavin synthase alpha chain) (Johnson et al. 2003), and 16–23 S rRNA intergenic spacer region (Maggi and Breitschwerdt 2005b) (Supplementary material 1).

DNA purification, sequencing and phylogenetic analysis

Amplicons obtained in the conventional PCR assays were purified by using ExoSAP-IT™ PCR Product Cleanup Reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and submitted to Sanger sequencing (Sanger et al. 1977) in both directions (forward and reverse) at the Centro de Estudos do Genoma Humano e Células Tronco (University of São Paulo, São Paulo, Brazil) using the BigDye™ Terminator v3.1 Cycle Sequencing Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA).

The received sequences were visualized, trimmed, and a consensus sequence was assembled using the softwares BioEdit 7.7 (Hall 1999), Phred/Phrap and Consed (Ewing et al. 1998). All consensus sequences were submitted to BLASTn analysis (https://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Blast.cgi) to check the percentage of identity with sequences deposited previously in the GenBank database. The alignments were performed using the software MAFFT 7.0 (Katoh and Standley 2013). For each alignment, the best-fitting evolutionary model was chosen by using the jModeltest v.2.1.10 (Darriba et al. 2012) software through the PAUP4* Version 4c software (Swofford 2003).

A Maximum Likelihood (ML) analysis was performed using the IQ-TREE2 software (Nguyen and Haeseler 2016) using 103 Ultra-Fast Bootstrap Replicates for each alignment. The resulting phylogenetic trees were visualized and edited using the FigTree 1.4.4 (https://tree.bio.ed.ac.uk/software/figtree/) software.

Results

Quantitative Real-Time PCR assays for Bartonella spp. based on the nuoG gene detected a positivity of 3.09% (9/291) in wild felid blood, spleen, and liver samples (1/44 Leopardus pardalis, 3/61 Leopardus geoffroyi, 1/66 Panthera onca, 1/3 Herpailurus yagouaroundi, and 3/32 Leopardus wiedii) (Supplementary material 2). Among the positive samples, the number of copies of a fragment of the Bartonella spp. nuoG gene per microliter could be estimated for seven of the nine samples (7/9) (Supplementary Material 2). The Efficiency, R2, Y-intercept and slope values of the qPCR assays varied between 99.2 and 104.9%, 0.980 to 0.998, 37.142 to 38.238 and − 3.209 to -3.342, respectively, according to MIQE recommendations (Bustin et al. 2009).

Among the nine qPCR-positive samples, amplification was obtained for the gltA gene in 2/9 (22.2%) samples, nuoG in 3/9 (33.3%), ftsZ in 1/9 (11.1%), and rpoB in 1/9 (11.1%). Of the PCR products submitted to Sanger sequencing, only three yielded high-quality readable sequences, corresponding to two gltA sequences obtained from L. geoffroyi and L. wiedii and one ftsZ sequence obtained from L. geoffroyi (Table 1).

The BLASTn analysis demonstrated that the gltA sequences obtained one from L. wiedii (PZ600596) and another one from L. geoffroyi (PX527374) had 100% identity with a Bartonella henselae sequence previously detected in Felis catus from China (MK729772.1) and 98.15% with Bartonella sp. sequence previously detected in Hylaemys perenensis from Peru (KF021602.1), respectively (Table 1). In the Maximum Likelihood analysis of the gltA region, the 372 bp Bartonella sp. sequence obtained from L. geoffroyi (PX527374) was positioned in the same sub-clade as Bartonella sp. sequences previously detected in wild rodents (KX086716.1; MN787564) and their associated ectoparasites (Craneopsylla minerva collected from an Oligoryzomys nigripes; MH279891.1) from Brazil. This sub-clade was closely related to a sub-clade containing Bartonella harrusi and Bartonella sp. sequences detected in rodents (Delomys dorsalis) (KX270238) and Thrichomys fosteri-associated Polygenys bohlsi bohlsi fleas (KY304482; KY304483). The 386 bp Bartonella sp. gltA sequence detected in L. wiedii (PZ600596) was positioned in the same sub-clade as Bartonella sp. sequences previously detected in domestic cats and their associated Ctenocephalides felis felis fleas from Chile (KX024511; KY913626) and Bartonella sp. previously detected in a Puma concolor from the USA (KF466248). This sub-clade was positioned in the same clade as B. henselae (Fig. 2).

Discussion

Despite the well-known relationship between Bartonella spp. and domestic cats, to date, there is still a paucity of reports on Bartonella spp. in wild felids, mainly in South America. Herein, we report molecular evidence of the circulation of Bartonella spp. in 3.09% (9/291) of free-living wild felids from municipalities in the states of Bahia (northeastern), Minas Gerais (southeastern), and Rio Grande do Sul (southern).

Wild felids are susceptible to many of the same pathogens that affect domestic cats, several of which can be transmitted both within and between species, especially in ecosystems increasingly threatened by the diminishing separation between domestic and wild mammals (Obiegala et al. 2024). Indeed, previous studies conducted in the southern and southeastern regions of Brazil have detected B. henselae and B. koehlerae in wild felids (Guimarães et al. 2010; Filoni et al. 2012; Souza et al. 2021). Interestingly, the present study detected not only B. henselae, a species well recognized whose main reservoirs are represented by cats, but also Bartonella species associated with wild small mammals and associated ectoparasites were identified. In a preliminary analysis, the molecular detection of wild rodents and marsupials-associated Bartonella spp. in blood samples from L. geoffroyi may suggest that predatory behavior possibly increases contact not only with prey but also with their ectoparasites, facilitating pathogen exchange. Similarly, Bartonella taylorii, a rodent-associated Bartonella species, has been reported in wild felid species across Europe, including Felis silvestris in Germany (Obiegala et al. 2024) and Lynx lynx in the Czech Republic (Danek et al. 2023).

In the phylogenetic analysis based on the gltA gene, the sequences obtained in the present study were positioned within two distinct groups. The sequence obtained from L. wiedii was positioned within a group containing B. henselae sequences, whereas the sequence obtained from L. geoffroyi was positioned within a group containing Bartonella sequences previously detected in the rodent-associated C. minerva fleas from southern Brazil (Schott et al. 2020) and P. bohlsi bohlsi from the Brazilian Midwest (de Sousa et al. 2018). However, the relatively short gltA fragments obtained in the present study limited the resolution of phylogenetic inference, and the observed clustering should therefore be interpreted cautiously. Both C. minerva and P. bohlsi bohlsi primarily parasitize wild rodents and marsupials, which are common prey of wild felids in Brazil (de Moraes et al. 2003). Similarly, the phylogenetic analysis based on the ftsZ gene showed that the sequence detected in L. geoffroyi clustered with Bartonella sequences previously detected in rodents and associated ectoparasites. These findings suggest that wild felids may act as accidental hosts of Bartonella species associated with wild rodents and marsupials and their respective ectoparasites, possibly as a consequence of predation (Danek et al. 2023; Obiegala et al. 2024).

Herein, a ftsZ sequence showing identity < 97% with B. machadoae was detected in L. geoffroyi from southern Brazil, suggesting that wild felids may be exposed to Bartonella spp. associated with wild rodents. However, considering that rodent-associated B. machadoae and marsupial-associated B. harrusi are phylogenetically closely related, distinction between these two Bartonella species cannot be feasible using cut-offs previously established for several molecular markers (do Amaral et al. 2022). Therefore, the real identity of the two Bartonella genotypes detected herein remain unknown. Nonetheless, the findings presented herein show, for the first time, the circulation of small mammals-associated Bartonella spp. in wild felids from southern Brazil.

Considering that previous studies have shown that the combination of pre-enrichment liquid culture (e.g., BAPGM, Bartonella AlphaProteobacteria Growth Medium) and isolation in blood/chocolate agar with qPCR assays can enhance the detection of Bartonella spp. (Dias et al. 2023), the molecular occurrence reported herein might be underestimated. Other approaches, including digital PCR (dPCR) (Maggi et al. 2020), the combination of PCR and serological assays, and microscopic examination, may also contribute to improving the detection of Bartonella spp. in biological samples and should be considered in future investigations. Future studies aiming at performing isolation and Whole Genome Sequencing of Neotropical wild-felids-associated Bartonella isolates are much needed to shed light on the diversity of bartonellae in these mammals.

The impact of Bartonella spp. on wild felid populations and associated zoonotic risks remain unknown, as does the potential role of these animals in maintaining and disseminating these pathogens. The lack of identification of Bartonella spp., usually found in domestic cats in L. geoffroyi suggests minimal interaction of the sampled animals with domestic cats, supporting a sylvatic pathogen life cycle in wild felids. The set of data in this study expands our knowledge of this emerging pathogens and highlights the need for further research on its impact on wildlife.

In a nutshell, these findings reinforce that besides harboring Bartonella species associated with cats, wild felids also may act as accidental hosts for Bartonella species associated with wild small mammals, possibly as a consequence of predation.
