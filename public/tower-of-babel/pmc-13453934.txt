From ICU Survival to Recovery: A Somalia-Adapted Nurse-Led Home Recovery Framework After Critical Illness in Low-Resource Settings
Authors: Abdishakur Mohamud Hassan Hidigow
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSurvival from critical illness is not equivalent to recovery, particularly in low-resource health systems where ICU survivors may return home with weakness, respiratory vulnerability, medication problems, poor wound care, malnutrition, psychological distress, caregiver burden, and limited follow-up. This commentary responds to the post-ICU care gap in Somalia by proposing a Somalia-adapted nurse-led home recovery framework. The framework is not presented as a fully validated intervention, but as a pragmatic implementation model for settings where ICU beds, rehabilitation services, transport, digital access, and specialist follow-up are constrained. It defines nurse-led home care as a tiered pathway coordinated by an ICU discharge nurse, supported by ward nurses, community health workers, physicians, rehabilitation providers, and referral facilities. The model includes risk stratification, discharge preparation, caregiver education, early phone follow-up, selective home visits, multidisciplinary referral, digital communication safeguards, and outcome tracking. Key implementation issues include workforce capacity, financing, supervision, quality indicators, privacy, medico-legal responsibility, transport barriers, gendered caregiver burden, and the digital divide. Strengthening post-ICU home recovery in Somalia requires local empirical evaluation, but immediate low-cost improvements can begin through structured discharge and follow-up systems.Keywords: critical care nursing, post-intensive care syndrome, home-based care, Somalia, transitional care, patient safety

Introduction

Survival from critical illness is increasingly recognized as only the first stage of recovery. Many patients leaving the intensive care unit (ICU) experience persistent physical, cognitive, psychological, and social impairments that affect daily functioning, family life, employment, and quality of life. The term post-intensive care syndrome describes new or worsening physical, cognitive, or mental health impairment after critical illness.1,2 These problems may include ICU-acquired weakness, fatigue, impaired mobility, memory problems, anxiety, depression, post-traumatic stress symptoms, sleep disturbance, and reduced ability to resume normal roles.1–3

In low-resource settings, the transition from ICU survival to safe recovery is particularly fragile. Patients may leave hospitals where ICU beds are scarce, rehabilitation services are limited, outpatient follow-up is poorly coordinated, and families carry most of the responsibility for home care. In Somalia, this challenge is intensified by workforce limitations, financing constraints, fragmented referral systems, and uneven access to specialized services. Somalia’s Health Sector Strategic Plan 2022–2026 emphasizes equitable and quality service delivery, while the national roadmap toward universal health coverage highlights the need to improve access and protect households from financial hardship.4,5 A recent nationwide assessment also reported important gaps in emergency and critical care readiness across Somali hospitals, including staffing, equipment, supplies, space, and clinical service capacity.6

This commentary proposes a Somalia-adapted nurse-led post-ICU home recovery framework. Its contribution is not the invention of transitional care itself, which is already well established, but the contextual adaptation of transitional care principles to a fragile health system where ICU nurses, family caregivers, community health workers, mobile communication, and referral facilities must work together despite resource constraints. The article defines the model, clarifies risk stratification, outlines implementation steps, and critically discusses feasibility, ethics, sustainability, and research needs.

The Post-ICU Recovery Gap in Somalia

The post-ICU gap begins before discharge. In low-resource hospitals, discharge may be shaped by bed pressure, patient affordability, staffing shortages, or the need to admit another critically ill patient. These pressures can result in discharge processes that focus on clinical stabilization rather than functional readiness, medication understanding, caregiver preparedness, rehabilitation needs, and follow-up planning. The result is a vulnerable transition from a monitored ICU environment to a household where professional support may be absent.

Post-ICU morbidity is multidimensional. Physical weakness may prevent safe walking, bathing, toileting, feeding, or return to work. Respiratory complications may follow pneumonia, acute respiratory distress, prolonged oxygen therapy, or mechanical ventilation. Wounds, pressure injuries, surgical sites, urinary catheters, feeding tubes, or tracheostomy-related needs may become sources of infection if families are not trained. Malnutrition can delay healing, while medication errors may occur when prescriptions are changed, duplicated, substituted, or misunderstood. Psychological distress and caregiver burden are often overlooked because attention remains focused on visible physical recovery.

Somalia-specific post-ICU outcome data remain scarce. This is a limitation and a research gap, not evidence that the problem is absent. A recovery-oriented ICU system should ask not only whether the patient survived admission, but whether the patient returned home safely, understood the care plan, obtained medications, received follow-up, avoided preventable complications, and regained function. Building even a minimal follow-up registry would help convert invisible post-discharge harm into measurable quality-improvement data.

Defining Nurse-Led Home-Based Care

In this commentary, nurse-led home-based care refers to a tiered post-discharge pathway coordinated by a trained ICU discharge nurse or senior critical care nurse. The nurse does not replace physicians, rehabilitation professionals, pharmacists, or community health workers. Instead, the nurse coordinates assessment, caregiver education, symptom monitoring, medication review, early warning recognition, documentation, and escalation. General ward nurses may support discharge preparation; community health workers may reinforce education and identify access barriers; physicians provide clinical backup; rehabilitation providers advise mobility and breathing exercises; and referral facilities manage deterioration.

This definition is important because home-based care in Somalia cannot rely only on ICU specialist nurses. Severe nursing shortages mean that a scalable model must use role division. ICU nurses should manage high-risk assessment and escalation decisions, while trained community health workers can support basic follow-up, appointment reminders, caregiver reinforcement, and referral linkage under supervision. This model requires written protocols, training, documentation tools, and clear responsibility for clinical decisions.

A Risk-Stratified Follow-Up Pathway

A practical model should prioritize patients most likely to deteriorate or be unable to access care. Risk stratification should be locally validated, but an initial pragmatic system can classify patients as low, moderate, or high risk. Low-risk patients are clinically stable, mobile with minimal assistance, have no complex wound or device, and have reliable caregiver support. Moderate-risk patients have one or more recovery risks such as persistent weakness, recent sepsis, multiple medications, poor nutrition, mild respiratory symptoms, or transport difficulty. High-risk patients include those with mechanical ventilation longer than 24 hours, ICU stay of three days or more, persistent oxygen requirement, delirium, severe mobility limitation, complex wound or device care, two or more major comorbidities, poor caregiver support, inability to obtain medicines, or repeated missed follow-up.

Follow-up intensity should vary by risk level. Low-risk patients may receive discharge education and an outpatient appointment. Moderate-risk patients should receive a nurse phone call within 48–72 hours and again within one to two weeks. High-risk patients should receive early nurse phone follow-up within 48 hours, physician escalation when warning signs are present, and selective home visits when transport, wound complexity, severe weakness, or caregiver burden makes facility review unrealistic. Warning signs should include worsening breathlessness, fever, confusion, reduced consciousness, chest pain, severe weakness, inability to eat or drink, reduced urine output, wound discharge, bleeding, uncontrolled pain, medication omission, or caregiver concern. Implementation in a Resource-Limited Health System

Implementation should begin with a discharge bundle rather than a complex post-ICU clinic. The bundle should include diagnosis, ICU course, medication plan, danger signs, wound or device instructions, nutrition advice, mobility and breathing exercises, follow-up date, and a named contact point. Caregiver education should use teach-back, because passive listening does not confirm understanding. Nurses should ask caregivers to explain medication timing, danger signs, and when to seek urgent care.

Low-cost digital tools may strengthen continuity, but only when linked to clinical action. Telephone calls, SMS reminders, voice messages, and secure messaging can support follow-up where transport costs and distance are barriers. The World Health Organization emphasizes that digital health interventions should strengthen health systems, not substitute for functioning services.7 Digital follow-up should therefore include consent, confidentiality, documentation, escalation protocols, and limits on informal messaging. For example, wound images should be sent only when consent is obtained, the platform is appropriate, access is restricted, and responsibility for clinical interpretation is clear.

Financing and sustainability require realistic planning. Hospitals may start with a small nurse coordinator role, use existing phones, integrate follow-up into discharge workflows, and reserve home visits for the highest-risk patients. Quality indicators should include follow-up completion, medication problems detected, warning signs identified, referral completion, readmission, death after discharge where known, caregiver concerns, and unresolved access barriers. These indicators can support quality improvement, advocacy, and future research.

Potential Benefits and Safety Concerns

Transitional care evidence suggests that structured discharge planning, caregiver coaching, and post-discharge follow-up can reduce rehospitalization and improve continuity in high-risk populations.8–10 However, these findings should not be uncritically transferred from high-income settings to Somalia. The expected benefit in Somalia is more modest and implementation-oriented: earlier detection of preventable complications, improved medication understanding, greater caregiver confidence, clearer referral decisions, and generation of local recovery data.

The model also has risks. Home visits may expose nurses to transportation difficulties or personal safety concerns. Phone follow-up may miss serious deterioration if patients cannot describe symptoms clearly. Digital messaging may create privacy risks, especially when wound images or family-shared phones are used. Female patients and caregivers may face cultural, mobility, or household-decision barriers that affect follow-up. A nurse-led model may also increase workload if no staffing time or incentives are provided. These concerns mean the model should be introduced as a supervised quality-improvement pathway, not as an unfunded expectation added to already overstretched nurses.

Practical Recommendations

Hospitals should first standardize ICU discharge preparation for high-risk patients. The discharge checklist should include medication reconciliation, caregiver education, warning signs, mobility advice, nutrition guidance, wound or device care, follow-up contact, and referral instructions. ICU nurses should be trained in post-ICU syndrome, caregiver teaching, medication safety, respiratory and wound monitoring, psychosocial screening, digital communication, and escalation decisions. Community health workers should be trained to reinforce education and identify access barriers, but complex clinical decisions should remain under nurse and physician supervision.

Policymakers should recognize post-ICU recovery as part of patient safety, rehabilitation, primary health care, and universal health coverage. Researchers should generate local evidence on post-ICU functional recovery, readmission, post-discharge mortality, caregiver burden, medication safety, feasibility of phone follow-up, digital privacy, and gendered barriers to care. Conclusion

ICU survival should not be the endpoint of critical care. In Somalia, many patients face their most vulnerable phase after discharge, when weakness, breathlessness, poor nutrition, medication confusion, wound problems, psychological distress, and caregiver burden become daily challenges at home. A Somalia-adapted nurse-led home recovery framework offers a practical pathway for safer transition from ICU to community care. Its value lies in risk stratification, role clarification, low-cost digital follow-up, caregiver education, escalation pathways, and outcome tracking adapted to local constraints. Future research should test feasibility, safety, cost, acceptability, equity, and clinical outcomes in Somali hospitals. Until such evidence is available, hospitals can begin with simple discharge checklists, early phone follow-up for high-risk patients, caregiver teach-back, and documented referral pathways.
