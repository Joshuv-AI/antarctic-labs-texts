ETV4 promotes colorectal cancer progression through SLC7A11-mediated ferroptosis inhibition
Authors: Zhouzhou Chao, Jinbao Yin, Fengxia Huang, Jinlian Xu, Yingyan Lv, Jiayao Chen, Aijing Xu, Wei Zhu, Jinxing Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundColorectal cancer (CRC) remains a leading cause of cancer-related mortality globally. Ferroptosis, a regulated form of cell death, has emerged as a promising frontier in CRC treatment. The transcriptional regulator ETV4 (ETS variant transcription factor 4) is implicated in CRC pathogenesis. However, its functional role has not been fully elucidated, and its potential to modulate ferroptosis in CRC is entirely unknown. This study aimed to investigate whether ETV4 modulates ferroptosis in CRC by regulating SLC7A11 and to explore the underlying mechanism involved.MethodsBioinformatic analysis was conducted to detect ETV4 expression and to identify pathways regulated by ETV4. Real‑time quantitative PCR (RT‑qPCR) and Western blot assays were used to measure gene expression at the mRNA and protein levels. The biological functions of ETV4 were assessed via CCK‑8, colony formation, wound‑healing, apoptosis analysis, transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and Transwell assays. Key ferroptosis markers—reactive oxygen species (ROS), malondialdehyde (MDA), mitochondrial membrane potential (JC‑1), and ferrous iron (FerroOrange) were examined to determine whether ETV4 knockdown promotes ferroptosis.ResultsETV4 is highly expressed in CRC tissues and cell lines, and its expression level is positively correlated with advanced TNM stages. Silencing ETV4 suppressed CRC cell proliferation, clonogenicity, and migration. Bioinformatic analysis confirmed that ETV4 may suppress the ferroptosis pathway. Functional assays revealed that ETV4 knockdown enhanced ferroptosis in CRC cells. Mechanistically, ETV4 depletion downregulated SLC7A11, whereas SLC7A11 overexpression reversed the ferroptosis phenotype induced by ETV4 knockdown.ConclusionETV4 promotes CRC progression by inhibiting ferroptosis through the upregulation of SLC7A11. Therefore, the ETV4/SLC7A11 axis represents a potential therapeutic target for CRC treatment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13062-026-00816-1.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is a common malignant tumor of the digestive tract with high morbidity and mortality [1]. Although surgery, radiotherapy, chemotherapy, targeted therapy, and immunotherapy prolong patient survival, distant metastasis and chemoresistance frequently lead to relapse, imposing a formidable challenge in clinical treatment [2, 3]. Therefore, identifying novel therapeutic strategies is essential for improving the clinical outcome of CRC patients. Ferroptosis, a recently identified form of cell death characterized by iron-dependent lipid peroxidation, glutathione depletion, and GPX4 inactivation, is distinct from other oxidative cell death modalities such as apoptosis, necroptosis, and pyroptosis, and has thus attracted considerable attention in cancer research [4, 5]. Accumulating evidence indicates that ferroptosis is closely linked to the survival and prognosis of CRC patients. The induction of ferroptosis suppresses CRC cell growth and dissemination while attenuating invasiveness and metastatic potential [6].

ETV4 (ETS variant transcription factor 4, also known as E1AF/PEA3), a member of the ETS transcription factor family, binds to promoters or enhancers of target genes to activate downstream transcriptional programs, thereby playing critical roles in cell proliferation, differentiation, and apoptosis [7]. ETV4 is frequently overexpressed in human cancers, including breast cancer, bladder cancer, hepatocellular carcinoma and colorectal cancer [8]. In breast cancer, ETV4 promotes tumor cell stemness by activating glycolysis and CXCR4-mediated Hedgehog signaling [9]. Additionally, ETV4-mediated transcriptional activation of SLC12A5 sensitizes breast cancer cells to ferroptosis [10]. The phosphorylation of ETV4 by the tyrosine kinase PTK6 increases the nuclear translocation of ETV4 and promotes the lymphatic metastasis of bladder cancer [11]. In CRC, ETV4 levels are more dramatically increased than those of other PEA3 group members, and ETV4 was reported to promote growth, metastasis, glycolysis, and oxaliplatin resistance by upregulating SLC38A5 [12, 13]. However, whether ETV4 can modulate CRC progression by regulating ferroptosis remains elusive.

The cystine/glutamate antiporter SLC7A11 (also known as xCT) plays a pivotal role in regulating ferroptosis [14]. In CRC, SLC7A11 promotes tumorigenesis by protecting tumor cells from ferroptosis and is highly correlated with GPX4 expression in CRC tissues [15]. Furthermore, SLC7A11 upregulation contributes to chemoresistance by inhibiting ferroptosis [16]. SLC7A11 also suppresses lipid peroxidation and ferroptosis, thereby facilitating the metastasis of CRC cells [17, 18]. However, it remains unclear whether ETV4 transcriptionally regulates SLC7A11 to inhibit ferroptosis and influence CRC progression. In this study, we demonstrated that ETV4 suppresses ferroptosis in CRC cells by modulating SLC7A11, which in turn promotes tumor progression. Our findings underscore the therapeutic potential of targeting ferroptosis in CRC treatment and highlight the ETV4/SLC7A11 axis as a promising target worthy of further investigation.

Materials and methods

Materials and reagents

SW480 and HCT116 cell lines were obtained from the Sun Yat-sen University Cancer Center (Guangzhou, China). Fetal bovine serum (FBS) was purchased from Zeta Life, Inc. (San Francisco, CA, USA). The universal DNA/siRNA transfection reagent was purchased from Polyplus Company (Illkirch, France). The PrimeScript™ RT Reagent Kit and TB Green® Premix Ex Taq™ II were purchased from Takara Bio (Kusatsu, Japan). ETV4 siRNA duplexes (si-ETV4) and the corresponding negative control (NC-ETV4) were obtained from GenePharma (Shanghai, China). The SLC7A11 overexpression plasmid (SLC7A11-OE) and its empty-vector control (SLC7A11-NC) were supplied by IGE Biotechnology (Guangzhou, China). The ROS assay kit and FerroOrange fluorescent probe were obtained from Dojindo Molecular Technologies (Kumamoto, Japan). Erastin and Z-VAD were purchased from MedChemExpress (Monmouth Junction, NJ, USA). Ferrostatin-1 (Fer-1) was obtained from APExBIO Technology (Houston, TX, USA). Apoptosis analysis kit was purchased from Vazyme company (Nanjing, China). Primary antibodies, such as ETV4 (10684-1-AP), SLC7A11 (26864-1-AP), GPX1 (67763-1-Ig) and GAPDH (60004-1-Ig), were purchased from Proteintech Group (Rosemont, IL, USA). Secondary antibodies, JC-1 assay kits, and MDA assay kits were purchased from Beyotime Biotechnology (Shanghai, China).

Cell culture and transfection

SW480 and HCT116 cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin–streptomycin at 37 °C in an incubator with 5% CO₂. For transfection purposes, cells in the exponential growth phase were seeded into 6-well plates and cultured until they reached 70~80% confluence. The JetPRIME reagent was used to transfect the cells with si-ETV4, NC-ETV4, SLC7A11-OE, or SLC7A11-NC, following the manufacturer’s protocol. After an incubation period of 24~48 h, the cells were collected for subsequent analyses.

RT‒qPCR

Total RNA was isolated via the use of the RNA-easy Isolation Reagent. Complementary DNA was synthesized via the PrimeScript™ RT Reagent Kit with gDNA Eraser, in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. Quantification of target gene expression was performed via real-time PCR via the TB Green® Premix Ex Taq™ II on a CFX96 system, with GAPDH serving as the endogenous reference gene. The sequences of primers used are listed below (5′→3′). GAPDH (F): CACCCACTCCTCCACCTTTGAC; GAPDH (R): GTCCACCACCCTGTTGCTGTAG; ETV4 (F): ACAGACGGACTTCGCCTACG; ETV4 (R): GCAGAGGTTTCTCATAGCCATAGC; SLC7A11 (F): ACGGTGGTGTGTTTGCTGTCTC; SLC7A11 (R): GCTGGTAGAGGAGTGTGCTTGC.

Western blot

The cells were lysed using RIPA buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Protein concentrations were determined using the bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay. For each sample, 20~40 µg of protein was separated to 10% SDS-PAGE. The proteins were subsequently transferred onto PVDF membranes and blocked with 5% skim milk for 2 h at room temperature. The membranes were incubated overnight (12~16 h) at 4 °C with primary antibodies. After three 10-min washes with TBST, the membranes were incubated with the appropriate HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 70 min at room temperature. Following three additional washes with TBST, immunoreactive bands were detected using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Band intensities were quantified via ImageJ software.

CCK-8 assay

A total of 3,000 transfected cells per well were seeded into 96-well plates and allowed to adhere for 4 h in an incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO₂. Then, 10 µl of CCK-8 solution was added to each well and incubated for 1 to 4 h at 37 °C. The absorbance was measured at 450 nm via a microplate reader to establish a baseline at 0 h. This procedure was repeated at 24, 48, 72, and 96 h, and the fold change in the absorbance value relative to that at 0 h was calculated to determine the proliferation rate. For drug treatment, the cells were harvested 24 h post transfection and seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 5,000 cells per well for an additional 24 h of culture to ensure complete attachment. Fer-1 (1 µM) and Erastin (5 µM) were subsequently administered and incubated for 24 to 72 h. Cell viability was measured via CCK-8 solution as described above.

Colony formation assay

The cells were seeded into 6-well plates at a density of 1,000 to 2,000 cells per well and subsequently cultured for 7 to 14 days. Upon completion of the culture period, the medium was removed, and the wells were gently rinsed with PBS. The cells were then fixed with 75% ethanol for 30 min and stained with 0.1% crystal violet for 30 min at room temperature. Following staining, the dye was aspirated, and the wells were washed twice with PBS. The plates were then photographed and colony numbers were counted.

Wound healing assay

The cells were seeded into 6-well plates at a density of 5 ~ 10 × 10⁵ cells per well and cultured until they reached 90% to 100% confluence. A sterile 200-µL pipette tip was used to create a linear scratch across the cell monolayer. The detached cells were gently washed away with PBS, and the initial image (0 h) was captured via a fluorescence microscope. The plates were subsequently returned to the incubator, and wound closure was documented by photographing the same field at intervals of 6, 12, 24, and 48 h.

Transwell assay

For the cell migration assay, the cells were subjected to serum starvation for 24 h in complete medium containing 1~2% FBS. A 24-well Transwell plate (8 μm pore size) was used for the assay. The lower chamber was filled with 500 µL of complete medium supplemented with 10% FBS, while 5 × 10⁴ cells suspended in 100 µL of serum-free medium were seeded into the upper chamber. Following 48 h of incubation, nonmigratory cells on the upper surface were carefully removed via a cotton swab. The chambers were subsequently rinsed with PBS, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, stained with 0.1% crystal violet for 20 min, washed, air-dried, and mounted on glass slides. The migrated cells were visualized under a light microscope and quantified via ImageJ software. For the cell invasion assay, the upper surface of the Transwell insert was precoated with 50 µL of Matrigel, diluted 1:10 in serum-free medium, and allowed to solidify at 37 °C for 2 h. Serum-starved cells, ranging from 2 ~ 4 × 10⁵ in number and suspended in 100 µL of serum-free medium, were introduced into the upper chamber. The lower chamber contained 500 µL of complete medium supplemented with 10% FBS, which served as a chemoattractant. After 48 h of incubation, the cells were fixed, stained, and quantified following the same protocol as described for the migration assay.

Reactive oxygen species (ROS) detection

The cells were initially seeded into 12-well plates and incubated overnight to facilitate attachment. Subsequently, Erastin was added, and the cells were cultured for an additional 24 ~ 48 h. The culture medium was subsequently discarded, and 1 mL of DCFH-DA dye working solution was added to each well in accordance with the manufacturer’s protocol. Following 20 ~ 30 min of incubation at 37 °C, the dye mixture was aspirated, and the cells were gently washed three times with HBSS (Hank's Balanced Salt Solution). ROS-dependent fluorescence was promptly visualized and documented via a fluorescence microscope with excitation at 488 nm and emission at 525 nm. The mean fluorescence intensity per field was quantified via ImageJ software.

JC-1 assay

The cells were seeded into 12-well plates and cultured overnight to allow for attachment. After 24 ~ 48 h of treatment with Erastin, the medium was replaced with 1 mL of JC-1 working solution, which was prepared according to the manufacturer’s guidelines, and incubated for 20 ~ 30 min at 37 °C. The cells were then gently washed twice with HBSS and immediately observed under a fluorescence microscope via the red channel with excitation/emission at 550/600 nm and the green channel at 485/535 nm. Representative images were captured, and the red-to-green fluorescence ratio was quantified via ImageJ software.

Malondialdehyde (MDA) assay

The cells were seeded into 10-cm dishes and cultured overnight to facilitate adherence. Subsequently, the cells were exposed to Erastin for 24 ~ 48 h. After drug treatment, the cells were harvested, washed with ice-cold PBS, and lysed in RIPA buffer supplemented with protease inhibitors. A small aliquot of the lysate was reserved for protein quantification via the BCA assay, while the remaining lysate was processed in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions for the MDA assay kit. The final reaction product was transferred to a 96-well plate, and the absorbance was measured at 532 nm via a microplate reader. The MDA content was normalized to the total protein concentration and expressed as nanomoles per milligram of protein.

FerroOrange assay

The cells were seeded into 12-well plates and cultured overnight to facilitate adherence. Following 24 ~ 48 h of treatment with Erastin, the medium was replaced with 1 mL of FerroOrange working solution, which was prepared in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions, and incubated for 20 ~ 30 min at 37 °C. The cells were subsequently gently washed twice with HBSS and immediately visualized via a fluorescence microscope (excitation at 543 nm and emission at 580 nm). Representative fields were captured, and the mean intracellular fluorescence intensity was quantified via ImageJ software.

Apoptosis analysis

Cells were transfected with si‑NC or si‑ETV4 duplexes. After 48 h of incubation, cells were collected and washed twice with PBS. Apoptosis was then analyzed using the Annexin V/PI apoptosis detection kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted utilizing GraphPad Prism 8. The normality assumptions were tested prior to performing parametric analyses. The data are presented as the mean ± standard deviations (SDs). Comparisons between two groups were carried out via an unpaired Student’s t test, whereas analyses involving multiple groups were performed using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test. A p value of less than 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

Results

ETV4 is highly expressed in CRC cells

As a member of the ETS transcription factor family, ETV4 has been implicated in tumor initiation and progression [9]. Analysis via the GEPIA database (http://gepia.cancer-pku.cn/index.html) revealed aberrant expression of ETV4 across various cancer types (Fig. 1a). Notably, ETV4 expression was significantly upregulated in CRC tissues compared with normal colorectal tissues (Fig. 1b). Consistently, the transcriptional levels of ETV4 were markedly elevated in a panel of CRC cell lines, particularly in HT29, SW480, and HCT116 cells, relative to those in the normal colonic epithelial cell line NCM460 (Fig. 1c). Further analysis via the UALCAN database (http://ualcan.path.uab.edu/index.html) revealed that ETV4 expression increased progressively with tumor stage (Fig. 1d, e). The survival analysis showed that in the TCGA‑COAD cohort, patients in the ETV4 high‑expression group had shorter survival, with a particularly significant reduction in progression‑free survival (Supplementary Fig. 1a). Collectively, these results indicate that ETV4 is highly expressed in CRC and may contribute to tumor progression.

Knockdown of ETV4 suppresses the proliferation and clonogenicity of CRC cells

To elucidate the functional role of ETV4 in CRC, we transfected SW480 and HCT116 cells with either ETV4-targeting siRNA (si-ETV4) or a nontargeting control (NC). The knockdown efficiency was confirmed at both the mRNA and protein levels via Western blot and RT‑qPCR assays, respectively. The results revealed a significant reduction in ETV4 expression in si-ETV4‑transfected cells compared with that in NC control cells (Fig. 2a-c). CCK‑8 assays demonstrated a substantial decrease in cell proliferation in ETV4 knockdown cells compared with NC cells (Fig. 2d). Consistently, colony formation assays revealed a marked suppressive effect on clonogenic potential upon ETV4 silencing (Fig. 2e, f). Together, these findings suggest that ETV4 knockdown impairs the proliferative capacity of CRC cells.

Knockdown of ETV4 suppresses the migratory capacity of CRC cells

Enhanced migratory capacity is a hallmark of increased malignancy in CRC [19]. Wound-healing assays revealed that cells transfected with si-ETV4 exhibited a significantly slower closure rate than NC cells (Fig. 3a, b). Consistently, Transwell migration and invasion assays showed a substantial decrease in the number of cells that traversed the membrane in the si-ETV4 groups compared with the NC groups (Fig. 3c–f). Taken together, these results demonstrate that ETV4 depletion impairs the motility and invasiveness of CRC cells, highlighting the role of ETV4 in CRC progression.

ETV4 regulates the ferroptosis pathway according to bioinformatic analysis

To investigate the downstream pathways associated with ETV4, we conducted a comprehensive bioinformatic analysis. Initially, utilizing the gene expression matrix of 58 intestinal tumor cell lines from the Cancer Cell Line Encyclopedia (CCLE) database, we systematically examined the correlation between the expression of ETV4 and other genes. Through preranked gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA), we revealed that genes that were positively correlated with ETV4 were significantly enriched in pathways that facilitate tumor malignant invasion, including the cell cycle, DNA replication, mismatch repair, and RNA degradation (Fig. 4a). Furthermore, applying a threshold of an absolute correlation coefficient ≥ 0.4 and a p-value < 0.001, we identified 1,854 core genes related to ETV4 from a total of 19,803 genes. We subsequently performed unsupervised clustering analysis on 701 colorectal samples (comprising 650 tumor tissues and 51 adjacent normal tissues) from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database on the basis of ETV4-related genes. At a cluster number of K = 2, the samples were distinctly divided into two subgroups: cluster 1 (n = 420) and cluster 2 (n = 281), with ETV4 expression being significantly higher in cluster 1 than in cluster 2 (Fig. 4b–e, p = 1.4e-12). The application of the ssGSEA algorithm to quantify the enrichment scores of ferroptosis-related genes revealed that the enrichment scores for ferroptosis suppressor genes were significantly greater in cluster 1 than in cluster 2. Conversely, ferroptosis activator genes presented higher enrichment scores in cluster 2 (Fig. 4f–g). This pattern indicates a positive correlation between ETV4 expression and a ferroptosis-suppressed state. Furthermore, analysis of single-cell data from 111,480 colorectal tumor cells in the GSE178341 dataset, which utilized the AUCell algorithm, allowed the quantification of ferroptosis suppressor gene activity. By applying a threshold of 0.22, we identified 77,312 tumor cells as exhibiting ferroptosis-suppressive characteristics (Fig. 4h). Within this subset, ETV4 expression demonstrated a significant positive correlation with the expression of SLC7A11, a pivotal ferroptosis suppressor gene (Fig. 4i, correlation coefficient = 0.4, p < 0.001). This finding further substantiates, at the single-cell level, the regulatory influence of ETV4 on the ferroptosis suppression pathway.

Knockdown of ETV4 promotes ferroptosis in CRC cells

Previous research has demonstrated that ETV4 regulates ferroptosis and drives Warburg-like metabolic reprogramming in breast cancer [10]. However, whether ETV4 modulates ferroptosis in CRC and thereby influences tumor progression remains unclear. Our results show that ETV4- knockdown cells are more sensitive to Erastin-induced cell death (Fig. 5a). The reduction in viability caused by ETV4 knockdown was largely reversed by co-treatment with the ferroptosis inhibitor Fer-1, whereas the apoptosis inhibitor Z-VAD had no significant effect (Fig. 5b). Moreover, ETV4 knockdown did not significantly increase cell apoptosis (Supplementary Fig. 2a, b). FerroOrange staining revealed a marked increase in orange-red fluorescence intensity in ETV4-knockdown cells, indicating elevated intracellular free iron levels (Fig. 5c). Consistently, cellular ROS levels were significantly higher in the si-ETV4 groups than in NC groups (Fig. 5d). JC-1 assays further demonstrated a marked decrease in mitochondrial membrane potential following ETV4 knockdown (Fig. 5e). In addition, transmission electron microscopy (TEM) showed that ETV4 knockdown induced mitochondrial morphological alterations, characterized by reduced mitochondrial volume and decreased cristae (Supplementary Fig. 2e). Lipid peroxidation, assessed by malondialdehyde (MDA) quantification, was also elevated in ETV4-knockdown cells treated with Erastin (Fig. 5f). Western blotting confirmed that the ferroptosis marker GPX4 protein level was significantly decreased in the si-ETV4 groups compared with NC groups, further verifying that ETV4 knockdown promotes ferroptosis (Supplementary Fig. 2c, d). Collectively, these findings suggest that ETV4 silencing enhances Erastin-induced increases in intracellular iron, ROS, and lipid peroxidation, as well as mitochondrial dysfunction, thereby promoting ferroptosis. We therefore propose that ETV4 facilitates CRC progression, at least in part, by suppressing ferroptosis.

ETV4 positively regulates SLC7A11 expression in CRC cells

SLC7A11, a solute carrier family member, mediates the import of extracellular cystine for glutathione synthesis and is essential for protection against ferroptosis [14]. Bioinformatic analysis revealed that SLC7A11 is also highly expressed in CRC tissues (Fig. 6a). RT-qPCR revealed that SLC7A11 mRNA levels were markedly reduced in ETV4-knockdown CRC cells (Fig. 6b), and Western blot confirmed a corresponding decrease in SLC7A11 protein levels (Fig. 6c, d). To functionally rescue this downregulation, we generated SLC7A11-overexpression (SLC7A11-OE) and control (SLC7A11-NC) plasmids and co-transfected them with si-ETV4 into SW480 and HCT116 cells. Western blot analysis verified robust restoration of SLC7A11 protein in both cell lines (Fig. 6e, f). Collectively, these data demonstrate that ETV4 positively regulates SLC7A11 expression and suggest that ETV4 modulates ferroptosis in CRC cells, at least in part, through the regulation of SLC7A11.

ETV4 inhibits ferroptosis in CRC cells by upregulating SLC7A11

To investigate the role of ETV4 in regulating ferroptosis in CRC cells via SLC7A11, we conducted co-transfection experiments using si-ETV4 and SLC7A11 overexpression (SLC7A11-OE) constructs in SW480 and HCT116 cells. Silencing ETV4 alone significantly decreased cell viability, whereas simultaneous overexpression of SLC7A11 partially restored cell viability (Fig. 7a). Consistently, re-expression of SLC7A11 substantially mitigated the increased ROS accumulation, mitochondrial membrane depolarization, and intracellular Fe²⁺ levels induced by ETV4 knockdown (Fig. 7b–d). These data demonstrate that ETV4 inhibits ferroptosis in CRC cells by upregulating SLC7A11.

Discussion

Colorectal cancer (CRC) remains a leading cause of morbidity and mortality among gastrointestinal malignancies worldwide, underscoring the urgent need to identify novel therapeutic targets to improve clinical outcomes [2]. ETV4, an ETS-domain transcription factor, plays a critical role in differentiation, embryogenesis, migration, and invasion. It is frequently overexpressed in various pathologies, including oral squamous cell carcinoma, osteoarthritis, and lung cancer, and has been established as an independent prognostic biomarker associated with poor survival [8, 20]. Functionally, ETV4 promotes the G1–S phase transition by transactivating Cyclin D1 in pancreatic and cholangiocarcinoma cells, thereby accelerating tumor cell cycle progression [21]. It also contributes to immune evasion, as high ETV4 expression is correlated with reduced infiltration of CD8⁺ T cells and natural killer (NK) cells, suggesting that its inhibition could synergize with immunotherapy [22]. In hepatocellular carcinoma and lung cancer, ETV4 enhances cellular migration and invasion by upregulating MMP1 and N-cadherin [23, 24]. ETV4 has also been shown to enhance TGF-β/Smad signaling, leading to increased proliferation, migration, invasion, and glycolysis in cholangiocarcinoma [25]. Here, we observed that ETV4 is significantly upregulated in CRC cells, with expression levels positively correlated with advanced TNM stages (Fig. 1). Furthermore, ETV4 knockdown in SW480 and HCT116 cells markedly reduced cell viability, proliferation, clonogenicity, and motility, indicating that targeting ETV4 effectively impedes CRC progression (Figs. 2 and 3).

To investigate the mechanisms through which ETV4 promotes CRC progression, we performed bioinformatic analysis. Our results showed a positive correlation between ETV4 expression and a ferroptosis-suppressed state. Furthermore, ETV4 expression demonstrated a significant positive correlation with the expression of SLC7A11, a pivotal ferroptosis suppressor gene. However, the inherent biases of bulk transcriptomic repositories like TCGA, both in sample composition and technical processing, are inevitable [26, 27]. Therefore, we performed validation using multiple databases, including the Human Colon Cancer Atlas single-cell transcriptomic dataset (https://singlecell.broadinstitute.org) and single-cell spatial transcriptomics data, to analyze the co‑localization of ETV4 and SLC7A11 expression (Supplementary Fig. 1b, c). Additionally, we knocked down ETV4 in CRC cells via siRNA and examined its downstream effects. Our results revealed that ETV4 knockdown led to increased intracellular Fe²⁺ levels and elevated lipid peroxidation (Fig. 5), both of which are hallmarks of ferroptosis [28]. These findings suggest a potential role for ETV4 in regulating ferroptosis. Among the solute carrier (SLC) superfamily, the cystine/glutamate transporter system antiporter components SLC3A2 and SLC7A11 are known to regulate ferroptosis by facilitating cystine import for glutathione synthesis [29]. The increased expression of SLC7A11 in hepatocellular carcinoma has been shown to inhibit ferroptosis and promote tumor progression [30]. Similarly, SLC7A11 has been identified as a downstream target of HTRA1 that mediates ferroptosis and chemoresistance in CRC cells [31]. Besides, N6-methyladenosine (m6A) modification of SLC7A11 regulates ferroptosis in hepatocellular carcinoma cells [32]. Our results showed that ETV4 knockdown significantly reduced SLC7A11 expression (Fig. 6). Furthermore, when SLC7A11 was overexpressed (SLC7A11-OE) in ETV4-knockdown cells, the increases in lipid peroxidation and intracellular Fe²⁺ levels were attenuated compared with those in ETV4-knockdown cells alone (Fig. 7). These findings indicate that ETV4 may suppress ferroptosis in CRC cells at least in part by regulating SLC7A11 expression.

The development of chemotherapy resistance is a major cause of CRC recurrence. Enhancing the susceptibility of CRC cells to ferroptosis represents a promising strategy to overcome such resistance. Actually, post-transcriptional and epitranscriptional modification, such as m6A, also plays a role in chemosensitization by increasing the ferroptosis susceptibility [33]. Although ETV4 reportedly promotes CRC growth, metastasis, and oxaliplatin resistance, its role in regulating ferroptosis in CRC remains unclear [12]. In this study, silencing ETV4 triggered ferroptosis and increased CRC cell sensitivity to the ferroptosis inducer Erastin, whereas the ferroptosis inhibitor Fer-1 restored the loss of viability caused by ETV4 depletion (Fig. 5). These findings suggest that targeting ETV4 could be a viable strategy for ferroptosis-based therapy.

Previous research has shown that targeting LGR4 activates ferroptosis and enhances chemotherapeutic toxicity by downregulating SLC7A11 [34]. Here, we identified SLC7A11 as a downstream target of ETV4 that mediates ferroptosis regulation in CRC cells (Figs. 6 and 7). Given that ferroptosis represents a redox-dependent death program and that SLC7A11 plays an important role in glutathione synthesis, the interruption of the ETV4/SLC7A11 axis may disrupt the redox balance and provide potential strategies for treating cancer [14, 35]. Our study reveals a novel regulatory network centered on the ETV4/SLC7A11 axis in modulating ferroptosis in CRC. However, this study has several limitations, including the lack of in vivo validation using animal models and clinical patient samples. Further research is needed to evaluate the translational potential of the ETV4/SLC7A11 axis as a therapeutic target. Collectively, our results indicate that ETV4 supports CRC progression by sustaining SLC7A11 expression and thereby suppressing ferroptosis.

Conclusion

ETV4 is highly expressed in CRC and upregulates SLC7A11, thereby inhibiting ferroptosis and promoting CRC progression. Our findings advance the understanding of ferroptosis regulation and identify the ETV4/SLC7A11 axis as a potential therapeutic target for CRC treatment.
