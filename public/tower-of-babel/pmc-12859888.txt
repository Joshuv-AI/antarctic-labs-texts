Clinical characteristics and survival among HIV-infected people undergoing maintenance hemodialysis: a comparative study from Shanghai, China
Authors: Xiaoqin Le, Nan Chen, Meipeng Wu, Qiongqiong Li, Qingmei Yang, Jun Chen, Renfang Zhang, Li Liu, Yinzhong Shen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMortality remains high among people living with HIV (PLHIV) undergoing maintenance hemodialysis (MHD). However, data on the survival of MHD patients in first-tier Chinese cities are limited. This study aimed to analyze the clinical characteristics and survival of PLHIV receiving MHD in Shanghai.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cohort study at the Blood Purification Center of Shanghai Public Health Clinical Center. Clinical data were collected for PLHIV and HIV-negative controls who initiated MHD between November 2011 and September 2023. Survival was compared using Kaplan-Meier curves, and risk factors were identified through Cox regression analyses.ResultsThe study included 45 PLHIV and 54 HIV-negative individuals who underwent MHD for over three months. PLHIV were significantly younger at MHD initiation and had a higher proportion of males compared to controls (54.4 ± 14.4 vs. 61.6 ± 16.5 years, p = 0.025; 88.9% vs. 64.8%, p = 0.005). At baseline, the mean CD4 count for PLHIV was 261.1 ± 155.2 cells/µL, and 66.7% (30/45) had undetectable viral loads. The maximum follow-up durations were 145 months for PLHIV and 107 months for controls, with median survival times of 45 months and 61 months, respectively. Survival rates at 12, 24, 36, and 60 months were lower in the HIV group than in the control group (74.8% vs. 88.7%, 71.4% vs. 80.2%, 67.2% vs. 67.7%, and 33.2% vs. 67.7%), with a statistically significant difference observed only at 60 months (p = 0.0343). The overall difference in cumulative survival probability between the two groups was not significant (Log-rank test, p = 0.15). After adjusting for covariates, age ≥ 60 years at MHD initiation was significantly associated with reduced survival in both PLHIV (hazard ratio [HR] 3.14, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.15–8.54, p = 0.025) and the entire patient cohort (HR 3.15, 95% CI: 1.31–7.58, p = 0.01).ConclusionPLHIV initiated MHD at a younger age but exhibited lower long-term survival rates, particularly at 60 months, compared to HIV-negative individuals. Age ≥ 60 years at dialysis initiation was an independent risk factor for mortality in PLHIV.Trial registrationNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-025-12489-8.

Introduction

Since the first case of AIDS was reported in 1981, approximately 88.4 million people have become infected with HIV, resulting in an estimated 42.3 million deaths by 2023 [1]. The widespread adoption of highly active antiretroviral therapy (ART) has significantly improved overall survival and life expectancy for people living with HIV (PLHIV). However, kidney disease remains a significant comorbidity in PLHIV [2]. In recent years, the introduction of ART has transformed the spectrum of renal diseases linked to HIV infection. There has been a decline in the prevalence of classic HIV-associated nephropathy (HIVAN) and an accompanying increase in other histological patterns, such as noncollapsing focal segmental glomerulosclerosis, immune complex glomerulonephritis, ART nephrotoxicity, and comorbid kidney disease in PLHIV [3, 4]. PLHIV are at an increased risk of developing end-stage renal disease (ESRD)—defined as the onset of long-term dialysis (hemodialysis or peritoneal dialysis) or receipt of kidney transplant, as one study has shown that the prevalence reached 1.9 per 1000 patients in 2010 [5]. Available evidence suggests that ESRD occurs approximately 5.5 months earlier in PLHIV compared to their HIV-negative counterparts [6]. Moreover, mortality among PLHIV receiving renal replacement therapy, including maintenance hemodialysis (MHD) and peritoneal dialysis, remains substantially high [7].

Several studies have assessed survival rates and influencing factors among HIV positive patients with dialysis in the post-ART era. Nonetheless, most of them were performed in the United States, Europe, or Africa. Limited data are available on the survival status of MHD patients in first-tier Chinese megacities, which typically have more advanced healthcare services. Therefore, this study aims to fill this knowledge gap by providing contemporary data from a major urban center in China, reflecting the outcomes within a relatively advanced healthcare system.

This study aimed to analyze the demographic and clinical characteristics, as well as survival rates, of PLHIV initiating MHD at the Shanghai Public Health Clinical Center (SPHCC), Fudan University, over the past twelve years. We compared these data with those of HIV-negative individuals undergoing MHD during the same period and sought to identify potential risk factors associated with survival and prognosis.

Materials and methods

Study population

A retrospective cohort study was performed in the Department of Nephrology at SPHCC, the sole designated hospital providing hemodialysis for HIV-positive patients in Shanghai. We collected the clinical data of HIV infected (HIV group) and non-HIV infected (non-HIV group) patients who initiated MHD from November 2011 to September 2023. The first HIV positive patient started MHD in November 2011 and the first HIV negative one in January 2015. Nevertheless, most participants in both groups were enrolled between 2017 and 2023 (Figure S1). The final following-up time was December 31st 2023. The inclusion criteria were listed as follows: age ≥ 18 years old; diagnosed with ESRD and initiating hemodialysis to sustain life for at least three months; HIV infection was confirmed at or before dialysis initiation through western blot test. The exclusion criteria were people with acute renal failure, acute congestive heart failure, poisoning, severe inflammatory response syndrome, etc., receiving blood purification therapy less than three months.

Clinical materials

The following clinical data were collected: sex, age at initiation of renal replacement therapy (including hemodialysis, peritoneal dialysis and renal transplant) and MHD, comorbidities, vascular access, weekly dialysis frequency, ART use and regimen at dialysis initiation, survival status, and blood parameters (baseline, lowest, and current/last CD4 cell count; baseline HIV viral load; baseline serum levels of hemoglobin, albumin, calcium, and phosphorus).

Statistical analysis

SPSS version 26.0 was used for data analysis and R software version 4.3.1 for graph drawing. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation if following a normal distribution or as median (inter quartile range, IQR) if not normal. Student t-test and Mann-Whitney U test were used to compare the differences, respectively. Categorical variables were expressed as percentages and the comparisons were done by Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. We applied Kaplan-Meier method to plot the survival curve in HIV infection group and non-HIV group, log-rank test to compare total cum survival probability, and Z test to compare the survival probabilities in different years. Cox proportional hazard regression model was conducted to estimate the factors associated with the survival of MHD. To confirm the assumption of proportionality, time-dependent covariate analysis was used. All variables with a P value < 0.05 in the univariate analysis or other potential confounding factors were included in the multivariate model. The mold fit was evaluated by Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) and concordance (C-index). A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Demographic and clinical characteristics of PLHIV

From November 2011, 45 PLHIV received MHD at our hospital. The mean age at MHD initiation was 54.4 ± 14.4 years, and 88.9% (40/45) were male. At baseline, the mean CD4 count was 261.1 ± 155.2 cells/µL, and 62.8% (27/43) had CD4 counts > 200 cells/µL (data unavailable for two patients). The lowest recorded CD4 count was 204.5 ± 118.8 cells/µL (data unavailable for two patients). The current or last CD4 count was 234.3 (IQR: 181.7–360.0) cells/µL (data unavailable for one patient). At MHD initiation, the median duration since HIV diagnosis was 3.0 years (IQR: 0.0–7.0). Overall, 68.9% (31/45) were receiving ART, and 66.7% (30/45) had undetectable HIV viral loads. All patients received ART after starting dialysis, with regimens based on integrase inhibitors (INSTIs) in 46.7% (21/45), efavirenz (EFV) in 24.4% (11/45), and ritonavir/lopinavir (LPV/r) in 6.7% (3/45); the remaining 22.2% (10/45) received mixed or unknown regimens. Comparison of clinical characteristics and survival between HIV group and non-HIV group

Fifty-four HIV-negative patients receiving MHD during the same period were included as controls. The HIV group had a significantly higher proportion of males (88.9% vs. 64.8%, p = 0.005) and were younger at MHD initiation (54.4 ± 14.4 vs. 61.6 ± 16.5 years, p = 0.025). The proportion of patients aged ≥ 60 years at MHD initiation did not differ significantly between groups. PLHIV also progressed to ESRD requiring renal replacement therapy at a younger age (53.9 ± 14.9 vs. 61.1 ± 16.7 years, p = 0.027). At baseline, the HIV group had significantly lower hemoglobin and serum albumin levels (both p < 0.05). Coinfections with syphilis and hepatitis B virus (HBV) were more prevalent in the HIV group (46.7% vs. 1.9%, p < 0.001; 11.1% vs. 0%, p = 0.04, respectively) (Table 1).

From November 2011 to September 2023, with maximum follow-up periods of 145 months for PLHIV and 107 months for controls, the median survival times were 45 months and 61 months, respectively. Mortality rates were 42.2% (19/45) in the HIV group and 16.7% (9/54) in the non-HIV group. Causes of death were documented for 17 patients in the HIV group; the leading causes were various infections, hemorrhagic shock, and acute cardiovascular events (acute myocardial infarction or sudden cardiac arrest). Survival rates at 12, 24, 36, and 60 months were lower in the HIV group than in the non-HIV group (74.8% vs. 88.7%, 71.4% vs. 80.2%, 67.2% vs. 67.7%, and 33.2% vs. 67.7%, respectively), but the difference reached statistical significance only at 60 months (p < 0.05) (Table 1). Short-term survival rates stratified by calendar year and HIV status are shown in Table S1. Due to the limited number of cases in certain calendar years, we aggregated the data into broader time periods (e.g., 2011–2020 and 2021– 2023), providing more robust statistical power.

Survival analysis comparison in individuals with and without HIV infection after MHD initiation

The survival curves were plotted through the Kaplan–Meier method in patients with and without HIV infection. Although the cumulative survival probability appeared higher in the non-HIV group, the log-rank test indicated no statistically significant difference (X² = 2.237, p = 0.15), as shown in Fig. 1. As most participants in both groups were enrolled between 2017 and 2023, a log-rank test of survival probabilities was performed, which also showed no significant difference (Figure S2).

The factors associated with survival probabilities in PLHIV and all people undergoing MHD

Cox regression models were used to identify predictors of survival in both univariate and multivariate analyses. For PLHIV, univariate analysis indicated that age ≥ 60 years at MHD initiation, history of tuberculosis, and hepatitis C virus (HCV) and syphilis coinfection were significantly associated with increased mortality risk (all p < 0.05). In the multivariable Cox regression analysis, only age ≥ 60 years remained significantly associated with mortality (HR = 3.14, 95% CI: 1.15–8.54, p = 0.025). The model demonstrated good discriminative ability (C-index = 0.764) with an AIC value of 110.58, indicating an improved model fit (Table 2).

For the entire cohort, univariate analysis identified older age (≥ 60 years) at MHD initiation, history of tumor, and HCV and syphilis coinfection as risk factors for reduced survival. In the multivariable analysis of 99 participants with 28 observed events, both the statistical significance of univariable analyses and potential confounding factors were considered. Several combinations of covariates were tested, and the differences in AIC values among these models were less than 2 (Table S2). The model with the highest concordance index (C-index) was selected as the final model. In this model, older age (≥ 60 years) was independently associated with an increased risk of mortality (HR = 3.15, 95% CI: 1.31–7.58, p = 0.01), whereas HIV infection, syphilis coinfection, and history of tumor were not statistically significant predictors (Table 3). The overall model demonstrated good discrimination (C-index = 0.73) and acceptable fit (Likelihood ratio test = 15.99, p = 0.003; AIC = 198.96) (Table S2).

Discussion

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) has become increasingly common in ART era. Several factors are significantly associated with the progression from CKD to ESRD in PLHIV, including high plasma viral loads, race, diabetes, hypertension, and cardiovascular disease. Studies from the USA and Europe have reported that the incidence of ESRD was two to three times higher in PLHIV compared to the general population between 1998 and 2010 [5, 8]. Furthermore, a study of American veterans found that HIV-infected adults were diagnosed with ESRD approximately 5.5 months earlier than uninfected individuals [6]. In our study, the average age at initiation of renal replacement therapy or MHD was 7.2 years younger in the HIV group—a statistically significant difference. This finding may suggest suboptimal management and more rapid progression of CKD among PLHIV in our setting, underscoring the urgent need to enhance CKD care for this population. Key strategies should include: (1) regular assessment and monitoring of kidney health in PLHIV, tailored to individual risk profiles; (2) development of appropriate antiretroviral regimens for patients with CKD; and (3) timely nephrology referral when indicated [9, 10].

At baseline, 68.9% patients have received ART regularly, 66.7% had undetectable HIV viral loads, and 62.8% had CD4 counts over 200 cell/µL. This profile, which is comparable to reports from Cameroon [7] but indicates poorer virological control than some European cohorts [11]. This suggests that the progression to end-stage renal disease in these patients is likely driven by factors beyond HIV itself, with comorbid conditions playing a critical role. The significantly higher prevalences of syphilis and HBV coinfection in our HIV group are particularly noteworthy. Syphilis can directly induce various glomerular pathologies, including membranous nephropathy, and appropriate antimicrobial treatment often improves proteinuria and renal function [12, 13]. Similarly, HBV is an established risk factor for CKD, proteinuria, and accelerated eGFR decline—a risk potentially amplified by HIV coinfection and the nephrotoxic potential of certain antivirals (e.g., tenofovir) [14, 15]. Thus, these coinfections are not merely comorbidities but likely significant contributors to both initial renal damage and the long-term survival outcomes observed.

The 12-, 36-, and 60-month survival rates in our HIV group (74.8%, 67.2%, and 33.2%, respectively) were higher than recent data from Guangxi Province, China [16], but lower than those from a Spanish cohort followed between 1999 and 2006 [17]. These differences may be attributable to variations in regional healthcare services and chronological improvements in ART and dialysis techniques. Compared with HIV-negative patients in our study, PLHIV had lower cumulative survival, but the difference was not statistically significant (log-rank test, p = 0.15). Consistent with this, Cox regression analysis indicated that HIV infection itself was not an independent risk factor for mortality in the entire MHD cohort. A French cohort study from 2002 to 2004 also found no significant mortality difference between HIV-infected and uninfected ESRD patients [18]. In contrast, other studies have reported worse prognosis for PLHIV on dialysis [7, 17]. This may be attributable to the demographic and clinical characteristics of the studied cohorts, such as lower rates of ART coverage and a higher prevalence of HCV coinfection.

The median survival time was shorter for PLHIV than for controls (45 vs. 61 months). Although the log-rank test showed no significant difference in overall cumulative survival, the five-year survival rate was markedly lower in the HIV group (33.2% vs. 67.7%, p < 0.05). The significantly lower five-year survival rate highlights a distinct long-term survival disadvantage for PLHIV, suggesting the detrimental effects accumulate over time. Contributing factors may include a higher comorbidity burden, delayed referral for dialysis, and challenges in CKD management specific to PLHIV, such as drug interactions, ART-related toxicities, and vascular access issues. These findings underscore the profound impact of HIV on long-term prognosis in MHD patients and highlight the need for early referral, intensified monitoring, and integrated care strategies.

Cox regression identified age ≥ 60 years at MHD initiation as an independent risk factor for mortality after adjustment (HR 3.14, 95% CI: 1.15–8.54, p = 0.025 for PLHIV; HR 3.15, 95% CI: 1.31–7.58, p = 0.01 for all patients). This aligns with findings by Atta et al., who reported a 1.3-fold increase in mortality risk per additional year of age at renal replacement therapy initiation [19]. Other factors, such as low CD4 count, low serum albumin, and absence of ART at dialysis initiation—established as poor prognostic indicators in other studies [16, 18, 19]—were not associated with survival in our cohort. This may be because most PLHIV in our study were already on ART before progressing to ESRD, resulting in controlled viremia, higher CD4 counts, and better nutritional status. Interestingly, a history of tuberculosis was associated with a 4.52-fold increased mortality risk in PLHIV in univariate analysis (95% CI: 1.24–16.49, p = 0.022), though this was not independent in multivariate analysis. Data on tuberculosis susceptibility and its impact on mortality in PLHIV on dialysis are scarce. One recent study identified tuberculosis as the most common cause of death in HIV/AIDS patients on hemodialysis admitted to the intensive care unit [20]. Large-scale epidemiological studies on tuberculosis in Chinese HIV/AIDS patients with ESRD are crucial to assess the current burden and to develop effective interventions to reduce mortality. HIV and tuberculosis coinfection significantly increases CKD-related morbidity and mortality, warranting special consideration in future research and clinical management [21].

Our study has several limitations. First, the skewed sex distribution, reflecting the local epidemiology of HIV, may introduce residual confounding, and results should be interpreted with caution. Second, due to incomplete data, serial blood tests and dialysis adequacy indices were not included in the risk factor analysis, potentially limiting the generalizability of our findings. Third, the relatively small sample size may have limited statistical power to detect other potential prognostic factors, including certain coinfections. Fourth, as a single-center study conducted at the sole designated hospital for HIV-positive patients in Shanghai, the potential for referral bias exists, which may affect the generalizability of our results. Future multicenter studies with larger sample sizes are warranted to validate these findings.

Conclusion

CKD and ESRD remain significant clinical challenges in the management and survival of PLHIV. In our cohort, PLHIV undergoing MHD had high rates of ART use and CD4 cell counts but initiated dialysis at a younger age than HIV-negative individuals. Their survival rates were generally lower, particularly at five years. Age ≥ 60 years at MHD initiation was a significant independent predictor of poor survival in PLHIV. Enhancing comprehensive management is essential to improving dialysis outcomes for PLHIV.
