Risk factors for catastrophic healthcare expenditure and high economic burden for children with anorectal malformations in Southwestern Uganda
Authors: Felix Oyania, Caroline Q. Stephens, Sarah Ullrich, Amy M. Shui, Meera Kotagal, Godfrey Zari Rukundo, Joseph Ngonzi, Ava Yap, Francis Bajunirwe, Dan Poenaru, Doruk E. Ozgediz
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAnorectal malformations (ARMs) are common congenital anomalies in low-and middle-income countries (LMICs), and they are often repaired in a staged manner. High out-of-pocket (OOP) payment for surgical care in many LMICs makes households vulnerable to catastrophic health expenditures (CHE). ARM patients often require multiple operations and hospitalizations, which may make them vulnerable to CHE. In this study, we sought to determine the prevalence of CHE and the factors driving these costs among families of children with ARMs in southwestern Uganda.MethodsThis was a combined retrospective and prospective cohort study of the OOP and CHE among families of children with ARMs at a Regional Referral Hospital between June 2021 and July 2023. CHE was defined as a cost exceeding 10% of annual income. Patient characteristics were compared, and multivariable modeling with best subset analysis was used to determine which factors were significantly associated with CHE and total OOP expenditure.ResultsThere were 236 study participants with a median age at diagnosis of 6 days, 51% were male, 71% lived in rural areas, and the median distance traveled was 175 km. 64% of patients experienced CHE, with almost all families incurring travel costs (99%). Following best subset analysis, distance traveled (OR 1.06, 95% CI: 1.03–1.08) and rurality (OR 1.83, 95% CI: 0.96–3.48) were significantly associated with CHE, suggesting that for every additional 10 km, a patient traveled for care, there were 6% higher odds of incurring CHE. In examining total cost, patients who had a two-stage repair incurred more than twice the costs compared to those who had a single-stage repair, and education level and repair type were also significantly associated.ConclusionIdentifying methods to provide financial protection from CHE is essential for all children. ARM patients are at particularly high risk for CHE and high OPP expenditures, especially those living far from healthcare services and in rural areas.

Introduction

The Lancet Commission on Global Surgery (2015) and the updated Utstein consensus report (2021) highlight financial healthcare protection as vital to equitable surgical care [1, 2]. In low-income settings, including Uganda, patients frequently pay out-of-pocket (OOP) for diagnostics and essential medical supplies, with these burdens even greater for those needing Pediatric surgery due to service scarcity and additional costs for travel, lodging, and food. Such expenses often lead to catastrophic health expenditure (CHE), forcing families to borrow, sell assets, or rely on others for support, which deepens poverty [3–10]. Persistent OOP payments and the lack of national health insurance leave patients vulnerable to CHE and prevent many from seeking surgery, especially among the most disadvantaged [11–15].

In addition to these economic challenges encountered while receiving care, it is difficult for children to receive care due to the limited pediatric surgical capacity in LMICs. Congenital anomalies, such as anorectal malformations (ARMs), require highly trained pediatric surgeons for reconstruction due to the technical nature of the repair. Some malformations are repaired in a staged manner, requiring anywhere from one to three surgeries. Thus, many ARM patients make several trips to the hospital before receiving definitive treatment [8–10]. This contributes to the significant costs incurred by families, exacerbated by long wait times for surgeries and limited access to surgical care [16–18].

We sought to determine the prevalence of CHE and associated risk factors driving these costs among families of children with ARMs in Southwestern Uganda. We also aimed to characterize CHE, the components of OOP expenditure, and the frequency by which families had to utilize distress health financing.

Methods

Study design & setting

This was a combined retrospective and prospective cohort study of the OOP and CHE among families of children with ARM who presented to Mbarara Regional Referral Hospital for surgical repair between June 2021 and July 2023. CHE was defined as a cost exceeding 10% of annual income. This definition was applied to the total summed OOP expenditure (direct + indirect costs), the family estimated total expenditure, and the amount of funds raised to pay for care. Patient/family characteristics were compared, and multivariable modeling was used to determine which factors were significantly associated with CHE and total OOP expenditure.

Participants, data collection, data sources

For the prospective cohort, all patients who presented between June 2021 and July 2023 with ARM were invited to participate in the study. For the retrospective cohort, families were contacted via phone and then traveled to the study site for participation. Survey data was collected through individual interviews. For the retrospective cohort, this interview occurred approximately 2 to 4 weeks after their final surgery. For the prospective cohort, interviews took place at the first postoperative visit after the last surgery. All interviews were performed by trained study personnel who were residents or nurses. The data were collected on paper surveys and then entered into REDCap [19] at a later time point due to internet unreliability. Informed consent was obtained from all study participants, and all participants were compensated for time and transport. Any family with incomplete income or total cost data was excluded from the study.

Patients were asked about the costs associated with their most recent surgical event. All cost data were collected in local currency (Ugandan Shillings). World Bank data was then used to convert Ugandan Shillings (Ugx) to purchasing power parity (PPP) and estimate the costs in international dollars ($I). The international dollar is a currency unit that represents the purchasing power of the US dollar at a point in time. The midpoint of this study (2022) was used to estimate this conversion (1,279 Ugx = 1 I$ PPP) [20].

Variables

All patients were administered an economic impact questionnaire. Demographic data collected included age at diagnosis (days), sex, parental education level, distance traveled for care, home district, and religion. The type of surgical repair was assessed for all patients, as well as whether the patient experienced any surgical or ostomy complications. All families provided their monthly household income, which was then used to calculate annual income. Families also provided the estimated total cost of ARM care and whether they felt paying for healthcare impacted their ability to provide for their household.

Direct medical and non-medical costs were assessed. In Uganda, the government system provides free healthcare in public health facilities for all its citizens. As a result, there are no doctor, nursing, or hospital fees that may be incurred by families in the process of receiving care. However, families often must purchase medications, medical supplies, and costs for diagnostic tests (imaging or laboratory) when they are not available at the government hospital. Non-direct medical costs assessed included travel costs and the cost of food/lodging during hospital stays. Participants listed transportation methods and the number of hospital visits before surgery was performed. Indirect cost was estimated through the cost of lost wages from work. Participants were asked if employed family members were staying with the patient during their hospital course. If so, families were asked about their method of employment. If the individual endorsed employment, the number of missed days of work was solicited, as well as the daily wage. For those with informal employment, lost wages were estimated from available data: monthly income was used to estimate daily wage, which was then multiplied by the average days of work missed by those with formal employment.

Other assessed economic factors included the need for families to sell possessions or borrow money to raise funds. The item sold and its cost was solicited from all participants. The impact of healthcare on sibling schooling was also assessed, along with the number of days missed school for each sibling who could not attend school.

Statistical methods

STATA 17 was used for all analyses. After summing all direct and indirect costs, the total OOP cost was estimated. This summed OOP cost was then divided by annual income to estimate CHE. The total family estimated cost and total funds raised were also divided by annual income to provide a comparative estimate of CHE for these alternate estimated costs. Patient characteristics were compared according to whether they experienced CHE according to the traditional definition of CHE (i.e., total summed OOP cost divided by annual income). Continuous variables were assessed using Mann-Whitney U tests, and categorical variables were assessed using chi-square tests. The potential explanatory variables to be considered were identified through a pre-determined directed-acyclic graph for catastrophic health expenditure. Best subset selection with logistic regression was used to identify the most parsimonious, best-fitting model for the dependent variable CHE (total OOP/annual income) [21]. Odds ratios for CHE were estimated using logistic regression to examine associations with the included covariates. Akaike information criterion (AIC) was used for model selection. Multicollinearity between variables was then assessed along with assessments for interaction effects. Best subset selection with a negative binomial regression was used to identify the best model for total OOP costs. The negative binomial regression models estimated the incidence rate ratio (IRR), which represents the expected ratio of international dollars (total direct and indirect costs) for the included covariates. As a secondary analysis, the variables selected for the negative binomial regression model were applied to a median regression model. Hypothesis tests were two-sided, and statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

A total of 241 patients underwent operative intervention for ARM; five patients who did not report costs were excluded. The median age at diagnosis was 6 days, and 51% (n = 120) of patients were male (Table 1). The patients came from districts around the country (Fig. 1), traveling a median distance of 175 km. Rural patients accounted for 71% of the cohort (n = 168). Most parents had received primary education (57%), 9.3% had no education, 21% had secondary education, and 12% had tertiary education. The majority were Christian (90%, n = 212). In examining the surgical approach used, 25% (n = 60) had 1-stage repair, 9.7% (n = 23) had 2-stage repairs, and 65% had 3-stage repairs. 24% (n = 57) experienced either a surgical or stoma complication during the surgical course.

Out of pocket costs & funds raised

The median annual income was I$ 1,876 (Q1-Q3 938–3,753). The median total OOP (indirect + direct costs) was I$ 327 (176–527). This was greater than the total cost estimated by families at I$ 208 (156–323, p < 0.001). A minority of families incurred direct medical costs: 39% with medication costs, 9.7% with diagnostic test costs, and 35% with medical supplies costs. Almost all families, conversely, incurred non-medical direct costs: 99% had travel costs, and 100% had food/lodging costs. 47% of families experienced indirect costs due to lost wages (Table 2).

Overall, 86% (n = 204) of families reported that ARM healthcare costs impacted their ability to pay for household needs. Travel costs were the largest direct cost that families incurred. Families reported utilizing up to 3 different methods of travel to reach the hospital for care. The most common method was a shared minibus (taxi, n = 165, 70%), followed by a boda-boda (motorcycle taxi, n = 159, 67%), bus (n = 60, 25%), private vehicle (n = 7, 3.0%), ambulance (n = 6, 2.5%), and charity vehicle (n = 1, 0.4%). For indirect cost, a total of 116 employed people from 111 families reported that they stayed with the patient during the hospital stay. Of these families, 41% (n = 45) reported formal employment, and 59% (n = 66) reported informal employment (i.e. subsistence farming). The median days of work missed was 7 (Q1-Q3 5–10). Twenty-four patients (10%) reported that healthcare costs impacted the ability of the family to afford school fees for the patient’s siblings. The median days of school missed by these siblings was 365 (238–730).

A majority (n = 197, 83%) reported either borrowing money or selling possessions to pay for healthcare. The median cost of possessions sold was I$ 391 (Q1-Q3 157–1173), and the median amount of funds borrowed was I$ 156 (70–321). Most families (80%, n = 188) sold livestock to pay for care, including 26% (n = 62) who sold goats/sheep, 17% (n = 41) who sold cows, 7.6% (n = 18) who sold chickens, and 2.1% (n = 5) who sold rabbits/pigs. Over 1 in 6 patients sold land (17%, n = 40). Other items sold included food crops (5.9%, n = 14), cash crops (5.1%, n = 12), motorcycles/bicycles (2.1%, n = 5), television sets (2.1%, n = 5), and bricks (1.2%, n = 3). Three families reported selling businesses, which included a coffee plantation, a eucalyptus plantation, and a hair salon.

Catastrophic healthcare costs

Utilizing a traditional definition of CHE (total OOP/annual income), 64% (n = 151) of families experienced CHE (Table 3). When the estimated total cost was used, this was 56% (n = 133), and when the funds raised were compared against annual income, this was 59% (n = 138). Demographic characteristics between those who did and did not experience CHE (total OOP/annual income) were examined (Table 1). This demonstrated that those who experienced CHE were more likely to travel from far distances (250 km vs. 97 km, p < 0.001) and to be of Islamic faith (11% vs. 4.7%, p = 0.02).

On multivariable regression analysis examining factors associated with CHE (total OOP/annual income), the model with the lowest AIC included distance traveled and rural families (Table 4). No multicollinearity issues (VIF 1.00) or significant interaction effects (p = 0.71) were found between rural families and distance traveled. This demonstrated that for every additional 10 km that a patient traveled for care, there was 6% higher odds of incurring CHE. When total cost (direct + indirect) was examined, the model with the lowest AIC included the variables distance traveled, education, and surgical repair type (Table 4). This model suggested that there was a 2% higher rate of costs for every additional 10 km that a patient traveled. Patients who underwent 2-stage repair had more than twice the rate of costs compared to 1-stage patients, and 3-stage patients had 1.7 times the rate of costs compared to 1-stage patients. Lower education level was associated with higher costs, but the confidence interval was too wide to rule out the possibility of no effect. Following multivariable median regression analysis, this translated to a median increased cost of $I 7.08 (95% CI: 4.45–9.72) for every 10 km traveled. For the type of surgical procedure performed, patients experienced a median increase of $I 176.72 (95% CI: 25.13–328.31) for those with 2-stage when compared with 1-stage and a median increase of $I 113.15 (95%CI: 16.96–209.35) for those with a 3-stage repair compared to 1-stage.

Discussion

Anorectal malformations (ARMs), a common congenital anomaly in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), refer to a group of birth defects where a child is born without an anal opening or with the anus located in an abnormal anatomical position. The incidence is 1 in 1,500 to 4,000 live births [22–26]. ARMs represent about 18% of the neonatal surgical cases in Uganda [27]. Due to their complex nature and the frequent requirement for multiple, staged operations, we have observed that families of ARM patients are particularly susceptible to financial hardship. In this study, we present the first formal analysis of healthcare expenditures for ARM patients in Southwestern Uganda. Our findings reveal that 64% of patients experienced catastrophic health expenditure (CHE), with nearly all families (99%) incurring travel-related costs. A previous study at our institution found that 45% of all Pediatric surgical patients experienced CHE [28]. We suspect that the rate of CHE is higher among ARM patients because many require staged repairs, involving multiple surgeries and hospitalizations.

Uganda has only two public hospitals that provide pediatric surgery, so many families have to travel long distances to reach appropriate care [8–10]. Oftentimes, patients sought care from multiple centers before reaching a hospital that could provide definitive ARM care [13, 29–35]. Previous studies in Uganda similarly demonstrated that children seeking surgical care incur significant travel costs, resorting to distress financing strategies such as borrowing or selling assets to cover travel costs [36, 37]. A systematic review identifying factors influencing OOP healthcare expenditures in LMICs found transportation to be a significant contributor to direct medical costs [38]. Despite this, strategies to address high travel costs for families are seldom addressed in efforts to achieve universal health coverage in LMICs.

The vast majority of families reported that ARM healthcare costs impacted their ability to pay for household needs. They either borrowed money or sold possessions to pay for healthcare, and none had health insurance, and all paid out of pocket [6, 39]. It is hypothesized that having to sell possessions to pay for care reduces care-seeking behavior, especially for poor and less educated patients [3, 8, 36]. Selling personal goods to pay for surgery can make it even more difficult for poor families to maintain their household’s subsistence needs, further impoverishing these vulnerable patients [6, 9, 11–13, 40, 41].

In LMICs, ARMs impose distinct socio-economic challenges for families where there are neither colostomy appliances nor colostomy care specialists available. Rather, families improvise by wrapping old bed sheets around the child to as colostomy bags, leading to isolation and restriction of children’s activities and child abandonment [41]. Often, children are not permitted to attend school and public functions [42–46]. Previous research found that 43% of male caregivers leave their families, and that two-thirds of children with a colostomy are not allowed to attend school, further worsening economic consequences [6, 41–45, 47]. Any intervention that can shorten the long waiting period of children with colostomy helps patients from feeling physically and socially restrained [46, 48–50]. Additionally, it enhances children’s interpersonal relationships with peers, relatives, and family members, helps them begin school on time or return promptly, and reduces school dropout rates [51–54].

The complex malformations require a staged repair – patients who had a two-stage repair incurred more than twice the costs compared to those who had a single-stage repair. Initiatives to reduce or eliminate time spent with a colostomy have the compound advantage of both reducing healthcare costs and reducing the medical and social morbidity of the colostomy [55–57]. This is worsened when hospital stays are lengthened due to surgeries being rescheduled or canceled due to emergency cases and limited operating room capacity [58–63]. A previous study found that over one-third of families incur CHE while caring for children with ARMs. The staged procedure, which results in a greater number of hospital trips and rescheduled or canceled surgeries, places patients at increased risk for CHE. Performing a single-stage repair when feasible offers the advantages of lower cost and lower social stigma burden to the family [50, 55, 56].

Limitations

The main limitation of this study was selection bias in the recruitment of the retrospective cohort, as all families required cell phones to be contacted for enrollment. This may have led to wealthier cellphone-owning patients participating in the study. As this data was captured retrospectively and relied completely on participant-supplied estimates, it is also subject to recall bias.

Conclusion

Identifying methods to provide financial protection from CHE is essential for all children. ARM patients are at particularly high risk for CHE and high OPP expenditures, especially those living far from healthcare services and in rural areas.
