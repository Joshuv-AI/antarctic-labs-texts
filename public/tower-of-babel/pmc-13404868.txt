Introduction

Background

Artificial intelligence (AI) has become increasingly embedded in health management practices through digital health applications, wearable devices, and remote monitoring systems [1, 2]. For older adults, these technologies take various forms. Smartphone-based health management applications, for instance, allow users to log symptoms and medication intake, receive tailored reminders and lifestyle prompts, and view trend summaries of chronic-condition indicators such as blood pressure, glucose, sleep patterns, and physical activity. Wearable devices and home-based sensors provide continuous monitoring of heart rate, activity, sleep, and fall detection, with automated alerts and data summaries that can be shared with family caregivers or healthcare professionals as part of remote follow-up and care coordination [3].

By enabling continuous data collection and algorithm-driven recommendations, these technologies promise enhanced efficiency, personalization, and support for long-term health management, features that are particularly salient in the context of population ageing and the rising burden of chronic disease [4].

Older adults are frequently positioned as key beneficiaries of AI-assisted health management due to their higher healthcare needs and the emphasis on preventive and continuous care [5]. However, alongside these anticipated benefits, AI technologies introduce complex ethical challenges, particularly concerning privacy, autonomy, and decision-making authority [6, 7]. These challenges are amplified among older adults, who may face age-related barriers in digital literacy, cognitive processing, and access to comprehensible information, rendering them both primary users and ethically vulnerable stakeholders [8].

Ethical concerns in AI-assisted health management for older adults

Privacy has emerged as a central ethical concern in AI-enabled healthcare. AI-assisted health management systems typically rely on large volumes of personal health data that are collected continuously and processed through opaque algorithmic mechanisms [9]. Although regulatory frameworks emphasize transparency and informed consent, older adults may struggle to meaningfully understand what data are collected, how they are used, and how algorithmic outputs inform health recommendations [10]. As a result, consent may remain procedural rather than substantively informed, raising concerns about the ethical legitimacy of data practices in this population [11].

In parallel, the growing epistemic authority attributed to algorithmic recommendations poses challenges to individual autonomy. AI systems are often perceived as objective, accurate, or scientifically superior, a phenomenon commonly described in the literature as automation bias or overreliance on automated systems [12]. For older adults, who may already experience reduced confidence in health-related decision-making, such algorithmic reliance risks subtly shifting agency away from the individual toward the technology itself [13]. This form of influence is non-coercive yet ethically significant, as it may erode autonomy through deference rather than explicit constraint.

Beyond individual autonomy: a relational perspective

Conventional bioethical frameworks often conceptualize autonomy as an individual capacity for self-determination. However, health decision-making in later life is rarely exercised in isolation [14]. Older adults’ engagement with AI-assisted health management is frequently facilitated by family members and healthcare professionals, who assist with technology use, interpret algorithmic outputs, and influence care decisions [15].

Relational autonomy offers a more appropriate ethical lens for understanding these dynamics. From this perspective, autonomy is shaped by interpersonal relationships, power asymmetries, and institutional contexts. In AI-assisted health management, technologies may interact with and potentially reconfigure existing relational dynamics, sometimes enabling shared decision-making and at other times reinforcing paternalistic practices [16]. Ethical evaluation of AI use among older adults therefore requires attention not only to individual consent and understanding, but also to relational and contextual conditions that structure decision-making.

Limitations and underexplored areas in existing research

Existing scholarship on AI in healthcare has developed along three interrelated but largely disconnected strands, each of which offers valuable insights while leaving critical questions unanswered.

The first strand consists of normative ethical analyses that examine issues such as informed consent, data governance, algorithmic fairness, and accountability [17–19]. While these studies have provided essential conceptual clarity regarding the ethical principles at stake, they are predominantly theoretical in nature and often remain detached from the lived experiences of those most directly affected, particularly older adults who navigate AI technologies in their everyday lives. What is missing from this literature is an empirically grounded understanding of how ethical principles are interpreted, negotiated, and sometimes compromised in real-world contexts.

The second strand encompasses empirical research on AI implementation in healthcare, focusing on clinical performance, safety, usability, and workflow integration [20–22]. These studies have generated important knowledge about how AI systems function in controlled or professional settings. However, their focus has been primarily physician-centered or hospital-based, leaving largely unexplored how older adults themselves experience AI-assisted health management in the everyday contexts of their homes and communities. In particular, older adults’ subjective perceptions of autonomy and privacy, which are central to their dignity and well-being, have received scant attention in this body of work.

The third strand comprises research on digital health and aging, which has extensively explored technology adoption, digital literacy, and user acceptance among older populations [23–25]. These studies have illuminated the factors that facilitate or hinder initial technology uptake. Yet their predominant focus on behavioral outcomes and acceptance models has meant that ethical constructs, such as autonomy understood as socially and relationally constituted, remain undertheorized and underinvestigated. The question of what happens after adoption, in terms of how technologies reshape older adults’ sense of agency and their relationships with others, has been largely overlooked.

Despite the growing body of scholarship across these three strands, significant gaps remain. First, there is a scarcity of empirical research that foregrounds older adults’ lived ethical experiences, specifically how they actually perceive and navigate issues such as privacy, algorithmic authority, and decision-making control in their daily use of AI health technologies [26, 27]. Second, relational factors, including family involvement and interactions with healthcare professionals, have been examined only tangentially, and have rarely been systematically integrated into empirical analyses of autonomy [28]. As a result, the relational conditions under which older adults engage with AI-assisted health management, conditions that may either support or undermine their sense of agency, remain poorly understood.

These gaps point to the need for an approach that integrates the strengths of all three strands while transcending their individual limitations. What is required is empirically grounded ethics research that centers the perspectives and experiences of older adults themselves, examines the interplay of privacy, algorithmic reliance, and relational dynamics in shaping autonomy, and combines the breadth of quantitative patterns with the depth of qualitative insights. The present study responds to this need by adopting a mixed‑methods empirical ethics design to investigate how older adults experience and negotiate autonomy in AI‑assisted health management, with particular attention to the roles of privacy awareness, algorithmic reliance, and family involvement. In doing so, it operationalizes autonomy as an ethically constituted outcome shaped by privacy awareness and algorithmic reliance rather than as a static individual attribute, empirically demonstrates how relational factors (specifically family involvement and healthcare professional dominance) function as contextual moderators of autonomy, and bridges normative ethical theory with empirical evidence, thereby contributing to the growing field of empirical bioethics in AI governance.

Conceptual foundations

The conceptual foundation of this study draws on three interrelated theoretical traditions.

First, it engages with principlism in biomedical ethics, particularly the principle of respect for autonomy as articulated by Beauchamp and Childress [29, 30]. This tradition emphasizes informed decision-making, voluntary choice, and the importance of respecting individuals’ capacity to make decisions about their own care. These principles remain foundational to ethical healthcare practice even as technological mediation introduces new complexities.

Second, the study is informed by relational autonomy theory, as developed by feminist bioethicists including Nedelsky [31], and Mackenzie [32, 33] and Sherwin [34]. This perspective critiques the individualistic conception of autonomy prevalent in traditional bioethics, arguing instead that autonomy is socially embedded and shaped by interpersonal relationships, care dynamics, and power structures. For older adults who often rely on family members and healthcare professionals for support, this relational understanding of autonomy is particularly salient.

Third, scholarship on algorithmic governance and automation bias provides a theoretical lens for understanding how technological systems may subtly shift epistemic authority and influence user decision-making in healthcare contexts [35–38]. This literature illuminates the psychological and sociotechnical mechanisms through which AI recommendations come to be perceived as authoritative, potentially leading users to defer judgment even when such deference conflicts with their own embodied knowledge or preferences.

Integrating these theoretical resources, this study conceptualizes autonomy in AI-assisted health management as an achievement that is: (1) mediated by technological systems that shape how information is presented and decisions are framed; (2) negotiated within relationships with family members and healthcare professionals who may support or undermine older adults’ agency; and (3) experienced subjectively in ways that may diverge from normative ideals of autonomous decision-making.

Research questions

Guided by this conceptual framework, the study addresses the following research questions:

RQ1:

How do older adults perceive and understand privacy practices in AI-assisted health management?

This question explores older adults’ awareness of data collection and sharing practices, their concerns about privacy, and the ways they navigate potential privacy risks in their daily use of AI health tools. It addresses the gap regarding the lack of empirical research on older adults’ subjective privacy experiences.

RQ2:

How is algorithmic reliance associated with older adults’ perceived autonomy in health-related decision-making?

This question examines the extent to which older adults trust and follow AI-generated health recommendations, and whether this reliance is associated with diminished or transformed experiences of autonomy. It responds to the need for empirical investigation of automation bias in real-world contexts.

RQ3:

How do relational factors, including family involvement and healthcare professional dominance, shape older adults’ experiences of autonomy in AI-assisted health management?

This question investigates the role of significant others, both family members and healthcare professionals, in moderating older adults’ engagement with AI health technologies. It addresses the gap regarding the neglect of relational dynamics in existing empirical research.

Together, these research questions guide an empirical inquiry that seeks to generate theoretically informed, empirically grounded insights into the ethical dimensions of AI-assisted health management for older populations.

Methods

Study design

This study adopted a mixed-methods empirical ethics design, combining a cross-sectional questionnaire survey with semi-structured qualitative interviews. This design was chosen for two reasons. First, the quantitative component enables the identification of patterns and associations among key constructs, namely privacy awareness, algorithmic reliance, relational factors, and perceived autonomy, across a larger sample. Second, the qualitative component allows for an in-depth exploration of how older adults themselves experience and make sense of these dynamics in their everyday lives, capturing meanings and contexts that quantitative methods alone cannot access.

The study was guided by the conceptual framework (see Fig. 1), which integrates principlism and relational autonomy theory to conceptualize autonomy as shaped by privacy awareness, algorithmic reliance, and relational dynamics. In the quantitative phase, the framework guided the operationalization of each construct into measurable variables. Privacy awareness was operationalized as participants’ concern about and awareness of data collection and sharing practices; algorithmic reliance was measured as the degree of trust in and deference to AI-generated health recommendations; relational factors were assessed in terms of the frequency and nature of family involvement and perceived influence of healthcare professionals; and perceived autonomy was measured as participants’ sense of control over health-related decisions.

In the qualitative phase, the framework informed the development of the semi-structured interview guide. Interview questions were designed to elicit narratives that could illuminate each of the framework’s core constructs, such as asking participants to describe situations where they felt their privacy might be compromised, instances where they chose to follow or disregard AI recommendations, and how family members or doctors were involved in these moments. This ensured that qualitative data collection remained theoretically grounded while remaining open to unexpected themes.

The framework also guided the analytical strategy. Quantitative analyses examined the hypothesized relationships among constructs, while qualitative analysis used the framework’s constructs as sensitizing concepts, which are initial lenses for coding that remained open to revision based on emergent themes. This integration of framework-driven and data-driven approaches is consistent with the study’s empirical ethics orientation, which seeks to bridge theoretical rigor with empirical richness.

Participants and setting

This study was conducted in Shanghai, China, between March and September 2024. Shanghai was selected due to its relatively high adoption rate of digital health technologies among older adults and the research team’s established collaborations with local community facilities.

Participants were community-dwelling older adults aged 60 years and above who had experience using, or had been exposed to, AI-assisted health management tools. These tools included wearable health devices, mobile health applications, and remote health monitoring systems used for daily health tracking or chronic disease management. Exposure to AI-assisted health management was defined as either direct use by the participant or indirect involvement, such as shared use facilitated by family members or healthcare professionals.

A two-phase sampling design was employed. Participants were recruited from five community-based facilities across three urban and suburban districts of Shanghai: three community health centers and two senior activity centers. In the quantitative phase, a convenience sampling strategy was used to recruit 312 participants due to the exploratory nature of the study and the practical challenges of accessing a dispersed older adult population [39]. Of the 312 participants, 208 (66.7%) were recruited from community health centers and 104 (33.3%) from senior activity centers. Subsequently, in the qualitative phase, a purposive subsample of 24 participants was selected from the survey respondents to ensure diversity in ethical experiences. This two-phase approach, moving from a broad convenience sample to a purposive subsample, is a recognized strategy in mixed-methods research for integrating statistical patterns with in-depth lived experiences [40].

Eligibility criteria included the ability to communicate effectively, understand study information, and provide informed consent independently. Older adults with severe cognitive impairment, significant communication difficulties, or conditions that precluded informed consent were excluded.

All study materials, including the participant information sheet, consent form, and questionnaire, were provided in simplified Chinese. Interviews were conducted in either Mandarin or the local Shanghai dialect, depending on each participant’s preference, to ensure comfort and ease of expression. All interviews were later transcribed into standard Mandarin Chinese for analysis.

Participant characteristics

A total of 312 older adults completed the questionnaire. From these, a purposive subsample of 24 participants was selected for semi‑structured interviews. The selection aimed to maximize diversity in age, gender, digital health experience, and family involvement. Specifically, we sought to include different age groups (60–69, 70–79, 80+), balanced gender representation, varying levels of digital technology experience (high, medium, low), and diverse patterns of family involvement in health management (active, occasional, minimal). This purposive approach was intended to capture a wide range of perspectives relevant to the research questions. Quantitative data collection

Questionnaire development

A structured questionnaire was developed for this study based on the conceptual framework and a review of existing literature on AI ethics, privacy, and autonomy. The questionnaire consisted of four main sections. The first section captured sociodemographic characteristics (age, gender, education level, and prior experience with digital technologies).

The second section measured privacy awareness in AI‑assisted health management (RQ1). Using twelve items (e.g., “I am aware of what types of health data are collected by the AI device”), participants rated their understanding of data collection purposes, data access and sharing practices, and the comprehensibility of privacy‑related information. In the current sample, this scale showed good internal consistency.

The third section assessed perceived autonomy and algorithmic reliance (RQ2). It comprised four items for perceived autonomy (e.g., “I feel I have the final say in health decisions even when the AI gives advice”) and four items for algorithmic reliance (e.g., “I tend to follow the AI’s recommendations because they are more objective”). Both scales demonstrated acceptable reliability.

The fourth section examined relational and contextual factors (RQ3) with six items covering family involvement, dominance of healthcare professionals in decision‑making, perceptions of relational autonomy, and experiences of being respected versus substituted. This scale also showed acceptable internal consistency.

Pilot testing and reliability

The questionnaire was pilot‑tested with 10 older adults meeting the inclusion criteria to assess item clarity, comprehensibility, and completion time. Minor wording adjustments were made based on feedback, but no substantial structural changes were introduced. Pilot data were not included in the final dataset. Internal consistency (Cronbach’s α) for each scale was computed from the main study sample (N = 312) and is reported above.

Qualitative data collection

To complement the survey findings and deepen ethical interpretation, semi-structured interviews were conducted with a purposive subsample of 24 survey participants. The interview guide was designed around the three research questions, focusing on participants’ understanding of data collection and use in AI‑assisted health management, their experiences of following or questioning AI‑generated health recommendations, their perceptions of autonomy in health‑related decisions, and the roles of family members and healthcare professionals in mediating these decisions.

Interviews were conducted in a quiet setting at the participating community facilities, each lasting between 35 and 65 minutes. All interviews were audio-recorded with participants’ explicit consent and transcribed verbatim for analysis. As noted above, interviews were conducted in either Mandarin or the local Shanghai dialect based on participant preference, and all transcripts were subsequently converted to standard Mandarin Chinese for coding and analysis.

Ethical considerations

This study received ethical approval from the Ethics Committee of Shanghai University of Medicine and Health Sciences and was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki [42]. In accordance with institutional regulations, all research involving human participants, including questionnaire-based studies, is required to undergo ethical review prior to data collection.

Informed consent was obtained through a multi-step process designed to ensure that all participants, particularly older adults, fully understood the study and their rights.

Prior to data collection, potential participants were provided with a detailed information sheet written in plain, large-font Chinese. The information sheet explained the study’s purpose, procedures, data usage (including that all data would be anonymized and used solely for academic research), confidentiality measures, the voluntary nature of participation, and the right to withdraw at any time without any consequences.

A trained researcher then reviewed the information sheet verbally with each potential participant in a one-on-one setting, allowing ample time for questions and clarification. The researcher specifically confirmed that the participant understood: (1) what their participation would involve; (2) that their data would be anonymized; and (3) that they could withdraw at any time.

Only after this verbal explanation and confirmation of understanding were participants asked to sign a written informed consent form. A copy of the signed form was offered to each participant for their records. For the qualitative interviews, additional verbal consent was obtained at the start of each interview session, including specific consent for audio recording.

All data were anonymized at the point of collection and securely stored. No personally identifiable information was retained, and access to the data was restricted to the research team only.

Data analysis

Quantitative analysis

Quantitative data were analyzed using SPSS version 26.0. Descriptive statistics (frequencies, percentages, means, and SD) were calculated for all variables. Where appropriate, independent samples t-tests or chi-square tests were used to compare subgroups (e.g., by gender, age group, or recruitment site). Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

To examine the relationships among privacy awareness, algorithmic reliance, relational factors, and perceived autonomy, hierarchical multiple regression analyses were conducted. Perceived autonomy was entered as the dependent variable, with sociodemographic variables entered as controls in Step 1, privacy awareness and algorithmic reliance in Step 2, relational factors in Step 3, and interaction terms (privacy awareness × relational factors, algorithmic reliance × relational factors) in Step 4 to test moderating effects. All continuous predictors were mean-centered prior to creating interaction terms to reduce multicollinearity [43].

To further test the hypothesized moderated mediation model, specifically whether privacy awareness influences perceived autonomy through algorithmic reliance (mediation) and whether this indirect effect is moderated by relational factors at the second stage, we used Hayes’ PROCESS macro for SPSS (version 4.2) with Model 14 [44]. The model estimated the indirect effect of privacy awareness on perceived autonomy via algorithmic reliance at low ( - 1SD) and high ( + 1SD) levels of relational factors. Bias-corrected bootstrap confidence intervals (5,000 resamples) were used to assess the significance of conditional indirect effects. All continuous variables were mean-centered before analysis.

Qualitative analysis

Qualitative data were analyzed using thematic analysis following the six-phase framework proposed by Braun and Clarke [45]: (1) familiarization with the data through repeated reading of transcripts; (2) generating initial codes; (3) searching for themes; (4) reviewing themes; (5) defining and naming themes; (6) producing the report.

Coding was performed independently by two researchers (first and second authors) using NVivo 12 software. The researchers met regularly to compare codes, discuss discrepancies, and reach consensus. Disagreements were resolved through discussion, with input from a third senior researcher (corresponding author) when necessary. Themes were refined through an iterative process of discussion among the research team.

Integration of quantitative and qualitative findings

Integration occurred at the interpretation stage through a weaving approach [46], where qualitative themes were used to contextualize and deepen understanding of quantitative patterns. For example, survey findings on the negative association between algorithmic reliance and perceived autonomy were explored through interview narratives that illuminated the mechanisms underlying this relationship, such as participants’ experiences of self-doubt when AI contradicted their embodied knowledge. A joint display table was constructed to systematically compare quantitative and qualitative findings across each research question, identifying areas of convergence, complementarity, and divergence.

RQ1: Privacy awareness in AI-assisted health management

Quantitative findings

Overall, participants reported moderate levels of privacy awareness related to AI-assisted health management (M=3.42, SD=0.85). Awareness of basic data collection practices was relatively higher (M = 3.82), whereas understanding of data sharing and secondary data use was comparatively lower (M = 3.12).

A substantial proportion of participants agreed that they were uncertain about who could access their health data (47.1%), and 42.6% indicated difficulty understanding privacy-related information provided by AI health platforms.

Correlation analysis indicated that higher privacy awareness was positively associated with perceived autonomy (r=0.28, p<0.001).

Qualitative findings

The interview data revealed that older adults’ understanding of privacy practices was often fragmented and characterized by a sense of resigned compliance. While participants were generally aware that their health data were being tracked, many viewed the privacy consent process as a perfunctory ritual rather than an informed choice. For instance, P05 (female, 67) described clicking ‘Agree’ as a “requirement” for using the monitor, suggesting that for many users, privacy concerns are often secondary to the functional necessity of the technology.

Furthermore, this lack of technical clarity often manifested as an underlying anxiety regarding data boundaries. Participants expressed significant uncertainty about the specific entities that could access their health records, with P01 (female, 65) noting that she did not “really know where it goes or who sees it.” This sense of pervasive monitoring created an existential discomfort for some individuals, as poignantly described by P12 (male, 71), who felt that the technology made it seem as if someone were “watching me through my wrist.” Collectively, these narratives suggest that privacy awareness among older adults is deeply influenced by a perceived lack of control over digital data flows.

Qualitative accounts illustrated a complex tension between reliance on AI and the maintenance of personal agency, echoing the variations identified in the quantitative analysis. This transfer of authority was evident in the experience of P03 (female, 74), who noted that she usually followed the app’s advice because the machine “must know better” than her, even when the recommendations felt unnecessary. For these individuals, the “objective” screen often began to overshadow their own bodily intuition, a process that P07 (female, 68) described as her “own judgment becoming less important” when faced with algorithmic disagreement.

Conversely, the findings also revealed a counter-narrative of critical distance, where participants actively negotiated the algorithm’s authority to preserve their autonomy. Rather than absolute reliance, these users treated AI as a supplementary reference while prioritizing their embodied experiences. For example, P09 (male, 69) demonstrated this by trusting his energetic feelings over a negative sleep report from the device, stating that he chooses to “trust my body, not the screen.” These contrasting narratives suggest that algorithmic reliance is a dynamic negotiation rather than a static trait, where autonomy is maintained through a constant dialogue between digital data and subjective health perceptions.

Qualitative findings

The qualitative findings highlighted the inherently relational nature of autonomy, illustrating how social dynamics can either facilitate or undermine an individual’s agency within the digital health ecosystem. The interviews revealed that while supportive family environments promoted shared decision-making, dominant or overly paternalistic interactions often led to a perceived erosion of the older adult’s role.

One prominent theme was the role of family members as digital mediators. For some, this involvement was perceived as empowering and collaborative. For instance, P19 (female, 66) described a supportive dynamic where her daughter explains the results so that they can “decide together” on subsequent health actions. Conversely, other participants experienced family involvement as a form of relational infantilization. P14 (male, 78) poignantly noted that having his children remotely monitor his activity and tell him to “walk more” made him feel like a “child being managed by my phone.” This suggests that when AI monitoring is used as a tool for familial surveillance, it can transform home-based care into a mechanism of managed dependency.

Furthermore, the influence of healthcare professionals emerged as a critical factor in shaping perceived autonomy. Participants reported that the introduction of AI tools into clinical consultations sometimes created a digital barrier between the patient and the provider. This phenomenon was strikingly illustrated by P22 (male, 73), who experienced a sense of being sidelined during a consultation. He observed that the physician “didn’t even look” at the personal health data collected by his watch, focusing exclusively on institutional tablet data instead. Such interactions led to a sense of being “invisible” or excluded from the room. These narratives indicate that when algorithmic data becomes the primary focus of the clinical encounter, the older adult’s subjective experience is often marginalized, thereby diminishing their sense of control over their own medical journey.

Integration of quantitative and qualitative results

Across all three research questions, quantitative trends were enriched and contextualized by qualitative findings. While survey data identified general patterns of privacy awareness, algorithmic reliance, and autonomy, interview narratives revealed how these factors were experienced in everyday decision-making contexts. Together, the findings illustrate that older adults’ autonomy in AI-assisted health management is shaped not only by individual understanding and trust in technology, but also by relational and institutional dynamics. Discussion

Ethical implications

This study advances empirical ethics scholarship by conceptualizing autonomy in AI-assisted health management as a relationally mediated ethical outcome, shaped by privacy awareness, algorithmic reliance, and social context. While prior research has often approached AI ethics from normative or system-level perspectives (e.g., Topol, 2019; Vayena et al., 2018) [47, 48], our findings provide empirically grounded insights into how ethical principles, particularly autonomy, are negotiated in everyday practice. By integrating quantitative patterns with qualitative accounts, this study demonstrates that autonomy in AI-assisted health contexts is not a static individual attribute but a relationally mediated outcome shaped by privacy awareness, algorithmic reliance, and social dynamics.

Theoretical synthesis

The empirical findings synthesize into an integrated conceptual model that elucidates the ethical mechanisms through which AI-assisted health management shapes older adults’ perceived autonomy. The model identifies privacy awareness and algorithmic reliance as two primary pathways linking AI technologies to autonomy-related outcomes. Higher levels of privacy awareness support informed engagement with AI systems, whereas increased reliance on algorithmic recommendations is associated with a reduction in perceived autonomy when decision-making authority is implicitly delegated to technological outputs.

Importantly, the model highlights the contextual role of relational factors, specifically family involvement and healthcare professional dominance, in shaping these relationships. Rather than operating independently, these relational dynamics moderate the impact of algorithmic reliance on autonomy, either attenuating or amplifying autonomy-related effects depending on whether decision-making is shared or hierarchical. In this sense, autonomy in AI-assisted health management emerges as a relational and context-sensitive ethical construct rather than a purely individual attribute.

By integrating individual-level perceptions with relational and institutional influences, the conceptual model advances existing ethical frameworks by demonstrating how AI technologies interact with social contexts to reshape autonomy in later life. This synthesis provides a theoretically grounded basis for evaluating ethical AI deployment among older adults and underscores the need for governance approaches that move beyond individual consent toward relationally informed and context-aware ethical oversight.

Privacy awareness as a precondition for ethical AI use

Findings related to RQ1 suggest that older adults’ privacy awareness plays a foundational role in shaping their ethical experiences with AI-assisted health management. While many participants acknowledged the convenience and perceived usefulness of AI technologies, limited understanding of data collection practices, data sharing, and algorithmic processing was common.

Previous research on older adults and digital health has primarily emphasized barriers to technology adoption and digital literacy [49]. Our findings extend this literature by demonstrating that limited privacy awareness is not merely a usability issue but has ethical implications for the validity of informed consent in AI-enabled health contexts. When privacy information is overly technical or opaque, older adults may formally agree to data practices without substantive comprehension.

From an ethical perspective, autonomy presupposes not only the availability of choices but also the capacity to understand the conditions under which those choices are made. In this sense, privacy awareness functions as an ethical precondition for autonomy rather than a peripheral informational concern. This finding contributes to ongoing debates in digital ethics by suggesting that transparency alone is insufficient if it does not translate into accessible, actionable understanding for older populations.

Algorithmic reliance and the reconfiguration of autonomy

RQ2 highlights a tension between trust in AI systems and the preservation of personal autonomy. Participants frequently described AI-generated recommendations as objective, accurate, or authoritative, which encouraged reliance on algorithmic outputs. This aligns with research on algorithm appreciation (Logg et al., 2019) [50], which suggests that individuals often defer to algorithmic judgments under conditions of perceived objectivity. At the same time, prior work has also documented resistance to medical AI (Longoni et al., 2019) [51], indicating ambivalent public attitudes.

Our findings nuance this literature by showing that among older adults, algorithmic reliance is neither simple acceptance nor resistance. Rather, it operates as an ethical mechanism through which decision-making authority may gradually shift. Reliance on AI-generated recommendations can enhance adherence to health advice, yet excessive deference risks transforming AI from a supportive tool into a de facto decision-maker.

This reframing moves beyond viewing reliance as a behavioral indicator of trust and instead conceptualizes it as a process that may subtly reconfigure autonomy. However, one might argue that clinicians also possess epistemic authority, and this has long been accepted as part of the therapeutic relationship. So why should AI’s authority be seen as potentially erosive?

We suggest that the distinction lies not in the mere presence of epistemic authority, but in the nature and transparency of that authority. Unlike clinicians, who can explain their reasoning, engage in dialogue, and be questioned or challenged, AI systems often operate as “black boxes” with opaque decision-making processes [52]. This lack of transparency, combined with the perceived objectivity and data-driven nature of AI, may foster a form of unquestioning deference that is qualitatively different from the negotiated trust in clinician-patient relationships. As one participant noted: “The machine doesn’t have emotions, so it must be objective.” This belief may lead individuals to suspend their own critical judgment more readily than they would with a human expert.

The erosion observed here is therefore not coercive but epistemic—grounded in the perceived superiority of technological judgment that operates in ways less accessible to scrutiny and negotiation. This distinction is critical: the concern is not that AI has authority, but that its authority operates outside the deliberative processes that characterize meaningful clinician-patient relationships, thereby threatening the very conditions under which autonomous decisions are made. In this way, the study contributes to discussions on algorithmic authority by foregrounding its distinctive ethical implications for older adults’ self-determination in healthcare contexts.

Relational autonomy in AI-Supported health decisions

RQ3 reveals that autonomy in AI-assisted health management is deeply relational rather than purely individual. Family members and healthcare professionals play critical roles in mediating older adults’ interactions with AI systems, sometimes enhancing understanding and shared decision-making, and at other times overshadowing older adults’ voices, particularly when AI recommendations are invoked to justify decisions.

The concept of relational autonomy has been extensively theorized in bioethics, emphasizing that self-determination is embedded within social relationships and power structures. However, empirical applications of relational autonomy within AI-assisted healthcare remain limited. By demonstrating how algorithmic recommendations intersect with family authority and professional expertise, this study extends relational autonomy theory into digitally mediated health contexts. Importantly, AI technologies may unintentionally amplify existing relational hierarchies by providing seemingly neutral, data-driven justifications for decisions made by others. As one participant described: “My daughter showed me the app and said, ‘See, it says your blood pressure is too high, so you can’t go out today.’ What could I say? The machine said so.” This illustrates how algorithmic outputs can be invoked to override older adults’ preferences, lending technological authority to paternalistic family dynamics. Ethical evaluation of AI-assisted health management must therefore move beyond individual consent models to address the distribution of epistemic authority and decision-making power within relational networks.

Policy implications

The ethical insights generated by this study carry important implications for policy development, healthcare practice, and the design of AI-assisted health management systems targeting older populations.

Enhancing privacy communication and consent practices

Given the central role of privacy awareness, policies should promote age-sensitive communication strategies. Building on research highlighting digital literacy gaps among older adults, AI health platforms should adopt layered, simplified, and context-specific explanations of data practices. Interactive consent mechanisms and periodic renewal processes may further strengthen meaningful engagement.

Designing AI to support, not replace, autonomy

Consistent with concerns about algorithmic authority in healthcare, AI systems should be designed to augment rather than substitute human judgment. Features that present alternative recommendations, communicate uncertainty, and prompt reflection can reinforce user agency and reduce uncritical reliance.

Addressing relational dynamics in policy and practice

Healthcare policies should recognize that older adults’ autonomy is exercised within relational networks. Training programs for healthcare professionals should address the ethical use of AI recommendations, emphasizing shared decision-making and respect for older adults’ agency. Similarly, family caregivers should be supported through education that promotes collaborative, rather than paternalistic, involvement in AI-mediated health decisions.

Toward context-sensitive AI governance for older adults

Finally, AI governance frameworks should explicitly consider older adults as a distinct user group with specific ethical vulnerabilities and strengths. Policies that integrate relational autonomy, digital literacy support, and safeguards against technological overreach can help ensure that AI-assisted health management contributes to ethical, equitable, and person-centered care.

Contribution to knowledge

This study makes three primary contributions to the field.

First, while prior research on older adults and digital health has focused predominantly on technology adoption and usability [23–25], this study provides empirically grounded evidence linking privacy awareness to the ethical quality of autonomy. By demonstrating that limited privacy awareness compromises not just usability but the very validity of informed consent, it extends normative ethical discussions into empirical territory.

Second, moving beyond binary conceptualizations of trust versus resistance that characterize much of the AI ethics literature [48, 49], this study reframes algorithmic reliance as an ethical and relational process that may gradually reconfigure decision-making authority. The finding that reliance operates through mechanisms of epistemic deference, rather than simple acceptance, offers a more nuanced account of how AI systems shape user agencies.

Third, while relational autonomy has been extensively theorized in bioethics [30, 31, 35, 36], its empirical application within AI-assisted healthcare has remained limited. This study advances relational autonomy theory by demonstrating, through qualitative narratives, how algorithmic outputs interact with family and professional authority in shaping older adults’ lived experiences of autonomy. It shows that autonomy in AI-mediated contexts is not merely situated within relationships but actively reconfigured by them.

Strengths and limitations

This study adopts an empirical ethics approach that integrates quantitative and qualitative data, allowing for a nuanced examination of ethical mechanisms in real-world AI use among older adults. The mixed-methods design, combining a relatively large survey sample (N = 312) with in-depth qualitative interviews (n = 24), enables both breadth and depth in understanding older adults’ ethical experiences with AI-assisted health management.

However, several limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, regarding sampling, this study employed a convenience sampling approach, recruiting participants from five community-based facilities in a single metropolitan city (Shanghai, China). While this approach was practical for an exploratory study, it limits the generalizability of our findings. The sample may not be representative of older adults in rural areas, other regions of China, or different cultural and healthcare contexts. Furthermore, approximately two-thirds of participants were recruited from community health centers rather than senior activity centers, which may have introduced selection bias, as older adults attending health centers may differ in health status, technology exposure, or ethical concerns from those recruited from social activity settings. Additionally, the study was conducted within a specific cultural context. The relational patterns observed may reflect Confucian familial norms in the Chinese context, where family involvement in health decision-making is socially expected and morally valued. Therefore, the moderating role of family support identified in this study may not generalize to societies with more individualistic orientations toward autonomy. Future cross-cultural research is needed to examine whether similar relational dynamics emerge in other socio-cultural settings.

Second, the cross-sectional design captures participants’ experiences and perceptions at a single point in time, which precludes causal inference and limits our ability to understand how ethical perceptions evolve as older adults gain more experience with AI technologies or as their health conditions change. Longitudinal studies would be valuable for examining the dynamic nature of privacy awareness, algorithmic reliance, and perceived autonomy over time.

Third, our questionnaire measured self-reported awareness of privacy issues and AI-related concerns, rather than objective measures of understanding or behavior. Self-reports may be subject to social desirability bias or may not accurately reflect participants’ actual knowledge or decision-making processes. The qualitative interviews partially address this limitation by allowing for deeper exploration of participants’ actual understanding through dialogue, with illustrative examples reported in the Results section (e.g., Table 4 and participant quotes). However, future research could further employ observational methods or experimental designs to assess actual awareness and behavior in real-world contexts.

Despite these limitations, the study’s mixed-methods empirical ethics design strengthens its contribution by linking normative ethical concepts with lived experiences, offering transferable insights for AI governance in ageing societies. The findings provide a foundation for future research in diverse geographical and cultural settings and highlight specific ethical mechanisms, namely privacy awareness, algorithmic reliance, and relational dynamics, that warrant further investigation.

Conclusion

This study set out to investigate how older adults experience autonomy in the context of AI-assisted health management, asking three interrelated questions: How do they perceive privacy? How does algorithmic reliance shape their sense of agency? And how do family members and healthcare professionals moderate these experiences? The findings provide clear answers: privacy awareness is often procedural rather than substantive; algorithmic reliance can subtly erode autonomy through epistemic deference; and relational dynamics actively moderate whether AI technologies support or undermine older adults’ decision-making authority.

These findings advance understanding in three ways. First, they demonstrate that autonomy in AI-assisted health contexts is not a static individual attribute but an ethically mediated outcome shaped by the interplay of privacy awareness, algorithmic reliance, and relational dynamics. Second, they extend relational autonomy theory into the digital domain, showing how technological artifacts become active participants in the relational networks that support or constrain older adults’ agency. Third, they provide empirical grounding for normative debates in AI ethics, revealing how abstract principles such as informed consent and respect for autonomy are actually negotiated in the everyday lives of older users.

The implications extend beyond academia. For technology designers, our findings underscore the need to move beyond one-size-fits-all privacy notices toward interfaces that support genuine understanding and meaningful choice. For clinicians and family caregivers, they highlight the importance of using AI recommendations as tools for dialogue rather than as authoritative directives that override older adults’ voices. For policymakers, they call for governance frameworks that recognize the relational nature of autonomy and address the distinctive vulnerabilities of ageing populations in an increasingly algorithm-mediated world.

Future research should build on these findings by examining how the dynamics identified here unfold over time, across different cultural contexts, and in relation to emerging AI applications. Longitudinal studies could reveal whether algorithmic reliance deepens or stabilizes with sustained use. Cross-cultural comparisons could illuminate how varying family structures and healthcare systems shape the relational moderation of autonomy. Investigations of newer AI technologies, for example conversational agents or predictive analytics, could extend the conceptual framework developed here to novel contexts.

At its core, this study is concerned with a fundamental question: as AI systems become increasingly embedded in the health management of older adults, what happens to their ability to remain the authors of their own lives? Our findings suggest that the answer depends not only on how these technologies are designed, but on how they intersect with older adults’ understanding, their trust in algorithmic authority, and the relational contexts in which they are used. Privacy awareness, algorithmic reliance, and relational dynamics are not separate ethical concerns; they are intertwined mechanisms through which autonomy is either supported or eroded. Ensuring that AI-assisted health management enhances rather than diminishes older adults’ agency will require not only technically robust systems, but ethically informed design, relationally aware practice, and governance frameworks that recognize the distinctive vulnerabilities and strengths of ageing populations. Ultimately, ensuring that AI serves to support, rather than supplant, human agency is not merely a technical challenge; it is a moral imperative.
