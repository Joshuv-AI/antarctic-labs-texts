Residential indoor air monitoring to assess long-term exposure to air pollution in susceptible populations for epidemiology: overview and early findings
Authors: Sun-Young Kim, Kyumin Kim, Seokmin Ji, Miyoun Shin, Hyunji Kim, Young Shin Park, Soyoung Han, Soyoun Bae, Younghyun Kim, Eehyun Park, Jiyoung Hwang, Seung-Hyun Cho, Hee-Young Yoon, Jin Woo Song
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
While there has been accumulating evidence of the association between long-term exposure to air pollution and various health endpoints, most epidemiological studies relied on ambient air pollution which showed substantial deviation from indoor. This difference could lead to biased or imprecise health effect estimates, particularly for susceptible populations who spend most time indoors. Although a few studies focused on indoor air pollution, most monitoring was limited to one-time and/or relatively short-term periods. We aimed to provide an overview of an indoor monitoring campaign that was designed to assess long-term exposure to residential indoor PM2.5 for epidemiology and applied to 130 older adults with idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis in the Seoul Metropolitan Area, South Korea, for 2021–2023. Specifically, our design focused on (1) representation of long-term exposure, (2) identification of pollution sources, (3) assessment of infiltration, and (4) implementation of non-contact monitoring. Using RTI MicroPEMs, we measured concentrations of indoor PM2.5 and chemical components for five days including weekdays and weekends in four seasons as well as household information. We also collected matched outdoor samples to assess infiltration, and applied non-contact monitoring using postal mails for repeat visits. Our preliminary analyses of indoor hourly PM2.5 measurements (mean: 14.90 µg/m3 [standard deviation: 36.12]) showed temporal patterns with higher concentrations in winter (20.17 [50.54]) than in summer (11.19 [32.76]) and during the daytime than nighttime. Indoor concentrations were lower than outdoor (20.44 [SD: 61.63]) with consistent seasonal patterns but contrasted hourly patterns. Thirty-six chemical components of PM2.5 showed mostly similar temporal patterns to those of PM2.5 with lower concentrations and smaller differences between indoors and outdoors in summer that in other seasons. The infiltration factor characterized by median indoor to outdoor ratios of sulfur, nickel, and iron were 51–70%. Average PM2.5 concentrations tended to be high at households with cooking activities (15.14 vs. 10.96 µg/m3 at those without), humidifiers (19.19 vs. 13.16), pets (24.09 vs. 13.35), and smokers (16.76 vs. 11.79). Our indoor monitoring approach provides a practical guidance to future indoor monitoring studies that intend to assess the health effect of PM2.5.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12940-026-01334-3.

Introduction

While there has been accumulating evidence of the association between long-term exposure to air pollution including fine particulate matter (PM2.5) and human health, one of the key limitations was indicated as the exposure assessment relying on ambient air pollution [38, 40]. Many epidemiological studies of long-term air pollution used ambient measurements mostly obtained from fixed monitoring sites without considering the level of exposure within domestic environments. Air pollution monitoring to assess indoor air quality across participants’ homes has been sparse or limited to small sample sizes because of high compliance requirement of participants and relatively large burden of technicians [29, 30]. However, there was a good understanding of significant deviation of indoor air pollution from outdoor, since indoor air quality is affected by indoor microenvironments which are different from outdoor sources [3, 26, 33, 39]. Furthermore, as people spend 80–90% of their time indoors [19], the exposure assessment solely based on ambient measurements could result in substantial exposure measurement error. These differences may subsequently affect health effect analyses.

The impact of the difference in indoor exposure from outdoor on health analyses could be large for susceptible populations who stay longer indoors mostly at homes than the general population. Individuals with chronic lung diseases spent more time at homes than those without diseases in the same age groups and older individuals spent an ample majority of time indoors mostly at their homes [19, 22]. Large health effects of long-term ambient air pollution found in such populations could be even larger, when replaced with indoor air pollution in their domestic dwellings. However, most epidemiological studies of indoor air pollution employed short-term measurements and investigated temporary changes in lung function or disease status within a few days, weeks, or months [29]. Indoor air pollution assessed over extended periods can elucidate the role of air pollution in disease progression or health deterioration of susceptible populations.

The design of indoor monitoring is crucial to assess and characterize individual long-term exposure to indoor air pollution at people’s residences, when the ultimate goal is the assessment of the health effect. Previous indoor monitoring of air pollution included several limitations in their designs to be overcome to achieve the goal. Indoor monitoring campaigns often collected samples for limited hours, days, and seasons, possibly because of financial and/or logistical constraints, which may not represent the collective exposures over a long period. Although household information questionnaires are commonly applied to identify potential pollution sources such as cooking, cleaning, and housing conditions, a few characteristics successfully explained the pattern of indoor air pollution across households [33]. For PM2.5, its chemical components related to specific sources can be a powerful resource to help the identification [1]. However, handy and affordable portable samplers frequently deployed to indoor monitoring mostly provide nephelometer measures of PM2.5 which do not allow the quantification of chemical components, as opposed to gravimetric measures. Another difficulty to assess indoor PM2.5 particularly in highly populated urban areas is the accurate quantification of infiltrated PM2.5. Since indoor PM2.5 is primarily originated from outdoor PM2.5, the amount of indoor PM2.5 infiltrated from outdoors needs to be distinguished from those emitted indoors [39]. In densely populated urban areas where people tend to live in domestic buildings, many studies located their samplers in gardens of buildings or indoor terraces of households where the outdoor measurements could be less comparable to corresponding indoor measurements [26, 49]. Lastly, indoor monitoring designs should adopt an approach to reduce visits to participants’ homes which is one of the major barriers to participate in residential monitoring particularly when there are severe public health concerns such as COVID-19.

Leveraging a well-established cohort of participants aged mostly over 65 years and with idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) in the Seoul Metropolitan Area, South Korea, we aimed to provide the overview of a monitoring campaign designed to assess the long-term exposure to indoor PM2.5 in a susceptible population for epidemiological application. Specifically, the design focused on the data collection to allow (1) representation of long-term exposure, (2) identification of potential pollution sources, (3) assessment of infiltration, and (4) implementation of non-contact sampling. These four focuses will help future studies accurately quantify individual exposures combined by indoor-originated and infiltrated PM2.5 over an extended period for subsequent health analyses. In this paper, we introduced the key features of our monitoring designs with respect to the four focuses and provided early results for PM2.5 measurements and household characteristics. Although this study was based on PM2.5 and participants with IPF, our indoor monitoring design can be applicable to future indoor monitoring studies that intend health analyses of various air pollutants and populations.

Overview of indoor monitoring campaign

Study population

Our study population included 130 people who are a part of an idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis cohort, lived in the Seoul Metropolitan Area (SMA), visited one of the 12 tertiary hospitals in the SMA between 2021 and 2023 (Fig. 1; Table 1). The SMA is the fourth largest metropolitan area worldwide and consists of Seoul, Incheon, and Gyeonggi Province where 26 million population resides in 12,685 km2 in 2020. Ambient PM2.5 concentrations in the SMA shows a strong seasonal variation with higher annual average concentrations (21 µg/m3 in 2020) compared to the 12 µg/m3 of the air quality standard proposed by the World Health Organization. IPF is a progressive lung disorder by scarring of the lungs from unknown causes, mostly prevalent in older males, with poor prognosis and an increasing pattern of incidence [21]. Given few identified risk factors other than smoking, recent studies of patients with IPF suggested air pollution as a potential risk factor of disease progression and mortality [4, 18, 31, 35, 44, 47] (Table S1). Air pollution could exacerbate disease progression through various biological mechanisms including oxidative stress, telomere, and inflammation [36]. In the IPF cohort, participants visited hospitals every three or six months for monitoring their disease status and carried out medical examinations including pulmonary tests. These regular visits provide the unique opportunity to investigate the association of short-term as well as long-term exposure to air pollution with disease prognosis when we monitor the level of air pollution. The details of the study participants and their health data collection are provided elsewhere [48]. The study participants were 87% of males and the average age was 69.6 years with 93% of people greater than 60 years (Table 1). This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of each site, including Asan Medical Center, Seoul, Republic of Korea (2021-0072).

IPF indoor monitoring campaign

We designed the IPF indoor monitoring campaign to measure PM2.5 concentrations within 130 homes of 130 participants for consecutive five days in four seasons from July, 2021 through August, 2023 with a subset also collecting outdoor PM2.5 measurements. The field team visited participants whose hospital visits were scheduled within the maximum four weeks and installed RTI MicroPEMs (RTI International, Research Triangle Park, NC, U.S.A.) in rooms where participants spend most of their time such as a living room or bedroom (Table S2, Figure S1). MicroPEMs operated continuously for five days at 0.4 L per minute and cycled 30 s on and 30 s off. The MicroPEM is a lightweight (~ 230 g) and quiet (3 dB above office background at 1 m) portable real-time PM sensor, which was widely applied in previous studies of indoor PM2.5 monitoring [9, 37]. Apart from most portable PM samples that provide optical measurements of PM2.5, MicroPEMs concurrently collect filter samples using PTFE filters that provide gravimetric measurements, in addition to real-time optical measurements, and allow the quantification of PM2.5 chemical components. The gravimetric concentration was used to adjust the real-time PM mass concentration for improved accuracy [6]. We multiplied each real-time measurement by a correction factor, which is the ratio of the gravimetric-based PM2.5 concentration to the average value of all real-time concentrations. The adjusted real-time concentrations were averaged to a 1-minute scale for further analyses. The filter sample was subsequently analyzed for black carbon (BC), environmental tobacco smoke (ETS), and brown carbon (BrC) by 6-wavelength integrating sphere and 33 inorganic elements by X-ray fluorescence (XRF) at RTI international laboratories [20, 46]. The details of quantification and quality control procedure of PM2.5, carbon species, and inorganic elements are provided in supplemental materials (See “S1. Details of quantification and quality control procedure of PM2.5 and of PM2.5 chemical components”). For the measurements below the minimum detection limit (MDL), we replaced by the MDL divided by 2. MicroPEMs were also suitable for our indoor monitoring campaign given a relatively long operation without battery replacement up to five or six days to avoid additional requests to participants. The simple operation procedure of MicroPEMs allowed participants self-deploy devices for repeat sampling.

Key features of indoor monitoring design

Representation of long-term exposure

To assess long-term exposure and annual-average PM2.5 concentration, we designed the IPF indoor monitoring campaign to collect continuous samples on different days and seasons. As shown in Table S3, previous studies that applied extensive indoor monitoring campaigns for epidemiological application were often limited to selective days and/or seasons. We carried out a five-day sampling for each of four seasons, including weekdays as well as weekends from Friday through Wednesday (Figure S2). The four seasons of spring, summer, fall, and winter were defined as March to May, June to August, September to November, and December to February (average temperature for 2021–2023: 12.8, 24.2, 14.9, and 0.3 °C, respectively). Before the visit to participants’ homes on Friday for installation of MicroPEMs, our preparation began from Monday of the week. We made phone calls to each participant in order to notify our visit and confirm the visit time, prepared MicroPEMs including installation of filters and correction of flow rate, and packed all sampling items in the lab. One technician visited up to 5–6 participants within a day, given long driving distances between participant’s homes with the maximum two driving hours (Table S2, Figure S4). We visited up to 11 participants a day depending on the recruitment of participants, the location of participants’ homes, and the availability of technicians. Four repeated weekly-based sampling across four seasons for each of the 130 participants required more than two years of monitoring.

Implementation of non-contact sampling

Given that study participants are less inclined to accept repetitive visits, we received the sensors via postal mails. In particular, since our IPF monitoring campaign began in July 2021 in the middle of the COVID-19 pandemic, we developed a design that minimizes physical interactions. For successful implementation particularly for older adults, we provided the participants with detailed written instruction and follow-up phone calls (Figure S3). During the two days before the ending of the sampling on Wednesday, we made reservation for the shipping service and made phone calls to help participants pack devices for the return shipping (Figure S2). We also provided packaging materials such as mailing boxes, packing tapes, and bubble wraps. Starting from the third visit, we switched completely to non-contact monitoring by mailing to participants’ homes for installation in addition to participants’ mails to return PM sensors.

Identification of pollution sources

PM2.5 chemical components and household information questionnaire were used to identify potential sources of indoor air pollution. We constructed household information questionnaire composed of more than 100 questions based on previous studies of indoor monitoring [8, 11, 12, 28, 43]. The questions consisted of demographic features, housing conditions, behavioral characteristics, and indoor and outdoor environments (Table S3). For housing conditions, each participant provided the constructed year of a dwelling building, the size, floor level, and dividers of a residential dwelling, materials for walls and floors, and condensation or mold. Behavioral characteristics included heating, cleaning, smoking, and pet. Questions for Indoor and outdoor environments were related to ventilation, air cleaner, humidifier, and proximity to roads. We particularly intended to obtain the specific information on the characteristics that derived the PM changes in households of middle and high income countries: solid fuel burning, cooking, candle and incense, cleaning, and humidifier, in addition to smoking [42]. For example, the questions included the frequency, duration, and application type of cooking. Moreover, PM2.5 chemical components, considered as surrogates of specific pollution sources, can help confirm major sources such as outdoor traffic, cooking, and cleaning. Although there were limited attempts that included PM2.5 chemical components to indoor monitoring, a study in older adults with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) showed indoor BC, considered as a marker of traffic, was associated with larger decline in lung function than indoor PM2.5, suggesting the need of further investigation of indoor chemical components [14].

Assessment of infiltration

We selected 20% of participants living at the 5th floor or lower to obtain matched outdoor measurements in order to assess infiltrated PM2.5 from outdoors. The matched outdoor and indoor measurements for the same participant can provide the indoor-outdoor (I/O) ratio, as the infiltration rate, for a specific PM2.5 chemical component that is considered to be primarily of ambient origins with few indoor sources. For matched outdoor sampling, we placed MicroPEMs outside participants’ homes with sampling devices hung on balcony bars or window bars, as opposed to gardens or within closed balconies often used in previous studies. This design allowed us to measure outdoor PM2.5 to be comparable with indoor PM2.5 at the same floor level as participants live. For the PM2.5 chemical components to assess the infiltrated PM2.5, we considered sulfur, iron, and nickel which were adopted in previous studies of infiltration using I/O ratios as shown in Table S4 [2, 5, 17, 23, 24, 25, 34, 41, 45, 50]. We will identify the pollutants that satisfy the criteria to assess the infiltration [10, 24]: few known indoor sources, sufficient quantities in both indoor and outdoor environments, and I/O ratio equal to or less than one as well as smaller than PM2.5 I/O ratio.

Data exploration

For the two years of monitoring period from July, 2021 through August, 2023 including nine 3-month periods that represent different seasons, we collected 579 PM2.5 filter samples (62,130 hourly measurements) from 130 participants. These samples were composed of 448 indoor samples from all 130 participants, 105 outdoor samples from 35 (27%) participants, and 26 duplicated samples from 25 (19%) participants (Table S5). In indoor monitoring for all 130 people, 95 participants (73%) completed all four visits. We excluded invalid samples resulting from instrument errors such as early stop and pump failure, and human errors such as filter damage, sample switch, lost real-time data file, and incorrect metadata (e.g. filter identification number).

PM2.5

Table 2; Fig. 2 show the summary and temporal patterns of hourly-average PM2.5 concentrations in 130 participants. The median and mean of indoor hourly PM2.5 concentrations were 8.23 (interquartile range (IQR) = 10.44) and 14.90 (standard deviation (SD) = 36.12) µg/m3, respectively, with the highest in the winter (median and mean: 9.19 and 20.17 µg/m3, respectively) and lowest in the summer (7.04 and 11.19 µg/m3) (Table 2, Figure S5). This seasonal contrast indicates the impact of the ambient air quality affected by constant heating in cold winter and frequent rain in humid summer. To investigate the representative long-term average concentrations depending on the season, we compared 1- to 3-season averages to 4-season averages in 95 people who provided all four seasonal samples. The investigation showed relatively small root mean square error for most 2-season averages (6.41–6.87 µg/m3 for spring-summer, spring-fall, summer-winter, and fall-winter) comparable to those for 3-season averages (4.43–6.99 µg/m3). This comparability suggests the inclusion of at least two seasons with one high- and one low-concentration days to represent long-term average concentrations (Figure S7). Daily average concentrations were similar between weekdays and weekends, suggesting stable time-activity patterns of older adults who were mostly retired. Hourly patterns showed higher concentrations during the daytime than the nighttime. In particular, morning and evening hours (7–10 a.m. and 6–8 p.m.) including cooking and cleaning activities gave high concentrations. Across four visits, indoor PM2.5 concentrations were more variable between households than within a household (Figure S6). Outdoor PM2.5 concentrations were higher and more variable (median [IQR] and mean [SD] = 12.41 [15.16] and 20.44 [61.63] µg/m3) compared to indoor PM2.5 (Table 2). Although daily and seasonal patterns were similar between outdoor and indoor PM2.5, the hourly pattern was reverse with higher outdoor concentrations in the nighttime (Fig. 2). These patterns were consistent when we restricted our analysis to 35 people who provided both indoor and outdoor measurements (Figure S8).

PM2.5 chemical components

Carbonaceous components of 460 samples showed relatively higher mean concentrations (0.57 [SD: 0.33], 1.27 [0.84], and 2.01 [4.88] µg/m3 for BC, BrC, and ETS, respectively) than 33 inorganic elements where sulfur gave the highest concentration (0.89 [0.72] µg/m3) (Table S6, Figure S7). Across four seasons, all PM2.5 components generally showed similar patterns with lower concentrations in the summer than the other seasons (Figure S9). Carbon and inorganic species comprised 50% of PM2.5 mass on average with some seasonal differences (48, 34, 60, and 57% on average for the spring, summer, fall, and winter, respectively). When we compared matching indoor and outdoor measurements of 35 participants, most PM2.5 chemical components showed higher concentrations in outdoors than indoors with larger differences in the winter than the other seasons (Table S6). The median I/O ratios of sulfur, nickel, and iron which are commonly used for assessing infiltration factor were ranged between 0.51 and 0.70, indicating 51–70% of infiltrated PM2.5 from outdoor air depending on the elemental tracer (Table S7).

Household information characteristics

About 85% of 130 participants spent more than 15 h at home and almost all participants performed regular ventilation by opening windows (98%) (Table 2). Three fourth of the participants used air cleaners (74%), while one third had humidifiers (33%). Most participants cook their meals at homes (93%) with cooking fuel of natural gas (92%). The most common heating fuel was also natural gas (69%). One fifth of participants had pets (17%). About 5% of participants smoked or had smokers in family members. Average PM2.5 concentrations were higher at 130 households with cooking activities (15.14 vs. 10.96 µg/m3), humidifiers (19.19 vs. 13.16), pets (24.09 vs. 13.35), and smokers (16.76 vs. 11.79) than those without (Table 1). Although PM2.5 concentrations were similar between the households with and without air cleaners (15.29 and 14.85, respectively), those having multiple air cleaners tended to show lower concentrations (12.56) compared to those having no or one cleaner (15.17 and 16.41, respectively). Households having ventilation behaviors using both open windows and machines showed lower PM2.5 (13.39) compared to those using open window only (15.53). These patterns were generally similar to those in 95 households that provided complete measurements across four seasons.

Discussion

This paper illustrated our intention to design a monitoring campaign with the specific interest in assessing the long-term residential exposure to indoor PM2.5 and identifying factors that influence indoor air quality for investigating the health effect. This targeted interest shaped our indoor monitoring campaign to extend temporal coverage to represent long-term exposure and to identify sources of indoor PM2.5 composed of indoor and ambient origins. Specifically, our monitoring design included different hours, days, and seasons that will allow us to compute annual-average indoor PM2.5 concentrations representative to each individual. Including PM2.5 chemical components, home questionnaires, and matching outdoor-to-indoor PM2.5 monitoring will help distinguish between indoor-generated and infiltrated PM2.5, and characterize total indoor PM2.5.

Our study is aligned with the emerging research interest in the health effect of long-term exposure to indoor air pollution. Given the extended time spent indoors and the reported difference in indoor air pollution from outdoor, there is a growing interest in studying the health effect of indoor air pollution. However, most of these studies examined relatively short-term changes over a day or a week in indoor air pollution. A study in Massachusetts, U.S. reported that increased weekly average concentrations of indoor PM2.5 and BC were associated with decreased lung function on the following week in 125 COPD patients with stronger associations than those of outdoor concentrations [14]. Another U.S. study for 44 children also showed the association of indoor PM2.5 for the previous 48 h with lung function decline but no association of outdoor [16]. Building upon the evidence of the association of long-term exposure to air pollution with disease development and progression relying on ambient air pollution, recent studies attempted to look at the same association by replacing with indoor air pollution. However, their monitoring was often based on limited temporal coverage such as a single day across two seasons or each month [11, 28]. Recently, the Subpopulations and Intermediate Outcome Measures in COPD and AIR (SPIROMICS AIR) study applied an extended indoor monitoring period over two weeks but limited to two or three seasons, instead of all four, for each of 216 adults from 12 clinical centers in the continental U.S. Hansel et al., [12]. Furthermore, based on the indoor monitoring data along with their household information questionnaire and infiltration rate obtained from another study, the SPIROMCIS AIR developed a prediction model (cross-validation R2 of 0.45) to estimate indoor PM2.5 concentrations for more than two thousands participants who did not have indoor PM2.5 measurements and investigated the association with lung function [13, 51]. More studies need to help elucidate the role of exposure to indoor air pollution for extended periods in disease progression and diverse populations and environments.

Our well-designed monitoring can contribute to our future work where we can address longstanding questions related to indoor air pollution assessment. First, we plan to investigate cost-effective monitoring designs that contain reduced hours, days, or seasons and provide the long-term exposure estimates comparable to those of the complete design including the extended temporal coverage as our study. Our suggestion of cost-effective monitoring designs can minimize efforts and resources with the maximized data quality. Second, we will identify the most responsible indoor sources. In the present study, we found little differences in indoor PM2.5 concentrations between households according to their air cleaner usage and cooking activities as opposed to previous findings in high-income countries [42]. Since these little differences could be due to the impact of other individual and/or household characteristics, we will apply regression approaches to investigate the association with potential indoor sources after accounting for such characteristics. PM2.5 chemical components will also help improve our understanding of potential sources, when they are applied to source apportionment approaches [7, 15]. Third, we will quantify infiltrated PM2.5 relying on the ratios of a specific PM2.5 component which best satisfies the criteria after the investigation of candidate components used in previous studies. Fourth, given infiltration and indoor sources as well as our previously-validated prediction models which allow us to estimate outdoor concentrations at people’s homes, we will develop a prediction model to estimate individual indoor concentrations of PM2.5 for people without indoor measurements.

In addition to the four focuses intended in our monitoring designs, this study has some additional design suggestions to future studies of indoor air pollution monitoring. Although our study was dedicated to PM2.5 and patients with IPF, our monitoring design targeting long-term exposure assessment for health analyses can be easily extended to other susceptible populations such as asthmatic children and cancer patients as well as the healthy population. In addition, we did not apply the emerging monitoring approach using low cost sensors with online data transfer service [27, 32]. Although these devices are powerful tools to obtain data without individual visits leading to the substantial reduction in logistical efforts, internet service was frequently not accessible or available in elderly households. Future studies should consider unique features of monitoring environments related to population characteristics in their choices for indoor monitoring.

In summary, recent increasing interests in the health effect of individual long-term exposure to indoor air pollution require active data collection in order to overcome the lack of monitoring data. However, the logistical and financial difficulties in indoor monitoring seek effective solutions in designs that help achieve data accuracy, understand the nature of exposure, and minimize the burdens of participants and technicians. Our practical guidance can provide such solutions and allow further epidemiological investigation.
