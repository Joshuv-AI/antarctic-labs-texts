Non-consensual sexting among adolescents from ethnocultural minority backgrounds: the role of native peer pressure, self-control, and sexual preoccupation
Authors: Dimitrios Chasapis, Thanos Touloupis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe non-consensual dissemination of sexually explicit electronic messages (non-consensual sexting) among adolescents from ethnocultural minority backgrounds is a severe form of digital aggression that remains internationally under-researched.MethodIntegrating Ecological Systems and Social Cognitive theories, the present study examined the prevalence of non-consensual sexting roles (sending, receiving, and forwarding) and the predictive contribution of perceived native peer pressure (i.e., peers from the Greek majority without a migrant background), self-control, and sexual preoccupation. The sample consisted of 293 students (59% self-identified as boys; Mage = 14.9, SD = 0.93) from ethnocultural minority groups, primarily originating from Albania (35.8%), Bulgaria (35.2%), and a consolidated group (29%) of other countries (Russia, Ukraine, Israel, Armenia). Students attended the third grade of randomly selected public junior high schools located in the two largest regions of Greece.ResultsFindings revealed that adolescents were predominantly involved in the sending and receiving of non-consensual sexual content, whereas unauthorized forwarding occurred at a significantly lower frequency. Adolescents of Albanian and Bulgarian origin were identified as the most vulnerable subgroups for direct digital involvement. Gender did not emerge as a statistically significant factor in differentiating involvement. Regarding predictive pathways, perceived native peer pressure emerged as the most potent positive predictor, followed by self-control as a secondary protective factor and sexual preoccupation as the weakest positive factor.ConclusionsOverall, the findings suggest that digital risk in youth from minority backgrounds is driven by the interplay between host-country social integration and internal self-control, necessitating culturally sensitive interventions that target social-integrative motives while bolstering individual protective mechanisms. While these findings provide a specialized perspective on the digital ecology of minority youth, they should be viewed as exploratory regarding the unique influence of ethnocultural background, as the study did not include a native-born comparison group.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s40359-026-05196-9.

Introduction

Adolescence in the contemporary digital era unfolds within a “digital culture” where the exchange of sexually suggestive electronic messages, known as sexting, has emerged as a normative aspect of youth sexual exploration [1–3]. Broadly defined, sexting encompasses the creation, sharing, or receiving of sexually explicit or suggestive text messages, photographs, or videos via mobile phones, social media, or other digital platforms [4, 5]. While consensual sexting involves the voluntary exchange of material to foster intimacy and romance [6], non-consensual sexting represents a severe form of digital sexual aggression [7]. It is defined as the sending, receiving, or forwarding of explicit content without the consent of the individuals involved, often blurring the boundaries between private trust and “digital ownership” [6, 8]. The psychosocial consequences of such involvement are profound, systematically linking to depression, anxiety, self-destructive tendencies, and broader behavioral maladjustment, including dating violence [9–11].

Adolescence serves as the primary developmental “window” for these behaviors. Driven by developmental impulsivity and low self-control, youth often fail to evaluate the long-term repercussions of their actions [12, 13]. This vulnerability is further exacerbated by “digital disinhibition”, where screen-mediated distance weakens moral barriers and diminishes empathy, allowing risky practices to be perceived as expected facets of identity formation [2, 14].

Non-consensual sexting among adolescents from ethnocultural minority backgrounds: what do we know?

While an adequate body of research has explored adolescent involvement in non-consensual sexting [1, 5, 15, 16], studies focusing on youth from ethnocultural minority backgrounds remain disproportionately scarce and internationally under-researched. This research gap is particularly concerning given that these adolescents encounter unique digital risks due to the intersection of conflicting systems: the traditional values of their culture of origin and the integration demands of the host country. In the context of the present study, the term “ethnocultural minority backgrounds” specifically encompasses first- and second-generation migrant youth. Socioculturally, within the Greek landscape, these groups predominantly originate from neighboring Balkan states and Eastern Europe. These adolescents navigate a complex acculturation process, and frequently experience systemic stereotyping, social distance, and varying degrees of marginalization from the native majority [17, 18].

According to Ecological Systems Theory [19], social marginalization and a perceived lack of social connectivity may drive minority adolescents to utilize sexting as an alternative channel to fight against psychological alienation or to cope with the isolation resulting from perceived discrimination [11, 20]. Furthermore, for these groups, non-consensual sexting may function as an extension of offline racism, where sexual exposure is weaponized by others to reinforce stereotypes and induce further social exclusion [11].

In the specific case of Greece, where the present study is conducted, the student population is characterized by a significant representation of ethnocultural minorities, a demographic reality shared by several other Mediterranean countries [17]. Despite this substantial presence, no relevant study has been identified to date that specifically addresses non-consensual sexting among adolescents from ethnocultural minority backgrounds in the Greek educational context.

Furthermore, existing findings in other contexts remain sparse and often yield contradictory results. Notably, Fleschler Peskin et al. [21] observed that ethnic minority urban high school students might engage in sexting as a strategic means to reinforce intra-group bonds and strengthen social relationships within their own community. Similarly, some studies indicate that adolescents from migrant backgrounds may encounter distinct digital risks, necessitating a focused examination of their unique digital experiences [22, 23]. However, a critical limitation of the current literature is the treatment of sexting as a unitary phenomenon, without distinguishing between its consensual and non-consensual dimensions. Furthermore, there is a lack of research distinguishing between specific ethnocultural subgroups. In the Greek context, limited previous evidence suggests that specific minority groups, particularly those of Albanian and Bulgarian origin, often face higher levels of social marginalization and negative perceptions from the native population, which has been linked to increased vulnerability in other risky online behaviors [17, 22].

Consequently, there is currently no clear empirical picture, especially in Greece, regarding the extent of non-consensual sexting among adolescents from (specific) ethnocultural minority groups. This research gap hinders the development of culturally sensitive and inclusive psychoeducational prevention strategies aimed at supporting and protecting this student population within an increasingly diverse digital society.

Gender differences in non-consensual sexting

While the role of gender in general sexting behaviors has been widely investigated, research specifically targeting gender-based differences in non-consensual sexting remains remarkably scarce. Available empirical evidence suggests that adolescent boys tend to be involved in non-consensual sexting, particularly as senders, at significantly higher rates than girls [5, 16, 24, 25]. This trend is often interpreted through the lens of social dominance and moral disengagement, as boys may perceive the non-consensual sendings of such content as a tool for asserting power, seeking recognition, or gaining social status within male peer groups [26–28]. Conversely, adolescent girls are more frequently identified as victims of such practices, experiencing more severe psychosocial repercussions, including heightened anxiety, depression, and self-harming tendencies [16, 29, 30].

Despite these initial findings, the scientific community cannot yet reach a reliable conclusion regarding gender differences in non-consensual sexting specifically among adolescents from ethnocultural minority backgrounds. This lack of certainty stems from the fact that existing studies have almost exclusively utilized samples of native-born adolescents or youth from majority populations in Western contexts. Generalizing these results to minority groups is methodologically and theoretically problematic because the intersection of gender and ethnocultural background may create distinct patterns of vulnerability. According to Ecological Systems Theory adolescents from ethnocultural minority backgrounds often navigate conflicting value systems, frequently feeling “torn between two worlds” as they attempt to maintain their heritage roots while embracing host-country norms [31]. Within this framework, traditional patriarchal norms of their culture of origin may interact with the digital culture of the host country in ways that differ from the majority population [19].

Furthermore, the social marginalization and racism often experienced by minority youth may drive distinct behavioral motivations; for example, minority boys might engage in risky digital behaviors as a misguided attempt to secure peer recognition that is otherwise denied to them in their offline social environment [11, 21]. Without research that accounts for these cultural filters and ecological pressures, any assumption that gender differences manifest identically across ethnocultural groups remains speculative. Consequently, there is an urgent necessity for research to explore how the interplay of gender and minority status shapes the extent of involvement in non-consensual sexting, particularly in diverse societies like Greece [17], where such evidence is currently absent.

The role of peer pressure, self-control and sexual preoccupation: a theoretical framework

To comprehensively understand the mechanisms driving adolescent involvement in non-consensual sexting, it is imperative to move beyond simple demographic profiling, such as gender-based differences, and investigate the contribution of both environmental variables and intrapersonal characteristics. The theoretical foundation for this approach is provided by the synthesis of Ecological Systems Theory [19] and Social Cognitive Theory [32], unified by the principle of reciprocal determinism [32]. Under this integrated lens, the triadic interaction between personal determinants (self-control capacity, sexual preoccupation), behavioral outcomes (non-consensual sexting), and environmental influences operates within the hierarchical structure of the ecological microsystem (native peer pressure, referring specifically to the participants’ peers from the native Greek majority who do not have a migrant background). Consequently, the adolescent is not viewed as a passive recipient of environmental influences, but as an agent of experience who interacts dynamically with their social context [19, 32]. According to these perspectives, adolescent behavior is understood through the presence of hierarchical environmental systems—specifically the “microsystem” representing immediate interpersonal relationships—alongside internal cognitive determinants. Consequently, this study emphasizes the parallel predictive roles of environmental contexts and personal traits to identify the factors that contribute to digital risks [19, 32, 33].

Within this framework, the present research focuses on perceived peer pressure as the primary environmental predictor, alongside self-control and sexual preoccupation as key intrapersonal traits. The selection of peer pressure is justified by the fact that the peer group constitutes the primary “developmental habitat” in adolescence, representing the microsystem [19], where the need for social belonging often overrides the influence of family or school [17, 19]. Peer pressure is defined as the influence exerted by equals to align an individual’s attitudes and behaviors with group norms to ensure acceptance [34, 35]. Regarding intrapersonal traits, self-control is conceptualized here—within the agentic perspective of Social Cognitive Theory [32]—as a self-regulatory mechanism that allows the individual to inhibit maladaptive impulses and resist environmental triggers [36]. Finally, sexual preoccupation, which is defined as a persistent cognitive investment in sexual thoughts and fantasies that exceeds normative curiosity, was chosen because it functions as a mechanism of “cognitive priming”, making individuals more susceptible to engaging in sexually charged behaviors when prompted by social cues [32, 37].

Empirical evidence specifically linking these variables to non-consensual sexting remains limited but illustrative. Regarding social influence, studies indicate that adolescents experiencing high levels of peer pressure are significantly more likely to participate in the unauthorized dissemination of sexual content, often driven by the fear of social exclusion or the enforcement of “toxic” group norms [5, 38, 39]. In our integrated model, this peer influence is not merely an external force; its impact is filtered through the adolescent’s internal capacity to evaluate risks and manage sexualized thoughts. However, it is important to note that the existing body of research typically examines peer pressure as a broad, undifferentiated construct within majority populations, without accounting for the ethnocultural background of the peers involved or the specific dynamics of the host-country environment (e.g. native peer pressure). Intrapersonally, low self-control has been systematically identified as a robust predictor of “sext dissemination”, as individuals with limited self-regulatory capacity are more prone to impulsive, aggressive, or vengeful digital actions without evaluating the long-term legal or psychological consequences [2, 13, 14]. Furthermore, regarding sexual preoccupation, at least to date, only Authors [15] found that awareness of one’s own sexual preoccupation can function protectively, whereas unmanaged preoccupation may facilitate involvement in non-consensual practices.

Despite these findings, there is a profound literature gap concerning the focused role of these variables (peer pressure, self-control, sexual preoccupation) in non-consensual sexting among adolescents from ethnocultural minority backgrounds. While these studied variables are expected to remain robust predictors due to their fundamental nature in adolescent development, their role within this specific adolescent population necessitates focused validation. For these youth, the experience of social marginalization or the intensified need for native peer validation within a host country provides a unique ecological context where social integrative motives may conceptually overshadow internal self-regulatory thresholds. Similarly, the dynamic interaction within the microsystem is further situated within the broader macrosystem, where the conflict between heritage values and host-country digital norms shapes the symbolic meaning of peer validation [31]. Crucially, the present study specifically addresses the gap of how pressure originating from native peers in the host country affects the digital risk-taking of minority youth. For youth from ethnocultural minority backgrounds, the experience of social marginalization or the intensified need for native peer validation within a host country may provide a unique ecological context for these predictors to manifest. Similarly, the roles of self-control and sexual preoccupation may be influenced by the negotiation between the traditional values of their culture of origin and the digital norms of the host country, which often entails differing perspectives on privacy and sexual expression. Exploring these variables specifically within this adolescent group is essential to confirm that established behavioral models maintain their predictive validity in contexts of increased psychosocial vulnerability. While the current investigation does not employ a comparative design with Greek-born peers, focusing on this specific student group provides a necessary first step in examining the applicability of these predictive models within this digital experience. This underscores the urgent necessity for targeted research to inform the development of culturally inclusive school-based prevention strategies.

Method

Participants

Exceeding the minimum requirement of 234 participants established by the a priori G*Power analysis (see subsection "Data analysis plan"), the final sample consisted of 293 junior high school1 students (59% self-identified as boys; Mage = 14.9, SD = 0.93) from ethnocultural minority groups (Albania [35.8%], Bulgaria [35.2%], Other countries (29%): Russia [8.5%], Ukraine [7.2%], Israel [6.8%], Armenia [6.5%]). While the study targeted both first- and second-generation migrants, the final sample consisted exclusively of second-generation adolescents. This final sample size was derived from an initial pool of 312 collected questionnaires, after excluding 19 datasets during the data screening phase due to extensive missing values. Classification into these specific ethnocultural minority groups was based on the parents’ country of origin, as reported in the participants’ demographic data. Students attended the third grade of randomly selected public schools located in the two largest regions of Greece, which also report the highest proportions of students with migrant ethnocultural backgrounds [41]. The selection of this grade level lies in its alignment with the developmental stage of mid-adolescence (ages 14–15), during which adolescents demonstrate the highest levels of engagement in risky online behaviors [42, 43], such as non-consensual sexting. Inclusion in the study required that participants use the internet and/or electronic/digital media. The response rate of the school units was 62%. The pilot sample (Ν = 24) was collected using the same sample technique but was not incorporated into the main sample to ensure methodological rigor and the validity of the final measurement, since the pilot administration led to minor wording refinements and clarifications in certain items. Eligibility for participation in both pilot and main study was determined by two inclusion criteria: (a) use of digital media and electronic devices, (b) migrant or foreign ethno-cultural background, and (c) adequate proficiency in the Greek language. Given that all participating students were enrolled in mainstream Greek public schools, their language proficiency to fully comprehend the self-report scales was ensured by their daily academic engagement and further verified by the teaching staff prior to the distribution of the consent forms.

Data analysis plan

The data analysis for the present study was conducted in four distinct phases to ensure the validity of the measurement models and the rigorous testing of the research hypotheses.

First, the psychometric properties of the self-report scales were evaluated for the specific sample of ethnocultural minority adolescents. To avoid analytical redundancy and ensure methodological rigor, a split-sample validation approach was employed [44, 45]. Specifically, the total sample (N = 293) was randomly divided into two independent subsamples. Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA) using principal axis factoring with oblique rotation was conducted on the first half of the sample (Subsample A; n = 146) to identify the factor structures, followed by Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) on the second half (Subsample B; n = 147) to validate these measurement models. Reliability was assessed for the total sample through Cronbach’s alpha (a) and Composite Reliability (CR) coefficients, ensuring internal consistency across all dimensions.

Furthermore, prior to data collection, an a priori power analysis was performed using G*Power 3.1 [46] to determine the minimum required sample sizes needed to detect meaningful effects across all research objectives, with parameters set at a power of (1-β) = 0.85 and a significance level of α = 0.05. The recruited sample (N = 293) safely exceeded the minimum requirement of 234 participants for the most demanding analysis.

Detailed parameters and actual power estimations for all analyses are provided in the supplemental file. A one-way within-subjects ANOVA was employed to address the first research objective, specifically examining whether adolescents’ involvement significantly differed across the three roles of non-consensual sexting (sending, receiving, forwarding). Prior to the analysis, the assumption of sphericity was assessed via Mauchly’s test, and where violated, Greenhouse-Geisser corrections were applied to ensure the validity of the F-statistic. Additionally, multivariate analyses of variance (MANOVAs) were conducted to address the second and third research objectives. The second objective examined whether involvement in non-consensual sexting (sending, receiving, forwarding) differed based on specific ethnocultural background. For this purpose, adolescents were categorized into three statistically robust groups: students of Albanian origin, Bulgarian origin, and a consolidated group of students from other ethnocultural backgrounds (Russia, Ukraine, Israel, Armenia). Similarly, the third research objective concerning gender-based differences was examined on the total sample of minority adolescents (N = 293) rather than separately within each ethnocultural group.

Prior to the regression analyses, Pearson correlation (r) analyses were conducted to map the preliminary associations among non-consensual sexting, native peer pressure, self-control, and sexual preoccupation. Finally, to address the fourth, fifth, and sixth research objectives regarding the simultaneous predictive contributions of native peer pressure, self-control, and sexual preoccupation, a series of Standard Multiple Regression Analyses were performed for each dimension of non-consensual sexting. In these models, native peer pressure, self-control, and sexual preoccupation were entered simultaneously as predictors to evaluate the unique variance explained by each variable while controlling others. The choice of standard simultaneous entry over a hierarchical approach was strategically guided by the exploratory nature of the investigation within the under-researched population of adolescents from ethnocultural minority backgrounds. Given the profound literature gap regarding the interplay of these specific predictors in this group, all variables were treated as theoretically equivalent in their potential influence. This simultaneous entry allowed for a robust assessment of each factor’s unique predictive contribution without the bias of an a priori hierarchical prioritization, which currently lacks a clear empirical basis in the existing literature for adolescents from ethnocultural minority backgrounds. All statistical assumption checks for ANOVA, MANOVA, and Multiple Regression are thoroughly detailed in the supplemental file.

Measures

Participants provided demographic data (gender, age, country of origin) followed by the self-report scales detailed below. It should be noted that the parents’ country of origin was utilized solely as a demographic proxy to categorize ethnocultural background. The study did not employ a specific psychometric scale to measure the strength of ethnic identity or the intensity of acculturation stress; therefore, group categorization reflects demographic affiliation rather than the subjective level of identification with Greek culture.

Non-consensual sexting

Participants’ engagement in non-consensual sexting behaviors was evaluated through a modified form of the Sex & Tech Scale [47], which has previously demonstrated robust psychometric performance within the Greek adolescent population [48]. For the current study, which focused on a sample of adolescents from ethnocultural minority backgrounds, a shortened 12-item version of the scale was utilized. The scale comprises three distinct dimensions, each containing four items: (a) Sending non-consensual sexually explicit content (e.g., “During the past year, have you sent a sexually explicit message [e.g., via email, Messenger, or mobile phone] to a known or unknown person with the intent to upset or offend them?”), (b) Receiving such content (e.g., “During the past year, has anyone, whether familiar or unfamiliar to you, posted a sexually explicit photo or video on your social media profile [e.g., Facebook, Instagram] that caused you distress or offense?”), and (c) Forwarding or unauthorized dissemination of sexual material involving third parties (e.g., “In the last year, have you shared a sexually explicit message concerning another person with someone you know or do not know [e.g., via email, Messenger, or mobile phone]?”). Adolescents recorded their responses on a 5-point Likert-type scale, ranging from 1 (Never) to 5 (Very often).

To validate the scale’s structure an E.F.A. using principal axis factoring with oblique rotation was conducted on the first randomly split half of the sample (Subsample A; n = 146), yielding a three-factor solution that accounted for 54.2% of the total explained variance (Sending: 21.5%; Receiving: 18.3%; Forwarding: 14.4%). Factor loadings ranged from 0.431 to 0.745, with no significant cross-loadings observed. Internal consistency was high, with Cronbach’s coefficients reaching 0.812 for Sending, 0.856 for Receiving, and 0.794 for Forwarding. Furthermore, C.F.A. conducted on the second independent half of the sample (Subsample B; n = 147) confirmed a satisfactory fit for the data collected: Standardized factor loadings were all statistically significant (χ2(51, n = 147) = 88.50, χ2/df = 1.73, p < .001; CFI = 0.948; TLI = 0.936; RMSEA = 0.071; SRMR = 0.050), ranging from 0.521 to 0.817. The scale’s reliability was further reinforced by CR indices, which yielded values of 0.838 for Sending, 0.889 for Receiving, and 0.821 for Forwarding, indicating that the instrument is psychometrically sound for assessing non-consensual sexting dynamics among adolescents from ethnocultural minority backgrounds in Greece.

Native peer pressure

Participants’ perceived peer pressure exerted by their native (Greek) peer group (i.e., adolescents without a migrant or foreign background) was evaluated using the Greek version of the Peer Pressure Scale [49], using the standard back-translation procedure. The instrument consists of eleven items designed to gauge an adolescent’s susceptibility to the influence of their peers (e.g., “My native friends could push me into doing just about anything”). Responses were recorded on a 5-point Likert-type scale, ranging from 1 (Strongly disagree) to 5 (Strongly agree).

To verify the instrument’s structural validity an E.F.A. was performed on Subsample A (n = 146) using principal axis factoring with oblique rotation. The analysis confirmed a unidimensional structure, consistent with the original theoretical framework, which accounted for 55.1% of the total explained variance. Factor loadings for the items ranged from 0.518 to 0.812, with no significant cross-loadings identified. The internal consistency was high, as evidenced by a Cronbach’s α coefficient of 0.884. Subsequently, a C.F.A. was conducted on Subsample B (n = 147) to further validate this single-factor model, yielding a good fit for the data collected: χ2(44, n = 147) = 80.25, χ2/df = 1.82, p < .001; CFI = 0.955; TLI = 0.944; RMSEA = 0.075; SRMR = 0.042. Standardized factor loadings were all statistically significant at p < .001, ranging from 0.548 to 0.802. Finally, the CR index was 0.901, reinforcing the scale’s robust internal reliability and its suitability for assessing native peer pressure dynamics among adolescents from ethnocultural minority backgrounds in Greece.

Self-control

Adolescents’ self-control capacity was evaluated using the Greek version of the Brief Self-control Scale [36], using the back-translation procedure. The scale consists of thirteen items designed to gauge an individual’s ability to inhibit maladaptive impulses and adhere to socially acceptable behavioral norms (e.g., “I am good at resisting temptation”). Participants recorded their responses on a 5-point Likert-type scale, ranging from 1 (Not at all like me) to 5 (Very much like me).

To verify the structural validity of the scale an E.F.A. was conducted on Subsample A (n = 146) using principal axis factoring with oblique rotation. The analysis supported a unidimensional structure, consistent with the scale’s original theoretical construct, accounting for 57.9% of the total explained variance. Factor loadings ranged from 0.658 to 0.811, with no substantial cross-loadings observed. The scale exhibited high internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s α coefficient of 0.894. Subsequently, a C.F.A. conducted on Subsample B (n = 147) further validated this single-factor model, yielding a satisfactory fit for the data collected from the independent subsample: χ2(65, n = 147) = 115.30, χ2/df = 1.77, p < .001; CFI = 0.941; TLI = 0.944; RMSEA = 0.073; SRM= 0.048. Standardized factor loadings were all statistically significant at p < .001, ranging from 0.531 to 0.806. Finally, the CR index was 0.903, reinforcing the scale’s robust internal reliability and its suitability for assessing the self-control mechanism among adolescents from ethnocultural minority backgrounds in Greece.

Sexual preoccupation

Adolescents’ sexual preoccupation was evaluated using the sexual preoccupation subscale of the “Sexual scale” of Snell and Papini [50], which has previously demonstrated robust psychometric performance within the Greek adolescent population [15]. The scale consists of ten items concerning individuals’ intense sexual thoughts or fantasies (e.g., “I think about sex all the time”). Participants recorded their responses on a 5-point Likert-type scale, ranging from 1 (Disagree) to 5 (Agree).

To verify the structural validity of the scale an E.F.A. was conducted on Subsample A (n = 146) using principal axis factoring with oblique rotation. The analysis supported a unidimensional structure, consistent with the scale’s original theoretical construct, accounting for 54% of the total explained variance. Factor loadings ranged from 0.493 to 0.791, with no substantial cross-loadings observed. The scale exhibited high internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s α coefficient of 0.886. Subsequently, a C.F.A. conducted on Subsample B (n = 147) further validated this single-factor model, yielding a satisfactory fit for the data collected from the independent subsample: χ2(35, n = 147) = 67.45, χ2/df = 1.92, p < .001; CFI = 0.958; TLI = 0.947; RMSEA = 0.054; SRM= 0.039. Standardized factor loadings were all statistically significant at p < .001, ranging from 0.536 to 0.814. Finally, the CR index was 0.897, reinforcing the scale’s robust internal reliability and its suitability for assessing sexual preoccupation among adolescents from ethnocultural minority backgrounds in Greece.

Procedure

Following formal approval from the Educational Research Committee of the Department of Primary Education of the University of the Aegean (Date: 18/04/2024, Protocol No.: 15831), data collection for the main study was conducted between October 2024 and June 2025. Schools were recruited through random selection and contacted via email, receiving a detailed overview of the study project alongside documentation of ethical clearance. Participants were identified based on school records indicating students of migrant or foreign national background, as provided by the school administration, and only eligible students meeting these predefined criteria were invited to participate. Within participating schools, administrators and teaching staff facilitated the distribution of informed consent forms to these students, which outlined the aims of the study and included full researcher contact details. Parental or guardian consent was required for participation. In total, 79% of adolescents returned signed consent forms. Self-report questionnaires were administered in person during scheduled 20-minute classroom sessions, supervised by the research team in the presence of school staff. The procedure was implemented without difficulties, and no external incentives were offered to participants. An identical protocol was followed for a preliminary pilot sample conducted between April and May 2024. Throughout the study, all ethical principles were strictly observed, including voluntary participation and anonymity.

Results

Prevalence of non-consensual sexting and role comparison

Descriptive statistics revealed that 87.8% of the total sample had engaged in at least one form of non-consensual sexting. Since the response scale ranges from 1 (“Never”) to 5 (“Very often”), this prevalence reflects participants scoring 2 or higher on any dimension, thereby capturing even isolated or infrequent incidents.

Also, the results of the one-way within-subjects ANOVA (with Greenhouse-Geisser corrections applied due to sphericity violation) revealed a statistically significant within-subjects effect for the role of involvement, F(1.82, 531.44) = 42.65, p < .001, partial η2 = 0.13, suggesting that the extent of engagement is not uniform across the different roles of non-consensual sexting. Discussion

The present study aimed to investigate non-consensual sexting among adolescents from ethnocultural minority backgrounds, while simultaneously examining the predictive contribution of interpersonal (native peer pressure) and intrapersonal factors (self-control, sexual preoccupation) within this under-researched population.

Prevalence of non-consensual sexting and role comparison

Regarding the first research objective, the findings revealed that adolescents from ethnocultural minority backgrounds are actively involved in non-consensual sexting, though the nature of this involvement is not uniform across different roles. Specifically, the results demonstrated that adolescents engage in the sending and receiving of non-consensual sexual content to a significantly greater extent than they engage in the forwarding of such material. Furthermore, while involvement as both senders and receivers fluctuated above the theoretical average of the scale, the unauthorized forwarding of content remained significantly lower, gravitating around the scale’s midpoint.

These findings fully confirm Hypothesis 1, which predicted a statistically significant differentiation in involvement across non-consensual sexting roles, with direct dyadic interactions being more prevalent than forwarding. While the elevated rates of sending and receiving align with previous international evidence indicating that adolescents from migrant backgrounds encounter specialized digital risk patterns [22, 23], the average levels of forwarding suggest that secondary dissemination may follow a different behavioral pattern within this group. This distinction refines the existing literature [4, 5, 51, 52], which often treats sexting as a unitary phenomenon without distinguishing between dyadic interactions (sending/receiving) and broader dissemination (forwarding). By confirming this internal hierarchy of risk, the study establishes a clear behavioral baseline for minority youth, highlighting how digital vulnerability manifests within this population regardless of comparative trends in the majority.

The statistically higher involvement in sending and receiving can be interpreted through the lens of Ecological Systems Theory [19]. Adolescents from ethnocultural minority backgrounds often face social marginalization, racism, and a perceived lack of social connectivity within their physical microsystems, such as the school/academic environment [20, 53]. In this context, engaging in dyadic non-consensual sexting may theoretically function as an alternative channel for identity expression or a potential form of agentic resistance against school-based inequality, as minority students act as agents of their experiences to secure validation denied in offline interactions [53]. These digital behaviors might serve to compensate for limited acceptance and affirmation in face-to-face contacts, where discrimination and labeling often hinder psychosocial adjustment [20, 53].

Furthermore, the heightened focus on sending and receiving—both of which involve direct, often reciprocal, digital interactions—reflects the “victim-perpetrator overlap” characteristic of digital aggression. Youth from minority groups may find themselves trapped in a cycle of risky sexualized interactions where the boundaries between being a sender and a recipient are fluid, especially during mid-adolescence, a period where the formation of sexual identity is a primary developmental challenge [54]. This theoretical assumption is explicitly supported by our data, as evidenced by the strong, statistically significant positive correlation observed between the sending and receiving of non-consensual content, empirically confirming this fluidity and overlap of roles within the specific population.

In contrast, the lower rates of forwarding (gravitating around the scale’s midpoint) suggest that while direct involvement is a prevalent risk for minority youth, the secondary dissemination of material involving third parties may be constrained by different social or cultural filters. It is possible that for adolescents from ethnocultural minority backgrounds, who often navigate traditional value systems from their culture of origin alongside host-country norms, the act of publicly shaming a peer through forwarding is perceived as a more severe transgression than private, dyadic exchanges. This suggests that while digital risks seem to be heightened for this adolescent group, they manifest primarily through direct digital vulnerability rather than the systematic secondary victimization of others within their peer networks. Nevertheless, further research is deemed necessary to confirm the pattern of engagement in non-consensual sexting and the corresponding interpretations for this adolescent group.

Differences of non-consensual sexting based on ethnocultural background

Regarding the second research objective (ethnocultural differentiation in non-consensual sexting), Hypothesis 2 was confirmed. The findings revealed that adolescents’ specific ethnocultural background was associated with significant variations in their reported digital behavior. Specifically, students of Albanian and Bulgarian origin reported being involved in the sending and receiving of non-consensual sexual content to a greater extent compared to their peers from other ethnocultural minority groups. Furthermore, while the involvement of Albanian and Bulgarian adolescents, as senders and receivers, fluctuated above the scale’s midpoint, involvement for the consolidated group of students from other backgrounds remained significantly lower. In contrast, involvement in forwarding sexual content remained relatively uniform across all ethnocultural groups and was found to gravitate around or below the scale’s midpoint, indicating that secondary dissemination is a less prevalent behavior compared to direct dyadic interactions.

The observed pattern reflects similar findings in the related literature regarding risky online behaviors among minority youth in Greece. For instance, Authors [17, 22] observed that while specific ethnocultural background was not the primary focus of their study, participating students of Albanian and Bulgarian origin generally demonstrated high rates of involvement in cyberbullying. This suggests that these specific minority groups may be more susceptible to a broader range of risky digital interactions, including both aggressive (cyberbullying) and sexualized (non-consensual sexting) behaviors.

The heightened susceptibility of Albanian and Bulgarian adolescents to sending and receiving non-consensual content can be interpreted through the lens of social marginalization and the specific psychosocial pressures these groups encounter in the host country. As noted by Authors [17] and Zeneli [18], adolescents of Albanian and Bulgarian origin are among the ethnocultural groups for which native populations often maintain the most negative perceptions and social distance. Such experiences of discrimination, labeling, and poor psychosocial adjustment within the school environment can make these adolescents more prone to engaging in “reactive deviant behaviors” [22].

In this context, involvement in direct non-consensual sexual exchanges (sending and receiving) may function as a compensatory mechanism. When adolescents perceive limited acceptance and affirmation in their face-to-face contacts at school, they may utilize digital interactions, even risky ones, to secure peer recognition or as an alternative channel for identity expression. The fact that forwarding was significantly lower and did not vary by ethnocultural background suggests that while direct dyadic vulnerability is high for marginalized groups, the act of unauthorized secondary dissemination may be constrained by social or cultural filters shared across different minority communities, prioritizing direct digital interaction as the primary mode of risk. Overall, these findings suggest that ethnocultural background is a critical demographic variable associated with digital risk, with Albanian and Bulgarian youth emerging as particularly vulnerable to the direct dimensions of non-consensual sexting.

Gender-based differences in non-consensual sexting

Regarding the third research objective, the findings of the present study indicated that gender did not emerge as a statistically significant factor in differentiating adolescents’ involvement in any of the three roles of non-consensual sexting (sending, receiving, and forwarding).

This result rejects Hypothesis 3, which anticipated that boys would demonstrate higher rates of involvement compared to girls. This finding is markedly inconsistent with the broader international literature, which frequently identifies adolescent boys as the primary perpetrators of digital sexual aggression and girls as the primary victims [1, 25]. While international evidence suggests that boys may use non-consensual sexting as a tool for social dominance or status seeking [26], the current data suggests a behavioral uniformity within this specific minority population that transcends traditional gender-based patterns.

This lack of differentiation can be interpreted through the lens of a shared “digital habitat” among minority youth. When adolescent population is characterized by universal behavioral patterns, such as high-frequency daily social media use, these shared conditions may function as common aggravating factors that override gender-specific socialization [17]. In this context, the pervasiveness of digital interaction among adolescents from ethnocultural minority backgrounds subjects both boys and girls to the same ecological risks, leading to a leveling effect in their self-reported risky digital behaviors.

Furthermore, the absence of gender differences may theoretically be attributed to the commonality of the acculturation process within the host country [31]. For adolescents from ethnocultural minority backgrounds, the overarching developmental task of navigating the intersection between their heritage values and the digital norms of the host country may create a unified behavioral trajectory. This shared experience of negotiating social integration and seeking a sense of belonging within a new cultural landscape may diminish the traditional gender-specific filters that typically guide digital behavior in majority populations. Consequently, the hypothesized drive to achieve social recognition within peer networks is likely to manifest as a generalized priority that influences both boys and girls equally, regardless of traditional gender norms.

Finally, the results may reflect the impact of “digital disinhibition”, a psychological mechanism that appears to transcend specific demographic boundaries. The structural anonymity and impulsivity inherent in digital environments may obscure the patriarchal or social filters that might otherwise constrain the digital behavior of girls, leading to a convergence of risk where both boys and girls are equally susceptible to peer-driven digital norms. This suggests that within contemporary digital ecology, vulnerability to non-consensual sexting is a generalized developmental challenge rather than a gendered one.

The predictive role of native peer pressure, self-control, and sexual preoccupation

Regarding the fourth, fifth and sixth research objectives, the results demonstrated that perceived native peer pressure, self-control and sexual preoccupation significantly contribute to the variance in the sending and receiving of non-consensual sexual content, although the model for forwarding did not reach statistical significance. Specifically, perceived native peer pressure emerged as the strongest positive predictor, while self-control functioned as a secondary negative (protective) predictor, and sexual preoccupation was identified as the weakest positive predictor. These findings confirm Hypotheses 4 (native peer pressure), 5 (self-control), and 6 (sexual preoccupation) for the sending and receiving roles.

The predictive patterns observed in this study provide a nuanced understanding of how perceived environmental and individual factors converge to shape digital risk among youth from ethnocultural minority groups. These results provide empirical support for the study’s theoretical framework, which synthesizes Social Cognitive Theory [32] and Ecological Systems Theory [19]. According to this synthesis, adolescent behavior is the product of reciprocal determinism between the microsystem (the immediate social environment) and internal cognitive-regulatory determinants. This suggests that the microsystem does not simply dictate behavior but provides the social-integrative context where an individual’s agentic self-regulation is tested. The dominance of native peer pressure over internal traits suggests how the (perceived) environmental microsystem may exert a primary influence on digital behavior, where social cues and group norms often define the parameters of acceptable online conduct for minority youth.

The finding that native peer pressure emerged as the most potent predictor underscores the critical role of social influence as the primary driver of the adolescent digital ecology within this specific subpopulation. However, it is essential to clearly distinguish between what the data actually showed and what constitutes a theoretical ex-post commentary. Empirically, the data strictly demonstrated a robust predictive relationship between perceived native peer pressure, self-control, and non-consensual sexting. The subsequent interpretation that, for adolescents from ethnocultural minority backgrounds, conforming to native peer group norms may serve as a critical strategy for securing social capital and seeking validation from the majority population, is a theoretical inference. Because the study did not directly measure acculturation stress or cultural identification, the notion that youth from minority backgrounds often engage in risky digital behaviors as a means to bridge social distances and foster acceptance within the host society, especially when they perceive the broader host society as exclusionary [11, 21] must be viewed as a conceptual speculation rather than a data-driven fact. Within this theoretical framework, the fear of social exclusion within the native peer microsystem potentially drives non-consensual sexual interactions as a misguided tool for social integration and maintaining standing in an environment where other forms of validation are limited.

Furthermore, the results imply a significant interplay where native peer pressure “overshadows” self-control. While self-control emerged as a negative predictor, its influence was secondary to that of native peer pressure. Within our integrated framework, self-control functions as the internal self-regulatory threshold that adolescents use to evaluate environmental triggers. The fact that its predictive power was lower than that of native peer pressure suggests that for this specific population, environmental social pressures may partially override internal self-control capacities. This is further exacerbated by the phenomenon of “digital disinhibition”, making even adolescents with moderate self-control more susceptible to impulsive digital actions when prompted by social dynamics. Consequently, even adolescents from ethnocultural minority groups with moderate self-control strength may find their internal “brakes” compromised when prompted by the immediate social demands of their peers.

Regarding sexual preoccupation, its identification as the weakest predictor highlights its role as a form of “cognitive readiness” or a “background facilitator”, acting as a mental priming mechanism where persistent sexual thoughts lower the psychological threshold for engaging in sexually charged actions once an external social trigger is presented. This suggests that while persistent sexual thoughts and fantasies may create the cognitive framework for sexualized digital behavior, this internal state might not be a sufficient trigger for action on its own. Instead, the transition from thought to the non-consensual act of sending or receiving sexual content seems to be more directly influenced by the immediate social demands of the peer group and the individual’s capacity for self-control. Within this framework, while sexual preoccupation provides the underlying cognitive susceptibility for engagement, the enactment of the behavior is ultimately dictated by the interplay between environmental native peer pressure and internal self-control strength.

Finally, the failure of the forwarding model to reach statistical significance suggests that the unauthorized secondary dissemination of sexual content involving third parties may be governed by different behavioral or cultural filters. This specific role (forwarder) might be less influenced by individual traits or dyadic pressure and more by situational group dynamics or cultural norms regarding public shaming that are not captured by the current intrapersonal predictors.

Overall, these findings imply a complex interplay between intrapersonal and interpersonal variables, suggesting that digital risk is a multifaceted phenomenon. This interplay could be more effectively mapped in future longitudinal research through a mediation model, which would clarify whether perceived native peer pressures first weaken internal self-control mechanisms before leading to the enactment of risky digital behaviors.

Limitations and future research directions

Despite the significance of the findings, the present study is subject to several limitations that necessitate a cautious interpretation of the results. First, the cross-sectional and correlational nature of the research design precludes the establishment of strictly causal relationships or temporal directions among native peer pressure, self-control, sexual preoccupation, and non-consensual sexting. Second, the study’s sample size and geographic scope, while statistically adequate, were restricted to specific urban regions in Greece, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to the broader population of ethnocultural minority groups across diverse national or rural contexts. Third, the unequal distribution and insufficient representation of specific ethnocultural subgroups (e.g., the smaller numbers of students from Russia, Ukraine, or Israel compared to those of Albanian and Bulgarian origin) hindered the ability to perform robust comparative analyses across all individual ethnic backgrounds. Fourth, the reliance on self-report measures introduces the possibility of social desirability bias or memory distortion, especially given the sensitive and potentially stigmatizing nature of digital sexual aggression. Fifth, the study’s exclusive use of quantitative methodology restricted the depth of exploration regarding the subjective meanings and situational nuances of adolescents’ digital experiences. Finally, the current model focused primarily on the school microsystem and individual traits, omitting other critical environmental systems, such as family dynamics or the specific immigrant-to-native ratio within school populations, which could further influence digital risk.

Finally, two critical limitations must be underscored regarding the ethnocultural dimension of the study. The absence of a Greek-born (native) control group prevents a direct comparison that would clarify whether the observed predictive power of native peer pressure is unique to minority youth or a generalized adolescent trend. Consequently, the findings regarding the relative importance of peer influence should be viewed as descriptive of the minority experience rather than as definitive proof of inter-group differentiation. Moreover, the study did not incorporate psychometric measures of cultural values, norms, or acculturation status (e.g., heritage vs. host-country identification). Since ethnic affiliation alone does not reflect how strongly an adolescent identifies with Greek culture, in the absence of such data, interpretations regarding “social-integrative motives” or “acculturation conflicts” remain exploratory and theoretically inferred rather than empirically observed.

Considering these limitations, future research should adopt a longitudinal perspective to empirically map the causal pathways and developmental trajectories of non-consensual sexting over time. Additionally, subsequent studies would benefit from utilizing larger, more geographically diverse, and representative samples to enhance the external validity of the predictive models. To strengthen the theoretical rationale, future investigations must include comparative samples of native-born adolescents and employ direct measures of acculturation and cultural orientation to empirically test the impact of ethnocultural background on digital risk. To refine ethnocultural comparisons, future investigations should employ stratified sampling techniques to ensure a more balanced and statistically robust representation of diverse minority subgroups. Methodologically, the inclusion of multi-informant data collection (e.g., gathering perspectives from parents, teachers, or peers) could mitigate the bias inherent in self-reports and provide a more objective assessment of adolescent digital behavior. Furthermore, the integration of qualitative or mixed methods approaches, such as semi-structured interviews, would allow researchers to explore the complex motivations and “digital feeling rules” that govern non-consensual exchanges. Finally, expanding the theoretical framework to include additional ecological layers, such as family-related factors and broader community-level variables, would facilitate a more holistic understanding of the interplay between various microsystems in shaping the digital ecology of adolescents from ethnocultural minority backgrounds.

Contribution of the Study

The present study significantly advances the literature by addressing the scarcity of empirical research on non-consensual sexting among ethnocultural minority youth. While the study does not employ a comparative design with native-born peers, its primary contribution lies in providing an in-depth, within-group analysis of digital risk patterns among minority youth. Using the Greek educational context as a focal point, it provides preliminary evidence on how migration status and specific ethnocultural backgrounds, particularly those of Albanian and Bulgarian origin, intersect with digital sexual risk. A key contribution is the refinement of the non-consensual sexting construct, which distinguishes between direct dyadic interactions and secondary dissemination, revealing that these roles follow distinct behavioral logics. Furthermore, by uncovering a gender-based behavioral convergence, the findings challenge traditional paradigms and suggest that shared digital habitats and the acculturation process may supersede traditional gender socialization. Crucially, the identification of perceived native peer pressure as the primary driver of digital deviance highlights how the pursuit of social integration can eclipse internal self-regulation, thereby extending the explanatory power of Ecological Systems and Social Cognitive theories to populations facing systemic social disadvantages. By establishing a clear behavioral baseline for this population, the findings provide a necessary empirical foundation for future research to further isolate the specific impact of ethnocultural identity on digital risk-taking. However, given the absence of a native-born control group and direct measures of cultural values, these contributions are presented as exploratory, providing a necessary empirical foundation for future comparative research to further isolate the specific impact of ethnocultural identity on digital risk-taking.

In terms of applied contribution, these findings provide fertile ground for school psychologists and educators to design culturally sensitive and inclusive prevention strategies. Given that adolescents of Albanian and Bulgarian origin emerged as particularly vulnerable within the studied sample, interventions should be tailored to the specific psychosocial pressures faced by these groups, particularly the “reactive deviance” stemming from social marginalization. Specifically, school-based programs should move beyond a generic focus on digital safety to address the complex interplay between peer group dynamics and individual self-control. Applied recommendations include the implementation of experiential and collaborative practices within both regular and reception classes to strengthen adolescents’ psychological connections to the school environment, thereby reducing the “need” to seek validation through risky digital exchanges. Moreover, interventions should incorporate modules on “digital disinhibition” and “cognitive readiness”, helping students recognize how the anonymity of the screen and persistent sexual thoughts can lower their moral barriers. Finally, a holistic, interdisciplinary approach involving teachers, parents, and school psychologists is essential to foster a school culture that promotes responsible digital citizenship and provides healthy alternatives for identity expression and social recognition among youth from ethnocultural minority backgrounds.

Conclusion

In conclusion, adolescents from ethnocultural minority backgrounds are involved in non-consensual sexting primarily as senders and receivers, while forwarding occurs at significantly lower rates. Ethnocultural background emerged as a critical demographic variable associated with significant variations in involvement in non-consensual sexting, with adolescents of Albanian and Bulgarian origin exhibiting the highest vulnerability within the digital ecology examined. Notably, gender does not influence involvement, suggesting a behavioral convergence within this specific digital ecology. Finally, engagement is driven by an interplay where perceived native peer pressure is the primary positive predictor, self-control serves as a secondary protective factor, and sexual preoccupation provides the underlying cognitive readiness. While these findings highlight the psychosocial vulnerability of minority youth, they should be interpreted strictly within the scope of the study’s non-comparative design, establishing a baseline for the minority experience rather than proving inter-group differentiation. This underscores the need for future research to incorporate broader cultural variables and native comparison samples to confirm these predictive pathways.
