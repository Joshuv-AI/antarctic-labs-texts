Engineered Muscle Tissue Analysis of Irisin Protection Against Glucocorticoid- and Cancer Cachexia-Induced Muscle Wasting and Cardiotoxicity
Authors: Young Hoon Son, Christina Y Sheng, Ji-Eun Jeong, Yuri Choi, Hyocheol Jung, Jihee Won, Audrey Atinsky, Junbeom Park, Jin Chul Kim, Keel Yong Lee, Young Il Park, Sung-Jin Park
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMuscle atrophy, a condition characterized by an imbalance between protein synthesis and degradation, leads to skeletal muscle wasting, exacerbating disease progression and increasing mortality rates. Exercise has been shown to counteract muscle wasting, with myokines—exercise-induced secretome proteins, and among them, irisin is known to mediate systemic benefits such as improved metabolism, reduction of oxidative stress, and promotion of tissue repair in skeletal muscle, thereby mediating beneficial effects of exercise against atrophy. Despite its promising therapeutic potential, the precise functions and signaling pathways of irisin in muscle and other organs remain insufficiently explored owing to the complexity and heterogeneity of in vivo environments. In this study, we present engineered skeletal muscle tissue models as biomimetic platforms to study irisin-mediated therapeutic interventions. Using this platform, we validated the ability of irisin to mitigate dexamethasone (DEX)-induced muscle atrophy by restoring myogenic markers, enhancing AKT-mediated protein synthesis, suppressing ubiquitin–proteasome–driven degradation, and preserving contractile function under catabolic stress. (e.g., irisin pretreatment increased tetanic stress in DEX-treated tissues by ~ 43% vs. DEX alone, n = 7–9 per group, p < 0.05). Furthermore, irisin effectively counteracted cancer cachexia-induced muscle wasting by improving myogenic gene expression, reducing atrophy markers, and enhancing muscle contractility (baseline stress restored by ~ 77%, n = 6–9 per group, p < 0.05). Extending these findings to cardiac tissues, we demonstrated irisin’s protective effects against DEX-induced cardiotoxicity using a human induced pluripotent stem cell-derived cardiomyocyte (hiPSC-CM) model (irisin significantly improved contractile amplitude and reduced cell death indices in DEX-treated hiPSC-CMs, p < 0.05). This study underscores the utility of engineered muscle platforms for investigating irisin’s therapeutic mechanisms. The demonstrated protective effects of irisin highlight its potential as a translatable therapeutic agent for muscle wasting and cardiac dysfunction. These platforms provide valuable insights into the systemic benefits of exercise-induced myokines and pave the way for developing exercise-mimetic therapies targeting muscle and cardiac disorders.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s13206-026-00268-9.Keywords: Myokine, Microphysiological system, Irisin, Myotube, Exercise, Muscle atrophy

Introduction

Muscle atrophy, driven by an imbalance between protein synthesis and degradation, results in skeletal muscle wasting that exacerbates disease progression, leads to debilitating outcomes, and significantly increases mortality rates, particularly in the aging population [1]. Muscle atrophy arises from diverse pathogenic conditions, including aging, diabetes, renal failure, acidosis, denervation, microgravity, and cancer cachexia [2–7]. Among these, drug- and cancer cachexia-induced muscle losses are among the most prevalent but remain challenging to manage due to individual differences in susceptibility and the heterogeneous mechanisms driving their progression. Despite the high prevalence of muscle atrophy, targeted therapies are limited, highlighting the urgent need for therapeutic interventions [1].

Physical exercise has emerged as a potential strategy for protecting muscles against atrophy [1, 8–11]. Over the past few decades, the discovery of myokines, protein factors secreted by skeletal muscle during exercise, has shed light on the mechanisms by which exercise mediates its protective effects [1, 8–11]. These myokines exert autocrine effects on muscle cells, paracrine effects on nearby tissues, and endocrine effects on distant organs, coordinating physiological responses across the body [12]. Among these myokines, the fibronectin type III domain-containing protein 5 (FNDC5)/irisin axis has gained significant attention due to its therapeutic potential. Irisin, a C-terminally cleaved fragment of FNDC5, is upregulated by peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor-gamma coactivator 1-alpha (PGC1-ɑ) and released in response to exercise [13–15]. Exercise-induced mechanical stimuli, such as cyclic strain or electrical pulse stimulation, significantly enhance irisin secretion [5, 16], mediating systemic benefits like improved metabolism, oxidative stress reduction, and tissue repair in skeletal muscle. Beyond its effects on skeletal muscle, irisin demonstrates significant roles in other tissues, including the heart, brain, adipose tissue, and bone, where it regulates glucose uptake, mitochondrial biogenesis, inflammation, and tissue regeneration [15, 17–22].

The therapeutic potential of irisin extends to conditions associated with muscle atrophy. Excessive or prolonged administration of glucocorticoids, such as dexamethasone (DEX), leads to skeletal muscle wasting by suppressing protein synthesis and enhancing protein degradation via the ubiquitin-proteasome system [23]. Similarly, cancer cachexia, a multifaceted syndrome characterized by severe muscle wasting, arises from complex interactions of pro-inflammatory, neuroendocrine, and tumor-related signaling pathways [24]. While irisin has been shown to counteract muscle atrophy by promoting myogenesis, reducing oxidative stress, and modulating inflammatory signaling pathways [3, 4], its specific role in cancer cachexia-induced muscle atrophy remains unexplored. This gap highlights the need to investigate whether irisin, as an exercise-induced myokine, can mitigate the unique and multifactorial mechanisms underlying cachexia-driven muscle wasting. In addition to its effects on skeletal muscle, exercise-induced irisin secretion provides cardiovascular benefits, including enhanced energy metabolism through mitochondrial biogenesis, reduced oxidative stress, and decreased inflammation and apoptosis [13, 17–22, 25, 26]. These roles suggest that irisin’s endocrine effects extend beyond skeletal muscle, making it a promising therapeutic agent for both skeletal and cardiac muscle diseases.

Engineered skeletal muscle tissues provide a tunable platform to model disease progression and evaluate therapeutic responses under controlled biomechanical and biochemical conditions [27–38]. By integrating anisotropic myotube alignment, electrical pacing, and contractility measurements, these bioengineered constructs recapitulate key aspects of native muscle physiology and allow real-time assessment of force generation, signaling dynamics, and atrophy phenotypes. In this study, we aimed to leverage an in vitro muscle platform for studying the protective effects of exercise-induced irisin against muscle atrophy. First, we fabricated anisotropic muscle tissues capable of enhanced myogenesis and evaluated AKT signaling and protein synthesis upon irisin administration. Next, we utilized the engineered muscle platforms to model DEX-induced muscle atrophy. Using these mouse and human muscle models, we validated the protective ability of irisin against DEX-induced muscle atrophy and further investigated the ability of irisin to mitigate maladaptive remodeling at the genetic and protein levels, thereby preserving muscle function under DEX exposure. Then we applied the same strategy to test protective effect of irisin against cancer cachexia-mediated muscle atrophy. Finally, we used human induced pluripotent stem cell-derived cardiomyocyte (hiPSC-CM) tissue model to explore the systemic endocrine effects of irisin, including its protective role against cardiac dysfunction associated with DEX-induced cardiotoxicity. Taken together, the study delineates irisin’s dual role in restoring AKT signaling, enhancing protein synthesis, and reversing catabolic responses while preserving contractile function. The findings provide mechanistic insights into irisin’s protective actions and establish a cross-tissue experimental framework for evaluating pharmacological interventions targeting muscle and cardiac atrophy in combined endocrine and oncogenic stress contexts. We hypothesized that irisin protects skeletal and cardiac muscle from glucocorticoid and cancer cachexia induced dysfunction by preserving anabolic signaling and contractile function in engineered tissue models.

Experimental Section

Cell Culture

C2C12 myoblasts (ATCC, CRL-1772) were expanded in growth media consisting of 10% (v/v) fetal bovine serum (Gibco 16140-071) and 100 U/mL penicillin (Sigma P7794-100MU) in high-glucose Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; Gibco 11995-065). Differentiation media comprised 2% (v/v) horse serum (Gibco 26050-088) and 100 U/mL penicillin in low-glucose DMEM (Gibco 11885-084).

Human skeletal muscle myoblasts (Lonza, CC-2580) were grown in SkGM-2 (Lonza) medium and used in experiments within two passages. Differentiation was induced using media containing 2% (v/v) horse serum (Gibco 26050-088), vitamin B12, penicillin, and insulin in low-glucose DMEM (Gibco 11885-084).

Human iPSC-CMs (Ncardia; Cor.4U ventricular-like cardiomyocytes) were used as pre-differentiated, quality-controlled cardiomyocytes. According to the manufacturer, these cells display ≥ 70–90% cardiac troponin T (cTnT)–positive purity by flow cytometry after 3 days in culture and express ventricular markers such as MLC2v and ACTN2, with organized sarcomeric structures and spontaneous contractile activity. Human iPSC-CMs (Ncardia) were cultured in human iPSC-CM culture medium (Ncardia). Cells were purified using a puromycin selection method driven by the cardiac alpha-myosin heavy chain promoter. After thawing, the medium was replaced with maintenance medium after 48 h, with media changes every two days.

Isotropic Muscle Tissue Culture

C2C12 myoblasts and human myoblasts were seeded in six-well plate (Corning) at a seeding density of 2 million cells per well. One day post-seeding after myoblasts reached nearly 100% confluency, the myoblasts are differentiated for 4–5 days prior to further experimentation. All cell cultures were kept in a HeraCell incubator (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at 37 °C and 5% CO2 atmosphere.

Anisotropic 2D Muscle Tissue Fabrication

Low-adhesive tape (Patco 5560) was applied to the entire surface of microscope cover glasses (22 wide × 22 mm long; VWR). A single square island (12 × 12 or 18.5 × 18.5 mm) was laser engraved into the tape using a CO2 laser (Epilog). Next, the square island was removed with the remainder of the coverslip masked. The coverslips were then immersed in 0.1 M NaOH for 5 min, followed by 0.5% APTES in 95% ethanol for 5 min and 0.7% glutaraldehyde for 30 min. The coverslips were rinsed and dried.

To make final solutions of 10 wt/v% gelatin and 2 w/v% microbial transglutaminase (mTG), equal volumes of 20 wt/v% gelatin (Sigma G2625) and 4 wt/v% mTG (Ajinomoto) were mixed together. The final solution was then quickly pipetted onto the exposed regions of the dried coverslips. Polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) stamps with channels of 30 (ridge width) × 4 (groove width) × 5 μm (groove depth) were used to pattern the solution. The stamps were left on the coverslips as gelatin cured for a minimum of 3 h. After curing, the coverslips and stamps were immersed in distilled water to re-hydrate the gelatin and remove the stamps. The rest of the tape were then removed carefully and water dried from the coverslips. The coverslips were then sterilized with UVO lamp for 90 s prior to seeding.

Anisotropic muscle tissues were generated via seeding C2C12 myoblasts and human myoblasts on top of the micromolded gelatin. A day after seeding, the myoblasts were differentiated in differentiation media consist of 2 v/v% horse serum (Gibco 26050-088) with 100 U/ml penicillin in low-glucose DMEM (Gibco 11885-084). The tissues were differentiated for 4–5 days prior to further experimentation.

3D Anisotropic Muscle Tissue Formation and Differentiation

All 3D C2C12 myofiber tissues were generated in PDMS molds. PDMS molds were sonicated in 70% ethanol and coated with 0.2% pluronic F-127 (Invitrogen) to prevent hydrogel adhesion. Nylon frames were sterilized in 70% ethanol, dried and placed within the molds as 3D tissue backbone support. Cell solution (2.5 M cells in 108 µL 2x media + 8 µL of 50 U / ml thrombin in 0.1% BSA in PBS) and a hydrogel solution (12 µL 2x media + 40 µL Matrigel + 40 µL of 20 mg / ml Fibrinogen in 0.9% (wt/v) NaCl solution) were prepared in each vial. Matrigel solution was added to the cell solution and mixed thoroughly with pipetting. The cell / hydrogel mixtures were then pipetted into the PDMS molds, polymerized at 37 °C for 30 min, and cultured in GM medium supplemented with 1.5 mg / mL 6-aminocaproic acid (ACA, Sigma) to reduce fibrinolysis for 3 days. The media were then switched to differentiation media supplemented with 2% horse serum and 2 mg / mL ACA. The media were changed every 2 days.

DEX-Induced and Cancer Cachexia-Mediated Muscle Atrophy Modeling and Irisin Treatment

Engineered muscle tissues were generated using C2C12 myoblasts and human skeletal muscle myoblasts. For glucocorticoid-induced muscle atrophy, tissues were treated with dexamethasone (DEX; Sigma-Aldrich) at a final concentration of 5 µM for 24 h. To assess the protective effects of irisin, tissues were pretreated with recombinant irisin (100 ng/ml; Abcam) for 24 h prior to DEX exposure.

Cancer cachexia was modeled using conditioned media derived from MDA-MB-231 breast adenocarcinoma cells (ATCC). To prepare for cancer cell-conditioned media, MDA-MB-231 cells were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 2% FBS for 24 h. The conditioned media were filtered through a 0.22 μm filter and stored at -80 °C until use. Differentiated engineered muscle tissues were treated with 50% (v/v) conditioned media diluted in differentiation medium for 24 h to induce cachexia-like muscle atrophy. Control tissues were maintained in standard differentiation medium without cancer cell-conditioned media. For protective studies, tissues were pretreated with recombinant irisin (100 ng/ml; Abcam) for 24 h prior to conditioned media exposure.

Following treatments, tissues were collected for analysis, including qRT-PCR for gene expression, Western blotting for protein expression, immunostaining for structural evaluation, and contractility assays to assess functional performance.

Real-Time Quantitative RT-PCR

RNA was isolated using the miRNeasy Mini Kit (QIAGEN). Reverse transcription was performed with Superscript II (Invitrogen). qRT-PCR was conducted using SYBR green supermix with StepOnePlus (Applied Biosystems). Primer sequences are listed in Tables S1 and S2 (Supporting Information).

Immunostaining

Cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 15 min. After the fixation period, aspirate PFA solution and wash PBS three times. Then Cells were then incubated with staining mixture containing primary antibodies for 2 h at room temperature. After washing with PBS, the cells were incubated with a secondary antibody for 1 h in the dark. The cells were then imaged using a Lecia microscope. The staining mixture used in this process consisted of 1 µL of DAPI and 1 µL of phalloidin with 198 µL of PBS. The following primary antibodies were used: Atroing1 (1:200, Santa Cruz, sc-166806), MuRF1 (1:200, Santa Cruz, sc-398608). Stained samples were imaged using a Leica STELLARIS 8 inverted confocal microscope, and images were analyzed in ImageJ/Fiji to quantify myotube morphology and Atrogin-1 expression.

For myotube width, F-actin images were thresholded to delineate myotube borders, and the width of individual myotubes was measured using the straight-line tool perpendicular to the long axis. For fusion index, nuclei (DAPI) and F-actin channels were merged, and the number of nuclei within multinucleated myotubes (≥ 3 nuclei) and the total number of nuclei in each field were counted; fusion index was calculated as the percentage of nuclei contained in multinucleated myotubes over total nuclei. To quantify Atrogin-1- or MuRF1-positive area, the Atrogin-1 and MuRF1 channels were thresholded to generate binary masks, and the positive area within the tissue region (defined from the F-actin channel) was measured; Atrogin-1 and MuRF1 expression were expressed as positive area normalized to total tissue area (area fraction).

Western Blot

Myotubes were washed twice with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and lysed in a buffer containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA). Protein concentrations were assessed using a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay kit (Pierce Biotechnology, Rockford, IL, USA). Equal amounts of protein were separated by SDS–PAGE and transferred onto nitrocellulose membranes (Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA). The membranes were blocked in Tris-buffered saline (TBS) containing 1% bovine serum albumin (BSA) and incubated with primary antibodies specific for Atrogin-1(MAFbx) (ECM Bioscience, Versailles, KY, USA), GDF15 and Ubiquitin (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Dallas, TX, USA), Irisin and Puromycin (Abcam, Cambridge, MA, USA), and α-Tubulin (Sigma-Aldrich). After washing, membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies for one hour at room temperature. Immunoreactive bands were detected using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) kit (Pierce, Rockford, IL, USA), and signal intensities were quantified using an LAS-3000 image reader (Fujifilm, Tokyo, Japan).

For puromycin incorporation, myotubes were treated with puromycin at a final concentration of 1 µM for 30 min, followed by cell collection in homogenization buffer for subsequent Western blot analysis. To assess the rate of puromycin clearance, myotubes were first incubated with puromycin for 30 min and then washed twice with PBS.

Contractility Assay Measurement and Analysis

Contractility assay construction, measurement, and analysis were previously described [39, 40]. Low-adhesive tape (Patco 5560) was applied to the entire surface of microscope cover glasses (22 wide × 22 mm long; VWR). A single square island (12 × 12 or 18.5 × 18.5 mm) was laser engraved into the tape using a CO2 laser (Epilog). Next, the square island was removed with the remainder of the coverslip masked. The coverslips were then immersed in 0.1 M NaOH for 5 min, followed by 0.5% APTES in 95% ethanol for 5 min and 0.7% glutaraldehyde for 30 min. The coverslips were rinsed and dried.

To make final solutions of 10 wt/v% gelatin and 2 w/v% microbial transglutaminase (mTG), equal volumes of 20 wt/v% gelatin (Sigma G2625) and 4 wt/v% mTG (Ajinomoto) were mixed together. The final solution was then quickly pipetted onto the exposed regions of the dried coverslips. Polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) stamps with channels of 30 (ridge width) × 4 (groove width) × 5 μm (groove depth) were used to pattern the solution. The stamps were left on the coverslips as gelatin cured for a minimum of 3 h. After curing, the coverslips and stamps were immersed in distilled water to re-hydrate the gelatin and remove the stamps. The rest of the tape was then removed carefully and water dried from the micromolded gelatin coverslips. Additionally, a cantilever-shaped pattern was laser engraved into the micromolded gelatin. The micromolded gelatin coverslips were then sterilized with UVO lamp for 90 s prior to seeding.

Anisotropic muscle tissues were generated via seeding C2C12 myoblasts, human myoblasts, or human iPSC-CMs on top of the micromolded gelatin. A day after seeding, the myoblasts were differentiated in differentiation media consisting of 2 v/v% horse serum (Gibco 26050-088) with 100 U/ml penicillin in low-glucose DMEM (Gibco 11885-084). The tissues were differentiated for 7 days prior to further experimentation. A day after seeding, human iPSC-CMs were cultured in human iPSC-CM culture medium (Ncardia).

The engineered tissues were stimulated with electrical pulse stimulation (EPS) (Ion Optix) of 10 ms over a range of frequencies from 1 to 3 Hz (cardiac muscle) and 1 and 10 Hz (skeletal muscle) and maintained at 37 °C during the experiments using a culture dish incubator (Warner Instruments). For each recording, cantilever bending imaging data were acquired at a frame rate of 100 Hz over at least 5 s.

Post-processing of the cantilever bending imaging data was conducted using custom software written in MATLAB (MathWorks) [41, 42]. First, we quantified the projected length of each MTF using image thresholding functions. Then, we calculated the projected length and the radius of curvature. where Es and νs are the Young’s modulus and Poisson’s ratio of the gelatin substrate, hs is the substrate thickness, hf is the average thickness of the muscle layer, and κ is the measured curvature of the cantilever. This analysis assumes linear elastic behavior of the gelatin substrate, small deflections, strong bonding between tissue and substrate, and a relatively thin muscle layer compared to the substrate thickness [41, 42], consistent with prior muscular thin film studies. Here, we used Young’s modulus Es = 56 kPa, and gelatin MTF thickness hs = 188 μm, as previously determined for this hydrogel system [39–41]. Statistical Methods

Statistical analysis was performed with JMP Pro 14 (SAS Institute, Inc) and Prism (version 10.3.0; GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA). For mRNA expression measurements, significance between groups was assessed using either the Tukey-Kramer honestly significant difference test or a one-way ANOVA for two-group comparisons. For comparisons involving more than two groups, one-way ANOVA was followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons test to evaluate pairwise differences among group means. Statistical analysis of Western blot data was conducted using the matched pairs test. Contractile stress differences were analyzed with the Wilcoxon nonparametric test, as the population of contractile stress data was not normally distributed.

Results

Engineered anisotropic muscle tissues exhibit enhanced myogenic differentiation and responsiveness to irisin

To establish and validate an in vitro muscle model for subsequent analysis of anabolic signaling and rescue mechanisms, we engineered isotropic and anisotropic 2D muscle tissues from C2C12 myoblasts (Fig. 1a, b). Consistent with previous reports, myotubes cultured on micro-molded gelatin substrates exhibited aligned architecture compared with those grown on standard flat surfaces, as confirmed by actin and DAPI staining (Fig. 1c). This aligned architecture significantly upregulated the expression of myogenic transcription factors MyoD, MyoG, and Myf5, confirming that our anisotropic 2D tissue model supports enhanced myogenic differentiation and provides a robust platform for downstream functional studies (Fig. 1d; p < 0.05–0.001).

Engineered anisotropic muscle tissues capable of enhanced myogenesis show upregulated AKT signaling and protein synthesis upon irisin administration. a, b Schematics of muscle tissue generation: isotropic tissues formed from myoblasts in standard culture dishes (a) and anisotropic tissues using micro-molded gelatin substrates to align myotubes after differentiation (b ). c Representative fluorescence images of isotropic and anisotropic C2C12 myofibers stained with DAPI (nuclei, blue) and actin (green), demonstrating enhanced alignment in the anisotropic group. d Relative mRNA levels of myogenic markers MyoD, MyoG, and Myf5 in isotropic (iso) versus anisotropic (aniso) tissues, with increased expression observed in anisotropic tissues (mean ± SEM, n = 6; *p < 0.05 **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, by t-test). e Schematic of irisin treatment on aligned (anisotropic) myotubes. f Representative immunoblots show AKT, phosphorylated AKT (pAKT), α-Tubulin, and puromycin incorporation (as a marker of protein synthesis) in control and irisin-treated samples. g Quantification of pAKT/AKT ratio and puromycin incorporation in anisotropic tissues indicates increased AKT signaling and protein synthesis upon irisin treatment (mean ± SEM, n = 3; *p < 0.05, by t-test)

Having established that structural anisotropy promotes enhanced myogenic differentiation, we selected the anisotropic muscle model for subsequent evaluation of irisin, a circulating peptide hormone implicated in muscle growth and metabolic regulation (Fig. 1e). Treatment of anisotropic myotubes with irisin resulted in a significant increase in AKT phosphorylation, without changes in total AKT levels, as shown by immunoblot analysis (Fig. 1f). Concurrently, puromycin incorporation assays demonstrated enhanced global protein synthesis following irisin exposure (Fig. 1g; p < 0.05), evidencing active translation and anabolic signaling via the AKT pathway.

These results demonstrate that the engineered anisotropic 2D muscle model exhibits robust myogenic differentiation and active responsiveness to irisin stimulation through enhanced AKT signaling and protein synthesis. The activation of anabolic and translational pathways by irisin suggests its potential to counteract catabolic processes, providing the rationale for subsequent investigations into its protective effects against muscle atrophy and cardiotoxic conditions.

Engineered Muscle Tissues for Modeling Protective Effects of Irisin against Muscle Atrophy

Building on these findings, we next applied the engineered 2D muscle platform to assess whether irisin treatment could attenuate muscle atrophy under catabolic stress conditions. The first disease target investigated was DEX-induced muscle atrophy (Fig. 2). A recent protein expression analysis study [5] highlighted the protective effects of irisin, an exercise-induced myokine, against muscle loss caused by DEX, a synthetic glucocorticoid. Irisin exerts these effects by counteracting the highly activated ubiquitin-proteasome signaling pathway associated with muscle atrophy. By leveraging the established 2D muscle platform, we examined whether DEX-induced atrophic signaling could be reproduced in vitro using the anisotropic muscle model and mitigated by irisin pretreatment.

Protective effect of irisin against dexamethasone (DEX)-induced muscle atrophy. a Experimental timelines for generating DEX-induced skeletal muscle atrophy models with and without irisin treatment. b, c mRNA expression levels of myogenic markers (MyoD and MyoG) and muscle atrophy markers (Atrogin-1 and MuRF1) in DEX-treated C2C12 myofibers (b) and human myofiber tissues (c) with and without irisin pretreatment. Data are presented as mean ± SEM (Tukey-Kramer honestly significant difference test, n = 9–12 tissues). d Representative immunostaining images of C2C12 myotubes showing nuclei (blue), F-actin (green), and Atrogin-1 (red), with corresponding quantification of myotube width, fusion index, and Atrogin-1-positive area normalized to total tissue area (Tukey-Kramer honestly significant difference test, n = 4 tissues per condition). White arrowheads highlight areas of increased Atrogin-1 expression in DEX-treated tissues. e, f Protein expression levels of Atrogin-1 in DEX-induced C2C12 tissues with and without irisin pretreatment. Western blot (e) and quantification (f) demonstrate that irisin significantly reduces DEX-induced Atrogin-1 expression (matched pairs test, n = 3 tissues)

Engineered 2D myofibers were differentiated for 6–7 days, followed by a 1-day challenge with DEX. Irisin was administered prior to DEX treatment to assess its protective effects. Control tissues were differentiated for 7–8 days without DEX and irisin treatment (Fig. 2a).

Quantitative PCR analysis showed that DEX treatment significantly downregulated the expression of myogenic markers MyoD and MyoG while upregulating atrophy markers MuRF1 and Atrogin-1 in both C2C12 myofibers (Fig. 2b) and human myofibers (Fig. 2c). Pretreatment with irisin preserved MyoD and MyoG expression and suppressed the DEX-induced upregulation of MuRF1 and Atrogin-1, indicating that irisin mitigates the molecular effects of DEX on muscle atrophy markers. Notably, 3D engineered muscle fibers treated with DEX and irisin showed similar changes in myogenic and atrophy-associated gene expression (Supplementary Fig. 1), supporting the relevance of our findings beyond the 2D anisotropic platform.

Immunostaining revealed that DEX treatment disrupted myofiber integrity, as shown by reduced F-actin organization and increased Atrogin-1 expression (Fig. 2d). Arrowheads in the images highlight the localized disruption of actin filaments and increased Atrogin-1 accumulation in DEX-treated tissues. Irisin pretreatment preserved F-actin structure and reduced Atrogin-1 expression, demonstrating its protective effects against DEX-induced structural damage.

Western blot analysis confirmed the upregulation of Atrogin-1 protein levels in DEX-treated tissues and the attenuation of this response by irisin pretreatment (Fig. 2e). Quantification of Atrogin-1 protein levels showed statistically significant differences between groups (Fig. 2f), consistent with the downregulation of ubiquitin-proteasome signaling observed in previous studies [5].

To validate key molecular features of glucocorticoid-induced muscle atrophy, we further evaluated changes in protein synthesis and ubiquitination. Immunoblot analysis demonstrated that DEX treatment significantly decreased phosphorylated AKT (pAKT) relative to total AKT, indicating suppression of anabolic signaling in engineered muscle tissues (Fig. 3a, d), consistent with previous reports. Moreover, DEX markedly reduced puromycin incorporation, reflecting diminished protein synthesis capacity (Fig. 3b, e), and increased protein ubiquitination levels (Fig. 3c, f), confirming activation of ubiquitin-mediated proteolysis. Importantly, irisin pretreatment partially preserved pAKT levels, significantly increased puromycin incorporation compared to DEX-only samples (p < 0.05), and normalized protein ubiquitination, suggesting protective modulation of AKT signaling and translational activity. Together, these findings confirm that DEX suppresses AKT-mediated anabolic processes and enhances protein degradation, while Irisin pretreatment effectively mitigates the catabolic effects of DEX in the engineered muscle tissue model.

Effects of DEX and irisin on AKT signaling, protein synthesis, and ubiquitination in cells. a-c Representative immunoblots for AKT, phospho-AKT (pAKT), and α-Tubulin in the Control (a), puromycin incorporation as a measure of protein synthesis (b), and ubiquitination (c) in the Control, +DEX, and +Irisin/+DEX conditions. d-f Quantification of pAKT/AKT levels (d), puromycin incorporation (e) and ubiquitination (f) normalized to Control (mean ± SEM, n = 4 per group)

To evaluate functional recovery, contractile stress measurements were performed under electrical pulse stimulation (EPS) at varying frequencies (Fig. 4). Contractile function was assessed using a contractility assay, wherein engineered muscle tissues were patterned, detached as thin films, and subjected to EPS; tissue deformation was recorded and analyzed to quantify contractile stress (Fig. 4a). Bright-field imaging showed that DEX-treated muscle constructs exhibited reduced contractile force, as evidenced by longer cantilever projection lengths during 10 Hz EPS, compared to controls (Fig. 4b; Supplemental Movie 1). Irisin pretreatment mitigated the decline in contractile performance, resulting in projection lengths comparable to those of untreated control tissues. Stress traces revealed that DEX treatment significantly reduced baseline and peak contractile stresses at both 1 Hz and 10 Hz EPS, mimicking the loss of muscle strength associated with muscle atrophy (Fig. 4b). Consistent with the genetic and protein data, irisin pretreatment significantly increased baseline, twitch, and tetanus stresses in DEX-treated atrophic myotubes (Fig. 4c–e). These data demonstrate that irisin, an exercise-responsive myokine, effectively prevents the DEX-induced loss of muscle strength.

(a) Schematic of contractility assay fabrication, showing laser engraving and cantilever detachment for mechanical measurement. b Representative bright-field images of myofiber cantilevers under 10 Hz EPS at peak stress for Control, +DEX, and +Irisin pretreatment + DEX groups. Irisin pretreatment mitigated the DEX-induced reductions in contractile performance. Data are presented as mean ± SEM; statistical significance assessed by Wilcoxon nonparametric test, *P < 0.05; n = 7–9 tissues per condition

Collectively, these findings validate the use of engineered muscle tissues as a model for studying muscle atrophy and highlight the potential of irisin as a therapeutic agent for protecting muscle integrity and function under atrophic conditions.

Assessing Protective Effect of Exercise-Induced Irisin against Cancer Cachexia-Induced Muscle Atrophy

Building on the demonstrated protective effect of irisin pretreatment against DEX-induced muscle atrophy, we extended our investigation to explore irisin’s role in mitigating other types of muscle atrophy. Among the muscle atrophies, cancer cachexia represents a multifaceted syndrome characterized by severe muscle atrophy driven by complex and heterogeneous mechanisms [24]. While circulating glucocorticoids play a fundamental regulatory role in these conditions [43], the mechanisms of cancer cachexia-induced muscle atrophy are distinct and multifactorial. Exercise has been shown to counteract the muscle wasting and increased protein turnover associated with cancer cachexia [44]. Therefore, we aimed to investigate the protective effects of exercise-induced irisin against cancer cachexia-induced muscle atrophy by analyzing gene and protein expression as well as muscle contractility (Fig. 5).

To model cancer cachexia, we developed an engineered muscle tissue system treated with cancer cell-conditioned media (Fig. 5a). Differentiated C2C12 myotubes were exposed to media conditioned by MDA-MB breast adenocarcinoma cells for one day to induce cachexia-like effects. Irisin was administered prior to the cancer supernatant treatment to assess its protective potential. Control tissues were differentiated for 7–8 days without exposure to cancer supernatant or irisin.

Quantitative PCR analysis revealed that cancer supernatant treatment significantly reduced the expression of myogenic markers MyoD and MyoG while markedly increasing the expression of muscle atrophy markers MuRF1 and Atrogin-1 (Fig. 5b). Importantly, irisin pretreatment preserved MyoD and MyoG expression while attenuating the upregulation of MuRF1 and Atrogin-1, suggesting its capacity to counteract transcriptional changes associated with cancer cachexia-induced muscle atrophy. Consistent with these findings, 3D engineered muscle fibers exposed to cancer cell-conditioned media and irisin showed similar changes in myogenic and atrophy-associated gene expression (Supplementary Fig. 1), further supporting the relevance of our 2D platform to 3D tissue contexts.

Immunostaining demonstrated a loss of myofiber integrity and increased Atrogin-1 expression in cancer supernatant-treated tissues (Fig. 5c). Actin filament organization was visibly disrupted, and Atrogin-1 accumulation was elevated, consistent with muscle atrophy. In contrast, irisin-pretreated tissues exhibited preserved actin structure and reduced Atrogin-1 expression, highlighting its protective effect.

Western blot analysis further confirmed the molecular alterations induced by cancer supernatant and the protective effects of irisin pretreatment (Fig. 5d). Cancer supernatant exposure significantly reduced the ratio of phosphorylated AKT to total AKT, indicating impaired anabolic signaling, while irisin pretreatment mitigated the reduction in pAKT/AKT levels (p < 0.05). Assessment of protein synthesis by puromycin incorporation revealed a pronounced decrease in the cancer supernatant group, which was significantly improved by irisin pretreatment (p < 0.05). Additionally, protein ubiquitination levels were elevated in cancer supernatant-treated tissues relative to controls, and irisin pretreatment mitigated this increase. Notably, this pattern paralleled our findings in the DEX-induced model (Fig. 3), where both cachectic and glucocorticoid stressors suppressed AKT-mediated anabolic signaling and promoted ubiquitin-mediated proteolysis, and irisin pretreatment effectively counteracted these catabolic changes. These results support the conserved role of irisin in mitigating diverse mechanisms of muscle atrophy in engineered muscle tissues.

Functional assessment of muscle contractility revealed significant impairments in contractile stress following cancer supernatant treatment, as evidenced by longer cantilever projection lengths during 10 Hz EPS (Fig. 6; Supplemental Movie 2). Stress traces indicated substantial reductions in baseline and peak contractile stress at both 1 Hz and 10 Hz EPS in cancer supernatant-treated tissues (Fig. 6a, b). Irisin pretreatment mitigated the decline in baseline stress (Fig. 6c), peak stress (Fig. 6d), and twitch stress (Fig. 6e) to levels comparable to control tissues.

These findings demonstrate that irisin, an exercise-induced myokine, effectively mitigates cancer cachexia-induced muscle atrophy in an engineered tissue model. Irisin’s ability to preserve muscle structure, key molecular features, and contractile function underscores its therapeutic potential for counteracting cachexia-associated muscle wasting.

Assessing the Interaction of Exercise-Induced Irisin between Skeletal Muscle and Heart in Protecting Against DEX-induced Cardiotoxicity

The development of engineered muscle tissues to identify the protective effects of irisin against DEX-induced muscle atrophy provided a foundation for evaluating the endocrine role of skeletal muscle-secreted myokines in mitigating remote organ diseases, such as heart disease. Myocardial infarction caused by pharmacological agents, including anti-inflammatory drugs, is a serious adverse effect on the cardiovascular system and is a common precursor to heart failure [45]. DEX, a synthetic glucocorticoid, is a prime suspect in drug-induced acute myocardial infarction [46, 47]. High doses of DEX are known to increase oxidative stress in the heart, induce myocardial degeneration, promote cardiomyocyte apoptosis, and impair cardiac contraction [47–49]. Consequently, therapeutic strategies to alleviate glucocorticoid-induced cardiotoxicity and prevent DEX-mediated cardiac complications are urgently needed. Irisin plays a vital role in maintaining cardiac structure and function by reducing oxidative stress, suppressing inflammation, and promoting mitochondrial biogenesis [13, 17–22, 25, 26]. Therefore, we assessed the endocrine effects of muscle-secreted irisin in protecting against cardiac dysfunction associated with glucocorticoid-induced cardiotoxicity.

To investigate the protective effects of exercise-induced irisin on cardiac muscle in a DEX-induced cardiotoxicity model, we developed an in vitro human induced pluripotent stem cell-derived cardiomyocyte (hiPSC-CM) system (Fig. 7a). hiPSC-CMs self-assembled into aligned tissues during a 5–6 day culture period. These aligned hiPSC-CMs were treated with DEX for 1 day to induce cardiotoxicity, while irisin was administered prior to DEX exposure. Control tissues were cultured for 7–8 days without DEX or irisin treatment.

The protective effects of irisin were first evaluated by measuring mitochondrial oxygen consumption in irisin- and DEX-treated cardiac tissues (Fig. 7b and c). Oxygen consumption assays revealed that irisin treatment significantly enhanced mitochondrial oxygen consumption in hiPSC-CMs compared to controls (Fig. 7b), consistent with the well-documented role of irisin in promoting mitochondrial biogenesis [13]. Furthermore, cardiac tissues treated with 100–500 ng/mL irisin maintained high oxygen consumption levels even after high-dose DEX exposure (Fig. 7c). These results confirm that irisin pretreatment at 100–500 ng/mL effectively protects mitochondrial biogenesis in engineered cardiac tissue from DEX-induced cardiotoxicity.

To further characterize irisin’s protective effects, we analyzed the expression of MuRF1, a marker of cardiac muscle atrophy (Fig. 6d and e). MuRF1 is known to be selectively expressed in both skeletal and cardiac muscles [50]. DEX treatment markedly increased MuRF1 expression in hiPSC-CMs (Fig. 7d), but irisin pretreatment significantly reduced MuRF1 levels, highlighting its potential to mitigate DEX-induced atrophic effects. Immunostaining revealed disrupted F-actin structure and elevated MuRF1 expression in DEX-treated tissues (Fig. 7e). In contrast, irisin pretreatment preserved F-actin organization and reduced MuRF1 accumulation, supporting its protective effect against DEX-induced cardiotoxicity.

Functional analysis of cardiac contractility demonstrated that DEX-treated tissues exhibited reduced contractile stress, with impaired systolic and diastolic function during 2 Hz EPS (Fig. 7f; Supplemental Movie 3). While all cardiac tissues responded 1:1 to EPS stimuli at frequencies of 1–3 Hz, DEX-treated tissues generated significantly lower systolic and twitch stresses compared to controls, with minimal impact on diastolic stress (Fig. 7g–j). Notably, irisin pretreatment significantly enhanced diastolic, systolic, and twitch stresses in DEX-treated cardiac tissues. This improvement in contractility closely mirrored the protective effects observed in irisin-pretreated skeletal muscle tissues (Fig. 4), further confirming the systemic endocrine effects of skeletal muscle-secreted irisin in mitigating heart disease.

These findings underscore the protective interaction between exercise-induced irisin and the heart, highlighting its role in counteracting DEX-induced cardiotoxicity. Irisin’s ability to preserve mitochondrial function, structural integrity, and contractile performance in hiPSC-CMs reinforces its therapeutic potential for addressing glucocorticoid-induced cardiac complications.

Discussion and Conclusions

This study highlights the utility of engineered anisotropic muscle tissue models for investigating myogenic differentiation, anabolic signaling, and protective responses under catabolic stress. Using these engineered constructs, we demonstrated that irisin exerts potent protective effects against DEX-induced muscle atrophy by preserving myofiber structural integrity, enhancing pro-anabolic and anti-catabolic signaling cascades, and counteracting atrophy-associated loss of contractile function. Moreover, irisin attenuated cancer cachexia induced muscle wasting and protected against DEX induced cardiotoxic response, highlighting its broader significance in maintaining muscle integrity and functional resilience.

Irisin emerged as a key myokine with robust therapeutic potential. Its roles in mediating exercise-induced benefits encompass metabolic regulation, anti-inflammatory actions, and both pro-anabolic and anti-catabolic effects [12, 14, 19–21, 51, 52]. Mechanistically, irisin promotes glucose uptake via ROS-mediated AMPK and MAPK pathways [53, 54], enhances myoblast proliferation and differentiation, and preserves muscle integrity by reducing senescence markers such as p53 and Bax [3, 4, 55, 56]. Its anti-inflammatory effects involve suppression of reactive oxygen species and modulation of cytokine expression, including downregulation of IL-6 and TNF-α and upregulation of IL-10 [19, 85–87]. Irisin also promotes protein synthesis through IGF-1–mediated AKT and ERK1/2 activation [3, 57, 58] and inhibits muscle atrophy by suppressing FoxO3a phosphorylation and atrogene expression [5].

Building on these collective findings, our study focused on delineating irisin’s protective effects against muscle atrophy, particularly its role in regulating protein synthesis and balancing anabolic and catabolic signaling pathways in skeletal muscle. Our findings demonstrate that irisin mitigates DEX-induced muscle atrophy by restoring myogenic markers (MyoD, MyoG) and suppressing atrophy markers (MuRF1, Atrogin-1) in both C2C12 and human muscle models, consistent with previous observations in isotropic tissue systems [5]. Irisin preserved myofiber integrity and contractile performance by enhancing AKT phosphorylation, increasing puromycin incorporation, and reducing protein ubiquitination, reinforcing its potential as an anti-atrophic agent. Notably, these molecular effects were consistently reproduced in the cancer cachexia-mimetic model, where irisin pretreatment mitigated the loss of myogenic gene expression, improved AKT-mediated anabolic signaling, augmented protein synthesis, and suppressed ubiquitin-mediated proteolysis.

Cancer is a well-known driver of cachexia, and tumor derived factors play a central role in promoting muscle wasting. Among various cancer types, breast cancer cells, particularly the MDA-MB-231 cell line, have been reported to secrete factors that directly induce muscle atrophy. These secreted factors include pro inflammatory, and cachexia associated mediators such as IL 6, TNF alpha, and GDF15, which impair anabolic signaling and promote protein degradation in muscle cells [59–63]. Accordingly, the use of MDA-MB-231 conditioned media provides a controlled in vitro approach to examine direct muscle cell responses to cachexia associated tumor derived factors, rather than the full systemic complexity of cancer cachexia observed in vivo. Functional analysis further revealed that irisin improved baseline, peak, and twitch stresses, effectively counteracting cachexia-induced muscle function loss. These results align with recent studies demonstrating irisin’s context-dependent activation of PI3K/AKT signaling, which promotes anabolic activity in skeletal muscle while regulating oxidative stress and metabolism in other tissues [64–66]. Contemporary findings also confirm that irisin significantly increased amino acid uptake—reflecting elevated protein synthesis rates—even though direct puromycin incorporation assays remain not investigated [65]. Furthermore, irisin suppresses ubiquitin–proteasome system activation by downregulating E3 ligases MuRF1 and Atrogin-1 across catabolic settings, including glucocorticoid exposure, denervation, microgravity-mediated unloading, and chronic kidney disease models [2–7]. Consistent with these mechanisms, exogenous irisin has also been shown to prevent hindlimb unloading–induced muscle fiber loss and mitochondrial disorganization in murine disuse models [4, 7]. Together, these mechanistic and translational insights reinforce our findings, establishing irisin as a potent modulator of AKT-mediated anabolic signaling, protein synthesis, and proteolytic control, with broad therapeutic potential for muscle wasting disorders arising from glucocorticoid exposure, cachexia, disuse, or renal dysfunction.

Extending these findings to cardiac tissues, irisin pretreatment protected hiPSC-derived cardiomyocytes from DEX-induced cardiotoxicity by maintaining mitochondrial respiration, F-actin organization, and contractile performance. Irisin reduced MuRF1 expression and mitigated DEX-induced hypo-contractility, consistent with prior evidence of its cardioprotective effects in ischemic and heart failure models [18–22]. These findings underscore irisin’s dual autocrine and endocrine functions in maintaining muscle and cardiac health by counteracting oxidative stress, metabolic impairment, and dysfunctional protein turnover. Collectively, these findings support a protective role for irisin in preserving cardiac function under glucocorticoid induced stress.

The experimental time scale used in this study reflects the differences between engineered in vitro muscle tissues and in vivo models of muscle atrophy. Short term catabolic stimulation is widely used in in vitro muscle atrophy studies, as early cellular responses such as reduced AKT signaling, increased MuRF1 and Atrogin-1 expression, and decreased protein synthesis typically emerge within 12–24 hours. In contrast, muscle atrophy in vivo develops gradually over longer periods and involves systemic interactions among multiple organs and hormonal and inflammatory signals. Accordingly, our findings are best interpreted as early, direct myofiber responses to catabolic stress and irisin treatment rather than a model of chronic systemic muscle wasting.This study has several additional limitations. First, our analysis of catabolic signaling focused on the canonical E3 ubiquitin ligases Atrogin-1 and MuRF1 and did not systematically profile other atrogenes or autophagy-related markers, which will be important to investigate in future work. Second, although the irisin concentration used here lies within the range reported to modulate muscle signaling in preclinical studies, its direct correspondence to physiological or exercise-induced levels in humans remains uncertain, underscoring the need for subsequent in vivo and translational validation. Third, the detailed functional and cachexia assays were performed primarily in C2C12-based engineered muscle tissues, with human skeletal myotubes used to confirm that key DEX- and irisin-induced gene expression changes are conserved in human cells; future studies will be needed to extend the full mechanical and biochemical characterization to human-only muscle constructs.”

In conclusion, this study demonstrates the relevance of engineered skeletal and cardiac tissue models for dissecting myokine-mediated mechanisms and therapeutic responses. The established microphysiological platforms enabled functional evaluation of irisin in DEX- and cancer cachexia-induced muscle atrophy and DEX-induced cardiotoxicity. The demonstrated anabolic, anti-catabolic, and cardioprotective effects of irisin highlight the potential of irisin mediated signaling as a protective mechanism against catabolic stress in muscle and cardiac tissues. Future applications of these models may accelerate the development of exercise-mimetic interventions that harness myokine signaling to treat muscle and cardiac disorders.
