Linear systems, spectral curves and determinants
Gordon Blower, Ian Doust

Abstract
. Let ( − A , B , C ) (-A,B,C) be a continuous time linear system with state space a separable complex Hilbert space H H , where − A -A generates a strongly continuous contraction semigroup ( e − t ​ A ) t ≥ 0 (e^{-tA})_{t\geq 0} on H H , and ϕ ⁡ ( t ) = C ​ e − t ​ A ​ B \phi(t)=Ce^{-tA}B is the impulse response function. Associated to such a system is a Hankel integral operator Γ ϕ \Gamma_{\phi} acting on L 2 ​ ( ( 0 , ∞ ) , ℂ ) L^{2}((0,\infty);{\mathbb{C}}) and a Schrödinger operator whose potential is found via a Fredholm determinant by the Faddeev-Dyson formula. Fredholm determinants of products of Hankel operators also play an important role in the Tracy and Widom’s theory of matrix models and asymptotic eigenvalue distributions of random matrices. This paper provide formulas for the Fredholm determinants which arise thus, and determines consequent properties of the associated differential operators. We prove a spectral theorem for self-adjoint linear systems that have scalar input and output: the entries of Kodaira’s characteristic matrix are given explicitly with formulas involving the infinitesimal Darboux addition for ( − A , B , C ) (-A,B,C) . Under suitable conditions on ( − A , B , C ) (-A,B,C) we give an explicit version of Burchnall-Chaundy’s theorem, showing that the algebra generated by an associated family of differential operators is isomorphic to an algebra of functions on a particular hyperelliptic curve.

L^{2}((0,\infty);{\mathbb{C}})
that is invariant under the adjoint shift semigroup
(S_{t}^{\dagger})_{t>0}
, so
Y_{\phi}^{\perp}=L^{2}\ominus Y_{\phi}
is invariant under the shift semigroup
(S_{t})_{t>0}
. The space
Y_{\phi}^{\perp}
is the null space of
\Gamma_{\phi}^{\dagger}
since

\displaystyle h\in Y_{\phi}^{\perp}

\displaystyle\iff\int_{0}^{\infty}\phi(x+y)^{\dagger}h(y)\,dy=0\qquad(x>0)

\displaystyle\iff\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\overline{\hat{\phi}_{(x)}(iy)}{\hat{h}(iy)}\,{\frac{dy}{\pi i}}=0\qquad(x>0).

Hankel integral operators
\Gamma
on
L^{2}((0,\infty);{\mathbb{C}})
are characterized by the intertwining relation
S_{t}^{\dagger}\Gamma=\Gamma S_{t}
for all
t>0
. Pöppe
[
43
]
called such operators additive in view of
\phi(x+t)
in their kernels (
2.2
). The notion of semi-additivity is related to products of Hankel operators, as in Proposition
2.8
below.

For ease of notation we shall write
\mathcal{L}^{2}
for
{\mathcal{L}}^{2}(L^{2}((0,\infty);{\mathbb{C}}))
in the lemma below.
For
t>0
define
\sigma_{t}:\mathcal{L}^{2}\to\mathcal{L}^{2}
by
\sigma_{t}(K)=S_{t}^{\dagger}KS_{t}
. A small calculation shows that if
K\in\mathcal{L}^{2}
has kernel

k(x,y)\in L^{2}((0,\infty)\times(0,\infty);{\mathbb{C}})
then
\sigma_{t}(K)=S_{t}^{\dagger}KS_{t}
is the integral operator with kernel
k(x+t,y+t)
. Let
\tilde{\sigma}_{t}(K)=S_{t}KS_{t}^{\dagger}
.

Proposition 2.8
.

Let
(\sigma_{t})_{t>0}
and
(\tilde{\sigma}_{t})_{t>0}
be as above.
Suppose that
K,L\in\mathcal{L}^{2}
. Then

(i)

(\sigma_{t})_{t>0}
is a strongly continuous contraction semigroup on
\mathcal{L}^{2}
.

(ii)

\sigma_{t}(K)\rightarrow 0
in the strong operator topology as
t\rightarrow\infty
.

(iii)

the infinitesimal generator of
(\sigma_{t})_{t>0}
is the diagonal derivative
\partial_{\Delta}
.

(iv)

(\tilde{\sigma}_{t})_{t>0}
is a strongly continuous contraction semigroup on
\mathcal{L}^{2}
.

(v)

\tilde{\sigma}_{t}(KL)=\tilde{\sigma}_{t}(K)\tilde{\sigma}_{t}(L)
and
\langle\tilde{\sigma}_{t}(K),L\rangle=\langle K,\sigma_{t}(L)\rangle
.

(vi)

If
K
is a product of vector-valued Hankel operators, then the kernel
\partial_{\Delta}k_{t}(x,y)
is of finite rank.

Proof.

(i)

This is straightforward. Note that

\|S_{t}^{\dagger}KS_{t}\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2}}\leq\|{S_{t}^{\dagger}}\|\|K\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2}}\|{S_{t}}\|=\|K\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2}}
.

(ii)

We have

(2.8)

\|\sigma_{t}(K)\|^{2}_{\mathcal{L}^{2}}=\iint_{(0,\infty)\times(0,\infty)}|k(x+t,y+t)|^{2}\,dxdy

which converges to
0
as
t\rightarrow\infty
.

(iii)

For suitable differentiable
k
the operator
d\sigma_{t}(K)/dt
has kernel
({\frac{\partial}{\partial x}}+{\frac{\partial}{\partial y}})k(x+t,y+t)
, so
\partial_{\Delta}
is the diagonal derivative.

(iv)

As in (ii), we have

(2.9)

\|K-\sigma_{t}(K)\|^{2}_{\mathcal{L}^{2}}=\iint_{(0,\infty)\times(0,\infty)}|k(x,y)-k(x+t,y+t)|^{2}\,dxdy

which converges to
0
as
t\rightarrow 0+
.

(v)

For the first identity we have that since
S_{t}^{\dagger}S_{t}=I
,

\tilde{\sigma}_{t}(KL)=S_{t}KLS_{t}^{\dagger}=S_{t}KS_{t}^{\dagger}S_{t}LS_{t}^{\dagger}=\tilde{\sigma}_{t}(K)\tilde{\sigma}_{t}(L).

For the second,

\mathop{\mathrm{trace}}(\tilde{\sigma}_{t}(K)L^{\dagger})=\mathop{\mathrm{trace}}(S_{t}KS_{t}^{\dagger}L^{\dagger})=\mathop{\mathrm{trace}}(KS_{t}^{\dagger}L^{\dagger}S_{t})=\mathop{\mathrm{trace}}(K\sigma_{t}(L)^{\dagger}).

(vi)

Suppose that
\phi,\psi\in L^{2}((0,\infty);{\mathbb{C}}^{n})
with
\phi
and
\psi
absolutely continuous; let
v^{\top}
be the transpose of a column vector
v
. Then by integration by parts, we find

\partial_{\Delta}\int_{0}^{\infty}\phi(x+z)^{\top}\psi(z+y)\,dz=-\phi(x)^{\top}\psi(y).\qed

Definition 3.3

Suppose that

(i)

(ii)

(iii)

Let

(iv)

Let

In what follows, we shall consider the primary variable to be the spectral parameter
\lambda

-h(x;\lambda)\end{bmatrix}

Proposition 3.4
.

Suppose that
\Omega(x;\lambda)=\lambda\Omega_{1}(x)+\Omega_{0}(x)

\Omega_{0},\Omega_{1}
real symmetric and
\Omega_{1}(x)\geq 0
. Suppose that
\Psi(x;\lambda)

3.8

(i)

\|\Psi(x;\kappa)\|^{2}{\frac{d\varphi}{d\kappa}}=\int_{0}^{x}\Psi(y;\kappa)^{\top}\Omega_{1}(y)\Psi(y;\kappa)\,dy\qquad(\kappa\in{\mathbb{R}}).

(ii)

The function
\Theta=E^{*}/E
is bounded and holomorphic on the upper half plane and
\varphi(\kappa)=(1/2)\mathop{{\rm Im}}\log\Theta(\kappa)
.

(iii)

There is a bounded Hankel operator
\Gamma_{\Theta^{*}}:H^{2}\rightarrow\overline{H^{2}}:

.

(iv)

Let

Then

(3.10)

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}{\frac{|F(t)|^{2}}{|E(t)|^{2}}}{\frac{dt}{\pi}}\geq\sum_{j=-\infty}^{\infty}{\frac{|F(\kappa_{j})|^{2}}{|E(\kappa_{j})|^{2}\varphi^{\prime}(\kappa_{j})}}\qquad(F\in K(E)).

(v)

Suppose that the limits

Then
\Gamma_{\Theta^{*}}

gives a compact Hankel operator such that

is a complex vector space of dimension equal to the winding number about
0
of
\Theta:[-\infty,\infty]\rightarrow{\mathbb{C}}
.

Proof.

(i) We verify that we have the conditions of Definition
3.3
. Simple calculations show that

(3.11)

|E(\lambda)|^{2}-|E(\bar{\lambda})|^{2}=4\mathop{{\rm Im}}\bigl(h(x;\lambda)f(x;\bar{\lambda})\bigr)={\frac{2}{i}}\Psi(x;\lambda)^{\dagger}J\Psi(x;\lambda).

By hypothesis,
\Psi(0;\lambda)
is real. Then
\Psi(0;\lambda)^{\dagger}J\Psi(0;\lambda)=0
, so for

(3.12)

\displaystyle{\frac{\Psi(x;\lambda)^{\dagger}J\Psi(x;\lambda)}{2i\varepsilon}}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{x}\Bigl({\frac{d}{dy}}{\frac{\Psi(y;\lambda)^{\dagger}J\Psi(y;\lambda)}{2i\varepsilon}}\Bigr)\,dy

(3.13)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{x}\Psi(y;\lambda)^{\dagger}\Bigl({\frac{\Omega(x;\lambda)-\Omega(x;\lambda)^{\dagger}}{2\varepsilon i}}\Bigr)\Psi(y;\lambda)\,dy

(3.14)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{x}\Psi(y;\lambda)^{\dagger}\Omega_{1}(y)\Psi(y;\lambda)\,dy,

We deduce that

for all

\displaystyle W(\kappa,\kappa)

\displaystyle=\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0+}{\frac{|E(\kappa+i\varepsilon)|^{2}-|E(\kappa-i\varepsilon)|^{2}}{4\pi\varepsilon}}

\displaystyle={\frac{1}{\pi}}\int_{0}^{x}\Psi(y;\kappa)^{\dagger}\Omega_{1}(y)\Psi(y;\kappa)\,dy\qquad(\kappa\in{\mathbb{R}}).

We observe that for real initial conditions and

(ii) We follow

(iii) By Proposition

\begin{bmatrix}T_{\Theta^{*}}&0\\

(iv) As in

Proposition 3.7
.

Suppose that
\lambda\mapsto\Psi_{\lambda}
gives a homomorphic function
{\mathbb{C}}\setminus{\mathbb{R}}\rightarrow L^{2}((0,\infty),{\mathbb{C}}^{2\times 1})
such that
\overline{\Psi_{\lambda}(x)}=\Psi_{\bar{\lambda}}(x)
and

\Psi(0;\lambda)=[a(\lambda),b(\lambda)]^{\top}.

Then there exists a reproducing kernel Hilbert space of holomorphic functions on
{\mathbb{C}}\setminus{\mathbb{R}}
with kernel

(3.20)

\Lambda(\lambda,\nu)={\frac{\bar{a}(\nu)b(\lambda)-a(\lambda)\bar{b}(\nu)}{\lambda-\bar{\nu}}}.

Proof.

By integrating the differential equation (

(3.21)

\displaystyle(\bar{\nu}-\lambda)\int_{0}^{x}\Psi(y;\nu)^{\dagger}\Omega_{1}(y)\Psi(y;\lambda)\,dy

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{x}{\frac{d}{dy}}\Bigl(\Psi(y;\nu)^{\dagger}J\Psi(y;\lambda)\Bigr)\,dy

(3.22)

\displaystyle=\Psi(x;\nu)^{\dagger}J\Psi(x;\lambda)-\Psi(0;\nu)^{\dagger}J\Psi(0;\lambda).

Then taking

(3.23)

\Lambda(\lambda,\nu)={\frac{\bar{a}(\nu)b(\lambda)-a(\lambda)\bar{b}(\nu)}{\lambda-\bar{\nu}}}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\Psi(x;\nu)^{\dagger}\Omega_{1}(x)\Psi(x;\lambda)\,dx.

We observe that
\Lambda(\lambda,\nu)=\overline{\Lambda(\nu,\lambda)}

\lambda\mapsto\Lambda(\lambda,\nu)

(ii) For
\Omega_{0}(x)=0

For differential equations on

(iv)
If the spectrum is absolutely continuous, then the density of states is determined by the diagonal of

at the Lebesgue points of the spectrum. As discussed in

Definition 4.1

Theorem 5.2

Let

We consider the spectral curve of the corresponding Schrödinger operator, and identify conditions under which the spectral curve is a hyperelliptic curve. In this case, the Schrödinger’s equation is integrable, in the sense that one can integrate

Let

Then

Definition 8.1
.

Let

Proposition 8.2
