Antigen exposure reshapes chromatin architecture in central memory CD8+ T cells and imprints enhanced recall capacity
Authors: Shaoqi Zhu, Jia Liu, Vanita Patel, Xiuyi Zhao, Weiqun Peng, Hai-Hui Xue
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
SignificanceMemory CD8+ T cells mount more rapid and robust responses to antigens than naive cells, constituting immunological basis for vaccination; however, the underlying mechanisms remain insufficiently understood. Here, we demonstrate that memory CD8+ T cells acquire de novo binding sites of the CTCF (CCCTC-binding factor) protein in their genome, where CTCF functions in two critical capacities: one is to act as a transcription cofactor to increase activities of local regulatory elements such as promoters and enhancers, and the other is to organize these elements into architectural proximity in the three-dimensional space. Our findings suggest that these CTCF-mediated actions prepare memory CD8+ T cells for heightened responsiveness to antigen restimulation, which may be explored as a therapeutic target to achieve enhanced antiviral/tumor immunity.

Formation of immunological memory is a cardinal feature of adaptive immunity and constitutes the underlying rationale for vaccines against infectious agents and tumor antigens (1, 2). In the CD8+ T cell lineage, memory CD8+ T (TM) cells persist for decades after initial encounter with pathogens via acute viral infection or live attenuated vaccine, being maintained at a quiescent state through slow turnover (3, 4). Upon re-encountering the same pathogen, TM cells respond by rapid proliferation and accelerated differentiation into cytotoxic effector cells, providing more heightened protection than naive CD8+ T (TN) cells (5, 6). Among various subsets of TM cells recognized thus far, CD62L– effector memory CD8+ T (TEM) cells are largely detected in circulation, while CD62L+ central memory CD8+ T (TCM) cells reside in lymphoid organs, persist for longer duration, and have more robust capacity of proliferation during the recall response (6, 7). Importantly, TEM cells have been demonstrated to convert into TCM cells through an in vivo maturation process over time (8). Compared with TN cells, TCM cells exhibit distinct transcriptomic profiles, which are associated with extensive changes in chromatin accessibility and epigenetic modifications of histones and genomic DNA per se (9–14). However, it remains unclear how these molecular changes at the DNA element level are integrated into the scheme of three-dimensional (3D) chromatin architecture in TCM cells, and if the higher-order genome organization has a role in controlling enhanced recall capacity by TCM cells (15–17).

As detected with high-throughput chromosome conformation capture assay (Hi-C), a cell genome is organized into self-associating structures called topologically associating domains (TADs), which usually span megabases in size (18, 19). Whereas such large TAD structures appear to be stably conserved among different cell types, more dynamic changes are observed at sub-TADs, which are nested within the TADs, and at specific chromatin loops during cell differentiation (20–22). The ubiquitously expressed CCCTC-binding factor (CTCF) is an essential genome organizer that establishes the TAD/sub-TAD structures and mediates long-range chromatin interactions (ChrInt) (23, 24). Recent studies revealed that CTCF cooperates closely with lineage-restricted transcription factors such as Tcf1, Batf, Runx3, and Tbet in T cells to provide constant supervision of CD8+ T cell identity, support homeostatic proliferation of TN cells, and promote differentiation of effector CD4+ and CD8+ T cells in response to viral or bacterial infections (25–30). Remarkably, CTCF acquires de novo binding sites in CD8+ T cells in response to homeostatic cytokines and TCR stimulation (25–27), and the redistributed CTCF binding sites forge new ChrInt among themselves and with existing invariant CTCF binding locations. It is also recognized that the chromatin loops not only directly connect promoters and enhancers but also span target genes to enclose the gene promoters and enhancers within architectural proximity and hence facilitate their interactions (12, 22, 31). Notably, application of a newly developed HiCHub algorithm (32) demonstrates that unidirectional ChrInt changes between two cell types/states frequently occur in aggregation, forming ChrInt hubs that are beyond single chromatin loops yet operative on an intermediate scale within TADs. The ChrInt hubs are enriched with redistributed, dynamic CTCF binding sites and show stronger connections with gene expression changes, as manifested in CD8+ T cell homeostasis and effector CD8+ T (TEFF) cell differentiation (25, 26).

Considering the dynamic nature of CTCF binding and chromatin architecture in antigen-responding CD8+ T cells, one outstanding question is whether TEFF and TCM cells share similar or have distinctive chromatin structural features. By applying the HiCHub algorithm to achieve multiple cell state comparisons, we found that a substantial portion of TEFF-acquired CTCF binding and ChrInt hubs was also observed in TCM cells, and these conserved changes constituted architectural basis for the distinct gene expression profile in TCM cells. Importantly, the CTCF binding sites and/or ChrInt hubs that were acquired in TCM over TN cells did not always cause expression changes in resting TCM cells established from the primary response, but showed critical contribution to regulating the magnitude of gene induction by recall stimulation. The CTCF-dependent, recall-induced genes encompassed broad biological activities including immune regulation, energy supply, RNA transcription and splicing, and protein translation. Our findings collectively suggest that de novo acquisition of CTCF binding and ChrInt hubs serves as an essential means to program the enhanced recall capacity by TCM cells.

Results

TCM Cells Acquire De Novo ChrInt Hubs to Support Their Unique Transcriptomic Changes during Primary Response.

Antigen-experienced T cells undergo extensive transcriptomic and epigenetic changes compared with naive T cells (9–14), but the underlying chromatin architectural basis is largely unknown. Through the use of adoptive transfer of naive CD8+ T cells expressing P14 transgenic TCR, which is specific for the glycoprotein 33 to 41 (GP33) epitope of lymphocytic choriomeningitis virus (LCMV), coupled with LCMV-Armstrong (LCMV-Arm) infection (Fig. 1A), we recently demonstrated extensive genome reorganization in KLRG1hiIL-7Ra– TEFF cells isolated on the 8th day postinfection (dpi), compared with TN cells (26, 28). It remains unknown whether TCM cells have similar or distinctive chromatin architectural changes. We previously mapped genome organization in TCM cells using the same in situ Hi-C approach as in TN and TEFF cells (31). These Hi-C data formed distinct clusters in TN, TEFF, and TCM cells (Fig. 1B). Quantitative comparison of ChrInt in more than two cell types/states has been challenging in the field. We recently developed the network-based HiCHub algorithm (32), which identifies cell type/state-specific ChrInt hubs that contain large patches of chromatin contacts with collective unidirectional ChrInt changes in one cell type/state over the other, going beyond punctate chromatin loops between two anchors (SI Appendix, Fig. S1A). By applying HiCHub to each pairwise comparison among TN, TEFF, and TCM cells, cell state-specific ChrInt hubs were identified, with TN and TEFF cells showing the most robust ChrInt differences (Fig. 1C). By applying K-means clustering analysis to all cell state-specific ChrInt hubs, a total of eight hub clusters were identified (Fig. 1D). ChrInt hubs in clusters 1-4 (Hub_C1-4) were acquired in TEFF over TN cells, and those in Hub_C1 were sustained and even stronger in TCM cells (Fig. 1 D and E). ChrInt hubs in Hub_C2 and Hub_C3 were attenuated to different degrees, while those in Hub_C4 were completely lost in TCM cells (Fig. 1 D and E). On the other hand, ChrInt hubs in clusters 5-8 (Hub_C5-8) were lost in TEFF compared to TN cells, and those in Hub_C5 and Hub_C6 remained decommissioned in TCM cells, while those in Hub_C7 and Hub_C8 were partly and almost completely restored in TCM cells, respectively (Fig. 1 D and E). These data demonstrate that CD8+ T cells undergo extensive chromatin architectural rewiring during the primary response, and the experience of antigen encounter poses a durable imprinting effect on TCM cells on the level of 3D genome reorganization.

When ChrInts are analyzed at the TAD level or focal chromatin loops, there is often a disconnect between ChrInt and gene expression changes. We next investigated if and how ChrInt hub dynamics related to transcriptomic changes. We previously used RNA-seq to analyze expression profiles in TN, TEFF, and TCM cells under the same experimental setting as in Fig. 1A (10). By criteria of ≥twofold changes and FDR < 0.05, pairwise comparison among the three cell states identified 4,735 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) (SI Appendix, Fig. S1B and Dataset S1). These DEGs were resolved into 12 expression clusters, and the DEG patterns largely mirrored the ChrInt hub dynamics (Fig. 1F). By stratifying DEGs with hub-covered anchor regions, concordant changes in ChrInt and gene expression were observed (Fig. 1G and SI Appendix, Fig. S1C). The strongest correlation was observed at genes induced in antigen-experienced TEFF and TCM cells, i.e., between expression clusters 1-4 (ExpC1-3) and Hub_C1-3 (Fig. 1G and Dataset S2). Specifically, Hub_C1 harbored key cytokines/chemokines and their receptors, such as Ifng (ExpC1) and Il12rb2 (IL-12 receptor b2, ExpC2), as well as Ccl5 (ExpC5) (Fig. 2 A and B). Hub_C2 encompassed several critical transcription regulators including Id2 (ExpC3), Tbx21, Bhlhe40 and Prdm1 (all in ExpC4), as well as adhesion molecules such as Itga1 (ExpC3) and Itgb1 (ExpC2) (Fig. 2 C and D and SI Appendix, Fig. S2 A and B). These ChrInt hubs (in Hub_C1 and C2) gained strength in TEFF compared to TN cells, and the strengthened ChrInt was also robust in TCM cells. Additionally, Hub_C3 was associated with genes with important immune functions such as Il2rb (IL-2 receptor b chain, ExpC2), Icos (inducible costimulator, ExpC3), and Pfkp (phosphofructokinase, a critical glycolytic enzyme, ExpC4) (Fig. 2 E and F), where ChrInt showed modest yet discernable gain in TEFF and TCM over TN cells. Notably, ExpC4 gene expression (such as Tbx21, Bhlhe40, and Prdm1) was substantially attenuated in TCM compared to TEFF cells (Fig. 1F), yet their associated hubs retained robust ChrInt strength in both TEFF and TCM cells (Fig. 2 C and D and SI Appendix, Fig. S2 A and B), suggesting that antigen exposure resulted in sustained rewiring of ChrInts, and such changes led chromatin to an architecturally poised state in TCM cells to facilitate their enhanced recall capacity.

On the other hand, a strong correlation was also observed at genes that were down-regulated in TEFF but exhibited varied expression in TCM cells, as observed between Hub_C6-8 and ExpC 9-12 (Fig. 1G and SI Appendix, Fig. S1C). ChrInt in Hub_C6 was greatly weakened in both TEFF and TCM cells, as observed at the Lef1 gene (ExpC8), which was silenced in antigen-experienced cells (SI Appendix, Fig. S2C). In contrast, ChrInt in Hub_C8 was diminished in TEFF but strongly restored in TCM cells (Fig. 1E), and their linked targets, including TCM-characteristic genes such as Il7r (encoding IL-7Ra, ExpC12) and Bcl2 (ExpC12), manifested similar expression pattern (SI Appendix, Fig. S2C). Additionally, Hub_C7 was associated with transcription factors Foxo1 (ExpC9) and Foxp1 (ExpC10), and telomerase reverse transcriptase Tert (ExpC11), which showed concordant partial restoration of expression and ChrInt in TCM cells after reduction in TEFF cells (SI Appendix, Fig. S2D). These observations collectively demonstrate that ChrInt on the hub scale shows robust correlation with gene expression profiles during effector and memory CD8+ T cell differentiation, and hence, the ChrInt rewiring constitutes architectural basis for dynamic transcriptomic changes during primary CD8+ T cell responses.

TCM Cells Acquire De Novo CTCF Binding to Support ChrInt and Transcriptomic Changes during Primary Response.

We recently demonstrated that CTCF is redistributed in genomic binding locations, which is accompanied by concordant chromatin architectural changes during TN to early TEFF (4 dpi) differentiation (26). It is unknown if these molecular features elicited by experiencing antigens are sustained in TM cells. To address this, TEFF (8 dpi) and TCM cells were analyzed for CTCF binding profile with CUT&RUN, which constituted distinct clusters distinct from that in TN cells (Fig. 3A). By focusing on CTCF binding sites within the ChrInt hub_C1-C3, the CTCF binding strength was evidently elevated in TEFF and TCM cells compared with TN cells, while it was lower in TCM than TEFF cells (Fig. 3B). These concordant changes in CTCF binding and ChrInt strength were consistent with the known role of CTCF in mediating genome reorganization during effector CD8+ T cell differentiation (26, 27). Pairwise comparisons among the three cell states identified a total of 15,123 differential CTCF binding sites, with TEFF and TCM cells showing strong diversification of CTCF binding from TN cells (Fig. 3C). These differential CTCF binding sites were resolved into 6 distinct clusters, where Ctcf_C1-3 clusters showed increased CTCF binding strength, while Ctcf_C4-6 were diminished in CTCF binding strength in TEFF and/or TCM cells (Fig. 3D and SI Appendix, Fig. S3A). Stratifying CTCF binding and expression clusters showed that CTCF binding sites acquired by TEFF and/or TCM cells (Ctcf_C2-3) were more frequently associated with induced genes in these cells (ExpC1-C5), whereas those attenuated in TEFF but partly restored in TCM cells (Ctcf_C6) were linked with genes with similar dynamic patterns in ExpC8-12 (Fig. 3 E and F and Dataset S3). Ctcf1_C1 sites uniquely gained binding strength in TCM cells and showed a broader association with multiple expression clusters (Fig. 3 D–F). These analyses demonstrate that CTCF clearly undergo genome-wide redistribution in TCM cells, showing shared and distinct binding sites and/or strength from TEFF cells, and these changes in CTCF occupancy may partly account for the transcriptomic dynamics during the primary response.

We then focused on Ctcf_C1-3 sites, which showed increased CTCF binding strength in TCM over TN cells. Over 40% of Ctcf_C1 sites were in gene promoters (defined as ±1 kb region flanking the transcription start site, TSS) (Fig. 3G). The Ctcf_C1 sites showed modest yet discernible increase in chromatin accessibility (ChrAcc) profile [as previously determined with ATAC-seq (26, 31)] and H3K4me3 [as previously determined with ChIP-seq (10)] in TCM over TN cells (Fig. 3H). For example, Tnf (ExpC3) and Fosb (ExpC1) promoters acquired stronger CTCF binding and elevated H3K4me3 in TCM cells (Fig. 4 A and B). These data suggest that the gene promoters with TCM-acquired CTCF binding sites were in a more permissive state for active transcription. On the other hand, ~80% of Ctcf_C2 and C3 sites were in distal regulatory regions (Fig. 3G) and showed a more evident increase in ChrAcc (26, 31) and H3K27ac [previously determined with ChIP-seq (10)] in TCM compared to TN cells (Fig. 3H). Specifically, multiple Ctcf_C2-3 sites were found in upstream regulatory regions and introns of Tnf, Il2rb (ExpC2), and Tbx21 (ExpC4) (Fig. 4A); Ctcf_C2 sites were observed upstream of Fosb and intron of Bcl21l (encoding Bcl-XL, ExpC2) (Fig. 4B), and these sites were frequently associated with increased ChrAcc and H3K27ac in TCM cells. Additionally, non-promoter-associating Ctcf_C1 sites, as observed downstream of Tbx21 (Fig. 4A) or upstream of Fosb (Fig. 4B), exhibited elevated ChrAcc and H3K27ac signals. These observations suggest that acquisition of increased CTCF binding in distal regulatory regions is linked to increased enhancer activity and elevated expression of associated genes in TCM cells.

These analyses also demonstrated that TCM-acquired CTCF binding sites in different clusters (i.e., Ctcf_C1-3) were frequently utilized in various combinations to achieve target gene regulation and that depending on their differential distribution in promoters vs. distal regions, they were associated with distinct molecular features such as ChrAcc and histone modifications. We therefore regrouped these TCM-acquired CTCF binding sites into three subsets, 1) promoter, 2) distal without CTCF motifs, and 3) distal with CTCF motifs for downstream analyses. De novo motif analyses showed that the first two subsets were enriched in Ets and AP1 motifs (Fig. 4 C and D), and the enrichment of AP1 motif suggested that CTCF likely gained access to ChrAcc sites opened by TCR-mobilized AP1 TFs in antigen-experienced cells. The CTCF sites in distal regulatory regions with CTCF motifs had CTCF as the top motif as expected (Fig. 4E), while those without CTCF motifs were enriched in Runx and Tbet motifs (Fig. 4D), consistent with Tbet and Runx3-dependent recruitment of CTCF as observed in TEFF cells and known Tbet-CTCF cooperativity in Th1 cells (26, 27). These analyses suggested that CTCF could engage a broad range of TFs at both promoters and enhancers to function as transcriptional cofactors to facilitate target gene transcription. Pile-up analysis of ChrInt centered on these TCM-acquired CTCF binding sites showed that all these groups mediated stronger ChrInt in both TEFF and TCM cells compared to TN cells (Fig. 4 F, Top three panels); this was in contrast to the sparse ChrInt centering on motif+ CTCF binding sites that were invariant among three cell states where they function as insulators (Fig. 4 F, Bottom). Consistently, Ctcf_C1-3 binding sites were highly prevalent in ChrInt hubs in hub_C1–C3 clusters (Fig. 2 A, C, and E and SI Appendix, Fig. S2A). Focusing on ExpC1–4 genes which were expressed at higher levels in TCM than TN cells (Fig. 1F), >30% of these genes were associated with TCM-acquired ChrInt hubs (in Hub_C1–3, Fig. 1D), and >50% of them were associated with TCM-acquired CTCF binding sites (in Ctcf_C1–3, Fig. 3D) (Fig. 4G). These data demonstrate that a large proportion (>60%) of genes with elevated expression in TCM over TN cells acquired novel CTCF binding sites and/or chromatin architectural changes during the primary CD8+ T cell responses to acute infection. We did note that many genes were solely associated with increased CTCF binding only, suggesting that not all TCM-acquired CTCF binding sites mounted to strengthened ChrInt hubs in TCM cells, such as those associated with Fosb and Bcl2l1 (Fig. 4B), where they acted locally through promoters and/or enhancers.

CTCF Is Essential for Recall Response by TCM Cells.

Acquisition of CTCF binding sites in TCM cells suggested pivotal contributions by CTCF to memory T cell biology, and one major biological function of TCM cells is to provide enhanced recall responses when encountering the same antigen. Genetic ablation of CTCF after CD8+ T cell activation severely impairs late-stage effector differentiation during the primary response, with virtually no CTCF-deficient memory T cells formed (26). TM cells are generated from CTCF knock-down CD8+ T cells via short-hairpin RNA and show diminished expansion upon rechallenge (27); this phenomenon is at least partly secondary to impaired primary effector responses, and the underlying mechanisms remain unknown. To specifically assess the requirements for CTCF in the recall capacity by TCM cells, we generated P14+CreER+Ctcf FL/FL mice, where the use of CreER allows inducible deletion of CTCF by tamoxifen treatment. We then performed adoptive transfer of untreated CreER+ WT and CreER+Ctcf FL/FL P14 cells followed by LCMV-Arm infection to allow unperturbed formation of TCM cells (Fig. 5A). At ≥30 dpi, the immune mice were treated with tamoxifen on two consecutive days. The treatment diminished CTCF protein by ~80% in sorted CreER+Ctcf FL/FL TCM cells as determined with immunoblotting (Fig. 5B), and the CreER+Ctcf FL/FL TCM cells were termed CTCF iKO cells herein. To capture the earliest impact of CTCF deficiency on TCM-mediated recall responses, we stimulated sort-purified P14 WT or CTCF iKO TCM cells with GP33 peptide for 24 h, which was sufficient to induce TCM but not TN cells to produce IFN-g (31, 33). After GP33 stimulation, CTCF iKO TCM cells showed similar capacity of producing IFN-g and TNF as WT cells (Fig. 5 C, Top), but were evidently smaller in size and showed impaired induction of CD69 and CD25 (Fig. 5 C, Bottom). In a parallel study, the sorted WT or CTCF iKO TCM cells were adoptively transferred into another cohort of B6.SJL mice followed by a secondary challenge with Listeria monocytogenes expressing GP33 (LM-GP33) (SI Appendix, Fig. S3B). While WT TCM cells underwent substantial secondary expansion on day 3 postinfection with LM-GP33, the secondary effector cells derived from CTCF iKO TCM cells failed to accumulate (SI Appendix, Fig. S3C), and the residual CTCF iKO P14 cells mostly escaped CTCF deletion. These data thus demonstrate that CTCF is indispensable for recall response by TCM cells.

As detailed above, TCM-acquired ChrInt hubs and de novo CTCF binding showed concordant changes with the gene expression dynamics during the primary response; however, some of these TCM-acquired molecular features were also found at genes in the ExpC5 cluster (such as Ccl6, Ccl9, Ccna2 and Ccnd1, which were similarly expressed in TN and TCM cells, in spite of strong induction in TEFF cells, Fig. 1F and Dataset S1), and at genes that showed relatively stable expression during the primary response (i.e., those did not meet the criteria for inclusion in ExpC1-12). We hypothesize that the antigen exposure leaves imprints in TCM cells in the form of newly acquired CTCF binding sites and/or ChrInt hubs to program their enhanced recall capacity. We therefore performed RNA-seq analysis on resting WT TCM and GP33-stimulated WT and CTCF iKO TCM cells, which formed distinct clusters on PCA (principal component analysis) (Fig. 5D). By the criteria of ≥twofold changes and FDR < 0.05, 2,199 genes were induced in GP33-stimulated WT TCM cells compared to resting WT TCM cells (Fig. 5 E, Left). Among the recall-induced genes, 846 genes showed diminished induction in GP33-stimulated CTCF iKO TCM cells, exhibiting ≥1.5-fold reduction compared to GP33-stimulated WT TCM cells (Fig. 5 E, Right), and were termed CTCF-dependent, recall-induced genes herein (Dataset S4). Functional annotation with the DAVID bioinformatics resources demonstrated that the CTCF-dependent, recall-induced genes were strongly enriched in functions of energy supply (mitochondrion) and macromolecule synthesis (translation, RNA polymerases, mRNA processing, and DNA replication) (Fig. 5F), which were all necessary for rapid proliferation of antigen-specific cells (34, 35). Specifically, these CTCF-dependent genes included those encoding eukaryotic translation initiation factors (eIFs, such as Eif1a and Eif3b), RNA polymerases in all three types that synthesize ribosome, messenger, and transfer RNAs (such as Polr1a, Polr2d, and Polr3d), and splicing factors that process pre-mRNA (such as Sf1 and Sf3a1) (Fig. 5G). Although enrichment scores were not ranked high, key cell cycle genes (such as Ccnd2 and Cdk4, encoding cyclin D2 and cyclin-dependent kinase 4, respectively) and glycolysis genes (such as Aldoa and Pgma1, encoding fructose-biphosphate aldolase A and phosphoglycerate mutase, respectively) depended on CTCF for sufficient induction by recall stimulation in TCM cells (Fig. 5G). These findings are consistent with known requirements for CTCF in cell proliferation (36) and further reveal the specific target genes/pathways controlled by CTCF in recall-stimulated TCM cells. Importantly, “immune system process”, “transcription regulation”, and “methyltransferases” were also highly enriched in CTCF-dependent, recall-induced genes, and these include cytokines and chemokines (such as Il2 and Xcl1), costimulatory receptor (Icos), kinases (Zap70), and prosurvival factors (Bcl2l1) (Fig. 5G). The CTCF-dependent transcriptional regulators included well-documented factors that control immune regulations, including Myc, Id3, Irf4, Bach2, factors in the early growth response factor family (Egr1 to 4), nuclear receptor subfamily 4 group A (Nr4a1 to 3), and NFkB family (Nfkb2, Rel, Rela, and Relb) (37–44) (Fig. 5G). These data demonstrate that CTCF has critical contributions to immune regulation in TCM cells, beyond its general requirements in basic cellular functions such as proliferation and survival.

We then investigated the connection between chromatin architecture and TCM recall capacity. Among all the recall-induced genes, ~20% of these genes were in ChrInt HubC1-3 which were acquired in TCM over TN cells, and >50% genes acquired stronger CTCF binding (in Ctcf_C1-3 clusters) in TCM over TN cells (Fig. 6A). The CTCF-dependent, recall-induced genes showed a similar ratio of association with TCM-acquired ChrInt hubs and/or CTCF binding (Fig. 6B). Interestingly, about 40% of these hub/CTCF-linked, recall-induced genes showed dynamic expression changes during primary response (Fig. 6C and Datasets S5 and S6). These analyses demonstrated that TCM cells acquired stronger ChrInt and/or CTCF binding at a substantial portion of gene loci, which did not immediately affect gene expression during TCM formation in the primary response, but was necessary for more potent induction in response to recall stimulation. For example, Traf6 and Dusp12 (encoding TNF receptor-associated factor 6 and dual specificity phosphatase 12, respectively) were not DEGs in the primary response but showed CTCF-dependent induction by recall stimulation, and these genes gained stronger ChrInt hubs in TCM over TN cells (Fig. 6D). On the other hand, Irf4 was in ExpC5 from primary responses, showing strong induction in TEFF cells but being reverted to similar expression in TCM as in TN cells (Fig. 1F); however, Irf4 was embedded in ChrInt Hub_C2 (Fig. 1D), partly retained increased ChrInt strength in TCM over TN cells (Fig. 6E), which may account for its CTCF-dependent induction in recall response (Fig. 5H).

In parallel, many recall-induced genes acquired de novo CTCF binding sites or gained CTCF binding strength in TCM compared to TN cells, but not all increased CTCF binding mounted to formation of long-range ChrInt hubs, suggesting that these CTCF binding events may have an impact on local regulatory elements. For example, several genes encoding eIFs (Eif3b and Eif3d, Fig. 6F), RNA polymerases (Polr2d and Polr2f, Fig. 6G), and NFkB transcription factors (Nfkb2 and Rel, Fig. 6H) were not DEGs during primary response (SI Appendix, Fig. S3D) but showed CTCF-dependent induction in recall response (Fig. 5H); these gene promoters gained stronger CTCF binding in TCM over TN cells. Although the densely CTCF-occupied promoters showed similar ChrAcc and H3K27ac signals between TCM and TN cells, they were marked with elevated H3K4me3 signals in TCM cells (Fig. 6 F–H), likely accounting for their rapid induction by recall stimulation. On the other hand, some CTCF-dependent, recall-induced genes did show dynamic expression changes during the primary response such as Bcl21l (encoding Bcl-XL) (Fig. 4B), Icam1 (encoding intercellular adhesion molecule 1), and those encoding Egr and Nr4a family transcription factors (Egr3, Nr4a1-3, Fig. 6I and SI Appendix, Fig. S3E). These genes frequently acquired stronger CTCF binding in distal regulatory regions in TCM over TN cells, with a concomitant increase in ChrAcc and H3K27ac (Figs. 4B and 6I), consistent with increased enhancer activity. These data demonstrate that acquisition of increased CTCF binding in TCM cells mediates modifications of local chromatin states as well as long-range ChrInts and further suggest that these changes imprinted in TCM cells through experiencing antigen exposure help facilitate their enhanced responsiveness to recall stimulation.

To further substantiate CTCF-mediated imprinting effect on TCM cells, ATAC-seq was performed on WT and CTCF iKO TCM cells sort-purified from tamoxifen-treated immune mice (Fig. 5A and SI Appendix, Fig. S4A). Induced deletion of CTCF in resting TCM cells resulted in decreased ChrAcc at 2,095 genomic locations (SI Appendix, Fig. S4B). These CTCF-dependent ChrAcc sites were strongly associated genes in immune system process, “regulation of cell proliferation”, and “cell activation”, based on the Genomic Regions Enrichment of Annotations Tool analysis (45) (SI Appendix, Fig. S4C), as observed at the upstream regulatory regions of Id2 and Cx3cr1 as well as Cx3cr1 promoter (SI Appendix, Fig. S4D). Importantly, among the 846 CTCF-dependent, recall-induced genes (defined in Fig. 5E), 202 genes were linked to the CTCF-dependent ChrAcc sites in resting TCM cells, and functional annotation of these genes showed strong involvement in immune system process and “transcription”, besides “ribosome biogenesis” (SI Appendix, Fig. S4E). Among the CTCF-dependent, recall-induced transcription factors, Egr3, Rel, and Nr4a1 had CTCF-dependent ChrAcc sites in their upstream regulatory regions, while Bach2 had one in its intron (SI Appendix, Fig. S4F). With regard to CTCF-dependent, recall-induced immune regulatory genes, Il2, Tnfsf11 (encoding RANKL), and Atp1a1 (encoding Na+/K+ ATPase subunit α1) harbored multiple CTCF-dependent ChrAcc sites, as individual sites or in clusters, in their upstream regions, while Pdcd1 had several such sites at its TSS, upstream and intron regions (SI Appendix, Fig. S4G). These observations demonstrate that loss of CTCF profoundly affected the ChrAcc landscape in TCM cells at the resting state, and the resulting changes were linked to recall-induced genes, further corroborating an essential role of CTCF in priming TCM cells for the readiness of mounting enhanced recall responses.

Discussion

By systematic comparison of directional ChrInt changes among the antigen-specific CD8+ T cells at different response stages, we demonstrated that TEFF and TCM cells undergo extensive ChrInt rewiring, forming distinct ChrInt hubs from TN cells. A substantial portion of TEFF-acquired hubs were conserved in TCM cells, while among the TEFF-lost hubs, the majority of those appeared to be permanently decommissioned, with only a small fraction restored in TCM cells. As a result, TCM cells adopted a hybrid feature of TN and TEFF cells in terms of ChrInt hub formation. At the effector phase, antigen-specific CD8+ T cells are heterogeneous in phenotypes and memory potentials. KLRG1hiIL-7Ra– TEFF cells retain the capacity of differentiating into all memory T cell lineages including TEM and TCM cells, while the KLRG1loIL-7Ra+ memory precursor (TMP) cells show higher efficacy than TEFF cells in giving rise to TCM cells (46). Consistent with our findings (26), a recent Hi-C analysis shows that TEFF and TMP cells exhibit extensive changes in ChrInt compared with TN cells; however, these two cell types are remarkably similar in 3D genome organization (27). It is therefore plausible to postulate that the de novo ChrInt hubs detected in TCM cells compared with TN cells are “inherited” from TEFF and/or TMP cells in the effector phase, representing unique architectural features of antigen-experienced CD8+ T cells. Although TEM cells convert into TCM cells over time (8), it remains to be determined if TEM cells exhibit a transitory chromatin configuration between TEFF and TCM cells or have their unique chromatin architectural features.

The differential ChrInt hubs identified with the HiCHub analytical tool showed strong correlation with the gene expression dynamics during the primary response; it should be noted, however, that the correlation is not linear. For example, the Ccl genes were in ExpC5 (i.e., their transcription was substantially dampened in TCM cells after robust induction in TEFF cells), yet their underlying ChrInt hub was similarly strong in TEFF and TCM cells. Furthermore, many genes harbored within TCM-acquired ChrInt hubs did not show dynamic expression during the primary response (such as Traf6 and Dusp12), but were instead strongly induced during the recall response by TCM cells. These observations suggest that the ChrInt rewiring in TEFF and TCM cells could imprint the code of “recall readiness” during the initial pathogen encounter, besides helping orchestrate transcriptomic changes during the primary response. It is an equally interesting question if and how secondary effector CD8+ T cells, derived from recall stimulation, reconfigure their chromatin architecture compared with primary TEFF and the resting TCM cells. Our efforts in this direction were impeded by the paucity of murine TCM cells and further diminished numbers of viable cells after 24-h ex vivo stimulation. However, a most recent study comprehensively mapped 3D genome organization in human naive CD4+ and memory TH2 cells at resting state and after 24-h ex vivo TCR stimulation, demonstrating extensive changes in compartment scores, TAD organization, and ChrInt loop strength between naive and memory T cells (47). Significantly, a sizable fraction of the memory-specific TADs and chromatin loops was linked to recall-induced genes, such as MAF and IL9 (47). Collectively, these advances strongly support the notion that the reprogrammed 3D genome topology in memory CD4+ and CD8+ T cells creates permissive chromatin environment to facilitate rapid transcriptional induction in response to recall stimulation by their cognate antigens.

Consistent with its well-established function as a genome organizer, CTCF is dynamically redistributed in TEFF cells, accompanying ChrInt hub changes (26). Approximately 40% of TEFF-acquired CTCF binding sites were detected with similar binding strength in TCM cells, while ~60% of TEFF-lost sites mostly remained absent in TCM cells; this CTCF binding pattern was highly concordant with ChrInt hub and expression dynamics. An interesting discrepancy was observed in Ctcf_C1 binding sites, which were uniquely acquired in TCM, but not TEFF cells, whereas TCM cells did not have an obvious gain in ChrInt hubs that were absent in TN or TEFF cells. The emergence of TCM-specific CTCF binding sites is likely due to the continuous maturation/solidification of unique TCM molecular features driven by cytokines (48). In fact, exposure of TN cells to IL-7 and IL-15 did result in CTCF relocation and ChrInt changes during homeostatic proliferation (25). Additionally, over 40% of these TCM-specific CTCF binding sites were found at gene promoters, while the CTCF binding inherited from TEFF to TCM cells were mostly detected in distal regulatory regions. Similar to differential ChrInt hubs, the TCM-acquired CTCF binding sites partly accounted for gene expression dynamics during primary response, but the correlation was nonlinear either. Many genes controlling transcription, translation, and NF-kB signal pathway acquired de novo CTCF binding at their gene promoters without obvious induction in resting TCM cells, but these genes were induced by recall stimulation in a CTCF-dependent manner. These observations indicate that the acquisition of CTCF binding in TCM cells serves as another means of imprinting the code of recall readiness during the primary response. It is also noteworthy that not all TCM-acquired CTCF binding sites amount to changes in ChrInt hubs or short-range chromatin loops, but they are frequently associated with increased H3K4me3 at gene promoters and elevated H3K27ac in distal regulatory regions. In the latter cases, CTCF can act locally to modulate promoter and enhancer activities.

Despite the major contribution of CTCF to 3D genome organization, we observed that not all ChrInt hubs were associated with dynamic CTCF binding changes. Several transcription factors have been shown to mediate chromatin loop formation without involving CTCF, such as AP1 in human monocytes and differentiated macrophages (49) and Tbet in T helper 1 cells via homodimerization (50, 51). On the other hand, CTCF has been shown to cooperate with lineage-specific transcription factors in various immune cells to function as a transcriptional cofactor (12). For example, CTCF is recruited by Tcf1 to form ChrInt hubs to supervise TN cell identity and promote their homeostatic proliferation (25, 28), and by TCR-induced BATF (an AP1 family TF) to mediate chromatin loops to promote differentiation of T helper 17 cells (29). While we placed an emphasis on the dynamic changes in CTCF binding strength during CD8+ T cell differentiation, the critical contribution of invariant CTCF binding, which often functions in the capacity of insulation, should not be ignored. Genetic ablation of CTCF, as employed in this work, cannot distinguish the functional differences between dynamically redistributed and invariant CTCF binding; nonetheless, this approach did reveal specific requirements for CTCF in immune regulation and metabolic mobilization during TCM recall response, beyond its known roles in basic cellular functions such as proliferation and survival. Future efforts should be directed toward differentiating the transcription cofactor versus insulation effect controlled by CTCF during immune responses.

Collectively, our systematic approaches comparing multiple cell states during CD8+ T cell differentiation revealed that TCM cells exhibited extensive rewiring of ChrInt hubs and redistribution of CTCF binding after TN cells encounter their cognate antigen. Such changes served at least two major functions: one is to constitute chromatin architectural basis for transcriptomic dynamics during the primary response, and the other is to prepare TCM cells for robust recall responses to ensure rapid energy supply, RNA transcription and splicing, and protein translation. Our findings highlight the critical requirements for CTCF to activate local promoters/enhancers and organize genomic architecture in TCM cells, which may be explored as a therapeutic target to achieve enhanced antiviral/tumor immunity.

Materials and Methods

Mice.

C57BL/6J (B6), B6.SJL, Rosa26Cre-ERT2/+, and Tcra−/− mice were from the Jackson Laboratory. Ctcf FL/FL mice were kindly provided by N. Galjart (Erasmus University Medical Center, Rotterdam, Netherlands). All compound mouse strains used in this work were from in-house breeding at the animal care facility of Center for Discovery and Innovation, Hackensack University Medical Center. The mice were housed at 18 to 23 °C with 40 to 60% humidity, with 12-h light/12-h dark cycles. All mice used in this study were 6 to 12 wk of age, and both sexes were used without randomization or blinding. All mouse experiments were performed under protocol approved by the Institutional Animal Use and Care Committees of Center for Discovery and Innovation, Hackensack University Medical Center.

Primary and Recall Responses by CD8+ T Cells.

To elicit a primary response, naive P14 CD8+ T cells isolated from the lymph nodes (LNs) of WT, CreER+Ctcf+/+, and CreER+CtcfFL/FL P14-TCR transgenic mice were adoptively transferred into B6.SJL recipients, followed by LCMV-Arm infection as previously described (26, 52). The immune mice were administered tamoxifen (T5648, Millipore-Sigma) at 0.2 mg/g body weight via oral gavage for two consecutive days at ≥30 dpi to achieve induced deletion of CTCF. CD62L+ TCM cells were sort-purified and used for immunoblotting, ATAC-seq, and flow cytometry analyses. For ex vivo recall response, the sorted TCM cells were seeded onto flat-bottom 96-well plate at 8 × 104 cells/well and incubated with GP33 peptide (200 nM) for 24 h, and the GP33-stimulated cells were collected for RNA-seq analysis. For detection of cytokine production, Brefeldin A was added during the last 5 h of incubation.

Flow Cytometry.

Single-cell suspensions were prepared from the spleen and LNs and surface or intracellularly stained as described (26, 52).

Multiomics Data Generation and Availability.

Previously published multiomics data in TN, TEFF, and TCM cells were downloaded and processed, which included RNA-seq, H3K4me3 and H3K27ac ChIPseq (10), ATAC-seq (26, 31), CTCF CUT&RUN data (25), and Hi-C (25, 26, 31). The following data were newly generated following standard protocols as described (26, 31) and deposited at the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) under the GSE228887 SuperSeries: ATAC-seq was performed on resting WT and CTCF iKO TCM cells (GSE244999), RNA-seq on WT and CTCF iKO TCM cells after 24-h GP33 stimulation as well as resting WT TCM cells (GSE228886), and CTCF CUT&RUN data on TEFF and TCM cells (GSE228885). Detailed data processing procedures are included in SI Appendix.
