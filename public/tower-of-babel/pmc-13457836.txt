Identification and external validation of a prognostic signature based on bone morphogenetic protein-related mRNAs for kidney renal clear cell carcinoma
Authors: Yajie Zhou, Zijian Hu, Lei Xie, Shuwen Zhang, Qi Huang, Nan Zhang, Yijiang Liu, Wenxiong Zhang, Kang Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe Bone Morphogenetic Protein (BMP) family acts as a critical modulator of cellular plasticity and lineage commitment in oncogenesis. However, the systematic contribution of BMP-related mRNAs (BRMs) to the progression and immunophenotypic landscape of kidney renal clear cell carcinoma (KIRC) remains poorly defined.MethodsIntegrating transcriptomic and clinical data, we established a BRM-based risk signature to refine KIRC prognosis. The model’s robustness was validated using the E-MTAB-1980 cohort and a comprehensive nomogram. We employed advanced computational frameworks—including tumor mutation burden (TMB), tumor microenvironment (TME) deconvolution, and drug sensitivity profiling—to dissect biological heterogeneity between risk subgroups.ResultsA nine-BRM signature (comprising L1CAM, MPP7, CLIC5, TUBB2B, PLG, CA8, ITGAX, PADI3, and PRR15L) was identified as an independent prognostic factor. Kaplan-Meier analysis demonstrated significantly inferior survival in the high-risk group (HRG) compared to the low-risk group (LRG) (P < 0.001). The BRM-integrated nomogram exhibited superior predictive accuracy (AUC = 0.747) and strong calibration. Functional enrichment revealed that while the LRG was associated with physiological renal tubular secretion, the HRG was predominantly characterized by immunoglobulin production and B cell-mediated immune dysregulation. Furthermore, the HRG exhibited a significantly higher TMB and distinct sensitivity to Dactolisib and Podophyllotoxin, whereas the LRG favored Docetaxel. RT-qPCR successfully validated the differential expression of core BRMs in clinical samples.ConclusionsThis nine-BRM prognostic model serves as a potential prognostic stratification tool that may complement existing clinical parameters in evaluating the outcomes of KIRC patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-026-05238-z.Keywords: Bone morphogenetic protein, Kidney renal clear cell carcinoma, Messenger RNA, Risk model, Immunotherapy

Introduction

Kidney renal clear cell carcinoma (KIRC) is characterized by genetic mutations in key regulators of the hypoxia signaling pathway. These mutations contribute to metabolic dysregulation, increased angiogenesis, enhanced tumor heterogeneity, and detrimental crosstalk within the tumor microenvironment (TME) [1, 2]. Traditional staging systems, such as the tumor-node-metastasis (TNM), are widely used in staging KIRC and evaluating patient prognosis, but its predictive accuracy has limitations [3, 4]. As a result, there is a growing need for more reliable tools to guide treatment decisions. Recently, prognostic models based on biomarkers have attracted considerable attention for their potential to improve outcome prediction.

Bone morphogenetic proteins (BMPs), originally identified for their capacity to induce bone formation in bone extracts, have more recently been acknowledged as key factors in cancer development and progression [5, 6]. Within the BMP family, BMP1 has been implicated in the initiation and progression of several malignancies, such as bladder cancer (BLCA), lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD), and KIRC [7]. Messenger RNA (mRNA) is increasingly regarded as a significant factor influencing tumor prognosis, with several mRNA-based prognosis models effectively predicting patient outcomes across various cancer types. For example, the mRNA model constructed by Zhao et al. has provided a reliable assessment of the prognosis in patients with endometrial cancer [8]. Li et al. developed an mRNA signature-based model for predicting the prognostic outcome of bladder cancer, which exhibited promising performance [9]. However, to date, no study has established an evaluation model for KIRC based on BMP-related mRNAs (BRMs).

Therefore, we established a prognostic model applicable to KIRC patients across different clinical stages and validated its predictive accuracy. To successfully construct this risk model and investigate its underlying mechanisms as well as clinical applicability in KIRC, the following methods were employed.

Materials and methods

Data acquisition

The primary source of data for this investigation was the Xena database (https://xena.ucsc.edu/, accessed until August 1st, 2025) [10]. Within this platform, the Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database furnished transcriptomic profiles for 607 KIRC cases, alongside clinical details for 979 patients and mutational information on tumor somatic cells. Following this, 597 samples (including 526 tumor samples and 71 normal samples) were identified through a rigorous screening process, which excluded patients with missing survival data or incomplete follow-up information to minimize potential selection bias. For clinical variables with high missing rates in the TCGA cohort, such as the N stage, we prioritized the comprehensive AJCC TNM stage (Stage I-IV) to maintain predictive accuracy and ensure the robustness of the subsequent multivariate analysis. To minimize technical variability from sequencing depth and platform artifacts in TCGA-KIRC data, we utilized FPKM-normalized transcriptomic data obtained from the UCSC Xena platform, which had been consistently preprocessed. In addition, the external validation dataset (E-MTAB-1980) was sourced from the ArrayExpress platform (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/biostudies/arrayexpress, accessed until August 1st, 2025). A total of 101 KIRC samples with complete transcriptome and clinical data were obtained through screening and utilized as an independent external validation cohort to evaluate the generalizability of the prognostic model across different platforms and populations. Because the TCGA-KIRC transcriptomic data used in the discovery phase were obtained from the UCSC Xena platform with consistent preprocessing, no additional batch-effect correction was applied within the TCGA cohort. In addition, the external validation dataset (E-MTAB-1980) was analyzed independently rather than merged with the TCGA cohort; therefore, cross-platform batch correction was not required for model construction.

Select BMP-associated genes and BRMs

With “Bone morphogenetic protein” as keywords in GeneCards website (https://www.genecards.org/, as of July 1, 2025), we identified BRMs for further analysis [11]. To delineate the associations between BRMs and the global mRNA profile, Pearson correlation analysis was performed (|R|>0.3 and P < 0.05). Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using the “limma” R package to obtain DEGs (The filters are: | log2Fold Change |>1 and P < 0.05) [12].

Moreover, we randomly divided 526 KIRC tumor patients into a train group and a test group in an equal 1:1 ratio. Subsequently, we conducted univariate Cox (uni-C), Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO), and multivariate Cox (multi-C) regression analyses on the train group to obtain the most meaningful BRMs.

Establish a prognostic model for BRMs

We obtained the corresponding risk scores for BRMs by calculating the product of each mRNA expression and its coefficient, then summing up all the products. Risk scores were calculated for each patient as the sum of the expression level of each signature gene multiplied by its corresponding regression coefficient. The median risk score of the train cohort was used as the cutoff value to classify patients into the high-risk group (HRG) and low-risk group (LRG), and the same cutoff was subsequently applied to the test cohort, the entire TCGA cohort, and the external validation cohort. Using R packages “survival” and “survminer”, we plotted Kaplan-Meier (K-M) curves to assess overall survival (OS) between the two groups. This analysis enabled us to evaluate the survival probabilities over time for different patient groups and determine any significant differences in survival outcomes. To assess the model we developed predictive accuracy, our study computed time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) values for subjects at 1, 3, and 5-year intervals. We performed principal component analysis (PCA) for all genes, all mRNAs, BRMs and risk signature to validate subgroup effects [13]. In addition, we applied risk scores and clinical information to perform uni-C and multi-C independent prognostic analyses to confirm the predictive performance of this model. Additionally, K-M curves based on clinical features were generated to evaluate survival differences between the HRG and LRG.

Develop a nomogram for patient survival prediction

To forecast the OS in KIRC populations at 1, 3, and 5 years, we developed a prognostic nomogram using the R package “RMS”, incorporating clinical features such as age, gender, and stage [14]. In addition, calibration curves were generated to evaluate the agreement between the nomogram-predicted and observed survival probabilities at 1, 3, and 5 years.

Enrichment analysis

To investigate functional differences between the two cohorts, we performed gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA). Pathways were considered significantly enriched at FDR < 0.25 and nominal P < 0.05. In addition, DEGs between the two groups were subjected to Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analysis using the “clusterProfiler” package, with adjusted P < 0.05 defined as the significance threshold [15].

TMB analysis

Somatic mutation data for the TCGA cohort were obtained and processed using the “maftools” package. The mutation landscape was visualized through waterfall representations to illustrate the frequency and distribution of major mutated genes. Furthermore, TMB levels were compared between the two groups to assess the potential association with the risk stratification model [16].

Tumor microenvironment analysis and immune checkpoint expression

Using the R package “ESTIMATE”, we assessed the differences in stromal, ESTIMATE and immune score between HRG and LRG. The immune scores were performed using various algorithms, such as XCELL, TIMER, QUANTISEQ, MCPCOUNTER and others [17]. We analyzed the infiltration levels of 22 immune cell types to characterize the immune cell composition. Furthermore, by conducting single sample GSEA (ssGSEA), we obtained immune function scores for each patient and compared difference in these scores between the two groups. By reading the immune checkpoints literature, we obtained the world-recognized immune checkpoint gene information and analyzed the expression of different checkpoints in HRG and LRG using the “limma” package.

Immunotherapy and chemotherapy response in KIRC patients

To investigate differences in immune dysfunction and rejection between the two groups, we obtained the scores from the Tumor Immune Dysfunction and Exclusion (TIDE) platform [18]. These scores are inversely correlated with the likelihood of responding to immunotherapy. TIDE scores were compared between the two groups to assess potential differences in immunotherapy responsiveness. Additionally, using the “oncoPredict” R package, we estimated the half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) of several antitumor agents and examined group-specific variations in drug sensitivity [19].

Verify protein and mRNA expression of BRMs

To validate the protein expression levels of the BRMs, we examined their protein levels in tumor (t-Ts) and normal tissues (n-Ts) using data from the Human Protein Atlas (HPA) database (https://www.proteinatlas.org/). In addition, the HK-2 normal renal tubular epithelial cell line and the 786-O, A-498, and ACHN renal carcinoma cell lines were obtained from Procell Life Science & Technology Co., Ltd. These cell lines were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium (Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere. Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA) and reverse-transcribed into cDNA using the PrimeScript RT Kit (Takara, Japan). RT-qPCR was then performed on a CFX96 Real-Time System (Bio-Rad, USA) to assess the mRNA expression levels of BRMs, with GAPDH used as the internal control. Relative expression levels were calculated using the 2^−ΔΔCt method. All primer sequences are listed in Table S1 [20].

External validation

We used the E-MTAB-1980 dataset for external validation [21]. We calculated the corresponding risk scores and divided the dataset into HRG and LRG. The prognostic characteristics of patients were subsequently assessed using survival analysis, time-dependent ROC curves, and independent prognostic analyses.

Establish and validate the prognosis model

We obtained risk scores for each KIRC patient sample based on 9 BRMs and classified the patients into HRG and LRG according to the median. The complete information of the HRG and LRG is shown in Table 2. Kaplan–Meier survival analysis demonstrated that patients in the HRG exhibited significantly poorer OS than those in the LRG across the train, test, and entire cohorts (all P < 0.001) (Fig. 3A-C). Time-dependent ROC curve analysis further demonstrated the predictive performance of the model. The AUC values were 0.786, 0.780, and 0.801 for the train group at 1, 3, and 5 years; 0.719, 0.696, and 0.720 for the test group; and 0.751, 0.733, and 0.757 for the entire cohort (Fig. 3D–F). The predictive value of our risk model in these three groups was demonstrated by the expression of 9 mRNAs (Figure S2A–C), as well as the risk curve (Figure S2D-F), the risk profile (Figure S2G-I), and the scatter plot (Figure S2J-L). The results of PCA indicated that HRG and LRG exhibit significant classification clustering (Fig. 4A–D).

Enrichment analysis

The functions associated with the differentially expressed genes were revealed through KEGG enrichment analysis, such as cytokine receptor interaction, cell adhesion molecules, and neuroactive ligand (Figure S5). The results of GSEA demonstrated the top 6 important enrichment pathways in the HRG and LRG of these 6 enrichment methods (BIOCARTA, GOBP, IMMUNE, KEGG, PID, and REACTOME). In the GOBP enrichment analysis, pathways such as immunoglobulin production and B-cell-mediated immune were enriched in the HRG (Figure S6). Table S4 provided the detailed pathway information.

Tumor mutation burden

We calculated the TMB for each sample and found that it was generally higher in the HRG (Fig. 7A). Waterfall plots were generated for the different subclusters, incorporating the top 20 frequently mutated genes. The top five genes with the highest frequency of mutations are PBRM1 (45%), VHL (44%), SETD2 (17%), TTN (17%), and BAP1 (16%) in HRG (Fig. 7B). Additionally, mutations are common in VHL (48%), PBRM1 (37%), TTN (17%), MUC16 (8%), and SETD2 (8%) in LRG (Fig. 7C).

Immune microenvironment analysis

The TME analysis indicated that in the HRG, the immune (P < 0.0001) and ESTIMATE scores(P < 0.01) were elevated, while the stromal score exhibited no marked difference (P > 0.05) (Fig. 8A-C). Furthermore, Fig. 8D showed the LRG had considerably worse tumor purity (P < 0.01). The relationship between immune cell types and risk scores was further explored using multiple algorithms (Fig. 8E). Figure 8F shows that the HRG exhibited higher proportions of activated memory CD4⁺ T cells, follicular helper T cells (both P < 0.01), regulatory T cells (Tregs), plasma cells, and M0 macrophages (all P < 0.0001). In contrast, the LRG displayed increased levels of NK cells (P < 0.05), monocytes (P < 0.01), naïve B cells (P < 0.001), M1 macrophages, dendritic cells, and mast cells (all P < 0.0001) The relationship between immune cell infiltration and risk score is presented in Figure S7. Immune function score analysis further revealed pronounced differences in eight immune-related pathways between the two groups. In the HRG, APC co-inhibition (P < 0.05) and type II IFN response (P < 0.001) were both markedly suppressed relative to the LRG. In contrast, APC costimulation and CCR (both P < 0.01); Check point and T cell coinhabiting (both P < 0.05); Para-inflammation (P < 0.001) and T cell costimulation (both P < 0.001) were markedly elevated in the HRG (Fig. 8G). It can be inferred that Para-inflammation and T cell costimulation may be associated with the occurrence of tumors in HRG.

Immunotherapeutic targets and chemotherapy

TIDE scoring analysis revealed that in HRG with BRMs, TIDE score (Figure S8A), T cell exclusion (Figure S8B) and T cell dysfunction (Figure S8C) were consistently high. This result may indicate that tumor cells in HRG are more likely to demonstrate immune evasion, making treatment more difficult. Therefore, we selected 20 differentially expressed immune checkpoints and observed elevated expression levels of these checkpoints such as CD86 and PDCD5 in HRG (Figure S8D). These results indicate that the HRG may be more responsive to immune-based therapeutic strategies. We also assessed the IC50 values of antitumor drugs in the HRG and LRG. Table S5 lists the drugs sensitive to tumors in the LRG, while Table S6 shows the drugs sensitive to HRG, and Table S7 presents drugs with no dramatic difference between the two groups. The HRG appears to exhibit greater sensitivity to certain drugs, such as Dactolisib and Podophyllotoxin, which belongs to the targeted the PI3K/MTOR signaling pathway. The IC50 values serve as an important reference for determining suitable treatment strategies.

Verify protein and mRNA expression of BRMs

We assessed BRM protein expression in both t- and n-Ts (Figure S9A). RT-qPCR analysis further showed that PRR15L, CLIC5, MPP7, PLG, and CA8 were mainly expressed in n-Ts, whereas L1CAM, TUBB2B, ITGAX, and PADI3 exhibited higher expression levels in tumor tissues (Figure S9B).

External validation

In order to more accurately evaluate the validity and applicability of this model, we used the E-MTAB-1980 group for external validation. The K-M curve showed similar results to those observed previously: the OS of patients in the HRG was markedly worse compared to those in the LRG (P < 0.001) (Figure S10A). As shown in the time-dependent ROC curve, the AUC values for predicting patient OS were as follows: 0.702 at 1 year, 0.791 at 3 years, and 0.763 at 5 years, demonstrating great accuracy (Figure S10B). The results of the ROC curve containing each clinical information showed different AUC values: risk score = 0.701, gender = 0.627, age = 0.571 and stage = 0.835 (Figure S10C). In the E-MTAB-1980 group, uni-C analysis revealed that stage and risk score (both P < 0.001) were independent predictors for KIRC, with HR and 95%CI as follows: stage = 2.28 (1.68–3.09) and risk score = 2.10 (1.64–2.68) (Figure S10D). The multi-C analysis had the similar results: stage and risk score (both P < 0.001) were also independent predictors for KIRC, with HR and 95%CI as follows: stage = 2.24 (1.58–3.12) and risk score = 2.01 (1.52–2.65) (Figure S10E).

Discussion

The management of KIRC remains a formidable clinical challenge due to its profound genetic heterogeneity and the limitations of the traditional TNM staging system, which often overlooks the molecular determinants of tumor progression [22, 23]. This limitation partly stems from an incomplete understanding of the biological processes driving tumor behavior. KIRC is increasingly recognized as a metabolically reprogrammed malignancy rather than a purely genetic disease. Extensive evidence has demonstrated that KIRC is characterized by profound alterations in cellular energy metabolism, including a shift toward aerobic glycolysis, impaired mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS), and dysregulated lipid metabolism [24–28]. Notably, metabolic flux through glycolysis is frequently rewired in KIRC, enabling tumor cells to sustain rapid proliferation and adapt to hypoxic conditions [29–32]. These metabolic alterations not only support tumor growth but also contribute to therapeutic resistance and the maintenance of cancer stem cell phenotypes [33–36]. Consequently, there is an urgent need for more precise prognostic tools that integrate molecular biomarkers with clinical parameters to achieve robust patient stratification and personalized therapeutic guidance [37]. Beyond their established roles in tumor progression, BMPs have also been implicated in the regulation of energy metabolism, lipid homeostasis, and glucose utilization, suggesting a potential link between BMP signaling and metabolic reprogramming in cancer cells [38–39]. We addressed this knowledge gap by constructing a BRM-based model, which successfully identified a pronounced survival disadvantage in the HRG compared to the LRG. By incorporating this model into a comprehensive nomogram alongside age, gender, and stage, we demonstrated a superior predictive capacity. Subsequent mechanistic investigations involving TMB, enrichment analysis, and TME characterization further elucidated the biological foundation of our risk model, while drug sensitivity analysis identified tailored treatment options, enhancing its clinical translatability.

In this study, the developed BRM-related prediction model and nomogram exhibited robust performance, which was further validated using the external E-MTAB-1980 cohort. The prognostic discrimination of our model was confirmed by significant AUC values in ROC analysis, aligning with the successful implementation of similar predictive frameworks in other malignancies [40–43]. Notably, our BRM-based model demonstrated competitive predictive potential compared to some existing frameworks for KIRC, with a C-index of 0.775 exceeding those of previously reported PANoptosis-related and SUMO-associated gene models [44–45]. The biological credibility of our framework is supported by the fact that the identified key BRMs are well-recognized contributors to tumor biology. For instance, L1CAM and ITGAX are pivotal in promoting tumor growth, invasion, and tumorigenesis in kidney cancer [46–47]. Similarly, MPP7 has been identified as a key regulator of cellular migration, while CLIC5 modulates ROS to promote oncogenic signaling [48–49]. Furthermore, the roles of TUBB2B in apoptosis, PLG in chemoresistance, and CA8 in cell proliferation underscore the multifaceted nature of the BRM signature [50–52]. Importantly, PADI3 promotes citrulline conversion, potentially leading to metabolic shifts such as enhanced glycolytic activity. This observation aligns with the well-established metabolic phenotype of KIRC, where increased glycolysis and impaired mitochondrial respiration collectively drive tumor aggressiveness [53].

Enrichment analysis offered deeper mechanistic insights into the divergent outcomes between the two risk groups. Notably, the HRG was characterized by a significant enrichment in immunoglobulin production and B cell-mediated pathways. While B cells are traditionally viewed as anti-tumor effectors, their role in the TME is increasingly recognized as functionally heterogeneous [54–56]. Emerging evidence suggests that specific B-cell subsets, particularly regulatory B cells (Bregs), can exert immunosuppressive effects by secreting cytokines such as IL-10 and by inhibiting cytotoxic T-cell responses, thereby promoting tumor progression [57]. In the context of KIRC, the increased immunoglobulin-related activity observed in the HRG may be associated with tumor progression rather than effective tumor control, although this interpretation requires further functional validation. Moreover, antibody production within the TME may facilitate immune complex formation and chronic inflammatory signaling, which can further reshape the local immune landscape. The complex nature of the HRG microenvironment is further evidenced by the upregulation of parainflammation and T cell co-stimulation. Although T cell co-stimulation typically implies an active immune response, the concurrent presence of parainflammation—a chronic, low-grade inflammatory state—is often a harbinger of poor clinical outcomes [58]. Chronic inflammatory signaling has been reported to promote T-cell exhaustion, impair antigen presentation, and facilitate the recruitment of immunosuppressive immune populations within the tumor microenvironment. This finding suggests that higher TMB in the HRG, although theoretically associated with increased neoantigen load and enhanced immunogenicity [59–60], does not necessarily translate into an effective anti-tumor response. In our study, this apparent contradiction may be partly explained by the coexistence of immune activation-related signatures with mechanisms of immune dysfunction and immune evasion. Persistent inflammatory stimulation, together with altered B-cell-related immune activity, may be associated with an ineffective immune response that favors immune escape rather than efficient tumor elimination. KIRC is recognized as one of the most immune-infiltrated tumor types, where the local immune landscape is profoundly shaped by the tumor’s metabolic state [61–62]. Emerging evidence suggests that specific metabolic pathways can dictate angiogenesis and inflammatory signatures within the TME [63–64]. In our study, the HRG exhibited a complex immune-related phenotype in which increased immune infiltration may coexist with functional immune exhaustion. These TME features may contribute to disease progression and may influence the response to systemic therapies [65–67]. This failure is largely explained by the elevated TIDE scores observed in our study, which point toward a dominant immune escape phenotype. High TIDE scores indicate increased T-cell dysfunction and exclusion, suggesting that although immune cells are present in the HRG microenvironment, their anti-tumor activity may be functionally impaired. In essence, the high-risk TME appears to be functionally exhausted, where substantial immune infiltration is rendered ineffective by intrinsic evasion mechanisms, ultimately driving the survival disadvantage observed in these patients.

Drug sensitivity analysis revealed distinct predicted therapeutic profiles between the two risk groups, providing a potential framework for personalized intervention in KIRC. The HRG exhibited significant sensitivity to agents such as Dactolisib and Podophyllotoxin. These compounds primarily target the PI3K/mTOR axis, a signaling pathway that is frequently hyperactivated in aggressive malignancies to support rapid metabolic demands and cell survival [68–71]. The predicted sensitivity in the HRG suggests that high-risk tumors may have greater dependence on this pathway, indicating a potential therapeutic vulnerability. However, this finding still requires further experimental and clinical validation. Furthermore, the elevated expression of immune checkpoints such as PDCD5 and CD86 in the HRG suggests that these patients may potentially benefit from immune checkpoint inhibitor-related therapeutic strategies [72]. However, the concurrent rise in TIDE scores suggests that the benefit of monotherapy might be limited by intrinsic immune escape mechanisms [73]. Consequently, a combined approach integrating PI3K/mTOR inhibitors with ICIs may be necessary to bypass resistance and enhance the anti-tumor immune response. In contrast, the LRG demonstrated greater responsiveness to traditional chemotherapeutic agents, including Dactinomycin and Docetaxel. This divergence emphasizes the importance of stratifying KIRC patients based on their molecular risk profiles rather than relying on a one-size-fits-all approach. Similar biomarker-driven strategies have already refined the treatment landscapes of Ovarian cancer (OV) and LUAD, leading to measurable improvements in patient outcomes [74–75]. By integrating predicted IC50 values into our prognostic model, we provide exploratory insights that align with the core objectives of precision oncology [76]. This approach can help guide future treatment evaluation and patient stratification, but further experimental and clinical validation is needed.

Our study introduces several significant contributions to the field of renal oncology. This research represents a pioneering effort to leverage BRMs for predicting the clinical outcomes of KIRC patients, yielding high predictive accuracy. By demonstrating superior performance compared to existing prognostic models, our findings suggest that BRMs possess substantial clinical utility and may capture essential biological nuances that previous frameworks have overlooked. Furthermore, the robust performance of our model across an independent external dataset reinforces the reliability and generalizability of the results. Despite these strengths, certain limitations must be acknowledged. While we have proposed several potential therapeutic targets and pharmacological candidates, these findings are based on bioinformatic predictions and lack validation in a prospective clinical setting using real-world patient samples. Additionally, the clinical heterogeneity and missing clinical data (e.g., N stage) inherent in retrospective public datasets may influence the model’s robustness. The prognostic model includes nine BRMs, which may increase the complexity of routine clinical application. However, multi-gene signatures are already widely used in oncology and can be assessed using relatively accessible PCR-based panels. Nevertheless, further simplification of the panel and prospective clinical validation will be necessary before routine clinical implementation. However, we must emphasize that our current validation evidence (RT-qPCR and HPA) is limited to expression-level validation and does not establish the biological functions or mechanistic relevance of these BRMs. Future studies will focus on further optimizing the gene panel and performing functional validation, including gene knockdown or overexpression assays, cell proliferation and migration analyses, and in vivo experiments, to elucidate the biological roles of these BRMs in KIRC progression. Moreover, the efficacy of the proposed drug combinations warrants further evaluation in well-designed clinical trials to confirm their safety and therapeutic potential in KIRC management.

Conclusion

In summary, both the BRM-based prognostic model and the integrated nomogram demonstrated strong predictive performance for OS in patients with KIRC. Our findings indicate that HRG-related pathways and elevated TMB are key determinants of poor clinical outcomes. Drug sensitivity analysis further revealed that HRG patients may derive greater benefit from targeted agents such as Dactolisib and Podophyllotoxin, offering a potential path toward personalized treatment. While this study provides preliminary expression-level validation of the BRM signature, the specific molecular mechanisms and biological roles of these BRMs in KIRC still need to be further investigated through in-depth functional experiments. Nevertheless, our findings offer a valuable reference for improving risk stratification and potentially informing therapeutic strategies in KIRC.
