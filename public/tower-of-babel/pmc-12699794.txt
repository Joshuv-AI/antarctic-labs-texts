Where low-income seniors receive their heart health and diabetes information: a cross-sectional survey
Authors: Pauneez Sadri, Momina Abbas, Christie Koester, Melissa Pirrie, Ricardo Angeles, Francine Marzanek, Gina Agarwal
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOlder adults (> 55 years), in particular low-income older adults, have lower health literacy than the rest of the Canadian population. Lower health literacy is related to several negative health outcomes such as poor diabetes control and other physical and mental health problems. Canada’s rising ageing population requires an age-friendly system that reduces the dependency on the Canadian health care system. This study investigated the Health Information Seeking Behaviour of low-income seniors living in social housing across five Ontario regions to determine how to improve healthcare outcomes and the performance of the Ontario healthcare system.MethodsThis cross-sectional study included in-person interviews guided by the Health Awareness and Behaviour Tool (HABiT) survey. Interviews were conducted with older adults from 16 social housing buildings in five Ontario communities between May 2014 and January 2015. Questionnaire responses were analyzed using descriptive statistics and simple logistics regressions.Results625 individuals completed the HABiT survey. The majority of participants sought out health information at the doctor’s office; 515 participants received health information from a doctor or nurse about keeping their heart healthy and 471 about preventing diabetes. Females were more than twice as likely to receive health information about heart health from family members, media sources, and pharmacists than males. Those aged > 84 years were the least likely to use media sources and were almost three times as likely to contact a doctor or nurse for heart health information compared to middle-aged participants. Adults with higher post-secondary education were more likely to use the Internet as a source of health information compared to high school graduates.ConclusionsFamily physicians with older adult patients could better supplement their health assessments by promoting and explaining educational brochures, and ensuring that they address these health topics to better communicate chronic disease prevention.Keywords: Diabetes, Heart health, Seniors, Health knowledge, Health behaviour

Background

It is widely known that low-income adults experience poorer health overall, have more medical conditions, have worse symptoms of illnesses and diseases, and thus have more significant health care needs than high-income adults [1]. Despite the continuous rise of Canada’s older adult population, the Canadian health care system was not constructed to be able to fulfill the challenges of this aging population [2]. Since 75–80% of Canadian seniors report having one or more chronic conditions, addressing these complex health issues is crucial [2]. The Canadian Medical Association recommends that the solution is to create an “age-friendly” system that aims to reduce dependency on the health care system and enhance current care for seniors [2]. Despite this reality, low-income Canadians are two times more likely than other Canadians to report unmet health care needs of which cost, lack of affordable transportation and health care coverage are common barriers [3, 4].

It is also well understood that older adults, particularly low-income older adults, have lower health literacy than the rest of the population [5]. According to the Canadian Council of Learning, health literacy represents an individual’s access and ability to comprehend, evaluate, and communicate information to improve health in different settings throughout one’s lifetime [6]. Lower health literacy is related to several negative health outcomes such as poor diabetes control and other physical and mental health problems [5]. A recent study revealed a significant relationship between functional health literacy and body mass index (BMI), education level, and pain and discomfort levels [5]. Those who had a high school education or lower, BMI in the normal range, or reported no regular pain or discomfort, had significantly lower odds of having adequate health literacy [5].

‘Health information seeking behaviour’ can be broadly viewed as the ways individuals obtain information about health, illness, health promotion, and risks to health [7]. It has been shown that adults with below-basic health literacy are less likely to obtain their health information from written sources (e.g. books, magazines, newspapers) and are more likely to receive information from television or other media sources compared to those with higher literacy levels [8]. Low-income seniors who live in social housing have a particularly lower literacy rate, which in turn includes a lower health literacy rate [9]. Lower levels of health literacy in a population hold particularly negative consequences on the healthcare system [10]. Current knowledge about health-related service used by low-income Canadians is largely drawn from studies about treatment-focused and preventive health care services [4]. These studies consistently demonstrate that compared to higher-income Canadians, low-income adults heavily rely on the services provided by their general practitioners, and services provided by mental health institutions and hospitals [4]. Canada has a universal healthcare system whereby there is no cost to the individual to visit their family doctor. The most commonly cited healthcare services used by participants were private practice physicians, community health clinics, walk-in facilities, and emergency departments [4]. When older adults visit their physicians or other health care professionals to ask health care questions that health professionals consider unnecessary, this can have major implications and create a significant burden on the healthcare system [11]. If older adults are able to better access health information without making frequent appointments with their healthcare providers and improve their health literacy they may also use fewer healthcare resources in general. By encouraging older adults to incorporate the use of the Internet, other media channels (e.g. radio, T.V, etc.), or written sources (e.g. brochures) we can help to change the ways they participate in health care [12]. This can enhance their decision-making skills so they do not rely solely on a physician-centred model of care, especially when it may not be as readily accessible to them [13].

The interest in understanding the health literacy of older adults is thus related to ensuring that the burden on the health care system can be mitigated through targeted health literacy programs. In this study, we aim to assess the Health Information Seeking Behaviour of low-income seniors living in social housing across five Ontario regions in an effort to determine how to improve health care outcomes and the performance of the Ontario healthcare system.

Methods

Design and setting

This cross-sectional study examined data on health information-seeking behaviour in older adults in several cities across Ontario. The Health Awareness and Behaviour Tool (HABiT) survey was distributed to participants living in social housing buildings. Research team members held surveying sessions on multiple occasions within each building to maximize the coverage of building residents. Using a structured and in-depth questionnaire, participants were surveyed one-on-one by trained research staff or paramedics. The surveys were completed in a common area in the social housing building (e.g., wellness room). All surveys were conducted in English and participation was voluntary. Written consent was obtained from participants before beginning the survey. This research study was approved by the Hamilton Integrated Research Ethics Board (HiREB) (#14–210).

Sample and recruitment

Older adults aged 55 + were recruited from 16 social housing buildings across five Ontario communities, including Hamilton, Guelph, Simcoe County, Sudbury, and York Region. This baseline participant data was collected between 2014 and 2015; the data for Hamilton residents were collected between May 2014 and June 2014, Sudbury data was collected in March 2015, Guelph-Wellington data was collected in December 2014, Simcoe County data was collected in July 2015, and York Region data was collected in January 2015. All residents 55 years and older in the selected buildings were included as potential participants; the only specific exclusion criteria was that participants were not included if they did not speak English. Participants were recruited by placing invitation letters in their mailboxes and placing posters in common areas of the building using a consecutive sampling strategy.

Data collection

The in-person one-on-one interviews were guided by a questionnaire and included a series of validated instruments. The interview lengths varied between 15 and 45 min each. All data was collected using the HABiT questionnaire, which has been described in depth elsewhere [14]. The questionnaire included demographic questions and questions related to sources of health information that participants sought and utilized, focusing on information related to their own health and the health information sources that best met their needs [14]. The HABiT survey was developed to specifically measure older adults’ behaviour and lifestyle factors such as healthcare utilization, health knowledge, health risk factors, and quality of life [14]. The tool is reliable, has internal consistency, and possesses content and face validity in the target population (older adults living in social housing) [12]. Questions were developed and included from 15 sources[14], but primarily three surveys: Canadian Community Health Survey[15], Canadian Diabetes Risk Questionnaire (CANRISK)[16], and a survey by the Canadian Hypertension Education Program [17].

Availability of healthcare services

Participants were asked, “Overall, how would you rate the availability of health care services in your community“ with the response options “Excellent, very good, good, fair, poor”. This question was taken from the Canadian Community Health Survey.

Sources of health information

Two questions were used to ascertain where the individual would typically receive information about heart health and diabetes. The first question was drawn from the Canadian Hypertension Education Program [18] and asked, “Where do you go to get information about keeping your heart healthy?” with the response options of “Family member or friend, media (print ads, television), educational brochures or pamphlets, internet, my doctor or nurse, my pharmacist, walk-in clinic, blood pressure booklet & ‘tracker’, blood pressure screening provided by paramedics in our building, other - specify”. Individuals were asked to select up to 3 useful sources. The second question followed this same format, but replaced the term “heart health” with “preventing diabetes.”

Health seeking behaviour

Two questions on health seeking behaviour were asked, both taken from the Canadian Community Health Survey. First, “Is there a place that you usually go to when you are sick or need advice about your health?” with the response options as “yes, no”. The second question asked “if yes, what kind of a place is it?” Response options were “Doctor’s office, community health centre/CLSC, walk-in clinic, telephone health line (ex Telehealth Ontario), Telecare, hospital emergency room, hospital outpatient clinic, other - specify”. Individuals could select all that applied.

Data analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to analyze survey respondents’ sociodemographics and health information seeking behaviour. We cross-tabulated outcomes of seeking health information for heart health and seeking health information for preventing diabetes with several variables of interest such as gender, age, education, and ethnicity. These variables were then evaluated further by performing simple logistic regressions for the outcomes of seeking health information for heart health and seeking health information for preventing diabetes to determine the relationships between each outcome with participant characteristics and social determinants of health. Only factors determined to be statistically significant were retained and included in multivariable logistic regression models for each outcome; these results have been displayed as adjusted odds ratios (AORs) in the results section. The significance level was set at a p-value < 0.05. All data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 17.0.

Results

Participant demographics, chronic disease risk factors, and quality of life

A total of 625 individuals participated in the HABiT survey, of which the majority were white (n = 510; 81.6%), female (n = 471; 75.4%), lived alone (n = 563; 90.1%), and had a family physician (n = 577; 92.3%). Most participants had some high school education or less (n = 280; 44.8%) and were 66 years old or older (n = 403; 64.5%), with a mean age of 72.2 years (standard deviation = 8.8 years). The most common chronic disease patients reported having was hypertension (n = 339; 54.2%), and most participants spend less than three hours a day on the computer or the Internet (n = 496; 79.3%). A summary of all survey demographic characteristics is presented in Table 1. Participants’ chronic disease risk factors and health status (Table 2) remained poor across different health-related questions with a majority of participants reporting a high BMI (n = 404; 64.7%), low physical activity (n = 285; 45.6%), and living with pain and discomfort (n = 450; 72.0%). For respondents without a diabetes diagnosis, over half (n = 210; 56.3%) received a high CANRISK score [18]. Despite 40.0% of participants reporting a poor to fair perceived health status, 55.6% reported their community healthcare services to be very good or excellent.

Most common sources of health information

Table 3 shows participant reports of the availability of healthcare services and where they receive their sources of health information. A consistent majority of participants received health information from a doctor or nurse about keeping their heart healthy (n = 515; 85.5%) and preventing diabetes (n = 471; 79.0%). For both health-related issues, the second leading source of health information came from pharmacists, with walk-in clinics, the Internet, and media sources being the least commonly reported options. Educational brochures, however, were used more commonly as a source of heart health information (n = 103; 19.3%) in comparison to health information related to diabetes prevention (n = 49; 7.8%). And although most participants reported seeking care when feeling unwell (n = 335; 54.4%), various reasons were given if they did not, such as language barriers, unawareness that they were unwell, or unawareness of where to obtain healthcare information from.

Further, those aged > 84 years were less likely to report using media sources (AOR = 0.10, 95% CI [0.001–0.79]) for heart health information than those aged 55–64 years (with a constant negative correlation with age). Additionally, those aged 84 years or older were almost three times as likely (AOR = 2.70, 95% CI [0.76–9.60]) to contact a doctor or nurse for heart health information than adults aged 55–64 years old. Adults with a high school graduate degree (AOR = 1.98, 95% CI [0.96–4.08]), those with some college or university education (AOR = 4.01, 95% CI [2.86–11.67]), and those with a college or university degree (AOR = 5.78, 95% CI [2.86–11.67]) were several times more likely to use the Internet as a source of heart health information than those with some high school education. However, high school graduates (AOR = 2.09, 95% CI [1.07–4.08]) and adults with some college or university (AOR = 2.29, 95% CI [1.07–4.91]) education were twice as likely to use media sources for healthy heart information than those with some high school education. Adults with college or university degrees were twice as likely (AOR = 2.15, 95% CI [1.15–4.01]) to read educational brochures than those with some high school. Not surprisingly, individuals who already had a family doctor were six times more likely (AOR = 6.07, 95% CI [3.15–11.11]) to gather healthy heart information from a doctor or nurse. Also, white adults were more likely to use media sources (AOR = 3.30, 95% CI [0.98–11.15]) and much more likely to utilize educational brochures (AOR = 5.04, 95% CI [1.35–16.61]) than non-white adults.

Discussion

Our study set out to understand where low-income older adults, over the age of 55, tend to receive health information related to diabetes and heart health. Within our sample of 625 participants living in social housing buildings across five Ontario municipalities, we found doctors and nurses were the most common source of health information, used by nearly 80% of participants. This is consistent with many studies in the general population of older adults that have found that, unlike their younger counterparts, older adults are more likely to rely on physicians for health information and support in medical decision-making [19, 20]. In contrast, the internet, media, and brochures were used as resources for health information by less than 12% of the study participants, indicating the importance of having direct access to health professionals to disseminate health education on chronic disease prevention and management.

Overall, almost 90% of study participants had a family physician, indicating that this critical resource of health information was available to them in some capacity. However, only 55% of participants described their access to healthcare services as being ‘very good’ or ‘excellent’, suggesting that some participants that do have a family doctor may still face barriers in accessing resources. This is also highlighted by the finding that virtual methods of connecting with health professionals (e.g., health telephone line) were not common sources of health information in this population, and a 2025 federal report shows that low-income seniors still have limited access to the internet [12]. Overall, in-person, individualized interactions were preferred as methods of obtaining healthcare information. A qualitative study showed that older patients highly value their general practitioners when they provide time for “social conversation”; specifically, older adults evaluate the socio-emotional behaviours of their family physicians such as their ability to be friendly and allot enough time for their patient consultations [21]. Although technology is constantly advancing, and the number of online health information sources are continuously increasing, older adults still tend to find healthcare information communicated by their physician easier to understand and more trustworthy [22]. Thus, doctors should ensure that their personal relationships with older patients are maintained and that they have access to regular visits for the appropriate distribution of health information.

Brochures and media campaigns are commonly used to disseminate health messaging, but the current study shows that these may not be effective for reaching low-income older adults. It is important to understand why educational brochures are not being utilized by this population since a significant amount of government expenditure is used to promote media campaigns, advertisements, and brochures [23]. In 2019, the Canadian government spent almost $45 million on traditional (e.g. print, television) and digital (e.g. social media) advertisements [23]. In 2020–2021, media expenditure increased drastically to $107 million [23]. Our survey results highlight that media and brochures (particularly for diabetes prevention) are not common sources of health information for older adults. It is likely that educational brochures are not being used effectively for two reasons: (1) they are not being promoted, distributed or discussed effectively by family physicians, or (2) the brochures are difficult to understand and are not accessible for older audiences or those with lower literacy levels. Our logistic regression shows that older adults with any post-secondary education are more likely to use brochures for heart health information. If we know that these brochures are reaching the older adult population for heart health information, it may not be the distribution or promotion of the brochures that is the concern, but that those who do not use brochures either do not trust the material or find the information too difficult to understand due to lower education and health literacy levels [5]. It is also possible that the layout of these brochures is too content-heavy or the aesthetics of media material needs to be better suited to gather the attention and trust of older adults. Brochures and media campaigns are also not individualized, as a conversation with a healthcare professional would be. Future qualitative studies are needed to determine how these types of resources could be improved for this population, which may, in turn, lead to fewer visits to the clinic or emergency departments for questions related to health that could be gathered through these paper and digital sources. Pharmacists and family members were also consulted, more than brochures, but less than family physicians, with 30% of participants reporting that they receive health information from pharmacists, and 15% of participants reporting that they receive health information from family members. This suggests that older adults value one-on-one individual interactions with their sources of health information. This aligns with another study that examined the health information sources of older adults and found that 85% of participants utilized their health care providers whereas only 14% used pamphlets and newsletters [24]. However, the study also explored how older adults are attempting to combine various sources of information and rarely used just one source of information, with the internet and their family and friends being highly utilized to supplement the information they receive from their providers [24]. Understanding how older adults consume their health information is critical to improving the accessibility and effectiveness of these delivery methods. The effort and behaviour of acquiring health care knowledge are considered a “health-protective” action influenced by a variety of factors [25].

This study had some major strengths and some limitations. Some limitations of this study include that self-reported measures were primarily used; thus, even though HABiT is a validated tool, there is still the potential for social desirability and recall biases. Additionally, this study was cross-sectional, meaning that we cannot determine temporality of associations observed. Also, random sampling was not feasible in this population, so consecutive sampling was used. This could lead to self-selection bias of those that chose to participate. Another limitation is the age of data, however, the current literature states that low-income seniors still have low internet usage rates in 2025[26]. Future research could examine if there have been changes in where this population gets its health information after the pandemic, with this study serving as a baseline. Some strengths are that it is difficult to obtain this type of information in this vulnerable population, and this study has allowed us to gain insight into the sources of health information used by low-income older adults, who are largely excluded from research.

Conclusions

Ultimately, patients from this population prefer one-on-one, in-person interactions with their family physicians and pharmacists. Family physicians and other healthcare professionals who engage with older adult patients could better supplement their health assessments by promoting and explaining educational brochures and ensure that they address these health topics more effectively to better communicate chronic disease prevention.
