Dietary Supplement Use Explains More Variance in Nutritional Biomarker Concentrations than 24-Hour Dietary Intake from Food Only: Evidence from a Cross-Sectional Survey of the United States Population (NHANES 2003–2006)☆
Authors: Maya R Sternberg, Christine M Pfeiffer, Carissa D Powers, Naman Ahluwalia, Heather Hamner, Arick Wang, Edwina Wambogo, Jaime J Gahche, WenYen Juan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNutritional biomarkers are used as objective measures of nutritional status to inform public health strategies. The relationship between dietary intake and biomarkers varies across population subgroups due to biological, demographic, and behavioral factors. Understanding these differences is important for identifying nutritional interventions.ObjectivesWe evaluated how sociodemographic, lifestyle, and physiologic factors modify the association between vitamin-specific dietary intake (food and supplements) and selected water- and fat-soluble vitamin biomarkers among US adults aged ≥20 y.MethodsData were used from the 2003 to 2006 National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey for selected biomarkers [serum folate (SFOL), red blood cell folate (RBF), pyridoxal-5ʹ-phosphate, vitamins B-12, A (VIA), C (VIC), E (VIE), and 25-hydroxyvitamin D] and either vitamin-specific dietary intake (24-h dietary recall) or self-reported supplement use (past 30 d). Demographic, lifestyle, and physiologic variables were: age, sex, race and Hispanic origin, education, poverty level, serum cotinine, alcohol use, physical activity, body mass index, estimated glomerular filtration rate, and serum C-reactive protein. Associations between vitamin-specific intake and biomarkers were assessed using both bivariate analyses and multivariable linear regression models accounting for the clustered sampling design.ResultsIn unadjusted models, supplement use explained (R2) from 3% (VIA) to 21% (SFOL) and dietary intake explained from 0.8% (VIE) to 8.8% (VIC) of the biomarker variability. Intake models indicated more confounding due to sociodemographic and physiologic variables. Supplement-use models demonstrated heterogeneous effects across subgroups (i.e., effect modification by age, sex, and race and Hispanic origin). After full adjustment, both dietary intake and supplement use were associated with biomarkers for all vitamins except for vitamin A intake and all supplement-use associations changed direction except for SFOL and RBF.ConclusionsThese findings highlight the need to include supplement use and account for heterogeneous demographic effects when interpreting intake–biomarker associations in models.

Introduction

Nutritional biomarkers are often used in surveillance and public health planning to provide an objective measure of nutritional status because nutrients are important for metabolic function and to prevent chronic disease. In addition to dietary intake and supplement use, nutritional status can be influenced by sociodemographic, lifestyle, and physiologic factors. Therefore, understanding intake–biomarker relationships is important for setting dietary reference intakes, fortification strategies, or detecting inadequacies or excesses not evident from intake data alone [[1], [2], [3]]. These relationships are complex and often vary across population subgroups [4]. Factors including race and Hispanic origin, sex, smoking, and BMI impact not just dietary patterns but also absorption, metabolism, and gene expression. For example, differences in vitamin D status by skin pigmentation and geographic region are well documented [5], whereas alcohol intake, BMI, physical activity, and supplement use further influence these associations [6]. Several studies using NHANES data have examined associations between dietary intake and nutritional biomarkers [[7], [8], [9], [10], [11], [12]]. These studies focused on individual nutrient–biomarker pairs within specific survey cycles, using various dietary assessment methods such as food frequency questionnaires or 24-h recalls, usual intake, and sometimes incorporating supplement use to estimate total intake. However, few studies have systematically compared intake using the same dietary assessment of foods compared with supplements or evaluated how sociodemographic, lifestyle, and physiologic factors jointly influence these relationships. In particular, confounding and effect modification have not been examined in a comprehensive and systematic manner.

This project builds on prior research that described how sociodemographic, lifestyle, and physiologic variables were associated with nutritional biomarker concentrations in a nationally representative sample [13]. Expanding on this work, we used the same data set from US adults participating in the 2003–2006 NHANES to examine the relationship between vitamin-specific intake from dietary intake (food only), dietary supplements and total intake (food plus dietary supplements) and several water- and fat-soluble vitamin biomarkers: serum folate (SFOL), red blood cell folate (RBF), pyridoxal-5′-phosphate (PLP), and vitamins B-12 (B-12), A (VIA), C (VIC), D (25-hydroxyvitamin D, 25OHD), and E (VIE). The 2003–2006 NHANES cycles were used because they included a wider range of relevant biomarkers than earlier or later survey cycles. Guided by a directed acyclic graph (DAG) framework, we conceptualized intake variables as exposures with a causal relationship to biomarker concentrations (Figure 1) [14,15]. We evaluated sociodemographic, lifestyle, and physiologic factors as potential confounders or effect modifiers. Our aims were to: 1) quantify the strength of the intake–biomarker association, 2) evaluate the role of each selected variable in the intake–biomarker relationship, and 3) assess how adjusting for these variables, individually and jointly, affects the association between dietary intake from food only or supplement use and biomarker concentration. The primary goal of this paper is to inform nutrition research methodology by identifying which variables act as potential confounders or effect modifiers within an observational framework. Methods

In 1999, the NHANES conducted by the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS) became a continuous series of cross-sectional surveys with data released every 2 y designed to provide national statistics on the health and nutritional status of the noninstitutionalized civilian US population. The data are obtained through household interviews, standardized health examinations, and biological specimen collection in mobile examination centers (MEC) using a stratified, multistage probability cluster design. The response rate in NHANES 2003–2006 for those aged ≥20 y for the MEC component was ∼70%. More details on survey design, methods, and response rates are available elsewhere [[16], [17], [18]]. Participants aged >18 y consented, parents provided consent for those aged <18 y, and youth aged 7–17 y assented. The NCHS Ethics Review Board approved NHANES [19].

For this analysis, we used NHANES 2003–2006 data from adults aged ≥20 y with reliable day one 24-h dietary recall who provided biological specimens at the MEC. Pregnant and lactating women were excluded. Pregnancy status at the time of the MEC exam was obtained from demographic datafile for females aged 8–59 y, whereas lactation status was based on self-reported current breastfeeding from the reproductive health questionnaire (females aged 12–50 y). We analyzed the 2003–2004 (n = 4205) and 2005–2006 (n = 4167) cycles. Some biomarkers were available across the full 4-y sample (n = 8372), whereas others (VIA, VIE, and PLP) were limited to the 2005–2006 cycle. To account for unequal selection probabilities and nonresponse, we applied the NHANES 2-y first-day dietary survey weights for biomarkers measured only in 2005–2006 and used a 4-y first-day dietary survey weights for those available from 2003 to 2006, calculated by dividing the corresponding 2-y first-day dietary weights by 2.

Biomarker measurements

The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention Nutritional Biomarkers Laboratory analyzed the following biomarkers during all or part of NHANES 2003–2006: SFOL, RBF, PLP, B-12, VIA, VIE, 25OHD, and VIC. All specimens were collected at the MEC as part of the examination procedures. Assay performance was monitored using Westgard-type quality control multirules [20]. The laboratory methods are described in detail elsewhere [21,22]. The available years, specimen matrix, and corresponding laboratory methods for each biomarker are listed in Supplementary Table 1, and sample sizes are presented in Supplementary Table 2.

Serum 25OHD was originally measured by the DiaSorin radioimmunoassay; however, we utilized the revised data released in 2015 by NCHS that were adjusted to an HPLC-MS/MS method based on a bridging study between the 2 assays [23] and are thus reporting HPLC-MS/MS-equivalent 25OHD data.

Dietary intake measures

As part of NHANES, the dietary component What We Eat in America was administered, consisting of a 24-h dietary recall conducted in person at the MEC, followed by a second recall by telephone [[24], [25], [26]]. Both recalls were collected using the Automated Multiple-Pass Method [27]. We used the first-day recall to estimate nutrient intakes for calcium, folate, and vitamins A, B-6, B-12, C, and E. Dietary intake data for vitamin D were not available in NHANES 2003–2006; we therefore used calcium intake as a proxy because these 2 nutrients are often consumed together in fortified foods and supplements [28]. This is supported by evidence from NHANES 2007–2014, showing a strong association between the 2 nutrients among participants with reliable dietary data: Spearman correlation of 0.62 between 24-h dietary intakes of vitamin D and calcium, and Spearman correlation of 0.61 between 30-d mean supplement dietary intake of vitamin D and calcium among supplement users (analyses not shown).

The dietary supplement questionnaire collected information on supplement use during the previous 30 d, which is used to estimate mean daily nutrient intake from supplements for folate, and vitamins A, B-6, B-12, C, D, and E. Trained interviewers administered this component using the Computer-Assisted Personal Interviewing system [26]. We calculated the mean daily intake from supplements and created a binary indicator of vitamin-specific supplement use (yes/no) for the same nutrients mentioned above. Total intake for each vitamin was defined as the sum of the first-day 24-h dietary intake and mean daily intake from 30-d supplement use and represents the combined contribution of the nutrient from diet and supplements. For the remainder of this manuscript, “dietary intake” refers exclusively to intake from foods unless otherwise specified, and “supplement use” refers to dietary supplement use.

Covariates

Sociodemographic, lifestyle, and physiologic variables previously identified as influencing nutritional status were considered in these analyses. Sociodemographic variables included age (y), sex (male or female), race and Hispanic origin (Mexican American [MA], non-Hispanic Black [NHB], and non-Hispanic White [NHW]), education (< high school or ≥ high school), and family poverty income ratio (PIR), which was categorized as either above (PIR ≥1) or below poverty level (PIR <1). Other race and Hispanic origin groups were included in all estimates but not presented separately per NCHS analytic guidelines [29]. Age was treated as a categorical variable (20–39, 40–59, and ≥60 y) or as a continuous variable, depending on the statistical analysis. These variables are available as part of the publicly released NHANES demographic files.

Lifestyle variables included serum cotinine (direct measure of smoking), physical activity, and alcohol intake. These variables are available in the publicly released NHANES interview files (alcohol use and physical activity), whereas cotinine is available in the publicly released NHANES laboratory files. Physical activity was calculated as 7 × [metabolic equivalent task (MET) value × frequency × duration] / 30, where frequency and duration were based on reported leisure-time activities in the past 30 d [30]. Participants reporting no activity were assigned a value of zero. Alcohol intake was calculated as mean drinks/day, with 1 drink defined as 12 oz beer, 5 oz wine, or 1.5 oz liquor (≈14 g ethanol). Participants reporting <12 drinks in the past year were classified as nondrinkers and assigned a value of zero.

Lastly, the physiologic variables included BMI, estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) as a proxy for renal function, and C-reactive protein (CRP) concentration (mg/L) as a direct measure for inflammation [31]. These variables are available in the publicly released NHANES examination files (body measures), whereas CRP and serum creatinine (used to calculate eGFR) are available in the publicly released NHANES laboratory files. BMI was calculated as weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared (kg/m2). eGFR [mL/(min × 1.73 m2)] was calculated using the chronic kidney disease epidemiology collaboration creatinine equation, which accounts for age and sex [32].

Statistical analysis

Based on previous diagnostics of NHANES biomarker data, the natural log transformation was used for all biomarkers considered in this paper, except for VIC and 25OHD [7]. All analyses accounted for the complex sampling design and applied first-day dietary survey weights to adjust for clustering, stratification, and unequal probabilities of selection. We calculated geometric means (arithmetic means where appropriate) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for biomarker concentrations, and medians with 95% CIs for vitamin-specific intake measures. Estimates were stratified by sociodemographic variables, supplement use, and weighted quartiles of dietary intake from food only or total intake. The weighted quartiles used as cut points for each vitamin-specific intake, from food alone and from total intake (food plus supplements), are presented in Supplementary Table 3. All estimates were evaluated for reliability and reportability according to the NCHS analytic guidelines and ensured that the relative SE was ≤30% and a minimum unweighted sample size of ≥40 observations with ≥8 degrees of freedom [29]. Global Wald F tests were used to assess whether ≥1 group mean (geometric or arithmetic, as appropriate) differed across categories. For variables with >2 categories, pairwise comparisons were performed using t tests of regression coefficients from survey-weighted simple linear regression, comparing each category with the reference group; no adjustment was made for multiple comparisons. For dietary intake from food alone and total intake (food plus supplements), which included zero values, global Wald F tests were conducted on the log(x + 1) transformed scale, as design-based tests for medians are not readily available.

Spearman correlations were used to assess associations between intake variables and corresponding biomarkers, both overall and stratified by supplement use. Survey-weighted Spearman correlations were estimated by regressing ranked, standardized values of 1 variable on the other and accounting for the sampling design. P values were derived from design-based t tests of the slope coefficient and, to be conservative, defined as the larger of the 2 regression-based P values (i.e., X compared with Y or Y compared with X).

Subsequent analyses focused on the 2 primary exposures, either dietary intake or supplement use, and their associations with nutritional biomarkers through regression modeling. A DAG framework was used to identify covariates as potential confounders or effect modifiers [14,15]. Each exposure, defined as log-transformed vitamin-specific dietary intake (intake+1 to account for zero values) or vitamin-specific supplement use (yes/no), was first evaluated separately using simple linear regression. Multiple linear regression models were then used to assess whether individual sociodemographic, lifestyle, or physiologic covariates acted as confounders or effect modifiers. There was an a priori assumption that the relationship of the dietary intake, physiologic, and lifestyle variables had either a monotonic or no relationship with the biomarkers. Although not a required assumption, linear regression performs more robustly when independent variables are approximately normally distributed [33]. Therefore, alcohol intake, physical activity, cotinine levels, BMI, CRP, and eGFR were treated as continuous variables and modeled on a log-transformed scale in all linear models. Confounding was evaluated before effect modification. Confounding was evaluated by comparing regression coefficients across hierarchical models; the percent change in the β coefficient was calculated as |%Δβ| = 100×∣β1 – βadj∣ / ∣β1∣, with changes ≥10% considered evidence of confounding [14] where β1 is the coefficient for a given covariate in the base (unadjusted) model and βadj denotes the corresponding coefficient for the same covariate from an adjusted model. Directionality of change was indicated as toward (→) or away from (←) the null.

To examine potential heterogeneity in associations, we evaluated pairwise interactions between the exposure (dietary intake or supplement use) and each potential modifier using basic regression models that included the exposure, the candidate modifying variable, and their interaction term. Statistical significance of effect modification was determined by whether the global interaction Wald F P value ≤ 0.05. Other indications of heterogeneity in associations were visually assessed in descriptive and/or adjusted results, including changes in the directionality of exposure–outcome associations following adjustment for specific covariates, in the absence of explicitly modeled interaction terms. Although a change in directionality is not proof of interaction, it can be suggestive of effect heterogeneity or model misspecification.

Three regression models were specified. Model 1 estimated unadjusted associations, including either dietary intake or supplement use, to estimate their independent contributions. Model 2 included both exposures jointly (dietary intake and supplement use) to estimate their joint contributions. Model 3 was fully adjusted for all covariates: age, sex, race and Hispanic origin, below poverty level, education, log-transformed BMI, log-transformed cotinine, log-transformed CRP, log-transformed eGFR, log(METs + 1), and log(drinks/d + 1). Analyses were conducted using SAS software, version 9.4 (SAS Institute), using survey procedures designed to analyze complex survey data.

Although there were statistically significant sex differences in vitamin status, the direction was not consistent across nutrients. Females had higher RBF, VIC, and VIE concentrations, whereas males had higher SFOL, PLP, and VIA concentrations; there were no sex differences for B-12 and 25OHD. Males reported higher dietary intakes for folate, vitamin B-6, vitamin B-12, and calcium; there were no sex differences in intakes of vitamins C, A, and E.

Significant differences were observed between race and Hispanic origin subgroups for all biomarker concentrations and nutrient intakes, with the exception of VIC concentrations. NHW persons generally had the highest biomarker concentrations and intakes, except for vitamin B-12 (biomarker only). For serum PLP, concentrations among NHW persons were significantly higher only when compared with NHB persons.

There was a dose–response relationship of biomarker concentrations across quartiles of vitamin-specific intake from diet alone and total intake (food plus supplements). The increase from the third to the fourth quartile was larger for total intake than for dietary intake alone. For example, total vitamin B-12 intake increased from 9.08 to 28.8 μg/d, compared with an increase from 6.01 to 12.3 μg/d for dietary intake alone, suggesting that individuals in the highest total intake quartile are more likely to be supplement users. In fact, ≥75% of participants in the fourth quartile of total vitamin-specific intake reported use of the corresponding vitamin-specific supplement (data not shown), with the exception of calcium. This pattern was also seen when comparing vitamin-specific supplement users to nonusers, where all biomarker concentrations were significantly higher among supplement users.

After stratifying biomarker concentrations by supplement use, many of the age, sex, and race and Hispanic origin differences observed in the overall analyses differed across strata, indicating heterogeneity by supplement use (Supplementary Table 4; for sample sizes, see Supplementary Table 5). For example, although age-related differences in PLP concentrations were small and not statistically significant in the unstratified analysis (Table 1), after stratifying by supplement use, different age patterns were observed. Specifically, among supplement users, PLP concentrations showed no statistically significant differences across age groups. In contrast, among nonsupplement users, PLP concentrations differed significantly by age and were highest among those aged 20–39 y compared with those aged 40–59 y (P = 0.0002) and ≥60 y (P < 0.0001). Additionally, the magnitude of the change in PLP concentrations between ages 20–39 and ≥60 y differed across the 2 supplement-use strata. These changes in the direction and magnitude of age-related patterns after stratification are suggestive of possible effect modification by supplement use.

Among supplement users, vitamin-specific intake from supplements accounted for an increasing portion of total vitamin intake with age, specifically for vitamins A, E, C, and B-12 (Supplementary Table 6; for sample sizes, see Supplementary Table 7). For example, total vitamin B-12 intake, derived from both supplements and food, more than doubled across age groups among supplement users, increasing from 12.2 μg/d among adults aged 20–39 y to 26.6 μg/d among adults aged ≥60 y, whereas among nonusers, vitamin B-12 intake, derived from food only, did not differ significantly across age groups. This pattern suggests heterogeneity in age-related intake patterns according to supplement-use status when total intake is considered. This is further corroborated by the observation that among supplement users, median vitamin B-12 intake from supplements increased with age (from 5.93 μg/d among adults aged 20–39 y to 24.2 μg/d among those aged ≥60 y), whereas median dietary intake from food alone declined over the same age range (4.64–3.88 μg/d) (Supplementary Table 8). Together, these findings suggest that supplement-derived intake contributes an increasing proportion of total vitamin B-12 intake at older ages. Similar effects can be observed for folate and vitamin B-6 (Supplementary Table 8) as well as fat-soluble vitamins (Supplementary Table 9).

Correlations

The statistically significant Spearman correlations between vitamin biomarkers and intake from diet alone were modest, ranging from 0.12 for B-12 to 0.34 for VIC (Figure 2 and Supplementary Table 10). In contrast, correlations between biomarkers and total intake (food plus supplements) were all statistically significant and substantially stronger, ranging from 0.23 for VIA to 0.53 for PLP and VIC. When stratified by supplement use, differences emerged. Among supplement users, correlations with diet alone were weak and often nonsignificant (e.g., SFOL, RBF, B-12, VIA, and VIE). Among nonsupplement users, correlations with dietary intake were consistently stronger (0.08 for VIE to 0.39 for VIC) and statistically significant, indicating stronger diet-biomarker associations in this group. Pairwise biomarker Spearman correlations showed that the correlations are stronger (∼2× higher) for supplement users compared with nonusers (Figure 3 and Supplementary Table 11). Together, these descriptive differences suggest that the relationship between dietary intake and biomarkers may differ by supplement-use status. Multivariable linear regression models

To further clarify the role of covariates in the intake–biomarker associations, we evaluated sociodemographic, lifestyle, and physiologic variables individually as potential confounders by assessing changes in the exposure β coefficient (|%Δβ| ≥ 10%) after adding each covariate to the unadjusted model. Overall, the pattern of confounding for the dietary intake models varied by nutrient, indicating that no single covariate consistently confounded all intake–biomarker associations. CRP emerged as the only confounder for PLP, sex for vitamin B-12, and cotinine for vitamin C (Table 3 and Figure 4). Age (SFOL, RBF, VIA, and VIE), sex (SFOL, RBF, B-12, VIA, and VIE), race and Hispanic origin (RBF, VIA, VIE, and 25OHD), and cotinine (SFOL, RBF, VIC, and VIE) acted as confounders for multiple biomarkers. However, the specific covariates identified as confounders differed depending on whether the exposure variable was dietary intake or supplement use. For example, sex changed the association between SFOL and folate intake by 23.5% in the dietary intake model compared with only 1.33% in the supplement-use model, whereas age impacted the association by 13.5% in the dietary intake model compared with 9.16% in the supplement-use model (Supplementary Table 12). For models using supplement use as the exposure, the changes in the β coefficients were modest (mostly |%Δβ| < 10%) (FIGURE 4, FIGURE 5; Supplementary Tables 12 and 13). However, in these models, the covariates acted more often as effect modifiers, suggesting that the strength of the association between supplement use and biomarker concentration varied across demographic, lifestyle, and physiologic groups, especially for water-soluble biomarkers (Table 3; Supplementary Tables 14–17). Age, sex, and/or race and Hispanic origin were repeatedly identified as effect modifiers (i.e., interactions with supplement use), except for VIA. In general, more statistically significant pairwise interactions were observed for supplement use than for dietary intake. This suggests that supplement–biomarker associations may vary more across groups defined by age, sex, race, and Hispanic origin, and other sociodemographic, lifestyle, and physiologic factors, whereas dietary intake associations varied across these groups less often (Supplementary Tables 14–17). Further evidence of a potential heterogeneity in associations by supplement use and these covariates is suggested in the direction of the regression coefficients in the full model, even though no explicit interaction term was included. The β coefficients for supplement use changed by ≥23% and, with the exception of folate, reversed direction from positive to negative after full adjustment (Table 4 and Figure 6). For example, PLP shifted from 0.769 log nmol/L (unadjusted model) to –0.708 log nmol/L (fully adjusted model). Across all nutrients, models with supplement use as the exposure explained substantially more variance in biomarker concentrations (model 1 R2) than models with dietary intake. For example, R2 values for supplement use ranged from ∼3% to 21%, compared with ≤9% for models with dietary intake (Table 4). Vitamin A supplements had the least impact on biomarker status (R2 = 3.1%), whereas vitamin C and folate supplements had the greatest (R2 = 15.6% and R2 = 20.9%, respectively). Discussion

This study adds to the body of nutrition research by systematically examining the relationship between 8 vitamin biomarkers and vitamin-specific dietary intake and supplement use, whereas assessing 11 sociodemographic, lifestyle, and physiologic variables for their potential confounding and effect-modifying influence. Our findings suggest that supplement use explains a larger portion of between-person differences in vitamin status than dietary intake, with the magnitude and direction of the associations varying across covariates.

Correlations between dietary intake (food only) and biomarkers were generally modest, consistent with the challenges of 24-h self-reported dietary intake and other factors influencing biomarker levels [25,34]. When supplement use was added to the dietary intake (food plus supplements), correlations strengthened, likely because supplement use introduces a wider range of nutrient exposure than diet alone which can increase the between-person variation in intake. Among supplement users, dietary intake alone is only weakly related to biomarker concentrations, reflecting both the limitations of dietary recall and that dietary intake represents only a portion of total nutrient exposure when supplements are used. In contrast, when supplement users and nonusers are pooled, the large differences in total intake between these groups further increase between-person differences, which can elevate the overall correlations. Therefore, examining dietary intake and supplement use separately provides insight into how well biomarkers reflect diet in the absence of supplements.

Observed demographic differences by age, sex, and race and Hispanic origin in unstratified analyses were partially explained by differences in supplement use, consistent with the presence of confounding found in our modeling analyses. Yet, interaction models also indicated that some of these variables function as effect modifiers of the supplement-use–biomarker associations. In regression analyses, supplement use explained substantially more variance in biomarker concentrations than dietary intake alone (e.g., model 1 R2 ≤21% compared with ≤9%). However, the 2 exposure variables (dietary intake or supplement use) typically explained more variance in biomarker concentrations than most sociodemographic, lifestyle, and physiologic variables, which rarely reached 10%.

Associations between supplement use and biomarkers had larger changes in magnitude and some reversals in direction following covariate adjustment. These direction changes may warrant further evaluation, as they are unlikely evidence of adverse effects of supplement use. Instead, it may be suggestive of the presence of heterogeneity in supplement–biomarker associations across subgroups. In such cases, it may be appropriate to consider whether a main-effects model is mis-specified with respect to supplement status. For vitamin B-6 (PLP) and vitamin D (25OHD), observed changes in direction in β coefficients may reflect physiologic influences, as both biomarkers are impacted by inflammation, kidney function, and BMI, and these factors may differentially modify supplement–biomarker relationships [35,36]. Prior work has shown that circulating β-carotene (a precursor contributing to vitamin A activity), as well as vitamins C and E, are influenced by factors such as smoking, alcohol consumption, obesity, age, and sex [37]. These factors may differentially modify supplement–biomarker relationships.

The change in direction for vitamin B-12, accompanied by a smaller gain in R2 (ΔR2 ∼ 5 percentage points compared with ΔR2 ∼13–20 percentage points for the other biomarkers) and no obvious confounder, is more consistent with confounding by indication, where individuals with poorer health or older age may take supplements yet still have lower biomarker levels, possibly as a result of reduced absorption [38]. On the other hand, the 2 folate biomarkers (SFOL and RBF), while impacted by smoking and kidney function, showed no change in direction and maintained stable positive associations with supplement use across models, suggesting that supplements remain the primary predictor of biomarker variability even after adjustment [39]. This likely reflects a stronger link between folate intake and status and a lower sensitivity of folate biomarkers to physiologic or lifestyle modifiers [40].

In summary, progressively adjusted models illustrate that dietary intake and supplement use contribute differently to intake–biomarker associations. When both exposures are considered simultaneously, supplement use explains a larger share of between-person variability, attenuating the apparent contribution of dietary intake. On the other hand, dietary intake associations tend to be weaker, and the estimated effects change less in magnitude and rarely in direction with additional covariate adjustment. Together, these patterns demonstrate that 24-h dietary intake provides a consistent but weaker prediction of biomarker status, whereas supplement use is a stronger predictor but subject to heterogeneous shifts due to effect modifications across subgroups. These findings underscore the need to consider both confounding and effect modification in nutritional epidemiology and illustrate how diet and supplement use contribute differently to biomarker variation, thereby enhancing confidence in inferences about diet–disease research.

This study has several limitations. Cross-sectional studies, such as NHANES, cannot establish causal relationships, and therefore, the associations should be interpreted as descriptive rather than causal. Our approach reflects how dietary intake is commonly modeled in NHANES using a single 24-h recall. Although this does not capture usual intake and is subject to within-person (day-to-day) random error, it is appropriate for estimating population-level means [25]. This type of error typically attenuates regression associations toward the null rather than introducing systematic bias [41]. Estimating usual intake requires multiple recalls, which can reduce the analytic sample size because not all participants complete both recalls and thus may introduce nonresponse bias. In addition, usual intake estimates are model-based and shrunk toward the mean, resulting in reduced variability that may affect observed associations and make them more difficult to interpret as predictors in a regression model. Given the cross-sectional nature of NHANES and the variability of dietary behavior over time, a single 24-h recall may be limited in capturing consistent exposure for long-term biomarkers, such as RBF, but may be more appropriate for short-term biomarkers, such as VIC and SFOL. Ultimately, both approaches rely on a single summary measure of dietary intake and are therefore limited in their ability to capture the complexity of dietary intake over time. For dietary supplements, product label information is used to estimate nutrient intake, where research has shown that analytically derived values for many nutrients tend to be higher than labeled amounts for multivitamin minerals [42]. Product formulations also change constantly, and although NHANES tries to ensure that the correct product formulation is matched over the data collection period, there may be errors in matching. Lifestyle factors such as alcohol intake and physical activity were also self-reported, introducing the potential for misclassification and recall bias. Although several covariates were modeled as continuous variables on a log-transformed scale, we cannot exclude the possibility that some associations are nonlinear, which may result in model misspecification with respect to those variables and potential attenuation of the estimates. Lastly, the 2003–2006 NHANES cycles represent data collected ∼20 y ago and may not reflect the current US population. However, this time period was selected because it is the most recent 4-y span that included the fullest possible panel of biomarkers for these analyses. More current NHANES cycles either lack several of these biomarkers or do not provide 4 consecutive years of data that can be pooled in a comparable manner.

This study has several strengths. NHANES provides a large, nationally representative sample of the US population, which allows the generalizability of findings. Dietary data were collected using the USDA’s Automated Multiple-Pass Method, a validated system designed to improve recall accuracy and reduce error. Standardized protocols for biospecimen collection and laboratory analyses ensured high-quality biomarker data. The survey also includes detailed demographic, dietary, and lifestyle information, allowing for adjustment of important covariates. Another strength is the use of a clearly defined conceptual framework: by specifying dietary intake and supplement use as the primary exposures and treating other variables as potential confounders within a DAG framework, we provide transparency in the analytic approach. Although the cross-sectional design of NHANES precludes causal inference, the DAG framework formalizes assumptions and aligns with best practices in nutritional epidemiology.

Our findings aid in the understanding of the relationship between nutrient intakes and biomarkers and demonstrate that dietary intake and supplement use need to be considered both separately and in combination when interpreting intake–biomarker relationship.
