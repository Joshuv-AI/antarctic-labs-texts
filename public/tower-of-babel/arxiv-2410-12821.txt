Uniform lower bound of arithmetic Hilbert–Samuel function of hypersurfaces
Chunhui Liu

Abstract
In this article, we give an explicit and uniform lower bound for the arithmetic Hilbert–Samuel function of projective hypersurfaces in the framework of Arakelov geometry. The bound has optimal dependence on the height of the varieties. As an application, we use this lower bound to study the determinant method.



\alttitle

Minoration uniforme de la fonction arithmétiques de Hilbert–Samuel des hypersurfaces

{altabstract}

Dans cet article, on donne une minoration explicite et uniforme de la fonction arithmétique de Hilbert–Samuel des hypersurfaces sous le cadre de la géométrie d’Arakelov, qui a la dépendance optimale de la hauteur de variétés. Comme une application, on applique cette minoration dans l’étude de la méthode de déterminant.

1
Introduction

In this article, we study the arithmetic Hilbert–Samuel function of arithmetic varieties in the framework of Arakelov geometry. More precisely, we give a uniform lower bound for the arithmetic Hilbert–Samuel function of projective hypersurfaces.

1.1
History

Let
X
be a closed subscheme of
\mathbb{P}^{n}_{k}
over the field
k
of dimension
d
, and
L
be an ample line bundle over
X
. We have (cf.
[
25
, Corollary 1.1.25, Theorem 1.2.6]
)

\dim_{k}\left(H^{0}(X,L^{\otimes D})\right)=\frac{\deg\left(c_{1}(L)^{d}\right)}{d!}D^{d}+o(D^{d})

when
D\to\infty
. We call
\dim_{k}\left(H^{0}(X,L^{\otimes D})\right)
the (geometric) Hilbert–Samuel function of
X
with respect to
L
in the variable
D\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
.

One of the central problems in Arakelov geometry is to find an arithmetic analogue of the Hilbert–Samuel function defined above. More precisely, let
K
be a number field,
\mathcal{O}_{K}
be the ring of integers, and
M_{K,\infty}
be the set of infinite places. We suppose that
\pi:\mathscr{X}\rightarrow\spec\mathcal{O}_{K}
is an arithmetic variety of dimension
d+1
, which means that
\mathscr{X}
is integral and the morphism
\pi
is flat and projective. Let
\overline{\mathscr{L}}=\left(\mathscr{L},(\|\raisebox{1.72218pt}{.}\|_{v})_{v\in M_{K,\infty}}\right)
be a Hermitian line bundle over
\mathscr{X}
(on the generic fiber). Let

\widehat{h}^{0}(\mathscr{X},\overline{\mathscr{L}}^{\otimes D})=\log\#\left\{s\in H^{0}(\mathscr{X},\mathscr{L}^{\otimes D})\mid\|s\|_{\sup,v}\leqslant 1,\forall\;v\in M_{K,\infty}\right\},

where
\|s\|_{\sup,v}=\sup\limits_{x\in\mathscr{X}(\mathbb{C}_{v})}\|s(x)\|_{v}
for all
v\in M_{K,\infty}
. Or equivalently, we consider
H^{0}(\mathscr{X},\mathscr{L}^{\otimes D})
as a normed vector bundle over
\spec\mathcal{O}_{K}
equipped with some induced norms, and we consider
\widehat{\deg}_{n}\left(\overline{H^{0}(\mathscr{X},\mathscr{L}^{\otimes D})}\right)
as a function of the variable
D\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
. We call it the
arithmetic Hilbert–Samuel function
of
\left(\mathscr{X},\overline{\mathscr{L}}\right)
.

Motivated by the geometric case, we expect an asymptotic expansion

\widehat{h}^{0}(\mathscr{X},\overline{\mathscr{L}}^{\otimes D})=\frac{\widehat{\vol}(\overline{\mathscr{L}})}{(d+1)!}D^{d+1}+o(D^{d+1})

for some
\widehat{\vol}(\overline{\mathscr{L}})\in\mathbb{R}_{\geqslant 0}
when
D\to\infty
, or at least it is valid for some special cases, and some reported results on this topic will be introduced below. In general, we have
\widehat{\vol}(\overline{\mathscr{L}})\geqslant\widehat{\deg}\left(\widehat{c}_{1}(\overline{\mathscr{L}})^{d+1}\right)
, and the equality holds in some special cases

Apart from asymptotic properties, uniform bounds of
\widehat{h}^{0}(\mathscr{X},\overline{\mathscr{L}}^{\otimes D})
for all
D\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
are also of interest.

1.1.1

Gillet and Soulé proved such an asymptotic result (
[
21
, Theorem 8]
) for a Hermitian line ample bundle as a consequence of their arithmetic Riemann–Roch theorem
[
21
, Theorem 7]
under the assumption that
\mathscr{X}
has a regular generic fiber. In
[
1
, Théorème principal]
, Abbes and Bouche proved the same result without using the arithmetic Riemann–Roch theorem, instead they applied some tools of asymptotic analysis of Demailly. In
[
45
, Theorem 1.4]
, S. Zhang proved this result without the condition of smooth generic fiber by some technique of analytic torsion. Later, Randriambololona generalized this result to the case of coherent sheaf induced from a sub-quotient of the normed vector bundle in
[
34
, Théorème A]
. X. Yuan considers this topic in
[
42
]
for the case where
\overline{\mathscr{L}}
is arithmetically big.

In
[
33
]
, Philippon and Sombra proposed another definition of the arithmetic Hilbert–Samuel function, and they proved an asymptotic formula for the case of toric varieties (see
[
33
, Théorème 0.1]
). Hajli proved the same asymptotic formula for general projective varieties in
[
22
]
using the above formulation.

1.1.2

Uniform bounds of the arithmetic Hilbert–Samuel function of arithmetic varieties are also an important topic. In
[
43
]
, X. Yuan and T. Zhang considered such a uniform upper bound for the case of arithmetic surface, and in
[
44
]
for the general case. H. Chen studied the uniform upper bound in
[
13
]
by the
\mathbb{R}
-filtration method of graded linear series. Compared with the asymptotic estimate, these results have the optimal dependence on the height of varieties.

1.1.3

The uniform lower bound seems more difficult to study. In
[
14
]
, David and Philippon gave an explicit uniform lower bound of the arithmetic Hilbert–Samuel function. This result was reformulated by H. Chen in
[
11
, Theorem 4.8]
for a study of the determinant method of Arakelov formulation. In fact, let
\mathscr{X}\rightarrow\spec\mathcal{O}_{K}
be an arithmetic variety, and
\overline{\mathscr{L}}
be an ample Hermitian line bundle over
\mathscr{X}
which determines a polarization in
\mathbb{P}^{n}_{\mathcal{O}_{K}}
such that
\deg\left(\mathscr{X}\times_{\spec\mathcal{O}_{K}}\spec K\right)=\delta
as a closed subscheme of
\mathbb{P}^{n}_{K}
. We denote by
\overline{F}_{D}
a particular normed sub-vector bundle of
\overline{H^{0}(\mathscr{X},\mathscr{L}^{\otimes D})}
over
\spec\mathcal{O}_{K}
(see §
2.3.5
for the precise definition original from
[
11
, §2.3]
), which equals
H^{0}(\mathscr{X},\mathscr{L}^{\otimes D})
when
D
is sufficiently large. In some cases, the Arakelov degree
\widehat{\deg}(\overline{F}_{D})
can play the role of the number of small sections in
H^{0}(\mathscr{X},\mathscr{L}^{\otimes D})
in the study of arithmetic Hilbert–Samuel functions, since

\lim_{D\to\infty}\frac{\widehat{\deg}(\overline{F}_{D})}{[K:\mathbb{Q}]D^{d+1}/(d+1)!}=\widehat{c}_{1}(\overline{\mathscr{L}}^{d+1})

is valid in this case by
[
34
, Théorème A]
.

We regard the slope
\widehat{\mu}(\overline{F}_{D})
as the arithmetic Hilbert–Samuel function of the above
\mathscr{X}
of the variable
D\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
. In the above literatures, the inequality

\frac{\widehat{\mu}(\overline{F}_{D})}{D}\geqslant\frac{d!}{\delta(2d+2)^{d+1}}h_{\overline{\mathscr{L}}}(\mathscr{X})-\log(n+1)-2^{d}

holds uniformly for all
D\geqslant 2(n-d)(\delta-1)+d+2
, where
h_{\overline{\mathscr{L}}}(\mathscr{X})
is the height of
\mathscr{X}
defined by the arithmetic intersection theory
[
20
]
.

However, this estimate is far from optimal; by this we mean that the lower bound does not have the optimal dependence on the height
h_{\overline{\mathscr{L}}}(\mathscr{X})
compared with that in the asymptotic formula.

1.2
Main result

In this article, we will give a lower bound of the arithmetic Hilbert–Samuel function of projective hypersurfaces with optimal dependence on the height of the arithmetic varieties.

1.2.1

Let
K
be a number field,
\overline{\mathcal{E}}
be a Hermitian vector bundle over
\spec\mathcal{O}_{K}
of rank
n+1
, and
\overline{\mathcal{O}(1)}
be the universal bundle of
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E})
equipped with the corresponding Fubini–Study norms for each
v\in M_{K,\infty}
. Let
X
be a hypersurface of degree
\delta
in
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}_{K})
, and
\mathscr{X}
be its Zariski closure in
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E})
. Let
\overline{F}_{D}
be the Hermitian vector bundle defined in §
1.1.2
, and
\widehat{\mu}(\overline{F}_{D})
be the arithmetic Hilbert–Samuel function of the variable
D\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
.

We prove the following result in §
5.2.5
; see (
26
).

\theoname

\the\smf@thm
.

We keep all the above notations, and we choose
\overline{\mathcal{E}}
as the one defined in §
5.2.5
. Then the inequality

\frac{\widehat{\mu}(\overline{F}_{D})}{D}\geqslant\frac{{n+D-\delta\choose n}}{D\left({n+D\choose n}-{n+D-\delta\choose n}\right)}h(X)+C_{1}(n)

holds uniformly for all
D\geqslant\delta
, where
h(X)
is a logarithmic height of
X
(Definition
5.2.1
), and the constants
C_{1}(n)
depending on
n
will be given explicitly in §
5.2.5
.

A direct calculation gives

\frac{{n+D-\delta\choose n}}{D\left({n+D\choose n}-{n+D-\delta\choose n}\right)}=\frac{1}{n\delta}+O(D^{-1}),

and we will give an explicit estimate of the remainder in Lemma
5.3.1
.

1.2.2

Compared with the asymptotic result,
1/n\delta
gives the optimal dependence on the height of
X
. Since can compare the relevant heights of
X
explicitly (see
[
6
, §3,§4]
,
[
24
, §B.7]
,
[
11
, Proposition 3.6]
,
[
26
]
, and Proposition
5.2.2
for the comparison), the choice of which height of
X
to use in the statement of Theorem
1.2.1
is not important if we do not care about the constant
B_{1}(n)
in that theorem.

In Theorem
1.2.1
, we consider a special case, but we obtain the optimal dependence on the height of varieties, which improves upon those in
[
14
]
and
[
11
, Theorem 4.8]
.

In
[
31
, Proposition 3.8]
, for a Hermitian line bundle
\overline{\mathcal{L}}
on an arithmetic variety
\mathscr{X}\rightarrow\spec\mathcal{O}_{K}
under certain conditions, the author proved the following asymptotic estimate as
D\to\infty
:

\widehat{\deg}_{n}(\overline{F}_{D})=\frac{\overline{\mathcal{L}}^{d+1}}{(d+1)!}D^{d+1}-\frac{\mathcal{L}^{d}}{4(d-1)!}D^{d}\log D-\frac{\overline{\mathcal{L}}^{d}\cdot\overline{K_{\mathscr{X}/\mathcal{O}_{K}}}}{2d!}D^{d}+o(D^{d}),

where
\overline{K_{\mathscr{X}/\mathcal{O}_{K}}}
is the canonical bundle equipped with some induced norms. This suggests that there may be further dependence on the height of
X
, rather than the asymptotic one
\displaystyle\frac{h(X)}{n\delta}
in the uniform lower bound provided above.

Theorem
1.2.1
also holds when
X
is a hypersurface in a linear subspace of
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}_{K})
; see §
5.2.5
for details.

1.3
An application in counting rational points

The determinant method is one of the main methods for counting rational points of bounded height. It was introduced by Bombieri and Pila
[
3
]
and generalized by Heath-Brown
[
23
]
to higher dimensions. Its key ingredient is the construction of several auxiliary hypersurfaces in
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}_{K})
which cover all rational points of bounded height in
X
but do not contain the generic point of
X
. One then optimizes the number and the maximal degree of these hypersurfaces.

The determinant method can be formulated in the framework of Arakelov geometry, where some estimates are from a calculation of determinants appearing in the application of Siegel’s lemma, which is replaced by the evaluation map in the slope method of Arakelov geometry
[
4
]
; see
[
12
, Theorem 3.1]
,
[
27
, Theorem 3.1]
and
[
28
]
for more details on this formulation.

If we study the Arakelov formulation of the determinant method, the uniform lower bound of the arithmetic Hilbert–Samuel function will be in a significant role.

1.3.1

Applying Theorem
1.2.1
to
[
11
, Proposition 2.12]
, we obtain the following result.

\theoname

\the\smf@thm
.

Let
K
be a number field,
X
be an integral hypersurface in
\mathbb{P}^{n}_{K}
of degree
\delta
, and
H_{K}(X)
be the absolute height of
X
(see Definition
5.2.1
). Let

be the set of rational points of

whose heights (see Definition

. For every

, if

then

can be covered by one hypersurface of degree smaller than or equal to

\left[\frac{(n+1)(\delta-1)(n\delta+\epsilon)}{2\epsilon}\right]+1,

which does not contain the generic point of

.

The implicit constant depending on

and

in Theorem

These are useful in the determinant method (see

for the classic version and
[

for the approach of Arakelov geometry). In

1.4
Organization of article

This article is organized as follows: in §
2
, we provide the basic setting of the whole problem. In §
3
, we provide an estimate of the Arakelov degree of the global section space of projective spaces equipped with some particular norms. In §
4
, we compare the norm on the global section spaces, and we apply these comparison results to a short exact sequence of global section spaces to obtain a lower bound of arithmetic Hilbert–Samuel function of hypersurface. In §
5
, we compare some height functions of arithmetic varieties, and apply the above lower bound to improve some estimates in the determinant method of Arakelov formulation.

In Appendix

[
18

.

2
Fundamental settings

In this section, we will provide the fundamental settings of this subject. Let
K
be a number field, and
\mathcal{O}_{K}
be its ring of integers. We denote by
M_{K,f}
the set of finite places of
K
, and by
M_{K,\infty}
the set of infinite places of
K
. In addition, we denote by
M_{K}=M_{K,f}\sqcup M_{K,\infty}
the set of places of
K
.

For every
v\in M_{K}
, we define the absolute value

|x|_{v}=\left|N_{K_{v}/\mathbb{Q}_{v}}(x)\right|_{v}^{\frac{1}{[K_{v}:\mathbb{Q}_{v}]}}

for each
v\in M_{K}
, which extends the usual absolute values on
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
or
\mathbb{R}
.

With all the above notations, we have the product formula (cf.
[
30
, Chap. III, Proposition 1.3]
)

\prod_{v\in M_{K}}|x|_{v}^{[K_{v}:\mathbb{Q}_{v}]}=1

(1)

for all
x\in K^{\times}
.

2.1
Normed vector bundles

First we introduce the notion of a normed vector bundle.

2.1.1

A
normed vector bundle
over
\spec\mathcal{O}_{K}
is a pair
\overline{E}=\left(E,\left(\|\raisebox{1.72218pt}{.}\|_{v}\right)_{v\in M_{K,\infty}}\right)
, where:

•

E
is a projective
\mathcal{O}_{K}
-module of finite rank;

•

\left(\|\raisebox{1.72218pt}{.}\|_{v}\right)_{v\in M_{K,\infty}}
is a family of norms, where
\|\raisebox{1.72218pt}{.}\|_{v}
is a norm over
E\otimes_{\mathcal{O}_{K},v}\mathbb{C}
invariant under the action of
\gal(\mathbb{C}/K_{v})
.

For a normed vector bundle
\overline{E}
over
\spec\mathcal{O}_{K}
, we define the
rank
of
\overline{E}
as that of
E
, denoted by
\rg_{\mathcal{O}_{K}}(E)
or
\rg(E)
if there is no ambiguity.

If for every
v\in M_{K,\infty}
, the norm
\|\raisebox{1.72218pt}{.}\|_{v}
is Hermitian, i.e. induced by an inner product, we call
\overline{E}
a
Hermitian vector bundle
over
\spec\mathcal{O}_{K}
. If
\rg_{\mathcal{O}_{K}}(E)=1
, we say that
\overline{E}
is a
Hermitian line bundle
over
\spec\mathcal{O}_{K}
.

2.1.2

Let
E
be a
\mathcal{O}_{K}
-module, and
F
be a sub-
\mathcal{O}_{K}
-module of
E
. We say that
F
is a
saturated
sub-
\mathcal{O}_{K}
-module if
E/F
is a torsion-free
\mathcal{O}_{K}
-module.

Let
\overline{E}=\left(E,\left(\|\raisebox{1.72218pt}{.}\|_{E,v}\right)_{v\in M_{K,\infty}}\right)
and
\overline{F}=\left(F,\left(\|\raisebox{1.72218pt}{.}\|_{F,v}\right)_{v\in M_{K,\infty}}\right)
be two normed vector bundles over
\spec\mathcal{O}_{K}
. If
F
is a saturated sub-
\mathcal{O}_{K}
-module of
E
and
\|\raisebox{1.72218pt}{.}\|_{F,v}
is the restriction of
\|\raisebox{1.72218pt}{.}\|_{E,v}
over
F\otimes_{\mathcal{O}_{K},v}\mathbb{C}
for every
v\in M_{K,\infty}
, we say that
\overline{F}
is a
Hermitian subbundle
of
\overline{E}
over
\spec\mathcal{O}_{K}
.

We say that
\overline{G}=\left(G,\left(\|\raisebox{1.72218pt}{.}\|_{G,v}\right)_{v\in M_{K,\infty}}\right)
is a
quotient normed vector bundle
of
\overline{E}
over
\spec\mathcal{O}_{K}
, if the module
G
is a projective quotient of
E
and
\|\raisebox{1.72218pt}{.}\|_{G,v}
is the induced quotient space norm of
\|\raisebox{1.72218pt}{.}\|_{E,v}
for every
v\in M_{K,\infty}
.

2.1.3

Let
\overline{E}
be a normed vector bundle over
\spec\mathcal{O}_{K}
,
\mathfrak{p}\in\spm\mathcal{O}_{K}
corresponding to a place
v\in M_{K,f}
. For all
x\in E\otimes_{\mathcal{O}_{K}}K_{\mathfrak{p}}
, we define

\displaystyle\|x\|_{v}=\|x\|_{E,\mathfrak{p}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\inf\{|a|_{\mathfrak{p}}\mid a\in K_{\mathfrak{p}}^{\times},\;a^{-1}x\in E\otimes_{\mathcal{O}_{K}}\widehat{\mathcal{O}}_{K,\mathfrak{p}}\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\inf\{|a|_{\mathfrak{p}}\mid a\in K^{\times},\;a^{-1}x\in E\},

where the last equality is valid for
x\in E\otimes_{\mathcal{O}_{K}}K
. This is called the
norm given by model
at
\mathfrak{p}\in\spm\mathcal{O}_{K}
.

2.1.4

Let

\begin{CD}0@>{}>{}>F@>{}>{}>E@>{}>{}>G@>{}>{}>0\end{CD}

be an exact sequence of
\mathcal{O}_{K}
-modules. If for all
v\in M_{K,\infty}
,
\overline{F}
is equipped with the subspace norms of
\overline{E}
, and
\overline{G}
is equipped with the quotient norms of
\overline{E}
, then we say that

\begin{CD}0@>{}>{}>\overline{F}@>{}>{}>\overline{E}@>{}>{}>\overline{G}@>{}>{}>0\end{CD}

is an exact sequence of normed vector bundles over
\spec\mathcal{O}_{K}
, and we denote
\overline{G}=\overline{E}/\overline{F}
.

For simplicity, we denote by
E_{K}=E\otimes_{\mathcal{O}_{K}}K
below.

2.2
Some Arakelov invariants

In this part we introduce the definitions and properties of some invariants in Arakelov geometry.

2.2.1

Let
\overline{E}
be a normed vector bundle over
\spec\mathcal{O}_{K}
, and
s_{1},\ldots,s_{r}
be a
K
-basis of
E_{K}
. We define the
Arakelov degree
of
\overline{E}
as

\widehat{\deg}(\overline{E})=-\sum_{v\in M_{K}}[K_{v}:\mathbb{Q}_{v}]\log\|s_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge s_{r}\|_{v},

where

\|s_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge s_{r}\|_{v}=\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}e_{1},\ldots,e_{r}\in E_{K}\\
e_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge e_{r}=s_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge s_{r}\end{subarray}}\|e_{1}\|_{v}\cdots\|e_{r}\|_{v}

for each
v\in M_{K,\infty}
. The Arakelov degree of a normed vector bundle is independent of the choice of the basis
\{s_{1},\ldots,s_{r}\}
by the product formula.

In particular, if
\overline{E}
is a Hermitian vector bundle over
\spec\mathcal{O}_{K}
, and
\{s_{1},\ldots,s_{r}\}
is a
K
-basis of
E_{K}
, then we have

\displaystyle\widehat{\deg}(\overline{E})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\sum_{v\in M_{K}}[K_{v}:\mathbb{Q}_{v}]\log\left\|s_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge s_{r}\right\|_{v}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\log\left(\#\left(E/\mathcal{O}_{K}s_{1}+\cdots+\mathcal{O}_{K}s_{r}\right)\right)-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{v\in M_{K,\infty}}\log\det\left(\langle s_{i},s_{j}\rangle_{1\leqslant i,j\leqslant r,v}\right),

where
\langle\raisebox{1.72218pt}{.},\raisebox{1.72218pt}{.}\rangle_{v}
is the inner product which induces the norm
\|\raisebox{1.72218pt}{.}\|_{v}
for
v\in M_{K,\infty}
, and
\langle s_{i},s_{j}\rangle_{1\leqslant i,j\leqslant r,v}
is the Gram matrix of the basis
\{s_{1},\ldots,s_{r}\}
.

We refer the readers to
[
19
, §2.4.1]
for a proof of the equivalence of the above two definitions.

We define

\widehat{\deg}_{n}(\overline{E})=\frac{1}{[K:\mathbb{Q}]}\widehat{\deg}(\overline{E})

as the
normalized Arakelov degree
of
\overline{E}
, which is invariant under the base-field extension.

2.2.2

Let
\overline{E}
be a normed vector bundle over
\spec\mathcal{O}_{K}
, and
s\in E_{K}
be a nonzero element. We define

\widehat{\deg}(s)=-\sum_{v\in M_{K}}[K_{v}:\mathbb{Q}_{v}]\log\|s\|_{v},\text{ and }\widehat{\deg}_{n}(s)=\frac{\widehat{\deg}(s)}{[K:\mathbb{Q}]}.

2.2.3

Let
\overline{E}
be a nonzero Hermitian vector bundle over
\spec\mathcal{O}_{K}
, and
\rg(E)
be the rank of
E
. The
slope
of
\overline{E}
is defined as

\widehat{\mu}(\overline{E}):=\frac{1}{\rg(E)}\widehat{\deg}_{n}(\overline{E}).

In addition, we denote by
\widehat{\mu}_{\max}(\overline{E})

Let

2.3.1

For every

(5)

Next, we will introduce the so-called

[

for a systematic introduction of this notion. In general, for a given symmetric convex body

For the

By the estimate (

\widehat{\mu}(\overline{E})-\frac{1}{2}\log r\leqslant\widehat{\mu}(\overline{E}_{J})\leqslant\widehat{\mu}(\overline{E}),

\propname

\the\smf@thm
.

Let
(E,\|\raisebox{1.72218pt}{.}\|)
be a Hermitian complex vector space of dimension
n+1
. Under a unitary identification
E\cong\mathbb{C}^{n+1}
, a homogeneous polynomial

f=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(i_{0},\ldots,i_{n})\in\mathbb{N}^{n+1}\\
i_{0}+\cdots+i_{n}=D\end{subarray}}a_{i_{0}\ldots i_{n}}T_{0}^{i_{0}}\cdots T_{n}^{i_{n}}=\sum_{|I|=D}a_{I}T^{I}

has norm

\|f\|_{\sym}^{2}=\sum_{|i|=D}\frac{I!}{D!}|a_{I}|^{2},

where
I=(i_{0},\ldots,i_{n})
,
I!=i_{0}!\cdots i_{n}!
. Under this identification, multiplication by a homogeneous polynomial is ordinary polynomial multiplication.

Proof.

We choose an orthonormal basis
e_{0},\ldots,e_{n}
of
E
. For a multi-index
I=(i_{0},\ldots,i_{n})
with
|I|=D
, let
u_{I}
be the average of the
D!
tensor words obtained from a word with
i_{k}
copies of
e_{k}
. Distinct

are orthogonal, and each distinct word occurs
I!
times in the average. Since there are

distinct choices, then

\|u_{I}\|^{2}=\frac{D!}{I!}\left(\frac{I!}{D!}\right)^{2}=\frac{I!}{D!}.

The quotient norm is the norm of the orthogonal projection onto the symmetric subspace. Hence the displayed formula follows. Concatenation of tensors induces multiplication of homogeneous polynomials, which proves the final assertion.
∎

4.2.1

We keep the above notation and equip

S^{2n+1}=\{z\in\mathbb{C}^{n+1}\mid\|z\|_{2}=1\}

with normalized
U(n+1)
-invariant probability measure
\sigma
. We have the following property.

\lemmname

\the\smf@thm
.

Let
n
be a nonnegative integer. For multi-indices
I=(i_{0},\ldots,i_{n}),J=(j_{0},\ldots,j_{n})\in\mathbb{N}^{n+1}
, write
|I|=\sum\limits_{k}i_{k}
,
I!=\prod\limits_{k}i_{k}!
, and
z^{I}=\prod\limits_{k}z_{k}^{i_{k}}
. Then we have

\int_{S^{2n+1}}z^{I}\overline{z^{J}}\,\mathrm{d}\sigma=\begin{cases}0,&I\neq J,\\[5.69054pt]
\dfrac{I!\,n!}{(|I|+n)!},&I=J.\end{cases}

Proof.

We put
q=n+1
for simplicity. A product of one-dimensional complex Gaussian integrals gives

\int_{\mathbb{C}^{q}}z^{I}\overline{z^{J}}\exp(-\|z\|_{2}^{2})\,\mathrm{d}V=\begin{cases}0,&I\neq J,\\
\pi^{q}I!,&I=J.\end{cases}

Indeed, polar coordinates in one complex variable give

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{C}}|z|^{2a}e^{-|z|^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}A=\pi a!
, while the angular integral vanishes for unequal exponents.

Write
z=\rho x
, with
\rho>0
and
x\in S^{2n+1}
. Since the sphere has surface area
2\pi^{q}/n!
, we have

\mathrm{d}V=\frac{2\pi^{q}}{n!}\rho^{2q-1}\,\mathrm{d}\rho\,\mathrm{d}\sigma(x).

Therefore

\int_{\mathbb{C}^{q}}z^{I}\overline{z^{J}}e^{-\|z\|_{2}^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}V=\frac{2\pi^{q}}{n!}\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}\rho^{|I|+|J|+2q-1}e^{-\rho^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}\rho\right)\left(\int_{S}x^{I}\overline{x^{J}}\,\mathrm{d}\sigma\right).

When
I\neq J
, independent coordinate phase rotations also force the sphere integral to vanish. When
I=J
, the radial integral is
(|I|+n)!/2
. Equating the two Gaussian evaluations gives the stated formula.
∎

\lemmname

\the\smf@thm

(Lemma 4.3.6,
[
6
]
)

Let

be a nonnegative integer and

If

then

\|f\|_{L^{2}(\sigma)}^{2}=\binom{n+m}{n}^{-1}\|f\|_{\sym}^{2}.

\lemmname

\the\smf@thm
.

We keep all the notations in Lemma

and an index

4.2.2

We begin with the following continuous Cauchy–Binet identity, generally called
Andréief identity
. We give a proof of the version used later.

\propname

\the\smf@thm
.

Let
(X,\sigma)
be a probability space, let
r\geqslant 1
,
e_{1},\ldots,e_{r}\in L^{2}(\sigma)
, and the function
w:X\to[0,\infty)
be bounded and measurable. Set the matrix
A=(A_{ij})_{1\leqslant i,j\leqslant r}
, where

A_{ij}=\int_{X}w(x)e_{i}(x)\overline{e_{j}(x)}\,\mathrm{d}\sigma(x),\text{ and }\Delta(x_{1},\ldots,x_{r})=\det(e_{i}(x_{j}))_{1\leqslant i,j\leqslant r}.

Then

\det A=\frac{1}{r!}\int_{X^{r}}|\Delta(x_{1},\ldots,x_{r})|^{2}\prod_{k=1}^{r}w(x_{k})\,\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\otimes r}.

Proof.

Cauchy–Schwarz inequality shows that every
e_{i}\overline{e_{j}}
is integrable, so boundedness of
w
permits Fubini’s theorem. The Leibniz formula gives

\det A=\sum_{\rho\in\mathfrak{S}_{r}}\operatorname{sgn}(\rho)I_{\rho},

where

I_{\rho}=\int_{X^{r}}\prod_{i=1}^{r}w(x_{i})e_{i}(x_{i})\overline{e_{\rho(i)}(x_{i})}\,\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\otimes r}.

On the other hand, we have

|\Delta(x_{1},\ldots,x_{r})|^{2}=\sum_{\tau,\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{r}}\operatorname{sgn}(\tau)\operatorname{sgn}(\pi)\prod_{j=1}^{r}e_{\tau(j)}(x_{j})\overline{e_{\pi(j)}(x_{j})}.

For fixed
(\tau,\pi)
, relabel
y_{i}=x_{\tau^{-1}(i)}
. The corresponding integral is
I_{\pi\circ\tau^{-1}}
, and

\operatorname{sgn}(\tau)\operatorname{sgn}(\pi)=\operatorname{sgn}(\pi\circ\tau^{-1}).

For each
\rho\in\mathfrak{S}_{r}
, there are exactly
r!
pairs satisfying
\pi\circ\tau^{-1}=\rho
. Thus the integral on the right is
r!\det A
, proving the identity.
∎

Using the Andréief identity, we obtain the following result.

\lemmname

\the\smf@thm
.

We keep the above notation. Let
e_{1},\ldots,e_{r}
be orthonormal in
L^{2}(\sigma)
, and we put

\Delta(x_{1},\ldots,x_{r})=\det(e_{i}(x_{j})).

Define a measure
P
on
X^{r}
by

\mathrm{d}P=\frac{1}{r!}|\Delta|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\otimes r}.

Then
P
is a probability measure, and its
k
-th coordinate marginal has density

\frac{1}{r}B(x)\text{, and }B(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{r}|e_{i}(x)|^{2}.

In particular, if
B=r
almost everywhere, every coordinate marginal is
\sigma
.

Proof.

Proposition
4.2.2
with
w\equiv 1
gives

\frac{1}{r!}\int_{X^{r}}|\Delta|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\otimes r}=1,

since the unweighted Gram matrix is the identity. Thus
P
has total mass one.

For a bounded measurable function
\varphi
, expand
|\Delta|^{2}
and integrate in
x_{2},\ldots,x_{r}
. The orthonormality gives

\int_{X}e_{\tau(j)}(x_{j})\overline{e_{\pi(j)}(x_{j})}\,\mathrm{d}\sigma(x_{j})=\delta_{\tau(j),\pi(j)},\ j\geqslant 2,

where
\delta_{i,j}
is the usual Kronecker symbol. A term survives only when
\tau(j)=\pi(j)
for all
j\geqslant 2
, which forces
\tau=\pi
. The surviving contribution is

(r-1)!\sum_{i=1}^{r}|e_{i}(x_{1})|^{2}=(r-1)!B(x_{1}).

After multiplying by
1/r!
, the first marginal has density
B/r
. Simultaneous coordinate permutations preserve
|\Delta|^{2}\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\otimes r}
, so all coordinate marginals agree.
∎

\propname

\the\smf@thm
.

Let
e_{1},\ldots,e_{r}
be orthonormal in
L^{2}(\sigma)
. Suppose

\sum_{i=1}^{r}|e_{i}(x)|^{2}=r\quad\text{for almost every }x,

and let
w\geqslant 0
be bounded with
\log w\in L^{1}(\sigma)
, and
T_{w}=((T_{w})_{ij})_{1\leqslant i,j\leqslant r}
be the matrix, where

(T_{w})_{ij}=\int_{X}w(x)e_{i}(x)\overline{e_{j}(x)}\,\mathrm{d}\sigma(x).

Then we have

\det T_{w}\geqslant\exp\left(r\int_{X}\log w\,\mathrm{d}\sigma\right).

Proof.

For
\epsilon>0
, let
w_{\epsilon}=w+\epsilon
. By Proposition
4.2.2
and Lemma
4.2.2
, we have

\det T_{w_{\epsilon}}=\int_{X^{r}}\prod_{k=1}^{r}w_{\epsilon}(x_{k})\,\mathrm{d}P.

The function
\log(\raisebox{1.72218pt}{.})
is concave, so Jensen’s inequality and the fact that every coordinate marginal of
P
is
\sigma
give

\log\det T_{w_{\epsilon}}\geqslant\int_{X^{r}}\sum_{k=1}^{r}\log w_{\epsilon}(x_{k})\,\mathrm{d}P=r\int_{X}\log(w+\epsilon)\,\mathrm{d}\sigma.

As
\epsilon
decreases to zero,
T_{w_{\epsilon}}
converges to
T_{w}
. The positive parts of
\log(w+\epsilon)
are uniformly bounded, while the negative parts increase monotonically to the negative part of
\log w

\log\det T_{w}\geqslant r\int_{X}\log w\,\mathrm{d}\sigma.

∎

In this part, we compare the spherical Mahler measure with the supremum norm.

\lemmname

5
An application in the determinant method

We have given a uniform explicit estimate of the arithmetic Hilbert–Samuel function of a projective hypersurface in Proposition
4.3.2
. As an application, suppose first that
X
is an integral closed subscheme of
\mathbb{P}^{n}_{K}
. We then construct some hypersurfaces in
\mathbb{P}^{n}_{K}
with bounded degree which cover rational points of bounded height in
X
but do not contain the generic point of
X
. We then optimize the number and the maximal degree of these auxiliary hypersurfaces. This is the key ingredient of the
determinant method
.

In the Arakelov-geometric formulation
[
11
,
12
,
27
,
28
]
, a better uniform lower bound of the arithmetic Hilbert–Samuel function is useful for improving estimates in the determinant method, which is significant in the study of uniform upper bounds of rational points with bounded height in arithmetic varieties.

Since we have obtained a uniform lower bound in the hypersurfaces case in §
4
, it will be useful in the application of the determinant method in this case. To this end, we need to compare some heights of
X
.

5.1

\definame

The height

Using Definition

of the point

For a subscheme

By Northcott property (cf.
[

), the cardinality

.

5.2

5.2.1

5.2.2

\propname

\the\smf@thm
.

Let
\overline{\mathcal{E}}
be the Hermitian vector bundle over
\spec\mathcal{O}_{K}
of rank

Proof.

By definition, it suffices to consider the contributions from all
v\in M_{K,\infty}

Upper bound.
– By the equality (
8

2.3.4

\log\|s\|_{J,v}=\log\|s\|_{\sym,v}+R_{0}(n,\delta),

where the constant

where

. In addition, we have the inequality

\|s\|_{\sup,v}\leqslant\|s\|_{J,v}\leqslant\sqrt{r(n,\delta)}\|s\|_{\sup,v}

by the equality (
7

2.3.2

By definition, for

\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}_{K})

To obtain an upper bound for

, suppose that

is defined by the nonzero homogeneous equation

\sup\limits_{x\in\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}_{K})(\mathbb{C}_{v})}\frac{|v(f)(x)|_{v}}{|v(x)|_{v}^{\delta}}\leqslant{n+\delta\choose\delta}\max\limits_{\begin{subarray}{x}(i_{0},\ldots,i_{n})\in\mathbb{N}^{n+1}\\

where we used the estimate

in the last inequality.

Lower bound.
– For every
v\in M_{K,\infty}

i_{0}+\cdots+i_{n}=\delta\end{subarray}}\{|a_{i_{0},\ldots,i_{n}}|_{v}\}.

\frac{1}{(2\pi i)^{n+1}}\int_{|z_{0}|_{v}=\cdots=|z_{n}|_{v}=1}f(z_{0},\ldots,z_{n})z_{0}^{-\alpha_{0}-1}\cdots z_{n}^{-\alpha_{n}-1}\mathrm{d}z_{0}\cdots\mathrm{d}z_{n}=a_{\alpha_{0},\ldots,\alpha_{n}}.

Thus

\displaystyle|a_{\alpha_{0},\ldots,\alpha_{n}}|_{v}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left|\frac{1}{(2\pi i)^{n+1}}\int_{|z_{0}|_{v}=\cdots=|z_{n}|_{v}=1}f(z_{0},\ldots,z_{n})z_{0}^{-\alpha_{0}-1}\cdots z_{n}^{-\alpha_{n}-1}\mathrm{d}z_{0}\cdots\mathrm{d}z_{n}\right|_{v}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left|\int_{[0,1]^{n+1}}f(e^{2\pi it_{0}},\ldots,e^{2\pi it_{n}})e^{-2\pi it_{0}\alpha_{0}}\cdots e^{-2\pi it_{n}\alpha_{n}}\mathrm{d}t_{0}\cdots\mathrm{d}t_{n}\right|_{v}

\displaystyle\int_{[0,1]^{n+1}}\left|f(e^{2\pi it_{0}},\ldots,e^{2\pi it_{n}})\right|_{v}\mathrm{d}t_{0}\cdots\mathrm{d}t_{n}

\displaystyle\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in\mathbb{C}_{v}^{n+1}\\
|x|_{v}\leqslant 1\end{subarray}}\left|f(x)\right|_{v}

\displaystyle\sup\limits_{x\in\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}_{K})(\mathbb{C}_{v})}\frac{\left|f(x)\right|_{v}}{\left|x\right|^{\delta}_{v}}.

Then we have

\log\|f\|_{\sym,v}\geqslant\max\limits_{\begin{subarray}{x}(i_{0},\ldots,i_{n})\in\mathbb{N}^{n+1}\\
i_{0}+\cdots+i_{n}=\delta\end{subarray}}\log\{|a_{i_{0},\ldots,i_{n}}|_{v}\}-\frac{1}{2}\log r(n,\delta)

∎

To prove Theorem
5.3.1
, we first prove the following numerical lemma.

\lemmname

\the\smf@thm
.

Let
r(n,D)={n+D\choose n}
,
r_{1}(n,D)=r(n,D)-r(n,D-\delta)
, and

R(D)=\frac{r(n,D-\delta)}{Dr_{1}(n,D)}.

When
\displaystyle D\geqslant\delta
, we have

R(D)\geqslant\frac{1}{n\delta}\left(1-\frac{(n+1)(\delta-1)}{2D}\right).

Proof.

Write

m=D-\delta\geqslant 0\text{, and }C(n,\delta)=\frac{(n+1)(\delta-1)}{2}

in this proof for simplicity; then
D=m+\delta
. A direct calculation gives

\frac{r(n,D)}{r(n,D-\delta)}=\frac{\binom{n+m+\delta}{m+\delta}}{\binom{n+m}{m}}=\prod_{j=1}^{\delta}\left(1+\frac{n}{m+j}\right).

Then we have

R(D)=\frac{r(n,D-\delta)}{D\,r_{1}(n,D)}=\frac{1}{D\left(\prod\limits_{j=1}^{\delta}\left(1+\frac{n}{m+j}\right)-1\right)}.

Thus it suffices to prove that, whenever
D>C(n,\delta)
, we have

\prod_{j=1}^{\delta}\left(1+\frac{n}{m+j}\right)-1\leqslant\frac{n\delta}{D-C(n,\delta)}=\frac{n\delta}{m+\delta-C(n,\delta)}.

We claim the following fact: for each integer
s\geqslant 1
, let

C_{s}=\frac{(n+1)(s-1)}{2}.

If
m\geqslant 0
and
m+s>C_{s}
, then

\prod_{j=1}^{s}\left(1+\frac{n}{m+j}\right)\leqslant 1+\frac{ns}{m+s-C_{s}}.

We prove the claim by induction on
s
.

Base case
s=1
. –

Here
C_{1}=0
, and

\prod_{j=1}^{1}\left(1+\frac{n}{m+j}\right)=1+\frac{n}{m+1}=1+\frac{n}{m+1-C_{1}},

so equality holds.

Induction step. –

Assume the statement holds for some
s\geqslant 1
; we prove it for
s+1
.

Suppose

m+s+1>C_{s+1}.

Since

C_{s+1}=C_{s}+\frac{n+1}{2}>C_{s},

we have

m+s>C_{s+1}-1=C_{s}+\frac{n-1}{2}\geqslant C_{s},

so the induction hypothesis applies.

By the induction hypothesis, we have

\prod_{j=1}^{s}\left(1+\frac{n}{m+j}\right)\leqslant 1+\frac{ns}{m+s-C_{s}}.

Multiplying both sides by

1+\frac{n}{m+s+1},

we obtain

\prod_{j=1}^{s+1}\left(1+\frac{n}{m+j}\right)\leqslant\left(1+\frac{ns}{m+s-C_{s}}\right)\left(1+\frac{n}{m+s+1}\right).

It remains to show that the right-hand side is at most

1+\frac{n(s+1)}{m+s+1-C_{s+1}}.

Consider the difference

\displaystyle\left(1+\frac{ns}{m+s-C_{s}}\right)\left(1+\frac{n}{m+s+1}\right)-\left(1+\frac{n(s+1)}{m+s+1-C_{s+1}}\right)

\displaystyle=n\left[\frac{s}{m+s-C_{s}}+\frac{1}{m+s+1}+\frac{ns}{(m+s-C_{s})(m+s+1)}-\frac{s+1}{m+s+1-C_{s+1}}\right].

A direct calculation using

C_{s+1}=C_{s}+\frac{n+1}{2}

gives the identity

\displaystyle\frac{s+1}{m+s+1-C_{s+1}}-\frac{s}{m+s-C_{s}}-\frac{1}{m+s+1}-\frac{ns}{(m+s-C_{s})(m+s+1)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\frac{n-1}{2}(s+1)C_{s+1}}{(m+s-C_{s})(m+s+1)(m+s+1-C_{s+1})}.

Since
n\geqslant 1
,
\frac{n-1}{2}\geqslant 0
. Also
C_{s+1}\geqslant 0
. Moreover, by the condition
m+s+1>C_{s+1}
,

m+s+1-C_{s+1}>0,

and

m+s-C_{s}=m+s+1-C_{s+1}+\frac{n-1}{2}>0.

Thus the right-hand side of the above identity is nonnegative. Consequently,

\frac{s}{m+s-C_{s}}+\frac{1}{m+s+1}+\frac{ns}{(m+s-C_{s})(m+s+1)}-\frac{s+1}{m+s+1-C_{s+1}}\leqslant 0.

This proves

\left(1+\frac{ns}{m+s-C_{s}}\right)\left(1+\frac{n}{m+s+1}\right)\leqslant 1+\frac{n(s+1)}{m+s+1-C_{s+1}}.

Hence the induction step is complete, and the claim follows.

Completion of the proof. –

If
D>C(n,\delta)
, apply the claim to the case of
s=\delta
and
m=D-\delta
. Then

\prod_{j=1}^{\delta}\left(1+\frac{n}{D-\delta+j}\right)-1\leqslant\frac{n\delta}{D-C(n,\delta)}.

Therefore

r_{1}(n,D)\leqslant r(n,D-\delta)\cdot\frac{n\delta}{D-C(n,\delta)}.

Consequently,

R(D)=\frac{r(n,D-\delta)}{D\,r_{1}(n,D)}\geqslant\frac{r(n,D-\delta)}{D\cdot r(n,D-\delta)\cdot\frac{n\delta}{D-C(n,\delta)}}=\frac{1}{n\delta}\left(1-\frac{C(n,\delta)}{D}\right).

If
D\leqslant C(n,\delta)
, then

1-\frac{C(n,\delta)}{D}\leqslant 0,

while
R(D)>0
, so the desired inequality holds trivially.

Hence for all
D\geqslant\delta
, we have

R(D)\geqslant\frac{1}{n\delta}\left(1-\frac{(n+1)(\delta-1)}{2D}\right),

which proves the assertion.
∎

A.1
Notation and preliminaries

This part collects the elementary estimates that remain uniform at
D=1
, together with the exact level-one and dimension recurrences.

\lemmname

\the\smf@thm
.

For every integer
D\geqslant 1
and every real number
p\geqslant 0
,

\frac{D^{p+1}}{p+1}\leqslant\sum_{m=1}^{D}m^{p}\leqslant D^{p+1}.

Proof.

Fix
D\geqslant 1
and
p\geqslant 0
. The function
x\mapsto x^{p}
is non-decreasing on
[0,\infty)
. For each integer
m
with
1\leqslant m\leqslant D
and each
x\in[m-1,m]
,

x^{p}\leqslant m^{p}.

Integrating over each such interval and summing gives

\int_{0}^{D}x^{p}\,\mathrm{d}x\leqslant\sum_{m=1}^{D}m^{p}.

The integral is
D^{p+1}/(p+1)
, which proves the lower bound.

For the upper bound,
m^{p}\leqslant D^{p}
for
1\leqslant m\leqslant D
. There are
D
summands, hence

\sum_{m=1}^{D}m^{p}\leqslant D\cdot D^{p}=D^{p+1}.

These arguments also apply at
D=1
. At that endpoint,

\sum_{m=1}^{1}m^{p}=1=1^{p+1}.

∎

\lemmname

\the\smf@thm
.

Let
\alpha>0
. For every real
x\geqslant 1
, we have

\log x\leqslant\frac{x^{\alpha}}{\mathrm{e}\alpha}.

Consequently, for every integer
D\geqslant 1
, every real
p\geqslant 0
, and every real
q\leqslant p-\alpha
, we have

\sum_{m=1}^{D}m^{q}\log m\leqslant\frac{D^{p+1}}{\mathrm{e}\alpha}.

In particular, for every integer
n\geqslant 1
, every integer
D\geqslant 1
, and every real
\delta>0
, we have

D^{n-\delta}\log D\leqslant\frac{D^{n}}{\mathrm{e}\delta},

\sum_{m=1}^{D}m^{n-1-\delta}\log m\leqslant\frac{D^{n}}{\mathrm{e}\delta},

and

\sum_{m=1}^{D}m^{n-1}\log m\leqslant\frac{D^{n+1}}{\mathrm{e}}.

Every inequality includes
D=1
.

Proof.

Fix
\alpha>0
, and define the function

f(x)=x^{-\alpha}\log x\qquad(x\geqslant 1).

Differentiation gives

f^{\prime}(x)=x^{-\alpha-1}(1-\alpha\log x).

The positive factor
x^{-\alpha-1}
shows that
f
is nondecreasing on

[1,\exp(1/\alpha)]
and nonincreasing thereafter. Its maximum is

f\bigl(\exp(1/\alpha)\bigr)=\frac{1}{\mathrm{e}\alpha}.

Thus
x^{-\alpha}\log x\leqslant 1/(\mathrm{e}\alpha)
, and multiplication by
x^{\alpha}
proves the first inequality.

Now fix
D\geqslant 1
,
p\geqslant 0
, and
q\leqslant p-\alpha
. Since
m\geqslant 1
, then

m^{q}\leqslant m^{p-\alpha}.

The first estimate gives

m^{q}\log m\leqslant m^{p-\alpha}\log m\leqslant\frac{m^{p}}{\mathrm{e}\alpha}.

After summation, Lemma
A.1
yields

\sum_{m=1}^{D}m^{q}\log m\leqslant\frac{1}{\mathrm{e}\alpha}\sum_{m=1}^{D}m^{p}\leqslant\frac{D^{p+1}}{\mathrm{e}\alpha}.

Applying the first inequality with
x=D
and
\alpha=1
and then multiplying by
D^{n-1}
gives the first special case. Applying it with
\alpha=\delta
and then multiplying by
D^{n-\delta}
gives the second. The third follows from the general sum estimate with
p=n-1
and
q=n-1-\delta
. Finally,

m^{n-1}\log m\leqslant\frac{m^{n}}{\mathrm{e}},

so Lemma
A.1
with exponent

proves the last estimate. At

, every logarithmic left-hand side is zero.
∎

We recall the following result from
[
35
]
.

\lemmname

\the\smf@thm
.

\propname

\the\smf@thm
.

For every integer
D\geqslant 1

C_{1}(D)=2\sum_{k=0}^{D}\log(k!)-(D+1)\log(D!),

Proof.

A.1

0<\frac{1}{2}\log(2\pi)<\frac{1}{2}\log 8<\frac{3}{2}.

It follows that

\left|R_{1}(1)\right|<\frac{3}{2}.

For
D\geqslant 2
, by summing the increment estimate, we obtain

\left|R_{1}(D)\right|\leqslant\frac{3}{2}+\frac{7}{12}\sum_{m=1}^{D-1}\frac{1}{m}.

The harmonic sum satisfies

\sum_{m=1}^{D-1}\frac{1}{m}\leqslant 1+\int_{1}^{D-1}\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{x}=1+\log(D-1)\leqslant 1+\log D.

Therefore

\left|R_{1}(D)\right|\leqslant\frac{3}{2}+\frac{7}{12}(1+\log D)=\frac{25}{12}+\frac{7}{12}\log D.

The same bound at
D=1
follows from the preceding estimate for
R_{1}(1)
.

\propname

\the\smf@thm
.

Let
n\geqslant 2
and
D\geqslant 1
be integers. Let

C(n,D)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i_{0},\ldots,i_{n}\geqslant 0\\
i_{0}+\cdots+i_{n}=D\end{subarray}}\log\left(\frac{i_{0}!\cdots i_{n}!}{D!}\right)

and

Q(n,D)=\sum_{m=0}^{D}\binom{m+n-1}{n-1}\log\binom{D}{m}.

We define
C(n-1,0)=0
, consistently with the displayed sum. Then we have

C(n,D)=\sum_{m=0}^{D}C(n-1,m)-Q(n,D).

Moreover,

Q(n,D)=\sum_{k=1}^{D}\left[\binom{k+n-1}{n}-\binom{D-k+n}{n}\right]\log k.

Both identities hold at
D=1
.

Proof.

For a tuple
(i_{0},\ldots,i_{n})
in the definition of
C(n,D)
, we denote

m=i_{0}+\cdots+i_{n-1}.

Then
i_{n}=D-m
, and

\log\left(\frac{i_{0}!\cdots i_{n-1}!(D-m)!}{D!}\right)=\log\left(\frac{i_{0}!\cdots i_{n-1}!}{m!}\right)-\log\binom{D}{m}.

For a fixed
m
, summing the first term over the nonnegative tuples

(i_{0},\ldots,i_{n-1})
with sum
m
gives
C(n-1,m)
. The number of these tuples is

\displaystyle\binom{m+n-1}{n-1}
. Summing the second term therefore gives

-\binom{m+n-1}{n-1}\log\binom{D}{m}.

Summation over
m=0,\ldots,D
proves the first identity.

For
0\leqslant m\leqslant D
, we have

\log\binom{D}{m}=\sum_{k=1}^{m}\log\left(\frac{D-k+1}{k}\right),

where the sum is empty for
m=0
. Since all sums are finite, then we have

\displaystyle Q(n,D)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=0}^{D}\binom{m+n-1}{n-1}\sum_{k=1}^{m}\log\left(\frac{D-k+1}{k}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{D}\left(\sum_{m=k}^{D}\binom{m+n-1}{n-1}\right)\log\left(\frac{D-k+1}{k}\right).

The finite hockey-stick identity gives

\sum_{m=k}^{D}\binom{m+n-1}{n-1}=\binom{D+n}{n}-\binom{k+n-1}{n}.

Also,

\sum_{k=1}^{D}\log\left(\frac{D-k+1}{k}\right)=\log\left(\frac{D!}{D!}\right)=0.

The constant term
\displaystyle\binom{D+n}{n}
therefore cancels, leaving

Q(n,D)=\sum_{k=1}^{D}\binom{k+n-1}{n}\bigl(\log k-\log(D-k+1)\bigr).

In the second logarithmic sum, substitute
j:=D-k+1
. Then

\sum_{k=1}^{D}\binom{k+n-1}{n}\log(D-k+1)=\sum_{j=1}^{D}\binom{D-j+n}{n}\log j.

Subtracting proves the second identity.

At
D=1
, the first argument is unchanged, and the final sum is

\left[\binom{n}{n}-\binom{n}{n}\right]\log 1=0=Q(n,1).

∎

The forcing quantity

The goal of this part is to obtain an explicit estimate for
Q(n,D)
whose constants remain valid at
D=1
.

\propname

\the\smf@thm
.

For integers
n\geqslant 2
and
D\geqslant 1
, let

r(n,x)=\binom{n+x}{n},\ x\in\mathbb{N},

S_{\mathrm{aux}}(k,D)=\sum_{m=2}^{D}\bigl((m-1)^{k}-(D-m)^{k}\bigr)\log m,\ k\geqslant 1,

and

Q(n,D)=\sum_{m=0}^{D}\binom{n-1+m}{n-1}\log\binom{D}{m}.

Then

\displaystyle Q(n,D)\geqslant{}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n!}S_{\mathrm{aux}}(n,D)+\frac{n+1}{2(n-1)!}S_{\mathrm{aux}}(n-1,D)

\displaystyle-(n-1)^{2}(D-1)^{n-1}\log D,

and

\displaystyle Q(n,D)\leqslant{}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n!}S_{\mathrm{aux}}(n,D)+\frac{n+1}{2(n-1)!}S_{\mathrm{aux}}(n-1,D)

\displaystyle+(n-1)^{2}(D-1)^{n-1}\log D.

At
D=1
, every displayed sum is empty and both sides are zero.

Proof.

Let

p_{n}(x)=\frac{x^{n}}{n!}+\frac{n+1}{2(n-1)!}x^{n-1}\text{, and }E_{n}(x)=r(n,x)-p_{n}(x).

We first derive an exact summation identity. For
1\leqslant j\leqslant D-1
, we denote

a_{j}=r(n-1,j),\ b_{j}=\log\binom{D}{j}\text{, and }A_{j}=\sum_{k=1}^{j}a_{k}.

Then we have

A_{j}=\sum_{k=1}^{j}\binom{n-1+k}{n-1}=\binom{n+j}{n}-1=r(n,j)-1.

Since
b_{D}=0
, by Abel’s summation, we obtain

\sum_{j=1}^{D-1}a_{j}b_{j}=\sum_{j=1}^{D-1}A_{j}(b_{j}-b_{j+1}).

Furthermore,

b_{j}-b_{j+1}=\log\frac{j+1}{D-j}.

The terms
m=0\text{ and }D
in the sum
Q(n,D)
vanish. Substitution of the preceding identities, reindexing
m=j+1
in the numerator logarithms and
m=D-j
in the denominator logarithms, and using the fact

\sum_{j=1}^{D-1}\log\frac{j+1}{D-j}=\log D=r(n,0)\log D,

we obtain

Q(n,D)=\sum_{m=2}^{D}\bigl(r(n,m-1)-r(n,D-m)\bigr)\log m.

For
D=1
, this is an empty sum. We may therefore assume
D\geqslant 2
and put
N=D-1
for simplicity.

The product formula

r(n,x)=\frac{1}{n!}\prod_{j=1}^{n}(x+j)

shows that the coefficients of
x^{n}
and
x^{n-1}
are respectively

\frac{1}{n!}\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{1+\cdots+n}{n!}=\frac{n+1}{2(n-1)!}.

Thus
E_{n}(x)
is a polynomial of degree at most
n-2
, all of whose coefficients are nonnegative. Also,

E_{n}(0)=1.

If
x\geqslant 1
, every monomial
x^{j}
satisfying
0\leqslant j\leqslant n-2
is at most
x^{n-2}
. Hence

0\leqslant E_{n}(x)\leqslant e_{n}x^{n-2},

where

e_{n}=E_{n}(1)=n+1-\frac{1}{n!}-\frac{n+1}{2(n-1)!}<n+1.

Substituting
r(n,x)=p_{n}(x)+E_{n}(x)
into the exact identity gives

Q(n,D)=\frac{1}{n!}S_{\mathrm{aux}}(n,D)+\frac{n+1}{2(n-1)!}S_{\mathrm{aux}}(n-1,D)+T_{n}(D),

where

T_{n}(D)=\sum_{m=2}^{D}\bigl(E_{n}(m-1)-E_{n}(D-m)\bigr)\log m.

Then it remains to prove

\left|T_{n}(D)\right|\leqslant(n-1)^{2}N^{n-1}\log D.

For
n=2
, we have

r(2,x)=\frac{x^{2}}{2}+\frac{3x}{2}+1,

so
E_{2}(x)=1
. Every summand defining
T_{2}(D)
is zero.

For
n=3
, we have

E_{3}(x)=1+\frac{11}{6}x.

Writing
j=m-1
, we obtain

T_{3}(D)=\frac{11}{6}\sum_{j=1}^{N}(2j-N)\log(j+1).

Since
0\leqslant\log(j+1)\leqslant\log D
and
\left|2j-N\right|\leqslant N
, then we have

\left|T_{3}(D)\right|\leqslant\frac{11}{6}N^{2}\log D<4N^{2}\log D.

Next, we suppose
n\geqslant 4
. Positivity and the bound for
E_{n}
give

\displaystyle\left|T_{n}(D)\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant\log D\left(E_{n}(N)+E_{n}(0)+2\sum_{x=1}^{N-1}E_{n}(x)\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\log D\left(e_{n}N^{n-2}+1+2e_{n}\sum_{x=1}^{N-1}x^{n-2}\right).

Since
x^{n-2}
is increasing, then

\sum_{x=1}^{N-1}x^{n-2}\leqslant\int_{1}^{N}t^{n-2}\,\mathrm{d}t\leqslant\frac{N^{n-1}}{n-1}.

For
n=4
,

e_{4}=5-\frac{1}{24}-\frac{5}{12}=\frac{109}{24}.

By the facts
N^{2}\leqslant N^{3}
,
1\leqslant N^{3}
, and the preceding sum estimate, we obtain

\left|T_{4}(D)\right|\leqslant\left(\frac{109}{24}+1+\frac{109}{36}\right)N^{3}\log D=\frac{617}{72}N^{3}\log D<9N^{3}\log D.

Finally, let
n\geqslant 5
. Since
e_{n}<n+1
,

\left|T_{n}(D)\right|<\left(n+2+\frac{2(n+1)}{n-1}\right)N^{n-1}\log D.

Here

\frac{2(n+1)}{n-1}=2+\frac{4}{n-1}\leqslant 3

and

n+5\leqslant(n-1)^{2},

because

(n-1)^{2}-(n+5)=(n-4)(n+1)\geqslant 0.

This proves the required estimate for
T_{n}(D)
, and substitution into the exact decomposition yields both asserted inequalities.
∎

\theoname

\the\smf@thm
.

For every pair of integers
n\geqslant 2
and
D\geqslant 1
, we denote

S_{\mathrm{aux}}(n,D)=\sum_{m=2}^{D}\bigl((m-1)^{n}-(D-m)^{n}\bigr)\log m,

\mathcal{H}_{n}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{1}{j},\qquad K_{n}=\frac{n^{2}+13n+62}{12}.

Then

\left|S_{\mathrm{aux}}(n,D)-\frac{\mathcal{H}_{n}}{n+1}D^{n+1}+\frac{1}{2}D^{n}\log D\right|\leqslant K_{n}D^{n}.

The inequality is uniform in every positive integer
D
, including
D=1
.

Proof.

We begin with a midpoint estimate. If
g
is twice continuously differentiable on

\left[m-\frac{1}{2},m+\frac{1}{2}\right]
, Taylor’s formula with integral remainder gives

\left|g(m)-\int_{m-\frac{1}{2}}^{m+\frac{1}{2}}g(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\right|\leqslant\frac{1}{24}\sup_{m-\frac{1}{2}\leqslant x\leqslant m+\frac{1}{2}}\left|g^{\prime\prime}(x)\right|.

After subtracting
g(m)+g^{\prime}(m)(x-m)
, the remainder is bounded by

\tfrac{1}{2}M(x-m)^{2}
, where
M
is the displayed supremum. The linear term integrates to zero, and

\frac{1}{2}\int_{-\frac{1}{2}}^{\frac{1}{2}}t^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t=\frac{1}{24}.

Assume first that
D\geqslant 2
, and put

f(x)=\bigl((x-1)^{n}-(D-x)^{n}\bigr)\log x,\ \frac{3}{2}\leqslant x\leqslant D+\frac{1}{2}.

Direct differentiation gives

\displaystyle f^{\prime\prime}(x)={}

\displaystyle-\frac{(x-1)^{n}-(D-x)^{n}}{x^{2}}

\displaystyle+\frac{2n\bigl((x-1)^{n-1}+(D-x)^{n-1}\bigr)}{x}

\displaystyle+n(n-1)\bigl((x-1)^{n-2}-(D-x)^{n-2}\bigr)\log x.

For
x\in\left[m-\tfrac{1}{2},m+\tfrac{1}{2}\right]
,
2\leqslant m\leqslant D
, we have

\left|x-1\right|\leqslant D,\ \left|D-x\right|\leqslant D,\ x\geqslant m-\frac{1}{2}.

The first term of
f^{\prime\prime}
, summed over the cells, contributes at most

2D^{n}\sum_{m=2}^{D}\frac{1}{\left(m-\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2}}\leqslant 2D^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{j^{2}}\leqslant 4D^{n},

where the last inequality follows from

\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{j^{2}}=1+\sum_{j=2}^{\infty}\frac{1}{j^{2}}\leqslant 1+\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{x^{2}}=2.

The second term contributes at most

4nD^{n-1}\sum_{m=2}^{D}\frac{1}{m-\frac{1}{2}}\leqslant 4nD^{n-1}(D-1)\leqslant 4nD^{n}.

For the third term, we have

\left|(x-1)^{n-2}-(D-x)^{n-2}\right|\leqslant\left|x-1\right|^{n-2}+\left|D-x\right|^{n-2}\leqslant 2D^{n-2}.

There are
D-1\leqslant D
cells, and

\log\left(D+\frac{1}{2}\right)\leqslant D

by
\log y\leqslant y-1
. Hence this term contributes at most

2n(n-1)D^{n-2}\cdot D\cdot\log\left(D+\frac{1}{2}\right)\leqslant 2n(n-1)D^{n}.

Applying the midpoint estimate on every cell, we obtain

\left|S_{\mathrm{aux}}(n,D)-J_{n}(D)\right|\leqslant\frac{2n^{2}+2n+4}{24}D^{n}=\frac{n^{2}+n+2}{12}D^{n},

where

J_{n}(D)=\int_{3/2}^{D+1/2}\bigl((x-1)^{n}-(D-x)^{n}\bigr)\log x\,\mathrm{d}x.

We compare this integral with two exactly evaluable integrals. Suppose

and

A_{0}=\int_{0}^{D}u^{n}\log u\,\mathrm{d}u,\ B_{0}=\int_{0}^{D}(D-x)^{n}\log x\,\mathrm{d}x.

Then
J_{n}(D)=A-B
. After substituting
u=x-1
for simplicity, we suppose

L=\int_{0}^{\frac{1}{2}}u^{n}\log u\,\mathrm{d}u,

U=\int_{D-\frac{1}{2}}^{D}u^{n}\log u\,\mathrm{d}u,

and

V=\int_{\frac{1}{2}}^{D-\frac{1}{2}}u^{n}\log\left(1+\frac{1}{u}\right)\mathrm{d}u.

The definitions give

A-A_{0}=-L-U+V.

Since
L\leqslant 0
, then

0\leqslant-L\leqslant\int_{0}^{1}(-\log u)\,\mathrm{d}u=1.

Also, we have
\log\left(1+\frac{1}{u}\right)\leqslant\frac{1}{u}
, so

0\leqslant V\leqslant\int_{\frac{1}{2}}^{D-\frac{1}{2}}u^{n-1}\,\mathrm{d}u\leqslant\frac{D^{n}}{n}.

Let
h(u)=u^{n}\log u
. On
\left[D-\tfrac{1}{2},D\right]
, we have

0\leqslant h^{\prime}(u)=u^{n-1}(n\log u+1)\leqslant D^{n-1}(n\log D+1).

Therefore

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{2}D^{n}\log D-U=\int_{D-\frac{1}{2}}^{D}\bigl(h(D)-h(u)\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle\leqslant D^{n-1}(n\log D+1)\int_{D-\frac{1}{2}}^{D}(D-u)\,\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{8}D^{n-1}(n\log D+1)\leqslant\frac{1}{8}\left(\frac{n}{\mathrm{e}}+1\right)D^{n},

where Lemma
A.1
was applied in the last line. Hence, we obtain

\left|A-A_{0}+\frac{1}{2}D^{n}\log D\right|\leqslant\left(1+\frac{1}{8}\left(\frac{n}{\mathrm{e}}+1\right)+\frac{1}{n}\right)D^{n}.

For the second integral, we have

B-B_{0}=-\int_{0}^{\frac{3}{2}}(D-x)^{n}\log x\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{D}^{D+\frac{1}{2}}(D-x)^{n}\log x\,\mathrm{d}x.

On
[0,\tfrac{3}{2}]
, we have
\left|D-x\right|\leqslant D
, and

\int_{0}^{\frac{3}{2}}\left|\log x\right|\,\mathrm{d}x=1+\int_{1}^{\frac{3}{2}}\log x\,\mathrm{d}x\leqslant 1+\int_{1}^{\frac{3}{2}}(x-1)\,\mathrm{d}x=\frac{9}{8}<2.

Thus, the first integral has absolute value less than
2D^{n}
. On

\left[D,D+\tfrac{1}{2}\right]
, we have
\left|D-x\right|\leqslant\tfrac{1}{2}
, so the second has absolute value at most

\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{n}D\leqslant\frac{1}{8}D\leqslant\frac{1}{8}D^{n}.

Consequently,

\left|B-B_{0}\right|<3D^{n}.

Using the fact
\frac{1}{\mathrm{e}}<1
and
\frac{1}{n}\leqslant\frac{1}{2}
, we obtain

\left|J_{n}(D)-(A_{0}-B_{0})+\frac{1}{2}D^{n}\log D\right|\leqslant(n+5)D^{n}.

It remains to evaluate
A_{0}-B_{0}
. An elementary integration gives

A_{0}=D^{n+1}\left(\frac{\log D}{n+1}-\frac{1}{(n+1)^{2}}\right).

After substituting
x=Dt
, we have

B_{0}=D^{n+1}\left(\frac{\log D}{n+1}+\int_{0}^{1}(1-t)^{n}\log t\,\mathrm{d}t\right).

We claim that

\int_{0}^{1}(1-t)^{n}\log t\,\mathrm{d}t=-\frac{H_{n+1}}{n+1}.

Indeed, put
u=1-t
and we have

-\log(1-u)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{u^{k}}{k}.

Monotone convergence gives

-\int_{0}^{1}u^{n}\log(1-u)\,\mathrm{d}u=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{k(n+k+1)}.

Since

\frac{1}{k(n+k+1)}=\frac{1}{n+1}\left(\frac{1}{k}-\frac{1}{k+n+1}\right),

the partial sums telescope to
\mathcal{H}_{n+1}/(n+1)
. By the fact

\mathcal{H}_{n+1}=\mathcal{H}_{n}+\frac{1}{n+1},

we conclude that

A_{0}-B_{0}=\frac{\mathcal{H}_{n}}{n+1}D^{n+1}.

Thus, we have

\left|J_{n}(D)-\frac{H_{n}}{n+1}D^{n+1}+\frac{1}{2}D^{n}\log D\right|\leqslant(n+5)D^{n}.

Combining this with the midpoint estimate gives

\displaystyle\left|S_{\mathrm{aux}}(n,D)-\frac{\mathcal{H}_{n}}{n+1}D^{n+1}+\frac{1}{2}D^{n}\log D\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant\left(n+5+\frac{n^{2}+n+2}{12}\right)D^{n}

\displaystyle=K_{n}D^{n}

for
D\geqslant 2
.

If
D=1
, then
S_{\mathrm{aux}}(n,1)=0
and
\log 1=0
. Since
\mathcal{H}_{n}\leqslant n
, then

\frac{\mathcal{H}_{n}}{n+1}<1<K_{n}.

The same estimate therefore holds at
D=1
.
∎

\eta_{n-1}(D)
satisfying

S_{n}(D)=\frac{\mathcal{H}_{n}}{n+1}D^{n+1}-\frac{1}{2}D^{n}\log D+\eta_{n}(D)\text{ with }\left|\eta_{n}(D)\right|\leq K_{n}D^{n},

and

S_{n-1}(D)=\frac{\mathcal{H}_{n-1}}{n}D^{n}-\frac{1}{2}D^{n-1}\log D+\eta_{n-1}(D)\text{ with }\left|\eta_{n-1}(D)\right|\leq K_{n-1}D^{n-1}.

Since

\frac{n+1}{2(n-1)!}\frac{\mathcal{H}_{n-1}}{n}=\frac{n+1}{2n!}\mathcal{H}_{n-1},

substitution gives

\displaystyle\frac{S_{n}(D)}{n!}+\frac{n+1}{2(n-1)!}S_{n-1}(D)-q_{n}(D)

\displaystyle=\frac{\eta_{n}(D)}{n!}+\frac{n+1}{2(n-1)!}\eta_{n-1}(D)-\frac{n+1}{4(n-1)!}D^{n-1}\log D+\frac{\beta_{n}}{n!}D^{n}.

Lemma
A.1
gives

D^{n-1}\log D\leqslant\frac{1}{\mathrm{e}}D^{n}

and

(D-1)^{n-1}\log D\leqslant D^{n-1}\log D\leqslant\frac{1}{\mathrm{e}}D^{n}.

Taking absolute values yields

\displaystyle\left|Q(n,D)-q_{n}(D)\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant\left[\frac{K_{n}}{n!}+\frac{n+1}{2(n-1)!}K_{n-1}+\frac{n+1}{4\mathrm{e}(n-1)!}\right.

\displaystyle\left.+\frac{\beta_{n}}{n!}+\frac{(n-1)^{2}}{\mathrm{e}}\right]D^{n}

\displaystyle=W_{n}D^{n}.

Every summand defining
W_{n}
is non-negative, and
K_{n}>0
, so
W_{n}>0
.

For the quadratic bound, the case
n=2
is

W_{2}=10=\frac{5}{2}\cdot 2^{2}.

Let
n\geqslant 3
. An elementary calculation gives

13n^{2}-(n^{2}+13n+62)=12n^{2}-13n-62\geqslant 7,

so

K_{n}\leqslant\frac{13}{12}n^{2}.

Likewise,

12n^{2}-(n^{2}+11n+50)=11n^{2}-11n-50\geq 16,

and hence

K_{n-1}=\frac{n^{2}+11n+50}{12}\leqslant n^{2}.

Furthermore,

n!\geqslant 6,\qquad\frac{n+1}{2(n-1)!}\leqslant 1.

The second inequality is an equality at
n=3
. For
n\geqslant 4
, it follows first at
n=4
from
2(n-1)!\geqslant 12>n+1
, and thereafter by induction.

Since
\mathrm{e}>2
, then

\frac{1}{2\mathrm{e}}<\frac{1}{4},\qquad\frac{1}{\mathrm{e}}<\frac{1}{2}.

By the fact
n^{2}\geqslant 9
and
0<\beta_{n}<1
, we obtain

\displaystyle W_{n}

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{13}{72}n^{2}+n^{2}+\frac{1}{4}+\frac{1}{2}(n-1)^{2}+\frac{1}{6}

\displaystyle\leqslant\left(\frac{13}{72}+1+\frac{1}{36}+\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{54}\right)n^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{373}{216}n^{2}<2n^{2}<\frac{5}{2}n^{2}.

∎

A.2
The proof of Theorem
A

In this part, we will give a proof of Theorem
A
.

\propname

\the\smf@thm
.

Let

\mathcal{H}_{n}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{1}{j},

and let
a_{n},b_{n},c_{n}
be the coefficients in Theorem
A
. For every integer
n\geqslant 1
and
D\geqslant 1
, let

M_{n}(D)=a_{n}D^{n+1}+b_{n}D^{n}\log D+c_{n}D^{n}.

For every integer
n\geqslant 2
, let

\kappa_{n}=-1+\frac{1}{2}\log(2\pi)-\frac{1}{2n}+\frac{n+1}{2}\mathcal{H}_{n-1}

and

q_{n}(D):=\frac{\mathcal{H}_{n}}{(n+1)!}D^{n+1}-\frac{1}{2n!}D^{n}\log D+\frac{\kappa_{n}}{n!}D^{n}.

We denote

F_{n}(D)=\sum_{m=1}^{D}M_{n-1}(m)-q_{n}(D)-M_{n}(D).

Then

\displaystyle F_{n}(D)={}

\displaystyle a_{n-1}\left(\sum_{m=1}^{D}m^{n}-\frac{D^{n+1}}{n+1}-\frac{D^{n}}{2}\right)

\displaystyle+b_{n-1}\left(\sum_{m=1}^{D}m^{n-1}\log m-\frac{D^{n}}{n}\log D+\frac{D^{n}}{n^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle+c_{n-1}\left(\sum_{m=1}^{D}m^{n-1}-\frac{D^{n}}{n}\right).

Moreover, if

E_{n}:=\frac{\left|a_{n-1}\right|}{2}+\left(\frac{1}{4}+\frac{1}{\mathrm{e}}\right)\left|b_{n-1}\right|+\left|c_{n-1}\right|,

then

\left|F_{n}(D)\right|\leqslant E_{n}D^{n}

for every
n\geqslant 2
and
D\geqslant 1
.

Proof.

Substitution of the explicit coefficients from Theorem
A
, followed by collection over a common denominator and the use of

\mathcal{H}_{n}=\mathcal{H}_{n-1}+\frac{1}{n},

we obtain

a_{n}=\frac{a_{n-1}}{n+1}-\frac{\mathcal{H}_{n}}{(n+1)!},

b_{n}=\frac{b_{n-1}}{n}+\frac{1}{2n!},

and

c_{n}=\frac{c_{n-1}}{n}-\frac{b_{n-1}}{n^{2}}+\frac{a_{n-1}}{2}-\frac{\kappa_{n}}{n!}.

Expanding the definition of
F_{n}(D)
, we obtain

\displaystyle F_{n}(D)={}

\displaystyle a_{n-1}\sum_{m=1}^{D}m^{n}+b_{n-1}\sum_{m=1}^{D}m^{n-1}\log m+c_{n-1}\sum_{m=1}^{D}m^{n-1}

\displaystyle-\frac{\mathcal{H}_{n}}{(n+1)!}D^{n+1}+\frac{1}{2n!}D^{n}\log D-\frac{\kappa_{n}}{n!}D^{n}

\displaystyle-a_{n}D^{n+1}-b_{n}D^{n}\log D-c_{n}D^{n}.

Insert and subtract

\frac{a_{n-1}}{n+1}D^{n+1}+\frac{a_{n-1}}{2}D^{n},

\frac{b_{n-1}}{n}D^{n}\log D-\frac{b_{n-1}}{n^{2}}D^{n},

and

\frac{c_{n-1}}{n}D^{n}.

The coefficient of
D^{n+1}
outside the three resulting remainders is

\frac{a_{n-1}}{n+1}-\frac{\mathcal{H}_{n}}{(n+1)!}-a_{n}=0.

The coefficient of
D^{n}\log D
is

\frac{b_{n-1}}{n}+\frac{1}{2n!}-b_{n}=0.

The remaining coefficient of
D^{n}
is

\frac{a_{n-1}}{2}-\frac{b_{n-1}}{n^{2}}+\frac{c_{n-1}}{n}-\frac{\kappa_{n}}{n!}-c_{n}=0.

This proves the exact formula for
F_{n}(D)
.

Since the function
x^{n}
is increasing on
[0,D]
, integral comparison gives

\int_{0}^{D}x^{n}\,\mathrm{d}x\leqslant\sum_{m=1}^{D}m^{n}\leqslant\int_{0}^{D}x^{n}\,\mathrm{d}x+D^{n}.

Therefore

0\leqslant\sum_{m=1}^{D}m^{n}-\frac{D^{n+1}}{n+1}\leqslant D^{n},

and hence

\left|\sum_{m=1}^{D}m^{n}-\frac{D^{n+1}}{n+1}-\frac{D^{n}}{2}\right|\leqslant\frac{D^{n}}{2}.

The same argument for
x^{n-1}
gives

0\leqslant\sum_{m=1}^{D}m^{n-1}-\frac{D^{n}}{n}\leqslant D^{n-1}\leqslant D^{n}.

For

f(x)=x^{n-1}\log x,

we have

f^{\prime}(x)=x^{n-2}\bigl((n-1)\log x+1\bigr)>0,\ x\geqslant 1.

Since
f(1)=0
, monotone integral comparison yields

\int_{1}^{D}f(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\leqslant\sum_{m=1}^{D}f(m)\leqslant\int_{1}^{D}f(x)\,\mathrm{d}x+f(D).

Direct integration gives

\int_{1}^{D}x^{n-1}\log x\,\mathrm{d}x=\frac{D^{n}}{n}\log D-\frac{D^{n}}{n^{2}}+\frac{1}{n^{2}}.

Thus

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{m=1}^{D}m^{n-1}\log m-\frac{D^{n}}{n}\log D+\frac{D^{n}}{n^{2}}

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{n^{2}}+D^{n-1}\log D.

Since
n\geqslant 2
and
D\geqslant 1
,

\frac{1}{n^{2}}\leqslant\frac{D^{n}}{4}.

Lemma
A.1
gives

D^{n-1}\log D\leqslant\frac{D^{n}}{\mathrm{e}}.

Therefore

0\leqslant\sum_{m=1}^{D}m^{n-1}\log m-\frac{D^{n}}{n}\log D+\frac{D^{n}}{n^{2}}\leqslant\left(\frac{1}{4}+\frac{1}{\mathrm{e}}\right)D^{n}.

Applying the triangle inequality to the exact formula for
F_{n}(D)
, we have

\left|F_{n}(D)\right|\leqslant E_{n}D^{n}.

∎

\lemmname

\the\smf@thm
.

For every integer
n\geqslant 2
, we have

E_{n}<4.

Consequently, we have

\left|F_{n}(D)\right|\leqslant 4D^{n},\ n\geqslant 2,\ D\geqslant 1.

Proof.

Put
k=n-1
for simplicity. Since

\mathcal{H}_{k+1}-1=\sum_{j=2}^{k+1}\frac{1}{j}\leqslant k

and
k!\geqslant k
, then

\left|a_{k}\right|=\frac{\mathcal{H}_{k+1}-1}{k!}\leqslant 1.

Also,

\left|b_{k}\right|=\frac{1}{2(k-1)!}\leqslant\frac{1}{2}.

It remains to give an estimate of
c_{k}
. We have
0<\log(2\pi)<3
, and a direct substitution gives

\left|c_{1}\right|<1,\ c_{2}=-1,\ c_{3}=-\frac{65/6+\log(2\pi)}{12},\ c_{4}=-\frac{137/6+2\log(2\pi)}{48}.

Thus
\left|c_{k}\right|<2
for
1\leqslant k\leqslant 4
.

For
k\geqslant 5
, the inequality
\mathcal{H}_{k}\leqslant k
gives

\left|c_{k}\right|\leqslant\frac{k^{3}+3k^{2}+4k-1}{2k!}.

At
k=5
,

k^{3}+3k^{2}+4k-1=219\leqslant 240=2k!.

Let
P(k)=k^{3}+3k^{2}+4k-1
, then

(k+1)P(k)-P(k+1)=k^{4}+3k^{3}+k^{2}-10k-8>0

for
k\geqslant 5
. Induction therefore gives
P(k)\leqslant 2k!
for all
k\geqslant 5
, so

\left|c_{k}\right|\leqslant 1.

Hence
\left|c_{k}\right|<2
for every
k\geqslant 1
.

Finally,
\mathrm{e}>2
, so

\frac{1}{4}+\frac{1}{\mathrm{e}}<\frac{3}{4}.

It follows that

E_{n}<\frac{1}{2}+\frac{3}{8}+2=\frac{23}{8}<4.

The assertion for
F_{n}(D)
now follows from Proposition
A.2
.
∎

Now we give the proof of Theorem
A
.

Proof of Theorem
A
.

Let

R_{n}(D)=C(n,D)-M_{n}(D).

Step 1. –

We establish the level-one bound. The coefficient formulas give

a_{1}=-\frac{1}{2},\qquad b_{1}=\frac{1}{2},\qquad c_{1}=-1+\frac{1}{2}\log(2\pi).

Thus

M_{1}(D)=-\frac{1}{2}D^{2}+\frac{1}{2}D\log D+\left(-1+\frac{1}{2}\log(2\pi)\right)D.

Expanding the definition of
C(1,D)
gives

C(1,D)=2\sum_{k=0}^{D}\log(k!)-(D+1)\log(D!)=C_{1}(D).

Proposition
A.1
therefore yields

\left|R_{1}(D)\right|\leqslant\frac{25}{12}+\frac{7}{12}\log D.

Lemma
A.1
gives
\log D\leqslant D/\mathrm{e}
, while

25/12\leqslant(25/12)D
. Consequently,

\left|R_{1}(D)\right|\leqslant\left(\frac{25}{12}+\frac{7}{12\mathrm{e}}\right)D=B_{0}D.

Step 2. –

We derive the exact remainder recurrence. For
n\geqslant 2
, let
Q(n,D)
be as in Proposition
A.1
, and let
q_{n}(D)
and
F_{n}(D)
be as in Proposition
A.2
. Proposition
A.1
gives

C(n,D)=\sum_{m=1}^{D}C(n-1,m)-Q(n,D),

because
C(n-1,0)=0
. By definition,

F_{n}(D)=\sum_{m=1}^{D}M_{n-1}(m)-q_{n}(D)-M_{n}(D).

Subtracting
M_{n}(D)
from the exact recurrence gives

R_{n}(D)=\sum_{m=1}^{D}R_{n-1}(m)-\bigl(Q(n,D)-q_{n}(D)\bigr)+F_{n}(D).

Step 3. –

We propagate the uniform estimate. Theorem
A.1
gives

\left|Q(n,D)-q_{n}(D)\right|\leqslant W_{n}D^{n}

and

0<W_{n}\leqslant\frac{5}{2}n^{2}.

Lemma
A.2
gives

\left|F_{n}(D)\right|\leqslant 4D^{n}.

Suppose that

\left|R_{n-1}(m)\right|\leqslant L_{n-1}m^{n-1}

for every integer
m\geqslant 1
, where
L_{1}=B_{0}
. The exact recurrence, the triangle inequality, and Lemma
A.1
give

\displaystyle\left|R_{n}(D)\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant L_{n-1}\sum_{m=1}^{D}m^{n-1}+(W_{n}+4)D^{n}

\displaystyle\leqslant L_{n-1}D^{n}+(W_{n}+4)D^{n}

\displaystyle=(L_{n-1}+W_{n}+4)D^{n}.

Suppose

L_{n}=L_{n-1}+W_{n}+4.

Starting with
L_{1}=B_{0}
, induction proves

\left|R_{n}(D)\right|\leqslant L_{n}D^{n}

for every
n\geqslant 2
and
D\geqslant 1
. Iterating the recurrence for
L_{n}
gives

L_{n}=B_{0}+\sum_{r=2}^{n}(W_{r}+4).

Step 4. –

We simplify the constant polynomially. For
r\geqslant 2
, we have
W_{r}\leqslant\frac{5}{2}r^{2}
and
4\leqslant r^{2}
. Hence, we have

W_{r}+4\leqslant\frac{7}{2}r^{2}.

Therefore

\displaystyle L_{n}

\displaystyle\leqslant B_{0}+\frac{7}{2}\sum_{r=2}^{n}r^{2}

\displaystyle=B_{0}+\frac{7}{2}\left(\frac{n(n+1)(2n+1)}{6}-1\right)

\displaystyle=B_{0}+\frac{7}{2}S_{n}.

Since
R_{n}(D)=C(n,D)-M_{n}(D)
, this proves the absolute-value estimate and the two-sided bracket. Every estimate used above includes
D=1
.
∎
