Organ-specific inflammatory profiles during sepsis: divergent macrophage polarization and oxidative stress response in lung and liver in mouse model of caecal ligation and puncture
Authors: Aude Bodin, Abdessalem Hammed, Mathieu Magnin, Pascale Bellaud, Charlotte Slek, Loris Commin, Orlando Musso, Anne Corlu, Vincent Lagente, Caroline Aninat, Vanessa Louzier, Tatiana Victoni
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSepsis is a life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated host response to infection. Although systemic, sepsis mechanisms vary between organs. Macrophage activation or inhibition within specific organs is thought to drive disease progression. Identifying organ-specific biomarkers could improve understanding of individual organ failure. This study compared the systemic inflammatory response in lung and liver, exploring macrophage involvement in compartmentalized responses using a murine polymicrobial sepsis model induced by cecal ligation and puncture (CLP).Materials and methodsModerate sepsis was induced in male C57BL/6 mice divided into seven groups: Basal, Control (1 and 5 days), Sham (1 and 5 days), and CLP (1 and 5 days). Analyses included histology, lung and liver function (assessed via the ratio of arterial oxygen partial pressure to fractional inspired oxygen (PaO2/FiO2), bilirubin, and hepatic enzymes), and the measurement of cytokines and chemokines in blood, liver, and lung. We further analyzed oxidative stress markers—specifically Superoxide Dismutase (SOD) and Catalase (CAT) gens expression—macrophage activation states (using Cluster of Differentiation 86 (CD86) and 206 (CD206)), and Programmed Death-Ligand 1 (PD-L1) expression via immunohistochemistry. Tissue remodeling was assessed through Matrix Metalloproteinase (MMP) and Tissue Inhibitor of Metalloproteinase (TIMP) gene expression.ResultsRespiratory and liver dysfunction were evident at day 5 post-CLP by histology, decreased PaO2/FiO2, and increased bilirubin. Plasma TNF-α, IL-6, IL-10, and CCL2 peaked within 24 h and decreased by day 5, while cytokine profiles in organs differed from systemic levels. The lung showed elevated Ccl2, Ccr2, and Cx3cr1 expression compared to the liver, whereas the liver had a more pronounced oxidative imbalance. Lung tissue remodeling featured higher MMP expression with relatively lower inhibitors compared to the liver. Both organs showed increased CD86 and CD206 macrophage activation at day 5, but CD86-positive cells were three times more numerous in the liver, and CD206-positive cells were twice as high in the lung. Additionally, PD-L1 expression increased markedly in lung but not liver macrophages after CLP, affecting CD206⁺ subsets and suggesting lung-specific late immunosuppression.ConclusionThese findings support inflammatory compartmentalization in sepsis, with macrophages central to organ-specific immune regulation. Differential monocyte recruitment (CCL2/CCR2), oxidative stress, MMP/TIMP profiles and distinct CD86/CD206 distributions PD-L1 upregulation was restricted to CD206⁺ lung macrophages, reflecting late lung-specific immunosuppression. Introduction

Sepsis is defined as life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated host response to infection [1]. The complex pathophysiology of sepsis is associated with pro and anti-inflammatory response, a pro-coagulant state, endothelial dysfunction and tissue hypoxia. In the early stages, there is a systemic activation of the inflammatory response, characterized by the presence of cytokines and acute phase proteins in plasma and stimulation of immune cells [2–4]. Although, sepsis presents a systemic process, the pathophysiological of sepsis differs from organ to organ, and from organ to peripheral blood, leading to the concept of compartmentalization [5, 6]. Compartmentalization of the inflammatory response could help explain why certain organs are particularly vulnerable to sepsis [4]. Indeed, organs such as the liver, lungs, kidneys, and heart often exhibit signs of dysfunction due to localized inflammation [5, 6].

Macrophages can regulate cytokine release and the host’s immune response in sepsis by polarizing into different functional phenotypes. Following infection, in the early stages of sepsis, macrophages are activated through Toll-Like Receptor (TLR) signaling and differentiates into the M1 phenotype by activating NF-κB, which leads to the release of large amounts of pro-inflammatory cytokines [7, 8]. At this stage, the host defense system is enhanced to eliminate invading pathogens or damaged tissue [9]. However, an excessive immune response can lead to tissue damage. In a later stage, an excessive immune response can cause organ damage and immune dysfunction. This immune dysfunction is increasingly recognized as a compartmentalized process that differs between organs [10]. Macrophages can shift toward the M2 phenotype, which suppresses the pro‑inflammatory response but also contributes to organ damage by increasing the production of metalloproteinases. These enzymes can degrade extracellular matrix components and thereby promote inflammatory tissue injury [7]. This regulation of macrophage polarization during the critical phase of sepsis-induced inflammation has been recognized as a key factor in the pathology of sepsis [11–14]. However, recent studies have shown that both pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory responses occur early and simultaneously in sepsis. Therefore, the inhibition or activation of the different macrophage phenotypes could be organ-dependent [4, 13, 15]. Several authors suggest that inhibiting activity of M1 macrophages can significantly reduce the tissue damage and mortality by limiting the release of pro-inflammatory factors [16–18]. In fact, Shu and colleagues [16] showed that blocking CD38, a marker of M1 macrophages, attenuates lipopolysaccharide-induced classical macrophage activation and acute kidney injury. On the other hand, an interesting study by Carestia et al. [19] demonstrated that in a sepsis model induced by intraperitoneal injection of E. coli, reprogramming monocytes towards the M1 phenotype reduced mortality by enhancing inducible nitric oxide synthase (Nos2) expression and improving bacterial clearance. This study suggests that local inhibition of macrophages plays a key role in the development of organ dysfunction during sepsis, and this may be modulated by organ-specific inflammatory signals. In addition to the classical M1/M2 polarization states, emerging evidence suggests that immune checkpoint molecules, such as Programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) expressed by macrophages, contribute to sepsis-induced immunosuppression and may also reflect tissue-specific immune regulation [20]. Elucidating the regulatory processes and molecular mechanisms of macrophage polarization during the different stages of sepsis can facilitate the identification of organ-specific biomarkers (beyond the blood) and improve of the mechanisms driving organ-specific failure.

The aim of this study was to compare the systemic inflammatory response in the lung and liver, two organs most frequently affected during sepsis, at both early and late stages of the disease. A further objective was to investigate the role of macrophages in this organ-specific compartmentalization. Specifically, we evaluated cytokines profile, oxidative stress and tissue remodeling parameters, all processes modulated by macrophages in a murine model of polymicrobial sepsis induced by caecal ligation and puncture (CLP). We also examined whether M1 and M2 macrophage activation differ between these compartments and across different phases of sepsis. Finally, to explore the development of tissue-specific immunoregulatory mechanisms, we analyzed PD-L1 expression in macrophage subsets within each organ.

Materials and methods

Sepsis models

This study was conducted in accordance with the guide for the care and use of laboratory animals and approved by the ethics committee of the institution (ethical agreement number: 1846. This article was written based on the ARRIVE 2.0 guidelines [21]. C57BL/6 male mice (8–12 weeks old, 20–24 g) were purchased from Charles River (L’Arbresle, France). Sixty-three mice animals were randomly assigned to seven experimental groups: basal (n = 5), control (n = 5) 1 day, control (n = 5) 5 days, sham 1 day (n = 10), sham 5 days (n = 10), CLP 1 day (n = 14) and CLP5 days (n = 14) (Fig. 1). Moderate CLP was performed as previously described [22]. Briefly, mice were pre-treated with subcutaneous analgesic buprenorphine (Buprecare®, Axience, Pantin, France) at 0.05 mg/kg 30 min prior to surgery. They were then anesthetized via intraperitoneal injection of ketamine (Virbac, Carros France) at 1.25 mg/mL per 100 g and xylazine (Rompun® 2% Sèvres, France) at 0.5 mg/mL per 100 g. Anesthesia was maintained with inhaled isoflurane throughout the procedure (induction at 5% and maintenance at 2%). After laparotomy, the cecum was exposed, and ligated at its distal external third, corresponding to approximately 30% of its length. Sepsis was induced by two single punctures of the ligated cecum using a 21-gauge needle (not through-and-through). After needle withdrawal, a small droplet of fecal material was gently extruded from each puncture site to ensure patency before repositioning the cecum into the abdominal cavity. The abdominal wall was then closed in layers. Sham-operated animals underwent identical laparotomy, where the cecum was exposed but not ligated or punctured. Finally, mice were resuscitated with saline (0,1 ml/10 g body weight). Animals were monitored every 8 h to detect signs of distress. Pain was managed by subcutaneous administration of buprenorphine (0.05 mg/kg, s.c.) given 5–6 h after CLP and twice daily for the following two days. The animal received also antibiotic imipenem/cilastatin (MSD, Puteaux, France) at a dose of 25 mg/kg in NaCl administered intraperitoneally 5 h after surgery and then twice daily for 2 days. Sham-operated received treatment with antibiotics and anesthetic. The control mice, not subjected to laparotomy, were treated with the same dose of antibiotics and analgesic. In order to rule out drug effects on our model, the basal group was added. The basal mice were not subjected to manipulation and did not receive treatment. Mice were euthanized at 24 h or 5 days after procedure by intraperitoneal overdose of ketamine/xylazine. Humane endpoints were predefined in a scoring grid: a cumulative score above 10, or the presence of at least four classic signs of distress (anorexia with > 20% weight loss, hunching, prostration, impaired mobility, labored breathing, ruffled fur, and dehydration), mandated euthanasia. Among the 63 animals monitored, only one reached the criteria for euthanasia. No animals were excluded from the study.

Mice were euthanized at 24 h or 5 days after the procedure for sample collection. All procedures were performed in accordance with institutional ethical guidelines and ARRIVE 2.0 recommendations. Blood analysis

Blood samples were taken using a heparinized needle to cardiac puncture. Blood lactates and oxygen saturation were analyzed with a ABL90 Flex (Radiometer, Neuilly-283 Plaisance, France). The Konelab 30i, an automated chemistry analyser (Thermo Scientific) was used to measure serum aspartate aminotransferase (ASAT), alanine aminotransferase (ALAT), Alkaline phosphatase activity (PAL) and total bilirubin according to the manufacturer’s instructions and using standard methods. After analyses blood was centrifuged for 15 min at 1500 × g and 4 °C and plasma was aliquoted and stored at − 80 °C before analysis of cytokines.

Cytokine assay

The levels of cytokines were measured in plasma using a Milliplex® assay kit (Milliplex MAP mouse cytokine/chemokine magnetic bead panel, #MCYTOMAG-70 K, Millipore Corp, Darmstadt, Allemagne) using the Luminex MAPIX system with data analysis using xPONENT 4.2 software (Luminex Corporation, Texas, USA).

Quantitative reverse transcription PCR analysis

Lung and liver were collected and was stored at – 80 °C. Tissue was homogenized in lyse buffer and total RNA was isolated using a NucleoSpin® RNAII kit (Macherey-Nagel, Dueren, Germany), according to the manufacturer’s protocol. RNA quantity and purity were assessed with a Nanodrop ND‐1000 spectrophotometer (Nyxor Biotech, Paris, France). Total RNA (1 µg) was reverse‐transcribed into first‐strand cDNA using a High‐Capacity cDNA Achieve Kit (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Quantitative real-time PCRs (RT-qPCRs) were then performed using the fluorescent dye SYBR Green method, with SYBR Green PCR Master Mix in 384‐well plates on an ABI PRISM 7900HT system (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA). Primer pairs for each transcript were chosen with NCBI software (www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov. Amplification curves were read with the QuantStudio™ Real-Time PCR Software (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA), using the comparative cycle threshold method. The relative steady-state mRNA level was normalized with Hprt and Cyclob mRNA. The primers used for quantitative PCR (qPCR) analyses are listed in Table 1.

Hematoxylin-eosin-saffron (HES) staining

Specimens from lung and liver were fixed in 4% buffered formaldehyde. Tissues were dehydrated in ethanol and embedded in paraffin. Section (4 μm) of lung and liver were cut and stained HES (Hematoxylin, Eosin, Safran) on the LEICA ST 5020 automaton according to different steps, at the H2P2 histology platform.

Immunohistochemistry

Immunohistochemistry was performed on the DISCOVERY Ultra automaton (Roche ®, Illkirch, France). Paraffin blocks with a thickness of 4 μm were sectioned with a Microm HM340 microtome. The sections were collected on Super Frost Plus glass slides and dried in an oven for 1 h at 58 °C. The sections were deparaffinized for 8 min at 75 °C with EZ Prep Roche®. The antigenic sites were unmasked for 8 min at 95 °C, foolwed by 28 min at 100 °C with CC1® buffer (Tris EDTA Ph8). Endogenous peroxidases were inhibited with Discovery Inhibitor (Roche) for 10 min at room temperature. The primary antibodies (anti-CD206, anti CD86 and anti PD-L1) were then incubated for 60 min at 37 °C. Samples were washed in Tris pH 7.4 buffer. The secondary antibody Goat anti-Rabitt coupled to HRP (Roche) was then incubated for 16 min at 37 °C. The whole was revealed with diaminobenzydine (OmniMap DAB® Roche) for 8 min. Finally, counterstaining was performed with Mayer’s. The set was revealed with the rhodamine kit (Roche) during 8 min. The nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. The samples were dehydrated and mounted between slide and coverslip. The slides were finally digitized using the Hamamatsu Nanozoomer 2.O RS, at the H2P2 histology platform. The quantification of CD86, CD206 and PD-L1 expression was carried out using QuPath 4 (version 0.4.3). Entire tissue sections were analyzed in a blinded manner. To ensure an exhaustive evaluation of the organ-specific responses, the total surface area of each specimen was processed. A standardized detection threshold of 8 optical density units was applied to define positive cells. Using these annotated training objects, an object classifier was trained based on intensity and morphological results were expressed as the percentage of positive cells per total tissue area.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism and R (RStudio, version 2023.09.1 + 494) software. We assessed the distribution of the data using graphical methods and Shapiro–Wilk tests. As most variables did not follow a normal distribution, we chose to perform only non-parametric statistical tests. Data were analyzed using the Kruskal-Wallis test for multiple group comparisons. When significant differences were found, Dunn’s post-hoc test was applied to compare selected pairs of columns (Sham vs. CLP at 24 h and 5 days), significant differences (p < 0.05) are indicated in the figures Graphs are represented as median interquartile range. Additionally, to comply with reporting standards for uncertainty, Hodges-Lehmann effect sizes and their 95% confidence intervals were calculated and are presented in the Supplementary Table. These two methods provide complementary information regarding the magnitude and reliability of the observed differences. A summary table reporting the median, minimum, maximum, and quartiles for each variable at each time point is provided in the supplementary data. Statistical differences between the CLP 1 day and the sham 1 day are marked with ‘*’. Differences found between the CLP 5 days and the sham 5 days are indicated by the symbol ‘α’.

Results

Liver and lung failure in CLP mice model

To determine the organ injury in response to CLP, we evaluated acute organ failure with a focus on the respiratory system (PaO2/FiO2) and the liver (plasma levels of bilirubin and hepatic enzyme expression (Fig. 2a and f)). Respiratory and liver failure were confirmed by a decrease in arterial blood oxygen pressure (PaO2) and an increase in bilirubin levels after 5 days of CLP (Fig. 2a and c). We also observed that 24 h after CLP, but not after 5 days, an increase of hepatic enzyme ASAT and ALAT levels (Fig. 2e and f). However, no significative changes of PAL were observed (Fig. 2d).

Sepsis causes an acute-phase response (APR), which is a systemic innate reaction resulting in liver activation and is characterized by altered levels of circulating acute-phase proteins (APPs) [1]. We therefore investigated two APPs: C-reactive protein (Crp) and serum amyloid A (Saa) expression in the liver and the lung. We observed an increase of saa in lung and liver at 24 h (Fig. 2g and h), but not of Crp (Fig. 2i and j). Serum lactate level (> 2 mmol/L) was associated with shock status [1]; in all animals, the lactate levels remained below 2 mmol /L. However, we observed a difference between the lactate levels between the mice in the sham and CLP groups at 5 days (Fig. 2b).

In addition, respiratory and liver injury were also confirmed by histology. Liver histological sections revealed normal liver structure in the sham group (Fig. 3a) and 24 h after CLP (Fig. 3b). Conversely, 5 days after CLP we observed areas of necrosis in the liver (Fig. 3c) suggesting that CLP induced delayed liver injury. Lung histological section analysis revealed a normal lung structure and no changes in the lung parenchyma in the sham groups (Fig. 3d). In contrast, in the lungs of mice subjected to CLP, there are numerous areas with intra-alveolar oedema with a high cell density after CLP (Fig. 3e and f). We also observed that the thickened alveolar walls were associated with a predominantly mononuclear inflammatory infiltrate. The vessels were dilated and filled with red blood cells when compared to the sham group. These areas of intra-alveolar oedema with a high cell density area were more important after 5 days of CLP (Fig. 3f).

Compartmentalization of the inflammatory response in sepsis

As expected, CLP induced an increase in the plasma level of the cytokines such as TNFα, IL-6 and IL-10 in the first 24 h after CLP, but no significant difference was observed at 5 days (Fig. 4a, b and c). In contrast, there was no statistically significant increase of IL1-β in plasma (Fig. 4d). Although IL-1β was detectable in all experimental groups, it was not detectable in all individual animals. Interestingly, our original results showed that the levels of various cytokines in the liver and the lung differed from the systemic levels during sepsis (Fig. 4e and l). Indeed, we observed an increase of Il6 expression in the liver, but not in the lung 24 h after CLP (Fig. 4e and i). In contrast, a high Il10 expression was observed in the lung while any alteration significative of this cytokine was observed in the liver (Fig. 4k and g). Regarding the Tnf levels, they were increased in the lung and liver 24 h after CLP (Fig. 4f and j), whereas Il1β levels were only altered 5 days after CLP in both organs, with no significant changes in the plasma (Fig. 4h, l and d). These data confirm a compartmentalized inflammatory response in the function of each organ in the CLP sepsis model.

The CCL2/CCR2 interaction is necessary for the migration of Ly6Chigh CCR2⁺ pro-inflammatory monocytes from the peripheral blood to the tissues during inflammatory response. Similarly, the CX3CL1/CX3CR1 interaction plays a key role in the recruitment of Ly6Clow CX3CR1⁺ immunoregulatory monocytes in mice [23]. We therefore analyzed the expression of these chemokines (Fig. 5a and e) and their receptors involved in the migration of monocytes (Fig. 6a and d). We observed an increase in CCL2 levels in three compartments: plasma, liver and lung (Fig. 5a and c, and 5e) 24 h after CLP but no significant difference was observed at 5 days of CLP. Additionally, we observed a decrease in plasma CX3CL1 levels 5 days after CLP (Fig. 5b). Interestingly, these results revealed a sixfold increase in Ccl2 in the lung, which was more pronounced than liver 5 days post-CLP, where only a twofold increase was observed (Fig. 5c and e). However, no significant changes were observed in Cx3cl1 level in lung and liver (Fig. 5d and f). Regarding Ccr2 and Cx3cr1 expression an increased was observed in the lung at 1 and 5 days, whereas no significant changes were observed in the liver (Figs. 6a-d).

Expression of enzymes involved in the production of reactive oxygen is altered in liver but not in lung after CLP, and this does not seem to involve cytochrome P450

Oxidative stress and inflammatory responses have been shown to influence macrophage polarization [24]. Furthermore, the downregulation or altered expression of CX3CR1 and CCR2, two G-protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs) has been proposed as a potential hallmark of immunosuppression during sepsis, possibly driven by oxidative stress [25, 26]. To explore the hypothesis that the lack of Cx3cr1 and Ccr2 increased expression in the liver may be linked to oxidative stress, we analyzed the expression of key enzymes involved in the production and regulation of reactive oxygen and nitrogen species (Figs. 7a-f). Our results showed an upregulation of superoxide dismutase (sod) and catalase in the liver (Fig. 7a and c), but no significant difference was observed in the lung (Fig. 7b and d). We also observed a sustained increase in Nos2 expression in the liver at both 24 h and 5 days post-CLP (Fig. 7e), whereas in the lung, Nos2 expression appeared to decrease over time (Fig. 7f). Furthermore, we demonstrated that this oxidative imbalance in the liver was not associated with cytochrome P450 expression, as evidenced by a decrease in Cyp3a13 expression (Fig. 7g) and no significative alterations in other cytochromes, such as Cyp2e1 (Fig. 7h).

Metalloproteases and their physiological inhibitors, TIMPs, are involved in the compartmentalisation of the response in CLP-induced sepsis

Another group of mediators involved in organ dysfunction during sepsis are metalloproteases. Indeed matrix metalloproteinase has been shown to influence cell death by regulating oxidative stress and inflammatory responses [27, 28].

We further analyzed the expression of mmp-2 and mmp-9, as well as their inhibitors timp1 and timp3 (Fig. 8a-h). Our results showed that CLP is associated with increased levels of mmp-2 and mmp-9 in both the liver and the lung (Fig. 8a, b, e and f). However, a more intense and immediate increase in mmps was observed in the lung (Figs. 7e and 8f) when compared to the liver (8a and 8b). This may be linked to a greater expression of its specific inhibitor, timp1, in the liver (Figs. 8c) compared to the lung (8 g) 24 h after CLP. No significant change in mmp-3 (data not showed) and timp3, a non-specific timp, expression was observed in either the liver or the lung.

Profiles of M1 and M2 macrophages by immunohistochemistry

To determine whether the compartmentalization of the inflammatory response involves different subtypes of macrophages, we analyzed the expression of the M1 marker CD86 (pro-inflammatory) and the M2 marker CD206 (immunoregulatory) by immunohistochemistry (Fig. 9a and f’). The CD86 expression was observed in sham and basal animals in the liver (Fig. 9a and b) whereas no significant differences were observed at 24 h after CLP (Fig. 9c and d). Similarly, no significant differences were observed in CD206 expression at 24 h after CLP in both liver and lung (Fig. 9g-j). However, CLP increased the expression of both CD86 and CD206 markers in the liver (Fig. 9e, f, l and m) at 5 days post-CLP. A similar result was observed in the lung (Fig. 9n-y). Interestingly, a reduction in CD86 labelling intensity was observed in the lung, whereas the liver exhibited no change in CD86 intensity (Fig. 9a’and 9b’). In contrast, the intensity of CD206 remained unchanged in both organs (Fig. 9c’and 9d’). Although the numbers of both M1 and M2 -like macrophages increased in the liver and lung, CD206-positive cells were more abundant in the lung when normalized to the total number of cells in each organ. In contrast, CD86 expression was more prominent in the liver (Fig. 9e’and 9f).

Next, we aimed to determine whether the same macrophages could express both markers, to assess whether resident macrophages could switch their profiles. To address this question, we performed a co-staining analysis of CD86 and CD206. Discussion

The objective of this study was to compare the systemic inflammatory response with the inflammatory response in the lung and liver compartments at different stages of sepsis and to investigate the potential role of macrophages in the compartmentalization of this response.

The liver and lung are central organs in the pathophysiology of sepsis, orchestrating both systemic inflammation and tissue-specific immune responses. These mechanisms are largely mediated by Kupffer cells and alveolar macrophages. Our findings reveal a clear organ-specific immune divergence characterized by differential monocyte recruitment (CCL2/CCR2), distinct oxidative stress patterns, altered MMP/TIMP balance, and differences in CD86/CD206 expression. Notably, PD-L1 expression was markedly increased in the lung but not in the liver, predominantly in CD206⁺ macrophages, suggesting the establishment of a lung-specific immunosuppressive microenvironment during the late phase of sepsis.

Severe sepsis is typically characterized by elevated plasma levels of both pro- and anti-inflammatory cytokines. Consistent with this, we are showed that plasma levels of TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-10 peaked within the first 24 h following CLP. A similar kinetic profile was detected in the liver for both Il6 and Tnf-α, which supports the role of the liver as a major contributor to the early systemic inflammatory response. In contrast, the lung showed increased Tnf-α and Il10 gene expression without significant Il6 induction, indicating a more regulatory local response.

The liver plays a central role in host defense through rapid activation of Kupffer cells via pattern recognition receptors, leading to the production of TNF-α, IL-6, and Nos2, and stimulation of acute-phase protein synthesis. In line with this, we observed increased hepatic Saa expression at 24 h post-CLP, whereas CRP remained poorly informative, consistent with previous reports in murine models [29, 30]. Early increases in ASAT and ALAT were followed by histological evidence of necrosis at day 5, suggesting that biochemical alterations precede structural damage. This delayed injury may result from progressive microcirculatory dysfunction, and sustained nitric oxide production in the liver. Indeed, sustained nitric oxide production may impair mitochondrial respiration, inhibit cytochrome P450 activity, and promote oxidative and nitrosative stress, thereby sensitizing hepatocytes to delayed injury. In line with this mechanism, we observed an early decreases expression of Cyp3a13-related genes and an increase of Nos2 expression altogether supporting as well as supporting a model in which early functional hepatic impairment precedes a role for oxidative and nitrosative stress in overt structural damage [31]. Moreover, ASAT is not liver-specific and may originate from cardiac or skeletal muscle injury, suggesting that its early elevation may reflect a systemic inflammatory response rather than exclusive hepatocellular necrosis. Additional biomarkers, such as cardiac troponins, would help clarify the contribution of extrahepatic tissues. At day 5 post-CLP, bilirubin levels were significantly increased and coincided with the appearance of necrotic lesions. Although hyperbilirubinemia may also reflect hemolysis, it is widely considered in the context of sepsis as a marker of multiple organ dysfunction syndrome [32].

The lung is highly susceptible to inflammatory injury due to its extensive vascular interface. Early exposure to circulating mediators activates alveolar macrophages, which produce both pro-inflammatory and regulatory cytokines. While this response contributes to pathogen control, excessive activation may promote alveolar-capillary barrier dysfunction and increase the risk of acute lung injury. The sustained elevation of Il10 observed in the lung suggests a shift toward local immunoregulation that may limit tissue damage but also predispose to secondary infections [33, 34].

TNF-α is considered a central mediator in sepsis. In our study, Tnf-α was consistently elevated in liver, lung, and plasma, supporting its role as a systemic signaling hub that coordinates inflammation across organs. In contrast, other cytokines displayed organ-specific patterns, suggesting that while TNF-α provides a common pro-inflammatory background, the local microenvironment dictates distinct immune responses in each tissue.

Macrophages appear to be key drivers of this compartmentalization. We observed increased CD86 expression in both organs at day 5 post-CLP, indicating persistent activation of pro-inflammatory subsets. However, the lung contained a significantly higher proportion of CD206⁺ macrophages compared with the liver. This enrichment in CD206⁺ cells, together with lower CD86 expression, likely contributes to the elevated IL-10 levels and supports the development of a regulatory or immunosuppressive pulmonary environment. In contrast, the liver maintained a macrophage profile consistent with sustained pro-inflammatory activation.

Differences in macrophage recruitment may further contribute to these organ-specific responses. The CCL2/CCR2 axis, which mediates the recruitment of Ly6Chigh inflammatory monocytes, was strongly induced in the lung compared with the liver. Ccr2 expression also increased progressively in the lung but remained unchanged in the liver, suggesting preferential monocyte influx into pulmonary tissue. In parallel, persistent ccx3cr1 expression in the lung may favor the accumulation of immunoregulatory Ly6Clow monocytes, further promoting local immune modulation.

The disruption between MMPs and TIMPs can result in either excessive ECM degradation or accumulation and organ failure [1]. Extracellular matrix remodeling also differed between organs. Whereas MMP-2 is expressed by both M1 and M2 subtypes in a more constitutive or tissue-dependent manner, recent findings have shown that MMP-9 expression is higher in M2 macrophages and that these cells promote tumor progression via MMP-9 [35]. In our study, MMP-9 expression was induced more rapidly and strongly in the lung than in the liver. This may be partly explained by the higher proportion of M2 macrophages in the lung, contributing to increased MMP-9 levels and early onset of lung injury. By contrast, TIMP-1, the majors endogenous MMP-9 inhibitor, was more strongly upregulated in the liver (9.1 ± 3.6-fold) than in the lung (3.2 ± 1.3-fold), which may limit MMP-9 activity in the hepatic compartment. These findings align with those of Chinnaiyan et al. [26], who reported approximately twofold higher TIMP expression in the liver compared to the lung 24 h after CLP in rats.

Regarding the temporal translation of our findings, the 5 day post-CLP point in our murine model aligns with the emerging window of immunoparalysis described in human sepsis [22, 36]. In clinical settings, however, this transition is more heterogeneous. While humans often show signs of immunosuppression within the first week (e.g., persistent low HLA-DR expression), this phase can extend far beyond two weeks depending on the patient’s genetic background and comorbidities [36]. Thus, while our day 5 data capture the onset of organ-specific immune remodeling equivalent to early human immunoparalysis, clinical translation must account for the broader and more variable temporal window seen in ICU patients.

The spatiotemporal divergence between the liver and lung signatures identified in our study suggests distinct therapeutic windows for sepsis management. The early, localized pulmonary inflammation, driven by CCL2/CCR2-mediated recruitment and an M2-dominant shift by day 5, points toward the lung as a critical site for immune checkpoint intervention. PD-L1 expression on CD206⁺ macrophages in the lung suggests the establishment of an immune checkpoint–mediated regulatory environment [37]. Recent studies have shown that PD-L1–expressing macrophages actively suppress T-cell function and contribute to sepsis-induced immunoparalysis [20]. In this context, our findings support the idea that pulmonary macrophages do not simply shift toward an M2 phenotype, but actively participate in immune evasion mechanisms, potentially increasing susceptibility to secondary infections.Our observation of a significant CD206+ population in the lung at 5 days post-CLP suggests that the lung may be particularly prone to PD-L1-mediated immune evasion, making it a potential candidate for localized anti-PD-L1 therapies to restore immune competence.

In contrast, the liver’s profile is characterized by sustained oxidative stress (Nos2, SOD, Catalase) and a predominant pro inflammatory macrophage activation states (CD86+) phenotype, aligns with more aggressive cell death pathways. In addition to classical oxidative injury, recent studies have highlighted the role of mitochondria-driven regulated cell death pathways, including pyroptosis, necroptosis, and more recently cuproptosis, in inflammatory conditions [38–41].These pathways share common features with the mechanisms observed in our model, particularly mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress. In the liver, the sustained induction of Nos2 and the downregulation of cyp-related genes suggest a persistent alteration of mitochondrial homeostasis, which may sensitize hepatocytes to delayed regulated cell death rather than immediate necrosis. This is consistent with emerging evidence indicating that oxidative stress and metabolic disruption act as central triggers of these cell death programs [42]. Regarding the oxidative imbalance, it is plausible that the liver’s inflammatory trajectory is driven by these conserved signaling oxidative stress and macrophage-derived cytokines prime the tissue for programmed cell death. Understanding this organ-specific molecular clock where the lung moves toward immunosuppression while the liver remains in a hyper-oxidative state is crucial. It suggests that systemic antioxidant or anti-inflammatory treatments may fail because they do not account for the fact that one organ may require immune stimulation (to combat lung immunoparalysis) while another requires oxidative protection (to prevent liver necrosis).

This study has limitations. First, no formal power analysis was performed due to the exploratory nature of the study and the complexity of multi-compartment analyses. Sample sizes varied between experiments due to technical constraints inherent to multi-organ analyses in murine models. To improve transparency, confidence intervals and exact sample sizes have been reported where appropriate. These limitations may affect the statistical power of certain comparisons and should be considered when interpreting the results. Second, interspecies differences must be considered when extrapolating murine findings to human sepsis, particularly regarding pulmonary anatomy and innate immune responses. Third, although the CLP model remains the reference for polymicrobial sepsis, variability in surgical parameters and microbial composition may influence the inflammatory response [43, 44].These limitations must be taken into account when interpreting and generalizing our findings to human pathophysiology.

Taken together, our results suggest that the compartmentalization of the inflammatory response is driven by organ-specific monocyte recruitment and distinct macrophage polarization profiles. These findings highlight the critical role of macrophage phenotypes and their mediators in the pathophysiology of sepsis, as well as their variability between organs. Although the underlying regulatory mechanisms remain to be fully elucidated, macrophages represent both promising therapeutic targets and potential biomarkers for organ-specific responses during sepsis.

Conclusion

This study highlights the organ-specific nature of the inflammatory response during sepsis, particularly in the lung and liver, two key immunometabolic organs. While the liver showed a more pronounced oxidative imbalance and increased levels of Il6, Saa, and Timp1, the lung exhibited higher Ccl2 expression and a greater number of CD206-positive macrophages, likely contributing to its elevated Il10 production. Moreover, we have showed a marked organ-specific immune divergence during sepsis, identifying the lung as a major site of PD-L1–mediated immunoregulation, predominantly within the CD206⁺ subset, indicating the establishment of a compartmentalized immunosuppressive environment.

Our findings suggest that the orchestration of the immune response during sepsis is not uniform across organs but instead tailored to the local microenvironment and cellular composition. Macrophages appear to play a central role in this process, not only as effectors of inflammation and resolution but also as potential regulators of tissue-specific immune dynamics through surface receptors such as CD86 and CD206. Furthermore, the differential expression of chemokines, Mmp/Timps and PD-L1 between compartments reinforces the concept of localized immune modulation. Although further studies are needed to elucidate the mechanisms underlying these compartmentalized responses our data provide new insights into how sepsis induces divergent inflammatory trajectories across organs. Ultimately, this work supports the development of organ-targeted therapeutic approaches and the identification of macrophage-related biomarkers that could refine sepsis prognosis and treatment.
