Pulmonary echinococcosis caused by Echinococcus canadensis G8 in a white-tailed deer (Odocoileus virginianus), Pennsylvania, USA, 2026
Authors: Kayla B Garrett, Ethan P Barton, Justin D Brown, Michael J Yabsley, Christopher A Cleveland
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCystic echinococcosis, caused by the two zoonotic cestodes Echinococcus granulosus and Echinococcus canadensis, is emerging in new regions of the United States and Canada. In January 2026, an adult female hunter-harvested white-tailed deer (Odocoileus virginianus) from Pennsylvania, USA exhibited pulmonary hydatid cysts. Morphologic identification of metacestodes in cystic fluid and subsequent PCR and sequencing confirmed this deer was infected with Echinococcus canadensis G8. Although E. canadensis commonly infects cervids at northern latitudes, recent infections in definitive wild canid hosts have been documented in the eastern USA. Published reports of E. canadensis in white-tailed deer, the most popular game species in the eastern USA, are rare and confirm that white-tailed deer are viable intermediate host species in the eastern USA. These findings further emphasize the presence of E. canadensis in the eastern United States, highlighting the risk that these parasites may pose to humans, domestic animals, livestock, and wildlife.Keywords: Ruminants, Odocoileus virginianus, Echinococcus, Parasites, Lung, Cysts

Introduction

Echinococcus species are globally distributed cestodes found in numerous domestic and wildlife species [1]. Several Echinococcus species are zoonotic posing significant health risks to humans [1]. In the United States, the two most common species (E. multilocularis and E. canadensis [G8 and G10 genotypes]) utilize wild and domestic canids as definitive hosts and rodents and cervids as intermediate hosts, respectively, and recent infections in humans have been noted in the eastern United States [1], [2], [3], [4], [5].

Methods

In January 2026, an adult female white-tailed deer (Odocoileus virginianus - WTD) in good physical condition (∼50 kg) was hunter harvested in Lycoming County, Pennsylvania, USA. During field dressing, 5–6 cysts were observed in the lungs. No other internal organs were examined. Three of the four intact cysts were found in the parenchyma of the diaphragmatic lobes of the lungs (both sides) while one was found in the medial accessory lung lobe. Two segments of lung (approximately 6-cm-diameter) containing cysts were preserved at −20 °C approximately three hours postmortem.

Lung samples containing one cyst each were submitted to the Southeastern Cooperative Wildlife Disease Study (SCWDS, Athens, Georgia) and Pennsylvania State University (University Park, Pennsylvania) for evaluation. Grossly, lung samples contained circular fluid filled cysts (∼2.5 cm × 2.5 cm) and fluid from the cysts contained numerous protoscoleces morphologically consistent with Echinococcus (Fig. 1).

A) Pulmonary hydatid cyst in a white-tailed deer (Odocoileus virginianus) from Pennsylvania, USA. B) Echinococcus canadensis G8 metacestode collected from the fluid of the cyst at 40× on an Olympus C30 microscope.

Genomic DNA was extracted from protoscoleces and the cyst wall using a QIAGEN DNeasy Blood and Tissue Extraction Kit (Germantown, Maryland, USA). A region of the cytochrome c oxidase (COI) gene was amplified using primers JB3 and JB4.5 (Garrett et al., 2023). Amplicons were purified from gels using the QIAquick gel extraction kit (Qiagen). Purified amplicons were submitted to GENEWIZ (South Plainfield, New Jersey, USA) for bidirectional Sanger sequencing. Chromatographs were analyzed and a phylogenetic tree constructed using Geneious Prime v2021.1.1 (Auckland, New Zealand, https://www.geneious.com).

Results

Sequences from the protoscoleces and cyst wall were identical and the 396 bp consensus sequence was 100% identical to several E. canadensis G8 sequences (i.e., MG561272, MG574826, MG574823–24, OP068166, AB235848). Phylogenetically, the sequence clustered with other E. canadensis G8 samples from the US and Canada (Fig. 2).

Conclusions

The morphologic and molecular data confirm the detection of E. canadensis G8 in a WTD from Pennsylvania. Although E. canadensis G8 has a circumpolar distribution in the Northern Hemisphere, it is increasingly reported in new areas of the eastern US, either due to enhanced surveillance or emergence. Previous reports of E. canadensis in Pennsylvania from coyotes (Canis latrans) were also G8, but these wild canid detections were clustered in northeastern Pennsylvania, while the current WTD case was from northcentral Pennsylvania [3], [4]. Additionally, E. canadensis has been reported in coyotes and moose (Alces alces) in the Northeastern US and Canada [6], [7]. Two human cases of presumed E. canadensis (reported as E. granulosus) have also been reported in New Hampshire, USA [2]. These data indicate that the risk of E. canadensis infection is broader than previously recognized and appears to be widespread in the Northeastern US.

The two cysts examined microscopically were both fertile with dozens of protoscoleces apparent, indicating WTD are suitable intermediate hosts. Although infections have been noted in WTD previously, prevalence of E. canadensis G10 was generally lower in WTD compared with sympatric elk (Cervus canadensis), caribou/reindeer (Rangifer tarandus), and moose in Canada [8]. Historical studies in California, USA, also reported a low prevalence (average 1.3%) in mule deer (Odocoileus hemionus), although prevalence at one site was high (24%) [9]. However, data for WTD are too limited to determine if they are more resistant to infection or if infection risk varies due to habitat use or other factors. Many regions in the eastern US have expanding populations of elk and moose, so these species should be monitored.

Surveillance for Echinococcus in the US has predominantly been focused on surveillance of domestic ruminants during routine abattoir inspections by food safety experts and researchers conducting sporadic necropsy of wild canids and conducting PCR testing of domestic dog fecal samples [3], [4], [10], [11]. Examination of wild cervids in the US has been limited and is often related to incidental detections of hunter-harvested animals (e.g., moose in Maine and the current report in a WTD) instead of systematic surveillance [6]. Importantly, one cyst in our current PA case was grossly visible; the remaining cysts were located within the lung parenchyma and were detected only after the lung was palpated. Thus, efforts to conduct surveillance for E. canadensis in cervids should ensure that lungs are carefully examined as many cysts could be missed if only the outside of the lung is examined. Also, although other organs were not examined in this study, most cysts are generally found in the lung [8].

There are also increasing reports of a related parasite, E. multilocularis, in the eastern US. This parasite also uses domestic and wild canids as definitive hosts but uses rodents as intermediate hosts instead of ungulates. The two parasites can be distinguished by cyst morphology and by sequence analysis. Most reports of Echinococcus infections in domestic dogs in the eastern US have been due to E. multilocularis, which may be related to dogs being more likely to ingest rodents than cervids. Because the eggs of both Echinococcus species are morphologically similar to Taenia, a common intestinal parasite of dogs, veterinarians should conduct molecular testing to distinguish these parasites.

Echinococcus species affect the health of humans, wildlife, domestic pets and livestock. Additionally, the eggs that are shed in wild canid feces can remain in the environment for extended periods of time, thus making the environment a possible source of infection for intermediate hosts and humans [12]. These factors make Echinococcus spp. an important One Health pathogen, especially as infections and transmission are reported from new regions. Most studies on these parasites in the US are focused on surveillance in intermediate hosts such as the one described here and description of clinical consequences of infections in domestic dogs, both of which are important. However, these parasites are of global concern and there are many unknowns. Examples that need additional research, especially in the United States, include a unified genetic typing scheme and population genetics data to help inform introduction and spread, the role of the wildlife community in transmission (e.g., globally there needs to be more work on the role of community structure and diversity on transmission [13]), and other transmission risks (e.g., are protoscoleces from livestock or wildlife directly infectious to other intermediate or aberrant hosts such as humans or domestic dogs). Further research is needed to address these knowledge gaps and also to to understand the role of white-tailed deer in the eastern US for the establishment and transmission of E. canadensis.

In conclusion, we detected E. canadensis G8 infection in a WTD, the most popular game species in the eastern United States, which expands the known range of this parasite in the Northeastern US and indicates that WTD may plan an important role in the maintenance of this emerging zoonotic pathogen in the region. Continued surveillance of wild and domestic canids, as well as cervids, is needed to evaluate infection risks in the US.
