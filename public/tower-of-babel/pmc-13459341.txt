Comprehensive epidemiological analyses of urinary stone diseases in eastern China: a regional population-based study
Authors: Dawei Wang, Yongle Zhan, Ning Zhang, Da Huang, Jingyi Huang, Jinlun Huang, Yu Zhu, Brian Sze-Ho Ho, Ada Tsui-Lin Ng, James Hok-Leung Tsu, Xiaohao Ruan, Rong Na
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundsUrinary stone disease (USD, or urolithiasis) is common and poses a substantial healthcare and economic burden on the working-age population. This study aimed to provide epidemiological insights into the prevalence, incidence, trends, and risk factors of USD among the Chinese population.MethodsWe conducted a prospective cohort study involving 966,481 participants from the CHinese Electronic health Records Research during 2009–2022 in Yinzhou, China (CHERRY). Temporal trends were estimated using annual percentage changes (APC) via Joinpoint regression analyses. Stratified Cox proportional hazards regression and propensity score analyses were employed to evaluate potential risk factors, population attribution factor (PAF) and number needed to prevent (NNTP).ResultsFrom 2014/2015 to 2021, there was an annualized increase in USD incidence by 5.3–6.8% (P < 0.05). Identified risk factors for USD included tobacco smoking, alcohol drinking, high body mass index, diabetes, hyperlipidemia, hypertension, and cardiovasculardisease while regular exercising served as a protective factor of USD. The PAF ranged from 1.3% for non-drinking behavior to 22.5% for regular exercising (weekly). Correspondingly, the NNTP ranged from 182 for non-smoking behavior to 21 for weekly exercising.ConclusionsUSD is a common disease affecting approximately 1 in 10 Chinese, with the incidence rate increasing by 5–6% annually over the past 6–7 years. Lifestyle factors and metabolic syndromes are potential risk factors for the development of USD.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-27890-4.Keywords: Chinese, Incidence, Lifestyle, Risk factors, Population growth, Urinary calculi

Introduction

Urinary stone disease (USD, or urolithiasis) is a widespread contemporary health issue defined by the presence of stones and calcifications within the urinary tract, including the kidneys, ureters, bladder, and urethra. Globally, prevalence rates vary significant ranging from 7 to 13% in North America, 5–9% in Europe [1], and 1%-19.1% in Asia. Within Asia, prevalence remains relatively low (approximately 1%–8%) in most of East and North Asia, but is notably higher (5%–19.1%) in West, Southeast, and South Asia, as well As in developed nations like Japan and South Korea [2]. In China, the prevalence reportedly rose from 6.6% before 2010, to 7.3% during 2011–2015, reaching 10.5% during the 2015–2020 period [3]. This disease poses a considerable healthcare and economic burden on the working-age population. However, incidence data based on disease burden models present a conflicting trend. According to the Global Burden of Disease Study 2021, while the total number of new cases increased by 26.7% from 2000 to 2021, the global age-standardized incidence rate of urolithiasis actually decreased by 17.5%. In Asia, incidence declined sharply in East Asia (e.g., China, -35.1%), while it increased in parts of Southeast Asia and South Asia [4]. This inconsistency between incidence (reflecting the speed of new case occurrence) and prevalence (reflecting the total burden of existing cases in the population) highlights the limitations and challenges of current research. Most existing studies in China are cross-sectional surveys [3], providing prevalence data that fails to capture dynamic long-term trends or precise risk factors for new incident cases. These studies often have limited sample sizes, rely on self-reported diagnoses, and exhibit significant heterogeneity among different regions and cities, compromising the comparability and generalizability of the results. Furthermore, the risk factor spectrum for USD remains ambiguous [5]. Previous research indicated that age, diet, and metabolic syndromes (obesity, diabetes, hypertension, etc.) could be the potential risk factors for USD [6, 7]. In mainland China, factors like diet [8, 9], metabolic disease history [7, 10, 11] and specific urine or serum indexes [12], have been repeatedly assessed in most observational research. Consequently, a comprehensive assessment of USD risk factors within a large-scale, population-based study is necessary.

Given a poor understanding of the epidemiology of urinary stone disease in China, we conducted a prospective population-based cohort study to investigate the incidence, prevalence, secular trends as well as risk factors of USD, aiming to provide evidence-based insights for prevention.

Materials and methods

Study population

Data were obtained from the CHinese Electronic health Records Research in Yinzhou (CHERRY) Study, which is the first population-based cohort study linking big data of integrated individual-level electronic health records, comprising 98% of permanent residents with a valid healthcare identifier in Yinzhou, a district/city in East China. Detailed information regarding study design, inclusion criteria, data collection and procedure of the CHERRY study was described elsewhere [13]. Participants are followed up for USD using records linkage with the regional system of electronic medical records based on diagnostic codes from the International Classification of Diseases, 10th revision (ICD-10, N20-N23). This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the University of Hong Kong/Hospital Authority Hong Kong West Cluster (HKU/HA HKW IRB, IRB reference number: UW 22–766).

In the present study, we excluded participants with missing sex or age data (n = 20) and those with a diagnosis prior to the baseline interview (n = 82,006), resulting in a final analytical cohort of 966,481 participants.

Assessment of covariates

Baseline surveys utilized structured questionnaires to assess demographic information (age, gender, education level, marital status, monthly income), body mass index (BMI, categorized by Chinese standard [8]: underweight: <18.5 kg/m2; normal: 18.5–23.9 kg/m2; overweight: 24–27.9 kg/m2; obese: ≥28 kg/m2), self-reported chronic disease history (diabetes, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, and coronary heart disease), lifestyle habits (smoking, drinking, sleep quality), and physical activity (never, monthly but less than weekly and weekly). Smoking and drinking status were defined based on self-reported history. Participants reporting any history of smoking (daily, occasional, or former) were classified as “ever smokers”; all others as “never smokers”. Similarly, those reporting any alcohol consumption (regular or occasional) were classified as “ever drinkers”; all others as “never drinkers”. Multiple imputation was applied to address the missing data issue adjusting for complete variables (age at baseline and sex). Covariates with missing rate exceeding 80% (monthly income and sleep quality) were excluded.

Statistical analysis

Follow-up person-years (PYs) was calculated from the record date to the date of USD diagnosis, last follow-up, or Aug 3rd, 2022, whichever comes first. Crude, age- and sex-standardized Incidence rates (IR) and prevalence rates were calculated based on the 2020 censuses. Poisson regression models with an offset term of log transformed PYs were used to estimate incidence rate ratio (IRR). Temporal trends were estimated by annual percentage changes (APC) and the best-fitting joinpoints using Joinpoint regression analyses [14].

Multivariable hazard Cox regression models were used to calculate adjusted hazard ratio (HR) with stratification jointly by age-at-disease-risk groups (in 5-year intervals) in the adult population. Subgroup analyses were conducted to test whether the associations between exposure factors and risk of diseases were consistent in men and women. Likelihood ratio tests were implemented for the interaction, comparing Cox regression models with or without interaction terms (including education level, marital status, smoking, drinking, BMI, physical activity and chronic disease history). We also conducted the following sensitivity analyses: (1) only containing participants who developed upper urinary tract calculus (kidney or ureters); (2) excluding participants who developed urolithiasis during the first two years of follow-up.

Propensity score matching in modifiable risk factor groups (modifiable risk factors: smoking, drinking, BMI and exercising) was performed by including major confounders: age, sex, and follow-up time. The population attributable fraction (PAF) was calculated by the Bruzzi method [15]. Risk differences (RD) and the number needed to prevent (NNTP) were estimated by multivariable log-binominal regression model. E value was also used to evaluate the potential unmeasured confounding [16, 17].

All P values were two-sided and P < 0.05 was considered statistical significance. The analyses were performed by Stata (version 17.0, StataCorp, College Station, Texas, USA), R Statistical Software (version 4.1.2, Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) with R packages: “dplyr”, “tableone”, “survival”, “survminer”, “MatchIt”, “AF”, “logbin” and “EValue”.

Results

Rates and annual trends

A total of 966,481 participants were included, with a median age of 37.4 years (IQR: 27.5–50.1) and a nearly equal sex distribution (51.8% male, 48.2% female) (Table 1). 84,591 incident urolithiasis (USD) cases were documented over a median follow-up of 8.4 years (7.8 million PYs). Kidney stones accounted for the majority of cases (64.7%), followed by ureteric (18.7%), urethral (4.5%), and bladder stones (0.6%) (Table S1). The crude incidence rate (IR) was significantly higher in men (1,324 per 100,000 PYs, 95% Confidence interval [CI]: 1,312-1,336) than in women (875 per 100,000 PYs,95%CI: 867–885), yielding an incidence rate ratio (IRR) of 1.55 (95% CI: 1.49–1.54, P < 0.001)(Table 2). Age-specific analysis revealed the highest IR in the 60–69 age group (1,815 per 100,000 person-years), which was 7.14 times (95% CI: 6.87–7.41) higher than that in the 0–19 age group (P < 0.001). The age- and sex-adjusted prevalence rates were 9.0% (95%CI: 8.9-9.0) and 10.4% (95% CI: 10.4–10.5; 12.5% in men and 8.4% in women), respectively (Table S2). Joinpoint regression analysis identified a trend inflection point around 2014. The IR showed a non-significant decrease from 2010 to 2014 (annual percent change, APC = -8.1%, 95% CI: -17.2 to 2.1%, P = 0.099), followed by a significant increase from 2014 to 2021 (APC = 6.5%, 95% CI: 2.8 to 10.4%, P = 0.004). Over the entire study period, the average annual percent change (AAPC) was 1.0% (95% CI: -2.7 to 4.7%, P = 0.605), indicating an overall stable trend (Fig. 1A). Similar patterns were observed for sex-adjusted and age-adjusted IRs (Fig. 1B and C).

Potential risk factors

Univariable Poisson regression models showed significantly different characteristics between cases and controls (Table 2). USD were more likely to occur in individuals with lower education level, non-single status (married or ever married), higher body mass index (BMI), tobacco smoking, alcohol drinking and no exercising, and comorbidities (including hypertension, hyperlipidemia, diabetes mellitus and cardiovascular disease). Multivariable Cox regression revealed that lower education level, non-single marital status, higher BMI, lack of regular exercise, and comorbidities (hypertension, hyperlipidemia, diabetes, cardiovascular disease) (all P < 0.001) were consistently associated with an increased risk of USD (Fig. 2). Notably, significant sex interactions were observed for smoking and alcohol consumption (P for interaction < 0.05). Among men, alcohol consumption increased USD risk (HR = 1.05, 95% CI: 1.02–1.08, P < 0.001), while smoking showed no significant association (P = 0.636). Conversely, among women, both smoking (HR = 0.65, 95% CI: 0.60–0.69, P < 0.001) and alcohol consumption (HR = 0.86, 95% CI: 0.83–0.90, P < 0.001) were associated with a significantly lower risk of USD. No significant sex interaction was found for exercise frequency (P = 0.208). Sensitivity analyses yielded consistent results (Tables S3 & S4).

Propensity score matching in modifiable risk factors

To further evaluate the risk factors for USD and diminish the confounding bias in the analyses, propensity score matching was performed to assess the independent effects of modifiable risk factors (smoking, alcohol consumption, BMI, exercise). All factors remained significantly associated with USD risk (all P < 0.05) (Table 3). Regular exercise (at least once weekly) showed the strongest protective effect (HR = 0.63, 95% CI: 0.60–0.66) and the highest population attributable fraction (PAF = 22.5%, 95% CI: 20.3–24.6%) with an NNTP of 21 (95% CI: 19–25), indicating that one USD case could be prevented for every 21 additional individuals exercising regularly. Maintaining a BMI below 24 kg/m² was also protective (HR = 0.92, 95% CI: 0.90–0.93; NNTP = 61, 95% CI: 55–69). In contrast, smoking and alcohol consumption showed weaker associations with risk (HR ≈ 1.03 for both) and low PAFs (1.3% and 1.5%, respectively). The E-value for regular exercise was 2.45, suggesting relative robustness to potential unmeasured confounding.

Discussion

The present large-scale prospective study demonstrates that: (1) the incidence of USD reached 1,275 per 100,000 PYs in 2021 and has grown significantly in recent years; (2) social status (such as marital status and education level), lifestyle and metabolic disease are critical risk factors for stone diseases; (3) lifestyle interventions aimed at modifying risk factors can substantially prevent a considerable proportion of USD incidence.

The burden of acute management may be more accurately assessed via annual cumulative incidence or APC rather than the prevalence rate. However, the incidence of new USD cases has not been well described in China in recent years. While the prevalence rate of USD in this cohort was similar to the self-reported rates in recent survey-based studies from mainland China, Taiwan, Japan, Korea and the United States [1, 7, 18–21]. However, our reported IRs (e.g. 1275 per 100,000 PYs in 2021) were higher than those observed among participants aged 30–79 years in the China Kadoorie Biobank (overall IR, 230 per 100,000 PYs) or the middle-aged elderly urban population in the Shanghai Women/Men’s health study (overall IR, 263.2 per 100,000 PYs) [22]. Furthermore, our IR was lower than the 12-month incidence of kidney stones reported in the U.S. National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) (2,054 per 100,000 adults aged > 20 yrs) in 2015–2018 [20]. It was, however, close to the estimated annual cumulative incidence of stone occurrence (~ 1%) by the Medical Expenditure Panel Survey in the United States [23]. Our cohort reflects a more representative and natural annual incidence rate (or incidence intensity) at a population level [24]. The heterogeneity observed across studies could be attributed to the pre-selection of population characteristics (e.g., elderly populations, urban vs. rural regions) and varying sampling strategies. The Joinpoint regression identified an inflection point around 2014–2015, likely reflecting a combination of improved disease detection due to increased imaging use (ultrasound or CT) accompanied by advances in lithotripsy technology, environmental changes such as climate warming, and major healthcare policy reforms that enhanced access and altered care-seeking behavior.

This is the first study to evaluate whether specific preventive measures aimed at modifying risk factors could substantially reduce the burden of kidney stones in the Chinese population. Smoking is hypothesized to increase the risk of kidney stone disease through various mechanisms such as, increased vasopressin levels, and the enrichment of urinary calcium and metabolic elements) [5]. At least four studies have shown a positive association between cigarette smoking and disease risk [5], which is concordant with our findings. Chen et al. also suggested that secondhand smoke could be a risk factor for urolithiasis formation [25]. However, the effect of alcohol intake appeared discrepant between genders in this cohort. Findings from the China Kadoorie Biobank and UK Biobank studies suggested that alcohol consumption might act as a protective factor with the risk of kidney stones decreasing as alcohol volume increased, primarily in European populations [9, 26]. Hypothesized mechanisms include alcohol-induced alkalinization of urine, which may inhibit uric acid stone formation [27]. Conversely, our results suggested that alcohol intake had a harmful effect in males and the overall population but a protective effect in females. Behavioral habits like smoking and alcohol consumption are often coupled and influenced by extrinsic factors such as education level, working conditions and region. The observed gender differences might result from the combined effects of sociocultural factors (which shape the frequency and patterns of behavior, e.g. the social stigma of smoking and alcohol consumption among Chinese females [28]) and biological factors, such as differences in alcohol metabolism enzymes [29] or hormonal influences [30]. Because data regarding the volume, frequency, or types of smoking and drinking were not available in the present study. Hence, the result of smoking or drinking effect should be interpreted with cautions. Future studies with detailed measurements of urinary composition and hormonal profiles are warranted to elucidate the exact biological pathways underlying these gender-specific associations.

The most robust finding in this study was that metabolic-related diseases, higher BMI and lower exercise frequency were associated with an increased risk of stones. These associations were also confirmed in several Chinese and Western cohort studies [22, 31–33]. However, previous studies in the Chinese population found conflicting results regarding exercising. Zhuo et al. observed that increased exercise duration was a protective factor against urolithiasis (OR = 0.84, 95%CI: 0.81–0.97) [32], while Zhao et al. showed that daily exercise exceeding 30 min raised the risk (OR = 1.6, 95%CI: 1.2–2.1) [34]. In these previous studies, selection bias and unadjusted potential confounders may have influenced the outcomes. Using a prospective study design, our data suggested that initiating regular exercise (at least once per week) would lead to a one-fifth reduction in USD cases, similar to results reported in the United States (weekly physical activity, HR = 0.69, 95% CI: 0.58–0.83) [33]. In theory, low BMI and regular exercise serve a protective role by decreasing the risk of metabolic syndromes. These three factors are inseparably interconnected. Our results provide strong support for advocating weight loss and regular exercising, especially as overweight and obesity emerge as significant public health problems in China.

The large-scale and long-term follow-up is a major strength of this study, enabling a comprehensive depiction of USD epidemiology. In addition, the study population consists of permanent residents in Yinzhou, ensuring the accuracy of the regional analysis. The calculated NNTP and PAF represent the “average” public health effect applicable to the residents of Yinzhou District, providing crucial quantitative evidence for evaluating population-wide interventions, such as community fitness programs. Simultaneously, we acknowledge that in subgroups with higher baseline risk (e.g., middle-aged men with high BMI), the NNTP to prevent one case may be lower, making interventions more cost-effective. The results of this study, particularly the identified risk factors, PAF and NNTP, are most directly generalizable to urban populations in eastern coastal China with similar socioeconomic and developmental profiles to Yinzhou. The district’s characteristics—such as its high degree of urbanization, economic status, and likely access to healthcare—shape the population’s risk factor exposure and disease patterns. Caution should be exercised when extrapolating these findings to rural populations, regions with different dietary customs (e.g., less seafood consumption), or areas with distinct socioeconomic challenges. This insight points the way for future research into more precise and stratified prevention strategies.

Nevertheless, our study has several limitations. Firstly, the follow-up records depended on patients’ willingness to undergo clinical check-ups, resulting in missing data and attrition in the CHERRY dataset. This may lead to selection bias and potentially affect the generalizability of our results, as individuals who adhere to regular follow-up might differ systematically from those who do not. Secondly, information on several covariates was poorly documented, and details of modifiable factors (such as smoking behavior, the amount of consumption, motion type and intensity) were unavailable for in-depth analyses. Future research is warranted to explore the potential dose-response relationship between these factors and USD. Thirdly, while understanding recurrence is critical for clinical management and secondary prevention, we were unable to analyze patterns or predictors of USD recurrence due to insufficient longitudinal event data. Epidemiological research focusing specifically on recurrence would provide valuable insights for long-term patient care and preventive strategies. Finally, as a regional study located in a developed area, the study population is not nationally representative. Caution should be exercised when extrapolating these findings to rural populations or regions with different dietary customs.

Conclusions

Urinary stone diseases are common among Chinese population, with about 1 in 10 affected and increasing by 5–6% over the past 6–7 years. The study supports the existing evidence of lifestyle factors and metabolic symptoms affecting stone formation in the Chinese population.
