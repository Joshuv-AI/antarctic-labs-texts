The Implications of the Non-precipitable Nature of Branched Amylose with Concanavalin A for the Branched Structures of Rice Amylose
Authors: Isao Hanashiro
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The branched structure of amylose was probed using concanavalin A (ConA) lectin, which forms precipitable aggregates with highly branched glucans, such as glycogen and amylopectin. Rice (japonica cultivar) amylose was fractionated from de-fatted, gelatinized starch by precipitation with 1-butanol (BuOH) and purified by ultracentrifugation and repeated crystallization. The purified amylose still has short side chains, whose chain-length (CL) distribution resembles that of amylopectin. More than 96 wt% of the amylose was not precipitated with ConA and remained in the resultant supernatant. The amylose recovered from the supernatant exhibited essentially the same size distributions of molecules and the CL distributions of main and side chains as those of amylose without ConA precipitation. The molar % of branched molecules was slightly decreased by ConA precipitation (-ConA, 11.6; +ConA, 8.1). These results suggest that the side chains detected in BuOH-precipitable amylose preparation are essentially attributable to amylose itself. Also, the non-precipitable nature of the branched molecules of amylose by ConA supports our previous proposal that the organization of the short side chains on amylose molecules is quite different from that found in amylopectin, in which the short side chains are arranged in a cluster fashion, and the branched glucan interacts with ConA to form precipitable aggregates. A tiny amount of ConA-precipitable glucan was detected, but its CL distribution was inconsistent with the size distribution of the branched molecules. Even if the precipitable glucans were fragments of amylopectin, their contribution to the branches detected in amylose should be minor.

INTRODUCTION

Amylose, together with highly branched amylopectin, consists of native starch granules and generally accounts for Approximately 20 % by weight. Amylose can be prepared from starch using various experimental methods, including precipitation from starch dispersion as a 1-butanol (BuOH) complex, which has been widely adopted. Amylose obtained this way has been shown to be a mixture of linear molecules that carry no side chains and branched molecules that have 5.3-17.1 side chains per main chain [1, 2], and the molar fraction of the branched molecules ranges from 0.27 to 0.70, depending on botanical sources of starch [2].

Structural analyses have shown similarities and dissimilarities in branched structures between amylose and amylopectin. The branch linkages in these α-glucans are all α-1,6 linkages [2, 3, 4], but their susceptibilities to debranching enzymes such as isoamylase and pullulanase are different, and even simultaneous or successive hydrolysis by these two enzymes is incapable of complete debranching of amylose [1, 3, 4, 5, 6]. On the other hand, amylopectin is debranched completely by isoamylase alone under suitable conditions [7]. Amylose shows similar size distributions of short unit-chains to that of amylopectin of the same starch source in the range of degree of polymerization (DP) of 10-30 [8]. However, they are distinguishable by comparing the distributions [9]. Furthermore, the ratio of A chain to B chain determined from the chain-length (CL) distribution of β-amylase limit dextrin (β-LD) differs significantly between amylose and amylopectin, suggesting that side chains of amylose are not arranged in a cluster fashion, which is commonly found in amylopectin [9].

These differences between amylose and amylopectin suggest dissimilar branched structure characteristics between the two species of α-glucans. Despite the seemingly amylose-specific characteristics in branching, the possibility that the side chains detected in the BuOH precipitate are from components other than amylose has not been ruled out. This could result from carry-over contamination of amylopectin and/or amylopectin with an unusual structure that is precipitable with BuOH and could coexist in the amylose preparation by BuOH precipitation from starch. The former possibility can be denied by confirming the absence of the high-molecular-weight material eluted in a void volume with gel-permeation chromatography, using a gel matrix with an exclusion limit suitable for amylose [5]. However, amylopectin molecules that are small enough to be included within the effective separation range of the gel used for the size separation can be escaped by this methodology, and such small amylopectins might be present originally in starch or arise under inadequate conditions during the fractionation procedure, such as excessive shear force [10, 11] and oxidative degradation [10]. For the latter possibility, a loose layer over the precipitate at the first precipitation with BuOH during the fractionation of sweet potato starch was found to be amylopectin [12].

It is important to note that in either of the two possibilities described above, amylopectin or amylopectin-derived fragments in amylose preparation are expected to have a cluster structure with regard to the arrangement of side chains, just as typical or intact amylopectin does. Therefore, this study validated these possibilities using the lectin concanavalin A (ConA). ConA is a lectin obtained from jack bean (Canavalia ensiformis) [13]. ConA recognizes non-reducing terminal α-D-mannosyl and α-D-glucosyl residues of oligo- and poly-saccharide chains and has been known to form an aggregate with glycogen and amylopectin [14]. Because of this property, ConA has been successfully utilized in the determination and fractionation of amylose and amylopectin in starch [15].

In this study, the branched structure of rice amylose was characterized and compared in detail with or without precipitation with ConA, using an analytical method developed for differential measurement of size distributions of main and side chains of amylose [9] by fluorescent labeling with 2-aminopyridine (2-AP) [16] of amylose and debranched amylose.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Materials.

Amylose was fractionated from rice starch (Oryza sativa L., cv. Nipponbare) and purified by ultracentrifugation and repeated recrystallization [17]. The purified amylose was confirmed to be free from amylopectin by Toyopearl HW-75F chromatography [5]. Crystalline Pseudomonas isoamylase was a product of former Hayashibara Biochemical Laboratories Inc. (Okayama, Japan. Equivalent enzymes are now available from other suppliers). Sweet potato β-amylase was a product of Sigma-Aldrich Co. (St. Louis, MO, USA) and was purified according to Marshall and Whelan's method [18] prior to use. ConA was purchased from MP Biomedicals (Santa Ana, CA, USA) and was purified according to Agrawal and Goldstein [19], with minor modifications for adaptation to a small scale. Ten mg of ConA protein was dissolved in the reaction buffer and applied to a column (φ = 1.5 cm) packed with 10 mL of Sephadex G-50 Fine pre-equilibrated with 1 M NaCl. 2-AP was of a grade for fluorescent labeling (FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corporation, Osaka, Japan).

Precipitation of glucans with ConA.

Figure 1 overviews the amylose preparations and the following structural analyses. Precipitation experiments of fractionated amylose or amylopectin with ConA were carried out according to Matheson and Welsh [15], with minor modifications. Purified ConA (ca. 6 mg) was used for each precipitation experiment with approximately 5 mg of α-glucan. The supernatant and precipitate were obtained by the first centrifugation step (14,000 × G at 20 °C for 15 min) after mixing glucan and ConA. When amylose was used, precipitate at this step was hardly obtained. Amylose in the supernatant fraction was recovered as follows: addition of saturated EDTA to the supernatant and heating in a boiling-water bath until denatured proteins coagulated; centrifugation (14,000 × G at 20 °C for 15 min) for removal of the coagulates; addition of 1/10 vol. of BuOH to the supernatant and cooling at 4 °C for 2 h; centrifugation (10,000 × G at 4 °C for 10 min) to collect insoluble amylose-BuOH complex. The obtained amylose was designated as ConA-sup. α-Glucan in the precipitate fraction was eluted by the addition of maltose, instead of methyl α-D-mannopyranoside in the original protocol [15].

Preparation of amyloses analyzed in this study and analyses for each amylose preparation.

The designation of amylose preparations (-ConA, ConA-sup, and ConA-ppt) and corresponding figure numbers are included.

After the removal of ConA proteins by denaturation as described above, glucan was precipitated by the addition of 1 vol. of EtOH and cooling on ice for 1 h. The precipitate was collected by centrifugation (10,000 × G at 4 °C for 10 min) and washed twice with cold 50 % EtOH. The washed pellet was redissolved in MilliQ water by heating, and maltose was removed by dialysis against water. Glucan in the dialysate is recovered by lyophilization and designated as ConA-ppt.

Differential measurement of main and side chains.

Main or side chain-specific size-distribution was examined as previously described [9]. Briefly, amylose or its isoamylolyzate was fluorescently labeled according to a method reported previously [16], and then the labeled specimen was subjected to high-performance size-exclusion chromatography (HPSEC) [20]. Depending on the sequence of debranching and labeling, the resulting fluorescence response represents either the sum of main and side chains or main chains only. The difference between the two cases corresponds to a side chain-specific response. Debranching of amylose was performed with isoamylase, as described previously [1].

Other methods.

Preparation of β-amylase limit dextrin (β-LD) of amylose by exhaustive β-amylolysis and determination of the molar fraction of branched and linear molecules by HPSEC were performed as described elsewhere [21]. Total carbohydrate was determined using the phenol-sulfuric acid method [22].

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

Precipitation of amylose with ConA.

Because of its property of agglutination of branched α-glucans, ConA has been successfully utilized to estimate the amylose content of starch [15, 23] and to fractionate amylose and amylopectin on a preparative scale [15]. In these cases, ConA was mixed with dispersed starch, which contains both amylose and amylopectin. The underlying principle in these methodologies is the selective agglutination of amylopectin by ConA.

In this study, amylose was exposed to ConA to see if the amylose preparation contained molecules precipitable with ConA. Note that the amylose used in this study was fractionated from dispersed starch by selective precipitation with BuOH, purified by repeated crystallization and ultracentrifugation, and confirmed to be free from amylopectin.

Table 1 summarizes carbohydrate yields in the supernatant and precipitate fractions obtained by exposure of amylose to ConA. In five of the six experiments, more than 96 % (average of the five experiments, excluding no. 2) of carbohydrate was recovered in the supernatant, and a visible precipitate was hardly obtained. The results suggest that branched molecules in the amylose preparation do not almost form a precipitable complex with ConA. One experiment (no. 2) showed a high yield in ConA-ppt. This observation cannot be explained, but the high yield can be considered exceptional.

Size distributions of amylose.

Size distributions of amylose molecules were compared among the amylose without ConA precipitation (-ConA) and the amylose recovered from the supernatant fraction (ConA-sup, Fig. 2). The distribution for -ConA was consistent with a previous report [9], showing one prominent peak with a shoulder observed by refractive index and two peak-tops by fluorescence detection. The distribution for ConA-sup was almost the same as those of -ConA, with a slightly higher second peak-top and reduced tailing of the peak in the fluorescence profile (Fig. 2B). Thus, treatment of purified amylose with ConA did not markedly affect the size distribution of the amylose that remained soluble in the supernatant.

The molar percentage of linear and branched molecules of amylose.

Fluorescently labeled amylose at its reducing end with 2-AP was hydrolyzed with β-amylase, and the β-amylolyzate was directly subjected to HPSEC (Fig. 3). This analysis enables determination of the ratio of linear and branched molecules in amylose preparation on a molar basis. Linear molecules are completely digested by the enzyme; hence, the fluorescently labeled maltose or maltotriose are released only from the reducing end of the parental molecules. Branched molecules remain molecules larger than maltotriose due to incomplete hydrolysis by the presence of branched side chains. Therefore, in the chromatogram by fluorescence detection, the area % of the peaks for labeled maltose and maltotriose represents the molar percentage of linear molecules, and the area % of the peaks for molecules larger than maltotriose indicates the percentage of branched molecules. The spikes in the fluorescence response at retention times of 73, 83, and 99 min (Fig. 3B) were supposed to be due to an unknown reason but not to the analytes.

Size distributions of β-amylolyzates.

(A) Amylose without ConA precipitation. (B) Amylose in the ConA-sup. Black line, fluorescence response; gray line, RI response; G2, maltose; L-G3 and L-G2, labeled maltotriose and maltose.

The molar % of linear and branched molecules were 88.4 and 11.6 for -ConA and 91.9 and 8.1 for ConA-sup. The molar % of branched molecules decreased (3.5 %) in ConA-sup, suggesting that the purified amylose might have contained branched molecules precipitable with ConA. The chromatograms by RI detection agreed with the possible presence of branched molecules. In the retention time range from 60 to 75 min, a peak assigned for β-LD was detected (inserts in Fig. 3), and their peak shape, peak area, and elution position were changed by precipitation with ConA. The decreased peak area and the retarded elution position indicated the removal of branched components by ConA, and the size of the removed components was larger than the remaining branched amylose.

The change in peak shape might be attributable to their elution positions. The peak of -ConA (Fig. 3A) was eluted at the void volume, but that of ConA-sup was within the effective separation range of the columns used. Because of the weak detector response, further considerations for the peaks of β-LD, such as weight percentage and DP determination, were impossible.

CL distributions of main and side chains of amylose.

To compare the branched structures of amyloses with or without ConA precipitation, a differential CL distribution analysis [9] was performed (Fig. 4). Amyloses were processed differently by debranching with isoamylase and labeling with 2-AP in different orders of these processes, and then the labeled isoamylolyzate was subjected to HPSEC (Fig. 4). When debranching precedes the labeling, the resulting CL distribution represents that of both the main and the side chains of amylose combined (Fig. 4A). In the reversed order of processing, the result shows the CL distribution of the main chains (Fig. 4B). Side chain-specific CL distribution (Fig. 4C) can be calculated by subtracting the main-chain distribution from whole unit chains.

The CL distribution of whole chains of -ConA had the same features as in a previous report [9]. The distribution could be divided into two regions at a retention time of 73.5 min. The first and second regions can be regarded as the main chains and side chains, respectively. The side-chain distribution was distinct from that of amylopectin in terms of the decreased ratio of long, cluster-connecting B chains [9], which were eluted at a retention time of 72 to 83 min. However, the ratio of the peak area of the second to the first region was much higher than that observed previously [9], and the elution of the debranched amylose took a somewhat longer time than the previous analyses [9], presumably due to the different conditions of the HPSEC apparatus, such as pumps and analytical columns.

The different ratios of peaks might be attributable to the different starch preparations used in this and previous studies. Although the starches were prepared from grains of the same rice cultivar (cv. Nipponbare), the grains were harvested in different years and places. In fact, the molar percentage of branched molecules in amylose preparation was determined to be 26 % for the starch used previously [9], which is almost twofold higher than the value (11.6 %) obtained in this study.

A comparison of the CL distribution of whole chains between ConA-sup and -ConA (Fig. 4A) indicated that the CL distributions were essentially the same and that ConA precipitation had no significant effect on those distributions. Similarly, the CL distributions of the main chains were the same between ConA-sup and -ConA (Fig. 4B), and consequently, no significant difference was detected between the two amylose specimens with regard to the side-chain distributions (Fig. 4C). The number of side chains attached to a main chain was estimated by the ratio of the peak area of the side chains to that of the main chain, and the values were close: 0.97 for -ConA and 0.95 for ConA-sup (Figs. 4B and 4C). Judging from the fluorescence peak area, 94 % of the main chains and 95 % of the side chains were recovered in ConA-sup, which was consistent with the results of carbohydrate yield in that fraction (> 96 %, Table 1).

Structural characterization of ConA-ppt.

As mentioned earlier, precipitation experiments of purified amylose with ConA hardly produced precipitate; however, extraction of α-glucans from the precipitate fraction was still carried out. Figure 5 shows comparisons of the glucan structures between ConA-ppt and ConA-sup. The HPSEC of the labeled glucan (Fig. 5A) showed that ConA-ppt had only one prominent peak at 75-80 min, where linear chains of DP 40-70 were eluted in the columns used. Thus, ConA-ppt can be regarded as a compact molecule with a much smaller hydrodynamic volume than the main chains of amylose. The height and area of the peak were one-fourth and 18 % of that of Con-A sup (shown in gray), respectively. On the other hand, the corresponding peak was not recognizable by the RI response, indicating that the amount by weight of ConA-ppt was negligibly low.

Comparisons of structures of glucans precipitable (ConA-ppt) and non-precipitable (ConA-sup) with ConA from fractionated amylose.

(A) DP distribution. (B) CL distribution. Black line, ConA-ppt; gray line, ConA-sup. The chromatograms in panel A were obtained for the directly labeled and analyzed glucan molecules without debranching. The chromatograms of the debranched and labeled glucans are shown in panel B.

CL distribution analyses (Fig. 5B), in which the glucan was debranched first and then labeled at the reducing end, revealed the composition of unit chains. ConA-ppt had only medium (78-83 min) and short chains (83-100 min) but no long chains (60-75 min). The elution positions of the medium and short chains correspond to the range of CL from approximately 80 to 36 and from approximately 36 to 6. The former covers the same range for B2 and B3 chains and the latter for A and B1 chains of amylopectin. In the case of ConA-sup, the long chains are assigned to the main chains of branched amylose and the medium and short chains to the side chains. The absence of long chains in ConA-ppt indicates that the structure of the glucan is quite different from the presumed structure of branched amylose in ConA-sup. With regard to the elution patterns of the medium- and short-chain regions, ConA-ppt and ConA-sup appeared similar in terms of the range (78-100 min) and peak-top position (ca. 94 min, DP 11). However, they differed in the relative amounts of chains of DP 6 and 7.

The disappearance of the fluorescence peak of the ConA-ppt molecules (Fig. 5A) by debranching (Fig. 5B) suggests that the molecules of ConA-ppt are branched, and hydrolysis of the branch linkages by isoamylase caused the reduction of the hydrodynamic volume of ConA-ppt. The ratio of the peak area of the unit chains to the main chains should indicate the number of chains per molecule (NC). This ratio for ConA-ppt was obtained as the ratio of the peak area of Fig. 5B to that of Fig. 5A, and it was too low (1.5) to be true. The NC value of 1.5 implies that one molecule consists of one main chain and 0.5 side chains on average, which can be interpreted as indicating the existence of only one side chain in every two main chains. If so, linear molecules were precipitated with ConA, constituting half of the molecules in ConA-ppt. However, this interpretation is inconsistent with the well-known properties of ConA, that is, the lectin forms precipitable aggregates almost exclusively with branched glucans [14, 15]. In addition, it appears that the CLs of the major constituent chains (from approximately 36 to 6 and a peak at 11) are too short to construct branched molecules of ConA-ppt. Even assuming a singly-branched molecule, which should have a twofold higher number of side chains per molecule than the observed (but questionably low) value (0.5), and the longest unit chains (CL 36) that are connected at positions near non-reducing end to achieve the possible length of the putative branched molecule to be the longest, the expected DP value should be 72 or less. A similar case for the most abundant unit chains (CL 11) would give the value of 22. These putative DP values appear lower than those expected from the result shown in Fig. 5A (higher than 40-70). Moreover, the peak area of ConA-ppt molecules (Fig. 5A) does not seem to coincide with the differences in peak areas of the main chains (60-72 min) between -ConA and ConA-sup by ConA precipitation (Figs. 4A and 4B).

These discrepancies between structures of the ConA-ppt before and after debranching make it difficult to confidently describe the structures of ConA-ppt, which could be fragments of branched amylose or amylopectin, or something else. The incomplete debranching of ConA-ppt by isoamylase is a possible explanation for the discrepancies. In this study, further examinations of the structures of ConA-ppt were suspended, owing to the tiny amounts of available specimens.

Implications of a failure to precipitate branched amylose with ConA.

Rice amylose fractionated and purified from starch still contains branched molecules [8, 9]. The purified amylose was exposed to ConA, which is capable of precipitating amylopectin, to see if the branched amylose molecules form a precipitable aggregate with the lectin and if alterations in the CL distributions of amylose are caused by the ConA precipitation as a result.

The vast majority (> 96 % by weight, ca. 95 % by mole) of the amylose molecules did not form a precipitate with ConA, and thus the lectin failed to precipitate branched amylose. The CL distribution of amylose was not markedly changed by ConA precipitation. Although the exact reasons for the failure are currently unknown, it is concluded that the branched structures of amylose are distinct from those of amylopectin. Also, the non-precipitable nature of branched amylose with ConA supports the previous proposal that the side chains of amylose are not arranged in a cluster fashion, which is commonly found in the amylopectin counterpart.

The existence of ConA-precipitable glucan in purified amylose preparation is insufficient to dismiss the above conclusions because its content was very low and the CL distribution of amylose was not affected by the removal of the precipitate. The precipitable glucan could be fragments of amylopectin, but their contribution to the observed side chains of amylose preparation should be limited to a minor extent.

Finally, it should be emphasized that the isoamylase-hydrolyzable branches that are dealt with in this study account only for the minor portion of the α-1,6-branch linkages in amylose molecules [1]. Further investigation is needed to elucidate the whole picture of branching in amylose.
