Automated quantitative analysis of osteoclast numbers and areas in cell culture plates using the YOLOv8 instance segmentation model
Authors: Jia Huang, Zefeng Li, Junyu Ouyang, Jialing Li, Yichen Fan, Die Huang, Mong Dong, Fujun Jin, YuJing Lu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractQuantitative assessment of osteoclasts by counting and measuring area proportions is a fundamental approach for analyzing in vitro osteoclastogenesis. However, there is a lack of user-friendly automated analysis tools that do not require programming expertise, leading researchers to rely on manual operations for quantitative analysis. To address this limitation, the present study developed an offline software application utilizing the YOLOv8 instance segmentation model. This software facilitates the automated detection of osteoclasts in single images and batches, achieving an inference speed of less than one second per image. Following detection, the system promptly generates a visualized prediction report for user reference, providing essential data support for subsequent analyses. To ensure optimal model performance, the model was trained on a high-quality dataset meticulously annotated by experts in bone histology. The model ultimately achieved a mean average precision of 95.3% in detecting osteoclasts derived from mouse bone marrow macrophages, facilitating swift feedback of quantitative analysis results and significantly enhancing analysis efficiency. In conclusion, this study developed an accessible quantitative analysis tool for osteoclasts, intended for researchers without programming expertise.•An automated quantitative analysis system for osteoclasts has been developed, enabling precise quantification of osteoclast number and area. This system demonstrates high accuracy and rapid detection, significantly improving research efficiency.•The model is packaged as a standalone offline application, enabling researchers without programming experience to use it effortlessly.•The YOLOv8l-seg model was evaluated on both segmentation and detection tasks to verify its performance.Keywords: Osteoclasts, Instance segmentation, Yolov8, Deep learning, Quantitative analysis, Artificial intelligence

Background

Osteoporosis is a metabolic bone disease characterized by diminished bone mass and deterioration of bone microarchitecture. Clinically, it presents with increased bone fragility and an elevated risk of fractures [[1], [2], [3]]. The fundamental pathological mechanism involves the excessive activation of osteoclasts (OCs), resulting in bone resorption that surpasses osteoblast-mediated bone formation, leading to irreversible structural bone damage [[4], [5], [6], [7]]. Consequently, investigating the mechanisms of osteoclastogenesis and identifying potential intervention targets are essential for the effective treatment of osteoporosis [8].

Bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMMs) are commonly utilized as precursors for differentiation into mature OCs [[9], [10], [11]]. These precursor cells proliferate in a medium enriched with macrophage colony-stimulating factor (M-CSF) [12,13]. Following proliferation, the cells are exposed to receptor activator of nuclear factor kappa-B ligand (RANKL) to induce differentiation and maturation [14]. Under the combined influence of RANKL and M-CSF, cells undergo fusion to form mature multinucleated OCs, typically containing more than three nuclei, with bone resorptive capabilities [15]. The identification and quantification of osteoclastogenesis in vitro, specifically the number and surface area of OCs, is primarily achieved using tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase (TRAP) staining of culture plates (Fig. 1). This method is fundamental for verifying OCs differentiation in vitro [[16], [17], [18], [19]].

TRAP staining images of the in vitro differentiated osteoclasts. The burgundy-colored giant cells are differentiated mature OCs, while the small cells are undifferentiated osteoclast precursor cells.

Despite histomorphometry being the gold standard for quantitative analysis of OCs, a significant technical bottleneck persists in current in vitro analysis methods. There is a notable absence of user-friendly, automated analysis tools accessible to researchers lacking programming expertise. Consequently, the analysis process relies heavily on manual counting and area measurement by researchers. This traditional approach is not only time-consuming (requiring a minimum of two minutes per image) but also susceptible to unavoidable subjective bias, with inter-observer variability exceeding 15%. To illustrate, a typical 96-well osteoclastogenesis assay contains over 400 images; manual counting would require over 13 h of continuous labor, rendering medium-throughput compound screens impractical. These limitations significantly hinder the efficiency of in vitro mechanistic research and impede new drug screening as well as the evaluation of intervention effects. Therefore, there is an urgent need to develop a supporting tool for the automated quantitative analysis of OCs.

In recent years, deep learning technology has achieved significant advancements and has been widely applied in areas such as target recognition and image segmentation [[20], [21], [22], [23]]. Concurrently, the rapid evolution of artificial intelligence has enabled deep integration of computer vision into research domains such as medical image analysis and pathological diagnosis, demonstrating considerable potential for automated analysis [24]. In this study, we have developed intelligent software the recognition of OCs based on deep learning, specifically utilizing the advanced YOLOv8 instance segmentation model [[25], [26], [27]]. This model synergizes the benefits of object detection and pixel-level segmentation, enabling precise localization of OCs, delineation of their intricate cellular boundaries, and the generation of corresponding segmentation masks. Even in complex microscopic imaging scenarios with high cell density, diverse morphologies, and partial cell overlap, the model maintains high recognition accuracy and robustness [28]. Moreover, the software is designed to operate completely offline, ensuring that all image processing and inference tasks are performed locally on the user's computer without requiring an internet connection. This design ensures data privacy and allows the tool to be used in bandwidth-restricted environments.

Method details

In vitro osteoclast differentiation model

Mouse primary osteoclasts were differentiated from bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMMs) by stimulation with M-CSF (Peprotech, #315–02) and RANKL (Peprotech, #315–11C) [13]. In brief, C57BL/6 J mice were euthanized via cervical dislocation and immersed in 75% ethanol for 5 min. The femurs and tibias were harvested and rinsed with α-MEM basic medium (Gibco, #12,571–500). Bone marrow was extracted by flushing the bones with α-MEM basic medium using a 1 ml syringe. The resulting bone marrow cells were suspended in α-MEM basic medium supplemented with 10% FBS (Gibco, #10,100–147) and cultured overnight. Unattached cells were collected by centrifugation and washed once with PBS. The cell concentration was adjusted to 3 × 10^6 cells/ml in α-MEM growth medium containing 50 ng/ml M-CSF and seeded into a cell culture dish for 24 h to obtain BMMs. Subsequently, unattached cells were discarded, and the adherent cells were washed once with PBS. The BMMs were then stimulated with growth medium containing 50 ng/ml M-CSF and 100 ng/ml RANKL, with the medium being renewed every 2 days for 4 days.

TRAP staining

The TRAP staining procedure was performed using the Tartrate-Resistant Acid Phosphatase Stain Kit (Solarbio, #G1492) according to the manufacturer's instructions: the culture medium was discarded, and the cells were washed twice with PBS. The cells were then fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min at room temperature. An appropriate volume of TRAP staining solution was prepared by mixing AS-BI staining solution, GBC staining solution, and TRAP staining solution in a 10:1:90 ratio. Subsequently, a volume of TRAP staining solution equivalent to half the culture medium was added to the cells and incubated at 37 °C in the dark for 1 hour. After discarding the staining solution, the cells were washed twice with deionized water, and air-dried in an inverted position before imaging and counting.

Osteoclast TRAP staining image acquisition

Imaging of fully differentiated osteoclasts in 12-well plates was performed using an inverted microscope (ECLIPSE Ti-E, Nikon) equipped with ×10 and ×20 objectives. To ensure sample representativeness, 3–5 non-overlapping fields of view were randomly selected per well for imaging. The light intensity was dynamically adjusted during imaging to simulate different bright-field conditions, thereby enhancing the generalization capability of the subsequent model. All images were saved in 16-bit format with a resolution of 5440 × 3648 pixels. Additionally, 200 images from published studies were incorporated, forming a final dataset of 634 images. To characterize dataset diversity, the 634-image dataset included a broad range of staining intensities (from light purple to deep red) and variations in imaging conditions, including brightness, focus, and field of view, arising from dynamic microscope illumination adjustments. The 200 images obtained from published studies were acquired using different microscope brands (e.g., Olympus and Zeiss) and staining protocols, thereby introducing real-world domain shift. This diverse dataset was constructed to improve model robustness.

Data annotation

Three co-authors (J.-L. L., Y.-C. F., and M. D.) received standardized training in osteoclast pixel-level segmentation prior to independently annotating the images using the LabelMe tool [29]. Mature OCs were segmented at the pixel level by manually delineating irregular polygons (Fig. 2). The criteria for identifying OCs were rigorously defined by nuclear count: structures containing three or more nuclei were classified as mature OCs, while structures containing fewer nuclei were classified as non-OCs. To systematically assess annotation reliability, three annotators independently annotated the same set of 50 images. During this process, all communication between annotators was prohibited to ensure the independence of the annotations. The Dice coefficient and Hausdorff distance were used to evaluate annotation consistency. The results were visualized using a box plot showing the distribution of Dice coefficients and a scatter plot depicting the relationship between Hausdorff distance and cell size (Fig. 3). Due to the diversity of image sources, all measurements were reported in pixels. While this restricts direct physical size comparisons with other studies, it does not affect the internal validity of the inter-annotator consistency analysis. Quantitative analysis demonstrated a high degree of consistency among the three annotators. The average Dice coefficient was 0.984 ± 0.024, reflecting substantial overlap between annotated regions, whereas the average Hausdorff distance was 25.7 ± 38.4 pixels (Table 1). In high-resolution images (5440 × 3648 pixels), the boundary annotation error was minimal, rendering the dataset suitable for training downstream deep-learning models.

Overall architecture of the YOLOv8 model

The instance segmentation model employed in this study is based on the Ultralytics YOLOv8-seg framework developed by Ultralytics [25]. We utilized its official pre-trained weights, which facilitate efficient computation and accurate segmentation through the optimized architecture of YOLOv8-seg. The instance segmentation component, YOLOv8-seg, employs CSPDarknet53 as the fundamental feature extractor and integrates the improved C2f module ("cross stage partial bottleneck with two fusions") to construct a feature fusion network. This network precisely predicts the instance segmentation mask of the input image through the implementation of dual segmentation heads. The model is deployed as a standalone Qt-based desktop application (.exe) powered by PyTorch. The application supports both GPU-accelerated and CPU-only inference, enabling users to operate the software without installing Python or additional dependencies. The model demonstrates strong performance in benchmark tests for object detection and instance segmentation and therefore shows promise for the automatic identification and quantitative analysis of OCs. Regarding model selection, YOLOv8-seg provides five pre-trained versions: nano (n), small (s), medium (m), large (l), and extra-large (x), to accommodate varying hardware resources and task requirements. In this study, YOLOv8l-seg was selected as the core model based on its network depth and feature extraction capacity. While maintaining high inference efficiency, the large-scale version improves feature representation in complex scenarios by increasing the network hierarchy and expanding feature map dimensions. This capability enables the model to effectively capture micro-anatomical structures, low-contrast targets, and regions of interest obscured by complex backgrounds in biological imaging.

Previous studies have shown that the YOLO series has consistently aimed to balance detection accuracy and computational efficiency across successive model generations [30]. Benchmark assessments using the COCO dataset, which comprises 80 categories of common objects and incorporates multi-scale detection tasks, demonstrate that YOLOv8 achieves an optimal balance between computational efficiency and competitive accuracy (Table 2). YOLOv8 is implemented using the PyTorch framework and incorporates the C2f module, a cross-stage partial (CSP) bottleneck structure designed to improve gradient flow while maintaining a low parameter count. This design improves computational efficiency without sacrificing accuracy. Additionally, YOLOv8 expands its multi-task capabilities to encompass instance segmentation (YOLOv8-seg), showcasing remarkable architectural flexibility and versatility. Compared to previous YOLO versions, YOLOv8 provides improved inference speed and enhanced deployment flexibility. These characteristics make it suitable for applications requiring high real-time performance. Its exceptional inference efficiency and generalization capability make it applicable to computationally demanding tasks, including biological image analysis, while facilitating practical deployment and reducing computational requirements.

Model training

Unlike traditional rule-based systems, deep learning models learn feature representations directly from data. The annotated dataset was partitioned into training, validation, and test sets in a 7:2:1 ratio, ensuring that the model was trained, tuned, and evaluated on mutually exclusive subsets. During training, a systematic data augmentation strategy was employed to enhance model robustness and generalization capacity, including the following: (1) Color Space transformation: Hue (ΔH = ±0.015), saturation (scaling factor = 0.7), and value (scaling factor = 0.4) adjustments were applied in the HSV color space. (2) Geometric transformation: Image translation (magnitude = 0.1), scaling (ratio = 0.5), horizontal flipping (probability = 0.5), rotation (± 10°), perspective transformation (coefficient = 0.0005), and shearing (intensity = 2.0) were applied. (3) Composite augmentation: Mosaic augmentation (probability = 1.0), MixUp augmentation (probability = 0.1), and copy-paste augmentation (probability = 0.1) were utilized. (4) Random feature masking: Random erasing was applied with a probability of 0.4. This augmentation strategy simulates variations commonly encountered in microscopic imaging and improves model robustness under heterogeneous imaging conditions. The Adam optimizer was employed for training, configured with a learning rate (η) of 0.01, β₁ = 0.9, β₂ = 0.999, and a weight decay (λ) of 0.0005. Training was conducted over 500 epochs with a batch size of 4. A cosine annealing scheduler was used to reduce the learning rate to 0.001 after 400 epochs. Regarding model checkpointing, the model with the lowest validation loss was saved as ”best.pt”, whereas the final model after training was saved as “last.pt”.

Model inference flow

Upon completion of model training, the best-performing model weights were selected for inference. The inference pipeline was implemented utilizing the official Ultralytics YOLOv8 API. To ensure methodological consistency and reproducibility, all preprocessing parameters and inference hyperparameters were kept consistent across all test images. These hyperparameters included the confidence threshold for filtering out low-confidence predictions, the non-maximum suppression (NMS) IoU threshold for removing redundant overlapping detections, and the standardized input image size. All inference tasks were performed using the best.pt weights.

The image preprocessing procedures employed during the inference phase were consistent with those used in the training phase, ensuring consistency in the input data distribution. Method validation

How the model performs on the test set

To evaluate model performance after training, 10% of the dataset was randomly selected as an independent test set. It is important to note that, although this is a single-class task, the YOLOv8 framework generates evaluation curves for both 'OCs' and 'all classes' lines. The latter is derived from the framework's internal multi-class confidence aggregation logic. In theory, these two should align; however, slight discrepancies may arise due to different computational pathways, which is a typical occurrence at the algorithmic level. All analyses in this study are based on the 'OCs' curve. The Precision-Recall (PR) curve (Fig. 5A) is considered the gold standard for evaluating the balance between precision and recall in object recognition, providing an intuitive reflection of the model's performance. The model achieved a mAP@0.5 of 95.8% (mean Average Precision at an IoU threshold of 0.5, i.e., considering a detection correct if the overlap between predicted and ground-truth bounding boxes is at least 50%), indicating excellent detection accuracy. The curve originates from the upper-left corner, indicative of high precision at low recall—suggesting that the model demonstrates highly reliable predictive performance under high confidence thresholds. As recall increases, precision gradually declines but remains at a relatively high level across most recall intervals. This trend indicates that the model maintains a favorable balance between precision and recall while minimizing false positive and false negative predictions.

To further evaluate the model's performance across various decision thresholds, we plotted the F1-confidence curve (Fig. 5B), the precision-confidence curve (Fig. 5C), and the recall-confidence curve (Fig. 5D). These curves illustrate the relationship between model performance and confidence thresholds and facilitate threshold selection for practical applications. The F1-confidence curve depicts the F1-score (the harmonic mean of precision and recall) across different confidence thresholds. It illustrates the trade-off between precision and recall: as the confidence threshold varies, this curve helps identify the threshold that optimizes both metrics for balanced performance. Analysis of the F1 curve reveals that the model achieves optimal overall performance (F1 = 0.91) at a confidence threshold of 0.557. Notably, within the threshold range of 0.2 to 0.7, the F1 score exhibits minimal fluctuation, indicating strong model robustness. Trend analysis of the precision-confidence curve reveals a positive correlation between the model's precision and confidence, reaching a perfect score of 1.00 at a threshold of 0.923. This indicates that the model's predictions are highly reliable when the confidence threshold is elevated. By adjusting the confidence threshold, users can effectively calibrate prediction accuracy. Furthermore, the recall-confidence curve shows that recall remained above 0.85 within the low-to-moderate confidence range (0–0.6). As confidence increases, the model applies more stringent criteria, leading to a decline in recall. This demonstrates that the model enables a controllable trade-off between precision and coverage, allowing users to flexibly adjust the confidence threshold according to specific application needs.

Inference speed and throughput

To comprehensively assess the model's inference efficiency, we benchmarked the inference time and measured throughput under varying batch sizes on both CPU and GPU platforms. The hardware environment consisted of a CPU with 20 physical cores and 40 logical threads (2.5 GHz clock speed, 32 GB RAM) and two NVIDIA GeForce RTX 2080 Ti GPUs (11 GB VRAM each) with CUDA version 12.1. A dataset of 200 images was used, and inference times were measured with batch sizes of 1 and 4. We recorded the total processing time, calculated the throughput in terms of images per second, and determined the average processing time per image, as presented in Table 3. The findings indicate that the average inference time per image is <1 s, regardless of whether the images are processed singly or in batches. This is substantially shorter than manual quantitative analysis, which typically requires at least 2 min per image. These results demonstrate that our method significantly reduces analysis time and improves analytical efficiency.

The model reduces subjective variability in ambiguous cell recognition

Despite employing three experienced and systematically trained annotators during dataset construction, capturing all morphological anomalies remains challenging (Fig. 6A). For cells exhibiting atypical morphology, annotators often rely on their personal expertise, which can introduce discrepancies and compromise the consistency of annotations. Moreover, the annotation task is inherently labor-intensive. Extended periods of high-concentration work can cause visual and cognitive fatigue among annotators, diminishing the ability to identify small or indistinct targets and increasing the risk of missed annotations.

Comparison of model detection performance against manual labeling omissions. (A) Representations of morphologically blurred or undifferentiated cells in the image. (B) Detection results generated by the proposed model. The blue bounding boxes and associated confidence scores (e.g., OC 0.87) indicate that the model identifies candidate regions that were missed during manual annotation. Such detections should be interpreted as auxiliary references and require expert verification. The model is intended to support, not replace, human biological judgment.

Furthermore, differentiating OCs is a continuous biological process rather than a binary state, requiring annotators to manually establish classification thresholds along a continuous morphological spectrum. For cells in intermediate states, discrepancies in judgment may arise among different annotators or even within the same annotator across different sessions. Out of caution, annotators may occasionally abstain from labeling ambiguous cells. These issues collectively impact the accuracy and completeness of the labeling process. Nevertheless, these challenges highlight the potential utility of the model proposed in this study (Fig. 6B). The model learns consistent morphological features from large-scale data, which may support manual annotation by reducing uncertainty and improving reproducibility for clear-cut targets. However, the model is intended as a tool to enhance efficiency and consistency rather than to replace expert biological judgment, especially for ambiguous or borderline cells. The final decision in such cases should always rely on expert assessment.

Software implementation and deployment

The final display interface

To enhance the practical usability of the proposed model for OCs analysis, we developed a user-friendly graphical user interface (GUI) based on the Qt framework (The Qt Company, Espoo, Finland) (Fig. 7). As illustrated, users can adjust two key parameters: the confidence threshold and the IoU threshold. Adjusting these parameters result in real-time updates to the detection outcomes, enabling users to dynamically optimize analytical accuracy. The interface's display area provides multi-dimensional information: (1) the response time of each detection process, aiding users in assessing computational efficiency; and (2) the total OCs count and the proportion of OCs area relative to the total image area. These two indicators constitute the core foundation for quantitative analysis in OCs-related research. Furthermore, the GUI features a selection function for individual cell targets. For selected target cells, the corresponding confidence scores and spatial information are systematically presented in the table below the display area. This arrangement enables researchers to closely examine the details of each target cell, thereby facilitating result verification. The application is capable of performing both single-image analysis and high-throughput batch processing. This batch processing capability markedly reduces the time required for large-scale data analysis, thereby facilitating high-throughput scientific research and improving experimental efficiency.

Software deployment and system requirements

To ensure reproducibility and facilitate use by researchers without programming expertise, the trained model has been packaged into a standalone desktop application. The software is provided as an executable (.exe) file that supports offline functionality, eliminating the need to configure a Python environment or install additional dependencies. This design choice streamlines the workflow for researchers.

Development Environment: Model training and development were conducted using Python 3.9.7, Ultralytics YOLOv8 (version 8.4.19), PyTorch 2.5.1, and CUDA 12.1.

Deployment Environment: The software is optimized and rigorously tested on Windows 10/11 (64-bit) operating systems. As a standalone Windows application, it currently runs only on Windows platforms and does not support Linux or macOS. The application functions entirely offline, obviating the need for network connectivity. The operational software environment is streamlined, as the application is compiled into a single executable (.exe) file, thereby eliminating the need for users to install Python or configure dependencies.

Hardware Requirements: Upon execution, the software autonomously detects compatible GPU resources within the system. If a GPU is available, the software leverages GPU acceleration for computational tasks. Otherwise, the software switches to CPU-based execution without user intervention. To ensure optimal and stable software performance, it is recommended that the system be equipped with a minimum of 16 GB of RAM. This recommendation is based on empirical performance benchmarks and is sufficient for typical workloads. It is important to note that, due to testing limitations, software compatibility and performance in environments with 8 GB of RAM or less have not been extensively validated.

Installation Procedure: To install the software, download the installer package (OCsDetector_Setup_v1.0.exe) from the shared resources available at https://zenodo.org/records/19045620. The hosted resources include the trained model weights (best.pt), the executable file, sample images, and documentation regarding the annotation format. During the installation process, users will be prompted to select a preferred destination directory.

Image Input: Upon launching the software, users can choose single-image or batch detection mode by clicking the "Open Image" or "Open Folder" buttons, which opens a file selector to load microscopic images from a local directory. The system is compatible with standard image formats, including .jpg, .jpeg, .png, .tif, .bmp, and .tiff. users can choose single-image or batch detection mode by clicking the "Open Image" or "Open Folder" buttons, which opens a file selector to load microscopic images from a local directory.

Inference Execution: After selecting the image(s) and clicking the “Open” button, the selected image(s) are processed by the model for automatic analysis.

Interface for Image Analysis Results: Upon completion of the analysis, the detection results and statistical data are displayed within the interface in real-time.

Report Export: By clicking the "Save" button, a comprehensive Microsoft Word report summarizing the detection results is automatically generated and saved locally.

Limitations

The high segmentation accuracy reported in our results provides strong support for reliable biological interpretation. The mAP@0.5 of 95.8% indicates that most OCs are correctly detected and segmented at the pixel level, ensuring that the calculated number and surface area of OCs accurately represent the true extent of osteoclast differentiation. High precision at elevated confidence thresholds (up to 1.00) minimizes false positives, while maintained recall across low to moderate thresholds reduces the risk of missing genuine OCs. Together, these performance metrics ensure that downstream quantitative analyses, such as evaluating drug effects on osteoclastogenesis, are based on reliable and accurate OCs measurements.

Despite the strengths of the developed OCs quantitative analysis system, image quality and acquisition conditions may affect model performance in real-world applications. Factors such as insufficient resolution (which may obscure fine cellular boundaries), overlapping osteoclast boundaries (hindering individual cell separation), staining variability (leading to inconsistent contrast between OCs and background), low contrast microscopy conditions, differences in microscope brands or settings (causing image brightness or color shifts), and potential domain shift may introduce variability in detection accuracy. Consequently, it is imperative to acquire high-resolution images during the imaging process. To mitigate these challenge, the model provides a practical optimization strategy: users can dynamically adjust the confidence threshold and the IoU threshold to filter out false-positive targets, thereby ensuring robust detection performance in practical applications. In future work, we will systematically assess model generalization using external datasets across varied imaging conditions and investigate domain adaptation methods to mitigate performance variability.
