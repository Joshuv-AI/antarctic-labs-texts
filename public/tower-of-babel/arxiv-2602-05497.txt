Convergence of the PML method for thermoelastic wave scattering problems
Qianyuan Yin, Changkun Wei, Bo Zhang

Abstract
This paper is concerned with the thermoelastic obstacle scattering problem in three dimensions. A uniaxial perfectly matched layer (PML) method is firstly introduced to truncate the unbounded scattering problem, leading to a truncated PML problem in a bounded domain. Under certain constraints on model parameters, the well-posedness for the truncated PML problem is then proved except possibly for a discrete set of frequencies, based on the analytic Fredholm theory. Moreover, the exponential convergence of the uniaxial PML method is established in terms of the thickness and absorbing parameters of PML layer. The proof is based on the PML extension technique and the exponential decay properties of the modified fundamental solution. As far as we know, this is the first convergence result of the PML method for the time-harmonic thermoelastic scattering problem. Keywords: Thermoelastic wave equations, uniaxial PML, well-posedness, exponential convergence



1
Introduction

In this paper, we study the thermoelastic obstacle scattering problem with the Dirichlet boundary condition.
In the pure elastic theory of the geophysical applications, the effect of temperature on the medium is often
ignored and only the mechanical properties of elastic solids is considered. However, to more accurately model
real-world scenarios such as subsurface exploration, the thermal effects must be taken into account when
interpreting geophysical data. For example, the hot dry rock geothermal model can be used to obtain the thermal
information of media by the thermoelastic wave field. The thermoelastic model reveals a connection between the
thermal and mechanical motions. We refer to
[
1
,
3
,
9
,
27
,
34
]
for the
mathematical analysis and numerical simulations for the thermoelastic wave scattering problems.

In practical numerical solutions to wave scattering problems, one usually needs to truncate the unbounded domain
in which the wave propagates into a bounded computational domain. The perfectly matched layer (PML) is an efficient
and effective numerical truncation technique, which was originally proposed by Bérenger in 1994 for solving the
time-dependent Maxwell’s equations
[
4
]
. The basic idea of the PML method is to construct an artificial
layer surrounding the computational domain to effectively attenuate outgoing waves. In general, the designed medium
needs to make sure the scattered waves decay rapidly (exponentially) in the absorbing layer of finite thickness
regardless of the wave incident angles. The PML method has an excellent numerical performance and has been widely used
in various types of wave scattering problems; see
[
20
,
29
,
30
,
31
]
for the numerical
solution of forward models in the full waveform inversion problems in geophysics and
[
23
,
33
,
37
]

for the numerical simulations in thermoelastic and poroelastic wave scattering problems.

Extensive research works are available on the convergence analysis of PML methods. The convergence of the circular and
spherical PML methods is studied in
[
2
,
7
,
11
,
13
,
18
,
21
,
28
,
35
]
, and
convergence of the uniaxial (Cartesian) PML methods can be found in
[
5
,
6
,
12
,
14
,
15
,
17
,
26
,
36
]
for acoustic and electromagnetic wave scattering problems. For the elastic wave scattering problems,
the exponential convergence of a spherical PML method was established in
[
8
]
, and the exponential convergence
of a Cartesian PML method was established in
[
16
]
. In the remarkable work
[
16
]
, the authors consider
the Cartesian PML problem with the mixed boundary condition at the outer boundary of the truncated domain to further
extend the reflection argument in the
[
5
,
6
]
. This work provides several important analysis techniques
and innovation, such as the integral representation formula with PML and the limiting absorption principle for elastic
wave scattering solution. For the elastic scattering problems in periodic structures, the exponential convergence for
the proposed PML method was established in
[
24
,
25
,
38
]
for both one-dimensional and two-dimensional
cases. To the best of our knowledge, there is no convergence result on the PML method for the thermoelastic wave
scattering problems, especially for three-dimensional case.

The purpose of this paper is to study the convergence of the uniaxial PML method for the three-dimensional
time-harmonic thermoelastic wave scattering problems. We adopt the complex coordinate stretching in the
following form
[
16
,
19
]

\widetilde{x}_{j}(x_{j})=x_{j}+z\int_{0}^{x_{j}}\alpha_{j}(s)ds,\;z=\zeta+{\bf i},\;j=1,2,3,

where
\zeta\geq 0
is a constant to be specified,
{\bf i}
denotes the imaginary unit and
\alpha_{j}(s)
is the PML medium
property. Motivated by the work

[
16
]
, the parameter
\zeta
can be appropriately chosen to ensure the ellipticity of the elastic part of the
thermoelastic PML operator. We remark that the PML analysis for the thermoelastic wave scattering problem is more
challenging compared with the purely elastic problem, due to the coexistence of fast and slow compressional waves and
shear waves, with both complex and real wavenumbers. More restrictions on the PML parameters need to be imposed to
obtain the well-posedness and exponential convergence for the PML method. Besides, the method for the well-posedness
analysis of elastic PML problems in
[
16
]
cannot be directly applied to the thermoelastic problem, since the
symmetry in the thermoelastic PML variational formulation is lacking. Instead, we employ analytic Fredholm theory and
carefully handle parameter dependencies, together with appropriate choices of frequency to prove the well-posedness
of the PML problem under certain conditions. The exponential convergence of the PML method is finally established in
terms of the thickness and parameters of the PML layer, based on the PML extension and the exponential decay properties
of the modified fundamental solution.

The remaining part of the paper is organized as follows. In section 2, we present the mathematical model for the
time-harmonic thermoelastic scattering problem and recall the results on the existence and uniqueness of solutions.
Section 3 is devoted to propose the PML method for the thermoelastic wave equation and estimate the thermoelastic
fundamental solution matrix elements with PML extension. In section 4, the well-posedness for the thermoelastic PML
equation in the truncated domain and layer are carefully studied and analyzed. In section 5, we give a hard analysis
of the
H^{1/2}
norm of the PML extension potential on the outer boundary of the layer and further show the error estimate
between the Dirichlet-to-Neumann (DtN) operators of the original scattering problem and the truncated PML problem.
The exponential convergence of the PML method is finally established in terms of the thickness and parameters of the PML layer.

2
Thermoelastic scattering problem

In this section, we give a brief mathematical description of the thermoelastic scattering model and recall the
results on the existence and uniqueness of solutions.

We begin with an outline of the geometric configuration of the model problem. Let
\Omega\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
be a bounded domain
with smooth boundary
\partial\Omega
. Assume that
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}\backslash\overline{\Omega}
is filled with a homogeneous and an isotropic elastic medium,
characterized by the constant mass density
\rho
and Lamé parameters
\lambda
,
\mu
satisfying that
\mu>0
and

3\lambda+2\mu>0
, and the thermal coupling constants
\gamma
and
\eta
given by

\gamma=(3\lambda+2\mu)\alpha_{T},\quad\eta=\frac{T_{0}\gamma}{\lambda_{0}},

where
\alpha_{T}
is the coefficient of volumetric thermal expansion,
T_{0}
represents a reference temperature, and
\lambda_{0}

denotes the thermal conductivity coefficient.

The thermoelastic scattering problem with homogeneous Dirichlet boundary condition can be then modeled by the following
time-harmonic Biot’s system of linearized thermoelasticity
[
27
]

\displaystyle\Delta^{*}\boldsymbol{u}+\rho\omega^{2}\boldsymbol{u}-\gamma\nabla p={\bf Q}\quad

\displaystyle{\rm in}\;\;{\mathbb{R}}^{3}\backslash\overline{\Omega},

(2.1a)

\displaystyle\Delta p+qp+{\bf{i}}\omega\eta\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{u}=0\quad

\displaystyle{\rm in}\;\;{\mathbb{R}}^{3}\backslash\overline{\Omega},

(2.1b)

\displaystyle(\boldsymbol{u}^{\top},p)^{\top}=0\quad

\displaystyle{\rm on}\;\;\partial\Omega,

(2.1c)

where
\boldsymbol{u}=(u_{1},u_{2},u_{3})^{\top}
is the elastic displacement vector,
p
is the temperature field,

q={\bf{i}}\omega/\kappa
with
\kappa>0
representing the thermal diffusivity, and the Lamé operator
\Delta^{*}
defined by

\Delta^{*}\boldsymbol{u}:=\mu\Delta\boldsymbol{u}+(\lambda+\mu)\nabla\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{u}=\nabla\cdot\sigma(\boldsymbol{u}).

Here, the symmetric stress tensor
\sigma(\boldsymbol{u})
is given by the generalized Hooke’s law

\sigma(\boldsymbol{u})=(\lambda\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{u})\mathbb{I}+2\mu\epsilon(\boldsymbol{u}),\quad\epsilon(\boldsymbol{u})=\frac{1}{2}(\nabla\boldsymbol{u}+(\nabla\boldsymbol{u})^{\top}),

where
\mathbb{I}
is the identity matrix and
\nabla\boldsymbol{u}
denotes the displacement gradient tensor:

\nabla\boldsymbol{u}=\left[\begin{matrix}\partial_{x_{1}}u_{1}&\partial_{x_{2}}u_{1}&\partial_{x_{3}}u_{1}\\
\partial_{x_{1}}u_{2}&\partial_{x_{2}}u_{2}&\partial_{x_{3}}u_{2}\\
\partial_{x_{1}}u_{3}&\partial_{x_{2}}u_{3}&\partial_{x_{3}}u_{3}\end{matrix}\right].

Throughout the paper, we always assume that the source
{\bf Q}
has compact support inside

B_{1}=\{(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})^{\top}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3}:\lvert x_{j}\rvert<l_{j},j=1,2,3\}
with some constants

l_{j}>0,j=1,2,3
. The matrix form of the problem (
2.1a
)-(
2.1c
) can be
alternatively obtained

\displaystyle LU=({\bf Q}^{\top},0)^{\top},

\displaystyle\rm{\displaystyle in}\;\;{\mathbb{R}}^{3}\backslash\overline{\Omega},

(2.2)

\displaystyle U=(\boldsymbol{u}^{\top},p)^{\top}=0,

\displaystyle\rm{\displaystyle on}\;\;\partial\Omega,

where

L=\begin{bmatrix}(\Delta^{*}+\rho\omega^{2})\mathbb{I}&-\gamma\nabla\\
q\eta\kappa\nabla\cdot&\Delta+q\end{bmatrix}.

For a smooth solution
U
of the homogeneous thermoelastic system in
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}\backslash\overline{B}_{1}
, using
Helmholtz decomposition we have
\boldsymbol{u}=\nabla\phi+\boldsymbol{\psi}
with
\operatorname{div}\boldsymbol{\psi}=0
.
By substituting it into homogeneous thermoelastic system (
2.2
), we can obtain equations

\displaystyle(\lambda+2\mu)\Delta\phi+\rho\omega^{2}\phi-\gamma p

\displaystyle=0,

(2.3a)

\displaystyle-\mu\nabla\times\nabla\times\boldsymbol{\psi}+\rho\omega^{2}\boldsymbol{\psi}

\displaystyle=0,

(2.3b)

\displaystyle\Delta p+qp+{\bf{i}}\omega\eta\Delta\phi

\displaystyle=0.

(2.3c)

Substituting the equation (
2.3a
) into the equation (
2.3c
) yields

\Delta^{2}\phi+\left(\frac{\rho\omega^{2}}{\lambda+2\mu}+\frac{{\bf{i}}\omega\eta\gamma}{\lambda+2\mu}+q\right)\Delta\phi+\frac{qp\omega^{2}}{\lambda+2\mu}\phi=0

(2.4)

Rewrite the equation (
2.4
) in the following form

(\Delta+\lambda_{1}^{2})(\Delta+\lambda_{2}^{2})\phi=0,

(2.5)

where

\lambda_{1}^{2}+\lambda_{2}^{2}=\frac{\rho\omega^{2}}{\lambda+2\mu}+\frac{{\bf{i}}\omega\eta\gamma}{\lambda+2\mu}+q,\quad\lambda_{1}^{2}\lambda_{2}^{2}=\frac{qp\omega^{2}}{\lambda+2\mu}.

(2.6)

Noting that
k_{p}=\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{\rho\omega}{\lambda+2\mu}}
is the wave number of compressional waves,
we reformulate equations (
2.6
) into the following characteristic relation equations

\lambda_{1}^{2}+\lambda_{2}^{2}=\frac{{\bf{i}}\omega}{\kappa}+\frac{{\bf{i}}\omega\gamma\eta}{\lambda+2\mu}+k_{p}^{2},\quad\lambda_{1}^{2}\lambda_{2}^{2}=\frac{{\bf{i}}\omega}{\kappa}k_{p}^{2}.

(2.7)

The thermoelastic wave field obeys the following decomposition lemma
[
3
]
.

Lemma 2.1
.

The solution
U
of homogeneous thermoelastic system in
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}\backslash\overline{B}_{1}

can be written in the form

\boldsymbol{u}=\boldsymbol{u}^{1}+\boldsymbol{u}^{2}+\boldsymbol{u}^{3},\quad p=p^{1}+p^{2},

where
\boldsymbol{u}^{1},\boldsymbol{u}^{2},\boldsymbol{u}^{3}
and

satisfy

\displaystyle(\Delta+\lambda_{1}^{2})\boldsymbol{u}^{1}

\displaystyle=0,\quad

\displaystyle(\Delta+\lambda_{2}^{2})\boldsymbol{u}^{2}

\displaystyle=0,\quad

\displaystyle(\Delta+\lambda_{3}^{2})\boldsymbol{u}^{3}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\nabla\times\boldsymbol{u}^{1}

\displaystyle=0,\quad

\displaystyle\quad\nabla\times\boldsymbol{u}^{2}

\displaystyle=0,\quad

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{u}^{3}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle(\Delta+\lambda_{1}^{2})p^{1}

\displaystyle=0,\quad

\displaystyle(\Delta+\lambda_{2}^{2})p^{2}

\displaystyle=0.

Here, the wave number
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}
are the roots of the characteristic relation equations
(
2.7
), and
\lambda_{3}=\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{\rho}{\mu}}\omega
is the the wave number of shear waves.

In addition, the scattered wave field

is assumed to satisfy the Kupradze radiation conditions

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{u}^{j}=o(r^{-1}),

\displaystyle\partial_{x_{i}}\boldsymbol{u}^{j}=O(r^{-2}),

\displaystyle p^{j}=o(r^{-1}),

\displaystyle\partial_{x_{i}}p^{j}=O(r^{-2}),

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{u}^{3}=o(r^{-1}),

\displaystyle r(\partial_{r}\boldsymbol{u}^{3}-i\lambda_{3}\boldsymbol{u}^{3})=O(r^{-1}).

For given

(cf.
[
9

The entries

In what follows, we introduce the thermoelastic single-layer potential for

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{ij}(\partial_{y},\nu(y),\omega)=

\displaystyle(1-\delta_{i4})(1-\delta_{j4})\Big(\delta_{ij}\mu\frac{\partial}{\partial\nu(y)}+\lambda\nu_{i}(y)\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{j}}+\mu\nu_{j}(y)\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}\Big)

E(\boldsymbol{f})=\frac{1}{2}\Big[\Psi_{SL}(\mathcal{N}\boldsymbol{f})-\Psi_{DL}(\boldsymbol{f})\Big]\quad{\rm in}\;{\mathbb{R}}^{3}\backslash\overline{B}_{1}.

The PML method for thermoelastic wave

Let
B_{2}=\{(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})^{\top}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3}:\lvert x_{j}\rvert<l_{j}+d_{j},j=1,2,3\}
be a cuboid domain
surrounding

. The truncated PML domain and the PML layer are denoted by
\Omega_{2}:=B_{2}\backslash\overline{\Omega}

and
\Omega_{{\rm PML}}:=B_{2}\backslash\overline{B_{1}}

We introduce even functions
\alpha_{j}(t)\in C^{2}({\mathbb{R}}),j=1,2,3

\alpha_{j}^{\prime}(t)\geq 0

, and
\alpha_{j}(t)=\alpha_{0}
for
\lvert t\rvert\geq\overline{l_{j}}

where

For example, for

\nabla F=\operatorname{diag}\{s_{1}(x_{1}),s_{2}(x_{2}),s_{3}(x_{3})\}

Direct calculations show that

\displaystyle\widetilde{\nabla}\cdot=J^{-1}\nabla\cdot J(\nabla F)^{-1},\quad J={\rm det}(\nabla F),

\displaystyle\widetilde{\nabla}=\left(\frac{1}{s_{1}(x_{1})}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{1}},\frac{1}{s_{2}(x_{2})}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{2}},\frac{1}{s_{3}(x_{3})}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{3}}\right).

Define the complex distance

A direct estimate gives (see also

has a positive lower bound, we have

\|v\|_{H^{1/2}(\partial B_{i})}\leq C\Big(d\|v\|_{L^{\infty}(\partial B_{i})}+d^{3/2}\|\nabla v\|_{L^{\infty}(\partial B_{i})}\Big).

(5.6)

The following lemma on the decay property of the PML extension will play an important role in the
convergence analysis of the PML method.

Lemma 5.1
.

For
\boldsymbol{f}=(f_{1},f_{2},f_{3},f_{4})^{\top}\in H^{1/2}(\partial B_{1})^{4}
, under the assumption (
5.1
)
we have the estimate for the PML extension

\|\widetilde{E}(\boldsymbol{f})\|_{{H^{1/2}(\partial B_{2})}^{4}}\leq C\Big[(1+\zeta\alpha_{0})^{2}+\alpha_{0}^{2}\Big]^{\frac{m}{2}}P(d)e^{-r_{0}\alpha_{0}d}\|\boldsymbol{f}\|_{{H^{1/2}(\partial B_{1})^{4}}},

(5.7)

where
m>0
is an integer,
r_{0}
appears in (
5.5
) and
P(d)
is a function that grows at most polynomially.

Proof.

Let
M_{(k)}
and
M^{(k)}
be the
k
-th row vector and column vector, respectively, of matrix
M
over
\mathbb{C}
.
For
x\in\partial B_{2}
, the
k
-th component of the PML extension
\widetilde{E}(\boldsymbol{f})

(see (
3.7
) for its definition) is given by

\widetilde{E}(\boldsymbol{f})_{k}(x)=\frac{1}{2}\Big[\widetilde{\Psi}_{SL}(\mathcal{N}\boldsymbol{f})_{k}(x)-\widetilde{\Psi}_{DL}(\boldsymbol{f})_{k}(x)\Big],

(5.8)

where

\displaystyle\widetilde{\Psi}_{SL}(\mathcal{N}\boldsymbol{f})_{k}(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{\partial B_{1}}\widetilde{\Phi}(x-y,\omega)_{(k)}(\mathcal{N}\boldsymbol{f})(y)ds(y),

\displaystyle\widetilde{\Psi}_{DL}(\boldsymbol{f})_{k}(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{\partial B_{1}}[\mathcal{S}(\partial_{y},\nu(y),\omega)\widetilde{\Phi}^{\top}(x-y,\omega)]^{(k)}\boldsymbol{f}(y)ds(y).

By using the estimate for
H^{1/2}
-norm (
5.6
) on
\partial B_{2}
, we have

\displaystyle\|\widetilde{\Psi}_{SL}(\mathcal{N}\boldsymbol{f})_{k}\|_{H^{1/2}(\partial B_{2})}\leq

\displaystyle Cd\Big\|\int_{\partial B_{1}}\widetilde{\Phi}(x-y,\omega)_{(k)}(\mathcal{N}{\boldsymbol{f}})(y)ds(y)\Big\|_{L^{\infty}(\partial B_{2})}

(5.9)

\displaystyle+Cd^{3/2}\Big\|\nabla_{x}\int_{\partial B_{1}}\widetilde{\Phi}(x-y,\omega)_{(k)}(\mathcal{N}{\boldsymbol{f}})(y)ds(y)\Big\|_{L^{\infty}(\partial B_{2})},

and

\displaystyle\|\widetilde{\Psi}_{DL}(\boldsymbol{f})_{k}\|_{H^{1/2}(\partial B_{2})}\leq\;

\displaystyle Cd\Big\|\int_{\partial B_{1}}\Big[\mathcal{S}(\partial_{y},\nu(y),\omega)\widetilde{\Phi}^{\top}(x-y,\omega)\Big]^{(k)}\boldsymbol{f}(y)ds(y)\Big\|_{L^{\infty}(\partial B_{2})}

(5.10)

\displaystyle+Cd^{3/2}\Big\|\nabla_{x}\int_{\partial B_{1}}\Big[\mathcal{S}(\partial_{y},\nu(y),\omega)\widetilde{\Phi}^{\top}(x-y,\omega)\Big]^{(k)}\boldsymbol{f}(y)ds(y)\Big\|_{L^{\infty}(\partial B_{2})}.

By using the dual pair
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{H^{-1/2}(\partial B_{1})\times H^{1/2}(\partial B_{1})}
and
taking the maximum on
\partial B_{2}
for (
5.9
) yield

\displaystyle\|\widetilde{\Psi}_{SL}(\mathcal{N}\boldsymbol{f})_{k}\|_{H^{1/2}(\partial B_{2})}\leq\;

\displaystyle Cd\max\limits_{x\in\partial B_{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{4}\|\widetilde{\Phi}(x-\cdot,\omega)_{kj}\|_{{H^{1/2}(\partial B_{1})}}\|f_{j}\|_{{H^{1/2}(\partial B_{1})}}

(5.11)

\displaystyle+Cd^{3/2}\max\limits_{x\in\partial B_{2}}\max\limits_{t\in\{1,2,3\}}\sum_{j=1}^{4}\Big\|\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{t}}\widetilde{\Phi}(x-\cdot,\omega)_{kj}\Big\|_{{H^{1/2}(\partial B_{1})}}\|f_{j}\|_{{H^{1/2}(\partial B_{1})}},

where the boundness of DtN operator
\mathcal{N}
is used. The estimate for (
5.10
) can be similarly
obtained in combination with the fact that
H^{-1/2}(\partial B_{1})
-norm is controlled by
H^{1/2}(\partial B_{1})
-norm
for smooth functions:

\displaystyle\|\widetilde{\Psi}_{DL}(\boldsymbol{f})_{k}\|_{H^{1/2}(\partial B_{2})}\leq\;

\displaystyle Cd\max\limits_{x\in\partial B_{1}}\sum_{i=1}^{4}\sum_{a=1}^{4}\|\mathcal{S}_{ia}\widetilde{\Phi}(x-\cdot,\omega)_{ka}\|_{{H^{1/2}(\partial B_{1})}}\|f_{i}\|_{{H^{1/2}(\partial B_{1})}}

(5.12)

\displaystyle+Cd^{3/2}\max\limits_{x\in\partial B_{1}}\max\limits_{t\in\{1,2,3\}}\sum_{i=1}^{4}\sum_{a=1}^{4}\Big\|\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{t}}\mathcal{S}_{ia}\widetilde{\Phi}(x-\cdot,\omega)_{ka}\Big\|_{{H^{1/2}(\partial B_{1})}}\|f_{i}\|_{{H^{1/2}(\partial B_{1})}}.

Using the
H^{1/2}
-norm estimate (
5.6
) on
\partial B_{1}
, we obtain the following bounds
for (
5.11
) and (
5.12
)

\displaystyle\|\widetilde{\Psi}_{SL}(\mathcal{N}\boldsymbol{f})_{k}\|_{H^{1/2}(\partial B_{2})}\leq

\displaystyle Cd\|\boldsymbol{f}\|_{{H^{1/2}(\partial B_{1})^{4}}}\sum_{j=1}^{4}\Big(d\|\widetilde{\Phi}(x-y,\omega)_{kj}\|_{L^{\infty}(\partial B_{1}\times\partial B_{2})}

\displaystyle+d^{3/2}\|\nabla_{y}\widetilde{\Phi}(x-y,\omega)_{kj}\|_{L^{\infty}(\partial B_{1}\times\partial B_{2})}\Big)

\displaystyle+Cd^{3/2}\|\boldsymbol{f}\|_{{H^{1/2}(\partial B_{1})^{4}}}\max\limits_{t\in\{1,2,3\}}\sum_{j=1}^{4}\Big(d\Big\|\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{t}}\widetilde{\Phi}(x-y,\omega)_{kj}\Big\|_{L^{\infty}(\partial B_{1}\times\partial B_{2})}

\displaystyle+d^{3/2}\Big\|\nabla_{y}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{t}}\widetilde{\Phi}(x-y,\omega)_{kj}\Big\|_{L^{\infty}(\partial B_{1}\times\partial B_{2})}\Big),

and

\displaystyle\|\widetilde{\Psi}_{DL}(\boldsymbol{f})_{k}\|_{H^{1/2}(\partial B_{2})}\leq

\displaystyle Cd\|\boldsymbol{f}\|_{{H^{1/2}(\partial B_{1})^{4}}}\sum_{i=1}^{4}\sum_{a=1}^{3}\Big(d\|\mathcal{S}_{ia}\widetilde{\Phi}(x-y,\omega)_{ka}\|_{L^{\infty}(\partial B_{1}\times\partial B_{2})}

\displaystyle+d^{3/2}\|\nabla_{y}\mathcal{S}_{ia}\widetilde{\Phi}(x-y,\omega)_{ka}\|_{L^{\infty}(\partial B_{1}\times\partial B_{2})}\Big)

\displaystyle+Cd^{3/2}\|\boldsymbol{f}\|_{{H^{1/2}(\partial B_{1})^{4}}}\max\limits_{t\in\{1,2,3\}}\sum_{i=1}^{4}\sum_{a=1}^{3}\Big(d\Big\|\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{t}}\mathcal{S}_{ia}\widetilde{\Phi}(x-y,\omega)_{ka}\Big\|_{L^{\infty}(\partial B_{1}\times\partial B_{2})}

This, combined with the representation (

∎

Based on Lemma
5.1
, we have the error estimate between DtN operators

\mathcal{N}
and
\hat{\mathcal{N}}
.

Lemma 5.2
.

For
\boldsymbol{f}\in H^{1/2}(\partial B_{1})^{4}
, we have

\|(\mathcal{N}-\hat{\mathcal{N}})\boldsymbol{f}\|_{{H^{-1/2}(\partial B_{1})}^{4}}\leq C\Big[(1+\zeta\alpha_{0})^{2}+\alpha_{0}^{2}\Big]^{\frac{m}{2}}P(d)e^{-r_{0}\alpha_{0}d}\|\boldsymbol{f}\|_{{H^{1/2}(\partial B_{1})^{4}}},

(5.13)

where
m>0
is an integer,
r_{0}
appears in (
5.5
) and
P(d)
is a function that grows at most polynomially.

Proof.

Given
\boldsymbol{f}\in H^{1/2}(\partial B_{1})^{4}
, let
\widetilde{E}(\boldsymbol{f})
be the PML extension potential defined in (
3.7
).
From the definition of DtN operator
\mathcal{N}
in (
2.10
), we know

\mathcal{N}\boldsymbol{f}=\mathcal{R}E(\boldsymbol{f})|_{\partial B_{1}}=\mathcal{R}\widetilde{E}(\boldsymbol{f})|_{\partial B_{1}}.

Now let
\widetilde{U}
be the solution of system (
5.3a
)-(
5.3d
) with
G=\boldsymbol{f}

on
\partial B_{1}
. From the definition of DtN operator
\hat{\mathcal{N}}
in (
5.2
), we know

\hat{\mathcal{N}}\boldsymbol{f}=\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}\widetilde{U}|_{\partial B_{1}}=\mathcal{R}\widetilde{U}|_{\partial B_{1}}.

Then
(\mathcal{N}-\hat{\mathcal{N}})\boldsymbol{f}=\mathcal{R}\widetilde{W}|_{\partial B_{1}}
, where

\widetilde{W}=\widetilde{U}-\widetilde{E}(\boldsymbol{f})
satisfies the system (
4.20a
)-(
4.20d
)
with
F=-\widetilde{E}(\boldsymbol{f})
on
\partial B_{2}
. By Theorem
4.6
, the following stability estimate

\|\widetilde{W}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega_{{\rm PML}})^{4}}\leq C(d)\|\widetilde{E}(\boldsymbol{f})\|_{{H^{1/2}(\partial B_{2})}^{4}}

(5.14)

therefore holds, where the constant
C(d)
depends on the thickness
d
and grows at most polynomially in
d
.

Let
\Psi\in H^{1/2}(\partial B_{1})^{4}
be extended to a function in
H^{1}(\Omega_{\text{PML}})^{4}

(still denoted by
\Psi
) such that
\Psi=0
on
\partial B_{2}
and
\|\Psi\|_{H^{1/2}(\partial B_{1})^{4}}\geq C\|\Psi\|_{H^{1}(\Omega_{\text{PML}})^{4}}
. Then it follows from the integration by parts and (

) that

\displaystyle\lvert\langle\mathcal{R}\widetilde{W}|_{\partial B_{1}},\Psi\rangle_{\partial B_{1}}\rvert

\displaystyle=\lvert\langle(\hat{\mathcal{N}}-\mathcal{N})\boldsymbol{f},\Psi\rangle_{\partial B_{1}}\rvert=\lvert\mathcal{B}_{{\rm PML}}(\widetilde{W},\Psi)\rvert

\displaystyle\leq C\|\widetilde{W}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega_{{\rm PML}})^{4}}\|\Psi\|_{H^{1}(\Omega_{{\rm PML}})^{4}}

\displaystyle\leq C(d)\|\widetilde{E}(\boldsymbol{f})\|_{{H^{1/2}(\partial B_{2})}^{4}}\|\Psi\|_{{H^{1/2}(\partial B_{1})}^{4}},

where the sesquilinear form
\mathcal{B}_{{\rm PML}}(\cdot,\cdot)
is defined in (
4.2
)
for

. The it follows from the definition of

that

\|\mathcal{R}\widetilde{W}|_{\partial B_{1}}\|_{{H^{-1/2}(\partial B_{1})}^{4}}=\sup\limits_{\Psi\in H^{1/2}(\partial B_{1})^{4}\backslash\{0\}}\frac{\lvert\langle\mathcal{R}\widetilde{W}|_{\partial B_{1}},\Psi\rangle_{\partial B_{1}}\rvert}{\|\Psi\|_{{H^{1/2}(\partial B_{1})}^{4}}}\leq C(d)\|\widetilde{E}(\boldsymbol{f})\|_{{H^{1/2}(\partial B_{2})}^{4}}.

arrive at the desired estimate (

Now we can establish the main result on the exponential convergence of the PML method.

Theorem 5.3

(Convergence Theorem)
.

Let conditions (
4.6
) be satisfied and
\alpha_{0}d
be sufficiently large. For given

{\bf Q}\in H^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{3}\backslash\overline{\Omega})^{3}
with compact support inside
B_{1}
, let
U
and
\widetilde{U}

be the solutions of the problems (
2.2
) with Kupradze radiation conditions (
2.8
)
and (
4.1a
)-(
4.1d
), respectively. Then we have the following error estimate

\|\widetilde{U}-U\|_{H^{1}(\Omega_{1})^{4}}\leq C\Big[(1+\zeta\alpha_{0})^{2}+\alpha_{0}^{2}\Big]^{\frac{m}{2}}P(d)e^{-r_{0}\alpha_{0}d}\|\widetilde{U}\|_{{H^{1/2}(\partial B_{1})^{4}}},

(5.15)

where
m>0
is an integer,
r_{0}
appears in (
5.5
) and
P(d)
is a function that grows at most polynomially.

Proof.

Noting that
{\bf Q}\in H^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{3}\backslash\overline{\Omega})^{3}
is compactly supported inside
B_{1}
, then
U
and

\widetilde{U}
satisfy the following variational problems

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{1}(U,\Psi)

\displaystyle=-\int_{\Omega_{1}}{\bf Q}\cdot\overline{\Psi}dx,\quad\forall\;\Psi\in H^{1}_{\partial B_{1}}(\Omega_{1})^{4}

(5.16)

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{\Omega_{2}}(\widetilde{U},\Psi)

\displaystyle=-\int_{\Omega_{1}}{\bf Q}\cdot\overline{\Psi}dx,\quad\forall\;\Psi\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega_{2})^{4}.

(5.17)

Simple decomposition of
\mathcal{B}_{\Omega_{2}}
and integration by parts imply

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{\Omega_{2}}(\widetilde{U},\Psi)=

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{1}(\widetilde{U},\Psi)+\langle\mathcal{N}\widetilde{U},\Psi\rangle_{\partial B_{1}}+\mathcal{B}_{\Omega_{{\rm PML}}}(\widetilde{U},\Psi)

(5.18)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{1}(\widetilde{U},\Psi)+\langle\mathcal{N}\widetilde{U},\Psi\rangle_{\partial B_{1}}+\langle-\hat{\mathcal{N}}\widetilde{U},\Psi\rangle_{\partial B_{1}}.

Subtracting the equations (
5.16
) and (
5.17
) and using (
5.18
) gives

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{1}(U-\widetilde{U},\Psi)=\langle(\mathcal{N}-\hat{\mathcal{N}})\widetilde{U},\Psi\rangle_{\partial B_{1}}.

This, combined with the trace theorem gives

\displaystyle\lvert\mathcal{B}_{1}(U-\widetilde{U},\Psi)\rvert

\displaystyle\leq C\|(\mathcal{N}-\hat{\mathcal{N}})\widetilde{U}\|_{H^{-1/2}(\partial B_{1})^{4}}\|\Psi\|_{H^{1}(\Omega_{1})^{4}},

which obviously implies

\frac{\lvert\mathcal{B}_{1}(U-\widetilde{U},\Psi)\rvert}{\|\Psi\|_{H^{1}(\Omega_{1})^{4}}}\leq C\|(\mathcal{N}-\hat{\mathcal{N}})\widetilde{U}\|_{H^{-1/2}(\partial B_{1})^{4}}.

(5.19)

The desired error estimate (
5.15
) follows from Lemma
5.2

and the inf-sup condition (
2.13
) for the sesquilinear form
\mathcal{B}_{1}
.

∎

Remark 5.4

(Uniform boundedness of
\widetilde{U}
with respect to large PML parameters)
.

Since
\widetilde{U}
is the solution of the truncated PML problem (
4.1a
)-(
4.1d
),

\|\widetilde{U}\|_{H^{1/2}(\partial B_{1})^{4}}
naturally depends on the PML parameters
d
and
\alpha_{0}
.
A natural question is: how does
\|\widetilde{U}\|_{H^{1/2}(\partial B_{1})^{4}}
grow as
d
and
\alpha_{0}
vary?
We discuss this problem below under the conditions of Theorem
5.3
.

From the triangle inequality

\|\widetilde{U}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega_{1})^{4}}-\|U\|_{H^{1}(\Omega_{1})^{4}}\leq\|\widetilde{U}-U\|_{H^{1}(\Omega_{1})^{4}},

and trace theorem

\frac{1}{C_{\Omega_{1}}}\|\widetilde{U}\|_{H^{1/2}(\partial B_{1})^{4}}\leq\|\widetilde{U}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega_{1})^{4}},

where
C_{\Omega_{1}}
is a positive constant independent of
d
and
\alpha_{0}
.
It then follows from the error estimate (
5.15
) that

\frac{1}{C_{\Omega_{1}}}\|\widetilde{U}\|_{H^{1/2}(\partial B_{1})^{4}}-C\left[(1+\zeta\alpha_{0})^{2}+\alpha_{0}^{2}\right]^{\frac{m}{2}}P(d)e^{-r_{0}\alpha_{0}d}\|\widetilde{U}\|_{H^{1/2}(\partial B_{1})^{4}}\leq\|U\|_{H^{1}(\Omega_{1})^{4}},

where
m
is a positive integer. It can be observed that when
\alpha_{0}d
is sufficiently large,

\|\widetilde{U}\|_{H^{1/2}(\partial B_{1})^{4}}
remains uniformly bounded.

6
Conclusion

In this paper, we studied the uniaxial PML method in the Cartesian coordinates for 3D time-harmonic
thermoelastic scattering problems. Under certain constraints on model parameters, we showed the existence
of unique solution except possibly for a discrete set of frequencies, for the PML problems both in the
truncated domain and PML layer by using the classical analytic Fredholm theory. Furthermore, the exponential
convergence of the uniaxial PML method was established in terms of the thickness and absorbing parameters of
the PML layer. The proof was based on the error estimate between the Dirichlet-to-Neumann (DtN) operators for
the original scattering problem and the truncated PML problem, which depends on the exponential decay properties
for the PML extensions and modified fundamental solutions.

Our method can be extended to other scattering problems such as the scattering by waveguide structures
or poroelastic obstacles. It is also interesting to study the convergence of proposed uniaxial PML method
with distinct boundary conditions at the outer PML interface, and spherical PML method for time-harmonic
thermoelastic scattering problems. We hope to report such results in the near future.
