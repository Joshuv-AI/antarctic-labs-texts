Quadratic Equations over Graph Products and the Exponent of Periodicity
Volker Diekert, Silas Natterer, Alexander Thumm

Abstract
In 1977, Makanin established the decidability of equations in free monoids. A key ingredient in his proof is the exponent of periodicity : for a word w w , it is the largest exponent e e such that w w contains a nonempty factor of the form p e p^{e} . Makanin showed that if a system of equations in a free monoid has a solution with sufficiently large exponent of periodicity, then it has infinitely many solutions. However, the converse – whether the existence of infinitely many solutions implies the existence of solutions with arbitrarily large exponent of periodicity – remains open. In this paper, we investigate the analogous problem for quadratic equations in finitely generated groups. Using normal forms to define the exponent of periodicity, we identify structural conditions on groups and their normal forms that guarantee that infinite solution sets of quadratic systems have an unbounded exponent of periodicity. We prove that these conditions are preserved under graph products and, in particular, hold for all finitely generated right-angled Artin groups. In addition, we show that they also hold for finitely generated (graph products of) torsion-free nilpotent and hyperbolic groups, and we characterize the Baumslag–Solitar groups satisfying them.

The breakthrough regarding the satisfiability problem for word equations in free monoids and semigroups is due to Makanin. He showed in 1977 in his seminal paper
[
42

that the existential theory in free semigroups is indeed decidable. In Makanin’s proof (of about 80 dense pages), the notion of the
exponent of periodicity
plays a central role. The exponent of periodicity
\exp(w)

w
is the largest natural number
e

w=up^{e}v
where
p
is nonempty.
3
3

3

This implies that
p
is a primitive word.

For a set
L
of words, the exponent of periodicity, denoted by
\exp(L)
, is defined as the supremum
\sup\{\exp(w)\mid w\in L\}\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}
.

The interest here is in the exponent of periodicity of the solution set
\mathrm{Sol}(\mathcal{S})
of a given finite system of word equations
\mathcal{S}
.
Let
\Gamma
and
\mathcal{X}
be finite sets of
constants
and
variables
, respectively.
Then
\mathcal{S}
is a set of equations
U=V
where
U,V\in(\Gamma\cup\mathcal{X})^{*}
,
and a
solution
of
\mathcal{S}
is given by a mapping
\sigma:\mathcal{X}\to\Gamma^{*}
such that substituting every occurrence of every variable
X\in\mathcal{X}
by its corresponding image
\sigma(X)\in\Gamma^{*}
yields an equality
\sigma(U)=\sigma(V)\in\Gamma^{*}
for all
(U=V)\in\mathcal{S}
.
We then define the exponent of periodicity of a solution
\sigma\in\mathrm{Sol}(\mathcal{S})
and of the system of word equations itself by
\exp(\sigma)=\exp(\sigma(\mathcal{X}))
and
\exp(\mathcal{S})=\sup\{\exp(\sigma)\mid\sigma\in\mathrm{Sol}(\mathcal{S})\}
, respectively.

In his paper, Makanin crucially used a result of Hmelevskiĭ
[
30
]
that the exponent of periodicity
\exp(\sigma)
of a shortest solution
\sigma:\mathcal{X}\to\Gamma^{*}
of a finite system word equation
\mathcal{S}
is bounded by a computable function
f
in the input size.
Moreover, if there is any
\sigma^{\prime}\in\mathrm{Sol}(\mathcal{S})
with a higher exponent than
f(n)
, where
n
is the input size of
\mathcal{S}
, then there are infinitely many solutions and, in addition, we have
\exp(\mathcal{S})=\infty
. The amazing fact is that we do not know whether the converse holds.

Problem 1
.

Does the existence of infinitely many
solutions for a finite system of word equations
\mathcal{S}
in a free semigroup imply
\exp(\mathcal{S})=\infty
?

We believe that Problem
1
has a positive answer, but so far we have only been able to prove this conjecture in restricted cases. The state of the art (as it is known to the authors to date) is reported in our paper
[
19
]
, which provides a positive result for quadratic equations (with certain regular constraints) and for equations with at most two variables.

The support for our conjecture (that Problem
1
has a positive answer) is based on the fact that Makanin’s algorithm, which decides whether
U=V
is solvable, can be modified to decide whether there are infinitely many solution. This has been shown by Plandowski and Schubert
[
52
]
.
Their reduction is astonishingly simply, so it is worth to explain it in a few lines (without taking into account complexity).

Consider any finite system of word equations
\mathcal{S}
.
All we need is an effective upper bound (in terms of the input size) on the maximal exponent of periodicity
\exp(\sigma)
for a solution
\sigma
minimizing the total length
\sum_{(U=V)\in\mathcal{S}}|\sigma(UV)|
. Given such a computable bound
f
, we can decide whether there are infinitely many solutions as follows:
Suppose the input size of the equation is
n
and we have
N=f(n)
.
Then Makanin’s algorithm produces, see e.g.
[
11
, p. 432]
, a finite directed
search graph

\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{S},N)
describing all solutions in the set

\mathrm{Sol}(\mathcal{S},N)=\{\sigma\in\mathrm{Sol}(\mathcal{S})\mid\exp(\sigma)\leq N\}.

(1)

Moreover,
\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{S},N)
has the property that it contains a nontrivial strongly connected component, then
|\mathrm{Sol}(\mathcal{S})|=\infty
.
Now, for the upper bound
N
on
f(n)
it holds that as soon as there is a solution
\sigma
such that

\exp(\sigma)\geq N
, then
|\mathrm{Sol}(\mathcal{S})|=\infty
.
In order to reduce the problem of whether
|\mathrm{Sol}(\mathcal{S})|=\infty
to the satisfiability problem of word equations, we guess a constant
a\in\Gamma
and a variable
X\in\mathcal{X}
.
Then we introduce three fresh variables
P,Z,Q
and add an additional equation

X=P(aZ)^{N}Q
.
This yields a system
\mathcal{S}_{N}
of word equations, and we can decide whether there is a solution using Makanin’s algorithm.
If so, then
|\mathrm{Sol}(\mathcal{S})|=\infty
.

If
\mathcal{S}_{N}
has no solution, then we construct the search graph
\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{S},N)
and check whether it contains a nontrivial strongly connected component.
If so, then
|\mathrm{Sol}(\mathcal{S})|=\infty
and if not, then
|\mathrm{Sol}(\mathcal{S})|<\infty
.
This gives an effective decision procedure to decide whether
\mathrm{Sol}(\mathcal{S})
has infinitely many solutions.

The paper yields a polynomial-time reduction of the finiteness problem for
\mathrm{Sol}(\mathcal{S})
to the satisfiability problem for a finite system of word equations.
Firstly, they use that
N

The remaining obstacle to resolving Problem
1
is that we cannot yet exclude that
\mathcal{S}
has infinitely many solutions

remains bounded.

In this paper, we consider equations over torsion-free groups. Makanin showed in
[
43
,
44
]
that both the existential theory of equations and the positive theory in free groups are decidable. In this setting, let
F=F(\Gamma)
be a free group over a finite basis
\Gamma\subseteq F
. Thus
\Delta=\Gamma\cup\Gamma^{-1}
is a set with an involution
a\mapsto\bar{a}={a}^{-1}
.
Likewise, the set of variables
\mathcal{X}
comes with an involution
X\mapsto\bar{X}
. Both involutions are without fixed points, and we only allow mappings
\sigma:\mathcal{X}\to F
that respect the involution: we require
\sigma(\bar{X})=\overline{\sigma(X)}
for all variables.

To solve an equation
W=1
in a free group, the immediate idea is to use the fact that there is a canonical bijection between the group and the regular set of reduced words
\mathrm{NF}\subseteq\Delta^{*}
(words without any factor
a\bar{a}
for
a\in\Delta
). We represent an element in
F
by its unique reduced normal form in
\mathrm{NF}
. More precisely, for
g\in F
, we let
\mathrm{nf}(g)\in\mathrm{NF}
be the unique word such that
\pi(\mathrm{nf}(g))=g
, where
\pi:\Delta^{*}\to F
is the canonical epimorphism.

This enables a reduction to the existential theory of word equations
U=V
in the free monoid with involution
(\Delta\cup\mathcal{X})^{*}
, under the constraint that a solution
\sigma
maps variables to reduced words. Crucially, in Makanin’s proof, the result regarding the exponent of periodicity can be extended to free monoids with involution. If
\sigma
is a shortest solution of an equation, then
\exp(\sigma)
can again be bounded by a singly exponential function.

Remarkably, the result also holds in the opposite direction: if a system
\mathcal{S}
of equations has infinitely many solutions
\sigma:\mathcal{X}\to F
, then
\exp(\mathcal{S})=\infty
where

\exp(\mathcal{S})=\sup\{\exp(\mathrm{nf}(\sigma(X)))\mid X\in\mathcal{X}\wedge\sigma\text{ solves }\mathcal{S}\text{ in }F\}.

According to personal communication with the authors, this positive result was established during the work on the positive solution of Tarski’s conjectures by Kharlampovich and Miasnikov
[
37
]
, as well as Sela
[
55
]
. Thus, the answer to Problem
1

There is another (much simpler) class of groups where we know that solution sets for systems with infinitely many solutions have an infinite exponent of periodicity (with respect to their usual normal forms): the class of finitely generated free Abelian groups.
This raises the natural question of what whether this is also true for classes ‘in between’.

The most well-studied class sitting between free groups and free Abelian groups consists of (torsion-free) free partially commutative groups, which are also called right-angled Artin groups.

Our Main Results

Our results concern the family
\mathcal{G}
of triples
[G,\pi,\nf]
where
G
denotes a group,
\pi:\Gamma^{*}\to G
an epimorphism, and
\nf:G\to\Gamma^{*}
a
normal form
such that the following conditions hold.
12
12

12

As usual objects in the family
\mathcal{G}
are abstract and defined up to isomorphism.

{bracketenumerate}

The group
G
is torsion free and the set
\sqrt[2]{g}=\{h\in G\mid h^{2}=g\}
is finite for every
g\in G
.

The domain of
\pi:\Gamma^{*}\to G
is a finitely generated free monoid – hence,
G
is finitely generated – and
\mathrm{nf}:G\to\Gamma^{*}
is a section of
\pi
, meaning that
\pi(\nf(g))=g
for all
g\in G
.

For all
u,p,v\in G
with
p\neq 1
and for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists an
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that the word
\mathrm{nf}(up^{N}v)
contains a nonempty factor
q^{n}\in\Gamma^{+}
. We denote this property (which depends on the normal form
\mathrm{nf}
) as
\exp(\mathrm{nf}(up^{*}v))=\infty
.

Membership in
\mathcal{G}
crucially depends on the choice of the pair
(\pi,\mathrm{nf})
.
If a finitely generated group
G
satisfies the first item, then we might artificially define
\pi:\Gamma^{*}\to G
and
\nf:G\to\Gamma^{*}
in such a way that
[G,\pi,\mathrm{nf}]\in\mathcal{G}
.
In our examples, we mostly investigate groups
G
alongside some ‘natural’ choice of the pair
(\pi,\mathrm{nf})
.
For instance,
[\mathbb{Z},\pi,\nf]
belongs to
\mathcal{G}
for the most obvious choice of
(\pi,\nf)
, viz. the epimorphism
\pi:\Gamma^{*}\to\mathbb{Z}
induced by the inclusion
\Gamma=\{\pm 1\}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}
and its unique section
\nf:\mathbb{Z}\to\Gamma^{*}
with image
\nf(\mathbb{Z})=\{-1\}^{*}\cup\{+1\}^{*}
.

Our first main result on the family
\mathcal{G}
is the following closure property.

Theorem 3
.

Let
\mathbb{G}
be a graph product over finitely many groups
G
with
[G,\pi_{G},\mathrm{nf}_{G}]\in\mathcal{G}
. Then
[\mathbb{G},\pi_{\mathbb{G}},\mathrm{nf}_{\mathbb{G}}]\in\mathcal{G}
for a natural construction of
(\pi_{\mathbb{G}},\mathrm{nf}_{\mathbb{G}})
depending only on the
(\pi_{G},\mathrm{nf}_{G})
.

Our second main result, and our primary motivation for studying the family
\mathcal{G}
, concerns the exponent of periodicity for solution sets of quadratic equations.

Theorem 4
.

Let
[G,\pi,\mathrm{nf}]\in\mathcal{G}
.
Further, let
\mathcal{S}
be a finite quadratic system of equations over the group
G
in a set of variables
\mathcal{X}
.
If the system
\mathcal{S}
has infinitely many solutions, then there exists a variable
X\in\mathcal{X}
such that
\exp(\mathrm{nf}(\{\sigma(X)\mid\sigma\textup{ solves }\mathcal{S}\}))=\infty
.

Combining the above results, we immediately obtain the following.

Corollary 5
.

Let
\mathbb{G}
be a graph product over finitely many groups
G
with
[G,\pi_{G},\mathrm{nf}_{G}]\in\mathcal{G}
, and let
(\pi_{\mathbb{G}},\nf_{\mathbb{G}})
be as in Theorem
3
.
If a finite quadratic system of equations
\mathcal{S}
over
\mathbb{G}
has infinitely many solutions, then
\exp(\mathrm{nf}_{\mathbb{G}}(\{\sigma(X)\mid\sigma\textup{ solves }\mathcal{S}\}))=\infty
for some variable
X
.

As an abstract result, Corollary
5
is most interesting when applied to natural triples. For example,
finitely generated free groups belong to
\mathcal{G}
with the standard presentation and normal forms given by reduced words.
This was shown first with a combinatorial proof by Bastien Laboureix (unpublished) during an internship
at Stuttgart in 2019.

13

13

His proof
followed an outline communicated by Olga Kharlampovich.

On the other hand, Corollary
5
is more powerful.
As shown above, we have
[\mathbb{Z},\pi,\nf]\in\mathcal{G}
with the most natural choices.
Hence, the example of free groups is just a special case of the following corollary for right-angled Artin groups.

Corollary 6

Suppose that
G=G(\Gamma,I)
is a right-angled Artin group.
Then
[G,\pi,\mathrm{nf}_{\mathrm{slex}}]\in\mathcal{G}
where
\pi:\Gamma^{*}\to G
is the canonical epimorphism and
\mathrm{nf}_{\mathrm{slex}}:G\to\Gamma^{*}
is the short-lex normal form with respect to any linear order
\leq

.

Preliminaries

We use standard notation of set theory.
Frequently, we identify an element with the singleton containing this element.
The restriction of a mapping
\psi:B\to C
to some
A\subseteq B
is still denoted
\psi
.
The set of mappings from
A
to
B
is denoted by
B^{A}
.
If
\varphi:A\to B
and
\psi:B\to C
are mappings then the composition
\psi\varphi:A\to C
is defined by
\psi\varphi(a)=\psi(\varphi(a))
.

By
\mathbb{N}
and
\mathbb{Z}
we denote the set of natural numbers and integers, respectively;
the set
\mathbb{N}_{+}
consists of the positive natural numbers.
For monoids
N,M
we write
N\leq M
if
N
is a submonoid of
M
, and if
M=G
is a group, then

N\trianglelefteq G
means that
N
is a normal subgroup.

Let
\Sigma
be a set, then
\Sigma^{*}
denotes the free monoid over
\Sigma
.
In this context
\Sigma
is a (possibly infinite)
alphabet
, the elements of
\Sigma
are called
letters
, and the elements of
\Sigma^{*}
are called
words
.
The empty word is denoted by
1
(as in other monoids).
If
w\in\Sigma^{*}
is a word, then
|{w}|
denotes its length and
|{w}|_{a}
denotes the number of occurrences of the letter
a\in\Sigma
within
w
so that, in particular,
|{w}|=\sum_{a\in\Sigma}|{w}|_{a}
.
If
\Gamma
is finite and
\leq
is a linear order on
\Gamma
, then every nonempty subset
L\subseteq\Gamma^{*}
has a
short-lex
first word, which is lexicographically first with respect to
\leq
among the words of minimal length in
L
.

A monoid is
finitely generated
, abbreviated
f.g.
, if there is an epimorphism of monoids
\pi:\Gamma^{*}\to M
where
\Gamma
is a finite set, and
therefore,
\pi(\Gamma)\subseteq M
is finite generating subset of
M
.

An element
x
of a monoid
M
with
x\neq 1
is called a

if there are
r,p\in\mathbb{N}
with
p\geq 1
such that
x^{r+p}=x^{r}
.
We call
M

torsion free
if it contains no such element.

For
x\in M
, we also define its set of
square roots
as
\sqrt[2]{x}=\{y\in M\mid y^{2}=x\}
and we say that
M
has the
finite square roots property

3
The Exponent of Periodicity

Throughout this section, let
\Gamma
be a finite alphabet.
We define the
exponent of periodicity
of a word
w\in\Gamma^{*}
as usual in combinatorics on words: it is the greatest integer
\exp(w)\in\mathbb{N}
such that there is a factorization

w=u\,p^{\exp(w)}\,v
with
p\neq 1
.
If
L\subseteq\Gamma^{*}
, then we also define

\displaystyle\exp(L)=\sup\{\exp(w)\mid w\in L\}\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}.

(3)

We are mainly interested in the asymptotical behavior of the exponent of periodicity.
To this end, we call a language
L\subseteq\Gamma^{*}

periodically nice
if the equivalence
\exp(L)=\infty\Leftrightarrow|L|=\infty
holds; thus a periodically nice language is either finite or has infinite exponent of periodicity.

Sometimes we also deal with
periodically perfect
languages
L\subseteq\Gamma^{*}
. These are those languages which satisfy a much stronger property:
for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
there exists an
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that
|w|\geq N
implies
\exp(w)\geq n
for all
w\in L
.
Informally, a language
L
is periodically perfect if every long word
w\in L
has high exponent of periodicity.
Theorem
12
below shows that, for regular languages, these two notions are closely related.

To extend the concept of the exponent of periodicity to arbitrary finitely generated monoids, we make use of normal forms.

Definition 7
.

Let
\pi:\Gamma^{*}\to M
be an epimorphism onto a monoid
M
.
A
normal form
(with respect to
\pi
) is a mapping
\nf:M\to\Gamma^{*}
such that
\pi(\nf(x))=x
for all
x\in M
.
We say that
\nf
is
geodetic
if the normal form
\pi(x)
of every element
x\in M
has minimal length in
{\pi}^{-1}(x)
.

The two specific types of normal forms that we are interested in
are defined next. We allow that
\nf{1}\neq 1\in\Gamma^{*}
because the
the proofs do not need
\nf{1}=1
. On the other hand, in all concrete
constructions and examples
\nf(w)
does not have a nontrivial
factor
u
with
\pi(u)=1
. This is also true for the ‘weird’ normal forms in Example
9
.

Definition 8
.

Let
\nf:M\to\Gamma^{*}
be a normal form with respect to
\pi:M\to\Gamma^{*}
.

•

The normal form
\nf
, and the pair

, are called

if the set
\nf(up^{*}v)
is periodically nice for all

. That is,
\exp(\nf(up^{*}v))=\infty

is infinite.

•

The normal form
\nf

\nf(up^{*}v)

.

Note that a perfectly admissible pair

is admissible (since we assume throughout that

Note also that using normal forms is essential for defining the exponent of periodicity for elements and sets of monoids.
For example, if a monoid

contains some subgroup

However, when using normal forms, the property whether

for some

The following example shows that it is quite easy to construct ‘weird’ normal forms which are geodetic but still not admissible.
The example uses the additive monoid

Lemma 13

Let

be an injective homomorphism for some finite alphabet

Since the homomorphism

Conversely, let

5.1

.
Having this (and recalling that

of the monoids

.

Note that the graph product

Let

be reduced traces such that

to be the set of positions in its dependence graph, and

to

A

\sigma:G[\mathcal{X}]\to G
satisfying
\sigma|_{G}={\mathrm{id}}_{G}
and
\sigma(W)=1
for every equation

.
Sometimes it can be useful to additionally constrain the values of

for each

to some subset

, where
L_{X^{-1}}=(L_{X})^{-1}

We call such a family

of subsets a

Equivalently, such a constraint can be given in the form of a homomorphism
\mu:G\to H
onto a finite group
H
and subsets
\mu_{X}\subseteq H
, where
L_{X}={\mu}^{-1}(\mu_{X})
.
Accordingly, a

of

is a solution
\sigma
of the system of equations
\mathcal{S}
which satisfies
\mu(\sigma(X))\in\mu_{X}
for all
X\in\mathcal{X}
.

Finally, we define the
(full) solution set
of a system of equations with recognizable constraints
(\mathcal{S},\mu)
as the set of all possible images of variables under solutions, that is,

\mathrm{Sol}(\mathcal{S},\mu)=\{\sigma(X)\mid\text{$\sigma$ is a solution of~$(\mathcal{S},\mu)$ and $X\in\mathcal{X}$}\}\subseteq G.

The following theorem is our main result about infinite
solution sets of quadratic systems of equations over groups and the exponent of periodicity.

Theorem 34
.

Let
G
be a finitely generated torsion-free group with the finite square roots property, and
\nf:G\to\Gamma^{*}
an admissible normal form.
Then, for every finite quadratic system of equations with recognizable constraints
(\mathcal{S},\mu)
, its solution set satisfies

|{\mathrm{Sol}(\mathcal{S},\mu)}|=\infty\text{ if and only if\ }\exp(\nf(\mathrm{Sol}(\mathcal{S},\mu)))=\infty.

Proof.

Let us call
(\mathcal{S},\mu)

nice
if
|\mathrm{Sol}(\mathcal{S},\mu)|<\infty
or
\exp(\nf(\mathrm{Sol}(\mathcal{S},\mu)))=\infty
; that is, if
\nf(\mathrm{Sol}(\mathcal{S},\mu))
is a periodically nice subset of
\Gamma^{*}
.
Thus, our goal is to prove that every quadratic system of equations with recognizable constraints is nice.

If the group
G
is finite, then the assertion is trivial.
Therefore, we assume that
G
is infinite and, hence, whenever we consider an epimorphism
\mu:G\to H
onto a finite group
H

|{\mu}^{-1}(h)|=\infty

Let

be a system of quadratic equations with recognizable constraints where, without restriction, every

Without loss of generality, we also assume that

Note that, whenever we write

Hence, it suffices to consider the case of recognizable constraints

Let us also note that, for each variable

Our goal is to construct further solutions

\exp(\nf(\sigma_{n}(X)))\geq n

{bracketenumerate}

Some variable does not occur, that is, we have

{bracketenumerate}

7.2
Baumslag-Solitar Groups

The Baumslag-Solitar groups belong to the most prominent classes of groups, and they are widely studied. They are
defined for any pair of integers
(p,q)
with
1\leq p\leq|q|
and constructed as an HNN extension of
\mathbb{Z}
by
\mathbb{Z}
as follows.
Letting
F(a,t)
be the free group of rank two with basis
\{a,t\}
, we encounter the Baumslag-Solitar group
\mathop{\mathrm{}}{BS}(p,q)
as the following quotient.

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathrm{}}{BS}(p,q)=F(a,t)/\{ta^{p}{t}^{-1}=a^{q}\}

(9)

As with all HNN extensions of a torsion-free group, the Baumslag-Solitar groups are torsion free.
We let
\Gamma=\{a,{a}^{-1},t,{t}^{-1}\}
and
\pi:\Gamma^{*}\to\mathop{\mathrm{}}{BS}(p,q)
the canonical epimorphism which is induced by the inclusion
\Gamma\subseteq\mathop{\mathrm{}}{BS}(p,q)
.
In this setting there exists a string-rewriting system (aka. semi-Thue system)
S\subseteq\Gamma\times\Gamma
for
\mathop{\mathrm{}}{BS}(p,q)
given by
the following rules.
38
38

38

For the notation and results about string rewriting system we refer to the textbook
[
3
]
. A terminating and confluent rewriting system is also called
convergent

\begin{array}[]{rcll}a^{kq+r}t&\to&a^{r}ta^{kp}&\text{for $k\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\},0\leq r<|q|$;}\\
a^{kp+r}t^{-1}&\to&a^{r}t^{-1}a^{kq}&\text{for $k\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\},0\leq r<|p|$;}\\
tt^{-1},t^{-1}t&\to&1&\text{for $y\in E$.}\end{array}

The system
S
is terminating and confluent, and
as usual, the system
S
thereby defines a normal form, which we denote by

There is another important rewriting system

called Britton reduction.
It is given by the rules

\begin{array}[]{rcll}ta^{kp}t^{-1}&\to&a^{kq}&\text{for $k\in\mathbb{Z}$;}\\

\end{array}

The system
B
is not convergent, but
it allows us to speak of cyclically Britton reduced words – that is words whose transpositions are all Britton reduced.
Moreover, it is not hard to see that every

S

is Britton reduced.
In general, every Britton reduced word

w=a^{n_{1}}t^{\varepsilon_{1}}a^{n_{2}}\cdots t^{\varepsilon_{m}}a^{n_{m+1}}

with

The sequence

Proposition 37
.

Let
\mathop{\mathrm{}}{BS}(p,q)
be a Baumslag-Solitar group and

with
w\neq 1
. Then we have
\exp(\nf_{S}(uw^{*}v))=\infty
. In other words, the normal form
\nf_{S}
is perfectly admissible.

Proof.

First note, that the normal form of
x\in\mathop{\mathrm{}}{BS}(p,q)
can be uniquely written as

\nf_{S}(x)=a^{r_{1}}t^{\varepsilon_{1}}a^{r_{2}}\cdots a^{r_{m}}t^{\varepsilon_{m}}a^{\beta(x)}

with
\beta(x)\in\mathbb{Z}
and such that
\varepsilon_{i}=-1
implies
0\leq r_{i}<p
, and
\varepsilon_{i}=1
implies
0\leq r_{i}\leq|q|
.
Having this, we can also define
\alpha(x)
by

\alpha(x)=\nf_{S}(x)a^{-\beta(x)}

The definition of
\alpha
and
\beta
is motivated by the following observation:
Given elements
u,v\in\Gamma^{*}
such that the product
uv
is Britton reduced, we observe the following relationship.

\displaystyle\nf_{S}(uv)=\alpha(u)\nf_{S}(a^{\beta(u)}v)

(10)

We start with the situation where
\nf_{S}(u)\nf_{S}(w)^{n}\nf_{S}(v)\in\Gamma^{*}
is Britton reduced for every

.
Thus, the previous observation applies.
Letting
k_{0}=\beta(u)
and
k_{i+1}=\beta(a^{k_{i}}w)

\nf_{S}(uw^{n}v)=\alpha(u)\alpha(a^{k_{0}}w)\alpha(a^{k_{1}}w)\cdots\alpha(a^{k_{n-1}}w)\alpha(a^{k_{n}}v)a^{\beta(a^{k_{n}}v)}

Thus, if the sequence numbers

Otherwise, we must have
\lim_{n\to\infty}|k_{n}|=\infty

In particular, this also implies

and hence the claim follows again.

We still have to deal with the case that

First, we find some conjugate
\tilde{w}\in\mathop{\mathrm{}}{BS}(p,q)

\nf_{S}(\tilde{w})
is cyclically Britton reduced; then, in particular,
\nf_{S}(\tilde{w})^{n}

We also set

\nf_{S}(\tilde{u}^{\prime})\nf_{S}(\tilde{w})

\nf_{S}(\tilde{w})\nf_{S}(\tilde{v}^{\prime})

We thus find that
\nf_{S}(\tilde{u}^{\prime})\nf_{S}(\tilde{w})^{n}\nf_{S}(\tilde{v}^{\prime})
is Britton reduced thus the claim holds for the language
\nf_{S}(\tilde{u}^{\prime}\tilde{w}^{*}\tilde{v}^{\prime})
by the previous considerations.
Now, the set
\nf_{S}(uw^{*}v)
is the union of this language with a finite set (which depends on

Theorem 39
.

Let
\mathop{\mathrm{}}{BS}(p,q)
be the Baumslag-Solitar group with

Then
\mathop{\mathrm{}}{BS}(p,q)

{bracketenumerate}

The sum of the parameters is nonzero: we have

.

Either
p=1
or
p
and
q
are odd (or both).

Moreover, if the finite square roots property holds, then square roots are unique and, in particular,
\sqrt[2]{1}=\{1\}

Proof.

We make a case distinction according to the parameters
1\leq p\leq|q|
.
We start with three cases where
\mathop{\mathrm{}}{BS}(p,q)
has the finite square roots property.

{bracketenumerate}

We have
p=1
and
q\neq-1
.

Then
\mathop{\mathrm{}}{BS}(1,q)
is isomorphic to the semi-direct product
\mathbb{Z}[1/q]\rtimes_{\alpha}\mathbb{Z}
where the group action of
\mathbb{Z}
on
\mathbb{Z}[1/q]
is given by
\alpha_{k}(x)=q^{k}x
.
Thus, we have
(x,k)^{2}=(y,m)
if and only if
k=m/2
and
x+q^{k}x=y
.
Since
1+q^{k}\neq 0
, we get a unique solution
x=y/(1+q^{k})\in\mathbb{Q}

\mathbb{Z}[1/q]
.

We have
p=q
and
p

Then there are well-defined epimorphisms

\begin{array}[]{ccc}\varphi:\mathop{\mathrm{}}{BS}(p,p)\to\mathbb{Z}*\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}=\langle t\rangle*\langle b\rangle:t\mapsto t,a\mapsto b&&\psi:\mathop{\mathrm{}}{BS}(p,p)\to\mathbb{Z}:t\mapsto 0,a\mapsto 1\end{array}

Of course, these induce a canonical morphism
\iota:\mathop{\mathrm{}}{BS}(p,p)\to G
, with
\varphi
and
\psi
as its components, into

.
Now, both
\mathbb{Z}

\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}

Consequently, we are done if we can show that
\iota

To this end, let
w\in\mathop{\mathrm{}}{BS}(p,p)
with

7.3
Strongly Polycyclic and Nilpotent Groups

A group
G
is
strongly polycyclic
if it possesses a subnormal series with infinite cyclic quotients.
In more detail, this means that there exist
1=G_{0}\trianglelefteq G_{1}\trianglelefteq\cdots\trianglelefteq G_{n}=G
such that
G_{i}/G_{i-1}\cong\mathbb{Z}
for all
1\leq i\leq n
.
Such groups are finitely generated, solvable, and torsion free.

Choosing elements
g_{i}\in G_{i}
for all
1\leq i\leq n
such that
g_{i}
generates
G_{i}/G_{i-1}
, we obtain a finite generating set
\Gamma=\{g_{1},\overline{g}_{1},\dotsc,g_{n},\overline{g}_{n}\}
with
\bar{g}={g}^{-1}
in
G
and, in particular, an epimorphism
\pi:\Gamma^{*}\to G
.
With respect to such a (strongly)
polycyclic generating sequence

g_{1},\dotsc,g_{n}\in G
, every
g\in G
can then be uniquely written as a product

g=g_{1}^{k_{1}}\cdot\ldots\cdot g_{n}^{k_{n}}\text{ with }k_{1},\dotsc,k_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}.

(11)

Reading the expression on right-hand side of (
11
) as a (reduced) word over
\Gamma
yields a mapping
\nf:G\to\Gamma^{*}
, which assigns to each
g\in G
its
polycylic normal form

\nf(g)\in\Gamma^{*}
.
Collectively, these normal forms constitute the regular language

\nf(G)=(g_{1}^{*}\cup\bar{g}_{1}^{*})\cdot\ldots\cdot(g_{n}^{*}\cup\bar{g}_{n}^{*})\subseteq\Gamma^{*}.

Lemma 40
.

The set of all polycyclic normal forms
\nf(G)
is periodically perfect.

Proof.

Using the above notation, every
w\in\nf(G)
has a factor
g_{i}^{m}
or
\bar{g}_{i}^{m}
with
m\geq|{w}|/n
and, therefore, we have that
\exp(w)\to\infty
as
|{w}|\to\infty
.
∎

It follows that any equation in
G
has infinitely many solutions if and only if it has solutions with arbitrarily large exponent of periodicity with respect to the pair
(\pi,\nf)
.

In general, the set of square roots
\sqrt[2]{g}=\{h\in G_{i}\mid h^{2}=g\}
need not be finite for an element
g
of a strongly polycyclic group.
For example the Baumslag-Solitar group
\mathop{\mathrm{}}{BS}(1,-1)
is strongly polycyclic, as it is the semi-direct product
\mathbb{Z}\rtimes\mathbb{Z}
where the outer copy of
\mathbb{Z}
acts by negation, and does not have the finite square roots property by Theorem
39
.
However, for an important class of strongly polycyclic groups, an even stronger property is known to hold:

Proposition 41
.

Let
G
be a finitely generated torsion-free nilpotent group.
Then:

{bracketenumerate}

The group
G
is strongly polycyclic.

The group
G
has unique roots, meaning that
g^{n}=h^{n}
for
g,h\in G
and
n\neq 0
implies
g=h
.

Proof.

The first item follows from the center
Z(G)
being a finitely generated
[
39
, 1.2.16]
torsion-free Abelian group (hence, strongly polycyclic), and the quotient
G/Z(G)
being a finitely generated torsion-free
[
39
, 2.3.9]
nilpotent group of smaller nilpotency class.

The second item follows from a similar induction: If
g^{n}=h^{n}
, then
gh^{-1}\in Z(G)
by induction and, thus,
(gh^{-1})^{n}=g^{n}h^{-n}=1
.
Since
G
is torsion free, this shows
g=h
.
∎

Nevertheless, by a result of Roman’kov
[
53
, Cor. 3.2]
, it is in general undecidable for a torsion-free nilpotent group of nilpotent of class two and higher whether an input word in a generating set represents a commutator in the group.
Since ‘being a commutator’ is clearly expressible as a quadratic equation, the satisfiability of such is also undecidable.

Remark 42
.

As a final remark in this subsection let us note that the class of strongly polycyclic groups with unique roots is strictly larger than the class of finitely generated torsion-free nilpotent groups.
To see this, consider the semi-direct product
(\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z})\rtimes\mathbb{Z}
where the outer group
\mathbb{Z}
acts on
\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}
via multiplication with the matrix
\left(\begin{smallmatrix}2&1\\
1&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)
.

\diamond

7.4
Hyperbolic Groups

Finally, let us consider another important class of finitely generated groups: the hyperbolic groups in the sense of Gromov
[
29
]
.
Ol’shanskii
[
50
]
has shown that
almost every group is hyperbolic

in the so-called few relator model for random groups.
39
39

39

In this model a random group is almost surely torsion free and
centralizers are cyclic.

Roughly speaking, a hyperbolic groups is a finitely generated group which, when viewed as a metric space with respect to the word metric, is negatively curved at large scales.
More precisely,
G
is hyperbolic if there is some constant
\delta
and a finite generating set
\Gamma={\Gamma}^{-1}\subseteq G
such that every geodesic triangle in the corresponding Cayley graph is
\delta
-thin.
For precise definitions, we refer to Bridson and Haefliger’s book
[
4
]
.

Free groups are hyperbolic, but
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
for
n\geq 2
is not.
In fact, a hyperbolic group cannot even contain any Baumslag-Solitar group as a subgroup.
This is a consequence of the following standard result on element centralizers in hyperbolic groups, which we state here for torsion-free hyperbolic groups only; for the general statement, we refer to
[
4
, Pro. III.
\Gamma
.3.9]
.

Lemma 43
.

Let
G
be a torsion-free hyperbolic group.
For every
g\in G
with
g\neq 1
, the centralizer
C_{G}(g)
is a quasi-convex subgroup of
G
and quasi-isometrically isomorphic to
\mathbb{Z}
.

An immediate consequence of Lemma
43
is the following well-known fact, which can also be proved by a reduction to free groups using so-called canonical representatives.

Lemma 44
.

Every torsion-free hyperbolic group has unique roots.

It follows from
[
27
]
and
[
31
]
that hyperbolic groups are biautomatic with respect to the full language of geodesics.
If, as in our situation, one requires unique representatives of group elements, a canonical choice is to then use short-lex normal forms instead.
The following proposition shows that these normal forms are indeed well-behaved.

Proposition 45
.

Let
G
be a torsion-free hyperbolic group with generating set
\Gamma={\Gamma}^{-1}\subseteq G

\nf_{\Gamma}:G\to\Gamma^{*}
be the associated short-lex normal forms.
Then the following hold.

{bracketenumerate}

The set of all normal forms
\nf_{\Gamma}(G)\subseteq\Gamma^{*}

The normal forms
\nf_{\Gamma}
are admissible in the sense of Definition
8

Proof.

For the first item see,
[
31
]
.
For the second item, let
u,p,v\in G
with
p\neq 1

We need to show that
\exp(\nf_{\Gamma}(up^{*}v))=\infty
.
To this extent, let us consider the set

L=\{\nf_{\Gamma}(u)(\nf_{\Gamma}(p))^{n}\nf_{\Gamma}(v)\mid n\in\mathbb{N}\}\subseteq\Gamma^{*}.

Clearly,
L\subseteq\Gamma^{*}
is a regular language.
Moreover, by Lemma

and biautomaticity, each of the words
\nf_{\Gamma}(u)(\nf_{\Gamma}(p))^{n}\nf_{\Gamma}(v)\in L

(\lambda,\varepsilon)
-quasi-geodesic representative of

Using a recent result by Carvalho and Silva

Hence, so is language

Since this language is infinite, the pumping lemma for regular languages implies that
