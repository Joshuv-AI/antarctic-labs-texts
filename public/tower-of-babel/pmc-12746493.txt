Sex differences in the efficacy, safety and persistence of tofacitinib in patients with rheumatoid arthritis: a post hoc analysis of phase III and long-term extension trials
Authors: Vibeke Strand, Hendrik Schulze-Koops, Samar Al-Emadi, Cassandra D Kinch, David Gruben, Rebecca Germino, Carol A Connell, Eduardo Mysler
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesEvaluate tofacitinib efficacy, safety and persistence by sex in rheumatoid arthritis (RA).DesignPost hoc analyses using data from phase III placebo-controlled randomised controlled trials (ORAL Scan, ORAL Sync and ORAL Standard).SettingORAL Scan, ORAL Sync and ORAL Standard were global, multicentre trials conducted across 111, 114 and 115 sites, respectively.ParticipantsThe trials enrolled adults with active RA and prior inadequate response to methotrexate (ORAL Scan/ORAL Standard) or ≥1 conventional synthetic or biologic disease-modifying antirheumatic drug (ORAL Sync). Post hoc analyses included 2265 patients (1870 female and 395 male).InterventionsPatients received tofacitinib 5 mg or 10 mg two times a day, adalimumab or placebo.Outcome measuresEfficacy outcomes to month 12 included American College of Rheumatology (ACR)20, 50 and 70 responses, Disease Activity Score in 28 joints (DAS28) (erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR))-defined low disease activity (LDA) and remission, DAS28 (C reactive protein (CRP)) ≤3.2 and <2.6 and normative thresholds for Health Assessment Questionnaire-Disability Index (HAQ-DI) and Functional Assessment of Chronic Illness Therapy-Fatigue (FACIT-F). Regression analyses included exploration of possible non-linearity in the relationship between age and treatment response in males and females. Safety was evaluated at month 24. Additionally, Kaplan-Meier survival analysis of two open-label, long-term extension studies (NCT00661661 and ORAL Sequel) assessed time to discontinuation of tofacitinib treatment.ResultsAt baseline, female patients had similar DAS28(CRP and ESR), slightly higher HAQ-DI and lower FACIT-F scores versus male patients (n=395). Sex differences were not consistently observed for HAQ-DI normative threshold ORs and, for most outcomes, when comparing responses within active treatment groups by sex. Polynomial modelling suggested age did not impact treatment response differences. Safety and persistence were similar across sexes.ConclusionsIn post hoc analyses, tofacitinib was efficacious across both sexes, with higher responses in males observed particularly for more stringent composite endpoints and patient-reported outcomes. Findings are generally consistent with studies of other advanced RA therapies. Safety and persistence were similar across sexes. Interpretation is limited by the small proportion of male patients (<20%) and baseline differences between sexes.Trial registration numberNCT00847613, NCT00856544, NCT00853385, NCT00661661 and NCT00413699.Keywords: Patient Reported Outcome Measures, Safety, THERAPEUTICS, RHEUMATOLOGY

Introduction

In patients with rheumatoid arthritis (RA), differences between sexes (male vs female) in disease activity and treatment responses have previously been reported. Multiple registry studies report greater disease activity, physical function impairment,1 2 lower remission rates2 3 and lower likelihood of attaining low disease activity (LDA)3 in females versus males. Inferior responses to conventional synthetic disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs (csDMARDs)4 and tumour necrosis factor inhibitors (TNFi) in early versus established RA5 have been observed in females versus males, although differences were not always clinically significant. In a clinical trial of first-line TNFi therapy, male sex was a positive predictor of LDA/remission in patients with RA.6 Differences in disease presentation and treatment response may be functions of gender (ie, socially constructed), sex (ie, biological) or a mixture of both.

Lack of sex/gender neutrality in the measures of disease activity and disability routinely employed in the assessment of RA may account for discrepancies between male and female patient responses.1 2 For example, Disease Activity Score in 28 joints (DAS28) tends to be higher in female versus male patients, as do core components of the American College of Rheumatology (ACR) response criteria (including swollen joint counts (SJC) and tender joint counts (TJC), erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) and Health Assessment Questionnaire-Disability Index (HAQ-DI)),1 2 which may impact ACR response rates. Additionally, variability in patient experience of disease has been previously reported. Patient-reported improvements in fatigue, pain and other aspects of health-related quality of life (Short Form (36) Health Survey (SF-36) scores) are dampened in female versus male patients receiving advanced treatments for RA.1 7 Differences in the clinical management of patients with RA may further impact sex differences in responses. Notably, higher levels of discordance between patient and physician global assessments of disease activity have been reported in female versus male rheumatology patients, particularly for functional impairment, fatigue and pain, which are likely to be higher when reported by patients compared with physicians.8 Such discordance has been shown to negatively impact medical care, adherence to therapy and disease outcomes.9

Indeed, the mechanisms responsible for sex-based differences among patients with RA are incompletely understood, and X-linked genetic differences and sex hormones may be contributing factors.10 Hormonal changes associated with menopause may affect RA aetiopathogenesis.11 Postmenopausal female patients have been shown to have worse joint destruction and physical disability than premenopausal patients,12 which may, in turn, account for differing treatment responses. Dampened responses to biologic DMARDs (bDMARDs) have been demonstrated in older (aged >65 years) versus younger patients with RA and in females versus males.13 14 A trend towards a reduced response to tofacitinib in older (aged ≥65 years) females versus males was observed in a post hoc analysis of clinical trials;15 however, it is unclear whether this may be accounted for by differences in physiological changes, such as hormonal shifts associated with menopause or other genetic/environmental/social factors. Epigenetic changes, which represent the confluence of gene and environment interaction, accumulate with age and the resultant impact on the immune system, and in turn on the host response to the microbiome, may play an important role in RA pathogenesis and sex-related differences.10 16

Tofacitinib is an oral Janus kinase (JAK) inhibitor for the treatment of RA. An Italian real-world multicentre analysis evaluated sex differences in patients’ responses to tofacitinib and another JAK inhibitor, baricitinib.17 Although improvements in patient-reported pain (pain; Visual Analogue Scale (VAS)) were significantly greater in males versus females, overall, there were no differences in TJC and SJC, C reactive protein (CRP) levels or DAS28-defined remission or LDA between sexes. Here, we evaluated the efficacy and safety of tofacitinib in patients with RA stratified by sex in a post hoc analysis of placebo-controlled randomised controlled trials (RCTs). We also assessed potential associations between age and treatment response over time in males and females to understand if physiological shifts or other factors associated with age account for any separation of response between males and females.

Methods

Study design and patients

In this post hoc analysis of pooled data from three phase III placebo-controlled RCTs (ORAL Scan (NCT00847613),18 ORAL Sync (NCT00856544)19 and ORAL Standard (NCT00853385)),20 we evaluated the impact of sex on tofacitinib clinical responses, including patient-reported outcomes (PROs), safety and persistence in patients with an inadequate response (IR) to DMARDs.

Briefly, ORAL Scan, ORAL Sync and ORAL Standard were global, multicentre trials conducted across 111, 114 and 115 sites, respectively. Eligible patients were aged ≥18 years with a diagnosis of active RA, defined by ACR 1987 Revised Criteria; active disease classified as ≥6 tender/swollen joints (≥4 in ORAL Sync) and ESR >28 mm/hour (Westergren method) or CRP level >7 mg/L. Patients had prior IR to methotrexate (MTX; ORAL Scan/ORAL Standard) or at least one csDMARD or bDMARD (ORAL Sync).

Patients received tofacitinib 5 mg or 10 mg two times a day, adalimumab 40 mg once every 2 weeks (ORAL Standard only as an active control) or placebo; all treatments were administered with csDMARDs (MTX in ORAL Scan/ORAL Standard). Placebo-treated patients were blindly advanced to receive tofacitinib 5 mg or 10 mg two times a day at month 3 (non-responders) or month 6 (all remaining patients).

Persistence analyses included pooled data from two long-term extension (LTE) studies (NCT0066166121 and ORAL Sequel (NCT00413699))22 23 where patients received open-label tofacitinib 5 mg or 10 mg two times a day as monotherapy or with background csDMARDs. Patients were categorised as having received an average tofacitinib dose of 5 mg or 10 mg two times a day based on their average total daily dose over the course of observation (<15 or ≥15 mg, respectively).

Patient and public involvement

Patients and/or the public were not involved in the design, or conduct, or reporting or dissemination plans of this research.

Clinical efficacy

Outcomes at months 1, 3, 6 and 12 included ACR ≥20/50/70% response criteria (ACR20/50/70 response rates), DAS28(CRP) ≤3.2 and <2.6, DAS28(ESR)-defined LDA (≤3.2) and remission (<2.6), Clinical Disease Activity Index (CDAI)-defined LDA (≤10) and remission (≤2.8)24 and changes from baseline in DAS28(CRP), DAS28(ESR), CDAI, TJC (28-joint count), SJC (28-joint count), Physician Global Assessment of arthritis, CRP and ESR.

Patient-reported outcomes

PROs assessed at months 1, 3, 6 and 12 included changes from baseline in Patient Global Assessment of disease activity (PtGA), Pain (VAS 0–100 mm), HAQ-DI, Functional Assessment of Chronic Illness Therapy-Fatigue (FACIT-F), and SF-36 physical (PCS) and mental (MCS) component summary scores and eight domain scores. Also assessed at the same timepoints were the proportions of patients reporting moderate (≥30%) or substantial (≥50%) improvements in Pain (VAS) scores and improvements in Pain ≥minimum clinically important differences (MCIDs; ≥10 (VAS 0–100 mm)).25

The proportions of patients with improvements ≥MCID in HAQ-DI scores (≥0.22),26 and scores ≥normative values (normative threshold: <0.25),27 improvements ≥MCID in FACIT-F scores (increase from baseline of ≥4.0)28 and scores ≥normative values (normative threshold: ≥43.5)29 were also reported at the same timepoints. Improvements ≥MCID in SF-36 PCS and MCS scores (≥2.5 for PCS and MCS),25 and scores ≥normative values (normative threshold: based on age- and sex-matched norms) were reported at month 3.

Safety

Safety was assessed through month 24 via the incidence of treatment-emergent adverse events (TEAEs), serious adverse events (SAEs), discontinuations due to adverse events (AEs), deaths and AEs of special interest (AESI), which included serious infection events, herpes zoster (HZ; including non-serious and serious events), opportunistic infections (OIs; excluding tuberculosis), malignancies excluding non-melanoma skin cancer (NMSC), NMSC, major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) and venous thromboembolism (VTE) (refer to the online supplemental file 1 for the full list).

Persistence

Treatment persistence was assessed via three time-to-event outcomes: time to all-cause discontinuation, time to discontinuation due to AEs and time to discontinuation due to lack of efficacy. The proportions of patients remaining on tofacitinib (ie, drug continuation rates) were also calculated at 2 years and 5 years.

Statistical analyses

Binary endpoints were assessed using logistic regression at each visit; this approach was taken to keep the analyses as similar as possible to those of the individual RCTs, specifically by-visit analyses, using logistic regression to add independent variables needed to produce the estimates of rates for female versus male patients. Continuous endpoints were assessed using a repeated-measures linear model. Independent variables included in the model (fixed effects) were geographic region, RCT, sex, treatment group (and sex:treatment group interaction), and, for the longitudinal model only, baseline value of the outcome of interest and study visit (and all-way interactions between sex, treatment group and study visit). Also, for the repeated measures model, a covariance matrix was specified using a compound-symmetry form. If convergence failed, the form applied was autoregressive (first order). The TJC, Physician Global Assessment of arthritis, SF-36 PCS and SF-36 vitality domain score used an autoregressive covariance. There was no imputation for missing data. Comparisons between treatment groups or between sexes were assessed as ORs for binary endpoints and mean differences for continuous endpoints, with 95% CIs. Two sets of model-based comparisons were performed to assess differences between treatment groups and sexes. The figures in this report present ORs and mean differences for active treatments versus placebo, within each sex. Except for SF-36 (month 3 only), these analyses were performed at the timepoints with placebo data available, that is, months 1, 3 and 6. ORs and mean differences for female versus male within each treatment group were also produced; results for these comparisons are provided in online supplemental file 1. Except for SF-36 (month 3 only), these analyses were performed at months 1, 3, 6 and, for active treatments only, month 12. Two-sided p values were generated for each comparison; these were not corrected for multiple comparisons and should be considered as descriptive and not hypothesis-testing.

For illustrative purposes and to facilitate the viewing of improvements at month 3 in SF-36 simultaneously across the eight domains, spydergrams were generated. These are plots of the mean values of the SF-36 domain scores (range: 0–100) at month 3, and at baseline, where at baseline the treatment group means were combined (a composite baseline), and the US population norms reweighted for the age-sex distribution of the data are also plotted for reference.

On account of previously cited hypotheses regarding higher disease activity and treatment resistance in female patients with RA experiencing perihormonal shifts (such as menopause),2 30 regression models were expanded to include increasing order polynomial terms in age to explore possible non-linearity in the relationship of age with efficacy response to tofacitinib. Menopause status was not robustly collected in the phase III RCTs, so patient age was used as a proxy measure. Further details regarding this analysis are in the online supplemental file 1.

Safety data are summarised descriptively.

Kaplan‐Meier survival curves were used to compare treatment persistence (difference between the treatment discontinuation and/or end‐of‐study date and the first tofacitinib dose date +1 day) for each of the doses of tofacitinib (average 5 mg two times a day, average 10 mg two times a day) in the LTE studies. Ongoing patients were censored at the LTE study cut‐off dates (final data cut; March 2017), and completers were censored at the end‐of‐study date. In addition, for Kaplan-Meier analyses of discontinuation due to AEs or lack of efficacy, discontinuations due to other reasons were censored. For each analysis, 2‐ and 5‐year continuation (survival) rates were derived, with 100% of the study population considered, as all patients entered the LTE studies.

Results

Patients

Overall, 2265 patients were included in the efficacy analyses; of these, 1870 (82.6%) were female and 395 (17.4%) were male. Demographics and baseline characteristics were generally comparable across sexes and treatment groups (online supplemental table S1). Some variability between males and females was observed, with females tending to have longer disease duration, lower CRP levels, slightly higher Pain (VAS) and HAQ-DI scores, lower FACIT-F scores and lower rates of prior TNFi use (online supplemental table S1).

Clinical efficacy

Through month 6, the proportions of patients with ACR20/50/70 responses were generally numerically higher (ORs >1) with tofacitinib or adalimumab versus placebo in both sexes, though these trends were generally more pronounced in males versus females (figure 1, online supplemental figure S1). Comparing ACR20/50/70 responses within each treatment group, a higher proportion of males than females had ACR20/50 responses at months 3 and 6 with tofacitinib 5 mg two times a day, with similar trends observed at some timepoints for tofacitinib 10 mg two times a day and adalimumab (online supplemental figure S1).

For the changes from baseline within each treatment group, greater improvements from baseline in DAS28(CRP), DAS28(ESR) and CDAI were observed in males versus females at most timepoints with tofacitinib 5 mg two times a day; generally similar trends were evident with the other active treatments (online supplemental figure S4). Changes from baseline in clinical assessments (TJC, SJC, Physician Global Assessment of disease activity, CRP and ESR) were generally higher with active treatment versus placebo across timepoints and generally similar between sexes, with some trends noted in favour of males, especially with tofacitinib 5 mg two times a day (online supplemental table S2).

Patient-reported outcomes

Mean changes from baseline in selected PROs, including PtGA, Pain, HAQ-DI, FACIT-F and SF-36 PCS and MCS scores, were greater with active treatments compared with placebo and generally similar between sexes or slightly larger in males compared with females (online supplemental table S3).

For HAQ-DI and FACIT-F, the proportions of patients reporting improvements from baseline ≥MCID or scores ≥normative values were generally greater (ORs >1) with tofacitinib or adalimumab versus placebo (online supplemental figure S6A,B and figure 3A,B). ORs for active treatments versus placebo were generally numerically larger in males than females for FACIT-F outcomes (online supplemental figure S6B and figure 3B). Within each active treatment group, there were generally no differences between the proportions of males versus females reporting HAQ-DI and FACIT-F improvements from baseline ≥MCID (online supplemental figures S7A and S8A,B), while a higher proportion of males versus females reported HAQ-DI and FACIT-F scores ≥normative values with both tofacitinib doses and adalimumab (online supplemental figures S7B and S8C,D).

Impact of age on response to tofacitinib

To explore whether differences in physiological processes associated with age between male and female patients with RA have an impact on responses to tofacitinib, a series of statistical models was fit to the clinical response data across the age spectrum for each treatment group. Following assessment of goodness of fit for the set of polynomial models of mean change from baseline in DAS28(ESR) and DAS28(CRP), a consistent trend in treatment response based on age was not identified in males or females, suggesting that sex-dependent factors across the age spectrum are unlikely to explain differences in response to treatment for these outcomes in this clinical dataset (online supplemental table S4). The preferred models by timepoint and sex are in online supplemental figure S12.

Safety

Rates of TEAEs were numerically higher in females versus males receiving tofacitinib 5 mg two times a day, and generally similar between sexes in patients receiving tofacitinib 10 mg two times a day and adalimumab (table 1). By month 24, SAEs were reported in 107 (15.1%), 71 (10.2%) and 13 (8%) females receiving tofacitinib 5 mg two times a day, tofacitinib 10 mg two times a day and adalimumab, respectively, and in 17 (12.8%), 24 (17.5%) and six (14.3%) males (table 1). A total of 87 (12.3%) and 10 (7.5%), 88 (12.6%) and 10 (7.3%) and 17 (10.5%) and five (11.9%) females and males receiving tofacitinib 5 mg two times a day, tofacitinib 10 mg two times a day and adalimumab, respectively, discontinued due to AEs (table 1). Ten deaths occurred, six females (0.8%) and four males (3%) with tofacitinib 5 mg two times a day; three deaths, all male (2.2%), occurred with tofacitinib 10 mg two times a day; and three deaths, one female (0.6%) and two males (4.8%), occurred with adalimumab.

AESIs were similar between sexes with both tofacitinib doses and adalimumab (table 1), although patient numbers were comparatively lower in the adalimumab group. By month 24, serious infections were reported in 28 (4%), 27 (3.9%) and two (1.2%) females receiving tofacitinib 5 mg two times a day, tofacitinib 10 mg two times a day and adalimumab, respectively, and in six (4.5%), six (4.4%) and one (2.4%) males, correspondingly. OIs were reported in three (0.4%) females receiving tofacitinib 5 mg two times a day (pneumonia, HZ disseminated and oesophageal candidiasis) and five (0.7%) females receiving tofacitinib 10 mg two times a day (sialadenitis, cytomegalovirus viraemia, cryptococcal pneumonia, oesophageal candidiasis and HZ disseminated). Among the 35 (5%) HZ cases reported in females receiving tofacitinib 5 mg two times a day and 43 (6.2%) HZ cases reported in females receiving tofacitinib 10 mg two times a day, four cases in each group were serious (one case in the tofacitinib 5 mg two times a day group was disseminated). No serious cases of HZ were reported in males receiving tofacitinib (HZ reported in seven (5.3%) and five (3.6%) patients with 5 and 10 mg two times a day, respectively) or in any patients receiving adalimumab (HZ reported in two (1.2%) and three (7.1%) females and males, respectively). MACE was reported in five (0.7%) patients receiving tofacitinib 5 mg two times a day (all female; acute myocardial infarction (MI), cardiac arrest, cardiac failure, cerebrovascular accident and MI), five patients receiving tofacitinib 10 mg two times a day (two (0.3%) females (infarction and MI) and three (2.2%) males (cardiac failure acute, lacunar infarction and MI)) and in three (7.1%) males receiving adalimumab (acute MI, cardiac arrest and MI). Malignancies excluding NMSC were reported in seven females (1%) and one male (0.8%) receiving tofacitinib 5 mg two times a day, nine females (1.3%) and one male (0.7%) receiving tofacitinib 10 mg two times a day and one male (2.4%) receiving adalimumab (further details provided in online supplemental table S5). NMSC events were reported in two females (0.3%) and five males (3.8%) receiving tofacitinib 5 mg two times a day, four females (0.6%) and two males (1.5%) receiving tofacitinib 10 mg two times a day and one female (0.6%) and one male (2.4%) receiving adalimumab. Two (0.3%) females receiving tofacitinib 10 mg two times a day had lymphoma events. Three (0.4%) females receiving tofacitinib 5 mg two times a day had VTE events (pulmonary embolism (two cases) and venous thrombosis of the limb) and no males had VTE events. In the tofacitinib 10 mg two times a day group, VTE events were reported in three (0.4%) females (deep vein thrombosis, pulmonary embolism and venous thrombosis limb) and one (0.7%) male (pulmonary embolism) (table 1).

Discussion

In this post hoc analysis evaluating the impact of sex on tofacitinib efficacy and safety in patients with RA, tofacitinib and adalimumab were efficacious in both sexes and had similar safety outcomes, and persistence was similar between sexes for tofacitinib. Overall, greater responses or improvements were observed with active treatments versus placebo, and these treatment differences were generally more pronounced in males versus females. Responses or improvements from baseline within each active treatment group were broadly comparable or tended to favour males, particularly for more stringent composite endpoints (eg, DAS28(ESR)) and selected normative PRO thresholds (eg, physical function and fatigue). These findings may be influenced by the imbalance in sample size between females and males (>4:1) in our study and baseline differences observed between sexes, with females having, on average, longer disease duration, lower CRP levels, slightly higher Pain (VAS) and HAQ-DI scores, lower FACIT-F scores and lower rates of prior TNFi use. However, gender-based differences in sociocultural factors, adherence and reporting biases may also have contributed to divergences in the outcomes reported here.31,33

Across clinical efficacy outcomes, including ACR20/50/70 response rates, DAS28 and CDAI, trends varied between active treatment groups, but differences between sexes in response rates were generally more common with tofacitinib 5 mg two times a day versus tofacitinib 10 mg two times a day and adalimumab. The fact that fewer sex-dependent differences in efficacy were observed for adalimumab likely reflects the limited sample size of this group, although a mechanistic difference between the two treatments cannot be ruled out. For outcomes associated with DAS28(ESR) in particular, we consistently observed differences between sexes across active treatment groups, such that higher proportions of males versus females showed improvement from baseline in DAS28(ESR) and reported DAS28(ESR)-defined LDA and remission. Sex-based differences in disease activity and inflammatory markers (ESR and CRP) are well substantiated in the RA literature1 2 34 and have been previously observed with tofacitinib treatment in patients with psoriatic arthritis. A post hoc analysis of two tofacitinib RCTs in psoriatic arthritis showed generally similar efficacy between sexes with the exception of minimal disease activity, a treatment target that male patients were more likely to achieve.35 In the present study, numerically larger decreases from baseline in ESR were reported in males versus females across treatment groups, aligned with the greater improvements in DAS28(ESR) outcomes in males.

To evaluate treatment efficacy in this placebo-controlled RCT cohort, we performed two sets of model-based comparisons to assess rates of response or improvement: males versus females, within each treatment group, and active treatments versus placebo, within each sex. There are conflicting data in the literature regarding potential sex differences in placebo responses. A literature review concluded that males responded more strongly to placebo treatment across 18 studies,36 while a review of meta-analyses reported that 3 out of 75 found a stronger placebo response in females, while others reported no significant sex difference,37 suggesting that effects may differ across outcomes or populations assessed. In our study, numerical trends in some endpoints showed larger responses in females than males receiving placebo, although the sample size of this treatment group was small. These differences in placebo responses appear to have contributed to the fact that sex differences were more evident when active treatments were compared with placebo, though generally similar findings were observed in both sets of model-based comparisons.

Efficacy observed in tofacitinib-treated females (ACR20/50/70 response rates of 66%/40%/18% at month 6 for females receiving tofacitinib 5 mg two times a day) generally aligned with previous findings with advanced therapies (eg, adalimumab, etanercept, rituximab and abatacept) in mixed populations.38 39 In contrast, response rates for selected ACR20/50/70 outcomes were comparatively higher in tofacitinib-treated males than females (eg, 79%/53%/25% at month 6 in patients receiving tofacitinib 5 mg two times a day). While these findings may be influenced by the imbalance in sample size between sexes, with males representing only 17.4% of the population, the impact of sex on the markers of disease activity, rather than on disease activity itself, has been mentioned previously. These effects may be more likely to be observed within a tightly controlled RCT,1 2 where any potential gender bias in treatment or clinical management is mitigated. Intersex differences in PROs, including the impact of physical strength on patient-perceived functionality and reporting/self-assessment of fatigue and health-related quality of life, should also be considered when interpreting these findings.

The impact of tofacitinib on PROs from these phase III RCTs has been previously reported.40,42 In contrast with real-world findings in treated patients with RA,1 2 17 only slight sex differences were observed in changes from baseline in Pain scores with active treatments. This difference may be reflective of the heterogeneity of real-world populations, compared with the stringently controlled RCT populations. The proportions of patients reporting moderate or substantial improvements in pain did not consistently differ between sexes in the active treatment groups, while the ORs for active treatments versus placebo generally favoured males, which may reflect the numerically greater proportions of females versus males reporting these improvements in the placebo group.

Consistent with real-world data,43 females in our study reported slightly greater levels of physical impairment at baseline than males. It follows, and has been widely reported, that males experience greater changes from baseline than females in physical function1 and fatigue.44 When looking at minimal thresholds of improvement (≥MCID), males and females appear to improve to a similar degree. However, in this analysis, patients who reported substantial improvements in physical function and fatigue, for example, scores ≥normative values following tofacitinib treatment, were predominantly male. It is possible that differences in the perception of fatigue may partly account for between-sex variations, but the lack of objective measures precludes further investigation into this. Normative values can be considered a high threshold for measuring response to treatment, and, in contrast to HAQ-DI, it is interesting to note that only numerical differences were observed between sexes in the SF-36 physical functioning domain. This likely relates to variations in the information evaluated by these scales, the SF-36 focusing on physical function (eg, mobility activities such as walking specified distances and carrying groceries) and the HAQ-DI on the ability to perform activities of daily living (eg, dressing and eating). This further suggests that sex differences may be partly embedded in the measure of disease activity or PRO used, rather than actual differences in disease activity per se. Additionally, the presence of comorbid disease, for example, fibromyalgia, which is known to be more prevalent among female than male patients with RA, has been associated with worse scores on the SF-36 and HAQ-DI.45 Collectively, our findings suggest that it may be appropriate to redefine normative thresholds, MCIDs and target cut-points based on sex to allow more meaningful comparisons between sexes in terms of response to RA treatment.

The average age of RA onset in women is lower than in men and often coincides with the timing of hormonal shifts associated with the onset of menopause.11 30 46 Given that RA joint destruction and associated physical disability may be negatively impacted by menopause,12 we examined the effects of age, which would capture typified periods of physiological/hormonal shifts during perimenopause, on efficacy outcomes following tofacitinib treatment. Polynomial modelling rejected the hypothesis that the response to tofacitinib (assessed via mean changes from baseline in DAS28(ESR) and DAS28(CRP)) declines more sharply in females versus males as a result of advancing age. These findings suggest that physiological processes or other factors (eg, social factors) associated with age may not play a major role in the trend towards a reduced response observed for older females (aged ≥65 years) versus males receiving tofacitinib as reported previously.15 Further research is needed using direct or quantitative measures to comprehensively explore the role of hormonal shifts in response to tofacitinib. Importantly, the impact of age on tolerability, treatment adherence and ultimately treatment response should be considered, particularly as a previous study showed higher rates of SAEs among females versus males in older adults (aged ≥65 years) receiving tofacitinib.15

Overall, safety and persistence data did not reveal a consistent pattern between males and females for tofacitinib and adalimumab through 24 months. Although females receiving tofacitinib 5 mg two times a day were more likely to report TEAEs and SAEs and discontinue treatment due to AEs, rates of treatment persistence did not show separation between sexes at this dose, and this is in contrast to the lower retention rates in females versus males taking bDMARDs reported in the literature.47 It is important to acknowledge that in the current setting, persistence data for both sexes were derived from LTE studies in patients selected from index studies based on their positive response to tofacitinib treatment and who, therefore, may have been inclined to continue treatment. Although we did not observe any difference between females and males in terms of discontinuation of the index trials, it is a limitation of the analyses that we did not account for a potential survivorship bias. Moreover, by taking part in the LTE studies, patients did not pay for their treatment, which may encourage greater persistence compared with real-world use in some countries. The higher rates of TEAEs in females treated with tofacitinib 5 mg two times a day observed here align with findings from the MetaGeM project, which examines sex-based differences in drug safety across different diseases.48 For AESI, event numbers were too low to draw conclusions; however, no obvious trend in the type of AESI was observed between sexes. Differences between sexes in the absolute risk of certain AESIs have previously been noted for RA therapies, including in the prospective ORAL Surveillance safety trial (NCT02092467), which randomised high CV-risk patients with RA to tofacitinib or TNFi treatment.49 In ORAL Surveillance, male sex was an independent risk factor for MACE and female sex was an independent risk factor for HZ across tofacitinib and TNFi treatment groups,50 51 consistent with trends in the general population.52 53

Limitations of this study include the post hoc nature and that studies were not designed to make comparisons between sexes. Additionally, the adalimumab dataset derives solely from ORAL Standard, which was not powered to make direct comparisons between tofacitinib and adalimumab. Furthermore, as most enrolled patients were female, there was a marked imbalance in sample sizes between the sexes. As a result, following stratification by sex, the sample sizes of male patients for some treatment groups were small, limiting interpretation. However, in line with our findings, RA patient data from the Veterans Affairs Rheumatoid Arthritis (VARA) registry and clinical trials show that sex differences in LDA and remission rates vary substantially across different measures of LDA/remission,3 and in contrast to our analysis, most patients in the VARA registry were male (89.8%). Their findings, therefore, substantiate the trends observed in our analysis and demonstrate that sex differences in response to RA treatment were likely not confounded by the low male sample size. In our analyses of associations between age and treatment response over time in males and females, age (in years) was a surrogate measure of menopausal status in female patients, as such data were not collected in these RCTs. As factors beyond menopause (eg, social or environmental factors) could have an influence on the treatment response with increasing age, the results of these analyses do not allow us to draw conclusions regarding the impact of hormonal influence on response to tofacitinib. A strength of this analysis is that the use of RCT data eliminates potential treatment selection and channelling biases associated with observational datasets. Females and males are likely to be clinically managed in a similar fashion in RCTs, which may explain differences compared with real-world studies.

Conclusion

Overall, the results of this post hoc analysis demonstrate that tofacitinib is efficacious in males and females, with similar safety outcomes and persistence. Greater responses and magnitudes of improvement for active treatments versus placebo were observed in males than in females for some endpoints, especially more stringent composite measures of disease activity and normative thresholds for selected PROs, including physical function and fatigue. Importantly, these findings may be influenced by the small proportion of male patients (<20% of the study population) and baseline differences in PROs across sexes, with higher HAQ-DI scores and lower FACIT-F scores observed in female versus male patients. These data may assist the clinician in determining sex-specific treatment targets for patients and may support future definition of sex-appropriate MCIDs. Ultimately, further studies are needed to understand the mechanisms underlying sex differences in response to RA treatment, with the purpose of achieving a more personalised and equitable management approach across men and women with RA.
