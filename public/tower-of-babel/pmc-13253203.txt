Comprehensive evaluation of tick infestation dynamics and host response in Kankrej and Holstein Friesian crossbred cattle
Authors: Ashok K Chaudhary, Jay Prakash Gupta, Jagdish D Chaudhari, Arth B Chaudhari, Shyma K․P․, Samir Raval, Sushil K Mohapatra, Dhara D Gor
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTicks and tick-borne diseases present significant challenges to cattle production. In this context, natural host resistance to ticks emerges as a promising and sustainable alternative. The present study aimed to clarify the phenotypic, immunological, and genetic factors contributing to the superior natural tick resistance observed in indigenous Kankrej cattle compared with Holstein Friesian (HF) crossbred cattle, managed under the same field conditions. We evaluated 116 naturally infested cattle (27 Kankrej and 89 HF crossbreds) from Gujarat, India, and assessed their tick burdens across anatomical sites, seasons, and breeds. The host’s cutaneous immune response was investigated through histopathological examination of skin biopsies from tick attachment sites, with a particular focus on mast cell infiltration. Four immune-related candidate gene SNPs were also analysed for their association with tick resistance, marking the first such evaluation in Kankrej cattle. Our findings revealed that HF crossbred cattle harboured significantly higher tick loads than Kankrej cattle, with infestation patterns strongly affected by anatomical location and seasonality. Histopathological analysis showed pronounced mast cell infiltration at tick bite sites and a higher baseline mast cell count in Kankrej cattle than HF crossbred, suggesting a breed-specific, innate cutaneous immune response. This study offers the first comprehensive characterisation of tick resistance in Kankrej cattle and highlights the value of indigenous breeds in developing sustainable tick control strategies.Keywords: Kankrej, Tick count, PCR-RFLP, Resistance, Skin biopsy

Introduction

Ectoparasite infestations represent a major obstacle to sustainable livestock production, resulting in significant economic losses, particularly in tropical and subtropical areas (Waldman et al., 2023). Ticks and tick-borne diseases represent a major global challenge to livestock health and productivity, causing substantial economic losses estimated at US$20–30 billion annually worldwide (Lew-Tabor & Rodriguez Valle, 2016; Strydom et al., 2023). In India specifically, these losses are estimated at approximately US$787.63 million per year (Singh et al., 2022).

The recent rise in tick-borne diseases globally has been attributed to factors such as climate change, land-use changes, expanding host populations, and increased outdoor activities (Beltrame et al., 2018; Diuk-Wasser et al., 2021; Ogden et al., 2021). Ticks show a marked preference for certain body regions, influenced by skin thickness, hair density, local microclimate, and ease of access for feeding (De Castro et al., 1991; Muchenje et al., 2008; Sajid et al., 2007). Gaining insight into tick phenology and their favoured attachment sites is essential for designing effective control measures, as it facilitates targeted acaricide application, improves treatment coverage of high-risk anatomical regions, and enhances early detection and monitoring of tick infestations (Bedouhene et al., 2022; Gharbi & Darghouth, 2014).

While a range of tick control strategies have been implemented to reduce the bovine tick burden, chemical acaricides have historically and continue to play a central role. Nevertheless, no single approach has achieved complete eradication of ticks (Mkize et al., 2021). Frequent or improper use of acaricides has led to the emergence of drug-resistant tick populations, diminishing the efficacy of conventional treatments over time (Obaid et al., 2022). These limitations emphasise the need for alternative tick control solutions that are both effective and economically viable for breeders (Aboagye et al., 2023; Waldman et al., 2023).

Host resistance to ticks is considered a low to moderately heritable trait (0.13–0.44), making it a sustainable and cost-effective approach for tick control that requires minimal additional labor or resource input (Shyma et al., 2015a). Traditionally, animal improvement programs have relied solely on phenotypic data, which can be limiting for traits like tick resistance that are challenging and expensive to measure. Natural resistance to bovine tick species involves an animal’s innate ability to limit tick survival, primarily through immune mechanisms (Utech et al., 1978). The genetic basis for differences in host response to tick infestation, both within and between breeds, has been acknowledged for decades (Cardoso et al., 2015; Francis, 1966; Wilkinson, 1955). Selecting animals with genetic resistance offers a long-term, sustainable solution to reducing costs associated with parasite control (Marima et al., 2020).

Bos taurus cattle are known for their high productivity but are also highly susceptible to tick infestations, which limits their suitability for tropical agro-ecosystems dominated by smallholder farmers. In contrast, zebu cattle (Bos indicus) possess natural tolerance to ectoparasites, although their productivity is generally lower (Mkize et al., 2021). The Kankrej, an indigenous zebu breed from the arid and semi-arid regions of Gujarat and Rajasthan, is well recognized for its resistance to ticks and other infectious diseases compared to both pure exotic breeds and HF crossbreds (Sodhi et al., 2006). Their adaptation to harsh climatic conditions makes Kankrej cattle particularly well-suited to their native environments. These observations suggest that genetic improvement for tick resistance is achievable through introgression of candidate genes by using crossbreeding and artificial selection (Shyma et al., 2015a). Identifying genetic variants linked to tick resistance offers opportunities for further progress via genomic selection.

In most host species, the cellular mechanisms that drive both innate and adaptive skin immune responses to tick infestation remain largely unknown or completely unexplored (Karasuyama et al., 2020; Ohta et al., 2017). The skin is the primary site of interaction between ticks and their hosts, and the host’s immune response is a critical factor in determining susceptibility or resistance (Kim et al., 2016; Tirloni et al., 2016). Among immune cells, mast cells are particularly important in initiating and regulating early inflammatory responses to ectoparasites (Tabakawa et al., 2018; Robbertse et al., 2017). By releasing histamine and other pro-inflammatory mediators, mast cells induce localised tissue remodelling at tick attachment sites (Brossard & Wikel, 2004; Steeves & Allen, 1991). Multiple studies have consistently demonstrated that Bos indicus cattle exhibit markedly greater resistance to ectoparasitic infestations compared with Bos taurus breeds, highlighting their superior innate adaptability and resilience under parasite-challenged tropical production systems (Burrow et al., 2019; Ibelli et al., 2012; Muchenje et al., 2008).

The present study was designed to comprehensively investigate tick resistance in cattle by integrating field-based phenotypic observations with immunological and genetic analyses. It evaluated breed-associated differences in tick burden across body regions, seasons, and villages, characterised host cutaneous immune responses at tick attachment sites, and explored polymorphisms in candidate genes potentially associated with resistance.

Materials and methods

Study sites

The study was conducted during 2021–2023 across 11 villages located in the Dantiwada (24.2775° N, 72.2614° E) and Deesa (24.2585° N, 72.1907° E) talukas of Banaskantha district, Gujarat, India. The region is characterized by a semi-arid climate, receiving an average annual rainfall of 578.8 mm, with a mean annual temperature of approximately 26.97°C. Seasonal temperatures range from a maximum of 41°C during May to a minimum of 9.8°C in January. Banaskantha district forms part of the North Gujarat region, which lies within the Gujarat plains and hills agro-climatic zone of the Indian subcontinent, where Rhipicephalus microplus is reported as the predominant tick species infesting cattle (Shyma et al., 2015b). The region is predominantly characterized by semi-arid vegetation comprising sparse grasslands, scrub vegetation, agricultural croplands, and seasonal pasture areas, which may influence local tick ecology, host exposure, and infestation dynamics under field conditions. Banaskantha is one of India’s major milk-producing districts and is home to Banas Dairy, Asia’s largest dairy cooperative, reflecting the region’s high dairy cattle density. The semi-arid climatic conditions, along with the predominance of Holstein Friesian (HF) crossbred cattle, likely favour the survival and persistence of tick populations in the study area.

Experimental animals

A total of 116 naturally tick-infested cattle were randomly selected from herds maintained by smallholder farmers across the study area. The sampled population comprised 27 Kankrej cattle and 89 Holstein Friesian (HF) crossbreds distributed across 11 villages, namely Akedi, Bhakhar, Chodungri, Gangapura, Lodpa, Nandotra, Nilpur, Shikariya, Sodapur, Vaghrol, and Velavas. The unequal distribution between breeds was primarily influenced by ethical considerations, farmer consent, field accessibility, and the predominance of HF crossbred cattle in the region. In accordance with animal ethics guidelines, the study was conducted exclusively under field conditions using naturally tick-infested animals, without any artificial induction of infestation. Ear-tagged cattle were specifically selected to ensure accurate animal identification, repeated tick counting at multiple time points, and reliable longitudinal sample collection throughout the study period.

Estimation of tick counts

The tick counting procedure was undertaken to identify preferred anatomical attachment sites of ticks, compare tick burdens between Kankrej and Holstein Friesian (HF) crossbred cattle, and evaluate seasonal variations in tick infestation. Since host resistance is influenced by several host-related factors, including age, sex, and lactation stage, animals exhibiting maximum possible uniformity for these parameters were selected to minimise confounding effects and improve the accuracy of resistance assessment, following the approach of Utech et al. (1978).

Field visits were conducted between March 2021 and February 2022 to perform half-body tick counts under natural field conditions. The study aimed to assess the distribution and intensity of tick infestation with particular emphasis on variations associated with breed, village, season, and anatomical regions of the host body. To ensure systematic evaluation and reduce counting errors, the body of each animal was divided into fifteen anatomical regions, namely the ear, head, neck, dewlap, hump, cranial limb, thorax, flank, abdomen, navel, rump, caudal limb, udder, crotch, and tail, as described by Neves and Vidotto (2018). This detailed anatomical classification enabled precise identification of major tick predilection sites in cattle.

A total of three tick counts were performed to capture seasonal fluctuations in tick burden: the first during summer (April-May 2021), the second during the monsoon season (July-August 2021), and the third during winter (December 2021-January 2022). Tick counting was carried out following the half-body counting procedure described by Wharton and Utech (1970), in which fully or partially engorged ticks present on one side of the animal body were counted as a representative estimate of infestation intensity. Species-wise differentiation of ticks was not performed during field counting, as the study primarily focused on overall tick burden and host resistance under natural field conditions. However, pooled tick samples collected from the study animals and examined in the laboratory were morphologically identified as Rhipicephalus microplus, which is also reported as the predominant cattle tick species in the North Gujarat region (Shyma et al., 2015b).

After each counting session, cattle were treated with a commercial formulation of deltamethrin 1.25% (Butox® Vet, MSD Animal Health, India) at the recommended dose, which was applied uniformly over the body surface using a hand-spraying method as part of routine field management practices. Subsequent seasonal sampling was conducted after sufficiently long intervals extending well beyond the reported residual efficacy period of deltamethrin under field conditions, thereby minimising potential carry-over effects on subsequent tick burden estimation. In addition, all animals were subjected to the same acaricide treatment protocol throughout the study period to reduce systematic treatment bias between groups.

To obtain a more reliable estimate of inherent tick resistance under field conditions, the average of the three seasonal tick counts was considered as the representative tick burden for each animal. Based on the mean number of fully or partially engorged ticks, cattle were classified into two groups using a modified approach adapted from Gonzales et al. (1993), Molento et al. (2013), and Passafaro et al. (2015): higher resistance (<20 ticks) and lower resistance (>20 ticks).

Histopathological examination of skin

Skin biopsies were collected to evaluate histopathological alterations associated with tick infestation, with particular emphasis on mast cell infiltration and differences in host immune responses between Kankrej and Holstein Friesian (HF) crossbred cattle. Due to ethical considerations, farmer consent, and the practical limitations of conducting invasive sampling under field conditions at farmers’ doorsteps, only a limited number of biopsy samples could be collected. Accordingly, a total of ten skin biopsies were obtained and examined for histopathological assessment and mast cell infiltration analysis.

The sampled animals included both tick-infested and non-infested cattle representing the two breeds; however, the number of samples within higher- and lower-resistance groups varied owing to field availability and ethical constraints. Nevertheless, microscopic examination of tissue sections was providing comparative insights into mast cell infiltration patterns and local host responses associated with tick infestation.

Among the ten biopsies, five were collected from tick-infested animals (2 Kankrej and 3 HF crossbred) and five from non-infested controls (3 Kankrej and 2 HF crossbred). In infested cattle, biopsies were specifically collected from sites showing active larval tick attachment, as host resistance reactions are reported to be most pronounced during the larval stage of infestation (Kemp et al., 1982; Roberts, 1968). Animals were restrained in a crush and lightly sedated with xylazine hydrochloride at the recommended dose rate. Local anaesthesia was administered using 2% lignocaine hydrochloride prior to sample collection. Skin cores measuring 6 mm in diameter and approximately 10 mm in depth were aseptically collected using disposable biopsy punches. The skin tissues were fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin and processed by a routine histopathological method which includes dehydration in graded alcohol, clearing in xylene followed by impregnation. The whole tissue processing was carried out in an Automatic Tissue Processor (Leica TP1020). The embedding (Block making) was carried out in a paraffin embedding station (MediMaes Model No 3003) using paraffin wax (58–60°C). Sections of 4–5µ thicknesses had been cut using Leica RM2245 semi-automated rotary microtome and stained with Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) and Toluidine blue to examine under the microscope (Luna, 1968). Mast cell infiltration was assessed through visual examination of multiple microscopic fields for each tissue section, and comparative inferences were drawn based on the overall histological observations and distribution patterns of mast cells within the examined sections.

Blood collection and DNA extraction

At the completion of the third tick count, 5–7 mL of blood was aseptically collected from the jugular vein of all 116 animals following proper restraint in a cattle crush. Samples were transferred into sterile EDTA vacutainers and stored at −20°C until further processing. Genomic DNA was extracted using the QIAamp® DNA Blood Mini Kit (QIAGEN, Germany; catalogue no 51,106) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The quality and integrity of the extracted DNA were assessed by horizontal submarine agarose gel electrophoresis using 0.8% agarose prepared in 1 × TBE buffer (Tris, Borate and EDTA) and stained with ethidium bromide (0.5 µg/mL). Electrophoresis was carried out at 50–60 V for approximately 2 h, and gels were visualized using a Gel Documentation System (Life Technologies, USA). Only samples showing intact, non-smeared DNA bands were retained for downstream analysis, while degraded samples were re-extracted. DNA purity and concentration were evaluated using a NanoDrop ND-1000 spectrophotometer (BioTek, USA). Samples with an OD260/OD280 ratio between 1.7 and 1.9 were considered acceptable for further analysis. DNA concentrations were subsequently normalized using nuclease-free water to obtain a final working concentration of 25 ng/µL for molecular analysis.

Selection of candidate SNPs and PCR-RFLP amplification

To elucidate the genetic basis underlying host response to tick infestation, four single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) located within immune-related candidate genes were selected to evaluate their polymorphism and potential association with tick resistance in Kankrej and Holstein Friesian (HF) crossbred cattle reared in the study region. The selected SNPs included variants within the CSCFD1, ADA, and ZSWIM6 genes, along with one SNP located in an intronic region, all of which had previously been reported to be associated with tick resistance traits or involved in host immune responses against tick infestation (Mapholi et al., 2016; Mukhund et al., 2020). These SNPs were therefore considered promising molecular markers for assessing genetic variability associated with tick resistance under field conditions (Table 1).

Polymerase Chain Reaction (PCR) amplification was carried out using Thermo Scientific DreamTaq Green PCR Master Mix (catalogue no. K1081) in a final reaction volume of 25 µL containing 12.5 µL master mix, 1 µL each of forward and reverse primers, 2 µL of genomic DNA template (25 ng/µL), and 8.5 µL nuclease-free water. The amplified PCR products were subsequently subjected to PCR-Restriction Fragment Length Polymorphism (PCR-RFLP) analysis following the manufacturer’s recommendations for the respective restriction enzymes. The restriction digestion mixture consisted of 2.7 µL of 10 × restriction enzyme buffer, one unit of the appropriate restriction enzyme, and approximately 22.3 µL of PCR product.

Genotyping of the animals

Agarose gel electrophoresis of restriction enzyme-digested PCR products was performed for large-scale genotyping of the study population. Following PCR amplification and enzymatic digestion, the resulting DNA fragments were separated by agarose gel electrophoresis, visualised under UV illumination, and documented using a gel documentation system. Genotypes were determined manually based on the observed restriction banding patterns and fragment sizes. Accordingly, animals were classified into homozygous genotypes for either allele or heterozygous genotypes for each of the four candidate genes under investigation. Based on the genotypic data obtained, genotype frequencies within the study population were calculated, and corresponding allele frequencies were subsequently derived from the observed genotype distributions.

Statistical analysis

Analysis of tick distribution

Tick count data generated from repeated observations across anatomical regions, animals, breeds, seasons, and villages were systematically compiled in Microsoft Excel and analysed using R software (v4.5.0) (R Core Team, 2020). Summary statistics, including mean, standard deviation, minimum, and maximum tick counts, were estimated for all study variables to characterise the magnitude and variability of infestation under field conditions.

To investigate the factors influencing tick burden, a factorial Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) within a general linear model framework was applied by considering breed, season, village, and anatomical region as fixed effects. This analytical approach enabled simultaneous evaluation of host, environmental, and anatomical determinants of tick infestation and allowed quantification of their relative contribution to variations in tick load observed among cattle. The model further facilitated identification of anatomical predilection sites and breed-associated differences in susceptibility under naturally prevailing infestation conditions.

Because tick infestation is a multidimensional biological trait influenced by multiple interrelated variables, correlation analysis among anatomical regions was performed and represented through a heatmap to identify patterns of tick aggregation and preferential attachment across different body parts. Furthermore, Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was employed to reduce the complexity of the large multivariable dataset and to extract the major underlying patterns governing tick distribution. PCA enabled the identification of clustering tendencies among breeds, seasons, villages, and anatomical regions, thereby providing an integrated interpretation of the ecological and host-associated factors contributing to tick infestation dynamics in cattle.

Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium (HWE)

Based on the PCR-RFLP banding patterns, individual animals were classified into their respective genotypic groups, including homozygous and heterozygous variants for each candidate gene. Genotype and allele frequencies were subsequently estimated from the observed distribution of genotypes within the study population following the method of Falconer and Mackay (1996). To further assess the genetic structure and allelic distribution of the population, polymorphic loci were tested for conformity with Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium (HWE) using the Chi-square test.

Results

Distribution and determinants of tick burden in cattle

Tick distribution on the animal body

The descriptive analysis (Fig. 1A) demonstrated significant variation in tick distribution across different anatomical regions of the host body (p < 0.001), indicating clear site-specific preferences for tick attachment. Among the examined regions, the crotch (2.51 ± 1.58; range 0–10) and udder (2.26 ± 1.32; range 0–8) exhibited the highest mean tick burdens, followed by the dewlap (1.59 ± 1.34; range 0–10) and neck (1.42 ± 1.49; range 0–9). In contrast, the rump (0.15 ± 0.49; range 0–4) and hump (0.07 ± 0.32; range 0–3) consistently recorded the lowest tick burdens.

Where, Blue dot indicate mean value. Statistical analysis revealed highly significant differences among body parts, villages, breeds, and seasons (***P < 0.001).

Village-wise tick prevalence

Overall tick counts across the 11 surveyed villages revealed substantial spatial variation in infestation intensity (Fig. 1B), with village-wise differences being highly significant (p < 0.001). Among the study locations, Bhakhar (23.07 ± 8.34) and Chodungri (16.96 ± 9.79) exhibited the highest mean tick burdens. In contrast, Gangapura (4.00 ± 1.41) and Sodapur (4.00 ± 1.79) recorded the lowest levels of infestation.

Breed-wise variation in tick load

Comparison of tick burden between Kankrej and Holstein Friesian (HF) crossbred cattle (Fig. 1C) revealed a highly significant breed-wise difference in susceptibility to tick infestation (p < 0.001). HF crossbred cattle exhibited substantially higher tick counts (17.47 ± 10.30; range 1–48) compared with Kankrej cattle (8.87 ± 10.03; range 2–44).

Seasonal variation in tick count

Tick infestation exhibited a pronounced seasonal pattern, with highly significant variation observed across seasons (p < 0.001) (Fig. 1D). The highest tick burden was recorded during the summer season (18.52 ± 12.92), followed by the monsoon season (15.82 ± 10.95), whereas the lowest infestation levels were observed during winter (11.40 ± 6.73).

Correlation among different body parts

Correlation analysis among anatomical regions (Fig. 2) revealed distinct patterns of tick aggregation across the host body. Strong positive correlations were observed among the dewlap, cranial limb, caudal limb, udder, crotch, neck, and naval regions (r > 0.60; p < 0.001), indicating that infestation in one of these anatomical sites was strongly associated with increased tick burden in the others.

Heatmap illustrating Pearson’s correlation coefficients of tick counts among different anatomical sites in cattle. Strong positive correlations were observed among the dewlap, cranial limb, caudal limb, udder, crotch, neck, and naval regions. Color intensity represents the strength and direction of correlation, with darker shades indicating stronger positive associations. Significant correlations were observed primarily among preferred tick attachment sites (P < 0.05).

The strongest associations were identified between the cranial limb and crotch (r = 0.708), udder and crotch (r = 0.695), and cranial limb and caudal limb (r = 0.690), all of which were highly significant.

In contrast, the hump region exhibited weak and predominantly non-significant correlations with most anatomical sites, including the ear (p = 0.952), head (p = 0.482), cranial limb (p = 0.560), thorax (p = 0.450), flank (P = 0.289), naval (p = 0.358), caudal limb (p = 0.445), tail (p = 0.803), and crotch (p = 0.939), indicating that the hump is comparatively less preferred for tick colonisation. However, limited but significant associations of the hump were observed with the dewlap (p = 0.001), abdomen (p = 0.007), rump (p = 0.030), and udder (p = 0.012), although the correlation strengths remained comparatively low.

Principal component analysis (PCA)

Principal Component Analysis (PCA) revealed that the first two principal components together explained 55.16% of the total variation in tick infestation patterns across anatomical regions (Fig. 3). Among these, PC1 accounted for the largest proportion of variance (46.59%), indicating that a major share of the observed variation in tick burden was driven by a specific group of highly infested anatomical sites. The highest loadings on PC1 were associated with the dewlap, cranial limb, udder, and crotch regions, identifying these body parts as the principal contributors to overall variability in tick distribution among cattle. PC2 explained an additional 8.57% of the variation and was mainly influenced by thoracic and abdominal regions, representing secondary variation in infestation patterns.

Principal component analysis (PCA) loading plot showing the contribution of different anatomical regions to tick infestation patterns in cattle. PC1 and PC2 explained 46.59% and 8.57% of the total variation, respectively. The dewlap, cranial limb, udder, and crotch regions showed the highest positive loadings on PC1.

Analysis of variance (ANOVA)

The general linear model analysis revealed highly significant effects (p < 0.001) of breed, season, village, and anatomical region on tick burden in cattle, demonstrating that tick infestation was strongly influenced by both host-related and environmental factors. Among all evaluated variables, anatomical region exhibited the strongest effect on tick distribution (F = 187.82), highlighting the pronounced site-specific preference of ticks across the host body. Seasonal variation represented the second most influential factor (F = 100.21), followed closely by breed (F = 98.36), indicating substantial effects of climatic conditions and host genetic background on infestation intensity. Village-wise variation also showed a significant influence on tick burden (F = 41.09), reflecting the contribution of local ecological and management-related conditions to the observed differences in infestation patterns under field conditions.

Cutaneous histopathological characteristics of skin in tick-infested and non-infested cattle

Based on microscopic examination of skin biopsy sections, mast cell infiltration was markedly higher in tick-infested cattle than in non-infested cattle, indicating activation of the local cutaneous immune response following tick attachment. Notably, Kankrej cattle exhibited comparatively greater mast cell infiltration than HF crossbred cattle in both infested and non-infested groups. In non-infested animals, Kankrej cattle showed a higher baseline mast cell population compared to HF crossbred cattle (Fig. 4A and B), indicating breed-specific differences in innate cutaneous immunity. Following tick infestation, mast cell infiltration increased substantially in both breeds; however, the response remained more pronounced in Kankrej cattle at tick attachment sites (Fig. 4C and D).

Polymorphism study of candidate genes

In the present study, four candidate genes, namely CSCFD1, ADA, ZSWIM6, and one locus from the intronic region, were selected based on previous reports describing their potential association with tick resistance. PCR–RFLP screening was performed to assess genetic polymorphism in Kankrej and HF crossbred cattle. Among the four loci examined, only ADA and ZSWIM6 exhibited polymorphism, whereas CSCFD1 and the intronic-region locus were found to be monomorphic and were therefore excluded from further downstream association analysis. The ADA gene displayed three genotypes, while ZSWIM6 showed two genotypes. Representative PCR–RFLP agarose gel electrophoresis patterns are presented in Fig. 5. Univariate logistic regression analysis was performed to evaluate the association of breed and candidate gene genotypes with tick infestation status, considering tick resistance as a binary response variable (Table 3). Breed and genotypes were fitted as independent variables, while infestation status was treated as the dependent variable. The analysis demonstrated a highly significant breed effect (p < 0.01), with Kankrej cattle exhibiting substantially lower odds of tick infestation (OR = 0.04) compared with HF crossbred cattle, thereby confirming the superior innate tick resistance of the indigenous breed under field conditions. There were no statistically significant association was detected between ZSWIM6 genotypes and tick infestation in the present study (p = 0.13), animals carrying the heterozygous AB genotype exhibited approximately 2.2-fold higher odds of tick infestation compared with animals carrying the homozygous AA genotype. Although the studied candidate genes exhibited observable differences in genotype distribution between resistance and susceptible animals, their associations with tick infestation status were not statistically significant (p > 0.05).

Discussion

The present study provides a detailed field-based evaluation of tick infestation patterns in cattle under natural tropical production conditions and demonstrates that tick burden is influenced by a complex interaction of anatomical, environmental, immunological, and genetic factors. Significant variation in infestation intensity was observed across anatomical regions, breeds, villages, and seasons, highlighting the multifactorial nature of host-tick interactions in dairy cattle populations.

Distinct anatomical predilection patterns were evident, with the crotch, udder, dewlap, and limb regions consistently harbouring the highest tick burdens, whereas exposed regions such as the hump and rump exhibited minimal infestation. These anatomical regions are characterised by loose skin folds, increased warmth, moisture retention, and rich vascularisation, creating favourable microenvironments for tick attachment, feeding, and survival. In contrast, exposed and less vascularised regions appear comparatively unsuitable for tick colonisation. Similar findings have been reported in previous studies, where ticks preferentially colonised soft, thin-skinned, and sheltered body regions with reduced grooming accessibility (Muchenje et al., 2008; Neves & Vidotto, 2018; Sajid et al., 2007). Mapholi et al. (2022) observed the highest tick infestation on the under-tail, perineum, and belly regions in Nguni cattle, while Kandi et al. (2022) similarly reported elevated infestation in the perineum and dewlap regions in Indian cattle populations. Collectively, these observations support the existence of conserved anatomical predilection sites for tick attachment across different cattle breeds and geographical regions.

The pronounced variation in tick burden among villages suggests that local ecological and management-related factors substantially influence infestation dynamics. Differences in grazing systems, vegetation cover, housing conditions, livestock density, microclimatic conditions, and acaricide application practices may collectively determine local tick survival and transmission patterns under field conditions. Villages with poor pasture management and higher animal density are likely to provide favourable environments for tick persistence and proliferation (Babayani & Makati, 2021; Kasaija et al., 2021; Nava et al., 2024).

Breed-wise comparisons clearly demonstrated greater susceptibility of Holstein Friesian (HF) crossbred cattle compared with indigenous Kankrej cattle, reinforcing the established understanding that Bos indicus cattle possess superior natural resistance to ectoparasites relative to Bos taurus derived breeds (Bianchin et al., 2007; Ibelli et al., 2012; Silva et al., 2007; Utech et al., 1978). The comparatively lower infestation observed in Kankrej cattle may be attributed to adaptive evolutionary traits such as thicker skin, enhanced grooming behaviour, and more efficient innate immune responses against ectoparasitic infestation (Gopalakrishnan et al., 2020; Kolte et al., 2017). Importantly, this is among the first studies directly comparing tick burden between Kankrej and HF crossbred cattle under natural infestation conditions, thereby providing valuable evidence regarding the adaptive resilience of indigenous cattle germplasm under tropical production environments.

Seasonal analysis further demonstrated significantly greater infestation during summer and monsoon periods, followed by reduced infestation during winter. Elevated tick burden during summer and monsoon may be associated with favourable environmental conditions, including increased temperature and humidity, which enhance tick development, survival, moulting, and host-seeking activity. Similar seasonal patterns have been reported in India and other tropical regions (Faccini et al., 2021; Kumar et al., 2023; Machado et al., 2010; Mapholi et al., 2022; Webb & David, 2002). These findings emphasise the importance of strategic seasonal interventions, particularly prophylactic acaricide treatment and intensified herd monitoring before peak infestation periods.

Correlation analysis and Principal Component Analysis (PCA) provided further insights into the spatial organisation of tick infestation across the host body. Significant positive correlations among the dewlap, udder, crotch, cranial limb, and caudal limb regions indicated coordinated spatial aggregation of ticks across anatomically connected body parts. These highly correlated regions predominantly represent ventral and concealed body parts characterised by loose skin folds, warmth, and favourable microclimatic conditions that facilitate tick attachment and feeding. The PCA clustering pattern further demonstrated that ventral and concealed body regions were closely associated and collectively contributed to the majority of variation in tick burden. Importantly, PCA indicated that infestation across the host body is strongly structured around a limited number of key anatomical regions. These findings suggest that assessment of selected high-loading regions, particularly the dewlap, udder, crotch, and limb areas, may provide reliable estimates of overall tick burden, thereby improving the practicality and efficiency of large-scale epidemiological monitoring and field-based surveillance programs (Neves & Vidotto, 2018). Factorial ANOVA further confirmed that breed, season, village, and anatomical region independently and significantly influenced tick burden, with anatomical region exerting the strongest effect on infestation variability.

Histopathological examination demonstrated marked mast cell infiltration at larval attachment sites in infested animals compared with non-infested controls, supporting the established role of mast cells in anti-tick immune responses. Matsuda et al. (1990) demonstrated the critical importance of mast cells in mediating resistance against tick infestation, while several subsequent studies have shown that larval tick attachment induces strong cutaneous inflammatory responses associated with acquired resistance (Brossard & Wikel, 2004; Constantinoiu et al., 2010; Kazimírová & Štibrániová, 2013; Kemp et al., 1982; Roberts, 1968). In the present study, Kankrej cattle exhibited comparatively greater mast cell infiltration than HF crossbred cattle in both infested and non-infested groups, suggesting stronger inherent immune responsiveness and enhanced natural resistance to tick infestation in the indigenous breed. Similar observations have been reported in resistant cattle breeds such as Nellore and Nguni, where higher baseline mast cell populations and enhanced inflammatory responses were associated with increased tick resistance (Carvalho et al., 2010; Franzin et al., 2017; Marufu et al., 2014). These cumulative findings suggest that resistant breeds maintain a more immunologically primed skin microenvironment capable of mounting rapid inflammatory responses following tick attachment.

The candidate gene polymorphism analysis provided additional insight into the possible genetic basis underlying host response to tick infestation in Kankrej and Holstein Friesian crossbred cattle. Among the investigated loci, polymorphism was detected for the ADA and ZSWIM6 genes, whereas CSCFD1 (rs109162468) and the intergenic SNP rs41661020 were monomorphic within the studied populations. Similar monomorphic patterns for CSCFD1 have also been reported in Vechur and crossbred cattle populations, suggesting possible conservation or allele fixation at this locus across certain indigenous and crossbred cattle populations (Kamble, 2019). However, Mapholi et al. (2016) reported significant associations of rs109162468 with tick counts in Nguni cattle, indicating that the informativeness and functional relevance of this marker may vary across breeds and geographical populations. The ADA (SNP rs41577070) gene exhibited polymorphism with relatively balanced allele frequencies in both breeds, while crossbred cattle showed comparatively higher heterozygosity than Kankrej cattle, likely reflecting the greater genetic admixture within crossbred populations. Previous studies have also suggested possible involvement of ADA-associated loci in tick resistance-related traits in cattle (Sollero et al., 2017). Similarly, polymorphism observed within ZSWIM6 supports earlier reports suggesting that zinc finger-associated proteins may participate in regulation of immune and inflammatory pathways relevant to host defence mechanisms (Rakhra & Rakhra, 2021). While this association did not reach statistical significance, the observed trend may suggest a possible influence of ZSWIM6 genetic variation on host susceptibility to tick infestation under field conditions.

The absence of significant associations in the present study may reflect the complex polygenic architecture of tick resistance, where numerous loci with relatively small additive effects collectively influence the phenotype alongside substantial environmental modulation under field conditions. In addition, the moderate sample size and naturally prevailing production conditions may have limited the statistical power required to detect subtle genetic associations. Nevertheless, the observed polymorphic patterns and breed-wise differences provide preliminary evidence supporting the need for larger population studies, high-density genomic approaches, and functional validation analyses to better understand the molecular mechanisms underlying tick resistance in indigenous and crossbred cattle populations.

The findings of the present study therefore provide an important foundation for future large-scale validation studies integrating larger populations, high-density genomic markers, and functional analyses. If consistently validated, these candidate loci may serve as useful genomic indicators for the selection of animals combining enhanced tick resistance with desirable production performance. Overall, the study contributes to a broader understanding of the ecological, immunological, and genetic mechanisms underlying ectoparasite resistance in cattle and supports the development of sustainable breeding and tick management strategies for improving cattle adaptability and resilience under tropical production systems.

Conclusion

The present study demonstrated that tick resistance in cattle is influenced by anatomical, environmental, immunological, and genetic factors under natural tropical field conditions. Indigenous Kankrej cattle exhibited significantly lower tick burdens and greater mast cell infiltration than Holstein Friesian crossbreds, indicating superior natural resistance and stronger cutaneous immune responsiveness against tick infestation. Ventral body regions, particularly the crotch, udder, dewlap, and limb areas, were identified as major predilection sites for tick attachment. Although significant associations were not detected for the investigated candidate gene polymorphisms, the findings highlight the complex polygenic nature of tick resistance and provide preliminary insights into the genetic basis of host response to tick infestation. Overall, this study provides one of the first integrated investigations of tick resistance in Kankrej cattle and supports the utilisation of indigenous breeds for sustainable tick management under tropical production systems.
