Parasitic risks and One Health implications of valorising slaughterhouse animal by-products in raw meat-based diets for pets
Authors: Fahad Ahmed, Lia Cavallo, Mariaelisa Marongiu, Antonella Marras, Muhammad Furqan Arshad, Francesca Nonnis, Claudia Tamponi, Antonio Varcasia, Antonio Scala
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe increasing popularity of raw meat-based diets (RMBDs) for companion animals has raised One Health concerns regarding their microbiological and parasitological safety, particularly when raw diets are used at home without following standard safety precautions. These diets may serve as a transmission route for zoonotic parasites, posing risks not only to animal health but also to human health and the environment. This study aimed to assess the parasitic risk of animal by-products (ABP's) from sheep and cattle slaughtered in Sardinia, with a focus on zoonotic parasites of veterinary and public health significance. A total of 672 sheep and 503 cattle were examined in seven slaughterhouses between March 2021 and July 2023. Organs were inspected macroscopically, with representative samples subjected to laboratory parasitological analysis. In sheep, Echinococcus granulosus was detected in 67.7 % of animals, with the liver being significantly more affected than the lungs (54.8 % vs. 49.1 %; P = 0.038). Fertile hydatid cysts were more prevalent in the lungs (9.5 %) than in the liver (5.5 %). Cysticercus tenuicollis and Dicrocoelium dendriticum were observed in 2.4 % and 14.7 % of sheep, respectively. Bronchopulmonary nematodes were detected in 44.8 % of cases, and macroscopic Sarcocystis gigantea cysts were identified in 27.7 % of examined oesophagi. In contrast, cattle showed a significantly lower parasitic burden, with E. granulosus detected in only 2.0 % of animals and fertile cysts being rare. Fasciola hepatica was found in 0.4 % of cattle livers, and no Dicrocoelium or bronchopulmonary nematodes were detected. The results demonstrate a high parasitological load in sheep-derived ABPs, reinforcing the need for strict sanitary controls before their inclusion in RMBDs. Given the zoonotic potential of the detected parasites, particularly in endemic regions, the adoption of targeted decontamination measures is essential. These findings underscore the importance of integrating a One Health approach into raw pet food safety, public health and ABP management strategies.Keywords: BARF, Animal by-products (ABPs), One Health, Pet food safety, Sardinia

Introduction

In recent years, the practice of feeding raw meat-based diets (RMBDs) to companion animals has gained notable popularity worldwide [1,2]. In the United States, it was estimated that between 2017 and 2018, approximately 4 % of cat owners and 3 % of dog owners purchased raw pet food. More recent trends suggest this has increased substantially, with 10–15 % of cats and 15–25 % of dogs currently receiving RMBDs as part of their regular feeding regimen [2]. RMBDs, also referred to as BARF (Biologically Appropriate Raw Food or Bones and Raw Food) typically comprise uncooked meat, offal, bones, fruits, and vegetables, often derived from both domestic and wild animals, and are promoted as a more natural and health-enhancing alternative to conventional commercial pet food [3,4].

Despite their popularity, claims regarding the health benefits of RMBDs remain largely anecdotal and lack robust scientific validation [5]. On the contrary, a growing body of literature highlights significant microbiological and parasitological risks associated with the preparation and feeding of such diets [1,3,4,[6], [7], [8]]. Numerous studies have documented the presence of zoonotic pathogens including bacteria (e.g., Salmonella spp., Listeria monocytogenes, Escherichia coli) and parasites (e.g., Toxoplasma gondii, Echinococcus granulosus) in commercial and homemade raw pet food products [1,6,7]. These agents pose health hazards not only to the animals themselves but also to their human caretakers through direct contact or environmental contamination, thereby raising public health concerns [4,8]. Additionally, handling, preparation and storage of raw diets may inadvertently facilitate indirect human exposure to different parasites at home [[6], [7], [8]].

From a One Health perspective, feeding raw diets to pets may inadvertently facilitate the environmental transmission of zoonotic agents by enabling definitive and intermediate hosts to interact within shared domestic or urban spaces [7]. This situation is particularly critical in the case of parasitic infections, where infected pets can act as definitive hosts, excreting parasite eggs or oocysts into the environment and increasing the risk of exposure for other animals and humans. Notably, parasites such as E. granulosus and T. gondii, which circulate across both urban and rural environments often with low prevalence may increasingly exploitnew ecological niches as raw feeding become more common in urban households [7,8].

While pet food production in Europe is regulated by stringent legislation namely EC Regulation No. 1069/2009 and EC Regulation No. 142/2011—only Category 3 animal by-products (ABPs) are permitted for use in pet diets [8,9]. These by-products must derive from animals that have passed both ante- and post-mortem inspection and pose no risk for human consumption, although they may have been excluded from the food chain for commercial reasons [1,8]. However, it is well recognised that routine inspection protocols are not specifically designed to detect all parasitic stages or low-grade lesions, particularly those that may be subtle, subclinical or embedded within tissues. Consequently, ABPs may still carry parasitic elements of veterinary or public health relevance, especially when incorporated into raw formulations without subsequent inactivation steps [1,8]. Therefore, despite their regulatory compliance, the microbiological and parasitological safety of these ingredients particularly when used in raw form requires further scrutiny and validation under both hygiene and One Health public health frameworks [7,8].

The present study aims to assess the parasitic burden of ABPs derived from sheep and cattle slaughtered in abattoirs across the island of Sardinia. Using both direct and indirect parasitological techniques, this investigation evaluates the possibility of valorising these by-products in the pet food sector. Such an approach not only offers potential economic benefits by offsetting the cost of ABP disposal but also aligns with the goals of a low-carbon, circular bioeconomy. However, this strategy must be critically assessed for its potential to serve as a reservoir of zoonotic parasites, such as E. granulosus, which remains endemic in Sardinia [10]. In this context, the sustainable use of ABPs for raw pet food production represents an innovative, yet potentially risky, intersection between food safety, animal health and environmental protection.

Materials and methods

Sampling

Between March 2021 and July 2023, a total of 672 sheep and 503 cattle were examined across seven slaughterhouses in Sardinia (Municipalities: Arborea, Tula, Lula, Thiesi, Baunei, Buddusò, Bortigali). The aim was to evaluate the parasitic burden in organs commonly used in raw meat-based diets (RMBDs) (Table 1).

In sheep, all liver and lung samples were examined regardless of fitness for human consumption. In cattle, however, only organs condemned for human consumption were examined due to their standard use for human food. Nevertheless, any parasitic findings from bovine samples were still recorded. All organs were initially assessed in situ, and representative samples showing parasitic lesions were collected and transported to the laboratory of parasitology and parasitic diseases, University of Sassari, Italy for further analysis.

Macroscopic examination

The liver was visually inspected on both diaphragmatic and visceral surfaces to detect signs of parasite migration (e.g., fluke tracks) and larval cestodes (Cysticercus tenuicollis and Echinococcus granulosus). Full-thick incisions were made through the liver, including the gallbladder, to identify adult trematodes (Fasciola hepatica and Dicrocoelium dendriticum) and related pathologies such as bile duct thickening.

The lungs of sheep were examined for hydatid cysts and signs of bronchopulmonary parasite lesions (e.g., Protostrongylid incubation nodules). Where present, hydatid cysts were counted and classified by consistency (liquid, caseous or calcified). Samples of liquid cysts were collected in sterile conditions, stored in refrigerated containers and examined for fertility (presence of protoscoleces) under a light microscope at 10× magnification. Viability was assessed based on morphology, motility, and presence of flame cells, following Scala et al. [11].

To evaluate bronchopulmonary parasitosis, a subset of whole lungs was collected and transported to Laboratory for further epidemiological analysis.

The esophageal musculature was inspected for macroscopic Sarcocystis cysts, which were subsequently classified using the criteria proposed by Dubey et al. [12]. In addition, approximately 50 g of myocardial tissue were collected from each animal, frozen at −20 °C, and later subjected to ELISA and PCR analyses for protozoan detection (Sarcocystis spp., Toxoplasma gondii) in future studies.

All organs were categorized using a three-tiered risk scoring system based on lesion severity, zoonotic potential, species involved and as per Regulation (EC) 1069/2009 [9]:

Laboratory analysis

Sheep lungs were subjected to anatomical-pathological inspection and Baermann analysis for bronchopulmonary nematodes. The trachea was dissected longitudinally beyond the bronchial bifurcation to identify adult worms of Dictyocaulus spp. and Protostrongylus rufescens. Lungs were then incised and palpated for other nematode related lesions.

For the Baermann test, two 1cm3 parenchymal tissue samples were collected from the dorso-caudal portions of each lung. These samples were wrapped in gauze and submerged in water-filled funnels at room temperature for 24 h. Larvae (L1) were allowed to migrate out of the tissues and sediment at the bottom. The sediment was then centrifuged (2000 rpm, 10 min), stained with Lugol's iodine, and examined under a light microscope (10× and 40×) for species identification using morphometric keys [13].

Statistical analysis

Each animal was assigned a unique alphanumeric identifier. Parasitological data collected at the slaughterhouses and in the laboratory were compiled using Microsoft Excel and statistically analyzed using EpiInfo version 7.2.4.0. Prevalence rates were compared using the Chi-square (χ2) test, with p-values <0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 672 sheep were examined across the slaughterhouses of Tula, Lula, Thiesi, Baunei, Buddusò, and Bortigali. Echinococcus granulosus hydatid cysts were detected in 67.7 % of animals (455/672), with a total of 2486 cysts recovered. This corresponds to an abundance of 3.7 cysts per animal and an average intensity of 5.5 cysts among infected animals. The distribution of hydatids varied by organ, with a significantly higher prevalence observed in the liver (54.8 %; 368/672) compared to the lungs (49.1 %; 330/672) (χ2 = 4.304; P = 0.038). Co-infection involving both organs was detected in 36.2 % of animals (243/672). Notably, massive infections involving ten or more cysts were observed in 2.5 % (17/672) of the sheep.

Organ-specific abundance was calculated as 2.1 cysts in the liver and 1.6 in the lungs, with average intensities of 3.1 and 2.4 cysts per infected animal, respectively. Morphological classification of the cysts revealed a significant difference between organs. In the liver, the majority of cysts were calcified (60.6 %; 844/1392), followed by liquid (34.8 %; 484/1392), and caseous cysts (4.6 %; 64/1392). In contrast, the lungs contained mostly liquid cysts (59.6 %; 652/1094), with lower proportions of caseous (21.2 %; 232/1094) and calcified cysts (19.2 %; 210/1094) (Table 3 and Fig. 1).

The metacestode Cysticercus tenuicollis was identified in 2.4 % of the animals examined (16/672). Regarding hepatic trematodiasis, Fasciola hepatica was detected in 2.1 % (14/672) of animals, whereas Dicrocoelium dendriticum was significantly more prevalent, occurring in 14.7 % (99/672) of cases (χ2 = 69.807; P < 0.0001). Lesions attributable to bronchopulmonary nematodes were present in 32.4 % (218/672) of lungs inspected.

Examination of the oesophagus was possible in only 296 of the 672 animals due to removal during evisceration. Among these, macroscopic Sarcocystis spp. cysts, oval and consistent in morphology with Sarcocystis gigantea, were observed in 27.7 % (82/296) of cases.

Further analysis of liquid hydatid cysts revealed the presence of protoscolices in 11.6 % (78/672) of animals. The fertility rate was slightly higher in lung cysts (9.5 %; 64/672) than in liver cysts (5.5 %; 37/672), with the difference being statistically significant (χ2 = 7.804; P = 0.005).

Among the 431 animals from which the complete respiratory tract was collected, bronchopulmonary nematodes were detected in 44.8 % (192/431). Adult specimens of Dictyocaulus filaria were observed in the trachea and main bronchi of 2.1 % (9/431) of the animals. Examination by the Baermann technique revealed the presence of first-stage larvae (L1) of Protostrongylid nematodes in 42.7 % (183/431) of the lungs. Within this group, Muellerius capillaris was the most prevalent species, detected in 36.4 % (157/431) of lungs, followed by Neostrongylus linearis in 22.7 % (98/431), and Cystocaulus ocreatus in 6.7 % (29/431). Larvae of D. filaria were found in 2.1 % (9/431), confirming the macroscopic findings. The prevalence difference among species was statistically significant (χ2 = 225.53; df = 3; P < 0.00001).

Monospecific infections accounted for 56.8 % (109/192) of positive cases, bispecific infections for 33.8 % (65/192), and trispecific infections for 9.4 % (18/192). The most common co-infection involved M. capillaris and N. linearis (26.6 %; 51/192). Notably, Protostrongylus rufescens were not identified in any of the lungs examined.

In cattle (n = 503), E. granulosus hydatids were found in 2.0 % (10/503) of animals, with a total of 46 cysts. The calculated abundance was 0.1 and the average intensity among positive animals was 4.6. Cysts were more frequently located in the lungs (1.8 %; 9/503) than in the liver (0.2 %; 1/503), a statistically significant difference (χ2 = 4.304; P = 0.038). Multi-organ infection was observed in only one animal (0.2 %) (Table 3).

Fasciola hepatica was identified in 0.4 % (2/503) of cattle livers, while no cases of D. dendriticum were recorded. The majority of cattle organs were categorized as Category A: 99.2 % of livers (499/503) and 97.8 % of lungs (492/503). Category B included 0.6 % of livers (3/503) and 0.4 % of lungs (2/503), while Category C included 0.2 % of livers (1/503) and 1.6 % of lungs (8/503), as summarized in Table 5.

Discussion

The findings of this study provide updated insights into the parasitological risks associated with raw animal by-products (ABPs) intended for use in raw meat-based diets (RMBDs). By employing standardized parasitological methodologies consistent with previous regional surveys, our investigation allows for reliable temporal comparisons and contributes to the growing body of literature addressing One Health implications of raw pet food. The high prevalence of cystic echinococcosis (CE) in sheep, with 67.7 % of animals found positive and hydatid cysts predominantly affecting the liver, confirms that CE remains hyperendemic in Sardinia [10]. Organ-specific analysis confirmed the liver as the most frequently affected site (54.8 %), with a significantly higher prevalence than the lungs (49.1 %). These findings mirror those of the previous study, which reported liver and lung prevalences of 56.4 % and 46 %, respectively. Similarly, the rate of multi-organized infections (36.2 %) remains comparable to the 37.1 % previously reported [10].

The fertility rate of hydatid cysts also aligns with previous findings, with lungs exhibiting higher fertility than livers, a trend consistent with observations dating back nearly two decades [14]. Despite the high prevalence, a considerable proportion of cysts showed signs of degeneration, with 54.3 % classified as either caseous (11.9 %) or calcified (42.4 %). However, a substantial number of active liquid cysts were still detected, especially in the lungs (59.6 %). This suggests a slower senescence process in pulmonary localizations, potentially due to a weaker peri parasitic immune response or reduced drug bioavailability in lung tissues, especially in animals treated with benzimidazoles for gastrointestinal parasites [15].

In contrast to previous surveys, the current study found a lower parasitic burden. Both the abundance (3.7 vs. 4.88) and average intensity (5.5 vs. 7.4) of hydatids were reduced, and the proportion of massive infections (≥10 cysts) decreased significantly from 14.8 % to 2.5 %. These observations may reflect an evolving resistance among sheep or improvements in parasite control strategies [10,14,15].

The detection of Cysticercus tenuicollis, the larval stage of Taenia hydatigena, in 2.4 % of sheep was expected, considering the sampled population consisted primarily of adults (4–7 years old). Cysticercosis is more commonly observed in lambs, as supported by recent studies in Sardinia that report a 14.6 % prevalence in younger animals, where the infection carries greater economic implications due to condemnation of affected organs [16].

Regarding hepatic trematodes, Fasciola hepatica was found in only 2.1 % of sheep, consistent with its generally low prevalence on the island. By contrast, Dicrocoelium dendriticum was identified in 14.7 % of livers. This figure represents a significant reduction compared to the 25.5 % prevalence reported six years ago [17]. As noted by various authors, post-mortem inspections at slaughterhouses are more sensitive than in vivo diagnoses, which often fail to detect light infections (i.e., <100 adult flukes), contributing to underreporting and uncontrolled spread of dicrocoeliosis in the territory [17].

The current study also identified Sarcocystis spp. macrocysts in the oesophagus of 27.7 % of examined sheep, a prevalence comparable to that reported by Pipia et al. [18], which reported a prevalence of 31.6 %. These ovoid cysts were morphologically consistent with Sarcocystis gigantea. However, this is likely an underestimation since the oesophagus is not the only anatomical site where macrocysts occur. A thorough examination of the entire carcass, especially the diaphragm, abdominal and intercostal muscles, is necessary to detect sarcocystosis. The fusiform shape and orientation of cysts along muscle fibres render them difficult to detect during routine post-mortem inspection, especially when the oesophagus is discarded as part of the byproducts [18].

The presence of macroscopic Sarcocystis cysts has both hygienic and regulatory implications. Although S. gigantea is not zoonotic, EU legislation (Reg. 2017/625) considers heavily infested carcasses unfit for human consumption. Moreover, consumption of raw or undercooked sheep meat may expose humans to sarcotoxins released upon cyst rupture [19]. From an epidemiological perspective, the high prevalence of feline-transmitted Sarcocystis species further highlights close contact between sheep and cats, an association also implicated in the transmission of Toxoplasma gondii, a protozoan of major concern for both public health and reproductive performance in sheep [20]. Thus, the presence of Sarcocystis may serve as a useful marker for T. gondii risk on farms.

Macroscopic lung examination revealed bronchopulmonary nematode (BPN) lesions in 32.4 % of sheep, significantly lower than the 54.2 % reported by Scala et al. [21]. Laboratory-based Baermann analysis confirmed BPN infections in 44.5 % of examined animals, with Protostrongylid larvae present in 42.5 % of cases. These figures closely align with previous data (40.7 %; [21]), confirming a persistent endemicity of these parasites. The identified parasite community also remained consistent, with Muellerius capillaris, Neostrongylus linearis, and Cystocaulus ocreatus detected, while Protostrongylus rufescens was absent, as in previous studies in domestic sheep but reported in wild mouflons [22].

Notably, M. capillaris was the most prevalent species in this study (36.4 %), a significant increase compared to (21.3 %) to study carried by Scale et al. [21]. Conversely, N. linearis was less prevalent in the present study (22.7 %) compared to study in 2000 (31.6 %). C. ocreatus prevalence has also declined from 12.6 % to 6.7 %. The prevalence of D. filaria remained constant at 2.1 %, reinforcing the view that BPNs are still widespread, though their species composition may be shifting [21]. Importantly, the BPN species detected do not pose zoonotic risks and are not capable of infecting dogs or cats; therefore, the associated pulmonary lesions were classified as low-risk (Table 2).

In cattle, the prevalence of CE (2.0 %) was considerably lower than in sheep but significantly higher than the 0.05 % previously reported in intensive farming systems in the same region [23]. Historically, much higher prevalences were observed, such as the 45.9 % recorded in the 2009–2010 [24]. These fluctuations indicate that CE in cattle is context-dependent and largely influenced by sheep co-habitation, illegal slaughter practices and the presence of stray dogs. Genotyping consistently identifies E. granulosus sensu stricto (sheep strain) in cattle, confirming that they act as accidental hosts and do not contribute to parasite transmission due to typically sterile cysts [23].

From a One Health perspective, the persistence of CE in Sardinia remains a serious concern for human health. Conchedda et al. [25] reported a human CE incidence of 6.62 per 100,000 residents during 2001–2005, with an annual hospitalization cost of approximately €4 million. The epidemiological pattern in Sardinia indicates that CE transmission is concentrated in rural pastoral areas, where sheep density and dog–livestock interactions are highest, while urban cases largely reflect past exposure or population mobility rather than active urban transmission [25]. Mastrandrea et al. [26] later confirmed an average annual discharge rate of 9.3/100,000, peaking at 25.8/100,000 in the province of Nuoro. Sardinia alone accounted for 12 % of Italy's CE-related hospital admissions.

The organ classification system adopted in this study revealed stark contrasts between species. Nearly all cattle organs (99.2 %) were classified as Category A, lacking visible parasitic lesions or involving non-pet zoonoses. In sheep, however, only 25.7 % of animals had both liver and lungs free from visible parasitic risks. Specifically, 50.45 % of lungs and 37.5 % of livers were in Category A. These data reflect the high prevalence of cestodes (E. granulosus and T. hydatigena), both of which use dogs as definitive hosts, thus posing direct risk when included in RMBDs without thermal or chemical treatment.

Post-mortem inspection is crucial in preventing zoonotic transmission and ensuring meat hygiene. Although EU Reg. 2017/625 and 2019/627 set guidelines for parasitic inspections, they cover only a few parasitic infections (e.g., cysticercosis, trichinellosis). Consequently, light parasitic infections, particularly hydatids buried deep in organs or Sarcocystis cysts in muscle tissues, can be overlooked, especially in slaughterhouses. While Category 3 ABPs may be used in oleochemical or feed sectors after processing, their use in RMBDs without sanitization poses health risks. EU Reg. 1069/2009 permits the use of ABPs fit for human consumption (but excluded for commercial reasons) in pet food, provided they are appropriately treated.

Beyond health considerations, there are economic and environmental implications. ABPs represent a resource that can be valorized through incorporation into raw pet foods with minimal technological input. Disposal costs for carcasses are substantial—€0.97/kg for bovine, €2.21/kg for sheep and goats, and €1.07/kg for equines (Ministry of Agriculture, 2020). Moreover, incineration and transport contribute significantly to greenhouse gas emissions, with an estimated CO₂ emission cost of €13/ton [27].

Conclusion

The findings of this study confirm that sheep ABPs from Sardinia carry a substantial parasitic burden particularly for CE while bovine organs show minimal infection. Although compliant with EU regulations, these raw by-products can still contain viable parasitic stages when incorporated into RMBDs underscoring a potential risk for pets and for the wider environment. From a One Health perspective, the persistence of CE in Sardinia and the increasing adoption of raw feeding practices highlight important points of convergence between livestock management, pet nutrition and human exposure pathways. Domestic handling of raw ingredients of pet food and incomplete sanitary processing all represent potential interfaces for parasitic transmission. Strengthened inactivation procedures, improved hygiene during raw diet preparation and coordinated veterinary public health oversight are therefore essential to mitigate these risks while enabling the sustainable valorisation of ABPs within a circular bioeconomy.
