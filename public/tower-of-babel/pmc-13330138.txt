NCAPD3 promotes osteosarcoma progression by modulating macrophage polarization and tumor cell proliferation
Authors: Ruiling Xu, Binfeng Liu, Sai Zhu, Lin Qi, Lu Wan, Ruiqi Chen, Lu Wang, Xiaolei Ren, Zhihong Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOsteosarcoma (OS) is the most common primary malignant bone tumor in children and adolescents, characterized by aggressive behavior and poor prognosis. Despite advances in multimodal therapies, outcomes for patients with metastatic or recurrent OS remain poor. Non-SMC Condensin II Complex Subunit D3 (NCAPD3) is a core component of the condensin II complex involved in chromosome condensation and segregation; however, its role in OS progression and the tumor immune microenvironment remains unclear.MethodsPublicly available transcriptomic datasets were analyzed to evaluate NCAPD3 expression and prognostic relevance in OS. Using single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq; n = 6), we characterized tumor cell heterogeneity and NCAPD3-expressing subsets within the osteosarcoma tumor microenvironment. In vitro, NCAPD3 knockdown was performed in RAW264.7 macrophages, primary murine macrophages, and THP-1–derived macrophages to assess changes in macrophage polarization markers at the transcriptional and protein levels. Functional assays, including conditioned-media experiments, evaluated tumor–macrophage crosstalk. The effects of NCAPD3 on osteosarcoma cell proliferation, migration, and tumor growth were assessed in vitro and in vivo. Pathway enrichment analyses were conducted in NCAPD3⁺ macrophages and tumor cells, and in silico drug sensitivity prediction was performed as an exploratory analysis.ResultsNCAPD3 was significantly overexpressed in OS tissues and associated with poorer overall survival. scRNA-seq analysis identified distinct tumor cell subsets and a population of NCAPD3⁺ macrophages characterized by reduced inflammatory activity. NCAPD3 knockdown in macrophages consistently upregulated pro-inflammatory markers and downregulated anti-inflammatory markers, indicating a shift toward a pro-inflammatory phenotype. Conditioned-media experiments demonstrated that NCAPD3-dependent tumor–macrophage crosstalk regulates osteosarcoma cell proliferation and migration. Pathway analyses revealed downregulation of IL2/STAT5 and IL6/JAK/STAT3 signaling in NCAPD3⁺ macrophages, while NCAPD3⁺ tumor cells exhibited reduced apoptotic signaling and enhanced DNA repair and PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway activity. Functional assays confirmed that NCAPD3 promotes osteosarcoma cell growth and tumor progression in vitro and in vivo.ConclusionsNCAPD3 promotes osteosarcoma progression through coordinated effects on tumor cell proliferation and suppression of macrophage-mediated inflammatory responses. These findings identify NCAPD3 as a context-dependent regulator of tumor–immune interactions in OS and support its potential as a prognostic biomarker and therapeutic target.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-026-08168-0.

Introduction

Osteosarcoma (OS) is the most common primary bone malignancy, primarily affecting children and adolescents [1]. The recurrence rate is high and it frequently metastasizes to distant organs like the lungs [2–4]. Neoadjuvant chemotherapy, tumor amputation, and postoperative chemotherapy are the primary approaches for treating OS, leading to enhanced rates of survival [3]. Nevertheless, the progress in recent years has not led to a substantial enhancement in the survival rate of individuals experiencing metastasis or relapse [5]. This limited progress is largely attributable to pronounced genomic instability, intratumoral heterogeneity, and an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment, highlighting the need to identify novel biomarkers and mechanistic regulators that integrate tumor-intrinsic biology with immune modulation.

The non-SMC condensin II complex subunit D3 (NCAPD3) gene is responsible for encoding a subunit of condensin II, which is a complex that plays a role in the condensation and segregation of chromosomes during mitosis [6]. Previous studies in several malignancies have reported aberrant NCAPD3 expression and primarily linked it to cell-cycle progression, chromosomal instability, and tumor cell proliferation [7–9]. However, the functional relevance of NCAPD3 in osteosarcoma, particularly beyond its canonical tumor cell–intrinsic mitotic functions, remains poorly understood. Moreover, the potential link between NCAPD3 and the tumor microenvironment, particularly in relation to immune response modulation, remains unclear [10–12]. Tumor microenvironment (TME) is infiltrated by many immune cells, but tumor cells escape the immune system and alter the microenvironment around them [10–12]. Certain subtypes of tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), which are present in large quantities within the tumor microenvironment, promote the spread of cancer by creating a conducive environment for cancer cells [13–15]. It is obvious that the TME is extremely complex, even when viewed in the narrow scope of TAM behavior [16, 17]. Multiple studies have indicated that TAMs have a vital function in the metastasis, invasion, angiogenesis, and immunosuppression of tumors [18–20].

In this context, we postulated that NCAPD3 dysregulation may influence macrophage inflammatory phenotypes not through direct cytoplasmic signaling functions, but indirectly via transcriptional reprogramming, genome instability–associated stress responses, or altered tumor–macrophage crosstalk [21]. This hypothesis was prompted by our single-cell RNA sequencing analyses, which identified distinct NCAPD3-positive tumor cell and macrophage subpopulations within the osteosarcoma microenvironment, suggesting potential functional interplay in a macrophage-enriched and genomically unstable tumor context. Given that osteosarcoma is characterized by pronounced chromosomal instability and dynamic tumor–immune interactions, and that chromatin-associated regulators and cell-cycle machinery can reshape inflammatory transcriptional programs, these findings provide a biologically plausible framework through which NCAPD3 dysregulation may contribute to immune modulation in OS [22].

In this study, we aimed to comprehensively investigate the expression pattern, prognostic value, and functional role of NCAPD3 in osteosarcoma tumor cell and macrophage. By integrating bulk transcriptomic analyses, single-cell RNA sequencing [23, 24], and updated in vitro and in vivo functional assays, including macrophage polarization and tumor–macrophage interaction studies, we examined how NCAPD3 contributes to OS progression and immune microenvironment remodeling. Our findings suggest that NCAPD3 may serve as a potential biomarker and therapeutic target for OS, providing a foundation for future research into novel treatment strategies for this aggressive cancer.

Methods

Data mining

Pan-cancer RNA-seq data and corresponding clinical information were obtained from the UCSC Xena database (https://xenabrowser.net/), which hosts the TCGA Pan-Cancer (PANCAN) cohort. For single-cell analysis, osteosarcoma (OS) scRNA-seq data were retrieved from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) under accession number GSE162454, comprising 6 OS tissue samples. Bulk OS transcriptomic data were sourced from the TARGET-OS project, while normal tissue RNA-seq data were obtained from the Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx) database. To ensure comparability between cancer and normal samples and to minimize technical variation, we utilized the UCSC Xena Toil RNA-seq Recompute compendium, which provides uniformly processed RNA-seq data across multiple projects and substantially reduces batch effects without requiring additional batch correction. The analysis included 88 OS tumor samples from TARGET and 396 normal tissue samples from GTEx. To minimize potential batch effects arising from differences in sequencing platforms and processing pipelines between TARGET and GTEx, batch correction was performed using the ComBat algorithm. Gene expression values were converted to transcripts per million (TPM) and subsequently log2(TPM + 1) transformed for downstream analyses. After excluding patients with missing survival information, 84 OS patients remained in the TARGET cohort for prognostic analyses. All data used in this study are publicly available.

Prognostic value assessment

We used the R package ‘survival’ to perform the Kaplan-Meier (K-M) survival analysis to investigate the correlation between NCAPD3 expression and the prognosis of OS. The optimal cut-off value for dichotomizing patients into high- and low-expression groups was determined using a pre-specified median value of gene expression, thereby minimizing data-driven bias. Furthermore, the R package ‘timeROC’ was utilized to produce the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves for assessing the predictive precision. Next, we performed survival analyses on specific clinical subgroups to investigate the prognostic significance of NCAPD3. Furthermore, the univariate Cox analysis and multivariate Cox analyses were employed to detect whether the expression of NCAPD3 and other parameters could be considered independent prognostic factors for OS.

Nomogram construction and performance evaluation

Nomograms are widely used tools for individualized prognostic assessment in oncology. To improve prognostic prediction for patients with osteosarcoma, we constructed a predictive nomogram to estimate 1-, 3-, and 5-year overall survival probabilities using the ‘rms’ R package. The discriminative ability of the nomogram was assessed using the concordance index (C-index). To reduce the risk of overfitting, internal validation was performed using 1,000 bootstrap resamples, and an optimism-corrected C-index was calculated. Model calibration was evaluated using bootstrap-corrected calibration curves at 1-, 3-, and 5-year time points, comparing nomogram-predicted survival probabilities with observed outcomes estimated by the Kaplan–Meier method. In addition, time-dependent Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curves and Decision Curve Analysis (DCA) were performed to further assess the predictive accuracy and clinical utility of the nomogram, using the ‘timeROC’, ‘caret’, and ‘rmda’ R packages, respectively.

Co-expression analysis

To further analyze the function and molecular mechanism of specific genes, it is beneficial to investigate genes that are expressed together. We utilized the Pearson correlation coefficient method to conduct correlation analysis and investigate the genes that are co-expressed with NCAPD3, displaying both positive and negative correlations. When the correlation coefficient is above 0.4 and the P value is below 0.01, genes are deemed significantly related to NCAPD3 based on the degree of correlation. Based on the magnitude of the correlation coefficient, we have chosen the top five genes that exhibit positive correlation and the top five genes that exhibit negative correlation for presentation.

Single-cell data processing

Data quality control and preliminary data exploration were performed using the R package called ‘Seurat V5’. The data was filtered based on the following conditions: (1) Genes detected in fewer than three cells were excluded; (2) Cells with less than 250 detected genes in total were excluded; (3) Cells with mitochondrial gene expression equal to or greater than 20% were excluded; (4) Cells with fewer than 500 or more than 6,000 detected genes were excluded, with cells exhibiting abnormally high gene counts considered potential doublets. The Log Normalization algorithm was utilized to normalize the data, followed by normalization of gene expression using a linear regression model. Next, the FindVariableFeatures function identified genes with high variability using the selection method ‘vst’ and a limit of 2000 features. When conducting batch analysis, ‘harmony’ method was used. Significant and effective dimensions were determined by principal component analysis (PCA). The clustering results were visualized using Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP). Next, the combined matrix was scaled using the ScaleData function with its default settings. To perform analysis on the differential expression of genes and identify marker genes for each cell cluster, we utilized the ‘Seurat’ software package. The identification of cell types was performed using the cell markers. The ‘ggplot2’ package was utilized for visualizing the data.

Functional enrichment analysis

To investigate the potential mechanisms of OS, Gene Ontology (GO), Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG), and Gene Set Variation Analysis (GSVA) were performed for NCAPD3. Differential gene analysis was initially conducted using the FindMarkers package to compare NCAPD3 + macrophages versus macrophages and NCAPD3 + tumor cells versus tumor cells. Genes with |log2FC| > 0.585 and an adjusted p-value < 0.05 were filtered out. Subsequently, the clusterProfiler package was employed for GO and KEGG enrichment analyses on the identified differential genes. The top three most significant results from the GO enrichment analysis, along with the findings from the KEGG analysis, were selected for presentation.

Immune microenvironment analysis

The Estimation of Stromal and Immune cells In Malignant Tumor tissues using Expression (ESTIMATE) was widely used to assess the presence of stromal and immune cells in the tumor microenvironment based on the expression levels of specific genes [25]. The ESTIMATE algorithm was utilized to assess the levels of immune infiltration and tumor microenvironment scores in patients with osteosarcoma. Furthermore, NCAPD3 expression level was analyzed using difference analysis and correlation analysis to uncover its correlation with the tumor microenvironment in OS. Afterwards, we employed single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) to compute the enrichment ratio of different immune cells and assessed the variances in immune cell infiltration and immune function between patients with varying levels of NCAPD3 expression using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test [26]. In the meantime, we conducted additional analysis on the correlation between NCAPD3 and immune checkpoints.

Drug sensitivity analysis

To explore potential therapeutics for OS based on NCAPD3 expression, drug sensitivity was predicted using the R package ‘pRRophetic’ with ridge regression. The model was trained on the GDSC database, which contains IC50 measurements for a broad panel of compounds across diverse cancer cell lines. To minimize batch effects between the OS RNA-seq data and the training dataset, ComBat normalization was applied prior to prediction. The half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) of each candidate drug was estimated for individual samples. Differences in predicted IC50 between NCAPD3-high and NCAPD3-low groups were assessed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Multiple testing across the eight candidate drugs (Doxorubicin, Sunitinib, Vinorelbine, Ispinesib, (5Z)-7-Oxozeaenol, CI-1040, CGP-60474, and JW-7-52-1) was controlled using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate procedure (FDR < 0.05).

Cell culture

Human bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (hBMSCs, HUXMA-01001) were obtained from Cyagen Biosciences Inc. (CA, US) and the human osteosarcoma cell lines HOS and 143B were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA). All cell lines were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium/high glucose (DMEM, Hyclone, UT, USA) with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco Laboratory, Grand Island, NY, USA) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco, Carlsbad, CA) at 37 °C and 5% CO2.

RT-qPCR

Total RNA was extracted from hBMSCs, HOS and 143B cells using AG’s RNA extraction kit (AG Scientific Inc., San Diego, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Subsequently, the expression level of each gene was quantified by Hieff qPCR SYBR Green Master Mix (High Rox Plus) (11203ES, YEASEN Biotech Co., Ltd, China). The primer sequences for NCAPD3 and GAPDH were as follows: NCAPD3 forward: 5’-GTGCCAAGGTGGTAGATAAATCA-3’, NCAPD3 reverse: 5’-AACATAGCGTAT TCCCCACAAG-3’; GAPDH forward: 5’- CAGGAGGCATTGCTGATGAT-3’, GAPDH reverse: 5’- GAAGGCTGGGG CTCATTT-3’. The cycling conditions were as follows: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 10 min, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 15 s, annealing at 60 °C for 30 s and extension at 72 °C for 30 s. The relative expression levels of NCAPD3 were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCT method and normalized to GAPDH as an internal control.

Gene perturbation of NCAPD3 in tumor cells and macrophages

To investigate the cell type–specific functions of NCAPD3, different gene perturbation strategies were employed in osteosarcoma cells and macrophages based on experimental requirements and biological considerations. In osteosarcoma cell lines, stable NCAPD3 knockout was achieved using a CRISPR–Cas9–mediated approach. Two single-guide RNAs (sgRNAs) targeting NCAPD3 were designed and synthesized: sgNCAPD3#1 (forward: 5′-caccgTAGAATGGGTTGACACAGTG-3′, reverse: 5′-aaacCACTGTGTCAACCCATTCTAc-3′) and sgNCAPD3#2 (forward: 5′-caccgTCCATGTTCTCCAGTAGCAA-3′, reverse: 5′-aaacTTGCTACTGGAGAACATGGAc-3′). Annealed oligonucleotides were ligated into the lentiCRISPR v2 vector linearized with Esp3I, and correct insertion was confirmed by Sanger sequencing. Lentiviral particles were generated by co-transfecting HEK293T cells with sgControl or sgNCAPD3 plasmids together with packaging plasmids psPAX2 and pMD2.G. Viral supernatants were collected at 48- and 72-hours post-transfection and used to infect target osteosarcoma cells overnight in the presence of 10 µg/mL polybrene. Transduced cells were selected with 2 µg/mL puromycin for 7 days, and stable NCAPD3 knockout was validated by Western blotting.

In contrast, transient siRNA-mediated knockdown was used in macrophages to avoid potential confounding effects of lentiviral infection and sustained Cas9/sgRNA expression on macrophage activation and polarization. For murine macrophages (RAW264.7 and primary murine macrophages), two independent siRNAs targeting mouse Ncapd3 were used: si-Ncapd3#1 (sense: 5′-CCAUCAUCACUUUAGGUAA(dT)(dT)-3′, antisense: 5′-UUACCUAAAGUGAUGAUGG(dT)(dT)-3′) and si-Ncapd3#2 (sense: 5′-GGUUCAAUGUCACUUCCAA(dT)(dT)-3′, antisense: 5′-UUGGAAGUGACAUUGAACC(dT)(dT)-3′). For human THP-1–derived macrophages, species-specific siRNAs targeting human NCAPD3 were used si-NCAPD3#1 (sense: 5′- GACGAAGACCUGUUGGUGA(dT)(dT)-3′, antisense: 5′- UCACCAACAGGUCUUCGUC(dT)(dT)-3′) and si-NCAPD3#2 (sense: 5′- GGUAGAUAAAUCAGAGUAU(dT)(dT)-3′, antisense: 5′- AUACUCUGAUUUAUCUACC(dT)(dT)-3′). A non-targeting siRNA served as the negative control. Macrophages were harvested 48 h after transfection, and knockdown efficiency was confirmed at the mRNA and/or protein level. Concordant results obtained using two independent siRNAs minimized potential off-target effects.

Western blot

To fully lyse cells and obtain cellular proteins, RIPA lysis buffer containing protease inhibitors and phosphatase inhibitors was used. Then, the concentration of proteins was determined using the BCA protein concentration assay kit following the manufacturer’s instructions. Equal amounts of proteins were then separated in 10% SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes. These membranes were subsequently blocked with 5% skim milk powder for 1 h at room temperature and incubated overnight at 4 °C. After washing the membrane three times with TBST, it was incubated with secondary antibodies (Proteintech; SA00001-1, 1:10,000 dilution) for 1 h at room temperature. Following three washes with TBST, the immunoreactions were detected using a Bio-Rad imaging system (Bio-Rad, USA). The primary antibodies used were NCAPD3 (Santa Cruz Biotechnology; sc-81597, 1:500 dilution), AKT (1:5000, 60203-2-Ig, proteintech), p-AKT (1:2000,66444-1-Ig, proteintech), H2AX (1:1000, ab81299,abcam), CD86 (1:1000,A1199,ABclonal), and GAPDH (Proteintech; 60004-1-lg, 1:50,000 dilution).

Cell counting kit-8 assay

A total of 1000 cells per well were seeded in a 96-well plate, and incubation and detection were performed at 24-, 48-, 72-, 96-, and 120-hours post-seeding. Cell viability was assessed by adding Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) solution (New Cell & Molecular Biotech Co.,Ltd, C6005) following the manufacturer’s instructions and incubating for 1 h. Subsequently, the absorbance of the samples was measured at 450 nm using a spectrophotometer, and the relative proliferation rate of the cells was calculated based on the absorbance values.

Colony formation

For the cell colony formation assay, 1000 OS cells were seeded in a 6-well plate. The medium was changed every 2–3 days, and the cells were cultured for 7–10 days. Subsequently, the cell colonies were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min and stained with crystal violet for 30 min at room temperature. The number of cell colonies was quantified using Image J software.

Wound healing assays

OS cells (3 × 105 cells per well) were seeded into 6-well plates. When cell density reached 100%, a sterile 200 µL pipette tip was used to make scratches in the monolayer of OS cells. At the 0- and 24-hour time points, a microscope was used to image the same location on the 6-well plate. Wound healing rate was measured as the average distance between the two edges of the cell-free area.

Animal study

Five- to six-week-old male BALB/c nude mice were used for the animal studies, which were approved by the Second Xiangya Hospital, Central South University. To establish a subcutaneous xenograft tumor model, human osteosarcoma 143B cells (5 × 106) were subcutaneously injected into the right posterior flank of the mice. The mice were then randomly assigned to different groups. Investigators responsible for tumor measurement were blinded to group allocation during data collection and analysis. Mice were monitored daily, and humane endpoints were predefined. Animals were euthanized if excessive tumor burden or signs of distress were observed. Tumor volumes were measured using the formula π/6 × length × width². At the conclusion of the experiments, the mice were euthanized, and the tumors were excised and weighed.

Flow cytometry

Flow cytometry was used to analyze macrophage surface and intracellular markers. Cells were washed twice with ice-cold PBS and stained with Fixable Viability Dye eFluor™ 780 (65-0865-14, Thermo Fisher Scientific) and a PE-conjugated anti-CD86 antibody (12-0869-42, eBioscience; 12-0862-82, eBioscience) in staining buffer for 30 min. After two washes with PBS, cells were fixed for 30 min at 4 °C. Following fixation and permeabilization, intracellular staining was conducted using an APC-conjugated anti-CD206 antibody (17-2069-42, eBioscience;17-2061-82, eBioscience) for 30 min in the dark. Cells were then washed three times and resuspended in 200 µL of staining buffer for analysis on a Beckman CytoFLEX flow cytometer (A00-1-1102). Data were analyzed using FlowJo software.

Co-culture assay

To investigate the impact of macrophage polarization on tumor cell proliferation, THP-1 or RAW264.7 macrophages (transfected with siConrtol or siNCAPD3) were seeded in 6-well plates (LABSELECT, 11110) at a density of 5 × 10⁵ cells per well and cultured for 48 h. The supernatant was then collected and filtered through a 0.22 μm filter. The resulting macrophage-conditioned medium was mixed with an equal volume of fresh complete DMEM. This 1:1 mixture was subsequently used to treat tumor cells for functional assays, including CCK-8 proliferation, colony formation, and wound healing assays.

Conversely, to assess the impact of osteosarcoma cells on macrophage polarization, 143B or K7M2 cells (transfected with siControl or siNCAPD3) were seeded in 6-well plates at 5 × 10⁵ cells per well. After 48 h of culture, the supernatant was collected, processed by centrifugation and 0.22 μm filtration. The tumor cell-conditioned medium was mixed 1:1 with fresh complete DMEM. This mixture was then used to culture THP-1 or RAW264.7 macrophages for an additional 48 h. Following this treatment, macrophages were harvested for analysis of polarization markers via RT-qPCR and flow cytometry.

Statistical analysis

Statistical significance was determined using two-tailed tests, with a P value < 0.05 considered statistically significant. Comparisons between two independent groups were performed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Paired comparisons were analyzed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Comparisons among three groups were conducted using the Kruskal–Wallis test followed by Dunn’s post hoc test. Survival analysis was performed using the Kaplan–Meier method, and differences between groups were assessed using the log-rank test. All statistical analyses and data visualization were performed using R software (version 4.2.1).

Results

NCAPD3 is upregulated in OS and is associated with prognosis

An overview of the experimental design and study workflow is shown in Fig. 1. To evaluate the role of NCAPD3 across multiple cancer types, we first analyzed its expression using TCGA dataset. The pan-cancer expression analysis (Fig. 2A) revealed that NCAPD3 is significantly upregulated in the majority of tumor types compared to normal tissue.

To further assess the potential prognostic value of NCAPD3, we conducted univariate Cox regression analysis across different cancers. The results (Fig. 2B) indicated that high NCAPD3 expression is associated with poor prognosis in several cancers, including adrenocortical carcinoma (ACC), kidney renal papillary cell carcinoma (KIRP), lung squamous cell carcinoma (LUSC), pancreatic adenocarcinoma (PAAD), and sarcoma (SARC). Given our specific focus on osteosarcoma, we proceeded to analyze NCAPD3 expression within osteosarcoma samples using the TARGET database. We observed that NCAPD3 is highly expressed in osteosarcoma tissues (Fig. 2C). Nevertheless, there was no notable variation in the expression of NCAPD3 among OS patients with diverse clinical characteristics (Figure S1A-C).

Next, we examined the prognostic impact of NCAPD3 expression on osteosarcoma patient outcomes by generating K-M survival curves (Fig. 2D). The results demonstrated that patients with high NCAPD3 expression had significantly worse overall survival compared to those with low NCAPD3 expression. Furthermore, we assessed the predictive accuracy of NCAPD3 expression for 1-year, 3-year, and 5-year survival in osteosarcoma patients using time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves. The area under the curve (AUC) values were 0.620, 0.661, and 0.656, respectively (Fig. 2E). Furthermore, univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses confirmed that NCAPD3 expression serves as an independent prognostic indicator for patients with osteosarcoma (Figure S1D).

Prognostic nomogram and immune correlation analysis

In order to enhance the accuracy of overall survival prognosis prediction, we developed a nomogram based on NCAPD3 expression and various clinical factors, including age, gender, and metastasis status. This nomogram enabled the prediction of 1-year, 3-year, and 5-year survival rates for osteosarcoma patients (Fig. 3A). Both metastasis status and NCAPD3 expression showed significant contributions to the model. The calibration curve (Fig. 3B) demonstrated that the nomogram had predictive accuracy for osteosarcoma survival outcomes. Meanwhile, the ROC curve (Fig. 3C) demonstrated that the nomogram’s AUC values for 1-year, 3-year, and 5-year survival were 0.906, 0.806, and 0.798, respectively, indicating satisfactory predictive performance. Moreover, the DCA curve suggested that the nomogram significantly contributes to clinical decision-making (Figure S1E). Additionally, we found that NCAPD3 was correlated with the survival time of older than or equal to 16 years old, male, and non-metastatic osteosarcoma patients. In comparison, among those younger than 16 years old, women and patients with metastases, the expression of NCAPD3 did not seem to be associated with the survival time (Figure S1F).

We also investigated the relationship between NCAPD3 expression and tumor microenvironment components. Tumor purity scores were significantly positively correlated with NCAPD3 expression (Fig. 3D). As shown in Fig. 3E, the stromal, immune, and ESTIMATE scores of osteosarcoma patients in the low NCAPD3 expression group were higher compared to those in the high NCAPD3 expression group.

To further explore immune-related functions, we performed ssGSEA analysis. The results (Fig. 3F) indicated that most immune-related functions and immune cell infiltration were stronger in patients with low NCAPD3 expression compared to those with high NCAPD3 expression. Additionally, we evaluated the association of NCAPD3 with several immune checkpoints. The expression levels of CD48, HAVCR2, LAIR1, CD40LG, and LGALS9 were higher in the low NCAPD3 expression group, and these immune checkpoints exhibited a significant negative correlation with NCAPD3 expression (Fig. 3G).

Single-cell analysis of NCAPD3 in OS

To further investigate the cellular context of NCAPD3 expression, we performed single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) analysis on six OS samples from the GSE162454 dataset. Rigorous quality control was applied, and the numbers of cells before and after filtering for each sample are shown in Figure S2A. We performed principal component analysis (PCA) on the top 2,000 genes with varying characteristics; we successfully reduced the complexity (Figure S2B). After batch correction, the UMAP plot showed successful integration of the samples (Fig. 4A). Cell clustering analysis identified 14 distinct cell clusters (Fig. 4B). We visualized the highly variable TOP 5 genes of each cluster (Figure S2C). Based on known immune cell markers, we annotated the clusters and found that NCAPD3 was highly expressed in cluster 3, which was identified as macrophages. This cluster was subsequently named NCAPD3 + macrophages (Fig. 4C). Further annotation of non-immune cells revealed that NCAPD3 was highly expressed in cluster 5, representing tumor cells. This cluster was named NCAPD3 + tumor cells (Fig. 4D). The resulting UMAP plot, showing the annotated clusters, is presented in Fig. 4E.

To gain further insight into the functional characteristics of NCAPD3 + macrophages and tumor cells, we analyzed the expression of specific markers. In NCAPD3 + macrophages, anti-inflammatory factors such as IL4 and ARG1 showed no significant differences compared to other macrophages, whereas pro-inflammatory factors TNF and IL1B were significantly decreased (Fig. 4F). In NCAPD3 + tumor cells, tumor proliferation markers MKI67 and PCNA were significantly upregulated, while tumor suppressor markers TP53 and CAS3 were significantly downregulated (Fig. 4F).

Function analysis based on single-cell data

To explore pathway differences between NCAPD3 + cells and their respective counterparts, we conducted pathway enrichment analyses based on single-cell data. First, we performed inflammatory pathway analysis on NCAPD3 + macrophages versus other macrophages. The results (Fig. 5A) showed that pathways such as HALLMARK_IL2_STAT5_SIGNALING, HALLMARK_IL6_JAK_STAT3_SIGNALING, and HALLMARK_INFLAMMATORY_ RESPONSE were significantly enriched in regular macrophages compared to NCAPD3 + macrophages. Next, we analyzed cancer-related pathways in NCAPD3 + tumor cells compared to other tumor cells (Fig. 5B). We observed that the HALLMARK_APOPTOSIS pathway was significantly downregulated in NCAPD3 + tumor cells, while pathways such as HALLMARK_DNA_REPAIR and HALLMARK_PI3K_AKT_MTOR_SIGNALING were significantly upregulated. These findings suggest potential mechanisms by which NCAPD3 might reduce the anti-tumor inflammatory properties of macrophages and increase the malignancy potential of tumor cells.

Further, we performed KEGG and GO enrichment analysis on differentially expressed genes in NCAPD3 + macrophages compared to other macrophages (Fig. 5C, Supplementary Materials 2). The results indicated that NCAPD3 may influence macrophage polarization and function through pathways such as Rheumatoid arthritis, Phagosome, and Tuberculosis. Similarly, KEGG and GO enrichment analysis of NCAPD3 + tumor cells versus other tumor cells revealed enrichment in pathways including the MAPK signaling pathway, PI3K/AKT signaling pathway, and p53 signaling pathway, suggesting that NCAPD3 may promote tumor cell proliferation and affect patient prognosis through these mechanisms (Fig. 5D, Supplementary Materials 3).

Additionally, drug sensitivity analysis revealed differences in sensitivity to specific drugs based on NCAPD3 expression levels (Fig. 5E). Patients in the low NCAPD3 expression group had higher IC50 values for doxorubicin, sunitinib, vinorelbine, and ispinesib Mesylate compared to the high expression group. Conversely, the IC50 values for (5Z)-7-Oxozeaenol, CI-1040, CGP-60,474, and JW-7-52-1 were lower in the low NCAPD3 expression group compared to the high expression group.

NCAPD3 promotes malignant biological behaviors in OS cells

Next, we validated the expression of NCAPD3 in OS using transcriptome sequencing and RT-qPCR. Transcriptome sequencing data from 13 normal tissues and 23 tumor tissues showed that NCAPD3 expression in OS was significantly higher than in the normal control group (Fig. 6A-B). Consistently, sequencing results from 13 paired tissues also indicated that NCAPD3 expression was upregulated in OS (Fig. 6C). Additionally, we found that the expression of NCAPD3 in OS cell lines was significantly higher compared to control hBMSC as detected by RT-qPCR (Fig. 6D).

We then investigated the impact of NCAPD3 on OS cells through in vitro experiments. Western blot analysis demonstrated that NCAPD3 expression in OS cell lines 143B and HOS was successfully downregulated using CRISPR-Cas9 (Fig. 6E). Subsequently, the CCK-8 assay revealed a significant decrease in the proliferation rate of 143B and HOS cells after NCAPD3 expression was downregulated (Figs. 6F-G). Furthermore, the cell colony formation assay demonstrated a lower number of colonies formed by NCAPD3 knockout cells compared to the control group (143B: sgControl: 164.0 ± 4.5, sgNCAPD3#1: 98.3 ± 5.6, sgNCAPD3#2: 118.7 ± 2.6; HOS: sgControl: 180.0 ± 5.0, sgNCAPD3#1: 62.3 ± 7.5, sgNCAPD3#2: 123 ± 11.6, Figs. 6H-J). Additionally, the wound healing assay indicated that at 24 h, the average wound healing percentage for 143B and HOS cells in the sgControl group was 71.95% and 41.32%, respectively, while the average percentages for 143B and HOS in the sgNCAPD3#1 and sgNCAPD3#2 groups were 45.21%, 29.62%, 40.92%, and 25.13%, respectively (Figs. 6K-N).

In vivo tumor formation results indicated that the tumor volume and tumor size in the NCAPD3 knockdown group were significantly reduced compared to the control group (Figs. 6O-Q).

Collectively, these findings indicate that NCAPD3 is upregulated in OS and contributes to enhanced proliferation and migration abilities of OS cells.

NCAPD3 knockdown promotes pro-inflammatory and reduces anti-inflammatory responses in macrophages

Knockdown of NCAPD3 using siRNA in RAW264.7 cells significantly increased the mRNA expression of pro-inflammatory markers Cd86, Cd80, Il-1β, and Tnfα compared to controls (Fig. 7A-B). Simultaneously, NCAPD3 knockdown notably decreased the mRNA levels of anti-inflammatory markers Cd206, Il-10, and Tgfβ in RAW264.7 cells (Fig. 7C). Similar results were observed in primary macrophages, with enhanced expression of pro-inflammatory markers (Cd86, Cd80, Il-1β, Tnfα; Fig. 7D-E) and reduced expression of anti-inflammatory markers (Cd206, Il-10, Tgfβ; Fig. 7F).

To further validate these findings at the cellular level, flow cytometry analysis revealed a significant increase in the proportion of CD86⁺ (M1-like) macrophages following NCAPD3 knockdown in both RAW264.7 and THP-1–derived macrophages (Fig. 7G). At the protein level, WB analysis further confirmed efficient NCAPD3 knockdown in RAW264.7 and THP-1–derived macrophages (Figure S3A). In parallel, the expression of the pro-inflammatory marker CD86 was increased upon NCAPD3 silencing in both macrophage models (Figure S3B).

Collectively, these results demonstrate that NCAPD3 knockdown shifts macrophages toward a pro-inflammatory phenotype at the transcriptional, cellular, and protein levels, supporting a role for NCAPD3 in restraining macrophage inflammatory activation within the osteosarcoma tumor microenvironment.

NCAPD3-dependent tumor–macrophage crosstalk regulates osteosarcoma cell proliferation and migration

Conditioned media (CM) experiments were performed to evaluate the functional crosstalk between macrophages and osteosarcoma cells. CCK-8 assays showed that 143B cells cultured with CM derived from NCAPD3-knockdown THP-1–derived macrophages exhibited significantly reduced cell viability compared with cells treated with control macrophage CM (Fig. 8A). Similarly, K7M2 cells displayed decreased proliferation when cultured with CM from NCAPD3-silenced RAW264.7 macrophages (Fig. 8B). Consistent with these findings, colony formation assays demonstrated that CM from NCAPD3-knockdown macrophages markedly reduced the clonogenic capacity of both 143B and K7M2 cells, as shown by representative images and quantitative analysis of colony numbers per field (Fig. 8C). Similarly, conditioned media from NCAPD3-knockdown macrophages markedly impaired wound closure in OS cells (Figure S3C).

To examine the reciprocal effects of tumor-derived signals on macrophages, macrophages were cultured with CM from osteosarcoma cells. Flow cytometry analysis showed that conditioned media from NCAPD3-suppressed osteosarcoma cells increased the proportion of CD86⁺ macrophages in both RAW264.7 and THP-1–derived macrophages, as evidenced by representative dot plots and quantitative analysis (Fig. 8D).

At the signaling level, Western blot analysis revealed reduced phosphorylation of AKT in 143B cells following NCAPD3 knockdown, while total AKT levels remained unchanged (Fig. 8E). In addition, NCAPD3 silencing in 143B cells resulted in increased γ-H2AX expression, indicating enhanced DNA damage–associated signaling (Fig. 8F).

Together, these results demonstrate that NCAPD3 modulates bidirectional interactions between osteosarcoma cells and macrophages, influencing tumor cell proliferation, migration, macrophage polarization, and intracellular signaling states.

Discussion

In children and adolescents, OS is the prevailing malignant tumor originating in the bones [27, 28]. Recent studies have increasingly emphasized the critical role of the tumor microenvironment (TME), particularly tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), in driving OS progression, metastasis, and therapeutic resistance [29–32]. In this study, we comprehensively investigated the role of NCAPD3 in both tumor cells and macrophages, revealing that NCAPD3 functions as a dual regulator of tumor proliferation and macrophage-mediated immune suppression. NCAPD3 was found to be highly expressed in OS tissues and associated with poorer overall survival. Functional assays in vitro and in vivo further confirmed that NCAPD3 knockdown markedly inhibits OS cell proliferation and migration, underscoring its potential as a prognostic biomarker.

We identified a unique subpopulation of NCAPD3⁺ macrophages characterized by reduced inflammatory activity, evidenced by lower expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNFα and IL-1β. These findings suggest that NCAPD3 contributes to an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment, potentially facilitating tumor immune evasion and progression. To experimentally validate these observations, we performed NCAPD3 knockdown in RAW264.7 macrophages, primary murine macrophages, and human THP-1–derived macrophages. The results consistently demonstrated increased expression of pro-inflammatory markers and decreased expression of anti-inflammatory markers, supported at both the transcriptional and protein levels, confirming that NCAPD3 restrains macrophage inflammatory activation.

Beyond macrophage-intrinsic effects, our conditioned media experiments further revealed a bidirectional interaction between tumor cells and macrophages regulated by NCAPD3. Macrophage-derived conditioned media following NCAPD3 knockdown significantly suppressed osteosarcoma cell proliferation, clonogenic capacity, and migration, while conditioned media from NCAPD3-silenced tumor cells promoted a pro-inflammatory macrophage phenotype. These findings suggest that NCAPD3 contributes to tumor–immune crosstalk within the OS microenvironment, rather than acting solely through cell-autonomous mechanisms. Conceptually, our data support a model in which NCAPD3 operates at the interface of chromatin organization and immune modulation. In tumor cells, NCAPD3 promotes proliferative signaling and supports genomic integrity under oncogenic stress. In macrophages, NCAPD3 appears to constrain inflammatory transcriptional programs, thereby contributing to a relatively immunosuppressive microenvironment. The integration of these tumor-intrinsic and immune-regulatory effects may collectively facilitate osteosarcoma progression.

Consistent with prior reports in other malignancies, NCAPD3⁺ tumor cells in our study exhibited enhanced proliferative characteristics, including upregulation of proliferation-associated markers such as MKI67 and PCNA and enrichment of oncogenic signaling pathways. Previous studies have primarily linked NCAPD3 to cell-cycle regulation, chromosomal condensation, and mitotic progression in cancer cells [33]. Our work extends these findings by integrating tumor-intrinsic proliferative programs with immune-regulatory effects in osteosarcoma, supported by evidence of altered PI3K/AKT signaling and increased γ-H2AX expression following NCAPD3 knockdown [34]. These molecular alterations may contribute to the observed growth-inhibitory and migration-suppressive effects in OS cells.

Taken together, this study advances the understanding of NCAPD3 in osteosarcoma by positioning it at the intersection of tumor cell proliferation and macrophage-mediated immune regulation, rather than as a purely mitotic regulator. By combining bulk and single-cell transcriptomics with functional validation in multiple macrophage and tumor models, our findings highlight NCAPD3 as a context-dependent modulator of tumor–immune interactions in OS. Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, although our data demonstrate consistent functional effects of NCAPD3 on macrophage inflammatory states and tumor–macrophage crosstalk, the precise molecular mechanisms through which NCAPD3 regulates macrophage transcriptional programs remain to be fully elucidated. Second, while both murine and human macrophage models were employed, validation in primary human osteosarcoma tissues and patient-derived immune cells will be required to strengthen clinical relevance. Third, drug sensitivity analyses were based on in silico prediction models, and no direct experimental validation was performed; therefore, these results should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating and warrant further experimental confirmation. Finally, our analyses primarily relied on retrospective transcriptomic datasets and experimental models; prospective studies will be necessary to determine the translational potential of targeting NCAPD3 in osteosarcoma.

Conclusions

In conclusion, NCAPD3 promotes osteosarcoma progression through coordinated effects on tumor cell growth and suppression of macrophage inflammatory responses. Its expression correlates with clinical outcomes and reflects both tumor-intrinsic and microenvironmental features of OS. These findings suggest that NCAPD3 represents a biologically meaningful biomarker and a potential therapeutic target, and they provide a foundation for future studies aimed at dissecting the molecular pathways through which chromatin-associated regulators influence tumor–immune crosstalk in osteosarcoma.
