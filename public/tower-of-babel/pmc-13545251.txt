Upcycling copper chips into Cu–Fe alloys via coating-assisted plasma sintering
Authors: M R Sabour, E Taherkhani, H Rahimi, A R Khatami, G Faraji
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractUpcycling metal machining waste into functional engineering materials offers a sustainable pathway for value-added manufacturing. In this work, a new coated chip plasma sintering (CCPS) strategy was introduced to produce Cu–Fe alloys from discarded copper chips. Copper chips generated by machining were first coated with Fe by arc physical vapor deposition and then consolidated through cold compaction followed by spark plasma sintering. The machining process imposed severe plastic deformation on the Cu chips, producing an ultrafine microstructure prior to consolidation. During sintering, disruption of the Fe coating and surface oxides facilitated direct metallic contact and accelerated interfacial diffusion, enabling effective densification and bonding. The consolidated alloy contained a Cu-rich matrix with a small Fe addition (~ 0.25 wt%) and exhibited twins, refined grains, and dispersed Fe/oxide phases. These features resulted in marked strengthening, with the alloy reaching a yield strength of ~ 280 MPa, ultimate tensile strength of ~ 307.9 MPa, and hardness of ~ 116.5 HV. Although elongation decreased to ~ 11.6%, the alloy retained relatively high electrical conductivity with only a slight increase in resistivity. The proposed CCPS route demonstrates an efficient and sustainable method for converting copper waste into Cu-based alloys with an attractive combination of structural and electrical properties.Keywords: Upcycling metal chips, Cu-Fe alloy, Electrical conductivity, Interfacial bondingSubject terms: Engineering, Materials science

Introduction

Copper remains one of the most widely used engineering materials owing to its high electrical and thermal conductivity, excellent corrosion resistance, and good formability. These attributes make copper indispensable in power transmission systems, electronic devices, heat exchangers, and transportation components1,2. The sustained growth of such applications has intensified global copper consumption, raising concerns related to the environmental burden, energy demand, and resource limitations associated with primary copper production. Consequently, the development of efficient recycling and upcycling strategies has become a central objective in sustainable materials engineering. Recycling copper offers substantial advantages over primary extraction, including significantly lower energy consumption and reduced greenhouse gas emissions3. Torrubia et al. evaluated the life cycle environmental performance of copper recycling from electronic waste4. Their assessment reported carbon footprint reductions of 71–96% for recycled copper compared with primary production, highlighting the sensitivity of these gains to processing scenarios and energy inputs. Another study conducted a comprehensive life-cycle assessment of copper production and demonstrated substantial environmental benefits associated with increased recycling5. Results showed that secondary copper production, relative to primary extraction, significantly reduces greenhouse gas emissions and energy consumption across the material’s life cycle, reinforcing the environmental rationale for recycling.

Among various forms of copper scrap, machining chips constitute a large fraction of industrial waste. Unlike end-of-life components, machining chips are generated continuously and possess a distinctive microstructural state resulting from the extreme deformation imposed during chip formation6. Several studies have demonstrated that the shear strains generated during machining can reach values comparable to those achieved in established severe plastic deformation (SPD) techniques, leading to pronounced grain refinement and high defect densities7–9. Courbon et al. reviewed the evolution of microstructure in machined metal chips, reporting that severe shear loading leads to notable grain refinement10. Electron microscopy analysis indicated the presence of equiaxed ultrafine grains along with a high density of high-angle grain boundaries in these chips. Zhilyaev et al. evaluated how copper microstructure and hardness evolve under extreme strain, comparing conventional SPD techniques with chip formation during machining11. Their work revealed that both processing methods produced ultrafine-grained structures with grain sizes smaller than 1 μm and high defect densities, hallmark features of severely deformed materials. Despite these advantages, the direct consolidation of copper chips remains technically challenging. The presence of stable surface oxide layers, combined with irregular chip geometries and limited effective contact areas, significantly inhibits metallurgical bonding during consolidation12,13.

Conventional recycling routes based on remelting are energy-intensive and often lead to oxidation losses, grain coarsening, and degradation of mechanical properties14–16. For these reasons, solid-state recycling approaches have gained increasing attention as alternatives capable of preserving the refined microstructure of chips while minimizing energy input16,17. A range of solid-state consolidation techniques has been explored for metallic chip recycling, including hot extrusion, friction stir processing, and spark plasma sintering (SPS)18–20. Among these, SPS has emerged as a particularly effective method due to its unique combination of rapid heating rates, short processing times, and the simultaneous application of uniaxial pressure and pulsed direct current21. These features promote enhanced diffusion, localized joule heating at interparticle contacts, and disruption of surface oxide layers, thereby facilitating densification and interfacial bonding22,23. Benkli and Korkmaz examined the direct recycling of industrial copper chips containing ~ 5% magnesium via an innovative patented modification of the classical solid-state sintering technique24. This approach preserved over 97% of the metallic copper phase, minimized oxide formation, and resulted in a uniform microstructure. SEM characterization revealed a homogeneous microstructure with crystallite size and morphology controlled by the sintering parameters. Zhu et al. employed SPS to produce fully dense copper samples in a nearly fully recrystallized state, with grain sizes in the sub-micrometer to micrometer range25. Using spherical powders with an average particle diameter of approximately 0.5 μm, the resulting samples exhibited average grain sizes between 0.5 and 3 μm. Microstructural analysis confirmed that the grains were dislocation-free, randomly oriented, and featured a high fraction of high-angle grain boundaries. In their study, Monnier et al. examined SPS processing of nanostructured copper powders in both oxide-covered and oxide-reduced conditions26. The as-received powder attained only about 90% density at 650 °C, a limitation attributed to surface oxide insulation, accompanied by severe grain growth that led to early failure under compressive loading. Conversely, the H₂-annealed powder achieved the same density at 260 °C while suppressing grain growth, resulting in improved microstructural stability.

However, recycled pure copper typically exhibits a trade-off between strength and electrical conductivity. While grain refinement and defect accumulation significantly enhance mechanical strength, they also introduce numerous electron scattering sites, such as grain boundaries and dislocations, leading to a reduction in electrical conductivity27,28. To overcome this limitation, alloying strategies have been proposed to strengthen copper while maintaining acceptable electrical performance. Among potential alloying elements, iron has attracted particular interest due to its low solubility in copper, high intrinsic strength, and relatively moderate effect on electrical conductivity when added in small amounts1,29. Cu–Fe alloys have been extensively investigated using casting, powder metallurgy, and deformation-based processing routes. In the work of Wang et al., a Cu–10 wt% Fe alloy was fabricated using double melt mixed casting and subsequent multi-step thermomechanical treatment to achieve a favorable balance between strength and conductivity30. Nanoscale Fe precipitates (20–150 nm) formed during the aging process, contributing to a tensile strength of 608 MPa and a yield strength of 583 MPa, while maintaining an electrical conductivity of 58% IACS. Another study examined the effects of varying Fe content (5, 10, 20, and 40 wt%) on the microstructure and mechanical–electrical properties of powder metallurgy Cu–Fe alloys fabricated by SPS, followed by cold rolling and aging treatments31. With increasing Fe content, the Fe phase morphology evolved from discrete spheroidal particles to a continuous staggered network, accompanied by an increase in particle size from 0.29 to 1.20 μm. Consequently, the yield strength of the aged alloys increased from 411.5 to 788.8 MPa, while the electrical conductivity decreased from 62.5% to 42.0% IACS.

Despite these advances, the combined use of coated metallic chips and plasma-assisted sintering techniques has received limited attention, particularly for copper-based systems. The interaction between coating fragmentation, oxide disruption, electric-field-assisted diffusion, and retained chip boundaries during SPS provides unique opportunities for tailoring microstructure and properties. Moreover, the effect of introducing iron via surface coating rather than bulk alloying on the mechanical–electrical property balance of recycled copper has not been systematically explored. In this work, a novel upcycling route termed Coated Chip Plasma Sintering (CCPS) is proposed for the fabrication of high-performance Cu–Fe alloys from waste copper chips. Unlike conventional alloying methods, iron is introduced in a controlled and localized manner, enabling effective strengthening at a very low overall Fe content while minimizing adverse effects on electrical conductivity. The CCPS process exploits the combined effects of severe plastic deformation inherited from machining, coating-induced reinforcement, oxide layer fragmentation, and pressure- and temperature-assisted diffusion during SPS. The primary objectives of this study are to develop a sustainable solid-state route for upcycling waste copper chips, to elucidate the microstructural evolution and bonding mechanisms active during CCPS, and to evaluate the resulting mechanical and electrical properties in comparison with annealed copper and reported Cu–Fe systems. The results demonstrate that CCPS offers a versatile and environmentally responsible strategy for transforming low-value copper scrap into functional materials with a favorable balance of strength and conductivity.

Experimental procedure

Pure copper rods were first annealed in a furnace at 600 °C for 2 h to relieve internal stresses and improve ductility. Following annealing, the rods were processed using a turning machine to produce copper chips. The turning operation was performed without lubrication to prevent oil contamination of the material. The resulting copper chips were subsequently cleaned in an ultrasonic bath containing pure acetone to remove residual oils, adsorbed contaminants, and native oxide layers. After cleaning, the samples were dried under ambient conditions.

Iron coating of the copper chips was carried out using arc physical vapor deposition (arc PVD), as schematically illustrated in Fig. 1. An iron target was mounted inside the vacuum chamber and served as the evaporation source. The chamber was evacuated to the required vacuum level prior to deposition, after which an arc discharge was initiated. The deposition parameters were maintained at a current of 90 A and a voltage of 200 V throughout the process. Under these conditions, ionized iron species were generated from the target and deposited uniformly onto the surfaces of the crumpled copper chips. The deposition duration was fixed at 60 min to ensure sufficient and homogeneous iron coverage within the interconnected chip network. A neutral gas atmosphere was introduced to stabilize the plasma and maintain controlled deposition conditions.

Illustration of the arc physical vapor deposition (arc PVD) system employed for coating copper chips with iron. The setup includes a vacuum chamber equipped with an iron target, high-voltage power supply, vacuum pumping unit, and neutral gas inlet.

Figure 1 Schematic illustration of the arc PVD system used for iron coating on the copper chips surface. The setup consists of a vacuum chamber equipped with an iron target, high-voltage power supply, vacuum pumping system, and neutral gas inlet. Ionized iron species generated by the arc discharge are deposited uniformly onto the copper chips placed on the substrate holder.

After coating, the iron-coated copper chips were carefully transferred into a cylindrical compaction mold, as shown in Fig. 2a. Cold compaction was performed at room temperature to produce a green compact of the Fe–Cu system (Fig. 2b). This step promoted intimate contact between neighboring coated chips and enhanced the overall packing density of the compact. The green compact was subsequently consolidated using spark plasma sintering (SPS) to obtain a dense Fe–Cu alloy. The SPS process was conducted under vacuum using a graphite die and graphite punches, as schematically depicted in Fig. 2c. During sintering, a uniaxial pressure was applied simultaneously with a pulsed direct current, enabling rapid Joule heating and enhanced interdiffusion between iron and copper. The temperature was increased at a constant heating rate of 100 °C min⁻¹ up to 780 °C and maintained at this temperature for 7 min. The combined action of pressure, electric current, and elevated temperature facilitated strong metallurgical bonding and alloy formation while effectively suppressing excessive grain growth. Through this processing route, iron coating of copper chips followed by cold compaction and SPS consolidation, which results a dense Fe–Cu alloy with a well-bonded microstructure was successfully fabricated. Above was the principle of the coated chip plasma sintering (CCPS).

Schematic illustration of the fabrication process of the Fe–Cu alloy. (a) Loading of iron-coated copper chips into a cylindrical mold for cold compaction, (b) formation of the green compact at room temperature, and (c) spark plasma sintering (SPS) setup.

Following sintering, the consolidated Fe–Cu alloy specimens were subjected to comprehensive characterization to evaluate their electrical, mechanical, and microstructural properties. Electrical conductivity measurements were performed to assess the influence of iron incorporation and alloy formation on charge transport behavior. Mechanical properties were evaluated using hardness and tensile tests to determine strength, deformation behavior, and load-bearing capability. Vickers microhardness measurements were carried out using a load of 200 g (HV0.2) and a dwell time of 10 s. Multiple indentations were performed on each sample, and the reported values represent the average hardness.

Microstructural analysis was conducted using scanning electron microscopy (SEM) to examine phase distribution, interfacial bonding, and microstructural features, while optical microscopy (OM) was employed to observe overall morphology, grain size, densification quality, and structural homogeneity. Prior to microscopic examination, polished samples were etched using a solution composed of 25 mL distilled water, 25 mL ammonium hydroxide, and 15 mL hydrogen peroxide (3%). These characterization techniques enabled a detailed correlation between processing conditions, resulting microstructure, and the final properties of the Fe–Cu alloy.

Results and discussion

Figure 3 presents the microstructure observed in commercially pure copper following a machining operation. Significant grain refinement is observed within the microstructure of the processed pure copper. The processed pure copper exhibits significant microstructural evolution, characterized by severe grain refinement. While the annealed starting material has a reported average grain size of approximately 59 μm32, machining reduces this to 2.5 μm in the chip’s central regions and further to 1.4 μm at the outer regions (Fig. 3). The refined microstructure consists predominantly of submicron-scale grains, with a majority measuring below 0.5 μm. It is important to mention that, the distinctive strain regime imposed by machining qualifies it as a severe plastic deformation (SPD) process33. The deformation mechanics inherent to chip formation during machining share key characteristics with established SPD processes like equal channel angular pressing (ECAP)34. The machining configuration permits the imposition of considerable shear strain, with reported values spanning approximately 1 to 12, depending on specific processing conditions35. Significant grain refinement in pure copper due to machining is a consistently reported phenomenon in materials research35,36. At lower strain levels within the machining process, nano-scale dislocation substructures develop, as evidenced by the TEM analysis reported by Brown et al.35. Further straining facilitates the reorganization of dislocations, resulting in cellular structures and elongated subgrains. The forest dislocations within their diffuse boundaries then stabilize into defined geometrically necessary boundaries37. Further straining ultimately produces an equiaxed grain/subgrain structure characterized by greater misorientations. An equiaxed grain morphology at the fine scale is clearly evident within the microstructure of the chip’s outer regions, see Fig. 3. Concurrently, boundary misorientations increase substantially, leading to a dominant population of high-angle grain boundaries (HAGBs) as strain is increased38,39. The observed microstructural features are indicative of continuous dynamic recrystallization (CDRX) occurring within the severely deformed regions of the machined chip. Notably, the central region exhibits a heterogeneous microstructure. Alongside the prevalent fine, equiaxed grains, several coarser and elongated grains persist, indicating incomplete recrystallization or localized variations in strain. It is noteworthy that the grain size distribution indicates that approximately 3.5% of the grains exceed 9 μm in size. The heat produced intrinsically during deformation, combined with increasing strain, significantly contributes to the activation of discontinuous dynamic recrystallization (DDRX)40. As strain and deformation temperature continue to rise, the microstructure evolves toward the presence of large grains on the micrometer scale.

Following the coating process, the coated copper chips were examined using scanning electron microscopy (SEM) to evaluate the surface morphology, coating continuity, and interfacial characteristics. SEM analysis provides detailed insight into the distribution and uniformity of the Fe coating. Accordingly, Fig. 4 is provided to illustrate the SEM images of the coated chips. The SEM observations indicate the presence of a narrow gap at the interface between the Fe coating and the Cu chip. The presence of this interfacial gap is likely associated with the mechanical polishing step employed during sample preparation. Mechanical polishing can induce localized material removal or smearing at the coating–substrate interface, particularly when there is a hardness mismatch between the Fe coating and the Cu substrate. Such effects may lead to partial detachment or the formation of artificial gaps that do not necessarily reflect the true interfacial condition in the as-coated state. Therefore, the observed gap should be interpreted with caution, as it may be a consequence of sample preparation rather than an intrinsic feature of the coating–substrate interface. The formation of oxide layers on the surface of the Cu chips and on the Fe coating is another factor that may influence the observed interfacial characteristics.

(a) SEM images of Cu chips coated with Fe, showing surface morphology, (b,c) corresponding EDS elemental maps, and (e,f) EDS point analysis results.

Copper readily forms a native oxide layer when exposed to air or subjected to machining process, and iron is similarly prone to surface oxidation during processing and subsequent handling. It should be highlighted that the presence of these oxide layers can hinder the formation of complete bonding, as they impede direct contact between the virgin material surfaces14,41. Overcoming this limitation necessitates the disruption or removal of the continuous oxide layer to enable direct material contact42,43. Accordingly, it has been reported that the application of adequate deformation is necessary to fragment the oxide layer44–46. In the cold compaction stage, slight deformation of the Fe layer on the Cu chip surfaces causes elongation of the coating, which facilitates oxide layer fragmentation47. The fragmentation of the hard surface layers, including the Fe coating and the oxide layer, is suggested to result from a combination of mechanical stresses and the applied electric field during SPS, with the electric field playing a role in both layer fracture and material consolidation23,47. SEM images in Fig. 5 clearly shows the fragmentation of oxide layer and Fe coating. Following the fragmentation of the two barrier layers (Fe coating and oxide layer), the pressure applied during SPS facilitates copper flow through the formed cracks from neighboring chips. As copper extrudes through these cracks, intimate contact is established between adjacent chips, leading to the formation of initial sintering necks at the contact points. The combined application of elevated temperature and uniaxial pressure during SPS further enhances atomic mobility, thereby promoting diffusion across the newly formed interfaces. This diffusion-assisted neck growth strengthens the inter-chip bonding and contributes to progressive densification of the compact, as shown in Fig. 5. Consequently, the synergistic effects of pressure- and temperature-assisted diffusion during SPS activate diffusion bonding mechanisms, resulting in the development of a consolidated microstructure with improved metallurgical continuity between the Cu chips. It should be noted that the isolated dark features observed in the micrographs are attributed to polishing artifacts and/or residual contamination introduced during metallographic preparation rather than intrinsic porosity. The consolidated samples exhibited a dense microstructure, and no evidence of significant processing-induced porosity was observed.

(a) Representative SEM micrographs of CCPS-ed Cu–Fe showing microstructural features, (b,c) EDS elemental maps, and (e,f) EDS point analyses at selected regions.

The consolidation of the machined copper chips into cohesive compact proceeds through a multi-step bonding formation procedure, driven primarily by the synergistic application of uniaxial pressure and elevated temperature during Spark Plasma Sintering (SPS), see Fig. 6. The initial barrier to bonding is the presence of native oxide layers on the Cu chip surfaces and the applied Fe coating, both of which impede direct metal-to-metal contact. During the initial cold compaction stage, the applied pressure induces slight deformation of the Fe layer, causing its elongation. This mechanical straining is the first step in fragmenting the continuous oxide scale. Subsequently, during the SPS process, the combination of mechanical stresses and the applied electric field lead to the definitive fragmentation of both the hard Fe coating and the underlying oxide layer. This fragmentation is critical, as it creates cracks and pathways through these barrier layers. With these barriers breached, the applied uniaxial pressure forces the softer copper from neighboring chips to flow through the cracks. This extrusion of copper establishes direct, intimate contact between virgin chip surfaces at discrete points, forming initial sintering necks. The simultaneous application of high temperature during SPS then becomes the dominant factor. Elevated temperature dramatically increases atomic mobility, activating diffusion across the newly formed Cu-Cu interfaces. This diffusion-assisted process promotes neck growth, strengthens inter-chip bonding, and progressively eliminates porosity. Therefore, the final bonding formation procedure is not solely reliant on solid-state diffusion. Instead, it is a mechanically-assisted diffusion bonding mechanism: pressure first enables oxide fragmentation and copper flow to achieve intimate contact, while temperature subsequently drives atomic diffusion across these contact points, resulting in a consolidated microstructure with enhanced metallurgical continuity between the copper chips.

Representative of consolidation of Fe coated Cu chips during CCPS.

For a more detailed understanding of the microstructural development in the recycled Cu–Fe alloys, optical microscopy (OM) and EDS analysis conducted to examine their microstructural features, which is illustrated in Fig. 7. An initial observation reveals that the fine equiaxed, twin-free grains experience substantial microstructural evolution, resulting in the formation of relatively coarse grains with a high density of twins after SPS. In general, twinning is closely associated with stacking faults, which can act as heterogeneous nucleation sites for twins48. A similar observation was reported by Zhang et al.49, who attributed the extensive twin formation in SPS-processed copper to the combined effects of high applied pressure and the unique heating characteristics of the SPS technique. They further noted that, at a given pressure, the sintering temperature plays a dominant role in controlling the twin width.

(a) Representative OM micrograph of CCPS-processed Cu chips (b) distribution of twin thickness, (c–g) higher magnification of twins, and (h) corresponding EDS analysis.

It is worth noting that the twin thickness distribution indicates an average twin thickness of approximately 9.9 μm, with the majority of twins having thicknesses below 2.5 μm. Twin thickening occurs via thermally activated formation and expansion of dislocation loops at established twin boundaries48. Another point to consider is that, although SPS processing promotes grain growth, the presence of chip boundaries acts as a barrier to grain coarsening14. Also, the presence of precipitated Fe and fragmented oxide particles along the chip boundaries produces a Zener pinning effect, which limits grain boundary migration and restrains grain growth50,51. Additionally, EDS analysis confirms a Cu-rich composition of the Cu–Fe alloy, with approximately 0.25 wt% Fe present.

To assess the mechanical behavior of the Cu–Fe alloy produced through the novel upcycling process, tensile tests were performed, and the corresponding stress–strain curves, together with that of annealed Cu, are presented in Fig. 8. Initial observations indicate that the Cu–Fe alloy demonstrates markedly enhanced mechanical performance relative to annealed Cu. In terms of yield strength (YS), the Cu–Fe alloy exhibits a value of approximately 280 MPa, nearly five times higher than that of annealed Cu, which has a YS of 55.5 MPa. Furthermore, the ultimate tensile strength (UTS) of annealed Cu and Cu–Fe alloy are 216 MPa and 307.9 MPa, respectively, indicating that the Cu–Fe alloy exhibits a UTS nearly 42.5% higher than that of annealed Cu, see Fig. 8b. A comparison with previously reported Cu–Fe alloys further highlights the effectiveness of the CCPS processing route. Shi et al.52 reported that a Cu–1 wt% Fe alloy produced via conventional casting achieved a UTS of only 194.4 MPa. In contrast, the present study demonstrates a significantly higher UTS of 307.9 MPa, despite the much lower Fe content (0.25 wt%).

This represents an improvement of more than 58% over the cast alloy, clearly indicating the superior strengthening efficiency of the CCPS approach. Regarding the strengthening mechanisms of Cu–Fe alloys produced via SPS, it has been suggested that the primary contributors are solid solution strengthening and grain boundary strengthening31. The strengthening contribution from grain boundaries follows the Hall–Petch relationship, according to which the strength of a material is directly influenced by the size of its grains39,53,54. In terms of solid solution atoms, they strengthen the material by distorting the lattice and restricting the mobility of dislocations. Regarding this, it has been reported that the contribution of Fe to the strengthening of the Cu matrix is limited, amounting to approximately 1.2 MPa in the Cu–10Fe alloy30. Based on the microstructural observations in this study, additional strengthening contributions can be attributed to twin boundaries and heterogeneous Fe/oxide-containing regions. Annealing twins introduce additional coherent interfaces that act as effective barriers to dislocation motion, thereby contributing to strength enhancement. Meanwhile, Fe-rich regions and fragmented oxide particles located mainly at interfacial regions associated with the original chip boundaries introduce microstructural heterogeneity. These features are expected to contribute to strengthening through dislocation pile-up, interfacial constraint, and localized load-bearing effects. However, the quantitative contribution of each mechanism requires further investigation. It is also important to mention that, although the individual contribution of each strengthening mechanism cannot be quantitatively separated using the available characterization techniques, these features are known to impede dislocation motion and/or grain boundary migration and are therefore considered likely contributors to the observed strength increase.

As a case in point, previous studies have indicated that the presence of a high-strength phase, like the Fe phase in this work, contributes to improved mechanical performance55. Also, it was emphasized that the presence of oxide phases creates additional obstacles to dislocation glide, leading to dislocation accumulation at specific sites56. This promotes dislocation pile-up in the vicinity of oxide particles57, while simultaneously restricting grain boundary migration58,59. Such behavior is consistent with the established strengthening concepts of metal matrix composites (MMCs)47,60,61. However, it is noticing worthy that, among the potential strengthening mechanisms, grain boundary strengthening is expected to play the dominant role due to the substantial grain refinement introduced during machining and retained after CCPS processing. The increased density of grain boundaries provides effective barriers to dislocation motion in accordance with the Hall–Petch relationship. Additional strengthening may arise from annealing twins and the dispersion of Fe and oxide particles, which can further restrict dislocation movement. However, the quantitative contribution of particle-related strengthening mechanisms, cannot be determined from the present dataset and therefore remains the subject of future investigation.

It is important to highlight that, although the CCPS-processed Cu-Fe alloy shows a significant strength increase, but the ductility of the alloy decreased. While the CCPS-processed Cu–Fe alloy demonstrates markedly improved strength, this improvement occurs at the expense of ductility. Figure 8b illustrates a pronounced reduction in uniform elongation, decreasing from about 27% for annealed Cu to 11.6% for the Cu–Fe alloy. This reduction in uniform elongation can be attributed to the combined effects of the presence of Fe-rich and oxide particles in the CCPS-processed Cu–Fe alloy. The hard Fe phase and dispersed oxide particles introduce strain incompatibilities within the Cu matrix, which promote localized stress concentrations and accelerate strain localization62. As a result, although these microstructural features significantly enhance strength, they reduce the strain-hardening capacity of the alloy, thereby limiting uniform plastic deformation and leading to decreased ductility. In other words, oxide particles and interfacial imperfections can exert both beneficial and detrimental effects on mechanical behavior. While finely dispersed particles may contribute to strengthening by impeding dislocation motion and restricting grain boundary migration63, excessive particle agglomeration or interfacial defects may act as local stress concentrators64. The enhanced mechanical performance of the CCPS-processed Cu–Fe alloy is closely associated with the microstructural evolution induced during the coating, compaction, and SPS consolidation stages. Optical and electron microscopy reveal a dense consolidated microstructure characterized by refined grains, a high density of annealing twins, retained chip-boundary interfaces, and Fe-rich and oxide-containing interfacial regions. It should be noted that the isolated dark features observed in the micrographs are attributed to metallographic preparation artifacts rather than processing-induced porosity. Therefore, the observed improvement in strength and hardness is interpreted in terms of the experimentally observed microstructural characteristics rather than defect-related effects.

Similar to the strength results, Fig. 9a shows a substantial increase in hardness for the Cu–Fe alloy compared to annealed Cu. It is evident that the hardness increases from approximately 70 HV for annealed Cu to about 116.5 HV for the developed Cu–Fe alloy, indicating that the addition of only 0.25 wt% Fe increases the hardness of Cu by nearly 1.6 times. The observed increase in hardness of the Cu–Fe alloy can be attributed not only to the presence of Fe addition but also to the dispersion of oxide particles throughout the microstructure. These oxide particles act as effective obstacles to dislocation motion, promoting dislocation pile-ups and increasing the resistance to plastic deformation. By hindering dislocation glide, the dispersed oxides contribute to the overall strengthening of the alloy, complementing the effects of grain refinement, twin boundaries, and the high-strength Fe phase.

(a) Hardness measurements of annealed Cu and CCPS-processed Cu–Fe alloy, and (b) hardness–electrical conductivity relationship comparing the properties achieved in this work with those reported in the literature65–68.

To evaluate the electrical performance of the CCPS-processed Cu–Fe alloy, electrical conductivity measurements were conducted and compared with those of annealed Cu. This comparison allows for the assessment of how the incorporation of Fe and the resulting microstructural changes, including grain refinement, twin formation, and oxide dispersion, influence the electrical behavior of the alloy. The measured conductivity values provide insight into the trade-offs between mechanical strengthening and electron transport, setting the stage for a broader comparison of the achieved properties with previously reported values in the literature. In this regard, Table 1 presents the electrical properties measured for both annealed Cu and the Cu–Fe alloy produced via the novel CCPS process. It is evident that the electrical resistivity of the Cu–Fe alloy increased from 0.17 Ω·m for annealed Cu to approximately 0.19 Ω·m, leading to a corresponding reduction in electrical conductivity. The observed increase in electrical resistivity of the Cu–Fe alloy can be attributed to several electron scattering mechanisms introduced by the Fe addition and the microstructural features of the processed material. In Cu–Fe alloys, each 1 wt% of Fe dissolved in the Cu matrix increases resistivity by approximately 9.2 µΩ·cm31. Electron scattering arises from phonons, dislocations, interfaces such as grain boundaries and Cu–Fe boundaries, as well as from impurity atoms, specifically Fe. Given that the resistivity of pure Fe (≈ 9.61 µΩ·cm) is significantly higher than that of Cu (≈ 1.7 µΩ·cm), even a small fraction of Fe markedly affects electron transport69. Consequently, the electrical conductivity of the alloy is predominantly governed by the Cu matrix, with reductions resulting from scattering effects associated with Fe atoms and microstructural interfaces formed during the CCPS process. In addition to the Fe atoms, the dispersed oxide particles present in the Cu–Fe alloy contribute to the observed reduction in electrical conductivity. These oxides act as additional scattering centers for conduction electrons, impeding their motion through the Cu matrix.

To further evaluate the overall performance of the CCPS-processed Cu–Fe alloy, the relationship between hardness and electrical conductivity was analyzed and compared with values reported in the literature, which showed in Fig. 9b. It should be noted that the literature data correspond to Cu–Fe alloys with different Fe concentrations and processing histories, including conventional casting, rolling, and post-processing treatments. Since both composition and processing route strongly influence microstructural evolution and the resulting mechanical and electrical properties, the comparison presented here is intended to provide a general performance benchmark rather than a direct comparison between equivalent material systems. As can be seen, this approach provides insight into the balance between mechanical strengthening and electron transport, highlighting the trade-offs inherent in alloy design. It is apparent that the recycled coated Cu chips maintain better electrical conductivity than previously reported Cu–Fe alloys. The superior electrical conductivity of the recycled coated Cu chips can be attributed to the combined effects of the relatively low Fe content and the efficient CCPS processing route. In many Cu–Fe alloys reported in the literature, higher Fe concentrations contribute to increased electron scattering, which reduces conductivity. In contrast, the present alloy contains only 0.25 wt% Fe, minimizing impurity scattering. Additionally, the post-processing steps, including severe plastic deformation and consolidation, promote pronounced grain refinement and uniform microstructural development. While grain refinement typically increases electron scattering at grain boundaries, the controlled microstructure achieved in the recycled chips optimizes the balance between mechanical strengthening and electrical transport, resulting in electrical conductivity that surpasses that of other Cu–Fe alloys with higher Fe content or less optimized processing. The measured hardness of the recycled Cu–Fe alloy indicates that its value is higher than that reported for cast Cu–10Fe alloys, reflecting the strengthening effects of oxide/Fe particle dispersion achieved through the CCPS process. However, the hardness is slightly lower than that of post-processed Cu–Fe alloys subjected to additional severe plastic deformation or heat treatments, where more pronounced microstructural refinement and defect generation further enhance resistance to deformation. A direct comparison with previously reported Cu–Fe alloys further highlights the effectiveness of the CCPS processing route. For instance, Wang et al.70 reported that a Cu–5 wt% Fe alloy produced via vacuum hot pressing followed by hot rolling achieved a UTS of approximately 300 MPa with an electrical conductivity of about 31%. In contrast, the present CCPS-processed Cu–0.25 wt% Fe alloy exhibits a comparable UTS of 307.9 MPa while maintaining significantly higher electrical conductivity. This is particularly noteworthy considering the substantially lower Fe content in the current study. Typically, increasing Fe content enhances strength at the expense of electrical conductivity due to intensified electron scattering. However, the results obtained here demonstrate that a superior balance between strength and conductivity can be achieved without relying on high alloying levels. Importantly, this process demonstrates the effective recycling of waste Cu chips to produce a Cu–Fe alloy with a favorable combination of hardness and electrical conductivity. By reprocessing discarded Cu materials, the CCPS method not only enhances mechanical performance compared to conventional cast Cu–Fe alloys but also retains relatively high electrical conductivity, making it a sustainable approach for upcycling copper waste into functional, high-performance materials. This highlights the potential of the process to transform low-value scrap into alloys suitable for applications requiring a balance of strength and electrical performance.

In conventional recycling routes for copper-based materials, casting remains the most widely adopted industrial process. However, casting typically involves melting at relatively high temperatures followed by solidification, which introduces significant thermal exposure to the material. Such prolonged high-temperature processing can promote grain coarsening, segregation effects, and defect formation, which may ultimately limit the achievable mechanical performance30. Moreover, repeated remelting steps in conventional recycling routes contribute to increased thermal energy demand and extended processing cycles, with life-cycle assessments showing that solid-state alternatives offer substantially lower environmental footprints24. In contrast, solid-state recycling approaches, particularly SPS, provide a distinct processing pathway in which waste metallic chips are consolidated without full melting25. SPS enables recycling under comparatively moderate thermal conditions and significantly reduced processing times due to the rapid heating rates and localized Joule heating characteristic of the technique22, 23. The simultaneous application of uniaxial pressure during SPS further enhances densification and can inhibit grain growth, allowing better retention of refined microstructural features such as fine grains, twin boundaries, and dispersed secondary phases26. As a result, SPS-based approaches not only avoid the drawbacks associated with conventional high-temperature casting routes but also offer improved control over microstructural evolution during recycling, which is beneficial for achieving enhanced mechanical and functional properties31.

In other words, from a sustainability perspective, the proposed CCPS process offers several potential advantages over conventional recycling routes. Traditional recycling of copper machining waste is commonly performed through remelting and casting, which require complete melting of the feedstock and prolonged thermal exposure during melting, holding, and solidification. These high-temperature operations are inherently energy-intensive and may also promote grain coarsening, thereby reducing the potential for microstructural strengthening. In contrast, the CCPS process consolidates recycled copper chips through solid-state spark plasma sintering, eliminating the need for complete melting. The rapid heating rates and short sintering duration associated with SPS reduce the overall thermal exposure43, 71 while preserving the refined microstructure inherited from chip formation72, 73. Although a quantitative assessment of energy consumption and carbon emissions was beyond the scope of the present work, the elimination of the melting stage together with the reduced processing time suggests that CCPS has the potential to provide a more energy-efficient and resource-conscious route for upcycling copper machining waste.

Conclusions

This work demonstrates an effective and sustainable strategy for recycling waste copper chips into high-performance Cu–Fe alloys using the novel Coated Chip Plasma Sintering (CCPS) process. Iron was deposited on the Cu chips via arc PVD, followed by cold compaction and spark plasma sintering to form a dense alloy. The resulting material demonstrates a remarkable balance of mechanical strength and electrical conductivity, which can be attributed to the synergistic effects of grain refinement, twin formation, particle dispersion, and retained chip boundaries. As a result, the CCPS-processed Cu–Fe alloy exhibits a yield strength of ~ 280 MPa, ultimate tensile strength of ~ 308 MPa, hardness of ~ 116.5 HV, and electrical conductivity of ~ 5.1 S/m. Compared to annealed Cu and conventional cast Cu–Fe alloys, the recycled material demonstrates an excellent balance of mechanical and electrical performance. Overall, CCPS offers a sustainable, versatile, and efficient strategy for transforming metallic waste into functional materials, highlighting its potential for environmentally responsible alloy production and resource conservation.
