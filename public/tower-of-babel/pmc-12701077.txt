Response of saffron (Crocus sativus L.) to irrigation water salinity following corm priming with cold plasma technology
Authors: Razieh Khalilzadeh, Elnaz Khalilzadeh, Zohreh Dehghani, Alireza Pirzad, Mahnaz Abdollahi, Nayereh Abdollahi, Narges Beigmohammadi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractCold plasma is a recent technical intervention for seed treatment to manage stress crop plants. In the present study, factorial experiment arrangement in randomized complete block design was used to test the impact of irrigation water salinity (2 (well water as control), 4, and 6 dS m− 1) using NaCl salt, and diverse exposure durations to plasma (0 (control) 5 (short-term), 10 (average-term), and 20 (long-term) min). The corm number was increased by salinity treatments, such that the highest corm number was assigned to 4 dS m-1. The protective effect of the cold plasma at average-term against salinity stress indicates the significant prominent increase of the corm weight, leaf number, leaf weight, stigma dry weight, photosynthetic pigments, and protein content. Medium salinity levels and average term plasma duration increased chlorophyll a by 21.78%, chlorophyll b by 12.78%, and total chlorophyll by 15.97%. Triggering enzymes activity was the most notable mechanism of cold plasma that led to alleviation of adverse effect of salt stress. The polyphenol oxidase activity was 104.34% higher in the leaves of plants exposed to long-term plasma duration in high salinity. Antioxidant activity was strongly induced under high salinity level: peroxidase activity doubled in the short term and the tripled in long term plasma treatment compared with non-plasma treatment. In conclusion, a significant increase in proline, soluble sugars, antioxidant activity, and plant growth associated with 20 min contact with plasma in the protection of plants at high salinity. However, molecular validation and stigma quality investigations are necessary to confirm these findings within a restricted salinity range.Keywords: Antioxidants, Cold plasma, Electrical conductivity, Stigma, Dry weightSubject terms: Physiology, Plant sciences

Introduction

A majority of the arid and semi-arid regions worldwide have experienced salinity issues during recent decades. Salinization-induced land and ecological degradation are critical challenges that substantially impact agricultural production1. Salinity is one of the serious environmental stresses, adversely influencing the plants’ growth and development, which causes considerable decreases in crop yields2. Plants have evolved mechanisms to respond such deleterious effects of excess salt, by activating the enzymatic (superoxide dismutase (SOD), catalase (CAT), peroxidase (POD) and polyphenol oxidase (PPO)) and non-enzymatic (proline, glycine betaine, soluble sugars, and other osmolytes) antioxidant defenses3,4, excluding Na+ and Cl− ions5, preferentially accumulating K+6, and adjusting osmotically7. Reactive oxygen species (ROS), e.g., hydroxyl radicals (●OH), hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), and superoxide anion () are localized in one or more cellular compartments (plasma membranes, mitochondria, and chloroplasts)8. When the level of ROS exceeds the defense mechanisms, the result of this imbalance is “oxidative stress”3,9.

Saffron (Crocus sativus L.) is the worlds’ most valuable plants, obtained from the flowers of the cultivated plant; each flower has only three long orange-colour stigmas10,11. Propagation of saffron is carried out exclusively by corms10; each year, daughter corms are produced from the mother corm based on cultivation conditions12. Saffron is typically grown in arid and semi-arid environments with cold winters, hot summers, high altitudes and low annual rainfall12. Due to its low water need, and high economic efficiency, it is conventionally planted in arid and semi-arid land areas of Iran13, resulting in water scarcity and salinity. Saffron is considered as a salt-sensitive species, however it can grow in semi-saline irrigation conditions14. The salinity threshold values for saffron corms and leaves are 1.2 and 1 dS m− 1, respectively15. Plasma is an ionized gas often described as a non-thermal technology produced at room temperature under atmospheric pressure16. It generates ultraviolet (UV) photons, ROS containing atomic oxygen, ozone, singlet oxygens, hydrogen peroxide, hydroxyl radicals, and superoxides, and reactive nitrogen species (RNS) containing nitrite, nitrate, peroxynitrite, nitric oxide, and nitrogen dioxide), are mostly generated by electrical discharges in a gas atmosphere17. This innovative technique has been well applied in various industries, which can mediate long-term effects on stress tolerance potential of the crop18,19. In recent years, the application of plasma technology developed by physicists could significantly increase crop yields and germination rates under salinity stress20, water scarcity (de Groot et al. 2018) and low-temperature21. Cold plasma (CP) seed priming enhances seed germination and seedling growth by affecting chemical and physical processes22, modifying macromolecular matrices such as; protein content, soluble carbohydrate, and starch23, increasing antioxidant enzyme activity, seed wettability, and decreasing membrane degradation from lipid peroxidation24. In general, an apparatus for cold plasma treatment comprises at least a treatment chamber for accommodating the electrodes, high-frequency electrodes for plasma generation, and electric power determined for each electrode pair. High voltage electrical discharge increased the ROS and RNS in air or an aqueous solution25, Song et al.17. Parameters of plasma standardization, like pressure, dose, discharge source, frequency, and treatment time, are important for fine-tuning plasma application2. There are different mechanisms for manufacturing CP, including atmospheric pressure plasma jet corona discharge, radio frequency discharge, glow discharge, gliding arc discharge, and dielectric barrier discharge26.

Studying the effects of plasma treatment on saffron yield (high economic value of saffron) requires careful consideration and further research. Expansion of the discharge area is the first significant difficulty for continuous processing. Challenges with power and voltage supply, system size, cost-effectiveness, and specimen surface interaction remain undetermined27. To our knowledge, the majority of research on plant-plasma interactions has focused on subjecting plant seeds and early growth stages of various species to cold plasma treatment28. In the current study, the saffron corms were subjected to direct plasma exposure. The results of several investigations mostly rely on factors such as plant species, developmental stage, plasma-generating equipment, exposure duration, and feed gases29.

We hypothesized that saffron demonstrates optimal growth and physicochemical properties under varying salinity levels in relation to plasma-generating devices and duration of exposure. This project involves designing a dielectric barrier discharge (DBD) plasma device suited to the dimensions of saffron corms and aims to investigate the potential of cold plasma corm priming to alleviate the detrimental effects of salinity. Some agronomical, physiological and biochemical attributes of Crocus sativus (leaves, flowers and corm) were examined after the treatment of saffron corm at different plasma exposure time.

Results

Corm number

In all salinity levels, no difference between treatments was found for the corm number between control and plants exposed to short-term plasma duration, whereas long-term expose to plasma increased significantly the corm number under medium salinity stress. Interestingly, in high salinity level, the corm number under plasma treatments of 20 min was significantly lower than the control by 19.87% (Table 1).

Corm fresh weight (FW) and dry weight (DW)

The data from this study indicated that a 10-minute plasma application significantly enhanced the corm FW compared to other plasma treatments across all salinity levels (Table 1). The highest corm FW at salinity levels of 2 (44.49 g), 4 (38.62 g), and 6 dS m−1 (29.23 g) was recorded with medium-term plasma exposure, yielding significant increases of 199.39%, 89.03%, and 208.33% relative to the control treatment (non-plasma treatment), respectively (Table 1). Plasma treatments enhanced the corm DW only at the maximum exposure duration and the highest salt concentration. Prolonged plasma exposure enhanced corm DW, although significantly reduced corm number (Table 1).

Flower FW and DW

Plants subjected to 10–20 min of cold plasma exhibited significant enhancements in flower FW under moderate salinity stress, while non-plasma plants and those treated with 5 min of cold plasma displayed the lowest flower FW at 2 dS m− 1. The increase in salinity level (at 4 dS m− 1) had a positive effect on flower FW and DW, in which high salinity stress significantly reduced the flower FW and DW in comparison with control and medium salinity stress (Table 1). Increasing the time exposure to plasma substantially increase the flower FW compared to non-plasma plants (Table 1).

Leaf number, leaf length and width, and leaf yield

The highest leaf number was detected in plants exposed to plasma for 10 min at 2 dS m− 1. In well water irrigation, long-term exposure to plasma significantly decreased leaf length by 22.22%, comapred to the control treatment (Table 2). However, the leaf width was increased significantly by 15 and 23% for 10 and 20 min of plasma exposure, respectively, at 2 dS m− 1 of salinity level (Table 2). When plants were treated with average-term of plasma (10 min), the leaf width was enhanced by 17.37% at 4 dS m− 1, and 9% at 6 dS m− 1 of salinity level, compared to the plasma control treatment (Table 2). The leaf yield in average-term plasma exposure treatment was higher than in all other treatments (Table 2).

Stigma FW and DW

The weight of flowers and stigmas increased when irrigated at salinity levels above 2 dS m− 1, and the highest values were achieved at salinity level of 4 dS m− 1 (Table 2). However, stigma weight is directly correlated with flower weight. Table 2 showed that the lowest stigma FW was gained in non-plasma plants at 6 dS m− 1 of salinity (Table 2).

Photosynthetic pigments

The maximum concentrations of chlorophyll-a, chlorophyll-b, and total chlorophyll were observed under medium salinity stress in plants exposed to plasma treatment for 10 min with values of 8.33, 13.67 and 22 mg g− 1 FW, respectively. The minimum values of chlorophyll-b, and total chlorophyll were recorded under high salinity stress in non-plasma-treated plants, with 7.50 and 10.87 mg g− 1 FW, respectively. The minimum value of chlorophyll-a was observed under low and medium salinity stress in plants exposed to plasma treatment for 20 min (Fig. 1A, B, and C). In all the plasma time treatments, the concentration of photosynthetic pigments was substantially influenced by increasing salinity level up to 4 dS m− 1 and then reduced significantly in 6 dS m− 1 (Fig. 1). For medium salinity levels, when plants are exposed to plasma treatment for a medium duration, the chlorophyll-a was higher by 91.93% compared to non-plasma treated plants. It is noteworthy to mention that, long-term plasma exposure significantly reduced the chlorophyll-a compared to non-plasma control plant at 2 dS m− 1 of salinity level. When plants were exposed to plasma treatment for an average duration, chlorophyll-b contents was found to be increased to 12.78 in medium salinity and decreased 13.20% in high salinity levels (Fig. 1B).

For the medium and high salinity levels, the concentration of carotenoids in 10 min expose to plasma was higher by 47% and 21.80% than the plasma control treatment, respectively (Fig. 1C).

Relative water content (RWC)

The lowest RWC (83%) was recorded in plants subjected to high salinity, after being exposed to different plasma treatment durations, except for those with average-term plasma exposure.

Electrical conductivity (EC)

Irrigation water salinity significantly improved EC (Fig. 1F). Slight salinity stress (2 dS m− 1) could not change EC of plants exposed to plasma duration and its increase started from 4 dS m− 1 (Fig. 1F).

Proline

For the plants grown in well water irrigation, long-term exposure to plasma increased the concentration of leaf proline by 176.95% than plasma control treatment (Fig. 2A). For the high salinity level, the proline content was significantly lower under plasma treatment of 20 min than control, 5, and 10 min by 17.67, 9.82 and 18%, respectively. When plants were subjected to plasma treatment for the average-term duration, it was observed that the concentration of proline in leaf was significantly higher by 267.64% in high salinity treatments than non-saline treatments (Fig. 2A). According to the results of our studies, it can be stated that at different levels of salinity and plasma exposure time, cold plasma treatment does not show a consistent trend for proline content.

Soluble sugars

At high salinity level, plant exposed to plasma for 5 and 10 min increased soluble sugars by 25.04 and 22.28% than control, respectively (Fig. 2B). In this context, there is no clear difference between non-plasma plants and those exposed to plasma in 20 min at all salinity levels.

Protein

Results show that, plasma treatment at different time duration did not affect the total soluble protein content in saffron leaf under salinity levels at 2 and 4 dS m− 1: whereas average-term expose to plasma significantly increased total soluble protein in leaf of saffron by 27.15% than plasma control treatment under high salinity stress at 6 dS m− 2 (Fig. 2C).

Antioxidant enzymes

When considering non-saline to saline conditions, it is clear that for the medium salinity stress for all plasma treatments, the activity of CAT was higher by 4.91 to 6.49% in than non-saline conditions (Fig. 2D). For the plants grown at medium salinity levels, the CAT activity under short and long-term plasma exposure treatments was significantly lower than plasma control treatment (Fig. 2D). For the high salinity stress for all plasma treatments, this enzyme was higher by 4.66 to 5.31% than in non-saline conditions. Although, the CAT activity of saffron leaf was affected by plasma exposure time in non-saline compared to saline irrigation water, regarding the reduction of activity of CAT in high-level salinity was marginal than medium level. This indicates that application of cold plasma at different exposure time did not play a positive role on CAT activity. The maximums enzyme activities were observed under high salinity stress in plants exposed to plasma treatment for 10 and 20 min with values of 69.26 and 69.92 µg protein min− 1 for peroxidase (POX), and 70.58, and 70.60 µg protein min− 1 for polyphenol oxidase (PPO), respectively. The minimum values of POD, and PPO were recorded under low salinity stress in non-plasma treated plants (Fig. 2E, and F). Based on the results (Fig. 2), the POX activity (stress-responsive defense agent) present in saffron leaf was found to be increased significantly by 68.09 and 257.37% in at 4 and 6 dS m− 1 salinity treatments, respectively, in the non-plasma control plants. As is evident, POX activity has raised significantly by 50 and 94% in the plant exposed to average-term plasma duration under medium and high salinity stress, respectively (Fig. 2E).

As was observed PPO activity for salinity stress at 4 dS m− 2, all plasma treatments, significantly increased the PPO activity in leaf than control by 3.92 to 30.63%. For the salinity stress at 6 dS m− 2,10 and 20 min of plasma exposure remarkably enhanced activity of PPO compared to control treatment by 46.67 and 46.71%, respectively (Fig. 2F).

Discussion

In this study, saffron can be classified as salt-resistant and is capable of producing an acceptable stigma yield even at 4 dS m− 1 which is considered the basic salinity level. Irrigation with 6 dS m− 1 salinity resulted in a 42% reduction in stigma DW compared to 2 dS m− 1 salinity in the non-plasma treatment. It is uncommon to find a crop able to produce satisfactory yield under such salinity conditions. In comparison, previous studies have investigated much lower salinity levels in this crop10,15. This degree of saffron salinity resistance may be attributed to mechanisms such as increased root density, adjustment of corm weight, and regulation of the K+/Na+ ratio by the crop, although the adaptive mechanism remains unclear6. In this study, the observed physiological and biochemical changes in saffron corms suggest that plasma priming can modulate stress-related responses under salinity conditions. However, the precise mechanisms underlying these effects remain uncertain and should be interpreted with caution. One possible explanation is that plasma exposure induces surface modifications in the corm coat, increasing its permeability and facilitating the penetration of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and reactive nitrogen species (RNS) into the inner tissues. The present research indicated that the most prominent peaks in the spectra are observed in a range of 337.5–714.25 nm, comprising of O3, N2, and OH species. N2 emission lines at 316, 337.5, 358.2, and 630.76 nm and an OH emission line at 313.54 nm may signify seed material on erosion processes (Fig. 4). Spectral emissions at 673.18 nm indicate the presence of O3 species in the DBD plasma. The results revealed that exposure to plasma gas (Fig. 5), superoxide (O2−), and hydroxyl radical (OH−) cause surface erosion of the seeds (Fig. 4)30. Reactive oxygen species (ROS) also induces histone modification levels and gene expression that help regulate plant growth and reproduction31.

The improving long-term (20 min) exposure to cold plasma (CP) by diverse devices on the vegetative growth phase of plants, including wheat (37), soybean32, and mung bean29 have been described. Mildažiene et al.33 indicated that treatment of C. sativus corms with cold plasma has potential applications for increasing the quality of saffron by affecting the growth parameters. Holc et al.34 found that there was an increase in water uptake during germination and early seedling growth stimulation of peeled garlic under low-pressure radio frequency oxygen plasma for 60 s under laboratory conditions. These documented responses mainly depend on the feed gases and plant developmental stage, exposure time, types of plasma generating devices, and plant species28,29,35. One of the explanations of differential result of different time of plant exposure to plasma is that prolonged contact with plasma had higher H2O2, in contrast, plants exposed to short-term (5 min) duration had higher superoxide and NO radicals31. These observations influence biochemical profile and differential gene expression pattern to regulate cellular processes36. Seed priming influences ion homeostasis by modulating ion transporters such as SOS1 and NHX. These molecular components regulate sodium (Na+) levels within cells, which is crucial for preventing salt toxicity, and improve potassium to sodium (K+/Na+) selectivity, vital for overall plant health and productivity37. The responses of immediate and long-term plants exposed to CP have been observed in recent studies38. This can be the result of the presence of H2O2 in the cold plasma21, the protective effect of H2O2 on the salt-stressed plants39, and NO-induced salt tolerance40.

The weight of saffron corms is an important determinant of progeny multiplication, flower and stigma production10. Corms with greater weight produce larger flower DW, stigma FW, higher leaf number, longer leaf length, and greater leaf yield. Results show the positive influence of plasma treatment on corm production with an increase in the exposure time in medium salinity level. The effect of cold plasma can be explained by the high H2O2 content21; which is involved in the activation of the pathway of redox signaling as shied mechanism under stress condition41,42. The fact that average-term (10 min) exposure to plasma that contains more H2O2 than long-term has increased the corm number under high salinity levels, whereas, under medium salinity stress, cold plasma is effective only after a prolonged exposure implies that H2O2 may play a more significant role in promoting corm growth under conditions of salinity stress.

In our study, as observed for the corm DW, our results were inconsistent over salinity levels and plasma exposure time treatments. For instance, in high salinity irrigation water, increasing the time exposure to plasma significantly increased the corm DW by 3.09 to 19.90% compared to the control. However, in medium salinity irrigation water, long-term exposure to plasma has not indicated a critical difference relative to control. No notable difference was observed between salinity levels in non-plasma plants concerning corm DW (Table 1). However, the corm FW strangely increased in medium salinity and decreased in high salinity stress in non-plasma plants. Differences between plants exposed to plasma might be related to the corm number under salinity stress. Hayashi et al.43 suggested that the increased plant growth in the plant exposed to plasma duration could be related to the enhanced production of polyphenols and glutathione, mediated by ROS.

The yields of saffron flowers are closely linked to environmental factors, especially soil salinity44. Our results revealed that flower and stigma weight of saffron is intensely dependent on the size and weight of corm45, and the corms with lower weights could not produce any flower and form justly leaf. It is concluded that saffron plants exposed to different time of plasma affect cellular physiology (Figs. 1, 2 and 3) and improving immunity and plant growth. Our results suggest that the sensitivity of different parts of saffron to salinity varied. The stigma DW as the economic yield was more sensitive, whereas leaf yield and the number of tubers showed less affected by soil salinity. This study shows that 10 min exposure to plasma can be an option to improve the stigma yield of saffron in saline soil at 4 dS m− 1. DBD plasma treatment had a positive effect on the photosynthesis efficiencies46, carotenoids and chlorophyll content (Fig. 1). It is probable that plasma has the potential to led to modifications in antioxidant system (Fig. 2) and enhance biochemical changes, thereby improving biomass accumulation. This improvement in enhanced pigment synthesis coupled with, nutrient availability, resulted in increased aboveground biomass46. ROS and RON also promote the biosynthesis of phytohormones and enhance nutrient absorption, resulting in improved seedling growth16,31. The increased concentration of photosynthetic pigments under osmotic stress suggests that chlorophyll concentration could serve as a preliminary selection criterion for osmotic stress tolerance in plants. Supporting our finding, Seyed Sharifi et al3. reported that high chlorophyll content, resulting from reduced leaf area under salinity stress, could be considered an avoidance mechanism that minimizes water loss. Moreover, we confirm the results of Habibi et al.47,48, as plants facing salt stress typically experience drought stress due to reduced water absorption by root. Therefore, plants with seeds primed with cold plasma, which develop stronger roots, may exhibit greater water use efficiency, increasing drought tolerance and enabling better photosynthesis.

Pigments act as effective antioxidants; safeguarding plants from oxidative damage by scavenging ROS (singlet oxygen and peroxyl radicals) upon salinity stress49. In photosynthesizing tissues, the carotenoids are predominantly located in the complexes of photosystem I and II or bound to the thylakoid membranes within the core50. It is well-known that 1O2 can directly oxidize carotenoids, and the oxidized products have the potential to influence the expression of various genes51. The cold plasma treatment at average-term duration alleviated the alteration of pigment composition upon salinity stress. The interaction of carotenoids with various radicals, including those from cold plasma, results in compounds similar to those formed during oxidative degradation52 involved in stress signaling pathways. These carotenoid radicals, which are involve in Ca2+ binding proteins, activation of phospholipid signaling and G‐proteins21, may also be involved in the ROS signaling pathway leading to the impact of cold plasma treatment. Furthermore, it is worth noting that cold plasma treatment can improve the availability of internal iron53 and increase the content of bioactive cytokinins within plants50, which ensures the optimal performance of photosystem II (PSII) and sustaining chlorophyll levels50 under stressful environmental conditions. In our results, a change in chlorophyll was observed at high salt stress when plants were subjected to the plasma for the short and medium term. The level of chlorophyll‐b, which harbors mainly in the light‐harvesting complexes (LHCs), declines more significantly than the core‐complex‐associated chlorophyll‐a and carotenoids under high salinity levels2,21. Previous studies have highlighted the impact of plasma treatment on enhancing chlorophyll content in wheat30, tomato54, and maize55 at no stress condition. Chlorophyll content decreased in plants exposed to prolonged plasma, and this decrease was attributed to reduction activity of chlorophyll biosynthetic enzymes56 and increased ROS production, which oxidized chlorophyll57.

RWC is a key indicator for assessing the balance between transpiration rate and water supply of leaf tissue58. Salinity impacts water in leaf tissue and is lower in plants that are high salinity stressed (Fig. 1E). Factors such as the plant’s water availability and its ability to regulate stomatal and osmotic movements influence the tissue water content12. The decreased RWC in saffron leaves is a strategy to conserve energy for survival by limiting specific biochemical processes59. The reduced RWC because of salinity stress has been documented in various studies3,49, as detected in the present study. However, it was found that saffron plants exposed to plasma increased relative humidity and the potential of water in comparison to plants not treated with cold plasma53. On the other hand, treatment of plasma induces physical alterations in the seed coat, increasing the valuable energy transfers of O2 or H2O by seed coat and increases the concentration of nitrogen and oxygen groups in the plants53.

Habibi et al.47 demonstrate that salt stress caused by sodium chloride reduces plant photosynthesis due to increased cell injury from electrolyte leakage and a rise in leaf surface temperature, both of which negatively impact the photosynthesis rate. However, for the resulting reduction in electrolyte leakage was noticed that aided in coping with all salinity irrigation levels. Electrical conductivity of leaves correlated negatively with all saffron traits. However, our study found that seed priming with medium-term duration of cold plasma was highly effective in increasing the photosynthesis pigments and electrolyte leakage. These findings align with previous reports60, which suggest that priming enhances membrane integrity, boosts antioxidant activity, and triggers pre-germinative molecular responses. Increased enzymatic activities were observed in saffron leaves treated with cold plasma (Fig. 2). This indicates the induction of oxidative stress and the activation of defense mechanisms that act to counteract high levels of ROS, which reduces electrolyte leakage53.

Osmotic regulation is one of the primary mechanisms that increase the resistance of plants to abiotic stresses through the accumulation of organic osmolytes (such as proline, soluble sugars, and other mineral ions)61. These osmolytes contribute to homeostasis maintenance and promote water uptake62. In terms of proline content, the interaction of ROS with the amino acids63 and reduction of α-helix and β-sheet as a secondary structure cause to protein denaturation by cold plasma treatment64. It has been shown that in plant cells under stress, the accumulation of proline to high levels can significantly inhibit ROS in said cells and reduce the potential for oxidative damage. Proline can not only be considered as a carbon and nitrogen storage, however, it can also act as a a scavenger of ROS, compatible osmolyte, a buffer for cytosolic pH, and also an aid to balance the cellular redox state12,65. This study revealed that proline correlated better with flower FW than with either corm or stigma FW.

The results clearly show that with increasing plasma exposure time, the trend of increasing the concentration of soluble sugars is not constant, so with increasing plasma treatment time to 20 min, a decrease in the plant’s defense response is observed, indicating that it cannot exhibit a precise defense mechanism. It has previously been studied that increasing the amount of soluble sugars in plant cells reduces the osmotic potential, which in turn facilitates the absorption of water by plant cells through a decrease in water potential66. Moreover, during the stress period, the physiological activities of soluble sugars can cause the stability of membranes through protein resistance, lipids preservation, and osmotic regulation67. It was also observed that an increase in soluble sugars content after long-term exposed to cold plasma, correlates with higher degree of polymerization and degradation of the macromolecule64. Arabasadi et al.53 reported that in response to abiotic stress, cold plasma priming can enhance the synthesis of improved phenolic derivatives by providing an available sugar source. Consequently, it is reasonable to assume that the accumulation of antioxidant capability and sugars in plants exposed to cold plasma, such as salinity stress, can rise the generation of ROS, thus stimulating the antioxidant system68.

It is clear that the 20-minute plasma treatment caused changes in the structure of proteins and decreased solubility under high salinity stress. Our results are confirmed by Ben Othman et al.19, who noted that plasma treatment durations positively affected the accumulation of soluble protein. When plants are exposed to cold plasma, the concentrations of soluble protein increase through enhanced enzyme digestion32, demethylation, and the expression of genes associated with metabolism and plant growth69. It is known that plants have different responses to cold plasma due to their biochemical and genetic status70. Some researchers revealed that that NO produced from plasma as a significant signaling molecule that is involved in hampering nutrients71, could activate the protein kinase cascade, which in turn activates gene expression28, revealeing the positive impact of CP through the conventional technologies71.

In the previous section, the changes in total soluble proteins were discussed in detail. It should be noted that similar results have been reported for enzymes as well. These results indicated that salinity alters the level of antioxidant enzyme activities in plants. Cold plasma treatment can lead to a spontaneous enhancement in enzyme activity, due to the unfolding of proteins and better access of substrates to the active site72. The reports showing the ability of cold plasma treatment to activate enzymes are minimal, so this area requires further studies. It has been previously investigated that under oxidative conditions, the amount of ROS can grow and plants decrease the level of free radicals due to the producing a variety of antioxidants and regulating cellular homeostasis12. One of the most pertinent enzymes is the CAT in the early growth stage of plants73 and afterward peroxidase plays supplementary important function74. Generally, overproduction of ROS during salinity stress reduces CAT activity leading to toxic events in cells under high salinity stress. Habibi et al.75 reported that enzymatic activity significantly increased under salt stress compared to the control, with increases of 83.87%, and 204.39% in CAT, and POD, respectively. Furthermore, the current study suggests that seed priming with cold plasma is effective in inducing enzymatic activity. Similarly, plasma-generated endogenous ROS and RNS species could act as a significant part of plasma. Alleviating effect of plasma exposure for 10 and 20 min regarding the POD and PPO activity was more affected at high salinity. The authors believe that this is because highly reactive nitrogen and oxygen species are produced during cold plasma treatment. In the work done by Zhang, it was shown that the high-density electron generation in argon caused more structural changes in the enzyme than nitrogen76.

These results indicate that plasma treatment with average exposure time at 4 dS m− 1salinity level can enhance cellular antioxidant systems and promote abiotic stress resilience without forcing oxidative stress. The accumulation of active species by salinity stress3, cold plasma treatment50, and antioxidant enzymes with high activity could also explain the high concentration of enzymes in this study. Also, a significant increase in ABA levels, and CAT activity was detected in plants exposed to cold plasma77. The cold plasma technique on the activity of antioxidant enzyme under salinity stress has been repeatedly documented in various researches19,28.

It has been investigated that reactive oxygen species can activate genes associated with histone modifications and subsequently cause epigenetic changes that are related to stress resistance and lead to the regulation of morphological and biochemical traits such as antioxidants and phytohormones53. Our study showed that high levels of hydroxyl radicals observed in plasma priming saffron corm plants (Fig. 5) are of great importance in activation of the cellular antioxidant system, which mainly helps in maintaining cellular redox homeostasis and plant’s tolerance53. The question of how cold plasma treatment induces stress responses in plants has not yet been well addressed. Although, studies have considered changes in the photosynthetic pigments and CAT, POD, and SOD activities29. In detail, the plasma treatment caused higher activity of superoxide, which acts as a precursor to highly stable hydroxyl radical like H2O229. Chao et al.56 found that when plants are exposed to cold plasma, the antioxidant defense system of the plant increases the activation of antioxidant enzymes, such as CAT, to prevent accumulation of harmful substances. These results illustrate that exposure of plasma, over time improves the activity of POD enzyme in salinity and non-salinity treatments. Basak and Annapure78, show that the increase enzymes activity after cold plasma treatment can be linked to more excellent absorption of water due to the etching phenomenon, which facilitated promoting germination.

It is important to note that cold plasma priming can be considered an environmentally friendly technique, as it does not require chemical reagents or leave harmful residues. The reactive species generated are short-lived and naturally decay without accumulating in soil or water. From a practical perspective, approach has potential value for farmers because priming improves the tolerance of saffron corms to salinity, which is a major constraint in saffron cultivation in many regions of Iran and worldwide. The current electrode design demonstrates that a large number of corms can be processed simultaneously, and with relatively simple engineering adjustments - such as enlarging the discharge area or using multiple electrode arrays - the system can be scaled up to handle larger batches. Overall, this technique shows promise as a sustainable, scalable, and economically viable approach for agricultural applications, including saffron production.

Materials and methods

Site description and climatic characteristics

The experiment was conducted at the Faculty of Agricultural Research Farm of Urmia University, Urmia, Iran, at 37° 42’ N, 45° 19’ E, and 1362 m above sea level, site in the north-west of Iran (Fig. 6). The average rainfall is at about 300–700 mm, the average yearly temperature is −5 °C to 10 °C with hot-summer/cold-winter. Physico-chemical properties of soil were clay (48.5% sand, 22.5% silt, and 29% clay) with pH 7.9, EC 2.27 dS m− 1, nitrogen 0.13%, phosphorus 13ppm, and potassium 375 ppm.

The map of the studied area.

Experimental design and treatments

Three levels of irrigation water salinity were including; 2 dS m− 1 (well water, S1), 4 dS m− 1 (medium level; S2), 6 dS m− 1 (high level; S3) of NaCl. Diverse exposure durations to plasma (0 (control), 5 (short-term), 10 (average-term), and 20 (long-term) min) were considered as the subplots with three replications. The corms of saffron were supplied from three-year-old farmland, sited from the main center of saffron production in Mashhad, Khorasan province, north-east Iran.

Uniform weight of saffron corms were approximately 10–15 g and a width of 3–4 cm, planted at a depth of 20–25 cm. Four rows were made in each plot (1 × 1 m) with a distance of 25 cm, and four corms with 25 cm within each row (16 corms m− 1). The soil of the study area before planting was EC 0.59 dS m− 1, and pH 7.84. Based on the conventional method, 10 Kg ha− 1 of cow manure is used as fertilizers before planting. The corms during dormancy period were planted on September 6th 2022 by hand. Crop irrigation with saline water (based on salinity treatments) was started on October 2th and the subsequent irrigations were applied approximately 30 days interval in no adequate rainfall conditions. EC of soil saturation on extract was measured at November 26th 2023 after harvesting the flowers. EC of soil in each treatment was 2.3, 4.5 and 6.6 (dS m− 1) based on the irrigation water EC treatments.

Plasma treatment process

The plasma source is the surface non-thermal dielectric barrier discharge (DBD) shown in Fig. 5. The plasma device comprises Pyrex glass dielectric tubes (diameter 14 mm, thickness 1.5 mm, and length 320 mm). In the center of the glass tubes are rod electrodes with 10 mm diameter and 250 mm length. The distance between the rod electrodes is 16.7 mm. The power supply utilized alternating current, delivering a discharge voltage of 10 kV and a frequency of 14 kHz. Air is fed into the reactor vessel at the gas flow rate of 1.5 L/min− 1. Since saffron corms are relatively large, volumetric treatment with atmospheric DBD was not feasible, but surface treatment was achievable. To enable this, a rod array electrode system was constructed in five rows, allowing a large number of bulbs to be exposed simultaneously under uniform conditions. The applied voltage and frequency were adjusted according to the power supply’s capabilities, ensuring reliable breakdown across the entire interelectrode area and stable plasma maintenance. Air was used as the working gas, with the airflow both cooling the electrodes and transporting reactive species towards the bulb surface. In this way, the system ensured reproducible surface treatment within the limits of our equipment and biological materials.

As it is clear in the Fig. 5, the treatment is of an indirect type, which was chosen according to the size of the saffron corms and the more effective plasma treatment. The saffron corms are placed on a shaker plate which is 50 mm away from the glass dielectric tubes. Plasma discharge was performed at different treatment times 5, 10, and 20 min.

In this study, we also moistened saffron corms before treatment and then irradiated them. Measurement of growth parameters

Corms from three sowing points of the middle row in each plot were harvested on June 19th at the corm dormancy stage. Corm number and corm weight from three points of the plot were recorded. Samples were carefully washed with distilled water to remove any adhered particles. After evaporating the surface moisture at room temperature, the samples were oven-dried at 70 °C for 48 h to a constant weight. The total flowering period was 15 days from November 11th to November 26th 2023. Flowers were picked every morning, and the saffron yield (weight of flower and stigma) was recorded immediately. The separation of saffron stigmas from fresh flowers were done by hands and then dried in appropriate air flow to calculate the dry weight.

Measurements of leaf length and width were recorded at the maximum growth stage of the plants in January 18th 20236. After leaf senescence in the growing plant stage, five mature leaves from each plant were harvested from the middle row to determine the leaf dry matter yield.

Measurement of plant physiological and biochemical properties

The physiological and biochemical processes of mature leaves in the maximum growth stage of them in January 18th 20236 sampling, were measured. Photosynthetic pigments (chlorophyll a, chlorophyll b, total chlorophyll, carotenoids) were quantified using spectrophotometry (Arnon, 194986). Relative water content (RWC, %) was determined following Tambussi et al.87 using fresh, turgid, and dry weights. Proline (mg g−1 FW) was quantified by the acid-ninhydrin method88. Soluble sugars (mg g−1 FW) were estimated by the phenol–sulfuric acid method89. Total soluble proteins (mg g¹ FW) were measured by the Bradford90 assay with bovine serum albumin as the standard. Enzymatic antioxidant activities were determined from fully expanded leaves. Samples were homogenized in chilled phosphate buffer (pH 7.0) and centrifuged; supernatants were used for enzyme assays of catalase (CAT; U mg¹ protein), peroxidase (POD; U mg¹ protein), and polyphenol oxidase (PPO; µg protein min¹), following Kar and Mishra91.

Statistical analysis

This study was conducted using factorial experiment based on a randomized complete block design (RCBD) with three replications. Pearson’s correlation analysis was performed on treatment means (corm number–Pr) using RStudio (version 2024.12.1) with the Hmisc and corrplot packages. A heatmap was constructed, with the upper matrix displaying color intensity to indicate correlation strength, and the lower matrix presenting numerical r values with significance.

Conclusions

The combined effects of plasma priming and irrigation water salinity on many features of saffron, encompassing morphological, physiological, and biochemical traits, were investigated. The protection of plants in elevated salinity conditions and enhanced antioxidant activity may elucidate the advantages of prolonged plasma exposure and the superior quality observed with cold plasma priming in this study. Priming saffron corms with cold plasma technology demonstrated promise for significant yields under moderately saline irrigation conditions. Further explanation is necessary concerning the energy requirements for plasma creation and the related infrastructure expenses, which are essential for assuring economic viability. Future research, including molecular confirmation and stigma quality assessment, is required to corroborate these findings within a restricted salinity range.
