Interaction between cell membranes and protein inclusions in the large-deformation regime Preprint: APS/123-QED
Gaetano Ferraro, Michele Castellana

Abstract
Biological membranes are dynamic surfaces whose shape and function are critically influenced by protein inclusions. While membrane deformations induced by protein inclusions have been extensively studied in the small-deformation regime, a variety of processes involves strong membrane deformations. We investigate the interaction between lipid membranes and protein inclusions in the large deformation regime, with the finite-element method. We develop an approximate analytical solution that captures key features of the large deformation regime. We show that the force exerted by the membrane on a protein inclusion displays non-monotonic behavior with respect to the protein inclusion vertical displacement. The qualitative features of this force appear to be independent of the protein geometry. For two interacting protein inclusions, the membrane-mediated potential exhibits sub-power-law decay with inter-protein distance, reflecting the complex nature of the elastic medium. The interaction potential shows that conical protein inclusions with identical and opposite orientations repel and attract, respectively, confirming the analogy between protein inclusion orientation and electric charge, in the large deformation regime. In the presence of membrane flows, we identify a characteristic velocity that separates two regimes in which bending rigidity and viscous effects dominate, respectively, implying the onset of flow-induced deformations above such velocity threshold. Overall, our results provide quantitative predictions for membrane-protein systems in biologically relevant scenarios involving large deformations, with implications for protein sorting, clustering, and membrane trafficking.



I
Introduction

Biological membranes are dynamic and deformable surfaces whose shape and function are critically influenced by the presence of protein inclusions
Alberts et al. 2022
;
Bethani et al. 2010
. Such protein inclusions often impose geometric constraints, e.g., contact angles between the protein and the membrane, or membrane displacement, that drive local membrane deformations
Manneville et al. 1999
and may govern membrane-mediated interactions
Koltover et al. 1999
. Understanding how these deformations arise and interact is central to elucidate key biological processes, such as cell-shape regulation and protein sorting
McMahon and Gallop 2005
.

Membrane deformations induced by protein inclusions have been the subject of numerous investigations
Marsh 2008
. From the modelling standpoint, they may be described by the shape equation
Jülicher and Seifert 1994
;
Derényi et al. 2002
;
Zhong-can and Helfrich 1989
, whose variational formulation stems from the free energy originally proposed by Helfrich
Helfrich 1973
. Studies on such membrane deformations in the literature focus on the small deformation regime
Goulian et al. 1993
;
Weikl et al. 1998
;
Dommersnes et al. 1998
;
Kim et al. 1998
, and rely on perturbative expansions about the flat-membrane configurations.

However, a wide variety of physical situations may fall beyond the small deformation scenario. For instance, a protein inclusion moving at large enough velocities may induce a strong membrane invagination. Also, multiple protein inclusions positioned close enough to each other, may induce strong membrane deformations and gradients which cannot be described as small deformations.

In this work, we explore the interaction between a lipid membrane and a protein inclusion, in the large deformation regime, leveraging the finite element method to solve the problem numerically. We then focus on the features resulting from this solution, such as the membrane shape, flows, the membrane-mediated interaction between two protein inclusions, and others, and discuss their physical interpretation.

II
State of the art

On top of numerous experimental studies—see for example
Koltover et al. 1999
;
Manneville et al. 1999
;
Bassereau et al. 2018
—membrane-mediated forces between proteins have been subject of interest and research in the past few decades from the theoretical standpoint. However, the existing theoretical studies are restricted to small deformations—see below.
In the pioneering study of Goulian et al., a power-law interaction between protein inclusions has been found in the small deformation limit
Goulian et al. 1993
, and later on studied for conical inclusions, in the small deformation regime, as a function of lateral membrane tension
Weikl et al. 1998
and of the inclusion contact angle
Dommersnes et al. 1998
.
Studies with more than two inclusions showed, for small deformations, the presence of non-pairwise forces between the inclusions, which allow for the formation of membrane-bound protein aggregates
Kim et al. 1998
.
In addition, the power-law dependence on inclusion symmetries was studied in the small deformation limit
Marchenko and Misbah 2002
. The nature of this interaction was then studied, in the small deformation regime, as function of the ‘hardness’ of the protein-protein interaction potential
Bartolo and Fournier 2003
.
A geometrical approach has been recently proposed and used to derive force-distance relations in the small deformation regime
Mueller et al. 2005a
. Expressions for the inter-particle forces, not restricted to small deformations, have been derived by leveraging the system symmetries
Mueller et al. 2005b
, but they lack quantitative predictive power in the large deformation regime due to the absence of a full numerical solution.
Finally, studies which go beyond the small deformation regime do exist, but they are limited to the analysis of membrane vesicles
Seifert et al. 1991
or tubes
Derényi et al. 2002
, both neglecting finite size of the proteins.

Regarding the effect of membrane flows on membrane shape, Arroyo and DeSimone
Arroyo and DeSimone 2009
showed that membrane viscosity plays a crucial role in the relaxation dynamics of fluid membranes, highlighting the importance of viscous effects in dynamical models.
In
Mahapatra et al. 2020
, coupled mean-field equations describing the time evolution of protein density and membrane shape were derived, accounting for the interplay between curvature elasticity and transport processes.
Finally, in
Tasso and Buscaglia 2013
, a finite element method for fluid membranes was developed and applied to the simulation of dynamical prolate-to-oblate transitions of simplified red blood cells under optical tweezing.

III.1.2
Solution with zero mean curvature

Analytical insights about the large deformation regime for the membrane can be obtained considering the following approximate solution.

In order to simplify the equation, it is convenient to introduce the quantity
\psi
, by the relation

\omega=\frac{\psi}{\sqrt{1-\psi^{2}}}.

(9)

In
Appendix

A
, we show all geometrical quantities expressed in terms of
\psi
and its derivatives. Substituting
Eq.

38
in
Eq.

1
, we obtain:

\displaystyle(1-\psi^{2})\frac{\partial^{2}H}{\partial r^{2}}+\left(\frac{1-\psi^{2}}{r}-\psi\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial r}\right)\frac{\partial H}{\partial r}+

\displaystyle\left(\frac{2\psi}{r}\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial r}+\frac{1}{\ell}-2H^{2}\right)H=0,

(10)

\displaystyle H=\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial r}+\frac{\psi}{r}.

(11)

Equation

11
admits a solution with zero mean curvature, of the form

\psi=\frac{C_{1}}{r}.

(12)

We observe that the two terms in the right-hand side of
Eq.

11
are, respectively, the radial and angular curvature
Marchiafava 2005
. As a result, solution
Eq.

12
does not imply that
z
is linear in
r
, i.e., that the radial curvature vanishes, but that the algebraic sum of radial and angular curvature is zero.

Although this solution is only valid for zero mean curvature, it constitutes an approximation of the exact solution in the general case where curvature is not zero. However, it allows for explicit, simple expressions of the membrane shape and height, which may be useful on a qualitative level. In particular, here we infer a relation between the contact angle
\alpha
and the corresponding spontaneous displacement of the protein. Indeed, substituting
Eq.

12
in
Eq.

9
, and integrating, we obtain that the corresponding profile of the membrane is:

z(r)=-C_{1}\ln\Big(r-\sqrt{r^{2}-C_{1}^{2}}\Big)+C_{2}.

(13)

The integration constants
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
can be determined from the boundary conditions
Eqs.

5
\crefpairconjunction
7
, leading to:

z(r)=-r_{0}\sin{\alpha}\ln\frac{r-\sqrt{r^{2}-r_{0}^{2}\sin^{2}{\alpha}}}{R-\sqrt{R^{2}-r_{0}^{2}\sin^{2}{\alpha}}}.

(14)

The displacement of the protein is then given by:

h=-r_{0}\sin{\alpha}\ln\frac{r_{0}-r_{0}\sqrt{1-\sin^{2}{\alpha}}}{R-\sqrt{R^{2}-r_{0}^{2}\sin^{2}{\alpha}}},

(15)

For
R\gg r_{0}
,
Eq.

15
becomes

h\simeq-r_{0}\sin{\alpha}\left[\ln\frac{r_{0}}{R}+\ln\left(1-\cos{\alpha}\right)\right]

(16)

The comparison between the numerical solution obtained with fluId layeR finitE-Element softwarE and the zero-mean-curvature solution
Eq.

14
is shown in
Fig.

4
. The Figure shows that the zero-mean-curvature solution yields a reasonable approximation for the numerically exact solution, see
Section

IV
.

IV
Discussion

We have studied, by combining analytical and numerical methods, the deformations in a cell membrane induced by protein inclusions in the large deformation regime. Our numerical analysis allows for going beyond current perturbative approaches
Goulian et al. 1993
;
Weikl et al. 1998
;
Dommersnes et al. 1998
;
Kim et al. 1998
;
Marchenko and Misbah 2002
;
Bartolo and Fournier 2003
;
Mueller et al. 2005b
, which cannot describe the large deformation regime.

We first focused on the steady state of a lipid membrane with a single protein inclusion with spherical symmetry, where the membrane is pinned on an outer circle with radius
R
and the protein inclusion is located at the center. We showed the limitations of perturbative solutions by comparing them to numerically exact, finite element solutions
Wörthmüller et al. 2025
, for large protein displacements or large protein inclusion contact angles, see
Fig.

2
. We developed an analytical solution for the shape equations in the large deformation regime, which assumes that the mean membrane curvature vanishes. Although this solution is only approximate, it yields a sensible, analytical expression of the membrane shape, see
Fig.

4
.
According to this solution, a protein inclusion with a fixed contact angle has a spontaneous displacement that scales, for large
R
, as
\sim\ln R
, see
Eq.

15
. Moreover, for a straight contact angle
\alpha=\pi/2
, the spontaneous displacement remains finite, see
Eq.

15
.
We show that the vertical component of the force exerted by the membrane on the protein inclusion—the only nonzero component due to rotational symmetry—displays a non-monotonic dependence on the vertical displacement
h
of the protein inclusion. This behavior is analogous to the one obtained when a point-like force is applied to a membrane
Derényi et al. 2002
, demonstrating that the detailed size of the protein inclusion does not affect qualitatively the force profile.

We then analyzed the force exerted by the membrane on the protein inclusion, in the large deformation regime. The force displays a non-monotonic dependence on the vertical displacement of the protein inclusion, reminiscent of that of a point-like force applied to a membrane
Derényi et al. 2002
. We assesses how this non-monotonic behavior is affected by the protein inclusion contact angle, as well as by the size of the membrane domain, see
Fig.

5
.

In addition, we numerically studied the membrane-mediated interaction between two protein inclusions in the large deformation regime.
As shown in
Fig.

8
A, for the values of the inter-protein distance
d
that we considered, the potential does not display a power-law behavior. Instead, the potential shape shows a sub-power-law decay, i.e., it decreases with
d
slower than a power law. This nontrivial structure is due to the complex medium which conveys the interaction—the lipid membrane
Johannes et al. 2018
. Finally,
Fig.

8
B shows that the potential is an odd function of the protein inclusion orientation, and that two protein inclusions with the same orientation yield a larger energy than two protein inclusions with opposite orientations. This behavior confirms the interpretation
Weikl et al. 1998
that the angle imposed by each protein can be thought of as a ‘charge’, i.e., protein inclusions with the same and opposite orientations repel and attract each other, respectively.

In the presence of membrane flows, we identified the emergence of a characteristic velocity
v_{\ast}
, of the order of a few microns per second, which reflects the competition between
bending and viscous forces. At low velocities, membrane flows have negligible impact on membrane shape. On the other hand, at higher velocities, flow-induced deformations appear. We provided a theoretical estimate for the critical velocity at which this deformation arises, which explains their characteristic length and aligns well with numerical results. The presence of this characteristic velocity scale suggests that protein inclusions motion may significantly influence long-range membrane deformations and thus, for instance, membrane-mediated interactions.

An example of how flow-induced membrane deformations can influence interactions between protein inclusions is given by protein clustering on giant unilamellar vesicles.
In this regard, we consider an example involving bacteriorhodopsin—the best understood ion-transport protein

Haupts et al. 1999
.
Multiple bacteriorhodopsin molecules have been incorporated and diffuse
Kahya et al. 2001
on the membrane of a giant unilamellar vesicle
Walde et al. 2010
. A large giant unilamellar vesicle with a
\sim 100\,\mu\text{m}
diameter populated with
\sim 10^{4}
bacteriorhodopsins, yields an average inter-protein distance
d\sim 2\,\mu\text{m}
. Given that the bacteriorhodopsin lateral diffusion coefficient of
\sim 1.2\,\mu\text{m}^{2}/\sec
, the typical values of bacteriorhodopsin lateral diffusion velocity is
v\sim 2\,\mu\text{m}/\sec
. According to the analysis of
Section

III.2
, this implies a flow-induced membrane deformation with wavelength
\lambda\sim\kappa/(\eta v)\sim 1.5\,\mu\text{m}
, comparable with the inter-protein spacing. Given that this wavelength is comparable to the spacing between bacteriorhodopsins, flow-induced membrane deformations may imply a potential, significant effect on bacteriorhodopsin interactions and patterning on the giant unilamellar vesicle surface.

Future work could leverage our results to study protein inclusion clustering in membranes.
In fact, our analysis may be extended to study systems with more than two protein inclusions, examining how collective effects influence membrane deformation and forces, both with and without membrane flows. This analysis can also provide novel insights on protein inclusion clusters
Radhakrishnan et al. 2012
;
Johannes et al. 2018
, as well as on the effect of membrane-mediated forces on the cluster assembly mechanisms and scaling laws. Finally, a full stability analysis of the governing equations may reveal the existence of flow-driven instabilities at larger velocities, with implications for membrane trafficking
Bethani et al. 2010
and mechanosensitive processes in cells
Di et al. 2023
.
