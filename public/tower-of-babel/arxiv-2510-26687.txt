Abstract
The properties of stars and planets are shaped by the initial conditions of their natal clouds. However, the spatial scales over which the initial conditions can exert a significant influence are not well constrained. We report the first evidence for parsec-scale spatial correlations of stellar magnetospheric inclinations ( i mag i_{\rm mag} ), observed in the Lupus low-mass star forming region. Applying consensus clustering with a hierarchical density-based clustering algorithm, we demonstrate that the detected spatial dependencies are stable against perturbations by measurement uncertainties. The i mag i_{\rm mag} correlation scales are on the order of ∼ \sim 3 pc, which aligns with the typical scales of the Lupus molecular cloud filaments. Our results reveal a connection between large-scale forces—in the form of expanding shells from the Upper Scorpius and Upper-Centaurus-Lupus regions—and sub-au scale system configurations. We find that Lupus has a non-uniform i mag i_{\rm mag} distribution and suggest that this results from the preferential elongation of protostellar cores along filamentary axes. Non-uniformity would have significant implications for exoplanet occurrence rate calculations, so future work should explore the longevity of these biases driven by the star-cloud connection.



I
Introduction

The development of pre-main-sequence stars and their surrounding protoplanetary disks occurs in the context of their larger-scale natal molecular clouds. The mass, velocity, angular momentum, and magnetic field structures of the progenitor interstellar medium (ISM) may have observable effects on the same features of T Tauri stars (TTSs) and disks.
It is well known that TTSs are influenced by their surrounding environments, such as through external irradiation from massive stars
(
Winter & Haworth 2022
;
Maucó et al. 2025
;
Allen et al. 2025
, e.g.,)
, Bondi-Hoyle-Lyttleton infall from the surrounding interstellar medium
(
Winter et al. 2024a
, ISM; e.g.,)
, and interactions with neighboring bodies such as binary companions
(
Kurtovic et al. 2022
, e.g.,)
and dynamical encounters from flybys
(
Cuello et al. 2023
;
Winter et al. 2024b
, e.g.,)
. Additionally, star formation trends have been found to be connected across parsec to kiloparsec scales
(
Mendigutía et al. 2018
)
.
The importance of the parsec-scale natal cloud conditions to the observed characteristics in the Class II stage (when the magnetospherically-accreting systems are called classical TTSs; CTTSs) has not been well established. Additionally, the physical scales over which initial conditions can create coherent structures are unclear.

The Lupus star-forming region (SFR) provides a strong sample within which to search for spatial dependencies, as it is relatively close
(
Luhman 2020
,
\sim
160 pc,)
and young
(
Galli et al. 2020
, 1–3 Myr)
, and it contains many well-characterized CTTSs in relatively low extinction regions
(
Manara et al. 2023
)
.
Lupus contains four subregions with CTTSs (Lupus I–IV), and its cloud morphologies have been shaped by expanding shells from high-mass stellar activity in the nearby Upper Scorpius (USco) and Upper-Centaurus-Lupus (UCL) regions
(
de Geus 1992
;
Tachihara et al. 1996
;
Tachihara et al. 2001
;
Moreira & Yun 2002
;
Preibisch et al. 2002
;
Comerón 2008
;
Gaczkowski et al. 2015
;
Gaczkowski et al. 2017
)
. Such large-scale flows are well-established “molecular cloud factories”
(
Dawson et al. 2011
;
Dawson et al. 2015
;
Dawson 2013
;
Krause et al. 2013
;
Pineda et al. 2023
)
and may be able to produce associated large-scale coherent kinematic structures.

Lupus I contains a primary filament that is 5–7 pc in length and 1.6 parsec in width. It is parallel to, and co-moving with, the edge of the USco HI shell
(
Tothill et al. 2009
;
Benedettini et al. 2015
;
Gaczkowski et al. 2015
;
Gaczkowski et al. 2017
)
.
Its CO maps suggest the presence of coherent rotation around the axis of the southeast portion of the filament, spanning over 1 pc
(
Tothill et al. 2009
)
, as has been found in other ISM filaments
(
Heyer et al. 1987
;
Dhabal et al. 2018
;
Pineda et al. 2023
, e.g.)
. Its large-scale magnetic field in the plane of the sky is oriented perpendicular to the filament axis
(
Myers & Goodman 1991
;
Rizzo et al. 1998
;
Franco & Alves 2015
;
Soler et al. 2016
)
, as has also been commonly found in other molecular cloud filaments
(
Heyer et al. 1987
;
Pereyra & Magalhães 2004
;
Alves et al. 2008
)
. It appears to be undergoing active star formation
(
Vilas-Boas et al. 2000
;
Benedettini et al. 2012
;
Rygl et al. 2013
)
.

The main Lupus II filament spans 40′
(
Moreira & Yun 2002
)
, which corresponds to 1.8 pc in projection when assuming a distance of 157 pc.
Its local magnetic field is nearly parallel to the filamentary axis
(
Myers & Goodman 1991
)
, which is more common in diffuse matter
(
Franco & Alves 2015
;
Planck Collaboration et al. 2016a
)
. This is consistent with Lupus II’s lower density compared to the other three subregions
(
Cambrésy 1999
;
Tachihara et al. 1996
)
. Velocity gradients in its CO observations may indicate large-scale streaming motions along the filament resulting from interaction with the USco and/or UCL shells
(
Moreira & Yun 2002
)
.

The Lupus III region is the most densely populated in terms of CTTSs, and it is likely the most evolved region
(
Benedettini et al. 2012
;
Rygl et al. 2013
;
Benedettini et al. 2018
)
. Its local magnetic field orientation is approximately perpendicular to the main filament
(
Myers & Goodman 1991
;
Planck Collaboration et al. 2016b
;
Aizawa et al. 2020
)
, which spans
\sim
4 pc with a width of 1 pc
(
Tothill et al. 2009
)
. Finally, Lupus IV has a fairly complex filamentary structure, but its longest filaments span
\sim
3 pc
(
Benedettini et al. 2015
)
. Its local magnetic field is particularly well-ordered and oriented perpendicular to the eastern filamentary axis and nearly parallel to the western axis
(
Rizzo et al. 1998
)
. It also shows velocity gradients that may originate from interaction with the UCL shell shock front
(
Moreira & Yun 2002
)
.

In this work, we examine spatial correlations of CTTS magnetospheric inclinations in Lupus. This connects scales below 0.1 au in individual systems to the parsec scales of the host region.
In Section
II
, we describe our sample and analysis methods. In Section
III
, we present our results, and we discuss the implications in Section
IV
. Finally, we present a summary in Section
V
.

We confirm that all CTTSs show detectable accretion emission above the chromosphere in H
\alpha
, and then perform accretion flow model fits to obtain
i_{\rm mag}
following
Pittman et al. 2025a
and using the stellar parameters of
Alcalá et al. 2014
;
Alcalá et al. 2017
;
Manara et al. 2023
, adjusted to
d_{\rm Gaia}
.
Because HST spectra are not available for these non-ULLYSES targets, we use a uniform
\rm T_{shock}
value of 5260 K, which is the median
\rm T_{shock}
found for the Lupus targets in
Pittman et al. 2025a
. As before, we mask out any absorption features that cannot be attributed to a magnetospheric origin.
We calculate these new model grids with
i_{\rm mag}
ranging from 0
∘
–85
∘
in steps of 5
∘
for all CTTSs, and we calculate the mean and standard deviation of
i_{\rm mag}
from the top 1000 best-fit models weighted by their associated exp(-
\chi^{2}
/2). When multiple epochs are available, the final fit values are taken to be the weighted means of the individual epochs.
Though our statistical uncertainties can be very small, we enforce a minimum
i_{\rm mag}
uncertainty of 5
∘
as a more conservative estimate that reflects the model grid resolution.
We perform tests in Appendix
A.2
to validate our
i_{\rm mag}
results.
The final sample of 61 CTTSs is listed in Table
1
. Eleven are in Lupus I, three are in Lupus II, 31 are in Lupus III, seven are in Lupus IV, and nine are off-cloud
(
Luhman 2020
)
.

II.1
Consensus clustering analysis with
HDBSCAN

To test for spatial correlations of
i_{\rm mag}
, we apply the density-based hierarchical clustering algorithm
HDBSCAN

(
Campello et al. 2013
)
as implemented in Python by
McInnes et al. 2017
.
HDBSCAN
has been used for open cluster identification
(
Kounkel & Covey 2019
;
Hunt & Reffert 2021
;
Vioque et al. 2023
, e.g.,)
and is particularly useful for detecting clusters of different densities
(
Campello et al. 2013
)
. For our case, we use
HDBSCAN
to assign each CTTS to a group based on its 4D characteristics (the three spatial coordinates plus
i_{\rm mag}
). To produce equal parameter weights, we apply robust scaling to the 4D inputs as implemented in the
RobustScaler
of
scikit-learn
, adjusting them to each have a median of zero and an interquartile range of one. This also ensures that the absolute range of observed
i_{\rm mag}
values does not influence the clustering, as even a sample biased towards high or low
i_{\rm mag}

and

For each MC realization,
HDBSCAN
assigns each star a label that either places it in a group or marks it as noise. Group labels are not connected across different
HDBSCAN

(
Monti et al. 2003

, which is a probabilistic analysis of the pairwise relationships between individual CTTSs. For each pair, we determine the co-membership probability (

) by calculating the percentage of the 5000
HDBSCAN
instantiations in which the pair is classified into the same group (ignoring cases in which both are marked as noise). If
P_{ij}
is above an empirically-defined probability threshold (
P_{\rm min}
), then the pair is marked as connected.

We construct an adjacency matrix
A
in which each element
A_{ij}
is 1 if
P_{ij}>P_{\rm min}
and 0 if
P_{ij}<P_{\rm min}
. We then apply the
connected_components

scipy.sparse.csgraph

P_{ij,{\rm mean}}
) is above
P_{\rm min}
. To remove very low-probability group members, we calculate the percentage of the 5000
HDBSCAN
instantiations in which each CTTS is marked as noise (
P_{\rm noise}
) and remove any with
P_{\rm noise}

P_{\rm noise,max}

A.1
describes the determination of
P_{\rm min}
and

HDBSCAN
requires three main user inputs:
min_cluster_size
, which is the minimum number of members required to be classified as a group;
min_samples

min_cluster_size

or changed to
leaf

We choose the

To choose the appropriate hyperparameters for
HDBSCAN
, we first optimize the model to group the CTTSs into their appropriate Lupus subregions based only on their 3D coordinates, which we call the
3D groups
. We create 5000 MC samples by perturbing

based on the associated uncertainties. We then choose physically-motivated

and
min_samples

P_{\rm min}
and
P_{\rm noise,max}
thresholds during consensus clustering so that all on-cloud CTTSs are successfully grouped, and any CTTS marked as noise is an off-cloud source. Details can be found in Appendix
A.1
.

Once we determine the best hyperparameter values and consensus clustering thresholds, we incorporate
i_{\rm mag}
as the fourth dimension and repeat the above process, the result of which we call the
4D groups
. If the optimized algorithm finds stable groups that correspond to the nominal spatial correlations of
i_{\rm mag}
in the plane of the sky, this provides evidence that the correlations are physical and robust against perturbation within the uncertainties.
We ensure that the 5000 MC samples produce converged results by testing runs with 10,000, 20,000, and 50,000 MC samples in size and confirming that the reported group assignments remain unchanged for both the 3D groups and the 4D groups.

To test for any systematic effects from introducing
i_{\rm mag}
as a fourth dimension, we repeat the 4D clustering process by assigning new randomly-generated
i_{\rm mag}
values that are consistent with the observed
i_{\rm mag}
distribution during each MC realization. Then, to test for any spurious correlations that could be introduced by perturbing
i_{\rm mag}
by the associated uncertainties (
\sigma_{i_{\rm mag}}
), we repeat the 4D clustering process a final time by creating an initial sample with randomly-assigned
i_{\rm mag}
, and then perturb it by our
\sigma_{i_{\rm mag}}
values. We repeat this with 100 different initial
i_{\rm mag}
samples. These tests will also ensure that the 4D groups do not result purely from the 3D distribution of the sources, and the results can be found in Appendix
A.1
.

Here is a summary of the procedure:

1.

Optimize the
HDBSCAN
user inputs by determining the values that produce a successful classification of the known spatial structure in the region (the Lupus I-IV subregions) when considering only their 3D spatial locations perturbed by their

uncertainties.

2.

Apply this optimized procedure with the addition of
i_{\rm mag}
as the fourth dimension, perturbed by the associated
i_{\rm mag}
uncertainties, to confirm the spatial correlations of
i_{\rm mag}
seen in the plane of the sky.

3.

Validate the addition of
i_{\rm mag}
as the fourth dimension by applying the same procedure, except with a new random set of
i_{\rm mag}
assigned during each MC realization.

4.

Validate the
i_{\rm mag}
measurement uncertainties by applying the same procedure, except by perturbing a single random set of

by the

measurement uncertainties during each MC realization (and repeat with different initial random sets).

IV.1

Winter et al. 2024a
found a spatial dependency of stellar accretion rates (

Using angular separations, these authors find higher

The

IV.2
Clusters in Context

Figure
1
(left) shows our sample in the context of dust overdensities in the Lupus clouds, and Figure

(

;
Preibisch et al. 2002
)
, and the Upper-Centaurus-Lupus (UCL) wind bubble from the southwest, which is actively driven by the UCL B stars
(

;
Gaczkowski et al. 2017
)
. These shells are plotted using the geometric information in
Gaczkowski et al. 2017
, where the inner and outer radii of the USco shell are 30 pc and 36 pc, respectively. Radii between 15–22 pc are plotted for the UCL bubble for ease of comparison with the Lupus CTTSs. Its total extent reaches a radius of
\sim 110
pc
(
de Geus 1992
)
, with the initial expansion coming from an HI shell.
The current UCL bubble is perpetually driven by the stellar emission, so it does not have a fixed radial extent.

The observed disk PAs may tend towards being parallel to one of the shells. This can be seen with respect to the UCL bubble in the PAs of Sz 65/Sz 66, all Lupus IV members, and the Lupus III systems that are more distant from the main filament (see Figure
3
, bottom left). The wide binary of Sz 65 and Sz 66 is particularly interesting, showing PA alignment within 28.2
∘
,
i_{\rm disk,gas}
alignment within 3.2
∘
, and nearly equivalent
i_{\rm mag}
values. The USco and UCL shell surfaces are nearly perpendicular near Sz 65/Sz 66, and this may have produced a coherent force that resulted in their similar configurations.

In Table
2
, we see that
s_{\rm med}
decreases monotonically from Group I NW through Group IV. This also corresponds to increasing distance from the USco shell. While the sample is small, this may indicate that proximity to the USco shell can produce larger correlation scales. The USco shell also marks a natural division between Group I NW, Group I SE, and Group II, suggesting a physical origin for these observed
i_{\rm mag}
groups.
In the following sections, we will discuss potential physical origins beginning with the largest subregion, Lupus III.

IV.2.1
Lupus III

Lupus III is significantly more populated than the other regions, with 32 members included in the associated
HDBSCAN
group. The Group III CTTSs show a strong tendency towards high
i_{\rm mag}
values, with a median/RMS of
75^{\circ}\pm 12^{\circ}
. In the plane of the sky, the cause of these high inclinations is not apparent. However, when viewed in three dimensions, it becomes clear that we are looking almost directly down the axis of a filamentary configuration of CTTSs (see Figure
1
, bottom), with the axis within
\sim
3
∘
of our line of sight.

Given that we find
i_{\rm mag}
is typically within 20
∘

i_{\rm disk,gas}

i_{\rm disk,gas}

∘
–65
∘

(
Yen et al. 2018
;
Trapman et al. 2025

. Similarly, fourteen out of sixteen Lupus III

∘
–81
∘

(
Tazzari et al. 2017
;
Guerra-Alvarado et al. 2025
)
.
The viewing geometry, combined with the high inclinations, implies that the disk planes are biased against being perpendicular to the major axis of the filamentary structure.
This is consistent with expectations for cloud collapse and angular momentum conservation in filaments, as protostellar cores tend to be elongated along the major axis
(
Myers et al. 1991
;
Lee & Myers 1999
;
Jones et al. 2001
;
Hartmann 2002
)
with rotation axes perpendicular to the major axis

;
Misugi et al. 2024

(
Kong et al. 2019

and have been found to show alignment on

(
Green et al. 2024
)

Our observation of high-
i_{\rm mag}
sources when looking down the filamentary axis suggests that the elongation observed in protostellar cores may propagate through to the Class II stage.

IV.2.2
Lupus I

The Lupus I subregion has likely been compressed between the USco shell and UCL bubble, causing its filamentary structure to be parallel to the USco shell
Gaczkowski et al. 2015
;
Gaczkowski et al. 2017
as expected from models of molecular cloud and star formation
(
Hartmann et al. 2001
;
Heitsch & Hartmann 2008
)
.
The northern portion has been found to have lower densities compared to the southern region
(
Gaczkowski et al. 2015

Our analysis of the 3D distribution indicates that the expanding shell has surpassed the northernmost off-cloud source, 2MASSJ15414081-3345188, which is now within the shell’s inner cavity. The six Group I NW group members tend to be near the inner wall, whereas the five Group I SE group members tend to be near the outer front. The southernmost source, Sz 77, is considered off-cloud and may be in, or just outside, the shell front.

We see that the six Group I NW members lie in two lines along our line of sight. One line consists of the wide binary Sz 65 and Sz 66, and the other consists of the remaining four members. Analysis of the 3D structure shows that they are physically aligned in 3D space as well, in two approximately parallel lines. These systems are also observed to be highly inclined, with a median/RMS
i_{\rm mag}
of
79^{\circ}\pm 7^{\circ}
. Considering the above discussion of Lupus III, it is reasonable to surmise that these systems also formed along a filament whose major axis lies along our line of sight, causing the initial core elongation to occur along the filament axis.

Conversely, the five Group I SE members are much more distributed in 3D space, with the strongest linear alignment being in the plane of the sky. Along with this, this group has the lowest
i_{\rm mag}
in Lupus, with a median/RMS
i_{\rm mag}
of
50^{\circ}\pm 6^{\circ}
.

IV.2.3
Lupus II

Lupus II is the smallest group, with 4 members, consistent with it being the lowest-mass Lupus subregion
(
Tachihara et al. 1996
)
. In the plane of the sky, the three on-cloud members fall on a line parallel to the main filament and the local magnetic field (see Figure
3
, middle right). Lupus II contains one additional CTTS (RU Lup) that falls in line with the other three on-cloud members, but its large RUWE value prohibits its inclusion in the 3D analysis. This region’s CO velocity gradient is oriented within 5
∘
of the UCL expansion direction
(
Moreira & Yun 2002
)
. This suggests a long-lasting effect on gas motions resulting from the initial interaction
\sim
2 Myr ago, and/or the continued influence of the UCL stellar-wind-driven bubble. The stars show only moderate alignment along our line of sight, consistent with their moderate median/RMS
i_{\rm mag}
of
65^{\circ}\pm 7^{\circ}
, and most likely did not form from a strong filamentary structure.

IV.2.4
Lupus IV

Star formation in the Lupus IV subregion was likely triggered by the initial UCL HI shell, as its C
18
O velocity gradients are consistent with a propagating shock front oriented within 5
∘
of the direction of the bubble’s spherical expansion
(
Moreira & Yun 2002
)
.
The four PAs in Lupus IV are all approximately parallel to the UCL expansion front, providing further validation that the initial conditions of their formation might have produced parsec-scale spatial correlations.
These appear in a line along our line of sight, but their overall 3D distribution is more coplanar than filamentary.
Their relatively high median/RMS
i_{\rm mag}
of
71^{\circ}\pm 8^{\circ}
may result from the initial collision between the UCL HI shell and the local ISM, rather than the purely filamentary collapse that seems more likely in Group I NW and Group III.

IV.2.5
Conclusions

The spatial correlations that we have found in Lupus I–IV suggest that the effects of initial conditions may persist on Myr timescales. Future work should search for similar spatial correlations in regions of different ages to determine whether these trends are eventually disrupted by turbulence. Additionally, diffuse regions with minimal influence from massive star feedback should be studied to test whether these spatial correlations can be produced by weaker forces. As discussed in Appendix
A.3
, the Orion OB1b and Taurus CTTSs in our sample show hints of
i_{\rm mag}
and PA spatial correlations, so our results may apply to regions with very different characteristics than Lupus.

Our analysis requires large samples of CTTSs with high spectral resolution H
\alpha
observations, homogeneously-determined stellar parameters, and reliable
d_{\rm Gaia}
measurements. Such data are currently available for
\sim
50 CTTSs in the Chamaeleon I region from
Manara et al. 2023
, as well as 37 CTTSs in Taurus from the GHOsT project
(
Alcalá et al. 2021
;
Gangi et al. 2022
)
. We will pursue this analysis in future work. Orion OB1b contains 65 CTTSs
(
Briceño et al. 2019
)
, but very few have high-resolution observations and homogeneous stellar parameter determinations. Therefore, additional observations and analysis are required to further study spatial correlations of
i_{\rm mag}
in Orion OB1b.

Our results have the important implication that while
i_{\rm mag}
(and other system orientations) may be uniform when considering all parts of the sky together (see Appendix
A.2.1
), surveys that focus on individual regions may not be able to assume uniformity.
If these inclination trends propagate to the ultimate star-planet orbital planes, there would be significant implications for exoplanet survey completeness corrections, which typically assume uniformly distributed viewing inclinations.

V
Summary

For the first time, we have drawn a direct observational connection between parsec-scale molecular cloud characteristics and sub-au scale CTTS properties. Future work should examine whether these trends occur in the absence of high-mass stellar feedback processes, as well as whether they persist over longer timescales (such as in older regions and in exoplanet orbital inclinations).

To summarize:

•

We have measured magnetospheric inclinations,
i_{\rm mag}
, for 61 CTTSs in Lupus, combining our previous results from the HST ULLYSES sample in
Pittman et al. 2025a
with 36 new
i_{\rm mag}
measurements from this work.

•

We applied consensus clustering with the hierarchical density-based clustering algorithm
HDBSCAN
to confirm a spatial correlation of
i_{\rm mag}
on parsec scales.

•

The observed correlation scales, cluster morphologies, and disk position angles point to a connection between the expanding shells that shape the Lupus clouds, the filamentary structure of the subregions, and the CTTS properties observed on sub-au scales.

A.1
Clustering Algorithm Design and Testing

Here we present the results of our tests to determine the design and reliability of our
HDBSCAN
clustering procedure.
We design the algorithm to be optimized to categorize the Lupus CTTSs into the four appropriate Lupus subregions based on their 3D spatial locations alone.
We choose
cluster_selection_method
=
excess of mass
to prioritize the most stable groups. The
min_cluster_size
hyperparameter defines the minimum number of CTTSs that must be connected for
HDBSCAN
to recognize them as a cluster. It must be greater than 1, as a group containing a single member is not meaningful, and it should not exceed the size of the smallest group that we aim to identify.
The smallest subregion is Lupus II, with 3 on-cloud CTTSs and 1 nearby off-cloud CTTS. These factors restrict
min_cluster_size
to be 2, 3, or 4. Then, the
min_samples
hyperparameter sets the strictness of the algorithm, with larger values causing more CTTSs to be marked as noise rather than being assigned to a group.
HDBSCAN
’s default choice is to set
min_samples
equal to
min_cluster_size
.

Only two sets of [
min_cluster_size
,
min_samples
] successfully classify the CTTSs into the four Lupus subregions: [3, 3] and [4, 3]. However, the latter results in a high probability (
\sim
0.6) of merging Lupus II sources into Lupus I, so we choose [3, 3] as the final values.

A.2.1
The measured
i_{\rm mag}
distribution

A sample of random inclinations should be uniform in
\cos(i_{\rm mag})
between 0 and 1, with a median
\cos(i_{\rm mag})
of 0.5 (corresponding to 60
∘
). Our 61
i_{\rm mag}
measurements for Lupus, which are dominated by highly-inclined CTTSs in the Lupus III region, have a median
\cos(i_{\rm mag})
of 0.3 (see Figure
8
, bottom left).
We wish to confirm that this bias towards higher
i_{\rm mag}
is not a product of a bias in the modeling procedure, so we examine
i_{\rm mag}
values measured from the flow model for other samples of CTTSs.

Pittman et al. 2025a
applied the same modeling procedure to 65 CTTSs in Taurus, Chamaeleon I,
\epsilon
Cha,
\eta
Cha, Lupus (25 of which are included in this work), Orion OB1b,
\sigma
Ori, and Corona Australis (CrA). The distribution of their
\cos(i_{\rm mag})
results are shown in Figure
8
(top left), and this has a median
\cos(i_{\rm mag})
of 0.5, in agreement with a uniform distribution.

To characterize the uniformity further, we perform the following Monte Carlo cumulative distribution function (CDF) analysis. For each of the
\cos(i_{\rm mag})
samples
(
Pittman et al. 2025a
, this work and that of)
, we first create 2000 CDFs drawn from a uniform distribution of the same sample size as the
\cos(i_{\rm mag})
measurements. We show the median of the 2000 CDFs as the thick gray lines in Figure
8
(right column), which is a straight line from 0 to 1. We then take the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the 2000 CDFs to calculate the 95% confidence interval (shown as the shaded gray region). This spans the range of CDFs that are likely to be consistent with
\cos(i_{\rm mag})
being uniform between 0 to 1. We then create 2000 CDFs drawn from the observed
\cos(i_{\rm mag})
distributions perturbed by their standard deviations (
\sigma_{i_{\rm mag}}
), and then calculate their 95% confidence interval (shown as the shaded black region). We see that the
(
Pittman et al. 2025a
)
sample from 8 SFRs is consistent with uniformity within the 95% confidence intervals (Figure
8
, top right). Conversely, the Lupus sample in this work is inconsistent with uniformity within the 95% confidence intervals (Figure
8
, bottom right).
Instead, the Lupus sample is approximately uniform in
\cos i_{\rm mag}
in the narrower band between 0 and 0.7, within uncertainties.
This comparison shows that the non-uniformity of
i_{\rm mag}
is a feature of the Lupus sample in particular, rather than of the modeling procedure itself. We discuss the geometric cause of this non-uniformity in Section
IV.2
.

A.2.2
Effect of inclination on the H
\alpha
profile

Figure
9
shows the effect of
i_{\rm mag}
on the observed H
\alpha
profile for 6 CTTSs in the sample. For this test, the values of
R_{\rm i}
,
W_{\rm r}
,
\dot{M}
, and
\rm T_{max}
are fixed to values near those in Table
3
. The exact H
\alpha
profile morphology associated with a given
i_{\rm mag}
value depends on both the stellar parameters and the other flow model parameters. In Sz 68, for example, the highly inclined models have brighter peaks on the blue wing, whereas in Sz 131, the brighter peaks are in the red wing. In V1094 Sco, the lower-inclination models show self-absorption on the blue side, whereas in Sz 90 they are on the red side. Therefore, there is no single H
\alpha
profile element that would cause different CTTSs to cluster towards having similar
i_{\rm mag}
values.

A.2.3
Degeneracy Tests

We checked whether the measured inclinations are correlated with any other system parameters. We found a moderate correlation between
i_{\rm mag}
and
\log_{10}
M
⋆
(with a Pearson correlation coefficient of
p=-0.4
), where M
⋆
comes from
Pittman et al. 2025a
and
Alcalá et al. 2014
;
Alcalá et al. 2017
. This can be attributed to the different mass distributions of the samples in each subregion, which also have different inclination distributions. In our sample, the median M
⋆
is 0.30 M
☉
in Lupus I, 0.37 M
☉
in Lupus II, 0.16 M
☉
in Lupus III, 0.21 M
☉
in Lupus IV, and 0.32 M
☉
in the off-cloud CTTS.
We found no correlation between
i_{\rm mag}
and the stellar-mass-normalized
\dot{M}
(
\dot{M}
/
{\rm M}_{\star}^{2}
).

In
Pittman et al. 2025a
, we noted that our corner plot analysis did not indicate any significant degeneracy between
i_{\rm mag}
and other flow model parameters, either on an individual-object scale or on the conglomerate results from the full sample. Therefore, it is unlikely that the measured
i_{\rm mag}
values result from degeneracy with the other model parameters.

A.2.4
Significance of the chromosphere

We also tested whether the inclusion of a Gaussian component representative of the chromosphere in the lower accretors influenced their measured inclinations.
There are eleven CTTSs whose best-fit inclinations change by more than 10
∘
when the Gaussian component is not included in the fitting procedure. Inspection of the fits shows that eight of these CTTSs clearly require a chromospheric contribution, as the magnetospheric model alone is unable to account for both the narrow central emission and the broad wings. The magnetospheric model alone can explain the H
\alpha
profiles of the remaining three CTTSs; however, they all have spectral types of M3 or later and accretion rates below
5\times 10^{-10}
M
☉
yr
-1
. In this parameter space, the chromosphere should be a significant contribution to the H
\alpha
profile. Therefore, we conclude that these eleven inclinations that depend on the inclusion of the chromospheric component remain trustworthy.

A.3
Other star-forming regions

Aizawa et al. 2020
did not find any significant nonuniformity in PAs in the Taurus Molecular Cloud, the Upper Scorpius OB Association, the
\rho
Ophiuchi Cloud Complex, or the Orion Nebula Cluster. Still, our results do not indicate random distributions of inclinations within individual star-forming regions (though the sample sizes are too small to be conclusive).
Five out of seven nominal magnetospheric inclinations in Orion OB1b are within the 8
∘
range between 54–62
∘
(with the remaining two being 33
∘
and 42
∘
; see Figure
10
, top left). This is notable given that the median stellar separation between these stars with reliable
d_{\rm Gaia}
is 20 pc. It could indicate the influence of nearby expanding shells from the massive stars in the region, but more observations and analysis are required to expand the sample to test for correlations in Orion OB1b.

The 5 northernmost Taurus systems (AA Tau, DE Tau, DG Tau A, DK Tau A, and DN Tau) have nominal inclinations in the 14
∘
range between 38–52
∘
,
and the PAs of the pairs AA/DN Tau and DK/DG Tau A are aligned within 7
∘
(Figure
10
, top right). In
Chamaeleon I, conversely,
i_{\rm mag}
spans the range between 26–78
∘
somewhat uniformly (Figure
10
, bottom). In the future, we will extend our analysis to include all
\sim
50 CTTSs in the Chamaeleon I region presented in
Manara et al. 2023
, as well as 37 CTTSs in Taurus from the GHOsT project
(
Alcalá et al. 2021
;
Gangi et al. 2022
)
, to examine whether statistically-significant
i_{\rm mag}
correlations appear when more CTTSs are included.
