Design optimization of battery enclosures under nonlinear impact loading using a K-Kriging hybrid surrogate model
Authors: Hongwei Li, Feng Chen, Changdong Wan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
With the rapid development of new energy vehicles (EVs), the battery enclosure’s design has become a critical bottleneck, requiring an optimal balance between extensive lightweighting and stringent crashworthiness standards. However, the high nonlinearity of crash responses and the excessive computational cost of high-fidelity finite element (FE) models pose significant challenges for structural optimization. This study proposes a comprehensive Simulation-Modeling-Optimization-Decision collaborative framework to address these issues. First, a high-precision adaptive surrogate model is developed by fusing the Kriging model with the K-Nearest Neighbor (KNN) algorithm (K-Kriging) to capture complex nonlinear responses efficiently. Second, a multi-objective optimization (MOO) problem is formulated, considering 16 component groups with both thickness and material selection variables (totaling 32 design variables). The objectives are to minimize material cost and structural mass while maximizing the first-order natural frequency, subject to stringent crashworthiness constraints derived from nonlinear crushing and impact simulations. The Non-dominated Sorting Genetic Algorithm II (NSGA-II) is employed to search for the Pareto frontier. To identify the optimal compromise solution, a Modified VIKOR (M-VIKOR) method, integrating Grey Relational Analysis (GRA), is implemented to mitigate the subjectivity of weight assignments. Results demonstrate that the optimized design achieves a 17.03% reduction in material cost, a 16.91% reduction in structural mass, and a 19.87% increase in the first-order natural frequency compared to the baseline design. The proposed framework not only ensures superior predictive accuracy (relative errors < 5%) but also provides a robust methodology for the multidisciplinary design of complex EV structural systems.

Introduction

In recent years, the intensifying global resource depletion and environmental challenges have necessitated a fundamental transition in energy structures1. Against this backdrop, New Energy Vehicles (EVs), as the primary catalysts for addressing the energy crisis and advancing the carbon peak and neutrality strategies, are spearheading a paradigm shift in the global automotive industry2. EVs, characterized by superior energy efficiency and zero-emission profiles, have emerged as the technological vanguard for major manufacturers. However, as the primary load-bearing component in EVs, the battery enclosure’s substantial mass and volume pose dual challenges to vehicle dynamics and safety while extending driving range3. Consequently, achieving significant weight reduction of battery enclosures while adhering to stringent safety standards has become a critical bottleneck for both academia and industry4.

The battery enclosure serves not only as a protective housing for battery modules but also as a pivotal structural component that protects the internal cells during extreme collision scenarios. Optimizing its structural attributes to balance weight and mechanical performance is essential for enhancing the energy density of battery packs. Over the past decade, extensive research has been conducted on the structural optimization of battery enclosures. For instance, Lu et al.4 proposed a multi-objective topology optimization method, resulting in an enclosure structure with enhanced dynamic and static performance. Shui5 introduced a four-stage design optimization framework that utilizes finite element (FE) analysis and multi-objective optimization (MOO) algorithms to achieve lightweighting while simultaneously improving anti-deformation capacity and the fundamental natural frequency. Xiong et al.6 validated the lightweight design and crashworthiness of a battery enclosure using Response Surface Methodology (RSM) and the Non-dominated Sorting Genetic Algorithm II (NSGA-II), achieving an 11.73% reduction in structural mass. Pan et al.7 developed a lightweight design strategy for automotive battery pack shells based on Advanced High-Strength Steels (AHSSs) and sizing optimization, achieving a 10.41% weight reduction and improved dynamic performance through multi-condition analysis. Wang et al.8 proposed a novel hybrid optimization strategy that successfully implemented multi-objective lightweighting, reducing total weight by 4.31% and crushing deformation by 5.97%, thereby significantly enhancing both crash safety and lightweight performance. Li9 developed a deformation prediction model for battery pack bottom shells under low-speed impact based on neural networks, providing reliable data support for lightweight design and safety warning systems. Xu10 evaluated the mechanical safety performance of battery pack systems (BPS) using five machine learning models, demonstrating their effectiveness in predicting the mechanical behavior of BPS under bottom impact, which offers critical insights for next-generation battery design.

In engineering practice, thin-walled components are widely adopted in battery enclosures to satisfy the dual requirements of energy absorption and lightweighting11. However, analyzing the crashworthiness of such structures involves complex nonlinear dynamic processes. Recent advances in structural optimization, such as the Bi-directional Evolutionary Structural Optimization (BESO) for I-beams under plastic-limit behavior12 and the Thermo-mechanical reliability-based topology optimization for imperfect elasto-plastic materials13, have successfully addressed complex nonlinearities and geometric imperfections. Despite their effectiveness in handling material nonlinearity and reliability constraints, these high-fidelity optimization strategies often require extensive computational resources. Direct iteration using high-fidelity FE models for optimization leads to an exponential increase in computational costs. To reconcile the conflict between computational accuracy and design efficiency, surrogate-model-based optimization strategies have demonstrated significant advantages in the design of rail transit and NEV bodies14. For example, Xiong et al.15 proposed a novel integrated optimization strategy for the multi-objective lightweight and crashworthiness optimization of car body side structures, verifying the method’s efficacy. Salmani et al.16 introduced a MOO approach based on the V-model and Taguchi-Grey Relational Analysis; by adjusting the materials and thicknesses of laminated structures, they achieved a 59.69% weight reduction while maintaining floor panel performance. Guo et al.17 presented a machine learning-based crashworthiness optimization framework for square-cone energy-absorbing structures in subway vehicles, utilizing the GRU model for performance prediction and the GRSM algorithm for parameter optimization. Their results showed a reduction in initial peak force to 618.41 kN and an increase in energy absorption to 297.99 kJ. Yao et al.18 proposed a collaborative crashworthiness optimization method for subway underframes based on weighted graph models and differential evolution, which maps structures to mathematical matrices for efficient optimization of complex configurations.

Despite these advancements, most existing studies focus on isolated performance indicators or geometric parameters, rarely examining the complex trade-offs between material cost, structural mass, and safety performance within a unified framework. The MOO of battery enclosures is, by nature, a multidisciplinary conflict-balancing problem: improvements in material performance often incur higher costs, while extreme lightweighting may compromise structural robustness.

Accordingly, this study aims to construct a comprehensive Simulation-Modeling-Optimization-Decision collaborative framework. Addressing the highly nonlinear response of battery enclosures, a high-precision adaptive surrogate model is proposed by fusing the Kriging model with the K-Nearest Neighbor (KNN) algorithm. By defining material cost, total mass, and the first-order frequency as conflicting objectives, a Modified VIKOR (M-VIKOR) multi-criteria decision-making (MCDM) method is employed to identify the optimal compromise solution. The results not only validate the superior performance of the adaptive surrogate model in handling complex nonlinear conditions but also provide a valuable methodology for the safe and lightweight design of EV battery systems.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows: Section "Model description and reliability verification" details the FE model and experimental validation of the battery enclosure. The section "Crushing and impact simulation" establishes the crushing and impact simulation models. The section "Multi-objective optimization design of the battery enclosure" elaborates on the MOO process based on the adaptive surrogate model. The section "Results and discussion" presents the verification of the optimal solution and an in-depth discussion. Finally, the section "Conclusions" concludes the paper and outlines future research directions.

Model description and reliability verification

Geometric topology and material system of the battery enclosure

As the primary load-bearing and protective unit of the electric vehicle, the battery enclosure plays a critical role in protecting the internal cells and absorbing energy under low-speed impact and complex crushing conditions. Its structural integrity is directly linked to the safety of the internal battery cells. The typical automotive battery enclosure investigated in this study comprises an upper cover, a bottom shell, internal stiffeners, and essential auxiliary components, as illustrated in Fig. 1. Specifically, the upper cover and the bottom shell are joined by high-strength bolts to ensure sealing performance and global structural stiffness under extreme operating conditions, while other structural members are predominantly secured via precision welding processes.

To explore the potential for lightweighting while maintaining safety margins, a multi-gradient material system was established. The battery enclosure primarily utilizes various grades of steel, spanning from conventional carbon structural steel (e.g., Q235) to advanced high-strength steel (e.g., DP980). Model inspection and verification

A high-performance FE model is the prerequisite for conducting nonlinear dynamic analysis. In this study, a full-scale FE model of the battery enclosure was developed using LS-DYNA software. To achieve an optimal balance between computational efficiency and simulation accuracy, a refined meshing strategy was implemented, resulting in a model consisting of 645,718 elements and 641,937 nodes.

During the pre-processing phase, the mesh quality underwent rigorous computer-aided precision inspections. By iteratively optimizing key parameters such as warpage, inner angle, Jacobian ratio, and aspect ratio, it was ensured that 99.9% of the elements met engineering accuracy requirements. Additionally, the material constitutive models (using the MAT24 piecewise linear plasticity model), boundary conditions, and complex contact-friction behaviors were precisely defined. These configurations provide a robust foundation for simulating the highly nonlinear responses characteristic of real-world impact scenarios.

To ensure that the FE model accurately reflects the dynamic characteristics of the structure, a comparative verification between simulated modal analysis and bench testing was performed. The experimental setup for the modal test is shown in Fig. 2. The impact hammer excitation method was employed, with data collected across 99 measurement points on the surface of the battery enclosure. Vibration signals were captured in real-time using eight PCB-356A16 accelerometers. Following processing by the LMS-SCM2E02 data acquisition system, the first six natural frequencies and corresponding mode shapes were extracted.

Table 3 presents the comparison between the simulated and experimental values for the first six modal frequencies. The results indicate that the relative errors for all modal frequencies are within 5%, with the error for the first-order natural frequency being only 1.24%. The simulated mode shapes (see Fig. 3) exhibit high fidelity with the experimental observations. This close agreement demonstrates that the established FE model possesses high credibility in characterizing the dynamic behavior of the battery enclosure, providing a reliable platform for the subsequent crashworthiness analysis and MOO design.

Crushing and impact simulation

Under extreme operating conditions such as vehicle collisions and crushing, the battery enclosure of an electric vehicle may be subjected to high stress and large structural deformations. Improper design could lead to the rupture of battery modules, potentially triggering severe safety issues including short circuits, explosions, or fires. Therefore, performing high-fidelity simulation analysis on the crashworthiness and crushing resistance of the battery enclosure is paramount for ensuring the operational safety of EVs.

Crushing analysis

The crushing analysis aims to simulate the severe intrusive deformation exerted by external obstacles on the battery pack during side-impact scenarios, thereby evaluating its protective capability for the internal cells. As illustrated in Fig. 4, the battery enclosure is positioned on a horizontal rigid plate. The power battery pack is subjected to crushing tests using a semi-cylindrical indenter with a radius of 75 mm in both the longitudinal (X-direction) and transverse (Y-direction) orientations. The loading continues until the crushing force reaches 100 kN or the structural deformation reaches 30% of the total dimension in the crushing direction. Notably, due to the asymmetric arrangement of battery modules along the X-axis, both the positive and negative X-directions are analyzed in this study. To enhance computational efficiency, the loading velocity of the indenter is set to 3 m/s. To ensure that this velocity does not introduce significant artificial inertial forces, a kinetic energy check was performed. The ratio of the total kinetic energy (KE) to the total internal energy (IE) was monitored throughout the simulation. As shown in Fig. 5 (refer to the figure description below), the maximum KE/IE ratio in both the X and Y-direction crushing simulations remained below 5%. This satisfies the quasi-static criterion, validating that the dynamic effects at 3 m/s are negligible and the results are equivalent to a standard quasi-static test. The boundary conditions are defined by constraining all six degrees of freedom (DOFs) of the rigid wall and five DOFs of the indenter, leaving only the DOF in the crushing direction. The simulations are performed using LS-DYNA, with total durations of 40 ms for the X-direction and 20 ms for the Y-direction.

Figure 6 presents the displacement contours of the battery enclosure during the crushing process. It can be observed that the deformation is predominantly localized on the impact side. When the crushing force reaches 100 kN, the maximum deformations in the X, -X, and Y directions are 44.48 mm, 21.85 mm, and 23.2 mm, respectively. The minimum clearances between the lower shell and the internal battery modules are recorded as 79.55 mm, 54.9 mm, and 44.6 mm, respectively. Comparative results demonstrate that a sufficient safety margin is maintained, indicating that the enclosure’s deformation remains within permissible limits during both longitudinal and lateral crushing. The simulation suggests that the deformation remains within permissible limits. It is predicted that the structure poses no threat to the battery modules under the defined loading conditions, indicating compliance with the safety requirements for battery pack crushing.

Impact simulation

The impact simulation is intended to verify the dynamic response characteristics of the battery enclosure during severe deceleration, thereby assessing the structural integrity and connection reliability under transient high-pulse acceleration loads. In this study, LS-DYNA is employed to simulate the impact responses in the X and Y directions, with the characteristics of the pulse wave illustrated in Fig. 7.

Figure 8 displays the stress contours of the battery enclosure during the simulated impact. The maximum equivalent stress is primarily concentrated near the lifting lugs. The peak stresses for the X- and Y-direction impact simulations are 248.04 MPa and 247.5 MPa, respectively. Although the maximum stresses exceed the Yield Strength of DC04, they remain below the Tensile Strength (300 MPa) of the material. This indicates that while plastic deformation may occur in localized areas, the structural integrity is maintained, and no rupture will happen, which is consistent with our safety constraints.

Multi-objective optimization design of the battery enclosure

Following the establishment of the high-fidelity FE models for various operating conditions, a MOO of the battery enclosure was performed. This section elaborates on the pivotal stages of the optimization process, encompassing variable screening, Design of Experiments (DOE), and the construction of surrogate models. The primary objective is to achieve an efficient trade-off between material cost, structural mass, and mechanical performance. The comprehensive optimization workflow is illustrated in Fig. 9.

Fig. 9Workflow of the optimization strategy.

Workflow of the optimization strategy.

Variable selection

The structural assembly of a battery enclosure comprises numerous components. To maximize the effectiveness of the optimization, the thicknesses and material types of all structural members were initially considered as design variables. To maximize the effectiveness of the optimization, the thicknesses and material types of 16 key structural components were identified as the design variables. Consequently, 32 design variables were established, comprising 16 thickness parameters (T1–T16) and 16 material discrete variables (M1–M16), as depicted in Fig. 10.

Furthermore, to achieve a multi-material property optimization, the material selection for these components was also defined as a discrete design variable. To facilitate the numerical computation and FE model invocation, a coding system was established where the 7 candidate materials (Material No. 101 to 107, as listed in Table 1) were represented by integer codes ranging from 101 to 107.

Selection of Design Variables.

Design of experiments

Given the high dimensionality and significant nonlinearity of the structural responses in this study, the Optimal Latin Hypercube Sampling (OLHS) method19 was employed. OLHS is favored for its superior capacity to achieve uniform space-filling properties and high-precision exploration of the design space with a relatively small number of sample points.

The design ranges for these variables are summarized in Table 4. It is noteworthy that, to comply with manufacturing constraints, the thickness variables were discretized with a step size of 0.1 mm within their respective bounds. Similarly, the material variables (M1–M16) were treated as discrete integers within the range of 101–107. During the simulation process, the FE model dynamically invokes the corresponding material properties (density, modulus, and cost) based on these assigned integer codes. Utilizing the initial design variables and their value ranges, 200 sets of sample points were generated via the OLHS method. This sample size was determined based on the rule of thumb for space-filling designs, which suggests that a sample size of 10×n (where n is the number of variables) is often sufficient to capture the global trend in the design space. These samples were subsequently used as inputs for the simulation models to obtain the corresponding performance response data, which are detailed in Table 5.

Construction of the adaptive K-Kriging surrogate model

The structural responses of the battery enclosure under crushing and impact loads exhibit highly nonlinear characteristics. In high-dimensional optimization problems, obtaining a large number of simulation samples is computationally prohibitive. Traditional individual surrogate models, such as Response Surface Methodology (RSM)20 or standard Kriging21, often encounter significant fitting errors and insufficient local prediction accuracy when dealing with complex, multi-variable coupled problems22. The proposed K-Kriging model introduces a novel hybrid architecture: it decomposes the prediction task into a global trend captured by Kriging and a local residual corrected by KNN. Unlike standard Kriging, which assumes residuals are purely random Gaussian noise, the K-Kriging model hypothesizes that part of the residual error exhibits local structure due to nonlinearity. By feeding the Kriging residuals into the KNN algorithm, the model adaptively learns these local patterns, thereby enhancing prediction accuracy in regions of high nonlinearity—such as the impact and crushing scenarios analyzed in this study. The construction strategy is outlined in Fig. 11 and proceeds as follows:

Fig. 11Construction process of the adaptive surrogate model.

Construction process of the adaptive surrogate model.

Step 1. The Kriging model assumes that the response value at an unknown point can be represented as a linear weighted combination of known sample points and a random error term.

where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\omega\:}_{i}$$\end{document} represents the weighting coefficients and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\epsilon \left({X}_{u}\right)$$\end{document} is the random error following a normal distribution \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:N(0,{\sigma\:}^{2})$$\end{document}. The Kriging model captures the global trend of the design space by describing the spatial correlation between sample points via a correlation function.

Step 2. The K model performs predictions based on the similarity of local sample points, making it particularly suitable for characterizing nonlinear and non-stationary error distributions. The prediction formula is given by.

where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{N}_{K}\left({X}_{u}\right)$$\end{document} denotes the set of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:K$$\end{document} nearest neighbor sample points to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{X}_{u}$$\end{document}.

Instead of treating the residual \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\epsilon \left({X}_{u}\right)$$\end{document} as pure noise, the model utilizes the KNN algorithm to approximate the residual function \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\widehat{\mathrm{E}}}_{KNN}\left({X}_{u}\right)$$\end{document}. Results and discussion

To evaluate the performance and efficacy of the proposed MOO framework, a systematic analysis and comparative validation were performed on the optimization results. This section focuses on the structural improvements in key performance indicators—namely material cost, mass, and modal frequency—relative to the baseline design. Furthermore, the performance of the optimal compromise solutions derived from different decision-making methodologies is extensively discussed.

The M-VIKOR method

The VIKOR method is extensively utilized in MCDM due to its structural simplicity and high adaptability. However, its outcomes are often susceptible to the subjectivity of weight assignments and the inherent uncertainty of evaluation values. In contrast, Grey Relational Analysis (GRA)24 effectively mitigates the subjectivity of weight distribution by quantifying the relational degrees between various indicators25. By integrating GRA with VIKOR, the resulting M-VIKOR approach can objectively characterize the interrelationships among attributes while comprehensively evaluating the merits of candidate solutions. Consequently, this hybrid methodology significantly enhances the robustness and reliability of the final decision-making results.

Verification of the optimal solution

To verify the reliability of the optimal compromise solution obtained via the adaptive surrogate model and the M-VIKOR decision-making framework, the optimized design variables were re-mapped into the high-fidelity FE model for a simulation-prediction bidirectional benchmarking verification. Table 8; Fig. 14 provide an exhaustive comparison between the surrogate model predictions and the FE simulation results.

Data analysis indicates that the optimal solution selected by the M-VIKOR method exhibits high consistency between the predicted and simulated values across the three core performance indicators: material cost, structural mass, and first-order frequency. Specifically, the material cost measured by FE simulation is 413.56 ¥, compared to the predicted value of 408.66 ¥, representing a relative error of only 1.18%. Regarding the lightweighting targets, the simulated and predicted total mass values are 66.73 kg and 68.74 kg, respectively, with the error contained within 3.01%. For the dynamic response, the simulated first-order modal frequency is 34.88 Hz, showing a deviation of 3.58% from the predicted value (36.13 Hz).

All relative errors for key indicators are significantly below the engineering tolerance threshold of 5%. This robustly demonstrates that the integrated K-Kriging adaptive surrogate model proposed in this study not only possesses excellent global convergence accuracy in high-dimensional nonlinear spaces but also exhibits strong robustness and predictive performance in the local region of the optimal solution.

Conclusions

This study addresses the challenges of structural optimization for electric vehicle battery enclosures under stringent crashworthiness constraints. By integrating nonlinear FE simulation, high-precision adaptive surrogate modeling, heuristic MOO algorithms, and advanced decision-making methods, a comprehensive collaborative optimization framework for battery enclosures has been established. Through engineering validation and comparative analysis, the following core conclusions are derived:Establishment of a High-Fidelity Evaluation Baseline: Through refined FE modeling and modal testing benchmarking, the reliability of the simulation model in characterizing the dynamic behavior of the battery enclosure was ensured, with a first-order frequency error of only 1.24%.Validation of Superior Adaptive Surrogate Performance: Addressing the complex multi-variable coupling and nonlinear characteristics of the battery enclosure, the proposed adaptive model—fusing Kriging and KNN algorithms—effectively reconciles the conflict between computational efficiency and predictive accuracy in high-dimensional spaces. Evaluation metrics demonstrate that this model possesses exceptional robustness in the local region of the optimal solution, significantly outperforming traditional individual surrogate models.Realization of Synergistic Multi-Objective Performance Gains: The Pareto non-dominated set obtained via the NSGA-II algorithm clearly elucidates the competitive trade-off relationships among material cost, structural mass, and the first-order frequency. The introduction of the M-VIKOR MCDM method effectively overcomes the subjective weight interference inherent in traditional decision-making. The final optimal compromise solution demonstrates that, while satisfying safety constraints, the material cost is reduced by 17.03% and structural mass by 16.91%, while the first-order modal frequency is simultaneously enhanced by 19.87%.
