Foliar application of Aloe vera and date palm extracts as natural biostimulants enhances yield, nutrient status and essential oil of cumin (Cuminum cyminum L.)
Authors: Fatimah Ali Hasan, Wurood Hantoosh Neamah, Widad A. Abd Al-Behadili, Ola Adnan Faeaz, Ayat Hussein Majeed, Heidar Meftahizade
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The study examined the individual and combined effects of Aloe Vera Leaf Extract (ALE) (0, 2, and 4 ml L− 1) and Date Palm Seed Extract (PSE) (2 and 4 g L− 1) on Cumin (Cuminum cyminum L.) growth, biochemical content/composition, nutrient content/composition, and essential oil profiles. Application of ALE 4 ml L− 1 and PSE 2 g L− 1 as foliar treatments improved the characteristics of reproductive development by increasing seed inflorescence weight and diameter by 122.84% and 58.32%, respectively, compared to the control. Spraying ALE (4 ml L− 1), PSE (4 g L− 1) + ALE (4 ml L− 1) + PSE (2 g L− 1) on foliage resulted in an increase of 8.33% in number of flowers per plant and 51.31% increase in total yield/plant. Total phenolic content increased by 10.40%, 8.50% and 8.76% with PSE 2 g L− 1, PSE 4 g L− 1 and ALE 2 ml L− 1 compared to the control, respectively. ALE at a concentration of 2 ml L− 1 significantly increased the total flavonoid content by 115.59% compared to the control. Carbohydrate content of seeds and leaves increased significantly by 30.27% and 102.55% with ALE 2 ml L− 1 compared to the control, respectively. The ALE 4 ml L− 1 treatment resulted in a 166.66% increase in sulfur, while the ALE 4 ml L− 1 + PSE 4 g L− 1 combination resulted in a 0.239% phosphorus content compared to the control. There were 4 total compounds that were identified from both the 4 ml L-1 ALE treatment and the 4 g L-1 PSE treatment, 1,3-benzodioxole, 4,5-dimethoxy-6-(2-propenyl)-, carvone, D-limonene. These studies suggest that both ALE and PSE can enhance the growth of plants, yield from their harvest, physicochemical properties, and phytochemical profiles; Therefore, enhancing the growth and development of plants through a more sustainable approach to enhance their production and increasing bioactive compound production will be achieved by using environmentally friendly, low-input chemicals such as ALE and PSE.

Introduction

The agricultural system has been confronted with a broad array of challenges that demand an increase in crop yield with minimal damage to the environment as a result of an overreliance on chemical fertilizers and pesticides during recent decades1. Due to growing concern for the safety of the food produced, as well as soil health, biodiversity, and climate change, sustainable food production is becoming more popular in the agriculture industry. Likewise, consumers have expressed a greater desire to purchase sustainable products2. Therefore, crop improvement and sustainable agriculture are two of the primary research focuses of the plant sciences in developing strategies that do not have adverse environmental effects3.

Plant biostimulants have become one of the promising strategies in respect of improving plant growth, yield and quality with consideration of sustainability4. Plant biostimulant refers to a naturally occurring substance or microorganism that, when applied to plants or their rhizosphere, has the ability to influence a plant’s physiological processes, enhance the efficiency of nutrient use, improve a plant’s tolerance of both biotic and abiotic stresses, and stimulate the production of bioactive compounds, in addition to providing direct nutrient input5,6. Biostimulants differ from conventional agronomic chemicals in that they regulate plant metabolism and signal pathway activity to support plant growth under both ideal and stressed situations7.

Extracts originating from plant sources are one of the most critical classifications of natural biostimulants because their concentration of bioactive materials (like phenolic compounds, amino acids, polysaccharides, vitamins, and phytohormones) is high relative to other categories of biostimulants8,9. Many studies have shown that the use of plant extracts increases food production by improving photosynthesis, root growth, nutrient absorption, antioxidant levels, and producing secondary metabolites in many types of crops9. Due to these qualities, plants extracts can be important tools for improving the yield and quality (mainly phytochemicals) of medicinal and aromatic plants due to their high level of yield and phytochemical composition.

Aloe Vera is an example of a biostimulant derived from plants that is heavily studied because they have a very large range of complex biochemical compounds. A. Vera gel extracts contain all the essential amino acids, vitamins, enzymes, phenolic compounds, and polysaccharides needed to help stimulate the growth and metabolism of plant growth10. Numerous research articles have provided ample evidence that A. Vera gel extracts enhance seed germination, encourage vegetative growth, improve nutrient absorption by plants and increase a plant’s resistance to various types of abiotic stress. Therefore, A. Vera can serve as an organic plant biostimulant11,12.

Likewise, extract obtained from date palm seeds (Phoenix dactylifera L.) is another by-product created from the cultivation of dates which has recently been identified as having potential for use as a natural antioxidant through the presence of polyphenols13. The use of date palm seeds in a biostimulant is consistent with the principles of sustainable agriculture as it adds value to currently unutilized waste materials and can also provide bioactive compounds that have been shown to enhance the physiological and chemical properties of plants14,15.

Cumin (Cuminum cyminum L.) is an important crop for agriculture and medicine that is commonly cultivated throughout arid/semiarid areas of the world16. The seeds from cumin plants contain numerous bioactive components and essential oils that provide several antioxidant, antimicrobial and pharmacological benefits to their users. Since agronomic management practices and environmental conditions have such great impacts on the amount and quality of different phytochemicals found in cumin seeds17,18, many people are interested in producing cumin crops by using more sustainable growing practices that maximize yields and phytochemical profiles for both farmers and the companies making products from cumin seeds through various methods of manufacturing19.

There are numerous studies that have looked at how biostimulants, along with plant-based extracts, affect many types of medical and aromatic plants such as Cuminum cyminum. Research has shown that applying seaweed extracts, amino acids, and microbial biostimulants can promote the growth, yield, and essential oil composition of cumin. Several studies have indicated that foliar application has affected the concentration of key essential oil constituents in cumin, including cuminaldehyde, y-terpinene, and p-cymene, which are essential for the medicinal and commercial value of cumin. However, the majority of studies have been done with one biostimulant source or conventional method, and therefore, the current study is introducing for the first time both A. vera and Date palm seed extracts combined in a foliar application. This study assesses the combined effects of using the extracts of A. vera and date palm seeds on the growth, yield, biochemical profile and GC-MS profile of cumin plants and the synergistic effects of these additives. Additionally, the results of this study will provide information to support environmentally-friendly agriculture and promote farmers globally to reduce their use of chemical fertilizer use.

Materials and methods

The experiment was conducted as a factorial arrangement within a randomized complete block design (RCBD) with two factors: A. vera leaf extract (ALE) with control (0, 2, and 4 ml L⁻1 as well as Date palm seed extract (PSE) (2 and 4 g L⁻1, resulting in six treatment combinations. Each treatment was replicated three times, with five plants per replicate considered as the experimental unit. Plants were grown under controlled environmental conditions (temperature: 25 ± 2 °C, relative humidity: 60–70% and natural photoperiod). Treatments were randomly assigned within each block to minimize the effects of environmental variability.

Seeds cultivation and treatment

Local cultivar seeds of C. cyminum L. were planted into containers filled with a substrate of soil/peat moss at a volume ratio of two to one and grown in controlled conditions until they reached about 15 cm height before being treated with their first spray application. Foliar treatments consisted of aqueous solutions of ALE at three concentrations (0 [control], 2, and 4 g L⁻¹) and PSE at two concentrations (2 and 4 ml L⁻¹). The control plants received distilled water only. All solutions were freshly prepared prior to application and uniformly sprayed onto the foliage until runoff.

Seed inflorescence traits

Weight of seed inflorescence

Seed-bearing inflorescence (umbels) were harvested manually from each experimental unit when 80 to 90% of the umbels were brown (full physiological maturity). When the seed umbel had dried on the plant (approximately 7 to 10 days), they were sent to the laboratory for air-drying at 25 ± 2 °C for a period of 7 to 10 days before being collected and then oven-dried at 70 °C to arrive at a constant weight. After both types of drying, a digital analytical balance (± 0.001 g) was used to weigh the entire seed-bearing inflorescence from each plant and report it as grams per plant (g plant⁻¹). Measurements from all plants within an experimental unit were made by treatment group, averaged, for statistical analysis, with results presented in grams per plant (g plant⁻¹)20.

Diameter of seed inflorescence

The assessment of inflorescence development of C. cyminum L. was carried out by measuring the diameters of seed-bearing umbels at the time of full bloom and at physiological maturity. Five randomly selected and well-developed primary umbels were tagged from each of the experimental units prior to measurement, with three replicates taken of the maximum horizontal measurement of each umbel (in mm) using a digital caliper (accuracy ± 0.01 mm)21.

Seed inflorescence number/plant

Each plant had its total number of inflorescences counted after completion of inflorescence emergence. For each plant there were three replicated counts of all inflorescences22.

Seeds yield/plant

At physiological maturity, seed yield was measured on a per plant basis (g/plant). All five plants were randomly harvested from each treatment and binned based on their corresponding treatment prior to hand threshing. Seed yield from each individual plant was weighed to produce an average from five plants per treatment and analyzed statistically using the mean number of individual means23.

Vegetative traits

Morphological traits of each plant were measured at maximum vegetative growth as follows:

Height of Plant: Height from the top of the plant (apex) to the surface of the soil using a tape measure.

Diameter of Stem: Measured using a digital vernier caliper (± 0.01 mm), 1–2 cm above the top of the soil surface.

Number of Leaves/Plant: Total number of fully developed (expanded) leaves counted manually.

For each treatment, mean values were determined by randomly selecting five representative plants from each replicate and determining the mean.

Chemical traits

Total phenol

Total phenolic contents of seeds have been assessed according to the Folin-Ciocalteu method24. To extract phenolics, 0.25 g of dried seeds were ground and then placed in a sealed vial with 5 ml of 80% methanol for 48 h. For analysis, 0.5 ml of the extract was added to 0.5 ml of 10% Folin-Ciocalteu reagent and 8 ml of distilled water in a suitable beaker. After 5 min, 1 ml of 20% sodium carbonate was added and the mixture was allowed to incubate in the dark for 30 min at room temperature before measuring absorbance at 760 nm using a spectrophotometer. The calibration curve for determining total phenolic content was prepared using gallic acid. Total phenolic content was expressed as mg of gallic acid equivalents per g dry weight of seeds (mg/g); this was used for reporting total phenolics in seeds.

Total flavonoids

Flavonoid content of seed was quantitatively measured using the aluminum chloride method according to25. 500 µL each of extract from methanol-based seed extracts, 80% methanol, and 5% sodium nitrite (NaNO2), in addition to 4000 µL of distilled water were all mixed together. Five minutes after adding the three hundred micrograms (µg) of 10% (10%) aluminum chloride (AlCl3) to the above solution, one molar (1 mol) of NaOH was also added, and then distilled water was used to bring the total solution to 10 ml. The absorbance was measured at 510 nm after incubation until orange/yellow color was achieved. Quercetin was used as the calibrating standard and the amount of amino acids in the seed was calculated as mg/g dry weight of quercetin equivalent.

Antioxidant activity

The protocol developed by Singleton and Rossi for antioxidant testing of methanol leaf extracts using DPPH scavenging experiments was followed. 200 µL of the methanol seeds extract were diluted into 3000 µL of 0.1 mM DPPH-ethanol solution and allowed to react in the dark at room temperature for 30 min; the absorbance of DPPH at 517 nm was then recorded. Carbohydrate of seed and leaves

The estimation of carbohydrate content in seed materials was determined by chemical analysis using the method described by26. For the preparation of the sample for the assay, 0.5 g of the seed material was finely ground, suspended in 70 ml of distilled water at 70 °C for one (1) hour. After the mixture cooled, the solution was filtered through a fine mesh paper into a graduated tube containing 30 ml of distilled water. A 1-ml aliquot of the sample, diluted with water, was pipetted to a second tube along with 5 ml of concentrated sulfuric acid to hydrolyze the total carbohydrate present. To produce a color reaction, 1 ml of 5% phenol was promptly added to the sample with gentle mixing of the contents. Absorbance was measured at a wavelength of 490 nm with a spectrophotometer.

Protein

Protein content was determined using the standard Kjeldahl method. Approximately 0.5 g of dried and ground plant material was digested with concentrated sulfuric acid (H2SO4) in the presence of a catalyst mixture (potassium sulfate and copper sulfate) until a clear solution was obtained. After digestion, the samples were neutralized with sodium hydroxide (NaOH) and distilled using a Kjeldahl distillation apparatus. The released ammonia was trapped in a boric acid solution and subsequently titrated with standardized hydrochloric acid (HCl) to determine total nitrogen content. The nitrogen percentage was then converted to protein content using the conventional conversion factor of 6.25 according to the following formula:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{Protein}}\left( \% \right) = {\mathrm{Nitrogen}}\left( \% \right) \times 6.25$$\end{document}

Evaluation of mineral elements

For nutrient analysis, the seeds from the apical end were collected from five randomly selected plants per replicate during mature stage27. The seeds were rinsed with distilled water to remove debris and airborne contaminants, then dried in an oven (+/− 70 °C) until it attained a constant weight, before being ground into fine powder form.

The amount of potassium (K) is determined using wet acid digestion of 0.5 g of leaf material or seed material to extract the potassium and then use a flame photometer to measure potassium concentration in the extracted solution (expressed as percent (K) based on dry weight)27.

The concentration of sulfur (S) was determined by the barium sulfate turbidimetric method after samples had been digested, with barium sulfate forming and being measured spectrophotometrically for turbidity. Results were expressed as %S on a dry weight basis27.

Using the Kjeldahl technique to analyze nitrogen in the sample, they digested the organic nitrogen using sulfuric acid that was concentrated down until only the ammonium ion was left for quantification via distillation and titration according to recognized methods28.

In determining phosphorus (P) concentrations, the method of use for the vanadomolybdate colorimetric assay after wet digestion of ground leaf or seed sample with a mixture of sulphuric acid and hydrogen peroxide was used to measure P concentrations. The absorbance values were taken at a wavelength of 420 nm using a spectrophotometer, and the results were reported as a percentage of dry weight27.

GC-MS analysis

Sample preparation

5 mL of an 80% methanol solution were used to homogenize the 0.250 g of ground seeds by vortexing until well-mixed. The sample was incubated, protected from light, at room temperature overnight. The supernatant was collected using a micropipette, filtered, and injected into the GC-MS instrument.

GC-MS conditions

An Agilent Technologies 7890B gas chromatography & mass spectrometry (GC-MS) system was utilized to perform GC-MS analysis. The GC-MS system was comprised of an Agilent Technologies 5977 A mass spectrometer, and an Agilent Technologies 7890B gas chromatograph equipped with a gas chromatography (GC) column (HP-5MS, 5% phenyl, 95% methyl siloxane, column length = 30 m, column inside diameter = 0.25 mm). The GC column was heated to a temperature of 40 °C for 5 min, after which the temperature was increased at a rate of 8 degrees Celsius per minute until it reached a temperature of 300 degrees Celsius, and held at this temperature for an additional 20 min. Helium was used as the operating gas for the chromatography separates at 1.0 mL/min, and samples were purged at a rate of 3.0 mL/min. A pulsed split less type of injection mode was utilized, with the injection temperature equal to 290 °C, and a sample injection volume of 0.50 µL. The mass spectrometer was operated with an ion source temperature equal to 230 °C; a scan speed equal to 1562 (N2); and a mass range of 44–750 amu. The identification of compounds was accomplished by comparing their mass spectral data, retention indices, and literature values to authentic standards or to the National Institute of Standards and Technology 2014 and 2020 databases. GC–MS analysis was conducted as a semi-quantitative assessment. The relative abundance of each compound was calculated based on peak area normalization without the use of internal standards. Therefore, the reported percentages represent relative, not absolute concentrations. Compound identification was considered tentative and was based solely on comparison with NIST library spectra and retention indices. No authentic standards were used for confirmation. Therefore, the detected compounds and their relative abundances should be interpreted cautiously, and further confirmation using authentic reference standards is recommended.

Sstatistical analysis

Prior to conducting ANOVA, the assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variance were evaluated using the Shapiro–Wilk and Levene’s tests, respectively. Data are presented as mean ± standard error (SE). Mean comparisons were performed using the least significant difference (LSD) test at the p ≤ 0.05 significance level. Analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to determine the effects of the treatments and to compare treatment means at p ≤ 0.05. All statistical analyses were conducted based on three replicates. For statistical analysis, the mean value of the five plants within each replicate was considered as a single experimental unit in order to avoid pseudo replication. All statistical analyses and graphs were generated using GraphPad Prism software (version 8; GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA) (https://www.graphpad.com/scientific-software/prism/). Principal component analysis (PCA) was conducted using XLSTAT version 2020 (Addinsoft, Paris, France) (www.xlstat.com). Hierarchical cluster analysis and Pearson correlation analysis were performed using R software (www.r-project.org) and SRplot (https://www.bioinformatics.com.cn/en), respectively.

Results and discussion

Effects of ALE and PSE on seed inflorescence

The results showed that ALE 4 ml L⁻¹ + PSE 2 g L⁻¹ significantly increased the seed inflorescence weight and inflorescence diameter to 4.39 ± 0.109 g and 14.26 ± 0.54 mm, respectively, compared with the control (p ≤ 0.05; Fig. 1a, b). The treatments PSE 4 g L⁻¹ and ALE 4 ml L⁻¹ + PSE 2 g L⁻¹ significantly increased the number of seed inflorescences per plant and seed yield per plant to 13.0 ± 1.24 per plant and 16.72 ± 0.53 yield/plant, respectively, compared with the control (p ≤ 0.05; Fig. 1c, d). Natural biostimulants usually result in improved nutrient uptake; improved metabolic efficiency; and improved physiological conditions in plants29,30. Previous findings demonstrated that using ALE can help to increase the number of flowers produced on a plant, as well as the amount of grain produced from each flower, under both normal and drought stress situations31. The use of foliar applications/elements, especially ALE, has improved growth rates and production of various growth and yield-related plant traits that are also related to inflorescence/flower development32. Additionally, organic matter spray applications have resulted in increased total number of flowers produced per plant, improved flower fruit set rates and fruit production off each plant and increased average fruit weight, along with a further increase in all parameters/average yield produced from all plants as a result of the chelated/humic acid treatment33. Similar results (increased number of hermaphrodite flowers produced per inflorescence) were obtained from the combination of foliar applications/chelation applications of dried yeast extract and iron in olea species of plant with increased concentration34.

ALE and PSE enhances plant growth

Plant height, stem diameter, and number of leaves showed significant increases under ALE at 2 ml L⁻¹, reaching 110 ± 4.96 cm, 8.63 ± 0.145 mm, and 15 ± 0.60 leaves per plant, respectively, compared to the control (p ≤ 0.05). These correspond to increases of 27.74%, 25.98%, and 37.86% relative to the untreated plants (Fig. 2a–c). Extracts of A. vera have many important nutrients, amino acids and growth-related compounds. Extracts of A. vera are thought to enhance plant metabolism, as they are frequently linked to increased chlorophyll levels and better overall photosynthesis performance35. In milk thistle (Silybum marianum), all concentrations tested for ALE increased growth and yield36. The most effective concentration for growth and production parameters was 60 mL L⁻¹. A different study found that foliar application of Aloe saponaria-derived ALE at 0.5% and 1% concentration resulted in significant increases in height, diameter of stem and number of leaves of the plant under normal soil conditions37.

ALE and PSE influence on plant bioactive compounds

Total phenol content was significantly increased by 10.40%, 8.50%, and 8.76% with PSE 2 g L⁻¹, PSE 4 g L⁻¹, and ALE 2 ml L⁻¹, respectively, compared to the control (Fig. 3a). The results showed that ALE 2 ml L⁻¹ significantly increased total flavonoid content by 115.59% compared to the control (Fig. 3b). Antioxidant activity increased by 1.99%, 2%, 3.50%, 3.39%, 3.14%, 3.35%, and 2.28% with PSE 2 g L⁻¹, PSE 4 g L⁻¹, ALE 2 ml L⁻¹, ALE 4 ml L⁻¹, ALE 2 ml L⁻¹ + PSE 4 g L⁻¹, ALE 4 ml L⁻¹ + PSE 2 g L⁻¹, and ALE 4 ml L⁻¹ + PSE 4 g L⁻¹, respectively, without significant differences compared to the control (p ≤ 0.05) (Fig. 3c). Although some treatments showed slight numerical increases in antioxidant activity, the magnitude of these changes was relatively small and should therefore be interpreted cautiously in terms of biological relevance. Reports of earlier studies suggest that increased concentrations of phenolic and flavonoid compounds present in various species of plant have been related to the induction or stimulation of phenylpropanoid metabolic pathway and to alterations in enzymatic activity of enzymes including phenylalanine-lyase (PAL), Previous studies have suggested that increases in phenolic and flavonoid compounds may be associated with changes in secondary metabolite metabolism and antioxidant-related enzymatic activities; however, such mechanisms were not directly investigated in the present study, thus leading to overall increase in antioxidant activity of the compound including DPPH scavenging activity38. Quantitative studies have demonstrated that ALE contains high levels of phenolics and flavonoids, with phenolic levels of 16.97 mg tannic acid equivalent g⁻¹ to 48.32 mg GAE g⁻¹ and flavonoid levels of 14.68 mg QE g⁻¹ to 57.08 mg QE g⁻¹. Furthermore, the DPPH radical scavenging ability of ALE has been shown to be as high as 88.23%39,40. In a similar study, researchers found that spraying amino acids and yeast extract on the leaves of hot peppers increased DPPH, lipid peroxidation, and phenolic compounds, suggesting an increase in both antioxidant status and production41. Lettuce plants have been found through their co-application with Arbuscular Mycorrhizal Fungi (AMF) and Seaweed Extracts (SWE) at 3 gr per liter have significantly increased the amounts of total antioxidant activity, as well a higher concentration of total phenolics and flavonoids in comparison to the control group of plants42. Further support of phenolics and flavonoids as antioxidants can be shown by ethanol extracts of leaf tissue from Sesbania sesban having high concentrations of these compounds and a significant correlation to an inhibitory effect against DPPH radicals43.

Impact of ALE on plant carbohydrate content

Seed and leaf carbohydrate contents under ALE 2 ml L⁻¹ reached 4.64 ± 0.25 mg/ g and 3.13 ± 0.96 mg/ g, respectively, showing significant increases compared with the control (p ≤ 0.05). These values represent 30.27% and 102.55% higher carbohydrate levels than untreated plants (Fig. 3d, e). Biostimulants have been reported to enhance photosynthetic efficiency and carbon metabolism, which can result in higher carbohydrate availability for growth and metabolic processes44,45. According to46. a foliar application of seaweed extract at 2.0 mL L⁻¹ and micronutrients at 0.5 g L⁻¹ has increased both carbohydrate levels within the leaves and bulbs of plants. In addition, both boosting with yeast extract foliar application and higher concentration levels has increased chlorophyll content47,48. found that applying yeast extract as a foliar treatment has enhanced plant growth through greater sugar concentration than plants will receive without treatment during salt stress.

Combined ALE and PSE treatments boost protein levels

The combined treatments of ALE 2 ml L⁻¹ + PSE 2 g L⁻¹ and ALE 4 ml L⁻¹ + PSE 2 g L⁻¹ increased protein content by 18.54% and 16.12%, respectively, compared to the control (p ≤ 0.05) (Fig. 3f). Biostimulants are known to improve nutrient uptake and assimilation efficiency, which can lead to increased protein synthesis through the stimulation of amino acid biosynthetic pathways49–51. Foliar applications using Spirulina platensis extract show to improve the growth and chlorophyll content and increase soluble proteins in beans stressed by salinity52. Consequently, 3% of the foliar application of panchagavya led to a significantly higher amount of protein content, yield and various growth parameters of groundnut vegetation compared to the other organic applications and controls53. The use of aqueous extracts from seeds as a foliar spray for chick-peas can increase protein content of the resulting seeds at various concentrations54. This has also been observed for use of algae/yeast-based extracts in snap beans, which have shown to increase total protein content of the green pods, as well as improved growth and yield characteristics55. Furthermore, foliar application of extracts from Elusine indica was shown to increase the amount of protein in maize that was under drought conditions, indicating that these extracts may help to alleviate some of the loss of protein associated with stress when maize has been growing under drought conditions56.

Correlation and similarity

In Fig. 4a, the heatmap analysis revealed that the strongest positive correlations for weight of seed inflorescence were observed under the treatments PSE 2 g L⁻¹, ALE 2 ml L⁻¹ + PSE 4 g L⁻¹, and ALE 4 ml L⁻¹ + PSE 2 g L⁻¹. These results indicate the significant effectiveness of these treatments in improving this trait. Furthermore, plant height showed a positive correlation with PSE 4 g L⁻¹, suggesting the beneficial role of this treatment in enhancing vegetative growth (Fig. 4a). A significant positive correlation was observed between leaf carbohydrate content and the ALE 2 ml L⁻¹ treatment. Moreover, seed yield per plant exhibited a positive correlation with the combined treatment ALE 4 ml L⁻¹ + PSE 2 g L⁻¹, indicating the beneficial effect of this treatment combination on yield improvement (Fig. 4b). The PCA results indicated a positive association of leaf number, stem diameter, total phenol content, plant height, and seed carbohydrate content with the PSE 4 g L⁻¹ treatment (Fig. 4c). In addition, seed inflorescence number per plant and DPPH radical scavenging activity were positively associated with ALE 2 ml L⁻¹ + PSE 4 g L⁻¹ as well as PSE 2 g L⁻¹ (Fig. 4c).

Effects of ALE and PSE on plant mineral nutrients

A significant decrease in potassium content (10.33%) was observed with the combined treatment ALE 2 ml L⁻¹ + PSE 2 g L⁻¹ compared to the control (Fig. 5a). The highest and lowest nitrogen content were recorded in the combined treatments ALE 2 ml L⁻¹ + PSE 2 g L⁻¹ and ALE 4 ml L⁻¹ + PSE 2 g L⁻¹, at 1.71% and 1.68%, respectively (Fig. 5b). Treatment with ALE 4 ml L⁻¹ significantly increased sulfur content by 166.66% compared to the control (Fig. 5c), while the combined treatment ALE 4 ml L⁻¹ + PSE 4 g L⁻¹ significantly increased phosphorus content by 0.239% relative to the control (p ≤ 0.05) (Fig. 5d). Plant extracts applied to the foliage have been shown to increase plant nutrient absorption during root absorption, enzymes which increase the activity of root transport and ion transport processes is possible due to changes in the ability of the roots to absorb the nutrients and due to increased enzyme activity in the roots57. A. vera extracts contain various bioactive compounds, including vitamins, minerals, and amino acids, which are considered to contribute to their effectiveness as foliar biostimulants58. According to prior research, applying algae, seaweed, and other plant materials (e.g., moringa, garlic, and turmeric) to the leaves of plants tends to boost their mineral content (such as N, P, K, Ca, Mg, Fe, Zn, Mn, and Cu) when compared with control treatments59–61. The addition of moringa leaf extract sprayed onto maize foliage at various phases resulted in an increase in the amount of nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium found in maize leaves, and an increase in biomass production. More pronounced results were obtained with foliar applications of moringa at 2, 4, and 6 weeks after maize had emerged62.

GC–MS profiling of plant metabolites under ALE and PSE treatments

The analysis of the essential oil chemical makeup by GC–MS showed substantial changes among the major constituent compounds between the various treatment conditions (p ≤ 0.05) (Table 1; Fig. 7a–i). The predominant compound found in each of the treatments was 1,3-benzodioxole, 4,5-dimethoxy-6-(2- propenyl)- with its maximum amount present in the control group, 71.79%, and gradually decreasing as a result of the ALE and PSE treatment conditions, from 59.83% to 69.98%. The primary components of carvone exhibited considerable variation for the two samples, with an increase from 11.03% in the control sample to 23.59% for PSE 4 g L− 1 and 24.83% for ALE 4 ml L− 1 indicating that monoterpenes with the highest levels of oxygen can be increased by means of either ALE or PSE treatments. The amount of D-Limonene decreased at all treatments, but was lowest with ALE 4 ml L⁻¹ + PSE 4 g L⁻¹ (2.01% compared to 6.67% found in the control). The occurrence of small amounts of different types of compounds like (+)-Dihydrocarvone, 10-Pentyl-10 H-acridin-9-one, and 1,2,4,5-Tetrazine with only specific ALE + PSE combinations could potentially indicate that the presence of alternative secondary metabolites may have been raised after treatment with these combinations however, this is only based off hypothetical identification and will need to be confirmed through the use of actual standards after they have undergone a mixed treatment (refer to Table 1).This shift may be associated with alterations in terpenoid-related metabolism; however, confirmation of the underlying biochemical mechanisms requires further molecular and metabolomic investigations63. A study conducted showed there was a benefit from using both salicylic acid and yeast extract together on the quality and composition of essential oils produced. The study showed that when treated in this manner, the amount of menthone, neomenthol, piperitone, γ-terpinene and isomenthol acetate produced increased by 42%, 60%, 39%, 59% and 34% respectively64. Likewise, foliar application of both seaweed extract and titanium oxide (TiO₂) to coriander and other types of medicinal plants have been shown to provide a significant increase in the essential oil yield and essential oil content. One of the most prominent types of essential oil is linalool, which was determined by analysis of relative abundance data to be significantly higher after the addition of seaweed-based treatments65,66. The foliar application of both algae and royal jelly extracts results in improved growth results and higher essential oil presence in the lemon verbena variety. Under ideal treatment conditions, D-limonene content of 25.9% has been achieved67.

Conclusion

This study looks at the effect of Aloe Vera extract (ALE), palm seed extract (PSE) alone and in combination on different growth parameters including yield, and biochemical composition as well as secondary metabolites content of cumin (Cuminum cyminum L.). The combination of 4 ml L[-1 of ALE and 2 g L[-1 of PSE produced the greatest benefits of the treatments assessed. The treatments had the effect of increasing flowering weight and total herbage yield, as well as increasing flavonoids and oxygen-containing monoterpenes. The increase in the quantity of minerals is evidence that mineral content has a role in promoting the development of secondary metabolites. Molecular mechanisms are yet to be defined and require further investigation. Thus, overall, the application of these treatments has the potential to enhance both the nutrition and functional quality of plants. The findings suggest that biostimulants, such as ALE and PSE, are environmentally friendly and can increase crop yield and/or phytochemical content. Nevertheless, the present study was limited to physiological and semi-quantitative biochemical analyses, and therefore the proposed mechanisms underlying the observed responses remain speculative and require further molecular validation. More research is needed to find out what rate and timing is best for applications, plus to compare these products for their effectiveness as biostimulants under real conditions with different types of crops. Ultimately, this will not only validate our research but will also help to further advance sustainable agriculture.
