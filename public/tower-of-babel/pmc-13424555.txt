An experimental and computational intelligence simulation for predicting the strength accuracy of self-compacting concrete using Linz–Donawitz Slag as industrial waste derivatives
Authors: M K Diptikanta Rout, Bibhu Prasad Mishra, Abhilash Gogineni, Regasa Yadeta Sembeta
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study investigates the mechanical performance of self-compacting concrete (SCC) incorporating Linz–Donawitz Slag (LDS) as a partial cement replacement, with an emphasis on developing an accurate predictive framework for compressive strength (CS). A total of 147 experimental data points were generated from seven SCC mix designs with varying LDS contents. To model the complex nonlinear relationships between input variables and CS test values, several machine learning (ML) models, including Random Forest (RF), Support Vector Machine (SVM), Artificial Neural Network (ANN), Convolutional Neural Network (CNN), Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM), and a hybrid CNN–LSTM model were developed to predict CS of LDS based-SCC. Among these, the hybrid CNN–LSTM model demonstrated superior predictive performance, achieving a coefficient of determination (R2) of approximately 0.931 and lower error metrics (RMSE = 0.059, MAE = 0.045) as compared to other models. To improve interpretability, Shapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) and Partial Dependence Plot (PDP) analyses were performed to identify the governing parameters and their interaction-driven effects on CS development. SHAP analysis revealed that cement content and LDS were the most influential variable for predicting the CS of SCC incorporating LDS, while water content was identified as the least significant factor. Partial Dependence Plot (PDP) analysis was employed to assess the impact of each input variable on CS. Consequently, the required amount of each input variable can be determined to meet a specific design strength. Therefore, the CNN-LSTM approach proved to be a reliable and efficient ML model, offering quick and accurate predictions for sustainable SCC performance. However, the present research is limited by the relatively small experimental dataset and the absence of external validation under field conditions. Future research should focus on incorporating larger and more diverse datasets, real-world validation, and multi-objective assessment including durability and long-term performance of LDS-incorporated SCC systems.Keywords: SCC, Industrial waste, Computational intelligence, Mechanical properties, SHAP interpretabilitySubject terms: Engineering, Materials science, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

The construction sector faces a critical need for sustainable materials that do not compromise on performance1. The widespread use of concrete presents major challenges, including resource depletion and environmental harm2. In this regard, the majority of researchers are actively investigating industrial by-products as viable alternatives for concrete production3–5. Linz-Donawitz Slag (LDS), derived from the steel industry, is gaining attention due to its potential application in sustainable construction6. LDS shows promise as an additive in self-compacting concrete (SCC), potentially boosting performance while supporting sustainability goals due to its rich content of oxides and minerals. SCC itself is a unique type of high-performance concrete known for its ability to flow and fill molds on its own, without the need for vibration7. This makes it especially useful for intricate structures, precast elements, and large infrastructure projects, offering benefits like excellent workability, reduced labor requirements, and greater durability8. The incorporation of LDS into SCC has the potential to enhance its overall performance by improving mechanical strength, refining the internal microstructure, and reducing environmental impact. Despite these advantages, accurately predicting the mechanical behavior of SCC containing LDS remains challenging due to the complex interactions among its constituent materials9. Previous studies have indicated that LDS can play a significant role in enhancing both the strength and durability of SCC through improved particle packing, pore refinement, and gradual secondary hydration reactions10,11. In particular, the partial replacement of cement and fine aggregates with LDS has been reported to improve strength characteristics while also contributing to a denser and more refined microstructure12–14. In addition to its mechanical benefits, the utilization of LDS in SCC contributes to sustainable construction practices by reducing reliance on conventional cement, thereby lowering carbon emissions associated with cement production15. The incorporation of LDS has also been reported to improve particle packing and reduce void content, leading to enhanced durability characteristics such as resistance to permeability and chemical attack16. However, the performance of SCC containing LDS is highly dependent on mix proportions and curing conditions, making its behavior difficult to predict using traditional empirical methods. Consequently, there is a growing need for advanced data-driven approaches to accurately model and predict the properties of such complex concrete systems17.

In recent years, with the continuous development of machine learning (ML) technologies, researchers have conducted extensive investigations into concrete strength prediction to achieve rapid, cost-effective, and accurate assessment of concrete performance.18,19. For instance, Inquid et al. used multiple ML algorithms with a limited dataset and found XGB achieved near-perfect prediction accuracy in terms of (R2 = 0.998)20. Similarly, Mai et al. reported that CatBoost model demonstrated superior accuracy for the estimation of CS of fibre-reinforced SCC with R2 = 0.986, RMSE = 4.78 MPa and a partial dependence plot (PDP) identified cement content, curing age, and superplasticizer as key influencers. Nguyan et al. applied ensemble learning techniques, using k-fold cross-validation, and performed sensitivity analysis, which emphasized the significance of cement and admixtures in strength prediction21. However, Amin et al. emphasized sensitivity analysis results where cement had the highest contribution (~ 16.3%), followed by superplasticizers and supplementary cementitious materials22. Moreover, Ozcan et al. reported that Support Vector Machine (SVM) achieved reliable and accurate prediction of concrete performance owing to its strong generalization capability and effectiveness in handling medium-sized datasets with complex non-linear relationships23. Based on above literature survey, previous studies on SCC incorporating industrial by-products have primarily focused on experimental characterization and the application of ML models for CS prediction. Although these studies reported satisfactory predictive performance, most of them relied on standalone ML approaches with limited capability to capture complex non-linear interactions among mix constituents and curing conditions. In addition, the majority of previous investigations lacked interpretability analysis and provided minimal discussion linking prediction behavior with underlying hydration mechanisms and microstructural development. Furthermore, limited attention has been given to LDS-based SCC systems, particularly in understanding the interaction-driven effects of LDS on strength evolution under varying mix compositions. Existing studies also rarely addressed variance behavior, overfitting assessment, and model generalization, which are critical for ensuring the reliability of predictive frameworks. To address these limitations, the present study integrates experimental investigation with a hybrid ML framework and explainable AI techniques to provide both accurate prediction of CS behavior in LDS-incorporated SCC.

The primary objective of this study is to investigate the mechanical performance of SCC incorporating LDS as a partial replacement material and to develop a robust predictive framework for CS estimation. This research aims to explore the influence of LDS on SCC properties through experimental analysis and to employ advanced ML techniques, including Random Forest (RF), Support Vector Machine (SVM), Artificial Neural Network (ANN), Convolutional Neural Network (CNN), Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM), and a hybrid CNN–LSTM model, for accurate strength prediction. Additionally, the study seeks to enhance model interpretability using explainable artificial intelligence (XAI) techniques such as SHAP, PFI, and PDP, thereby providing insights into the contribution of individual input variables and enabling data-driven optimization of sustainable SCC mixtures. The novelty of this study lies in systematically linking mix design parameters and curing conditions with strength development in LDS-incorporated concrete through an integrated experimental and data-driven framework. It provides new insights into the interaction-driven behavior and long-term performance of LDS-based systems, contributing to more reliable and optimized material design.

For the present research, OPC-53 grade of cement was utilized as a binding material for the preparation and casting of the concrete sample. Linz–Donawitz Slag

LDS, a by-product of the basic oxygen steelmaking process was obtained from Tata Steel, Jamshedpur, India. After grinding in a ball mill for 30 min, the material was sieved through a 90 μm sieve to improve its fineness and reactivity. The processed LDS was used in the concrete mixtures as both a partial cement replacement and a micro-filler. The utilization of LDS in cementitious systems is motivated by its chemical composition contains significant amounts of CaO, Fe₂O₃, SiO₂, and MgO37. Although LDS exhibits lower intrinsic reactivity compared to conventional supplementary cementitious materials, its finely ground form contributes to both physical and chemical effects38. Physically, LDS acts as a filler material, improving particle packing density and reducing voids within the matrix, which enhances the compactness of the microstructure39. This filler effect is particularly beneficial in SCC for its where improved packing capabilities, better flowability and reduced segregation40. Superplasticizer

This study utilized a Poly-carboxylic Ether (PCE) based SP along with a VMA as chemical additives to improve the concrete’s strength.

Water

As per the recommendations of IS 456:200041, potable water was used for the preparation of SCC specimens.

Mix proportions for SCC

All SCC specimens were cured under controlled and uniform environmental conditions for 7,14 and 28-days curing age to ensure consistent strength development. Among these, the mix designated as SLD00 was used as the control without the inclusion of LDS content, while the remaining mixes—SLD05, SLD10, SLD15, SLD20, SLD25, and SLD30 contained varying percentages of LDS content as a partial replacement for cement. Data preprocessing and analysis

Data preprocessing is essential for ensuring the reliability and accuracy of ML models. The dataset was organized in Microsoft Excel with standardized feature naming conventions and clearly defined input and output features. The input features comprised key parameters associated with fresh properties and the output variable was defined with the hardened properties of SCC. Prior to model development, the dataset was carefully examined for inconsistencies and outliers. Subsequently, min–max normalization technique was applied to standardize the entire variables, ensuring uniform scaling and improving model convergence. These preprocessing steps facilitated effective learning and enhanced the predictive performance of the developed models. The complete dataset, consisting of 147 experimental data points, was obtained from different SCC mix designs with multiple replicates for each mix to ensure statistical reliability. The dataset was randomly partitioned into 70% training and 30% testing subsets to assess the predictive performance and generalization ability of the developed models. Moreover, K-fold cross-validation was used to evaluate model generalization by repeatedly training and validating the model across different data subsets.

Data transformation and distribution assessment

Prior to model development, the dataset was examined to assess data quality, feature distribution, and the presence of anomalous observations. Statistical distribution analysis and graphical visualization techniques were employed to evaluate skewness, variability, and consistency among the input variables. Since ML models are sensitive to differences in feature scale and distribution, data normalization was performed to ensure balanced feature contribution during training and to improve convergence behavior.

In addition, advanced transformation approaches such as logarithmic and Box–Cox transformations were initially considered for variables exhibiting non-normal or skewed distributions in order to improve data symmetry and stabilize variance.

RF is an ensemble-based ML algorithm that constructs multiple decision trees during training and aggregates their predictions to improve accuracy and reduce overfitting49. The RF algorithm operates by generating a collection of decision trees using bootstrap sampling from the original dataset50,51. At each node of a tree, a random subset of input features is selected to determine the optimal split, thereby introducing diversity among the trees23. The final prediction is obtained by averaging the outputs of all individual trees, which enhances model stability and generalization capability. Proposed hybrid model of CNN-LSTM

The hybrid CNN-LSTM model integrates the spatial feature extraction capabilities of CNN with the sequential data processing strength of LSTM networks. This innovative approach is particularly suited for estimating the CS of SCC, where both the composition of materials and the temporal aspects of the curing process significantly influence the final properties. The use of a hybrid CNN-LSTM model is justified by its ability to capture complex, non-linear interactions among input features. The CNN extracts local feature patterns, while the LSTM models dependencies between variables when arranged sequentially. This combined approach enhances the model’s ability to learn intricate relationships in tabular data, improving predictive performance. The model operates by initially processing inputs through convolutional layers. After convolution and pooling operations, the processed features are passed to the LSTM layers. Model interpretability methods

To improve the interpretability and transparency of the developed predictive framework, four model interpretability techniques include Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP), Permutation Feature Importance (PFI), SHAP force plot (SHAP-FP), and Partial Dependence Plots (PDP) were employed to analyze the influence of input variables on the predicted CS values of SCC. These methods provide both global and local insights into model behavior, enabling a comprehensive understanding of feature contributions and their interactions with the predicted outcomes.

SHAP interpretability analysis

SHAP interpretability analysis is a game-theoretic approach used to explain the output of ML models by fairly distributing a model prediction among its input features73,74. SHAP values provide insights into how much each feature contributes to a model prediction compared to a baseline expectation75. However, a mean SHAP plot shows the average impact of each input feature on the model’s predictions across the entire dataset. The higher the mean absolute SHAP value for a feature, the more influence that feature has on the model’s output.

PFI analysis

PFI analysis was conducted to quantify the relative importance of each input variable in influencing the predictive performance of the developed models76. This technique evaluates feature significance by randomly permuting the values of a single input variable and measuring the resulting increase in prediction error. A higher increase in error indicates a greater contribution of that feature to the model’s predictive capability. This approach provides a global measure of feature sensitivity, complementing the feature attribution obtained from SHAP analysis.

SHAP-FP for instance level explanation

SHAP-FP was employed to provide a detailed local interpretation of individual predictions generated by the developed model. This visualization technique illustrates how each input feature contributes to shifting the prediction from the base value (expected output) to the final predicted value for a specific instance77. Features that push the prediction higher are represented in one direction, while those that decrease the prediction are shown in the opposite direction, with the magnitude of contribution indicated by the length of each segment.

PDP interaction analysis

PDP analysis is a technique used in ML model to visualize the marginal effect of one or more input features on the predicted outcome31. It helps interpret complex, black-box models by showing the effect of changes in a particular feature influence predictions while averaging out the effects of other variables. PDPs are particularly useful for understanding non-linear relationships and interactions between features78,79.

Statistical validation of model performance

To further validate the comparative performance of the developed ML models, a statistical significance analysis was conducted using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. This non-parametric test is well-suited for paired data and does not require the assumption of normal distribution, making it appropriate for evaluating prediction errors obtained from different models. In this study, the Wilcoxon signed-rank test was applied to the prediction errors of the proposed CNN–LSTM model and the competing models (RF, SVM, ANN, CNN, and LSTM). The test examines whether the median difference between paired observations is statistically significant. The null hypothesis assumes that there is no significant difference between the prediction performances of the models, while the alternative hypothesis suggests a significant difference. Table 9 represent the Wilcoxon Signed-rank test result of ML models. In contrast, the SVM and RF models demonstrated greater deviations from the reference, suggesting a relatively lower correlation and higher prediction errors. Discussion

The ML findings can be closely linked to the fundamental material behavior governing strength development in cementitious systems. The strong influence of the water-to-binder ratio and binder content identified through SHAP and PDP analyses aligns well with hydration theory, where lower water content reduces capillary porosity and leads to a denser and stronger microstructure. Similarly, curing age emerges as a critical parameter because strength gain is directly associated with the progression of hydration reactions and the continuous formation of C–S–H gel, which enhances matrix cohesion. The role of LDS is particularly significant in this context. The ML models indicate improved performance at later curing stages, which is consistent with the latent hydraulic and pozzolanic nature of LDS. Initially, LDS reacts slowly compared to ordinary cement; however, over time it contributes to secondary hydration reactions, consuming calcium hydroxide and generating additional C–S–H gel. This results in pore refinement, reduced permeability, and improved long-term strength. The models’ ability to capture interaction effects between LDS content, curing conditions, and water-to-binder ratio further reflects the synergistic mechanisms of particle packing, filler effects, and delayed reactivity. The non-linear relationships identified by the ML framework correspond to the complex kinetics of hydration and microstructure evolution, where small changes in mix proportions can significantly alter nucleation, growth of hydration products, and pore connectivity. Therefore, the ML predictions are not merely statistical correlations but are strongly supported by physically meaningful processes such as hydration kinetics, microstructural densification, and the time-dependent reactivity of supplementary cementitious materials.

Practical implications of the proposed model

The proposed hybrid CNN–LSTM model provides a robust data-driven framework for the prediction and optimization of CS in SCC incorporating LDS content. From an engineering design perspective, the model can be utilized as a surrogate predictive tool to estimate CS as a function of mix design parameters, thereby significantly reducing reliance on iterative laboratory trials. The integration of convolutional layers enables the model to capture complex interactions among material constituents, such as binder–aggregate relationships and admixture effects, while the LSTM component effectively incorporates the influence of curing age as a temporal parameter governing strength development. This combined capability allows the model to simulate the nonlinear and time-dependent behavior of SCC with high accuracy. From a practical implementation standpoint, the model can be integrated into computational design tools for real-time prediction, quality control, and performance monitoring. Engineers can input desired mix proportions and curing conditions to obtain immediate strength predictions, enabling rapid evaluation of alternative designs. Additionally, the model supports sustainability-driven design by quantifying the effect of LDS replacement levels on mechanical performance, thereby facilitating the selection of environmentally optimized mixtures. In addition, the proposed framework can be extended to multi-objective optimization problems, where strength, durability, cost, and environmental impact are simultaneously considered. This makes the model highly suitable for sustainable infrastructure development and smart construction practices. By considering the threshold effect, Lower water-to-binder ratios improved strength up to an optimal limit, while LDS replacement enhanced performance, with diminishing returns at higher contents. Strength gain showed a clear increase at later curing ages due to delayed LDS hydration.

Limitation of this research and future expectations

Despite the promising results, this study has certain limitations that should be acknowledged. Firstly, the dataset size is relatively limited and primarily derived from controlled experimental conditions, which may affect the robustness and generalization capability of the developed models when applied to unseen datasets. In addition, the dataset does not fully capture regional variations in raw material characteristics, environmental exposure conditions, and field construction practices, which may influence the transferability of the proposed framework to real-world applications. Secondly, while the hybrid CNN-LSTM model performs well, further improvements can be made by integrating advanced deep learning architectures. Additionally, this study primarily focuses on mechanical properties, while other critical factors, such as durability, sustainability, and long-term performance, should also be investigated. Future research should explore real-time monitoring of SCC properties using IoT and sensor-based data collection, allowing for continuous model refinement. Furthermore, the hybrid approach could be extended to multi-objective optimization techniques, enabling better mix proportioning based on multiple performance criteria. Finally, large-scale experimental validation across different geographic regions and material sources would further enhance the practical applicability of the proposed framework for sustainable concrete design and field implementation.
