Reduction of Ovarian Cysts After Endoscopic Surgery for Follicle-Stimulating Hormone-Producing Pituitary Adenoma
Authors: Reo Ishii, Nozomi Harai, Tadatsugu Hosokawa, Ippei Tahara, Masakazu Ogiwara, Kyoichiro Tsuchiya
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractA 49-year-old woman presented with irregular menstrual bleeding, elevated estradiol (E2) (665 pg/mL [2441.21 pmol/L]) (reference range [RR]: menstrual period [MP] 20-50 pg/mL; 73.42-183.55 pmol/L), unsuppressed follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) (19.3 mIU/mL [19.3 IU/L]) (RR: MP 3.5-10.0 mIU/mL; 3.5-10.0 IU/L), and cystic ovarian enlargement (right ovary, 109 mL; left ovary, 146 mL). A 7-mm pituitary microadenoma was also observed, and 6 months after referral, endoscopic transsphenoidal surgery was performed, resulting in a diagnosis of FSH-producing pituitary adenoma. Nine months postoperatively, the ovarian cysts had markedly shrunk. Although FSH-producing pituitary adenomas are rare, approximately 64% of nonfunctioning pituitary adenomas are positive for gonadotropin immunostaining. FSH-producing pituitary adenomas are often endocrinologically silent, with symptoms typically triggered by pituitary tumor enlargement. Early diagnosis can be facilitated by measuring FSH and E2 levels in cases of irregular vaginal bleeding, abnormal menstruation, ovarian enlargement, ovarian hyperstimulation syndrome, or infertility. If E2 is elevated but FSH is not suppressed, pituitary magnetic resonance imaging should be performed to identify FSH-producing pituitary adenomas. In cases of FSH-producing pituitary adenomas, including microadenomas, symptoms may improve after tumor resection, making surgery the preferred treatment option.

Introduction

Follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH)-producing pituitary adenomas are rare, but approximately 64% of nonfunctioning pituitary adenomas are identified as gonadotropin-producing tumors by immunohistologic testing [1-3]. Many FSH-producing pituitary adenomas are asymptomatic and do not clinically manifest excess FSH. Common triggers for detection include visual disturbances, visual field defects, headaches, and other pituitary hormone secretion disturbances due to compression from the enlarged pituitary tumor [4, 5]. Symptomatic FSH-producing pituitary adenomas are associated with abnormal menstruation, ovarian enlargement, ovarian hyperstimulation syndrome (OHSS), and infertility [6-10]. Pituitary tumor resection can improve ovarian enlargement and OHSS, normalize the menstrual cycle, facilitate spontaneous pregnancy, and prevent unnecessary surgery for ovarian cysts [11-14].

This report describes a case of FSH-producing pituitary adenoma diagnosed through irregular menstrual bleeding. Marked shrinkage of ovarian cysts was observed after pituitary tumor resection. This report also offers insight for early diagnosis.

Case Presentation

A 49-year-old woman with irregular menstrual bleeding visited her local gynecologist 7 months before our evaluation. Ultrasonography revealed bilateral ovarian enlargement. Estradiol (E2) was 431 pg/mL (1582.20 pmol/L) (reference ranges [RR]: follicular preceding phase 20-85 pg/mL; 73.4-311.95 pmol/L, follicular latter phase 25-350 pg/mL; 91.75-1284.5 pmol/L, ovulatory phase [OP] 50-550 pg/mL; 183.5-2018.5 pmol/L, luteal phase [LP] 45-300 pg/mL; 165.15-1101 pmol/L). FSH was 16.5 mIU/mL (16.5 IU/L) (RR: follicular phase [FP] 3.01-14.72 mIU/mL; 3.01-14.72 IU/L, OP 3.21-16.60 mIU/mL; 3.21-16.60 IU/L, LP 1.47-8.49 mIU/mL; 1.47-8.49 IU/L). Progestin replacement therapy (dydrogesterone 10 mg/day) was initiated 6 months before our evaluation, which improved the irregular vaginal bleeding. However, her E2 increased to 1300 pg/mL (4772.30 pmol/L), and her bilateral ovaries further enlarged. Progestin replacement therapy was discontinued, and the patient visited our hospital 3 months later. She had no relevant medical, medication, or family history.

Diagnostic Assessment

Her height, weight, body mass index, blood pressure, and pulse rate were 157.3 cm, 52.7 kg, 21.3, 106/69 mm Hg, and 58 beats/min, respectively. The endocrinological laboratory test results at the time of referral are shown in Table 1. Evaluation of MPs revealed E2 at 665 pg/mL (2441.21 pmol/L), progesterone at 1.17 ng/mL (3.72 nmol/L) (RR: ≤ 10 ng/mL; ≤ 31.8 nmol/L), FSH at 19.3 mIU/mL (19.3 IU/L), and luteinizing hormone (LH) at 1.3 mIU/mL (1.3 IU/L) (RR: 1.5-7.0 mIU/mL; 1.5-7.0 IU/L). Pelvic contrast-enhanced magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) revealed multifocal cystic masses in the bilateral ovaries (right ovary, 109 mL; left ovary, 146 mL) (Fig. 1A). Cytological examination of the uterovaginal area and the endometrium revealed no malignant cells. Despite the high E2 level, FSH was not suppressed, and with the presence of the ovarian cystic mass, FSH-producing pituitary adenoma was suspected. Pituitary contrast MRI showed a 7-mm microadenoma with a weak contrast effect in the anterior pituitary gland (Fig. 1B). These findings led to a clinical diagnosis of FSH-producing pituitary adenoma.

Treatment

Six months after referral, endoscopic transsphenoidal surgery (eTSS) was performed. The tumor was completely resected using extra pseudocapsule. Histopathologic examination revealed a ribbon-like growth of tumor cells with eosinophilic cytoplasm and round nuclei forming a rosette (Fig. 2A). FSH staining was positive, confirming the diagnosis of FSH-producing pituitary adenoma (Fig. 2B); prolactin staining was slightly positive; LH, adrenocorticotropic hormone, growth hormone, and thyrotropin staining were negative (Fig. 2C-2G); and Ki-67 index was 1% to 2%.

Outcome and Follow-up

FSH, LH, and E2 values are shown in Fig. 3. On the first postoperative day, FSH decreased to 4.2 mIU/mL (4.2 IU/L), while LH increased to 2.8 mIU/mL (2.8 IU/L). However, FSH and LH levels gradually increased over the next several months, whereas E2 levels tended to decrease. The patient’s menstrual cycle, which was relatively regular, became prolonged after surgery. Pituitary contrast-enhanced MRI at 5 months postoperatively showed no residual tumor or recurrence, while pelvic contrast-enhanced MRI at 9 months postoperatively showed a marked reduction in the ovarian cystic masses (see Fig. 1A).

Discussion

The key points of this case were the symptomatic detection of FSH-producing pituitary adenoma at the microadenoma stage, significant shrinkage of ovarian cystic masses after eTSS, and a rapid postoperative drop in FSH, which later returned to preoperative levels. FSH-producing pituitary adenomas are rare adenomas with fewer case reports than other pituitary adenomas. However, approximately 64% of nonfunctioning pituitary adenomas have been identified through immunohistochemistry as gonadotropin-producing tumors [1-3]. Many cases diagnosed by immunostaining are endocrinologically silent FSH-producing pituitary adenomas with no clinical or serologic evidence of excessive FSH secretion at diagnosis [4, 5]. Therefore, some asymptomatic FSH-producing pituitary adenomas may be hidden among nonfunctioning pituitary adenoma.

FSH-producing pituitary adenoma may take a long time to diagnose because of its asymptomatic endocrine features and lack of recognition owing to the paucity of case reports. Current diagnoses are often triggered by visual disturbances, visual field defects, headaches, and other pituitary hormone secretion disorders due to compression from an enlarged pituitary tumor. It is rare for a microadenoma to be detected at the time of development, as in the present case [6-10, 14-16]. In some premenopausal women, excess FSH can influence the ovaries and cause abnormal menstruation, bilateral cystic masses, infertility, and OHSS [11-14]. OHSS is usually a side effect of fertility treatment associated with abdominal symptoms, such as bloating and abdominal pain, infertility, and menstrual irregularities, due to increased ovarian cysts and follicular fluid retention [17, 18]. In severe cases, it can lead to thromboembolism, multiple organ failure, and even death [19-21]. FSH production by FSH-producing pituitary adenomas is known to correlate poorly with tumor size [22]. Even a microadenoma, such as that in the present case, can be positive for FSH staining, elevated blood FSH levels, and excessive FSH stimulation, leading to serious consequences for the ovary. Fortunately, this case was diagnosed relatively early and was triggered by irregular vaginal bleeding without OHSS or pituitary tumor pressure symptoms.

Recent studies have highlighted the importance of early detection and intervention in managing FSH-producing pituitary adenomas. A 2023 study by Tsukaguchi et al [19] emphasized that advanced imaging techniques, including high-resolution MRI, are crucial for the early identification of pituitary microadenomas. Their findings suggest that regular monitoring protocols for women presenting with unexplained OHSS or irregular bleeding could lead to earlier detection and improved outcomes.

Furthermore, a recent review by Ali and Mathur [13] explored new therapeutic strategies for managing OHSS, which can occur as a complication of FSH-producing pituitary adenomas. This review discussed the potential benefits of using gonadotropin-releasing hormone antagonists to mitigate the effects of excessive FSH secretion, thereby reducing the risk of OHSS in patients with FSH-producing pituitary adenomas.

The role of genetic factors in the development of FSH-producing pituitary adenomas has recently become a topic of research. Kolnes et al [22] investigated the genetic and epigenetic changes associated with nonfunctioning pituitary adenomas and found significant correlations with FSH levels and E-cadherin expression. This study suggests that genetic screening could become an integral part of diagnosing and managing FSH- producing pituitary adenomas in the future.

The removal of pituitary adenoma for FSH-producing pituitary adenoma positively affects the ovaries, significantly reducing bilateral ovarian cystic masses. There have been reports of ovarian mass reduction after eTSS [11-13], menstrual cycle normalization, and spontaneous pregnancy [14]. We emphasize that eTSS, even microadenomas, is the preferred treatment for ovarian cysts due to its effectiveness in improving symptoms and preventing unnecessary surgeries.

The postoperative FSH transition was unique, decreasing relatively quickly after eTSS but increasing again over the following months. In contrast, preoperative suppression of LH secretion was reversed. The patient’s menstrual cycle, which had been relatively regular, was disturbed after surgery, and the pre-MP was prolonged. Considering the patient's age, we considered her reaction normal for a woman approaching menopause. The average age at diagnosis of FSH-producing pituitary adenomas is 28 ± 12 years, and there are few reports on preoperative and postoperative outcomes in menopausal women [6]. This case suggests that FSH may increase again due to ovarian dysfunction in women of menopausal age, even if the FSH excess is resolved postoperatively. It would be useful to discuss the possibility of menopause or menopausal symptoms due to a marked postoperative decrease in E2 levels in the patient before surgery and consider countermeasures. If FSH increases postoperatively, residual tumors or recurrence should be considered. However, a loading test could not be performed because of patient availability, which was a limitation.

Early diagnosis of FSH-producing pituitary adenomas is critical. FSH and E2 should be measured in cases of irregular vaginal bleeding, bilateral ovarian enlargement or mass, abnormal menstruation, infertility, and OHSS. If FSH is not suppressed while E2 is elevated, it is essential to perform pituitary MRI scans and actively search for FSH-producing pituitary adenomas. In addition, patients diagnosed with nonfunctioning pituitary adenomas with positive FSH immunostaining should be closely monitored.

In conclusion, we report a case of FSH-producing pituitary adenoma where eTSS significantly reduced the size of the ovarian cystic masses. Early diagnosis and accurate treatment of FSH-producing pituitary adenoma are crucial because of the risk of OHSS and pituitary tumor compression symptoms during the course of the disease. Clinicians must deepen their understanding of this disease by analyzing case reports and accumulating data. They should integrate all findings to make comprehensive judgments, considering the interorgan relationships typical of hormones, even when the disease is found in seemingly unrelated distant organs such as the pituitary gland and ovaries.
