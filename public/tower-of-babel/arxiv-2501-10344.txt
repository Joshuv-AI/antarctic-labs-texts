FC-Datalog as a Framework for Efficient String Querying Thanks: This work was supported by EPSRC grant EP/T033762/1.
Owen M. Bell ORCID:0009-0007-8262-2813, Joel D. Day ORCID:0000-0002-3660-7766, Dominik D. Freydenberger ORCID:0000-0001-5088-0067

Abstract
. Core spanners are a class of document spanners that capture the core functionality of IBM’s AQL. 𝖥𝖢 \mathsf{FC} is a logic on strings built around word equations that when extended with constraints for regular languages can be seen as a logic for core spanners. \textcolor blackThe recently introduced 𝖥𝖢 \mathsf{FC} - 𝖣𝖺𝗍𝖺𝗅𝗈𝗀 \mathsf{Datalog} extends 𝖥𝖢 \mathsf{FC} with recursion, which allows us to define recursive relations for core spanners. Additionally, as 𝖥𝖢 \mathsf{FC} - 𝖣𝖺𝗍𝖺𝗅𝗈𝗀 \mathsf{Datalog} captures 𝖯 \mathsf{P} , it is also a tractable version of 𝖣𝖺𝗍𝖺𝗅𝗈𝗀 \mathsf{Datalog} on strings. This presents an opportunity for optimization. We \textcolor blackpropose a series of 𝖥𝖢 \mathsf{FC} - 𝖣𝖺𝗍𝖺𝗅𝗈𝗀 \mathsf{Datalog} fragments with desirable properties in terms of complexity of model checking, expressive power, and efficiency of checking membership in the fragment. This leads to a \textcolor blackrange of fragments that all capture 𝖫𝖮𝖦𝖲𝖯𝖠𝖢𝖤 \mathsf{LOGSPACE} , which we further restrict to obtain linear combined complexity. This gives us a framework to tailor fragments for particular applications. To showcase this, we simulate deterministic regex in a tailored fragment of 𝖥𝖢 \mathsf{FC} - 𝖣𝖺𝗍𝖺𝗅𝗈𝗀 \mathsf{Datalog} .

1.
Introduction

As a vast amount of valuable information is stored in unstructured textual data, the operation of extracting structured information from such data is crucial. This operation is the classical task known as
Information Extraction
(IE), and has applications from healthcare (see e.g.
[
47
]
) to social media analytics (see e.g.
[
24
]
). The rule-based approach to IE can be understood as querying a text in the same way as one queries a relational database. Here we consider a recursive model for rule-based IE called
\mathsf{FC}
-
\mathsf{Datalog}
, which is based on the logic on strings
\mathsf{FC}
and the query language
\mathsf{Datalog}
.

In
\mathsf{FC}
and
\mathsf{FC}
-
\mathsf{Datalog}
, we reason over factors (contiguous or non-breaking subwords) of a string.
\mathsf{FC}
has tight connections to the well-studied rule-based IE framework of document spanners, which reasons over intervals of positions in a string. By reasoning directly over factors, we can simplify the way we express queries, whilst maintaining the ability to express precise intervals if this is required. We now give an introduction to the two components of our model,
\mathsf{FC}
and
\mathsf{Datalog}
, as well as a detailed explanation of the connection to document spanners, before giving an introduction to
\mathsf{FC}
-
\mathsf{Datalog}
.

FC

The
theory of concatenation
(short:
\mathsf{C}
) is a logic on strings which has the infinite universe
\Upsigma^{\ast}
. Introduced in
[
17
]
,
\mathsf{FC}
is a finite model version of
\mathsf{C}
which has a finite universe of a single word and all of its factors (contiguous or non-breaking subwords). As a result of this restriction,
\mathsf{FC}
has decidable
model checking
and
evaluation
(see Section 4 of
[
17
]
).
\mathsf{FC}
is built on
word equations
, that is equations of the form
xx\doteq yyy
, where variables
x
and
y
represent words over a finite alphabet
\Upsigma
. As a result, in
\mathsf{FC}
we can reason directly over factors rather than intervals of positions as is the case for other logics on strings such as
monadic second order logic
(
\mathsf{MSO}
) over a linear order. Furthermore, in
\mathsf{FC}
we can compare factors of unbounded length, which is not possible in
\mathsf{MSO}
(see
[
17
]
for details).

Word equations themselves are widely studied and are a natural way of expressing many typical string properties such as if the string is imprimitive or if it contains a square (see e.g.
[
30
]
), and have previously been used as a model for data management in other areas such as graph databases (see
[
6
]
). Whilst here we are concerned with information extraction,
\mathsf{FC}
also has applications outside this domain; in particular,
\mathsf{FC}
and its various extensions, such as those defined later in the this article, represent a unifying framework for combining parsers in the context of Language-Theoretic Security (LangSec) (see
[
8
]
for details).

Document Spanners and FC

Introduced in
[
14
]
,
document spanners
(or
spanners
) are a rule-based framework for Information Extraction. Spanners were introduced to capture the core functionality of AQL, a query language used for IBM’s SystemT. Informally, spanners are functions that take a text document as input and output a relation over intervals (called
spans
) from the document.
\textcolor
blackIntuitively, primitive extractors (which are commonly regular expressions with capture variables called
regex formulas
) extract relations of spans, and these are then combined using relational algebra.

Many works on spanners, particularly in the area of enumeration (see e.g.
[
3
,
15
,
41
]
), have been concerned with the subclass of
regular spanners
, which are regex formulas extended with projection (
\pi
), union (
\cup
) and natural join (
\Bowtie
). However,
[
14
]
showed that this subclass cannot express more than the recognizable relations. The full class of spanners introduced in
[
14
]
are called the
core spanners
as these achieve the original motivation of capturing the core functionality of AQL. Core spanners extend the regular spanners with string equality (denoted
\zeta^{=}
), an operation necessary to perform fundamental tasks such as reading multiple occurrences of a string. This added expressivity comes at the cost of reduced efficiency (see e.g.
[
20
,
16
]
). Further extending the core spanners with set difference gives the class of
generalized core spanners
.

The logic
\mathsf{FC}
has a tight connection to core spanners. Although “pure”
\mathsf{FC}
cannot define all the regular languages (see
[
46
]
), we can define
\mathsf{FC[REG]}
, the extension of
\mathsf{FC}
with constraints that decide membership of regular languages.
\mathsf{FC[REG]}
captures the expressive power of generalized core spanners, and its existential-positive fragment
\mathsf{EP}
-
\mathsf{FC[REG]}
captures the expressive power of core spanners. Furthermore, there are polynomial time conversions between
\mathsf{FC[REG]}
and generalized core spanners, and
\mathsf{EP}
-
\mathsf{FC[REG]}
and core spanners (see Section 5.2 of
[
17
]
). Whilst spanners reason over intervals of positions,
\mathsf{FC}
reasons directly over factors. When dealing with factors, unlike with intervals of positions, the default is not to distinguish between duplicates. However, simulating different intervals containing the same factor is easily done: we can simply store in addition to the factor, the prefix preceding it. On the other hand, eliminating duplicates when working with intervals, such as in spanners, is not so easily achieved. Due to the connection between the two models, we can use
\mathsf{FC}
to gain insights into spanners; previous examples of work on
\mathsf{FC}
that has produced results for spanners are
[
19
]
for tractability
\textcolor
blackand
[
44
]
for inexpressibility.

Datalog.

A query language for relational databases,
\mathsf{Datalog}
was introduced to perform operations that were not possible in earlier database languages such as graph transitive closure (see e.g.
[
1
]
). A
\mathsf{Datalog}
program has a database of prepopulated
extensional
relations and a set of recursive rules that define new
intensional
relations.
\textcolor
blackSemi-positive
\mathsf{Datalog}
, which allows negation for atoms with extensional relation symbols, captures
\mathsf{P}
on ordered structures (see e.g.
[
34
]
).
\textcolor
blackLinear
\mathsf{Datalog}
permits at most one atom with an intensional relation symbol in the body of every rule, and semi-positive linear
\mathsf{FC}
-
\mathsf{Datalog}
captures
\mathsf{NLOGSPACE}
on ordered structures (see e.g.
[
22
]
). A more general definition of linear
\mathsf{Datalog}
permits, in the body of every rule, at most one atom with an intensional relation symbol mutually recursive with the head relation symbol (see e.g
[
1
]
). We can evaluate body atoms that have other intensional relation symbols as subroutines, and so this extended definition does not affect the complexity. Linear
\mathsf{Datalog}
has been investigated in the context of capturing linear recursion in SQL (see
[
36
]
).

The combined complexity of model checking
\mathsf{Datalog}
is
\mathsf{EXP}
-
\textcolor
blackcomplete, even if the input database is empty, the universe is made up of only two elements, and the program has only a single rule (see e.g.
[
12
,
22
]
). For linear
\mathsf{Datalog}
it is
\mathsf{PSPACE}
-complete, again even with an empty input database, a two-element universe, and a single rule (see
[
22
]
).

An earlier approach to adapting
\mathsf{Datalog}
for querying strings is Sequence
\mathsf{Datalog}
(see
[
9
]
), but this has an undecidable model checking problem. Furthermore, in the spanner setting
[
39
]
introduced
\mathsf{RGXlog}
,
\mathsf{Datalog}
over regex formulas.
\mathsf{RGXlog}
was motivated by the SystemT developers’ interest in recursion (for example, to implement context free grammars for natural language processing), and captures the complexity class
\mathsf{P}
.
\textcolor
blackAs introduced in
[
38
]
,
\mathsf{Spannerlog}\langle\mathsf{RGX}\rangle
generalizes the spanner and relational model and has recently been implemented in
[
35
]
.
\mathsf{Spannerlog}\langle\mathsf{RGX}\rangle
with stratified negation and restricted to string extensional relations also captures
\mathsf{P}
(see Section 6 of
[
39
]
).

FC-Datalog

Together with
\mathsf{FC}
,
[
17
]
also introduced the
\mathsf{FC}
-analog of
\mathsf{Datalog}
called
\mathsf{FC}
-
\mathsf{Datalog}
, which extends
\textcolor
blackexistential-positive
\mathsf{FC}
(
\mathsf{EP}
-
\mathsf{FC}
) with recursion analogously to how
\mathsf{Datalog}
extends existential-positive
\mathsf{FO}
. It is worth pointing out that
\mathsf{EP}
-
\mathsf{FC}
is able to express the inequality of two strings (see e.g. Example 5.3 in
[
20
]
). As in
\mathsf{FC}
, we have the finite universe of a single word and all of its factors.
\mathsf{FC}
-
\mathsf{Datalog}
has word equations in place of the extensional relations of classical
\mathsf{Datalog}
. That is,
\mathsf{FC}
-
\mathsf{Datalog}
atoms are word equations or relations. In
\mathsf{FC}
-
\mathsf{Datalog}
we adopt the
fixed point

\mathsf{Datalog}
semantics.

{exa}

The rules of an
\mathsf{FC}
-
\mathsf{Datalog}
program
P
:

\displaystyle\text{Ans}()

\displaystyle\shortleftarrow\mathfrak{u}\doteq yz,\hskip 5.0ptE(y,z);

\displaystyle E(x,y)

\displaystyle\shortleftarrow x\doteq\upvarepsilon,\hskip 5.0pty\doteq\upvarepsilon;

\displaystyle E(x,y)

\displaystyle\shortleftarrow x\doteq\mathsf{a}u,\hskip 5.0pty\doteq\mathsf{b}v,\hskip 5.0ptE(u,v).

The program
P
defines the language
\mathcal{L}(P)\coloneqq\{\mathsf{a}^{n}\mathsf{b}^{n}\mid n\in\mathbb{N}\}
. Informally,
\mathfrak{u}
represents our input word,
Ans
is our output relation, and a word equation holds if both sides are mapped to the same string. See Definition
2
for the full semantics.

By Theorem 4.11 of
[
17
]
,
\mathsf{FC}
-
\mathsf{Datalog}
captures the complexity class
\mathsf{P}
. When considering efficiency, we are primarily interested in model checking, which relates practically to deciding if a tuple is in a relation.
\textcolor
blackWe can see
\mathsf{FC}
-
\mathsf{Datalog}
as a language for expressing relations that can be used in spanner selections. Because spanners reason over intervals of positions, expressing relations can become cumbersome as a relation holds such intervals, including all those that represent the same factor. In contrast, we can neatly express relations in
\mathsf{FC}
-
\mathsf{Datalog}
as we reason directly over factors and so a relation holds the factors themselves.

{exa}

In
\mathsf{FC}
-
\mathsf{Datalog}
, we would express a relation that contains all factors that are squares with
R(x)\shortleftarrow x\doteq yy
. In core spanners, we would express a relation that contains the positions of all factors that are squares with
\pi_{x}(\zeta^{=}_{y_{1},y_{2}}(\Upsigma^{\ast}x\{y_{1}\{\Upsigma^{\ast}\}y_{2}\{\Upsigma^{\ast}\}\}\Upsigma^{\ast}))
. See
[
14
]
for the full definition of core spanners.

Parallel to this, where model checking Sequence
\mathsf{Datalog}
is undecidable, the same problem for
\mathsf{FC}
-
\mathsf{Datalog}
is
\textcolor
blackin
\mathsf{P}
. Hence we can also see
\mathsf{FC}
-
\mathsf{Datalog}
as a tractable recursive query language for strings, independent of the connection to spanners. We aim to identify techniques that make recursion less expensive. We thus look to define restrictions that lead to more efficient fragments of
\mathsf{FC}
-
\mathsf{Datalog}
which also retain other desirable properties.

We focus on the model checking problem primarily through two different lenses:
data complexity
and
combined complexity
. In many cases for query languages it is reasonable to use data complexity as the queries are often significantly smaller than the data.
In text-based settings on the other hand, features such as regular expressions can make queries large. Consequently, combined complexity remains an important consideration.

Deterministic Regex.

As the regular languages are often not enough to express what is required in practice, almost all modern programming languages (such as e.g. PERL, Python, and Java) do not implement only classical
regular expressions
, as introduced in
[
31
]
, but
regex
, regular expressions extended with
back-references
. These are operators that match a repetition of a previously matched string, and whilst they do increase expressivity, they also lead to intractability
\textcolor
blackof membership (see
[
2
]
). Regex were combined with the notion of determinism in
[
18
]
to define
\mathsf{DRX}
, the class of
deterministic regex
, a tractable class of regex with more expressive power than deterministic regular expressions.

Other Related Models.

As well as
\mathsf{FC}
-
\mathsf{Datalog}
, there also exist other related models that capture
\mathsf{P}
. These include positive Range Concatenation Grammars (
\mathsf{PRCG}
) (see e.g.
[

), and Hereditary Elementary Formal Systems (
\mathsf{HEFS}
) (see e.g.
[

), which do not have finite model semantics. The key difference of these models to
\mathsf{FC}
-
\mathsf{Datalog}
is in

-
\mathsf{Datalog}

\textcolor

Let
\mathbb{N}\coloneqq\{0,1,2,\ldots\}
. We denote the
empty set
by
\emptyset
and the
cardinality
of a set

by
\absolutevalue{S}
. Let
S\setminus S^{\prime}
be the
set difference
of
S
and
S^{\prime}
, and let
\mathcal{P}(S)
be the
power set
of
S
. Let
A
be some alphabet. For any two

, we use
u\cdot v
, or simply
uv
, for the
concatenation
of
u
and
v
. For
u\in A^{\ast}
, let
\absolutevalue{u}
denote its length. Let
\Upsigma
be a finite alphabet that we call
terminal symbols
(or just
terminals
). We call any
w\in\Upsigma^{\ast}
a
word
(or
string
) and use
\upvarepsilon
to denote the
empty word
. A
factor
of a word

is a word
t
such that there exist
u,v\in\Upsigma^{\ast}
with
w=u\cdot t\cdot v
, and is denoted
t\sqsubseteq w
. In literature, a factor is sometimes called a contiguous or non-breaking subword.

For a
tuple

\vec{t}
, let
\textcolor
blackits
size

\absolutevalue{\vec{t}}
be the number of components in
\vec{t}
and let
x\in\vec{t}
denote that
x
is a component of
\vec{t}
. We refer to tuples where
\absolutevalue{\vec{t}}=1
as
singletons
and omit the brackets in this case. A
relation
is a set of tuples of the same size, and is represented by a
relation symbol
.

Let
\Xi
be a countably infinite set of
variables
where
\Upsigma\cap\Xi=\emptyset
. A
pattern
is a word
\alpha\in(\Upsigma\cup\Xi)^{\ast}
. Let
\text{Var}(\alpha
) be the set of variables in
\alpha
. For two alphabets
A
and
B
, a
homomorphism
is a function
g:A^{\ast}\shortrightarrow B^{\ast}
where
g(u)

\cdot

g(v)
=
g(u\cdot v)
holds for all
u,v\in A^{\ast}
. A
pattern substitution

\theta
is a homomorphism
\theta:(\Upsigma\cup\Xi)^{\ast}\shortrightarrow(\Upsigma\cup\Xi)^{\ast}
where
\theta(\mathsf{a})=\mathsf{a}
for all
\mathsf{a}\in\Upsigma
. We usually refer to a pattern substitution as simply a
substitution
. We denote the image of a pattern
\alpha
under a substitution
\theta
by
\theta(\alpha)
. If
\theta(x)=\upvarepsilon
for some variable
x
, we say
\theta
is
erasing
. For a tuple
\vec{t}=(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n})
and a substitution
\theta
, let
\theta(\vec{t})=(\theta(\alpha_{1}),\ldots,\theta(\alpha_{n}))
.

For two patterns
\alpha,\beta\in(\Upsigma\cup\Xi)^{\ast}
, an equation of the form
\alpha\doteq\beta
is called a

. Under a substitution
\theta
, the word equation
\varphi\coloneqq\alpha\doteq\beta
holds if
\theta(\alpha)=\theta(\beta)
, and we then say
\theta
is a

of
\varphi
. If there exists a solution for
\varphi
, then we say
\varphi
is

. We say a conjunction of two word equations

is satisfiable if there exists a substitution
\theta
that is a solution to both
\varphi_{1}
and
\varphi_{2}
. We say two word equations
\varphi_{1}
and
\varphi_{2}

\mathsf{Datalog}

3.
Efficient FC-Datalog

\mathsf{FC}
-
\mathsf{Datalog}
captures

Theorem 2
.

\textcolor

blackCombined complexity of
\mathsf{FC}
-
\mathsf{Datalog}
is
\mathsf{EXP}
-complete.

We show this with two intermediate results, Lemma

and Lemma

\mathsf{EXP}
upper bound, using the naive

-
\mathsf{Datalog}
evaluation algorithm is enough. It is straightforward to see that as our universe is finite, the number of tuples we can add to the relations is exponential, and that the naive evaluation algorithm consists of a loop with an exponential bound on the number of iterations and an exponential number of steps in each iteration.

Lemma 3
.

\textcolor

blackCombined complexity of
\mathsf{FC}
-
\mathsf{Datalog}
is in
\mathsf{EXP}
.

Proof 3.1
.

We begin with all relations initialized to
\emptyset
. As the universe is finite, we can run a loop that, for every relation symbol
R
and every tuple
\vec{t}
that is not in the relation
R
, attempts to add
\vec{t}
to
R
. We then terminate this loop when nothing can be added. The number of tuples
\vec{t}
is exponential in the size of the input. As every iteration of this loop adds at least one tuple, we have an exponential bound on the number of iterations of this loop. Also, as each iteration can add an exponential number of tuples, we have an exponential bound on the number of steps of each iteration. Therefore, evaluation is in
\mathsf{EXP}
.

For
\mathsf{EXP}
-hardness, we use a reduction from classical
\mathsf{Datalog}
evaluation, which is again straightforward.

Lemma 4
.

\textcolor

blackCombined complexity of
\mathsf{FC}
-
\mathsf{Datalog}
is
\mathsf{EXP}
-hard.

Proof 3.2
.

Given a
\mathsf{Datalog}

and an input database

\mathsf{FC}
-
\mathsf{Datalog}

and a word

such that

if and only if

accepts

We can use factors of

to represent elements of the active domain of

, that is all the tuples in the relations in

and all the constants in

. We can do this by encoding each element of the active domain as a unary factor of

directly with their encoding, and for every tuple

in every relation

to the relation

.

Our goal in this section is to identify fragments with lower data and combined complexity. In Section

3.1.
Linearity

\textcolor

blackThere exists a fragment of classical

on ordered structures (see e.g.
\textcolor
black
[

\textcolor
blacksemi-positive

For an
\mathsf{FC}
-
\mathsf{Datalog}

, we define a relation
R\shortleftarrow R^{\prime}
over relation symbols

if there exists a rule

where

is the head relation symbol and

\overset{+}{\shortleftarrow}

\overset{+}{\shortleftarrow}

{defi}
\textcolor

{exa}

-

\displaystyle\text{Ans}(z)

\displaystyle\shortleftarrow z\doteq xy,\hskip 5.0ptL(x),\hskip 5.0ptL(y);

Checking if a given

of a directed graph. A

(SCC) of a graph is a maximal subset of nodes in which every node is reachable from every other node.

Proposition 5
.

The linearity of an
\mathsf{FC}
-
\mathsf{Datalog}
program can be decided in linear time.

Proof 3.3
.

Let
P=(\mathfrak{u},\mathcal{R},\Phi)
be an
\mathsf{FC}
-
\mathsf{Datalog}
program. Initialize
E=\emptyset
. We parse every rule of
\Phi
and for all pairs
R_{1},R_{2}\in\mathcal{R}
where
R_{1}\shortleftarrow R_{2}
, add
(R_{1},R_{2})
to
E
. We can do this in
\mathcal{O}(\Phi)
time. We then build the graph
\mathcal{G}_{P}=(\mathcal{R},E)
and use Tarjan’s strongly connected components algorithm (see Theorem 13 of
[
43
]
), to compute the strongly connected components of
\mathcal{G}_{P}
in time
\mathcal{O}(\absolutevalue{\relationsymbols}+\absolutevalue{E})
. As in
[
43
]
, each SCC has a root node. We can thus associate each element of a SCC with its root node in a dictionary.

We then perform a pass over each rule in
\Phi
. If the number of relation atoms in the body is less than two, we proceed to the next rule. Otherwise, we look up in our dictionary the SCC roots of the head relation symbol and the relation symbol of every relation atom in the body. For every body relation symbol, we check if its SCC root node is the same as the SCC root node of the head relation symbol, and maintain a counter for the number of cases where it is. If this counter exceeds one, we reject. Otherwise, we proceed to the next rule. We can therefore achieve this in
\mathcal{O}(\Phi)
time.

Moreover, whilst unrestricted
\mathsf{FC}
-
\mathsf{Datalog}
captures
\mathsf{P}

\mathsf{FC}
-
\mathsf{Datalog}
substantially improves the data complexity.

Theorem 6
.

Linear
\mathsf{FC}
-

We show the two directions of the proof in Lemma

and Lemma

[

Lemma 7
.

\textcolor

blackData complexity of linear
\mathsf{FC}
-
\mathsf{Datalog}
is in
\mathsf{NLOGSPACE}
.

Proof 3.4
.

Let
w
be the input word. Fix a linear
\mathsf{FC}
-
\mathsf{Datalog}
program
P=(\mathfrak{u},\mathcal{R},\Phi)
. Our pointers are modelled as positions in
w
. We perform the procedure recursively by rule, as with the definition of
P
. In each step, for each rule
\rho\in\Phi
, we represent each free variable
x\in\mathsf{free}(\rho)
by two pointers, representing the start and end of an occurrence of
\theta(x)
. As
P
is fixed, we have a fixed number of free variables, and therefore a fixed number of pointers.

A rule
\rho\in\Phi
with head relation symbol
H
is nondeterministically “guessed”, as are the values of all free variables
x\in\mathsf{free}(\rho)
. In the first iteration we have
H=\text{Ans}
. Deciding if we accept can take up to three steps and at any point, if an atom doesn’t hold then we reject.

In the first step, we check all of the pattern equation atoms. For every atom
\varphi\in\rho
that is a pattern equation
x\doteq\alpha
, we need to check if
\theta(x)=\theta(\alpha)
. We do this by, inside the part of
w
that represents
\theta(x)
, trying to match all positions
\alpha_{i}\in(\Xi\cup\Upsigma)
of the pattern
\alpha
. This requires an additional constant number of pointers, such as the position
i
and the location inside
\theta(\alpha_{i})
. If all of these hold and there does not exist any relation atoms in the body of
\rho
, then we accept. Otherwise, we proceed to the next step.

In the second step, we check all of the relation atoms of the form
N(\vec{x})
where
N
is not mutually recursive with
H
. Each of these is solved as a subroutine where the set of relation symbols is
\mathcal{R}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{R}\setminus\{H\}
. If all of these hold and there does not exist an atom of the form
M(\vec{x})
where
M
is mutually recursive with the head relation symbol, then we accept. Otherwise, we proceed to the next step.

In the third step, we check the relation atom of the form
M(\vec{x})
. We nondeterministically “guess” a rule
\chi
with head relation symbol
M
, and retain only the values of the free variables of
\rho
that are passed to
\chi
. We then perform a further iteration of these three steps for
\chi
, and where
H=M
.

This process maps the steps of the proof tree, and if the word is accepted, then there exists some sequence of steps such that when the final step is reached, the program will terminate. We see that each time, we save only the free variables of the rule in question, and it is therefore possible to recurse an unbounded amount of times whilst maintaining a constant number of pointers.

Remark 8
.

The astute reader may notice that the algorithm given in the proof of Lemma
7
may enter an infinite loop. However, it is well-known (see e.g. Theorem 4.2 of
[
5
]
) that if we have only
\log n
space, then the algorithm can run for only

time until it enters a repeated configuration and hence an infinite loop. Thus, as in Remark 4.3 of
[
5
]
, we can simply use a fixed number of pointers to maintain a step count, and if the step count exceeds this bound, we reject.

We show the other direction, that we can express all of
\mathsf{NLOGSPACE}
, by demonstrating how we can simulate multi-headed two-way nondeterministic finite automata, which are equivalent to nondeterministic Turing machines with logarithmic space (see e.g.
[
25
,
33
]
). See e.g.
[
26
]
for a survey on the computational and descriptional complexity of two-way finite automata. We discuss the connection between automata and
\mathsf{FC}
-
\mathsf{Datalog}
further in Section

{defi}

(

•

•

•

•

•

is the accepting state.

If
(q,d)\in\updelta(p,\mathsf{a})
and

is in state

with head

may enter state

and move

-
\mathsf{Datalog}

As linear

{exa}

We show a top-down evaluation of the program

Based on this top-down evaluation model, we define two categories of variables.

{defi}

Let
\rho=R_{h}(x_{1},\ldots x_{m})\shortleftarrow R_{m}(y_{1},\ldots y_{n}),\varphi_{1},\ldots,\varphi_{\ell}
be a linear
\mathsf{FC}

of

, denoted

of
\rho

.

\textcolor

blackIn top-down evaluation, a rule’s top variables contain values that were passed down from a preceding rule’s evaluation, and its bottom variables contain the values that are passed down. A variable can be both a top and bottom variable.

{exa}

Let

When evaluating a linear program, nondeterminism can occur in two ways: choosing the values of the variables when processing a rule, which we call

, and choosing which rule to process, which we call

that holds the possible values for a rules top and bottom variables.

{defi}

be all pairs

\vec{t_{b}}
a

Recall that as that we are working under finite model semantics, each value is a factor of the word

{defi}

{defi}

\mathsf{Datalog}

We first take all the rules with head relation symbol

4.
A Framework for Efficient FC-Datalog

In Section

-
\mathsf{Datalog}

Finally, in Section

4.3.
FC-Datalog as Generalized Automata

Throughout Section
3
and the current section, we have defined a series of fragments of
\mathsf{FC}
-
\mathsf{Datalog}
. Furthermore, as discussed in Section
4.1
, we have in fact defined the endpoints of an infinite range of fragments that all capture
\mathsf{LOGSPACE}
; at one end we have
\mathsf{DOLLA}

\mathsf{FC}
-
\mathsf{Datalog}
, which has efficient determinism checking but severely restricted syntax, and the other we have deterministic linear
\mathsf{FC}
-
\mathsf{Datalog}
, which has a richer syntax but expensive checking of determinism. These fragments capture
\mathsf{LOGSPACE}
as they are able to simulate multi-headed two-way deterministic finite automata, and thus we can view our
\mathsf{FC}
-
\mathsf{Datalog}
fragments as generalizations of this automata model, or its nondeterministic counterpart if the fragment is not deterministic.

As
\mathsf{DOLLA}

\mathsf{FC}
-
\mathsf{Datalog}
, by design, permits just enough to simulate these automata, we can naturally see a
\mathsf{DOLLA}

\mathsf{FC}
-
\mathsf{Datalog}
program as a generalization of multi-headed two-way DFA: every relation acts as a state, every rule acts as a transition, and every string variable acts a head which can be moved, added and deleted (as the number of heads can change per state). In each transition we read one letter and move, add, or delete the heads accordingly.

The other fragments of
\mathsf{FC}
-
\mathsf{Datalog}
discussed in this paper can be seen as further generalizations. For example, Section
5
introduces
\mathsf{DOLLA+}

\mathsf{FC}
-
\mathsf{Datalog}
which additionally allows pattern equations such as
x\doteq yy
. Instead of just moving a head by one letter, this is an operation that either splits a memory in half or doubles it. We can then see a
\mathsf{DOLLA+}

\mathsf{FC}
-
\mathsf{Datalog}
program as an generalized multi-headed two-way DFA that can perform nonregular string computations in the transitions and where heads can read words rather than letters. Further fragments from our infinite range that permit other syntactic additions could then also be seen as further generalizations.

Akin to how we can see a
\mathsf{DOLLA}

\mathsf{FC}
-
\mathsf{Datalog}
program as a generalized multi-headed two-way DFA, we can see an
\mathsf{OLLA}

\mathsf{FC}
-
\mathsf{Datalog}
program as a generalized multi-headed two-way NFA. Then, as our syntax grows, the connection to traditional automata gradually loosens. We can see a linear
\mathsf{FC}
-
\mathsf{Datalog}
program as a further generalization of a multi-headed two-way NFA, where reading letters at head positions is replaced with a declarative programming language that checks if the rule applies and computes the operations. A deterministic linear
\mathsf{FC}
-
\mathsf{Datalog}
program is an analogous generalization of a multi-headed two-way DFA. The increasing gap to traditional automata is reflected in the fact that determinism is now expensive to verify.

Finally, for unrestricted
\mathsf{FC}
-
\mathsf{Datalog}
, we can understand every relation symbol in the body of a rule as a call of a subroutine, which splits the automaton into parallel copies, each of which must terminate. At this general level, the gap to traditional automata is so large that the connection is no longer natural.

\absolutevalue{\fcdatalogruleset_{R}}=1

6.
Conclusions and Future Work

The logic
\mathsf{FC}
was proposed in
[
17
]
with the original motivation of capturing classes of spanners, a well-studied tool for information extraction. That said, independent of the spanner connection,
\mathsf{FC}
is also as a general framework for logic on strings. To this end,
\mathsf{FC}
-
\mathsf{Datalog}
was proposed in
[
17
]
as an extension of the logic
\mathsf{FC}
with recursion, which captures the complexity class
\mathsf{P}
. We can thus view
\mathsf{FC}
-
\mathsf{Datalog}
as a version of
\mathsf{Datalog}
on strings with a decidable model checking problem, where the same problem for a previous approach towards
\mathsf{Datalog}
on strings is undecidable. We could also view it as a language for expressing relations that can be used in spanner selections. We first showed that combined complexity of
\mathsf{FC}
-
\mathsf{Datalog}
is
\mathsf{EXP}
-complete, presenting an opportunity for optimization of model checking. In this paper we identified fragments of
\mathsf{FC}
-
\mathsf{Datalog}
with an efficient model checking problem, for both data and combined complexity, by performing an analysis of four restrictions: linearity, determinism, one letter lookahead and strictly decreasing.

In Section
3.1
we proposed linear
\mathsf{FC}
-
\mathsf{Datalog}
, which captures
\mathsf{NLOGSPACE}
. Then in Section
3.2
we identified and eliminated nondeterminism. Thus, deterministic linear
\mathsf{FC}
-
\mathsf{Datalog}
captures
\mathsf{LOGSPACE}
. However, checking membership in this fragment is expensive.

In Section
4.1
we imposed the one letter lookahead restriction on the pattern equations in a program to obtain deterministic
\mathsf{OLLA}
(
\mathsf{DOLLA}
)
\mathsf{FC}
-
\mathsf{Datalog}
, which has both desirable properties: it captures
\mathsf{LOGSPACE}
, and membership in the fragment can be checked in polynomial time. We also showed that combined complexity for both linear and
\mathsf{DOLLA}

\mathsf{FC}
-
\mathsf{Datalog}
is
\mathsf{PSPACE}
-complete. We thus added the further restriction of strictly decreasing (
\mathsf{SD}
) in Section
4.2
, and showed that
\mathsf{SD}
-
{\mathsf{DOLLA}}

\mathsf{FC}
-
\mathsf{Datalog}
has linear combined complexity.

We hence established the endpoints of a range of
\mathsf{FC}
-
\mathsf{Datalog}
fragments that all capture
\mathsf{LOGSPACE}
, and showed how we can restrict this further to reduce combined complexity to linear time. We have therefore paved the way to construct further fragments which can be tailored for particular applications. We illustrated tailoring a fragment in Section
5
, where we constructed
\mathsf{DOLLA+}

\mathsf{FC}
-
\mathsf{Datalog}
. This allows us to straightforwardly model deterministic regex without the need for a technically involved automata model. Yet, as for
\mathsf{DOLLA}

\mathsf{FC}
-
\mathsf{Datalog}
: we capture
\mathsf{LOGSPACE}
, membership in the fragment can be checked in polynomial time, and the strictly decreasing programs have linear combined complexity. We then showed how deterministic regex can be used as atoms, and how we can restrict where they are used so as to not compromise on these desirable properties.

Where we added additional forms of pattern equations in our example tailored fragment, there are various other syntactic additions that we could consider. As
\mathsf{LOGSPACE}
is closed under complement, we could add stratified negation without affecting the combined complexity. Other convenient additions could include predicates that express properties of a string such as “is a letter”, and functions that build on these to express properties such as “is the first/last letter”. We could also add regular constraints: atoms that work in the same way as
\mathsf{DRX}
-constraints, but match a classical regular expression rather than a deterministic regex, as in
\mathsf{FC[REG]}
. Using these or other syntactic additions, we could further map the range between
\mathsf{DOLLA}
and deterministic linear
\mathsf{FC}
-
\mathsf{Datalog}
, and investigate how these additions affect checking membership in the fragment. Furthermore, we could also look to find sufficient conditions to ensure membership can be easily checked.

As demonstrated in
[
19
]
, there are structure criteria such as acyclicity for conjunctive queries in
\mathsf{FC}
(
\mathsf{FC}
-
\mathsf{CQ}
) that improve efficiency. As we mentioned in Section
2
, every rule of an
\mathsf{FC}
-
\mathsf{Datalog}
program can be seen as a conjunctive query in
\mathsf{FC}
. We could therefore add such structure criteria to
\mathsf{FC}
-
\mathsf{Datalog}
rules and examine how this improves the complexity of
\mathsf{FC}
-
\mathsf{Datalog}
fragments further. Another direction for research is to look at inexpressibility for these fragments. However, even for
\mathsf{FC}
(as opposed to
\mathsf{FC}
-
\mathsf{Datalog}
), finding inexpressibility results is challenging (see
[
44
]
). Another logical next step is to use our restrictions for
\mathsf{FC}
-
\mathsf{Datalog}
to identify natural fragments in the spanner setting with desirable properties.

Finally, we can also see
\mathsf{FC}
-
\mathsf{Datalog}
as a generalization of range concatenation grammars (
\mathsf{RCG}
s). See
[
10
]
for details of
\mathsf{RCG}
s and see
[
17
]
for details on the generalization. Thus parsing techniques for these grammars such as those in
[
29
]
could give rise to further efficient
\mathsf{FC}
-
\mathsf{Datalog}
fragments. In the other direction, further results for
\mathsf{FC}
-
\mathsf{Datalog}
could bring about further developments in the parsing of
\mathsf{RCG}
s.
