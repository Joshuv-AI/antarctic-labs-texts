Incidence and recognition of acute kidney injury in a semirural pediatric ward in Cameroon: a single-center prospective study
Authors: Hermine Danielle epse Ebana Menye Fouda, Ritha Carole Betoko Mbono, Divine Louise B Teky Kaisse, Vincent Ngamby, Lionel Elimby, Arlette Géraldine Ndjame Nguea, Léon Jules Manga Owono
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEarly detection of acute kidney injury (AKI) remains a significant hurdle. This study evaluated the incidence and recognition of AKI in a semirural pediatric ward in Cameroon.MethodsWe conducted a prospective observational study over a four-months period at the Kribi Regional Annex Hospital. Children aged 2 to 15 years who were admitted within less than 24 h before recruitment were included. All patients were followed up for at least 48 h. AKI was defined and staged according to the modified KDIGO (Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes) 2012 criteria. The knowledge and attitudes of healthcare providers were assessed via a self-administered questionnaire.ResultsOut of 300 admissions, 150 children were included (median age 7 years [IQR 3–10]). The overall incidence of AKI was 14.7% (n = 22). Among children at risk (69%, n = 104), the incidence reached 21.1%. AKI was exclusively community-acquired. It was prerenal in 91% (n = 20) and stage 1 in 59% (n = 13) of patients. The recognition rate was only 9% (2/22). Both recognized cases were stage 3 with overt clinical signs (malnutrition and edema). Healthcare provider’s knowledge was rated as poor, insufficient, or fair among all healthcare providers. Other barriers to AKI recognition included the lack of clinical protocols (36.4%), the absence of specific clinical signs (36.4%), and overreliance on serum creatinine alone (urine output monitoring was not utilized).ConclusionAKI is frequent in a semirural setting in Cameroon but seems to remain largely invisible to clinicians. Background

The 0by25 initiative, launched by the International Society of Nephrology (ISN) in 2013, aimed to eliminate all preventable deaths from acute kidney injury (AKI) by 2025, focusing particularly on low- and lower-middle-income countries (LLMICs) [1]. This initiative has increased awareness and provided key epidemiological data on AKI in both adult and pediatric populations; it has also become a vital advocacy tool by framing AKI as a human rights issue and promoting action to reduce disparities in kidney care [2–6]. Despite these efforts, AKI remains a major public health challenge, affecting more than 13 million people annually and causing 1.7 million deaths worldwide [3, 4, 7]. Among pediatric admissions, including those to neonatal and intensive care units, the incidence of AKI is estimated to range from 26 to 33.7%, confirming that AKI is an independent risk factor for prolonged hospitalization and mortality [7–10]. Furthermore, survivors of pediatric AKI face a higher incidence of chronic kidney disease (CKD), hypertension, and proteinuria [11–13], thus further increasing the long-term burden.

Identifying risk factors and ensuring early recognition are critical to mitigating the impact of the disease. Non-nephrologist healthcare providers are on the front line of identifying and managing AKI, especially in LLMICs. However, this condition is often unrecognized, even in resource-rich areas. In England, a multicenter study revealed that only 25% of pediatric AKI cases were recognized and managed [14]. Similarly, in a tertiary children’s hospital in the USA, only 34% of AKI cases were documented [15]. Another study in the USA on community-acquired AKI in children revealed that only 19% of AKI cases were identified by clinicians in the pediatric emergency department and 62% by the inpatient team; AKI was mostly correctly diagnosed in older children and severe cases [16]. A study in Romania also revealed that only 1 in 3 neonates and 1 in 4 children had their AKI recognized [17].

In sub-Saharan Africa, these challenges are amplified. In Nigeria and Ghana, studies report high frequencies of AKI (up to 31%) in children admitted to a nephrology ward with malaria, infections, and nephrotoxins as the primary etiologies [18–20]. In Cameroon, pediatric AKI is the leading cause of admission for children to nephrology units [21–23], with an estimated incidence of 9.6 to 12.3% [24, 25]. However, data regarding the actual recognition of AKI by clinicians in sub-Saharan Africa, including Cameroon, are lacking. Shortages of trained personnel, limited access to diagnostic tests, and the scarcity of kidney replacement therapy (KRT) constitute significant barriers. These issues are likely exacerbated in peripheral and semirural areas far from nephrologist expertise. We sought to evaluate the incidence and frequency of AKI recognition and identify the potential barriers hindering early detection in a semirural pediatric ward in Kribi, Cameroon.

Methods

Study design and setting

We conducted an observational study over a four-month period from January 1st to April 30th, 2025, in the pediatric ward of the Kribi Regional Annex Hospital in Cameroon. Cameroon is a lower-middle-income country with nearly 30 million inhabitants, 59% of whom live in urban areas. Kribi is a small city located more than 100 km away from major cities such as Douala and is therefore far from nephrology centers and expertise. Kribi Regional Annex Hospital is among the largest primary care facilities in the town, providing various health services and receiving cases from nearby facilities and smaller towns for better management. During the study period, the medical staff of the hospital included only general practitioners and 6th -year medical students. The pediatric ward consists of 22 beds. The laboratory services are restricted to daytime hours (8 a.m. to 3 p.m.). The biochemistry unit was equipped with a small automated analyzer for routine tests such as blood urea nitrogen, serum creatinine, transaminases, C-reactive protein, bilirubin, serum electrolytes, blood sugar, lipid profiles and uric acid.

Participants

We prospectively screened children aged 2 to 15 years who were admitted within 24 h of recruitment and who had or did not have any risk factors for AKI. We also included medical healthcare providers working in the emergency unit or pediatric ward who were actively participating in children’s admission, management, and follow-up. Assenting children with parental consent were included, as were consenting medical personnel. We excluded all children with known CKD and non-consenting personnel.

The sample size for children was calculated using the Cochran formula. Based on the 9% frequency of AKI (representing 19 cases among 211 admitted patients) reported by Fouda et al. in the pediatric ward of primary health facilities in Douala [25], the minimum required sample size was determined to be 126 patients. We employed an exhaustive sampling method to include all eligible patients. Among the 300 new pediatric admissions, 210 children were aged between 2 and 15 years; 60 were excluded (40 discharge before the second blood sample and 20 withdrawing consent), and 150 children were ultimately included (Fig. 1). Among healthcare providers, 11 were included: seven were general practitioners with a mean professional experience of 1 year (range 1–5 years), and four were 6th -year medical students.

Procedure

We first obtained ethical clearance from the University of Douala and administrative authorizations from the Kribi Regional Annex Hospital.

Pediatric population

We systematically performed risk assessment and screening for AKI in all patients admitted to the pediatric ward. For each participant, upon inclusion, we collected relevant sociodemographic and clinical data after a complete physical examination. We performed dipstick urinalysis and monitored urine output (monitoring started at admission or at inclusion). Urine was collected in a container for children who could void voluntarily, while a urinary collection device or a urinary catheter was used for unconscious patients.

AKI risk factors, including dehydration, hypotension, infection, nephrotoxic agent exposure, major surgery, previous AKI, and decompensated anemia, were assessed according to the pediatric applications of the 22nd Acute Disease Quality Initiative Conference [26].

Random venous blood samples were collected for serum creatinine assays, which were repeated after 48 h to determine the diagnosis of AKI. Serum creatinine levels were measured using the kinetic Jaffe colorimetric method. All participants were followed up until hospital discharge. For participants with AKI, we identified severity, plausible mechanism, and etiology. Creatinine assays were repeated on day 7 to evaluate renal recovery. Laboratory parameters such as hemoglobin levels, white blood cell counts, C-reactive protein levels and malaria tests were recorded if available.

AKI was defined based on serum creatinine levels and urine output according to the modified Kidney Disease Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) 2012 criteria [5, 6] and classified using KDIGO severity staging [27].

Ethical considerations

The study was conducted in accordance with the Helsinki Declaration (related file). The study was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of the University of Douala (Ethical Clearance No. 4776 CEI-Udo/03/2025/T) and received administrative authorization from the management of the Kribi Regional Annex Hospital. The parents or legal guardians of eligible patients were first informed verbally and subsequently in writing regarding the study’s objectives, procedures, potential benefits, and risks. Written informed consent was obtained from the guardian of every participating child. Additionally, verbal assents were obtained from all the children, whereas written assent was specifically required for participants older than 12 years prior to their inclusion.

Confidentiality was strictly maintained by anonymizing all the data collection forms and laboratory sample tubes. No financial contribution was required from the families for serum creatinine and urine dipstick tests. All laboratory results were promptly communicated to the parents and the attending physicians as soon as they became available to ensure appropriate clinical management.

Statistical analysis

The data were analyzed using SPSS version 26. Continuous variables are expressed as medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs) or means with standard deviations (SDs), depending on the normality of the distribution, whereas categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages. The cumulative incidence of AKI was calculated as the ratio of the number of patients diagnosed with AKI to the total number of eligible patients admitted to the pediatric ward during the study period, expressed as a percentage. Additionally, we determined the incidence within the at-risk subgroup using the total number of children at risk of AKI admitted to the pediatric ward during the study period. Comparisons between groups were performed using Student’s t test or the Mann-Whitney U test for continuous data and the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical data. A two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Incidence and characteristics of AKI

A total of 22 children developed AKI, yielding a cumulative incidence of 14.7% in the overall study population. When restricted to children at risk of AKI (n = 104), the incidence increased to 21.1%. AKI was exclusively community-acquired, predominantly prerenal (n = 20, 91%), and was mostly classified as stage 1 (n = 13, 59%) (Table 3). Oliguria was noted in 9 patients (41%), anuria in one (4.5%), and AKI was diagnosed solely with urine output criteria in 3 patients (13.7%). Infections (n = 19, 86.4%), particularly malaria (n = 13, 59%), dehydration (n = 6, 27.3%), and nephrotoxins (NSAIDs and herbal remedies; n = 5, 22.7%), were the main etiological factors. There were no indications for KRT. Both children with stage 3 AKI were malnourished with edema; one was referred, and the other was anuric and died. Renal recovery was complete for all the other patients by day 7.

Children with AKI were significantly younger (4.73 ± 2.8 vs. 7 ± 4 years, p = 0.013), had higher parental income ($250 ± 130 vs. $200 ± 100, p = 0.007), had higher WBC counts (10,310 ± 9,690 vs. 7,030 ± 5,060 cells/mm³, p = 0.007), and had longer hospital stays (3.64 ± 3.1 vs. 2.7 ± 0.98 days, p = 0.012). Recognition of AKI by healthcare providers

Among the 22 identified AKI cases, only 2 were recognized by healthcare providers, representing a recognition rate of 9%. Both recognized cases involved patients with stage 3 AKI presenting with overt clinical signs, specifically malnutrition and edema. Furthermore, these cases were identified by the same general practitioner, who possessed the most extensive professional experience (5 years) among the staff. A response to AKI after notification by the investigator was recorded in only 9 patients (41%), consisting of increased hydration (n = 8) and one referral.

The level of knowledge was rated as “insufficient” or “fair” for 91% (n = 10) of the healthcare providers; none demonstrated a “good” level of knowledge (Table 4). Most participants reported being uncomfortable managing AKI (n = 6, 54.6%). Most clinicians (n = 8, 72.7%) suspected AKI only if the creatinine level was above the laboratory normal reference range, and 63.6% (n = 7) never compared the current results with previous creatinine values. Generalized edema (45.5%, n = 5), anuria (36.4%, n = 4), and dyspnea (9.2%, n = 1) were the primary clinical signs used to suspect AKI. Serum creatinine was the sole criterion used for diagnosis; none of the staff used urine output. Self-reported barriers included the absence of protocols (36.4%, n = 4), a lack of specific clinical signs (36.4%, n = 4), and limited laboratory accessibility (only open during the daytime) (27.2%, n = 3).

Discussion

Identifying patients at risk of AKI is the cornerstone of prevention and early diagnosis, as outlined in the ISN 5R approach (risk assessment, recognition, response, renal support, and rehabilitation), especially among non-nephrologist healthcare providers [3]. Risk factors were highly prevalent in our cohort (69.3%), primarily infections (notably malaria), nephrotoxic exposure, and hypovolemia. These findings align with previous studies in Cameroon and other sub-Saharan African countries [6, 7, 24, 25]. In primary health facilities in Douala, 82% of children presented with risk factors at admission, although hypovolemia was more frequent in that urban setting [25]. Since most of these risk factors are preventable or treatable, proactive management is essential to reduce the incidence of AKI and improve clinical outcomes.

We reported an incidence rate of 14.7%, which is higher than those previously reported in Cameroon and other sub-Saharan African regions [22–25, 28–30]. For instance, studies in Nigeria and Romania reported incidences between 1.7% and 1.8% among general pediatric admissions [17, 18, 28]. Although the incidence of AKI in high-income countries often exceeds 30% in intensive care units [7], data from primary health facilities in LLMICs remain scarce. ICU-associated AKI is a complex, multifactorial syndrome usually involving sepsis, hypovolemia, major surgery, nephrotoxic exposure and multiorgan failure. In contrast, in primary health facilities, hypovolemia and sepsis typically serve as the main underlying pathophysiological drivers of AKI as shown in our study. The average WBC count in children with AKI was significantly higher, also suggesting that severe inflammatory or infectious processes were major drivers of renal impairment in our setting.

The higher incidence in our study compared to the 9% reported in Douala [25] may be attributed to the semirural setting of Kribi. According to the 2022 Cameroon Malaria Indicator Survey [31], the prevalence of malaria among children (6–59 months) is significantly higher in the South region (46%), where Kribi is located, than in Douala (28%). Furthermore, a higher frequency of waterborne diseases and the use of herbal remedies in semirural areas likely contributed to this increased AKI burden.

Community-acquired AKI is highly prevalent in LLMICs, particularly in primary care settings, as evidenced by our study. In these contexts, patients often present at admission with already elevated serum creatinine levels or during the recovery phase, as the initial injury occurred in the community prior to admission. Traditional KDIGO 2012 criteria, which focus exclusively on rising creatinine trend, frequently fail to identify these cases. Consequently, the modified KDIGO criteria (also referred as extended criteria) have been proposed to address this diagnosis gap. These criteria define AKI as an increase or a decrease in serum creatinine ≥ 0.3 mg/dl within 48 h or ≥ 1,5 times baseline within 7 days [5, 6]. This approach allows for the retrospective diagnosis of AKI, significantly improving detection accuracy and preventing under-reporting of community-acquired AKI. Recent evidence from Wainstein et al., confirms that using these modified criteria increases the detection rate, especially for community-acquired AKI and AKI occurring early in hospital admission [32, 33]. Although these cases may appear milder in severity compared to those meeting traditional criteria, they remain independently associated with adverse outcomes including higher mortality [32, 33], highlighting the clinical necessity of this modified framework.

Consistent with previous reports from non-nephrology pediatric wards [16, 17, 24, 25], AKI was predominantly prerenal and stage 1 in our study. In contrast, studies from specialized nephrology units often report severe stage 3 AKI with high indications for KRT [19–23, 28, 29]. This disparity underscores the need for early recognition by non-nephrologist staff to initiate prompt intervention and prevent progression to severe stages.

Children with AKI were younger, a finding corroborated by other authors [14, 17, 34], as these children are more susceptible to severe malaria, diarrheal diseases, and dehydration. Notably, AKI was more common in children from families with higher parental income. While higher socioeconomic status is typically a protective factor [7, 18, 30], in our context, it may reflect selection bias due to the study design. Given that two blood draws spaced by 48 h were required for the diagnosis of AKI, wealthier families who could afford extended hospital stays in a system where health insurance is largely unavailable may have been selected. This is supported by the high rate of discharge against medical advice (14.7%), which often occurs on the second day of admission because of financial constraints. This phenomenon occurred more frequently in the AKI group (40% vs. 10.1%, p = 0.001). Although these patients had achieved renal recovery at discharge, such premature departures increase the risk of readmission and long-term renal complications.

Overall outcomes were favorable, reflecting the high incidence of stage 1 and prerenal AKI. The single death in a stage 3 patient may have been driven by underlying sepsis and severe malnutrition as well as AKI itself.

The alarmingly low recognition rate (9%) in our study is lower than the rates reported in the USA (19–25%) and the UK (26%) [14, 16, 17]. Consistent with Bernardo et al., the clinicians in our study primarily recognized AKI at more severe stages (stage 3) associated with overt clinical signs such as generalized edema or anuria [16]. Moreover, a clinical response following notification by the investigator was documented in only 41% of cases, highlighting a significant gap between diagnosis and management. Overwork, financial constraints and a lack of knowledge regarding the clinical complications and prognosis of AKI could explain this. Most healthcare providers also reported being uncomfortable with the management of AKI, which may also contribute to the low rate of clinical response.

Barriers to AKI recognition included insufficient knowledge of the KDIGO criteria, the absence of standardized protocols, and limited laboratory access. Notably, none of the healthcare providers utilized urine output—a key diagnostic pillar—or compared current creatinine levels with previous values. Instead, clinicians relied heavily on absolute thresholds rather than dynamic changes, a practice that overlooks the kinetic nature of AKI. Diagnostics were largely driven by over clinical signs such as generalized oedema or anuria. Our findings mirror a study conducted in Rwanda across 10 hospitals and 193 healthcare providers, which revealed that insufficient knowledge and deficiencies in diagnostic tools as well as medical supplies constituted significant barriers to the optimal AKI management in primary healthcare settings [35]. Furthermore, the lack of standardized clinical protocols for AKI in the pediatric ward was cited by 36.4% of our providers as a major hurdle. This absence of guidance leads to a ‘reactive’ rather than ‘proactive’ management style, where AKI is only addressed once it reaches a life-threatening stage. These systemic barriers, combined with a shortage of trained personnel in remote areas, highlight a critical gap in pediatric care. Bridging this gap requires multi-faceted interventions, including improving laboratory accessibility—notably expanding services beyond daytime hours—and implementing electronic or paper-based AKI alerts. As suggested by the ISN 5R strategy, integrating risk assessment into routine pediatric admission charts is a low-cost, high-impact intervention that could be easily piloted in semirural settings like Kribi.

Limitations of the study

Despite its clinical importance, several limitations of this study should be acknowledged.

First, the single-center design and relatively small sample size may limit the generalizability of our findings to all semirural areas in Cameroon or sub-Saharan Africa. However, our exhaustive sampling method and prospective design provide a realistic snapshot of AKI in primary healthcare settings.

Another important limitation is selection bias due to the study design, as two blood samples spaced by 48 h were needed for every participant. This may have resulted in selecting wealthier families who can afford extended hospital stays while inadvertently excluding children with short hospital stays, early discharge, rapid death or those with financial barriers to prolonged hospitalization. As only newly hospitalized children were included, another selection bias could have been introduced and contributed to the high frequency of community-acquired AKI.

Second, the four-month study duration may have introduced a seasonal bias, potentially influencing the frequency of malaria-associated AKI.

Third, the assessment of medical staff knowledge was based on a small cohort of 11 healthcare providers. While this represents the total medical staff of the pediatric unit during the study period, the results should be interpreted as reflective of local challenges rather than a national trend.

Fourth, our reliance on the Jaffe colorimetric method for serum creatinine measurement, rather than the enzymatic method (which is less prone to interference), might have slightly influenced our results. Nonetheless, the Jaffe method remains the most widely used and accessible tool in resource-limited settings.

Finally, owing to the short duration of follow-up (until hospital discharge or day 7), we could not assess long-term renal outcomes or the development of CKD among survivors. Future multicenter studies with longer follow-up periods are needed to evaluate the long-term burden of pediatric AKI in rural Africa.

Conclusion

Our study revealed that AKI seems to be frequent in semirural pediatric settings in Cameroon, although it appears to remain largely invisible to frontline clinicians, with a staggering 91% of cases going unrecognized. The alarmingly low recognition rate may be driven by insufficient clinician knowledge, an overreliance on overt clinical signs (such as edema or anuria), and the lack of standardized protocols. To bridge this gap and align with the ISN 0by25 initiative, the “5R” framework (risk, recognition, response, renal support, and rehabilitation) needs to be implemented. This could involve systematic screening of at-risk children using KDIGO criteria, intensive training of non-nephrologist medical staff, and improved laboratory accessibility for serum creatinine monitoring in peripheral health facilities.
