Long‐Term Durability of Transcatheter Aortic Valve Prostheses in Patients With Bicuspid Versus Tricuspid Aortic Valve
Authors: Hong‐De Li, Wei‐Ya Li, Jun‐Li Li, Shi‐Qin Peng, Yuan Feng, Yong Peng, Jia‐Fu Wei, Zhen‐Gang Zhao, Tian‐Yuan Xiong, Yuan‐Wei Xiang Ou, Yan Wang, Qing Li, Hao‐Ran Yang, Cheng‐Xiang Song, Yi‐Jun Yao, Zhong‐Kai Zhu, Qi Liu, Xi Wang, Mao Chen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCurrently, there is a lack of evidence for the long‐term bioprosthetic valve durability of patients with bicuspid aortic valve (BAV) following transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR).Methods and ResultsThis study aimed to evaluate hemodynamic outcome, structural valve deterioration, and bioprosthetic valve failure during long‐term follow‐up after TAVR in patients with BAV versus patients with tricuspid aortic valve (TAV). Patients with BAV and TAV who underwent TAVR between 2012 and 2020, with echocardiography followed for at least 3 years, were included. Baseline characteristics, long‐term valve hemodynamic performance, structural valve deterioration, and bioprosthetic valve failure were compared between patients with BAV and TAV. A total of 170 patients with BAV and 145 patients with TAV were included. The mean duration of follow‐up for patients with BAV and TAV was 5.2±1.8 and 5.0±1.7 years. No significant differences were observed in the rates of structural valve deterioration and bioprosthetic valve failure between patients with BAV and TAV: structural valve deterioration, BAV 20 (11.8%) versus TAV 16 (11.0%) at last follow‐up (P=0.861); bioprosthetic valve failure, BAV 3 (1.8%) versus TAV 7 (4.8%) at last follow‐up (P=0.196). More than moderate intravalvular aortic regurgitation (1.8% versus 4.8%, P=0.196) and paravalvular leak (6.5% versus 3.4%, P=0.305) were rare in both patients with BAV and patients with TAV.ConclusionsThis study indicated satisfactory long‐term valve durability of TAVR in patients with BAV. Comparable hemodynamic outcome, structural valve deterioration, and bioprosthetic valve failure could be achieved in patients with BAV and TAV during long‐term follow‐up after TAVR.

In patients presenting with symptomatic aortic valve stenosis classified as high or intermediate risk, transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) has been deemed either superior or on par with surgical aortic valve replacement (SAVR). 1 Initially, TAVR was used exclusively for patients at high risk; however, recent outcomes from randomized controlled trials have established its safety and efficacy across a wider range of risk categories, extending to individuals considered to be at low risk. 2 , 3 , 4

Nevertheless, with the expanded use of TAVR in younger, lower‐risk individuals, who generally possess a longer expected life span compared with the original cohort of patients umdergoing TAVR, the long‐term durability of the bioprosthetic valve has become a matter of heightened significance. The NOTION (Nordic Aortic Valve Intervention) trial revealed that the 10‐year incidence of both structural valve deterioration (SVD) and bioprosthetic valve failure (BVF) were similar between SAVR and TAVR groups. 5

Bicuspid aortic valve (BAV) typically presented as an isolated anomaly but was often associated with congenital conditions such as aortic valve stenosis. 6 In our study, patients with BAV comprised 54% of the total population, consistent with the prevalence of BAV in China. 7 In Western societies, patients with BAV constituted a small percentage of the population, indicating variances in the prevalence of BAV across races or regions. In recent years, the proportion of patients with BAV undergoing TAVR in Western countries has increased substantially. 8 , 9 Meanwhile, given that patients with BAV had an earlier onset of aortic stenosis, as patients undergoing TAVR become younger, there may be more patients with BAV in the future. 6

Owing to characteristics such as severe calcium burden and annular eccentricity in patients with BAV, there was a concern that these might result in insufficient valve expansion and stent frame deformation, raising worries about the long‐term durability of valves in BAV patients. 10 However, due to the exclusion of patients with BAV from major landmark trials, evidence regarding the long‐term durability of TAVR in patients with BAV remains scarce. Furthermore, the earlier onset age of BAV compared with patients with tricuspid aortic valve (TAV) underscored the urgency of providing long‐term durability results for valves in patients with BAV. 10 This article presents, for the first time, an analysis of the valve durability in patients with BAV during long‐term follow‐up and examines potential differences in hemodynamic performance compared with patients with TAV.

Methods

Study Design

The data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request. The study was conducted as a single‐center investigation at West China Hospital of Sichuan University. The study included consecutive patients who underwent TAVR from 2012 to 2020, all of whom were followed up with echocardiography for a minimum of 3 years. Patients with unicuspid or quadricuspid valve or prior aortic valve replacement were excluded. The decision for TAVR was made after consultations within the local cardiac team. A dedicated, predefined database was used for the prospective collection of baseline demographics, clinical and anatomic features, procedural specifics, and in‐hospital occurrences. In the computed tomographic analyses, valve subtypes were categorized and measurements related to anatomic structures were conducted by imagers with substantial experience. The morphologic findings of the BAV were assessed on the basis of the Sievers classification criteria. 11 Follow‐up using transthoracic echocardiography was systematically carried out at discharge, and then at 1, 3, and 6 months, as well as annually thereafter. The methodologies used for TAVR were in accordance with those previously described. 12 The study period primarily saw the use of first‐generation self‐expanding valves, such as the Venus‐A (n=194; Venus MedTech, Hangzhou, China), CoreValve (n=27; Medtronic, Minneapolis, MN), TaurusOne (n=35; Peijia Medical, Suzhou, China), Vitaflow (n=14; MicroPort, Shanghai, China). The Venus‐A valve, with specific parameters detailed in Figure S1, was designed similarly to the CoreValve/Evolut valve. The size of the valve, depth of implantation, and oversizing ratio are shown in Table S1. The last follow‐up echocardiography was based on the most recent transthoracic echocardiogram conducted >3 years following TAVR. Approval for the study was granted by the local institutional ethics committee, and all participants provided written informed consent.

End Point Definition

Hemodynamic SVD and BVF were adapted from the 2017 European Association of Percutaneous Cardiovascular Interventions/European Society of Cardiology/European Association for Cardio‐Thoracic Surgery (EAPCI/ESC/EACTS) consensus statement on standardized definitions of SVD and BVF. 13 The criteria were defined as follows: Moderate SVD: (1) mean gradient ≥20 and <40 mm Hg or ≥10 and < 20 mm Hg increase from baseline; or (2) moderate new or worsening intraprosthetic aortic regurgitation (AR).Severe SVD: (1) mean gradient ≥40 mm Hg or ≥20 mm Hg increase from baseline; or (2) severe new or worsening intraprosthetic AR.BVF: (1) Severe hemodynamic SVD; or (2) aortic valve reoperation or reintervention; or (3) valve‐related death.

The modification to the 2017 EAPCI/ESC/EACTS definitions was as follows: To be classified as SVD, patients with a mean gradient ≥20 mm Hg at discharge should also meet either an increase in mean gradients ≥10 mm Hg compared with baseline or moderate new or worsening intraprosthetic AR. SVD was also classified according to the Valve Academic Research Consortium 3 criteria in the Supplemental Material. The baseline echocardiographic data were obtained from average values at discharge, 1 month, 3 months, and 6 months after TAVR.

Statistical Analysis

The normal distribution of variables was evaluated using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Mean±SD represented continuous variables, and depending on their distribution, either the Student's t‐test or the Mann–Whitney U test was applied for comparisons. The χ2 or Fisher's exact test was used for the comparison of categorical variables on the basis of their suitability. The cumulative incidence functions for SVD and BVF were estimated, accounting for the competing risk of death. Kaplan–Meier estimates (number of events) depicted time‐to‐event variables, and the log‐rank test was used for their comparison. All Kaplan–Meier curves and Nelson–Aalen cumulative risk curves represent time from 0 to 7 years after TAVR. To explore both univariate and multivariate associations with the occurrence of SVD in patients with BAV and TAV, Cox proportional hazards models were used. Hazard ratios (HRs) were reported alongside 95% CIs. Variables showing a P value of <0.20 in univariate analyses were considered as candidate covariates for multivariate analysis. All P values were 2‐sided, with a threshold of <0.05 denoting statistical significance. IBM SPSS Statistics software version 27.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY) and R version 4.1.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria), were used for all data analyses.

Results

Study Population and Baseline Characteristics

As shown in Figure 1, among patients who underwent TAVR at West China Hospital from 2012 to 2020, 333 individuals who had multiple echocardiogram data within the first 6 months after TAVR and a minimum follow‐up duration of ≥3 years were included in our analysis. Following the inclusion and exclusion criteria, 315 participants were included in the final analysis: 170 with BAV and 145 with TAV. Using the Sievers classification, the distribution of BAV subtypes was as follows: 104 patients (61.2%) were classified as type 0, 65 patients (38.2%) as type 1, and 1 patient (0.6%) as type 2. Baseline information, in‐hospital clinical outcome, and as much echocardiographic data as possible after TAVR were obtained for these patients. There were 81 men (47.6%) in the BAV group and 83 men (57.2%) in the TAV group. The average age of BAV and TAV population was 72.3±6.7 and 74.0±6.4 years, respectively. In contrast, patients with BAV had higher calcification volume, sinotubular junction, ascending aortic maximal diameter, Agatston calcium score, mean transprosthetic gradient, and maximum aortic valve velocity, while the TAV group had a higher proportion of aortic regurgitation. Procedural Characteristics and In‐Hospital Outcomes

Table 2 shows the comparison of procedural characteristics and hospitalization outcomes in the BAV and TAV groups. Compared with the TAV group, the BAV group had a higher proportion of balloon predilation (95.3% versus 82.1%, P<0.001) and balloon postdilation (56.8% versus 35.4%, P<0.001). The primary valve type used in both BAV and TAV groups was the self‐expandable valve (91.2% versus 84.8%, P=0.113). No statistical difference was found between the 2 groups in any comparison of in‐hospital outcomes, although patients with BAV appeared to have a higher incidence of postoperative new atrial fibrillation (2.9% versus 0.0%, P=0.065) and a lower risk of coronary obstruction after TAVR (0.6% versus 4.2%, P=0.051).

Structural Valve Degeneration

Overall, the incidence of SVD in the BAV group was similar to that in the TAV group during follow‐up (Table 3; 20 [11.8%] versus 16 [11.0%]; P=0.861). For the total sample, moderate SVD was mainly composed of the criteria of mean gradient >20, while severe SVD was mainly caused by severe central AR. BAV participants had a higher proportion of moderate SVD (Table 3; 18 [10.6%] versus 12 [8.3%]; P=0.565), and the TAV group had a higher proportion of severe SVD (Table 3; 2 [1.2%] versus 4 [2.8%]; P=0.419). There was no significant difference in median SVD time between the BAV and TAV groups (5.9 years versus 5.5 years, P=0.573). As shown in Figure 4, assuming death as a competing risk that could prevent SVD from occurring, the actual analysis of the cumulative incidence functions for SVD showed no significant difference between BAV and TAV patients (BAV versus TAV; 16.3% versus 15.2%, P=0.605). The incidence rate of SVD expressed in person‐years was 2.30 cases per 100 person‐years for both BAV and TAV. Patients with SVD were also classified according to the Valve Academic Research Consortium 3 criteria, as shown in Table S3 and Figure S2. The Kaplan–Meier curves for survival free from SVD are shown in Figure S3. In both the BAV and TAV groups, the vast majority of patients (BAV versus TAV, 88.2% versus 89.0%) did not have SVD.

Baseline and Predictors of SVD

There were no significant differences of baseline and procedural characteristics between patients with and without SVD in either the BAV or TAV group (Table 4 and Table S4). As shown in Table 5, variables with P value <0.2 in the univariate Cox regression of SVD in the BAV group, including sex, Society of Thoracic Surgeons Predicted Risk of Mortality, and balloon postdilation entered the multivariate Cox analysis. Finally, we found that balloon postdilation was an independent predictor of SVD in patients with BAV. In the TAV group, multivariate Cox regression analysis did not identify any independent predictors of SVD (Table S5).

As shown in Figure 5, patients with BAV and patients with TAV experienced a similar level of BVF through 7 years (P=0.794). Throughout the entire follow‐up period, the incidence of BVF was rare in both patients with BAV and patients with TAV patients (1.8% and 4.8% respectively; Table 3). The Kaplan–Meier curves for survival free from BVF are shown in Figure S4. The incidence rate of BVF expressed in person‐years was 0.34 cases per 100 person‐years for BAV and 1.01 cases per 100 person‐years for TAV. Sensitivity Analysis

Figure S5 demonstrates that in the subgroup analysis of patients with BAV, there was no significant difference in the cumulative incidence of SVD and BVF between Sievers type 0 and Sievers type 1. Additionally, Figure S6 demonstrated that including TAVR patients with <3 years of follow‐up in the analysis yielded results consistent with the primary findings of this study: there was no significant difference in the cumulative incidence of SVD between patients with BAV and patients with TAV (P=0.768).

Discussion

The principal findings of this study could be summarized as follows: (1) The incidence of SVD in BAV patients was comparable to that in TAV patients, being 11.8% and 11.0%, respectively; (2) the incidence of BVF was slightly lower in patients with BAV compared with patients with TAV, at 1.8% and 4.8%, respectively; and (3) throughout long‐term follow‐up, extending up to 10 years after TAVR, hemodynamic performance in patients with BAV was similar to patients with TAV, characterized by the absence of significant increases in mean transprosthetic gradient, maximum aortic velocity, and AR.

Valve Durability in TAVR

Both previous studies and our study showed that THVs in patients who have undergone TAVR maintain a low incidence of SVD and excellent hemodynamic performance over a period of 5 to 10 years. 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 , 20 , 21 Using the EAPCI/ESC/EACTs criteria for SVD and BVF, these studies revealed a low incidence of both conditions, indicating superior valve durability in the general population over the long term. It is noteworthy that the recently published Valve Academic Research Consortium 3 criteria slightly diverged from the EAPCI/ESC/EACTS criteria regarding SVD, particularly by emphasizing an increase in mean gradient ≥10 mm Hg to a resultant mean gradient ≥20 mm Hg, rather than simply reaching a mean gradient ≥20 mm Hg. 22 Furthermore, early post‐TAVR echocardiographic data could assist in distinguishing between nonstructural valve deterioration and early SVD; patients with a mean gradient ≥20 mm Hg at early post‐TAVR should be classified as nonstructural valve deterioration. 23 Therefore, we excluded patients with a mean gradient ≥20 mm Hg at both discharge and last follow‐up, with an increase of <10 mm Hg at last follow‐up compared with baseline. In addition, compared with studies of TAVR procedural data reported in Western societies, our study showed a higher proportion of second THV implantation and a slightly lower rate of conversion to surgery. 24 , 25 The higher rate of second THV implantation was attributed to the predominant use of nonretrievable first‐generation self‐expanding valves in this study. The lower rate of conversion to surgery could partly have been due to the limited sample size in our study, especially considering that the incidence of conversion to surgery is generally around 0.4% to 0.5%. 24 , 25 Additionally, our comprehensive pre‐TAVR discussions and preparations involving multiple teams might have contributed to reducing the occurrence of such events.

BAV Valve Durability in TAVR

However, previous studies did not conduct a distinct analysis of long‐term valve durability specifically in patients with BAV. To our knowledge, the longest available follow‐up data for the durability in patients with BAV extend only up to 4 years and the study did not compare the durability in patients with BAV to patients with TAV. 26 Consequently, uncertainties regarding the long‐term valve durability in patients with BAV persist. According to the updated Valve Academic Research Consortium 3 definitions, the study exhibited a 4‐year cumulative incidence of SVD of 4.1%, whereas in our cohort of patients with BAV, the incidence was at 4.7% during whole follow‐up. In their study, the mean transprosthetic gradient increased higher from the third year, reaching 13±6.5 mm Hg by the fourth year, a mean increase of 2.5 mm Hg (P=0.031) from baseline without a significant decrease in effective valve area. 26 However, in our study, the mean transprosthetic gradient in patients with BAV showed a similar result at last follow‐up compared with baseline (baseline versus follow‐up, 14.0 versus 13.4 mm Hg; P=0.332). In addition, there is variability in echocardiographic blood flow that is influenced by various factors. 21 Therefore, we took the average of 4 time points (discharge, 1 month, 3 months, and 6 months) when setting the baseline to try to provide reliable baseline data.

BAV Versus TAV Durability in TAVR

Patients with BAV were thought to warrant intervention earlier than patients with TAV, making evidence of long‐term durability in patients with BAV particularly important. 27 As far as we know, our analysis provided initial evidence that patients with BAV exhibit comparable long‐term durability following TAVR to patients with TAV, in terms of SVD, BVF rates, and hemodynamic performance. In our cohort, the incidence of moderate SVD in patients with BAV was slightly higher compared with patients with TAV (10.6% versus 8.3%; P=0.565), primarily driven by a greater proportion of patients with BAV exhibiting a mean gradient ≥20 mm Hg (9.9% versus 5.5%). This could be attributed to the mean gradient being ≈10 mm Hg higher in patients with BAV compared with those with TAV before TAVR. Intriguingly, the incidence of BVF was found to be slightly lower in patients with BAV than in their TAV counterparts (1.8% versus 4.8%; P=0.196). This trend might be due to a higher prevalence of more than mild regurgitation among patients with TAV before TAVR because the majority of patients with TAV underwent reintervention during the follow‐up primarily owing to regurgitation of moderate to severe intensity. The rates of moderate or greater PVL, a significant clinical concern in patients with BAV, were 6.5% in patients with BAV versus 3.4% in patients with TAV in our study, with no statistical significance (P=0.305). The latest‐generation self‐expanding valve has been proven with less PVL at discharge in TAVR recipients. 28 Advancements in devices and technology are expected to further diminish PVL incidence in patients with BAV after TAVR.

BAV Durability in TAVR Versus SAVR

As for valve durability of patients with BAV following SAVR, the definition of SVD for most studies was the need for reoperation. 29 Therefore, a direct comparison of the incidence of SVD and BVF between patients with BAV who underwent TAVR and SAVR poses challenges. The NOTION trial, a randomized controlled trial that excluded patients with BAV, demonstrated a similar incidence of SVD and BVF between TAVR and SAVR. 5 According to recent published studies of SAVR with a follow‐up duration of ≥5 years, patients with BAV could maintain hemodynamic performance comparable to those of patients with TAV during long‐term follow‐up after SAVR. 30 , 31 Both mean transprosthetic gradient and maximum velocity at last follow‐up in our patients with BAV were comparable to those observed in patients with BAV who underwent SAVR. The incidence of moderate or greater PVL among our cohort of patients with BAV was 6.5%, higher than conventional SAVR, and lower than rapid‐deployment aortic valve replacement, a modified surgery with advantages in reduced operative time and enhanced recovery. This was consistent with previous findings in patients with TAV, where the proportion of patients with moderate or greater PVL at 10 years was lower in patients who underwent SAVR than in TAVR, according to the 10‐year results of the NOTION trial (TAVR versus SAVR, 18.0% versus 5.2%). 5

Predictors of SVD

Compared with SAVR, an accelerated SVD process in TAVR was linked to possible leaflet damage during the crimping process, asymmetric expansion with suboptimal leaflet coaptation, and incomplete frame expansion that increased mechanical stress and caused leaflet‐frame interaction. 32 , 33 In TAVR, female sex, small valve annulus, history of prior percutaneous coronary intervention, atrial fibrillation, and hypertension were potential independent predictors of the occurrence of SVD. 34 , 35 In this work, we incorporated relevant characteristics wherever possible, and only balloon postdilation (HR = 6.46 [95% CI, 1.78–23.44]; P=0.005) was the independent predictor of SVD at multivariable analysis in patients with BAV. Even after incorporating all factors analyzed in univariate analysis into the forward/backward selection analysis of multivariate COX regression, postdilation remained the sole risk factor identified. However, these findings were not observed in patients with TAV. This might be because patients requiring postdilation are more susceptible to SVD due to their severe calcium burden and annular eccentricity, which are indicative of their inherently complex anatomic features. In addition, potential damage to structures such as valve leaflets during repeated manipulations during operation in patients with BAV, suggesting that further optimization may be needed in TAVR strategies and instruments when dealing with the complex anatomic structures of patients with BAV. However, it is worth noting that balloon postdilation might mitigate the risk of prosthesis–patient mismatch, necessitating cautious interpretation of this result. 36

Study Limitations

Primarily, the study's limitations are rooted in its single‐center and retrospective design. Second, a portion of the patients without long‐term follow‐up data had passed away, leaving the cause of death ambiguous for many cases. In some cases, valve‐related death cannot be ruled out. Due to the relatively small sample size in our study, this may reduce the statistical power to detect significant differences between groups. Moreover, the study primarily used first‐generation self‐expanding THVs. This caution stemmed from the observation that the generation and type of bioprosthetic valve might differentially impact valve durability. 32 Finally, the mean duration of follow‐up was 5.1 years, with <8.9% of participants followed for >8 years. More valid studies require longer‐term follow‐up.

Conclusions

Comparable valve durability could be achieved with TAVR in patients with BAV and TAV morphology during long‐term follow‐up. Similar to patients with TAV, hemodynamic data in patients with BAV remained stable over 10 years, with a low incidence of SVD and BVF. This study indicated satisfactory long‐term valve durability of TAVR in patients with BAV. However, corroboration through large‐scale, randomized studies is required.
