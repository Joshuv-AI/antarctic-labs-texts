Unveiling dual-functional umami peptides with antioxidant effects from eggshell membrane byproducts: A machine learning-guided approach integrating molecular simulation and quantum chemistry
Authors: Lingjiao Zhu, Jingyan Li, Vincent Guyonnet, Yang Xu, Qiwei Dai, Miaoyan Zhang, Hongxun Wang, Yang Yi, Wei Xu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study developed an efficient strategy for exploring novel dual-functional umami peptides with antioxidant activity from eggshell membrane (ESM), a protein-rich byproduct of the food industry, by integrating machine learning, molecular docking, molecular dynamics simulations, quantum chemistry and in vitro validations. Three novel peptides (CDDDF, WHDCHR, and CWDVYR) were identified from in silico ESM hydrolysates. The synthesized peptides exhibited pronounced umami taste with low recognition thresholds (0.04–0.10 mM), significant umami-enhancing effects, and potent free radical scavenging and reducing capacities. Quantum chemical analyses indicated that Cys, Asp, and Trp residues served as key active sites for both umami recognition and antioxidant activities. Molecular docking and dynamics simulations revealed that these peptides could stably bind to key amino acids in T1R1/T1R3 through hydrogen-bonding and hydrophobic interactions. This work provides robust guidance for discovering functional umami peptides from sustainable protein sources and offers novel insights into their structure-activity relationships and taster mechanisms.Keywords: Umami peptides, Eggshell membrane, Antioxidant activity, Quantum chemistry, Molecular simulation, Taste mechanism

Introduction

Umami, along with sweet, salty, sour, and bitter, is one of the five basic flavors playing a pivotal role in the perception of food taste (An et al., 2024). The molecules contributing to umami flavor include amino acids, organic acids, nucleotides, and peptides, with umami peptides having recently gained significant attention for their great potential in food applications (Elhadad & Wu, 2025; Feng et al., 2024). In comparison with traditional umami enhancers, umami peptides often provide a more complex and persistent umami perception, along with nutritional benefits and a favorable safety profile (Li et al., 2022). The taste mechanism of umami peptides primarily involves their specific interaction with the umami taste receptor T1R1/T1R3, a class C G-protein-coupled receptor (GPCR) heterodimer (Wu et al., 2024). This binding stabilizes the active conformation of the umami receptor, triggering intracellular signal transduction and ultimately eliciting umami perception (Hao et al., 2024). In addition, umami peptides with antioxidant effects can scavenge free radicals and reduce oxidative damage, making them excellent functional ingredients to reduce the oxidative deterioration of foods and enhance their health benefits (Wu et al., 2024). Recent studies have reported several umami peptides with antioxidant activity. Umami peptides derived from fermented soybean paste have been found to exhibit significant antioxidant activity and umami characteristics (Zhang, Chen, et al., 2024). Furthermore, three umami peptides (SGCVNEL, EPLCNQ, and ESCAPQL) extracted from Stropharia rugosoannulata have been identified as possessing antioxidant properties (Li et al., 2024). These dual-functional peptides hold potential as natural additives or preservatives in meat products, sauces, beverages, and snacks to enhance the food antioxidant capacity while imparting savory flavors (Wang et al., 2024; Zhang, Guan, et al., 2024). Hence, the development of dual-functional umami peptides with antioxidant effects will satisfy both the demand for healthy foods and flavor quality, and boost the innovation in seasonings and additives in the food industry.

Traditional approaches for exploring umami peptides rely primarily on enzymatic hydrolysis, sensory-guided multi-step separation and purification, and mass spectrometry for identification (Li et al., 2022). However, these approaches present some limitations, such as being time-consuming, labor-intensive, and costly, thus hindering the rapid development of novel umami peptides (Zhao et al., 2024). Currently, with the rapid development of emerging technologies such as artificial intelligence, bioinformatics, and quantum chemistry, these approaches have gradually become frontier hotspots at the junction between food science and computational chemistry (Chang et al., 2025; Senadheera et al., 2022; Su et al., 2025). Bioinformatics tools such as BIOPEP-UWM and PeptideCutter can be used for in silico digestion of known protein sequences and allow the creation of a large amount of derivative peptide data (Feng et al., 2024; Zhao et al., 2024). The artificial intelligence technology, including machine learning with its deep learning models, accelerates the rapid prediction and development of active peptides (Chang et al., 2025; Coscueta et al., 2022). Quantum chemistry will clarify the properties of electron donor and acceptor regions of peptide molecules by calculating their frontier molecular orbitals (FMOs), providing key theoretical insights for the mechanism of umami peptides (Su et al., 2025; Wang, Yang, et al., 2022). Furthermore, molecular simulations, including molecular docking and molecular dynamics (MD) simulations, will facilitate the precise identification of peptides and elucidate molecular mechanisms (Zhang, Guan, et al., 2024). Therefore, integrating these emerging approaches should be a promising strategy to efficiently identify novel functional umami peptides from food protein sources, especially alternative proteins derived from food by-products.

Eggshell Membrane (ESM) is a protein-rich (approximately 90 %) byproduct of egg processing, usually viewed as a waste and underutilized (Kulshreshtha et al., 2022; Shi et al., 2021). It contains several functional proteins, including collagen, lysozyme, and ovotransferrin (Kulshreshtha et al., 2022), and is particularly abundant in umami-related amino acids such as Glu (10.29 %) and Asp (7.69 %) (Zhu, Ma, et al., 2022), indicating strong potential as a source of umami peptides. Previous studies by our group and others have demonstrated that ESM-derived peptides exhibit antioxidant, anti-diabetic (DPP-IV inhibitory), and anti-inflammatory activities (Shi et al., 2021; Zhu et al., 2024; Zhu, Xiong, et al., 2022). Nevertheless, the umami potential of ESM-derived peptides, especially those with dual umami and antioxidant functions, and their underlying taste mechanisms remain largely unexplored. Utilizing ESM for umami peptide production presents a sustainable strategy to diversify umami peptide sources while upcycling an industrial byproduct into high-value ingredients.

Therefore, this study aimed to investigate novel dual-functional umami peptides from ESM byproducts with antioxidant activity and elucidate their molecular mechanism by combining simulated hydrolysis, machine learning-based screening, molecular simulations, quantum chemistry, and in vitro validation. We hypothesized that novel peptides with dual umami and antioxidant functions could be efficiently identified from the ESM proteins through this integrated approach, and that their dual functions would be governed by specific structural features and stable binding to the T1R1/T1R3 receptor. The present study lays the foundation for the efficient identification of umami peptides with antioxidant activity derived from egg byproducts, promotes innovation of food umami agents, and provides new insights into their structure-activity relationships and taste mechanisms.

Materials and methods

Materials and chemicals

The dietary salt, monosodium glutamate (MSG), and sucrose were purchased from local supermarkets. Food-grade citric acid and L-isoleucine were purchased from Zhengzhou Sanhe Food Additives Co., Ltd. (Zhengzhou, China). Other reagents and chemicals were analytically pure and obtained from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China).

In silico hydrolysis of ESM proteins

The sequences of the primary ESM proteins, namely lysyl oxidase-like 2, CREMPs, lysozyme C, collagen X (α1 chain), and ovalbumin (Ahmed et al., 2019), were obtained from the UniProtKB database (https://www.uniprot.org/) (Table S1). The potential of these ESM proteins to release umami peptides and antioxidant peptides was first evaluated using the BIOPEP database (http://www.uwm.edu.pl/biochemia/index.php/pl/biopep) by counting their frequency of occurrence (Zhu et al., 2024). Then the five proteins were digested using nine commonly used commercial proteases (Table S2) with the BIOPEP-UWM enzymatic digestion program.

Screening of potential umami peptides

PeptideRanker (http://distilldeep.ucd.ie/PeptideRanker) with a scoring threshold of 0.50 was used for preliminary screening of potential bioactive peptides. Next, the critical properties of water solubility, human intestinal absorption (HIA) capability, toxicity, and allergenicity were analyzed using bioinformatics tools including Innovagen, admetSAR, and AllerTOP. The umami taste of the peptides was predicted using three machine learning models (UMPred-FRL, Umami_YYDS, TastePeptidesDM). The peptide could be considered to have an umami taste if its probability of umami exceeds 0.5 in all three machine learning models. (Charoenkwan et al., 2021; Xu et al., 2024). Their antioxidant properties were evaluated using UniDL4BioPep, with scores above 0.5 indicating antioxidant peptides (Du et al., 2023). The information on the above-mentioned bioinformatics tools and screening criteria is listed in Table S3.

Homology modeling and molecular docking

The amino acid sequences of umami taste receptor T1R1 (Uniprot ID: Q7RTX1) and T1R3 (Uniprot ID: Q7RTX0) were obtained from the UniProtKB database (Song et al., 2023). The homology model of the T1R1/T1R3 was constructed using AlphaFold v2.3 and evaluated using the SAVES v6.1 online tool (https://saves.mbi.ucla.edu/) for Ramachandran plot and ERRAT analyses, as well as the ProSA servers (https://prosa.services.came.sbg.ac.at/prosa.php). The 3D structure of the peptides was then constructed using ChemDraw 3D 20.0 software, and the energy minimization of the peptides was performed using the MMFF94 force field (Li et al., 2024). The molecular docking of peptides and the T1R1/T1R3 receptor was performed using AutoDock Vina v1.2.0. The grid box size for coordinates x, y, and z is 50 Å, and the grid center is located at x = −19.864, y = 26.998, and z = 12.460. After docking, the structure of the ligand-receptor complex was visualized using Discovery Studio software. Besides, the docking of three potential umami peptides with DPPH and ABTS was referring to Wen et al. (2021). Briefly, the 3D structures of DPPH (CID: 2735032) and ABTS (CID: 5360881) are acquired from the PubChem database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). Docking was conducted using AutoDock 4.2 software for the peptides and ligands (DPPH and ABTS), and the binding pocket position in the receptor was specified using the molecular viewer of AutoDock tools. The obtained docked conformations were optimized based on the principle of minimizing docking energy, and were viewed using Discovery Studio 2018 software. The binding energy and interactions between receptor and ligands were analyzed to evaluate the potential umami and antioxidant activity of the peptides.

In vitro synthesis of selected peptides

The sequences of potential umami peptides were synthesized with purity levels above 95 % by Hefei National Peptide Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Hefei, China). Taking into account the safety of synthesized peptides during sensory evaluation, trifluoroacetate was entirely replaced by food-grade acetate during the synthesis process. Their structure and purities were verified by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) and mass spectrometry (MS) (Fig. S1).

Electronic tongue analysis

The taste characteristics of the peptides were evaluated using the electronic tongue system (TS-5000Z, INSENT, Japan). Each synthetic peptide was dissolved in deionized water at 1 mg/mL, and the pH was adjusted to 6.5 using 0.1 M NaOH to ensure optimal conditions for taste evaluation. The instrument was equipped with five specific taste sensors: AAE (umami), CT0 (saltiness), CA0 (sourness), C00 (bitterness), and AE1 (astringency). To ensure data reliability, each sample was measured independently in triplicate, and the average values were calculated to minimize systematic errors.

Sensory evaluation

Sensory evaluation of synthetic peptides referred to Xu et al. (2024) with some modifications. The sensory evaluation team consisted of 10 selected laboratory members (6 females and 4 males, aged 20–30 years). Ethical clearance was not required for this study. All participants signed an informed consent form following the Declaration of Helsinki before participation in the sensory evaluation. The rights and privacy of all participants were protected during the execution of the study.

Prior to the experiment, evaluators received specialized training and passed a test demonstrating their accurate identification of the five basic tastes. The standard solutions were 1 % sucrose, 0.35 % NaCl, 0.35 % MSG, 0.08 % citric acid, and 1 % leucine. After dissolving the synthetic peptides in deionized water (1 mg/mL), their taste profiles were assessed using a 10-point scale: 0 for tasteless, 5 for standard solution strength, and 10 for an extremely strong taste. Meanwhile, according to Li et al. (2020), each peptide solution was progressively diluted at a 1:1 (v/v) ratio until the group members could not identify umami. This dilution was recorded as the umami threshold. Additionally, 1 mg/mL of each synthetic peptide was added to a 0.35 % MSG solution to evaluate its umami-enhancing effect.

Antioxidant activity assays

The antioxidant activities of the peptides were evaluated using DPPH and ABTS radical scavenging assays and Fe3+ reducing capacity. The DPPH radical scavenging activity was determined according to Yang et al. (2020). After mixing 1 mg/mL sample solution with 0.1 mM DPPH solution and incubating for 30 min in the dark at room temperature, the absorbance was measured at 517 nm. Based on the method of Zhao et al. (2019), the ABTS solution was diluted to an absorbance of 0.7 at 734 nm, after which a 1 mg/mL sample was added, and the absorbance was determined after a 5-min reaction at 25 °C. For the Fe3+ reducing ability assay, referring to Yang et al. (2020), the 1 mg/mL peptide solution, 0.2 M PBS, and 1 % K3 [Fe(CN)6] were mixed for 20 min at 50 °C. Next, the trichloroacetic acid was added to stop the reaction, and the absorbance at 700 nm was measured after centrifugation.

Active site analysis based on quantum chemical calculations

The density functional theory (DFT) calculations for the umami peptides were conducted utilizing the Gaussian 16 and GaussView 6.0 software packages (Gaussian Inc., Wallingford, CT, USA), referring to Wang, Yang, et al. (2022). The initial conformations of the peptides were constructed using Chem 3D software and subsequently optimized by using the B3LYP/6-311G (d, p) basis set. The highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) and the lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) for peptides were computed. The quantum chemical calculations of the umami peptides allow the analysis of the active sites at the molecular level, thereby clarifying their taste-presenting mechanisms (Su et al., 2025).

MD simulations

Following the method of Zhao et al. (2024), a topology file of each peptide was constructed in Gromacs 2023 software using the Amber FF99SB/ILDN force field file. A 100 ns MD simulation of the peptide-T1R1/T1R3 complex was then performed at a static temperature of 300 K and atmospheric pressure (1 Bar). The simulation employed the AMBER99SB-ILDN force field with the TIP3P water model, and the system charge was neutralized by adding appropriate sodium ions. The system initially underwent energy minimization using the steepest descent algorithm. Equilibration was then conducted with 100,000 steps under both the NVT and NPT ensembles, using a coupling constant of 0.1 ps for 100 ps. Finally, the MD simulation was conducted in 100 ns with 50,000,000 steps. After the simulation, the root mean square deviation (RMSD), radius of gyration (Rg), root mean square fluctuation (RMSF), solvent-accessible surface area (SASA), and the number of hydrogen bonds were calculated using the Gromacs program for MD analysis.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics version 19. A one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was employed, with a significance threshold set at P < 0.05. Each experimental group underwent a minimum of three repetitions. Data are reported as mean ± standard deviation and were visualized using Origin 2021 software.

Results and discussion

In silico evaluation and hydrolysis of ESM proteins as peptide precursors

Since ESM consists of five dominant proteins, lysyl oxidase-like 2, CREMPs, lysozyme C, collagen X (α₁ chain), and ovalbumin, accounting for 83 % of the total protein (Ahmed et al., 2019), these five proteins were evaluated for the potential to release umami peptides and utilized as proteolytic precursors by the BIOPEP-UWM database. This bioinformatics-based tool can integrate computer and laboratory experiments by searching for biologically active or taste-affecting fragments in protein or peptide sequences, calculating quantitative parameters that describe proteins as specific peptide precursors, and simulating protein hydrolysis (Iwaniak et al., 2024; Zhao et al., 2024). The potential of a protein to serve as a precursor of specific peptides can be assessed based on the frequency of relevant peptide sequences within its primary structure (Gu et al., 2023). As shown in Fig. 1A–B, all five ESM proteins exhibited favorable characteristics as precursors of both umami and antioxidant peptides. The frequency of umami peptides ranged from 0.102 to 0.255, which was higher than that of antioxidant peptides (0.074–0.182). Our previous studies have demonstrated the ability of ESM proteins to release antioxidant peptides (Zhu et al., 2024; Zhu, Ma, et al., 2022). These results highlighted the promise of ESM as a novel source of umami peptides.

The in silico proteolysis approach can minimize laborious and expensive experiments and is fast and efficient in comparison to traditional in vitro enzymatic methods (Gu et al., 2024). Through simulated hydrolysis of the five major ESM proteins using nine common commercial proteases, a total of 3596 peptides were generated. As illustrated in the upset plot (Fig. 1C), the peptide profiles varied considerably among different proteases, with each enzyme producing a substantial number of unique peptides (ranging from 43 to 194), underscoring the critical influence of enzyme specificity on hydrolysate composition. Similar results were found in the case of type I collagen hydrolysates in silico (Gu et al., 2024), suggesting that employing diverse proteases is essential for understanding peptide release patterns from ESM proteins. Notably, umami peptides are typically short-chain peptides consisting of 2–9 amino acid residues (Xu et al., 2025). As shown in Fig. 1D, the majority of ESM-derived peptides (90.53 %) fell within this length range. These peptides were mainly derived from and lysyl oxidase-like 2 (34.1 %) and collagen X (α₁ chain) (29.8 %), as displayed in Fig. 1E. Moreover, the umami characteristics of peptides are strongly influenced by their amino acid composition (An et al., 2024). Analysis of the amino acid profiles (Fig. 1F) revealed that hydrolysates rich in umami-related amino acids (Glu, Asp, Gln, Asn), sweet amino acids (Ser, Pro, Gly, Ala) (Li et al., 2022; Zhang, Yan, et al., 2023), and antioxidant-related amino acids (Cys, His, Trp, Tyr) (Wen, Zhang, Zhang, et al., 2020). These findings further support the potential of ESM proteins as valuable precursors for generating umami and antioxidant peptides.

Machine learning-guided screening for dual-functional umami peptides

PeptideRanker is an in silico tool to predict peptide bioactivities, with a score greater than 0.5 considered to be potentially active (Wang et al., 2023). A total of 1287 peptides with a length of 2–9 amino acids and a PeptideRanker score exceeding 0.5 were selected. After removing duplicates and previously reported active peptides, candidates remained for further evaluation (Table S4). Good water solubility is essential for umami perception and normal metabolism (Wu et al., 2024), while allergenicity and toxicity are the main factors limiting the use of peptides in the food industry (Gu et al., 2023; Zhao et al., 2022). In addition, the degree of human intestinal absorption (HIA) is also one of the most critical conditions for assessing food-derived peptides (Amigo & Hernández-Ledesma, 2020). In this study, 64 peptides derived from ESM possessed good water solubility, non-allergenicity, non-toxicity, and absorbability. These peptides were subsequently evaluated using machine learning models for dual umami and antioxidant functions.

Three machine-learning models (UMPred-FRL, Umami_YYDS, and Tastepeptides_DM), known for their high accuracy and reliability in predicting umami taste (Charoenkwan et al., 2021; Chen, Zhou, et al., 2024), were applied to identify potential umami peptides. By combining multiple machine learning models, the false positive rate of prediction can be significantly reduced and the screening efficiency and accuracy improved (Xu et al., 2025). In this study, eleven potential umami peptides were screened out by the three machine learning models for taste properties. Since smaller peptides (MW < 1000 Da) typically exhibit stronger umami intensity (An et al., 2024), eight peptides meeting this criterion were shortlisted (Table 1). Finally, the antioxidant properties of these peptides were assessed using the deep learning model UniDL4BioPep, which was reported to accurately predict the antioxidant capacity of peptides based on a convolutional neural network approach (Du et al., 2023). After the above layer-by-layer screening, three peptides, CDDDF, WHDCHR, and CWDVYR, were identified as the most promising umami peptides to exhibit both umami and antioxidant effects. These peptides originated primarily from in silico hydrolysates of lysyl oxidase-like 2 treated with bromelain or papain. In addition, none of these peptides have been previously reported in the BIOPEP-UWM database, confirming their novelty.

Molecular docking of promising dual-functional umami peptides

Molecular docking has become an important tool for predicting the biological activity of peptide molecules, enabling efficient screening of potential active peptides and revealing their binding mechanisms with targets (Wang, Liu, et al., 2022). In this study, the peptides with potential umami and antioxidant properties screened based on machine learning were further verified through molecular docking.

The T1R1/T1R3 heterodimer has been reported as the primary umami receptor, capable of binding umami peptides via its Venus flytrap (VFT) domain (Zhang et al., 2019). Therefore, the T1R1/T1R3 was used as a receptor for molecular docking with peptides to further predict potential umami peptides. The homology model of the T1R1/T1R3 was constructed (Fig. 2A), and its reliability was verified using SAVES v6.0 and the ProSA program. As shown in the Ramachandran plot (Fig. 2B), 99.9 % of the amino acid residues in the model are located in permissible regions, of which 87.9 % are in the most favored region, 11.8 % are in the additional allowed region, and only 0.1 % are in the prohibited area, suggesting the reliability of the homology model. Furthermore, the overall ERRAT quality factor was found to be 88.80 % (Fig. S2A), which is higher than the 84.51 % reported for a T1R1/T1R3 model in a study of umami peptides from fermented grains (Han et al., 2024). According to Saleh (2022), an ERRAT score above 80 % indicates an accurate model structure. The results suggested that the distribution of non-bonding atomic interactions in this model was of a high quality, comparable to that of experimental structures. Additionally, ProSA was used to evaluate the quality of the global model, yielding Z-scores of −10.1 for T1R1 and −10.91 for T1R3 (Fig. S2B–C). These values are within the typical range for native, X-ray-resolved protein structures and similar to those reported by Zou et al. (2024) for the established T1R1/T1R3 models (−10.20 and −11.57). Furthermore, analysis of local residue energies revealed a predominantly negative profile (Fig. S3D), indicating stable and favorable conformations. Together, these evaluations confirm the high reliability of this model for molecular docking.

Homology modeling of the umami receptor and molecular docking. (A) The homology model of T1R1/T1R3 constructed using AlphaFold. (B) The Ramachandran plot of the T1R1/T1R3 model. Molecular docking of CDDDF (C), CWDVYR (D), and WHDCHR (E) to the T1R1/T1R3 receptor.

After performing the molecular docking of three potential umami peptides with the T1R1/T1R3 receptor, the binding energies of CDDDF, CWDVYR, and WHDCHR with T1R1/T1R3 were −9.1, −10.04, and −10.17 kcal/mol, respectively (Table 2). Binding energy is a key indicator used to evaluate molecular docking between ligands and receptors. A lower binding energy indicates a stronger binding affinity (Yang et al., 2024). In this study, the ESM-derived umami peptides exhibited comparable binding energies to those previously reported for umami peptides derived from chicken soup (−8.2 to −10.2 kcal/mol) and tauco (−9.1 to −9.4 kcal/mol) (Zhang, Zhang, et al., 2023; Herlina et al., 2024). Therefore, all three peptides could bind to the VFT domain of T1R1/T1R3, further indicating their high potential for umami taste. In addition, the antioxidant properties of these peptides were further investigated by molecular docking to DPPH and ABTS radicals. The binding energies between DPPH and the three peptides (CDDDF, CWDVYR, and WHDCHR) were −4.80, −5.29, and −4.16 kcal/mol, respectively, while the binding energies between ABTS and these peptides are −5.14, −7.09 and −6.43 kcal/mol, respectively (Fig. 3). These docking values are lower than those of antioxidant peptides derived from watermelon seeds (DPPH: −1.99 to −3.44 kcal/mol; ABTS: −2.28 to −3.87 kcal/mol), indicating their strong radical scavenging potential for DPPH and ABTS. Therefore, these three potential dual-functional umami peptides with antioxidant effects were identified based on combining machine learning and molecular docking analysis. These peptides were then synthesized to validate their umami tastes and antioxidant activities.

Taste characteristics of potential umami peptides

Electronic tongue analysis

To determine the taste properties of the umami peptide candidates, these peptides were synthesized in vitro (Fig. S1) and evaluated by the electronic tongue evaluation (Fig. 4A). The electronic tongue system is an artificial lipid membrane sensor that mimics the taste sensation of the human tongue and is capable of providing an objective and digital assessment of basic tastes (Calvini & Pigani, 2022). As shown in Fig. 4A, the three synthetic peptides exhibited similar multiple taste profiles, with umami playing the dominant role. The umami values of CDDDF, CWDVYR, and WHDCHR were 15.48 ± 0.01, 15.60 ± 0.27, and 16.17 ± 0.08, respectively. These peptides all contained the umami residue of Asp and hydrophilic amino acids (Cys or Arg), which may contribute to their umami flavor (Zhang et al., 2023, Zhang, Zhang, Liang, Sun and Zhang, 2023). In addition, the presence of Arg as a C-terminal residue has been shown to help the intensity of the umami flavor of peptides (Li et al., 2020). Consistent with the molecular docking results, the umami value of WHDCHR was significantly higher than that of the other two peptides (P < 0.05). These peptides also possessed a bitter flavor, concurring with results reported during the electronic tongue analysis of other synthetic umami peptides (Liang et al., 2022; Zhao et al., 2024). The bitterness of the peptides may originate from hydrophobic amino acid residues (Liang et al., 2022). Moreover, since the human taste bud cells and biosensors may respond differently to taste (Chen, Zhou, et al., 2024), the joint application of sensory evaluation and electronic tongue techniques will elucidate the taste characteristics of synthetic peptides more comprehensively and accurately (Feng et al., 2024). Therefore, the sensory evaluation was further performed to fully capture the taste properties of these potential umami peptides.

The electronic tongue analysis (A), sensory evaluation (B), umami-enhancing characteristics (C), and antioxidant activities (D) of the synthetic peptides. Different letters indicate significant differences between the data (P < 0.05).

Sensory evaluation of umami taste and umami-enhancing effects

The sensory evaluation results of the three synthetic peptides are displayed in Fig. 4B. These peptides exhibited prominent umami taste, accompanied by bitter, sweet, and salty tastes. The umami taste scores of CDDDF, CWDVYR, and WHDCHR were 3.92, 3.90, and 4.33, respectively, which were similar to the scores of tuna-derived umami peptides by sensory evaluation (Zhao et al., 2024). The peptide WHDCHR had the highest umami score, while the peptides CDDDF and CWDVYR exhibited comparable umami values, which were aligned with the findings of the electronic tongue analysis.

The umami thresholds of the three synthetic peptides were 0.04 mM (WHDCHR), 0.08 mM (CWDVYR), and 0.10 mM (CDDDF) (Table 2), results similar to those of umami peptides from corn baking powder (0.0315–0.0625 mM) (Huang et al., 2025). Besides, these thresholds were lower than those of reported umami peptides derived from mushrooms (0.21–0.40 mM) and goose bones (0.41–1.15 mM) (Feng et al., 2024; Xu et al., 2024). Notably, all three peptides significantly enhanced the umami intensity of the 0.35 % MSG solution (Fig. 4C, P < 0.05), with WHDCHR and CWDVYR exhibiting the most significant umami-enhancing effects (60 % and 53 %, respectively), which were significantly higher than that of CDDDF (33 %, P < 0.05). The combination of electronic tongue and sensory evaluation revealed that these three ESM-derived peptides possessed significant umami taste and umami-enhancing effects, pointing to their high potential as novel umami agents. However, the relatively small size of the sensory panel (n = 10) is a limitation of this study. Future studies with a larger and more diverse panel are warranted to further validate and generalize these sensory findings.

Furthermore, although most current research on the umami properties of purified peptides employs solid-phase synthesis methods (Hao et al., 2024), this approach may exert some influence on the taste characteristics of peptide substances. Future studies could explore the production of recombinant umami peptides through genetic engineering techniques. This method would not only enhance the yield of umami peptides and enable large-scale production but also mitigate the shortcomings associated with chemically synthesized peptide substances (Hua et al., 2025).

The antioxidant activities of novel umami peptides

The DPPH and ABTS radical scavenging activities, along with the Fe3+ reducing capacity of these peptides, were assessed to validate the antioxidant activity of these umami peptides. The DPPH and ABTS radical scavenging ability are widely recognized as reliable methods for evaluating the antioxidant activity of bioactive peptides (Sun et al., 2025). The Fe3+ reducing capacity also serves as a crucial indicator for determining the antioxidant potential of peptides (Yang et al., 2020).

As depicted in Fig. 4D, the DPPH radical scavenging rates were 96.03 % for CDDDF, 95.67 % for CWDVYR, and 87.48 % for WHDCHR. The CDDDF and CWDVYR peptides exhibited significantly higher DPPH scavenging rates than WHDCHR (P < 0.05), consistent with WHDCHR having the highest docking energy to DPPH among the three peptides. In addition, all the synthetic peptides exhibited potent ABTS radical scavenging abilities at 1 mg/mL, with values of 93.54 % (CWDVYR), 93.21 % (CDDDF), and 93.16 % (WHDCHR) (P > 0.05). Notably, the ABTS scavenging rates of the three peptides were higher than those of umami peptides derived from lentil (84.14 %–97.28 %, 7 mg/mL) (Rezvankhah et al., 2023). The Fe3+ reducing capacities of CDDDF, CWDVYR, and WHDCHR were 0.960, 0.46, and 0.49, respectively. These results collectively demonstrated the potent antioxidant activities of the three umami peptides at a concentration of 1 mg/mL. This concentration was selected due to its wide application in evaluating the antioxidant properties of peptides (Li et al., 2025; Yan et al., 2024). Compared to previously reported antioxidant peptides derived from sheep hoof and grass carp, the ESM-derived umami peptides exhibited stronger antioxidant activity at the same concentration (1 mg/mL). Specifically, the antioxidant peptides derived from sheep hoof at 1 mg/mL showed 48.56 % and 51.97 % DPPH and ABTS scavenging activities, respectively (Yan et al., 2024). Similarly, the antioxidant peptides derived from grass carp exhibited 35.41 %–49.98 % and 40 %–63.31 % DPPH and ABTS scavenging activities, respectively, at a concentration of 1 mg/mL (Li et al., 2025). Notably, CDDDF showed significantly higher Fe3+ reducing capacity than WHDCHR and CWVDYR (P < 0.05). As reported previously, the antioxidant activity of peptides is closely related to their amino acid composition, sequences, and molecular structure characteristics (Wen, Zhang, Zhang, et al., 2020). All three umami peptides contained the antioxidant-related amino acids, including hydrophobic amino acids (Val, Phe), aromatic amino acids (Tyr, Trp, His), sulfur-containing amino acids (Cys), and acidic amino acids (Asp). Besides, sequences with repeated amino acids exhibit enhanced biological properties (Wen, Zhang, Feng, et al., 2020). The structure of the repeated amino acids of CDDDF may contribute to its superior antioxidant activity.

Additionally, the strong radical scavenging activity of these peptides may be contributed by the strong interaction between the peptides and the DPPH/ABTS radicals. Molecular docking showed that all three peptides could form hydrogen bonds, hydrophobic interactions, and van der Waals forces with DPPH, while the peptides also formed electrostatic interactions (π‑sulfur bonds) with ABTS in addition to these three types of interactions (Fig. 3). Similar to the findings of this study, the antioxidant peptides derived from watermelon seeds formed hydrophobic interactions and hydrogen bonds with DPPH/ABTS radicals and enhanced their antioxidant capacity (Wen et al., 2021). Additionally, the differences between DPPH and ABTS may reflect the different radical scavenging mechanisms of the umami peptides. Hydrogen atom transfer (HAT) is considered to be the main mechanism for DPPH scavenging, while electron transfer (SET) is the main mechanism for ABTS scavenging (Ma et al., 2022). The presence of π‑sulfur bonds indicates that the scavenging of ABTS radicals may involve the SET mechanism, which was similar to the radical scavenging mechanism of peptides from Stropharia rugosoannulata (Chen, Li, et al., 2024). Taken together, all three umami peptides were validated for possessing strong umami taste and antioxidant activities, which were consistent with the prediction results of machine learning and molecular docking analysis.

Quantum chemical analysis

The frontier molecular orbitals (FMOs) based on DFT calculation play a key role in molecular active site studies (Su et al., 2025). By analyzing the HOMO and LUMO of umami peptides, the electron-donating and electron-accepting regions can be characterized, which helps clarify the active sites involved in taste perception (Chang et al., 2023; Su et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2021). In this study, the HOMO and LUMO energies and the HOMO-LUMO energy gap (Egap) were calculated for the three umami peptides (Table 2). The Egap reflects the chemical reactivity of the molecule, and a smaller Egap indicates a higher probability of interaction with taste receptors (Su et al., 2025). In this study, the Egap of the peptides were CDDDF (5.51 eV) > CWDVYR (5.09 eV) > WHDCHR (4.83 eV), respectively, consistent with both molecular docking binding energies and sensory evaluation results. WHDCHR, with the smallest Egap, exhibited the strongest umami intensity, demonstrating a strong correlation between quantum chemical properties and perceived umami taste.

In general, molecular active sites typically correspond to regions with high electron density variability, as indicated by HOMO and LUMO localization (Chang et al., 2023; Wang, Yang, et al., 2022). In this study, the HOMO orbitals of CDDDF were mainly distributed on the residue Cys (C), whereas the LUMO orbitals were primarily distributed on Phe (F) and Asp (D) at the penultimate and third-to-last positions. Moreover, the HOMO and LUMO orbitals are also closely related to the antioxidant capacity of compounds (Sun et al., 2025). Consistent with the active sites of the three peptides calculated by FMOs analysis, the amino acids C, D, and W are widely reported as key antioxidant amino acids within peptides (Wen, Zhang, Feng, et al., 2020; Wen, Zhang, Zhang, et al., 2020). In this study, the active amino acids C and D of the three umami peptides formed hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic interactions with DPPH, and the amino acid W formed electrostatic interactions with ABTS+ (Fig. 3), which played a key role in removing free radicals.

Interaction mechanisms of umami peptides with the T1R1/T1R3 receptor

To further elucidate the taste mechanisms of the three novel peptides, their binding modes to the T1R1/T1R3 umami receptor were analyzed through intermolecular interaction analysis. As shown in Fig. 2C–E, all three peptides could access the VFT-binding domains of T1R1 and T1R3. It is widely accepted that T1R1 is primarily responsible for ligand recognition, while T1R3 stabilizes the complex and assists in signal transduction (Nuemket et al., 2017). As summarized in Table 2, the peptides interacted with 34 amino acid residues of T1R1/T1R3, primarily through hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic interactions.

Specifically, peptide CDDDF formed 10 hydrogen bonds with residues Lys193, Ala227, Asp219, Gln226, Asn230, Thr175, Arg220, and Lys155, along with 3 hydrophobic interactions (Pi-Alkyl) (Fig. 2C). Peptide CWDVYR established 14 hydrogen bonds with Asp219, Gln231, Arg220, Gln181, Thr175, Lys155, Ser173, Gln226, and Gln221, in addition to 9 hydrophobic interactions (Fig. 2D). Peptide WHDCHR formed 14 hydrogen bonds with Glu45, Arg180, Asp216, Glu148, His278, Glu105, Arg247, Leu51, Arg252, and Asp215, and 9 hydrophobic interactions (Fig. 2E). A minor unfavorable donor-donor interaction was also observed between WHDCHR and Arg252. Overall, hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic interactions were identified as the dominant forces facilitating peptide-receptor binding, consistent with findings reported for umami peptides derived from Boletus edulis (Song et al., 2023). Notably, residues Thr175, Arg220, Gln226, Ala227, and Arg180 demonstrated high docking frequency and strong hydrogen-bonding capability, suggesting their critical role as anchor sites within the T1R1/T1R3 binding pocket. Among these, Thr175 and Arg220 were also reported as key binding residues for umami peptides from Yanjin black-bone chicken and chicken breast (Guo et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2025).

Importantly, the key active residues identified through earlier FMO analysis, i.e., C1 and D4 in CDDDF, W2 in CWDVYR, and W1 and D3 in WHDCHR, contributed substantially to hydrogen bonding (forming 5, 4, and 7 bonds, respectively). Hydrogen bonding is crucial for umami peptide recognition and stable binding to T1R1/T1R3 (Zhao et al., 2022). This finding indicated that quantum chemically active sites were more probably to interact with the umami receptor and play a key role in the binding to T1R1/T1R3 for umami recognition, which was consistent with previous reports (Su et al., 2025). Similar observations were reported by Zhang, He, et al. (2024), where quantum chemically active sites (Tyr, Ser, and Asn) in peptide NEFGYSNR formed multiple hydrogen bonds with the TMC4 receptor, significantly enhancing the affinity of the peptide-taste receptor binding.

MD simulation analysis

MD is an effective computational technique for investigating the dynamic behavior of biomolecular systems at the atomic level, providing insights into structural stability and interaction mechanisms between receptors and ligands (Jia et al., 2024; Zhao et al., 2024). In this study, a 100 ns MD simulation was performed to evaluate the binding stability of the three umami peptides to the T1R1/T1R3 receptor.

The RMSD serves as an indicator of the stability of peptide-receptor complexes, where a smoother RMSD curve suggests a more stable complex structure (Wang, Liu, et al., 2022). As shown in Fig. 6A, the RMSD values for the CDDDF-T1R1/T1R3, CWDVYR-T1R1/T1R3, and WHDCHR-T1R1/T1R3 complexes fluctuated between 0.3 and 0.5 nm, indicating that all systems reached equilibrium and formed stable complexes. Among them, the WHDCHR complex exhibited the smallest RMSD fluctuations, suggesting superior binding stability. The Rg is an indicator to assess the structural compactness of the complexes (Feng et al., 2024). Through the MD simulation, the Rg values of all complexes varied within a narrow range of 0.2 nm (Fig. 6B), indicating that peptide binding did not induce significant structural loosening or unfolding of the receptor. Additionally, the RMSF values are employed to evaluate the volatility of protein residues, with higher values indicating greater fluctuations in residues (Feng et al., 2024). As depicted in Fig. 6C, the complexes formed by three peptides with T1R1 (chain A) and T1R3 (chain B) exhibited similar RMSF curves. Residues within the VFT domain binding site exhibited low fluctuations, indicating enhanced stability upon peptide binding, while peripheral regions showed higher flexibility. Furthermore, the SASA parameter can predict the extent of conformational changes during binding (Zhao et al., 2024). After the initial simulation stage, the SASA of WHDCHR-T1R1/T1R3 and CWDVYR-T1R1/T1R3 tended to stabilize, while CDDDF-T1R1/T1R3 gradually stabilized after 80 ns (Fig. 6D). Hydrogen bonding plays a critical role in stabilizing peptide-receptor interactions (Zhao et al., 2022). Throughout the simulation, all three peptides maintained at least two hydrogen bonds with the receptor (Fig. 6E), further confirming their stable binding. In summary, the MD simulations demonstrated that all three umami peptides formed stable complexes with T1R1/T1R3. Among them, WHDCHR exhibited the strongest binding stability. These results were consistent with those from molecular docking, sensory evaluation, and quantum chemical calculations.

Conclusion

In summary, three novel dual-functional umami peptides (CDDDF, CWDVYR, and WHDCHR) with antioxidant effects were identified from ESM by integrating in silico enzymolysis, machine learning, molecular simulation, and in vitro approaches, demonstrating that ESM is a new source of functional umami peptides. These peptides were confirmed to possess significant umami tastes, umami-enhancing effects in MSG solution, and antioxidant effects. Through quantum chemical calculations and molecular docking, the amino acid residues C, D, and W within the peptides were identified as key active sites contributing to both umami perception and antioxidant functionality. Molecular simulations further revealed that these peptides could stably interact with the receptor via hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic interactions, primarily through the residues of Thr175, Arg220, Gln226, Ala227, and Arg180. This research provides a framework for efficiently developing novel functional umami peptides from food by-products through integrating food science and computational technology, and offers novel insights into understanding the structure-activity relationships and molecular mechanisms of dual-functional umami peptides.
