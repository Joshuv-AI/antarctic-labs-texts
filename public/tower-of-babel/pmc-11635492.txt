Petrographic and Molecular Characterization of Organic-Rich Mudstones with Petroleum Generative Potential from The Lower Triassic Montney Formation, Western Canada
Authors: Daniela Becerra, Lavern Stasiuk, Christopher R. Clarkson, Humberto Carvajal-Ortiz, Amin Ghanizadeh, Thomas Moslow
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The Triassic Montney Formation hosts major oil and gas resources in Western Canada. Despite significant historical development of these resources, the origin of its hydrocarbons remains unclear, partly due to limited evidence of primary organic matter within the formation. Most of the hydrocarbons in the Triassic Montney Formation are trapped in low-permeability siltstone facies. For the purposes of this study, however, we investigated the lesser-known and understudied organic- and clay-rich mudstone beds, typically ranging from 2 to 8 cm in thickness, which are interbedded with the siltstone reservoir facies. Petrographic and organic geochemical analyses were conducted on core samples from a well located in west-central Alberta with thermal maturity of 0.95–1.16%Ro. Petrographic analysis, including kerogen maceral composition and fluorescence of petroleum fluid inclusions, reveals that the organic matter in the Montney Formation is closely linked to its host lithology. Organic-rich mudstone facies, with TOC values up to 3.5 wt %, contain primary kerogen (32–74 vol %) and solid bitumen (29–68 vol %). In contrast, the more organic-lean siltstone facies (TOC < 0.8 wt %) contain primarily pore-filling solid bitumen and lack structured kerogen. Kerogen within the organic-rich mudstone facies is mainly composed of liptinite macerals, including amorphinite, liptodetrinite, and alginite, subordinate amounts of acritarch and sporinite, and traces of inertinite and vitrinite macerals. The primary organic matter in the mudstone facies is presumed to be derived from marine sources (i.e., phytoplanktonic algae and bacterial biomass). Its preservation may result from the balance between periodic high productivity (potentially driven by river floods), rapid sedimentation to bury and preserve organic matter, and low dilution by siliciclastic material. Organic petrology analysis suggests an in situ generation of hydrocarbons in the mudstone beds and a potential subsequent short-distance migration to adjacent siltstone beds. Biomarker analysis supports the genetic linkage between rock extracts from these beds. Additionally, three families of petroleum fluid inclusions were systematically observed (using fluorescence microscopy) in several sets of contiguous siltstone and mudstone beds. These findings suggest that the organic-rich mudstone facies partially contributed to the present-day hydrocarbons of the Middle Montney Member in the study area.

Introduction

The Lower Triassic Montney Formation in the Western Canada Sedimentary Basin (WCSB) is a siltstone-dominated succession (with minor mudstone) hosting significant accumulations of oil and gas. Conventional reservoirs (high porosity, permeability) within Montney have been developed since the 1950s, while unconventional (low porosity, permeability) reservoirs have been developed since 2005.1 Currently, almost half of Western Canadian gas production comes from the Montney Formation;2 therefore, it is critical to refine the understanding of the Montney petroleum systems.

The distribution of petroleum fluids in Montney reservoirs generally grades from dry gas in the down-dip regions, transitioning to condensate and light oil along the basin margin.3,4 Despite its extensive resources, the origin of the present-day hydrocarbons in the Montney Formation (i.e., petroleum source rocks) is still not fully understood. Currently, there is limited evidence for substantial primary organic matter or oil-prone kerogen in the Montney. Based on early geochemical and biomarker analyses, the source of the majority of Montney hydrocarbons was interpreted to be the Middle Triassic Doig Formation or the Jurassic Nordegg Formation.5−7 More recently, some authors have suggested that organic-rich intervals within the Montney Formation may have sourced some of the hydrocarbons in place.4,8−11 While the Doig and Nordegg formations are proven source rocks for some Triassic oil accumulations in the WCSB, very few studies have been conducted to evaluate the capability of Montney to generate hydrocarbons. Therefore, this study aims to assess the petroleum potential of kerogen and understand the origin of related petroleum products in the mudstone and siltstone facies of the Middle Montney Member of west-central Alberta.

Recently, several organic petrology studies conducted in the thermally mature to overmature part of the WCSB have described the Montney Formation as an organic-lean siltstone with the majority of the total organic carbon (TOC) corresponding to solid bitumen.12−14 These studies suggest that oil from an external source initially migrated to the pore network of siltstone reservoirs and subsequently was, in part, degraded to solid bitumen; an intraformational kerogen source for the origin of the solid bitumen was not considered or postulated. In contrast, support for intraformational oil-prone kerogen rich beds in the Montney Formation has been demonstrated by organic petrology studies conducted in the immature to early mature part of the WCSB.15,16 These studies have documented the presence of oil-prone kerogen, including fluorescing amorphous kerogen (i.e., amorphinite), alginite, and acritarch, along with solid bitumen. TOC values in these Type II, potential source units range from 1.8 to 3.11 wt % with hydrogen index values of 469 to 512 mg/g.16

None of the aforementioned studies have documented the association between organic matter type and sedimentary facies (i.e., lithology), nor have they proposed a depositional model. In a preliminary study, Becerra et al.17 documented the presence of primary organic matter within specific clay-rich facies of the Montney Formation. Later, Ardakani et al.18 supported the conclusions of Becerra et al.17 by performing an in-depth re-examination of samples analyzed in Beaton’s study,15 as well as additional samples. The Ardakani et al.18 study confirms the potential of organic-rich mudstone beds to serve as an intraformational source of hydrocarbons within the Montney Formation. In this study, organic geochemical and petrographic methods are used to characterize the organic matter and its association with host rock lithology using core samples from a well located in west-central Alberta, where the Middle Montney Member is in the peak-to-late oil window (Figure 1A). The main objectives of this study are to determine the type, amount, maturity, and origin of the primary kerogen and secondary organic matter, as a function of their depositional environment; and to assess whether hydrocarbons could have migrated from the organic-rich mudstones into interbedded organic-lean siltstone beds.

Geological Setting

The Montney Formation was deposited during the Early Triassic within the WCSB on the northwest margin of Pangea.20 Although primarily a Lower Triassic stratigraphic unit, the Montney records deposition from the (latest) Permian to just prior to the Middle Triassic.21,22 While originally considered to be a passive margin,20,23 more recent geodynamic studies propose a back-arc basin,24 fore-arc basin25 and foreland basin26 tectonic setting. During the Triassic, the WCSB was situated in a midlatitudinal setting (∼30°N) with an arid climate and significant aeolian sediment input, resulting in mixed siliciclastic and carbonate deposition and extensive dolomite and evaporite formation.27 The units are interpreted to be deposited primarily within a shallow marine environment (shoreface through offshore) on a broad westerly dipping ramp or inner shelf.20,28,29

The Montney Formation covers 130,000 km2 from northeast British Columbia to western Alberta (Figure 1A). Its thickness ranges from less than 1 m at the easterly subcrop to over 350 m in the west.30 In Alberta, the base and top of the Montney Formation are recorded by erosional unconformities in the Permian Belloy Formation and the Middle Triassic Doig Formation, respectively.20 Stratigraphically, Montney is subdivided into three unconformity-bounded units: Lower Montney, Middle Montney, and Upper Montney.22 The Middle Montney in west-central Alberta is defined as the Smithian substage of the Lower Triassic (Figure 1B), as interpreted from conodont and palynomorph biostratigraphy, and is a regionally correlatable interval of strata within the Montney Formation.21,22 The Montney Formation was deposited in the study area within the Karr-Kakwa paleo low, which was subtly influenced by two paleo highs underlain by Devonian Leduc reefs and platform margins.22

Data Set and Experimental Methods

A continuous 230 m Montney core from the 2/04–14–064–05W6 well located in the Kakwa Field in central-west Alberta was examined in this study (Figure 1A). The full diameter (8.0 cm) core includes the base of the Doig Formation to the top of the Belloy Formation (Figures 1B and 2). The stratigraphic framework developed by Davies et al.22 was followed for the internal Montney subdivisions (Figures 1B and 2). In total, 20 samples were collected from the 1/3 slabbed core obtained from the upper Middle Montney interval. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) was used to characterize the distribution and composition of the inorganic fractions of the rock samples. Representative polished blocks (∼2 cm3) were analyzed using a FEI Quanta FEG 250 environmental field emission scanning electron microscope. The microscope was fitted with secondary and backscattered electron (BSE) detectors and energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX).

Programmed pyrolysis was performed on approximately 60 mg of powdered mudstone samples by a commercial laboratory using the Rock-Eval 6 method. Parameters determined include S1 (mg HC/g rock), S2 (mg HC/g rock), TOC (wt %), and HI mg (HC/g TOC).31 Additionally, programmed pyrolysis data from 41 Montney siltstone samples from the subject well were incorporated into this study; these legacy data were facilitated by the Alberta Energy Regulator.32

Organic petrography was carried out on two types of rock samples: (1) polished, epoxy impregnated pellets (3.2 cm diameter) of gently crushed and riffled, randomly oriented rock particles (20–35 mesh: 0.5–0.8 mm), and (2) polished blocks (∼2 × 5 cm2) covering portions of adjacent mudstone and siltstone beds oriented perpendicular to bedding. Both sets of samples were ground and polished according to the ASTM standard D2797.33 Samples were examined using an incident light Zeiss Axio Imager.Z2m microscope, equipped with white and UV-fluorescent light sources (Zeiss VIS-LED with wavelength illumination 400–700 nm peak at 460 nm). The Hilgers Diskus-Fossil system was used for reflectance measurements using a synthetic standard of 0.885%Ro for calibration. Maceral point counting was performed on the pellet samples following the ASTM standard D7708.34 The point counting was performed using the graticule method (100 points square graticule at 800× magnification), and the sample was traversed with a stepping stage interval of 300 μm. The maceral classification and terminology applied in the current study are based on the ICCP System.35,36 Random reflectance was measured on various groups of organic matter (e.g., solid bitumen, inertinite, vitrinite) under oil immersion (n = 1.518; objective 50x, total 800× magnification) following the standard method of ASTM.37 The measured solid bitumen reflectance (B%Ro) values of the medium-reflecting population were converted to the equivalent vitrinite reflectance values (V%Roeqv) using the Jacob equation V%Roeqv = (0.618 * B%Ro + 0.4),38 which has been shown to be the best conversion for determining maturity in the WCSB39,40 and in other basins around the world.41−43

Fluorescence analyses were conducted on the surface of polished block samples, using a LEJ HXP-120 mercury short arc reflector lamp, and a J&M TIDAS S800 UV/NIR detector. The following filter block combination was used during spectral analysis: 365 nm excitation, 395 nm beam splitter, and 397 nm barrier filter. Spectra were corrected for background noise and blackbody radiation. Two parameters were used to evaluate the fluorescence properties and spectral characteristics of the fluid inclusions for relative comparison, the wavelength of maximum emission Lmax (in nm) and the Red to Green quotient Q = Intensity650 nm/Intensity500 nm. The timing of fluid inclusions was estimated by assessing the diagenetic minerals hosting the hydrocarbon-bearing fluid inclusions (HCFI) in a simplified scheme as 1) Early cements not fracture related, 2) late cements not fracture related, and 3) latest cements, usually related to fractures that cross-cut minerals in cement stages 1 and 2.

Four samples comprised of two sets of contiguous mudstone and siltstone beds were analyzed by gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC-MS). First, five grams of pulverized sample was cold extracted with multiple aliquots of dichloromethane (DCM) and methanol (MeOH) [93:7 v:v] by vortexing and centrifuging (1,800 rpm) until the supernatant was colorless. The rock extracts were separated using open column chromatography with silica gel based on the method of Bastow et al.44 Each fraction (i.e., saturated and aromatic) was analyzed by using an Agilent 7890B gas chromatograph interfaced with an Agilent 5977 mass spectrometer. The GC oven was programmed to heat from 30 to 325 °C at 4 °C/min. The saturated and aromatic fractions were analyzed in SIM-scan mode (selected ion monitoring), scanning 50–550 mz, and collecting 60 selected ions. Compound concentrations were determined by integrating GC-MS signals using internal standards.

Results

Lithology, Mineralogy, and Fabric of Rock Samples

Nearly 300 black mudstone beds were identified, with individual thicknesses ranging from 1 to 8 cm in the studied core (Figure 2). While these beds are more abundant in the upper part of the Middle Montney, scattered beds are also present in the lower part of the Middle Montney (refer to Davies et al.22 for stratigraphic nomenclature). In slabbed cores, the black mudstone beds have a massive appearance and sedimentary structures are typically absent (Figure 2). The bounding surfaces of the mudstone beds are abrupt, and individual beds are interbedded with a wide variety of fine- to coarse-grained siltstone facies that are massive in appearance, rippled, laminated, heterolithic, or soft-sediment deformed (Figure 2). Of significance, most of the mudstone beds are below the resolution of standard well logs (e.g., gamma ray, bulk density, resistivity) due to their relatively small thickness (<10 cm).

Compositionally, the mudstone facies have a distinctive clay-rich illitic matrix (Figure 3). Suspended in this matrix are poorly sorted framework grains, which mainly consist of feldspars, quartz, pyrite, and muscovite (Figure 3). In contrast, the siltstone facies are dominated by well-sorted grains of quartz, dolomite, feldspars, and only minor amounts of clay and pyrite (Figure 3). Pyrite is present as individual anhedral or euhedral crystals, as well as framboids and aggregates in both facies (Figure 4). However, the mean pyrite content is significantly higher (by 20–40×) in the mudstone facies compared to the siltstone facies (Figure 3). Petrographic observations revealed similar size distributions of pyrite framboids in both mudstone (<2–35 μm) and siltstone facies (<3–30 μm).

Programmed Pyrolysis Rock-Eval

The Rock-Eval parameters TOC (%), S1 (mg of HC/g of rock), S2 (mg of HC/g of rock), Tmax (°C), Hydrogen Index (HI) (mg of HC/g of TOC), and Production Index (PI) (S1/[S1+S2]) are summarized in Table 1. The TOC content for 20 mudstone samples ranges from 1.15 to 3.50 wt % with an average of 2.00 wt %, except for one sample with a lower TOC content of 0.86 wt % (Table 1, Figure 2). In contrast, TOC values for the 41 siltstone samples are all below one percent, with an average of 0.46 wt % (Table 1, Figure 2). Similarly, S1 and S2 values are below 1 mg of HC/g of rock for siltstone samples, whereas these values are up to 10 and 4 mg of HC/g of rock, respectively, for the mudstone samples (Table 1). An S1 versus TOC plot suggests that most of the mudstone samples have a relatively higher in situ oil saturation, as indicated by the excess S1 over TOC values (Figure 5A); this is known as the crossover effect and suggests the presence of potentially producible oils.45

Tmax values for the mudstone samples range from 433 to 448 °C with a mean value of 442 °C, which equates to a vitrinite reflectance range of 0.63–0.90%Ro, derived by using the Barnett shale model (Calculated %Ro = 0.0180 × Tmax −7.16).46 Most siltstone samples within this interval yielded very low S2 amounts (<0.5 mg HC/g rock), resulting in unreliable Tmax values for the purpose of thermal maturity assessment.47 Therefore, the Tmax was measured in two siltstone samples only, with values of 447 and 452 °C (Table 1). As for the mudstone facies, the Tmax values are in agreement with the regional maturity trends that place the Montney Formation within the peak-to-late oil generation window in the study area.4,5,48,49 Furthermore, the production indices (PI) in the mudstone samples range from 0.33 to 0.75, with an average of 0.52, supporting thermal maturity within the peak-to-late oil window (Table 1). Contaminant-free samples are identified by cross plotting PI and Tmax values (Figure 5B); the results are consistent with organic petrography observations, which show no evidence of oil-based drilling mud or other contaminants. Additionally, the Total Ion Chromatograms (TIC) from the GC-MS analyses did not indicate contamination.

The hydrogen index (HI) values for the mudstone samples range from 80 to 210 mg/g and from 7 to 60 mg/g for the siltstone samples (Table 1). When cross-plotting HI versus Tmax values, the samples exhibit maturity trends similar to that of Type III kerogen (Figure 5C). However, organic petrology observations do not support a Type III kerogen interpretation, and do not indicate significant amounts of terrigenous organic matter (Type III kerogen) in any of the mudstone samples. This discrepancy is likely due to hydrogen loss during thermal maturation of oil-prone kerogen and its conversion to hydrocarbons, which produces lower S2 and subsequently lower HI values.50−52 The presence of solid bitumen may also suppress Tmax values.42

Organic Petrology

Maceral Composition

Point counting was conducted on seven mudstone samples. Overall, the samples consist of two broad categories of organic matter: (1) primary kerogen and (2) secondary solid bitumen. The percentage of kerogen macerals components ranges from 32 to 74 (vol %), whereas the secondary solid bitumen ranges from 26 to 68 (vol %) (Table 3). The kerogen is mainly comprised of liptinite macerals and significantly lower amounts of terrigenous macerals (e.g., vitrinite, inertinite, sporinite). Characteristics of the Primary Macerals and Secondary Products Found in Organically Rich Mudstone Samples

Liptinite macerals are composed of amorphinite, liptodetrinite, alginite, and lower amounts of acritarch and sporinite (Table 3; Figure 6). Amorphinite, constitutes the primary maceral from the liptinite group in all the mudstone samples analyzed (Table 3). It is generally understood that the terms amorphinite, amorphous organic matter (AOM), and bituminite are used interchangeably to describe unstructured kerogen.53,54 Amorphinite, following the ICCP classification system, occurs in two distinct morphologies within the samples: the first morphology constitutes small granular lenses which are frequently found in mineral interstices (Figures 6 and 7); the second, and more common morphology, consists of discontinuous to continuous networks of smooth to granular amorphinite, sulfides (e.g., framboidal and euhedral pyrite), and solid bitumen (Figure 6). Both amorphinite varieties are nonfluorescent, and thus are classified as hebamorphinite.54,55 Alginite occurs either as solitary or colonial (i.e., coccoidal) morphotypes (Figure 7). Unicellular prasinophyte alginite (Leiosphaeridia-like) occur as flattened discs with distinctive external forms which are relatively undeformed (Figure 7); in a few cases, some specimens are wrapped around framework grains (Figure 4). Generally, alginite does not display a preferred orientation within the rock matrix; some specimens occur at high angles with respect to the depositional bedding fabric (Figure 4). The unicellular alginite typically emit low-intensity orange fluorescence when observed using blue light (400–440 nm) excitation (Figure 7). Partial to complete permineralization of alginite is common, with pyrite being the main replacement mineral (Figures 4 and 7). Traces of coccoidal alginite (Botryococcus-like) showing spherical, ovoid shapes were found in some samples, and like the unicellular alginite, they emit low-intensity orange fluorescence (Figure 7).

Liptodetrinite is a persistent component found in all mudstone samples (Table 3) and emits moderate to low intensity orange-to-red-orange fluorescence, suggesting that much of the liptodetrinite may have affinities to alginite. Relatively well preserved spiny acanthomorphic acritarchs (Veryachium- and Michystridium-like) ranging in size from 10 to less than 30 μm in diameter are also present in some samples (Table 3; Figure 6). Most acritarch are partially permineralized by pyrite and other carbonate minerals (e.g., dolomite). Sporinite was observed only in the shallowest mudstone sample (sample #5, Table 3), occasionally with an internal morphology of thin, contorted, brown fluorescing walls. Some structured sporinite show reticulation and Corollina-like complex striations (Figure 6), typical of Triassic spore morphology.56,57

Terrigenous macerals of the vitrinite group are found only in trace amounts in the samples and occur mainly as small particles of vitrodetrinite and telovitrinite (10–20 μm) of light-gray color (Ro 0.95–1.16%) (Table 3; Figure 6). Traces of higher reflecting corpohuminite (Ro 1.4%) with sharp distinct edges, suggesting an algal akinete cell source,40,58 were also found in one sample (Figure 6). Macerals of the inertinite group are also present in trace amounts and include fusinite, semifusinite, inertodetrinite, and graphite-like particles (Table 3, Figure 6). Semifusinite occurs as irregularly shaped masses with remnants of cellular structure in some particles; it is gray to light gray with very high reflectance (Ro 1.6–3.2%). Fusinite (pyrofusinite) fragments are rare; they are light gray, display high relief, and have the highest reflectance of this group (Ro 3.2%). The distinctive fusinite cellular structure is visible in some particles (Figure 6D). Inertodetrinite is present as isolated light-gray curved fragments (<30 μm) and has a high reflectance value (Ro 1.8%) (Figure 6A). Traces of highly reflective graphite-like particles (Ro 5.7%) were also observed in most samples with remnant areas with 15.0%Ro max; most particles did not yield accurate reflectance values.

The secondary products (e.g., solid bitumen; oil inclusions) are dominated by nonfluorescing isotropic or homogeneous solid bitumen with low bireflectance and no optical mosaic microtextures.59 These comprise 26 to 68 vol % of the total organic matter within the mudstone samples (Table 3). They are present in a wide range of reflectance from low to high values (0.4–1.5%Ro) (Tables 2 and 3). A representative reflectogram of solid bitumen %Ro values showing a trimodal distribution with low, medium, and high %Ro populations is presented in Figure 8B. The solid bitumen comprises a variety of morphologies within the samples: as isolated elongated particles (<150 μm), as networks in the clay-rich matrix, in micropores, microfractures, commonly associated with pyrite (crystals, framboids, or clusters), and within large pores associated with silty aggregates (Figures 6 and 7). Traces of optically anisotropic solid bitumen were identified in two samples (Table 3); they occur as isolated particles and display fine to medium-grained mosaic microtexture60 and medium to high reflectance values (1.1–3.6%Ro). Three types of oil inclusions are present in trace amounts in some samples, with fluorescence colors ranging from orange-yellow to yellow to white-blue to blue; further details are presented in the following section.

Detailed maceral point counting was not deemed necessary in the siltstone facies due to the lower organic content found in these facies (TOC < 0.8 wt %; Table 1); from visual observation of polished block samples, no primary (structured) kerogen macerals were noted in these facies. Only secondary products are present in the siltstone facies, including low to high reflecting isotropic solid bitumen infilling interparticle porosity (Figures 6 and 7), with %Ro values similar to those in the mudstones.

Thermal Maturity–Reflectance Analysis

Thermal maturity was assessed from reflectance measurements on the medium-reflecting population of solid bitumen particles due to the limited amount of vitrinite within the samples. Between 10 and 35 measurements were recorded for each sample. A range of 0.77 to 1.29 B%Ro, with an average value of 1.02 B%Ro, was found for the seven analyzed samples (Figure 8). Equivalent vitrinite reflectance (V%Roeqv) values calculated using the Jacob38 equation vary between 0.98 and 1.10%, with an average value of 1.03% VRoeqv, which is consistent with the reflectance of a few vitrinite particles (0.95–1.16% VRo; Figure 8A). Overall, the range of thermal maturity between samples is within the margin of expected uncertainty for the reflectance and V%Roeqv (calculated) assessment methods. These values are also in agreement with the pyrolysis Tmax values (443–448 °C), indicating that the samples are within the peak-to-late oil window. Further, these values are consistent with the fluorescence colors of alginite, liptodetrinite, and acritarch.61 Additionally, V%Roeqv values measured on a Middle Triassic Doig sample are included for reference (VRoeqv 0.97–1.10%; Figure 8A). The %Ro versus depth plot shows no increase in maturity, as is expected over a relatively short interval (∼100 m) (Figure 8A).

Fluorescence Characteristics of Petroleum Fluid Inclusions

Fluorescing petroleum-oil inclusions were observed in both facies (mudstones and siltstones) within all seven block samples when viewed under UV light. They occur mainly as primary inclusions in diagenetic cement, such as quartz and dolomite overgrowths, and in unhealed microfractures within detrital grains or diagenetic cement (Figure 10). VIS (visible light) spectra were measured for a total of 60, two-phase inclusions that had relatively consistent liquid to vapor ratios. The fluorescence color of the observed oil inclusions ranges from orange-yellow to yellow to white-blue to blue (Figure 10).

Three populations of oil inclusions were identified based on the spectral shape, Lmax, and Q factor, Type A (n= 14), Type B (n= 25), and Type C (n= 21) (Figure 10). The three populations of inclusions were observed in both mudstone and siltstone facies but with varying dominance within the facies. Paragenetically, the orange-yellow petroleum fluid inclusions predate the white yellow, and blue fluorescing inclusions (Figure 10). Type A oil inclusions have the shortest wavelength emission, with Lmax values ranging from 430 to 446 nm and Q values ranging from 0.09 to 0.22. They are interpreted to represent the highest maturity oils with an estimated API > 45°.62−64 The relative abundances of Type A inclusions in the mudstone and siltstone samples were very similar (Figure 10). Type B oil inclusions are characterized by intermediate wavelength emissions with Lmax values ranging from 449 to 495 and Q values ranging from 0.07 to 0.36, with an estimated gravity between 30 and <40° API.62−64 Although Type B oil inclusions occur in both facies, they are more frequent within the mudstone samples compared with the siltstones (Figure 10). Type C oil inclusions show yellow to orange fluorescence and have Lmax values ranging from 501 to 545 nm and Q values ranging from 0.18 to 0.59 (Figure 10). Type C oil inclusions represent the lowest maturity, earliest oils, and have an estimated API below 30°.62−64 Type C oil inclusions occur mainly in the mudstone samples, with a few occurrences also observed within the siltstone facies, very close to the mudstone-siltstone contact (within ∼10–50 μm).

Discussion

Paleodepositional Environment: Productivity and Preservation of Oil-Prone Organic Matter in the Middle Montney

Based on quantitative evaluation of kerogen composition, it is concluded that the organic-rich mudstone facies of the Montney are dominated by liptinite macerals, chiefly amorphinite, followed by liptodetrinite and alginite, subordinate amounts of acritarch and sporinite, and traces of inertinite and vitrinite macerals. Considering this association of organic macerals (which are mainly of a marine origin), it can be concluded that the origin of primary organic matter found in the mudstone facies was most likely a Type II kerogen.68 This is consistent with recent palynomorph analysis conducted on the Montney, providing evidence of primary kerogen (Type II) derived from planktonic sources,69 and pyrolysis data from Montney samples within the oil window, where TOC and HI values of 1.8 to 3.11 wt % and 469 to 512 mg/g, respectively, have been reported.16

While the amorphinite alone does not reflect shoreline proximity or water depth, the coexistence of small Prasinophyte alginite, coccoidal alginite (Botryococcus-like), and acanthomorphic spiny acritarch (also observed by Reyes et al.70 in the Montney) suggest that deposition occurred primarily in a shallow marine environment (e.g., Dorning71 and Tyson72). The relatively limited variability in the maceral assemblage in the seven analyzed samples suggests minor variations in the environment of deposition of the upper Middle Montney interval (Figure 9). The similarities in the lithology and sedimentary characteristics in the organic-rich mudstone facies also corroborate the minimal variability in the depositional setting.

The accumulation and preservation of organic matter in marine sedimentary environments is mainly driven by three processes: (1) primary productivity of organic matter, (2) preservation of the organic matter under oxygen-deficient water conditions, and (3) burial efficiency and dilution by non-organic particles.73−75 Additionally, mineralogical controls, such as clay content, can also impact organic matter preservation.76−78 The persistent presence of well-preserved primary organic matter (Type II kerogen) in the organic-rich mudstone facies is a direct indication that oxygen-deficient conditions in the bottom waters prevailed during the deposition of these facies. Also, the elevated TOC content, coupled with the excessive amounts of pyrite (likely very early, e.g., framboidal and cryptocrystalline within amorphinite) in the organic-rich mudstones, suggest deposition under oxygen-deficient conditions at the sediment surface.79 Furthermore, populations of small framboids in the mudstone facies (Figure 4) also suggest that during phytoplankton blooms anoxia may have developed in the water column.80,81

Even though global anoxia was prevalent during the Early Triassic,82,83 recent sulfur isotopic studies in the Montney outcrop equivalent Sulfur Mountain Formation demonstrated that Early Triassic sea waters were rich in sulfide.84 This is evidenced by the occurrence of pyrite crystals along cleavage planes of chlorite grains observed in SEM images (Figure 3D), which are interpreted to form during burial, confirming the availability of free sulfide in pore waters.85 More recently, Liseroudi et al.86 carried out isotopic and petrographic work investigating the sulfur cycle in the Montney Formation. They concluded that early H2S and pyrite originated from microbial processes (i.e., microbial sulfate reduction). Based on the significant amount of framboidal pyrite observed in the mudstone facies, two interpretations can be drawn. First, the oxygen levels below the sediment-water interface must have been low enough during the deposition of the Montney to promote bacterial sulfate reduction.87−89 Second, because microbial sulfate reduction requires primary organic matter as one of the essential reactants, considerable amounts of reactive organic matter (e.g., “blooming periods”) were potentially associated with the deposition of organic-rich mudstone facies.

Mineralogical (abundant mica and feldspars) and textural (poor sorting and massive appearance with respect to sedimentary structures) evidence infer that episodic suspended load discharge (i.e., hypopycnal flows) from ephemeral rivers during flood conditions was likely the principal mechanism for the deposition of the organic-rich mudstones. This is consistent with recent sedimentological studies that postulate that marine sediments of the Montney Formation were mainly sourced by ephemeral and perennial rivers flowing from the craton interior to the Pangean coastline.29,90−92 It is, therefore, plausible that higher production rates of organic matter could have resulted from nutrient-rich fluvially derived detritus.93−95 Alternatively, wind-blown sediments, which have been linked to the deposition of the Montney,27 could have potentially contributed to the initiation of phytoplankton blooms.96 Because the deposition of the Montney Formation occurred in middle paleolatitudes (30°N) with an arid climate,97 only minor amounts of terrigenous organic matter (i.e., vitrinite and inertinite) were possibly transported to the basin (as recorded in Table 3); therefore, transported nutrients are most likely related to inorganic nutrients (e.g., Fe, N).

According to Van der Zwaan and Jorissen98 the riverine inflow of nutrients can lead to important eutrophication (i.e., nutrient-induced increase in phytoplankton productivity) of shallow seas, which is otherwise mainly attributed to upwelling. Although paleoclimatic reconstructions of the WCSB have suggested seasonal coastal upwelling during the Early Triassic,99 the absence of siliceous microfossils in this core, which are typically associated with upwelling zones,100,101 refutes the occurrence of this process in the Middle Montney (i.e., Smithian) at least within this region of the WCSB. On the other hand, higher sedimentation rates associated with episodic depositional events (siltstone facies) potentially contributed to the rapid burial of the organic-rich mudstones.102−105 In addition, the clay-rich matrix and clay aggregates in the organic-rich mudstones likely shielded the organic matter from chemical alteration, preventing complete oxidation of the organic matter (Figures 3 and 4).106,107 The presence of primary organic matter with oil-generative potential in the organic-rich mudstone facies, as demonstrated in this study, combined with a similar organic matter type previously reported by other authors in a broader area of the basin,15,16 challenges the assumption that the entire Montney was deposited under oxic conditions,12,108 and therefore the hypothesis that present-day hydrocarbons within the Montney Formation are the result of oil migrated from external sources and then degraded in situ only. It is unlikely that the organic-rich mudstone facies and adjacent Montney siltstones were deposited under markedly different redox conditions (e.g., similar size distributions of pyrite framboids). In contrast, it can be suggested that the significant input of silt-sized detritus in the Montney contributed to the dilution of sedimentary organic matter within the siltstone facies.74 Based on these observations, we hypothesize that in the study area (1) intermittent dysoxia prevailed during the deposition of the upper Middle Montney (Late Smithian) and (2) the abundance of primary organic matter in the organic-rich mudstone beds is the result of the balance between periodic high productivity, rapid input of sediment burying, and preserving organic matter with lower dilution by siliciclastic material.

Origin of Kerogen in Organic-Rich Mudstone Facies

The kerogen composition of the mudstone facies of the Montney Formation is dominated by liptinite macerals, including amorphinite, liptodetrinite, alginite, and minor amounts of acritarch and sporinite (Table 3). Terrigenous macerals from the vitrinite and inertinite groups were recognized only in trace amounts (Table 3). It is worth noting that the described maceral association agrees with previous petrographic studies conducted in the Montney Formation.5,15,16,109

Amorphinite is consistently the main kerogen maceral recognized in the organic-rich mudstones (Tables 2 and 3). Due to the unstructured nature of the amorphinite, it is usually not possible to determine its origin with certainty, but, in marine environments, it is generally related to biological, or physicochemical degradation of bacterial biomass (e.g., green sulfur bacteria), and algal phytoplankton.54,110−112 Wehner and Hufnagel113 attributed the origin of amorphinite to marine snow,106,114,115 due to flocculent amorphous macroscopic components of the nearshore epipelagic water, which was inhabited by various kinds of microorganisms. Additionally, the morphology of amorphinite occasionally resembles algal forms, suggesting that these accumulations could partially result from the degradation of structured kerogen during early burial; such genetic association has been widely documented in organic-rich shales.58,116−119 Therefore, it can be hypothesized that the precursor of the diffuse and intergranular hebamorphinite could be the degradation and transformation of marine snow (i.e., bacterial biomass and phytoplankton), while the hebamorphinite lenses could have originated from algae that lost their structure during thermal maturation and/or degradation. Microbial consumption of algae and other organic matter by anaerobic bacteria during early diagenesis has been proposed as an important process in amorphinite formation111,120 The amorphinite in the organic-rich mudstones is often associated with abundant framboidal pyrite (Figures 6 and 7), pointing to microbial decomposition of organic matter under oxygen-deficient conditions.88,116,118,119

Further, marine-derived amorphinite in the organic-rich mudstones was identified as hebamorphinite, based on the lack of fluorescence under UV light.55 Typically, the amorphinite loses its VIS fluorescence at a vitrinite reflectance of about 0.8 to 0.9%54 and arguably, the Montney amorphinite may have originally been fluorescent (fluoramorphinite)55 but lost its VIS fluorescence during thermal maturation. However, the extensive organic petrology study by Beaton et al.15 suggested the presence of non-fluorescing amorphinite (hebamorphinite) in samples from a number of Montney wells across Alberta that are at different thermal maturity stages (Figure 1A). Nonetheless, as present-day amorphinite certainly does not entirely represent the originally deposited amorphous kerogen (as it is expected to be modified in the depositional environment or by extensive thermal maturation), the hypothesis of initially fluorescent amorphinite cannot be discarded.

The second most abundant maceral from the liptinite group is liptodetrinite (Table 3); it is believed to be derived from mechanical or biochemical degradation of other liptinite macerals, including alginite, sporinite, cutinite, and resinite.54,110 Based on the similarity of fluorescence properties of liptodetrinite to those of alginite macerals in the mudstone samples, it is probably mainly derived from Prasinophyte-like algae (e.g., marine chlorophytes).121,122 Liptodetrinite has been extensively recognized as one of the dominant liptinite macerals in organic petrology studies conducted on the Montney from different stratigraphic intervals across the basin.5,15 Unicellular alginite macerals (thin-walled Prasinophyte-like), which were identified in relatively minor amounts (<4 vol %; Table 3), are indicative of a shallow, open marine environment.74 The morphology of the Prasinophyte alginite identified in the mudstones is very similar to that reported by Beaton et al.15 in the Montney, with high amounts reported in immature samples (<0.6%Ro), and only minor to trace amounts in mature samples (0.8–1.0%Ro). This may indicate that Prasinophyte alginite have been wholly or partly converted to oil.123

Traces of coccoidal or colonial alginite (Botryococcus-like) were observed in only two samples (Table 3), and have been previously reported in the Montney.70,109 Coccoidal alginite are derived from unicellular green algae or cyanobacteria, which, after cell division, group together in a colonial aggregate within an extracellular envelope.121 Coccoidal alginite are typically indicative of fresh or brackish water depositional settings in shallow marine environments.68,74,124 Acanthomorphic acritarch (Veryachium- and Michystridium-like) were identified in minor proportions in some samples, with a predominance toward the upper part of the Middle Montney section (Figure 9; Table 3). Similarly, only minor to rare amounts of spiny acanthomorphic acritarch have been previously documented for the Montney Formation.5,15,70 Acritarch are fossilized organic cysts derived from unicellular green microalgae (i.e., phytoplankton),74 and have been related to dinoflagellates.125 The observed acritarch display spines that are strongly indicative of shallow water settings.126 Although in a more distal portion of the WCSB, the presence of abundant calcispheres in certain intervals of the Montney has been associated with dinoflagellate blooms,127−129 further work is deemed necessary to relate the spiny acritarch in the organic-rich mudstone beds to the Montney calcispheres. As an example, recent studies have linked the origin of calcispheres to acritarch in Devonian rocks.130

The only terrigenous land plant-derived maceral from the liptinite group observed in the mudstone facies is sporinite and occurs only in the shallowest samples in relatively minor amounts (9.0 vol %; Table 3). Sporinite within the upper part of the Smithian Middle Montney (∼3060 m; Figure 9) might potentially indicate a higher riverine input during this period,131 which may be related to a well-documented sea-level drop toward the late Smithian (marked by a third-order boundary);132 Alternatively, sporinites could have also been transported into the basin by winds.133,134

Finally, traces of terrigenous particles from vitrinite (telovitrinite and vitrodetrinite) and inertinite (semifusinite, inertodetrinite, and reworked graphite-like particles) maceral groups were recognized in most samples (Table 3). The chemical and botanical precursors of inertinite and vitrinite are generally the same, namely lignin from the cell walls of plants.54,135Figure 7 shows telovitrinite with a delicate cell structure, probably indicating an herbaceous vegetation (non-woody) source, which is consistent with an arid continent by the time of deposition of the Montney Formation.97 These macerals were possibly transported by ephemeral rivers during seasonal flooding or wind-blown, although the latter mechanism is believed to be a smaller contributor compared to rivers.136,137 In summary, kerogen within the organic-rich mudstone facies of the Montney Formation was mainly derived from bacterial biomass (e.g., green sulfur bacteria), with secondary contributions from phytoplanktonic algae (comprising cell walls of resistant biomacromolecules) that were incorporated into sediments from the overlying water column as marine snow (sensu Alldredge and Silver106). Previous biomarker studies by Hays et al.138 suggested the abundant and pervasive presence of green sulfur bacteria (Chlorobiaceae) throughout the Griesbachian-Dienerian Montney section in west-central Alberta.

The interpreted mechanisms for kerogen formation and preservation in the organic-rich mudstone facies of the Middle Montney are illustrated in Figure 16, based on previous models proposed by Stasiuk and Goodarzi139 and Chow et al.40 During “blooming periods” (i.e., periods of higher biological productivity), potentially driven by the episodic discharge of rivers (i.e., nutrient overloading), a low-oxygen zone is created within the water column (i.e., anoxia/suboxia driven by the demand of bacterial and/or phytoplankton algae) (Figure 16). The bacterial and algae remains are incorporated with the inorganic fraction of sediments in suspension (i.e., marine snow) and, subsequently, sink to the sediment-water interface and undergo degradation by anaerobic bacteria in the zone of sulfate reduction (Figure 16).

Depositional model for organic matter accumulation and preservation in the organic-rich mudstone facies of the Middle Montney Formation in the study area.

Below the sediment-water interface, in the aerobic zone, part of the phytoplankton and bacterial remains begin to oxidize by aerobic bacteria and/or oxygen-rich waters, and part of it remains unaltered (i.e., escaping oxidation). Rapid depletion of oxygen partially driven by the demand of aerobic bacteria inhibits the complete oxidation of organic matter. Then, the unaltered portion of bacterial and algae remains is subjected to degradation by anaerobic bacteria (BSR) in the zone of sulfate reduction resulting in the formation of amorphinite.140 Again, a part of it escapes anaerobic degradation and is preserved as kerogen (e.g., unicellular alginite). Previous studies investigating organic facies models for Devonian source rocks in the WCSB, have related the preservation of relatively large amounts of amorphinite to “blooming periods”.40,122,141

Origin of Solid Bitumen in the Organic-Rich Mudstone Beds

The term solid bitumen is used by organic petrographers to describe organic matter representing secondary products of hydrocarbon generation.38,142 Solid bitumen is amorphous in nature and conforms to the shape of the void or space it occupies (pore spaces, intergranular spaces, and fractures).143 In this study, solid bitumen constitutes up to 68 vol % of the total amount of microscopically defined organic matter in the mudstone facies (Table 3; Figure 9).

Solid bitumen is formed during different stages of the postburial, organic matter evolution continuum. As highlighted by Mastalerz et al.,143 determining the specific processes responsible for solid bitumen formation is not easy, mainly due to the similarity in properties of the final product, and because often, more than one process contributes to the final product. Due to the lack of specific geochemical analysis (e.g., stable carbon isotope composition of oil), we cannot determine the definitive timing and origin of the solid bitumen in the mudstone facies. However, considering the close spatial association of solid bitumen with amorphinite and framboidal- and cryptocrystalline-pyrite,142 it can be hypothesized that most of the solid bitumen in the mudstone facies formed as a consequence of the thermal alteration of amorphinite. Therefore, solid bitumen analyzed in this study can be classified as preoil solid bitumen.144 Geochemical work conducted by Cook145 demonstrated that bitumen and oil generation from amorphinite begins at lower maturity levels (as early as Ro 0.4%), compared to other liptinite macerals.141 Thus, a continued increase of thermal maturity (from Ro 0.4% to VRoeqv 1.03%) would have potentially led to the formation of later phases of solid bitumen; this could explain the observed low, medium, and high %Ro solid bitumen (Figure 8).

Among the few organic petrology studies conducted on the Montney Formation, Romero-Sarmiento et al.16 interpreted a similar origin for solid bitumen. Those authors analyzed several samples from Montney wells located across different maturity levels (Ro 0.43–1.77%) in Alberta (Figure 1). They found that immature to early mature samples contained abundant liptinite macerals (alginite), whereas mature samples contained abundant solid bitumen. The authors attributed the generation of solid bitumen to primary organic matter within the rocks (i.e., liptinite macerals). The petrographic analysis from Beaton et al.15 conducted on more than 200 Montney samples also suggested that the solid bitumen in the Montney was generated from pervasive amorphinite. Additionally, Chalmers and Bustin146 reported dominant pyrobitumen with minor amounts of primary kerogen in overmature samples (VRoeqv 2.06%) collected from Montney wells in British Columbia (Figure 1), and suggested that most of the primary kerogen was converted to oil and bitumen within the reservoir. The Cambrian Alum Shale also serves as an example of significant transformations of kerogen macerals into solid bitumen as a result of petroleum generation. Where immature, the organic matter in the Alum Shale is dominated by alginite, zooclasts and bitumen, and where overmature, a network of finely dispersed solid bitumen is the principal organic component, and alginite macerals are absent.147

Our interpretations, similar to Romero-Sarmiento et al.,16 are contrary to observations from the work conducted by Sanei et al.12 and Wood et al.13,148 using mature Montney samples from wells primarily located in British Columbia (Figure 1). In the latter studies, the authors did not report kerogen in any amount and suggested that solid bitumen was originated from the degradation of oil (unknown source) that migrated into the Montney reservoirs. We consider that the contrast in interpretations could be partially due to (1) differences in the thermal maturity of the analyzed samples (i.e., peak oil versus late oil-wet gas) and (2) the lithology of the analyzed samples (i.e., mudstone versus siltstones).

Geochemical Comparison of Adjacent Mudstone/Siltstone Beds

Results from GC-MS analyses were used to determine the biomarker composition of rock extracts from two sets of adjacent mudstone/siltstone beds (Table 4; Figures 11–15). The main goal was to determine whether a genetic relationship exists between the rock extracts, which could support the case for the short-distance migration of hydrocarbons from the organic-rich mudstone into adjacent siltstones. The genetic relationship was investigated by comparing selected biomarker ratios using cross-plots and ternary plots.149,150

The reliability of a genetic relationship evaluation increases when the thermal maturity of the oil or rock extracts falls within the same range.150 When cross-plotting some of the biomarker ratios, the two sets of samples (i.e., mudstone/siltstone) show a fair cluster, implying a very close maturity ranking (Figures 12 and 13). For example, the carbon preference index (CPI),151,152 the sterane isomerization parameters C29αααS/(S + R) and C29ββ/(ββ + αα),153 the Ts/(Ts + Tm),154−156 the ratio of C27 diasterane/regular sterane,155 and the 29Ts/(29Ts + norhopane)157,158 parameter are considered effective maturity indicators. The Methylphenanthrene Index (MPI) and the calculated vitrinite reflectance values (Rc) are also identical for the two sets of samples (Table 4). The fact that the thermal maturity levels of the mudstone and siltstone extracts are very similar may suggest that the bitumen in the siltstone has not migrated long distances.159

Various biomarker ratios from the terpane, hopane, and sterane groups were also used to compare the origin of the source rocks for the mudstones and siltstones. Overall, the four rock extracts plot very closely (Figures 12 and 13), indicating a potential genetic relationship. For example, a C26/C25 TT ratio lower than 1.0,160,161 and moderate values (∼0.2) of the C31R/C30H ratio150 consistently indicate marine source rocks. Additionally, the CPI values around 1.0 may indicate a significant contribution of marine organic matter derived from planktonic algae and bacteria,68 especially where the terrigenous input is negligible, as is the case of the Montney Formation. The range in pristane/phytane ratios (0.7–1.2; Table 4) suggests a marine depositional environment under anoxic to suboxic conditions.162−164 However, the utility of this indicator is challenged by the fact that both compounds can be derived from multiple sources.165

Ternary plots of the relative amounts of C27, C28, and C29 steranes largely reflect original source input.150 The relative abundance of C27:C28:C29 regular steranes and diasteranes is almost identical for the two mudstone/siltstone pairs (Figure 14). Moreover, the dominance of C27 sterols suggests that the organic matter is of planktonic or algae origin.166 Also, significantly higher tricyclic terpanes compared to hopanes are clearly seen for both sets of samples and could potentially suggest a higher contribution of unicellular alginite and bacterial organic matter.167−169 Similarly, relatively low hopane/sterane ratios are characteristic of marine organic matter with major contributions of algal marine input.150,170 Additionally, both sets of samples display relatively low values for the C24/C23 and C22/C21 tricyclic terpane ratios, as well as moderate C29/C30 hopane ratios (<1.0) (Figure 13), suggesting that the organic matter for both extracts is derived from clay-rich marine source rocks.150,171,172

In terms of the age of the source rock for bitumen, two age-specific parameters were analyzed for this study. First, the extended tricyclic terpane ratio, ETR = (C28TT + C29TT)/Ts is useful to distinguish Triassic (ETR ≥ 2.0) from Jurassic oils.173 The ETR ratio for the samples analyzed lies between 5.1 and 6.1, which is relatively high, and this suggests a Triassic age (Table 4; Figure 12). Second, the C28/C29 ββ-steranes is another useful parameter to determine the age of the source rock for oils and bitumen.174 This ratio tends to increase with decreasing geological age. The analysis of the samples under study revealed a C28/C29 ββ-steranes consistent ratio, with a narrow range of 0.7 to 0.8. and suggests an age span from the Upper Paleozoic to the Lower Jurassic for the bitumen (Table 4; Figure 12). Based on these findings, it can be inferred that the bitumen samples collected from mudstone and siltstones are most likely linked to the Triassic period in terms of source rock age.

The biomarker fingerprint of the bitumen in siltstone samples suggests that the source rocks that generated the bitumen originated under similar conditions (i.e., depositional environment and organic matter precursors) (Figures 12–14). This suggests that the rock extracts from adjacent siltstone and mudstone beds are likely genetically related. Supporting this interpretation are the three families of petroleum fluid inclusions observed in both the siltstone and mudstone adjacent beds of seven block samples (Figure 10). This is expected as the fluorescence properties (e.g., lambda max) of petroleum inclusions reflect their chemical composition [Stasiuk and Snowdon62 and references therein]. Considering the lack of petrographic evidence of kerogen in the siltstone facies and the moderate level of thermal maturity, the solid bitumen in these facies appears most likely to be migrated. In fact, the solid bitumen found within intergranular pores near the mudstone/siltstone contact is consistent with a migrated solid bitumen (Figures 6 and 7).14 Furthermore, the geochemical evaluation of the organic-rich mudstones suggests they are an effective source rock that already generated hydrocarbons (VRoeqv ≈ 1.03%), which were partly retained in the sample (i.e., solid bitumen) and partly migrated into adjacent siltstone beds. Examples of short-distance petroleum migration from mudrock sources to adjacent reservoir facies have been documented by Jubb et al.175 and Hackely et al.176 in the Stonewall and Wolfcamp Formations, respectively.

Whether the organic-rich mudstones of the Montney Formation are the major or only source of the hydrocarbons in the adjacent siltstones is beyond the scope of the present study. However, it is worth mentioning that previous work has also recognized the potential of hydrocarbon generation of the Montney Formation in the WCSB.5,7,9

The percentage of kerogen macerals components ranges from 32 to 74 (vol %), whereas the secondary solid bitumen ranges from 26 to 68 (vol %).

Microbial decomposition of organic matter under oxygen-deficient conditions is suggested as an important mechanism for the formation of kerogen in organic-rich mudstones. The preservation of primary organic matter in the Montney organic-rich mudstones is interpreted to be caused by a combination of factors, including periodic high productivity (induced by nutrient-rich river flows or wind-blown), rapid input of clay-rich sediments to bury and preserve organic matter, and low dilution by silt-sized siliciclastic material.
