Abstract
Multi-focus color image fusion refers to integrating multiple partially focused color images to create a single all-in-focus color image. However, existing methods struggle with complex real-world scenarios due to limitations in handling color information and intricate textures. To address these challenges, this paper proposes a quaternion multi-focus color image fusion framework to perform high-quality color image fusion completely in the quaternion domain. This framework introduces 1) a quaternion sparse decomposition model to jointly learn fine-scale image details and structure information of color images in an iterative fashion for high-precision focus detection, 2) a quaternion base-detail fusion strategy to individually fuse base-scale and detail-scale results across multiple color images for preserving structure and detail information, and 3) a quaternion structural similarity refinement strategy to adaptively select optimal patches from initial fusion results and obtain the final fused result for preserving fine details and ensuring spatially consistent outputs. Extensive experiments demonstrate that the proposed framework outperforms state-of-the-art methods.



I
Introduction

A single color image often fails to capture all objects in focus due to optical limitations of cameras
[
1
]
. When imaging scenes with objects at varying distances, only regions within the focal plane are focused while areas outside this plane exhibit defocus blur. To circumvent this limitation, multi-focus color image fusion (MCIF) techniques integrate multiple partially focused color images of the same scene using focus detection and adaptive fusion strategies to obtain an all-in-focus color image
[
2
]
. This technology has numerous applications across diverse domains including digital photography, preprocessing operations
[
3
]
, and target recognition
[
4
,
5
]
.

Existing MCIF methods can be broadly categorized into deep learning-based and conventional methods.
Currently, deep learning-based methods have extensively promoted the development of MCIF to enhance the ability of learning multi-scale features to distinguish focused pixels
[
6
,
7
]
. Since it is impractical to obtain perfect ground truth (
i
.
e
., all-in-focus color images) for real-world scenes
[
8
]
, many deep learning-based works heavily rely on synthetic training data with a large-scale training process. Unlike deep learning-based MCIF methods, conventional methods only depend on human prior knowledge to distinguish focused objects without the training phase
[
9
]
.

Existing conventional MCIF methods often treat color image fusion as a direct extension of multi-focus grayscale image fusion techniques by channel-wise processing
[
10
]
or grayscale conversion
[
11
]
. For channel-wise processing, these MCIF methods simply treat color channels as three independent grayscale images and directly apply image fusion techniques to each channel to obtain the fused color image.
For instance, Yang et al.
[
10
]
extended sparse coding techniques to MCIF by separately measuring focus levels in each channel and fusing sparse coefficients channel-by-channel. This may introduce spectral-spatial inconsistencies and color distortions in the fused color images
[
12
,
13
]
.

For grayscale conversion, existing conventional MCIF methods first convert the input color images into grayscale images and perform intensity-driven focus detection
[
14
,
11
]
to derive focus maps from grayscale inputs to guide the fusion of original color channels
[
15
]
. For instance,
[
16
]
averaged color channels into a grayscale image and obtained focus maps by multi-scale morphological filtering.
[
17
]
transformed the color image into the YUV color space and detected focus levels employing the zero crossing and canny edge detectors.
Although these methods avoid explicit channel separation, they fail to detect multi-channel sharpness variations among the input color images and may produce inaccurate focus maps under complex real-world scenarios. This leads to ghosting artifacts or blurred boundaries in the fused results
[
15
,
13
]
. Therefore, it remains a challenge to perform high-quality color image fusion in real-world applications. The limitations of conventional MCIF methods lie mainly in two key challenges: (1) small-scale focus detection failure: existing techniques struggle to distinguish and retain small focused regions within images containing extensive defocused areas based on grayscale focus detection
[
4
]
; (2) boundary artifacts: focus maps derived from intensity-based or edge-based focus detection often produce inaccurate results with visual artifacts introduced particularly in uncertain boundary regions (between focused and defocused areas) and low-gradient regions due to defocus spread effects
[
15
,
18
]
.

Recently, a quaternion higher-order singular value decomposition (QHOSVD) model
[
19
]
was developed to perform quaternion-domain multi-focus color image fusion by fully considering highly-correlated color information. QHOSVD treats three color channels as a unified entity of three imaginary parts in a pure quaternion matrix using quaternion representation
[
20
,
21
]
and extends this representation into a third-order quaternion tensor.
This effectively preserves cross-channel relationships during color image processing
[
22
,
23
,
24
]
. Taking advantage of higher-order singular value decomposition in the quaternion domain, QHOSVD extracted focus levels corresponding to each source color image and employed a simple patch-wise fusion strategy to ensure spatial consistency and color fidelity
[
19
]
.
However, QHOSVD still fails in focus information preservation and artifact suppression in complex real-world scenarios due to defocus spread effects
[
18
]
.

To address these issues, we propose a quaternion multi-focus color image fusion (QMCIF) framework to produce high-quality fused color images.
Our main contributions are presented as follows:

•

We propose a QMCIF framework to perform high-quality color image fusion completely in the quaternion domain. QMCIF is flexibly extended from fusing only two color images at a time into fusing multiple color images simultaneously under various challenging scenarios.

•

To effectively measure the focus level in both small focused areas and uncertain boundary regions, QMCIF introduces a quaternion sparse decomposition model to jointly learn fine-scale image details and structure information of color images in an iterative fashion.

•

To preserve color structure and detail information, QMCIF introduces a patch-wise quaternion base-detail fusion strategy to fuse multiple partially focused color images in detail-scale and base-scale individually.

•

To balance the trade-off between focus information preservation and artifact removal, QMCIF further introduces a quaternion structural similarity refinement strategy to flexibly form the final fused results.

•

Extensive experiments on various datasets demonstrate that QMCIF outperforms the state-of-the-art methods.

III
Proposed Framework

This section introduces our quaternion multi-focus color image fusion (QMCIF) framework. Section A provides an overview of our QMCIF framework. Section B introduces our quaternion sparse decomposition model and its optimization in detail. Section C presents our quaternion base-detail fusion strategy. Section D presents our quaternion structural similarity refinement strategy.
Section E extends our framework to multiple-color image fusion.

III-A

Overview

Our QMCIF framework is a patch-wise image fusion framework as shown in Fig.
1
. Different from most existing works that perform color image fusion independently, our framework can handle all color channels simultaneously within a single fusion process. We first encode the input color images into their quaternion representations and then use the quaternion sparse decomposition model to jointly learn the optimal coefficient matrix and detail layer for each input image for high-precision focus detection. We employ the quaternion base-detail fusion strategy to fuse the color images in base and detail scales. In the detail-scale fusion process, the two-directional gradients of the detail layers are used to estimate the focus level of each input quaternion representation and select focused patches, generating a detail-scale fused result. In the base-scale fusion process, coefficient matrices are combined with the gradients of the corresponding detail layers to assess the focus level of each input quaternion representation and select focused patches to form the base-scale fused result.
To further improve the fusion quality, we design a quaternion structural similarity refinement strategy to merge these fused results patch-by-patch to produce the final fused result using a quaternion weighted structural similarity index measure.

III-C

Quaternion base-detail fusion strategy

This subsection proposes a quaternion base-detail fusion (QBDF) strategy to achieve high-quality color image fusion in base and detail scales. Fig.
3
illustrates our QBDF strategy. Given quaternion representations
{\{\dot{\bm{\mathrm{I}}}^{(i)}\}}_{i=1}^{2}

\in\mathbbm{H}^{2\times M\times N}
of two input color images, our QBDF strategy first uses the QSD model to learn their detail-scale layers
{\{\dot{\bm{\mathrm{D}}}^{(i)}\}}_{i=1}^{2}
and coefficient matrices
{\{\dot{\bm{\mathrm{Z}}}^{(i)}\}}_{i=1}^{2}
, and then performs two-scale image fusion.

To fully utilize the complementary features of coefficient matrices and detail-scale layers, QBDF strategy introduces a two-scale fusion process to obtain base-scale and detail-scale fusion results at the patch level. The patch-wise fusion rule is adopted to reduce the sensitivity to the undetermined pixels directly generated by focus detection. First, we define two-scale focus measures as below.

\displaystyle l_{b,j}=\mathop{\arg\max}_{i}\ \ \sum_{t=1}^{2}{\|\nabla_{t}\dot{\bm{\mathrm{D}}}{{}^{(i)}_{j}}\|}_{1}+\theta{\|\dot{\bm{\mathrm{Z}}}_{j}^{(i)}\|}_{2},

(13)

\displaystyle l_{d,j}=\mathop{\arg\max}_{i}\ \ \sum_{t=1}^{2}{\|\nabla_{t}\dot{\bm{\mathrm{D}}}{{}^{(i)}_{j}}\|}_{1},

(14)

where
l_{b}
and
l_{d}
denote the patch-wise focus maps of base-scale and detail-scale fused results respectively,
j
defines the center spatial location of each patch and parameter
\theta
is here set to 1 by default.
\dot{\bm{\mathrm{D}}}{{}^{(i)}_{j}}
represents the
j^{th}
patch of
\dot{\bm{\mathrm{D}}}^{(i)}
and
\dot{\bm{\mathrm{Z}}}{{}^{(i)}_{j}}
corresponds to the
j^{th}
column vector of
\dot{\bm{\mathrm{Z}}}^{(i)}
. The detail-scale focus measure uses the two-directional gradients of the detail-scale layers. The gradients that intuitively represent variations in the quaternion pixels can substantially reflect the focus levels of color images. Based on the greater amplitude of Eq. (
14
),
l_{d,j}
is assigned with the corresponding
i
in
j^{th}

To address this issue, the base-scale focus measure integrates the gradients of the detail layers with their corresponding coefficient matrices. III-D

Quaternion structural similarity refinement strategy

To obtain a high-quality fused result, we introduce a quaternion structural similarity refinement (QSSR) strategy to refine the base-scale and detail-scale fused patches of
{\{\dot{\bm{\mathrm{P}}}^{(i)}\}}_{i=1}^{2}
using a selection rule with a maximum quaternion image structural similarity value. We assume that the patch to be selected should be most similar to the corresponding region of the source image with the largest focus information. Therefore, we propose a quaternion weighted structural similarity (QWSSIM) strategy to refine the distorted patches that are wrongly detected as focused ones based on the theory of Definition
5
. QWSSIM is defined in the following equation.

\displaystyle QWSSIM({\dot{\bm{\mathrm{P}}}}_{j}^{(1)})=\sum_{i=1}^{2}{\tau}_{i,j}QSSIM({\dot{\bm{\mathrm{P}}}}_{j}^{(1)},{\dot{\bm{\mathrm{I}}}}_{j}^{(i)}),

(17)

\displaystyle QWSSIM({\dot{\bm{\mathrm{P}}}}_{j}^{(2)})=\sum_{i=1}^{2}{\tau}_{i,j}QSSIM({\dot{\bm{\mathrm{P}}}}_{j}^{(2)},{\dot{\bm{\mathrm{I}}}}_{j}^{(i)}),

where
j\in[1,P]
denotes the center
spatial location of each patch where
P
denotes the number of overlapping patches.
{\tau}_{i,j}
is an assigned weight to
j^{th}
quaternion patch of
i^{th}
input quaternion representation. The patches selected from the two-scale fused results by our QBDF strategy should be quite similar to the corresponding focused patches of
{\{\dot{\bm{\mathrm{I}}}^{(i)}\}}^{2}_{i=1}
. In other words, each local quaternion patch of the final fused result has a maximum QWSSIM value
with the corresponding region of
{\{\dot{\bm{\mathrm{I}}}\}}^{2}_{i=1}
.
{\tau}_{i,j}
is significantly larger when the corresponding region of
{\dot{\bm{\mathrm{I}}}}^{(i)}
is focused. Fig.
3
illustrates the flowchart of our QSSR strategy. Our QSSR strategy further compares two-scale fused results with the input quaternion representations patch-by-patch to assess the patch-wise structural similarity using our QWSSIM measure. Patches with higher QWSSIM values are selected to form the final fused result.

To adaptively compute
{\tau}_{i,j}
, we utilize the detail-scale measure to generate each weight map with the size of
n\times P
where n depends on the number of inputs. The detail-scale measure is enhanced using a Laplacian function
\phi(x)=1-e^{-\frac{x}{\gamma}}
where
\gamma
is
=0.2
by default. Then the weight map is formulated in the following equation.

{\tau}_{i,j}=\frac{\phi({({\sum}_{t=1}^{2}{\|\nabla_{t}\dot{\bm{\mathrm{D}}}{{}^{(i)}_{j}}\|}{{}_{1}})})}{\sum_{i}\phi(({\sum}_{t=1}^{2}{\|\nabla_{t}\dot{\bm{\mathrm{D}}}{{}^{(i)}_{j}}\|}{{}_{1}}))}.

(18)

We apply a maximum selection rule based on the QWSSIM measure at the patch level.
It effectively corrects defocused regions in the two-scale fused images. Additionally, this strategy eliminates visual artifacts caused by inaccurate focus maps. The selection rule is defined as follows.

{\bm{\mathrm{W}}}_{j}=\left\{\begin{aligned} &1,&QWSSIM({\dot{\bm{\mathrm{P}}}}_{j}^{(1)})>QWSSIM({\dot{\bm{\mathrm{P}}}}_{j}^{(2)})\\
&0,&QWSSIM({\dot{\bm{\mathrm{P}}}}_{j}^{(1)})\leq QWSSIM({\dot{\bm{\mathrm{P}}}}_{j}^{(2)}).\end{aligned}\right.

(19)

The weight matrix
\bm{\mathrm{W}}
denotes a refined patch-wise focus map. When
{\bm{\mathrm{W}}}_{j}=1
, we select the
j^{th}
patch from
{\dot{\bm{\mathrm{P}}}}^{(1)}
. Otherwise, we choose the
j^{th}
patch from
{\dot{\bm{\mathrm{P}}}}^{(2)}
. The final fused result is obtained at the patch level as below:

\dot{\bm{\mathrm{F}}}_{j}=\left\{\begin{aligned} &{\dot{\bm{\mathrm{P}}}}_{j}^{(1)},&{\bm{\mathrm{W}}}_{j}=1\\
&{\dot{\bm{\mathrm{P}}}}_{j}^{(2)},&{\bm{\mathrm{W}}}_{j}=0.\end{aligned}\right.

(20)

The patches are aggregated to form a quaternion fused result
{\dot{\bm{\mathrm{F}}}}
. After the inverse transformation from the quaternion domain into the real domain, we obtain a fused color image
\bm{\mathrm{F}}

\in\mathbbm{R}^{M\times N\times 3}
.

V
Conclusion

We proposed a quaternion multi-focus color image fusion framework to perform high-quality color image fusion completely in the quaternion domain. This framework is flexible to be extended from fusing two color images at a time into fusing multiple color images simultaneously under complex challenging scenarios. This framework proposed a quaternion sparse decomposition model for high-precision focus detection of color images. This model jointly learns a low-rank structured coefficient matrix and a detail-scale layer. They both were utilized to estimate the focus level of the image. To solve the optimization problem of the QSD model, we developed an iterative algorithm under the framework of the quaternion alternating direction methods of multipliers. A quaternion base-detail fusion strategy was also introduced for high-quality base-scale and detail-scale color image fusion individually. A quaternion structural similarity strategy was further introduced to balance
the trade-off between focus information preservation and artifact removal. Our framework fuses two-scale quaternion matrices and compares them with the input quaternion representations to adaptively select the focused patches with a higher QWSSIM value to form the final fused results. Extensive experiment
results have shown that our framework outperforms the state-of-the-art methods in terms of focus information preservation and artifact removal under real-world challenging scenarios.
