Distinct learner profiles of attitudes toward biostatistics in medical education: a latent profile analysis
Authors: Fulden Cantas Turkis, Bugra Varol
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBiostatistics is essential for interpreting evidence and clinical decision-making, yet medical learners may value it while experiencing anxiety, low enjoyment, and perceived difficulty. Average scores may fail to capture distinct attitude patterns. This study aimed to identify latent profiles of attitudes toward biostatistics among first-year medical students and medical residents and compare their sociodemographic and academic characteristics.MethodsThis cross-sectional study included 193 participants: 132 first-year medical students and 61 medical residents. Attitudes were assessed using the 33-item Statistics Attitude Scale, with responses framed in relation to biostatistics in medical education. Item-level responses were treated as continuous indicators in latent profile analysis. Models with one to seven profiles were estimated using an equal-variance, zero-covariance parameterisation. Model selection considered fit indices, profile size, and interpretability. Characteristics were compared across profiles.ResultsA five-profile solution was retained because it had the lowest Bayesian information criterion, high classification quality (entropy = 0.970), adequate profile sizes, and clear interpretation. The six- and seven-profile solutions included profiles with only one participant. The retained profiles were Low-Engagement and Anxious (24.4%), Value-Aware but Anxious (28.0%), Moderately Receptive (18.7%), Highly Disengaged and Anxious (10.9%), and Highly Receptive and Confident (18.1%). All five attitude dimensions differed across profiles (all p < 0.001), with the largest between-profile difference observed for Relation of Statistics to Professional Life (ε² = 0.821). The Value-Aware but Anxious profile combined high professional relevance and importance with greater anxiety, lower enjoyment, and greater perceived difficulty. Educational level also differed across profiles (p < 0.001; Cramér’s V = 0.449): first-year students were more common in three profiles, whereas residents were more common in the Value-Aware but Anxious and Highly Receptive and Confident profiles.ConclusionsAttitudes toward biostatistics among medical learners varied and could not be represented on a single scale from negative to positive. Distinguishing learners who valued biostatistics but remained anxious from those showing low engagement may help inform clinically relevant and tailored educational support. The profiles should be regarded as cross-sectional and preliminary rather than as fixed learner types.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-026-10124-x.

Background

Modern clinical practice increasingly requires physicians to interpret evidence expressed through probabilities, effect estimates, uncertainty, and risk. Physicians therefore require sufficient statistical understanding not only to conduct research, but also to interpret diagnostic tests, evaluate treatment effects, appraise clinical guidelines, communicate risk, and judge whether published findings are applicable to individual patients. Practising doctors have reported that they often failed to recognise the relevance of statistics during undergraduate education but subsequently found it important in clinical practice and research [1]. Similarly, medical students, residents, and faculty have largely acknowledged the importance of biostatistics for evidence-based medicine, while comparatively few have considered their training adequate or felt confident in evaluating statistical methods or conducting analyses independently [2]. Biostatistics education must therefore address not only the acquisition of technical knowledge, but also learners’ willingness and confidence to engage with statistical reasoning throughout their professional development.

A persistent challenge is that recognising the importance of biostatistics does not necessarily translate into confidence, enjoyment, or a perception that the subject is manageable. Attitudes toward statistics are multidimensional and may encompass perceived professional relevance, anxiety or fear, enjoyment, perceived importance, and perceived difficulty [3]. Graduate-entry medical students have been shown to appreciate the professional relevance of statistics and to be willing to invest effort in learning it, while simultaneously reporting insecurity, stress, frustration, and a tendency to perceive the subject as difficult [4]. Medical postgraduates have similarly expressed generally favourable attitudes while continuing to regard statistics as particularly difficult [5]. Among medical undergraduates, favourable perceptions of value and cognitive competence have also coexisted with less favourable perceptions of difficulty [6]. These findings suggest that attitudes toward biostatistics cannot be adequately represented by a single continuum from negative to positive: learners may value the subject professionally while remaining anxious, unconfident, or disengaged from the learning process.

Attitudes are educationally relevant because they have been associated with achievement, engagement, and learners’ readiness to apply statistical knowledge; however, they are neither uniformly changed by instruction nor necessarily sustained over time. More favourable attitude dimensions have been positively associated with course achievement among medical undergraduate and postgraduate students [5, 6]. Nevertheless, studies evaluating change following instruction have produced contrasting results. A compulsory biostatistics course for dentistry students was followed by improved attitude scores, although examination performance did not show a corresponding improvement [7]. In contrast, medical postgraduates showed negative changes in affect, perceived cognitive competence, and perceived difficulty after completing a systematic statistics course [5]. Among doctoral research scholars, intensive coursework produced substantial immediate improvements in both knowledge and attitudes, but these gains declined two to three years later, particularly for attitudes [8]. Collectively, these findings indicate that exposure to biostatistics does not produce a uniform or enduring response and that average pre-course and post-course changes may conceal important differences between learners.

Most research on attitudes toward biostatistics in medical education has used variable-centred approaches, including total or subscale means, correlations with achievement, comparisons between demographic groups, and average changes following instruction. These approaches are useful for identifying which attitude dimensions are relatively favourable or unfavourable at the group level, but they do not show how these dimensions combine within individual learners. For example, a learner who recognises the professional value of biostatistics but remains anxious and perceives it as difficult may require different educational support from a learner who shows low perceived value, limited enjoyment, and broader disengagement. Such configurations may be obscured when attitude dimensions are examined separately or combined into an overall score.

Person-centred methods complement rather than replace variable-centred approaches by examining how multiple attitude dimensions co-occur within learners, rather than only how the dimensions relate to one another across a sample [9]. Using multilevel latent class analysis, Mutz and Daniel identified four distinct attitude types toward research methods and statistics among psychology students and demonstrated that these types differed in academic experiences and educational outcomes [10]. However, that study addressed general attitudes toward research methods and statistics in psychology education rather than attitudes toward biostatistics in medical education. Profile-based evidence on attitudes toward biostatistics among medical learners remains limited. In particular, it is unclear whether professional relevance, perceived importance, anxiety, enjoyment, and perceived difficulty form qualitatively distinct configurations within the same learner population, or whether the distribution of such configurations differs between learners at early undergraduate and postgraduate stages of medical education. Consequently, the learner heterogeneity most relevant to differentiated educational support remains insufficiently characterised.

Accordingly, this study used item-level latent profile analysis to identify distinct configurations of attitudes toward biostatistics among first-year medical students and medical residents. These groups represent different stages of medical education and differ in their proximity to formal undergraduate instruction, clinical experience, and previous opportunities to engage with research; however, their comparison was intended to characterise cross-sectional heterogeneity rather than longitudinal development. The study further aimed to describe the identified profiles across the original attitude dimensions and to compare their sociodemographic and academic characteristics. By moving beyond average scores, the study sought to provide a more differentiated account of how medical learners relate to biostatistics and to establish an empirical basis for future evaluation of appropriately targeted, but not rigidly profile-based, educational support.

Methods

Study design and setting

This cross-sectional study examined attitudes toward biostatistics among first-year medical students at the Faculty of Medicine of (blinded for peer review) University and medical residents undergoing postgraduate specialty training at (blinded for peer review) Training and Research Hospital, the university’s affiliated tertiary teaching hospital. Data were collected between April and June 2025 using a self-administered questionnaire comprising a sociodemographic and academic information form and the Statistics Attitude Scale. A total of 193 participants were included, comprising 132 first-year medical students (68.39%) and 61 medical residents (31.61%).

All first-year medical students were enrolled in the same mandatory biostatistics course during the 2024–2025 academic year. The course was delivered by the same instructor, who was also the corresponding author, using a standardized curriculum. The course covered introductory biostatistics, data collection and sampling methods, descriptive statistics, tabular and graphical presentation of data, research designs, hypothesis testing, including t-tests, analysis of variance and chi-square tests, nonparametric tests, linear correlation and regression, logistic regression, receiver operating characteristic curve analysis, survival analysis, interpretation of statistical findings in scientific publications, and hands-on use of SPSS software. By the time of data collection, the first-year medical students had completed their biostatistics instruction and related examinations across three curricular blocks delivered between September 2024 and the end of March 2025. Data were collected during the fourth curricular block, when no further biostatistics teaching was scheduled.

The medical residents had completed their undergraduate biostatistics education at different medical schools in Türkiye and had therefore been taught by different instructors. Although the specific course content and instructional approaches may have varied across institutions, undergraduate medical education programmes in Türkiye are guided by a national core curriculum.

Participants and recruitment

The target population comprised 270 first-year medical students and 404 medical residents who were enrolled or employed at the study institution during the data-collection period. A voluntary response sampling strategy was used, and the survey link was distributed via the existing WhatsApp groups of eligible first-year medical students and medical residents. All eligible individuals who voluntarily submitted a completed questionnaire were included in the study.

Eligibility criteria were enrolment as a first-year medical student during the 2024–2025 academic year or active employment as a medical resident during the data-collection period. Questionnaires showing an invariant response pattern across all 33 scale items were prespecified for exclusion as a potential indicator of inadequate response quality. No questionnaire met this exclusion criterion; therefore, all submitted questionnaires were retained. Because all questionnaire items were set as mandatory in Google Forms, there were no incomplete responses.

Data were collected anonymously through Google Forms. No names, student identification numbers, personnel numbers, or other directly identifying information were recorded. Participants were informed that participation was voluntary and that they could withdraw at any time before submitting the questionnaire.

To minimise the possibility that students might perceive participation as related to academic evaluation, data collection began only after completion of the biostatistics instruction and all related examinations, during a curricular block in which no further biostatistics teaching was scheduled. The survey invitation was not distributed by the biostatistics instructor; instead, another faculty member shared the survey link with the student group during a separate course and invited participation. The corresponding author had no access to information that could identify individual respondents.

At the study institution, formal biostatistics instruction is confined to the first year of medical education, and no additional course explicitly designated as biostatistics is provided in subsequent years. In the second year, students participate in a Special Study Module, in which small groups are assigned to faculty supervisors. Depending on the supervisor and the nature of the assigned project, some groups may participate in research or publication activities requiring the use of biostatistical analyses. However, such exposure is incidental and not standardised across students and therefore does not constitute systematic biostatistics instruction.

No a priori sample-size calculation was performed because the study sought to recruit all accessible and eligible volunteers within the defined target population. Because the number of individuals who actually received or viewed the survey link could not be determined, separate response rates for first-year medical students and medical residents could not be calculated.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the Health Sciences Ethics Committee of (blinded for peer review) University (Protocol No. 240126, Decision No. 150; December 4, 2024). The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Participation was voluntary, and all participants provided electronic informed consent before taking part in the study.

Data collection instruments

Data were collected using a questionnaire consisting of two sections.

Sociodemographic and academic information form

The sociodemographic and academic information form collected data on participants’ educational level (first-year medical student or medical resident), gender, age, language of instruction, and type of accommodation. Type of high school attended was categorized as either a selective science-focused high school, characterized by a competitive admission process and an intensive science and mathematics curriculum, or a general academic high school, which follows a standard academic curriculum without a specific science focus.

Additional variables included whether studying medicine had been the participant’s own choice, regret about studying medicine, self-reported interest in mathematics during high school, self-rated academic motivation, and self-assessed computer skills. Self-reported interest in mathematics during high school, academic motivation, and computer skills were each rated on a five-point scale ranging from 1 to 5, with higher scores indicating greater interest, motivation, or self-perceived skill.

Statistics attitude scale

Attitudes toward biostatistics in the context of medical education were assessed using the Statistics Attitude Scale, originally developed and validated in Turkish by Yaşar (2014) [3]. The original Turkish version was administered to all participants. Before completing the scale, participants were instructed to interpret and answer all items in relation to biostatistics as taught and encountered in medical education, rather than statistics in a general or non-medical context. An English translation of the scale items was prepared by the authors for reporting purposes and is provided in Supplementary Table S3.

The scale comprises 33 items rated on a nine-point Likert-type response format ranging from 1, “I definitely do not agree,” to 9, “I definitely agree.” It includes five subscales: Relation of Statistics to Professional Life, Statistical Anxiety-Fear, Enjoying Statistics, Importance of Statistics, and Perceived Statistical Difficulty. In the original validation study, Cronbach’s α coefficients were 0.862, 0.837, 0.839, 0.749, and 0.810 for these subscales, respectively, and 0.927 for the overall scale. Internal consistency in the present sample was assessed using Cronbach’s α for each subscale and for the overall scale.

Data preparation

Scoring of the statistics attitude scale

All 33 items of the Statistics Attitude Scale were retained for analysis in accordance with the original structure of the instrument. Item responses were recorded on a nine-point Likert-type scale ranging from 1 to 9. The original item-level responses, rather than categorized or aggregated response formats, were used as continuous indicators in the latent profile analysis to preserve the full variability of the data.

For descriptive interpretation of the resulting profiles, scores for the five original subscales - Relation of Statistics to Professional Life, Statistical Anxiety-Fear, Enjoying Statistics, Importance of Statistics, and Perceived Statistical Difficulty - were calculated for each participant by averaging the corresponding items. Subscale scores therefore ranged from 1 to 9. These subscale scores were not used as indicators in the latent profile models; they were calculated after profile estimation to facilitate substantive interpretation and comparison of the identified profiles.

Reverse coding of negatively worded items

Before conducting the analyses, negatively worded items were reverse-coded so that higher scores consistently represented more favourable attitudes toward statistics. Items 2, 4, 5, 6, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 22, 24, 27, 28, 29, 32, and 33 were reverse-coded using the transformation:

Reverse-coded score = 10 - original item score.

Following reverse coding, higher scores on the Statistical Anxiety-Fear and Perceived Statistical Difficulty subscales indicated lower anxiety or fear and lower perceived difficulty, respectively. This directional alignment enabled the profile patterns to be interpreted consistently across all five subscales, with higher values reflecting more favourable attitudes.

Handling of missing data

The dataset was examined for missing values before analysis. No missing responses were identified for the 33 scale items or for the variables included in the profile comparisons. Therefore, no imputation or other missing-data procedure was required, and all 193 participants were included in the analyses.

Statistical analysis

Latent profile analysis [11] was conducted using all 33 item-level responses from the Statistics Attitude Scale. The original nine-point item scores were retained and treated as continuous indicators. Candidate models with one to seven profiles were estimated in the mclust package to avoid selecting a solution solely because it represented the upper boundary of the model search [12]. An EEI parameterization was specified, allowing item means to vary across profiles while constraining item variances to be equal and within-profile covariances to zero. Models were estimated by maximum likelihood.

The optimal number of profiles was determined using the Akaike information criterion (AIC), Bayesian information criterion (BIC), sample-size-adjusted Bayesian information criterion (aBIC), entropy, the Lo-Mendell-Rubin adjusted likelihood ratio test, and the bootstrap likelihood ratio test. Lower information-criterion values indicated better fit, entropy values closer to 1 indicated better classification quality, and significant likelihood ratio tests supported the model with k profiles over the corresponding model with k-1 profiles. Profile size and substantive interpretability were also considered in selecting the final model, in addition to the statistical fit criteria [13]. Solutions containing profiles too small to support meaningful substantive interpretation were not retained. The bootstrap likelihood ratio test was conducted with 1000 bootstrap replications, and the Lo-Mendell-Rubin adjusted likelihood ratio test was calculated in the tidyLPA package [14].

Participants were assigned to the profile associated with their highest posterior membership probability. As an alternative, more parsimonious solution, the three-profile model was also examined using the same indicators and model specification. The characteristics of this alternative solution are presented in the supplementary materials.

After profile estimation, categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages, whereas continuous and ordinal variables were presented as medians and interquartile ranges. Sociodemographic and academic characteristics were compared across latent profiles using Pearson’s chi-square test for categorical variables when expected cell-count assumptions were satisfied; otherwise, the Fisher-Freeman-Halton exact test with Monte Carlo simulation was applied. Continuous and ordinal variables were compared using the Kruskal-Wallis test. After profile estimation, item-averaged scores were calculated for the five original subscales—Relation of Statistics to Professional Life, Statistical Anxiety-Fear, Enjoying Statistics, Importance of Statistics, and Perceived Statistical Difficulty. These scores were not used as indicators in the latent profile models but were compared across profiles using the Kruskal-Wallis test solely to facilitate descriptive interpretation of the profile patterns; these comparisons were not considered external validation of the latent profile solution. Statistically significant Kruskal-Wallis tests were followed by pairwise comparisons based on mean-rank differences, with Bonferroni adjustment for multiple comparisons. Cramér’s V was reported as the effect size for categorical variables, and epsilon-squared (ε²) was used for Kruskal-Wallis comparisons. All statistical analyses were conducted in R version 4.6.1, and all tests were two-sided, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

Participant characteristics

A total of 193 participants were included in the analysis, comprising 132 first-year medical students (68.4%) and 61 medical residents (31.6%). The median age was 20 years (interquartile range: 19–28; range: 18–53), and 97 participants (50.3%) were female. Among the first-year medical students, 91 (68.9%) were enrolled in the Turkish-language medical programme and 41 (31.1%) in the English-language programme. Overall, 90 participants (46.6%) lived at home, while 103 (53.4%) lived in non-home accommodation. Ninety-eight participants (50.8%) had attended a selective science-focused high school, and 129 (66.8%) reported that studying medicine had been their own choice. Among the first-year medical students, 27 (20.5%) reported regretting their decision to study medicine. The median self-rated scores were 5 (interquartile range: 4–5) for interest in mathematics during high school, 3 ([3–4]) for academic motivation, and 3 [2–4] for computer skills.

Internal consistency of the scale

In the present sample, Cronbach’s α coefficients were 0.921 for Relation of Statistics to Professional Life, 0.842 for Statistical Anxiety-Fear, 0.835 for Enjoying Statistics, 0.853 for Importance of Statistics, and 0.782 for Perceived Statistical Difficulty. The overall scale coefficient was 0.942.

Latent profile model selection

Models specifying one to seven latent profiles were estimated and compared using multiple fit indices (Table 1). The AIC and aBIC values decreased progressively as additional profiles were added, reaching their lowest values in the seven-profile model (AIC = 26,632.44; aBIC = 26,658.08). In contrast, the BIC reached its minimum in the five-profile model (BIC = 27,483.90) and increased in the six- and seven-profile solutions. Entropy values were high across all multi-profile solutions, ranging from 0.955 to 0.976, and were 0.970 for the five-profile model, indicating good classification quality. Both the Lo-Mendell-Rubin adjusted likelihood ratio test and the bootstrap likelihood ratio test were statistically significant for each successive model comparison, supporting the model with k profiles over the corresponding model with k − 1 profiles.

Despite the lower AIC and aBIC values and slightly higher entropy observed for the six- and seven-profile models, both solutions contained extremely small profiles. The six-profile model included one profile comprising 0.5% of the sample (n = 1), whereas the seven-profile model included two profiles comprising 0.5% of the sample each (n = 1 each). These profiles were not retained because their trivial size precluded meaningful substantive interpretation and suggested possible overextraction.

In the five-profile solution, profile proportions ranged from 10.9% to 28.0%, and the smallest profile comprised 21 participants. Considering the minimum BIC value, high classification quality, adequate profile sizes, and substantive interpretability together, the five-profile model was retained as the final solution for subsequent analyses. The three-profile solution was also examined as a more parsimonious alternative and is presented in the supplementary materials.

Characteristics of the five latent profiles

The retained five-profile solution comprised 47 participants in Profile 1 (24.4%), 54 in Profile 2 (28.0%), 36 in Profile 3 (18.7%), 21 in Profile 4 (10.9%), and 35 in Profile 5 (18.1%). Based on their relative patterns across the five subscales, the profiles were labelled Low-Engagement and Anxious (P1), Value-Aware but Anxious (P2), Moderately Receptive (P3), Highly Disengaged and Anxious (P4), and Highly Receptive and Confident (P5). The profile patterns are illustrated in Fig. 1 and summarized in Table 2.

Alternative three-profile solution

As an alternative, more parsimonious representation of the data, the three-profile solution was also examined using the same 33 continuous indicators and EEI model specification. The profiles were labelled Moderately Engaged but Anxious (P1; n = 98, 50.8%), Highly Receptive and Confident (P2; n = 41, 21.2%), and Highly Disengaged and Anxious (P3; n = 54, 28.0%) based on their relative patterns across the five subscales. The profiles differed significantly across all five item-averaged subscale scores (all p < 0.001), with effect sizes ranging from ε2 = 0.266 for Perceived Statistical Difficulty to ε2 = 0.689 for Relation of Statistics to Professional Life. Differences in educational level, age, type of accommodation, regret studying medicine, interest in mathematics during high school, academic motivation, and self-assessed computer skills were also observed across the three profiles. Discussion

This study identified five distinct profiles of attitudes toward biostatistics among first-year medical students and medical residents: Low-Engagement and Anxious, Value-Aware but Anxious, Moderately Receptive, Highly Disengaged and Anxious, and Highly Receptive and Confident. The profiles differed not only in overall favourability but also in how professional relevance, perceived importance, anxiety, enjoyment, and perceived difficulty were combined. The alternative three-profile solution-Moderately Engaged but Anxious, Highly Receptive and Confident, and Highly Disengaged and Anxious-retained the broader intermediate, receptive, and disengaged patterns, whereas the five-profile solution provided additional differentiation among learners with intermediate or unfavourable attitudes. In particular, it separated learners who recognised the professional value of statistics but remained anxious from those who showed broader disengagement. Because first-year students were assessed only after completing the course and related examinations, these findings should be understood as a cross-sectional post-course snapshot rather than as effects of instruction.

The three-profile solution offered a useful parsimonious summary of the principal patterns. Nevertheless, the five-profile solution was retained because it yielded the lowest BIC and maintained high classification certainty, substantively interpretable distinctions, and reasonable profile sizes. Although the AIC and aBIC continued to decrease and entropy increased slightly in the six- and seven-profile solutions, these models produced one or two profiles comprising only a single participant. The additional profiles were therefore considered too small to support meaningful substantive interpretation. The added value of the five-profile solution does not imply that educators should create five separate instructional tracks. Rather, it suggests that learners within the same broad attitudinal category may benefit from different instructional emphases. The Low-Engagement and Anxious profile combined limited engagement with anxiety and may require both stronger links to professional relevance and low-threat scaffolding. The Moderately Receptive profile showed comparatively more favourable attitudes and lower anxiety, suggesting greater readiness for authentic application and reinforcement. Most importantly, the Value-Aware but Anxious profile identified learners who already recognised the importance of statistics but remained emotionally uncomfortable; for them, further emphasis on relevance alone may be insufficient, and support may need to focus on guided practice, successful learning experiences, and perceived competence. Thus, the three-profile solution may offer a broad descriptive framework, whereas the five-profile solution provides additional interpretive nuance. These implications remain provisional and require prospective evaluation.

These findings support the value of a person-centred approach in biostatistics education. Mutz and Daniel demonstrated that learners could be classified into distinct latent attitude types toward research methods and statistics, highlighting the heterogeneity of student attitudes. The present findings extend this person-centred perspective to medical education by showing that learners with similar overall attitudes may differ meaningfully in the combination of professional relevance, anxiety, enjoyment, perceived importance, and perceived difficulty [10]. Although some profiles could be broadly ordered from unfavourable to favourable, the Value-Aware but Anxious profile represented a distinct configuration: participants considered statistics professionally relevant and important but reported greater anxiety, less enjoyment, and greater perceived difficulty than those in the Highly Receptive and Confident profile. Such a pattern would be difficult to identify through a total scale score alone.

The coexistence of perceived value with emotional discomfort, perceived difficulty, or low confidence is consistent with previous research. Medical undergraduates have been reported to recognise the value and professional relevance of statistics while continuing to perceive it as difficult [6]. Graduate-entry medical students similarly acknowledged the professional relevance of statistics but reported less favourable feelings and tended to perceive the subject as difficult [4]. Zhang et al. found generally positive attitudes among medical postgraduates alongside the belief that statistics was particularly difficult [5]. West and Ficalora also reported that most medical students, residents, and faculty considered biostatistics important for evidence-based medicine, whereas relatively few considered their training adequate or felt confident in identifying appropriate statistical methods, designing research projects, or conducting statistical analyses [2]. Together, these findings reinforce an important distinction: recognising the value of statistics does not necessarily translate into confidence, positive feelings, or lower perceived difficulty.

The strongest differentiation between profiles was observed for Relation of Statistics to Professional Life. This suggests that the extent to which learners connect biostatistics with their future professional roles may be an important component of broader attitude patterns. Practising physicians have reported that many did not recognise the value of their undergraduate statistics teaching at the time, but later found these skills relevant to clinical practice, research, and the interpretation of scientific literature [1]. Their recommendations included using actual research studies, clinically relevant examples, and explicit links to day-to-day medical practice. Bland similarly argued that teaching statistics and research methods outside the mainstream medical curriculum risks marginalising these subjects, whereas integration with clinical and problem-based learning may make their relevance more apparent [15]. Professional relevance should therefore not be treated only as an introductory justification; it may be reinforced throughout the curriculum through clinical questions, published research, evidence appraisal, and patient-related decision-making.

Educational level was strongly associated with profile membership. First-year medical students predominated in the Low-Engagement and Anxious, Moderately Receptive, and Highly Disengaged and Anxious profiles, whereas residents were more frequently represented in the Value-Aware but Anxious and Highly Receptive and Confident profiles. This distribution may reflect multiple differences between the groups, including age, clinical exposure, prior educational experiences, and possible self-selection into postgraduate training. The present data cannot determine the independent contribution of any of these factors. Previous use of statistical software has been associated with more favourable attitudes toward statistics among medical students, while participation in research and research-methodology training have been associated with greater use of such software [16]. However, the current cross-sectional comparison should not be interpreted as evidence that attitudes naturally improve as training progresses. First-year students and residents represented substantially different groups, and residents had received undergraduate biostatistics instruction at different universities under heterogeneous curricular conditions. Accordingly, the findings indicate different profile distributions between the two groups in this sample, not transitions of individual learners over time.

Previous evidence also indicates that advancing through medical training or completing a statistics course does not necessarily eliminate unfavourable attitudes. Karaibrahimoğlu and Karaoğlu reported that first-year medical students had higher statistics anxiety scores than second-year students [17]. Negative attitudes and poor biostatistics knowledge have also been reported among family medicine residents [18]. Zhang et al. reported an overall negative change in medical postgraduates’ attitudes after a systematic statistics course, particularly in affect, cognitive competence, and perceived difficulty, while perceived value remained largely unchanged [5]. Together, these findings suggest that exposure to statistics alone may be insufficient and that the design, timing, and contextualisation of instruction warrant careful consideration.

This issue is particularly relevant to the study institution, where formal biostatistics instruction is confined to the first year and is not systematically revisited later. In the second year, students participate in a Special Study Module in which small groups are assigned to faculty supervisors. Depending on the supervisor and project, some students may engage in research or publication activities requiring statistical analysis; however, this exposure is variable, project-dependent, and not standardised across students. Some learners may therefore apply statistical concepts, whereas others may receive little or no further exposure after the first-year course. The absence of systematic reinforcement provides a rationale for evaluating a longitudinally distributed approach in which statistical learning is revisited as it becomes increasingly relevant to clinical practice and research. A Delphi study involving biostatistics educators in Türkiye recommended distributing introductory, basic, and advanced biostatistics content across the first, third, and fifth years of medical education rather than concentrating it in a single early course [19]. The present data do not test this approach or establish that curricular discontinuity caused the observed profiles; rather, they describe a curricular context in which the potential value of such an approach warrants prospective evaluation.

The timing of data collection also requires caution. Surveys were completed after the first-year course and all related examinations, during a curricular block with no further biostatistics teaching. The profiles therefore reflect post-course attitudes rather than anticipatory anxiety immediately before an examination. Because no pre-course assessment was conducted, the study cannot determine whether instruction improved, worsened, or did not change individual attitudes, or whether post-course responses were influenced by relief, fatigue, or other reactions to the completed teaching and assessment period.

The Highly Disengaged and Anxious profile may warrant particular educational attention because it combined low professional relevance, importance, and enjoyment with substantial anxiety and perceived difficulty. Among first-year students, regret about studying medicine was highest in this profile and absent in the Highly Receptive and Confident profile. Mutz and Daniel similarly reported that students with more positive attitudes toward research methods and statistics were less worried that they had chosen the wrong subject and were less likely to intend to change their field of study [10]. However, the direction of the present association cannot be determined. More general dissatisfaction or adjustment difficulties may contribute to disengagement from biostatistics, or both regret and unfavourable attitudes may reflect broader motivational challenges. Because regret was assessed only among first-year students and subgroup sizes were limited, this finding should be treated as hypothesis-generating. Although profile membership should not be used as an individual screening tool, the co-occurrence of broad disengagement and regret may indicate that some learners could benefit from broader academic advising or adjustment support in addition to biostatistics-specific educational support.

Academic motivation and self-assessed computer skills also differed across profiles, but their effect sizes were small. Hasabo et al. found that students with prior statistical software experience had more favourable attitudes toward statistics, while participation in research activities was associated with greater use of statistical software [16]. The present study, however, assessed general self-reported computer skills rather than objective competence or statistical software experience. The association may therefore reflect broader technical confidence or previous learning opportunities rather than objective computer proficiency. Accordingly, the present finding is not directly comparable with studies assessing actual statistical software use. Gender and accommodation type also differed across profiles, but neither association followed a clear progression from less favourable to more favourable attitudes. In particular, living at home was more common in profiles containing larger proportions of residents, suggesting that the association may partly reflect differences in age and educational composition across profiles. These associations should therefore be regarded as exploratory contextual findings rather than direct explanations of profile membership.

The findings provide a rationale for evaluating active, clinically contextualised, and differentiated forms of instruction, rather than five separate curricula or fixed instructional pathways. A common core curriculum would still be needed to ensure shared foundational competencies. Learners showing broad disengagement may benefit from explicit links between statistical reasoning, clinical decisions, and patient care. Those who recognise the value of statistics but remain anxious may benefit more from guided analyses, worked examples, low-stakes formative assessment, and gradual progression towards independent work. Moderately receptive learners may be supported through collaborative projects and authentic applications, whereas highly receptive learners could be offered optional research or peer-support opportunities. In a cluster-randomised trial, seminar-case learning produced higher scores on several application-oriented examination components, greater satisfaction with the practical course, and higher self-reported benefit than lecture-based practical teaching [20]. Students in health-related programmes also perceived a practical model based on reviewing research articles and conducting guided analyses as likely to be less stressful, more satisfying, and more useful than a traditional model centred on reproducing statistical procedures in software [21]. Together, these studies support the prospective evaluation of learning-by-doing approaches involving research articles, case-based discussion, guided analysis, and opportunities to apply statistical methods. Profile-informed education should nevertheless avoid rigid tracking because latent profiles are probabilistic summaries of response patterns rather than fixed learner identities.

Attitudes toward statistics may change following instruction, but improvement is neither consistent nor necessarily sustained. Suner and Ersoy reported more favourable group-level attitude scores after a compulsory biostatistics course among first-year dentistry students, without a corresponding significant difference between midterm and final examination performance [7]. Conversely, Zhang et al. observed negative post-course changes in affect, perceived cognitive competence, and perceived difficulty among medical postgraduates, while perceived value remained largely unchanged [5]. The authors suggested that encountering more advanced and complex statistical content may partly explain these changes. Among doctoral research scholars, Philip found substantial immediate improvements in statistical knowledge and attitudes after intensive coursework, followed by significant declines from the immediate post-course levels two to three years later [8]. Together, these findings provide a rationale for monitoring and supporting attitudes across the curriculum rather than assuming that a single course will produce uniform or lasting improvement.

A major strength of this study was the use of a person-centred approach to examine the joint patterning of several attitude dimensions rather than relying only on average or total scores. All 33 items of the validated scale were retained in their original nine-point format, avoiding information loss through categorisation. The study also examined learners at two distinct stages of medical education, evaluated candidate solutions ranging from one to seven profiles, and examined an alternative three-profile solution, showing that the broader receptive and disengaged patterns were retained in a more parsimonious representation.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the sample included first-year students and residents but no students from the intermediate preclinical and clinical years. This sampling gap creates a comparison between two markedly different educational stages and prevents assessment of when profile differences may emerge during undergraduate medical education. Second, first-year students were assessed only after completing the biostatistics course and related examinations, with no pre-course measurement. The study therefore cannot distinguish pre-existing attitudes from changes that may have occurred during the course or attribute the observed profiles to instruction.

Third, the voluntary-response sample was obtained from a single institutional setting, and the number of eligible individuals who viewed the invitation was unknown. Selection bias and limited generalisability are therefore possible. Fourth, the sample size was modest relative to the 33 indicators and five retained profiles. Models with up to seven profiles were examined; however, the six-profile solution contained one profile comprising a single participant, and the seven-profile solution contained two such profiles. These higher-order solutions were therefore not considered substantively interpretable. Although classification quality was high and all five profiles in the retained solution were substantively interpretable, entropy alone does not establish stability or replicability. The five-profile solution should therefore be considered provisional until replicated in larger, independent, and preferably multicentre samples.

Fifth, the EEI specification assumed equal item variances across profiles and zero within-profile covariances; alternative variance-covariance assumptions may yield different solutions. Participants were assigned to their most likely profile, and subsequent comparisons did not fully account for classification uncertainty. Sixth, the subscale scores used to describe the profiles were calculated from the same items that generated them and therefore facilitate interpretation but do not provide independent validation. Finally, objective outcomes such as examination performance, statistical reasoning, research involvement, and subsequent evidence-based practice were not assessed, while academic motivation, mathematics interest, and computer skills were self-reported.

Future multicentre studies should test the reproducibility of the five-profile structure across institutions and educational stages and include objective academic and professional outcomes. Longitudinal latent transition studies could examine profile stability and identify curricular, clinical, or research experiences associated with change. Intervention studies could then evaluate whether clinically integrated and profile-responsive teaching improves attitudes, statistical competence, and sustained engagement more effectively than uniform instruction.

Conclusions

This study identified five distinct profiles of attitudes toward biostatistics among first-year medical students and medical residents, showing that apparently similar overall attitudes may reflect different combinations of professional relevance, perceived importance, anxiety, enjoyment, and perceived difficulty. The distinction between learners who recognised the value of biostatistics but remained anxious and those who were broadly disengaged was particularly informative. Relation of Statistics to Professional Life showed the greatest differentiation across profiles, highlighting the importance of making the clinical and research relevance of biostatistics visible throughout medical education.

First-year medical students and residents showed different profile distributions in this sample; however, the cross-sectional design does not indicate that learners progress from one profile to another during training. Repeated, clinically contextualised, and active learning opportunities warrant prospective evaluation as an alternative to reliance on a single isolated biostatistics course. Profile information may help educators recognise differing learning needs, but it should be treated as an interpretive framework rather than a basis for fixed instructional allocation. Larger multicentre and longitudinal studies are needed to replicate the five-profile structure and determine whether clinically integrated educational strategies improve attitudes, statistical competence, and sustained engagement.
