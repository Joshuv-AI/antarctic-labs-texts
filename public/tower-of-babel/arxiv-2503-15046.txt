Abstract
. We address the enumeration of Eulerian orientations of quartic (i.e., 4-valent) planar maps according to three parameters: the number of vertices, the number of alternating vertices (having in/out/in/out incident edges), and the number of clockwise oriented faces. This is a refinement of the six vertex model studied by Kostov, then Zinn-Justin and Elvey Price, where one only considers the first two parameters. Via a bijection of Ambjørn and Budd, our problem is equivalent to the enumeration of Eulerian partial orientations of general planar maps, counted by the number of edges, the number of undirected edges, and the number of vertices. We first derive from combinatorial arguments a system of functional equations characterising the associated trivariate series 𝖰 ⁡ ( t , ω , v ) {\sf Q}(t,\omega,v) . We then derive from this system a compact characterisation of this series. We use it to determine 𝖰 ⁡ ( t , ω , v ) {\sf Q}(t,\omega,v) in three two-parameter cases. The first two cases correspond to setting the variable ω \omega counting alternating vertices (or undirected edges after the AB bijection) to 0 0 or 1 1 : when ω = 0 \omega=0 we count Eulerian orientations of general planar maps by edges and vertices, and when ω = 1 \omega=1 we count Eulerian orientations of quartic maps by vertices and clockwise faces. The final forms of these two series, namely 𝖰 ⁡ ( t , 0 , v ) {\sf Q}(t,0,v) and 𝖰 ⁡ ( t , 1 , v ) {\sf Q}(t,1,v) , refine those obtained by the authors in an earlier paper for v = 1 v=1 . The third case that we solve, namely v = 1 v=1 (but ω \omega arbitrary), is the standard six-vertex model, for which we provide a new proof of the formula of Elvey Price and Zinn-Justin involving Jacobi theta functions. This new derivation has the advantage that it remains purely in the world of formal power series, not relying on complex analysis. Our results also use a more direct approach to solving the functional equations, in contrast to the guess and check approaches used in previous work.

Introduction

The enumeration of
planar maps
(connected multigraphs embedded in the sphere) is a classical topic in enumerative combinatorics. It started in the sixties, with the seminal work of Tutte based on recursive constructions (see e.g.
[
49
,
50
]
). About fifteen years later, the topic had a second, and independent birth in physics, where new approaches related to matrix integrals were developed
[
19
,
8
]
. Since then, the theory of maps has been enriched by enlightening bijections with families of trees, which explain combinatorially why many families of maps are counted by simple numbers and/or simple generating functions (see e.g.
[
47
,
16
,
17
]
). Beyond the raw enumeration of maps, both the combinatorics and the physics communities showed a very early interest in counting
maps equipped with an additional structure
such as a spanning tree, a colouring, a self-avoiding walk or a classical statistical mechanics model
[
27
,
40
,
45
,
52
,
51
]
. This paper follows this line of research, by studying planar maps equipped with an
Eulerian orientation
of its edges: at each vertex, one finds as many incoming as outgoing edges (Figure
1
, right).

This question was first raised a few years ago by Bonichon et al.
[
10
]
. The question then attracted more interest
[
28
]
, and was finally solved by the authors in a 2020 paper
[
12
]
, both on general planar maps and on
quartic
maps (those in which all vertices have degree
4
). It turned out that the latter problem, and in fact a refinement of it known as the
6
-vertex model
, had been studied and solved much earlier in theoretical physics, in a different form, by Kostov
[
41
]
(although his solution appeared to be incorrect). The second author and Zinn-Justin then revisited Kostov’s approach and derived the correct solution of the 6-vertex model, in terms of Jacobi’s theta functions
[
30
]
. In this refined version, every vertex at which the in/out orientations alternate is assigned
a weight
\omega

[
30
]
to recover the solution obtained in
[
12
]
when
\omega=1
, which takes a completely different, much simpler form.

Left: a rooted planar map, which is 4-valent (or:

In this paper, we refine the above enumeration results for quartic Eulerian orientations, by recording, in addition to the total number of vertices and the number of alternating vertices, the number of clockwise oriented faces. Via a bijection of Ambjørn and Budd
[
2
]
, this problem is equivalent to the enumeration of Eulerian
partial
orientations of
general
planar maps, counted by the
number of edges, the number of undirected edges, and the number of vertices. In a
partial
Eulerian orientation, only some edges are oriented, and one requires again that the number of in- and out-edges coincide at each vertex. The case
\omega=0
corresponds to the enumeration of Eulerian orientations of planar maps, counted by edges and vertices.

We address this three-variate counting problem through recursive constructions of quartic Eulerian orientations. These constructions differ from those of earlier papers. They yield a system of functional equations for series that involve, in addition to the three variables corresponding to the three parameters of interest (denoted
t
,
\omega
and
v
), two additional variables
x
and
y
, called
catalytic
. Manipulating this system, we derive a new characterisation of the solution, involving this time a single catalytic variable
x
. Finally, we use this to determine the solution in three two-parameter cases:

•

The case
\omega=0
:
we then count Eulerian orientations of general planar maps by edges and vertices (Theorem
1.1
); this refines
[
12
]
, where only the edge number was recorded.

•

The case
\omega=1
:
we then count Eulerian orientations of quartic maps by vertices and clockwise faces. (Theorem
1.2
); this refines
[
12
]
, where only the vertex number was recorded.

•

The case

:
this is the standard six-vertex model (Theorem

), and we recover the result of
[

in terms of theta functions.

Our derivations differ significantly from those of
[

30

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Planar maps

A
planar map
is a proper
embedding of a connected planar graph in the
oriented sphere, considered up to orientation preserving
homeomorphism. Loops and multiple edges are allowed
(Figure
3
). The
faces
of a map are the
connected components of its complement. The numbers of
vertices, edges and faces of a planar map
M
, denoted by
\vv(M)
,

\ee(M)
and
\ff(M)
, are related by Euler’s relation

\vv(M)+\ff(M)=\ee(M)+2
.
The
degree
of a vertex or face is the number
of edges incident to it, counted with multiplicity. A
corner
is
a sector delimited by two consecutive edges around a vertex;
hence a vertex or face of degree
k
is incident to
k
corners.
The
dual
of a
map
M
, denoted
M^{*}
, is the map obtained by placing a
vertex of
M^{*}
in each face of
M
and an edge of
M^{*}
across each
edge of
M
; see Figure
3
, right. A map is
quartic
if every vertex has degree 4. Duality transforms
quartic maps into
quadrangulations
, that is, maps in which every face has
degree 4. A planar map is
Eulerian
if every vertex has
even degree. Its dual, with even face degrees, is then
bipartite.
We call a face
of degree 2 (resp. 4) a
digon
(resp.
quadrangle
).

Figure 3.
Left: a rooted planar map, with the root edge and root corner
shown. Right: the dual map, in dashed edges.

For counting purposes it is convenient to consider
rooted
maps.
A map is rooted by choosing an edge, called the root edge, and
orienting it. The starting point of this oriented edge is then the

root vertex
, the other endpoint is the
co-root vertex
. The
face to the right of the root edge is the
root face
. The face
to the left of the root edge is the
co-root face
. Equivalently, one can root the map by selecting a
corner. The correspondence between these two rooting conventions is
that the oriented root edge follows the
root corner in counterclockwise
order around the root vertex.
In figures, we usually choose the root face
as the infinite face (Figure
3
).
This explains why we often call the root face the
outer face
,
its degree the
outer degree
(denoted
\od(M)
), and its edges the
outer edges
. The other faces
are called
inner faces
. Similarly, we call the corners of the
outer face
outer corners
and all other corners
inner
corners
.

From now on, every map
is
planar
and
rooted
, and these precisions will
often be omitted. Our convention for rooting the dual of a map is
illustrated on the right of Figure
3
. Note that it makes duality of rooted
maps a transformation of order 4 rather than 2. By convention,
we include among rooted planar maps the
atomic map

having one vertex and no edge.

2.2.
Orientations and labelled maps

An
Eulerian orientation
is a (rooted, planar) map in
which all edges are oriented, in such a way that at each vertex,
the in- and out-degrees coincide (Figure
1
, right). An
Eulerian partial orientation
is a map in
which
some
edges are oriented, with the same local condition. Note that
the underlying map of an Eulerian orientation must be Eulerian but this need not be the case for an Eulerian partial orientation. As in
[
12
]
, the orientation chosen for the root edge for an Eulerian orientation is required to be consistent with its
orientation coming from the rooting, however we do not make this requirement for Eulerian partial orientations. As a result, for any fixed number of edges, the number of Eulerian partial orientations with no undirected edges is twice the number of Eulerian orientations. A vertex is
alternating
if the in/out orientations of the incident edges that are oriented alternate. A face is
oriented clockwise
if all incident edges that are oriented are clockwise.

We find it convenient to work with
duals of Eulerian (partial) orientations, which turn out to be equivalent to
certain
(weakly) labelled maps
.

Definition 2.1
.

A labelled map is a rooted planar map with integer labels on
its vertices, such that adjacent labels differ by
\pm 1
. Such a map is necessarily
bipartite. We also consider the atomic map, with a single unlabelled vertex,
to be a labelled map. We say that the map is rooted from
\ell
to
\ell^{\prime}
if
\ell
(resp.
\ell^{\prime}
) is the label of the root (resp. co-root) vertex.

A weakly labelled map is a rooted planar map with integer labels on
its vertices, such that adjacent labels differ by
0
,
\pm 1
. An edge is monochromatic if the two incident labels are equal. A face is bicoloured if it contains only two distinct labels (possibly repeated). A vertex is a local minimum if no adjacent vertex has a smaller label.

A labelled map is shown in Figure
4
. Note that in
[
12
]
, labelled maps were, by default, rooted from
0

2.3.
Patches

We first recall the definitions of three families of labelled maps already used in
[
12
]
.

Definition 2.7
.

A patch is a labelled map, rooted from
0
to
1
, in which each inner face has degree
4
,
and the vertices around the outer face are alternately labelled
0
and

1
. We include the atomic map in the set of patches. For
\ell\in\mathbb{Z}
, an
\ell
-patch is a labelled map obtained by adding
\ell
to all labels of a patch.

A C-patch is a patch satisfying two additional
conditions: all neighbours of the root vertex are labelled

1
, and the root corner is the only outer corner at the root
vertex. By convention, the atomic patch is not a C-patch.

D-patches resemble patches but may include digons. More
precisely, a D-patch is a labelled map, rooted from
0
to
1
, in which each inner face
has degree
2
or
4
, those of degree
2
being incident to
the root vertex, and the vertices around the outer face
are alternately labelled
0
and
1
. We also require that all
neighbours of the root vertex are labelled
1
. We include the atomic map in the set of D-patches.

We now introduce a new family of labelled maps, which are reminiscent of lattices or maps with
Dobrushin boundary conditions
considered in the Ising model
[
23
,
32
,
33
]
.

Definition 2.8
.

An E-patch is a labelled map such that each inner face has degree
4
, the outer face has degree say
2m
, and there exists
k\in\llbracket 0,m\rrbracket

such that the outer corners, taken in counterclockwise order starting at the root corner, have labels
0,\ell_{1},0,\ell_{3},\ldots,0,\ell_{2m-1}

where
\ell_{2j+1}=1
for
0\leq j<k
and
\ell_{2j+1}=-1
for
k\leq j<m
. We include the atomic map in the set of E-patches.

If an E-patch satisfies
k=m
in this definition then it is a patch, while if it satisfies
k=0
then it is, up to a shift of the root edge, a
(-1)
-patch.

2.4.
Formal power series

Let
A
be a commutative ring and
x
an indeterminate. We denote by

A[x]
(resp.
A[[x]]
) the ring of polynomials (resp. formal power series) in
x

with coefficients in
A
. If
A
is a field, then
A(x)
denotes the field
of rational functions in
x
, and
A((x))
the field of Laurent series in
x
, that is, series of the form

\sum_{n\geq n_{0}}a_{n}x^{n},

with
n_{0}\in\mathbb{Z}
and
a_{n}\in A
. The coefficient of
x^{n}
in a series
F(x)
is denoted by
[x^{n}]F(x)
.

This notation is generalised to polynomials, fractions
and series in several indeterminates. For instance, the generating function of
Eulerian orientations, counted by edges (variable
t
) and vertices
(variable
v
) belongs to

\mathbb{Q}[v][[t]]
.
If
F(x,x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})
is a series in the
x_{i}
’s whose
coefficients are Laurent series in
x
, say

F(x,x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})=\sum_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{d}}x_{1}^{i_{1}}\cdots x_{d}^{i_{d}}\sum_{n\geq n_{0}(i_{1},\ldots,i_{d})}a(n,i_{1},\ldots,i_{d})x^{n},

then we define the
non-negative part of
F
in
x
as the
following formal power series in
x,x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}
:

[x^{\geq 0}]F(x,x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})=\sum_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{d}}x_{1}^{i_{1}}\cdots x_{d}^{i_{d}}\sum_{n\geq 0}a(n,i_{1},\ldots,i_{d})x^{n}.

We define similarly the
positive part
of
F
in
x
, denoted

[x^{>0}]F
. We will use, more generally, the notation
[x^{>i}]F
for any integer
i
when extracting coefficients with a power of
x
larger than
i
.

If
A
is a field, a power series
F(x)\in A[[x]]

is
algebraic
(over
A(x)
) if it satisfies a
non-trivial polynomial equation
P(x,F(x))=0
with coefficients in

A
. It is
differentially algebraic
(or
D-algebraic
) if it satisfies a non-trivial polynomial
differential equation
P(x,F(x),F^{\prime}(x),\ldots,F^{(k)}(x))\\
=0
with
coefficients in
A
. It is
D-finite
if it satisfies a
linear
differential equation with coefficients in
A(x)
. For
multivariate series, D-finiteness and D-algebraicity require the
existence of a differential equation
in each variable
. We refer to
[
43
,
44
]
for
general results on D-finite series, and to
[
7
, Sec. 6.1]
for
D-algebraic series.

2.5.
Four generating functions

We will count the four families of labelled maps from Section
2.3
(patches, C-patches, D-patches and E-patches) according to three main statistics corresponding to the second column of Table
1
:

•

the number of inner quadrangles (variable
t
)

•

the number of inner bicoloured quadrangles (variable
\omega
)

•

the number of vertices that are local minima, including the unique vertex in the case of the atomic map (variable
v
).

Moreover, we will also record, depending on the class of maps under consideration, one or two additional parameters, which are required to write functional equations (such parameters are sometimes called
catalytic
). More precisely, for all types of patches defined above, a variable
y
records the number of outer corners labelled
1
. For patches, C-patches and D-patches this is also the number of outer corners labelled
0
, or equivalently half the outer degree. Finally, another variable
x
records either the degree of the root vertex (for C-patches) or the number of
inner digons (for D-patches), or the number of outer corners labelled
-1
(for E-patches).

We denote by
{\sf P}(y)
,
{\sf C}(x,y)
,
{\sf D}(x,y)
and
{\sf E}(x,y)
the corresponding generating functions. Despite the notation, these also depend on
t
,
\omega
, and
v
. More precisely, if we denote by
\mathbb{K}
the field
\mathbb{Q}(\omega,v)
of rational functions in
\omega
and
v
, then

•

{\sf P}(y)
belongs to
\mathbb{K}[[y,t]]
,

•

{\sf C}(x,y)
belongs to
\mathbb{K}[x][[y,t]]
,

•

{\sf D}(x,y)
and
{\sf E}(x,y)

belong to
\mathbb{K}[[x,y,t]]
.

This is justified in
[
12
, Sec. 3]
for the first three series. For the fourth one, namely
{\sf E}(x,y)
, we simply note that there are finitely many labelled maps with
n
inner faces, all of degree
4
, and an outer face of bounded degree. The outer degree is twice the sum of the exponents of
x
and
y
in the series
{\sf E}(x,y)
. Finally, note that we could replace the field
\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{Q}(\omega,v)
above by the polynomial ring
\mathbb{Q}[\omega,v]
(or even
\mathbb{Z}[\omega,v]
).

We define the contracted map

Proposition 3.2

Let
M
be a labelled map in which all inner faces are either quadrangles or digons, and all inner digons lie in the subpatch
S
of
M
.
Assume moreover that the root vertex
v
has label
\ell
, and that the outer corners satisfy the (shifted) Dobrushin condition: starting from the root corner

and moving in counterclockwise order around
M
, the corners have labels
\ell,\ell_{1},\ell,\ell_{3},\ldots,\ell,\ell_{2m-1}
where
\ell_{2j+1}=\ell+1
for
0\leq j<k
and
\ell_{2j+1}=\ell-1
for
k\leq j<m
, for some
k\in\llbracket 0,m\rrbracket
.

Then all inner faces of the subpatch
S
and of the contracted map
C
have degree
2
or
4
. All digons of
C
are incident to the root vertex. The outer corners of
S
are labelled alternately
\ell
and
\ell-1
, with
\ell
at the root corner. The outer corners of
C
are labelled alternately
\ell
and
\ell+1
, with
\ell
at the root corner. The root vertex of
C
is only adjacent to vertices labelled
\ell+1
.

Moreover, the number of inner digons, of inner quadrangles, and of bicoloured inner quadrangles of

are given by

\id(M)=\id(S),\quad\iq(M)=\iq(S)+\iq(C)+\id(C),\quad\iq_{\mathrm{bic}}(M)=\iq_{\mathrm{bic}}(S)+\iq_{\mathrm{bic}}(C),

the number of local minima of
M
is

\quad\vv_{\min}(M)=\vv_{\min}(S)+\vv_{\min}(C)-1,

and finally the numbers of outer corners labelled
\ell+1
and
\ell-1
in

are

\oc_{\ell+1}(M)=\oc_{\ell+1}(C),\quad\oc_{\ell-1}(M)=\oc_{\ell-1}(S)-\id(C).

Again, we hope the notation to be self-explanatory.

Proof.

With use the notation of Definition
3.1
. It follows immediately from the definition of
M^{\prime}
and
S
that they share
the same outer face. Moreover, all inner faces of
S
are also inner faces
of
M
. Hence they have degree
2
or
4
. All outer vertices of
S
must also be vertices of

M^{\prime}
, so their labels are no more than
\ell
, by definition of
M^{\prime}
.

Let us prove that the outer labels of
S

cannot be less than
\ell-1
. For any outer vertex
u
of

S
, there is some face
f
of
M
, containing
u
, that is not
a face of
S
. If
f
is
the outer face of
M
, then by the Dobrushin assumption, the label of
u
is
\ell
or
\ell-1
. Otherwise
f
is an inner quadrangle of
M
,
so it must contain a vertex
u^{\prime}

with label at least
\ell+1
(otherwise
f
would be contained in
S
). Since

u
and
u^{\prime}
are both incident to face

, and

has label at most
\ell
, this label can only be
\ell
or
\ell-1
. Hence the outer
vertices of
S
are labelled
\ell
or
\ell-1
. By definition of labelled maps, the labels alternate on the outer face of

Now, every edge in

that connects a vertex in

to a vertex not in

must have endpoints labelled
\ell
(in

) and
\ell+1

(not in

).

into a single (root) vertex, all neighbours of this vertex thus have label

\ell-1

Let us now discuss the inner face degrees in

is contracted into a single vertex, the inner quadrangles of

Let us now examine the quantitative statements of the proposition. The first three follow from the the above discussion on face degrees, since the quadrangles that get contracted into a digon are not bicoloured. For the fourth one, we observe that the root vertex of

The transformation

(3)

be the number of inner digons of

is not larger than the number of outer corners labelled

in

(and give rise to a digon of

) are

. This observation tells us how to reconstruct

from

and

. Let us still call

to the next corner of

and

form an inner digon of

, and to

otherwise. We thus reconstruct

from

and

, proving Point (1).

(19)

{\mathcal{P}}(y)=tv+y{\mathcal{P}}(y)\left({\mathcal{P}}(y)-t(v-1)\right)+\frac{\omega}{y}[y^{>1}]{\mathcal{P}}(y)+2[y^{>0}]\left({\mathcal{P}}(y)[z^{1}]{\mathcal{D}}\!\left(\frac{1}{y},z\right)\right).

We note that the generating function of patches is
{\sf P}(y)={\sf E}(0,y)
. Equivalently, we have
{\mathcal{P}}(y)={\mathcal{E}}(0,y)
, and Eq. (

{\mathcal{P}}(y)=[x^{0}]\left({\mathcal{D}}\!\left(\frac{1}{x},y\right){\mathcal{P}}(x)\right).

We will use in Section

[y^{1}]{\mathcal{P}}(y)=[x^{0}y^{1}]\left({\mathcal{D}}\!\left(\frac{1}{x},y\right){\mathcal{P}}(x)\right).

Finally, recall that the generating function of quartic Eulerian orientations that we want to determine, given by (

{\sf Q}=\frac{1}{t^{2}}[y^{1}]{\mathcal{P}}(y)-v.

4.
A new characterisation of the series
{\sf Q}(t,\omega,v)

In this section, we derive from the functional equations of the previous section a new characterisation of the series
{\sf Q}\equiv{\sf Q}(t,\omega,v)
, which will be the starting point of the proofs of Theorems
1.1
to
1.3
.
This characterisation is simpler than the functional equations in that it involves series with a single “catalytic” variable,
x
. The main series
mixes
positive and negative powers of the variable
x
(hence the notation
{\mathcal{M}}
), and is defined by:

(22)

{\mathcal{M}}(x)=\frac{1}{x}{\mathcal{P}}\left(\frac{1}{x}\right)+[y^{1}]{\mathcal{D}}(x,y).

This is an element of
\mathbb{K}[\frac{1}{x},\frac{1}{1-x}][[t]]
, which we can see as an element of
\mathbb{K}((x))[[t]]
, that is, a series in
t
whose coefficients are Laurent series in
x
(themselves with coefficients in
\mathbb{Q}[\omega,v]
). Note also that
{\mathcal{M}}
has no constant term in
t
. The first two coefficients read

(23)

{\mathcal{M}}(x)=\left(\frac{v}{x}+\frac{1}{1-x}\right)t+\left(\frac{v}{x^{2}}+\frac{1+\omega v}{\left(1-x\right)^{2}}+\frac{\omega}{\left(1-x\right)^{3}}\right)t^{2}+\mathcal{O}(t^{3}).

We actually give two equivalent formulations of our
characterisation of
{\mathcal{M}}(x)
: the first one (Proposition
4.1
) will be used in Section
5
to
solve the cases
\omega=0
and
\omega=1
, while the second
(Proposition
4.3
) will be used in Section
6
to
solve the case
v=1
. Finally, Proposition
1.4
in the
introduction is a mere reformulation of Proposition
4.3
, and will be used in Appendix
B
.

Proposition 4.1
.

The series

{\mathcal{Z}}(x,y):=\frac{\left(1-\frac{1}{x}{\mathcal{M}}(y)\right)\left(1-x-{\omega}{y}+\frac{t}{y}(v-1)-{\mathcal{M}}(y)\right)}{\left(1-\frac{1}{y}{\mathcal{M}}(x)\right)\left(1-y-{\omega}{x}+\frac{t}{x}(v-1)-{\mathcal{M}}(x)\right)},

which belongs a priori to
\mathbb{K}[x,\frac{1}{x},\frac{1}{1-x},y,\frac{1}{y},\frac{1}{1-y},\frac{1}{1-y-{\omega}{x}}][[t]]
, actually lies in the smaller ring
\mathbb{K}[x,\frac{1}{1-x},y,\frac{1}{1-y},\frac{1}{1-y-{\omega}{x}}][[t]]
. That is, expanding
{\mathcal{Z}}(x,y)
in
t
, then in
x
and
y
(in any order), yields a series of
\mathbb{K}[[x,y,t]]
.

Moreover, this property, combined with the initial condition
[x^{-1}]{\mathcal{M}}(x)=tv
,
defines
{\mathcal{M}}(x)
uniquely in the ring
t\mathbb{K}((x))[[t]]
.

Finally, the generating function of quartic Eulerian orientations
can be recovered from
{\mathcal{M}}
by

(24)

{\sf Q}=\frac{1}{t^{2}}[x^{-2}]{\mathcal{M}}(x)-v.

This characterisation of
{\mathcal{M}}(x)
and
{\sf Q}
also holds for numeric values of
v
and
\omega
.

The first terms of
{\mathcal{Z}}
are found to be

(25)

{\mathcal{Z}}(x,y)=\frac{1-x-\omega y}{1-y-\omega x}+\frac{\omega\left(\omega-1\right)\left(x-y\right)}{\left(1-y-\omega x\right)^{2}\left(1-x\right)\left(1-y\right)}t-\frac{\omega\left(\omega-1\right)\left(x-y\right)\Pol(x,y)}{\left(1-x\right)^{3}\left(1-y\right)^{3}\left(1-y-\omega x\right)^{3}}t^{2}+\mathcal{O}(t^{3})

for some polynomial
\Pol(x,y)
in
\mathbb{K}[x,y]
. Observe the factors
\omega\left(\omega-1\right)
in the second and third terms. We will prove in Section
5
that these factors persist in higher order terms, and use this to solve the cases
\omega=0
and
\omega=1
explicitly.

Proof.

Equations (
16
) and (
18
) (the latter written at
1/x
rather than
x
), combined with the definition (
22
) of
{\mathcal{M}}(x)
, give

{\mathcal{D}}(x,y)+\frac{y}{x}{\mathcal{E}}\!\left(\frac{1}{x},y\right)=1+y\,{\mathcal{D}}(x,y){\mathcal{M}}(x).

Moreover, Equation (
17
) reads:

(1-x){\mathcal{D}}(x,y)=1-x+[y^{>0}]\left({\mathcal{D}}(x,y)\left(\frac{\omega}{y}+yt(1-v)+{\mathcal{M}}\left(\frac{1}{y}\right)\right)\right).

The first identity holds in
\mathbb{K}[y]((x))[[t]]
, while the second involves series in the ring
\mathbb{K}((1/y))[[x,t]]
. Both rings are subrings of
\mathbb{K}((1/y))((x))[[t]]
.
Equivalently,

{\mathcal{D}}(x,y)\left(1-y{\mathcal{M}}(x)\right)=1-\frac{y}{x}{\mathcal{E}}\!\left(\frac{1}{x},y\right)

and

[y^{>0}]\left[{\mathcal{D}}(x,y)\left(1-x-\frac{\omega}{y}-yt(1-v)-{\mathcal{M}}\left(\frac{1}{y}\right)\right)\right]=0.

Let us now replace
y
by
1/y
in these two equations:

{\mathcal{D}}\left(x,\frac{1}{y}\right)\left(1-\frac{1}{y}{\mathcal{M}}(x)\right)=1-\frac{1}{xy}{\mathcal{E}}\!\left(\frac{1}{x},\frac{1}{y}\right)

and

[y^{<0}]\left[{\mathcal{D}}\left(x,\frac{1}{y}\right)\left(1-x-{\omega}{y}-\frac{t}{y}(1-v)-{\mathcal{M}}\left(y\right)\right)\right]=0,

with all series considered as elements of
\mathbb{K}((y))((x))[[t]]
. Note that there trivially holds

[x^{<0}]\left[{\mathcal{D}}\left(x,\frac{1}{y}\right)\left(1-x-{\omega}{y}-\frac{t}{y}(1-v)-{\mathcal{M}}\left(y\right)\right)\right]=0.

In other words, the series

{\mathcal{D}}\left(x,\frac{1}{y}\right)\left(1-x-{\omega}{y}-\frac{t}{y}(1-v)-{\mathcal{M}}(y)\right)=\frac{\left(1-\frac{1}{xy}{\mathcal{E}}\left(\frac{1}{x},\frac{1}{y}\right)\right)\left(1-x-{\omega}{y}-\frac{t}{y}(1-v)-{\mathcal{M}}\left(y\right)\right)}{\left(1-\frac{1}{y}{\mathcal{M}}(x)\right)}

actually belongs to
\mathbb{K}[[y]][[x]][[t]]=\mathbb{K}[[x,y,t]]
. Note that all series involved here lie in
\mathbb{K}[x,\frac{1}{x},\frac{1}{1-x},y,\frac{1}{y},\frac{1}{1-y}][[t]]
, so that expanding them first in
x
and then in
y
, or vice-versa, gives the same series. In particular, we can exchange
x
and
y
in the above identity. Remembering that
{\mathcal{E}}(x,y)=t{\sf E}(xt,yt)
is symmetric in
x
and
y
(Proposition
3.9
), we finally conclude that the ratio
{\mathcal{Z}}(x,y)
defined in the proposition,
expanded in
\mathbb{K}((y))((x))[[t]]
(or in
\mathbb{K}((x))((y))[[t]]
, as argued), contains no negative exponents in
x
or
y
.

Initial condition and uniqueness.
By definition (
22
) of
{\mathcal{M}}(x)
, the coefficient of
x^{-1}
in this series is

[x^{-1}]{\mathcal{M}}(x)={\mathcal{P}}(0)=t{\sf P}(0)=tv.

We will now show that this condition, combined with the above property of
{\mathcal{Z}}(x,y)
,
uniquely defines
{\mathcal{M}}(x)
as an element of
t\mathbb{K}((x))[[t]]
. So let
M(x)
be a series satisfying all these conditions, and consider the modified ratio

(26)

\overline{{\mathcal{Z}}}(x,y):=\frac{1-y-\omega x}{1-x-\omega y}\,{\mathcal{Z}}(x,y),

(with
{\mathcal{M}}
replaced by
M
), which expands as a series in
t
with constant term
1
, and coefficients in
\mathbb{K}[[x,y]]
.
Then we have in particular the following equation:

[y^{-1}]\frac{(1-x-\omega y)}{(1-x)(1-\omega y)}\log(\overline{{\mathcal{Z}}}(x,y))=0.

Using the expressions of
\overline{{\mathcal{Z}}}
and
{\mathcal{Z}}
, and simplifying, yields the equation:

0=[y^{-1}]\frac{(1-x-\omega y)}{(1-x)(1-\omega y)}\left(\log\left(1-\frac{1}{x}M(y)\right)+\log\left(1-\frac{t(1-v)+yM(y)}{y(1-x-\omega y)}\right)-\log\left(1-\frac{1}{y}M(x)\right)\right),

because neither
\log(1-y-\omega x)
, nor the second term involving
M(x)
contribute to the coefficient of
y^{-1}
.
Now we write each term
\log(1-u)
as the sum
-\sum_{n\geq 1}u^{n}/n
. The three terms obtained for
n=1
contribute

-\frac{(1-x-\omega y)}{(1-x)(1-\omega y)}[y^{-1}]\left(\frac{1}{x}M(y)+\frac{t(1-v)+yM(y)}{y(1-x-\omega y)}-\frac{1}{y}M(x)\right)\\
=[y^{-1}]\left(\frac{(1-x-\omega y)M(x)}{y(1-x)(1-\omega y)}-\frac{M(y)}{x\left(1-x\right)}-\frac{t\left(1-v\right)}{y\left(1-x\right)\left(1-\omega y\right)}\right)\\
\hskip 71.13188pt=M(x)+\frac{t(xv-x-v)}{x(1-x)}\hskip 51.21495pt\text{because}\ \ [y^{-1}]M(y)=tv\ \ \text{by assumption}.\hskip 56.9055pt

Including now all values of
n
gives:

(27)

M(x)=\frac{t(x+v-xv)}{x(1-x)}+\sum_{n\geq 2}\frac{x\omega^{n-1}M(x)^{n}}{n(1-x)}\\
+[y^{-1}]\sum_{n\geq 2}\left(\frac{(1-x-\omega y)M(y)^{n}}{nx^{n}(1-x)(1-\omega y)}+\frac{\left(\frac{t(1-v)}{y}+M(y)\right)^{n}}{n(1-x)(1-\omega y)(1-x-\omega y)^{n-1}}\right).

This equation, plus the fact that
M
has, by assumption, no constant term in
t
, recursively determines the coefficient of
t^{n}
in
M(x)
. This proves the second statement of the proposition.

Finally, the expression (
24
) of
{\sf Q}
follows from its earlier expression (
21
) in terms of
{\mathcal{P}}
and the definition (
22
) of
{\mathcal{M}}
.

Remark 4.2
.

Equation (
27
) gives a reasonably efficient algorithm for computing the coefficients of
{\mathcal{M}}(x)
and hence of
{\sf Q}
, which only requires handling series in
one
catalytic variable.

We will reformulate the above characterisation of
{\mathcal{M}}
in terms of the following series:

(28)

{\mathcal{F}}(x):=(x{\mathcal{M}}(x)-t(v-1))(1-\omega x-{\mathcal{M}}(x)).

A priori, this is a series in
t
with coefficients in
\mathbb{K}[x,1/x,1/(1-x)]
(and no constant term in
t
), but in fact a stronger property holds.

Proposition 4.3
.

The series
{\mathcal{F}}(x)
belongs to
\mathbb{K}[x,1/(1-x)][[t]]
.
Moreover,

(29)

{\mathcal{F}}({\mathcal{M}}(x))=\left(x{\mathcal{M}}(x)-{t(v-1)}\right)(1-x-\omega{\mathcal{M}}(x)).

The series
{\mathcal{F}}(x)
is the only element of
t\mathbb{K}[[x,t]]
such that the (unique) series
{\mathcal{M}}(x)
that solves the quadratic equation (
28
) and
is a multiple of
t
, that is,

(30)

{\mathcal{M}}(x)=\frac{x(1-\omega x)+t(v-1)}{2x}-\frac{x(1-\omega x)-t(v-1)}{2x}\sqrt{1-\frac{4x{\mathcal{F}}(x)}{\left(x(1-\omega x)-t(v-1)\right)^{2}}},

also satisfies (
29
) and the initial condition
[x^{-1}]{\mathcal{M}}(x)=tv
.

Again, this characterisation of
{\mathcal{M}}(x)
also holds for numeric values of
v
and
\omega
.

Proof.

To prove the first statement, we get back to the definition of
{\mathcal{M}}
in terms of
{\mathcal{P}}
and
{\mathcal{D}}
(see (
22
)) and use the functional equation (
19
), taken at
y=1/x
. After a few reductions, we obtain

(31)

{\mathcal{F}}(x)=t(1-\omega x)-\omega[x^{-1}]{\mathcal{P}}\!\left(\frac{1}{x}\right)-2[x^{\geq 0}]{\mathcal{P}}\!\left(\frac{1}{x}\right){\mathcal{D}}_{1}(x)+x{\mathcal{D}}_{1}(x)\left(1-\omega x-{\mathcal{D}}_{1}(x)\right)+t(v-1){\mathcal{D}}_{1}(x),

where
{\mathcal{D}}_{1}(x)
stands for the coefficient of
y^{1}
in
{\mathcal{D}}(x,y)
. This proves that
{\mathcal{F}}(x)
contains no negative power of
x
. The fact that it is a multiple of
t
is already clear on its definition (
28
) in terms of
{\mathcal{M}}
, since
{\mathcal{M}}
itself is a multiple of
t
.

To prove the second statement, we note that the series
{\mathcal{Z}}(x,y)
of Proposition
4.1
can be written

\Big((xy-t(v-1))(1-x-\omega y)-{\mathcal{F}}(y)\Big)/\Big((xy-t(v-1))(1-y-\omega x)-{\mathcal{F}}(x)\Big).

More precisely, the numerator of
{\mathcal{Z}}(x,y)
is the numerator of the above expression divided by
xy
.
This gives

(32)

(xy-t(v-1))(1-x-\omega y)-{\mathcal{F}}(y)=\Big((xy-t(v-1))(1-y-\omega x)-{\mathcal{F}}(x)\Big){\mathcal{Z}}(x,y),

where we recall that
{\mathcal{Z}}(x,y)\in\mathbb{K}[[x,y,t]]
. Recall moreover that
{\mathcal{F}}(y)\in\mathbb{K}[[y,t]]
. Let us now replace
y
by
{\mathcal{M}}(x)
in the above identity: since
{\mathcal{M}}(x)
is a multiple of
t
, this substitution yields well defined series in
\mathbb{K}((x))[[t]]
. By definition of
{\mathcal{F}}(x)
, the right-hand side then vanishes. Hence the left-hand side vanishes too, and this gives (
29
). Note that this is an application of the so-called
kernel method
, see e.g.
[
4
,
15
,
5
]
.

Let us finally prove uniqueness. Assume that a series
F(x)
of
t\mathbb{K}[[x,t]]
satisfies the conditions stated in the lemma. We want to prove that the corresponding series
M(x)
satisfies the conditions of Proposition
4.1
, so that
M
, and thus
F
, are uniquely defined. Since the initial condition on
M
holds by assumption, it suffices to prove that the series
Z(x,y)
defined by (
32
) (with
{\mathcal{F}}
replaced by
F
of course) belongs to
\mathbb{K}[[x,y,t]]
. A priori, due to the form of the denominator of
Z(x,y)
, the coefficient of
t^{n}
in this series is an element of
\mathbb{K}[[x,y]]
, divided by
x^{n}y^{n}
.

It follows from (
30
) that
M(x)
is a series of
t/x\mathbb{K}[[t/x,x]]
. By assumption, the left-hand side of (
32
) vanishes at
y=M(x)
. Hence

\frac{(xy-t(v-1))(1-x-\omega y)-F(y)}{y-M(x)}

expands as a formal power series in
y
with coefficients in
\mathbb{K}[[x,t/x]]
. Since
F(y)
is a multiple of
t
, this still holds if we divide the above expression by
x
. Hence the same holds for

Z(x,y)=\frac{(y-\frac{t(v-1)}{x})(1-x-\omega y)-\frac{1}{x}F(y)}{(y-M(x))\left(1-y-\omega x+\frac{t(v-1)}{x}-M(x)\right)},

which thus belongs to
\mathbb{K}[[x,t/x,y]]
. That is, the coefficient of
t^{n}
in
Z(x,y)
is a series of
\mathbb{K}[[x,y]]
divided by
x^{n}
.

Let us also note that

Z(x,y)=\frac{1}{Z(y,x)}.

We have just proved that
Z(y,x)
belongs to
\mathbb{K}[[x,y,t/y]]
. Since the coefficient of
t^{0}
in
Z(y,x)
is
({1-y-\omega x})/({1-x-\omega y})
, the above formula shows that
Z(x,y)
belongs to
\mathbb{K}[[x,y,t/y]]
as well. Hence the coefficient of
t^{n}
in
Z(x,y)
has no pole at

either, and finally
Z(x,y)
lies in
\mathbb{K}[[x,y,t]]
. By Proposition
4.1
, the series
M(x)
coincides with
{\mathcal{M}}(x)
and hence
F(x)
coincides with
{\mathcal{F}}(x)
.

Let us now prove that the characterisation of
{\mathcal{M}}(x)
given in the introduction, namely Proposition
1.4
, is a reformulation of the above proposition.

1.4
.

The first two

, while the expression (

(see

In order to determine

of (

integrating over the circle of radius

The right-hand side is a meromorphic function of
y
on the portion of the complex plane where
\left|(1-y)(y-y_{c}(2y_{c}-1))^{2}\right|>4|y|y_{c}(1-y_{c})^{3}

This portion has an unbounded component
\mathcal{G}(y_{c})
, lying outside a pair of “glasses” meeting at the point
y_{c}
, as illustrated in Figure
14
for various values of
y_{c}
in
(1/4,1)

is what is left from the segment

at

. Also, for

a large positive real in

\frac{y+y_{c}(2y_{c}-1)}{2y}-\frac{y-y_{c}}{2y}\sqrt{\frac{y-(1-2y_{c})^{2}}{{y-1}}}.

This is meromorphic on

Moreover, it has an explicit anti-derivative, namely

\widehat{M}(y):=y_{c}(2y_{c}-1)\log\left(\frac{y-1+2y_{c}+(y-1)\sqrt{\frac{y-(2y_{c}-1)^{2}}{y-1}}}{2y_{c}}\right)-\frac{y-1}{2}\left(\sqrt{\frac{y-(2y_{c}-1)^{2}}{y-1}}-1\right),

A careful expansion of

yields (

\arccos\frac{1-3y_{c}}{2y_{c}^{3/2}}=3\arctan(\sqrt{4y_{c}-1})
.

Our starting point is Proposition

and

. As already noted, this forces

to be a series in

\mathbb{K}((x))
, and more precisely a series of

The solution of this case is significantly less direct than in the cases

We will first construct a collection of series

satisfying (

6.2
, while Section

In Appendix

, we give an alternative argument to deduce the same result using complex analysis, somewhat along the lines of

. In particular, this argument justifies our Ansatz

6.1.
An Ansatz

As explained in the introduction, it is convenient to write
\omega=-2\cos(2\alpha)
.
We will make an
Ansatz
on the form of
M(x)
, and hence of
F(x)
, by assuming that, up to a change of variables,
M
acts as a shift. That is,

(55)

M(\chi(z))=\chi(z-\gamma)

for some series
\chi(z)
, depending on
z
and
t
, while
\gamma
would only depend on
t
. Then by (
52
), we would have

(56)

F(\chi(z))=\chi(z)\chi(z-\gamma)(1-\omega\chi(z)-\chi(z-\gamma)),

so that, upon replacing

by

,

F(M(\chi(z)))=F(\chi(z-\gamma))=\chi(z-\gamma)\chi(z-2\gamma)(1-\omega\chi(z-\gamma)-\chi(z-2\gamma)).

On the other hand, by (
54

F(M(\chi(z)))=\chi(z)\chi(z-\gamma)(1-\chi(z)-\omega\chi(z-\gamma)).

Comparing the last two identities shows that the change of variables
\chi
should satisfy either
\chi(z-2\gamma)=\chi(z)
or

\chi(z)+\omega\chi(z-\gamma)+\chi(z-2\gamma)=1,

or equivalently

(57)

\chi(z+2\gamma)+\omega\chi(z+\gamma)+\chi(z)=1.

We will proceed with the second choice (the first one did not appear to lead to a solution to the original equation). Combining (
56
) and (
57
) yields

(58)

F(\chi(z))=\chi(z-\gamma)\chi(z)\chi(z+\gamma).

Remark 6.1
.

In the general case where
v
is not necessarily equal to
1
, we prove in Appendix
B
(Proposition
B.4
) that, at an analytic level, the connection (
55
) between
{\mathcal{M}}
and a meromorphic function

indeed holds true, but that the relation (

\chi(\gamma+z)+\omega\chi(z)+\chi(z-\gamma)=1+\frac{t(v-1)}{\chi(z)}.

It would be extremely interesting to solve this equation, as we solve the

Let us return to the case
v=1
. Our first objective here is to find a function
\chi(z)
that not only satisfies the difference equation (
57
), but has also interesting properties as a series in

, so that (
58

.

One obvious solution of the difference equation (
57
) is the constant function

\chi_{0}(z):=\frac{1}{2+\omega}=\frac{1}{4\sin^{2}\!\alpha}.

Hence we can focus on the homogeneous equation

\psi(z+2\gamma)+\omega\psi(z+\gamma)+\psi(z)=0,

which can be written as

\left(\psi(z+2\gamma)-e^{2i\alpha}\psi(z+\gamma)\right)-e^{-2i\alpha}\left(\psi(z+\gamma)-e^{2i\alpha}\psi(z)\right)=0,

or as

\left(\psi(z+2\gamma)-e^{-2i\alpha}\psi(z+\gamma)\right)-e^{2i\alpha}\left(\psi(z+\gamma)-e^{-2i\alpha}\psi(z)\right)=0.

These two forms allow us to reduce the order to
1
, and to consider the equations

(59)

\psi_{+}(z+\gamma)=e^{2i\alpha}\psi_{+}(z)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\psi_{-}(z+\gamma)=e^{-2i\alpha}\psi_{-}(z).

Solutions to such equations are known in terms of Jacobi’s theta functions. More precisely, let us introduce the following function, closely related to Jacobi’s function
\vartheta_{1}(z,q^{1/2})
(see
[
1
, Ch. 16]
):

(60)

\displaystyle\theta(z)

\displaystyle:=\sum_{n\geq 0}(-1)^{n}q^{n(n+1)/2}\sin((2n+1)z)

(61)

\displaystyle=\sin z\prod_{n\geq 1}(1-q^{n})\left(1-q^{n}e^{-2iz}\right)\left(1-q^{n}e^{2iz}\right).

So far we consider
\theta(z)
, classically, as an analytic function of two complex variables
q
and
z
, with
|q|<1
. Then
\theta(-z)=-\theta(z)
and, if we write
q=e^{2i\gamma}
(with
\Im\gamma>0
), then it follows from (
61
) that

\theta(z+\gamma)=-e^{-2iz-i\gamma}\theta(z).

These two identities imply that the series

\psi(z):=\frac{\theta(z+\alpha)}{\theta(z)}

satisfies the second equation in (
59
), while

\psi(-z)=\frac{\theta(z-\alpha)}{\theta(z)}

satisfies the first one. We will thus consider solutions
\chi(z)
of the second order equation (
57
) of the form

\chi(z)=c_{+}\psi(z)+c_{-}\psi(-z)+\frac{1}{4\sin^{2}\!\alpha}.

Note that
\psi(z)
has a pole of order 1 at
z=0
. However, in order to invert (
58
), we would rather handle a function
\chi
that vanishes at
z=0
. This leads us to choose
c_{-}=c_{+}:=c
(so as to cancel the pole), and then to choose
c
such that
\chi(0)=0
. After a few calculations based on the definition of
\psi
in terms of
\theta
, we are led to

(62)

c=-\theta^{\prime}(0)/(8\sin^{2}\!\alpha\,\theta^{\prime}(\alpha)),

so that

(63)

\chi(z)=\frac{1}{4\sin^{2}\!\alpha}\left(1-\frac{\theta^{\prime}(0)}{2\theta^{\prime}(\alpha)}\left(\psi(z)+\psi(-z)\right)\right).

Lemma 6.2
.

The above series
\chi
is a formal power series in
q
with coefficients that are polynomials in
\omega=-2\cos(2\alpha)
and
\sin^{2}\!z
. More precisely, it expands as

(64)

\chi(z)=4q\sin^{2}\!z\left(1+\sum_{n\geq 1}q^{n}\Pol_{n}(4\sin^{2}\!z,\omega)\right)

where each
\Pol_{n}
is a bivariate polynomial with integer coefficients, of degree at most
n
in its first variable. Hence
\chi
can also be seen as a formal power series in
q
and
u:=4q\sin^{2}\!z
with coefficients in
\mathbb{Z}[\omega]
.

Finally,

(65)

\lim_{z\rightarrow 0}\frac{\chi(z)}{\sin^{2}\!z}=\frac{1}{24\sin^{2}\!\alpha}\left(\frac{\theta^{(3)}(0)}{\theta^{\prime}(0)}-\frac{\theta^{(3)}(\alpha)}{\theta^{\prime}(\alpha)}\right)=4q+\mathcal{O}(q^{2}).

Proof.

Let us first observe that for all
n
,
\frac{\cos((2n+1)\alpha)}{\cos\alpha}
is a polynomial in
\omega=-2\cos(2\alpha)
,
and
\frac{\sin((2n+1)z)}{\sin z}
is a polynomial in
4\sin^{2}\!z
of degree
n
, both with integer coefficients.

We start by showing that
(\omega+2)\chi
is a formal power series in
\mathbb{Z}[4\sin^{2}\!z,\omega][[q]]
.
From the definition (
60
), we have

\displaystyle\frac{\theta(z)}{\sin z}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\geq 0}(-1)^{n}q^{n(n+1)/2}\,\frac{\sin((2n+1)z)}{\sin z}

\displaystyle\in 1+q\mathbb{Z}[4\sin^{2}\!z][[q]],

\displaystyle\frac{\theta(z+\alpha)+\theta(z-\alpha)}{2\sin z\cos\alpha}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\geq 0}(-1)^{n}q^{n(n+1)/2}\,\frac{\sin((2n+1)z)\cos((2n+1)\alpha)}{\sin z\cos\alpha}

\displaystyle\in 1+q\mathbb{Z}[4\sin^{2}\!z,\omega][[q]],

(66)

\displaystyle\frac{\theta^{\prime}(\alpha)}{\cos\alpha}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\geq 0}(-1)^{n}q^{n(n+1)/2}(2n+1)\frac{\cos((2n+1)\alpha)}{\cos\alpha}

\displaystyle\in 1+q\mathbb{Z}[\omega][[q]],

(67)

\displaystyle\theta^{\prime}(0)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\geq 0}(-1)^{n}q^{n(n+1)/2}(2n+1)

\displaystyle\in 1+q\mathbb{Z}[[q]].

Moreover, in these series the exponent of
\sin^{2}\!z
never exceeds the exponent of
q
. It follows that the series

(\omega+2)\chi(z):=1-\frac{\theta^{\prime}(0)}{2\theta^{\prime}(\alpha)}\cdot\frac{\theta(z+\alpha)+\theta(z-\alpha)}{\theta(z)}=1-\theta^{\prime}(0)\cdot\frac{\cos\alpha}{\theta^{\prime}(\alpha)}\cdot\frac{\theta(z+\alpha)+\theta(z-\alpha)}{2\sin z\cos\alpha}\cdot\frac{\sin z}{\theta(z)}

belongs to
q\mathbb{Z}[4\sin^{2}\!z,\omega][[q]]
, with again the same bound on the exponents of
\sin^{2}\!z
.

We note additionally that the expression above
converges to
0
when either
\alpha\to 0
(that is
\omega\to-2
) or
z\to 0
, so that the coefficient of
q^{n}
in this series must be a multiple of
(\omega+2)4\sin^{2}\!z
(again with integer coefficients). That is,
\chi(z)\in 4q\sin^{2}\!z\mathbb{Z}[4\sin^{2}\!z,\omega][[q]]
, and again the exponent of
\sin^{2}\!z
never exceeds the exponent of
q
. Simply computing the first term of this series explicitly yields (
64
), while expanding
\chi(z)
as a series in
z
using
\theta^{\prime\prime}(0)=\theta(0)=0
yields (
65
).

6.2.
Construction of
F(x)

Guided by the above considerations, we will now construct from scratch a collection of series
F(x)
(and
M(x)
) satisfying
Conditions (
52
) and (
54
). They depend on a variable
q
, which will be specialised later to a well-chosen series in
t
, so as to satisfy
[x^{-1}]M(x)=t
(Section
6.3
). Underlying this construction
are three levels of Laurent series in
q
, whose coefficients involve respectively:

•

the variable
z
of the previous subsection,

•

a variable
u
that will be often instantiated to
4q\sin^{2}\!z
as in Lemma
6.2
,

•

the original variable
x
.

The series involving
z
will be denoted with greek letters (as above), those involving
u
will be denoted in roman capital letters (not italicised) and those involving
x
in the usual font.
In the first part of this subsection, we define various formal power series in
q
, with coefficients in
\mathbb{K}(u)
, that are formal counterparts of the series of the previous subsection, in the sense that they specialise to these series when
u=4q\sin^{2}\!z
. Then in Proposition
6.6
, we perform the final step that takes us to series in
q
with coefficients in
\mathbb{K}(x)
.

Analogue of
e^{2iz}
.
First, we introduce a series of
\mathbb{Q}(u)[[q]]
that corresponds to
e^{2iz}
when
u=4q\sin^{2}\!z
:

\mathrm{E}(u)=1-\frac{u}{2q}\left({1-\sqrt{1-4q/u}}\right)=-\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{1}{n+1}\binom{2n}{n}\frac{q^{n}}{u^{n}}.

The notation
\mathrm{E}(u)
should be reminiscent of the exponential
e^{2iz}
.
Note that

(68)

u=q\left(2-\mathrm{E}(u)-\frac{1}{\mathrm{E}(u)}\right).

Consequently, any symmetric polynomial in
\mathrm{E}(u)
and
1/\mathrm{E}(u)
(say, with coefficients in
\mathbb{Q}
and degree
d
), is a polynomial in
u/q
of degree
d
.

Analogue of the shift.
The shift transformation
z\mapsto z-\gamma
of the previous subsection gives
e^{2iz}\mapsto q^{-1}e^{2iz}
(since we had taken
q=e^{2i\gamma}
). It should thus correspond to
\mathrm{E}(u)\mapsto q^{-1}\mathrm{E}(u)
, hence, by the above identity, to
u\mapsto\mathrm{S}(u)
with

(69)

\displaystyle\mathrm{S}(u)

\displaystyle:=2q-\mathrm{E}(u)-\frac{q^{2}}{\mathrm{E}(u)}

\displaystyle=\frac{(1+u)^{2}}{u}q+\mathcal{O}(q^{2}).

We now examine the effect of the shift
u\mapsto\mathrm{S}(u)
in certain series involving
\mathrm{E}(u)
.

Lemma 6.3
.

Given a series
\mathrm{G}(u)\in\mathbb{K}[[u,q]]
,
the substitution
u\mapsto\mathrm{S}(u)
in
\mathrm{G}(u)
is well defined, as a series of
\mathbb{K}(u)[[q]]
.

Let
\mathrm{H}(v)
be a series of
\mathbb{K}[v+1/v][[q]]
, say
\mathrm{H}(v)=\sum_{m\geq 0}\mathrm{H}_{m}(v+1/v)q^{m}
for some polynomials
\mathrm{H}_{m}
, and assume that

\forall m\geq 0,\ \deg\mathrm{H}_{m}\leq m\qquad\text{and}\qquad m-\deg\mathrm{H}_{m}\rightarrow\infty.

Then
\mathrm{G}(u):=\mathrm{H}(\mathrm{E}(u))
is a well-defined series in
\mathbb{K}[u][[q]]
. Moreover,
\mathrm{G}(\mathrm{S}(u))=\mathrm{H}(q^{-1}\mathrm{E}(u))
.

Proof.

The first point follows from the fact that
\mathrm{S}(u)
is a series in
q
(with coefficients in
\mathbb{Q}(u)
) that has no constant term.

Now let
\mathrm{H}(v)
satisfy the assumptions of the lemma. As noticed above, it follows from (
68
) that
\mathrm{H}_{m}(\mathrm{E}+1/\mathrm{E})=\mathrm{H}_{m}(2-u/q)
is a polynomial in
u/q
of degree
\deg\mathrm{H}_{m}
. The assumptions on the degree of
\mathrm{H}_{m}
then imply that
q^{m}\mathrm{H}_{m}(\mathrm{E}+1/\mathrm{E})
is a polynomial in
q
and
u
, and that its valuation in
q
tends to infinity as
m
increases. Hence

\mathrm{G}(u)=\sum_{m\geq 0}q^{m}\mathrm{H}_{m}(2-u/q)

is well defined in
\mathbb{K}[u][[q]]
.
In particular,
\mathrm{G}(\mathrm{S}(u))
is well defined by the first statement of the lemma. We have

\mathrm{G}(\mathrm{S}(u))=\sum_{m\geq 0}q^{m}\mathrm{H}_{m}(2-\mathrm{S}(u)/q)=\sum_{m\geq 0}q^{m}\mathrm{H}_{m}(\mathrm{E}(u)/q+q/\mathrm{E}(u))

by (
69
), and this proves the final statement.

Analogue of the series
\theta
.
We now introduce a series
\mathrm{T}(u)
that is the counterpart of
\theta(z)
(up to a factor
\sin z
):

\mathrm{T}(u)=\sum_{n\geq 0}(-1)^{n}q^{n(n+1)/2}\,\frac{\mathrm{E}(u)^{n+1}-\mathrm{E}(u)^{-n}}{\mathrm{E}(u)-1}.

Note that

\frac{\mathrm{E}(u)^{n+1}-\mathrm{E}(u)^{-n}}{\mathrm{E}(u)-1}=\frac{1}{\mathrm{E}(u)^{n}}+\cdots+\mathrm{E}(u)^{n}

is a symmetric polynomial of degree
n
in
\mathrm{E}(u)
and
1/\mathrm{E}(u)
, hence a polynomial in
u/q
of degree
n
. By Lemma
6.3
,
\mathrm{T}(u)
is a series of
\mathbb{Q}[u][[q]]
.
It starts as follows:

\mathrm{T}(u)=1+u+(u^{2}-3)q+\mathcal{O}(q^{2}).

By construction,

(70)

\sin z\,\mathrm{T}(4q\sin^{2}\!z)=\theta(z),

where
\theta(z)
is defined by (
60
).
We also define the following counterparts of
\theta(z+\alpha)+\theta(z-\alpha)
and
\theta(z+\alpha)-\theta(z-\alpha)
:

\mathrm{T}^{+}(u):=2\sum_{n\geq 0}(-1)^{n}q^{n(n+1)/2}\cos((2n+1)\alpha)\,\frac{\mathrm{E}(u)^{n+1}-\mathrm{E}(u)^{-n}}{\mathrm{E}(u)-1},

\mathrm{T}^{-}(u):=-2\sum_{n\geq 0}(-1)^{n}q^{n(n+1)/2}\sin((2n+1)\alpha)\frac{\mathrm{E}(u)^{n+1}+\mathrm{E}(u)^{-n}}{\mathrm{E}(u)+1}.

Lemma
6.3
applies again, and both series (in
q
) have polynomial coefficients in
u
. More precisely, given that
\omega=-2\cos(2\alpha)=2(1-2\cos^{2}\!\alpha)
, the former series has coefficients in
\cos\alpha\mathbb{Q}[\cos^{2}\alpha,u]=\cos\alpha\mathbb{Q}[\omega,u]

and the latter one in
\sin\alpha\mathbb{Q}[\cos^{2}\alpha,u]=\sin\alpha\mathbb{Q}[\omega,u]
. The first terms read:

\frac{\mathrm{T}^{+}(u)}{\cos\alpha}=2(1-u(\omega+1))+\mathcal{O}(q),\qquad\frac{\mathrm{T}^{-}(u)}{\sin\alpha}=2(-1+u(\omega-1))+\mathcal{O}(q).

By construction,

(71)

\sin z\ \mathrm{T}^{+}(4q\sin^{2}\!z)=\theta(z+\alpha)+\theta(z-\alpha)\quad\text{and }\quad-\cos z\ \mathrm{T}^{-}(4q\sin^{2}\!z)=\theta(z+\alpha)-\theta(z-\alpha).

Lemma
6.3
also allows us to determine the values of the three
\mathrm{T}
-series at
\mathrm{S}(u)
.

Lemma 6.4
.

We have the following identities:

\displaystyle(q-\mathrm{E})\mathrm{T}(\mathrm{S}(u))

\displaystyle={\mathrm{E}(\mathrm{E}-1)}\mathrm{T}(u),

\displaystyle(q-\mathrm{E})\mathrm{T}^{+}(\mathrm{S}(u))

\displaystyle=\cos(2\alpha)\mathrm{E}(\mathrm{E}-1)\mathrm{T}^{+}(u)+\sin(2\alpha)\mathrm{E}(\mathrm{E}+1)\mathrm{T}^{-}(u),

\displaystyle(q+\mathrm{E})\mathrm{T}^{-}(\mathrm{S}(u))

\displaystyle=\sin(2\alpha)\mathrm{E}(\mathrm{E}-1)\mathrm{T}^{+}(u)-\cos(2\alpha)\mathrm{E}(\mathrm{E}+1)\mathrm{T}^{-}(u).

Proof.

Use Lemma
6.3
and the expressions of
\mathrm{T}
,
\mathrm{T}^{+}
and
\mathrm{T}^{-}
in terms of
\mathrm{E}
.

Analogue of the series
\chi
.
We now introduce the following series in
q
:

(72)

\mathrm{X}(u):=c\frac{\mathrm{T}^{+}(u)}{\mathrm{T}(u)}+\frac{1}{4\sin^{2}\!\alpha}=\frac{1}{4\sin^{2}\alpha}\left(1-\frac{\mathrm{T}(0)}{\mathrm{T}^{+}(0)}\cdot\frac{\mathrm{T}^{+}(u)}{\mathrm{T}(u)}\right),

where
c
is given by (
62
).
From the second expression, this series vanishes at
u=0
.
The first term reads

\mathrm{X}(u)=\frac{u}{1+u}+u\mathcal{O}(q),

Let us prove that this series has coefficients in
\mathbb{Q}[\omega,u,1/(1+u)]\subset\mathbb{Q}[\omega][[u]]
. First, it follows from the above properties of
\mathrm{T}
and
\mathrm{T}^{+}
,
that
4\sin^{2}\!\alpha\mathrm{X}(u)
has coefficients in
\mathbb{Q}[\omega,u,1/(1+u)]
. Then, if we specialise at
\alpha=0
, that is,
\omega=-2
, the definition of this series, we find that it vanishes for this value of
\alpha
(because
\mathrm{T}^{+}=2\mathrm{T}
when
\alpha=0
). Given that
\omega+2=4\sin^{2}\!\alpha
, this proves our claim. Also, observe that
\mathrm{X}(4q\sin^{2}\!z)
is
\chi(z)
(cf. Lemma
6.2
).
We finally denote

\mathrm{X}^{+}(u)=c\,\left(\cos 2\alpha\,\frac{\mathrm{T}^{+}(u)}{\mathrm{T}(u)}+{\sin 2\alpha}{\sqrt{1-4q/u}}\,\frac{\mathrm{T}^{-}(u)}{\mathrm{T}(u)}\right)+\frac{1}{4\sin^{2}\!\alpha},

and

(73)

\mathrm{X}^{-}(u)=c\,\left(\cos 2\alpha\,\frac{\mathrm{T}^{+}(u)}{\mathrm{T}(u)}-{\sin 2\alpha}{\sqrt{1-4q/u}}\,\frac{\mathrm{T}^{-}(u)}{\mathrm{T}(u)}\right)+\frac{1}{4\sin^{2}\!\alpha}.

Both series have coefficients in
\mathbb{K}(u)
. More precisely, we can argue as above to prove that they have coefficients in
\mathbb{Q}[\omega,u,1/u,1/(1+u)]
(and in fact the coefficients of
\mathrm{X}^{-}(u)
have no pole at
u=-1
). We have

(74)

\mathrm{X}^{+}(u)=\frac{1-u+4u\cos^{2}\!\alpha}{1+u}+\mathcal{O}(q),\quad\text{and}\quad\mathrm{X}^{-}(u)=\frac{(1+u)^{2}}{u}q+\mathcal{O}(q^{2}).

The specializations of these series at
u=4q\sin^{2}\!z
are
not
well defined, but they should be thought of as counterparts of
\chi(z+\gamma)
and
\chi(z-\gamma)
, respectively. In particular, observe that

(75)

\mathrm{X}^{+}(u)+\omega\mathrm{X}(u)+\mathrm{X}^{-}(u)=1,

which is the counterpart of
\chi(z+\gamma)+\omega\chi(z)+\chi(z-\gamma)=1
.

Let us now examine the effect of the shift
u\mapsto\mathrm{S}(u)
on the series
\mathrm{X}(u)
and
(\mathrm{X}^{-}\mathrm{X}^{+})(u)
.

Lemma 6.5
.

We have

\mathrm{X}(\mathrm{S}(u))=\mathrm{X}^{-}(u)\qquad\text{and}\qquad(\mathrm{X}^{-}\mathrm{X}^{+})(\mathrm{S}(u))=\mathrm{X}(u)(1-\mathrm{X}(u)-\omega\mathrm{X}^{-}(u)).

Proof.

First,
\mathrm{X}(\mathrm{S}(u))
is well defined since
\mathrm{X}(u)
is a series in
q
and
u
(see Lemma
6.3
). Moreover,

\mathrm{X}(\mathrm{S}(u))=c\frac{\mathrm{T}^{+}(\mathrm{S}(u))}{\mathrm{T}(\mathrm{S}(u))}+\frac{1}{4\sin^{2}\alpha}.

We then use the expressions of
\mathrm{T}^{+}(\mathrm{S}(u))
and
\mathrm{T}(\mathrm{S}(u))
given in Lemma
6.4
, and conclude using

\frac{1+\mathrm{E}}{1-\mathrm{E}}=\sqrt{1-4q/u}.

We proceed similarly for the series

(76)

u\cdot(\mathrm{X}^{-}\mathrm{X}^{+})(u)=u\,\left(c\cos 2\alpha\frac{\mathrm{T}^{+}(u)}{\mathrm{T}(u)}+\frac{1}{4\sin^{2}\!\alpha}\right)^{2}-c^{2}\sin^{2}(2\alpha)(u-4q)\left(\frac{\mathrm{T}^{-}(u)}{\mathrm{T}(u)}\right)^{2},

which is indeed a series in
u
and
q
. We replace
u
by
\mathrm{S}(u)
in this identity, inject the expressions of
\mathrm{T}(\mathrm{S}(u))
,
\mathrm{T}^{+}(\mathrm{S}(u))
and
\mathrm{T}^{-}(\mathrm{S}(u))
given in Lemma
6.4
, and finally the expression (
69
) of
\mathrm{S}(u)
in terms
\mathrm{E}
. This gives the desired identity.

We can now explicitly describe a series
F(x)
satisfying (
52
) and (
54
).

Proposition 6.6
.

There exists a unique series
F(x)
in
\mathbb{K}[[x,q]]
such that

(77)

F(\mathrm{X}(u))=\mathrm{X}^{+}(u)\mathrm{X}(u)\mathrm{X}^{-}(u).

This series is in fact a multiple of
q
. The unique series
M(x)
of
q\mathbb{K}((x))[[q]]
that solves

(78)

F(x)=xM(x)(1-\omega x-M(x))

satisfies

(79)

M(\mathrm{X}(u))=\mathrm{X}^{-}(u).

It also satisfies, as desired

F(M(x))=xM(x)(1-\omega M(x)-x).

Proof.

As argued below (
72
), the series
\mathrm{X}(u)
can be seen as a series in
u
, with constant term
0
and coefficients in
\mathbb{Q}[\omega][[q]]
. Recall that
\mathrm{X}(4q\sin^{2}\!z)=\chi(z)
. The coefficient of
u
in
\mathrm{X}(u)
is thus

(80)

\mathrm{X}_{1}:=\lim_{u\rightarrow 0}\frac{\mathrm{X}(u)}{u}=\lim_{z\rightarrow 0}\frac{\mathrm{X}(4q\sin^{2}\!z)}{4q\sin^{2}\!z}=\lim_{z\rightarrow 0}\frac{\chi(z)}{4q\sin^{2}\!z}=1+\mathcal{O}(q),

by the last result of Lemma
6.2
.
Hence
\mathrm{X}(u)
has a compositional inverse
U(x)
. By the Lagrange inversion formula, its coefficients are polynomials in the coefficients
\mathrm{X}_{i}
of
\mathrm{X}(u)
, divided by powers of
\mathrm{X}_{1}
: hence they are formal power series in
q
. Moreover,

U(x)=\frac{x}{\mathrm{X}_{1}}+\mathcal{O}(x^{2}).

Let us now consider the right-hand side of (
77
), namely
\mathrm{X}^{+}(u)\mathrm{X}(u)\mathrm{X}^{-}(u)
. When we expand the product
\mathrm{X}^{+}(u)\mathrm{X}^{-}(u)
, the square roots disappear, and, as already observed in the proof of Lemma
6.5
,
u\mathrm{X}^{+}(u)\mathrm{X}^{-}(u)
is a formal power series in
u
and
q
. Moreover,
\mathrm{X}(u)
is a formal power series in
u
and
q
, and a multiple of
u
as already discussed. Hence the right-hand side of (
77
) is a formal power series in
q
and
u
, and the substitution
u\mapsto U(x)
in (
77
) defines
F(x)=(\mathrm{X}^{-}\mathrm{X}\mathrm{X}^{+})(U(x))
as a formal power series in
x
and
q
. The fact that
\mathrm{X}^{-}(u)
is a multiple of
q
(see (
74
)) implies that the same holds for
F(x)
.

Let us now define
M(x)
in
q\mathbb{K}((x))[[q]]
by (
78
), or equivalently (
53
). The coefficient of
q^{n}
in this series is a Laurent series in
x
with valuation at least
-n
. In other words,
M(x)
lies in
q/x\mathbb{K}[[x,q/x]]
. Now
\mathrm{X}(u)=u/(1+u)+u\mathcal{O}(q)
belongs to
u\mathbb{K}[[u,q]]
, and
q/\mathrm{X}(u)
can be seen to be in
q/u\mathbb{K}[[u,q]]
. Hence the substitution
M(\mathrm{X}(u))
is well-defined as a series in
u
and
q/u
, and by definition of
M(x)
,

\displaystyle F(\mathrm{X}(u))

\displaystyle=\mathrm{X}(u)M(\mathrm{X}(u))(1-\omega\mathrm{X}(u)-M(\mathrm{X}(u)))

\displaystyle=\mathrm{X}(u)\mathrm{X}^{+}(u)\mathrm{X}^{-}(u)

\displaystyle\text{by definition of }F,

\displaystyle=\mathrm{X}(u)\mathrm{X}^{-}(u)(1-\omega\mathrm{X}(u)-\mathrm{X}^{-}(u))

by (
75
).

Comparing the first and third lines shows that
M(\mathrm{X}(u))
is either
\mathrm{X}^{-}(u)
or
1-\omega\mathrm{X}(u)-\mathrm{X}^{-}(u)=\mathrm{X}^{+}(u)
. Since
M(\mathrm{X}(u))
is a multiple of
q
, while
\mathrm{X}^{+}(u)
is not, we conclude that

M(\mathrm{X}(u))=\mathrm{X}^{-}(u)
, as stated in (
79
).

Let us now prove the final statement of the proposition. It suffices to prove it for
x
of the form
\mathrm{X}(u)
. We have

\displaystyle F(M(\mathrm{X}(u)))

\displaystyle=F(\mathrm{X}^{-}(u))

\displaystyle\text{by\penalty\ \eqref{MXu}},

\displaystyle=F(\mathrm{X}(\mathrm{S}(u)))

\displaystyle\text{by Lemma\penalty\ \ref{lem:shiftX}},

\displaystyle=\mathrm{X}(\mathrm{S}(u))\left(\mathrm{X}^{-}\mathrm{X}^{+}\right)(\mathrm{S}(u))

\displaystyle\text{by definition of }F,

\displaystyle=\mathrm{X}^{-}(u)\mathrm{X}(u)(1-\mathrm{X}(u)-\omega\mathrm{X}^{-}(u))

\displaystyle\text{by Lemma\penalty\ \ref{lem:shiftX} again},

\displaystyle=\mathrm{X}(u)M(\mathrm{X}(u))(1-\mathrm{X}(u)-\omega M(\mathrm{X}(u))),

\displaystyle\text{by\penalty\ \eqref{MXu} again},

as desired.

6.3.
Determination of
q\equiv q(t)
. End of the proof of Theorem
1.3

We are nearly there! Now let
q
be
any
formal power series in
t
with no constant term, having coefficients in
\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{Q}(\omega)
. Then the series
F(x)
becomes a series in

that satisfies all conditions of Proposition

(for

), except for the last one: we still need

Lemma 6.7

Proposition 7.1

Remark 7.2

We find this result to be reminiscent of an old result of Tutte on properly coloured planar triangulations: when the number of colours is of the form

One could alternatively start from our theta-function expressions to show that

Lemma 7.3

:

–

recursively use the algorithm to sample a patch

with outer degree

,

–

choose a non-negative integer

with probability

,

–

recursively use the algorithm to sample a patch
P_{2}
with outer degree

,

–

construct from

a D-patch

with outer degree
2
and
j
inner digons as described in the proof of Proposition

, uniformly in one of the

possible ways,

–

construct an E-patch

with one outer corner labelled

and
\ell
outer corners labelled
-1
using

as subpatch and

as contracted map, as in the proof of Proposition

(see also Figure

–

finally, construct

in one of two ways from

(with equal probability) as in the proof of Lemma

.

In order to apply this algorithm, the values
p_{j}
and
d_{j}
must first be computed to a sufficient precision.
In fact we will obtain exact values by computing
{\mathcal{P}}(x;t_{c})
explicitly below.
For fixed
t\in[0,1/(4\pi)]
, corresponding to
{\sf R}_{0}\in[0,\frac{1}{16}]
, it follows from (
100
) that we can determine
{\mathcal{P}}
by the integral

\frac{1}{y}{\mathcal{P}}\left(\frac{1}{y}\right)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{\mathcal{C}}\frac{1}{y-x}{\mathcal{M}}(x)dx,

where
\mathcal{C}
is a counterclockwise contour within the annulus where
{\mathcal{M}}(x)
converges and
y
is taken to be outside the contour
\mathcal{C}
. Recall that this annulus shrinks to a circle of radius
y_{c}=1/2
centered at the origin when
t\rightarrow t_{c}
, as discussed below Prediction
5.2
, and we take
\mathcal{C}
to be this circle.
At the critical point,
{\sf R}_{0}=\frac{1}{16}
, and we can compute the above integral exactly:

{\mathcal{P}}(x;t_{c})=\frac{1}{4x}-\frac{2-x}{4\pi x\sqrt{1-x}}\arccos\left(\frac{x}{2-x}\right).

Rearranging this expression yields

[y^{1}]{\mathcal{D}}\left(x,y;t_{c}\right)=\frac{1}{1-x}{\mathcal{P}}\left(\frac{1}{1-x};t_{c}\right)=\frac{1}{4}-\frac{1-2x}{4\pi\sqrt{x(x-1)}}\arccos\left(\frac{1}{1-2x}\right).

Both series
{\mathcal{P}}
and
{\mathcal{D}}
are D-finite, and their
coefficients satisfy linear recurrences of order
2
with polynomial coefficients, which allows one to compute them very quickly. One even obtains closed form expressions: for

,

p_{n}=\frac{1}{\pi 4^{n+1}}\binom{2n}{n-1}\left(2-\frac{\pi}{2}-\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\frac{2^{k}(k-1)!k!}{(2k+1)!}\right)

and

d_{n}=\frac{4^{n}}{2\pi}\frac{(n-1)!(n+1)!}{(2n+1)!}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\frac{(2k+1)!}{2^{k}k!(k+2)!},

with
p_{0}=1/(4\pi)
and
d_{0}=1/4-1/(2\pi)
.

10.
Final remarks

In this section we briefly mention some further directions of research and open questions on labelled quadrangulations.

In the last 20 years, a common direction of study on planar map models has been to consider their geometric properties under either a local limit
[
3
]
or scaling limit
[
42
]
as their size goes to infinity. Understanding the geometry of labelled quadrangulations under either of these limits would be very interesting.

An additional parameter that one can study in labelled quadrangulations is the collection of labels themselves. For the analogous model on the square lattice, with
v=1
and
\omega\geq 0
, there have been several recent advances in this direction
[
25
,
35
,
26
]
.
While a complete understanding of the limiting behaviour of the labels and the underlying map is probably beyond our reach, we suggest below several observables that one may be able to determine using our methods or extensions thereof.

•

The distribution of the label of a uniform random vertex in a uniform random labelled quadrangulation with
n
faces. Indeed, in a subsequent article the second author will derive this distribution for
v=1
and general
\omega\geq 0
.

•

The number of quadrangles in a Boltzmann random patch with a given boundary length (see Section
9.3
)

•

The length of a
level line
, a curve passing through
0
’s in the labelled quadrangulation, or perhaps through edges joining
0
’s to
1
’s. This appears to be particularly natural in E-patches, in connection with the decomposition of E-patches that underlies Proposition
3.9
. If such a level line is suitably defined, one would expect it to converge to a certain Schramm-Loewner evolution, namely SLE(4), as is the case of level lines in the Gaussian free field
[
48
]
.

For each of these observables, one could hope to verify that the limiting distribution is consistent with predictions based on physics, while the dependence of the distribution on
\omega
and
v
could be used to identify phase transitions in the model.

Finally, let us recall that many geometric results on random maps have been proven using bijection with trees, so this aim serves as an additional motivation to find bijections for the results in Sections
9.1
and
9.2
. We note that for the case
\omega=1
, Albenque, Duchi and Schabanel have made progress on finding such a bijection (personal communication).

(because the series
{\mathcal{M}}
has real coefficients).
Writing
r=|x|=|y|
, let
\ell
be the shortest arc of the circle of radius
r
around
0
between
x
and
y
. Then the length of
\ell
is at most
\frac{\pi}{2}|x-y|
(this is equivalent to the inequality
\theta\leq\pi\sin\frac{\theta}{2}
for
0\leq\theta\leq\pi
). Moreover, for
z
on this arc, we have, according to (
109
):

\left|{\mathcal{M}}^{\prime}(z)+\frac{tv}{z^{2}}\right|<\frac{vt}{2|z^{2}|}=\frac{vt}{2r^{2}}.

Hence, taking the integral along this arc, we have

\frac{vt}{2r^{2}}\frac{\pi}{2}|x-y|>\left|\int_{x}^{y}\left({\mathcal{M}}^{\prime}(z)+\frac{tv}{z^{2}}\right)dz\right|=\left|{\mathcal{M}}(y)-{\mathcal{M}}(x)-\frac{tv}{y}+\frac{tv}{x}\right|\\
=\left|\bar{x}-\bar{y}-\frac{tv}{y}+\frac{tv}{x}\right|=|x-y|\left|1+\frac{tv}{r^{2}}\right|,

which is a contradiction (because
v
and
t
are positive and
\pi<4
).
Therefore, for
x\in\partial\Gamma
, we have
{\mathcal{M}}(x)=\overline{x}
. This concludes the proof of
ii)
.

Let us now address Point
iii)
. Since
\partial\Gamma
is in the interior of
A_{4}
, where
{\mathcal{M}}(x)
converges absolutely, this is precisely Cauchy’s formula, as
[x^{-1}]{\mathcal{M}}(x)=tv
.

We now embark on the proof of Point
iv)
.
As a formal power series,
{\mathcal{F}}(x)
is related to
{\mathcal{M}}(x)
by (
28
):

{\mathcal{F}}(x):=(x{\mathcal{M}}(x)-t(v-1))(1-\omega x-{\mathcal{M}}(x)).

Moreover, as already discussed,
{\mathcal{F}}(\sigma;\sigma)
converges absolutely, so
{\mathcal{F}}(x;t)
converges for
|x|<\sigma
.
In particular, for
x\in A_{1}
, the series
{\mathcal{F}}(x;t)
and
{\mathcal{M}}(x;t)
both converge, so they must be related by the equation above. We now define the function
{\mathcal{L}}(x)
, for
|x|<\sigma
, as the discriminant of the above quadratic equation in
{\mathcal{M}}(x)
:

{\mathcal{L}}(x):=\left(x(1-\omega x)-t(v-1)\right)^{2}-4x{\mathcal{F}}(x).

It is also holomorphic for
|x|<\sigma
, and in particular in
\Gamma
.
Moreover, for
x\in A_{1}
, we also have:

(112)

{\mathcal{L}}(x)=\left(x(1-\omega x)+t(v-1)-2x{\mathcal{M}}(x)\right)^{2}.

By the argument principle, the number of roots of
{\mathcal{L}}(x)
in
\Gamma
, counted with multiplicity, is

\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{\vec{\partial\Gamma}}\frac{{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}(x)}{{\mathcal{L}}(x)}dx,

assuming that
{\mathcal{L}}
does not vanish on the contour, which we will now check. Writing
{\mathcal{K}}(x)=1-\omega x+\frac{t(v-1)}{x}-2{\mathcal{M}}(x)
, we have
{\mathcal{L}}(x)=x^{2}{\mathcal{K}}(x)^{2}
and for
x\in{\partial\Gamma}\subset A_{4}
, we have
|{\mathcal{M}}(x)|=|x|
, so
|1-{\mathcal{K}}(x)|<|\omega|\sigma^{4}+|v-1|\sigma^{4}+2\sigma^{4}<1
, where the second inequality is due to (
107
). In particular,
{\mathcal{L}}
does not vanish on
\partial\Gamma
, and moreover the function
\log({\mathcal{K}}(x))
, defined with the principal value of
\log
, is analytic on a neighborhood of
\partial\Gamma
. Therefore

(113)

\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{\vec{\partial\Gamma}}\frac{{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}(x)}{{\mathcal{L}}(x)}dx=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{\vec{\partial\Gamma}}\left(\frac{2}{x}+\frac{2{\mathcal{K}}^{\prime}(x)}{{\mathcal{K}}(x)}\right)dx=2.

Hence
{\mathcal{L}}(x)
has exactly two roots in
\Gamma
, counted with multiplicity.
We will now show that these lie on the real line. To do this we note that
0
and
\sigma^{-2}t
belong to
\Gamma
, and we will show that
{\mathcal{L}}(0),{\mathcal{L}}({\sigma^{-2}t})\geq 0
while
0<(v+1)t<{\sigma^{-2}t}
and
{\mathcal{L}}((v+1)t)<0
, as then it follows from the intermediate value theorem that
{\mathcal{L}}(x)
has roots
c_{0}\in[0,(v+1)t)
and
c_{1}\in((v+1)t,{\sigma^{-2}t}]
, since for
x\in\mathbb{R}\cap\Gamma
we have
{\mathcal{L}}(x)\in\mathbb{R}
.
The first three inequalities are fairly simple:
{\mathcal{L}}(0)=t^{2}(v-1)^{2}\geq 0
, while
{\sigma^{-2}t}\in A_{1}
where (
112
) holds, so
{\mathcal{L}}({\sigma^{-2}t})\geq 0
. The inequality
0<(v+1)t<{\sigma^{-2}t}
follows from (
107
). Finally, combining the definition of
{\mathcal{L}}
in terms of
{\mathcal{F}}
with (
110
) yields

{\mathcal{L}}((v+1)t)\leq(2t-\omega t^{2}(v+1)^{2})^{2}-4t(v+1)\left(t-\frac{vt}{2(v+1)}\right)=t^{2}\left(\left(2-\omega t(v+1)^{2}\right)^{2}-4-2v\right)

(note that (
110
) applies because
t(v+1)<\sigma
).
Then the fact that
{\mathcal{L}}((v+1)t)<0
follows from
|\omega t(v+1)^{2}|<\sigma^{6}
and
(2+\sigma^{6})^{2}<4+2v
, which both follow from (
107
) and
t<\sigma^{9}
. Hence by the intermediate value theorem,
{\mathcal{L}}(x)
has roots
c_{0}\in[0,(v+1)t)
and
c_{1}\in((v+1)t,{\sigma^{-2}t}]
, so these are the only two roots of
{\mathcal{L}}(x)
in
\Gamma
(see Figure
21
, right). This completes the proof of
iv)
.

We now show that
{\mathcal{M}}(x)
, which we have so far proved to be analytic in
A_{1}
, has a meromorphic continuation to
\Gamma\setminus[c_{0},c_{1}]
. From (
112
), it suffices to show that
{\mathcal{L}}(x)
has a holomorphic square root in
\Gamma\setminus[c_{0},c_{1}]
which extends

(114)

\sqrt{{\mathcal{L}}(x)}:=x(1-\omega x)+t(v-1)-2x{\mathcal{M}}(x),

defined on
A_{1}
. We apply Lemma
B.2
below with
\Omega=\Gamma\setminus[c_{0},c_{1}]
,
\Pi=\Gamma\cap A_{1}
,
h={\mathcal{L}}
and
s
the right-hand side of (
114
). This lemma implies that
such a square root exists if and only if for any smooth closed curve
\gamma
in
\Gamma\setminus[c_{0},c_{1}]
,

\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{\gamma}\frac{{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}(x)}{{\mathcal{L}}(x)}dx\in 2\mathbb{Z}.

This holds in our case because the left-hand side
is
2
when
\gamma=\vec{\partial\Gamma}
(see (
113
)) and the integrand
has no singularities in
\Gamma\setminus[c_{0},c_{1}]
, so the left-hand side above is
twice the number of times
\gamma
winds counterclockwise around the cut
[c_{0},c_{1}]
. We thus conclude that
\sqrt{{\mathcal{L}}(x)}
extends analytically to
\Gamma\setminus[c_{0},c_{1}]
.

Since
{\mathcal{L}}(x)
has roots at
c_{0}
and
c_{1}
, the function
\sqrt{{\mathcal{L}}(x)}
must have branch cuts at these points. Moreover,
{\mathcal{L}}(x)
has no other roots in
\Gamma
, so
\sqrt{{\mathcal{L}}(x)}
has no other branch cuts in
\Gamma
. This implies that for
x_{c}\in[c_{0},c_{1}]
, the limit of
\sqrt{{\mathcal{L}}(x)}
as
x
approaches
x_{c}
from above the cut and the same limit as
x
approaches
x_{c}
from below the cut must sum to
0
. The relation between
\sqrt{{\mathcal{L}}(x)}
and
{\mathcal{M}}(x)
implies

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}{\mathcal{M}}(x+i\varepsilon)+{\mathcal{M}}(x-i\varepsilon)=1-\omega x+\frac{t(v-1)}{x}.

This completes the proof of
vi)
, and of the whole proposition.

Below we give an elementary proof of the result used at the end of the last proof about the existence of an analytic square root of a given analytic function.
We found an alternative explanation using algebraic topology in a blog post by Evan Chen
[
22
]
(see Theorem 2). We searched several textbooks for this result, but were not able to find it. Nonetheless, we expect it exists somewhere.

Lemma B.2
.

Let
\Pi\subset\Omega\subset\mathbb{C}
be path connected regions and let
h:\Omega\to\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
and
s:\Pi\to\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
be an analytic functions satisfying
s(z)^{2}=h(z)
for
z\in\Pi
. Then
s
extends to an analytic function on
\Omega
satisfying
s(z)^{2}=h(z)
for
z\in\Omega
if and only if, for every smooth closed curve
\gamma
in
\Omega
, the following holds

(115)

\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{\gamma}\frac{h^{\prime}(z)}{h(z)}\in 2\mathbb{Z}.

Proof.

We first prove the following: for any
x,y\in\Omega
we have

(116)

h(y)=h(x)\exp\left(\int_{x}^{y}\frac{h^{\prime}(z)}{h(z)}dz\right),

where the integral is taken along any smooth curve
\rho:[0,1]\to\Omega
with
\rho(0)=x
and
\rho(1)=y
(the integral is well defined as
h
does not vanish in
\Omega
). Writing
\xi(u):=h(\rho(u))
and
\beta(u):=\int_{0}^{u}\frac{\xi^{\prime}(v)}{\xi(v)}dv
for
u\in[0,1]
, the above equation is equivalent to

\xi(1)=\xi(0)\exp\left(\beta(1)\right).

From the definition of
\beta(u)
, we have immediately
\beta^{\prime}(u)=\frac{\xi^{\prime}(u)}{\xi(u)}
, which implies that

\frac{d}{du}\left(\xi(u)e^{-\beta(u)}\right)=\xi^{\prime}(u)e^{-\beta(u)}-\xi(u)\beta^{\prime}(u)e^{-\beta(u)}=0.

Hence
\xi(u)e^{-\beta(u)}
is a constant for
u\in[0,1]
, and, in particular

\xi(1)e^{-\beta(1)}=\xi(0)e^{-\beta(0)}=\xi(0),

as required.

We will now use (
116
) to prove the lemma. First, if
s(z)
does extend to
\Omega
, we can apply (
116
) with
h
replaced by
s
. Applying this to a smooth closed curve
\gamma
starting and ending at some point
x\in\Omega
then dividing both sides by
s(x)=s(y)
yields

1=\exp\left(\oint_{\gamma}\frac{s^{\prime}(z)}{s(z)}dz\right)=\exp\left(\oint_{\gamma}\frac{h^{\prime}(z)}{2h(z)}dz\right).

Hence

\oint_{\gamma}\frac{h^{\prime}(z)}{2h(z)}dz\in 2\pi i\mathbb{Z},

as required.

For the other direction, assume that (
115
) holds for any smooth closed curve
\gamma
in
\Omega
. Fix
x\in\Pi
. By (
116
), with
h
replaced by
s
and
\Omega
replaced by
\Pi
, for any
y\in\Pi
we have

(117)

s(y)=s(x)\exp\left(\int_{x}^{y}\frac{s^{\prime}(z)}{s(z)}dz\right)=s(x)\exp\left(\int_{x}^{y}\frac{h^{\prime}(z)}{2h(z)}dz\right),

where the integral may be along any smooth curve in
\Pi
from
x
to
y
. For

y\in\Omega
, let us now define
\tilde{s}(y)
by

\tilde{s}(y):=s(x)\exp\left(\int_{x}^{y}\frac{h^{\prime}(z)}{2h(z)}dz\right),

where the integral is taken along any smooth curve in
\Omega
from
x
to
y
. Note that by (
115
), any two integrals for the same points
x
and
y
differ by a multiple of
2\pi i
, so the value of
\tilde{s}(y)
defined above does not depend on the chosen curve from
x
to
y
. Hence this defines an analytic function
\tilde{s}
on
\Omega
which coincides with the original function
s
on
\Pi
(see (
117
)). Finally squaring both sides in the definition of
\tilde{s}(y)
and comparing to (
116
) yields
\tilde{s}(y)^{2}=h(y)
, as required.

We will now parametrise the domain
\Gamma\setminus[c_{0},c_{1}]
using the following classical result (see for example
[
36
, Chap. 5, Sec. 1]
for an equivalent statement with “cylinder” replaced by “annulus”).

Theorem B.3
.

Any doubly connected domain
B
other than the punctured disk and punctured plane is conformally equivalent to some cylinder
{\mathcal{C}}:=\{x+ciy:x\in\mathbb{R},y\in(0,1)\}/\pi\mathbb{Z}
with
c>0
. Moreover, the value of
c
is uniquely determined by
B
. The function
\chi
mapping this cylinder onto
B
is uniquely determined once we choose the point
\lim_{z\rightarrow 0}\chi(z)
on the boundary of
B
.

We shall apply this to the domain
B:=\Gamma\setminus[c_{0},c_{1}]
, with
\chi(0)=c_{0}
. See Figure
21
for an illustration of the parametrising function
\chi
, with
\gamma=2ic
. We will extend
\chi
meromorphically from the cylinder to
\mathbb{C}
.
The characterisation of
{\mathcal{M}}(x)
, once rewritten at
x=\chi(z)
, will translate into the following simpler characterisation of
\chi
, which justifies and generalises (to the case
v\neq 1
) the Ansatz of Section
6.1
.

Proposition B.4
.

Fix
v,\omega\in\mathbb{R}
with
v>0
. For
t>0
, sufficiently small, there is a value
\gamma\in i\mathbb{R}_{>0}
and a meromorphic function
\chi:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}
satisfying the following equations for
z\in\mathbb{C}
:

\displaystyle\chi(z)

\displaystyle=\chi(z+\pi),

\displaystyle\chi(z)

\displaystyle=\chi(-z),

\displaystyle\chi(\gamma+z)+\omega\chi(z)+\chi(z-\gamma)

\displaystyle=1+\frac{t(v-1)}{\chi(z)},

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\pi}\chi\left(\frac{\gamma}{2}-z\right)\chi^{\prime}\left(\frac{\gamma}{2}+z\right)dz

\displaystyle=-2{\pi i}tv.

Moreover,
\chi
has one root and no poles in the closed cylinder

\overline{{\mathcal{C}}}:=\{x+y\gamma/2:x\in\mathbb{R},y\in[0,1]\}/\pi\mathbb{Z}
, while
\chi(z)\chi(\gamma-z)
has no poles in this region.

Finally, for
z
in the strip
{\mathcal{S}}:=\{x+y\gamma/2:x\in\mathbb{R},y\in(0,1)\}
, we have

(118)

{\mathcal{M}}(\chi(z))=\chi(\gamma-z).

Proof.

The region
B:=\Gamma\setminus[c_{0},c_{1}]
is doubly connected, and it is neither
the punctured disk nor the punctured plane, so by Theorem
B.3
, there is a unique value
\gamma\in i\mathbb{R}_{>0}
(where
\gamma=2ic
in the notation of the theorem) and a unique bijective conformal map

\chi
from the cylinder
{\mathcal{C}}:={\mathcal{S}}/\pi\mathbb{Z}
to
\Gamma

satisfying
\chi(0)=c_{0}
when
\chi
is extended by continuity to the closed cylinder
\overline{{\mathcal{C}}}
(which is
\overline{{\mathcal{S}}}/\pi\mathbb{Z}
).
The map
\chi
sends the top boundary
(\mathbb{R}+\gamma/2)/\pi\mathbb{Z}
of the cylinder
bijectively to
\partial\Gamma
.
The bottom boundary
\mathbb{R}/\pi\mathbb{Z}
is sent to
[c_{0},c_{1}]
, with
c_{0}
and
c_{1}
each having a unique inverse while each point in
(c_{0},c_{1})
has two inverses accounting for the limits coming from above and below. Thus
\chi
is 1-to-1 from
\overline{{\mathcal{C}}}\setminus(0,\pi)
to

\overline{\Gamma}\setminus(c_{0},c_{1})
,
and
2
-to-
1
from
(0,\pi)
to
(c_{0},c_{1})
.
In particular, since
c_{0}\geq 0
, (see Proposition
B.1

iv)
) the function
\chi
has a unique root in
\overline{{\mathcal{C}}}
, as claimed in the proposition. The fact that it has no pole is obvious, since the closed cylinder is sent by
\chi
to
\overline{\Gamma}
.

For convenience we will now see
\chi
as a function on
\overline{{\mathcal{S}}}\subset\mathbb{C}
rather than
\overline{{\mathcal{C}}}=\overline{{\mathcal{S}}}/\pi\mathbb{Z}
, satisfying
\chi(z)=\chi(z+\pi)
. We notice that the function sending
z
to
\overline{\chi(-\overline{z})}
is also a
\pi

{\mathcal{S}}
, and a conformal map from
{\mathcal{C}}
to
\Gamma
(which is closed by conjugation) sending

to
c_{0}
.
By Theorem
B.3
this function must actually be equal to
\chi
, that is,

(119)

\chi(-\overline{z})=\overline{\chi(z)}\qquad\text{ for all }z\in\overline{{\mathcal{S}}}.

In particular, any point
z\in\mathbb{R}
is sent by
\chi
to a point in
[c_{0},c_{1}]
, and then we have

\chi(-z)=\chi(z)=\chi(\pi-z),

which implies in particular that
\chi(\pi/2)=c_{1}
.
Then, for
z\in\mathbb{R}+\gamma/2
, we have

(120)

{\mathcal{M}}(\chi(\gamma-z))={\mathcal{M}}(\chi(-\overline{z}))={\mathcal{M}}\left(\overline{\chi(z)}\right)=\chi(z),

where we have first used the fact that
\gamma-z=-\overline{z}
, then the identity (
119
) on
\chi
, and finally Proposition
B.1

ii)
.

\chi(z)

\chi(z)=\chi(-z)\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \text{and}\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ {\mathcal{M}}(\chi(z))=\chi(\gamma-z).

In the case
