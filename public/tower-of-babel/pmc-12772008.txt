Efficacy and safety of lidocaine versus sufentanil in patient-controlled intravenous analgesia after laparoscopic colorectal cancer surgery: a prospective, randomized, double-blind clinical trial
Authors: Yumin Wu, Bin Tian, Lewen Li, Ruijuan Liu, Zhenying Zhang, Wenjie Zhang, Song Qu, Wenjun Yan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study aimed to compare the efficacy and safety of lidocaine-based patient-controlled intravenous analgesia (PCIA) with sufentanil-based PCIA for managing postoperative pain in patients undergoing laparoscopic colorectal cancer surgery.MethodsA total of 126 patients were randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio to lidocaine group (group L, n = 63) or the sufentanil group (group S, n = 63). The primary endpoint was visual analogue scale (VAS) pain score during movement at 24 h after surgery. Secondary endpoints included VAS pain scores at rest and during movement at 6, 12, 36, and 48 h after surgery, the time of first flatus and defecation after the surgery, postoperative hospitalization time and total hospitalization costs, patient satisfaction scores, and the incidence of adverse events.ResultsThere was no significant difference between the two groups in VAS pain scores at rest and during movement at 6, 12, 24, 36, and 48 h after surgery (all p > 0.05). The time to first flatus and defecation, as well as postoperative hospitalization time and total hospitalization expenses, were also similar between the two groups (all p > 0.05). However, satisfaction score was significantly higher in group L compared to group S (p < 0.05), and the incidence of postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV) was significantly lower in group L than in group S (p < 0.05).ConclusionsLidocaine-based PCIA can effectively relieve postoperative pain, decrease the incidence of PONV, and improve patient satisfaction in patients undergoing laparoscopic colorectal cancer surgery.Trials registrationThe study was registered on the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR) with the registration number ChiCTR2300068854, with the registration date being March 1, 2023.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12871-025-03506-z.Keywords: Lidocaine, Colorectal neoplasms, Patient-Controlled analgesia, Postoperative period

Introduction

Radical tumor resection is the primary treatment for improving the prognosis and survival rate of patients with colorectal cancer [1]. However, approximately 70.8% of patients experience moderate-to-severe pain within 5 h after surgery, and the incidence remains as high as 56.67% on the following day [2]. Pain after laparoscopic colorectal surgery involves multiple components, including somatic, visceral, referred shoulder, and possible neuropathic pain [3, 4]. Inadequate pain control increases the incidence of postoperative complications, prolongs hospitalization stays, and significantly reduces patient satisfaction [5]. Current analgesic modalities after colorectal surgery include patient-controlled epidural analgesia (PCEA), patient-controlled intravenous analgesia (PCIA), and nerve block analgesia [6, 7]. PCEA may impair intestinal function recovery and is associated with a high incidence of urinary retention compared with PCIA, and is therefore no longer recommended to use it routinely for pain management after colorectal cancer surgery [8, 9]. Nerve blocks are an emerging analgesic modality in recent years, but using only a single block may result in incomplete analgesia and short duration of pain relief, limiting their widespread application in colorectal surgery [10].

PCIA offers several advantages, including rapid onset, relatively stable blood concentration, timely relief of breakthrough pain through patient control, and high patient satisfaction, making it an ideal analgesic method following colorectal cancer surgery [11]. Opioids remain the principal analgesics used in PCIA, but their use is associated with adverse events such as postoperative nausea and vomiting, respiratory depression, pruritus, and intestinal obstruction, which hinder early recovery and increase healthcare burden [11, 12]. These adverse events hinder early recovery, prolong hospital stay, and increase patients’ economic burden. Therefore, there is an urgent clinical need to identify safe and effective analgesics with fewer side effects.

Lidocaine is a commonly used amide local anesthetic in clinical practice, which can inhibit the activation of sodium ion channels in nerve cells, block nerve pain signals, and significantly alleviate pain [13]. In recent years, it has been widely recognized that intravenous injection of lidocaine can alleviate peripheral neuropathic pain, hyperalgesia, and complex local pain syndrome [14]. Numerous clinical have demonstrated that perioperative intravenous lidocaine infusion provides analgesic and anti-inflammatory effects and promotes recovery of intestinal function [15–18]. Current evidence indicates that the safe intraoperative infusion dose is generally 1–2 mg/kg/h [18–22], with a duration of up to 24 h, 48 h, or even longer [23–25].

Given clinical considerations, postoperative intravenous lidocaine infusion may offer additional benefits in pain control and intestinal recovery for patients undergoing laparoscopic colorectal cancer surgery. However, the efficacy and safety of prolonged postoperative lidocaine infusion via PCIA remain unclear. To our knowledge, this study is the first to evaluate lidocaine-based PCIA in this patient population. Pain during movement (turning in bed or ambulation) is the most clinically relevant endpoint after laparoscopic colorectal surgery, as early mobilization that prevents venous thromboembolism, accelerates first flatus/defecation, and shortens hospital stay. We therefore selected visual analogue scale (VAS) during active movement at 24 h as the primary objective. Secondary objectives included evaluation of pain at other time points, PCIA usage, rescue analgesia requirements, gastrointestinal recovery, patient satisfaction, hospitalization parameters, inflammatory markers, serum lidocaine concentrations, and the incidence of adverse events.

Methods

Study design and patients

This single-center, randomized, double-blind clinical trial was conducted to evaluate the efficacy and safety of postoperative lidocaine-based PCIA, with the hypothesis that it would improve pain control and facilitate intestinal recovery. A total of 126 patients who underwent laparoscopic colorectal cancer surgery at an academic hospital were enrolled between March and October 2023. All procedures were conducted in accordance with the Helsinki Declaration and adhered to the Enhanced Recovery After Surgery (ERAS) protocol, emphasizing opioid-sparing and opioid-free analgesia strategies. The study protocol was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Gansu Provincial Hospital (No. 2022 − 440) and registered at the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR) under the number ChiCTR230006885. All patients provided written informed consent prior to surgery, and their personal information was kept confidential.

Eligible patients were 18 to 65 years old, had a body mass index (BMI) of 18–28 kg/m2, were classified as American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status I or II, and were scheduled for laparoscopic colorectal tumor resection under general anesthesia. Exclusion criteria included heart rate < 50 beats/min, severe atrioventricular block, hepatic or renal insufficiency, cardiac function grade 3–4, uncontrolled hypertension, history of lidocaine allergy, conversion from laparoscopy to open surgery, and withdrawal from the PCIA pump during the study period. In addition, patients weighing more than 80 kg were excluded due to the capacity limits of the PCIA pump and patient safety considerations.

Patients were randomized 1:1 to either the lidocaine-based PCIA group (group L) or the sufentanil-based PCIA group (group S) using a simple randomization method. The random allocation sequence was generated by an independent investigator using a computer-generated random table (http://www.Randomization.com). Allocation concealment was ensured by using sequentially numbered, sealed, opaque envelopes, which were opened only after patient enrollment. The study drugs were prepared by anesthesia nurses who were not involved in data collection and analysis. Both PCIA solutions were identical in appearance, volume, and labeling to ensure blinding. All eligible patients, anesthesiologists, surgeons, anesthesia nurses, blood specimen collection personnel, and data collection and analysis personnel were blinded throughout the trial.

Anesthesia and intervention procedures

Upon arrival in the operating room, patients were routinely monitored using electrocardiogram, non-invasive arterial blood pressure, pulse oxygen saturation, and bispectral index (BIS). Anesthesia was induced with midazolam 0.05 mg/kg, sufentanil 0.5 µg/kg, propofol 2.0 mg/kg, and rocuronium 0.6 mg/kg. Following tracheal intubation, mechanical ventilation was initiated with an oxygen flow rate of 2 L/min, a tidal volume of 8–10 ml/kg, and a respiratory rate of 12 breaths/min, in order to maintain end-expiratory carbon dioxide partial pressure of 35–45 mmHg. Anesthesia was maintained with sevoflurane, propofol, remifentanil, and cisatracurium, with BIS values maintained between 40 and 60, and the blood pressure and heart rate kept within 20% of the baseline values. If the heart rate dropped below 50 beats/min, 0.5 mg of atropine was administered intravenously; if systolic blood pressure fell below 90 mmHg, 6 mg of ephedrine was given intravenously. Thirty minutes before the end of surgery, 50 mg of flurbiprofen axetil was administered intravenously. Postoperative analgesia was provided using a PCIA pump.

For group L, the PCIA solution was prepared by calculating the total lidocaine as 1.5 mg/kg/h × 48 h and dissolving this amount in 0.9% saline to a total volume of 300 ml [18–26]. For group S, the PCIA solution was prepared by dissolving sufentanil 2.0 µg/kg (as referenced in previous studies [13, 24, 27]) in 0.9% saline to a total volume of 300 ml. The PCIA pump settings were identical between the two groups; all PCIA pumps were administered continuously for 48 h postoperatively. The PCIA pump was programmed with the following parameters: a background infusion rate of 6 ml/h, a patient-controlled bolus dose of 2 ml, and a lockout interval of 20 min. According to the patient’s body weight and the reservoir concentration, the background infusion delivered lidocaine at a rate of 1.44 mg/kg/h and sufentanil at a rate of 0.04 µg/kg/h.

After surgery, if patients experienced pain, they were instructed to press the PCIA button as needed until adequate pain relief was achieved. If the VAS pain score remained ≥ 4 despite patient-controlled analgesia, 100 mg of tramadol was administered intramuscularly as rescue analgesia, given its rapid onset, minimal risk of respiratory depression, and proven efficacy in our preliminary trial where post-rescue VAS scores were consistently ≤ 3. For postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV), 8 mg of ondansetron was given intravenously.

Serological testing

For safety monitoring, all patients were observed postoperatively for the occurrence of adverse events. In the lidocaine group, venous blood samples were collected at 24 and 48 h after surgery, and the serum lidocaine concentration was measured using liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS). If a patient exhibited symptoms of systemic local anesthetic toxicity—such as dizziness, drowsiness, blurred vision, tinnitus, perioral or tongue numbness, convulsion, or arrhythmia—the infusion was discontinued immediately. Additionally, a 20% fat emulsion was prepared in advance to manage potential severe local anesthetic toxicity.

Venous blood samples were also collected from both groups preoperatively, as well as at 24 and 48 h postoperatively. The serum levels of C-reactive protein (CRP), interleukin-6 (IL-6), and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) were measured using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA). All blood samples were collected by ward nurses who were blinded to group allocation.

Endpoints

The primary endpoint was VAS (0–10 cm, where 0 indicates no pain and 10 indicates the most severe pain) pain score during movement (turning over or getting out of bed activity) at 24 h after surgery. Secondary endpoints included VAS pain scores at rest and during movement (turning over or getting out of bed activity) at 6, 12, 36, and 48 h after surgery; the effective compression times of PCIA between 0 and 24 h and between 24 and 48 h after surgery; incidence of salvage analgesia within 0 to 24 h and 24 to 48 h postoperatively; time of first flatus and defecation following surgery; patient satisfaction was assessed using the Likert scale (0–10, where 0 indicates “entirely unsatisfied” and 10 indicates “fully satisfied”) at 48 h after surgery; postoperative hospitalization time and total hospitalization expenses; levels of inflammatory markers (CRP, IL-6, and TNF-α) measured preoperatively, and at 24 and 48 h postoperatively; serum concentration of lidocaine at 24 and 48 h after surgery; and the incidence of adverse reactions (such as PONV, delirium, lethargy, dizziness, tinnitus, metallic taste, seizure, intractable hypotension, and bradycardia) within 48 h postoperatively.

Statistical analysis

Sample size calculation was performed using the “repeated measures analysis” module in PASS version 2021 software. According to a preliminary study involving 16 patients in each group, the mean VAS pain scores during movement at 6, 12, 24, 36, 48 h postoperatively were 2.75, 2.19, 2.06, 1.88, and 1.56 in the group L, and 2.69, 2.38, 2.19, 1.75, 1.56 in the group S, respectively. With a significance level (α) of 0.05 and power (1-β) of 80%, the estimated required sample size was 50 patients per group. Taking into account an anticipated 20% loss to follow-up, 63 patients were needed for each group. Therefore, a total of 126 patients were included in this study.

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and GraphPad Prism version 9.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). The Shapiro-Wilk test and Q-Q plot were used to evaluate the normality of the data distribution. Continuous variables with a normal distribution are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and between-group comparisons were conducted using an independent samples t-test when the variance was homogeneous. Non-normally distributed continuous variables are expressed as median (interquartile range, IQR), and the Mann-Whitney U test was used for between-group comparisons. Categorical variables are reported as counts and percentages [n (%)], and group comparisons were performed using the χ² test. A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 140 patients were initially screened for eligibility. Of these, 11 patients did not meet the inclusion criteria, and three patients refused to participate. Consequently, 126 patients were randomly into two groups from March 2023 to October 2023 to receive the assigned interventions. During the study, six patients were excluded due to conversion to laparotomy or discontinuation of the analgesia pump. Finally, 120 patients completed the study and were included in the per protocol analysis. A detailed flow chart is shown in Fig. 1. There were no statistically significant differences in the baseline characteristics of patients between the two groups, including gender, age, BMI, ASA physical status, surgical procedure, and duration of surgery (all p > 0.05) (Table 1).

Discussion

This randomized, double-blind study demonstrated that lidocaine-based PCIA was as effective as sufentanil-based PCIA in relieving postoperative pain in patients undergoing laparoscopic colorectal cancer surgery. VAS scores, bowel function recovery, and hospitalization outcomes were comparable between groups, while patient satisfaction was higher and PONV incidence lower in the lidocaine group.

Importantly, lidocaine-based PCIA provided mild opioid or even opioid-free analgesia, supporting its role as a promising strategy in postoperative pain management. Furthermore, this strategy aligns with the principles of ERAS, which advocate for opioid-sparing methods to promote optimal patient recovery. Intravenous lidocaine has been reported to confer several perioperative benefits, including effective analgesia, attenuation of the surgical stress response, suppression of systemic inflammation, reduction of opioid requirements, and shortened hospital stays [28–31]. Consistent with these findings, the present study showed that lidocaine-based PCIA achieved comparable analgesic efficacy to sufentanil-based PCIA, while significantly reducing postoperative inflammatory markers (CRP, IL-6, and TNF-α). In addition, patients in the lidocaine group experienced a lower incidence of PONV and reported higher satisfaction. Together, these results indicate that PCIA with lidocaine may provide outcomes comparable to continuous intravenous infusion, while maintaining the advantage of patient-controlled administration. Notably, patients in the lidocaine group had, on average, approximately 1,000 RMB (Renminbi, the official Chinese currency) lower total hospitalization costs (including all expenses from admission to discharge) compared to those in the sufentanil group, indicating potential economic benefits, although this difference did not reach statistical significance.

We hypothesize that lidocaine may control postoperative pain in colorectal cancer surgery via multiple mechanisms. First, surgical trauma induces a systemic stress response and promotes the release of inflammatory mediators, including CRP, IL-6, and TNF-α [32, 33]. These inflammatory mediators accumulate locally in the intestine, causing intestinal wall edema, intestinal dilatation, and visceral pain [34]. In this study, patients receiving lidocaine-based PCIA had significantly lower serum levels of these inflammatory markers at 24 and 48 h post-surgery, suggesting that lidocaine may inhibit the release of inflammatory factors, attenuate inflammatory response, and thus reduce visceral pain caused by colorectal distension. Second, heightened visceral pain hypersensitivity is mediated by spinal N-methyl-D-aspartate (NMDA) receptors [35], and lidocaine has been shown to inhibit the NMDA receptors expression, thus alleviating pain [1, 5]. Finally, studies have shown that Nav1.8 sodium channels are involved in the formation and maintenance of acute postoperative hyperalgesia in incision pain model rats [36, 37]. As an amide local anesthetic, lidocaine can inhibit the activation of sodium channels (including Nav1.8) in nerve cells, block the transmission of nerve pain signals, and relieve incision pain [13, 38].

The effect of lidocaine on gastrointestinal function recovery has been variably reported. Herzog et al. reported that lidocaine did not significantly reduce the time to first flatus and defecation in patients [39], whereas the study by Dai et al. suggests that intravenous lidocaine can promote earlier flatus and defecation, thereby improving postoperative intestinal function [13]. This discrepancy may be related to surgical site differences and the profound impairment of gastrointestinal function in patients with colorectal cancer. In this study, no significant differences were observed between lidocaine-based PCIA and sufentanil-based PCIA regarding time to first flatus or defecation. The impact of lidocaine-based PCIA on gastrointestinal recovery remains uncertain and warrants further investigation.

The therapeutic plasma concentration of lidocaine is generally considered to range from 1.5 to 5 µg/ml, with toxic reactions typically occurring at concentrations of approximately 9–10 µg/ml [24, 26, 40, 41]. Considering the safety of patients, we monitored the serum lidocaine concentration at 24 and 48 h postoperatively. In this study, four patients experienced mild adverse effects, including dizziness, drowsiness, and transient bradycardia; all symptoms resolved following discontinuation of the lidocaine infusion. The serum lidocaine concentrations in these four patients were 2.7, 2.3, 1.7, and 2.1 µg/ml at 24 h postoperatively, and 3.9, 4.5, 2.7, and 3.4 µg/ml at 48 h postoperatively. Notably, all values remained within the established therapeutic range for safe serum concentrations.

This study has several limitations. First, we only monitored serum lidocaine concentrations at 24 and 48 h postoperatively, which may not accurately reflect the dynamics of serum concentrations. Second, all patients received flurbiprofen axetil (a nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drug) 30 min before the end of the operation. The anti-inflammatory effects of flurbiprofen may have confounded the assessment of lidocaine’s impact on inflammatory marker levels. Therefore, the specific anti-inflammatory effect of lidocaine requires further investigation. Third, midazolam was used for induction because of its anxiolytic and anterograde amnesic effects, which help prevent intraoperative awareness. However, potential cognitive effects, particularly in elderly patients, were not considered, which represents a limitation of this study. Fourth, the cumulative dose of intramuscular tramadol was not recorded, which limits the evaluation of its total analgesic contribution. Fifth, although a statistically significant difference in patient satisfaction scores was observed between the two groups, the minimal clinically important difference for the 10-point Likert satisfaction scale has not been established, and thus the clinical significance of this difference remains uncertain. Sixth, a composite recovery scale such as the QoR-15 could have provided a more comprehensive assessment of postoperative recovery; however, it was not used because the QoR-15 includes a pain domain, and patient satisfaction was instead used as a surrogate measure. Finally, this was a single-center clinical study with a limited number of patients, and further expansion of the sample size is needed to validate our findings.

Conclusion

Lidocaine-based PCIA can effectively relieve postoperative pain, decrease the incidence of PONV, and improve patient satisfaction in patients undergoing laparoscopic colorectal cancer surgery.
