Safety and Effectiveness of Embolization via the Ophthalmic Artery with N-butyl Cyanoacrylate for Anterior Skull Base Meningiomas
Authors: Takeru HIRATA, Satoshi KOIZUMI, Hideaki ONO, Satoru MIYAWAKI, Shigeta FUJITANI, Motoyuki UMEKAWA, Nobuhito SAITO
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Anterior skull base meningiomas are often supplied by ethmoidal arteries branching from the ophthalmic artery. Preoperative embolization can reduce intraoperative bleeding and facilitate safer resection; however, its use is limited by the risk of visual complications. This study evaluates the safety, effectiveness, and technical considerations of embolization via the ophthalmic artery using n-butyl-2-cyanoacrylate (n-BCA). This retrospective study included patients with meningiomas who underwent preoperative embolization via the ophthalmic artery between 2017 and 2025. Demographics, clinical characteristics, technical details, surgical outcomes, and complications were analyzed. Five patients (median age of 58 years) underwent embolization. Two meningiomas were at the olfactory groove, two at the planum sphenoidale, and one at the sphenoid ridge (median diameter of 41.9 mm). Unilateral embolization was performed in four patients; one underwent bilateral embolization. The working angle was set at an anterior oblique and cranial position to optimize visualization of the central retinal and ethmoidal arteries. The microcatheter was advanced beyond the central retinal artery to cannulate the ethmoidal artery and to position it near the tumor. N-BCA, heated to 80°C, was used at concentrations ranging from 20% to 33%. All patients showed reduced tumor blush without neurological complications, including vision loss or impairment. Simpson grade II-III resection was achieved in all patients, with a median intraoperative blood loss of 350 mL. One patient demonstrated postoperative improvement in the visual field defects. Embolization via the ophthalmic artery can be safely performed to facilitate the resection of skull base meningiomas using standardized techniques and n-BCA.

Introduction

Meningiomas are often highly vascular benign neoplasms, and their surgical resection is frequently complicated by significant blood loss.1,2) Preoperative embolization of meningiomas has been shown to effectively reduce intraoperative blood loss, shorten surgery duration, and decrease the need for blood transfusion.3,4) However, despite these advantages, its use remains controversial because of the risk of ischemic and hemorrhagic complications.5,6)

The ethmoidal arteries can serve as the primary blood supply for anterior skull base meningiomas.7,8) These arteries, which branch from the ophthalmic artery, consist of the anterior and posterior ethmoidal arteries, and supply blood to both the orbit and the anterior skull base. Given their vascular contribution, the ethmoidal arteries are potential targets for preoperative embolization in anterior skull base meningiomas.7) However, because they arise directly from the ophthalmic artery, embolization poses a risk of visual complications such as blindness.9)

Although a few small case series have reported outcomes, the safety and effectiveness of embolization via the ophthalmic artery remain uncertain.10-13) There are few comprehensive case reports regarding preoperative tumor embolization via the ethmoidal artery, including those detailing the technical procedures.

This study aims to share our experience and to evaluate the safety and effectiveness of ethmoidal artery embolization in anterior skull base meningiomas, with the goal of establishing protocols that could improve surgical outcomes and reduce hemorrhagic complications in anterior skull base meningiomas.

Materials and Methods

Patient selection and indication for embolization via the ophthalmic artery

This study was approved by the institutional review board of our hospital (approval number #2231) and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent, including consent for participation in this study and for the publication of images, was obtained from the patients. We conducted a retrospective cohort study of consecutive patients with meningioma who underwent tumor embolization as a preoperative procedure prior to surgical resection at our institution from 2017 to 2025. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) endovascular tumor embolization via the ophthalmic artery, (2) availability of detailed intraoperative records and digital subtraction angiography (DSA) images from the embolization procedure, and (3) documentation of the patient's clinical course before and after embolization, including details of the surgical procedure and any complications.

Embolization via the ophthalmic artery was performed only after carefully weighing the potential benefits against the risks of complications. It was undertaken when preoperative DSA demonstrated substantial tumor supply from the ophthalmic artery and favorable angiographic anatomy, including an ethmoidal artery diameter of at least 0.5 mm and the anticipated feasibility of advancing the microcatheter sufficiently distally into the ethmoidal artery, without severe tortuosity between the ophthalmic artery origin and the ethmoidal artery. When non-ophthalmic artery feeders were also present, we assessed the relative contribution of each feeder and selected the embolization targets accordingly.

Patient symptoms and medical history prior to hospitalization, along with clinical and endovascular procedural data, were obtained from the electronic medical records. Preoperative evaluations, including neurological, ophthalmological, and radiological assessments, were conducted. Radiological imaging data of meningiomas and details of the embolization procedure were collected, including target vessels for embolization, procedural techniques, embolic materials used, and changes in tumor staining on angiography before and after the embolization. The clinical course after embolization, including the immediate outcomes and follow-up findings, was comprehensively evaluated. All patients who underwent preoperative embolization were evaluated using contrast-enhanced magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI)-MRI within 48 h of the procedure.

These imaging studies assessed safety (detection of ischemic complications, including asymptomatic cerebral infarction) and effectiveness (reduction in tumor enhancement and necrosis) before tumor resection. Additional data on intraoperative hemorrhagic complications, transfusion requirements during surgery, and postoperative neurological changes were evaluated.

Technical considerations

Embolization procedures were performed under local or general anesthesia. The puncture sites included the right radial or right common femoral arteries. For vascular access, a 4-French (Fr) guiding sheath or a 6-Fr guiding catheter was used. Heparin was administered to all patients to maintain an activated clotting time of approximately 250 s.

A 4-Fr diagnostic catheter was inserted into the guiding sheath or catheter, which was then advanced into the cervical segment of the internal carotid artery using standard techniques. Preoperative imaging, including 3D rotational angiography, was performed to evaluate the extent of the tumor blush and to determine the optimal working angle. The working angle was set at the anterior oblique view on the affected side with cranial angulation to optimize the visualization of the intraorbital course of the ophthalmic artery (Figure 1). Imaging was performed to identify the locations of the central retinal and posterior ciliary arteries based on the visualization of choroidal blush. Subsequently, the diagnostic catheter was replaced with a 3.2-Fr distal access catheter (Guidepost; Tokai Medical Products, Aichi, Japan or Tactics plus; Technocrat Corporation, Aichi, Japan). In all patients, a 1.3-Fr catheter (DeFrictor Nano; Medico's Hirata Inc., Osaka, Japan) was used as the microcatheter. Using a micro-guidewire (ASAHI CHIKAI X10; Asahi Intecc Co., Ltd., Nagoya, Japan or Tenrou; Kaneka Medix Corporation, Osaka, Japan), the microcatheter was navigated into the ophthalmic artery for subsequent embolization. During microcatheter navigation, the tip of the distal access catheter was shaped into a gentle curve,14) and was navigated to the orifice of the ophthalmic artery to enhance the microcatheter pushability. The final embolization targets were the posterior and anterior ethmoidal arteries (AEAs) and the recurrent meningeal artery. The embolization material used was n-butyl-2-cyanoacrylate (n-BCA), with concentrations ranging from 20% to 33%, which was customized for each patient.15) When a sufficient distance from the central retinal artery was maintained―typically when the microcatheter could be advanced distally beyond the ethmoidal foramen to the dural segment of the anterior skull base―the risk of reflux was considered low, allowing for the use of lower concentrations to achieve deeper tumor penetration. In some cases, when the catheter tip was in a wedge position, a low concentration of n-BCA, typically 20%, could be used to facilitate intratumoral penetration. Conversely, when such distal advancement could not be achieved, a higher concentration of 33% was used to safely achieve proximal occlusion. To reduce viscosity, the n-BCA was warmed using an industrial heat gun set to 115°C, with the tip positioned 10 cm away, for at least 90 s. The warmed n-BCA was then injected using a slow, pulsatile technique to prevent reflux into the central retinal artery. The fluoroscopy monitor layout was carefully optimized using reference images, a blank roadmap, and native images arranged side by side. This setup allowed the operator to easily monitor the embolization status with a quick lateral glance, enabling real-time assessment of the acceptable extent of reflux during the injection. The goal of embolization was not only to occlude the main feeder of the tumor but also to allow the infiltration of a liquid embolic agent into the tumor parenchyma. Post-embolization DSA imaging was performed to confirm the absence of stenosis or occlusion in vessels, such as the ophthalmic and central retinal arteries, as well as to assess the reduction in tumor blush.

Results

Patient and clinical characteristics

During the study period, 57 anterior skull base meningiomas were resected at our institution. Of the 51 patients with preoperative DSA, 25 had tumor feeders from the ophthalmic artery. In 18 of these 25 patients, the ophthalmic artery contribution was minor and/or non-ophthalmic feeders were dominant. In two additional patients, embolization via the ophthalmic artery was deemed anatomically unfavorable―one due to severe tortuosity at the ophthalmic artery origin and the other due to marked tortuosity preventing adequate distal catheterization―so embolization was performed via alternative feeders (Figure 2). Finally, five patients underwent trans-ophthalmic artery embolization for skull base meningiomas (Table 1). The cohort included four males and one female patient, with a median age of 58 years (range: 37-78 years). Unilateral embolization was performed in four patients, whereas one patient underwent bilateral embolization at a single session. Two meningiomas were located at the olfactory groove, two at the planum sphenoidale, and one at the sphenoid ridge, with a median maximum diameter of 41.9 mm (range: 38.5-71.5 mm). Preoperative symptoms varied: one patient presented with frontal lobe dysfunction and gait disturbance, one experienced olfactory dysfunction, another presented with visual impairment, and two remained asymptomatic. The main arterial feeders were the unilateral posterior ethmoidal artery in three patients, whereas in one patient with a planum sphenoidale meningioma, the tumor was supplied by both the unilateral posterior ethmoidal artery and the contralateral AEA. In another patient with a sphenoid ridge meningioma, the main feeder was the recurrent meningeal artery.

Endovascular procedure

Embolization procedures were performed under local anesthesia in four patients and general anesthesia in one. The puncture sites were the right radial artery in two patients and the right common femoral artery in three patients. A 4-Fr guiding sheath was used in three patients, and a 6-Fr guiding catheter in two. The working angle was set at an oblique view of the affected side with cranial angulation. Table 1 provides a detailed list of the neurointerventional equipment and the precise working angles. The final embolization targets were the left posterior ethmoidal artery in three patients, the right posterior ethmoidal artery and the left AEA in one patient, and the left recurrent meningeal artery in one patient.

Outcomes

Preoperative embolization was successful in all five patients. Postoperative angiography revealed a substantial reduction in tumor blush in all patients. The central retinal artery and choroidal blush were also visualized. No neurological complications, including vision loss or impairment, were observed after the embolization procedure, and post-embolization imaging showed no hemorrhage or ischemic stroke. One patient exhibited worsening deep venous thrombosis associated with antiphospholipid syndrome, while another patient presented with fever. Gadolinium-enhanced T1-weighted MRI showed a reduction in tumor enhancement with corresponding high-intensity areas on DWI, suggesting tumor necrosis. At our institution, tumor resection was typically planned 1-2 days after embolization. However, two patients in this series underwent delayed resection: one because of worsening deep venous thrombosis and the other because of fever after embolization. During surgical resection, the median blood loss was 350 mL. Although one patient experienced difficulty in achieving hemostasis because of anticoagulant therapy, resulting in 1,455 mL blood loss and transfusion during the surgery, the other four patients underwent uneventful resection with effective blood control, with blood loss limited to less than 400 mL. Simpson grade II resection was achieved in three patients, whereas the remaining two underwent grade III resection.16) Notably, one patient with preoperative optic nerve symptoms experienced postoperative improvement in their visual field defects.

Illustrative case description

A 62-year-old woman was referred to our neurosurgical department following the detection of abnormalities on head imaging at her previous hospital. MRI findings suggested a meningioma located at the anterior skull base. Follow-up imaging revealed progressive enlargement over time, prompting a decision to proceed with surgical resection. Preoperative DSA demonstrated that the bilateral ethmoidal arteries were supplying the tumor.

Two days before surgery, tumor embolization was performed. Under general anesthesia, the right common femoral artery was punctured, and a 6-Fr catheter was inserted. The procedure was initiated on the right side, followed by the left. The following sections provide a detailed description of the techniques performed on the left side. The working angle was set at a left-anterior-oblique angle of 64° and cranial angle of 31° (Figure 3a and b). Prior to embolization, imaging was performed to identify the location of the target anterior ethmoidal and central retinal arteries (Figure 3a), the latter being identified by its characteristic choroidal blush. A gentle curved 3.2-Fr Guidepost catheter was used as a distal access catheter and advanced close to the origin of the ophthalmic artery. Using a 0.010-inch guidewire (ASAHI CHIKAI X 010; Asahi Intecc Co., Ltd., Aichi, Japan), the ophthalmic artery was selected, and a 1.3-Fr DeFrictor Nano microcatheter was advanced beyond the second segment of the ophthalmic artery. The microcatheter was navigated beyond the tortuous part of the AEA, wedged into the vessel, and positioned close to the tumor (Figure 3c). To ensure an adequate distance from the central retinal artery, embolization was performed using heated 33% n-BCA (Figure 3d and e). The tumor blush visibly reduced after embolization, and preservation of blood flow to the central retinal artery was confirmed (Figure 3f). Post-embolization CT showed no hemorrhagic changes, and MRI conducted the following day indicated tumor necrosis (Figure 4a, b and c). Surgical resection was performed two days after embolization, with no hemorrhagic complications. The surgery was completed successfully, achieving a Simpson grade II resection (Figure 4d), with no intraoperative bleeding complications (blood loss: 350 mL) and no need for transfusion.

Discussion

Anterior skull base meningiomas are often supplied by ethmoidal arteries.7,8) Embolization via their parent artery, the ophthalmic artery, carries a potential risk of visual dysfunction, and its safety and effectiveness remain uncertain. However, our study demonstrated that embolization via the ethmoidal artery effectively reduced tumor staining on DSA in all patients, with no visual or major complications observed. Although similar reports exist, to the best of our knowledge, none have specifically demonstrated the effectiveness of embolization using n-BCA. In four of the five patients, tumor resection was completed without significant intraoperative bleeding or the need for a blood transfusion.

Surgical resection of hypervascular skull base tumors is challenging because of their deep-seated vascular supply.3,4,9,12) In anterior skull base meningiomas, early ligation of the ethmoidal artery feeders can reduce blood loss,7,17) but vessel wall hypertrophy and encasement in hyperostotic sclerosed foramina can cause challenges.8) Additionally, large tumor size and brain edema can limit surgical access.8)

A systematic review of preoperative embolization for skull base meningiomas, commonly located in the sphenoid wing, petroclival region, cavernous sinus, and anterior skull base, reported reduced intraoperative blood loss (median, 225-580 mL) and improved resection. Simpson grade I-III resection was achieved in 74% of patients,18) highlighting its effectiveness in improving surgical outcomes.

Limited literature exists on the safety and effectiveness of endovascular embolization via the ophthalmic artery, with several small case series reporting the outcomes.10-13) Trivelatto et al.12) evaluated the outcomes of preoperative Onyx (ev3; Irvine, California, USA) embolization of meningiomas via the ophthalmic artery in five patients, noting no vision changes, but one patient developed transient oculomotor palsy. Terada et al.10) demonstrated that embolization of feeders to hypervascular meningiomas arising from the ophthalmic artery is both feasible and effective, reducing intraoperative bleeding with preserved vision in most cases using particles and/or coils. Lefkowitz et al.11) reported the effectiveness and safety of embolization for neurosurgical lesions such as arteriovenous malformations, dural arteriovenous fistulae, angiofibromas, and meningiomas involving the ophthalmic artery. Waldron et al.13) reported the outcomes of embolization for skull base meningiomas originating from the internal carotid circulation, including one case involving the ophthalmic artery, with low complication rates.

However, serious complications, including visual impairment because of unintentional retrograde embolization of the ophthalmic artery, have also been reported.7,9-11,19) Terada et al.10) described a case in which embolization with Gelfoam powder (Phadia, Uppsala, Sweden) resulted in a post-embolization visual deficit, with a fluorescein retinal angiogram revealing occlusion of the right upper branch of the retinal artery. Bendszus et al.9) reported two cases of amaurosis following particle embolization. A previous study suggested that embolization of feeders from the ophthalmic artery carries a low risk of complications if the microcatheter is selectively placed distal to the central retinal artery and embolic agents are injected gently to prevent reflux.10)

Most prior reports of embolization via the ophthalmic artery have predominantly used particles, and a smaller number have described Onyx; studies specifically focusing on n-BCA remain limited.9,10,12,20) Particle embolization is widely used, and can achieve intratumoral devascularization. However, because particles are not continuously visible under fluoroscopy, reflux can be difficult to detect and control, which may increase the risk of visual complications.9,10) Onyx offers good visibility and controllability; however, if the margin of reflux is relatively short―as in the case of the ethmoidal artery―the plug-and-push technique may increase the risk of proximal migration of the embolic material. Indeed, major complications after trans-ophthalmic embolization have been reported.20) Therefore, we have not adopted Onyx in this setting. In contrast, n-BCA allows real-time visualization of cast progression and reflux under fluoroscopy. We believe that the controlled, slow injection of n-BCA under roadmap guidance enables more precise management of the embolization area. Because this study included only n-BCA cases, further case accumulation is needed to determine the optimal embolic material.

To ensure the safe administration of n-BCA, we have established standardized procedures aimed at minimizing complications related to retinal artery embolization. A 2024 retrospective study on transarterial embolization with n-BCA noted that the anatomical features of the ophthalmic artery make it difficult to navigate microcatheters, often causing hesitation in performing n-BCA injection.21) Previous studies have emphasized the importance of identifying the central retinal artery and carefully positioning the catheter at a safe distance from it.10,12) At our institution, we prioritize establishing a working angle that provides optimal visualization of the second segment of the ophthalmic artery for as long as necessary.

The ophthalmic artery is divided into three orbital segments. The first runs anteromedially along the inferolateral optic nerve, the second crosses medially (83% over and 17% below the nerve), and the third follows the medial orbital wall to the superomedial orbit.22) Since the central retinal artery branches from the second segment, embolization beyond this point preserves visual function.23) Positioning the working angle at an anterior oblique and cranial position allows for extended visualization of the second segment of the ophthalmic artery and provides a clearer view of the ethmoidal artery's branching point. This approach enables the microcatheter to be guided as distally as possible, well beyond the central retinal artery, allowing for safe cannulation of the ethmoidal artery and for precise positioning near the tumor.

Positioning the microcatheter closer to the tumor enables intratumoral embolization rather than proximal feeder occlusion. Previous studies using dynamic susceptibility contrast-enhanced perfusion-weighted imaging have demonstrated that the flow reduction effect of intratumoral embolization was superior to that of proximal feeder occlusion.24) Therefore, intratumoral embolization promotes tumor necrosis, softening the tumor and facilitating suction and removal during resection.

Advancements in neurointerventional equipment play a crucial role in the success of endovascular treatments. Injecting high-concentration n-BCA from a wedged position as close to the tumor as possible is crucial for effective embolization. Achieving this requires the use of a low-profile, soft-tipped catheter, such as the DeFrictor Nano microcatheter, to ensure precision and safety.

This study had some limitations. First, the sample size was small, with only five patients included, which limits the generalizability of our findings. Therefore, the accumulation of more cases is essential to clarify the safety and effectiveness of this procedure. Additionally, the retrospective nature of this study introduces potential biases because data collection and interpretation relied on medical records. The variability in embolization materials and techniques, tailored for each patient, may also have influenced the outcomes, making it challenging to establish standardized protocols for the procedure. Further research is necessary to overcome these limitations and to clarify the safety and effectiveness of embolization via the ophthalmic artery.

Conclusions

Preoperative embolization via the ophthalmic artery can be safely performed to facilitate the resection of anterior skull base meningiomas. By using standardized techniques and n-BCA as the embolic agent, this approach can enhance surgical outcomes and reduce the risk of hemorrhagic complications.
