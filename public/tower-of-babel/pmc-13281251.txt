Genomic and transcriptomic analysis reveals chitin metabolic pathways in the marine bacterium Microbulbifer harenosus CGMCC 1.13584T
Authors: Xuebing Ren, Xin Song, Hang Zhang, Guangqiang Wang, Fan Xie, Haerlihashi Muhetaerhan, Lianzhong Ai, Yanjun Tian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChitin is the second most abundant polysaccharide in nature, and its degradation by marine microorganisms plays a critical role in the global carbon and nitrogen cycles. This study investigated the marine bacterium Microbulbifer harenosus CGMCC 1.13584T to elucidate its chitin metabolic pathway through genomic and transcriptomic analyses.ResultsWhen cultured with chitin as the carbon source, the strain exhibited an extended lag phase and enhanced extracellular chitinase activity. Genome sequencing revealed the presence of genes involved in both hydrolytic and oxidative chitin degradation pathways. Transcriptomic analysis showed that genes associated with the hydrolytic pathway were significantly upregulated upon chitin induction. In contrast, within the oxidative degradation pathway, only early-stage response genes (such as those encoding LPMOs) were markedly upregulated, while genes involved in subsequent metabolic steps (converting GlcNAc1A to KDG-6-P) did not show significant upregulation. Furthermore, a gene encoding a GH10 domain-containing protein was found to be substantially upregulated during growth on chitin.ConclusionThese findings indicate that Microbulbifer harenosus CGMCC 1.13584T utilizes a coordinated chitin degradation mechanism, where the hydrolytic pathway dominates carbon flux during active growth, while the oxidative pathway (via LPMOs) likely provides critical initial structural disruption.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12866-026-05115-3.Keywords: Chitin metabolic pathways, Microbulbifer harenosus, Genomic analysis, Transcriptomic analysis, Chitinase

Introduction

Chitin, a natural polysaccharide composed of N-acetylglucosamine (GlcNAc) linked by β-1,4-glycosidic bonds, is the second most abundant biomass in nature after cellulose [1]. Chitin functions as a key structural component in many organisms, such as fungi, crustaceans, insects, and algae [2], serving as an important renewable resource. Despite an estimated global chitin production of approximately 10¹¹ tons per year, chitin does not accumulate significantly in marine sediments. This is primarily attributed to its efficient enzymatic degradation mediated by marine heterotrophic bacteria [2]. Chitinolytic enzymes primarily include chitinase, N-acetylglucosaminidase and LPMO. Chitinase (EC 3.2.1.14) is a glycoside hydrolase whose main function is to hydrolyze the β-1,4-glycosidic bonds between GlcNAc residues in chitin, thereby degrading it into chitooligosaccharides. Based on differences in sequence similarity, three-dimensional structure, and catalytic mechanisms, chitinases are primarily classified into glycoside hydrolase (GH) families 18 and 19 [3]. Among them, GH18 family chitinases are the most widely distributed and are found in various organisms including bacteria, archaea, fungi, plants, insects, and vertebrates. The GH19 family was initially thought to exist only in plants, but was later discovered in a few microbial sources as well [4–6]. N-acetylglucosamin-idase often acts on chitooligosaccharides, degrading them into GlcNAc [7]. LPMOs are a class of metalloenzymes capable of oxidatively cleaving crystalline polysaccharides, playing a key role in chitin degradation. They were first experimentally characterized in 2010 and are primarily classified under the AA10, AA11, and AA15 in the Carbohydrate-Active Enzymes database [8]. LPMOs enhance the hydrolytic efficiency of chitinases through oxidative cleavage [9], with the reaction primarily yielding oxidized chitooligosaccharides.

Bacterial chitin metabolism generally bifurcates into two routes: the chitin hydrolysis pathway initiated by chitinases, and the chitin oxidative degradation pathway initiated by LPMOs [10]. Comparative genomic studies have shown that this complete oxidative pathway, consisting of LPMOs and associated metabolic gene clusters, is widely present and highly conserved among marine Gammaproteobacteria [10]. In marine environments, chitin degradation is driven by a diverse array of microorganisms, including members of the genera Aquimarina, Pseudoalteromonas, Vibrio, Microbulbifer, among others [11–13]. The genus Microbulbifer was first described by González et al. as a member of the family Alteromonadaceae within the class Gammaproteobacteria [14]. Members of this genus are Gram-negative, strictly aerobic bacteria and play an important ecological role in the marine carbon cycle [15]. As of the time of writing, the genus comprises a total of 35 species, many of which are capable of chitin degradation, are found in a wide range of natural habitats [14, 16–18]. Howard et al. found marine bacterium Microbulbifer degradans 2–40 possesses a dedicated hydrolytic chitinolytic system, which encompasses extracellular chitinases, periplasmic processing enzymes, and intracellular catabolic genes for the complete degradation and utilization of chitin [19]. However, these investigations were confined to the canonical hydrolytic pathway, leaving the potential existence of oxidative mechanisms unexplored in this genus.

In recent years, transcriptomic approaches have become increasingly important for elucidating how marine bacteria sense, deconstruct, and assimilate complex polysaccharides in diverse oceanic habitats. For example, transcriptomic analysis of the deep-sea bacterium Maribellus comscasis WC007 demonstrated that cellulose induces the specific upregulation of a polysaccharide utilization locus (PUL 32) together with multiple glycoside hydrolases, thereby linking cellulose depolymerization to central carbon metabolism and the tricarboxylic acid cycle [20]. In Colwellia echini A3T, transcriptome profiling revealed coordinated activation of a carrageenan-responsive gene cluster, in which carrageenases, oligosaccharide-hydrolyzing enzymes, and sulfatases were simultaneously induced to support the stepwise degradation of sulfated galactans [21]. Similarly, in the marine flavobacterium Zobellia galactanivorans DsijT, growth on fresh macroalgal tissues triggered the co-induction of multiple PULs targeting alginate and fucose-containing sulfated polysaccharides, highlighting substrate-specific transcriptional regulation during algal biomass utilization [22]. In the context of chitin catabolism, combined transcriptomic and proteomic analyses of Jeongeupia wiesaeckerbachi showed extensive transcriptional reprogramming in response to chitin, including the coordinated upregulation of chitinases, hexosaminidases, and chemotaxis-related genes associated with substrate colonization [23]. Together, these studies demonstrate that transcriptomics is a powerful tool for resolving substrate-induced polysaccharide utilization strategies in marine bacteria. However, despite the recognized chitin-degrading potential of the genus Microbulbifer, transcriptome-based insights into its chitin metabolic pathway, particularly the relative involvement of hydrolytic and oxidative processes, remain limited.

This study found that the type strain Microbulbifer harenosus CGMCC 1.13584T (hereafter strain CGMCC 1.13584) is capable of degrading chitin [24]. Its chitin metabolic pathways were systematically elucidated through whole-genome sequencing and transcriptome analysis. We examined the growth characteristics and extracellular enzyme activity of this strain in chitin medium, completed its whole-genome sequencing, and annotated key genes involved in both hydrolytic and oxidative chitinolytic pathways. Transcriptome sequencing was employed to analyze differential gene expression under chitin-induced conditions. By delineating the comprehensive transcriptional profiles of both hydrolytic and oxidative chitin degradation pathways, this study elucidates the unique expression dynamics of strain CGMCC 1.13584, underscoring its potential as a valuable microbial resource for chitin bioconversion.

Materials and methods

Preparation of resting cells

The strain CGMCC 1.13584 was cultured in TYS liquid medium (5 g/L tryptone, 1 g/L yeast extract, 30 g/L sea salt) until reaching the mid-exponential growth phase. The cells were then collected by centrifugation at 6000 rpm for 3 min, washed three times with sterile artificial seawater, and incubated at 30°C for 12 h to deplete endogenous carbon reserves. Afterward, the cells were washed three more times with sterile artificial seawater and subsequently placed at 4°C for 12 h.

Growth kinetics in glucose and chitin media

The prepared resting cells of strain CGMCC 1.13584 were inoculated into glucose medium (5 g/L glucose, 10 g/L tryptone, 30 g/L NaCl, 0.5 g/L NH4Cl, 3 g/L MgCl2·6H2O, 2 g/L K2SO4, 0.2 g/L K2HPO4, 0.01 g/L CaCl2, 0.006 g/L FeCl3·6H2O, 0.005 g/L Na2MoO4·7H2O, 0.004 g/L CuCl2·2H2O, 6 g/L Tris, pH7.6) and chitin medium (10 g/L colloidal chitin, 30 g/L NaCl, 0.5 g/L NH4Cl, 3 g/L MgCl2·6H2O, 2 g/L K2SO4, 0.2 g/L K2HPO4, 0.01 g/L CaCl2, 0.006 g/L FeCl3·6H2O, 0.005 g/L Na2MoO4·7H2O, 0.004 g/L CuCl2·2H2O, 6 g/L Tris, pH7.6), respectively. Samples were collected from the cultures at designated time intervals. For the chitin medium, the collected cells were subjected to serial dilution, plated on TYS solid medium, and incubated at 30 °C for 48 h before colony counting. For the glucose medium, samples were taken to measure the optical density at 600 nm (OD₆₀₀).

Chitinase activity curve determination

While monitoring the growth of the strain CGMCC 1.13584 in the chitin medium, the extracellular chitinase activity was also assessed. At specified time intervals, bacterial cells were collected and centrifuged at 6000 rpm for 3 min. The resulting supernatant was used for chitinase activity measurement. Specifically, 100 µL of the extracellular supernatant was mixed with 100 µL of 50 mM Tris-HCl buffer containing 2% colloidal chitin and incubated at 45°C for 2 h. The reaction was terminated by heating at 100°C for 5 min, followed by centrifugation at 6000 rpm for 3 min. Then, 110 µL of the supernatant was combined with 110 µL of DNS reagent and heated at 100°C for 10 min. After centrifugation at 6000 rpm for 3 min, 200 µL of the supernatant was taken to measure its absorbance at 550 nm. The reducing sugar content was quantified using a standard curve prepared with GlcNAc. One unit of enzyme activity (U) was defined as the amount of enzyme required to release 1 µmol of reducing sugar per minute under the assay conditions.

Genome sequencing, assembly, and annotation

Strain CGMCC 1.13584 was inoculated into TYS liquid medium and cultured at 30°C with shaking at 200 rpm. When the bacterial culture reached the mid-exponential growth phase, cells were collected and the samples were sent to Shanghai Majorbio Bio-pharm Technology Co., Ltd for genome sequencing. Genomic DNA of strain CGMCC 1.13584 was extracted using the SDS lysis method combined with phenol-chloroform-isoamyl alcohol extraction and isopropanol precipitation. The quality and concentration of the extracted DNA were assessed using a NanoDrop One spectrophotometer and a Qubit 3.0 Fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). A hybrid sequencing strategy was employed using both Illumina and Oxford Nanopore Technologies (ONT) platforms. For short-read sequencing, the DNA library was constructed using the NEBNext® Ultra™ II DNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (NEB, USA) and sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform in Paired-End 150 bp (PE150) mode. For long-read sequencing, the library was prepared using the Ligation Sequencing Kit (SQK-LSK110, ONT) alongside the Native Barcoding Expansion kit (EXP-NBD196, ONT), and subsequently sequenced on a PromethION sequencer (Oxford Nanopore Technologies, UK). For in silico analysis, raw sequencing reads were subjected to quality control using fastp (v0.25.0) to remove adapter sequences and low-quality reads. The high-quality short and long reads were de novo assembled into a complete genome using Unicycler. The obtained genomic DNA sequences were subjected to gene prediction using RAST (https://rast.nmpdr.org/), and whole-genome functional annotation was performed in combination with the COG database. In addition, a local BLAST search was conducted to predict genes related to the oxidative degradation of chitin in the strain’s genome, with an E‑value threshold set at 1E‑5.

Transcriptome sequencing and analysis

Resting cells were inoculated into glucose and chitin media, respectively. When the cultures reached the mid-exponential growth phase, cells were harvested, washed three times with distilled water, and immediately frozen at -80°C for subsequent RNA extraction and transcriptome sequencing. Three independent biological replicates were prepared for each treatment. Total RNA was extracted using the CTAB method, and residual genomic DNA was removed prior to library construction. Only high-quality RNA samples were used for transcriptome analysis. For strand-specific RNA-seq library preparation, ribosomal RNA (rRNA) was depleted using the RiboCop rRNA Depletion Kit for Mixed Bacterial Samples (Lexogen, USA). The enriched RNA was then fragmented into short fragments of approximately 200 nt. First-strand cDNA was synthesized using random hexamer primers, followed by second-strand cDNA synthesis in the presence of dUTP instead of dTTP to preserve strand specificity. The resulting double-stranded cDNA was subjected to end repair, phosphorylation, A-tailing, and adaptor ligation according to the Illumina stranded library preparation protocol. The libraries were sequenced in paired-end mode on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). Raw reads were filtered to remove adaptor-contaminated reads, low-quality reads, and reads containing more than 10% ambiguous bases (N), and the resulting clean reads were used for downstream analyses. To assess possible rRNA contamination, a subset of raw reads from each sample was aligned against the Rfam database. Clean reads were mapped to the reference genome using Bowtie2. Gene expression levels were quantified using RSEM and normalized as TPM and FPKM values. Differential expression analysis between the glucose and chitin treatment groups was performed using DESeq2 [25]. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were defined based on the thresholds of a p-value < 0.05 and a fold change > 2 (|log2FC| > 1). Functional enrichment analysis of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) was conducted based on Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) annotations. GO enrichment analysis was performed using GOATOOLS, and KEGG pathway enrichment analysis was conducted using KOBAS. GO terms and KEGG pathways with adjusted p-value < 0.05 were considered significantly enriched. All bioinformatic analyses were performed using the Majorbio Cloud Platform (www.majorbio.com, Shanghai Majorbio Bio-pharm Technology Co., Ltd., China).

Bioinformatic analysis of chitinolytic enzymes

Chitinolytic enzymes were analyzed for signal peptides using SignalP 6.0 [26] and for domains using InterPro [27]. The GH10 family chitinases were aligned with characterized GH18, GH19, GH20, and GH10 family sequences, and the alignment was imported into MEGA X to construct a phylogenetic tree [28].

Results

Growth and chitinase activity dynamics of strain CGMCC 1.13584

Strain CGMCC 1.13584ᵀ demonstrated the ability to utilize both glucose and chitin (Fig. 1a and b). A prolonged lag phase was observed in both medium (11 h in glucose medium and 29 h in chitin medium). Compared with glucose, the strain cultured in chitin medium required an even longer lag phase, likely because the utilization of chitin necessitates the prior secretion of chitin-degrading enzymes to break it down into absorbable oligosaccharides. In the chitin medium (Fig. 1b), the chitinase activity began to increase significantly during the later stage of the lag phase. As microbial growth entered the mid-exponential phase the rise in enzyme activity showed a similar trend. When microbial growth reached the stationary phase at 35 h, the rate of increase in enzyme activity slowed down markedly.

Genomic mining of chitin metabolism pathways

The genome of strain CGMCC 1.13584 has a total length of 4,720,407 bp, with a GC content of 58.1338%, and contains 3,834 protein-coding genes (Fig. S1). Combined annotation by RAST and comparative analysis via BLAST revealed that the genome of strain CGMCC 1.13584 harbors genes associated with chitin metabolism (Fig. 2). The genes involved in chitin hydrolysis encode chitinase, non-ongA-type beta-hexosaminidase, N-acetylglucosamine transporter (NagP), N-acetylglucosamine-regulated TonB-dependent outer membrane receptor (TBDR), N-acetylglucosamine related transporter (NagX), N-acetylglucosamine kinase (NagK), N-acetylglucosamine-6-phosphate deacetylase (NagA), and glucosamine-6-phosphate deaminase (NagB). It is inferred that through the action of these enzymes, chitin is hydrolyzed into monosaccharides, which are subsequently phosphorylated to form N-acetylglucosamine-6-phosphate, then deacetylated to yield glucosamine-6-phosphate, and finally deaminated to produce fructose-6-phosphate. Genes associated with oxidative degradation encode 2-keto-3-deoxygluconate kinase (KdgK), lytic polysaccharide monooxygenase (LPMO), oligosaccharide transporter (OngOT), beta-hexosaminidase (OngA), GlcNAc1A deacetylase (OngB), GlcN1A deaminase (OngC), chitin-inducible transporter (OngIT), Rid family protein (OngD), and OngR. The putative functions of these genes suggest that under the coordinated action of these enzymes, chitin is oxidatively degraded into GlcNAc1A, which is transported into the cell. Inside the cell, it is first deacetylated to GlcN1A, then deaminated to KDG, and finally phosphorylated to KDG-6-P to enter the ED pathway.

Domain composition of chitinolytic enzymes

Genomic analysis revealed that the strain CGMCC 1.13584 harbors two genes encoding LPMOs (LPMO_RS17300 and LPMO_RS19160), four genes encoding chitinases (Chitinase_RS08145, Chitinase_RS09270, Chitinase_RS09275, and Chitinase_RS09590), one gene encoding a GH10 domain-containing protein (GH10_RS19145), and two genes encoding β-hexosaminidase (β-hexosaminidase_RS19485 and β-hexosaminidase_RS02180) (Fig. 3). Domain analysis of these genes indicated that all the chitinolytic enzymes contain signal peptides, suggesting they are secreted into the periplasmic space or extracellular environment. Both LPMOs contain only the catalytic domain, lacking any carbohydrate-binding modules (CBMs). The four chitinases, the GH10 domain-containing protein, and one beta-hexosaminidase all possess CBMs. in addition to their catalytic domains. The CBM of chitinase can specifically recognize the N‑acetyl groups on chitin chains and synergistically work with the catalytic domain to guide the substrate into the active site, thereby significantly enhancing the binding and degradation efficiency toward both chitin and chitooligosaccharides [29]. Notably, the protein GH10_RS19145 possesses a catalytic domain of the GH10 family (typically associated with xylanases), whereas chitinases generally fall into the GH18 and GH19 families. Phylogenetic analysis revealed that protein RS19145 clusters with xylanases and exhibits 28.57% similarity to the beta-xylanase XynA (7D88) from Bacillus sp. KW1, with an E-value of 2E-29 (Fig. 4).

Transcriptional profiles of the chitin catabolic pathway under chitin induction

To examine the transcriptional activity of key genes in the predicted core chitin catabolic pathway of strain CGMCC 1.13584, the strain was cultured in chitin medium and glucose medium, respectively, followed by comparative transcriptomic analysis using cells harvested at the mid-exponential growth phase. The complete list of differentially expressed genes (DEGs), including raw read counts, fold changes, and p-values, is provided in Table S1. As shown in Fig. 5a, based on the defined statistical criteria (|log2FC|>1 and p-value < 0.05), among the genes related to chitin hydrolytic metabolism, all four chitinase genes exhibited significant transcriptional upregulation in the presence of chitin, indicating their transcriptional activity during chitin degradation. Notably, the gene encoding the GH10 domain-containing protein (GH10_RS19145) also showed clear transcriptional activation. Similarly, both β-hexosaminidase genes were transcriptionally upregulated. The genes encoding the outer membrane transporter TBDR for chitin oligosaccharides and the inner membrane transporters NagP and NagX were also significantly upregulated, suggesting that these three genes are involved in the transport of chitooligosaccharides or monosaccharides. Moreover, within the intracellular GlcNAc metabolic gene cluster, genes nagK (NagK_RS01435), nagA (NagA_RS01445) and nagB (NagB_RS01440) all displayed marked upregulation. As presented in Fig. 5b, among the genes associated with oxidative chitin degradation, only one of the two genes encoding lytic polysaccharide monooxygenases (LPMOs) showed significant transcriptional upregulation (LPMO_RS17300), whereas gene LPMO_RS19160 did not, implying that only one LPMO gene may be functionally active in oxidative chitin degradation. The gene (β‑hexosaminidase_RS19485) encoding ongA-type β‑hexosaminidase, which cleaves oxidized chitooligosaccharides into GlcNAc1A, was upregulated to a lesser extent than the LPMO. Similarly, the inner membrane transporter gene for oxidized chitooligosaccharides exhibited lower upregulation compared to the outer membrane transporter gene. Genes involved in the subsequent phosphorylation, deacetylation, and deamination of oligosaccharides to monosaccharides did not show notable transcriptional induction. Based on these findings, we propose that hydrolytic metabolism plays the dominant role in chitin degradation in this strain, while oxidative metabolism likely serves an auxiliary function.

Discussion

Transcriptomic analysis of the marine bacterium Microbulbifer harenosus grown on colloidal chitin revealed a distinct dominance of the hydrolytic degradation pathway during the mid-exponential growth phase. Within the oxidative pathway, significant upregulation was restricted to the LPMO gene, leaving downstream intracellular metabolic genes largely uninduced. This transcriptomic profile may be driven by both structural and temporal factors. Structurally, the identified LPMO lacks a carbohydrate-binding module (CBM), which likely reduces localized cleavage and limits the generation of soluble oxidative products, thereby funneling metabolic flux toward hydrolysis [31]. Temporally, since the transcriptomic profiling was conducted solely during the exponential phase, it may fail to capture subsequent temporal shifts. While this single-time-point snapshot suggests hydrolytic dominance during active growth, it is highly probable that the oxidative pathway is not inherently dormant, but rather dynamic and phase-dependent.

Based on sequence homology, the protein GH10_RS19145 is classified into the GH10 family. While GH10 enzymes are primarily xylanases, some exhibit bifunctional properties, such as Xyn10D-Fae1A (xylanase/feruloyl esterase) from Prevotella ruminicola 23 [32] and XynA (xylanase/cellulase) from Bacillus sp. KW1 [33]. Concurrently, the recognized diversity of chitinases is also expanding. Although predominantly found in the GH18 and GH19 families [34], novel chitinases have been identified in atypical families, including a GH48 chitinase devoid of cellulase activity [35] and a GH23 chitinase from Ralstonia sp. A-471 [36]. In light of this enzymatic diversity, the high expression levels of GH10_RS19145 observed in our study raise the possibility that it acts as a GH10 chitinase. If confirmed, this would significantly broaden the known spectrum of chitinolytic families. Nevertheless, transcriptional upregulation does not inherently guarantee direct chitinolytic activity. It is plausible that GH10_RS19145 is part of a shared regulatory network that co-regulates the degradation of multiple complex polysaccharides. Until direct biochemical evidence is obtained, the function of this GH10 protein remains hypothetical. Future research must clarify whether this enzyme possesses actual substrate promiscuity or if its induction is simply a bystander effect of the global chitin response.

The findings in this study reveals that the unique structural configurations of its enzymatic arsenal provide M. harenosus CGMCC 1.13584 with the essential structural foundation for its ecological adaptation. From an energetic perspective, the structural limitation of the CBM-lacking LPMO (LPMO_RS17300) likely serves a distinct ecological purpose. By generating only minimal soluble oxidized products, It introduces just enough localized cleavages to loosen the crystalline chitin matrix, thereby bypassing the substantial energetic burden of fully activating the downstream intracellular oxidative pathway. Instead, this strategy naturally channels the primary carbon flux toward the highly efficient hydrolytic chitinases. Concurrently, the induction of atypical protein like GH10_RS19145 reflects an evolutionary adaptation for nutritional versatility, enabling the strain to simultaneously scavenge diverse, co-occurring polysaccharides in complex marine environments.Ultimately, this combination of energetically frugal matrix disruption, versatile hydrolytic scavenging, and community-level interaction underpins the evolutionary success of Microbulbifer in nutrient-competitive marine ecosystems.

From a biotechnological standpoint, the enzymatic repertoire delineated in this study offers a robust theoretical basis for optimizing chitinous waste bioconversion. Specifically, the early-induced LPMO (LPMO_RS17300) exhibits immense potential as an industrial biocatalyst by fracturing the highly crystalline network of recalcitrant chitin. Building upon this structural modification, a rationally designed multi-enzyme cocktail can be formulated to couple the oxidative priming of the LPMO with the robust depolymerizing activity of the highly expressed hydrolytic chitinases. This synergistic interplay not only maximizes degradation efficiency but also minimizes reliance on conventional commercial enzyme loadings. Furthermore, recognizing that industrial marine feedstocks are complex heterogeneous matrices rather than pure chitin, the inclusion of the protein harboring a GH10 domain (GH10_RS19145) provides a critical advantage for the concurrent saccharification of co-occurring polysaccharides. Collectively, formulating enzyme cocktails that integrate oxidative LPMOs, hydrolytic chitinases, and the protein harboring a GH10 domain presents a highly viable and forward-looking strategy for the industrial valorization of mixed chitinous biomass.

Conclusion

In summary, this study provides a comprehensive genomic and transcriptomic investigation into the chitin metabolic pathways of the marine bacterium Microbulbifer harenosus CGMCC 1.13584T. Physiological assays demonstrated that the strain utilizes colloidal chitin as a sole carbon source, exhibiting an extended lag phase during which extracellular chitinase activity increases significantly, aligning with the need for enzyme secretion prior to substrate assimilation.

Importantly, transcriptomic profiling provided specific new insights into the distinct metabolic strategies of this strain. First, regarding regulatory patterns, while hydrolytic pathway genes (chitinases, transporters, and intracellular metabolic genes) were globally upregulated, only one early-stage LPMO gene and a limited subset of oxidative pathway genes were induced. Second, regarding enzyme expression profiles, we observed an unexpected but substantial upregulation of a protein harboring a GH10 domain. While traditionally associated with xylan degradation, its induction under chitinous conditions points to complex co-regulation within a shared polysaccharide metabolic network or potential substrate promiscuity, highlighting an intriguing target for future biochemical verification.

Overall, by integrating genomic annotation with transcriptional profiling, this study captures the gene expression of strain CGMCC 1.13584 at the mid-exponential growth phase during chitin utilization. These genetic and transcriptional clues highlight the distinct metabolic profile of this marine bacterium, providing specific genetic targets for the biotechnological bioconversion of marine chitin.
