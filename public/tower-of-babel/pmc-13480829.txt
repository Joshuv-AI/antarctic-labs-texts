Liver-associated findings and outcomes in a telomere biology disorders clinic
Authors: Kianna M. Nguyen, Laura Ongie, Peng Xu, Daniel D. Penrice, Yael Kusne, Ana C. Zamora, Blanca Lizaola-Mayo, Filippo Pinto e Vairo, Eva M. Carmona, Rachel A. Simon, Christy Finke, Terra Lasho, Abhishek A. Mangaonkar, Mrinal M. Patnaik, Douglas A. Simonetto, Alejandro Ferrer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Telomeres are repetitive sequences at the end of linear chromosomes that preserve genetic information. Patients with telomere biology disorders (TBDs) are subject to multiorgan manifestations, which include the liver. However, due to the rare nature of the disorder, these manifestations are not fully characterized.Methods:Here, we describe the hepatic manifestations of patients with TBD at a large tertiary center within a specialized TBD clinic; 121 consecutive patients with TBD seen from 2017 to 2024 were included. The median age of the TBD diagnosis was 43 years old, with 60% male. The most common pathogenic/likely pathogenic variant was TERT (25%), followed by RTEL1 (10%) and TERC (7%).Results:Evidence of hepatic involvement in TBD was observed in 60% of patients (73 of 121), as determined by imaging studies or abnormal liver chemistries. Hepatic steatosis was the most prevalent imaging finding (56%), while a cholestatic pattern of liver injury was the predominant laboratory abnormality (73%). Biopsy-proven portosinusoidal vascular disorder was present in 4 patients. Findings of portal hypertension were observed in 25 patients (34%), with decompensation observed in 12 patients (16%). Traditional liver imaging modalities (computer tomography, ultrasound, and MRI) were compared with magnetic resonance elastography in 41 patients. Of these, 7 patients had abnormal magnetic resonance elastography with normal traditional imaging (17%), indicating higher sensitivity of magnetic resonance elastography to detect liver involvement.Conclusions:This study describes the liver manifestations within a cohort of patients with TBD with findings of portosinusoidal vascular disorder and hepatopulmonary syndrome similar to other cohorts and a noted higher prevalence of hepatic steatosis as compared with the general population. Liver stiffness measurements, integrated with imaging and laboratory testing, further improve characterization of hepatic manifestations in TBD.

INTRODUCTION

Telomeres are repetitive sequences of DNA located at the end of linear chromosomes that maintain chromosome integrity and stability.1,2 As organisms age, chromosomes are replicated and telomeres become shortened until they reach a certain threshold, triggering cellular senescence and/or age-related pathology. 3 Telomere biology disorders (TBDs) are a collection of diseases in which genetic pathogenic variants cause critically shortened average telomere length, which can present with multisystem disease, most commonly affecting the bone marrow, lungs, and liver.1,4

Hepatic manifestations of TBD have been broadly described in prior cohorts of TBD, with frequency ranging from 3% to 77%,5–10 reflecting heterogeneity in cohorts and the lack of standardized liver screening. The most commonly described hepatic manifestations of TBD are advanced fibrosis and portosinusoidal vascular disorder (PSVD), 11 with heterogeneity of imaging and liver test elevations. Shorter telomere length, irrespective of genetic mechanism, has been associated with an increased risk of cirrhosis in population studies.12,13 PSVD, characterized clinically with portal hypertension in the absence of cirrhosis and histologically by regions of nodular regenerative hyperplasia, obliterative portal venopathy/portal vein stenosis, and incomplete septal fibrosis/cirrhosis, has been linked to extremely short telomeres, implicating undiagnosed TBD.14,15 TBD-associated liver disease can also affect other organs, the most well-described of which is hepatopulmonary syndrome.

Liver involvement in TBD is often found incidentally by abnormalities in liver chemistries or imaging. Imaging modalities such as CT, MRI, or ultrasound (US) can provide indications of liver disease, such as hepatic nodularity, steatosis, or abnormal morphology, as well as findings of portal hypertension. Noninvasive fibrosis staging using liver stiffness measurement (LSM) with vibration-controlled transient elastography or magnetic resonance elastography (MRE) can also be used in this population and may enable earlier detection of disease.16–18

The exact mechanism of TBD in liver disease is not clear and may vary based on the specific genetic pathogenic/likely pathogenic variant.19,20 There is a need for more descriptive data on the hepatic manifestations of TBD to better understand these populations. For that reason, we evaluated the patients who presented to our TBD clinic for hepatic manifestations with the results described here.

METHODS

Study cohort

We have established a TBD specialty clinic that includes clinical evaluation with telomere length and genetic testing. All research was conducted in accordance with both the Declarations of Helsinki and Istanbul and approved by our Mayo Clinic Institutional Review Board. (ID: 16-004173) All patients provided written consent. Fluorescent in situ hybridization flow cytometry (flowFISH) is used to determine average telomere length in different peripheral blood cell lineages. Tests were performed as part of routine clinical evaluation in a CLIA-approved lab. 21 Patients with lymphocyte telomere length below the 10th percentile compared with age-matched controls and/or presence of a pathogenic variant associated with TBD were included in this study. Patients are routinely evaluated for pulmonary, hematologic, and hepatic manifestations of TBD.

Genetic testing

Germline variants related to TBD were evaluated using a CLIA-approved in-house designed gene panel including all TBD-related genes. Variants were curated according to ACMG guideline standards 22 and reported back to the clinical team for evaluation as described.23–25

Imaging of the liver

Liver imaging included US, CT, and/or MRI. Abnormal imaging was determined by the presence of any findings of steatosis, cirrhosis, or hepatic masses, or sequelae of portal hypertension, such as ascites or portosystemic shunts. The first date of abnormal imaging was considered the date of liver disease diagnosis unless patients had previously presented with abnormal liver tests.

From the MRE, when available, liver and spleen stiffness were obtained, as well as the proton density fat fraction. The stage of liver fibrosis was determined from liver stiffness; stiffness was considered normal if <2.5 kPa, normal to inflamed if between 2.5 and 2.9 kPa, stage 1–2 from 2.9 to 3.5 kPa, stage 2–3 from 3.5 to 4 kPa, stage 3–4 from 4 to 5 kPa, and stage 4 to cirrhosis for >5 kPa. Hepatic steatosis was considered if the proton density fat fraction was >5%.

Patients were determined to have portal hypertension if they had the presence of portosystemic shunts or ascites on imaging, esophageal varices or portal hypertensive gastropathy on EGD, or an HVPG >5.

Liver chemistries

Laboratory liver tests were routinely obtained, including AST, ALT, total bilirubin, indirect bilirubin, and ALP. R-factor calculations were utilized to determine the pattern of liver injury (cholestatic, hepatocellular, or mixed). The first presentation of elevated liver tests was considered for the date of liver disease diagnosis, unless patients had previously presented with abnormal liver imaging, and was utilized for R-factor calculations.

Four patients had single episodes of elevated liver tests with subsequent returns to baseline and thus were considered not to have hepatic manifestations of TBD. Another patient had elevated liver tests following danazol treatment that normalized after discontinuing the treatment; this patient was also excluded from the cohort with hepatic manifestations of TBD.

Liver biopsy

Liver biopsies were taken for clinical indications, and considered if at least 10 portal tracts were visible. Evaluations of the biopsies were performed by board-certified pathologists.

RESULTS

Population characteristics

A total of 180 patients were referred to our TBD clinic (Figure 1). The majority of patients (30%) are referred from Pulmonary Medicine, with the other major sources being self-referral (20%), Hematology and Oncology (20%), and Medical Genetics (20%). All patients subsequently underwent both germline and somatic genetic testing and telomere length screening via FlowFISH. A total of 121 patients met the TBD criteria of lymphocyte telomere length below the tenth percentile for age, 108 at Mayo Clinic in Rochester, Minnesota and 14 in Scottsdale, Arizona.

Hyperlipidemia was also more frequent in these patients, although not statistically significant. Other risk factors for liver disease, such as alcohol use disorder and chronic hepatitis B and C infection, were not significantly different between the 2 groups.

Of these, 73 patients (60.3%) had hepatic manifestations, 50 patients (41.3%) had bone marrow failure, and 79 (65.3%) patients had pulmonary manifestations (Table 2). Most recipients of transplant underwent lung transplantation (26 patients, 21.5%), followed by bone marrow transplantation (8 patients, 6.6%) and liver transplantation (3 patients, 2.5%) (Table 2). There were 34 deaths (28.1%) within the overall cohort, primarily attributed to pulmonary causes and some resulting from multiple contributing factors. Solid malignancy was observed in 46 patients (38.0%). In addition, 19 patients (15.7%) started androgen therapy for TBD.

In considering pathogenic/likely pathogenic variants in these patients, TERT was the most commonly affected gene in both those with and without liver disease (n=15 in both cases). In the overall TBD cohort, RTEL1 (n=12), TERC (n=8), and then NAF1 (n=4) were the next most common. There were no significant differences in genetic variants between those with hepatic manifestations and those without.

Characterization of liver disease

Among the 73 patients with liver involvement, 48 patients (65.8%) had abnormalities in either laboratory tests or imaging, while 25 patients (34.2%) had abnormal findings in both (Table 3). When considering the first instance of liver test elevation for these patients, liver injury patterns were classified as hepatocellular, cholestatic, or mixed based on the relative elevations of aminotransferases and ALP (R-ratio), a framework commonly used to describe patterns of acute liver injury and to guide diagnostic evaluation. Although this classification is not intended to define chronic liver disease, it characterizes the initial biochemical presentation. The pattern was primarily cholestatic (n=33, 73.3%) with fewer patients presenting mixed (n=7, 15.6%) or hepatocellular (n=5, 11.1%) patterns.

In those with abnormal imaging, assessment of the earliest abnormal imaging demonstrated that the majority had findings of steatosis (n=28, 56%). Hepatic nodularity was the initial abnormal imaging finding in 14 patients (28%). Further analysis of subsequent imaging studies in patients with liver involvement identified features of portal hypertension in 25 patients (34.2%), primarily splenomegaly (n=20, 27.4%) and ascites (n=17, 23.2%). Portosystemic shunts were identified in 7 patients (9.6%). A total of 44 patients with hepatic manifestations underwent an upper endoscopy, of which 8 patients (11%) had gastroesophageal varices and 10 patients (13.7%) had portal hypertensive gastropathy.

Noninvasive liver disease assessments were also calculated with laboratory values closest to the time of first detection of liver involvement (based on abnormal liver chemistries or imaging). The median Albumin-Bilirubin Grade for Hepatocellular Carcinoma (ALBI) was −2.85, with the majority of patients being classified as grade 1 (≤−2.60) (34 patients, 46.6%), then Grade 2 (>−2.60 to ≤−1.39) (11 patients, 15.1%), and only 3 patients (4.1%) being Grade 3 (>−1.39). The median AST to Platelet Ratio Index (APRI) was 0.75, with 14 patients (19.2%) having an APRI >1.5. The median Fibrosis-4 Index for Liver Fibrosis (FIB-4) was 2.84, with 23 patients (31.5%) having an FIB-4 >2.67, warranting further investigation and a high likelihood of advanced fibrosis. Of patients with APRI above 1.5 or FIB-4 >2.67 (28 patients), 16 underwent either biopsy or elastography. Of these, 5 (31.3%) had normal liver stiffness, 3 (18.8%) had early-stage fibrosis (stage 1–2), 6 (37.5%) had advanced fibrosis (stage 3–4), and 2 (12.5%) had PSVD.

Liver biopsies were obtained in 17 patients. Sixteen of the biopsies had some degree of fibrosis, with 6 demonstrating stage 1 fibrosis, 4 having stage 2–3 fibrosis, and 6 demonstrating stage 3 fibrosis to cirrhosis. Two patients had biopsies with steatosis. Findings indicative of nodular regenerative hyperplasia were observed in 2 patients; this included findings of zone 3 congestion with alternating areas of hepatocyte regeneration and atrophy. An additional 2 patients had findings of chronic venous outflow impairment without clear regions of regenerative nodules. Both of these patients had findings of portal hypertension with negative cardiac workup and no evidence of venous outflow obstruction (eg, Budd-Chiari syndrome), likely indicating PSVD. None of these patients with suspected PSVD had PVT.

MRE as a screening method for liver disease

Of our cohort of 121 patients, 50 underwent MRE. Of these, 26 had normal LSM (<2.5 kPa) on MRE, with 8 patients having elevated LSM (>4 kPa) suggestive of advanced fibrosis (stage 3–4). Half of the patients evaluated met the criteria for steatosis (>5% proton density fat fraction) (Table 4). When considering patients with liver disease who had undergone MRE (n=28), those who had findings of portal hypertension (n=12) had a higher liver stiffness of 4.06 kPa as compared with a liver stiffness of 2.47 in those without portal hypertension (n=16) (p=0.017). In addition, patients with portal hypertension also had stiffer spleens (8.41 kPa vs. 4.69 kPa, p=0.012) (Supplementary Table 1, https://links.lww.com/HC9/C443). However, the spleen-to-liver stiffness ratio was similar in patients with and without findings of portal hypertension (2.15 vs. 1.95, p=0.61).

Forty-one patients underwent both MRE and at least 1 additional imaging modality (ie, CT, US, or MRI). Routine imaging findings and MRE results were congruent in 27 cases; however, discrepancies were noted in 14 patients (Table 4). Among the 4 patients with normal MRE yet abnormal findings on other imaging modalities, 2 had significant intervals between their normal MRE and subsequent abnormal imaging, likely reflecting disease progression during that period. Another case had a noted nodular liver contour on CT scan, but a liver stiffness on MRE of 1.9 kPa, therefore suggestive of PSVD. The majority of discrepancies were observed in those with abnormal MRE and normal imaging (n=7). Of these cases, 3 patients had steatosis that was not observed on other imaging, 3 had mild to moderate fibrosis, and 1 patient had advanced fibrosis.

Ten patients had both MRE and liver biopsies. In 9 of these, fibrosis staging by histology was concordant with MRE results. The patient with discordant findings had histological features of PSVD and stage 1 fibrosis, with clinical findings of portal hypertension, while the MRE showed a liver stiffness of 4.8 kPa (Table 4).

Long-term liver outcomes

The mean follow-up time from the first abnormal liver chemistry or imaging of the cohort is 5.86 years. Of the 73 patients with TBD with liver disease, 12 had a decompensating event (16.4%) (Table 5). Patients who decompensated had higher ALBI (−2.16 vs. −2.87, p=0.0020) when compared with patients who did not decompensate (Supplemental Table S2, https://links.lww.com/HC9/C444). Of these, 3 patients had a pathogenic/likely pathogenic variant in TERT, with 1 patient having a pathogenic/likely pathogenic variant in RTEL1. Eight patients had no specifically identified pathogenic/likely pathogenic variants in TBD-related genes based on our CLIA-approved in-house gene panel. The mean time to first decompensation was around 3.2 years from the initial abnormal laboratory or imaging test. Nine patients had ascites, 8 patients had HE, and 5 patients had variceal bleeding. Within those with PSVD (n=4), 3 patients decompensated, with all 3 having ascites and 2 having HE. One patient was diagnosed with HCC in the cohort and underwent liver transplant with no identified pathogenic/likely pathogenic variants in TBD-related genes. Eleven patients (15%) in our cohort had hepatopulmonary syndrome as diagnosed by a transthoracic contrast echocardiogram.

Three patients underwent liver transplantation in our cohort. None of them had any significant transfusion requirements or cytopenias following transplant. One patient died four years following transplant due to squamous cell lung carcinoma.

DISCUSSION

Our study describes a cohort of 121 patients, of which 73 (60%) patients were found to have hepatic manifestations related to TBD, well within the range of published TBD studies.5–10 Steatosis was the most common liver abnormality in our cohort, present in 38% among those who underwent hepatic imaging and 44% of those who underwent MRE. In a population study of Olmsted County, MN, where our clinic is located, 23% of MRE-screened community members were found to have steatosis. 26 Given this, our MRE-screened patient population demonstrates a higher prevalence of steatotic liver disease than the local population. In our cohort, patients with TBD with liver disease have a higher prevalence of arterial hypertension and diabetes mellitus type 2 as compared with those with TBD without liver disease, suggesting a metabolic risk factor component to the development of steatosis. While steatosis in patients with TBD has not been described in other studies, it is important to consider, given its high prevalence in the general population and potential progression toward fibrosis and cirrhosis. Decreased telomere length has been associated with a higher metabolic risk profile,27,28 which is theorized to be due in part to oxidative stress. 29 Elucidation of the molecular interplay between metabolic syndrome and telomere length will be essential to outline the underpinnings of these risk factors that may contribute to the development of hepatic steatosis. Further longitudinal studies are needed to determine the path of progression of steatosis in patients with TBD and if the TBD genetic background predisposes patients with steatosis to a higher risk of cirrhosis progression.

PSVD is a hepatic manifestation commonly described in TBD cohorts.15,16,30 Within the 17 patients who had undergone liver biopsy, 4 had findings suggestive of PSVD. Of them, 3 had clinical complications of portal hypertension. This aligns with the findings of Coukos et al, 14 who described a higher rate of portal hypertension in patients with PSVD with short telomeres. These findings underscore the importance of considering PSVD in patients with TBD with hepatic manifestations and assessing for portal hypertension.

Hepatopulmonary syndrome is another manifestation commonly described in TBD populations. Eleven patients (15.1%) were found to have hepatopulmonary syndrome in our cohort; however, it is important to note that only 34 patients were screened with a contrast echocardiogram, thus limiting the ability to detect mild hepatopulmonary syndrome in the full cohort. Similarly, a study from Gorgy et al 31 described the patients with TBD with progressive dyspnea and found that 21% of patients had hepatopulmonary syndrome. Of the 3 patients in our cohort who underwent liver transplantation, one had hepatopulmonary syndrome and another had portopulmonary hypertension, both with documented resolution of their oxygen requirements following transplantation. An international multicenter cohort of patients who received liver transplantation due to TBD-associated liver disease similarly found a high incidence of hepatopulmonary syndrome (40%) with improved respiratory status following transplantation in 57% of these patients with pretransplant hypoxemia. 32 Pulmonary disease was the leading cause of mortality in our cohort, which has been similarly reported by a study of the UK Biobank, which found the most significant association for mortality in patients with reduced telomere length being respiratory diseases. 33 Pulmonary manifestations in our cohort were observed to be trending toward a higher proportion of patients with liver disease than those without liver disease (72.3% vs. 54.2%, p=0.0589). TBD diagnosed later in life is associated with pulmonary and hepatic manifestations, more so than bone marrow failure, which could be the cause of this association. However, the genetic and clinical determinants of each, individually or together, warrant further investigation. Of note, RTEL1 was the most prevalent gene affected, which may be due to the largest proportion of patients being referred from Pulmonary Medicine, which does highlight that pulmonologists are generally more familiar with TBD than hepatologists, leading to bias in patient selection and referral.

A major strength of this study was the use of MRE for screening for hepatic abnormalities in a large cohort of patients with TBD. Our large subset of patients who underwent MRE at the time of presentation to the TBD clinic underscores the potential role of MRE to screen for liver involvement in patients with TBD. MRE has been shown to be more accurate than US-based transient elastography in detecting both fibrosis and steatosis.34–36 When screening with MRE alone, half of our patients were found to have liver abnormalities, which is greater than the 39% captured by traditional imaging. MRE also demonstrated higher liver and spleen stiffness in patients with portal hypertension. While higher liver stiffness likely correlates with increased fibrosis stage subsequently leading to portal hypertension, increased spleen stiffness is a novel predictor of portal hypertension agnostic to disease etiology. This is particularly relevant in a population at increased risk of noncirrhotic portal hypertension, such as patients with TBD. Previous studies have shown that liver stiffness of ≤4.7 kPa and findings of portal hypertension are suggestive of PSVD, and, while this cannot be confirmed in the absence of a biopsy, it is an important finding with significant clinical implications.

Some key limitations should be recognized in our study. Our multidisciplinary TBD clinic was launched in 2017; consequently, the short follow-up interval restricts longitudinal assessment of liver disease development in patients without baseline involvement. For those presenting with liver disease at baseline, the median follow-up duration was 6 years, which likewise constrains analysis of long-term outcomes and comprehensive characterization of disease progression. Our criteria for inclusion in this study were lymphocyte telomere length below the tenth percentile, which could exclude patients with TBD due to telomere misfolding. While the TBD genetic screening panel is comprehensive, it is well understood that nearly half of patients with TBD do not have an identifiable pathogenic/likely pathogenic variant 4 which represents another limitation of the study and current clinical practice.

In summary, we describe a large cohort of adult patients with TBD whose liver disease has been characterized thoroughly with imaging, laboratory tests, and biopsy. Given the importance of early detection of liver disease for risk stratification, surveillance, and prevention of progression, our experience suggests a role for systematic screening with emphasis on LSM in patients with TBD.
