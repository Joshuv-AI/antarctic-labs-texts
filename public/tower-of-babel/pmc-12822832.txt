Multimedia tools in preoperative patient education: A systematic review of their role in neuraxial anesthesia
Authors: Kimia Khonakdar, Raheleh Charmchi, Parisa Moradimajd, Atiyeh S. Sajadi, Shahnam S. Maroufi, Alireza Babajani
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Preoperative anxiety is a major concern for patients undergoing neuraxial anesthesia, often leading to increased physiological stress and reduced satisfaction. Recently, multimedia-based education has been encouraged as a new approach to enhancing patient understanding and reducing anxiety. This paper investigates whether multimedia-based patient education improves preoperative anxiety, patient satisfaction, and hemodynamic stability in patients undergoing neuraxial anesthesia. According to PRISMA 2020 guidelines, a comprehensive search was conducted across multiple databases, including Scopus, Web of Science, PubMed, and Google Scholar. The selection criteria included interventional studies assessing multimedia-based preoperative education compared to conventional methods. Anxiety levels, patient satisfaction, and hemodynamic parameters were analyzed. A total of 10 studies involving 1016 patients were included. Nine studies demonstrated a significant reduction in preoperative anxiety among patients receiving multimedia education, while one study found no significant difference. Patient satisfaction was significantly higher in multimedia-educated groups, suggesting an improved patient experience. Additionally, three studies reported better hemodynamic stability, including lower heart rates and blood pressure fluctuations, in patients exposed to multimedia education. Multimedia-based education effectively reduces preoperative anxiety, improves patient satisfaction, and enhances hemodynamic stability in neuraxial anesthesia. However, variations in content, duration, and study design highlight the need for further high-quality research to standardize multimedia interventions and assess long-term outcomes.

Introduction

Anxiety is a subjective state characterized by a vague sense of unease or dread, frequently accompanied by autonomic nervous system activation, arising from the anticipation of potential danger.[1] Surgical patients commonly experience preoperative anxiety, which can commence as soon as the surgical intervention is scheduled. A significant contributor to this anxiety is the fear associated with anesthesia and its potential complications. Fears about surgery and anesthesia may change both physiological and psychological parameters. Elevated anxiety levels can induce sympathetic nervous system hyperactivity, manifesting as hypertension, arrhythmia, and palpitations. Additionally, it has been observed that these patients may experience a higher incidence of postoperative complications, including nausea, vomiting, and pain, as well as delayed recovery compared to those with lower preoperative anxiety.[2] These factors may lead to decreased patient satisfaction.

Neuraxial anesthesia provides exceptional analgesic benefits, facilitating early rehabilitation and discharge. Nevertheless, surgical procedures performed under neuraxial anesthesia present specific challenges to anesthesiologists as awake patients are susceptible to a range of anxiety-provoking visual and auditory stimuli.[2,3,4] In a study evaluating patients’ fears regarding anesthesia, the most common concerns reported in patients undergoing neuraxial anesthesia (such as spinal anesthesia) were the postoperative pain (49.8%) and fear of awakening during surgery (41.2%). Additionally, patients frequently expressed concerns about permanent paralysis and spinal injury.[5]

Audiovisual patient education has been shown to significantly increase patient satisfaction by enhancing understanding of treatment and reducing anxiety.[6,7] Using multimedia materials to deliver both visual and auditory information simultaneously activates various cognitive areas in individuals.[8,9,10] Several studies indicate that patients receiving multimedia education, such as videos, report higher satisfaction and better comprehension of procedures compared to those without such interventions.[11,12,13]

Previous systematic reviews have not comprehensively assessed anxiety, patient satisfaction, and hemodynamic stability during neuraxial anesthesia. Unlike prior research, this study aims to uniquely investigate the effect of multimedia-based video information on these outcomes, providing a broader perspective on its role in enhancing perioperative care and optimizing patient experience.

Materials and Methods

Study design and setting

This systematic review was conducted in alignment with the PRISMA 2020 guidelines (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses).[14] The present study aims to determine the effect of multimedia-based video information on perioperative anxiety and hemodynamic stability in patients undergoing neuraxial anesthesia.

Search study

The eligibility criteria for this systematic review were specified using the PICO framework including Population, Intervention, Comparison, and Outcomes. The operational definitions for the key variables in the research question that drive this systematic review are presented in Table 1. A comprehensive search strategy was developed by integrating key elements of the PICO framework, including terms like “instruction” (such as “patient education”, “patient information”, “patient teaching” and “health education”), “multimedia” (such as “multi-media”, “video”, “clip”, “audiovisual”, “audio-visual” and “animation”), and “neuraxial” (such as “spinal”, “epidural”, “caudal” and “subarachnoid”) [Table 2]. The first author (Kh K) conducted an initial search on July 22, 2024, across Scopus, Web of Science, PubMed, and Google Scholar for studies relevant to this systematic review. Publications were organized, and duplicates were removed using End Note (version 21). The comprehensive search strategy was formally registered on PROSPERO (Study Registration ID: CRD42025640307).

Study selection

The first author (Kh. K.) and corresponding author (Ch. R.) screened identified studies for eligibility based on titles and abstracts. Six authors evaluated the articles’ full text for eligibility until a consensus was reached. The first author (Kh. K.) and corresponding author (Ch. R.) independently verified the final article selection for eligibility, resolving discrepancies through discussion.

Selection criteria

Inclusion criteria

In this systematic review, we only included (1) studies that evaluated multimedia-based education’s effect on anxiety, patient satisfaction, and hemodynamic parameters in patients undergoing neuraxial anesthesia; (2) studies published between 2010 and 2024; (3) studies written in English; (4) participants aged 18 years or older; (4) interventional studies with two or more comparative groups; (5) educational approaches that included oral and/or written instruction as the control group, while the intervention group received multimedia-based education (videos, clips, audiovisual tour, or interactive voice over PowerPoint); and (7) studies reporting one or more outcomes (include anxiety, satisfaction, and hemodynamic instability).

Exclusion criteria

(1) Books, theses, gray literature, or conference abstracts; (2) unclear educational interventions; and (3) literature reviews, systematic reviews, scoping reviews, and meta-analyses.

Risk of bias

The Cochrane Risk of Bias-2 tool (RoB 2) was used to evaluate the RCTs (n = 9). The RoB 2 tool evaluated five domains: bias arising from the randomization process, deviations from planned interventions, missing outcome data, measurement of outcomes, and selection of the reported results. This tool categorizes the risk of bias for study outcome as “low risk,” “some concerns,” or “high risk.” The overall risk of bias rating is determined based on evaluations across five domains. Two reviewers (Kh. K. and Ch. R.) independently conducted assessments for all studies and resolved any differences collaboratively.[15] The Joanna Briggs Institute Critical (JBI) Appraisal Tool[16] was applied to the single pre-experimental study, which showed a moderate risk of bias due to the absence of a control group, limited control of confounding variables, and the outcome assessors’ awareness of group assignments [Appendix 1].

Results

Literature search

The initial search identified 162 citations. After removing duplicates, 108 citations were retained. A review of the titles and abstracts of these studies yielded 23 articles that appeared to be relevant. The studies were carefully examined and analyzed to confirm they met the inclusion criteria of the review. Finally, ten studies were considered potentially eligible. Figure 1 shows the PRISMA flow diagram for screening and selecting studies. Four studies came from India, three from Turkey, one from Jamaica, one from Indonesia, and one from Kenya. Nine studies were randomized controlled trials, and one was a pre-experimental study without the control group. In nine studies, patients underwent spinal anesthesia, while in one study, patients underwent combined spinal-epidural anesthesia. The types of surgeries include elective cesarean surgery in three studies, total knee joint replacement in one study, inguinal hernia repair in one study, lower abdominal surgeries except for cesarean section in another, one lower limb elective surgery study, and four studies of elective surgeries that can be performed with regional anesthesia.

Sample characteristics

These ten studies included a total of 1016 participants, including 531 (52%) in the multimedia-based education group. The remaining participants (48%) were included in the control group. The number of participants in each study varied from 38 to 200. The mean age varied from 35 to 66 years, so all participants were over 18. Women made up 60.3% of all participants. Three studies only included women due to the nature of cesarean surgery.[17,18,19] In seven studies, participants were classified as American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) class I-II[19,20,21,22,23,24,25]; one study included ASA class I-II-III,[11] while the classification was not mentioned in two studies.[17,18] Seven articles reported the level of education.[11,17,18,19,22,24,25] 28.5% of the participants had a tertiary level of education. Most of these studies excluded participants who had cognitive or sensory disorders, psychiatric disorders, visual or hearing impairment, psychiatric disorders, history of anxiety or depression, or previous regional anesthesia or were scheduled for general anesthesia. In one study, patients with a predicted surgery duration of more than 120 minutes were excluded from our research.[24]

Educational objectives

The objectives of education somewhat varied among the studies. Four studies focused on the neuraxial technique being performed on the patient.[20,23,24,25] Two research studies also focused on the university hospital’s surgical areas, including the preoperative room, the operating theater, and the recovery room; one of them referred to it as an audiovisual tour along with technique and physical space.[18,22] Three studies provided further insights into patient preparation, monitoring during surgery, appropriate positioning for the neuraxial block, and the expectations throughout the perioperative phase, right up to discharge from the recovery room.[11,19,21] One study focused on cesarean-section surgery with spinal anesthesia.[17]

Formats of multimedia

In all studies, the multimedia used to educate patients before regional anesthesia was in the form of video clips. In nine different studies, all participants completed a standard preoperative assessment, while one study failed to mention this aspect.[17] In one study, the intervention group received verbal and written explanations in addition to the video-based education.[11] A study mentioned the validity and standardization of the developed multimedia that was done by five anesthesiologists with more than 5 years of experience in anesthesia administration.[22]

Accessibility and duration and flexibility

The studies differed significantly in terms of how participants accessed the multimedia files as well as the duration and frequency of viewing.

In five studies, patients had access to multimedia the day before surgery.[20,22,23,24,25] Three studies only specified that multimedia viewing occurred during the preanesthesia checkup.[11,21,24] One study indicated that viewing took place immediately before surgery in the operating room,[18] while another simply stated that multimedia was provided to patients preoperatively.[17] The duration of the multimedia interventions varied among the studies, with video durations between 2 minutes and 45 seconds to 12 minutes, and two studies failed to mention.[17,20] Patients were allowed to rewatch the video as often as they wanted in two studies.[18,21]

Type of neuraxial anesthesia

The type of neuraxial anesthesia used varied to some extent across these studies. Eight studies referred to spinal anesthesia. In one study, spinal anesthesia was predominantly used, along with epidural anesthesia and combined spinal-epidural anesthesia.[19] In another study, only combined spinal-epidural anesthesia was employed.[20]

Control group

Out of the ten studies, nine included a control group for comparison, while one study did not have a control group.[17] In four studies, the standard preanesthetic review was considered as training for the control group.[18,19,22,25] In another four studies, training in the control group was provided orally (face-to-face),[20,21,23,24] and in two of these studies, this type of oral training was also provided to the intervention group.[20,21] A study found that the control group was given a combined approach of written and oral education.[11]

Outcome measures

Anxiety

All studies assessed anxiety as an outcome. A total of ten studies collected baseline data before the intervention: Five studies were conducted during the preanesthetic checkup,[11,18,21,23,24] three studies took place a day before the surgery,[19,20,22,25] and one study did not clearly specify the timing.[17]

The follow-up evaluations of anxiety levels after the intervention varied across the studies. Five studies conducted one follow-up assessment of anxiety measurements postintervention within 24 hours following the educational intervention before surgery.[17,18,19,20,21] Four studies conducted follow-up assessments twice, one preoperatively (on the day of surgery, before the patient underwent the procedure of the subarachnoid block) and one postoperatively (primarily in the recovery room).[11,22,23,25] Rajput et al.[24] evaluated anxiety three times as a follow-up: after the intervention, immediately before surgery, and following the surgery.

The tools employed to evaluate participant anxiety were diverse. Seven studies used the State-Trait-Anxiety Inventory (STAI).[18,19,20,21,23,24,25] The STAI has demonstrated sound internal consistency reliability and construct validity.[26] Among the studies, four also utilized additional tools alongside it, with two employing the Visual Analogue Scale (VAS),[19,23] one using the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS) and VAS,[25] and another incorporating the Amsterdam Preoperative Anxiety Information Scale (APAIS).[20] The HADS has shown reliability and validity in evaluating medical patients.[27,28] APAIS was integrated into four studies in total.[17,20,22,24] The APAIS has demonstrated internal consistency, construct, and content validity in adult patients before surgery.[29,30] In two of these studies, it was utilized independently,[17,24] while in one instance, it was combined with the VAS,[5] and in another, it was paired with the STAI as stated.[20]

Baran et al.[20] demonstrated significant changes in anxiety scores between the pretest and post-test in both groups (P = 0.001; P < 0.05) While the scores were reduced in the multimedia group (−4.8 ± 10.1), they increased in the control group (3.2 ± 6). Similar to this study, there was a significant increase in anxiety scores among the control group and a drop in anxiety scores within the multimedia group in another study.[21] Kumar et al.[22] showed a significant reduction in anxiety scores measured with VAS and APAIS as recorded in the preoperative room after being shown the prerecorded video of the operation theater with a P value of 0.001. In the study by Cakmak et al.,[11] no differences in the VAS score were found between the groups either before or after the information period. However, the change in the STAI-S score was significant in the intervention group (P < 0.001); on the other hand, statistically significant reduction in the intervention group (P = 0.018) and no significant difference between groups (P = 0.99) were revealed in Singh et al.[19] study. In Rajput et al.[24] study, patients in the test group (video) showed better/lower anxiety levels than the control group (nonvideo) at two-time evaluation after the intervention and showed a statistically significant difference (P < 0.001). One study without a control group showed a decrease in anxiety level from baseline after intervention (<0.05).[17] In two other studies, there was a significant reduction in state anxiety scores in the video-informed group compared to the control group.[23,25] Kanyeki et al.[18] revealed no statistically significant difference in change in STAI score between the video and control arms (P > 0.05).

Patient satisfaction

Four studies assessed patient satisfaction as an outcome measure.[11,19,22,23] Three studies assessed the satisfaction of patients, while one study evaluated the satisfaction levels of the anesthesiologists who performed the subarachnoid block.[23] In two studies, the researchers utilized a questionnaire featuring a 5-point Likert scale, and in another study, a 6-point Likert scale was used.[22] Singh et al.[19] used The Maternal Satisfaction Scale Score for Cesarean Section (MSSSCS) on the first postoperative day. The research did not offer details about the validity and reliability of the questionnaires. The satisfaction questionnaires had varying content. Cakmak et al.[11] evaluated satisfaction levels with the entire anesthesia procedure from the beginning of the preanesthetic interview to the end of surgery. One study focused on assessing the multimedia’s content.[19] Pandey et al.[23] evaluated physicians’ satisfaction based on how easily the procedure was executed. Each of the four studies compared the findings of the intervention group to a control group and consistently demonstrated statistically higher levels of satisfaction among the intervention group participants.

Hemodynamic parameters

Three studies measured hemodynamic parameters in patients as an outcome.[21,22,24] A variety of parameters were investigated in these studies. One study examined heart rate, mean arterial pressure, and respiratory rate.[22] The second study focused on systolic blood pressure, diastolic blood pressure, and heart rate.[24] Last, another study primarily investigated heart rate and mean arterial pressure as hemodynamic parameters.[21] Each of the three studies involved comparing baseline measurements with subsequent measurements at various time points as well as conducting comparisons against a control group. The increase in baseline values of these variables was reported to be less in the intervention group compared to the control group. However, one study did not find a significant difference in respiratory rate and mean arterial pressure between the two groups.

Conceptual framework

Discussion

Preoperative patient education can positively impact postoperative outcomes, improving patient satisfaction and ensuring that healthy knowledge, attitudes, behaviors, and habits are acquired.[31,32] Multimedia-delivered patient education can present information in new ways to adult patients undergoing neuraxial anesthesia. Our study examined the potential benefits of multimedia-based neuraxial anesthesia education for patients with various elective surgeries and discusses its clinical implications. Four main potential benefits for patients emerged in the synthesis of this systematic review: 1. reduced anxiety, 2. increased patient satisfaction, 3. increased anesthesiologist satisfaction, and 4. more stable patient hemodynamics. The ability to apply the findings more broadly is restricted by some heterogeneities such as variability in the content, duration, and format of multimedia files, poor methodological quality, and the small number of studies that may have impacted the direct comparison of results.

This systematic review found that multimedia-based education approaches can decrease anxiety in patients receiving neuraxial anesthesia. Of the ten studies, nine showed a significant reduction in perioperative anxiety, except Kanyeki’s study,[18] which reported no significant effect. A possible explanation for this discrepancy could be differences in content structure, presentation style, and patient demographics. All included studies had baseline measurements, with only one conducted without a control group.[17] Cakmak et al.[11] found a lower STAI Anxiety score in the multimedia group. Pandey et al.[23] demonstrated a significant reduction in STAI scores compared to face-to-face education. Similarly, Singh et al.[19] reported lower VAS scores, and Kumar et al.[22] observed significant decreases in APAIS and VAS scores following multimedia-based education. This is likely because this approach provides accurate and easy-to-understand information, helping patients alleviate their fears and concerns when facing medical or surgical procedures. These findings align with the theories of multisensory learning, which emphasize that combining visual, auditory, and textual elements can enhance the learning process. This, in turn, increases a sense of reassurance and leads to a better understanding of medical procedures, ultimately reducing the stress and anxiety associated with them. However, Kanyeki et al.[18] found no statistically significant difference in the changes in anxiety levels between the multimedia and control groups. This can depend on factors such as the content designed, the presentation method, the quality of multimedia tools, and the characteristics of the target population.

Multimedia-based education has shown strong potential to enhance patient satisfaction regarding preoperative information and care. In this review, three studies assessed patient satisfaction.[11,19,22] all of which showed that this method during the preanesthetic interview increased satisfaction in patients undergoing neuraxial anesthesia. Singh et al.[19] discovered that patients experienced enhanced satisfaction when a straightforward educational anesthetic multimedia was utilized. Similarly, Cakmak et al. and Kumar et al.[11,22] found that patients in the multimedia group reported greater confidence and comfort regarding anesthesia procedures. A key explanation for this increased satisfaction is that multimedia tools simplify complex information through interactive and engaging formats, catering to different learning styles. By integrating text, images, and audio, multimedia provides a more immersive educational experience, reducing confusion and enhancing recall. Moreover, patients feel more in control of their perioperative experience, which improves their psychological readiness. However, variations in sample size and patient satisfaction measurement tools across studies may affect the generalizability of these findings, underscoring the need for standardized assessment methods.

The findings of this systematic review suggest that multimedia-based patient education may also benefit anesthesiologists, although this aspect remains underexplored in current literature. Among the ten reviewed studies, only one[23] specifically investigated anesthesiologists’ satisfaction, reporting that multimedia education significantly improved their experience by reducing patient anxiety, increasing cooperation, and minimizing the need for repeated explanations. This finding is crucial as enhanced patient preparedness can lead to more efficient preoperative procedures and reduced workload for medical staff. However, the limited number of studies focusing on anesthesiologists’ satisfaction underscores the need for further research. Future studies should examine factors such as the influence of multimedia format (e.g., videos vs animations), patient demographics, and the duration of educational exposure on anesthesiologist satisfaction. Additionally, investigating how multimedia education impacts communication between patients and medical staff could provide deeper insights into its overall clinical benefits.

Multimedia-based information has a beneficial impact on hemodynamic stability in patients undergoing neuraxial anesthesia, aligning with the study’s aim of assessing its role in reducing anxiety-related physiological responses. Three studies have demonstrated that this educational approach can improve key hemodynamic parameters, including heart rate, blood pressure, and respiratory rate, by alleviating patient anxiety.[21,22,24] Dias et al.[21] reported that patients within the video group exhibited significantly reduced HR and MAP preoperatively. Other studies similarly found that hemodynamic parameters were more effectively controlled, exhibiting less deviation from baseline values in the multimedia group compared to the control group. These findings align with psychophysiological theories suggesting a direct relationship between the mental state and hemodynamic responses. By providing accurate, understandable, and interactive information, multimedia tools may facilitate patient comprehension of the treatment process, thereby reducing fear-induced physiological stress responses that contribute to hemodynamic instability.

There are several limitations in the present review. The limited number of studies and lack of standardized designs reduce the generalizability of the findings. Methodological heterogeneity, including variations in assessment tools for anxiety, satisfaction, and hemodynamic stability, complicates result interpretation. Most studies focus on short-term effects, with little exploration of long-term impacts. Additionally, limited demographic diversity may affect applicability across different populations. The review was limited to studies from 2014 to 2024 and therefore might have omitted relevant research. Searches of gray literature databases and clinical trial registries were not conducted; thus, comprehensiveness is limited and the risk of publication bias might be increased. Despite these limitations, this study has several strengths. It follows PRISMA 2020 guidelines, uses a clear PICO-based approach, and includes studies from diverse countries, making the findings more applicable. By examining anxiety, patient satisfaction, and hemodynamic stability, this review offers a well-rounded view of how multimedia education can benefit patients undergoing neuraxial anesthesia.

Further research

Future research should examine additional factors influencing the effectiveness of multimedia education in neuraxial anesthesia. Long-term studies are needed to assess its sustained impact on anxiety and patient satisfaction across multiple procedures. Comparing different formats, such as animations, virtual reality, or interactive tools, could help identify the most effective approach. Additionally, examining cultural and demographic influences can help optimize its implementation for diverse patient populations. Moreover, studying its impact on patient–provider interaction during neuraxial anesthesia could highlight its role in improving communication and perioperative experiences.

Conclusion

Multimedia education effectively enhances patient understanding, reduces anxiety, and improves communication before neuraxial anesthesia. It is also linked to better hemodynamic stability. These findings support health policies emphasizing patient education, advocating for the integration of multimedia methods into standard educational guidelines. By incorporating these strategies into clinical practice, healthcare providers can ensure that patients are well-informed about the procedure, its benefits, risks, and expectations. This approach not only enhances patient comfort and understanding but also empowers individuals to actively engage in their care decisions, ultimately leading to better health outcomes. However, Further research with standardized designs, larger samples, and rigorous methods is needed. Examining long-term effects, cultural and social influences, and patient preferences for anesthesia choices can enhance multimedia education’s effectiveness across diverse healthcare settings.
