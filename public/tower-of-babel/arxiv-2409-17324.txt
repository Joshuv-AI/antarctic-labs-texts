A new systems theory perspective on canonical Wiener-Hopf factorization on the unit circle
S. ter Horst, M. Kurula, A.C.M. Ran

Abstract
. We establish left and right canonical factorizations of Hilbert-space operator-valued functions G ⁡ ( z ) G(z) that are analytic on neighborhoods of the complex unit circle 𝕋 {\mathbb{T}} and the origin 0 0 and that have the form G ⁡ ( z ) = I + F ⁡ ( z ) G(z)=I+F(z) with F ⁡ ( z ) F(z) taking strictly contractive values on 𝕋 {\mathbb{T}} . Such functions can be realized as transfer functions of infinite dimensional dichotomous discrete-time linear systems, and we employ the strict bounded real lemma for this class of operators, together with associated Kreĭn space theory, to derive explicit formulas for the left and right canonical factorizations.

1.
Introduction

Let
G(z)
be an operator-valued function which takes bounded and invertible values on a closed rectifiable contour
\Gamma
, and assume that
G(z)
is analytic on the contour; throughout this paper, “operator” will always mean bounded linear Hilbert space operator and when an operator is said to be invertible this implies that the inverse is bounded. We say that
G(z)
admits a
right canonical Wiener-Hopf factorization
on the contour
when
G(z)=V_{-}(z)V_{+}(z)
, where
V_{+}(z)
and
V_{+}(z)^{-1}
extend analytically on the domain inside
\Gamma
, while
V_{-}(z)
and
V_{-}(z)^{-1}
extend analytically on the domain outside
\Gamma
. Likewise, we say that
G(z)
admits
left canonical Wiener-Hopf factorization
on the contour when
G(z)=W_{+}(z)W_{-}(z)
, where
W_{+}(z)
and
W_{-}(z)
have the same properties as
V_{+}(z)
and
V_{-}(z)
, respectively. Such factorizations play an important role in establishing invertibility of Toeplitz, singular integral and Wiener-Hopf integral operators, and several other applications; See, e.g.,
[
11
,
12
,
15
,
16
,
17
,
19
,
20
,
21
]
. An extensive review of the literature and an overview of results concerning these factorizations and their applications can be found in
[
22
]
.

In the seventies a series of papers by Gohberg and Leiterer appeared around the topic of canonical factorization. Most of these papers are in Russian; in English, most of the results contained in them can be found in
[
23
]
. One of the main results is the following: Let
G(z)=I+F(z)
be a Hilbert space operator-valued function that is analytic on a neighborhood of the unit circle
{\mathbb{T}}
, with
I
indicating the identity operator and with
\max_{z\in{\mathbb{T}}}\|F(z)\|<1
; since
F
is analytic on a neighborhood of
{\mathbb{T}}
, the function
\|F(z)\|
is continuous on the compact set
{\mathbb{T}}
and hence the maximum indeed exists. Then
G(z)
admits both left and right canonical Wiener-Hopf factorization (cf., Theorem 8.5.3 and Corollary 8.5.4 in
[
23
]
). A similar result holds for operator-valued functions with positive real part on the unit circle. In fact, these results hold true on any circular contour in the extended complex plane, that is, on circles and lines (which are interpreted as circles through infinity in the extended complex plane). What makes the result on the strictly contractive case so fascinating is that, in fact, this property characterizes the circular contours, in the following sense: If on a given Jordan curve
\gamma
, any rational matrix function
G(z)=I+F(z)
, with
\|F\|_{\infty,\gamma}<1
on
\gamma
, allows both left and right canonical Wiener-Hopf factorization, then it was shown in
[
29
,
32
]
that this curve must be a circle or a line; for this it in fact suffices to have both left and right canonical Wiener-Hopf factorization for
2\times 2
rational matrix functions.

Also in the seventies, the concept of
realization
of a rational matrix function was developed in the control engineering literature. This concept was applied to factorization of matrix and operator valued functions in
[
9
]
, which later on was revised, expanded and updated in
[
11
,
12
]
. In particular, in
[
12
]
the focus is on canonical factorization of rational matrix valued functions and applications as diverse as the transport equation, optimal control and
H^{\infty}
-control. In
Chapter 16 the bounded real lemma is discussed, see also, e.g.
[
28
]
. The bounded real lemma states that a rational matrix function in realized form takes contractive values on the unit circle or on the imaginary line if and only if a certain associated algebraic Riccati equation has a stabilizing solution.
Using the solution to the algebraic Riccati equation, for a strictly proper rational matrix function
F(z)
which has contractive values on the imaginary line, it is then shown that
G(z)=I+F(z)
admits canonical Wiener-Hopf factorizations, both left and right. These results are based on
[
24
]
. The analogue for rational matrix functions with a positive real part on the imaginary line was treated in
[
31
]
, in the same manner.

The proof of the result of Gohberg and Leiterer (see
[
23
]
) is based on fairly deep analytical tools. In contrast, the approach via realization theory using the bounded real lemma may be viewed as falling out of a combination of the main factorization approach of
[
9
]
with some theory of indefinite inner product spaces (see
[
4
,
18
]
). This is the approach taken in
[
24
]
. In the present paper we shall return to the theorem of Gohberg and Leiterer, in an infinite dimensional Hilbert space setting, but starting from a realization approach. Instead of the Riccati equation from the bounded real lemma, we use the Kalman-Yakubovich-Popov inequality (abbreviated to KYP-inequality). This inequality and its use for properties of the transfer function and the corresponding system was discussed in great detail in the infinite dimensional case in a series of papers by several groups of authors, e.g.
[
1
,
2
]
and
[
5
,
6
,
7
]
in discrete time, and for the continuous-time case in
[
3
,
8
]
. Our starting point will be the transfer function of a dichotomous system as in
[
7
]
. This will enable us to use methods and results from the theory of Kreĭn spaces, which can be found in
[
4
,
18
,
25
]
.
The theory of Kreĭn spaces has been applied to many factorization problems for matrix and operator functions that have special properties. For rational matrix valued functions see, e.g.,
[
12
]
. Perhaps the first application of methods of indefinite inner product spaces to factorization problems for operator polynomials are to be found in work of M.G. Kreĭn and H. Langer, see
[
26
,
27
]
.

We observe that the class of functions considered in
[
23
]
is slightly larger than the class of functions we consider. It turns out that the transfer function of a dichotomous system is a function which is analytic around the unit circle as well as at the origin, whose values are bounded linear operators between Hilbert spaces. In contrast,
[
23
]
considers functions which may not be analytic at the origin. The methods we apply enable us to provide explicit formulas for the factors, in contrast to
[
23
]
.

In
[
7
]
, basic theory of Hilbert-space linear discrete time invariant
dichotomous systems
of the form

\left\{\begin{aligned} x(n+1)&=Ax(n)+Bu(n)\\
y(n)&=Cx(n)+Du(n),\quad n\in{{\mathbb{Z}}^{+}},\quad x(0)=x_{0}~\text{given},\end{aligned}\right.

(1.1)

is discussed; here the word
dichotomous
refers to the main operator
A
, more specifically to the fact that
A
has no spectrum on the complex unit circle
{\mathbb{T}}
. The
transfer function
of (
1.1
) is

F(z)=D+zC(I-zA)^{-1}B,

defined for all
z\in{\mathbb{C}}
, such that
I-zA
is invertible:
z\in\rho(A)^{-1}\cup\{0\}
. We will, in particular, be interested in the case where

\|F\|_{\infty,{\mathbb{T}}}:=\max_{z\in{\mathbb{T}}}\|F(z)\|<1.

Our main result, for which we shall present much more detailed versions in Section 3, is the following.

Theorem 1.1

(main theorem, simplified)
.

Assume that the Hilbert space operator valued function
G(z)
is of the following type, with
A
dichotomous and
I+D
invertible:

G(z)=I+F(z),\qquad F(z)=D+zC(I-zA)^{-1}B,\qquad\|F\|_{\infty,{\mathbb{T}}}<1.

Then
G(z)
has a
right canonical Wiener-Hopf factorization

G(z)=V_{-}(z)\,V_{+}(z)
, i.e.,
V_{+}(z)
and
V_{+}(z)^{-1}
are analytic on a neighborhood of the closed unit disc
\overline{{\mathbb{D}}}
, while
V_{-}(z)
and
V_{-}(z)^{-1}
are analytic on a neighborhood of the closed complement
\overline{{\mathbb{E}}}
of
{\mathbb{D}}
.
There is also a
left
canonical Wiener-Hopf factorization
G(z)=W_{+}(z)\,W_{-}(z)
, where
W_{\pm}(z)
have the same properties as
V_{\pm}(z)
.

The paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we discuss the class of infinite dimensional, discrete-time dichotomous systems, focusing on the case where the transfer function has strictly contractive values on the unit circle. The strict KYP inequality provides us with an invertible selfadjoint operator
H
, which we use as the Gram operator for an indefinite inner product on the state space. That turns the state space into a Kreĭn space, and it turns out that in our case, the main operator
A
is a bicontraction in this Kreĭn space. Well-known consequences of this fact (e.g.,
[
4
]
, Theorem 3.2.1) are discussed concerning the existence of invariant maximal semidefinite subspaces, which are in fact uniformly definite.

In Section 3 the main results are presented in more detail. The proof makes use of the factorization approach of
[
9
]
. In particular, a large role is played by the main operator
A^{\times}
of the inverse system. It is shown that this operator too is a bicontraction in the Kreĭn space. Consequently, it is dichotomous as well. A result on matching of invariant subspaces of
A
and
A^{\times}
is then proved, and that is used to prove the main results.

The following notation will be used throughout the paper. We denote by
{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
(by
{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{X}})
) the sets of bounded linear operators from the Banach space
{\mathcal{X}}
to the Banach space
{\mathcal{Y}}
(linear operators on
{\mathcal{X}}
). For an operator
A
, we denote by
\sigma(A)
the spectrum of
A
, and by
\rho(A)
the resolvent set of
A
. The symbols
\prec
and
\succ
will be used to partially order selfadjoint operators on a Hilbert space
{\mathcal{X}}
; more precisely,
H\succeq 0
or
0\preceq H
means that
H
is positive semidefinite, while we write
H\succ 0
or
0\prec H
when
H\in{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{X}})
is uniformly positive, that is, for some
\eta>0
, we have

\langle Hx,x\rangle\geq\eta\,\|x\|^{2},\quad x\in{\mathcal{X}}.

By writing
H\preceq G
or
H\prec G
, we mean that
G-H\succeq 0
or
G-H\succ 0
, respectively. Otherwise, our operator theory notation and terminology follows the standard conventions, cf.,
[
9
,
20
,
21
]
.

Let
{\mathcal{K}}
be a Kreĭn space with indefinite inner product
[\cdot,\cdot]
. Then
{\mathcal{K}}
can be decomposed as
{\mathcal{K}}={\mathcal{K}}_{-}[\dotplus]{\mathcal{K}}_{+}
, where
{\mathcal{K}}_{+}
is a Hilbert space and
{\mathcal{K}}_{-}
is an anti-Hilbert space in the Kreĭn space inner product
[\cdot,\cdot]
; here
[\dotplus]
denotes a direct sum that is orthogonal with respect to that indefinite inner product. Such decompositions are called fundamental decompositions, and the norms associated to different fundamental decompositions via

\|x_{+}+x_{-}\|_{\mathcal{K}}^{2}:=\langle x_{+},x_{+}\rangle_{{\mathcal{K}}_{+}}-\langle x_{-},x_{-}\rangle_{{\mathcal{K}}_{-}},\qquad x_{\pm}\in{\mathcal{K}}_{\pm},

are all equivalent, and they turn
{\mathcal{K}}
into a Hilbert space.

For an operator
A
on
{\mathcal{K}}
, the Kreĭn space adjoint of
A
is denoted by
A^{[*]}
. For a subspace
{\mathcal{M}}
of
{\mathcal{K}}
, the space
{\mathcal{M}}^{[\perp]}
, called the orthogonal companion of
{\mathcal{M}}
in
{\mathcal{K}}
, is the set of all vectors
y\in{\mathcal{K}}
such that
[x,y]=0
for all
x\in{\mathcal{M}}
. A subspace
{\mathcal{M}}
of
{\mathcal{K}}
is called positive semidefinite (or negative semidefinite) when
[x,x]\geq 0
(or
[x,x]\leq 0
) for all
x\in{\mathcal{M}}
. The subspace is said to be maximal semidefinite when it is semidefinite and it is not contained in a strictly larger semidefinite subspace. The subspace
{\mathcal{M}}
is uniformly positive when
[x,x]\geq\delta\|x\|^{2}
for some
\delta>0
. The subspace
{\mathcal{M}}
is uniformly negative if
-{\mathcal{M}}
, the same space but with a change of sign in the inner product, is uniformly positive. A closed uniformly positive (closed uniformly negative) subspace is a Hilbert space (an anti-Hilbert space).

Every invertible selfadjoint operator
H
on a Hilbert space
{\mathcal{X}}
induces a Kreĭn space structure on
{\mathcal{X}}
via the indefinite inner product
[x,y]=\langle Hx,y\rangle,

x,y\in{\mathcal{X}}
. Then
H
is called the Gram operator of the induced Kreĭn space, and for an operator
S\in{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{X}})
, the adjoint in the indefinite inner product becomes
S^{[*]}=H^{-1}S^{*}H
.

2.
The strict bounded real lemma for dichotomous systems and associated Kreĭn spaces

In this section, we recall the strict bounded real lemma for dichotomous systems from
[
7
]
, and we present some new results in which we associate Kreĭn spaces with the selfadjoint operators that appear from the bounded real lemma. First we give a short review of infinite dimensional dichotomous systems, based on Section 2 of
[
7
]
.

2.1.
Dichotomous systems

Consider a linear discrete-time input-state-output system
\Sigma
over the nonnegative integers
{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}
given by

\Sigma:\quad\left\{\begin{aligned} x(n+1)&=Ax(n)+Bu(n),\quad x(0)=x_{0},\\
y(n)&=Cx(n)+Du(n),\quad n\in{{\mathbb{Z}}^{+}}.\end{aligned}\right.

(2.1)

Here the input sequence
(u(n))_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}}
, state sequence
(x(n))_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}}
and output sequence
(y(n))_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}}
take values in Hilbert spaces
{\mathcal{U}}
,
{\mathcal{X}}
and
{\mathcal{Y}}
, called the input, state and output spaces, respectively,
x_{0}\in{\mathcal{X}}
is the given initial state at time
n=0
, and
A\in{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{X}})
,
B\in{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{U}},{\mathcal{X}})
,
C\in{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
,
D\in{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{U}},{\mathcal{Y}})
are the main, control, observation and feedthrough operators, respectively. A triple
(u(n),x(n),y(n))_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}}
is called a
trajectory
of (
2.1
), if
u(n)\in{\mathcal{U}}
,
x(n)\in{\mathcal{X}}
,
y(n)\in{\mathcal{Y}}
for each
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}
, and (
2.1
) is satisfied. Sometimes we identify the system
\Sigma
with the
system colligation
given by the block operator matrix
\left[\begin{smallmatrix}A&B\\
C&D\end{smallmatrix}\right]
, also called the
system matrix
of
\Sigma
. The transfer function of
\Sigma
is given by

F_{\Sigma}(z)=D+zC(I-zA)^{-1}B\mbox{ for $z\in\rho(A)^{-1}\cup\{0\}$.}

(2.2)

It should be noted that although the transfer function is formally defined only for
z\in\rho(A)^{-1}\cup\{0\}
, it may happen that it has an analytic continuation to a larger domain. We shall use for this analytic continuation, if it exists, the same notation
F_{\Sigma}(z)
. The adjoint system
\Sigma^{*}
of
\Sigma
is the linear system with system matrix
\left[\begin{smallmatrix}A&B\\
C&D\end{smallmatrix}\right]^{*}=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}A^{*}&C^{*}\\
B^{*}&D^{*}\end{smallmatrix}\right]
.

The system
\Sigma
is called
dichotomous
if the main operator
A
is
dichotomous
, meaning that its spectrum
\sigma(A)
is disjoint from the complex unit circle
{\mathbb{T}}
, or, equivalently, there is a direct sum decomposition
{\mathcal{X}}={\mathcal{X}}_{-}\dotplus{\mathcal{X}}_{+}
of the state space such that

A=\begin{bmatrix}A_{-}&0\\
0&A_{+}\end{bmatrix}:{\mathcal{X}}_{-}\dotplus{\mathcal{X}}_{+}\to{\mathcal{X}}_{-}\dotplus{\mathcal{X}}_{+},

(2.3)

with
A_{-}=A|_{{\mathcal{X}}_{-}}\in{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{X}}_{-})
with
\sigma(A_{-})\subset{\mathbb{E}}:={\mathbb{C}}\setminus\overline{{\mathbb{D}}}
and
A_{+}=A|_{{\mathcal{X}}_{+}}\in{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{X}}_{+})
with
\sigma(A_{+})\subset{\mathbb{D}}
. In particular,
A_{-}
is invertible, and
A_{-}^{-1}
and
A_{+}
are stable. Observe that these subspaces are uniquely determined by
A
; in fact
{\mathcal{X}}_{-}
and
{\mathcal{X}}_{+}
are the spectral subspaces of
A
corresponding to
{\mathbb{E}}
and
{\mathbb{D}}
, respectively. We then call
(\mathcal{X}_{-},\mathcal{X}_{+})
the
dichotomous pair (of subspaces)
of
A
. More precisely, let

P_{+}:=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\mathbb{T}}(zI-A)^{-1}\,{\mathrm{d}}z

be the Cauchy integral of the resolvent of
A
over the unit circle (counterclockwise); then
P_{+}
is the so-called
spectral projection
of
A
corresponding to the unit disc. Its image
{\rm Im\,}P_{+}
is the subspace
{\mathcal{X}}_{+}
and its kernel
{\rm Ker\,}P_{+}
is the subspace
{\mathcal{X}}_{-}
.

2.2.
Realization

If
G(z)
is the transfer function of a dichotomous system, then it is clear that
G(z)
is analytic on a neighborhood of the unit circle
{\mathbb{T}}
, as well as on a neighborhood of zero.
In this subsection we show that the converse also holds.
Let
G(z)
be a
{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{U}},{\mathcal{Y}})
-valued function, for Hilbert spaces
{\mathcal{U}}
and
{\mathcal{Y}}
, which is analytic on a neighborhood of the unit circle
{\mathbb{T}}
as well as on a neighborhood of
0
. We claim that
G(z)
has a realization of the form

G(z)=D+zC(I-zA)^{-1}B,

(2.4)

where
A\in{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{X}})
is dichotomous,
B\in{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{U}},{\mathcal{X}}),C\in{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
and
D\in{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{U}},{\mathcal{Y}})
. To see this, we shall use results from
[
11
]
, in particular, from Section 4.2. First we observe that
G(z)
can be written as
G(z)=G_{i}(z)+G_{o}(z)
, where
G_{i}(z)
is analytic inside and on the unit circle, and
G_{o}(z)
is analytic outside and on the unit circle with value zero at infinity.

Let
0<r_{i}<1<r_{o}<\infty
be such, that the annulus
r_{o}\overline{{\mathbb{D}}}\cap r_{i}\overline{{\mathbb{E}}}
around
{\mathbb{T}}
is contained in the domain of analyticity of
G(z)
. Then
G_{i}(z)
is analytic on a neighborhood of
r_{o}\overline{{\mathbb{D}}}
and
G_{o}(z)
is analytic on a neighborhood of
r_{i}\overline{{\mathbb{E}}}
. In order to determine a realization for
G_{i}(z)
we follow the construction in Section 4.2, specifically in the proof of Theorem 4.3 in
[
11
]
, but now carried out on the Hilbert space
L^{2}(r_{o}{\mathbb{T}},{\mathcal{Y}})
, the space of square integrable measurable functions on the contour
r_{o}{\mathbb{T}}
with values in
{\mathcal{Y}}
, rather than the space
C(r_{o}{\mathbb{T}},{\mathcal{Y}})
of continuous functions on
r_{o}{\mathbb{T}}
with values in
{\mathcal{Y}}
. This leads to a realization of
G_{i}(z)
of the form

G_{i}(z)=\delta_{i}+\gamma_{i}(zI-\alpha_{i})^{-1}\beta_{i},

(2.5)

with
\delta_{i}\in{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{U}},{\mathcal{Y}})
,
\gamma_{i}\in{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{X}}_{i},{\mathcal{Y}})
,
\alpha_{i}\in{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{X}}_{i})
,
\beta_{i}\in{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{U}},{\mathcal{X}}_{i})
, where
{\mathcal{X}}_{i}=L^{2}(r_{o}{\mathbb{T}},{\mathcal{Y}})
, and
\sigma(\alpha_{i})=r_{0}{\mathbb{T}}
, so that

r_{o}{\mathbb{D}}\subset\rho(\alpha_{i})
. In particular,
\alpha_{i}
is invertible and we can rewrite (
2.5
) as

\displaystyle G_{i}(z)

\displaystyle=\delta_{i}-\gamma_{i}(I-z\alpha_{i}^{-1})^{-1}\alpha_{i}^{-1}\beta_{i}

\displaystyle=\delta_{i}-\gamma_{i}\alpha_{i}^{-1}\beta_{i}-z\gamma_{i}\alpha_{i}^{-1}(I-z\alpha_{i}^{-1})^{-1}\alpha_{i}^{-1}\beta_{i}.

For
G_{o}(z)
we consider the function
\widehat{G}_{o}(z):=G_{o}(1/z)
which is analytic on a neighborhood of
r_{i}^{-1}\overline{{\mathbb{D}}}
and apply in the same way the techniques from the proof of
[
11
, Theorem 4.3]
, modified as above, leading to a realization of
\widehat{G}_{o}(z)
of the form

\widehat{G}_{o}(z)=\delta_{o}+\gamma_{o}(zI-\alpha_{o})^{-1}\beta_{o}

with
\delta_{o}\in{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{U}},{\mathcal{Y}})
,
\gamma_{o}\in{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{X}}_{o},{\mathcal{Y}})
,
\alpha_{o}\in{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{X}}_{o})
,
\beta_{o}\in{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{U}},{\mathcal{X}}_{o})
, where
{\mathcal{X}}_{o}=L^{2}(r_{i}^{-1}{\mathbb{T}},{\mathcal{Y}})
, and
\sigma(\alpha_{o})=r_{i}^{-1}{\mathbb{T}}
, so that

r_{i}^{-1}{\mathbb{D}}\subset\rho(\alpha_{o})
. For
G_{o}(z)
We then have

G_{o}(z)=\delta_{o}+\gamma_{o}(z^{-1}I-\alpha_{o})^{-1}\beta_{o}=\delta_{o}+z\gamma_{o}(I-z\alpha_{o})^{-1}\beta_{o}.

Combining the two realizations for
G_{i}(z)
and
G_{o}(z)
above, we see that
G(z)
admits a realization of the form (
2.4
) with

D=\delta_{o}+\delta_{i}-\gamma_{i}\alpha_{i}^{-1}\beta_{i},\ \ C=\begin{bmatrix}-\gamma_{i}\alpha_{i}^{-1}&\gamma_{o}\end{bmatrix},\ \ A=\begin{bmatrix}\alpha_{i}^{-1}&0\\
0&\alpha_{o}\end{bmatrix},\ \,B=\begin{bmatrix}\alpha_{i}^{-1}\beta_{i}\\
\beta_{o}\end{bmatrix}.

Since
\sigma(\alpha_{i})=r_{o}{\mathbb{T}}
, it follows that
\sigma(\alpha_{i}^{-1})=r_{o}^{-1}{\mathbb{T}}
, and in particular
\alpha_{i}^{-1}
is stable. Then
\sigma(A)=r_{o}^{-1}{\mathbb{T}}\cup r_{i}^{-1}{\mathbb{T}}
, which does not intersect
{\mathbb{T}}
; thus
A
is dichotomous.

2.3.
The strict Bounded Real Lemma

We shall be interested in the situation where the supremum norm of the transfer function
F_{\Sigma}
on
{\mathbb{T}}
is strictly less than one, that is,
\|F_{\Sigma}\|_{\infty,{\mathbb{T}}}:=\sup_{z\in{\mathbb{T}}}\|F_{\Sigma}(z)\|<1
. Note that the supremum is actually a maximum by continuity of
F_{\Sigma}
and compactness of
{\mathbb{T}}
. The strict Bounded Real Lemma provides a necessary and sufficient criterium for this to happen, in terms of an operator matrix inequality of Kalman-Yakubovich–Popov (KYP) type; the next result is Theorem 7.1 from
[
7
]
.

Theorem 2.1
.

For a dichotomous system (
2.1
), the transfer function (
2.2
) satisfies
\|F\|_{\infty,{\mathbb{T}}}<1
if and only if there exists an invertible, selfadjoint operator
H
in
{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{X}})
which solves the
strict KYP inequality
for
\Sigma
, i.e.,

\begin{bmatrix}A&B\\
C&D\end{bmatrix}^{*}\begin{bmatrix}H&0\\
0&I_{\mathcal{Y}}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}A&B\\
C&D\end{bmatrix}\prec\begin{bmatrix}H&0\\
0&I_{\mathcal{U}}\end{bmatrix}.

(2.6)

In this case, the dimensions of the spectral subspaces of
A
over
{\mathbb{D}}
and
{\mathbb{E}}
correspond to the dimensions of the spectral subspaces of
H
over
(0,\infty)
and
(-\infty,0)
, respectively, in the sense that they are isomorphic as subspaces of
{\mathcal{X}}
.

2.4.
The associated Kreĭn spaces and bicontractivity of
\Sigma

Set
\Sigma=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}A&B\\
C&D\end{smallmatrix}\right]
, and assume that the strict KYP inequality (
2.6
) is satisfied for an invertible and selfadjoint operator
H
. We define the Kreĭn space
{\mathcal{K}}^{\mathcal{U}}
, which is equal to
{\mathcal{X}}\oplus{\mathcal{U}}

We have so far established that

Note that (

Lemma 2.3
.

Let

is dichotomous on

\pm{\mathcal{X}}_{\pm}
are maximal positive semidefinite subspaces of

and

is the dichotomous pair of

\pm{\mathcal{X}}_{\pm}
are Hilbert spaces in the inner product inherited from
{\mathcal{K}}
.

The operator

is uniformly bicontractive in

if and only if it is uniformly biexpansive in the Kreĭn space
-{\mathcal{K}}
by
[

, and by
[
4
, Thm 2.4.31]
it then holds that

. The invariance, existence and uniqueness of

follows from
[
4
, Thm 3.2.1]
, and the fact that
\mathcal{X}={\mathcal{X}}_{-}+{\mathcal{X}}_{+}
is given in (2.2) in the proof of that result. Theorem 3.2.1 in
[
4
]
also states that

is uniformly positive and

uniformly negative in

, and since every maximal semidefinite subspaces is closed, this implies that

and

are Hilbert spaces in the inner product inherited from

. From that fact, it follows that

, since every vector in this intersection satisfies

, so that

.

By the proof of

Assume that the transfer function

(3.2)

The proof will use the concept of a Banach limit, which we briefly recall here for self-containment. Let
\ell^{\infty}
denote the Banach space of bounded sequences
{{\mathbb{Z}}^{+}}\to{\mathbb{C}}

with the following four properties:

(1)

\phi({\bf x})=\lim_{n\to\infty}{\bf x}(n)

(2)

(3)

\phi(S{\bf x})=\phi({\bf x})

(S{\bf x})(n)={\bf x}(n+1)

​​, and

Property (4) makes comparison possible: If

for all

. Banach limits exist, by the Hahn-Banach theorem, but there does not exist a unique Banach limit. For the sake of the proof of the next lemma, it does not matter which Banach limit we choose, so we just fix one.

Let
H\in{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{X}})
be an invertible and selfadjoint operator, such that (
2.6
) holds. Write
{\mathcal{K}}
for the Kreĭn space obtained by equipping
{\mathcal{X}}
with the indefinite inner product with Gram operator
H
. By Lemma
3.3
, the operator
A^{\times}
is uniformly bicontractive in
{\mathcal{K}}
. By Lemma
2.3
, the operator
A^{\times}
is dichotomous, and in the dichotomous pairs
({\mathcal{X}}_{-},{\mathcal{X}}_{+})
and
({\mathcal{X}}_{-}^{\times},{\mathcal{X}}_{+}^{\times})
of
A
and
A^{\times}
, respectively, the spaces
\pm{\mathcal{X}}_{\pm}
and
\pm{\mathcal{X}}_{\pm}^{\times}
are all maximal positive semidefinite subspaces of
{\mathcal{K}}
, with

{\mathcal{X}}={\mathcal{X}}_{-}\dotplus{\mathcal{X}}_{+}={\mathcal{X}}_{-}^{\times}\dotplus{\mathcal{X}}_{+}^{\times};

(3.3)

moreover,
\pm{\mathcal{X}}_{\pm}
and
\pm{\mathcal{X}}_{\pm}^{\times}
are Hilbert spaces with respect to the Kreĭn space inner product of
{\mathcal{K}}
.
It then follows from
[
4
, Cor. 1.8.14]
that
-({\mathcal{X}}_{+})^{[\perp]}
and
({\mathcal{X}}_{-}^{\times})^{[\perp]}
are Hilbert subspaces of
{\mathcal{K}}
, with
[\perp]
indicating the Kreĭn space orthogonal complement. This again implies that
({\mathcal{X}}_{+})^{[\perp]}\cap({\mathcal{X}}_{-}^{\times})^{[\perp]}=\{0\}
. In order to establish that
{\mathcal{X}}_{+}+{\mathcal{X}}^{\times}_{-}
is dense in
{\mathcal{X}}
, let now
y\perp({\mathcal{X}}_{+}+{\mathcal{X}}^{\times}_{-})
. Set
x=H^{-1}y
, so that
Hx\perp({\mathcal{X}}_{+}+{\mathcal{X}}^{\times}_{-})
, i.e.,
x\in({\mathcal{X}}_{+})^{[\perp]}\cap({\mathcal{X}}_{-}^{\times})^{[\perp]}
; then
y=0
, and it follows that
{\mathcal{X}}_{+}+{\mathcal{X}}^{\times}_{-}
is dense. It remains only to prove that the sum
{\mathcal{X}}={\mathcal{X}}_{+}+{\mathcal{X}}^{\times}_{-}
is also direct and closed, because then
{\mathcal{X}}={\mathcal{X}}_{-}\dotplus{\mathcal{X}}^{\times}_{+}
follows by symmetry.

In order to obtain that the sum
{\mathcal{X}}_{+}+{\mathcal{X}}^{\times}_{-}
is closed and direct, we will apply
[
30
, Lemma 5.2]
. For this purpose let
\ell^{\infty}({{\mathbb{Z}}^{+}};{\mathcal{X}})
denote the Banach space of
{\mathcal{X}}
-valued bounded sequences over
{{\mathbb{Z}}^{+}}
together with the supremum norm, and, following
[
14
]
, set

{\mathcal{N}}:=\left\{{\bf x}\in\ell^{\infty}({{\mathbb{Z}}^{+}};{\mathcal{X}})\mid\langle{\bf x},{\bf x}\rangle=0\right\},

where

\langle{\bf x},{\bf y}\rangle:=\phi\big((\langle{\bf x}(n),{\bf y}(n)\rangle_{\mathcal{X}})_{n\in{{\mathbb{Z}}^{+}}}\big),\qquad{\bf x},{\bf y}\in\ell^{\infty}({{\mathbb{Z}}^{+}};{\mathcal{X}}),

(3.4)

is defined using our arbitrarily fixed Banach limit
\phi
. Then one can easily show that (
3.4
) defines an inner product on the quotient space
\ell^{\infty}({{\mathbb{Z}}^{+}};{\mathcal{X}})/{\mathcal{N}}
, whose Hilbert-space completion we denote by
\widetilde{\mathcal{X}}
. The Hilbert space
{\mathcal{X}}
is embedded into
\widetilde{\mathcal{X}}
by identifying
x\in{\mathcal{X}}
with the constant sequence with entries all equal to
x
, and it is clear that the restriction of the inner product on
\widetilde{\mathcal{X}}
to (the embedding of)
{\mathcal{X}}
corresponds to the inner product of
{\mathcal{X}}
via

\langle\iota x,\iota y\rangle=\langle x,y\rangle_{\mathcal{X}},\qquad\text{where}\qquad\iota x=(x,x,x,\ldots)\in\ell^{\infty}({{\mathbb{Z}}^{+}};{\mathcal{X}}),\quad x\in{\mathcal{X}}.

Likewise, any subspace
{\mathcal{M}}\subset{\mathcal{X}}
can be embedded into
{\widetilde{{\mathcal{X}}}}
, and we write
{\widetilde{{\mathcal{M}}}}
for the closure of
\iota{\mathcal{M}}
in
{\widetilde{{\mathcal{X}}}}
.

As in
[
14
, Section 4]
, for an operator
K
on
{\mathcal{X}}
, we note that the operator

{\bf x}+{\mathcal{N}}\mapsto(K{\bf x}(n))_{n\in{{\mathbb{Z}}^{+}}}+{\mathcal{N}},\qquad{\bf x}\in\ell^{\infty}({{\mathbb{Z}}^{+}};{\mathcal{X}}),

maps
\ell^{\infty}({{\mathbb{Z}}^{+}};{\mathcal{X}})/{\mathcal{N}}
boundedly into itself, with the same norm as
K
, and we denote by
\widetilde{K}
its continuous extension to a linear operator on
\widetilde{\mathcal{X}}
, which has norm
\|{\widetilde{K}}\|_{{\mathcal{B}}({\widetilde{{\mathcal{X}}}})}=\|K\|_{{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{X}})}
. In particular, the Gram operator
H
extends to an operator
{\widetilde{H}}
on
{\widetilde{{\mathcal{X}}}}
, and it is easily checked that
{\widetilde{H}}
is also selfadjoint and invertible, and hence the Gram operator of a Kreĭn space consisting of the vectors in
{\widetilde{{\mathcal{X}}}}
, which we denote by
{\widetilde{{\mathcal{K}}}}
.

By Lemma 5.1 in
[
30
]
, it follows that the closures
{\widetilde{{\mathcal{X}}}}_{+}
and
{\widetilde{{\mathcal{X}}}}_{-}^{\times}
in
{\widetilde{{\mathcal{X}}}}
of
\iota{\mathcal{X}}_{+}
and
\iota{\mathcal{X}}_{-}^{\times}
, respectively, are such that
{\widetilde{{\mathcal{X}}}}_{+}
is a maximal positive semidefinite subspace of
{\widetilde{{\mathcal{K}}}}
and
{\widetilde{{\mathcal{X}}}}_{-}^{\times}
is a maximal negative semidefinite subspace of
{\widetilde{{\mathcal{K}}}}
.
Recall that
{\mathcal{X}}_{+}
is unformly positive and
{\mathcal{X}}_{-}
is uniformly negative in the Kreĭn space
{\mathcal{K}}
. Taking any fundamental decomposition
{\mathcal{K}}={\mathcal{K}}_{+}[\dot{+}]{\mathcal{K}}_{-}
, it follows that with respect to this fundamental decomposition

{\mathcal{X}}_{+}={\rm Im\,}\begin{bmatrix}I\\
R_{+}\end{bmatrix},{\rm\ and\ }{\mathcal{X}}_{-}={\rm Im\,}\begin{bmatrix}R_{-}\\
I\end{bmatrix}.

Here
R_{+}
and
R_{-}
, the so-called angular operators,
are strict contractions because of the uniform positivity of
{\mathcal{X}}_{+}
and uniform negativity of
{\mathcal{X}}_{-}
.
Notice that

{\widetilde{{\mathcal{X}}}}_{+}={\rm Im\,}\begin{bmatrix}{\widetilde{I}}\\
{\widetilde{R_{+}}}\end{bmatrix}{\rm\ and\ }{\widetilde{{\mathcal{X}}}}_{-}={\rm Im\,}\begin{bmatrix}{\widetilde{R_{-}}}\\
{\widetilde{I}}\end{bmatrix},

and that
{\widetilde{R_{\pm}}}
are strict contractions.
It is then easily checked that the restrictions of the indefinite inner product of
{\widetilde{{\mathcal{K}}}}
to
{\widetilde{{\mathcal{X}}}}_{+}
and
-{\widetilde{{\mathcal{X}}}}_{-}
makes these subspaces into Hilbert spaces; then
\widetilde{\mathcal{X}}_{+}\cap\widetilde{\mathcal{X}}_{-}^{\times}=\left\{0\right\}
, and it follows from
[
30
, Lemma 5.2]
that the sum
{\mathcal{X}}_{+}+{\mathcal{X}}_{-}^{\times}
is both direct and closed.
∎

We remark that, in the last paragraph of the preceding proof,
{\widetilde{{\mathcal{X}}}}_{+}
is moreover invariant under
{\widetilde{A}}
and
{\widetilde{{\mathcal{X}}}}_{-}^{\times}
is invariant under
{\widetilde{A}}^{\times}
, again by Lemma 5.1 in
[
30
]
.

The following lemma is a rephrasing of parts of
[
11
, Theorems 2.1 and 2.8]
specialized to the function
G(z)
as in our main theorems. Since the proof is essentially the same as the proof in
[
11
]
, we refrain from giving the details here.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let

G(z)=I+D+zC(I-zA)^{-1}B,

with
I+D
invertible. Define
A^{\times}=A-B(I+D)^{-1}C
.
The following statements hold:

(i)

Assume that
z\in\rho(A)^{-1}\cup\{0\}
. Then
G(z)
has an inverse if and only if
z\in\rho(A^{\times})^{-1}\cup\{0\}
,
and in that case, for
z\in\rho(A^{\times})^{-1}\cup\{0\}
we have

G(z)^{-1}=(I+D)^{-1}-z(I+D)^{-1}C\left(I-zA^{\times}\right)^{-1}B(I+D)^{-1}.

(3.5)

(ii)

Assume that
{\mathcal{X}}
can be decomposed into a (not necessarily orthogonal) direct sum
{\mathcal{X}}={\mathcal{L}}\dotplus{\mathcal{L}}^{\times}
, where
A{\mathcal{L}}\subset{\mathcal{L}}
and
A^{\times}{\mathcal{L}}^{\times}\subset{\mathcal{L}}^{\times}
, and let
\Pi
denote the projection in
{\mathcal{X}}
onto
{\mathcal{L}}^{\times}
along
{\mathcal{L}}
. If
I+D=D_{1}D_{2}
with
D_{1}
and
D_{2}
invertible, then
G
factors as

G(z)=W_{1}(z)\,W_{2}(z),

where

\displaystyle W_{1}(z)

\displaystyle:=D_{1}+zC\left(I-zA\right)^{-1}(I-\Pi)BD_{2}^{-1}\qquad\text{and}

(3.6)

\displaystyle W_{2}(z)

\displaystyle:=D_{2}+zD_{1}^{-1}C\Pi\left(I-zA\right)^{-1}B,\quad z\in\rho(A)^{-1}\cup\{0\}.

(iii)

Under the assumption and with the notation of item (ii), the values of the functions
W_{1}(z)
and
W_{2}(z)
in (
3.6
) are invertible for
z\in\big(\rho(A)^{-1}\cap\rho(A^{\times})^{-1}\big)\cup\{0\}
with inverses given for
z
in the extended domain
\rho(A^{\times})^{-1}\cup\{0\}
by

\displaystyle W_{1}(z)^{-1}

\displaystyle=D_{1}^{-1}-zD_{1}^{-1}C(I-\Pi)\left(I-zA^{\times}\right)^{-1}B(I+D)^{-1}\qquad\text{and}

(3.7)

\displaystyle W_{2}(z)^{-1}

\displaystyle=D_{2}^{-1}-z(I+D)^{-1}C\left(I-zA^{\times}\right)^{-1}\Pi BD_{2}^{-1}.

We now turn to the proof of the main theorems. In this proof
the analytic extensions will be addressed and made explicit. In particular, for the functions
V_{+}(z)
and
V_{-}(z)
and their inverses we have formulas
(
3.10
), (
3.11
) and (
3.12
), below, where the operators involved are obtained from the block operator formulas of
A
,
A^{\times}
,
B
and
C
with respect to
{\mathcal{X}}={\mathcal{X}}_{-}\dot{+}{\mathcal{X}}^{\times}_{+}
given in (
3.8
), with the analytic extension claims following from (
3.9
). Similarly, the claims regarding
W_{+}(z)
and
W_{-}(z)
and their inverses follow from (
3.15
), (
3.13
) and (
3.14
), below.

Proof of Theorems
3.1
and
3.2
.

By assumption,
A
is dichotomous, and
A^{\times}
is dichotomous by Lemma
3.4
. The dichotomous pair
({\mathcal{X}}_{-},{\mathcal{X}}_{+})
of
A
provides two invariant (even reducing) subspaces of
A
, and likewise the dichotomous pair
({\mathcal{X}}_{-}^{\times},{\mathcal{X}}_{+}^{\times})
of
A^{\times}
gives two invariant subspaces of
A^{\times}
. From Lemma
3.4
we further get the decompositions
{\mathcal{X}}={\mathcal{X}}_{+}\dotplus{\mathcal{X}}^{\times}_{-}
and
{\mathcal{X}}={\mathcal{X}}_{-}\dotplus{\mathcal{X}}^{\times}_{+}
of
{\mathcal{X}}
. In particular, the projections
\Pi_{r}
and
\Pi_{\ell}
are well-defined.

Applying item (ii) of Lemma
3.5
to the decomposition
{\mathcal{X}}={\mathcal{X}}_{+}\dotplus{\mathcal{X}}^{\times}_{-}
, provides the factorization
G(z)=W_{+}(z)W_{-}(z)
of Theorem
3.2
, while applying the lemma to
{\mathcal{X}}={\mathcal{X}}_{-}\dotplus{\mathcal{X}}^{\times}_{+}
yields the factorization
G(z)=V_{-}(z)V_{+}(z)
of Theorem
3.1
. In both cases, the formulas for the inverses of the functions in the factorization follow directly from item (iii) of Lemma
3.5
.

It remains to prove that the functions
V_{+}(z)
,
V_{+}(z)^{-1}
,
W_{+}(z)
and
W_{+}(z)^{-1}
extend analytically to a neighborhood of
{\mathbb{D}}
, while
V_{-}(z)
,
V_{-}(z)^{-1}
,
W_{-}(z)
and
W_{-}(z)^{-1}
extend analytically to a neighborhood of
{\mathbb{E}}
. To see that this is the case for the factorization in Theorem
3.1
we consider the operators
A
,
A^{\times}
,
B
and
C
as block operator with respect to the decomposition
{\mathcal{X}}={\mathcal{X}}_{-}\dot{+}{\mathcal{X}}^{\times}_{+}
and note that they take the form

A=\begin{bmatrix}A_{11}&A_{12}\\
0&A_{22}\end{bmatrix},\ \ A^{\times}=\begin{bmatrix}A_{11}^{\times}&0\\
A_{21}^{\times}&A_{22}^{\times}\end{bmatrix},\ \ C=\begin{bmatrix}C_{1}&C_{2}\end{bmatrix},\ \ B=\begin{bmatrix}B_{1}\\
B_{2}\end{bmatrix}.

(3.8)

By the properties of the dichotomous pairs
A
and
A^{\times}
it follows that
\sigma(A_{11})\subset{\mathbb{E}}
and
\sigma(A_{22}^{\times})\subset{\mathbb{D}}
. Since we also have the decomposition
{\mathcal{X}}={\mathcal{X}}_{-}\dot{+}{\mathcal{X}}_{+}
and
{\mathcal{X}}_{+}
is also an invariant subspace of
A
, it follows from Lemma 5.9 in
[
11
]
that
A_{22}
and
A|_{{\mathcal{X}}_{+}}
are similar, and hence
\sigma(A_{22})=\sigma(A|_{{\mathcal{X}}_{+}})\subset{\mathbb{D}}
. By an analogous argument it follows that
\sigma(A_{11}^{\times})\subset{\mathbb{E}}
. Hence, we have

\sigma(A_{11})\subset{\mathbb{E}},\quad\sigma(A_{22})\subset{\mathbb{D}},\quad\sigma(A_{11}^{\times})\subset{\mathbb{E}},\quad\sigma(A_{22}^{\times})\subset{\mathbb{D}}.

(3.9)

Since
\sigma(A_{11})\cap\sigma(A_{22})=\emptyset
, we have that
\sigma(A)=\sigma(A_{11})\cup\sigma(A_{22})
, and likewise
\sigma(A^{\times})=\sigma(A_{11}^{\times})\cup\sigma(A_{22}^{\times})
, again by Lemma 5.9 in
[
11
]
. Next observe that with respect to the decomposition
{\mathcal{X}}={\mathcal{X}}_{-}\dot{+}{\mathcal{X}}^{\times}_{+}
, we have
\Pi_{r}=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}0&0\\
0&I\end{smallmatrix}\right]
and
I-\Pi_{r}=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}I&0\\
0&0\end{smallmatrix}\right]
. It then follows (see also
[
11
, Theorem 2.8]
) that

\displaystyle V_{+}(z)

\displaystyle=D_{2}+zD_{1}^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}C_{1}&C_{2}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}0&0\\
0&I\end{bmatrix}\left(I-z\begin{bmatrix}A_{11}&A_{12}\\
0&A_{22}\end{bmatrix}\right)^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}B_{1}\\
B_{2}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=D_{2}+zD_{1}^{-1}C_{2}(I-zA_{22})^{-1}B_{2},

(3.10)

from which it becomes clear that
V_{+}(z)
can be extended analytically via (
3.10
) to
\rho(A)^{-1}\cup\rho(A_{22})^{-1}\cup\{0\}=\rho(A_{22})^{-1}\cup\{0\}={\mathbb{C}}\setminus\sigma(A_{22})^{-1}
, which is an open neighborhood of
\overline{{\mathbb{D}}}
. Using item (i) of Lemma
3.5
shows that

V_{+}(z)^{-1}=D_{2}^{-1}-z(I+D)^{-1}C_{2}(I-zA_{22}^{\times})^{-1}B_{2}D_{2}^{-1}

(3.11)

which extends
V_{+}(z)^{-1}
analytically to the open neighborhood
{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\sigma(A_{22}^{\times})^{-1}
of
\overline{{\mathbb{D}}}
. Similarly, it follows that
V_{-}(z)
and
V_{-}(z)^{-1}
can be extended analytically to open neighborhoods of
\overline{{\mathbb{E}}}
via the formulas

\displaystyle V_{-}(z)

\displaystyle=D_{1}+zC_{1}(I-zA_{11})^{-1}B_{1}D_{2}^{-1},\quad z\in{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\sigma(A_{11})^{-1},

(3.12)

\displaystyle V_{-}(z)^{-1}

\displaystyle=D_{1}^{-1}-zD_{1}^{-1}C_{1}(I-zA_{11}^{\times})^{-1}B_{1}(I+D)^{-1},\quad z\in{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\sigma(A_{11}^{\times})^{-1}.

The analytic extensions of
W_{+}(z)
,
W_{+}(z)^{-1}
,
W_{-}(z)
and
W_{-}(z)^{-1}
follow in a similar way. For completeness, we add the formulas of the analytic extensions. For this, note that with respect to the decomposition
{\mathcal{X}}={\mathcal{X}}_{-}^{\times}\dot{+}{\mathcal{X}}_{+}
the operators
A
,
A^{\times}
,
B
and
C
take the form

A=\begin{bmatrix}A_{11}^{\prime}&0\\
A_{21}^{\prime}&A_{22}^{\prime}\end{bmatrix},\ \ A^{\times}=\begin{bmatrix}A_{11}^{\prime\times}&A_{12}^{\prime\times}\\
0&A_{22}^{\prime\times}\end{bmatrix},\ \ C=\begin{bmatrix}C_{1}^{\prime}&C_{2}^{\prime}\end{bmatrix},\ \ B=\begin{bmatrix}B_{1}^{\prime}\\
B_{2}^{\prime}\end{bmatrix}

(3.13)

with, using similar arguments as above,

\sigma(A_{11}^{\prime})\subset{\mathbb{E}},\quad\sigma(A_{22}^{\prime})\subset{\mathbb{D}},\quad\sigma(A_{11}^{\prime\times})\subset{\mathbb{E}},\quad\sigma(A_{22}^{\prime\times})\subset{\mathbb{D}}.

(3.14)

The formulas for
W_{+}(z)
,
W_{+}(z)^{-1}
,
W_{-}(z)
and
W_{-}(z)^{-1}
then turn out to be

\displaystyle W_{+}(z)

\displaystyle=D_{1}+zC_{2}^{\prime}(I-zA_{22}^{\prime})^{-1}B_{2}^{\prime}D_{2}^{-1},\quad z\in{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\sigma(A_{22}^{\prime})^{-1},

(3.15)

\displaystyle W_{+}(z)^{-1}

\displaystyle=D_{1}^{-1}-zD_{1}^{-1}C_{2}^{\prime}(I-zA_{22}^{\prime\times})^{-1}B_{2}^{\prime}(I+D)^{-1},\quad z\in{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\sigma(A_{22}^{\prime\times})^{-1},

\displaystyle W_{-}(z)

\displaystyle=D_{2}+zD_{1}^{-1}C_{1}^{\prime}(I-zA_{11}^{\prime})^{-1}B_{1}^{\prime},\quad z\in{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\sigma(A_{11}^{\prime})^{-1},

\displaystyle W_{-}(z)^{-1}

\displaystyle=D_{2}^{-1}-z(I+D)^{-1}C_{1}^{\prime}(I-zA_{11}^{\prime\times})^{-1}B_{1}^{\prime}D_{2}^{-1},\quad z\in{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\sigma(A_{11}^{\prime\times})^{-1}.
