Flexibility of German gas-fired generation: evidence from clustering empirical operation
Chiara Fusar Bassini, Alice Lixuan Xu, Jorge Sánchez Canales, Lion Hirth, Lynn H. Kaack

Abstract
A key input to energy models are assumptions about the flexibility of power generation units, i.e., how quickly and often they can start up. These assumptions are usually calibrated on the technical characteristics of the units, such as installed capacity or technology type. However, even if power generation units technically can dispatch flexibly, service obligations and market incentives may constrain their operation. Here, we cluster over 60% of German national gas generation (generation units of 100 MWp or above) based on their empirical flexibility. We process the hourly dispatch of sample units between 2019 and 2023 using a novel deep learning approach, that transforms time series into easy-to-cluster representations. We identify two clusters of peaker units and two clusters of non-peaker units, whose different empirical flexibility is quantified by cluster-level ramp rates. Non-peaker units, around half of the sample, are empirically less flexible than peakers, and make up for more than 83% of sample must-run generation. Regulatory changes addressing the low market responsiveness of non-peakers are needed to unlock their flexibility.



1
Introduction

Ensuring adequate flexibility of conventional power generation units is critical in electricity systems with a high penetration of renewable energy sources (RES). Flexible units that can operate in high cycling mode – meaning that they can adapt to market conditions by rapidly switching on and off – can better complement the daily fluctuations of RES generation. In energy models, assumptions about the flexibility of a generation unit, such as its maximum ramp rate, are often based on technical attributes (age, installed capacity, etc.). For instance, an old combined heat and power (CHP) unit will be assumed to be less flexible than a modern unit. However, in practice, the empirical flexibility of a power generation unit can differ significantly from its technical capability.

A broader range of economic factors, such as contract obligations and market conditions, can have an impact on the empirical flexibility of power generation units. For example, Hermans and Delarue
[
1
]
show that maintenance contracts between plant operators and turbine manufacturers may include penalties for frequent or fast cycling, discouraging flexible generation. Cross-model comparisons
[
2
,
3
]
highlight the sensitivity of energy models to CHP utilization rates, i.e., the extent to which a unit is assumed to cogenerate heat and electricity. Finally, Beykirch et al.
[
4
,
5
]
demonstrate that adjusting the cost parameters of gas-fired generation units based on their dispatch can explain differences in the empirical flexibility of two gas-fired units with similar technical characteristics.

To estimate the actual flexibility of a power generation unit, we need to go beyond its technical characteristics and consider its empirical dispatch, which more closely reflects existing constraints. We aim to find groups of units with different empirical flexibility by observing their dispatch over a prolonged time period. Here, we analyze over 60% of German yearly gas-fired generation, provided by 49 units of at least 100 MWp whose hourly dispatch is reported on ENTSO-E Transparency
[
6
]
.
We consider a five-year period characterized by several market changes (2019 - 2023): a rapid transition to renewables, the shutdown of nuclear and coal plants, and the 2021-2022 gas crisis, which led to exceptionally high gas and electricity prices in many European markets.

To process this high-dimensional time series data, we adapt a neural network to create compressed representations of time series which can be clustered more easily. We obtain four groups based on the empirical operation of sample units, and analyze the most relevant techno-economic characteristics of each cluster. Our unsupervised method reveals two types of clusters: fast-ramping, market-oriented units (peakers) and non-peakers units, with limited empirical flexibility. We find that the generation of non-peakers, around half of the units, is not significantly correlated with national load, and constitutes over 83% of sample generation during negative price hours. Our findings underscore that empirical flexibility is not solely determined by technical characteristics and is shaped by market preferences and economic incentives.

The paper is structured as follows. In Section
2
, we provide a brief overview of the German market. In Section
3
, we describe the deep clustering approach used to obtain the clusters of gas-fired generation units. In Section
4
, we examine the resulting clusters and highlight the differences between peaker and non-peaker units. We discuss the policy implications and limitations of our results in Section
5
and conclude in Section
6
.

2
The role of natural gas in the German energy market

To meet its 2050 carbon neutrality goals, the European Union has embraced an extensive energy transition strategy
[
7
]
. Natural gas is considered a ”bridge fuel” that can facilitate the phase-out of coal-fired units and help balance RES variability. Therefore, many EU countries have invested significantly in gas generation and distribution networks; the German government, in particular, has strongly advocated for expanding its fleet of gas-fired generation units
[
8
]
. Less attention was paid to the empirical flexibility of existing units, whose total capacity in 2024 amounted to 36.7 MWp, or 48.6% of the capacity of all conventional assets
[
9
]
.

Between 2015 and 2021, the share of natural gas in the German electricity generation mix increased from 2.9% to 10.1%, and gas-fired units went from being rarely price-setting (less than 3%) to setting prices nearly 24% of the time
[
10
]
. In particular, during 2019 and 2020, higher coal costs accelerated the switch to gas-fired generation, which became a cheaper alternative to coal-based generation in many west European countries
[
11
]
. However, between 2021 and 2022, Russia significantly reduced its natural gas exports to Europe, triggering a severe energy crisis. Geopolitical uncertainty and 10-fold price increases (compared to pre Covid-19 prices) severely affected the German power generation sector and gas units in particular
[
12
]
.

Beyond market conditions, existing market incentives may also discourage some market participants from dispatching their gas-fired units flexibly. Using new data from EEX, the largest energy trading platform in Europe, we identify the companies operating German gas generation units and their market orientation
[
13
]
. According to EEX, units are considered market-oriented if their output is primarily traded on the EEX spot market; otherwise, they are classified as not market-oriented. As shown in Figure
1
, most of the sample units are located in South-West Germany. Almost all industrial units are in western Germany, the main industrial pole of the nation, while units in the north are owned by major utilities. Although a unit can have multiple owners, we assumed that the company reporting on EEX is its primary stakeholder. More details on unit ownership can be found in
A
.

Companies are divided into four categories – industry, major utilities, municipal utilities, and other utilities – which are likely to behave differently on electricity markets
[
14
]
. Germany’s major utilities – RWE, EnBW, Uniper, Vattenfall and LEAG – play a major role in setting national electricity prices. In 2021, they accounted for 66.9% of the national unsubsidized electricity generation
[
15
]
. Municipal utilities (German:
Stadtwerke
) are local entities responsible for municipal energy production, district heating, water supply, urban transport, and waste management. In Germany, there exist over 1,000 of them
[
16
]
. Finally, some companies in the chemical, steel, iron, and refinery industries own power generation units, co-located within their production sites.

3
Methodology

We want to compare the hourly operation of multiple power generation units over several years, resulting in time series with almost 44,000 data points per unit. Traditional clustering methods face significant challenges when applied to high-dimensional time series data. For instance, Euclidean distance metrics are sensitive to temporal shifts, while dynamic time warping suffers from poor scalability
[
17
,
18
]
. To address these limitations, we introduce a novel deep clustering approach, which we briefly outline here and describe in depth in
B
.

Deep clustering methods are a class of methods that cluster high-dimensional data with deep neural networks
[
19
]
. They entail a representation learning module, which leverages a deep neural network to compress raw data to low-dimensional representations, and a clustering module, which clusters these representations. Previous energy-related research has mainly deployed deep clustering for residential load profiling and RES forecasting
[
20
,
21
,
22
]
, while other applications remain largely unexplored. Here, we design a deep clustering procedure which is fully unsupervised and does not make use of the techno-economic characteristics of the units.

Our methodology is based on an adaptation of the encoder-decoder neural network from Mirzavand et al.
[
23
]
. Our main model contribution is the addition of unit vectors (embeddings) to the model, which capture unit-specific patterns in a compressed form. We proceed as follows: first, we train the neural network to predict the next day operation of the gas-fired units (Figure
2
a), obtaining an accuracy comparable to that of the original model from Mirzavand et al. (84.6%). Then, we construct a matrix of distances between embeddings, which we use to perform hierarchical clustering (Figure
2
b). We find that cohesion (similarity between cluster units) and separation (difference between clusters) are optimized when using four clusters (Figure
2
c and d).

. Our primary data source is the disaggregated hourly generation of 49 gas-fired units with an installed capacity of 100 MWp or more, published on ENTSO-E Transparency
[

, gas prices
[

4
Results

Our unsupervised method reveals four clusters within the sample. Clusters 1 and 2, comprising 49.0% of the units, include peaker units with high empirical flexibility. The remaining units in Clusters 3 and 4 are non-peakers, and maintain relatively stable dispatch levels over time. In
4.1
, we analyze the distinguishing characteristics of each cluster. In
4.2
, we underpin the limited empirical flexibility of non-peaker units through a regression. Finally, in
4.3
, we evaluate the sensitivity of our results to the time horizon and the clustering method.

4.1
Cluster characteristics

Units in different clusters show dispatch patterns that demonstrate different levels of empirical flexibility (Figure
3
). Here, we compare the differences in the dispatch of the units in the four clusters. Using a hand-curated dataset from three public sources
[
27
,
13
,
28
]
, we also identify the most representative characteristics for each cluster. Cluster-level statistics are reported in detail in
D
.

Cluster 1 includes 15 units, which are mainly owned by major utilities or others (80.0%); none of the units is owned by an industrial company. They are all market-oriented (their output is traded on the EEX platform) and relatively modern: 73.3% of them are 20 years or less. This cluster contains the least dispatched peakers, with an average share of full-load hours of only 13.3%. Cluster 1 has the highest average market values, which exceed yearly sample averages by 10–12%. Between 2019 and 2023, half of the top 10 sample units by market value belonged to this cluster. This confirms their peaker role, i.e., they mostly generate when residual load is high, and therefore obtain higher revenues.

Cluster 2 displays similar characteristics to Cluster 1. Its 9 units are mostly owned by major utilities or others: while there is one industrial unit in the cluster, it is managed by a major utility
[
29
]
. Similarly to Cluster 1, units in Cluster 2 are relatively younger and larger, with a mean installed capacity of 396.1 MWp (+33.7% compared to the sample mean). However, units in Cluster 2 are dispatched more frequently, with an average share of full-load hours of 34.4% (Cluster 1: 13.3%). Cluster-level yearly market values are consistently above sample-level averages, yet slightly below market values for Cluster 1 (8%-11% lower for all years except 2020).

Cluster 3 contains 17 non-peaker units and is the largest by number of units. 52.9% of the units in Cluster 3 are owned by municipal utilities: here, we include units that between 2019 and 2023 were owned by Vattenfall, a big utility with municipal branches in the cities of Berlin and Hamburg
[
30
]
. Units in this cluster are likely to be smaller and the majority of them have a capacity of below 250 MWp. Most of the units in Cluster 3 are also equipped with a CHP component (88.2%). As we can see from Figure
4
, their correlation with heat demand is higher than sample average (in 2019-2020, +59.9% or more), which is consistent with district heating obligations. The synthetic heat demand for Germany is taken from Ruhnau et al.
[
31
]
.

Finally, Cluster 4 contains predominantly industrial units. As appears from Figure
3
h), these 8 units are mostly off (56.1% of the time) or dispatched at min-load (28.2%). Cluster 4 also shows the lowest average correlation with market prices. Interestingly, our method captures a distinction between industrial facilities of the chemical and refinery industry (mainly in Cluster 3) and those of the steel industry (mainly in Cluster 4). Anecdotal evidence from power plant owners reveals that these units mostly utilize on-site produced coal-based gas, a by-product of iron-related industrial processes, to supply electricity to the co-located facilities.

4.2
Non-peakers display limited flexibility

Given their limited empirical flexibility, non-peaker units are unlikely to respond strongly to market dynamics. In particular, their dispatch behavior and ownership structure, described in
D
, suggest that their dispatch may be constrained by local demand and district heating. Their low responsiveness to market conditions can be concealed when considering the total national gas-fired generation, and become visible when analyzing specific subsets – a phenomenon named Simpson’s effect
[
32
]
.

To assess the market responsiveness of different clusters, we construct a fixed-effects linear regression for each cluster, which we fit to the average weekly generation in the pre-crisis period (January 2019 - June 2021) and evaluate on the crisis period, where gas prices were significantly higher (July 2021 - December 2023). The target variable is the scaled generation (%/MWp) of each unit in the respective cluster. The model uses the weekly aggregated input introduced in Section
3
– gas prices, total load, and RES generation – along with a one-week lag and quarterly time dummies.

We obtain
R^{2}
values of 0.897 and 0.865 for Clusters 3 and 4, and lower values for Clusters 1 and 2 (0.717 and 0.805 respectively). The regression table is reported in
E
. While a sample-level regression shows a statistically significant sensitivity to national load (+0.006 per GWh increase) and gas prices (-0.003 per €/MWh increase), the generation of units in Clusters 3 and 4 before the crisis shows no statistically significant response to either variable. If non-peaker units have limited responsiveness to market changes, the regression should generalize well for the crisis period; this should not be the case for peaker units.

In fact, units in non-peaker clusters exhibit higher autocorrelation in their weekly generation values (around 0.8), suggesting that they may operate under slower dynamics than those of the day-ahead market. They generate significantly more during the cold season (October to March), displaying prominent seasonality patterns that are consistent with the provision of heat services. The validation mean squared error (MSE) for these clusters (crisis period) increased by around a fourth compared to the training MSE (pre-crisis period) of 0.03 - 0.04. In other words, the results of our regression can be used to approximate the behavior of non-peaker units during the energy crisis (out of sample), because their behavior did not change significantly.

In contrast, peaker clusters show statistically significant coefficients for gas prices (negative) and total national load (positive). All else kept equal, they generate around 0.006%/MWp less per 1 €/MWh increase in retail gas prices and 0.011%/MWp or more per 1 GWh increase in load. Units in these clusters also lack strong seasonal patterns and are less influenced by the average generation of the previous week. As expected, the validation MSE for these clusters (crisis period) is about 4-fold that of the training MSE (pre-crisis): in this case the regression fails to capture market dynamics that emerged during the energy crisis.

4.3
Clusters remain stable during gas crisis

To validate our results, we explore the effect of different algorithms and time lengths on the clusters obtained. We measure the consistency of our results by the level of cluster agreement, as described in more detail in
C
). To assess the impact of varying the clustering technique, we compare results obtained with K-Means, single and average linkage hierarchical clustering. Here, we find that different techniques obtain very similar clusters (agreement level above 85.9%), indicating that the deep neural network successfully encodes the variations between generation units in a lower dimension, and that the results are not strongly affected by the choice of clustering algorithm.

Second, we want to assess how clusters change over time. In particular, we first consider only the pre-crisis period (January 2019 - June 2021), then only the crisis period (July 2021 - December 2023). As shown in Figure
5
, most units maintain their (non-)peaker status over time. During the gas crisis, only 12.2% of the units modified their dispatch enough to switch between peaker and non-peaker clusters. One such example is Knapsack 1, a peaker unit that transitioned from Cluster 1 to Cluster 4 during the crisis, following visible changes in its production patterns (Figure
5
b). However, 22.4% of the units moved between Cluster 1 and 2, maintaining their status as peakers, but generating less during one of the periods. For example, München Süd 1 (turbine no. 2), which was not active in 2022 due to modernization work
[
33
]
, moved from Cluster 2 to Cluster 1.

5
Discussion

To guide different audiences, the discussion is divided into three sections: insights for energy modelers, insights for policymakers, and study limitations.

5.1
Insights for energy modelers

In Table
2
, we report the average maximum ramp rates observed empirically for each cluster, comparing them with model assumptions for coal and gas generation from Krishnamurthy et al.
[
34
]
. While the empirical ramp rates for Clusters 1 and 2 (5.7 and 6.0 MW/min on average) are in line with the assumed rates for gas units, the empirical ramp rates for Clusters 3 and 4 are closer to the flexibility of coal units (3.2 and 2.6 MW/min on average, respectively). This suggests that the empirical flexibility of some of the units may be overestimated. Here, modelers can use our results to attribute different ramp rates to gas units depending on their clusters.

In Table
3
, we evaluate which unit-level attributes are particularly distinct in different clusters. We use Anova and Chi-square tests to identify attributes that are not statistically independent from the obtained clusters. We find that technical characteristics, such as the type of gas turbine technology, are less distinct in clusters than more economic factors, such as their market orientation or owner. These factors, which affect the economic dispatch of conventional units, are often neglected in energy models. Introducing ”flexibility discounts” or owner-specific optimization strategies (e.g., Barazza and Strachan
[
14
]
) could help to better capture the flexibility constraints of certain units.

5.2
Insights for policymakers

Our results suggest that the empirical flexibility of part of the German gas fleet may be significantly limited, as 51.0% of the sample we analyzed operate as non-peakers and are price-insensitive. Using new data from EEX, we find that over half of the non-peaker units are mostly not selling their output on the spot market (56.0%), hence are likely not to dispatch in a flexible and price-driven manner. Moreover, despite major price increases during the gas crisis, most units (87.8%) did not change between non-peaker and peaker, suggesting that major regulatory or technical adjustments are necessary for non-peaker units to increase their responsiveness to market signals.

As RES penetration grows, the flexible operation of conventional units becomes increasingly critical. When demand is low and RES generation is high, their must-run generation should be reduced. Between 2019 and 2023, most of the must-run generation in the sample (83.0%) was due to non-peaker units, which generated on average 1.3 GWh during low load and negative price hours (Figure
6
). This could be in part due to CHP must-run constraints, estimated by previous research to lie between a total of 2 and 8 GWh
[
35
]
. Here, techno-economic strategies to reduce the must-run output should be explored (e.g., retrofitting).

Finally, we show that the ownership of a gas unit can significantly affect its empirical flexibility. Most non-peaker units are owned by municipal utilities or by industries. However, in areas like Hamburg and Berlin, where big utilities act as providers of municipal services, their units also operate as non-peakers. Given their obligation to provide electricity and heating to local customers, municipal utilities may be less willing to generate flexibly. Industries benefit from consumption-specific grid fee benefits, which were found to limit their overall flexibility
[
36
]
. Regulatory adjustments, particularly with respect to grid fees and CHP regulations
[
37
,
38
]
, may be needed to unlock this flexibility potential.

5.3
Limitations

Given that our study empirically assesses the flexibility of generation units, most of its limitations are related to the quality and scope of our data. We refer to Hirth et al.
[
39
]
for a complete review of ENTSO-E Transparency data platform. ENTSO-E only reports generation units with a minimum installed capacity of 100 MWp, making our results not representative for smaller gas units, which could not be included in the sample. Additionally, data on unit-level outages is not available in a compatible format and thus omitted. Finally, we find some discrepancies in the reporting of unit attributes. For example, the reported owner can differ between sources (our definition is provided in
A
).

Our method uses historical dispatch data, providing a retrospective view of the empirical flexibility of German gas-fired units between 2019 and 2023. Although the methodology presented in this study may be broadly applicable, the specific findings cannot be directly generalized to other regions or historical contexts.
The method does not allow to differentiate between end uses: for instance, it is not possible to determine how much electricity an industrial unit generates for self-consumption versus how much it sells on the electricity market. Nonetheless, the cluster analysis indicates that empirical flexibility remains relatively consistent over time, suggesting the existence of long-lasting dispatch constraints.

6
Conclusion

We cluster a sample of 49 German gas-fired units, responsible for more than 60% of national gas generation, using their empirical dispatch. To this end, we design an unsupervised deep clustering approach that clusters time series of unit dispatch without requiring unit-level technical information. Units are clustered in two groups of peakers (Clusters 1 and 2) and two of non-peakers (Clusters 3 and 4). Peaker units are flexible and price-sensitive, whereas the empirical flexibility of non-peakers appears to be constrained by service obligations, e.g. district heating (Cluster 3) or industrial cogeneration (Cluster 4).

Despite exceptionally high gas prices in 2021-2022, non-peaker units are relatively insensitive to price changes, while peakers are more flexible and price-sensitive. Using new data from EEX, we show that around half of the non-peaker units are not market-oriented, i.e. limitedly or not participating to the spot market. These units, many of which are owned by industry and municipal utilities, likely lack strong incentives to operate flexibly under existing German rules. Without clustering, this behavior remains hidden and can lead to overconfident conclusions about the market responsiveness of German gas generation.

Our study demonstrates that the empirical flexibility of gas generation units can substantially differ from their technical capabilities. In fact, our method offers a way to track changes in the role of dispatchable generation over time and can uncover trends and deviations in the empirical flexibility of multiple generation units. Future research may explore this approach in other contexts: markets where units have to meet several energy obligations, such as countries with a high share of district heating (e.g., Denmark
[
40
]
), could deliver further insights, particularly with their increasing RES integration and electrification.

Appendix A
Note on unit-level characteristics

We manually compile a dataset of unit-level characteristics, such as age, fuel type, and power production facility, which we use to evaluate the characteristics of the different clusters. We derive technical attributes such as primary fuel, technology type (combined cycle, open cycle), commissioning year and capacity from Marktstammdatenregister
[
28
]
and Open Power System Data
[
27
]
. In addition, the reporting company and the market orientation of the unit are extracted from EEX
[
13
]
, whereby a unit is considered market oriented if it is trading on the EEX spot market. Differently from other sources, EEX tracks ownership changes of generation units over time; given that some major acquisitions and ownership changes occurred during the period under consideration (2019-2023), we select 2021 as the year of reference for the attribution of ownership.

Appendix B
Deep clustering

We design an encoder-decoder model that forecasts the 24 day-ahead operation of a generation unit, receiving as input its historical operation, aggregated market variables and learnable embedding weights. We build upon the model architecture from Mirzavand et al.
[
23
]
, which uses an encoder-decoder with Gated Recurrent Units (GRU) to model temporal dependencies. We equip the model with an additional embedding layer, which learns unit-level representations. At each iteration step, the model predicts the state of one generation unit (0: off, 1: min-load, 2: full-load) for the next 24 hours, based on past states and temporal variables for the previous 24 hours. Following Beykirch et al.
[
4
,
5
]
, states are defined as:

y_{it}=\begin{cases}0&g_{it}\leq 1/3\;G^{\max}_{i}\\
1&1/3\;G^{\max}_{i}<g_{it}\leq 2/3\;G^{\max}_{i}\\
2&g_{it}>2/3\;G^{\max}_{i}.\\
\end{cases}

where
g_{it}
is the generation of unit
i
at time
t
and
y_{it}
the discretized target.

We want to exclude units that we were almost never in operation, as they are likely to be used only as capacity reserves. To identify them, we evaluate the unit-level entropy of the distribution of the three possible states:

H_{i}=-\sum_{k=1}^{3}p(y_{it}=k)\log_{2}p(y_{it}=k),

where
p(y_{it}=k)
is the relative frequency of state
k
(0: off, 1: min-load, 2: full-load) for unit
i
. We select a sample of 49 units with a minimum entropy of 0.3: units with lower entropy are off for more than 95% of the five-year period.

Then, we process the time dataset into samples, where each sample contains a unit identifier and a temporal subsequence of 48 hours. The final dataset contains 42,913 point observations, which are randomly split into training, validation, and testing datasets with a ratio of 70%, 20%, and 10%, respectively. The first 24 hours include both the previous states for the unit and the min-max scaled temporal variables (encoder input), while the subsequent 24 hours serve as the forecasting window and contain temporal variables only (decoder input). The history length of 24 hours was determined by hyperparameter tuning, while the forecasting window of 24 hours was chosen in alignment with European regulation, which requires power plant operators to submit their day-ahead generation forecasts to the Transmission System Operator
[
41
]
. Finally, we use a step size of 48 hours between samples to ensure that subsequences do not overlap.

The encoder block processes the temporal input into a fixed-dimensional hidden state that is fed to the decoder. The decoder block also receives a static variable, which serves as the unit identifier, so that it can distinguish which unit the input time series belongs to. Instead of one-hot encoded unit identifiers, which are exogenous, we use learnable embeddings. Embeddings are initialized as vectors of zeros, learned by the model during training, and then used as input for deep clustering. The embedding matrix has dimension
\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
, where
n
is the number of units and
m
the length of the embedding vectors, which is set to 8 after hyperparameter tuning. Ultimately, the decoder block generates its predictions step by step, and these are passed through an output layer to predict the output classes.

The model is trained by minimizing the cross-entropy loss using the Adam optimizer
[
42
]
; the training parameters are reported in Table
4
. Given the skewness of the class distributions, we monitor both regular and balanced accuracy, i.e., the average recall over all classes. Our implementation with 8-dimensional embeddings achieves a similar performance to the original model with one-hot encodings, with a simple accuracy of 84.6% (original model: 84.4%) and a balanced accuracy of 78.0% (original model: 78.4%).

We use the unit embeddings obtained from the model training to derive clusters. We apply hierarchical clustering with an agglomerative strategy, a fully deterministic algorithm whose decision steps can be easily traced by means of a dendogram. At initialization, each cluster contains a single unit; the algorithm iteratively merges the clusters that minimize the farthest distance between any pair of points (complete linkage). Complete linkage mitigates the chaining phenomenon and obtains more even-sized clusters
[
43
]
.

One of the main advantages of hierarchical clustering is that it allows distance metrics that are not Minkowski distances. Moreover, hierarchical clustering has already been successfully deployed for deep clustering, e.g. in Yang et al.
[
44
]
. Following previous literature on similarity between embeddings
[
45
,
46
]
, we use cosine similarity as a measure of agreement between vectors and its complement, the cosine distance, as a distance measure. We define the distance matrix
D\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
as:

d_{ij}=1-\frac{e_{i}e_{j}}{\|e_{i}\|\|e_{j}\|},

where
e_{i}
is the embedding vector of unit
i
, and
n
the number of units in the sample. The cosine distance takes on values between 0 and 2 (included), where a cosine distance of 1 corresponds to a pair of orthogonal vectors, while values of 0 and 2 respectively correspond to exactly identical and opposite vectors (Figure
2
, b). To determine the optimal number of clusters, we deploy the Silhouette Score
[
47
]
, an internal clustering evaluation method that evaluates cluster cohesion and separation without requiring ground truth labels. The score is computed using the previously defined cosine-based distance matrix and ranges between -1 and 1, whereby a score of 1 corresponds to well-defined clusters, 0 to ambiguous or random clusters, and -1 to poorly defined clusters. The best Silhouette Score is achieved with a small number of clusters: we therefore select the optimal number of clusters to be 4 (Figure
2
, c).

Once the clusters have been obtained, we use dimensionality reduction to visualize and inspect them. In particular, we compare results from t-distributed stochastic neighbor (t-SNE) and principal component analysis (PCA). Both the t-SNE and the PCA projection reveal a circular arrangement of the units in two dimensions, whereby the four clusters are located approximately at the four corners of the figure (Figure
2
, d). The first 2 dimensions of the PCA reduction make up respectively for 72.5% and 27.4% of its explained variance ratio. There is a positive relationship between the first dimension and the share of hours at full-load (increasing from left to right), and a positive relationship between the second dimension and the share of hours at min-load (increasing from bottom to top).

Appendix C
Sensitivity analysis

To evaluate cluster robustness with respect to clustering algorithms and time periods, we apply the previously described deep clustering procedure with varying configurations, keeping the number of clusters fixed to 4, and changing either the clustering algorithm or the observation period. We then evaluate cluster agreement (i.e., the share of units that are in the same cluster in both configurations) between the obtained clusters and the original ones.

First, we analyze the impact of different linkage rules on hierarchical clustering: average linkage – based on the mean distance between clusters – achieves an agreement score of 95.9% with respect to clusters obtained by complete linkage, while single linkage – based on the shortest distance between clusters – obtains a lower score (85.9%). Single linkage, which is known to create imbalanced clusters
[
48
]
, also reduces the size of Cluster 2 to less than half its original size (8 units), aggregating these units into Cluster 1.

Second, we test another parametric clustering method, K-means, a widely adopted algorithm with previous applications for deep clustering
[
49
,
22
]
. K-means clusters achieve a strong agreement score with the original hierarchical cluster set (93.9%), which further improves when using a variation of K-means with cosine distance (95.9%). Overall, the different techniques obtain almost the same clusters, indicating that the deep neural network successfully encodes the variations between generation units in a lower dimension, and the results are not strongly affected by the choice of clustering algorithm.

Finally, we divide the dataset into two time periods – pre-crisis (January 2019 - June 2021) and crisis (July 2021 - December 2023) – and derive new clusters using these time subsets. 57.1% of the units do not change cluster between pre-crisis and crisis period. Furthermore, most of the units that change cluster do not alter their operating modality, i.e., peakers transition between Clusters 1 and 2 (22.4%), and non-peakers between Clusters 3 and 4 (8.2%). We find that, while peakers transition between Cluster 1 and 2 if they generated less during one of the two periods, major changes in dispatch are needed for a unit to transition between a non-peaker and a peaker cluster.
