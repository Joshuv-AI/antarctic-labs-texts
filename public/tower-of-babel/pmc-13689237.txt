Psychological factors associated with medication non-adherence after kidney transplant: a systematic review
Authors: Hui Ling Michelle Neo, Kuhanesan N C Naidu, Isabella Jia Hui Tang, Ho Yee Tiong, Shawn Yong Shian Goh, Cyrus Su Hui Ho
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMedication non-adherence remains a persistent and critical challenge among kidney transplant recipients, who demonstrate higher rates of non-adherence than other solid organ transplant populations. Given the associated risks of graft rejection, graft loss, and mortality, a clearer synthesis of underlying psychological factors is needed. This systematic review synthesized evidence on psychological factors associated with self-reported medication non-adherence among adult kidney transplant recipients, integrating quantitative and qualitative research to capture patients’ perspectives. Following Joanna Briggs Institute guidelines, five electronic databases (Embase, PubMed, PsycINFO, Scopus, and Web of Science) were searched from inception to 5 May 2026. Eligible studies included quantitative and qualitative research involving adult recipients. Methodological quality was appraised using the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool, and findings were integrated using a convergent integrated approach. Forty-four studies comprising 11,180 participants were included. Non-adherence prevalence ranged from 12.0% to 89.2%. Four overarching themes were identified: psychological distress, perceptions and beliefs, coping strategies, and social-contextual factors. Depressive symptoms and low social support were the most consistent correlates. Qualitative findings highlighted emotional burden, stigma, and regimen complexity as additional barriers. Medication non-adherence is multifactorial and context dependent. Introduction

Chronic Kidney Disease (CKD) is a life-threatening condition marked by irreversible loss of kidney function, leading to the accumulation of toxic metabolic waste and excess fluids in the bloodstream [1]. This progressive process results in widespread cellular, tissue, and organ damage, with common symptoms including fatigue, oedema, breathlessness, nausea, and poor appetite [1]. Kidney replacement therapies (KRT), namely dialysis and kidney transplantation, are required to maintain physiological stability in advanced disease [1, 2]. Most patients initiate dialysis while awaiting transplantation, although some undergo pre-emptive transplantation following close monitoring [2]. Kidney transplantation is widely recognised as the preferred treatment for CKD Stage 5, offering superior survival and quality-of-life outcomes compared with long-term dialysis [1, 2].

Following transplantation, all solid organ transplant recipients require lifelong immunosuppressive therapy to prevent acute and chronic rejection and prolong graft survival [2]. However, medication non-adherence, defined as any deviation in the initiation, implementation, or discontinuation of prescribed regimens [3], remains a significant challenge, particularly among kidney transplant recipients [4]. Evidence indicates that non-adherence is more prevalent in kidney transplantation than in other solid organ transplants [4]. A large meta-analysis of 147 studies involving over 29,000 recipients reported non-adherence rates of 35.6% in kidney recipients, compared with 14.5% in heart and 6.7% in liver transplant recipients [4]. Importantly, non-adherent kidney transplant recipients face substantially poorer outcomes, including a 2.66-fold higher risk of mortality, a 6.44-fold increased risk of graft loss, and a 2.28-fold higher risk of graft rejection [5]. These findings underscore the need to address medication adherence promptly globally.

Early kidney graft dysfunction is often asymptomatic, unlike liver or heart graft dysfunction, which may reduce kidney transplant recipients’ perceived need for strict medication adherence [6]. Additionally, graft loss in kidney transplantation is viewed as less immediately life-threatening due to the availability of dialysis as an alternative therapy [4]. In contrast, liver and heart transplant recipients have far fewer life-sustaining options [4]. Together, these factors highlight the organ-specific nature of medication adherence challenges in transplant care.

Research has extensively examined factors influencing medication non-adherence among adult kidney transplant recipients [7, 8]. A recent systematic review identified higher non-adherence risks among younger, male, unemployed patients, those with lower educational levels, living-donor transplant recipients, and individuals further from transplantation [9]. Psychosocial factors, including low social support, negative beliefs and behaviours, depression, and anxiety, were also strongly associated with non-adherence. Importantly, the authors recommended greater use of patient self-reports in future adherence research, emphasising the value of engaging patients as experts in understanding their own medication-taking behaviours.

Emerging research on heart [10, 11] and liver [12, 13] transplantation similarly highlights the growing importance of patient-reported outcomes (PROs). PROs capture patients’ own accounts of how they feel and function in relation to their condition and treatment, offering clinicians direct insight into patient perspectives [14]. Incorporating PROs can enhance patient-centred care and support more personalised treatment planning [14]. This trend supports a shift away from traditional adherence measures, such as serum drug levels, toward approaches that engage patients as authoritative sources of information about their medication adherence behaviours, including self-report tools and qualitative interviews.

Although the latest scoping review on medication non-adherence in kidney transplant recipients was conducted by Corr and colleagues in 2025 [15], its broad scope and the lack of critical appraisal of study quality limited the ability to draw definitive conclusions and to achieve a deeper understanding of patients’ perspectives in this field.

Aim

Hence, this systematic review endeavours to address the identified gap in the literature by offering a timely and comprehensive overview of the psychological factors associated with self-reported medication nonadherence among adult kidney transplant recipients, providing updated insights into patients’ perspectives and experiences.

Method

Review design

This review followed the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) methodology for mixed methods systematic reviews, using a convergent integrated approach to synthesise qualitative and quantitative evidence within a single framework [16]. This approach was chosen because the review focused on psychological factors underlying self-reported medication non-adherence, which are experiential constructs measured quantitatively yet grounded in subjective experiences. Although most included studies were quantitative, they relied primarily on self-report psychometric instruments to assess psychological states rather than on objective clinical measures. These findings were therefore suitable for transformation into conceptual meaning units aligned with qualitative constructs. A convergent, integrated approach enabled the synthesis of statistical associations and experiential insights into a coherent whole, offering greater interpretive depth than parallel analyses. The review protocol was registered with PROSPERO (CRD42024575353).

Search protocol, information sources, search strategy

Relevant studies were identified through a systematic search across five electronic databases: Embase (Elsevier), PubMed (NLM), PsycINFO (OvidSP), Scopus (Elsevier), and Web of Science (Thomson Reuters). The search covered publications from each database’s inception to 31 July 2025. To ensure the review remained current before publication, the search was updated to include studies published up to 5 May 2026. The search strategy was developed based on previous systematic reviews, input from the expert panel, and consultation with a research librarian. A broad keyword search was used to maximize sensitivity and maintain consistency across databases with different indexing systems (e.g., MeSH and Emtree). The final search combined terms related to kidney transplantation, psychological factors, and medication adherence, using the following structure: (renal transplant OR kidney transplant*) AND (depression OR anxiety OR transplant effects OR regret OR personality OR cognitive deficits OR cognitive impairment OR coping OR social support) AND (adherence OR compliance)*. Complete, database-specific search strategies are available in Supplementary File 1 (Table S1: Keyword Combination and Search Hits).

Selection criteria

Eligible studies included quantitative research using standardised self-report questionnaires and qualitative interview-based studies involving adult kidney transplant recipients (≥ 18 years) who had undergone living- or deceased-donor transplantation. Excluded studies were reviews, meta-analyses, case studies, editorials, interventions, non-English publications, duplicate datasets, unavailable full texts, and studies with incomplete or non-combinable data. Independent reviewers conducted study selection according to predefined eligibility criteria, and the PRISMA 2020 guidelines (Supplementary File 2) were used to guide transparent reporting of the review process [17]. Screening was conducted independently by two reviewers (KN and IT), with disagreements resolved by a third reviewer (MN).

Quality assessment of included studies

All included studies were appraised using the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool (MMAT) [18, 19], which evaluates qualitative, quantitative, and mixed methods studies within a single framework. Consistent with MMAT guidance, no overall quality scores were assigned. Methodological quality was assessed across relevant domains, with independent appraisals by KN and IT, discrepancies resolved with MN, and results reported in Table 1.

Data extraction and synthesis

Data extraction was conducted independently by two reviewers (KN and MN) using a JBI-standardised form adapted for mixed methods reviews. Extracted data included publication details, study characteristics, sample demographics, measurement instruments, and key findings related to medication non-adherence (Table 2). Owing to substantial heterogeneity in psychological measures and outcome definitions, a meta-analysis was not undertaken [17–19]. Qualitative data were extracted as thematic statements or participant quotations, accompanied by interpretations. Quantitative findings that were both significant and non-significant were extracted, considered, and qualitised by transforming into thematic meaning units reflecting underlying psychological constructs (e.g., emotional distress undermining treatment engagement). Integration prioritised effect direction, consistency, and conceptual relevance over statistical magnitude, with quantitative findings coded inductively alongside qualitative data using a shared analytic framework.

Methodological quality informed the integration by prioritising themes supported by higher-quality and consistent studies. Results

Search outcomes

The database search across five electronic databases yielded 649 records. After removing 280 duplicates, 369 records remained for screening. Of these, 270 were excluded based on titles and abstracts. A full-text review of 99 articles led to the exclusion of 55, leaving 44 studies included. The study selection process is detailed in the PRISMA diagram (Fig. 1).

PRISMA diagram

Quality appraisal

Critical appraisal using the MMAT [64, 65] showed most studies met screening and design-specific criteria, indicating a generally robust evidence base. Common limitations included inadequate control of confounders and limited representativeness, resulting in a moderate risk of bias. While findings are credible for synthesis, cautious interpretation is warranted, especially for studies examining anxiety and cognitive functioning.

Study characteristics

In total, 44 articles [20–63] encompassed 11,180 participants, with individual sample sizes ranging from N = 25 to 1357, and mean participant ages ranging from 25.6 to 61.7 years. The studies were conducted across 21 countries and can be grouped by region as follows. Europe contributed the largest number of studies (n = 21), followed by Asia (n = 12), North America (n = 8). Oceania was represented by Australia (n = 1), while South America and Africa were each represented by one study from Brazil (n = 1) and Ethiopia (n = 1), respectively. The most frequently investigated factor was depressive symptoms, followed by beliefs about medications, anxiety symptoms, and health literacy. Detailed characteristics of the included studies and the review findings are presented in Table 2.

Prevalence of medication-non-adherence

Medication non-adherence rates varied widely, ranging from 12.0% [39] to 89.2% [54]. Several studies reported non-adherence rates exceeding 50.0% [20, 24, 29, 42], underscoring the persistence of this issue.

Integration of findings

The integration of both quantitative and qualitative synthesis led to the identification of four main themes related to medication non-adherence in adult kidney transplant recipients: (1) psychological distress, (2) perceptions and beliefs, (3) coping strategies, and (4) social and contextual factors, as illustrated by Fig. 2.

Psychological distress

Depressive symptoms

Depressive symptoms emerged as a prominent psychological correlate of medication non-adherence across studies from multiple countries, including the United States, Europe, and Turkey [21, 23, 24, 26, 29, 55]. Higher levels of depression were consistently associated with greater non-adherence. Some evidence suggests that physiological features of depression, such as fatigue and sleep disturbances, may disrupt routine medication-taking behaviours [23]. Notably, depressive symptoms were linked more strongly to intentional non-adherence than unintentional non-adherence, indicating that mood-related cognitive and motivational factors may influence deliberate decisions to deviate from prescribed regimens [32].

Anxiety symptoms

Anxiety symptoms [24, 55] and higher worry about the transplanted kidney and health [53] were also positively correlated with higher medication non-adherence. In contrast, some studies [34, 52] did not find such associations with psychological distress, suggesting that contextual or methodological differences may be at play.

Cognitive functioning and mental quality of life

Several studies have identified cognitive functioning and mental health as key factors affecting non-adherence. Non-adherent patients showed poorer problem-solving ability [31, 47], greater depressive [31], anxiety symptoms and cognitive impairment [60], and lower mental quality of life, highlighting the influence of cognitive and psychological well-being on medication-taking behaviours. Meanwhile, studies using quality of life measures (e.g., SF-36, WHOQoL-BREF) frequently identified lower mental quality of life as a key barrier to adherence [36, 52, 57].

Perceptions and beliefs

Beliefs about medications

Beliefs about medications, including both necessity and concerns, were another recurring factor. Studies [22, 33, 38, 40, 56] using the Beliefs about Medicines Questionnaire (BMQ) have consistently shown that greater medication concerns and lower perceived necessity consistently affect non-adherence.

Perceived barriers

Perceived barriers to medication adherence also emerged as a decisive factor. Saravanakumar et al. [51], Weng et al. [58], and Zhang et al. [62] found that greater perceived barriers were significantly associated with non-adherence. Similarly, Yang et al. [59] highlighted the role of lower intentions to adhere, consistent with the Theory of Planned Behaviour models.

Health literacy

Health literacy, defined as the ability to understand and use health information [66], has been frequently linked to medication non-adherence. Several studies have identified lower health literacy as a significant factor that affects non-adherence [21, 29, 48, 63], whereas others have found no association [27]. These mixed findings likely reflect methodological differences, particularly the use of varied health literacy measurement tools across studies.

Acceptance and treatment satisfaction

Zachciał et al. [61] provided additional insight into patients’ beliefs and perspectives by noting the influence of illness acceptance in influencing medication adherence, while several studies [20, 37, 54] found that lower treatment satisfaction with convenience, side effects, and medication information is associated with poorer adherence.

Coping strategies

Maladaptive coping strategies, in particular avoidance-oriented coping [49] and palliative coping (religion/fantasy thoughts and emotion-focused coping) [28], were associated with poorer medication adherence. In addition, Liu et al. [40] found that having lower perceived self-efficacy in managing anger-irritation, despondency-distress, and positive affect was also associated with non-adherence.

Social and contextual factors

Multiple studies highlight social support as a key factor in promoting adherence to immunosuppressive therapy among kidney transplant recipients. Higher perceived social support has been consistently associated with better adherence, suggesting that emotional and practical assistance helps patients manage long-term medication demands [25]. Higher perceived social support has been consistently associated with better adherence [35], suggesting that emotional and practical assistance helps patients manage long-term medication demands. Supportive relationships can reinforce routine medication-taking behaviours and buffer psychosocial stressors. Evidence also indicates that relationship quality, particularly within couples, plays an important role, with stronger relationships linked to improved adherence [45]. Conversely, low social support has been identified as a significant risk factor for non-adherence [52]. Collectively, these findings emphasise the importance of incorporating psychosocial and relational assessments into post-transplant care to support sustained medication adherence.

Qualitative insights

Qualitative studies have provided important insights into the lived experiences of medication adherence among kidney transplant recipients. Low et al. [41] explored Australian recipients’ experiences and found that strict immunosuppressive regimens generated significant psychological stress, driven by fear of missed doses and potential graft failure. Social stigma, limited understanding from others, and disruptions to daily routines further increased the burden, while a strong sense of responsibility for transplant outcomes motivated adherence.

Memory et al. [43] examined post-transplant self-management and identified key facilitators, including internal motivation, strong patient–clinician relationships, and adequate medication knowledge. Barriers included limited health literacy, medication side effects, emotional distress, and difficulties integrating complex regimens into everyday life. Similarly, Muduma et al. [44] found that non-adherence was mainly unintentional, driven by forgetfulness, disruptions to routine, pill burden, and difficulties integrating medication schedules into daily life.

Collectively, these findings highlight how emotional, physical, and practical challenges interact to undermine adherence. They underscore the need for targeted psychoeducation, peer support, and practical tools, such as reminders, to help patients sustain long-term medication adherence.

Discussion

This systematic review represents the first comprehensive synthesis of psychological factors associated with self-reported medication non-adherence among adult kidney transplant recipients, foregrounding patients’ subjective experiences in line with contemporary patient-reported outcomes research. Across 44 studies, medication non-adherence emerged as a persistent challenge, with prevalence frequently exceeding 50%.

Repeated associations across multiple higher-quality studies were identified between higher psychological distress and poorer adherence, most robustly for depressive symptoms, corroborating earlier reviews [8, 9]. Notably, one study distinguishing intentional from unintentional non-adherence found depressive symptoms to be specifically associated with intentional non-adherence [39], highlighting motivational and cognitive–affective processes rather than forgetfulness alone and underscoring the importance of proactive screening for sub-clinical depression among transplant populations. By contrast, findings related to anxiety were less consistent, likely reflecting heterogeneity in measurement approaches across studies. Anxiety was assessed using instruments with differing conceptual emphases and psychometric properties, potentially obscuring true associations. Evidence from studies using transplant-specific tools, such as the Transplant Effects Questionnaire (TxEQ) [67], suggests that context-sensitive measures may better capture anxiety-related processes relevant to adherence.

Medication beliefs consistently emerged as a key factor affecting adherence, aligning with the Necessity–Concerns Framework (NCF), which posits that adherence is shaped by patients’ perceived medication necessity balanced against concerns about adverse effects [68]. These findings highlight the potential role of motivational interviewing and cognitive behavioural interventions in addressing maladaptive medication beliefs.

Health literacy was also identified as a relevant factor, although interpretation is constrained by measurement variability. Beyond measurement issues, limited health literacy may impair patients’ readiness to engage in complex treatment decisions. Kalantar-Zadeh and Zisman-Ilani [69] argue that shared decision-making frameworks often assume baseline literacy levels that may not exist, particularly among underserved populations, contributing to decision unreadiness and compromised self-management. These findings reinforce the importance of literacy-sensitive, culturally tailored tools that support proactive engagement in self-management and adherence behaviours.

Social support was consistently associated with better adherence, reinforcing findings from prior reviews [8, 9], emphasising its importance as a protective factor. However, most evidence derives from Western contexts, limiting its applicability to non-Western or collectivist settings where sociocultural factors such as filial piety, multigenerational living, family obligations, and beliefs about traditional or complementary medicine may shape adherence differently [70]. These considerations warrant further investigation.

Overall, this review highlights the multifactorial and context-dependent nature of medication non-adherence following kidney transplantation. Psychological distress, medication beliefs, literacy-related factors, and social contexts interact to shape adherence behaviours. Clinically, the findings support routine psychosocial screening using structured tools such as the Stanford Integrated Psychosocial Assessment for Transplant (SIPAT) [71], which systematically evaluates the psychosocial readiness and risk of transplant candidates, may assist clinicians in identifying patients at elevated risk of non-adherence and in tailoring interventions accordingly.

However, effective implementation requires multidisciplinary coordination and sensitivity to resource constraints. Screening should occur at multiple points (pre- and post-transplant) to address evolving psychosocial stressors. Barriers such as limited mental health access and clinic time pressures are especially pronounced in low-resource settings. Applying implementation science frameworks may help adapt interventions to local contexts and support sustainable improvements in post-transplant outcomes. Future studies should integrate such frameworks to enhance real-world impact.

Limitations

Several limitations warrant consideration. Medication non-adherence was operationalised heterogeneously across studies, with multiple measures differing in conceptual focus, recall periods, and psychometric quality, potentially contributing to inconsistent findings. Although a “less than perfect” adherence definition was applied [3], construct differences remained. Prevalence variability may reflect both true behavioural differences and methodological heterogeneity, and should be interpreted with caution, taking instrument characteristics into account. Reliance on self-reported adherence introduces recall and social desirability biases. Limiting inclusion to English-language and predominantly Western studies may restrict generalisability. While methodological quality was generally acceptable, variability in design and measurement approaches necessitates cautious interpretation of the integrated findings. Furthermore, the predominance of cross-sectional designs precludes determining temporal ordering, and the relationships between adherence and psychological variables may be reciprocal and dynamic rather than unidirectional.

Future directions

Future research should standardise adherence measures, with clear justification and reporting of the dimensions assessed. Sensitivity or subgroup analyses may address unavoidable heterogeneity. There is a need for future longitudinal and mixed-methods research to better clarify temporal and causal pathways. Beyond identifying correlates of non-adherence, future work should deepen understanding of the reasons underlying non-adherence from patients’ perspectives, including the emotional, cognitive, relational, and contextual processes that shape medication-taking behaviour over time. Such work is essential for informing interventions that not only reduce barriers to adherence but also empower kidney transplant recipients to take an active and confident role in managing their long-term health. Expanding studies to non-Western and underrepresented regions, including Southeast Asia, Africa, and Latin America, is essential to enhance cultural validity, equity, and the relevance of interventions.

Conclusion

This systematic review synthesises psychological factors linked to self-reported medication non-adherence in adult kidney transplant recipients. Strong evidence supports associations with depressive symptoms and social support, while emerging evidence highlights medication beliefs, health literacy, cognition, and coping. Clinically, the findings underscore the need for integrated psychosocial assessment and targeted, psychologically informed interventions within post-transplant care pathways.
