Correlation networks of blood proteins in the neuroimmunology of schizophrenia—replication and extension
Authors: Clark D Jeffries, Chris A Bizon, John R Ford, Jean Addington, Carrie E Bearden, Kristin Cadenhead, Tyrone D Cannon, Barbara Cornblatt, Matcheri Keshavan, Daniel Mathalon, Larry Seidman, William S Stone, Elaine F Walker, Scott W Woods, Diana O Perkins
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe Pearson sample correlation between two biomarkers across a group of individuals can sometimes be much stronger than expected by chance. In the context of psychosis risk, we previously analyzed blood plasma protein data from initial presentations as collected in the North American Prodrome Longitudinal Study 2 (NAPLS2). We found enhanced correlation between proteins SERPINE1 and TIMP1, both promoters of coagulation and inhibitors of remodeling of extracellular matrix (ECM). Participants were unaffected community controls vs. others of clinical high risk. The SERPINE1-TIMP1 correlation was consistently higher in individuals at clinical high risk for psychosis who later converted to a psychotic disorder vs. participants who were nonconverters or unaffected community controls. Here, we extend those findings using data from a larger cohort, the North American Prodrome Longitudinal Study 3 (NAPLS3). Again, the correlation between SERPINE1 and TIMP1 remained higher in psychosis high-risk converters vs. the other groups. In NAPLS3 we added an assay for PLAT (anti-coagulation plasminogen activator strongly inhibited by SERPINE1). Comparing the three NAPLS3 groups we found a decreased correlation between SERPINE1 and PLAT in converters. In summary, the increased correlation of SERPINE1 and TIMP1 in converters is consistent with restricted brain circuit remodeling and increased tendency to coagulation. Rigorous application of permutation testing yielded NAPLS2 vs. NAPLS3 consistency of SERPINE1-TIMP1 correlation patterns with empirical p-value 0.03.Subject terms: Molecular neuroscience, Predictive markers, Schizophrenia, Learning and memory

Introduction

Schizophrenia-spectrum psychotic disorders are typically chronic and disabling illnesses that emerge in adolescence and early adulthood. Clinical research criteria, mainly involving attenuated, psychotic-like symptoms, identify persons at clinical high risk (CHR) with an estimated 20% risk of conversion to a frank psychotic disorder within two years of initial presentation [1]. Longitudinal studies of CHR cohorts might someday identify biochemical mechanisms driving the emergence of psychosis, enabling targeted interventions.

The present work (using data from NAPLS3) was undertaken to replicate a previous report (from NAPLS2) regarding proteins SERPINE1 and TIMP1 [2]. We find in both cohorts that SERPINE1-TIMP1 correlations were consistently higher in CHR psychosis converters (CHR-C) vs. CHR nonconverters (CHR-NC). The protein pair drew our attention because they are both powerful agents that impair fibrinolysis (thus connecting with increased risk of cardiovascular diseases (CVD) in schizophrenia [3]) and ECM remodeling (consistent with accelerated loss of gray matter predicting psychosis in CHR cohorts [4, 5]). That is, SERPINE1 inhibits PLAT (plasminogen activator used, e.g., in treatment of thrombotic stroke) while TIMP1 inhibits certain matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs). Thus, elevated correlation of SERPINE1 and TIMP1 may result from shared mechanisms controlling fibrinolysis or ECM remodeling. SERPINE1 also inhibits PLAU (another plasminogen inhibitor). We added PLAT and PLAU assays in NAPLS3 because SERPINE1 is a known inhibitor of both PLAT and PLAU [6] with a mature molecular-level literature (e.g., Rahman [7]). In fact, SERPINE1 is known as a master regulator of PLAT; drugs based upon PLAT itself may dissolve clots and are credited with marked reduction of mortality risk due to ischemic stroke [8]. The roles of PLAT and PLAU in homeostasis of coagulation and also ECM remodeling suggest that SERPINE1 should be in physiologic conditions at least somewhat correlated with PLAT and PLAU.

Other researchers have studied levels of SERPINE1, TIMP1, and MMPs in schizophrenia. For example, altered levels of SERPINE1 in postmortem brain have been reported [9]. However, we failed to find consistently and strongly altered peripheral blood plasma levels of these proteins and instead sought informative changes in their correlations.

Materials and methods

Study participants

The aims of the NAPLS studies included investigation of predictors and mechanisms of conversion to psychosis in persons at CHR based on the Criteria of Psychosis-Risk States [10]. We employed similar methods in NAPLS2 and NAPLS3 related to ascertainment, syndrome determination, inclusion and exclusion criteria, demographics, cross-site reliability, and clinical and biological measures [10, 11]. CHR status was determined by the Structured Interview for Prodromal Syndromes [12]. CHR participants were followed until psychosis conversion, loss to follow-up, or end of study at two years. The recorded sex, race, and parental educational level (used as an indicator of socioeconomic status) of each participant was self-declared.

The full NAPLS2 cohort included 764 CHR participants and 279 UC participants, and the full NAPLS3 cohort included 710 CHR participants and 96 UC participants. Psychiatric diagnoses (estimation of risk of development of schizophrenia or a related psychotic disorder) were determined by the Structured Clinical Interview for DSM IV [13].

In the present report we analyzed NAPLS3 baseline blood samples from 58 CHR-C participants who converted during follow-up and 58 demographically similar CHR-NC participants. In NAPLS3 (only) BMI was determined at the time of blood draw. Since obesity is associated with dysregulation of immune system related pathways overlapping with those hypothesized to be relevant to psychosis risk [14], in NAPLS3 we excluded participants with BMI > 35, that is, one UC, one CHR-NC, and three CHR-C, leaving 32 UC, 57 CHR-NC, and 55 CHR-C. With NAPLS2 we analyzed baseline blood samples from 35 CHR-C and 40 demographically similar CHR-NC followed at least two years. The demographics of included participants in both studies were like those of excluded participants (Table 1).

The studies were approved by the Institutional Review Board at each site, and each participant provided written informed consent or assent, with a parent or guardian also consenting for minor participants. Available assays and financial limits determined sample sizes.

Plasma analytes

For both studies, blood was collected in EDTA tubes that were processed immediately after the blood draw and stored in −80C freezers. Plasma samples were sent on dry ice to Myriad RBM (Austin, Texas). NAPLS2 samples were analyzed on the Human Discovery MAP®1.0 assays that included 185 analytes associated with immune system function, hormonal responses, oxidative stress, and metabolism. Due to changes in Myriad RBM panels and budgetary limitations we were not able to include all analytes examined in NAPLS2 in our NAPLS3 analyses. NAPLS3 samples were analyzed using a selection of 70 analytes that: (1) overlapped or extended our previous findings (especially PLAT and PLAU); (2) tested correlation relationships suggested by NAPLS2; or (3) appeared otherwise in standard panels.

We excluded analytes with levels below the lowest limit of detection in >10% of samples, leaving 157 analytes for NAPLS2 and 62 analytes for NAPLS3 (see Supplement for detection limit levels). For the remaining analytes, values below the limits of detection were replaced with their respective minima of detection. We then standardized within each cohort to z-scores, meaning, the z-normalization for each analyte over all participants, using the average and SD values of the UC. (Thus, for UC groups and each analyte, average = 0 and SD = 1.) We performed correlation analyses of the 38 remaining proteins in the intersection of the two studies, thus providing 703 protein pairs. The clearest and most consistent signal we found involved the protein pair SERPINE1-TIMP1.

Basis of data analyses

Pearson correlation [15] of two n-dimensional (n ≥ 3) real vectors X and Y (a statistical sample) requires that neither vector is proportional to 1 = (1,1,…,1). Linearly dependent vectors X and Y with Y = m∗X + b∗1, where m and b are scalars and m is positive, have correlation 1. In particular, the correlation of a vector with itself is 1. Conversely, if X and Y have correlation = 1, then they are linearly dependent with positive m (Supplement). However, high correlation does not imply “closeness.” For example, the two vectors X = (0,1,2) and Y = (−5,−2,1) have correlation = 1 (note Y = 3∗X-5∗1), but dot product X⋅Y = 0). Such examples of pairs with correlation = 1 and dot product = 0 exist in all higher dimensions (Supplement). In short, a correlation near 1 means approximately linear dependency with positive slope, not entrywise approximation.

As stated above, from NAPLS3 data we discarded five participants with BMI > 35. Also, we converted the value of each biomarker to its z-score using the average and SD of the UC. We then discarded biomarkers in NAPLS2 data with more than one z-score above 6 in any of the three groups, leaving 139 biomarkers; the same exclusion in NAPLS3 left 54 biomarkers. The intersection of the biomarkers in both studies was 38 proteins, hence 703 pairs including SERPINE1-TIMP1. Pearson correlations over random subsets with replacement of each group were calculated with built-in functions in Excel and macros (available from corresponding author). All this yielded our focus on SERPINE1-TIMP1.

Direct intergroup comparison of vector correlations requires a common dimension. Using NAPLS2 data we drew 1000 random subsets (with replacements) of 26 participants each from the 35 community controls, 40 CHR nonconverters, and 32 CHR converters participants. Thus, the fraction of selected participants in each draw varied from 65 to 81%.

Similarly, from NAPLS3 data we drew 1000 random subsets of 26 of 32 community controls (81%), 44 of 57 CHR nonconverters (77%), and 44 of 55 CHR converters (80%), enabling direct comparison of CHR-NC and CHR-C correlations.

Results

Highly correlated protein pairs

We proposed examining the correlations of pairs of plasma proteins between groups. In both the current and previous study, the overall pattern of protein-protein correlations across all participants cannot be reasonably attributed to chance (Fig. 1). Specifically, a small subset of all protein pairs shows strong positive correlations that significantly exceed the magnitudes of all negative correlations, highlighting certain robustly correlated pairs as candidates for further investigation. In Fig. 1 such high correlations are in the blue boxes.

Shown are probability density functions of all Pearson correlations of all 9870 pairs of 141 biomarkers over all 107 participants in NAPLS2 and all 1891 correlations of 62 biomarkers of 144 participants in NAPLS3.

Both distributions have some positive correlations (in blue boxes) greater than the magnitudes of all negative correlations (122 pairs in the NAPLS2 box, 76 in the NAPLS3 box). (We used EasyFit (MathWave, Dnepropetrovsk, Ukraine) to generate all distribution fits in this paper.) Shown fits are both Johnson SU distributions suggesting consistent data generation processes underlying both cohorts.

Bonferroni analysis of these correlation values (Supplement) supports the following conclusion: in the NAPLS2 data, the expected number of such highly correlated (blue box) protein pairs under the null hypothesis (normally distributed data) is 0.0011 (observed: 122); and in NAPLS3 data the expected number is 0.0030 (observed: 90). These findings suggest that while most correlations likely have little or no predictive power, a subset of protein pairs must be strongly coregulated as a basic feature of physiological functions. Among them, pairs differentiated by correlations over CHR-C vs. either CHR-NC or UC may provide biomarkers indicative of elevated psychosis risk.

A relevant example in NAPLS3 (only) involves PLAT-SERPINE1, which would be expected to show a strong correlation across all participants because SERPINE1 is the master inhibitor of PLAT [7]. The correlation of PLAT and SEPINE1 over all 144 participants was 0.54, ranking 19th among the 1891 possible protein pairs. For independent, normally distributed 144-dimensional vectors, the probability of a correlation ≥ 0.54 is 1.43E-12, hence well below the Bonferroni value 0.05/1891 = 2.64E-5.

While numerically nonidentical, results using our algorithm with random selections exhibit good visual reproducibility and indeed are not easily distinguished by casual inspection.

Reproducibility of algorithmic results.

The same procedure was applied three times to NAPLS3 data, resulting in three histograms for each of the three groups of participants. The underlying numbers (not shown) represented in the histograms are indeed different, but for each of the three groups patterns of the graphs are similar. This suggests reproducibility.

Permutation tests

It is important to estimate the chance likelihood of results like those suggested in Fig. 2, that is, the histogram patterns in which constitute the heart of this paper. Accordingly, we experimented with a simple metric for SERPINE1-TIMP1 correlations over the three groups and two cohorts. That is, three times (three versions labeled 0.1, 0.2, 0.3) we ran in both NAPLS2 and NAPLS3 we calculate correlations over the choices of 1000 random subsets. For each of the six histograms we defined a six-dimensional summary vector consisting of the three averages (ave) of correlations and the three standard deviations (SD) of the correlations. Table 2 shows the metric vectors for the same three versions of random subset calculations in Fig. 3. We note the similarities among values within each cohort.

SERPINE1 Correlations with PLAT and PLAU

SERPINE1 regulates PLAT as well as a second plasminogen activator, PLAU. Thus, we expected in CHR converters reduced correlation (suggesting breakdown of coagulation homeostasis) for the pairs PLAT-SERPINE1 and PLAU-SERPINE1. Figure 4 shows the results.

Compared to the other groups, positive but lower correlations for CHR-C are evident. For PLAU-SERPINE1 horizontal axis values were partitioned into 30 bins covering the interval [−1, 1]. In more detail, taking 1000 random 44-participant subsets of nonconverters and converters yielded average PLAT-SERPINE1 correlations of 0.60 among nonconverters and 0.49 among converters. However, the shown negative correlation values of PLAU-SERPINE1 in CHR converters suggest the underlying control mechanisms for PLAU-SERPINE1 are more complex than those of PLAT-SERPINE1.

Discussion

We previously reported that SERPINE1 and TIMP1 were more strongly correlated in CHR converters than CHR nonconverters or unaffected community controls [2]. Earlier, regarding blood from patients with frank schizophrenia vs. controls, Domenici et al. [17] already amassed a large data set showing much higher SERPINE1-TIMP1 correlation in schizophrenia patients vs. controls (also schizophrenia patients vs. patients with major depressive disorder). The present study tested data from a new NAPLS3 cohort to compare with our SERPINE1-TIMP1 finding for CHR converters vs. CHR nonconverters (also converters vs. unaffected community controls). We sought to extend our analysis to include additional protein pairs important in coagulation homeostasis.

NAPLS3 results reconfirmed increased SERPINE1-TIMP1 correlation for CHR converters compared to CHR nonconverters or unaffected community controls. We also hypothesized and then found reduced correlation of SERPINE1 with PLAT and PLAU, consistent with degradation of coagulation homeostasis. PLAT and PLAU are among agents of fibrinolysis (hence connecting CVD comorbidity observed in schizophrenia [8]) and agents of remodeling of extracellular matrix, which may cloak glia or (as PNN) neurons.

TIMP1 inhibits selected matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) [18] which, like plasmin, are proteolytic enzymes that play crucial roles in extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling [19]. Moreover, some MMPs impact blood clotting by degrading key components of the clotting cascade [20]. And regarding neuroinflammation, the mechanisms of plasminogen activation generally impact modification of blood–brain barrier integrity, leukocyte diapedesis, removal of fibrin deposits in nervous tissues, microglial activation, and neutrophil functions [21]. In summary, we expect these impacts as well as direct dysregulation of ECM remodeling and coagulation homeostasis may be reflected in altered correlations of protein pairs combining SERPINE1, TIMP1, PLAT or PLAU.

Regarding a mechanism, we hypothesize that proteins become more correlated in plasma when common factors trigger linear dependency of their co-secretion. For example, TGFB1 triggers secretion of TIMP1 in fibroblasts [22], and fibroblasts are present in meninges, perivascular spaces, and choroid plexus of human brain [23]. In a study relevant to intracerebral hemorrhage using murine hippocampal neuronal cell line HT-22, Tgfb1 induced activation of SerpinE1 [24]. Thus, TGFB1 might be a common agent of correlation (near linear dependency) of SERPINE1-TIMP1 in brain.

Other agencies of SERPINE1 have been reported. For example, Zhu et al. reported alteration of blood-brain barrier associated with inflammation in the postmortem midbrain from schizophrenia patients with increased levels of SERPINE1 in a high inflammatory subgroup [9].

The additional proteins we selected in NAPLS3 were plasminogen activator tissue type (PLAT) and plasminogen activator urokinase (PLAU) because SERPINE1 is a known inhibitor of both PLAT and PLAU [6]. In fact, SERPINE1 is known as a master regulator of PLAT. Drugs based upon PLAT may dissolve clots and are credited with marked reduction of mortality risk in treatment of ischemic stroke [8]. The roles of PLAT and PLAU in homeostasis of coagulation and also ECM remodeling would seem to suggest that SERPINE1 should be in physiologic conditions at least somewhat correlated with PLAT and PLAU. Figure 3 seems to support this hypothesis with PLAT but not PLAU.

A further connection of schizophrenia with PLAT, SERPINE1, TIMP1, and other proteins discussed above is proposal of common effects of very distinct psychedelic agents by Dölen and her team [25]. They propose a mechanism of reopening of social learning windows common to ketamine, psilocybin, LSD, ibogaine, and MDMA (MDMA considered as a mimic of LSD and psilocybin). Thus, the altered consciousness induced by psychedelics may reflect the participative experience of reopening critical learning periods (brain circuit remodeling), possibly by targeting ECM generally or PNN specifically.

Application of psychedelics in treatment of neuropsychiatric disorders is an active research arena. For example, Tuck et al. [26] recently reported a structural analogue of LSD that promotes neuroplasticity but has reduced hallucinogenicity. Specifically, it promotes spinogenesis in the cortex, leading to therapeutic effects in behavioral assays possibly relevant to depression and cognition. Progress is being made in explaining psychedelic mechanisms at the molecular level despite chemical diversity of drugs [27].

Interestingly, Jankowska et al. [28] have analyzed the proteome of PC12 cell cultures (a commercially available, neuron-like cell line discovered in 1976 by Greene and Tischler [29] and since used extensively in neuroscience and toxicology). They treated PC12 cells with antipsychotics clozapine, risperidone, and haloperidol. At times 12 and 24 h, the cells were assayed, yielding significant alterations of levels of 510 proteins. However, only 23 proteins had log2 fold change magnitude ≥ 1.17 in at least one of the six experiments, and exactly one protein—SERPINE1—did so in all six experiments. In fact, SERPINE1 fold change was ≤ −1.24 in all six groups.

Moreover, PNN are known to be altered in some brain regions in certain neurological and psychiatric disorders, including schizophrenia [30–35]. In some of the foundational work on learning windows, Hensch et al. reported that in (only) young mice, PLAT increased with monocular deprivation [36]. Thus, PLAT may be part of scheduling stabilization of PNN cloaking of neurons to alter learning windows [37]. TIMP1 inhibits certain matrix metalloproteinases which in turn are broad agents of ECM digestion and tissue remodeling [19]. Increased correlation of SERPINE1 and TIMP1 may therefore be associated with activation of a common control mechanism leading to accumulation and persistence of some forms of ECM and inhibition of brain circuit remodeling.

Berretta and colleagues have explored the potential role of ECM abnormalities in schizophrenia [30]. Micrographs and other evidence of ECM accumulation associate schizophrenia with a massive increase in CSPG-positive glial cells in deep amygdala nuclei and entorhinal cortex (layer II). However, regarding PNN cloaking of neurons, densities were greatly decreased in layers 3 and 5 of the prefrontal cortex in schizophrenia (but not in bipolar disorder), Different CSPG abnormalities may affect distinct glial populations [34]; more recent work pertains to thalamic nuclei [32].

PNN are features of the specifically-built basement membrane of the central nervous system [38]. PNN cloaks partly cover the neuron cell body and proximal neurite, axons, and perisynaptic structures. As mesh-like structures within ECM, components of the PNN include semaphorins [39] as well as CSPGs, hyaluronic acid, and tenascins. Cortical PNN are mainly associated with inhibitory interneurons [36] and are modified in development to enable critical periods, that is, learning intervals in which learning is programmed by first establishing new circuits and then appropriately cloaking them. Pioneering work by Pizzorusso et al. [40] on this dynamic aspect of PNN inspired many other investigators. For example, proteolytic mechanisms enabling critical periods were described by Hensch et al. in the primary visual cortex [41, 42]; Ye and Miao also reported the timing of PNN construction vs. visual learning [43]. Lipachev et al. [44] observed microstructure of the complex of GABAergic synapses with PNN in the mouse somatosensory cortex, noting that sensory deprivation causes flattening of the 3D PNN mesh geometry. This possible loss of PNN volume invites investigation within general neurodegeneration or mental disorders and specific cortical thinning or volume loss in schizophrenia [4, 45, 46].

In particular, Fawcett et al. [47] reported that PNN provide a cartilage-like ECM cloak of inhibitory GABAergic parvalbumin-positive (PV) interneurons. Thus, regarding memory dysfunctions, there may be potential treatment targets in PNN. Moreover, Nahar et al. asserted PV neurons pervasively influence downstream brain functions [48], linked by Roach and Mathalon in electroencephalogram studies directly to schizophrenia [49]. PV interneurons function as fast-spiking regulators of pyramidal neurons and thereby control excitatory signals projecting from the cerebral cortex, as described in experiments of Lensjo et al. [50]. Evidence points to oxidative stress impairing PV interneurons in some models of schizophrenia [51].

Because PNN critically ensheathe portions of neurons in particular brain structures, van ‘t Spijker and Kwok [52] proposed that PNN might be regarded as parts of those neurons. Their scheduled actions occur at different ages, and their hierarchical structures and assembly programs are similar but not identical across brain regions. While learning windows generally are programmed to end, plasticity can be renewed with enzymes that digest the hyaluronan backbone of PNN. The van ‘t Spijker and Kwok review goes on to tabulate and explain PNN plasticity findings in cortex, amygdala, hippocampus, cerebellum, and spinal cord.

Especially relevant to the present work, Berretta et al. reasoned that ECM and neuronal and glial populations might be dysregulated in schizophrenia [31]. Astrocytes play a key role in regulating ECM properties by synthesizing CSPG and building PNN. As reviewed in Pantazopoulos et al. [30], CSPG-positive glial cells and PNN, GFAP-positive astrocytes, and total numbers of PV neurons were altered in the deep amygdala nuclei and entorhinal cortex. Results from schizophrenia patients vs. unaffected community controls included large increases in CSPG-positive glial cells in the deep amygdala nuclei and additional increases in entorhinal cortex layer II. However, PNN were reduced in the amygdala lateral nucleus and lateral entorhinal cortex layer II [53, 54].

A direct connection with neurologic and psychiatric illness and impaired prefrontal gamma oscillation power required for high levels of cognitive control was suggested by Lewis et al. [55] and elaborated by many subsequent researchers. PV GABAergic interneurons, mostly fast spiking, are excited by glutamate signals from cortical neurons. Schizophrenia is associated with decreased expression of proteins PVALB (parvalbumin) and GAD1 in PV GABAergic interneurons [56, 57]. Subtypes of PV cells then inhibit selected compartments of pyramidal neurons [58]. Some downstream cognitive effects are known from study of evoked-response potentials [59]. In fact, PVALB is a marker for certain cortical GABAergic interneurons, and event-related potentials, specifically gamma changes, constitute a signature of schizophrenia [49]. Bovine albumin (analogous to human serum albumin ALB) can induce epileptiform activity in rats by indirectly activating a TGFB1 pathway [60]. This may be important to schizophrenia researchers because the antihypertensive Losartan blocks a TGFB1 receptor in rat astrocytes, thereby blocking epileptiform activation in the brain [61].

Thus, a variety of ECM agencies may connect SERPINE1, TIMP1, and other exceptionally correlated proteins with the etiology of schizophrenia.

Limitations and pitfalls

Additional assays were constrained by finances and fixed panel inventories. There could be pairs of blood plasma analytes with correlations that are at least as consistent and informative as the ones described herein (Supplement). Also beyond our scope are protein pairs with high correlations in all three groups, that is, not distinguished by CHR status but evidently required to be correlated as a condition for life (Supplement).

As noted above, high correlation does not necessarily imply quantitative proportionality or closeness; vectors X and Y can be orthogonal and yet be perfectly correlated (Supplement). Moreover, a strong correlation between two vectors in, say, 50-dimensional space, can be dependent upon only a few of the 50 participants. For example, high correlation can occur if one participant has anomalously high values for X and Y while levels for the other participants are normally distributed random numbers (Supplement). Since schizophrenia is known to be a heterogeneous disorder [62], it could happen that a strong correlation of a specific pair of biomarkers arises from inclusion of a few participants in a subtype of the spectrum.

Furthermore, informative correlations might be found in subtypes of schizophrenia data. For example, Byrne et al. [63] studied variation of clinical evaluation (SOPS) scores and found certain complement and coagulation regulator levels to be prognostic of negative symptoms.

Regarding clinical application, high correlation of two proteins may suggest etiology. However, it is not in itself a signal that can be applied to diagnose new participants. A possible diagnosis method employs scatterplots of values in a group of analyte pairs and calculating distance from a new datum to the historical regression line of each group. If two analytes were perfectly correlated, then that distance would be consistently zero. Thus, perpendicular distance from a new datum to the historical regression line could reveal likelihood of membership in one group or another. Advances require more high-quality biomarker data with large sample sizes.

In the scatterplots (Supplement) we see many data that are simply clustered near the origin. Given the heterogeneity of schizophrenia and the possibly cyclic or transient episodes of inflammation, we expect that not all patients at all times will exhibit consistent diagnostic signals. In fact, the consistency of SEPINE1-TIMP1 correlation signals in Fig. 2 is remarkable. Patients with both high SERPINE1 and high TIMP1 z-scores relative to UC values contribute strongly to high correlations (Supplement). But they may arise from exceptional subsets.

Conclusion

Presently, the reliable prediction of risk of schizophrenia in first-episode psychosis by a simple biochemical assay is not considered possible. We have focused on blood plasma proteins with correlation changes that seem plausibly connected with mental illness.

The study of correlations in data from NAPLS2 and NAPLS3 assays has suggested SERPINE1 and TIMP1 levels are differentiated in converters vs. nonconverters. As always seems to be the case in schizophrenia research, additional data of high quality is required to further test or extend our preliminary findings. However, even more is needed: improved mathematical methods must be invented and applied to identify causality. There should be ways to reverse engineer biological control of sets of proteins and then to propose interventions. The controls are likely not apparent in simple, linear chains of causality but in feedback loops that presently elude explanation. Furthermore, suppression of a pathway (not enhancement) could accompany higher correlation—another topic beyond the scope of the present work.

Biological control circuits must first attend to existential requirements such as the balance of PLAT and SERPINE1 discussed above. Relatively nuanced responses—perhaps quite localized in the brain—may be thereafter explored, such as responses to immunological challenges or psychological stress.

Much expertise and effort have already been applied to convert primary data to networks, e.g., STRING, and to devise dynamical systems that advance “circuit-based” [64] or “wiring diagram” [65] approaches as models. However, as noted by Fond et al. [66], immunoinflammatory, oxidative, and hormonal biomarkers of first-episode psychoses have been widely suggested but not yet translated to clinical practice. Needed are more data and more analyses, including correlation studies, followed by development of new algorithms to reliably predict risk and clarify causality from data.

Lastly, the algorithmic methods developed for this study might lend themselves to application in various other medical and even nonmedical research endeavors.
