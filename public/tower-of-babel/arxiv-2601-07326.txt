Introduction

Adaptive gradient methods have become the predominant optimizer for training deep neural networks, especially in large language models. The development of adaptive gradient algorithms has followed two distinct lineages: diagonal preconditioning and non‑diagonal preconditioning. The former has developed through the progression of AdaGrad
(
9
;
22
)
, RMSProp
(
28
)
, Adam
(
16
)
, and finally AdamW
(
21
)
, which have served as the de facto optimizer for training deep networks over the past decade. The latter lineage, originating from full-matrix AdaGrad
(
9
)
and advancing to methods such as Shampoo
(
12
)
, SOAP
(
29
)
, and Muon
(
14
)
, now demonstrates the potential to outperform its diagonal counterparts.

Diagonally preconditioned methods typically employ coordinate-wise scaling to the gradient. For instance, AdaGrad treats the network’s parameters as a single high-dimensional vector and updates them according to the following procedure

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mathbf{x}_{k+1}=\mathbf{x}_{k}-\eta\Lambda_{k}^{-1/2}\mathbf{g}_{k},\quad\Lambda_{k}=\diag\hskip-1.9919pt\left(\hskip-0.56917pt\sum_{t=1}^{k}\diag(\mathbf{g}_{t}\mathbf{g}_{t}^{T})\hskip-2.56064pt\right)\hskip-1.13791pt,\end{aligned}

where
\Lambda_{k}
is a diagonal preconditioning matrix whose entries are the element-wise sum of squared historical gradients.

In contrast, non-diagonally preconditioned methods exploit the inherent matrix structure of parameters in deep networks. For example, the Shampoo optimizer operates according to the following formulation with two-sided preconditioning

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\hskip 8.5359pt\mathbf{X}_{k+1}=\mathbf{X}_{k}-\eta\mathbf{L}_{k}^{-1/4}\mathbf{G}_{k}\mathbf{R}_{k}^{-1/4},\\
&\mathbf{L}_{k}=\sum_{t=1}^{k}\mathbf{G}_{t}\mathbf{G}_{t}^{T},\quad\mathbf{R}_{k}=\sum_{t=1}^{k}\mathbf{G}_{t}^{T}\mathbf{G}_{t},\end{aligned}

where the gradient
\mathbf{G}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
is a matrix and
\mathbf{L}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
and
\mathbf{R}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
are non-diagonal preconditioners consisting of sum of historical gradient outer products, though in practice an exponential moving average is commonly used instead of a plain summation.

A key advantage of non-diagonally preconditioned methods is their ability to capture the cross-parameter correlations within the gradient, thereby yielding a more informed search direction and potentially superior convergence compared to diagonal approaches. Recently, an implementation based on the distributed Shampoo
(
2
;
26
)
won the external tuning track of the AlgoPerf neural network training algorithm competition
(
15
)
, demonstrating that non-diagonally preconditioned training algorithms can outperform currently popular diagonal conditioning methods, such as Adam. The winner implementation achieved significantly accelerated training, with an average speedup of
28\%
than the NAdamW
(
8
)
baseline across eight deep learning workloads. Algorithm
1
presents the core characteristics of the Shampoo implemented in
(
2
;
26
)
in a non-distributed manner, including the exponential moving average of the first and second moments, two-sided preconditioning with a tunable exponent, and decoupled weight decay.

1.2
Problem Settings, Notations, and Assumptions

We study the following non-convex problem with matrix parameter in this paper

\min_{\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}}f(\mathbf{X})

(2)

where
f(\mathbf{X})=\mathbb{E}_{\zeta\in\mathcal{P}}[f(\mathbf{X};\zeta)]
and
\zeta
is the sample drawn from the data distribution
\mathcal{P}
.

We denote vectors by lowercase bold letters and matrices by uppercase bold letters. We use
\mathbf{I}_{m}
for the identity matrix in
\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
. For vectors, denote
\|\cdot\|
as the
\ell_{2}
Euclidean norm. For matrices, denote
\|\cdot\|_{F}
,
\|\cdot\|_{op}
, and
\|\cdot\|_{*}
as the Frobenius norm, spectral norm (largest singular value), and nuclear norm (sum of singular values), respectively. The trace of a square matrix is written as
\tr(\cdot)
. Denote the singular values of
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
by
\sigma_{1}(\mathbf{A}),\cdots,\sigma_{r}(\mathbf{A})
with
r=\min\{m,n\}
. Denote
\mathcal{F}_{k}=\sigma(\zeta_{1},\zeta_{2},\cdots,\zeta_{k})
to be the sigma field of the stochastic samples up to
k
, denote
\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{F}_{k}}[\cdot]
as the expectation with respect to
\mathcal{F}_{k}
and
\mathbb{E}_{k}[\cdot|\mathcal{F}_{k-1}]
the conditional expectation with respect to
\zeta_{k}
given
\mathcal{F}_{k-1}
. Finally, let
f^{*}
denote the lower bound of
f(\mathbf{X})
.

We make the following assumptions throughout this paper:

1.

Smoothness:

\|\nabla f(\mathbf{Y})-\nabla f(\mathbf{X})\|_{F}\leq L\|\mathbf{Y}-\mathbf{X}\|_{F},\forall\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y}
,

2.

Unbiased estimator:
\mathbb{E}_{k}\left[\mathbf{G}_{k}\big|\mathcal{F}_{k-1}\right]=\nabla f(\mathbf{X}_{k})
,

3.

Bounded noise variance:

\mathbb{E}_{k}\hskip-0.85355pt\left[\hskip-0.85355pt\left(\hskip-0.85355pt\mathbf{G}_{k}\hskip-0.85355pt-\hskip-0.85355pt\nabla f(\mathbf{X}_{k})\hskip-0.85355pt\right)\hskip-0.85355pt\left(\hskip-0.85355pt\mathbf{G}_{k}\hskip-0.85355pt-\hskip-0.85355pt\nabla f(\mathbf{X}_{k})\hskip-0.85355pt\right)^{T}\big|\mathcal{F}_{k-1}\hskip-0.85355pt\right]\hskip-0.85355pt\preceq\hskip-0.85355pt\Sigma_{L}
,

\mathbb{E}_{k}\hskip-0.85355pt\left[\hskip-0.85355pt\left(\hskip-0.85355pt\mathbf{G}_{k}\hskip-0.85355pt-\hskip-0.85355pt\nabla f(\mathbf{X}_{k})\hskip-0.85355pt\right)^{T}\hskip-0.85355pt\left(\hskip-0.85355pt\mathbf{G}_{k}\hskip-0.85355pt-\hskip-0.85355pt\nabla f(\mathbf{X}_{k})\hskip-0.85355pt\right)\big|\mathcal{F}_{k-1}\hskip-0.85355pt\right]\hskip-0.85355pt\preceq\hskip-0.85355pt\Sigma_{R}

with symmetric positive semidefinitive matrices
\Sigma_{L}
and
\Sigma_{R}
.

The first two assumptions are identical to the standard assumptions used in the analysis of SGD, while the third assumption is more restrictive than that in SGD analysis. In fact, from the third assumption, it readily follows that

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}_{k}\hskip-0.85355pt\left[\left\|\mathbf{G}_{k}\hskip-0.85355pt-\hskip-0.85355pt\nabla f(\mathbf{X}_{k})\right\|_{F}^{2}\big|\mathcal{F}_{k-1}\right]\hskip-0.85355pt\leq\hskip-0.85355pt\frac{\tr\left(\Sigma_{L}\right)\hskip-0.85355pt+\hskip-0.85355pt\tr\left(\Sigma_{R}\right)}{2}\equiv\sigma^{2},\end{aligned}

which is the standard bounded variance assumption in SGD analysis.

Discussions on
\varepsilon
and

. In practice, the parameter

as used in

4
Conclusion

This paper studies the convergence properties of AdamW-style Shampoo with both one-sided and two-sided preconditioning. We establish the convergence rate
\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathbb{E}\left[\|\nabla f(\mathbf{X}_{k})\|_{*}\right]\leq{\cal O}(\frac{\sqrt{m+n}C}{K^{1/4}})
measured by nuclear norm. It can be considered to be analogous to the optimal
\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathbb{E}\left[\|\nabla f(\mathbf{X}_{k})\|_{F}\right]\leq{\cal O}(\frac{C}{K^{1/4}})
convergence rate of SGD in the ideal case of
\|\nabla f(\mathbf{X})\|_{*}=\Theta(\sqrt{m+n})\|\nabla f(\mathbf{X})\|_{F}
.

&18\nu C_{2}^{2}K^{3/2}\hskip-1.84949pt+\hskip-1.84949ptKC_{1}C_{2}\hskip-1.84949pt\leq\hskip-1.84949pt\left(\hskip-1.84949ptm\hskip-1.84949pt+\hskip-1.84949ptn\hskip-1.84949pt+\hskip-1.84949pt25\hat{\sigma}\hskip-1.84949pt\sqrt{\frac{m\hskip-1.84949pt+\hskip-1.84949ptn}{\hat{\varepsilon}}}\right)\hskip-1.84949pt\sqrt{K^{3}\hat{\sigma}^{2}L\hskip-1.84949pt\left(f(\mathbf{X}_{1})\hskip-1.84949pt-\hskip-1.84949ptf^{*}\right)}\hskip-1.84949pt\leq\hskip-1.84949pt\left(\frac{27}{2}(m\hskip-1.84949pt+\hskip-1.84949ptn)\hskip-1.84949pt+\hskip-1.84949pt\frac{25}{2}\frac{\hat{\sigma}^{2}}{\hat{\varepsilon}}\right)\hskip-1.84949pt\sqrt{K^{3}\hat{\sigma}^{2}L\hskip-1.84949pt\left(f(\mathbf{X}_{1})\hskip-1.84949pt-\hskip-1.84949ptf^{*}\right)}.\end{aligned}

So we have

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \frac{1}{2}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\nabla f(\mathbf{X}_{k})\right\|_{*}\right]\right)^{2}\leq&\left(\sqrt{m+n}+\frac{57\hat{\sigma}}{\sqrt{\hat{\varepsilon}}}\right)\sqrt[4]{K^{3}\hat{\sigma}^{2}L\left(f(\mathbf{X}_{1})-f^{*}\right)}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\nabla f(\mathbf{X}_{k})\right\|_{*}\right]\\
&+\left(\frac{27}{2}(m+n)+\frac{25}{2}\frac{\hat{\sigma}^{2}}{\hat{\varepsilon}}\right)\sqrt{K^{3}\hat{\sigma}^{2}L\left(f(\mathbf{X}_{1})-f^{*}\right)}.\end{aligned}

Solving inequality
x^{2}-ax-b\leq 0
, we have
x\leq\frac{a+\sqrt{a^{2}+4b}}{2}\leq a+\sqrt{b}
and

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\nabla f(\mathbf{X}_{k})\right\|_{*}\right]\leq&\left(2\sqrt{m+n}+\frac{114\hat{\sigma}}{\sqrt{\hat{\varepsilon}}}+\sqrt{27(m+n)+25\frac{\hat{\sigma}^{2}}{\hat{\varepsilon}}}\right)\sqrt[4]{K^{3}\hat{\sigma}^{2}L\left(f(\mathbf{X}_{1})-f^{*}\right)}\\
\leq&\left(8\sqrt{m+n}+\frac{119\hat{\sigma}}{\sqrt{\hat{\varepsilon}}}\right)\sqrt[4]{K^{3}\hat{\sigma}^{2}L\left(f(\mathbf{X}_{1})-f^{*}\right)}\\
=&\left(8\sqrt{m+n}+\frac{119\hat{\sigma}}{\sqrt{\hat{\varepsilon}}}\right)\max\left\{\sqrt[4]{K^{3}\sigma^{2}L\left(f(\mathbf{X}_{1})-f^{*}\right)},\sqrt{\frac{KL\left(f(\mathbf{X}_{1})-f^{*}\right)}{\gamma}}\right\}.\end{aligned}

Dividing both sides by
K
, we have the conclusion. At last, Lemma
C.1
guarantees

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \lambda\|\mathbf{X}_{k}\|_{op}\leq\frac{3\sqrt{\nu}}{K^{1/4}}=\frac{3}{\sqrt{1152}}\sqrt[4]{\frac{L\left(f(\mathbf{X}_{1})-f^{*}\right)}{K\hat{\sigma}^{2}}}<1\end{aligned}

by the setting of
\hat{\sigma}^{2}
.
∎

Lemma C.1
.

Let

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \lambda\|\mathbf{X}_{k}\|_{op}\leq\frac{3\sqrt{\nu}}{K^{1/4}},\quad\forall k=1,2,\cdots,K.\end{aligned}

Proof.

From the update of
\mathbf{X}_{k+1}

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \|\mathbf{X}_{k+1}\|_{op}-\frac{2}{\lambda}=&\left\|(1-\lambda\eta)\mathbf{X}_{k}-\eta\mathbf{L}_{k,\varepsilon}^{-\frac{1}{2p}}\mathbf{M}_{k}\mathbf{R}_{k,\varepsilon}^{-\frac{1}{2q}}\right\|_{op}-\frac{2}{\lambda}\\

\overset{(1)}{\leq}&(1-\lambda\eta)\left\|\mathbf{X}_{k}\right\|_{op}+2\eta-\frac{2}{\lambda}\\

\leq&(1-\lambda\eta)^{k}\left(\left\|\mathbf{X}_{1}\right\|_{op}-\frac{2}{\lambda}\right)\\

where we use Lemma

\eta\lambda\leq\frac{\sqrt{\nu}}{2K^{5/4}}\leq\frac{1}{2}

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} k\ln(1-\eta\lambda)=-k\ln\frac{1}{1-\eta\lambda}\geq-K\left(\frac{1}{1-\eta\lambda}-1\right)=-\frac{K\eta\lambda}{1-\eta\lambda}\geq-\frac{\sqrt{\nu}}{K^{1/4}},\end{aligned}

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} (1-\eta\lambda)^{k}\geq e^{-\frac{\sqrt{\nu}}{K^{1/4}}}\geq 1-\frac{\sqrt{\nu}}{K^{1/4}},\end{aligned}

and

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \|\mathbf{X}_{k+1}\|_{op}-\frac{2}{\lambda}\leq&-\frac{1}{\lambda}\left(1-\frac{\sqrt{\nu}}{K^{1/4}}\right)\left(2-\frac{\sqrt{\nu}}{K^{1/4}}\right)\leq-\frac{2}{\lambda}+\frac{3}{\lambda}\frac{\sqrt{\nu}}{K^{1/4}}.\end{aligned}

Lemma C.3
.

When each element of
\mathbf{G}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
is generated from Gaussian distribution with
\mu
mean and
\xi
variance independently, we have

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{G}\mathbf{G}^{T}\right]\succeq\frac{\xi^{2}}{m(\xi^{2}+\mu^{2})}\mathbb{E}\left[\|\mathbf{G}\|_{F}^{2}\right]\mathbf{I}_{m},\quad\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{G}^{T}\mathbf{G}\right]\succeq\frac{\xi^{2}}{n(\xi^{2}+\mu^{2})}\mathbb{E}\left[\|\mathbf{G}\|_{F}^{2}\right]\mathbf{I}_{n}.\end{aligned}

Proof.

When
\mathbf{G}_{i,j}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu,\xi)
, we have

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}\left[\left(\mathbf{G}\mathbf{G}^{T}\right)_{p,q}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathbf{G}_{p,j}\mathbf{G}_{q,j}\right]=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{G}_{p,j}\mathbf{G}_{q,j}\right]=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{G}_{p,j}\right]\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{G}_{q,j}\right]=n\mu^{2}\quad\mbox{if}\quad p\neq q\end{aligned}

and

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}\left[\left(\mathbf{G}\mathbf{G}^{T}\right)_{p,q}\right]=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{G}_{p,j}^{2}\right]=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\mathbf{G}_{p,j}-\mu\right)^{2}\right]+\mu^{2}\right)=n(\xi^{2}+\mu^{2})\quad\mbox{if}\quad p=q.\end{aligned}

So

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{G}\mathbf{G}^{T}\right]=n\mu^{2}\mathbf{1}_{m}\mathbf{1}_{m}^{T}+n\xi^{2}\mathbf{I}_{m}\succeq n\xi^{2}\mathbf{I}_{m},\end{aligned}

where
\mathbf{1}_{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
is the vector with all ones. We also have

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}\left[\|\mathbf{G}\|_{F}^{2}\right]=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{G}_{i,j}^{2}\right]=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\mathbf{G}_{i,j}-\mu\right)^{2}\right]+\mu^{2}\right)=mn(\xi^{2}+\mu^{2}).\end{aligned}

So we have

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{G}\mathbf{G}^{T}\right]\succeq n\xi^{2}\mathbf{I}_{m}=\frac{\xi^{2}}{m(\xi^{2}+\mu^{2})}\mathbb{E}\left[\|\mathbf{G}\|_{F}^{2}\right]\mathbf{I}_{m}.\end{aligned}

Similarly, we also have

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{G}^{T}\mathbf{G}\right]\succeq\frac{\xi^{2}}{n(\xi^{2}+\mu^{2})}\mathbb{E}\left[\|\mathbf{G}\|_{F}^{2}\right]\mathbf{I}_{n}.\end{aligned}

∎
