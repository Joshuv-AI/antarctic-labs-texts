Resting-state brain network alterations in adolescent idiopathic scoliosis using functional near-infrared spectroscopy
Authors: Wei Wang, Fanyuan Meng, Kerong Li, Chu Wang, Fengqi Hu, Oluwarotimi Williams Samuel, Lijuan Ao, Guanglin Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAdolescent Idiopathic Scoliosis (AIS) is a common spinal deformity arising during adolescence, a critical period for brain development. Although most AIS analyses predominantly focus on spinal or behavioral aspects, it remains unclear what brain functional alteration is associated with AIS. This gap in knowledge impedes a holistic understanding of the condition’s pathophysiology. In this study, functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) was used to explore the resting-state brain network in AIS.MethodsThe study recruited 25 AIS patients and 25 age-matched healthy controls and measured their brain activities during resting-state lying using fNIRS. Brain functional connectivity and network topology were evaluated for the two groups and their correlations with demographic and pathological variables were examined.ResultsThe functional connectivity in the patients, particularly single-curve patients, decreased and was sparser in the parietal and prefrontal regions in comparison to the healthy controls. The regional nodal metrics in the patients were significantly altered, with smaller nodal degrees and efficiency observed in certain nodes. Among the affected regions, the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex emerged repeatedly as a hub showing altered connectivity in patients, particularly in relation to parietal and sensorimotor regions. In addition, different correlations between brain network metrics and demographic as well as pathological parameters were identified within patients. For instance, a larger primary Cobb angle was associated with poorer small-world properties, suggesting greater structural deformity may be linked to less efficient functional network organization. Furthermore, a support vector machine-based classification model using functional connectivity achieved an average accuracy of 81.0%, demonstrating that fNIRS-based network features are sensitive to group-level neurofunctional differences.DiscussionThese findings provide insights into how scoliosis, including curvature, physical parameters, and pathological conditions, affects AIS patients in shaping abnormal brain network organization. Such network-based approaches may facilitate future studies aimed at assessing neurofunctional involvement associated with AIS rehabilitation interventions.Keywords: Adolescent idiopathic scoliosis, Brain network, Functional near-infrared spectroscopy, Network topology, Resting state functional connectivity

Introduction

Adolescent idiopathic scoliosis (AIS) is a complex spinal deformity characterized by twisted three-dimensional curvature of the spine without a known clear etiology [1, 2]. The disease is typically diagnosed in individuals aged 10 to 18 years, coinciding with critical periods of brain development. AIS can be regarded as a posture control disorder. Normal postural control requires well-integrated sensory inputs with central nervous system processing and functional output. However, traditionally the evaluation and treatment of AIS have largely focused on the functional output aspects in the spine area [3], such as bone curvature and physical functionality (e.g., limited joint mobility, muscle imbalance, and postural abnormalities) [4–6]. While these aspects are essential, they do not address the potential neural factors that may contribute to or result from AIS. The mechanism underlying the central nervous system involved in AIS remains largely unexplored.

Recent advances in brain science have begun to shed light on the possible involvement of the central nervous system in AIS, suggesting that functional neural disorder might play a role in the pathophysiology of this condition [7, 8]. Studies employing functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) [9], diffusion tensor imaging [10], and electroencephalography (EEG) [11, 12] reported structural and functional brain abnormalities in AIS patients. Studies using MRI have identified differences, such as reduced white matter integrity in the corpus callosum and the left internal capsule [13], and asymmetric hypertrophy of the brainstem, suggesting interhemispheric imbalance [10]. EEG investigations documented atypical brainwave patterns, including increased alpha and theta waves in specific brain regions [11], and differences in EEG oscillations in the sensorimotor cortex of the brain during standing balance control [12], which are thought to reflect compensatory mechanisms for sensorimotor dysfunction due to altered body shape. These findings collectively point towards abnormal neural development in AIS.

Despite these findings, many aspects of the relationship between AIS and brain function remain unclear. For instance, the functional connectivity and network topology of the brain have shown value in evaluating brain function as an important biomarker [14, 15]. The current methodologies, such as EEG and fMRI, while valuable, have limitations in terms of temporal resolution, spatial resolution, and signal-to-noise ratio [16], which highlight the need for further research into the brain's functional mechanisms under natural conditions. Functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) has emerged as a promising tool for investigating brain function [16, 17], which noninvasively measures hemodynamic responses associated with neural activity by detecting changes in oxygenated and deoxygenated hemoglobin concentrations. The fNIRS technique offers several advantages over traditional imaging modalities. It provides a balance between temporal and spatial resolution, being relatively cost-effective, portable, and less susceptible to motion artifacts compared to EEG and MRI [18, 19]. Moreover, fNIRS can be used in naturalistic and dynamic activity settings, offering higher ecological validity for studying brain function, making it suitable for various research in brain development, cognitive science, and psychology [18–21]. Nonetheless, to the best of the author’s knowledge, no study has applied fNIRS to explore the functional connectivity and brain network of AIS patients. More importantly, while prior MRI-based studies primarily characterized structural or topological alterations of cortical networks, and EEG studies focused on oscillatory dynamics or regional activation during balance tasks, these studies have rarely addressed large-scale network-level functional interactions in AIS patients. In particular, subtype-related differences (e.g., single-curve versus double-curve AIS) and their associations with individual anthropometric or clinical characteristics have not been systematically examined at the functional connectivity level to date. Given its suitability for studying brain function under relatively natural and posture-related conditions, fNIRS offers a unique window into AIS-related neurofunctional organization that complements existing MRI and EEG findings.

Given the limited knowledge of brain functional connectivity of AIS and the strengths of fNIRS, this study proposes to leverage fNIRS to explore functional connectivity in patients with AIS. Considering the clinical features of AIS, including impaired posture and movement coordination, and previous findings of altered brain structures, we hypothesize that AIS may be associated with atypical functional connectivity, which could reflect neurodevelopmental alterations affecting such as motor planning, sensory integration, and cognitive control during adolescence. By mapping resting-state functional connectivity patterns in the resting brain, we aim to identify specific neural networks of AIS patients. This could not only enhance our understanding of the condition but also pave the way for the development of rehabilitation interventions that address both the biomechanical and neurological aspects of AIS.

Results

Differences in functional connectivity

34 measurement channels were analyzed, which covered eight right and eight left pairs of regions of interest (ROI) based on the Brodmann areas, including primary somatosensory cortex (PSC), primary motor cortex (PMC), pre-motor and supplementary motor cortex (PSMC), frontopolar cortex (FPC), dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC), pars triangularis Broca's area (PTB), orbitofrontal cortex (OFC), and inferior frontal gyrus (IFG). The functional connectivity of the healthy and patient groups, as well as the single-curve and double-curve patient subgroups, is presented in Fig. 1 (Fig. 1a illustrates channel-to-channel connectivity and Fig. 1b depicts ROI-to-ROI connectivity). All the four groups exhibited relatively strong within-network connectivity within the prefrontal network and the sensorimotor system. However, the overall connection strength in the patient group was relatively reduced compared to the healthy controls. The differences were more distinct when considering specific patient subgroups. Single-curve patients demonstrated sparse prefrontal lobe connectivity, while double-curve patients showed denser prefrontal connectivity, more similar to healthy subjects.

Functional connectivity patterns across different groups. a The maps of averaged correlation matrices between channel-to-channel pairs over the healthy subjects, the patient, the single-curve patient, and the double-curve patient, respectively. b Averaged ROI-to-ROI functional connectivity matrices for the same four groups. c Differences in the ROI pairs of functional connectivity between different groups. Note that in c, the box of light yellow indicates statistically greater connectivity in the former group compared to the latter group with p < 0.05, while the box of blue indicates statistically smaller connectivity in the former group with p < 0.05. d The distribution of the ROI-to-ROI correlation coefficients

To quantify the differences, the ROI pairs that showed statistically significant differences (two-sample t-test, uncorrected p < 0.05) were depicted in Fig. 1 (c). In general, when considering all AIS patients as a single group, several ROI-to-ROI connections showed reduced functional connectivity compared with healthy controls at the uncorrected significance level (p < 0.05). These decreased connections mainly involved the parietal–prefrontal network (e.g., left PSC–left DLPFC), intra-hemispheric sensorimotor areas (e.g., right PMC–right PSMC), motor–language interface regions (e.g., right PMC–bilateral PTB and right PMC–right IFG), and inter-hemispheric connections (e.g., bilateral FPC).

When stratifying AIS patients by curve pattern, the single-curve AIS group exhibited more widespread decreases in functional connectivity relative to healthy controls. These reductions primarily involved parietal–prefrontal regions (e.g., right PSC–right DLPFC, left PMC–right DLPFC, left PSMC–bilateral DLPFC), sensorimotor areas and their connections with frontal regions (e.g., PMC/PSMC with FPC, PTB, and IFG), as well as multiple inter-hemispheric frontal connections (e.g., bilateral PSC, bilateral FPC, and bilateral OFC). In contrast, the double-curve group showed a more limited pattern of altered connectivity. Only a small number of connections differed from healthy controls, predominantly involving parietal–frontal and sensorimotor-related connections (e.g., left PSC–left DLPFC and right PMC–right PSMC). In addition, double-curve patients exhibited a unique presence of an enhanced connection between the right OFC and left DLPFC.

Direct comparison between patient subtypes revealed that single-curve patients had significantly more extensive connectivity reductions than double-curve patients. These decreases mainly involved inter-hemispheric parietal and frontal connections (e.g., bilateral PSC, bilateral DLPFC, and bilateral OFC) and sensorimotor–frontal connections (e.g., left PMC/PSMC with bilateral DLPFC and right FPC). Overall, the DLPFC emerged repeatedly as a region showing altered connectivity in the direct comparison between the two subtypes.

It is important to emphasize that, although these patterns were consistent across analyses, none of the reported connectivity differences survived FDR correction. Accordingly, the present results should be interpreted as exploratory and descriptive of group-level trends rather than as definitive evidence of altered functional networks.

In terms of the distribution of correlation coefficients (Fig. 1d), the average distribution is comparable between the patient and the healthy groups (t test, p = 0.34, Cohen's d = − 0.27). The averaged correlation coefficients over all ROI-to-ROI pairs and across all subjects within each group were 0.30 (standard deviation (STD) = 0.13) for the healthy group and 0.26 (STD = 0.11) for the patient group, respectively. In specific, however, there were large differences in terms of the correlations between the single-curve patient and double-curve patient when compared within the patient subgroups, and also when compared to the healthy group. The averaged correlation coefficients between the single-curve and the double-curve patients were 0.19 (STD = 0.08) vs. 0.30 (STD = 0.10, with t test p = 0.01, Cohen's d = − 1.17), with double-curve patients showing higher correlations. Statistical difference was also shown between single-curve patients and healthy group (p = 0.04, Cohen's d = − 1.17), while no difference was found between double-curve patients and healthy group (p = 0.91, Cohen's d = 0.04).

The support vector machine (SVM) classifier using the functional connectivity features achieved a mean classification accuracy of 81.60% (± 4.30%) across 10 repeated runs, indicating a stable group-level discriminability between AIS patients and healthy controls based on resting-state functional connectivity features. The model demonstrated a high sensitivity of 86.80% (± 4.24%) and a specificity of 76.40% (± 5.48%). The receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve of the SVM classifier, as illustrated in Fig. 2, showed a mean area under the curve (AUC) of 0.88 (± 0.03) across 10 repeated cross-validation runs, reflecting a good overall separability between the two groups.

Relationship between brain network features and demographic as well pathological variables

The relationship between brain network metrics and demographics (height, weight, BMI, age) and pathological variables (Cobb angle, number of curves, scoliosis type, Risser sign) revealed diverse patterns. In terms of the global network topology metrics (Tables 2, 3), in both healthy and AIS groups, height was positively correlated with small-worldness (σ) and global efficiency, and negatively correlated with clustering coefficient, shortest path length, and local efficiency. Weight was negatively correlated with shortest path length and positively correlated with global efficiency. Age showed negative correlations with shortest path length. Specifically, for the healthy subject, weight, and age was also positively correlated with global efficiency. BMI showed negative correlations with the clustering coefficient and local efficiency. In terms of the age-dependent trajectories of brain network properties, the general linear model (GLM) revealed a significant group-by-age interaction effects for shortest path length (Fig. 4c, p = 0.043, at a network sparsity of 25%). Healthy controls showed a pronounced age-related decrease in path length (slope = − 0.079), whereas AIS patients exhibited a markedly attenuated slope (− 0.001), indicating divergent developmental trajectories between groups at this topological scale.

Discussion

In this study, we explored the resting-state functional connectivity and brain network topology in AIS patients compared to healthy controls. This work represents a novel step toward elucidating neural alterations associated with AIS. The results highlight significant group-level differences in brain connectivity and network properties between these groups.

Functional connectivity

The analysis of functional connectivity between healthy subjects and AIS patients, including single-curve and double-curve subgroups, revealed several noteworthy patterns. In general, the AIS group, particularly the single-curve patients, exhibited decreased correlations in the prefrontal lobe regions and the parietal lobe. The prefrontal lobe plays a critical role in executive functions and higher cognitive processes [22]. The decreased correlations in the prefrontal regions of patients suggest disruptions in the prefrontal networks that may impact decision-making, problem-solving, and social behavior [22, 23].

The study further found distinct functional connectivity patterns in single-curve and double-curve AIS patients, which suggests that the type of spinal curvature impacts brain connectivity and highlights the distinct neural adaptations. Single-curve patients demonstrated sparse prefrontal connectivity, while double-curve patients showed denser connectivity, more similar to healthy subjects. The sparse functional connectivity in the prefrontal lobe of single-curve patients may reflect a localized disruption in neural connectivity and impaired executive functioning and cognitive control capabilities, possibly due to the structural constraints imposed by the spinal curvature. The more normative functional connectivity in double-curve patients' prefrontal lobes, indicates compensatory neural mechanisms attempting to maintain functional integrity and posture balance. The findings in the brain connectivity can possibly explain the results of the postural control test by Gauchard et al., who found that the different types of major curves had different effects on lateral disequilibrium and vestibular symmetry, and patients with double major curves performed better in both static and dynamic postural tests than those with single curves [24].

Notably, the recurrent involvement of the DLPFC in altered connectivity patterns across both single-curve and double-curve patients suggests significant disruptions in this region's functional integration within the brain network. The DLPFC is a critical area involved in executive functions, such as working memory and cognitive flexibility, and also involved in motor planning, organization, and regulation [25, 26]. The absent correlations involving the DLPFC and motor-related regions (PSC, PMC, PSMC) suggest that AIS may impact sensorimotor integration and cognitive processing (coordinating motor planning and execution), potentially explaining some of the cognitive and behavioral difficulties observed in AIS patients [27, 28]. In addition, the increased DLPFC's connections with the OFC in double-curve patients, which is involved in emotional regulation and decision-making, could indicate potential impacts on motor disorders in AIS patients [29].

The differences in the disrupted correlations between single-curve and double-curve patients can be attributed to the nature of the spinal deformities. The involved double-curve patients in this study all had a right thoracic curve and a left lumbar curve. While the single-curve patients had a variety of single-curve types (four cases of single left lumbar curves, two cases of single left lumbar curve, and three cases of single right thoracic curve). The right DLPFC's reduced correlations in single-curve patients, particularly with left hemisphere regions (left PMC, left PSMC), may indicate lateralized impairments or the body's compensation for the spinal deformity predominantly affecting one side. In double-curve patients, the left DLPFC showed absent correlations with the left PSC. This could suggest a different pattern of lateralization or a more balanced but globally disrupted connectivity pattern due to the more extensive spinal involvement and the bilateral nature of the spinal curves. While the left and right DLPFC share functions related to executive control and working memory, the right DLPFC is more involved in spatial processing, emotional regulation, and creative thinking, and the left DLPFC is more specialized for verbal and analytical tasks [30]. The spinal deformity in single-curve patients is unilateral, which might necessitate greater reliance on the right DLPFC for compensatory mechanisms in spatial processing and emotional regulation. The reduced correlations could indicate that these compensatory efforts strain the connectivity involving the right DLPFC.

The machine learning analysis based on resting-state functional connectivity demonstrated promising classification performance, with an overall accuracy exceeding 81% and an AUC of 0.88. These results suggest that altered functional brain network patterns in AIS patients can be effectively captured and leveraged for individual-level prediction, highlighting the potential of functional connectivity as a biomarker for AIS-related neural alterations.

Comparison with MRI and EEG modalities

The observations in this study of reduced connectivity within the parietal–prefrontal and inter-hemispheric networks align well with existing MRI-based studies. Specifically, the systemic decrease in functional connectivity parallels the structural deficits reported by Xue et al. [10] and Wang et al. [13], who identified reduced white matter integrity in the corpus callosum and decreased cortical thickness correlations in AIS patients. Chaloupka et al. [9] demonstrated decreased functional connectivity of the secondary motor area, basal ganglia, cuneus, and cerebellum. This consistency suggests that the sparse functional network captured via fNIRS is a direct functional manifestation of underlying neuroanatomical disruptions. Furthermore, our findings of impaired connectivity related to the PSC and PMC regions are consistent with the sensorimotor dysfunction previously suggested by EEG studies [11, 28]. While EEG has identified atypical oscillatory patterns (e.g., increased alpha peak frequency) during balance tasks [12, 31], our fNIRS data provides the spatial precision to localize these disruptions to specific cortical hubs, confirming that the sensorimotor-related integration issues in AIS are present even in a resting, supine state.

Our study extends current knowledge by identifying the DLPFC as a critical hub of functional impairment. While most EEG studies emphasize sensorimotor regions (such as the PMC) [12, 31], our results show that the DLPFC is repeatedly involved in various comparisons (AIS vs. healthy controls, single- vs. double-curve patients). This suggests that AIS pathology may extend beyond basic motor control to higher-order executive networks. Another contribution of this study is that the divergence in subtype characteristics and developmental trajectories offers unique insights. Unlike previous MRI- or EEG-based studies that often treat AIS as a homogeneous group [9, 10, 12, 13, 28, 31], our analysis reveals that single-curve patients exhibit more extensive connectivity reductions compared to double-curve patients, particularly involving parietal–prefrontal and inter-hemispheric frontal connections (e.g., bilateral PSC, DLPFC, and OFC). This functional divergence might explain the clinical heterogeneity in balance strategies between subtypes. Moreover, while EEG has shown age-related changes in cortical dynamics [32], this fNIRS study specifically captured a significant group-by-age interaction effect on the shortest path length. The attenuated developmental slope in AIS patients (slope = − 0.001) compared to healthy controls (slope = − 0.079) suggests that AIS may involve a "maturation lag" in brain network efficiency during adolescence—a finding that complements the static structural snapshots provided by MRI [13].

Brain network topology

The analysis of brain network topology in AIS patients provides valuable insights into the neural consequences of this condition. The differences in regional nodal metrics between AIS patients and healthy controls were particularly evident at several sparsity thresholds. Specifically, the parietal node in the PSC, as well as the prefrontal node in the DLPFC, demonstrated smaller nodal degree and efficiency in AIS patients compared to healthy subjects. This difference points to a potential delay or immaturity in the development of specific brain regions responsible for motor-sensory processing and executive functions in AIS patients, which are less efficiently connected and could be linked to postural control deficits and neurodevelopmental disorders [15, 33].

Additionally, there were also notable differences in nodal metrics between single-curve and double-curve AIS patients. Single-curve patients exhibited greater nodal efficiency in the left PMC and PSMC as compared to double-curve patients, suggesting the latter group might experience more significant adaptions in regions associated with motor control. Conversely, single-curve patients had larger nodal degrees in the prefrontal nodes, indicating potential compensatory mechanisms in executive function areas to maintain balance and posture.

Despite these regional differences, the overall global network properties, such as small-worldness, clustering coefficient, shortest path length, global efficiency, and local efficiency, were not statistically different between patients and healthy controls. These comparable characteristics indicate that the fundamental organizational principles of the brain's network architecture remain intact in AIS patients, even though the efficiency of information transfer within a specific region network is compromised. Such findings are compatible with the previous structural brain network study [13], which demonstrated that AIS patients preserve the small-world architecture and organization but show altered nodal characteristics.

Relationship with demographic and pathological variables

The correlation analysis between brain network metrics and demographic as well as pathological variables revealed complex interactions, which further characterized the integration and segregation of the brain in healthy development and pathological conditions. Among the demographic variables, height emerges as a notable parameter correlated with several brain network metrics in both healthy individuals and AIS patients. Generally, greater height is associated with a higher clustering coefficient, greater global efficiency, and shorter path length, which suggest that physical growth and maturation enhance brain network efficiency. These findings align with the previous research that indicated similar trends in the development of brain cognitive ability with respect to height [34].

Age, as a marker in the developing brain, shows a negative correlation with the shortest path length and a positive correlation with global efficiency in healthy adolescents. These phenomena align with the findings of Cai et al. [24], who demonstrated that global and local network efficiencies are lower in children and adolescents while increasing into adulthoods, suggesting enhanced integration of functional networks during development. They also found that small-worldness and modularity remained consistent across developmental stages, indicating the maturity of these critical network properties in the measured stage. However, in AIS patients, age is only correlated with the shortest path length and small-worldness but not with global efficiency. Notably, a significant Group × Age interaction was observed for characteristic path length at a network sparsity of 25%, indicating a divergence in age-related developmental trajectories between AIS patients and healthy controls. This finding implies that factors other than age might interfere with the typical maturation process of functional network integration in AIS patients, possibly due to the pathological alterations in their neurophysiological development. Although this interaction did not persist across all sparsity levels or in AUC-based metrics, its presence at a specific network density suggests that AIS-related neurofunctional alterations may emerge at particular topological scales rather than as a global shift across thresholds.

The relationship between scoliosis severity and brain network organization is particularly noteworthy. Our findings indicate that a larger primary Cobb angle is associated with poorer small-world properties, which suggests that more severe spinal curvatures are associated with less optimal brain network organization. The deterioration in small-world properties reflects a disruption in the balance between local specialization and global integration within the brain network. This is supported by literature indicating that efficient small-world networks are crucial for optimal cognitive functioning and neural processing [15].

More specifically, the findings suggest that single-curve and double-curve AIS patients exhibit fundamentally different brain network organization and interactions with their demographic and pathological parameters. For example, in single-curve patients, higher body weight correlates with better small-world properties and shorter path length (similar to the healthy group), possibly indicating that increased physical robustness supports better neural integration. In contrast, the correlation in double-curve patients between greater weight and longer shorter length might imply that the increased body mass may exacerbate the inefficiencies in brain network connectivity, potentially due to the additional biomechanical and metabolic demands placed on the body. Furthermore, the pathological parameters (such as major Cobb angle, secondary Cobb angle, and Risser sign) in double-curve patients are statistically significantly correlated with the global and local brain network topology, while such significant correlations were not shown in single-curve patients. The negative correlations in double-curve patients between primary and secondary curve angles and key network properties such as small-worldness, clustering coefficient, and efficiency suggest that more severe spinal curvatures in double-curve patients are associated with less optimal brain network organization. These differences between single- and double-curve patients further underscore the profound impact of different types of spinal deformity on neural architecture and adaptations to scoliosis.

The variability in significant correlations across different sparsity thresholds reflects the sensitivity of network metrics to network density. Lower sparsity thresholds capture stronger connections, while higher thresholds include weaker connections, highlighting different aspects of network organization. This variability underscores the importance of considering multiple thresholds in network analysis.

Limitations and future directions

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the results. Firstly, the sample size of AIS patients, particularly when divided into subgroups, was relatively small, potentially reducing the statistical power of our analyses and influencing the robustness of our findings. This limitation directly impacted on the statistical analyses of functional connection differences between different groups: after applying the FDR correction to control for multiple comparisons across all connection pairs, no individual pairs survived the rigorous threshold. This outcome underscores that, while observable trends may exist, the present study is underpowered to definitively identify specific dysregulated connections at the current statistical level. Therefore, consistent with the exploratory nature of this work and the constraints of the sample size, the group-level differences in functional connectivity based on uncorrected thresholds should be interpreted as descriptive of trends rather than conclusive evidence for alterations. Future studies with larger cohorts are warranted to validate these preliminary observations with adequate statistical power. In addition, this study also did not account for the specific curve direction or other detailed classifications of AIS subtypes due to the limited sample size of some subgroups. Future research should aim to expand the sample size to explore the neural characteristics of more specific subtypes and enhance generalizability. Additionally, our analysis focused on specific ROIs and might have overlooked other critical brain regions that could be affected by AIS. For instance, we did not analyze occipital and temporal lobe data, which have shown structural abnormalities in previous MRI studies [13]. Including these regions could provide a more comprehensive understanding of brain alterations associated with AIS. Moreover, this study focused on resting-state functional connectivity, which does not capture task-related brain function. Future studies should combine task-based fNIRS with spine motion or physiological analysis methods [6, 35, 36] to provide insights into the cognitive and motor impairments associated with AIS. While this study focused on the most impacted group of AIS (female adolescents), it may limit the generalizability of the findings to male patients and other age groups. Future research should include a more diverse cohort to comprehensively understand AIS across different demographics. Last, while alterations observed in relevant brain regions—such as the DLPFC—may suggest potential vulnerabilities in these cognitive functions, these interpretations are speculative and based on the known functional roles of these regions, rather than direct functional assessments in the current cohort, and further studies are needed to directly evaluate such functional outcomes.

It is important to emphasize that the SVM-based machine learning analysis in this study was not intended to compete with established clinical diagnostic criteria for AIS, which rely primarily on radiographic and physical assessments such as the Cobb angle. Instead, the machine learning analysis was used to demonstrate that resting-state fNIRS functional connectivity captures disease-related group-level neurofunctional alterations. Functional outcomes such as motor function are clinically meaningful measures of patient’s motor performance, balance, or functional ability. In future fNIRS studies, integrating functional outcome measures with predictive modeling will be essential to determine the added value of fNIRS-based connectivity beyond established clinical indicators in clinical decision-making.

Conclusion

To the best of the authors’ knowledge, this study is the first to employ fNIRS to explore functional connectivity and brain network topology in AIS patients, offering new insights into the neural impacts underlying AIS and highlighting the potential of fNIRS as a valuable tool for investigating brain function in clinical populations. The findings of decreased connectivity particularly interactions between prefrontal and sensorimotor-related regions and reduced network nodal efficiency in AIS patients suggest that AIS is associated with disruptions in brain network organization, which may contribute to the postural control deficits, sensory-motor integration, and emotional regulation in these patients. Particularly, single-curve and double-curve AIS demonstrate distinct functional connectivity patterns, which suggests different neural adaptations to their respective spinal deformities. The observed correlations between height, age, and brain network metrics in healthy individuals reflect typical neurodevelopmental patterns, while the deviations seen in AIS patients highlight the impact of scoliosis on brain connectivity. These patterns provide insights into how scoliosis, including different types of curvature, physical parameters, and pathological conditions, affects AIS patients in shaping brain network organization. These findings also provide a basis for future research to explore potential personalized rehabilitation strategies that may address the specific neural disruptions associated with different types of scoliosis to improve neural and functional outcomes for AIS patients.

Methods

Subjects

This study recruited 25 AIS female patients and 25 healthy female subjects as the control group from the Second Affiliated Hospital of Kunming Medical University (Table 6). The inclusion criteria of the patients were: (1) being diagnosed with AIS through X-ray examination; (2) aged between 10–18 years old; and (3) with primary Cobb angle 10–40°. Exclusion criteria were (1) patients with other diseases; and (2) patients who were previously treated with orthoses or surgery. The reason that only female subjects were recruited because AIS is significantly more prevalent and severe in females, especially during adolescence [3]. And the age range of 10–18 years was selected to capture the typical period of rapid spinal growth and neurological development when AIS commonly manifests and progresses. This study was approved by the Ethics committee of Kunming Medical University (KMMU2023MEC156). The trial protocol was registered at the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (www.chictr.org.cn, ChiCTR2300074016, 2023/07/27). All participants signed a statement of informed consent before participating in the study.

Experimental setup

During the experiment, the subjects were asked to lie down on a bed, keeping relaxed and awake with their eyes closed for 5 min. The light intensity signal was collected using a portable fNIRS device (NirsSmartII-3000B, Huichuang Medical Equipment Co., Ltd, China) with a sampling rate of 11 Hz. The fNIRS cap (with 32 optodes, including 24 emitters and 11 detectors) was placed parallel to the coronal plane, through the vertex (Cz), according to the Brodmann areas and in the international 10–20 system format [37] (Fig. 5). The measurement channels were named symmetrical between the right and left hemispheres, with odd numbers in the right hemisphere and even numbers in the left hemisphere. These selected sensorimotor (PSC, PMC, PSMC) and prefrontal (FPC, DLPFC, PTB, OFC, IFG) regions were selected based on their established involvement in motor control, sensorimotor integration, executive regulation, and balance-related processes [7, 8, 38], which are highly relevant to the neurofunctional mechanisms underlying postural control and compensatory strategies possibly in AIS.

Experimental setup of the fNIRS data collection: a the position of the sources and detectors, b the used measurement channels, and c the regions of interest

Data preprocessing

The collected raw optical density data was converted and processed using the NIRS-KIT toolkit [39] to obtain the preprocessed time course of concentration changes of the oxygenated (HbO) and deoxygenated (HbR) hemoglobin via the modified Beer-Lambert law [40]. Specifically, the data of the first 60 s and last 15 s of the entire time course was trimmed as the functional connectivity was found usually to be stable after 1 min [41]. Then the data was detrended using a first-order polynomial regression model to subtract linear detrends and avoid long-term physiological shifts or instrumental instability. The temporal derivative distribution repair method [42] was applied to correct for motion-related spike artifacts and abrupt baseline shifts, enhancing the temporal continuity of the signal without requiring short-separation channels. Finally, the data was band-pass filtered at 0.01–0.08 Hz using a third-order infinite impulse response filter to remove physiological noise. In this study, we mainly used the HbO signals for analysis as they show a better signal-to-noise ratio than the HbR signal [43].

Analysis

Figure 6 depicts the workflow of the analysis procedure. We first calculated the functional connectivity between the time courses of each measurement channel using the Pearson correlation method in MATLAB(‘corrcoef’ function), which resulted in a 34 × 34 correlation matrix for each subject. Given the unclear meaning of the biological interpretation of negative correlations in resting-state fNIRS data [44], a sensitivity analysis was performed to evaluate the potential influence of negative correlations on functional connectivity analysis. Three commonly used strategies were compared: raw correlations, positive-only correlations, and absolute correlations (Detailed in the supplementary material). The results demonstrated highly consistent group-level statistics, effect sizes, and functional connectivity patterns across all three approaches (e.g., pattern similarity correlation r > 0.93), indicating that the main analyses are not sensitive to the treatment of negative correlations. Therefore, in this study raw Pearson correlation coefficients were used for the primary functional connectivity analyses in order to preserve both positive and negative inter-regional relationships.

Schematic overview of the fNIRS-based analysis pipeline

Based on the graph theory of the brain [15], we used metrics such as the clustering coefficient to characterize the topological organization of the brain's functional networks. Graph-theoretical analyses were performed at the channel level to preserve network resolution and ensure the stability of topological metrics. The calculation formula and definition of the network metrics are summarized in Table 7. These measures were chosen because they capture both integration (how efficiently information is exchanged across the entire network) and segregation (the degree of local clustering), which are fundamental features of brain functional organization. Detailed descriptions can be found in [33, 44]. The analysis was performed using the GRETNA toolkit [45], a graph theoretical network analysis toolbox. The toolbox took the correlation matrix in the form of absolute and binary as input. A range of network sparsity from 5 to 50% with a step size of 5% was used in running the analysis. In addition, for each metric, the area under curve (AUC) of all sparsity conditions for a network was also calculated, with a prefix of the letter ‘a’ for identification (e.g., aCp).

Statistical analyses were performed in MATLAB as follows.

Group differences in resting-state functional connectivity were examined at the regional (ROI) level rather than the original channel level, as the former approach enhances the signal-to-noise ratio and provided anatomically interpretable unit of analyses. Specifically, channel-level time series were first averaged within each pre-defined region of interest (ROI) to obtain representative ROI-level signals. Functional connectivity matrices were then constructed by computing the Pearson correlation between the time series of all ROI pairs. Differences in the functional connectivity of each unique ROI pair, global mean functional connectivity (the average of all unique connections), and the brain network metrics between the AIS patients and healthy controls were evaluated using the two-sample Student’s t test, typically with a statistical probability value p being less than 0.05 indicating a statistical significant level. Note that only the upper triangular elements of the symmetric ROI × ROI matrix were tested, thereby avoiding redundant comparisons of identical pairs and excluding self-connections. To control multiple ROI-to-ROI edge-wise comparisons, False Discovery Rate (FDR) correction was applied, with the threshold set at q < 0.05.

We further analyzed the relationship between brain network features and the demographic variables of the subjects (including the height, weight, and age of the two groups and specifically the core symptoms of the patient group). The Pearson correlation analysis was used for exploring correlations between brain features and continuous variables, including height, weight, BMI, age, major Cobb angle, and secondary Cobb angle (treated as zero for patients without a secondary curve). And the analysis of one variance (ANOVA, MATLAB function ‘anova1’) was used for categorical variables (e.g., the type of single or double curve(s)). Moreover, as significant differences were found between single-curve and double-curve patient subgroups, the above analyses were also performed for the two subgroups of patients.

In addition, adolescence represents a critical period for brain network maturation, and prior neurodevelopmental studies have demonstrated that deviations from typical age-related trajectories may reflect pathological alterations in brain network development (e.g., Ouyang et al. [46]; Zhu et al. [47]). Accordingly, to explicitly investigate age-dependent effects, we conducted additional analyses examining the relationship between age and global brain network metrics. A general linear model (GLM) was applied to each global network metric, with network property as the dependent variable and age, group (healthy controls vs. AIS patients), and their interaction (Group × Age) as independent variables. This modeling framework allows direct assessment of whether age-related developmental trajectories of brain network properties differ between groups.

To further evaluate whether the resting-state brain functional connectivity are sensitive to group-level neurofunctional differences between AIS patients and healthy controls, a supervised machine learning approach was implemented. Specifically, a support vector machine (SVM) classifier with a linear kernel was trained using the pairwise functional connectivity matrices from patients and controls as input features in Matlab (the SVM model was selected after preliminary comparisons due to its superior balance between sensitivity and specificity). Each subject’s upper triangular connectivity matrix (excluding diagonal) was vectorized, resulting in a 561-dimensional feature vector. Model performance was assessed using stratified fivefold cross-validation repeated 10 times to ensure stability. The evaluation metrics included classification accuracy, sensitivity, the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) and the AUC of ROC.
