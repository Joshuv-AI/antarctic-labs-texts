Effectiveness of a nurse-led digital reminiscence program on social interaction, affect and engagement in residential dementia care: a quasi-experimental study
Authors: Pablo de Lorena-Quintanal, Gina Lladó-Jordan, Esther Domínguez-Valdés, Antonia Rodríguez-Rodríguez, Carmen Sarabia-Cobo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDigital reminiscence therapy (DRT) shows promise in dementia care, but evidence on social and affective outcomes in long-term care settings remains limited.MethodsA quasi-experimental study was conducted with 60 residents with dementia (Global Deterioration Scale stages 4–5) from two long-term care facilities. The intervention group received 12 nurse-led DRT sessions over six weeks, while the control group received usual care. Primary outcomes included social interaction, observable affect, and engagement, assessed at baseline (T0), post-intervention (T1), and four-week follow-up (T2) using linear mixed-effects models.ResultsThe intervention group showed improved engagement, positive affect, and social interaction compared with the control group across the study period. Improvements in engagement and positive affect were of large magnitude, while communication outcomes showed moderate-to-large effects. No clear changes were observed in global quality of life, although improvements were identified in the QoL-AD domains of personal relationships and meaningful activities.ConclusionsNurse-led DRT appears to be a feasible psychosocial intervention associated with improved social and affective outcomes in residential dementia care. These findings support the potential value of structured, digitally supported reminiscence in routine nursing practice, although confirmation in larger and more diverse samples is still needed. Background

Dementia affects more than 55 million people worldwide, with projections exceeding 139 million by 2050. A substantial proportion of people living with moderate to advanced dementia reside in long-term care facilities, where daily life may be characterized by limited stimulation, reduced opportunities for meaningful interaction, and high levels of psychosocial deprivation. In these settings, social withdrawal, apathy, and diminished affective engagement are frequent and clinically relevant manifestations, associated with poorer quality of life and reduced participation in everyday activities [1, 2]. Previous studies have highlighted that institutional routines, although necessary for care organization, may unintentionally limit opportunities for individualized and emotionally meaningful engagement when structured psychosocial interventions are not incorporated [3, 4].

Digital Reminiscence Therapy (DRT) has emerged as a promising approach to caring for people with dementia, particularly in institutional settings. It combines traditional reminiscence techniques, such as recalling significant autobiographical events, with digital technologies that enable personalised content to be created using photos, music or videos relevant to each individual. Previous studies have demonstrated that DRT can alleviate depressive symptoms and enhance engagement during sessions, yielding superior outcomes compared to traditional therapies [1, 2]. Furthermore, its implementation in small groups over periods of eight to twelve weeks has been shown to improve mood and functionality, and in some cases cognition, in individuals with mild to moderate Alzheimer’s disease [5, 6].

Despite these positive results, much of the DRT literature has prioritised measuring cognitive outcomes for evaluation purposes, paying less attention to behavioural, emotional, or relational variables. However, there is growing evidence that variables such as active participation, observable affection and social interaction are sensitive and relevant indicators of the effect of psychosocial interventions, and that they play a key role in modulating the progression of deterioration [7, 8]. Social interaction and positive emotional experiences are considered relevant therapeutic targets in dementia care because they may activate relatively preserved relational and affective capacities [9, 10]. While traditional reminiscence therapy has shown promising effects on communication, mood, and social participation, fewer studies have examined whether these benefits are also observed in digital reminiscence formats, particularly when social interaction, observable affect, and engagement are defined as primary outcomes.

Using digital technologies in this type of intervention has several advantages: it enables greater adaptability and personalisation of stimuli, facilitates the integration of engaging multimedia elements and provides an immersive sensory experience, which can improve memory and emotional expression [11–13]. This potential is particularly important in long-term care settings, where a repetitive care routine can lead to withdrawal and apathy if meaningful, emotionally relevant activities are not incorporated.

In this context, nursing plays a key role. Due to their proximity to patients, their in-depth knowledge of their life history and their central role in care teams, nurses are particularly well positioned to lead these types of interventions. Furthermore, their active involvement in designing, implementing and evaluating programmes such as DRT strengthens their digital skills while promoting person-centred, humanised care [14, 15]. However, integrating digital therapies into clinical practice requires overcoming organisational barriers and fostering a culture of care that values psychosocial benefits as much as traditional clinical outcomes [16].

The present study differs from previous research in several ways. Firstly, it incorporates a structured, standardised, group-based digital intervention facilitated by trained nursing staff and implemented in two long-term care facilities. Secondly, in addition to mood and communication scales, validated observational measures of social interaction, affect and behavioural participation were included, areas that have been little explored in previous literature. Finally, the programme design and training can be easily replicated in other centres, enabling this intervention to be considered a transferable model of technology-based nursing practice.

The present study addresses these gaps by implementing a standardized, group-based DRT protocol facilitated exclusively by trained nursing staff, and by positioning social interaction, observable affect, and behavioral engagement as primary outcomes, not secondary or exploratory variables. This framing reflects a deliberate theoretical stance: that psychosocial responsiveness constitutes a clinically meaningful intervention target in its own right, regardless of effects on global cognition [16].

This study positions digital reminiscence not only as a therapeutic activity but as a structured, nurse-led digital intervention model, with potential for integration into routine care in long-term care settings. The main contribution of this study lies in framing digital reminiscence as a structured, nurse-led intervention model rather than a standalone activity, highlighting its potential scalability and integration into routine care.

Aims

The aim of this study was to evaluate whether participation in a personalized digital reminiscence therapy program was associated with changes in social interaction, observable affect, and engagement in nursing home residents with dementia, compared with usual care.

Study design. A quasi-experimental longitudinal study with a non-equivalent control group was conducted, involving repeated measurements at three time points. The study adhered to the Transparent Reporting of Evaluations with Non-randomised Designs (TREND) guidelines. A randomised controlled design was not feasible given the organisational structure of the facilities; to minimise contamination between experimental and control conditions, one facility was assigned to each group, consistent with cluster-level allocation procedures in real-world nursing home research.

Methods

Study setting and participants. The research was conducted in two long-term care facilities belonging to the same religious order and located in Italy. As both institutions shared the same care model and organisational structure, it was possible to standardise procedures and ensure data comparability.

The sample consisted of residents of both sexes who had been diagnosed with mild to moderate dementia according to clinical criteria (Global Deterioration Scale, GDS, by Reisberg et al.,) [17]. Inclusion criteria were: institutionalisation in one of the two participating nursing homes; a diagnosis of mild or moderate dementia according to the medical report (GDS 4–5); sufficient basic communication skills (verbal or gestural) to participate in group sessions; and clinical and functional stability over the previous month. Informed consent was required, signed either by the residents themselves if they had legal capacity or by their legal representatives. Residents with advanced dementia (GDS stages 6–7), severe uncompensated sensory impairment (visual or auditory), severe psychiatric comorbidities or participation in other therapeutic programmes with a reminiscence or cognitive component during the study period were excluded.

At the start of the study, the demographic and clinical characteristics (age, sex and stage of dementia according to the Global Deterioration Scale) of the participants in the experimental and control groups were similar, thus ensuring comparability for analysis of intervention effects.

Group assignment and sample size. Due to the organisational nature of the nursing homes, one was assigned to the experimental group (EG) and the other to the control group (CG). The sample size was estimated using the G*Power 3.1 programme, considering a medium effect size (f = 0.25), statistical power of 95%, and a significance level of 0.05. The calculation indicated a minimum sample size of 42 participants (21 per group), to which a safety margin was added to account for potential dropouts. A total of 60 participants were recruited, initially distributed in equal numbers across the two groups.

Description of the intervention. The experimental group participated in a Digital Reminiscence Therapy (DRT) programme designed for institutionalised people with dementia. The intervention consisted of 12 structured sessions, each lasting 60 min and delivered twice weekly over six consecutive weeks. Although 30 participants were allocated to the experimental group, the intervention was delivered in small-group sessions rather than to the full group simultaneously. Each session involved approximately 6–8 residents, allowing individualised content to be presented and participants to be supported adequately. Sessions were led by a nurse and an occupational therapist who had received specific training in geriatrics and digital skills. A participant flow diagram summarises the number of residents assessed for eligibility, included in the experimental and control groups, and retained at each study time point (T0, T1 and T2).

Electronic tablets were used to display personalised multimedia content, including photographs, videos, music, and virtual tours of places that were meaningful to participants. Personalised multimedia materials were developed using information provided by family members when available and supplemented by professional caregivers familiar with the resident’s life history and preferences. The intervention followed a standardised protocol based on the life review model, and each session included an introduction to the topic, a central phase of reminiscence and group conversation, and a closing phase focused on reinforcing emotional bonding and interpersonal interaction. Family members contributed information and materials for personalisation but did not usually attend the sessions, and no volunteers were involved in delivering the intervention.

Control group conditions. Participants in the control group continued to receive usual care, consisting of standard nursing home routines, including basic care, routine recreational activities available at the facility, and non-structured social interaction, without participation in the digital reminiscence programme. Upon completion of the study, an adapted version of the programme was offered to the control group as an ethical equity measure.

Outcome assessment and blinding. Outcome assessments based on observation were conducted by two external evaluators trained in the use of the study instruments. Due to the visible nature of the intervention, full blinding of observational assessors during sessions was not feasible. Data were collected at three time points: before the intervention (T0), immediately after the last session (T1), and four weeks after the end of the programme (T2). These repeated assessments were used to examine both immediate change and short-term maintenance of effects.

For both groups, observational measures (engagement, social interaction and affect) were collected at each time point during structured group situations of comparable duration. In the experimental group, observations were conducted during DRT sessions and their brief post-session activities, whereas in the control group they were carried out during usual group activities or communal periods scheduled at similar times of day within the routine programme. This procedure was intended to ensure that the context and length of observation were as comparable as possible across groups.

Variables and instruments. The primary outcome was social interaction, assessed using the Holden Communication Scale (HCS) [18], administered through structured observation. The HCS evaluates communication difficulties, with higher scores indicating greater impairment; therefore, lower scores reflect better communication performance.

Two co-primary process outcomes were also assessed because of their sensitivity to short-term psychosocial change. Engagement during sessions was measured using an observational protocol adapted from the Menorah Park Engagement Scale [19], including indicators such as verbal interaction, sustained attention, eye contact, turn-taking, and activity tracking. Observable affect was assessed using the Observed Emotion Rating Scale (OERS) [20], with coding focused on positive and negative emotional expressions during the DRT sessions and brief, structured group activities conducted immediately afterwards. These brief activities followed a standardised format, were facilitated by the same staff with the same group composition, and were planned as an extension of the intervention rather than as unrelated care events.

Global cognitive function was measured using the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) [21], which was included primarily as a marker of cognitive stability over the study period. Quality of life was assessed using the Quality of Life in Alzheimer’s Disease scale (QoL-AD) [22]. In addition to the total QoL-AD score, item-level analyses were conducted for the domains “personal relationships” and “engagement in meaningful activities” because these were considered conceptually relevant to the intervention targets. Mood was assessed using the 15-item Geriatric Depression Scale [23], and perceived caregiving environment was additionally explored through selected QoL-AD items and a brief ad hoc questionnaire completed by nursing staff facilitators. This questionnaire was developed ad hoc for the present study, based on previous qualitative work and expert consensus, to capture feasibility and acceptability aspects (e.g. staff satisfaction with the use of tablets, perceived ease of integrating DRT into routine care). It was not intended as a validated outcome scale, but rather as a descriptive process measure.

Demographic and clinical covariates included age, sex, years of education when available, dementia severity according to the Global Deterioration Scale, and duration of institutionalisation. Because short-term effects of reminiscence interventions are more commonly observed in affective, behavioural, and social domains than in global cognition or overall quality of life, MMSE and total QoL-AD scores were treated mainly as contextual or stability indicators.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS version 29. Longitudinal changes in outcomes across T0, T1, and T2 were examined using linear mixed-effects models. Group, time, and the group × time interaction were entered as fixed effects. Random effects were included to account for repeated observations within individuals and clustering by residential facility. This approach was selected to address the non-independence of observations arising from the clustered quasi-experimental design. A significance level of p < .05 was used for all statistical tests. The Results section should report the mixed-model estimates consistently and avoid presenting repeated-measures ANOVA statistics, which were used in the earlier draft. For each outcome, we report the p-value for the group × time interaction together with the estimated between-group differences in change and their 95% confidence intervals.

Ethical considerations

Although prospective registration was not completed prior to study initiation, the study protocol is available upon request and all procedures adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval was granted by the Bioethics Committee of the CR Santa Lucía Religious Congregation (Approval Code: 01/2025). All participants or their legal representatives provided written informed consent after receiving both verbal and written information regarding the study objectives, procedures, and their rights as participants. Confidentiality and data anonymity were strictly maintained throughout, and participants were informed of their right to withdraw at any time without affecting their access to care.

Results

A total of 60 participants completed the study, including 30 residents in the experimental group and 30 in the control group. No dropouts or losses to follow-up were recorded during the study period. A flow diagram summarising recruitment, group allocation, follow-up and analysis is presented in Fig. 1. Although all 30 participants allocated to the experimental group remained in the study until T2, attendance to intervention sessions was not identical for all participants. The mean number of sessions attended was 10.8 out of 12 (range: 8–12). Occasional absences were mainly related to intercurrent minor illness, temporary fatigue, family visits, or scheduling conflicts with routine care activities. These absences did not result in withdrawal from the study.

Engagement during sessions

Engagement during the reminiscence sessions was assessed using an observational checklist adapted from the Menorah Park Engagement Scale. Linear mixed-effects models showed a significant group × time interaction across T0, T1, and T2 (p = .003). Engagement scores increased over time in the experimental group, whereas no relevant changes were observed in the control group. The estimated between-group difference in change was 1.7 points at T1 (95% CI 1.1 to 2.3) and 1.7 points at T2 (95% CI 1.1 to 2.4), indicating a large effect. Participants in the experimental group demonstrated higher levels of verbal interaction, eye contact, and active participation during the intervention sessions, particularly at T1 and T2.

Observable affect

Observable affect was evaluated using the Observed Emotion Rating Scale (OERS). Mixed-effects analyses revealed a significant group × time interaction for positive affect scores (p = .008). Participants in the experimental group showed higher levels of positive affect at T1 and T2 compared with the control group, with partial maintenance of improvement at follow-up. The estimated between-group difference in change was 1.8 points at T1 (95% CI 1.1 to 2.5) and 1.7 points at T2 (95% CI 1.0 to 2.4), corresponding to a large effect. Expressions of smiling, laughter, and positive emotional tone were more frequently observed during intervention sessions in the experimental group.

Social interaction

Social interaction was assessed using the Holden Communication Scale. A significant group × time interaction was identified (p = .004). Holden scores decreased over time in the experimental group, indicating fewer communication difficulties, whereas the control group showed no substantial changes across measurement points. The estimated between-group difference in change was − 3.9 points at T1 (95% CI − 5.4 to − 2.4) and − 3.7 points at T2 (95% CI − 5.2 to − 2.1), reflecting moderate-to-large improvements in communication-related outcomes in the experimental group. Improvements in the experimental group were observed in communicative initiative, coherence of verbal responses, and emotional involvement during group interaction.

Quality of life and perceived social environment

No statistically significant group × time interaction was found for the total QoL-AD score. The estimated between-group differences in change were small and imprecise, amounting to 1.1 points at T1 (95% CI − 0.1 to 2.3) and 1.0 point at T2 (95% CI − 0.3 to 2.3). However, item-level analyses revealed statistically significant improvements in the experimental group in the domains “personal relationships” (p = .04) and “engagement in meaningful activities” (p = .03) at T1 and T2 compared with the control group. For personal relationships, the estimated between-group difference in change was 0.5 points at T1 (95% CI 0.1 to 0.9) and 0.5 points at T2 (95% CI 0.1 to 0.8), indicating a small-to-moderate effect. For meaningful activities, the corresponding differences were 0.6 points at T1 (95% CI 0.2 to 1.0) and 0.6 points at T2 (95% CI 0.2 to 1.0), consistent with a moderate effect.

Correlation analyses and conceptual framework

Exploratory Spearman correlation analyses within the experimental group revealed moderate positive associations between engagement scores and positive affect (r = .58, p < .01), as well as between engagement and social interaction outcomes (r = .51, p < .01). Based on these observed associations and the longitudinal patterns identified across outcome measures, a preliminary conceptual framework was developed to represent the relationships among engagement, emotional expression, social interaction, and perceived interpersonal environment within the intervention context (Fig. 2).

Preliminary conceptual framework based on observed associations. Note. The framework represents exploratory associations observed in the experimental group. Arrows indicate hypothesized associations based on correlation analyses and longitudinal patterns

The framework illustrates engagement during digital reminiscence sessions as a central process variable associated with observable positive affect and social interaction outcomes. These domains were additionally linked to improvements in selected QoL-AD interpersonal dimensions. Although the current study was not designed to formally test mediation pathways or structural relationships, the proposed framework provides a descriptive representation of the observed associations and may support future confirmatory analyses in larger samples.

Discussion

This study adds to the growing body of evidence supporting digital reminiscence therapy (DRT) as a psychosocial intervention in institutional settings for people living with dementia, particularly when social and affective outcomes are prioritised. In line with this focus, the intervention was associated with improvements in engagement, observable positive affect and social interaction in the experimental group, while global cognitive function and overall quality of life remained stable across both groups. These changes occurred without relevant differences in baseline characteristics or medication patterns and were accompanied by high levels of acceptance by nursing staff, reinforcing the feasibility of implementing nurse-led DRT within routine care in long-term care facilities.

The observed improvements in interaction and affect are consistent with previous research indicating that these domains are sensitive and early indicators of response to non-pharmacological interventions in dementia care [7, 8, 24]. Positive affect was recorded during the DRT sessions and the brief, standardised post-session group activities that formed part of the intervention context. Engagement has been shown to correlate positively with affective states such as interest and pleasure, reflecting preserved capacities that can be activated through structured, person-centred activities [8]. In this study, engagement increased over time in the experimental group, and was moderately associated with both positive affect and social interaction outcomes, suggesting that active participation during DRT sessions may be an important process variable linking the intervention to observable behavioural change. The integration of personalised multimedia content, including familiar photographs, music and biographical materials, likely enhanced emotional resonance and stimulated social memory, thereby facilitating more frequent and sustained involvement in group interactions.

These findings align with those of previous trials and reviews, which have highlighted the short-term psychosocial benefits of reminiscence interventions, particularly when delivered through group formats and digital platforms [6, 25, 26]. Digital formats provide key advantages, including enhanced accessibility for individuals with motor or verbal limitations, customizable content that can adapt to different cultural and personal histories, and visual-auditory stimulation that may sustain attention more effectively than conventional materials [10]. Moreover, group-based interventions appear to amplify the therapeutic effect, as shared reminiscence promotes bonding, reduces loneliness, and encourages verbal and non-verbal communication, outcomes difficult to achieve through individual therapy alone [27, 28].

Importantly, our study was conducted entirely by nursing staff in long-term care facilities, underscoring the feasibility of implementing structured digital interventions within standard care routines. Nurses, due to their continuous presence and close relationship with residents, are ideally positioned to facilitate these interventions, monitor responses, and adapt sessions according to individual needs and cognitive stages [14, 15], However, for such initiatives to be scalable and sustainable, institutional support is essential, including digital resources, training in reminiscence facilitation, and a shared organizational commitment to person-centered care [16].

Although no changes were detected in MMSE scores or general QoL metrics, this aligns with existing literature suggesting that such measures may be less sensitive to short-term psychosocial changes [26]. In contrast, the improvements observed in engagement, observable affect, social communication and specific interpersonal QoL-AD items support the idea that more dynamic, behaviourally anchored indicators may be more appropriate primary outcomes for this type of intervention. The fact that statistically significant changes were detected in “personal relationships” and “engagement in meaningful activities” at item level, but not in the overall QoL-AD score, also highlights the relevance of examining dimensions closely aligned with the hypothesised mechanisms of action of DRT rather than relying solely on global indices.

Taken together, the findings suggest that structured, nurse-led digital reminiscence may facilitate improvements in social and affective domains that are particularly sensitive to psychosocial approaches, rather than demonstrating broad, generalised effects across all domains of functioning [27]. The exploratory correlations and the preliminary conceptual framework derived from the observed patterns provide a descriptive representation of how engagement, affect, social interaction and perceived interpersonal environment may be interrelated within the context of DRT. However, these associations should be interpreted cautiously and viewed as hypothesis-generating rather than as evidence of causal mediation, since the study was not designed or powered to formally test complex structural pathways.

Limitations and future directions

Several limitations should be acknowledged when interpreting these results. First, the quasi-experimental, cluster-allocated design, while compatible with the organisational constraints of the participating facilities, limits causal inference and may introduce selection bias. Second, the sample was recruited from two nursing homes belonging to the same religious organisation in Italy, which may restrict the generalisability of the findings to other care models, cultural contexts or community-based settings. Third, the follow-up period was relatively short (four weeks post-intervention), so the durability of the observed improvements over longer periods remains unknown. Fourth, although external assessors were used, full blinding during the intervention sessions was not feasible due to the visible nature of the programme, which may have introduced observer bias. Fifth, the absence of an active comparison condition (for example, a non-digital reminiscence group) prevents disentangling the specific contribution of the digital component from that of reminiscence or structured group activity per se.

Future research should therefore aim to replicate these findings in larger and more diverse samples, ideally using randomised or stepped-wedge designs and including active control conditions. Longer follow-up periods would help determine the extent to which gains in engagement, affect and social interaction can be maintained over time and whether they translate into downstream benefits in broader quality of life or functional outcomes. In addition, formally testing the preliminary conceptual framework through mediation or path analyses could clarify the mechanisms linking engagement during DRT sessions to emotional and social outcomes, and identify which resident characteristics or implementation factors moderate intervention effects. Finally, studies exploring staff-related outcomes, such as perceived workload, burnout and digital competence, would contribute to understanding how nurse-led DRT can be integrated sustainably into routine dementia care.

Conclusions

In this quasi-experimental study, participation in a personalised digital reminiscence programme was associated with improvements in engagement, observable positive affect and social interaction in nursing home residents with dementia. While no changes were detected in global cognition or overall quality of life, the findings highlight social and affective domains as particularly responsive to this type of nurse-led psychosocial intervention.

From a nursing perspective, the results support the feasibility of integrating structured, digitally supported reminiscence into routine care in long-term care facilities. However, confirmation of these effects in larger, more diverse samples and in studies with stronger designs is needed before firm conclusions can be drawn about the effectiveness and generalisability of digital reminiscence therapy in dementia care.
