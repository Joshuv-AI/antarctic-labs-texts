Introduction/background

Every pregnant woman faces the risk of sudden, unpredictable complications that could end in maternal death or injury to herself or to the infant, which are related to obstetric danger signs. According to WHO (2024) [1], maternal mortality is unacceptably high, with 287,000 deaths during pregnancy and childbirth globally in 2020. Almost 95% of all maternal deaths occurred in low and middle-income countries in 2020, and most could have been prevented if ODS had been identified and managed early [1]. According to the Sustainable Development Goals, the national department of health policy in South Africa aims to reduce the rates of maternal, perinatal, and neonatal morbidity and mortality by 50% by 2030 [2]. According to Bolanko, Namo, Minsamo, Addisu, and Gebre (2021) [3], in 2015, complications related to pregnancy and childbirth attributed to obstetric danger signs contributed to the death of 830 women daily throughout the world.

Obstetric Danger Signs (ODS) are warning signs that women encounter during pregnancy [4]. ODS are not reliably predictable, but knowing these early warning signs is very important for pregnant women to eliminate serious complications and start treatment immediately [5]. Known symptoms during pregnancy are high fever, severe per vaginal (PV) bleeding, swollen hands/face, blurred vision, persistent headache, decreased fetal movement, pain/burning sensation when urinating, convulsions, foul-smelling, discolored PV discharge, loss of consciousness, severe weakness, leaking fluids PV [6]. Recognizing these ODS during pregnancy can reduce maternal morbidity and mortality rates if women and their families quickly seek health care services.

According to a 2022 study conducted in Nigeria by Nkecho, Okeoghene, and Vincent [7], more than half of the pregnant women had unfavorable opinions regarding ODS. Similarly, an Ethiopian study found that women’s attitude towards ODS is not up to the same level as what is expected of them to maintain a healthy pregnancy and delivery [8]. About the practices pregnant women take to lessen their risk of ODS, an Egyptian study found that most pregnant women who suffered ODS took care of themselves before talking to a friend or family, consulting a traditional healer, or at the very least visiting a hospital [9]. Another study claims that women in the Democratic Republic of Congo (DRC) delay receiving the required emergency obstetric care due to poor counseling and ignorance of ODS, increasing the risk of maternal death [10].

WHO (2013) [11] recommends that health services collaborate with women, their families, and the broader community to provide them with appropriate and comprehensible information on obstetric danger signs during pregnancy. This information is taught to pregnant women during antenatal care, as they can develop complications, and to ensure that all are aware of where to seek care in the case of an emergency. In South Africa, the National Integrated Maternal and Perinatal Care guidelines state that all pregnant women receive crucial information on danger signs during pregnancy, both verbally and in writing (NDoH, 2024) [12]. Currently, with each visit, healthcare professionals educate pregnant women about the obstetric danger signs (ODS). In addition, all pregnant women who visit the health care facility are provided with a maternity case record (MCR), which includes ODS education.

Despite having attended these prenatal visits, we have observed during community service from February 2024 to date, with this applying to the previous years’ statistics, making it a trend, that about 100 pregnant women return to the healthcare facility with complicated cases, with some leading to maternal death. Preeclampsia, Uterine Rupture, Intrauterine Fetal Death (IUFD), and Birth Before Arrival (BBA) with retained placentas are only a few of the complications to mention. According to WHO (2024) [1], the global maternal mortality ratio (MMR) decreased by 34% between 2000 and 2020, from 451,000 to 287,000. Regardless of this fact, the number is still unacceptably high, as ODS accounts for 75% of all maternal deaths [1]. This study will be conducted to assess the knowledge, attitudes, and practices of pregnant women towards obstetric danger signs in a selected hospital in Gauteng province, South Africa.

Hypothesis

A hypothesis is defined as an official declaration of the predicted relationship between two or more variables in a given population [14]. In this study, the researcher assumes that there is a significant relationship between pregnant women’s knowledge, attitudes, and practices towards ODS in a selected hospital in South Africa.

Research methodology

According to Heever (2024) [15], research methodology refers to the precise strategy or procedures used to locate, select, process, and analyze information concerning research issues. The research methodology followed in this study is described below.

Research approach and designs

This study will employ a quantitative research approach, where numerical data on the knowledge, attitudes, and practices of pregnant women regarding ODS will be collected. In addition, this study will employ a non-experimental, descriptive cross-sectional research design, where participants will describe their knowledge, practices, and attitudes toward obstetric danger signs. Data will be collected through a single-point-in-time survey. The survey will be done on paper.

Study setting

The location where the research is conducted is referred to as the setting [13]. This study will be conducted in South Africa at a regional hospital in a selected District in the Gauteng province. This hospital’s maternity units consist of an antenatal clinic, antenatal ward, labour ward, postnatal ward, neonatal ward, and a Neonatal Intensive Care Unit (NICU). Services like ANC, Caesarean Section, vaginal deliveries, management of pregnancy-related conditions, post-natal care, and neonatal care are offered in this hospital. It also receives referrals for high-risk pregnant women from about 15 nearby clinics. The maternity units have about 65 midwives. Approximately 150 pregnant women are seen daily. It was chosen for this study because the researcher noticed this problem while serving in its maternity units as part of their community service.

Population

The population is a whole group of individuals or a particular kind of element that embodies the research’s focus [13]. The target population for this study will be all pregnant women attending antenatal care (ANC) visits at a selected hospital Antenatal Clinic who are booked and have a gestational age of 20 weeks or less. The researcher chose this population because women who have attended ANC have been exposed to ODS education at the facility, as per the Basic Antenatal Care Plus (BANC+) protocol, and have been provided with a Maternity Case Record (MCR), which contains written information about ODS. According to recent hospital statistics, 370 pregnant women who fall under this category were attended to at TMH ANC in the previous month, making up the population for this study (TMH stats, 2025).

Sampling

A sample is a portion of the available population that the researcher selects to participate in a study [16]. In this study, the researcher will choose a sample of pregnant women attending a designated hospital. Pregnant women who booked before 20 weeks of gestation. Despite being a quantitative study, the researcher will employ convenience sampling, a non-probability sampling method. This is mainly because the participation of pregnant women will be contingent upon their availability and access to the clinic on the days of data collection, as well as their willingness to participate.

Inclusion

Inclusion sampling criteria refer to the qualities that an item or subject must possess to be included in the target population [14]. The study will cover pregnant women who attend antenatal care at a selected hospital and have booked for Antenatal Care (ANC) before 20 weeks of gestation, and who will give consent to participate.

Exclusion

The qualities that can lead to a person or thing being omitted from the target population are known as exclusion sampling criteria [14]. All pregnant women who booked before 20 weeks and are in labor or severely ill will be excluded from the study.

Pilot study

According to Polit and Beck (2010) [16], a pilot study is an experimental test or small-scale version conducted in advance of a larger investigation. In this study, the researcher will conduct a pilot study in preparation for the main study at the nearby Boksburg North Clinic. This clinic also renders antenatal care to pregnant women. 10% of the total sample size of the main study of pregnant women will be used as a sample for the pilot study. This will be 10% of 192 pregnant women, which is 19. The researcher will use convenience sampling, a non-probability sampling method, despite the study being quantitative, because data collection will be determined by the availability of pregnant women and their willingness to participate on the designated collection days. The primary setting in which the study will be conducted will not be part of the pilot study, and the sample used to perform the pilot study will not be used to participate in the main study. The researcher is conducting this pilot study to refine the questions, structure, and scales, which will also help confirm the study’s validity. The participants in the pilot study will be 19 pregnant women who will be asked to complete questionnaires by hand.

Data collection

Data collection is a systematic process of gathering information from various sources to address research questions, test hypotheses, and evaluate results [17]. It entails selecting the most effective approach to collect relevant data for a specific study [17]. For research conclusions to be legitimate and credible, proper data gathering is crucial [17]. Data collection for this study is scheduled to begin in January 2026 and end in February 2026, after securing all necessary permissions from relevant authorities. Before starting data collection, the researcher will obtain formal approval from the National Department of Health and the respective hospital management to ensure compliance with institutional and national ethical standards. This preparatory phase will ensure that all procedural and ethical requirements are met before data gathering starts. The study will remain in its pre-data-collection phase until January 2026, when data collection officially begins and is expected to be completed by the end of February 2026.

The researcher will employ self-administered questionnaires with brief, straightforward, and closed-ended questions to collect data from the pregnant women. Based on the literature review, the researcher created a questionnaire with four sections (Appendix D). Section A includes biographical data, Section B inquiries about knowledge of ODS, Section C assesses attitudes toward ODS, and Section D examines practices among pregnant women. To gather data, the researcher will go to a selected hospital on prearranged days. The study’s goal will be reinforced during the recruitment process. This recruitment process will take place at the hospital. On days of antenatal visits, pregnant mothers will be approached, and consent will be obtained to participate. Participants will take approximately 20 min to complete the questionnaire. Pregnant women will choose by ticking or circling the answer that’s correct to them from the multiple-choice questions, or yes or no from the questionnaire. Since IsiZulu is the native language of Gauteng province, it will be used to translate the English questionnaire, which will serve as the basis for the study’s questions. Therefore, the language used to ask questions of pregnant women will be Isizulu. The researcher will help to clarify any questions that the subject does not understand. To ensure high-quality data collection, the researcher will maintain participant privacy by remaining in another room when participants feel uncomfortable with the researcher being present while they answer questions. The estimated timeframe for data collection is four months.

Data analysis

Data analysis, according to Hassan (2022) [17], is the methodical process of examining, purifying, converting, and modelling data to find significant insights, aid in decision-making, and address issues to analyse the collected data. This researcher will employ the descriptive statistical method. This method summarizes data using measures such as the mean, median, and standard deviation. If a pregnant woman receives a mean or above average score on the ODS questionnaire, she will be deemed to have good knowledge, attitude, and practices of the ODS during pregnancy. Additionally, if a pregnant woman receives a score below the mean on the questionnaire, she will be deemed to have poor knowledge, attitude, and practices. Each KAP component will be analyzed in this manner. In close collaboration with the statistician, the researcher will tabulate data using Microsoft Excel and analyse it with the Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS) version 29 for cross-tabulations. For data to be understandable, it will also be graphically shown as frequencies, percentages, tables, and figures. Regression analysis will be employed to examine and evaluate the relationship between pregnant women’s knowledge, attitudes, and practices regarding ODS. The information will be kept on password-protected computers and USB drives.

Validity and reliability

Allude to methods employed in data collecting and analysis that support the truthfulness, accuracy, and consistency of the conclusions and conclusions reached at the end of a study [18]. These approaches encompass sampling methods, pilot studies, and data analysis techniques.

Face validity

Face validity will be ensured in this study by providing the questionnaire to experts in the maternal health field to measure its validity before its use.

Content validity

To ensure content validity, the research instrument was developed using literature and consultation with specialists in maternal healthcare services to ensure that it covers the necessary topics.

Reliability

To ensure reliability, concepts will be discussed logically and clearly to avoid ambiguous and perplexing statements at the beginning of the questionnaire. Methods and instruments used throughout the study will remain the same. With precise and thorough documentation of data, the researcher will maintain consistency across investigations and guarantee reliability.

Bias

Any factor or activity that distorts the results of a study away from what is true or anticipated. A misrepresentation. It can also be used to describe a viewpoint that deviates from the objective reality [14]. To avoid cognitive bias, the researcher will not favor particular results or interpret data in a way that supports their preexisting notions. The researcher will ensure that participants’ answers accurately reflect their knowledge, opinions, or practices using the tool and will also prevent them from feeling intimidated, thereby minimizing bias. The researchers will not share their views on the study topic and will refrain from assisting pregnant women in completing the questionnaires. However, they will ensure that the concepts are clearly outlined so that respondents understand, thereby minimizing information bias. The researcher will be able to prevent sampling bias in the sample by using the Taro Yamane formula to construct the sample from the population obtained from earlier statistics on pregnant women.

Participants will be given copies of the questionnaire to fill out. The researcher will not distribute the questionnaire if the participants are unwilling to participate.

Before gathering data, the researcher will obtain participants' consent, allowing each person to decide whether to participate in the study. To ensure that consent is obtained, participants will be provided with a form to complete before participating in this study. The forms will be kept in a safe, locked location as evidence.

Confidentiality

The researcher's handling of a subject's private information, which cannot be disclosed to third parties without the subject's consent, is known as confidentiality [13]. By ensuring that the information participants provide will not be shared with anyone outside the research project, the researcher will maintain confidentiality. Except for the researcher, no one else will have access to the data that has been gathered. Data will be stored on a laptop with an encrypted file, and raw data will be stored safely in a locked and secure cupboard.

Anonymity

Anonymity implies that neither the researcher nor the subject can determine the other's identity based on their specific answers [13]. To maintain participant anonymity, the researcher will use codes, numbers, or letters instead of names. Additionally, the researcher will refrain from identifying the participants' conditions or states, including any deformities or clothing that may be related to the information they provide on surveys.

Privacy

The right to privacy refers to a person's ability to choose when, how much, and under what general circumstances personal information is disclosed to or kept private. One's attitudes, beliefs, behaviors, opinions, and records make up this information [13]. The study will protect participants' privacy by ensuring they are in a closed room to complete the questionnaires they will be given for the study. The room should be free from distractions, allowing participants to provide answers without fear of their responses being used against them later. Any personal property, including participant files, should not be used without permission, such as the use of videos or tape recordings, without their knowledge or consent.

Justice

According to the ethical notion, justice refers to the idea that human subjects should be treated equally, both as individuals and as groups [13]. In this study, the researcher will ensure that the ethical duty to distribute the benefits and burdens of the study project is fulfilled reasonably. The researcher will ensure that the methods are fair and equal; therefore, the researcher will use convenience sampling, a non-probability sampling method based on the availability of pregnant women on the collection date.

Beneficence

A fundamental ethical principle that seeks to minimize harm and maximize benefits for research participants [16]. The investigator will ensure that the questionnaires don't include any questions that could compromise their dignity. Through one-on-one meetings in a private setting, the researcher will ensure that any participants who have been traumatized by responding to the research questionnaire receive counseling. Additionally, data collection will be communicated to a psychologist so that they are prepared to accept individuals who may experience distress.

Autonomy

Acknowledging the right to self-determination and decision-making of every individual [19]. In this study, the researcher will ensure that participants make informed decisions about whether they want to participate or not. They will also be free to make their own decisions about whether to continue participating or stop without any fear of facing punishment.

Non-maleficence and risk of harm

To be non-maleficent is not to harm [19]. Pregnant women will be protected from harm by the researcher maintaining their name and privacy; those who require counselling, the researcher will stop the data collection process and refer them to the psychologists who will be prepared before the data collection process.

Theoretical framework

The theoretical framework is a conceptual and logical structure of meaning that guides a study’s development, enabling the researcher to connect the results to the existing body of nursing knowledge [13]. The Model guiding this study is described below.

The knowledge, attitudes, and practices (KAP) theoretical model

In this study, the researcher will apply the KAP (Knowledge, Attitudes, and Practices) theory model. American Everett M. Rodgers proposed the KAP theory model in the late 1950 s and early 1960s. It is a behavioral intervention theory that can effectively change patients’ bad practices [20]. The KAP theory includes three stages: acquiring knowledge, building attitude, and constructing practices [20]. This theory is based on knowledge and driven by individuals’ attitudes, ultimately achieving practice change [20].

Acquiring knowledge

According to Kang and Bagaoisan (2024), knowledge refers to the information, understanding, and skills that people acquire through education and the accumulation of experience. Rodger’s KAP theory model states that the acquisition and accumulation of knowledge are essential for people to develop positive attitudes and thoughts [20]. The accumulation of information in this research study relates to pregnant women's understanding of ODS, which is crucial for cultivating a positive attitude that can help reduce obstetric issues, such as maternal mortality.

Building attitude

Roger (1960) defined the motivation behind practice change as being caused by an attitude that is founded on the knowledge the person has internalized and comprehended, creating a solid and enduring psychological state. In this study, the attitude towards ODS will be determined by the knowledge of pregnant mothers regarding ODS, which will, in turn, lead to good practices concerning ODS, thereby reducing obstetric complications.

Constructing practice

Rodger's 1960 practice, which is directly impacted and controlled by knowledge and attitude, is the acts and responses of people taken in specific contexts and circumstances. In this study, the practices of pregnant women towards ODS during pregnancy are influenced by their knowledge and attitudes towards ODS. In this study, the pregnant women’s practices towards danger signs in pregnancy are influenced by their knowledge and attitudes towards these ODS, which in turn result in obstetric complications.

Discussion

The study focuses on assessing the knowledge, attitudes, and practices (KAP) of pregnant women regarding obstetric danger signs (ODS) in a selected hospital in South Africa. The study addresses a critical public health concern, as ODS contribute significantly to maternal morbidity and mortality globally, particularly in low- and middle-income countries. The application of the KAP theoretical model enhances the study’s conceptual framework by demonstrating how knowledge can influence attitudes and, subsequently, practices. This approach is suitable given the study’s objectives and its behavioural focus.

Methodologically, the study employs a quantitative, cross-sectional, descriptive design, with a sample size of 192 calculated using the Taro Yamane formula. The use of a pilot study and translation of the questionnaire into the local language (isiZulu) are strengths that promote validity and reliability. The data collection strategy, using a self-administered questionnaire divided into four logical sections, aligns well with the research objectives. Ethical considerations are robust and well-detailed, addressing informed consent, privacy, anonymity, and participant well-being, which reflects a high standard of research ethics. The anticipated outcomes of this study have important implications for education, clinical practice, research, and policy. Findings may inform curriculum development for midwives, improve the quality of antenatal education, and support evidence-based policymaking to address maternal health challenges in South Africa.

Conclusions

This study aims to assess the knowledge, attitudes, and practices of pregnant women towards ODS in a selected hospital in Gauteng province, South Africa. The findings of this study may contribute to the existing body of knowledge on maternal health and provide valuable insights for healthcare providers, policymakers, and stakeholders to develop targeted interventions that reduce maternal morbidity and mortality. By identifying gaps in knowledge, attitudes, and practices among pregnant women, this study may inform the development of effective health education programs to promote maternal health and well-being. The study may help improve the outcomes for pregnant women and their unborn babies, contributing to the Sustainable Development Goal of reducing the maternal mortality ratio to 70 per 100,000 live births by 2030.
