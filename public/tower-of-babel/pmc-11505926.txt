Traumatic durotomy and entrapment of neural elements in a lumbar burst and laminar fracture diagnosed on preoperative imaging: illustrative case
Authors: Ian J Jarin, Yigal Samocha, John K Houten, Merritt D Kinon
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBACKGROUNDTraumatic dural lacerations can be caused by thoracolumbar burst fractures and, infrequently, can be associated with the entrapment of neural elements within a laminar fracture. The diagnosis of both is difficult to make on preoperative imaging, as the conditions are typically appreciated during surgical exploration. The authors present a case of traumatic durotomy with entrapment of neural elements in a laminar fracture that they believed could be appreciated on preoperative magnetic resonance imaging (MRI).OBSERVATIONSA young male involved in a motor vehicle collision presented to the authors’ trauma center with a lumbar burst and laminar fracture. Preoperative MRI demonstrated epidural hemorrhage and entrapment of neural elements within the laminar fracture, which was confirmed intraoperatively. The patient underwent a successful decompression, release of nerve roots, fusion, and recovery.LESSONSTraumatic durotomy and entrapment of neural elements can occur after a traumatic spinal fracture, and a diagnosis made upon preoperative MRI can allow for effective preoperative planning. Clinicians should have a high index of suspicion for these pathologies when encountering certain fracture patterns and could therefore tailor the surgical approach to avoid further neurological injury during surgery. https://thejns.org/doi/10.3171/CASE24455 Keywords: traumatic durotomy, dural tear, nerve root, neural element entrapment, burst fracture, laminar fractureABBREVIATIONS: CSF = cerebrospinal fluid, CT = computed tomography, MRI = magnetic resonance imaging.

Traumatic lacerations of the dura can occur following high-energy spinal injuries and have previously been documented in association with thoracolumbar burst fractures and lamina fractures.1–4 Significant sequelae can occur following traumatic dural tears, including infection, persistent cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) leakage, pseudomeningocele, and tonsillar cerebellar herniation.2, 5 The presence of neurological deficits in patients with traumatic dural tears has been reported in up to 83% of cases, with 48% of patients having a complete neurological injury.2

A rare phenomenon resulting from traumatic dural tears is the herniation of roots and entrapment within bony fracture clefts.1, 6–8 Imaging findings predictive of dural tears in patients with spinal burst fractures include increased interpedicular distance, laminar fracture, decreased central canal diameter, and acute angle of a retropulsed segment.9 Occasionally, entrapment of neural elements can also be identified on preoperative imaging, but reports of this are rare.7 Recognition of this phenomenon is crucial, as the entrapped structure could be further injured during inadvertent surgical manipulation. The purpose of this study was to review the literature and to demonstrate the clinical course of a patient with a traumatic dural tear and nerve root entrapment identified preoperatively.

Illustrative Case

Clinical Presentation and Imaging

A 20-year-old male presented to the emergency department after a motor vehicle collision, with severe low-back pain, bilateral hip flexion, knee extension weakness, and decreased sensation in the right calf, in addition to multiple facial injuries. Computed tomography (CT) revealed an L3 burst fracture with significant retropulsion and resulting spinal stenosis, as well as a vertically oriented split fracture of the lamina (Fig. 1A and B). Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) revealed additional epidural hemorrhage and a possible dural tear with herniation and entrapment of nerve roots posteriorly into the vertical laminar fracture (Fig. 1C and D). Given that this was an unstable spine injury with neurological deficits, decompressive lumbar laminectomies from L2 to L4, evacuation of the compressive epidural hemorrhage, repair of the traumatic dural laceration, and long-segment stabilization from L1 to L5 were recommended.

B: Preoperative axial CT demonstrating an L3 burst fracture with an associated vertical fracture through the right lamina and spinous process. C: Preoperative sagittal T2-weighted MRI demonstrating significant central stenosis and cord compression at the L3 level with epidural hemorrhage. D: Preoperative axial T2-weighted MRI demonstrating entrapment of neural elements within the L3 vertical laminar fracture (red circle).

Surgery

The patient was positioned prone, and a midline incision with standard subperiosteal posterior dissection was performed. Once the bony anatomy was defined, lumbar pedicle screws were placed bilaterally from L1 to L5 using stereotactic navigation. Decompressive lumbar laminectomies from L2 to L4 were accomplished using a combination of a Leksell rongeur, Kerrison rongeur, and high-speed operative drill. Special care and attention were taken while performing the L3 laminectomy. Once the cranial portion of the L3 spinous process was removed, we readily encountered the vertical laminar fracture with visible entrapped nerve roots. The exposed entrapped nerve roots were protected with a cottonoid while we thinned the lamina with the high-speed operative drill. The majority of the L3 lamina was removed with a Kerrison rongeur, leaving a thin rim of bone around the trapped nerve roots. Using microdissectors, the trapped nerve roots were freed from the bone, and the remaining lamina was removed. We encountered a large, linear, midline traumatic durotomy that spanned from L2 to L4, approximately 6 cm in length (Fig. 2A). The epidural and subdural spaces were copiously irrigated, and any traumatic hemorrhage was removed. The cauda equina nerve roots were then reduced to the thecal sac, and the durotomy was closed primarily with a running 4-0 Nurolon suture (Fig. 2B). A Valsalva maneuver revealed a watertight closure.

A: After the laminectomy, the entrapped neural elements were released and returned to the thecal sac. An approximately 6-cm longitudinal traumatic durotomy is visualized. The nerve roots were returned to the thecal sac. B: Primary repair of the durotomy. The Valsalva maneuver resulted in no extravasation of CSF.

Postoperative Course

Postoperatively, modest improvement was noted in both sensation and motor function. No postoperative spinal fluid leak was encountered. The patient was discharged home on postoperative day 6. At the initial 3-week follow-up, the patient had minimal pain and was ambulating with a walker. At the last follow-up at 7 weeks, the patient was doing extremely well with only modest right iliopsoas weakness.

Discussion

Observations

Traumatic dural lacerations can occur following a traumatic injury to the lower spine.2–4, 10, 11 Traumatic dural tears can have significant, albeit rare, sequelae and morbidity, including infection, meningitis, persistent CSF leakage, and pseudomeningocele.2 A less common phenomenon with traumatic spinal injury is entrapment of neural elements. Associated fractures of the lamina have also been recognized as a risk factor for both a dural injury and entrapment of neural elements.1, 8, 10 Kareem et al. reported a series of patients with entrapped neural elements in lumbar burst fractures.8 Shankar et al. described a case of lumbar nerve root entrapment within the spinous process.7 While certain characteristics on MRI can raise suspicion for a dural tear, diagnosis is usually confirmed by direct visualization during surgery.9 Lee et al. found different MRI findings more likely to be associated with dural tear in a series of burst fractures, including high-grade laminar fracture, increased interpedicular distance, decreased central canal ratio, and more acute angle of the retropulsed fragment.9 With regard to entrapment of neural elements, the vast majority of cases are diagnosed during surgery but occasionally can be seen on preoperative MRI.8 Without thoughtful recognition of and forewarning from preoperative imaging, however, an unintended injury to the entrapped neural elements can occur during surgery. We report a case in which nerve root entrapment within a laminar fracture was detected on MRI, and we believe that foreknowledge of the potential entrapment aided in achieving a successful patient outcome.

Lessons

We describe a case in which traumatic dural laceration with entrapment of neural elements was diagnosed on preoperative imaging, and successful surgical treatment resulted in neurological improvement. Understanding the significance of certain findings on imaging can aid in the safe execution of surgical decompression, release of entrapped roots, primary dural repair, fusion, and avoidance of an intraoperative nerve injury. Thus, spine surgeons should be suspicious of dural laceration and entrapment of neural elements in thoracolumbar burst and laminar fractures, and a particularly close examination of preoperative MRI is advisable.

The limitations of this study are intrinsic to its nature. We only demonstrated one case in which preoperative diagnosis of entrapped neural elements and dural tears occurred, limiting the generalizability of the lessons.

In conclusion, high-energy thoracolumbar burst and laminar fractures should prompt a close examination of MRI for associated dural tears and entrapment of neural elements. Preoperative recognition of these pathologies is highly valuable, as it aids in performing a successful surgery and producing a favorable outcome for the patient.
