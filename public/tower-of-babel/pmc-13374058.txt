Association between stock market returns and outpatient visits for hypertension: evidence from a large city in East China
Authors: Shibao Wang, Minghua Ye, Siqi Zhong, Xiaojun Shao, Tongjiang Wang, Bei Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAs more household assets are allocated to the stock market, the health externalities of stock market returns have become a focal point of interdisciplinary research. However, prior evidence has largely focused on medium to long term associations with healthcare-seeking activities, leaving the immediate healthcare seeking response in emerging economies underexplored. Leveraging data on both regular outpatient visits for hypertension (ROV) and major illness outpatient visits for hypertension (MOV), this study examines the immediate association between stock market returns and such visits.MethodsThe sample for ROV and MOV is constructed from 30,715,658 regular outpatient records and 94,370 major illness outpatient records from a major city in East China. Background

Hypertension is a major, modifiable risk factor that contributes substantially to heart attack worldwide [1]. Economic losses attributable to cardiovascular diseases, including hypertension, are projected to reach approximately US$3.7 trillion across low- and middle-income countries between 2011 and 2025, posing a major challenge to the resilience of national public health systems and sustainable economic development worldwide [2]. Given the substantial burden that hypertension places on the healthcare system, further investigations into risk factors are urgently needed. Research on hypertension has focused primarily on biomedical determinants such as genetics, endocrine regulation, and lifestyle factors [3]. Traditional risk factors such as high-sodium and low-potassium diets, obesity, excessive alcohol consumption, and physical inactivity also explain the regional heterogeneity in hypertension prevalence [4].

Moreover, the instantaneous effects and cumulative impacts of external environmental stress on blood pressure have received growing academic attention in recent years [5]. For example, a growing number of studies have investigated the link between financial markets and hypertension globally [6, 7]. Evidence suggests that a 10% loss in wealth can reduce physical and mental health indicators by approximately 2–3 standard deviations, with the strongest effect on hypertension and heart disease [8]. During Iceland’s financial crisis, the prevalence of hypertension rose significantly among males [9]. In China, falling stock returns have been associated with increased outpatient visits and mortality from cardiovascular diseases, with particularly strong effects on hypertensive heart disease and stroke [10]. Studies have found that negative stock returns have a stronger impact on health than positive returns, and this effect can spill over to investors’ family members [11]. Data from the Health and Retirement Study (HRS) indicate that among high‑stockholding households, a one‑standard‑deviation increase in cumulative wealth shocks over a two‑year period is associated with a 9.5% higher probability of elevated blood pressure [7] .

Specifically, regarding the transmission mechanism, existing studies have explored the impact of wealth losses on health outcomes [6–8]. Some recent research has investigated how stock market fluctuations affect individual health through two key mechanisms: the wealth effect and psychological expectations. The wealth effect pathway suggests that stock market fluctuations directly alter personal asset values, creating wealth shocks that impact health [7]. The psychological expectations pathway highlights the role of media, which reports stock indices almost daily, shaping individuals’ expectations about future income, employment, and consumption [12, 13]. Together, these pathways generate immediate psychological stress, potentially leading to elevated blood pressure and increased cardiovascular risk [14], and subsequently creating potential medical demand [6]. Importantly, because the psychological expectation effect has broad social permeability, the adverse association between stock market returns and hypertension is not confined to direct investors but may also extend to the broader population [11]. This spillover effect underscores the widespread public health relevance of financial market dynamics.

However, the existing literature has several gaps in terms of research subjects (developed economies vs. emerging economies), research perspectives (disease incidence vs. healthcare‑seeking behavior), and research methods (simple correlation or linear models vs. nonlinear modeling using large‑scale hospital visit data). First, most previous studies have focused on economically developed regions, utilizing data from California [6] and Iceland [9], while fewer studies have examined the relationship between stock markets and hypertension in emerging economies. Second, previous studies have primarily focused on the relationship between financial markets or stock markets and the incidence of hypertension, i.e., the finance‑disease link [7, 14], with less attention paid to the relationship between stock markets and healthcare‑seeking behavior from a behavioral finance perspective. Finally, most existing studies have relied on simple correlation analyses or linear models. For example, Agarwal et al. [39] analyzed the associations between stock returns, alcohol consumption, and hypertension, but their analysis was largely correlation‑based, with limited use of large‑scale hospital visit data and nonlinear modeling.

To help fill these gaps, the Chinese context offers a particularly valuable setting for several reasons. Driven by persistent trade conflicts and geopolitical shocks, global financial markets have exhibited increased fluctuations in recent years [15, 16], with emerging economies such as China being especially vulnerable to such instability [17]. In China, approximately 29% of the 270 million adults aged 30–79 years are hypertensive, placing China among the countries with the heaviest hypertension burden worldwide. Moreover, there are approximately 240 million individual investors in the A-share market, who hold only 20–25% of the market capitalization but account for 80% of the trading volume [18]. This unique investor structure, combined with rapid population aging and increasing financialization of household asset allocation, makes middle-aged and older adults key participants in the stock market. Their health vulnerability, coupled with high sensitivity to stock market returns, renders them particularly susceptible to health impacts.

Behavioral finance research provides theoretical grounds for understanding why Chinese retail investors may respond so strongly to market downturns. First, due to loss aversion, individuals are far more sensitive to losses than to gains, which forms the physiological basis for psychological stress [19]. This, in behavioral terms, manifests as a pronounced disposition effect: investors are reluctant to realize paper losses and therefore tend to hold high-risk assets and avoid stop-losses [20]. Second, mental accounting leads investors to treat stock market gains and losses in a separate account, so that a sharp contraction in that account cannot be psychologically hedged by other assets [21]. In China’s fragmented, retail-dominated market, investors exhibit strong gambling preferences [22]. Such speculative motives not only amplify extreme price fluctuations but also significantly increase systemic crash risk [23, 24]. Under this fragile microstructure, the rapid spread of irrational sentiment further magnifies extreme downside risk [24] and deeply shapes the disposition effect [25]. Studies have shown that China’s disposition effect follows a unique “asymmetric V-shaped” pattern and is significantly negatively correlated with investor sentiment [26, 27]. This dynamic asymmetry further intensifies investors’ psychological stress under loss pressure. Figure 1 illustrates this pattern, providing visual motivation for our empirical analysis.

Together, these behavioral and institutional features make China a valuable setting for examining the immediate health impacts of stock market fluctuations, particularly through the lens of psychological stress and healthcare-seeking behavior.

To translate these theoretical insights into empirical analysis, we pursue three objectives. First, we test whether there is an immediate (same‑day) association between stock market returns and outpatient visits for hypertension using daily and monthly data on ROV and MOV. Second, we employ a regression discontinuity in time (RDiT) design and other robustness checks to mitigate endogeneity concerns and strengthen the credibility of the association. Third, we examine how individual characteristics (age, baseline health) and environmental factors (cold temperatures) moderate the association.

This study differs from previous research in several important aspects. First, instead of focusing on hospitalization or mortality (which are relatively rare events), we examine outpatient visits for hypertension—a more immediate and sensitive indicator of health deterioration. Second, we leverage a large‑scale database with over 30 million regular outpatient records and nearly 100,000 major‑illness records, providing daily frequency and finer granularity than previous studies. Third, we employ a multi‑method approach that combines baseline regressions, wavelet coherence analysis, a regression discontinuity in time (RDiT) design, and placebo tests, thereby strengthening the credibility of our estimates. Fourth, we separately model regular outpatient visits (ROV) and major‑illness outpatient visits (MOV), revealing heterogeneity in health responses by disease severity.

Three key findings emerge from our analysis. First, stock market declines are associated with an immediate (same‑day) increase in outpatient visits for hypertension. Second, this association is stronger among older adults and those with poorer baseline health. Third, cold weather amplifies the negative association between market downturns and major‑illness outpatient visits.

The remainder of this paper is structured as follows. Section 2 describes the data sources and the empirical strategy. Section 3 presents the main results, focusing on the immediate daily association and the monthly cumulative association between stock market returns and outpatient visits for hypertension, as well as the potential role of temperature. Section 4 discusses the key findings and their implications, along with limitations and future research. Section 5 concludes with a brief summary.

Methods

Data sources

The data used in this study were obtained from the social medical insurance database of a major city located in East China, the most economically developed area in China where residents exhibit active involvement in financial market transactions. Due to data use agreements, the city name must remain anonymous. Moreover, the region’s well-established public health system ensures standardized outpatient records for chronic diseases such as hypertension. As of 2018, the walk‑in option for both ROV and MOV, together with immediate digital recordings in the medical insurance system, further ensures that visit timing reflects real‑time health responses rather than administrative delays or periodic institutional cycles. These conditions enhance the reliability of analyzing the temporal association between stock market returns and outpatient visits for hypertension. They consist of two main datasets:

Outpatient visit records: This dataset includes detailed records of regular outpatient visits, including admission and discharge dates, disease diagnosis, and partially anonymized personal identification numbers (first 15 digits). The data cover January 2016 to October 2018, totaling 30,715,658 records, of which 481,225 are identified as hypertension cases. Gender information is not available, precluding sex-based analyses.

Outpatient visit records: This dataset includes detailed records of regular outpatient visits, including admission and discharge dates, disease diagnosis, and partially anonymized personal identification numbers (first 15 digits). The data cover January 2016 to October 2018, totaling 30,715,658 records, of which 481,225 are identified as hypertension cases. Gender information is not available, precluding sex-based analyses.

Major-illness outpatient database: This dataset contains records of major-illness outpatient visits, including two variables: the start date of treatment and the classification of visits. The data cover October 2000 to September 2018, with 94,370 records, representing 26,476 patients diagnosed with stage II or III hypertension.

Major-illness outpatient database: This dataset contains records of major-illness outpatient visits, including two variables: the start date of treatment and the classification of visits. The data cover October 2000 to September 2018, with 94,370 records, representing 26,476 patients diagnosed with stage II or III hypertension.

In China’s public healthcare system, regular outpatient visits for hypertension (ROV) are covered by basic insurance and primarily manage stage I and II hypertension, with patients able to drop in at designated insurance hospitals at various levels without an appointment as of 2018. In contrast, major-illness outpatient visits (MOV) target severe or chronic conditions (stage II and III hypertension), receive higher reimbursement rates, and also allow on-site queueing for consultations.

To accommodate different analytical needs, two temporal frequencies were employed: (a) daily data, which encompass ROV (January 2016–October 2018) and MOV (February 2011–May 2018). Daily data for MOV were systematically recorded from mid-January 2011; hence, the period from February 2011 to May 2018 was selected for analysis to ensure data completeness. (b) monthly data for MOV covers the period from October 2000 to September 2018.

During this period, the daily returns of the Shanghai Composite Index (SSECI) ranged from − 7.04% to 4.26%. In contrast, the median daily number of MOV was 6, with a maximum of 446; during this sub-period, the SSECI returns fluctuated between − 8.49% and 5.76%. On a monthly basis, the mean MOV was 126, with a maximum of 1,906, while the monthly stock market returns ranged from − 1.37% to 1.18%. Research design

This study employs a multi‑pronged strategy to examine the association between stock market returns and outpatient visits for hypertension. First, high‑dimensional fixed effects regressions are used to control for confounding factors. Second, a regression discontinuity in time (RDiT) design is adopted to further account for omitted variable bias and strengthen the credibility of the association (see Appendix A2.2). Third, heterogeneity analysis using mixed models with interaction terms (see Appendix A2.5), together with robustness checks employing Poisson and negative binomial regressions for daily MOV (see Appendix A2.6), are performed to further validate the main findings.

To examine the association between stock market returns and outpatient visits for hypertension, our empirical strategy extends the linear framework of Engelberg and Parsons [6] in several key dimensions. First, we incorporate a multilag operator (τ) to analyze temporal dynamics. Second, we introduce week, month, and year fixed effects to control for cyclical variations. To account for heterogeneity in medical data, we stratify individuals by health status using their hospitalization records. Results

We first describe the association between daily stock market returns and regular outpatient visits (ROV), then present the daily results for major-illness outpatient visits (MOV), and finally report the monthly analysis for MOV; the monthly results for ROV were not presented as monthly data for ROV (January 2016–October 2018) are too limited to support a meaningful interpretation. The key findings are as follows. First, stock market declines are associated with significant increases in both ROV and MOV, with the increase in MOV concentrated during periods of negative returns. Second, the association exhibits an age gradient: older adults and those in poorer baseline health show larger increases. Third, the association is immediate (τ = 0) and not significant at any lagged period. Fourth, lower temperatures are associated with higher MOV, and the increase is particularly pronounced when winter months coincide with severe market downturns. Additionally, major-illness outpatient patients follow rigid treatment cycles and are less affected by holidays, whereas regular outpatient visits are more sensitive to holiday effects.

As shown in Table 3, Panel A indicates that the regression coefficients for positive stock market returns are positive for both the LDB and HDB groups, with a larger and statistically significant association observed among the HDB groups. In contrast, negative returns are significantly and adversely associated with ROV for both groups. The coefficient for the HDB group is -15.76, which is substantially greater in magnitude than that for the LDB group (-4.708). These findings suggest that patients in the HDB group exhibit greater sensitivity to market-decline days, consistent with their higher baseline disease burden. This pattern is clinically plausible: patients with prior hospitalization history - indicative of more advanced hypertension, target organ damage, or significant comorbidities - are more likely to experience acute exacerbations of their conditions under psychological stress induced by market downturns, which in turn leads to an increase in the volume of the ROV. Conversely, patients in the LDB group — those managed exclusively through outpatient prescriptions without prior hospitalization — appear to retain greater physiological and psychological reserve capacity, which may be associated with a weaker response to financial market stress, corresponding to a relatively smaller increase in outpatient visits. Overall, these results suggest asymmetric associations between stock market fluctuations and individual health, whereby financial stressors are more strongly associated with health‑vulnerable populations. Building on this, we further test the robustness of the preceding conclusions by incorporating the interaction term between age and disease burden (see Appendix A2.5). The results provide additional support for the robustness of our findings.

Association between daily stock market returns and MOV

Data preprocessing

During stock market crashes, MOV data exhibit pronounced abnormal variations, and daily stock returns are an inadequate proxy for capturing the health outcomes associated with such systemic risks, making it challenging to identify the overall association with market crashes. Therefore, when processing data for this period, extreme values corresponding to the crash periods were excluded to avoid the distortion of estimation results by extraordinary market events. To account for the specific patterns during market crashes, monthly aggregated data were further employed for model fitting, enabling a more comprehensive characterization of the association between stock returns and MOV during crash periods.

The boxplot of daily MOV in Fig. 2 reveals that there were pronounced outliers in outpatient visits for hypertension between December 18 and December 31, 2015. To assess whether extremely low temperatures could explain the observed anomalies, we compared the concurrent temperature data. The comparison shows that the temperatures during this period were largely consistent with those of the previous year, indicating that unusual weather conditions are unlikely to account for the observed patterns. After accounting for extreme cold, we observe that this period corresponds to the aftermath of China’s stock market “crash”, characterized by fragile market confidence and large negative returns. In particular, the Shanghai Composite Index experienced another sharp decline in December, amid investors’ anticipation of the upcoming “circuit breaker” policy, a situation that may have placed market participants under considerable uncertainty and psychological stress [10, 38, 39] Against this backdrop, the surge in acute hypertension cases in late December 2015 may partly reflect collective stress responses triggered by intense capital market turbulence.

Boxplot of MOV (original data vs. outlier-removed data) (February 1, 2011–May 31, 2018)

On December 4, 2015, the Shanghai and Shenzhen Stock Exchanges jointly announced the introduction of an index-based circuit breaker mechanism, which was officially implemented on January 1, 2016. Three days after its implementation, the A-share market triggered the circuit breaker for the first time, resulting in a trading halt. However, as the mechanism exacerbated market movements and contributed to liquidity tightening, it was suspended on January 8. Notably, abnormal outpatient visits for hypertension began to appear on December 15, 2015, coinciding closely with the heightened market uncertainty and investor panic surrounding the circuit breaker implementation (see Fig. A1 in the Appendix). Given that daily stock return data cannot effectively capture the association between such systemic risk and the volume of MOV, these extreme periods were excluded when the daily data were analyzed with models.

Controlling for month fixed effects slightly increases the coefficient to -27.64 in magnitude (which means that a 4% decrease in returns is associated with an increase of MOV by about 1 person). Finally, adding year fixed effects changes the coefficient to -26.79. This suggests that the association is partially accounted for by long-term trends, although the specific mechanisms (e.g., improvements in medical conditions) cannot be identified with the current data. As we sequentially incorporate fixed effects, the estimated coefficient of daily stock market returns remains negative and statistically significant, converging toward a stable estimate. As shown in Appendix A2.1, a comparison between the model incorporating holidays and that using an expanded range of MOV values shows a decrease in both the estimated coefficient and the R-squared. Given that excluding extreme observations during financial crises might underestimate the magnitude of the association between stock market fluctuations and residents’ health and thus lead to estimation bias, this study reports full-sample estimation results including outliers in Table A3, serving as a complement and robustness check to the baseline model. In addition, we employ Poisson and negative binomial models to perform robustness tests on the daily MOV. The results provide additional support for the aforementioned conclusions (see Appendix A2.6). To rule out routine healthcare‑seeking habits, we decomposed the outpatient series using the Hodrick‑Prescott (HP) filter. The results indicate that the observed association is in line with acute stress from stock market fluctuations rather than routine behavioral patterns (see Appendix A2.4).

Association between monthly stock market returns and MOV

Monthly evidence

Owing to high-frequency fluctuations in daily stock market returns, the data contain substantial noise. As shown in Fig. A3 (online Appendix), the daily returns of the Shanghai Composite Index exhibit larger fluctuations than monthly returns, resulting in a lower signal-to-noise ratio. Consequently, the association between market crashes and MOV cannot be robustly estimated using daily data.

To address this, we employ the monthly mean stock return as the key independent variable. This choice is motivated by both theoretical and empirical considerations. Theoretically, monthly percentage returns, rather than absolute values, are better suited to capture the persistent associations of wealth shocks with health [43] and satisfy standard stationarity requirements in financial econometrics [44]. Empirically, using a monthly window filters high‑frequency noise and better reflects the cumulative health association with sustained financial stress, consistent with the allostatic load model [7]. Moreover, monthly returns have been shown to be associated with adverse health outcomes [45], and in our data they provide the best fit among alternative measures. Furthermore, as shown in Appendix A2.7, the higher goodness-of-fit for the monthly mean returns (compared with the returns of indices such as the Shanghai Composite Index or the Shenzhen Component Index) indicates that the monthly mean return has greater explanatory power for the volume of MOV.

Discussion

Summary of main findings

This study finds that stock market declines are associated with an immediate increase in outpatient visits for hypertension, with effect modification by age, baseline health, and cold weather.

Comparison with prior literature

These findings both align with and extend prior research. Consistent with [10] and [40], we find that stock market returns are associated with outpatient visits for hypertension. However, unlike those studies that examined cumulative effects over years, we document an immediate, same-day association between stock market returns and outpatient visits for hypertension, consistent with an acute psychological stress pathway documented in prior literature [7, 39]. This indicates that the association of stock market returns with health-seeking behavior operates on a much shorter timescale. Moreover, while existing research has largely assumed linear relationships [6], our nonlinear model captures threshold effects, particularly during extreme downturns. Finally, few prior studies have examined the moderating role of temperature; our findings show that cold weather exacerbates the association, highlighting an important environmental interaction.

Contributions

The contributions of this study are threefold.

First, from a health economics perspective, this study provides robust evidence of an association between negative stock market returns and outpatient visits for hypertension, thereby indicating a micro‑level pathway through which economic shocks may be associated with individual physical health. Our findings add empirical evidence from outpatient visits for hypertension in China to the health economics literature on the link between stock market downturns and health deterioration.

Second, from a public health policy perspective, by identifying the elderly and those with poorer baseline health as vulnerable groups, and by highlighting high‑risk periods of extreme market declines (stock market crashes), this study offers empirically validated and actionable policy targets for public health early warning systems. These results support the design of targeted public health interventions during periods of intense financial market volatility.

Third, from a behavioral finance perspective, this study finds that the association between stock market movements and outpatient visits for hypertension is immediate and transient rather than persistent over long periods. This finding may challenge the conventional understanding that stock market returns may coincide with healthcare-seeking behavior through long‑term accumulated stress, and provides new empirical evidence for a short‑cycle chain from investor sentiment to healthcare-seeking behavior.

Practical implications

These findings offer several practical insights. First, given the vulnerability of elderly investors to negative returns, target-date funds could strengthen downside risk controls to mitigate the dual physical and psychological consequences of sharp market movements. Second, high-frequency medical data (e.g., hypertension or psychiatric outpatient visits) may serve as alternative data sources for market sentiment monitoring, helping assess investors’ stress levels. Third, more prudent risk assessments and volatility control mechanisms could be applied to elderly clients and those with underlying health conditions to reduce their exposure to extreme market returns.

From a financial regulation perspective, the immediate and transient nature of the association suggests that regulators could consider incorporating health impact assessments into market stability frameworks. For instance, circuit‑breaker mechanisms or trading halts might be evaluated not only for their financial effects but also for their potential to mitigate stress‑induced health surges among vulnerable populations.

From a public health standpoint, the findings imply that health surveillance systems could integrate real‑time financial market data as early warning signals for potential spikes in hypertension‑related outpatient demand. Targeted health alerts and resource allocation (e.g., staffing in hypertension clinics) could be activated during periods of sharp market declines, particularly when combined with cold weather. Moreover, the age‑gradient effect underscores the need for community‑based interventions focusing on older adults during economic downturns.

Limitations and future directions

This study has several limitations. First, the outcome is outpatient visit for hypertension, not clinically diagnosed incidence. Second, individual‑level data on stock holdings or wealth are unavailable, preventing separation of wealth‑loss effects from sentiment‑driven mechanisms. Third, the single‑city sample limits generalizability to other regions in China.

It should be noted that days of sharp market decline may coincide with major macroeconomic policy announcements or intensive media coverage, which could independently influence healthcare‑seeking behavior. Our alternative event window analysis (see Appendix A2.3) helps to rule out such coincidence, but individual‑level wealth and health records would be needed to fully exclude this possibility. Moreover, the outcome captures outpatient visit counts rather than directly measured blood pressure, leaving open the possibility that shifts in healthcare-seeking motivation partly explain the same-day association. These considerations do not negate the robustness of our findings but highlight the need for future research using individual‑level stockholding data and quasi‑experimental designs to better isolate the specific pathways involved.

Future research should address these limitations by linking medical claims with clinical data, combining individual financial accounts with health records, and using multi‑city or national datasets. In particular, when individual‑level wealth and health data become available, a deeper investigation into the micro‑level transmission mechanisms through which wealth changes affect health outcomes would be of both high academic value and practical significance. Understanding these pathways—whether through direct financial stress, changes in healthcare‑seeking behavior, or other channels—could inform targeted interventions and public health policies. Additional promising directions include examining market volatility separately from returns, strengthening causal identification (e.g., using quasi‑natural experiments), conducting sex‑disaggregated analyses, and exploring other forms of financial shocks, such as housing price declines or exchange rate fluctuations.

Conclusions

This study provides new evidence on the immediate and transient association between stock market returns and outpatient visits for hypertension. In contrast to prior studies that focused on medium- to long-term association with healthcare-seeking activities, our findings highlight that this association is amplified among older adults, those with varying disease burdens, and during cold weather. These findings advance our understanding of how short-term stock market returns affect healthcare-seeking behavior for hypertension and underscore the need to integrate financial regulation with public health considerations, particularly for vulnerable populations during periods of market instability.

Ethics approval and consent to participate

This study was based on secondary data obtained from the social medical insurance database of a major city in East China. Prior to access, the data were anonymized and de-identified by the data custodian. All direct personal identifiers, such as names and residential addresses, were removed. While the full national ID number was not provided, the data included a derived variable (date of birth, extracted from the 7th to 15th digits of the ID number), which was used solely to calculate age. No other potentially identifying information was available to the researchers. According to Article 39 of the National Health and Family Planning Commission Decree No. 11 (2016) and its subsequent amendments, research using such anonymized data is exempt from the requirements for ethical review and informed consent.
