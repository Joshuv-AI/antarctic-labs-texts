Clinician perspectives on the clinical utility of Belay Summit™ 2.0 cerebrospinal fluid test – A mixed methods study
Authors: Matthias Holdhoff, Chen Hu, Vindhya Udhane, DeElegant Robinson, Alexandra Larson, Qian Nie, Kala F Schilter, Chetan Bettegowda, Honey V Reddi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeNational Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) Central Nervous System (CNS) Guidelines recommend utilizing next-generation sequencing (NGS) to enable comprehensive genomic profiling (CGP) as standard of care for molecular characterization of CNS malignancies. The restrictive nature of the blood–brain barrier (BBB) makes plasma-based liquid biopsy an ineffective alternative, however cerebrospinal fluid (CSF)–based liquid biopsy offers a minimally invasive alternative for genomic assessment. This study aimed to evaluate the clinician perspective of clinical utility of Belay's CSF-based genomic assay, Summit™ 2.0, and to assess how clinicians use test results in routine clinical practice.MethodsClinical utility was assessed using clinician-reported survey responses from cases where Summit™ 2.0 testing was ordered for patients with known or suspected CNS disease. Survey questions evaluated indications for testing, test positivity, impact on clinical decision-making, report clarity, and perceived overall utility.ResultsThe survey response rate was 52% (95 surveys sent). Among 49 surveyed cases, 74% yielded positive genomic findings. Clinicians most frequently ordered testing to address diagnostic uncertainty or inform clinical management, with treatment selection as a less frequent primary indication. Clinical utility was reported in 86% of cases including those with negative findings, of which some results were useful for confirming existing diagnoses or management strategies. Clinicians also reported high confidence in the clarity and usability of test reports.ConclusionClinician-reported outcomes indicate that Summit™ 2.0 CSF-based genomic testing influenced clinical decision-making, including treatment selection, diagnostic clarification, confirmation of expected diagnoses, and care planning and provides meaningful information that supports diagnostic evaluation and management of CNS tumors.Keywords: CSF liquid biopsy, Clinical utility, Summit test, Comprehensive genomic profiling, Clinician surveys

Introduction

Tumors of the central nervous system (CNS) represent a clinically and biologically heterogeneous group of malignancies associated with substantial morbidity and mortality [1,2]. Both primary and secondary metastatic CNS tumors pose significant diagnostic and therapeutic challenges in clinical practice, driven by aggressive disease biology and limited effective treatment options [3]. Consequently, clinical outcomes remain poor for many patients, highlighting the need for improved diagnostic and management strategies. Comprehensive genomic profiling (CGP) of tumor tissue by next-generation sequencing (NGS) has become vitally important and is now recommended in the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) CNS Guidelines as standard care to inform tumor classification, prognostication, and therapeutic decision-making in CNS tumors [4,5]. Identification of clinically relevant genomic variants can refine diagnostic accuracy, stratify risk, guide targeted and other systemic therapy, and support enrollment in clinical trials [[6], [7], [8], [9]], making reliable access to molecular data critical for effective clinical decision making in patients with CNS disease.

Despite the recognized importance of molecular profiling in CNS tumor management, access to representative tumor material is frequently limited by the risks, feasibility, and sampling constraints of neurosurgical biopsy and resection [[10], [11], [12], [13]]. In recent years, liquid biopsy approaches using plasma/serum have been used in molecular testing for early detection and monitoring of tumors [14]. However, the CNS presents a unique challenge in detection and tracking using plasma due to the blood-brain barrier, limiting the circulating nucleic acids shed by brain tumors [15]. In contrast, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), which is in direct contact with the central nervous system, has been shown to more reliably contain tumor-derived DNA (tDNA), enabling detection of clinically relevant genomic alterations that can inform the status and management of primary and metastatic CNS cancers [[16], [17], [18], [19]].

Although CSF-based liquid biopsy offers advantages for molecular assessment of CNS tumors, until recently, clinical implementation has been challenging [17]. CSF specimens typically yield low quantities of fragmented tDNA and variant allele fractions are often significantly lower than those observed in tissue-based testing [20]. For this reason, assays designed for CSF molecular analysis must achieve high analytical sensitivity to detect clinically relevant variants accurately and reproducibly from low-input tDNA. To address the technical and clinical challenges associated with CSF-based molecular testing, we developed the Summit™ 1.0 assay, a targeted next-generation sequencing approach designed for genomic analysis of low-input, fragmented tumor-derived DNA from cerebrospinal fluid [18]. The assay was optimized to achieve high analytical sensitivity and reproducibility while interrogating genomic alterations across 32 genes with established clinical relevance in CNS tumors. To further enhance clinical applicability, the assay was subsequently expanded to Summit™ 2.0 (https://belaydiagnostics.com/belay-summit/).

The Belay Summit 2.0 CGP test in CSF is intended for use as a less invasive alternative when molecular testing of tissue biopsy or resection is infeasible and/or unsafe to simultaneously detect single nucleotide variants (SNVs), multi-nucleotide variants (MNVs), insertions/deletions (indels), copy number variants (CNVs), fusions, tumor mutational burden (TMB) and microsatellite instability (MSI) in 521 clinically relevant cancer associated genes using NGS on CSF derived nucleic acid [21]. The test does not utilize paired normal specimen assessment, so some germline variants may not be readily distinguished from somatic alterations, though rigorous bioinformatic efforts are performed to minimize this occurrence. The genes and genomic positions required for CNS tumors are listed in Category 1 or 2A of the NCCN CNS Guidelines and Biomarkers Compendium for the recommended molecular pathology workup of CNS malignancies. Clinically significant variants are reported based on the joint Association of Molecular Pathology (AMP), Americal Society of Clinical Oncology (ASCO) and College of American Pathologists (CAP) consensus guidelines for interpretation of sequence variants in cancer [22]. The test is intended to inform diagnosis, prognosis, treatment, and management of new and previously diagnosed primary and metastatic CNS tumors. The results of this test are intended to be interpreted by a board-certified clinical molecular geneticist or equivalent and should be used in conjunction with other available laboratory and clinical information. This test is not indicated for stand‐alone diagnostic purposes.

Summit™ 2.0 has been utilized in clinical settings for known or suspected CNS disease to achieve standard care consistent with NCCN Guidelines. Clinical utility of a diagnostic test in oncology is defined as the extent to which the test results are used to guide patient management that actually improves patient health outcomes or the overall benefit-to-harm balance compared to not using the test. In the context of patients with suspected or confirmed new or previously diagnosed CNS malignancies, a high proportion of patients will not be able to receive standard care molecular testing due to absence of tissue available for testing, thus the clinical utility of Summit™ 2.0 is intended to enable all CNS patients to access and receive standard care in the absence of tissue-based testing.

Survey methodology is widely used in clinical research to systematically assess clinician preferences and decision-making related to diagnostic and therapeutic practices, including the adoption of emerging innovations [23,24]. To ensure the quality of such research, the development of high-quality surveys requires careful attention to methodological rigor, including ensuring that survey items adequately capture the construct of interest, generate stable and internally consistent responses, and are designed to facilitate respondent engagement through clear wording, logical flow, and appropriate survey length [[24], [25], [26]]. In addition, foundational work in survey research emphasizes that well-constructed questions, appropriate sampling strategies, and the integration of qualitative and quantitative approaches are critical to improving interpretability and minimizing bias in clinician surveys [26,27]. Clinician surveys have been widely used to evaluate the perceived clinical utility, as well as the barriers and facilitators, associated with novel diagnostic technologies. For example, surveys examining CSF biomarkers for neurodegenerative diseases such as Alzheimer's disease (AD) have provided insights into clinician perspectives on the adoption and integration of these diagnostics into practice, thereby underscoring the value of survey methods for early evaluation and implementation planning [28].

To assess clinical utility beyond analytical performance, clinicians ordering Summit™ 2.0 were surveyed regarding the impact of test results on diagnosis, prognosis, treatment decision-making, and patient management. Responses were collated to summarize clinician reported outcomes highlighting the clinical utility of Summit™ 2.0 in the management of CNS tumors.

Methods

Study design and analysis population

The study was set up as a mixed methods study to target clinicians who ordered the Summit™ 2.0 test for at least one patient during September 30, 2025, to October 30, 2025. Clinicians were sent a survey questionnaire (detailed in Supplement 1) within 2 weeks of reporting test results. The unit of analysis was the patient/test-level clinician survey response to document changes to clinical recommendations made according to test findings and to capture clinician-reported changes in patient management and perceived patient impact. Because some clinicians may complete multiple surveys, responses could be clustered within providers; provider-level summaries were used to contextualize multiple responses contributed by the same clinician. All returned surveys were included in descriptive analyses. All surveys with a blank or "unsure" response for the primary endpoint were excluded from the primary analysis. Surveys were included in descriptive summaries of secondary items (e.g., report clarity) and surveys with missing responses to individual secondary items were analyzed on an available-case basis. The survey instrument and analysis of deidentified and aggregated data were approved by an independent institutional review board (Advarra IRB, Pro00090464). In addition to clinician responses received, data collected for associated specimens included provisional diagnosis and patient demographics.

Clinician-provided data

The survey included 6 questions regarding clinical context for ordering the test, whether the result influenced patient care (e.g., treatment changes, additional testing, referrals, and perceived usefulness of Summit™ 2.0 results, including whether they prompted changes in surgical strategy; chemotherapy, or radiation treatment; and/or follow-up plan) that were specifically attributable to the Summit™ 2.0 findings. Responses to questions 4 and 5 were based on a 5-point Likert scale for agreement from "strongly disagree" (1) to "strongly agree" (5), providing a balanced, easy-to-use metric. Survey responses were analyzed to assess clinicians' perceptions of the clinical utility and clarity of the Summit™ 2.0 test. The survey also solicited suggestions for improving report content and/or quality. Clinicians were also asked to assess whether Summit™ 2.0 testing had a positive impact on patient health outcomes, which was defined as being beneficial to clinical management and/or the patients’ overall health and well-being at the time of survey completion.

Statistical analysis

Analyses were descriptive. The primary endpoint proportion was reported with a two-sided exact (Clopper–Pearson) 95% confidence interval. Associations between patient characteristics, Summit™ results, and clinician-reported changes to clinical recommendations and perceived outcomes were summarized using cross-tabulations and descriptive comparisons. Secondary survey items were summarized using available responses, with item-level denominators reported as applicable; provider-level summaries were included to contextualize clinicians contributing more than one survey.

Results

Clinician and institution response rate

A total of 95 questionnaires were sent out to ordering clinicians for specimens received between September 30 and October 30, 2025. The cohort comprised of 51 unique clinicians across 30 institutions, and depending on the number of cases ordered by a clinician, each could get multiple unique surveys. The 95 surveys included 60 cases that were positive for Summit™ 2.0 in that a clinically significant variant was detected and 35 that were negative for Summit™ 2.0. Of the 95 surveys sent out, 49 were received, corresponding to a 52% survey return rate (49/95, exact 95% CI 41%–62%). These 49 surveys were contributed by 22 unique providers (provider participation rate 43%, 22/51, exact 95% CI 28%–56%) across 17 institutions (institution participation rate 57%, 17/30, exact 95% CI 34%–72%). A majority of the respondents (64%) were neuro-oncologists (14/22, exact 95% CI 44%–84%), plus 2 neuro-oncology advanced practice providers. Remaining respondents included 2 medical oncologists, 1 neurosurgeon, 2 neurologists, and 1 internal medicine provider.

Summit™ 2.0 indications for testing

Survey responses were received from providers in 17 states within the continental US (Fig. 2A) and identified several key clinical scenarios in which Summit™ 2.0 test was ordered (Fig. 2B). The most frequently reported primary indication was diagnostic uncertainty in the absence of tissue for testing and clinical management decisions, cited in 27 of the 49 cases (55%, exact 95% CI 41%–69%), reflecting use of the test to clarify equivocal clinical or radiological findings. Informing clinical management was also commonly reported, with 24 survey responses indicating that the test was requested to support decision-making in complex clinical settings. In addition, Summit™ 2.0 was used to guide treatment selection in 16 cases, highlighting its role in identifying actionable somatic alterations. A smaller number of survey responses indicated that the test was ordered for treatment monitoring (14%, 7 of 49, exact 95% CI 5.9%–27.2%), reflecting its application in longitudinal assessment of disease status. Overall, survey findings demonstrate that the CSF liquid biopsy was predominantly utilized in contexts of diagnostic uncertainty and clinical management, with additional roles in treatment selection and disease monitoring (Fig. 2B).

Survey responses indicated that the CSF liquid biopsy test influenced clinical decision-making in the majority of cases. Overall, 42 of 49 survey respondents (86%) reported that the test impacted their clinical decisions (Fig. 2C). Per the prespecified primary analysis definition, excluding “Unsure” (n = 3), the proportion reporting impact was 42/46 = 91.3% (exact 95% CI 79.2%–97.6%). Among surveys who reported that the test influenced care, different forms of impact on clinical management were described. The most frequently reported effect was guidance of treatment choice (n = 22), followed by informing patient or caregiver decision-making (n = 21) and informing prognosis (n = 18). The test led to a definitive diagnosis in 17 cases and confirmed the expected diagnosis in 15 cases. Additional reported impacts included prompting referral to another specialty (n = 7), changes to therapy planning (n = 7), and leading to additional diagnostic testing (n = 3). In one case, the test result contributed to the avoidance of biopsy, surgery, or resection. No response was recorded for this item in 7 cases (Fig. 2D).

Among surveys that reported the test did not influence clinical decision-making (n = 4), the most commonly cited reason was that the result was unclear or uninformative (n = 3). One survey indicated that the result confirmed an existing treatment or management plan (Fig. 2E). For those surveyed who reported they were unsure whether the test influenced care (n = 3), uncertainty was attributed to unclear or uninformative results (n = 1), while no reason was selected in 2 survey responses (Fig. 2E).

Utility and usability of Summit™ 2.0

Clinicians were asked whether the test had clinical utility for the case in question. Responses were distributed as follows: Neutral (n = 5; 1 negative case, 4 positive cases), Agree (n = 6; 1 negative case, 5 positive cases), and Strongly Agree (n = 38; 11 negative cases, 27 positive cases). No responses of disagree or strongly disagree were recorded. Overall, a majority of survey respondents (44/49 = 89.8%, exact 95% CI 77.8%–96.6%) agreed or strongly agreed that the test had clinical utility for the cases assessed (Fig. 3A).

Clinicians were also asked whether the test report was clear and understandable. Responses included neutral (n = 1, negative case), agree (n = 11; 7 positive, 4 negative), strongly agree (n = 34; 26 positive, 8 negative), and no response (n = 3, all positive cases). There were no responses of disagree or strongly disagree (Fig. 3B). Overall, 45/49 (91.8%, exact 95% CI 80.4%–97.7%) survey responses agreed or strongly agreed that the test report was clear and understandable (Fig. 3C). When asked whether they would order the test again, 47/49 survey responses (95.9%, exact 95% CI 86.0%–99.5%) indicated “Yes,” with no “No” or “Unsure” responses, and 2 surveys with no response (Fig. 3C).

Limited qualitative feedback on the utility of the test report format emphasized that the ability to modify and customize the report structure was particularly valuable, as it allowed clinicians to receive information in a format that met their specific needs. Suggestions for improvement included providing clearer statements regarding the interpretation of absent variant allele frequency (VAF) values to facilitate communication with patients.

Cohort demographics

The cohort consisted of responses received from corresponding clinicians for 49 cases of patients for whom Summit™ testing was ordered with 18 primary and 31 metastatic CNS cancers (Table 1). Demographics of the patient cohort included 12 males and 37 females (Table 1). The majority of patients were of White race (n = 27; 55.1%, information on race was missing/not available for 15 cases.) (Table 1). Most individuals were aged 40–65 years (n = 29; 59.1%), while those aged >65 years, 19–39 years, and 0–18 years accounted for 28.5% (n = 14), 10.2% (n = 5), and 2.0% (n = 1) of the cohort, respectively (Table 1). Primary source of CSF was lumbar puncture in 82 % of cases (40 of 49), with Ommaya reservoir used in 6 cases and CASF aspiration in 1 case. The median volume of CSF received was 6-7.25 mL of CSF with a median nucleic acid yield ranging from 13 to 24 ng in primary cases and 12-37 ng in metastatic cases (Table 1).

Metastatic CNS tumors had a higher detection rate of somatic alterations with Summit™ 2.0

Of the 49 surveys returned, 36 (73.5%; 36/49, exact 95% CI 58.9%–85.1%) had a positive Summit™ 2.0 result and 13 (26.5%) tests were negative for a clinical significant variant (Fig. 1A), with a variety of provisional diagnoses, including 18 primary and 31 metastatic cases (Fig. 1B). Comparison between metastatic central nervous system tumors (MCNS) and primary central nervous system (PCNS) tumors showed a higher proportion of positive results among metastatic cases than primary cases. Positivity was 83.9% (26/31, exact 95% CI 66.3%–94.5%) in metastatic cases and 55.6% (10/18, exact 95% CI 30.8%–78.5%) in primary cases (Fig. 1A). Stratified analysis of CSF samples revealed differences in the prevalence of somatic alterations between patients with MCNS and PCNS tumors, with the highest rate of detectable somatic alterations observed in the MCNS group. These findings are descriptive and suggest a higher molecular yield in metastatic disease within this respondent sample.

Discussion

Recent studies indicate that CSF liquid biopsy can provide clinically actionable information in patients with central nervous system malignancies, particularly when tissue is unavailable, imaging is equivocal, or conventional diagnostic approaches are insufficient. Prior reports have shown that CSF-derived tumor DNA may clarify difficult diagnoses, identify targetable alterations, and accelerate treatment decisions in both primary and metastatic CNS tumors. For example, CSF liquid biopsy has been used to distinguish secondary glioma from relapsed medulloblastoma and support timely trial enrollment, accelerate diagnosis and treatment initiation in central nervous system lymphoma, and identify leptomeningeal disease and therapeutically relevant driver alterations earlier than cytology or imaging in metastatic cancer [[29], [30], [31], [32], [33]].

In this context, our study evaluated the clinical utility of the Summit™ 2.0 CSF-based genomic assay using clinician-reported outcomes from routine practice. Approximately 74% of cases had positive genomic findings, and most survey responses reported that Summit™ 2.0 influenced clinical decision-making, supporting the role of CSF-based genomic testing as a clinically informative tool in CNS tumor care. These findings are descriptive and reflect clinician-reported impact rather than measured patient outcomes. Overall, the results suggest that CSF-based genomic testing can provide clinically relevant molecular information in most cases, especially in cases with diagnostic uncertainty or limited tissue availability.

The clinical scenarios for Summit™ 2.0 use provide important context for its utility. Survey responses indicated that clinicians most often ordered testing for diagnostic uncertainty or overall management, with treatment selection as a less common indication. This reflects the challenges of CNS tumor care, where imaging may be inconclusive, and biopsy or resection may not be feasible or safe [[10], [11], [12], [13],34,35]. Summit™ 2.0 was used for both primary and metastatic CNS tumors, with higher proportions of positive results in metastatic cases, consistent with previous reports of tumor DNA shedding into CSF [36,37].

Clinician responses further suggest that Summit™ 2.0 results influenced several aspects of patient care. Most commonly, test results contributed to treatment selection, diagnostic clarification, and confirmation of an expected diagnosis. Clinicians also reported that test results informed prognosis, prompted referrals, influenced imaging or treatment planning, and supported discussions with patients and caregivers. These findings highlight that the clinical utility of genomic testing in CNS tumors extends beyond identifying targetable alterations to broader decision-support roles.

Clinical utility was observed even in subset of cases with negative genomic findings e.g., abnormal magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), spine lesion etc. which could mean that there was in fact no cancer. Among these limited responses, negative results were often reported as useful for confirming existing diagnoses or management plans for patients. While most clinicians reported that Summit™ 2.0 influenced management, a small number reported no change in care. Although based on a small sample, these observations suggest that CSF-based genomic testing reduced diagnostic uncertainty and support decision-making, even when no clinically relevant variants are found.

The survey indicated high levels of clinician confidence in the assay's overall utility and usability. Nearly all respondents agreed or strongly agreed that Summit™ 2.0 had clinical utility, with no disagreement reported. Most clinicians also found the test report clear and understandable, which is essential for genomic assays intended for use by clinical care teams. Clinician feedback highlighted the value of report customization in supporting the interpretation and application of results.

These findings complement existing studies on the analytical validity and feasibility of CSF-based liquid biopsy for detecting tumor-derived genomic alterations in CNS malignancies including Summit™ 1.0[1], [18], [20], [32], [38], [39], [40], [41], [42]. While previous work has focused on detection rates, concordance with tissue-based testing, and technical performance, this study provides evidence of clinicians' practical use of CSF-based genomic results. Clinician-reported outcomes offer insight into the utility of CSF-based genomic testing as an adjunct to established diagnostic and management strategies.

This study has several limitations. Clinical utility was assessed through clinician-reported surveys, which may introduce recall and desirability bias, and does not provide independent adjudication of decision-making or objective patient benefit. The cohort was small, and respondents were clinicians who elected to order Summit™ 2.0, which may limit generalizability. The patient cohort analyzed was selected based on clinicians who answered the survey, which is subject to confounding and may not be fully representative of the population of patients for which the Summit 2.0 test is ordered. In addition, survey and provider participation was incomplete, and nonresponse may be associated with perceived test value, potentially biasing descriptive estimates upward. Because the unit of analysis was the patient/test survey response, some clinicians contributed multiple surveys; as such, results primarily reflect survey-level summaries rather than independent clinician-level observations. Downstream patient outcomes and long-term effects were not evaluated. Future studies with prospective designs, larger cohorts, and outcome-based measures are needed to further define the clinical value of CSF-based genomic testing in CNS tumor management.

Overall, clinician-reported outcomes indicate that Summit™ 2.0 CSF-based genomic testing was frequently used to provide standard care molecular testing when tissue-based testing was not possible to inform diagnosis and management across various CNS disease contexts, influencing diagnostic confidence, treatment selection, and care planning. These findings support a role for CSF-based genomic testing to enable NCCN recommended standard care molecular characterization as a useful adjunct in clinical settings when tissue-based testing is unsafe or infeasible.
