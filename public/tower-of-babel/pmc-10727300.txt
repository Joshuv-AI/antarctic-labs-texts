Trajectories of Blood Lipids Profile in Midlife Women: Does Menopause Matter?
Authors: Bingjie Wu, Bingbing Fan, Yanlin Qu, Chunxia Li, Jiahao Chen, Ying Liu, Jiali Wang, Tao Zhang, Yuguo Chen
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIt remains controversial whether changes of lipids over menopause transition (MT) are more age‐related or more menopause‐related. We aimed to classify women into different trajectory groups based on pattern and level of total cholesterol, low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol, apolipoprotein B (ApoB), high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL‐C), triglyceride, and apolipoprotein A‐I over the MT, as well as examine the effect of MT‐related factors on lipid trajectory groups and levels.Methods and ResultsThe cohort included 2582 subjects from the Study of Women's Health Across the Nation. Different trajectory patterns of lipids during the MT were determined using the latent class growth mixture model. The predictors of distinct blood lipids trajectory groups were determined by multiple linear regression models and multinomial logistic regression models. Women were categorized into either inverse U‐shape or progressing trajectory group in each blood lipids measurement. The inverse U‐shape total cholesterol, low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol, apolipoprotein B, high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol, log(TG), and apolipoprotein A‐I trajectories showed an increasing trend before menopause but a decreasing trend after menopause. The U‐shape total cholesterol, low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol, and apolipoprotein B trajectories started to rise 5 years before menopause. Age at menopause, follicle‐stimulating hormone, vasomotor symptoms, and estradiol predicted the shape and level of the women's lipids over the MT.ConclusionsDistinct lipid trajectories were identified during the MT, and the existence of at least 1 trajectory in each lipid parameters suggested a contribution of menopause. Our study highlights the need for earlier and continuous surveillance of lipids during the MT.

Dyslipidemia is a major modifiable risk factor for atherosclerotic cardiovascular diseases progression. 1 , 2 It is now estimated that ≈40% of adult women worldwide have dyslipidemia. Age and sex are primary physiological factors that have a strong influence on blood lipid levels. 3 With increasing age, lipid levels increased among both men and women, but prevalence of dyslipidemia was significantly higher among women in midlife than men. 4 , 5 This suggests that the menopausal transition (MT) may contribute substantially to dyslipidemia in women in midlife. 6

Previous works about the relationship between the MT and lipid profiles were controversial. Some studies report that there was no change in lipid profiles before and after menopause, suggesting the possible effect of chronological aging. 7 , 8 Several studies have revealed a significant association between the MT and lipids, as evidenced by substantial changes in lipid profiles after the final menstrual period (FMP). 9 , 10 Matthews et al showed an association between MT and lipids profiles, with age at menopause playing an important role in lipid changes during the MT. 11 However, with the exception of the study by Badon et al, which explored heterogeneous subgroups of lipids in midlife women (it did not consider the effect of the MT), the remaining studies only used piecewise linear models and did not examine whether there were different trajectory groups of lipids over the MT. These studies may have overlooked the existence of heterogeneous lipid subgroups among study populations and that lipids may be susceptible to menopause or MT‐related factors only in women with specific characteristics.

MT‐related factors included decline of estradiol levels, increase of follicle‐stimulating hormone (FSH) levels, and vasomotor symptoms (VMS). 12 , 13 , 14 It has been argued that age at menopause and MT‐related factors possibly affect lipid levels. 15 , 16 , 17 However, no studies have explored the relationship between MT‐related factors and heterogeneous blood lipids trajectories. We suppose that blood lipids in perimenopausal women have heterogeneous patterns of change during the MT and that MT‐related factors can affect blood lipids trajectories.

Using data from a community‐based cohort, the SWAN (Study of Women's Health Across the Nation), this research aims to identify blood lipids trajectories during the MT and determine the effect of MT‐related factors on lipid trajectory groups and levels over the MT.

METHODS

The data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request. Part of the SWAN data is publicly available at https://www.swanstudy.org.

Study Cohort

SWAN is an ongoing, multiethnic, multisite, population‐based study that was designed to characterize the psychological and biological changes that accompany the MT. The SWAN study design has been described in detail previously. 18 In short, 3302 women aged 42 to 52 years were enrolled at 7 different locations in the United States during 1996 and 1997 (Boston, Massachusetts; Chicago, Illinois; Detroit, Michigan; Los Angeles, California; Newark, New Jersey; Oakland, California; and Pittsburgh, Pennsylvania). Criteria for SWAN enrollment included the following: (1) not pregnant or breastfeeding during enrollment, (2) had an intact uterus and at least 1 ovary, (3) had at least 1 menstrual period in the last 3 months before enrollment, (4) identified themselves as Black, Chinese, Japanese, White, and Hispanic women, and (5) with no use of hormone therapy.

The SWAN study has up to 17 follow‐ups, and the baseline and the first 10 annual follow‐ups have been made public. The baseline and first 10 annual follow‐ups of the SWAN study were included in our study, and individuals with missing information on lipid parameters were excluded (n=46) and they had fewer than 3 follow‐up visits (n=674). After exclusion, 2582 adult subjects with 3 to 7 repeated visits (mean age 45.97 years at baseline, 14 930 observations) were enrolled in the present study. Follow‐up years were a mean of 6.5 years (range, 1.2 to 8.5 years). The subjects included were all in the early or late perimenopausal period.

Approval of the study protocols were obtained from the institutional review board at each site, and study participants provided written informed consent at each visit. Further details of the SWAN protocol have been previously published. 19

Imputation of the FMP

Women at the baseline and the follow‐up questionnaires reported the date of the first day of their last menstrual period, and the FMP was determined retrospectively as the first day of the last menstrual period before 12 months before amenorrhea. Yet the FMP was not observed in 1232 (37.3%) women because of the use of exogenous hormones, hysterectomy, or not having reached their natural menopause. Obtaining FMP dates for all SWAN participants is critical for describing the changes related to menopause and generalizing the findings for the women the SWAN represented. Thus, we used multiple imputation to fill in the unobserved FMP dates by referring to a large number of baseline and longitudinal demographic characteristics influencing FMP in previous studies. Furthermore, an optimal imputation method was chosen based on a comprehensive comparison among these 5 multiple imputation methods (predictive mean matching, weighted predictive mean matching, random sample from observed values, classification and regression trees, and random forest imputations). Data S1 and Figure S1 provide a fuller description of the imputation process.

Measurement of Blood Lipids

Blood samples were collected after a minimum 10‐hour fast. After centrifugation, the plasma was separated and frozen at −80 °C, and the samples were sent on dry ice to the Laboratory of Medical Research in Lexington, Kentucky (baseline through visit 7) and the Laboratory of Pathology at the University of Michigan in Ann Arbor, Michigan (visit 8 through visit 13). All lipids fractions were measured from EDTA‐treated plasma, using a Hitachi 747‐200 analyzer at the Laboratory of Medical Research and on an ADVIA 2400 automated chemistry analyzer (Siemens, Washington, DC) at the Laboratory of Pathology, University of Michigan. Laboratory indexes include total cholesterol (TC), low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL‐C), apolipoprotein B (ApoB), high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL‐C), triglyceride, and apolipoprotein A‐I (ApoA‐I). Interlaboratory comparisons were conducted between the 2 laboratories to ensure comparability.

Covariates

Information on race/ethnicity, age, financial strain, income, education, smoking, alcohol drinking, body mass index (BMI), diabetes, estradiol, FSH, and VMS were collected at each visit. Due to the dynamic changes of estradiol and FSH during the menstrual cycle, the cycle day of blood draw (within 2–5 days of the menstrual cycle or not) was adjusted when analyzing these hormones. Education status was divided into below high school, some college, and college degree or higher. Income was defined as <$35 000 per year, $35 000–$75 000 per year, and >$75 000 per year. Smoking status was categorized as never smoker, past smoker, and current smoker based on the tobacco use section. Alcohol consumption was defined as none, <1 drink, 1 to 7 drinks, and >7 drinks. BMI was based on self‐reported values and was calculated as weight in kilograms divided by square of height in meters. Participants were defined as having diabetes at any 1 follow‐up visit if they had 1 or more of the following: (1) fasting serum glucose level ≥126 mg/dL; (2) self‐reported diabetes; and (3) self‐reported use of insulin or oral hypoglycemic medications. All serum hormones were measured using the Automated Chemiluminescent System−180 automated analyzer at the University of Michigan Endocrine Laboratory. Estradiol was measured by a modified, off‐line ACS: 180 (estradiol‐6) immunoassay. FSH was measured by a 2‐site chemiluminometric immunoassay. Women who had hot flashes or night sweats or both hot flashes and night sweats were classified as having VMS. Estradiol, FSH, VMS, and diabetes were time‐varying covariates. The covariates were all carried out within the baseline visit.

Statistical Analysis

To illustrate the effect of MT on changes in blood lipids and to exclude the effect of chronological aging, blood lipid trajectories were best described as a function of time since FMP, rather than age. The latent class growth mixed model (LCGMM) was used to determine different trajectory patterns of TC, LDL‐C, ApoB, HDL‐C, log(triglyceride), and ApoA‐I over the MT, separately. 20 The latent class trajectories of blood lipids were specified as functions of time since the FMP. By setting the longitudinal measure as a linear or nonlinear function of FMP, we expressed it as the FMP, the quadratic or cubic term of FMP, and traversed 2 to 4 potential groups. 21 We chose the best‐fitting model for blood lipids trajectories according to the following criteria: Necessary criteria, (1) high mean posterior probabilities (≥0.7); (2) acceptable proportion of the population (≥5.0%); and (3) lowest Bayesian information criterion (BIC). Advanced criteria, (1) prior knowledge of the research; (2) high mean posterior class membership probabilities (≥0.65); (3) model entropy close to 1.0. LCGMM was implemented with package “lcmm” (version 2.0.0) in R (version 4.1.3). Data S2 shows the detailed fitting process of the LCGMM.

After each TC, LDL‐C, ApoB, HDL‐C, log(triglyceride), and ApoA‐I trajectory group was determined by LCGMM, using locally weighted polynomial regression to determine the inflection points surrounding the FMP. The change in lipids was calculated before and after inflection points were estimated using mixed effects piecewise linear spline models. A piecewise linear spline model divides the x‐axis into 2 segments and fits a linear model to each segment. The linear segments are constrained to join at the break points. In our models, a knot was placed at inflection points surrounding the FMP, allowing different slopes before and after the inflection. Subsequently, a linear hypothesis test was performed using Wald χ2 tests to compare the slope difference before and after the inflection point with the “linearHypothesis” function in the “car” package (version 3.1‐2). 22 If there was a statistical difference in slope before and after the inflection point, it indicated a contribution of menopause. In addition, if no obvious inflection point was detected, slopes of the trajectories were estimated by the linear mixed‐effect model.

The selection of predictors (MT‐related factors) was based on previous studies, 12 , 13 , 14 , 23 including race and ethnicity, BMI, age at menopause, estradiol, FSH, VMS, and diabetes. Using a single LCGMM with the same specification (number of respective lipid groups and terms of time), we estimated the odds ratio of trajectory group membership for baseline variables and β‐coefficient of lipid level for time‐varying covariates, simultaneously. The model further adjusted for lipid‐lowering treatment and cycle day of blood draw. Furthermore, we performed sensitivity analyses restricted to women who observed the FMP dates only (N=1879) or excluded individuals who received lipid‐lowering treatment at any follow‐up (N=2237).

Characteristics were compared using mean (SD), median [interquartile range], and n (%). In our study, all analyses were conducted using R (version 4.1.3). Hypothesis tests were 2‐sided, and P <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

The Figure presents the longitudinal trajectories of TC, LDL‐C, ApoB, HDL‐C, log(triglyceride), and ApoA‐I during 20 years of the MT for 2582 individuals. All lipid parameters had 2 distinct trajectories and showed similar trajectory patterns. The inverse U‐shape trajectories of TC, LDL‐C, ApoB, HDL‐C, log(triglyceride), and ApoA‐I (in 19.3%, 15.3%, 16.0%, 22.6%, 25.5%, and 19.2% of the study) were increasing before the FMP but indicated a sharp decline after the FMP. The U‐shape trajectories of TC, log(triglyceride), LDL‐C, and ApoB (in 80.7%, 74.5%, 84.7%, and 84.0% of the cohort, respectively) showed a decline 5 years before the FMP and then rose. The increasing ApoA‐I trajectory (80.8%) was rising monotonically during the MT. In the low‐stable HDL‐C trajectory group (77.4%), predicted concentration level of lipids persisted at a low level of 53 mg/dL during the MT.

The lipid trajectory model fitting process results are shown in Tables S1 through S6. We traversed the LCGMM from 1 class to 4 classes for linear, quadratic, and cubic curves. A quadratic parametric model with 2 classes was selected as the best fit for all lipid parameters based on the above statistical criteria. Tables S7 through S12 summarize the best‐fitting 2‐class quadratic LCGMM parameter estimates for all lipid parameters, respectively. All the parameters were statistically significant (P <0.05 for all). For ApoB and LDL‐C, although the cubic model with 2 classes had a lower BIC and higher proportions with posterior probability >0.70 than the quadratic model, shapes of the cubic model with 2 classes were different from previous studies in Figure S2 (LDL‐C showed a 7‐year earlier decline group, and ApoB showed a continuous decline group, which was not consistent with previous studies). 10 , 11 Thus, fit quadratic model with 2 classes for ApoB and LDL‐C is a reasonable choice.

Hispanic women were more probable to be in the inverse U‐shape log(triglyceride), low‐stable HDL‐C, and increasing ApoA‐I trajectory groups. There were no significant differences in race and ethnicity among the 2 different trajectory groups within each of TC, LDL‐C, and ApoB. Tables 4 and 5 and Tables S18 through S21 show the MT‐related factors of TC, log(triglyceride), LDL‐C, ApoB, HDL‐C, and ApoA‐I trajectories. Women who were older (>53 years) at the FMP were more likely to be in the inverse U‐shape log(triglyceride) and ApoA‐I trajectories than those in the U‐shape group (or increasing group for ApoA‐I) and women aged 51 to 53 years at the time of the FMP. Individuals who were younger (<51 years) at the FMP were more likely to be in the inverse U‐shape LDL‐C trajectories than those in the U‐shape group and women aged 51 to 53 years at the time of the FMP. Relative to the increasing group and Black women, Japanese and White women were more likely to be in the inverse U‐shape log(triglyceride), and ApoA‐I trajectories; HDL‐C and LDL‐C were less likely to be Hispanic. Compared with the progressing group, individuals who were higher baseline BMI were more likely to be in the inverse U‐shape log(triglyceride) and TC trajectories; individuals who were lower baseline BMI were more likely to be in the inverse U‐shape ApoA‐I, ApoB, HDL‐C, and LDL‐C trajectories. Higher FSH predicted a higher blood lipid level over time in all blood lipid trajectories except all log(triglyceride) and inverse U‐shape ApoB trajectories. VMS predicted a higher blood lipids level over the MT in the inverse U‐shape log(triglyceride), TC, ApoA‐I, and HDL‐C and U‐shape TC, ApoB, and LDL‐C trajectories. Higher estradiol predicted a lower blood lipids level over the MT in the U‐shape LDL‐C and ApoB trajectories. However, in all log(triglyceride), HDL‐C, and ApoA trajectories but inverse U‐shape log(triglyceride) trajectory, greater estradiol predicted a higher blood lipids level over time.

DISCUSSION

In this longitudinal study, we identified 2 heterogeneous groups of women according to common trajectories of TC, LDL‐C, ApoB, HDL‐C, log(triglyceride), and ApoA‐I during the MT in the United States population as shown in the Figure. We determined the inverse U‐shape TC, LDL‐C, ApoB, HDL‐C, log(triglyceride), and ApoA‐I trajectory group and the U‐shape TC, LDL‐C, and ApoB trajectory group, which showed an inflection point around the FMP and 5 years before the FMP, respectively, suggesting the role of menopause in Table 3. We further investigated the effect of predictors on distinct blood lipids trajectory groups and observed that MT‐related factors showed a significant relationship with blood lipids trajectory groups in Tables 4 and 5 and Tables S18 through S21. Age at menopause after 51 years predicted the inverse U‐shape log(triglyceride) and ApoA‐I trajectories; a higher FSH level predicted a greater blood lipid level during the MT in all blood lipid trajectories except all log(triglyceride) and U‐shape ApoB trajectories; VMS predicted a greater blood lipids level during the MT in the inverse U‐shape log(triglyceride), TC, and HDL‐C and increasing TC, LDL‐C, and ApoB trajectories, and a greater estradiol predicted a higher blood lipids level over time. The observations of this study suggest that lipid parameters may increase at an accelerated rate during the MT and emphasize the importance of aggressive surveillance of blood lipids and MT‐related factors in women over the MT.

The trajectories identified in this study extend prior observations that ignored the existence of heterogeneous lipid subgroups among study populations or effects of the menopause. Matthews et al did not seek to identify lipid trajectory groups but identified the pattern of change anchored to each woman's age at FMP. They found the presence of inflection points for TC, log(triglyceride), LDL‐C, ApoA‐I, and ApoB in the 1‐year period before and after the FMP. 10 , 11 Khoudary et al found that HDL‐C and total HDL particles increased during the MT, while ApoA‐I started to decline across the perimenopause. 9 Badon et al used group‐based trajectory modeling fitted distinct log(triglyceride), HDL‐C, and LDL‐C trajectories by age, but ignored the effects of the MT. 7 In contrast to previous investigations that did not attempt to identify lipid trajectory groups during the MT or ignored the effects of the MT, we applied a longitudinal trajectory clustering approach, the LCGMM, to observe distinct trajectories of blood lipids in women with different levels, patterns of change, and association with MT and MT‐related factors.

We determined that the inverse U‐shape TC, LDL‐C, ApoB, HDL‐C, log(triglyceride), and ApoA‐I trajectory groups showed a piecewise‐linear pattern with an inflection point near the FMP consistent with the contribution of menopause, which is consistent with other studies. 9 , 10 , 11 , 15 Intriguingly, we determined that the U‐shape TC, log(triglyceride), LDL‐C, and ApoB trajectory groups presented a piecewise‐linear pattern with an inflection point 5 years before the FMP, which were different from the blood lipids profile of previous studies that showed inverse U‐shape or linear increase patterns over the MT. 9 , 10 , 11 We speculate that it might be due to estrogen levels beginning to decline several years before the FMP, 24 , 25 , 26 and a decrease in estrogen levels can cause an increase in TC, triglycerides, LDL‐C, and ApoB. 27 , 28 This result demonstrated that we need to monitor lipid profiles earlier and consistently during the MT.

BMI is an important factor affecting lipid changes in menopausal women. Matthews et al observed a decreasing trend in lipids (TC and LDL‐C) after the FMP in women with heavier weights, which suggests that weight is an important modifier of lipid profiles before and after the FMP. 10 Derby et al found an interaction between baseline weight and menopausal status with lipid levels. 15 Interestingly, the effect of BMI in our study on the inverse U‐shape of ApoB appears to be consistent with previous studies, whereas the effect on TC and LDL‐C is not. 10 However, previous studies have not considered heterogeneous lipid subgroups, and we were more interested in the BMI characteristics of populations with different patterns of lipid changes. Our findings may provide new insights that different patterns of lipid changes exist in populations with different BMI levels.

Prior studies found that as the level of estradiol elevated, HDL‐C and apolipoprotein levels increased, but LDL‐C and ApoB levels decreased. 29 , 30 , 31 Therefore, estradiol depletion potentially linked to blood lipids over the MT, which was also consistent with our findings. Moreover, we also found that triglyceride level was elevated with estradiol, which was different from previous studies. In fact, most previous studies have focused on women of reproductive age, or women on hormone replacement therapy, without assessing changes in estradiol over time. Future studies should focus on the effects of estradiol on blood lipids in women during the MT and their mechanisms.

Little research has evaluated the association between age at FMP and lipids. As previously noted by Matthews et al, in women with an older age at FMP, TC, LDL‐C, TG, ApoB, and ApoA‐I increased during the MT up to the FMP and was followed by a decline in the postmenopausal years, 11 while O'Keeffe et al found that age at FMP was not associated with trajectories of blood lipids throughout the MT. 8 Our observations were partially consistent with previous findings, such that an older age at FMP predicted the inverse U‐shape log(triglyceride) and increasing ApoA‐I group. Alternatively, we found that most lipids trajectories had no relationship with age at FMP, except for the inverse U‐shape log(triglyceride) and ApoA‐I group. Given that previous studies have been limited to cross‐section studies or have ignored the potential heterogeneity of populations, we used LCGMM to detect the existence of different lipid trajectories during the MT. Because we considered heterogeneous subgroups of lipids, our findings provide a more accurate relationship between age at FMP and lipids changes.

VMS occurrence constitutes a frequent troublesome midlife experience for most women. 32 , 33 A large meta‐analysis showed that women who reported night sweats had higher levels of TC compared with those who did not. 34 Thurston reported that VMS were linked with higher level of TC, LDL‐C, HDL‐C, ApoA‐I, and ApoB. 35 A result in SWAN suggested that frequent VMS were associated with a higher level of LDL‐C. 36 As for the mechanisms linking VMS to blood lipids, there is currently much debate. Among them, VMS reflecting changes in the hormonal environment and elevated inflammatory markers might be reasonable explanations. 37 , 38 , 39 , 40 Our results are consistent with these studies and extend them by considering heterogeneous lipid trajectories, showing that VMS predicted a higher blood lipid level over the MT in the inverse U‐shape log(triglyceride), TC, ApoA‐I, and HDL‐C and U‐shape TC, LDL‐C, and ApoB trajectories.

So far, several studies have been carried out to explore the role of FSH in the physiological and pathological processes of other nongonadal tissues and organs, 41 , 42 , 43 including that FSH stimulates the promotion of lipid synthesis. 41 , 43 Several cross‐sectional studies found that FSH was positively associated with TC and LDL‐C. 44 , 45 Our results further extend previous results by highlighting that FSH predicts higher TC, HDL‐C, LDL‐C, and ApoA‐I over time, regardless of trajectory. The mechanisms remain speculative, but the fact that FSH interacts with its receptors in hepatocytes to elevate circulating LDL‐C levels is likely to play an important role. 45 However, the mechanisms underlying the effects of FSH on other lipid parameters remain to be investigated. Further investigations are required to determine the possible mechanisms.

There are some strengths and limitations to our study. The present research is a population‐based longitudinal study with large sample size and concurrent repeated measurements of lipids, endogenous sex hormones, and MT‐related factors. It enabled us to use LCGMM to explore lipid profiles during the MT and link MT‐related factors with distinct lipids trajectories. On the other hand, our study has several limitations. First, the FMP was not observed in 37.3% of women, and not all women in the cohort had become postmenopausal when the data were analyzed. Thus, women who transitioned later (ie, after the last follow‐up included in this analysis) may be different in terms of the patterns of lipid changes. Nevertheless, when excluding women for whom the FMP was not observed, the sensitivity analysis results were generally consistent with the primary analysis. Secondly, the Hispanic women were missing lipid data beyond the fifth study visit due to administrative reasons, so the results of race/ethnic differences for Hispanic women may be biased. Moreover, while these findings were statistically and potentially medical meaningful, these results were regarded as hypothesis generating. Finally, we have only included the published baseline and the first 10 annual follow‐up visits. A full data set may thus be required for further validation.

CONCLUSIONS

In conclusion, we identified 2 distinct developmental trajectories of lipid parameters during the MT by LCGMM, the inverse U‐shape and the progressing patterns. Furthermore, the existence of at least 1 trajectory in each blood lipid parameter suggested a contribution of menopause. Blood lipids have started to rise 5 years before menopause, which emphasizes the need for earlier and continuous surveillance of lipid profiles over the MT. Furthermore, MT‐related factors including FSH, estradiol, age at menopause, and VMS predicted lipids pattern and level over the MT. The biological mechanisms of the impact of MT on blood lipid profile should be focused on in future studies.
