Mixtures of Transparent Local Models
Niffa Cheick Oumar Diaby niffa-cheick-oumar.diaby.1@ulaval.ca, Thierry Duchesne thierry.duchesne@mat.ulaval.ca, Mario Marchand mario.marchand@ift.ulaval.ca

Abstract
The predominance of machine learning models in many spheres of human activity has led to a growing demand for their transparency. The transparency of models makes it possible to discern some factors, such as security or non-discrimination. In this paper, we propose a mixture of transparent local models as an alternative solution for designing interpretable (or transparent) models. Our approach is designed for the situations where a simple and transparent function is suitable for modeling the label of instances in some localities/regions of the input space, but may change abruptly as we move from one locality to another. Consequently, the proposed algorithm is to learn both the transparent labeling function and the locality of the input space where the labeling function achieves a small risk in its assigned locality. By using a new multi-predictor (and multi-locality) loss function, we established rigorous PAC-Bayesian risk bounds for the case of binary linear classification problem and that of linear regression. In both cases, synthetic data sets were used to illustrate how the learning algorithms work. The results obtained from real data sets highlight the competitiveness of our approach compared to other existing methods as well as certain opaque models.

Introduction

Interpretable machine learning models are important for fairness testing, causality assessment and reliability or robustness screening. This is especially true in high-stakes sectors such as finance, transportation and health care. Consequently, several state laws have emerged to regulate the use of machine learning models and enforce some level of interpretability and transparancy such as Law

(

Following these growing attentions, two main strategies have been developed to address transparency and explainability issues.

The first is the use of intrinsic interpretable model design techniques. Intrinsic interpretable models are prediction models whose decision-making process is understandable by humans without the need of any additional tool or algorithm. Example of models that belong to this family are generalized linear models (GLMs) (linear regression, log-linear model, etc.)

(

The second solution is to resort to methods for explaining complex models, also called black box models. These methods usually rely on transparent models to explain locally or globally non-transparent models. They include, in particular, instruments such as TREPAN
(

In this paper, we therefore propose to focus on alternative solutions for designing interpretable models. Our solution is based on the use of multiple model techniques to design a
mixture of transparent local models
. Succinctly, a mixture of transparent local models divides the problem space into several subspaces in order to learn interpretable predictors (transparent local models) in the different regions. Instead of designing a single complex model, which is potentially difficult to interpret, it might be more interesting to build simple and interpretable local models around certain points of interest. A point of interest can be an instance for which we want to predict the label based on similar instances present in the training samples.

Related Work:

Multiple model techniques in interpretable machine learning involve combining two or more models to increase performance gain or interpretability. Here are some works based on this approach.

45
proposed Oblique Treed Sparse Additive Models (OT-SpAMs) which divide the input space into regions with sparse oblique tree splitting and assign local sparse additive experts to individual regions. Other works are Hybrid Interpretable Models
(
31
;
46
;
15
)
, which are systems that involve the cooperation of a transparent model and a complex black box. At inference time, any input of the hybrid model is assigned to either its interpretable or complex component based on a gating mechanism.
43
proposed The League of Experts. They adapt a particular case of ensemble learning, in which each ensemble member is associated with a specific part of the input space. More specifically, they introduce an algorithm that adaptively learns a partition of the input space in order to assign one specialized ensemble member—the expert—to each subset of the partition.

Our work differs from
45
in that the division of the input space is carried out around certain points of interest instead of using decision trees or some other mechanism of partitionning the input space without overlap. It is clear that our proposal is totally different from Hybrid Interpretable Models, since it doesn’t necessitate the collaboration of a transparent model and a complex black box. As for
43
, they divide the input space around some points of interest without overlaps. In contrast we do not use a partition and tolerate overlaps. Finally, we also study the case where the points of interest are given by the user (such as the typical clients of an insurance company) as well as the case where the points of interest must be learned.

2
PAC-Bayesian Machine Learning

In this section, we present the PAC-Bayesian setting that provides guarantees (in the form of risk bounds) and the associated learning algorithm that optimizes these risk bounds and that will be used subsequently to construct mixtures of transparent local models.

2.1
Basic Notions of Machine Learning

Consider a data-generating distribution
{\mathcal{D}}
on
{\mathcal{X}}\times{\mathcal{Y}}
where
{\mathcal{X}}
is the instance (or input) space and where
{\mathcal{Y}}
is the label space. We assume that each training and testing example
({\mathbf{x}},y)\in{\mathcal{X}}\times{\mathcal{Y}}
is generated according to
{\mathcal{D}}
so that a training sample
S\overset{{\rm\mbox{\tiny def}}}{=}\{({\mathbf{x}}_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{m}
of
m
examples is drawn according to
{\mathcal{D}}^{m}
. We also consider learning algorithms that select a predictor among a class
{\mathcal{H}}
of available functions
h:{\mathcal{X}}\to{\mathcal{Y}}
. Moreover, we are given a loss function
\ell:{\mathcal{Y}}\times{\mathcal{Y}}\to{\mathds{R}}_{+}
such that
\ell(h({\mathbf{x}}),y)
gives the loss incurred by
h
on example
({\mathbf{x}},y)
. Given a training sample
S
, the goal of the learning algorithm is to find a predictor
h\in\mathcal{H}
that minimizes the true risk
L_{\mathcal{D}}(h)
defined as the expected loss according to
{\mathcal{D}}
:

L_{{\mathcal{D}}}(h)\overset{{\rm\mbox{\tiny def}}}{=}\underset{({\mathbf{x}},y)\sim{\mathcal{D}}}{{\mathds{E}}}\left[\ell(h({\mathbf{x}}),y)\right]\,\textrm{.}

This quantity is usually impossible to calculate since
{\mathcal{D}}
is unknown. So, instead, the learning algorithm uses the empirical risk
L_{S}(h)
of
h
evaluated on
S
, defined by

L_{S}(h)\overset{{\rm\mbox{\tiny def}}}{=}\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\ell(h({\mathbf{x}}_{i}),y_{i})\,\textrm{.}

Note that in this paper, the input space
{\mathcal{X}}
represents the feature space.

2.2
PAC-Bayesian Risk Bounds

The PAC-Bayes approach, introduced by
25
, is a tool to produce probably approximately correct (PAC) guarantees for learning algorithms in the form of tight risk bounds. In this framework, given a loss function
\ell
and a class
{\mathcal{H}}
of predictors, a learning algorithm
A_{{\mathcal{H}}}
receives, as input, a sample
S
of
m
examples and a
prior
distribution
P
on
{\mathcal{H}}
and returns a
posterior
distribution
Q
on
{\mathcal{H}}
.

The effectiveness of training is captured by the accuracy of the predictors
h
drawn according to
Q
. More formally, the loss incurred over an example
({\mathbf{x}},y)
is defined as

\ell(Q,({\mathbf{x}},y))\overset{{\rm\mbox{\tiny def}}}{=}\underset{h\sim Q}{{\mathds{E}}}\left[\ell(h({\mathbf{x}}),y)\right]\,\textrm{.}

The true risk is meanwhile expressed by

L_{{\mathcal{D}}}(Q)\overset{{\rm\mbox{\tiny def}}}{=}\underset{({\mathbf{x}},y)\sim{\mathcal{D}}}{{\mathds{E}}}\left[\ell(Q,({\mathbf{x}},y))\right]=\underset{h\sim Q}{{\mathds{E}}}L_{{\mathcal{D}}}(h)\,\textrm{.}

The empirical risk evaluated on the sample
S
is defined as

L_{S}(Q)\overset{{\rm\mbox{\tiny def}}}{=}\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\ell(Q,({\mathbf{x}}_{i},y_{i}))=\underset{h\sim Q}{{\mathds{E}}}L_{S}(h)\,\textrm{.}

As the PAC-Bayes framework returns a stochastic predictor called the
Gibbs predictor
, the risks
L_{{\mathcal{D}}}(Q)
and
L_{S}(Q)
are often called, respectively, the true risk of Gibbs and the empirical risk of Gibbs. The PAC generalization bounds established via PAC-Bayes approach depend on the Kullback-Leibler divergence between the
posterior

Q
and the
prior

P
.

Definition 1
.

The Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence is a measure of dissimilarity between two probability distributions. Let
p(h)
and
q(h)
be the probability densities of
P
and
Q
at point
h\in\mathcal{H}
. Note that
Q
is absolutely continuous with respect to
P
, denoted by
Q\ll P
if and only if

\forall h\in{\mathcal{H}}:p(h)=0\implies q(h)=0\,\textrm{.}

For any distributions
Q
and
P
on
{\mathcal{H}}
, the KL divergence between
Q
and
P
, denoted

KL(Q||P)
, is defined as

KL(Q||P)\overset{{\rm\mbox{\tiny def}}}{=}\begin{cases}\underset{h\sim Q}{{\mathds{E}}}\ln\left[\frac{q(h)}{p(h)}\right]&\text{if $Q\ll P$}\\
\infty&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

The following theorem can be considered as a “core” of the PAC-Bayes approach, since many PAC-Bayes generalization bounds can be retrieved through this theorem.

Theorem 2
.

(
24
)

Consider some distribution
{\mathcal{D}}
over the space
{\mathcal{X}}\times{\mathcal{Y}}
, a class of predictors
{\mathcal{H}}:{\mathcal{X}}\rightarrow{\mathcal{Y}}
, and a prior distribution
P
on
{\mathcal{H}}
. Let
\phi:{\mathds{R}}_{+}\rightarrow{\mathds{R}}_{+}
, and
f:{\mathcal{H}}\times({\mathcal{X}}\times{\mathcal{Y}})^{m}\rightarrow{\mathds{R}}
be mesurable functions satisfaying

\forall\lambda>0,\forall h\in{\mathcal{H}}:\;\underset{S\sim{\mathcal{D}}^{m}}{{\mathds{E}}}e^{\lambda f(h,S)}\leq\phi(\lambda)\,\textrm{.}

(1)

Then, for any
\lambda>0
and for any
\delta\in(0,1)
, with probability at least
1-\delta
over the draws of
S\sim{\mathcal{D}}^{m}
, we have

\forall Q\ll P:\ \underset{h\sim Q}{{\mathds{E}}}f(h,S)\leq\frac{1}{\lambda}\left[KL(Q||P)+\ln\frac{\phi(\lambda)}{\delta}\right]\,\textrm{.}

(2)

As a consequence of this theorem, if we choose
f(h,S)=L_{{\mathcal{D}}}(h)-L_{S}(h)
, the previous upper bound on
\underset{h\sim Q}{\mathds{E}}f(h,S)
automatically gives an upper bound on
L_{{\mathcal{D}}}(Q)-L_{S}(Q)
. The same observation applies to
f(h,S)=L_{S}(h)-L_{{\mathcal{D}}}(h)
.

Throughout this paper, we will assume the loss function
\ell
is
{\sigma}
-sub Gaussian, which implies that we have

\forall h\in{\mathcal{H}},\forall\lambda\in{\mathds{R}},\;\underset{S\sim{\mathcal{D}}^{m}}{{\mathds{E}}}e^{\lambda\left[L_{{\mathcal{D}}}(h)-L_{S}(h)\right]}\leq e^{\frac{\lambda^{2}{\sigma}^{2}}{2m}}=\phi(\lambda)\,\textrm{.}

(3)

By using the union bound on the upper bounds of
L_{{\mathcal{D}}}(Q)-L_{S}(Q)
and
L_{S}(Q)-L_{{\mathcal{D}}}(Q)
, we obtain the following corollary, which is similar to a result of
16
.

Corollary 3
.

For any distribution
{\mathcal{D}}
on
{\mathcal{X}}\times{\mathcal{Y}}
, any distribution
P
on
{\mathcal{H}}
, any
{\sigma}
-sub Gaussian loss function
\ell
, any
\lambda>0
, and any
\delta\in(0,1)
, with probability at least
1-\delta
over the draws of
S\sim{\mathcal{D}}^{m}
, we have

\forall Q\ll P:\ |L_{{\mathcal{D}}}(Q)-L_{S}(Q)|\leq\frac{1}{\lambda}\left[KL(Q||P)+\ln\frac{2}{\delta}\right]+\frac{\lambda{\sigma}^{2}}{2m}\,\textrm{.}

2.3
The PAC-Bayesian Learning Algorithm

According to the Corollary
3
, given a training set
S
, a
prior
distribution
P
on a class
{\mathcal{H}}
of predictors, and
\lambda>0
, the learning algorithm that optimizes the PAC-Bayesian guarantee on
L_{{\mathcal{D}}}(Q)
is given by

\mathbf{PB}_{{\mathcal{H}}}^{\lambda}(S)\overset{{\rm\mbox{\tiny def}}}{=}\underset{Q\ll P}{\mathrm{argmin}}\Biggl[L_{S}(Q)+\frac{1}{\lambda}KL(Q||P)\Biggr]\,\textrm{.}

Thus, the learning algorithm
\mathbf{PB}_{{\mathcal{H}}}^{\lambda}(S)
aims to minimize the empirical risk
L_{S}(Q)
and the complexity term
\frac{1}{\lambda}KL(Q||P)
. In the following, we will propose such learning algorithms for parametrized
posterior
distributions on mixtures of interpretable local models.

3
Basic Elements for Mixtures of Transparent Local Models

Our approach is based on the fact that the label of instances that are close to some instance or point of interest should be predictable by a simple model. Consequently, we will try to train simple models only on the instances that are within some distance to a reference point. For this task, we will make use of a vicinity function which assigns a value of
1
to the instances that are within some distance to a reference point and
0
to all other instances. Hence, the simple models will be trained on instances located within a ball with respect to a metric which should be compatible with our notion of similarity or dissimilarity among pair of instances. This is to be contrasted with other approaches that try to find a partition of the input space, such as the one obtained by a decision tree or a tiling of Voronoïd cells, which are not balls defined with respect to some metric compatible with a notion of similarity between instances.

3.1
Vicinity Function

Given some metric
d:{\mathcal{X}}\times{\mathcal{X}}\to{\mathds{R}}_{+}
, the measure of vicinity between a point of interest
{\mathbf{c}}
and some given instance
{\mathbf{x}}
is captured by the vicinity function
K({\mathbf{c}},{\mathbf{x}},{\beta})
defined as

K({\mathbf{c}},{\mathbf{x}},{\beta})\overset{{\rm\mbox{\tiny def}}}{=}\begin{cases}1&\text{if $d({\mathbf{c}},{\mathbf{x}})\leq{\beta}$}\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

(4)

where
{\beta}\in{\mathds{R}}_{+}
denotes the locality parameter. Then, the complement of
K({\mathbf{c}},{\mathbf{x}},{\beta})
, denoted
\bar{K}({\mathbf{c}},{\mathbf{x}},{\beta})
, is defined as

\bar{K}({\mathbf{c}},{\mathbf{x}},{\beta})\overset{{\rm\mbox{\tiny def}}}{=}1-K({\mathbf{c}},{\mathbf{x}},{\beta})\,\textrm{.}

(5)

As is often the case, we consider that the locality parameter
\beta
belongs to a set
\tilde{{\mathcal{B}}}\subseteq\mathds{R}_{+}
. Thus, the set
\tilde{\mathcal{B}}
parameterizes the set of vicinity functions available to us.

3.2
A Loss Function Defined on a Set of Models and Localities

Consider
n
spaces of points of interest
\{{\mathcal{C}}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
and let
\bm{c}\overset{{\rm\mbox{\tiny def}}}{=}({\mathbf{c}}_{1},\dots,{\mathbf{c}}_{n})\in{\mathcal{C}}_{1}\times\dots\times{\mathcal{C}}_{n}
denote the vector containing
n
points of interest. Then, consider
n+1
classes of predictors
{\mathcal{H}}_{1},\cdots,{\mathcal{H}}_{n}
,
{\mathcal{H}}_{ext}
, and some loss function
\ell(h({\mathbf{x}}),y)
that quantify the loss incured by
h
on example
({\mathbf{x}},y)
. Let
\bm{h}
denote the vector containing the
n
predictors
(h_{1},\cdots,h_{n})\in{\mathcal{H}}_{1}\times\cdots\times{\mathcal{H}}_{n}
.

Consider
K({\mathbf{c}},{\mathbf{x}},{\beta})
a vicinity function and
{\beta}\in\tilde{{\mathcal{B}}}
. Let us denote by
\boldsymbol{{\beta}}
the vector containing
({\beta}_{1},\cdots,{\beta}_{n})\in\tilde{{\mathcal{B}}}_{1}\times\cdots\times\tilde{{\mathcal{B}}}_{n}
. Thus, for all
(\bm{c},\bm{h},\boldsymbol{{\beta}},h_{ext})\in\{{\mathcal{C}}_{i}\times{\mathcal{H}}_{i}\times\tilde{{\mathcal{B}}}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}\times{\mathcal{H}}_{ext}
, we propose the following loss function:

\ell\Bigl[\bm{c},\bm{h},\boldsymbol{{\beta}},h_{ext},({\mathbf{x}},y)\Bigr]\overset{{\rm\mbox{\tiny def}}}{=}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Bigl[\ell(h_{i}({\mathbf{x}}),y)K({\mathbf{c}}_{i},{\mathbf{x}},{\beta}_{i})\Bigr]+\ell(h_{ext}({\mathbf{x}}),y)\prod_{i=1}^{n}\bar{K}({\mathbf{c}}_{i},{\mathbf{x}},{\beta}_{i})\,\textrm{.}

(6)

Three scenarios are taken into account when we formulate the loss as in (
6
):

•

The first scenario is the case where the instance
{\mathbf{x}}
only falls into one locality
{\beta}_{r}
with
1\leq r\leq n
, which means
K({\mathbf{c}}_{i},{\mathbf{x}},{\beta}_{i})=1
when
i=r
and
K({\mathbf{c}}_{i},{\mathbf{x}},{\beta}_{i})=0
for
i\neq r
. In this case, the loss depends only of the output of predictor
h_{r}
.

•

The second scenario takes care of the situation where the instance
{\mathbf{x}}
doesn’t belong to any locality, which means
\forall\;i\in[n],K({\mathbf{c}}_{i},{\mathbf{x}},{\beta}_{i})=0\Leftrightarrow\prod_{i=1}^{n}\bar{K}({\mathbf{c}}_{i},{\mathbf{x}},{\beta}_{i})=1
. In this case the loss depends only on the output of
h_{ext}
.

•

The last scenario concerns the case where the instance
{\mathbf{x}}
falls into several localities at the same time. In this circumstance, the loss depends on the output of all these predictors and we have zero loss only if each of them predicts the correct label
y
.

Consequently, this (uncommon) multi-predictor loss function penalizes contradictory predictions in overlapping neighborhoods given by the vicinity functions. However, this loss does not penalize overlapping neighborhoods when all the corresponding predictors are accurate in their neighborhoods. Finally, note that nothing in the current model specifies what to predict when a test instance falls into several (overlapping) neighborhoods with contradictory predictions. We feel that in such ambiguous cases, it is mostly important to specify the ambiguity and leave the decision of what to predict to the user.

In the PAC-Bayesian learning setting, we have to provide on each parameter class
\mathcal{C}_{i}
,
\mathcal{H}_{i}
,
\tilde{\mathcal{B}}_{i}
and
\mathcal{H}_{ext}
, some
prior

P
and
posterior

Q
distributions. For
priors
, we have,
\forall\;i\in[n]
:
\{\mathbf{c}_{i},h_{i},\beta_{i}\}\sim\{P_{\mathcal{C}_{i}},P_{\mathcal{H}_{i}},P_{\tilde{\mathcal{B}}_{i}}\}
and
h_{ext}\sim P_{\mathcal{H}_{ext}}
. With
posteriors
, we have,
\forall\;i\in[n]
:
\{\mathbf{c}_{i},h_{i},\beta_{i}\}\sim\{Q_{\mathcal{C}_{i}},Q_{\mathcal{H}_{i}},Q_{\tilde{\mathcal{B}}_{i}}\}
and
h_{ext}\sim Q_{\mathcal{H}_{ext}}
. All of these elements allow us to define the loss

\ell\Bigl[Q,(\mathbf{x},y)\Bigr]\overset{{\rm\mbox{\tiny def}}}{=}\underset{Q}{\mathds{E}}\left\{\ell\Bigl[\bm{c},\bm{h},\boldsymbol{\beta},h_{ext},(\mathbf{x},y)\Bigr]\right\}\,,

(7)

where
Q\overset{{\rm\mbox{\tiny def}}}{=}\left(\{Q_{\mathcal{C}_{i}},Q_{\mathcal{H}_{i}},Q_{\tilde{\mathcal{B}}_{i}}\}_{i=1}^{n},Q_{\mathcal{H}_{ext}}\right)
.

The same loss can also be applied to the prior
P\overset{{\rm\mbox{\tiny def}}}{=}\left(\{P_{\mathcal{C}_{i}},P_{\mathcal{H}_{i}},P_{\tilde{\mathcal{B}}_{i}}\}_{i=1}^{n},P_{\mathcal{H}_{ext}}\right)
.

3.3
Risk Function

From the loss function (
7
), for all
(\bm{c},\bm{h},\boldsymbol{\beta},h_{ext})\in\{\mathcal{C}_{i}\times\mathcal{H}_{i}\times\tilde{\mathcal{B}}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}\times\mathcal{H}_{ext}
, the goal is to find the vector
\bm{c}
of
n
points of interest, the vector
\bm{h}
of predictors, the vector
\boldsymbol{\beta}
of locality parameters, and the external predictor
h_{ext}
which minimize the risk
L_{\mathcal{D}}(Q)
defined as

L_{\mathcal{D}}(Q)\overset{{\rm\mbox{\tiny def}}}{=}\underset{(\mathbf{x},y)\sim\mathcal{D}}{\mathds{E}}\Biggl\{\ell\Bigl[Q,(\mathbf{x},y)\Bigr]\Biggr\}\;\textrm{.}

(8)

Then, the empirical risk is given by

L_{S}(Q)\overset{{\rm\mbox{\tiny def}}}{=}\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\Biggl\{\ell\Bigl[Q,(\mathbf{x}_{j},y_{j})\Bigr]\Biggr\}\;\textrm{.}

(9)

3.4
The Kullback-Leibler (KL) Divergence Between Q and P

In this paper, we consider
1
1

1

A priori, we have no reason to consider the more complicated case where there would be association/correlation between
\left\{\mathbf{c}_{i},h_{i},\beta_{i}\right\}_{i=1}^{n},h_{ext}
.
that the
priors
and
posteriors
respectively assigned to variables
\left\{\mathbf{c}_{i},h_{i},\beta_{i}\right\}_{i=1}^{n}
and
h_{ext}
are such that all these variables are independent. The global
KL
-divergence thus becomes a sum of
KL
-divergences for each random variable, i.e., we have

KL(Q||P)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Bigl[KL\left(Q_{\mathcal{C}_{i}}||P_{\mathcal{C}_{i}}\right)+KL\left(Q_{\mathcal{H}_{i}}||P_{\mathcal{H}_{i}}\right)+KL\left(Q_{\tilde{\mathcal{B}}_{i}}||P_{\tilde{\mathcal{B}}_{i}}\right)\Bigr]+KL\left(Q_{\mathcal{H}_{ext}}||P_{\mathcal{H}_{ext}}\right)\textrm{.}

(10)

It is important to note that this global
KL
-divergence will ensure that the expectations and variances of
posteriors
do not deviate too much from the corresponding values of the
priors
. Hence, combining the expression of
KL
from (
10
) with that of empirical risk
L_{\mathcal{S}}(Q)
, the PAC-Bayesian learning algorithm
\mathbf{PB_{\left\{\mathcal{C}_{i}\times\mathcal{H}_{i}\times\tilde{\mathcal{B}}_{i}\right\}_{i=1}^{n}\times\mathcal{H}_{ext}}^{\lambda}}(S)
which optimizes a PAC-Bayesian bound on
L_{\mathcal{D}}(Q)
is given by

\mathbf{PB_{\left\{\mathcal{C}_{i}\times\mathcal{H}_{i}\times\tilde{\mathcal{B}}_{i}\right\}_{i=1}^{n}\times\mathcal{H}_{ext}}^{\lambda}}(S)\overset{{\rm\mbox{\tiny def}}}{=}\underset{Q\ll P}{\mathrm{argmin}}\Biggl[L_{\mathcal{S}}(Q)+\frac{1}{\lambda}KL(Q||P)\Biggr]\textrm{.}

(11)

Equation (
11
) will return some quite accurate
posterior

Q
over all variables. Then, we will construct deterministic predictors using the means of the
posterior

Q
, which means that the variables
\left\{{\mathbf{c}}_{i},h_{i},{\beta}_{i}\right\}_{i=1}^{n}
and
h_{ext}
will be estimated by, respectively, the expectations
\left\{{\mathds{E}}_{Q_{{\mathcal{C}}_{i}}}\left[{\mathbf{c}}_{i}\right],{\mathds{E}}_{Q_{{\mathcal{H}}_{i}}}\left[h_{i}\right],{\mathds{E}}_{Q_{\tilde{{\mathcal{B}}}_{i}}}\left[{\beta}_{i}\right]\right\}_{i=1}^{n}
, and

3.6
Classes of Distributions

For each space of point of interest
\mathcal{C}_{i}=\mathds{R}^{d}
, we choose a multivariate Gaussian distribution
\mathcal{G}_{\mathcal{C}_{i}}\overset{{\rm\mbox{\tiny def}}}{=}\mathcal{N}\left({\mathbf{c}}_{i_{0}},\mathbf{I}_{i}{\varepsilon}_{i}^{2}\right)
with the expectation
{\mathbf{c}}_{i_{0}}\in\mathds{R}^{d}
, the identity matrix
\mathbf{I}_{i}\in\mathds{R}^{d\times d}
and the standard deviation
{\varepsilon}_{i}\in\mathds{R}_{+}
.

For each weight space
\mathcal{W}_{i}=\mathds{R}^{d}
, we also use a multivariate Gaussian distribution
\mathcal{G}_{\mathcal{W}_{i}}\overset{{\rm\mbox{\tiny def}}}{=}\mathcal{N}\left(\mathbf{w}_{i},\mathbf{I}_{i}\rho_{i}^{2}\right)
with expectation
\mathbf{w}_{i}\in\mathds{R}^{d}
and standard deviation
\rho_{i}\in\mathds{R}_{+}
. Similarly, for the space
\mathcal{W}_{ext}=\mathds{R}^{d}
, we have a distribution
\mathcal{G}_{\mathcal{W}_{ext}}\overset{{\rm\mbox{\tiny def}}}{=}\mathcal{N}\left(\mathbf{w}_{ext},\mathbf{I}_{ext}\rho_{ext}^{2}\right)
.

For each class of biases
\mathcal{B}_{i}=\mathds{R}
, we choose a univariate Gaussian distribution
\mathcal{G}_{\mathcal{B}_{i}}\overset{{\rm\mbox{\tiny def}}}{=}\mathcal{N}\left(\mu_{i},\sigma_{i}^{2}\right)
with expectation
\mu_{i}\in\mathds{R}
and standard deviation
\sigma_{i}\in\mathds{R}_{+}
. Similarly, for the bias space
\mathcal{B}_{ext}=\mathds{R}
, we use a univariate Gaussian distribution
\mathcal{G}_{\mathcal{B}_{ext}}\overset{{\rm\mbox{\tiny def}}}{=}\mathcal{N}\left(\mu_{ext},\sigma_{ext}^{2}\right)
.

Finally, for each set of vicinity function parameters
{\tilde{\mathcal{B}}}_{i}=\mathds{R}_{+}
, we choose a Gamma distribution
\mathcal{G}_{{\tilde{\mathcal{B}}}_{i}}\overset{{\rm\mbox{\tiny def}}}{=}\Gamma\left(k_{i},\tau_{i}\right)
with shape
k_{i}\in\mathds{R}_{+}^{*}
and rate
\tau_{i}\in\mathds{R}_{+}^{*}
. Recall that the probability density function of a Gamma distribution
\Gamma\left(k,\tau\right)
is given by

q_{k,\tau}(\beta)=\frac{\beta^{k-1}\tau^{k}}{\Gamma(k)}e^{-\tau\beta},\;\beta\in\mathds{R}_{+}\,\textrm{,}

where
\Gamma(\cdot)
denotes the Euler Gamma function defined as

\forall\;x>0,\quad\Gamma(x)=\int_{0}^{+\infty}t^{x-1}e^{-t}dt\,\textrm{.}

Furthermore, according to
14
;
41
, the KL divergence between two multivariate Gaussian distributions in

\mathds{R}^{d}
, denoted by
KL\left[\mathcal{G}_{\mathcal{W}_{1}}||\mathcal{G}_{\mathcal{W}_{2}}\right]\overset{{\rm\mbox{\tiny def}}}{=}KL\left[\mathcal{N}\left(\mathbf{w}_{1},\mathbf{I}_{1}\rho_{1}^{2}\right)||\mathcal{N}\left(\mathbf{w}_{2},\mathbf{I}_{2}\rho_{2}^{2}\right)\right]
, is given by

KL\left[\mathcal{G}_{\mathcal{W}_{1}}||\mathcal{G}_{\mathcal{W}_{2}}\right]=\frac{1}{2}\Biggl[\ln{\left\{\left(\frac{\rho_{2}^{2}}{\rho_{1}^{2}}\right)^{d}\right\}}-d+d\left(\frac{\rho_{1}^{2}}{\rho_{2}^{2}}\right)+\frac{1}{\rho_{2}^{2}}(\mathbf{w}_{2}-\mathbf{w}_{1})^{T}(\mathbf{w}_{2}-\mathbf{w}_{1})\Biggr]\,\textrm{,}

(12)

and that between two Gamma distributions, denoted by
KL\left[\mathcal{G}_{{\tilde{\mathcal{B}}}_{1}}||\mathcal{G}_{{\tilde{\mathcal{B}}}_{2}}\right]\overset{{\rm\mbox{\tiny def}}}{=}

KL\left[\Gamma\left(k_{1},\tau_{1}\right)||\Gamma\left(k_{2},\tau_{2}\right)\right]
, is given by

KL\left[\mathcal{G}_{{\tilde{\mathcal{B}}}_{1}}||\mathcal{G}_{{\tilde{\mathcal{B}}}_{2}}\right]=(k_{1}-k_{2})\psi(k_{1})+\ln\left(\frac{\Gamma(k_{2})}{\Gamma(k_{1})}\right)+k_{2}\ln\left(\frac{\tau_{1}}{\tau_{2}}\right)+k_{1}\frac{\tau_{2}-\tau_{1}}{\tau_{1}}\,\textrm{,}

(13)

where
\psi
designates the Digamma function defined as

\forall\;x>0,\quad\psi(x)\overset{{\rm\mbox{\tiny def}}}{=}\frac{d}{dx}\ln[\Gamma(x)]=\frac{\Gamma^{{}^{\prime}}(x)}{\Gamma(x)}\,\textrm{.}

Remark 4
.

The simplicity and interpretability of the internal and external predictors of our mixture of transparent models are the focus of this paper. However, it is possible to design, using exactly the same approach, a mixture of opaque local models. To do this, just consider some feature space
\phi:{\mathcal{X}}\rightarrow\mathds{R}^{p}
for some, possibly very large, feature-space dimension
p
. So instead of
{\mathbf{x}}
and
{\mathbf{c}}
, we would have
\phi({\mathbf{x}})
and
\phi({\mathbf{c}})
. The set of predictors to consider would be such that their output on any input
{\mathbf{x}}
would be given by some function of
\langle\mathbf{w},\phi({\mathbf{x}})\rangle
. Thanks to the representer theorem, we then have that
{\mathbf{w}}=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\alpha_{j}\phi({\mathbf{x}}_{j})
for some
(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{m})
and, consequently,
\langle\mathbf{w},\phi({\mathbf{x}})\rangle=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\alpha_{j}K({\mathbf{x}}_{j},{\mathbf{x}})
where
K({\mathbf{x}}_{j},{\mathbf{x}})=\langle\phi({\mathbf{x}}_{j}),\phi({\mathbf{x}})\rangle
is the kernel associated to the feature space. As for the geometry of the localities, it would also be determined by the choice of the metric
d({\mathbf{c}},{\mathbf{x}})
where
\|\phi({\mathbf{c}}),\phi({\mathbf{x}})\|
is one possible choice. In that last case
d({\mathbf{c}},{\mathbf{x}})
is also expressible using kernels. This implies that much of what we do allows the use of kernels at the cost of a substantial reduction in interpretability, but we not investigate this research direction in this paper.

\displaystyle\times\left\{\left\|{\mathbf{x}}\right\|^{2}\rho_{ext}^{2}+{\sigma}_{ext}^{2}+\Bigl(\langle{\mathbf{w}}_{ext},{\mathbf{x}}\rangle+\mu_{ext}-y\Bigr)^{2}\right\}\textrm{.}

The expression of

of (

) yields an expression of the true risk

and of the empirical risk

given by (

) and (

). Combining that empirical risk

with the obtained closed-form expression of the

As in the Subsection

, the minimization objective is non convex in the parameters. Hence, several restarts at randomly selected positions are needed to explore the parameter space with some success.

5.3
Model Training

5.3.1
Non-Convex Problems and Softwares

Unlike the convex optimization problems, non-convex problems do not guarantee a convergence to a global minimum due to the presence of multiple local minima, saddle points, and regions of flat curvature during their optimization process. This has led in literature, to the development of specialized optimization algorithms aimed at improving convergence to useful minima in non-convex settings. Optimization techniques such as stochastic gradient descent (SGD) and its adaptive variants like Adam
(
19
)
, AdaGrad
(
13
)
, NAdam
(
12
)
and RMSProp
(
42
)
are some examples. In this paper, we will simply use the NAdam optimization technique with a number of random restarts to solve optimization problems (
19
), (
22
), (
44
), and (
47
).

5.3.2
Randomly Splitting of Data

For learning purpose, each data set is randomly partitioned into a training set, a validation set and a test set; with ratios of
70\%
,
15\%
and
15\%
, respectively. The training set is used for learning the model, while the validation set is for tuning the regularization hyperparameter
\lambda
. The test set serves to evaluate the performance of the model. As many practitioners have observed that the best
\lambda
would generally be
\lambda^{{}^{\prime}}\times m\_train
, where
m\_train
is the training set sample size and
\lambda^{{}^{\prime}}
some unknown constant; by following this concept, tuning the regularization hyperparameter
\lambda
is equal of seeking the best value of the constant
\lambda^{{}^{\prime}}
. Our best value of the constant
\lambda^{{}^{\prime}}
will be chosen from the set of values
\{1,1.25,1.5,1.75,2,2.25,2.5,2.75,3,3.25,3.5,3.75,4,4.25,4.5,4.75,5\}
.

5.3.3
Choice of the Distance Metric

The correct choice of the distance metric
d({\mathbf{c}},{\mathbf{x}})
used in the vicinity function
K({\mathbf{c}},

{\mathbf{x}},

{\beta})
is crucial, because it will determine the shape of the locality that will be used for a predictor. More specifically, the metric
d({\mathbf{c}},{\mathbf{x}})
affects only the loss function of the
posterior

Q
through the vicinity function
K({\mathbf{c}},{\mathbf{x}},{\beta})
, and does not affect the KL divergence. Moreover, any metric
d({\mathbf{c}},{\mathbf{x}})
could be used for the computation of
\ell\Bigl[Q,(\boldsymbol{\psi},{\mathbf{x}},y)\Bigr]
and
\ell\Bigl[Q,({\mathbf{x}},y)\Bigr]
when the points of interests are given (Section
4
). However, this is not the case when the points of interest must be found (Section
5
) where the provided functions
\ell\Bigl[Q,(\boldsymbol{\psi},{\mathbf{x}},y)\Bigr]
and
\ell\Bigl[Q,({\mathbf{x}},y)\Bigr]
are valid only for the Euclidean metric. In all our experiments below, the Euclidean norm was used.

5.3.4
Total Number of Executions of the PAC-Bayesian Learning Algorithm

Note that as the number
n
of points of interest increases, the number of local minima increases. Therefore, for any random split of data into train-valid-test sets, a given value of
n
and a given value of
\lambda
(meaning here
\lambda^{{}^{\prime}}
), we do
10\times n
random restarts of the algorithm and select the best solution based on the numeric values of Equations (
19
), (
22
), (
44
) and (
47
). So, with a set containing
17
values of
\lambda
, we need
17\times 10\times n=170\times n
executions of the algorithm to complete the learning task. The best value of
\lambda
among the
17
values is chosen based on the performance of the deterministic predictors on the validation set.

7
Conclusion

We presented an alternative interpretable model design that contributes to the advancement of research focused on interpretability. Our proposal of mixtures of transparent local models with the linear model as the transparent model seems to be very relevant. We derived PAC-Bayesian guarantees in the scenario where the points of interest are given and also when they aren’t but only their number is given. In the first scenario, the provided guarantee is valid for any metric
d({\mathbf{c}},{\mathbf{x}})
. However, this is not the case in the second scenario, where the provided guarantee is valid for the Euclidean metric only.
Therefore, it would be interesting to examine in future work the possibility of introducing flexibility in the choice of the metric
d({\mathbf{c}},{\mathbf{x}})
in the case of unknown points of interest. Another extension of our work would be to use other types of transparent models instead of linear models.

Proof of the Expression of Loss Function in Section
4.1

This appendix aims to prove the expression of the loss
\ell\Bigl[Q,(\boldsymbol{\psi},{\mathbf{x}},y)\Bigr]
given by (
18
) in Section
4.1
. Starting from the definition of the loss
\ell\Bigl[Q,(\boldsymbol{\psi},{\mathbf{x}},y)\Bigr]
given by (
15
), we have

\displaystyle\ell\Bigl[Q,(\boldsymbol{\psi},{\mathbf{x}},y)\Bigr]

\displaystyle\overset{{\rm\mbox{\tiny def}}}{=}\underset{Q}{{\mathds{E}}}\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Biggl[\mathds{1}\left[\Bigl(\langle{\mathbf{v}}_{i},\boldsymbol{\psi}\rangle+yb_{i}\Bigr)\leq 0\right]K({\mathbf{c}}_{i},{\mathbf{x}},{\beta}_{i})\Biggr]\right\}

(48)

\displaystyle+\underset{Q}{{\mathds{E}}}\left\{\mathds{1}\left[\Bigl(\langle{\mathbf{v}}_{ext},\boldsymbol{\psi}\rangle+yb_{ext}\Bigr)\leq 0\right]\prod_{i=1}^{n}\bar{K}({\mathbf{c}}_{i},{\mathbf{x}},{\beta}_{i})\right\}\textrm{,}

where
\boldsymbol{\psi}\overset{{\rm\mbox{\tiny def}}}{=}y{\mathbf{x}}
.

Knowing that the predictors’s parameters
\left\{{\mathbf{v}}_{i},b_{i}\right\}_{i=1}^{n}
and their corresponding locality parameters
\left\{{\beta}_{i}\right\}_{i=1}^{n}
are all independent, Equation (
48
) becomes

\displaystyle\ell\Bigl[Q,(\boldsymbol{\psi},{\mathbf{x}},y)\Bigr]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Biggl[\underset{Q}{{\mathds{E}}}\left\{\mathds{1}\left[\Bigl(\langle{\mathbf{v}}_{i},\boldsymbol{\psi}\rangle+yb_{i}\Bigr)\leq 0\right]K({\mathbf{c}}_{i},{\mathbf{x}},{\beta}_{i})\right\}\Biggr]

(49)

\displaystyle+\underset{Q}{{\mathds{E}}}\left\{\mathds{1}\left[\Bigl(\langle{\mathbf{v}}_{ext},\boldsymbol{\psi}\rangle+yb_{ext}\Bigr)\leq 0\right]\prod_{i=1}^{n}\bar{K}({\mathbf{c}}_{i},{\mathbf{x}},{\beta}_{i})\right\}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[\underset{({\mathbf{v}}_{i},b_{i})\sim\left\{Q_{{\mathbf{w}}_{i},{\mathbf{I}}_{i}}\times Q_{\mu_{i},{\sigma}_{i}^{2}}\right\}}{{\mathds{E}}}\mathds{1}\left[\Bigl(\langle{\mathbf{v}}_{i},\boldsymbol{\psi}\rangle+yb_{i}\Bigr)\leq 0\right]\times\underset{{\beta}_{i}\sim Q_{k_{i},\tau_{i}}}{{\mathds{E}}}K({\mathbf{c}}_{i},{\mathbf{x}},{\beta}_{i})\right]

\displaystyle+\underset{({\mathbf{v}}_{ext},b_{ext})\sim\left\{Q_{{\mathbf{w}}_{ext},{\mathbf{I}}_{ext}}\times Q_{\mu_{ext},{\sigma}_{ext}^{2}}\right\}}{{\mathds{E}}}\mathds{1}\left[\Bigl(\langle{\mathbf{v}}_{ext},\boldsymbol{\psi}\rangle+yb_{ext}\Bigr)\leq 0\right]

\displaystyle\times\prod_{i=1}^{n}\left[\underset{{\beta}_{i}\sim Q_{k_{i},\tau_{i}}}{{\mathds{E}}}\bar{K}({\mathbf{c}}_{i},{\mathbf{x}},{\beta}_{i})\right]\textrm{.}

As defined in Subsection
4.1
, Equation (
17
), we know that

\underset{{\beta}_{i}\sim Q_{k_{i},\tau_{i}}}{{\mathds{E}}}K({\mathbf{c}}_{i},{\mathbf{x}},{\beta}_{i})=\underset{{\beta}_{i}\sim{\Gamma}\left(k_{i},\tau_{i}\right)}{{\mathds{E}}}\mathds{1}\Bigl[d({\mathbf{c}}_{i},{\mathbf{x}})\leq{\beta}_{i}\Bigr]\overset{{\rm\mbox{\tiny def}}}{=}\Theta_{k_{i},\tau_{i},d({\mathbf{c}}_{i},{\mathbf{x}})}\textrm{.}

Consequently, Equation (
49
) can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\ell\Bigl[Q,(\boldsymbol{\psi},{\mathbf{x}},y)\Bigr]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[\Theta_{k_{i},\tau_{i},d({\mathbf{c}}_{i},{\mathbf{x}})}\times\underset{({\mathbf{v}}_{i},b_{i})\sim\left\{Q_{{\mathbf{w}}_{i},{\mathbf{I}}_{i}}\times Q_{\mu_{i},{\sigma}_{i}^{2}}\right\}}{{\mathds{E}}}\mathds{1}\left[\Bigl(\langle{\mathbf{v}}_{i},\boldsymbol{\psi}\rangle+yb_{i}\Bigr)\leq 0\right]\right]

\displaystyle+\prod_{i=1}^{n}\left[1-\Theta_{k_{i},\tau_{i},d({\mathbf{c}}_{i},{\mathbf{x}})}\right]

\displaystyle\times\underset{({\mathbf{v}}_{ext},b_{ext})\sim\left\{Q_{{\mathbf{w}}_{ext},{\mathbf{I}}_{ext}}\times Q_{\mu_{ext},{\sigma}_{ext}^{2}}\right\}}{{\mathds{E}}}\mathds{1}\left[\Bigl(\langle{\mathbf{v}}_{ext},\boldsymbol{\psi}\rangle+yb_{ext}\Bigr)\leq 0\right]\textrm{.}

Let’s now consider two quantities
{\mathcal{A}}\left({\mathbf{w}}_{i},\mu_{i},{\sigma}_{i}\right)
and
{\mathcal{A}}\left({\mathbf{w}}_{ext},\mu_{ext},{\sigma}_{ext}\right)
such that

\displaystyle{\mathcal{A}}\left({\mathbf{w}}_{i},\mu_{i},{\sigma}_{i}\right)\overset{{\rm\mbox{\tiny def}}}{=}\underset{({\mathbf{v}}_{i},b_{i})\sim\left\{Q_{{\mathbf{w}}_{i},{\mathbf{I}}_{i}}\times Q_{\mu_{i},{\sigma}_{i}^{2}}\right\}}{{\mathds{E}}}\mathds{1}\left[\Bigl(\langle{\mathbf{v}}_{i},\boldsymbol{\psi}\rangle+yb_{i}\Bigr)\leq 0\right]\quad\textrm{and}

\displaystyle{\mathcal{A}}\left({\mathbf{w}}_{ext},\mu_{ext},{\sigma}_{ext}\right)\overset{{\rm\mbox{\tiny def}}}{=}\underset{({\mathbf{v}}_{ext},b_{ext})\sim\left\{Q_{{\mathbf{w}}_{ext},{\mathbf{I}}_{ext}}\times Q_{\mu_{ext},{\sigma}_{ext}^{2}}\right\}}{{\mathds{E}}}\mathds{1}\left[\Bigl(\langle{\mathbf{v}}_{ext},\boldsymbol{\psi}\rangle+yb_{ext}\Bigr)\leq 0\right]\textrm{.}

Hence, we obtain

\displaystyle\ell\Bigl[Q,(\boldsymbol{\psi},{\mathbf{x}},y)\Bigr]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[\Theta_{k_{i},\tau_{i},d({\mathbf{c}}_{i},{\mathbf{x}})}\times{\mathcal{A}}\left({\mathbf{w}}_{i},\mu_{i},{\sigma}_{i}\right)\right]

(50)

\displaystyle+\prod_{i=1}^{n}\left[1-\Theta_{k_{i},\tau_{i},d({\mathbf{c}}_{i},{\mathbf{x}})}\right]\times{\mathcal{A}}\left({\mathbf{w}}_{ext},\mu_{ext},{\sigma}_{ext}\right)\textrm{.}

Note that the expressions of
{\mathcal{A}}\left({\mathbf{w}}_{i},\mu_{i},{\sigma}_{i}\right)
and
{\mathcal{A}}\left({\mathbf{w}}_{ext},\mu_{ext},{\sigma}_{ext}\right)
differ only by the subscript notations
i
and
ext
. Thus, it is sufficient to obtain the analytical expression of
{\mathcal{A}}\left({\mathbf{w}}_{i},\mu_{i},{\sigma}_{i}\right)
to similarly deduce that of
{\mathcal{A}}\left({\mathbf{w}}_{ext},\mu_{ext},{\sigma}_{ext}\right)
. From definition, we have

\displaystyle{\mathcal{A}}\left({\mathbf{w}}_{i},\mu_{i},{\sigma}_{i}\right)

\displaystyle\overset{{\rm\mbox{\tiny def}}}{=}\underset{({\mathbf{v}}_{i},b_{i})\sim\left\{Q_{{\mathbf{w}}_{i},{\mathbf{I}}_{i}}\times Q_{\mu_{i},{\sigma}_{i}^{2}}\right\}}{{\mathds{E}}}\mathds{1}\left[\Bigl(\langle{\mathbf{v}}_{i},\boldsymbol{\psi}\rangle+yb_{i}\Bigr)\leq 0\right]

\displaystyle=\underset{b_{i}\sim Q_{\mu_{i},{\sigma}_{i}^{2}}}{{\mathds{E}}}\left[\underset{{\mathbf{v}}_{i}\sim Q_{{\mathbf{w}}_{i},{\mathbf{I}}_{i}}}{{\mathds{E}}}\mathds{1}\left[\Bigl(\langle{\mathbf{v}}_{i},\boldsymbol{\psi}\rangle+yb_{i}\Bigr)\leq 0\right]\right]\textrm{.}

Then, as
Q_{{\mathbf{w}}_{i},{\mathbf{I}}_{i}}\overset{{\rm\mbox{\tiny def}}}{=}{\mathcal{N}}\left({\mathbf{w}}_{i},{\mathbf{I}}_{i}\right)
, thus we have

\displaystyle{\mathcal{A}}\left({\mathbf{w}}_{i},\mu_{i},{\sigma}_{i}\right)

\displaystyle=\underset{b_{i}\sim Q_{\mu_{i},{\sigma}_{i}^{2}}}{{\mathds{E}}}\left[\underset{{\mathbf{v}}_{i}\sim{\mathcal{N}}\left({\mathbf{w}}_{i},{\mathbf{I}}_{i}\right)}{{\mathds{E}}}\mathds{1}\left[\Bigl(\langle{\mathbf{v}}_{i},\boldsymbol{\psi}\rangle+yb_{i}\Bigr)\leq 0\right]\right]

(51)

\displaystyle=\underset{b_{i}\sim Q_{\mu_{i},{\sigma}_{i}^{2}}}{{\mathds{E}}}\left[\int_{\mathds{R}^{d}}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\right)^{d}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\left\|{\mathbf{v}}_{i}-{\mathbf{w}}_{i}\right\|^{2}}\mathds{1}\left[\Bigl(\langle{\mathbf{v}}_{i},\boldsymbol{\psi}\rangle+yb_{i}\Bigr)\leq 0\right]d{\mathbf{v}}_{i}\right]\textrm{.}

Now, consider any non-zero vector
\boldsymbol{\psi}
. Let’s decompose the vector
{\mathbf{v}}_{i}
into its part
v_{i\parallel}
parallel to
\boldsymbol{\psi}
and its part
{\mathbf{v}}_{i\perp}
perpendicular to
\boldsymbol{\psi}
. We then have

{\mathbf{v}}_{i}=\left(v_{i\parallel},{\mathbf{v}}_{i\perp}\right)\textrm{.}

Furthermore, if we define
\psi\overset{{\rm\mbox{\tiny def}}}{=}\left\|\boldsymbol{\psi}\right\|=\left\|y{\mathbf{x}}\right\|=\left\|{\mathbf{x}}\right\|
we obtain the following

\langle{\mathbf{v}}_{i},\boldsymbol{\psi}\rangle=v_{i\parallel}\psi\textrm{.}

Similarly, let’s decompose the weight vector
{\mathbf{w}}_{i}
into
\left(w_{i\parallel},{\mathbf{w}}_{i\perp}\right)
constituted of parallel and perpendicular parts to
\boldsymbol{\psi}
. We then get

\left\|{\mathbf{v}}_{i}-{\mathbf{w}}_{i}\right\|^{2}=\left(v_{i\parallel}-w_{i\parallel}\right)^{2}+\left\|{\mathbf{v}}_{i\perp}-{\mathbf{w}}_{i\perp}\right\|^{2}\textrm{.}

With all these elements, Equation (
51
) becomes

\displaystyle{\mathcal{A}}\left({\mathbf{w}}_{i},\mu_{i},{\sigma}_{i}\right)

\displaystyle=\underset{b_{i}\sim Q_{\mu_{i},{\sigma}_{i}^{2}}}{{\mathds{E}}}\Biggl[\int_{\mathds{R}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\left(v_{i\parallel}-w_{i\parallel}\right)^{2}}\mathds{1}\left[\Bigl(v_{i\parallel}\psi+yb_{i}\Bigr)\leq 0\right]dv_{i\parallel}

\displaystyle\times\int_{\mathds{R}^{d-1}}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\right)^{d-1}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\left\|{\mathbf{v}}_{i\perp}-{\mathbf{w}}_{i\perp}\right\|^{2}}d{\mathbf{v}}_{i\perp}\Biggr]\textrm{.}

Since

\int_{\mathds{R}^{d-1}}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\right)^{d-1}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\left\|{\mathbf{v}}_{i\perp}-{\mathbf{w}}_{i\perp}\right\|^{2}}d{\mathbf{v}}_{i\perp}=1\textrm{,}

we obtain

\displaystyle{\mathcal{A}}\left({\mathbf{w}}_{i},\mu_{i},{\sigma}_{i}\right)

\displaystyle=\underset{b_{i}\sim Q_{\mu_{i},{\sigma}_{i}^{2}}}{{\mathds{E}}}\Biggl[\int_{\mathds{R}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\left(v_{i\parallel}-w_{i\parallel}\right)^{2}}\mathds{1}\left[\Bigl(v_{i\parallel}\psi+yb_{i}\Bigr)\leq 0\right]dv_{i\parallel}\Biggr]

(52)

\displaystyle=\underset{b_{i}\sim Q_{\mu_{i},{\sigma}_{i}^{2}}}{{\mathds{E}}}\Biggl[\int_{\mathds{R}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-\frac{1}{2}t^{2}}\mathds{1}\left[\Bigl\{(t+w_{i\parallel})\psi+yb_{i}\Bigr\}\leq 0\right]dt\Biggr]

(53)

\displaystyle=\underset{b_{i}\sim Q_{\mu_{i},{\sigma}_{i}^{2}}}{{\mathds{E}}}\Biggl[\underset{t\sim{\mathcal{N}}\left(0,1\right)}{{\mathds{E}}}\mathds{1}\left[\Bigl(\psi t+\langle{\mathbf{w}}_{i},\boldsymbol{\psi}\rangle+yb_{i}\Bigr)\leq 0\right]\Biggr]

(54)

\displaystyle=\underset{t\sim{\mathcal{N}}\left(0,1\right)}{{\mathds{E}}}\Biggl[\underset{b_{i}\sim Q_{\mu_{i},{\sigma}_{i}^{2}}}{{\mathds{E}}}\mathds{1}\left[\Bigl(\psi t+\langle{\mathbf{w}}_{i},\boldsymbol{\psi}\rangle+yb_{i}\Bigr)\leq 0\right]\Biggr]

(55)

\displaystyle=\underset{t\sim{\mathcal{N}}\left(0,1\right)}{{\mathds{E}}}\Biggl[\underset{b_{i}\sim{\mathcal{N}}\left(\mu_{i},{\sigma}_{i}^{2}\right)}{{\mathds{E}}}\mathds{1}\Bigl[yb_{i}\leq-\psi t-\langle{\mathbf{w}}_{i},\boldsymbol{\psi}\rangle\Bigr]\Biggr]\textrm{.}

(56)

Here, Equation (
53
) is a consequence of the change of variable
t\overset{{\rm\mbox{\tiny def}}}{=}v_{i\parallel}-w_{i\parallel}
, and (
54
) is derived by replacing
w_{i\parallel}
by
\frac{\langle{\mathbf{w}}_{i},\boldsymbol{\psi}\rangle}{\psi}
. Equation (
55
) results from the change in the order of expectation, and (
56
) follows from the definition of
Q_{\mu_{i},{\sigma}_{i}^{2}}\overset{{\rm\mbox{\tiny def}}}{=}{\mathcal{N}}\left(\mu_{i},{\sigma}_{i}^{2}\right)
.

Taking advantage of the fact that
y\in\left\{-1,+1\right\}
, Equation (
56
) can be reformulated as

\displaystyle{\mathcal{A}}\left({\mathbf{w}}_{i},\mu_{i},{\sigma}_{i}\right)

\displaystyle=\underset{t\sim{\mathcal{N}}\left(0,1\right)}{{\mathds{E}}}\Biggl[\underset{yb_{i}\sim{\mathcal{N}}\left(y\mu_{i},{\sigma}_{i}^{2}\right)}{{\mathds{E}}}\mathds{1}\Bigl[yb_{i}\leq-\psi t-\langle{\mathbf{w}}_{i},\boldsymbol{\psi}\rangle\Bigr]\Biggr]

(57)

\displaystyle=\underset{t\sim{\mathcal{N}}\left(0,1\right)}{{\mathds{E}}}\Biggl[\Phi\left(\frac{-\psi t-\langle{\mathbf{w}}_{i},\boldsymbol{\psi}\rangle-y\mu_{i}}{{\sigma}_{i}}\right)\Biggr]

\displaystyle=\underset{t\sim{\mathcal{N}}\left(0,1\right)}{{\mathds{E}}}\Biggl[\Phi\left(\frac{-\left\|{\mathbf{x}}\right\|t-y\left(\langle{\mathbf{w}}_{i},{\mathbf{x}}\rangle+\mu_{i}\right)}{{\sigma}_{i}}\right)\Biggr]

\displaystyle=\Phi\left(\frac{-y\left(\langle{\mathbf{w}}_{i},{\mathbf{x}}\rangle+\mu_{i}\right)}{\sqrt{{\sigma}_{i}^{2}+\left\|{\mathbf{x}}\right\|^{2}}}\right)

\displaystyle=1-\Phi\left(\frac{y\left(\langle{\mathbf{w}}_{i},{\mathbf{x}}\rangle+\mu_{i}\right)}{\sqrt{{\sigma}_{i}^{2}+\left\|{\mathbf{x}}\right\|^{2}}}\right)\textrm{,}

where
\Phi
is the cumulative distribution function (CDF) of the standard normal distribution.

Similarly, we obtain

{\mathcal{A}}\left({\mathbf{w}}_{ext},\mu_{ext},{\sigma}_{ext}\right)=1-\Phi\left(\frac{y\left(\langle{\mathbf{w}}_{ext},{\mathbf{x}}\rangle+\mu_{ext}\right)}{\sqrt{{\sigma}_{ext}^{2}+\left\|{\mathbf{x}}\right\|^{2}}}\right)\;\textrm{.}

(58)

Plugging (
57
) and (
58
) into (
50
) finally gives us

\displaystyle\ell\Bigl[Q,(\boldsymbol{\psi},{\mathbf{x}},y)\Bigr]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[\Theta_{k_{i},\tau_{i},d({\mathbf{c}}_{i},{\mathbf{x}})}\times\left\{1-\Phi\left(\frac{y(\langle{\mathbf{w}}_{i},{\mathbf{x}}\rangle+\mu_{i})}{\sqrt{{\sigma}_{i}^{2}+\left\|{\mathbf{x}}\right\|^{2}}}\right)\right\}\right]

\displaystyle+\prod_{i=1}^{n}\left[1-\Theta_{k_{i},\tau_{i},d({\mathbf{c}}_{i},{\mathbf{x}})}\right]\times\left\{1-\Phi\left(\frac{y(\langle{\mathbf{w}}_{ext},{\mathbf{x}}\rangle+\mu_{ext})}{\sqrt{{\sigma}_{ext}^{2}+\left\|{\mathbf{x}}\right\|^{2}}}\right)\right\}\textrm{.}

which completes the proof.

Appendix B
Proof of the Expression of Loss Function in Section
4.2

The proof of the loss
\ell\Bigl[Q,({\mathbf{x}},y)\Bigr]
of (
21
) in Section
4.2
closely follows the procedure carried out in Appendix
A
. The loss
\ell\Bigl[Q,({\mathbf{x}},y)\Bigr]
of posterior
Q
on example
({\mathbf{x}},y)
, by definition, is given by

\displaystyle\ell\Bigl[Q,({\mathbf{x}},y)\Bigr]

\displaystyle\overset{{\rm\mbox{\tiny def}}}{=}\underset{Q}{{\mathds{E}}}\left\{\ell\Bigl[\Bigl(\bm{c},\bm{v},\bm{b},\boldsymbol{\beta},{\mathbf{v}}_{ext},b_{ext}\Bigr),({\mathbf{x}},y)\Bigr]\right\}

(59)

\displaystyle=\underset{Q}{{\mathds{E}}}\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[\Bigl(\langle{\mathbf{v}}_{i},{\mathbf{x}}\rangle+b_{i}-y\Bigr)^{2}K({\mathbf{c}}_{i},{\mathbf{x}},{\beta}_{i})\right]\right\}

\displaystyle+\underset{Q}{{\mathds{E}}}\left\{\Bigl(\langle{\mathbf{v}}_{ext},{\mathbf{x}}\rangle+b_{ext}-y\Bigr)^{2}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\bar{K}({\mathbf{c}}_{i},{\mathbf{x}},{\beta}_{i})\right\}\textrm{.}

Since variables
\left\{{\mathbf{v}}_{i},b_{i},{\beta}_{i}\right\}_{i=1}^{n}
are all independent, therefore, Equation (
59
) becomes

\displaystyle\ell\Bigl[Q,({\mathbf{x}},y)\Bigr]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[\underset{Q}{{\mathds{E}}}\left\{\Bigl(\langle{\mathbf{v}}_{i},{\mathbf{x}}\rangle+b_{i}-y\Bigr)^{2}K({\mathbf{c}}_{i},{\mathbf{x}},{\beta}_{i})\right\}\right]

(60)

\displaystyle+\underset{Q}{{\mathds{E}}}\left\{\Bigl(\langle{\mathbf{v}}_{ext},{\mathbf{x}}\rangle+b_{ext}-y\Bigr)^{2}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\bar{K}({\mathbf{c}}_{i},{\mathbf{x}},{\beta}_{i})\right\}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[\underset{({\mathbf{v}}_{i},b_{i})\sim\left\{Q_{{\mathbf{w}}_{i},{\mathbf{I}}_{i}\rho_{i}^{2}}\times Q_{\mu_{i},{\sigma}_{i}^{2}}\right\}}{{\mathds{E}}}\Bigl(\langle{\mathbf{v}}_{i},{\mathbf{x}}\rangle+b_{i}-y\Bigr)^{2}\times\underset{{\beta}_{i}\sim Q_{k_{i},\tau_{i}}}{{\mathds{E}}}K({\mathbf{c}}_{i},{\mathbf{x}},{\beta}_{i})\right]

\displaystyle+\underset{({\mathbf{v}}_{ext},b_{ext})\sim\left\{Q_{{\mathbf{w}}_{ext},{\mathbf{I}}_{ext}\rho_{ext}^{2}}\times Q_{\mu_{ext},{\sigma}_{ext}^{2}}\right\}}{{\mathds{E}}}\Bigl(\langle{\mathbf{v}}_{ext},{\mathbf{x}}\rangle+b_{ext}-y\Bigr)^{2}

\displaystyle\times\prod_{i=1}^{n}\left[\underset{{\beta}_{i}\sim Q_{k_{i},\tau_{i}}}{{\mathds{E}}}\bar{K}({\mathbf{c}}_{i},{\mathbf{x}},{\beta}_{i})\right]\textrm{.}

Recall that from Subsection
4.1
, Equation (
17
), we already have

\underset{{\beta}_{i}\sim Q_{k_{i},\tau_{i}}}{{\mathds{E}}}K({\mathbf{c}}_{i},{\mathbf{x}},{\beta}_{i})=\underset{{\beta}_{i}\sim{\Gamma}\left(k_{i},\tau_{i}\right)}{{\mathds{E}}}\mathds{1}\Bigl[d({\mathbf{c}}_{i},{\mathbf{x}})\leq{\beta}_{i}\Bigr]\overset{{\rm\mbox{\tiny def}}}{=}\Theta_{k_{i},\tau_{i},d({\mathbf{c}}_{i},{\mathbf{x}})}\textrm{.}

Then, Equation (
60
) can be expressed as

\displaystyle\ell\Bigl[Q,({\mathbf{x}},y)\Bigr]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[\Theta_{k_{i},\tau_{i},d({\mathbf{c}}_{i},{\mathbf{x}})}\times\underset{({\mathbf{v}}_{i},b_{i})\sim\left\{Q_{{\mathbf{w}}_{i},{\mathbf{I}}_{i}\rho_{i}^{2}}\times Q_{\mu_{i},{\sigma}_{i}^{2}}\right\}}{{\mathds{E}}}\Bigl(\langle{\mathbf{v}}_{i},{\mathbf{x}}\rangle+b_{i}-y\Bigr)^{2}\right]

(61)

\displaystyle+\prod_{i=1}^{n}\left[1-\Theta_{k_{i},\tau_{i},d({\mathbf{c}}_{i},{\mathbf{x}})}\right]

\displaystyle\times\underset{({\mathbf{v}}_{ext},b_{ext})\sim\left\{Q_{{\mathbf{w}}_{ext},{\mathbf{I}}_{ext}\rho_{ext}^{2}}\times Q_{\mu_{ext},{\sigma}_{ext}^{2}}\right\}}{{\mathds{E}}}\Bigl(\langle{\mathbf{v}}_{ext},{\mathbf{x}}\rangle+b_{ext}-y\Bigr)^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[\Theta_{k_{i},\tau_{i},d({\mathbf{c}}_{i},{\mathbf{x}})}\times{\mathcal{B}}\left({\mathbf{w}}_{i},\rho_{i},\mu_{i},{\sigma}_{i}\right)\right]

\displaystyle+\prod_{i=1}^{n}\left[1-\Theta_{k_{i},\tau_{i},d({\mathbf{c}}_{i},{\mathbf{x}})}\right]\times{\mathcal{B}}\left({\mathbf{w}}_{ext},\rho_{ext},\mu_{ext},{\sigma}_{ext}\right)\textrm{,}

where the estimates
{\mathcal{B}}\left({\mathbf{w}}_{i},\rho_{i},\mu_{i},{\sigma}_{i}\right)
and
{\mathcal{B}}\left({\mathbf{w}}_{ext},\rho_{ext},\mu_{ext},{\sigma}_{ext}\right)
are defined as

{\mathcal{B}}\left({\mathbf{w}}_{i},\rho_{i},\mu_{i},{\sigma}_{i}\right)\overset{{\rm\mbox{\tiny def}}}{=}\underset{({\mathbf{v}}_{i},b_{i})\sim\left\{Q_{{\mathbf{w}}_{i},{\mathbf{I}}_{i}\rho_{i}^{2}}\times Q_{\mu_{i},{\sigma}_{i}^{2}}\right\}}{{\mathds{E}}}\Bigl(\langle{\mathbf{v}}_{i},{\mathbf{x}}\rangle+b_{i}-y\Bigr)^{2}\textrm{,}

(62)

and

{\mathcal{B}}\left({\mathbf{w}}_{ext},\rho_{ext},\mu_{ext},{\sigma}_{ext}\right)\overset{{\rm\mbox{\tiny def}}}{=}\underset{({\mathbf{v}}_{ext},b_{ext})\sim\left\{Q_{{\mathbf{w}}_{ext},{\mathbf{I}}_{ext}\rho_{ext}^{2}}\times Q_{\mu_{ext},{\sigma}_{ext}^{2}}\right\}}{{\mathds{E}}}\Bigl(\langle{\mathbf{v}}_{ext},{\mathbf{x}}\rangle+b_{ext}-y\Bigr)^{2}\textrm{.}

(63)

Beyond the index notations
i
and
ext
, the expressions of (
62
) and (
63
) are identical. In the following, we will find the analytical expression of (
62
) and deduce that of (
63
).
Let’s first rewrite (
62
) as follows

\displaystyle{\mathcal{B}}\left({\mathbf{w}}_{i},\rho_{i},\mu_{i},{\sigma}_{i}\right)

\displaystyle\overset{{\rm\mbox{\tiny def}}}{=}\underset{({\mathbf{v}}_{i},b_{i})\sim\left\{Q_{{\mathbf{w}}_{i},{\mathbf{I}}_{i}\rho_{i}^{2}}\times Q_{\mu_{i},{\sigma}_{i}^{2}}\right\}}{{\mathds{E}}}\Bigl(\langle{\mathbf{v}}_{i},{\mathbf{x}}\rangle+b_{i}-y\Bigr)^{2}

(64)

\displaystyle=\underset{b_{i}\sim Q_{\mu_{i},{\sigma}_{i}^{2}}}{{\mathds{E}}}\left[\underset{{\mathbf{v}}_{i}\sim Q_{{\mathbf{w}}_{i},{\mathbf{I}}_{i}\rho_{i}^{2}}}{{\mathds{E}}}\Bigl(\langle{\mathbf{v}}_{i},{\mathbf{x}}\rangle+b_{i}-y\Bigr)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\underset{b_{i}\sim Q_{\mu_{i},{\sigma}_{i}^{2}}}{{\mathds{E}}}\left[\int_{\mathds{R}^{d}}\left(\frac{1}{\rho_{i}\sqrt{2\pi}}\right)^{d}e^{-\frac{1}{2\rho_{i}^{2}}\left\|{\mathbf{v}}_{i}-{\mathbf{w}}_{i}\right\|^{2}}\Bigl(\langle{\mathbf{v}}_{i},{\mathbf{x}}\rangle+b_{i}-y\Bigr)^{2}d{\mathbf{v}}_{i}\right]\textrm{.}

As in previous Appendix, let’s now consider any non-zero vector
{\mathbf{x}}
and decompose the vector
{\mathbf{v}}_{i}
into
\left(v_{i\parallel},{\mathbf{v}}_{i\perp}\right)
constituted of parallel and perpendicular parts to
{\mathbf{x}}
. We then have

{\mathbf{v}}_{i}=\left(v_{i\parallel},{\mathbf{v}}_{i\perp}\right)\textrm{.}

Besides, if we define
\phi\overset{{\rm\mbox{\tiny def}}}{=}\left\|{\mathbf{x}}\right\|
we obtain the following

\langle{\mathbf{v}}_{i},{\mathbf{x}}\rangle=v_{i\parallel}\phi\textrm{.}

Similarly, let’s decompose the weight vector
{\mathbf{w}}_{i}
into
\left(w_{i\parallel},{\mathbf{w}}_{i\perp}\right)
constituted of parallel and perpendicular parts to
{\mathbf{x}}
. We then get

\left\|{\mathbf{v}}_{i}-{\mathbf{w}}_{i}\right\|^{2}=\left(v_{i\parallel}-w_{i\parallel}\right)^{2}+\left\|{\mathbf{v}}_{i\perp}-{\mathbf{w}}_{i\perp}\right\|^{2}\textrm{.}

With all these elements, Equation (
64
) becomes

\displaystyle{\mathcal{B}}\left({\mathbf{w}}_{i},\rho_{i},\mu_{i},{\sigma}_{i}\right)

\displaystyle=\underset{b_{i}\sim Q_{\mu_{i},{\sigma}_{i}^{2}}}{{\mathds{E}}}\Biggl[\int_{\mathds{R}}\frac{1}{\rho_{i}\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-\frac{1}{2\rho_{i}^{2}}\left(v_{i\parallel}-w_{i\parallel}\right)^{2}}\left(v_{i\parallel}\phi+b_{i}-y\right)^{2}dv_{i\parallel}

\displaystyle\times\int_{\mathds{R}^{d-1}}\left(\frac{1}{\rho_{i}\sqrt{2\pi}}\right)^{d-1}e^{-\frac{1}{2\rho_{i}^{2}}\left\|{\mathbf{v}}_{i\perp}-{\mathbf{w}}_{i\perp}\right\|^{2}}d{\mathbf{v}}_{i\perp}\Biggr]

\displaystyle=\underset{b_{i}\sim Q_{\mu_{i},{\sigma}_{i}^{2}}}{{\mathds{E}}}\Biggl[\int_{\mathds{R}}\frac{1}{\rho_{i}\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-\frac{1}{2\rho_{i}^{2}}\left(v_{i\parallel}-w_{i\parallel}\right)^{2}}\left(v_{i\parallel}\phi+b_{i}-y\right)^{2}dv_{i\parallel}\Biggr]

\displaystyle=\underset{b_{i}\sim Q_{\mu_{i},{\sigma}_{i}^{2}}}{{\mathds{E}}}\Biggl[\int_{\mathds{R}}\frac{1}{\rho_{i}\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-\frac{1}{2\rho_{i}^{2}}t^{2}}\left\{(t+w_{i\parallel})\phi+b_{i}-y\right\}^{2}dt\Biggr]\textrm{,}

where
t\overset{{\rm\mbox{\tiny def}}}{=}v_{i\parallel}-w_{i\parallel}
. Then, with
w_{i\parallel}=\frac{\langle{\mathbf{w}}_{i},{\mathbf{x}}\rangle}{\phi}
we obtain

\displaystyle{\mathcal{B}}\left({\mathbf{w}}_{i},\rho_{i},\mu_{i},{\sigma}_{i}\right)

\displaystyle=\underset{b_{i}\sim Q_{\mu_{i},{\sigma}_{i}^{2}}}{{\mathds{E}}}\Biggl[\underset{t\sim{\mathcal{N}}\left(0,\rho_{i}^{2}\right)}{{\mathds{E}}}\Bigl(\phi t+\langle{\mathbf{w}}_{i},{\mathbf{x}}\rangle+b_{i}-y\Bigr)^{2}\Biggr]

\displaystyle=\phi^{2}\underset{b_{i}\sim Q_{\mu_{i},{\sigma}_{i}^{2}}}{{\mathds{E}}}\Biggl[\underset{t\sim{\mathcal{N}}\left(0,\rho_{i}^{2}\right)}{{\mathds{E}}}\left(t+\frac{\langle{\mathbf{w}}_{i},{\mathbf{x}}\rangle+b_{i}-y}{\phi}\right)^{2}\Biggr]

\displaystyle=\phi^{2}\underset{b_{i}\sim Q_{\mu_{i},{\sigma}_{i}^{2}}}{{\mathds{E}}}\Biggl[\underset{t\sim{\mathcal{N}}\left(0,\rho_{i}^{2}\right)}{{\mathds{E}}}\left[t^{2}\right]+\frac{2\Bigl(\langle{\mathbf{w}}_{i},{\mathbf{x}}\rangle+b_{i}-y\Bigr)}{\phi}\underset{t\sim{\mathcal{N}}\left(0,\rho_{i}^{2}\right)}{{\mathds{E}}}\left[t\right]

\displaystyle+\frac{\Bigl(\langle{\mathbf{w}}_{i},{\mathbf{x}}\rangle+b_{i}-y\Bigr)^{2}}{\phi^{2}}\Biggr]

\displaystyle=\phi^{2}\underset{b_{i}\sim Q_{\mu_{i},{\sigma}_{i}^{2}}}{{\mathds{E}}}\Biggl[\rho_{i}^{2}+0+\frac{\Bigl(\langle{\mathbf{w}}_{i},{\mathbf{x}}\rangle+b_{i}-y\Bigr)^{2}}{\phi^{2}}\Biggr]

\displaystyle=\phi^{2}\rho_{i}^{2}+\underset{b_{i}\sim Q_{\mu_{i},{\sigma}_{i}^{2}}}{{\mathds{E}}}\Bigl(\langle{\mathbf{w}}_{i},{\mathbf{x}}\rangle+b_{i}-y\Bigr)^{2}

\displaystyle=\phi^{2}\rho_{i}^{2}+\underset{b_{i}\sim{\mathcal{N}}\left(\mu_{i},{\sigma}_{i}^{2}\right)}{{\mathds{E}}}\Bigl(\langle{\mathbf{w}}_{i},{\mathbf{x}}\rangle+b_{i}-y\Bigr)^{2}

\displaystyle=\phi^{2}\rho_{i}^{2}+\underset{s\sim{\mathcal{N}}\left(0,{\sigma}_{i}^{2}\right)}{{\mathds{E}}}\Bigl(s+\langle{\mathbf{w}}_{i},{\mathbf{x}}\rangle+\mu_{i}-y\Bigr)^{2}\textrm{,}

where
s\overset{{\rm\mbox{\tiny def}}}{=}b_{i}-\mu_{i}
. Then, we have

\displaystyle{\mathcal{B}}\left({\mathbf{w}}_{i},\rho_{i},\mu_{i},{\sigma}_{i}\right)

\displaystyle=\phi^{2}\rho_{i}^{2}+\underset{s\sim{\mathcal{N}}\left(0,{\sigma}_{i}^{2}\right)}{{\mathds{E}}}\left[s^{2}\right]+2\Bigl(\langle{\mathbf{w}}_{i},{\mathbf{x}}\rangle+\mu_{i}-y\Bigr)\underset{s\sim{\mathcal{N}}\left(0,{\sigma}_{i}^{2}\right)}{{\mathds{E}}}\left[s\right]

(65)

\displaystyle+\Bigl(\langle{\mathbf{w}}_{i},{\mathbf{x}}\rangle+\mu_{i}-y\Bigr)^{2}

\displaystyle=\phi^{2}\rho_{i}^{2}+{\sigma}_{i}^{2}+0+\Bigl(\langle{\mathbf{w}}_{i},{\mathbf{x}}\rangle+\mu_{i}-y\Bigr)^{2}

\displaystyle=\left\|{\mathbf{x}}\right\|^{2}\rho_{i}^{2}+{\sigma}_{i}^{2}+\Bigl(\langle{\mathbf{w}}_{i},{\mathbf{x}}\rangle+\mu_{i}-y\Bigr)^{2}\textrm{.}

Similarly, we obtain

{\mathcal{B}}\left({\mathbf{w}}_{ext},\rho_{ext},\mu_{ext},{\sigma}_{ext}\right)=\left\|{\mathbf{x}}\right\|^{2}\rho_{ext}^{2}+{\sigma}_{ext}^{2}+\Bigl(\langle{\mathbf{w}}_{ext},{\mathbf{x}}\rangle+\mu_{ext}-y\Bigr)^{2}\textrm{.}

(66)

Combining Equations (

) and (

\displaystyle\ell\Bigl[Q,({\mathbf{x}},y)\Bigr]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[\Theta_{k_{i},\tau_{i},d({\mathbf{c}}_{i},{\mathbf{x}})}\times\left\{\left\|{\mathbf{x}}\right\|^{2}\rho_{i}^{2}+{\sigma}_{i}^{2}+\Bigl(\langle{\mathbf{w}}_{i},{\mathbf{x}}\rangle+\mu_{i}-y\Bigr)^{2}\right\}\right]

\displaystyle+\prod_{i=1}^{n}\left[1-\Theta_{k_{i},\tau_{i},d({\mathbf{c}}_{i},{\mathbf{x}})}\right]

\displaystyle\times\left\{\left\|{\mathbf{x}}\right\|^{2}\rho_{ext}^{2}+{\sigma}_{ext}^{2}+\Bigl(\langle{\mathbf{w}}_{ext},{\mathbf{x}}\rangle+\mu_{ext}-y\Bigr)^{2}\right\}\textrm{,}

which completes the proof.

Appendix C
Quasi-Monte Carlo Approximation’s of Equation

In this appendix, we show how we use the Quasi-Monte Carlo method to efficiently calculate
{\Upsilon}_{d,\left\|{\mathbf{c}}_{i_{0}}-{\mathbf{x}}\right\|,{\varepsilon}_{i},k_{i},\tau_{i}}
from (
38
) in Subsection
5.1
. Recall that
{\Upsilon}_{d,\left\|{\mathbf{c}}_{i_{0}}-{\mathbf{x}}\right\|,{\varepsilon}_{i},k_{i},\tau_{i}}
represents the probability that the instance
{\mathbf{x}}
will be located within a ball centered at the point
{\mathbf{c}}_{i_{0}}
with some variance
{\varepsilon}_{i}^{2}
and the unknown ball radius is expressed in terms of
{\varepsilon}_{i}
,
k_{i}
and
\tau_{i}

{\Upsilon}_{d,\left\|{\mathbf{c}}_{i_{0}}-{\mathbf{x}}\right\|,{\varepsilon}_{i},k_{i},\tau_{i}}\overset{{\rm\mbox{\tiny def}}}{=}\underset{{\beta}_{i}\sim Q_{k_{i},\tau_{i}}}{{\mathds{E}}}\left[P\left(\frac{{\beta}_{i}^{2}}{{\varepsilon}_{i}^{2}};d,\frac{\left\|{\mathbf{c}}_{i_{0}}-{\mathbf{x}}\right\|^{2}}{{\varepsilon}_{i}^{2}}\right)\right]\textrm{,}

where
P\left(\frac{{\beta}_{i}^{2}}{{\varepsilon}_{i}^{2}};d,\frac{\left\|{\mathbf{c}}_{i_{0}}-{\mathbf{x}}\right\|^{2}}{{\varepsilon}_{i}^{2}}\right)

\chi^{2}

d

\frac{\left\|{\mathbf{c}}_{i_{0}}-{\mathbf{x}}\right\|^{2}}{{\varepsilon}_{i}^{2}}

\chi^{2}

\beta_{i}
randomly drawn from the

{\Upsilon}_{d,\left\|{\mathbf{c}}_{i_{0}}-{\mathbf{x}}\right\|,{\varepsilon}_{i},k_{i},\tau_{i}}

\left(d,\left\|{\mathbf{c}}_{i_{0}}-{\mathbf{x}}\right\|,{\varepsilon}_{i},k_{i},\tau_{i}\right)
