Photoacoustic imaging-based assessment of vascular changes in scars: a retrospective analysis of factors influencing PDL treatment efficacy
Authors: Xiaohan Liu, Yusang Hu, Wanyu Xu, Yixin Zhang, Zheng Zhang, Xinxian Meng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Pulsed dye laser (PDL) is widely used for hypervascular scars, but real-time evaluation of vascular responses remains limited. Photoacoustic imaging (PAI) enables noninvasive, high-resolution assessment of skin microvasculature. To assess whether PAI can visualize post-PDL vascular changes in real time and identify factors influencing efficacy. This single-center retrospective study analyzed 32 scars treated with 595-nm PDL. Patients were grouped by pulse duration/fluence combination (0.45–6.0 J/cm², 1.5–8.0 J/cm², or 3.0–10.0 J/cm²) or by endpoint (purpuric or nonpurpuric response). PAI, Antera 3D, and Vancouver Scar Scale (VSS) assessments were performed at baseline, immediately after treatment, and 1 month later. PAI enabled real-time, layer-specific visualization of vascular change, a capability that is less readily achievable with the VSS or Antera 3D. No statistically detectable association was identified between the parameter combinations and PDL outcomes. By contrast, the treatment endpoint was significantly associated with vascular outcomes: compared with purpuric treatment, nonpurpuric treatment showed lower adjusted full-dermis vessel density (30.25% vs. 49.29%; difference, − 19.04%; 95% CI, − 28.12 to − 9.97; Holm-adjusted P < 0.001) and smaller vessel diameter (278.74 μm vs. 428.36 μm; ratio, 0.651; 95% CI, 0.500–0.846; Holm-adjusted P = 0.002). Layer-specific outcomes also showed a similar direction of effect. In the exploratory repeated-treatment subset, NPUR was associated with progressive vascular improvement. PAI helps characterize vascular features and may provide additional information to support overall treatment parameter selection. Treatment endpoint was significant correlated to vascular efficacy. Introduction

Immature and pathological scars often present as red, raised lesions. The persistent erythema in these scars arises from endothelial dysfunction and sustained inflammatory angiogenesis [1, 2]. Based on the principle of selective photothermolysis, pulsed-dye laser (PDL) targets hemoglobin. It disrupts the microvascular structure thereby limiting the progression of pathological scars [3, 4]. Purpura, the transient purple discoloration following vascular injury, is one of clinical endpoints of PDL treatment. It represents an integrated tissue response to the combined treatment parameters and multiple related factors. Although most clinical studies on PDL treatment for scars have compared different combinations of pulse duration and fluence [5–7], some studies suggest that long-pulse, low-fluence protocols with a nonpurpuric endpoint may improve outcomes [5, 8]. However, objective vascular-level evidence to support such parameter selection is still lacking. The absence of direct, in-depth and high-resolution bedside monitoring methods for real-time vascular assessment make it difficult to optimize.immediate treatment response.

Photoacoustic imaging (PAI) is an emerging technology that provides high-resolution images of different skin layers [9, 10]. It operates by using a pulsed laser to irradiate tissue, where light-absorbing chromophores generate heat and induce thermoelastic expansion, thereby producing ultrasonic waves. These waves are then detected and reconstructed into structural images. PAI integrates the high contrast of optical imaging with the deep penetration of ultrasound imaging, thereby overcoming the shallow depth limitation of conventional optical techniques and the poor contrast of standard ultrasound, providing further support for biological and clinical research [9, 11, 12]. Therefore, incorporating PAI into scar assessment can provide clinicians with noninvasive, convenient, and more comprehensive and reliable data for diagnosis and treatment [13, 14].

This study used PAI to visualize dermal microvessels at different depths in pathological scars, providing preliminary evidence for its feasibility as a noninvasive tool for scar assessment. By comparing the baseline results with those at the one-month follow-up, we used PAI to track real-time vascular regression. We retrospectively analyzed how different parameter combinations and clinical endpoints influence treatment efficacy by using PAI, Antera 3D imaging, and the Vancouver Scar Scale (VSS). In an exploratory subset, we further assessed whether the observed vascular changes persisted after repeated treatment.

Materials and methods

Patient and scar characteristics

We retrospectively analyzed 32 scars patients treated with 595-nm PDL between June 2024 and October 2024 were retrospectively analyzed. On the basis of the preset pulse duration and fluence combinations, the patients were categorized into three predefined parameter groups: (1) P1: 0.45 ms with 6.0 J/cm² (n = 14), (2) P2: 1.5 ms with 8 J/cm² (n = 11), and (3) P3: 3 ms with 10 J/cm² (n = 7). Additionally, based on the comprehensive vascular response at the treatment endpoint (the presence or absence of visible purpura within minutes after irradiation), the patients were categorized into a purpura group (PUR, n = 14) and a nonpurpura group (NPUR, n = 18). A comparative analysis of patient characteristics, including sex, age, Fitzpatrick skin phototypes, disease duration, scar location and type, revealed no statistically significant differences (Table 1).

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria: ① the presence of scars resulting from trauma, burns and scalds; ② age between 18 and 60 years old; and ③ complete clinical data.

The exclusion criteria: ① infection, eczema, or ulceration at the scar site; ② systemic or local conditions that could influence wound healing or laser response; and ③ incomplete clinical data or participation in other clinical trials during the study period.

Treatment methods

All patients were treated by the same physician using a PDL system (Candela Corporation, USA). The fixed treatment parameters : wavelength 595 nm; spot diameter 7 mm; spot overlap 25%; and treatment interval 4 weeks. Immediately after treatment, ice packs were applied for 30 min. Patients were instructed to avoid smoking, alcohol, and spicy foods during the treatment period.

Observation indicators and assessment methods

All patients underwent PAI, VSS assessment, and Antera 3D imaging at baseline (pre-treatment), immediately after treatment (post-treatment), and at the 1-month follow-up. A subset of 13 patients completed a second treatment session and a 1-month follow-up after that session.

VSS assessment

The VSS has a maximum score of 15 according to the sum of the subscores of pigmentation (0–3), pliability (0–5), vascularity (0–3), and height (0–4). One physician assessed scar characteristics using the VSS in real time or via photographs.

PAI

PAI was performed in all patients by the same physician using a Hadatomo™ Z photoacoustic microscope (WEL5200; Advantest Corporation, Saitama, Japan). The imaging depth of the PAI system (approximately 500–600 μm) corresponds to the PDL treatment depth. As the PAI captures three-dimensional data (Supplementary 1), the scars were realigned parallel to the horizontal plane along the z-axis. Previous studies have shown that vascular remodeling in pathological scars may be depth dependent. Superficial vessels probably reflect dermal microvascular and angiogenic activity, whereas deeper vessels are related to feeding vessels network and tissue perfusion [15, 16]. Under this consideration, the stratification was divided into three regions on the basis of the vascular signal strength observed in the lateral view: the region of the epidermis (ROE) (0–200 μm), the superficial layer of the dermis (SOD) (200–400 μm), and the deep layer of the dermis (DOD) (400–600 μm). The SOD and DOD were combined to represent the full dermis FOD. (Fig. 1a, b and c). Horizontal maximum intensity projection (MIP) images were generated and captured for the FOD, SOD, and DOD regions, after which vascular features, including average diameter (DM) and density (DS), were extracted (Fig. 1d, e and f). In this study, DM refers specifically to the vessel diameter derived from the MIP-based imaging and analysis framework, rather than a direct measurement of the anatomical vessel caliber. There are excellent repeatability and stability for both DS and DM measurements under the repeated-scan conditions (Supplementary 2).

Antera 3D

All patients were assessed by another physician using an Antera 3D device (Miravex, Ireland). The images were analyzed using the manufacturer’s software to calculate the “redness” score.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are presented as mean±standard deviation. Categorical variables are presented as frequencies. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 26.0 : Baseline characteristics were compared using Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables, the independent-samples t-test and one-way ANOVA for continuous variables. Within-group pre- and post-treatment comparisons were performed as descriptive analyses using paired t-test. Separate baseline-adjusted ANCOVA models were fitted for each outcome. Holm correction was applied separately to the two co-primary outcomes and to the three secondary outcome families for endpoints, parameter combinations, and interaction hypotheses. Pairwise comparisons among parameter combinations were Holm-adjusted within each outcome. Model assumptions were assessed using covariate-by-group interactions, residual diagnostics, the Jarque-Bera test, the Breusch-Pagan test, HC3 robust standard errors, and Cook’s distance. All tests were two-sided. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05, denoted as *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, and ****p < 0.0001.

Results

Overall response to PDL therapy

For the overall patient cohort, the total VSS decreased was 0.34 ± 0.87 (p < 0.05). However, the vascularity subscore of the VSS did not significantly change(Fig. 2a). By contrast, the mean reduction in the Antera 3D red score was 2.41 ± 3.94 (p < 0.01) (Fig. 2b). These findings showed measurable improvement after PDL treatment, with Antera 3D exhibiting superior sensitivity to the VSS. PAI suggested that the measurable vascular response was mainly reflected by reduced vessel density. Specifically, the reduction in vessel density was 5.84% ± 12.65% for FOD and 2.84% ± 7.46% for DOD (both p < 0.05). In contrast, no significant changes in dermal vessel diameter were observed (Fig. 2c).

After confirming the overall efficacy of PDL, we subsequently performed subgroup analysis and baseline-adjusted ANCOVA models based on parameter combination and treatment endpoint to identify the primary factors influencing therapeutic outcomes.

Responses according to parameter combination and treatment endpoint

We first conducted separate subgroup analyses. In the comparison of different parameter combinations, the VSS scores did not significantly differ from baseline in any group at the one-month follow-up (Supplementary 3 Fig. 1a). Only the 1.5-ms group showed reduction in both Antera 3D (2.75 ± 2.99, p < 0.05) (Supplementary 3 Fig. 1b) and PAI-derived FOD-DS (8.18% ± 11.57%, p < 0.05) (Supplementary 3 Fig. 1c and d), whereas no significant differences were observed for the other outcomes.

In the comparison of responses to different treatment endpoints, the pattern differed. In the PUR group, standard photographs and Antera 3D images showed visible purpura, while PAI suggested disruption of the vascular pattern and redistribution of hemoglobin-related signals (Fig. 1d). These findings are consistent with a intense acute vascular photothermal response, which may involve vessel-wall injury and, under some conditions, erythrocyte extravasation [17]. In the NPUR group, although no clear changes were visible on clinical or Antera 3D, PAI showed reduced vascular signals (Fig. 1e). This may reflect vascular coagulation or functional occlusion.

At the one-month follow-up, VSS scores did not change significantly in neither the PUR nor NPUR group(Supplementary 3 Fig. 1e). In contrast, the Antera 3D data indicated a significant decrease in scar vascularity of NPUR therapy, (3.29 ± 4.25, p < 0.01)(Supplementary 1f). Similarly, PAI showed no significant vascular change in the PUR group, whereas the NPUR group showed reductions in both DS and DM. Quantitatively, NPUR significantly decreased the FOD, SOD and DOD vessel density by 11.3% ±10.8% (p < 0.001), 7.7% ±10.3% (p < 0.01) and 6.8% ±5.9% (p < 0.0001), respectively, and decreased the diameter by 121 ± 105 μm (p < 0.001), 66 ± 70 μm (p < 0.001) and 43 ± 67 μm (p < 0.05) (Supplementary 2 Fig. 1g and h). Pearson correlation analysis showed that the Antera 3D vascular index was positively correlated with the PAI-derived FOD_DS at baseline (r = 0.439, 95% CI: 0.107–0.683, P < 0.05) and at the 1-month follow-up (r = 0.484, 95% CI: 0.163–0.713, P < 0.01) (Supplementary 3 Table 1). These preliminary analyses suggested that the immediate treatment endpoint may be more closely associated with treatment efficacy than the specific parameter combinations.

Baseline-adjusted ANCOVA of parameter combination and treatment endpoint

To determine whether the treatment endpoint could serve as a significant influencing factor for PDL vascular efficacy, we fitted baseline-adjusted ANCOVA models. After adjustment for the corresponding baseline measurement and PDL parameter combination, the treatment endpoint was significantly associated with both co-primary outcomes after Holm correction (Fig. 2f). For FOD-DS, the adjusted mean was 49.29% in PUR and 30.25% in NPUR at the 1-month follow-up; the adjusted difference was − 19.04% (95% CI −28.12% to −9.97%; Holm-adjusted P < 0.001). For FOD-DM, the adjusted geometric mean was 428.36 μm in PUR and 278.74 μm in NPUR at 1 month-follow-up. The NPUR-to-PUR ratio was 0.651 (95% CI 0.50–0.85; Holm-adjusted P < 0.01), corresponding to 34.9% lower adjusted DM in NPUR than PUR (Table 2). In contrast, no significant main effect or endpoint×parameter interaction was detected for either co-primary outcome in parameter combination groups (Fig. 2g). In the layer-specific DS family, SOD-DS showed a significant adjusted reduction in the NPUR group compared with PUR (95% CI −16.48 to −1.43; Holm-adjusted P < 0.05), whereas DOD-DS did not reach statistical significance. In the layer-specific DM family, both SOD-DM and DOD-DM were lower in the NPUR group. The adjusted NPUR-to-PUR ratios were 0.74 for SOD-DM (95% CI 0.57 to 0.96; Holm-adjusted P < 0.05) and 0.79 for DOD-DM (95% CI 0.66 to 0.95; Holm-adjusted P < 0.05). In contrast, conventional assessment outcomes did not show any significant differences (Table 2).

Serial vascular changes after repeated PDL treatment

A subset of participants received two laser treatment sessions, each followed by a posttreatment evaluation (Fig. 3a). Representative clinical photographs showed a visible reduction in scar erythema. Following the second treatment, the PUR group still showed no significant deviation from baseline in either Antera 3D or PAI. In contrast, the NPUR group exhibited a progressive decrease in FOD vessel density measured by PAI, with an initial reduction of 11.40% ± 9.04% (p < 0.05) after the first treatment, followed by an additional 7.40% ± 3.78% (p < 0.05) after the second treatment (Fig. 3b and c).

Discussion

Although vascular modulation is a key target in pathological scar management [18, 19], direct real-time visualization of vascular responses has been limited by inadequate assessment tools. Current assessment tools, including clinical scales and spectroscopic imaging, may not fully capture dynamic vascular alterations : the dermoscopy is rapid but mainly provides surface information; reflectance confocal microscopy offers near-cellular resolution but has limited penetration depth and requires specialized interpretation; high-frequency ultrasound can image deeper tissues but has lower spatial resolution [20, 21]. Among available techniques, only PAI and OCT enable three-dimensional, layer-specific vascular imaging of scar tissue [22]. However, OCT is limited by light scattering and shallow penetration [23]. In contrast, PAI allows real-time 3D mapping of scar vasculature with micrometer-scale resolution based on hemoglobin absorption, simultaneously capturing vessel morphology, density, diameter, and branching changes after PDL treatment [24, 25]. Thus, PAI provides superior precision for monitoring vascular responses following PDL therapy.

A comprehensive series of personalized scar evaluation is such essential, because of the heterogeneity of scars in thickness, vascular distribution, vessel diameter, and density [25, 26]. Bedside instruments that can rapidly and accurately map the dermal microvasculature to guide treatment parameter selection remain scarce. VSS can be used for the comprehensive evaluation of scar treatment but often fails to detect intergroup differences [26], as demonstrated in our study. Antera 3D, relying on on surface multispectral optical data, can reconstruct superficial skin features and quantify hemoglobin-related vascularity, but it cannot accurately characterize subepidermal three-dimensional vascular networks [27]. In this study, Antera 3D vascularity indices correlated with PAI metrics, suggesting that PAI, as a higher-resolution and more objective imaging tool. Although with our image processing method, DM reflects the apparent vascular width rather than the histological lumen diameter, it remains informative for vascular classification and statistical comparisons. Further three-dimensional vascular analysis may provide more accurate assessment. Accordingly, depth-resolved PAI may provide complementary information for future treatment stratification, although its clinical relevance requires prospective validation.

With respect to different PDL parameters, previous PDL studies on scar treatment focused on pulse duration and fluence but did not assess variations in treatment endpoints, the immediate clinical manifestation of the combined effects of parameters and scar characteristics [5, 7, 8, 28]. This bias may explain why different scars respond differently to identical parameters. Thus, re-evaluating PDL efficacy with careful endpoint consideration is essential to clarify the true determinants of scar response. This study showed that the treatment endpoint was significantly associated with vascular outcomes in PDL efficacy. A possible explanation is that vascular dysregulation is central to scar progression. Compared with normal skin, scar tissue has higher microvessel density and endothelial dysfunction, leading to plasma extravasation and immune cell infiltration, which fosters a self-sustaining cycle of chronic inflammation [29, 30]. Purpura in inflamed scars may trigger excessive inflammation and promote hypoxia pathways, which further promote angiogenesis [31, 32]. This counterproductive response may offset the intended vascular remodeling effect, thereby explaining the lack of improvement in the PAI metrics. All these factors may explain why subthreshold doses are no inferior to, or even superior to conventional high-dose irradiation regimens because of fewer adverse effects. This phenomenon is not limited to scar treatment; similarly, a study involving Nd: YAG lasers also observed this outcome [33]. Therefore, unlike vascular lesions, in which purpura is often used as a therapeutic endpoint [34–36], these preliminary findings suggest that the use of relatively lower fluence settings, with vascular closure without visible purpura considered as a potential clinical endpoint, may warrant further investigation in PDL treatment protocols.

Even specifing clinical endpoint, selection of pulse duration remains challenging. Current evidence is limited and heterogeneous because pulse duration is often evaluated alongside different fluences and treatment endpoints [37–40]. Expert opinion has suggested shorter pulses for immature scars and longer pulses for more stable scars, although this approach has not been supported by robust comparative evidence [41]. From a theoretical perspective, the pulse durations evaluated in this study—0.45, 1.5, and 3.0 ms—may differ in their suitability according to vessel size, thermal relaxation time, and scar maturity [42]. Shorter pulses may preferentially affect smaller vessels but may also increase rapid heating and vessel rupture, whereas longer pulses may allow more gradual coagulation of enlarged or deeper vessels, although potentially with greater nonspecific thermal exposure [43, 44]. Given the small retrospective sample and the coupling of pulse duration with fluence, further prospective studies that independently vary pulse duration and fluence are required to clarify their respective clinical roles.

PDL scar therapy is commonly administered at 2–4-week intervals, with repeated sessions potentially improving scar scores until a plateau is reached [7, 37]. In our exploratory follow-up, additional treatments were not clearly associated with further vascular improvement in the PUR group, whereas a gradual improvement was observed in the nonpurpura group.

This study has several limitations. Its single-center retrospective design and small sample size limit the generalizability of the findings, and long-term PAI monitoring remains insufficient. PAI reliability may also be reduced on nonplanar surfaces. Future algorithmic improvements may enable three-dimensional quantification. Because vascular imaging alone cannot fully characterize scar remodeling, PAI could be combined with complementary techniques to assess collagen architecture. In the future, larger prospective studies with standardized treatment, detailed clinical characterization, and independent adjustment of pulse duration and fluence are needed.

Conclusion

This study provides preliminary evidence supporting the feasibility of PAI for scar assessment. PAI may provide sensitive, depth-resolved visualization of vascular changes, thereby complementing clinical assessment and supporting future treatment optimization. In this retrospective cohort of 32 pathological scars, exploratory analyses suggested that the immediate treatment endpoint was associated with subsequent vascular outcomes, with vascular closure with nonpurpura being associated with more favorable vascular changes than a purpuric endpoint.
