What impact did the Brazilian Federal Government’s intervention have on malaria cases in the Yanomami Indigenous lands?
Authors: Jacqueline de Aguiar Barros, Fabiana Granja, Pedro Eduardo Lima Siqueira, Ivyson da Silva Epifânio, Ronan Rocha Coelho, Maria Fátima Ferreira-da-Cruz
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe northern Brazilian state of Roraima has recently experienced an increase in malaria cases, mainly caused by illegal mining activities on indigenous lands. These activities mainly affect the Yanomami Indigenous people, whose territory accounts for nearly 30% of Roraima. In January 2023, the severity of the malaria outbreak led to a Public Health Emergency declaration in the Yanomami Indigenous Land (YIL) and prompted government intervention. This study examines malaria trends before and after the 2023 federal government’s response in the YIL.MethodsA descriptive retrospective study was performed using secondary data from Brazil’s Health Information System (HIS), covering reported malaria cases, hospitalizations, and deaths in Roraima from 2021 to 2024.ResultsBetween January 2021 and December 2024, Roraima reported 117,214 indigenous malaria cases. After the federal intervention in the YIL in 2023, cases increased significantly (52,210 to 65,004; p = 0.0005), while hospitalizations decreased (787–638; p = 0.0304), and deaths remained steady (111 total; p = 0.9089). Throughout the study, Plasmodium vivax was the dominant species. Cases were highest among men, Indigenous people, and the 20–39 age group. Post-intervention, case detection increased for all species but shifted from adults (20–59 years) to vulnerable groups (0–19, 60 + years). Indigenous cases rose sharply. Hospitalizations for P. vivax, declined after the intervention, especially in men and the 20–39 age group. Mortality was highest in children under 10 and Indigenous populations. Within the Yanomami Special Indigenous Health District (Yanomami-DSEI), cases decreased in Mucajaí, Iracema, and Caracaraí but increased in Alto Alegre in 2024. Exported cases from Roraima to Maranhão (− 95.5%), Pará (− 86.8%), and Amazonas (− 71.5%) dropped sharply.ConclusionsAfter federal government intervention in the YIL, malaria case detection increased, hospitalizations decreased, and mortality stayed steady. Cases among the Yanomami in Alto Alegre rose, while exports to other states declined. Ongoing progress relies on strengthening primary care, enhancing surveillance, and fostering cross-border cooperation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12936-025-05744-5.Keywords: Malaria, Plasmodium, Indigenous health, Amazon Yanomami, Miners

Background

Malaria remains a major global public health challenge. In 2023, countries with endemic cases reported 263 million cases and 597,000 deaths. In the Americas, malaria incidence decreased by 65% from 2000 to 2023. Brazil, Venezuela, and Colombia account for 77% of the region’s cases, with Plasmodium vivax making up 72% of those cases [1].

In Brazil, malaria transmission is endemic in the Legal Amazon, where ecological conditions support the proliferation of the vector, Anopheles darlingi. This region accounts for 99.9% of the country’s locally acquired cases [2]. Although Brazil has set goals to eliminate Plasmodium falciparum by 2030 and Plasmodium vivax by 2035 [3], illegal mining in Indigenous territories has become a major obstacle to achieving these targets [4].

Illegal mining significantly impacts malaria transmission patterns through multiple interconnected mechanisms. These include environmental changes caused by such activities, like digging holes that become breeding sites for mosquito vectors and deforestation, which allows these insects to migrate from forested areas to populated regions. This process increases exposure to mosquito vectors in indigenous communities [5, 6], especially among the Yanomami, whose villages are located near illegal mining sites.

Furthermore, increased cross-border movement of gold miners helps reintroduce parasites into vulnerable populations. These issues, paired with limited healthcare access in these communities, elevate the risk of severe cases and sustain ongoing transmission. Together, these interconnected factors keep active malaria spread ongoing, especially in the border regions of Brazil, Colombia, Venezuela, and Guyana [1, 7–9].

This situation particularly affects indigenous people and traditional communities, who already experience systemic health disparities. These include high rates of illness and death from preventable diseases, acute malnutrition, and high maternal and infant mortality rates. Such disparities are intensified by social factors influencing health, such as structural poverty, stigma, and geographical and cultural barriers to healthcare access [1].

In fact, there has been a significant rise in malaria cases in indigenous villages and mining areas [10], supported by MapBiomas data, which is closely linked to the expansion of mining in the Brazilian Amazon. The data show that after a period of relative stability and moderate growth from the late 1980s to the mid-2010s, mining activity has increased sharply and consistently since 2016 [11].

Between 2019 and 2021, the Brazilian Amazon Indigenous Lands (ILs) saw a 195% rise in severe deforestation compared to the previous three years, with illegal mining being the most common human activity [12].

In 2023, autochthonous malaria cases in ILs accounted for 40% (55,974 cases) of all reported cases in Brazil. A state-level analysis showed that Amazonas and Roraima had the highest incidence rates. Among the Special Indigenous Health Districts (DSEIs), the Yanomami region was the most affected, representing 56% (31,206 cases) of all cases reported within these territories [2].

The significant increase in malaria cases in Roraima state impacted the Yanomami people, whose territory covers much of the state’s northwest, primarily due to the expansion of illegal mining within their lands [7, 9].

Following reports of over 20,000 malaria cases in YIL, the severity of the crisis prompted the declaration of a Public Health Emergency of National Importance in 2023, highlighting the urgent need for government action [13]. The federal government implemented structural measures, including the mandatory removal of illegal miners and increased support for the Yanomami community. Key steps involved reopening inactive health posts, expanding the health workforce, and ensuring prompt malaria diagnosis and treatment. Therefore, assessing the impact of these measures on epidemiological patterns is essential.

This study aims to assess temporal trends in malaria indicators before and after the 2023 federal intervention in the YIL. The analysis will explore patterns in cases, hospitalizations, and deaths over time, across different locations, and among various demographic groups, including data on parasite species, as well as the spatial and temporal distribution of the disease and its potential to spread to other Brazilian regions. The findings are expected to help develop more effective public policies and enhance control strategies for endemic areas. Ultimately, this research seeks to support national malaria elimination efforts and align with global initiatives to reduce the disease’s impact on vulnerable populations.

Methods

A retrospective descriptive study was conducted using secondary data from the HIS to analyse confirmed malaria cases, hospitalizations, and deaths in Roraima from 2021 to 2024. The investigation covers two distinct periods: 2021–2022 (pre-intervention) and 2023–2024 (post-intervention), with the YIL federal intervention starting in January 2023. This division allows for comparison of epidemiological conditions before emergency measures and after implementing structural health and territorial protection actions, enabling an evaluation of the intervention’s impact on malaria incidence, hospitalizations, and mortality.

The federal intervention in the YIL involved four main coordinated components: (1) Removing illegal miners and dismantling their infrastructure through police and military operations; (2) Restructuring health services, including aeromedical evacuation of critical patients, reopening “Polo Base” (the main operational unit of the Brazilian Indigenous Health Care System), and increasing health professionals to expand coverage and access to malaria diagnosis and treatment; (3) Conducting emergency food distribution via air bridges to ensure proper nutrition; (4) Using remote sensing and intelligence to monitor and prevent the return of illegal miners and to evaluate the effectiveness of the actions [13].

The state of Roraima is located in the northernmost part of Brazil, bordering Venezuela and Guyana, as well as the Brazilian states of Amazonas and Pará. It covers an area of 224,298.98 km2 and has an estimated population of 652,713 [14]. The state includes 15 municipalities and has two DSEIs: Yanomami and Leste (Fig. 1)

Map of Roraima's territorial layout: municipalities, indigenous lands, mining sites, rivers, and borders

The Yanomami-DSEI covers approximately 96,650 km2 and provides healthcare to an estimated Indigenous population of 33,335 people across the states of Roraima and Amazonas. In Roraima, the YIL includes the municipalities of Alto Alegre, Amajari, Caracaraí, Iracema, and Mucajaí [15]. The Leste-DSEI, which spans 39,129 km2 within a total territory of 69,754 km2, serves the entire eastern half of Roraima state. It has an estimated 61,121 Indigenous residents across 11 municipalities, including the state capital, Boa Vista, along with Alto Alegre, Amajari, Bonfim, Cantá, Normandia, Pacaraima, Uiramutã, São João da Baliza, São Luiz do Anauá, and Caroebe [16]. Notably, Rorainópolis is the only municipality without approved ILs, while Alto Alegre and Amajari are included in both DSEIs (Fig. 1)

The data on notified malaria cases were collected from the Malaria Epidemiological Surveillance Information System (SIVEP-Malaria). The probable infection site is identified using the SIVEP-Malaria notification and investigation form. This platform links data on malaria risk activities to their specific locations (country, state, municipality, and locality), enabling the inference of the most likely infection site. Thus, all cases were classified as autochthonous to Roraima. Laboratory confirmation of cases was established by detecting Plasmodium spp. through thick blood smear examination and/or rapid diagnostic test (RDT). Positive cure verification slides (CVS) were excluded from the analysis to focus solely on new cases. Cases were evaluated based on the year of notification, parasite species, and demographic factors such as age, sex, and race. Geographically, transmission was categorized as occurring in indigenous areas (Yanomami-DSEI and Leste-DSEI) or non-indigenous areas (rural/settlement, urban, and mining). To specifically assess the impact within the YIL, the analysis focused on the five municipalities covering this territory: Amajari, Alto Alegre, Mucajaí, Iracema, and Caracaraí. Indigenous cases from these municipalities were further stratified by DSEI of origin (Yanomami or Leste) to analyse malaria distribution within each population.

To investigate the interstate spread of malaria, data from SIVEP-Malaria and the Notifiable Diseases Information System (SINAN) were used, representing the malaria epidemiological surveillance systems for the Amazon and Extra-Amazon regions of Brazil, respectively. An exported case was identified using the following operational definition: cases classified as autochthonous in Roraima that were reported by other states. This criterion enabled mapping dispersal flows from Roraima to other parts of the country.

Data on deaths and hospitalizations were collected from the Mortality Information System (SIM) and the Hospital Information System (SIH), respectively, using ICD-10 codes B50–B54 specific to malaria. The records were organized by year, parasite species, sex, and age group to analyse the profile of severe cases.

Statistical analyses were conducted in two phases. In the first phase, a comparative assessment was performed across the entire study period (by month/year), and in the second phase, between the pre-intervention period (2021–2022) and the post-intervention period (2023–2024). In both phases, autochthonous malaria cases, hospitalizations, and deaths, as well as cases in specific areas of municipalities with YIL and the exportation of malaria cases from Roraima to other states, were calculated using case distribution variables (parasite species, age, gender, and race). Means were calculated for both periods, and normality was evaluated using the Shapiro–Wilk and Kolmogorov–Smirnov tests. Depending on the data distribution, either parametric tests (one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post-hoc test) or non-parametric tests (Mann–Whitney U, Kruskal–Wallis with Dunn’s post-hoc test, Wilcoxon, and Friedman tests) were applied. All analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software (version 10.5.0) and/or BioEstat 5.33, with a significance level set at p < 0.05.

The maps were created using QGIS software (version 3.28.10; QGIS Development Team, 2023), including spatial data on mining areas in Roraima state from the MapBiomas platform (Collection 7.1; MapBiomas, 2022). Geopolitical boundaries of Brazil and Indigenous Land polygons were obtained from the Brazilian Institute of Geography and Statistics [17].

This study was exempt from ethics approval because it only used anonymized secondary data, in accordance with Resolution No. 446/2011 of the National Health Council.

Results

From January 2021 to December 2024, the state of Roraima reported 117,214 autochthonous malaria cases. Analysis showed a significant rise in incidence during the post-intervention period (n = 65,004 cases) compared to the pre-intervention period (n = 52,210 cases; p = 0.0005). In contrast, malaria-related hospitalizations decreased markedly, from 787 in the pre-intervention period to 638 in the post-intervention period (p = 0.0304), totaling 1425 hospitalizations. Meanwhile, there was no significant difference in malaria-related deaths between the two periods (p = 0.9089). Of the 111 recorded deaths, 57 occurred before the intervention and 54 afterward (Fig. 2).

Regarding hospitalizations, P. vivax was the most frequent species (p < 0.0001), with predominance in the 20–39 age group (p < 0.0001) and males (p = 0.0029). Non-Indigenous people had a higher number of hospitalizations (p < 0.0001). However, the annual number of exported cases has been steadily decreasing. Discussion

The YIL, the largest semi-isolated indigenous group in the Americas, faces significant healthcare challenges due to extreme geographic isolation. Their territory depends almost entirely on-air transportation. This reliance on transportation, combined with high population mobility and dispersed villages, creates structural barriers to malaria control. This results in treatment delays, ongoing transmission, and increased pressure on health services [18]. The study findings show a complex malaria situation in Roraima from 2021 to 2024, characterized by a notable rise in reported malaria cases after interventions, while hospitalizations decreased and deaths remained steady.

Here, we examined malaria morbidity and mortality indicators to understand disease patterns following the federal intervention in the YIL. While nationwide surveillance data provides extensive coverage, the study recognizes limitations, including potential underreporting. The observational nature of the analysis means the findings show temporal associations rather than direct causality, but they still offer valuable insights for guiding public health policy.

During the pre-intervention period, illegal mining activities grew significantly, especially in the Alto Alegre and Mucajaí regions. The Uraricoera River, a long-standing key area for this activity, serves as the primary entry point for miners into Yanomami territory. Remote sensing data show that 52.3% of the affected area is within its riverbed, followed by the Mucajaí-Couto de Magalhães hydrographic system (28.7%) and the Parima-Catrimani basins (19%), which are critical waterways for the survival of indigenous communities (Fig. 1) [19].

In early 2023, approximately 5224 indigenous people still lacked healthcare access because the primary Health Base Poles in the Yanomami villages of Kayanaú, Homoxi, Hakoma, Ajaraní, Haxiú, Xitei, and Palimiú were inactive [20].

To address this critical gap, the federal government prioritized reactivating the health network. After the government’s action, several “Polo Bases” with major operational problems were reactivated, creating a network of 12 units to coordinate health services in the region (Alto Mucajaí, Aratha-U, Baixo Mucajaí, Hakoma, Haxiu, Palimiú, Parafuri, Surucucu, Uraricoera, Waikás, Waputha, and Xitei) [18].

The increase in health professionals also enabled broader surveillance and better access to quick diagnosis and prompt treatment of malaria. As a result, in 2024, the number of diagnostic tests reached 260,251—a 44% increase from 2023 (180,906). Overall, diagnostic testing went up by 4.4%, with active case detection rising by 5% and passive detection by 3%. Active detection, which involves intentionally searching for cases in communities through mass testing, including testing asymptomatic individuals during home visits, proved vital for immediate treatment and proper follow-up. Therefore, the subsequent rise in reported malaria cases reflects both improved disease detection and an actual increase in the disease’s spread due to prior neglect.

Vector control measures were also intensified, including environmental assessments, removal of breeding sites such as mining holes, sanitation efforts, and indoor spraying to reduce transmission. These actions have strengthened the malaria response in the YIL [20].

During the post-intervention period, malaria cases declined in most municipalities within the Yanomami DSEI, including Amajari, Caracaraí, Iracema, and Mucajaí. In contrast, Alto Alegre saw a steady increase. This difference is related to the region’s distinct demographic makeup, as Alto Alegre accounts for 58% of the Yanomami-DSEI’s indigenous population in Roraima (11,871 out of 20,601 residents) and is dispersed across 165 villages [18]. Additionally, illegal mining sites along the Venezuelan border remain active, supporting ongoing malaria transmission in this frontier zone. Heavy mining activity on both sides of the border enables frequent movement of Yanomami people and miners [9, 12].

Indeed, some infections diagnosed at the Roraima YIL but originating from cross-border mining areas were later introduced into villages, extending local transmission despite emergency measures [21, 22].

Surprisingly, among the Yanomami, 54 cases of P. malariae were diagnosed in 2024, compared to the figures from 2021–2022. The indigenous Yanomami territory includes, besides Roraima, the state of Amazonas. The Yanomami are considered nomadic, and their way of life involves moving through the forest, which is linked to their subsistence methods and cultural beliefs. In light of that, the cases of P. malariae might have been introduced via Amazonas, which recorded 152 cases of P. malariae among the Yanomami in 2023. Understanding the extent of transnational malaria spread could help address this issue.

Plasmodium malariae is responsible for a small percentage of malaria cases and is widely considered the least likely to cause death among the human-infecting Plasmodium species. Therefore, it is more likely that the patient who died from malaria caused by P. malariae had a comorbidity or co-infection related to it, but a death surveillance procedure was not performed.

To enhance treatment adherence and prevent relapses of malaria vivax, the Brazilian Ministry of Health is implementing a new protocol for the Yanomami population that replaces the standard 7-day primaquine regimen with a single dose of tafenoquine after G6PD testing [20].

It is well known that deaths from malaria are most often a consequence of delayed diagnosis and treatment. Although the number of deaths from P. vivax was significantly higher than from P. falciparum, the case-fatality rate was 0.07% for P. vivax compared to 0.1% for P. falciparum, confirming, as expected, P. falciparum’s higher morbidity.

The worsening of malnutrition caused by malaria definitely contributed to the higher number of deaths from vivax malaria, considering that adults affected by the disease are unable to provide food, because indigenous communities depend on family labour for their subsistence.

In fact, underreporting, inconsistent completion of death certificates, and a lack of laboratory confirmation hinder the accuracy of data. To enhance data quality and improve surveillance, it is crucial to strengthen monitoring of malaria deaths in the state. Additionally, targeted studies are needed to evaluate comorbidities, gaps in the care process, and factors contributing to fatal outcomes, as well as investigations to identify potential genetic profiles linked to P. vivax disease severity.

Although the difference is not statistically significant, women made up at least half of the deaths in the years analysed here, peaking at 63% in 2022. These data indicate a considerable female involvement in malaria mortality in the state, even though men have a higher disease incidence. The fact that women are present in high-risk areas highlights the need for targeted health strategies, especially for pregnant women [25, 26].

Children under five are also a highly vulnerable group, facing increased risks of severe malaria complications and long-term sequelae [7, 9]. Consistent use of insecticide-treated nets, regular testing, and community-based education initiatives should be adopted.

Although the study’s limitations include the lack of systematic symptom assessment, the high prevalence of asymptomatic P. vivax infections in the Yanomami population serves as a hidden reservoir, sustaining malaria transmission and posing significant challenges for control efforts [23, 24]. This highlights the urgent need for targeted treatment strategies for these individuals.

Notably, Pará, Amazonas, and Maranhão were the main states reporting malaria cases from Roraima during the pre-intervention period, with cases decreasing after the federal intervention. Simultaneously, a new spatial pattern emerged, showing increased imported cases in Mato Grosso and Amapá. These findings are supported by recent studies from Roraima that highlight gold mining-related malaria as a challenge for control. Significant dispersal of gold miners’ malaria cases has been recorded not only in neighbouring states in the North but also in the Southeast and South regions of Brazil, along with cross-border importation from Venezuela and the Guianas.

The high mobility of miners in the Amazon creates a major challenge to eliminating malaria across South America. This temporary population, often in unstable conditions, frequently avoids formal health services, leading to delays in diagnosis, self-medication, and poor adherence to treatment plans. Such behaviour allows parasites to be reintroduced into controlled areas and accelerates the spread of drug-resistant malaria strains, making surveillance and treatment more difficult [26–30].

Achieving elimination in border areas therefore requires strong international cooperation, including coordinated data sharing to monitor cross-border case importation and aligned public health policies. Concerning Amazon, subregional platforms could focus on key transmission routes, such as the Venezuela-Guyana-Brazil and Suriname-French Guiana-Brazil pathways. Addressing this challenge calls for innovative, targeted approaches for mobile populations, such as administering tafenoquine, along with enhanced border surveillance to develop effective, region-specific responses [30].

Conclusions

The federal intervention in the YIL improved access to health services by increasing the number of healthcare professionals and expanding the availability of malaria diagnosis and treatment. This resulted in active case detection, a decrease in hospitalizations, and stable malaria-related mortality.

To maintain these gains, it is essential to shift from emergency measures to a long-term strategy that focuses on strengthening primary healthcare, training local community health workers, expanding integrated surveillance, and improving management within the DSEI-Yanomami. The success of these efforts depends on stable funding, efficient logistics, and ongoing institutional commitment, in line with national malaria elimination guidelines.

The YIL intervention highlights the importance of coordinated regional policies that combine medical innovations with cross-border governance, balancing rapid response systems with sustainable health infrastructure investments. Effectively protecting vulnerable populations requires a unified and collaborative approach that addresses the social and environmental factors that contribute to malaria transmission.
