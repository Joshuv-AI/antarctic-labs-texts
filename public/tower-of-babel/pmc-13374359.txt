Determinants of pro- and anti-inflammatory cytokine profiles across populations
Authors: Samantha E Leonard, Mohsen Ghanbari, Pawel Sowa, Joyce BJ van Meurs, Costanza Vallerga, P Martin van Hagen, Henry Völzke, Sabine Ameling, Uwe Völker, Marcin Kondraciuk, Marlena Dubatowka, Karol Kamiński, Alexander Teumer, Layal Chaker, Virgil ASH Dalm
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCytokine dysregulation contributes to chronic inflammation and immune-mediated diseases, yet population-level determinants of pro- and anti-inflammatory cytokines remain poorly characterized. We aimed to identify demographic and lifestyle determinants of plasma cytokine levels and evaluate reproducibility of inflammatory patterns across European populations.MethodsIn the population-based Rotterdam Study cohort (n = 3,456; mean age 57 years; 56% female), we examined associations between plasma levels of nine cytokines (Olink Inflammation Panel) and age, sex, smoking, body mass index (BMI), and alcohol consumption. Linear and non-linear regression models were applied, with stratified analyses where appropriate. Cytokine clustering was assessed using principal component analysis (PCA). Findings were replicated in two independent European cohorts using identical protocols. Additionally, associations between raw IL-6 levels and determinants were meta-analyzed across the two replication cohorts (total n > 4,000).ResultsPlasma IL-10, IL-6, IL-17 A, TNF, IFN-γ, IL-18, and IL-17 C increased with age; IL-18 and IL-17 C followed non-linear trends (P < 0.01). Females had lower IL-18 and IL-17 C levels (β: − 0.41 and − 0.32, respectively) but higher IFN-γ (β: 0.17). Smoking was associated with higher IL-10, IL-6, IL-18, and IL-17 C (β range: 0.10–0.54) and lower IFN-γ (β: -0.15) and IL-13 (β: − 0.10). BMI was positively associated with IL-6, IL-18, TNF, and IL-17 A levels (P < 0.05), and inversely with IL-10 (P < 0.001). Sex-specific associations were observed for alcohol use. PCA revealed stable pro-inflammatory cytokine clustering, consistent across cohorts. Replication in analyses confirmed a robust, shared pro-inflammatory signature, and meta-analysis supported consistent associations with older age, higher BMI, and current smoking.ConclusionsOlder age, higher BMI, male sex, and current smoking are consistent and reproducible determinants of inflammatory cytokine profiles across European populations. These findings support the use of cytokine profiling in enhancing risk stratification for inflammation-related diseases.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12916-026-04941-6.Keywords: pro-inflammatory cytokines, anti-inflammatory cytokines, systemic inflammation, aging, body mass index, smoking, sex differences, population-based cohort, determinants, immune profiling

Background

Pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as IL-6 and TNF, play a central role in regulating the immune response by facilitating localized and systemic inflammation in reaction to injury or infection [1]. In contrast, anti-inflammatory cytokines help mitigate and control the pro-inflammatory cytokine response, minimizing potential tissue damage and reducing the risk of chronic disease development [2]. While cytokines often exhibit pleiotropic behavior, complicating strict classification, certain cytokines are generally recognized for their predominant pro- or anti-inflammatory roles [3–13].

Dysregulation of the balance between pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory cytokines has been implicated in a wide range of pathological conditions, including cancer progression [14, 15], autoimmune diseases [7, 16], psychiatric disorders [17], allergy [18], cardiovascular diseases [6, 19, 20], COVID-19 [21], neurodegenerative diseases [22] and other chronic conditions. Although aspects of cytokine biology remain enigmatic, identifying how key factors such as age, sex, and lifestyle, govern cytokine levels can provide valuable insights into the underlying immune pathways driving disease risk, severity, and progression.

Despite the growing recognition of the role of inflammation in chronic disease, few large-scale population studies have comprehensively assessed how demographic and lifestyle factors influence both pro- and anti-inflammatory cytokine levels [23–32]. Most existing work focuses on single biomarkers or smaller cohorts, leaving critical gaps in our understanding of how these factors shape multi-cytokine profiles. Moreover, cytokines such as IL-6 and IL-10 are used in clinical settings, and fluctuations in their levels may precede the onset of overt clinical symptoms [33–36], highlighting the need to pinpoint determinants that drive these changes at the population level. A better understanding of how these cytokine levels vary across populations can enhance clinical interpretation, support early detection of immune dysregulation, and ultimately guide development of targeted interventions.

The primary objective of this study was to identify the major determinants of common pro- and anti-inflammatory cytokines in a population-based setting, focusing on modifiable factors such as smoking, BMI, and alcohol consumption, as well as non-modifiable factors like age and sex. These determinants were selected based on biological plausibility, previous literature, and availability in our cohort. Furthermore, we applied principal component analysis (PCA) to uncover underlying patterns within pro- and anti-inflammatory cytokine profiles, which could provide further insights into their coordinated regulation. The findings were replicated in two independent European cohorts to strengthen the generalizability of the results.

Methods

Study participants

This study was conducted in the Rotterdam Study (RS), a large prospective population-based cohort study ongoing since 1990. The RS consists of citizens living in the Ommoord region of Rotterdam, The Netherlands, comprising of 4 sub-cohorts, including RS-I (N = 7983), RS-II (N = 3011), RS-III (N = 3932), and RS-IV (N = 3005). Participants, aged 40 years and older, were examined at baseline and invited for assessment every 3–5 years.

We replicated our findings in two independent population-based cohorts: the Study of Health in Pomerania (SHIP) in northeast Germany and the Polish Longitudinal University Study (PLUS) in Bialystok, Poland. SHIP, initiated in 1997, includes two baseline cohorts—SHIP-START (N = 4,308) and SHIP-TREND-0 (N = 4,420)—comprising adults aged 20 to 79 years with follow-up every 5 and 7 years, respectively. PLUS, modeled after SHIP, was launched at the Medical University of Bialystok and randomly selected residents aged 20 to 80 years for baseline and repeated follow-up assessments every five years (8320 invitations). Proteomic profiling was conducted in a randomly selected subsample (N = 745) in PLUS, and as matched controls free of dilated cardiomyopathy (N = 342) from a separate project in SHIP-TREND.

RS, SHIP, and PLUS comply with the declaration of Helsinki and have been approved by the medical ethics committee of the Erasmus University Medical Center (registration number MEC 02.1015) and the Dutch Ministry of Health, Welfare and Sport (Population Screening Act WBO, license number 1071272–159,521-PG), by the Ethics Committee of the University of Greifswald, and by the Ethics Committee of the Medical University of Bialystok, respectively. A detailed description of the RS, SHIP, and PLUS have been previously provided [37–40]. Supplementary information for RS, SHIP, and PLUS is provided in Additional Files 1–3, respectively.

Assessment of plasma proteins

Blood was drawn at the RS research center in Ommoord from 2006 to 2008, from SHIP-TREND-0 2008–2012, and from PLUS 2017–2021 and the time of blood collection was designated as the study baseline. In RS, serum samples were then stored at -80 °C, until plasma proteomic measurements were conducted in 2016–2018. The Olink Inflammation Panel (Olink Proteomics, Uppsala, Sweden) was used to quantify 92 inflammation-related proteins in 3,517 participants via Proximity Extension Assay (PEA) technology. In this assay, dual DNA‐labeled antibodies bind their respective protein targets in proximity, enabling sensitive and specific detection by qPCR. The resulting Ct (Cycle threshold) values are presented as Normalized Protein eXpression (NPX) unit on log2 scale. An increase of one NPX unit corresponds to a twofold increase in protein concentration. A comprehensive list of measured biomarkers (including cytokines, chemokines, and growth factors) is provided in: Olink Inflammation Panel. Quality control (QC) procedures included calculating a limit of detection (LOD) for each protein, excluding proteins for which > 90% of samples fell below the LOD, and removing samples that failed Olink’s internal QC. Of the 3,517 individuals and 92 proteins measured, 61 participants and 8 proteins were excluded based on these QC criteria, yielding the final RS dataset. Plate effects were assessed using linear regression across 40 plates, with no significant plate effects detected. Detailed information on SHIP-TREND and PLUS can be found in the Additional Files 2 and 3; Tables S1. Absolute IL-6 levels were additionally measured in PLUS and SHIP-START (from 1997 to 2001) using the electrochemiluminescence immunoassay “ECLIA” on COBAS (Roche, Basel, Switzerland) and the Immulite 2500 system (Siemens Healthineers, Munich, Germany) in pg/mL, respectively, as detailed previously [37, 39].

Pro- and anti-inflammatory cytokines

Cytokines were classified as pro- or anti-inflammatory based on an extensive review of current literature [2–6, 17–21, 28, 41–74] (Additional File 4, Table S1), with emphasis on their roles in chronic diseases, including cancer, cardiovascular, neurodegenerative, autoimmune, and allergic diseases. Pro-inflammatory cytokines included IL-6, TNF, IFN-γ, IL-18, IL-17 A, IL-17 C, and IL-1β, selected for their roles in immune activation and chronic inflammation. Anti-inflammatory cytokines, IL-4, IL-13, IL-10, and TGF-β, were included based on their established roles in immune regulation and resolution of inflammation. Of the biologically relevant cytokines identified (n = 11), only 9 were available on the Olink Inflammation Panel (Additional File 5) and were therefore included in the final analyses.

Baseline measurements of potential determinants

The determinants included in the study were age, sex, smoking status, alcohol consumption, and body mass index (BMI). Covariates were selected based on biological plausibility and previous literature, and a directed acyclic graph illustrating variable relationships can be found in Additional File 1, Figure S1. Smoking status and alcohol consumption were obtained through questionnaires. Smoking status was defined as never, current, or former smoker and alcohol consumption was measured in grams per day. Alcohol consumption was categorized according to WHO guidelines with a daily consumption of 0–10 g/day considered mild, 10–20 g/day moderate, and > 20 g/day as heavy [75]. Anthropometric data was collected at respective research centers during physical examination in all three cohorts. Height (m) and weight (kg) were measured, from which BMI was calculated (kg/m2). Further details of determinant measurement methods are available elsewhere, as well as detailed description of SHIP and PLUS cohorts [37, 39, 40].

Statistical analyses

After NPX on log2 scale proteins received from the Olink Platform, plasma cytokine levels were then z-standardized for comparability in all cohorts. Associations of the demographic and lifestyle factors with the cytokine levels were assessed using multiple linear regression (unadjusted and adjusted for age, sex, BMI, smoking, and alcohol). Stratified analyses were conducted by sex. Non-linear effects were modeled using natural cubic splines with 3 degrees of freedom, after fitting linear models. The decision to use splines or linear models was assessed via AIC, BIC and visual inspection of spline plots, which explains the variability in linear terms across cytokines. A uniform multiple testing correction was not applied in order to decrease the risk of Type II errors and prioritize pattern discovery [76–78]. The significance threshold was set at 0.05, with an FDR sensitivity correction applied solely to the determinant-cytokine associations, including only the overall spline term and excluding intercepts, in the main cohort to enhance transparency and robustness (Additional File 1, Table S3). Missingness was ≤ 1.27%; complete case and multiple imputation by chained equations was performed (5 imputations, 20 iterations) for independent variables. PCA was performed on standardized cytokine data to identify major patterns. Component loadings were examined to assess cytokine contributions to biological pathways. Associations between PC1 and age were examined in all cohorts using linear and spline-based regression models, with additional adjustment for sex, smoking status, alcohol use, and BMI. Additional analysis using absolute IL-6 levels in SHIP-START and PLUS were analyzed with the same regression framework, using both raw and log-transformed IL-6 models. Raw IL-6 models were considered the primary models and assessed for clinical interpretability, while log-transformed models were used as sensitivity models due to skewed IL-6 distribution. Residual plots for both models were examined to assess normality and homoscedasticity. Next, cohort-specific regression coefficients and their standard errors were pooled using a random-effects meta-analysis (DerSimonian and Laird method) with inverse-variance weighting to account for between-cohort heterogeneity, measured by I2.

All data analyses were conducted using R software (R-4.2.1; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) using the following packages: reshape2, splines, tibble, tidyverse, broom, dplyr, naniar, mice, rlang, jtools, table1, meta, and FactoMineR.

Determinants of pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory cytokines

Demographic factors

Overall, age was positively associated with pro-inflammatory cytokines. Age increased IL-6, TNF, IFN-γ, and IL-17 A linearly (all P < 0.01; Fig. 1), while IL-18 and IL-17 C in RS showed non-linear (reverse S- and J-shaped) associations (P < 0.0001).

Similar trends were observed in PLUS and SHIP-TREND, with age increasing all pro-inflammatory cytokines (P < 0.05), with the exception of IL-17 A in SHIP-TREND (Additional Files 2 and 3, Figures S1). Age-related changes in anti-inflammatory cytokines were consistent for IL-10 in RS (β: 0.007, P < 0.0001), SHIP-TREND (β: 0.012, P < 0.011; Additional File 2; Figure S1) and PLUS (β: 0.007, P < 0.014; Additional File 3; Figure S1). However, age decreased IL-4 in RS (P < 0.041) but not in SHIP-TREND or PLUS.

Sex differences were evident in cytokine profiles, with females exhibiting lower levels of IL-18 (β: -0.41, 95% CI: -0.48; -0.34), IL-17 C (β: -0.32, 95% CI: -0.39; -0.25), and IL-10 (β: -0.083, 95% CI: -0.15; -0.013) compared to males (Fig. 2).

IL-6, TNF, IL-17 A, and other cytokines showed no consistent sex-related differences across cohorts.

Determinants stratified by sex

The sex stratified analyses can be seen in Table 1, and Additional Files 1–3. In RS males with heavy vs. mild alcohol consumption (β: 0.15, P < 0.04) had higher IL-6, while females with heavy and moderate alcohol consumption (P < 0.050, P < 0.043) had lower IL-18. These patterns were not replicated in SHIP-TREND or PLUS. IL-13 decreased with age in RS males (P < 0.0047), with no effect in PLUS or SHIP-TREND. Smoking effects on IL-13 were sex-specific in RS: lower in females (former vs. never) (β: -0.11) and marginally lower in RS males (current vs. never) (β: -0.12; P < 0.1). In contrast, in PLUS male current smokers had higher IL-13 (β: 0.35; P < 0.012), with no female effect. Male former smokers had lower IL-10 (β: -0.206, P < 0.04). BMI was linked to lower IL-17 A (P < 0.01) and higher IL-18 in males (P < 0.03), and higher IFN-γ in females in PLUS.

Principal component analysis

PCA showed that PC1 explained 24.2% of the variance in RS, similar to SHIP-TREND (29%) and PLUS (26.9%) (Additional File 1: Figure S6.1 and Additional Files 2 and 3, Figures S10.1). The next seven PCs each captured 8-13.6%, reflecting a similar pattern across cohorts. In all cohorts, PC1 was driven by IL-6, IL-17 C, IL-17 A, IL-18, TNF, IFN-γ and IL-10. PC2 was shaped by IL-13 and IL-4 in RS and SHIP-TREND, whereas PC3 showed this pattern in PLUS. Remaining PCs reflected the multidimensional cytokine structure. PC1 showed a robust positive association with chronological age in unadjusted and adjusted models in all cohorts, with spline-based analyses indicating a non-linear increase in PC1 at older ages (Additional File 1, Table S6 and Figure S7, Additional Files 2 and 3, Table 5 and Figures S11).

Absolute IL-6 analysis with determinants

In PLUS (N = 367), age (β = 0.005, P < 0.001), BMI (β: 0.019, P < 0.006), and male sex (β: − 0.165, P < 0.020) were associated with IL-6 (sensitivity log-transformed model); only male sex was significant in the primary raw model (β: − 1.69, P < 0.042) (Additional File 3, Tables S7 and S8). For illustration, two hypothetical males with mild alcohol use showed IL-6 levels of 4.0 pg/mL (75 years, BMI 38, current smoker) vs. 2.2 pg/mL (30 years, BMI 22, never smoker). In SHIP-START (N = 4,139), age (Raw: β = 0.023; Log: β = 0.005), BMI (Raw: β = 0.034; Log: β = 0.009), male sex (Raw: β = 0.223; Log: β = 0.046), and current smoking (Raw: β = 0.306; Log: β = 0.071), all P < 0.05, were associated with IL-6 (Additional File 2, Tables S7 and S8). Moderate alcohol use was inversely associated (Raw: β = − 0.311; Log: β = − 0.051). Meta-analysis confirmed associations in primary models, a 10-year age or 10-unit BMI increase corresponded to ~ 0.06 pg/mL and ~ 0.09 pg/mL higher IL-6, respectively, and smokers had 0.083 pg/mL higher IL-6 than non-smokers (Fig. 6 and Additional File 6: Tables S1 and S2).

Discussion

In this large, multi-cohort, population-based study, we assessed how demographic and lifestyle factors shape systemic cytokine profiles involved in chronic inflammation and immune regulation. Our findings reveal robust, often non-linear associations between age, sex, BMI, and smoking with both pro- and anti-inflammatory cytokines, including IL-6, IL-18, IL-17 C, and IL-10. Pro-inflammatory patterns were consistent across replication cohorts and highlight distinct immunological signatures linked to modifiable risk factors. These findings underscore the immunological imprint of aging, adiposity, and tobacco exposure in driving systemic inflammation—factors directly relevant to chronic disease risk and immune-mediated conditions.

Higher age showed the most consistent association, independent of other determinants, with elevated pro-inflammatory cytokines, supporting the concept of “inflammaging,” a chronic, low-grade inflammation linked to diseases like cardiovascular and neurodegenerative disorders [79, 80]. This aligns with previous reports of age-related IL-6 increases [23, 29, 30, 81–85], though literature on TNF changes were inconclusive [29, 81–83, 86, 87]. Mechanistically, aging promotes a senescence‑associated secretory phenotype and mitochondrial dysfunction, which drive chronic inflammation via cytokine release and activation of NF‑κB and inflammasomes [88, 89]. Interestingly, the age-related non-linear increase in IL-18 is not likely driven by sex hormones, as both males and females exhibit similar non-linear increases. This increase may reflect a transition from its role in innate immunity during early adulthood [90], responding to infections and stressors, to inflammaging in later life [91]. On the other hand, IL-17 C’s involvement in aging aligns with broader evidence of Th17-axis potentiation and inflammation in older adults [92]. This novel finding for IL-17 C is important, given the limited research on IL-17 C in aging. Furthermore, we also observed a consistent age-related increase in IL-10, suggesting a compensatory anti-inflammatory response [93]. While previous studies have been inconsistent [23, 24, 86], our findings are replicated in both PLUS and SHIP cohorts. This is also evidenced by data from IL-10 where overexpressing mice showed protection against age-related insulin resistance [94], support a protective regulatory role for IL-10 in aging.

Beyond age-related trends, we observed notable sex differences in cytokine levels, with males having higher IL-18 and IL-17 C in RS, SHIP, and PLUS. Elevated IL-18 in males aligns with prior reports in cancer [95, 96], while IL-17 C differences are novel. Sex hormones may contribute—mouse studies link estrogen depletion to increased IL-18 and IL-17 via Th17 activation, which suggest lower estrogen in males may reduce protection against related inflammation [97, 98], and explain higher male susceptibility to certain inflammatory diseases. In contrast, IFN-γ levels were higher in females in RS, with similar but non-significant trends in SHIP and PLUS. Although IL-18 induces IFN-γ [99], estrogen may enhance IFN-γ independently, as seen in multiple sclerosis (MS) and other patient populations [100, 101]. Interestingly, sex differences in anti-inflammatory cytokines were less consistent, with lower IL-10 in SHIP and higher IL-4 in PLUS. Experimental studies found higher IL-10 males following TLR8/9 stimulation or viral infections [102], supporting the role of hormonal or environmental influences. No significant sex differences were seen in IL-6, TNF, IL-17 A, or IL-13, with most literature inconclusive, likely due to small, disease-focused studies [28, 65, 69, 85, 103–108].These findings underscore the importance of sex- and population-specific immune variation and the value of large-scale studies in detecting subtle cytokine differences.

Lifestyle factors such as smoking also demonstrated distinct immunological signatures, including current vs. never smokers having higher IL-6, IL-18, IL-17 C, and IL-10, and lower IL-13 and IFN-γ. Current smoking showed the most consistent IL-6 elevation across RS, SHIP-TREND, SHIP-START and PLUS, aligning with prior research [109]. Smoking increased IL-17 C, but not IL-17 A in RS—likely reflecting its epithelial origin and distinct function [92, 110, 111]. While SHIP and PLUS showed similar, non-significant trends for IL-17 C, current smokers had higher IL-17 A in PLUS, aligning with previous findings [112]. Former smokers generally showed cytokine levels comparable to never smokers, suggesting potential immune recovery. In RS, IL-10 increased and IL-13 decreased, suggesting disrupted anti-inflammatory signaling. While, IL-4, IL-13 and IL-10 were not associated with smoking in SHIP or PLUS, prior studies suggest IL-13 polymorphisms enhance Th2 responses and asthma risk after tobacco exposure [113]. These differences, along with regional factors like pathogen exposure and low cytokine detectability, particularly in SHIP-TREND, may also explain cohort variability. While prior studies, often limited by small or disease-specific populations, reported mixed findings [25], [114], [115], our large, population-based data reinforce smoking’s pro-inflammatory impact while highlighting cohort-specific variation in anti-inflammatory responses.

Higher BMI was strongly associated with pro-inflammatory cytokines, demonstrating non-linear patterns. IL-6, IL-18, and TNF increased in J-shaped patterns, while IL-17 A was U-shaped and IL-10 decreased, indicating immune dysregulation at both BMI extremes. Elevated absolute IL-6 levels reinforced its role as a key marker across of adiposity-linked inflammation, consistent with secretion from white adipose tissue, an active endocrine organ producing IL-6 and TNF [46, 116]. BMI-related differences in IL-17 A across cohorts, specifically the U-shape association observed in the older RS cohort compared with SHIP and PLUS, may reflect age-related frailty in older adults, supported by evidence linking elevated IL-17 A to sarcopenia and reduced BMI in older adults [117]. Reduced IL-10 at higher BMI could reflect reduced anti-inflammatory capacity and increased cardiometabolic disease risk. Further, polymorphisms in the IL-10 gene may contribute to differences in IL-10 regulation across populations [118]. Prior studies have reported mixed findings, often limited by small sample sizes or stratification [25, 26]. While one large study aligned with our IL-6 and IL-18 results but not IL-17 A [114], this may reflect differences in statistical power; two additional cohorts support our IL-6, IL-18, and TNF associations [32, 85]. Overall, elevated BMI appears to disrupt cytokine balance through inflammatory signaling from endocrine-active adipose tissue.

In contrast, alcohol consumption showed weaker and less consistent associations with cytokines; however, sex-stratified analysis revealed higher IL-6 levels in males with heavy alcohol use, a finding not replicated in SHIP or PLUS. The lack of replication could be attributed to sex-specific effects, reporting bias, or smaller sample sizes. Findings in RS align with prior evidence linking heavy alcohol consumption to IL-6 and TNF [119], possibly through inflammation driven by increased gut permeability [120]. Further research is needed to clarify the role of alcohol-related inflammation and its sex-specific variability across populations.

To explore underlying cytokine patterns beyond individual associations, we performed PCA. PCA revealed two dominant cytokine groupings. PC1, driven by pro-inflammatory cytokines and IL-10, likely reflect the inflammaging axis [79, 80, 114, 121–127]. Importantly, this interpretation is supported by the robust positive association with PC1 and chronological age in all cohorts, reinforcing PC1 as an age-associated inflammaging-related cytokine axis. These distinct principal components highlight the presence of coordinated cytokine responses, reflecting underlying immunological axes that may vary by cohort or context.

Our study is limited by its predominantly European population, reliance on self-reported smoking and alcohol consumption, its cross-sectional design, and limited cytokine panel. While the directionality of most determinants is relatively well-established, the relationship with BMI and cytokine levels is assumed and warrants further investigation. PCA may not fully capture non-linear or complex cytokine relationships. Further, differences in assay platforms and sampling periods may introduce residual heterogeneity. However, the consistency in direction and magnitude of standardized associations across cohorts supports the robustness of the observed relationships. Additionally, in SHIP-TREND IL-17 A, IL-13 and IL-4 required extrapolation of values below the detection limit. Extrapolation introduces uncertainty and potential bias, as values below the detection limit may not accurately reflect true cytokine levels. However, similar findings in the other cohorts, with fewer non-detectable values, suggest that the extrapolation did not materially impact the results. Despite these limitations, key strengths include a large population-based cohort, replication in two independent cohorts, and comprehensive analysis of demographic and lifestyle factors. The inclusion of absolute IL-6 levels enhances clinical relevance, and PCA provided insights into cytokine clustering and inflammatory pathways beyond individual markers.

Conclusions

In this large, population-based study, higher age, male sex, higher BMI, and current smoking emerged as key determinants of systemic cytokine profiles, with replication in two independent cohorts. Consistent pro-inflammatory cytokine patterns suggest stable, systemic immune activation, while variability in anti-inflammatory markers highlights possible population-specific regulatory differences. These findings suggest the role of chronic inflammation in age-related disease and point to key demographic and lifestyle targets for prevention. Longitudinal studies are needed to clarify the temporal dynamics of cytokine regulation and inform personalized strategies for managing inflammation-driven conditions.
