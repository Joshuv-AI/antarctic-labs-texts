Preoperative pterygium extension rate predicts postoperative corneal optical recovery after pterygium excision
Authors: Akio Miyakoshi, Naoki Toyoda, Hiroshi Katayama, Atsushi Hayashi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPterygium progression induces corneal optical distortion, and delayed surgery may limit postoperative recovery of corneal optics. However, quantitative criteria indicating the latest acceptable timing of surgery remain unclear. This study aimed to determine extension-rate thresholds predicting postoperative normalization of corneal asymmetry and higher-order irregularity (HOI).MethodsThis retrospective observational study included 134 eyes with primary pterygium that underwent surgical excision. The pterygium extension rate was calculated from slit-lamp photographs as the ratio of the apex-to-limbus distance to the horizontal corneal diameter. Corneal asymmetry and HOI within the central 3-mm zone were measured preoperatively and 1 month postoperatively using swept-source anterior segment optical coherence tomography. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analyses were used to determine cutoff values predicting postoperative normalization.ResultsThe pterygium extension rate correlated significantly with preoperative corneal asymmetry (r = 0.5899, p < 0.001) and HOI (r = 0.5462, p < 0.001). ROC analysis showed good predictive ability for postoperative corneal asymmetry (AUC 0.80; cutoff 0.359; sensitivity 58.0%, specificity 89.3%) and moderate predictive ability for HOI (AUC 0.74; cutoff 0.292; sensitivity 72.3%, specificity 64.7%). Postoperative normalization occurred in 62.7% of eyes for asymmetry and 38.1% for HOI.ConclusionsThe pterygium extension rate is a simple clinical metric that predicts postoperative optical recovery. These extension-rate thresholds may help support clinical estimation of how long pterygium surgery may be deferred while maintaining the possibility of postoperative optical recovery.

Background

Pterygium is a common ocular surface disorder characterized by the progressive extension of conjunctival fibrovascular tissue onto the cornea, often causing corneal irregularity and degrading optical quality. When the lesion approaches the visual axis or results in clinically significant visual disturbance, surgical excision remains the only effective treatment option [1]. The primary goals of surgery are to prevent recurrence and preserve visual function. However, determining the optimal timing of surgery remains challenging because surgeons must balance recurrence risk against the possibility of persistent or irreversible corneal optical distortion [2].

The risk of recurrence after pterygium excision has been documented extensively, and several clinical factors, including younger age, larger lesion size, and increased vascularity, have been associated with increased recurrence rates [3]. Because younger patients are at a particularly high risk of recurrence, many clinicians prefer to defer surgery in this population. However, continued progression of the pterygium can exacerbate corneal asymmetry and increase higher-order aberrations [4–6], some of which may persist even after excision and limit optical recovery. These postoperative optical irregularities are often difficult to correct with spectacles and may have lasting adverse effects on optical quality [7–10]. Continued progression of pterygium may compromise postoperative optical recovery, creating a clinical dilemma regarding surgical timing. Previous studies have demonstrated significant associations between pterygium size and corneal optical distortion, including irregular astigmatism and higher-order aberrations [5, 6, 11, 12]. However, these investigations did not establish quantitative thresholds indicating when surgery should ideally be performed to preserve postoperative optical recovery.

Several grading systems based on pterygium size or corneal topography have been proposed to guide surgical indications [13]. However, existing systems primarily assess disease severity and do not provide quantitative guidance regarding how long surgery may be safely deferred while preserving the possibility of postoperative optical recovery.

Earlier intervention may reduce the risk of irreversible corneal remodeling, whereas delayed surgery is often preferred in younger patients because of their higher risk of recurrence. These considerations highlight the need for a simple and quantitative preoperative indicator that may help predict postoperative corneal optical normalization.

Although several size-based criteria have been proposed to guide indications for surgery [11, 13], they do not define how long surgery can be safely deferred while preserving the likelihood of postoperative optical recovery, nor do they establish thresholds beyond which normalization of corneal asymmetry and higher-order aberrations becomes less likely. To address this gap, the present study aims to estimate the latest clinically acceptable window based on the likelihood of corneal parameter recovery at 1 month after surgery. Using objective measurements of the pterygium extension rate and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis, we sought to identify quantitative thresholds that may support clinical decision-making regarding surgical timing.

Methods

Study design and participants

This retrospective observational study was conducted at Toyama University Hospital and included patients who underwent pterygium excision between December 2019 and December 2025. Eligible participants were those diagnosed with primary pterygium and who were followed for at least 1 month postoperatively. Patients with pseudopterygium, recurrent pterygium, or insufficient postoperative data were excluded.

Measurements

All examinations were conducted preoperatively and at 1 month postoperatively. The pterygium extension rate was calculated from slit-lamp photographs as the ratio of the distance from the nasal limbus to the pterygium apex divided by the horizontal corneal diameter. A schematic illustration of the measurement method is provided in Fig. 1. In this figure, the pterygium extension rate is defined as A/B, where A represents the distance from the nasal limbus to the pterygium apex and B represents the horizontal corneal diameter measured on the same slit-lamp photograph. The horizontal corneal diameter was measured preoperatively from slit-lamp photographs, using the same image on which the limbus-to-apex distance was determined. All measurements for calculating the pterygium extension rate were therefore obtained preoperatively to reflect a clinically applicable presurgical index. Because the pterygium extension rate was calculated as a ratio using two measurements obtained from the same slit-lamp photograph, the influence of image magnification was minimized. Corneal measurements, including corneal asymmetry and higher-order corneal irregularity (HOI) within the central 3-mm zone, as well as mean keratometric power and corneal astigmatism, were obtained using a swept-source anterior segment optical coherence tomography device (CASIA2; TOMEY Corporation, Nagoya, Japan). Corneal asymmetry and HOI were Fourier-derived indices automatically calculated by the CASIA2 system from the anterior corneal power distribution within the central 3-mm zone. Corneal asymmetry represents the asymmetric component of corneal power distribution, whereas HOI represents the higher-order irregular component that cannot be explained by lower-order regular astigmatism. These indices were used as objective parameters of corneal optical irregularity. Corneal endothelial cell density was assessed using a noncontact specular microscope (EM-4000; TOMEY Corporation).

Outcome measures and definitions

The primary outcome was the cutoff value of the pterygium extension rate associated with postoperative normalization of corneal asymmetry and HOI at 1 month. The thresholds for postoperative normalization were based on the normative reference ranges provided by the CASIA2 system and derived from previously published corneal morphological data in healthy Japanese eyes [14]. Although these values are widely used in clinical interpretation of CASIA2 measurements, they should be regarded as device-specific reference values rather than universally established standards. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis was performed to assess the predictive ability of the pterygium extension rate, with normalization status as the dependent variable. The area under the curve (AUC) was calculated, and the optimal cutoff value was identified using the Youden index.

Secondary outcomes included safety and postoperative changes in corneal morphology. Safety was evaluated using pre- to postoperative changes in corneal endothelial cell density. Morphological changes were assessed by comparing preoperative and postoperative values of mean keratometric power, corneal astigmatism, corneal asymmetry, and higher-order irregularity.

Surgical procedure

Under local anesthesia, the pterygium head was excised, followed by the application of a microsponge soaked in 0.04% mitomycin C to the subconjunctival space for 3 min. The area was then irrigated thoroughly with 350 mL of normal saline. The cut edge of the pterygium was inverted and secured with two interrupted 6 − 0 polyglactin sutures to provide adequate tension along the excision margin. The procedure was completed with the scleral bed left exposed.

Statistical analysis

Normality of distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Because the variables did not follow a normal distribution, preoperative and postoperative values of corneal parameters (corneal endothelial cell density, mean keratometric power, corneal astigmatism, corneal asymmetry, and higher-order irregularity) were compared using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Correlations between the pterygium extension rate and preoperative optical parameters were evaluated using Pearson correlation coefficients because visual inspection of scatter plots demonstrated approximately linear relationships despite non-normal distributions. Scatter plots with regression lines were generated for visualization.

For ROC analyses, postoperative corneal asymmetry (≤ 0.52 D vs. > 0.52 D) and higher-order irregularity (≤ 0.20 D vs. > 0.20 D) were dichotomized, and ROC curves were constructed using the pterygium extension rate as a continuous predictor variable in 134 eyes. Additional ROC analyses were performed using other potentially relevant preoperative parameters, including mean keratometric power, corneal astigmatism, preoperative corneal asymmetry, and preoperative HOI, in order to compare their discriminatory ability with that of the pterygium extension rate. AUC values, optimal cutoff values determined by the Youden index, sensitivity, and specificity were calculated for each parameter. AUC values with 95% confidence intervals were calculated, and optimal cutoffs were determined by maximizing the Youden index. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

The pterygium extension rate demonstrated a significant positive correlation with preoperative corneal asymmetry (r = 0.5899, p < 0.001; Fig. 2A). Linear regression showed that a higher extension rate was associated with greater asymmetry (slope β = 6.92; intercept = − 1.12). Similarly, the extension rate correlated positively with preoperative HOI (r = 0.5462, p < 0.001; Fig. 2B), with regression analysis indicating a significant association (slope β = 14.85; intercept = − 2.86).

Postoperative corneal asymmetry was within the normal range (≤ 0.52 D) in 84 eyes (62.7%) and exceeded this threshold in 50 eyes (37.3%). ROC analysis demonstrated good discriminative ability for postoperative corneal asymmetry, with an AUC of 0.80 (95% CI, 0.71–0.87; Fig. 3A). The optimal cutoff value for predicting postoperative asymmetry > 0.52 D was 0.359, corresponding to a sensitivity of 58.0% and a specificity of 89.3%.

Postoperative HOI was within the normal range (≤ 0.20 D) in 51 eyes (38.1%), whereas 83 eyes (61.9%) exceeded this value. ROC analysis showed a moderate ability of the pterygium extension rate to discriminate between postoperative HOI categories, with an AUC of 0.74 (95% CI, 0.64–0.82; Fig. 3B). The optimal cutoff value for predicting postoperative HOI > 0.20 D was 0.292, providing a sensitivity of 72.3% and a specificity of 64.7%.

Additional ROC analyses were performed to compare the predictive ability of other preoperative corneal parameters with that of the pterygium extension rate (Table 3). Mean keratometric power demonstrated poor discriminatory ability for predicting postoperative normalization of both corneal asymmetry and HOI (AUC 0.562 and 0.538, respectively). In contrast, preoperative corneal astigmatism, corneal asymmetry, and HOI showed good predictive performance, with AUC values ranging from 0.742 to 0.832.

Among these parameters, preoperative HOI demonstrated the highest predictive performance for postoperative normalization of both corneal asymmetry (AUC 0.832) and HOI (AUC 0.800). Although the pterygium extension rate showed slightly lower AUC values than several OCT-derived optical parameters, its predictive ability remained clinically useful despite requiring only routine slit-lamp photographs without specialized corneal imaging.

Discussion

This study demonstrated that the pterygium extension rate was significantly associated with both preoperative corneal optical distortion and the likelihood of postoperative normalization of corneal optical metrics. The extension rate correlated linearly with increases in corneal asymmetry and HOI, and ROC-derived thresholds identified specific values beyond which postoperative normalization was less likely to occur. These findings indicate that extension rate may help estimate the acceptable window for surgical intervention.

Greater pterygium extension was significantly associated with increased Fourier-derived corneal asymmetry and HOI within the central optical zone. These results are consistent with previous Fourier and wavefront analyses showing that advancing pterygium induces progressive corneal distortion. Tomidokoro et al. reported proportional increases in asymmetry and HOI with lesion size [15], and Minami et al. noted sharp increases in Fourier components beyond specific encroachment levels [16]. Studies using AS-OCT have similarly documented increases in coma-like and total higher-order aberrations with pterygium enlargement [11, 16]. By applying consistent Fourier-based indices, the present findings extend prior work and support the association between increasing pterygium size and worsening corneal optics. Corneal asymmetry and HOI are not merely morphological indices but are also clinically relevant optical parameters. Previous studies have demonstrated that irregular astigmatism and higher-order aberrations degrade retinal image quality and visual performance [8–10, 17–19], particularly because these optical disturbances are often not fully correctable with spectacles [7]. Therefore, improvements in these indices may reflect meaningful recovery of corneal optical quality, although they do not directly represent subjective visual function.

Pterygium excision led to significant improvements in corneal asymmetry and HOI, indicating a partial recovery of optical quality. However, a considerable proportion of eyes failed to return to normal postoperative indices, consistent with earlier reports showing persistent irregularities in advanced lesions [15]. Histopathologic studies have demonstrated Bowman’s layer disruption, fibrovascular ingrowth, and elastotic stromal degeneration in long-standing pterygia, all of which may result in irreversible remodeling of the anterior stroma even after excision [1, 20]. The strong dependence of postoperative recovery on preoperative extension suggests that once morphological changes exceed a certain threshold, recovery of corneal optical quality within the early postoperative period becomes limited.

A key contribution of this study is the identification of quantitative size-based thresholds that can assist clinicians in determining not only when surgery becomes advisable but also how long surgery may be deferred safely, i.e., without compromising postoperative optical recovery. The pterygium extension rate demonstrated good predictive ability for postoperative corneal asymmetry (AUC 0.80; cutoff 0.359) and moderate predictive ability for HOI (AUC 0.74; cutoff 0.292). Because corneal asymmetry and HOI have been associated with degradation of optical quality in previous studies, the ability to predict postoperative normalization of these parameters may provide clinically useful information when considering the timing of surgical intervention. These thresholds may help clinicians estimate the likelihood of postoperative optical normalization when considering whether surgery can be deferred in individual patients. Additional ROC analyses demonstrated that several OCT-derived optical parameters, particularly preoperative HOI and corneal astigmatism, showed higher predictive performance than the pterygium extension rate. However, these parameters require specialized corneal imaging or topographic assessment. In contrast, the pterygium extension rate can be obtained using routine slit-lamp photographs alone, making it a simple and widely accessible surrogate marker despite slightly lower discriminatory performance. By defining a measurable limit beyond which optical recovery becomes less likely, the present findings support more practical clinical decision-making regarding the timing of surgery.

Several limitations should be considered. First, this was a retrospective single-center study, which may introduce selection bias and limit generalizability. Second, postoperative assessment was restricted to the 1-month time point; longer-term changes in corneal optical parameters remain unknown, and delayed recovery beyond the early postoperative period cannot be excluded. Third, although the pterygium extension rate was measured preoperatively to establish a clinically applicable presurgical index, pterygium-induced corneal distortion may have influenced the accuracy of horizontal corneal diameter measurements. Such distortion could introduce minor bias into the calculated extension rate. In addition, variations in photographic angle or ocular alignment may also have introduced minor measurement error. Fourth, other morphological characteristics, including lesion thickness, vascularity, and stromal depth, were not quantitatively evaluated and may also contribute to postoperative optical outcomes. Fifth, functional visual outcomes such as best-corrected visual acuity, contrast sensitivity, and patient-reported visual quality were not incorporated into the predictive model. Therefore, although corneal asymmetry and HOI are clinically relevant optical indices, the present findings primarily address early structural and optical normalization rather than direct functional visual recovery. Future studies incorporating functional visual assessments are needed to clarify the relationship between postoperative optical normalization and patient-perceived visual outcomes. In addition, the predictive performance of the identified cutoff values was moderate, particularly for HOI, and no internal or external validation cohort was available. Therefore, these thresholds should be interpreted as exploratory clinical reference points rather than definitive decision-making criteria. Finally, the normative thresholds used in the present study were based on device-provided reference ranges derived from previously published healthy-eye data in a Japanese population. Although these thresholds provide practical and objective criteria for defining postoperative normalization, they are not universally standardized across devices or populations. Therefore, the present cutoff values should be interpreted as exploratory reference values requiring further validation in other ethnic groups and clinical settings. Future prospective studies incorporating patient-reported visual quality, refractive outcomes, contrast sensitivity, and longer postoperative follow-up periods are needed to further establish the clinical significance and long-term validity of the proposed thresholds. External validation using independent cohorts would also strengthen the generalizability of the present findings.

In conclusion, the timing of surgical intervention plays an important role in postoperative optical recovery in primary pterygium. An extension rate of 0.359 was identified as the optimal cutoff for predicting failure of postoperative normalization of corneal asymmetry, whereas 0.292 was the corresponding cutoff for higher-order irregularity. These cutoff values differed between corneal asymmetry and HOI, suggesting that surgical timing may need to be considered differently depending on the optical parameter of interest. Because the pterygium extension rate can be obtained preoperatively from routine slit-lamp photographs, it may serve as a simple and clinically accessible adjunctive indicator to assist in clinical decision-making regarding the timing of surgery. By providing quantitative reference points linked to complementary optical endpoints, the present findings may support more balanced patient counseling when considering whether surgery can be safely deferred.
