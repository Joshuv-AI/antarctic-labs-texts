Haematological and Biochemical Parameters of COVID‐19 Patients Hospitalised at Selected Central Hospitals in Malawi: A Retrospective Study
Authors: Ackim Munthali, Ethel M'kandawire, Nozyechi Ngulube Chidumayo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsCOVID‐19 is an infectious respiratory disease caused by SARS‐CoV‐2. It was first reported in China in 2019 and spread rapidly to the rest of the world, leading to alarming morbidity and mortality. Haematological and biochemical parameters have been used in routine practice as prognostic markers of disease severity. However, this information has not been adequately documented in some African countries, particularly Malawi.MethodsA retrospective study analysed records of 367 RT‐PCR‐confirmed COVID‐19 patients hospitalised between 2020 and 2022 in three central hospitals in Malawi. Patients' records were studied for clinical, haematological, and biochemical parameters against reference intervals. Parameters were compared by hospital, comorbidity status, oxygen saturation (SpO2) (≤ 93% vs. > 93%), and survival status using Chi‐square or Fisher's exact tests for categorical variables and Kruskal–Wallis or Mann–Whitney U tests for continuous variables, as appropriate. Binary logistic regression was performed to identify independent predictors of non‐survival (p < 0.05).ResultsAmong the 367 reviewed cases, median age was 46 years, 51.5% were males, 51.23% had comorbidities, and 59.13% survived. Most haematological and biochemical parameters were within reference ranges, except haemoglobin, haematocrit, mean corpuscular haemoglobin concentration (MCHC), aspartate transaminase (AST), gamma‐glutamyl transferase (GGT), lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), and blood urea nitrogen (BUN). SpO2 of ≤ 93% was associated with low red blood cell distribution width and high leucocyte and neutrophil counts. Compared with survivors, non‐survivors were older and had lower SpO2 and higher respiratory rate, leucocyte and neutrophil counts, AST, alanine transaminase (ALT), GGT, alkaline phosphatase (ALP), LDH, BUN, and creatinine. Regression analysis showed age, ALT, ALP, and LDH as predictors of non‐survival.ConclusionAltered levels of leucocytes, neutrophils, AST, ALT, GGT, ALP, LDH, and BUN were associated with non‐survival. Age, ALT, ALP, and LDH were identified as predictors of adverse outcomes. These can help monitor COVID‐19 patients and inform disease prognosis.

Introduction

Coronavirus disease‐2019 (COVID‐19) is an infectious respiratory disease caused by severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus‐2 (SARS‐CoV‐2). The disease first came to global attention in December 2019 when cases of pneumonia of unknown cause were reported in Hubei Province, China [1]. From this initial outbreak, SARS‐CoV‐2 spread rapidly to the rest of the world and led to alarming morbidity and mortality [1]. Although the severity of COVID‐19 has declined due to immunity, vaccination, and improved care, the disease still presents with the emergence of new SARS‐CoV‐2 variants and mild to fatal outcomes [2]. As of 29 June 2026, COVID‐19 had caused at least 779 million cases and 7.1 million deaths globally. This burden includes more than 89,000 cases and over 2600 deaths in Malawi [3].

Structurally, SARS‐CoV‐2 is an enveloped, positive‐sense, single‐stranded RNA virus. It is classified within the family Coronaviridae and genus Betacoronavirus [1]. SARS‐CoV‐2 primarily affects the respiratory system and triggers inflammation in the alveoli and lung tissue, thereby compromising gas diffusion [4]. Infected individuals exhibit respiratory signs, which include an increased respiratory rate, low oxygen saturation, and fever [4, 5]. These clinical features have been used to distinguish severe from non‐severe COVID‐19. An oxygen saturation (SpO2) level below 93% on room air, a respiratory rate greater than 30 breaths per minute, and a persistent fever of 38°C or higher may indicate severe COVID‐19 [5]. SARS‐CoV‐2 can also infect other body systems and trigger inflammatory reactions. This results in severe damage to various body organs, including the liver, heart, colon, and kidneys [4, 6]. Thus, the clinical features of COVID‐19 are diverse and can occur as asymptomatic, mild, or severe pneumonia, with the latter exhibiting signs of multiorgan dysfunction [6]. In addition, the elderly and individuals with comorbidities such as pulmonary diseases, malignancy, diabetes mellitus, chronic kidney disease, and human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infection are severely affected by SARS‐CoV‐2 infection due to impaired immunity associated with these conditions [7].

Since the emergence of COVID‐19, studies across different geographical regions have demonstrated that disease severity is associated with distinct laboratory parameters, although the magnitude of these abnormalities varies between populations. A study from Brazil indicated that patients who were hospitalised and did not survive COVID‐19 had higher white blood cell counts, neutrophilia, and elevated prothrombin time and d‐dimer [8]. In India, severe cases were characterised by higher levels of interleukin‐6, neutrophil‐to‐lymphocyte ratio, C‐reactive protein (CRP), ferritin, and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) [9]. One review with studies from various developed countries highlighted neutrophilia, lymphopenia, thrombocytopenia, and distinctive blood cell morphological changes as recurring features of more advanced disease [10]. Thus, laboratory parameters like lymphocytopenia, neutrophilia, thrombocytopenia, increased CRP, d‐dimer, ferritin, LDH, liver enzymes, and renal function markers have been documented as important predictors of disease progression and multi‐organ injury [11, 12, 13, 14]. Evaluation of laboratory parameters in COVID‐19 patients is again essential for improving patient management and treatment approaches [15].

Despite various studies highlighting laboratory abnormalities as prognostic indicators of severe COVID‐19 [1, 14, 15], most of them have limited or no data from Sub‐Saharan African populations [16, 17], particularly Malawi. Studies on the COVID‐19 pandemic have also shown variations in morbidity, mortality, and laboratory parameters [7, 16, 18]. Further, the relationship between comorbidities and COVID‐19 has been described [7], but there is limited information on the interplay of comorbidities, as well as SpO2 and haemato‐biochemical parameters in COVID‐19 patients. Therefore, a retrospective study was conducted to evaluate the haematological and biochemical characteristics of hospitalised COVID‐19 patients in Malawi and their association with comorbidities, SpO2, and survival status. The results of this study can help monitor COVID‐19 patients, inform prognosis, optimise management approaches, and provide information on the association between comorbidities and laboratory parameters.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Records

We conducted a retrospective study using a consecutive sampling method. All medical records of hospitalised patients with confirmed SARS‐CoV‐2 infection by reverse transcriptase polymerase chain reaction (RT‐PCR) at Mzuzu Central Hospital (MZCH), Queen Elizabeth Central Hospital (QECH), and Zomba Central Hospital (ZMCH) between April 2020 and December 2022 were screened for eligibility. Records containing complete data on patient demographics (age and sex), clinical characteristics (oxygen saturation, respiratory rate, and temperature), and haematological and biochemical test results were included, while records with incomplete or missing data for the study variables were excluded. Haematological parameters used in this study were analysed using Sysmex XN‐1000 analysers at MZCH and ZMCH and a Sysmex XP‐300 analyser at QECH (Sysmex Corporation, Japan). Biochemical parameters were measured by Mindray BS‐360E, Mindray BS‐420, and Mindray BS‐120 chemistry analysers at the three respective hospitals (Mindray Bio‐Medical Electronics Co. Ltd, Shenzhen, China).

Ethical clearance was obtained from the National Health Sciences Research Committee (NHSRC) in Malawi (Protocol #23/04/4076). Since this was a retrospective study without direct contact with the patients, consent was waived by the same ethical review body. The hospitals also granted permission to conduct the study at their respective study sites through their Research Ethics Committees.

Haematological and Biochemical Data

The data on the haematological test results collected were on complete blood count (CBC) parameters, including red blood cells (RBC), haemoglobin, mean corpuscular volume (MCV), mean corpuscular haemoglobin (MCH), mean corpuscular haemoglobin concentration (MCHC), red cell distribution width (RDW), total leucocyte count, neutrophils, lymphocytes, and platelets (PLT). We also extracted the following biochemical test results: aspartate transaminase (AST), alanine transaminase (ALT), alkaline phosphatase (ALP), gamma‐glutamyl transaminase (GGT), total serum protein (TP), albumin (ALB), total bilirubin (TBIL), lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), and creatinine (Cr).

Data Analysis

The data collected were analysed using IBM SPSS version 26 and Microsoft Excel 2016. All statistical tests were two‐tailed, and a p‐value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Categorical variables were reported as counts (n) and percentages. The normality of numerical data was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Numerical variables were reported as medians and interquartile ranges. Haematological and biochemical parameters were interpreted using a standardised set of reference ranges adopted from the Malawi Ministry of Health Laboratory standard operating procedures, most of which are consistent with widely accepted reference ranges [19, 20]. The demographic, clinical, haematological, and biochemical parameters were compared among the three hospitals by Chi‐square test (χ 2) or Fisher's exact test for categorical variables and Kruskal–Wallis test for numerical variables as appropriate. Further, the records were analysed based on comorbidity status (present vs. absent), oxygen saturation levels (≤ 93% vs. > 93%) to differentiate between those with low oxygen saturation and normal oxygen saturation [5] and survival status (survivors and non‐survivors) by Chi‐square test or Fisher's exact test and Mann–Whitney U test as appropriate and p‐value < 0.05 was defined as statistically significant. To account for multiple comparisons, p‐values were interpreted after adjustment using the Bonferroni method. Multivariable analysis, binary logistic regression using the Enter method, was performed to identify factors independently associated with survival status among hospitalised COVID‐19 patients. Survival status was the dependent variable. All clinically relevant variables were included in the multivariable analysis. Accordingly, age, oxygen saturation, respiratory rate, temperature, and haematological and biochemical results were entered as continuous variables. Hospital, sex, and having any comorbidities were entered as categorical variables. Adjusted odds ratios (AORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were used to quantify the independent association between each predictor and survival status. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Discussion

SARS‐CoV‐2 can infect various systems and activate inflammatory reactions, thereby causing severe damage to multiple organs [4, 6], which may lead to abnormal laboratory test parameters, particularly in patients with severe disease [14]. Haematological and biochemical changes in RBC, leucocytes, platelets, hepatic and renal markers have been reported among COVID‐19 patients [21, 22, 23]. Similarly, our findings revealed deviation in haemoglobin, haematocrit, AST, GGT, LDH, and BUN from their normal ranges. Abnormal erythrocyte parameters in SARS‐CoV‐2 infection have been attributed to alterations in haematopoiesis, RBC membranes, size, shape, rigidity, and aggregation, as well as thrombotic processes [22, 23].

An increase in the median values of AST, GGT, and LDH could be due to liver injury and possibly cholestasis [21, 24]. However, other markers of cholestasis, such as ALP, showed a slight increase, and total bilirubin had a normal median value. An elevated GGT with normal ALP and bilirubin may reflect early or subclinical hepatobiliary stress, drug‐induced hepatotoxicity, and non‐hepatic sources of GGT [25]. High levels of LDH may indicate potential multiple organ injury in COVID‐19 cases [14]. The changes in serum biochemistry parameters in our study correspond to results reported in other populations [14, 26]. Renal function markers such as BUN and Cr were normal to high, probably due to kidney impairment, in agreement with other researchers [14, 27]. High levels of BUN can also reflect inflammation, catabolism, and renal hypoperfusion from various causes [25].

Further comparisons of our haematological and biochemical findings with studies from other populations suggest both similarities and differences in the manifestations of COVID‐19. Similar elevations in liver enzymes and LDH have been reported in African populations [17, 28] and others [14, 15]. On the other hand, lymphocytopenia and thrombocytopenia have been widely reported in the literature [10, 15, 28], but lymphocyte and platelet counts in our cohort showed no substantial changes. The differences may be attributed to ethnic and genetic diversity [18], variations in disease severity, and differences in laboratory methodologies [10]. As such, our findings provide additional insights into the interpretation of laboratory abnormalities.

Analysis of oxygen saturation, respiratory rate, body temperature, and haematological and serum biochemistry parameters in COVID‐19 patients revealed no significant association with comorbidity. This may suggest minimal influence of confounding factors on predicting COVID‐19 mortality at hospital admission, supporting the findings of Cocoş et al [29]. We also found that COVID‐19 patients with SpO2 ≤ 93% were significantly associated with reduced RDW and elevated leucocyte and neutrophil median counts. In others, abnormal RBC indices have been associated with poor COVID‐19 outcomes [17, 30]. Moreover, higher leucocyte counts in patients with SpO2 ≤ 93% are possibly due to SARS‐CoV‐2 infection of the lungs, leading to immune responses [4, 6]. Leucocytosis and neutrophilia have also been reported in previous studies [21, 27, 28], although these studies did not assess the effect of oxygen saturation on white blood cells.

With regard to clinical outcomes, survivors had a lower median age than non‐survivors, which is similar to the previous findings [31, 32]. Poor outcomes of infectious diseases among elderly individuals have long been attributed to ageing‐related alterations in immune and biochemical mechanisms [7, 33]. Deranged median readings of respiratory rate and oxygen saturation, especially in COVID‐19 severe cases and non‐survivors, have also been documented in the past [34]. SARS‐CoV‐2 primarily affects the respiratory system, causing alveoli and lung tissue inflammation, which compromises gas diffusion [35]. Consequently, individuals display respiratory clinical signs like dyspnoea, high respiratory rate and low oxygen saturation [34].

In this study, 51% of hospitalised COVID‐19 patients had at least one comorbidity, including HIV infection, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, asthma, tuberculosis, cancer, chronic lung disease, and kidney disease, consistent with findings from other regions [5, 36]. However, no statistically significant difference in comorbidity prevalence was observed between survivors and non‐survivors. In contrast, past studies have shown that pre‐existing comorbidities increase the risk of severe disease and mortality among COVID‐19 patients because of impaired immune function and physiology [7, 36]. Our findings could differ because of the relatively small sample sizes of some diseases, and the influence of other factors such as age, severity, and clinical management of comorbidities.

In the non‐survival group, the total median leucocyte and neutrophil counts were significantly higher than in the survival group. Similar prior studies also indicated that elevated leucocyte count, neutrophil count, and lymphocytopenia are effective at discriminating severe from mild COVID‐19 cases [10, 16, 27] or survival from non‐survival states [31]. According to the previous observations, SARS‐CoV‐2 causes a dysfunctional innate and adaptive immune response, leading to an increased activation of neutrophils and inflammatory cytokines, which are also associated with lymphopenia, inflammation, and tissue damage [10, 37].

Most serum biochemical characteristics in this study were more altered in non‐survivors than survivors. These results are consistent with other populations [14, 16, 31]. As explained previously, elevated levels of biochemical analytes in COVID‐19 patients can be due to SARS‐CoV‐2 infection, which exhibits multiorgan dissemination and extensive tissue damage [14, 38]. Routine biochemistry analytes that showed significant increases in non‐survivors primarily pertain to liver injury (AST, ALT, GGT, and ALP), renal system injury (BUN), and the indication of organ injury or multiple organ injury (LDH). This aligns with the notion that organs such as the liver, urinary system, and other vital organs may be affected by SARS‐CoV‐2 [38]. In related studies, high levels of biochemical parameters such as AST, ALT, LDH, BUN, and Cr were reported to significantly associate with severe disease states [27], ICU admission [39] and death among COVID‐19 patients [17, 39].

Multivariable logistic regression analysis identified age, ALT, ALP, and LDH as independent predictors of survival among hospitalised COVID‐19 patients, confirming previous findings [5, 15, 26]. Advanced age is a well‐established risk factor for poor outcomes, likely due to weakened immunity, which impairs the ability to respond effectively to infection [5, 7]. Elevated ALT may reflect liver injury resulting from the virus induced injury, systemic inflammation, and hypoxia [14, 38]. Similarly, elevated ALP was associated with non‐survival, which is consistent with the findings observed earlier [26, 30]. Although previous studies suggest that SARS‐CoV‐2‐related liver injury is predominantly hepatocellular [14, 26], there is a possibility that the virus may also interact with angiotensin‐converting enzyme receptor‐2 positive cholangiocytes and contribute to dysregulated liver function [40]. Increased LDH is a marker of tissue damage and has been consistently associated with severe disease and mortality [15, 38]. Thus, our findings suggest that older age and laboratory evidence of organ and tissue injury are important determinants of adverse outcomes in COVID‐19 patients.

This study had a number of limitations. The first limitation was its retrospective design, which restricted the availability of certain laboratory tests such as interleukins, C‐reactive protein, and serum ferritin. It has been reported that these parameters are also altered in COVID‐19 patients [21, 27]. The study was also limited in that it did not include healthy individuals, preventing the comparison of parameters with a control group.

Conclusion

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first multicentre retrospective study to assess the haemato‐biochemical changes in hospitalised COVID‐19 patients in Malawi. Our findings showed that altered levels of leucocytes, neutrophils, AST, ALT, GGT, ALP, LDH, and BUN were associated with non‐survival. Age, ALT, ALP, and LDH were identified as independent predictors of adverse outcomes. These parameters may serve as cost‐effective prognostic indicators of COVID‐19 patients and optimise management approaches.
