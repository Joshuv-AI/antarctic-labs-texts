Targeted social and behavior change communication (SBCC) in a high adolescent pregnancy setting of Bangladesh: a cluster-randomized study protocol engaging key family members
Authors: Seohyun Lee, Gohtbyeol Kim, SeYeon Kim, Narae Kim, Jihye Kim, Jieun Kim, Mohammad Sarwar Basher, Md Atikur Rahman, Syeda Jamila Siddika, Jahanara Hridita, Golam Fakhruddin, Sharmin Sultana Urmi, Uzzal Kumar Roy, Golam Mothabbir

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMaternal health service utilization remains low in Bangladesh, while maternal health outcomes are poor and adolescent pregnancy rates are high. In this context, targeted social and behavior change communication (SBCC) can be an effective approach. This targeted SBCC research was participatorily designed in collaboration with Bangladesh local partners and was funded as part of the 2023–27 KOICA Strategic Partnership Program (SPP)- Strengthening Maternal and Neonatal Health System in Rangpur, Bangladesh (No. 2023–0515). The study was informed by implementation and behavior change theories, engaging key family members in the intervention.MethodsA hybrid effectiveness-implementation type II cluster-randomized study was designed to measure key maternal health service utilization outcomes as well as implementation outcomes. A total of 750 participants classified into four targeted groups-1) adolescent pregnant women, 2) adult pregnant women, 3) husbands and 4) mothers-in-law, will be invited to participate in group-based interactive community courtyard sessions. These 5-month curriculum targeted SBCC sessions cover maternal and newborn health topics such as antenatal care (ANC), safe delivery, postnatal care (PNC), and neonatal care. The primary outcomes include four or more ANC visits, facility delivery, Postnatal Care (PNC) and neonatal care within 48 h after delivery. Secondary outcomes are perceptions on early marriage, maternal health-seeking behaviors, family support, decision making, and satisfaction with the information gained from the targeted SBCC sessions. To assess the effectiveness, baseline and endline surveys, along with endline focus group discussions (FGDs) will be conducted before and after the 5-month curriculum. Additionally, implementation outcomes such as adoption, penetration, acceptability, coverage, feasibility, fidelity, and sustainability will be measured.DiscussionThis study engages key decision-makers such as pregnant women, their husbands, and mothers-in-law, to enhance maternal health service utilization. As Bangladesh is a patriarchal society, men within households are often the primary decision-makers regarding marriage and reproductive matters. Moreover, the child marriage and adolescent pregnancy rates remain high, contributing to persistently elevated maternal and neonatal mortality. Background

In rural communities of Bangladesh, early marriage and adolescent pregnancy remain both prevalent and culturally accepted. According to the 2022 Bangladesh Demographic and Health Survey (BDHS), 50.1% of women aged 20 to 24 were married by the age of 18, with the percentage rising to 57.7% in Rangpur Division, a northwestern region in Bangladesh [1]. Also, the teenage pregnancy rate defined as having ever been pregnant between the ages of 15 and 19, was 29.4% in Rangpur Division compared to the national average of 23.5%. These figures, though potentially underreported, reflect how deeply rooted these practices are in terms of cultural and social aspects of the country.

From the perspective of physical and social health, early marriage and pregnancy pose significant concerns. Physically, teenage pregnancies are associated with increased risks, including preterm birth, low birth weight, preeclampsia, and systemic infections [2, 3]. What further exacerbates the situation in Bangladesh is that the utilization of maternal health services such as antenatal care (ANC) or facility deliveries is still relatively low. For instance, in 2022, only 41% of pregnant women in Bangladesh had at least four ANC visits, compared to the global average of 70% reported by UNICEF in 2024 [1, 4]. Similarly, while 86% of births globally were attended by skilled health professionals in 2023, the rate was 70% in Bangladesh in 2022 [1, 5]. Beyond physical health, early motherhood often leads to girls dropping out of school, limiting their opportunities for future socioeconomic engagement and the pursuit of lifelong goals. In Rangpur Division of Bangladesh, 79.1% of women aged 15–49 discontinued their education after marriage despite having been in school just before, a proportion closely aligned with the national average of 82% in 2022 [1].

Under these circumstances, local communities in Bangladesh should address preventable maternal and neonatal mortalities and morbidities by taking immediate action and encouraging pregnant women to use maternal health services in a timely manner. One of the long-term agendas for these communities is a social dialogue where discussions are held to critically reflect on the negative consequences associated with low utilization of maternal health services and find ways to move away from the practices of early marriage and adolescent pregnancy. To support this goal, government initiatives have been introduced as well as development assistance programs funded by multiple international donors. In this context, maternal and child health programs proliferated during the Millennium Development Goal (MDG) era and continue today [6].

Nevertheless, changing culturally and socially accepted behaviors remains a complex and gradual process. Such a change cannot be imposed by external forces, as it must be grounded in local community engagement. In turn, incremental changes in social norms can be driven by stakeholder empowerment along with support from diverse actors including government, non-governmental organizations, or other international development partners. To effectively embrace local perspectives and encourage community participation, group-based interactions are considered as one of the most practical and impactful approaches in many low-and middle-income country (LMIC) contexts. Previous studies from Bangladesh indeed reported various social and behavior change communication (SBCC) approaches involving group-based interactive dialogue sessions to improve maternal and child health outcomes [7, 8]. Save the Children Bangladesh Suchana program for ending cycle of undernutrition or USAID-Save the Children MaMoni maternal and newborn health project are such examples that integrated courtyard meetings for increased utilization of maternal health services or behavior change among mothers in rural Bangladesh [9, 10]. Studies from other LMICs such as Ghana and India also demonstrated the effectiveness and feasibility of interactive SBCC interventions [11–13]

However, gaps exist in the literature regarding two critical aspects. First, limited empirical evidence is available in terms of SBCC intervention design and its implementation strategies. Despite the importance of systematic formative research for effective implementation, previous studies report little detail on the design phase of the study. Second, previous SBCC studies tend to focus on pregnant women themselves and little attention has been paid to the key family members who also influence maternal health service utilization. In fact, pregnancy and childbirth are often viewed as a family matter but each family member has their own unique perspectives, lived experiences, and information needs. Although previous literature acknowledges power imbalances in family decision-making and the significance of engaging family members in maternal and child health-seeking behaviors, approaches to tailored or targeted messaging for individual family members have not been widely discussed. To be specific, studies from Bangladesh have shown how husbands and mothers-in-law are the key decision-makers for health-seeking behavior in the context of reproductive, maternal, and newborn health without incorporating strategies for targeted messaging for them [14, 15]. To a limited extent, efforts have been made to engage fathers and grandmothers or to deliver couple-based interventions for child nutrition programs [13, 16].

To address these gaps, separate courtyard sessions for pregnant adults, pregnant adolescents, their husbands and mothers-in-law are proposed in this cluster-randomized study protocol. In doing so, the present study outlines the design, implementation, and evaluation process of the targeted SBCC intervention in Rangpur Division of Bangladesh where adolescent pregnancy rates are high and maternal health service utilization is low. The study aims to assess the feasibility and effectiveness of targeted SBCC intervention involving key family members in a separate courtyard session. This study is designed as a hybrid effectiveness-implementation type II cluster-randomized study that measures both effectiveness and implementation outcomes [17].

Methods

This hybrid effectiveness-implementation type II cluster-randomized study involves multiple stakeholders within the framework of an official development assistance (ODA) program funded by the Korea International Cooperation Agency (KOICA) under the Ministry of Foreign Affairs. The study was initiated as part of the 2023–27 KOICA Strategic Partnership Program (SPP)- Strengthening Maternal and Neonatal Health Systems in Rangpur, Bangladesh (No. 2023–0515). This project is being implemented in Rangpur and Lalmonirhat districts under the strategic guidance of Directorate General of Health Services (DGHS) and Directorate General of Family Planning (DGFP) with technical support from Save the Children International in Bangladesh; with RDRS Bangladesh engaged as implementing partner and icddr,b as the research and evaluation partner.

In line with a hybrid effectiveness-implementation type II design, this research seeks to evaluate both the effectiveness and implementation outcomes of the targeted SBCC intervention over the five-month period. A hybrid design that investigates these outcomes is salient for future scale-up beyond the selected unions in two districts of Rangpur Division. Additionally, assessing effectiveness and implementation outcomes is essential for an integration of the intervention into routine practice in resource limited settings, particularly after the termination of ODA program funding.

Another key consideration for the study design is the cluster-randomization component. The randomization was conducted at the union level, which is the lowest administrative unit that covers villages. The rationale behind this cluster-randomization procedure is as follows. First, a group-based courtyard session inherently involves cluster-level engagement in delivering SBCC messages, thus increasing the possibility of spillover effects through word of mouth or even the mere presence of community health workers (CHWs) facilitating a community dialogue in a village. For this reason, random assignment of individuals within a union may result in contaminated exposure as members of the community frequently interact with each other. Second, randomization at the community level is suitable for a group-based courtyard session due to logistical arrangement of the face-to-face meetings involving pregnant women and their families. It would not be practical to randomly assign pregnant individuals from distant regions to gather in a single location. Also, randomizing at the union level, rather than at the smaller village level, facilitates the secure recruitment of sufficient pregnant women, adolescents and their family members for a courtyard session. To ensure rigorous design and reporting, this study follows the guidelines and reporting standards of the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) statement, specifically the CONSORT extension to cluster randomized trials [18].

Conceptual framework for implementation strategy

To capture implementation strategies throughout the intervention cycle, the fundamental components that outline the entire structure of the well-established Exploration, Preparation, Implementation, and Sustainment (EPIS) framework was employed [19]. The implementation strategies were integrated into each intervention cycle so that the delivery of the targeted SBCC intervention can be strengthened. In alignment with the stages of the EPIS framework, this study explicitly incorporates key considerations from each of the four phases into the design, launch, and future expansion of the targeted SBCC intervention. As illustrated in Fig. 1, the implementation strategies are described to match each of the four implementation phases of the EPIS framework. These implementation strategies were devised following the Expert Recommendations for Implementing Change (ERIC) compilation and mapped onto each of the four phases of the implementation cycle articulated in the EPIS [20, 21].

Conceptual framework for implementation strategy (adapted from Moullin et al. 2019)

First, the implementation strategies for exploration phase involve the iterative assessment of health needs and best evidence-based practices in the local context. As part of the local needs assessment addressed in the ERIC compilation, desk research was conducted to gather evidence from previous SBCC interventions in similar contexts in literature. In addition, mixed-methods formative research was undertaken by a local external research consultancy to examine the current practice of SBCC in the context of maternal health in the target area. To embrace current efforts, government-approved eToolkit materials that are being used to promote SBCC in maternal, adolescent, and child health were thoroughly reviewed. In developing targeted SBCC resources for this study, the eToolkit played a key role as a reference information, education, and communication (IEC) material. While the eToolkit can be used to deliver general public health messages adapted to the local context, it provides little practical value in rural areas due to limited access to digital devices such as computers or smartphones. Furthermore, it can be underutilized without facilitation or interactive outreach. Also, although the eToolkit is organized around key public health topics, the content is not tailored to specific target audiences within each topic. Therefore, the materials were carefully adapted to align with the specific goals and audience of the current targeted intervention as suggested by the implementation strategies from ERIC.

Second, potential barriers and facilitators were identified during the design and preparation phase. One of the implementation strategies adapted from the ERIC list included four separate focus group discussions (FGDs) with 10 adult pregnant women, 9 pregnant teenagers, 8 husbands, and 10 mothers-in-law in each group. Through these FGD sessions, different perspectives of key family members were captured regarding maternal health service utilization. Another important implementation strategy informed by the ERIC compilation was a collaborative workshop with local implementing partners as champions to develop targeted messages and draft transcripts tailored to each of the four target groups. Building upon core constructs from behavior change theories, the transcripts of courtyard sessions were systematically organized around four main components: (1) problem awareness, (2) perceived benefits of utilizing maternal and neonatal health services, (3) social support, and (4) self-support. During the workshop, local implementing partners and CHWs participated in group sessions to draft key messages tailored to each of the four target groups. The workshop was a brainstorming session to communicate challenging issues in a way that would resonate with each target group. Critical problems and their subject category along with relevant messages for each target group were structured by synthesizing discussions from the workshop. With these insights, the final transcript of the courtyard sessions was drafted and will be used by CHWs who will facilitate the sessions.

Third, efforts were made for the implementation phase to launch targeted courtyard sessions led by 20 CHWs in 20 unions. Drawn from the ERIC compilation, the implementation strategies to form a learning collaborative involved training of these 20 CHWs to improve their skills for facilitation and logistical adjustments. Key logistical issues were discussed with stakeholders to ensure that husbands who mostly work during the day are available to participate in the sessions, and the overall session timetables are arranged accordingly. Lastly, implementation strategies for sustainment phase prioritize continuation of the evidence-based intervention by generating long-term health impact in broader settings. To achieve sustainment, relevant implementation strategies such as refresher training for CHWs, efforts for the integration into routine practice, or engagement in policy advocacy with local government are planned. Additionally, the targeted SBCC courtyard sessions will be refined based on ongoing feedback and contextual needs. These implementation strategies are intended to set the stage for the scale up and to provide opportunities for reexamining the implementation as recommended by the ERIC list.

Throughout the entire implementation cycle, the EPIS framework embraces bridging factors, outer and inner context, and innovation factors. Focusing on the innovation factor of targeting or tailoring within SBCC intervention, key outer and inner contextual factors were identified in the framework as shown in Fig. 1. Also, the bridging factor highlights the important role of a coordinated relationship among the donor, the program team, the local implementing partner, and the academic partner.

Study setting

This study is conducted in Rangpur Division, one of the eight administrative divisions of Bangladesh. Located in the northwestern region of the country, Rangpur Division exhibits high rates of early marriage (median age at first marriage of 16.0 among women aged 20–49), teenage pregnancy (median age at first birth of 18.6 among women aged 20–49), and neonatal mortality (30 deaths per 1,000 live births). Utilization of maternal and neonatal health services is relatively low, with only 62% of births taking place in health facilities [1]. According to the 2022 BDHS, Rangpur Division also demonstrates relatively poor socioeconomic indicators, including a 17.3% of no formal education and a 26.9% of illiteracy among ever-married women aged 15–49 [1].

Of the eight districts within Rangpur Division, Rangpur and Lalmonirhat were selected as the target districts for the ODA program funded by the KOICA, which encompasses this study. Rangpur district, which hosts the divisional capital, was selected for strengthening the accountability and engagement of government stakeholders under the ODA program. Additionally, Lalmonirhat district was prioritized because it was one of the lowest-performing districts in key maternal and newborn health indicators and had no major relevant project underway at the time the ODA program was initiated. Within these two districts, a subset of lower administrative units known as unions were selectively included in the ODA program. The total number of unions is subject to change due to local government policy decisions related to public service delivery, demographic changes, and electoral boundary adjustments. In Rangpur and Lalmonirhat districts, there are approximately 115 to 121 unions. Among these unions, the ODA program strategically selected 40 unions to support maternal and child health system strengthening. Several criteria were considered to select these 40 unions, such as the capacity of union-level facilities, their location, and the estimated catchment population of 20,000 or more which allows for coverage of 2–3 adjacent unions. These criteria were adopted through an iterative process of consultations with local government, Save the Children’s technical team, and the implementing partner.

For the purposes of this study, three distinct arms were established. Of the 40 unions supported by the ODA program, 20 unions were randomly selected to receive the intervention. The remaining 20 unions within the supported group will continue to be eligible to participate in non-targeted regular awareness-raising campaign as part of the routine ODA program for maternal health without exposure to additional targeted SBCC intervention. In addition, another 20 unions outside these 40 unions were randomly assigned as a control group. By involving an intervention group, a standard ODA practice group, and a control group, this study will conduct comparative analysis to evaluate the effectiveness of the targeted SBCC intervention.

Participants and recruitment

The participants who will be eligible for the targeted SBCC intervention are four key groups of family members who are considered as decision makers for maternal health service utilization in the local context. The four groups are (1) pregnant women aged above 18, (2) pregnant adolescents aged 18 or below, (3) mothers-in-law, and (4) husbands or male partners. In other words, each pregnant woman will be recruited with their husbands and mothers-in-law if they are willing and available to participate in the targeted courtyard sessions. The inclusion criteria are mainly applicable for pregnant individuals, such as a gestational age of 16 weeks or less and consent to provide their own names, husbands’ names, and contact information for follow up. Although the targeted SBCC intervention is designed as a 5-month program, pregnant individuals unable to attend the entire 5-month sessions are still allowed to participate and will not be excluded from the initial enrollment. Close monitoring will be performed for those who are absent from any of the targeted SBCC sessions.

Participants for the targeted SBCC intervention will be enrolled by identifying eligible pregnant females. This process will be facilitated by a local NGO, RDRS Bangladesh as a project implementing partner and the quality of the targeted SBCC intervention will be monitored by the Save the Children’s technical team. Furthermore, trained CHWs, who are already familiar with each union, will assist in the recruitment process of participants (adult and adolescent pregnant women, husbands and mothers-in-law) and facilitate the courtyard sessions. The recruitment process begins with the baseline survey, which involves household visits to pregnant females, who will then be invited to participate in the upcoming targeted SBCC courtyard sessions. The names of the husbands and their contact information will be collected for tracking and follow-up purposes. To capture the actual difference in maternal health service utilization of pregnant women, only adult and adolescent pregnant women will be included in the baseline and endline surveys. Although no specific interventions will be offered for the non-intervention groups, the pregnant females with a gestational age of 16 weeks or less will still be recruited as baseline and endline survey participants, and all four groups of family members will participate in endline FGDs.

Sample size and power considerations

The sample size for the baseline and endline surveys was determined through feasibility checks and a priori power analysis. To estimate the feasible number of participants from each union, the numbers of identified pregnant women in the most recent month was examined using midwife monthly reports compiled by the ODA project team and DGFP across the 20 intervention unions. On average, 223 pregnant women were identified per union, with a total of 4,469 pregnant women for 20 intervention unions in Rangpur and Lalmonirhat districts. Based on the 2022 BDHS data from the two districts, approximately 1,475 women or 33% of the total pregnant women are expected to be pregnant for 16 weeks or less and thus eligible to participate in the study [22].

In the context of this cluster-randomized study design, the unit of randomization is the administrative unit known as a union. For a priori sample size calculation, several key factors should be considered, including the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC), cluster size, effect size, and the number of clusters. The ICC can vary depending on the extent to which individuals within each cluster share similar characteristics. Based on a simulation study, the ICC is generally reported to range between 0.01 and 0.50 [23]. Since a union is a small administrative unit within a district, between-cluster variance is expected to be minimal, and thus, the ICC was assumed to be 0.01. The cluster size for this study is 20, as each of the 20 unions will be assigned to one of the three groups: (1) intervention with additional targeted SBCC sessions, (2) standard ODA practice with regular awareness-raising sessions, or (3) control. The sample size was calculated based on the primary outcome of maternal health service utilization among pregnant females, specifically the average percentages of facility deliveries and deliveries attended by skilled birth attendants, which is 60%. This information was collected from a separate baseline survey conducted in Rangpur and Lalmonirhat districts in 2023–24 for purposes of ODA program evaluation.

The required sample size to detect 10 percentage-point difference in success rates across 3 arms is 900 participants (300 per cluster), assuming 80% power, an alpha level of 0.05, and an ICC of 0.01. To ensure feasibility however, a lower power of 70% was assumed due to challenges in recruiting enough eligible participants who are known to be pregnant for 16 weeks or less. Consequently, a total of 720 participants or 240 participants per cluster were required. The final target number of pregnant participants in each arm is 250 with a total of 750, considering the possibility of dropout. This sample size represents 17% of the estimated 1,475 eligible women with a gestational age of 16 weeks or less. Figure 2 shows the required sample sizes at cluster and individual levels, assuming either 80% or 70% power levels. In fact, limited statistical power is known to be a general concern for cluster-randomized studies and increasing the number of individuals within clusters often does not fully address the issue [24]. It is also known that increasing cluster sizes yields diminishing returns in statistical power [25]. Nevertheless, such studies can still contribute evidence to literature and inform future research [24].

Randomization

Of the 40 unions receiving ODA support, 20 were randomly assigned to the targeted SBCC intervention, while the remaining 20 unions will receive support through the standard ODA program. The targeted SBCC intervention consists of a 5 month-curriculum delivered across the pregnancy care continuum, beginning in the early stages of pregnancy and concluding before the participants’ expected date of delivery. Another 20 unions, located outside the 40 ODA-supported unions, were randomly selected as a control group. The randomization process was conducted using R software’s sample() function and managed by an external research team leader, who has no direct involvement with the local implementing partner or the CHWs. All of the 20 CHWs who will facilitate the targeted SBCC courtyard sessions were informed of their assignments to the intervention so that they can attend the tailored training before the rollout. Given the practical constraints of a community-based intervention, blinding was not considered for both CHWs and the participants who will experience the targeted session firsthand.

Participatory intervention

The overall plans for targeted SBCC courtyard sessions were organized and developed by Save the Children. The tailored messages were integrated into the targeted SBCC intervention spanning for 5 months and articulates key maternal and newborn health issues such as antenatal care, safe delivery, and postnatal care for both mothers and newborns. Based on the input co-created through participatory approach during the exploration and preparation phases, supporting materials for each of the four target groups were prepared. These materials include four sets of flip charts with images and corresponding message transcripts that the CHWs can utilize while facilitating the targeted SBCC session. The transcript consists of interactive quiz sessions, conversations between fictional characters, or a story that can appeal to each target group. Therefore, the intervention is designed to encourage active participation from each target group in the process of learning.

Twenty CHWs were trained to facilitate the sessions across 20 unions in the intervention area, following a structured process to ensure quality and accountability. Before implementation, a two-day training was held where CHWs were trained on how to facilitate the targeted SBCC sessions and mocked through scenario-based peer presentations. Month-wise session plan was also shared during the training to ensure all 20 CHWs follow the synchronized schedule and topics (Table 1).

Targeted SBCC session schedule

Four target groups of participants will be recruited using CHW’s household lists within their catchment area, which are updated every two months on average. These pre-listed attendance sheets based on inclusion criteria such as 16 weeks or less pregnancy will be provided to CHWs, so that the attendance can be marked at the start of each session. Sessions will be conducted in an interactive manner, using role-play, demonstrations, and feedback discussions. CHWs are required to track absentees and record reasons for absence. Moreover, reminders will be sent out through follow-up calls or home visits one day before the session. Session quality will be jointly monitored by Save the Children and RDRS Bangladesh to ensure consistency and accountability.

Intervention effectiveness outcomes

The primary outcomes will be the percentages of (1) mothers who received 4 or more ANC services, (2) births attended by skilled health professionals, (3) facility delivery, (4) mothers who received postnatal care (PNC) within 48 h after delivery, and (5) neonates who received neonatal care within 48 h after birth. To measure these primary outcomes, the endline survey will be conducted after each participant has completed their pregnancy, either through delivery, still birth, or miscarriage. In cases of pregnancy loss, only relevant outcomes will be tracked. The baseline survey, conducted before the initial courtyard session, is intended to collect background information such as previous pregnancy and delivery history.

The secondary outcomes of the study include (1) changes in perception towards early marriage, (2) perceptions on maternal health seeking behavior and its motivation, (3) perceived support from family members, (4) women’s empowerment represented by the involvement in decision making, and (5) the level of satisfaction with the information gained from the targeted SBCC intervention. For secondary outcomes, both the baseline and endline surveys will be descriptively analyzed. The findings from the FGDs will complement the survey results by providing cross-validation and an in-depth investigation into the reasons and mechanisms behind the findings. Data analysis

Statistical analysis for intervention effectiveness outcomes evaluation

The statistical analysis will be performed using the survey data from participating pregnant adults and adolescents. To compare the rates of primary outcomes related to maternal and neonatal health service utilization across three arms, mixed effects logistic regression analysis will be performed. In doing so, both fixed and random effects will be incorporated to capture treatment effects and potential cluster-level variability. An intention-to-treat (ITT) approach will be employed so that all participants will be included in the analysis according to their group assignment. Therefore, the participants will be included in the analysis regardless of their adherence to the targeted SBCC intervention or full attendance of pregnant females and their family members over the 5-month period. The participants whose pregnancy ends with miscarriage or stillbirth will also be included in the analyses except for outcomes related to delivery and postnatal care. Potential heterogeneity in terms of sociodemographic characteristics or obstetric history across three arms will be examined using chi-square test and ANOVA. Secondary outcome analysis will be performed to examine changes in perceptions among pregnant women and these are related to the issues such as early marriage, maternal health seeking behavior, and decision-making power. The secondary outcomes will be measured as categorical variables and will be compared before and after the targeted SBCC session rollout using appropriate statistical tests for categorical data. Supporting evidence will be explored through the endline FGDs to delve deeper into quantitative findings.

In addition to evaluating intervention effectiveness outcomes through statistical analysis, key implementation outcomes such as feasibility and acceptability will be descriptively examined as Table 3 shows [26]. At the service delivery level, it was hypothesized that adoption would be enhanced through the adaptation of eToolkit which is already familiar to CHWs or a collaborative workshop and subsequent training and coaching for CHWs. Additionally, a high level of penetration would be achieved through the Training of Trainers (ToT) model and policy advocacy with local government to set the stage for scale up.

Ethical considerations

The protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Institute of Health Economics, University of Dhaka (IHE/IRB/DU/16/2025) and Save the Children Ethics & Evidence Generation team (SC-EEG-FY2024-328). Consent and assent forms will be provided in the local language for both survey and FGD participants. Special attention will be given to pregnant adolescents aged 18 or younger, for whom parental or guardian assent will be obtained. Detailed explanations will be provided to the parents or guardians of pregnant adolescents, including information about the data to be collected, potential benefits and risks, voluntary participation, and contact information of the research team.

Discussion

The present study protocol outlines the design and evaluation of a targeted SBCC intervention within a maternal and child health ODA program in Bangladesh. This hybrid effectiveness-implementation type II cluster-randomized study is both timely and critical for many LMICs where over 90% of all maternal deaths occur but the maternal health service utilization remains low [28]. Although previous studies have reported the potential for SBCC strategies in low-resource settings, little is known about the formative research process for effective design and evaluation of an intervention involving participatory approaches. Particularly, the vast majority of studies have focused on demonstrating the effectiveness of intervention and there remains a missing link between effectiveness outcomes and implementation learning. A piecemeal approach to maternal health behavior change intervention addresses whether the intervention is effective in motivating a pregnant individual, failing to account for how it achieves high uptake.

To bridge the gaps, this study adapted recommendations from ERIC compilation and mapped the implementation strategies onto each implementation cycle suggested by EPIS framework. The implementation outcomes informed by Proctor’s conceptualization will be assessed to provide insights into the mechanisms on how the intended implementation outcomes are achieved through the specified implementation strategies. This study contributes to the implementation science literature by presenting the potential for replication in similar settings where issues of low maternal health service utilization, high adolescent population, and complex family dynamics persist. Given that implementation science research is comparatively limited in LMIC settings, this protocol can reach a wider readership and broaden the scope for implementation science literature.

Moreover, empirical evidence on the tailored messaging for SBCC strategies is limited despite the importance of family engagement in maternal health-seeking behavior. To tackle this challenge, this study is designed to demonstrate the effectiveness and feasibility of a targeted SBCC intervention in community settings. The intervention incorporates participatory processes such as interviews and discussions with potential target groups ranging from pregnant women and their family, CHWs, and local implementing partners. In so doing, it aimed to deliver targeted messages that are contextually relevant and responsive to the needs of each target group. Additionally, the targeted SBCC intervention presented in this protocol draws on behavior change theories from the literature such as health belief model, theory of planned behavior and theory of reasoned action. Targeted messages that will be delivered to each target group are structured around four key constructs adopted from behavior change theories including problem awareness, perceived benefits, social support, and self-support. By drawing on theories for behavior change, this study aimed to ensure effective implementation, sustainability, and scale-up. The findings from this study can contribute to future program design and evaluation efforts in Bangladesh and other similar settings where ongoing support for maternal and child health sector is warranted.

Building upon previous literature that focuses on family dynamics and power imbalances, this study engages key family members to improve maternal health service utilization [13–16]. To be specific, husbands and mothers-in-law will be invited as key household influencers who might play a key role in maternal health-seeking behavior. By organizing each session separately with these family members, this study aimed to provide new insights into the targeted messaging strategy distinguished from conventional SBCC approaches such as public billboards or information leaflet available for interested clients. In this way, key decision-makers as well as pregnant women themselves are actively involved in calls for actions to achieve healthy pregnancy and delivery. Ultimately, the targeted SBCC intervention is intended to create a supportive environment for pregnant women who have limited decision-making power in households. This is enabled by tailored messaging that can resonate with each group of family members’ information needs and motivation. Through group-based courtyard sessions, the targeted SBCC intervention allows for interactive discussion, reflection, and social reinforcement that can lead to greater impact.

From the perspective of global development agenda, this SBCC intervention seeks to contribute to achieving Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) 3, targets 3.1 and 3.2 which aims to reduce global maternal mortality ratio and end preventable deaths of newborns and children under age 5. At the national level, the targeted SBCC intervention is also in line with Bangladesh’s Health, Population and Nutrition Sector Program (4th HPNSP, 2017–2022). This national strategy emphasizes the need for improvement of maternal and child health outcomes by prioritizing this area for health sector planning. If proven effective and feasible, this targeted SBCC model for maternal health could be integrated into the current community health systems and potentially scaled up nationally. This study protocol is generalizable to similar settings where high adolescent pregnancy rates and low maternal health service utilization are the key challenges. Other LMICs can also learn from the experience of Bangladesh and adapt this approach accordingly.

Although this study has the potential for contribution to implementation science literature, there are several challenges and limitations. First, intervention in real-world settings makes logistical challenges inevitable. For example, dropout rates can be high due to practical reasons such as pregnant mothers’ relocation to their parental homes in different unions for delivery, leading to incomplete attendance at courtyard sessions. Engaging husbands and mothers-in-law can also be challenging because they may frequently go out for work or be indifferent to maternal health issues due to the prevalence of patriarchal norms. Another potential challenge is the weather condition during the rainy season that can lead to road flooding and transportation difficulties for the participants. To minimize logistical challenges, the monitoring and evaluation team from Save the Children, the implementing partner in the field and CHWs will communicate closely with the research team and routinely track progress. Measures will be taken promptly within the available capacity if any noticeable issue arises.

Another concern is the potential tension between CHWs and TBAs. The targeted messaging strategy may be perceived to challenge or interfere with the practices of TBAs, which can lead to cultural resistance or conflict. In this context, TBAs may fear losing their income from home deliveries due to increasing number of deliveries at health facilities. Therefore, coordination among different stakeholders is essential to ensure effective implementation. Still another key limitation stems from the inherent risk of spillover effects, given the community-based design of the intervention. Due to geographic proximity, the non-intervention groups may be influenced by the unintended spillover despite separate courtyard session arrangement for each target group. Not only the target population but the CHWs as a facilitator can also inadvertently alter standard practice by sharing elements of the targeted SBCC intervention across the entire 40 ODA-supported unions. To address this challenge, an additional control group outside the 40 ODA-supported unions is included for comparison. Nevertheless, future research should consider a larger sample size and multiple rounds of the 5-month session cycles to secure sufficient statistical power.

Within the framework of ODA program, this study is designed as a multidisciplinary collaboration to test the effectiveness and feasibility of the targeted SBCC strategy in a low-resourced setting. By leveraging this opportunity, the study is expected to provide evidence for design, implementation, and sustainment of a community-based intervention. If proven effective and feasible in the local context, the targeted SBCC intervention can be translated into an example of a localization effort driven by coordinated partnership among the donor, program team, local implementing partner and academic partner. This localization effort can then pave the way for successful transition to local government and community for institutionalization.
