1.
Introduction

Let
L
be a positive self-adjoint operator on
L^{2}(X)
where
X
is a metric measure space, with metric
\varrho
and measure
\mu
. We may define the heat operators
H_{z}:=\exp(-zL)
, where
z\in\mathbb{C}
and
\arg(z)\leq\pi/2
, by spectral theory; these operators form an analytic semigroup.
Some of them extend continuously to
L^{p}(X)
for some other values of
p
(see
[
3
]
).

When
0<\alpha<1
, the fractional powers
L^{\alpha}
can be defined.
The study of fractional powers
L^{\alpha}
, where
L
is a Laplace operator, or a Schrödinger operator, or a Hardy operator, has attracted considerable interest recently: see, for example
[
1
,
2
]
.
These fractional powers of operators arise from partial differential equations that model real-world phenomena, as well as from applications in the mathematical sciences.
For instance, the fractional Laplacian on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is used to model a Lévy jump process by superdiffusion in euclidean spaces, and the fractional Laplace–Beltrami operator is used for this process in curved manifolds.

The fractional powers
L^{\alpha}
generate semigroups
\{\exp(-tL^{\alpha}):t\in\mathbb{R}^{+}\}
on
L^{2}(X)
.
It is well known that the operators
\exp(-tL^{\alpha})
can be expressed in terms of the operators
\exp(-sL)
by subordination, hence one can study
\exp(-tL^{\alpha})
via the
\exp(-sL)
.
It is interesting that the operators
\exp(-tL^{\alpha})
have some properties that the operators
\exp(-sL)
need not possess.
For example one can find upper bounds on the kernels of the time derivatives
{d^{k}\exp(-tL^{\alpha})/dt^{k}}
, but not the kernels of the time derivatives
{d^{k}\exp(-tL)/dt^{k}}
, in terms of upper bounds for the kernels of
\exp(-tL)
.
The aim of this paper is to study the subordinated semigroups
\{\exp(-tL^{\alpha}):t\in\mathbb{R}^{+}\}
, and some of their applications.

This paper is structured as follows.
In Section 2, we take
\alpha
to be
1/2
and study the Poisson semigroup
\{\exp(-tL^{1/2}):t\in\mathbb{R}^{+}\}
.
In this case, the subordination formula is explicit and we can obtain sharp estimates for the Poisson operators
\exp(-tL^{1/2})
and their time derivatives
L^{k/2}\exp(-tL^{1/2})
when

t\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
, and for their analytic continuations
\exp(-zL^{1/2})
, when
z\in\mathbb{C}
and
\left|\arg z\right|<{\pi/4}
.
Our results include estimates of maximal operators associated to the Poisson semigroup on a sector in the case of Markov semigroups.

In Section 3, we recall the properties of Lévy distributions, and then use these to study the
subordinated operators
\exp(-tL^{\alpha})
for
t\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
.
We show that if
L
is the generator of a symmetric Markov semigroup
\{\exp(-tL):t\in\mathbb{R}^{+}\}
, then the maximal operator
f\mapsto\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}^{+}}|\exp(-tL^{\alpha})f|
is of weak type
(1,1)
.
In
[
5
]
, the
L^{p}
boundedness of this maximal operator was proved for all
p\in(1,\infty]
; this result covers the endpoint when
p=1
.

In Section 4, we assume that the quasi-metric space
X
is Ahlfors–David regular, that is, the volume of a ball satisfies
\mu(B(x,r))\eqsim r^{n}
, for some
n
, and that
L
is a positive self-adjoint operator on
L^{2}(X)
for which the semigroup
\{\exp(-tL):t\in\mathbb{R}^{+}\}
on
L^{2}(X)
has Poisson-type upper bounds.
We show that the Hardy space associated to the operator
L
can be characterised by the area function and by the vertical square function of the subordinated semigroup
\{\exp(-tL^{\alpha}):t\in\mathbb{R}^{+}\}
for
0<\alpha<1
.
This new result proves that the Hardy space associated to
L
is equivalent to the Hardy space associated to the fractional powers
L^{\alpha}
.

A note on notation: we write
\Gamma_{\beta}
for the open sector
\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\left|\arg z\right|<\beta\}
and
\bar{\Gamma}_{\beta}
for its closure; we take the branch cut for
\arg
(and hence also for complex powers) along the negative real axis.

2.
Poisson semigroups

In this section, we study the Poisson semigroup, which is generated by
L^{1/2}
, that is,
P_{t}=\exp(-tL^{1/2})
for all
t\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
.
In this case, there is an explicit subordination formula that represents the Poisson semigroup in terms of the heat semigroup.
We will employ this formula to continue
t\mapsto P_{t}
analytically and obtain operators
P_{z}=\exp(-zL^{1/2})
for
z\in\bar{\Gamma}_{\beta}
, where
\beta<\pi/4
, and estimate the time derivatives
P_{z,k}=(zL^{1/2})^{k}P_{z}=(-1)^{k}\,z^{k}\,{d^{k}P_{z}}/{dz^{k}}
in terms of the operators
\exp(-tL^{1/2})
.

We first show that the time derivatives of the Poisson semigroup are bounded by the Poisson semigroup itself.
Let
T
be a bounded operator on
L^{2}(X)
.
We say that
T
has an associated kernel
k
on
X\times X
when
k
is locally integrable on
X\times X
with respect to the product measure
\mu\times\mu
, and moreover

\langle Tf,g\rangle=\int_{X}\int_{X}k(x,y)f(y)f(x)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(y)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x)

for all
f,g\in L^{2}(X)
.

Theorem 2.1
.

Assume that

(i)

L
is a positive self-adjoint operator on
L^{2}(X)
;

(ii)

the heat semigroup
\{H_{t}=\exp(-tL):t\in\mathbb{R}^{+}\}
on
L^{2}(X)
is positivity-preserving, in the sense that
\exp(-tL)f\geq 0
if
f\geq 0
.

Then

(a)

for each positive integer
k
and
\theta\in(0,1)
,

|P_{t,k}f(x)|=|(tL^{1/2})^{k}\exp(-tL^{1/2})f(x)|\lesssim_{k,\theta}P_{\theta t}(|f|)(x)

for all
x\in X
, all
t\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
and all
f\in L^{2}(X)
.

(b)

Assume that the heat operators
H_{t}
have kernels
h_{t}(x,y)
.
Then the operators
P_{t}
have kernels
p_{t}
and the operators
P_{t,k}
have kernels
p_{t,k}
and, for all positive integers
k
and
\theta\in(0,1)
,

|p_{t,k}(x,y)|\lesssim_{k,\theta}p_{\theta t}(x,y)\qquad\forall x,y\in X\quad\forall t\in\mathbb{R}^{+}.

Proof.

We start with the subordination formula: for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
,

\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-s}\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda^{2}/4s}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}s}{s^{1/2}},

so for a positive self adjoint operator
L
,

\exp(-tL^{1/2})f=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-s}\exp(-t^{2}L/4s)f\,\frac{\mathrm{d}s}{s^{1/2}}\qquad\forall f\in L^{2}(X)\quad\forall t\in\mathbb{R}^{+}.

After the change of variable
u=t^{2}/4s
, we obtain

(2.1)

\exp(-tL^{1/2})f=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{0}^{\infty}t\mathrm{e}^{-{t^{2}}/{4u}}\exp(-uL)f\,\frac{\mathrm{d}u}{u^{3/2}}\,.

Hence

(2.2)

\displaystyle(tL^{1/2})^{k}\exp(-tL^{1/2})f

\displaystyle=(-1)^{k}\frac{t^{k}}{2\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\partial^{k}_{t}(t\mathrm{e}^{-{t^{2}}/{4u}})\exp(-uL)f\,\frac{\mathrm{d}u}{u^{3/2}}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{k+1}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{k}\partial^{k+1}_{t}(\mathrm{e}^{-{t^{2}}/{4u}})\exp(-uL)f\,\frac{\mathrm{d}u}{u^{1/2}}.

This, along with assumption (ii), yields the inequality

(2.3)

\displaystyle|P_{t,k}f|

\displaystyle}{{\displaystyle\leq{}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{t^{k}}{u^{1/2}}\left|\partial^{k+1}_{t}(\mathrm{e}^{-{t^{2}}/{4u}})\right|\exp(-uL)(|f|)\,\mathrm{d}u.

Let
u\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
.
By Faà di Bruno’s formula, we can write

\partial^{k+1}_{t}(\mathrm{e}^{-{t^{2}}/{4u}})=\sum\frac{(-1)^{m_{1}+m_{2}}}{(2m_{1})!\,m_{2}!}\mathrm{e}^{-{t^{2}}/{4u}}\Big(\frac{t}{4u}\Big)^{m_{1}}\Big(\frac{1}{4u}\Big)^{m_{2}},

where the sum is taken over all pairs
(m_{1},m_{2})
of nonnegative integers satisfying
m_{1}+2m_{2}=k+1
.
For every such pair
(m_{1},m_{2})
, and any positive
\epsilon
,

\displaystyle\frac{t^{k}}{u^{1/2}}\Big(\frac{t}{4u}\Big)^{m_{1}}\Big(\frac{1}{4u}\Big)^{m_{2}}=\frac{1}{4^{m_{1}+m_{2}}}\frac{t}{u^{3/2}}\frac{t^{2m_{1}+2m_{2}-2}}{u^{m_{1}+m_{2}-1}}\leq\frac{t}{u^{3/2}}\frac{(m_{1}+m_{2}-1)!}{4(4\epsilon)^{m_{1}+m_{2}-1}}\mathrm{e}^{{\epsilon t^{2}}/{4u}}.

This implies that for all
\theta\in(0,1)
there exists
c(\theta,k)
such that

(2.4)

\frac{t^{k}}{u^{1/2}}\left|\partial^{k+1}_{t}\mathrm{e}^{-{t^{2}}/{4u}}\right|\leq c(\theta,k)\frac{t}{u^{3/2}}\mathrm{e}^{-{(\theta t)^{2}}/{4u}}.

From (
2.3
) and (
2.4
), we deduce that

\displaystyle|P_{t,k}f|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{c(\theta,k)}{\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{0}^{\infty}t\mathrm{e}^{-{(\theta t)^{2}}/{4u}}\exp(-uL)(|f|)\,\frac{\mathrm{d}u}{u^{3/2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{c(\theta,k)}{\theta\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{0}^{\infty}(\theta t)\mathrm{e}^{-{(\theta t)^{2}}/{4u}}\exp(-uL)(|f|)\,\frac{\mathrm{d}u}{u^{3/2}}

\displaystyle=C(\theta,k)P_{\theta t}(|f|),

say, where in the last equality we used (
2.1
).
This completes the proof of (a).

We now prove (b).
For all
f,g\in L^{2}(X)
,

\langle\exp(-tL)f,g\rangle=\int_{X}\int_{X}h_{t}(x,y)f(x)g(y)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(y).

Since
L
is positive and self-adjoint,
|\langle\exp(-tL)f,g\rangle|\leq\|f\|_{2}\|g\|_{2}
.
From the subordination formula, we see that

\bigl\langle\exp(-tL^{1/2})f,g\bigr\rangle=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{X}\int_{X}\int_{0}^{\infty}t\mathrm{e}^{{-t^{2}}/{4u}}h_{u}(x,y)f(x)g(y)\,\frac{\mathrm{d}u}{u^{3/2}}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(y).

We change the order of integration, integrating first with respect to
x
and
y
, and obtain

\bigl|\bigl\langle\exp(-tL^{1/2})f,g\bigr\rangle\bigr|\leq\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\pi}}\|f\|_{2}\|g\|_{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}t\mathrm{e}^{{-t^{2}}/{4u}}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}u}{u^{3/2}}.

A further integration now gives

\bigl|\bigl\langle\exp(-tL^{1/2})f,g\bigr\rangle\bigr|\leq\|f\|_{2}\|g\|_{2}.

We now show that the kernel

(x,y)\mapsto\int_{0}^{\infty}t\mathrm{e}^{{-t^{2}}/{4u}}h_{u}(x,y)\frac{\mathrm{d}u}{u^{3/2}}

is locally integrable in
X\times X
.
If
A
is a bounded set in
X\times X
, then
A\subset\Omega\times\Omega
for some bounded set
\Omega
in
X
, and

\displaystyle\iint_{A}\biggl|\int_{0}^{\infty}t\mathrm{e}^{{-t^{2}}/{4u}}h_{u}(x,y)\,\frac{\mathrm{d}u}{u^{3/2}}\biggr|\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(y)

\displaystyle=\iint_{A}\int_{0}^{\infty}t\mathrm{e}^{{-t^{2}}/{4u}}h_{u}(x,y)\,\frac{\mathrm{d}u}{u^{3/2}}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(y)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{X}\int_{X}\int_{0}^{\infty}t\mathrm{e}^{{-t^{2}}/{4u}}h_{u}(x,y)1_{\Omega}(y)1_{\Omega}(x)\,\frac{\mathrm{d}u}{u^{3/2}}\,d\mu(x)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(y).

From above,

\displaystyle\iint_{A}\biggl|\int_{0}^{\infty}t\mathrm{e}^{{-t^{2}}/{4u}}h_{u}(x,y)\,\frac{\mathrm{d}u}{u^{3/2}}\biggr|\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(y)\leq\|1_{\Omega}\|_{2}\|1_{\Omega}\|_{2}\,,

which shows that

(x,y)\mapsto\int_{0}^{\infty}t\mathrm{e}^{{-t^{2}}/{4u}}h_{u}(x,y)\,\frac{\mathrm{d}u}{u^{3/2}}

is locally integrable.

Thus the kernel of
\exp(-tL^{1/2})
is given by

p_{t}(x,y)=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{0}^{\infty}t\mathrm{e}^{{-t^{2}}/{4u}}h_{u}(x,y)\,\frac{\mathrm{d}u}{u^{3/2}}\,,

that is,

\bigl\langle\exp(-tL^{1/2})f,g\bigr\rangle=\int_{X}\int_{X}p_{t}(x,y)f(x)g(y)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(y)\qquad\forall f,g\in L^{2}(X).

Now, as in the proof of (
2.2
), we obtain the kernels of the derivatives:

p_{t,k}(x,y)=(-1)^{k+1}\frac{t^{k}}{\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\partial^{k+1}_{t}(\mathrm{e}^{-{t^{2}}/{4u}})h_{u}(x,y)\,\frac{\mathrm{d}u}{u^{1/2}}\,.

From the assumption (i), we see that
p_{t}(x,y)\geq 0
for all
x,y\in X
and
t\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
.
Finally, arguing as in the proof of (a), we obtain (b).
∎

We now give similar estimates for complex Poisson semigroups.

Theorem 2.2
.

Assume that
L
satisfies the assumptions (i) and (ii) of Theorem
2.1
.
Fix
\beta\in(0,\pi/4)
, and set
\gamma=(1-\tan^{2}\beta)^{1/2}
.
Then

(a)

the Poisson semigroup
\{P_{t}:t\in\mathbb{R}^{+}\}
can be extended to a complex semigroup

\{P_{z}:z\in\bar{\Gamma}_{\beta}\}
.
Moreover, if
z\in\bar{\Gamma}_{\beta}
and
\tau\leq\operatorname{Re}z\leq 4\tau
, then

|P_{z}f(x)|\leq 4\sqrt{2}\gamma^{-1}P_{\gamma\tau}(|f|)(x)\qquad\forall x\in X.

(b)

Assume that the heat operators
H_{t}
have kernels
h_{t}(x,y)
.
Then the Poisson operators
P_{z}
have kernels
p_{z}
, and if
z\in\bar{\Gamma}_{\beta}
and
\tau\leq\operatorname{Re}z\leq 4\tau
, then

|p_{z}(x,y)|\leq 4\sqrt{2}\gamma^{-1}p_{\gamma\tau}(x,y)\qquad\forall x,y\in X.

Remark 2.3
.

The number
4
may be replaced by any other number greater than
1
, at the cost of changing the constant
4\sqrt{2}\gamma^{-1}
.

Proof.

Recall the subordination formula (
2.1
):

\exp(-tL^{1/2})f(x)=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{0}^{\infty}t\mathrm{e}^{-{t^{2}}/{4u}}\exp(-uL)f(x)\,\frac{\mathrm{d}u}{u^{3/2}}\qquad\forall f\in L^{2}(X).

The assumption (i) implies that the heat and Poisson semigroups extend analytically to
\Gamma_{\pi/2}
as semigroups on
L^{2}(X)
.
Fix
\tau\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
and
z\in\bar{\Gamma}_{\beta}
such that
\tau\leq\operatorname{Re}z\leq 4\tau
.
By analytic continuation,

\exp(-zL^{1/2})f(x)=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{0}^{\infty}z\mathrm{e}^{-{z^{2}}/{4u}}\exp(-uL)f(x)\,\frac{\mathrm{d}u}{u^{3/2}}\,.

This, along with the assumption (ii), yields

(2.5)

|\exp(-zL^{1/2})f(x)|=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\left|z\mathrm{e}^{-{z^{2}}/{4u}}\right|\exp(-uL)(|f|)(x)\,\frac{\mathrm{d}u}{u^{3/2}}\,.

Write
z=t+is
, where
t,s\in\mathbb{R}
.
Then
|s|\leq t\tan\beta<t
since
\beta<{\pi}/{4}
.
Therefore

|z|^{2}=|t|^{2}+|s|^{2}\leq 32\tau^{2}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\operatorname{Re}(z^{2})=t^{2}-s^{2}\geq(1-\tan^{2}\beta)t^{2}\geq(\gamma\tau)^{2}.

Hence

\left|z\mathrm{e}^{-{z^{2}}/{4u}}\right|\leq\frac{4\sqrt{2}}{\gamma}(\gamma\tau)\mathrm{e}^{-{(\gamma\tau)^{2}}/{4u}}.

Inserting this into (
2.5
), we obtain

\displaystyle|\exp(-zL^{1/2})f(x)|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{4\sqrt{2}}{\gamma}\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{0}^{\infty}(\gamma\tau)\mathrm{e}^{-{(\gamma\tau)^{2}}/{4u}}\exp(-uL)(|f|)(x)\,\frac{\mathrm{d}u}{u^{3/2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{4\sqrt{2}}{\gamma}P_{\gamma\tau}(|f|)(x),

where we used (
2.1
) in the last inequality, which proves (a).

The proof of (b) is similar and we omit the details.
∎

The argument of Theorem
2.2
also proves Theorem
2.1
, because the derivatives of an analytic function can be expressed via the Cauchy formula in terms of the function.

Corollary 2.4
.

Theorem
2.2
implies Theorem
2.1
.

Proof.

Take
\theta\in(0,1)
.
To obtain the estimate of Theorem
2.1
(a), namely

|P_{t,k}f(x)|=|(tL^{1/2})^{k}\exp(-tL^{1/2})f(x)|\leq C_{\theta}P_{\theta t}(|f|)(x)\qquad\forall t\in\mathbb{R}^{+},

we choose
\beta\in(0,\pi/4)
such that
(1-\tan^{2}\beta)^{1/2}(1-\sin\beta)=\theta
, we let
\gamma
be
(1-\tan^{2}\beta)^{1/2}
and
\tau
be
(1-\sin\beta)t
, and we define
C
to be the circle centred at
t
with radius
r=t\sin\beta
.

From the Cauchy integral formula,

P_{t,k}f=(-t)^{k}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{k}}{dt^{k}}\exp(-tL^{1/2})f=\frac{(-t)^{k}k!}{2\pi i}\int_{C}\frac{\exp(-zL^{1/2})f}{(z-t)^{k+1}}\,\mathrm{d}z.

Every point on the circle
C
has the property that
\tau\leq\operatorname{Re}z\leq 4\tau
, so, from Theorem
2.2
,

\left|P_{t,k}f(x)\right|\leq\frac{t^{k}k!}{2\pi}\int_{C}\frac{|\exp(-zL^{1/2})f(x)|}{r^{k+1}}\,\mathrm{d}z\leq\frac{t^{k}k!}{r^{k}}P_{\gamma\tau}(|f|)(x)=\frac{4\sqrt{2}\,k!}{(\sin\beta)^{k}\gamma}P_{\theta t}(|f|)(x).

This proves part (a) of Theorem
2.1
.
The proof of part (b) is similar.
∎

We can replace assumption (ii) by a weaker assumption: instead of assuming that the heat semigroup is positivity-preserving, we can assume that the heat semigroup is dominated by a positivity-preserving semigroup, and then obtain similar results to Theorems
2.1
and
2.2
.
We state this in the next theorem.

Given a semigroup
\{G_{t}:t\in\mathbb{R}^{+}\}
with generator
M
, we write
\{Q_{t}:t\in\mathbb{R}^{+}\}
for the associated Poisson semigroup, that is,
Q_{t}=\exp(-tM^{1/2})
, and write
q_{t}
for the associated kernel, if it exists.

Theorem 2.5
.

Assume that

(i)

L
is a positive self-adjoint operator on
L^{2}(X)
;

(ii)

the heat operators
H_{t}
are dominated by the operators
G_{t}
of a positivity-preserving semigroup
\{\exp(-tM):t\in\mathbb{R}^{+}\}
, in the sense that
|H_{t}f|\leq CG_{t}|f|
for all
f\in L^{2}(X)
and all
t\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
.

Fix
\beta\in(0,\pi/4)
.
Then

(a)

for each positive integer
k
and
\theta\in(0,1)
, for all
f\in L^{2}(X)
,

|P_{t,k}f(x)|=|(tL^{1/2})^{k}\exp(-tL^{1/2})f(x)|\lesssim_{k,\theta}{Q}_{\theta t}(|f|)(x)\qquad\forall x\in X.

(b)

the Poisson semigroup
\{P_{t}:t\in\mathbb{R}^{+}\}
can be extended to a complex semigroup

\{P_{z}:z\in\bar{\Gamma}_{\beta}\}
.
Moreover there exists
\gamma=\gamma(\beta)\in(0,1)
such that

|P_{z}f(x)|=|\exp(-zL^{1/2})f(x)|\leq\sqrt{2}\gamma^{-1}{Q}_{\gamma t}(|f|)(x)\qquad\forall x\in X,

where
t=\operatorname{Re}z
.

Assume further that the heat operators
H_{t}
and the dominating operators
G_{t}
have kernels
h_{t}
and
g_{t}
in
L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(X\times X)
.

(c)

Then the operators
Q_{t}
,
P_{t}
and
P_{t,k}
have kernels
q_{t}
,
p_{t}
and
p_{t,k}
in

, and further, for each positive integer

and
\theta\in(0,1)
,

|p_{t,k}(x,y)|\lesssim_{k,\theta}{q}_{\theta t}(x,y)\qquad\forall x,y\in X.

(d)

For
\left|\arg z\right|\leq\beta
with any fixed
\beta<{\pi/4}
, there exists
\gamma=\gamma(\beta)\in(0,1)
such that

|p_{z}(x,y)|\leq\sqrt{2}\gamma^{-1}g_{\gamma t}(x,y)\qquad\forall x,y\in X,

where
t=\operatorname{Re}z
and
p_{z}(x,y)
is the kernel of
P_{z}
.

Proof.

The proof is similar to those above.
We will only show the modification for the proof of (a), since the modifications for parts (b), (c), (d) are similar.

As in (
2.2
),

(2.6)

(tL^{1/2})^{k}\exp(-tL^{1/2})f=(-1)^{k}\frac{t^{k}}{4\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\partial^{k+1}_{t}(\mathrm{e}^{-{t^{2}}/{4u}})\exp(-uL)f\,\frac{\mathrm{d}u}{\sqrt{u}}

which implies that

|(tL^{1/2})^{k}\exp(-tL^{1/2})f|\leq\frac{Ct^{k}}{4\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\left|\partial^{k+1}_{t}(\mathrm{e}^{-{t^{2}}/{4u}})\right|\exp(-uM)|f|\,\frac{\mathrm{d}u}{\sqrt{u}}.

The rest of the proof proceeds as before, but with
\exp(-tM)|f|
in place of
\exp(-tL)|f|
.
∎

We now consider the case where
\{H_{t}:t\in\mathbb{R}^{+}\}
is a symmetric Markov semigroup, that is,
L
is positive self-adjoint and
\{H_{t}:t\in\mathbb{R}^{+}\}
extends continuously to a positivity-preserving contraction semigroup on
L^{p}(X)
for all
p
in
[1,\infty]
.
It then follows from the subordination formula that the Poisson semigroup
\{P_{t}:t\in\mathbb{R}^{+}\}
is also a Markov semigroup.

Let
P^{*}f=\sup_{t>0}|P_{t}f|
for all
f\in L^{p}(X)
.
The operator
P^{*}
is bounded on
L^{p}(X)
when
1<p\leq\infty
(see
[
12
,
5
]
).
The following result is a direct consequence of Theorems
2.1
and
2.2
.

Theorem 2.6
.

Assume that
\{H_{t}:t\in\mathbb{R}^{+}\}
is a symmetric Markov semigroup.
For each nonnegative integer
k
, set

M^{*}_{k}f=\sup_{z\in\bar{\Gamma}_{\beta}}\left|z^{k}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{k}}{dz^{k}}P_{z}f\right|.

Then the operators
M^{*}_{k}
are bounded on
L^{p}(X)
for all
p
in
(1,\infty]
.

Proof.

Theorem
2.6
follows from the pointwise estimates in Theorem
2.1
(a) and
Theorem
2.2
(a) together with the
L^{p}
boundedness of
P^{*}
.
∎

Remark 2.7
.

A standard example of a symmetric Markov semigroup is
\{\exp(-t\Delta):t\in\mathbb{R}^{+}\}
, where
\Delta
is (minus) the Laplace–Beltrami operator on a complete Riemannian manifold
X
.

3.
Lévy distributions and subordinated semigroups

3.1.
Lévy distributions

We shall now study the subordinated semigroup
\{\exp(-tL^{\alpha}):t\in\mathbb{R}^{+}\}
when
\alpha\in(0,1)
.
While the Poisson semigroup in the special case when
\alpha=1/2
has an explicit subordination formula, the general case leads us to the strictly stable Lévy distribution with stability parameter
\alpha
. For information about these see, for example, Sato
[
11
, §14]
or Zolotarev
[
14
]
.
In what follows, many objects depend on
\alpha
, but we do not indicate this explicitly.

A Laplace transform and contour integration argument shows that

\mathrm{e}^{-us^{\alpha}}=\int_{0}^{\infty}p_{u}(t)\,\mathrm{e}^{-ts}\,\mathrm{d}t\qquad\forall s,u\in\mathbb{R}^{+}

if and only if

\displaystyle p_{u}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{0-i\infty}^{0+i\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-uz^{\alpha}+tz}\,\mathrm{d}z

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-u(iy)^{\alpha}+ity}\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=\mathcal{F}^{-1}m_{u}(t)\qquad\forall t\in\mathbb{R}^{+},

where
\mathcal{F}
denotes the Fourier transformation and

m_{u}(y)=\mathrm{e}^{-u(iy)^{\alpha}}=\mathrm{e}^{-u[\cos(\pi\alpha/2)+i\sin(\pi\alpha/2)\operatorname{sgn}(y)]\,|y|^{\alpha}}\qquad\forall y\in\mathbb{R}.

Since
m_{u}
extends continuously and boundedly to the lower half-plane
\mathbb{C}_{-}
of complex numbers with nonpositive real part,
\mathcal{F}^{-1}m_{u}(t)
vanishes when
t<0
.
It is therefore consistent to suppose that
p_{u}
is defined on
\mathbb{R}
and vanishes on
\mathbb{R}^{-}
.
Once we do this, we may view
m_{u}
as
\mathcal{F}p_{u}
, or its analytic extension to the half-plane as a version of the Laplace transform of
p_{u}
that has been rotated in the complex plane.

The function
m_{u}
vanishes at infinity faster than any polynomial, and is infinitely differentiable away from
0
; its derivatives also vanish faster than any polynomial at infinity.
However, it is not smooth at
0
.
Indeed, take a compactly supported bump function
\phi
that takes the value
1
near
0
, and write

m_{u}(y)=(1-\phi(y))m_{u}(y)+\phi(y)+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\phi(y)\,\frac{(-u)^{n}}{n!}\,\omega(y)^{n}\,|y|^{n\alpha},

where
\omega(y)=[\cos(\pi\alpha/2)+i\sin(\pi\alpha/2)\operatorname{sgn}(y)]
.
The first two terms are smooth, and the least smooth of the other terms is when
n=1
.
This implies that
p_{u}
is smooth but
p_{u}(t)\eqsim t^{-1-\alpha}
when
t\to\infty
.
The derivatives of
p_{u}
behave better:
p^{\prime}_{u}(t)\eqsim t^{-2-\alpha}
,
p^{\prime\prime}_{u}(t)\eqsim t^{-3-\alpha}
, and so on when
t\to\infty
.
We summarise this in a family of inequalities:

(3.1)

\displaystyle|p_{1}(t)|\lesssim(1+t)^{-1-\alpha}\qquad\forall t\in\mathbb{R}^{+}

\displaystyle|t\,p^{\prime}_{1}(t)|\lesssim(1+t)^{-1-\alpha}\qquad\forall t\in\mathbb{R}^{+}

\displaystyle|t^{2}\,p^{\prime\prime}_{1}(t)|\lesssim(1+t)^{-1-\alpha}\qquad\forall t\in\mathbb{R}^{+}

and so on; here the implicit constants depend only on the order of the derivative and
\alpha
.

It is important for us that
p_{u}
is the probability density function for the Lévy stable distribution, and hence
p_{u}
is nonnegative and has total mass
1
.
Finally, the different
p_{u}
form a semigroup and are related to each other by a change of variable:

p_{u}*p_{v}=p_{u+v}\qquad\text{and}\qquad p_{u}(t)=u^{-1/\alpha}\,p_{1}(u^{-1/\alpha}t)

for
u,v\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
.

We now discuss analytic continuation; to facilitate this, we write
H^{\infty}(\mathbb{C}^{-})
for the Banach space of bounded holomorphic functions on the lower complex half plane (with the topology of uniform convergence on compacta in
\mathbb{C}^{-}
).
The functions
m_{u}
and
p_{s}
admit analytic continuations in several different ways.
Indeed,

m_{u}(y)=\mathrm{e}^{-u(iy)^{\alpha}}\qquad\forall y\in\mathbb{R},

and this function extends to the function
w\mapsto\mathrm{e}^{-u(iw)^{\alpha}}
in
H^{\infty}(\mathbb{C}^{-})
, which implies that the tempered distribution
\mathcal{F}^{-1}m_{u}
is supported in
[0,+\infty)
, by the Paley–Wiener theorem.
But it is also possible to continue
m_{u}(y)
analytically in
u
; indeed, for
w
in the lower half plane,

|\arg((iw)^{\alpha})|<\frac{\pi\alpha}{2}\,,

so that when moreover
|\arg(u)|<\pi(1-\alpha)/2
,

|\arg(u(iw)^{\alpha})|<\frac{\pi}{2}

and
w\mapsto\mathrm{e}^{-u(iw)^{\alpha}}
is still bounded and analytic in the lower half plane.
Thus
u\mapsto m_{u}
may be extended to a holomorphic map of the cone
\Gamma_{\pi(1-\alpha)/2}
into the space
H^{\infty}(\mathbb{C}^{-})
.

By using the inverse Fourier transformation and the Paley–Wiener theorem, we deduce that
u\mapsto p_{u}
may be extended to a holomorphic map of the cone
\Gamma_{\pi(1-\alpha)/2}
to the space of tempered distributions on
\mathbb{R}
which are supported in
[0,+\infty)
.
Then, by repeating the analysis described above of decay at infinity and the discontinuity at
0
of
m_{u}
, considered as a function of
\mathbb{R}
, we may conclude that each
p_{u}
is in fact given by integration against a smooth function which is integrable at infinity; finally, we may observe that
p_{u}(t)
may be viewed as an analytic function in both variables
t
and
u
.
Of course, the analytically continued functions need not be nonnegative.

3.2.
Spectral theory

Let
X
be a set, equipped with a positive measure
\mu
; suppose that
X
is
\sigma
-finite.
Denote the corresponding Lebesgue space by
L^{p}(X)
.
If
L
is a positive operator on
L^{2}(X)
, such that the operator
\exp(-tL)
, defined initially on
L^{2}(X)
by spectral theory, extends continuously to a contraction operator on
L^{p}(X)
. for all
p\in[1,\infty)
, then we may also write

\exp(-us^{\alpha}L^{\alpha})=\int_{0}^{\infty}p_{u}(t)\,\exp(-tsL)\,\mathrm{d}t\qquad\forall s\in\mathbb{R}^{+}

by spectral theory, and then

|\!|\!|\exp(-us^{\alpha}L^{\alpha})|\!|\!|_{p}\leq\int_{0}^{\infty}p_{u}(t)\,|\!|\!|\exp(-tsL)|\!|\!|_{p}\,\mathrm{d}t\leq\int_{0}^{\infty}p_{u}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t\leq 1,

where
|\!|\!|\cdot|\!|\!|_{p}
denotes the operator norm on
L^{p}(X)
.
Hence the operator
\exp(s^{\alpha}L^{\alpha})
also extends continuously from
L^{2}(X)
to
L^{p}(X)
for all
p\in[1,\infty)
.

The operators
\exp(-tL)
form a symmetric Markov semigroup, of the sort that was treated by Stein
[
12
]
and Cowling
[
5
]
.
In particular, these authors considered averaging operators
A^{s}
, defined by

A^{s}=\frac{1}{s}\,\int_{0}^{s}\exp(-tL)\,\mathrm{d}t,

which behave better than the original operator
\exp(-tL)
, and may be treated using the Hopf–Dunford–Schwartz maximal ergodic theorem (see
[
6
, Section VIII.6.4]
).
More precisely, for
f\in L^{p}(X)
, where
1\leq p\leq\infty
, we write
A^{*}f
for the maximal function
\sup_{s\in\mathbb{R}^{+}}|A^{s}f|
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
A^{*}
be as just defined.
If
p=1
, then

\mu\{x\in X:A^{*}f(x)>\lambda\}\leq 2\,\frac{\|f\|_{1}}{\lambda}\qquad\forall\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{+}\quad\forall f\in L^{1}(X),

while if
1<p\leq\infty
, then

\|A^{*}f\|_{p}\leq 2\left(\frac{p}{p-1}\right)^{1/p}\,\|f\|_{p}\qquad\forall f\in L^{p}(X).

3.3.
The maximal function

We have already and will continue to consider the suprema of uncountable familes of measurable functions (modulo identification almost everywhere), and at first sight, these might lead to serious problems with measurability.
Fortunately, some results of Dunford and Schwartz
[
6
, IV.12]
resolve this issue.
To state these with appropriate care, we need some definitions.

We consider the space
\mathcal{M}(X)
of measurable real-valued functions, each of which is defined on a subset of
X
of full measure.
We say that two such functions
f
and
g
are equivalent when the set
\{x\in X:f(x)=g(x)\}
is of full measure in
X
, and write
M(X)
for the space of equivalence classes of functions in
\mathcal{M}(X)
modulo this equivalence; temporarily we denote the equivalence class of
f
in
\mathcal{M}(X)
by
\dot{f}
.
Thus
M(X)
may be ordered:
\dot{f}\leq\dot{g}
if and only if the set
\{x\in X:f(x)\leq g(x)\}
is of full measure in
X
for one, and hence every, pair of representatives
f
and
g
of the equivalence classes
\dot{f}
and
\dot{g}
.
Now we may define upper bounds and least upper bounds for sets of equivalence classes in the obvious way; we write
\operatorname{l{.}u{.}b{.}}
for a least upper bound, if it exists.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
X
be a set, equipped with a positive measure
\mu
; suppose that
X
is
\sigma
-finite.
Let
\mathcal{F}
be a family of functions
f
in
\mathcal{M}(X)
, and
\dot{\mathcal{F}}
denote the corresponding family of equivalence classes
\dot{f}
in
M(X)
.
Suppose that
\dot{\mathcal{F}}
is bounded above in
M(X)
by
\dot{g}
.
Then
\operatorname{l{.}u{.}b{.}}\dot{\mathcal{F}}
exists in
M(X)
, and is the equivalence class of
\sup\{f:f\in\mathcal{F}_{0}\}
, for a suitable countable subfamily
\mathcal{F}_{0}
of
\mathcal{F}
.
If
1\leq p\leq\infty
and
g\in L^{p}(X)
, then
\operatorname{l{.}u{.}b{.}}\dot{\mathcal{F}}\in L^{p}(X)
too.
The corresponding result also holds if
\dot{g}
is in a Lorentz space on
X
.

In light of the lemma, we may write
\sup\{f:f\in\dot{\mathcal{F}}\}
in place of
\operatorname{l{.}u{.}b{.}}\dot{\mathcal{F}}
.
We now revert to the standard practice of not distinguishing between functions and equivalence classes of functions.

Now we generalise Stein
[
12
, Section III.3]
.
Suppose that
X
,
\mu
, and
L
are as in Subsection
3.2
.
For a continuously differentiable function
h:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{C}
that vanishes on
(-\infty,0]
, we write
\tilde{h}
its Laplace transform, and define the corresponding operator
\tilde{h}(L)
as follows:

\tilde{h}(L)=\int_{0}^{\infty}h(t)\,\exp(-tL)\,\mathrm{d}t.

We now give the main result of this subsection.

Theorem 3.3
.

Suppose that
C
is a constant and the continuously differentiable function
h:[0,+\infty)\to\mathbb{C}
satisfies the conditions

\lim_{t\to 0+}|t\,h(t)|=\lim_{t\to+\infty}|t\,h(t)|=0

and

\int_{0}^{\infty}\,|t\,h^{\prime}(t)|\,\mathrm{d}t\leq C.

Then for all
f\in L^{p}(X)
,

|\tilde{h}(L)f|\leq C\,A^{*}f.

Consequently , if
\mathcal{H}_{C}
denotes the family of functions
h
satisfying these conditions, then for all
f\in L^{p}(X)
,

\sup\{|\tilde{h}(L)f|:h\in\mathcal{H}\}\leq C\,A^{*}f.

Proof.

Observe that

\displaystyle\tilde{h}(L)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}h(t)\,\exp(-tL)\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}h(t)\,\frac{\mathrm{d}}{dt}\left(\int_{0}^{t}\exp(-sL)\,\mathrm{d}s\right)\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=-\int_{0}^{\infty}h^{\prime}(t)\int_{0}^{t}\exp(-sL)\,\mathrm{d}s\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=-\int_{0}^{\infty}t\,h^{\prime}(t)\left(\frac{1}{t}\,\int_{0}^{t}\exp(-sL)\,\mathrm{d}s\right)\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=-\int_{0}^{\infty}t\,h^{\prime}(t)\,A^{t}\,\mathrm{d}t.

It follows that, for
f\in L^{p}(X)
,

\displaystyle|\tilde{h}(L)f|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\int_{0}^{\infty}t\,h^{\prime}(t)\,A^{t}f\,\mathrm{d}t\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}|t\,h^{\prime}(t)|\,\mathrm{d}t\right)\,A^{*}f

\displaystyle\leq CA^{*}f.

The result now follows from the previous lemma.
∎

We want to apply this result to families of functions
h
, and want the estimates to be dilation invariant, so that we get the same estimate for all
\exp(-tL^{\alpha})
as
t
varies onver
\mathbb{R}^{+}
.
The next lemma does this.
Recall that
\tilde{h}
is the Laplace transform of
h
, and note that the Laplace transform of the function
t\mapsto t\,h^{\prime}(t)
is the function
\lambda\mapsto\tilde{h}(\lambda)+\lambda\,\tilde{h}^{\prime}(\lambda)
.

Lemma 3.4
.

The inequality

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{+\infty}|t\,h^{\prime}(t)|\,dt

\displaystyle\lesssim\sup_{s}|\tilde{h}(is)|+\sup_{s}|s\tilde{h}^{\prime}(is)|+\sup_{s}|s^{2}\tilde{h}^{\prime\prime}(is)|+\sup_{s}|s^{3}\tilde{h}^{\prime\prime\prime}(is)|

\displaystyle+\biggl(\sup_{s}|s^{2}\tilde{h}(is)|\sup_{s}|\tilde{h}^{\prime\prime}(is)|+\sup_{s}|s^{2}\tilde{h}(is)|\sup_{s}|s\tilde{h}^{\prime\prime\prime}(is)|

\displaystyle+\sup_{s}|s^{3}\tilde{h}^{\prime}(is)|\sup_{s}|\tilde{h}^{\prime\prime}(is)|\biggr)^{1/2}

holds for all continuously differentiable functions
h:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{C}
supported in
[0,+\infty)
, whose Laplace transform
\tilde{h}
is analytic on
\mathbb{C}^{-}
and continuously differentiable and bounded on the closure of
\mathbb{C}^{-}
.

Proof.

Suppose that
g:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{C}
satisfies

(3.2)

\sup_{s\in\mathbb{R}}|(1+a^{2}s^{2})\,(1-b^{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}}{ds^{2}})g(s)|<\infty

for all
a,b\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
.
Then

\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}|(1-b^{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}}{ds^{2}})g(s)|\,ds\leq\frac{\pi}{a}\sup\{(1+a^{2}s^{2})\,(1-b^{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}}{ds^{2}})g(s)|:s\in\mathbb{R}\},

whence, taking Fourier transforms, we see that

(1+b^{2}t^{2})\hat{g}(t)|\leq\frac{\pi}{a}\sup\{(1+a^{2}s^{2})\,(1-b^{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}}{ds^{2}})g(s)|:s\in\mathbb{R}\}

for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
, and so

\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}|\hat{g}(t)|\,dt\leq\frac{\pi^{2}}{ab}\sup\{(1+a^{2}s^{2})\,(1-b^{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}}{ds^{2}})g(s)|:s\in\mathbb{R}\}.

Take
b
to be
a^{-1}
and then minimise in
a
; then

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}|\hat{g}(t)|\,dt

\displaystyle\leq\pi^{2}\min\{\|g(s)\|_{\infty}+a^{2}\|s^{2}g(s)\|_{\infty}+a^{-2}\|g^{\prime\prime}(s)\|_{\infty}+\|s^{2}g^{\prime\prime}(s)\|_{\infty}:a\in\mathbb{R}^{+}\}

\displaystyle=\pi^{2}(\|g(s)\|_{\infty}+\|s^{2}g(s)\|_{\infty}^{1/2}\|g^{\prime\prime}(s)\|_{\infty}^{1/2}+\|s^{2}g^{\prime\prime}(s)\|_{\infty}).

We apply this estimate to the functions
\tilde{h}(\lambda)
and
\lambda\tilde{h}^{\prime}(\lambda)
, remembering that Laplace transforms are rotated versions of Fourier transforms.
∎

We now apply our previous results to functions
m
of Laplace transform type.
In particular, it is easy to check that all the functions
m(\lambda)=\mathrm{e}^{-u\lambda^{\alpha}}
may be treated using this lemma.

Corollary 3.5
.

Suppose that
0<\theta<(1-\alpha)\pi/2
.
Then there exists a constant
C
such that

\sup\{|\exp(-uL^{\alpha})f|:u\in\Gamma_{\theta}\}\leq CA^{*}f.

Hence the operator
f\mapsto\sup\{|\exp(-uL^{\alpha})f|:u\in\Gamma_{\theta}\}
is of weak type
(1,1)
.

Cowling
[
5
]
found
L^{p}
estimates for this maximal function when
p>1
, but did not prove a weak-type
(1,1)
estimate.

4.
Characterisations of Hardy spaces by subordinated semigroups
\exp(-tL^{\alpha})
for
0<\alpha<1

In section, we assume that
(X,d,\mu)
is a metric space endowed with a metric
d
and a nonnegative Borel measure
\mu
; our arguments work in quasimetric spaces but this involves extra constants that may obscure the proof.
We assume that there exists
n\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
such that

(4.1)

\mu(B(x,r))\eqsim r^{n}\qquad\forall x\in X\quad\forall r\in\mathbb{R}^{+},

where
B(x,r)=\{y\in X:\varrho(x,y)<r\}
.

Suppose that
L
is a positive self-adjoint operator on
L^{2}(X)
. Suppose further that the kernel
p_{t}
of
\exp(-tL)
admits a Poisson upper bound, that is, there exist
m
and
\delta_{0}
in
\mathbb{R}^{+}
such that for all
x,y\in X
and
t\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
,

(4.2)

|p_{t}(x,y)|\lesssim\frac{1}{t^{n/m}}\Big(\frac{t^{1/m}}{t^{1/m}+\varrho(x,y)}\Big)^{n+\delta_{0}}.

Denote by
p_{k,t}(x,y)
the kernel of
(tL)^{k}\exp(-tL)
for
k\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
.
Note that conditions (
4.1
) and (
4.2
) imply that for any
\delta\in(0,\delta_{0})
and
k\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
,

(4.3)

|p_{k,t}(x,y)|\lesssim\frac{1}{t^{n/m}}\Big(\frac{t^{1/m}}{t^{1/m}+\varrho(x,y)}\Big)^{n+\delta}

for all
t\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
and
x,y\in X
.
See, for example,
[
4
]
.
Note that if
\exp(-tL)
admits a Gaussian upper bound, that is, there exist
m\geq 1
and
c\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
such that for all
x,y\in X
and
t\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
,

|p_{t}(x,y)|\lesssim\frac{1}{t^{n/m}}\exp\Big(-\frac{\varrho(x,y)^{m/(m-1)}}{ct^{1/(m-1)}}\Big),

then the estimate (
4.2
) holds for all
\delta_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
.

Definition 4.1
.

The area square function associated to
L^{\alpha}
is defined by

S_{L^{\alpha}}f(x):=\biggl(\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\varrho(x,y)<t}|t^{m\alpha}L^{\alpha}\exp(-t^{m\alpha}L^{\alpha})f(y)|^{2}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu(y)\,\mathrm{d}t}{t^{n+1}}\biggr)^{1/2}.

For
p\in({n}/{(n+\delta_{0})},1]
, the Hardy space
H^{p}_{L^{\alpha}}(X)
is defined to be the completion of the set

\left\{f\in L^{2}(X):S_{L^{\alpha}}f\in L^{p}(X)\right\}

in the quasi-norm
\|f\|_{H^{p}_{L^{\alpha}}}:=\|S_{L^{\alpha}}f\|_{L^{p}}
.

We now verify that
\|\cdot\|_{H^{p}_{L^{\alpha}}}
in Definition
4.1
is indeed a quasi-norm.
As argued in
[
9
]
, if
f\in L^{2}(X)
,
p\in(0,1)
, and
\|S_{L^{\alpha}}f\|_{L^{p}}=0
, then
tL^{\alpha}\exp(-tL^{\alpha})f(x)=0
for almost all
x\in X
and all
t\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
.
Since

\exp(-tL^{\alpha})-I=-\int_{0}^{t}L^{\alpha}\exp(-sL^{\alpha})\,\mathrm{d}s,

\exp(-tL^{\alpha})f(x)=f(x)
for almost all
x\in X
and all
t\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
.
On the other hand, from the upper bound on the kernel of
\exp(-tL^{\alpha})
(see Lemma
4.8
below),

\|f\|_{L^{\infty}(X)}=\|\exp(-tL^{\alpha})f\|_{L^{\infty}(X)}\lesssim t^{-\frac{n}{2m\alpha}}\|f\|_{2}

for all
t\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
.
Letting
t\to\infty
, we conclude that
f=0
almost everywhere.
This confirms that
\|\cdot\|_{H^{p}_{L^{\alpha}}}
in Definition
4.1
is a quasi-norm.

It is natural to ask whether the Hardy spaces
H^{p}_{L^{\alpha}}(X)
depend on
\alpha
.
Our first main result shows that these spaces coincide and have equivalent norms.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
\alpha\in(0,1)
and
p\in({n}/{(n+\delta_{0})},1]
. Then the Hardy spaces
H^{p}_{L^{\alpha}}(X)
and
H^{p}_{L}(X)
coincide and have equivalent norms.

The equivalence of
H^{p}_{L^{\alpha}}
and
H^{p}_{L}
was shown earlier in
[
9
]
for the case where
p=1
and
\alpha=1/2
, and where
L
is a positive self-adjoint operator satisfying Davies–Gaffney estimates.
To the best of our knowledge, our paper is the first to prove the equivalence for general
\alpha\in(0,1)
.

As a consequence of Theorem
4.2
we obtain the equivalence of BMO spaces associated to
L^{\alpha}
. We first recall the definition of these BMO spaces.
For all
\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
, we define the class
\mathcal{M}_{\beta}
to be the set of all functions
f\in L^{2}_{\mathrm{loc}}(X)
such that

(4.4)

\|f\|_{\mathcal{M}_{\beta}}:=\Big(\int_{X}\frac{|f(x)|^{2}}{(1+|x|)^{n+\beta}}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x)\Big)<\infty.

For
\alpha\in(0,1]
, we set
\mathcal{M}_{L^{\alpha}}=\bigcup_{0<\beta<\delta_{0}}\mathcal{M}_{\beta}
.

Definition 4.3
.

Let
\alpha\in(0,1]
and
0\leq\nu<{\delta_{0}}/{n}
.
We say that
f\in\mathcal{M}_{L^{\alpha}}
is in
\mathrm{BMO}^{\nu}_{L^{\alpha}}
if

(4.5)

\|f\|_{\mathrm{BMO}^{\nu}_{L^{\alpha}}}:=\sup_{B}\biggl(\frac{1}{|B|^{1+2\nu}}\int_{B}|(I-\exp(r_{B}^{m\alpha}L^{\alpha}))f(x)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x)\biggr)^{1/2},

where the supremum is taken over all balls
B
, and
r_{B}
is the radius of the ball
B
.

The following result is a consequence of Theorem
4.2
.

Theorem 4.4
.

Let
\alpha\in(0,1)
and let
0\leq\nu<{\delta_{0}}/{n}
.
Then

\mathrm{BMO}^{\nu}_{L^{\alpha}}\equiv\mathrm{BMO}^{\nu}_{L}.

Our next main result is a characterisation of the Hardy spaces
H^{p}_{L^{\alpha}}
in terms of the Littlewood–Paley square function.
We define the square function associated to
L^{\alpha}
by

G_{L^{\alpha}}f:=\biggl(\int_{0}^{\infty}|t^{m\alpha}L^{\alpha}\exp(-t^{m\alpha}L^{\alpha})f|^{2}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}t}{t}\biggr)^{1/2}.

Our second result is the following theorem.

Theorem 4.5
.

Let
\alpha\in(0,1]
and
p\in({n}/{(n+\delta_{0})},1]
, and take
f\in L^{2}(X)
.
Then
f\in H^{p}_{L^{\alpha}}(X)
if only if
G_{L^{\alpha}}f\in L^{p}(X)
; moreover,

(4.6)

\|f\|_{H^{p}_{L^{\alpha}}(X)}\eqsim\|G_{L^{\alpha}}f\|_{L^{p}(X)}.

From Theorem
4.2
and Theorem
4.5
, the following result follows immediately.

Corollary 4.6
.

Let
\alpha\in(0,1]
and
p\in({n}/{(n+\delta_{0})},1]
, and take
f\in L^{2}(X)
.
Then
f\in H^{p}_{L}(X)
if only if
G_{L^{\alpha}}f\in L^{p}(X)
; moreover,

(4.7)

\|f\|_{H^{p}_{L}(X)}\eqsim\|G_{L^{\alpha}}f\|_{L^{p}(X)}.

The problem of characterising the Hardy spaces associated to an operator by a Littlewood–Paley square function has attracted some attention.
In
[
7
]
, the authors assume that
L
is a positive self-adjoint operator, has a Gaussian upper bound and satisfies a Moser-type inequality, and prove (
4.7
) for the particular cases where
\alpha=1
and
\alpha=1/2
.
Recently, under the weaker conditions that
L
is a positive self-adjoint operator and has a Gaussian upper bound, the equivalence (
4.7
) was obtained in
[
10
]
for the case
\alpha=1
.
Our results in Theorem
4.5
and Corollary
4.6
are significant improvements of those in
[
7
,
10
]
, as we are able to prove (
4.7
) for the full range of
\alpha\in(0,1)
under the weaker condition of the Poisson upper bound (
4.2
).

Recall that an argument using Laplace transforms and contour integration shows that

(4.8)

\mathrm{e}^{-ts^{\alpha}}=\int_{0}^{\infty}p_{t}(u)\,\mathrm{e}^{-us}\,\mathrm{d}u\qquad\forall s,t\in\mathbb{R}^{+}

if and only if

\displaystyle p_{t}(u)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{0-i\infty}^{0+i\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-tz^{\alpha}+uz}\,\mathrm{d}z

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-t(iy)^{\alpha}+iuy}\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=\mathcal{F}^{-1}m_{t}(u),

where
\mathcal{F}
denotes the Fourier transformation and

m_{t}(y)=\mathrm{e}^{-t(iy)^{\alpha}}=\mathrm{e}^{-t[\cos(\pi\alpha/2)+i\sin(\pi\alpha/2){\rm sgn}(y)]\,|y|^{\alpha}}\qquad\forall y\in\mathbb{R}.

We now recall some basic properties of

(see the discussion leading up to (

) or
[

]

Lemma 4.7
.

Let

. Then

(i)

p_{t}(u)=t^{-1/\alpha}\,p_{1}(t^{-1/\alpha}u)
for all

;

(ii)

p_{1}(u)\lesssim_{N}u^{N}
for all

when
u<1
;

(iii)

p_{1}(u)\lesssim u^{-1-\alpha}
when
u\geq 1
.

Let

, and denote by

Lemma 4.8

For all

|q^{\alpha}_{t}(x,y)|+|p^{\alpha}_{t}(x,y)|\lesssim_{\delta}\frac{1}{t^{{n}/{m\alpha}}}\left(\frac{t^{{1}/{m\alpha}}}{t^{{1}/{m\alpha}}+\varrho(x,y)}\right)^{n+\delta}

for all

and all

It follows from
[

that for all

Coupled with

In what follows, we write

.
Setting

the kernel of

Lemma 4.9
.

Let
\alpha\in(0,1)
. Then for all
\delta\in(0,\delta_{0})

(4.11)

|h_{s,t}(x,y)|\lesssim\Big(\frac{s\wedge t}{s\vee t}\Big)^{m\alpha}\frac{1}{(s\vee t)^{n}}\Big(\frac{s\vee t}{s\vee t+\varrho(x,y)}\Big)^{n+\delta}

for all
s,t\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
and all
x,y\in X

Proof.

From (
4.8
),

\exp(-t^{m\alpha}L^{\alpha})=\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{-m}\,p_{1}(t^{-m}u)\,\exp(-uL)\,\mathrm{d}u.

We change variables and rewrite:

\exp(-t^{m\alpha}L^{\alpha})=\int_{0}^{\infty}p_{1}(u)\,\exp(-ut^{m}L)\,\mathrm{d}u.

By differentiating with respect to
t
and then multiplying by
t
, we obtain

-m\alpha t^{m\alpha}L^{\alpha}\exp(-t^{m\alpha}L^{\alpha})=-m\int_{0}^{\infty}p_{1}(u)\,ut^{m}L\exp(-ut^{m}L)\,\mathrm{d}u.

This implies that

Q_{t^{\alpha}}(L^{\alpha})=\alpha^{-1}\int_{0}^{\infty}p_{1}(u)\,ut^{m}L\exp(-ut^{m}L)\,\mathrm{d}u.

Hence

Q_{s}(L)Q_{t^{\alpha}}(L^{\alpha})=\alpha^{-1}\int_{0}^{\infty}p_{1}(u)\,ut^{m}s^{m}L^{2}\mathrm{e}^{-(ut^{m}+s^{m})L}\,\mathrm{d}u.

From (
4.3
), for any
\delta\in(0,\delta_{0})
,

(4.12)

\displaystyle|h_{s,t}(x,y)|

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{0}^{\infty}p_{1}(u)\frac{ut^{m}s^{m}}{(ut^{m}+s^{m})^{2}}\frac{1}{(ut^{m}+s^{m})^{n/m}}\Big(\frac{(ut^{m}+s^{m})^{1/m}}{(ut^{m}+s^{m})^{1/m}+\varrho(x,y)}\Big)^{n+\delta}\,\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\ldots+\int_{1}^{\infty}\ldots=:I_{1}+I_{2}.

First we treat the term
I_{1}
.
By using Lemma
4.7
(i), (ii) and the inequalities

\frac{1}{ua+b}\leq\frac{1}{u(a+b)}\qquad\text{and}\qquad ua+b\leq a+b

for all
a,b\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
and all
u\in(0,1]
, we see that
I_{1}
is dominated by

\int_{0}^{1}u^{N}\frac{ut^{m}s^{m}}{u^{2}(t^{m}+s^{m})^{2}}\frac{1}{u^{n/m}(t^{m}+s^{m})^{n/m}}\frac{1}{u^{{(n+\delta)}/{m}}}\Big(\frac{(t^{m}+s^{m})^{1/m}}{(t^{m}+s^{m})^{1/m}+\varrho(x,y)}\Big)^{n+\delta}\,\mathrm{d}u,

where

in Lemma
4.7
(ii) is chosen so that
N-1-\frac{2n+\delta}{m}\geq 0
. This implies that

(4.13)

\displaystyle I_{1}

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{t^{m}s^{m}}{(t^{m}+s^{m})^{2}}\frac{1}{(t^{m}+s^{m})^{n/m}}\Big(\frac{(t^{m}+s^{m})^{1/m}}{(t^{m}+s^{m})^{1/m}+\varrho(x,y)}\Big)^{n+\delta}

\displaystyle\eqsim\Big(\frac{s\wedge t}{s\vee t}\Big)^{m}\frac{1}{(s\vee t)^{n}}\Big(\frac{s\vee t}{s\vee t+\varrho(x,y)}\Big)^{n+\delta}.

We next take care of the term
I_{2}
. By Lemma
4.7
(iii),

I_{2}\lesssim\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{u^{\alpha}}\frac{ut^{m}s^{m}}{(ut^{m}+s^{m})^{2}}\frac{(ut^{m}+s^{m})^{\delta/m}}{[(ut^{m}+s^{m})^{1/m}+\varrho(x,y)]^{n+\delta}}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}u}{u^{1/2}}\,.

We now consider two cases:
s\leq t
and
s>t
.

If
s\leq t
, then
ut^{m}+s^{m}\eqsim ut^{m}\geq t^{m}
as

. Hence

(4.14)

\displaystyle I_{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{u^{\alpha}}\frac{ut^{m}s^{m}}{u^{2}t^{2m}}\frac{u^{\delta/m}t^{\delta}}{(t+\varrho(x,y))^{n+\delta}}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}u}{u}

\displaystyle\eqsim\Big(\frac{s}{t}\Big)^{m}\frac{t^{\delta}}{(t+\varrho(x,y))^{n+\delta}}.

If
s>t

I_{2}
into two parts:

I_{2}=\int_{1}^{(s/t)^{m}}\ldots+\int^{\infty}_{(s/t)^{m}}\ldots=:I_{21}+I_{22}.

Note that

as

. Therefore

(4.15)

\displaystyle I_{21}

\displaystyle\eqsim\int_{1}^{(s/t)^{m}}\frac{1}{u^{\alpha}}\frac{ut^{m}s^{m}}{s^{2m}}\frac{s^{\delta}}{(s+\varrho(x,y))^{n+\delta}}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}u}{u}

\displaystyle\eqsim\Big(\frac{s}{t}\Big)^{m\alpha}\frac{s^{\delta}}{(s+\varrho(x,y))^{n+\delta}}\,.

Since
ut^{m}+s^{m}\eqsim ut^{m}\geq s^{m}
as
u>(s/t)^{m}
,

(4.16)

\displaystyle I_{22}

\displaystyle\lesssim\int^{\infty}_{(s/t)^{m}}\frac{1}{u^{\alpha}}\frac{ut^{m}s^{m}}{u^{2}t^{2m}}\frac{u^{\delta/m}t^{\delta}}{(s+\varrho(x,y))^{n+\delta}}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}u}{u}

\displaystyle\eqsim\Big(\frac{t}{s}\Big)^{m\alpha}\frac{s^{\delta}}{(s+\varrho(x,y))^{n+\delta}}.

Taking (
4.12
), (
4.13
), (
4.14
), (
4.15
) and (
4.16
) into account, we deduce (
4.11
), as desired.

We prove similarly that

Proof of Theorem

In order to prove Theorem

Lemma 4.10
.

Let
\epsilon\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
.

\int_{X}\frac{1}{s^{n}}\Big(1+\frac{\varrho(x,y)}{s}\Big)^{-n-\epsilon}|f(y)|\,\mathrm{d}\mu(y)\lesssim\mathcal{M}f(x).

Recall that
Q(z)=z\mathrm{e}^{-z}

(4.19)

1=c_{m}\int_{0}^{\infty}Q_{t}(z)Q_{t}(z)\,\frac{\mathrm{d}t}{t}=c_{m}\int_{0}^{\infty}(t^{m}z)^{2}\mathrm{e}^{-2t^{m}z}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}t}{t}.

We now define

(4.20)

(4.21)

\displaystyle\Phi_{t}(z):=\Phi(t^{m}z)

\displaystyle=c_{m}\int_{1}^{\infty}((st)^{m}z)^{2}\mathrm{e}^{-2(st)^{m}z}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}s}{s}

\displaystyle=\frac{c_{m}}{m}\int_{t^{m}z}^{\infty}s\mathrm{e}^{-2s}\,\mathrm{d}s.

(4.22)

where

),

\frac{|Q_{t2^{j-2}}(L)f(z)|}{(1+\varrho(x,z)/2^{j})^{\lambda}}\Big(\frac{s2^{j}}{t2^{\ell}}\wedge\frac{t2^{\ell}}{s2^{j}}\Big)^{\epsilon}\lesssim Q^{*}_{L,\epsilon}f(t2^{\ell},x).

This and (
4.30
) yield that

(4.31)

\displaystyle\frac{|A(y)|}{(1+\varrho(x,y)/(s2^{j}))^{\lambda}}\Big(\frac{s2^{j}}{t2^{\ell}}\wedge\frac{t2^{\ell}}{s2^{j}}\Big)^{\epsilon}

\displaystyle\lesssim 2^{-\theta\epsilon|j-\ell|}[Q^{*}_{L,\epsilon}f(t2^{\ell},x)]^{1-\theta}\int_{X}\frac{1}{2^{jn}}\Big(1+\frac{\varrho(x,z)}{2^{j}}\Big)^{-\lambda\theta}|Q_{t2^{j-2}}(L)f(z)|^{\theta}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(z).

From (
4.26
), (
4.29
) and (
4.31
) we find that, for all
\ell,j\in\mathbb{Z}
and
s,t\in[1,2]
,

\displaystyle\frac{|Q_{s2^{j}}(L)f(y)|}{(1+\varrho(x,y)/(s2^{j}))^{\lambda}}\Big(\frac{s2^{j}}{t2^{\ell}}\wedge\frac{t2^{\ell}}{s2^{j}}\Big)^{\epsilon}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}2^{-|\ell-k|\theta\epsilon}[Q^{*}_{L,\epsilon}f(t2^{\ell},x)]^{1-\theta}\int_{X}\frac{1}{2^{kn}}\Big(1+\frac{\varrho(x,z)}{2^{k}}\Big)^{-\lambda\theta}|Q_{t2^{k-1}}(L)f(z)|^{\theta}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(z)

\displaystyle+\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}2^{-|\ell-k|\theta\epsilon}[Q^{*}_{L,\epsilon}f(t2^{\ell},x)]^{1-\theta}\int_{X}\frac{1}{2^{kn}}\Big(1+\frac{\varrho(x,z)}{2^{k}}\Big)^{-\lambda\theta}|Q_{t2^{k-2}}(L)f(z)|^{\theta}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(z)

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}2^{-|\ell-k|\theta\epsilon}[Q^{*}_{L,\epsilon}f(t2^{\ell},x)]^{1-\theta}\int_{X}\frac{1}{2^{kn}}\Big(1+\frac{\varrho(x,z)}{2^{k}}\Big)^{-\lambda\theta}|Q_{t2^{k}}(L)f(z)|^{\theta}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(z).

Taking the supremum over all
j\in\mathbb{Z}
and
s\in[1,2]
we obtain

\displaystyle Q^{*}_{L,\epsilon}f(t2^{\ell},x)^{\theta}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}2^{-|\ell-k|\theta\epsilon}\int_{X}\frac{1}{2^{kn}}\Big(1+\frac{\varrho(x,z)}{2^{k}}\Big)^{-\lambda\theta}|Q_{t2^{k}}(L)f(z)|^{\theta}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(z).

Consequently, by Minkowski’s inequality,

\displaystyle\biggl[\int_{1}^{2}Q^{*}_{L,\epsilon}f(t2^{\ell},x))^{2}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}t}{t}\biggr]^{\theta/2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}2^{-|\ell-k|\theta\epsilon}\int_{X}\frac{1}{2^{kn}}\Big(1+\frac{\varrho(x,z)}{2^{k}}\Big)^{-\lambda\theta}\biggl[\int_{1}^{2}|Q_{t2^{k}}(L)f(z)|^{2}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}t}{t}\biggr]^{\theta/2}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(z).

A change of variable implies that

\displaystyle\biggl[\int_{2^{\ell}}^{2^{\ell+1}}Q^{*}_{L,\epsilon}f(t,x))^{2}\,\,\frac{\mathrm{d}t}{t}\biggr]^{\theta/2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}2^{-|\ell-k|\theta\epsilon}\int_{X}\frac{1}{2^{kn}}\Big(1+\frac{\varrho(x,z)}{2^{k}}\Big)^{-\lambda\theta}\biggl[\int_{2^{k}}^{2^{k+1}}|Q_{t}(L)f(z)|^{2}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}t}{t}\biggr]^{\theta/2}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(z).

We now apply Lemma
4.10
and see that

\displaystyle\biggl[\int_{2^{\ell}}^{2^{\ell+1}}Q^{*}_{L,\epsilon}f(t,x))^{2}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}t}{t}\biggr]^{\theta/2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}2^{-|\ell-k|\theta\epsilon}\mathcal{M}\biggl(\biggl[\int_{2^{k}}^{2^{k+1}}|Q_{t}(L)f(z)|^{2}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}t}{t}\biggr]^{\theta/2}\biggr)(x),

so

\displaystyle\biggl[\int_{2^{\ell}}^{2^{\ell+1}}Q^{*}_{L,\epsilon}f(t,x))^{2}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}t}{t}\biggr]^{1/2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}2^{-|\ell-k|\epsilon}\mathcal{M}_{\theta}\biggl(\biggl[\int_{2^{k}}^{2^{k+1}}|Q_{t}(L)f(z)|^{2}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}t}{t}\biggr]^{1/2}\biggr)(x),

where
\mathcal{M}_{\theta}f=[\mathcal{M}(|f|^{\theta})]^{1/\theta}
.
Therefore

\sum_{\ell\in\mathbb{Z}}\int_{2^{\ell}}^{2^{\ell+1}}Q^{*}_{L,\epsilon}f(t,x))^{2}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}t}{t}\lesssim\sum_{\ell\in\mathbb{Z}}\biggl[\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}2^{-|\ell-k|\epsilon}\mathcal{M}_{\theta}\biggl(\biggl[\int_{2^{k}}^{2^{k+1}}|Q_{t}(L)f(z)|^{2}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}t}{t}\biggr]^{1/2}\biggr)(x)\biggr]^{2}.

From Young’s inequality, it follows that

\biggl[\int_{2^{\ell}}^{2^{\ell+1}}Q^{*}_{L,\epsilon}f(t,x))^{2}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}t}{t}\biggr]^{1/2}\lesssim\biggl[\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathcal{M}_{\theta}\Big(\biggl[\int_{2^{k}}^{2^{k+1}}|Q_{t}(L)f(z)|^{2}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}t}{t}\biggr]^{1/2}\Big)^{2}(x)\biggr]^{1/2}.

From the Fefferman–Stein maximal inequality, we deduce that

(4.32)

\bigg\|\biggl[\int_{0}^{\infty}Q^{*}_{L,\epsilon}f(t,x))^{2}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}t}{t}\biggr]^{1/2}\biggr\|_{L^{p}(X)}\lesssim\biggl\|\biggl[\int_{0}^{\infty}|Q_{t}(L)f|^{2}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}t}{t}\biggr]^{1/2}\biggr\|_{L^{p}(X)}.

On the other hand, it is obvious that

S_{L}f(x)\lesssim\biggl[\int_{0}^{\infty}Q^{*}_{L,\epsilon}f(t,x))^{2}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}t}{t}\biggr]^{1/2}.

This, together with (
4.32
), yields the inequality

\|S_{L}f\|_{L^{p}(X)}\lesssim\|G_{L}f\|_{L^{p}(X)},

which completes the proof.
