Calling for a GeoHealth Decadal Survey: Accelerating Solution‐Oriented Research and Applications to Address Environmental and Health Problems
Authors: D. Q. Tong, M. E. Gorris, A. M. Abadi, S. Anenberg, K. Ardon‐Dryer, J. R. Bratburd, K. Cromar, Y. Hang, R. D. Harp, A. Hoffman‐Hall, C. J. Horwell, G. H. Kerr, Y. Ma, S. J. Ryan, J. D. Stowell, M. Usmani, M. C. Wimberly, B. F. Zaitchik, K. Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGeoHealth is a rapidly growing, transdisciplinary field that integrates Earth and health sciences to understand the complex interactions between these domains. In 2017, the GeoHealth section of the American Geophysical Union and affiliated journal were established and both have grown rapidly since. Despite this growth, the field faces current and foreseeable challenges in translating scientific insights into action, including fragmented data infrastructure, funding gaps, and an evolving policy landscape. This commentary piece advocates for a GeoHealth Decadal Survey, a consensus‐driven report to identify priority research questions, guide workforce development, build community consensus, and inform strategic investments over the next decade.

Introduction

GeoHealth is an emerging scientific field that integrates Earth, environmental, health, and data sciences to advance our knowledge of the complex interactions among these areas of study (AGU, 2025a, 2025b). GeoHealth aims to generate new scientific knowledge and transform it into action by engaging and collaborating with community and policy‐making partners. As a broad and inherently global, transdisciplinary field, GeoHealth research spans a diversity of topics. Recently published commentary pieces highlight several GeoHealth themes, including community and collaboration (Abadi et al., 2026b; Akakpo et al., 2024; Ardon‐Dryer et al., 2024; Rosofsky & Vorhees, 2023; Stowell et al., 2024), extreme weather (Manware et al., 2026; Yoon et al., 2024), air quality (Ardon‐Dryer et al., 2023; Laidlaw et al., 2023), mental health (Abadi et al., 2026a), artificial intelligence (Anderson et al., 2023), and policy (Calder & Schartup, 2023). The importance of this field became especially clear through research and engagement during the COVID‐19 pandemic, as the pandemic inadvertently provided an opportunity to study how human response impacted the Earth system, how the Earth system impacted the pandemic, and the capacity of GeoHealth to inform real‐time policy (Gorris et al., 2021).

GeoHealth research seeks to empower global communities through clear communication and targeted education, while advancing equitable public health outcomes and highlighting disparities in the ways different populations are affected (Hoffman‐Hall et al., 2022). With the continued growth of GeoHealth, coupled with contemporary and foreseeable challenges, the GeoHealth community calls for a Decadal Survey to accelerate the growth of the field through continued progress and achievement. A decadal survey is a consensus‐driven report that provides a comprehensive assessment of the state of a scientific field, identifies key scientific questions that will drive future progress in the field, and outlines a strategic plan for investing in the field over the next decade. These surveys are “decadal” in that they target a mid‐range planning horizon of approximately 10 years: long enough to allow for a sequence of meaningful investments and initiatives, but short enough to allow for concrete implementation plans.

The History of GeoHealth

The history of GeoHealth spans time immemorial. Thousands of years ago, people in ancient China, Egypt and India used their knowledge of the environment and geology to treat diseases. In Ancient Greece, lung diseases in quarry workers were recognized as being caused by exposure to silica dusts (Upadhyay et al., 2024). Huainanzi, an early Chinese book published between 180 and 123 BC, describes the relationship between human health status and climate, geography, drinking water, and food (Finkelman, 2025; Vankeerberghen, 2013). Various geological materials, including mercury compounds, pearls, coral, turquoise, and other minerals were used for health purposes in Africa, Greece, Rome, Central America, Arabia, and Europe (Finkelman, 2025). Since the Middle Ages, the subject of mineralogy was part of British medical training and many medical practitioners have since contributed to different aspects of Medical Geology and GeoHealth (Duffin et al., 2017). More recently, several GeoHealth‐related forums or societies have been established to stimulate collaborations between environmental and health researchers and practitioners, including the GeoHealth Section of the American Geophysical Union (AGU).

The AGU GeoHealth Section was officially formed in 2017, coinciding with the launch of the affiliate AGU GeoHealth journal (GeoHealth, 2025; Figure 1). Since AGU is the world's largest society of Earth and space scientists, the GeoHealth Section is uniquely positioned to bridge Earth scientists with health researchers. The GeoHealth Section has grown in both membership and involvement at the AGU Annual Meeting since its inception. Starting with just 8 active members upon inauguration in 2017, the section boasted 606 members in the following year (Figure 2a). While AGU, as a whole, experienced a large membership decline during the COVID‐19 pandemic (Figure 2b), GeoHealth rebounded and continued to grow, reaching 973 members in 2024 (AGU, internal communications), with membership across 47 countries.

At the AGU Annual Meeting, GeoHealth has greatly increased its contributions to the scientific program. GeoHealth has climbed from 20th out of 24 sections in total abstract submissions in 2018 (325 submissions) to 16th in 2025 (421 submissions), reaching a record high number of abstract submissions in 2024 (435 submissions; Figure 2c). This upward trend reflects the rapid growth of the Section and the rising prominence of GeoHealth science, as well as a strong recovery from the COVID‐19 pandemic, which had suppressed abstract submissions across all Sections. Another indicator of strong GeoHealth interest is that, while all AGU abstract submissions dropped an average of 26% in 2025, which was most likely a reflection of the U.S. government shutdown and restrictions on U.S. federal travel, GeoHealth submissions only dropped by 3% (AGU, internal communication).

Research interests and efforts similar to those of the AGU GeoHealth section are present across other agencies, societies, and organizations, emphasizing the importance of this growing field. While an exhaustive list of efforts would be too long to report, here we mention several examples. The GEO (Global Earth Observations) Health Community of Practice is a global network of governments, organizations, and observers, which seeks to use environmental observations to improve health decision‐making at the international, regional, country, and district levels (GEO Health CoP, 2025). The World Health Organization (WHO) has a department on Environment, Climate Change, and Health (WHO, 2025), and both the World Meteorological Organization (WMO) and United National Environment Program support GeoHealth‐related efforts (e.g., WMO, 2025; WMO, 2023; UNEP, 2025). The WMO launched a new initiative, called the Air Pollution ‐ Climate Change ‐ Health Effects Nexus (ARCH) Working Group, to identify important gaps and propose action plans related to health impacts of weather and environmental changes (WMO, 2026). There are many parallels in interest and advocacy between AGU GeoHealth and The Planetary Health Alliance (PHA). PHA is a global network of organizations that promote Planetary Health research on the relationships between human health and Earth systems to secure Earth's future through science, collaboration, and transformative action (PHA, 2025). This includes research on the relationships between human health and Earth systems. AGU GeoHealth also holds many similar goals and values to One Health research. One Health addresses the interconnections between human health, animal health, and environmental health research and includes organizations such as the One Health Society (One Health Society, 2026).

In addition to those already mentioned, other professional societies have related sections; examples of U.S.‐based societies include the Ecological Society of America's Disease Ecology section (ESA, 2025); the American Meteorological Society's Board on Environment and Health (AMS, 2025); the American Association of Geographers (AAG) Health and Medical Geography Specialist Group (HMGSG, 2025), and the Geological Society of America's Geology and Health Division (GSA, 2025). Some examples of international societies include the International Medical Geology Association (IMGA, 2025) and the International Society for Environmental Epidemiology (ISEE, 2025). Federal agencies and academic research institutions in the U.S. have also formed GeoHealth‐related initiatives, such as the Health and Air Quality program at the U.S. National Aeronautics and Space Administration (NASA, 2025), the GeoHealth Non‐Academic Research Internships for Graduate Students (INTERN) program at the U.S. National Science Foundation (NSF, 2020), the Center for Health and the Global Environment at the University of Washington (University of Washington, 2025). These efforts extend globally, such as the European Centre for Environment and Human Health at the University of Exeter, U.K. (University of Exeter, 2025), Afrique One‐Research Excellence for African Challenges in Health (REACH) program (Afrique One, 2023), the Climate Health Initiative based in Norway (University of Bergen, 2026), and the Lancet Countdown (The Lancet Countdown, 2026).

Contemporary and Foreseeable Challenges in GeoHealth

The contemporary and foreseeable challenges in GeoHealth are marked by the mounting complexity at the intersection of environmental and human systems, particularly as environmental changes propel societies toward critical tipping points with health impacts that may be both unpredictable and irreversible (Armstrong McKay et al., 2022; Lenton, 2011; Lenton et al., 2019). Progress is stymied by fragmented data infrastructure, where a lack of standardized protocols and limited data harmony across environmental, epidemiological, and social domains inhibits robust, large‐scale analyses (Mueller et al., 2020). A transdisciplinary paradigm uniting Earth sciences, social sciences, epidemiology, and computational analytics is essential to respond effectively. Limited funding opportunities and siloed approaches undermine such integration. Moreover, competing priorities and delays in implementing interventions further hinder the translation of scientific insights into action. Other key challenges are communicating GeoHealth science with the public more effectively and creating actionable data‐driven solutions. Establishing and maintaining both global and national networks spanning academic and professional fields, particularly linking academic researchers with practitioners and decision‐makers, is imperative to ensure the public continues to benefit from new research in this field (Horwell et al., 2023). Addressing grand challenges in GeoHealth demands ethical research engagement, improved capacity for data synthesis, sustainable funding for GeoHealth across all career stages, and long‐term investment to build solutions that anticipate and prevent socio‐environmental crises in different geographical and cultural settings.

Since the formal emergence of GeoHealth as a field, funding opportunities have generally increased, reflecting the recognition of the important links between the environment and health. Early growth in the U.S. was driven by targeted federal and philanthropic initiatives, which enabled cross‐sector collaborations, expanded monitoring networks, and strengthened applied research capacity. The NSF recognizes GeoHealth as a bridge between Earth systems and health and has supported GeoHealth research through programs like the Dear Colleague Letter: Directorate for Geosciences (GEO) Opportunity for Graduate Students Supplemental Funding to Link Geosciences and Human Health (GeoHealth INTERN) (NSF, 2023) and other interdisciplinary funding calls. The U.S. National Institutes of Health (NIH) has supported GeoHealth research on geophysical determinants of disease, for example, through the NIH Program on Health and Extreme Weather (NIH, 2024). The U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) has provided recurring cooperative agreements to state, tribal, and local health agencies (CDC, 2024). The U.S. National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA) has advanced health‐linked adaptation research through programs such as the Extreme Heat Risk Initiative, while NOAA's National Integrated Drought and Information System has funded drought‐ and health‐relevant decision support research. NASA supports applications‐focused projects through Research Opportunities in Space and Earth Science (ROSES) programs like Health & Air Quality Applied Sciences Team (HAQAST) and Public Health initiatives. The U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) has also supported GeoHealth research through Science to Achieve Results (STAR) grants, including topics such as wildfire smoke preparedness, air pollution monitoring, early lifestage vulnerabilities to environmental stressors, and wellbeing and ecosystem services. Philanthropic contributions such as the Health Effects Institute, the Burroughs Wellcome Fund, the Wellcome Trust, and the Robert Wood Johnson Foundation have further expanded the reach and diversity of GeoHealth funding. However, the U.S. GeoHealth funding landscape, as well as funding outside of the U.S., has fluctuated and recently relied more heavily on philanthropic and state‐level programs. These funding sources, too, are sensitive to changes in socioeconomic priorities and geopolitical factors. Sustained progress in GeoHealth research requires stabilizing funding opportunities to ensure continuity, long‐term data collection, and the ability to translate scientific insights into effective public health action.

Calling for a GeoHealth Decadal Survey

To address the current state of the field and identify future directions for growth, the AGU GeoHealth Section is calling for the organization of, and inviting participation in, a GeoHealth Decadal Survey. A decadal survey would serve multiple purposes in nurturing and growing the emerging field of GeoHealth by providing an unprecedented opportunity for the entire community to come together to network and discuss both the challenges and opportunities facing the discipline. Through community discussions and organized events, the GeoHealth Decadal Survey would help build consensus on scientific priorities and strategies. It would also convene educators and academic leaders, such as Earth sciences and environmental health departmental chairs (Turpin et al., 2022), to explore educational approaches that better align classroom training with the skills and scientific demands of this fast‐evolving field. Finally, through the recommendations in its final report and other resulting publications, the GeoHealth Decadal Survey would provide valuable, community‐informed guidance to funding agencies and private funders as they make decisions on future investments.

Previous Earth science decadal surveys have been produced by the U.S. National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine (NASEM), such as those undertaken in Ocean Sciences, Materials Research, Astronomy & Astrophysics, and Earth Observations from Space. To work toward such a formal GeoHealth Decadal Survey, we authors seek interested collaborators and propose to: (a) mobilize an ad hoc working group through this call for action; (b) organize one or more workshops targeting GeoHealth topics to help refine the decadal survey process and key participants; (c) work with relevant federal agencies to generate a formal request and statement of task; and (d) provide support, as requested, in the committee formation process and the solicitation of community input during the preparation of the report. Given the intertwined processes between Earth system changes and human health from local to global scales, the GeoHealth Decadal Survey should actively encourage international involvement.

In working toward consensus on the most important scientific questions and strategic investments, the GeoHealth Decadal Survey would channel the intellectual energy of the field to set research priorities, guide workforce education, and influence funding directions. Through its community‐driven process, it would gather a broad set of scientific and social interests and distill them into a clear, prioritized, and consensus‐based strategy. Simultaneously, the GeoHealth Decadal Survey would be an exciting opportunity for the GeoHealth field to enhance our identity and recognition by the broader geoscience and health sciences disciplines. A successful GeoHealth Decadal Survey would inform the funding priorities of U.S. government agencies and international and private funders, helping these programs direct investments toward areas with the greatest scientific and societal impact. While informing these decisions is a central objective for the GeoHealth Decadal Survey, the survey would also serve as an intellectual lodestar for the broader research community. In addition, the survey would highlight emerging subdisciplines and identify skill gaps in the technical, analytical, and interdisciplinary expertise needed for the next generation of GeoHealth researchers and practitioners, offering valuable guidance for universities, funding agencies, and professional societies in shaping workforce education.

As a community‐driven initiative, the GeoHealth Decadal Survey invites wide and active participation of researchers, educators, and practitioners at every career stage. Broad representation across the field is essential to identify current and emerging societal needs, highlight new opportunities for interdisciplinary collaborations, and develop recommendations to strengthen GeoHealth workforce education. Volunteer leaders will play a key role in connecting researchers with stakeholders, facilitating community discussions, and supporting the culminating GeoHealth Decadal Survey workshop and the final report.

Further information on the GeoHealth Decadal Survey, including how to engage with the effort, is available on the AGU website: https://connect.agu.org/geohealth/events/decadal‐survey.
