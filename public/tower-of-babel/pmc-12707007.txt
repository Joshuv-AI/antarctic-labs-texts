Social ecological model for developing health-promoting schools and improving healthy eating in female students: a mixed-method protocol study in Iran
Authors: Farzaneh Pourjalil, Nahid Shahabi, Zahra Hosseini, Teamur Aghamolaei
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe concept of health-promoting schools, initiated by the World Health Organization, aims to move beyond individual behavior change and includes changes in the organizational structure, such as improvements in the physical and social environment of the school. By integrating health promotion into all aspects of life, health-promoting schools aim to equip students with the knowledge, skills, and supportive environment required to make healthy choices and develop lifelong healthy behaviors. The present study will aim to apply the social-ecological model in establishing health-promoting schools and improving healthy eating behavior in female students on the basis of social cognitive theory (SCT) in Bastak County, Hormozgan Province, southern Iran.MethodsThe present exploratory mixed-methods study will be conducted in four phases: qualitative, instrumental, cross-sectional, and interventional. The qualitative phase will continue until data saturation. On the basis of the concepts that will be extracted in the qualitative phase, the SCT questionnaire will be designed and psychometrically validated. In the cross-sectional phase, effective factors for establishing a health-promoting school and promoting healthy eating behaviors will be identified, and in the intervention phase, necessary interventions will be designed in accordance with health promotion strategies and will be implemented in the selected school serving as the intervention group. To evaluate the impact of the intervention, both intervention and control groups will be assessed in terms of healthy eating behavior before and 3 months after the intervention. The social-ecological model will be employed as the guiding framework for the development and implementation of school-based health promotion interventions throughout the study.DiscussionThe aim of this study will be to design a program to establish a health-promoting school and improve healthy eating behaviors in students on the basis of the Health-Promoting School Checklist and the SCT model. The social-ecological model will also be used as a guiding framework in the design and implementation of the school-based interventions. The outcomes of this intervention will contribute to a deeper understanding of the factors that will influence the promotion of healthy eating behaviors in students. The findings will be effective in the process of developing health-promoting schools and fostering healthy eating behaviors in adolescents.Trial registrationIranian Registry of Clinical Trials IRCT20241123063812N1. Introduction

Background and rationale {6a}

Today, protecting people’s health is a significant challenge faced by many countries [1]. The most recent World Health Organization report (World Health Statistics, 2023) indicates that noncommunicable diseases now represent the greatest share of global illness and death, with their prevalence worsening over the last 20 years. The mortality rate of these diseases increased by more than one-third from 2000 to 2019, from 31 to 41 million; that is, approximately three out of every four deaths worldwide are due to noncommunicable diseases (https://www.who.int/data/gho/publications/world-health-statistics). Cardiovascular diseases, cancers, chronic respiratory diseases, diabetes, and mental health conditions are considered the main types of noncommunicable diseases [2]. In 2011, the United Nations adopted a declaration for the prevention and control of noncommunicable diseases, emphasizing the reduction of risk factors and the creation of healthy environments [3]. Schools are recognized among the most important settings for health promotion, as the determinants of noncommunicable diseases emerge from childhood and adolescence. Therefore, schools play a major role in initiating prevention and health promotion activities [4]. As healthy and supportive environments, schools play a pivotal role in fostering students’ physical, emotional, and social health. By ensuring safety, promoting communication, teaching essential life skills, providing health services, and engaging families and communities, schools pave the way for student success and healthier communities [5].

A study in Germany revealed that only 51% of girls and 59% of boys eat breakfast on a daily basis. Only 10% of girls and 17% of boys meet the World Health Organization recommendations for physical activity. The rate of fruit and vegetable consumption is lower than desirable among girls and boys, and there are gender differences in physical activity and diet [6]. A body of research in Iran has shown that students face problems such as overweight, obesity, lack of physical activity, and tobacco use. A study from 2014 to 2015 involving 14,274 Iranian students revealed that approximately 20.6% of children and adolescents aged 7–18 years in 31 provinces of the country were obese or overweight [7]. Schools are not only places where children and adolescents are found but also capable of providing structures and conditions to foster physical activity and a friendly educational environment. The possibility of planning healthy nutrition and physical activity affects students’ health and healthy behavior [8, 9]. Recent research suggests that factors such as physical inactivity [10], overweight status [11], and mental health issues can adversely affect school performance and compromise the core mission of schools [12]. Compared with interventions that focus only on individuals or determinants of health, holistic approaches to school health promotion hold more promise [13]. These approaches address not only individual behaviors but also the physical and social environment and involve all members of the school and community [13]. The health-promoting school approach was introduced at the First International Conference on Health Promotion in 1986. The Ottawa Charter contends that health promotion is a process of empowering individuals to take greater control over their own health and the environment around them [14]. Health-promoting schools aim not only to change individual behavior but also to make organizational and structural changes, such as improving the physical and social environment of the school, curricula, and teaching methods [15].

The Health Promotion School Program in Iran has eight components: (1) Comprehensive health education program, (2) provision of clinical services at school, (3) environmental health at school, (4) improvement of nutrition at school, (5) physical activity at school, (6) promotion of health of school staff, (7) mental health and counseling services at school, and (8) parent and community participation in school health programs and the student health volunteer network. The ranking of health-promoting schools in Iran is based on national and regional guidelines and aims to evaluate and improve the quality of health programs in schools. This ranking is usually performed by awarding stars to schools, which vary from one to five stars to indicate the extent to which health indicators and criteria are met in schools [16].

Health promotion programs prevent noncommunicable diseases such as cancer, heart disease, and diabetes through education, health promotion, and supportive measures. Children and adolescents who enjoy adequate education, a balanced diet, a healthy environment, and emotional support are better prepared to face future challenges. A well-educated and informed adolescent has high self-esteem and avoids undesirable behaviors. To cause behavior change, a theory that has been proven to be effective in predicting and changing behaviors should be selected. This theory should include measures that practically achieve the desired goals [17]. The success of health education depends on the use of appropriate theories [18]. Interventions that are based on a theory are more likely to be successful. Social cognitive theory (SCT) is suitable for understanding health behaviors because it explains the interactions among the individual, the environment, and behavior [19].

In SCT, human behavior is explained by a three-dimensional model that includes cognitive influences (self-efficacy, collective efficacy, outcome expectations, knowledge), environmental influences (observational learning, normative beliefs, social support, barriers and opportunities), and behavioral factors (behavioral skills, intentions, reinforcement, and punishment). These factors mutually influence each other and shape individual behavior [20]. In addition to SCT, the social ecological model of health is also an influential model in this field. This model focuses on various factors that affect health [21].

The social ecological model suggests that health is influenced by the interaction between the individual, group, community, and physical, social, and political environments [22]. This model affirms that behavior is shaped by the interaction between the environment and the individual, and that different levels, such as individual, social, physical, and political, are actively involved [21].

The ecological health model and SCT can help create a health-promoting school and develop healthy behaviors in adolescents that are likely to continue into adulthood. All that should be considered in health-promoting schools is empowerment and the ability to make decisions and choose. Given the prevalence of unhealthy lifestyles in southern Iran, such as Bastak County, students are also affected by this lifestyle [23]. The present study will aim to understand the factors that will affect the development of a health-promoting school and the promotion of healthy eating behaviors at different levels from the perspectives of school staff and students. It will also aim to design an intervention program that will be based on the combined ecological social health model and SCT in Bastak city.

Objectives {7}

The objective of this study will be to understand the factors affecting the establishment of a health-promoting school and the promotion of healthy eating behaviors at different levels from the perspectives of school staff and students, design a valid and reliable instrument to assess healthy eating behaviors based on SCT, determine the effectiveness of an intervention based on the social ecological model of health to establish a health-promoting school, and determine the effectiveness of an intervention based on SCT to promote healthy eating behaviors in female students.

The hypothesis will be that the application of the health ecological model with a multilevel influence approach will facilitate the establishment of a health-promoting school, and that the SCT approach, in which human behavior will be formed by a combination of cognitive, environmental influences, and behavioral skills, can effectively modify students’ nutritional behavior.

Trial design {8}

This exploratory mixed-methods study consists of four phases as follows. The research population will include female lower secondary school students in Bastak city, aged 12 to 14 years. The nutritional behaviors and lifestyles of female students at school age can significantly affect their future health and that of subsequent generations; therefore, modifying these behaviors may contribute to improving community health. Moreover, female students, as a target group, can play an effective role in transferring knowledge and healthy nutritional behaviors to their families and society [24]. The feasibility of the intervention will be assessed during the initial phase through monitoring recruitment, participant attendance, acceptability, and implementation challenges. If necessary, study procedures will be refined based on these findings before full-scale implementation.

In the first phase of the study, two qualitative studies will be conducted. In the first qualitative study, in-depth individual and semi-structured interviews will be conducted with health-promoting school staff in Bastak city, and schools will be selected on the basis of the health-promoting school checklist [16]. In the second qualitative study, on the basis of SCT, in-depth individual and semi-structured interviews and group discussions will be conducted with school students, parents, and staff regarding healthy eating behaviors.

In the second phase, an instrument will be designed on the basis of the results of the qualitative phase, and the instrument will be psychometrically validated.

The third phase will be a cross-sectional study to determine healthy eating behavior and the role of SCT constructs in predicting the eating behavior of students. Additionally, by analyzing the data from the cross-sectional study, the most important influential constructs will be identified.

Considering the predictive values of each construct as well as the nutritional behavior status of the sample of students, educational content will be designed to implement an effective intervention.

In the fourth phase (final phase), the intervention is designed and, after implementation, is evaluated via a posttest. Both the intervention content and the researcher‑developed questionnaire will be grounded in findings from previous phases and aligned with the eight components of health-promoting schools. In the intervention phase, the most appropriate communication and educational methods for students are used, considering the results obtained from the previous phases of study.

The educational methods used to promote healthy eating habits for students are varied and practical. They are designed to increase knowledge, change attitudes, and foster healthy eating behaviors. Key approaches include interactive classroom teaching, practical and participatory teaching, teaching through family and adult role models, and the use of creative activities and educational games.

A health-promoting school is a system that, with a comprehensive and systemic approach, promotes students’ health through empowerment, a supportive environment, continuous education, active participation, and healthy policymaking. This approach requires careful planning, implementation, and continuous evaluation to institutionalize a culture of health in the school environment and enable students to maintain a healthy lifestyle throughout their lives.

The feasibility of the school-based intervention will be evaluated as part of the main study. We intend to closely monitor recruitment rates, participant engagement, acceptability among students and staff, and any logistical issues that may arise during the early stages. If significant challenges are identified, appropriate modifications will be made prior to scaling up the intervention.

Inclusion criteria: qualitative phase

Eligible participants will be those employed as the teaching staff at school, female students in junior high schools, and individuals who will be willing to share experiences and information with the researcher during the interviews.

To run the abovementioned programs, a team will be formed of stakeholders (school principals, student representatives at all three grades of junior high school, school administrators, education health officers, and parent representatives). Notably, the above intervention will be based on the framework of the ecological model to implement the intervention program at school.

Another intervention program will be designed to improve healthy eating behavior among students based on the social cognitive theory (SCT). The program materials will focus on behavioral predictors identified through a qualitative study and a pretest via a researcher-made questionnaire.

At the beginning of the program, all students will be assured that the information they provide will remain strictly confidential. They will participate in the educational program voluntarily and with written consent. The SCT-driven intervention will consist of 30-min educational sessions, delivered twice a week over a 12-week period (total: 24 sessions). Fidelity will be ensured through standardized training for facilitators, regular monitoring using attendance logs, and periodic on-site supervision by research team members. The broader school-wide intervention will extend across the school year, with activities scheduled per the school calendar.

Implementation fidelity and reach will be assessed via the proportion of planned sessions delivered, the number and type of environmental changes enacted, and attendance/engagement logs. Acceptability among participants will be evaluated through post-intervention feedback, and process data (including barriers and facilitators) will be collected to inform future scaling.

For better interaction between participants and teachers, a group on a web-based social network will be created for parents to receive educational content. A nutrition expert will collaborate with the research team to plan to encourage participants to adopt and maintain healthy eating behaviors.

In this study, various health promotion strategies will be used to facilitate educational activities. Support will be sought from different sectors, including the education system, municipalities, comprehensive health service centers, and private organizations, to ensure effective cooperation.

In this educational program, in accordance with the characteristics of the target population, the following educational methods are used: critical thinking, brainstorming, group discussion, lectures, and Q&A sessions. To enhance learning a variety of supplementary activities will be implemented, including the preparation and use of posters, pamphlets, essay writing competitions, recreational camps, health fairs focused on healthy nutrition, healthy breakfast programs, cooking competitions, counseling sessions, and instructional videos.

Sample size {14}

Qualitative stage

In qualitative research, the sample size will not be determined in advance. Instead, it will be estimated on the basis of the data collected and analyzed. Sampling will continue until data saturation is reached. Data saturation will be determined during the qualitative data collection process when no new codes, themes, or insights emerge from the interviews, and additional data no longer contribute to the development of new categories. The research team will carefully review and analyze the data after each round of interviews to identify the point at which saturation is reached.

Instrumentation and cross-sectional phase

Given that the purpose of the cross-sectional phase will be to test the psychometric properties of the instrument, the sample size will be considered according to the standards of psychometric studies that require at least five respondents per item [25]. For the cross-sectional study, which will aim to examine the predictive power of the model constructs, if the effect size is considered 0.10 and the number of predictors will be 11 model constructs, the sample size will be 179. Since the samples will be clustered, this value should be multiplied by 179 by the design effect size of 2, which results in a final sample size of 358. Confirmatory factor analysis in AMOS will be used for construct validation, and Cronbach’s alpha and the ICC will be used to test the reliability of the instrument.

The sampling frame will consist of students from public schools in the city of Bastak. The quantitative study will include a total of nine classes: three classes from grade 7, three classes from grade 8, and three classes from grade 9.

Intervention and control groups

One public school in the city of Bastak will be selected to receive the intervention, and another public school in the same city will be designated as the control group. In each school, all students in the nine selected classes (three classes from each grade level) will be invited to participate in the study.

The allocation of intervention and control groups will be conducted at the school level (rather than at the class level) to minimize the potential for contamination. The selection of schools and classes will be carried out using a random sampling method.

Recruitment {15}

The first author will arrange meetings with the education officials, principals, and vice principals of the selected school to explain the study procedures and seek their cooperation in implementing the program. The first phase of the qualitative interviews will begin in May 2025, the intervention will be implemented in September 2025, and the study will continue until May 2026.

Assignment of interventions: allocation

Sequence generation {16a}

A statistician will randomly select two schools from among the eligible girls’ junior high schools that will not be classified as health-promoting. The selected schools will then be randomly assigned, using a computer-generated randomization sequence, to either the intervention or control group. All eligible students in the selected classes of each school will be invited to participate.

Concealment mechanism {16b}

The allocation sequence will be concealed from both the investigators and the participants until group assignments are finalized. The allocation sequence will be generated and securely kept by an independent statistician who will not be involved in recruitment or data collection.

Implementation {16c}

After obtaining signed informed consent forms from participants and their guardians, the first author will enroll eligible students according to the predetermined allocation sequence and will assign them to the respective intervention or control group.

Assignment of interventions: blinding

Who will be blinded {17a}

The researchers, participants, and statisticians will not be blinded in this study.

Procedure for unblinding if needed {17b}

This study will be conducted as an open-label study, so blinding will not be possible.

Data collection and management

Plans for assessment and collection of outcomes {18a}

Qualitative phase

To identify the factors that will affect the development of health-promoting schools and healthy eating behaviors, in-depth individual and semi-structured interviews will be conducted with health-promoting school staff in Bastak city. The data collection will be from a selected school on the basis of the health-promoting school checklist, as well as in-depth individual and semi-structured interviews and group discussions with school students, parents, and staff regarding healthy eating behaviors. After ethical approval and written informed consent have been obtained from all participants, the interviews will be conducted by the first author. Notably, in this study, due to the importance of preserving the participants’ confidentiality of information, participation will be anonymous and numerically coded. Before the interviews, the participants will be assured that they are familiar with the topic and have relevant experience. The questions will be stated according to the topics under investigation on the basis of the interview guide. The questions will be exploratory in type. Additionally, exploratory and clarifying questions will be used when necessary. The latter will provide the researcher with the opportunity to guide the interviews and focus on points of ambiguity. The interviews will be conducted somewhere the participants feel comfortable and will last approximately 40–60 min. Findings and implications will be shared with a number of participants to provide feedback. Two experts outside the study will be asked to review the data.

In this study, the Lincoln and Guba criteria will be used to ensure the trustworthiness of the data [27].The researcher will attempt to ensure the credibility of findings by reviewing the notes and extracted codes, sending the data to peers and using their debriefing, persistent checks, and prolonged data collection. Additionally, to increase the internal validity of findings, participants will review the findings and comment on them (member checks). In persistent observation, the researchers will simultaneously collect and analyze the data. The interview process will be prolonged enough. To increase the transferability of findings, a sample with maximum diversity will be selected. Additionally, all the research procedures and characteristics of the population of the study will be described in detail by the researchers so that other researchers can follow the research path.

Questionnaire and intervention design

In this study, a researcher-made SCT questionnaire on the nutritional behavior of adolescents aged 6–18 years and a checklist of health-promoting schools will be used.

The research team will categorize the results of the qualitative phase on the basis of the SCT constructs. Then, on the basis of the resulting categories and subcategories, a questionnaire will be designed to promote students’ healthy eating behaviors. All data will be collected by the principal investigator.

This questionnaire will have two parts. The first will involve demographic variables, such as age, educational level, family size, parental education, and parental occupation. The second part will be based on SCT constructs: self-efficacy, collective efficacy, outcome expectations, knowledge, observational learning, normative beliefs, social support, barriers and opportunities, behavioral skills, intentions, reinforcement, and punishment.

Adolescent eating behavior questionnaire

This questionnaire, which will inquire about students’ nutritional behaviors, will be derived from the Ministry of Health standard questionnaires for screening adolescent nutritional behavior and eating frequency and will have 14 items. This questionnaire will be based on the particular features of Bastak city, including people’s food preferences [28, 29].

The researcher-made questionnaire will be evaluated and validated in terms of qualitative and quantitative content validity, face validity, and construct validity through confirmatory factor analysis (CFA). Reliability will be assessed by measuring both internal and external reliability. All data will be collected by the principal investigator.

Health promotion school checklist

This checklist will consist of two parts. Checklist “A” will assess the general characteristics of the school, including the school name, the code assigned to the school by the national program officer, the address and telephone number, the region (urban, rural), the level of education, the type of school, the number of students by grade, and the number of people participating in the school health promotion program (parents, caregivers, students, teachers, and other staff, members of local councils).

Checklist “B” will rate the health-promoting school indicators, including the following: (1) comprehensive health education program, (2) provision of clinical services at school, (3) environmental health at school, (4) improving nutrition at school, (5) physical activity at school, (6) promoting the health of school staff, (7) mental health and counseling services at school, and (8) parent and community participation in school health programs and student health volunteer networks. All data will be collected by the principal investigator.

Plans to promote participant retention and complete follow-up {18b}

Researchers will be in touch with the school principal, deputy principal, and students’ parents via text messages and phone calls.

Data management {19}

Qualitative stage

Names or surnames will not be recorded in the interviews (in the qualitative phase) to maintain the confidentiality of the participants’ information. The interview recordings will be securely stored until the transcription process is completed and the accuracy of the transcripts is verified. After that, the recordings will be deleted to ensure participant confidentiality, in accordance with our institutional ethical guidelines. Regarding coding, two independent researchers will be involved in coding the transcripts to enhance the reliability and validity of the qualitative analysis.

The interview transcripts will be stored on a password-protected computer and will be accessible only to the first and corresponding authors. After obtaining permission from the participants, all interviews will be audio-recorded using a voice recorder and transcribed verbatim. Data will be analyzed using researcher-guided content analysis.

Two independent researchers will review and code the transcripts using an inductive approach to identify initial codes and potential themes. The codes will be discussed between the coders and then organized and categorized based on their similarities and meanings. Any discrepancies will be resolved through consensus. Data management will be performed in MAXQDA 10.

The process of data collection and analysis will continue until data saturation is reached, meaning that no new codes or themes emerge. To ensure the trustworthiness of findings, member checking with participants and peer debriefing with other researchers will be conducted.

The training sessions will be recorded with an encrypted voice recorder, and the files will be transferred to a secure system. All identifiable information, such as names or locations, is removed. The audio files will be stored in a separate password-protected folder and will only be accessible to the first author.

Statistical methods

Statistical methods for primary and secondary outcomes {20a}

The data analysis will be performed in SPSS 26. Descriptive statistics include the mean, standard deviation, and minimum and maximum to describe the interval variables of interest. Frequencies and percentages will be used to measure the non-interval variables. In this phase, independent-samples t-tests, paired-samples t-tests, and analysis of covariance will be run after the assumptions of each test are checked, and if the assumptions are not met, equivalent nonparametric tests, such as the Mann‒Whitney U-test, Wilcoxon test, and Kruskal‒Wallis test, will be used. In this phase, effect size indicators such as Cohen’s d and eta-square will also be reported.

In this exploratory mixed-methods study, qualitative data will first be collected and analyzed using content analysis to identify themes and categories grounded in the social cognitive theory (SCT). The findings from the qualitative phase will then be used as the basis for developing the quantitative instrument, effectively transforming qualitative results into quantifiable variables and questionnaire items.

For quantitative analysis, statistical methods such as descriptive statistics, reliability analysis (e.g., Cronbach’s alpha), and inferential tests (such as t-tests, ANOVA, or regression analysis) will be used as appropriate to evaluate outcomes.

Finally, the results from both qualitative and quantitative phases will be integrated during the interpretation phase. The integrated analysis will provide a comprehensive understanding of factors influencing healthy eating behaviors among adolescents, supporting both the development and evaluation of the SCT-based intervention.

Integration and triangulation strategies

This study will be conducted using an exploratory mixed-methods design grounded in the theoretical framework of SCT, with the aim of promoting healthy eating behaviors among students.

In the qualitative phase, experiences, barriers, and facilitators of healthy eating from the students’ perspective will be explored, with particular focus on key constructs of SCT, such as self-efficacy, outcome expectations, observational learning, and social support. The findings of this phase will form the basis for designing and culturally adapting the quantitative instrument, ensuring that the constructs and variables related to SCT are accurately incorporated into the questionnaire.

Following instrument development based on the qualitative results and SCT, the quantitative phase will be conducted to assess the prevalence and predictors of healthy eating behaviors. Findings from both phases will be integrated during tool development and results interpretation—whereby qualitative findings will guide the formulation of SCT-based constructs and sub-constructs and will contribute to the deeper interpretation of quantitative data.

To achieve a more comprehensive integration of data, a joint display table will be created to compare and analyze the themes and results of both phases, with special emphasis on theoretical constructs. In cases where discrepancies are identified, qualitative findings will be used to provide the necessary explanations. The entire integration process will be carefully documented to produce a comprehensive and theoretically grounded understanding of promoting healthy eating behaviors based on SCT.

Discussion

Behavioral, environmental, occupational, and metabolic risk factors, including high cholesterol, hypertension, obesity, tobacco smoking, and alcohol consumption, are responsible for half of all deaths globally. Because the determinants of noncommunicable diseases emerge early in life, health promotion and primary prevention activities should begin in childhood and adolescence [30]. From this perspective, schools serve as a critical and influential environment and are recognized as key settings for promoting health and preventing illness for multiple reasons [4]. The present study will employ the social cognitive theory of health and the social ecological model of health as the theoretical framework. The health-promoting school approach considers students’ health in a comprehensive and multidimensional manner taking into account all key aspects of health (physical, mental, social, and environmental). Because the geographical, cultural, economic, and social conditions of each region are different, health promotion programs in schools should be tailored to the specific conditions and needs of each region. For example, a program that works well in one school may not be as effective in another school due to differences in school climate, leadership and community involvement, and even different geographical and cultural circumstances. This requires flexibility in program design and implementation. This approach ensures that programs respond effectively to the distinctive challenges and opportunities within each educational setting [31]. The results of a study conducted in India revealed that the use of multicomponent, multilevel interventions in schools, including capacity building, technical visits, supportive supervision, the sensitization of policymakers and key stakeholders, the implementation of policy plans, the use of social media, technical support, and monitoring, resulted in an increase in schools at the gold level from 1 to 71 schools and schools at the silver level from 9 to 57 schools after the intervention[32]. A study was conducted in Iran to investigate the effect of a health promotion school program on the social health behavior of female elementary school students. In terms of the social health behavior components of environmental health, clinical services, disease detection, health education, and school safety, there was a significant difference at the 1% level between health promotion schools and regular schools. With respect to the components of parental participation, group behavior, establishing interpersonal relationships, and student participation, there was no significant difference between health promotion schools and regular schools [26].

Owing to the culture and customs of Hormozgan Province (a local diet high in sugar and fat), residents are at risk of noncommunicable diseases. Given the importance of the role of health-promoting schools in shaping and maintaining student behavior in this region and the effectiveness of educational and health promotion interventions in research, it seems necessary to implement interventions accompanied by health promotion programs based on models. A proper understanding of the factors affecting the formation of health-promoting schools and healthy eating behavior can facilitate planning and policymaking at different levels of the health sector.
