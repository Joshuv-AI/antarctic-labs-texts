Incidence of gestational diabetes and inequalities in adverse pregnancy outcomes in England: contemporary, cross sectional observational study of 2.7 million births
Authors: Carlos Sánchez-Soriano, Niamh-Maire McLennan, Sara L White, Nithya Sukumar, Ponnusamy Saravanan, Robert S Lindsay, Thomas Bolton, Fionna Chalmers, Laura A Magee, Rebecca M Reynolds, Genevieve Cezard
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo quantify the incidence of gestational diabetes mellitus in England, determine inequalities in diagnoses and pregnancy outcomes, and assess the indirect effects of the covid-19 pandemic.DesignContemporary, cross sectional observational study.SettingPrimary and secondary care data from NHS England, based on the Hospital Episode Statistics Admitted Patient Care dataset, 1 January 2018 to 31 December 2022.Participants2 314 365 women, aged 16-50 years, who gave birth to 2 758 170 babies in 184 hospitals.Main outcome measuresRates of gestational diabetes, infants born small for gestational age or large for gestational age, and emergency caesarean births, summarised yearly and monthly, based on socioeconomic deprivation and ethnic group.ResultsDiagnoses of gestational diabetes increased from about 8% in 2018 to >12% in 2022. The increase was greatest for individuals from non-white ethnic groups (23% for Asian women) or those living in deprivation (14%). Mothers from these backgrounds were also at greater risk of adverse pregnancy outcomes, such as emergency caesarean birth (odds ratio for black mothers 1.36, 95% confidence interval 1.34 to 1.38), preterm birth (odds ratio for deprived mothers 1.29, 1.27 to 1.31), or having an infant small for gestational age (odds ratio for Asian mothers 2.42, 2.39 to 2.45). Also, a diagnosis of gestational diabetes for these women was associated with an increase in the odds of preterm birth. Changes to screening methodology for gestational diabetes during the covid-19 pandemic had no significant effect on the number of diagnoses.ConclusionsThe study found that one in eight mothers in England had a diagnosis of gestational diabetes, and that profound health inequalities exist in their outcomes. Strategies are urgently needed to provide and improve care for this high risk group, given the implications of gestational diabetes on the short and long term health outcomes for mothers and babies.

Introduction

Gestational diabetes mellitus is the most common pregnancy complication, estimated to affect 14% of pregnancies globally.1 Gestational diabetes is associated with an increased risk of pregnancy complications, such as infants born large for gestational age or preterm, and interventional birth,23 as well as long term cardiometabolic disorders for mothers and offspring.45 The relation between gestational diabetes and adverse outcomes is complex, with numerous confounders that are associated with both, such as older maternal age, obesity, socioeconomic deprivation, and minority ethnic group.6–8

Estimates of the incidence of gestational diabetes in the UK have been determined largely through clinical audit.7 These incidences ranged from 1% to 25% and highlight wide regional, temporal, and seasonal variations in incidence. More recently, estimates have ranged from about 7% to 15%,9–12 but were generally single centre local approaches. These findings emphasise the value of large scale studies to provide accurate nationwide estimates, because a better understanding of the gestational diabetes case load is needed for planning maternity services, which are already highly challenged.13 Demographic analyses are particularly relevant for gestational diabetes, given the known outcome inequalities in pregnancies linked to obesity, socioeconomic deprivation, and non-white ethnic groups.14

Changes to the diagnostic criteria for gestational diabetes and testing methodology directly affect the incidence of the disease.15 Recommendations introduced during the covid-19 pandemic to reduce face-to-face contact by avoiding the oral glucose tolerance test and using instead levels of haemoglobin A1c and glucose16 were reported to influence the incidence of gestational diabetes.1011 We found no effect of these recommendations on pregnancy outcomes in women with gestational diabetes based on data from nine health boards and trusts in England and Scotland,12 but larger studies with greater geographical representation are needed, given reports of inequalities during the pandemic in the overall maternity population.1718

We present the findings of a cross sectional observational study of 2 758 170 births in England, from 2018 to the end of 2022, based on routinely collected electronic healthcare records obtained from NHS hospitals. Our aim was to quantify the incidence of gestational diabetes and determine its relation to maternal personal characteristics and guidelines for screening and diagnosis, such as those established during the covid-19 pandemic. We hypothesised that pregnancy outcomes would be worse for women with gestational diabetes from socioeconomically deprived backgrounds or from non-white ethnic groups.

Methods

Study design and data sources

We identified births in England, 1 January 2018-31 December 2022, from routinely collected healthcare data made available for research, with the ability to link birth records to longitudinal health details. All data curation and analyses were performed in R Studio Server (R version 4.1.3).19 The analysis plan, as well as the dataset development pipeline, phenotyping, and analysis code are available on our GitHub repository (https://github.com/BHFDSC/CCU064_01). Data were accessed within the NHS England Secure Data Environment through the British Heart Foundation Data Science Centre's CVD-COVID-UK/COVID-IMPACT Consortium. Online supplemental figure 1 has a schematic description of the data manipulation and dataset development process.

Birth definition

The Hospital Episode Statistics Admitted Patient Care dataset,20 with information on admissions to NHS hospitals in England, was used to identify birth events through ICD-10 (international classification of diseases, 10th revision) and OPCS-4 (Office of Population Censuses and Surveys Classification of Interventions and Procedures, version 4) codes (online supplemental table 1). Only women aged 16-50 years and admitted to hospital from 1 January 2018 to 31 December 2022 were included. Data for sex were taken from information in the Hospital Episode Statistics Admitted Patient Care dataset rather than from patient reported gender. Patient details for births or loss episodes were linked to the maternity tail of Hospital Episode Statistics (Hospital Episode Statistics MAT), with additional information on pregnancy and birth. To merge multiple or duplicate records referencing one birth event and identify subsequent births, we developed a clustering pipeline assessing the time difference between consecutive maternity episodes for each individual. Admissions within the same week were combined as the same birth, and admissions more than six months apart were considered different births; any episodes occurring between seven days and six months apart were flagged for exclusion. These thresholds were determined by assessing the distribution of consecutive episodes for each individual. Birth date was calculated with the antenatal and postnatal days of stay variables, or with the episode start date when these were not available (for 27.6% of episodes).

Definition of diabetes

Diagnoses of diabetes were identified by ICD-10 codes based on the Hospital Episode Statistics Admitted Patient Care dataset (online supplemental table 1) for records retrieved from 1 January 1997. The concatenated diagnoses variable was used. A diagnosis of gestational diabetes mellitus was assigned only when other types of diabetes had not been diagnosed previously. Conflicting diagnoses of type 1 and type 2 diabetes for the same individual were resolved by selecting the type amounting to >90% of the total number of diagnoses for that individual. Otherwise, a diagnosis of other diabetes was assigned.

Definition of additional covariates

The Index of Multiple Deprivation 201921 category was derived from the geographical Lower Super Output Areas 2011 categorisation, available from Hospital Episode Statistics. The Index of Multiple Deprivation is categorised as five equal groups (divided by quintiles), with high and low socioeconomic deprivation defined as belonging to group 1 and group 5, respectively.

Self-reported ethnic group was obtained from Hospital Episode Statistics, and supplemented when unavailable from the General Practice Extraction Service Data for Pandemic Planning and Research dataset (available from 2019), selecting the most recent record. Both sources followed the NHS England ethnic group categorisation, and were re-coded into six groups (Asian, black, mixed, other, white, and unavailable) based on the UK Office for National Statistics Census 2021 categorisation.22 An additional category was created combining all non-white backgrounds (ie, Asian, black, mixed, and other) into one group.

Birth weight, gestational age at birth (as complete weeks of gestation), and birth method were obtained from Hospital Episode Statistics MAT and, if unavailable, supplemented with observations from the Maternity Services Dataset Baby Demographics and Baby Activities tables. Recordings of birth weight were restricted to liveborn infants, removing birth weight for stillborn infants and fetal loss episodes <20 weeks’ gestation. The sex adjusted World Health Organization Fetal Growth charts23 were used to categorise offspring into small for gestational age or large for gestational age based on birth weight, completed weeks of gestation, and infant sex. Preterm birth was defined as birth between 20 and 37 weeks’ gestation.

Body mass index was obtained from the Maternity Services Dataset Pregnancy Care Activities and Findings and Observations tables, with SNOMED (Systematised Nomenclature of Medicine-Clinical Terms) codes for height, weight, and direct body mass index measurements (online supplemental table 1). The earliest measurement for each pregnancy was selected, removing observations made <20 weeks before the birth date to exclude gestational weight gain. Online supplemental table 2 shows the yearly percentage of complete records for each covariate.

Statistical analyses

Descriptive summaries

In accordance with NHS England data exporting guidelines, all counts presented have been rounded to the nearest five. To more accurately investigate birth outcomes, we created a subset of the dataset that included only singleton births, determined by the number of fetus and birth order variables available in Hospital Episode Statistics and Maternity Services Dataset. Birth date (ie, date of delivery) was used to summarise the study population by year or month. The term gestational diabetes births is used to refer to births from women with a recorded diagnosis of gestational diabetes. The number and percentage of these were summarised yearly, by deprivation level and ethnic group.

Comparative analyses by screening changes during the covid-19 pandemic

The guidelines from the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) for the diagnosis and management of gestational diabetes24 were modified during the covid-19 pandemic according to the recommendations of the Royal College of Obstetricians and Gynaecologists published in March 2020.16 The new guidelines recommended replacing when possible the in-person oral glucose tolerance test with levels of haemoglobin A1c and fasting or random glucose measurements from routine blood collection. Because screening for gestational diabetes is performed in early to mid pregnancy, the timeframes were corrected to capture births from women screened with these new guidelines. Based on a 40 week pregnancy, 14 October 2020 was estimated to be the earliest birth date for individuals screened for gestational diabetes from 1 April 2020, when the change to the screening recommendations of the Royal College of Obstetricians and Gynaecologists occurred. Therefore, 1 January 2018 to 13 October 2020 constituted the reference period (before the intervention, based on NICE guidelines), and 14 October 2020 to 31 December 2022 constituted the intervention period, based on the changes recommended by the Royal College of Obstetricians and Gynaecologists.

To assess the effect of this intervention on the incidence of gestational diabetes, the daily rates of total births, gestational diabetes births, and gestational diabetes percentages were compared separately through interrupted time series analyses adjusted by calendar month to account for autocorrelation from seasonality.1125 The seasonal effects were consistent and independent of the status of gestational diabetes or the pre-intervention or intervention period. Models included birth date, intervention, and the interaction term between both (termed slope change in the results) as covariates, with the formula: outcome~birth date + intervention + (birth date × intervention) + calendar month.

Regression analyses of obstetric outcomes

Within the subset of singleton births, we used logistic regression to investigate the association between key covariates and risk of emergency caesarean birth, preterm birth, small for gestational age, and large for gestational age. Emergency caesarean birth was modelled against spontaneous birth (ignoring elective caesarean births or other delivery methods). Two sets of models were built for each of the four outcomes, including socioeconomic deprivation or ethnic background separately, to account for their intercorrelation, as well as their interaction with a diagnosis of gestational diabetes. We refer to the odds ratios resulting from these analyses as odds ratio adjusted for Index of Multiple Deprivation, for models that included deprivation, and odds ratio adjusted for ethnic group, for models that included ethnic group. These models also included the intervention covariate representing the period of changes in screening for gestational diabetes during the covid-19 pandemic, termed intervention. Subsequent births represented 16.1% of all births in our dataset, but all observations were modelled as independent births to maximise sample size because information on parity was missing for more than a third of the records. Conducting preliminary sensitivity analyses with a subset including only the first recorded pregnancy for each individual, however, produced similar results (data not shown). For all models with preterm birth as the outcome variable, any observations from 1 October to 31 December 2022 were removed because of an apparent change in the recording of gestational age compared with the rest of the dataset, artificially increasing the frequency of births recorded as preterm.

Patient and public involvement

The study concept and outcome measures were informed by partnerships with patient representative bodies, including individuals with gestational diabetes, and healthcare providers, such as the James Lind Alliance Priority Setting Partnership and the Lothian Maternity Voices Partnership, who provided feedback on what should be prioritised in gestational diabetes mellitus research, the patient experience dealing with the management of gestational diabetes, and how to disseminate our findings to the public. A lay summary will be published on the Edinburgh Pregnancy Research Group website. Patients and lay members from the British Heart Foundation and Health Data Research UK also gave input into this project, reviewing the proposal, providing feedback, and supporting the development of the study and the manuscript.

Results

Yearly breakdown of maternity population

Table 1 summarises the study population by year of birth. The study included 2 314 365 unique mothers and a total of 2 758 170 births from hospitals in England from 1 January 2018 to 31 December 2022, with an average of 551 635 births per year and 45 970 births per month. The study covered 91.7% of the total number of live births and stillbirths in England for that period, as reported by the Office for National Statistics.25 Birth numbers decreased across the study period, with nearly 5000 fewer births per month in 2022 compared with 2018. The percentage of mothers aged ≥35 years increased over time, with an overall decrease in the percentage of mothers aged <25 years. The percentage of births from mothers of non-white ethnic groups also increased, and we found an improvement in the recording of ethnic group. In a subset of 2 727 945 singleton births (98.9% of the total number of births) for which pregnancy outcomes could be determined, we saw no clear trend for birth weight or gestational age at delivery, but the percentage of caesarean births steadily increased to >35% in 2022, corresponding to a reduction in the rate of spontaneous births.

We identified 274 500 pregnancies with a diagnosis of gestational diabetes mellitus, 9.9% of the total births. In contrast with the decrease in the number of total births, both the number and percentage of gestational diabetes births increased over the study period (figure 1). In 2018, 8.1% of pregnancies had a diagnosis of gestational diabetes, increasing to nearly 12% in 2022, a relative increase of 47.9% (95% confidence interval (CI) 45.5% to 50.2%). The percentage of births from women who had previously received a diagnosis of type 1 or type 2 diabetes also increased but to a lesser degree, with relative increases of 8.2% (95% CI 0.8% to 17.0%) for type 1 diabetes and 25.8% (19.1% to 34.4%) for type 2 diabetes. Both variables seemed to increase during the study period, but estimates were considered unreliable because of the limited number of measurements that could be retrieved, especially for the first half of the dataset. Availability of body mass index in the Maternity Services Dataset increased from <0.1% in 2018 to >62% in 2022, but was still considered insufficient for analysis of an association with gestational diabetes (online supplemental figure 2).

Trends in gestational diabetes mellitus by deprivation and ethnic group

The percentage and number of diagnoses of gestational diabetes increased for all deprivation and ethnic subgroups (table 2, and figures 3 and 4). Every year, gestational diabetes was most common in those in the high deprivation group and identifying as non-white compared with the low deprivation and white ethnic categories. The greatest absolute increase in gestational diabetes percentage (about 5%) was in individuals from black and Asian ethnic groups. Nonetheless, those identified as white individuals had the highest relative increase (49.8%, 95% CI 46.8% to 53.3%) for the same period, compared with individuals from non-white backgrounds, both separately and combined (31.9%, 28.6% to 35.2%).

Comparison of diagnostic trends after covid-19 related screening changes

Online supplemental table 4 shows the linear regression models exploring the trends in total births (model 1), gestational diabetes births (model 2), and gestational diabetes birth percentages (model 3) after the change in screening approach because of the covid-19 pandemic. The intervention variable represents the immediate effect of the change in diagnostic criteria for gestational diabetes corrected to capture birth date, whereas the slope change variable represents its effect over time. Although the models identified a slight and significant immediate increase in the number and percentage of gestational diabetes births after the intervention (P<0.001 for both), we found no significant differences in the slopes between the two periods for daily gestational diabetes births (P=0.67) or gestational diabetes birth percentages (P=0.20). Investigation of obstetric outcomes

Tables 3 and 4 show the results of the logistic regression analyses of emergency caesarean birth, preterm birth, small for gestational age, and large for gestational age. After adjusting for socioeconomic deprivation or ethnic group, a diagnosis of gestational diabetes was significantly associated with an increased risk of emergency caesarean birth (odds ratio adjusted for Index of Multiple Deprivation 1.32, 95% CI 1.30 to 1.34; odds ratio adjusted for ethnic group 1.32, 1.30 to 1.34), preterm birth (odds ratio adjusted for Index of Multiple Deprivation 1.35, 1.32 to 1.37; odds ratio adjusted for ethnic group 1.25, 1.22 to 1.27), and having an infant born large for gestational age (odds ratio adjusted for Index of Multiple Deprivation 1.80, 1.77 to 1.83; odds ratio adjusted for ethnic group 2.16, 2.13 to 2.19). With mothers aged 25-34 years as the reference, maternal age ≥35 years was also associated with an increased risk of emergency caesarean birth (odds ratio adjusted for Index of Multiple Deprivation 1.14, 1.13 to 1.15; odds ratio adjusted for ethnic group 1.13, 1.12 to 1.14) and preterm birth (odds ratio adjusted for Index of Multiple Deprivation 1.15, 1.14 to 1.17; odds ratio adjusted for ethnic group 1.12, 1.11 to 1.14) (tables 3 and 4).

Conversely, and with the same reference, maternal age <25 years was associated with a decreased risk of emergency caesarean birth (odds ratio adjusted for Index of Multiple Deprivation 0.84, 95% CI 0.83 to 0.85; odds ratio adjusted for ethnic group 0.86, 0.85 to 0.86) and delivering an infant large for gestational age (odds ratio adjusted for Index of Multiple Deprivation 0.80, 0.78 to 0.81; odds ratio adjusted for ethnic group 0.74, 0.73 to 0.75), but an increased risk of preterm delivery (odds ratio adjusted for Index of Multiple Deprivation 1.13, 1.11 to 1.15; odds ratio adjusted for ethnic group 1.20, 1.19 to 1.22) and having an infant small for gestational age (odds ratio adjusted for Index of Multiple Deprivation 1.21, 1.19 to 1.22; odds ratio adjusted for ethnic group 1.36, 1.34 to 1.38) (tables 3 and 4).

Individuals living in deprived areas had an increased risk of delivering a preterm infant (odds ratio 1.29, 95% CI 1.27 to 1.31) or an infant small for gestational age (1.28, 1.26 to 1.29). Mothers living in deprivation with a diagnosis of gestational diabetes had 21.8% increased odds of preterm birth (1.57, 1.47 to 1.68) and 32.8% lower odds of having an infant small for gestational age (0.86, 0.80 to 0.91), compared to mothers living in deprivation without a diagnosis of gestational diabetes (women in average deprivation and with no diabetes was the overall model baseline). We found no significant association between high deprivation and emergency caesarean birth. Living in a low deprivation setting was associated with a lower risk of emergency caesarean birth (0.95, 0.94 to 0.96), preterm birth (0.83, 0.82 to 0.85), and having an infant small for gestational age (0.79, 0.78 to 0.80), but with an increased risk of having an infant large for gestational age (1.12, 1.11 to 1.14) (table 3).

Individuals from non-white ethnic groups also had higher rates of emergency caesarean and preterm births, and having an infant small for gestational age, but a decreased risk of an infant large for gestational age. Asian women had about 32% higher odds of emergency caesarean birth (odds ratio 1.32, 95% CI 1.30 to 1.33), 11% higher odds of preterm birth (1.11, 1.09 to 1.13), and nearly 2.5 times the odds of having an infant small for gestational age (2.42, 2.39 to 2.45) compared to white women. Asian women with a diagnosis of gestational diabetes had 14.2% higher odds of emergency caesarean birth (1.51, 1.43 to 1.59), 38.2% higher odds of a preterm birth (1.53, 1.42 to 1.66), and 23.8% lower odds of having an infant small for gestational age (1.84, 1.72 to 1.98), compared to Asian women without a diagnosis of gestational diabetes (white women without diabetes was the overall model baseline). Black women had 36% higher odds of emergency caesarean birth (1.36, 1.34 to 1.38), 17% higher odds of preterm birth (1.17, 1.14 to 1.20), and 70% higher odds of having an infant small for gestational age (1.70, 1.66 to 1.73), compared to white women. Black women who had a diagnosis of gestational diabetes had 35.5% higher odds of preterm birth (1.58, 1.41 to 1.76) and 37.4% lower odds of offspring small for gestational age (1.06, 0.95 to 1.18) compared to black women without gestational diabetes (white individuals without a diagnosis was the overall model baseline) (table 4).

The intervention variable represents the time corrected immediate effect of the change in screening recommendations for gestational diabetes. Both sets of models identified significant immediate decreases in the risk of preterm birth (odds ratio adjusted for Index of Multiple Deprivation 0.77, 95% CI 0.73 to 0.82; odds ratio adjusted for ethnic group 0.76, 0.72 to 0.81) and having an infant small for gestational age (odds ratio adjusted for Index of Multiple Deprivation 0.85, 0.81 to 0.88; odds ratio adjusted for ethnic group 0.85, 0.81 to 0.89) after the intervention, and increases in the risk of having an infant large for gestational age (odds ratio adjusted for Index of Multiple Deprivation 1.13, 1.08 to 1.19; odds ratio adjusted for ethnic group 1.12, 1.07 to 1.18). Women giving birth during the intervention period (compared with the period before the intervention) had 23% lower odds of delivering a preterm infant and 15% lower odds of having an infant small for gestational age, but about 13% increased odds of delivering an infant large for gestational age. The over time change in the odds after the intervention, calculated through the interaction term, consistently showed odds ratios close to one, and nearly identical to the odds ratio from the birth date covariate. Despite being significant, the interaction terms highlight a small effect of the intervention over the overall monthly change in the odds represented by the birth date covariate, which itself was minor.

Discussion

Principal findings

We have developed the largest UK contemporary cross sectional birth study dataset of nearly 2.76 million births from 2018 to 2022, derived from routinely collected data from 184 healthcare providers in England. The percentage of women with a diagnosis of gestational diabetes mellitus increased during the study period, from about 7.5% in January 2018 to >12% five years later. Our estimate of 11.1% for 2021 is higher than the European unstandardised estimate (7.8%) from the International Diabetes Federation,1 but comparisons should be made with caution because of the lack of internationally agreed screening and diagnostic standards. Nonetheless, although global estimates for gestational diabetes are variable, other international studies have also reported local increases in the incidence of gestational diabetes over time.26–29 This continued rise in diagnoses is likely related to the general increase in average maternal age and obesity, as well as to general improvements in data provisioning and the ability to capture wider sectors of the population,30 rather than changes in clinical practice guidelines, such as during the covid-19 pandemic.

In our study, the recent increase in incidence of gestational diabetes was greater for individuals from deprived socioeconomic groups and non-white ethnic backgrounds, who were also at higher risk of adverse obstetric outcomes, highlighting that the inequalities were exacerbated by social and economic factors.6–8 In 2022, two in every 21 white mothers received a diagnosis of gestational diabetes; for black mothers, the rate was two in every 13, and for Asian mothers, two in every nine.

We found an increase in the number and percentage of caesarean births corresponding to a reduction in the rate of spontaneous births, a trend seen since 2010.31 In 2022, two in every six mothers gave birth by caesarean section, one being an emergency caesarean section. We identified strong associations between a diagnosis of gestational diabetes and adverse obstetric outcomes, with an increased risk of infants being born large for gestational age, preterm, and by emergency caesarean. Although these individuals were most likely attending clinics for glycaemic control management, gestational diabetes still had detrimental consequences for their pregnancy.3

We found associations between the non-white ethnic group and an increased risk of emergency caesarean, preterm birth, and having an infant small for gestational age, confirming the association between non-white ethnic group and adverse pregnancy outcomes.3233 Also, non-white women with a diagnosis of gestational diabetes had increased odds of emergency caesarean and preterm birth compared to non-white mothers without diabetes. For example, Asian mothers with gestational diabetes had an increase in the odds of preterm birth of 38% compared with Asian mothers without diabetes.

Similar associations were found between high socioeconomic deprivation and increased risk of delivering a preterm infant or an infant small for gestational age. Women with gestational diabetes living in deprived areas had 22% higher odds of delivering preterm than women without diabetes living in deprivation. Conversely, non-white or deprived women who had a diagnosis of gestational diabetes had a reduced risk of having an infant small for gestational age because of the association between maternal diabetes and increased fetal growth.34 Our analyses highlight the independent contribution of gestational diabetes over adverse obstetric outcomes, worsening the risk of emergency caesarean and preterm birth for the more socially or economically vulnerable women.8

The covid-19 pandemic introduced severe pressures on the national healthcare system, and pragmatic approaches to the diagnosis and management of gestational diabetes were established to protect patients and clinicians.1635 According to our analyses, diagnoses of gestational diabetes during the pandemic followed the growing trend established before the change in screening and diagnostic approach. We identified an overall increased risk of infants born large for gestational age and a reduced risk of preterm birth and infants born small for gestational age for women giving birth during the intervention period when the covid-19 modifications to screening and diagnosis of gestational diabetes were incorporated. The association between the intervention and increased risk of infants born large for gestational age may be a result of reduced maternal exercise during the pandemic, leading to increased weight gain.11 In contrast, the over time change in risk of these adverse outcomes during the intervention period was considered minimal and likely clinically negligible. These results should be interpreted with caution because the intervention point was time corrected to capture births from women screened according to the guidelines of the Royal College of Obstetricians and Gynaecologists. In conjunction with the rapidly changing and exceptional circumstances during the pandemic, we acknowledge the existence of factors not considered in this study that directly influence gestational diabetes and obstetric outcomes, such as maternal physical activity, mental wellbeing, or even disruptions to healthcare access.113637 Our study also assumed that obstetric units were compliant with the change in recommendations, but estimates are lacking.38 Although we found significant effects of the intervention, these effects may have been a result of the large sample size available and may not be clinically relevant.

Strengths and limitations of this study

The key strength of our study was the sample size. With our study design, we produced accurate yearly and monthly estimates of the incidence of gestational diabetes in England. Despite covering more than 90% of the births captured by the Office for National Statistics, however, our values might be slightly underestimated when considering the whole maternity population in England, because diagnoses of gestational diabetes were sourced from hospital records and only hospital births were included. Other limitations include poor data provisioning for some variables, which prevented us from adjusting the regression analyses for maternal body mass index, birth order, or smoking habits,739 which may result in unaccounted residual confounding. Online supplemental figure 3 shows a directed acyclic graph of gestational diabetes and birth outcomes. The datasets available are structured for the collection of detailed clinical information, but many fields were incomplete and thus unusable. Also, the Maternity Services Dataset and General Practice Extraction Service Data for Pandemic Planning and Research dataset were not available for the whole of 2018, and changes in how gestational age and stillbirths were recorded in Hospital Episode Statistics and Maternity Services Dataset during the last months of the study period limited their use in the analyses.

Study implications

Future efforts should focus on better understanding and improving maternity care in England. Currently, one in every eight mothers receive a diagnosis of gestational diabetes and profound health inequalities are deeply rooted. The availability of large scale information from integrated health data systems, such as those within NHS England providing access for multiple research initiatives, is necessary to inform clinical practice. The dataset that we developed represents a valuable resource to be expanded over time and used to investigate and describe the nationwide incidence of obstetric disorders and perinatal outcomes.

Conclusions

With the development of a contemporary birth study from routinely collected healthcare data, we have examined the maternity population in England and estimated the increase in the incidence of gestational diabetes, which reached 12% by the end of 2022, a relative increase of 60% in five years. This increase was greater in individuals from deprived socioeconomic groups and non-white ethnic groups, who also had a higher risk of adverse obstetric outcomes, and for whom a diagnosis of gestational diabetes may worsen their prospects. Provisioning and preservation of routine healthcare data are essential to understand maternity care over time.
