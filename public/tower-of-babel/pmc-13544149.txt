Identification of a Novel CDH2 Gene Variant in an ACOGS Patient with Concurrent Respiratory Tract Infection: A Case Report
Authors: Yuanyuan Lu, Feng Fang, Hua Zhou, Sainan Shu, Xinglou Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeAgenesis of corpus callosum, cardiac, ocular, and genital syndrome (ACOGS) is a rare disease that has only been described in recent years. To date, nineteen cases have been reported. ACOGS often leads to severe complications and is potentially fatal. However, its pathogenesis and clinical spectrum remain poorly understood by clinicians, and several genetic variants associated with the syndrome have yet to be fully characterized.Patients and MethodsWe present the case of a five-month-old infant exhibiting dysmorphic facial features, corneal leucoma, esotropia, nystagmus, agenesis of the corpus callosum, and tricuspid regurgitation. The child was admitted to our hospital with a respiratory tract infection, and eventually diagnosed with ACOGS. Whole exome sequencing identified a novel de novo heterozygous variant in the cadherin 2 (CDH2) gene (c.744G>T). During hospitalization, the patient developed multiple symptoms including respiratory distress, tachycardia, and arrhythmia.ResultsDespite intensive interventions—including invasive mechanical ventilation—his condition continued to deteriorate, and he ultimately succumbed to complications.ConclusionACOGS is a rare disease with complex clinical presentations and generally poor outcomes. Based on current limited evidence, it may be reasonable for clinicians to maintain a high index of suspicion and to consider early genetic testing. When ACOGS is suspected, whole exome sequencing could be considered as part of the diagnostic workup.

Introduction

Agenesis of corpus callosum, cardiac, ocular, and genital syndrome (ACOGS; OMIM #618929) is an autosomal dominant inherited disease caused by pathogenic variants in the CDH2 gene. It was first described by Accogli et al in 2019,1 who identified novel heterozygous pathogenic variants in the CDH2 gene in nine individuals. These patients exhibited a spectrum of clinical features, including global developmental delay, intellectual disability, facial abnormalities, callosal agenesis, and cardiac, ocular, and genital malformations.1 Based on these findings, the authors designated the condition as agenesis of corpus callosum, cardiac, ocular, and genital syndrome (ACOGS). The CDH2 protein is encoded by the CDH2 gene and consists of 906 amino acids.2 CDH2 is essential for neural development, and its dysfunction can lead to neurodevelopmental abnormalities such as agenesis of the corpus callosum, as seen in our patient.3

ACOGS is a rare syndromic developmental disorder. To date, only nineteen cases have been reported in the literature. Consequently, the current understanding of this condition remains limited, and further case reporting is essential to better characterize its clinical spectrum and underlying pathological mechanisms. Moreover, the reported cases have involved diverse mutations in the CDH2 gene. Although the clinical presentations share common features, they are not entirely identical. Some cases have presented with ureteropelvic junction obstruction, pectus excavatum, as well as neuropsychiatric symptoms such as autism spectrum disorder behaviors.1,4 Clear genotype–phenotype correlations are lacking, as the same CDH2 variant can lead to discordant phenotypes.5 Whether disease severity correlates with specific variants remains unknown. Prognosis is poorly defined, with insufficient data on survival and long-term quality of life. It is therefore plausible that additional variant sites—and their correlations with phenotypic expression—remain to be identified. In this report, we describe a five-month-old infant presenting with dysmorphic features, corneal leucoma, and agenesis of corpus callosum associated with a novel CDH2 variant. The relevant clinical manifestations and therapeutic interventions are detailed in the following sections.

Case Presentation

The patient was a five-month-old male infant with a congenital corneal abnormality that had remained untreated since birth. At three months of age, ophthalmologic evaluation at our hospital revealed corneal leucoma. He was then referred to a local hospital for further workup. Brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) showed agenesis of the corpus callosum (Figure 1), indicating cerebral dysplasia. Cardiac ultrasound confirmed congenital heart disease (patent foramen ovale and tricuspid regurgitation). At four months, he developed his first episode of bronchopneumonia, which resolved with treatment at the same local hospital. The patient was born full-term via uneventful vaginal delivery, with no notable family history of genetic disorders. According to the parents, his growth and developmental milestones had been within normal limits, though they were unable to recall specific measurements. Physical examination at admission indicated a weight of 10 kg (>97 percentile), and an occipitofrontal circumference of 43 cm (75~90 percentile). Facial features included sparse hair, flat occiput, ocular hypertelorism, esotropia, nystagmus, and bilateral corneal leucoma.

On admission, the patient had stable breathing despite pulmonary rales, and cardiac auscultation was normal. A series of laboratory tests were performed after admission (Table 1). The complete blood count showed normal white blood cells and neutrophils levels. Together with normal C-reactive protein (CRP) and procalcitonin (PCT) levels, these findings suggested a viral etiology. Biochemical testing indicated elevated alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST) levels at 48 U/L (reference: ≤41 U/L) and 149 U/L (reference: ≤40 U/L), respectively, consistent with impaired liver function. Lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) was also elevated at 409 U/L (reference: ≤300 U/L). Cardiac troponin I (cTnI) and brain natriuretic peptide (BNP) levels were within the normal range. Electrocardiogram (ECG) demonstrated sinus tachycardia with ST-segment and T-wave depression in leads V4-V6, indicating myocardial injury. Immunological assays showed reduced complement C3 and C4, measuring 0.45 g/L (reference: 0.65–1.39 g/L) and 0.09 g/L (reference: 0.16–0.38 g/L), respectively. Furthermore, immunoglobulin levels were as follows: IgG 7.4 g/L (reference: 2.0–6.9 g/L), IgA 0.28 g/L (reference: 0.05–0.57 g/L) and IgM 0.98 g/L (reference: 0.17–1.00 g/L). Urine cytomegalovirus (CMV) culture results were graded as – (negative), + (positive, low level), ++, or +++. This patient had a result of +. Because the infant was full-term and five months old, CMV was not determined to be the primary pathogen, and no specific treatment was given. Meanwhile, respiratory pathogen nucleic acid testing from a throat swab confirmed respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) infection. Chest CT revealed mild pulmonary infection (Figure 2). The patient was initially treated for RSV pneumonia with sputum aspiration, aerosol inhalation, and hydrocortisone. However, on the third day of hospitalization, his respiratory condition worsened, manifesting as mild tachypnea along with severe coughing and wheezing. Oxygen therapy via nasal cannula and gamma globulin were then administered to alleviate symptoms. By the fifth day, his status declined rapidly: he developed respiratory distress with aggravated cough, wheezing, and inspiratory retractions. His respiratory rate increased to 51 breaths per minute, accompanied by stridor and pulmonary crackles. Continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) was initiated immediately. Meanwhile, the infant developed tachycardia, with a heart rate rising to approximately 160 bpm. Repeat laboratory tests at this time showed a normal troponin I level (15.2 pg/mL; reference <26.2 pg/mL) but a markedly elevated BNP level of 883 pg/mL (reference <300 pg/mL), indicative of myocardial injury. To determine the cause of clinical deterioration, further investigations were undertaken, including (1,3)-β-D-glucan detection (G test), galactomannan detection (GM test), and sputum fungal culture. Despite a serum (1,3)-β-D-glucan level of 98.4 pg/mL (reference<70 pg/mL), antifungal therapy was ultimately initiated due to the infant’s young age, two recent episodes of wheezing-associated respiratory infections, and poor clinical response to appropriate RSV-directed treatment.

On the seventh day, respiratory failure progressed further. The infant exhibited tachypnea, inspiratory retractions, and diffuse stridor and crackles across both lung fields. Tachycardia and arrhythmia persisted, and both cTnI and BNP levels rose significantly to 178.9 pg/mL and 1,166 pg/mL, respectively. Invasive mechanical ventilation via endotracheal intubation was required. By the eighth day, both respiratory and cardiac function continued to deteriorate, with the heart rate surpassing 200 bpm. cTnI increased sharply to 833.2 pg/mL and BNP rose to 13,924 pg/mL (Figure 3). Therapeutic measures were intensified, including strict fluid restriction, aggressive diuresis, and administration of sildenafil and dopamine to address cardiopulmonary dysfunction. Given the patient’s rapid clinical deterioration, along with observed dysmorphic features, cardiac structural anomalies, and neurological abnormalities, a genetic disorder was strongly suspected. Whole exome sequencing was performed on the fifth hospital day using peripheral blood samples from the patient and both parents. A novel heterozygous variant in CDH2 was identified (NM_001792:c.744G>T; chr18:25585916; p.E248D), which was subsequently confirmed by Sanger gene sequencing (Figure 5). This variant was not documented in the ClinVar or the Human Gene Mutation Database (HGMD) databases. Segregation analysis confirmed that neither parent carried the variant, supporting its de novo origin. This missense variant results in the substitution of a highly conserved amino acid and was classified as likely pathogenic according to the American College of Medical Genetics (ACMG) guidelines (PS2+PM2+BP4). Discussion

ACOGS is an autosomal dominant disorder caused by pathogenic variants in the CDH2 gene. It was first delineated in 2019 by Accogli et al, who identified novel heterozygous CDH2 variants in nine individuals presenting with a syndromic neurodevelopmental phenotype, including global developmental delay, intellectual disability, facial abnormalities, callosal agenesis, cardiac, ocular, and genital malformations.1 The authors designated the condition ACOGS, reflecting its multisystem involvement. Subsequently, several case reports have further characterized this rare developmental disorder. In 2020, Reis et al described four patients with CDH2 variants, one of whom exhibited isolated ocular anomalies (Peters anomaly and glaucoma or elevated intraocular pressure), while the other three displayed a combination of ocular abnormalities, dysmorphic features, and agenesis of the corpus callosum.6 The first cases from China and Turkey were reported by Meng et al (2021) and Kanjee et al (2022), respectively.4,7 In 2024, a fetus at 19+1 weeks was diagnosed with ACOGS via amniocentesis.8 Following termination of the pregnancy, a postmortem examination confirmed agenesis of corpus callosum, a congenital diaphragmatic hernia, and micrognathia. Most recently, in 2025, Abdul Rahman et al reported an 8-month-old female with ACOGS and Scimitar syndrome (right pulmonary vein draining into an anomalous Scimitar vein that enters the inferior vena cava, with right lung hypoplasia and pulmonary sequestration), who died at 19 months.9 She had recurrent respiratory infections attributed to her cardiac condition, and despite multiple surgeries, she died from right heart failure and moderate pulmonary hypertension with Scimitar vein stenosis.9 In 2026, a novel case of presumed monozygotic twins carrying the same pathogenic CDH2 variant but displaying discordant phenotypes was reported.5 One twin presented with a broad spectrum of symptoms, whereas the other only had an isolated ventricular septal defect, pulmonary hypertension, and club feet.5

To date, nineteen individuals have been reported with ACOGS; our proband represents the twentieth documented case. Among previous reports, the majority of the variants (sixteen cases) have been de novo mutations. In one of these cases, the patient had inherited the variant from a parent. The remaining two patients lacked family history. The variants include fourteen missense variants, two frameshift variants, one splicing variant, and two nonsense variants. Consistent with the predominant pattern, our patient also carried a de novo missense variant in CDH2.

The most frequently manifestation in ACOGS is corpus callosum abnormality (17/20), followed by dysmorphic facial features (16/20), and ocular anomalies (15/20). Other frequent clinical findings include cardiac abnormality, intellectual disability, and genital malformations. Among the reported cases, males (n = 12) have been affected more frequently than females (n = 7). Consistent with this trend, our patient was a male infant who exhibited typical features of ACOGS, including ocular hypertelorism, esotropia, nystagmus, bilateral corneal leucoma, agenesis of corpus callosum, and tricuspid regurgitation. Additionally, he presented with sparse hair, platybasia, ECG abnormalities (sinus tachycardia, ST segment and T wave depression of V4-V6 leads), a patent foramen ovale, and an abnormal electroencephalogram (EEG) suggestive of a borderline cerebral electrical activity.

CDH2 is a classic member of the type I cadherin family and is generally expressed in neural, cardiac, mesenchymal, and endothelial cells.10,11 As a transmembrane glycoprotein, it is composed of an extracellular calcium dependent functional domain, transmembrane domain, and cytoplasmic protein binding domain.2 The extracellular functional domain is important for mediating cell adhesion, while the cytoplasmic domain binds to signaling molecules such as β-catenin and p120 to regulate cell proliferation, apoptosis, migration, and differentiation.12 As reported, CDH2 facilitates brain development by maintaining the normal architecture of neuroepithelial cells.13 It also participates in regulating the proliferation and fate commitment of neural precursor cells.14,15 Furthermore, CDH2 has been found to promote the neural differentiation of mouse-induced pluripotent stem cells.16 In zebrafish models, CDH2 has been found to coordinate with myosin-II, and the absence of CDH2 leads to defective internalization and neural plate morphogenesis.17

As noted in the clinical course, the patient developed arrhythmias on the fifth hospital day. CDH2 is known to be highly expressed in cardiomyocytes and has been implicated in arrhythmogenic right ventricular cardiomyopathy (ARVC).18,19 ARVC, traditionally associated with variants in desmosomal protein genes, is histologically characterized by fibrofatty replacement of the myocardium and clinically presents with severe arrhythmias and an increased risk of sudden cardiac death.20,21 Researchers have found that CDH2 can ensure structural integrity and functional synchronization in the heart.18 Therefore, we hypothesize that the identified CDH2 variant may be associated with the arrhythmias observed in our patient. Beyond the acute infectious insult, electrical instability potentially related to the CDH2 mutation may be associated with accelerated disease progression. In addition to cardiac involvement, the patient also showed signs that raised the possibility of immune dysfunction. More cases are needed to further expand our understanding of this condition.

Specifically, the patient experienced recurrent respiratory tract infections at four and five months of age, both requiring hospitalization. Beyond potential external factors such as inadequate care, it is possible that an underlying immune deficiency may be associated with his susceptibility. He was admitted to our hospital with a one-day history of cough, wheezing, and fever. Despite prompt and aggressive treatment, his clinical status declined rapidly. In addition to RSV, laboratory studies also suggested possible evidence of fungal infection. The presence of polymicrobial infections—viral and fungal—along with his rapid deterioration, raises the hypothesis that the CDH2 missense variant may be associated with compromised immune function. This hypothesis might be considered given the decreased C3 and C4 levels, though more evidence would be needed.

Several limitations should be noted. Autopsy was not permitted by the family, and functional studies of the CDH2 variant were not performed because of the patient’s critical condition and parental concerns. Moreover, the concurrent severe infection and genetic syndrome complicate the distinction between infection-related injury and genetic effects. Hence, all mechanistic interpretations are speculative and need further validation. Nonetheless, this detailed case may still contribute to expanding the clinical and genetic understanding of ACOGS.

Conclusion

We report a case of a pediatric patient carrying a novel de novo missense variant in CDH2 (c.744G>T). The variant was absent in both parents, confirming its de novo origin. The patient presented with a mixed respiratory tract infection and subsequently experienced a complex clinical course leading to death. Whether the infection was associated with disease progression remains unclear, but this case raises the possibility that concurrent infection may influence outcomes in ACOGS. Such an observation is preliminary and requires further validation. Currently, understanding of ACOGS pathogenesis is limited due to the scarcity of reported cases. Additional systematic documentation will be needed to clarify the natural history of this condition. Given that this is a single case, no definitive clinical recommendations can be made; however, when ACOGS is suspected, clinicians might consider early genetic testing and a multidisciplinary approach, acknowledging that the potential benefit is uncertain.
