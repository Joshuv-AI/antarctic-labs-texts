Abstract
. In this paper, we investigate the relationships between Harder-Narasimhan filtrations and derived Hall algebras. We extend several results from abelian categories to triangulated categories, including Reineke inversions, wall-crossing formulas, and Joyce’s elements ϵ γ \epsilon_{\gamma} . 1.
Introduction

In
[
9
]
, Reineke introduced the notion of HN filtration in representation categories of quivers, as an analog of a concept with the same name in the theory of moduli of vector bundles on curves. He established the relationship between stability functions and the Ringel-Hall algebras of representation categories.

Reineke’s result was rewritten and summarized by Bridgeland and Toledano Laredo in
[
3
]
as the right part of the following diagram:
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces(\epsilon)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces(\delta)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces(\kappa)}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\exp}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\log}
Reineke inversion
HN filtration

The precise definition of the symbols in the diagram will be introduced in Section 3. Roughly speaking,
(\kappa)
and
(\delta)
are characteristic functions, supported on all and semistable objects of a certain dimension vector, respectively.

In the left part of the diagram, the symbol
(\epsilon)
denotes the elements defined by Joyce in
[
5
]
. These are not characteristic functions in general, but they are supported on indecomposable elements. Thus, the diagram illustrates the relationships among all objects, semistable objects, and indecomposable objects.

Reineke also proved an identity
[
9
, Proposition 4.12]
, which is written as

1_{\mathcal{A}}=\prod^{\leftarrow}_{\phi\in(0,1]}\mathrm{SS}^{Z}_{\phi}

in the notation of Bridgeland and Toledano Laredo. Although it does not appear in the diagram, it plays a major role in the theory. We will call it the wall-crossing formula in this paper. It characterizes the existence and uniqueness of the Harder–Narasimhan filtration for a certain object in
\mathcal{A}
, while implying that the descending product on the right-hand side of the identity is an invariant under the change of stability functions.

Reineke’s work was generalized by Joyce in
[
6
]
and
[
7
]
, where Joyce raised the question
[
7
, Problem 7.1]
of introducing a parallel theory on triangulated categories. Following this question, Bridgeland
[
2
]
introduced the notion of stability conditions on triangulated categories, which can be defined on bounded derived categories of abelian categories. Regarding Hall algebras, Töen
[
12
]
constructed an associative algebra for each derived category, called the derived Hall algebra. This construction was later extended by Xiao and Xu
[
14
]
to triangulated categories satisfying certain conditions. They also provided a direct proof of the associativity of the derived Hall algebras.

With the preparation above, we are now ready to generalize Reineke’s work to triangulated categories satisfying both the conditions of Bridgeland and those of Xiao and Xu. The categories satisfying all the conditions do exist. For example, the bounded derived category of a
\mathrm{Hom}
-finite Krull-Schmidt abelian category satisfies all the conditions.

In such a category, Wang and Chen
[
13
]
investigated the HN filtrations using the language of derived Hall algebras. Their result is similar in form to that of Reineke. The present paper follows their approach and recovers the entirety of Reineke’s theory. We summarize all our results again in a diagram that shares the same structure as the one by Bridgeland and Toledano Laredo:

where the meaning of each symbol may differ slightly from that in abelian categories, and we will define and examine them thoroughly in Section 4. We also present the wall-crossing formula within the context of triangulated categories:

1_{\mathcal{D}}=\prod^{\leftarrow}_{\phi\in\mathbb{R}}\mathrm{SS}^{\sigma}_{\phi}.

As an application of our results, we determine all the stability conditions on the category
D^{b}(\mathrm{rep}A_{2})
, classified by their hearts and the phases of objects in those hearts. We list all

The result of this paper might be a partial answer to the question
[
7

raised by Joyce. However, providing a complete answer to this question remains a very challenging task.

Organization of the Paper

In Section 3, we revisit the summary by Bridgeland and Toledano Laredo on Reineke’s work in

Stability Functions on Abelian Categories

In this section, we briefly review some fundamental concepts and results in abelian categories. Our primary focus will be on the representation categories of quivers. For a more detailed exploration, we refer the reader to
[

]

2.1.
Quivers and Representations

First, we provide our conventions regarding quivers.

Definition 2.1
.

A
quiver
is a quadruple
Q=(Q_{0},Q_{1},s,t)
, where
Q_{0}
and
Q_{1}
are two finite sets, called the sets of
vertices
and
arrows
, respectively, and
s,t:Q_{1}\rightarrow Q_{0}
are two maps, known as the
source
and
target
maps, respectively.

Definition 2.2
.

A
path
of length
l
in a quiver
Q
is defined to be a sequence
a_{1},\dots,a_{l}
of arrows in
Q
, such that
t(a_{i})=s(a_{i+1})
for
1\leqslant i\leqslant l-1
. A path above is denoted by
a_{l}\dots a_{1}
. A path
a_{l}\dots a_{1}
is called a
cycle
if
s(a_{1})=t(a_{l})
. A quiver with no cycles is said to be
acyclic
.

Let
k
be a field, we denote the
path algebra
of
Q
over
k
by
kQ
. It is the
k
-algebras with basis the set of all paths in
Q
, as an
k
-vector space, with multiplication defined by connecting paths. We have the canonical equivalence between the category
\mathrm{rep}_{k}Q
of (finite dimensional) representations of
Q
over
k
and the category
\mathrm{mod}kQ
of (finite dimensional) left
kQ
-modules. It is well known that both categories are abelian
k
-categories. See
[
1
]
. We will always identify them and denote them by
\mathcal{A}
.

2.2.
Slope Functions

Let us fix an integral linear form
\Theta
on
\mathbb{Z}Q_{0}
, that is, a
\mathbb{Z}
-linear function

\Theta:\mathbb{Z}Q_{0}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}.

Then we obtain a function

\displaystyle\mu:\mathbb{N}Q_{0}\backslash\{0\}

\displaystyle\rightarrow\mathbb{Q}

\displaystyle d=(d_{i})_{i\in I}

\displaystyle\mapsto{\frac{\sum_{i\in I}\Theta(d_{i})}{\sum_{i\in I}d_{i}}},

called the
slope function
associated to
\Theta
. For
M\in\mathcal{A}
, we always write

\mu(M)=\mu(\underline{\dim}M).

Definition 2.3
.

Let
\mu
be as above. An object
0\neq M\in\mathcal{A}
is said to be
\mu
-
semistable
if
\mu(U)\leqslant\mu(M)
for any
0\neq U\subseteq M
.
M
is said to be
\mu
-
stable
if
\mu(U)<\mu(M)
for any
0\neq U\subsetneqq M
.

The following three lemmas are all easy and well known. We refer the reader to
[
9
]
.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
0\rightarrow L\rightarrow M\rightarrow N\rightarrow 0
be a short exact sequence in
\mathcal{A}
. Then

(1)

\mu(L)\leqslant\mu(M)\iff\mu(M)\leqslant\mu(N)\iff\mu(L)\leqslant\mu(N)
;

(2)

\min(\mu(L),\mu(N))\leqslant\mu(M)\leqslant\max(\mu(L),\mu(N))
;

(3)

if
\mu(L)=\mu(M)=\mu(N)
, then
M
is
\mu
-semistable if and only if
L
and
N
are both
\mu
-semistable.

Lemma 2.5
.

For
0\neq M\in\mathcal{A}
.

(1)

If
M
is
\mu
-stable, then
M
is indecomposable.

(2)

If
M
is simple, then
M
is
\mu
-stable.

Lemma 2.6
.

If
\mu(M)>\mu(N)
, then
\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathcal{A}}(M,N)=0
.

2.3.
Stability Functions

Now consider the group homomorphism

\displaystyle Z:K_{0}(\mathcal{A})=\mathbb{Z}Q_{0}

\displaystyle\rightarrow\mathbb{C}

\displaystyle d_{j}

\displaystyle\mapsto 1+\Theta(d_{j})i.

Again, we write
Z(M)
for
Z(\underline{\dim}M)
, then we have

Z(M)\in\mathbb{H}=\{e^{i\pi\phi}|\phi\in(0,1)\}

for any
0\neq M\in\mathcal{A}
. Now we can give the definition of stability functions.

Definition 2.7
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be an abelian category (no need to be a representation category), and
K_{0}(\mathcal{A})
be its Grothendieck group. A
stability function
on
\mathcal{A}
is a group homomorphism
Z:K_{0}(\mathcal{A})\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
, such that
Z(M)\in\mathbb{H}\cup\mathbb{R}_{-}
for
0\neq M\in\mathcal{A}
.

For each
0\neq M\in\mathcal{A}
there is a unique
\phi(M)\in(0,1]
such that
Z(M)=m_{M}e^{i\pi\phi(M)}
for some
m_{M}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
. We call
\phi(M)
the
phase
of
M
under
Z
.

Definition 2.8
.

Let
Z
be a stability function on
\mathcal{A}
. An object
0\neq M\in\mathcal{A}
is said to be
Z
-
semistable
if
\phi(U)\leqslant\phi(M)
for any
0\neq U\subseteq M
.
M
is said to be
Z
-
stable
if
\phi(U)<\phi(M)
for any
0\neq U\subsetneqq M
.

The notion of stability functions is a generalization of the notion of slope functions, and similarly we have the following three lemmas.

Lemma 2.9
.

Let
0\rightarrow L\rightarrow M\rightarrow N\rightarrow 0
be a short exact sequence in
\mathcal{A}
. Then

(1)

\phi(L)\leqslant\phi(M)\iff\phi(M)\leqslant\phi(N)\iff\phi(L)\leqslant\phi(N)
;

(2)

\min(\phi(L),\phi(N))\leqslant\phi(M)\leqslant\max(\phi(L),\phi(N))
;

(3)

if
\phi(L)=\phi(M)=\phi(N)
, then
M
is
Z
-semistable if and only if
L
and
N
are both
Z
-semistable.

Lemma 2.10
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be an abelian category and
0\neq M\in\mathcal{A}
.

(1)

If
M
is
Z
-stable, then
M
is indecomposable.

(2)

If
M
is simple, then
M
is
Z
-stable.

Lemma 2.11
.

If
\phi(M)>\phi(N)
, then
\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathcal{A}}(M,N)=0
.

Example 2.12
.

Let
\mathcal{A}=\mathrm{Coh}(\mathbb{P}^{1})
be the category of coherent sheaves over
\mathbb{P}^{1}
, which is an abelian category. The function

Z(E)=-\deg E+i\mathrm{rank}(E)

defines a stability function
Z
on
\mathcal{A}
. It is easy to see that
Z(K_{0}(\mathcal{A}))\not\subseteq\mathbb{H}
since
Z(k_{x})=-1
for any degree
1
point
x\in\mathbb{P}^{1}
.

2.4.
HN Filtrations

We now introduce the concept of HN filtrations.

Definition 2.13
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be an abelian category with a stability function
Z
. For
0\neq M\in\mathcal{A}
, we call the filtration

0=M_{0}\subseteq M_{1}\subseteq\dots\subseteq M_{s}=M,

such that
A_{i}:=M_{i}/M_{i-1}
is
Z
-semistable,
1\leqslant i\leqslant s
, with

\phi(A_{1})>\phi(A_{2})>\dots>\phi(A_{s})

the
Harder-Narisimhan filtration
or
HN filtration
for short. It is unique up to isomorphism. The tuple of dimension vectors
(\underline{\dim}A_{1},\dots,\underline{\dim}A_{s})
is called the
HN type
of
M
.

The stability function
Z
is said to
have the HN property
if any
0\neq M\in\mathcal{A}
possesses an HN filtration.

Next, we will mainly consider the finite dimensional module categories over a finite dimensional algebra, or equivalently, finite dimensional representation categories of quivers (with relations). It is easy to see that those categories are all of finite length, and we have the following proposition:

Proposition 2.14
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be an abelian category of finite length. Then every stability function
Z
on
\mathcal{A}
has the HN property.

Proof.

It is a natural consequence of
[
2
, Proposition 2.4]
.
∎

Example 2.15
.

Let
\mathcal{A}=\mathrm{rep}_{k}(A_{2})
, where
A_{2}:1\rightarrow 2
. Then
\mathcal{A}
has three indecomposable objects:

S_{1}:k\rightarrow 0,S_{2}:0\rightarrow k,P_{1}:k\rightarrow k

up to isomorphism, with a short exact sequence

0\rightarrow S_{2}\rightarrow P_{1}\rightarrow S_{1}\rightarrow 0.

The Grothendieck group
K_{0}(\mathcal{A})=\mathbb{Z}^{2}
is the free abelian group generated by
\underline{\dim}S_{1}=(1,0)
and
\underline{\dim}S_{2}=(0,1)
. So a stability function on
\mathcal{A}
is uniquely determined by an assignment for
Z(S_{1})
and
Z(S_{2})
.

By
Lemma
2.10
(2),
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
are always
Z
-stable, hence
Z
-semistable, no matter how
Z
change. But for
P_{1}
, the question is a little more difficult. The only nonzero proper subobject (up to isomorphism) of
P_{1}
is
S_{2}
. So
P_{1}
is
Z
-semistable if and only if
\phi(S_{2})\leqslant\phi(P_{1})
and hence if and only if
\phi(S_{2})\leqslant\phi(S_{1})
by
Lemma
2.9
(1). So the HN filtration of
P_{1}
is
0\subseteq P_{1}
when
\phi(S_{2})\leqslant\phi(S_{1})
and
0\subseteq S_{2}\subseteq P_{1}
when
\phi(S_{2})>\phi(S_{1})
.

In the case of
Example

2.15
, we observe that when the stability function (Z) changes, differences emerge on the two “sides” of the “wall”
\phi(S_{2})=\phi(S_{1})
. This phenomenon is also present in general cases. However, during the change of the stability function, certain properties remain invariant. Our goal is to characterize some of them.

3.
Ringel-Hall Algebras and Wall-Crossing Formulas

3.1.
Ringel-Hall Algebras

For an abelian category
\mathcal{A}
, there are many ways to define its Hall
algebra. Here we follow the choice of
[
3
]
and
[
9
]
.

Let
k=\mathbb{C}
and
\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{A})
be the
\mathbb{Q}
-vector space with basis the set of isomorphism classes of objects in
\mathcal{A}
. Now we define a multiplication on it to make it an associative
\mathbb{Q}
-algebra.

For
M_{1},\dots,M_{r},X\in\mathcal{A}
, the set of filtrations

V(M_{1},\dots,M_{r};X)=\{0=X_{0}\subseteq X_{1}\subseteq\dots\subseteq X_{r}=X|X_{i}/X_{i-1}\cong A_{i},1\leqslant i\leqslant r\}

has the structure of a complex variety. We denote its Euler characteristic number (under the analytic topology) by
\chi(V(M_{1},\dots,M_{r};X))
. The following theorem was first proved by Schofield and a detailed study of it by Reidtmann can be found in
[
10
]
.

Theorem 3.1
.

The operation

u_{[M]}*u_{[N]}=\sum_{[L]}\chi(V(M,N;L))u_{[L]}

defines an associative multiplication on
\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{A})
with the identity element
1=u_{[0]}
. The resulting associative
\mathbb{Q}
-algebra
(\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{A}),*,1)
is called the
Ringel-Hall algebra
of
\mathcal{A}
.

We have another point of view on the elements in
\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{A})
. For
f=\sum_{[M]}f_{[M]}u_{[M]}\in\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{A})
, we may regard
f
as a function from the set of isomorphism classes of objects in
\mathcal{A}
to the complex field via

f([M]):=f_{[M]}.

So
\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{A})
can be regarded as the set of functions on isomorphism classes of objects in
\mathcal{A}
that vanish on all but finitely many points.
It is the viewpoint taken by Bridgeland and Toledano Laredo
[
3
]
, and we will maintain it throughout this section.

3.2.
Characteristic Functions

In
\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{A})
, we define some important functions, using the same notation as in
[
3
]
and differing from those in
[
9
]
and
[
13
]
.

Let us fix a stability function on
\mathcal{A}
. For
\gamma\in K_{0}(\mathcal{A})
, we define

\displaystyle\kappa_{\gamma}(M)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}1,&\underline{\dim}M=\gamma,\\
0,&\underline{\dim}M\neq\gamma,\end{cases},

\displaystyle\delta^{Z}_{\gamma}(M)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}1,&\underline{\dim}M=\gamma\text{ and $M$ is $Z$-semistable},\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

These can be realized as characteristic functions supported on all objects and
Z
-semistable objects, respectively, with dimension vector
\gamma
. By
[
9
]
, we have the following result.

Theorem 3.2
.

For any
0\neq\gamma\in K_{0}(\mathcal{A})
, we have

\displaystyle\kappa_{\gamma}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\\
\gamma_{1}+\dots+\gamma_{n}=\gamma\\
\phi(\gamma_{1})>\dots>\phi(\gamma_{n})\end{subarray}}\delta^{Z}_{\gamma_{1}}*\dots*\delta^{Z}_{\gamma_{n}},

\displaystyle\delta^{Z}_{\gamma}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\\
\gamma_{1}+\dots+\gamma_{n}=\gamma\\
\phi(\gamma_{1}+\dots+\gamma_{i})>\phi(\gamma),1\leqslant i\leqslant n-1\end{subarray}}(-1)^{n-1}\kappa_{\gamma_{1}}*\dots*\kappa_{\gamma_{n}}.

The second identity is called the
Reineke inversion
.

The proof of the first identity follows immediately from the uniqueness of the HN filtration. The second was proved by Reineke in
[
9
]
.

The following subalgebra of
\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{A})
is very important.

Definition 3.3
.

The subalgebra

C(\mathcal{A})=\left\langle\kappa_{\gamma}\middle|\gamma\in K_{0}(\mathcal{A})\right\rangle

of
\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{A})
is called the
composition subalgebra
of
\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{A})
.

We can then deduce from
Theorem
3.2
that:

Corollary 3.4

(
[
9
, Theorem 4.9]
)
.

For any stability function
Z
on
\mathcal{A}
and
\gamma\in K_{0}(\mathcal{A})
, we have
\delta^{Z}_{\gamma}\in C(\mathcal{A})
.

3.3.
Comultiplication

The importance of the composition algebras is that it has an structure of bialgebra and hence the set of primitive elements forms a Lie subalgebra.

Let us to introduce it for several steps, follow
[
3
]
. We first define a map
\Delta:\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{A})\rightarrow\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{A})
via

\Delta(f)(M,N)=f(M\oplus N).

We have the following important result.

Theorem 3.5

(
[
3
, Theorem 4.3]
)
.

(1)

The image of
C(\mathcal{A})
under
\Delta
is contained in the tensor product

C(\mathcal{A})\otimes C(\mathcal{A})\subseteq\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{A})\otimes\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{A})\subseteq\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{A\times A}),

where the second inclusion is understood as

(f\otimes g)(M,N)=f(M)g(N).

(2)

The restriction of
\Delta
on
C(\mathcal{A})
can be explicitly written as

\Delta(\kappa_{\gamma})=\sum_{\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2}=\gamma}\kappa_{\gamma_{1}}\otimes\kappa_{\gamma_{2}.}

(3)

Define
\eta(f)=f(0)
, then
(C(\mathcal{A}),*,1,\Delta,\eta)
is a cocommutative bialgebra.

We say that an element
p\in C(\mathcal{A})
is
primitive
if
\Delta(p)=p\otimes 1+1\otimes p
. The following lemma shows the relationship between primitive elements in
C(\mathcal{A})
and indecomposable objects in
\mathcal{A}
.

Lemma 3.6

(
[
11
]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{S}
be a Krull-Schimidt category and
C(\mathcal{S})
be the vector space with basis the isomorphism classes of objects in
\mathcal{S}
. Let
\Delta
be a comultiplication defined as above, then an element
p\in C(\mathcal{S})
is primitive if and only if
p
is supported on indecomposable objects in
\mathcal{S}
.

Let
\mathfrak{n}(\mathcal{A})
be the subset of
C(\mathcal{A})
that consists of all primitive elements. It is a Lie subalgebra of
C(\mathcal{A})
by
[
10
]
and
[
11
]
, called the
Ringel-Hall Lie algebra
.

3.4.
Wall-crossing Formulas

Let
\hat{C}(\mathcal{A})
and
\hat{\mathfrak{n}}(\mathcal{A})
be the completion of
C(\mathcal{A})
and
\mathfrak{n}(\mathcal{A})
, respectively, in the meaning of
[
3
]
. Denote by
\hat{N}(\mathcal{A})
the subset of
\hat{C}(\mathcal{A})
that consists of all
grouplike elements
, namely the elements
g
satisfying
\Delta(g)=g\otimes g
. Then the exponential map

\exp x:=\sum_{n\geqslant 0}\frac{x^{n}}{n!}

gives a bijection from
\hat{\mathfrak{n}}(\mathcal{A})
to
\hat{N}(\mathcal{A})
, with the inverse

\log y=\sum_{n\geqslant 1}\frac{(-1)^{n-1}}{n}(y-1)^{n}.

\hat{N}(\mathcal{A})
is a subgroup of the unit group
\hat{C}(\mathcal{A})^{\times}
of
\hat{C}(\mathcal{A})
, called the
Ringel-Hall group
of
\mathcal{A}
. It is a pro-unipotent Lie group with Lie algebra
\hat{\mathfrak{n}}(\mathcal{A})
.

We now define the following elements in
\hat{C}(\mathcal{A})
. For
\phi\in(0,1]
, set

\mathrm{SS}^{Z}_{\phi}=1+\sum_{\gamma\in K_{0}(\mathcal{A}),\phi(\gamma)=\phi}\delta^{Z}_{\gamma}.

Then we can write the famous wall-crossing formula.

Theorem 3.7

(
[
9
]
)
.

Let
1_{\mathcal{A}}
be the element

1_{\mathcal{A}}=\prod^{\leftarrow}_{\phi\in(0,1]}\mathrm{SS}^{Z}_{\phi}.

In the identity above, the left hand side

Example 3.8

In

If we set for every indecomposable

that

then we have

1_{\mathcal{A}}=\prod^{\leftarrow}_{\phi\in(0,1]}\mathrm{SS}^{Z}_{\phi}=\Phi_{S_{2}}*\Phi_{S_{1}}.

When
\phi(S_{2})=\phi(S_{1})=\phi

-semistable with phase

Combining the two identity above yields

which is known as the pentagon identity.

3.5.
Joyce’s Element

Now for
\gamma\in K_{0}(\mathcal{A})^{+}
, we define

5
]

\epsilon^{Z}_{\gamma}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\\
\gamma_{1}+\dots+\gamma_{n}=\gamma\\
\phi(\gamma_{i})=\phi(\gamma),1\leqslant i\leqslant n\end{subarray}}\frac{(-1)^{n-1}}{n}\delta^{Z}_{\gamma_{1}}*\dots*\delta^{Z}_{\gamma_{n}}\in C(\mathcal{A}).

By calculating using the definition of the logarithm function, we obtain

\displaystyle\log\mathrm{SS}^{Z}_{\phi}

\displaystyle=\log\left(1+\sum_{\phi(\gamma)=\phi}\delta^{Z}_{\gamma}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\geqslant 1}\frac{(-1)^{n-1}}{n}\left(\sum_{\phi(\gamma)=\phi}\delta^{Z}_{\gamma}\right)^{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\geqslant 1}\frac{(-1)^{n-1}}{n}\sum_{\phi(\gamma_{1})=\dots=\phi(\gamma_{n})=\phi}\delta^{Z}_{\gamma_{1}}*\dots*\delta^{Z}_{\gamma_{n}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\gamma\in K_{0}(\mathcal{A})^{+},\phi(\gamma)=\phi}\epsilon^{Z}_{\gamma}

,

or equivalently

\mathrm{SS}^{Z}_{\phi}=\exp\left(\sum_{\gamma\in K_{0}(\mathcal{A})^{+},\phi(\gamma)=\phi}\epsilon^{Z}_{\gamma}\right).

Consequently,
\epsilon_{\gamma}
is primitive and hence supported on indecomposable objects.

4.2.

We follow the introduction in

Definition 4.4
.

Let
\mathcal{D}
be a triangulated category. A

on
\mathcal{D}
is a pair
\sigma=(Z,\mathcal{P})
, where

•

;

•

\mathcal{P}=\{\mathcal{P}(\phi)|\phi\in\mathbb{R}\}
is a family of full additive extension-closed subcategories of
\mathcal{D}
,

satisfying the following conditions:

(1)

If

, then
Z(M)=m_{M}e^{i\pi\phi}
for some
m_{M}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
.

(2)

\mathcal{P}(\phi+1)=\mathcal{P}(\phi)[1]
for any
\phi\in\mathbb{R}
.

(3)

For
A_{i}\in\mathcal{P}(\phi_{i}),i=1,2
with
\phi_{1}>\phi_{2}
, we have
\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathcal{D}}(A_{1},A_{2})=0
.

(4)

(HN filtration) For any
0\neq M\in\mathcal{D}
, there is a sequence of triangles
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 0=M_{0}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces M_{1}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces M_{2}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\cdots}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces M_{n-1}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces M_{n}=M}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces A_{1}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces A_{2}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces A_{n}}

such that each

\phi_{i}

, we derive the Reineke inversion in our context within triangulated categories.

Proposition 4.16

(Reineke inversion for triangulated categories)
.

Let
0\neq\gamma\in K_{0}(\mathcal{D})
. We have

\delta^{\sigma}_{\gamma}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\\
\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2}+\dots+\gamma_{n}=\gamma\\
{\phi_{m_{j}}}(\gamma_{1}+\dots+\gamma_{i})>\phi_{m}(\gamma),1\leqslant j\leqslant i<n\\
\end{subarray}}(-1)^{n}\,{{}^{m_{1}}}\kappa^{\sigma}_{\gamma_{1}}*\dots*{{}^{m_{n}}}\kappa^{\sigma}_{\gamma_{n}}.

Proof.

By
Proposition
4.15
, we obtain

{{}^{m}}\delta^{\sigma}_{\gamma}={{}^{m}}\kappa^{\sigma}_{\gamma}-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\\
n\geqslant 2,{\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2}+\dots+\gamma_{n}=\gamma},m_{n}=m\\
{\phi_{m_{1}}(\gamma_{1})>\phi_{m_{2}}(\gamma_{2})>\dots>\phi_{m_{n}(\gamma_{n})}}\end{subarray}}{{}^{m_{1}}}\delta^{\sigma}_{\gamma_{1}}*{{}^{m_{2}}}\delta^{\sigma}_{\gamma_{2}}*\dots*{{}^{m_{n}}}\delta^{\sigma}_{\gamma_{n}}.

Let us consider the set
K_{0}(\mathcal{D})\backslash\{0\}\times\mathbb{Z}
, with a partial order

(\gamma_{1},m_{1})\leqslant(\gamma_{2},m_{2})\iff\phi_{m_{1}}(\gamma_{1})\leqslant\phi_{m_{2}}(\gamma_{2}).

We can also define an addition on
K_{0}(\mathcal{D})\backslash\{0\}\times\mathbb{Z}
:

(\gamma_{1},m_{1})+(\gamma_{2},m_{2}):=(\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2},\min(m_{1},m_{2})),

making
K_{0}(\mathcal{D})\backslash\{0\}\times\mathbb{Z}
a commutative subgroup. Now consider the map

\displaystyle\phi:K_{0}(\mathcal{D})\backslash\{0\}\times\mathbb{Z}

\displaystyle\rightarrow\mathbb{R}

\displaystyle(\gamma,m)

\displaystyle\mapsto\phi_{m}(\gamma),

and rewrite the identity we have obtained:

{{}^{m}}\delta^{\sigma}_{\gamma}={{}^{m}}\kappa^{\sigma}_{\gamma}-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\\
n\geqslant 2,(\gamma_{1},m_{1})+(\gamma_{2},m_{2})+\dots+(\gamma_{n},m_{n})=(\gamma,m)\\
\phi(\gamma_{1},m_{1})>\phi(\gamma_{2},m_{2})>\dots>\phi(\gamma_{n},m_{n})\end{subarray}}{{}^{m_{1}}}\delta^{\sigma}_{\gamma_{1}}*{{}^{m_{2}}}\delta^{\sigma}_{\gamma_{2}}*\dots*{{}^{m_{n}}}\delta^{\sigma}_{\gamma_{n}}.

By the same argument as in abelian categories (see
[
9
, Theorem 5.1]
), we deduce

\displaystyle{{}^{m}}\delta^{\sigma}_{\gamma}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\\
(\gamma_{1},m_{1})+(\gamma_{2},m_{2})+\dots+(\gamma_{n},m_{n})=(\gamma,m)\\
\phi((\gamma_{1},m_{1})+\dots+(\gamma_{i},m_{i}))>\phi(\gamma,m),1\leqslant i<n\\
\end{subarray}}(-1)^{n}\,{{}^{m_{1}}}\kappa^{\sigma}_{\gamma_{1}}*\dots*{{}^{m_{n}}}\kappa^{\sigma}_{\gamma_{n}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\\
\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2}+\dots+\gamma_{n}=\gamma,m_{n}=m\\
{\phi_{m_{j}}}(\gamma_{1}+\dots+\gamma_{i})>\phi_{m}(\gamma),1\leqslant j\leqslant i<n\\
\end{subarray}}(-1)^{n}\,{{}^{m_{1}}}\kappa^{\sigma}_{\gamma_{1}}*\dots*{{}^{m_{n}}}\kappa^{\sigma}_{\gamma_{n}}

By summing over all
m\in\mathbb{Z}
, we finish the proof.
∎

4.7.
Composition Subalgebras

As in the case of abelian categories, we now define the composition subalgebra of
\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{D})
. The
composition subalgebra

C(\mathcal{D})
is defined to be the subalgebra of
\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{D})
generated by the set

\{u_{S[n]}|S\text{ is simple in }\phi(0,1],n\in\mathbb{Z}\}.

Lemma 4.17
.

For any
0\neq\gamma\in\mathcal{D}
and
m\in\mathbb{Z}
,
{{}^{m}}\kappa^{\sigma}_{\gamma},{{}^{m}}\delta^{\sigma}_{\gamma}\in C(\mathcal{D})
.

Proof.

Let us show that
{{}^{m}}\delta^{\sigma}_{\gamma}\in C(\mathcal{D})
, and then by
Proposition
4.15
, we obtain that
{{}^{m}}\kappa^{\sigma}_{\gamma}\in C(\mathcal{D})
as well.

Consider the heart
\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{P}(0,1]
, then the function
{{}^{m}}\delta^{\sigma}_{\gamma}
is supported on
\mathcal{A}[2m-1]
or
\mathcal{A}[2m]
, depending on
\gamma
. Note that
\mathcal{A}[2m-1]
and
\mathcal{A}[2m]
are both abelian, so we can use the same method as Reineke
[
9
, Lemma 4.4]
to deduce the result.
∎

It is easy to see that
C(\mathcal{D})
has a natural
K_{0}(\mathcal{D})
-grading. We denote by
\hat{C}(\mathcal{D})
the completion of
C(\mathcal{D})
, namely the vector space

\prod_{\gamma\in K_{0}(\mathcal{D})}C(\mathcal{D})_{\gamma},

with multiplication in the same form as in
C(\mathcal{D})
.

4.8.
Wall-crossing Formulas

We can define a comultiplication
\Delta
on
C(\mathcal{D})
and
\hat{C}(\mathcal{D})
via the same form as in the case of abelian categories, namely

\Delta(f)(M,N)=f(M\oplus N),

where we identify
\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{D})\otimes\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{D})
and a subalgebra of
\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{D}\times\mathcal{D})
via

(f\otimes g)(M,N)=f(M)g(N).

Then we can also consider the set
\hat{\mathfrak{n}}(\mathcal{D})
of primitive elements and
\hat{N}(\mathcal{D})
of grouplike elements. As in the case of abelian categories, the exponential map

\exp x:=\sum_{n\geqslant 0}\frac{x^{n}}{n!}

gives a bijection from
\hat{\mathfrak{n}}(\mathcal{D})
to
\hat{N}(\mathcal{D})
, with the inverse

\log y=\sum_{n\geqslant 1}\frac{(-1)^{n-1}(y-1)^{n}}{n}.

For
\phi\in(2m-1,2m+1]
, we define

\mathrm{SS}^{\sigma}_{\phi}=1+\sum_{\phi_{m}(\gamma)=\phi}{{}^{m}}\delta^{\sigma}_{\gamma}\in\hat{C}(\mathcal{D}).

In other words,
\mathrm{SS}^{\sigma}_{\phi}
is the characteristic function supported on
\mathcal{P}(\phi)
.

Lemma 4.18
.

For any
\phi\in\mathbb{R}
,
\mathrm{SS}^{\sigma}_{\phi}
is grouplike and hence belongs to
\hat{N}(\mathcal{D})
.

Proof.

For any
M,N\in\mathcal{D}
, let us consider
\Delta(\mathrm{SS}^{\sigma}_{\phi})(M,N)
and
(\mathrm{SS}^{\sigma}_{\phi}\otimes\mathrm{SS}^{\sigma}_{\phi})(M,N)
. We have

\Delta(\mathrm{SS}^{\sigma}_{\phi})(M,N)=\mathrm{SS}^{\sigma}_{\phi}(M\oplus N)=\begin{cases}1,&M\oplus N\in\mathcal{P}(\phi),\\
0,&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

and

(\mathrm{SS}^{\sigma}_{\phi}\otimes\mathrm{SS}^{\sigma}_{\phi})(M,N)=\mathrm{SS}^{\sigma}_{\phi}(M)\mathrm{SS}^{\sigma}_{\phi}(N)=\begin{cases}1,&M,N\in\mathcal{P}(\phi),\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

We finish the proof by
Lemma
4.8
.
∎

It is easy to see that the function
1_{\mathcal{D}}
, which takes the value
1
on every isomorphism class of objects in
\mathcal{D}
, is a grouplike element, and we have the following identity.

Theorem 4.19

(Wall-crossing formula for triangulated categories)
.

1_{\mathcal{D}}=\prod^{\leftarrow}_{\phi\in\mathbb{R}}\mathrm{SS}^{\sigma}_{\phi}.

Proof.

Let us calculate from the right-hand side.

\displaystyle\prod^{\leftarrow}_{\phi\in\mathbb{R}}\mathrm{SS}^{\sigma}_{\phi}

\displaystyle=\prod^{\leftarrow}_{\phi\in\mathbb{R}}\left(1+\sum_{\phi_{m}(\gamma)=\phi}{{}^{m}}\delta^{\sigma}_{\gamma}\right)

\displaystyle=1+\sum_{\phi_{1}>\phi_{2}>\dots>\phi_{s}}\left(\sum_{\phi_{m_{1}}(\gamma_{1})=\phi_{1}}{{}^{m_{1}}}\delta^{\sigma}_{\gamma_{1}}\right)*\dots*\left(\sum_{\phi_{m_{s}}(\gamma_{s})=\phi_{s}}{{}^{m_{s}}}\delta^{\sigma}_{\gamma_{s}}\right)

\displaystyle=1+\sum_{\phi_{m_{1}}(\gamma_{1})>\phi_{m_{2}}(\gamma_{2})>\dots>\phi_{m_{s}}(\gamma_{s})}{{}^{m_{1}}}\delta^{\sigma}_{\gamma_{1}}*{{}^{m_{2}}}\delta^{\sigma}_{\gamma_{2}}*\dots*{{}^{m_{s}}}\delta^{\sigma}_{\gamma_{s}}

\displaystyle=1+\sum_{\gamma\in K_{0}(\mathcal{D})}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\\
{\phi_{m_{1}}(\gamma_{1})>\phi_{m_{2}}(\gamma_{2})>\dots>\phi_{m_{s}(\gamma_{s})}}\\
\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2}+\dots+\gamma_{s}=\gamma\end{subarray}}{{}^{m_{1}}}\delta^{\sigma}_{\gamma_{1}}*{{}^{m_{2}}}\delta^{\sigma}_{\gamma_{2}}*\dots*{{}^{m_{n}}}\delta^{\sigma}_{\gamma_{s}}

\displaystyle=1+\sum_{\gamma\in K_{0}(\mathcal{D})}\kappa_{\gamma}\text{ (by {Proposition \ref{HN,fine}})}

\displaystyle=1_{\mathcal{D}}.

∎

4.9.
Joyce’s Elements and the Left Part of the Diagram

In this subsection we generalize the context of Joyce’s element. Recall in abelian categories, the Joyce’s element is defined to be

\epsilon^{Z}_{\gamma}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\\
\gamma_{1}+\dots+\gamma_{n}=\gamma\\
\phi(\gamma_{i})=\phi(\gamma),1\leqslant i\leqslant n\end{subarray}}\frac{(-1)^{n-1}}{n}\delta^{Z}_{\gamma_{1}}*\dots*\delta^{Z}_{\gamma_{n}}\in C(\mathcal{A}).

We have shown that it lies in
\mathfrak{n}(\mathcal{A})
, so it is supported on indecomposable objects. See Subsection 3.5. Now we consider the element in the same form:

\tilde{\epsilon^{\sigma}_{\gamma}}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\\
\gamma_{1}+\dots+\gamma_{n}=\gamma\\
Z(\gamma_{i})\in\mathbb{R}_{+}Z(\gamma),1\leqslant i\leqslant n\end{subarray}}\frac{(-1)^{n-1}}{n}\delta^{\sigma}_{\gamma_{1}}*\dots*\delta^{\sigma}_{\gamma_{n}}.

However, the following example implies that it might not be the correct generalization of Joyce’s element, since it takes nonzero value on some decomposable objects.

Example 4.20
.

Let
Q
be an acyclic quiver and
\mathcal{D}=D^{b}(Q)
, so
\mathcal{D}
is heredietary and satisfies our two conditions. Let
S
be the simple representation of
Q
associated to any vertex and let
\gamma=2\underline{\dim}S
. Then

\displaystyle\tilde{\epsilon}^{\sigma}_{\gamma}

\displaystyle=\delta^{\sigma}_{\gamma}-\frac{1}{2}(\delta^{\sigma}_{\underline{\dim}S}*\delta^{\sigma}_{\underline{\dim}S}+\delta^{\sigma}_{\underline{\dim}S}*\delta^{\sigma}_{\underline{\dim}S})

\displaystyle=\delta^{\sigma}_{\gamma}-\delta^{\sigma}_{\underline{\dim}S}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}u_{S[2m]\oplus S[2m]}-\sum_{m,n\in\mathbb{Z}}u_{S[2m]\oplus S[2n]}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{m\neq n\in\mathbb{Z}}u_{S[2m]\oplus S[2n]}.

So
\tilde{\epsilon}^{\sigma}_{\gamma}
takes the value
-1
on
S[2m]\oplus S[2n],m\neq n
, which are decomposable objects.

Now we provide a possible way to generalize Joyce’s elements. Recall in Subsection 3.5 we have calculated that

\log\mathrm{SS}^{Z}_{\phi}=\sum_{\gamma\in K_{0}(\mathcal{A})^{+},\phi(\gamma)=\phi}\epsilon^{Z}_{\gamma}

in the context of abelian categories. So let us calculate the logarithm of
\mathrm{SS}^{\sigma}_{\phi}
:

\displaystyle\log\mathrm{SS}^{\sigma}_{\phi}

\displaystyle=\log\left(1+\sum_{\phi_{m}(\gamma)=\phi}{{}^{m}}\delta^{\sigma}_{\gamma}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\geqslant 1}\frac{(-1)^{n-1}}{n}\left(\sum_{\phi_{m}(\gamma)=\phi}{{}^{m}}\delta^{\sigma}_{\gamma}\right)^{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\geqslant 1}\frac{(-1)^{n-1}}{n}\sum_{\phi_{m}(\gamma_{1})=\phi_{m}(\gamma_{n})=\phi}{{}^{m}}\delta^{\sigma}_{\gamma_{1}}*\dots*{{}^{m}}\delta^{\sigma}_{\gamma_{n}}.

If we set

{{}^{m}}\epsilon^{\sigma}_{\gamma}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\\
\gamma_{1}+\dots+\gamma_{n}=\gamma\\
\phi_{m}(\gamma_{1})=\dots=\phi_{m}(\gamma_{n})=\phi_{m}(\gamma)\end{subarray}}\frac{(-1)^{n-1}}{n}{{}^{m}}\delta^{\sigma}_{\gamma_{1}}*\dots*{{}^{m}}\delta^{\sigma}_{\gamma_{n}},

then we have

\log\mathrm{SS}^{\sigma}_{\phi}=\sum_{\phi_{m}(\gamma)=\phi}{{}^{m}}\epsilon^{\sigma}_{\gamma}.

Let

\epsilon^{\sigma}_{\gamma}=\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}{{}^{m}}\epsilon^{\sigma}_{\gamma}\in\hat{C}(\mathcal{D}).

By
Lemma
4.18
,
\mathrm{SS}^{\sigma}_{\phi}\in\hat{N}(\mathcal{D})
, hence its logarithm lies in
\hat{\mathfrak{n}}(\mathcal{D})
and is primitive. Hence
{{}^{m}}\epsilon^{\sigma}_{\gamma}
, and then
\epsilon^{\sigma}_{\gamma}
are all primitive, or equivalently, supported on indecomposable objects.

When
\mathcal{D}
is hereditary, we have

Theorem 4.21
.

Let
\ell
be a ray in the complex plane with its vertex at the origin and argument
\phi/\pi\in(-1,1]
, then

\exp\left(\sum_{Z(\gamma)\in\ell}\epsilon^{\sigma}_{\gamma}\right)=\prod_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathrm{SS}^{\sigma}_{\phi+2m}.

Proof.

We first note that

\displaystyle\sum_{Z(\gamma)\in\ell}\epsilon^{\sigma}_{\gamma}

\displaystyle=\sum_{Z(\gamma)\in\ell}\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}{{}^{m}}\epsilon^{\sigma}_{\gamma}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\phi_{0}(\gamma)=\phi}\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}{{}^{m}}\epsilon^{\sigma}_{\gamma}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}\sum_{\phi_{m}(\gamma)=\phi+2m}{{}^{m}}\epsilon^{\sigma}_{\gamma}.

By the heredity of
\mathcal{D}
, when
m\neq n
, we have

[u_{A[2m]},u_{B[2n]}]=0

for any
A,B\in\mathcal{D}
indecomposable with
\phi_{0}(\underline{\dim}A)=\phi_{0}(\underline{\dim}B)
. Hence,
{{}^{m}}\epsilon^{\sigma}_{\gamma}
and
{{}^{n}}\epsilon^{\sigma}_{\gamma}
are commutative. Therefore,

\displaystyle\exp\left(\sum_{Z(\gamma)\in\ell}\epsilon^{\sigma}_{\gamma}\right)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}\sum_{\phi_{m}(\gamma)=\phi+2m}{{}^{m}}\epsilon^{\sigma}_{\gamma}\right)

\displaystyle=\prod_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}\exp\left(\sum_{\phi_{m}(\gamma)=\phi+2m}{{}^{m}}\epsilon^{\sigma}_{\gamma}\right)

\displaystyle=\prod_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathrm{SS}^{\sigma}_{\phi+2m}.

∎
