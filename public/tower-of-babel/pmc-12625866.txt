Enhancing healthcare providers’ advance care planning competence with large language models: protocol for the development of an AI chatbot and its evaluation in a randomised controlled trial
Authors: Minghui Tan, Siyuan Tang, Shichao Kan, Haojie Zhang, Bei Wu, Zhao Ni, Chia Chin Lin, Jinfeng Ding
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionAdvance care planning (ACP) can support individuals to express their autonomy in the decision-making process for future care. Traditional ACP training for healthcare providers faces significant challenges related to interactivity, accessibility, scalability and sustainability. Cutting-edge generative artificial intelligence (AI) holds promise in enabling intelligent and interactive chatbots for ACP. The present protocol outlines the development and evaluation of a large language model (LLM)-based ACP chatbot for healthcare providers.Methods and analysisThe development of the LLM-based ACP chatbot will follow four stages: construction of dialogue data sets, fine-tuning, multi-LLM orchestration and ablation studies. A randomised controlled trial will then evaluate the LLM-based ACP chatbot’s effectiveness in enhancing ACP competence among healthcare providers. A total of 66 healthcare providers will be recruited from China and randomly assigned (1:1) to either: (1) The LLM-based ACP chatbot intervention or (2) An ACP knowledge manual. The primary outcome will be ACP competence, while secondary outcomes will include (1) ACP knowledge, (2) Attitudes/beliefs, (3) Practice willingness, (4) Readiness, (5) Self-efficacy, (6) Processes of change, and (7) Decisional balance. Both primary and secondary outcomes will be assessed to evaluate the immediate impact (postintervention) and short-term impact (3-month follow-up and 6-month follow-up) of the chatbot on ACP.Ethics and disseminationThe research was approved by the Ethical Review Board, Xiangya School of Nursing, Central South University (E202442). Study modifications will be discussed among the research team members until a consensus is reached. Amendments reflecting study modifications will be submitted for institutional review board approval at all sites, updated on the clinical trial registry, and fully detailed and explained in the manuscript reporting the results of the study. All participants will provide written informed consent. The study will be conducted according to the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. The results of this study will be submitted for publication in peer-reviewed journals and presented at (inter)national conferences.Trial registration numberChiCTR2400091022

Introduction

Advance care planning (ACP) is a process that supports adults at any age or stage of health to understand and communicate their personal values, life goals and preferences for future healthcare.1 Evidence indicates that ACP effectively improves communication quality, aligns care more closely with an individual’s preferences and wishes, reduces low-value care and financial burdens, and ultimately enhances the individual’s quality of death.2 3 Despite the recognised importance, ACP adoption remains suboptimal in clinical practice. Our meta-analysis of 4237 participants revealed that a significant majority of individuals globally were either unaware of ACP or had not engaged in the process, with a maximum pooled prevalence of 72%.4 Additionally, Chinese individuals exhibited a significantly lower level of ACP engagement compared with their counterparts from other countries.4

As both initiators and facilitators of decision-making, healthcare providers play a crucial role in the ACP process.4 5 Healthcare providers often report that inadequate education and training, communication challenges, and uncertainties about legal and ethical responsibilities are significant barriers to effective ACP implementation.6 7 Traditional ACP trainings, including lectures, videos and workshops, have been employed to address these gaps. Nevertheless, these strategies often exhibit limitations such as poor interactivity, limited sustainability and scalability, and a lack of personalised feedback.810 In the context of China’s rapidly evolving healthcare system, these challenges may be more pronounced by the diverse range of healthcare settings, regional disparities and the pressing demand for scalable solutions to address the needs of the country’s vast population.11 12 Technological advancements have facilitated the creation of online interactive modules for ACP across various platforms to enhance accessibility.13 14 However, these modules often fall short in meeting the unique needs of Chinese healthcare providers, particularly regarding language barriers, cultural nuances and varying levels of expertise.10 Additionally, they face persistent challenges in accommodating individual expertise and replicating the subtleties of real-time, personalised provider-patient communication.

In contrast, the integration of AI, particularly generative AI powered by large language models (LLMs), offers a transformative approach to overcoming these limitations. LLMs are AI systems trained on billions of words that can understand and generate human language.15 Unlike existing ACP interactive modules, LLMs have demonstrated remarkable potential in understanding and generating human-like language with unprecedented sophistication,16 offering crucial support in the context of ACP through enriched human-computer interaction. By leveraging large-scale pretraining and fine-tuning, LLM-based chatbots can simulate realistic and nuanced individual-provider dialogues, deliver instant, contextually relevant feedback, and tailor their responses to the user’s expertise.17 18 In healthcare education, similar LLM-based tools have been shown to improve diagnostic reasoning, enhance communication skills and support clinical decision-making.1922 However, their application in ACP training for healthcare providers remains largely unexplored. Given the unique cultural, legal and institutional contexts in China, there is significant potential for an LLM-based chatbot to address specific barriers to ACP implementation in the country.

Hence, we aim to develop an LLM-based ACP chatbot to enhance ACP adoption among healthcare providers in China. The specific objectives are: (1) To develop an LLM-powered chatbot tailored for ACP discussion; (2) To conduct a pilot to test the usability, feasibility and acceptability of the chatbot among healthcare providers; (3) To evaluate the chatbot’s effectiveness in enhancing ACP competence through a randomised controlled trial (RCT).

Methods and analysis

This protocol and the RCT design are guided by the Standard Protocol Items: Recommendations for Interventional Trials–Artificial Intelligence extension.23 This study will be conducted in two phases. Phase I will focus on developing the LLM-based ACP chatbot, and phase II will evaluate its effectiveness through a pilot study and a larger-scale RCT. The project commenced in January 2024 and is expected to conclude by June 2026. Recruitment for the RCT began in May 2025. As of September 2025, the study is currently underway in the RCT phase.

Fine-tuning: Fine-tuning an LLM involves adapting a pretrained model to a specific task or domain by further training it on a smaller, task-specific data set. This process enables the model to perform specialised tasks effectively. The fine-tuning process includes: (i) Pretrained model selection: The base LLM used in this study will be selected based on its capacity for fine-tuning, extensive medical knowledge and robust conversational skills. We intend to use the ChatGPT-4o mini model. (ii) Data collection and preparation: as detailed in section 1. (iii) Training process: To optimise the fine-tuning of the LLM, we will use supervised fine-tuning.24 Supervised fine-tuning is a process where a pretrained model is further trained on a labelled data set with specific task-oriented examples, enabling it to learn patterns and improve performance for particular tasks by adjusting its weights through gradient descent based on the labelled input-output pairs. After this process, ACP-specific LLMs (ACP-LLMs) will be generated.

Multi-LLM orchestration: Swarm,25 an educational framework proposed by the OpenAI Solutions team, will be used to support ergonomic and lightweight multiagent orchestration. This multiagent system will be built on the ACP-LLMs with different prompts (online supplemental appendix A), in which users can interact with different agents. An assistant agent will role-play as an assistant, helping users understand a topic, answer questions and provide instruction. A vignette agent will act as a patient or family member, adapting to users’ needs. An evaluator agent will review user-vignette agent transcripts, summarise users’ performance and provide follow-up advice. The orchestration of these agents will be designed to replicate the key pedagogical roles required for effective ACP training: instruction, practical application and formative assessment.

Ablation studies: Ablation studies will be performed by leave-out test to determine the impact of each component on the overall performance of the LLM-based ACP chatbot. Four ablated models will include: (i) The off-the-shelf GPT-4o mini model; (2) ACP-LLM (GPT-4o mini + fine-tuning); (3) GPT-4o mini + swarm; (4) LLM-based ACP chatbot (GPT-4o mini + fine-tuning + swarm). Both human and automatic evaluation will be employed to evaluate the performance of the generated dialogues in ablation studies. A panel of healthcare professionals with expertise in ACP will conduct human evaluation, while the ChatGPT-4o model will be used for automatic evaluation. The evaluation will follow a framework for human evaluation of LLMs,26 which assesses five aspects of dialogue performance, including quality of information, understanding and reasoning, expression style and persona, safety and harm, and trust and confidence (QUEST), with each criterion rated on a 5-point scale.

Phase II: Pilot test and RCT

Eligibility criteria

Participants eligible for inclusion must meet the following criteria: (1) Be adults aged 18 years or older; (2) Be healthcare providers (including physicians, nurses and allied health professionals) who regularly interact with individuals with potential palliative care needs (such as patients with cancer or elderly individuals); (3) Be proficient in smartphone use.

Intervention pilot study

A pilot study involving 10 eligible participants will be conducted to assess the usability, feasibility and acceptability of the LLM-based ACP chatbot. On providing consent, participants will be invited to engage with the chatbot as needed over a 1 week period. Feedback from participants will be systematically collected and analysed, and modifications will be made to the chatbot prior to the main trial.

Sample size and participant recruitment

The sample size for this study was calculated based on the primary outcome of ACP competence, measured by the 10-item Decision Support Analysis Tool (DSAT-10) at the immediate postintervention timepoint. Prior studies reported an effect size of 0.91–1.35 for DSAT-10.27 28 Using Power Analysis and Sample Size Software (PASS) software for sample size estimation, with a statistical power of β=0.90 and a significance level of α=0.05, the maximum sample size per group was calculated to be around 27 individuals. Furthermore, with an expected dropout rate of 20%,28 the recruitment target was set at 33 individuals per group, totalling 66 individuals for both groups.

Participant enrolment will be self-initiated through electronic posters and leaflets distributed via a diverse set of professional channels in China, including national and regional professional societies, hospital communications (intranet/newsletters), educational forums and public/professional WeChat group channels. Recruitment materials will be standardised and identical across channels. All platforms used will be documented, and participant demographics will be monitored to guide supplementary recruitment if needed, reducing the risk of selection bias. Moreover, recruitment materials will include a link to an online screening questionnaire to determine participant eligibility. Individuals will be given 48 hours to consider the study details. After this period, written informed consent for trial participation will be obtained online (online supplemental appendix B). Participants will be asked to complete assessments at baseline (−t1), postintervention (t1), 3-month follow-up (t2) and 6-month follow-up (t3; see figure 2 and table 2). The t1 timepoint represents the immediate postintervention assessment, while t2 and t3 represent short-term follow-up assessments to examine the persistence of intervention effects over time.

Outcomes

The study will assess ACP competence as the primary outcome, while ACP knowledge, attitudes/beliefs, practice willingness, readiness, self-efficacy, processes of change and decisional balance will serve as secondary outcomes. The chatbot usage will be evaluated through operational logs and feedback interviews.

Demographic data will include age, gender, ethnicity, marital status, health status, education, occupation, discipline, years of working experience, as well as their experiences with death, decision-making and ACP.

ACP competence will be measured by DSAT-10,28 specifically designed to evaluate healthcare providers’ use of knowledge and communication skills during a clinical encounter. DSAT-10 consists of five elements: (1) Decision-making status, (2) Knowledge, (3) Values/preference, (4) Others’ involvement in the decision, and (5) Next steps. A higher total score indicates a greater level of competence, with the overall κ coefficient across all items recorded at 0.55.29

Knowledge, attitudes/beliefs, and practice willingness will be assessed using a 33-item questionnaire that measures ACP knowledge (12 items), attitudes/beliefs (13 items) and willingness to practise (8 items).30 Higher scores indicate better outcomes. The overall Cronbach’s α coefficient was 0.83, and the split-half reliability was 0.89.

Readiness and self-efficacy will be assessed using the Advance Care Planning Engagement Survey,31 a 29-item tool rated on a 5-point Likert Scale. Higher scores denote greater readiness and self-efficacy. The scale demonstrates high internal consistency, with Cronbach’s α coefficients ranging from 0.60 to 0.88, and excellent test-retest reliability at 0.85.32

Measurement of decisional balance and processes of change for ACP will be conducted using the Decisional Balance, Medical and Religious Beliefs, and Processes of Change for ACP Scale.33 It features 34 items rated on a 5-point Likert Scale, with higher scores indicating better outcomes. Cronbach’s α coefficients ranged from 0.76 to 0.93, and dimension correlation coefficients were between 0.43 and 0.89.33

The chatbot will automatically record operational logs to understand participants’ behavioural habits, usage frequency, specific topics discussed and so on. Feedback interviews will be conducted using semistructured methods, focusing on participants’ prior experiences with the LLM and ACP training, their participation in and reflections on specific intervention components, perceived changes in ACP practice following the intervention, barriers and facilitators to participation, and their willingness and suggestions for future participation. A complete interview guide is provided in online supplemental appendix B.

Intervention assignment and monitoring

Randomisation will be computer-generated by a statistician who is not attached to the research team. The allocation sequence and block size will be unknown to the involved researcher. A researcher will draw a sealed envelope, which is packed by an uninvolved researcher and contains information about the randomisation group. Participants will be randomised into the intervention group and the control group in a 1:1 ratio.

In the control group, participants will be given an ACP knowledge manual, which has been developed by the research team through evidence synthesis and expert consultations. The intervention group will receive a human-machine dialogue intervention based on the LLM-based ACP chatbot (figure 3), in addition to the ACP knowledge manual. The human-machine dialogue intervention will be organised into three key components: (1) Discussing any ACP-related questions with the assistant agent; (2) Participating in a minimum of 15 simulated conversations with the vignette agent, with at least three vignettes randomly selected at each stage based on the transtheoretical model; (3) Reading feedback summaries generated by the evaluator agent, which can be used to support subsequent learning.

Single blinding will be used in this intervention. Due to the particularity of the intervention, it is impossible to apply blindly to the participants. However, blinding can be applied to outcome assessors and data analysts, who will remain unaware of participants’ group assignments and will not have access to identifiers during data collection and analysis.

Data collection and analysis

An online questionnaire based on Star Questionnaire software (https://www.wjx.cn/) will be used to capture all data collection points. It is commonly used in China due to its compatibility with mobile devices, customisability and automatic export features. The assessment will take approximately 20 minutes to complete. Participants can complete the measurements individually or with the assistance of trained researchers.

Analysis of quantitative data will be carried out using the Stata statistical software. Recruitment rates, dropout rates and missing data in the two groups will be reported. The obtained data will be presented as frequencies, means±SD, or 95% CIs. Multiple imputations will be used to handle missing data, and all analyses will follow the intention-to-treat principle. Repeated measures will be analysed with mixed-effects models including fixed effects for group, time and group × time interaction, and random intercepts for participants.

For the qualitative interviews, we will invite participants from among those who participated in the intervention. Recruitment will continue until thematic saturation is reached. Interviews will be conducted via online conferencing (eg, Tencent Meeting) or telephone. Participants will be invited at the end of the intervention to participate in a follow-up interview. Each interview is expected to last 30–45 min. All interviews will be conducted by trained doctoral-level researchers with prior experience in communication skills research and qualitative methods. Interviewers will not be involved in participants’ direct training to minimise social desirability bias.

Qualitative data will be analysed using the Nvivo software. Two researchers with qualitative research training will independently conduct data analysis using a five-step thematic analysis method: iteratively reading textual materials, identifying analysis units, extracting themes, coding and categorising. The researchers will then integrate their analyses through collaborative discussion.

Quality control

To maintain the quality and validity of the intervention, we will employ a series of quality control strategies: (1) Prevent contamination: each participant will be assigned a unique account and password to log in to the LLM-based ACP chatbot; (2) Standardised training: research assistants will undergo standardised training, including the recruitment process, the use of assessment tools and the provision of customer support, ensuring they will be equipped to promptly address any issues or questions that participants may encounter; (3) Improve participant engagement: the chatbot will provide weekly reminders, encouraging participants to log in to the system and engage with the intervention. In addition, the chatbot will automatically track participants’ login frequency and duration, offering rewards to incentivise sustained involvement.

Discussion

The incorporation of an LLM-based ACP chatbot into healthcare education offers a valuable approach for enhancing the competence of healthcare providers in the field of ACP. This protocol outlines an RCT designed to evaluate the impact of an LLM-based chatbot on improving healthcare providers’ ACP competence. The proposed intervention holds the potential to fill current educational gaps and contribute to the delivery of person-centred care.

A major strength of this study lies in the application of cutting-edge AI technology to deliver an interactive and personalised learning experience. Traditional methods of ACP education frequently lack the ability to provide real-time feedback and adjust to the specific learning requirements of individuals. In contrast, the LLM-based ACP chatbot can simulate realistic individual-provider conversations, offering healthcare providers a risk-free environment to practise and refine their ACP competence. This experiential learning approach aligns with adult learning,34 which highlights the value of active engagement and practical application in the development of knowledge and skills.

The scalability and widespread accessibility of the LLM-based ACP chatbot also offer significant advantages for broad implementation. By leveraging widely available digital platforms,35 the chatbot can be deployed to support asynchronous, on-demand ACP training, thereby overcoming common barriers such as geographical isolation, scheduling conflicts and limited access to expert facilitators. This feature is particularly valuable in the context of continuing professional development, as these constraints commonly restrict involvement in traditional educational initiatives.

This study, focusing on healthcare providers, reflects a strategic approach to address ACP’s unique challenges. First, healthcare providers are often the primary source of information and guidance for patients and families navigating healthcare decisions.4 36 The role of the healthcare provider is particularly critical in Chinese culture, where hierarchical relationships in healthcare, family-centredness and taboos on death influence these decisions.37 Enhancing the competence of healthcare providers, particularly in the context of these cultural dynamics, can improve the quality of ACP conversations and ultimately benefit patients. Second, prioritising training in healthcare providers will maximise the chatbot’s impact within the healthcare system.38 Third, healthcare providers are interested in getting further education and training in AI to improve clinical outcomes and workflow efficiencies.38

However, several limitations and challenges need to be acknowledged. First, a known limitation of LLMs is the potential for generating hallucinations35—plausible-sounding but inaccurate or fabricated information. This risk is particularly critical in the context of ACP, where trust, accuracy and clarity are essential. To address this, our study incorporates both human and automated evaluations to detect and mitigate hallucinations during the model development phase. In future deployment, user feedback loops will further enable ongoing monitoring and safety assurance.

In this study, the LLM-based ACP chatbot will act as an educational supplement rather than a replacement for human educators or patient interactions. It is essential to emphasise this distinction in order to maintain trust and clarify the intended use of the technology. While this study frames the LLM-based ACP chatbot as an educational intervention, data privacy remains a critical concern.18 To safeguard privacy, all data will be de-identified and securely stored on encrypted, access-controlled servers. To safeguard privacy, all data will be de-identified and securely stored on encrypted, access-controlled servers. No real patient information will be used, and all scenarios will be simulated. Access to the data will be limited to the individual users and the authorised research team. Participants will be fully informed of data use through a consent process aligned with both institutional ethics approval and relevant data protection laws.

If the intervention proves effective, it could be integrated into current educational frameworks to expand ACP training on a broader scale. Healthcare institutions could adopt the LLM-based ACP chatbot as part of continuing education. This could lead to a more widespread improvement in ACP practices, ultimately benefiting person-centred care by ensuring that end-of-life preferences are clearly understood and respected.

Future research could explore the long-term effects of such interventions on clinical practice and patients. Furthermore, adapting the LLM-based ACP chatbot to accommodate different specialties, cultural contexts or populations could expand its applicability. Additionally, investigating the integration of multimedia elements, such as virtual reality, could further enrich the interactive aspect of the learning experience.
