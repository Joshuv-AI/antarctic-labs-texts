Improving early initiation and exclusive breastfeeding practice through PDSA framework: the experience at a private hospital in Tanzania
Authors: Rukhsar Osman, Idda Katigula, Richard Charles, Agnes Ayubu, Paula Mkakilwa, Yaser Abdallah, Mariam Noorani
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionOptimal breastfeeding can save the lives of over 800 000 children annually. WHO recommends that all babies initiate breastfeeding within first hour of life, exclusively breastfeed for the first 6 months and continue breastfeeding up to 2 years and beyond. In Tanzania, 70% of babies initiate breastfeeding within first hour and only 64% of infants under 6 months are exclusively breastfed.ObjectiveImprove rate of early initiation and exclusive breastfeeding from birth to hospital discharge, with the aim of achieving 80% as per Baby Friendly Hospital Initiative standard through implementing quality improvement project.MethodsA quality improvement project was conducted using a Plan-Do-Study-Act (PDSA) framework. Baseline rates of early initiation and exclusive breastfeeding were determined. Sequential strategies were implemented including drafting and implementing hospital policy on breastfeeding and use of formula milk, staff training on lactation management and antenatal education of pregnant women using breastfeeding videos. These were followed by immediate skin-to-skin contact after caesarean birth and modification of newborn feeding charts. Subsequently, daily lactation rounds were implemented, and monthly antenatal classes were conducted for women identified as high risk for breastfeeding challenges. Progress was monitored through monthly audits.ResultsAfter three PDSA cycles, early breastfeeding initiation improved from 22% to 84% and exclusive breastfeeding rate increased from 45% to 90%.ConclusionEarly and exclusive breastfeeding rates in hospital can be improved by multidimensional approach including policies, staff training, antenatal education, interdepartmental involvement and having dedicated staff to support lactation.

Introduction

‘Breastfeeding is a mother’s gift to herself, her baby and the earth,’ as Wiggins quoted.1 Optimal breastfeeding can save the lives of over 800 000 children annually2 and has numerous recognised health benefits to the infant, the mother, the community and the environment. Breastfed infants have lower rates of neonatal sepsis, respiratory and gastrointestinal tract infections and improved neurodevelopmental outcomes,3 while breastfeeding mothers are at reduced risk of both breast and ovarian cancer, as well as decreased cardiovascular diseases and rheumatoid arthritis.3 Community and economic benefits of breastfeeding include lower overall expenditure on formula milk and healthcare, and decreased absenteeism from school and at work by children and parents, respectively.4 Lastly, breastmilk is natural and environmentally friendly reducing the energy, water and plastics required for production, packaging, transport and preparation of artificial milk.4

Given the benefits, WHO and United Nations International Children’s Emergency Fund (UNICEF) recommend that all babies initiate breastfeeding within 1 hour of life, exclusively breastfeed for the first 6 months and continue to breastfeed up to 2 years and beyond.5 To achieve that, The Baby Friendly Hospital Initiative (BFHI) was launched in 1991 to help promote, protect and support breastfeeding.6

The BFHI recommends immediate skin-to-skin contact between mother and baby after birth and early initiation of breastfeeding (EIBF) within 1 hour of birth.6 Early initiation not only increases the chances of a successful breastfeeding journey but also empowers mothers, enhances their parenting skills, allows the baby to relax as it transitions into the new environment and is an opportunity for emotional bonding between the mother and her child.7

Despite the evidence and policies, globally less than half of newborn babies are placed on the breast within the first hour of life; and only 48% of infants of 0–6 months of age are exclusively breastfed.8

It is well recognised that early breastfeeding (EBF) support can improve long-term breastfeeding outcomes.9 However, many challenges are faced in hospitals that hinder early and exclusive breastfeeding, including inadequate knowledge among the health workers to initiate and support EBF,10 poor follow-up on breastfeeding in the postnatal ward due to lack of sufficient staff, reliance on accompanying caregivers and prioritisation of emergencies.11

Tanzania adopted BFHI about 32 years ago, however, it is suboptimally adopted.12 In 2018, Tanzania scored 3/10 on BFHI uptake compared with score of 4.5/10 in 2015, with the number of certified Baby Friendly Hospitals dropping to just 11 health facilities.13 The national prevalence for exclusive breastfeeding in infants under 6 months is 64%, while EIBF is 70% which is lower than the set benchmark of 80%.14 While the national percentage of babies exclusively breastfed for the first 2 days after birth is 90.4%, it is the lowest in Dar es Salaam region at 82.4% in mainland Tanzania.14 Although there have been improvements in the indicators over the last 10 years, the slow progress could be explained by inadequate knowledge and skills among the healthworkers to initiate and support EBF.10

Focused quality improvement (QI) project can identify the gaps and overcome these barriers. Publications from various parts of the world have shown how this can be implemented with successful outcomes. A QI initiative by Takako et al in Japan resulted in threefold increase in breastmilk consumption rate,15 while in a hospital in India, EBF initiation rates improved to 95% while exclusive breastfeeding rates increased to 98%.16

In 2020, the exclusive breastfeeding rate from birth to discharge at our hospital was 45% while EIBF was less than 20%. These rates were far below the global targets set by WHO/UNICEF; therefore, we initiated a QI project in 2021 to improve the in-hospital breastfeeding outcomes.

Methodology

This quality project was initiated in January 2021 at the Aga Khan Hospital Dar es Salaam with the aim of improving breastfeeding from birth to discharge; and the target population included all mothers, and their newborns delivered within the facility. To implement this project, Plan-Do-Study-Act (PDSA) cycle model was used.

The Aga Khan Hospital is a tertiary level, private, not-for-profit hospital in Dar es Salaam that is Joint Commission International (JCI) accredited. Approximately, 165 deliveries happen every month, 60% of which are through caesarean section. Of the babies delivered, 15% are admitted to the neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) or neonatal high dependence unit (NHDU) for various reasons. Antenatal care clinics are obstetrician led and every high-risk delivery and caesarean section delivery is attended by a Neonatal Resuscitation Programme trained midwife and paediatric resident or resident medical officer (RMO), while women in labour and delivery are under 1 on one midwife care with obstetric residents and RMO’s available in case of emergencies. All the newborns are reviewed within an hour of delivery with a follow-up assessment at 6 hours of life by a paediatric resident or RMO, and a 24-hour assessment and daily newborn rounds by neonatologist. The hospital also admits newborns referred from other centres.

At the facility, nutrition of every newborn is monitored using feeding charts that have records for the time of first breastfeeding initiation, type of feed, daily weight and urine and stool output.

The team comprised of neonatologist, paediatrician who is also a board-certified lactation consultant (IBCLC), paediatric resident, obstetrician, nurse in charge at the obstetric and neonatal unit, nutritionist with specialised training in lactation, theatre manager and a pharmacist. Data collection was done by the in-charge nurse and paediatric resident.

PDSA cycle I: January 2021; policy formulation, antenatal education using videos and initiation of staff training. A hospital policy on breastfeeding and use of formula feeds was drafted, this mandated obtaining an informed consent from the parents in the instance where healthcare team deemed it necessary to start formula feeds. If formula feed was initiated at parents’ request despite education and lactation support, informed refusal form would be signed by parents. It also prohibited entry of any infant formula into the wards except that supplied or approved by the pharmacy. Pharmacy was made the centre for mixing and supplying formula feeds to the wards, on prescription order with a reason stated for the initiation of formula. Breastfeeding videos on EIBF, attaching baby to the breast and breastfeeding position from the global health media website,17 were projected in local Swahili language at the antenatal clinic waiting lounge, and a team of two doctors and four nurses were also trained in a 1-year online lactation programme on physiology, endocrinology, nutrition and pathology in breastfeeding, and breastfeeding skills and techniques.

By January 2022, the rate of EBF had increased from 45% to 70%. At this stage, a baseline assessment of EIBF was done and was found to be only 22% and all babies born by caesarean delivery had delayed initiation of breastfeeding. A review of the infant feeding records showed incomplete filling of the charts and a missing section on breastfeeding observation. About 50% of babies are delivered by caesarean section, this could have been a possible reason for such low EBF initiation rates at the facility.

PDSA cycle II: January 2022; early skin-to-skin and breastfeeding initiation within operating room and newborn feeding charts modification. Theatre nurses, anaesthesiologists, obstetricians and midwives were trained by the nutritionist and paediatrician on initiation of skin-to-skin within 1 hour of delivery. Immediate skin-to-skin contact and breastfeeding within the operating room under the observation and assistance of anaesthetic team was tried for mothers receiving spinal anaesthesia, it was found to be practical and did not interfere with the anaesthetic or obstetric procedures. For the mothers who underwent general anaesthesia, the skin-to-skin contact and breastfeeding were initiated in the theatre recovery room after they were fully conscious.

Existing newborn feeding chart was reviewed and modified to incorporate a section on breastfeeding observation. Midwives were encouraged to initiate breastfeeding early within labour suites; postnatal ward nurses and midwives were educated on correct utilisation of the feeding records and appropriate charting was enforced.

It was also noted that a large proportion of babies who were started on the infant formula were large for gestational age (LGA) babies and infants of diabetic mothers. It is a hospital policy to admit LGA babies and infants of diabetic mothers to the NHDU for observation and blood sugar monitoring; hence periodic transfer of these babies from NHDU to their mother’s bedside for breastfeeding was done instead of initiating them on infant formula, no baby sustained hypoglycaemia.

These interventions resulted in an increase in EBF to 77% and early initiation to 70% by January 2023. Analysis performed at this stage, established challenges with identifying mothers at high risk of having breastfeeding complications and insufficient breastfeeding support to the mothers in the postnatal ward.

PDSA cycle III: January 2023; daily postnatal lactation rounds, monthly lactation support classes. Obstetricians were engaged and educated on early identification of mothers at high risk of having breastfeeding challenges during antenatal clinics; monthly lactation support classes were then conducted with these mothers, and daily postnatal rounds were performed—by the nurses who graduated from the online lactation training programme—to support mothers with breastfeeding.

Results

Discussion

This quality project demonstrates that breastfeeding outcomes in hospitals can be improved using a PDSA approach. The gradual attainment of the set targets that was possible through this quality project shows that multidiciplinary involvement, staff training and leadership support and continuous monitoring of results are important for successful breastfeeding. The role of the postpartum environment in successful breastfeeding cannot be overemphasised as highlighted in one of the Lancet series quote ‘…universal access to improved breastfeeding-supportive maternity care, evidence-based breastfeeding counselling, and public and health worker education are crucial for preventing common early lactation problems…’.18

Implementing hospital breastfeeding policies and structured programmes has been associated with improved early initiation and in-hospital exclusive breastfeeding rates.19 20 Comparably, we realised a close to doubling rate of exclusive breastfeeding with having a policy in place along with antenatal training. However, results still were not impressive with EBF rates ranging between 60% and 70%. To improve these rates, early skin-to-skin contact and breastfeeding within the operating rooms were implemented.

Early skin-to-skin care has been associated with increased rate of early initiation and exclusive breastfeeding. When we implemented it among mothers who delivered by caesarean section our initiation rate improved from 22% to 70% and our EBF rate improved to 80%. Studies by Sankalp et al and Daniya et al similarly showed improved EIBF rates after caesarean deliveries from 0% to 80% and 0% to 93%, respectively; however, these studies did not look at the effect on EBF rates.21 22

While there was a constant improvement in the EBF rates, a drop of about 5% occurred in December every year, as seen figure 3. This can be explained by interruption in daily lactation rounds and support to the breastfeeding mothers as a result of smaller lactation team being available during that period.

Rooming-in is one among the 10 BFHI steps to support breastfeeding; however, not all babies can be successfully roomed-in because they require nursery admission, and this negatively impacts EBF. A similar finding was noted in our LGA babies who were admitted to the NHDU for blood sugar monitoring because of the hypoglycaemia risk. Prior to PDSA II, 100% of the LGA babies were initiated on infant formula; however, following the introduction of separate care (the baby is either brought to the mother for breastfeeding or she walks to the nursery) the formula rate prescription in these babies dropped to 28%. We could not find any published literature to compare our studies to; however, study by Sharifa et al looking at effects of rooming‐in versus separate care for increasing the duration of breastfeeding could not find sufficient evidence to support or reject either of the practises.23

Despite being a private tertiary centre, we had a shortage of staff skilled in lactation support which contributed to delayed initiation of breastfeeding, poor breastfeeding follow-up after postnatal counselling which ultimately resulted in increased formula milk prescription. Staffing shortages resulting in suboptimal breastfeeding support is a common challenge faced in many low-income and middle-income countries.11 24 25 This challenge was overcome by starting daily lactation rounds in the second year of the project by the nurses who underwent an online training programme in lactation management as seen in figure 5. Systemic reviews and meta-analysis have shown that educational interventions improve staff knowledge, attitudes and compliance towards BFHI guidelines and contribute towards longer EBF duration and fewer infant formula prescriptions.26 27

Progress towards the target was challenging, as it required introducing a new policy, educating staff and changing perceptions among those working in neonatal and postnatal wards as well as operating theatres. Furthermore, 45% of HIV-exposed infants are initiated on infant formula, for various reasons.

QI undertakings aim at improving the quality of healthcare delivered, and several published breastfeeding QI projects have demonstrated positive results with similar interventions as ours; however, these interventions were done in preterm and low birthweight babies admitted to NICU.28 While our project adds to the list of those successful initiatives, it also showcases the successful initiation of early skin-to-skin and breastfeeding within operating rooms, which has been described previously from India.21 22 Whereas the Indian QI projects focused only on the newborns whose mothers had undergone spinal anaesthesia, this project focused on all stable newborns ≥35 weeks irrespective of the type of anaesthesia; this was achieved by initiating skin-to-skin and breastfeeding within theatre recovery once mothers were conscious.

Monitoring rates of EBF initiation and exclusive breastfeeding from birth to hospital discharge is conducted monthly after PDSA cycle 3; these rates have consistently been sustained above BFHI benchmark of 80%. However, we did not monitor the HIV transmission rates among HIV-exposed newborns who were exclusively breastfed, this should be considered in similar projects.

Conclusion

Exclusive breastfeeding rates in hospital can be improved by multidimensional approach including policies, staff training and antenatal education, interdepartmental involvement and having dedicated staff to support lactation. Similar QI approaches in other hospitals can be done which will identify the existing challenges and tailored interventions can be implemented. However, this project was done at a single private hospital that has JCI accreditation, all interventions may not be feasible in other settings. Staff training is the cornerstone of success and a nationwide training programme adapted from the BFHI materials can establish a cadre of trained lactation experts. Revamping the BFHI certification nationally will also motivate institutional leaders to invest in breastfeeding activities at their facilities.

To sustain the gains made in hospital, the next step will be to establish community support groups which will provide ongoing support once mothers and babies are discharged in keeping with the step 10 of the BFHI.
