Prevalence of polypharmacy among older adults with diabetes: A systematic review and Meta-Analysis
Authors: Prakasini Satapathy, Abhay M. Gaidhane, Nasir Vadia, Soumya V. Menon, Kattela Chennakesavulu, Rajashree Panigrahi, Muhammed Shabil, Mahendra Singh, Sanjit Sah, Doddolla Lingamaiah, S. Govinda Rao, Khang Wen Goh, Edward Mawejje, Ganesh Bushi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectivePolypharmacy, the concurrent use of five or more medications, is prevalent among older adults with Diabetes, a population at elevated risk for medication-related complications. Complex treatment regimens for Diabetes and its comorbidities exacerbate challenges such as medication non-adherence, drug interactions, adverse events, and increased hospitalization risk. This review aimed to estimate the prevalence of polypharmacy among older adults with Diabetes.MethodFollowing PRISMA 2020 guidelines, we performed a systematic search of PubMed, Embase, and Web of Science. Screened articles using Nested Knowledge software. Eligible studies included those aged 60 years or older with Diabetes, reporting on the prevalence of polypharmacy. Study quality was assessed using a modified Newcastle-Ottawa Scale, and meta-analysis was performed using random-effects models in R software version 4.4.1. Publication bias was indicated by a Doi plot with an LFK index.ResultsThe pooled prevalence of polypharmacy was 59% (95% CI, 48%-70%). Subgroup analyses showed a prevalence of 55% in retrospective studies and 62% in cross-sectional studies. Sensitivity analyses confirmed the stability of the results. Significant geographic variability was noted, with lower prevalence in high-income countries compared to regions in Southern Europe and Asia.ConclusionsPolypharmacy is highly prevalent among older adults with Diabetes, with significant variability across study designs and geographic regions. Targeted interventions and further research are essential to address its associated risks and optimize medication management.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40520-025-03240-z.

Introduction

Polypharmacy, generally defined as the concurrent use of five or more medications, has become a pervasive issue among older adults, particularly those with chronic conditions such as Diabetes mellitus [1, 2]. As the global population ages, the incidence of polypharmacy has risen, especially among older adults who often experience multiple comorbidities requiring complex treatment regimens [3]. Diabetes mellitus, a prevalent and chronic disease among the elderly, often necessitates the use of multiple medications, including oral hypoglycaemic agents, insulin, and drugs to manage associated comorbidities such as hypertension, dyslipidaemia, and cardiovascular disease [4, 5]. While pharmacological interventions are essential for managing Diabetes and its complications, polypharmacy in this population is associated with several risks, including medication non-adherence, drug-drug interactions, adverse drug reactions, and an increased risk of hospitalization. The combination of these factors can exacerbate health disparities and negatively impact the overall health and quality of life of older adults [6–8].

Older adults with Diabetes are especially vulnerable to the negative effects of polypharmacy. Age-related changes in pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics such as altered drug absorption, metabolism, and renal clearance further increase the risk of drug toxicity and adverse reactions [6, 9, 10]. Consequently, while the goal of polypharmacy in this population is to improve health outcomes, inappropriate medication regimens may do the opposite, leading to adverse effects such as hypoglycaemia, polypharmacy-induced hospitalizations, and a decline in functional status [11, 12]. In this context, understanding the prevalence of polypharmacy in older adults with Diabetes is essential for developing strategies to minimize its risks and optimize pharmacological treatments.

Despite the growing recognition of polypharmacy as a critical issue in older adults with Diabetes, the literature on its prevalence remains inconsistent. Existing studies often report varying estimates of polypharmacy rates, influenced by factors such as differences in study design, populations, and the definitions used for polypharmacy [11, 13]. Some studies focus on the number of prescribed medications, while others include over-the-counter drugs, further complicating efforts to standardize prevalence estimates [14]. Moreover, many studies suffer from methodological limitations, such as small sample sizes, lack of adjustment for confounding factors (e.g., multi-morbidity), and regional or demographic biases. These discrepancies in the literature contribute to an incomplete understanding of the extent of polypharmacy among older adults with Diabetes, as well as the factors influencing its occurrence.

The objective of this systematic review and meta-analysis is to estimate the global prevalence of polypharmacy among older adults with diabetes and to synthesize existing evidence. Findings from this study will address a critical gap in understanding polypharmacy in this population and provide insights to inform clinical practice, policy development, and research.

Methods

This meta-analysis and systematic review adhered to the PRISMA 2020 guidelines [15] (Table S1). The protocol was registered with the PROSPERO (CRD42024618191). The Nested Knowledge software platform was employed to streamline and standardize screening, data extraction, and analysis.

Eligibility criteria

The review included studies of adults aged 60 years or older with a confirmed diagnosis of diabetes mellitus. The cutoff of 60 years was chosen because several included studies and World Health Organization guidelines define older persons as individuals aged 60 years and above, particularly in low- and middle-income country contexts [16]. Eligible studies were required to report the prevalence of polypharmacy, defined as the concurrent use of 5 or more medications, and to employ a quantitative design (cross-sectional, cohort, or case-control). Only full-text articles published in English were included. Studies were excluded if they focused on non-geriatric populations; were qualitative in nature (e.g., letters, commentaries, or abstracts-only publications); or were case series, case reports, reviews, or discussion papers. Articles with unavailable full texts despite retrieval efforts were also excluded (Table S2).

Literature search

The literature search was conducted using three electronic databases: PubMed, Embase, and Web of Science. Keywords and controlled vocabulary terms related to polypharmacy (e.g., “polypharmacy,” “drug combinations,” “drug interactions”), Diabetes (e.g., “Diabetes mellitus,” “Diabetes”), and older adults (e.g., “aged,” “geriatric,” “elderly,” “older adults”) were used. Boolean operators were applied to optimize the search strategy, and the search included all available records through November 2024. Additional relevant studies were identified by reviewing the reference lists of included articles (Table S3).

Screening and data extraction

Screening and data extraction were conducted systematically using Nested Knowledge software. After removing duplicates, two independent reviewers assessed titles and abstracts for relevance, followed by a full-text review of potentially eligible studies based on predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Discrepancies during screening or data extraction were resolved through discussion, with a third reviewer consulted if consensus was not achieved. Extracted data included study characteristics (e.g., author, publication year, study design, and location), participant demographics (e.g., sample size, mean age, and comorbidities), and outcome measures (e.g., prevalence of polypharmacy and its operational definition). All data were organized into structured tables to ensure consistency and facilitate analysis.

Quality assessment

The quality of the included studies was assessed using a modified Newcastle-Ottawa Scale [17], evaluating representativeness of the study population, adequacy of sample size, clarity and consistency in the definition of polypharmacy, and reliability of outcome ascertainment and reporting. Each study received a score ranging from 0 to 6, with higher scores indicating stronger methodological quality. Studies with lower quality scores were flagged for sensitivity analyses to evaluate their potential impact on the pooled prevalence estimates.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using R software version 4.4.0 with the “meta” and “metafor” packages. A random-effects model was used to calculate the pooled prevalence of polypharmacy, presented as proportions with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) [17]. Logit-transformed proportions were applied to stabilize variance and normalize the data, with back-transformed results for interpretation. Forest plots were created to summarize individual study estimates and the pooled prevalence.

Heterogeneity was assessed using the I² statistic, with thresholds of 25%, 50%, and 75% representing low, moderate, and high heterogeneity, respectively. Prediction intervals were calculated to estimate the expected range of prevalence in future populations. Subgroup analyses examined sources of heterogeneity based on factors such as study design and geographic region. Sensitivity analyses, including leave-one-out methods, were conducted to assess result stability. Publication bias was evaluated using Doi plots and quantified with the Luis Furuya-Kanamori index, with values exceeding ± 2 indicating major asymmetry [18, 19].

Results

Literature search

A total of 6,614 records were identified through database searches, including 3,889 from Embase, 1,749 from PubMed, and 976 from Web of Science (Fig. 1). After removing 1,877 duplicate records, 4,737 titles and abstracts were screened for relevance. Of these, 4,622 records were excluded, including 3,621 that were not relevant, 67 animal studies, 946 reviews, and 38 guidelines. Full texts of 115 articles were retrieved for further assessment, and 65 were evaluated for eligibility. Ultimately, 44 studies were excluded for lacking relevant outcomes, leaving 21 studies included in the final review.

Summary of study characteristics

The review included 21 studies conducted across various regions, including North America, Europe, Asia, and South America. Most studies employed cross-sectional (n = 11) or retrospective (n = 8) designs, with one prospective study. Sample sizes ranged from 127 to 202,496 participants (Table 1). The mean age of participants varied, with most studies focusing on individuals aged ≥ 65 years. Studies were conducted in diverse healthcare contexts, with notable contributions from the United States, Canada, Europe, and Asia. This broad representation highlights the global burden of polypharmacy in older adults with Diabetes. The quality of the included studies, was moderate to high. Most studies scored well on representativeness of the population and outcome assessment but varied in sample size adequacy and clarity of polypharmacy definition. A substantial proportion of the included studies were retrospective in design, which may limit methodological rigor compared with prospective approaches. Summary of study characteristics

Meta-analysis

A total of 21 studies involving 520,956 participants were analyzed to estimate the prevalence of polypharmacy among older adults with diabetes. The pooled prevalence was 59% (95% CI, 48%–70%). Significant heterogeneity was observed (I² = 100%). Individual study prevalence estimates ranged from 6% (95% CI, 6%–6%) in Raval 2015 [20] to 88% (95% CI, 81%−93%) in Geitona 2016 [21] (Fig. 2). A prediction interval of 14%–93% was calculated. Studies with larger sample sizes, such as Raval 2015 [20] and Gagnon 2019 [22], carried more weight in the meta-analysis, whereas smaller studies contributed to increased variability.

Sensitivity analysis

The leave-one-out sensitivity analysis confirmed the stability of the pooled prevalence estimate at 59.1% (95% CI, 47.5%−69.8%) (Fig. 3). Excluding individual studies resulted in prevalence estimates ranging from 57.3% to 62.7%, all within the original confidence interval. Omission of larger studies, such as Raval 2015, slightly reduced heterogeneity (I² = 99.8%) but did not alter the overall findings. These results demonstrate that no single study significantly influenced the pooled estimate, supporting the reliability of the analysis despite high heterogeneity.

Subgroup analysis based on study design

To explore potential sources of heterogeneity, a subgroup analysis was performed based on study design (Fig. 4). The pooled prevalence of polypharmacy was 55% (95% CI, 27%−80%) across 8 retrospective studies involving 483,386 participants, and 62% (95% CI, 51%−72%) among 11 cross-sectional studies with 36,987 participants. A single prospective study reported a prevalence of 57% (95% CI, 48%−66%) in 133 participants. High heterogeneity persisted within both retrospective studies (I² = 100%) and cross-sectional studies (I² = 99.4%), reflecting variability in populations, healthcare settings, and methodological approaches. The findings indicate differences in prevalence estimates by study design while emphasizing the variability observed in reported outcomes.

Publication bias

The Doi plot shows substantial asymmetry with an LFK index of 2.31, indicating potential publication bias (Fig. 5). The uneven distribution of data points suggests an overrepresentation of studies with extreme prevalence estimates of polypharmacy. The degree of asymmetry warranting caution in interpreting the pooled prevalence results.

Discussion

This systematic review assessed the prevalence of polypharmacy among older adults with diabetes from 21 studies involving 520,956 participants. The pooled prevalence was 59% (95% CI, 48%−70%), reflecting the substantial medication burden in this population. Individual study prevalence estimates ranged from 6% to 88%, with significant heterogeneity (I² = 100%). Subgroup analyses revealed prevalence rates of 55% in retrospective studies and 62% in cross-sectional studies, with sensitivity analyses confirming the stability of the pooled estimates. The Doi plot (LFK index = 2.31) suggested potential publication bias, necessitating cautious interpretation of the results.

The reported prevalence of polypharmacy aligns with prior findings in older adults with Diabetes and multi-morbidity, where multiple medications are frequently required to manage Diabetes and associated conditions such as cardiovascular disease, hypertension, and dyslipidaemia [23, 24]. A systematic review similarly highlighted high polypharmacy rates in this population, driven by the complexities of managing comorbidities [25]. Polypharmacy has been linked to increased healthcare utilization, adverse drug events, and non-adherence, significantly elevating risks of hospitalization and mortality [26, 27]. One study in our review, conducted by Raval et al. (2015), reported a markedly lower prevalence of polypharmacy compared with other included studies. This study was a large retrospective analysis of Medicare beneficiaries in the United States, focusing primarily on 30-day hospital readmissions among elderly patients with type 2 diabetes. Polypharmacy was assessed as a secondary outcome using administrative claims data from the Humana Medicare Advantage Prescription Drug plan, covering more than 200,000 beneficiaries (mean age, 75.8 years). Because the analysis relied on claims data, over-the-counter medications and some prescriptions may not have been captured, leading to an underestimation of polypharmacy prevalence [20].

The wide prediction interval (14%–93%) reflects the substantial variability in prevalence estimates that could be expected in future studies. Importantly, sensitivity analyses indicated that no single study disproportionately influenced the pooled estimate, supporting the robustness of our findings despite the high heterogeneity observed. Differences in prevalence estimates were influenced by variability in populations, healthcare settings, and methodological approaches. For example, retrospective studies relying on administrative data tended to report lower prevalence, while cross-sectional studies in hospital-based settings reported higher rates, highlighting the impact of study design on reported outcomes.

Variability in polypharmacy prevalence reflects methodological and geographic differences. Retrospective studies relying on administrative data may underreport prevalence by excluding over-the-counter medications, while cross-sectional studies in hospital-based settings often report higher rates due to the inclusion of patients with severe comorbidities [28, 29]. Geographic disparities were also notable, with lower prevalence reported in high-income countries like the United States and Canada compared to developing regions. For example, a Canadian cohort study found a prevalence of 35%, significantly lower than estimates from Southern Europe and Asia, where systemic and cultural differences in prescribing practices likely contribute to higher medication use [30, 31]. These findings highlight the need for standardized definitions and harmonized methodologies to improve comparability and advance the global understanding of polypharmacy patterns.

These findings feature critical clinical and theoretical challenges in managing older adults with Diabetes. Polypharmacy is strongly linked to adverse drug events, including hypoglycaemia, falls, cognitive decline, and hospitalizations, exacerbated by age-related changes in pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics [32–34]. Diabetes further amplifies these risks due to the frequent use of medications requiring precise dosing, such as insulin and oral hypoglycaemic agents, which complicate adherence and elevate the risk of complications like retinopathy and neuropathy [35].

Targeted interventions, including medication reviews and deprescribing protocols, are vital for mitigating polypharmacy risks. Pharmacist-led strategies have demonstrated efficacy in reducing inappropriate prescriptions and improving outcomes, particularly when integrated into primary and outpatient care settings [36]. Multidisciplinary models involving pharmacists, physicians, and Diabetes educators are especially effective in managing complex regimens. The prevalence of polypharmacy also reflects systemic issues in multi-morbidity management, where disease-focused guidelines often prioritize individual metrics over holistic care [37]. A shift toward patient-centered approaches that emphasize quality of life and functional outcomes is essential to balance the benefits and harms of treatment, particularly in older adults with Diabetes.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, heterogeneity was high (I² = 100%), reflecting differences in populations, settings, and definitions of polypharmacy. Although subgroup and sensitivity analyses addressed some sources of variability, substantial residual heterogeneity remained. The Doi plot (LFK index = 2.31) suggested potential publication bias, possibly overrepresenting studies with extreme estimates. Second, definitions of polypharmacy varied; some studies included over-the-counter medications, whereas others focused only on prescription drugs, complicating comparisons. Third, although overall study quality was moderate to high, many studies were retrospective, raising concerns about incomplete data capture and misclassification, which may have affected prevalence estimates and contributed to heterogeneity. The reliance on cross-sectional and retrospective designs also limited causal inference. Fourth, geographic representation was uneven, with minimal data from Africa and South America, limiting global applicability. Fifth, most studies did not provide information on the specific drug classes contributing to polypharmacy, restricting our ability to identify prescribing patterns or assess the contribution of particular categories (e.g., cardiovascular, antidiabetic, psychotropic agents). Future research should include drug-specific data to better characterize prescribing practices and evaluate their clinical implications. Sixth, meta-regression analyses could not be performed to examine heterogeneity sources such as mean age, publication year, or geographic region because of the limited number of studies in these subgroups. Finally, functional performance measures such as activities of daily living (ADL), instrumental activities of daily living (IADL), or cognitive assessments (e.g., MMSE) were not reported in most studies. As a result, we could not evaluate the relationship between polypharmacy and functional status. Differences in study settings and operational definitions of polypharmacy likely contributed to the heterogeneity observed in our analysis. While most studies defined polypharmacy as the concurrent use of ≥ 5 medications, some applied alternative thresholds or included only prescription drugs, whereas others also considered over-the-counter medications. In addition, hospital-based studies often reported higher prevalence than community-based studies, reflecting differences in disease severity and prescribing practices.

Future research must prioritize the development of standardized definitions of polypharmacy to enhance methodological consistency and facilitate comparability across studies. Longitudinal studies are critical to elucidate the long-term impacts of polypharmacy on clinical outcomes in older adults with Diabetes, particularly concerning its role in morbidity, mortality, and quality of life. Rigorous interventional research is needed to evaluate the efficacy of deprescribing protocols, pharmacist-led medication reviews, and integrated care models in mitigating the risks associated with polypharmacy. Furthermore, expanding investigations into underrepresented regions is essential for capturing global variability and informing culturally and contextually tailored strategies for optimizing medication use and minimizing adverse outcomes.

Conclusion

Polypharmacy is prevalent among older adults with Diabetes, influenced by variations in study design, geographic location, and definitions. The associated risks, including adverse drug events and complications from multimorbidity, are significant. Key strategies such as medication reviews, deprescribing initiatives, and multidisciplinary care are essential to optimize therapeutic outcomes. Addressing methodological inconsistencies and geographic disparities in research is critical for refining clinical approaches and improving patient care. These findings highlight the need for evidence-based interventions to mitigate polypharmacy risks and improve outcomes in this vulnerable population.
