Complications After Cytoreductive Surgery and Hyperthermic Intraperitoneal Chemotherapy ‐ Can We Do Better?
Authors: Miguel E. Alberto V., Daniela Zuluaga, Axel Winter, Johann Pratschke, Beate Rau, Safak Gül
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe main aim of this review is to analyze postoperative complications after cytoreductive surgery and intraperitoneal chemotherapy. Although the literature in this field is ever growing, reporting on complication is not a given. Nevertheless, some main risk factors such as prior surgery, high tumor burden and the center's expertize have an impact on patient outcomes. Transparency is key, if we want to continue to improve.

Introduction

Peritoneal surface malignancies (PSM) have a vastly different prognosis depending on its origin. Whilst metastatic disease to the peritoneum from colorectal, gastric or ovarian cancers have dire prognoses, mesothelioma and pseudomyxoma peritonei can be cured. Cytoreductive surgery (CRS) and hyperthermic intraperitoneal chemotherapy (HIPEC) have been the surgical answer to this growing problem, by showing its benefits in oncological outcomes. Nevertheless, it comes at a cost, since CRS and HIPEC is an extensive and aggressive treatment for fairly frail oncologic patients, and as such adverse events are second to the demanding surgical technique needed to reach complete cytoreduction. The reduction of postoperative complications and, should they occur, their optimal treatment must be of utmost importance for PSM surgeons because the benefit of radical surgery (sometimes marginal) may be lost to these adverse events [1]. Major morbidity after CRS and HIPEC indirectly affects overall (OS) and disease‐free survival (DFS) because initiation of postoperative adjuvant therapy may be delayed or not even initiated. The goal of this manuscript is to scrutinize our current literature regarding postoperative complications, raise awareness of where we need to get better and call attention to shortcomings in the reporting of postoperative morbidity and mortality.

Methods

A literature search in MEDLINE and Embase was performed to select papers in which postoperative complications after CRS and HIPEC were documented. The decision to only search in these two databases stems from the need to isolate peer‐reviewed studies with the highest quality possible. We included meta‐analysis, systemic reviews, randomized clinical trials (RCT) and original papers as long as postoperative complications were reported. The search period was from January 2014 to April 2024. Nevertheless, because of the lack of information available some exceptions were made, and earlier publications were taken into consideration. We used specific search queries for each of the databases that is, MeSH Terms for MEDLINE, to thereby select all publications indexed under these terms. We included publications with abstracts in English, German or Spanish. From all publications found, we proceeded to exclude all studies that did not report on postoperative complications. Publications using the term “Debulking Surgery” without the use of intraperitoneal chemotherapy were also excluded. Next, for each selected paper the following information was extracted: bibliographic data, survival analysis (overall survival, DFS, recurrence free survival), morbidity, mortality, type of postoperative complication, completeness of cytoreduction (CCR‐Score) and the details of the intraperitoneal chemotherapy (HIPEC, drug, dosage and duration). Publications regarding exclusively early postoperative intraperitoneal chemotherapy were also excluded.

Results

Postoperative Complications According to Entity

Colorectal Cancer

In 2017, Huang et al. [16] published a very important systematic review with meta‐analysis of the literature available at that time regarding CRS and HIPEC and its impact in survival in peritoneal metastasis from colorectal cancer. Herein they reported mean morbidity rates in 19.8% of patients in HIPEC groups and 20.5% in traditional groups, with mortality being 4.5% and 6.3% respectively. Meaning that no significant mortality or morbidity was detected amongst both groups (p = 0.815). The PRODIGE 7 [12] trial was published in 2021 by Quenet et al. In this much discussed trial one group receives HIPEC and the second one CRS alone. The authors decided to record their postoperative complications in two different timepoints, 30‐ and 60‐days post intervention. At 30 days, 42% patients in the HIPEC group and 32% CRS alone group had CTCAE grade 3 or worse complications. After 60 days, 26% of patients in the surgery plus HIPEC group had severe complications (including 27 patients who had also had severe complications within 30 days and in the CRS alone group 15% of patients (including 14 who had also had severe complications at Day 30). At this second timepoint, complications were significantly more common in the HIPEC group than in the CRS alone group (p = 0.035). The most common intraabdominal complications were gastrointestinal fistulae (11% in the HIPEC group vs. 7% in the CRS alone group) and abscesses (5% vs. 3%, respectively) [75]. Another good data source regarding postoperative complications is provided in the multicentric Dutch CAIRO6 randomized control trial published in 2021 by Rovers et al., [7] where safety and feasibility of perioperative systemic therapy was compared to CRS and HIPEC alone. Firstly, in the 79‐patient cohort there was no statistically significant difference in any CD grade 2 or higher postoperative morbidity between both groups. They reported a 28% and 6% incidence of CD grade 3 and 4 complications, respectively with no perioperative mortality. Most common complications were anastomotic leakage (12% of patients with bowel anastomosis), intraabdominal abscess (6%) and fascia dehiscence, ileus and gastroparesis (4% each). Nineteen percent of patients needed a reoperation. The most common systemic therapy‐related toxic effects were of gastrointestinal origin: ileus and diarrhea (8% each, CTCAE grade ≥ 3), and nausea/vomiting (5%, CTCAE grade ≥ 3). Colleagues Lundy et al. [14] published in 2022 a 29.4% morbidity rate (CD ≥ 3, 70.6% CD 0–2) from their 30‐year single center experience. In Norway, Frøysnes et al. [8] reported 18 patients (ca. 15%) with CD > 3 complications with no mortality after 100 days in a national cohort analysis. Most complications were intraabdominal abscesses and three fistulae and anastomotic leak (AL) each.

The involvement of urological resections significantly affects overall postoperative complications as shown by Braam et al. [5] Patients with an urological procedure needed more rectosigmoid resections, partiall small bowel resections and hysterectomies and yet the most common complications remained gastrointestinal leakages and intraabdominal abscesses. Furthermore, 47% of the patients in the urological procedure group had complications higher than CD grade 3a compared to 20% (OR = 3.58, 95% CI = 1.75–7.31; p < 0.001) and 11% of the patients (4) had a urin leakage versus one patient in the control group (0.4%) which showed, as expected, a statistical significance (p < 0.001). A second study [72] involving urological procedures concludes that although genitourinary involvement is associated with more extensive disease and therefore extensive surgery, this did not affect postoperative oncologic outcomes. Leapman et al. [72] only report persistent urologic sequalae in 14.7% of their patients but no overall morbidity.

In 2019, a great international multicentric collaboration reported on iterative CRS (iCRS) and HIPEC. Also here, the prevalence of CD grade 3 and 4 complications was 23.4%. These patients also had a significantly reduced OS (18.9 vs. 30.6 months; p = 0.002) [10]. The presence of mutiple metastatic disease (i.e., liver and peritoneum or lung and peritoneum) does not neceserily means higher postoperative morbidity as demostrated in the following monocentric studies. Lorimiert et al. [11] published no statiscally significant difference between global and major morbidity after CRS and HIPEC in patients with peritoneal metastasis only or with peritoneal and liver metastasis (45.4% vs. 33.3%, p = 0.19). Dominant complications were SSI‐3 and gastroinstestinal fistulae, followed by intraabdominal hemorrhages. Ninety‐day mortality from surgical complications was 6.8% for the whole cohort. They also demonstrated how grade 3 and 4 morbidity was a predictive factor for poor overall survival (OS) [HR: 2.1, 95% CI: 1.06–4.14, p = 0.03] [11].

The group of Chang et al. [15] in China published their experience in open versus laparoscopic CRS (l‐CRS) and HIPEC. The overall morbidity in both groups was not significantly different, nevertheless surgical postoperative complications where lower in the laparoscopic group (14.7% vs. 38.2%, p = 0.028). A single CD grade 3 or higher was documented in the L‐CRS and HIPEC group (intra‐abdominal abscess) whereas four patients had postoperative complications rated 3 or higher in the open group: two intra‐abdominal abscess, 1 empyema and 1 wound dehiscence), all the while achieving a complete resection in 89% of the cases [15].

Regarding mortality, a study published in 2016 by Simkens et al. [6] raises a valid point denoting how our current parameters underestimate treatment related mortality. In the study's 245 patient cohort, 13.9% of patients died within 12 months of the procedure, whereas the 30‐day and in‐hospital rates were 1.6% and 2.4%, respectively. Furthermore, the main cause of 1‐year mortality after CRS and HIPEC appeared to be early recurrence (7.3%) and not treatment‐related complications (4.9%) [6]. Even after iCRS, 90‐day mortality was reported at 2.55% and a hospital mortality rate of 1.7% (combined cohort of 231 patients) [10]. In the PRODIGE 7 [75] trial, treatment‐associated mortality rate was 3% in the CRS and HIPEC cohort and 2% in the CRS alone, for a total of seven patients.

Gastric Cancer

The indication of CRS and HIPEC for gastric cancer is reserved for extremely well selected patients due to nonsuperiority over standard of care. Studies to contradict this notion are scarce and difficult to carry out and in turn data on postoperative complications is scarce. The NCI'S GYMSSA Trial published in 2014 by Rudloff et al. [76] was one of the first prospective randomized trials comparing systemic therapy versus CRS + HIPEC and systemic therapy. Only 17 patients were enrolled in the study and yet the results were somewhat astonishing with OS in the multimodal treatment arm of 11.3 versus 4.3 months. Nevertheless, it was clear that the cost would be high perioperative morbidity with “major surgical complications” being present in eight out nine patients of patients, and four needing a reoperation due to anastomotic leaks or intraperitoneal hemorrhage. Interestingly, 30‐day mortality remained 0% and after 90 days only one patient had died (due to the complications following the hemorrhagic shock).

Rau et al. [33] conclude in their GASTRIPEC‐I RCT published in 2023, among other things, that HIPEC with cisplatin and mitomycin C (MMC) added no major comorbidity to CRS and thus deeming it safe and feasible, and specially leaving the debate open for its palliative benefits. With an OS of 14,9 months for both groups (CRS ± HIPEC) 1.7% of patients had a bleeding complication and 6.9% an anastomotic leak. The most common complication was neutropenia, reported in 20% of the patients. The mortality rate was 2.6% (one patient in the CRS + HIPEC group) associated with renal insufficiency. Some similarities could be drawn with to a prospective non randomized study from Spain by Rihuete Caro et al. [77] in 2018. Instead of MMC, doxorubicin was used in combination with cisplatin. Thirty‐five patients got CRS and HIPEC at their center and reported serious adverse events (described by the group as CD ≥ 3b) of 20% with a 90‐day mortality of 5.7%. Their most frequent reported complication being anastomotic leak in 4 (11,4%) patients. A year later, in 2019, Manzanedo et al. [29] published a “Multicenter Study of (the) Spanish Group of Peritoneal Oncologic Surgery (GECOP)”. This was a retrospective study from a prospective national database containing 88 patients from seven institutions. With maximal radicality (CC‐0) in 90.9% of the cases, 31% of patients had a CD grade 3 or 4 complication with 90‐day mortality at 3.4%.

The same research question was asked by the FREGATE and BIG‐RENAPE Networks in France but with a different study setting. Bonnot et al. [78] published in 2019 a multicentric observational study adjusting for HIPEC with inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) based on propensity score concluded that CRS + HIPEC improved both OS (18.6 vs. 11.4 months p = 0.002) and RFS (11.6 vs. 7.6 months p = 0.001) even with higher PCI scores (median PCI 2 vs. 6 p < 0.001), without significant differences in morbidity and mortality. A total of 252 patients were reported (162 CRS + HIPEC vs. 90 CRS alone) with an overall CD > 3 complication rate of 54.3% and no statistical difference amongst both groups. The most common complication was deep abscess/peritonitis (overall 21.7%) closely followed by anastomotic leakage with a 21.3% incidence. Reoperation before discharge was needed in 26.1% of the cases and 15.8% needed a radiological intervention (drainage or embolization). The most common medical complications were pleural effusion (15.2% ‐ overall respiratory complications 23.4%), prolonged anorexia after 3 months (15.8% ‐ overall GI toxicities 20.5%) and anemia requiring a blood transfusion (14.8% ‐ overall hematologic toxicities 19.3%). The 30‐day mortality was reported at 2.5% for the CRS + HIPEC group and at 4.5% for the CRS alone group (overall 3.2% ‐ 8 patients). We would like to commend the group for their exceptional transparency and reporting of medical and surgical adverse events.

In 2017, Fugazzola et al. [25] used paclitaxel in most patients as an intraperitoneal agent with cisplatin. Seventeen patients underwent the procedure and had median OS rates of 16 months for synchronously metastasized (SPC) gastric cancer versus 6 months for metachronous metastasis (MPC). Postoperative morbidity was reported using CTCAE and major morbidity was categorized as CTCAE > 2. All patients in the SPC group and 61% had postoperative complications, with no statistical difference amongst both groups. The most frequent complications were sepsis (23.5%) and, abdominal abscess and anastomotic leak (17.6% each). Recently, Buckarma et al. [39] published a retrospective cohort study also from a single institution sharing the same regime of cisplatin and paclitaxel. Twenty‐two patients underwent the procedure, of which 11% had a complication graded CD ≥ 3 within 30 days and 27% after 90 days. The rate of anastomotic leak was 14%. One patient (5%) hat experienced a CTCAE grade 4 cytopenia and another an acute kidney injury.

Aiming for a most robust cohort for data interpretation, researchers from the NCI and MD Anderson pooled 41 patients in an analysis of their to phase II Trials [38]. Green and colleagues report major postoperative complications in over a quarter of patients with a pooled anastomotic leakage rate of 22%. Again, here, 27% of patients were treated with percutaneous pleura and/or abdominal drains. Although no 30‐day mortality was reported, one patient died 2 months later after developing a disseminated intravascular coagulopathy on the first postoperative day.

Pseudomyxoma Peritonei

One of the clearest indications for CRS and HIPEC is PMP, as the procedure offers a cure for these patients. The largest multicentric registry study to report on outcomes after CRS and HIPEC in PMP was delivered from Chua et al. [45] in 2012. In an impressive cohort of 2298 patients from 16 centers, major postoperative complications, defined also as CD grade ≥ 3, occurred in 24% (10% CD grade 4) of patients with a mortality rate of 2%. Fifty one percent of patients had a complete cytoreduction (coming up to 83% when CCR0/1 were added together) and 89% received HIPEC. ﻿The authors found four factors to be significantly associated with major operative complications: male sex, more than two prior operations, PCI over 20 and the center's level of expertize (emerging vs. established).

In 2023, Ma et al. [61] published out of Beijing a cohort of 1008 patients of which 750 patients received CRS and HIPEC. The main outcome being OS in two groups: patients who received neoadjuvant chemotherapy versus surgery with neoadjuvancy. Complications graded CD grade ≥ 3 were reported in 95 patients (12.4%) and a mortality of under 1% (6 patients). Furthermore, they concluded that in their study the only risk factor for postoperative complications was intraoperative bleeding (loss over 1 L–p = 0.026). Zhang et al published a fairly updated version of the previous study with a 5‐year overlap of the cohort, now between 2014 and 2020. A total of 668 patients were included in the retrospective analysis of their center. They reported an incidence of postoperative surgical complications (CD grade over 2) of 10.9%, and the most common complications were of circulatory origin (18.1% ‐ congestive heart failure–12%). Within surgical complications, postoperative hemorrhages had a 5.2% incidence followed by fistulae of different origins (gastrointestinal, urinary and anastomotic–4.6%). Just as common were thrombo‐embolic events (4.6%), with most of them being deep venous thrombosis (25 patients). This concurs with the experience in our center (data not yet published). Lastly, as expected, infections also had an important incidence just under 4%. The study demonstrated the 35% of patients experienced a major postoperative complication and concluded that ﻿patient‐baseline albumin and hemoglobin levels, surgical duration, blood loss, median arterial pressure (MAP) and method of intraperitoneal hyperthermic perfusion(HIPEC vs. HIPEC and EPIC) were associated with major post‑operative complications.

Mesothelioma

A systematic review published in 2021 by Helm and colleagues reports on 20 publications published between the years 1992 and 2013. Only one stems from Japan and the rest from Western countries. All of them are retrospective and report varying morbidity rates between 8.3% and 90%. Also, the number of patients analyzed per study is in all except 4 under 50 patients, and half of those under 20 patients. The largest cohort is from Yan et al, [64] which due to its international character summarizes 401 patients with 274 (68%) patients having grade 1 and 2 complications and 127 (32%) patients having grade 3 and 4 postoperative complications (CTCAE). Regretfully, details concerning the complications were not reported. A smaller cohort (N = 60) described in the work done by Kepenekian et al. [65] in multicystic peritoneal mesothelioma, was subject to a 22% prevalence of adverse events, of which 13% were categorized as surgical: postoperative bleeding (5%), evisceration (3%), digestive fistula, wall abscess and intraabdominal abscess (each 2%). The most nonsurgical complication reported was fever at 13% followed by hematologic and respiratory complications each at 3% [65].

A study describing a single center experience from China reported a 37.3% incidence of adverse events from 110 patients, including 3 (2.7%) postoperative deaths. The most common postoperative being pleura effusion (19.1%), abdominal infections and septicemia (each with 7.3%) and, anemia and renal dysfunction each with a 5% prevalence [67]. Interestingly, the colleagues discuss the possible benefit for routine chest drainage placement when carrying out peritonectomy of the diaphragm. Nevertheless, there isn't enough data to formally recommend this and would go against the actual ERAS recommendations.

Discussion

This systemized review set out to investigate postoperative complications after CRS and HIPEC. The first conclusion that can be drawn from this systemized literature search is that there is an important deficit of information that needs to be addressed, and a consensus in the reporting of surgical complications needs to be reached. The surgical community seems somewhat reluctant to report its complications, this in turn hampers a truthful representation on feasibility and safety of the procedures we carry out. Even though we might extrapolate conclusions and results from studies of surgical complications after major abdominal surgery (colectomies, esophageal resections, hepatic‐pancreatic‐biliary operations), the fact remains that these results cannot be pooled due to heterogenous patient groups, differences in oncologic radicality of the procedures and different use of definitions for postoperative complications. In 2006, some consensus was reached after a Delphi round and presented at the International Workshop on Peritoneal Surface Malignancy in Milan regarding the question of choosing the most appropriate classification system. The National Cancer Institute's (NCI) Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE) Version 3.0 had an 87.5% approval rate in the second round.

The NCI CTCAE Version 5.0 [79] published in 2017, although extremely thorough and specific for the classification of medical complications after that is, chemotherapy, is vague and maybe even not helpful regarding surgical complications. A new revised version due in summer 2024 will hopefully address this issue. It is the view of the authors, that the CD Classification [80] best classifies postoperative surgical complications, as it addresses the type of intervention needed to solve the complication and includes the need for ICU management. We strongly encourage our colleagues to avoid using terminology like minor and major complications. We are aware of the work done by Alyami et al. [81] from the same year which compared both classification systems in a 30‐ and 90‐day period, showing clear advantages for the CTCAE classification specially by avoiding underrepresentation of 90‐day mortality. In 2016, Lehman et al. [82] made the case, that CD was better for surgical complications because it was simple and yet correctly assessed postoperative complications. Additionally, they suggested that the CTCAE classification should be used to describe critical medical complications that are not addressed in the CD counterpart. We too believe this, in addition to extending the observation time (30‐ to 90‐day) as in the former, to be a sensible approach to finally reach a consensus in reporting postoperative complications after CRS and HIPEC.

The proper study of postoperative complications is of extreme relevance because of its influence in oncologic outcomes. Across entities, patients with higher morbidity were at risk of poorer OS [1, 10, 11], with studies reporting hazard ratios of 2.1 [11], and 1.85 in iCRS [10].

When carried out in specialized centers in the hand of experienced teams that are familiar with the procedure and the complications, CRS and HIPEC is as safe as any other major abdominal surgery. As a point of comparison, the complication rate of major abdominal surgery in a center in the Netherlands recorded postoperative complications in 42.4% of patients, half of these being major complications (CD Grade 3 or higher). Operations dealing with PMP which tend to be time consuming to achieve CCR‐0 with multiple visceral resections, postoperative complications can go up to 50% [74]. In the Chua et al multicenter study, the units level of expertize was a factor associated with complications outcomes (established unit, 23% major complications versus emerging unit, 35% major complications; p < 0.001) [45].

The most common complication across all entities are surgical site infections (SSI), with SSI‐3 (organ/space SSI) needing radiological intervention that is, drainage or even relaparotomy. Anastomotic leakages, on the other hand, not only remain one of the most common complications after CRS and HIPEC, but perhaps the reason for the most re‐operations. These seem to always be early complications as they are not a reason for readmission 60‐days after discharge [83]. Even when including urological procedures, which adds a significant morbidity [5] to the procedure, the most common complications remain the same. The protective factor of minimally invasive techniques in major abdominal surgery [84] is a major absence in CRS and HIPEC, even if current efforts have shown that minimal invasive surgery may have a place in PSM [15, 40, 44, 55].

The American College of Surgeons–National Surgical Quality Improvement Program (ACS‐NSQIP) took it upon themselves to investigate the perioperative morbidity of gastrectomy during CRS and HIPEC. Among the 1168 patients who underwent CRS + HIPEC in a span of 11 years, 43 (4%) underwent partial or total gastrectomy (20 and 23 respectively). Paredes et al. [37] published these findings in 2019 and conclude that patients who have undergone gastrectomy have a significantly greater complication rate (60.5% vs. 27.9%, p < 0.001) without a statistically significant higher mortality (4.7% vs. 1.4%, p = 0.09). Additionally, atop an American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) status above 4, malnutrition, liver resection and colectomy, gastrectomy was the strongest predictor for postoperative morbidity (OR = 3.52, p < 0.001). The experience of two Phase II trials published by Green et al. [38] showed a 20% CD grade 3 rate and a 12% CD grade 4 rate within 30 days. Furthermore, 22% of patients developed an AL that required intervention. In this sense, patient selection must be strict for an entity that is prone to postoperative complications due to the needed aggressive local surgical treatment.

In regard to Mesothelioma, the colleagues Su et al. reported on CC‐Score 2/3 being the only factor independently associated with postoperative complications (HR = 2.3, 95% CI = 1.3–4.1, p = 0.004) related to surgical technique and perioperative management (others were innate tumor biology i.e., Ki‐67 index), making the irrefutable argument that peritoneal mesotheliomas should be treated in specialized reference centers with expert surgeons. This argument though is also valid for all other PSM. CRS and HIPEC regimens have been for many entities not yet standardized and yet for entities like Mesothelioma and PMP, the data demonstrates remarkable median OS superior to the expected natural history of the disease [68]. Further information regarding HIPEC protocols for peritoneal mesothelioma can be found in the systematic review published in 2023 by Kepenekian et al. [71].

Some general considerations valid for all entities are reported in the work of Kelly et al. [83] Patients undergoing CRS and HIPEC are generally multimorbid, have generally poorer Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group or ASA status and will be subject to a procedure that entails multi‐visceral resection, extensive operative times and meaningful blood loss. Kelly et al propose prolonged operative time and blood loss are likely factors that contribute to thrombo‐embolic events [83] and thus readmissions, which is a relation that we has also caught our attention and are currently investigating. They also report the creation of a diverting stoma as a very frequent cause of readmission due to ileus or obstruction (OR = 6.04, 95% CI = 1.56–12–14, p = 0.01).

Several newer trials and studies [38] have reported using sodium thiosulfate to prevent acute kidney injury or failure after CRS and HIPEC. A study published by Kurreck et al. [85] showed a significant reduction in the incidence of acute kidney failure by prophylactically using sodium thiosulfate with a loading dose during HIPEC or immediately after and a maintenance infusion over 12 or 24 h. Nevertheless, other metabolic changes during HIPEC that affect morbidity still need to be addressed. Rubio‐López and colleagues showed that pre‐HIPEC glycemia (OR = 1.012, 95% CI = 1.004–1.020, p = 0.005) and post‐HIPEC potassium (OR = 3.447, 95% CI = 1.369–8.678, p = 0.009) were identified as risk factors for major postoperative complications [86].

The question in the title has a simple answer: yes, we can do better. Nevertheless, it is our opinion that our guild of PSM surgeons is doing everything to get and be better. The installment of specialized centers across all geographical regions shows that we have learned from shared experiences, learning curves, specialized and continued training with PSM experts, strict patient selection, among other aspects. The growing interest in ground‐breaking translational research is driving knowledge in our field at its fastest pace yet.

In accordance with our methodology a number of studies have purposely been left out. Studies that included early postoperative intraperitoneal chemotherapy were not included because of the heterogeneity of the settings it was used in. This meant also longer dwelling catheters or prolonged intensive care unit stays. The somewhat limited number of studies analyzed herein do not necessarily reflect the lack of publications in this field, rather the failing to report morbidity outcomes in these studies.

Conclusions

CRS and HIPEC is one of the most extensive abdominal surgeries that can be carried out. It is therefore of paramount importance to strictly select patients, where the benefits outweigh the risks. Overall, the factors that have an influence in survival are the male sex, number of prior operations, high PCIs and the center's level of expertize. Postoperative complications of any origin must be held at a minimum and when present, must be optimally and swiftly dealt with to avoid depriving the patient of the survival benefits the procedure may offer. Standardization of perioperative management and the constate updating of protocols is a must to ensure the best quality of care for our patients and offer them the best possible quality of life.
