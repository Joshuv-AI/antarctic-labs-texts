The coronal plane alignment of the knee classification cannot ignore the joint line convergence angle
Authors: Guanghui Zhao, Chengyuan Ma, Jianbing Ma, Jianpeng Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRecently, several classification systems have been proposed, among which the coronal plane alignment of the knee (CPAK) classification has gained widespread adoption. Nevertheless, existing calculation methods for CPAK do not account for the joint line convergence angle (JLCA), potentially compromising classification accuracy.MethodsA modified CPAK classification incorporating JLCA was developed using the mechanical hip-knee-ankle angle (mHKA) and actual joint line obliquity (aJLO). A retrospective review was conducted on 120 patients (240 knees) with no or mild knee osteoarthritis (OA). Radiographic parameters—including mHKA, medial proximal tibial angle (MPTA), lateral distal femoral angle (LDFA), JLCA, and mechanical joint line angle (MJLA)—were measured, and arithmetic HKA (aHKA) and CPAK JLO were calculated accordingly. Knee alignment, joint line apex positions, and phenotypic classifications were compared between the conventional and the modified CPAK.ResultsThe mean JLCA was 1.0° ± 1.1°, with only 46.7% of cases falling within the range of 0° ± 1°. The mean mHKA was 178.4° ± 3.3°, while the aHKA was − 0.5° ± 3.3°. The mean aJLO was 3.2° ± 1.7°, and the CPAK JLO was 173.1° ± 3.4°. Concordance between conventional CPAK and modified CPAK in terms of knee alignment was 75.4%, joint line apex position was 64.6%, and phenotypes was 51.7%. The most prevalent CPAK types were Type II (n = 109; 45.4%), followed by Type I (n = 57; 23.8%) and Type III (n = 51; 21.3%). In contrast, the most common modified CPAK types were Type II (n = 73; 30.4%), Type IV (n = 50; 20.8%), and Type V (n = 50; 20.8%).ConclusionThe CPAK classification remains clinically relevant in knee arthroplasty, incorporating JLCA into CPAK evaluation is essential to improve classification precision and ensure more accurate representation of native knee.Keywords: Coronal plane alignment of the knee, Joint line convergence angle, Arithmetic hip-knee-ankle angle, Joint line obliquity, Mikulicz joint line angle

Background

For decades, achieving neutral mechanical alignment—defined as a hip-knee-ankle angle (HKA) of 0°—has been a standard objective in total knee arthroplasty (TKA) [1]. However, growing evidence suggests that restoring native limb alignment, rather than enforcing neutral alignment, may lead to improved soft-tissue balance and superior long-term clinical outcomes, thereby attracting increasing interest among orthopedic surgeons [2, 3]. Nevertheless, the onset of arthritic deformity alters the knee joint gap and shifts the lower limb’s mechanical axis, often exaggerating the pre-existing alignment. This progression complicates the accurate estimation of an individual’s pre-arthritic state and introduces uncertainty in defining an optimal postoperative alignment target [4].

Classification of coronal plane knee alignment offers a systematic approach to inferring pre-arthritic alignment. In 2018, Lin et al. [5] proposed a phenotypic classification based on three key parameters: the mechanical hip-knee-ankle angle (mHKA), lateral distal femoral angle (LDFA), and medial proximal tibial angle (MPTA). This framework was the first to classify lower limb alignment using independent assessments of femoral and tibial components. Although the system allows for 27 theoretical phenotypes, only five were identified as clinically prevalent. Subsequently, Hirschmann et al. [6] introduced a functional knee phenotype classification encompassing 125 possible combinations, with 43 considered clinically relevant. While this model provides comprehensive coverage of anatomical variation, its complexity and extensive categorization pose practical challenges in clinical application. In 2021, MacDessi et al. [7] developed the Coronal Plane Alignment of the Knee (CPAK) classification (Fig. 1), which simplifies phenotyping into nine distinct categories using arithmetic hip-knee-ankle angle (aHKA) and joint line obliquity (JLO). Despite having been introduced only four years ago, the CPAK system has demonstrated excellent interrater reliability and has gained rapid international adoption in both research and clinical practice.

However, the conventional calculation methods of aHKA and JLO do not account for the joint line convergence angle (JLCA) [4]. The JLCA reflects the combined effect of intra-articular deformity due to osteoarthritis (OA) and concomitant soft tissue laxity. It is generally accepted that JLCA does not necessarily equal 0° and tends to increase progressively with advancing OA [8]. A systematic review reported that in native knees without OA, the mean JLCA ranged from 0.47° ± 0.98° in men to 1.9° ± 1.4° in women, indicating a non-negligible deviation from neutrality [9]. Furthermore, Mabrouk et al. [10] demonstrated that JLCA increases significantly with the severity of OA, underscoring its dynamic nature during disease progression. For Chinese patients with no or mild OA, it remains unknown whether JLCA is equal to zero, which may affect the CPAK classification and its application of in Chinese patients. Therefore, this study had two primary objectives: first, to measure JLCA in a cohort of Chinese patients with no or mild knee OA to assess whether the assumption of 0° alignment holds true in this population; second, to perform an modified CPAK classification based on measured mechanical hip-knee-ankle angle (mHKA) and actual joint line obliquity (aJLO), and compare it with the standard CPAK classification derived from calculation, thereby evaluating the influence of JLCA on the accuracy of phenotypic categorization.

Methods

Patient selection study design

This retrospective study included patients who underwent long-leg standing radiographs (LLSRs) at a single medical center between January 1, 2025, and August 31, 2025. Inclusion criteria were: (1) patients aged ≥ 18 years; and (2) no or mild knee osteoarthritis (OA; Kellgren-Lawrence [K-L] grade 0 or 1). Exclusion criteria included: moderate-to-severe knee OA (K-L grade ≥ 2); a history of lower limb trauma, deformity, or prior surgery; obvious lower limb rotation on X-ray images; or poor image quality. The study was approved by the hospital’s institutional review board (approval number: 2025-KY-033-01). Radiographic data were retrieved from the hospital’s picture archiving and communication system (PACS) after obtaining verbal informed consent from patients via telephone interview.

Radiological analysis

This study obtained LLSRs using the protocol described by Paley et al. [11]: fibular heads overlap the tibia by approximately one-third bilaterally, the patella is vertically centered over the knee joint, and bilateral lesser trochanters exhibit symmetric morphology. The following radiographic parameters were measured: mHKA, MPTA, LDFA, JLCA, and Mikulicz joint line angle (MJLA) [12] (Fig. 2). All measurements were performed by two fellowship-trained orthopaedic specialists using the hospital’s PACS, which includes a built-in angle measurement tool with a precision of 0.1°.

Measurement of different alignment parameters on the weight-bearing long-leg radiograph. mHKA, mechanical hip-knee-ankle angle; LDFA, lateral distal femoral angle; MPTA, medial proximal tibial angle; JLCA, joint line convergence angle; MJLA, Mikulicz joint line angle

The MJLA is defined as the medial angle between the JLCA bisector and the lower limb weight-bearing line (Mikulicz line); normal values range from 87° to 93. The aJLO is calculated as 90° minus MJLA [12]. The aHKA was derived as MPTA minus LDFA, and the CPAK-JLO as MPTA plus LDFA, per the method proposed by MacDessi et al. [7]. Neutral aHKA was defined as 0° ± 2° (inclusive); varus aHKA as < -2°, and valgus aHKA as > 2°. Neutral JLO was defined as 0° ± 3° (inclusive); apex distal JLO as > 3°, and apex proximal JLO as < -3°.

mHKA was compared with the aHKA, similarly, aJLO was compared with the CPAK-derived JLO. The concordance was defined as identical results, while discordance was defined as differing coronal alignment or apex position.

Measurement reliability and intraclass correlation coefficient

Sample size was determined using G*Power software (Version 3.1.9; Heinrich-Heine-Universität Düsseldorf, Düsseldorf, Germany). Based on previous studies, a minimum sample size of 89 patients was required for the planned linear regression analysis, assuming an effect size (f²) of 0.15, type I error (α) of 0.05, and statistical power (1 - β) of 0.95 [13]. To assess intra-observer and inter-observer reliability of angle measurements, two investigators independently evaluated the first 10 consecutive knees twice, with a 1-week interval between sessions. Intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) were calculated to quantify reliability (Table 1).

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables (age, mHKA, LDFA, MPTA, MJLA) were summarized as means ± standard deviations (SD). Categorical variables [sex, limb side (left/right)] were summarized as frequencies and percentages. Normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Continuous variables were compared using the independent-samples t-test (normally distributed data) or Mann-Whitney U test (non-normally distributed data). Scatterplots were generated to visualize alignment distributions for the conventional versus the modified CPAK classification.

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), with a two-sided p < 0.05 considered statistically significant. Graphical illustrations were created using GraphPad Prism (Version 9.5.1; GraphPad Software, Boston, MA, USA).

The mean JLCA was 1.0° ± 1.1°, with 46.7% (112/240 knees) were within the reference range of 0° ± 1°.

Discussion

The primary finding of this study demonstrated that JLCA values are predominantly not aligned with 0°, even in knees without OA or those in the early OA stage, which caused a certain degree of bias in the actual classification of CPAK. Quantitative analysis revealed that over 50% of JLCA measurements deviated beyond 0° ± 1°, while approximately 20% exceeded 0° ± 2°. The concordance between aHKA and mHKA was 75.4%, between CPAK JLO and aJLO was 64.6%, and between the conventional and the modified CPAK was only 51.7%. These discrepancies primarily arise because JLCA not only influences the mHKA but also alters the aJLO, ultimately resulting in a mismatch between the conventional and the modified CPAK.

The modified CPAK classification system in this study incorporates JLCA, enabling a more precise preoperative assessment of patients. Accurate preoperative assessment of the knee joint phenotype in arthritic patients is critical for surgical planning, particularly given the contemporary emphasis on “knee functional alignment” [14, 15]. Prior research had highlighted that classifying coronal alignment solely into varus, valgus, or neutral categories is inadequate, as it captures only a static alignment state without incorporating JLO [16]. Additionally, Hirschmann et al. [6] identified distinct coronal knee phenotypes based on tibial and femoral joint line orientations and overall limb alignment in both OA and non-OA knees, underscoring that a single alignment target cannot adequately restore all preoperative phenotypes. Although a postoperative HKA angle within 1 standard deviation (± 3°) of the aHKA was traditionally considered indicative of pre-arthritic/kinematic alignment, the JLO must also be rigorously addressed [7, 17, 18]. Moreover, the traditional goal of knee arthroplasty—prioritizing “stability above all else”—focused on achieving flexion-extension gap balance and medial-lateral gap symmetry [19]. However, this approach clearly falls short of addressing the demands of modern knee arthroplasty. Therefore, it is imperative to precisely classify the knee phenotype, restore both its pre-arthritis HKA and JLO, and optimize the achievement of “knee functional alignment” to ensure patient-specific outcomes.

Introduced in 2021 by MacDessi et al. [7], the CPAK classification system has gained rapid global acceptance, a testament to its excellent interrater reliability and reliance only on readily available long-leg radiographs [20]. According to our previous research, a total of 29 studies used CPAK to classify healthy individuals or patients with arthritis comprised 4,082 healthy knees from 7 countries and 21,632 arthritic knees from 13 countries [21]. The utility of CPAK extends beyond mere classification; for instance, the aHKA can be used preoperatively in medial unicompartmental knee arthroplasty (UKA) to predict postoperative alignment and identify patients at risk for valgus outliers [22]. Furthermore, growing evidence suggests that restoring a patient’s native CPAK phenotype during TKA leads to superior functional outcomes, underscoring the importance of considering JLO in personalized alignment strategies [23, 24]. Thus, the major contribution of the CPAK system is its integration of both overall limb alignment and JLO to create a clear, concise, and clinically relevant classification of knee phenotypes.

However, a fundamental limitation of the conventional CPAK classification is that its calculations for both the aHKA and JLO assume a neutral JLCA, which may limit its concordance with anatomical reality [7]. Furthermore, the distribution of phenotypes within the original nine CPAK subtypes had been questioned. In response, Hsu et al. [25] advocated for a modification, defining JLO by the formula: actual JLO = 90° – (mLDFA + MPTA)/2. This “Modified CPAK” approach resulted in a more even distribution of patients across the nine subtypes. This adjustment implicitly accounted for the common clinical scenario where the JLCA deviates from zero. Nevertheless, this calculation remains based on the constitutional alignment in a neutral position and may not be fully applicable in knees with significant varus or valgus deformity. In a study by Şahbat et al. [26], the CPAK method for locating the knee joint apex was found to be correct in fewer than half of the knees evaluated. This approach, based on the angle between a tangent to the tibial plateau and the horizontal plane, shares a critical limitation with the original CPAK by not incorporating the JLCA. Additionally, this measurement is confounded by extra-articular factors such as pelvic rotation (influencing the apparent hip position) and hindfoot alignment, which can artifactually alter the obliquity of the joint line.

In this study, we utilized the modified nine-type CPAK classifications including JLCA in the calculation. The mHKA was categorized as varus (< 178°), neutral (178°–182°), or valgus (> 182°). The actual JLO was classified as varus (i.e., medial inclination) (> 3°), neutral (-3° to 3°), or valgus (i.e., lateral inclination) (< -3°). Unlike the original methodology described by MacDessi et al. [7], our calculations for both mHKA and aJLO incorporated the JLCA. Through trigonometric analysis incorporating the mHKA, MPTA, LDFA, and JLCA, we derived the relationship mHKA = MPTA-LDFA-JLCA, a finding consistent with prior literature [8]. Furthermore, to determine the aJLO, we employed the MJLA using the formula aJLO = 90° – MJLA. This method was selected over the conventional approach of measuring the angle between the tibial condylar tangent and the horizontal, as the latter is susceptible to error from hip abduction/adduction on long-leg radiographs—a limitation not shared by the MJLA-based calculation [27].

The incorporation of the JLCA and the MJLA enables the straightforward calculation of the actual mHKA and aJLO, thereby facilitating a more accurate CPAK classification. The clinical feasibility of obtaining these measurements is enhanced by the growing adoption of robot-assisted arthroplasty. According to our experience, a stratified protocol for applying this classification is proposed based on disease severity: (1) Healthy Individuals or Mild OA: In the absence of significant joint space narrowing, the constitutional JLCA and mHKA remain largely unaltered. Therefore, these parameters can be measured directly from standard long-leg radiographs. (2) Moderate OA without Severe Structural Changes: For patients without extensive osteophyte formation or soft tissue contracture, JLCA and MJLA should be measured from varus-valgus stress radiographs. The resulting mHKA and aJLO can then be calculated to determine the patient’s modified CPAK phenotype. (3) Severe OA with Advanced Joint Degeneration: In cases of severe joint space narrowing and substantial osteophyte formation, the intraoperative state following osteophyte resection and soft tissue release provides the optimal condition for assessment. Utilizing computer navigation or robotic systems, a varus-valgus stress can be applied intraoperatively to measure JLCA and MJLA, from which the true mHKA and aJLO are derived to guide a personalized, phenotype-restoring alignment.

The greatest advantage of this study lies in the modification of the CPAK classification system, and contemporary knee arthroplasty increasingly requires integration of soft-tissue considerations. For achieving optimal, physiological, and natural results, both bony alignment and soft-tissue balance must be considered simultaneously. Recently, Bell et al. [28] introduced and internally validated the Knee Balancing Score (KBS), a simplified and reproducible method based solely on mediolateral gap asymmetry measured at full extension (when achievable) and 90° flexion using a robotic-assisted platform. They offered a practical, physiological and objective system to quantify intraoperative balance, transforming subjective perception into a standardized and reproducible assessment applicable in modern TKA practice. Therefore, in future research, it is essential to combine the quantification of soft tissue assessment with the CPAK classification.

Several limitations warrant acknowledgment. Firstly, the study participants were recruited from a hospital setting, initially presenting with symptoms such as knee pain or swelling. Their classification as KL grade 0 or 1 was radiographically confirmed, but the cohort may not be representative of truly asymptomatic individuals. Secondly, the radiographic measurements used are subject to inherent variability. However, the methodology is supported by previous validation studies showing no significant differences compared to CT-based measurements. Thirdly, the limitation of CPAK was its primary focus on bony mechanical alignment, without adequately incorporating dynamic soft-tissue behavior. This study merely modified the CPAK without quantifying the assessment of soft tissues. Lastly, the exclusively Chinese populations of our cohort necessitate caution in extrapolating these results, as anatomical phenotypes and alignment may vary across different ethnicities and regions.

Conclusions

For the CPAK classification to fully realize its potential in guiding knee arthroplasty, the calculation of key parameters must be refined. The standard methods for determining aHKA and JLO, which ignore the JLCA and may cause a certain degree of bias in the actual classification of CPAK. We advocate for the routine incorporation of JLCA into the CPAK framework to enhance its clinical accuracy and reliability.
