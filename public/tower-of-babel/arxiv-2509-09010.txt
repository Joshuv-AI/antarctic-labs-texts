Cryogenic geometric anti-spring vibration isolation system Preprint: AIP/123-QED
L.Feenstra, S. Domínguez-Calderón, K. van Oosten, H.S.M. Bohemen, T. Benschop, M. Brinkman, M. Li, E. Hennes, R. Cornelissen, B.J. Hensen, A. Bertolini, M.P. Allan

Abstract
The combination of low temperature and low vibration levels is key for ultrasensitive sensing applications such as scanning probe microscopy, large-mass quantum mechanics, and gravitational wave detection. Unfortunately, closed-cycle cryostats using pulse tube or GM coolers introduce strong low-frequency vibrations starting at 1 Hz. Mass-spring systems allow passive isolation, but for low-frequency applications the required spring constants and masses become impractical. Blade-based geometric anti-spring systems are compact isolators that operate from sub-Hz frequencies, but have not been demonstrated at cryogenic temperatures. Here, we characterize a geometric anti-spring system tuned to operate at cryogenic temperatures. Our cryogenic filter uses radially arranged titanium blade springs whose effective spring constant can be tuned in-situ using a magnetic actuator. Our system achieves a vertical resonance frequency of 185 mHz at 7K, which allows reduction of vibrations at the problematic 1 Hz cooler frequency by an order of magnitude.



I
Introduction

Scanning probe microscopy (SPM)
Battisti et al. 2018
and ultra-sensitive force sensing for quantum gravity experiments
Bose et al. 2017
both require ultra-low vibrations at cryogenic temperatures – either to reduce noise in the tunneling current to a few fA
Battisti et al. 2018
, or to detect effects of gravity with sensitivities in the sub-fN regime
Fuchs et al. 2024
.
The same requirements apply to operational and future interferometer-based gravitational wave detectors, where the mirrors are suspended inside a cryogenic environment
Akutsu et al. 2021
;
Koroveshi et al. 2023
;
Utina et al. 2022
.
The challenge of achieving low vibrations is compounded by the growing popularity of closed-system cryostats, driven by helium shortage and correspondingly high prices
survey
.
These systems use pulse tube or GM coolers, which introduce very strong vibrations from pressure changes, typically around
1\text{\,}\mathrm{H}\mathrm{z}
, as can be seen in Fig.
1
.
Therefore, vibration attenuation especially at low frequencies is the key to realize ultra-sensitive experiments in closed-cycle cryostats.

Figure 1:
A comparison of vertical vibrations of the 4K-plate of our GM based cryostat (see Fig 2 for a schematic), measured by a geophone. If the GM cooler is on, vibrations are stronger by orders of magnitude. The strongest noise is visible at a frequency of

and harmonics thereof, the first four of which are indicated with arrows.

Vibration attenuation can be achieved by both active and passive systems, which are often combined to optimize the attenuation properties.
In this paper, we focus entirely on the latter type.
Passive vibration insulation conventionally consists of classical mass-spring systems, where the experiment is located on the mass.
Such mass-spring systems act as a low-pass filter for vibrations going from ground to mass, since vibrations at frequencies above the resonance frequency
\omega_{0}
are damped as
\nicefrac{{1}}{{\omega^{2}}}
.
This property implies that the performance of a mass-spring system can be optimized by minimizing the resonance frequency, given by
\omega_{0}=\sqrt{{k}/{M}}
,
where
k
is the spring constant and
M
is the suspended mass.
Hence, by reducing the spring constant or increasing the mass,
\omega_{0}
can be minimized, and the vibration attenuation at frequencies
\omega~>\omega_{0}
is improved.
Lowering the resonance frequency of conventional mass-spring systems has the problem of increasing system length, which can be in the order of meters for sub-Hz frequencies. In cryostats such length scales are not viable as space is generally limited.

To overcome the limitations of conventional mass-spring systems, we turn towards geometric anti-spring (GAS) filters to achieve resonance frequencies around a few
100\text{\,}\mathrm{m}\mathrm{H}\mathrm{z}
in a cryogenic environment.
These systems, first proposed by Bertolini et al.
Bertolini et al. 1999
, are brought close to a mechanical instability by a compressive force perpendicular to the direction of the mass movement.
This so-called anti-spring effect results in a greatly reduced effective spring constant, as we describe in more detail later.

GAS filters have been realized at room temperature in gravitational wave detectors KAGRA and VIRGO
Akutsu et al. 2021
;
Koroveshi et al. 2023
;
Beker et al. 2012
;
Kumar et al. 2016
, operating at resonance frequencies of a few 100 mHz.
As this is far below the operating frequency of closed-system cryostats, GAS filters are a promising candidate to attenuate pulse tube noise.
This possibility has been explored by Eßer et al.
Eßer et al. 2024
and the company Four Nine Design
Deshpande et al. 2025
, who both used a GAS filter at room temperature to suspend a cryogenic STM chamber via a thin wire.
The experiment is then connected to the cryostat with thermal connections, which is a challenge as they introduce external vibrations to the experiment.

In this paper, we present a GAS system that is designed to operate at cryogenic temperatures as a platform for ultra-low vibration cryogenic experiments.
We choose to keep the whole system at base temperature, to keep the system simple and reduce vibrational short cuts due to thermalization wires.
The goal of this paper is to demonstrate that geometric anti-springs can be tuned an operated in a cryogenic environment, paving the way for GAS-based cryogenic vibration isolation.

We start the paper with an overview of the GAS and introduce the main equations governing the system.
Starting from conventional mass-spring systems, a linear model is developed to demonstrate the mechanics and attenuating properties of GAS filters.
Then we describe the experimental design of the GAS and the cryostat.
Next, we test the performance of our system and explain the measurement procedures.
Finally, we identify the next steps to realizing cryogenic GAS filters for ultra-sensitive measurements.

II
Theory of geometric anti-spring systems

We start by developing the theoretical model for the dynamics of GAS filters, mainly describing them as tunable conventional mass-spring systems.

II.1
Conventional mass-spring systems

Conventional mass-spring systems consist of a spring, fixed to some base at one end, and to a freely hanging mass on the other.
The experiment will be on the mass, the source of the vibrations will be the base, and the goal is to reduce vertical vibrations of the mass above a certain frequency.

The system is characterized by the spring constant
k
as well as the suspended mass
M
and spring mass
m
.
The spring has an unloaded length
L_{0}
and we define the loaded spring length
L
as
L=L_{0}+Mg
, where
g
is the gravitational acceleration.
We further include viscous damping, described by
\gamma
, and structural damping of the spring, described by the loss angle
\phi
.
We restrict motion to the vertical
y
-axis and define
y_{0}(t)
and
y(t)
as the respective positions of the base and mass w.r.t. some arbitrary origin.

The equation of motion of such a mass-spring system is

M\ddot{y}=-k(1+i\phi)\left(L-(y-y_{0})\right)-\alpha m\ddot{y}_{0}-\gamma\dot{y}\text{.}

(1)

Note that the term
\alpha m\ddot{y}_{0}
describes the center of percussion effect, which describes the effect of non-aligned forces on both spring ends.
As for a simple mass-spring system,
\alpha=0
, this term can be dropped when treating harmonic oscillators.
Because it is non-zero in the case of a GAS
Stochino et al. 2007
, we will include the term here.

We then introduce an outside source of vibrations to the spring base.
By Fourier transform of Eq.
1
, we obtain the transfer function
H_{y}(\omega)
, defined as the ratio between the mass’ and the spring base’s vertical motion, also called the filter’s transmissibility.
The transfer function is given by:

H_{y}:=\frac{Y(\omega)}{Y_{0}(\omega)}=\frac{\omega_{0}^{2}(1+i\phi)+\alpha\frac{m}{M}\omega^{2}}{\omega_{0}^{2}(1+i\phi)-\omega^{2}+i\frac{\gamma}{M}\omega}\text{,}

(2)

where
Y(\omega)
and
Y_{0}(\omega)
are the respective Fourier transformations of
y(t)
and
y_{0}(t)
, and
\omega_{0}=\sqrt{\nicefrac{{k}}{{M}}}
.

The low-pass filtering properties become clear when considering the limit of a massless undamped spring,
m=0,\gamma=0,\phi=0
.
At
\omega\ll\omega_{0}
, Eq.
2
reduces to
H_{y}=1
, hence mass and base move in unison.

\omega\approx\omega_{0}
results in an amplified motion of the mass as
H_{y}
diverges, and
\omega\gg\omega_{0}
leads to the desired damping of
\nicefrac{{1}}{{\omega^{2}}}
.
Thus, as seen in Eq.
2
the vibration attenuation performance of the mass spring system is optimized by minimizing
\omega_{0}
.
For conventional mass-spring systems, reduction of
k
or increasing of
M
is cumbersome and competes with spatial constraints, effectively limiting resonance frequencies to around
1\text{\,}\mathrm{H}\mathrm{z}
.
For example, a system with
0.5\text{\,}\mathrm{H}\mathrm{z}
resonance requires a spring extension under load of
1\text{\,}\mathrm{m}
, as one can calculate by equating
F_{g}=Mg
to
F_{s}=-kx
, and using that
\omega_{0}=\sqrt{\nicefrac{{k}}{{M}}}
.

II.2
Linear GAS model

Next, we will discuss the theory of GAS systems using a linear model, and show that they have the potential to effectively tune the spring constant towards zero, reaching resonance frequencies well below
1\text{\,}\mathrm{H}\mathrm{z}
.
We start with a simple two-dimensional model by only considering two compressed blade springs along the horizontal axis, and constraining the movement of the mass in the vertical axis (Fig.
3
b).
This treatment is based on Blom
Blom 2015
.

Figure 2:
a)
To develop the linear model for GAS, the vertical and compressive horizontal force components are separated and represented by conventional springs. The (arbitrary) origin for the vertical coordinates
y
and
y_{0}
is indicated at the top of the figure. Adapted from Blom
Blom 2015
.
b)
Sketched S-curves in three different regimes. Blue: The undercompressed regime, with a high
k_{\text{eff}}
in the working point. Green: If the compression is optimally tuned,
k_{\text{eff}}=0
in the working point, corresponding to the curve being vertical at the working point. Red: In the over-compressed regime, the system is bistable.

In this depiction, the force of each blade can be separated into a horizontal and a vertical component.
The linearization of the system is then achieved by modeling these force components as conventional springs as shown in Fig.
2
a.
In equilibrium, the mass hangs from a vertical spring, and two compressed horizontal springs push radially inward with mutually canceling forces.

Out of equilibrium, the vertical mass-spring system acts to restore the equilibrium position, while the two compressed springs will push the mass further out of equilibrium (See Fig.
2
a), hence the name anti-spring.
The total vertical force in this configuration is

\displaystyle F

\displaystyle=k_{y}(l_{y}-(y-y_{0}))+k_{x}(l_{x}-l_{x,0})\sin(\theta)

(3)

\displaystyle=\left(k_{y}+k_{x}\left(1-\frac{l_{x,0}}{l_{x}}\right)\right)(l_{y}-(y-y_{0}))\text{,}

(4)

where
k_{y}
is the spring constant for the vertical spring and
k_{x}
the combined spring constant of the two individual horizontal springs. In equilibrium, the vertical spring has length
l_{y}
.
l_{x,0}
denotes the uncompressed length of the horizontal springs and
l_{x}
,
x_{0}
can be inferred from Fig.
2
a.

Based on Eq.
4
we define an effective spring constant which can be rewritten as

k_{\text{eff}}(y)=k_{y}+k_{x}\left(1-\frac{l_{x,0}}{\sqrt{(l_{y}-(y-y_{0}))^{2}+x_{0}^{2}}}\right)\text{.}

(5)

From this equation it becomes clear that the spring constant can be modified by tuning
x_{0}
and thereby the compression of the horizontal springs.
The situation of minimal compression is called the working point of the filter.
In this configuration, the minimal resonance frequency ensures optimal filtering properties.
In principle, even zero stiffness could be achieved, providing ideal filtering behavior.

Eq.
5
also shows the possibility of
k_{\text{eff}}<0
for some range of
y
if the springs are compressed beyond the working point.
In this situation, the system has three equilibrium positions, the middle one of which is unstable.
Under sufficiently large forces, the mass can jump between the top and the bottom position.

Experimentally, we extract the effective spring constant from measurements of the so-called S-curve.
This curve shows the equilibrium position as a function of load
M
.
The effective spring constant is inversely proportional to the slope of the S-curve.
The working point is the point where the S-curve is steepest.

In this model, the transfer function of a GAS filter follows Eq.
2
, where we need to consider the non-zero center-of-percussion effect.
For a monolithic GAS, it is characterized by
\nicefrac{{1}}{{5}}\leq\alpha\leq\nicefrac{{1}}{{8}}
, depending on blade geometry
Stochino et al. 2007
.
In our system, we have an overall blade mass
m
of around 60 g, leading to saturation of the
\nicefrac{{1}}{{\omega^{2}}}
-decay for frequencies
\gtrsim$5\text{\,}\mathrm{H}\mathrm{z}$
.
Note that Stochino et al.
Stochino et al. 2007
proposed a method to compensate the center-of-percussion effect, lowering the saturation level significantly.

Design of a cyrogenic GAS and cryostat

For our cryogenic vibration attenuation system, we chose a monolithic GAS design that is well-established in gravitational wave detectors
Akutsu et al. 2021
;
Beker et al. 2012
.
It consists of quasi-triangular blade springs, arranged in a radial geometry (Fig
3
a).
The outer end of each blade is anchored in its own base, the inner side of the blades come together and are joined at the key stone, from where the mass is suspended.

An exact 1 dimensional model of this geometry has been worked out by Cella et al.
Cella et al. 2005
, but the linearized model presented above describes the key characteristics.
The main difference between both models are the exact dynamics at the working point.
For instance, the ability to reach
k_{\text{eff}}=0
depends heavily on the blades.

III.1
Blade design

The most important considerations when designing such a GAS system concern the (i) shape, (ii) material, and (iii) size of the blade.
The combination of these three determines both the load that one can suspend from the filter and the lowest resonance frequency.

(i) The shape of the blades determines the bending profile under load.
As the resonance frequency
\omega_{0}\propto M^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}
, it seems natural to design a blade shape that maximizes the load it can carry before exceeding the material’s yield strength.
Furthermore, the saturation level of the
\nicefrac{{1}}{{\omega^{2}}}
decay is decreased by reducing the ration
\nicefrac{{m}}{{M}}
(See Eq.
2
).
Load maximization for limited blade size is achieved by the so-called constant-curvature solution
Blom 2015
;
Cella et al. 2005
, where the blade bends uniformly when loaded.
However, as shown by Cella et al.
Cella et al. 2005
, this blade shape will not be able to reach the desired zero stiffness.
They propose a shape that does reach zero stiffness, while approximating the constant curvature solution.

(ii) To maximize the load further, it is advisable to select a blade material with a high yield strength, such as titanium, (maraging) steel or beryllium copper - a choice that is also influenced by the cryogenic properties of the materials, as discussed below.

(iii) The size of the GAS blades also plays an important role in the filter performance.
From the detailed model in
Cella et al. 2005
, one finds that the resonance frequency
\omega_{0}\propto L^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}
, where
L
is the length of the blade.
In a cryostat, this poses a serious challenge, as space inside a cryostat is generally limited.

Our blades are designed based on these three considerations.
The blade profile
\Gamma
follows a fifth-order polynomial as a function of
\nicefrac{{x}}{{L}}
, the normalized coordinate along the blade length
x
.

\Gamma
is given by:

\Gamma\left(\frac{x}{L}\right)=W_{0}\sum_{i=0}^{5}c_{i}\left(\frac{x}{L}\right)^{i}\text{,}

(6)

where the coefficients
c_{0}=0.8317,c_{1}=0.9328,c_{2}=-1.9631,c_{3}=5.8534,c_{4}=-12.22,c_{5}=6.6757
.
W_{0}
is the width at the spring base. In our system the dimensions are
W_{0}=$33.55\text{\,}\mathrm{m}\mathrm{m}$
and
L=$133.64\text{\,}\mathrm{m}\mathrm{m}$
. These were chosen to maximize the blade length and carrying strength, given the limited space inside the cryostat.
The blades are made of
0.6\text{\,}\mathrm{m}\mathrm{m}
thick grade 5 titanium (Ti-6Al-4V), which has a yield strength exceeding
1\text{\,}\mathrm{G}\mathrm{P}\mathrm{a}
.
In their working point, the springs are compressed, such that they depart from their base at an angle of
45\degree
and reach the key stone at an angle of
33\degree
with respect to the horizontal plane (Fig.
3
b).

A compact, fast turn-over cryostat

We load our GAS-system into a home-built, fast turn-over cryostat, shown in Figs.

c, e, based on a design of the Steele group at Delft University of Technology

.
This home-built cryostat was specifically designed for this experiment.
The requirements were (i) fast turn-over time and (ii) an easily accessible cold plate, with a base temperature below
\sim 10
K.

(i) The fast turn-over time was achieved by the use of a simple structure where the GM-cooler is directly connected to the base plate. We use a GM-cooler of type RDK-408D2 which has
40\text{\,}\mathrm{W}
of cooling power at
40\text{\,}\mathrm{K}
, and
1\text{\,}\mathrm{W}
at
4\text{\,}\mathrm{K}
to reach base temperature in under
24\text{\,}\mathrm{h}\mathrm{o}\mathrm{u}\mathrm{r}\mathrm{s}
.
Heaters on both stages and at the GAS base have a total power of
450\text{\,}\mathrm{W}
, allowing a warm-up within a few hours.

(ii) The accessibility of the 4K-plate was achieved by placing the cooler and all feed-throughs on the bottom of the system, leaving the top position free for the 4K-plate (see Fig.
3
c, e).
The cylindrical vacuum and radiation shields connect to the rest of the cryostat at the height of the 4K-plate, making the entire cold space freely accessible from all directions.
The cold space has a diameter of 35 cm, and a height of 40 cm.

Three copper pillars are mounted on the 4K plate, to support the plate hosting the blade springs, such that the GAS height over the 4K plate can be chosen freely.
This height mainly depends on the suspended mass, which is located below the spring plate and is connected through a hole in the plate center.
This connection consists of a bronze rod, rigidly connected to the mass, which can be screwed into the key stone.

If the mass is only thermalized via the titanium blade springs, the turn-over time increases drastically.
This issue can be resolved by clamping the suspended mass to the 4K-plate.
The clamp (see Figs.
3
c, d) consists of a lever extending to the floating mass, connected to a vertical bellow at the other side.
By regulating the He-gas pressure in the bellow between 0.5 and 1.5 bar, the bellow extends or retracts vertically.
In the extended state, the lever arm presses the mass to the 4K-plate, providing thermalization of this otherwise floating mass when cooling down or warming up.

IV
Performance

The first step in testing our system is to tune the compression of the spring blades to achieve the minimal effective spring constant, or equivalently stiffness, at the working point.
After reaching this working point, we characterize the system and its performance.

until reaching a near-vertical slope. The voice coil current is converted to a mass difference

IV.1
Tuning the system

The optimal compression is found by iteratively changing the compression based on measured S-curves, as illustrated in Fig.
4
.
The goal is to maximize the slope, or equivalently, minimize the stiffness at the working point.
If the measured stiffness is too high, one should increase the compression.
In case of bistability, compression should be decreased.

The vertical position of the mass is recorded using a lock-in amplifier to read out a linear capacitive position sensor.
The sensor consists of two concentric, partially overlapping brass rods, that are electrically isolated from their environment and each other.
The wider one is connected to the spring plate, the narrower one to the key stone.
The rod connecting the key stone and the suspended mass runs through the center of the tubes.
If the mass moves up and down, the overlap between the two tubes changes, leading to a change in capacitive value.

To tune the load on the filter, we can simply change the
\sim$8\text{\,}\mathrm{k}\mathrm{g}$
of suspended mass, consisting of a cylindrical copper block by adding copper disks and lab weights.
For in-situ fine tuning, we equipped the system with a vertically oriented voice coil (see Fig.
3
d) which slightly pushes the mass up or down, corresponding with 85 mg/mA.
To record S-curves in-situ, we sweep the coil current, thus sweeping the filter load.
From these curves, we extract the spring constant to tune the compression accordingly.

We can tune compression by moving the base of each spring individually.
Each base consists of two brass blocks between which the spring is clamped, as shown in Fig.
3
a, b.
These bases are clamped onto the spring plate with vertical screws mounted through radially configured slits in the spring plate.
On the outside of the spring plate, each spring base has a corresponding vertical tangential plate, with a fine-threaded screw (pitch 0.5 mm/turn) through it.
This screw pushes the base block inward and compensates the outward force from the blade spring, keeping the base in position.
By adjusting the fine screw, the compression can be tuned with an accuracy of 20
\mu
m, corresponding to 1/24th screw turn.
Note that differences of less than
100\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}
are enough to turn a near-zero stiffness filter bistable, or significantly increase the resonance frequency.

After tuning at room temperature, the cryogenic working point load is estimated based on the linear relation between the blades’ force and its Young’s modulus
Cella et al. 2005
.
For grade 5 titanium, they increase by
\sim 10\%
from ambient to cryogenic temperatures
Naimon, Weston, and Ledbetter 1974
, causing our mass to rest on the 4K-plate at ambient conditions.
Over the same temperature range, the optimal compression also changes due to different thermal expansion coefficients of the blades and spring plate.
The thermal expansion difference
Marquardt, Le, and Radebaugh 2001
between our titanium blades and aluminum spring plate of
2.5\cdot 10^{-3}
corresponds to
\sim$250\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}$
spring base movement.
Therefore, we lower the compression by
250\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}
or
\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}
fine screw turn to approach optimal cryogenic compression.
With this estimated tuning as a starting point, we repeat the iterative process, this time recording S-curves at cryogenic temperatures and warming up to change the compression.

IV.2
Characterization of the working point

With this iterative process we manage to reach near-zero stiffness at cryogenic temperatures, as we characterize in Fig.
5
.
In Fig.
5
a we show an S-curve at
T=$7\text{\,}\mathrm{K}$
, highlighting the need for the voice coil to achieve the sub-gram load tuning precision in-situ required for reaching the working point.
In this S-curve we also determined the resonance frequency as the load is changed, observing a minimal resonance frequency of 185 mHz.
This is shown in Fig.
5
b, demonstrating the strong dependence of the resonance frequency on the load: a few grams are enough to double the resonance frequency.

To investigate the temperature dependence of the working point, we use the heaters on the spring plate.
This introduces a temperature gradient to the springs, which can be of the order of tens of Kelvins, due to the poor heat conduction of titanium.
In Fig.
5
c, we plot the working point stiffness and load as a function of the average temperature of both spring ends.
Over the investigated temperature range, the stiffness changes only on the order of 10%, the working point load however decreases by tens of grams.
Hence, the performance of a GAS system is stable below
T\approx$60\text{\,}\mathrm{K}$
, as long as the coil can carry the current needed to reach the working point.

To measure the transmissibility of the filter, we use geophones, unidirectional accelerometers of the type Geospace GS-ONE-LF.
These geophones act as a second-order high pass filter, with resonance frequency
4.5\text{\,}\mathrm{H}\mathrm{z}
, which we compensate with a home-built, analog second-order correction filter, as demonstrated in e.g.
Gilyazov et al. 2023
.
This combination gives velocity response that can be considered linear for the scope of this work, providing a signal of
5.9\ \nicefrac{{\text{V}}}{{\text{mm s}^{-1}}}
for
f>$100\text{\,}\mathrm{m}\mathrm{H}\mathrm{z}$
.

We compare the vertical vibrations of the 4K-plate and the suspended mass to determine the attenuation properties of our GAS filter.
We obtain an average spectral density by Fourier transforming a 2000 s time series in bins of 100 s spaced at 50 s intervals.
This spectrum is shown in Fig.
6
.

In the mass spectrum, the vertical resonance is just below 200 mHz and the following damping can be identified below 1 Hz.
Around 1.45 Hz, there is a second mode, which we attribute to a pendulum mode.
This corresponds to a pendulum with a length of around 10 cm, which matches the distance between the center of mass and the key stone, which acts as pivot.
Around 10 Hz, further modes can be identified, whose origin is less clear.
One could be a torsional mode in which the mass rotates back and forth in the horizontal plane, putting torsional load on the blades.
Around 10 Hz we also observed rolling modes around the combined center of mass of the main mass and the tuning disks, putting a slight asymmetric load on the springs.

As Fig.
6
demonstrates, these additional modes at frequencies above the vertical resonance limit the damping performance of our GAS system.
It does however demonstrate that a GAS system can be tuned to a cryogenic working point with a vertical resonance well below 1 Hz, paving the way to realization of a GAS-based vibration isolation system for cryogenic experiments.

. II) The GAS provides attenuation of vertical vibrations. III) The pendulum mode at 1.45 Hz corresponds to our 10 cm pendulum length. IV) Around 10 Hz, apparent resonance peaks can be observed that may be attributed to modes breaking some symmetry of the system.

V
Outlook

In closed-system cryostats, there is strong
1\text{\,}\mathrm{H}\mathrm{z}
noise present, which limits the feasibility of scanning probe microscopy or ultra-sensitive force measurements in such systems.
Starting from designs for gravitational wave observatories
Akutsu et al. 2021
;
Beker et al. 2012
, we have shown the realization of a geometric anti-spring, that is designed for operation inside a cryostat.

In our system, we reduced the dimensions of the GAS filter and have included solutions for readout and mass fine tuning in situ at cryogenic temperatures.
This small GAS filter can be tuned to a vertical resonance frequency below
200\text{\,}\mathrm{m}\mathrm{H}\mathrm{z}
.
In the vibration spectrum, further modes can be identified as a pendulum and rotational modes, which should be damped before the low resonance frequency of the GAS filter can be used to optimally dampen vibrations for sensitive cryogenic experiments such as scanning probe microscopy.

Currently, we only use a single GAS, providing little attenuation for vibrations in the horizontal plane. This may be resolved by adding inverted pendulums, which can also reach extremely low resonance frequencies
Akutsu et al. 2021
;
Beker et al. 2012
