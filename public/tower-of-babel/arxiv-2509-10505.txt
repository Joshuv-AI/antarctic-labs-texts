CWT-LSTM Autoencoder: A Novel Approach for Gravitational Wave Detection in LIGO Data Publication type: Paper
Jericho Cain 1,∗ \orcid 0000-0003-4731-9142

Abstract
Gravitational wave detection requires sophisticated signal processing to identify weak astrophysical signals buried in instrumental noise. Traditional matched filtering approaches face computational challenges with diverse signal morphologies and non-stationary noise. This work presents a deep learning methodology integrating Continuous Wavelet Transform (CWT) preprocessing with Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) autoencoder architecture for gravitational wave detection. The CWT provides optimal time-frequency decomposition capturing chirp evolution and transient characteristics essential for compact binary coalescence identification. We first develop the model using synthetic datasets incorporating binary black hole merger signals with masses ranging from 10 to 80 solar masses. These signals are then embedded in colored Gaussian noise representative of Advanced LIGO sensitivity. The trained model demonstrates strong performance metrics. We then apply the CWT-LSTM model to gravitational wave data from multiple LIGO observing runs. We use 418 clean noise samples for training the anomaly detection model, while the test dataset contained a mix of 24 confirmed gravitational wave events and 161 noise samples. The model demonstrates strong performance with an AUC of 0.979 and Average Precision (AP) of 0.932, achieving a precision of 1.0 at the optimal threshold with a recall of 0.83. The reconstruction error distribution shows clear separation between noise and gravitational wave signals, with noise samples clustering around 0.45 and signals around 0.65. This unsupervised approach enables discovery of signals with unknown morphologies that could provide complementary “blind search” capability for detecting exotic astrophysical sources and novel physics beyond current theoretical models.

Introduction

The need for discovery-oriented, template-free detection methods has become increasingly apparent as the field moves toward the next-generation detectors.

Unsupervised deep learning offers a template-independent approach for anomaly detection. This technology has been used to detect anomalies in a variety of fields including astrophysics. Rather that searching for known signal templates, unsupervised approaches learn the statistical properties of detector noise so that when deviations occur they get “flagged” as anomalous. In the context of gravitational wave astrophysics, these deviations could be gravitational waves. CWT Preprocessing

The CWT provides optimal time-frequency decomposition for gravitational wave signals, preserving both temporal localization and frequency resolution essential for chirp detection. For a given strain time series
h(t)
, the CWT is defined as:

W(a,b)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{a}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}h(t)\psi^{*}\left(\frac{t-b}{a}\right)dt

(1)

where
\psi(t)
is the mother wavelet,
a
is the scale parameter inversely related to frequency,
b
is the translation parameter corresponding to time, and
*
denotes complex conjugation.

We employ the Morlet wavelet as the mother wavelet due to its optimal time-frequency localization properties and resemblance to gravitational wave chirp morphology:

\psi(t)=\pi^{-1/4}e^{i\omega_{0}t}e^{-t^{2}/2}

(2)

where
\omega_{0}=6
provides the optimal trade-off between time and frequency resolution for gravitational wave analysis. The CWT is computed over logarithmically spaced scales corresponding to frequencies from 20 Hz to 512 Hz, matching the sensitive frequency band of Advanced LIGO detectors.

The resulting time-frequency representation forms a 2D scalogram
|W(a,b)|^{2}
that captures the characteristic frequency evolution of gravitational wave chirps. This scalogram serves as input to the subsequent neural network architecture, providing rich feature representations that preserve both the temporal evolution and spectral content of potential signals.

The diagonal band in the spectrogram reveals the frequency evolution from approximately 100 Hz to 800 Hz over 0.75 seconds, corresponding to the inspiral phase of the binary black hole merger. Figure

The gravitational wave data exhibits clear chirp patterns and merger signatures absent in the noise data, particularly the vertical feature around 2.3 seconds in the higher frequency bands representing the merger and ringdown phases.

LSTM Autoencoder Architecture

The core detection system employs an LSTM autoencoder designed to learn compressed representations of gravitational wave time-frequency patterns. The architecture consists of three main components operating in sequence: encoder network, bottleneck layer, and decoder network as shown in Fig.
3
.

Figure 3:
Schematic of the CWT-LSTM autoencoder architecture. The input CWT representation (8 scales × 4096 time points) is processed through an LSTM encoder to produce a compressed latent representation, which is then reconstructed through an LSTM decoder. The reconstruction error serves as the anomaly score for gravitational wave detection.

The encoder processes CWT scalograms through a series of LSTM layers with progressively reducing hidden dimensions, implementing the standard LSTM formulation:

h_{t}^{(l)}=\text{LSTM}^{(l)}(x_{t}^{(l)},h_{t-1}^{(l)})

(3)

where
h_{t}^{(l)}
represents the hidden state at time
t
for layer
l
, and
x_{t}^{(l)}
is the input at time
t
for layer
l
.The encoder component consists of a bidirectional LSTM layer with 64 hidden units, followed by a fully connected layer that compresses the temporal information into a latent representation of dimension 32. The bidirectional LSTM processes the CWT data in both forward and backward directions. The encoder transforms the input CWT representation with dimensions corresponding to 8 frequency scales and 4,096 time points into a compact 32-dimensional latent space, capturing hierarchical temporal patterns at multiple scales through standard gating mechanisms:

\displaystyle f_{t}

\displaystyle=\sigma(W_{f}\cdot[h_{t-1},x_{t}]+b_{f})

(4)

\displaystyle i_{t}

\displaystyle=\sigma(W_{i}\cdot[h_{t-1},x_{t}]+b_{i})

(5)

\displaystyle\tilde{C}_{t}

\displaystyle=\tanh(W_{C}\cdot[h_{t-1},x_{t}]+b_{C})

(6)

\displaystyle C_{t}

\displaystyle=f_{t}\cdot C_{t-1}+i_{t}\cdot\tilde{C}_{t}

(7)

\displaystyle o_{t}

\displaystyle=\sigma(W_{o}\cdot[h_{t-1},x_{t}]+b_{o})

(8)

\displaystyle h_{t}

\displaystyle=o_{t}\cdot\tanh(C_{t})

(9)

where
\sigma
denotes the sigmoid activation function,
W
and
b
represent learned weight matrices and bias vectors, and
f_{t}
,
i_{t}
,
o_{t}
are the forget, input, and output gates respectively.

The decoder component mirrors the encoder structure. It consists of a bidirectional LSTM layer with 64 hidden units followed by a fully connected output layer. The decoder reconstructs the original CWT representation from the compressed latent representation, aiming to minimize the reconstruction error for normal samples while producing larger errors for anomalous samples.

The network is trained using mean squared error (MSE) loss between the input and reconstructed scalograms:

\mathcal{L}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}||X_{i}-\hat{X}_{i}||^{2}

(10)

where
X_{i}
represents the input scalogram,
\hat{X}_{i}
is the reconstruction, and
N
is the batch size. The model was trained using the Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD) optimizer with a learning rate of 0.001, momentum of 0.9, and weight decay of 1e-5. The network trained with early stopping based on validation loss plateau to prevent overfitting.

Gravitational wave detection operates on the principle that signals containing true astrophysical events will exhibit higher reconstruction error compared to pure noise segments. For each test sample, we compute the reconstruction error:

E=||X-\hat{X}||^{2}

(11)

Samples with reconstruction error exceeding a predetermined threshold
\tau
are classified as potential gravitational wave candidates. The threshold is optimized using precision-recall analysis on validation data to maximize recall while maintaining precision above 90%, ensuring both high detection sensitivity and acceptable false alarm rates.

Model performance is evaluated using standard binary classification metrics:

Precision

\displaystyle=\frac{TP}{TP+FP}

(12)

Recall

\displaystyle=\frac{TP}{TP+FN}

(13)

F1-Score

\displaystyle=\frac{2\cdot\text{Precision}\cdot\text{Recall}}{\text{Precision}+\text{Recall}}

(14)

where
TP
,
FP
, and
FN
represent true positives, false positives, and false negatives respectively. Additionally, we compute the Area Under the Precision-Recall Curve (AUPRC) as a threshold-independent performance measure particularly relevant for imbalanced datasets typical in gravitational wave detection scenarios.

4
Synthetic Data

While real LIGO data provides authentic gravitational wave signals, synthetic data generation offers controlled experimental conditions essential for method validation and parameter sensitivity analysis. Synthetic waveforms allow precise control over signal characteristics, noise levels, and astrophysical parameters, enabling systematic evaluation of the CWT-LSTM autoencoder’s performance across different signal types and signal-to-noise ratios. This controlled environment facilitates comparison with established detection methods and provides insights into the model’s behavior under idealized conditions before applying it to the more complex and variable real LIGO data.

4.1
Synthetic Data Generation

We generate realistic synthetic gravitational wave signals representing binary black hole (BBH) coalescences using post-Newtonian waveform approximations. The gravitational wave strain is modeled as:

h(t)=h_{+}(t)\cos(2\psi)+h_{\times}(t)\sin(2\psi)

(15)

where
h_{+}(t)
and
h_{\times}(t)
are the plus and cross polarizations, and
\psi
is the polarization angle randomly sampled from
[0,2\pi]

The frequency evolution follows the post-Newtonian expansion for the inspiral phase:

f(t)=f_{0}\left(\frac{\tau}{\tau_{0}}\right)^{-3/8}

(16)

where
f_{0}=35
Hz is the initial frequency,
\tau=t_{c}-t
is the time to coalescence, and
\tau_{0}
is the initial time to coalescence. The instantaneous phase evolves as
\phi(t)=2\pi\int_{0}^{t}f(t^{\prime})dt^{\prime}

\mathcal{M}_{c}

(17)

where

is the gravitational constant and

is the speed of light. Binary system parameters are drawn from astrophysically motivated distributions:

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{c}

We model realistic detector noise using the Advanced LIGO design sensitivity curve, incorporating both fundamental noise sources and instrumental artifacts. The power spectral density (PSD) follows the analytical approximation:

S_{n}(f)=S_{0}\left[\left(\frac{f}{f_{0}}\right)^{-4.14}+5+3\left(\frac{f}{f_{0}}\right)^{2}\right]

(20)

where

Hz

and

Hz represent the characteristic noise amplitude and knee frequency respectively. Colored Gaussian noise matching the LIGO PSD is generated using the frequency-domain method: generating white Gaussian noise
\tilde{n}_{\text{white}}(f)
in the frequency domain, applying spectral shaping
\tilde{n}(f)=\tilde{n}_{\text{white}}(f)\sqrt{S_{n}(f)}

n(t)=\text{IFFT}[\tilde{n}(f)]

Signals are injected into noise with optimal signal-to-noise ratios (SNRs) distributed according to:

\rho_{\text{opt}}=\sqrt{4\int_{f_{\text{low}}}^{f_{\text{high}}}\frac{|\tilde{h}(f)|^{2}}{S_{n}(f)}df}

SNRs are drawn from the range [8, 25], representing the spectrum from threshold-level to highly significant detections. Each sample represents a 4-second time series sampled at 512 Hz, yielding 2048 data points per observation. This duration captures the entire inspiral phase for the considered mass range while maintaining computational tractability.

5
LIGO Data

The transition from synthetic to real LIGO data presents unique challenges inherent to authentic gravitational wave observations. Real detector noise exhibits complex spectral characteristics, non-stationary behavior, and instrumental artifacts absent in synthetic simulations. Additionally, gravitational wave signals in LIGO data span diverse astrophysical sources with varying signal morphologies, requiring robust detection methods capable of identifying signals across different frequency ranges and temporal evolution patterns. This section evaluates the CWT-LSTM autoencoder’s performance on authentic LIGO observations from the first observing run, demonstrating the method’s applicability to real-world gravitational wave detection scenarios.

5.1
Data Sources

The gravitational wave data used in this study originates from multiple LIGO observing runs (O1, O2, and O3a) which are publicly available through the Gravitational Wave Open Science Center (GWOSC)
[
13
]
. The O1 observing run spanned from September 12, 2015, to January 19, 2016, shortly after the initial detection of GW150914. The O2 run occurred from November 30, 2016, to August 25, 2017, while O3a ran from April 1, 2019, to October 1, 2019. This multi-run dataset provides comprehensive validation for our anomaly detection approach because it contains labeled gravitational wave events spanning different detector configurations and noise characteristics, along with extensive periods of clean detector noise from each observing run.

During training anomalies ideally should not be present which is why we use the clean noise data detected at the Hanford (H1) detector. Instrumental artifacts, data quality issues, or maintenance activities are actively avoided if known so that the training data is not contaminated. A total of 418 clean noise samples were collected for training.

For testing, we make use of confirmed gravitational wave events from the O1, O2, and O3a catalogs mixed with additional clean noise samples to create a realistic evaluation scenario. The gravitational wave events were obtained from the same H1 detector to maintain consistency across observing runs. Each event was extracted as a 4-second segment centered on the gravitational wave arrival time, providing sufficient context for signal analysis. A total of 24 gravitational wave events were successfully processed, with one additional event discarded due to data quality issues (NaN or infinite values) during preprocessing.

The data acquisition process utilized the GWpy Python library
[
14
]
, which provides direct access to GWOSC data through the
TimeSeries.fetch_open_data()
method. This approach ensures reproducibility and eliminates the need for complex data authentication procedures, while maintaining access to the full resolution and quality of the original LIGO data.

5.2
Data Preprocessing

Raw gravitational wave data from LIGO observing runs consists of 4-second segments sampled at 4096 Hz, which results in 16,384 data points per segment. These segments represent the strain measurements from the H1 detector, capturing the minute distortions in spacetime caused by passing gravitational waves.

The first preprocessing step involves downsampling the data from 4096 Hz to 1024 Hz to reduce computational requirements while maintaining sufficient frequency resolution for gravitational wave detection. This downsampling reduces each segment from 16,384 to 4,096 data points. The downsampling is performed using standard signal processing techniques to avoid aliasing artifacts.

The second preprocessing step applies CWT to convert time-domain strain data into time-frequency representations. We employ the Morlet wavelet with scales spanning 1 to 64, corresponding to frequencies from approximately 16 Hz to 1024 Hz. This frequency range encompasses the primary gravitational wave emission from compact binary mergers. The CWT transformation generates complex-valued time-frequency representations with dimensions corresponding to scales and time points. For the downsampled data, this produces representations with 8 frequency scales and 4,096 time points, where the magnitude captures signal strength and the phase contains temporal information.

For the the third preprocessing step, the CWT representation is normalized using z-score normalization across the entire dataset to prepare it to be ingested by the LSTM autoencoder. This normalization ensures that the input features have zero mean and unit variance, which is essential for stable training of deep learning models. The normalization parameters are computed from the training data and applied consistently to both training and test sets to prevent data leakage.

The final preprocessing step involves reshaping the normalized CWT data into the format required by the LSTM autoencoder. The data is organized with dimensions corresponding to batch size, sequence length, and features, where sequence length corresponds to the time dimension and features correspond to the frequency scales. This format enables the LSTM to process the temporal evolution of the frequency content, which is crucial for detecting the characteristic chirp patterns of gravitational wave signals.

5.3
Results

The CWT-LSTM autoencoder model achieved an average precision of 0.932. The precision-recall analysis (Figure

At the optimal threshold, the model achieved a perfect precision of 1.0 with a recall of 0.833. The confusion matrix shown in Figure

This distinct separation indicates robust anomaly detection capabilities, with the optimal threshold at 0.596 effectively distinguishing between normal detector noise and anomalous gravitational wave events.

These results demonstrate that the CWT-LSTM autoencoder approach scales effectively to LIGO data. The successful detection of 20 confirmed gravitational wave events out of 24 validates the practical applicability of this template-free detection method for gravitational wave astronomy.

8
Discussion and Conclusion

The CWT-LSTM autoencoder demonstrated strong performance on both synthetic gravitational wave data and real LIGO data. When applied to LIGO data, the CWT-LSTM autoencoder achieved an AUC of 0.979 and AP of 0.932 and perfect precision with a recall of 0.833, correctly identifying 20 out of 24 gravitational wave signals with zero false positives.

The 4 missed gravitational wave events are challenging cases characterized by lower signal-to-noise ratios or more complex signal morphologies. The missed events include GW151226 (SNR
\sim
13, 8.9+7.7
M_{\odot}
binary), GW151012 (SNR
\sim

M_{\odot}

\sim

The missed events fall near the optimal threshold in the reconstruction error distribution. Training on larger datasets with more diverse noise characteristics and signal morphologies could potentially improve detection sensitivity for these missed events because the model would learn more robust representations of the noise-signal boundary.

This work demonstrates template-free gravitational wave detection using CWT-coupled unsupervised deep learning. Unlike matched filtering approaches that require theoretical templates, our method learns the statistical properties of detector noise and identifies deviations that may correspond to gravitational wave signals. This approach does not require prior knowledge of signal characteristics and can potentially identify novel gravitational wave sources. This represents a paradigm shift from matched filtering techniques requiring pre-computed template banks based on theoretical waveform models.

The integration of CWT with unsupervised deep learning provides time-frequency decomposition for gravitational wave signals, capturing temporal evolution and frequency content. When coupled with LSTM autoencoders, CWT representations enable learning of noise characteristics while maintaining sensitivity to anomalous signals. This CWT-deep learning combination has been validated on real LIGO data for the first time.

Future work will focus on scaling up data volume with additional noise samples and gravitational wave events as more data becomes available, extending validation to additional observing runs where the model can be applied to unlabeled data to identify potential gravitational wave candidates for community analysis. Alternative architectures will be explored, including transformer-based autoencoders and ConvLSTM architectures. ConvLSTM approaches are particularly promising for CWT scalogram data, as the convolutional operations in the LSTM gate equations naturally capture the 2D spatial structure inherent in time-frequency representations, potentially improving detection of frequency-localized features and temporal correlations within spectrograms.
