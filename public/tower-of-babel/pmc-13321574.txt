Tumor-targeted liposomal RNAi therapy suppresses breast cancer and modulates tumor microenvironment
Authors: Sujeong Lee, Seung Hee Choi, Hui Bang Cho, Chae Won Cho, Jungsun Lee, Gyuwon So, Hye Jin Kim, Kyung-Soon Park, Keun-Hong Park
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLiposomes are widely used nanocarriers, but their clinical translation remains limited by poor tumor selectivity and inefficient nucleic acid delivery. To address these challenges, we designed multifunctional liposomes incorporating phenylboronic acid (PBA) for tumor targeting, ginsenoside Rh2 (Rh2) as both a cholesterol-mimetic stabilizer and intrinsic anticancer agent, and small interfering RNA against vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF). VEGF was selected for its pivotal role in tumor angiogenesis and to counteract the paradoxical pro-angiogenic effect of Rh2. PBA-modified liposomes exhibited enhanced tumor accumulation and cellular uptake compared with non-targeted controls. Rh2 induced direct cytotoxicity, while VEGF silencing further suppressed angiogenesis together producing synergistic antitumor activity. In vivo, Rh2–PBA–siVEGF liposomes (RhPLIPO-siVEGF) and Rh2–PBA–Negative Control siRNA (siNC) liposomes (RhPLIPO-siNC) were evaluated in a 4T1 orthotopic breast tumor model. Both formulations elicited effects characterized by increased infiltration of T cells and M1 macrophages. Notably, RhPLIPO-siVEGF treatment significantly reduced tumor growth and microvascular density compared with RhPLIPO-siNC, confirming synergy between Rh2-mediated cytotoxicity and VEGF knockdown. Overall, this multifunctional liposomal system integrates tumor targeting, intrinsic cytotoxicity, and RNA interference-mediated anti-angiogenesis with minimal adverse effects, offering a potent and versatile nanoplatform for targeted breast cancer therapy.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12951-026-04450-x.Keywords: Phenylboronic acid (PBA), Ginsenoside Rh2, Multifunctional liposomes, SiRNA targeting VEGF (siVEGF), Immunomodulation, Triple-negative breast cancer

Introduction

Liposomes are nanoscale vesicles composed of phospholipid bilayers that mimic the structure of cell membranes, making them attractive carriers for biomedical applications. Their excellent biocompatibility, capacity to encapsulate both hydrophilic and hydrophobic molecules, and versatility in surface modification have led to widespread use in drug delivery and nanomedicine. Despite these advantages, liposomes face critical challenges when delivering hydrophobic agents, as their encapsulation efficiency and stability are often insufficient for clinical translation [1–4]. In addition, nonspecific biodistribution and rapid clearance from the bloodstream limit their therapeutic efficacy, underscoring the need for strategies that combine structural stability, tumor-targeting capability, and therapeutic potency [5–8].

One promising approach is to engineer liposomal surfaces with functional ligands that selectively recognize tumor-associated biomarkers. Sialic acid, frequently overexpressed on the surface of cancer cells, plays a pivotal role in cell–cell interactions, tumor growth, and metastasis [9–11]. Phenylboronic acid (PBA) exhibits high affinity for sialic acid residues, making it an effective ligand for tumor-selective delivery [12–14]. Functionalizing liposomes with PBA can enhance their accumulation in tumors and promote intracellular uptake through receptor-mediated pathways, while minimizing off-target interactions [15–17].

Beyond targeting, the therapeutic payload is critical for achieving meaningful anticancer activity. Ginsenoside Rh2 (Rh2), a pharmacologically active saponin derived from red ginseng, has been widely investigated for its antitumor properties. Rh2 can inhibit proliferation, induce DNA damage, and trigger apoptosis in cancer cells [18–20]. It also exerts immunomodulatory effects [21–25]. Importantly, Rh2 is amphiphilic and can be incorporated directly into lipid bilayers, allowing it to serve both as a structural component and an intrinsic anticancer agent [26–28]. However, at higher concentrations, Rh2 paradoxically promotes angiogenesis by upregulating vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), potentially offsetting its anticancer benefits. This dual effect highlights the need for a combinatorial strategy that retains Rh2’s cytotoxic activity while suppressing its undesired pro-angiogenic signaling.

Small interfering RNA (siRNA) offers a complementary therapeutic modality, as it can silence oncogenes and angiogenesis-related factors. In particular, siRNA targeting VEGF (siVEGF) effectively suppresses angiogenesis, thereby counteracting Rh2-induced VEGF upregulation while simultaneously enhancing apoptotic pathways. Nonetheless, the inherent instability of siRNA and its susceptibility to enzymatic degradation necessitate a protective and efficient delivery system, for which liposomes are well suited [29–31].

Here, we present multifunctional liposomes that integrate three synergistic components: (i) Rh2 incorporated into the lipid bilayer to confer intrinsic anticancer activity, (ii) surface-conjugated PBA to achieve sialic acid–mediated tumor targeting, and (iii) encapsulated siVEGF to inhibit angiogenesis and deprive tumors of nutrients (Scheme 1). We hypothesized that this design would enable stable siRNA delivery, enhance tumor selectivity, and generate synergistic anticancer effects. Structural and functional analyses confirmed successful liposome formation, efficient siVEGF encapsulation, and improved stability. In vitro studies demonstrated enhanced cellular uptake, DNA damage induction, and apoptosis, while in vivo experiments revealed selective tumor accumulation, significant tumor regression, and immunomodulatory effects. Collectively, this work establishes a strategy that combines bioactive lipids with gene-silencing therapeutics in a targeted nanocarrier platform, offering broad potential for advancing cancer nanomedicine.

Experimental section

Materials

1,2-di-O-octadecenyl-3-trimethylammonium propane (chloride salt, DOTMA), 1,2-distearoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphoethanolamine-N-(amino[polyethylene glycol]−2000) (DSPE-PEG2000-amine), 1,2-distearoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine (DSPC), 1,2-dioleoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphoethanolamine-N-(Cyanine 7) (18:1 Cyanine 7 PE), 1,2-dioleoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphoethanolamine-N-(lissamine rhodamine B sulfonyl) (ammonium salt, 18:1 Liss Rhod PE), and plant-derived cholesterol were purchased from Avanti Polar Lipids (Alabaster, AL, USA) and used without further purification. Ginsenoside Rh2 [20(S)-Ginsenoside Rh2; 20(S)-Rh2] was obtained from MedChemExpress (MCE, Monmouth Junction, NJ, USA). 4-Carboxy-3-fluorophenylboronic acid (PBA) was purchased from FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corporation (Tokyo, Japan), and 4-hydrophenylboronic acid was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). Enoxacin sesquihydrate (≥ 98% purity, CAS No. 74811-93-1) was obtained from Supelco (Bellefonte, PA, USA). Predesigned siRNAs targeting vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) and a negative control siRNA were purchased from Bioneer (Daejeon, Republic of Korea).

Preparation of liposomes

Liposomes were prepared using the thin-film hydration method. Briefly, lipids dissolved in chloroform were mixed at a molar ratio of DOTMA: Rh2 (or cholesterol): PBA-DSPE-PEG2000: DSPC: fluorescent lipid = 1:4:1:3:0.04. The solvent was removed by heating above 50 °C to form a thin lipid film. After complete evaporation of chloroform, the lipid film was hydrated with ultrapure distilled water. The resulting lipid suspension was sonicated in a water bath sonicator (Branson Bransonic 3800, Emerson Electric Co., Canada) for 10 min. For cargo loading, liposomes were mixed with siRNA (100 pmol) and enoxacin (28.9 µg; an optimized ratio for cellular treatment) at an N/P ratio of 3.9. The mixture was rapidly pipetted, gently vortexed, and incubated at room temperature (25 °C) for 10 min to allow cargo incorporation.

Characterization of liposomes

The hydrodynamic size and surface charge (zeta potential) of liposomes were measured by dynamic light scattering (DLS; Zetasizer Nano ZS, Malvern Panalytical, Malvern, UK). The morphology of liposomes was observed by transmission electron microscopy (TEM), which was performed at Eulji University (Seongnam-si, Korea).

siRNA encapsulation and stability

siRNA encapsulation efficiency was assessed by agarose gel electrophoresis. siRNA-loaded liposomes and naked siRNA (control) were electrophoresed on a 2% agarose gel containing Midori Green at 100 V for 20 min. Gels were visualized using a ChemiDOC imaging system (Bio-Rad, US), and band intensities of free siRNA versus liposome-encapsulated siRNA were quantified using ImageJ software (NIH).

The stability of encapsulated siRNA was evaluated following RNase A challenge. Briefly, siRNA-loaded liposomes were incubated with RNase A (30 µg/mL) at 37 °C for 0, 1, and 8 h. RNase A was subsequently inactivated by proteinase K treatment (100 µg/mL, 37 °C, 30 min. To release siRNA from liposomes, heparin (10-fold molar excess relative to siRNA) was added and incubated for 10 min. siRNA integrity was analyzed by agarose gel electrophoresis.

Protein corona formation

To evaluate protein corona formation, siRNA-loaded liposomes were incubated with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) solution at 37 °C for 8 h. Unbound BSA was removed by centrifugation at 5,000 × g for 10 min using Amicon Ultra-4 centrifugal filter units (100 kDa MWCO, Millipore), and liposomes with adsorbed protein corona were collected. The harvested samples were analyzed by SDS-PAGE (10% polyacrylamide gel, 100 V), followed by Coomassie Brilliant Blue staining. Stained gels were imaged using a ChemiDOC imaging system (Bio-Rad, US).

Cellular uptake kinetics

4T1 cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 1 × 10⁴ cells per well and incubated overnight. Liposomes were formulated with 18:1 Liss Rhod PE (Avanti Polar Lipids, US) as a fluorescent probe. Cells were treated with liposomes, and rhodamine fluorescence was measured at 10, 30, and 60 min post-treatment using a SpectraMax iD5 microplate reader (Molecular Devices, US) (Ex/Em ≈ 560/583 nm). Fluorescence values were normalized to untreated controls. At 60 min, cells were counterstained with 4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) and visualized using a confocal laser scanning microscope (CLSM; Olympus FV3000).

Sialic acid-mediated uptake

To assess sialic acid (SA)-dependent uptake of PBA-modified liposomes, 4T1 cells were seeded in 96-well plates at 2 × 10⁴ cells per well, and MCF10A cells were seeded at 4 × 10⁴ cells per well, followed by overnight incubation. For SA blocking, 4T1 cells were pretreated with 500 µM 4-hydroxyphenylboronic acid for 30 min prior to treatment with rhodamine-labeled liposomes. For comparative analysis, both 4T1 and MCF10A cells were incubated with rhodamine-labeled liposomes for 1 h. After treatment, the medium was removed, cells were washed, and rhodamine fluorescence was measured using a SpectraMax iD5 microplate reader (Molecular Devices). Fluorescence values were normalized to untreated controls.

Endosomal escape

4T1 cells were seeded in 96-well black plates (Cat. No. 33296, SPL) at a density of 1 × 10⁴ cells per well and incubated overnight. FAM-siRNA-loaded liposomes were added and incubated with cells for 4 h. After treatment, the medium was replaced with staining medium containing LysoTracker® probes (L12492, Invitrogen) at a final concentration of 75 nM, followed by 1 h incubation. Cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) at room temperature for 15 min and counterstained with DAPI for 5 min. The fluorescence intensity of FAM-siRNA was measured using a SpectraMax iD5 microplate reader (Molecular Devices), and cellular localization was visualized by CLSM (Olympus FV3000). Colocalization coefficients were calculated using cellSens Dimension software (v3.2, Olympus).

Immunofluorescence staining

4T1 cells were seeded in 96-well black plates at a density of 1 × 10⁴ cells per well and incubated overnight. Cells were treated with liposomes in complete medium and incubated for 24 h. Following treatment, cells were fixed with 4% PFA at room temperature for 15 min, washed, and permeabilized with 0.2% Triton X-100 in 0.1% BSA for 30 min. Cells were then incubated with anti-γH2A.X (phospho Ser139) antibody (1:1000, ab22551, Abcam) and counterstained with DAPI. Immunofluorescence imaging was performed using CLSM (Olympus FV3000), and γH2A.X foci were quantified using cellSens Dimension software (Olympus).

Cell cycle and apoptosis analysis

4T1 cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 1 × 10⁵ cells per well and incubated overnight. Cells were treated with liposomes, and after 6 h, the medium was replaced with fresh culture medium. At 24 h post-treatment, single-cell suspensions (1 × 10⁶ cells/mL) were prepared using trypsin–ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) (T/E) buffer and immediately subjected to flow cytometry.

For cell cycle analysis, cells were stained with FxCycle™ propidium iodide (PI)/RNase Staining Solution (F10797, Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Apoptosis was assessed using the eBioscience™ Annexin V Apoptosis Detection Kit (88–8007-74, Invitrogen). Briefly, cells were harvested, resuspended in Annexin V binding buffer at 5 × 10⁵ cells/mL, and stained with Annexin V-APC for 15 min at room temperature in the dark, followed by PI staining. Samples were immediately analyzed by flow cytometry to quantify apoptotic populations. Data acquisition was performed on a CytoFLEX flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter, US), and analysis was conducted using FlowJo software (Tree Star Inc.).

Proliferation and cell viability

4T1 cells were seeded in 12-well plates at a density of 5 × 10⁴ cells per well and incubated overnight. Liposomes were added at amounts equivalent to those used for siRNA transfection. After 6 h, the medium was replaced with fresh culture medium (designated as 0 h). Cell viability was measured assessed at 0, 24, and 48 h using the CellVia Cell Viability Assay Kit (LF-EZ1001, Abfrontier, Korea). Absorbance was recorded at 440 nm using a SpectraMax microplate reader (Molecular Devices), and data were analyzed with SoftMax Pro software.

siRNA transfection

4T1 cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 1 × 10⁵ cells per well and incubated overnight. siRNA transfection was performed using liposomes or Lipofectamine 2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific). For liposome-mediated transfection, 60 pmol of siRNA was mixed with liposomes at an N/P ratio of 3.9, with enoxacin co-loaded to achieve a final concentration of 50 µM in the culture medium. For Lipofectamine transfection, 60 pmol of siRNA was complexed according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Liposome–siRNA mixtures were diluted in serum-free medium and incubated at room temperature for 10 min before being added to cells. Cells were cultured at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂. After 6 h, the medium was replaced with complete medium, and gene knockdown efficiency was evaluated at 24 h post-transfection by quantitative reverse transcription PCR (qRT-PCR).

Analysis of mRNA levels

Total RNA was extracted using an RNA prep kit (K-3140, Bioneer, Korea), and complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized with M-MLV Reverse Transcriptase (28025013, Invitrogen). Real-time PCR was performed using SYBR Green Master Mix (Takara, Japan). Primer sequences were as follows: glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) forward: 5′-CTCCCACTCTTCCACCTTCG-3′, GAPDH reverse: 5′-GCCTCTCTTGCTCAGTGTCC-3′, vascular endothelial growth factor A (VEGFA) forward: 5′- CTGCTGTAACGATGAAGCCCTG-3′, VEGFA reverse: 5′- GCTGTAGGAAGCTCATCTCTCC-3′.

Analysis of protein levels

VEGF protein levels were quantified using a Mouse VEGFA ELISA Kit (RK00028, ABclonal, US) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Cell culture supernatants were collected 6 h after replacing the medium with serum-free medium at 24 h post-siRNA transfection.

HUVEC tube formation

Matrigel (growth factor-reduced, Corning, US) was thawed on ice, and 150 µL was added to each well of a 24-well plate and spread evenly using a chilled pipette tip. Plates were gently swirled and incubated at 37 °C for 30–60 min to allow polymerization. Human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs, 8 × 10⁴ cells/well) were seeded in 1 mL of endothelial growth medium on the polymerized Matrigel. After 2 h of incubation, the medium was replaced with conditioned medium collected from 4T1 cell cultures. After 8 h of incubation, cells were fixed and stained with phalloidin-conjugated probes (A22287, Invitrogen). Samples were imaged using a confocal fluorescence microscope. Tube formation was quantified using ImageJ software (NIH, Bethesda, MD, US) with the Angiogenesis Analyzer plugin, including number of junctions, meshes, and total tube length.

Biodistribution

Female BALB/c mice (6–8 weeks old) were orthotopically implanted with 4T1 breast cancer cells (2 × 105 cells in 100 µL phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), injected into the right mammary fat pad). When tumors reached ~200–300 mm³, Rh2-liposomes containing Cy7-conjugated lipid with varying PBA content (0, 10, or 20%) were administered via tail vein injection.

At 12, 24, and 48 h post-injection, mice were anesthetized with isoflurane in a respiratory anesthesia chamber and subjected to whole-body fluorescence imaging under identical exposure times and settings using an in vivo imaging system (UINV-420, Davinch K, Korea).

At each time point, mice were euthanized and major organs (tumor, liver, spleen, lung, kidney, heart) were excised and rinsed with PBS. Ex vivo fluorescence imaging was performed immediately using identical acquisition settings. Fluorescence intensity in for each region of interest (ROI) was quantified with Multi ROI Analysis software (UINV-420), and mean fluorescence signals for each organ were normalized to background.

In vivo antitumor efficacy

The antitumor efficacy of PBA–Rh₂ liposomes was evaluated in female BALB/c mice (6–8 weeks old). Mice were orthotopically implanted with 2 × 10⁵ 4T1 breast cancer cells into the right mammary fat pad. Treatment commenced when tumors reached approximately 50 mm³ in volume. Mice were administered 20 µg of siRNA per mouse (N/P ratio = 3.9) together with RhPLipo, delivering Rh2 at 31 mg/kg (Rh2 equivalent) per injection. As a control, an equivalent volume of PBS was injected. Liposomes formulations were delivered via intravenous injections three times per week for two weeks. An additional liposome dose was administered one day prior to euthanasia to further assess siRNA effects. Tumor growth was monitored with digital calipers three times per week, and tumor volume was calculated as V = (L×W2)/2 (where L = length and W = width). Body weights were measured throughout the study to assess systemic toxicity. At study endpoint, tumors were excised and weighed to determine tumor burden. Tissue apoptosis was assessed using the DeadEnd™ Fluorometric TUNEL System kit (G3250, Promega).

All animal experiments were conducted in compliance with protocols approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of CHA University (IACUC240127, IACUC250123). Mice were obtained from JA Bio (Gyeonggi-do, Republic of Korea) and housed in a semi-specific pathogen-free facility at CHA University (Seongnam, Republic of Korea).

Tumor tissue clearing, 3D reconstruction, and volumetric analysis of blood vessels

Tumor tissues were sectioned into 1 mm slices and subjected to tissue clearing using the CytoVista™ Tissue Clearing/Staining Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) following the manufacturer’s protocol. For immunostaining, samples were incubated with anti-CD34 antibody (EP373Y, Abcam) as the primary antibody. After washing, tissues were co-incubated with goat anti-rabbit IgG (H + L) cross-adsorbed secondary antibody, Alexa Fluor™ 633 (A-21070, Thermo Fisher Scientific), together with DAPI for nuclear staining. The cleared and stained tissues were imaged using a CLSM. CD34-stained vasculature was reconstructed in three dimensions and quantified using IMARIS software (Bitplane, Oxford Instruments). Vascular volume was calculated using the Surface Rendering function of IMARIS after background subtraction and threshold-based segmentation.

Tumor tissue immunofluorescence staining

Tumor tissues were excised, fixed in 4% PFA for 48 h, and cryoprotected in 30% sucrose at 4 °C overnight. Samples were embedded in optimal cutting temperature (OCT) compound, and cryosections (10 μm) were prepared using a Leica CM3050S cryostat. Sections were blocked with 5% BSA and permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 for 15 min. For immunostaining, sections were incubated with fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC) anti-mouse CD8a antibody (100706, BioLegend) and PerCP/Cyanine5.5 anti-mouse F4/80 antibody (123128, BioLegend) for 3 h. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Fluorescence images were acquired using a CLSM.

Spleen immune cell profiling by flow cytometry

At the experimental endpoint, spleens were harvested and processed into single-cell suspensions. Red blood cells (RBCs) were lysed using RBC lysis buffer. Cells were stained with fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies against CD45, CD3, CD8, CD4, F4/80, CD86, and CD206 to define immune subsets. Flow cytometry was performed, and populations were quantified as follows: CD8⁺ cytotoxic T cells were expressed as a percentage of CD45⁺CD3⁺ cells; the CD4/CD8 ratio was calculated from CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ subsets; macrophages were defined as F4/80⁺ cells, with CD86⁺ representing M1-like, and CD206⁺ representing M2-like populations, expressed as percentages of CD45⁺ cells.

Statistical analyses

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism V.7.0 software (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA, USA). Differences between groups were assessed by Student’s t-test. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical significance was defined as follows: ns, not significant; *p < 0.05; **p < 0.01; ***p < 0.001; and ****p < 0.0001.

Results and discussion

Rh2 enhances siRNA delivery compared with cholesterol in liposomal formulations

Multifunctional liposomes were fabricated using a rationally designed lipid mixture composed of phenylboronic acid–distearoylphosphatidylethanolamine–polyethylene glycol 2000 (PBA–DSPE–PEG2k), 1,2-di-O-octadecenyl-3-trimethylammonium propane (DOTMA), distearoylphosphatidylcholine (DSPC), fluorescent lipids, and either cholesterol or ginsenoside Rh2 (Rh2) (Fig. 1A). This formulation was designed to simultaneously achieve stability, tumor selectivity, nucleic acid delivery, and therapeutic activity, thereby establishing a multifunctional nanoplatform.

Physicochemical characterization and siRNA stability of multifunctional liposomes. (A) Schematic illustration of siRNA-loaded liposome formulation using PBA–DSPE-PEG2k, DOTMA, DSPC, fluorescent lipids, and either cholesterol or ginsenoside Rh2. (B) DLS measurements of liposome size for sterol-free, cholesterol-containing, and Rh2-containing formulations (left). Zeta potential measurements of liposomes (right). (C) siRNA encapsulation efficiency assessed by Ribogreen assay. (D) TEM images of sterol-free, cholesterol-containing, and Rh2-containing liposomes. (E) Serum stability of liposomes over time. (F) SDS-PAGE analysis of liposome stability after 8 h incubation in serum. (G) Stability of siRNA encapsulated in liposomes over time. PBA–DSPE–PEG2k was synthesized via 1-ethyl-3-(3-dimethylaminopropyl)carbodiimide (EDC)-mediated coupling between the carboxyl group of PBA and the terminal amine of DSPE–PEG2k, as illustrated schematically in Figure S1. Successful conjugation of PBA to DSPE–PEG2k was confirmed by 1H NMR and Fourier transform infrared (FT-IR) spectroscopy (Figures S2–S3). Incorporation of PBA–DSPE–PEG2k into liposomes resulted in only minimal changes in surface charge compared with unmodified controls (Figure S4). To determine the optimal degree of PEGylation, the ratios of other lipid components were kept constant while the DSPE–PEG2k content was varied (Figure S5).

We next investigated the role of sterols, which are critical determinants of liposomal stability and membrane permeability. Conventional cholesterol was compared with Rh2, which functions as both a sterol-like structural component and an anticancer agent. Seven liposomal formulations were prepared: PBA-liposomes without sterol (NoPLipo), PBA-liposomes containing 20–50% cholesterol (ChPLipo), and PBA-liposomes containing 20–50% Rh2 (RhPLipo). Physicochemical properties of these formulations were first evaluated (Fig. 1B). Liposomes lacking sterol (NoPLipo) or containing 20% cholesterol exhibited the largest diameters, whereas those containing 40–50% cholesterol or 20–50% Rh2 maintained smaller and more uniform sizes, indicating enhanced suitability for intravenous administration. Zeta potential values were approximately +40 mV for most formulations, reflecting their cationic characteristics. However, liposomes with 50% Rh2 exhibited a slightly reduced surface charge (~10 mV lower), suggesting excessive Rh2 incorporation into the lipid bilayer.

Encapsulation efficiency of siRNA was assessed using a Ribogreen assay (Fig. 1C). NoPLipo exhibited an encapsulation efficiency of approximately 80%. RhPLipo showed comparable efficiencies (~80%) at 20–40% Rh2, with a slight decrease to ~70% at 50% Rh2. In contrast, ChPLipo displayed lower encapsulation efficiencies, with values of 53%, 46%, and 42% at 20%, 40%, and 50% cholesterol, respectively. These results indicate that RhPLipo maintain relatively high encapsulation efficiency across compositions, whereas ChPLipo exhibit a concentration-dependent decrease, suggesting that RhPLipo may be more favorable for siRNA delivery than ChPLipo.

Optimization of DOTMA content was also performed (Figure S6). Ratios of DOTMA: DSPC ranging from 10%:70% to 80%:0% were evaluated. Zeta potential measurements revealed +40 mV at 10% DOTMA, + 47–48 mV at 20–40%, and up to +60 mV at 50–80%. Because excessively high surface charge (+60 mV) can be unfavorable, formulations containing ≥ 50% DOTMA were excluded. Gene knockdown experiments using RhPLipo confirmed that 20% DOTMA provided the most balanced performance, and this ratio was selected for subsequent studies.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) confirmed that the optimized liposomes maintained a stable spherical morphology with uniform size distribution (Fig. 1D). Serum stability was assessed by incubating liposomes in 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) and monitoring size changes. All liposomes remained stable for up to 6 h, but marked changes were observed at 8 h (Fig. 1E, S7). In particular, ChPLipo, which measured < 200 nm at 6 h, increased in size to approximately 800 nm at 8 h. In contrast, NoPLipo and RhPLipo showed little to no significant change, suggesting superior bloodstream stability for RhPLipo compared to ChPLipo. Sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS–PAGE) at 8 h revealed protein corona formation on all liposomes, with the most prominent BSA band observed for ChPLipo (Fig. 1F). Consistent with the size increase in Fig. 1E, this indicates that ChPLipo adsorb more serum proteins. Because liposomes that generate fewer protein coronas are more likely to reach their target sites, these results highlight the superiority of Rh2 over cholesterol as a liposomal component for in vivo applications. siRNA leakage was further evaluated following exposure to ribonuclease A (RNase A) at 0, 1, and 8 h (Fig. 1G, S8). Free siRNA degraded rapidly, while liposome-encapsulated siRNA remained detectable in all groups, demonstrating stable retention for up to 8 h.

Finally, co-encapsulation of siRNA and enoxacin, a known RNA interference (RNAi) enhancer, was investigated (Figure S9). Co-loaded liposomes displayed negligible differences in zeta potential (~3 mV) compared with siRNA-only liposomes. Enoxacin loading efficiency reached ~90%, and gel retardation assays confirmed that enoxacin did not interfere with siRNA encapsulation.

Collectively, these results demonstrate that replacing cholesterol with Rh2 not only confers structural stability but also enhances siRNA delivery efficiency, establishing Rh2-based multifunctional liposomes as advanced carriers for RNAi therapy.

PBA-functionalized liposomes enhance tumor targeting, intracellular RNA release, and VEGF silencing

Conjugation with PBA played a pivotal role in enhancing the tumor-targeting capability of liposomes. As shown in Fig. 2A, PBA-conjugated liposomes selectively bound to sialic acid residues abnormally overexpressed on tumor cell membranes, thereby enabling efficient receptor-mediated internalization compared with non-specific endocytosis. This specific PBA–sialic acid interaction represented a clear improvement over conventional passive targeting approaches, allowing selective tumor accumulation while minimizing off-target uptake in normal tissues. Importantly, the efficiency of this process was strongly dependent on ligand density. In addition, similar to sialic acid–mediated uptake, PBA-conjugated liposomes were able to interact with glycoproteins of the endosomal or lysosomal membrane after uptake, thereby accelerating intracellular release [13, 17, 32, 33].

When comparing RhPLipo with equivalent PEG content but varying PBA density, cellular uptake efficiency was clearly dependent on the degree of PBA conjugation over time. The 20% PBA liposomes exhibited markedly higher internalization than the 0% and 10% formulations (Fig. 2B). In 4T1 cells, 20% PBA liposomes displayed substantial uptake even at early time points, whereas the 0% and 10% formulations showed limited uptake. Confocal microscopy at 1 h confirmed liposome uptake in all groups (0–20% PBA); however, strong cytoplasmic fluorescence was observed only in the 20% PBA group, while non-conjugated liposomes displayed weak intracellular signals.

To validate sialic acid–mediated uptake, 4T1 cells were preincubated with free phenylboronic acid (PB) prior to liposome exposure. As shown in Fig. 2C, PB pretreatment did not alter uptake of 0% or 10% PBA liposomes but significantly reduced uptake of 20% PBA liposomes, confirming that high PBA incorporation leads to sialic acid–dependent uptake. To further assess the role of sialic acid, we compared cellular uptake between tumorigenic 4T1 cells (sialic acid–high) and non-tumorigenic MCF10A cells (sialic acid–low) [11, 16, 34–38]. Consistent with the competitive inhibition results, 20% PBA-liposomes exhibited markedly higher uptake in 4T1 cells compared to MCF10A cells (Fig. 2C, left). Together, these findings demonstrate that PBA-functionalized liposomes achieve selective cellular uptake through recognition of sialic acid residues, with selectivity dependent on PBA surface density. Notably, the data suggest a threshold effect: once PBA incorporation surpasses a critical level, multivalent interactions strongly enhance tumor cell–specific recognition and internalization.

As mentioned above, PBA can facilitate rapid endosomal or lysosomal escape by binding to glycoproteins on the endosomal or lysosomal membrane following sialic acid–mediated uptake. To examine whether PBA promotes intracellular release, 6-carboxyfluorescein (FAM)-labeled siRNA (FAM-siRNA) was encapsulated into liposomes containing 0–20% PBA, and colocalization with lysosomes was evaluated using Lysotracker. At 4 h post-treatment, 0% PBA liposomes showed minimal detectable FAM-siRNA fluorescence, which was largely colocalized with Lysotracker. Similarly, 10% PBA liposomes exhibited extensive overlap, though partial separation of signals was observed. In contrast, 20% PBA liposomes showed markedly reduced colocalization and widespread cytoplasmic distribution of FAM-siRNA (Fig. 2D). Because FAM fluorescence is quenched in acidic pH and restored under neutral conditions, intracellular fluorescence intensity of FAM-siRNA was also quantified. Consistent with confocal microscopy results, the 20% PBA liposomes displayed the highest fluorescence intensity, whereas no significant difference was observed between the 0% and 10% PBA groups. Quantitative colocalization analysis further revealed coefficients approaching zero in the 20% PBA group, indicating nearly complete endosomal/lysosomal escape. These findings demonstrate that PBA conjugation promotes efficient intracellular release while minimizing endosomal/lysosomal degradation, thereby maximizing bioavailability of the therapeutic payload. To further investigate intracellular release, colocalization with an endosomal marker was assessed under conditions where endosomal trafficking was blocked by wortmannin (Figure S11). Liposomes were incubated for 30 min to capture early events. Although colocalization was observed across all groups, the extent was lower in the 20% PBA liposomes than in the 0% or 10% formulations, suggesting that higher PBA density accelerates escape from endosomes at early time points.

Functionally, siRNA-loaded PBA liposomes significantly reduced VEGF mRNA and protein expression compared with non-targeted formulations (Fig. 2E–F). Both mRNA and protein knockdown efficiencies were highest in the 20% PBA group. Notably, VEGF secretion by 4T1 cells was reduced by 66% with Lipofectamine (LFM), 49% and 50% with 0% and 10% PBA liposomes, and 64% with 20% PBA liposomes, confirming that enhanced endosomal escape translated into superior gene silencing.

These results can be attributed to two synergistic mechanisms. First, sialic acid–mediated uptake facilitates selective internalization into tumor cells, maximizing intracellular payload delivery. Second, high density PBA promotes rapid endosomal/lysosomal escape, reducing siRNA degradation and increasing cytoplasmic availability. Together, these findings highlight the critical role of ligand density in enabling multivalent interactions that ensure both selective uptake and effective intracellular delivery, suggesting that optimizing PBA incorporation is a promising strategy to enhance liposomal siRNA efficacy in tumor-specific pathways.

Rh2-PBA liposomes induce potent anticancer effects by triggering DNA damage and apoptosis

As described earlier, ginsenoside Rh2, a cholesterol-analogous compound, has been reported to exert anticancer activity. In this study, we specifically evaluated the anticancer capability of Rh2 when incorporated into liposomes. Figure 3A illustrates the proposed mechanism: Rh2 induces DNA damage in the nuclei of cancer cells, leading to cell cycle arrest and subsequent cell death.

(B) Immunofluorescence (IF) staining of γ-H2A.X (red) showing DNA damage in 4T1 cells treated with different liposome formulations. CLSM images (top) and quantification of γ-H2A.X foci per cell (n = 20) (bottom). To assess these effects, 4T1 breast cancer cells were incubated with different liposome formulations for 6 h. The experimental groups included NoPLipo, ChPLipo, and RhPLipo, with doses matched to those used in siRNA delivery experiments. DNA damage was evaluated by immunofluorescence staining of γ-H2A.X foci, a marker of DNA double-strand breaks (DSBs) (Fig. 3B). The NoPLipo and ChPLipo groups displayed γ-H2A.X foci levels comparable to those of untreated controls, indicating minimal DNA damage. In contrast, RhPLipo treatment resulted in a marked increase in γ-H2A.X staining, demonstrating extensive DNA damage and substantial induction of DSBs. These findings indicate that RhPLipo induce significantly greater DNA damage than other liposomal formulations.

DNA damage leads to cell cycle arrest. As shown in Fig. 3C, cell cycle analysis revealed that 4T1 cells treated with NoPLipo or ChPLipo displayed a similar distribution of cells in the G0/G1 phase (~53%) as untreated controls (NT). In contrast, RhPLipo-treated cells showed a reduction in the G0/G1 phase to 43% (–10%). In the S phase, the NT, NoPLipo, and ChPLipo groups contained ~19% of cells, whereas the RhPLipo group exhibited a significant decrease to ~9%, representing more than a two-fold reduction. Rh2 is known to induce cell cycle arrest at the G1 phase, which is typically accompanied by decreases in both G1 and S populations with a concomitant increase in the G2/M population. Consistent with this, the proportion of cells in the G2/M phase increased from ~27% in NT, NoPLipo, and ChPLipo groups to 46.7% in the RhPLipo group, a 1.7-fold increase. Cells arrested at the G2/M phase are unable to complete division and are subsequently driven into programmed cell death.

Cell cycle arrest ultimately suppresses cell proliferation. To evaluate this, proliferation was measured at multiple time points using the WST-1 assay (Fig. 3D). The difference at 0 h reflected reduced viability in the RhPLipo group following 6 h of treatment. While NoPLipo and ChPLipo groups exhibited survival rates comparable to NT, RhPLipo treatment reduced viability to ~62%. At 24 and 48 h, NoPLipo and ChPLipo again showed growth similar to NT, whereas RhPLipo displayed a marked decrease in proliferation. This effect is attributable to Rh2-induced DNA damage and subsequent cell cycle arrest (Figure S12).

Because Rh2 activates both apoptotic and necrotic pathways, apoptosis was further assessed using annexin V/propidium iodide (PI) staining (Fig. 3E). NT, NoPLipo, and ChPLipo groups all showed > 97% live cells with ~0.6% necrosis. Apoptotic ratios were 1.2% and 1.5% in NoPLipo and ChPLipo groups, respectively. In contrast, RhPLipo treatment resulted in 72% live cells, 22% necrotic cells, and 5% apoptotic cells, indicating that Rh2 induces cell death via both necrosis and apoptosis.

The direct impact of Rh2 on cell death was further confirmed at the transcript level (Figure S13). In 4T1 cells treated with RhPLipo, mRNA expression of the antiapoptotic genes BCL2 and BIRC5 (survivin), as well as the DNA repair marker RAD51, was reduced by more than 50%. These findings demonstrate that RhPLipo not only enhance structural stability but also exert potent anticancer activity through DNA damage, cell cycle arrest, and induction of cell death pathways.

Rh2-PBA-siVEGF liposomes suppress angiogenesis by silencing tumor-derived VEGF in HUVEC cells

We observed that ginsenoside Rh2, while effectively inducing cancer cell death, simultaneously exerts a pro-angiogenic effect. This was evaluated by treating 4T1 cells with ChPLipo, free Rh2 at various concentrations, and RhPLipo-Negative Control siRNA (siNC). After 24 h, VEGF expression and secretion, as well as human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVEC) tube formation, were assessed. ChPLipo treatment showed no significant difference compared with untreated controls (NT), whereas free Rh2 markedly upregulated VEGF expression and secretion in a concentration-dependent manner (Fig. 4A–B). Correspondingly, enhanced endothelial tube formation was observed in HUVECs exposed to conditioned media from Rh2- or RhPLipo-treated 4T1 cells, which promoted dense and highly interconnected capillary-like networks (Fig. 4C).

Recognizing this pro-angiogenic liability is essential for the rational design of Rh2-based therapeutics. Rather than discarding Rh2, these findings underscore the need for combination strategies—such as co-delivery with siVEGF—that suppress undesired vascularization while preserving Rh2’s intrinsic cytotoxic potential. To address this limitation, we co-delivered VEGF-targeting siRNA (siVEGF) within RhPLipo, aiming to silence VEGF expression at the molecular level and thereby neutralize pro-angiogenic signaling without diminishing Rh2’s anticancer activity.

For this, siNC or siVEGF was encapsulated into liposomes and applied to 4T1 cells, followed by VEGF quantification after 24 h. Both LFM-siVEGF and ChPLipo-siVEGF significantly reduced VEGF mRNA and protein levels. Similarly, RhPLipo-siVEGF effectively suppressed Rh2-induced VEGF upregulation, restoring expression to NT levels (Fig. 4D–E). Conditioned media from 4T1 cells treated with each formulation were then applied to HUVECs for tube formation assays. Consistent with the VEGF results, siVEGF-treated groups disrupted endothelial tubule integrity, resulting in fragmented and disorganized networks with significant reductions in node number and tube length. Importantly, RhPLipo-siVEGF produced a similar effect compared with RhPLipo-siNC (Fig. 4F).

Collectively, these results demonstrate that siVEGF counteracts the pro-angiogenic liability of Rh2, converting a potential therapeutic drawback into a synergistic advantage. The successful integration of RNA interference highlights its role as a corrective safeguard in multifunctional nanoplatforms, enabling Rh2 to be repurposed into a safer anticancer agent. Notably, this co-delivery strategy preserves Rh2’s antiproliferative and pro-apoptotic effects while simultaneously preventing tumor-promoting angiogenesis, underscoring the therapeutic value of multifunctional liposomal systems that balance efficacy and safety.

Rh2-PBA liposomes effectively target tumors and improve biodistribution

To validate the in vitro binding and uptake advantage of PBA observed in Fig. 2 in an in vivo context, and to determine the optimal ligand density for tumor targeting, liposomes containing 0%, 10%, and 20% PBA were prepared (Fig. 5A). For noninvasive biodistribution tracking, the liposomes were labeled with a Cy7-conjugated lipid. A 4T1 breast tumor model was established in BALB/c mice, and intravenous administration of each formulation was performed once tumors reached ~200–300 mm³. At 12 h post-injection, whole-body fluorescence imaging revealed a clear PBA density–dependent distribution pattern (Fig. 5B). PBA-free liposomes produced weak, diffuse signals; 10% PBA liposomes showed moderate tumor localization; and 20% PBA liposomes exhibited markedly stronger fluorescence in tumors as well as liver tissues. Biodistribution profiling confirmed that the 20% PBA formulation achieved the highest tumor selectivity (Fig. 5C–D). This effect is consistent with enhanced ligand–receptor binding avidity [13, 14, 16, 17, 32], whereby clustered PBA moieties increase binding affinity for sialic acid while diminishing reliance on passive accumulation. To further characterize tumor accumulation and systemic clearance, time-resolved biodistribution analysis was performed in the 4T1 mouse model following intravenous injection of 20% PBA liposomes. Fluorescence was detectable in tumors at 12 h, became more pronounced at 24 h, and declined gradually with hepatic and renal clearance by 48 h (Fig. 5E–F, S14). Such prolonged tumor residence is advantageous for siRNA therapeutics, which require sustained intracellular presence for effective gene silencing. From a translational perspective, these findings highlight the importance of ligand density optimization in nanocarrier design. Insufficient PBA density results in poor tumor targeting, whereas excessive decoration could promote off-target uptake or alter biodistribution. The 20% PBA formulation provided an optimal balance between tumor selectivity and systemic clearance, supporting both therapeutic efficacy and safety. Consistently, this formulation significantly suppressed tumor growth compared with non-PBA-conjugated Rh2-PEG liposomes (RhLipo) (Figure S15). Collectively, the 20% PBA formulation represents a rationally optimized platform that integrates tumor selectivity, circulation persistence, and safety, thereby maximizing the therapeutic efficacy of multifunctional liposomal systems.

Rh2-PBA-siVEGF liposomes achieve potent in vivo tumor suppression via TME modulation

To evaluate the therapeutic efficacy of RhPLipo, a 4T1 breast tumor xenograft model was established in BALB/c mice. When tumors reached ~50 mm³, mice received intravenous injections of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, control), ChPLipo-siVEGF, RhPLipo-siNC, or RhPLipo-siVEGF three times per week for two weeks (Fig. 6A). Representative images of tumor-bearing mice revealed marked tumor suppression in the RhPLipo-siVEGF group compared with the control and other liposome groups (Fig. 6B). Body weight remained stable across all groups, indicating negligible systemic toxicity (Fig. 6C). In addition to monitoring body weight, we conducted plasma biochemical analyses (ALT and BUN) and histological examination (H&E staining) to assess systemic safety. No significant evaluation in liver or kidney injury markers were observed, and major organs showed no treatment-related pathological abnormalities (Figures S16), further supporting the safety of our liposomal delivery system. Tumor growth curves confirmed that RhPLipo-siVEGF significantly inhibited tumor progression (Fig. 6D), while excised tumors displayed markedly reduced size and weight relative to other groups (Fig. 6E–F). Histological analysis further supported these findings: TUNEL staining revealed a substantially higher proportion of apoptotic cells in the RhPLipo-siVEGF group than in the control or other treatment groups (Fig. 6G), indicating that enhanced antitumor efficacy is associated with increased tumor cell apoptosis. These results demonstrate that RhPLipo-siVEGF not only delays tumor progression but also prevents adaptive escape mechanisms frequently observed with monotherapies. Notably, RhPLipo-siNC failed to sustain efficacy due to paradoxical VEGF upregulation, whereas ChPLipo-siVEGF did not induce sufficient cytotoxicity. The superior efficacy of RhPLipo-siVEGF highlights the necessity of simultaneously suppressing angiogenesis and inducing apoptosis to achieve durable tumor control.

To further confirm the in vivo siRNA delivery efficiency of RhPLipo-siVEGF, VEGF mRNA levels in excised tumors were analyzed by quantitative PCR (qPCR). As shown in Fig. 7A, tumors treated with RhPLipo-siVEGF exhibited a pronounced reduction in VEGF expression compared with PBS, RhPLipo-siNC, or ChPLipo-siVEGF groups, demonstrating that the dual-functional liposomes efficiently delivered siVEGF and achieved potent gene silencing in vivo. Consistent with this, immunohistochemical staining for CD34 revealed reduced neovascularization in both ChPLipo-siVEGF and RhPLipo-siVEGF groups, whereas RhPLipo-siNC paradoxically increased vessel density due to Rh2-induced VEGF upregulation (Fig. 7B). Building on previous reports demonstrating the immunomodulatory potential of Rh2 [21, 22, 25, 39], we next examined the effects of RhPLipo-siVEGF on systemic immune cell populations. Splenocytes from treated mice were analyzed by flow cytometry to assess T cell and macrophage subsets. RhPLipo-siVEGF treatment significantly increased CD8⁺ T cells and decreased the CD4/CD8 ratio compared with controls or other liposome treatments (Fig. 7C–D), indicating a shift toward a cytotoxic T cell–dominant immune profile. Moreover, F4/80⁺ macrophage populations were elevated, with a pronounced enrichment of M1-like subsets in the spleen (Fig. 7E–G), suggesting activation of pro-inflammatory, antitumor macrophages. Consistently, immunofluorescence analysis of tumor tissues revealed increased infiltration of CD8⁺ T cells and M1-like macrophages (Fig. 7H), confirming that RhPLipo-siVEGF enhance immune activation within the tumor microenvironment. To further validate whether the observed immune cell infiltration correlates with functional activation, we analyzed cytokine production in tumor lysates using ELISA (Figure S17). The levels of IFN-γ and TNF-α, key mediators of anti-tumor immunity, were significantly elevated in the RhPLipo-siVEGF-treated group compared to control groups. These results demonstrate that our formulation not only increases the number of immune cells but also potentiates their functional effector activity, thereby promoting a robust pro-inflammatory microenvironment conductive to tumor suppression.

Collectively, these findings demonstrate that RhPLipo-siVEGF not only exert direct tumor-suppressive effects through VEGF knockdown and apoptosis induction but also amplify antitumor immunity by promoting CD8⁺ T cell expansion and M1 macrophage polarization.

Conclusion

In summary, this study aimed to overcome the limitations of conventional liposomes and develop a system capable of stably delivering siRNA while exerting intrinsic therapeutic effects. To achieve this, we employed a multifaceted strategy: (1) leveraging the structural and functional advantages of ginsenoside Rh2 to enhance liposomal stability and exploit its intrinsic anticancer and immunomodulatory activities; (2) incorporating phenylboronic acid (PBA) to enable tumor-specific targeting and improve intracellular siRNA release; and (3) utilizing VEGF-targeting siRNA to counteract the pro-angiogenic side effects of Rh2, thereby promoting anti-angiogenesis and enhancing tumor suppression. By integrating these elements, we designed a multifunctional RhPLipo-siVEGF nanoplatform that combines tumor-targeted delivery, VEGF silencing, and apoptosis induction within a single system. Beyond direct cytotoxicity, this formulation also remodels the tumor microenvironment, promoting immunomodulation and sustained antitumor efficacy. Collectively, our findings highlight the potential of RhPLipo as a next-generation nanocarrier for synergistic cancer therapy.
