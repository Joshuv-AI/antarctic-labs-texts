Developing pilot ecological indicator framework for detecting climate refugia, instability, and extinction debt using orchids
Authors: Jeewan Singh Jalal, Amira Sharief, Yasaswinee Rout, Amar Paul Singh, Lalit Kumar Sharma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryMountain forest biodiversity is highly vulnerable to climate-driven shifts in temperature and microclimate, yet conventional metrics often fail to capture long-term ecological risk. Using epiphytic orchids as climate-sensitive indicators, we modeled current and future distributions of 10 species in the Northern Western Ghats, India, under RCP2.6 and RCP8.5 scenarios for 2050 and 2070 using ensemble species distribution models (SDMs; receiver operating curve [ROC] = 0.80). We developed a spatially explicit conservation framework integrating four ecological indicators: priority index (PI), ecological sensitivity zoning (ESZ), habitat stability index (HSI), and extinction debt (ED). Precipitation of the warmest quarter and climatic seasonality were dominant drivers of distribution. Suitable habitats remained relatively stable under low emissions but contracted and shifted under high emissions. PI and ESZ identified climate refugia, HSI revealed limited long-term hotspot persistence, and ED highlighted hidden future biodiversity losses. Integrating these indicators provides a robust framework for climate-resilient conservation planning.Keywords: ecological indicators, climate change vulnerability, extinction debt, habitat stability, spatial prioritization

Introduction

The orchid family is considered to be one of the largest, morphologically and ecologically diverse families in the flowering plant kingdom, with an estimate of about 693 genera and 31,072 species (https://powo.science.kew.org/). They are valued for their ornamental, medicinal, conservation, and evolutionary significance and are found in nearly all vegetated parts of the globe with the notable exceptions of polar areas and extremely arid deserts.1,2,3 However, the majority of orchid species are distributed in tropical regions.1,2 Despite their global abundance and widespread occurrence, the majority of orchid species are rare, with limited population sizes, specialized ecological requirements, and narrow geographic ranges. These features, including obligate relationships with specific mycorrhizal fungi and pollinators, render them particularly susceptible to environmental change.4,5 Globally, orchids represent the largest group of plants listed under the Convention on International Trade in Endangered Species of Wild Fauna and Flora (CITES), accounting for over 90% of all plant species covered by the treaty. This has earned them the status of a “flagship group” for global plant conservation efforts.6,7 In India, orchid richness is primarily concentrated in the Northeast Himalaya, Western Himalaya, and the Western Ghats. The Western Ghats, along with Sri Lanka, form one of the 36 recognized global biodiversity hotspots.8,9 The region is renowned for its complex geological history and varied ecosystems, which contribute to high levels of species endemism. The Western Ghats also serve critical ecological functions by modulating local climate, conserving water, and supporting ecosystem services.10,11 The current estimates of orchid diversity estimate 230 species, of which 123 are endemic in the Western Ghats.12

Orchids are under significant threat due to over extraction, habitat fragmentation caused by anthropogenic pressures, and shifting climatic conditions that disrupt their delicate ecological interactions. These threats are directly contributing to the loss of species distribution and a decline in population abundance.1,13 Additionally, climate change has emerged as a significant driver of species range shifts, altering phenology, habitat suitability, and species interactions. Due to their narrow climatic tolerance and dependency on biotic interactions, orchids are vulnerable to the effects of global environmental change.14,15,16,17 Despite growing recognition of the vulnerability of orchids to climate change, most existing assessments rely on single-species distribution models or aggregate metrics such as species richness. More broadly, biodiversity research has often focused on individual or a few focal species, as also highlighted in studies across taxa where single-species approaches dominate18,19,20,21,22 or are limited to a small set of species.23,24 Although such approaches provide important insights, they assume that species respond independently to environmental gradients and therefore fail to capture interspecific variability and community-level dynamics. This limitation is particularly critical for long-term conservation planning, as it overlooks potential shifts in species assemblages and co-occurrence patterns under changing environmental conditions.

Given that biodiversity loss is widely attributed to habitat destruction and fragmentation driven by expanding human activities,22 there is an increasing need to move beyond single-species frameworks and generate ecological insights at the multi-species level. In this context, orchid assemblages shaped by both shared environmental drivers and species-specific sensitivities require approaches that can better represent community responses. To address this gap, the present study adopts a multi-species distribution modeling framework, integrating the responses of 10 orchid species to evaluate patterns of habitat suitability under current and future climate scenarios. This approach provides a more comprehensive understanding of community-level responses and offers improved guidance for conservation prioritization in the face of climate change.25

Species distribution models (SDMs) have become essential tools for assessing potential habitat suitability and predicting range dynamics under future climate scenarios.26 The SDMs help to identify key environmental variables that influence species distributions and enable conservation planning at regional and global scales.27,28,29,30

The present study selected 10 epiphytic orchid species based on the availability of occurrence records, ensuring robust modeling outputs. Epiphytic orchids typically grow on various host plants, including trees, shrubs, evergreen, and deciduous species, without deriving nutrients from the host itself.31,32 Their dependence on specific microclimatic conditions makes them highly sensitive to environmental fluctuations. Consequently, epiphytic orchids are widely recognized as effective biological indicators of ecological change and forest health.16,33

Recent advances have led to the development of a suite of spatial indices designed to better capture the multi-dimensional impacts of climate change on ecological communities. Traditional species richness mapping, while informative, often overlooks critical aspects such as spatial turnover, persistence, and vulnerability under future climate trajectories. In this study, we integrated several cutting-edge indices, including the priority index (PI), ecological sensitivity zoning (ESZ), habitat stability index (HSI), and extinction debt (ED), to provide a more nuanced, climate-responsive assessment of orchid diversity in the Western Ghats. Together, these indices provide an innovative, multi-dimensional framework for climate-smart conservation planning, surpassing conventional approaches by explicitly incorporating spatial, temporal, and process-based perspectives into biodiversity prioritization.

We aimed to (1) map multi-species distribution of 10 epiphytic orchid species under current and future climate change scenario; (2) quantify the spatial extent of gain, loss, and stable areas of suitable habitat between current and future climate scenarios; and (3) identify long-term stable habitats and priority areas for conservation of epiphytic orchids in the Northern Western Ghats.

Results

Model performance

An ensemble modeling approach was employed to predict the current distribution of orchid diversity in the Western Ghats. Model selection criteria included receiver operating curve (ROC) values exceeding 0.7 (Figure S1). Among all algorithms, random forest (RF) exhibited the highest average ROC value of 0.81 and true skill statistic (TSS) value equal to 0.64. The ensemble model achieved an ROC weighted mean value of 0.80, indicating the best performance of all the models in predicting species distribution. The TSS weighted mean value of ensemble model exhibited a value of 0.60. Although we have provided both ROC and TSS values, our results are based on ROC value only.

The analysis indicates that precipitation of warmest quarter (bio18), temperature seasonality (bio4), and precipitation seasonality (bio15) are crucial variables for the distribution of orchid diversity (Figures S2–S11). Further, we found that bio18 and bio4 have a variable importance of 0.24, whereas bio15 has a variable importance of 0.22 (Figure S12).

Potential distribution and habitat mapping

The study area encompasses a total of 41724.56 km2 of suitable habitat for the selected orchid species of the northern Western Ghats. Within this area, 38846.93 km2 is classified as low suitable, 1824.66 km2 as medium suitable, and 1052.98 km2 as highly suitable for orchid diversity in the current scenario (Figure 1). For low suitability areas, there is a decrease observed in RCP26_2020_2040, 39747.70 km2, with a marginal increase projected by the end of the century, particularly under the RCP85_2050_2070 scenario (17054.26 km2) (Figure 1). Conversely, medium suitable habitats show a consistent increase in suitability over time, with minimal change between the lower and higher emissions scenarios (Table 1). For high suitability areas, a notable initial increase in suitability of about 4971.01 km2 is seen under RCP26_2020_2050, followed by a decrease of 2493.90 km2 by RCP8.5_2050_2070 (Table 1). Since our aim was to understand the multispecies distribution of orchids, single-species habitat suitability maps have been provided in the supplementary file (Figures S13–S22).

Additionally, the spatially explicit analysis revealed a consistent northward and upslope shift in suitable habitats across all climate scenarios (Figure 3). The elevational distributions differed significantly between current and future suitable habitats across all scenarios (Wilcoxon test, p < 0.001), with future habitats consistently occurring at higher elevations, confirming a statistically significant upslope shift. The displacement of habitat centroids indicated a clear latitudinal movement toward higher northern regions, while elevational analysis showed a significant upward shift ranging from 105.5 to 199.2 m (Figure 3 and Table 3). The magnitude of elevational shift was highest under the high-emission scenario (RCP8.5), with increases of approximately 196.2 and 199.2 m by 2050 and 2070, respectively, suggesting strong climate forcing. In contrast, the relatively lower shift under RCP2.6, particularly in 2070, indicates partial stabilization of suitable habitats under milder climate conditions. These results collectively demonstrate that species are tracking suitable climatic conditions along both latitudinal and altitudinal gradients, highlighting potential risks of habitat compression and reduced availability at higher elevations.

PI and eco-sensitive zonation mapping

The spatial distribution of the orchid conservation PI revealed pronounced heterogeneity across the Western Ghats, with several persistent high-priority areas emerging under all scenarios (Figure 4). Our results depicted that PI values were highest in the central and northern Western Ghats, corresponding to regions with both high projected orchid richness and low climate velocity, indicating robust climate refugia. In contrast, southern and low-elevation zones showed consistently lower PI values, suggesting either decreased richness or increased climate velocity. The comparative analysis of climate change scenarios revealed that the extent of high-priority areas (top 10%) declined markedly under RCP8.5 scenarios (high emissions), especially by 2070 (Table S1). The findings of the study revealed the potential contraction of climate-resilient orchid hotspots under more severe climate change scenarios.

HSI and ED

The HSI analysis revealed that only a small fraction of the Western Ghats landscape acted as persistent orchid diversity hotspots (HSI = 3) across all time periods (current, 2050, and 2070). These high-stability hotspots were mainly located in the central and northern Western Ghats. Much of the regions exhibited intermediate (HSI = 1–2) or low (HSI = 0) stability, with transient hotspot status or loss under future scenarios, especially under RCP8.5 (Table S4). Quantitatively, the area classified as stable refugia, unstable or non-hotspot regions were relatively similar with minor decline in intermediate class and increase in low as well as stable refugia (Figure 7). These results emphasize the rarity and conservation importance of temporally persistent biodiversity hotspots and identify key spatial targets for climate-resilient orchid conservation.

Discussion

Orchid species diversity distribution and habitat mapping

Situated within a global biodiversity hotspot, the NWG represent a climatically sensitive landscape and are known for the high biodiversity endemism.8 In such a mountain forest ecosystem, orchids are vulnerable because of their micro-climatic changes and yet are being ignored.34 Limited ecological knowledge of orchids hampers their management, often forcing conservation practitioners to rely on unverified information rather than robust scientific evidence. Therefore, the present study utilized an ensemble modeling framework incorporating 10 representative epiphytic orchid species to access the impact of climate change on orchid diversity across the NWH and represents the first regional-scale modeling effort of its kind.

Our analysis identified precipitation of the warmest quarter (Bio18), temperature seasonality (Bio4), and precipitation seasonality (Bio15) as the most influential predictors of orchid distribution across the NWG (Figure S3). These associations suggested the strong ecological relevance such as moisture availability during the warm season, directly governs the growth of epiphytic orchids.35,36,37 Similarly, the strong contribution of temperature seasonality revealed the sensitivity of epiphytic orchids to even slight fluctuations in air temperature and vapor pressure, which was found to influence the photosynthetic efficiency and water retention of these orchids.38,39 Following these, the influence of elevation suggested, integration of multiple environmental gradients such as temperature, humidity, and forest type that collectively govern host availability and microclimatic suitability.37

Being a well-known ecologically sensitive area, the Western Ghats reflects their ecological fragility and high conservation values.40 Hence, our model revealed that within NWG, suitable habitats for epiphytic orchids are predominantly concentrated at the southernmost region in moist deciduous and semi-evergreen forests, particularly in regions such as Amboli-Sawantwadi-Phonda, Koyna-Chandoli-Mahabaleshwar, and the Bhimashankar-Tamhini corridor. These zones correspond closely with the ESZ-1 category,40 characterized by minimal anthropogenic interference and high canopy, which sustains the humidity and host tree diversity essential for epiphytic orchids survival.41,42 The high suitability of these regions may also be driven by high rainfall,43 which maintains humidity in the region, thereby providing favorable conditions for epiphytes.37 Moreover, similar spatial clustering of epiphytic richness along low to mid-elevation humid zones has also been documented in the Himalayas,43 which may suggest that the southern NWG ecosystem of the Western Ghats acts as climatic refugia for orchids, offering microclimatic stability.

Our future projection analysis suggested that under low emission scenarios, suitable areas were expected to expand slightly by 2050 and 2070, particularly along the central tracts (Figure 1). This mild warming under low-emission scenarios may enhance the canopy humidity and active growth of the host plants, which likely favors the epiphytes in the region,31 whereas a pronounced contraction of suitable habitat was projected under the high-emission scenario toward the northern and central regions of the NWG, where increased temperature seasonality and declining monsoonal precipitation may intensify moisture stress and become unfavorable for the epiphytes and their host plants. This pattern is aligned with the projections for other Indian orchids, such as Habenaria suaveolens and H. longicorniculata, which showed southward or elevational shifts in suitable range under high-emission conditions.12,44

Furthermore, our empirical findings of the study suggested a habitat decline of epiphytic orchid diversity in the near future and indicated that the NWGs serve as a long-term refuge for climate-sensitive orchid species. With this study, we recommend landscape prioritization toward the low to mid-elevational zones of NWGs, which we predict as prime habitats for the orchid diversity. The observed northward and upslope shifts in suitable habitats are consistent with global patterns of species redistribution under climate warming (Figure 3). The magnitude of elevational shift (∼100–200 m) suggests a substantial reorganization of habitat within the Western Ghats, a region characterized by steep environmental gradients. Under high-emission scenarios, increased habitat fragmentation and spatial displacement may reduce connectivity and limit dispersal, potentially constraining species’ ability to track suitable climates. These findings highlight the importance of incorporating spatially explicit and multi-species approaches in conservation planning, particularly in montane biodiversity hotspots where elevational limits may impose constraints on future range expansion.

Climate-stable refugia and contraction of conservation priorities

Our PI analysis provides compelling evidence that climate change is likely to substantially reorganize orchid conservation priorities across Western Ghats (Figure 4). The concentration of high PI values in the central and northern Western Ghats across all scenarios highlights these regions as robust climate refugia, where high projected orchid richness coincides with relatively low climate velocity. These findings are consistent with earlier studies identifying mid-elevation and topographically complex regions as buffers against rapid climatic shifts due to microclimatic heterogeneity and steep environmental gradients.45,46,47 Such landscapes can slow the pace at which species must track changing climates, thereby enhancing long-term persistence.48,49 In contrast, the progressive decline of high-priority areas under RCP8.5, particularly by 2070, indicates a contraction of climate-resilient orchid hotspots under high-emission scenarios.

It is noteworthy that the contraction is especially concerning because it suggests that even historically diverse and climatically buffered regions may lose their conservation advantage under increased warming, a pattern reported for other biodiversity hotspots globally.50,51 The southern Western Ghats, despite harboring high contemporary orchid diversity, exhibited consistently lower PI values due to elevated climate velocity and projected richness losses. Our results infer a critical conservation paradox: areas rich in biodiversity today may not remain viable strongholds under future climates if climate change outpaces ecological adaptation or dispersal.48,49

ESZ: Interpreting statistical significance and ecological meaning

The ESZ mapping provided a complementary, process-oriented view of vulnerability by explicitly combining richness and climate velocity. The highly significant chi-square association between climate scenario and ESZ confirms that climate change scenarios influence the spatial distribution of refugia, at-risk, and marginal zones. However, the low effect size (Cramer’s V = 0.056) indicates that these changes are spatially diffuse rather than dominated by abrupt, landscape-wide transitions (Figures 5 and 6).

This apparent contradiction high statistical significance but weak effect size is ecologically informative rather than problematic. It reflects the large spatial extent of the Western Ghats, where even modest proportional changes in ESZ classes translate into substantial absolute area shifts with real conservation consequences.49,52 The standardized residuals provide clearer ecological insight, revealing a directional erosion of refugia from 2050 RCP2.6 to 2070 RCP8.5 and a concomitant expansion of at-risk and marginal zones. The pairwise proportion tests further demonstrate that refugia decline is not gradual but accelerates under high-emission pathways, underscoring the non-linear nature of biodiversity responses to climate forcing.45,47

Temporal persistence of hotspots: Insights from the Habitat Stability Index

The HSI adds a critical temporal dimension to hotspot analysis by distinguishing transient richness peaks from truly persistent biodiversity strongholds. Our results show that only a small fraction of the Western Ghats qualify as stable orchid hotspots (HSI = 3) across current, 2050, and 2070 projections (Figure 7). These areas are predominantly located in the central and northern Western Ghats, reinforcing their role as long-term climate refugia.46,47

The dominance of intermediate (HSI = 1–2) and unstable (HSI = 0) classes highlights the dynamic nature of orchid diversity under climate change. Many areas function as hotspots only temporarily, losing their status under future scenarios particularly under RCP8.5. These findings challenge conservation strategies that rely solely on present-day richness patterns and emphasize the need to prioritize areas with demonstrated temporal persistence rather than transient diversity peaks.50,51 The relative stability of total refugial area, despite shifts among stability classes, suggests that while some refugial capacity may persist, its spatial configuration and ecological integrity are likely to change.

ED reveals hidden future biodiversity loss

The ED mapping revealed pronounced spatial gradients in delayed biodiversity loss, with the southern Western Ghats emerging as a major hotspot of future orchid extirpations. High ED values in these regions indicate that current species richness masks substantial future losses already “committed” by climatic changes. This phenomenon of delayed extinction following environmental change has been widely documented, particularly in species-rich but climatically sensitive regions.53,54 This pattern is especially concerning for orchids, many of which exhibit narrow ecological niches, specialized pollination systems, and limited dispersal capacity.55,56 The increase in both magnitude and spatial extent of ED from 2050 to 2070—most pronounced under SSP8.5—highlights the temporal lag between environmental change and biodiversity response (Figure 8). Northern regions, by contrast, exhibited consistently low ED, suggesting greater resilience or adaptive capacity. These spatial contrasts reinforce the urgency of proactive conservation in high-ED landscapes, where delaying intervention could result in irreversible losses despite apparently intact current diversity.47

Integrating multiple indices for climate-resilient conservation planning for orchids

One of the key strengths of this study lies in the integration of multiple complementary indices PI, ESZ, HSI, and ED into a unified analytical framework. Each index captures a distinct dimension of climate risk: PI identifies spatial priorities based on richness and velocity49; ESZ categorizes functional vulnerability states; HSI quantifies temporal persistence of biodiversity hotspots; and ED exposes delayed losses not evident from static projections. Together, these indices move beyond single-metric assessments and provide a more holistic, process-based understanding of climate impacts on biodiversity.50,51

It is noteworthy to mention that areas identified as high priority by PI but also exhibiting high ED represent conservation trade-offs: regions where immediate action is essential, but long-term persistence is uncertain. Conversely, areas with high HSI and low ED emerge as ideal habitats for long-term conservation investments, such as protected area expansion, climate-adaptive management, and connectivity enhancement. This integrated framework therefore, offers a robust decision-support tool for climate-resilient conservation planning in biodiversity hotspots facing rapid environmental change.

The spatial and temporal analysis of orchid diversity hotspots across the Western Ghats reveals that the hotspots are highly clustered, and much of the area is not able to hold the persistent populations of orchids in the study landscape. The HSI maps demonstrate that only a small proportion of the landscape functions as long-term refugia, primarily concentrated in the central and northern Western Ghats, which are recognized as biodiversity hotspots. The marked decline in the extent of stable hotspots from RCP2.6 to RCP8.5 scenarios indicates that orchid-rich refugia are especially vulnerable to intensifying climate change, echoing findings from other biodiversity hotspot systems.47,51

The stable refugia with high HSI values represent critical, climate-resilient cores that should be prioritized for protection, long-term monitoring, and potentially for translocation or restoration interventions.45,46 Conversely, dynamic zones where hotspot status is transient or lost will require adaptive management, habitat connectivity, and landscape-scale restoration to buffer against projected turnover.50 Our results emphasize the value of integrating climate velocity, ED, and hotspot stability indices to support climate-smart prioritization, as advocated by recent frameworks in climate adaptation science.48,49 The rare overlap between stable diversity hotspots and low-velocity refugia under future scenarios highlights the urgency for proactive, spatially explicit action in these critical zones. These findings can directly inform systematic conservation planning and mainstreaming of climate resilience in orchid and broader biodiversity conservation across the Western Ghats and similar tropical mountain systems.

While each of the index independently captures a distinct dimension of climate risk, their integration reveals important trade-offs that are critical for conservation decision-making. Notably, areas identified as high priority based on the PI do not always coincide with areas of low ED. In several regions, particularly in parts of the southern Western Ghats, high PI values co-occur with elevated ED, indicating landscapes that are currently biodiversity-rich but are projected to experience substantial future losses. This pattern reflects the well-established concept of ED, where species persistence lags behind the environmental change.53,54 This spatial mismatch highlights a key conservation dilemma: whether to prioritize areas with high current conservation value (high PI) or those with greater long-term persistence (low ED and high HSI). From a management perspective, such areas represent “high risk, high reward” zones, where immediate intervention is essential to prevent irreversible biodiversity loss.47

Moreover, the conservation strategies in these regions may require a combination of approaches, including habitat restoration, connectivity enhancement, and assisted migration. Furthermore, the areas with high HSI and low ED represent stable refugia and may serve as long-term conservation anchors. Such refugia have been widely recognized as critical for biodiversity persistence under climate change.46,47 Therefore, prioritization should follow a tiered approach: (1) protect stable refugia (high HSI and low ED), (2) intervene in vulnerable but high-value areas (high PI and high ED), and (3) monitor transitional landscapes (ESZ marginal zones). Hence, this proposed multi-indicator perspective allows for more nuanced and adaptive conservation planning compared to single-metric approaches.

Moreover, the spatial patterns of refugia and at-risk zones identified in this study show partial alignment with existing conservation prioritization frameworks in India, including ESZs (ESZ-1) and protected area networks. The regions identified as refugia in the central and northern Western Ghats overlap with several established protected areas and biodiversity-rich landscapes, supporting their recognized conservation importance. However, our analysis also highlights emerging high-risk areas, particularly in the southern Western Ghats, that are not adequately captured within current conservation zoning frameworks.

Generality and transferability of the multi-dimensional framework

A key strength of the present study lies in the development of an integrative, four-dimensional assessment framework combining PI, ESZ, HSI, and ED. Although demonstrated here for orchid diversity in the Western Ghats, this framework can be extended to other taxonomic groups, spatial scales, and climate-sensitive ecosystems. The underlying structure of the framework is not species-specific but process-based, relying on general ecological principles such as species-environment relationships, spatial turnover, temporal persistence, and lagged extinction dynamics.

The framework can be readily adapted to other ecosystems by substituting species richness with alternative biodiversity metrics, such as functional diversity, phylogenetic diversity, species occupancy, or biomass estimates. For example, in marine systems, PI can be computed using coral reef diversity or fish biomass, while climate velocity can be derived from ocean temperature gradients. Similarly, in alpine or Arctic ecosystems, the framework can be applied to assess vulnerability of endemic species under rapid warming scenarios, where HSI can identify persistent refugia, and ED can reveal delayed biodiversity loss.

Furthermore, the ESZ classification provides a universally applicable decision-support tool by categorizing landscapes into refugia, at-risk, and marginal zones based on the interaction between biodiversity patterns and climate velocity. This classification can be integrated with land-use planning, protected area expansion, and climate adaptation strategies across diverse ecosystems. Importantly, the developed multi-index approach enables identification of conservation trade-offs, where areas of high current importance may also exhibit high future vulnerability, thereby guiding both immediate and long-term interventions. Moreover, our framework can also incorporate additional dimensions such as land-use change, anthropogenic pressure, connectivity, or socio-ecological constraints, making it suitable for large-scale conservation planning under global change. Thus, the proposed approach provides a generalizable and scalable tool for climate-smart biodiversity conservation, with strong potential for application across global biodiversity hotspots and vulnerable ecosystems.

Limitations of the study

The present study provides a broad climate-responsive assessment of orchid diversity in the Northern Western Ghats; however, certain limitations are acknowledged. The models primarily incorporated macroclimatic and broad-scale environmental variables, while fine-scale ecological factors such as host tree specificity, canopy humidity, and mycorrhizal associations could not be included due to limited spatial datasets. Only 10 representative epiphytic orchid species were analyzed, and therefore the results may not fully represent the complete orchid diversity of the region. Future projections are also dependent on climate model assumptions and emission scenarios, particularly regarding monsoon variability in tropical mountain ecosystems. Despite these limitations, the study provides one of the first integrative multi-species assessments of orchid diversity dynamics and offers a useful framework for climate-smart conservation planning in the Western Ghats.

Experimental model and study participant details

Study area

The Northern Western Ghats (NWG) is a ∼750 km stretch between the latitudes 15° 48′ - 21° 00′ N latitude (Figure 9). It is a chain of top mountains running parallel to the western coast of the Indian peninsula from south of the Tapti River, Gujarat, through Maharashtra and reaching Goa, representing an ecologically rich and high.56 Geologically, the entire NWG is composed of a massive Deccan basalt trap with a narrow coastal belt on the west and plateaus and plains on the east. The dominant vegetation is tropical semi-evergreen, dry evergreen and moist deciduous forests. The tropical climate of NWG includes hot summers (March-May; maximum temperature 32°C–33°C), monsoons (June-September: maximum precipitation 1073 mm), and mild winters (October-February; minimum temperature 14°C–16°C).

Study species

A total of ten epiphytic orchid species were selected for ecological niche modeling based on the availability of reliable presence records and their ecological importance across diverse forest types in India. The selected species are Acampe praemorsa (Roxb.) Blatt. & McCann, Aerides crispa Lindl., Aerides maculosa Lindl., Bulbophyllum sterile (Lam.) Suresh, Cottonia peduncularis (Lindl.) Rchb.f., Dendrobium barbatulum Lindl., Dendrobium ovatum (L.) Kraenzl., Eria microchilos (Dalzell) Lindl., Rhynchostylis retusa (L.) Blume, and Vanda testacea (Lindl.) Rchb.f. These species represent a range of elevations, forest types, and growth forms, and are distributed across peninsular India, particularly in the Western Ghats and other biodiversity-rich zones.

The Acampe praemorsa is a robust, monopodial epiphyte characterized by thick stems and leathery leaves, producing small, fragrant, red-striped flowers between March and June. It is typically found in dry deciduous and moist deciduous forests, with an elevational range from sea level up to approximately 950 m. Aerides crispa and Aerides maculosa are both endemic to India and belong to the monopodial growth type. These species inhabit moist deciduous to tropical evergreen forests, thriving at elevations between 600 and 1300 m. Bulbophyllum sterile is a pseudobulbous epiphyte with a more compact growth form, usually observed in semi-evergreen and moist deciduous forests, though specific habitat preferences require further study. Cottonia peduncularis, commonly referred to as the “bee orchid” due to its uniquely shaped, bee-like labellum, grows predominantly in lowland dry deciduous forests and is considered taxonomically and morphologically distinct within its genus. Dendrobium barbatulum is a medium-sized, pseudobulbous orchid with purplish-brown stems that become leafless during its flowering phase. It is found in semi-evergreen, moist deciduous, and dry deciduous forest habitats, generally between 600 and 1200 m elevation. Dendrobium ovatum, another Indian endemic, is widely distributed throughout the Western Ghats and occurs from as low as 50 m to elevations of 1300 m. It is commonly found growing on tree trunks in moist deciduous and semi-evergreen forests, and often flowers during the early monsoon period. Eria microchilos is a small-sized epiphyte bearing tiny (∼4 mm), compressed pseudobulbs that are typically sheathed and often colonize tree trunks and branches in moist deciduous or semi-evergreen forests at mid-elevations, particularly around 1100 m. Rhynchostylis retusa is known for its striking, pendulous inflorescences comprising densely packed, pink-spotted white flowers. It is epiphytic on tree trunks, favoring open areas and forest edges at elevations ranging from 300 to 800 m, and is widely appreciated for its horticultural and cultural value. Lastly, Vanda testacea is a showy epiphytic orchid found on the trunks and large branches of trees in semi-evergreen, moist deciduous, and dry deciduous forests. Its broad ecological amplitude and distinctive floral traits make it a valuable indicator species for habitat health in fragmented forest landscapes.

Method details

Data collection

Field surveys were conducted across the northern Western Ghats between 2012 and 2020 as part of an intensive orchid documentation project undertaken by the Botanical Survey of India. The surveys aimed to assess the distribution of epiphytic orchid species in the region. A handheld Global Positioning System (Garmin eTrexH) was used to accurately record the geographic coordinates of observed orchid occurrences in the field. In total, 1,639 occurrence records were compiled from both field observations and herbarium specimens (List is provided in supplementary file set). However, due to the limited number of records for certain species, those with fewer than 30 occurrence points were excluded from the analysis. Consequently, 828 records representing 10 orchid species were retained for modeling species distribution within the landscape. To reduce spatial autocorrelation and improve model reliability, rarefaction of presence locations was performed using the SDM Toolbox in ArcGIS 10.6. After this process, 529 spatially independent occurrence records were retained for further ecological niche modeling.

Predictor variable

A set of 23 variables were initially used which were extracted from satellite imagery using ArcGIS 10.6 software (ESRI, Redlands, CA). These variables were grouped into three classes, i.e., Topographic variables, Climatic Variables, and anthropogenic variables (Table S5). Within the topographic predictors, DEM (Digital Elevation Model), slope and aspect, has been prepared. Further preliminary set (n = 19) of bioclimatic predictors for current and future climate change scenario at 1 km resolution was downloaded from the WorldClim database (https://www.worldclim.org/). For climate change projections in different (2.6 and 8.5 RCP) future scenarios for the year 2050 and 2070 we have used the MIROC5 (Model for Interdisciplinary Research on Climate) General Circulation Model (GCM). MIROC5 was chosen due to its demonstrated ability to simulate monsoon dynamics, precipitation seasonality, and large-scale climate variability.57,58 Human footprint dataset as a surrogate of anthropogenic disturbance (https://sedac.ciesin.columbia.edu) was used for modeling. For reliable outputs, it is recommended to removing highly correlated variables for species distribution modeling. Hence, before performing any analysis, the variables were tested for collinearity and variables with values < 0.759 were used for further analysis (Figure S23). Therefore, a total of 11 variables were used to model the distribution of orchid diversity in the study landscape.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Multispecies distribution modeling

The present study implemented an ensemble modeling approach, using R package (‘biomod2’)60 for understanding the distribution of orchid diversity in current and future climate change scenario. We defined the modeling procedure, which was applied to each of the species of interest simultaneously using the biomod2_wrapper function. Data was formatted for modeling by organizing it into the required format to closely simulate actual distributions and reduce spatial bias. Specifically, 1000 pseudoabsence points were randomly selected, and this process was repeated three times during model construction.61 Model calibration was conducted using 70% of the data, while the remaining 30% was used for validation, respectively, with three repetitions.

The model performance was evaluated using the model evaluation score’s Receiver Operating Curve (ROC) and True Skill Statistic (TSS). Models with a ROC value exceeding 0.7 were retained, and the best-performing algorithms ANN, Random Forest, and MARS were utilized to construct the ensemble model. The variable importance scores were extracted from the models using in-built ‘biomod2’ functions. Variable importance scores were computed using ‘biomod2' functions, and mean scores across algorithms were considered for analysis. Evaluation of the final ensemble model was based on ROC (Receiver Operating Characteristic) values, categorized as poor (0.6-0.7), fair (0.7-0.8), good (0.8-0.9), or excellent (0.9-1.0).62

The continuous habitat suitability map was transformed into binary probability surfaces, where values ranged from 0 (representing ‘absence’) to 1 (representing ‘presence’), using a designated cutoff threshold. We used biomod2’s built-in binary transformation using binary.meth = “ROC”. The threshold (cutoff) was automatically derived by the internal function used by biomod2 (bm_FindOptimStat with metric.eval = “ROC”). Areas with probabilities above this threshold were classified as suitable habitat, while those below were classified as unsuitable.

To incorporate spatially explicit patterns of habitat redistribution, we quantified both the direction and elevational gradients of habitat shift between current and future scenarios. The centroid of suitable habitat (presence = 1) was calculated for each scenario, and the displacement vector between current and future centroids was used to estimate the magnitude (km) and direction (latitudinal and longitudinal components) of range shifts. Positive and negative changes in latitude and longitude were interpreted as north-south and east-west movements, respectively.

In addition, we assessed shifts in the elevational distribution of suitable habitats by extracting elevation values from a digital elevation model (DEM) and calculating the difference in mean elevation between current and future suitable areas. To capture ecologically meaningful changes, we further identified the largest contiguous suitable patch (core habitat) under each scenario and quantified its centroid displacement and elevational shift. This approach allowed us to evaluate whether suitable habitats are migrating upward and/or along specific geographic gradients under climate change.

Priority indexing of orchids and ecological sensitivity zoning for climate-resilient conservation planning

The Priority Index (PI) demarcates locations that combine high richness with climate stability, guiding conservation toward regions with the greatest likelihood of long-term persistence. To identify spatial priorities for orchid conservation under future climate scenarios, we developed a PI by integrating projected species richness and forward climate velocity. Species richness layers were generated using the SDMs stated above for the current and the future climate scenarios for the years 2050 and 2070 under RCP2.6 and RCP8.5. We calculated the forward climate velocity using a spatial gradient approach, where velocity was defined as the ratio of the absolute change in richness between future and current scenarios to the spatial rate of change in present-day richness. We then normalized all the velocity layers for different scenarios with values between 0 and 1, ensuring comparability across time frames and scenarios. Further, the PI was computed by multiplying the future orchid richness and the inverse of normalized velocity (PI = Richness × (1-Velocity)), highlighting regions where high biodiversity coincides with low climatic displacement pressure. Habitat stability indexing and extinction debt mapping

The Habitat Stability Index (HSI) offers a novel approach to temporal hotspot persistence, identifying areas that remain biodiversity strongholds across current and projected climates. We computed the HSI to quantify the temporal persistence of orchid diversity hotspots under climate change.
