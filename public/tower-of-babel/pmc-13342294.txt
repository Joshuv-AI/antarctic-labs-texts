Cryotolerance in the Antarctic lichen Umbilicaria antarctica is driven by supercooling and structural confinement
Authors: Aleksandra Andrzejowska, Sebastian Lalik, Karol Kubat, Kazimierz Strzałka, Angelica Casanova–Katny, Konrad Szajna, Maria Olech, Franciszek Krok, Monika Marzec, Hubert Harańczyk
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe Antarctic lichen Umbilicaria antarctica exhibits exceptional resilience to extreme cold, enabling survival in harsh polar environments. This study investigates the molecular and structural adaptations underlying its tolerance to subzero temperatures. Nuclear Magnetic Resonance analysis showed that at intermediate hydration level, water undergoes a transition from loosely bound to more strongly bound states between − 17 °C and − 24 °C indicating a non-cooperative water immobilization process rather than conventional freezing. Activation energy and average distances between relaxing proton pairs were determined using the Bloembergen–Purcell–Pound (BPP) model. Differential Scanning Calorimetry showed that freezing strongly depends on hydration, with all thermal peaks occurring below − 10 °C, confirming the presence of supercooled water. Heating protocols incorporating isothermal holding steps revealed an additional peak, consistent with diffusion of supercooled water toward pre-existing ice crystallites. Scanning Electron Microscopy detected sub-micrometer compartments within the thallus that physically confine water and solutes. This confinement–synergy effect stabilizes supercooled water, suppresses ice nucleation, and modulates molecular mobility, playing an important role in freezing avoidance. This integrative approach advances our understanding of cold tolerance mechanisms and highlights how coupled structural confinement and physicochemical interactions govern water behavior in extremophiles, offering broader insight into adaptation strategies in polar environments.Subject terms: Climate sciences, Ecology, Ecology

Introduction

Antarctica is mainly associated with low temperatures and lack of vegetation. It is, however, populated by the organisms which are some of the most resistant to the harsh environment -lichens1. Lichens are formed by a partnership relation between a fungus (mycobiont) and alga or cyanobacteria (photobiont). Such a partnership relation makes these organisms unique and tolerant to many abiotic stressors such as desiccation, UV radiation, vacuum, and low temperature2–4. Moreover, not only do the lichens survive extreme environmental conditions, but they also adapt to living in such, maintaining photosynthesis or other metabolic activity in lower than zero Celsius temperatures5–7.

What is essential for lichens to maintain their activity is the presence of liquid water in their thalli. This fundamental need inextricably links the two stressors of low temperature and of desiccation. Being poikilohydric organisms, lichens have developed coping strategies such as production of osmotically active compounds (polyols or sugars), antifreeze proteins, and non-cooperative immobilization of water molecules8–11. All these processes can lead to overcooling of water thereby enabling these organisms to survive in sub-zero temperatures.

It is also worth mentioning that the ice nucleation activity (INA) in lichen thallus can be found at temperatures higher than their photosynthesis low limit12,13, thus confirming the coexistence of liquid water and ice at sub-zero temperatures. It is, however, believed that ice crystals formed at relatively high temperatures are formed in extracellular spaces of mycobiont to avoid formation of ice crystallites inside photobiont cells14.

What is often overlooked when considering resistance to low temperatures in living organisms are the physical properties of water. It is, thus, important to emphasize that not only the structure of water, but also its local environment (compartment size, interactions between molecules and localization) has a key influence on the freezing temperature of water. Experiments with distilled water have shown that the freezing point depends on the diameter of the containing tube, a phenomenon known as confinement-induced freezing point depression15. A more precise relationship was described by Gibbs–Thomson equation stating that the higher the curvature of the boundary interface, the lower the melting temperatures16.

But what makes these findings more interesting in context of an actual organism is so-called Solute-Confinement synergy effect17. This phenomenon applies to small spaces (less than 1 μm) in which the presence of the cryoprotective substance lowers the freezing completion temperature Tfc (temperature at which 95% of the water is frozen) significantly more than the sum of the effects from the solvent (known as Blagden law) and the size and curvature of the space (Gibbs–Thomson effect) would indicate18. Thus, confirming the need to also consider the size of the compartment in which water is located as an additional factor in low-temperature resistance studies.

In the present study, we investigated the molecular mechanisms of water freezing in the Antarctic lichenized fungus, Umbilicaria antarctica. This study allowed us to observe the mechanisms taking place in these organisms at low temperatures, such as overcooling of water or increasing the fraction of tightly bound water at the expense of loosely bound water with decreasing temperature. To study water behavior and dynamics during cooling 1H-NMR (proton Nuclear Magnetic Resonance) was used. DSC (Differential Scanning Calorimetry) method was used to determine the temperature of phase transitions (melting and freezing) and observe the diffusion of overcooled water molecules. Using ESEM (Environmental Scanning Electron Microscopy) measurements we observed anatomical structure of lichen thallus.

Materials and methods

Samples collection and preparation

The thalli of Umbilicaria antarctica were collected on Robert Island, South Shetland Archipelago of Maritime Antarctica during 54. ECA (54 Expedición Científica Antárctica). They were dried out naturally in a cold place during expedition, then transported in dry state to the laboratory. Subsequently stored at − 20 °C in the darkness. Before the experiments samples were put in a fridge (4 °C) for 24 h and dried over silica gel (p/p0 = 0%) for at least 72 h in room temperature (21 °C). Exact hydration levels for the experiments were reached via gaseous phase hydration in air-controlled humidity over the surface of saturated solutions: CaCl2 (p/p0 = 32%), Kr2CrO3 (p/p0 = 88%), H2O (p/p0 = 100%) at room temperature with ambient pressure. To determine dry mass (m0) each sample was heated for 72 h at 70 °C.

Nuclear magnetic resonance

The proton nuclear magnetic resonance (1H-NMR) spectra were recorded on Bruker Avance III spectrometer (Bruker Biospin) operating at the resonance frequency of 300 MHz (at B0 = 7 T) with pulse length π/2 = 2.2 µs (repetition time 2 s, dead time 7.5 µs and transmitter power equal to 400 W. Measurements were taken within temperature range of + 22 °C to − 63 °C stabilized by a stream of gaseous nitrogen. Before the first impulse samples were incubated for 5 min at every temperature.

The data obtained was analyzed using OriginPro® 2023 software. The spectra were fitted using Levenberg–Marquardt algorithm with R2 ≥ 0.98.

Differential scanning calorimetry

The differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) method was used to observe phase transitions (corresponding to freezing and melting). Perkin Elmer DSC 8000 calorimeter (Waltham, MA, USA) calibrated from the melting points of water and indium has been employed. Measurements were performed during heating and cooling in the temperature range from + 22 °C down to − 70 °C with 20 °C/min rate. A relatively fast rate was chosen since the onset temperature was almost identical between 10 °C/min and 15 °C/min as the rate applied. Furthermore, 20 °C/min rate was also used for other lichens in similar measurements19. In addition to these measurements we performed an incubation experiment in which the samples were cooled from + 22 °C down to − 70 °C, then heated to − 30 °C, where they were incubated for 2 h, and then heated to + 22 °C. Temperature accuracy was better than 0.05 °C while enthalpy accuracy was better than 0.01 J/g. Lichen thalli were cut to fit perfectly in 30 µl aluminum pans and sealed with a press. Their masses varied between 6 and 12 mg depending on the hydration level. The results were analysed by using Pyris Software. The onset temperature was determined based on the Pyris Software as follows: the temperature range of the peak occurrence was selected, then the software matched the tangent to the ascending part (heating) or descending part (cooling) of the peak, which was extrapolated to the intersection with the baseline. The point of intersection of the extrapolated tangent with the baseline determines the onset temperature. In the same way, the onset temperature was determined for a low-temperature component of peak composed of multiple peaks (after the incubation process). The onset temperature for the main and high-temperature peak components was determined as follows: deconvolution was performed into three peaks in Origin 2018 software and the same procedure as in Pyris software was applied to the split peaks (the tangents was matched to the ascending part, extrapolated to the baseline, and the point of intersection with the baseline indicated the onset temperature). The deconvolution process was performed only for DSC curves registered after the incubation process.

Scanning electron microscopy

We used electron microscopy to observe anatomic structures of U. antarctica. The environmental scanning electron microscopy (ESEM) with a Quanta 3D FEG microscope (FEI, USA) and Everhart-Thornley detector was used. The images were obtained using 5 keV energy of an electron beam and 53 pA current. The images were analyzed using ImageJ software.

The distinction between loosely bound water (or free) and tightly bound water refers primarily to differences in molecular mobility and interactions with the surrounding matrix rather than to strictly separate phases. The loosely bound water fraction exhibits relatively high mobility and behaves similarly to bulk-like water, although it may still be confined within the biological structure. In contrast, tightly bound water consists of molecules strongly interacting with macromolecular surfaces (e.g., cell walls, proteins, or polysaccharides), resulting in restricted rotational and translational motion. Due to these constraints, bound water does not readily reorganize into a crystalline lattice and therefore typically does not exhibit a distinct freezing transition detectable by conventional experimental methods. Instead, its contribution gradually diminishes with decreasing temperature as molecular motion becomes increasingly hindered. This distinction is essential for interpreting the observed spectral components and their temperature-dependent evolution in Umbilicaria antarctica.

DSC

DSC measurements were conducted for seven different hydration levels of Umbilicaria antarctica thallus (Δm/m0 = 0.07, 0.15, 0.20, 0.40, 0.60, 0.80, 1.00) with 20 °C /min rate. One cycle consists of cooling from + 22 °C down to − 70 °C and then heating from − 70 °C back to + 22 °C. For each hydration level, two such cycles (cooling and heating) were measured to check the effect of temperature changes on the shape or positions of the peaks corresponding to the phase transition. Figure 4 presents DSC curves for hydration level Δm/m0 = 0.20 and higher, since no phase transition was observed for lower hydration level. The ice melting peak appears to be a convolution of at least three peaks for samples hydrated to the three highest hydration levels, which is manifested mainly by a low-temperature shoulder or broadening of the main peak. No significant differences were found between the first and second cooling. The only noteworthy change is that there is a convolution of three or two peaks for hydration levels Δm/m0 of 0.60, 0.80 and 1.00 at the first cooling, while the ice melting peak consists of three overlapping contributions for the 0.60 hydration level, and only a single one for 0.80 and 1.00 at the second cooling (see Fig. 4a, b).

In addition to the DSC measurements as a function of temperature at a constant rate, incubation studies were performed. After the first cycle, in the second cycle, the samples were cooled down to − 70 °C, heated up to − 30 °C, incubated at this temperature for 2 h, after which they were heated to + 22 °C. The incubation process affected the ice melting peak by introducing a third apparent peak near − 2.5 °C (Fig. 4d). The incubation period induced a small, low-temperature peak for Δm/m0 = 0.40, which was not visible during the first heating. The presence of this new peak suggests diffusion of supercooled water molecules to preexisting microcrystallites of ice, that were below the calorimeter sensitivity. However, no peak was detected for lower hydration levels. Another explanation for the appearance of this new peak may be the occurrence of cold crystallization in this type of system, which is also indicated by the higher enthalpy of melting during the second heating. However, the peak responsible for cold crystallization is not visible either during heating at the DSC curve (perhaps due to too high heating rate, 20 °C/min) or during the incubation process.

SEM

The three phase transitions for Umbilicaria antarctica thallus hydrated to relatively high level discovered using the DSC scans may be due to not only the active avoidance of freezing, but also due to the anatomical structure of the mycobiont. Therefore, high-resolution SEM images of a thallus cross-section as well as upper and lower side of U. antarctica in different magnifications (see Fig. 7a, b) were taken. The magnitudes of one hundred random compartments were measured on a randomly selected part of the image. The results were summarized in the histogram shown in Fig. 7c). It seems that the largest depositions of loosely bound water, dominant for the highest levels of hydration fraction, will be in free spaces between fungal shreds and photobiont cells. Individual fungal shreds can be observed as indicated by arrows (Fig. 7b). Presence of free spaces between them while not directly visually demonstrable can be easily deduced from the photograph. On the other hand, small-diameter compartments (especially those below 1 μm) that reduce water freezing point can be sought in the structure of the filaments, shown here, and/or cells of the algae. Presence of water in compartments of differing sizes could explain the multitude of peaks observed in DSC measurements.

Discussion

SEM

If water is confined to small spaces, such as micrometer-sized pores of the lichen thallus, the behavior of water molecules may be strongly influenced by the size and nature of the confinement. As the characteristic pore size decreases, the surface-to-volume ratio increases, enhancing interactions between water molecules and confining matrix. This increased surface interaction leads to modifications in the hydrogen-bonding network of water, affecting its ability to organize into the crystalline structure typical of ice. Consequently, the freezing point of confined water is generally lower than that of bulk water, a phenomenon known as confinement-induced freezing point depression21. However, the extent of this depression depends not only on geometrical factors such as pore size and shape (Gibbs–Thomson effect), but also on the presence and concentration of solutes, which are ubiquitous in biological systems22. Importantly, these contributions are not necessarily additive, as demonstrated in recent studies on confined aqueous systems containing both inorganic and organic solutes23,24. Ginot et al. demonstrated the so-called solute–confinement synergy effect in sub-micrometer pores25, showing that the combined influence of confinement and solutes can lead to a significantly greater freezing point depression than expected from their independent contributions. For example, in concentrated KCl solution ([KCl] = 0.37 M), the temperature at which 95% of water froze was reduced by more than 3 °C compared to the case where solute and confinement effects were considered separately.

In this context, our observations suggest that a similar synergistic mechanism may operate in the thallus of the Antarctic lichen Umbilicaria antarctica, where sub-micrometer compartments are present. Although the solute–confinement synergy effect has been primarily demonstrated in non-biological systems, it is likely to be highly relevant for lichen samples due to their complex morphology and the presence of cryoprotective compounds. A water-soluble fraction has been identified in U. antarctica using 1H-NMR, revealing osmotically active substances such as sucrose, galactose, and xylose26. Similar water-soluble fractions have also been reported in other Antarctic lichens, such as Cetraria aculeata and Evernia prunastri, albeit with different saturation levels27,28. These solutes can act not only as cryoprotectants but also as regulators of cellular hydration. Additionally, various polyols are synthesized by different symbiotic partners (e.g., mannitol by the fungal partner and ribitol by the algal partner)29. Taken together, the interplay between confinement and solute composition may play a crucial role in stabilizing supercooled water and suppressing ice formation, thereby contributing to the remarkable cold tolerance of lichens.

DSC

Calorimetric measurements revealed a linear dependence of the freezing point temperature of water on hydration level, consistent with a heterogeneous nucleation process. Such behavior is expected, as the nucleation temperature is known to depend not only on the presence of nucleation sites but also on sample volume, with larger volumes increasing the probability of nucleation events and thus shifting freezing to higher temperatures30. We therefore attribute this effect to an increase in water volume within occupied compartments of the lichen thallus.

The recorded peaks are highly asymmetric and consist of three components: the main peak and the low- and the high-temperature shoulders. The low-temperature shoulder is likely related to the gradual freezing or melting process of very tightly bound water in the most insulated lichen chambers31. The presence of small compartments where the melting point of water is much lower than it is in the rest of the thallus may explain such a phenomenon. The reason why it is not manifested as a single peak may be caused by the association between small compartments’ diameters and freezing/melting temperatures. The low-temperature shoulder might also indicate the presence of the Solute-Confinement synergy effect in U. antarctica thallus as a part of the mechanisms of freezing-avoidance strategy employed by lichens. As is known, during freezing, water molecules form an ordered network of ice crystal. Any admixture to water (e.g. sugar, salt, etc.) makes it difficult to organize water molecules into an ice structure, so in order to form a crystal the temperature must be strongly decreased. Thermodynamically, the chemical potential of water decreases after doping, which causes the ice-liquid equilibrium point (freezing point) to shift towards lower temperatures.

The high-temperature shoulder can be associated with a part of loosely bound water fraction present in the thallus. Loosely bound water, being the most mobile, is believed to have the highest freezing temperature32. This assumption corresponds to 1H-NMR measurements, where this pool was distinguished for middle hydration level (Δm/m0 = 0.30).

Finally, the main peak is an averaged signal from tightly bound water fraction and from loosely bound water fraction. It is at this peak that most of the water in the lichen thallus freezes, disturbing physiological and metabolic processes. For the highest hydration level, the peak occurs at a temperature of − 9 °C, at which a drastic decrease of photosynthetic parameters (Quantum Yield and Fv/Fm) was observed for U. antarctica during a linear cooling in chlorophyll fluorescence study33.

The curves recorded after the incubation process are different from those recorded for samples that have not been incubated. In particular, ice formation and melting are evident for a sample with a hydration level of Δm/m0 = 0.40 after incubation - the incubation process resulted in the formation of ice crystals for this sample, which did not occur when the sample was not incubated. Most biological systems show heterogeneous nucleation of ice crystals – at first the separated micro crystallites can be formed. With time these can grow into larger, detectable ice crystallites34. Analysis of the freezing of water in the thalli revealed that from the threshold hydration level of 0.40 freezing occurs, which is probably connected with the formation of already large ice crystals (combined micro crystallites). On the other hand, below the hydration level of 0.40, the formation of separated ice micro crystallites probably occurs, but no large crystals are formed. These micro crystallites of ice for low hydration samples are below the resolution of the calorimeter31.

1H-NMR

The half-width of both Gaussian and Lorentzian lines increased as the temperature decreased, which is related to the gradual immobilization of solid fraction and water molecules. Well distinguished total transfer of loosely bound water fraction into tightly bound water fraction was observed for intermediate hydration level (Δm/m0 = 0.30) at two lowest temperatures (− 20 °C and − 24 °C) while liquid signal was still present. The increase in total AL/AS at those temperatures confirms that water from loosely bound water fraction in these temperatures actually changes its properties rather than completely freeze. This suggests that additional active mechanism is taking place in U. antarctica thallus. While the Solute-Confinement synergy effect may explain the presence of low-temperature shoulder in calorimetry, it does not explain changes in water dynamics as the temperature decreases. We speculate that increasing amount of tightly bound water fraction in lower temperatures is caused by the increase in liquid-solid interface. The same phenomenon was observed in Turgidosculum complicatulum but only below the threshold hydration level which was Δm/m0 0.3835. This can be explained by a semi-solid state structure formed from substances present in lichen thallus, mainly cryoprotectants. In addition, a known defense mechanism against freezing for lichens is the partial removal of water from photobiont cells36,37. Such an active process can also significantly affect the dynamics of water in lichen thallus. Such a process - immobilization of water was also observed in other Antarctic lichen species - Cladonia mitis38.

The Bloembergen–Purcell–Pound (BPP) theory shows good agreement with the experimental data, suggesting its potential applicability to biological systems. Due to its simplicity, BPP is often considered inadequate for describing relaxation processes in systems more structurally and functionally complex than single proteins such as whole biological organisms. It is therefore typically applied in material science or protein-level studies39. However, in this study the BPP model provides a satisfactory description of the observed relaxation behavior.

The activation energy (Ea) for solid state is considerably low for dehydrated thallus. This could be explained by the presence of electron paramagnetic centers in the sample. A well-known paramagnetic center for lichen samples (within algal component) is manganese (Mn) - a part of photosystem II (PSII) in oxygen-evolving complex. This Manganese Cluster (Mn₄CaO₅ Cluster) can strongly influence the local magnetic field in its vicinity and therefore change relaxation processes within the photobiont40. Additionally, Iron-Sulfur Cluster in Photosystem I (PSI) and in special circumstances chlorophyll a or pheophytin, can similarly influence the local fields leading to decreased value of Ea in dehydrated sample41. Such an effect was not detected at higher hydration levels. This shows that increased presence of water has noticeable effect on the relaxation processes not only for liquid but also for solid state. For the intermediate and higher hydration levels activation energies are consistent with hydrogen bonds of differing strength.

The average distances between a pair of relaxing protons (ra) are relatively low, in every hydration level of the thallus. For both solid and liquid, the obtained values are lower than the Walrafen pentamer, which is the distance between oxygen atoms in neighboring molecules, which is approximately 2.8 Å42. However, the values are higher than distance between hydrogen atoms in one molecule which is approximately 1.5 Å43. That suggests that pairs of relaxing protons come also from two different water molecules not only from the two hydrogens in the same water molecule.
