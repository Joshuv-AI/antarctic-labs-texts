Cross-Sectional Asset Retrieval via Future-Aligned Soft Contrastive Learning
Hyeongmin Lee, Chanyeol Choi, Jihoon Kwon, Yoon Kim, Alejandro Lopez-Lira, Wonbin Ahn, Yongjae Lee

Abstract
. Asset retrieval—finding similar assets in a financial universe—is central to quantitative investment decision-making. Existing approaches define similarity through historical price patterns or sector classifications, but such backward-looking criteria provide no guarantee about future behavior. We argue that effective asset retrieval should be future-aligned : the retrieved assets should be those most likely to exhibit correlated future returns. To this end, we propose Future-Aligned Soft Contrastive Learning ( FASCL ) , a representation learning framework whose soft contrastive loss uses pairwise future return correlations as continuous supervision targets. We further introduce an evaluation protocol designed to directly assess whether retrieved assets share similar future trajectories. Experiments on 4,229 US equities demonstrate that FASCL consistently outperforms 13 baselines across all future-behavior metrics. The source code will be available soon.



1.
Introduction

Financial machine learning has long been dominated by the forecasting
paradigm—directly predicting future prices or returns. Yet the extremely
low signal-to-noise ratio
(
11
)
and non-stationarity of
financial markets fundamentally limit the generalization of point-prediction
models, motivating a shift toward
representation learning
: mapping
assets into embedding spaces that capture latent market structure rather than
predict exact future values.

A natural application of such representations is
asset retrieval
:
given a query asset, finding other assets from a universe whose market
behavior is most similar. This task underpins portfolio construction, where
identifying behaviorally similar assets enables diversification beyond
static sector labels
(
3
)
; pairs trading, where
co-moving pairs form the basis of statistical
arbitrage
(
8
)
; and risk management, where uncovering
hidden correlations reveals concentration risks invisible to sector-based
analysis
(
6
)
. Retrieval also offers inherent
explainability: rather than producing opaque point predictions, it presents
interpretable evidence—
“Asset A’s current pattern resembles assets
B and C, which subsequently rose 5%.”

But what does “similar” mean? Traditional methods rely on Pearson
correlation of historical returns, DTW-based pattern matching, or
sector/industry membership. These criteria are
backward-looking
:
they identify assets that
have
moved together, not those that

will
. We contend that, for asset retrieval to be practically useful,
the notion of similarity must be
future-aligned
—retrieved assets
should be those most likely to exhibit correlated future behavior.

Despite this intuitive appeal, existing
retrieval-oriented

methods neither
train for
nor
evaluate against
future
behavior similarity: self-supervised methods define positive pairs
through augmentation
(
2
;
29
;
25
)
,
temporal neighborhood
(
22
)
, or
ticker identity
(
13
)
—none grounded in future
outcomes—while evaluation relies on indirect downstream tasks such as
pairs-trading profitability
(
13
)
rather than directly
measuring future trajectory similarity. Although recent prediction
studies have incorporated future outcome signals (e.g., binary price
direction
(
5
)
), they target single-asset
forecasting rather than cross-sectional retrieval, and reduce future
behavior to coarse categorical labels. Yet the financial domain offers
a unique opportunity: future returns are continuous, directly observable
quantities that provide a natural and rich supervision signal for

ranking
assets by their degree of future co-movement.

We propose
Future-Aligned Soft Contrastive Learning (
FASCL
)
,
a representation learning framework that closes both gaps. At its core is
a
soft contrastive loss
in which pairwise future return
correlations—the Pearson correlation of daily return series over a future
horizon—serve as continuous supervision targets, transformed into a target
probability distribution via temperature-scaled softmax. The model minimizes
the KL divergence between this target distribution and the embedding
similarity distribution, so that pairs with highly correlated futures are
pulled together while uncorrelated pairs are pushed apart, all on a
continuous scale rather than through binary positive/negative assignments.

To evaluate whether retrieval is truly future-aligned, we introduce three
metrics that capture complementary aspects of future behavior consistency:
directional agreement (
Trend Consistency@
K
), trajectory-level
correlation (
Future Return Correlation@
K
), and cross-sectional
prediction quality derived from retrieval consensus (
Information
Coefficient@
K
)
(
10
)
. Together with Sector
Precision@
K
for structural awareness, these form a comprehensive
evaluation protocol for asset retrieval.

We conduct extensive experiments on 4,229 US equities spanning NASDAQ and
NYSE, comparing
FASCL
against 13 baselines from five categories:
statistical methods, time series self-supervised models, forecasting-based
feature extractors, a financial domain model, and time series foundation
models.
FASCL
achieves state-of-the-art performance across all
future-behavior metrics on both validation and held-out test sets, with
particularly strong gains on FRC@
K
and IC@
K
.

Our work makes three contributions toward establishing future-aligned asset
retrieval as a well-defined research problem:

(1)

Future-Aligned Soft Contrastive Loss.
We introduce a
contrastive objective that uses pairwise future return correlations as
continuous soft targets, directly aligning embedding similarity with
future behavior similarity—unlike existing methods that rely on
augmentation, temporal proximity, or ticker identity.

(2)

Future Behavior Evaluation Metrics.
We propose TC@
K
,
FRC@
K
, and IC@
K
—metrics that directly measure whether retrieved
assets exhibit similar future behaviors across multiple horizons, moving
beyond the indirect downstream evaluation prevalent in prior work.

(3)

Standardized Benchmark.
We design a
rigorous evaluation framework with temporal data splits that prevent
look-ahead bias, same-period retrieval constraints that reflect
realistic investment scenarios, and 13 baselines from five method
categories—all trained and evaluated under identical conditions—
providing the first systematic comparison for financial asset retrieval.

2.
Related Work

2.1.
Financial Asset Similarity and Retrieval

Traditional quantitative finance defines asset similarity through
backward-looking measures: Pearson correlation of historical
returns
(
6
)
, Dynamic Time Warping
(DTW)
(
21
)
for non-linear temporal alignment, or static
sector/industry classifications such as GICS. These criteria capture whether
assets have co-moved in the past, but offer no guarantee of future
behavioral similarity.

Learning-based approaches have begun to address this limitation.

SimStock

(
13
)
pioneered systematic research on
stock similarity, employing a hypernetwork attention mechanism with triplet
loss to learn temporally invariant representations from rolling OHLCV
features. While it demonstrated that learned embeddings can identify
structurally similar stocks, its reliance on ticker identity as supervision
and on indirect downstream evaluation (pairs-trading profitability, index
tracking error) leaves open the question of whether retrieved assets
actually share similar
future
behaviors. Our work differs by using
future return correlations as explicit training supervision, evaluating
directly on future behavior consistency metrics, and operating on a
substantially larger universe (4,229 vs.
{\sim}
500 tickers).

A parallel line of work models inter-stock relationships through graph
neural networks (GNNs). HIST
(
28
)
constructs predefined
and hidden concept graphs for trend forecasting, while temporal relational
methods
(
7
)
learn inter-stock dependencies for
prediction. However, these approaches are optimized for per-asset
forecasting rather than cross-sectional retrieval, and their reliance
on explicit graph construction scales quadratically with universe size,
making them less suited to efficient nearest-neighbor search over large
universes.
More recently, FinSeer
(
27
)
introduces a domain-specific
retriever for financial time series within a retrieval-augmented
generation (RAG) framework, but there retrieval serves as input
augmentation for language models rather than as the primary task.

Beyond retrieval, a growing body of work incorporates future outcome
signals into financial representation learning.
Du et al.
(
5
)
use contrastive learning for
explainable stock price movement prediction, defining positive pairs as
historical patterns that share the same
binary
next-day price
direction and hard negatives as patterns with opposite future movement.
ContraSim
(
24
)
applies weighted contrastive
learning to financial news headlines, clustering days with similar
subsequent market responses. Both demonstrate the value of
outcome-aligned supervision, yet they target single-asset

prediction
tasks—temporal pattern retrieval and market
forecasting, respectively—rather than
cross-sectional
asset
retrieval. Moreover, their supervision reduces future behavior to
coarse categorical labels (binary direction or market-level movement),
whereas
FASCL
operates on
continuous pairwise
future return
correlations derived solely from raw market data, preserving the graded,
ranking-based structure needed for retrieval at scale.

2.2.
Time Series Representation Learning

Given the limitations of domain-specific financial methods, general-purpose
time series representation learning offers an alternative source of
embeddings for asset retrieval. We survey three families of approaches,
all of which we include as baselines.

Contrastive methods.

TS2Vec
(
29
)
performs hierarchical contrastive learning
with contextual consistency at both instance and temporal levels, using
augmented views of the same series as positives.
CoST
(
25
)
extends this with disentangled seasonal-trend
representations through a momentum contrastive framework.
TNC
(
22
)
defines positives based on temporal
neighborhood—segments close in time. All three define positive pairs
through
temporal proximity
or
augmentation
, which captures
pattern self-similarity but carries no signal about future outcome
similarity. When repurposed for asset retrieval, their embeddings risk
grouping assets with visually similar patterns that subsequently diverge.

Forecasting-based models.

PatchTST
(
19
)
applies a patch-based Transformer with
masked prediction or supervised forecasting.
TimesNet
(
26
)
reshapes 1D series into 2D tensors via
FFT-detected periods to capture intra- and inter-period variations.
iTransformer
(
17
)
inverts the standard Transformer
by applying attention across variates rather than time steps. When these
models’ encoder representations are extracted for retrieval, they are
optimized for next-step prediction accuracy rather than inter-asset
behavioral similarity.

Foundation models.

MOMENT
(
9
)
is pre-trained on a large corpus of
public time series through masked reconstruction, while
Chronos
(
1
)
tokenizes values into discrete bins and
trains a T5-based language model for probabilistic forecasting. Both
provide zero-shot embeddings, but their general-purpose objectives do not
specifically encode financial similarity structure and may not distinguish
assets that merely look alike from those that will co-move.

2.3.
Contrastive Learning Objectives

The standard contrastive framework
(
20
;
2
;
12
)
optimizes an InfoNCE-style loss with binary pair assignments:
each anchor has one (or few) positives defined through data augmentation,
and all others are negatives. This binary treatment discards the nuanced,
continuous nature of similarity among financial assets.
Supervised Contrastive Learning (SupCon)
(
15
)
extends
this to discrete class labels, treating all same-class samples as
positives; yet applying SupCon to finance would require discretizing
returns into coarse categories, losing the rich continuous structure.

Recent work has begun bridging this gap.
Rank-N-Contrast
(
30
)
contrasts samples by their ranking in
the target space, preserving ordinal structure of continuous labels, but
operates on scalar-valued targets for individual samples rather than
pairwise relational targets.
SoftCLT
(
16
)
generalizes binary contrastive assignments
to soft labels for time series, where the degree of “positiveness”
is determined by
input-space
proximity: temporal distance and
instance-level similarity (e.g., DTW distance). While this preserves
the continuous nature of similarity, the supervision remains

observation-aligned
—grouping series that look alike in the past,
with no guarantee of similar
future
behavior.

FASCL
adopts the same soft contrastive philosophy but redirects
the supervision source to
pairwise future return
correlations
—a signal grounded in the outcome space rather than
the observation space. This naturally handles the graded nature of
financial similarity and directly optimizes the embedding space for
future-aligned retrieval. Our ablation study
(Section
5.5
) confirms that the choice of supervision
source—not the soft formulation itself—is the key driver of
retrieval performance.

3.
Proposed Method

We present
FASCL
, a framework for learning asset embeddings that
capture future behavioral similarity.
Given a universe of assets observed over a common time window,
FASCL

(i) encodes each asset’s raw market time series into a
fixed-dimensional embedding via a patch-based Transformer encoder, and
(ii) trains the encoder with a soft contrastive loss that directly
aligns embedding similarity with pairwise future return correlation.
At inference time, retrieval reduces to nearest-neighbor search in
embedding space.

3.1.
Problem Formulation

Consider a universe of
M
assets
\mathcal{U}=\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{M}\}

observed at a common time point
t
. For each asset
a_{i}
, we observe
a historical window
\mathbf{X}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times C}

spanning the preceding
T
trading days, where
C
denotes the number
of market features. Additionally, the subsequent
H
trading days
yield a daily return series

\mathbf{r}_{i}=(r_{i,1},\ldots,r_{i,H})
,
which serves as the supervision signal for future behavior.

Asset retrieval
is then defined as: given a query asset
a_{q}

with observation
\mathbf{X}_{q}
, retrieve
K
assets from the universe
whose future behaviors are most similar to that of
a_{q}
.
To operationalize this, we learn an encoder

f_{\theta}\colon\mathbb{R}^{T\times C}\to\mathbb{R}^{D}

such that the similarity ranking of embeddings aligns with
the ranking of future return correlations:

(1)

\rho(\mathbf{r}_{i},\mathbf{r}_{j})>\rho(\mathbf{r}_{i},\mathbf{r}_{k})\;\Longrightarrow\;\mathrm{sim}\!\bigl(f_{\theta}(\mathbf{X}_{i}),\,f_{\theta}(\mathbf{X}_{j})\bigr)>\mathrm{sim}\!\bigl(f_{\theta}(\mathbf{X}_{i}),\,f_{\theta}(\mathbf{X}_{k})\bigr),

where
\mathrm{sim}(\cdot,\cdot)
denotes cosine similarity and

\rho(\cdot,\cdot)
the Pearson correlation of future daily returns.

3.2.
Encoder Architecture

We employ a Transformer encoder
(
23
)
adapted for
multivariate financial time series, following the patch embedding
paradigm of ViT
(
4
)
and
PatchTST
(
19
)
.

Input normalization.

Each window
\mathbf{X}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times C}
is z-score
normalized per feature across the time dimension:

\tilde{x}_{t,c}=({x}_{t,c}-\mu_{c})/\sigma_{c}
,
where
\mu_{c}
and
\sigma_{c}
are the mean and standard deviation of
feature
c
within the window. This removes absolute price and volume
scales, forcing the encoder to learn from relative patterns.

Patch embedding.

The normalized window

\tilde{\mathbf{X}}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times C}

is split into
N=T/P
non-overlapping patches of size
P
along the
time axis. A shared linear projection—implemented as a 1D convolution
with kernel size and stride equal to
P
—maps each

P\times C
patch to a
D
-dimensional token, followed by layer
normalization:

(2)

\mathbf{e}_{n}=\mathrm{LN}\!\bigl(\mathrm{Conv1d}(\tilde{\mathbf{X}}_{\,(n-1)P+1\,:\,nP})\bigr),\quad n=1,\ldots,N.

Positional encoding and [CLS] token.

A learnable
[CLS]
token

\mathbf{e}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}

is prepended to the patch sequence, and learnable positional embeddings

\mathbf{P}\in\mathbb{R}^{(N+1)\times D}
are added:

(3)

\mathbf{H}^{(0)}=[\mathbf{e}_{0};\;\mathbf{e}_{1};\;\ldots;\;\mathbf{e}_{N}]+\mathbf{P}.

Transformer blocks.

The token sequence is processed by
L
pre-norm Transformer
blocks
(
23
)
, each consisting of multi-head
self-attention (MHSA) and a position-wise feed-forward network (FFN)
with GELU activation:

(4)

\displaystyle\mathbf{H}^{(\ell^{\prime})}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{H}^{(\ell)}+\mathrm{MHSA}\!\bigl(\mathrm{LN}(\mathbf{H}^{(\ell)})\bigr),

(5)

\displaystyle\mathbf{H}^{(\ell+1)}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{H}^{(\ell^{\prime})}+\mathrm{FFN}\!\bigl(\mathrm{LN}(\mathbf{H}^{(\ell^{\prime})})\bigr),

for
\ell=0,\ldots,L{-}1
, where LN denotes layer normalization
and FFN consists of two linear layers separated by GELU and dropout.

Pooling.

The final embedding is obtained by mean-pooling over the patch token
outputs (excluding
[CLS]
) after a final layer normalization:

(6)

\mathbf{z}_{i}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mathbf{h}_{n}^{(L)},

where
\mathbf{h}_{n}^{(L)}
denotes the
n
-th patch token of the
final Transformer block’s normalized output.

In our experiments, we set
T{=}64
,
C{=}6
,
P{=}4
(
N{=}16

patches),
D{=}384
,
L{=}8
blocks with 8 attention heads and FFN
expansion ratio 4, yielding approximately 14M parameters.

3.3.
Future-Aligned Soft Contrastive Loss

The core training objective aligns embedding similarity with future
return correlation through a soft contrastive loss that operates on
continuous supervision targets.

Same-period batch construction.

Each training batch is constructed by first sampling a random
date
t
from the training period, then sampling
B
assets that have
sufficient data at date
t
. This ensures all samples within a batch
share the same temporal context, making pairwise future return
correlations a meaningful cross-sectional similarity signal.

Target distribution.

Given a batch of
B
assets with future daily return series

\{\mathbf{r}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{r}_{B}\}

(each
\mathbf{r}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{H}
),
we compute the pairwise Pearson correlation matrix:

(7)

C_{ij}=\frac{(\mathbf{r}_{i}-\bar{\mathbf{r}}_{i})^{\top}(\mathbf{r}_{j}-\bar{\mathbf{r}}_{j})}{\|\mathbf{r}_{i}-\bar{\mathbf{r}}_{i}\|\;\|\mathbf{r}_{j}-\bar{\mathbf{r}}_{j}\|}.

For each anchor
i
, a target probability distribution is constructed
via temperature-scaled softmax over the off-diagonal entries:

(8)

p_{ij}=\frac{\exp(C_{ij}/\tau_{t})}{\sum_{k\neq i}\exp(C_{ik}/\tau_{t})},\quad j\neq i,

where
\tau_{t}
is the
target temperature
that controls the
sharpness of the distribution. A lower
\tau_{t}
concentrates
probability mass on the most correlated pairs; a higher
\tau_{t}

produces a more uniform distribution.

Predicted distribution.

The encoder embeddings

\{\mathbf{z}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{z}_{B}\}

induce a predicted distribution via cosine similarity:

(9)

q_{ij}=\frac{\exp\!\bigl(\mathrm{sim}(\mathbf{z}_{i},\mathbf{z}_{j})\,/\,\tau\bigr)}{\sum_{k\neq i}\exp\!\bigl(\mathrm{sim}(\mathbf{z}_{i},\mathbf{z}_{k})\,/\,\tau\bigr)},\quad j\neq i,

where
\tau
is the
embedding temperature
.

Loss.

The soft contrastive loss minimizes the KL divergence between the
target and predicted distributions, averaged over all anchors:

(10)

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SC}}=\frac{1}{B}\sum_{i=1}^{B}D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\mathbf{p}_{i}\,\|\,\mathbf{q}_{i})=\frac{1}{B}\sum_{i=1}^{B}\sum_{j\neq i}p_{ij}\log\frac{p_{ij}}{q_{ij}}.

Unlike standard InfoNCE
(
20
)
, which assigns binary
positive/negative labels,
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SC}}
preserves
the continuous, graded nature of financial similarity: assets that
are 80% correlated in future returns receive proportionally more
“pull” than those at 30%. The separate temperatures
\tau

and
\tau_{t}
decouple the sharpness of the model’s similarity
distribution from the target signal’s scale. We set
\tau=0.01
and
\tau_{t}=0.05
.

3.4.
Training

The model is trained to minimize the soft contrastive loss

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SC}}
(Eq.
10
) using
AdamW
(
18
)

(
\beta_{1}{=}0.9
,
\beta_{2}{=}0.999
, weight decay 0.05) with
a cosine learning rate schedule with linear warmup over 5 epochs,
peak learning rate
10^{-3}
, and minimum learning rate
10^{-6}
.
Training runs for 50 epochs of 300 steps each, with batch
size
B{=}4{,}096
and gradient clipping at norm 1.0.
Mixed-precision training (bfloat16) is used throughout.

3.5.
Retrieval

At inference time, all assets in the evaluation universe are encoded
in a single forward pass. Retrieval for a query asset
a_{q}
is
performed by computing cosine similarities between
\mathbf{z}_{q}

and all corpus embeddings, then returning the top-
K
most similar
assets subject to the constraints detailed in
Section
4
.

4.
Evaluation Protocol

4.1.
Dataset

We construct our dataset from daily market data of 4,229 US equities
listed on NASDAQ (
{\sim}
2,573 tickers) and NYSE (
{\sim}
1,656
tickers). For each ticker, we collect six features—open, high, low,
close, volume, and trading value—spanning from 2010 to 2024.

To prevent information leakage, we adopt strict temporal splits:

•

Train
(2010–2022): Same-period windows are sampled
on-the-fly during training.

•

Validation
(2023): Two non-overlapping 64-day windows
per ticker (first and last available), yielding
{\sim}
16,900
evaluation samples.

•

Test
(2024): Same structure as validation,

{\sim}
16,900 samples. All main results are reported on this
held-out set.

Because the soft contrastive loss requires
H{=}64
future trading days
as supervision, the last usable training window ends approximately three
months before the validation period begins, providing a natural temporal
gap between the training targets and the evaluation inputs.

4.2.
Retrieval Setup

We evaluate under
same-period retrieval
: each query retrieves
only from assets observed in the same time window. This reflects
realistic investment scenarios in which all assets share the same
macro-economic context at the time of comparison. We additionally
exclude the query’s own ticker from retrieval candidates to avoid
trivial self-matches.

Similarity is measured by cosine similarity of encoder embeddings.
Top-
K
retrieval (
K\in\{1,5,10,20\}
) selects the
K

highest-scoring candidates after applying the above constraints.

4.3.
Metrics

We evaluate retrieval quality through four complementary metrics.
The first three directly measure future behavior consistency of
retrieved assets; the fourth assesses structural awareness.

Trend Consistency @
K
(TC@
K
).

The fraction of top-
K
retrieved assets whose future cumulative
return has the same sign (direction) as the query’s:

(11)

\mathrm{TC@}K=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{Q}|}\sum_{q\in\mathcal{Q}}\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathds{1}\!\bigl[\mathrm{sign}(R_{q}^{(h)})=\mathrm{sign}(R_{k}^{(h)})\bigr],

where
R^{(h)}
denotes the cumulative return over horizon
h
.
We report TC@
K
for
h\in\{1,5,20,60\}
days.
A random baseline achieves
{\approx}50\%
.

Future Return Correlation @
K
(FRC@
K
).

The average Pearson correlation between the query’s and retrieved
assets’ future daily return series:

(12)

\mathrm{FRC@}K=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{Q}|}\sum_{q\in\mathcal{Q}}\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathrm{Pearson}(\mathbf{r}_{q},\;\mathbf{r}_{k}),

where
\mathbf{r}_{q},\mathbf{r}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{H}
are the

H
-day future daily return series.
FRC@
K
captures trajectory-level similarity beyond directional
agreement. A random baseline achieves
{\approx}0.15
rather than
exactly zero, reflecting the market-wide common factor (market beta)
that induces positive correlation among equities in general.

Baselines

We compare against 13 baselines spanning five categories, all
evaluated under the identical same-period retrieval protocol:

Statistical methods
(train-free).

assigns random embeddings as a lower bound.

uses flattened historical return vectors, so that

(

Time series self-supervised learning.

(

(

(

SoftCLT

(
16
)

Forecasting models.

)

Financial domain model.

Foundation models
(zero-shot).

5.6.
Downstream Application: Spread Trading

To evaluate whether future-aligned retrieval translates into practical
investment value, we conduct a stylized spread trading
backtest
(
8
)
designed to measure
signal quality

rather than realized profitability; transaction costs and market-impact
frictions are excluded so that Sharpe ratios reflect the purity of the
co-movement signal.
For each query asset
a_{q}
at time
t
, the top-
K
retrieved peers form
an equal-weight basket. The daily spread return is defined as:

(15)

s_{q,d}=r_{q,d}-\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}r_{k,d},\quad d=1,\ldots,H,

where
r_{q,d}
and
r_{k,d}
are the daily returns of the query and
the
k
-th peer, respectively. A mean-reversion signal trades against
the cumulative spread: the position at day
d{+}1
is

-\mathrm{sign}\!\bigl(\sum_{d^{\prime}=1}^{d}s_{q,d^{\prime}}\bigr)
,
and the daily P&L is the product of this position and the next-day
spread. Portfolio-level P&L is obtained by averaging daily P&L
across all queries, and we report the annualized Sharpe ratio
(
\text{mean}/\text{std}\times\sqrt{252}
) of the resulting
portfolio time series.

6.
Conclusion

We proposed
FASCL
, a representation learning framework that aligns
embedding similarity with pairwise future return correlations through a
soft contrastive loss, establishing
future-aligned asset retrieval

as a well-defined research problem. Experiments on 4,229 US equities
against 13 baselines demonstrate state-of-the-art performance on all
future-behavior metrics (FRC@
K
, TC@
K
, IC@
K
) across retrieval
depths and horizons. Ablation studies confirm the soft contrastive
objective as the essential driver, and a spread trading backtest shows
that
FASCL
’s retrieval quality scales favorably with basket size—a
property absent in all baselines. We additionally contribute a
standardized evaluation protocol and comprehensive baseline suite
for future research in financial asset retrieval.

Limitations and future work.

The temperature-scaled softmax in the target distribution concentrates
gradient signal on highly correlated pairs, which benefits precision-oriented
retrieval but means the embedding space does not explicitly distinguish
uncorrelated from anti-correlated assets—an extension relevant to
hedging applications.
Additionally, this work addresses
same-period
retrieval, where
queries and candidates share the same observation window. An important
extension is
cross-time retrieval
: matching a current asset’s
pattern against historical observations from different time periods,
effectively building a retrieval-augmented forecasting system.
