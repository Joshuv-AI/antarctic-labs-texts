Pontine infarction isolated to trigeminal sensory fibers: a case report
Authors: Lin Wang, Yanliang Niu, Lijun Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBrainstem infarction involving the trigeminal nerve is not uncommon in clinical practice. However, isolated infarction that selectively damages the sensory fibers of the trigeminal nerve while sparing motor function is extremely rare. Due to its atypical presentation—distinct from complete nerve infarction—this condition is prone to misdiagnosis, posing a significant clinical challenge.Case reportWe report a 59-year-old male with a history of hyperlipidemia, fatty liver disease, and hyperuricemia who presented with progressive swelling and numbness of the left gingiva and face for 20 days. He was initially misdiagnosed with trigeminal neuritis at an external dental clinic and treated with prednisone without symptom relief. Neurological examination revealed decreased pinprick and temperature sensation in the left trigeminal nerve distribution, with normal masseter muscle strength. Brain magnetic resonance imaging showed a diffusion-restricted lesion along the trigeminal sensory pathway in the left pontine tegmentum. A diagnosis of subacute pontine infarction selectively involving the trigeminal sensory fibers was made. Treatment consisted of dual antiplatelet therapy (aspirin 0.1 g at bedtime plus clopidogrel 75 mg once daily), rosuvastatin 20 mg at bedtime, gabapentin 0.3 g three times daily for numbness relief, and loratadine 10 mg once daily for angioneurotic edema. Three-week in-hospital follow-up showed marked improvement of facial swelling and partial relief of numbness.ConclusionIsolated infarction of the trigeminal sensory fibers resulting from occlusion of small penetrating pontine arteries is a rare stroke subtype. Early recognition and targeted neuroimaging are crucial for accurate diagnosis and timely intervention.

Introduction

The trigeminal nerve is the largest mixed cranial nerve and emerges from the pons. The first-order neurons of its sensory fibers are located in the trigeminal (Gasserian) ganglion. Its peripheral branches—the ophthalmic (V1), maxillary (V2), and mandibular (V3) nerves—supply the anterior scalp, facial skin, and the mucous membranes of the eye, nose, and oral cavity. The motor fibers innervate the masticatory muscles and the tensor tympani muscle, mediating chewing and mouth opening. Therefore, damage to the trigeminal nerve typically presents clinically with facial sensory abnormalities and masticatory muscle weakness or paralysis.

Although trigeminal nerve involvement secondary to cerebral infarction is relatively common, pontine infarction selectively affecting only the sensory fibers of the trigeminal nerve is extremely unusual. Cases of pontine infarction involving the entire trigeminal nucleus and its peripheral fibers have been reported, and those patients typically present with trigeminal neuralgia (1–5). To date, only two reported cases resemble the present one, in which facial sensory deficits without trigeminal neuralgia developed after pontine infarction injuring the trigeminal nerve (6, 7). Unlike our case, those two cases had sudden onset upon awakening, whereas our patient experienced progressive symptom development over one week, with facial and gingival swelling as the initial manifestation. Furthermore, multiple sclerosis (MS) is another important cause of isolated pontine lesions, particularly when the lesion involves the trigeminal sensory pathway, presenting with identical facial numbness and sensory disturbances. MS predominantly affects young to middle-aged women, typically shows multiple white-matter demyelinating plaques on FLAIR/T2 MRI, and often responds favorably to glucocorticoid therapy. Therefore, in the differential diagnosis of an isolated pontine trigeminal lesion, ischemic infarction must be rigorously distinguished from a first demyelinating event (clinically isolated syndrome) of MS. Because typical stroke symptoms such as limb weakness, dysarthria, or facial palsy are absent, the diagnosis is often delayed, compromising timely intervention.

This case report aims to highlight the unique diagnostic challenges posed by this rare stroke subtype and to elucidate the pathophysiological mechanisms underlying its atypical presentation—particularly the progressive evolution of symptoms, isolated sensory loss, and accompanying angioneurotic edema—so as to assist clinicians in recognizing and managing similar patients.

Case description

Medical history and previous treatment

The patient is a 59-year-old Asian male with a past medical history of hyperlipidemia, fatty liver disease, and hyperuricemia (untreated due to absence of symptoms). He underwent “thyroid cystectomy” approximately five years ago. He has no history of hypertension, diabetes mellitus, smoking, or alcohol use, and his family history is unremarkable.

The patient presented with “progressive swelling and numbness of the left gingiva and face for 20 days”. He initially experienced left facial and gingival swelling upon awakening without any identifiable trigger, along with a sensation of bruxism, but did not seek medical attention; the symptoms persisted without relief. Within one week, he gradually developed numbness involving the left half of the face (from the perioral region to the vertex) and nocturnal drooling. Because of pronounced gingival swelling, he visited an external dental clinic. Non-contrast CT of the oral floor, maxillofacial region, and salivary glands showed mild widening of the left submandibular and sublingual ducts, with no evidence of abscess, sialolith, or space-occupying lesion. The dentist made a preliminary diagnosis of trigeminal neuritis or facial neuritis and prescribed mecobalamin and oral prednisone (dose and duration unknown). The patient reported no improvement; instead, he felt that the numbness had slightly worsened. (Notably, the patient's neurological symptoms evolved gradually over several days, rather than reaching a peak within minutes to hours as seen in typical ischemic stroke—this atypical pattern initially steered clinicians toward other diagnoses).

The patient subsequently visited the dental department of our hospital. Oral examination revealed swelling of the left gingiva and buccal mucosa, but no mucosal lesions, ulcers, fistulas, local warmth, tenderness, purulent discharge, or carious pathology. No masses or space-occupying lesions were identified. The dentist therefore referred the patient to the neurology department for further investigation of a neurogenic or neurovascular etiology.

Inpatient work-up

Neurological examination

Neurological examination: The patient was alert, fully oriented, and fluent. Vital signs were stable, with blood pressure 115/85 mmHg. Cranial nerve examination: Selective sensory loss in the left trigeminal nerve distribution—markedly decreased pinprick and temperature sensation over the left cheek, upper lip, lower lip, and gingiva, with partially preserved touch sensation. Masticatory muscle strength was normal, no deviation of the jaw on opening, and the jaw reflex was normal. No facial weakness (symmetrical forehead wrinkles, complete eyelid closure). The remaining cranial nerves were intact (no diplopia, hearing loss, dysphagia, or dysarthria).

Motor system: Normal muscle bulk and tone in all four limbs, strength 5/5, no ataxia or incoordination. No pyramidal or extrapyramidal signs. No hemisensory loss outside the trigeminal distribution. Deep tendon reflexes were symmetric and normal. Bilateral plantar responses were flexor. Gait was steady.

Complete blood count, coagulation profile, liver and kidney function tests, autoantibodies (ANA, ANCA), and serologies for infectious diseases (HIV, syphilis, EBV, CMV) were all normal (Table 1).

MR angiography

No significant stenosis or occlusion of the major intracranial vessels; only mild atherosclerotic changes were noted, supporting a small penetrating artery occlusion as the etiology (Figure 1).

Diagnosis and treatment

Based on the acute-to-subacute onset, symptoms strictly confined to the trigeminal sensory fibers with no motor or long-tract signs, presence of vascular risk factors, and MRI confirmation of a focal subacute infarct in the left pontine tegmentum, a diagnosis of subacute pontine infarction selectively involving the trigeminal sensory fibers was made, most likely due to occlusion of a small penetrating artery supplying the trigeminal sensory pathway. Follow-up and outcome

During hospitalization

On hospital day 2, the patient reported decreased numbness in the lower lip. Facial swelling gradually resolved during the hospital stay. No adverse events occurred.

3-week follow-up

At the outpatient follow-up visit three weeks after discharge, the patient's facial swelling had almost completely resolved (Figure 3), and gingival swelling was no longer prominent. Only mild perioral numbness persisted, which did not affect eating or daily activities. Given the marked improvement in the patient's clinical symptoms, the absence of neurological deficits or other new complaints, and the patient's own refusal to undergo further imaging, no follow-up MRI of the brain or maxillofacial region was arranged after discharge.

Discussion

This case report describes a rare instance of isolated pontine infarction selectively involving the trigeminal sensory fibers, presenting as progressive facial swelling followed by numbness over one week. The clinical presentation was initially misleading, leading the patient to first consult a dentist and receive preliminary diagnoses of trigeminal neuritis and orofacial infection.

Unusual symptom progression

Why did symptoms progress over days rather than peaking within minutes? The symptom evolution in this patient—progressive worsening over one week—is atypical for classic ischemic stroke. Several factors may explain this unusual course:

Vascular territory and mechanism

The affected vessel is a tiny penetrating artery supplying the trigeminal sensory root and the root entry zone. These vessels have extremely poor or absent collateral circulation (14). Ischemia may manifest as progressive hypoperfusion rather than instantaneous complete occlusion, leading to cumulative daily deterioration of neural function rather than sudden loss (15). This “progressive stroke” pattern, although less common in large-artery atherosclerosis, is well documented in small penetrating artery disease of the deep regions or brainstem.

Partial vs. complete nerve injury

The lesion likely did not cause complete necrosis of all trigeminal sensory fibers. Instead, some fibers sustained ischemic functional impairment while others remained structurally intact. Damaged fibers may develop conduction abnormalities and spontaneous ectopic discharges (16), and as ischemic injury accumulates and the inflammatory cascade evolves, symptoms may gradually worsen over the initial days (17). Thus, the patient did not present with complete sensory loss, but rather progressive numbness and swelling—a manifestation of partial, evolving nerve injury rather than immediate complete infarction.

Atypical phenotype masking the classic stroke tempo

Classic stroke presents with sudden motor or hemisensory deficits—“alarm symptoms” that prompt immediate medical attention. The phenotype in this case—pure sensory abnormalities confined to a single cranial nerve, accompanied by swelling—was not immediately recognized as a stroke, thereby delaying diagnosis and allowing more time for symptom progression before treatment initiation. The relatively slow progression may also be a combined result of true infarct evolution and delayed presentation.

Reassuringly, a subacute course does not exclude stroke

Although hyperacute (<4.5 h) thrombolysis was not applicable, subacutely progressive small-vessel stroke is well documented (18, 19). The eventual stabilization and improvement of symptoms after antiplatelet and statin therapy confirm the underlying ischemic etiology.

Clinical takeaway: In patients with progressive isolated cranial neuropathy, especially those with vascular risk factors, a low threshold for MRI should be maintained even in the absence of typical stroke symptoms.

Pathophysiology of facial swelling

The patient's swelling was objectively present on examination, yet masticatory muscle function was intact, ruling out motor fiber involvement. Several mechanisms may explain this unusual manifestation:

Neurogenic inflammation from neuropeptide release

Trigeminal sensory fibers (especially the V2 and V3 branches) contain peptidergic nerve fibers, including calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP) and substance P (20, 21). After infarction, damaged C-fibers release these pre-stored neuropeptides, which act on local orofacial vessels to cause vasodilation and plasma protein extravasation—i.e., neurogenic vasodilatory edema (22). Moreover, surviving sensory fibers may undergo sustained depolarization, leading to continued neuropeptide release and prolonged edema.

Autonomic dysregulation

The trigeminal system has reflex connections with the superior salivatory nucleus (parasympathetic) and the superior cervical ganglion (sympathetic) (23–26). Interruption of trigeminal sensory input may lead to relative parasympathetic predominance and relative sympathetic inhibition, promoting vasodilation, hypersalivation (which could explain nocturnal drooling), and impaired venous return, collectively contributing to edema formation.

Sterile neuroinflammation

Ischemic neurons release damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), activating perivascular macrophages and mast cells, which release pro-inflammatory cytokines that disrupt endothelial tight junctions, further exacerbating edema (27, 28).

Most of the above mechanisms are theoretical, derived from experimental models of trigeminal activation (e.g., migraine models) rather than direct evidence from human post-stroke edema. Nevertheless, they provide a plausible explanatory framework for this unusual presentation.

Clinical implications and teaching points

This case offers several pedagogical lessons. First, it reminds clinicians that acute ischemic stroke can present as a progressive isolated cranial neuropathy, thereby mimicking inflammatory or infectious disorders. Second, it demonstrates that targeted neuroimaging—particularly MRI—is indispensable for establishing the correct diagnosis in such atypical presentations. Third, it shows that even in subacutely progressive stroke, appropriate secondary prevention and symptomatic treatment can lead to meaningful clinical improvement. Fourth, it highlights the importance of multidisciplinary collaboration: although the initial dental evaluation did not identify a dental pathology, the appropriate referral to neurology ultimately led to the correct diagnosis.

Limitations

This report has several limitations. First, no neurophysiological studies (e.g., trigeminal evoked potentials, blink reflex) were performed, which could have provided objective quantitative measures of sensory fiber dysfunction. Second, serum or salivary neuropeptide (CGRP, substance P) levels were not measured; therefore, the proposed neurogenic inflammation mechanism remains indirect inference. Third, the follow-up period was only 3 weeks, and the long-term outcomes of residual numbness and functional recovery are unknown. Fourth, the adjuvant traditional Chinese medicine preparations (Xuesaitong, Ginkgo biloba extract) have limited evidence in acute ischemic stroke; their use is documented only descriptively and does not constitute a treatment recommendation. Fifth,the patient presented with left gingival edema on admission,to rapidly relieve clinical discomfort, an empiric antihistamine was initially administered for symptomatic decongestion. Subsequent literature review and mechanistic studies indicate that targeted therapy against the bradykinin pathway—such as plasma kallikrein inhibitors or bradykinin B2 receptor antagonists—is preferred for such edema (29). This is noted here as a supplementary clarification. Finally, as a single case report, the conclusions cannot be generalized; however, the detailed description of this rare presentation may inform future case series or observational studies.

Conclusion and clinical takeaways

Pontine infarction selectively involving the trigeminal sensory fibers is a rare stroke subtype that can present with subacutely progressive facial swelling and numbness—without pain or motor deficits—and may evolve over days rather than minutes, making it easily misdiagnosed. Patient perspective (expressed in the patient's own first-person voice)

“When my face first started swelling, I thought it was a dental problem. I went to the dentist, but the medicine they gave me didn’t work, and the numbness gradually spread. I was very worried. After I was referred to the neurology department and had an MRI, I finally learned that I had had a small stroke. The doctor explained why my face became swollen and numb. After treatment, the swelling went away quickly. The numbness in my lip is still a little bit present, but it's much better. I am very grateful to have received the correct diagnosis and treatment. I agree to share my story because it might help other patients with similar symptoms get the right diagnosis earlier”.
