Analysis of tumor protein p53 expression levels in different stages of breast cancer
Authors: Daniyal Arab, Haniyeh Bashi Zadeh Fakhar, Fatemeh Rouhollah, Mohammad Esmaiel Akbari, Melika Jalalian, Parnian Sadat Shahidi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBreast cancer (BC) is a major contributor to cancer-related mortality, emphasizing the need to identify molecular biomarkers for effective disease management, accurate prognosis prediction, and preventive interventions. The objective of this research was to examine the association between tumor protein p53 (Tp53) expression and the stage of the disease. The study involved the participation of 70 individuals diagnosed with BC. RNA samples were extracted, and the expression levels of Tp53were evaluated using real-time RT-PCR. The collected data were subsequently analyzed utilizing SPSS software. The findings revealed an up-regulation of p53 expression in the subjects with BC. The study found that the expression of the Tp53 gene varies across different stages of BC. Stage 4 showed the highest expression, while stage 1 had no detectable expression. Grades 2 and 3 had higher expression levels, suggesting a correlation between tumor aggressiveness and increased Tp53 gene expression (P < 0.001). Patients aged 40 or above had higher expression levels compared to those below 40 (P < 0.001). Individuals who had a familial history of BC displayed notably elevated levels of Tp53 gene expression (P < 0.001). These findings demonstrate the dynamic nature of Tp53 gene expression in relation to disease stage, patient age, familial history, disease duration, grade, and childbirth number. This indicates the potential of the Tp53 gene as a biomarker for early-stage BC, as its minimal expression or absence may indicate early-stage disease.Keywords: Breast cancer, Tp53, Gene expression, MetastasisSubject terms: Cancer, Molecular biology

Introduction

Breast cancer (BC) is the leading malignancy affecting women globally, with approximately 1.7 million new cases each year. It constitutes around 25% of all cancers in women and represents 12% of all newly diagnosed cancer cases1. BC is the most commonly reported cancer in Iran. Based on the latest Iranian national database, the age-standardized rate of BC is 33.21 per 100,000 individuals. The overall 5-year survival rate for women is 72%, while for men it is 60%. The mortality rate for BC is 14.2 per 100,000 individuals2. The 5-year and 10-year survival rates are reported to be 81% and 77%, respectively3.

Numerous factors have been associated with an elevated risk of developing BC in women. These factors include genetic predisposition, family history, age, and environmental factors. Certain high-risk genetic variants are linked to a significantly increased lifetime risk of developing BC. These include mutations in genes such as BRCA1, BRCA2, TP53, PTEN, STK11, NF1, and CDH-14. Thorough and accurate genetic testing for mutations in high-risk genes such as BRCA1, BRCA2, PTEN, or TP53 can be highly beneficial for women in the high-risk group5.

TP53 is a tumor suppressor gene frequently subject to mutations in various cancer types, including BC5. Mutations in the TP53 gene can lead to dysregulation in the expression of multiple genes that are directly or indirectly controlled by Tp53 at the transcriptional level. Consequently, this can result in impaired DNA damage repair pathways, disrupted cell-cycle arrest, altered chromatin remodeling, and decreased apoptosis6. Multiple studies exploring the dysregulation of Tp53 in BC have produced conflicting results. However, the majority of studies have supported the overexpression of mutant Tp53 in BC cases, while some have reported reduced expression of p53 in the context of BC7. Several studies have reported of a significant positive correlation between Tp53 expression and high proliferation index (MIB1) in BC. Furthermore, Tp53 expression and steroid receptor status have a negative correlation in BC. Additionally, Tp53 expression is significantly associated with younger age, larger tumor size, ductal morphology, and high tumor grade in BC cases8. In their study, Vogiatzi et al. demonstrated that gain-of-function mutant Tp53 has the ability to enhance the folding of N-glycoproteins through the involvement of ENTPD5 within the calnexin/calreticulin cycle of the endoplasmic reticulum. The presence of mutant Tp53 variants can result in elevated expression of pro-metastatic cell surface proteins9. The expression of Tp53, which refers to the levels and activity of the Tp53 protein in cancer cells, can vary depending on the stage of cancer10. In early-stage cancer, Tp53 expression may be normal or elevated, functioning as a tumor suppressor. However, as cancer advances, Tp53 can become mutated or inactivated, allowing cancer cells to grow and spread11. In certain types of cancer, it has been observed that mutations in the Tp53 gene can actually increase its expression. However, these mutations often result in the loss of Tp53’s ability to induce cell death, which is a critical mechanism for controlling cell growth. As a consequence, the impaired Tp53 function promotes uncontrolled cell growth and contributes to the development of tumors12. Assessing Tp53 expression and mutations can provide diagnostic and prognostic information. However, the relationship between Tp53 expression and cancer stages can vary depending on the specific cancer type and patient characteristics13. Therefore, Tp53 status should be evaluated alongside other factors when determining cancer progression and treatment strategies14.

Studies conducted in various countries have consistently demonstrated that the expression of the Tp53 gene in BC serves as a significant factor in disease prognosis15. The study aimed to assess the expression level of the Tp53 gene in different stages of BC based on I–IV staging system to improve our understanding of its role in cancer progression.

Materials and methods

Samples and patients

BC tissue samples were obtained from Shohadaye Tajrish Hospital in Tehran, Iran. This is cross-sectional research which comprised a sample of 70 patients who were diagnosed with BC during the period from 2020 to 2023, and their cancer stage was confirmed by the surgeon and attending physician. During the surgical procedure, a one square centimeter sample of tissue was extracted from the BC mass. Additionally, a 5-cc sample of EDTA was collected from each patient. The study protocol received approval from the Ethics Committee of Islamic Azad University, and all participating patients provided informed consent, adhering to the principles stated in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Inclusion criteria

Voluntary participation with signed informed consent, age between 21 and 57 years old, clinical stage I, II, or III breast cancer as confirmed by the hospital’s pathology department, and not currently undergoing neoadjuvant treatment. Thorough examination of medical records was conducted to collect clinicopathological and gynecological characteristics of the patients.

Exclusion criteria

A history of previous cancer, cancer recurrence, and pregnancy. Additionally, patients with insufficient biopsy material and incomplete clinical history were excluded from the analysis.

Histopathological analysis of breast cancer tissue

Upon collection, the fresh tissues obtained from the patients were divided into two parts to facilitate comprehensive analysis. The first part was promptly frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C for subsequent molecular studies. The second part was fixed in 10% neutral-buffered formalin and submitted to the pathology laboratory at Shohadaye Tajrish Hospital for histopathological evaluation. Tissues were embedded in paraffin, sectioned, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) for microscopic examination.

Pathological staging was performed according to the criteria outlined in the eighth edition of the AJCC (American Joint Committee on Cancer) Cancer Staging Manual, based on tumor size, lymph node involvement, and presence of distant metastasis. Staging in this study was pathological (pTNM), as determined by histopathological assessment of resected specimens. Tumor grading was conducted using the Nottingham grading system (modified Bloom-Richardson scheme), which evaluates tubule formation, nuclear pleomorphism, and mitotic count.

Importantly, all histopathological evaluations—including staging and grading—were performed blinded to the molecular findings to prevent potential bias. The pathologists were unaware of the molecular or genetic data associated with each sample, ensuring an objective and independent pathological assessment.

RNA extraction

To isolate total RNA from the tumor tissues, an RNA Extraction Kit (Qiagen, Germany) was utilized in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. The concentration and purity of the extracted RNA were assessed using a Qubit 3.0 Fluorometer (Thermo Scientific, USA) and a NanoDrop spectrophotometer, respectively, with all samples exhibiting A260/A280 ratios between 1.9 and 2.1, indicating high purity. RNA integrity was evaluated using the Agilent Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, USA), and the RNA Integrity Number (RIN) values were recorded for all samples. The typical RIN values ranged from 7.0 to 8.5, indicating good RNA quality suitable for downstream molecular analyses. Additionally, RNA yields averaged between 3.5 and 6.2 µg per 30 mg of tissue, depending on tumor cellularity and sample preservation conditions. To further confirm RNA integrity, a subset of samples was also analyzed by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis, which revealed intact 18 S and 28 S ribosomal RNA bands with a clear 2:1 intensity ratio. The isolated RNAs were then stored at −80 °C to prevent degradation until use in subsequent experiments.

cDNA synthesis

To eliminate genomic DNA contamination, DNase I treatment was performed prior to reverse transcription. A DNase reagent (Sinnaclon, Iran) was used according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, 1 µg of total RNA was incubated with 1 µL of DNase I in a 10 µL reaction volume containing 1× DNase buffer at 37 °C for 30 min. The enzymatic reaction was subsequently terminated by adding 1 µL of DNase inactivation reagent and incubating at 65 °C for 10 min. The integrity and purity of RNA were assessed both before and after DNase treatment using a Qubit 3.0 Fluorometer (Thermo Scientific, USA) and agarose gel electrophoresis to confirm RNA quality and the absence of genomic DNA contamination.

For cDNA synthesis, the TOPscript™ cDNA Synthesis Kit (Enzynomics, Republic of Korea) was used according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Reverse transcription was performed using 1 µg of DNase-treated RNA in a total reaction volume of 20 µL, including random hexamer primers and dNTPs. The thermal cycling conditions were as follows: 25 °C for 10 min (primer annealing), 50 °C for 30 min (reverse transcription), and 85 °C for 5 min (enzyme inactivation). As a negative control to confirm the absence of residual genomic DNA, “no reverse transcriptase” (–RT) controls were included for each sample, in which RNase-free water was substituted for the reverse transcriptase enzyme. The resulting cDNA was immediately aliquoted and stored at − 20 °C for short-term use or at − 80 °C for long-term storage.

Quantitative real-time PCR (RT-qPCR)

For quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR), 1 µL of cDNA was amplified using SYBR Green qPCR Master Mix (2X) (Yekta-Tajhiz-Azma, Iran) in a final reaction volume of 20 µL, according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The qPCR was performed on a Rotor-Gene Q real-time PCR cycler (Qiagen, Germany) under the following thermal cycling conditions: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 10 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s (denaturation) and 60 °C for 30 s (annealing and extension).

Gene-specific primers TP53 (NM_000546.6) were designed using Oligo 7 (Molecular Biology Insights, USA), OligoAnalyzer Tool (IDT, USA), and Protocol Online to span exon-exon junctions where possible, minimizing the risk of genomic DNA amplification. Primer sequences are provided in Table 1. To confirm primer specificity, in silico BLAST analysis (NCBI, https://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov) was performed against the human transcriptome, ensuring unique amplification of the target gene.

Experimental validation of specificity was further confirmed by melting curve analysis, which was conducted at the end of each qPCR run (temperature ramp from 65 °C to 95 °C, increment 0.5 °C/5 s). All primer pairs exhibited a single sharp peak, indicating amplification of a specific product without primer-dimers or non-specific amplicons. PCR efficiency was evaluated using a 5-point standard curve generated from serially diluted cDNA (1:5 dilutions). Efficiency values ranged between 92% and 105%, with correlation coefficients (R²) > 0.99, meeting the criteria for reliable quantification.

All qPCR assays were performed using three independent biological replicates, each representing a distinct patient sample or biological condition. Each biological replicate was analyzed in triplicate (technical replicates) to ensure reproducibility and minimize technical variability.

The β-actin (ACTB) gene was selected as the internal reference gene due to its stable expression in breast cancer tissues, as supported by previous studies16. To validate its stability under the experimental conditions of this study, we assessed the coefficient of variation (CV) of Cq values across all samples, which was < 1.5 cycles, indicating consistent expression. Although stability was not formally assessed using geNorm or NormFinder, the low variation in Cq values supports its suitability as a reference gene in this cohort).

Relative gene expression was calculated using the comparative Cq (2⁻ΔΔCq) method, with normalization to β-actin. All data were reported as fold-change relative to the control group.

Statistical analysis

The CT values obtained from reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) were analyzed using the 2⁻ΔΔCt method to calculate the relative fold change in gene expression, with normalization to the reference gene β-actin. Prior to statistical analysis, fold change values were log₂-transformed to achieve normal distribution and stabilize variance, which is essential for parametric testing.

For comparisons between two independent groups (e.g., tumor vs. normal tissues), an unpaired two-tailed t-test was used. When comparing more than two groups (e.g., tumor stages I, II, and III), one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed, followed by Tukey’s post hoc test for pairwise comparisons to control for family-wise error rate. Categorical variables were analyzed using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test where appropriate.

To account for multiple comparisons in analyses involving several genes or clinical parameters, the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure was applied to control the false discovery rate (FDR), particularly in exploratory gene expression analyses. All statistical tests were two-sided, and a p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Wherever possible, exact p-values are reported (e.g., p = 0.023) rather than threshold-based expressions (e.g., p < 0.05), to enhance transparency and facilitate interpretation. In cases where p-values were extremely small, they are reported as p < 0.001.

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics software (version 16) and GraphPad Prism (version 8.0) for data visualization.

Results

Variable characteristics

In this study, a comprehensive examination was conducted on 70 women diagnosed with BC. The demographic and clinical characteristics of the participants were thoroughly assessed and analyzed. The average age of the patients was 36.17 ± 8.26 years, ranging from 21 to 57 years, representing a broad range of ages. The disease duration was measured, and the median duration, represented by the interquartile range (IQR), was determined to be 0.5 years. A notable finding was that 21.43% of patients reported a cancer family history. Cancer stage distribution was analyzed. It was observed that the highest frequency was attributed to stage T1, accounting for 48.57% of cases. This suggests that the majority of participants were diagnosed with early-stage BC, which could lead to more favorable treatment outcomes. Assessing the cancer grade, it was found that the highest frequency, 47.14%, was associated with grade 0. In addition to clinical characteristics, the study also examined the reproductive history of affected women. The average number of childbirths among the participants was 1.61 ± 0.88 (Table 2).

Gene expression of Tp53

A total of 14 individuals had positive expression of the Tp53 gene, according to research findings, which corresponds to 20% of the patients (Fig. 1).

The expression of the Tp53 gene in women diagnosed with breast cancer.

Figure 2 presents the level of Tp53 gene expression categorized by stage of the disease. The results indicate that the highest gene expression level was observed in stage 4, with a prevalence of 100%, while the lowest level was observed in stage 1, with no detectable expression. Furthermore, the analysis revealed statistically significant variation in gene expression across different stages. Specifically, expression was 28.57% in stage 2 and 71.43% in stage 3 (P < 0.001).

The expression of the Tp53 gene in women with breast cancer is classified according to the stage of the disease.

Tp53 gene expression was analyzed in BC patients, and several significant findings were revealed. Firstly, in the age group younger than 40 years, the expression of the Tp53 gene was 6.38%, whereas in women aged 40 years and above, the expression was considerably higher at 47.83% (P < 0.001). Moreover, women with a family history of BC exhibited a significantly higher expression of the Tp53 gene than women without such a history, with expression levels of 66.67% versus 4.27%, respectively (P < 0.001). Additionally, the Tp53 gene expression was the highest in patients with more than one year’s history of cancer. Furthermore, the grade of the disease was significantly associated with Tp53 gene expression. The highest level of gene expression was observed in grades 2 and 3, with a prevalence of 75% (P < 0.001). Lastly, the number of childbirths in women with BC influences Tp53 gene expression. Women who had undergone more than one childbirth demonstrated significantly higher expression of the Tp53 gene than those with fewer childbirth, with expression levels of 35% versus 0, respectively (P = 0.001) (Table 3).

Discussion

In many cancers, including BC, the TP53 tumor suppressor gene is mutated17. Tp53 is activated in the presence of genotoxic stress to facilitate DNA repair and cell cycle arrest, as well as apoptosis. The activation of p53’s tumor-suppressing activities in BC is often caused by overexpression of its negative regulator, MDM2, or by mutations, which account for 30–35% of all BC cases18. Most hormone receptor-positive or luminal subtypes of BC maintain wild-type Tp53 status19. In contrast, hormone receptor-negative patients are more likely to carry p53 mutations with gain-of-function oncogenic effects. In metastatic BC across all subtypes, p53 mutations are associated with shorter overall survival20. Patients with advanced-stage and high-grade tumors also have higher Tp53 mutation frequencies. These mutations are also associated with aggressive BC subtypes such as triple-negative BC (TNBC) and HER2-amplified BC21. Several variables, including tumor stage, were associated with the level of Tp53 gene expression in our study. The cancer stage refers to the extent or spread of the disease within the body. The staging system varies depending on the cancer type. In general, cancer is categorized into different stages, often numbered from 0 to IV, with higher numbers indicating a more advanced disease22. The specific criteria for each stage vary, but they usually consider factors such as tumor size, lymph node involvement, and metastasis23.

Numerous studies have consistently demonstrated the predictive significance of Tp53 across different stages of human malignancies. In early-stage malignancies, such as stage I and II, studies have shown that Tp53 mutations or overexpression can serve as predictive markers for disease aggressiveness and poor prognosis24. Higher Tp53 expression levels or the presence of p53 mutations in these stages are often associated with increased tumor invasiveness, higher rates of metastasis, and reduced overall survival rates for patients25. In a study conducted by Rahadiani et al., patients diagnosed with stage IB, II, and IIIA hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) were included. The study revealed that the expression of Tp53 was highest in individuals with stage II HCC, followed by those with stage IB and stage IIIA26. This finding contrasts with a another study that demonstrated a positive correlation between Tp53 expression and tumor staging, where patients with stage III-IV HCC exhibited a 90.9% rate of positive Tp53 expression27. According to a study conducted by Li et al., the mutation rate of Tp53 in stage I to II gastric cancer was found to be 64.46%, with the majority of cases exhibiting overexpression of the gene28. In a study conducted by Wang et al., it was found that positive Tp53 expression in stage II and III colorectal cancer (CRC) showed an inverse correlation with the five-year recurrence-free survival (RFS). Furthermore, the results of multivariate analysis indicated that positive Tp53 expression independently predicted a poor prognosis for patients with CRC29. Intriguingly, we found that stage 1 patients exhibited no detectable Tp53 gene expression. This finding suggests a potential role for the Tp53 gene as a biomarker for early-stage BC since its minimal expression or absence may indicate an early-stage disease. As cancer progresses to advanced stages, such as stage III and IV, the correlation between p53 alterations and disease outcomes becomes more complex30. While Tp53 mutations remain common, alterations in Tp53 expression levels may vary, with some studies reporting increased Tp53 expression and others observing decreased or absent expression31. Nevertheless, abnormal Tp53 function, whether through mutations or dysregulated expression, is generally associated with unfavorable clinical outcomes, including resistance to therapy, increased tumor recurrence, and decreased patient survival rates32,33. According to our analysis, higher levels of Tp53 gene expression correlate with increasing disease stage. Notably, stage 4 BC patients exhibited the highest expression levels. This finding suggests a possible correlation between advanced disease progression and Tp53 gene upregulation. With the progression of BC into more advanced stages, a combination of genetic alterations and cellular stresses may trigger an adaptive response that increases Tp53 gene expression34. More research and validation in larger patient cohorts is necessary before Tp53 gene expression can be used as a biomarker for early-stage BC35. Identifying the underlying molecular mechanisms driving BC progression by understanding the relationship between disease stage and Tp53 gene expression can provide valuable insights36.

There were also significant correlations between other variables and Tp53 expression levels in our study. Patients younger than 40 had lower expression compared to those older than 40. The findings suggest that age may influence the regulation of the Tp53 gene in BC, which may contribute to the disease’s pathogenesis. In a study by Calvocoressi et al., it was found that very elderly men often develop biologically aggressive prostate cancer due to the overexpression of TP53 in older men37. The increased risk of cancer with age is a result of TP53 mutations being acquired somatically and increasing with age. Patients with a family history of BC demonstrated significantly higher expression levels than those without such a history. This observation suggests that genetic factors associated with hereditary BC may influence the expression of the Tp53 gene. This highlights the importance of familial history in studying BC molecular characteristics. In addition to familial history, cancer duration also affected Tp53 gene expression. Patients with a history of BC for more than one year exhibited higher expression levels than those with a shorter duration. This finding suggests that disease progression over a period of time may contribute to the Tp53 gene upregulation. The findings of Huo’s study indicated that Tp53 serves as a barrier to hereditary breast cancer development in the presence of DNA damage and oxidative stress induced by PALB2 loss38. Moreover, the grade of the disease was significantly associated with p53 gene expression. Higher expression levels were observed in grades 2 and 3, indicating a potential relationship between tumor aggressiveness and increased Tp53 gene expression. This finding underscores the importance of assessing BC grade when studying the molecular characteristics of the disease. As Hodgson et al. demonstrated, in high grade bladder cancer patients with missense TP53 mutations, p53 was overexpressed in 50% of tumor cells, including 67% of cases with strong p53 immunoexpression in 100% of tumor cells39. Lastly, the number of childbirths was identified as a variable influencing p53 gene expression. Women with multiple childbirths exhibited higher expression levels than those with fewer childbirths. As a consequence of this observation, the regulation of the Tp53 gene may be affected by hormonal and physiological changes associated with pregnancy and childbirth. In contrast, Ida et al. found that the Tp53signature was significantly lower in parous women and pregnant women with precursors of high grade serous ovarian cancer (HGSOC). A possible mechanism for the reduced risk of HGSOC in parous women could be the reduced incidence of early phase serous carcinogenesis40.

However, it is important to acknowledge the limitations of the present study. These limitations include the fact that it was conducted at a single center, the sample size was small, there was no gene sequencing performed to confirm the presence of Tp53 mutations, and the study did not assess overall survival due to limited time and access to complete clinical and therapeutic information. Therefore, further studies are needed to investigate additional predictive or prognostic factors. These studies could involve assessing the relationship between Tp53 expression and treatment response as well as overall survival.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates significant associations between TP53 gene expression and key clinicopathological features in a cohort of Iranian women with breast cancer (BC). A particularly striking finding is the complete absence of detectable TP53 expression in all Stage I tumors, in contrast to a progressive increase in advanced stages, culminating in 100% expression in Stage IV disease. This pattern suggests that TP53 dysregulation may not drive tumor initiation in BC but rather emerges as a hallmark of disease progression and aggressive phenotypes. Such stage-dependent expression underscores its potential utility as a molecular biomarker for risk stratification and disease monitoring.

Elevated TP53 expression was significantly associated with older age (≥ 40 years), positive family history of cancer, higher histological grade (G2/G3), prolonged disease duration, and increased number of childbirths, all of which are indicators of adverse prognosis. While TP53 overexpression is commonly observed in malignancies such as colorectal (CRC) and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), where mutations often occur early, its nearabsence in earlystage BC may reflect tissue-specific differences in carcinogenesis. This implies that breast cancer may initially evolve through alternative oncogenic pathways, with TP53 dysfunction becoming prominent in later stages.

It is important to note that RT-qPCR measures mRNA levels without distinguishing between wild-type and mutant TP53. Therefore, observed overexpression may reflect stabilization of mutant transcripts—known for their prolonged half-life—rather than functional tumor suppressor activity.

A key limitation is the lack of protein-level validation (e.g., immunohistochemistry) and mutational analysis, which prevents definitive conclusions about p53 functionality. Future studies should integrate DNA sequencing and IHC to differentiate molecular subtypes and assess protein accumulation.

Despite these constraints, our findings provide novel insights from an underrepresented population and contribute to the understanding of TP53 dynamics in BC. Further research with multi-omics and longitudinal designs is warranted to explore its clinical implications.

Limitations and future directions

We acknowledge that direct protein-level validation (e.g., IHC or Western blot) and sequencing for TP53 mutations would further strengthen our conclusions. However, due to limited tissue availability and resource constraints, these analyses were not feasible in the current phase. We have clearly stated these limitations in the revised manuscript and propose that future studies incorporate IHC for p53 and Sanger/NGS sequencing to distinguish between wild-type and mutant forms, especially in high-expression cases.
