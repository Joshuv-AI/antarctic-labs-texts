Reducing Spatial and Temporal Dimensionality in the Multidimensional Caldeira–Leggett Model
Hongfei Zhan, Ernest W.Z. Pan, Zhenning Cai

Abstract
Focusing on the real-time dynamics of the reduced density matrix of the multidimensional Caldeira-Leggett model, several techniques are adopted in this paper to reduce the spatial and temporal dimensionality, combined into an efficient algorithm. From a spatial perspective, an equivalent formulation of the Dyson series is presented. With the aid of a low-rank approximation, the spatial dimensionality of open quantum system simulations is halved. From a temporal perspective, the frozen Gaussian approximation is used to approximate both the evolution operator and the interaction operator in the multidimensional Caldeira-Leggett model. This reduces the high-dimensional integrals to one- and two-dimensional integrals independent of the truncation level of the Dyson series. Through these techniques, we design an efficient algorithm whose validity is verified through several numerical experiments, including a two-dimensional double slit simulation. Keywords : Open quantum system, Caldeira-Leggett model, path integral method, frozen Gaussian approximation

Introduction

An open quantum system refers to a quantum-mechanical system coupled to an external environment, with interactions that can significantly influence their quantum dynamics, leading to quantum effects such as quantum dissipation and quantum decoherence
[
17
,
45
]
.
Due to its universality, the theory of open quantum systems has been widely used in diverse fields, encompassing quantum computing
[
22
]
, quantum communication
[
41
]
, and quantum optical systems
[
2
]
.

The open quantum system coupled to a harmonic bath is widely employed as a simplified model to study the dissipative effects of condensed-phase environments
[
7
]
and also to simulate the effects of environments composed of normal mode vibrations, lattice phonons, and other more complex unstructured environments
[
11
,
31
]
. A notable feature of the harmonic bath is that its degrees of freedom can be analytically integrated out in the path integral formulation, motivating the development of diverse numerical techniques for simulating open quantum systems.
A typical example is the spin-boson model
[
10
,
50
,
57
]
, which describes a two-level system interacting with a bosonic environment. For open systems with more possible states, current research predominantly focuses on systems with special structures such as open spin chains
[
34
,
52
,
46
]
. In these systems, matrix product state representations are used to make the simulation tractable, but extending such approaches beyond one dimension remains challenging
[
58
,
52
,
13
,
46
]
.
The spin-boson model can also be viewed as an implementation of the Caldeira-Leggett model
[
6
,
7
,
8
]
, in which a particle is located in a double-well potential.
However, most existing approaches remain limited to finite-dimensional systems, and numerical investigations of the Caldeira-Leggett model are confined to one-dimensional cases
[
54
]
.

Various models and methods have been developed to simulate open quantum systems.
The Nakajima-Zwanzig equation
[
40
,
60
]
captures the temporal non-locality through a memory kernel. In the weak system-bath coupling regime, the process can be approximated by a Markovian process described by the Lindblad equation
[
24
]
. However, in more general cases, it would be difficult to bypass the non-Markovian nature during the numerical simulation.
Due to the decay of the memory effect, a finite memory length prevents unlimited growth of storage or computational cost. The transfer tensor method (TTM)
[
9
]
uses this technique to derive a discretized form of the Nakajima-Zwanzig equation. Alternatively, the hierarchical equations of motion (HEOM)
[
47
,
49
,
48
]
can be applied to the harmonic bath.

The path integral
[
14
,
16
]
plays a crucial role in the simulation of open quantum systems. It offers a classical-like picture of the quantum process.
A variety of methods have been developed based on the path integral framework. The quasi-adiabatic propagator path integral (QuAPI)
[
28
]
uses the influence functional developed by Feynman and Vernon
[
15
]
for non-Markovian dynamics.
Building upon QuAPI, many approaches have been proposed to improve simulation efficiency by reducing computational complexity or enhancing accuracy, including the iterative QuAPI method (i-QuAPI)
[
29
,
30
]
, the blip decomposition of the path integral
[
32
,
33
]
, the differential equation-based path integral method (DEBPI)
[
51
]
, and the kink sum method
[
38
]
.
Recently, the small matrix path integral (SMatPI)
[
36
,
37
,
35
,
53
]
successfully addressed the high memory demands associated with the summation over numerous paths by compactly representing their contributions through small matrices. Although the starting points are different, SMatPI yields a formulation akin to the Nakajima-Zwanzig equation.

Due to the large number of paths arising from the non-Markovian intrinsicality, a natural approach is the Monte Carlo methods. For example, the diagrammatic quantum Monte Carlo method (dQMC) uses diagrams to intuitively represent the coupling between the system and the bath
[
42
,
55
]
. However, Monte Carlo methods often suffer from the numerical sign problem
[
26
,
4
]
. Different techniques, such as the application of bold lines
[
43
,
44
,
12
,
3
]
, the inclusion-exclusion principle
[
1
,
59
]
, and the combination of thin lines and bold lines
[
5
]
, have been developed to mitigate the numerical sign problem or accelerate the computation.

In this paper, an efficient algorithm is developed for real-time simulations of the reduced density matrix for the multidimensional Caldeira-Leggett model, with considerable effort devoted to reducing both the spatial and temporal dimensionality of the model.

In the spatial domain, the open quantum system is depicted by the Dyson series, formulated in an equivalent form to the commonly used Keldysh contour. This representation allows for efficient algorithms through the reuse of intermediate variables. Moreover, a low-rank approximation is introduced for the two-point correlation function, facilitating the decomposition of diagrams associated with the Dyson series. Consequently, our formulation requires computation of the wave function rather than the density matrix, halving the spatial dimensionality. In addition, we also factorize numerous diagrams into the product of single diagrams, further reducing the computational cost.

For the temporal dimensionality, separation between the interaction operator and the reduced density matrix is achieved through the frozen Gaussian approximation, which leads to a commutable scalar description of the interaction operator. The resulting multidimensional integrals in the expression of the reduced density matrix are further decomposed into one- and two-dimensional integrals.
Building upon these techniques, we design an efficient algorithm to solve the multidimensional Caldeira-Leggett model. The temporal dependency of the computational cost is reduced to that of the first non-trivial term in the Dyson series.
Numerical results, including the simulation of the two-dimensional double slit phenomenon, validate the effectiveness of the proposed method. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first algorithm capable of simulating the two-dimensional Caldeira-Leggett model.

The paper is organized as follows. In Sect.
2
, we introduce the open quantum system along with a low-rank approximation of the two-point correlation function. Next, the general-dimensional Caldeira-Leggett model is presented in Sect.
3.1
and the frozen Gaussian approximation is introduced in Sect.
3.2
. We describe our main algorithm in Sect.
3.3
, with corresponding numerical results in Sect.
4
. Lastly, we provide an overall conclusion in Sect.
5
.

2
Open quantum systems

Consider the von Neumann equation for quantum evolution

i\epsilon\frac{d\rho}{dt}=[H,\rho],

(1)

where
\epsilon
is the dimensionless parameter quantifying the ratio of quantum to classical scales，
\rho(t)
is the density matrix at time
t
, and
H
is the Schrödinger picture Hamiltonian in the form

\displaystyle H

\displaystyle=H_{0}+\epsilon W,

(2)

\displaystyle H_{0}

\displaystyle=H_{s}\otimes\id_{b}+\id_{s}\otimes H_{b},

(3)

\displaystyle W

\displaystyle=W_{s}\otimes W_{b}.

(4)

Here
H_{0}
is the Hamiltonian consisting of the system Hamiltonian
H_{s}
and the bath Hamiltonian
H_{b}
, and
W
depicts the interaction between the system
W_{s}
and the bath
W_{b}
.
Assuming that the initial system is in the pure state
\psi_{s}^{(0)}
, with the bath in the thermal equilibrium state, we can express the initial density operator as

\rho_{0}=\rho_{s}^{(0)}\otimes\rho_{b}^{(0)},

(5)

where

\displaystyle\rho_{s}^{(0)}

\displaystyle:=|\psi_{s}^{(0)}\rangle\langle\psi_{s}^{(0)}|,

(6)

\displaystyle\rho_{b}^{(0)}

\displaystyle:=\frac{e^{-\beta H_{b}}}{\tr_{b}\left(e^{-\beta H_{b}}\right)},

(7)

\beta
is the inverse temperature, and
\tr_{b}
refers to the partial trace of the bath part. The solution of (
1
) can be given by

\rho(t)=e^{-iHt/\epsilon}\rho_{0}e^{iHt/\epsilon}.

(8)

Then we focus on the reduced density matrix of the system as follows.

\rho_{s}(t)=\tr_{b}\Big(\rho(t)\Big)=\tr_{b}\Big(e^{-iHt/\epsilon}\rho_{0}e^{iHt/\epsilon}\Big).

(9)

In the following subsections, the Dyson series is introduced to approximate the reduced density matrix, while Wick’s theorem is used to simplify the bath influence function. In particular, two equivalent forms of the bath influence function are presented, motivating the separation in Sect.
2.2
, and illustrating the possibility of further simplification in Sect.
3.3
. Consequently, a separated formulation of the reduced density matrix is derived with the aid of a low-rank approximation in Sect.
2.3
.

2.1
Dyson series

Regarding the coupling term
\epsilon W
as a perturbation of
H_{0}
, one can derive the Dyson series of the evolution operators as follows.

\displaystyle e^{-iHt/\epsilon}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{n=0}^{\infty}\int_{\boldsymbol{s}\in\mathcal{S}_{t}^{n}}(-i)^{n}e^{-iH_{0}(t-s_{n})/\epsilon}We^{-iH_{0}(s_{n}-s_{n-1})/\epsilon}W\cdots We^{-iH_{0}s_{1}/\epsilon}d\boldsymbol{s},

(10)

where
\boldsymbol{s}=(s_{1},s_{2},\dots,s_{n})
, and the integral over an
n
-dimensional simplex of size
t
is formulated as

\int_{\boldsymbol{s}\in\mathcal{S}_{t}^{n}}d\boldsymbol{s}:=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{s_{n}}\cdots\int_{0}^{s_{2}}ds_{1}\cdots ds_{n-1}ds_{n}.

(11)

Consequently, one can derive the Dyson series of the reduced density matrix in (
9
) ,

\displaystyle\rho_{s,S}(t)

\displaystyle=e^{-iH_{s}t/\epsilon}\rho_{s,I}(t)e^{iH_{s}t/\epsilon},

(12)

\displaystyle\rho_{s,I}(t)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{n_{1},n_{2}=0}^{\infty}\int_{\boldsymbol{s}^{(1)}\in\mathcal{S}_{t}^{n_{1}}}\int_{\boldsymbol{s}^{(2)}\in\mathcal{S}_{t}^{n_{2}}}\Big((-i)^{n_{1}}G_{s}(\boldsymbol{s}^{(1)})\Big)|\psi_{s}^{(0)}\rangle\langle\psi_{s}^{(0)}|

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\cdot\Big((-i)^{n_{2}}G_{s}(\boldsymbol{s}^{(2)})\Big)^{\dagger}\mathcal{L}_{b}(\boldsymbol{s}^{(1)},\boldsymbol{s}^{(2)})d\boldsymbol{s}^{(1)}d\boldsymbol{s}^{(2)},

(13)

where
\rho_{s,S}(t),\rho_{s,I}(t)
are the system density matrices in the Schrödinger picture and the interaction picture, respectively.
Also,
\boldsymbol{s}^{(1)}=(s_{1}^{(1)},\ldots,s_{n_{1}}^{(1)})
,
\boldsymbol{s}^{(2)}=(s_{1}^{(2)},\ldots,s_{n_{2}}^{(2)})
, and

G_{s}(\boldsymbol{s})=\mathcal{T}\prod\limits_{k=1}^{n}W_{s,I}(s_{k}),\qquad W_{s,I}(s_{k}):=e^{iH_{s}s_{k}/\epsilon}W_{s}e^{-iH_{s}s_{k}/\epsilon},

(14)

with
\mathcal{T}
being the time-ordering operator. We also have the non-Markovian bath influence functional, defined by

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{b}(\boldsymbol{s}^{(1)},\boldsymbol{s}^{(2)})

\displaystyle=\tr\left(e^{-iH_{b}(t-s_{n_{1}}^{(1)})}W_{b}\cdots W_{b}e^{-iH_{b}s_{1}^{(1)}}\rho_{b}^{(0)}e^{iH_{b}s_{1}^{(2)}}W_{b}\cdots W_{b}e^{iH_{b}(t-s_{n_{2}}^{(2)})}\right).

(15)

By Wick’s theorem
[
56
]
, it holds that

\mathcal{L}_{b}(\boldsymbol{s}^{(1)},\boldsymbol{s}^{(2)})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}0,&\text{if $n_{1}+n_{2}$ is odd},\\
\overline{\mathcal{L}}_{b}(s_{1}^{(1)},\ldots,s_{n_{1}}^{(1)},s_{n_{2}}^{(2)},\ldots,s_{1}^{(2)}),&\text{if $n_{1}+n_{2}$ is even},\end{array}\right.

where the function
\overline{\mathcal{L}}_{b}(\boldsymbol{s})
with a given
\boldsymbol{s}=(s_{1},\cdots,s_{n})
is defined by

\overline{\mathcal{L}}_{b}(\boldsymbol{s})=\sum_{P\in\mathscr{P}_{n}}\prod_{(i,j)\in P}B(s_{j},s_{i}).

(16)

Here
\mathscr{P}_{n}
contains all possible pairings of the integer set
\{1,\cdots,n\}
, and in each pair, the first element is smaller than the second. For instance,

\displaystyle\mathscr{P}_{2}

\displaystyle=\big\{\{(1,2)\}\big\},

\displaystyle\mathscr{P}_{4}

\displaystyle=\big\{\{(1,2),(3,4)\},\,\{(1,3),(2,4)\},\,\{(1,4),(2,3)\}\big\},

\displaystyle\mathscr{P}_{6}

\displaystyle=\big\{\{(1,2),(3,4),(5,6)\},\,\{(1,3),(2,4),(5,6)\},\,\{(1,4),(2,3),(5,6)\},\,\cdots,\,\{(1,6),(2,5),(3,4)\}\big\}.

In general, for an even
n
,
\mathscr{P}_{n}
contains
(n-1)!!
elements.
The function
B(\cdot,\cdot)
in (
16
) is known as the bath correlation function, which can be computed via

B(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})=\tr\left(W_{b,I}(\tau_{1})W_{b,I}(\tau_{2})\rho_{b}^{(0)}\right),\qquad W_{b,I}(\tau)=e^{iH_{b}\tau/\epsilon}W_{b}e^{-iH_{b}\tau/\epsilon}.

(17)

Alternatively,
\overline{\mathcal{L}}_{b}(\boldsymbol{s})
can also be defined recursively:

\overline{\mathcal{L}}_{b}(\boldsymbol{s})=\sum_{k=2}^{n}B(s_{k},s_{1})\overline{\mathcal{L}}_{b}(\boldsymbol{s}\backslash\{s_{1},s_{k}\}),\qquad\overline{\mathcal{L}}_{b}(\emptyset)=1.

The definition of
B(\cdot,\cdot)
is postponed to Sect.
4
.

Remark 2.1
.

(
13
) shows that a system initially in a pure state can evolve into a mixed state due to its interaction with the environment, hence we cannot use a wave function to describe an open quantum system.

Following Feynman’s idea, the Dyson series (
13
) can be understood as the sum of diagrams:

\rho_{s,I}(t)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n_{1},n_{2}=0\\
n_{1}+n_{2}\text{ is even}\end{subarray}}^{\infty}\sum_{P\in\mathscr{P}_{n_{1}+n_{2}}}\text{diag}(n_{1},n_{2},P),

where the sum over
\mathscr{P}_{n_{1}+n_{2}}
comes from the bath influence functional (
16
).
Each diagram
\text{diag}(n_{1},n_{2},P)
has the following structure:

1.

The diagram consists of two axes, with the top axis representing the integral with respect to
\boldsymbol{s}^{(1)}
and the bottom axis representing the integral with respect to
\boldsymbol{s}^{(2)}
. Any features on the bottom axis correspond to the conjugate transpose of the same feature on the top axis.

2.

Both axes start with a red cross
\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\boldsymbol{\times}
denoting
|\psi_{s}^{(0)}\rangle
or
\langle\psi_{s}^{(0)}|=|\psi_{s}^{(0)}\rangle^{\dagger}
in the first line of (
13
). A dashed line connecting the red crosses shows the relative positions of
|\psi_{s}^{(0)}\rangle
and
\langle\psi_{s}^{(0)}|
being beside each other in (
13
).

3.

On the top axis, there are
n_{1}
nodes corresponding to the operators
-iW_{s,I}(s_{k}^{(1)})
. Similarly, on the bottom axis, there are
n_{2}
nodes corresponding to the operators
\left(-iW_{s,I}(s_{k}^{(2)})\right)^{\dagger}
. For both axes, the index

increases from left to right. The proximity of the nodes to the cross on the same axis corresponds to the proximity of the respective operators to

in (

|\psi_{s}^{(0)}\rangle\langle\psi_{s}^{(0)}|
and those on the bottom axis being to the right of
|\psi_{s}^{(0)}\rangle\langle\psi_{s}^{(0)}|
.

4.

The nodes are paired using arcs, each representing a bath correlation function

whose parameters are the
s
-values represented by the nodes. These pairings are determined by
P
, and the position of

in (

\boldsymbol{s}^{(2)}

. Otherwise, the variables associated with
\boldsymbol{s}^{(2)}

2.3
Low-rank approximation of two-point correlation function

Factorization of the entire diagram remains a challenge due to the integration with respect to
\boldsymbol{s}^{(1)}
and
\boldsymbol{s}^{(2)}
.
The purpose of this section is to separate the integrals of these two variables so that they can be integrated independently, allowing for a complete factorization of the diagrams. Consequently, the evaluation of the reduced density matrix can be achieved by solely computing the wave function, halving the spatial dimensionality.
Since
\boldsymbol{s}^{(1)}
and
\boldsymbol{s}^{(2)}
are related by the function
\mathcal{L}_{b}^{\rm cross}(\cdot,\cdot)
, our plan is to apply low-rank approximation to this function, which is essentially a low-rank approximation of the bath correlation function
B(\cdot,\cdot)
.

We assume that the two-point correlation function, which satisfies
B(t_{1},t_{2})=[B(t_{2},t_{1})]^{*}
, has rank
r
,
i.e.
, there exist scalars
\lambda_{j}
and univariate functions

,
j=1,\cdots,r
, such that

B(t_{1},t_{2})=\sum\limits_{j=1}^{r}\lambda_{j}V_{j}^{*}(t_{1})V_{j}(t_{2}).

(27)

Note that

\prod\limits_{k=1}^{n}V_{j_{k}}(s_{\sigma(k)})=\prod\limits_{k=1}^{n}V_{j_{\sigma^{-1}(k)}}(s_{k}),

for some permutation
\sigma\in\mathscr{Q}_{n}
.
Substituting the low-rank approximation into (

\displaystyle\rho_{s,I}(t)=

\displaystyle\left(\prod\limits_{k=1}^{n}V_{j_{k}}(s_{k}^{(1)})\right)\mathcal{L}_{b}^{\rm same}(\boldsymbol{\tau}^{(1)})G\left([\boldsymbol{s}^{(1)},\boldsymbol{\tau}^{(1)}]\right)|\psi_{s}^{(0)}\rangle\langle\psi_{s}^{(0)}|

\displaystyle\cdot\left(\prod\limits_{k=1}^{n}V_{j_{\sigma(k)}}(s_{k}^{(2)})\right)^{*}\mathcal{L}_{b}^{\rm same}(\boldsymbol{\tau}^{(2)})^{*}G\left([\boldsymbol{s}^{(2)},\boldsymbol{\tau}^{(2)}]\right)^{\dagger}d\boldsymbol{\tau}^{(1)}d\boldsymbol{\tau}^{(2)}d\boldsymbol{s}^{(1)}d\boldsymbol{s}^{(2)},

3
An efficient algorithm for Caldeira-Leggett model

In this section, we apply the conclusion in Sect.
2
to the Caldeira-Leggett model, where a quantum particle is coupled to a harmonic bath.
Instead of direct discretization of the wave function, we apply the frozen Gaussian approximation that can effectively handle high-frequency waves in the solution. This also facilitates the design of an efficient algorithm for computing the density. In what follows, we will first review the Caldeira-Leggett model and the technique of frozen Gaussian approximation before introducing our numerical solver to compute the density.

3.1
Caldeira-Leggett model

In most literature, the Caldeira-Leggett model is described as a one-dimensional particle coupled to a bath.
Here, we provide a multidimensional version of the model, which can be considered as several interacting one-dimensional particles coupled to separate heat baths.
This type of model has been studied extensively for many-particle open quantum systems
[
34
,
23
,
39
]
. In the
D
-dimensional Caldeira-Leggett model, the Hamiltonian can be formulated as

H_{s}=-\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{2}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{x}}^{2}+V(\boldsymbol{x})+\sum\limits_{l=1}^{L}\frac{c_{l}^{2}}{2\omega_{l}^{2}}|\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}|^{2},\qquad H_{b}=\sum\limits_{l=1}^{L}\left(-\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{2}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{z}_{l}}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\omega_{l}^{2}|\hat{\boldsymbol{z}_{l}}|^{2}\right),

(35)

and the interaction operator describes the coupling between the particle and harmonic oscillators:

W=\sum\limits_{d=1}^{D}W_{s}^{(d)}\otimes W_{b}^{(d)},

(36)

where

W_{s}^{(d)}=\hat{x}_{d},\qquad\text{and}\qquad W_{b}^{(d)}=\frac{1}{\epsilon}\sum\limits_{l=1}^{L}c_{l}\hat{z}_{l,d}.

(37)

Here we provide a list of notations used in the equations above:

•

\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}
(
\hat{x}_{d}
): The position operator of the particle in the system,
\psi(t,\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{z})\mapsto\boldsymbol{x}\psi(t,\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{z})
(
\psi(t,\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{z})\mapsto x_{d}\psi(t,\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{z})
).

•

\hat{\boldsymbol{z}_{l}}
(
\hat{z}_{l,d}
): The position operator of the particle in the bath,
\psi(t,\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{z})\mapsto\boldsymbol{z}_{l}\psi(t,\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{z})
(
\psi(t,\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{z})\mapsto z_{l,d}\psi(t,\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{z})
).

•

\omega_{j}
: The frequency of the
l
-th harmonic oscillator.

•

c_{j}
: The coupling intensity between the particle and the
l
-th harmonic oscillator.

•

V
: The potential function which is real and smooth.

The density matrix in the Caldeira-Leggett model can also be represented as a Dyson series like (
13
).
However, when defining the propagator
G_{s}(\boldsymbol{s})
(see (
14
)), the coupling operator
W_{s}
now depends on the dimension
d
, leading to the definition

G_{s}(\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{d})=\mathcal{T}\prod\limits_{k=1}^{n}W_{s,I}^{(d_{k})}(s_{k}),\quad W_{s,I}^{(d_{k})}(s_{k}):=e^{iH_{s}s_{k}}W_{s}^{(d_{k})}e^{-iH_{s}s_{k}},

(38)

where
\mathcal{T}
is again the time-ordering operator that arranges the
W_{s,I}
operators chronologically according to the time variable
s_{k}
, and
\boldsymbol{d}=(d_{1},\cdots,d_{n})
is a multi-index in
\{1,\cdots,D\}^{n}
.
The bath influence functional
\mathcal{L}_{b}
will also depend on the dimension
d_{k}
.
Given
\boldsymbol{s}^{(1)}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n_{1}}
,
\boldsymbol{d}^{(1)}\in\{1,\cdots,D\}^{n_{1}}
and
\boldsymbol{s}^{(2)}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n_{2}}
,
\boldsymbol{d}^{(2)}\in\{1,\cdots,D\}^{n_{2}}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{b}\Big(\boldsymbol{s}^{(1)},\boldsymbol{d}^{(1)};\boldsymbol{s}^{(2)},\boldsymbol{d}^{(2)}\Big)

(39)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}0,&\text{if $n_{1}+n_{2}$ is odd},\\
\overline{\mathcal{L}}_{b}\Big((s_{1}^{(1)},\cdots,s_{n_{1}}^{(1)},s_{n_{2}}^{(2)},\ldots,s_{1}^{(2)}),(d_{1}^{(1)},\cdots,d_{n_{1}}^{(1)},d_{n_{2}}^{(2)},\ldots,d_{1}^{(2)})\Big),&\text{if $n_{1}+n_{2}$ is even},\end{array}\right.

where

\overline{\mathcal{L}}_{b}\Big((s_{1},\cdots,s_{n}),(d_{1},\cdots,d_{n})\Big)=\sum_{P\in\mathscr{P}_{n}}\prod_{(i,j)\in P}\delta_{d_{i},d_{j}}B(s_{j},s_{i}).

(40)

Thus, the Dyson series expansion of the density matrix turns out to be

\displaystyle\rho_{s,I}(t)=\sum\limits_{n_{1},n_{2}=0}^{\infty}

\displaystyle\int_{\boldsymbol{s}^{(1)}\in\mathcal{S}_{t}^{n_{1}}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{d}^{(1)}\in\{1,\cdots,D\}^{n_{1}}}\int_{\boldsymbol{s}^{(2)}\in\mathcal{S}_{t}^{n_{2}}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{d}^{(2)}\in\{1,\cdots,D\}^{n_{2}}}\Big((-i)^{n_{1}}G_{s}\left(\boldsymbol{s}^{(1)},\boldsymbol{d}^{(1)}\right)\Big)|\psi_{s}^{(0)}\rangle\langle\psi_{s}^{(0)}|

(41)

\displaystyle\cdot\Big((-i)^{n_{2}}G_{s}\left(\boldsymbol{s}^{(2)},\boldsymbol{d}^{(2)}\right)\Big)^{\dagger}\mathcal{L}_{b}\Big(\boldsymbol{s}^{(1)},\boldsymbol{d}^{(1)};\boldsymbol{s}^{(2)},\boldsymbol{d}^{(2)}\Big)d\boldsymbol{s}^{(1)}d\boldsymbol{s}^{(2)}.

3.2
Frozen Gaussian Approximation

Although much simplification is considered in the former section, direct computations of
D
-dimensional
|\phi_{j}\rangle
in (
34
) remain expensive. In this subsection, the Frozen Gaussian Approximation (FGA) is introduced to discretize the system wave function. This validates a simple approximation of the interaction operator, as well as acceleration by parallel implementation.

FGA
[
18
,
27
,
54
]
utilizes the ansatz

\psi_{\rm FGA}(t,\boldsymbol{x})=\frac{1}{(2\pi\epsilon)^{3D/2}}\iint_{\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{q}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}}\int_{\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}}a(t,\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{q})e^{i\phi(t,\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{q})/\epsilon}\psi_{s}^{(0)}(\boldsymbol{y})d\boldsymbol{y}d\boldsymbol{p}d\boldsymbol{q},

(46)

where

\phi(t,\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{q})=S(t,\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{q})+\frac{i}{2}|\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{Q}|^{2}+\boldsymbol{P}\cdot(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{Q})+\frac{i}{2}|\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{q}|^{2}-\boldsymbol{p}\cdot(\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{q}).

(47)

Then

\psi_{\rm FGA}(0,\boldsymbol{x})=\psi_{s}^{(0)}(\boldsymbol{x}),\qquad\psi_{\rm FGA}(t,\boldsymbol{x})=e^{-iH_{s}t/\epsilon}\psi_{s}^{(0)}(\boldsymbol{x})+O(\epsilon).

(48)

For brevity, we take

\tilde{V}(\boldsymbol{x})=V(\boldsymbol{x})+\sum\limits_{l=1}^{L}\frac{c_{l}^{2}}{2\omega_{l}^{2}}|\boldsymbol{x}|^{2}.

As functions of
(t,\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{q})
, the variables
\boldsymbol{P}
,
\boldsymbol{Q}
,
S
,
a
satisfy the following dynamics:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{P}}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=-\nabla\tilde{V}(\boldsymbol{Q}),\qquad

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{Q}}{\partial t}=\boldsymbol{P},

(49)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial S}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=\frac{|\boldsymbol{P}|^{2}}{2}-\tilde{V}(\boldsymbol{Q}),\qquad

\displaystyle\frac{\partial a}{\partial t}=\frac{a}{2}\tr\left(\boldsymbol{Z}^{-1}\left(\nabla_{\boldsymbol{z}}\boldsymbol{P}-i\nabla_{\boldsymbol{z}}\boldsymbol{Q}\nabla^{2}\tilde{V}(\boldsymbol{Q})\right)\right),

where

\nabla_{\boldsymbol{z}}=\nabla_{\boldsymbol{q}}-i\nabla_{\boldsymbol{p}},\qquad\text{and}\qquad\boldsymbol{Z}=\nabla_{\boldsymbol{z}}\boldsymbol{Q}+i\nabla_{\boldsymbol{z}}\boldsymbol{P}.

The initial conditions are

\boldsymbol{P}(0,\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{q})=\boldsymbol{p},\qquad\boldsymbol{Q}(0,\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{q})=\boldsymbol{q},\qquad S(0,\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{q})=0,\\
\qquad a(0,\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{q})=2^{D/2}.

(50)

To solve this differential equation system, we also need the equations

\frac{\partial(\nabla_{\boldsymbol{z}}\boldsymbol{P})}{\partial t}(t,\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{q})=-\nabla_{\boldsymbol{z}}\boldsymbol{Q}(t,\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{q})\nabla^{2}\tilde{V}(\boldsymbol{Q}(t,\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{q})),\qquad\frac{\partial(\nabla_{\boldsymbol{z}}\boldsymbol{Q})}{\partial t}(t,\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{q})=\nabla_{\boldsymbol{z}}\boldsymbol{P}(t,\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{q}),

(51)

with initial conditions

\nabla_{\boldsymbol{z}}\boldsymbol{P}(0,\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{q})=-iI_{D},\qquad\nabla_{\boldsymbol{z}}\boldsymbol{Q}(0,\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{q})=I_{D}.

(52)

It follows
[
27
,
54
]
that FGA provides the following approximations,

W_{s,I}^{(d)}(s)=Q_{d}(s,\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{q})+O(\epsilon),

(53)

and

\displaystyle e^{-iH_{s}(t-t_{0})/\epsilon}\bigg(\frac{1}{(2\pi\epsilon)^{3D/2}}\iint_{\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{q}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}}\int_{\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}}f(\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{q})a(t_{0},\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{q})e^{i\phi(t_{0},\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{q})/\epsilon）}\psi_{s}^{(0)}(\boldsymbol{y})d\boldsymbol{y}d\boldsymbol{p}\boldsymbol{q}\bigg)

(54)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(2\pi\epsilon)^{3D/2}}\iint_{\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{q}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}}\int_{\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}}f(\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{q})a(t,\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{q})e^{i\phi(t,\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{q})/\epsilon）}\psi_{s}^{(0)}(\boldsymbol{y})d\boldsymbol{y}d\boldsymbol{p}\boldsymbol{q},

for an arbitrary factor
f(\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{q})
. Hence,

\displaystyle\rho_{s}(t,\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle=\langle\boldsymbol{x}|e^{-iH_{s}t/\epsilon}\rho_{s,I}(t)e^{iH_{s}t/\epsilon}|\boldsymbol{x}\rangle

(55)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum\limits_{|\boldsymbol{N}|=n}\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\boldsymbol{N}}\boldsymbol{N}!\Big(\langle\boldsymbol{x}|e^{-iH_{s}t/\epsilon}I_{n,\boldsymbol{N}}(t)|\psi_{s}^{(0)}\rangle\Big)\Big(\langle\boldsymbol{x}|e^{-iH_{s}t/\epsilon}I_{n,\boldsymbol{N}}(t)|\psi_{s}^{(0)}\rangle\Big)^{*},

where

\displaystyle I_{n,\boldsymbol{N}}(t,\boldsymbol{x}):=\langle\boldsymbol{x}|I_{n,\boldsymbol{N}}(t)|\psi_{s}^{(0)}\rangle

(56)

\displaystyle\approx\int_{\boldsymbol{s}\in\mathcal{S}_{t}^{n}}\sum\limits_{(\boldsymbol{j},\boldsymbol{d})\in\mathcal{J}(\boldsymbol{N})}\left(\prod\limits_{k=1}^{n}V_{j_{k}}(s_{k})\right)\sum\limits_{m=0\atop\text{$m$ even}}^{\infty}(-1)^{\frac{m}{2}}\int_{\boldsymbol{\tau}\in\mathcal{S}_{t}^{m}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{\kappa}\in\{1,\cdots,D\}^{m}}\mathcal{L}_{b}^{\rm same}(\boldsymbol{\tau},\boldsymbol{\kappa})

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(2\pi\epsilon)^{3D/2}}\iint_{\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{q}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}}\int_{\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}}\prod\limits_{l=1}^{n}Q_{d_{l}}(s_{l},\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{q})\prod\limits_{\theta=1}^{m}Q_{\kappa_{\theta}}(\tau_{\theta},\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{q})e^{i\phi(t,\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{q})/\epsilon}\psi_{s}^{(0)}(\boldsymbol{y})d\boldsymbol{y}d\boldsymbol{p}d\boldsymbol{q}d\boldsymbol{\tau}d\boldsymbol{s}.

Next, numerical integration is used to evaluate the integral with respect to
\boldsymbol{p}
,
\boldsymbol{q}
in (

), i.e., consider a set of discrete points and quadrature weights,
\{(\boldsymbol{p}_{k},\boldsymbol{q}_{k},w_{k})\}_{k=1}^{N_{\rm qp}}
.
Let

\psi_{k}(t,\boldsymbol{x})=\frac{1}{(2\pi\epsilon)^{3D/2}}\int_{\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}}e^{i\phi(t,\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{p}_{k},\boldsymbol{q}_{k})/\epsilon}\psi_{s}^{(0)}(\boldsymbol{y})d\boldsymbol{y}.

(57)

Similarly, we have
\boldsymbol{P}_{k}(t)
,
\boldsymbol{Q}_{k}(t)
,

and

subject to (

) with

,

Hence,

\displaystyle I_{n,\boldsymbol{N}}(t,\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{m=0\atop\text{$m$ even}}^{\infty}I_{n,\boldsymbol{N}}^{(m)}(t,\boldsymbol{x})

(58)

\displaystyle\approx\sum\limits_{m=0\atop\text{$m$ even}}^{\infty}\sum\limits_{k=1}^{N_{\rm qp}}w_{k}\psi_{k}(t,\boldsymbol{x})\underbrace{\int_{\boldsymbol{s}\in\mathcal{S}_{t}^{n}}\sum\limits_{(\boldsymbol{j},\boldsymbol{d})\in\mathcal{J}(\boldsymbol{N})}\left(\prod\limits_{l=1}^{n}V_{j_{l}}(s_{l})Q_{k,{d_{l}}}(s_{l})\right)d\boldsymbol{s}}_{=:J_{k,\boldsymbol{N}}(t)}

Remark 3.1
.

We note that it is numerically unaffordable to apply the Inchworm FGA method in
[

]
to two-dimensional cases due to the computational cost of

for

pairs of

To maintain the accuracy of FGA, it is necessary to choose

For example, when

. This requires
\sim 1
TB to store the intermediate variables as single-precision complex numbers.

In contrast, our approach only costs

[

Remark 3.2
.

The space complexity of

is

\binom{rD+n-1}{n}\approx\frac{(rD)^{n}}{n!},

when

is relatively larger than

The FGA ansatz transforms the interaction operator to a scalar, allowing for an exchange of variables in the integral. Based on this, two simplifications are derived in the next subsection, after which the whole process is concluded as an algorithm.

3.3
An efficient algorithm for solving density of the open quantum system

We note that the
n
- and
m
-dimensional integrals in (
58
) limit the number of terms in the Dyson series that can be computed.
In this subsection, we focus on simplifying these integrals to obtain expressions that are independent of the number
n=|\boldsymbol{N}|
in the Dyson series.

First, we reduce the
n
-dimensional integral
J_{k,\boldsymbol{N}}(t)
to a one-dimensional integral.

Proposition 3.1
.

The integral in the first line of (
58
) can be simplified as

\displaystyle J_{k,\boldsymbol{N}}(t)

\displaystyle:=\int_{\boldsymbol{s}\in\mathcal{S}_{t}^{n}}\sum\limits_{(\boldsymbol{j},\boldsymbol{d})\in\mathcal{J}(\boldsymbol{N})}\left(\prod\limits_{l=1}^{n}V_{j_{l}}(s_{l})Q_{k,{d_{l}}}(s_{l})\right)d\boldsymbol{s}=\frac{1}{\boldsymbol{N}!}\prod\limits_{d=1}^{D}\prod\limits_{j=1}^{r}\left(I_{k}^{(j,d)}(t)\right)^{N_{j}^{(d)}},

(59)

where

I_{k}^{(j,d)}(t):=\int_{0}^{t}V_{j}(s)Q_{k,d}(s)ds.

(60)

Proof.

By the definition of
\mathcal{J}(\boldsymbol{N})
in (
43
),
(\boldsymbol{j},\boldsymbol{d})\in\mathcal{J}(\boldsymbol{N})
covers all the permutations for a specific
\boldsymbol{s}
, hence the integral is symmetric with respect to the exchange of variables. We have

\displaystyle J_{k,\boldsymbol{N}}(t)

\displaystyle=\int_{\boldsymbol{s}\in\mathcal{S}_{t}^{n}}\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathscr{Q}_{n}}\sum\limits_{(\boldsymbol{j},\boldsymbol{d})\in\mathcal{J}(\boldsymbol{N})}\left(\prod\limits_{l=1}^{n}V_{j_{\sigma(l)}}(s_{l})Q_{k,{d_{\sigma(l)}}}(s_{l})\right)d\boldsymbol{s}

\displaystyle=\int_{\boldsymbol{s}\in\mathcal{S}_{t}^{n}}\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathscr{Q}_{n}}\sum\limits_{(\boldsymbol{j},\boldsymbol{d})\in\mathcal{J}(\boldsymbol{N})}\left(\prod\limits_{l=1}^{n}V_{j_{l}}(s_{\sigma^{-1}(l)})Q_{k,{d_{l}}}(s_{\sigma^{-1}(l)})\right)d\boldsymbol{s}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n!}\int_{0}^{t}\cdots\int_{0}^{t}\sum\limits_{(\boldsymbol{j},\boldsymbol{d})\in\mathcal{J}(\boldsymbol{N})}\left(\prod\limits_{l=1}^{n}V_{j_{l}}(s_{l})Q_{k,{d_{l}}}(s_{l})\right)ds_{1}\cdots ds_{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n!}\frac{n!}{\boldsymbol{N}!}\prod\limits_{d=1}^{D}\prod\limits_{j=1}^{r}\int_{0}^{t}V_{j}(s)Q_{k,d}(s)ds=\frac{1}{\boldsymbol{N}!}\prod\limits_{d=1}^{D}\prod\limits_{j=1}^{r}\left(I_{k}^{(j,d)}(t)\right)^{N_{j}^{(d)}}.

∎

Remark 3.3
.

Computation of (
60
) only requires discrete values of
V_{j}
when introducing temporal discretization. In fact, using
N_{t}
timesteps of equal size gives

\tilde{B}=[b_{jk}],\qquad b_{jk}=B(k\Delta t,j\Delta t),\qquad\Delta t=\frac{t}{N_{t}},

which is a Hermitian matrix. Therefore,
V_{j}(s)
in the numerical integration is an element of the truncation of the spectral expansion of the discrete two-point correlation function
\tilde{B}
.

Next, we reduce the
m
-dimensional integral
J_{k}^{(m)}(t)
to a two-dimensional integral, using a similar method to
[
25
]
.

Proposition 3.2
.

In (
58
), the integral on the single interval
[0,t]
can be rewritten as

\displaystyle J_{k}^{(m)}(t)

\displaystyle:=(-1)^{\frac{m}{2}}\int_{\boldsymbol{\tau}\in\mathcal{S}_{t}^{m}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{\kappa}\in\{1,\cdots,D\}^{m}}\mathcal{L}_{b}^{\rm same}(\boldsymbol{\tau},\boldsymbol{\kappa})\prod\limits_{\theta=1}^{m}Q_{k,\kappa_{\theta}}(\tau_{\theta})d\boldsymbol{\tau}

(61)

\displaystyle=\frac{(-1)^{\frac{m}{2}}}{(m/2)!}\left(\int_{0\leq\tau_{1}\leq\tau_{2}\leq t}B(\tau_{2},\tau_{1})\left(\sum\limits_{d=1}^{D}Q_{k,d}(\tau_{1})Q_{k,d}(\tau_{2})\right)d\tau_{1}d\tau_{2}\right)^{\frac{m}{2}}=\frac{(J_{k}^{(2)}(t))^{\frac{m}{2}}}{(m/2)!}.

Proof.

Noting the contribution of the delta function in (
40
), one can derive

J_{k}^{(m)}(t)=(-1)^{\frac{m}{2}}\int_{\boldsymbol{\tau}\in\mathcal{S}_{t}^{m}}\sum\limits_{\boldsymbol{q}\in\mathscr{L}_{s}(\boldsymbol{\tau})}\prod\limits_{(t_{1},t_{2})\in\boldsymbol{q}}\Big(\boldsymbol{Q}_{k}(t_{1})\cdot\boldsymbol{Q}_{k}(t_{2})\Big)B(t_{2},t_{1})d\boldsymbol{\tau}.

For even
m\geq 2
, let
\mathcal{P}_{k}(t_{1},t_{2})=(\boldsymbol{Q}_{k}(t_{1})\cdot\boldsymbol{Q}_{k}(t_{2}))B(t_{2},t_{1})
and
\boldsymbol{\tau}_{m}=(\tau_{1},\tau_{2},\cdots,\tau_{m})
, then

\displaystyle(-1)^{\frac{m}{2}}J_{k}^{(m)}(t)\cdot(-1)^{\frac{2}{2}}J_{k}^{(2)}(t)

\displaystyle=\int_{\boldsymbol{\tau}_{m}\in\mathcal{S}_{t}^{m}}\int_{0\leq\tau_{m+1}\leq\tau_{m+2}\leq t}\left(\sum\limits_{\boldsymbol{q}\in\mathscr{L}_{s}(\boldsymbol{\tau}_{m})}\prod\limits_{(t_{1},t_{2})\in\boldsymbol{q}}\mathcal{P}_{k}(t_{1},t_{2})\right)\mathcal{P}_{k}(\tau_{m+1},\tau_{m+2})d\boldsymbol{\tau}_{m+2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\binom{m+2}{2}(m-1)!!}{(m+1)!!}\cdot(-1)^{\frac{m+2}{2}}J_{k}^{(m+2)}(t).

Specifically, adding one additional pair to the diagram consisting of
m/2
pairs produces
\binom{m+2}{2}
new diagrams. Conversely, each diagram of
m/2+1
pairs generates
m/2+1
diagrams when removing one pair, meaning that the factor in the second equality above can be computed by the quotient between diagram numbers.
Therefore

J_{k}^{(m)}(t)=\frac{J_{k}^{(m-2)}(t)J_{k}^{(2)}(t)}{m/2}=\frac{J_{k}^{(m-4)}\left(J_{k}^{(2)}(t)\right)^{2}}{(m/2)(m/2-1)}=\cdots=\frac{\left(J_{k}^{(2)}(t)\right)^{\frac{m}{2}}}{(m/2)!}.

∎

Using (

(62)

\displaystyle I_{n,\boldsymbol{N}}^{(m)}(t,\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{k=1}^{N_{\rm qp}}w_{k}\psi_{k}(t,\boldsymbol{x})J_{k,\boldsymbol{N}}(t)J_{k}^{(m)}(t).

Remark 3.4

The low-rank approximation of the two-point correlation function can be generated by a low-rank approximation of the matrix corresponding to the discrete two-point function. It is actually a truncated spectral expansion of that matrix.

All values of

4
Numerical results

In this section, several numerical experiments are performed to validate the proposed method. Firstly, a one-dimensional double well is demonstrated to validate our algorithm by recovering numerical results in the existing literature. Next, a one-dimensional harmonic oscillator is presented to display the effectiveness of the low-rank approximation, followed by a two-dimensional version in which the anticipated density can be observed. Finally, a two-dimensional double slit is simulated.

The two-point correlation function can be formulated as

B(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})=\tilde{B}(\Delta\tau)=\frac{1}{2}\sum\limits_{l=1}^{L}\frac{c_{l}^{2}}{\epsilon\omega_{l}}\left(\coth\left(\frac{\beta\epsilon\omega_{l}}{2}\right)\cos(\omega_{l}\Delta\tau)-i\sin(\omega_{l}\Delta\tau)\right),

(66)

where
\Delta\tau=\tau_{1}-\tau_{2}
.
For all our simulations, we use the Ohmic spectral density
[
10
,
31
]
, where the frequencies
\omega_{l}
and the coupling intensities
c_{l}
are given by

\displaystyle\omega_{l}

\displaystyle=-\omega_{c}\log\left(1-\frac{l}{L}(1-\exp(-\omega_{\rm max}/\omega_{c}))\right),

(67)

\displaystyle c_{l}

\displaystyle=\epsilon\omega_{l}\sqrt{\frac{\xi\omega_{c}}{L}(1-\exp(-\omega_{\rm max}/\omega_{c}))},

(68)

for
l=1,2,\cdots,L
. Additionally, we choose the parameters to be

L=400,\qquad\omega_{\rm max}=10,\qquad\omega_{c}=2.5,\qquad\beta=5.

(69)

In this paper, a low-rank approximation is numerically attained for the discrete two-point correlation function. An analog focusing on the continuous form may be obtained via the discrete Lehmann representation
[
19
,
21
]
.

5
Conclusion

In this work, we reformulate the Dyson series for open quantum systems by separating the contributions in the reduced density matrix. By applying a low-rank approximation of the two-point correlation function, a complete separation of contributions along each axis is achieved, halving the spatial dimensionality. Furthermore, factorization of diagrams provides an additional reduction in computational cost.

Focusing on the multidimensional Caldeira–Leggett model, the frozen Gaussian approximation is used to lower the temporal dimensionality. These approximations enable the interaction operators to be represented as commutable scalars, thereby reducing high-dimensional integrals to one- and two-dimensional forms. Consequently, the dependence on time discretization is reduced to the same as that of the first non-trivial term in the Dyson series.
Through these techniques, we develop an efficient algorithm whose effectiveness is validated by several numerical experiments, including a two-dimensional double-slit setup.

In future works, we aim to further enhance the efficiency of the method to enable simulations of the three-dimensional Caldeira-Leggett model.
One possible direction is the adoption of a higher-order frozen Gaussian approximation.
Alternatively, we can address the computational bottleneck associated with the discretization of FGA. Direct discretization of the integrals arising from FGA, combined with Monte Carlo methods to evaluate these high-dimensional integrals, may offer a promising solution.
