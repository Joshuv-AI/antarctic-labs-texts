Evaluating chest-worn light logger adherence: development and application of a wear/non-wear model
Authors: Carlyn Patterson Gentile, Adah Thomas, Ryan Shah, Blanca Marquez De Prado, Nichelle Raj, Christina L Szperka, Geoffrey K Aguirre
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLight exposure plays an important role in overall health because it entrains circadian rhythms. Recent technological advances in wearable light loggers allow measurement daily light exposure habits. Using a chest-worn light logger, our goal was to (1) develop methodology for differentiating adherent versus non-adherent use, and (2) define differences in lighting intensity in indoor and outdoor environments, to improve data reliability in future clinical studies using this technology. Four testers used a 10-channel chest worn light logging device under different conditions of wear and non-wear (experiment 1), and another tester made measurements with the light logger across a variety of indoor and outdoor lighting environments (experiment 2). In experiment 1, measurements from the light logger (photopic illuminance, device orientation, accelerometer data, time of day) were used to train models to differentiate wear from non-wear and correct nighttime placement. The most accurate model was then tested with the remaining data from testers (T1 – T4). This model was then applied to 20 adolescents and young adults with migraine who wore the light logger device for one week. In experiment 2, measurements of photopic illuminance and melanopic equivalent daytime illuminance (mEDI) of darker indoor versus brighter outdoor lighting environments were compared to identify the optimal indoor/outdoor distinction point for the chest-worn light logger. Movement, device orientation, light, and time-of-day used as predictors in a random forest model had excellent differentiation between wear, non-wear and nighttime use (overall accuracy 0.95), and retained good-to-excellent differentiation when applied to the test dataset (overall accuracy 0.76). When this model was applied to 20 participants with migraine, we found that 92.1% of participant-days and 77.9% of participant-nights demonstrated at least 80% appropriate use. Applying the wear/non-wear model to this dataset led to modest changes in light exposure measurements but did not change conclusions of our prior study. For differentiating indoor and outdoor lighting environments, the optimal cut-point was 442 lx for photopic illuminance, and 412 lx for mEDI. We demonstrate that internal measurements from a chest-worn light logging device can reliably differentiate wear from non-wear. We also found that the optimal cut-off to differentiate indoor and outdoor lighting environments was lower than 1,000 lx cut-offs traditionally used to define bright light conditions. These findings can be used to improve data reliability in studies of everyday light exposure in clinical populations using chest-worn light loggers.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-54493-1.Keywords: Daily light exposure, Continuous light measurements, Melanopic equivalent daylight illuminance, Photopic illuminance, Data reliabilitySubject terms: Engineering, Health care

Introduction

Wearable light loggers are a rapidly advancing technology that can measure an individuals’ light exposure during everyday life. Daily light exposure has important health implications, as reduced light exposure during the day and increased light exposure at night is linked to sleep disruption and early mortality1–4. Wearable light loggers provide a tool to address this modifiable risk factor to improve health.

Wearable light loggers can isolate melanopic equivalent daylight illuminance (mEDI i.e. “blue light”), giving rise to signals important to circadian photoentrainment5, from photopic illuminance that provides the basis for image-forming vision. This capability has great potential to be used in clinical populations to understand how exposure to different light signals impact health and disease. Additionally, design changes allow light loggers to be worn on the chest (i.e. clipped to the chest or as a pendant around the neck), capturing light closer to the visual plane than a wrist worn watch6,7. We conducted an exploratory study of adolescents and young adults with migraine using chest-worn light loggers that showed participants with temporal 24-hour mEDI profiles shifted to later in the day reported more frequent headache, highlighting the clinical relevance of these measures8.

Given the recency of this technology, factors remain that limit the interpretation of light logging results. First, there is not a standardized approach to confirm when participants are wearing the light logger, constraining data reliability. Indeed, wear-time tracking has been identified as one of the most essential additions to studies evaluating everyday light exposure9. Importantly, studies have begun to address this issue. Periods of low variability in light exposure have been proposed as a means to classify light logger non-wear10. One study demonstrated that non-wear periods can be effectively identified by having the light logger placed in a black bag and measuring periods of non-movement11. However, this method still relies on participants being adherent to how the device should be stored during non-wear. Second, “bright light” environments have been defined as > 1,000 lx (either photopic illuminance or mEDI) to approximate time spent outdoors (1,000 to 100,000 lx) from indoors (generally < 1,000 lx, depending on lighting conditions)12–14, but this distinction has not been directly tested using chest-worn light loggers.

In this pre-registered study, we use the internal sensors within a chest-worn light logger to develop and test a model that identifies adherent use. To evaluate the model further, we apply wear/non-wear to the dataset from our previously published study measuring daily light exposure in adolescents and young adults with migraine8. We hypothesized that the light logger’s orientation sensor, accelerometer, light measurements, and time-of-day would be sufficient to separate wear from non-wear periods. Furthermore, we report on light measurements of indoor and outdoor environments to determine the optimal cut-point for a chest-worn light logger.

Materials and methods

ActLumus measurements

ActLumus devices (Condor Instruments, São Paolo, Brazil) are light loggers that consist of 10-channel sensitive to a range of spectra. These wearable light loggers that provide 24-hour continuous collection of photopic illuminance and melanopic equivalent daytime illuminance (mEDI) with an operating range of 1–100,000 lx. Data were collected at a sampling rate of 1/minute. We have validated the tabular spectral sensitivity functions of the light logger to confirm it was reliably capturing illuminance, which we previously described8.

Study design, experiment 1: differentiating wear from non-wear

Data collection

Four testers (T1 – T4) collected data from light loggers between 10 and 21 days each. As they were authors to the study, and were collecting data under specific testing conditions, Human Ethics and Consent to Participate was not applicable for the testers. Data were collected over two seasons (summer and winter) under the following conditions: wear during active periods, wear during sedentary periods, wear in indoor environments, wear in outdoor environments, nighttime placement with the light logger facing up (adherent), nighttime placement with the light logger facing down (non-adherent), non-wear periods in stationary places with light (e.g. sitting on a counter), non-wear periods in stationary dark places (e.g. inside a drawer), non-wear periods where the device is in a mobile dark place (e.g. in a pocket or bag). Light diary data were collected through REDCap (Research Electronic Data Capture)15,16 hosted by the institution (see Supplemental Materials for the form). The hours of wear, non-wear, and night data collected from each tester (T1 – T4) is shown (Fig. 1a).

Differentiating wear from non-wear

Internal light logger measurements including two device orientations (hanging, typical of wear) and face down (typical of non-adherent nighttime placement) as binary variables, Proportion Integrating Measure (PIM) of the accelerometer capturing movement, and photopic illuminance were used to build the model distinguishing wear, non-wear, and appropriate night-time placement (Fig. 1b). PIM and photopic illuminance were log transformed (log(x + 1)) to provide a more normal distribution. Time-of-day was used to help differentiate non-wear from nighttime use. Time windows of 1 min (sampling rate), and 5, 10, 20, and 30 min were used to determine the optimal epochs to separate wear from non-wear.

Models were developed using publicly available custom software developed in Matlab (Mathworks®). Logistic regression, support vector machine (SVM), and random forest models were compared, and time-of-day, PIM, photopic illuminance, and orientation were subjected to principal component analysis and used within a logistic regression model (Fig. 1b). Receiver operating curves (ROC) were determined and under the curve (AUC), sensitivity, and specificity were calculated. AUC of 0.8–0.9 was considered good discrimination, and AUC of 0.9 and above was considered excellent discrimination. Cross-validation with a leave-one-out approach applied to testers was used to evaluate overfitting. Accuracy was also calculated, with 70–90% defined as good accuracy, and above 90% defined as excellent accuracy. Once the final model was determined, the remaining data from T1–T4 (3–14 days each per tester) were used to test the model.

Model application, participants with migraine

To demonstrate application of the model, data from adolescents and young adults who were part of a single-center prospective observational exploratory study conducted within the pediatric headache program within the Children’s Hospital of Philadelphia (CHOP) neurology department between October 2024 and March 2025 were analyzed. The protocol received approval from the Institutional Review Board at CHOP and was in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Participant consent (≥ 18 years) or participant consent and parental consent (< 18 years) was obtained prior to participation in the study. Methods and results from this study have been previously reported8. In brief, participants were asked about headache characteristics at a virtual visit prior to the start of the recording week, which can be found in greater detail (See8,17. Relevant for this set of experiments, any headache was determined by asking the question, “How many days in last month have you had ANY headache, even if the pain was mild?”, bad headache days were determined by asking the question, “How many days in last month have you had BAD headache?” Participants then continuously wore the light logger for 7 days. The light logger was removed while sleeping to avoid wearing a cord around the neck and to prevent the device from being obscured by bedsheets. Participants were instructed to keep the light logger face up on a bedside table to allow for night recording. They were also instructed to avoid wearing it in the shower or submerging the device in water. Those who participated in an activity that prevented them from wearing the light logger were instructed to keep the device in the same area with the sensor unobscured.

We recalculated light metrics previously described in our exploratory study8, by applying the wear/non-wear model. 24-hr light profiles were visually represented as the mean log-photopic illuminance with 95% confidence intervals calculated by bootstrap analysis. Light intensity was captured as photopic luminous exposure (i.e. the total 24-hour photopic light exposure expressed in kilolux*hr) and minutes per day exposed to bright light (photopic illuminance > 1,000 lx). Light timing was defined by percent time spent within recommended mEDI limits for optimal melatonin activity18. Specifically, we determined the percent time spent at a minimum 250 lx mEDI from 7:00–17:00, percent time at a maximum of 10 lx 20:00–23:00, and 1 lx or less from 0:00–6:00, which were previously defined daytime, pre-bed, and night times in our sample8. Fixed epochs were based on the photoperiod and the school schedule. We estimated shifts in the mean 24-hr light profile by calculating the correlation between a shifted version (+/-200 min) of each participant’s profile to the mean across participants and taking the highest correlation value to determine the size of the shift, as previously described8.

Study design, experiment 2: Differentiating indoor and outdoor lighting environments

Data collection

Co-author and tester (T5) collected detailed measurements for at least 5-minute periods to compare indoor and outdoor lighting conditions. As testers were authors to the study, and were collecting data under specific testing conditions, Human Ethics and Consent to Participate was not applicable. Outdoor recordings were collected after sunrise and before sunset to capture natural daytime light. Indoor recordings included residential, office, retail, and transportation environments. All observations were recorded during July 2025 in the Philadelphia area or Washington DC. The light logger device was worn as a pendant or placed face-up on a surface during observations.

Analysis

Analyses were conducted in R Studio version 4.4.2. LightLogR package19 was used to visualize the data and calculate the geometric mean of photopic illuminance and mEDI across indoor and outdoor observations. Above ground transportation was also shown for reference. Youden’s index and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis were then used to determine the optimal cut-points between indoor and outdoor light levels for both photopic illuminance and mEDI. Observations of above ground transportation were excluded from the analysis as they represented environments in between outdoor and indoor but are reported in the figures. Analysis was conducted using published and publicly available pROC, dplyr, and tidyverse R packages9,20,21. Cut-points were then applied to T1–T4 data from experiment 1 to determine the sensitivity and specificity of identifying indoor from outdoor environments.

Pre-registration

This study was pre-registered22. This section details deviations from the pre-registration document, and rationale for the change. First, while we had initially planned to only test logistic regression, SVM, and logistic regression with PCA models, we added the random forest model given the sub-optimal fitting of the other models, and the nature of the data being conducive to this type of model. This model yielded the best performance, thus was ultimately the model chosen. Second, we had initially planned to use 50% of data from each tester to train and test the wear/non-wear model. However, because different amounts of data were collected by each tester based on availability during the recording periods, we used the same number of days from each participant maintain relatively equal contributions of each tester to the model (7 randomly selected days for each participant). Furthermore, we added cross-validation with a leave-one-out approach to add an additional validation step to the model, addressing the small number of testers used. Finally, we did not include analysis on infrared light being used to predict when the light logger was obscured by a shirt due to a lack of consistent findings.

Results

Experiment 1: Identifying wear versus non-wear time

We used internal accelerometer, light, clock-time, and orientation data from the light loggers to develop a wear/non-wear model. testers in a variety of wear and non-wear conditions with corresponding light diary information. Data collected from four testers (T1 – T4) across 7 days each were used to train the model.

The models classified three conditions: wear during the day, non-wear, and appropriate night placement. Non-wear included incorrect nighttime placement (face down) and non-wear during the day. Cut points were used that optimized differentiation of the three classes. We did not find that smoothing or binning the data into larger epochs (5, 10, 20, and 30 min) improved model performance. Thus, we used the original 1-minute sampling rate for the model input. Of the models tested, a random forest model performed the best (Table 1). The random forest model had excellent mean accuracy across testers (0.95) with good mean leave-one-out cross-validation accuracy (0.80). differentiation between wear (AUC > 0.99, sensitivity = 0.98, specificity > 0.99), non-wear (AUC = 0.99, sensitivity = 0.91, specificity = 0.98), and nighttime periods (AUC = 0.98, sensitivity = 0.98, specificity > 0.99). The remaining data from testers T1 - T4 were used to test the model. The model retained good mean accuracy in the validation dataset (0.76), with excellent differentiation for wear periods (AUC = 0.92, sensitivity = 0.87, specificity = 0.89) and nighttime periods (AUC = 0.94, sensitivity = 0.91, specificity = 0.88) and good differentiation for non-wear (AUC = 0.85, sensitivity = 0.62, specificity = 0.92), when applied to the validation dataset.

We aimed to determine how the wear/non-wear model classified circumstances where the device may be worn but is not accurately collecting illuminance data. Examples include when the device is being worn backwards with the light sensor facing towards the chest, or when a shirt or jacket is obstructing the light. Testers 1–4 captured 633 min where the device was either worn backwards or obstructed by fabric (e.g., seatbelt, jacket, shirt). In these instances, over half (347 min; 54.8%) were classified as non-wear time, with the remaining classified as wear time.

To empirically evaluate the wear/non-wear model in individuals not used in model generation, we applied it to 20 adolescents and young adults with migraine (findings previously published)8. Participants wore a light logger for 1 week, comprising 140 participant-days. We used the predicted wear and non-wear habits to define adherent daytime and nighttime use. Adherence was defined as at least 80% wear/proper use during a defined time window. To avoid throwing out reliable data, non-adherence was only counted if there was 10 min or more of consecutive non-wear time. We separated adherent day use (10:00–20:00) and adherent night use (0:00–06:00), comparing wear and night use, respectively, to periods of non-wear. These time frames were chosen to account for differences between weekdays and weekends8. Using these criteria, 92.1% of days and 77.9% of nights were adherent (Fig. 2a). One participant did not have any days or nights that met the threshold for adherence and was removed from further analysis. This is largely consistent with what they reported during the study – they responded that they did not wear the device at all for 3 of the days, and the model indicated there was only partial wear on the other days. The remaining 19/19 participants had 6 days or more of useable daytime data, and 17/19 had at least 3 days of useable night data. There was a substantial reduction in photopic illuminance on non-adherent compared to adherent days. This indicates these criteria are capturing periods of time when the device was not exposed to light, consistent with non-wear. There was a significant increase in nighttime light exposure captured on adherent compared to non-adherent nights, consistent with the device being face up versus face down, respectively. While this difference was observed throughout the night, it was most dramatic from 20:00–24:00, even though this period was not used to define nighttime adherence. This suggests that the parameters can capture periods of time when the device was placed face down for the night.

We used the adherent data to calculate percent time spent within recommended light exposure levels. Removing days and nights the model determined to be non-wear time did not substantially change the group averages of the intensity or timing of light exposure (Fig. 2b). The largest difference was seen in percent time spent under 10 lx mEDI between 20:00–23:00: 78.1% from the original study vs. 72.9% when only the adherent data was used. This indicates that when the device is not face down it is able to capture light use before bed more effectively. Finally, the association we observed between light shift and headache (Rho = 0.66, p = 0.002 vs. Rho = 0.50, p = 0.029) and bad headache (Rho = 0.60, p = 0.005 vs. Rho = 0.47, p = 0.044) days per month was weaker but remained significant.

Experiment 2: Differentiating indoor from outdoor lighting environments

Sixty-six environments (21 outdoor, 45 indoor) representing a variety of light conditions were captured by Tester 5. One observation with an unusually low lux level was excluded, as the device may have been obscured by clothing or recorded improperly. Outdoor environments ranged from urban and forested areas with clear sky days to completely overcast days with precipitation, and in shaded and exposed areas. Indoor lighting environments included areas with LED, incandescent, and fluorescent lights, artificial light only, natural light only, and a combination, and ranged from dimly to brightly lit. Indoor environments also included smart phones as the main light source. The geometric mean of outdoor photopic illuminance was 10,241 lx [range of 484 − 44,427 lx], and the geometric mean of indoor photopic light was 202 lx [range of 0–1,652 lx] (Fig. 3b). Above ground transportation fell in a similar range to outdoor values. ROC curves were calculated. The cut-point of 442 lx had excellent accuracy for photopic illuminance (AUC = 0.99, sensitivity = 0.89, specificity = 1; Fig. 3c), and as did a cut-point of 412 lx for mEDI (AUC = 0.99, specificity = 1, sensitivity = 0.91). We then applied these cut-points to continuous data logged by Testers 1–4 (Table 2). Sensitivity for indoor environments remained high across the different cut-points, but there were improvements in specificity with the 442 lx (photopic illuminance) and 412 lx (mEDI) cut-points. We performed a sub-analysis to compare cut-points from measurements collected during different seasons and found that they were modestly more sensitive but less specific for indoor environments measured during the winter compared to summer (Table 2).

Discussion

In this study, we developed a model that reliably differentiated periods of wear from non-wear and determined optimal cut-offs for distinguishing indoor from outdoor lighting conditions for a chest-worn light logger. This will support reliable data collection and interpretation for future studies that measure the impacts of light exposure on health in clinical populations.

Accuracy of our wear/non-wear model ranged from 0.76 to 0.95 for the model training and testing, comparable to models used to identify non-wear for actigraphy devices23. Days with at least 80% wear time demonstrated significantly higher photopic illuminance as compared to days with less than 80% wear time. Comparing model-derived adherent to non-adherent nights demonstrated significantly higher photopic illuminance in the late evening, which indicates evening light exposure was underestimated on nights when the device was not placed correctly. These findings suggest that it is prudent to apply the wear/non-wear model.

Our findings are consistent with prior work that has demonstrated the feasibility of classifying non-wear periods in wearable light logger studies. Guidolin and colleagues successfully identified non-wear periods with a near-corneal light logger by having the device placed in a black bag and measuring periods of non-movement11. Others have proposed using periods of low variability in light exposure to identify periods of light logger non-wear, though indicated that this may be optional in high adherence cohorts10. Our model predicted high adherence (over 90% for day and 70% for night use, respectively), which was consistent with device use by the participants. However, data from one participant was removed from analysis based on the model output. Consistent with prior studies11, application of our wear/non-wear model did not substantially change light exposure outcomes. Applying the wear/non-wear model reduced the correlation between delayed daily light exposure and headache frequency, though these relationships remained significant. This effect may be due to the small sample size in this exploratory dataset – where the participant removed from analysis due to non-adherence reported high headache frequency. It is important to note that trying to differentiate wear from non-wear time may lead to underestimating sedentary time when accelerometer data are used24. Our model was more effective at classifying wear time than non-wear time, which may help avoid this underestimation.

To distinguish indoor and outdoor lighting environments, we found that 442 lx photopic illuminance and 412 lx mEDI were optimal cut-points. While these values are in general agreement with prior work, they are lower than the 1,000 lx cut-point which has been traditionally used to differentiate bright outdoor from dimmer indoor environments12–14. This indicates that for a chest-worn light logger device, a lower definition of “bright light” may better differentiate indoor from outdoor spaces. Further study is needed to determine the generalizability of these results, comparing it to different geographic regions and time of year.

Our study has limitations that are important to consider when interpreting the results. First, the primary wear/non-wear model is based on data collected from a relatively small number of testers (4). However, the model still has good-to-excellent discrimination between wear and non-wear periods when applied to the data of the testers individually and performed sensibly with agreement from participant report when applied empirically to 20 adolescents and young adults with migraine. Our model also relied on time-of-day information, thus may not be applicable to those, for instance, who do night-shift work. There may also be scenarios where the model is more likely to over or under-estimate wear time, including prolonged periods commuting or lying down during the day (e.g. napping). In these circumstances, it would be helpful to augment the model with participant-reported activities. Additionally, there may be times where a participant is wearing the light dosimeter, but it is not accurately capturing illuminance data because it is being worn facing the chest or obstructed by a jacket. We found our wear model identified over half of the time that the device was worn facing the chest or obscured by fabric as non-wear, indicating many of these instances are appropriately removed by the wear/non-wear model. However, this still leaves many instances where an obstructed device is classified as wear time, which could contribute to underestimating light exposure. Finally, this study did not explore all features that are important to the success of using wearable light dosimeter data including user acceptability and usability, which other studies have addressed25.

Conclusion

We demonstrate that internal measurements from a chest-worn light logger device can be used reliably to determine wear from non-wear and present a test case where this improved data quality. We also report that cut-point values around 500 lx, compared to 1,000 lx, may modestly improve separation of indoor and outdoor environments when chest-worn light loggers are being used.
