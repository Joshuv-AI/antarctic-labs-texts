Health equity in the wake of disasters and extreme weather: evidence from an umbrella review
Authors: Jim P Stimpson, Amir L Rashed, Jay Pandya, Emily C Baudot, Jill Whitfill, Alexander N Ortega
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT

Introduction

Natural disasters and climate-related extreme weather events have profound and lasting effects on population health, particularly among socially and economically marginalized communities.1 The frequency and severity of such events have increased in recent decades due to climate change, further exacerbating existing health disparities for socially vulnerable populations due to greater disaster exposure, higher rates of adverse health outcomes, and systemic barriers to care and recovery.2-6 Understanding the health impacts of disasters on vulnerable populations is important for developing equitable disaster response strategies, improving public health preparedness, and informing policy interventions.

This review is conceptually grounded in the social determinants of health framework and informed by structural vulnerability theory which emphasize how intersecting social, economic, and environmental conditions shape both exposure to disasters and their subsequent health consequences.7-9 While many disaster studies invoke vulnerability as an individual or group-level risk factor, few explicitly attend to the structural production of vulnerability such as how systems of inequality, discrimination, and exclusion create the conditions that place certain populations at greater risk. By applying this more critical lens, we seek to elucidate how disasters exacerbate pre-existing inequities in health outcomes, access to care, and recovery.

While multiple systematic reviews have examined the health consequences of specific disasters and their effects on vulnerable populations, to our knowledge, a review of reviews, also known as an umbrella review, has not been conducted. Unlike scoping or traditional systematic reviews of primary studies, an umbrella review enables a higher-level synthesis of existing syntheses, facilitating the evaluation of consistency, strength, and quality across diverse findings.10 Therefore, the objective of this umbrella review is to synthesize evidence on physical health, mental health, and health care access/utilization outcomes associated with natural disasters and climate-related extreme weather events among socially and economically marginalized populations. By consolidating findings across multiple systematic reviews and meta-analyses, this study will evaluate the strength of existing evidence, assess methodological quality, and identify gaps requiring further investigation. The findings will contribute to a comprehensive understanding of disaster-related health disparities, with implications for public health preparedness, emergency response, and policy development aimed at reducing inequities in disaster impact and recovery.

Methods

This umbrella review followed the PRISMA guidelines and was registered in PROSPERO (CRD420250651503).11 This study did not involve human participants or the collection of identifiable private information; therefore, it did not require review by an institutional review board or ethics committee.

A health sciences librarian developed and implemented a comprehensive search strategy across Ovid MEDLINE, Ovid MEDLINE EPub Ahead of Print & In-Process, Ovid Embase Classic + Embase, Ovid PsycINFO, Cochrane CENTRAL Trials, Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews, and Scopus on December 5, 2024. The complete search strategies for Ovid MEDLINE and Ovid Embase are provided in the Supplementary material. In addition to backward citation tracking, we conducted a manual search in April 2025 to identify newly published or previously missed reviews to ensure the evidence base remained current and comprehensive.

The eligibility criteria restricted the results to articles published in English within the past 20 years, from 2005 to 2025. Eligible studies included systematic reviews, meta-analyses, or integrative reviews that reported physical health, mental health, or healthcare access and utilization outcomes following exposure to natural disasters or climate-related extreme weather events among populations defined as experiencing health disparities. Although this review was initially guided by the NIMHD definition of health disparity populations, we also drew from international frameworks—including the WHO Commission on Social Determinants of Health and the PROGRESS-Plus equity framework—to ensure applicability across diverse global contexts.12-15

Inclusion criteria

Studies were included if they focused on human populations affected by natural disasters or extreme weather events, with particular attention to socially disadvantaged populations, which included racial and ethnic minorities, individuals with low socioeconomic status, residents of rural communities, sexual and gender minority groups, immigrants, and people with disabilities. Eligible exposures encompassed a range of natural disasters and climate-related extreme weather events, including hurricanes, tornadoes, floods, wildfires, earthquakes, heatwaves, and droughts. We excluded man-made or technological disasters such as nuclear accidents, chemical spills, and industrial explosions, as these events have distinct etiologies, population exposures, and policy frameworks that differ substantially from natural and climate-related events. Comparators included studies that examined predisaster and postdisaster health outcomes, variations in disaster exposure, or disparities in health outcomes between different population groups. Eligible outcomes were categorized into 3 domains: (1) physical health outcomes, including morbidity and mortality indicators, disease symptoms, new diagnoses, or exacerbation of existing chronic conditions (eg, cardiovascular disease, respiratory illness, diabetes); (2) mental health outcomes, including psychological symptoms (eg, distress, anxiety, depression), psychiatric diagnoses (eg, PTSD, major depressive disorder), and behavioral health indicators (eg, suicidality, substance use); and (3) healthcare access and utilization outcomes, such as disruptions in medical care, service use patterns, emergency department visits, and barriers to accessing healthcare services. Reviews were excluded if their primary focus did not include at least one of these eligible outcomes (eg, studies centered exclusively on economic losses, housing displacement, or environmental exposure without associated health impacts). To ensure methodological rigor, scoping reviews, narrative reviews, rapid reviews, primary research studies, commentaries, and preprints were excluded, as they do not employ a method for assessing study quality and synthesizing findings.

Study selection

Titles and abstracts were independently screened by 2 reviewers using Covidence systematic review software. Agreement during the title and abstract screening phase was 83%, reflecting high consistency at this stage. Next, articles underwent full-text review by 2 independent reviewers. A third reviewer resolved disagreements when consensus could not be reached. Common sources of disagreement, such as whether a study explicitly addressed disparity populations, were identified early and discussed among the review team to improve consistency.

Data extraction

One member of the review team extracted data using a standardized template within Covidence, ensuring a structured and systematic approach to capturing key study details. A second team member independently checked the extracted data for accuracy and completeness. Any discrepancies were resolved through discussion and consensus. Extracted information included general study characteristics, methodological details, population characteristics, exposure type, health outcomes, key findings, policy or practice implications, and future research directions. After data extraction was completed in Covidence, extracted data were exported to Excel for further processing, including quality assessment scoring and synthesis of findings.

Quality assessment

The quality assessment process followed the same protocol as data extraction. One reviewer conducted the initial quality appraisal using an adapted version of the JBI Critical Appraisal Checklist for Systematic Reviews and Research Syntheses, and a second reviewer verified the ratings.10 The assessment evaluated the clarity of the review question, appropriateness of inclusion criteria, adequacy of the search strategy, critical appraisal of included studies, methods for data extraction and synthesis, assessment of heterogeneity, evaluation of publication bias, and justification of conclusions. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion and consensus.

Overlap analysis

We conducted a citation overlap analysis to assess redundancy in the evidence base across 33 systematic reviews.16 Primary studies were extracted into an Excel workbook and deduplicated. The analysis identified 1670 unique primary studies and 59 redundant occurrences, for a total of 1729 study entries. Overlap across reviews was minimal, with a Corrected Covered Area (CCA) of 0.11%.

Data synthesis

A narrative synthesis was conducted due to heterogeneity in study designs, populations, and outcomes. Disasters were classified according to the EM-DAT disaster classification system which categorizes natural disasters into geophysical (eg, earthquakes, volcanic activity, tsunamis), meteorological/hydrological (eg, storms, floods, hurricanes, typhoons), and climatological (eg, droughts, wildfires, heatwaves) groups based on the primary physical process driving the event.17 This system remains the most widely used in disaster epidemiology and global health research, ensuring comparability with prior reviews. Reviews synthesizing evidence across unspecified or multiple hazard types were designated all-hazard (generalized), while those examining sequential or interacting events were considered compound or cascading. No included reviews met criteria for the latter. Geographic focus was coded according to each review's stated study scope. Key thematic patterns in health outcomes and population disparities were identified within and across disaster categories. Policy and research implications were synthesized across reviews to identify recurring themes. Greater weight was given to findings from methodologically rigorous reviews. The online Appendix provides more methodological details about the search strategy, extracted data, quality analysis, and overlap analysis.

Results

Study selection

We identified 2127 records through database searches. After removing duplicates, 1596 unique records were screened, and 105 underwent full-text review. Common reasons for exclusion included study design, population, or outcome misalignment. The final synthesis included 33 studies that met the inclusion criteria.18-50 The PRISMA flowchart summarizing the study selection process is provided in Figure 1.

Study characteristics

Among the 33 included reviews, publications increased after 2020, peaking in 2023. As shown in Table 1, reviews were categorized according to the EM-DAT disaster classification: geophysical (n = 12), hydrological/meteorological (n = 8), climatological (n = 12), and all-hazard (generalized) (n = 6). No reviews met criteria for compound or cascading events. Ten reviews were global in scope, 12 focused on Asia and the Pacific, 4 on the Americas, 3 on Europe, 2 on Africa, and 2 were cross-regional. Reviews labeled “global” frequently included studies from the United States and other high-income settings, even when not restricted to them. Only 1 review focused exclusively on the U.S., and few stratified results by income level, underscoring a persistent gap in the geographic balance of disaster evidence.

Quality of studies

As shown in Table 2, the 33 included reviews varied in methodological rigor: 15 were rated high quality, 15 moderate, and 3 low. Reviews were assessed for factors such as review question clarity, inclusion criteria, search strategy, critical appraisal methods, and reporting transparency. A detailed description of the appraisal criteria and findings is provided in online Supplementary material (Appendix 2). High-quality reviews consistently demonstrated methodological rigor and adherence to best practices. Moderate- and low-quality reviews often lacked key elements such as comprehensive search strategies or robust bias assessment. Additionally, only 7 reviews (21%) reported the funding sources of the studies they included, limiting insight into potential funding bias.

Geophysical disasters (earthquakes, volcanic eruptions, tsunamis)

Systematic reviews of geophysical disasters examined the physical and mental health consequences of earthquakes, tsunamis, and volcanic eruptions.18-25,30,32,34,48 Pooled PTSD prevalence among survivors ranged from 15% to 28%, with pooled depression at 32% and anxiety at 20%.20,21,32 Women were 41% more likely than men to experience severe PTSD.20 Across reviews, psychological distress typically peaked within the first 3 months but persisted beyond 6 months in displaced and low-income populations.

Rehabilitation and continuity-of-care disruptions were common, particularly where health infrastructure sustained damage. Studies highlighted shortages of providers, infection control lapses, and medication access challenges in temporary facilities. Consistent with several Asian and Caribbean reviews, community-based interventions such as culturally tailored mental health training for local providers were associated with improved screening and referral. Evidence also noted elevated maternal distress and lifestyle changes (eg, weight gain, reduced activity) during long-term recovery, reflecting broader social and behavioral post disaster impacts.18,19,22-25,30,34,48

Hydrological and meteorological disasters (floods, hurricanes, typhoons, storms)

Meta-analyses demonstrated that flood exposure significantly increased diarrhea incidence and psychological morbidity. Floods were associated with a 40% higher risk of diarrheal disease (RR = 1.40) and nearly a 2-fold rise in bacterial and parasitic infections (RR = 1.82, 95% CI 1.61-2.03). Women exhibited higher susceptibility (RR = 1.55) than men (RR = 1.35).33 Standardized mean differences for psychological distress were modest but significant following floods (SMD = 0.07, 95% CI, 0.02-0.12) and storms/cyclone (SMD = 0.18, 95% CI, 0.03-0.32.32

Systematic reviews without meta-analysis reported consistent patterns of postdisaster distress, sleep disturbance, and post-traumatic symptoms, especially among women, children, rural residents, and Black and Latine populations in U.S. studies.19,38,43,44,47,48 Barriers to healthcare were amplified by evacuation, prolonged displacement, and the loss of transportation or communication networks. Several reviews identified sustained primary care and chronic disease management disruptions for months following hurricanes and floods, with mortality spikes in nursing home and geriatric populations. Collectively, these studies illustrate that infrastructure robustness and local capacity determine the persistence and magnitude of health impacts.

Climatological disasters (heatwaves, droughts, wildfires)

Meta-analyses quantified consistent heat-related health risks. Each 1°C rise in temperature increased mental-health mortality by 2% and morbidity by 1%; heatwaves increased mental-health morbidity by 6%.29 Heat exposure was significantly associated with diabetes mortality (RR = 1.14) and morbidity (RR = 1.01), with stronger effects among adults aged ≥ 60 years (RR = 1.04).40 In a pooled analysis of 61 studies, older adults (>65 years) and those of low socioeconomic status had elevated heat-related mortality (RRR = 1.02-1.04 and 1.03, respectively).49 One meta-analysis estimated the pooled burden of extreme weather events on PTSD and depression to be 29%.31

Systematic reviews documented compounding burdens from extreme heat, droughts, and wildfires, particularly among older adults, low-SES groups, Indigenous communities, and outdoor workers.26,28,35-37,42,45,46 Wildfire smoke exposure was linked to acute respiratory illness, exacerbations of asthma and COPD, and increased anxiety and PTSD symptoms. Droughts were tied to economic hardship, food insecurity, and suicide risk in agricultural populations. Structural conditions such as housing quality, labor protections, access to cooling, and transportation shaped vulnerability and recovery, reinforcing existing socioeconomic and spatial inequities in health outcomes.

All-hazard (generalized)

There were no meta-analyses for multiple or unspecified hazard types. Despite heterogeneity in settings, findings from systematic reviews converged on identifying elevated mental health symptoms and metabolic risk following disasters.27,32,39,41,48,50 Reported mechanisms included chronic stress, sleep disruption, displacement, and constrained access to mental health and social services. Reviews commonly highlighted gaps in continuity of care, preparedness planning, and population-specific outreach for groups facing linguistic, economic, or disability-related barriers. While few intervention models were evaluated, the literature emphasized the need for scalable, culturally responsive strategies adaptable across hazard contexts.

Policy and research implications

Thirty of the 33 included reviews proposed at least one policy or practice recommendation, and 29 identified specific priorities for future research (Table 3). Across disaster types, the most common policy implications emphasized strengthening equity-centered preparedness, expanding access to mental health and primary care services in disaster prone regions, and integrating social vulnerability and resilience indicators into emergency planning. Reviews also called for improved communication strategies for linguistically diverse and rural populations, greater investment in public health infrastructure, and coordination between health, housing, and labor agencies during recovery. Future studies should employ longitudinal designs, standardized outcome measures, intersectional frameworks, and quasi-experimental methods with appropriate control groups to better quantify how socioeconomic disadvantage, geography, and demographic factors jointly shape disaster-related health inequities.

Discussion

This umbrella review brought together evidence from 33 systematic reviews and meta-analyses spanning disasters and extreme weather events to assess how health outcomes and healthcare access disruptions unfold among socially marginalized populations. Across disaster types, women, older adults, people with disabilities, rural residents, low-income communities, and racial and ethnic minoritized groups consistently shouldered disproportionate burdens of mental and physical morbidity. These patterns reflect more than hazard exposure: they expose structural inequities in health, infrastructure, social protection, and readiness. In effect, disasters act less as independent shocks and more as stress tests for systems already shaped by disadvantage.1-3

Interpretation of mental health consequences is constrained by outcome heterogeneity; many studies rely on screening tools rather than clinical diagnosis, and longitudinal follow-up remains rare. Symptom spikes appear within the first month and may remain elevated after 6 months among displaced or resource-limited populations. Workforce shortages, housing instability, and transportation or broadband disruption, especially in designated health professional shortage areas, amplify trauma and hinder recovery beyond the direct effects of facility damage. In multiple reviews, “community resilience” was achieved through concrete interventions (lay counselors, mobile outreach, culturally tailored peer-support programs) rather than abstract narratives of “social cohesion.” Future research must align outcomes across assessment type and timing and evaluate these models consistently across hazards.

The literature reveals a gap in intersectional and structural inquiry: few studies explicitly examine how poverty, disability, linguistic isolation, immigration status, and geographic remoteness overlap to generate compounding disaster risk.2 Still, themes converge on upstream determinants such as housing security, labor protections, and broadband/transportation access. These contexts shape exposure, coping, and long-term outcomes, making social policy an integral part of disaster health planning.

The structural fragility of predisaster systems emerged repeatedly: damaged hospitals, disrupted rehabilitation services, and insufficient surge capacity consistently slowed recovery and drove persistence of care deficits. At the same time, promising intervention models emerged pointing to scalable equity-oriented reforms such as peer-led outreach, integration of mental health into primary care, mobile clinics in rural/displaced communities. Telehealth expands access but remains constrained without reliable broadband and digital literacy support.18-25,32,34

Current policy frameworks offer operational pathways to integrate equity into disaster policy. The Sendai Framework for Disaster Risk Reduction situates health at the center of disaster risk reduction and emphasizes investment in risk governance, vulnerability reduction, preparedness, response, recovery, and “build-back-better” reconstruction.51 The Health Emergency and Disaster Risk Management (Health-EDRM) Framework developed by the World Health Organization extends this with 10 core components including risk assessment, coordination, health information systems, and monitoring supported by equity and community participation as cross-cutting principles. The Health-EDRM framework emphasizes linking public health and emergency management systems to address pre-existing social vulnerabilities, particularly those arising from poverty, disability, and displacement.52 Complementing these, the OECD Health System Resilience Framework underscores that resilient systems depend on sustained investment in prevention, workforce capacity, and data infrastructure. It recommends targeted annual investment equivalent to 1.4% of GDP across OECD countries, emphasizing governance, trust, and population health as the foundations for resilience.53 Embedding health equity metrics into disaster preparedness scorecards and recovery plans, as these frameworks recommend, converts equity from aspiration into measurable performance.3,13,14,44

To make policy more concrete: in the preparedness phase, disaster management plans must integrate social vulnerability indices (eg, income, disability, rurality, language access) and equity metrics (eg, healthcare access gaps, ubiquity of broadband) into hazard profiles. In the response phase, targets should include, for example, first mental health contact within 7 days, continuity of chronic disease treatment within 14 days, and accessible evacuation housing within 48 hours disaggregated by disability, language, and socioeconomic status. In the recovery phase, rebuilding must integrate housing security programs, transportation connectivity, income replacement schemes, and workforce training, thereby treating housing, labor, and health as interlinked systems of resilience. The policy roadmap emerging from our review aligns with these actionable levers and provides measurable endpoints (eg, percentage of displaced households with income continuity, time to primary care access after event, proportion of rural residents receiving outreach within 7 days).

The geographic imbalance of the evidence base is a limitation: most reviews focus on Asia and the Pacific, and only one review centered exclusively on the U.S. Few stratified results by national income group or region, limiting external validity in low- and middle-income country (LMIC) contexts. Universal health system coverage, social protection infrastructure, and differing disaster governance regimes likely moderate equity outcomes. Methodologically, disparities in measurement (screening vs diagnosis), limited follow-up beyond twelve months, and few evaluations of policy or program effectiveness remain major gaps for translational research.

Looking ahead, research must adopt longitudinal designs, mixed methods, and intersectional stratification (by income, disability, language, rurality). It should link administrative health data with environmental exposures and social policy indicators to assess how protective policy actions such as income replacement, housing security, and eviction moratoria mediate health impacts of disasters. Implementation science frameworks such as RE-AIM (Reach, Effectiveness, Adoption, Implementation, Maintenance) and the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR) should be used to evaluate interventions like mobile mental health units, culturally tailored outreach, and cross-sector disaster recovery coalitions, tracking equity sensitive outcomes such as reach among underserved groups, adoption by local agencies, and sustainability of service delivery.54-56

In sum, disasters magnify social inequities, but they also offer an opportunity. This umbrella review demonstrates that equitable preparedness, sustained access to care, and inclusive recovery planning are not aspirational ideals but measurable determinants of population resilience. Translating these principles into practice requires embedding equity indicators in national preparedness plans, investing in community-based health infrastructure, and aligning recovery funding with social protection and workforce strengthening policies. Together, these steps place a clear imperative before policymakers, funders, and health systems: invest in equity before disasters strike or bear the compounded costs of inequity afterward.
