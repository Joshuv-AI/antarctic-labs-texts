Genomic epidemiology of antimicrobial resistance in Proteus mirabilis: core genome and plasmid-mediated drivers
Authors: Peng Zhang, Zhikang Cheng, Yang Cao, Shuangqing Liu, Meiqi Zhao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractProteus mirabilis has emerged as a clinically significant multidrug-resistant (MDR) pathogen, yet the genomic drivers and dissemination mechanisms of its antimicrobial resistance (AMR) remain poorly characterized. To address this gap, we conducted a pangenome analysis of 2,013 P. mirabilis genomes, including 1,990 publicly available strains and 23 newly sequenced clinical isolates, to delineate the species-wide AMR landscape. Our study identified 197 AMR gene subtypes spanning 12 antibiotic classes, with seven resistance determinants embedded in the core genome. Clinically critical resistance phenotypes—notably to third-generation cephalosporins and carbapenems—were strongly associated with the proliferation of β-lactamase genes (blaTEM-1, blaCTX-M-15, blaNDM-1) across lineages. Strikingly, 36.4% of AMR genes resided on mobile genetic elements, with conjugative IncC plasmids acting as primary vectors for high-risk resistance cassettes, including β-lactamase (blaTEM-1, blaNDM-1) and aminoglycoside-modifying [aac(6’)-Ib-cr, aph(3’’)-Ib] genes. Phylogenetic reconstruction revealed that blaNDM-1-carrying IncC plasmids formed interspecies clusters with homologs from Klebsiella pneumoniae, Escherichia coli, and Salmonella enterica, demonstrating active cross-genera transmission within Enterobacteriaceae. These findings highlight two parallel evolutionary strategies in P. mirabilis: the conservation of core genome resistance mechanisms and horizontal acquisition of plasmid-borne MDR traits, which provide comprehensive understanding of AMR transmission in the bacterium.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12866-025-04651-8.Keywords: Antimicrobial resistance, Plasmid, Proteus mirabilis, AMR transmission

Introduction

Proteus mirabilis (P. mirabilis), a Gram-negative bacterium within the Enterobacteriaceae family, is characterized by its distinctive flagellar motility and urease activity [1]. This microorganism is widely distributed across all major continents (except for antarctica) and has been isolated from diverse environments–including soil, freshwater, wastewater, livestock, wildlife, and human clinical settings–reflecting its broad global prevalence [2]. Although typically a commensal organism within normal flora, P. mirabilis can become pathogenic, causing a range of infections in humans, including urinary tract infections (UTIs) and wound infections [3]. It has also been reported as a causative agent of infections in companion animals and livestock [4]. Due to the increasing antimicrobial resistance (AMR), multi-drug resistant (MDR) strains of P. mirabilis are emerging as a significant global health threat to both humans and animals [5, 6]. Despite this growing concern, there remains a critical gap in the comprehensive understanding of AMR profiles in P. mirabilis.

The prevalence of AMR in P. mirabilis holds significant clinical implications, as it informs treatment strategies [7]. P. mirabilis exhibits inherent resistance to various antimicrobial agents such as polymyxin, nitrofurantoin, and tetracycline, with the precise resistance mechanisms remaining unclear [8]. Additionally, prior studies have indicated that the prevalence of genes encoding AmpC β-lactamases (blaAmpC) and extended-spectrum β-lactamases (ESBLs) like blaCTX−M, blaSHV, and blaTEM in P. mirabilis leads to increasing resistance to cephalosporins and penicillin antibiotics [9, 10]. These AMR genes including blaKPC, blaNDM, blaVIM, and blaOXA−48 in P. mirabilis, are particularly alarming due to their ability to confer resistance to vital antibiotics like meropenem and imipenem [11]. Significantly, a growing body of evidence demonstrates that clinically important carbapenemase resistance genes, such as blaKPC and blaNDM, are commonly present in P. mirabilis isolates. ESBL genes, including blaCTX, are also frequently detected. These genes have been found in isolates from diverse environments, including livestock farms, retail meat, aquatic products, and wildlife [12–14]. This emphasizes the broad distribution of MDR P. mirabilis in natural ecosystems, while the transfer potential of AMR between various ecological niches and healthcare settings is still unknown.

Mobile genetic elements (MGEs), particularly plasmids and bacteriophages (phages), serve as critical vectors for bacterial adaptation and AMR dissemination [15]. Plasmids drive the rapid inter- and intraspecies spread of resistance genes through conjugation, enabling MDR phenotypes across Enterobacteriaceae [16]. Emerging evidence also implicates phages in AMR gene transmission via transduction [17], though their role remains understudied compared to plasmid-mediated transfer. In P. mirabilis, the relative contributions of these two mechanisms remain unresolved. While plasmids are established carriers of high-risk resistance determinants in P. mirabilis [18], phage-associated ARGs have yet to be systematically characterized, despite the abundance of prophages within its genome [19]. This knowledge gap obscures the evolutionary dynamics of resistance acquisition in P. mirabilis and limits the development of strategies targeting MGE-mediated transmission. A dual focus on plasmid and phage mobilization networks is essential to disentangle their synergistic or competitive roles in AMR spread, particularly in hospital environments where P. mirabilis thrives.

In our study, the aim was to investigate the genomic basis of AMR in P. mirabilis. We performed a comprehensive pangenomic and phylogenomic analysis of 2,013 genomes, including 1,990 publicly available sequences and 23 newly sequenced clinical isolates, to characterize the distribution of AMR genes, assess the role of plasmids in AMR dissemination, and identify key resistance determinants relevant for surveillance and infection control.

Methods

Preparation of clinical isolates

Clinical isolates of P. mirabilis were acquired from patient samples that were processed in accordance with standard clinical protocols for the specific specimen type at The Second Hospital of Tianjin Medical University. From January 2019 to December 2023, we collected 1,904 P. mirabilis isolates, which were identified and documented at the species level in the electronic medical record as part of routine clinical care procedures. The specimens primarily consisted of midstream urine, sputum, blood, and rectal discharge, all of which were cultured on MacConkey medium. The samples were all incubated at 37 °C for further identification, as these strains exhibited characteristics suggestive of Proteus spp., such as the microbe’s distinct odor or swarming motility on the culture medium. Identification of the recovered organisms was carried out through MALDI-ToF MS (Bruker Biotyper), following which each organism was allocated a study number. The antibiotic resistance in the clinical isolates was determined using the disk diffusion (Kirby–Bauer) method, and the results were interpreted according to the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) guidelines. They were then frozen with glycerol and maintained at − 80 °C. A total of 23 strains were randomly chosen from the collected isolates for subsequent whole-genome sequencing. These isolates were obtained from urine (11/23), followed by perianal discharge (5/23), blood (3/23), sputum (2/23), and wound (2/23).

DNA sequencing and genome assembly

Bacterial genomic DNA was prepared using a bacterial genomic DNA extraction kit (Solarbio, China) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. The extracted DNA was sequenced with the PE150 strategy (Illumina HiSeq4000) at BIOMARKER TECHNOLOGIES (Beijing, China), yielding about 1 Gb of data (paired ends) per sample. The raw data were then subjected to adapter removal and quality control using Trimmomatic (version 0.39) [20]. High-quality genome (including plasmids) of P. mirabilis isolates were assembled using Unicycler with default parameters (unicycler − 1 *_1.fq.gz -2 *_2.fq.gz -o -t 48) [21]. Genome quality (completeness and contamination) was assessed using checkM2 (Table S1) and taxonomy was verified using GTDB-Tk (version 2.4.0) [22] and Kraken2 [23].

Genome collection and quality control

The study utilized publicly available whole-genome sequences for P. mirabilis obtained from the NCBI GenBank database (last accessed on May 30, 2024). The metadata available in the GenBank database are provided in Supplementary Table S2. In total, 2224 genomes were downloaded from the GenBank database with keyword ‘Proteus mirabilis’. To rule out the possible mis-assigned taxonomic identification, the average nucleotide identity (ANI) values of all genomes were calculated against the P. mirabilis reference strain HI4320 (GenBank no. GCA_000069965.1) using FastANI (version 1.3.4) [24]. Genomes with ANI values ≥ 0.95 (2198) were initially classified as the same species [25]. These identified P. mirabilis genomes were filtered with L90 ≤ 100 (the minimum number of contigs necessary to get at least 90% of the whole genome). After quality control, a total of 1990 genomes were included in the subsequent analyses. Strains isolated from clinical specimens, such as urine, perianal discharge, blood, sputum, and wound samples, were defined as isolates of clinical source; strains obtained from livestock farms, retail meat and aquatic products, wildlife and companion animals, waste water, and other environmental samples were designated as isolates of environmental source. For the purpose of analyzing the spatial distribution of AMR genes in the P. mirabilis population, all the genomes were assigned into four distinct geographical regions: Asia (382), Europe (218), North America (1370), and other regions (43). The world map was created using the maps package (version 3.4.2) in R and sourced from the Natural Earth project at https://www.naturalearthdata.com/.

Pan– and core genome analysis

Pangenome analysis of all P. mirabilis genomes (2,013) were performed using Panaroo (version 1.5.0) [26]. In brief, the genomes were initially annotated by Prokka (version 1.14.5) with default settings [27]. The annotation files (GFF format) were then subjected to Panaroo using a core threshold value of 99% under strict mode. The core genome alignment was converted into a newick tree using IQ-TREE (version 2.3.6) [28]. Single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) were extracted from the core genome alignment file with SNP-sites (version 2.5.1) [29], and phylogenic tree based on the SNPs details were built with IQ-TREE. These resulting newick files were visualized as an unrooted phylogenetic tree using the iTOL website [30].

The identification of plasmids and phages in assembled genomes

Plasmid sequences were identified in P. mirabilis genomes using the tools of Plasmer [31] and geNomad (version 1.8.0) [32]. Plasmer was executed with default, and the parameter ‘-m 2500’ was applied to filter out short plasmid hits. And geNomad was also executed using default settings with ‘end-to-end’ option, the predicted sequences of plasmids or phages short than 2500 was discarded. Plasmid hits with a score greater than 0.8 from either Plasmer or geNomad were selected for further analysis. To identify additional phages within P. mirabilis genomes, VirSorter2 (version 2.2.4) was employed with default settings, and the “-min-length” parameter was set to 2500 to eliminate short prophage hits [33]. Phage hits meeting the score threshold of > 0.8 from VirSorter2 and geNomad were retained for subsequent analysis.

Analysis of AMR genes

The AMR genes in plasmids, phages, and genomes were predicted by the Resistance Gene Identifier (rgi main --input_sequence --local --clean --include_loose --num_threads 80) software with the Comprehensive Antibiotic Resistance Database (CARD) [34]. These AMR genes identified in multiple genomes (> 1) were chosen for further analysis. The abundance of AMR genes was defined as the average copy number (ACN) of AMR genes per strain. Specifically, “core resistance genes” refer to AMR genes that were shared by 99% of the P. mirabilis strains. In this study, we defined multidrug-resistant (MDR) P. mirabilis clones as isolates harboring three or more classes of AMR gene types in addition to core resistance genes. The AMR gene types of carbapenem, cephalosporin, cephamycin, monobactam, oxacephem, penam were categorized as the type of β-lactam. Non-metric multidimensional scaling (NMDS) was performed using the Bray-Curtis dissimilarity matrix of AMR genes to assess the spatial distribution and isolation source of AMR genes within P. mirabilis populations, followed by an analysis of similarities (ANOSIM) to examine differences between groups.

The analysis of types, mobility, and phylogenic relation for plasmids

The IncC plasmids were extracted from the PLSDB database by filtering for the parameter ‘IncC’ in the ‘Replicon Type’ column, and complete plasmids originating from P. mirabilis were selected using the term ‘Proteus mirabilis’ in the ‘NUCCORE Description’ column. Plasmid types and predicted mobility were analyzed using MOB-suite (v3.1.9) with default parameters ‘--infile’ [35], and the output files were subsequently parsed and merged using a Python command. The core genome of plasmids was derived through Panaroo, followed by alignment of core-genome segments using MAFFT (v7.526) [36]. Plasmid CP098445.1 (blaNDM−1-carrying IncC plasmid identified in P. mirabilis) were chosen to conduct phylogenomic inference with 35 phylogenetically related plasmids using IQ-TREE (v 2.3.4) with the parameters ‘-m MFP -bb 1000 -nt AUTO’ [37]. And phylogenetic trees were visualized with iTOL (version 6.9.1).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses and data visualization were conducted using Microsoft Excel (version 2402), R (version 4.4.0), and RStudio (version 2024.04.1). Microsoft Excel was utilized for calculating averages and standard deviations. NMDS and ANOSIM were performed using the vegan (version 2.6–6.1) in R. Graphs were generated using the ggplot2 (version 3.5.1) in R.

Results

The current antimicrobial resistance status of P. mirabilis

To assess the AMR profile of P. mirabilis in clinical settings, 1,904 strains were isolated from a range of clinical specimens-including urine, sputum, stool, rectal discharge, bronchoalveolar lavage fluid, wound exudates, prostatic fluid, and blood-collected between 2019 and 2023. The analysis revealed high resistance rates to trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (62.54%) and ciprofloxacin (56.97%), followed by levofloxacin (41.14%) and gentamicin (38.04%) (Fig. 1a). Notably, resistance to β-lactam antibiotics exhibited an increasing trend over time, with rates rising from 37.36% to 56.59% for cefuroxime, 31.61% to 38.98% for ceftriaxone, 10.34% to 24.52% for cefepime, and 16.89% to 29.88% for imipenem (Fig. 1b). In contrast, resistance to meropenem, ceftazidime, and cefoxitin remained below 10%. These findings highlight the extensive AMR of P. mirabilis in clinical isolates and underscore the growing threat posed by resistance to β-lactam antibiotics.

Geographical distribution of P. mirabilis populations

In the present study, we selected 2,013 P. mirabilis genomes for subsequent analysis, representing isolates from 31 countries or regions (Fig. 2a). The P. mirabilis genomes included in this study were globally distributed, with the largest proportions originating from North America and Asia, followed by Europe and other regions. A detailed breakdown of isolate numbers by country is provided in Table S2. Notably, most of these genomes (1,944 out of 2,013) were isolated and sequenced after 2020 (Figure S1), providing a current snapshot of the P. mirabilis population and its global distribution.

The population structure of P. mirabilis. a The geographical distribution of all P. mirabilis genomes included in this study, comprising both publicly available genomes from NCBI GenBank and the 23 newly sequenced clinical isolates. b Phylogenetic trees from the core genome showed the relationships among P. mirabilis, and the major subspecies–subspecies 1 (1966) was shown in grey elliptic region. c Phylogenetic trees from the core genome showed the relationships among P. mirabilis, the clades of subspecies 2 and subspecies 3 were collapsed, and our isolates were shown in red dots, the genomes were annotated with geographical regions and isolation resources. d Core genome SNP phylogenetic tree annotated with geographical distributions and isolate sources, the clades of subspecies 2 and subspecies 3 were collapsed, and our isolates were shown in red dots

In order to precisely define the global population structure of P. mirabilis, we perform phylogenetic analysis on the core genome. The maximum likelihood phylogeny inferred from these genomes was robust, with a median bootstrap support 100%, delineating three distinct, deeply branching lineages (Fig. 2b). The first lineage encompassed the majority of strains (1,966). Strains from Asia, Europe, and North America, were uniformly distributed across the branches of the phylogenetic tree (Fig. 2c & Figure S2a). The strains identified in this study appeared across all primary branches, highlighting the worldwide dissemination pattern of P. mirabilis populations (Fig. 2c). This observation was further supported by the phylogenetic analyses based on core genome SNPs (Fig. 2d & Figure S2b). Additionally, isolates from both environmental and clinical samples were dispersed across all lineages of the phylogenetic tree (Fig. 2c, d), indicating their broad representation and suitability for subsequent analysis concerning spatial distribution or isolation sources.

The high diversity of AMR genes in P. mirabilis

Genomic analysis of 2,013 P. mirabilis isolates revealed a diverse AMR gene repertoire, with 197 AMR genes spanning 12 categories, including aminoglycosides, β-lactams, diaminopyrimidines, fluoroquinolones, glycopeptides, macrolide-lincosamide-streptogramin (MLS), phenicols, phosphonic acids, rifamycins, sulfonamides, tetracyclines, and others (Table S3). Aminoglycoside resistance genes were the most abundant (average copy number [ACN] = 3.40 ± 0.34 per genome), followed by β-lactamases (2.84 ± 0.26 ACN), MLS (1.76 ± 0.31 ACN), phenicols (1.59 ± 0.35 ACN), fluoroquinolones (1.45 ± 0.35 ACN), sulfonamides (1.24 ± 0.77 ACN), and tetracyclines (1.14 ± 0.15 ACN). Efflux pump genes (rsmA, qacJ, adeF) were ubiquitous (detected in > 99.9% of genomes), alongside the global regulatory gene CRP (99.95%), which modulates multidrug efflux (Fig. 3). Key resistance determinants included penicillin-binding protein 3 (PBP3; β-lactam resistance, 99.90%), vanG (vancomycin resistance, 99.90%), tet(J) (tetracycline resistance, 96.52%), and catA4 (phenicol resistance, 88.47%) (Table S4). Among aminoglycoside resistance genes, sat-2 (68.36%) that encodes a streptothricin acetyltransferase conferring resistance to streptothricin-class aminoglycosides and aadA (46.80%) dominated, while sul1 (60.36%) and sul2 (56.98%) prevailed in sulfonamide resistance. Trimethoprim resistance was primarily mediated by dfrA1 (43.42%), and β-lactam resistance by blaTEM−1 (43.02%).

The frequencies of AMR genes detected in the P. mirabilis population. Only these AMR genes with a frequency exceeding 1% (the proportion of genomes in which each AMR gene was detected) were displayed, and AMR genes were annotated with antibiotic types

Notably, a striking diversity of β-lactamase genes was observed, with 69 different AMR genes encompassing ESBL (e.g. blaCTX−M−2, blaCTX−M−14, blaCTX−M−15, blaCTX−M−65, blaSHV−12, blaTEM−1, blaTEM−2) and carbapenemase genes (e.g. blaIMP−27, blaKPC−2, blaKPC−3, blaNDM−1, blaNDM−5, blaNDM−7, blaOXA−1, blaOXA−10, blaOXA−23, blaOXA−48, blaVIM−1, blaVIM−4) (Table S4 & Fig. 3). blaTEM−1 emerged as the predominant β-lactamase, while blaOXA−1 (27.87%) was the most frequent class D β-lactamase, followed by blaCTX−M−15 (13.41%) and blaNDM−1 (10.28%).

Clinical isolates of P. mirabilis harbored a broader diversity of AMR genes

The NMDS analysis demonstrated a significant difference in AMR gene profiles between clinical and environmental P. mirabilis isolates (R = 1, p = 0.001; Figure S3). Environmental strains exhibited higher ACN of AMR genes per genome, particularly for aminoglycosides (5.27 ± 0.30 vs. 3.03 ± 0.25 in clinical isolates), β-lactamases (3.48 ± 0.38 vs. 2.72 ± 0.30), MLS resistance genes (2.66 ± 0.49 vs. 1.38 ± 0.40), fluoroquinolones (2.14 ± 0.16 vs. 1.31 ± 0.14), and sulfonamides (2.34 ± 0.27 vs. 1.02 ± 0.20) (Fig. 4a). Conversely, clinical isolates harbored a broader diversity of AMR determinants, with only six genes absent from clinical strains, whereas 88 genes present in clinical isolates were not detected in environmental counterparts. Clinically restricted resistance included high-risk carbapenemases (e.g., blaIMP−27, blaKPC−2, blaOXA−23, blaOXA−48, blaNDM−7, blaVIM−1) and ESBL genes (e.g., blaCTX−M−2, blaTEM−2, blaTEM−135), which were absent in environmental strains (Fig. 4b & Table S5). In summary, clinical P. mirabilis isolates demonstrate a broader spectrum of AMR gene diversity, while environmental isolates present a higher ACN of AMR genes per isolate. These findings outline distinct AMR evolutionary trajectories: gene amplification is favored in environmental reservoirs, whereas clinical settings foster resistance diversity through horizontal acquisition of high-risk determinants.

Plasmids are the major carriers of AMR genes in P. mirabilis

In this study, we investigated the prevalence of AMR genes in plasmids and phages, revealing that plasmids are dominant AMR carriers. Among 2,013 P. mirabilis isolates, 1,883 harbored plasmids, with 1,570 of these plasmids carrying AMR genes. These plasmids carried 178 unique AMR genes classified into 12 classes, accounting for 36.35% (11,116/30,578) of the total AMR gene copies detected in P. mirabilis (Table S6). In contrast, while all 2,013 P. mirabilis isolates contained phages, only 29 phages carried AMR genes (0.46% of total AMR copies), limited to β-lactam and sulfonamide resistance subtypes.

Furthermore, detailed profiling revealed aminoglycoside resistance genes emerged as the most commonly detected AMR genes on plasmids, followed by genes conferring resistance to sulfonamide, β-lactam, and phenicol (Fig. 5a). Of which, the qacJ gene (1,091) was the most prevalent plasmid-borne AMR gene, followed by sul2 (885), qacEdelta1 (664), sul1 (655), APH(6)-Id (409), arr-3 (326), APH(4)-Ia (300), catB3 (275), and dfrA1 (274) (Fig. 5b). The majority of β-lactam resistance genes, including blaKPC−2 (97.06%), blaCTX−M−65 (83.27%), blaNDM−1 (67.15%), blaCTX−M−15 (62.96%), blaCMY−2 (58.74%), and blaOXA−48 (51.61%), were carried by plasmids. Similarly, aminoglycoside-modifying enzymes (APH(4)-Ia, 76.9%; aadA5, 77.7%) and sulfonamide resistance genes (sul2, 77.2%) exhibited strong plasmid linkage. Notably, 38 AMR genes—including carbapenemases (blaOXA−181, blaOXA−58), 16 S rRNA methyltransferases (rmtC), and novel β-lactamases (blaPER−4, blaVIM−4)—were exclusively plasmid-borne, though most occurred at low frequencies (< 0.1%). These findings highlight plasmids as both reservoirs for emerging resistance mechanisms and primary vehicles for established high-risk determinants, cementing their role in shaping multidrug-resistant P. mirabilis populations.

Wide distribution of transmissible AMR plasmids in P. mirabilis

To investigate the mobility dynamics of plasmid-borne ARGs in P. mirabilis, we analyzed 163 complete plasmid sequences originating from this species in the PLSDB database, of which 124 (76.1%) harbored ARGs (Table S7). The predominant plasmid type, Col3M (n = 47), was exclusively non-mobilizable and carried a single quinolone resistance gene (qnrD1) (Fig. 6a, b). Mobilizable IncN (n = 14) and IncU (n = 10) plasmids mainly harbored the blaKPC−1 ARG, providing carbapenem resistance via the production of KPC β-lactamase (Figure S4). Notably, all 12 conjugative IncC plasmids exhibited multidrug resistance profiles, as each plasmids carrying ≥ 5 distinct ARGs that provide resistance to nine antibiotic classes (Fig. 6c). High-prevalence ARGs in IncC plasmids included sul1 (75%), floR (66.7%), sul2 (66.7%), and aminoglycoside-modifying enzymes APH(3’’)-Ib and APH(6)-Id (both 50%). And these plasmids harbored the most diverse aminoglycoside resistance repertoire, including APH(3’’)-Ib, APH(6)-Id, AAC(6’)-Ib-cr, and armA, as well as β-lactamase genes (blaTEM−1, blaNDM−1, blaCTX−M−14) that were rarely detected in other plasmid types. Host range analysis revealed IncC plasmids were widely distributed among Enterobacteriaceae species (P. mirabilis, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Escherichia coli, Salmonella enterica). And the phylogenetic evidence further supported these plasmids were likely shared within these Enterobacteriaceae species (Fig. 6d). The co-localization of extended-spectrum β-lactamases (blaNDM−1, blaTEM−1) with multidrug resistance cassettes in mobile genetic elements underscores the critical role of IncC plasmids in disseminating pan-drug resistance across clinical pathogens (Fig. 6e & Figure S5).

Transmissible plasmids facilitated the transfer of blaNDM−1 among P. mirabilis and other pathogen species

The global dissemination of blaNDM−1, a carbapenemase gene associated with extensive antibiotic resistance and horizontal transfer capacity, represents a critical public health challenge, yet its genomic epidemiology in P. mirabilis remains poorly characterized. blaNDM−1 genetic environments in IncC plasmids of P. mirabilis shared conserved integration sites, their backbones displayed significant modular variation, including interchangeable ARGs and stability modules (Fig. 6e), which may enhance compatibility across Enterobacteriaceae. Analysis of 978 blaNDM−1-carrying plasmids from the PLSDB database revealed broad plasmid type diversity, predominantly IncC (23.3%, n = 228), IncFIB (21.8%, n = 213), and IncFII (17.8%, n = 174), with 89.6% exhibiting transmissible features (conjugative/mobilizable) (Fig. 7a & Figure S6). Notably, IncC plasmids harbored the most diverse resistance profiles (9.01 ± 4.37 ARGs/plasmid), exceeding the cohort average (7.01 ± 4.82) (Fig. 7b). This plasmidome-wide analysis underscores IncC as a major multidrug resistance vector for NDM-1 (Table S8), with structural plasticity enabling persistence across diverse hosts, while highlighting lineage-specific differences in resistance accumulation among plasmid types.

The IncC-type of plasmids drive the dissemination of blaNDM−1 between P. mirabilis and other pathogen species. a The frequency of different types of blaNDM−1-carrying plasmids. b The frequency of AMR genes in different type of plasmids across the P. mirabilis population. c The core genome-based phylogenetic tree of plasmid CP098445.1 (blaNDM−1-carrying IncC plasmid identified in P. mirabilis) and 35 phylogenetically related plasmids (identity > 90%, p < 0.1)

Moreover, we analyzed plasmid CP098445.1 and 35 phylogenetically related plasmids (identity > 90%, p < 0.1) through core genome-based phylogeny (Table S9). The resulting phylogenetic tree revealed no geographical clustering, with plasmids from identical countries dispersed across clades and those from distinct regions intermingled (Fig. 7c), demonstrating their global dissemination. Notably, IncC-type plasmids from P. mirabilis exhibited close evolutionary relationships with homologs from Klebsiella pneumoniae and Escherichia coli, followed by Salmonella enterica, while additional clusters included plasmids from Enterobacter hormaechei, Providencia stuartii, and Aeromonas salmonicida (Fig. 7c). This phylogenetic incongruence—where plasmid lineages transcend host species boundaries—indicates cross-species plasmid transfer within Enterobacteriaceae and beyond. In line with this results, analogous phylogenetic patterns were observed in blaTEM−1-carrying plasmids, which also formed shared clades across Klebsiella pneumoniae, Escherichia coli, and Salmonella enterica (Figure S7).

Discussion

Understanding the prevalence and mobility potential of AMR genes within P. mirabilis populations is of great significance for public health. Previous research has primarily focused on profiling AMR genes and virulence factors in P. mirabilis isolates [38, 39]. However, few studies have comprehensively investigated the whole landscape of AMR genes within P. mirabilis populations, as well as their potential for mobility. These gaps in knowledge have critical implications for effective management of P. mirabilis infections.

In this study, we noted the growing resistance of P. mirabilis to commonly used antibiotics for treating infections, especially β-lactam antibiotics. Then, we profiled AMR genes in a dataset comprising 2,013 genomes of P. mirabilis obtained from the NCBI GenBank database and isolates from our study, shedding light on the mechanisms underlying the organism’s broad resistance to nearly all available antimicrobial agents [40]. Among the 2,013 P. mirabilis genomes analyzed, 197 distinct AMR gene subtypes were detected, with β-lactam resistance genes being the most prevalent and diverse, followed by aminoglycoside resistance genes. Notably, environmental isolates of P. mirabilis were found to carry higher copy numbers of AMR genes, spanning 12 AMR types. Previous studies have shown that many environmental P. mirabilis isolates originate from pig and dairy farms, poultry production, and aquatic products—settings characterized by substantial agricultural antibiotic use [11, 41–43]. This finding underscores the role of environmental areas with high antibiotic pollution as reservoirs of multidrug-resistant P. mirabilis strains, posing a significant risk to human health. On the other hand, clinical P. mirabilis isolates demonstrated greater diversity in AMR genes, especially the β-lactam resistance genes, highlighting the need for ongoing supervision of antibiotic use in healthcare settings.

Of special significance were plasmids in the P. mirabilis population, which harbored nearly 40% of all detected AMR genes, including 163 unique subtypes across 12 categories. The widespread presence of AMR plasmids facilitates the horizontal transfer of these genes among Enterobacteriaceae, including P. mirabilis, significantly enhancing the spread of resistance [44, 45]. Notably, plasmid-borne β-lactam resistance genes, such as blaKPC−2, blaNDM−7, blaOXA−10, blaDHA−1, blaCTX−M−15, blaOXA−1, blaOXA−48, blaOXA−23, blaCTX−M−65, and blaNDM−1, could potentially complicate the empirical use of β-lactams in treating P. mirabilis infections [46]. In contrast, phages–a key mobile genetic element–carried only 0.43% of all AMR genes detected within the P. mirabilis population.

Furthermore, comprehensive analysis of complete plasmids identified in P. mirabilis revealed distinct mobilization patterns and AMR gene carriage profiles. While the majority of plasmids exhibited transmissible capacities (mobilizable or conjugative), non-mobilizable Col3M plasmids were exceptional, exclusively carrying the quinolone resistance gene qnrD1. Mobilizable IncN and IncU plasmids primarily carried the blaKPC−1 gene within genetic contexts, while conjugative IncC plasmids exhibited broad MDR profiles, imparting resistance to nearly all classes of antibiotics via various resistance determinants. Notably, IncC plasmids emerged as the primary reservoir for blaNDM−1 in P. mirabilis, accounting for the majority of metallo-β-lactamase gene dissemination within this species. Phylogenetic reconstruction demonstrated that blaNDM−1-carrying IncC plasmids clustered with homologs from Klebsiella pneumoniae, Escherichia coli, and Salmonella enterica, providing genomic evidence of recurrent interspecies transmission events among clinically relevant Enterobacteriaceae. Clinical surveillance data corroborated this finding, revealing that rising antimicrobial resistance rates in these pathogens correlated with the expanding mobility of multidrug-resistant plasmids [47, 48]. The persistence of IncC plasmids across genera, coupled with their capacity to accumulate broad-spectrum resistance cassettes, underscores their role as high-risk vectors for pan-drug resistance, critically compromising therapeutic efficacy in healthcare settings.

The study has limitations including the broad definition of “environmental source” which may obscure differences in AMR patterns among different niches. Plasmid prediction using Plasmer and geNomad is computational and probabilistic, potentially including false positives. Experimental validation would be needed to confirm plasmid localization.

Conclusion

Overall, our integrative pangenomic and comparative genomic framework provided high-resolution insights into the AMR landscape of P. mirabilis populations, revealing widespread AMR gene carriage conferring resistance to β-lactams, aminoglycosides, fluoroquinolones, and other critical antibiotic classes. Central to these findings was the identification of transmissible plasmids—particularly conjugative IncC-type plasmids—as key drivers of AMR dissemination. These plasmids exhibited broad host compatibility, facilitating horizontal transfer of high-risk resistance determinants such as blaNDM−1 (metallo-β-lactamase) and blaTEM−1 (β-lactamase) across P. mirabilis and phylogenetically divergent Enterobacteriaceae pathogens, including Klebsiella pneumoniae and Escherichia coli. Phylogenetic reconstructions confirmed interspecies plasmid sharing, with IncC plasmids forming cohesive clades spanning multiple genera, indicative of active cross-boundary transmission networks. The convergence of plasmid mobility, multidrug resistance cassettes, and broad-host adaptability positions IncC plasmids as critical accelerators of pan-drug resistance, directly correlating with rising treatment failures in clinical isolates. This genomic epidemiology underscores the urgent need for plasmid-centric surveillance strategies to mitigate resistance spread in healthcare ecosystems.
