1.
Introduction and Main Results

In this paper, we study solutions to the fractional Gelfand equation of the form

(1.1)

(-\Delta)^{s}u=Ke^{u}\quad\mbox{in $\mathbb{R}$}\quad\mbox{with}\quad\int_{\mathbb{R}}Ke^{u}\,dx<+\infty,

where
K:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is a prescribed function. Our main focus will be put on the important case of constant

K(x)\equiv 1.

However, further results for non-constant positive
K(x)>0
will be discussed as well. Following standard conventions, we use
(-\Delta)^{s}
to denote the fractional Laplacian of order
s\in(0,1)
. For sufficiently regular and decaying functions
\varphi
, we recall the singular integral expression

(-\Delta)^{s}\varphi(x)=C_{s}\mathrm{PV}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{\varphi(x)-\varphi(y)}{|x-y|^{1+2s}}\,dy=C_{s}\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\int_{|x-y|\geq\varepsilon}\frac{\varphi(x)-\varphi(y)}{|x-y|^{1+2s}}\,dy

with the normalization constant
C_{s}=\frac{4^{s}}{\sqrt{\pi}}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{1}{2}+s)}{|\Gamma(-s)|}
. To have a mathematically meaningful notion of solutions to (
1.1
), we shall always assume that
e^{u}\in L_{\mathrm{loc}}^{1}(\mathbb{R})
and
u\in L_{s}(\mathbb{R})
, where

L_{s}(\mathbb{R})=\big\{u\in L_{\mathrm{loc}}^{1}(\mathbb{R})\mid\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{|u(x)|}{1+|x|^{1+2s}}\,dx<+\infty\big\}.

We recall that
u
is said to be a (distributional) solution to (
1.1
) whenever we have

\int_{\mathbb{R}}u(-\Delta)^{s}\varphi\,dx=\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{u}\varphi\,dx

for all test functions
\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R})
.

We mention that fractional Gelfand equations (in various space dimensions as well as on bounded domains) can be seen as a natural nonlocal generalization of the Liouville type equation
-\Delta u=e^{u}
, which has been intensively studied in the area of nonlinear elliptic PDEs. We refer the reader to

1.1.
Existence, Symmetry, and Uniqueness for

A particularly important case of (

We start with the following result about regularity and symmetry of solutions. In fact, the proofs are straightforward adaptations of classical regularity estimates and the moving plane method. For the reader’s convenience, we provide the details of the proofs in Section

Theorem 1.1

(Smoothness and Symmetry)
.

Suppose

2.
Strategy of Proofs and Outlook

Let us summarize the arguments needed to prove the main results in this paper. For the ease of presentation, we focus on the fractional Gelfand equation with
K\equiv 1
, i. e., we consider

(2.1)

(-\Delta)^{s}u=e^{u}\quad\mbox{with}\quad\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{u}\,dx<+\infty,

where
s\in(\frac{1}{2},1)
is given. In what follows, we give a sketch of the argument whilst leaving technical points like the choice of function spaces etc. aside.

As a starting point, we observe that any solution
u
of (
2.1
) satisfies a corresponding integral equation, whose formal differentiation yields that

(2.2)

\partial_{x}u=-H_{\alpha}(e^{u}).

Here the operator
H_{\alpha}:=H\circ(-\Delta)^{-\alpha}=H\circ|\nabla|^{-2\alpha}
with
\alpha=s-\frac{1}{2}
is the
conjugate Riesz potential
in one space dimension of order
2\alpha\in(0,1)
and
H
denotes the Hilbert transform on the real line. Inspired by the recent work
[
1
]
, we now introduce the auxiliary function
v:=\sqrt{e^{u}}
. Since this means
u=\log(v^{2})
, we arrive at the equation

(2.3)

\partial_{x}v=-\frac{1}{2}H_{\alpha}(v^{2})v.

In fact, this reformulation in terms
v
turns out to be highly beneficial, as it provides us with a differential equation with a nonlocal nonlinearity that can be handled in a convenient way. Indeed, we can easily recast (
2.3
) into a fixed point equation of the form

(2.4)

v=\mathbf{T}_{\lambda}[v]\quad\mbox{with}\quad\mathbf{T}_{\lambda}[v](x):=\lambda e^{-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{x}H_{\alpha}(v^{2})(y)\,dy},

where the parameter
\lambda>0
corresponds to the initial-value
v(0)=\lambda
. Thanks to the symmetry result in Theorem
1.1
, it suffices to consider even solutions
v
. Hence we can work on a suitable Banach space
X_{\alpha}\subset L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
of even functions and we seek fixed points of the map
\mathbf{T}_{\lambda}
on
X_{\alpha}
for given
\lambda>0
. The search for fixed points of
\mathbf{T}_{\lambda}
can be seen as a nonlocal ersatz of a ‘shooting argument’ prescribing the initial-valued
v(0)=\lambda
.

2.1.
Existence of Fixed Points

To prove existence of fixed points of
\mathbf{T}
, we wish to use a-priori bounds and compactness methods in the spirit of Schauder’s fixed point theorem. However, a first obstruction lies in the fact that
\mathbf{T}_{\lambda}[0]\equiv\lambda\not\in X_{\alpha}\subset L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
. More generally, it seems far from obvious how to single out a (non-empty) closed convex subset
K\subset X_{\alpha}
such that
\mathbf{T}_{\lambda}:K\to K
holds. To deal with this challenge, we introduce an auxiliary class of maps given by

(2.5)

\mathbf{T}^{(\sigma)}_{\lambda}[v]=e^{-\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}x^{2}}\mathbf{T}_{\lambda}[v].

with a parameter
\sigma\in\mathbb{R}
. We remark that finding fixed points of
\mathbf{T}^{(\sigma)}_{\lambda}
corresponds to looking for even solutions
u\in L_{s}(\mathbb{R})
of
(-\Delta)^{s}u=Ke^{u}
with
Ke^{u}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
for the Gaussian function
K(x)=e^{-\sigma^{2}x^{2}}
. If
\sigma\neq 0
, the map
\mathbf{T}^{(\sigma)}_{\lambda}:X_{\alpha}\to X_{\alpha}
is seen to be well-defined, continuous and compact. Furthermore, by rather straightforward a-priori bounds on possible fixed points, we deduce that
\mathbf{T}^{(\sigma)}_{\lambda}
does have a fixed point in
X_{\alpha}
for all
\lambda>0
and
\sigma\neq 0
by applying the classical Schaefer–Schauder fixed point theorem.

However, the passage to the original problem with
\mathbf{T}^{(0)}_{\lambda}=\mathbf{T}_{\lambda}
requires a careful analysis when taking the limit
\sigma\to 0
. Here, we shall make use of Pohozaev-type identities combined with the
reverse Hardy–Littlewood–Sobolev (HLS) inequality
: For all
\mu>0
and
q\in(0,1)
it holds that

(2.6)

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\!\int_{\mathbb{R}}\rho(x)|x-y|^{\mu}\rho(y)\,dx\,dy\geq C_{\mu,q}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}\rho(x)\,dx\right)^{\beta}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}\rho(x)^{q}\,dx\right)^{(2-\beta)/q}

for nonnegative
\rho\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})\cap L^{q}(\mathbb{R})
, provided that
q>\frac{1}{1+\mu}
and with a suitable choice of
\beta=\beta(q,\mu)
. Here we use the convention that
\rho\in L^{q}(\mathbb{R})
for
q\in(0,1)
if
\rho
is measurable and
\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\rho(x)|^{q}\,dx
is finite. In particular, we shall only need the reverse HLS inequality in the so-called conformally invariant case when
\beta=0
which is exactly the case when
q=\frac{2}{2+\mu}
. See
[
4
,
2
,
21
,
7
]
for various proofs of (
2.6
) and related results.

With the help of the reverse HLS inequality with
\rho=v^{2}
and the fact that all fixed points are shown to be symmetric-decreasing functions, we gain sufficient control to prove existence of fixed points for the limiting problem when
\sigma=0
.

2.2.
Uniqueness and Nondegeneracy of Fixed Points

To prove uniqueness of fixed points
v\in X_{\alpha}
of
\mathbf{T}^{(\sigma)}_{\lambda}
for given
\lambda>0
and
\sigma\in\mathbb{R}
, we make use of an implicit function argument and a global continuation argument with respect to the parameters
(\lambda,\sigma)
. As a key step in this procedure, we show that the map

\mathbf{F}(v,\lambda,\sigma)=v-\mathbf{T}^{(\sigma)}_{\lambda}[v]

has an invertible Fréchet derivative
D_{v}\mathbf{F}
at any fixed point
v
of
\mathbf{T}^{(\sigma)}_{\lambda}
. By compactness of
D_{v}\mathbf{T}^{(\sigma)}_{\lambda}[v]
and standard Fredholm theory, this amount to proving that
1
is not an eigenvalue of
D_{v}\mathbf{T}^{(\sigma)}_{\lambda}[v]
. By ideas that are reminiscent to the monotonicity formula for the fractional Laplacian
(-\Delta)^{s}
, we prove this spectral ‘nondegeneracy’ property. However, in contrast to
[
12
,
13
]
, we emphasize the fact that we do not use the
s
-harmonic extension. Instead, we work with arguments based on the Laplace transform, thereby vastly generalizing the recent approach in
[
1
]
to study the fractional Liouville equation
(-\Delta)^{1/2}u=Ke^{u}
in
\mathbb{R}
with suitable conditions on the function
K
. See also the proof of Theorem
1.5
, which further elaborates on this circle of ideas and exhibits a close connection to the Birman–Schwinger principle in the spectral analysis of Schrödinger type operators
H=(-\Delta)^{s}-V
.

Thanks to the general linearized invertibility result, we can construct locally unique branches
(\lambda,\sigma)\mapsto v(\lambda,\sigma)
of fixed points. With the help of a-priori bounds, these branches can be extended globally to all
\lambda>0
and
\sigma\in\mathbb{R}
. On the other hand, for
\sigma\neq 0
and
0<\lambda\ll 1
sufficiently small, we also see that
\mathbf{T}^{(\sigma)}_{\lambda}
is a strict contraction and we hence obtain global uniqueness in this regime. Finally, by straightforward arguments, we can extend this to global uniqueness to all values of
\lambda>0
and
\sigma\in\mathbb{R}
. In particular, this completes the general uniqueness result stated in Theorem
1.3
, which constitutes the central result of this paper.

2.3.
Outlook on Future Work

In an ongoing companion work
[
18
]
, we consider the corresponding parabolic problem

\partial_{t}u+(-\Delta)^{s}u=e^{u}\quad\mbox{for $t>0$ and $x\in\mathbb{R}$}

with
s\in[\frac{1}{2},1)
and we study stability and instability (by finite-time blowup) of the steady state solutions
u=Q_{s}\in L_{s}(\mathbb{R})\cap C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
provided by Theorem
1.2
.

Clearly, the present work allows for many other future directions. Here a natural question would be to extend the arguments to higher space dimensions

(-\Delta)^{s}u=e^{u}\quad\mbox{in $\mathbb{R}^{n}$}

with general
s>n/2
. We hope to address this problem in future work.

3.
Preliminaries

We first collect some preliminaries facts whose proofs are quite elementary but worked out for the reader’s convenience.

3.1.
Integral Equation and Conjugate Riesz Potential

It will be convenient to introduce the parameter

\alpha:=s-\frac{1}{2}\in(0,\frac{1}{2})

for
s\in(\frac{1}{2},1)
given. By straightforward arguments, we obtain the following result.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
s\in(\frac{1}{2},1)
and
\rho\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
. Then
u\in L_{s}(\mathbb{R})
solves
(-\Delta)^{s}u=\rho
if and only if

u(x)=-c_{\alpha}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(|x-y|^{2\alpha}-|y|^{2\alpha}\right)\rho(y)\,dy+A+Bx

with some constants
A,B\in\mathbb{R}
and
c_{\alpha}=-\pi^{-1/2}2^{-2\alpha-1}\frac{\Gamma(-\alpha)}{\Gamma(\alpha+\frac{1}{2})}>0
.

Proof.

Define the function

(3.1)

\tilde{u}(x):=-c_{\alpha}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(|x-y|^{2\alpha}-|y|^{2\alpha}\right)\rho(y)\,dy.

From the elementary inequality
||x-y|^{2\alpha}-|y|^{2\alpha}|\leq|x|^{2\alpha}
for
\alpha\in(0,\frac{1}{2})
, we deduce the pointwise bound
|\tilde{u}(x)|\leq c_{\alpha}|x|^{2\alpha}\|\rho\|_{L^{1}}
for
x\in\mathbb{R}
. This implies that
\tilde{u}\in L_{s}(\mathbb{R})
. We claim that

(-\Delta)^{s}\tilde{u}=\rho\quad\mbox{in $\mathbb{R}$}.

To show this, let
(\rho_{k})_{k\geq 1}\subset C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R})
be a sequence with
\rho_{k}\to\rho
in
L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
and define the functions
\tilde{u}_{k}\in L_{s}(\mathbb{R})
accordingly by replacing
\rho
with
\rho_{k}\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R})
on the right side in (
3.1
). From the well-known fact
(-\Delta)^{s}(G_{s}+\mbox{const.})=\delta
in
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
with the Green’s function
G_{s}(x)=-c_{\alpha}|x|^{2\alpha}
, we deduce that
(-\Delta)^{s}\tilde{u}_{k}=\rho_{k}
. Now, let
\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R})
be given. Since
\rho_{k}\to\rho
in
L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
, we see that
\int_{\mathbb{R}}\rho_{k}\varphi\to\int_{\mathbb{R}}\rho\varphi
. On the other hand, using the well-known fact that
|(-\Delta)^{s}\varphi(x)|\leq C\langle x\rangle^{-1-2s}
for
\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R})
together with the bound
|\tilde{u}_{k}(x)-\tilde{u}(x)|\leq c_{\alpha}|x|^{2\alpha}\|\rho_{k}-\rho\|_{L^{1}}
, we conclude that
\int_{\mathbb{R}}w_{k}(-\Delta)^{s}\varphi\to\int_{\mathbb{R}}w(-\Delta)^{s}\varphi
. Hence by passing to the limit
k\to\infty
, we find that
\tilde{u}\in L_{s}(\mathbb{R})
solves
(-\Delta)^{s}\tilde{u}=\rho
.

Now, assume that
u\in L_{s}(\mathbb{R})
solves
(-\Delta)^{s}u=\rho
. Then
w:=u-\tilde{u}\in L_{s}(\mathbb{R})
solves
(-\Delta)^{s}w=0
in
\mathbb{R}
. By known results (see, e. g.,
[
9
]
), this means that
w(x)=A+Bx
is an affine function. [Note that
A+Bx\in L_{s}(\mathbb{R})
for all
A,B\in\mathbb{R}
when
s>\frac{1}{2}
.] This proves the claimed formula for
u
.

On the other hand, let
u
be defined by the formula for given
\rho\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
with some constants
A,B\in\mathbb{R}
. Since
|u(x)|\leq c_{\alpha}|x|^{2\alpha}+|A|+|Bx|
for
x\in\mathbb{R}
, we conclude that
u\in L_{s}(\mathbb{R})
holds. From the discussion above we deduce that
u
solves
(-\Delta)^{s}u=\rho
.
∎

In view of Proposition
3.1
, we introduce the following definition.

Definition
.

Let
s\in(\frac{1}{2},1)
and
\rho\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
. We say that
u\in L_{s}(\mathbb{R})
is an
integral solution
of
(-\Delta)^{s}u=\rho
provided that

u(x)=O(|x|^{2\alpha})\quad\mbox{as}\quad|x|\to\infty,

i. e., we have that
B=0
holds in Proposition
3.1
.

It is easy to see that even solutions
u(x)=u(-x)
above must be necessarily integral. Also, we have the following sufficient condition with regard to solutions of fractional Gelfand-type equations in
\mathbb{R}
.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
s\in(\frac{1}{2},1)
. Suppose that
K(x)=K(-x)\geq 0
is an even, non-negative and measurable function on
\mathbb{R}
with
e^{\mu|x|}K\not\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
for all
\mu>0
. Then any solution
u\in L_{s}(\mathbb{R})
of

(-\Delta)^{s}u=Ke^{u}\quad\mbox{with}\quad\int_{\mathbb{R}}Ke^{u}\,dx<+\infty

must be integral. In particular, any solution
u\in L_{s}(\mathbb{R})
of
(-\Delta)^{s}u=e^{u}
with
\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{u}\,dx<+\infty
is integral.

Proof.

Define
0\leq\rho:=Ke^{u}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
. By Proposition
3.1
and its proof, we deduce that

u(x)\geq-c_{\alpha}|x|^{2\alpha}\|\rho\|_{L^{1}}+A+Bx\quad\mbox{for $x\in\mathbb{R}$}.

with some
A,B\in\mathbb{R}
. Since
K\geq 0
is even and
e^{\mu|x|}K\not\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
for all
\mu>0
, we have that
\int_{0}^{\infty}K(x)e^{\mu x}\,dx=+\infty
for all
\mu>0
. Suppose now that
B>0
holds. Since
\alpha\in(0,\frac{1}{2})
, we can find for any
0<\varepsilon<B
and a constant
C=C(\varepsilon,\|\rho\|_{L^{1}})>0
such that
-c_{\alpha}\|\rho\|_{L^{1}}|x|^{2\alpha}\geq-\varepsilon x-C
for
x\geq 0
. Therefore, we get

+\infty>\int_{0}^{\infty}K(x)e^{u(x)}\,dx\geq e^{-C+A}\int_{0}^{\infty}K(x)e^{(B-\varepsilon)x}\,dx=+\infty,

which is a contradiction. The case
B<0
can be treated in the same way by taking integrals over
(-\infty,0]
. Thus we see that
B=0
holds and hence
u
is an integral solution.
∎

Next, we rewrite the integral term in Proposition
3.1
using the
conjugate Riesz potential operator
defined as

(3.2)

\boxed{(H_{\alpha}f)(x):=d_{\alpha}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{x-y}{|x-y|^{2-2\alpha}}f(y)\,dy=d_{\alpha}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{\mathrm{sgn}(x-y)}{|x-y|^{1-2\alpha}}f(y)\,dy}

for suitable functions
f
, where
\alpha\in(0,\frac{1}{2})
and the positive constant
d_{\alpha}=2\alpha c_{\alpha}>0
with
c_{\alpha}>0
taken from Proposition
3.1
above. On suitable functions spaces, we deduce that

H_{\alpha}=H\circ(-\Delta)^{-\alpha}.

Here

Hf(x)=\frac{1}{\pi}\mathrm{p.v.}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{f(y)}{x-y}\,dy,\quad(-\Delta)^{-\alpha}f(x)=|\nabla|^{-2\alpha}f(x)=\frac{1}{\gamma_{2\alpha}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{f(y)}{|x-y|^{1-2\alpha}}\,dy

denote the Hilbert transform and the Riesz potential operator in one space dimension with
\alpha\in(0,\frac{1}{2})
and
\gamma_{s}=\pi^{1/2}2^{s}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{s}{2})}{\Gamma(\frac{1-s}{2})}
, respectively. We have the following mapping properties of
H_{\alpha}
, which will be relevant below.

Lemma 3.1
.

For any
\alpha\in(0,\frac{1}{2})
, the map
H_{\alpha}:L^{1}(\mathbb{R})\cap L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})\to L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
is bounded.

Moreover, if
f\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})\cap L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
, then the function
(H_{\alpha}f)(x)
is continuous. If in addition
f=f^{*}
is symmetric-decreasing, then
(H_{\alpha}f)(x)
is an odd function with
(H_{\alpha}f)(x)\geq 0
for
x\geq 0
and we have strict positivity
(H_{\alpha}f)(x)>0
for all
x>0
whenever
f\not\equiv 0
.

Remarks
.

1) We refer to
[
20
]
for a general background on symmetric-decreasing rearrangements.

2) Using the weak Young inequality, we can show that
H_{\alpha}:L^{p}(\mathbb{R})\to L^{q}(\mathbb{R})
is bounded for any
1<p<\frac{1}{2\alpha}
and
1<q<\infty
with
\frac{1}{q}=\frac{1}{p}-2\alpha
. We will not need this fact here.

Proof.

We write
H_{\alpha}f=k_{\alpha}\ast f
with
k_{\alpha}(x)=\frac{\mathrm{sgn}(x)}{|x|^{1-2\alpha}}\mathds{1}_{|x|\leq 1}+\frac{\mathrm{sgn}(x)}{|x|^{1-2\alpha}}\mathds{1}_{|x|>1}\in L^{p_{1}}(\mathbb{R})+L^{p_{2}}(\mathbb{R})
for some
1<p_{1}<p_{2}<\infty
. Applying Young’s and Hölder’s inequality, it follows that

\|H_{\alpha}f\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq C\left(\|f\|_{L^{q_{1}}}+\|f\|_{L^{q_{2}}}\right)\leq\|f\|_{L^{1}\cap L^{\infty}}

for
q_{1},q_{2}\in(1,\infty)
with
\frac{1}{p_{i}}+\frac{1}{q_{i}}=1
for
i=1,2
. This shows that
H_{\alpha}:L^{1}(\mathbb{R})\cap L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})\to L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
is bounded. Moreover, it is a well-known fact that
(g\ast f)(x)
is a continuous function provided that
g\in L^{p}(\mathbb{R})
and
f\in L^{q}(\mathbb{R})
with
1<p,q<\infty
and
\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{q}=1
.

Next, we note

(3.3)

(H_{\alpha}f)(x)=d_{\alpha}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{x-y}{|x-y|^{2-2\alpha}}f(y)\,dy=d_{\alpha}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{y^{1-2\alpha}}\left(f(x-y)-f(x+y)\right)dy,

which shows that
(H_{\alpha}f)(x)
is an odd function in
x
whenever
f
is even. Furthermore if
f=f^{*}
is symmetric-decreasing, we see that
f(x-y)\geq f(x+y)
for all
x,y\geq 0
, which implies that
(H_{\alpha}f)(x)\geq 0
for
x\geq 0
. Finally, if we assume that
f\not\equiv 0
holds. For any
x>0
fixed, the nonnegative function
\varphi(y)=f(x-y)+f(x+y)\geq 0
satisfies
\varphi\not\equiv 0
and we deduce from (
3.3
) that
(H_{\alpha}f)(x)>0
for
x>0
.
∎

Next, we have the following identity involving the conjugate Riesz potential in our problem.

Proposition 3.3
.

For any
\rho\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})\cap L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
, it holds that

-c_{\alpha}\int_{\mathbb{R}}(|x-y|^{2\alpha}-|y|^{2\alpha})\rho(y)\,dy=-\int_{0}^{x}H_{\alpha}(\rho)(y)\,dy\quad\mbox{for $x\in\mathbb{R}$}.

Remark
.

The condition that
\rho\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
is also bounded could be relaxed, but this choice turns out to be sufficient and convenient for our purposes here.

Proof.

Let
u(x)
denote the left-hand side of the claimed equality. We claim that

(3.4)

\partial_{x}u=-H_{\alpha}(\rho)

in the sense of weak derivatives. Indeed, let
\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R})
be given. We readily check that we can apply Fubini’s theorem to deduce that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}u(x)\partial_{x}\varphi(x)\,dx

\displaystyle=-c_{\alpha}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}(|x-y|^{2\alpha}-|y|^{2\alpha})\rho(y)\,dy\right)\partial_{x}\varphi(x)\,dx

\displaystyle=-c_{\alpha}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\rho(y)\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}(|x-y|^{2\alpha}-|y|^{2\alpha})\partial_{x}\varphi(x)\,dx\right)dy

\displaystyle=2\alpha c_{\alpha}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\rho(y)\left(\frac{x-y}{|x-y|^{2-2\alpha}}\varphi(x)\,dx\right)dy

\displaystyle=2\alpha c_{\alpha}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{x-y}{|x-y|^{2-2\alpha}}\rho(y)\,dy\right)\varphi(x)\,dx,

where we also used that
\partial_{x}(|x-y|^{2\alpha}-|y|^{2\alpha})=2\alpha\frac{x-y}{|x-y|^{2-2\alpha}}
in the sense of weak derivatives. This proves (
3.4
). Since
H_{\alpha}(\rho)(x)
is continuous for
\rho\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})\cap L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
by Lemma
3.1
, we conclude that
\partial_{x}u
is of class
C^{0}
and hence the claimed identity follows from integration and noticing that
u(0)=0
.
∎

3.2.
Regularity, Asymptotic Behavior, and Symmetry

For an integer
k\geq 0
and a real number
\beta\in(0,1]
, we use
C_{\mathrm{loc}}^{k,\beta}(\mathbb{R})
the set of functions
u:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
of class
C^{k}
such that
\partial^{k}u
is locally Hölder continuous of order
\beta
. For a real number
\gamma>0
, we use the standard notation
C_{\mathrm{loc}}^{\gamma}(\mathbb{R})\equiv C_{\mathrm{loc}}^{\lfloor\gamma\rfloor,\gamma-\lfloor\gamma\rfloor}(\mathbb{R})
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
s\in(\frac{1}{2},1)
and suppose
u\in L_{s}(\mathbb{R})
solves
(-\Delta)^{s}u=\rho
with
\rho\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
. If
\rho\in C_{\mathrm{loc}}^{\gamma}(\mathbb{R})
with some
\gamma>0
, then
u\in C_{\mathrm{loc}}^{\gamma+2s}(\mathbb{R})
.

Proof.

This follows from standard Schauder type arguments. Indeed, in view of Proposition
3.1
and since affine functions are smooth, it suffices to consider the case

u(x)=-c_{\alpha}\int_{\mathbb{R}}(|x-y|^{2\alpha}-|y|^{2\alpha})\rho(y)\,dy.

Let
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
be given and take
j\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R})
with
0\leq j\leq 1
and
j(x)=1
for
x
in an open ball
B
centered at
x_{0}
. We write

\rho=j\rho+(1-j)\rho=:\rho_{1}+\rho_{2}.

Let
u_{k}
with
k=1,2
be given by the above integral expression with
\rho_{k}
for
k=1,2
on the right-hand sides. Since
(-\Delta)^{s}u_{2}=\rho_{2}=0
in
B
, it follows that
u_{2}\in C^{\infty}(B)
. Furthermore, we note that
u_{1}=j\rho\in C_{\mathrm{loc}}^{\gamma}(\mathbb{R})
has compact support. Thus by following standard Schauder-type estimates (e. g. take an adaption of the proof
[
20
]
[Theorem 10.3] for Poisson’s equation in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
), we deduce that
u_{1}\in C_{\mathrm{loc}}^{\gamma+2s}(\mathbb{R})
. Therefore we deduce that
u=u_{1}+u_{2}\in C_{\mathrm{loc}}^{\gamma+2s}(\mathbb{R})
.
∎

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
s\in(\frac{1}{2},1)
and
\rho\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
with
\rho\geq 0
. Suppose
u\in L_{s}(\mathbb{R})
is an integral solution of
(-\Delta)^{s}u=\rho
. Then, for any
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, there exists
R>0
such that

u(x)\leq-c_{\alpha}(1-\varepsilon)|x|^{2\alpha}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}\rho(y)\,dy\right)+A\quad\mbox{for}\quad|x|\geq R

with some constant
A\in\mathbb{R}
.

Proof.

Since
u\in L_{s}(\mathbb{R})
is an integral solution, we have

u(x)=-c_{\alpha}\int_{\mathbb{R}}(|x-y|^{2\alpha}-|y|^{2\alpha})\rho(y)\,dy+A

with some constant
A\in\mathbb{R}
. Suppose that
\rho\not\equiv 0
, since otherwise the result follows trivially.

Let
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
be given and take
\delta>0
sufficiently small such that
|1-t|^{2\alpha}-|t|^{2\alpha}\geq 1-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}
for
|t|\leq\delta
. Furthermore, let
1>\mu>0
be given and take
M>0
sufficiently large such that

\int_{|y|>M}\rho(y)\,dy\leq\mu\|\rho\|_{L^{1}}

Thus if we define
R:=\delta^{-1}M>0
, we obtain
|x-y|^{2\alpha}-|y|^{2\alpha}\geq(1-\frac{\varepsilon}{2})|x|^{2\alpha}
for
|y|\leq M
and
|x|\geq R
. Using that
\rho\geq 0
and
|x-y|^{2\alpha}-|y|^{2\alpha}\geq-|x|^{2\alpha}
for
\alpha\in(0,\frac{1}{2})
, we deduce

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}(|x-y|^{2\alpha}-|y|^{2\alpha})\rho(y)\,dy\geq(1-\frac{\varepsilon}{2})|x|^{2\alpha}\int_{|y|\leq M}\rho(y)\,dy-|x|^{2\alpha}\int_{|y|>M}\rho(y)\,dy

\displaystyle\geq\left((1-\frac{\varepsilon}{2})(1-\mu)-\mu)\right)|x|^{2\alpha}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}\rho(y)\,dy\right)\quad\mbox{for $|x|\geq R$}.

Choosing now
\mu>0
sufficiently small such that
(1-\frac{\varepsilon}{2})(1-\mu)-\mu\geq 1-\varepsilon
, we obtain the claimed estimate by multiplying the above bound with
-c_{\alpha}<0
.
∎

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
s\in(\frac{1}{2},1)
and suppose
K\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R})
is even, non-negative and monotone-decreasing in
|x|
. Then any integral solution
u\in L_{s}(\mathbb{R})
of

(-\Delta)^{s}u=Ke^{u}\quad\mbox{with}\quad\int_{\mathbb{R}}Ke^{u}\,dx<+\infty

is even and monotone-decreasing with respect to some point
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
. Moreover, we have
x_{0}=0
whenever
K\not\equiv\mathrm{const.}

Proof.

We assume that
K\not\equiv 0
, since otherwise
u
is an integral solution of
(-\Delta)^{s}u=0
and hence
u(x)\equiv A
for some constant
A\in\mathbb{R}
and the result trivially follows.

Now let
\rho:=Ke^{u}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
. Since
\rho\geq 0
with
\rho\not\equiv 0
, we deduce from Lemma
3.3
that
u(x)\leq-a|x|^{2\alpha}+b
for
|x|\geq R
with some constants
a>0
and
b\in\mathbb{R}
and some radius
R>0
. Thus
0\leq\rho(x)=K(x)e^{u(x)}\leq K(0)e^{-a|x|^{2\alpha}+b}
for
|x|\geq R
and we easily deduce that
|x|^{m}\rho\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
for any
m\geq 0
. If we choose
m=2\alpha
, we deduce that

u(x)=-c_{\alpha}\int_{\mathbb{R}}|x-y|^{2\alpha}K(y)e^{u(y)}\,dy+C

with some constant
C\in\mathbb{R}
. By applying the moving plane method detailed in Appendix
B
below, we finish the proof.
∎

3.3.
Proofs of Theorem
1.1

Let
s\in(\frac{1}{2},1)
and suppose that
u\in L_{s}(\mathbb{R})
solves
(-\Delta)^{s}u=e^{u}
in
\mathbb{R}
with
\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{u}\,dx<+\infty
. From Proposition
3.2
we deduce that
u
is an integral solution, i. e.,

u(x)=-c_{\alpha}\int_{\mathbb{R}}(|x-y|^{2\alpha}-|y|^{2\alpha})e^{u(y)}\,dy+A

with some constant
A\in\mathbb{R}
, whence in particular we have
u(x)=O(|x|^{2\alpha})
as
|x|\to+\infty
. Using the elementary estimate
||x-y|^{2\alpha}-|z-y|^{2\alpha}|\leq|x-z|^{2\alpha}
for
x,y,z\in\mathbb{R}
and
2\alpha\in(0,1)
, we obtain the global Hölder estimate

|u(x)-u(z)|^{2\alpha}\leq c_{\alpha}\|e^{u}\|_{L^{1}}|x-z|^{2\alpha}\quad\mbox{for all $x,z\in\mathbb{R}$}.

In particular, we see that
u\in C_{\mathrm{loc}}^{\gamma}(\mathbb{R})
with
\gamma=2\alpha=2s-1>0
and thus
e^{u}\in C_{\mathrm{loc}}^{\gamma}(\mathbb{R})
. Applying Lemma
3.2
, we conclude that
u\in C_{\mathrm{loc}}^{\gamma+2s}(\mathbb{R})
. By iterating this argument, we find that
u\in C_{\mathrm{loc}}^{\gamma+k\cdot 2s}(\mathbb{R})
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
, whence it follows that
u\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
is smooth.

Next, by Lemma
3.3
, we find for any
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
a radius
R>0
such that

u(x)\leq-c_{\alpha}(1-\varepsilon)|x|^{2\alpha}\|\rho\|_{L^{1}}+A\quad\mbox{for $|x|\geq R$}.

By this bound and the continuity of
u
, we deduce that
u(x)\leq M
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
with some constant
M\in\mathbb{R}
. Thus
u
is bounded from above, i. e., we have
u_{+}=\max\{u,0\}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
.

Next, we apply the moving plane method in Lemma
3.4
to deduce that

u(x)=u_{0}(|x-x_{0}|)

with some even function
u_{0}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
satisfying the monotonicity property
u(r)\leq u(s)
for
r\geq s\geq 0
. We claim that
u_{0}(r)
is indeed strictly monotone-decreasing. To see this, we note that
u_{0}\in L_{s}(\mathbb{R})
is also an integral solution of the problem
(-\Delta)^{s}u=e^{u}
in
\mathbb{R}
with
e^{u}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
. Since
u_{0}
is bounded from above (by
u_{0}(0)
due to monotonicity), we see that
\rho:=e^{u_{0}}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})\cap L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
. From Proposition
3.3
, we obtain that

\partial_{x}u_{0}=-H_{\alpha}(\rho_{0}).

Finally, since
\rho_{0}=\rho_{0}^{*}
is symmetric-decreasing and not identically zero, we find that
H_{\alpha}(\rho_{0})(x)>0
for all
x>0
by Lemma
3.1
. Thus
\partial_{x}u_{0}(x)<0
for all
x>0
, which implies that
u_{0}
is strictly monotone-decreasing.

This finishes the proof of Theorem
1.1
.
∎

4.
Setting up the Fixed Point Scheme

The goal of this section is to setup a fixed point scheme associated to (
1.1
) in terms of the function
v=\sqrt{Ke^{u}}
. Throughout the following discussion, we fix
s\in(\frac{1}{2},1)
and we set
\alpha=s-\frac{1}{2}\in(0,\frac{1}{2})
. We assume that the given function
K:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}_{>0}
satisfies Assumption
(A)
above. We remind the reader that we use the notation
a\lesssim b
to mean that
a\leq Cb
with some constant
C>0
, which may change from line to line but it only depends on
s
(or equivalently on
\alpha
) and the function
K
.

4.1.
Reformulation of the Problem

We introduce the (real) Banach space

(4.1)

X_{\alpha}:=\{v\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R},|x|^{2\alpha}\,dx)\cap L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})\mid\mbox{$v(x)=v(-x)$ for a.\,e.\penalty\ $x\in\mathbb{R}$}\}

endowed the norm
\|v\|_{X^{\alpha}}:=\|v\|_{L^{2}}+\||x|^{\alpha}v\|_{L^{2}}+\|v\|_{L^{\infty}}
. Note the continuous embedding
L^{2}(\mathbb{R},|x|^{2\alpha}\,dx)\cap L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})\subset L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
due to the elementary estimate

\|v\|_{L^{2}}=\|\mathds{1}_{|x|\leq 1}v\|_{L^{2}}+\|\mathds{1}_{|x|>1}v\|_{L^{2}}\lesssim\|v\|_{L^{\infty}}+\||x|^{\alpha}v\|_{L^{2}}.

However, it will turn out to be convenient to include the term
\|v\|_{L^{2}}
in our definition of the norm
\|v\|_{X^{\alpha}}
henceforth.

Lemma 4.1
.

An even function
u\in L_{s}(\mathbb{R})
with
Ke^{u}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
solves the integral equation

u(x)=-c_{\alpha}\int_{\mathbb{R}}(|x-y|^{2\alpha}-|y|^{2\alpha})K(y)e^{u(y)}\,dy+u(0)

with some
u(0)\in\mathbb{R}
if and only if
v=\sqrt{Ke^{u}}>0
belongs to
X_{\alpha}\cap C^{1}
and solves

(4.2)

\partial_{x}v=\frac{1}{2}(\partial_{x}\log K)v-\frac{1}{2}H_{\alpha}(v^{2})v

and
v(0)=\sqrt{K(0)e^{u(0)}}
.

Proof.

We divide the proof as follows.

Step 1.
Suppose that
u\in L_{s}(\mathbb{R})
is an even function with
Ke^{u}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
that solves the integral equation. From the decay estimate in Lemma
3.3
, we find that
u(x)\leq-a|x|^{2\alpha}+b
for
|x|\geq R
with some constants
a>0
,
b\in\mathbb{R}
and
R>0
. Since
u
is continuous, this implies that
|e^{u(x)}|\leq Ce^{-a|x|^{2\alpha}}
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
with some constant
C>0
. Since
K(x)>0
is bounded, the even function
v:=\sqrt{Ke^{u}}>0
belongs to
X_{\alpha}
. Furthermore, we see that
v
is
C^{1}
, since
K>0
and
e^{u}
are both of class
C^{1}
. From Proposition
3.3
, we deduce that

\partial_{x}u=-H_{\alpha}(Ke^{u}).

Since
v^{2}=Ke^{u}
and thus
\partial_{x}u=\partial_{x}\log(K^{-1}v^{2})=2\frac{\partial_{x}v}{v}-\partial_{x}\log K
, we readily obtain the claimed equation satisfied by
v
. Clearly, we have that
v(0)=\sqrt{K(0)e^{u(0)}}
holds.

Step 2.
Let
v\in X_{\alpha}\cap C^{1}
be a solution of stated equation with
v(0)>0
. By Lemma
4.2
below, we have that

v(x)=\lambda\sqrt{K(x)}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{x}H_{\alpha}(v^{2})(y)\,dy}

with the constant
\lambda=v(0)/\sqrt{K(0)}>0
. Thus we see that
v(x)>0
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
. Hence we can define the even
C^{1}
-function
u:=\log(K^{-1}v^{2})
, which is seen to satisfy
\partial_{x}u=-H_{\alpha}(Ke^{u})
. Hence

u(x)=-\int_{0}^{x}H_{\alpha}(Ke^{u})(y)\,dy+u(0)

with
u(0)=\log(K^{-1}(0)v(0)^{2})
which is the same as
v(0)=\sqrt{K(0)e^{u(0)}}
. Since
Ke^{u}=v^{2}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})\cap L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
, we can apply Proposition
3.3
to deduce that
u
satisfies the integral equation. Finally, from the integral equation, we easily see that
|u(x)|\leq c_{\alpha}|x|^{2\alpha}\|v\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+|u(0)|
for
x\in\mathbb{R}
, which implies
u\in L_{s}(\mathbb{R})
.
∎

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
v\in X_{\alpha}
. Then
v
is
C^{1}
and solves (
4.2
) if and only if

v(x)=\lambda\sqrt{K(x)}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{x}H_{\alpha}(v^{2})(y)\,dy}

with some constant
\lambda
.

Proof.

Let
v\in X_{\alpha}
. Suppose that
v
is given by the right-hand side of the stated equation. From Proposition
4.1
below, we find that
v
is
C^{1}
and differentiation yields that
v
solves (
4.2
).

Assume now that
v\in X_{\alpha}\cap C^{1}
solves (
4.2
). Using the
C^{1}
-function
f(x)=\frac{1}{2}\log K(x)-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{x}H_{\alpha}(v^{2})(y)\,dy
, an elementary calculation shows that
\partial_{x}(e^{-f}v)=0
. Thus
e^{-f}v=\lambda
with some constant
\lambda
and we infer that
v
is given by the asserted formula.
∎

4.2.
Fixed Point Setup and Compactness Properties

In view of Lemmas
4.1
and
4.2
, we setup the following fixed point scheme. For the parameter
\lambda>0
, we define the nonlinear map

(4.3)

\boxed{\mathbf{T}_{\lambda}[v](x):=\lambda\sqrt{K(x)}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{x}H_{\alpha}(v^{2})(y)\,dy}\quad\mbox{for $v\in X_{\alpha}$}}

where we recall that
H_{\alpha}=H\circ(-\Delta)^{-\alpha}
denotes the conjugate Riesz potential operator; see Section
3
for elementary mapping properties. For later use, we introduce the following extension of
\mathbf{T}_{\lambda}
given by

(4.4)

\boxed{\mathbf{T}^{(\sigma)}_{\lambda}[v](x):=e^{-\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}x^{2}}\mathbf{T}_{\lambda}[v](x)}

with the parameter
\sigma\in\mathbb{R}
, which is equivalent to replacing
K(x)
with
e^{-\sigma^{2}x^{2}}K(x)
in the definition of
\mathbf{T}_{\lambda}
above. In what follows, we shall tacitly assume that
\lambda>0
and
\sigma\in\mathbb{R}
holds, unless we explicitly state that
\sigma\neq 0
.

Proposition 4.1
.

For all
v\in X_{\alpha}
, it holds

0<\mathbf{T}^{(\sigma)}_{\lambda}[v](x)\lesssim\lambda e^{-\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}x^{2}}e^{-d_{\alpha}\|v\|_{L^{2}}^{2}|x|^{2\alpha}+2d_{\alpha}\||x|^{\alpha}v\|_{L^{2}}^{2}}\quad\mbox{for $x\in\mathbb{R}$}

with some constant
d_{\alpha}>0
depending only on
\alpha
. Moreover, the function
x\mapsto\mathbf{T}^{(\sigma)}_{\lambda}[v](x)
is even and
C^{1}
with the pointwise bound

|\partial_{x}\mathbf{T}^{(\sigma)}_{\lambda}[v](x)|\lesssim\lambda e^{-\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}x^{2}}e^{-d_{\alpha}\|v\|_{L^{2}}^{2}|x|^{2\alpha}+2d_{\alpha}\||x|^{\alpha}v\|_{L^{2}}^{2}}\left(\sigma^{2}|x|+\|v\|_{X^{\alpha}}^{2}+1\right)\quad\mbox{for $x\in\mathbb{R}$}.

Remark 4.1
.

From the pointwise bound above, we readily see that the map

\mathbf{T}^{(\sigma)}_{\lambda}:X_{\alpha}\to X_{\alpha}

is well-defined provided that
\sigma\neq 0
. However, for the case
\sigma=0
, we have in general that

\mathbf{T}^{(0)}_{\lambda}=\mathbf{T}_{\lambda}:X_{\alpha}\setminus\{0\}\to X_{\alpha}.

For instance, if
K(x)\equiv 1
, we obviously see that
\mathbf{T}_{\lambda}[0](x)\equiv\lambda\not\in X_{\alpha}
.

Proof.

Let
v\in X_{\alpha}
be given and write
\mathbf{T}^{(\sigma)}_{\lambda}[v](x)=\lambda e^{-\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}x^{2}}\sqrt{K(x)}e^{w(x)}
with

w(x):=-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{x}H_{\alpha}(v^{2})(y)\,dy=-d_{\alpha}\int_{\mathbb{R}}(|x-y|^{2\alpha}-|y|^{2\alpha})v(y)^{2}\,dy

with some constant
d_{\alpha}>0
, where the second equality is taken from Section
3
. Using the elementary inequality
-|x-y|^{2\alpha}\leq-|x|^{2\alpha}+|y|^{2\alpha}
for
\alpha\in(0,\frac{1}{2})
together with
v^{2}\geq 0
, we estimate

w(x)\leq-d_{\alpha}\|v\|_{L^{2}}^{2}|x|^{2\alpha}+2d_{\alpha}\||x|^{\alpha}v\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\quad\mbox{for $x\in\mathbb{R}$}.

We thus obtain the claimed pointwise bound for
0<\mathbf{T}^{(\sigma)}_{\lambda}[v](x)\leq\lambda\|\sqrt{K}\|_{L^{\infty}}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}x^{2}}e^{w(x)}
.

Next, we note that
w(x)
is even, since
v(y)^{2}
is even. Hence it follows
\mathbf{T}^{(\sigma)}_{\lambda}[v](-x)=\mathbf{T}^{(\sigma)}_{\lambda}[v](x)
by the fact
K(x)=K(-x)
. Since
K(x)>0
is
C^{1}
, we see that
\sqrt{K(x)}
is
C^{1}
. Thus it remains to show that
w(x)
is
C^{1}
. To see this, we note that
H_{\alpha}(v^{2})=k_{\alpha}\ast v^{2}
with a kernel
k_{\alpha}\in L^{p_{1}}(\mathbb{R})+L^{p_{2}}(\mathbb{R})
with some
1<p_{1}<p_{2}<\infty
. Since
v^{2}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})\cap L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
, we deduce that
H_{\alpha}(v^{2})=k_{\alpha}\ast v^{2}
is a bounded and continuous functions. This shows that
w
is
C^{1}
.

Finally, with
w(x)
as above, we find that

\partial_{x}\mathbf{T}^{(\sigma)}_{\lambda}[v](x)=\mathbf{T}^{(\sigma)}_{\lambda}[v](x)\left(-\sigma^{2}x-\frac{1}{2}H_{\alpha}(v^{2})(x)\right)+\lambda e^{-\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}x^{2}}e^{w(x)}\partial_{x}\sqrt{K(x)}.

Since
\|\partial_{x}\sqrt{K}\|_{L^{\infty}}\lesssim 1
by assumption and from
\|H_{\alpha}(v^{2})\|_{L^{\infty}}\lesssim\|v\|_{X^{\alpha}}^{2}
, we deduce the claimed estimate for
\partial_{x}\mathbf{T}^{(\sigma)}_{\lambda}[v](x)
.
∎

Lemma 4.3
.

The map

\mathbf{T}^{(\sigma)}_{\lambda}:X_{\alpha}\setminus\{0\}\to X_{\alpha}

is continuous and locally compact in the sense that for every
v\in X_{\alpha}\setminus\{0\}
and
0<\varepsilon<\|v\|_{L^{2}}
the image
\mathbf{T}^{(\sigma)}_{\lambda}(B_{\varepsilon}(v))
is relatively compact in
X_{\alpha}
.

Proof.

Let
\lambda>0
and
\sigma\in\mathbb{R}
be given. We divide the proof into the following steps.

Local Compactness.
Let
v\in X_{\alpha}\setminus\{0\}
be given assume that
0<\varepsilon<\|v\|_{L^{2}}
. Then
B_{\varepsilon}(v)\subset X_{\alpha}\setminus\{0\}
and
\|w\|_{L^{2}}\geq\|v\|_{L^{2}}-\varepsilon>0
for any
w\in B_{\varepsilon}(v)
. From Proposition
4.1
we deduce the uniform pointwise bounds

|\mathbf{T}^{(\sigma)}_{\lambda}[w](x)|\leq Ce^{-d|x|^{2\alpha}}\quad\mbox{and}\quad|\partial_{x}\mathbf{T}^{(\sigma)}_{\lambda}[w](x)|\leq C\quad\mbox{for $x\in\mathbb{R}$ and $w\in B_{\varepsilon}(v)$}

with some constants
C>0
and
d>0
. Applying Lemma
A.2
below, we conclude that the image
\mathbf{T}^{(\sigma)}_{\lambda}(B_{\varepsilon}(v))
is relatively compact in
X_{\alpha}
.

Continuity.
Suppose
(v_{k})_{k\geq 1}\subset X_{\alpha}\setminus\{0\}
is a sequence such that
v_{k}\to v
in
X_{\alpha}
with some
v\in X_{\alpha}\setminus\{0\}
. Since
H_{\alpha}(v_{k}^{2})\to H_{\alpha}(v^{2})
in
L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
, it follows from dominated convergence that
\int_{0}^{x}H_{\alpha}(v_{k}^{2})(y)\,dy\to\int_{0}^{x}H_{\alpha}(v^{2})(y)\,dy
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
. Hence we have the pointwise convergence

\mathbf{T}^{(\sigma)}_{\lambda}[v_{k}](x)\to\mathbf{T}^{(\sigma)}_{\lambda}[v](x)\quad\mbox{for a.\,e.\penalty\ $x\in\mathbb{R}$}.

On the other hand, by local compactness shown above, we deduce that
(\mathbf{T}^{(\sigma)}_{\lambda}[v_{k_{j}}])_{j\geq 1}
is strongly convergent in
X_{\alpha}
after passing to a subsequence. But from above we deduce that the limit must be
\mathbf{T}^{(\sigma)}_{\lambda}[v](x)
and hence independent of the chosen subsequence. This shows that
\mathbf{T}^{(\sigma)}_{\lambda}[v_{k}]\to\mathbf{T}^{(\sigma)}_{\lambda}[v]
in
X_{\alpha}
.
∎

If
\sigma\neq 0
, we can strengthen the previous result as follows.

Lemma 4.4
.

If
\sigma\neq 0
, then the map
\mathbf{T}^{(\sigma)}_{\lambda}:X_{\alpha}\to X_{\alpha}
is continuous and compact.

Proof.

Let
(v_{k})_{k\geq 1}\subset X_{\alpha}
be a bounded sequence. Since
\sigma\neq 0
, we deduce from Proposition
4.1
that
|\mathbf{T}^{(\sigma)}_{\lambda}[v_{k}](x)|\leq Ce^{-\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}x^{2}}\leq Ce^{-d|x|^{2\alpha}}
and
|\partial_{x}\mathbf{T}^{(\sigma)}_{\lambda}[v_{k}](x)|\leq C
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
and
k\geq 1
, where
C>0
and
d>0
are some constants independent of
k
. By Lemma
A.2
, there exists a strongly convergent subsequence
(\mathbf{T}^{(\sigma)}_{\lambda}[v_{k_{j}}])_{j\geq 1}
in
X_{\alpha}
. Thus the map
\mathbf{T}^{(\sigma)}_{\lambda}:X_{\alpha}\to X_{\alpha}
is compact, provided that
\sigma\neq 0
.

The continuity of
\mathbf{T}^{(\sigma)}_{\lambda}:X_{\alpha}\to X_{\alpha}
follows in analogous way as in the previous proof.
∎

5.
Existence of Fixed Points

As in the previous section, we set
\alpha=s-\frac{1}{2}\in(0,\frac{1}{2})
and we suppose that
K:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}_{>0}
satisfies Assumption
(A)
. Unless explicitly stated otherwise, we assume that
\lambda>0
and
\sigma\in\mathbb{R}
.

5.1.
A-Priori Bounds and Existence

We first record the important facts that fixed points of the map
\mathbf{T}^{(\sigma)}_{\lambda}
must be symmetric-decreasing functions. For a general background on symmetric-decreasing rearrangement, we refer to
[
20
]
.

Proposition 5.1
.

If
v=\mathbf{T}^{(\sigma)}_{\lambda}[v]
for some
v\in X_{\alpha}
, then
v=v^{*}
is symmetric-decreasing.

Proof.

Let
v\in X_{\alpha}
solve
v=\mathbf{T}^{(\sigma)}_{\lambda}[v]
which clearly implies that
v(x)>0
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
. From Lemma
4.1
we know that
u=\log(K^{-1}v^{2})\in L_{s}(\mathbb{R})
is an even solution of (
1.1
) with
Ke^{u}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
. Since
u
is an integral solution, we can apply Lemma
3.4
to deduce that
u
symmetric-decreasing with respect to some point
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
. Since
u
is even, we must have
x_{0}=0
. Thus
v(x)=\sqrt{K(x)e^{u(x)}}
is symmetric-decreasing, which shows that
v=v^{*}
.
∎

Next, we derive a straightforward a-priori bound for fixed points in the case
\sigma\neq 0
.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
\sigma\neq 0
. Then there exists a constant
M=M(\sigma,\alpha)>0
such that, for all
v\in X_{\alpha}
, we have the implication

\mbox{$v=\beta\mathbf{T}^{(\sigma)}_{\lambda}[v]$ for some $\beta\in[0,1]$}\quad\Rightarrow\quad\|v\|_{X^{\alpha}}<\lambda\|K\|_{L^{\infty}}^{1/2}M.

Proof.

Let
\sigma\neq 0
and
\lambda>0
be given. Assume that
v\in X_{\alpha}
with
v=\beta\mathbf{T}^{(\sigma)}_{\lambda}[v]
for some
\beta\in[0,1]
. Then

0\leq v(x)=\beta\lambda e^{-\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}x^{2}}\sqrt{K(x)}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{x}H_{\alpha}(v^{2})(y)\,dy}\leq\lambda\|K\|_{L^{\infty}}^{1/2}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}x^{2}}\quad\mbox{for $x\in\mathbb{R}$}.

Here we used that
\sqrt{K}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
by assumption together with
H_{\alpha}(v^{2})(y)\geq 0
for
y\geq 0
and
H_{\alpha}(v^{2})(y)\leq 0
for
y\leq 0
by Lemma
3.1
, since
v=v^{*}
is symmetric-decreasing. Hence
\|v\|_{X^{\alpha}}\leq\lambda\|K\|_{L^{\infty}}^{1/2}\|e^{-\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}x^{2}}\|_{X^{\alpha}}<\lambda\|K\|_{L^{\infty}}^{1/2}M
with some constant
M=M(\sigma,\alpha)>0
.
∎

Corollary 5.1
.

For all
\sigma\neq 0
and
\lambda>0
, there exists a fixed point
v\in X_{\alpha}
of the map
\mathbf{T}^{(\sigma)}_{\lambda}:X_{\alpha}\to X_{\alpha}
.

Proof.

Since
\mathbf{T}^{(\sigma)}_{\lambda}:X_{\alpha}\to X_{\alpha}
is continuous and compact when
\sigma\neq 0
, the result follow from the a-priori bound in Lemma
5.1
and the classical Schaefer–Schauder fixed point theorem; see, e. g.
[
15
]
[Theorem 11.3].
∎

Next, we turn to the more delicate case when
\sigma=0
holds. Here the simple a-priori bound in Lemma
5.1
breaks down in general, since we see that
M\to+\infty
as
\sigma\to 0
. Moreover, we recall that the map
\mathbf{T}_{\lambda}=\mathbf{T}_{\lambda}^{(0)}
is only locally compact and it is only defined on the non-convex set
X_{\alpha}\setminus\{0\}
. Thus we need a more sophisticated analysis to deduce existence of fixed point of the map
\mathbf{T}_{\lambda}
.

Indeed, we have the following bound on fixed points for
\mathbf{T}^{(\sigma)}_{\lambda}
independent of
\sigma\in\mathbb{R}
. As interesting aside, we remark that the proof involves the
reverse Hardy–Littlewood–Sobolev inequality
, where the so-called conformally invariant case is sufficient for our purposes.

Lemma 5.2
.

Every fixed point
v\in X_{\alpha}
of
\mathbf{T}^{(\sigma)}_{\lambda}
satisfies the bound

\|v\|_{L^{p}}\leq C(\lambda,p,\|K\|_{L^{\infty}})\quad\mbox{for}\quad\mbox{$\frac{2}{1+\alpha}\leq p\leq\infty$},

with some constant
C=C(\lambda,p,\|K\|_{L^{\infty}})>0
independent of
\sigma\in\mathbb{R}
.

Remark 5.1
.

Note that
\frac{4}{3}<\frac{2}{1+\alpha}<2
which follows from
0<\alpha<\frac{1}{2}
. The fact that we obtain
L^{p}
-bounds for fixed points
v
also for
p<2
will become useful further below, when showing existence of fixed points for the map
\mathbf{T}_{\lambda}=\mathbf{T}^{(0)}_{\lambda}
.

Proof.

Let
\sigma\in\mathbb{R}
and
\lambda>0
be given. Suppose that
v\in X_{\alpha}
solves
v=\mathbf{T}^{(\sigma)}_{\lambda}[v]
. We recall that
v
is
C^{1}
and solves the equation

(5.1)

\partial_{x}v=Wv-\sigma^{2}xv-\frac{1}{2}H_{\alpha}\left(v^{2}\right)v,

where we set
W:=\frac{1}{2}\partial_{x}\log K
. Multiplying both sides with
-xv
, followed by using that
(\partial_{x}v)v=\frac{1}{2}\partial_{x}\left(v^{2}\right)
and integration over
\mathbb{R}
, we find

(5.2)

-\int_{\mathbb{R}}x\partial_{x}(v^{2})\,dx=-\int_{\mathbb{R}}Wxv^{2}\,dx+2\sigma^{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}x^{2}v^{2}\,dx+\int_{\mathbb{R}}xv^{2}H_{\alpha}(v^{2})\,dx.

We remark that
|v(x)|\leq Ce^{-d|x|^{2\alpha}}
with some constants
C,d>0
, which is easily shows that the integrals are absolutely convergent. Next, we observe that

\sigma^{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}x^{2}v^{2}\,dx\geq 0\quad\mbox{and}\quad-\!\int_{\mathbb{R}}Wxv^{2}\,dx\geq 0,

since
-Wx=-\frac{1}{2}(\partial_{x}\log K)x=-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial_{x}K}{K}x\geq 0
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
due to the fact that
K=K(x)>0
is even and monotone decreasing in
|x|
. Since
-\int_{\mathbb{R}}x\partial_{x}(v^{2})=\int_{\mathbb{R}}v^{2}
, we have shown that

(5.3)

\int_{\mathbb{R}}v^{2}\,dx\geq\int_{\mathbb{R}}xv^{2}H_{\alpha}(v^{2})\,dx.

Let us now introduce the function
\rho:=v^{2}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})\cap L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
. For the term on the right-hand side in the inequality above, we calculate (with the positive constant
d_{\alpha}>0
):

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}x\rho(x)H_{\alpha}(\rho)(x)\,dx

\displaystyle=d_{\alpha}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\!\int_{\mathbb{R}}\rho(x)x\frac{x-y}{|x-y|^{2-2\alpha}}\rho(y)\,dx\,dy

\displaystyle=d_{\alpha}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\!\int_{\mathbb{R}}\rho(x)(x-y+y)\frac{x-y}{|x-y|^{2-2\alpha}}\rho(y)\,dx\,dy

\displaystyle=d_{\alpha}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\!\int_{\mathbb{R}}\rho(x)|x-y|^{2\alpha}\rho(y)\,dx\,dy

\displaystyle\quad+d_{\alpha}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\!\int_{\mathbb{R}}\rho(x)y\frac{x-y}{|x-y|^{2-2\alpha}}\rho(y)\,dx\,dy.

By interchanging
x
and
y
in the last integral, we arrive at the identity

(5.4)

\int_{\mathbb{R}}x\rho(x)H_{\alpha}(\rho)(x)\,dx=\frac{d_{\alpha}}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\!\int_{\mathbb{R}}\rho(x)|x-y|^{2\alpha}\rho(y)\,dx\,dy.

In view of (
5.3
), we thus obtain the estimate

(5.5)

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\rho(x)\,dx\gtrsim\int_{\mathbb{R}}\!\int_{\mathbb{R}}\rho(x)|x-y|^{2\alpha}\rho(y)\,dx\,dy.

To proceed further, we make use of the
reverse Hardy–Littlewood–Sobolev (HLS) inequality
stated in (
2.6
) above. In fact, we shall only need the following so-called conformally invariant case of the reverse HLS-inequality, which in our case reads

(5.6)

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\!\int_{\mathbb{R}}\rho(x)|x-y|^{2\alpha}\rho(y)\,dx\,dy\gtrsim\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}\rho(x)^{q}\,dx\right)^{2/q}\quad\mbox{with}\quad q=\frac{1}{1+\alpha}\in(0,1).

Next, we use that
q\in(0,1)
and
0\leq\rho(x)\leq\|\rho\|_{L^{\infty}}
to find

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(\frac{\rho(x)}{\|\rho\|_{L^{\infty}}}\right)^{q}\,dx\geq\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{\rho(x)}{\|\rho\|_{L^{\infty}}}\,dx.

Combining (
5.5
) with (
5.6
) and recalling that
2/q=2+2\alpha
and
1-q=\frac{\alpha}{1+\alpha}
, we conclude

(5.7)

\|\rho\|_{L^{\infty}}^{\frac{\alpha}{1+\alpha}}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}\rho(x)^{q}\,dx\right)\gtrsim\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}\rho(x)^{q}\,dx\right)^{2+2\alpha}

Since
2+2\alpha>1
, we deduce that

(5.8)

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\rho(x)^{q}\,dx\lesssim\|\rho\|_{L^{\infty}}^{\frac{\alpha}{1+\alpha}\cdot\frac{1}{1+2\alpha}}.

Note that
\rho^{q}=v^{2q}=v^{\frac{2}{1+\alpha}}
and we also have
\|\rho\|_{L^{\infty}}=\|v\|_{L^{\infty}}^{2}=v(0)^{2}
because
v=v^{*}
is symmetric-decreasing. From the fixed point equation we see that
v(0)^{2}=\lambda^{2}K(0)=\lambda^{2}\|K\|_{L^{\infty}}
, where the last equality holds since
K
is symmetric-decreasing. In summary, we conclude hat

(5.9)

\|v\|_{L^{\frac{2}{1+\alpha}}}\lesssim(\lambda\|K\|_{L^{\infty}}^{\frac{1}{2}})^{\frac{\alpha}{1+2\alpha}}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\|v\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq\lambda\|K\|_{L^{\infty}}^{\frac{1}{2}}.

The claimed bound for
\|v\|_{L^{p}}
now follows from simple interpolation using Hölder’s inequality.
∎

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
\lambda>0
be given. Suppose that
\sigma_{n}\neq 0
is a sequence such that
\sigma_{n}\to 0
and let
(v_{n})_{n\geq 1}\subset X_{\alpha}
be a sequence of fixed points of
\mathbf{T}_{\lambda}^{(\sigma_{n})}
. Then, after passing to a subsequence, it holds that
v_{n}\to v
in
X_{\alpha}
and
v\not\equiv 0
is a fixed point of
\mathbf{T}_{\lambda}^{(0)}
.

Proof.

We divide the proof into the following steps.

Step 1.
From the uniform bounds
\|v_{n}\|_{L^{p}}
for
p\in[\frac{2}{1+\alpha},\infty]
derived in Lemma
5.2
and the fact that the
v_{n}=v_{n}^{*}
are symmetric-decreasing functions, we infer from Lemma
A.3
(after passing to a subsequence) that

(5.10)

v_{n}\to v
in
L^{p}(\mathbb{R})
for any
\frac{2}{1+\alpha}<p<\infty
and
v_{n}\to v
almost everywhere in
\mathbb{R}

with some symmetric-decreasing function
v=v^{*}
with
0\leq v(x)\leq\lambda\sqrt{K(0)}
for a. e.
x\in\mathbb{R}
. We claim that

e^{-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{x}H_{\alpha}(v_{n}^{2})(y)dy}\to e^{-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{x}H_{\alpha}(v^{2})(y)\,dy}\quad\mbox{for almost every $x\in\mathbb{R}$}.

To see this, we note that
\|H_{\alpha}(v_{n}^{2})\|_{L^{\infty}}\lesssim\|v_{n}^{2}\|_{L^{1}\cap L^{\infty}}=\|v_{n}\|_{L^{2}\cap L^{\infty}}\lesssim 1
. Thus, by dominated convergence,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{0}^{x}H_{\alpha}(v_{n}^{2})(y)dy=\int_{0}^{x}\lim_{n\to\infty}H_{\alpha}(v_{n}^{2})(y)dy=\int_{0}^{x}H_{\alpha}(v^{2})(y)dy

for every
x\in\mathbb{R}
, where in the second step we use that
H_{\alpha}(v_{n}^{2})(y)\to H_{\alpha}(v^{2})(y)
for a. e.
y\in\mathbb{R}
, which follows from
v_{n}\to v
in
L^{p}
for any
p\in(2,\infty)
and the properties of
H_{\alpha}
.

In the equation

(5.11)

v_{n}(x)=\lambda\sqrt{K(x)}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\sigma_{n}^{2}x^{2}}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{x}H_{\alpha}(v_{n}^{2})(y)dy}

we can pass to the limit
n\to\infty
on both sides of the equation to conclude that the limit
v=v^{*}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})\cap L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
satisfies

(5.12)

v(x)=\lambda\sqrt{K(x)}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{x}H_{\alpha}(v^{2})(y)dy}\quad\mbox{for almost every $x\in\mathbb{R}$}.

In particular, this equation shows that
v\not\equiv 0
.

Step 2.
We demonstrate that there exist constants
C,d>0
such that

(5.13)

|v_{n}(x)|\leq Ce^{-d|x|^{2\alpha}},\quad|\partial_{x}v_{n}(x)|\leq C\quad\mbox{for all $x\in\mathbb{R}$ and $n\geq 1$}.

To this end, we consider the sequence of functions

w_{n}(x)=-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{x}H_{\alpha}(v_{n}^{2})(y)\,dy=-c_{\alpha}\int_{\mathbb{R}}(|x-y|^{2\alpha}-|y|^{2\alpha})v_{n}(y)^{2}\,dy.

Since
v_{n}\to v
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
with
v\not\equiv 0
, there exists a constant
c>0
and
n_{0}\geq 1
so that

\int_{\mathbb{R}}v_{n}(y)^{2}\,dy\geq c\quad\mbox{for all $n\geq n_{0}$}.

Moreover, by the uniform decay estimate
v_{n}(x)^{2}\lesssim|x|^{-2/p}
with
p=\frac{2}{1+\alpha}<2
, we find that the sequence
(v_{n})
is
tight
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
, i. e., for every
\varepsilon>0
there exists
M>0
such that

\int_{|y|>M}v_{n}(y)^{2}\,dy\leq\varepsilon\quad\mbox{for all $n\geq 1$}.

Next, we argue as in the proof of Lemma
3.3
. We can take some
M>0
such that

\int_{|y|>M}v_{n}(y)^{2}\,dy\leq\frac{c}{4}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\int_{|y|\leq M}v_{n}(y)^{2}\,dy\geq\frac{3c}{4}\quad\mbox{for all $n\geq n_{0}$}.

Let
\delta>0
be sufficiently small such that
|1-t|^{2\alpha}-|t|^{2\alpha}\geq\frac{1}{2}
for
|t|\leq\delta
. Thus if we define
R:=\delta^{-1}M>0
, we obtain

|x-y|^{2\alpha}-|y|^{2\alpha}\geq\frac{1}{2}|x|^{2\alpha}\quad\mbox{for $|y|\leq M$ and $|x|\geq R$}.

Using that
v_{n}^{2}\geq 0
and the general bound
|x-y|^{2\alpha}-|y|^{2\alpha}\geq-|x|^{2\alpha}
, we deduce

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}(|x-y|^{2\alpha}-|y|^{2\alpha})v_{n}(y)^{2}\,dy\geq\frac{1}{2}|x|^{2\alpha}\int_{|y|\leq M}v_{n}(y)^{2}\,dy-|x|^{2\alpha}\int_{|y|>M}v_{n}(y)^{2}\,dy

\displaystyle\geq\frac{3c}{8}|x|^{2\alpha}-\frac{c}{4}|x|^{2\alpha}=\frac{c}{8}|x|^{2\alpha}\quad\mbox{for $|x|\geq R$}.

Choosing now
d=c_{\alpha}\cdot\frac{c}{8}>0
, we obtain the bound

w_{n}(x)\leq-d|x|^{2\alpha}\quad\mbox{for $|x|\geq R$ and $n\geq n_{0}$}.

Therefore,

0<v_{n}(x)=\lambda\sqrt{K(x)}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\sigma_{n}^{2}x^{2}}e^{w_{n}(x)}\leq\lambda\|K\|_{L^{\infty}}^{1/2}e^{-d|x|^{2\alpha}}\quad\mbox{for $|x|\geq R$ and $n\geq n_{0}$}.

Since also
0<v_{n}(x)\leq v_{n}(0)=\lambda\sqrt{K(0)}=\lambda\|K\|_{L^{\infty}}^{1/2}
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
, we readily deduce that the first estimate in (
5.13
) holds for some sufficiently large constant
C>0
independent of
n
. Also, from the general estimate in Proposition
4.1
, we deduce

|\partial_{x}v_{n}(x)|\leq Ce^{-d|x|^{2\alpha}}(\sigma_{n}^{2}|x|+1)\leq C\quad\mbox{for all $x\in\mathbb{R}$ and $n\geq 1$}

with some constant
C>0
independent of
n
. This proves (
5.13
).

By Lemma
A.2
, we infer (after passing to a subsequence) that
v_{n}\to v
in
X_{\alpha}
and
v\not\equiv 0
is a fixed point of
\mathbf{T}_{\lambda}=\mathbf{T}_{\lambda}^{(0)}
thanks to (
5.12
). The proof of Lemma
5.3
is now complete.
∎

6.
Uniqueness of Fixed Points

We now turn to the uniqueness of fixed points of
\mathbf{T}^{(\sigma)}_{\lambda}
for given
\lambda>0
and
\sigma\in\mathbb{R}
. Again, we start with the easier case when
\sigma\neq 0
holds and we first show uniqueness in the regime of small
\lambda>0
.

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
\sigma\neq 0
be given. Then there exists
\lambda_{0}=\lambda_{0}(\sigma,\alpha,\|K\|_{L^{\infty}})>0
sufficiently small such that the fixed points of
\mathbf{T}^{(\sigma)}_{\lambda}:X_{\alpha}\to X_{\alpha}
are unique for
\lambda\in(0,\lambda_{0}]
.

Proof.

Let
\sigma\neq 0
be fixed. In Section
A
we prove that
\mathbf{T}^{(\sigma)}_{\lambda}:X_{\alpha}\to X_{\alpha}
is Fréchet differentiable and that its derivative
D_{v}\mathbf{T}^{(\sigma)}_{\lambda}[v]\in\mathcal{L}(X_{\alpha})
depends continuously on
(\lambda,v)\in(0,\infty)\times X_{\alpha}
. Since
D_{v}\mathbf{T}^{(\sigma)}_{\lambda}[0]=0
and by continuity, we deduce that

\|D_{v}\mathbf{T}^{(\sigma)}_{\lambda}[v]\|_{\mathcal{L}(X_{\alpha})}\leq\frac{1}{2}\quad\mbox{if}\quad\mbox{$\|v\|_{X^{\alpha}}\leq r_{0}$ and $0<\lambda\leq\lambda_{0}$}

with some sufficiently small constants
r_{0}>0
and
\lambda_{0}>0
.
From the mean-value estimate due to Taylor’s theorem in Banach spaces, we deduce

(6.1)

\|\mathbf{T}^{(\sigma)}_{\lambda}[v]-\mathbf{T}^{(\sigma)}_{\lambda}[\tilde{v}]\|_{X^{\alpha}}\leq\frac{1}{2}\|v-\tilde{v}\|_{X^{\alpha}}\quad\mbox{if}\quad\mbox{$\|v\|_{X^{\alpha}},\|\tilde{v}\|_{X^{\alpha}}\leq r_{0}$ and $0<\lambda\leq\lambda_{0}$}.

Now, let
M=M(\sigma,\alpha)>0
be the constant from Lemma
5.1
. By choosing
\lambda_{0}>0
even smaller, we can ensure that
\lambda_{0}\|K\|_{L^{\infty}}^{1/2}M\leq r_{0}
holds. Thus if
\lambda\in(0,\lambda_{0}]
and
v,\tilde{v}\in X_{\alpha}
are fixed points of
\mathbf{T}^{(\sigma)}_{\lambda}
, we deduce that
\|v\|_{X^{\alpha}}\leq r_{0}
and
\|\tilde{v}\|_{X^{\alpha}}\leq r_{0}
by Lemma
5.1
. The contraction estimate (
6.1
) now implies that
v=\tilde{v}
.
∎

Our next goal is to extend the previous uniqueness result to all
\lambda>0
by means of a global continuation argument using the implicit function theorem. To this end, we introduce the following map

\mathbf{F}:(0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}\times(X_{\alpha}\setminus\{0\})\to X_{\alpha},\quad(\lambda,\sigma,v)\mapsto\mathbf{F}(\lambda,\sigma,v):=v-\mathbf{T}^{(\sigma)}_{\lambda}[v].

Obviously, we have the equivalence

\mathbf{F}(\lambda,\sigma,v)=0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad v=\mathbf{T}^{(\sigma)}_{\lambda}[v].

From the known properties of
\mathbf{T}^{(\sigma)}_{\lambda}
, we directly deduce that
\mathbf{F}
is Fréchet differentiable with respect to
v
and its derivative is given by

D_{v}\mathbf{F}(\lambda,\sigma,v)=\mathrm{Id}-D_{v}\mathbf{T}^{(\sigma)}_{\lambda}[v]

and it depends continuously on
(\lambda,\sigma,v)
. We note that
D_{v}\mathbf{F}(\lambda,\sigma,v):X_{\alpha}\to X_{\alpha}
is Fredholm, since
D_{v}\mathbf{T}^{(\sigma)}_{\lambda}[v]
is a compact linear operator. The following general nondegeneracy result for the linearization around fixed points is essential.

Lemma 6.2

(Nondegeneracy of Fixed Points)
.

Let
\sigma\in\mathbb{R}
and
\lambda>0
. Suppose that
v\in X_{\alpha}
satisfies
\mathbf{F}(\lambda,\sigma,v)=0
. Then
D_{v}\mathbf{F}(\lambda,\sigma,v):X_{\alpha}\to X_{\alpha}
is invertible.

Proof.

Let
(\lambda,\sigma,v)
be as above. By standard Fredholm theory, we know that
D_{v}\mathbf{F}(\lambda,\sigma,v)
is invertible if (and only if) the compact operator
\mathcal{K}:=D_{v}\mathbf{T}^{(\sigma)}_{\lambda}[v]\in\mathcal{L}(X_{\alpha})
satisfies

(6.2)

\mathrm{ker}(\mathrm{Id}-\mathcal{K})=\{0\}.

To show this, we argue by contradiction and we suppose that there exists some
f\in X_{\alpha}\setminus\{0\}
with
\mathcal{K}f=f
. Since
v=\mathbf{T}^{(\sigma)}_{\lambda}[v]
is a fixed point, we deduce from Proposition
A.1
that
f
satisfies the equation

(6.3)

f(x)=-v(x)\int_{0}^{x}H_{\alpha}(vf)(y)\,dy.

We claim that this identity will imply that
f\equiv 0
, completing the proof of (
6.2
).

Indeed, let us consider the quantity

(6.4)

I[f]:=\int_{0}^{\infty}(vf)(x)H_{\alpha}(vf)(x)\,dx.

Note that this integral is absolutely convergent with

|I[f]|\lesssim\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{|v(x)f(x)||v(y)f(y)|}{|x-y|^{1-2\alpha}}\,dx\,dy\lesssim\|vf\|_{L^{\frac{2}{1+2\alpha}}}^{2}<+\infty,

using the Hardy–Littlewood–Sobolev inequality and that
vf\in L^{p}(\mathbb{R})
for all
1\leq p\leq\infty
whenever
f,v\in X_{\alpha}
. We claim that

(6.5)

I[f]\geq 0\quad\mbox{with}\quad\mbox{$I[f]=0$ if and only if $f=0$}.

To see this, let us set

w:=vf.

From the explicit form of the integral kernel of
H_{\alpha}
we find (with some positive constant
d_{\alpha}>0
) that

\displaystyle I[f]

\displaystyle=d_{\alpha}\int_{0}^{\infty}w(x)\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{\mathrm{sgn}(x-y)}{|x-y|^{1-2\alpha}}w(y)\,dy\right)dx

\displaystyle=d_{\alpha}\int_{0}^{\infty}w(x)\left(\int_{-\infty}^{0}\frac{\mathrm{sgn}(x-y)}{|x-y|^{1-2\alpha}}w(y)\,dy+\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\mathrm{sgn}(x-y)}{|x-y|^{1-2\alpha}}w(y)\,dy\right)dx

\displaystyle=d_{\alpha}\int_{0}^{\infty}w(x)\int_{-\infty}^{0}\frac{\mathrm{sgn}(x-y)}{|x-y|^{1-2\alpha}}w(y)\,dy\,dx=d_{\alpha}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{w(x)w(y)}{(x+y)^{1-2\alpha}}\,dx\,dy.

Here we used that the second integral in the second line vanishes by skew-symmetry. In the last step, we used that
w(-y)=w(y)
is an even function. Next, we recall that

\frac{1}{(x+y)^{1-2\alpha}}=\frac{1}{\Gamma(2\alpha+1)}\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{2\alpha}e^{-t(x+y)}\,dt

for
x,y>0
. This leads us to

I[f]=d_{\alpha}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{w(x)w(y)}{(x+y)^{1-2\alpha}}\,dx\,dy=\frac{d_{\alpha}}{\Gamma(2\alpha+1)}\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{2\alpha}|\mathsf{L}w(t)|^{2}\,dt\geq 0,

where
\mathsf{L}w(t)=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-ts}w(s)\,ds
denotes the (one-sided) Laplace transform of
w:[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
. This shows that
I[f]\geq 0
and clearly
I[0]=0
. Moreover, if
I[f]=0
then
\mathsf{L}w(t)=0
for a. e.
t\geq 0
. Since
w=vf:[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
is easily seen to be continuous and bounded, we can apply well-known arguments for the Laplace transform (stated in Lemma
A.5
below) to conclude that
w=vf=0
. By the strict positivity
v(x)>0
, this implies that
f=0
. This completes the proof of (
6.5
).

On the other hand, we claim that

(6.6)

I[f]\leq 0.

To see this, we mutliply (
6.3
) with
v(x)>0
yielding

v(x)f(x)=v(x)^{2}\psi(x),

where we define the
C^{1}
-function

\psi(x)=-\int_{0}^{x}H_{\alpha}(vf)(y)\,dy,

which satisfies the estimate
|\psi(x)|\leq|x|\|H_{\alpha}(vf)\|_{L^{\infty}}\lesssim|x|\|vf\|_{L^{1}\cap L^{\infty}}\lesssim|x|\|v\|_{X^{\alpha}}\|f\|_{X^{\alpha}}
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
. We have

\displaystyle I[f]

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}(vf)(x)H_{\alpha}(vf)(x)\,dx=-\int_{0}^{\infty}v(x)^{2}\psi(x)\partial_{x}\psi(x)\,dx=-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}v(x)^{2}\partial_{x}\left\{\psi(x)^{2}\right\}\,dx

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}v(0)^{2}\psi(0)^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\partial_{x}\{v(x)^{2}\}\psi(x)^{2}\,dx=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\partial_{x}\left\{v(x)^{2}\right\}\psi(x)^{2}\,dx,

where we integrated by parts using that
\psi(0)=0
and that
v(x)^{2}\psi(x)\to 0
as
x\to+\infty
due to
v(x)\leq Ce^{-d|x|^{2\alpha}}
with some
d>0
and the fact that
\psi(x)=O(x)
as
|x|\to+\infty
. Since
v=v^{*}
is a symmetric-decreasing
C^{1}
-function, we see that
\partial_{x}(v(x)^{2})\leq 0
for all
x\geq 0
. Hence we conclude that (
6.6
) holds.

In view of (
6.5
) and (
6.6
), we deduce that
\mathcal{K}f=f
implies that
I[f]=0
and hence
f=0
. This proves (
6.2
) and completes the proof of Lemma
6.2
.
∎

We are now ready to prove the following uniqueness result for fixed points of
\mathbf{T}^{(\sigma)}_{\lambda}
.

Lemma 6.3
.

For all
\sigma\in\mathbb{R}
and
\lambda>0
, we have uniqueness of fixed points for
\mathbf{T}^{(\sigma)}_{\lambda}
in
X_{\alpha}
.

Proof.

We discuss the cases
\sigma\neq 0
and
\sigma=0
separately as follows.

Case
\sigma\neq 0
.
Assume that
\sigma\neq 0
is fixed and let
\lambda_{*}>0
be given. We argue by contradiction and we suppose that
v_{*},\tilde{v}_{*}\in X_{\alpha}
are two fixed points of
\mathbf{T}_{\lambda_{*}}^{(\sigma)}
with
v_{*}\neq\tilde{v}_{*}
. Since
\mathbf{F}(\lambda_{*},\sigma,v_{*})=\mathbf{F}(\lambda_{*},\sigma,\tilde{v}_{*})=0
, we can apply the implicit function theorem (using the nondegeneracy result in Lemma
6.2
) together with the a-priori bound in Lemma
5.1
to conclude that there exist two continuous branches
v(\lambda),\tilde{v}(\lambda)\in C((0,\lambda_{*}];X_{\alpha})
with

v(\lambda)=\mathbf{T}_{\lambda}^{(\sigma)}[v(\lambda)]\quad\mbox{and}\quad\tilde{v}(\lambda)=\mathbf{T}_{\lambda}^{(\sigma)}[\tilde{v}(\lambda)]\quad\mbox{for $\lambda\in(0,\lambda_{*}]$}

with
v(\lambda_{*})=v_{*}
and
\tilde{v}(\lambda_{*})=\tilde{v}_{*}
. By local uniqueness thanks to the implicit function theorem, these branches can never intersect, i. e., we have
v(\lambda)\neq\tilde{v}(\lambda)
for
\lambda\in(0,\lambda_{*}]
. However, by Lemma
6.1
, there exists a sufficiently small constant
\lambda_{0}>0
such that
v(\lambda)=\tilde{v}(\lambda)
for
\lambda\in(0,\lambda_{0}]
. This is a contradiction and completes the proof for the case
\sigma\neq 0
.

Case
\sigma=0
.
Let
\lambda>0
be given and suppose
v,\tilde{v}\in X_{\alpha}
are two fixed points of
\mathbf{T}_{\lambda}=\mathbf{T}_{\lambda}^{(0)}
such that
v\neq\tilde{v}
. By the implicit function theorem and the nondegeneracy result in Lemma
6.2
, we can construct two continuous branches
v(\sigma),\tilde{v}(\sigma)\in C((-\varepsilon,\varepsilon);X_{\alpha})
with some
\varepsilon>0
such that

v(\sigma)=\mathbf{T}_{\lambda}^{(\sigma)}[v(\sigma)]\quad\mbox{and}\quad\tilde{v}(\sigma)=\mathbf{T}_{\lambda}^{(\sigma)}[\tilde{v}(\sigma)]\quad\mbox{for $\sigma\in(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)$},

with
v(0)=v
and
\tilde{v}(0)=\tilde{v}
. Now let
\sigma_{n}\neq 0
be a sequence such that
\sigma_{n}\to 0
. By the uniqueness result shown above for
\sigma\neq 0
, we conclude that
v(\sigma_{n})=\tilde{v}(\sigma_{n})
for all
n\geq 1
. On the other hand, since
v(\sigma_{n})\to v
and
\tilde{v}(\sigma_{n})\to\tilde{v}
in
X_{\alpha}
, we deduce that
v\neq\tilde{v}
cannot hold.

The proof of Lemma
6.3
is now complete.
∎

Proof of Theorem

Let

). By Theorem

7.
Finite Morse Index and Nondegeneracy

7.1.
Proof of Proposition
1.1

Let
s\in(\frac{1}{2},1)
and suppose that
u\in L_{s}(\mathbb{R})
solves
(-\Delta)^{s}u=e^{u}
with
e^{u}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
. Let
\mathcal{L}=(-\Delta)^{s}-e^{u}
denote the linearized operator viewed as a quadratic form on
C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R})
. Recalling Theorem
1.4
, we know that its Morse index satisfies
n_{-}(\mathcal{L})\geq 1
.

We claim that
n_{-}(\mathcal{L})<+\infty
holds. [In fact, we obtain a quantitative bound below.] From the decay estimate in Lemma
3.3
and the fact that
u
is continuous (and thus locally bounded), it follows that
|u(x)|\leq Ce^{-a|x|^{2\alpha}}
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
with some constants
a>0
and
C>0
. Let us define
V:=e^{u}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})\cap L^{1}(\mathbb{R};|x|^{2\alpha}\,dx)
and consider the self-adjoint Schrödinger operator

H:=(-\Delta)^{s}-V

acting on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
with operator domain
H^{2s}(\mathbb{R})
. From
[
3
]
[Theorem 1.1] we deduce that its number of strictly negative eigenvalues
N_{<0}(H)
satisfies the bound

N_{<0}(H)\leq C_{s}(\||x|^{2\alpha}V\|_{L^{1}}+1)<+\infty

with some constant
C_{s}>0
. We claim that the Morse index satisfies

n_{-}(\mathcal{L})\leq N_{<0}(H).

[In fact, we can show that we must have equality here, but we only need the upper bound.] We argue by contradiction and suppose that
n_{-}(\mathcal{L})>N_{<0}(H)
holds. Thus there exist linearly independent functions
\varphi_{1},\ldots,\varphi_{N+1}\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R})
with
N=N_{>0}(H)
such that
\mathcal{L}<0
is negative definite on
V=\mathrm{span}\{\varphi_{1},\ldots,\varphi_{N+1}\}
. But by the min-max principle, this implies that
H
has at least
N+1
negative eigenvalues. But this is a contradiction.

Next, we show that the fact that
n_{-}(\mathcal{L})
is finite implies that
u
is stable outside some compact subset
\mathcal{K}\subset\mathbb{R}
. Indeed, let
n=n_{-}(\mathcal{L})\geq 1
and take
V=\mathrm{span}\{\varphi_{1},\ldots,\varphi_{n}\}\subset C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R})
such that
\langle\varphi,\mathcal{L}\varphi\rangle<0
for all
\varphi\in V\setminus\{0\}
. By the maximality of
n=\dim V
, it directly follows that
\langle\psi,\mathcal{L}\psi\rangle\geq 0
for any
\psi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}\setminus\mathcal{K})
with the compact set
\mathcal{K}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}\mathrm{supp}(\varphi_{i})
.

Finally, we assume that
u\in L_{s}(\mathbb{R})
solves
(-\Delta)^{s}u=e^{u}
under the weaker assumption
e^{u}\in L_{\mathrm{loc}}^{1}(\mathbb{R})
. Suppose that
u
is stable outside some compact set
\mathcal{K}\subset\mathbb{R}
. Using the Gagliardo seminorm

[\varphi]_{\dot{H}^{s}}^{2}=\frac{C_{s}}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\!\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{|\varphi(x)-\varphi(y)|^{2}}{|x-y|^{1+2s}}\,dx\,dy=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi(-\Delta)^{s}\varphi\,dx,

we have

[\varphi]_{\dot{H}^{s}}^{2}\geq\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{u}\varphi^{2}\,dx\quad\mbox{for all $\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}\setminus\mathcal{K})$}.

Let
R>0
now be sufficiently large such that
\mathcal{K}\subseteq\overline{B}_{R}=\{|x|\leq R\}
. Next, we take a function
\chi:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
with
\chi^{\prime}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
such that
0\leq\chi\leq 1
with the properties
\chi(x)\equiv 0
for
|x|\leq R
and
\chi(x)\equiv 1
for
|x|\geq 2R
. Furthermore, let
\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R})
with
0\leq\varphi\leq 1
and
\varphi(x)\equiv 1
for
|x|\leq 1
and
\varphi(x)\equiv 0
for
|x|\geq 2
. For
k\geq 1
, we set
\varphi_{k}(x):=\varphi(x/k)
and we consider the sequence of test functions
\psi_{k}\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}\setminus\overline{B}_{R})\subseteq C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}\setminus\mathcal{K})
given by

\psi_{k}(x):=\chi(x)\varphi_{k}(x)
for
k\geq 1
. Applying an elementary fractional Leibniz type estimate in Lemma
A.1
, we find

[\psi_{k}]_{\dot{H}^{s}}\leq C+[\varphi_{k}]_{\dot{H}^{s}}=C+k^{\frac{1}{2}-s}[\varphi]_{\dot{H}^{s}}\leq K

with some constant
K>0
independent of
k\geq 1
. By monotone convergence and the stability of
u
outside the compact set
\mathcal{K}\subset\overline{B}_{R}
, we deduce that

\int_{|x|\geq 2R}e^{u}\,dx=\lim_{k\to\infty}\int_{|x|\geq 2R}e^{u}\psi_{k}^{2}\,dx\leq\sup_{k\geq 1}\,[\psi_{k}]_{\dot{H}^{s}}^{2}<+\infty\,.

Since
\int_{|x|\leq 2R}e^{u}\,dx<+\infty
by assumption, we obtain that
e^{u}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
holds.

This completes the proof of Proposition
1.1
.
∎

7.2.
Proof of Theorem
1.5
(Nondegeneracy)

Let
s\in(\frac{1}{2},1)
and assume that
u\in L_{s}(\mathbb{R})
solves
(-\Delta)^{s}u=e^{u}
with
e^{u}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
. By translation and scaling symmetry, it suffices to consider the case

u(x)=Q_{s}(x)

with the unique smooth, even and monotone-decreasing function
Q_{s}
from Theorem
1.3
.

Let
\mathcal{L}=(-\Delta)^{s}-e^{u}
denote the linearized operator acting on

W_{s}=\{\psi\in L_{s}(\mathbb{R}):\mbox{$\psi(x)=O(|x|^{2\alpha})$ as $|x|\to\infty$}\}.

We recall that
\mathcal{L}T=\mathcal{L}R=0
with the smooth functions

R=\partial_{x}u,\quad T=x\partial_{x}u+2s.

Since
R=\partial_{x}u=-H_{\alpha}(v^{2})
, we conclude that
R(x)=o(1)
as
|x|\to\infty
and hence
R\in W_{s}
. Furthermore, we see that
T(x)=o(x)
as
|x|\to+\infty
. Applying Proposition
3.1
with
\rho=e^{u}T\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
, we see that
T(x)=O(|x|^{2\alpha})
as
|x|\to\infty
, which implies that
R\in W_{s}
.

It remains to show that

\mathrm{ker}\,\mathcal{L}=\mathrm{span}\{R,T\}.

Indeed, suppose that
\psi\in W_{s}
solves
\mathcal{L}\psi=0
, which we can write as

(-\Delta)^{s}\psi=v^{2}\psi

with
v=\sqrt{e^{u}}
. We decompose
\psi=\psi_{e}+\psi_{o}
into even and odd parts, where it is easy to see that
\psi_{\#}\in L_{s}(\mathbb{R})
with
\psi_{\#}(x)=O(|x|^{2\alpha})
as
|x|\to\infty
, where
\#
either stands for
e
or
o
. Since
v^{2}=e^{u}
is even, we obtain that

(-\Delta)^{s}\psi_{\#}=v^{2}\psi_{\#}.

Note also that
v^{2}\psi_{\#}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})\cap L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
due to the pointwise bound
v^{2}(x)\leq Ce^{-a|x|^{2\alpha}}
with some
a>0
. By Proposition
3.1
(where
B=0
holds due to the assumed growth for
\psi
) and Proposition
3.3
, we deduce that
\psi_{\#}
is
C^{1}
and satisfies the equation

(7.1)

\psi_{\#}(x)=-\int_{0}^{x}H_{\alpha}(v^{2}\psi_{\#})(y)\,dy+\psi_{\#}(0).

We next discuss the cases
\#\in\{e,o\}
separately as follows.

Even Case.
Let
\psi_{e}\in W_{s}
be an even solution of (
7.1
). We claim that

(7.2)

\psi_{e}=\gamma T

for some
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
. Indeed, let us define
h=\psi_{e}-\gamma T
with some
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
. Since
T(0)=2s\neq 0
, we can choose
\gamma
such that
h(0)=0
holds. By linearity of (
7.1
), we deduce
h
solves
h(x)=-\int_{0}^{x}H_{\alpha}(v^{2}h)(y)\,dy
. By multiplying this equation with
v(x)>0
, we see that the function
f:=vh
with
v(x)>0
satisfies

f(x)=-v(x)\int_{0}^{x}H_{\alpha}(vf)(y)\,dy.

From the estimate
0<v(x)\leq Ce^{-a|x|^{2\alpha}}
with some
a>0
, we readily check that
f\in X_{\alpha}
. But since
f(0)=0
, we deduce from the proof of Lemma
6.2
that
f\equiv 0
. By the strict positivity of
v(x)>0
, this implies
h\equiv 0
. This proves that (
7.2
) holds.

Thus we have shown that any even solution
\psi_{e}\in W_{s}
of (
7.1
) must satisfy
\psi_{e}\in\mathrm{span}\{T\}
.

Odd Case.
Assume that
\psi_{o}\in W_{s}
is an odd solution of (
7.1
). We claim that

(7.3)

\psi_{o}\in\mathrm{span}\{R\}.

Suppose that
\psi_{o}\not\equiv 0
, since the claim is trivially true. By odd symmetry, we have
\psi_{o}(0)=0
and using that
v^{2}\psi_{0}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})\cap L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
, we use Proposition
3.3
to conclude

\psi_{o}(x)=-c_{\alpha}\int_{\mathbb{R}}|x-y|^{2\alpha}(v^{2}\psi_{o}(y))\,dy

in view of the fact that
\int_{\mathbb{R}}|y|^{2\alpha}v^{2}(y)\psi_{o}(y)\,dy=0
, since
v^{2}\psi_{o}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R};|x|^{2\alpha}\,dx)
is an odd function (recall that
v^{2}
is even and the decay estimate
|v(x)|\leq Ce^{-d|x|^{2\alpha}}
). Using once again the
\psi_{0}
is odd, we can rewrite the integral equation as

\psi_{0}(x)=c_{\alpha}\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(|x+y|^{2\alpha}-|x-y|^{2\alpha}\right)v(y)^{2}\psi_{o}(y)\,dy.

Multiplying this equation with
v(x)>0
and introducing the function
g(x):=v(x)\psi_{o}(x)
, we deduce

(7.4)

g(x)=\int_{0}^{\infty}a(x,y)g(y)\,dy\quad\mbox{for $x>0$},

with the positive kernel

a(x,y):=c_{\alpha}v(x)\left(|x+y|^{2\alpha}-|x-y|^{2\alpha}\right)v(y)>0\quad\mbox{for $x,y>0$}.

Thanks to the bound
|v(x)|\leq Ce^{-a|x|^{2\alpha}}
, we readily check that
a\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}_{+})
. Thus the linear operator
A:L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+})\to L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
with
A\varphi(x):=\int_{0}^{\infty}a(x,y)\varphi(y)\,dy
is Hilbert–Schmidt and hence compact. Note also that
A=A^{*}
is self-adjoint, since
a(x,y)=a(y,x)
for the real-valued kernel
a(x,y)
.

Since
g=v\psi_{0}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
, we find from (
7.4
) that
Ag=g
holds, i. e.,
g
is an eigenfunction of
A
with eigenvalue
1
. We claim that

(7.5)

g\in\mathrm{span}\{h\}\quad\mbox{with $h:=-vR$}.

Note that the strict positivity
h(x):=-v(x)R(x)>0
for
x>0
, since
v(x)>0
and
R(x)=\partial_{x}u=-H_{\alpha}(v^{2})<0
for
x>0
by Lemma
3.1
and the fact that
v=v^{*}
is symmetric-decreasing. From
\mathcal{L}R=0
we conclude that
Ah=h
by the discussion above.

Next, we claim that

(7.6)

\mbox{$r=1$ is the largest eigenvalue of $A$ and it is simple}.

Indeed, let
r=\max\sigma(A)
be the largest eigenvalue of the compact self-adjoint operator
A:L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+})\to L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
. By applying the Perron–Frobenius type result in Lemma
A.6
, we deduce that
r>0
exists and it is simple and its corresponding eigenfunction satisfies
\pm\psi(x)>0
for a. e.
x>0
. Since
Ah=h
, we must have that
r\geq 1
. Suppose now that
r>1
holds. Since
A=A^{*}
, we see that
A\psi=r\psi
and
Ah=h
implies that
(\psi,h)=0
. But since
\pm\psi(x)>0
and
h(x)>0
for a. e.
x>0
, this is impossible. Hence we can conclude that
r=1
is the largest eigenvalue of
A
and it is simple. In view of
Ag=g
and
Ah=h
, we deduce that (
7.5
) holds. Thus we see that
g=\gamma h
for some
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
. Since
g=v\psi_{o}
and
h=-vR
together with
v(x)>0
, this implies that
\psi_{o}=-\gamma R
, which shows that (
7.3
).

The proof of Theorem
1.5
is now complete.
∎

8.
Generalizations to Nonconstant

In this section, we provide the proof of Theorem
1.6
, which considers solutions

of

(-\Delta)^{s}u=Ke^{u}\quad\mbox{in $\mathbb{R}$}

with

. Moreover, we assume that

holds, since the case of constant

has already been covered by the previous discussion.

8.1.
Proof of Theorem
1.6

We divide the proof into the following steps.

(i) Existence.
Let
\lambda>0
be given and consider the map
\mathbf{T}_{\lambda}:X_{\alpha}\setminus\{0\}\to X_{\alpha}
defined in (
4.3
). By Corollary
5.2
, there exists a fixed point

of
\mathbf{T}_{\lambda}
. We see that
v(x)>0
is a

-solution of

\partial_{x}v=\frac{1}{2}(\partial_{x}\log K)v-\frac{1}{2}H_{\alpha}(v^{2})v

with

. From applying Lemma
4.1
and Proposition
3.1
, we obtain that the even function

solves
(-\Delta)^{s}u=Ke^{u}
with
Ke^{u}=v^{2}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
.

(ii) Regularity.
Let
u\in L_{s}(\mathbb{R})
solve (
8.1
). By trivially adapting the proof of Theorem
1.1
, we find that
u\in C_{\mathrm{loc}}^{\gamma}(\mathbb{R})
with
\gamma=2\alpha=2s-1>0
. Since
K
is
C^{1}
, this implies that
Ke^{u}\in C_{\mathrm{loc}}^{\gamma}(\mathbb{R})
. From Lemma
3.2
it follows that
u\in C_{\mathrm{loc}}^{\gamma+2s}(\mathbb{R})
.

(iii) Symmetry.
Assume that
u\in L_{s}(\mathbb{R})
solves (
8.1
) with
u(x)=O(|x|^{2\alpha})
as
|x|\to\infty
. From Lemma
3.4
we deduce that

u
is an even function that is monotone-decreasing in
|x|
.

(iv) Uniqueness.
Next, we show that even solutions
u\in L_{s}(\mathbb{R})
of (
8.1
) are uniquely determined by its initial value
u(0)
at the center of symmetry
x=0
. We recall that every even solution
u\in L_{s}(\mathbb{R})
must be integral by Proposition
3.1
, i. e., we have that
B=0
holds there and consequently we havet
u(x)=O(|x|^{2\alpha})
as
|x|\to\infty
.

Suppose now that
u\in L_{s}(\mathbb{R})
is an even solution of (
8.1

4
we deduce that

is a fixed point of the map

with

. By Lemma

, we have uniqueness fixed points of

(v) Finite Morse Index.
Suppose that

is an even solution of (

Appendix A
Auxiliary Results

We collect some technical results needed in the paper.

A.1.
Fractional Leibniz Estimate

Lemma A.1
.

Let
R>0
be given and suppose that
\chi\in W^{1,\infty}(\mathbb{R})
with
0\leq\chi\leq 1
such that
\chi(x)=0
for
|x|\leq R
and
\chi(x)=1
for
|x|\geq 2R
. Then, for any
s\in(0,1)
, we have

[\chi\varphi]_{\dot{H}^{s}}\leq C\left(\|\varphi\|_{L^{\infty}}+[\varphi]_{\dot{H}^{s}}\right)\quad\mbox{for all $\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R})$}\,,

with some constant
C=C(\|\chi^{\prime}\|_{L^{\infty}},R,s)>0
.

Proof.

This follows from a fractional Leibniz estimate, which can be deduced by elementary means. Indeed, we note that

\displaystyle[\chi\varphi]^{2}_{\dot{H}^{s}}

\displaystyle=\frac{c_{s}}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\!\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{|\chi(x)\varphi(x)-\chi(y)\varphi(y)|^{2}}{|x-y|^{1+2s}}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\leq C\left(\|\varphi\|_{L^{\infty}}^{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\!\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{|\chi(x)-\chi(y)|^{2}}{|x-y|^{1+2s}}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\mathbb{R}}\!\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{|\varphi(x)-\varphi(y)|^{2}}{|x-y|^{1+2s}}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y\right)

\displaystyle\leq C\left(\|\varphi\|_{L^{\infty}}^{2}[\chi]_{\dot{H}^{s}}^{2}+[\varphi]_{\dot{H}^{s}}^{2}\right)\,,

which follows from the elementary inequality
|ab-cd|^{2}\leq|a-c|^{2}(|b|^{2}+|d|^{2})+|b-d|^{2}(|a|^{2}+|c|^{2})
together with the uniform bounds
|\chi(x)|\leq 1
and
|\varphi(x)|\leq\|\varphi\|_{L^{\infty}}
. It remains to show that

[\chi]_{\dot{H}^{s}}^{2}\leq K

with some constant
K>0
depending only on
\|\chi^{\prime}\|_{L^{\infty}},R
and
s
. To this end, we note that
\chi(x)-\chi(y)=0
on the sets
\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:|x|\leq R,|y|\leq R\}
and
\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:|x|\geq 2R,|y|\geq 2R\}
. Using this fact together with the symmetry in
x
and
y
, we conclude

\displaystyle[\chi]_{\dot{H}^{s}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{|x|\leq 2R}\int_{|y|\geq R}\frac{|\chi(x)-\chi(y)|^{2}}{|x-y|^{1+2s}}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=\int_{|x|\leq 2R}\int_{|y|\geq 2R}(\mathds{1}_{\{|x-y|\leq 1\}}+\mathds{1}_{\{|x-y|>1\}})\frac{|\chi(x)-\chi(y)|^{2}}{|x-y|^{1+2s}}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\leq C\|\chi^{\prime}\|_{L^{\infty}}^{2}\int_{|x|\leq 2R}\int_{|x-y|\leq 1}|x-y|^{1-2s}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\quad+C\int_{|x|\leq 2R}\int_{|x-y|>1}|x-y|^{-1-2s}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y\leq CR(\|\chi^{\prime}\|_{L^{\infty}}^{2}+1)\,.

This completes the proof.
∎

Compactness Results

We have the following compactness result concerning subsets of the Banach space

with parameter

defined in Section

above.

Lemma A.2
.

Let

and

be constants. Suppose that

|v(x)|\leq Ce^{-d|x|^{2\alpha}}\quad|\partial_{x}v(x)|\leq C\quad\mbox{for all $v\in\mathcal{F}$ and a.\,e.\penalty\ $x\in\mathbb{R}$}\,.

.

Lemma A.3
.

Let
1\leq p_{1}<p_{2}<\infty
be given. Suppose that
f_{n}=f_{n}^{*}
is a sequence of symmetric-decreasing functions satisying

\|f_{n}\|_{L^{p}}\leq C\quad\mbox{for all $p\in[p_{1},p_{2}]$}

(f_{n_{k}})_{k=1}^{\infty}
and a symmetric-decreasing function

such that

and

\mbox{$f_{n_{k}}\to f$ in $L^{p}(\mathbb{R})$ for all $p\in(p_{1},p_{2})$}.

Using that

with

. Since each

Now, since

Lemma A.4
.

Let
X
be a Banach space and suppose
\Omega\subseteq X
is open. If
F:\Omega\to X
is a compact map whose Fréchet derivative

exists at some

, then

is a compact linear operator.

Proof.

Suppose that the Fréchet derivative

exists, but fails to be a compact linear map. Thus there exist
\varepsilon_{0}>0
and some sequence

with
\|y_{n}\|\leq 1
such that

\|DF(v)(y_{k}-y_{\ell})\|\geq\varepsilon_{0}\quad\mbox{for $k\neq\ell$}.

By the definition of Fréchet differentiability there exists some

Let

. By the triangle inequality, it follows

\displaystyle\|F(v+\tau y_{k})-F(v+\tau y_{\ell})\|

\displaystyle\geq\|DF(v)(\tau y_{k}-\tau y_{\ell})\|-\|F(v+\tau y_{k})-F(v)-DF(v)\tau y_{k}\|

\displaystyle\quad-\|F(v+\tau y_{\ell})-F(v)-DF(v)\tau y_{\ell}\|\geq\frac{\varepsilon_{0}}{2}\tau

for

. But this contradicts our assumption that

A.3.

, and

, we need to show that

(A.2)

\mathbf{T}^{(\sigma)}_{\lambda}(v+h)-\mathbf{T}^{(\sigma)}_{\lambda}(v)=Lh+o(\|h\|_{X^{\alpha}}),

where
L\in\mathcal{L}(X_{\alpha})
is the linear bounded operator given by

(Lh)(x)=-\mathbf{T}^{(\sigma)}_{\lambda}[v](x)\int_{0}^{x}H_{\alpha}(vh)(y)\,dy.

Indeed, for
h\in X_{\alpha}
with
\|h\|_{X^{\alpha}}<\varepsilon
, we define

w_{h}(x):=-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{x}H_{\alpha}((v+h)^{2})(y)\,dy=-d_{\alpha}\int_{\mathbb{R}}(|x-y|^{2\alpha}-|y|^{2\alpha})(v+h)(y)^{2}\,dy.

By following the proof of Proposition
4.1
and using that
\|v+h\|_{L^{2}}\geq\|v\|_{L^{2}}-\varepsilon>0
, we obtain the pointwise bound

(A.3)

w_{h}(x)\leq-a|x|^{2\alpha}+b\quad\mbox{for $x\in\mathbb{R}$ and $h\in B_{\varepsilon}(0)$},

with some positive constants
a,b>0
. Let us now write

w_{h}(x)=w_{0}(x)+g_{h}(x),

where

g_{h}(x)=-\int_{0}^{x}H_{\alpha}(vh)(y)\,dy-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{x}H_{\alpha}(h^{2})(y)\,dy.

By Taylor expansion, we get

(A.4)

e^{w_{h}(x)}=e^{w_{0}(x)+g_{h}(x)}=e^{w_{0}(x)}+e^{w_{0}(x)}g_{h}(x)+\frac{1}{2}e^{\xi_{h}(x)}g_{h}(x)^{2}

with some
\xi_{h}(x)\leq-a|x|^{2\alpha}+b
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
and
h\in B_{\varepsilon}(0)
thanks to (
A.3
). Thus,

\displaystyle\|e^{\xi_{h}}g^{2}_{h}\|_{X^{\alpha}}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|e^{-a|x|^{2\alpha}+b}g_{h}^{2}\right\|_{L^{2}}+\left\||x|^{\alpha}e^{-a|x|^{2\alpha}+b}g_{h}^{2}\right\|_{L^{2}}+\left\|e^{-a|x|^{2\alpha}+b}g_{h}^{2}\right\|_{L^{\infty}}

\displaystyle\leq C\left(\|(1+|x|^{\alpha})|x|^{4\alpha}e^{-a|x|^{2\alpha}+b}\|_{L^{2}}+\||x|^{4\alpha}e^{-a|x|^{2\alpha}+d}\|_{L^{\infty}}\right)\|h\|_{L^{2}}^{2}=o(\|h\|_{X^{\alpha}}).

Here we also used the uniform pointwise bound

|g_{h}(x)|\leq C|x|^{2\alpha}(\|vh\|_{L^{1}}+\|h^{2}\|_{L^{1}})\leq C|x|^{2\alpha}(\|v\|_{L^{2}}\|h\|_{L^{2}}+\|h\|_{L^{2}}^{2})\leq C|x|^{2\alpha}\|v\|_{L^{2}}\|h\|_{L^{2}}

noticing that
\|h\|_{L^{2}}<\varepsilon<\|v\|_{L^{2}}
in the last step. Next, we note that

g_{h}(x)=-\int_{0}^{x}H_{\alpha}(vh)(y)\,dy+r_{h}(x)\quad\mbox{with}\quad r_{h}(x)=-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{x}H_{\alpha}(h^{2})(y)\,dy.

From the pointwise bound
|r_{h}(x)|\leq C|x|^{2\alpha}\|h\|_{L^{2}}^{2}
, we deduce

\|e^{w_{0}}r_{h}\|_{X^{\alpha}}\leq C\|h\|_{L^{2}}^{2}=o(\|h\|_{X^{\alpha}}).

By multiplying (
A.4
) with
\lambda\sqrt{K(x)}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}x^{2}}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
, we deduce that (
A.2
) holds true.

Finally, since the map
\mathbf{T}^{(\sigma)}_{\lambda}:X_{\alpha}\setminus\{0\}\to X_{\alpha}
is locally compact, it follows that its Fréchet derivative
D_{v}\mathbf{T}^{(\sigma)}_{\lambda}[v]
is a compact operator; see Lemma
A.4
for a short proof of this general fact. Also, the continuity

\|D_{v}\mathbf{T}^{(\sigma)}_{\lambda}[v]-D_{v}\mathbf{T}_{\lambda_{0}}^{(\sigma_{0})}[v_{0}]\|_{\mathcal{L}(X_{\alpha})}\to 0\quad\mbox{as}\quad(\lambda,\sigma,v)\to(\lambda_{0},\sigma_{0},v_{0})

follows by straightforward arguments, which we omit here.
∎

A.4.
Injectivity of the Laplace Transform

Recall that
\mathsf{L}w(t)=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-st}w(s)\,ds
denotes the (one-sided) Laplace transform of a function
w:[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
. We have the following well-known fact, whose proof we provide for the reader’s convenience.

Lemma A.5
.

Let
w:[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
be bounded and continuous. Then
\mathsf{L}w(t)=0
for all
t>0
if and only if
w(s)=0
for all
s\geq 0
.

Remark
.

The arguments below easily generalize to continuous
w
that are of exponential type, i. e., we have
|w(s)|\leq Me^{cs}
for all
s\geq 0
with some constants
M,c\geq 0
.

Proof.

Suppose that
w:[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
be bounded and continuous. Clearly, we have that
|\mathsf{L}w(t)|\leq t^{-1}\|w\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+})}
for
t>0
and it follows that
\mathsf{L}w(t)
is continuous in
t>0
by dominated convergence. Assume now that
\mathsf{L}w(t)=0
for
t>0
. In particular, we can take
t=2+n>0
for any integer
n\geq 0
, which yields

0=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-2s-ns}w(s)\,ds=\int_{0}^{1}y^{n+1}w(-\!\log y)\,dy=\int_{0}^{1}y^{n}v(y)\,dy,

using the substitution
y=e^{-s}
and setting
v(y):=yw(-\!\log y)
for
y\in(0,1]
. Note that
v
is continuous on
(0,1]
and it extends continuously to
y=0
by setting
v(0)=0
, since
|v(y)|\leq y|w(-\!\log y)|\leq y\|w\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+})}\to 0
as
y\to 0^{+}
. Hence we deduce that

(A.5)

\int_{0}^{1}y^{n}v(y)\,dy=0\quad\mbox{for $n=0,1,2,\ldots$}

for the function
v\in C^{0}([0,1])
. But this implies that
v(y)\equiv 0
. Indeed, let
\varepsilon>0
be given and choose a polynomial
p(x)
such that
\|v-p\|_{L^{\infty}([0,1])}\leq\varepsilon
thanks to the Weierstrass approximation theorem. Observe

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}v(y)^{2}\,dy

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}(v(y)-p(y))v(y)\,dy+\underbrace{\int_{0}^{1}p(y)v(y)\,dy}_{\displaystyle=0\ \ \mbox{by \eqref{eq:moments}}}

\displaystyle\leq\|v-p\|_{L^{\infty}([0,1])}\cdot\|v\|_{L^{\infty}([0,1])}\leq\varepsilon\|v\|_{L^{\infty}([0,1])}.

Since
\varepsilon>0
is arbitrary, we conclude that
v\in C^{0}([0,1])
must be
v\equiv 0
, which implies that
w(s)=e^{s}v(e^{-s})=0
for all
s\geq 0
.
∎

A.5.
Perron–Frobenius Type Result

We have the following quite elementary version of a Perron–Frobenius type result, which is sufficient for our purposes to prove Theorem
1.5
. Since readers might be less familiar with Perron–Frobenius theory (or the more general Krein–Rutnam theory), we provide the details needed in our context.

Lemma A.6
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be open and non-empty. Suppose that
A:L^{2}(\Omega)\to L^{2}(\Omega)
is a compact operator with

Af(x)=\int_{\Omega}a(x,y)f(y)\,dy

such that
a(x,y)>0
for almost every
x,y\in\Omega
. Then
r=\max\sigma(A)>0
is a simple eigenvalue of
A
. Moreover, every eigenfunction
\psi\in L^{2}(\Omega)
with
A\psi=r\psi
satisfies
\pm\psi(x)>0
for almost every
x\in\Omega
.

Proof.

We set
\|\cdot\|=\|\cdot\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}
in what follows and define

r:=\sup_{\varphi\in L^{2},\|\varphi\|=1}(\varphi,A\varphi).

Note that
r>0
holds, which follows from taking some
\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega)
with
\varphi\geq 0
and
\varphi\not\equiv 0
, which implies that
(\varphi,A\varphi)>0
using that
a(x,y)>0
almost everywhere. This shows that
r>0
holds. Furthermore, since
A
is compact it is easy to that the supremum above is attained. Let
\psi\in L^{2}(\Omega)
be a maximizer. Since
A=A^{*}
is self-adjoint, we find that
\psi
is an eigenfunction with
A\psi=r\psi
. In particular, we see that
r>0
is an eigenvalue and it is easy to see that
r=\max\sigma(A)
holds from the construction.

Suppose now that
\psi\in L^{2}(\Omega)
is an eigenfunction with
A\psi=r\psi
. We claim that
|\psi|\geq 0
is also an eigenfunction for the same eigenvalue
r
. Indeed, using that
a(x,y)>0
almost everywhere, we find

r=(\psi,A\psi)\leq(|\psi|,A|\psi|)\leq r\||\psi|\|^{2}=r\|\psi\|^{2}=r.

Hence
|\psi|
is also a maximizer and must satisfy
A|\psi|=r|\psi|
. From
|\psi|\geq 0
with
|\psi|\not\equiv 0
and
r>0
, this implies that
|\psi(x)|>0
for almost every
x\in\Omega
. Now we consider
\varphi:=|\psi|-\psi\geq 0
, which also satisfies
A\varphi=r\varphi
. If
\varphi(x)=0
almost everywhere, then
\psi(x)=|\psi(x)|>0
almost everywhere. If
\varphi\not\equiv 0
, we re-run the argument above to conclude that
|\varphi(x)|=||\psi(x)|-\psi(x)|>0
almost everywhere. But this implies that
\pm\psi(x)>0
almost everywhere.

Finally, we prove that
r
is a simple eigenvalue. To show this, let
\psi_{1},\psi_{2}\in L^{2}(\Omega)
be two linearly independent eigenfunctions with
A\psi_{k}=r\psi_{k}
for
k=1,2
. Thus we can find another eigenfunction
\varphi=\psi_{1}+\gamma\psi_{2}\not\equiv 0
by choosing
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
such that
(\varphi,\psi_{1})=0
. Since
A\varphi=r\varphi
, we deduce that
\pm\varphi(x)>0
almost everywhere. Since also
\pm\psi_{1}(x)>0
almost everywhere by the previous discussion, we obtain that
\pm(\varphi,\psi_{1})>0
. But this contradicts the orthogonality condition
(\varphi,\psi_{1})=0
. Hence the eigenvalue
r
must be simple.
∎

Appendix B
Moving Plane Argument

In this section, we provide the details of the moving plane method used to complete the proof of Lemma
3.4
above. We adapt the arguments in
[
1
]
and reference given there.

For
\lambda>0
and
x\in\mathbb{R}
, we set

\displaystyle\Sigma_{\lambda}:=[\lambda,\infty),

\displaystyle x_{\lambda}:=2\lambda-x,

\displaystyle u_{\lambda}(x):=u(x_{\lambda}),

\displaystyle\Sigma_{\lambda}^{u}:=\{x\in\Sigma_{\lambda}:u(x)>u_{\lambda}(x)\}.

We recall the integral representation

\displaystyle u(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}G(x-y)K(y)e^{u(y)}\,\mathrm{d}y+C\quad\mbox{with}\quad G(x)=-c_{\alpha}|x|^{2\alpha}

and some constant
C\in\mathbb{R}
. We initiate the moving plane method by showing that
\Sigma_{\lambda}^{u}
is empty in the regime of sufficiently large
\lambda
.

Proposition B.1
.

There exists
\lambda_{0}>0
such that
\Sigma_{\lambda}^{u}=\emptyset
for all
\lambda>\lambda_{0}
.

Proof.

Let
\lambda>0
. Using the integral representation and the fact that
G
is an even function, a calculation yields

\displaystyle u(x)-u_{\lambda}(x)=\int_{\Sigma_{\lambda}}(G(x-y)-G(x_{\lambda}-y))\left(K(y)e^{u(y)}-K(y_{\lambda})e^{u_{\lambda}(y)}\right)\,dy.

Since
G
and
K
are even and monotone decreasing in
|x|
, we have

0\geq G(x-y)\geq G(x_{\lambda}-y)\quad\mbox{and}\quad 0\leq K(y)\leq K(y_{\lambda})\quad\mbox{for $x,y\in\Sigma_{\lambda}$}.

For any
x,y\in\Sigma_{\lambda}^{u}\subset\Sigma_{\lambda}
, we thus estimate

\displaystyle u(x)-u_{\lambda}(x)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\Sigma_{\lambda}^{u}}(G(x-y)-G(x_{\lambda}-y))\left(K(y)e^{u(y)}-K(y_{\lambda})e^{u_{\lambda}(y)}\right)\,dy

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\Sigma_{\lambda}^{u}}(G(x-y)-G(x_{\lambda}-y))F_{\lambda}(y)\,dy,

where we denote

\displaystyle F_{\lambda}(y):=K(y)e^{u(y)}(u(y)-u_{\lambda}(y)).

Note that
F_{\lambda}(y)\geq 0
for
y\in\Sigma_{\lambda}^{u}
. Next, we observe the upper bound

-G(x_{\lambda}-y)=c_{\alpha}|2\lambda-x-y|^{2\alpha}\leq c_{\alpha}|x+y|^{2\alpha}\leq c_{\alpha}\left(|x|^{2\alpha}+|y|^{2\alpha}\right),

for
x,y\in\Sigma_{\lambda}
. Thus, for
x\in\Sigma_{\lambda}^{u}
,

\displaystyle 0<u(x)-u_{\lambda}(x)\leq c_{\alpha}\int_{\Sigma_{\lambda}^{u}}\left(|x|^{2\alpha}+|y|^{2\alpha}\right)F_{\lambda}(y)\,dy.

Using the standard notation
\langle x\rangle=\sqrt{1+x^{2}}
, we deduce that

\displaystyle\left|\left|\langle x\rangle^{-2}(u-u_{\lambda})\right|\right|_{L^{1}(\Sigma_{\lambda}^{u})}

\displaystyle\leq c_{\alpha}\int_{\Sigma_{\lambda}^{u}}\int_{\Sigma_{\lambda}^{u}}\langle x\rangle^{-2}\left(|x|^{2\alpha}+|y|^{2\alpha}\right)F_{\lambda}(y)\,\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq C(\alpha)\int_{\Sigma_{\lambda}^{u}}\left(1+|y|^{2\alpha}\right)F_{\lambda}(y)\,\mathrm{d}y

with some constant
C(\alpha)>0
independent of
\lambda>0
and using that
\int_{\mathbb{R}}\langle x\rangle^{-2}|x|^{2\alpha}\,dx<+\infty
for
\alpha\in(0,\frac{1}{2})
. Therefore, we have found that

\displaystyle\left\|\langle x\rangle^{-2}(u-u_{\lambda})\right\|_{L^{1}(\Sigma_{\lambda}^{u})}\leq C(\alpha)\int_{\Sigma_{\lambda}^{u}}\left(1+|y|^{2\alpha}\right)K(y)e^{u(y)}(u(y)-u_{\lambda}(y))\,dy

\displaystyle\leq C(\alpha)\sup_{y\geq\lambda}\left(\left(1+|y|^{2\alpha}\right)\langle y\rangle^{2}K(y)e^{u(y)}\right)\left\|\langle x\rangle^{-2}(u-u_{\lambda})\right\|_{L^{1}(\Sigma_{\lambda}^{u})},

where the constant
C(\alpha)>0
is independent of
\lambda
. Recalling the decay estimae
0\leq Ke^{u}\leq Ce^{-a|x|^{2\alpha}}
with some
a>0
, we can find some constant
\lambda_{0}>0
sufficiently large such that

\displaystyle\left|\left|\langle x\rangle^{-2}(u-u_{\lambda})\right|\right|_{L^{1}(\Sigma_{\lambda}^{u})}\leq\frac{1}{2}\left|\left|\langle x\rangle^{-2}(u-u_{\lambda})\right|\right|_{L^{1}(\Sigma_{\lambda}^{u})},

for
\lambda>\lambda_{0}
. Hence the set
\Sigma^{u}_{\lambda}
has measure zero for
\lambda>\lambda_{0}
, which implies that
\Sigma_{\lambda}^{u}=\emptyset
for
\lambda>\lambda_{0}
by continuity of
u-u_{\lambda}
.
∎

As a next step, we establish the following continuation property.

Proposition B.2
.

Suppose that
\lambda_{0}>0
satisfies
\Sigma_{\lambda}^{u}=\emptyset
for all
\lambda>\lambda_{0}
and assume that
u\not\equiv u_{\lambda_{0}}
on
\Sigma_{\lambda_{0}}
. Then there exists
\varepsilon>0
such that
\Sigma_{\lambda}^{u}=\emptyset
for all
\lambda>\lambda_{0}-\varepsilon
.

Proof.

We divide the proof into the following steps.

Step 1.
By assumption, we have
u(x)\leq u_{\lambda}(x)
for all
x\geq\lambda
and
\lambda>\lambda_{0}
. By continuity, we conclude that
u(x)\leq u_{\lambda_{0}}(x)
for all
x\geq\lambda_{0}
. This shows that
\Sigma_{\lambda_{0}}^{u}=\emptyset
holds. To show that we indeed have
\Sigma_{\lambda}^{u}=\emptyset
for any
\lambda>\lambda_{0}-\varepsilon
with some
\varepsilon>0
, we argue as follows. First, we claim that the strict inequality holds:

(B.1)

\displaystyle u(x)<u_{\lambda_{0}}(x)\quad\mbox{for all $x>\lambda_{0}$}.

We argue by contradiction. Suppose that
u(x)=u_{\lambda_{0}}(x)
for some
x>\lambda_{0}
. Note that
(-\Delta)^{s}u=Ke^{u}
and from our assumption that
K\in C^{1}
, we can deduce that
u\in C_{\mathrm{loc}}^{2s+\gamma}(\mathbb{R})
(see also the proof of Theorem
1.6
(iii) for details). Thus
(-\Delta)^{s}u(x)=e^{u(x)}
holds pointwise in
\mathbb{R}
. Since
K
is monotone-decreasing in
|x|
, we find

(B.2)

(-\Delta)^{s}(u_{\lambda_{0}}-u)(x)=(K(x_{\lambda_{0}})-K(x))e^{u(x)}\geq 0.

On the other hand, using that
u(x)=u_{\lambda_{0}}(x)
for some
x>\lambda_{0}
, we conclude

\displaystyle(-\Delta)^{s}(u_{\lambda_{0}}-u)(x)

\displaystyle=-C_{s}\,\mathrm{PV}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{u_{\lambda_{0}}(y)-u(y)}{|x-y|^{1+2s}}\,dy

\displaystyle=-C_{s}\,\mathrm{PV}

\displaystyle\int_{\lambda_{0}}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{|x-y|^{1+2s}}-\frac{1}{|x-y_{\lambda_{0}}|^{1+2s}}\right)(u_{\lambda_{0}}-u)(y)\,dy\leq 0.

In view of (
B.2
), we must have equality to zero above. Since
x>\lambda_{0}
, we deduce that
u\equiv u_{\lambda_{0}}
on
\Sigma_{\lambda_{0}}
. This contradicts our assumption that
u\not\equiv u_{\lambda_{0}}
on
\Sigma_{\lambda_{0}}
. This proves (
B.1
).

Step 2.
Similarly to the proof of Proposition
B.1
, we get the estimate

\left|\left|\langle x\rangle^{-2}(u-u_{\lambda})\right|\right|_{L^{1}(\Sigma_{\lambda}^{u})}\leq C(\lambda)\sup_{y\geq\lambda}\left(\left(1+|y|^{2\alpha}\right)\langle y\rangle^{2}K(y)e^{u(y)}\right)\left\|\langle x\rangle^{-2}(u-u_{\lambda})\right\|_{L^{1}(\Sigma_{\lambda}^{u})}.

Now, in view of (
B.1
), we conclude that the set

\displaystyle\overline{\Sigma_{\lambda_{0}}^{u}}:=\{x\in\Sigma_{\lambda_{0}}:u(x)\geq u_{\lambda_{0}}(x)\}=\{x=\lambda_{0}\}

has Lebesgue measure zero. Since
\lim_{\lambda\nearrow\lambda_{0}}\Sigma_{\lambda}^{u}\subset\overline{\Sigma_{\lambda_{0}}^{u}}
, and by inspecting the expression for
C(\lambda)
in the proof of Proposition
B.1
, we deduce that
C(\lambda)\rightarrow 0
as
\lambda\nearrow\lambda_{0}
. Thus, for some
\varepsilon>0
sufficiently small, we find that

\displaystyle\left\||\langle x\rangle^{-2}(u-u_{\lambda})\right\|_{L^{1}(\Sigma_{\lambda}^{u})}\leq\frac{1}{2}\left\|\langle x\rangle^{-2}(u-u_{\lambda})\right\|_{L^{1}(\Sigma_{\lambda}^{u})}

for
\lambda>\lambda_{0}-\varepsilon
. It follows that
\Sigma_{\lambda}^{u}
has measure zero and thus
\Sigma_{\lambda}^{u}=\emptyset
by the continuity of
u-u_{\lambda}
. This completes the proof.
∎

We are now ready to complete the proof of the symmetry result stated in Lemma
3.4
as follows. Let us consider

\lambda_{*}:=\inf\left\{\lambda>0:\Sigma_{\lambda^{\prime}}^{u}=\emptyset\text{ for all }\lambda^{\prime}>\lambda\right\}\geq 0,

which is is well-defined thanks to Proposition
B.1
. Suppose now that
\lambda_{*}>0
holds. By Proposition
B.2
, we must have
u\equiv u_{\lambda_{*}}
on
\Sigma_{\lambda_{*}}=[\lambda_{*},\infty)
. But this implies that
u
is even with respect
\lambda_{*}
. Indeed, let
x>0
be arbitrary. Since
\lambda_{*}+x\in\Sigma_{\lambda_{*}}
and
u\equiv u_{\lambda_{*}}
on
\Sigma_{\lambda_{*}}
, we conclude
u(\lambda_{*}+x)=u_{\lambda_{*}}(\lambda_{*}+x)=u(\lambda_{*}-x)
as claimed, and the later identity trivially extends to all
x\in\mathbb{R}
.

Assume now that
\lambda_{*}=0
holds. Since
K(x)=K(-x)
is even, the function
\tilde{u}(x)=u(-x)
is also a solution of
(-\Delta)^{s}u=Ke^{u}
. By re-running the argument above, we conclude that
\tilde{u}
is either symmetric around some
\lambda_{*}>0
and thus,
u
is symmetric around
-\lambda_{*}
, or we have
\lambda_{*}=0
and hence
u
is symmetric around
0
. In summary, we conclude that
u
is symmetric around some point
x_{*}=\pm\lambda_{*}\in\mathbb{R}
, i. e., it holds

u(x_{0}+x)=u(x_{0}-x)\quad\mbox{for all $x\in\mathbb{R}$}.

Next, we show that
u
is symmetric-decreasing around
x_{*}
. Without loss of generality we assume that
y>x>x_{*}\geq 0
. We define
\lambda:=\frac{x+y}{2}>x_{*}
, which implies that
x=2\lambda-y=y_{\lambda}
. By construction, we have
\Sigma^{u}_{\lambda}=\emptyset
and thus it follows

u(y)\leq u_{\lambda}(y)=u(y_{\lambda})=u(x).

This shows that
u
is symmetric-decreasing with respect to
x_{*}
.

Finally, we show that
x_{*}\neq 0
implies that
K(x)\equiv\mbox{const}.
holds. Indeed, we can assume
x_{*}=\lambda_{*}>0
without loss of generality and thus
u\equiv u_{\lambda_{*}}
on
\Sigma_{\lambda_{*}}
. From the argument in the proof of Proposition
B.2
above, we deduce that

0=(-\Delta)^{s}(u_{\lambda_{*}}-u)(y)=(K(y_{\lambda_{*}})-K(y))e^{u(y)}\quad\mbox{for $y\in\Sigma_{\lambda_{*}}$}.

Thus
K_{\lambda_{*}}\equiv K
on
\Sigma_{\lambda_{*}}
, which means that
K
is symmetric with respect to
\lambda_{*}>0
, i. e.,

K(\lambda_{*}-x)=K(\lambda_{*}+x)\quad\mbox{for $x\in\mathbb{R}$}.

In particular, this implies that
K(0)=K(2\lambda_{*})
. On the other hand, since
K(x)=K(-x)
is assumed to be even and monotone-decreasing in
|x|
, we find
K(\lambda_{*})\leq K(0)=K(2\lambda_{*})
. Since
K
is monotone-decreasing in
|x|
, this implies
K(x)\equiv K(0)
on
[-2\lambda_{*},2\lambda_{*}]
. Next, we note that
K(3\lambda_{*})=K(-\lambda_{*})=K(0)
. Likewise, we find that
K(x)\equiv K(0)
on
[-3\lambda_{0},3\lambda_{0}]
. A simple iteration now yields
K(x)\equiv K(0)
on
[-n\lambda_{0},n\lambda_{0}]
for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
, which implies that
K(x)\equiv K(0)
on
\mathbb{R}
.
∎
