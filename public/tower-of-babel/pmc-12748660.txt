Data-driven machine learning models for predicting engineering properties in deep-sea sediments
Authors: Jungmin Yun, Junghee Park, Hyunwook Choo, Hyung-Min Lee, Jongmuk Won
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Predicting the properties of deep-sea sediments offers critical insights into past oceanic conditions, including sediment composition, stratigraphy, and geochemical signals. However, accurate prediction is hindered by the high spatial variability of these sediments. This study presents a data-driven machine learning framework to predict five key sediment properties. Five prediction scenarios were developed with tailored preprocessing and hyperparameter tuning, and Shapley additive explanations were employed to assess feature importance and the relationships between depth and sediment properties. Among the five tested algorithms, the extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost) model achieved the highest predictive performance. Depth and compressional wave velocity emerged as the most and second most influential features for estimating porosity, grain density, calcite content, and thermal conductivity. The depth-dependent predictions with quantified uncertainties generated by the XGBoost model demonstrate that the proposed framework provides a robust approach for predicting deep-sea sediment properties.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-29402-7.

Introduction

Investigating deep-sea sediments provides important information for understanding the history of Earth’s climate change, ocean circulation, and ecosystems. Because the ocean properties (e.g., temperature, salinity, and ocean currents) can affect the properties of deep-sea sediments1–3, investigating the properties of deep-sea sediments can provide insights into past ocean environmental conditions. The characterization of deep-sea sediment can be achieved by many sampling techniques such as ocean drilling, gravity coring, piston coring, etc.4–8. Those sampling techniques can provide engineering insights from the obtained samples by direct measurement of properties in the area of interest. However, measuring engineering properties cannot cover a wide area as the sampling techniques are typically time-consuming and expensive. Therefore, the prediction of properties of deep-sea sediments is required to estimate the engineering properties in unsampled areas and to derive representative characteristics of typical deep-sea sediments.

Many previous studies investigated machine learning (ML)-based prediction models for predicting engineering properties of sediments9,10 and sea level variations11–13 because of their high uncertainty and low predictability using empirical and theoretical-based models. Among many engineering properties of sediments, porosity (n) is a critical property in offshore geotechnical engineering for drilling projects, deep-sea mining, offshore wind power, and the analysis of landslides14–16. Therefore, Graw et al.9 documented the random forest (RF) model to predict density (can be converted to n using grain density (ρg)) of deep-sea sediments. In addition, many ML-based models for predicting the thermal conductivity (λ) of sediments were documented, as it is a critical engineering property in petroleum geology17–20. Compressional wave velocity (Vp) is also of engineering significance, as it can be used to estimate Young’s modulus and sediment strength, several ML-based models for predicting Vp have been reported21,22.

Apart from the abovementioned engineering properties of sediments, CaCO3 content has been also considered an important property of deep-sea sediments because it provides insights into past ocean conditions and the global carbon cycle23–26. In addition, ρg can be used in the estimation of environmental phenomena of deep-sea sediments, such as microplastic accumulation27,28. However, predicting n, Vp, λ, CaCO3 content, and ρg of deep-sea sediments using ML models is not available to date, even though ML techniques can be efficiently used in predicting such spatially variable sediment properties29–31. Therefore, the objective of this study is to develop ML models to predict n, Vp, λ, CaCO3 content, and ρg of deep-sea sediments using available data from the Ocean Drilling Program (ODP) and the Integrated Ocean Drilling Program (IODP). Five ML algorithms were selected to determine the best ML algorithms for predicting those properties, and Shapley additive explanations were selected to evaluate the feature importance of the developed ML models. In addition, the prediction of those properties as a function of depth with uncertainty quantification was presented, and the engineering implications of the developed ML models were discussed.

Sample and database

Sample description

The seabed properties versus depth were obtained from 39 sites worldwide (Fig. S1 in Supplementary Materials) in the ODP32–38 and IODP39–49. For example, Sites 1225 to 1231 were sampled by ODP Leg 201 drillship from the seafloor near Peru32,33, Sites 1244, 1245, 1251 were sampled by the ODP Leg 204 drillship from the Cascadia margins34, Sites 1319, 1320, 1322, 1324 were sampled by the IODP Leg 308 drillship and are located in the Gulf of Mexico42, Sites 1331 to 1338 were sampled near Hawaii by the ODP Leg 320 &321 drillship35, Sites 1342 and 1343 were sampled by the ODP Leg 323 and are located in the Bering Sea36,37, and Sites 1366 to 1371 measured several soil properties of seafloor sediments in the South Pacific Gyre38. Five sediment properties were selected to develop ML models: porosity, grain density, CaCO3 content, P-wave velocity, and thermal conductivity.

Database and preprocessing

A total of 28,914 pairs of data were used to develop the ML models. Each pair of data consists of five sediment features (porosity (n), grain density (ρg), CaCO3 content, P-wave velocity (Vp), and thermal conductivity (λ)) and corresponding depth. The data of these five sediment features reported for 39 sites were manually extracted, which showed different depth intervals and final depth depending on the sediment feature and the site location. Therefore, the extracted data were merged and linearly interpolated with the depth interval of 0.01 m at depth = 0.14–401 m (extracted data above 401-m depth were eliminated). In addition, the database was preprocessed based on the typical range of porosity of seabed (36.7–79.6%50), grain density (2–3 g/cm351), P-wave velocity (1400—1800 m/s52), and thermal conductivity (0–2 W/mK53). The values outside these ranges and corresponding depths were considered physically unrealistic and deleted before developing ML models. The statistical information and histograms of the database used in this study are summarized in Table S1 and Fig. S2. Note that the original data of CaCO3 content without deleting any data was used in this study because of the wide range of CaCO3 content in the seabed (e.g., presence of CaCO3 particles54).

The high variability of the range of feature values can downgrade the performance of ML models and biased models with high sensitivity to the magnitude of input features. Therefore, min–max scaling was applied to standardize the dataset, improve the model performance, and increase the robustness to outliers55–57. The low Pearson coefficient between two features (Fig. S3) indicates that all features used in this study are relatively independent (absolute Pearson coefficient > 0.6 represents strong dependency between two features55), which can all be included in the ML models. Machine learning techniques and predictor importance

Machine learning techniques

To determine the best-performing ML models for five scenarios, three single ML algorithms (K-nearest neighbor (KNN), neural network (NN), and decision tree (DT)) and two ensemble learning algorithms (random forest (RF) and extreme gradient boosting (XGB)) were used in this study. The performance and the generalization capacity of ML models were evaluated as the coefficient of determination (R2) value for the testing dataset and k-fold cross-validation for the whole dataset (k = 10). The split ratio of 80:20 for training and test datasets was selected in developing ML models. To quantitatively account for the split-dependent performance of ML models, 100 ML models were developed to evaluate the mean and standard deviation for a given ML algorithm and scenario. In addition, the mean R2 value of k-fold cross-validation was evaluated in each model development to provide the mean and standard deviation of mean R2 values for 100 ML models. Hyperparameter tuning

In this study, Bayesian optimization was selected to tune selected hyperparameters of each ML algorithm because of its efficiency and fast convergence compared to other tuning techniques (e.g., grid search algorithm)65. The training dataset was randomly divided into five sub-datasets during the tuning process to obtain the best hyperparameters. In addition, one hundred iterations were implemented to minimize Root-Mean-Square-Error (RMSE) using the optimizer of the Gaussian process. Shapley additive explanations

Shapley additive explanation (SHAP) is a widely used game theory-based approach to explain ML models66,67. Through evaluated Shapley values, the impact of individual feature value on predicted outcome (positively or negatively) can be assessed57,68,69. In addition, the sum of absolute Shapley values can provide feature importance. Because Shapley values are evaluated from a rigorous mathematical background, the Shapley value can provide insight from ML models with great consistency and unbiasedness. Therefore, this study selected SHAP to explain the developed ML models for the engineering implications. The SHAP analysis reveals that n, Vs, and λ exhibit consistent depth‐dependent trends, which align with established geophysical mechanisms. As sediments compact with depth, porosity decreases, leading to an increase in grain contact. This leads to higher Vs, and λ at higher depth. This coupling reflects the interplay between mechanical and thermal aspects of deep-sea sediments. While our statistical analysis shows feature independence in a mathematical sense (Figure S3), n, Vs, and λ remain physically linked through compaction‐driven processes. The agreement between the SHAP‐identified trends and these known abovementioned mechanisms supports the physical validity of the data‐driven model shown in this study.

Model implementation

The developed ML models in the previous sections provide the estimation of the predictand as a function of depth by taking four predictors and depth as input parameters for the developed ML models (e.g., ρg, CaCO3, Vp, λ, and depth as input parameters for scenario No. 1 to predict n). In addition, the dependency of hyperparameters on training datasets leads to the stochastic nature of the predictand. Therefore, the maximum, minimum, and average predictand as a function of depth in each scenario for 100 XGB models were evaluated as presented in Fig. 6. Note that the average values of four predictors were used as input parameters in Fig. 6. The average n, ρg, CaCO3, Vp, λ were 58.4%, 2.66 g/cm3, 50%, 1554 m/s, and 1.05 W/mK, respectively.Fig. 6Maximum, minimum, and average predictand for 100 XGB models as a function of depth: (a) scenario No. 1, (b) scenario No. 2, (c) scenario No. 3, (d) scenario No. 4, and (e) scenario No. 5.

Maximum, minimum, and average predictand for 100 XGB models as a function of depth: (a) scenario No. 1, (b) scenario No. 2, (c) scenario No. 3, (d) scenario No. 4, and (e) scenario No. 5.

As seen in Fig. 6a, a slight decrease in n as a function of depth was obtained with a range of average n = 45 – 70%. This is physically realistic as the lower n at higher depth can be anticipated because of higher effective stress applied to the sediment. This trend is consistent with the trend observed in previous studies75. In addition, the range of average n shown in Fig. 6a is consistent with typical n values shown in previous studies for deep-sea sediments50. Therefore, the framework proposed in this study (averaged n values of 100 XGB models) suggests the chance of predicting n as a function of depth using well-trained ML models.

An increasing trend with depth was observed for λ (Fig. 6e). This is also physically realistic as λ increases as n decreases for saturated sediments (i.e., pore space of sediments is filled with seawater), which can be attributed to the high λ of solid particles76. In addition, a slight increase in ρg and Vp profiles with depth (only up to the depth of ~ 230 m for Vp) (Fig. 6b and d) was obtained. The decreasing trend of Vp at depth > 230 m can be attributed to the insufficient Vp data in the database used in this study, which led to a physically unrealistic estimation of Vp at depth > 230 m. Overall, the range of average values shown in Fig. 6b, d, and e were consistent with those values observed in typical soils (ρg = 2.5–2.7 g/cm3, Vp = 1500 – 1600 m/s, and λ = 0.7–1.1 W/mK)14,51–53. The estimation of n, ρg, Vp, and λ as a function of depth for deep-sea sediments can also be obtained from available empirical relationships or assumed as reasonable values (e.g., ρg = 2.6 g/cm3). However, the advantage of using the framework proposed in this study is the ability to quantify the uncertainty of sediment properties as a function of depth, as shown in Fig. 6. For example, the maximum n can be considered as a conservative design parameter for engineering applications in deep-sea sediments. In addition, the utilization of a range of sediment properties enables evaluating the desired output (e.g., factor of safety) in a probabilistic manner rather than a deterministic manner. More reliable uncertainty quantification can be obtained when a large database can be secured.

The CaCO3 content can be a function of the geological formation of deep-sea sediment, the empirical relationships to estimate CaCO3 content may not be available at the site. The estimated CaCO3 content shown in Fig. 6c implies the applicability of ML techniques in estimating difficult-to-estimate properties. Note that the estimated CaCO3 content with depth shown in Fig. 6c provides a possible range of CaCO3 content in deep-sea sediment as 20–60%. This highly variable range of CaCO3 content at deep-sea sediments can also be found in the literature77, which is caused by oceanographic processes such as thermohaline circulation and remineralization78.

Engineering implications

Several studies have investigated the estimation of properties of deep-sea sediments using ML regression models10,79. However, those studies used only limited numbers of data (< 3,000 data) and predicted one targeted predictand (e.g., porosity). The higher number of data points (28,914 data points) used in this study than in previous studies implies that reliable and comprehensive ML models for predicting properties of deep-sea sediments were obtained in this study. The higher R2 and lower RMSE values obtained in this study (Table 4) than the abovementioned previous studies suggest that the preprocessing and the hyperparameter tuning techniques can be applied to develop ML models for predicting the properties of deep-sea sediments.

The most important feature in the five scenarios was evaluated as depth in this study (Fig. 5), which is more or less physically intuitive, as shown in previous studies14,75,80. For example, the asymptotic exponential model81,82 for estimating void ratio well describes depth-dependent porosity in deep-sea sediments14 (data also obtained from the ODP), which is qualitatively consistent with the decreasing trend of n with depth as observed in Fig. 5a. The semi-empirical relationships shown in14 may be relatively easy to implement for estimating n as a function of depth. However, the abovementioned semi-empirical relationships may not provide an accurate estimation of n because n at very low and very high effective stress should be given to implement this empirical equation. Because those two n values are strongly dependent on the sediment type, the accurate estimation of n may not be achieved without knowing the sediment type. In contrast, the high-performing ML models (e.g., RF and XGB models) presented in this study enable providing a relatively accurate estimation of n.

The mathematical background of Shapley values implies that each feature’s Shapley value represents its contribution to the model prediction for all possible combinations of features. Therefore, Shapley values can demonstrate physical inference from the feature importance that has not been comprehensively studied to date. For example, it is notable that Vp is the most important feature (except for depth) in predicting n, ρg, CaCO3, and λ (Tables 5 and S2). The order of feature importance presented in Table 5 also inferred that the relatively important features can be used to estimate predictand when the rest of the features are unavailable. For example, depth and Vp can provide a reasonable estimation of n using the XGB model for scenario No. 1 by taking averaged values of other properties (ρg, CaCO3, and λ). Physical or empirical relationships between properties in deep-sea sediments were relatively less studied because of high spatial variability across the region and a variety of interested properties (e.g., acoustic, biological, physical, and magnetic properties) depending on the field. The framework shown in this study enables providing the order of feature importance to predict each feature in the database.

The limitation of ML models presented in this study can be an inability to cover all oceans in the world. For example, the database used in this study did not contain the properties in the South Atlantic Ocean, the Indian Ocean, and the middle of the North Pacific Ocean (Figure S1). Although the number of data points selected in this study may be high enough to develop reliable ML models, including the additional data points for the uncovered region in this study would provide more comprehensive and rigorous ML prediction models for predicting n, ρg, CaCO3, Vp, and λ of deep-sea sediments.

Conclusions

This study presented ML models to predict n, ρg, CaCO3, Vp, and λ of deep-sea sediments using three single ML algorithms (K-nearest neighbor (KNN), neural network (NN), and decision tree (DT)) and two ensemble learning algorithm (random forest (RF) and extreme gradient boosting (XGB)). The preprocessing and hyperparameter tuning were performed to obtain the best-performing ML models for the database. In addition, Shapley values were evaluated to investigate the feature importance of five scenarios and the relationship between features. From the results obtained in this study, the following conclusions can be drawn.The performance of developed ML models was in the order of XGB > RF > DT > KNN > NN for all scenarios except for scenario No. 1 (RF > XGB > DT > KNN > NN). This indicates that ensemble learning algorithms outperformed single ML learning algorithms in this study.The relatively high variation of tuned hyperparameters for 100 simulations for RF compared to that for XGB suggests that the XGB was the best ML algorithm among five selected ML algorithms with high performance and independence on the training/test split.The evaluated feature importance showed that depth is the most important feature among the four features in all scenarios for five ML algorithms, which is consistent with the established geophysical mechanisms for n, Vs, and λ.Except for depth, Vp was evaluated as the most important feature in predicting n, ρg, CaCO3, and λ.
