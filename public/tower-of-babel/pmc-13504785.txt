Timing of hemoadsorption in sepsis: redefining early through mediator-guided therapy
Authors: Prit Kusirisin, John A Kellum, Nattachai Srisawat
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHemoadsorption is gaining acceptance as an adjunctive extracorporeal blood purification strategy in sepsis and septic shock; however, its clinical value remains contingent on two closely linked variables: when therapy is initiated and in whom it should be used. This review synthesizes the pathophysiological rationale, conceptual models of mediator removal, available devices, and clinical evidence for hemoadsorption in sepsis, with particular attention to patient selection.Main bodySepsis is driven by a self-amplifying mediator cascade in which exogenous pathogenic substances and host response mediators increase rapidly, progressively committing potentially salvageable organs to injury and failure. Mechanistic theories of blood purification provide a rationale for early hemoadsorption, when mediator concentrations are high and dysregulated. Clinical evidence, however, remains limited: polymyxin B hemoadsorption guided by the endotoxin activity assay has demonstrated a survival benefit in a randomized trial, whereas supportive findings for cytokine adsorption derive largely from small trials, observational cohorts, and subgroup or post hoc analyses. These findings remain hypothesis-generating and require prospective validation in biomarker-enriched, phenotype-guided randomized trials.ConclusionCurrent evidence supports moving beyond a fixed hours-from-onset definition of “early” treatment toward a biological definition, in which hemoadsorption is initiated in patients with a dysregulated hyperinflammatory host response, after key inflammatory mediators have crossed a toxic threshold and before irreversible organ injury becomes established.Keywords: Cytokines, Endotoxin, Extracorporeal blood purification, Hemoadsorption, Hemoperfusion, Host response, Sepsis, Septic shock

Background

Sepsis remains one of the largest contributors to global mortality [1]. An estimated 49 million cases occur worldwide each year, resulting in approximately 11 million sepsis-related deaths and accounting for nearly one in five deaths globally [2]. Once septic shock develops, as defined under the Sepsis-3 framework, mortality remains between 30 and 40% [3]. Critically, while timely administration of antibiotics, effective source control, and appropriate hemodynamic resuscitation are cornerstones of sepsis shock management, many patients progress despite these interventions. This residual mortality has driven interest in rescue therapies that target the dysregulated host response rather than the pathogen alone.

The concept of removing circulating toxins and inflammatory mediators from septic blood remains attractive [4]. Extracorporeal blood purification techniques have evolved from early plasma exchange and high-volume hemofiltration toward increasingly selective and semi-selective adsorption technologies, paralleling the evolution of sepsis from a purely infectious paradigm to one characterized by a dysregulated host response [4, 5]. Hemoadsorption represents one such strategy, aiming to attenuate the inflammatory response, stabilize hemodynamics, and limit progression of multiple organ dysfunction [6]. Although cumulative evidence from hemoadsorption trials has historically been insufficient to support the routine use of individual devices and has therefore precluded a recommendation in the 2026 Surviving Sepsis Campaign guidelines, recent large randomized trials suggest a progressive refinement in treatment effects when therapy is evaluated through the lenses of timing and biological phenotype [7]. The central premise of this review is that hemoadsorption is not a generic intervention for all patients with sepsis or septic shock. Rather, its effectiveness appears to depend on two fundamental variables: when therapy is initiated, and in whom it is applied.

Sepsis and the immunoinflammatory cascade

Sepsis is characterized by a dynamic and self-amplifying interaction between invading pathogens and the host immune response rather than by a single pathogenic event (Fig. 1). Pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs), including endotoxin (e.g., lipopolysaccharide [LPS] from gram-negative bacteria, as well as microbial components from gram-positive bacteria and fungi), are recognized by pattern-recognition receptors (PRRs) on host immune cells, including Toll-like receptors (TLRs) at the cell surface and within endosomes, C-type lectin receptors (CLRs) on the cell surface, and retinoic acid-inducible gene 1-like receptors (RLRs) and nucleotide-binding oligomerization domain-like receptors (NLRs) within the cytoplasm [8]. Activation of these receptors initiates intracellular signaling pathways, including nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB) and mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK), resulting in the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), interleukin-1 (IL-1), and IL-6 [1].

Immunoinflammatory cascade in sepsis. Infection initiates an inflammatory response through the interaction of pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs), including endotoxins (e.g., lipopolysaccharide from Gram-negative bacteria) or components from other microbes (e.g., Gram-positive bacteria or fungi), with pattern-recognition receptors (PRRs) on host immune cells, which include Toll-like receptors (TLRs), C-type lectin receptors (CLRs), retinoic acid-inducible gene 1-like receptors (RLRs), and nucleotide-binding oligomerization domain-like receptors (NLRs). Activation of these receptors triggers intracellular signaling pathways, including nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB) and mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK), leading to the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), interleukin-1 (IL-1), and IL-6. The resulting dysregulated host response promotes microvascular thrombosis through concurrent activation of coagulation, impairment of endogenous anticoagulant pathways, suppression of fibrinolysis, and release of neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) from dying neutrophils, resulting in tissue hypoperfusion, which is aggravated by vasodilation, hypotension, and reduced red cell deformability. In parallel, endothelial dysfunction develops through disruption of vascular endothelial cadherin, alterations in endothelial tight junctions, elevated angiopoietin 2 concentrations, and preferential activation of sphingosine-1 phosphate receptor 3 (S1P3) through protease-activated receptor 1 (PAR1), resulting from a reduced activated protein C-to-thrombin ratio and disruption of the balance between S1P1 and S1P3 within the vascular wall. Concurrent endothelial dysfunction aggravated by the loss of vascular barrier integrity increases vascular permeability, leading to capillary leak and interstitial edema. Collectively, tissue hypoperfusion, endothelial barrier disruption, and reactive oxygen species (ROS)-mediated mitochondrial dysfunction impair tissue oxygenation, resulting in cellular injury and necrotic cell death. In addition, tissue injury may occur directly through microbial invasion, while complement activation further promotes leukocyte activation and tissue damage. Damaged tissues release damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) (e.g., high mobility group box 1 [HMGB1], heat shock proteins [HSPs], and uric acid), which also activate PRRs, creating a self-perpetuating inflammatory cycle that ultimately culminates in multiple organ dysfunction syndrome (MODS)

Although these responses are essential for pathogen clearance, excessive or dysregulated inflammation promotes microvascular thrombosis through concurrent activation of coagulation, suppression of endogenous anticoagulant pathways, and impaired fibrinolysis [9]. Activation of coagulation is mediated primarily by tissue factor, whereas endogenous anticoagulant activity is diminished through reduced activation of protein C secondary to decreased expression of thrombomodulin and the endothelial protein C receptor, as well as reduced antithrombin activity and tissue factor pathway inhibitor. Fibrinolysis is further suppressed by increased release of plasminogen activator inhibitor type 1 (PAI-1). Thrombus formation is also facilitated by neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) released from dying neutrophils. Together, these processes contribute to tissue hypoperfusion, which is further aggravated by nitric oxide-mediated vasodilation, hypotension, and reduced red blood cell deformability [9].

In parallel, endothelial dysfunction develops through disruption of vascular endothelial cadherin, alterations in endothelial tight junctions, elevated angiopoietin 2 concentrations, and preferential activation of sphingosine-1 phosphate receptor 3 (S1P3) through protease-activated receptor 1 (PAR1), resulting from a reduced activated protein C-to-thrombin ratio and disruption of the balance between S1P1 and S1P3 within the vascular wall [9]. These changes increase vascular permeability, leading to capillary leak and interstitial edema [9]. Collectively, tissue hypoperfusion, endothelial barrier disruption, and reactive oxygen species (ROS)-mediated mitochondrial dysfunction impair tissue oxygenation, resulting in cellular injury and necrotic cell death. Tissue injury may also occur directly through microbial invasion, while complement activation further promotes leukocyte activation and tissue damage [10].

Damaged tissues subsequently release damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), such as high mobility group box 1 (HMGB1), heat shock proteins (HSPs), and uric acid, which further activate PRRs and amplify the inflammatory response, creating a self-perpetuating inflammatory cycle. Simultaneously, pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory pathways are activated in parallel, with their relative balance varying according to host and pathogen characteristics. In some patients, persistent immune dysregulation evolves into immune dysfunction characterized by monocyte deactivation, reduced human leukocyte antigen-DR (HLA-DR) expression, lymphocyte dysfunction, and impaired host defence, increasing susceptibility to secondary infections [11]. The combined effects of these pathophysiological processes ultimately culminate in multiple organ dysfunction syndrome (MODS).

The rationale for blood purification

Conceptual models of mediator removal

A key concept underlying extracorporeal blood purification is that organ injury in sepsis is driven not merely by the presence of the pathogen but also by the magnitude and persistence of circulating inflammatory mediators. Although antimicrobial therapy and source control can eliminate the infectious focus, they do not directly remove endotoxin, cytokines, or other mediators that have already accumulated in the circulation. Consequently, a therapeutic window may exist during which mediator removal can attenuate the dysregulated host response before irreversible organ injury becomes established. This concept provides the biological rationale for hemoadsorption and suggests that its effectiveness is closely linked to careful patient selection and timing of intervention [12, 13].

Four conceptual models underpin the rationale for blood purification and, importantly, all predict that hemoadsorption is most likely to be effective when initiated early, while mediator concentrations are high and the host response is dysregulated [14].

1. Peak concentration hypothesis. Hemoadsorption reduces excessive peak circulating concentrations of both pro- and anti-inflammatory mediators toward more balanced physiologic thresholds, where immune homeostasis can be restored [15]. Under this model, the principal benefit lies in halting the (pro- and anti-) inflammatory cascade, limiting organ injury, and consequently reducing the incidence of MODS.

2. Threshold immunomodulation hypothesis. Removal of inflammatory mediators from the intravascular compartment increases the concentration gradient between the blood and tissues, thereby leading to the removal of mediators located at the tissue level through equilibration of their concentrations between these two compartments [16]. This theoretical driving force explains why blood purification may improve outcomes despite little or no change in circulating mediator concentrations, as mediators from the tissue continuously replace those removed from the blood.

3. Mediator delivery hypothesis. The large volumes of replacement fluid used during high-volume hemofiltration enhance lymphatic flow, promoting the drag and displacement of inflammatory mediators into the circulation, where they can subsequently be removed [17].

4. Cytokinetic hypothesis. This model proposes that the principal effects of blood purification may occur by redirecting the immune response rather than through direct removal of cytotoxic mediators [18, 19]. Based on theoretical models, the inflammatory response, which begins in the tissues, spills over into the circulation, where it activates immune effector cells that further amplify the response. These cells may be less effectively recruited to the source of infection when cytokine gradients are disrupted. Blood purification may modify leukocyte trafficking by altering the inflammatory milieu in the circulation, thereby potentially restoring the cytokine gradient. Experimental evidence demonstrates that artificially enhancing the blood-to-tissue gradient by injecting cytokines into the tissue can redirect immune effector cells to the site of infection and away from healthy tissue [20]. Similar findings have been observed in preclinical models in which the blood cytokine concentration was reduced by hemoadsorption [18, 19]. In this framework, hemoadsorption acts as a potential immunoregulatory intervention that may help re-establish an appropriate host response. The cytokinetic model therefore provides a theoretical rationale for early intervention before remote tissues are irreversibly damaged by the dysregulated host response.

These models are not mutually exclusive and likely represent complementary mechanisms of action. Importantly, all share a common implication: therapeutic benefit is greatest when mediator burden is highest. Conversely, all predict diminishing benefit once mediator concentrations have receded, or irreversible organ injury has developed.

Hemoadsorption devices

In recent decades, several extracorporeal adsorption devices have been developed for the treatment of sepsis [21]. Currently available devices differ in sorbent composition, adsorptive properties, and target molecules (Table 1). Most are designed to remove middle molecular weight substances, typically within the range of 5–60 kDa, including inflammatory cytokines, DAMPs, and even circulating viruses, whereas endotoxin naturally aggregates into large molecular complexes exceeding 100–1,000 kDa and therefore requires chemical affinity-based adsorption rather than size-dependent removal (Table 2). Broad-spectrum adsorption of inflammatory mediators in patients with excessive levels may be beneficial; however, application in patients without excessive inflammation will not be helpful and could even theoretically remove mediators required for immune recovery and host defence.

Broadly, resin-based sorbents, including HA330, HA380, and CA330, as well as the polymer bead adsorber CytoSorb, remove a broad spectrum of pro- and anti-inflammatory cytokines, which underlies their applicability across a range of hyperinflammatory conditions [22–24]. In contrast, polymyxin B-immobilized fiber columns (Toraymyxin), the Alteco LPS Adsorber, and the Efferon LPS selectively bind the lipid A component of endotoxin and are therefore primarily used for endotoxin-directed therapy [25–28]. The oXiris membrane, a 3-layer structure composed of acrylonitrile 69, polyethyleneimine, and grafted heparin, is integrated into a continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT) circuit and combines endotoxin adsorption, cytokine adsorption, and renal support within a single device [29]. Coupled plasma filtration adsorption (CPFA) combines plasma separation, adsorption, and hemodialysis to facilitate removal of inflammatory mediators, providing both renal and liver support [30]. Lastly, the Seraph-100 filter is designed to adsorb circulating pathogens rather than targeting inflammatory mediators directly [31]. Collectively, these technologies represent distinct therapeutic approaches to hemoadsorption, each targeting different components of the sepsis pathophysiology [32].

Why patient selection and timing matter: the narrow therapeutic window

The clinical effectiveness of hemoadsorption depends on the biological state of the individual patient. Four complementary lines of reasoning support the concept that hemoadsorption is likely to confer benefit only when initiated within a specific therapeutic window (Fig. 2).

Proposed biological therapeutic window for hemoadsorption in sepsis. Following infection, inflammatory mediator burden, including endotoxin and cytokines, increases rapidly and may become dysregulated in a subset of patients. Pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory responses coexist throughout the disease course, with their relative balance varying over time. Most patients present after inflammatory activation has already occurred, and the majority spontaneously resolve their inflammation (Trajectory A) and are therefore unlikely to derive substantial benefit from extracorporeal blood purification. In contrast, some patients fail to resolve the dysregulated host response and develop persistently elevated mediator burden (Trajectory B). In these patients, hemoadsorption may facilitate mediator clearance and potentially shorten immune dysfunction when initiated during persistent hyperinflammation (green zone), before progressive organ injury becomes irreversible (orange zone). Once advanced multiple organ dysfunction syndrome (MODS) is established (red zone), the potential benefits of mediator removal are likely to diminish. Distinguishing these trajectories at the bedside remains challenging. Features that may identify patients more likely to follow Trajectory B include refractory vasopressor-dependent septic shock (e.g., norepinephrine ≥0.2 µg/kg/min), high illness severity (e.g., MODS > 9 or Sequential Organ Failure Assessment [SOFA] > 11), and, for endotoxin-directed therapy, an endotoxin activity assay (EAA) level ≥ 0.60. These should be regarded as potential enrichment criteria rather than validated criteria for defining Trajectory B. Serial reassessment during the early course of resuscitation may be more informative than any isolated threshold. An escalating vasopressor requirement despite adequate volume resuscitation and source control, progressive organ dysfunction, worsening hyperlactatemia, and persistently elevated or increasing inflammatory biomarkers may collectively suggest an ongoing, dysregulated host response rather than one that is resolving. Conversely, declining vasopressor requirements and improving organ function may argue against a persistent Trajectory B and reduce the rationale for extracorporeal intervention. Whether trajectory-based criteria can be prospectively operationalized remains uncertain and requires evaluation in biomarker-enriched clinical trials with prespecified enrichment criteria

1. Hyperinflammatory phenotype. The majority of patients with sepsis and even septic shock have modest inflammation compared to a subset of patients in which inflammation appears excessive [33–37]. Sinha and colleagues proposed a hyperinflammatory/hypoinflammatory dichotomy for sepsis and acute respiratory distress syndrome using latent class analysis [36]. This classification relies on cytokines such IL-6 and IL-8 and clinical laboratory variables such as bicarbonate and platelets. Across various cohorts, roughly 20–30% of patients with sepsis are classified as hyperinflammatory, exhibiting much higher mortality. Multiple other groups have developed similar classifications with good discrimination for risk of death. Importantly, high endotoxin activity in patients with septic shock enriches for the hyperinflammatory phenotype—55% of patients [37]. The greatest opportunity for therapeutic intervention aimed at circulating inflammatory mediators therefore exists for patients exhibiting a hyperinflammatory phenotype, and before they develop irreversible endothelial dysfunction, microcirculatory injury, and organ failure.

2. Organ dysfunction is still reversible. Patients exhibiting early organ dysfunction in sepsis may be rescued with immune modulation, whereas persistent inflammation and tissue injury may progress to irreversible organ damage. Recovery from MODS involves multiple pathways but ongoing inflammation, oxidative stress, nephrotoxicity and injury from mechanical support (e.g., renal replacement therapy [RRT], mechanical ventilation) all reduce the chances for organ recovery. While mediator removal alone is unlikely to reverse underlying organ injury, it may have a role in select patients who continue to exhibit hyperinflammation. Patterns of organ dysfunction even in the first 24 h, can also predict outcomes and response to therapy [38, 39]. Across multiple cohorts, approximately 60% of patients with sepsis exhibit subphenotypes identified using machine learning that associate with hospital mortality of 5% or less. Conversely, 13% of adults with sepsis manifest the so-called delta phenotype that conveys a 32% hospital mortality risk. This phenotype also associates with much greater kidney, liver and endothelial dysfunction [38]. A nearly identical phenotype is seen in 14% of children with sepsis and is associated with nearly identical hospital mortality (33%) [39]. Therapies such as activated protein C appear to have a beneficial effect when confined to cohorts enriched with delta phenotype whereas interventions like early goal-directed therapy exhibit just the opposite relationship [38]. Even for endotoxin removal, efficacy appears greatest for patients with thrombocytopenia and hyperlactatemia [40]. Features which also portend high mortality and associate with hepatic, endothelial and renal dysfunction.

3. Matching the treatment to the target. The importance of precision medicine is well established in sepsis care. We prescribe antibiotic therapy based on careful clinical evaluation and narrow our coverage when culture data are available. Hourly delays in effective antimicrobial therapy consistently associated with increased mortality. We titrate fluids and vasoactive medications to the type and severity of shock, and the evaluation of tissue perfusion. Historically, the application of blood purification in sepsis has been imprecise and guided by clinical evaluation alone. However, studies have clearly shown dramatic heterogeneity in host response across patients with sepsis and even septic shock [33, 34]. Cytokine removal cannot benefit patients with low or rapidly decreasing mediator levels. While clinical variables have demonstrated correlations with mediator concentrations, they lack discrimination for individual components of the immune response [35]. It is simply impossible to tell which patients have excessive IL-8 using clinical variables and no amount of artificial intelligence will change this fact. For upstream triggers such as endotoxin, molecular biology itself explains why this is so. Endotoxin signals mainly through TLR4. However, there are more than 30 other ligands that also bind TLR4 and propagate the same downstream signal transduction. It is impossible to work backwards from clinical manifestations to the root cause when multiple causes all signal through the same receptor. The only way to practice precision medicine for endotoxin removal is to measure endotoxin [41].

4. Recognizing potential for harm. Hemoadsorption, like other forms of extracorporeal blood purification is not risk free. While risks of establishing vascular access and providing anticoagulation are low, they are still important. Initiating RRT in patients who do not require it, exposes them not only to these risks but also to the artificial circuit itself. While commercially available blood purification devices are generally biocompatible, they may still activate immune cells and complement especially once inflammatory mediators, white blood cells and even pathogens are adsorbed to the surface. Prolonged exposure to sorbents may result loss of albumin and platelets and can result in de-adsorption of mediators as well [32]. Although unlike RRT, hemoadsorption does not result in fluid removal with resultant risk for hemodynamic instability, it does result in off-target solute removal including drugs (e.g., antibiotics) and hormones (e.g., insulin). Mediator removal that is beneficial during the hyperinflammatory phase may become less effective or even potentially harmful once inflammation has resolved.

Patient selection: endotoxin versus cytokine removal

Because hemoadsorption can target either upstream pathogenic triggers or downstream inflammatory mediators, a longstanding debate has focused on the optimal point of intervention within the septic cascade. Both strategies share the same fundamental objective: to attenuate the inflammatory response an appropriately selected patient. They differ primarily in their therapeutic targets, either the driving force, “the fire (e.g., endotoxin)”, or the downstream mediators responsible for much of the ensuing tissue injury, “the smoke (e.g., cytokines)” [42].

Endotoxin-directed therapy

Endotoxin removal aims to address the “fire” that leads to ongoing immune activation and MODS. Although the ASSET study, which evaluated the Alteco LPS adsorber in patients with abdominal or urogenital septic shock, demonstrated the feasibility of reducing circulating endotoxin levels, it did not show improvements in major clinical outcomes [43]. In contrast, the evidence for polymyxin B provides one of the clearest illustrations of how patient selection may determine the success or failure of hemoadsorption.

The EUPHAS trial randomized patients with abdominal septic shock to polymyxin B hemoadsorption and demonstrated improvements in hemodynamic parameters together with a signal toward reduced mortality [44]. However, the subsequent ABDOMIX trial, conducted in a lower severity population, failed to demonstrate a significant clinical benefit [45]. Likewise, the EUPHRATES trial, which enrolled patients with septic shock and an endotoxin activity assay (EAA) value ≥ 0.60, did not demonstrate a reduction in 28-day mortality in the overall study population [46]. Nevertheless, polymyxin B hemoadsorption has also been shown to improve monocyte HLA-DR expression in patients with severe sepsis, suggesting additional immunomodulatory effects [47]. Furthermore, a meta-analysis indicated that the survival benefit of polymyxin B hemoadsorption is most apparent in studies where control group mortality exceeds 30–40%, consistent with greater benefit among patients with more severe disease and more likely to have high endotoxin activity [48].

Important insights emerged from a post hoc analysis of the EUPHRATES trial, which suggested that treatment effects varied according to the degree of endotoxemia [49]. Patients with high endotoxin activity (EAA 0.60–0.89) appeared more likely to benefit than those with extremely high endotoxin activity (EAA ≥ 0.90), supporting the hypothesis of a biologically defined “treatable zone”. Importantly, given the limitations of the assay for measurements ≥ 0.90, patients may actually have several-fold greater endotoxin activity, well beyond the device’s ability to control. This hypothesis was subsequently evaluated prospectively in the Tigris trial, a multicenter, open-label, Bayesian, randomized phase 3 study that enrolled patients with EAA values between 0.60 and 0.89 together with a high burden of organ dysfunction (MODS > 9 or Sequential Organ Failure Assessment [SOFA] score > 11) [50]. Tigris met its primary endpoint, demonstrating a high probability of improved 28-day survival, with an even greater treatment effect observed at 90 days.

Collectively, the experience from EUPHAS to Tigris suggests that the effectiveness of endotoxin removal depends on selecting patients with demonstrable endotoxemia (predictive enrichment) as well as a high burden of organ dysfunction (prognostic enrichment) during a biologically responsive phase of septic shock. A final lesson from Tigris is that endotoxin removal had the greatest effect on mortality late in the clinical course. This appears to be because artificial organ support (e.g., RRT, mechanical ventilation, extracorporeal membrane oxygenation [ECMO]) can sustain life, and thus obscure benefit early; it does not reverse organ damage and may, as discussed above, even make it worse—and thus not result in long-term benefit.

Cytokine adsorption

Cytokine adsorption offers a broadly applicable therapeutic strategy for sepsis due to bacterial, viral (e.g., COVID-19) and fungal infections, and possibly other inflammatory syndromes [51]. By reducing peak concentrations of both pro- and anti-inflammatory mediators, this approach may help restore immune homeostasis [15]. Clinical studies evaluating CytoSorb, HA330, HA380, CA330, and oXiris have reported reductions in circulating inflammatory mediators and improvements in physiological parameters; however, these effects have not been consistently observed in controlled studies, likely reflecting substantial clinical heterogeneity in patient populations, disease severity, and timing of intervention.

CytoSorb has demonstrated improvements in hemodynamic parameters and reductions in circulating IL-6 concentrations in preclinical studies, in case series and in some randomized studies involving patients with sepsis and septic shock; however, its effect on mortality remains inconclusive [52–54]. In contrast, the CYCOV trial showed that even early initiation of CytoSorb in patients with severe COVID-19 requiring extracorporeal membrane oxygenation failed to reduce serum IL-6 concentrations and was associated with worse survival [55]. These findings highlight that early initiation alone is insufficient to ensure clinical benefit. Appropriate patient selection, disease severity, and underlying inflammatory phenotype are likely to be equally important determinants of treatment response.

Meanwhile, small RCTs and some observational studies support HA330 and HA380 hemoadsorption in sepsis (Table 3). A single-center randomized trial demonstrated that adjunctive HA330 hemoadsorption reduced circulating IL-6 and IL-8 concentrations and lowered SOFA score at day 14 in patients with severe sepsis, although no difference in 28-day mortality was observed [56]. An observational case series of children with refractory septic shock and high illness severity scores also suggests a potential role for HA330 hemoadsorption, with rapid improvement in organ dysfunction observed after treatment [57]. Moreover, cytokine adsorption using HA330 or HA380 combined with CRRT effectively reduces inflammatory mediator burden and may improve clinical outcomes in septic acute kidney injury (AKI). A case series reported an approximately 92% reduction in IL-6 concentrations together with a 50% reduction in norepinephrine requirements after two HA380 sessions combined with continuous venovenous hemodialysis (CVVHD) [58]. A prospective study demonstrated a significant reduction in C-reactive protein (CRP) concentrations following HA330 combined with continuous venovenous hemodiafiltration (CVVHDF) [59]. Likewise, a small randomized trial reported lower IL-6, procalcitonin, lactate concentrations, SOFA score, and norepinephrine requirements, together with improved 28-day survival, in patients treated with HA330 plus CVVHDF [60]. Regarding the importance of timing, early administration of HA330 in an Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation (APACHE) II-matched cohort of patients with septic shock, initiated at a mean of 28 h after shock onset, was associated with improved 28-day survival, together with significant improvements in SOFA score, shock index, oxygenation, and lactate concentrations, without an increased risk of bleeding [61]. In contrast, another cohort evaluated patients in whom hemoadsorption was initiated substantially later, during advanced MODS, at approximately 62 h after shock onset [62]. Mortality reached 69%, with no significant reductions in CRP, procalcitonin, or neutrophil counts, suggesting that delayed initiation after the establishment of irreversible organ injury may substantially limit treatment effectiveness.

More recently, the CLEANSE trial evaluated adjunctive HA330 hemoadsorption in phenotype-based patients with norepinephrine-resistant septic shock, using refractory vasopressor requirements as the indication for treatment [63]. Although the trial did not meet its overall endpoint, an adjusted analysis demonstrated a significant reduction in 28-day mortality among patients with high IL-6 concentrations and high vasoactive-inotropic scores (adjusted hazard ratio 0.62; 95% confidence interval 0.39–0.97; p = 0.037). These findings support the hypothesis that the benefits of cytokine adsorption may be concentrated within a cytokine-driven hyperinflammatory phenotype. Collectively, these findings parallel the experience with endotoxin-directed therapy, suggesting that the average treatment effect across an unselected population is likely to be limited, whereas clinically meaningful benefit may be confined to biologically defined subgroups.

Hemofilters with adsorptive capacity

Hemofilters used for CRRT also can adsorb solutes and some have been modified to enhance this capacity [64]. Multiple clinical studies have evaluated the early use of the oXiris membrane in patients with sepsis and septic shock; however, consistent reductions in endotoxin and cytokine concentrations, improvements in hemodynamic stability, and enhanced organ function have been difficult to demonstrate because of substantial clinical heterogeneity [65–70]. Given the lack of robust evidence for a survival benefit, expert consensus statements from Europe and the Asia-Pacific region suggest that oXiris may be considered in appropriately selected patients with septic shock and AKI requiring CRRT, primarily to improve hemodynamic stability and support organ function [71, 72].

In contrast, major randomized trials of CPFA, including COMPACT-1, ROMPA, and COMPACT-2, failed to demonstrate a survival benefit in patients with septic shock, leading to early termination because of futility, along with concerns regarding circuit clotting, technical complexity, and high treatment costs despite receiving a high treatment dose (> 0.2 L/kg/day of treated plasma volume) [73–75]. Therefore, these findings reinforce the broader concept that achieving an adequate treatment dose alone is insufficient; appropriate patient selection remains the critical determinant of treatment success.

Practical points for hemoadsorption in sepsis

A major challenge in the clinical implementation of hemoadsorption is the absence of universally accepted biomarker thresholds that define when therapy should be initiated. Rather than identifying a universally superior blood purification strategy, a more practical approach is to match the therapeutic target to the underlying biological phenotype. In clinical practice, the onset of septic shock is often uncertain, and patients present at different stages of the inflammatory cascade. Consequently, defining “early” treatment solely by elapsed time (e.g., within X hours) is both impractical and biologically imprecise. Instead, the therapeutic window should be defined biologically according to mediator burden and host-response phenotype [76]. Based on the available clinical evidence, treatment benefits have been observed most consistently when hemoadsorption is initiated in patients with sepsis who exhibit a hyperinflammatory phenotype before irreversible MODS develops (Table 4). 1. Septic shock with endotoxemia. Polymyxin B hemoadsorption may be considered in patients with a demonstrable endotoxin burden (EAA ≥ 0.60). Importantly, the source of endotoxin is usually the gut, so the type of primary infection is not a major determinant.

2. Cytokine storm. Cytokine adsorption may be considered in patients with a cytokine-driven hyperinflammatory phenotype characterized by markedly elevated inflammatory mediators (e.g., IL-6), refractory vasoplegic shock requiring escalating vasopressor support, and evolving MODS, irrespective of the underlying pathogen.

3. Concurrent endotoxemia, cytokine storm, and AKI. Sequential extracorporeal therapy (SET) may be considered when overlapping indications are present [42]. Blood purification using adsorptive hemofilters, such as the oXiris membrane or cytokine sorbents integrated within a CRRT circuit, could provide the combined benefits of endotoxin and cytokine removal while simultaneously delivering renal support. However, the evidence for these hybrid therapies is still quite limited. Furthermore, current evidence does not support the routine use of CPFA in this setting.

Several caveats should temper any strong recommendation regarding hemoadsorption in sepsis. Most of the available evidence is derived from observational studies or small clinical trials evaluating surrogate or physiological endpoints, making confounding by indication difficult to exclude. Furthermore, the thresholds commonly used to define a treatable phenotype, including IL-6 concentrations, vasopressor requirements, and lactate levels, are based largely on clinical experience rather than validated treatment cutoffs. The sole exception is endotoxin-directed therapy based on the EAA, which uses validated cutoffs and a clinical assay with regulatory approval in most jurisdictions.

Beyond determining when to initiate hemoadsorption lies the equally important question of how to monitor treatment response and determine treatment duration. Most published protocols have employed empirical treatment regimens, for example, typically consisting of 2- to 4-h HA330 or HA380 sessions administered over 2–3 consecutive days. The optimal treatment dose, including session duration, treatment frequency, total number of sessions, and blood flow rate, has yet to be established in adequately powered clinical trials. However, because IL-6 is both a central mediator of septic inflammation and a rapidly responsive biomarker, it may serve as both a selection marker and a monitoring tool. Markedly elevated IL-6 concentrations may identify patients most likely to benefit from cytokine adsorption, whereas declining IL-6 levels during treatment may indicate effective mediator removal and help guide treatment discontinuation. This strategy reinforces the concept of a biological treatment window in which therapy is guided by mediator trajectories rather than by a predetermined number of treatment sessions. A pragmatic approach is to continue treatment while substantial mediator burden persists and discontinue therapy once the hyperinflammatory phenotype resolves.

Technically, hemoadsorption requires vascular access, typically through a double-lumen catheter placed in a central vein, although an arteriovenous fistula may also be used [77]. Direct hemoadsorption is the simplest approach, in which blood is circulated through a cartridge containing sorbent material using a blood pump. When hemoadsorption is integrated with an intermittent RRT or CRRT circuit, the adsorption cartridge can generally be positioned before or after the hemofilter, depending on the circuit configuration and device specifications [77]. In practice, placement after the hemofilter may facilitate integration with contemporary CRRT systems and reduce risk of clotting within the adsorption cartridge from larger blood components. Blood flow should be prescribed according to the specifications of the hemoadsorption device and the RRT circuit. Anticoagulation should be initiated before blood enters the extracorporeal circuit, with unfractionated heparin or regional citrate anticoagulation selected according to the clinical context, circuit configuration, and manufacturer recommendations. Because hemoadsorption is relatively non-specific, off-target adsorption of endogenous substrates (e.g., nutrients) and exogenous compounds (e.g., antimicrobials, antiepileptics, direct oral anticoagulants, and other medications) may occur [77]. Drug dosing and therapeutic drug monitoring should therefore be considered, particularly for medications with a narrow therapeutic index. When drug-level monitoring is unavailable, dosing should be guided by the expected adsorption characteristics, treatment duration, circuit configuration, and clinical response.

Future research should focus on refining biological patient selection and optimizing treatment timing. Ongoing clinical trials are evaluating HA380 combined with continuous venovenous hemofiltration (CVVH) (ChiCTR2400082281), HA380 with high-flux hemodialysis in septic AKI (NCT05941624), HA380 integrated with oXiris in CVVHDF mode (NCT04997421), and HA380 in critically ill patients with sepsis (NCT04306419). Notably, many of these studies enroll patients according to clinical or biological phenotypes rather than predefined time windows, reflecting the growing recognition that the effectiveness of hemoadsorption depends primarily on host-response characteristics.

Conclusions

The potential value of hemoadsorption in sepsis depends not only on the device itself but also on “when” it is initiated and “in whom” it is applied. The pathophysiology of sepsis, conceptual models of mediator removal, and available clinical evidence suggest that extracorporeal blood purification may be most beneficial when initiated within a biologically defined therapeutic window characterized by persistent mediator burden, a dysregulated hyperinflammatory phenotype, and potentially reversible organ dysfunction. Conversely, treatment is less likely to be effective when initiated late, applied to unselected populations, or delivered after irreversible organ injury has become established. Rather than selecting patients solely on clinical criteria or determining treatment timing based on elapsed time from sepsis onset, a mediator-guided approach integrating biological phenotype, inflammatory biomarkers, and clinical severity may help identify patients most likely to benefit. However, most of the current supportive clinical evidence is derived from subgroup or post hoc analyses of large randomized trials and observational studies. This approach therefore remains hypothesis-generating and requires prospective validation in biomarker-enriched, phenotype-guided randomized trials to determine whether hemoadsorption can become an integral component of precision medicine for sepsis.
