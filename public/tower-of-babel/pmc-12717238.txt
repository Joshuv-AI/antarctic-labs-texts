Characteristics of salivary IgA responses to oral microbiota in patients with oral lichen planus
Authors: Yuya Takeda, Nahoko Kato-Kogoe, Shoichi Sakaguchi, Shinsuke Ieda, Yukari Tasaka, Naoki Mizobata, Michi Omori, Wataru Hamada, Shota Nakamura, Takashi Nakano, Takaaki Ueno, Tatsushi Matsumura
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractOral lichen planus (OLP) is a chronic inflammatory disease of the oral mucosa with a risk of malignant transformation. Oral bacteria are associated with OLP development and progression; however, the immune response, especially the salivary immunoglobulin A (IgA) response to these bacteria remains poorly understood. Therefore, this study aimed to characterize the salivary microbiota in patients with OLP and evaluate the corresponding salivary IgA response. Stimulated saliva samples were collected from 21 patients with OLP and 56 control participants, and 16S rRNA metagenomic analysis was performed to characterize the composition of the microbiota. In addition, IgA-enriched and non-enriched fractions from the saliva samples were separated via magnetic-activated cell sorting, followed by 16S rRNA metagenomic analysis. To evaluate differences in IgA responses to each bacterium between the two groups, we calculated the IgA index. The diversity and bacterial composition of the salivary microbiota differed considerably between the OLP and control groups. Several bacterial genera, including Leptotrichia, Fusobacterium, and Streptococcus, showed markedly lower IgA index in the OLP group than the control group. In conclusion, patients with OLP exhibited a distinctive salivary IgA response to salivary microbiota, suggesting a potential association between OLP and this altered response.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-27882-1.Keywords: OLP, Microbiota, Saliva, Immunoglobulin A, 16S rRNA, IgA-seqSubject terms: Biomarkers, Diseases, Microbiology

Introduction

Oral lichen planus (OLP) is a chronic inflammatory disease affecting the oral mucosa, with an estimated global prevalence of approximately 1%1,2. OLP lesions are often chronically persistent, associated with recurrent pain, and difficult to cure completely3,4. Furthermore, OLP is classified as a potentially malignant oral disorder5 owing to the potential risk of malignant transformation6–8. Treatment for OLP is only symptomatic and not curative, and it requires long-term management3,9. Characterizing the oral environment of patients with OLP facilitates comprehensive evaluation and effective management.

OLP is an immune-mediated inflammatory disease of the oral mucosa; however, its pathogenesis remains unclear9–11. Serum IgA levels are elevated in patients with OLP, and tissue IgA levels are associated with disease severity12. Thus, systemic and local humoral immune responses may play important roles in the immunopathology of OLP. Although they do not directly cause OLP, oral bacteria play an important role in the development and progression of OLP13–15. Specific oral bacteria, such as Helicobacter pylori, periodontopathic bacteria, and Escherichia coli, can promote OLP progression by damaging the oral mucosal epithelial barrier, invading the lamina propria, stimulating inflammatory cytokine production, and inducing immune dysfunction16–19. OLP characterized by tissue responses that lead to loss of tolerance and specific alterations in mucosal immunity. Recent evidence indicates that changes in the balance of the oral microbiota are associated with these changes in tissue integrity and immune regulation. That is, several recent studies have shown that the diversity and composition of the salivary microbiota20–22 as well as the microbiota of the oral mucosal tissues23–25 in patients with OLP differ from those in healthy individuals. Since the oral microbiota is closely related to local oral mucosal homeostasis and immune responsiveness26,27, characterization of the oral microbiota of patients with OLP may be useful in its management. However, information regarding this purpose is lacking.

Salivary immunoglobulin A (IgA) antibodies are the first line of host defense against oral immunity and play an important role in the immune defense of oral mucosal surfaces28–30. IgA has been recognized for decades as a key component of mucosal defense against pathogens; more recently, its role in regulating the gut microbiota has also gained increasing attention31,32. In the gut, IgA contributes to establishing symbiotic relationships among host microbiota, which are essential for maintaining homeostasis and preventing disease. Evidence is accumulating regarding the relationship between IgA and the gut microbiota in human pathophysiology33,34. However, understanding of IgA’s interaction with the oral microbiota remains limited, and reports on its association with disease are scarce35,36. Since previous studies have suggested an association between the oral microbiota and OLP, this study focused on the involvement of IgA responses in OLP-related oral microbiota.

Since OLP is associated with chronic inflammation and an abnormal immune response in the oral mucosa, we hypothesized that the salivary IgA response to the oral microbiota may be altered. Therefore, in the present study, we investigated the characteristics of the salivary IgA response to oral microbiota in patients with OLP using a combination of magnetic-activated cell sorting (MACS) and 16S rRNA gene sequencing (IgA-SEQ). This study is the first to evaluate IgA responses to oral microbiota using saliva from patients with OLP. Previous findings suggest an association between OLP and the oral microbiota; however, little is known about the host’s immune responses to this microbiota. This study suggests the importance of evaluating the oral microbiota in OLP from the perspective of the host’s responses. Elucidating the interactions between oral microbiota and salivary IgA responses in OLP may lead to the development of novel approaches for oral management of OLP, such as modulating immune responses and promoting changes in the oral microbiota.

Results

Characteristics of the participants

A total of 77 participants were included, with a median age of 71 years (1st–3rd quartiles: 67–76); 27% were male. No significant differences were observed in the basic characteristics and oral status between participants in the total OLP (n = 21) and control groups (n = 56) or between the OLP (n = 12) and control groups (n = 42) evaluated for IgA response, except for BMI (Table 1 and Supplementary Table 1). Salivary IgA concentration averaged 238.8 ± 25.9 μg/mL in the OLP group and 244.5 ± 15.9 μg/mL in the control group, with no significant difference between the two groups (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Differences in salivary microbiota between OLP and control groups

The salivary bacteria were classified into 17 phyla, 27 classes, 44 orders, 78 families, and 119 genera. At the phylum level, the predominant taxa (accounting for >10% of the total sequences in all groups) were Firmicutes, Bacteroidetes, Proteobacteria, and Actinobacteria (Fig. 1a). These four phyla comprised 90.7% of the OLP group and 90.2% of the control group. At the genus level, 76 genera were present in the OLP group and 117 genera were present in the control group. The 20 most abundant genera in the OLP and control groups accounted for 89.8% and 87.6% of total abundance, respectively (Fig. 1b).

Analysis of alpha diversity in the salivary microbiota among the OLP and control groups revealed that bacterial species richness was significantly reduced in the OLP group compared to that in the control group (observed operational taxonomic unit (OTU) index, p = 0.043; Fig. 2a), and no significant difference was observed in Shannon index, (p = 0.131).

Furthermore, the control group showed significantly increased abundances of Prevotella, Peptostreptococcus, and Bulleidia compared to those in the OLP group (Fig. 2e).

Differences in microbiota between IgA-enriched and IgA-nonenriched fractions

Alpha diversity was not significantly different between the IgA-enriched and IgA-non-enriched fractions in the OLP group (observed OTU index, p = 0.644; Shannon index, p = 0.773; Fig. 3a). However, in the control group, alpha diversity was significantly higher in the IgA-enriched fraction than in the IgA-non-enriched fraction (observed OTU index, p < 0.001; Shannon index, p < 0.001; Fig. 3b). In the OLP and control groups, Principal Coordinate Analysis (PCoA) with unweighted and weighted UniFrac distances demonstrated that the microbiota differed between IgA-enriched and IgA-non-enriched fractions. This difference in composition between the two groups was validated via PERMANOVA based on unweighted and weighted UniFrac distance metrics (p < 0.05; Fig. 3c–f).

The LEfSe analysis presented in Fig. 4 revealed that Prevotella, Neisseria, Haemophilus, Veillonella, Granulicatella were abundant in the IgA-enriched fraction in the OLP group, while in the control group, in addition these genera, Streptococcus, Peptostreptococcus, and Fusobacterium were abundant. Selenomonas was abundant in the IgA-nonenriched fraction in the OLP group, and in addition this, Rothia and Bradyrhizobium were abundant in the control group. Overall, IgA responses differed between bacterial genera, and the control group tended to have many bacterial genera with significantly different IgA responses in addition to the same bacterial genera as the OLP group.

Differences in IgA responses to specific bacteria between the OLP and control groups

To evaluate the differences in the IgA responses to each bacterium between the OLP and control groups, the IgA index was calculated for 17 bacterial genera with average abundances above 1% in each fraction. The bacterial genera associated with the OLP and control groups were identified (Fig. 5a). Several bacterial genera, including Leptotrichia, Fusobacterium, and Streptococcus, showed significantly lower IgA indices in the OLP group than in the control group (p < 0.01; Fig. 5b). However, none of the bacterial genera showed significantly higher IgA indices in the OLP group than in the control group. These results revealed differences in IgA responses to several bacterial genera between the OLP and control groups.

Discussion

In this study, we characterized the salivary microbiota in patients with OLP and examined salivary IgA responses to them. We found that salivary IgA responses varied among bacterial genera and that the characteristics of bacteria-specific IgA responses to the salivary microbiota differed between patients with OLP and control groups. These data suggest that OLP may be associated with the salivary IgA response to microbiota. Previous studies have shown that the oral microbiota in patients with OLP differs from that in control groups. This pilot study is the first to investigate the relationship between OLP and salivary IgA responses to the oral microbiota. This study emphasizes the importance of understanding specific IgA responses to oral bacteria, rather than focusing solely on their relative abundance. This may provide a novel perspective on interactions between the oral microbiome and the host immune system in OLP.

Alpha-diversity analysis of the salivary microbiota showed that species richness was significantly lower in the OLP group than in the control group. This decreasing trend in microbial diversity was consistent with the results of previous studies20,24. This decreased species richness may be a result of inflammation of the oral mucosa in patients with OLP, with previous reports indicating that high levels of inflammation in the oral mucosa reduce the diversity of the oral microbiota37.

The bacterial composition of the salivary microbiota of patients with OLP was clearly different from that of the control group, and LEfSe analysis revealed that the abundances of Lautropia, Actinomyces, and Campylobacter were higher in the OLP group than in the control group, while the abundances of Prevotella and Peptostreptococcus were lower in the OLP group than in the control group, confirming the results of previous studies20,22,24,38. Notably, Campylobacter spp. has previously been reported to be abundant in the gut microbiota of patients with ulcerative colitis, a condition characterized by chronic inflammation39. Thus, it may be involved in the pathogenesis of OLP, a chronic inflammation of the oral mucosa. The oral microbiota of patients with OLP varies depending on age and sex22,24 and the severity of OLP23,40. In addition, since the oral microbiota is also affected by factors such as race, lifestyle, hygiene, diet, and systemic conditions, such as diabetes and hypertension. This study did not analyze these factors, but future large-scale studies that incorporate them may prove valuable.

In this study, we evaluated salivary IgA responses to the oral microbiota. In recent years, the role of the IgA response to the intestinal microbiota in health and disease has attracted attention, and knowledge on this topic is increasing41,42. The IgA response to the intestinal microbiota is altered by specific conditions, such as inflammatory bowel disease, aging, and nutritional status31,43. However, little is known about the IgA response to oral microbiota28,44. Our results showed that the bacterial composition of the IgA-enriched fraction differed significantly from that of the non-IgA-enriched fraction, and salivary IgA responses specific to bacteria in the salivary microbiota were observed. Notably, the strong IgA responses to Haemophilus, Veillonella, and Granulicatella as well as the weak IgA responses to Selenomonas observed in both the OLP and control groups in this study were consistent with our previous study44. These salivary IgA responses are worthy of further validation as they may be related to the maintenance of the oral microbiota by limiting or promoting bacterial colonization28. The IgA immune responses to the oral microbiota observed in this study result from a complex immune process. The oral microbiota is likely reciprocally associated with immune responses to the enzymes released from each bacterium and to the bacteria themselves. For instance, some oral commensal bacteria produce IgA proteases, which may influence the oral microbiota and the corresponding IgA response. In addition, recently, a new bacterium that degrades IgA associated with the gut microbiota has been discovered in mice, demonstrating the role of commensal bacteria in immunodeficiency45. Therefore, unknown bacteria capable of degrading IgA may also exist within the oral microbiota. These bacteria could be an important role in the interaction between the oral microbiota and the immune response, which is noteworthy.

Our study identified several specific bacteria that differed in salivary IgA responses between the OLP and control groups. Although the factors contributing to the interaction between the salivary IgA response and oral microbiota are not fully understood, evidence of this interaction changing under certain conditions is increasing. Animal experiments have reported that IgA-deficient mice elicit oral dysbiosis35, and changes in oral microbiota due to poor oral hygiene and experimental periodontitis could induce changes in the salivary IgA response36,46. The results of this study on the characteristics of the interaction between salivary IgA response and oral microbiota in patients with OLP suggested that further research may identify microbial components that interact with the host immune system in OLP. Although this study could not perform analyses considering oral hygiene status or the effects of periodontitis, we believe conducting large-scale studies that incorporate these factors in the future may prove valuable.

Leptotrichia, Fusobacterium, and Streptococcus, which showed significantly lower salivary IgA responses in the OLP group than in the control group, were the major bacteria that play important roles in dental plaque structure and function47,48. Notably, a weak salivary IgA response to Fusobacterium spp. was associated with OLP in this study. Fusobacterium spp. is abundant in the mucosa of OLP24,25, and the potential association between OLP severity has been suggested19,49. The high abundance of Fusobacterium spp. in the oral cavity of patients with OLP may be related to a low salivary IgA response to this bacterium, which prevents the elimination of bacteria in the lesion. On the other hand, Streptococcus is less abundant in the mucosa of OLP21,25,50. Salivary IgA recognizes Streptococcus spp. and recruits bacteria to the mucus layer of the oral mucosal surface28. Thus, the low IgA response to this bacterium may be related to the low abundance of Streptococcus in OLP. In summary, our results suggested that the salivary IgA response to the oral microbiota may influence the proportion of certain bacteria and may be associated with the composition of the microbiota; however, the role of IgA in the regulation of the microbiota warrants further investigation.

Our study had several limitations. First, the 16S rRNA metagenome analysis used in this study emphasized the composition of bacterial species and community diversity and evaluated the relative abundance of bacteria. In the future, additional information on the absolute quantity and functionality of specific bacteria should be obtained through supplementation with high-resolution metagenome sequence analysis technology. Furthermore, analysis of transcripts, proteins, and metabolites can help identify the functional characteristics of microbial communities. Furthermore, besides bacteria, various viruses are also present in the oral cavity. In recent years, advances in viral analysis techniques have revealed that viruses play an important role in oral health51. The results obtained in this study suggest an association between the IgA response to oral bacteria and OLP, although viral infection may also be involved in this association. Viral infection has been suggested to potentially induce changes in the oral microbiota and IgA response. For example, there are reports showing an association between the induction of IgA in saliva due to SARS-CoV-2 infection and changes in the oral microbiota52. Therefore, we believe it will be valuable to evaluate the oral virome and its associated IgA response in patients with OLP, in conjunction with our data, in future studies.

Second, although this study confirmed that salivary IgA secretion exhibited no difference between the groups, further research is needed to gain a deeper understanding of the effects of salivary IgA on the microbiota of patients with OLP. Our findings emphasize the need to focus not only on the quantity of IgA but also on its function. Third, as this was an observational study, causality could not be established. Further research, including animal experiments, is needed to elucidate the functional relationship between the salivary IgA response and oral microbiota in patients with OLP. Furthermore, this study cannot exclude the influence of confounding factors that may affect the oral microbiota. Since the type and severity of OLP have been reported to influence the oral microbiota23,40, identifying an IgA response characteristic of each type could potentially lead to the prediction and diagnosis of OLP using saliva. Future studies should therefore conduct analyses focusing on these factors. However, this study was preliminary and provided a basis for characterizing the effect of the salivary IgA response on oral microbiota in patients with OLP.

In conclusion, we found that the salivary IgA response is distinctive in patients with OLP. Further elucidation of specific salivary IgA responses associated with OLP may contribute to the development of novel diagnostic and therapeutic strategies, including approaches to modulate immune responses and alter the oral microbiota.

Methods

Participants

The study was conducted in accordance with the guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki and its latest revisions. This study was approved by the ethics review committees of Wakayama Medical University, Kimiidera, Wakayama, Japan (approval number 3916) and Osaka Medical and Pharmaceutical University, Takatsuki, Japan (approval number 2145). Written informed consent was obtained from all the participants.

This study was a case control study included 21 patients with OLP and 56 control participants. Patients with OLP were defined as those diagnosed with OLP based on pathological examinations at the Department of Oral and Maxillofacial Surgery, Wakayama Medical University Hospital, Kimiidera, Wakayama, Japan, or the Department of Oral and Maxillofacial Surgery, Osaka Medical and Pharmaceutical University Hospital, Takatsuki, Japan. All patients with OLP had biopsies confirming their diagnoses. Patients who received OLP treatment within at least three months prior to sample collection were excluded. Control participants were defined as those with no history of OLP.

The exclusion criteria for the OLP and control groups were as follows: participants with other oral mucosal diseases; those who had received antibiotic treatment at least one month prior to sample collection; patients currently receiving treatment for infectious diseases, autoimmune disease, renal or liver failure, or malignant tumors; and those who were currently pregnant. Participants were excluded based on self-reports and medical records. Participants’ characteristics are presented in Table 1.

Saliva sample collection and oral examination

Saliva samples were collected via the cotton method using a SalivaBio® oral swab and swab storage tube (Salimetrics, Irvine, PA, USA) according to previously reported methods53. Briefly, saliva samples were collected at least 2 h after eating or brushing, immediately frozen, and stored at − 80 ℃ until DNA extraction. Some of the saliva samples used in this study were part of our previously reported samples44. Following sample collection, a full-mouth clinical examination was performed by a dental specialist. Oral health status was assessed by evaluating the number of remaining teeth, denture use, periodontal disease status, and the presence of other inflammatory findings such as abscess formation. Periodontitis was staged according to the CDC–AAP case definitions for periodontitis surveillance54.

Quantification of IgA in saliva samples

The amount of secretory IgA in the saliva samples was quantified according to our previously reported method44 using a salivary secretory IgA indirect enzyme immunoassay kit (Salimetrics LLC, Carlsbad, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

DNA extraction, 16S rRNA sequencing, and taxonomic classification

DNA extraction, 16S rRNA sequencing, and taxonomic classification were performed according to our previous methods55. Briefly, DNA was extracted from saliva samples after homogenization using GENE PREP STAR PI-480 (Kurashiki Spinning Co., Ltd., Osaka, Japan) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The V1-V2 region of the 16S rRNA gene was amplified using PCR, Index (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) was added, and library preparation was performed to confirm the quality. A MiSeq Reagent Kit v2 (Illumina) was used to generate a 250-bp paired-end sequence over 500 cycles. An average of 28,758 sequence reads were denoised using DADA2 in Quantitative Insights into Microbial Ecology 2 (QIIME2) version 2021.04 and then quality filtered. The minimum depth cutoff for rarefaction was set at 10,000, and each OTU was assigned to the curated Greengenes 13_8 reference database.

Evaluation of IgA response to salivary microbiota

To characterize the IgA response to salivary microbiota, antibody-based bacterial sorting combined with 16S ribosomal RNA gene sequencing (IgA-SEQ) was performed according to our previously reported methods44. Among the participants described above, patients with OLP (n = 12) and control participants (n = 42) were selected, and IgA-enriched and IgA-non-enriched fractions were separated from their saliva. The baseline characteristics of the study participants are presented in Table S1.

Following the method reported in our previous study44, saliva samples were stained with phycoerythrin (PE)-labeled mouse anti-human IgA (Miltenyi Biotec Cat# 130-093-128), whereafter anti-PE magnetically-activated cell sorting beads (Miltenyi Biotec Cat# 130-105-639) and MS columns (Miltenyi Biotec Cat# 130-042-201) were used to separate and collect IgA-enriched (IgA( +)) and IgA-nonenriched (IgA(−)) fractions. For each of the presort, IgA( +), and IgA(−) fractions, DNA extraction, 16S rRNA sequencing, and taxonomic classification were performed as described in the previous section.

To compare IgA response levels against specific bacteria between the OLP and control groups, we calculated the IgA index using our previously reported method47. In this study, the IgA index was evaluated for bacterial genera with an average abundance exceeding 1% in both the IgA( +) and IgA(−) fractions. The IgA index was calculated as the log ratio of the difference between IgA( +) and IgA(−) bacteria over the sum of IgA( +) and IgA(−) bacteria, (IgA index = − (log(IgA( +) taxon) − log(IgA(−) taxon))/(log(IgA( +) taxon) + log(IgA(−) taxon), according to previous reports56,57.

Statistical analysis

Intergroup comparisons of each parameter were performed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. The statistical software JMP Student Edition 18 (SAS Institute, Inc., Cary, NC, USA) was used for database construction and data analyses. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

As described in our earlier study47, to determine the richness and evenness of bacterial communities, alpha-diversity was assessed using the observed OTU and Shannon index and was compared among groups using the Kruskal–Wallis test. The Shannon index was used as a measure of alpha diversity, reflecting both species richness and the evenness of species distribution. Beta diversity among groups was assessed using phylogenetic tree-based indices and unweighted and weighted UniFrac distance metrics and visualized using PCoA. In addition, the significance of the compositional differences between the groups was assessed using PERMANOVA. QIIME2 software was used for these analyses. A linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe) algorithm58 was used to detect bacteria with different abundance ratios among groups. The alpha parameter for the LEfSe pairwise test was set to 0.05, and the threshold for the log score was set to 3.0.
