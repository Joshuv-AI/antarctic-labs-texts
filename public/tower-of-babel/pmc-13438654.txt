Charger placement optimization in wireless sensor networks using hybrid graph coloring and Enhanced Aquila Optimization
Authors: P Neelagandan, S Balaji
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe sustainability of wireless sensor networks critically depends on intelligent and efficient charger deployment. This paper proposes a two-stage optimization framework that determine the total number of chargers needed to recharge and the optimal position of charger. In the first stage, a hybrid algorithm combining degree-based saturation and Grundy coloring efficiently determines the minimal number of chargers required. In the second stage, an Enhanced Aquila Optimization algorithm, inspired by eagle hunting behavior, identifies optimal charger locations under coverage and power constraints. Experimental results show that the enhancement method improves coverage by 6% compared to the standard algorithm. The Enhanced Aquila Optimization achieves 99% sensor coverage with faster convergence than Raindrop (82%), Grey Wolf (85%), Black Hole (89%), Dragonfly (89%), and standard Aquila Optimization (93%). Statistical analysis confirms the significant performance difference between the proposed and existing algorithms. Overall, the proposed approach provides a practical, scalable, and energy-efficient solution for sustainable wireless sensor network deployment.Keywords: Wireless chargers, Degree of saturation, Grundy coloring, Enhanced Aquila Optimization, Optimal position of chargersSubject terms: Energy science and technology, Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Wireless sensor networks (WSNs) have become fundamental infrastructure across diverse domains including environmental monitoring, industrial automation, and national security1,2. The widespread deployment of these battery-constrained devices has intensified research into sustainable energy solutions, particularly wireless rechargeable sensor networks (WRSNs)3. While recent advances in metaheuristic optimization4 and wireless power transfer5 show promise, the critical challenge of optimal charger placement remains unresolved, especially in large-scale random deployments6.

The energy sustainability of WRSNs depends on two key factors: (1) minimizing the number of charging devices to reduce infrastructure costs, and (2) optimizing their spatial distribution for maximum coverage efficiency. Traditional approaches have treated these as separate problems, leading to suboptimal solutions. Evolutionary algorithms such as particle swarm optimization7 and genetic algorithms8 focus solely on placement optimization without considering the minimum charger requirement, while graph-theoretic methods address conflict resolution but neglect spatial constraints. This paper presents a novel unified framework that combines graph theory with bio-inspired optimization to simultaneously address both challenges. The research objective of this paper:

To optimize charger deployment in wireless rechargeable sensor networks the proposed together approach Degree of Saturation-Grundy Coloring Algorithm (DS-GCA) and Enhanced Aquila Optimizer (EAO) form a computationally efficient framework that outperforms standalone optimization techniques in both charger reduction and placement accuracy. The practical implications of this work are significant for several application domains9. In precision agriculture, where soil sensors require frequent recharge, our method reduces infrastructure costs while maintaining complete coverage. For industrial IoT deployments, the optimized charger placement minimizes energy waste during simultaneous charging operations10. Border security networks benefit from the extended operational lifetime enabled by efficient recharge scheduling.

Related work

The problem of optimal charger placement in WSNs has been extensively studied due to its significance in prolonging network lifetime and reducing maintenance costs. Effective placement ensures that sensor nodes can be recharged wirelessly without human intervention, which is critical for large-scale and inaccessible deployments.

Early foundational studies laid the groundwork for understanding energy provisioning and wireless power transfer in WSNs. Balaji and Arivudainambi11 proposed an approach using the Daubechies wavelet algorithm to optimize charger placement. This algorithm leverages the translation and dilation properties of Daubechies wavelets, allowing chargers to adjust both vertically and horizontally, as well as repositioning redundant chargers to more effective locations. The method was compared with four other wavelets: Haar, Daubechies 2 (db2), Biorthogonal 55 (bio55), and Symlets and demonstrated superior performance. He et al.12 addressed the challenge of energy scheduling and routing for mobile chargers in rechargeable networks, proposing models for efficient energy delivery. Similarly, Xie et al.13 explored static and mobile charging strategies under different constraints, highlighting trade-offs between cost, efficiency, and energy balance.

As the problem grew in complexity, researchers began employing metaheuristic optimization algorithms to tackle the non-linear, multi-objective nature of charger placement. For instance, Zhang et al.14 modeled the problem as an optimization task and applied heuristic search strategies to determine optimal charger locations. Alshahrani and Shoaib15 implemented a GA to optimize placement while satisfying coverage constraints, demonstrating the potential of evolutionary strategies for this domain. Qureshi et al.16 used PSO to deploy multiple static chargers and evaluated its performance in terms of convergence rate and energy efficiency.

Graph-based methods have also been explored to reduce charger redundancy by identifying recharge-compatible sensor groups. Molla and Rha17 extended this idea by combining Voronoi diagram partitioning with a hybrid GA-PSO framework to improve charger placement accuracy, especially in irregularly shaped deployment areas.

Recent advancements have incorporated intelligent and adaptive techniques, such as reinforcement learning and swarm-based algorithms. Zhang et al.18 applied a deep reinforcement learning (DRL) approach for dynamic charger placement, allowing chargers to adapt their positions based on real-time sensor data. Patil and Patil19 utilized the Gray Wolf Optimizer to optimize the deployment of static chargers, achieving faster convergence and higher solution quality compared to conventional methods. Tariq et al.20 proposed an enhanced whale optimization algorithm to minimize the number of chargers and improve sensor coverage reliability.

Despite these efforts, most existing methods focus solely on spatial optimization or on minimizing the number of chargers, without effectively combining both. Neelagandan et al.21 proposed a Grundy coloring algorithm to determine the minimum number of chargers and used the black hole algorithm to find their optimal placement. Neelagandan and Balaji22 proposed a Degree of Saturation approach to determine the minimum number of chargers and employed the Enhanced Grey Wolf Optimization algorithm for optimal charger placement. However, the enhancement phase in their method primarily identified ideal charger positions without considering sensors that were already covered, resulting in an incomplete enhancement process However, their results provided limited improvements compared to the outcomes presented in this work. This paper proposes an enhanced framework that achieves superior solutions by improving both the number of chargers and the placement efficiency. Furthermore, emerging metaheuristics, such as the Aquila Optimizer (AO), inspired by the hunting strategies of Aquila eagles, remain underexplored in this context. AO has shown strong global search capability and robustness in high-dimensional spaces, yet its application to charger placement is still limited.

To address these gaps, the present work proposes a novel hybrid framework that integrates a graph-coloring approach (using DSATUR and Grundy heuristics) to minimize charger count and an EAO for determining their optimal spatial placement. This dual-phase method ensures both charger efficiency and optimal deployment, making it particularly well suited for dense and large-scale sensor networks Table 1.

Determining charger requirement using hybrid degree of saturation and grundy coloring approach

Efficient charger deployment is vital for ensuring reliable sensor recharging while minimizing energy consumption in wireless sensor networks. The proposed hybrid coloring framework, DS-GCA, integrates the Degree of Saturation Algorithm (DSA) and the Grundy Coloring Algorithm (GCA) to exploit the strengths of both heuristics. As shown in Table 2, DS-GCA consistently uses fewer colors (chargers) than either algorithm individually. This improvement stems from the complementary nature of the two strategies rather than the strengths of either one alone.

Individually, DSA excels at dynamic constraint management. It prioritizes the sensor with the highest saturation degree—that is, the number of distinct chargers already assigned to its neighbors. This ensures that highly constrained sensors (those with limited feasible charger groups) are handled early, preventing the need for additional chargers later. However, DSA becomes less effective when many sensors initially share the same saturation degree, especially in sparse graphs or early stages of coloring.

Grundy Coloring, on the other hand, is driven by ordinary degree, giving priority to sensors with more neighbors. This topological awareness ensures that structurally influential sensors—those likely to create conflicts later—are assigned colors earlier. However, Grundy’s method alone lacks color-awareness and may unnecessarily introduce new colors if used in isolation.

The hybrid DS-GCA method resolves the weaknesses of both. DSA serves as the primary selection rule, ensuring color-efficient assignments, while GCA provides a powerful tie-breaking mechanism when saturation degrees are equal. In such situations, selecting the sensor with the highest ordinary degree allows the algorithm to proactively handle the most interconnected nodes, preventing cascading conflicts later in the process. Finally, selecting the lowest-index sensor in persistent ties ensures deterministic and reproducible behavior.

Aquila optimizer: biological basis and mathematical modeling

The Aquila Optimizer is a recent addition to the family of nature-inspired metaheuristic algorithms, introduced by Abualigah et al24.. AO is inspired by the hunting strategies of eagles belonging to the genus Aquila, which exhibit a diverse range of predatory behaviors adapted to different environmental contexts. These behaviors have been mathematically modeled and incorporated into the AO framework to balance exploration and exploitation in the solution search space.

Biological inspiration

The AO algorithm emulates four key hunting strategies observed in Aquila eagles, as illustrated in Fig. 4.

Simulation result

Simulation experiments were conducted using the 12th Gen Intel (R) Core (TM) laptop i3-1215U (1.20 GHz) in MATLAB version R2025a to validate the performance of the proposed EAO. Prior works have explored wavelet-based methods for sensor recharging26, and techniques for enhancing network coverage27,28. In this study, EAO was compared with several nature-inspired algorithms including Raindrop, Grey Wolf Algorithm (GWA), Blackhole Algorithm (BHA), Dragonfly Algorithm (DA), and the standard AO to assess its effectiveness. The location of sensors significantly impacts the QOC; therefore, sensor positions were kept constant across all algorithms to ensure a fair comparison. The DS-GCA was used to determine the minimum number of chargers necessary to efficiently recharge sensors within the network. Simulations were carried out for sensor counts ranging from 50 to 1250, and charger ranges from 10 to 250, across two different deployment areas: a smaller region of 100 × 100 units and a larger region of 1000 × 1000 units. The number of iterations and the population size were both fixed at 100 for all the evaluated algorithms, and each experiment was conducted over 10 independent runs to ensure statistical reliability.

The primary goal of each simulation was to identify the optimal charger positions to ensure full recharging of all sensors while maintaining minimal deployment cost. In the proposed framework, deployment cost is associated with the number of chargers required to recharge the sensor network. By applying the IMDG algorithm, the minimum number of chargers necessary for complete coverage is determined, thereby reducing deployment cost. Energy efficiency is represented by the Quality of Coverage (QoC), which measures the number of sensors that can be recharged by the deployed chargers. Maximizing QoC ensures efficient utilization of charger resources while minimizing redundant coverage and unnecessary energy consumption.

ANOVA is a statistical technique that determines whether there are significant differences among the means of three or more independent groups. It evaluates the ratio of variance between group means to variance within the groups using the F-statistic. The hypotheses are defined as:

Null hypothesis (): All algorithm performance means are equal.

Null hypothesis (): All algorithm performance means are equal.

Alternative hypothesis (): At least one algorithm’s mean differs significantly.

Conclusion

This paper presents a comprehensive two-stage optimization framework for efficient charger deployment in wireless sensor networks, addressing both the minimization of the number of chargers and their optimal placement to ensure maximum sensor coverage. The integration of degree-based saturation with Grundy coloring in the first stage successfully determines the minimal number of chargers required, reducing infrastructure costs compared to traditional methods.

In the second stage, the Enhanced Aquila Optimization algorithm, inspired by the hunting strategies of eagles, effectively optimizes charger placement by minimizing energy transfer distances while satisfying coverage and power constraints. Experimental results demonstrate that Enhanced Aquila Optimization not only achieves superior sensor coverage of 99% but also converges significantly faster than other state-of-the-art metaheuristic algorithms such as Raindrop, Grey Wolf, Black Hole, Dragonfly, and the standard Aquila Optimization.

Furthermore, Enhanced Aquila Optimization balances computational efficiency and solution quality, making it suitable for real-time and resource-constrained wireless sensor network environments. The proposed framework thus offers a practical, scalable, and robust solution for prolonging the network lifetime and ensuring uninterrupted sensor operation in wireless sensor networks. But in this study is based entirely on theoretical modeling and simulation to determine the minimum number of chargers and their optimal placement for recharging all sensors. A real-world testbed implementation has not yet been conducted, which is a limitation of this work.

Future work may explore dynamic charger deployment adapting to network changes, fault tolerance improvements, and integration with energy harvesting techniques to further enhance wireless sensor network sustainability.
