Alternative isoforms and phase separation of Ref1 repress morphogenesis in Cryptococcus
Authors: Nathan K. Glueck, Xiaofeng Xie, Xiaorong Lin
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SUMMARYCryptococcus neoformans, the causative agent of cryptococcosis and a representative of the Basidiomycota phylum of Fungi, is a valuable model for our understanding of eukaryotic/fungal biology. Negative feedback is a well-documented mechanism across Eukarya to regulate developmental transitions. Here, we describe a repressor of the yeast-to-hypha transition, Ref1, which completes a negative feedback loop driven by the master regulator of hyphal morphogenesis, Znf2, during sexual development. Alternative transcription of Ref1, driven by Znf2, produces a functionally distinct Ref1 isoform. Isoform-specific capacity for phase separation imparts this functional distinction, making Ref1 a stronger repressor and more vulnerable to proteolytic degradation. The multimodal nature of Ref1 provides versatility that allows cells to fine-tune Ref1 activity to suit developmental context. This work reveals a mechanism by which phase separation allows a transcriptional program to tailor its own repression to guide an organism through morphological transition.

INTRODUCTION

The rise of higher-order eukaryotes depended on the ability of cells to differentiate and organize into multicellular, specialized bodies. This multicellular development necessitates tight regulation of intra- and intercellular signaling pathways. Developmental signaling requires precision tuning to avoid detrimental cellular responses, robustness to ensure cells receiving signals will respond, and versatility to elicit context-dependent responses. Feedback loops, both positive and negative, represent a well-documented strategy for achieving these requirements.1,2 Negative feedback occurs when activation of a signaling pathway generates a signal that dampens or sets spatiotemporal limits on the activated pathway. For example, the transforming growth factor β (TGF-β) family of growth factors, which control a wide range of development and growth processes, are transduced to the nucleus by Smad family proteins where they form transcriptional complexes. This transcriptional program then produces a subclass of inhibitory Smads to block the TGF-β signaling that produced them.3–6 TGF-β signaling is conserved in higher eukaryotes, including mammals, worms, and flies, and deficiency/dysregulation of inhibitory Smads causes human disease such as congenital heart disease and malignancy, emphasizing the importance of negative feedback in this pathway. Kingdom Fungi is composed of unicellular and multicellular organisms, and several popular model systems have been established to study development, including yeasts Saccharomyces cerevisiae and Schizosaccharomyces pombe and filamentous fungi Aspergillus nidulans and Neurospora crassa. All of them belong to the phylum Ascomycota. The past two decades have seen the rise of the clinically important fungal pathogen Cryptococcus neoformans as a model, which is dimorphic and represents another main branch of the fungal phyletic tree that includes mushroom species, Basidiomycota. Accordingly, study of C. neoformans promises to contribute to a more holistic understanding of fungal biology in particular and eukaryotic biology more generally.

C. neoformans is a ubiquitous environmental fungus capable of opportunistic infection of human hosts. C. neoformans can gain access to alveolar passages when airborne spores or desiccated yeast cells are inhaled. When this happens, the fungus is cleared by the host immune system or remains dormant. However, a weakened immune system can allow for its propagation and systemic dissemination to other organs including the brain where the infection leads to fatal cryptococcal meningoencephalitis (CME).7 CME diagnoses and CME-related deaths each number in the hundreds of thousands annually, with HIV+/AIDS patients comprising a majority of cases,8 making C. neoformans a World Health Organization-sanctioned high-priority subject of research for its impact on global public health.9

A defining aspect of many fungal pathogens is the ability to switch cell morphology between yeast and hyphae. For Cryptococcus, the yeast morphotype is the predominant morphotype associated with human infection and the preferred morphotype for vegetative growth in nutrient-replete conditions. However, in response to external mating stimuli and/or environmental stressors such as nutrient limitation and dehydration, Cryptococcus can participate in sexual reproduction, which necessitates hyphal morphogenesis followed by sporulation (Figure 1A).10 The resilience of hyphae and sexual spores in the face of environmental stress11–13 and the genetic diversity generated through sexual reproduction contribute to cryptococcal adaptation and persistence in a broad range of climates to maintain the vast environmental reservoir to which potential human hosts are exposed.14 Additionally, our recent work established a role for sexual development in mediating cryptococcal immunogenicity and, potentially, cryptococcal latency and activation in mammalian hosts.15–17 Our lab continues to exploit the immunogenic and protective capability of hyphal cells for cryptococcal vaccine development even under the condition of host deficiency in CD4+ T cells (which mimics AIDS patients).18

During bisexual reproduction, yeast cells of opposite mating types, MATα and MATα, recognize each other through pheromone signaling and fuse to form a dikaryotic zygote (Figure 1A). A dikaryotic hypha extends from the zygote, eventually culminating in the formation of a club-like structure called a basidium at the apical tip. In the basidium, the two parental nuclei fuse and undergo meiosis to generate four daughter nuclei that undergo successive rounds of mitosis before being packaged into spores that bud off the basidium (Figure 1A). Sexual development is controlled through transcriptional activation by Mat2 and Znf2.19 Mat2 activates expression of various components of the pheromone-sensing pathway that allow for non-self recognition by α and a yeast cells and subsequent cell-cell fusion to form the zygote.20–22 Mat2 also plays a central role in the prezygotic determination of uniparental mitochondrial DNA inheritance in the progeny from the a parent,23 reinforcing the notion that Mat2 shepherds sexual development in the earliest stages. Acting downstream of Mat2 is Znf2, the master regulator of hyphal morphogenesis. Znf2 transcriptionally activates an arsenal of genes required for the morphological transition from yeast to hyphae.19,21,24 To date, no conditions or genetic manipulations have been found that can override the requirement of Znf2 for filamentation.

As mentioned earlier, development pathways across eukaryotes frequently rely on negative regulatory pressure to exert the control necessary to accomplish complex morphogenic processes.25–29 Specific to fungi, there is precedent for such negative regulation to control hyphal morphogenesis. The human commensal and opportunistic pathogen Candida albicans is dimorphic, and both yeast and filamentous forms are required for biofilm formation.30 An intricate web of regulators is employed to control Candida morphogenesis, among which are multiple examples of mutually antagonistic transcriptional regulators, including Efg1/Wor131,32 and Brg1/Nrg1.33 In the dimorphic plant pathogen Ustilago maydis, the filamentous and pathogenic phase of the life cycle is induced when mating occurs, allowing the formation of a heterodimeric homeodomain transcription factor (TF) complex bE/bW. bE/bW activates clp1, which is required for clamp formation during filamentous growth. Clp1, through interaction with bW, counteracts bE/bW-dependent functions such as cell-cycle arrest and filamentation. Clp1 also represses pheromone signaling through interaction with Rbf1, another bE/bW-induced regulator of filamentation. Thus, Clp1 participates in multiple negative feedback interactions to help guide development in the early stages of pathogenesis and hyphal morphogenesis.34–37 We reasoned that negative regulators of filamentation should exist in C. neoformans given the prevalence of negative feedback mechanisms across biology, particularly for transcriptional regulation of multi-step, complex, and dynamic processes such as fungal morphogenesis. Here, we set out to identify and characterize these regulators.

RESULTS

Deletion of uncharacterized gene CNAG_07982/CND00490 enhances filamentation

In Cryptococcus, transcriptional activators governing the yeast-hypha morphological transition such as Mat2 and Znf2 have been well characterized,19,21,22 but negative regulators of these TFs have not. To identify candidate genes encoding regulators that provide negative feedback during hyphal morphogenesis, we combined transcriptome correlational analysis with phenotypic screening. We postulated that, like MAT2 and ZNF2, genes encoding regulators that provide negative feedback during hyphal morphogenesis are upregulated under conditions that induce filamentation. This would especially be the case if transcription of negative feedback regulators is directly driven by Mat2 or Znf2. However, unlike Mat2 and Znf2, we expected that candidate negative feedback regulators would suppress filamentation and, thus, deletions of these candidates should enhance filamentation.

We therefore reanalyzed previously published RNA-sequencing (RNA-seq) data38 of the hyperfilamentous serotype D reference strain XL280 and six TF deletion mutants cultured on filamentation-inducing V8 juice medium. We calculated correlation coefficients of transcript levels for each cryptococcal gene in relation to ZNF2 and generated a list of top candidate genes that positively correlate with ZNF2 (Table S1). We then screened the knockout mutants of the orthologs of the top ten candidates in the serotype A clinical and reference isolate H99 using the gene knockout library developed by the Hiten Madhani lab. H99 does not self-filament on V8 medium, but we discovered that it can filament on yeast peptone (YP) medium supplemented with glucosamine (YPGlcN).39 By comparing the ability of these mutants to filament on YPGlcN, we identified CNAG_07982/CND00490 as a gene of interest, as its deletion dramatically enhanced filamentation (Figure S1). As expected, CND00490 transcript level was upregulated during hyphal morphogenesis in XL280, similar to MAT2 and ZNF2 (Figure 1B). We noted that a previous study from our lab showed via chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing (ChIP)-seq that Znf2 directly binds the promoter of CND00490, likely driving the increased expression.40 This important context was unsurprising given the strong correlation in expression between the two genes. Critically, deletion of CND00490 in XL280 also enhanced filamentation (Figure 1D). On filamentation-suppressing yeast peptone dextrose (YPD) medium, the XL280 colony displayed smooth yeast morphology, while the CND00490 knockout colony was wrinkled and filamentous. The CNAG_07982 deletion in H99 did not produce this phenotype on YPD medium (Figure S2), but this was unsurprising given that H99 is not normally filamentous, in contrast to XL280. On filamentation-inducing V8 medium, XL280 primarily filamented at the periphery of the colony (white fluffy edge) while remaining mostly in yeast growth in the center. The CND00490 knockout colony, however, displayed robust filamentation across the entire colony, which was in stark contrast to smooth yeast colonies of the mat2D and znf2D strains. Thus, deletion of this gene in serotype D strain enhanced filamentation similar to the deletion of CNAG_07982 in H99 on YPGlcN medium (Figure 1C). These observations indicate that CNAG_07982/CND00490 normally plays a suppressive role in filamentation.

Overexpression of CNAG_07982 suppresses filamentation

We reasoned that CNAG_07982/CND00490, if truly acting as a negative regulator of hyphal morphogenesis, would suppress filamentation when overexpressed. To test this hypothesis, we fused CNAG_07982 with mNeonGreen (mNG) to its C terminus and placed its expression under control of the constitutively active promoter for the translation elongation factor 1α TEF1. XL280 displayed a wrinkled colony morphology associated with the early stages of hyphal morphogenesis on yeast nitrogen base (YNB) medium, but the colony morphology became smooth with CNAG_07982-mNG overexpression. On V8 medium, XL280 showed robust filamentation, which was visibly suppressed with CNAG_007982 overexpression (Figure 2A).

To examine the potential genetic relationship between CNAG_07982 and other known regulators of filamentation, we overexpressed CNAG_07982 in several genetic backgrounds known to confer hyperfilamentous phenotypes. Nrg1 is a transcriptional repressor regulated through cyclic AMP signaling in response to carbohydrate availability.41 As expected, deletion of NRG1 in XL280 induced wrinkled/filamentous colony morphology on YPD medium. Overexpression of CNAG_07982 shifted the colony back to smooth yeast morphology (Figure 2B). In the XL280 background, overexpression of MAT2 and ZNF2 on both YPD and V8 media drove extremely robust filamentation such that CNAG_07982 overexpression did not yield an obvious suppressive effect on filamentation (Figure S2). However, overexpression of MAT2 and ZNF2 in the H99 background induced filamentation on V8 medium, which was nearly abolished by CNAG_07982 overexpression (Figure 2C). Based on these observations, we named this heretofore uncharacterized gene repressor of filamentation 1, or REF1. Importantly, the observations of Ref1’s filamentation-suppressing effect in XL280 nrg1Δ and H99 MAT2OE and ZNF2OE indicate that this factor functions downstream of the filamentation pathways and likely downstream of Znf2, the only known terminal master regulator of filamentation. To put our findings in the broader context of Cryptococcus sexual development, of which filamentation is a crucial stage, we also checked the impact of REF1 deletion and overexpression on the bisexual mating process, specifically cell-cell fusion and sporulation (before and after filamentation, respectively; see Figure 1). We noted that while REF1 deletion and overexpression perturbed cell-cell fusion efficiency, neither was detrimental to the completion of bisexual reproduction, evidenced by sporulation (Figure S3).

Ref1 acts antagonistically to hyphal morphogenesis master regulator Znf2 at the transcript level

We observed that overexpressed Ref1-mNG localized to the nucleus (Figure 3A) and postulated that Ref1 may affect transcription of hyphal morphogenesis genes, possibly through inhibition of Znf2. As the chief activator of hyphal morphogenesis in Cryptococcus,19,21,22 Znf2 activates transcription of many genes associated with the morphological switch. Thus, if Ref1 operates antagonistically to Znf2, we predict that genes upregulated by ZNF2OE may also be upregulated by the deletion of REF1. To assess this possibility, we performed RNA-seq analysis of ref1Δ and ZNF2OE in both XL280 and H99 cultured in YPD medium. In comparing the log2 fold change of transcripts during ZNF2 overexpression in XL280 to the log2 fold change of those same transcripts in the XL280 ref1Δ strain, we found the changes to the transcriptome in each case to be markedly similar. As examples, two previously characterized downstream targets of Znf2 are the hyphal flocculin Cfl124 and the regulatory factor Pum1.21 In the ZNF2OE strain, the log2 fold change in transcript levels for CFL1 and PUM1 were 6.92 and 2.80, respectively. Similarly, in the ref1Δ strain, the log2 fold changes were 8.77 for CFL1 and 2.92 for PUM1 (Figure 3B). 92 out of 122 genes differentially expressed due to ZNF2 overexpression with the log2 fold change greater than 2 overlapped with those due to deletion of REF1 (Figure 3B). By plotting each gene with a significant transcriptional change on a scatterplot in which the x axis is the transcriptional change in the ZNF2OE strain and the y axis is the transcriptional change in ref1Δ, the correlation between the two is clear, with R2 = 0.695 (Figure 3B). Performing this same analysis in the H99 background did not yield comparable results. This difference in the RNA-seq data between the two strains is not unexpected, as all RNA-seq was done for strains cultured on YPD medium, where ref1Δ in the H99 background appeared indistinguishable from wild type (WT). Indeed, analysis of RNA-seq performed for H99 strains cultured on YPGlcN medium, for which ref1Δ is hyperfilamentous compared to WT, did show a correlation between transcript level changes in ZNF2OE and ref1Δ (Figure S4). Consistent with the differences in phenotypes, deletion of REF1 in XL280 resulted in a statistically significant increase in transcript levels of 1,556 genetic loci with 196 having a log2 fold change >2 (Figure 3B), while deletion of REF1 in H99 only increased the transcript levels of 118 genetic loci, with only eight having a log2 fold change >2 under YPD conditions.

If Ref1 affects transcription of hyphal morphogenesis genes through inhibition of Znf2, we predict that the upregulation of genes by ZNF2OE will be suppressed by simultaneously overexpressing REF1. To assess this possibility, we performed RNA-seq analysis of (1) ref1Δ, (2) REF1-mNGOE, (3) ZNF2OE, and (4) REF1-mNGOE ZNF2OE. Following the logic that analyzing transcriptional changes between phenotypically distinct strains should prove more fruitful, we chose to discern whether Ref1 has a suppressive effect on gene regulation by Znf2 using the H99 background because REF1-mNG overexpression was not sufficient to suppress filamentation induced by ZNF2 overexpression in XL280. To assess this possibility, we compared ZNF2OE-induced transcriptional changes in the H99 background to that of REF1-mNGOE ZNF2OE in the H99 background (Figure 3D). We found that for most genes upregulated in ref1Δ, reflected as a positive fold change on the x axis, we generally observed a negative fold change on the y axis as a result of complementation with REF1-mNGOE (Figure 3C), resulting in an overall negative correlation (R2 = 0.684). Similarly, we observed a negative correlation when applying this analysis to the ZNF2OE background (Figure 3D, R2 = 0.476). To use the aforementioned examples of Znf2 downstream targets CFL1 and PUM1, ZNF2OE in the H99 background results in log2 fold changes in transcript levels of 8.84 and 0.64, respectively. Introducing REF1OE into the ZNF2OE strain results in log2 fold changes of −1.95 for CFL1 and −0.76 for PUM1. Taken together, these results confirmed our hypothesis that Ref1 functions antagonistically to Znf2 to regulate the expression of hyphal morphogenesis genes.

Ref1 undergoes phase transition during hyphal morphogenesis

Having established Ref1 as a negative regulator of filamentation, we wanted to explore Ref1 dynamics during hyphal morphogenesis. To this end, we expressed REF1-mNG under control of the native REF1 promoter. Interestingly, while Ref1-mNG driven by the native promoter showed diffused nuclear localization like the overexpressed Ref1-mNG on filamentation-suppressing YPD medium (PTEF1-REF1-mNG in Figure 4A), the native promoter-controlled Ref1-mNG aggregated into nuclear foci when switched to filamentation-inducing V8 medium (Figure 4A), indicative of phase separation or formation of biomolecular condensates. Importantly, when the PREF1-REF1-mNG construct was introduced into the ref1Δ background, the resultant strain was indistinguishable from XL280 on both filamentation-suppressing YPD medium and filamentation-inducing V8 medium (Figure S6), indicating that the N-terminal fluorescent tag did not affect Ref1 activity. Consistent with the initial finding that REF1 transcript level is increased during hyphal morphogenesis, protein abundance per cell of Ref1-mNG driven by its native promoter increased 2-fold on V8 medium (PREF1-REF1-mNG in Figure 4B). We were cognizant that the increased Ref1-mNG concentration could promote phase separation. However, overexpressed Ref1-mNG concentration was considerably higher per cell (10-fold higher PTEF1-controlled than PREF1-controlled Ref1-mNG on V8), and we did not observe any discernible phase separation (Figures 4A and 4B), suggesting that this phase-separation capability might be specific to the protein produced under its native promoter when cells were cultured on V8 medium. Lastly, when we cultured the PREF1-REF1-mNG strain on other filamentation-inducing media, we observed similar nuclear foci (Figure S6), indicating that the phase separation was a general response during early hyphal morphogenesis rather than a response to V8-medium-specific stimuli.

Examination of the transcript profile of REF1 revealed that its transcription begins at the annotated transcription start site (TSS) on YPD medium, but it starts downstream of that on V8 medium (Figure 4C). Notably, when we examined our previous Znf2 ChIP-seq,40 we noticed that Gaussian distribution peaks of Znf2 binding are ~200 bp upstream of the annotated REF1 TSS (~500 bp upstream of the alternative TSS), while the peak is ~500 bp upstream of the CFL1 TSS (Figure S7), suggesting that Znf2 likely binds to the REF1 promoter and drives transcription from this alternative TSS. Consistent with this idea, mNG-Znf2 driven by its native promoter was undetectable on YPD and was only expressed on V8 medium (Figure S7). Thus, we only observed REF1 transcription from the alternative TSS under conditions where Znf2 was present. Furthermore, while there is an overall dramatic increase in transcript levels of REF1 upon initiation of hyphal morphogenesis (Figure 1B), there is actually a decrease in the transcript isoform starting from the annotated TSS (Figure 4C). Together, the apparent binding of Znf2 on top of the annotated REF1 TSS and the increase in the REF1 transcript isoform from the alternative TSS under conditions where Znf2 is produced imply that Znf2 is not responsible for the basal level transcription of REF1 during vegetative yeast growth. Rather, Znf2 may block transcription of REF1 from the annotated TSS when bound to the REF1 promoter and initiate transcription of the alternative isoform.

The alternative TSS is within the first intron of REF1, eliminating the 5′ splice site from the transcript and thus transforming a portion of the first intron into a coding region for the N terminus of the protein. This alternative 5′ region of the transcript contains two closely positioned in-frame start codons that encode isoforms, labeled as isoforms 2 and 3, predicted to encode larger proteins than the original isoform 1 (Figure 4F). This finding was validated via immunoblot showing that cells harboring PREF1-REF1–2×FLAG produce a larger Ref1 isoform when cultured on V8 medium than when cultured on YPD medium (Figure 4D). Given that the Ref1 protein driven by the native promoter forms condensates on V8, we hypothesized that it is the isoform with an alternative N terminus. To test this, we expressed REF1iso2/3-mNG with the TEF1 promoter and found that both isoforms recapitulated the formation of phase-separated foci in the nucleus even in YPD medium (Figure 4E). This indicates that the alternative N terminus allows Ref1 to form condensates regardless of growth conditions. By contrast, overexpressed isoform 1 at the annotated starting codon or isoform 4 beginning just downstream of the first intron (Figures 4C and 4F) showed nuclear diffuse localization (Figure 4E), demonstrating that the alternative N terminus translated under filamentation-inducing conditions facilitates phase transition.

The phase-separating Ref1 isoform is more potent in inhibiting filamentation and prone to proteolytic degradation

We questioned what the functional differences between these Ref1 isoforms are and how they are better suited to their respective roles. Ref1-mNG produced by the native promoter showed diffused nuclear localization in yeast cells growing on YPD medium and became nuclear puncta after these yeast cells were transferred to filamentation-inducing V8 medium (24 h) (Figures 4A and 5A). As cells transitioned to hyphae on V8 medium (48 h), Ref1 was exported from the nucleus and trafficked to vacuoles, where it was presumably proteolytically degraded (Figure 5A).

As the larger alternative isoform of Ref1 is produced on V8, we considered whether the larger Ref1 isoform was prone to proteolytic degradation. To this end, we cultured cells harboring the constitutively expressed mNG-tagged Ref1 isoforms in YPD medium. We detected that the Ref1iso1-mNG only diffused in the nuclei (Figures 5B and 6), consistent with previous observations (Figures 3A, 4A, and 4E). In comparison, Ref1iso2/3-mNG were not only observed as puncta in the nucleus but were also regularly observed in degradative vacuoles, resulting in a significant reduction in Ref1 per cell quantified by fluorescence intensity (Figures 5B and 6). As all strains were growing as vegetative yeast cells in YPD medium, the observed proteolytic degradation of Ref1iso2/3 was unlikely to be a result of a checkpoint in hyphal morphogenesis but rather an inherent difference between isoforms. Additionally, the native promoter-driven Ref1-mNG changed from nuclear foci on V8 to nuclear diffuse localization within 1 h after transfer to YPD medium. Immunoblot of FLAG-tagged Ref1 with identical growth conditions showed that the larger isoform was substantially degraded in cells transferred back to YPD (Figure 5C). Taken together, these findings indicate that cells preferentially degrade the larger, phase-separating Ref1 isoform. We further validated these findings by expressing FLAG-tagged Ref1 isoforms under control of the inducible CTR4 promoter. In this experiment, after inducing expression of REF1iso1/2-2×FLAG, cells were transferred to media supplemented with CuSO4 to shut down the promoter. By harvesting cells at subsequent time points, we determined via immunoblot that Ref1iso1-2×FLAG persisted longer than Ref1iso2-2×FLAG (Figure S8).

Lastly, we observed that overexpressed Ref1iso2/3 suppressed filamentation on V8 medium more strongly than Ref1iso1 (Figure 5D), despite these strains only carrying about 35% and 40% of isoform 1 (Figure S8). The result indicates that the larger isoform associated with hyphal morphogenesis is more potent in repressing filamentation. It is possible that the less potent and more stable nuclear diffuse isoform provides sufficient global negative regulatory pressure during steady-state vegetative yeast growth to maintain yeast morphology, and the longer Ref1 half-life would be economically favorable in these conditions. Alternatively, the loss of Ref1 stability for the larger isoform would ensure that the feedback being generated is an accurate reflection of the real-time transcriptional landscape of cells coordinating the morphological transition, and the increased potency of the larger isoform would, in this scenario, also confer economic benefit by providing stronger feedback signal per translated protein. For the cells that successfully switch to hyphal growth, degradation of these Ref1 proteins will help maintain continuous hyphal growth.

DISCUSSION

We identified Ref1 as a negative transcriptional regulator that acts downstream of the terminal master regulator of filamentation Znf2, thus inhibiting filamentation. As mentioned in the results, we showed previously that Znf2 directly binds the promoter of REF1,40 defining this case as an example of a classic paradigm in which transcriptional activators induce expression of their own antagonists to generate negative feedback to provide tight regulatory control and stability to a system (Figure 7). It is worth noting that, as is the case with ZNF2, REF1 orthologs are confined to the Cryptococcus species complex and other closely related basidiomycetes such as Kwoniella and Tremella,42 suggesting that the REF1-ZNF2 regulatory circuit likely governs morphogenesis in these species. Lastly, we found that Znf2 induces expression of an alternative isoform of Ref1 that undergoes phase separation, which confers functional differences more suitable for sexual development.

The molecular mechanism by which Ref1 inhibits transcription remains to be elucidated. Ref1 is a protein with no known domains. Much of this protein is composed of intrinsically disordered regions (IDRs) (Figure S10). Ref1 may bind DNA directly to prevent transcription of either ZNF2 and/or genes in the Znf2 regulon. However, REF1 overexpression can suppress filamentation driven by Znf2 produced from other promoters (such as TEF1), suggesting that Ref1 binding to the promoter of ZNF2 to suppress its expression could not be the sole mechanism by which Ref1 suppresses filamentation. Alternatively, Ref1 may inhibit Znf2’s ability to activate gene expression, albeit indirectly, based on three observations: (1) we did not detect Znf2 via Ref1 immunoprecipitation followed by mass spectrometry; (2) we have never observed Znf2 phase separation via fluorescence microscopy when its expression is driven by either the native promoter or an inducible overexpression promoter (Figure S9); and (3) our separate study suggests that Ref1 is unlikely to be a regulator directly binding to ZNF2’s promoter. Another possibility not mutually exclusive with the DNA-binding hypothesis is that Ref1 regulates the chromatin state. Our lab previously showed that the Snf5/Brf1 SWI/SNF family chromatin remodeling complex was required for filamentation by Cryptococcus. SWI/SNF complexes regulate the chromatin state by sliding histones along DNA or ejecting histones from DNA, and the Snf5/Brf1 complex does this at Znf2-regulated genetic loci in a Znf2-dependent manner without directly binding to Znf2.15 A growing body of evidence supports the model in which local density of chromatin depends on the capacity of constituent nucleosomes to undergo biomolecular condensation, specifically liquid-liquid phase separation (LLPS).43,44 Thus, given that (1) there is established precedent for Znf2-directed chromatin remodeling that affects gene expression in the context of hyphal morphogenesis, (2) epigenetic repression via biomolecular condensation is broadly utilized, and (3) biomolecular condensation appears to be a key aspect of Ref1 function (discussed below), the possibility that Ref1 achieves transcriptional repression through chromatin remodeling is worth exploring in the future.

We were excited to discover an elegant mechanism by which the cell modulates Ref1 function dependent upon the morphological context in which Ref1 is produced. While transcription of REF1 starts at the previously annotated TSS during yeast growth, the vast majority of the transcripts generated during the nascent stages of hyphal morphogenesis (in which REF1 transcripts are increased >20-fold) start at an alternative TSS (REF1iso2/3 TSS) ~300 bp downstream of the original TSS and encode a Ref1 isoform with an increased propensity for biomolecular condensation. Biomolecular condensation, understood to be LLPS in many cases, is garnering increasing attention as a means of regulating cellular processes in fungi specifically and higher eukaryotes more broadly.45–49 Eukaryotic cells contain intracellular compartments, from large organelles to small transport vesicles, delineated by lipid membranes, to afford the cell the opportunity to curate the appropriate mix of molecules within the compartments. However, over time researchers have come to appreciate the extent to which macromolecules in living systems can “de-mix” into a concentrated phase that is immiscible with a surrounding dilute phase. This condensation concentrates subsets of molecules and minimizes molecular exchange between condensate and surroundings, leading to functional compartmentalization without the need of a lipid membrane. Biomolecular condensates specific to the nucleus, as appears to be the case for Ref1, are well documented and have been implicated in virtually all nuclear functions, including DNA replication and repair, RNA processing, transcription, and pre-ribosome assembly.48,50

While biomolecular condensation/phase separation has been studied in fungi, much of our understanding comes from in vitro experimentation, the biological significance of which remains elusive in most cases.49 However, there are notable exceptions. For one, the Gladfelter group has developed the filamentous fungal plant pathogen Ashbya gossypii as a model for studying cell polarity and asynchronous nuclear division in multinucleated cells. They showed that the protein Whi3 regulates cell polarity and nuclear division through formation of phase-separated liquid droplets in association with specific RNAs distinct from those Whi3-regulated processes.51–53 They also showed that Whi3 condensates display different physical properties based on the mRNA transcripts contained within.53 Another example involves the C. albicans PP2C phosphatase Ptc2.54 A characteristic IDR region of Ptc2 acts as a carbon dioxide sensor, probably through interaction between CO2 and a serine/threonine-rich polar region within the IDR, inducing condensation in elevated CO2 conditions, which in turn activates phosphatase activity to trigger a wide variety of CO2-responsive biological processes. In the case of Ref1, there are functional distinctions between isoforms that are likely dependent on condensation potential, the biological significance of which is discussed below.

Multiple types of domains have been shown to promote condensation, including IDRs, structured modular domains, and oligomerization domains, all of which involve multivalent interactions that enable one protein to associate with several others simultaneously.50 Our observation that the artificially expressed Ref1iso1/4-mNG isoforms were diffuse within the nucleus while the Ref1iso2/3-mNG isoforms were punctate suggests that the N terminus of Ref1iso2/3 induces Ref1 condensation. An analysis with PONDR (predictors of natural disordered regions)55 predicts that much of Ref1 is disordered, including the N terminus of Ref1iso1. However, the N terminus of Ref1iso2/3 is not (Figure S10). The Ref1iso2/3 N terminus (see Figure 4F) is rich in proline (>20%), and proline-rich domains have been implicated in the formation of biomolecular condensates.56 Collectively, these analyses suggest that the Ref1iso2/3 N-terminal condensation-promoting domain would be better described as a structured modular domain or an oligomerization domain rather than an IDR. If so, that may imply greater interaction specificity for the Ref1iso2/3 condensation-promoting domain. It is likely that enzymatically active condensates that facilitate transcription or RNA processing require many molecular components to complete their respective tasks. By contrast, sequestration of DNA to prevent transcription only requires binding to a DNA target and subsequent condensation, which physically blocks transcriptional machinery from accessing the DNA recognition site. This model of Ref1 function would provide an explanation for why we see enhanced inhibition of filamentation when overexpressing REF1iso2/3 compared to REF1iso1. Both isoforms inhibit transcription, but the formation of Ref1iso2/3 condensates would presumably make associated DNA less accessible to transcription machinery. It remains to be elucidated whether Ref1 condensation disproportionately affects the transcription of some genes over others but, if so, this would also impart added precision of Ref1-facilitated repression.

Sexual development and morphogenesis are fundamentally and evolutionarily associated characteristics. The Cryptococcus species complex is used as a model for studies of sexual reproduction.10,57–60 Cryptococcus progresses through sexual development in a coordinated sequence of stages, i.e., pheromone signaling and cell fusion, followed by hyphal initiation and elongation, basidium formation, meiosis, and finally sporulation. Our findings establish that Cryptococcus is able to modulate Ref1 function via alternative transcription in addition to regulating its transcript level to suit the current needs of the cell. The yeast cell expresses REF1iso1 at basal levels sufficient to inhibit hyphal morphogenesis and thus allow vegetative yeast growth. However, under conditions that induce sexual development, REF1iso1 expression is turned off in favor of REF1iso2/3 to produce an isoform that is both a stronger repressor of hyphal morphogenesis and prone to proteolytic degradation. We can imagine multiple non-mutually exclusive benefits for this. First, given the dynamic nature of hyphal morphogenesis, a shorter half-life for Ref1iso2/3 would provide a benefit by ensuring that the nuclear concentration of Ref1 be an accurate representation of the real-time state of the cell. Additionally, the rapid depletion of Ref1 could be instrumental in changing the transcriptional landscape at a precise checkpoint in the morphogenesis process. Indeed, we find that Ref1 nuclear abundance remains high early in hyphal development before sudden, rapid nuclear export and degradation. This dynamic could provide a critical bet-hedging role for the fungus. Applying negative regulatory pressure early in sexual development would preserve the ability of hyphae to switch back to yeast as well as maintain a mix of yeast and hyphae within the population to provide flexibility in a changing environment. It would also prevent premature commitment of cellular resources before the time is ripe for basidial maturation, meiosis, and sporulation. In conclusion, the transcriptional repressor Ref1 serves as a bimodal tool that allows the master morphogenesis regulator Znf2 to self-regulate in order to guide Cryptococcus through sexual development.

Limitations of the study

While this study establishes Ref1’s function as a transcriptional repressor and the importance of alternative transcription and phase separation in regulating Ref1 function, the definitive molecular mechanism by which Ref1 achieves transcriptional repression remains to be determined. Additionally, it is reasonable to presume that targeted degradation of the phase-separating Ref1 isoform is critical for the later stages of sexual development, but the mechanism by which this occurs, specifically the discerning between different isoforms, remains to be elucidated. We note at the beginning of the discussion that the ZNF2-REF1 regulatory circuit appears to be specific to Cryptococcus and its closely related species. Thus, while the regulatory dynamics in this study undoubtedly have analogs in more distantly related organisms, this specific case is not generalizable to all fungi or even to other yeast-to-hyphae dimorphic fungi such as Candida. Lastly, Cryptococcus is a pathogen that affects the lives of millions globally, and links between the morphotype transition and virulence have been previously established. While Ref1’s role in cryptococcal pathogenesis and disease lies beyond the scope of this study, future investigation is warranted.

EXPERIMENTAL MODEL AND STUDY PARTICIPANT DETAILS

Strains and culture conditions

For a complete list of strains used in this study, see Table S2. All strains were stocked in 15% glycerol and stored at −80°C. Fresh cultures were used for experiments. Fungal strains were maintained on yeast peptone dextrose (YPD, 1% w/v yeast extract, 2% w/v peptone, 2% w/v dextrose) agar at 30°C. All strains were derived from single cells. All strains were mating type MATα unless otherwise indicated.

METHOD DETAILS

Correlational analysis

RNA sequencing data from Hu, P. et al., Nat. Commun. (2022)38 (accession number GEO: GSE135566) for XL280 WT, ada2Δ, fzc49Δ, gat6Δ, hob7Δ, nrg1Δ, and vel1Δ strains grown on V8 medium at 25°C for 12 h were analyzed with Microsoft Excel. The CORREL function was used to calculate correlation coefficients for individual genes in relation to ZNF2 using FPKM values as inputs.

Generation of gene deletion mutants

For complete lists of plasmids and primers used in this study, see Tables S3 and S4. Strains were transformed using the Transient CRISPR-Cas9 Coupled with Electroporation (TRACE) system.61 DNA encoding GPD1 promoter-driven Cas9 fused to the nuclear localization signal of polyomavirus simian virus 40 (PGPD1-CAS9-NLSSV40) was amplified from pYF24 plasmid with Cas9-F and Cas9-R primers. DNAs encoding U6 promoter-driven guide RNAs were constructed by (i) amplifying U6 promoter from Cryptococcus neoformans serotype D strain JEC21 genomic DNA with U6 promoter far left forward primer and the respective U6 promoter right reverse primer, (ii) amplifying the guide RNA scaffold from pDD162 plasmid with Cas9 gRNA far right reverse primer and the respective gRNA scaffold left forward primer, and (iii) performing fusion PCR with DNA fragments from (i) and (ii), U6 promoter left forward primer, and Cas9 gRNA right reverse primer. Knockout constructs consisting of drug marker replacing all or most of the gene ORF flanked by ~1.0 kb homologous arms were constructed by (i) amplifying 5′ and 3′ arms from H99 or XL280 genomic DNA with far left forward/left reverse with M13 overhang primer pair and right forward with M13 overhang/far right reverse primer pair, respectively, (ii) amplifying drug marker from pPZP-NAT plasmid with M13F and M13R primers, (iii) performing fusion PCR with fragments from (i) and (ii), and (iv) amplifying the full KO construct with (iii) as template and left forward and right reverse primers. Cells were grown in YPD shaking overnight at 30°C, sub-cultured into 50 mL YPD to initial OD600 = 0.2–0.3, and grown to final OD600 = 0.6–1.0. Cells were harvested via centrifugation, washed with cold H2O, and incubated in 10 mL cold electroporation buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5, 1 mM MgCl2, 270 mM sucrose) + 1 mM dithiothreitol (DTT) on ice for 30–60 min, and resuspended in ~200 μL electroporation buffer (with no DTT). 45 μL of cell suspension was added to DNA (~1 μg gene knockout construct, ~200 ng gRNA DNA, ~100 ng PGPD1-CAS9-NLSSV40 DNA) up to a final volume of 50 μL, electroporated in bacterial mode of an Eppendorf multiporator (V = 2 kV, τ = 5 ms), resuspended in 1 mL YPD, grown for 3 h shaking at 30°C, and plated on selection medium. Deletions were confirmed with PCR.

Generation of gene complementation/overexpression mutants

For gene complementation and overexpression, genes were amplified from H99 or XL280 genomic DNA and cloned into vectors via T4 ligation. Constructs were PCR amplified from vectors, and transformations were performed via TRACE, same as the gene deletions. Constructs were inserted into intergenic regions Safe Haven 2 (SH2)62 and Safe Haven 3 (SH3)27 for H99 and XL280 strain backgrounds, respectively, via non-homologous end-joining (NHEJ), directed by SH2- and SH3-specific Cas9 guide RNA. Safe Haven integrations were confirmed with diagnostic PCR. Primers used were listed in Table S4.

Filamentation assay

Fresh cells were washed with PBS and ~1 × 107 cells were resuspended in 1 mL PBS. 5 μL of cell suspensions were then spotted on the indicated solid media, grown at 21°C for 6 days, and imaged with a SZX16 stereoscope (Olympus Life Sciences, Tokyo, Japan). For images of filamentous colony edges, 3–5 images were taken at different focal planes, and Adobe Photoshop software was used to load image files into stacks (File>Scripts>Load Files into Stack …) and auto-blend them (Edit>Auto-Blend Layers) to generate a sharp focus composite image.

DAPI staining

Cells were washed with PBS and fixed in PBS +3.7% formaldehyde at 21°C for 30 min. Cells were resuspended in 2 μg/mL DAPI in PBS and incubated in the dark at 21°C for 15 min. Cells were then harvested, washed, and imaged.

Microscopy

Fluorescence microscopy was performed using a Zeiss Imager M2 microscope (Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany). Images were acquired with an AxioCam MRm camera and processed with the software ZEN pro. Zeiss Filter Set 38 High Efficiency (HE) was used for mNeonGreen imaging, Filter Set 49 was used for DAPI, and Filter Set 63 HE was used for mCherry and FM4–64.

RNA extraction, RNA sequencing, and qRT-PCR

Fresh cells were washed with PBS, and ~1 × 107 cells were resuspended in 1 mL PBS, plated on solid media in replicates, and grown for 24 h at room temperature in the dark. Cells were then scraped, collected via centrifugation, and freeze-dried in a lyophilizer at −80°C and 0.008 mBar (Labconco corp., Kansas City, MO). Freeze-dried cells were mechanically broken in 15 mL conical tubes by hand shaking with 1 mL of 4 mm glass beads. RNA was extracted using the PureLink RNA Mini Kit according to the instructions from the manufacturer (Life Technologies, Calsbad, CA). For RNA sequencing, ~10 μg of RNA per sample were DNase treated (TURBO DNA-free) and submitted to Novogene Corporation Inc. for sequencing (polyadenylated RNA enrichment, strand-specific, paired end 150 bp on Illumina NovoSeq platform). As described previously,63 the raw reads were trimmed using Trim Galore! (0.6.5) and aligned to either Cryptococcus neoformans H99 or XL280 reference genomes using STAR (2.7.1a). The alignment files (Bam) were used to generate read counts and Fragments Per Kilobase of transcript per Million mapped reads (FPKM) with Cufflinks (2.2.1). Differential gene expression analysis was performed using DESeq2 with false discovery rate (FDR) adjusted p-value ≤ 0.05 as threshold. The raw sequencing reads from this study are deposited at the NCBI Sequencing Read Archive (BioProject ID: PRJNA1104229). For qRT-PCR, 1 μg of DNase-treated RNA per sample was used for cDNA synthesis (SuperScript free III First-Strand Synthesis System). RT-PCR was performed in technical triplicate with Power SYBR Green PCR Master Mix in a Realplex PCR machine (Eppendorf).

Protein extraction and western blotting

Cells were taken from solid YPD medium, washed with PBS, and 1 OD600 of cells was resuspended in 1 mL PBS. Cells were spotted in 5 μL increments on YPD or V8 medium and grown in the dark at 21°C for 24 h. For cells transferred from V8 back to YPD for 1 h, cells were scraped from V8, washed with PBS, resuspended to a concentration of 10 OD600 per mL, and plated on YPD. For protein extraction, cells were scraped from plates, washed with cold PBS, and equal numbers of cells as measured by absorbance at 600 nm were resuspended in 1 mL ice-cold 0.2 M NaOH +0.2% β-mercaptoethanol. 200–300 μL of 0.5 mm Zirconia/Silica beads were added to samples and cells were broken with a Next Advance Bullet Blender (5 cycles, 1 min on, 1 min off at 4°C). Protein was precipitated by adding 75 μL 100% trichloric acid, incubating on ice for 10 min, and centrifuging at 15,000 × g for 5 min at 4°C. Precipitated pellets were resuspended in 1 M Tris-HCl, mixed 1:1 with 2x SDS loading dye, and boiled at 95°C for 10 min. Equal volumes of samples were separated on 13% SDS-PAGE gels and immunoblotted for indicated proteins using monoclonal antibodies (1:2000) in blocking buffer (TBS +1% casein). Goat anti-mouse (1:20 000, Thermo Fisher Scientific) IgGs conjugated to HRP were used as secondary antibodies. Blots were developed with SuperSignal West Pico PLUS chemiluminescent substrate (Thermo Scientific). Blot were imaged with an Analytik Jena UVP ChemStudio using VisionWorks software by Analytik Jena.

FM4–46 staining

Cells were grown in 3 mL liquid YPD shaking at 30°C overnight. Cells were harvested and resuspended in 1 mL of the overnight YPD medium. FM4–64 was added to the cell suspension at a final concentration of 8 μM, and cells were shaken at 30°C for an additional 20 min. Cells were then harvested, washed, resuspended in the other 2 mL of overnight YPD with no FM4–64, and grown shaking at 30°C for an additional 120 min. Cells were then harvested, washed and imaged.

Mating and cell fusion assays

Cells were taken from solid YPD medium, washed with PBS, and 1 OD600 of cells was resuspended in 1 mL PBS. MATα and MATa mating pairs were mixed 1:1 and 5 μL spots were plated on V8 medium (pH 5 for H99/KN99 pairs and pH 7 for XL280/JEC20 pairs). For mating, cells were incubated at 22°C in the dark for 14 days. For cell fusion assays, 60 5 μL spots were plated on V8 medium and incubated at 22°C in the dark for 24 h, then scraped, resuspended in PBS, plated on double drug-supplemented YPD, and incubated at 37°C for ~3 days.

QUANTIFICATION AND STATISTICAL ANALYSIS

For RNA-seq, all experiments were performed and samples submitted in replicate. Individual data points on scatterplots of RNA-seq data represent averages of the replicates. For violin plots, fluorescence quantification was performed with Fiji software by ImageJ. The circular selection tool was used to select whole cells and the “Analyze>Measure” command was used to measure mean gray value and area. For each image field of cells, “Analyze>Measure” command was used to measure the mean gray value for 10 circular selections outside of cells and averaged to determine average background fluorescence. Total fluorescence for individual cells was calculated like so: Total fluorescence = [(cell mean gray value – background mean gray value) x cell area]. 50 cells (n = 50) were measured and plotted per strain per condition. Statistical analyses were performed with Prism software by GraphPad. For qRT-PCR, data are represented as 2−ΔΔCt with error bars are +SD and compared via one-way ANOVA (Tukey’s multiple comparison test), * = p < 0.05, ** = p < 0.01, *** = p < 0.001, **** = p < 0.0001. On violin plots of fluorescence quantification data, dashed lines represent the median value and dotted lines represent 1st and 3rd quartiles. Fluorescence quantification data were compared via unpaired, two-tailed t test with Welch’s correction (**** = p < 0.0001). R2 values for scatterplots comparing log2 fold changes of gene transcript levels (Figure 3) were calculated via simple linear regression.
