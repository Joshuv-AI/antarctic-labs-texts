Foliar application of allantoin and serotonin mitigates salinity stress in bitter melon (Momordica charantia L.)
Authors: Masoomeh Amerian, Gholamreza Gohari, Saeid Jalalihonarmand, Sima Panahirad, Georgia Ntatsi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Bitter melon (Momordica charantia L.) is a climbing species frequently employed in traditional medicine to treat a wide number of diseases. Soil salinity represents a primary abiotic constraint on plant growth, impacting nearly 50% of irrigated farmland and constantly increasing. Previous studies revealed the individual benefits of allantoin and serotonin, in mitigating abiotic stresses. Therefore, in the current study, we investigated their ability, both individually and combination, ameliorating the damaging consequences of salt stress on the development and physiological and biochemical attributes of bitter melon. A factorial experiment arranged within a completely randomized design, replicated three times, was performed. Two variables were examined: (a) three salinity levels (0, 50 and 100 mM NaCl) and (b) foliar application treatments (control (distilled water), allantoin (1000 µmol), serotonin (100 µmol) and allantoin (1000 µmol) +serotonin (100 µmol)). At the end of the experiment, critical parameters related to morphology, biochemistry, and physiology were measured from leaf and root tissues, and fruit secondary metabolites was extracted. Vegetative traits (plant height, number of leaves, leaf area), and fruit-related parameters (fruit mean weight and yield) of bitter melon were suppressed under increasing salinity stress. Exposure to 100 mM NaCl reduced photosynthetic efficiency (SPAD (soil-plant analysis development), Fv/Fm), relative content of water and potassium and phosphorus concentration in bitter melon leaf, while increasing electrolyte leakage, proline, sodium, chloride, hydrogen peroxide, malondialdehyde and antioxidant enzyme activities. However, the combined application of allantoin and serotonin alleviated these effects by increasing antioxidant enzyme activity, proline accumulation, total soluble sugar, total protein, ascorbic acid, relative content of water, potassium and phosphorus, while decreasing malondialdehyde and hydrogen peroxide and sodium and chloride accumulation. In fruit, the content of p-coumaric acid (14.64%), ferulic acid (10.48%), caffeic acid (15.74%), gallic acid (22.04%), and cinnamic acid (37.77%) increased in plants treated with allantoin + serotonin compared to the control. The highest amount of phenolic acids was produced by plants treated with allantoin and serotonin. The application of allantoin and serotonin had a positive effect on the quality characteristics and yield of bitter melon fruit compared to the control. In summary, the findings suggest that treating bitter melon with of allantoin and serotonin, particularly in combination, effectively alleviated the adverse impacts of salinity by inducing critical modifications in bitter melon’s physiological and biochemical processes. Further investigation might be useful for translating these results to other salt-stressed crops.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-52357-2.

Introduction

Salinity, an important environmental stress, significantly affects many plant physiological functions resulting in impaired development, and subsequently yield and quality losses1. The plant’s response to salinity is species, developmental stage, and stress severity and duration depended2. At cellular level salinity decreases membrane stability, inhibits the activity of photosynthetic enzymes and impairs the overall photosynthetic mechanism3. These disruptions coupled with reduced biomass accumulation and compromised leaf expansion alongside the impaired water and nutrient uptake contribute to reduced vegetative growth and yield with subsequent economic losses4. Furthermore, the negative effect of salinity could be linked to a decreased osmotic potential in the root environment and higher ionic toxicity resulting from the excessive accumulation of sodium (Na+) and chloride (Cl−) ions in the leaves and nutritional imbalance that impair the absorption and/or translocation of vital nutrients to the shoots5. In order to maintain the cellular turgor and prevent water efflux, plants accumulate compatible osmolytes like soluble sugar and protein and proline6. To counteract these effects several management practices have been employed7. Among them, exogenous plant hormone application is widely used to improve plant stress resilience. Exogenous jasmonic acid8 and melatonin (150 µM)9 increased salt stress tolerance of bitter melon through metabolism modifications, while melatonin, increased key fruit compounds (charantin, momordicin, cucurbitacin) and antioxidant capacity10. Chitosan–selenium nanoparticle (CTS-Se NPs at 20 mg L− 1) significantly enhanced salinity tolerance of bitter melon, by increasing antioxidant enzyme activity, proline, relative content of water (RWC), and potassium (K), while decreasing malondialdehyde (MDA), hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), and Na accumulation, resulting in improving yield and essential oil production11. CTS-Se NPs also improved growth, photosynthesis, nutrient balance, and stress gene expression under salinity3. Recently, allantoin and serotonin have gained attention as compounds that contribute to enhanced stress resistance in various plants species12,13.

Bitter melon (Momordica charantia L.), from the Cucurbitaceae family, is native in subtropical and tropical regions, with its main cultivation areas being East Africa, Asia and South America14. Phytochemical compounds found in different parts of bitter melon plant (i.e., fruit, seed, leaf and root) are used to treat diabetes, cancer, AIDS, high blood pressure, obesity and viral and bacterial infections. The insulin-like compounds found in bitter melon fruit and seeds are particularly significant for managing of type 2 diabetes. In terms of its nutritional value, bitter melon is a rich source of carbohydrates, proteins, minerals, vitamins (e.g. vitamin C) and dietary fiber15. The medicinal properties of bitter melon could be attributed to triterpenoids, saponins, phenols, flavonoids, tannins and other phenolic compounds16. Quarantin and momordicin are two triterpenoid compounds found in the plant with strong antidiabetic properties17. Xanthine oxidase, a key enzyme in the inducing gout and cancer, is significantly inhibited by cucurbitacin present in bitter melon10. All the above emphasize the importance of this plant in human diet and highlight the necessity for special attention on its production particularly, especially under stress conditions.

Allantoin (N-(2,5-Dioxo-4-imidazolidinyl) urea), a heterocyclic N-containing compound and intermediate product of purine catabolism, and its hydrolyzed form (allantoate) are collectively known as ureides18. Allantoin is a well-established commercial substance with numerous uses in the pharmaceutical and cosmetic sectors19. Known to be involved in nitrogen (N) metabolism in plants, mainly increases the activity of enzymes related to seed germination and photosynthetic intensity, improves germination speed and chlorophyll content and prevents aging20. Allantoin improves crop yield21 and tolerance to drought stress22, salinity23, high light intensity24 and heavy metals25. Currently, two primary functions for allantoin in plant abiotic stress responses have been propped including a possible role as a ROS protectant26 and/or an abscisic acid (ABA) metabolism activator27. External application of allantoin increases ABA levels in Arabidopsis by stimulating its biosynthesis and the hydrolysis of ABA-glucose conjugates27. Despite these benefits, the mechanisms of exogenous allantoin in stress mitigation remain under investigation28.

Serotonin (5-hydroxytryptamine) is an indoleamine with antioxidant effects29 playing a major role in plant development, phytohormonal status, gene regulation, morphogenesis, flowering and stress adaptation under various environmental challenges30. Primarily known as a neurotransmitter in the animal kingdom, serotonin has been detected in more than 90 plant species across 37 families. Its structural similarities to auxin, suggest a possibility to interact with the auxin receptors and transport mechanisms in plants31. As a pseudo-hormone, serotonin plays a role similar to that of cytokinin, influencing lateral root growth, cell division and differentiation, biomass production, seed germination, somatic embryogenesis, and aging. During the germination stage, serotonin increased coleoptile weight and hypocotyl length of soybean (Glycine max L.)32. In Arabidopsis, serotonin concentrations of 10 to 160 µmol increased lateral root formation, while the reverse was the case 160 µmol33 emphasizing its dose-dependent behavior. Serotonin increased the growth of primary roots by 20% in sunflower (Helianthus annuus L.) under salt stress conditions34. According to the results obtained in tomato (Solanum lycopersicum L.), serotonin (5 µM) reduced the effects of salinity stress (200 mM NaCl) and increased the antioxidant capacity35. In Ethiopian mustard (Brassica carinata L.), foliar application of serotonin (200 mmol L− 1) improved gas exchange, chlorophyll levels, photosystem II efficiency, antioxidant activities, proline content, nitrate reductase activity, water use efficiency, and growth metrics across a range of salinity levels (0, 50, 100 and 150 mM)30.

Serotonin is a well-studied plant hormone that, in plants, it plays a pivotal role in plant growth and development, phytohormonal crosstalk, gene modulation, morphogenesis, flowering and stress responses and adaptability under various uncertain conditions. Serotonin shares structural similarities with auxin, which brings the possibility to surrogate the auxin receptors and transport in plants. Under conditions of salinity stress, serotonin antioxidant metabolism, chlorophyll biosynthesis, osmolyte production, and defense-related genes that ultimately result in better growth and productivity. Allantoin plays multifaceted roles in purine metabolism, nitrogen storage/utilization, and stress protection. With the goal of developing an eco-friendly strategy to enhance salinity tolerance bitter melon a study was designed to investigate the synergistic potential of foliar-applied allantoin (1000 µmol), serotonin (100 µmol) individually and in combination, to mitigate salt stress (0–100 mM NaCl) in this economically important crop by evaluating their effects on morpho-physiological traits (growth, yield, photosynthesis), biochemical responses (antioxidant activity, osmolyte accumulation, oxidative stress markers), and ion homeostasis. Taking all the above into consideration, along with the increasing need for food production, a research was designed to assess the impact of the foliar-applied serotonin and allantoin, both separately and in combination, on the salinity tolerance of bitter melon. In this study, the combined use of allantoin and serotonin in the bitter melon plant was performed for the first time.

Materials and methods

Plant material and treatments

An experiment was carried out in 2024 in the research greenhouse of Razi University in the city of Kermanshah at 47 º 4ˈ minutes east and 19º 34ˈ north, and altitude of 1,200 m above sea level. The experimental design was a complete randomized factorial with three replicates. Prior to sowing, bitter melon seeds, obtained from Fardin Kasht Company (Product ID: FK14456), were surface-sterilized by immersion in a 1% sodium hypochlorite solution for a duration of 5 min. Sowing took place in seedling trays containing perlite and cocopeat (1:1) and fertigated every other day by applying a half-strength Hoagland nutrient solution. After two weeks, seedlings were transplanted to 7-L pots containing equal proportions of agricultural soil, sand, and well-rotted manure. Prior to the experiment initiation, soil samples were and collected their physical and chemical characteristics were recorded (Table 1). One week after transplanting the salt stress treatments (0, 50 and 100 mM NaCl were applied via irrigation water and continued until harvest. Foliar spraying of allantoin and serotonin (allantoin (1000 µmol)22, serotonin (100 µmol)33 and allantoin (1000 µmol) +serotonin (100 µmol)), as moderators, was carried out in two stages: one week after stress initiation and one week after the first foliar spray, both in the evening. Control plants were cultivated under non-saline conditions and foliar spayed with distilled water in the same manner (two stages).

The growth stages of bitter melon plant included vegetative growth (Fig. 1S), fruit set (Fig. 2S), immature fruits (Fig. 3S), ripe fruits (Figs. 4S and 5S), and seed ripening (Fig. 6S).

Morphometric parameters, yield, firmness, TSS and stomatal resistance

Two weeks after the second foliar spraying, several morphological and physiological traits were measured. The morphological characteristics examined were plant height, number of lateral branches, leaf number and area, fresh weight of root, shoot and fruit, fruit number, plant yield (Kg), and total yield (Kg m− 2). About 50 days after sowing, the first fruits were harvested, and subsequent harvests occurred weekly (5 times in total). At the end of the growing season, yield and yield components ((fruit number, fresh and dry weight across all 5 harvests) were examined. The number of seeds per fruit, seed dry weight, and weight of 1000 seeds were also examined.

Key fruit quality parameters were measured using specialized instruments: firmness with an XTPlus-TA tester, dimensions with a digital caliper (Model NO: 41050, Germany), and total soluble solids (TSS) with a hand-held refractometer. (Atago 0–32 ºBrix Refractometer - Japan). Prior to harvest, stomatal resistance was assessed on the abaxial side of leaves between the hours of 11 a.m. and 1 p.m. using a steady-state porometer (model EA 540 − 026 AP4).

Physiologic parameters

Photosynthetic pigments, SPAD, performance index (PIABS) and chlorophyll fluorescence parameter (Fv/Fm)

Following a 14-day period after the second foliar application, leaf photosynthetic pigments were analyzed using the protocol established by Lichtenthaler36. A SPAD-502 m was used to record chlorophyll content (Minolta Camera Co. Ltd., Japan). Chlorophyll fluorescence parameters (Fv/Fm, PIABS) were quantified with a Dual-PAM-100 fluorometer following a 20-minute dark adaptation period for the leaf samples (Heinz Walz, Efeltrich, Germany)37.

Relative water content (RWC) and electrolyte leakage (EL)

RWC was determined following the protocol of Schonfeld et al38., while Electrolyte Leakage (EL) was assessed according to the method described by Ben Hamed et al39..

Measurement of biochemical properties

Proline according to Irigoyen et al.40, total soluble sugar according to Paquin and Lechasseur41, ascorbic acid in leaf and fruit according to Bor et al.42,, total anthocyanin according to Masukasu et al.43, total flavonoids according to Bor et al.42, and total alkaloid according to Shamsa, et al.44. Total phenol content was measured using Folin-Ciocalteau reagent45. The antioxidant capacity of leaf samples was evaluated using the 2-2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) measurement method46. The phenolic acids profile (p-coumaric acid, ferulic acid, gallic acid and caffeic acid) in leaf and fruit47, ABA in leaf48, secondary metabolites and associated compounds (charantin, momordicin, momordicozide and momordin) in fruit49 were determined by HPLC.

The leaf content of H2O₂ was determined using the trichloroacetic acid (TCA) precipitation protocol described by Velikova and Loreto50. To assess oxidative membrane damage, lipid peroxidation was measured by quantifying the amount of malondialdehyde (MDA), a breakdown product, following the methodology of Heath and Packer51.

To determine the activity of catalase (CAT), peroxidase (POX), superoxide dismutase (SOD), and ascorbate peroxidase (APX) enzymes, extracts were prepared from frozen tissue, and the corresponding reactions were monitored via spectrophotometry. To prepare the enzyme extract, 100 mg of fresh bitter melon leaves were weighed and mixed with 1 mL of extraction buffer containing 100 mM potassium phosphate (K₂HPO₄/KH₂PO₄) with pH 7.8, 0.1 mM EDTA and 1% PVP in a cold porcelain mortar and homogenized on ice. Then, the resulting extracts were centrifuged at 13,000 rpm and at a temperature of 4 °C for 30 min and the resulting supernatant was collected. The resulting supernatant was used as enzyme extract to measure the activity of antioxidant enzymes. Total soluble protein content was quantified utilizing the Bradford52 method, employing bovine serum albumin (BSA) for the standard curve. Catalase (CAT) activity was measured by the Bergmeyer method53 based on the disappearance of H2O2 at a wavelength of 240 nm. The activity of ascorbate peroxidase (APX) enzyme were measured according to Nakano and Asada54 and peroxidase (POX) enzyme activity according to Herzog and Fahimi55. Superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity was measured by measuring its ability to prevent the photoreduction of nitroblutotetrazolium using the method of Dhindsa et al56. The 200 µL assay mixture consisted of 25 mM K₂HPO₄/KH₂PO₄ buffer (pH 7.0), 0.1 mM EDTA, 10 mM H2O₂, and 10 µL of plant extract.

Leaf mineral concentrations

For the analysis of leaf nutrient concentrations, samples were oven-dried at 75 °C for 72 h and subsequently ground. Following a wet digestion process with concentrated nitric acid (65%), K⁺ and Na⁺ levels were quantified using a flame photometer (Kruse, model G 405, Germany). Cl⁻ and P contents were determined colorimetrically according to Munns et al57. and Adesanwo et al58, respectively, while N content was assessed via the Kjeldahl method59.

Statistical analysis

The experiment employed a two-factorial design combining three salinity levels with four biostimulant treatments, each replicated three times. Data were analyzed using factorial ANOVA. In cases where a main effect was significant but the interaction was not, means for salt stress levels and/or biostimulants were compared using Duncan’s multiple range test (P < 0.05). When the interaction was significant, the same test was applied to separate the means of all treatment combinations. All analyses were performed with SAS software, version 9.01 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA).

Results

Growth characteristics of bitter melon plant

The results revealed a significant effect of both treatments and their interaction on plant height, number of lateral branches, number of leaves and leaf area (Table S1). A significant reduction in bitter melon growth was observed under salt stress. However, the application of allantoin and serotonin effectively mitigated this reduction, significantly improving growth characteristics under salinity. (Table 2; Fig. 1). The most favorable results were achieved by the combined application of allantoin + serotonin under control conditions (0 mM NaCl). The highest plant height (186.66 cm), plant fresh weight (694.67 g), plant dry weight (209.00 g), number of lateral branches (22.00), number of leaves (74.00), leaf area (65.13 cm2) were observed in the later mentioned treatment (allantoin + serotonin under control condition) (Table 2). In all three salinity levels, foliar applications improved the growth characteristics of bitter melon (Table 2).

Yield and yield components of bitter melon decreased with increasing salinity stress. Conversely, the lowest yield and yield components were observed in bitter melon grown under 100 mM NaCl. Under salinity stress conditions, combined application of allantoin and serotonin was the most effective in improving yield and yield components of bitter melon (Table 3).

Physiological characteristics

Photosynthetic pigments

Analysis of variance revealed that the main effects of salt stress, foliar application, and their interaction significantly influenced all measured photosynthetic parameters. This included chlorophyll a, chlorophyll b, total chlorophyll, carotenoids, the fluorescence parameter Fv/Fm, and performance index (PIABS) (all at p ≤ 0.01), as well as the SPAD index (p ≤ 0.05) (Table S3). Salt stress decreased the amount of chlorophyll a, chlorophyll b and total chlorophyll in bitter melon leaves. The highest amount of chlorophyll a (19.99 mg g− 1 FW), chlorophyll b (8.62 mg g− 1 FW), total chlorophyll (28.61 mg g− 1 FW), carotenoid (5.35 mg g− 1 FW), Fv/Fm (0.84), PI (17.93) and SPAD (34.10) was observed in the treatment of allantoin + serotonin under control conditions. The combined foliar application of allantoin and serotonin resulted in a significant increase in photosynthetic pigments, performance index (PIABS), SPAD values, and the Fv/Fm ratio across all salinity stress levels when compared to the untreated control (Table 4).

Relative water content (RWC), electrolyte leakage (EL) and stomatal resistance

According to the analysis of variance (Table S4), salt stress, foliar applications and their interaction had a significant effect on RWC, EL and stomatal resistance of bitter melon leaves (p ≤ 0.01). Based on the results obtained, salinity decreased RWC of the leaves. The highest amount of RWC (80.40%) was recorded in bitter melon treated with allantoin + serotonin under salt stress conditions. In contrast to salinity, allantoin and serotonin decreased the rate of EL and stomatal resistance. The highest EL and stomatal resistance were observed in 100 mM NaCl treated bitter melons. The lowest EL (33.52%) and stomatal resistance (6.13 mmol/m2s) were recorded in allantoin + serotonin-treated plants under control conditions (Table 5).

Antioxidant compounds and capacity, compatible metabolites, ascorbic acid, ABA and soluble proteins

Proline and total soluble sugar content were significantly affected by salt stress, foliar application, and their interaction), total phenols and flavonoids, anthocyanins (antioxidant compounds), antioxidant capacity, ascorbic acid, ABA and soluble proteins (Table S5). Proline, total soluble sugars, soluble protein, total phenols and flavonoids, anthocyanins and antioxidant capacity content in the leaves of bitter melon increased with increase in salinity level, reaching the highest contents at 100 mM NaCl. The application of allantoin and serotonin positively influenced proline, total soluble sugars, soluble proteins, total phenols and flavonoids, anthocyanins and antioxidant capacity content of bitter melon under salt stress conditions. In specific, the highest amount of proline (28.38 μm g− 1), total soluble sugars (6.39 mg g− 1), soluble proteins (6.83%), total phenols (33.91 mg g− 1 FW), total flavonoids (10.01 mg g− 1 FW), anthocyanins (1.97 mg g− 1 FW), antioxidant capacity (44.85%) and ABA (55.34 ng g− 1 FW) content were recorded in allantoin + serotonin treated plants under 100 mM NaCl (Table 6). Salinity reduced ascorbic acid content while allantoin and serotonin reversed the effect. The highest amount of ascorbic acid (22.49 mg 100 g− 1) was observed in the allantoin + serotonin-treated plants under control conditions. In addition, allantoin + serotonin increased the amount of ascorbic acid in bitter melon leaves in all three levels of salinity stress (Table 6).

Our data demonstrate a significant effect of salt stress, the application of biostimulants (the moderator), and the interaction between these factors on key oxidative stress parameters in bitter melon leaves, including the activities of catalase (CAT), peroxidase (POX), superoxide dismutase (SOD), and ascorbate peroxidase (APX), as well as malondialdehyde (MDA) and hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) content (p ≤ 0.01) (Table S6). Allantoin and serotonin application mostly enhanced the enzymes activities under control and salinity conditions. As the salinity level increased from 0 to 100 mM NaCl concentrations, the activity of catalase (CAT), peroxidase (POX), superoxide dismutase (SOD) and ascorbate peroxidase (APX) enzymes increased. The highest activity levels of catalase (CAT) (10.09 IU g− 1), peroxidase (POX) (4.83 IU g− 1), superoxide dismutase (SOD) (6.20 IU g− 1) and ascorbate peroxidase (APX) (3.90 IU g− 1) were observed in allantoin + serotonin treated plants under the highest salinity level (Fig. 2a). According to the obtained results, unlike allantoin and serotonin, salinity increased the content of malondialdehyde (MDA) and hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) with the highest content of malondialdehyde (MDA) (8.77 µ mol g− 1) and hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) (5.40 µ mol g− 1) to be recorded at 100 mM NaCl. Allantoin, serotonin and their combined application reduced malondialdehyde (MDA) and hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) under control and salinity conditions and their lowest content was obtained in allantoin + serotonin treated plants under control conditions (Fig. 2b).

Leaf K, Na, P and Cl concentration

The results indicated that both salt stress and foliar application significantly influenced the concentration of nutritional elements (i.e., K, Na, P, Cl and N) of bitter melon leaf (p ≤ 0.01) (Table S7). Likewise, the interaction effect was significant on all mentioned elements; K, P and Cl− (p ≤ 0.01), Na and N (p ≤ 0.05) (Table S7). The results indicated that leaf potassium (K) and phosphorus (P) concentrations decreased progressively with increasing salinity. However, the application of allantoin and serotonin significantly increased the content of these elements under salt stress conditions. The highest amounts of Na (51.61 mg 100 g DW− 1) and Cl (8.49 mg g DW− 1) in leaf were observed in 100 mM NaCl. On the other hand, the highest amount of K (2015.7 mg 100 g− 1) and P (980.00 mg 100 g DW− 1) was found in allantoin + serotonin treated plants under control conditions. Unlike salt stress that reduced N content, the application of modifiers increased the amount of N under control and salinity conditions reaching the highest amount (4459.0 mg 100 g DW− 1) in allantoin + serotonin treated plants under control conditions (Table 7).

In the leaf and fruit, at all three salinity levels, the modifiers increased the amount of phenolic acids (Table 9).

The biplot (Fig. 3) typically displays the first two principal components (PC1 and PC2) as the axes, as these components capture the most of the variability. In grouping the studied treatments based on principal component analysis, the grouping results showed that the studied treatments were placed in separate groups based on the studied traits (Fig. 3). The results of this grouping showed that, based on the principal components, traits such as stomatal resistance, Na, Cl, EL, and MDA were included in the S2M1, S2M2 and S3M1 treatment groups. Also, the fruit dry weight and carotenoids are related to treatments S1M1 and S1M2. Biochemical characteristics were in treatment group S1M3, S1M4 and S2M4 (Fig. 3).

Discussion

The escalating demand for food is severely threatened by salt stress, a major issue in the world’s arid and semiarid zones60. In this study, salt stress restricted bitter melon growth and yield. Similar results were obtained in tomato (Solanum lycopersicum L.)61, lettuce (Lactuca sativa L.)62 and cucumber (Cucumis sativus L.)63. Salt-induced plant growth decrease could be attributed to impaired water64 and nutrient absorption, disrupted ion homeostasis and osmotic adjustment, altered soil water potential, and inhibition of photosynthetic efficacy62,65. Accumulation of Na in the aerial plant parts and reduced cellular turgor and cell elongation66 contribute to reduced growth and consequential decrease in lateral branching67. The observed reversal of salt stress effects on plant growth with the combined foliar application of allantoin and serotonin, identified as the most effective treatment, suggests a potential synergistic interaction. This positive outcome could be attributed to their combined hormonal and signaling roles in regulating growth-related processes. As a N source, allantoin may improve salt tolerance through the upregulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) scavenging systems, the preservation of ion homeostasis, and the enhancement of osmotic adjustment68,69. The increased accumulation of allantoin in plants during stress conditions indicates its important role in stress modulation70. In the present study, allantoin regulated salt tolerance mechanism in bitter melon seedlings via improved growth and yield characteristics, in agreement with previous reports in sugar beet (Beta vulgaris L.), Arabidopsis (Arabidopsis thaliana L.) and rice (Oryza sativa L.)23,26,71. A possible mechanism is the NH4+ increase via allantoin catabolism that contributes to N homeostasis acting as a signaling molecule24,72. The application of serotonin effectively mitigated the detrimental impacts of salt stress and enhanced the growth of bitter melon plants. This finding aligns with the work of Arya et al73, who suggested that the underlying mechanism may involve the modulation of gene expression in auxin-responsive pathways, a concept previously supported by74. Serotonin exhibits a cytokinin-like activity, essential for cell division promotion and antioxidative stress protection, all leading to promoted growth of salt-stressed bitter melon75, as already found in sunflower (Helianthus annuus L.)34. Our study’s positive effect of foliar serotonin application on salinity, likely via scavenging ROS and osmotic pressure regulation5, is further supported by its ability to mediate the influx of ions into the chloroplasts76 and enhanced chlorophyll biosynthesis12,31. The application of allantoin and serotonin had a positive effect on the quality characteristics and yield of bitter melon fruit compared to the control.

In this study, the content of photosynthetic pigments, chlorophyll index (SPAD) and chlorophyll fluorescence (Fv/Fm) were significantly declined under salt stress conditions, as previously found in chili pepper77 and cucumber78. This decline can likely be attributed to a combination of factors, including ROS accumulation, structural damage to chloroplasts and thylakoid membranes, and an energy imbalance caused by stomatal closure limiting CO₂ fixation. Furthermore, impaired water translocation, as indicated by reduced RWC, likely restricted mineral nutrient uptake, exacerbating the negative effects on the photosynthetic apparatus3,12,23,30,79. The application of allantoin and serotonin, individually and in combination, led to increases in SPAD index and Fv/Fm as well as photosynthetic pigments in bitter melon leaves under salt stress conditions. Allantoin has been known to play function in N metabolism and transport13 therefore, its positive effect in photosynthetic pigments; SPAD and Fv/Fm could be justified. Similarly, results were found in salt-stressed zinnia (Zinnia elegans L.) plants6. Nitrogen serves as a fundamental constituent of nucleotides and proteins, which are essential for photosynthetic efficiency and the activity of critical enzymes80. Research on pea chloroplasts indicates that serotonin can enhance the flux of magnesium and calcium, thereby contributing to the protection of photosynthetic tissues29. As a metabolic precursor to melatonin, serotonin may improve photosynthetic performance through analogous pathways. These mechanisms include: 1) elevating photosynthetic pigment and carotenoid levels to bolster antioxidant capacity and protect photosystem II efficiency (Fv/Fm)12,81 augmenting net photosynthetic rate (Pn) via the modulation of stomatal conductance82preserving chlorophyll by suppressing the expression of genes responsible for its degradation under stress83.

Salt-stressed plants commonly experience intracellular water deficit11, as observed in the current study, evidenced by reduced RWC. Similar results were also observed in cucumber63 and tomato70. In the present study allantoin and serotonin application improved RWC23, maintaining cell turgor22. Allantoin can improve osmoregulation and nutrient uptake, which helps to prevent cellular dehydration under abiotic stress13. Increase in RWC by serotonin application might be due to higher root growth that improves water uptake12.

Salinity leads to enhanced EL and MDA content due to damages to membrane integrity7,64,84. By improving osmoregulation and nutrient uptake, allantoin helps protect cells from dehydration caused by environmental stresses12,23. Moreover, exogenous allantoin reduced EL values in salt-stressed plants in our experiment thanks to its intermediate role in ureide metabolism22.

One way to deal with salinity-induced water deficit is reduced stomatal conductance85. Decreased stomatal conductance (increased stomatal resistance) under salt stress conditions has also been reported in maize86 and tomato87. In this study application of allantoin and serotonin, unlike salt stress, reduced stomatal resistance of bitter melon leaves. This could be attributed to the positive effect on N metabolism in plant root growth and water uptake88. Increased stomatal conductance from serotonin application boosted CO₂ fixation, reducing excess energy and photoinhibition, thereby raising the Fv/Fm ratio. The current results were in line with the findings of He et al89.. Research on rice has demonstrated that serotonin application can enhance stomatal conductance, spikelet fertility, and ultimately yield under conditions of mild drought stress90. Furthermore, serotonin promotes lateral root formation in a dose-dependent fashion by activating the growth of pre-existing lateral root primordia91. As a result, water absorption by the root increases and stomatal resistance decreases.

Salt stress triggers osmotic accumulation (proline, soluble sugars and soluble proteins) in plants, a key strategy for osmotic adjustment7 and cellular protection11. Our findings in bitter melon align with Janah et al92. for increased levels under salt stress. Ferchichi et al93. reported that proline is implicated in gene expression, redox homeostasis, and membrane and protein stability. Its accumulation under salinity stress might be due to ABA-induced stomatal closure94 and enzymes activity (NADH-dependent glutamate dehydrogenase and pyrroline-5-carboxylate synthetase), suggesting an ABA-independent synthesis of proline74. By promoting the buildup of osmolytes like proline and soluble sugars, allantoin helps plants in preserving osmotic balance and turgor pressure, increasing their resilience to dehydration and osmotic stress26. External application of allantoin under salt stress conditions increased the proline content of bitter melon leaves, consistent with the results of Irani and Todd24. Exogenous application of allantoin increased the mRNA levels of the proline biosynthesis enzyme, P5CS (pyrroline-5-carboxylate synthase), in wild-type Arabidopsis under abiotic stress conditions26,27. In this study, exogenous application of serotonin also increased the content of proline, total soluble sugars and proteins under salinity, consistent with the results of Liu et al5. in Rapeseed (Brassica napus L.) seedlings. Serotonin increased soluble protein contents under salt stress condition possibly because serotonin acts as a reactive oxygen species (ROS) scavenger to reduce excess intracellular ROS and repair protein damage under the stress due to structural similarities to melatonin and potential related roles.

To protect against oxidant damage, plants utilize enzymatic and non-enzymatic antioxidants to remove ROS95. Key non-enzymatic compounds include phenolic compounds, flavonoids, ascorbate, glutathione, carotenoids, and α-tocopherols96. Under salinity stress, oxidative stress emerges as a secondary effect, where accumulated ROS cause oxidative damage to proteins and lipids, leading to cell death63. Under salinity, increase in phenolic compounds observed in some plants including sweet basil97. Anthocyanins and flavonoids play a protective role for the photosynthetic system by absorbing surplus light energy, thereby mitigating photoinhibition under environmental stresses. Furthermore, phenolic substances aid in preserving the structure and function of cellular membranes when plants face adverse conditions. As a result, these metabolites are crucial for safeguarding plant tissues against injury from salinity64. In bitter melon, salt stress leads to a reduction in ascorbic acid content. This occurs due to an overproduction of ROS that overwhelms the plant’s detoxification capabilities, causing ascorbate to be depleted faster than it can be regenerated98.

The phytohormone ABA, synthesized from isoprenoid precursors, is a key regulator of numerous plant functions. It controls stomatal aperture, modulates gene expression, and facilitates adaptation to abiotic challenges like drought, salinity, and low temperatures. ABA also serves as an essential signaling molecule that coordinates a plant’s physiological adjustments to diverse environmental constraints99. Salinity stress in tomato increased ABA concentration that then accompanied by stomatal closure of100, in line with our findings. In our experiment, secondary metabolites and non-enzymatic antioxidants such as ascorbic acid, anthocyanins and flavonoids were higher in allantoin and serotonin treated bitter melon plants under salt stress conditions26. Ascorbic acid is a potent ROS scavenger101, and the stimulation of ascorbic acid by allantoin under salinity conditions (100 mM NaCl) may be due to the effect of allantoin on increasing the levels of antioxidant compounds under salt stress conditions. The allantoin-induced increase in anthocyanin may be due to a stronger antioxidant defense system and exogenous allantoin increases ABA content in Arabidopsis plant, thereby enhancing stress tolerance in plants26,27. Since ABA enhances anthocyanin biosynthesis102, there is a high possibility that ABA produced by allantoin stimulates anthocyanin biosynthesis in bitter melon leaf.

This study demonstrated that bitter melon plants exposed to high salinity exhibited significantly greater activity of POD, SOD, APX, and CAT compared to unstressed controls, a finding consistent with reports in other species such as red beet103. This can be considered as a sign of the increased ROS generation and a common protective mechanism to mitigate oxidative damage induced by salinity7. Plants use antioxidant systems to eliminate ROS. In line with our results, other studies have shown that the application of allantoin and serotonin enhanced antioxidants levels in plants73,104 probably be triggered by increased ROS production or as a protective mechanism adopted by bitter melon plants against oxidative damage. Ureides, which are nitrogen-rich intermediates generated from purine catabolism, represent a class of metabolites that help shield plants from oxidative injury caused by various stresses. These compounds play vital roles in growth, development, and nitrogen recycling. Evidence suggests ureides function as antioxidants, aiding in ROS detoxification, particularly during early growth stages26. The findings of the current investigation align with prior research demonstrating that externally applied allantoin boosts salt stress tolerance in Arabidopsis seedlings by reinforcing the plant’s antioxidant defense mechanisms68. Serotonin exhibits significant antioxidant properties, directly neutralizing ROS and influencing the phenylpropanoid pathway. This interaction involves the regulation of polyphenol oxidase (PPO) and leads to the synthesis of phenolic conjugates such as feruloylserotonin and 4-coumaroylserotonin, thereby enhancing the plant’s antioxidative capacity75. It also serves as a precursor for the biosynthesis of various phenolic and amide-based protective compounds73. Furthermore, serotonin functions as a growth regulator with cytokinin-like activity, participating in processes like cell division. Its combined antioxidative and growth-promoting actions contribute to an overall anti-stress effect, which aligns with the findings of the present study73. The interplay between serotonin and phenylpropanoid metabolism, along with its inherent antioxidant power, also supports the stability of key plant pigments, including chlorophyll and anthocyanins105. The combined use of allantoin and serotonin has not been studied so far, but according to the results of this study their combined application increased the antioxidant compounds of bitter melon under salt stress conditions that play an important role in plant tolerance to salt stress.

A correlative pattern was observed between MDA levels and the activities of SOD, POD, and CAT in the leaves of salt-stressed bitter melon (Fig. 2a). Exposure to 100 mM NaCl resulted in substantial elevations in reactive oxygen species (H₂O₂) and MDA content, indicating severe oxidative damage (Fig. 2b). This oxidative burst was effectively mitigated by the application of allantoin and serotonin, both individually and in combination, which significantly lowered the accumulation of H₂O₂ and MDA. This aligns with established literature on allantoin, which has been shown to preserve membrane integrity in Arabidopsis under salinity by reducing MDA, a key indicator of lipid peroxidation24. This membrane-stabilizing effect, coupled with a decrease in H₂O₂, helps maintain cellular compartmentalization and reduces electrolyte leakage106. A parallel effect is documented for serotonin, which alleviates salt stress by diminishing MDA content and suppressing membrane lipid peroxidation70. Supporting this, Liu et al5. reported that exogenous serotonin application reduced MDA and H₂O₂ levels while concurrently restoring chlorophyll content in rapeseed under salt stress. Collectively, these previous findings are in direct concordance with the current results, highlighting the potent, synergistic capacity of allantoin and serotonin to bolster the antioxidant system, scavenge reactive oxygen species, and protect cellular membranes from oxidative degradation.

Our findings indicate a significant decline in K⁺ content under saline conditions. This reduction is likely due to the displacement of vital cations like K⁺ and calcium (Ca²⁺) by the high Na⁺ concentrations typical of saline environments. Potassium is fundamental to plant physiology, acting as a major osmoticum that maintains turgor pressure, a cofactor for enzyme activation in metabolism and synthesis, and a key regulator of stomatal aperture107. Its role extends to enhancing overall stress resilience by supporting nutrient transport, optimizing photosynthetic gas exchange, and mitigating the adverse impacts of abiotic stressors77. The observed reduction in N uptake in salt-stressed cotton plants108 aligns with the nutrient deficiencies noted in our study. Consistent with these mechanisms, our results demonstrate that allantoin and serotonin counteracted the ionic imbalance caused by salinity. Both compounds increased leaf K⁺ and P concentrations while reducing Na⁺ and Cl⁻ accumulation. Furthermore, their combined application significantly enhanced N content in bitter melon leaves, corroborating previously reported findings and proposed explanations. Allantoin appears to counteract these effects through multiple mechanisms. It promotes root development, thereby expanding the surface area available for nutrient acquisition7, and may also upregulate genes responsible for nutrient transporter proteins, enhancing the assimilation and distribution of vital elements70. Furthermore, its antioxidant capacity mitigates oxidative damage in root tissues, preserving their functional integrity for nutrient uptake106. Our findings confirm that allantoin application improved osmoregulation and reduced oxidative injury in bitter melon under salinity. The role of allantoin in salt stress response is further supported by its identification as a key metabolic marker in the roots of rice (Oryza sativa L.), where it accumulates in salt-tolerant cultivars109. It also contributes to ionic homeostasis by modulating the uptake of Na⁺, K⁺, Ca²⁺, and magnesium (Mg²⁺)71. Finally, functioning as a nitrogen source, allantoin directly increased the nitrogen content in the leaves of bitter melon subjected to salt stress110,111. Serotonin modulates the transport of specific ions across biological membranes in plants112. NR plays a key role in NO3− assimilation that catalyzes the conversion of nitrate to nitrite. The application of serotonin maximized NR activity while increasing salinity concentration hindered its activity due to the reduced uptake of nitrate under salt stress12.

As a major secondary metabolite, secondary metabolites production is significantly affected by salt stress, which can alter its yield and composition to improve antioxidant capacity. Under favorable conditions, however, most energy is channeled into primary, not secondary, metabolism113. Increase in secondary metabolites at low levels of salinity stress was also found in bitter melon11, Alcea rosea114 and cucumber64. In Lamiaceae family, the quantitative and qualitative composition of the dew secondary metabolites was reduced by salinity115. Allantoin serves as a plant-accessible nitrogen (N) source. Since nitrogen is a critical factor affecting secondary metabolites biosynthesis, as demonstrated in E. purpurea116, allantoin’s role is significant. The importance of N extends to its fundamental role in building organic compounds like enzymes, proteins, and amino acids, which are essential for the formation of secondary metabolites117.

Exposure to environmental stressors often leads to an increase in plant alkaloid concentrations118. Similarly, saponins another class of secondary metabolites accumulate in reaction to biotic and abiotic challenges. For instance, Yang et al119. demonstrated that saponin application improved quinoa seed germination under salinity, likely due to its ROS-scavenging properties. Given that allantoin is a nitrogen-rich intermediate of purine catabolism and a key molecule in nitrogen transport120, it could directly contribute to the synthesis of nitrogen-containing alkaloids in bitter melon fruit. In a parallel manner, serotonin—a tryptophan-derived compound—is part of a broader metabolic network involved in stress adaptation. Tryptophan serves as a precursor for a wide array of stress-responsive molecules, including auxins, alkaloids, and phytoalexins121. Therefore, the application of serotonin provides a plausible explanation for the observed increase in alkaloid content in bitter melon, linking it to the mobilization of key biosynthetic pathways under stress.

Salinity stress is known to alter fruit carbohydrate levels, though the effect is species-specific and dependent on stress intensity122. While data on bitter melon is limited, research on tomato has shown that salt stress can enhance fruit quality by elevating dry matter, soluble solids, amino acids, and concentrations of sugars like glucose, fructose, and sucrose123. This accumulation is partly governed by the transcriptional regulation of invertases, key enzymes that modulate sugar metabolism in response to environmental challenges122, indicating that stress-induced changes in gene expression for these enzymes can direct sugar partitioning to the fruit. Research in wheat has demonstrated that allantoin application promotes the accumulation of osmolytes such as amino acids, soluble proteins, sugars, and proline, which contribute to osmotic adjustment and ROS scavenging106. In a complementary finding, exogenous melatonin was shown to enhance plum fruit quality by modulating metabolic pathways to increase soluble sugar content and reduce organic acids124. Given the structural and functional similarities between melatonin and serotonin, it is plausible that serotonin exerts a comparable positive influence on fruit quality. In the present study, the application of allantoin and serotonin, especially in combination, led to a significant increase in secondary Essential in bitter melon fruit under salt stress. This enhancement can be reasonably attributed to the mechanisms outlined in the aforementioned studies, collectively indicating a beneficial role in improving the qualitative attributes of bitter melon.

The concentration of phenolic acids in both the leaf and fruit of bitter melon was elevated in response to salinity and biostimulant treatments. These compounds perform diverse biological roles, including defense against ultraviolet radiation, pathogens, and the reactive oxygen species generated by environmental stresses like salinity114. This response is not unique to bitter melon; for instance, salt stress has been shown to enhance levels of caffeic, ferulic, and chlorogenic acids in pepper73. In our study, the rise in cinnamic acid and other phenolic acids was closely associated with improved growth and physiological performance under salt stress. The significant accumulation of p-coumaric acid likely helped mitigate oxidative pressure due to its potent hydroxyl group-mediated radical scavenging capacity. The presence of ferulic acid under osmotic stress may contribute to the fortification of the plant cell wall, potentially restricting excessive cell elongation125. Furthermore, ferulic acid is implicated in salt tolerance mechanisms by reducing lipid peroxidation through the activation of antioxidant enzymes and by promoting the accumulation of proline and soluble sugars in bitter melon leaves.

N availability is a key factor regulating the biosynthesis of phenolic compounds. For instance, nitrogen fertilization has been shown to elevate concentrations of phenolic acids like ferulic acid and vanillic acid126. As allantoin can serve as a nitrogen source for plants, its application provides a plausible explanation for the observed increase in phenolic acids in bitter melon. A parallel mechanism exists for compounds like melatonin; in salt-stressed barley, melatonin application upregulated key phenylpropanoid pathway genes and enzymes (phenylalanine ammonia-lyase and cinnamate 4-hydroxylase), leading to a significant accumulation of phenolic acids such as ferulic acid and p-coumaric acid. Similarly, Sheikhalipour et al3. reported that melatonin treatment enhanced phenolic acid content in bitter melon under salinity. In the current investigation, the combined foliar application of allantoin and serotonin significantly boosted phenolic acid levels in both the leaves and fruits of salt-stressed bitter melon, a finding that aligns with the mechanisms and results described in these previous studies.

Conclusion

To mitigate the detrimental effects of salt stress, plants initiate a suite of intricate physiological and defensive adaptations. Allantoin has emerged as a significant compound in enhancing salinity tolerance and improving agricultural productivity. Its function as a nitrogen source, coupled with its role in stress resilience, positions it as a promising agent for boosting crop yields and supporting global food security. Exogenous serotonin has antioxidation properties, regulates osmotic activity and promotes growth, which then could effectively improve salt tolerance of plants. In the present study, allantoin and serotonin have been found to be an essential player in plant processes particularly mitigating salt stress in bitter melon as they played a role in antioxidant properties, ion homeostasis and water balance. Allantoin and serotonin exhibited the capacity to improve chlorophyll content and nutrient uptake in leaves, particularly in essential elements like P, N, and K+, crucial for bitter melon growth. Furthermore, the combined application of allantoin and serotonin showed a pronounced capacity to modulate the plant’s antioxidant defense system. To confirm these promising results, subsequent research should focus on field-scale trials to verify the treatment’s effectiveness in enhancing the growth of bitter melon and other economically significant crops under saline conditions. The simultaneous application of allantoin and serotonin improved the studied traits in bitter melon, which could be due to potential interference between abscisic acid (ABA) signaling (activated by allantoin) and auxin/cytokinin pathways (influenced by serotonin) or the joint effects of both compounds on ROS inhibition. The application of allantoin and serotonin in bitter melon was associated with an increase in the quantitative and qualitative characteristics of the fruit.
