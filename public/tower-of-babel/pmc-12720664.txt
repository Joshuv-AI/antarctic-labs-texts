Oral Prevotella induces fat taste impairment, visceral lipid accumulation and insulin resistance by downregulating Hedgehog signaling in taste buds
Authors: Xiaohuan Liu, Jiehan Zhang, Yi He, Qiao Zhang, Shuaixian Du, Tianshu Zeng, Jiaoyue Zhang, Hao Zhang, Han Luo, Huiqing Li, Ying Wang, Miaomiao Peng, Nan Zhang, Qi Chen, Hantao Huang, Ping Wang, Lulu Chen, Xiang Hu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFat taste impairment has been implicated in visceral lipid accumulation and insulin resistance, with emerging evidence linking it to the oral microbiota. However, the role and mechanisms of the oral microbiota in this process remain unclear.ObjectiveWe aimed to explore the manifestations of Prevotella in fat taste, visceral lipid accumulation and insulin sensitivity, as well as to elucidate the mechanism involved.DesignWe characterized the oral microbiota in humans with fat taste impairment, visceral lipid accumulation and insulin resistance, as well as in catch-up fat rats. Fat taste sensitivity, serum biochemistry and tissue morphology were assessed in rats colonized orally with Prevotella to explore potential mechanisms.ResultsReduced fat taste sensitivity correlated with visceral lipid accumulation and insulin resistance in both individuals and rats. Prevotella was enriched in individuals and rats with low fat taste sensitivity. Additionally, rats with visceral lipid accumulation and insulin resistance were associated with lower proliferation in taste buds and inhibition in Hedgehog (Hh) signaling. Prevotella colonization downregulated the Hh signaling, fat taste impairment, visceral lipid accumulation and insulin resistance, whereas Hh pathway agonist supplementation mitigated these effects.ConclusionsOral microbiota and fat taste impairment are associated with visceral lipid accumulation and insulin resistance, and Prevotella may play a vital role in fat taste impairment, visceral lipid accumulation and insulin resistance by downregulating the Hh signaling in taste buds.Keywords: Oral microbiota, fat taste impairment, visceral lipid accumulation, insulin resistance, Hedgehog signaling, taste buds

Introduction

In recent years, obesity-related diseases have become a global public health concern, posing serious threats to health and increasing socioeconomic burden [1,2]. Fat taste, arising from the binding of dietary fat metabolites to specific receptors on taste bud cells, is increasingly implicated in obesity development [3]. Recent studies have indicated that obese individuals exhibit reduced fat taste sensitivity [4], with a negative linear correlation between BMI and fat taste sensitivity [5]. It is reported that the decreased fat taste perception can lead to a preference for high-fat foods and increased fatty food intake, which is considered to be a key driver of excessive intake of fat-rich foods [4]. Our previous research showed that visceral lipid accumulation and insulin resistance were associated with significantly reduced fat taste sensitivity in catch-up fat rats [6], suggesting that impaired fat taste perception may contribute to both visceral lipid accumulation and insulin resistance.

Taste buds are essential for fat taste perception and regulation [7], and reduced fat taste perception is significantly associated with fewer fungiform papillae in individuals [8]. Similarly, obese mice show a marked reduction in fungiform papillae, and a negative correlation exists between papillae number and abdominal obesity in individuals [9], suggesting that the loss of taste buds is closely related to impaired fat taste perception and obesity.

Tongue papillae are vital habitats of the oral microbiota [10], and taste buds maintain the complex host–microbe symbiosis essential for oral homeostasis [11]. Recently, it has been demonstrated that some bacterial taxa on the tongue dorsum are associated with gustatory function and dietary patterns, such as vegetable-rich and protein/fat-rich diets [12]. The oral microbiota has also been implicated in obesity-related diseases such as prediabetes and diabetes [13]. Moreover, obese children and adolescents show greater α-diversity and higher Proteobacteria abundance [14]. Our preliminary study indicated that increased colonization of Tannerella forsythia on the tongue dorsum was correlated with fatty food preference, fat accumulation and insulin resistance [6]. However, the specific oral microbial alterations underlying impaired fat taste perception, visceral adiposity and insulin resistance, and the potential mechanisms involved may be highly complex and remain unclear.

In this study, we aimed to investigate changes in fat taste perception and the oral microbiota in humans and animals with visceral lipid accumulation and insulin resistance, and explore the role of the oral microbiota in these changes and their underlying mechanisms.

Materials and methods

Subjects and study design

91 subjects of five families were selected to eliminate the influence of genetics, the living environment and dietary habits on taste perception, and the oral microbiota in this study from the REACTION (Risk Evaluation of Cancers in Chinese Diabetic Individuals: a longitudinal study) cohort, which is a nationwide, multicenter, prospective cohort study initiated in 2011, aiming to investigate the associations between abnormal glucose metabolism and cancer risk among Chinese adults aged over 40 years and collecting comprehensive data on metabolic phenotypes, lifestyle factors and biological samples for long-term follow-up, as described previously [15].

Data collection was performed by the trained staff, and a questionnaire was completed to collect information on demographic characteristics, lifestyle and medical history. Weight, height, waist circumference (WC), hip circumference (HC), blood pressure, BMI, waist-to-hip ratio (WHR) and waist-to-height ratio (WHtR) were measured and calculated as described previously [16]. We defined abdominal obesity (male WC ≥ 90 cm, female WC ≥ 85 cm) and non-abdominal obesity (male WC ≤ 85 cm, female WC ≤ 80 cm) [17], respectively.

Animals and experiment design

Male Sprague-Dawley rats (aged 6 weeks, n = 12) were housed individually, with free access to water and subjected to controlled lighting (lights at 06:00–18:00), temperature (22 ± 3 °C) and relative humidity (50 ± 10%). All the rats were randomly allocated to the normal controls (NC) and catch-up fat rats developed by refeeding with normal chow after caloric restriction (RN), which are characterized by visceral lipid accumulation and insulin resistance with no obvious subcutaneous lipid deposition [18]. The NC group was maintained on a normal chow diet, which provided 13.68% of energy from fat, 64.44% from carbohydrates and 21.88% from protein for 12 weeks, while the RN group was developed by refeeding with normal chow for 8 weeks after 4 weeks of caloric restriction, which was restricted to 60% of normal chow food intake that was eaten by their ad libitum-fed companions of the same weight while caloric restriction.

Additionally, based on the preliminary data that Prevotella and Hh signaling might play a crucial role in the development of fat taste impairment, visceral lipid accumulation and insulin resistance mentioned above, male rats (n = 21), which were maintained with free-choice feeding, were further randomly divided into the normal control (NC), oral Prevotella intermedia colonization (Pi) and oral P. intermedia colonization combined with 4 µl of 0.1 mg/ml SAG, a Hh signaling pathway agonist (PS) [19]. The inoculation of the tongue dorsum was performed with P. intermedia (109 CFU/ml) daily for 2 weeks.

Body weight and food intake were recorded, and venous blood was collected for biochemical analyses. The tongue, subcutaneous adipose tissue (AT), perirenal AT and epididymal AT were harvested for further assays.

Fat taste sensitivity evaluation

The fat taste threshold of the participants was assessed using the 3-alternative forced choice (3-AFC) method [20]. Twelve gradient concentrations (0.5−29.4 g/ml) of fat solutions were prepared by dissolving varying amounts of cream (Nestle, Switzerland) in skimmed milk (Nestle, Switzerland), with the skimmed milk serving as a control solution. The participants were provided with multiple sample sets with three solutions (2 controls and 1 containing fat) in a random order to identify the one containing fat. Incorrect identification of the fat sample would result in a new sample set with a higher concentration of fat. The concentration of the fat-containing solution that a participant correctly identified in three consecutive trials was recorded as their fat taste threshold. Based on the results of the fat taste threshold test, scores were assigned from 1 to 13, ascending from the lowest to the highest threshold described previously [21]. The individuals with scores lower than the first quartile (P1) or higher than the third quartile (P4) were defined as high-fat taste sensitivity (HTS) and low-fat taste sensitivity (LTS), respectively.

The fat taste sensitivity of the rats in the NC and RN groups was assessed using a licking test with fat solutions as described previously [22]. Corn oil solutions were classified into lower (0, 0.03, 0.06 and 1%) and higher (16, 32 and 64%) concentrations, and each bottle of the seven different concentration solutions was licked by the rats for 10 s going back and forth in cycles for 30 min in total, and the lick counts were recorded. The two-bottle choice test was employed to assess the preference for fat in the NC, Pi and PS rats as described previously [22].

Serum biochemical analysis

Fasting plasma glucose (FPG), fasting insulin (FINS), glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), serum total triglycerides (TG), total cholesterol (TC), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) were measured as described previously [23].

Insulin sensitivity and visceral lipid accumulation evaluation

The homeostatic model assessment of insulin resistance (HOMA-IR) was calculated to assess insulin sensitivity, and the intraperitoneal insulin tolerance test (IPITT) was performed in animals after overnight fasting as described previously [23]. Individuals with HOMA-IR less than or equal to the first quartile (P1) were defined as the insulin-sensitive (IS) group, while those with HOMA-IR greater than the third quartile (P4) were defined as the insulin-resistant (IR) group.

The Chinese visceral adiposity index (CVAI), which accounts for the characteristics of body fat in Asians and has been confirmed by CT for its relevance to VAT, was calculated for the study population to assess visceral lipid accumulation based on age, gender, BMI, WC, TG and HDL-C using the formula as follows: CVAI = −267.93 + 0.68age + 0.03BMI + 4.00WC + 22.00Log10TG − 16.32HDL-C for males, CVAI = −187.32 + 1.71age + 4.23BMI + 1.12WC + 39.76Log10TG − 11.66HDL-C for females [24–26].

The ratio of subcutaneous fat (g) to body weight (g) fat (SAT) and the ratio of visceral fat (epididymal and perirenal fat (g)) to body weight (g) (VAT) were calculated to evaluate the fat distribution in the present study.

Oral microbiota sequencing and analysis

Oral microbiota were collected from subjects and experimental animals after fasting to exclude the potential effect of feeding as described previously to make the microbiome composition relatively stable [27,28]. The DNA of the oral microbiota was extracted as described previously [29]. The highly variable regions V4-V5 of bacterial 16S rDNA were selected for sequencing using the following primers: forward (F) 5ʹ- GTGCCAGCMGCCGCGGTAA -3ʹ and reverse (R) 5ʹ-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT−3ʹ. The TruSeq Nano DNA LT Library Prep Kit (Illumina, USA) was used for library preparation, and paired-end sequencing was performed on the NovaSeq Sequencing Platform (Illumina, USA) using the NovaSeq 6000 SP Reagent Kit (Illumina, USA).

The sequencing data were used for species composition, α- and β-diversity, and linear discriminant analysis effect size (LefSe) analysis [30]. Additionally, random forest analysis, which is a promising technique in the machine learning subset of artificial intelligence, was conducted using the ‘classify_samples_ncv’ function along with nested stratified cross-validation. Internal validation was performed using 10-fold cross-validation.

Morphological assays and analysis

The number and distribution of taste buds in the lingual root and tip of the rats were investigated by hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining as described by Yan Li. et al. [31].

Ki67 immunostaining was used to assess cell proliferation in taste buds as described by Kumari et al. [32]. Sonic hedgehog (Shh) and glioma-associated oncogene 1 (Gli1) expression in the Hh signaling pathway were evaluated using immunofluorescence-based confocal microscopy. The cell apoptosis of taste buds was detected by TUNEL staining using TUNEL assay kits following the manufacturer's instructions (Roche, Switzerland) [33].

Real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction

RT-PCR was conducted using a qPCR kit (Vazyme, Nanjing, China), LightCycler 480 II (Roche, Germany) and a PCR amplifier (Bio-Rad, USA) to detect the mRNA expression levels of Hh and Gli1 in tongue epithelial tissues as described previously (23) using the following primers: Hh, (F) 5ʹ- ACAGGGACCGCAGCAAGTAT−3ʹ, (R) 5ʹ-GGATTCATAGTAGACCCAGTCG; Gli1 (F) 5ʹ-CTTCAAGGCCCAGTACATGC−3ʹ, (R) 5ʹ-GATCTGTGTAGCGCTTGGTG−3ʹ; β-actin(F) 5ʹ-GGTCATCACCATTGGCAA−3ʹ, (R) 5ʹ-GAGTTGAAGGTAGTTTCGTGGA−3ʹ.

Statistical analysis

α-Diversity was used to assess the microbial diversity within samples, while β-diversity was evaluated using PCoA to compare differences between samples. Biomarker identification was performed using Linear Discriminant Analysis Effect Size (LEfSe; LDA score > 2.0, p < 0.05), and feature importance was further prioritized using a random forest algorithm. Continuous variables normally distributed were expressed as means ± standard errors, while those with a skewed distribution were represented by medians and interquartile ranges. Categorical variables were described using frequencies and percentages. Comparison among the groups was performed using independent samples t-tests between two groups and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) among three or more groups, with post hoc pairwise comparisons made using the least significant difference (LSD) test for normally distributed continuous variables, non-parametric Mann–Whitney U-tests for skewed continuous variables and Chi-square tests for categorical variables. Spearman's correlation was applied to determine the relationship between two variables and/or measures, and variance in distance matrices was analyzed using the Adonis test. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 22.0 (SPSS, Illinois, USA), with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

Impairment of fat taste sensitivity is associated with visceral lipid accumulation and insulin resistance in individuals and animals

A total of 91 participants (44 males and 47 females) were included in this study. Among them, 49 participants (25 males and 24 females) had abdominal obesity and 42 participants (19 males and 23 females) did not. Based on HOMA-IR levels, 21 individuals (14 males and 7 females) were classified as insulin-sensitive, and 21 individuals (11 males and 10 females) were classified as insulin-resistant. Based on the fat taste threshold levels, individuals with a threshold ≤9 were defined as the HTS group (n = 24), and those with a threshold ≥13 were defined as the LTS group (n = 21). Compared with those in the HTS group, individuals in the LTS group exhibited significantly higher values of weight, BMI, WC, WHtR, WHR, CVAI, FINS and HOMA-IR (p < 0.05), while no obvious difference was observed in age, sex, lifestyle habits, including smoking and drinking, systolic and diastolic blood pressure, heart rate, FPG, HbA1c, lipid profile, and liver function tests (p > 0.05) (Table S2). Further Spearman correlation analysis revealed a positive linear correlation of fat taste threshold with WC (R = 0.341, p = 0.001), WHtR (R = 0.343, p = 0.001), WHR (R = 0.280, p = 0.008), CVAI (R = 0.284, p = 0.007), HOMA-IR (R = 0.219, p = 0.048), weight (R = 0.277, p = 0.008) and BMI (R = 0.358, p = 0.001) (Figure 1).

In rodents, the catch-up fat group (RN) showed significant increases in the ratio of VAT, FINS and HOMA-IR levels compared to the normal controls (NC) (p < 0.05) (Figure 2A–C), with no significant differences in the ratio of SAT, and FPG (p > 0.05) (Figure 2D,E). The IPITT results demonstrated higher glucose levels at 30 and 60 min post-insulin injection, and a greater area under the curve in the RN group compared to the NC group (p < 0.05) (Figure 2F,G).

Fat taste sensitivity test indicated significantly more licks for the higher concentration lipid solutions (16–64%) in the RN group (p < 0.05), suggesting impaired fat taste perception, while no significant differences in licking responses to low-concentration lipid solutions (0−1%) was monitored between the two groups (p > 0.05) (Figure 2H,I).

Impaired fat taste perception, abdominal obesity and insulin resistance are associated with altered oral microbiome in humans

Analysis of oral bacterial communities revealed significant compositional differences between the HTS and LTS groups at the phylum, class, order, family and genus levels (p < 0.05, Figure S1A–E), despite no significant differences in the number of taxonomic units at these levels (p > 0.05, Figure S1F). In detail, the LTS group showed significantly lower relative abundances of Actinobacteria, Firmicutes, Streptococcus and Actinomyces, and higher proportions of Fusobacteria, Proteobacteria, Bacteroidia, Neisseria, Prevotella, Fusobacterium, Porphyromonas and Leptotrichia compared to the HTS group (p < 0.05, Figure S1A–E).

Analysis of microbial seven α-diversity indices revealed a significant increase in the Chao1 index (Richness) and decreases in Goods_coverage index (Richness) and Pielou_e index (Evenness) in the LTS group compared to the HTS group (p < 0.05), while no significant differences were observed in Faith_pd, Shannon, Simpson and Observed_species indices (p > 0.05) (Figure 3A). Additionally, β-diversity analysis based on the Jaccard distance and visualized via principal coordinates analysis (PCoA) demonstrated distinct clustering between the two groups. Furthermore, Adonis analysis revealed that there was a statistically significant difference in sample distribution between the two groups (p < 0.05) (Figure 3B).

LEfSe analysis revealed that there were significant increases in the abundance of Bacteroidales (order), Bacteroidia (class), Fusobacteriia (class), Fusobacteriales (order), Fusobacteria (phylum), Fusobacteriaceae (family), Fusobacterium (genus), Peptococcus (genus) and Peptococcaceae (family), and remarkable decreases in Actinomycetaceae (family), Actinomyces (genus), Aerococcaceae (family), Granulicatella (genus), Ralstonia (genus) and Rothia (genus) in the LTS group (linear discriminant analysis (LDA) score > 2, p < 0.05). Noteworthy, the Bacteroidetes (phylum) had the highest LDA values and was considered as the most significantly enriched bacteria in this group (Figure 3C).

Random forest analysis evaluating the importance and enrichment of bacteria showed that Peptococcus, Prevotella and Fusobacterium were enriched in the LTS group, while the enriching bacteria were Actinomyces, Sphingomonas, Streptococcus, Capnocytophaga, Campylobacter, Atopobium and Lachnoanaerobaculum in the HTS group. Furthermore, the prediction model developed using the random forest algorithm with the top 25 important bacterial genera had an accuracy of 0.78 in predicting LTS individuals (Figure 3D).

The α-diversity analysis indicated that there was no significant difference between the abdominal obesity group (A-Ob group, n = 11) and the non-abdominal obesity group (NC, n = 23) (p > 0.05) (Figure 3E), while statistically significant differences were observed in the results of PCoA between the two groups (p < 0.05) (Figure 3F). The results of LEfSe revealed that there were significant increases in the abundance of Porphyromonadaceae (family), Porphyromonas (genus), Fusobacteriaceae (family), Fusobacterium (genus), Gammaproteobacteria (class) and remarkable decreases in Actinobacteria (phylum), Actinomycetales (order) and Actinobacteria (class) in the A-Ob group (Figure 3G). Further analysis using random forests illustrated that Kingella, Porphyromonas, Haemophilus, Prevotella, and Peptococcus were the predominant enriched genera in the A-Ob group (Figure 3H).

In addition, the α-diversity analysis revealed that there was no significant difference between the insulin resistance group (IR group, n = 8) and the insulin sensitivity group (IS group, n = 9) (p > 0.05) (Figure 3I), while statistically significant differences were observed in the results of PCoA (p < 0.05) (Figure 3J). The LEfSe analysis revealed that there were significant increases in the abundance of Comamonadaceae (family) in the IS group (Figure 3K). Furthermore, the random forest analysis demonstrated that Haemophilus, Peptococcus, and Prevotella were enriched in the IR group (Figure 3L).

Fat taste impairment, visceral lipid accumulation and insulin resistance are associated with altered oral bacterial colonization in rats

The comparison of the quantities of oral bacteria categories, revealed that there seemed no significant difference in the number of taxonomic categories at different classification levels including phylum (p = 0.41), class (p = 0.41), order (p = 0.80), family (p = 0.95), genus (p = 0.28) and species (p = 0.09) between the NC group and the RN group (Figure S2 A).

Further comparison of the oral bacterial composition differences between the NC and RN groups at various taxonomic levels (phylum, class, order, family, genus and species) revealed that obvious increases were observed in the abundance of Clostridia (class), Bacteroidia (class), Clostridiales (order), Bacteroidales (order), Ruminococcaceae (family), Gemmiger (genus), Prevotella (genus) and Rubrivivax (genus) (p < 0.05), and significant decreases were detected in the abundance of Aggregatibacter (genus) and Streptococcus (genus) (p < 0.05) in the RN group compared to the NC group (Figure S2B–F).

The α-diversity analysis showed remarkable differences were observed in Shannon index (p = 0.014), and there were no significant differences in Chao1 index (p = 0.05), Goods_coverage index (p = 0.81), Pielou_e index (p = 0.05), Faith_pd (p = 0.46), Simpson (p = 0.81) and Observed_species index (p = 0.05) between the RN and NC groups (Figure 2M). Further β diversity analysis indicated that the contributions of PCo1 and PCo2 were 14.4 and 13.1%, individually, and Adonis analysis indicated that significant differences were observed in sample distribution between the two groups (p < 0.05) (Figure 3N).

The LEfSe analysis revealed that the predominant bacterial flora in the RN group were Clostridiales (order), Clostridia (class), Bacteroidia (class), Bacteroidales (order), Ruminococcaceae (family), Bacteroidaceae (family), Bacteroides (genus) and Faecalibacterium (genus) compared to the NC group (LDA scores > 2, p < 0.05), while the dominant bacteria in the NC group included Aggregatibacter (genus), Polaromonas (genus), Asticcacaulis (genus), Hydrogenophaga (genus) and Xanthobacteraceae (family) (LDA score > 2, p < 0.05). Additionally, Clostridia (class) and Bacteroidia (class) had the highest LDA values in the RN group and were the most significantly enriched bacteria in this group (Figure 3O).

Further random forest analysis results revealed that Bacteroides, Dorea, Prevotella, Anaerococcus, Peptoniphilus, Sutterella, Butyricimonas and Faecalibacterium were the most predominant among the top 10 genera in the RN group (Figure 3P).

Visceral lipid accumulation and insulin resistance in rats are associated with reduced proliferation in taste buds and impaired Hh signaling

The H&E staining and immunofluorescence staining showed that there were significant decreases in the number of taste buds and visible taste pores in circumvallate papillae in the RN group compared to the NC group (p < 0.05). However, no significant difference was observed in the length of circumvallate papillae and the area of taste buds (p > 0.05) (Figure 4A,B).

Assessment of cell proliferation using Ki67 immunostaining revealed a significant reduction in Ki67-positive cells within the circumvallate (Figure 4C,D) and fungiform papillae, and further counting in different sections indicated fewer Ki67-positive cells around the taste buds and at the base of the papillae in the RN group compared to the NC group (p < 0.05) (Figure 4E,F). Interestingly, there was no significant difference in cell proliferation in the filiform papillae, which are not considered as bud-bearing papillae (p > 0.05) (Figure 4G,H). TUNEL staining showed no significant differences in the number of apoptotic cells in taste buds of the circumvallate and fungiform papillae between the RN and NC groups (p > 0.05) (Figure 4I,J).

The RT-PCR and immunofluorescence analyses showed decreased mRNA and protein expression levels of Shh and Gli1 in fungiform papillae taste buds, which play crucial roles in the Hh signaling, were decreased in the RN group compared to the NC group (p < 0.05) (Figure 4K,L), suggesting the downregulation of Hh signaling in taste buds of RN rats.

Prevotella colonization induces fat taste perception impairment, visceral lipid accumulation and insulin resistance by downregulating the Hh signaling

There were significant decreases in expression levels of Gli in the Pi group compared to the NC group (p < 0.05) (Figure 5A,B), while obvious increases were observed in the PS group compared to the Pi group (p < 0.05), suggesting that the inhibition of Hh signaling induced by Prevotella colonization could be attenuated by SAG, a Hh signaling pathway agonist.

Our results indicated that the proliferation level based on Ki67 labelling of taste bud cells in the circumvallate papillae was significantly reduced in the Pi group compared to the NC group and increased in the PS group compared to the Pi group (Figure 5C,D). Additionally, in the fungiform papillae, significant decreases were observed in the number of Ki67-positive cells in the perigemmal cells in the Pi group compared to the NC group, while the number of Ki67-positive cells at the base of the papillae or in the whole papillae was significantly elevated in the PS group compared to the Pi group (Figure 5E,F).

Our results showed that increases were observed in body weight, SAT and VAT, FINS, HOMA-IR and TG levels (p < 0.05) (Figure 6A‒F) in the Pi group compared to the NC group, although there were no significant changes in FPG, TC, HDL-C, or LDL-C (p > 0.05) (Figure 6G–J). Remarkably, considerable decreases were detected in body weight, VAT, FINS and HOMA-IR in the PS group compared to the Pi group (p < 0.05) (Figure 6A,C–E), although there were no obvious changes in SAT, FINS, TG, TC, HDL-C or LDL-C (p > 0.05) (Figure 6B,D,F,H–J).

Additionally, the two-bottle preference test showed that the NC group had a higher percentage of intake of the 0.2% linoleic acid solution compared to the control solution (p < 0.05), while the Pi group did not show a significant difference in intake percentage between the linoleic acid solution and the control solution (p > 0.05), suggesting that the NC group exhibited a spontaneous preference for the low-concentration fat solution and the Pi group rats showed reduced spontaneous preference for the fat solution, which indicated that the fat taste perception was impaired in the Pi group. The PS group showed a significantly higher intake percentage of the linoleic acid solution compared to the control solution (p < 0.05), suggesting that supplementation with SAG restored the rats' preference for the low-concentration fat solution and improved fat taste sensitivity (Figure 6K).

Discussion

In this study, we showed that visceral lipid accumulation and insulin resistance in humans and animals were closely related to fat taste perception impairment and changes in the oral microbiota, characterized by increased Prevotella abundance, which was associated with lower proliferation in taste buds and downregulation of Hh signaling. Remarkably, Prevotella colonization induced visceral lipid accumulation, insulin resistance, fat taste perception impairment, proliferation and downregulation of Hh signaling in taste buds, which could be attenuated by Hh signaling pathway agonism.

Our results indicated that increases in WC, WHtR, WHR, CVAI and HOMA-IR, which are widely considered to be closely associated with visceral lipid accumulation [34–37] and insulin resistance [38], were positively correlated with fat taste thresholds, with consistent findings in animal experiments. These findings suggest a close correlation between impaired fat taste sensitivity and the development of visceral lipid accumulation and insulin resistance, which is consistent with previous studies. Moreover, it is reported that individuals with reduced fat taste perception consume more dietary fat and calories and exhibit higher obesity risk [39,40], indicating that fat taste impairment might be an important factor in the development of visceral lipid accumulation and insulin resistance.

We observed that visceral lipid accumulation, insulin resistance and fat taste impairment in humans and animals were closely related to changes in the oral microbiota, particularly increased abundance of Prevotella within the Bacteroidales. While gut microbiome changes are well recognized in obesity, insulin resistance and dyslipidemia [41], recent evidence shows that elevated oral Bacteroidales are linked to fat taste impairment in individuals [42,43], suggesting that Prevotella may represent a key genus driving fat taste impairment and consequently instigating visceral lipid accumulation and insulin resistance. In the LTS group, the increased Chao1 index alongside decreased Pielou evenness suggested an increase in microbial richness but a decrease in community uniformity. This pattern may reflect an increase in rare taxa, which elevates richness (as captured by Chao1) without achieving dominance, thereby reducing evenness [44]. Such ecological shifts could be relevant in the context of oral microenvironment alterations associated with fat taste impairment, though further studies are needed to clarify the functional implications of these structural changes.

Our study found that rats with visceral lipid accumulation and insulin resistance exhibited impaired fat taste, reduced proliferation of taste buds in circumvallate papillae and downregulation of Hh signaling. Recent studies have indicated that taste perception depends on maintaining a stable number of taste buds, which are rapidly renewed epithelial structures [45], and fat taste sensitivity is closely linked to taste bud abundance [7]. The Hh signaling pathway is critical for regulating the renewal and balance of taste buds [46]. Inhibition of this pathway reduces taste bud proliferation, leading to loss and taste disorders [46,47], as shown with Sonidegib treatment in fungiform and circumvallate papillae [32], whereas Hh activation by SAG restores proliferation and regeneration [48]. Thus, our results suggest that the Hh signaling downregulation and the consequent reduction in taste bud proliferation might play a key role in the fat taste impairment associated with visceral lipid accumulation and insulin resistance. We also found that oral Prevotella colonization in rats induced visceral lipid accumulation, insulin resistance, impaired fat taste and downregulation of Hh signaling, while supplementation with the Hh pathway agonist SAG attenuated these effects, suggesting that Prevotella mediates fat taste impairment, visceral lipid accumulation and insulin resistance through Hh signaling suppression in taste buds.

Yet, it should be noted that our study had several limitations. First, although we performed in vivo colonization experiments to establish a causal role for oral Prevotella in fat taste impairment, visceral lipid accumulation and insulin resistance, and further demonstrated that activation of the Hedgehog signaling pathway can ameliorate these effects, the initial correlative analyses in humans were not adjusted for potential confounders due to the modest sample size and the number of covariates. Therefore, these human associations should be viewed as exploratory and require validation in larger, well-controlled cohorts. Furthermore, while our animal studies provide compelling evidence for the causal role of Prevotella, the complex interplay between diet, metabolic status and microbiome composition warrants further investigation in models that better account for these interacting factors. Moreover, due to ethical and practical constraints, we could not validate these findings in humans by Prevotella colonization and/or SAG supplementation. Additionally, we utilized a catch-up fat model to induce visceral lipid accumulation and insulin resistance, minimizing subcutaneous fat deposition, instead of high-fat diet-induced obesity models to exclude the impact of subcutaneous lipid accumulation. However, there must be some other models of visceral lipid accumulation and insulin resistance, and we could not confirm our findings across all of them. Thus, further research is probably needed, and the interpretation should be cautious. Moreover, beyond Prevotella, we identified other oral microbiota, including Haemophilus, associated with visceral lipid accumulation, insulin resistance and fat taste impairment in humans and animals, but their roles and mechanisms were not explored here. More attention should be given to their probable roles in subsequent scientific investigations in this field.

Conclusion

Despite these limitations, we tentatively put forward that the oral microbiota is closely related to visceral lipid accumulation, insulin resistance and fat taste impairment in humans and animals, and Prevotella among them induces fat taste impairment, visceral lipid accumulation and insulin resistance by downregulating of Hedgehog signaling in taste buds.
