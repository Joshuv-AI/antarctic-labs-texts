Abstract
. The lattice cohomology of a reduced curve singularity was introduced in [ 4 ] . It is a bigraded ℤ ⁡ [ U ] \mathbb{Z}[U] -module ℍ ∗ = ⊕ q , n ℍ q 2 ​ n \mathbb{H}^{*}=\oplus_{q,n}\mathbb{H}^{q}_{2n} , that categorifies the δ \delta -invariant and extracts key geometric information from the semigroup of values. In the present paper we prove three structure theorems for this new invariant: (a) the weight-grading of the reduced cohomology is – just as in the case of the topological lattice cohomology of normal surface singularities [ 21 ] – nonpositive; (b) the graded ℤ ⁡ [ U ] \mathbb{Z}[U] -module structure of ℍ 0 \mathbb{H}^{0} determines whether or not a given curve is Gorenstein; and finally (c) the lattice cohomology module ℍ 0 \mathbb{H}^{0} of any plane curve singularity determines its multiplicity.

1.
Introduction

1.1.

The theory of curves has occupied a central place throughout the history of algebraic geometry, formulated productive programs, produced strong results, constructed deep invariants and
formidable bridges to other areas of mathematics. The theory was necessarily extended to the case of singular curves, thus creating the theory of local complex algebraic/complex analytic curve singularities.
In this later theory, complex plane curve singularities played a distinguished role. In the case of their study, in addition to the analytic/algebraic tools, low-dimensional topology also had an enormous contribution, and altogether they created
a series of algebraic and topological invariants and deep results. Some recent developments have provided more impetus and new
invariants such as multivariate Poincaré series, the local punctual Hilbert scheme,
new deformation invariants on the analytical side, Jones and HOMFLY polynomial, Heegaard Floer Link and Khovanov theory on the topological side.

On the other hand, for nonplanar curve singularities, topological tools can no longer be used, and
algebraic/analytical methods are much more difficult and less advanced too. In particular, the need for new invariants (which might even replace the missing topological invariants) is a real necessity.

Lattice cohomology is such a new invariant. For reduced curve singularities
it was introduced in
[
4
]
.
Since it has a rather rich structure, it is able to encode deep information about the curve germ. It is a bigraded
\mathbb{Z}[U]
-module analogously to several cohomology theories in low dimesional topology, such as the Heegaard Floer (HF) or Heegaard Floer Link (HFL) homology. In fact,
for
plane curve singularities
it can be related with the HFL homology
(see
[
15
,
23
]
), however, it is defined by analytic methods valid
to curve germs with arbitrarily large embedded dimension.
Hence, it can be viewed as an algebraic analogue of the HFL theory
valid for non-planar singularities as well.

1.2.

The lattice cohomology of reduced complex curve singularities is a member of a family of lattice cohomology theories:
one can define a topological lattice cohomology
[
21
,
20
]
and an analytic lattice cohomology
[
2
]
associated to a normal surface singularity, and the analytic construction can be extended to any dimension
[
3
]
.

The structure of all lattice cohomologies is the following: first,
\mathbb{H}^{*}
is a bigraded
\mathbb{Z}[U]
-module with a cohomological grading given by (the direct sum decomposition of
\mathbb{Z}[U]
-modules)

\mathbb{H}^{*}=\oplus_{q\geq 0}\mathbb{H}^{q}
and a weight grading given by

\mathbb{H}^{q}=\oplus_{n}\mathbb{H}^{q}_{2n}
(where we use a
2\mathbb{Z}
grading because of the HFL parallelism). The
\mathbb{Z}[U]
-module structure is given by a map
U:\mathbb{H}^{q}_{2n}\to\mathbb{H}^{q}_{2n-2}
for every
q
and
n
.

In fact,
\mathbb{H}^{*}
appears as the cohomology of a certain filtered space
\cdots\subset S_{n}\subset S_{n+1}\subset\cdots
as follows:
\mathbb{H}^{q}_{2n}:=H^{q}(S_{n},\mathbb{Z})
and the
U
-action is given by the maps induced by the inclusions
S_{n}\hookrightarrow S_{n+1}
. The
reduced

lattice cohomology
(as a bigraded
\mathbb{Z}[U]
–module)
also appears naturally:
\mathbb{H}^{*}_{\rm red}=\oplus_{q}\mathbb{H}^{q}_{\rm red}
,
\mathbb{H}^{q}_{\rm red}=\oplus_{n}\widetilde{H}^{q}(S_{n},\mathbb{Z})
.

This filtered space satisfies the facts that
S_{n}=\emptyset
for
n\ll 0
and is contractible for
n\gg 0
, and it is a finite CW complex for any
n
.
Therefore,

\mathbb{H}^{*}_{\rm red}
has a finite
\mathbb{Z}
-rank. It follows that
\mathbb{H}^{*}
is determined by
the smallest weight
\min\{n\,|\,S_{n}\not=\emptyset\}

(the analogue of the
d
-invariant used in HF homology
[
24
]
)
and

\mathbb{H}^{*}_{red}
. Using them
one also defines the Euler characteristic by:

(1.2.1)

{\rm eu}(\mathbb{H}^{*}):=-\min\{n\,|\,S_{n}\not=\emptyset\}+\textstyle{\sum_{q}}\,(-1)^{q}\ {\rm rank}_{\mathbb{Z}}\ \mathbb{H}^{q}_{\rm red}.

1.3.

In this article we study the lattice cohomology
\mathbb{H}^{*}=\mathbb{H}^{*}(C,o)

associated with a reduced complex curve singularity
(C,o)
.
In this case
the Euler characteristic of
\mathbb{H}^{*}(C,o)
is the delta invariant
\delta(C,o)
, that is,

\mathbb{H}^{*}(C,o)
is the categorification of
\delta(C,o)

[
4
]
.

Our goal here is to present three structure theorems for
\mathbb{H}^{*}
and list certain properties of
\mathbb{H}^{*}
which
identify concrete families of curve singularities, or provide new conceptual geometric information about distinguished
classical invariants. The present note provides the first list of results in this direction.

As we already mentioned the set
\{n\,|\,\mathbb{H}^{*}_{\text{red},\,2n}(C,o)\not=0\}
is bounded both from above and below. The lower bound is an interesting new invariant of the curve singularity
(C,o)
, which will be not discussed in this note. On the other hand, the upper bound is universal:

Theorem 1.3.1
.

(see the Nonpositivity Theorem
6.1.1
and Theorem
6.1.2
)
The spaces
\{S_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
used to define
\mathbb{H}^{*}(C,o)
are contractible for all
n>0
. Thus,
\mathbb{H}^{q}_{\text{\rm red},\,2n}(C,o)=0
for all
n>0,q\geq 0
.

This statement leads to the following coarse classification of germs in terms of
\mathbb{H}^{0}
, based on how ‘far’ the lower bound is from the upper one, which also shows that the bound
n>0
is optimal.

Proposition 1.3.2
.

(see Proposition
2.7.3
)

(a)
\mathbb{H}^{0}_{\rm red}=0
if and only if
(C,o)
is smooth, i.e. its multiplicity
mult(C,o)=1
.

(b)
\mathbb{H}^{0}_{\rm red}\not=0
but
\mathbb{H}^{0}_{2n}=0
for
n<0

(equivalently,
\min\{n|S_{n}\not=\emptyset\}\geq 0
)
if and only if
mult(C,o)=2
. In this case
(C,o)
is a plane curve singularity with a local equation of type
x^{2}+y^{k}=0
,
k\geq 2
.

(c)
\mathbb{H}^{0}_{2n}\not=0
for some
n<0

(equivalently,
\min\{n|S_{n}\not=\emptyset\}<0
)
if and only if
mult(C,o)>2
.

The simplest germs with
\mathbb{H}^{0}_{\rm red}\not=0
(that is, when

\mathbb{H}^{0}_{\rm red}=\mathbb{H}^{0}_{{\rm red},0}\simeq\mathbb{Z}
and
\mathbb{H}^{\geq 1}=0
) are characterized in
Remark
2.7.4
.

Regarding case
(c)
we have the next characterizations of the Gorenstein property and of
mult(C,o)
.

Theorem 1.3.3
.

If
mult(C,o)>2
then the following hold.

(a) (Theorem
4.1.1
)
(C,o)
is Gorenstein if and only if

\text{rank}_{\mathbb{Z}}\ker\big(U:\mathbb{H}^{0}_{0}(C,o)\to\mathbb{H}^{0}_{-2}(C,o)\big)\geq 2
.

(b) (Theorem
5.2.5
) For plane curves

mult(C,o)=2-\max\{n\,|\,{\rm ker}(U:\mathbb{H}^{0}_{2n}\to\mathbb{H}^{0}_{2n-2})\not=0,\ n<0\}.

Part
(b)
is a generalization of
[
17
]
, where the identity
was proved for irreducible
plane germs.

The main theorems (Theorem
1.3.1
, Theorem
1.3.3

(a)
and

(b)
) connect three geometric properties with three different weight–zones of
\mathbb{H}^{*}
:
cohomological vanishing for
n>0
, Gorenstein property determined at weight level
n=0
and multiplicity formula
extracted from the
n<0
zone.

The main tool of the note is the
weight function
of
(C,o)
(see (
2.1.1
)).
Not only that it is closely related with the classical invariants (it can be defined either from the Hilbert function or from the semigroup of values, see subsection
2.2
),
but it is the new ingredient which leads to the definition of the lattice cohomology too.
Basically, its level sets and critical points determine a new type of geometry of the singularity. The local minima of this weight function impose all
the results of the note.
For a pictorial summary see Example
3.1.4
.

1.4.

The structure of the paper is the following. In section 2 we recall the definition of the lattice cohomology and several other invariants of curve singularities (Hilbert function associated with normalization, semigroup of values, delta invariant, conductor, multiplicity)
and we recall/establish certain properties of them. We introduce the weight function, whose level sets and critical points will play a crucial role in the subject. In section 3 we study the properties of the (generalized) local minima of the weight function and the corresponding local minimum values.
In section 4 we characterize the Gorenstein curve germs by several equivalent properties, one of them is in terms of
\mathbb{H}^{0}
. In section 5 we prove the Multiplicity Formula for any plane curve singularity.
We provide several examples showing the subtlety of the
problem in the non-planar case.
Finally, section 6 contains the proof of the contractibility of the spaces
\{S_{n}\}_{n>0}
.

2.1.
Definition of the lattice cohomology

[
4
]
Let
(C,o)
be a reduced curve singularity with irreducible decomposition
(C,o)=\bigcup_{i\in\mathcal{I}}(C_{i},o)
, where
\mathcal{I}=\{1,\dots,r\}
. The local algebra of
(C,o)
and of a branch
(C_{i},o)
will be denoted by
\mathcal{O}=\mathcal{O}_{C,o}
and
\mathcal{O}_{i}=\mathcal{O}_{C_{i},o}
respectively. Let
n_{i}:(\mathbb{C},0)\to(C_{i},o)
be the normalization map inducing the inclusion of rings
n_{i}^{*}:\mathcal{O}_{i}\hookrightarrow\overline{\mathcal{O}_{i}}=\mathbb{C}\{t_{i}\}
where
\overline{\mathcal{O}_{i}}
denotes the integral closure of
\mathcal{O}_{i}
. Denote the corresponding discrete valuations by

\mathfrak{v}_{i}:\mathcal{O}\longrightarrow\mathcal{O}_{i}\longrightarrow\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\cup\{\infty\}\ \ \ ;\ \ \ g\longmapsto\text{ord}_{t_{i}}(n_{i}^{*}g).

They induce the
\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{r}
-filtration
\mathcal{F}
on
\mathcal{O}
given by
\mathcal{F}(\ell)=\{\,g\in\mathcal{O}\ |\ \mathfrak{v}_{i}(g)\geq\ell_{i}\text{ for all }i\in\mathcal{I}\,\}
, where
\ell=(\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{r})\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{r}
. Let
\mathfrak{h}:\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{r}\to\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
denote the Hilbert function given by the (finite) codimension
\mathfrak{h}(\ell)=\dim_{\mathbb{C}}\mathcal{O}/\mathcal{F}(\ell)
(see also
[
9
]
).
By definition,
\mathfrak{h}(\ell)\geq\mathfrak{h}(\ell^{\prime})
whenever
\ell\geq\ell^{\prime}
.
Define the
weight function

w_{0}:\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{r}\to\mathbb{Z}
as

(2.1.1)

w_{0}(\ell)=2\cdot\mathfrak{h}(\ell)-|\ell|,

where
|\ell|=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\ell_{i}
. It carries the same information as
\mathfrak{h}
(cf. (
2.2.4
) and (
2.2.6
)) but via its level sets
it describes and characterizes
more naturally several geometric phenomena.

The positive orthant
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{r}
has a natural decomposition into cubes. The
set of zero-dimensional cubes consists of the lattice points

\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{r}
. Any
\ell\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{r}
and subset

I\subset\mathcal{I}
of
cardinality
q
defines a closed
q
-dimensional cube
(\ell,I)
, having as its
vertices the lattice points
\ell+\sum_{i\in K}e_{i}
, where

K
runs over all subsets of
I
, and

e_{i}=(0,\dots,0,1,0,\dots,0)\in\mathbb{Z}^{r}_{\geq 0}
denotes the
i^{\text{th}}
standard basis vector.
For closed cubes
(\ell,I)
we use the notation
\square
as well, open cubes will be denoted by
\square^{\circ}
.

We define the weight of a

q
-cube (
q\geq 0
) by

(2.1.2)

w_{q}(\square)=\max\{\,w_{0}(\ell)\ |\ \ell\in\square\cap\mathbb{Z}^{r}\,\}\ \ \ \ (\text{for any $q$-cube $\square$}),

and the ‘sublevel sets’
S_{n}
of the weight function by

S_{n}=\bigcup\{\,\square\ |\ \square\ \mbox{is a $q$-cube for any $q\geq 0$ with}\ w_{q}(\square)\leq n\,\}.

It turns out that
w_{0}
is bounded from below and
\{\ell\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{r}\ |\ w_{0}(\ell)\leq n\}

is finite for every
n\in\mathbb{Z}
. Hence
S_{n}
is
a finite closed cubical complex and it is empty for
n\ll 0
.
Moreover
S_{n}\subset S_{n+1}
.

Definition 2.1.3
.

The lattice cohomology of the reduced
curve singularity
(C,o)
is defined to be

\mathbb{H}^{*}(C,o)=\bigoplus_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}H^{*}(S_{n},\mathbb{Z}).

It is a
(\mathbb{Z}\times 2\mathbb{Z})
-graded
\mathbb{Z}[U]
-module. The first (homological) grading is
\mathbb{H}^{*}=\oplus_{q}\mathbb{H}^{q}
, while the second (weight) grading
\mathbb{H}^{q}=\oplus_{n}\mathbb{H}^{q}_{2n}
is defined as
\mathbb{H}^{q}_{2n}(C,o)=H^{q}(S_{n},\mathbb{Z})
. The
U
–action is the morphism
H^{q}(S_{n+1},\mathbb{Z})\to H^{q}(S_{n},\mathbb{Z})
induced by the inclusion. It is homogeneous of degree
(0,-2)
.

The
reduced lattice cohomology

\mathbb{H}^{*}_{\text{red}}(C,o)
is also defined by setting
\mathbb{H}^{q}_{\text{red},\,2n}(C,o)=\widetilde{H}^{q}(S_{n},\mathbb{Z})
, where
\widetilde{H}^{*}
denotes reduced singular cohomology.

Remark 2.1.4
.

(a)
We prefer the convention of using this
(\mathbb{Z}\times 2\mathbb{Z})
-grading instead of the
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
-grading in order to keep the compatibility with the Heegaard-Floer (Link) theory, see
[
23
]
.

(b) By another construction,
\mathbb{H}^{*}(C,o)
arises as the homology of a cochain complex associated with the weight function and the cubical decomposition described
above, see
[
4
]
.

(c) From the very construction it follows that
\mathbb{H}^{\geq r}(C,o)=0
.

(d) In the case of a complex
plane
curve singularity
(C,o)\subset(\mathbb{C}^{2},0)
, the Hilbert function can be recovered from the multivariate Alexander polynomial of the link
C\cap S^{3}_{\varepsilon}\subset S^{3}_{\varepsilon}

[
15
]
(see also
[
27
]
). It follows that in this planar case
\mathbb{H}^{*}(C,o)
is an embedded topological invariant.

Definition 2.1.5
.

The graded
\mathbb{Z}[U]
-module
\mathbb{H}^{0}(C,o)
has an improvement, which is also a convenient pictorial presentation.
It is the
graded root

\mathfrak{R}(C,o)
, a
\mathbb{Z}
-graded connected graph. The vertices
\mathfrak{V}
are
\mathbb{Z}
-graded,
\mathfrak{V}=\sqcup_{n}\mathfrak{V}_{n}
, the vertices
\{v_{n,i}\}_{i}\in\mathfrak{V}_{n}
correspond bijectively to the connected components
\{S_{n,i}\}_{i}
of
S_{n}
. The edges are defined as follows. If two components
S_{n,i}
and
S_{n+1,j}

of
S_{n}
and
S_{n+1}
satisfy
S_{n,i}\subset S_{n+1,j}
then we connect
v_{n,i}
and
v_{n+1,j}
by an edge. From
\mathfrak{R}(C,o)
one can read
\mathbb{H}^{0}(C,o)
: each vertex of degree
n

generates a free
\mathbb{Z}
-summand in
\mathbb{H}^{0}_{2n}=H^{0}(S_{n},\mathbb{Z})\cong\oplus_{i}H^{0}(S_{n,i},\mathbb{Z})
, and the
U
-action is marked by the edges.
For more see e.g.
[
20
,
21
,
22
]
.

2.2.
Connection to the semigroup of values

[
4
,
12
,
13
,
14
]
The
semigroup of values
of the reduced
curve singularity
(C,o)=\bigcup_{i\in\mathcal{I}}(C_{i},o)
is defined to be

(2.2.1)

\mathcal{S}_{C,o}=\big\{\,\big(\mathfrak{v}_{1}(g),\dots,\mathfrak{v}_{r}(g)\big)\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{r}\ \big|\ g\in\mathcal{O}\text{ is not a zero-divisor}\,\big\}.

The Hilbert function
\mathfrak{h}
determines the semigroup
\mathcal{S}_{C,o}
as follows:

(2.2.2)

\mathcal{S}_{C,o}=\{\,\ell\ \in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{r}\ |\ \mathfrak{h}(\ell+e_{i})>\mathfrak{h}(\ell)\text{ for all }i\in\mathcal{I}\,\}.

Definition 2.2.3
.

[
12
,
13
,
14
]

Given the semigroup of values
\mathcal{S}_{C,o}\subset\mathbb{Z}^{r}_{\geq 0}

and
\ell\in\mathbb{Z}^{r}

we set

\Delta_{i}(\ell):=\{\,s\in\mathcal{S}_{C,o}\ |\ s_{i}=\ell_{i}\text{ and }s_{j}>\ell_{j}\text{ for all }j\not=i\,\}

and
\Delta(\ell):=\bigcup_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\Delta_{i}(\ell).

Similarly, let us define the ‘closures’ of the above sets as

\overline{\Delta}_{i}(\ell):=\{\,s\in\mathcal{S}_{C,o}\ |\ s_{i}=\ell_{i}\text{ and }s_{j}\geq\ell_{j}\text{ for all }j\not=i\,\}

and
\overline{\Delta}(\ell):=\bigcup_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\overline{\Delta}_{i}(\ell).

Conversely to (
2.2.2
), the semigroup
\mathcal{S}_{C,o}

also determines the Hilbert function
\ell\mapsto\mathfrak{h}(\ell)
as follows:

(2.2.4)

\mathfrak{h}(\mathbf{0})=0\ \ \ \text{and}\ \ \ \mathfrak{h}(\ell+e_{i})-\mathfrak{h}(\ell)=\begin{cases}\ 1\ \text{ if and only if }\ \overline{\Delta}_{i}(\ell)\not=\emptyset\\
\ 0\ \text{ if and only if }\ \overline{\Delta}_{i}(\ell)=\emptyset\end{cases}

for all
i\in\mathcal{I}
and
\ell\geq\mathbf{0}
. In particular, if
(C,o)
is irreducible, then
\mathfrak{h}(\ell)=\#\{\,s<\ell\ |\ s\in\mathcal{S}_{C,o}\,\}
.

One may reformulate these facts using the weight function
w_{0}:\mathbb{Z}^{r}_{\geq 0}\to\mathbb{Z}
as follows:

Corollary 2.2.5
.

Let
w_{0}
be the weight function defined
in (
2.1.1
). Then

w_{0}(\mathbf{0})=0
and

(2.2.6)

w_{0}(\ell+e_{i})-w_{0}(\ell)=\begin{cases}\ +1\ \text{ if and only if }\ \overline{\Delta}_{i}(\ell)\not=\emptyset\\
\ -1\ \text{ if and only if }\ \overline{\Delta}_{i}(\ell)=\emptyset\end{cases}

for all
i\in\mathcal{I}
and
\ell\geq\mathbf{0}
. In particular, if
(C,o)
is an irreducible curve singularity, then

(2.2.7)

w_{0}(\ell)=\#\{\,s<\ell\ |\ s\in\mathcal{S}_{C,o}\,\}-\#\{\,0\leq k<\ell\ |\ k\not\in\mathcal{S}_{C,o}\,\}\ \ \ \mbox{ for all $\ell\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}$.}

Notation 2.2.8
.

Given two lattice points
a,b\in\mathbb{Z}^{r}
, let

R(a,b):=\{\,x\in\mathbb{R}^{r}\ |\ a_{i}\leq x_{i}\leq b_{i}\text{ for all }i\in\mathcal{I}\,\}.

Remark 2.2.9
.

Let
\mathbf{1}:=\sum_{i=1}^{r}e_{i}
. If
s\in\mathcal{S}_{C,o}\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\}
then necessarily
s\geq\mathbf{1}
(compare also with
2.8
).
Hence
\mathfrak{h}(\ell)=1
for all
\ell\in R(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{1})\cap\mathbb{Z}^{r}\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\}
.

2.3.
The conductor and the
\delta
-invariant

[
30
]
Assume that
(C,o)
is not smooth. Let
{\mathfrak{c}}=(\mathcal{O}:\overline{\mathcal{O}})
be the
conductor ideal
of the normalization
\overline{\mathcal{O}}=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{r}\mathbb{C}\{t_{i}\}
of
\mathcal{O}
, i.e. the largest (proper) ideal of
\mathcal{O}
that is also an ideal of
\overline{\mathcal{O}}
. It has the form

(t_{1}^{c_{1}},\ldots,t_{r}^{c_{r}})\overline{\mathcal{O}}
. The lattice point
\mathbf{c}=(c_{1},\ldots,c_{r})\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{r}
is called the

conductor
of
\mathcal{S}_{C,o}
. It is the smallest lattice point (with respect to the coordinate-wise partial ordering) with the property
\mathbf{c}+\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{r}\subset\mathcal{S}_{C,o}
.
In particular,
\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{c})=\mathfrak{c}
(for
\mathcal{F}
see
2.1
).

Definition 2.3.1
.

The
delta invariant
of
(C,o)
is defined as
\delta(C,o):=\dim_{\mathbb{C}}\overline{\mathcal{O}_{C,o}}/\mathcal{O}_{C,o}
.

The delta invariant is zero if and only if
(C,o)
is smooth.
It is connected to the conductor via the following facts:
\dim\overline{\mathcal{O}}/\mathfrak{c}=|\mathbf{c}|

and
\dim\mathcal{O}/\mathfrak{c}=\mathfrak{h}(\mathbf{c})
, hence

\delta(C,o)=|\mathbf{c}|-\mathfrak{h}(\mathbf{c})
.

Theorem 2.3.2
.

[
4
]
For any lattice point
\ell\geq\mathbf{c}
the inclusion
S_{n}\cap R(\mathbf{0},\ell)\hookrightarrow S_{n}
is a homotopy equivalence. In particular,
S_{n}
is empty for
n\ll 0
and contractible for
n\geq\max\{w_{0}|_{R(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{c})}\}
.

Therefore, for any lattice point
\ell\geq\mathbf{c}
there exists a canonical bigraded
\mathbb{Z}[U]
-module isomorphism between
\mathbb{H}^{*}(C,o)
and
\bigoplus_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}H^{*}\big(S_{n}\cap R(\mathbf{0},\ell),\mathbb{Z}\big)
.
(Similarly,
\mathfrak{R}(C,o)
can also be reconstructed from the connected components of

\{S_{n}\cap R(\mathbf{0},\ell)\}_{n}
via the usual construction from
2.1.5
.)

Remark 2.3.3
.

(a)
By Theorem
2.3.2

each
\mathbb{H}^{q}_{\text{red}}(C,o)
has finite
\mathbb{Z}
-rank.
Recall also that
\mathbb{H}^{q}_{\text{red}}(C,o)=0
for
q\geq r
.
This allows us to define the Euler characteristic
{\rm eu}(\mathbb{H}^{*})
of
\mathbb{H}^{*}
(see (
1.2.1
)).

(b) By
[
4
]

{\rm eu}(\mathbb{H}^{*})=\delta(C,o)
.

(c) According to Theorem
6.1.2
,
if
\mathbb{H}^{\geq 1}=0
then
{\rm eu}(\mathbb{H}^{*})=\delta
can be computed from the graded root
\mathfrak{R}
as follows (this can be useful in the examples presented below).
Delete all the vertices
v
of the root
with
w_{0}(v)>0
. Then
{\rm eu}(\mathbb{H}^{*})+1
is the number of remaining vertices.

Example 2.3.4
.

(
Decomposable germs
)
A curve singularity
(C,o)
is called ‘decomposable’ (into

(C^{\prime},o)
and
(C^{\prime\prime},o)
),
if it is isomorphic to the one-point union in

(\mathbb{C}^{n}\times\mathbb{C}^{m},(0,0))
of
(C^{\prime},o)\times\{o\}\subset(\mathbb{C}^{n},0)\times\{o\}
and

\{o\}\times(C^{\prime\prime},o)\subset\{o\}\times(\mathbb{C}^{m},0)
.
We denote this by
(C,o)=(C^{\prime},o)\vee(C^{\prime\prime},o)

[
26
]
.

Let
r^{\prime}
and
r^{\prime\prime}
be the number of
irreducible components of
(C^{\prime},o)
and
(C^{\prime\prime},o)
. Then,
by
[
23
, 6.3]
,
the semigroup of values of a decomposable germ satisfies

(2.3.5)

\mathcal{S}_{C,o}=\{(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{0})\}\cup\,\big((\mathcal{S}_{C^{\prime},o}\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\})\times(\mathcal{S}_{C^{\prime\prime},o}\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\})\big)\subset\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{r^{\prime}}\times\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{r^{\prime\prime}}=\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{r^{\prime}+r^{\prime\prime}},

hence
\mathbf{c}(C,o)=(\mathbf{c}(C^{\prime},o),\mathbf{c}(C^{\prime\prime},o))
, and

(2.3.6)

w^{C}_{0}(\ell^{\prime},\ell^{\prime\prime})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}w_{0}^{C^{\prime}}(\ell^{\prime})&\ \ \mbox{if $\ell^{\prime\prime}=0$},\\
w_{0}^{C^{\prime\prime}}(\ell^{\prime\prime})&\ \ \mbox{if $\ell^{\prime}=0$},\\
w_{0}^{C^{\prime}}(\ell^{\prime})+w_{0}^{C^{\prime\prime}}(\ell^{\prime\prime})-2&\ \ \mbox{if $\ell^{\prime}>0$ and $\ell^{\prime\prime}>0$}.\end{array}\right.

2.4.
Increasing paths and a new characterization of the conductor

Definition 2.4.1
.

An
increasing path
starting from
a\in\mathbb{Z}^{r}_{\geq 0}

is a sequence of lattice points
\gamma=\{x^{k}\}_{k=0}^{t}
such that

x^{0}=a
,
x^{k+1}=x^{k}+e_{i(k)}
for some index
i(k)\in\mathcal{I}
.
If
t
is finite then we say that the path connects
a
with
x^{t}
(or it is an increasing path from
a
to
x^{t}
).
If
t
is infinite then we require for the infinite increasing path
to be unbounded in every coordinate.

The next Lemma is the converse of the obvious fact that for any
a\geq\mathbf{c}

there exists an infinite increasing path starting from
a
and having all its elements in
\mathcal{S}_{C,o}
.

Lemma 2.4.2
.

Assume that there exists an infinite increasing path

\{x^{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
such that

x^{k}\in\mathcal{S}_{C,o}
for every
k\geq 0
. Then
x^{0}\geq\mathbf{c}
.
In particular,
the conductor
\mathbf{c}
is the smallest lattice point such that for any
a\geq\mathbf{c}
there exists an infinite increasing path
\gamma\subset\mathcal{S}_{C,o}
starting at
a
.

Proof.

Fix an arbitrary lattice point
\ell\geq x^{0}
. We want to prove that
\ell
is a semigroup element by checking that
w_{0}(\ell+e_{i})>w_{0}(\ell)
for any
i\in\mathcal{I}
(cf. (
2.2.2
)). For any fixed coordinate
i
we can choose a finite increasing path

\{y^{k}\}_{k=0}^{t}
(where
t
is sufficiently large depending on
\ell
and
i
)
such that
y^{0}=x^{0}
,
y^{t}=x^{t}
, and for some index
0\leq j<t
we have
y^{j}=\ell
and
y^{j+1}=\ell+e_{i}
(in fact,
j=|\ell-x^{0}|
and then
i(j)=i
).
Since
w_{0}
is increasing along
\{x^{k}\}_{k=0}^{t}
(cf. (
2.2.6
)),

w_{0}(y^{t})-w_{0}(y^{0})=w_{0}(x^{t})-w_{0}(x^{0})=t
. Moreover, since
\{y^{k}\}_{k=0}^{t}
has length
t
too,
w_{0}
should be increasing along it as well, hence
w_{0}(\ell+e_{i})>w_{0}(\ell)
.
Thus, we got that for any
\ell\geq x^{0}
we have
\ell\in S_{C,o}
and hence
x^{0}\geq\mathbf{c}
.
∎

2.5.
Stability properties

[
4
]

The fact, that the Hilbert function
\mathfrak{h}
is defined by a valuative filtration
\mathcal{F}
(cf. subsection
2.1
),
implies the
‘matroid rank inequality’
:

(2.5.1)

\mathfrak{h}(\ell_{1})+\mathfrak{h}(\ell_{2})\geq\mathfrak{h}\big(\min\{\ell_{1},\ell_{2}\}\big)+\mathfrak{h}\big(\max\{\ell_{1},\ell_{2}\}\big)

for all lattice points
\ell_{1},\ell_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}^{r}_{\geq 0}
. This can be translated to a
‘stability property’
of
\mathfrak{h}
, which in terms of the
weight function
w_{0}:\mathbb{Z}^{r}_{\geq 0}\to\mathbb{Z}
reads as follows:

Corollary 2.5.2
.

For any
\ell,\bar{\ell}\in\mathbb{Z}^{r}_{\geq 0}
such that
\bar{\ell}\geq 0
and
i\not\in{\rm supp}\big(\bar{\ell}\big):=\{j\ |\ \bar{\ell}_{j}\not=0\}
(i.e.
\bar{\ell}_{i}=0
)

(2.5.3)

w_{0}(\ell+e_{i})=w_{0}(\ell)-1\ \ \Rightarrow\ \ w_{0}(\ell+\bar{\ell}+e_{i})=w_{0}(\ell+\bar{\ell})-1.

Equivalently,

(2.5.4)

w_{0}(\ell+e_{i})=w_{0}(\ell)+1\ \ \Rightarrow\ \ w_{0}(\ell-\bar{\ell}+e_{i})=w_{0}(\ell-\bar{\ell})+1.

2.6.
Restricting the weight function

Let
(C,o)\subset(\mathbb{C}^{N},0)
denote a reduced curve singularity with irreducible branches
(C_{i},o)
for
i\in\mathcal{I}=\{1,\dots,r\}
. Given any subset
I\subseteq\mathcal{I}
denote by
(C_{I},o)
the singularity having irreducible branches
(C_{i},o)
with
i\in I
. Let us denote the weight function corresponding to this singularity by
w_{0}^{C_{I}}:\mathbb{Z}^{\#I}_{\geq 0}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}
.
\mathbb{Z}^{\#I}
is a sublattice of
\mathbb{Z}^{r}
in a natural way.

The following lemma is well-known (it follows from the definitions,
or one can use (
2.2.4
) as well).

Proposition 2.6.1
.

Let
J\subset I\subset\mathcal{I}
be two subsets and
\ell=\sum_{j\in J}\ell_{j}e_{j}\in\mathbb{Z}^{r}_{\geq 0}
a lattice point with support in
J
(i.e.
\ell_{j}=0
if
j\not\in J
).
Then
\mathfrak{h}^{C_{I}}(\ell)=\mathfrak{h}^{C_{J}}(\ell)
, hence
w_{0}^{C_{I}}(\ell)=w_{0}^{C_{J}}(\ell)
as well.

2.7.
The multiplicity of
(C,o)

Let
(C,o)
be a reduced curve singularity as above. Let
\mathfrak{m}_{C,o}
denote
the maximal ideal of
\mathcal{O}_{C,o}
. The multiplicity
m=m(C,o)
is defined as the degree
of a generic linear projection
L:(C,o)\to(\mathbb{C},0)
(i.e.

L\in\mathfrak{m}_{C,o}
is generic).
In particular,
m=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}m_{i}
, where
m_{i}=m(C_{i},o)
.
Equivalently, if
\dim_{\mathbb{C}}(\mathcal{O}_{C,o}/\mathfrak{m}_{C,o}^{k})=ak+b
(
k\gg 0
)
is the Hilbert–Samuel polynomial of
(C,o)
, then
m(C,o)
is the coefficient
a
of
k
.

Set the ‘multiplicity vector’

\mathbf{m}=(m_{1},\dots,m_{r})\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{r}.

It is the image of a generic linear function under the valuation map
(\mathfrak{v}_{1},\dots,\mathfrak{v}_{r}):\mathcal{O}_{C,o}\to(\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\cup\infty)^{r}
and hence the smallest non-zero element of the semigroup
\mathcal{S}_{C,o}
.
This fact (together with properties (1)-(2) from subsection
2.8
)
shows that
\mathcal{S}_{C,o}\setminus R(\mathbf{m},\infty)=\{\mathbf{0}\}
. Thus (using Corollary
2.2.5
) we have

(2.7.1)

w_{0}(\mathbf{m})=2-m(C,o).

Clearly,
m=1
if and only if
(C,o)
is smooth, and this happens exactly when

\mathbb{H}^{*}_{\text{red}}(C,o)=0
, see e.g.
[
4
, Example 4.6.1]
.
The case
m=2
can be classified as well.
By Abhyankar’s inequality
[
1
]

(2.7.2)

\dim(\mathfrak{m}_{C,o}/\mathfrak{m}_{C,o}^{2})=\text{ embdim}(C,o)\leq m(C,o).

In particular, if
m(C,o)=2
, then the embedding dimension
\text{embdim}(C,o)

is necessarily 2, hence
(C,o)
is a plane curve singularity with multiplicity 2, i.e. in some local coordinates it has equation
x^{2}+y^{k}
,
k\geq 2
, whose weight function
w_{0}
is easily computable and it satisfies
w_{0}\geq 0
.

These facts together with (
2.7.1
) imply the following

Proposition 2.7.3
.

For a reduced curve singularity the following facts hold:

(a)
m(C,o)\leq 2
if and only if

\mathbb{H}^{*}_{2n}(C,o)=0
for all
n<0
(i.e.

w_{0}(\ell)\geq 0
for all
\ell\in\mathbb{Z}^{r}_{\geq 0}
),

(b)

m(C,o)=1
if and only if

\mathbb{H}^{0}_{\text{red}}(C,o)=0
,

(c)

m(C,o)=2
if and only if

\mathbb{H}^{0}_{\text{red}}(C,o)\not=0
, but
\mathbb{H}^{0}_{2n}(C,o)=0
for all
n<0
.

In all these cases (by a direct computation)
\mathbb{H}^{>0}(C,o)=0.

This already shows that the graded module
\mathbb{H}^{0}(C,o)
determines whether
m(C,o)
is
1
,
2
, or
\geq 3
.

Remark 2.7.4
.

It is natural to ask for the characterization of all
those germs with
\mathbb{H}^{0}_{{\rm red}}\not=0
, but with simplest possible
\mathbb{H}^{0}_{{\rm red}}
.

If
\mathbb{H}^{0}_{{\rm red}}\not=0
then
S_{0}
has at least two connected components, one of them
consists of the single lattice point
\mathbf{0}
, an other one contains
\mathbf{1}
.
This follows from the structure of
\mathfrak{h}
-values in
R(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{1})
, cf. Remark
2.2.9
.
In particular, the simplest case when
\mathbb{H}^{0}_{{\rm red}}\not=0
is

\mathbb{H}^{0}_{{\rm red}}=\mathbb{H}^{0}_{{\rm red},0}\simeq\mathbb{Z}
,
\mathbb{H}^{\geq 1}=0
.
These conditions can also be characterized either by
the identity
{\mathbf{c}}={\mathbf{m}}
, or, equivalently, in terms of the semigroup, by

\mathcal{S}_{C,o}=\{\mathbf{0}\}\cup(\mathbf{c}+\mathbb{Z}^{r}_{\geq 0})
(See Example
3.2.3
).
A non-smooth ordinary

r
-tuple (
[
26
,
7
]
)
is a particular case satisfying additionally
{\bf c}={\bf m}={\bf 1}
and
r=2-\min\{w_{0}\}
.

2.8.
Good semigroups

(a) Recall that the (sub)semigroup of values
\mathcal{S}_{C,o}\subset\mathbb{Z}^{r}_{\geq 0}

has the special property that it admits a conductor: there exists

\mathbf{c}\in\mathbb{Z}^{r}_{\geq 0}
such that
\mathbf{c}+\mathbb{Z}^{r}_{\geq 0}\subset\mathcal{S}_{C,o}
.

If
r=1
, then any numerical semigroup
\mathcal{S}\subset\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
(with
0\in\mathcal{S}
)
which admits a conductor can be realized as
\mathcal{S}_{C,o}
for
a certain germ
(C,o)
: take e.g.

(C,o)=({\rm Spec}\,\mathbb{C}[\mathcal{S}],0)
.

However, a similar statement does not hold for any subsemigroup of
\mathbb{Z}^{r}_{\geq 0}
with
r>1
, even the uniqueness of
\mathbf{c}
might fail (and the
\mathbb{C}
-algebra

\mathbb{C}[\mathcal{S}]
might not be finitely generated). In the
r>1
case the literature
lists several additional properties which are satisfied by any semigroup of values
\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{S}_{C,o}
(the validity of the next properties (1)-(4)
is rather straightforward, see e.g.
[
12
, p. 350]
):

(1)
\mathbf{0}\in\mathcal{S}
, moreover,
if
s\in\mathcal{S}
and
s_{i}=0
for some
i
then
s=\mathbf{0}
;

(2) if
s^{\prime},\,s^{\prime\prime}\in\mathcal{S}
then
\min\{s^{\prime},s^{\prime\prime}\}\in\mathcal{S}
;

(3) if
s^{\prime},\,s^{\prime\prime}\in\mathcal{S}
and
s^{\prime}_{i}=s^{\prime\prime}_{i}
for some
i
, then there exists

t\in\mathcal{S}
,
t\geq\min\{s^{\prime},s^{\prime\prime}\}
such that
t_{i}>s_{i}^{\prime}=s^{\prime\prime}_{i}
and

t_{j}=\min\{s^{\prime}_{j},s^{\prime\prime}_{j}\}
if
j\not=i
and
s^{\prime}_{j}\not=s^{\prime\prime}_{j}
;

(4)
\mathcal{S}
admits a conductor (via (2), in fact, there exists a unique minimal

\mathbf{c}
with
\mathbf{c}+\mathbb{Z}^{r}_{\geq 0}\subset\mathcal{S}_{C,o}
).

A subsemigroup
\mathcal{S}\subset\mathbb{Z}^{r}_{\geq 0}
with these properties is called

good (local)
, see e.g.
[
5
,
11
]
.

(b) Not any good semigroup can be realized as a semigroup of values of a curve germ.
For a concrete example see
[
5
, Example 2.16]
.

(c) Usually when we study properties of curve germs starting from their semigroups, we basically prove statements for good semigroups (that is, we use properties (1)-(4), but usually not more).

(d) Having in mind the semigroup
\leftrightarrow
Hilbert function correspondence from
subsection
2.2
, valid for curve germs, one can try to define a ‘combinatorial Hilbert function’ starting from any semigroup
\mathcal{S}\subset\mathbb{Z}^{r}_{\geq 0}
analogously to Definition
2.2.3
and (
2.2.4
). However, in the case of arbitrary
\mathcal{S}
this procedure will not provide a well-defined
\mathfrak{h}
–function. On the other hand, if we start with a
good
semigroup
then this procedure does produce a well-defined
\mathfrak{h}
–function (in fact, this is equivalent to property (3) above),
which even satisfies the matroid rank inequality (
2.5.1
)
(this is an exercise left to the reader). In particular,
it provides a weight function
w_{0}
(via (
2.1.1
)), hence a lattice cohomology as well. Basically,
most of our theorems (and several theorems of the literature about curve germs)
are valid in this general context.
In this note the following results remain valid if we state them in the context of
(the lattice cohomology associated with) a good semigroup:
Theorem
2.3.2
, Lemma
2.4.2
,
properties of the local minima of the weight function from section 3, the combinatorial parts

(3)
\Leftrightarrow
(4)
\Leftrightarrow
(5)
of Theorem
4.1.1
, the characterization of these properties via

\mathbb{H}^{*}
(as in Theorem
4.2.2
), or the Nonpositivity theorem

6.1.1
.

(e) Let us exemplify statement (c):
from the properties (1)-(4) of a good semigroup
we deduce another one, which will be applied later for semigroups of values:

(5)
\Delta(\mathbf{c}-\mathbf{1})=\emptyset
.

This identity for the semigroups of plane curve singularities
was proved in
[
12
, (2.7)]
. Here we provide a short proof for any good semigroup based on the ‘path technique’ of Lemma
2.4.2
.

Let us suppose indirectly that
\Delta(\mathbf{c}-\mathbf{1})\neq\emptyset
, i.e. there exists an index
i\in\mathcal{I}
and a semigroup element
s\in\Delta_{i}(\mathbf{c}-\mathbf{1})
. This means that
s_{i}=c_{i}-1
and
s_{j}\geq c_{j}
for all
j\neq i
. By property (2)
applied to
s
and
\mathbf{c}
we get

\mathbf{c}-e_{i}\in\mathcal{S}
.
Now, we can construct an infinite increasing path
\mathbf{c}-e_{i},\mathbf{c},\ldots

starting at

\mathbf{c}-e_{i}
, with all elements in
\mathcal{S}
.
Then Lemma
2.4.2
reads as
\mathbf{c}-e_{i}\geq\mathbf{c}
, a contradiction.

3.
Local minima of the weight function

The semigroup of values is a rather complex invariant of curve germs; for several
properties see e.g.
[
12
,
13
]
.
It is hard to extract conceptual geometric information from its combinatorics. Therefore,
it is very desirable to introduce some other invariants, which might help to
understand the complexity of the semigroup.
The novelty of the present note is that it establishes several new properties
via the level sets of the weight function
w_{0}
.
In this way, even the older results of the literature related with the semigroup of values might
get new deep geometric reinterpretations in terms of the weight function and
lattice cohomology. From the technical point of view, these connections are
provided by the local minimum points of the weight function and their
w_{0}
-values.

3.1.
Local minimum points

[
17
]

In this subsection we define the local minimum points of the weight function and collect several correspondences between them and the bigraded lattice cohomology
\mathbb{Z}[U]
-module (and the respective graded root).

Let
(C,o)=\bigcup_{i}(C_{i},o)
be a reduced curve singularity with semigroup of values
\mathcal{S}_{C,o}\subset\mathbb{Z}^{r}_{\geq 0}
and weight function
w_{0}:\mathbb{Z}^{r}_{\geq 0}\to\mathbb{Z}
.
Sometimes we will shorten
w_{0}
into
w
.

Definition 3.1.1
.

A lattice point
p\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{r}
is said to be a
local minimum point
of the weight function
w
if
w(p)<w(p+e_{i})
for all
i\in\mathcal{I}

and
w(p)<w(p-e_{i})
for all
i\in\mathcal{I}
with
p\geq e_{i}
.
All local minimum points are semigroup elements
(cf. (
2.2.2
), (
2.2.4
) and (
2.2.6
)). In particular, if
p
is a local minimum point, then either
p={\mathbf{0}}
or
p\geq{\mathbf{1}}
.

The weight
w(p)
of a local minimum point
p
is said to be a
local minimum value
.

Remark 3.1.2
.

The extension of
w_{0}
to higher dimensional cubes (cf. (
2.1.2
)) assures, that
p
is a local minimum point if and only if
w_{0}(p)<w_{q}(\square_{q})
for every
q
-cube
\square_{q}
strictly containing
p
as one of its vertices. Also, by (
2.2.6
), the property
w(p)<w(p\pm e_{i})
is equivalent to

w(p)\leq w(p\pm e_{i})
.

Let us compare this notion with the local minimum points of the corresponding graded root.

Definition 3.1.3
.

v\in\mathfrak{V}
is a
local minimum point
of the
graded root
\mathfrak{R}(C,o)
if all of its neighbours (adjacent vertices) have higher
n
-degree. By the formal definition of graded roots (see
[
19
, Definition 3.2]
) this condition is equivalent to
v
having a single neighbour (i.e.
v
is a
leaf
of the tree
\mathfrak{R}(C,o)
), or,
v
having no neighbour with lower degree.

Notice that, by its definition, a local minimum point
p\in\mathbb{Z}^{r}_{\geq 0}
of the weight function
w
with
w(p)=n
gives a distinct connected component
\{p\}
of
S_{n}
. Also,
\{p\}\cap S_{n-1}=\emptyset
, hence the vertex corresponding to this connected component is a local minimum point with degree
n
of the graded root
\mathfrak{R}(C,o)
. By (
2.2.6
), all local minimum points of
\mathfrak{R}(C,o)
arise in this way. Thus we have the following correspondence:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{c}\text{local minimum points of }w\\
\text{ with weight }w=n\end{array}\right\}\overset{1-1}{\longleftrightarrow}\left\{\begin{array}[]{c}\text{local minimum points of }\mathfrak{R}(C,o)\\
\text{ with degree }n\end{array}\right\}.

Turning to the lattice cohomology
\mathbb{Z}[U]
-module itself, notice that on the right hand side of this commutative diagram the direct summands corresponding to the connected components consisting of a single local minimum point span exactly the kernel of the morphism. Hence
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathbb{H}^{0}_{2n}=H^{0}(S_{n})}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\oplus_{i}H^{0}(S_{n,i})}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathbb{H}^{0}_{2n-2}=H^{0}(S_{n-1})}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\oplus_{j}H^{0}(S_{n-1,j})}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\cong}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces U}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\cong}

\#\left\{\begin{array}[]{c}\text{loc. min. pts of }w\\
\text{ with weight }w=n\end{array}\right\}=\#\left\{\begin{array}[]{c}\text{loc. min. pts of }\mathfrak{R}\\
\text{ with degree }n\end{array}\right\}=\text{rank}_{\mathbb{Z}}\ker(U:\mathbb{H}^{0}_{2n}\to\mathbb{H}^{0}_{2n-2}),

i.e. we can recover the number of local minimum points with a given weight from the graded
\mathbb{Z}[U]
-module structure of
\mathbb{H}^{0}(C,o)
.

The above correspondence
manifests itself in the three theorems
presented in the paper in the following way. The Nonpositivity Theorem
6.1.1
regarding the
weights of the reduced cohomology follows from the fact that all local minimum points have nonpositive weight. The behaviour of the local minimum points of weight
0
allows for the lattice cohomology to detect whether or not the curve singularity is Gorenstein (cf. Theorem
4.2.1
). Finally, in the
plane curve singularity
case, the fact that the ‘multiplicity vector’
has the largest weight among the non-zero local minimum values enables one to read off the multiplicity from the lattice cohomology module (cf. Theorem
5.2.5
).

3.2.
Generalized local minima

It turns out that in order to understand the local minima of the weight function, it is convenient to use a slightly more general notion, namely that of
generalized local minima
. In this subsection we give a detailed discussion of this notion. It forms the technical backbone of this paper and will be used in the proofs of all three main results.

Definition 3.2.1
.

A lattice point
p\in\mathbb{Z}^{r}_{\geq 0}
satisfying the condition

w(p)<w(p-e_{i})
for any
i\in\mathcal{I}
with
p\geq e_{i}

is said to be a
generalized local minimum point
of the weight function.

Lemma 3.2.2
.

Let
p>{\mathbf{0}}
be a nonzero lattice point.
Then (for notation see Definition
2.2.3
)

(a)
p
is a generalized local minimum point
if and only if
\Delta(p-\mathbf{1})=\emptyset
;

(b)
p
is a local minimum point if and only if

p\in\mathcal{S}_{C,o}
and
\Delta(p-\mathbf{1})=\emptyset
.

Proof.

(a)

By (
2.2.6
), the property
w(p-e_{i})>w(p)
, for a certain
i\in\mathcal{I}
, is equivalent to
\overline{\Delta}_{i}(p-e_{i})=\emptyset
. But
\overline{\Delta}_{i}(p-e_{i})=\Delta_{i}(p-\mathbf{1})

and

\Delta(p-\mathbf{1})=\bigcup_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\Delta_{i}(p-\mathbf{1})
.

(b)
Use
(a)
and the fact that

w(p+e_{i})>w(p)
for all
i
is equivalent to
p\in\mathcal{S}_{C,o}
, cf. (
2.2.2
).
∎

Example 3.2.3
.

There exist three ‘canonical’ local minimum points, namely
\mathbf{0}
,

\mathbf{m}
and

\mathbf{c}
.

For
r=1
the fact that
\mathbf{c}
is a local minimum point follows directly from the definition. For
r\geq 2
use (5) from subsection
2.8
.

If
\mathbf{0}
,
\mathbf{m}
and
\mathbf{c}
are all distinct then
the number of the minimal
\mathbb{Z}[U]
-module generators of

\mathbb{H}^{0}_{{\rm red}}
is at least two. On the other hand,

\mathbf{m}=\mathbf{c}
is equivalent with
{\rm rank}_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{H}^{0}_{{\rm red}}=1

(and also with
{\rm rank}_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{H}^{*}_{{\rm red}}=1
). (Compare with Remark
2.7.4
.)

The very existence of the conductor
\mathbf{c}

of the semigroup of values guarantees that there are only finitely many generalized local minimum points. Indeed, Lemma
3.2.2
implies the following.

Corollary 3.2.4
.

If
p
is a generalized local minimum point then
\mathbf{0}\leq p\leq\mathbf{c}
.

Lemma 3.2.5
.

Let
p\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{r}
be a generalized local minimum point. Then the two statements

\ell\in\mathcal{S}_{C,o}\ \ \ \ \text{ and }\ \ \ \ \Delta(p-\mathbf{1}-\ell)\not=\emptyset

cannot be true at the same time for any lattice point
\ell\in\mathbb{Z}^{r}
.

Proof.

Assume indirectly that
\ell\in\mathcal{S}_{C,o}
and that there exists an element
\ell^{\prime}\in\Delta(p-\mathbf{1}-\ell)
. Then
\ell+\ell^{\prime}\in\Delta(p-\mathbf{1})
, a fact which contradicts Lemma
3.2.2
(a)
.
∎

Corollary 3.2.6
.

Let
p\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{r}
be a generalized local minimum point and
i\in\mathcal{I}
. Then

(3.2.7)

w(\ell)<w(\ell+e_{i})\ \ \text{ implies }\ \ w(p-e_{i}-\ell)>w(p-\ell)

for any lattice point
\ell\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{r}
satisfying
\ell+e_{i}\leq p
.

Proof.

The inequality
w(\ell)<w(\ell+e_{i})
is equivalent to the existence of an element
\ell^{\prime}\in\overline{\Delta}_{i}(\ell)
, see (
2.2.6
). Since
\ell^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}_{C,o}
,
by Lemma
3.2.5
it follows that

\Delta(p-\mathbf{1}-\ell^{\prime})=\emptyset
. But
\Delta(p-\mathbf{1}-\ell^{\prime})\supseteq\Delta_{i}(p-\mathbf{1}-\ell^{\prime})\supseteq\overline{\Delta}_{i}(p-e_{i}-\ell)
, so
\overline{\Delta}_{i}(p-e_{i}-\ell)=\emptyset
, which implies

w(p-e_{i}-\ell)>w(p-\ell)
.
∎

Proposition 3.2.8
.

Let
p\in\mathbb{Z}^{r}_{\geq 0}
be a generalized local minimum and let

\gamma=\{x^{k}\}_{k=0}^{t}
be an increasing path from
\mathbf{0}
to
p
. Then

(3.2.9)

w(p)=-\#\{x^{k}\,|\,w(x^{k+1})=w(x^{k})-1\text{ and }w(p-x^{k})=w(p-x^{k+1})-1\text{ with }0\leq k<t\}.

In particular, the right hand side is independent of the increasing path and
w(p)\leq 0
for any generalized local minimum
p
.

Proof.

Let us choose an increasing path
\gamma=\{x^{k}\}_{k=0}^{t}
from
\mathbf{0}
to
p
and define the dual path
\overline{\gamma}=\{\overline{x}^{k}\}_{k=0}^{t}
given by
\overline{x}^{k}:=p-x^{t-k}
. Clearly, this is also an increasing path from
\overline{x}^{0}=p-x^{t}=\mathbf{0}
to
\overline{x}^{t}=p-x^{0}=p
. By telescoping summation we have

\displaystyle w(p)=w(p)-w(\mathbf{0})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\bigg(\sum_{k=0}^{t-1}\big(w(x^{k+1})-w(x^{k})\big)+\sum_{k=0}^{t-1}\big(w(\overline{x}^{t-k})-w(\overline{x}^{t-k-1})\big)\bigg).

By Lemma
3.2.6
, an increase in
w
along one of the two paths implies a decrease in
w
along the other one, whence the total sum in the right hand side (hence
w(p)
too) cannot be positive,
and it equals
(-1)
-times the number of symmetric pairs of edges
((x^{k},x^{k+1}),(\overline{x}^{t-k-1},\overline{x}^{t-k}))
with decreasing
w
along both.
∎

Theorem 3.2.10
.

(a) If
p\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{r}
is a generalized local minimum point with weight
w(p)=0
, then

(3.2.11)

w(p-\ell)=w(\ell)\hbox{ for any lattice point }\ell\in R(\mathbf{0},p)\cap\mathbb{Z}^{r},

i.e.
the weight function restricted to the rectangle
R(\mathbf{0},p)
is symmetric. Furthermore if
p\geq\mathbf{m}
then
p
is in fact a local minimum point (i.e.
p\in\mathcal{S}_{C,o}
).

(b) Assume that
m(C,o)\geq 3
.
If
p
is a local minimum point with weight
w(p)=0
, then
p\in\{\mathbf{0},\mathbf{c}\}
.

Proof.

(a)
First we verify that
the converse implication of the statement (
3.2.7
) also holds.

Consider an increasing path
\gamma=\{x^{k}\}_{k=0}^{t}\subset R(\mathbf{0},p)
with
x^{0}=\mathbf{0}
and
x^{t}=p
. Then using (
3.2.9
) and the vanishing
w(p)=0

we obtain the following identity for any
0\leq k<t
:

w(p-x^{k})-w(p-x^{k+1})=-\big(w(x^{k+1})-w(x^{k})\big).

However, for any pair of lattice points
\ell,\ell+e_{i}\in R(\mathbf{0},p)\cap\mathbb{Z}^{r}
we can choose an increasing path
\gamma
from
\mathbf{0}
to
p

containing both
\ell
and
\ell+e_{i}
, and thus

(3.2.12)

w(p-\ell-e_{i})-w(p-\ell)=w(\ell+e_{i})-w(\ell)\ \hbox{ for any }\ \ell,\ell+e_{i}\in R(\mathbf{0},p)\cap\mathbb{Z}^{r}.

Next, we prove the symmetry (
3.2.11
)
by induction on
|\ell|=\sum_{i}\ell_{i}
for the lattice points

\ell\in R(\mathbf{0},p)\cap\mathbb{Z}^{r}
. The starting case of the induction
(
|\ell|=0
)
is just the assumption:
w(p)=0=w(\mathbf{0})
.

Suppose we have
w(p-\ell)=w(\ell)
for every lattice point
\ell\in R(\mathbf{0},p)\cap\mathbb{Z}^{r}
with
|\ell|\leq j
. Now, any lattice point
\ell^{\prime}\in R(\mathbf{0},p)\cap\mathbb{Z}^{r}
with
|\ell^{\prime}|=j+1
can be written as
\ell+e_{i}
for some
\ell\in R(\mathbf{0},p)\cap\mathbb{Z}^{r}
with
|\ell|=j
. Then
w(p-\ell)=w(\ell)
and by (
3.2.12
) we get

w(p-\ell-e_{i})=w(\ell+e_{i})
.

Finally, we show that
p\in\mathcal{S}_{C,o}
if
p\geq\mathbf{m}
. Using the symmetry of
w
established above,
\mathbf{m}
being a local minimum point implies that
p-\mathbf{m}
is a local minimum point as well. In particular,
p-\mathbf{m}\in\mathcal{S}_{C,o}
, whence
p=(p-\mathbf{m})+\mathbf{m}\in\mathcal{S}_{C,o}
.

(b)
Assume that
p\geq\mathbf{m}
.
Since
p
is a generalized local minimum point with weight
w(p)=0
,
by part
(a)
of this Theorem
3.2.10
, the weight function is symmetric in the rectangle
R(\mathbf{0},p)\cap\mathbb{Z}^{r}
, i.e.
w(\ell)=w(p-\ell)
for all
\ell\in R(\mathbf{0},p)\cap\mathbb{Z}^{r}
.

As
\mathcal{S}_{C,o}\cap\big(R(\mathbf{0},p)\setminus R(\mathbf{m},p)\big)=\{\mathbf{0}\}
(see subsection
2.7
), we have that
w(\mathbf{0})=0
and
w(\ell)=2-|\ell|
for any
\ell\in R(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{m})\cap\mathbb{Z}^{r}\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\}
. Now, using the symmetry of
w

on
R(\mathbf{0},p)

and the fact that
p\in\mathcal{S}_{C,o}
, then via (
2.2.2
) and property (2) of subsection
2.8
we get

\mathcal{S}_{C,o}\cap R(p-\mathbf{m},p)=R(p-\mathbf{m},p)\cap\mathbb{Z}^{r}\setminus\{p-e_{i},\dots,p-e_{r}\}.

By adding the multiplicity vector
\mathbf{m}\in\mathcal{S}_{C,o}
to every element of this set, we get that

R(p,p+\mathbf{m})\cap\mathbb{Z}^{r}\setminus\{p+\mathbf{m}-e_{1},\dots,p+\mathbf{m}-e_{r}\}\subseteq\mathcal{S}_{C,o}.

Notice that
p+\mathbf{m}-e_{i}\in\mathcal{S}_{C,o}
for at least one
i\in\mathcal{I}
: if this was not the case, then by property (2) of subsection
2.8
and Lemma
3.2.2
,
p+\mathbf{m}
would be a local minimum point with
w(p+\mathbf{m})=m(C,o)-2\geq 1
contradicting Proposition
3.2.8
. (This is where we use the assumption
m(C,o)\geq 3
.)
This ‘new’ semigroup element yields an increasing path
\gamma=\{x^{k}\}_{k=0}^{m}
from
p
to
p+\mathbf{m}
with
x^{m-1}=p+\mathbf{m}-e_{i}
consisting of semigroup elements only. It follows that

\bigcup_{k=0}^{\infty}(k\cdot\mathbf{m}+\gamma)

is an infinite increasing path starting at the point
p
and consisting of only semigroup elements.
Then
p\geq\mathbf{c}
by Lemma
2.4.2
. But by Corollary
3.2.4

p\leq\mathbf{c}
too.
∎

Example 3.2.13
.

If

4.
First application. The Gorenstein property

4.1.
Gorenstein symmetry of the weight function

In this subsection we prove two characterizations for Gorenstein curve singularities.
The first one, Theorem
4.1.1
, shows the relevance of the weight function
w
and its strong connection with the semigroup of values and the Hilbert function. The equivalences
(1)

\Leftrightarrow

(2)

\Leftrightarrow

(5)
are known (see references in the proof), parts

(3)
–
(4)
are new. They formulate the Gorenstein property
in a natural and useful way in terms of
w
.

Theorem 4.1.1
.

Let
(C,o)
be a curve singularity. Then the following conditions are equivalent:

(1)

(C,o)
is Gorenstein, i.e. the local ring
\mathcal{O}_{C,o}
is Gorenstein;

(2)

\dim_{\mathbb{C}}(\overline{\mathcal{O}}/\mathcal{O})=\dim_{\mathbb{C}}(\mathcal{O}/\mathfrak{c})
;

(3)

w(\mathbf{c})=w(\mathbf{0})=0
;

(4)

w
is ‘Gorenstein symmetric’, i.e.
w(\mathbf{c}-\ell)=w(\ell)
for any lattice point
\ell\in R(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{c})\cap\mathbb{Z}^{r}
;

(5)

the semigroup of values
\mathcal{S}_{C,o}
is symmetric (in the sense of Garcia-Delgado), i.e.

\ell\in\mathcal{S}_{C,o}\ \Leftrightarrow\ \Delta(\mathbf{c}-{\bf 1}-\ell)=\emptyset.

Specifically, for irreducible curves the semigroup
\mathcal{S}_{C,o}
is symmetric if and only if

\ell\in\mathcal{S}_{C,o}\ \Leftrightarrow\ \mathbf{c}-1-\ell\notin\mathcal{S}_{C,o}.

In particular, if
(C,o)
is Gorenstein, then by (4) the involution

\ell\leftrightarrow\mathbf{c}-\ell
induces a
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
–symmetry of the graded root and of the lattice cohomology
\mathbb{H}^{*}
as well.

Proof.

By Serre
[
25
]
or Bass
[
6
]
(see also
[
16
]
)
(C,o)
is Gorenstein if and only if

\dim(\overline{\mathcal{O}}/\mathcal{O})=\dim(\mathcal{O}/\mathfrak{c})
, i.e.
(1)

\Leftrightarrow

(2)
. Also,
(2)

\Leftrightarrow

(3)
follows as (see also subsection
2.3
)

\displaystyle\dim(\overline{\mathcal{O}}/\mathcal{O})=\dim(\mathcal{O}/\mathfrak{c})

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\dim(\overline{\mathcal{O}}/\mathfrak{c})=2\dim(\mathcal{O}/\mathfrak{c})

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow|\mathbf{c}|=2\mathfrak{h}(\mathbf{c})

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow w(\mathbf{c})=0.

The equivalence
(2)

\Leftrightarrow

(5)
was proved by Kunz (
r=1
)

[
18
]
,
Garcia and Waldi
[
28
,
14
]
(
r=2
) and Delgado (
r\geq 2
)
[
13
]
.
See also
[
10
]
.

The
(4)

\Rightarrow

(3)
implication is trivial,

(3)

\Rightarrow

(4)

is just Theorem
3.2.10
(a)
(via Example
3.2.3
).

This basically ends the proof of the theorem. However,
we give below two direct and short proofs for the
(4)

\Rightarrow

(5)

and
(5)

\Rightarrow

(3)
implications based on
the tool of increasing paths and the
correspondence between the weight function
w
and the semigroup
\mathcal{S}_{C,o}
.
(In this way we provide a self-contained proof for

(2)

\Leftrightarrow

(3)

\Leftrightarrow

(4)

\Leftrightarrow

(5)
replacing the proofs from

[
18
,
28
,
14
,
13
]
.
The implication
(1)

\Rightarrow

(4)
was proved in
[
4
]
as well.)

(4)

\Rightarrow

(5)

By (
2.2.2
) and (
2.2.6
)
\ell
is a semigroup element if and only if
w(\ell+e_{i})>w(\ell)
for every
i\in\mathcal{I}
. But, by the
\ell\leftrightarrow\mathbf{c}-\ell
symmetry of the weight function, this is equivalent to the fact that
\mathbf{c}-\ell
is a generalized local minimum point, i.e. by Lemma
3.2.2
(a)

\Delta(\mathbf{c}-\mathbf{1}-\ell)=\emptyset
.

(5)

\Rightarrow

(3)
. For any
\ell\in R(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{c})\cap\mathbb{Z}^{r}
set
Dw(\ell):=w(\ell)-w(\mathbf{c}-\ell)
.
First, we construct an increasing path
\{x^{k}\}_{k}
connecting
\mathbf{0}
with
\mathbf{c}

such that
\ell\mapsto Dw(\ell)
is constant along this path.

Assume that
x^{k}<\mathbf{c}
was already constructed, and we wish to find
x^{k+1}=x^{k}+e_{i(k)}\leq\mathbf{c}
.

Set
J:=\{j\in\mathcal{I}\,|\,x^{k}_{j}<c_{j}\}
. Note that
J\not=\emptyset
.
Assume that there exists a certain
j\in J
such that for

x^{k+1}=x^{k}+e_{j}
one has

w(x^{k+1})>w(x^{k})
. Then, by Corollary
3.2.6

we also have
w(\mathbf{c}-x^{k})<w(\mathbf{c}-x^{k+1})
.
Hence with the choice
x^{k+1}=x^{k}+e_{j}
we have
x^{k+1}\leq\mathbf{c}
and

Dw(x^{k+1})=Dw(x^{k})
(see
2.2.6
).

Now assume the contrary, i.e.
w(x^{k}+e_{j})<w(x^{k})
for every
j\in J
. Then
x^{k}\not\in\mathcal{S}_{C,o}
.
Hence, by
(5)
,
there exists some
i
such that

({\dagger})

\Delta_{i}(\mathbf{c}-\mathbf{1}-x^{k})\not=\emptyset
. Note that for any

l\not\in J
one has
\Delta_{l}(\mathbf{c}-\mathbf{1}-x^{k})=\emptyset
.
Hence, such an
i
with
({\dagger})
should be in
J
.
But this implies
that
\overline{\Delta}_{i}(\mathbf{c}-e_{i}-x^{k})\not=\emptyset
, hence

w(\mathbf{c}-e_{i}-x^{k})<w(\mathbf{c}-x^{k})
. For such an

set

, hence again

and

.

Then, by the existence of such a path,

, i.e.

, hence
w(\mathbf{c})=0
.
∎

Example 4.1.2
.

It is a slightly surprising fact that the Gorenstein property is non-hereditary. That is, from the
fact that
(C,o)=\bigcup_{i\in\mathcal{I}}(C_{i},o)
is Gorenstein, it does not necessarily follow that
any
(C_{J},o)=\bigcup_{i\in J}(C_{i},o)
(
\emptyset\not=J\subset\mathcal{I}
) is Gorenstein.

Assume for example that
(C,o)
is the union of four generic lines in
(\mathbb{C}^{3},0)
. It can be
realized as the zero set of two homogeneous quadratic equations. In particular,
it is a complete intersection, hence Gorenstein. On the other hand, the union of any three generic lines
(C^{\prime},o)
in
(\mathbb{C}^{3},0)
is
\vee_{i=1}^{3}(\mathbb{C},0)
, which is
not Gorenstein.
E.g., the conductor is
(1,1,1)
with
w(1,1,1)=-1
.
(The reader is invited to work out and compare the semigroups and
w
–tables of
(C,o)
and
(C^{\prime},o)
.)

4.2.
Gorenstein property and lattice cohomology

Next we prove that the graded
\mathbb{Z}[U]
-module
\mathbb{H}^{0}(C,o)
of a reduced curve singularity
(C,o)
determines whether

(C,o)
is Gorenstein.

Theorem 4.2.1
.

The reduced curve singularity
(C,o)
is Gorenstein if and only if

\textit{either}\ \ \ \mathbb{H}^{0}_{\text{red}}(C,o)=0\ \ \ \textit{or}\ \ \ \text{rank}_{\mathbb{Z}}\ker\big(U:\mathbb{H}^{0}_{0}(C,o)\to\mathbb{H}^{0}_{-2}(C,o)\big)\geq 2.

The more detailed version of this theorem states the following.

Theorem 4.2.2
.

Let
(C,o)
be a reduced curve singularity. Then we have the following facts.

(a)

If
\mathbb{H}^{0}_{\text{red}}(C,o)=0
, then
(C,o)
is smooth, hence Gorenstein.

(b)

If
\mathbb{H}^{0}_{\text{red}}(C,o)\not=0
but
\mathbb{H}^{0}_{2n}(C,o)=0
for all
n<0
, then
m(C,o)=2
,
(C,o)
is a plane curve singularity
(hence Gorenstein)
and
\text{rank}_{\mathbb{Z}}\ker\big(U:\mathbb{H}^{0}_{0}(C,o)\to\mathbb{H}^{0}_{-2}(C,o)\big)\geq 2
.

(c)

If
\mathbb{H}^{0}_{2n}(C,o)\not=0
for some
n<0
and
\text{rank}_{\mathbb{Z}}\ker\big(U:\mathbb{H}^{0}_{0}(C,o)\to\mathbb{H}^{0}_{-2}(C,o)\big)=2
, then
(C,o)
is Gorenstein.

(d)

In any other case
\text{rank}_{\mathbb{Z}}\ker\big(U:\mathbb{H}^{0}_{0}(C,o)\to\mathbb{H}^{0}_{-2}(C,o)\big)=1
and
(C,o)
is not Gorenstein.

In cases
(b)
and
(c)

\ker\big(U:\mathbb{H}^{0}_{0}(C,o)\to\mathbb{H}^{0}_{-2}(C,o)\big)
contains the free

\mathbb{Z}
-submodule of rank 2 generated by connected components of
S_{0}
corresponding to the local minimum points
\mathbf{0}
and
\mathbf{c}
.

Proof.

For (a) and (b) use subsection

2.7
and Proposition
2.7.3
. Otherwise, the multiplicity is
>2
and

\mathbb{H}^{0}_{<0}(C,o)\not=0
(see again Proposition
2.7.3
).

If
\text{rank}_{\mathbb{Z}}\ker\big(U:\mathbb{H}^{0}_{0}(C,o)\to\mathbb{H}^{0}_{-2}(C,o)\big)\geq 2
, then there exists at least one non-zero local minimum point
p\in\mathbb{Z}^{r}_{\geq 0}
with
w(p)=0

(cf. subsection
3.1
).
But by Theorem
3.2.10
(b)
any such
p
must be the conductor, whence
\text{rank}_{\mathbb{Z}}\ker\big(U:\mathbb{H}^{0}_{0}(C,o)\to\mathbb{H}^{0}_{-2}(C,o)\big)=2

(generated by
\{\mathbf{0},\mathbf{c}\}
) and
(C,o)
is Gorenstein by Theorem
4.1.1
(3)
.

In any other case
\text{rank}_{\mathbb{Z}}\ker\big(U:\mathbb{H}^{0}_{0}(C,o)\to\mathbb{H}^{0}_{-2}(C,o)\big)=1
, so
\mathbf{0}\in\mathbb{Z}^{r}_{\geq 0}
is the only local minimum with
w=0
(see Remark
2.7.4
), whence
w(\mathbf{c})<0
and
(C,o)
cannot be Gorenstein again by Theorem
4.1.1
(3)
.
∎

5.
Second application. Multiplicity and lattice cohomology

5.1.
The Multiplicity Formula

Let
(C,o)
be a reduced curve singularity.
Our goal is to identify
w(\mathbf{m})=2-m(C,o)
from the graded
\mathbb{Z}[U]
–module
\mathbb{H}^{0}(C,o)
.

By Theorem
6.1.1
,
\mathbb{H}^{*}_{{\rm red}}
is supported in nonpositive weights, i.e.
\mathbb{H}^{*}_{\text{red},>0}=0
.

Definition 5.1.1
.

For any
(C,o)
with
0^{th}
lattice cohomology

\mathbb{H}^{0}=\mathbb{H}^{0}(C,o)
we define

M(\mathbb{H}^{0})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1&\mbox{if $\mathbb{H}^{0}_{\text{red}}=0$},\\
0&\mbox{if $\mathbb{H}^{0}_{\text{red}}\not=0$, $\mathbb{H}^{0}_{<0}=0$},\\
\max\{w\,|\,{\rm ker}(U:\mathbb{H}^{0}_{2w}\to\mathbb{H}^{0}_{2w-2})\not=0,\ w<0\}&\mbox{if $\mathbb{H}^{0}_{<0}\not=0$}.\end{array}\right.

We say that
(C,o)
satisfies the ‘Multiplicity Formula’ (in short ‘MF’) if

(MF)

w(\mathbf{m})=M(\mathbb{H}^{0}),\ \mbox{i.e.}\ m(C,o)=2-M(\mathbb{H}^{0}).

If
\mathbb{H}^{0}_{<0}=0
then the identity
2-m(C,o)=M(\mathbb{H}^{0})
holds by Proposition

5.2.
Proof of Multiplicity Formula for plane germs

Though
the Gorenstein property does not imply MF, still, for some minima
p
in a ‘large’ zone
we have
w(p)\leq w({\bf m})
.

Proposition 5.2.1
.

Assume that
(C,o)
is Gorenstein, and
let
p\in\mathcal{S}_{C,o}
be a local minimum point
with
2\mathbf{m}\leq p\leq\mathbf{c}-2\mathbf{m}
. Then

w(p)\leq w(\mathbf{m}).

In the proof we need the following Lemma.

Lemma 5.2.2
.

Let
\ell\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{r}
and
s\in\mathcal{S}_{C,o}
. (The Gorenstein assumption is not needed here.) Then

w(\ell+s)-w(\ell)\leq w(\ell+s+e_{i})-w(\ell+e_{i})\ \ \ \mbox{for any $i\in\mathcal{I}$.}

Proof.

The formula can be rearranged as

w(\ell+e_{i})-w(\ell)\leq w(\ell+s+e_{i})-w(\ell+s).

Since both sides are equal to either
-1
or
+1
(cf. (
2.2.6
)), the only nontrivial case is if the left hand side is equal to
+1
. This is equivalent to the existence of a semigroup element
x\in\overline{\Delta}_{i}(\ell)\not=\emptyset
but then
x+s\in\overline{\Delta}_{i}(\ell+s)\not=\emptyset
too, so the right hand side is equal to
+1
as well.
∎

Proof of Proposition
5.2.1
.

First, we show that we may assume that

(5.2.3)

w(p-\mathbf{m})\geq w(p).

This follows from the fact that

(i)\ w(p-\mathbf{m})<w(p)\ \ \ \text{ and }\ \ \ (ii)\ w(\mathbf{c}-p-\mathbf{m})<w(\mathbf{c}-p)

cannot hold simultaneously. Indeed, if
(i)
holds, then applying Lemma
5.2.2
several times resulting a positive shift with vector
\mathbf{m}
, we get that

w(p)<w(p+\mathbf{m})
too. But this, by Gorenstein symmetry, is

w(\mathbf{c}-p)<w(\mathbf{c}-p-\mathbf{m})
, contradicting
(ii)
.
Hence (
5.2.3
) is true either for
p
or for

p^{\prime}=\mathbf{c}-p
, where by symmetry
p^{\prime}
is also a local minimum and
w(p)=w(p^{\prime})
.

Hence, we assume
w(p)\leq w(p-\mathbf{m})
and thus it is sufficient to show that

w(p-\mathbf{m})\leq w(\mathbf{m})
.

Choose an increasing path
\{x^{k}\}_{k=0}^{t}\subset R(\mathbf{m},p-\mathbf{m})
from
\mathbf{m}
to
p-\mathbf{m}
(here we use the assumption
p\geq 2\mathbf{m}
). Define the dual path
\{\overline{x}^{k}\}_{k=0}^{t}
given as
\overline{x}^{k}:=p-x^{t-k}
. We now have

\displaystyle w(p-\mathbf{m})-w(\mathbf{m})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{t}\big(w(x^{k+1})-w(x^{k})\big)=\sum_{k=0}^{t}\big(w(\overline{x}^{k+1})-w(\overline{x}^{k})\big).

Then by Corollary
3.2.6
, an increase in
w
along one of the two paths implies a decrease in
w
along the other one, whence the total difference
w(p-\mathbf{m})-w(\mathbf{m})
cannot be positive.
∎

Having in mind the examples listed above
it is hard to find out the exact limits of MF. In the next theorem we provide two rather large families, covering
the most important key applications, including e.g. the plane curves singularities.

In the proof we will use the following analytic statement.

Proposition 5.2.4
.

Assume that
(C,o)
is a reduced plane curve singularity with
mult(C,o)\geq 3
. Then there exists no ‘linear’ function

L\in\mathfrak{m}_{C,o}\setminus\mathfrak{m}_{C,o}^{2}
such that
\mathfrak{v}(L)=\mathbf{c}
.

Proof.

(a) First assume that
r=1
. Fix a Puiseux parametrization

x=t^{m_{1}},\ y=t^{n_{1}}+\cdots

with
m_{1}<n_{1}
and
m_{1}\nmid n_{1}
, say
\pi
.
Then we claim that for any
L\in\mathfrak{m}_{C,o}\setminus\mathfrak{m}_{C,o}^{2}

the intersection multiplicity
(C,L=0)_{o}
(what in the sequel will be abbreviated by
(C,L)_{o}
and equals

\mathfrak{v}(L)
) is an element of the set

\{0,m_{1},2m_{1},\dots,km_{1},n_{1}\}
, where

k
is the maximal integer such that
km_{1}<n_{1}
.
Indeed, write
L(x,y)
as
ax+by+
higher order terms.
If
a\not=0
then
\text{ord}_{t}(L\circ\pi)=m_{1}
.
Otherwise we must have
b\not=0
so
\text{ord}_{t}(L\circ\pi)\in\{2m_{1},\dots,km_{1},n_{1}\}
.

The elements of the set
\{0,m_{1},2m_{1},\dots,km_{1},n_{1}\}
are bounded by
n_{1}
, which is an element of the set of minimal generators of

\mathcal{S}_{C,o}
. On the other hand, for any
(C,o)
with
m_{1}=mult(C,o)\geq 3
,
it is known that the largest element of the minimal set of generators of
\mathcal{S}_{C,o}
is
strictly smaller than the Milnor number, which equals the conductor. (For properties of the generators of
\mathcal{S}_{C,o}
see e.g.
[
8
,
29
,
30
]
.) Hence if
r=1
and

mult(C,o)\geq 3
, then
\mathfrak{v}(L)<\mathbf{c}
for any

L\in\mathfrak{m}_{C,o}\setminus\mathfrak{m}_{C,o}^{2}
.

Notice also that if
r=1
and
mult(C,o)=2
then
n_{1}-1
is the conductor, i.e. in this case
\mathfrak{v}(L)\leq\mathbf{c}+1
for any
L
.
(If
mult(C,o)=1
then
\mathfrak{v}(L)
can be any positive number.)

(b) Next assume that
r\geq 2
. Recall that the
i^{th}
component
c_{i}
of
\mathbf{c}
satisfies

c_{i}=\mathbf{c}(C_{i},o)+(C_{i},\cup_{j\not=i}C_{j})_{o}
.
Hence, if there is a component
(C_{i},o)
with
mult(C_{i},o)\geq 2
then
\mathbf{c}(C_{i},o)+2\leq c_{i}
, since
(C_{i},D)_{o}\geq mult(C_{i})\cdot mult(D)\geq 2
for any
D
. On the other hand,
since both
(C,o)
and
(C_{i},o)
have embedded dimension 2, the restriction
\mathfrak{m}_{C,o}/\mathfrak{m}^{2}_{C,o}\to\mathfrak{m}_{C_{i},o}/\mathfrak{m}^{2}_{C_{i},o}
is an isomorphism
and thus takes
L
to
\mathfrak{m}_{C_{i},o}\setminus\mathfrak{m}^{2}_{C_{i},o}
. In particular,

\mathfrak{v}_{i}(L)\leq\mathbf{c}(C_{i},o)+1
for any
L\in\mathfrak{m}_{C,o}\setminus\mathfrak{m}_{C,o}^{2}
by part (a), thus
\mathbf{c}\not=\mathfrak{v}(L)
.

Hence we can assume that all components are smooth and (since
mult(C,o)\geq 3)
)
r\geq 3
too.

Assume indirectly that
\mathfrak{v}(L)=\mathbf{c}
. Then
(L,C_{1})_{o}=\mathfrak{v}_{1}(L)=c_{1}>(C_{1},C_{2})_{o}
.
Similarly,
(L,C_{2})_{o}>(C_{1},C_{2})_{o}
. That is, under this assumption

(C_{1},C_{2})_{o}<\min\{(L,C_{1})_{o},(L,C_{2})_{o}\}.

But this fact cannot happen. Indeed, assume that
L
is given by
x=0
, parametrize

(C_{1},o)
by
\pi
as
x=t^{a}+\ldots,

y=t^{b}+\ldots
, and let the equation of
(C_{2},o)
be

f_{2}=xg(x,y)+y^{d}h(y)=0
,
h(0)\not=0
. Then
(L,C_{1})_{o}=a
,
(L,C_{2})_{o}=d
, but
\text{ord}_{t}(f_{2}\circ\pi)\geq\min\{a,d\}
.
∎

The main result of this section is the following.

Theorem 5.2.5
.

Assume that
(C,o)
is

(I) either irreducible, Gorenstein and it satisfies the following property:

(5.2.6)

\mathcal{S}_{C,o}\cap(m,2m)
contains at most 1 element;

(II) or,
(C,o)
is an isolated plane curve singularity.

Then the Multiplicity Formula holds for
(C,o)
.

Proof.

(I) If the multiplicity is
\leq 2
then MF holds. Hence we can assume that
mult(C,o)\geq 3
.
Then, via Proposition
5.2.1
, we need to check that

w(p)\leq w(m)
for any local minimum with
m<p<2m
. But, by assumption,
if such a
p
exists, then it is the unique semigroup element of
(m,2m)
, and

\mathfrak{h}(p)=\mathfrak{h}(m)+1
. Hence
w(p)=w(m)+2+m-p
. But
p\geq m+2
.
Indeed,
p
is a local minimum, hence
p-1\not\in\mathcal{S}_{C,o}
, but
m\in\mathcal{S}_{C,o}
, so necessarily
p-1>m
.

(II)
If
r=1
, then
(C,o)
is Gorenstein, and its semigroup satisfies (
5.2.6
) (for the structure of the semigroups of
plane curve singularities see e.g.
[
8
,
29
,
30
]
). Hence MF is guaranteed by (I).

We proceed by induction on the cardinality
r
of
\mathcal{I}
.
We assume that MF holds for any plane curve with less than
r
irreducible components, and we
consider a plane curve
(C,o)
with
r
components (
r>1
).
If its multiplicity is
\leq 2
then there is nothing to prove, hence we assume
mult(C,o)\geq 3
.
Then we need to show that
w(p)\leq w({\bf m}
for any local minimum point

p=(p_{1},\ldots,p_{r})\not\in\{\mathbf{0},\mathbf{c}\}
.

Again, via Proposition
5.2.1
and
Gorenstein symmetry,
we can assume that

p\in R(\mathbf{m},\mathbf{c})\setminus R(2\mathbf{m},\mathbf{c})
,
p>\mathbf{m}
.

The point
p
, being a semigroup element, is a
\mathfrak{v}
–value. On the other hand,
for any
g\in\mathfrak{m}_{C,o}^{2}

one has
\mathfrak{v}(g)\geq 2\mathbf{m}
. Thus, our
p
satisfies the following:

(5.2.7)

there exists
L\in\mathfrak{m}_{C,o}\setminus\mathfrak{m}_{C,o}^{2}
such that
\mathfrak{v}(L)=p
.

Moreover, since
p\not\geq 2\mathbf{m}
,
there exists some
i\in\mathcal{I}
such that
m_{i}\leq p_{i}<2m_{i}
.

(i) Assume that there exists some
i\in\mathcal{I}
such that
p_{i}=m_{i}
.

Let us fix some
i
with
p_{i}=m_{i}
. By re-indexing we can assume that
i=1
.
Write
p
as
(p_{1},\bar{p})
, where
\bar{p}=(p_{2},\ldots,p_{r})
. Below we will use the
\,\bar{\cdot}\,

notations for invariants associated with the plane curve singularity
(\bar{C},o)=\bigcup_{i>1}(C_{i},o)
.

Then
\mathfrak{h}(p_{1},\bar{p})=\mathfrak{h}(0,\bar{p})
by analysing the lack of semigroup elements in the corresponding region

R((0,\bar{p}),(m_{1}-1,\infty))
. But by Proposition
2.6.1
,
\mathfrak{h}(0,\bar{p})=\bar{\mathfrak{h}}(\bar{p})
. Moreover, analysing the weights of the neighbours of
p
and
\bar{p}
,
a similar argument shows that
if
(m_{1},\bar{p})
is a local minimum of
w
then
\bar{p}
is a local minimum for
\bar{w}
.
Furthermore,

(5.2.8)

w((m_{1},\bar{p}))=2\mathfrak{h}((m_{1},\bar{p}))-m_{1}-|\bar{p}|=2\bar{\mathfrak{h}}(\bar{p})-m_{1}-|\bar{p}|=\bar{w}(\bar{p})-m_{1}.

Here we wish to apply induction for
(\bar{C},o)
. Note that
\bar{p}
is a local minimum of
\bar{w}
with
\bar{p}>\overline{\mathbf{m}}
.
In particular,
(\bar{C},o)
cannot be smooth. If it has multiplicity 2 then
\bar{w}(\bar{p})=0=\bar{w}(\overline{\mathbf{m}})
.
Finally, assume that it has multiplicity
\geq 3
. Then, as in the proof of Proposition
5.2.4
, the restriction

\mathfrak{m}_{C,o}/\mathfrak{m}^{2}_{C,o}\to\mathfrak{m}_{\bar{C},o}/\mathfrak{m}^{2}_{\bar{C},o}
is an isomorphism, hence
L\not\in\mathfrak{m}^{2}_{\bar{C},o}
and by this Proposition,
\bar{p}=\bar{\mathfrak{v}}(L)
is not the conductor of

(\bar{C},o)
. Thus, by the inductive step,
\bar{w}(\bar{p})\leq\bar{w}(\overline{\mathbf{m}})
.

This combined with (
5.2.8
) gives

w(p)=w((m_{1},\bar{p}))=\bar{w}(\bar{p})-m_{1}\leq\bar{w}(\overline{\mathbf{m}})-m_{1}=w(\mathbf{m})
.

(ii) Assume that
m_{j}<p_{j}
for all
j\in\mathcal{I}
.

Recall that there exists an index
i
with
m_{i}<p_{i}<2m_{i}
.
By property (

) of irreducible plane curve germs, if such

\mathcal{S}_{C_{i},o}

in
(m_{i},2m_{i})
. In order to run the induction,

\mathcal{S}_{C_{i},o}

Then,

The property

says that the tangent line to the smooth germ

agrees with the tangent line

for all components

. Hence all the tangent cones are the same. In particular, if for a certain semigroup element

for some

must be linear and

. In other words,

The property (

) will be used in two ways: for the inductive computation of

and also

\bar{p}
is a local minimum for
\bar{w}
(when it is).

First, from (

, so there is no

\mathfrak{h}(p_{1},\bar{p})=\mathfrak{h}(0,\bar{p})
.
Similarly as in case (i), by Proposition
2.6.1
,
\mathfrak{h}(0,\bar{p})=\bar{\mathfrak{h}}(\bar{p})
.
Therefore

(5.2.10)

w(p)=w((p_{1},\bar{p}))=2\mathfrak{h}((p_{1},\bar{p}))-p_{1}-|\bar{p}|=2\bar{\mathfrak{h}}(\bar{p})-p_{1}-|\bar{p}|=\bar{w}(\bar{p})-p_{1}.

Next, we wish to know whether or not
\bar{p}
is a local minimum for
\bar{w}
.
We split the discussion into two subcases.

Case (a)
Assume that
(\bar{C},o)
is not irreducible.
Then using the fact that there is no semigroup element of
\mathcal{S}_{C_{1},o}
in the interval
(m_{1},p_{1})
,
and
\overline{\Delta}_{1}(m_{1},\bar{p}-e_{j})=\emptyset
for any

, we get that

\mathfrak{h}(p\pm e_{j})=\bar{\mathfrak{h}}(\bar{p}\pm e_{j})
, hence
\bar{p}
is indeed a local minimum point of
\bar{w}
.
Then we apply induction as in case (i), hence
\bar{w}(\bar{p})\leq\bar{w}(\overline{\mathbf{m}})
.
This combined with (

) gives

.

Case (b)
Assume that
(\bar{C},o)
is irreducible, i.e.
(\bar{C},o)=(C_{2},o)
.

Now, if
p_{2}>m_{2}+1
then all the arguments of Case (a) can be repeated, hence

.

If (
{\ddagger}
)
p_{2}=m_{2}+1
, then we cannot conclude that

is a local minimum of

.
On the other hand, we notice that

in the interval

(though

might happen when

except when the other coordinate also has the pathological equality

Remark 5.2.11
.

(b) In the proof of Theorem

for a certain

,

(and Proposition

) implied that
\bar{p}
cannot be the conductor of

.

Here the fact that
\mathfrak{m}_{C,o}/\mathfrak{m}^{2}_{C,o}\to\mathfrak{m}_{\bar{C},o}/\mathfrak{m}^{2}_{\bar{C},o}
is an isomorphism (i.e. both germs
(C,o)
and
(\bar{C},o)
are planar)
is crucial.
Indeed, in Example
5.1.4

p=(m_{1},c_{2})
is realized as
\mathfrak{v}(L)
for a linear
L
, however the projection of
L
is in

\mathfrak{m}^{2}_{C_{2},o}
, and even more,
c_{2}
is the conductor of
(C_{2},o)
.

This phenomenon (together with the lack of property (
5.2.9
)) obstructs the inductive step in the (present) proof of the theorem for more general non-planar germs.

6.
Third application. The nonpositivity of the
\mathbb{H}^{*}_{\text{red}}
support

6.1.
Statement of the theorem

Recall that the reduced lattice cohomology of a reduced curve singularity
(C,o)
has the form

\mathbb{H}_{\text{red}}^{q}(C,o)=\bigoplus_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{H}_{\text{red},\,2n}^{q}(C,o).

In this section we will prove the following structure theorem:

Theorem 6.1.1

(Nonpositivity theorem)
.

The weight-grading of the reduced lattice cohomology is nonpositive, i.e.

\mathbb{H}_{\text{red},\,2n}^{q}(C,o)=0

for all
q\geq 0
and
n>0
. In other words,

\mathbb{H}_{\text{red}}^{q}(C,o)=\bigoplus_{n\leq 0}\mathbb{H}^{q}_{\text{red},\,2n}(C,o).

In fact, we will prove a stronger version of Theorem
6.1.1
, namely:

Theorem 6.1.2
.

The spaces
\{S_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
used to define
\mathbb{H}^{*}(C,o)
are contractible for all
n>0
.

The proof of Theorem
6.1.2
follows the next strategy:
we construct a strong deformation retraction from
the (contractible) space
R(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{c})
to its subspace
S_{n}\cap R(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{c})

for any
n>0
. This is done inductively
via a strong deformation retraction from
S_{n}\cap R(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{c})

to
S_{n-1}\cap R(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{c})
, which will be the composition of repeatedly
choosing ‘independent’ lattice points

\ell\in S_{n}\cap R(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{c})
of maximal weight
n\geq 2
and then deforming all cubes containing
\ell
into
S_{n-1}\cap R(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{c})
, in this way ‘eliminating’
\ell
.

6.2.
The M-vertices of cubes

For cubes we used several notations, namely
(\ell,J)
, or
R(\ell,\ell+e_{J})
, where
e_{J}:=\sum_{i\in J}e_{i}
. In this way we basically distinguish the vertices
\ell
and
\ell+e_{J}
. However, in certain discussions it is convenient to distinguish some other vertices, e.g. a vertex
\ell+e_{K}
(
K\subset J
) identified by

w(\ell+e_{K})=\max\{w(\ell^{\prime})\,|\,\ell^{\prime}\ \text{is a vertex of }\ (\ell,J)\}
.
Then we can redefine the cube
(\ell,J)
, viewed from
\ell+e_{K}
in ‘positive and negative’ coordinate directions, see below.

Notation 6.2.1
.

For a lattice point
\ell\in\mathbb{Z}^{r}_{\geq 0}
and index sets
J^{+},J^{-}\subset\mathcal{I}
with
J^{+}\cap J^{-}=\emptyset
and
\ell\geq e_{J^{-}}
, let
(\ell,J^{+},J^{-})
denote the cube with vertices
\ell+e_{K^{+}}-e_{K^{-}}
, where
K^{+}
and
K^{-}
run over all subsets of
J^{+}
and
J^{-}
respectively. In this case the dimension of the cube is
q=\#\{J^{+}\cup J^{-}\}
.

Definition 6.2.2
.

The lattice point
\ell
is called
an M-vertex of the cube

(\ell,J^{+},J^{-})
if

(i)

w_{0}(\ell+e_{i})<w_{0}(\ell)
for all
i\in J^{+}
, and

(ii)

w_{0}(\ell-e_{i})<w_{0}(\ell)
for all
i\in J^{-}
.

A lattice point having maximal weight in a cube is an
M
-vertex of that cube. The lemma below states the converse.

Lemma 6.2.3
.

If
\ell
is an M-vertex of
(\ell,J^{+},J^{-})
then
w(\ell^{\prime})\leq w(\ell)

for any vertex
\ell^{\prime}
of
(\ell,J^{+},J^{-})
.

Proof.

(I) Part (i) of Definition
6.2.2

is equivalent with
\mathfrak{h}(\ell)=\mathfrak{h}(\ell+e_{i})
for any
i\in J^{+}
. Then induction on the cardinality
\#K^{+}
, for
K^{+}\subset J^{+}
, and
(
2.5.1
) imply
\mathfrak{h}(\ell)=\mathfrak{h}(\ell+e_{J^{+}})
. Hence

w(\ell)-w(\ell+e_{J^{+}})=\#J^{+}
.

(II) Similarly, (ii) reads as

\mathfrak{h}(\ell-e_{i})=\mathfrak{h}(\ell)-1
for any
i\in J^{-}
. Then, induction on the cardinality
\#K^{-}
, for

K^{-}\subset J^{-}
, and
(
2.5.1
) show that
\mathfrak{h}(\ell-e_{J^{-}})=\mathfrak{h}(\ell)-\#(J^{-})
. Hence

w(\ell)-w(\ell-e_{J^{-}})=\#J^{-}
.

(III) Assume that there exists a vertex
\ell^{\prime}
of
(\ell,J^{+},J^{-})
such that

w(\ell^{\prime})>w(\ell)
. Then take an increasing path
\{x^{k}\}_{k=0}^{t}
of length
t=\#J^{-}+\#J^{+}
, so that
x_{0}=\ell-e_{J^{-}}
,
x_{t}=\ell+e_{J^{+}}
, and for a certain

k^{\prime}
we have
x^{k^{\prime}}=\ell^{\prime}
. Then

(a)

w(x^{k^{\prime}})-w(\ell-e_{J^{-}})>w(\ell)-w(\ell-e_{J^{-}})=\#J^{-},

(b)

w(x^{k^{\prime}})-w(\ell+e_{J^{+}})>w(\ell)-w(\ell+e_{J^{+}})=\#J^{+}.

Then (
2.2.6
) and (a) (resp. (b)) show that
from
\ell-e_{J^{-}}
to
x^{k^{\prime}}
we need strictly more than
\#J^{-}
steps in the path, respectively
from
x^{k^{\prime}}
to
\ell+e_{J^{+}}
we need strictly more than
\#J^{+}
steps. Hence the
total number of steps in the path is
>\#J^{-}+\#J^{+}=t
, which is a contradiction.
∎

6.3.
Good directions associated with lattice points

In this subsection we prove that for any lattice point
\ell
with
w_{0}(\ell)\geq 2
we can find a coordinate direction along which a (weight decreasing) strong deformation retraction can
be constructed.

Lemma 6.3.1
.

Let
\ell\geq\mathbf{0}
be a lattice point and consider a positively directed cube
(\ell,J^{+},\emptyset)\subset\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{r}
(for some index set
J^{+}\subset\mathcal{I}
). Suppose that

(a)

w(\ell)>w(\ell+e_{i})
for all
i\in J^{+}
(i.e.
\ell
is an M-vertex of
the cube), and

(b)

\ell^{+}:=\ell+e_{J^{+}}
is a generalized local minimum (cf. Lemma
3.2.2
).

Then
w(\ell)\leq 1
.

Proof.

Let us consider an increasing path
\gamma=\{x^{k}\}_{k=0}^{t}
from
x^{0}=\mathbf{0}
to
x^{t}=\ell^{+}
through
x^{t-\#J^{+}}=\ell
(clearly
t\geq\#J^{+}
).
Using (a) and induction (as in part (I) of the proof
of Lemma
6.2.3
) we have (
{\dagger}
)
w(\ell+e_{K^{+}})=w(\ell)-\#K^{+}
for any
K^{+}\subset J^{+}
. This implies that

w(x^{k+1})<w(x^{k})\ \ \hbox{for any index }\ \ t-\#J^{+}\leq k\leq t-1.

Then the first
\#J^{+}
steps of the dual path
\{\ell^{+}-x^{t-k}\}_{k}
connect

\mathbf{0}
with
e_{J^{+}}
.
Along this path the first step is
w
-increasing, but all the others are
w
-decreasing, cf. Remark
2.2.9
.
Hence, by (
3.2.9
) we obtain
w(\ell^{+})\leq 1-\#J^{+}
. Finally, by (
{\dagger}
) we have

w(\ell)=w(\ell^{+})+\#J^{+}
, hence
w(\ell)\leq 1
.
∎

Theorem 6.3.2
.

Let
\ell
be a lattice point with
w(\ell)\geq 2
. Then there exists a coordinate direction
i\in\mathcal{I}
, such that for any cube
\square_{q}=(\ell,J^{+},J^{-})
admitting

\ell
as an M-vertex and satisfying
i\notin J^{+}\cup J^{-}
(i.e.
\square_{q}
is contained in the hyperplane
\{x_{i}=\ell_{i}\}
), we have

(6.3.3)

w(\ell^{\prime}-e_{i})<w(\ell^{\prime})

for any vertex
\ell^{\prime}
of
\square_{q}
. In addition, this coordinate direction
i
also satisfies the following:

(6.3.4)

w(\ell-e_{i})<w(\ell)<w(\ell+e_{i}).

Such a direction will be called a good direction of the vertex
\ell
.

Proof.

Set
I^{+}:=\{i\in\mathcal{I}\,|\,w(\ell+e_{i})<w(\ell)\}
. We will argue that in the absence of a good coordinate direction predicted by the theorem, we would have at
\ell^{+}:=\ell+e_{I^{+}}
a generalized local minimum and could use Lemma
6.3.1
for the positively oriented cube
(\ell,I^{+},\emptyset)
to get
w(\ell)\leq 1
, which contradicts the assumption.

First notice that
by the stability property (
2.5.3
) from
w(\ell+e_{i})<w(\ell)
we get

(6.3.5)

w(\ell^{+})<w(\ell^{+}-e_{i})\ \ \mbox{ for every index $i\in I^{+}$.}

On the other hand, if we set
I^{-}:=\{i\in\mathcal{I}\,|\,w(\ell-e_{i})>w(\ell)\}
, then using the stability property again,
we get

(6.3.6)

w(\ell^{+})<w(\ell^{+}-e_{i})\ \ \mbox{ for every index $i\in I^{-}$. }

Now
I^{+}\cup I^{-}=\mathcal{I}
cannot happen since in that case
\ell^{+}
would be a generalized local minimum with
w(\ell)>1
, contradicting Lemma
6.3.1
used for the positively oriented cube
(\ell,I^{+},\emptyset)
. On the other hand, for any

i\in\mathcal{I}\setminus(I^{+}\cup I^{-})
we automatically have

w(\ell-e_{i})<w(\ell)<w(\ell+e_{i}).

We claim that at least one of the ‘remaining’ coordinates
i\in\mathcal{I}\setminus(I^{+}\cup I^{-})

satisfy the needed requirements.

Assume on the contrary that for every coordinate index
i\in\mathcal{I}\setminus(I^{+}\cup I^{-})

there exists a cube
\square_{q,i}=(\ell,J^{+},J^{-})
with
i\notin J^{+}\cup J^{-}
, admitting
\ell
as an M-vertex, and having a vertex
\ell^{\prime}
with

(6.3.7)

w(\ell^{\prime}-e_{i})>w(\ell^{\prime}).

(Note that they cannot be equal, see (
2.2.6
)).
Then clearly,
J^{+}\subset I^{+}
(because
\ell
is an M-vertex),
hence

\ell^{\prime}\leq\ell+e_{J^{+}}\leq\ell+e_{I^{+}}=\ell^{+}
.
Then, applying the stability property to (
6.3.7
),

(6.3.8)

w_{0}(\ell^{\prime})=w_{0}(\ell^{\prime}-e_{i})-1\ \ \Rightarrow\ \ w_{0}(\ell^{+})=w_{0}(\ell^{+}-e_{i})-1.

But if this is true for every remaining coordinate
i\in\mathcal{I}\setminus(I^{+}\cup I^{-})
, then via (
6.3.5
), (
6.3.6
) and (
6.3.8
) the lattice point
\ell^{+}
is a generalized local minimum, a contradiction. Therefore, there must exist a good coordinate direction
i\in\mathcal{I}\setminus(I^{+}\cup I^{-})
, which satisfies
(
6.3.4
) and the needed properties for any
\square_{q}

contained in the hyperplane
\{x_{i}=\ell_{i}\}
.
∎

6.4.
The deformation theorem

In this subsection we sketch the proof of the Deformation Theorem and present how it implies the Nonpositivity Theorem. The rigorous technical discussion of the deformation will be given in the following subsection.

Theorem 6.4.1

(Deformation Theorem)
.

Let
(C,o)
be a reduced curve singularity. Then for any
n\geq 1
there exists a strong deformation retraction

R(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{c})\searrow S_{n}\cap R(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{c}).

In particular,
R(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{c})
and

S_{n}\cap R(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{c})
are homotopy equivalent and both are contractible.

Corollary 6.4.2
.

Theorems
2.3.2
and
6.4.1
imply that for any
n\geq 1
, the cubical complex
S_{n}
is contractible. Consequently, this proves the Nonpositivity Theorem (Theorem
6.1.1
).

Proof of the Deformation Theorem.

We will give a sequence of strong deformation retractions

R(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{c})\overset{r_{k}}{\searrow}S_{k-1}\cap R(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{c})\overset{r_{k-1}}{\searrow}\ldots S_{n}\cap R(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{c})\overset{r_{n}}{\searrow}S_{n-1}\cap R(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{c})\ldots\overset{r_{3}}{\searrow}S_{2}\cap R(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{c})\overset{r_{2}}{\searrow}S_{1}\cap R(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{c}),

where
k=\max\{w(\ell)\,|\,\ell\in R(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{c})\cap\mathbb{Z}^{r}\}
(thus

R(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{c})\subset S_{k}
).

Even more, each
r_{n}
above will itself be the composition of strong deformation retractions, one for each lattice point in
(S_{n}\setminus S_{n-1})\cap R(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{c})
. Let us denote this set by

W_{n}:=\{\ell\in\mathbb{Z}^{r}\,|\,\ell\in(S_{n}\setminus S_{n-1})\cap R(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{c})\}=\{\ell\in\mathbb{Z}^{r}\cap R(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{c})\,|\,w(\ell)=n\}.

Also, let us order this set in any convenient way, such that
W_{n}=\{\ell^{1},\ldots,\ell^{\#W_{n}}\}
and denote the closed cubical complex spanned by
(S_{n-1}\cap R(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{c}))\cup\{\ell^{1},\ell^{2},\ldots,\ell^{m}\}
by
R_{n}^{m}
(i.e. this contains every closed cube, whose vertex set is contained in
(S_{n-1}\cap R(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{c})\cap\mathbb{Z}^{r})\cup\{\ell^{1},\ell^{2},\ldots,\ell^{m}\}
). Then, in the next subsection we will construct a sequence of strong deformation retractions

S_{n}\cap R(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{c})=R_{n}^{\#W_{n}}\overset{r^{\#W_{n}}_{n}}{\searrow}R_{n}^{\#W_{n}-1}\searrow\ldots R_{n}^{m}\overset{r^{m}_{n}}{\searrow}R_{n}^{m-1}\ldots\overset{r^{2}_{n}}{\searrow}R_{n}^{1}\overset{r_{n}^{1}}{\searrow}R_{n}^{0}=S_{n-1}\cap R(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{c}).

The strong deformation retraction
r_{n}^{m}
shall eliminate the lattice point
\ell^{m}
and every closed cube containing it from the cubical complex
R_{n}^{m}
. Let us denote the closed cubical complex consisting of those cubes which have
\ell^{m}
as their vertex
(the
‘closed star’ of

\ell^{m}
) by

(6.4.3)

St_{\ell^{m}}:=\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\square_{q}\subset R_{n}^{m}\\
\ell^{m}\text{ vertex of }\square_{q}\end{subarray}}\square_{q}.

Then
St_{\ell^{m}}\cup R_{n}^{m-1}=R_{n}^{m}
.
Note that the ‘open star’ of
\ell^{m}
is
St_{\ell^{m}}^{\circ}=\bigcup\{\,\square_{q}^{\circ}\ |\ \square_{q}\subset R_{n}^{m}\text{ and }\ell^{m}\text{ is a}

\text{vertex of }\square_{q}\,\}
, with
St_{\ell^{m}}^{\circ}=R_{n}^{m}\setminus R_{n}^{m-1}
.

Let us denote by
T_{\ell^{m}}
the cubical subcomplex of
St_{\ell^{m}}
consisting of those closed faces in
St_{\ell^{m}}
, which do not contain
\ell^{m}
as a vertex (i.e.
T_{\ell^{m}}:=\bigcup_{\square_{q}\subset St_{\ell^{m}},\ \ell^{m}\notin\square_{q}}\square_{q}
). Then clearly
St_{\ell^{m}}\cap R_{n}^{m-1}=T_{\ell^{m}}
. Therefore, we would need a strong deformation retraction
St_{\ell^{m}}\searrow T_{\ell^{m}}
, which then could be extended to
R_{n}^{m}
with the identity map on
R_{n}^{m-1}
, to get a continuous strong deformation retraction
r_{n}^{m}:R_{n}^{m}\searrow R_{n}^{m-1}
.
This desired strong deformation retraction
St_{\ell^{m}}\searrow T_{\ell^{m}}
is constructed in the following subsection (cf. Corollary
6.5.9
).
∎

In order for the construction to work, we need the following observation:

Lemma 6.4.4
.

For a good coordinate direction
i
corresponding to the lattice point
\ell^{m}
, with
w_{0}(\ell^{m})=n\geq 2
(cf. Theorem
6.3.2
)

(6.4.5)

St_{\ell^{m}}=\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}(\ell^{m},J^{+},J^{-})\subset R_{n}^{m}\\
i\in J^{-}\end{subarray}}(\ell^{m},J^{+},J^{-})

as a topological subspace.

Proof.

As
R_{n}^{m}\subset S_{n}
and
w(\ell^{m})=n
, (
2.2.6
) implies that

\ell^{m}
is an M-vertex of every

q
–cube
\square_{q}\subset St_{\ell^{m}}
containing
\ell^{m}
(compare with Lemma
6.2.3
).

As
n\geq 2
, we can use Theorem
6.3.2
, which implies the existence of a good coordinate direction
i\in\mathcal{I}
.
Using the fact that a cube is contained in
S_{n}
if and only if all its vertices
\ell^{\prime}

satisfy
w(\ell^{\prime})\leq n
, we conclude that
there are no cubes
(\ell^{m},J^{+},J^{-})
in
St_{\ell^{m}}
with
i\in J^{+}
, because
w(\ell^{m}+e_{i})>w(\ell^{m})=n
.

On the other hand, part (
6.3.3
) of Theorem
6.3.2
also implies that if
(\ell^{m},J^{+},J^{-})\subset St_{\ell^{m}}
with
i\notin J^{+}\cup J^{-}
, then

(\ell^{m},J^{+},J^{-}\cup\{i\})\subset St_{\ell^{m}}
as well. Indeed, for every vertex
\ell^{\prime}
of
(\ell^{m},J^{+},J^{-})

w(\ell^{\prime}-e_{i})<w(\ell^{\prime})\leq n,\ \ \mbox{hence }\ \ \ell^{\prime}-e_{i}\in S_{n-1}\subset R_{n}^{m}.

Therefore, as a topological space
St_{\ell^{m}}
is a union of closed cubes containing both
\ell
and
\ell-e_{i}
.
∎

6.5.
Deformation of a closed cube

In this subsection we fill out the technical details of the previous subsection.
For
\ell
with
w(\ell)\geq 2
we fix a good direction
i
and
we will construct a strong deformation retraction of any union of cubes, containing both
\ell
and
\ell-e_{i}
, to their faces furthest from
\ell
. (This vertex
\ell
plays the role of some
\ell^{m}
from the previous subsection.) At the first step we construct the deformation of one such cube and by the properties of the good direction and the virtue of the construction
all of these deformations glue together.

6.5.1
.

Let
\ell
be a vertex of a cube
\square_{q}
, such that there exists a coordinate
i
with
\ell-e_{i}
also a vertex of
\square_{q}
(i.e.
\square_{q}=(\ell,J^{+},J^{-})
with
i\in J^{-}
). We want to construct a strong deformation retraction from the cube
\square_{q}
to the cubical subcomplex
T(\square_{q})
consisting of all those faces (subcubes), for which
\ell
is not a vertex of:

(6.5.2)

T(\square_{q}):=\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\square_{q^{\prime}}\text{ face of }\square_{q}\\
\ell\notin\square_{q^{\prime}}\end{subarray}}\square_{q^{\prime}}.

We will use a central homothety from the lattice point
\ell+e_{i}
.

Remark 6.5.3
.

We can characterize the generic real points
x
of the
(q-1)
-dimensional faces of

\square_{q}
sitting in
T
by having a coordinate
j
for which
x_{j}\in\mathbb{Z}
, but
x_{j}\neq\ell_{j}
(more precisely,
x_{j}
can be
\ell_{j}\pm 1
, the sign depending on whether
j\in J^{+}
or
j\in J^{-}
).

Definition 6.5.4
.

Let us define the following family of ‘homotheties’ with center
\ell+e_{i}
:

\varphi:[0,1]\times\square_{q}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{r},\ \varphi(t,x)=\varphi_{t}(x)=\varphi_{t}\Bigg(\sum_{j}x_{j}e_{j}\Bigg)=x+t\Bigg(\sum_{j\neq i}\dfrac{x_{j}-\ell_{j}}{\ell_{i}+1-x_{i}}e_{j}-e_{i}\Bigg).

\displaystyle\ell_{i}+1-x_{i}
\displaystyle x_{i}-\ell_{i}+1
\displaystyle x
\displaystyle\ell
\displaystyle\ell+e_{j}
\displaystyle\ell+e_{i}
\displaystyle x_{j}-\ell_{j}
\displaystyle 1
\displaystyle\varphi_{\tau(x)}(x)

One can easily check that if
\varphi_{t_{1}}(x)\in\square_{q}
, then

\hskip-58.32814pt\varphi_{t_{1}+t_{2}}(x)=\varphi_{t_{2}}(\varphi_{t_{1}}(x)),

i.e.
\varphi
respects the
group law
. For a given point
x\in\square_{q}
set

\hskip-58.32814pt\tau_{j}(x):=\begin{cases}x_{i}-(\ell_{i}-1),\hskip 59.75078pt\text{ if }j=i;\\
\infty,\hskip 106.69783pt\text{ if }x_{j}=\ell_{j},i\neq j;\\
(\ell_{i}+1-x_{i})\dfrac{1-|x_{j}-\ell_{j}|}{|x_{j}-\ell_{j}|},\ \text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

By this definition, if
x_{j}\neq\ell_{j}
(
j\neq i
), then
\varphi_{\tau_{j}(x)}(x)
has
j
-coordinate
\ell_{j}+{\rm sgn}(x_{j}-\ell_{j})\in\mathbb{Z}
, whereas
\varphi_{\tau_{i}(x)}(x)
has
i
-coordinate
\ell_{i}-1
. Then set

(6.5.5)

\tau(x):=\min\{\tau_{j}(x)\,|\,j\in\{1,\ldots,r\}\},

i.e. the smallest
t\in[0,1]
such that
\varphi_{t}(x)\in T(\square_{q})
. Notice, that this characterization implies that if
\varphi_{t}(x)\in\square_{q}
, then by the group law

(6.5.6)

\tau(\varphi_{t}(x))=\tau(x)-t;

thus for any point
x\in\square_{q}
the property
x\in T(\square_{q})
is equivalent to
\tau(x)=0
(use Remark
6.5.3
).

We can now define the desired strong deformation retraction
\phi^{\square_{q},i}:\square_{q}\searrow T(\square_{q})
:

(6.5.7)

\phi^{\square_{q},i}:[0,1]\times\square_{q}\rightarrow\square_{q},\ \phi^{\square_{q},i}_{t}(x)=\begin{cases}\varphi_{t}(x),\hskip 22.76219pt\text{ if }t\leq\tau(x);\\
\varphi_{\tau(x)}(x),\hskip 9.95845pt\text{ if }t\geq\tau(x).\end{cases}

Clearly, points
x\in T(\square_{q})
have
\phi^{\square_{q},i}_{t}(x)\equiv x
for any
t
. On the other hand for any
x\in\square_{q}
we have
\tau(x)\leq 1
(
\tau_{i}\leq 1
on
\square_{q}
), so
\phi^{\square_{q},i}_{1}(x)=\varphi_{\tau(x)}(x)
which is indeed contained in
T(\square_{q})
as
\tau(\varphi_{\tau(x)}(x))=0
, see (
6.5.6
). Thus we indeed defined a strong deformation retraction
\square_{q}\searrow T
.

Claim 6.5.8
.

Let
\ell\in\mathbb{Z}^{r}
be a lattice point,
i\in\mathcal{I}
a coordinate and
\overline{St}\subset\mathbb{R}^{r}
a union of closed cubes containing both
\ell
and
\ell-e_{i}
as vertices:

\overline{St}:=\bigcup_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\square^{a},\text{ for some finite index set }\mathcal{A}\text{ and cubes }\square^{a}=(\ell,J^{+,a},J^{-,a})\text{ with }i\in J^{-,a}.

Let us define
\overline{T}:=\bigcup_{a\in\mathcal{A}}T(\square^{a})
(where
T(\cdot)
was defined in (
