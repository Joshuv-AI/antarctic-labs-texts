Clinical and aesthetic outcomes after laterally closed tunnel for isolated mandibular gingival recessions: Case series with 18-month follow-up
Authors: Tuğba Şahin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGingival recession, particularly in the anterior mandible, causes aesthetic and sensitivity problems for patients and is typically managed using various grafting techniques. However, data on the long-term outcomes of the laterally closed tunnel (LCT) technique in isolated mandibular recessions remain limited. This case series uniquely presents an 18-month clinical follow-up evaluating soft tissue stability and aesthetic outcomes following the LCT procedure, thereby contributing valuable evidence on this minimally invasive approach.MethodsSeven healthy patients with isolated mandibular Cairo RT I–III recessions were treated via the LCT technique with subepithelial connective tissue grafts (SCTGs). The clinical parameters evaluated in this study were gingival recession width (GRW) and depth (GRD), gingival thickness (GT), keratinized tissue width (KTW), and the root coverage aesthetics score (RES); data were recorded at baseline and at 1.5 months (T1), 6 months (T2), and 18 months (T3) after surgery. Additionally, the plaque index, gingival index, bleeding on probing, periodontal pocket depth, and clinical attachment level were assessed only at baseline to determine the patients’ periodontal health status. Owing to nonnormal data (p < 0.05), the Friedman and Bonferroni-adjusted Wilcoxon tests were used with a significance level of 0.05.ResultsSeven patients (mean age: 31.29 ± 8.92 years) were included. Statistically significant improvements were observed in GRD (p = 0.0008) and GT (p = 0.0117), whereas other parameters were not significantly changed after treatment. Most clinical improvements occurred within the early postoperative period and remained stable over time. At 18 months, all patients achieved partial root coverage, with a mean coverage rate of 42.98%. The aesthetic outcomes improved by 6 months and remained stable thereafter, with no significant differences between time points (p = 0.93).ConclusionsThe LCT technique appears to be a clinically effective approach for managing isolated mandibular gingival recessions, demonstrating improvements early after treatment. However, the lack of complete root coverage (RC) highlights the technique’s sensitivity and the need for its validation through larger, controlled clinical studies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-025-07342-y.Keywords: Gingival thickness, Gingival recession, Keratinized tissue thickness, Laterally closed tunnel, Root coverage aesthetic score

Background

Gingival recession (GR) is defined as the exposure of the tooth root surface (RS) due to migration from the gingival margin to the cementoenamel junction (CEJ) in the apical direction [1]. Autogenous tissue grafting is considered the “gold standard” for treating mucogingival deformities [2]. The development and progression of GR are not associated with increased tooth mortality. However, GR causes aesthetic concerns in many patients and is often related to excessive sensitivity of exposed RSs and the formation of carious and non-carious cervical lesions [3]. GR in the anterior mandible is challenging due to anatomical factors, such as the thickness of the tissue, the presence of a shallow vestibule, and the high frenular tension, which limit grafting success, especially in deep recessions [4]. Several methods have been employed to treat GR, including free gingival grafts [5], connective tissue grafts [6], coronally positioned flaps [7], tunnel [8], and vestibular incision subperiosteal tunnel access [9]. In addition to these methods, modified versions of these techniques have been used to perform laterally closed tunnel (LCT) [10] surgery. The root coverage aesthetic score (RES) [11] can be used to evaluate root aesthetics. The purpose of this case series is to clinically assess changes in the width of the keratinized gingiva, recession parameters, and RES following the use of the LCT technique in the treatment of isolated GRs. This case series presents long-term (18-month) clinical and aesthetic outcomes of the laterally closed tunnel technique applied to isolated mandibular gingival recessions, an area with limited evidence in the current literature.

Methods

Seven patients, aged 21–43 years (one man and six women), presented with isolated mandibular Cairo RT I, II, or III recessions in the mandibular anterior area between August 2022 and January 2025 at Abant İzzet Baysal University. Before participation, all patients were thoroughly briefed on the purpose and content of the study, and written informed consent was obtained. The study protocol was approved by the Abant İzzet Baysal University Clinical Research Ethics Committee (decision number 2022/197, dated 05.07.2022). This research complied with the World Medical Association Declaration of Helsinki on medical research [12].

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

This study included systemically healthy individuals aged 21 to 43 years with clinically diagnosed isolated GRs and a periodontally healthy status. Individuals who were smokers, had diabetes, were pregnant, or were breastfeeding were excluded from the study.

Clinical approach

GR in patients visiting the clinic was determined according to the Cairo classification [13]. The plaque index [14], gingival index [15], bleeding on probing [16], periodontal pocket depth, clinical attachment levels, gingival recession width (GRW), gingival recession depth (GRD), gingival thickness (GT), keratinized tissue width (KTW), presence/absence of CEJ, and presence/absence of the cervical step were measured at baseline (T0). Based on the clinical indices obtained, the patient’s diagnosis was established [17], and phase 1 treatment was completed. Following topical anaesthesia of the gingival tissue, a size 06 endodontic file equipped with a rubber stop or calibrated measuring device was inserted perpendicularly at a midpoint between the free gingival margin and the mucogingival junction. Gingival thickness was measured using an endodontic file. Other measurement was obtained via a calibrated UNC-15 periodontal probe (PCP15; Hu-Friedy, Chicago, IL, USA) at each tooth. All measurements were performed twice by the same examiner. The values were recorded by an assistant so that the examiner was blinded to the previous readings. Before data collection, the examiner was calibrated through repeated pilot measurements to ensure consistency. The KTW was measured as the perpendicular distance from the mucogingival junction to the gingival margin using a millimeter-scaled UNC-15 periodontal probe (PCP15; Hu-Friedy, Chicago, IL, USA). RES assesses five parameters—gingival margin, marginal tissue contour, soft tissue texture, mucogingival junction alignment, and gingival color—with a maximum score of 10. Higher scores indicate more favorable esthetic results, with complete root coverage corresponding to the ideal score.

The percentage of recession coverage was calculated using the gingival recession depth values obtained from the patient before the operation and at 18 months postoperatively. Gingival recession depth and percentage of root coverage was calculated using the following formula:

Percentage of RC = (Preoperative GRD - Postoperative GRD)/Preoperative GRD × 100.

The primary endpoint of the study was the percentage of root coverage (RC) at 6 and 18 months. Secondary endpoints included GRD, GT, KTW, and RES.

All measurements were carried out by a single trained examiner (T.Ş.) to minimize variability.

Surgical approach

The procedure begins with the administration of local anaesthesia, followed by thorough mechanical debridement of the exposed RS via Gracey curettes (Hu Friedy®, Chicago, USA) to create a biologically compatible surface. Slightly bevelled intrasulcular incisions were then performed via SB003 microsurgical blades (MJK Instruments, Marseille, France), ensuring preservation of the interdental papilla and avoiding vertical releasing incisions to maintain optimal vascularization. The mucogingival complex was carefully undermined using specially designed tunneling instruments (PROCUT®, Solingen, Germany) to form a full-thickness tunnel extending to the adjacent teeth, allowing adequate flap mobility and preservation of the vascular supply.

A subepithelial connective tissue graft (SCTG) approximately 1.0–1.5 mm in thickness was harvested from the palate via a single-incision technique. The SCTG was trimmed to an appropriate dimension to ensure passive insertion into the tunnel and optimal adaptation over the exposed RS. 24% EDTA gel (Promida, Türkiye) was applied over the exposed root surface for demineralization for 2 min to remove the smear layer and expose collagen fibers, providing a biologically favorable surface for connective tissue attachment, and then rinsed thoroughly with sterile saline.

The SCTG was inserted into the prepared tunnel and secured at its mesial and distal margins with single or horizontal mattress 5 − 0 absorbable sutures (Pegesorb, Doğsan, Trabzon, Türkiye). A sling 5 − 0 suture (Propilen, Doğsan, Trabzon, Türkiye) was also employed when needed to adapt the SCTG coronally at the level of the CEJ. The overlying tunnel flap was subsequently approximated over the SCTG and sutured with interrupted microsutures, ensuring complete or near-complete SCTG and root surface coverage (RSC) in a tension-free manner. Postoperative care instructions prescribed the use of a mouth rinse containing 0.12% chlorhexidine gluconate and 0.05% cetylpyridinium chloride (Perio-Aid Intensive Care, Dentaid, Italy), administered twice daily for 1 min over the first 21 days following surgery, in conjunction with adherence to a soft diet.

Sutures were removed after 10 days at the palatal site and after 3 weeks, if necessary, at the surgical site. The GRW, GRD, GT, KTW, presence/absence of CEJ, and presence/absence of the cervical step were measured at 1.5 months (T1), 6 months (T2), and 18 months (T3) after surgery (Fig. 1). All surgical procedures were performed by a physician (T.Ş.).

Clinical photographs of the patient were taken at baseline, immediately after surgery, and at 1.5, 6, and 18 months

Statistical methods

The Shapiro-Wilk test was applied to determine if the data distributions were normal across four time intervals (T0, T1, T2, and T3 after surgery) before inferential analysis was performed. Some variables did not follow a normal distribution, as indicated by the results (p < 0.05). Nonparametric statistical techniques were used. Overall changes in periodontal markers throughout time were assessed via the Friedman test. Pairwise comparisons across time points were conducted via the Bonferroni-adjusted Wilcoxon signed-rank test for variables that showed statistically significant results from the Friedman test. The Bonferroni correction was used to account for type I errors in multiple comparisons, and the significance level was set at 0.05.

Results

The study included a total of 7 patients with a mean age of 31.29 ± 8.92 years, ranging from 21 to 43 years. The mean height was 1.64 ± 0.11 m (range: 1.55–1.87 m), and the mean body weight was 68.14 ± 7.73 kg (56–76 kg). Among the participants, six were female (85.7%) and one was male (14.3%).

To assess the significant differences in periodontal parameters measured at the four time points, the Friedman test was performed. The analysis revealed statistically significant changes over time in GRD (χ² = 16.83, p = 0.0008) and GT (χ² = 11.00, p = 0.0117). In contrast, GRW (χ² = 3.36, p = 0.3389), KTW (χ² = 5.07, p = 0.1666), and the RES (χ² = 0.15, p = 0.9260) did not significantly differ. These findings suggest that specific clinical parameters, particularly GT, showed a more pronounced significant change following the timeline (Fig. 2).

The results are presented in Fig. 2. Although several comparisons between baseline and subsequent follow-up points showed nominal significance (e.g., T0 vs. T1 and T0 vs. T2 for both parameters), none of the comparisons remained statistically significant after applying the Bonferroni correction (adjusted α = 0.0083). Notably, comparisons between intermediate and long-term follow-ups (e.g., T2 vs. T3) demonstrated no significant differences, indicating a relative stabilization of clinical outcomes after the early postoperative period. These findings suggest that most of the measurable clinical improvements occurred early after surgery and were sustained over time, with no further significant changes (Fig. 3).

At the T3 follow-up, the RC outcomes were classified for each patient. All seven patients were categorized as having partial root coverage (PRC), with no cases of complete coverage, no change, or worsening. The mean RC percentage was calculated to be 42.98%, indicating moderate clinical improvement across the sample (Fig. 4).

At T1 after surgery, the mean RES was 4.43 ± 1.81, with a median of 5.00 and scores ranging from 1 to 7. By 6 months, the mean RES had increased to 4.86 ± 0.38, with a median of 5.00 and a narrower range of 4 to 5, indicating more consistent aesthetic outcomes. At the T3 follow-up, it was observed that the mean RES remained stable at 4.86 ± 0.69, with a median of 5.00 and scores ranging from 4 to 6. The analysis revealed no significant difference between the time points (χ²(2) = 0.15, p = 0.93), suggesting that the aesthetic results were stable over time after the initial healing phase.

Discussion

In this case series, the effects of the LCT technique on aesthetic and gingival parameters were evaluated. Following surgery, patients showed a significant reduction in GRD, while GT also increased.

In a study by Sculean et. (2018), the mean GRD following treatment with the LCT technique decreased significantly from 5.14 ± 1.26 mm at baseline to 0.2 ± 0.37 mm at 12 months after surgery, indicating the high efficacy of the applied treatment approach in achieving RC. Moreover, the mean KTW increased from 1.41 ± 1.00 mm at baseline to 4.14 ± 1.67 mm at 12 months after, resulting in a KTW gain of 2.75 ± 1.52 mm [10]. The significant increase in the mean KTW from baseline to follow-up suggests that the procedure effectively enhances the quantity of keratinized tissue, which may contribute to improved long-term soft tissue stability, as observed in another study [18]. The observed increase in the mean KTW from 1.6 ± 0.8 mm at baseline to 4.9 ± 1.3 mm at follow-up demonstrated a statistically and clinically significant improvement in another study [19]. Lavu et al. (2021) reported a mean KTW of 3.63 mm (SD 1.34) at the 3-month follow-up [20]. Similarly, in the LCT group, the mean KTW increased significantly from 0.3 ± 0.5 mm at baseline to 3.7 ± 1.1 mm at follow-up, resulting in a mean gain of 3.4 ± 1.3 mm. In contrast, the LCT group presented a mean increase in GT of 1.0 ± 0.1 mm in seven individuals [21]. Another study reported an increase in the KTW from 0.81 ± 0.88 mm at baseline to 3.30 ± 0.67 mm at 6 months [22]. In a study conducted on 18 patients, there was a significant decrease in GRD and a significant increase in KTW after LCT surgery [23]. A case report demonstrated that isolated lingual recession defects can be effectively and predictably managed using the LCT technique, reaffirming the connective tissue graft as a gold standard for achieving successful RC [24]. In another case report, GRs in the mandibular anterior region of two patients were successfully managed via the LCT. Both patients presented with a thin periodontal biotype, favour, and a history of altered mandibular incisor inclination due to previous orthodontic treatment. Despite the presence of a thin biotype, favourable outcomes, including successful RC, an increase in the KTW, and aesthetically pleasing results with good tissue integration in terms of colour and contour, were achieved [25]. In this study, positive changes in GRD and GT were observed.

Statistically significant RC was achieved in all patients at 12 months. Complete root coverage (CRC) was achieved in five patients, encompassing a total of 21 recessions, with a mean RC of 92.9% (i.e., 3.75 mm). Furthermore, in seven patients (63.6%), RC exceeded 93%, with the minimum individual RC recorded at 83.76% [26]. Sculean et al. (2018) achieved 100% closure in their study of 24 patients [10]. High levels of RC were observed in the majority of patients, with several patients achieving over 90% coverage. Although CRC was not consistently obtained, the overall mean RC of 85.1% ± 23.2% reflects a favorable clinical outcome, with some interpatient variability in another study [18]. Guldener et al. (2020) reported that statistically significant RC was achieved in all treated defects after a mean follow-up period of 18.9 ± 10 months. CRC was obtained in 50% of the cases, whereas four additional cases demonstrated RC exceeding 95%, reflecting both the predictability and consistency of the treatment. The overall mean RC of 96.09% is comparable to, or in some cases exceeds, the results reported in previous literature for similar techniques. These outcomes highlight the effectiveness of the LCT in achieving both functional and aesthetic improvements, particularly in patients with multiple or complex GR defects [19]. The mean percentage of RC was 86.9% ± 28.0% in the Double Lateral Sliding Bridge Flap group compared with 96.4% ± 9.4% in the LCT group in another study [21]. A mean RC percentage (MRC%) of 91.40% + 10.25% was observed, with 11 out of 20 sites (55%) showing CRC [22]. However, the mean root coverage was 42.98%, which is lower than the outcomes reported in single recessions in this study. This finding may be attributed to advanced defect stage, patient-related factors, relapse over extended follow-up, and differences in surgical technique.

Lanzerin et al. (2020) reported that the mean RES was 7.9 ± 1.9 at six months after surgery in a study that included 15 cases [18]. The final RES score was 9.0 ± 1.8 out of 10 for the LCT group. Given that all patients had a score of zero on the RES scale at the beginning of the study, these results indicate that there was an improvement of 90% for the LCT group [21]. The RES increased from 4.43 at 1.5 months to 4.86 at 6 months, indicating more consistent outcomes, and remained stable at 1.5 years.

This study provides valuable clinical insights into the effectiveness of the LCT technique in treating isolated mandibular GRs, an area with limited prior research. The standardized surgical protocol, applied by a single experienced clinician, ensured procedural consistency. A relatively long follow-up period of 1.5 years allowed for the evaluation of both short- and midterm stability. Despite the anatomical challenges of the anterior mandible, the technique demonstrated significant improvements in GRD and GT, with aesthetic outcomes remaining stable over time. These findings contribute to the growing evidence supporting minimally invasive soft tissue grafting approaches in periodontally sensitive regions. This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. The small sample size and case series design limit the statistical power and generalizability of the findings. In addition, the absence of a control group prevents direct comparison of the LCT technique with other established surgical procedures. Another potential limitation of the present study is that examiner calibration was not performed. Nevertheless, all clinical measurements were conducted by a single trained and experienced examiner, which is expected to have minimized intra-examiner variability.

Conclusions

Within the limitations of this case series, the LCT technique demonstrated favourable clinical outcomes in the treatment of isolated mandibular GRs. Statistically significant improvements were observed in GRD and GT, with stable esthetic results maintained over the years. Although CRC was not achieved in any case, PRC and early clinical improvements were preserved long-term. These findings indicate that the LCT technique is a minimally invasive option for the mandibular anterior region; however, it is a surgically demanding, technique-sensitive procedure that requires a high level of operator skill and attention. Further randomized controlled studies with larger sample sizes are needed to validate and optimize these outcomes.
