Biomechanical implications of femoral neck system removal following femoral neck fracture treatment in low bone mineral density conditions
Authors: Jeongah Pak, Se-Won Lee, Duk-Young Jung, Bongju Kim, Dohyung Lim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study aimed to investigate the biomechanical implications of Femoral Neck System (FNS) retention and removal in femoral neck fractures under varying bone mineral density (BMD) conditions, with a focus on structural stability and bone resorption risk. A finite element (FE) analysis was conducted using 3D models including intact femurs, FNS-retained (Group I), and FNS-removed (Group R) cases across three BMD levels of 0%, 20%, 40%, and 60%. Structural stability was assessed based on peak von Mises stress (PVMS) in proximal femur, while bone resorption risk was evaluated by analyzing maximum principal stress patterns and measuring strains below the critical bone-damage strain threshold (≤ 200 µstrain). Group I models exhibited localized stress concentrations around the FNS components, with cortical PVMS values of 30.9–34.0 MPa and cancellous PVMS 2.4–3.4 MPa, leading to a stress-shielding effect. The proportion of cancellous bone strains below the 200 µstrain threshold increased to 20.1–18.5% for Models I-0 to I-60, indicating a bone resorption risk. After FNS removal, cortical PVMS increased to 47.0–50.0 MPa, with cancellous bone PVMS returning to near-native levels (0.5–0.8 MPa). Strain analysis revealed a progressive decrease in sub-threshold strains from 3.7% to 0.1%, suggesting restored loading and reduced bone resorption risk. FNS removal can restore physiological stress distribution and reduce bone resorption risk, particularly in patients with low BMD. However, this benefit must be weighed against the potential for increased cortical bone stress, which could compromise structural stability in osteoporotic bone. Therefore, preoperative BMD assessment and postoperative monitoring should be considered to guide clinical decisions regarding FNS removal. the trade-off between restoring physiological load transfer and risking cortical overload must be carefully considered to achieve patient-specific benefit.

Intoduction

The global incidence of hip fractures has been steadily increasing, primarily due to the growing proportion of the population surviving to advanced age, coupled with a continuous rise in age-specific incidence rates1. Given the aging demographic, projections estimate that by 2050, the annual incidence of hip fractures may range from 7.3 to 21.3 million. Substantial efforts have been dedicated to optimizing fixation devices for femoral neck fractures (FNF), with various options, such as cannulated screws (CS) and dynamic hip screws (DHS), being widely used2–4. However, these traditional devices have been associated with high rates of fixation failure, nonunion, and complications, such as avascular necrosis5,6.

In recent years, the Femoral Neck System (FNS) (DePuy Synthes, Raynham, MA, USA) has gained increasing utilization since its introduction in 20187. Designed to overcome the limitations of traditional devices, the FNS provides robust mechanical resistance to shear forces and rotational deformation, ensuring stable fixation, particularly in Pauwels type III fractures7–10. Clinical outcomes have demonstrated their effectiveness in reducing femoral neck shortening and internal fixation failure rates while improving fracture healing times and preserving femoral neck length, even in patients with poor cortical bone quality11–14. Despite these advantages, studies have not extensively explored the structural integrity and safety of the femur following FNS removal, particularly in cases involving low bone mineral density (BMD). Approximately 30% of postmenopausal women have osteoporosis at the hip, lumbar spine, or distal forearm, and the lifetime risk of osteoporotic fractures in women over 50 years reaches up to 53%, underscoring the clinical importance of BMD in hip fracture treatment15. This gap indicates the need to evaluate the biomechanical performance of the femur after FNS removal, especially under conditions of reduced BMD.

There remains ongoing debate regarding the optimal management of the FNS once bony union has been achieved following femoral neck fracture treatment11,14,16,17. Some clinicians advocate for the removal of the FNS to prevent potential long-term complications, such as implant-related irritation, bone resorption, and pain16,18,19. Others, however, argue that the risks associated with secondary surgery may outweigh the potential benefits3. While previous studies have explored various aspects of this debate, there remains a lack of conclusive evidence regarding the structural stability of the femur following FNS removal16,20,21. This study aims to address this issue by evaluating the biomechanical consequences of FNS removal through finite element analysis under varying bone mineral density conditions. Although the model is based on a single patient, it provides controlled insight into mechanical stress redistribution after implant removal, particularly in osteoporotic bone. The findings are intended to serve as foundational data that can inform future clinical and computational studies across broader patient populations.

Furthermore, limited research has investigated the risk of bone resorption caused by stress shielding when the FNS is retained. The stress-shielding effect may potentially lead to bone resorption, compromising the long-term integrity of the femur. This challenge is further exacerbated in patients with reduced BMD, who are at a higher risk of compromised structural stability and bone resorption. Despite this, studies specifically examining the biomechanical effects of FNS removal under reduced BMD conditions remain scarce. Understanding the impact of FNS removal in these cases is critical for informing clinical decisions and optimizing patient outcomes. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the structural stability of the femur in its final bony union state and after FNS removal, with a specific focus on conditions of reduced BMD. Additionally, it investigated whether FNS removal could diminish the risk of bone resorption caused by stress shielding. By addressing these critical gaps, this study seeks to provide essential biomechanical evidence to guide clinical decisions, ultimately contributing to improved surgical practices and better patient outcomes.

Materials and methods

Pelvis and proximal femur finite element (FE) model

A three-dimensional (3D) model of the native pelvis and proximal femur was reconstructed using computed tomography (CT) data with a slice thickness of 0.1 mm, obtained from a 64-channel CT scanner (Somatom Sensation 64, Siemens Healthcare, Germany). This study did not involve direct experimentation on human participants. The CT data were derived from a 60-year-old female subject and were used to construct the finite element model. The use of the CT data was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Yeouido St. Mary’s Hospital (Approval No. SC24RISI0040). All methods were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. The requirement for informed consent was waived by the IRB. The FE model, incorporating the native pelvis and proximal femur, was developed using four-node tetrahedral elements (C3D4) and is referred to as the native FE model (Model N). This model comprised 255,009 elements, based on a 3D reconstruction of the CT data (Fig. 1). The material properties of the FE model, shown in Table 1, were assumed to be linear elastic, isotropic, and homogeneous.

Femoral neck fracture FE models with/without FNS

The Pauwels classification system was employed to categorize femoral neck fractures, with fracture angles of 30°, 50°, and 70° incorporated into the pelvis and proximal femur FE model to simulate Pauwels types I, II, and III, respectively (Fig. 1). The Femoral Neck System (FNS) (DePuy Synthes, Indiana, United States) was implemented in the FE models with Pauwels-classified femoral neck fractures to replicate surgical fixation (Fig. 2).

The elastic modulus and Poisson’s ratio for each model with BMD reductions of 20%, 40% and 60% were calculated using established correlation equations from the literature21–24, which describe the relationship between apparent density and elastic modulus (Table 1). Similarly, the yield strength for cortical and cancellous bone under conditions of reduced BMD were derived using correlation equations linking elastic modulus and yield strength in the cortical and cancellous bone (Table 1). These parameter values were then applied to simulate the material properties for the FE models with reduced BMD.

All models, including Groups I and R, were constructed using four-node tetrahedral elements (C3D4). The material properties assigned to each model, derived from the calculated values and literature5,25–27, were assumed to exhibit linear elasticity, isotropy, and homogeneity, as detailed in Table 1.

Loading, contact, and boundary conditions

Physiological loading and boundary conditions, corresponding to approximately 3.0 times body weight during single-leg stance, were applied based on established literature8 (Fig. 3). A uniform vertical load of 2100 N was applied perpendicular to the iliac crest to replicate this condition25,27–31. A surface-to-surface contact algorithm was employed to model the interactions at the contact surfaces of the FE models, such as between the acetabulum and the femoral head.

Subsequently, a finite sliding, frictionless hard contact algorithm was implemented to prevent penetration at the contact surfaces, with the friction coefficient set to 0.3. Additionally, a tie contact algorithm was applied to the femoral neck fracture surfaces in the union state for both the Group I and Group R models. The boundary conditions were defined as follows: Constraint 1 was applied to the sacroiliac joint surface of the pelvis, allowing translational and rotational freedom along the superior-inferior axis while fully constraining the remaining four degrees of freedom. Constraint 2 was applied to the pubic region of the pelvis with the same boundary conditions as Constraint 1. Constraint 3 was imposed at the distal end of the femur, fully constraining all six degrees of freedom. All simulations were conducted under quasi-static conditions using Abaqus v14.0 (Dassault Systèmes, France).

Finite element (FE) model validation

To validate the FE model of the native femur, a sensitivity (convergence) test was initially conducted, followed by a comparative analysis to assess the accuracy of the FE model by comparing our results with those reported in previous studies16,32,33. First, the sensitivity test was performed to evaluate the influence of element quantity on the FE results by applying a uniform vertical load of 2100 N perpendicularly to the iliac crest. The number of elements in the FE model was either reduced (coarsest mesh model) or increased (finest mesh model) by 10% relative to the baseline mesh density used in this study. Second, to verify the accuracy of the FE model, the stresses obtained from our FE analysis were examined and compared with those reported in the literature32,33.

Data analysis

The von Mises stresses in the femur and the FNS were evaluated to quantitatively assess structural stability in Model N, Group I, and Group R. This assessment was performed by comparing the peak von Mises stresses (PVMS) obtained from FE analysis with the yield strengths of cortical bone, cancellous bone, and the Ti6Al4V alloy employed in the FNS (Table 1). For each bone component under the various BMD reduction conditions (0%, 20%, 40%, and 60%), the ratio of the PVMS to the corresponding yield strength was calculated. A ratio of ≤ 100% indicated that the local stress remained within the material’s elastic limit, thereby implying structural stability. Conversely, a ratio exceeding 100% was considered indicative of instability, as the applied stresses would surpass the yield strength of the bone. To adopt a conservative stance, cases where the PVMS-to-yield strength ratio ranged between 80% and 100% were classified as semi-stable, acknowledging that even marginal increases in stress could predispose the structure to sub-critical damage under prolonged loading conditions.

Additionally, the principal stress flow inside the femur was analyzed to characterize stress transfer patterns in Model N, Group I, and Group R. Furthermore, the potential for bone resorption due to stress shielding caused by FNS implantation was evaluated at the femoral head and neck within the cancellous bone. This analysis involved assessing the critical bone-damage strain threshold (≤ 200 µstrain), which can impair bone remodeling capacity and lead to bone degeneration, as described by Frost’s Mechanostat theory32,34.

Results

Accuracy of FE model

A sensitivity (convergence) analysis demonstrated that the differences in von Mises stress results between the baseline mesh and both the coarsest and finest mesh adjustments were generally below 5%. Therefore, the FE model utilizing the baseline mesh density was deemed sufficiently accurate, eliminating the need for further mesh refinement.

Subsequently, a comparative validation was conducted to assess the predictive accuracy of the FE model. This validation involved benchmarking the von Mises stress results from our FE model (Model N) against those reported in previous studies33,35. Specifically, the studies by Zhang et al.35 and Wu et al.33 reported von Mises stresses of 28.4 MPa and 60.0 MPa, respectively, in the femoral neck under a uniform vertical load of 2100 N. In comparison, our FE model predicted a von Mises stress of 40.3 MPa in the femoral neck, which falls within the range of previously reported values. This consistency confirms the validity and reliability of our FE model in accurately predicting stress distribution patterns in the femoral head and neck under physiological loading conditions.

Structural stability of the femur and FNS

Figure 4 illustrates that the von Mises stresses in the cortical bone are predominantly concentrated in the femoral neck region across all groups. For the native femur (Model N), the PVMS was 40.3 MPa. In Group I, Models I-0, I-20, I-40, and I-60 exhibited PVMS values of 34.0, 32.8, 34.4, and 30.9 MPa, respectively. The corresponding PVMS-to-yield strength ratios for Models I-0, I-20, I-40, and I-60 were approximately 24%, 35%, 62%, and 115%, indicating that Models I-0, I-20, and I-40 maintained stability (ratio < 80%), while Model I-60 exceeded the yield strength, signifying instability at 60% BMD reduction. In contrast, Group R models displayed higher PVMS values. Models R-0, R-20, R-40, and R-60 showed PVMS values of 50.0, 48.8, 47.3, and 47.0 MPa. The corresponding PVMS-to-yield strength ratios for Models R-0, R-20, R-40, and R-60 were approximately 36%, 52%, 85%, and 175%, respectively. As a result, Models R-0 and R-20 were classified as stable (ratio < 80%), Model R-40 as semi-stable (ratio between 80% and 100%), and Model R-60 as unstable (ratio > 100%).

Figure 5 illustrates the von Mises stress distribution in the cancellous bone, with high stresses predominantly concentrated in the trabecular regions near the femoral head and neck across all groups. In Group I, Models I-0, I-20, I-40, and I-60 exhibited PVMS values of 3.4, 3.2, 2.9, and 2.4 MPa, respectively. The corresponding PVMS-to-yield strength ratios were approximately 56%, 75%, 108%, and 181%. Therefore, Models I-0 and I-20 were considered stable (with ratios of 56% and 75%, respectively), while Models I-40 and I-60 exceeded 100%, indicating instability at 40% and 60% BMD reductions. In Group R, Models R-0, R-20, R-40, and R-60 showed PVMS values of 0.8, 0.7, 0.6, and 0.5 MPa, respectively. The resulting PVMS-to-yield strength ratios were approximately 12%, 16%, 22%, and 34%. These ratios indicate that Models R-0, R-20, R-40, and R-60 all remain stable (ratio < 80%), with no model surpassing the 80% threshold for instability.

The von Mises stress distribution in the FNS revealed that high stresses were predominantly concentrated in the bolt region and the anti-rotation screw across all models in Group I (Fig. 6). This observation was based on PVMS predicted by the finite element simulations, where the stress magnitude was visualized using a color gradient from green to red in the contour maps. Regions of highest stress were represented by red zones, which consistently appeared around the bolt and anti-rotation screw components, regardless of the BMD condition. These localized stress concentrations reflect the mechanical load transfer and geometric constraints within the FNS design. Among the Group I models, the PVMS increased from 264.4 MPa in Model I-0 to 291.5 MPa in Model I-60 with the reduction of BMD. However, the PVMS for all Group I models remained below the yield strength of the FNS (ratio < 35%), indicating sufficient structural stability under the applied loading conditions. As the FNS was not present in the native femur (Model N), no stress data was available for this group. Similarly, no stress data was available for the Group R models due to the removal of the FNS.

Risk of bone resorption

The maximum principal stress magnitudes and distributions within the proximal femur for Model N, Group I, and Group R models are shown in Fig. 7. While notable differences were observed between the groups, the variations in maximum principal stress magnitudes and distributions among the models with different BMD levels within each group were minimal. In Model N, the maximum principal stress distribution was moderate and evenly distributed, with no significant areas of stress concentration, reflecting natural load transfer without stress shielding or structural compromise under physiological loading conditions. In Group I, high principal stresses were concentrated near the bolt and anti-rotation screw insertion sites due to the presence of the FNS, resulting in stress-shielding effects. These effects were accompanied by low principal stress magnitudes within the cancellous bone, indicating reduced mechanical loading in these regions. In Group R, the maximum principal stress distribution was more uniform and closely resembled that of Model N. The removal of the FNS redistributed stresses across the proximal femur, reducing the localized stress concentrations observed in Group I.

Discussions

The findings of this study may have important clinical relevance, particularly for orthopedic surgeons who need to decide whether to retain or remove the FNS following femoral neck fracture treatment in patients with low BMD. This research provides valuable insight by suggesting that FNS removal may not significantly compromise femoral structural stability, even under reduced BMD conditions in certain cases, although it might not be universally beneficial across all BMD reductions36. Previous cadaveric biomechanical studies have also demonstrated that extramedullary implants can significantly reduce cortical strain levels (up to 89%) after fracture fixation, leading to stress shielding and localized bone loss, while removal of these implants can restore strain distribution to near pre-fracture levels, thereby potentially preventing further bone deterioration associated with stress shielding37. Additionally, it may be one of the first to indicate that removing the FNS could eliminate the stress-shielding effect, which may often lead to bone resorption in the cancellous bone. By removing the implant, the femur may redistribute mechanical stresses, potentially reducing the risk of further bone deterioration. Previous biomechanical study has demonstrated that retained extramedullary implants can significantly reduce cortical strain levels, leading to stress shielding and localized bone loss even after fracture healing38. Removal of these implants has been shown to restore strain distribution to near pre-fracture levels, potentially preventing further bone deterioration associated with stress shielding. These findings imply that FNS removal could be a viable option in clinical practice, potentially offering the dual benefit of reducing implant-related complications and mitigating the risk of bone resorption, thereby contributing to improved patient outcomes39. This evidence may guide surgeons in making more informed decisions about FNS retention or removal, ultimately supporting improved recovery and long-term success for patients, particularly those with low BMD.

The results from the PVMS analysis of the cortical bone may highlight the potential impact of FNS removal on femoral structural stability under varying BMD conditions. In cases of normal or mild BMD reduction, the PVMS values appeared to remain below the yield strength, suggesting that the femur’s structural integrity may be maintained even after FNS removal. However, in patients with significant BMD loss exceeding 60%, the PVMS may surpass the yield strength, indicating a potential compromise in structural stability. This observation seems to align with existing studies that suggest a direct relationship between reduced BMD and increased fracture risk due to diminished bone strength, which could lower both the elastic modulus and yield strength of cortical bone, rendering it more susceptible to mechanical failure40. This finding suggests that, despite the increased stress observed following FNS removal, the cortical bone may retain sufficient intrinsic mechanical capacity to resist fracture under physiological loading, particularly in cases of mild BMD reduction (< 60%). Clinically, these results indicate that FNS removal could be considered a viable option in patients with mild BMD reduction, as their cortical bone structure may maintain adequate stability despite increased stress. However, for patients with significant osteoporotic bone loss, the risk of structural instability may rise markedly when PVMS exceeds the yield strength41. Therefore, careful preoperative assessment of BMD should be an integral part of the surgical planning process. Utilizing advanced imaging techniques, such as dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA), to evaluate bone density could aid in identifying patients at elevated risk for instability and guiding individualized treatment decisions. Additionally, these findings highlight the potential importance of postoperative monitoring, particularly in high-risk populations, to detect early signs of compromised mechanical integrity and prevent potential complications. Ultimately, while FNS removal may be biomechanically feasible in selecting patient populations, caution may be warranted in individuals with significant BMD reductions, as the increased PVMS levels in the cortical bone could predispose them to structural failure under load.

The results from the PVMS analysis of the cancellous bone may highlight the potential impact of FNS removal on femoral structural stability under varying BMD conditions. The findings suggest that the presence of the FNS could significantly increase PVMS in the cancellous bone. Group I models appeared to exhibit markedly higher PVMS values compared to the native femur, indicating a localized contact stress effect potentially caused by FNS implantation. These findings may align with previous research suggesting that implant placement can often induce stress concentrations within cancellous bone structures, potentially affecting trabecular integrity under load16. In models with reduced BMD, PVMS did not show substantial variation; however, the risk of structural instability may increase with decreasing BMD. Particularly, Models I-40 and I-60 exhibited stress levels that appeared to exceed the yield strength by 108% and 181%, respectively. This observation may underscore the potential vulnerability of cancellous bone to mechanical overload in osteoporotic conditions, where diminished trabecular density could compromise the bone’s load-bearing capacity42. Importantly, under severe BMD reduction, localized stress increases in cancellous bone may predispose the retained FNS to loosening, which could, in turn, compromise the stability of the fixation and potentially lead to surgical failure. Conversely, the Group R models exhibited only slightly elevated PVMS levels compared to the native femur, with stress values that consistently remained below both the yield strengths. This indicates that a reduced contact stress effect in the cancellous bone may occur following FNS removal, highlighting the potential for mechanical recovery post-implant extraction. From a clinical perspective, these findings suggest that FNS implantation could increase structural instability within cancellous bone, particularly under reduced BMD conditions. Therefore, in patients with significant BMD loss, careful consideration might be warranted to avoid excessive trabecular stress that could lead to structural instability. Preoperative BMD evaluation should be considered an integral part of the decision-making process, as it may help ensure implant stability while minimizing the risk of cancellous bone damage in osteoporotic patients.

The analysis of maximum principal stress distribution and cancellous bone strain in this study may provide important insights into the biomechanical implications of FNS implantation and removal with respect to bone resorption risk. The results suggest that the insertion of the FNS could induce a notable stress-shielding effect, characterized by altered maximum principal stress patterns and an increased proportion of strains below the critical bone-damage strain threshold (≤ 200 µstrain). Conversely, FNS removal appears to mitigate these effects, potentially restoring the natural stress distribution and reducing bone resorption risk. In Group I, the presence of the FNS appeared to result in high principal stresses concentrated near the bolt and anti-rotation screw insertion sites, potentially disrupting the natural load transfer patterns observed in the native femur. This stress-shielding effect may lead to decreased mechanical loading in the surrounding cancellous bone, as evidenced by the increased proportion of strains below the critical bone-damage strain threshold. The proportions of sub-threshold strains were 20.1%, 19.3%, 18.8%, and 18.5% in Models I-0, I-20, I-40, and I-60, respectively. The relatively stable proportions across different BMD conditions suggest that the stress-shielding effect and localized stress concentrations near the implant sites might dominate the cancellous bone’s mechanical environment. Consequently, reduced mechanical stimulation in these areas may compromise bone remodeling activity and increase the risk of bone resorption. In contrast, in Group R, the removal of the FNS seemed to lead to a redistribution of stresses across the proximal femur, with the maximum principal stress pattern more closely resembling that of the native femur. This stress redistribution may alleviate the stress-shielding effect, resulting in a notable decrease in the proportion of strains below the critical bone-damage strain threshold. Specifically, the sub-threshold strain proportions were observed to decrease progressively from 3.7% to 0.1% with increasing BMD reduction, with values of 3.7%, 1.9%, 0.3% and 0.1% in Models R-0, R-20, R-40, and R-60, respectively. This pattern may suggest that, unlike the FNS-inserted models, BMD reduction in the absence of the implant could significantly influence the cancellous bone’s mechanical environment. The observed decrease in sub-threshold strain proportions with increasing BMD reduction in Group R could be attributed to cancellous bone weakening and the presence of voids left by the removed FNS components. These structural voids may increase internal deformation, leading to relatively higher strain magnitudes and a corresponding decrease in the proportion of strains below the critical bone-damage strain threshold. As the cancellous bone weakens with progressive BMD loss, structural integrity may diminish, resulting in larger deformations under physiological loading conditions. Clinically, these findings suggest that FNS removal might offer a biomechanical advantage in terms of bone resorption risk. By eliminating the stress-shielding effect and promoting a more natural stress distribution, FNS removal could help restore normal mechanical stimulation to the cancellous bone, thereby potentially supporting ongoing bone remodeling processes. Consequently, from a bone resorption perspective, the removal of FNS may yield a more favorable surgical outcome, particularly in patients with reduced BMD who are at heightened risk of bone loss. However, given the presence of overall structural instability, the decision to remove the FNS should be made with careful consideration.

While this study provides valuable biomechanical insights into stress redistribution following FNS removal under various BMD conditions, it must be acknowledged that the findings are based on a single-subject finite element model. As such, the generalizability of the results to broader patient populations is inherently limited. Nonetheless, this modeling framework offers a controlled platform for isolating mechanical effects and identifying key trends in stress transfer and bone remodeling risk, especially in osteoporotic settings. These findings represent an incremental but meaningful step toward addressing the lack of quantitative evidence in the literature. Future investigations should incorporate a larger number of anatomically diverse models and clinical data to validate and expand upon the current results.

This study may provide valuable biomechanical insights into the potential effects of FNS retention and removal concerning bone resorption risk and structural stability under varying BMD conditions. Additionally, while this study did not incorporate soft tissue stabilizers such as the ligamentum teres in the finite element model, previous studies have demonstrated that finite element analyses under compressive loading conditions can maintain model validity even without these stabilizers43,44. However, the ligamentum teres play an important role in limiting hip rotation, particularly at higher flexion angles, indicating its potential contribution to joint stability under multi-directional or rotational loading45. Future studies will aim to incorporate these soft tissue structures and rotational loading conditions to further enhance the physiological relevance and clinical applicability of the finite element model used for evaluating FNS retention and removal. Furthermore, regarding boundary conditions, this study applied tie contact at the bone–implant interface under the assumption of complete bony union, consistent with previous post-union evaluations46–48. We acknowledge that employing frictional contact would be appropriate for simulating the immediate postoperative period before bony union is achieved, and plan to address this in future work. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the study did not account for variations in fracture angle, as the analysis assumed complete bone union with 100% healing. Consequently, the potential impact of fracture angle changes on structural stability and bone resorption risk may not have been accurately captured. In clinical practice, fracture healing characteristics and corresponding material properties could vary significantly based on fracture angle, which may influence mechanical stability outcomes. The lack of relevant data on the material properties associated with different fracture angles appeared to prevent the inclusion of such factors in this study. Biological and mechanical factors may contribute to diverse bone healing patterns, making it challenging to obtain and apply such data in a computational study. Future clinical trials examining the influence of fracture angles on FNS retention and removal decisions might help address this gap. Although our study assumed complete fracture union and did not incorporate variable fracture angles, we acknowledge that such variations can significantly affect healing dynamics and mechanical stability. Future research incorporating a range of fracture geometries and healing stages is warranted. Additionally, the study’s assumptions included simplified loading and boundary conditions, a single implant type, and the exclusion of dynamic motion conditions, as well as variations in patient-specific anatomical and physiological characteristics49. Despite these limitations, this study could represent the first investigation into the relationship between bone quality changes and FNS retention or removal. By assuming complete bone union, the results may provide a reliable foundation for guiding clinical decisions regarding FNS use. Furthermore, the study’s findings seem to align with established biomechanical principles, potentially supporting their validity. This work may lay the groundwork for future studies incorporating more diverse fracture characteristics and dynamic loading scenarios to enhance the clinical relevance of FNS-related decision-making.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study reveals that FNS removal after fracture healing can restore near-physiological stress distribution in the proximal femur and reduce cancellous bone resorption risk. However, it also leads to increased cortical stress, especially under osteoporotic conditions. The clinical decision to remove the FNS should be guided by preoperative BMD assessment and balanced consideration of long-term implant-related risks and potential instability post-removal. These findings provide biomechanical evidence to support patient-specific surgical decision-making.
