Deconformer: efficient cell-type composition profiling of cfRNA across diverse cell types
Authors: Chaoxing Wang, Xuetao Tian, Shuo Yan, Jinghua Sun, Sujun Zhu, Jiayi Li, Chu Shi, Tingyu Yang, Yulong Qin, Zhongzhen Liu, Juan Zeng, Dongyue Yu, Wentao Liu, Chengbin Hu, Qing Zhou, Xin Jin, Wen-Jing Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractResolving cell-type contributions in cell-free RNA (cfRNA) profiles is important for liquid-biopsy research, but profiling many potential cellular sources remains computationally challenging. Here, we present Deconformer, a biologically informed deep-learning framework that integrates pathway knowledge into a Transformer architecture to estimate relative cfRNA contributions across 60 major human cell types. In systematic benchmarks using cross-platform simulations selected for similarity to real plasma cfRNA, Deconformer showed improved robustness from pathway-informed modeling and achieved competitive accuracy with substantially lower computational cost than established deconvolution methods. Applied to real cfRNA cohorts, it recapitulated known gestational trajectories and detected disease-associated composition shifts, supporting its use as a practical framework for large-scale cfRNA profiling.Keywords: liquid biopsy, cell-free RNA, deep learning, cell-type composition

Introduction

Cell-free RNA (cfRNA) is a mixture of transcripts continuously released into body fluids by tissues throughout the body [1]. Previous studies have demonstrated cfRNA’s potential as a noninvasive measurement for health monitoring, disease prediction, diagnosis, and prognosis [2–5], and cfRNA is detectable in biofluids such as plasma, saliva, and urine [6]. Analyzing the cellular origins of cfRNA is essential for cancer diagnosis and for monitoring organ development and disease progression [2, 4]. For instance, Vorperian et al. [7] applied nu support vector regression (nu-SVR), using the Tabula Sapiens project (TSP) [8] reference, to perform cell-type composition analysis of plasma cfRNA. Moufarrej et al. [9] and Rasmussen et al. [10] assessed cell-type composition scores by calculating the cumulative expression of cell-specific genes.

However, despite the availability of deconvolution methods tailored for bulk tissue RNA-Seq (e.g. CIBERSORTx (CBx) [11], MuSiC [12], BayesPrism [13], MIXTURE [14]), cell-type composition analysis of cfRNA remains challenging. As blood flows through many human organs, no consensus exists on plasma cfRNA cell-type origins [1]. Potential cell-type sources of plasma cfRNA are particularly abundant, whereas specific tissue RNA contains limited sources. For bulk RNA-Seq deconvolution methods, computational cost grows unacceptably beyond roughly 30 cell types, forcing trade-offs (fewer or merged cell types, often with downsampling) that preclude panoramic analysis of the system-wide cellular changes reflected in blood.

Unlike traditional deconvolution methods, deep neural network (DNN) methods like Scaden [15] and UniCell Deconvolve (UCD) [16] generate simulated data with known cell-type composition by randomly mixing single-cell expression profiles. These simulated data are used to train the deep learning model’s cell-type composition analysis capabilities. Benefiting from graphics processing unit (GPU) parallel computing, DNN-based tools can now handle cell-type composition analysis involving hundreds of cell types [16]. Moreover, computational resources and runtime are mainly concentrated in training, while trained models require minimal resources for analysis. However, existing DNN-based methods are purely data-driven with unstable optimization and poor interpretability, making it difficult to judge biological rule capture versus overfitting.

Inspired by single-cell perturbation models that leverage biological knowledge under limited-sample challenges [17], we developed Deconformer, a DNN framework that embeds gene expression into pathway-level representations and adaptively weights them for panoramic cell-type composition analysis of cfRNA. Controlled comparisons against a gene-level variant (Deconformer-gene) and with perturbation of the pathway information encoded in the mask show that this pathway-integration module itself improves accuracy. Benchmarking against established methods on simulations tuned to reproduce the statistical properties of 368 real plasma cfRNA samples ranked Deconformer most consistent overall on out-of-domain, cross-platform Smart-seq2 mixtures. On real cfRNA cohorts, Deconformer recapitulated gestational trajectories and identified compositional shifts associated with coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19), liver cancer, preeclampsia, and gestational diabetes, while attention analyses indicated that the model engages group-relevant pathways, grounding its predictions in biologically meaningful knowledge. The model trains on a consumer-grade GPU at negligible inference cost, making it an efficient discovery tool for liquid-biopsy research.

Materials and methods

Deconformer framework and model architecture

Deconformer is a deep neural network designed for efficient cell-type composition analysis of cfRNA across diverse cell types under constrained computational resources. The model architecture integrates biological pathway information with a Transformer encoder to improve prediction accuracy and interpretability (Fig. 1A). Detailed architectural specifications, hyperparameters, and mathematical formulations are provided in Supplementary Methods.

Briefly, input expression vectors are mapped onto Gene Ontology Biological Process (GOBP) pathways: a mask matrix of the top 5000 GOBP pathways ranked by similarity to high-variance genes restricts the data to pathway-specific genes, which are linearly transformed into pathway representations. A learnable [CLS] token is prepended and passed through a four-layer Transformer encoder (multi-head attention, feed-forward networks, layer normalization) without positional encoding, and its final representation is projected through a fully connected layer with softmax to output cell-type proportions. During training, 20% of input values are randomly masked to address cfRNA noise.

Training data generation

We generated 800 000 simulated cfRNA samples from TSP scRNA-seq reference data spanning 60 major human cell types; the Deconformer-p variant adds 6 placental trophoblast cell types from early- and late-stage placental datasets. Each sample mixed a random subset of cell types at random proportions (Supplementary Methods); 0.25% of the samples (2000) were held out as a validation set and the remainder used for training, and Smart-seq2 data were simulated to evaluate cross-platform generalization. Models were optimized with mean squared error loss and the Adam optimizer (learning rate 0.0001, batch size 64). Training details, including the scGPT-based Deconformer-gene ablation variant, are described in Supplementary Methods.

Benchmarking of pathway- versus gene-level embedding

To isolate the contribution of pathway information integration, we first compared Deconformer with its gene-level variant, Deconformer-gene. Both models were trained on the same 800 000 simulated samples and evaluated on independently generated test sets of 100 simulated samples each from 10x and Smart-seq2 data: the 10x test set shares its source cells with the training simulations but consists of newly sampled cell-type combinations and proportions, whereas the Smart-seq2 test set additionally introduces a platform shift. Deconformer was further trained with perturbed or completely random pathway masks to verify that the mask contributes meaningful co-expression structure (Supplementary Methods).

Benchmarking against established deconvolution methods

We then benchmarked Deconformer against five established deconvolution methods: BayesPrism [13], MuSiC [12], CIBERSORT [11], MIXTURE [14] and Scaden [15]. UCD [16] was not included because it does not allow customizing the reference data or the cell types to be inferred. All 10x scRNA-seq data were used as the single-cell reference, and all test mixtures were constructed from Smart-seq2 data, so that every method was evaluated in a cross-platform setting. Since CIBERSORT and MIXTURE both require a prebuilt signature matrix, each of them was run with two signature matrices constructed with the CIBERSORTx web portal (CBx) [11] and the DeconBenchmark package (DBM) [18], respectively, yielding four configurations: CBx-CIBERSORT, CBx-MIXTURE, DBM-CIBERSORT, and DBM-MIXTURE. All single-cell reference and mixture inputs were provided to every method as raw counts, satisfying the input requirements of all deconvolution tools. The usage and configuration of every deconvolution tool strictly followed the recommendations of its official documentation and guidelines, so that each tool was applied correctly and at its best-performing setup (Supplementary Methods).

To avoid any bias toward our own data-generating process, benchmark data were generated with frameworks from published benchmarking studies [18, 19] rather than the Deconformer simulation scheme, covering three steps (cell-type proportion generation: BSP, PEP, FDP, BDP; pseudo-bulk construction: homo, heter; and noise modeling: no noise, Dropout, Gaussian, NB) in all 32 combinations with 100 samples each (3200 samples; Fig. 2A, Table 1, Supplementary Methods). Following the deconvBenchmarking framework [19], the similarity between each simulated dataset and the 368 quality-controlled Shenzhen Maternity and Child Healthcare Hospital (SZMCHH) plasma cfRNA samples (see below) was quantified in terms of detected gene numbers, per-gene and pathway-level coefficients of variation, and gene–gene and sample–sample correlations. In addition, 5 single-cell-type samples were generated for each of the 52 Smart-seq2 cell types (260 samples) to evaluate cell-type identification. The combined 3460 samples were deconvolved by every method, and the agreement between inferred and true proportions was assessed using Lin’s concordance correlation coefficient (CCC) [20], root mean square error (RMSE), and Pearson’s R (Supplementary Methods).

Real-world cfRNA datasets and preprocessing

Deconformer was applied to three public cfRNA datasets (COVID-19, liver cancer, preeclampsia) and to a longitudinal prospective cohort of 233 pregnant women (389 plasma samples) from the SZMCHH. Gene-level expression was quantified and normalized to transcripts per million (TPM); samples failing RNA-detection thresholds (mRNA >10 000, lncRNA >2400, miRNA >400) were excluded (Supplementary Fig. S10). Before inference, each expression matrix was restricted to the model’s mRNA gene set and rescaled to a fixed total of 104 without log transformation. Cohort details and clinical metadata are provided in Supplementary Methods.

Downstream analysis and interpretability

Inferred cell-type compositions were used to characterize disease-specific cellular dynamics. Differential composition analysis was performed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. To assess whether the inferred cell-type compositions carry group-discriminative information, we constructed binary classification models using support vector machines (SVMs) with leave-one-out cross-validation (LOOCV), assessing performance via ROC and precision-recall curves (Supplementary Methods).

To interpret Deconformer’s decisions, we analyzed the self-attention scores of the final Transformer-encoder layer between the [CLS] token and pathway representations. Differential attention networks were constructed by calculating Cohen’s d effect sizes for pathway pairs across disease states, identifying pathway interactions that the model differentially weights between disease states (Supplementary Methods).

Statistical analysis

Benchmark accuracy was quantified using Lin’s CCC (primary metric) [20], Pearson’s R, and RMSE between inferred and true proportions. On real cfRNA datasets, between-group differences in inferred cell-type fractions were tested with two-sided Wilcoxon rank-sum tests, with Benjamini–Hochberg adjustment across all tested cell types within each dataset and rank-biserial r as the effect size. Differential [CLS] attention and inter-pathway attention were quantified with Cohen’s d, ranking pathway pairs by |d| and reporting the top 100 without formal significance testing, as this analysis is effect-size-based. Trait associations used Spearman’s R with Benjamini–Hochberg adjustment; categorical variables were compared with the Chi-square test; classification was evaluated with LOOCV and 500-bootstrap 95% CIs. Tests were two-tailed with a significance threshold of 0.05 unless otherwise indicated (Supplementary Methods).

Results

Overview of Deconformer

Deconformer is a DNN framework for cfRNA cell-type composition analysis (Fig. 1A), trained in a supervised manner on scRNA-seq-derived simulated data. Rather than operating directly on genes, the model embeds each sample’s expression profile into pathway-level representations: expression values are replicated across the rows of a pathway mask matrix, linearly projected into a shared low-dimensional space, and prepended with a learnable [CLS] token. A four-layer Transformer encoder without positional encoding then models pathway crosstalk through self-attention [21], and the updated [CLS] representation predicts cell-type composition through a multilayer perceptron (Materials and Methods). This design serves three purposes: pathway crosstalk is explicitly modeled; each pathway’s contribution is adaptively weighted per sample via attention scores; and the resulting attention weights reveal which pathways the model primarily relies on, enabling direct examination of whether its predictions engage biologically meaningful pathway knowledge. Because the ground-truth composition of real cfRNA is unobtainable, Deconformer was trained on random mixtures simulated from 60 potential cfRNA-contributing cell types, spanning arbitrary cell-type subsets and proportions, so that robust performance can be expected regardless of the true cfRNA distribution (Materials and Methods).

Before benchmarking against established tools, we first asked whether the pathway-based design itself is beneficial, through two internal evaluations: (i) a direct comparison with Deconformer-gene (Supplementary Fig. S1), an otherwise identical variant in which pathway embedding is replaced by gene-level embedding adapted from the single-cell foundation model scGPT [22]; and (ii) a perturbation analysis of the pathway mask, testing whether the encoded pathway structure, rather than any arbitrary gene grouping, drives performance.

Internal evaluation of Deconformer

Specifically, Deconformer and Deconformer-gene were compared on 100 test samples simulated from 10x data under the simulation scheme described above (Materials and Methods), with robustness further probed by randomly dropping expression values at rates of 0.1–0.5 (Fig. 1B–D, Supplementary Data S2). Deconformer achieved the best agreement with the ground truth at low dropout rates (mean CCC = 0.990, Pearson R = 0.993, RMSE = 5 × 10−3 at a dropout rate of 0) and was largely insensitive to dropout: increasing the dropout rate from 0 to 0.5 reduced the mean CCC only from 0.990 to 0.953 and the mean R from 0.993 to 0.955, with the mean RMSE rising from 5 × 10−3 to 1 × 10−2. Deconformer-gene showed nearly flat performance across all dropout rates (mean CCC ≈ 0.980) and slightly outperformed Deconformer on 10x data at dropout rates of 0.4–0.5. However, when evaluated on Smart-seq2-derived mixtures, the performance of Deconformer-gene dropped sharply (mean CCC = 0.491 at a dropout rate of 0), whereas Deconformer remained substantially more accurate (mean CCC = 0.759 at a dropout rate of 0 and 0.671 at 0.5; Fig. 1E–G, Supplementary Data S2). These results indicate that the adaptive pathway integration of Deconformer confers robustness to shifts in the distribution of the input mixtures, such as those between 10x- and Smart-seq2-derived data.

To further verify that the pathway mask contributes meaningful biological structure rather than serving as an arbitrary gene grouping, we performed progressive perturbations on the original pathway mask matrix, including a 20% reduction of non-zero values to simulate partial information loss (PartMask) and a 20% addition of random ones to mimic noise (NoisyMask), and additionally generated two completely random mask matrices with non-zero element ratios matched to the perturbed matrices (RandMask-PM and RandMask-NM) (Fig. 1H and I, Supplementary Fig. S2, Supplementary Data S2). Deconformer was trained separately with each mask matrix and evaluated on simulated data at dropout rates of 0 and 0.5 across training epochs. The original mask matrix (OrigMask) consistently achieved the best CCC and RMSE, followed by the two perturbed masks, whereas models trained with completely random masks plateaued at CCC values below 0.8 regardless of the number of training epochs, under both dropout conditions. These results show that the co-expression structure encoded in the pathway mask, rather than any generic grouping of genes, improves model performance.

Benchmarking against established deconvolution methods

Having established the contribution of pathway integration, we next benchmarked Deconformer against five established deconvolution methods (Fig. 2A, Materials and Methods). The benchmark was designed to evaluate how accurately each method infers the relative contribution of individual cell types to the plasma cfRNA pool, building on published benchmarking frameworks rather than a custom data-generating process (Discussion). Three design choices followed. First, all test mixtures were built from Smart-seq2 data with the 10x data as reference, so that training and evaluation never overlap and every method faces a cross-platform shift, a deliberately stringent setting: a method that cannot tolerate platform differences is unlikely to withstand real cfRNA variability. Second, simulated data were assembled from these frameworks [18, 19], with multiple strategies per step yielding 32 combinations of 100 samples each (Fig. 2A, Table 1, Materials and Methods). Third, all methods were run on unified compute nodes under identical software environments with dedicated resource monitoring (Table 2, Supplementary Data S3, Materials and Methods).

Because the similarity between simulated benchmark data and real cfRNA data determines whether a benchmark can reflect deconvolution performance on real-world cfRNA data, we compared each simulated dataset with the 368 quality-controlled plasma cfRNA samples from the SZMCHH cohort, following the deconvBenchmarking framework [19] (Materials and Methods). NB noise produced simulated data most similar to real cfRNA across most evaluated aspects (Supplementary Figs S3–S5; detailed results in Supplementary Methods).

We therefore focused on the eight NB-noise simulation settings. Deconformer ranked first in both CCC and Pearson’s R in seven of the eight settings (Fig. 2B, Supplementary Data S2), with a mean CCC of 0.509 compared with 0.443 for the second-best method, BayesPrism, and showed the lowest RMSE in most settings. When all 32 simulation settings were considered, Deconformer remained slightly ahead of BayesPrism in the overall ranking across CCC, Pearson’s R, and RMSE (mean CCC = 0.585 versus 0.578; first place in 17 versus 15 of 32 settings by CCC; Fig. 2C, Supplementary Fig. S6A), and both methods clearly outperformed the remaining tools. Scaden was a special case: it failed entirely on Smart-seq2-derived mixtures, outputting cell-type scores close to the uniform mean regardless of the input (mean CCC = −0.004). Its seemingly competitive RMSE (0.027) is an artifact of this collapse (predicting near-mean proportions minimizes squared error without recovering any biological signal), which illustrates why CCC and RMSE must be interpreted jointly (Supplementary Methods).

To assess cell-type identification, we generated 5 single-cell-type pseudo-bulk samples for each of the 52 Smart-seq2 cell types (Fig. 2A). Deconformer achieved the best identification performance (mean CCC = 0.906; 46 of 52 cell types with CCC > 0.8), followed by BayesPrism (mean CCC = 0.831; 40 of 52), with the mature conventional dendritic cell being the only cell type that Deconformer failed to identify (Supplementary Fig. S6B, Supplementary Data S2). We further examined the eight cell types present in the 10x reference but absent from the Smart-seq2 data (unseen cell types). All methods assigned small non-zero fractions to these non-existent cell types (mean total fraction per sample ranging from 0.037 to 0.139), and no method showed a clear advantage; the assigned fractions were relatively stable across samples, suggesting a limited impact on between-group differential analysis (Supplementary Fig. S6C, Supplementary Data S2).

Finally, the recorded computing resources provide direct evidence of the efficiency advantage of Deconformer (Table 2, Supplementary Data S3). Deconformer completed the inference of all benchmark samples in 0.16 h with a peak memory of 25.85 GB, whereas BayesPrism and MuSiC required 10.48 h and 9.64 h, and the CIBERSORT/MIXTURE runs required up to 53.41–69.56 h with peak memory exceeding 216–248 GB. In addition, Deconformer training completed in 7.25 h on a consumer-grade NVIDIA RTX 5090 GPU, demonstrating that both training and inference are feasible without data-center hardware.

Deconformer model characterizes cellular dynamics in COVID-19 severity

COVID-19 is an infectious disease caused by the Severe Acute Respiratory Syndrome Coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2). Severe COVID-19 is marked by dysregulated immune hyperactivation, notably of monocytes and neutrophils [23]. We applied Deconformer to a plasma cfRNA dataset of COVID-19 [24] and found distinct inferred cell-type contributions between mild and severe patients (Fig. 3A and B, Supplementary Data S4). Platelets were the predominant inferred contributor in both groups and were significantly lower in severe patients (mean inferred contribution 83.6% versus 66.6%, effect size r = 0.28; Fig. 3C, Supplementary Data S5), a direction consistent with the lower platelet counts reported in severe infection [25]. Monocytes and neutrophils were higher in severe patients (monocytes: r = 0.31; neutrophils: mean 0.16% versus 3.9%, r = 0.40), in agreement with the increases in these populations reported in severe COVID-19 [24, 26–28]. T cell and NK cell fractions were also higher in severe patients (T cells: mean 0.24% versus 0.75%, r = 0.38; NK cells: mean 0.57% versus 2.67%, r = 0.35). This direction differs from the decline of circulating T and NK cells reported in severe disease [29–33]. Unlike the platelet and myeloid changes above, which agree with clinical cell-count findings, cfRNA contributions can diverge from cell counts for individual cell types because they are shaped by cell-type-specific RNA release, degradation and stability in addition to cellular abundance. We therefore do not read this increase as evidence of T- and NK-cell expansion, but the altered cfRNA contributions still identify these disease-relevant populations as part of the cfRNA response in severe COVID-19. Severe patients additionally showed higher fractions of type II pneumocytes, pulmonary ionocytes, and secretory epithelial cells, and a lower fraction of ciliated cells (Fig. 3C; effect sizes r = 0.34–0.43). Such lung-related changes are biologically plausible given the pulmonary involvement of COVID-19 [34–36], although their tissue origin cannot be confirmed from cfRNA alone.

We next asked whether the pathway-level attention of Deconformer reflects biologically coherent pathway usage. Unsupervised clustering of the standardized differential [CLS] attention scores separated mild from severe samples (Fig. 3D, Supplementary Data S6). Among the top 100 pathway pairs ranked by absolute Cohen’s d, the most enriched hub was regulation of platelet activation (29 pairs), followed by RAS protein signal transduction (8 pairs), positive regulation of myoblast differentiation (7 pairs), and leukocyte aggregation (6 pairs) (Fig. 3E, Supplementary Data S7). The prominence of platelet- and leukocyte-related pathways mirrors the dominant compositional changes described above (platelets, monocytes, neutrophils, and lymphocytes), indicating that the attention analysis preferentially engages pathways related to the cell types showing the largest differences. Analogous to the mask-perturbation tests (Fig. 1H and I), we view this as evidence that the adaptive pathway-integration module exploits biological pathway knowledge.

Deconformer model identifies cellular dynamics associated with liver cancer

cfRNA has shown promise in non-invasive cancer detection [5, 37–40]. We applied Deconformer to a plasma cfRNA dataset of liver cancer patients and non-cancer controls from Tao et al. [39] and found clear differences in the inferred cell-type contributions (Fig. 4A and B, Supplementary Data S4). Platelets, the largest inferred contributor in controls, decreased markedly in patients (roughly three-fold lower mean contribution, effect size r = 0.46; Fig. 4C, Supplementary Data S5), a direction consistent with the clinical relevance of platelet alterations in liver cancer [41–43]. Multiple immune cell types (including macrophages, dendritic cells, neutrophils, basophils, mast cells, plasmablasts, and plasma cells) increased in patients (effect sizes r = 0.38–0.68, largest for macrophages and plasmablasts), which is plausible given the immune microenvironment changes reported in liver cancer [44–46]; T cells showed a nominal increase (P < .05, = .055, r = 0.28). Hepatocytes, the dominant liver-specific cell type, were also higher in patients (r = 0.61), as were several epithelial and lung-related cell types (club cell and type I pneumocytes, pulmonary ionocytes, and ciliated cells; r = 0.34–0.59) and hematopoietic stem cells. The hepatocyte change is consistent with the liver origin of the disease [47–49], while the other cross-tissue assignments should be interpreted with caution, since their tissue origin cannot be confirmed from cfRNA alone. To test whether these compositional differences carry group-discriminative information, we trained SVM classifiers with leave-one-out cross-validation: the inferred composition distinguished patients from controls (AUROC = 0.888, 95% CI 0.789–0.988; AUPRC = 0.859, 95% CI 0.709–0.957; Supplementary Fig. S7, Supplementary Data S8). Given the small cohort size and the lack of an independent validation cohort, this result does not establish the cell-type profile as a diagnostic marker; it instead indicates that the differentially contributed cell types carry disease-associated information and are biologically plausible in the context of liver cancer.

As in the COVID-19 analysis, unsupervised clustering of the standardized differential [CLS] attention scores separated patients from controls (Fig. 4D, Supplementary Data S6). Among the top 100 pathway pairs ranked by absolute Cohen’s d, 55 pairs involved the negative regulation of lipase activity, 14 the smooth muscle cell apoptotic process, and 5 the serine family amino acid catabolic process (Fig. 4E, Supplementary Data S7). The dominant lipase-activity hub is biologically plausible in the context of liver biology, where lipid metabolism is a prominent and extensively studied process [50]. These attention patterns are associational, but together with the mask-perturbation experiments (Fig. 1H and I), they indicate that the adaptive pathway-integration module engages group-relevant pathway information.

Deconformer-p model traces changes of trophoblasts and characterizes preeclampsia

cfRNA is also a promising non-invasive biomarker for tracking placental development and predicting pregnancy-complication risk [9, 10]. Thus, we integrated single-cell transcriptomic data of first- and third-trimester placental trophoblasts (Supplementary Fig. S8A) into our reference single-cell datasets, followed by resimulation and retraining of Deconformer to establish Deconformer-p, a model capable of tracing placental signals (Materials and Methods). We evaluated its capacity to distinguish transcriptionally similar early- and late-stage trophoblast populations by generating simulated samples each containing a single trophoblast cell type: the inferred fractions of the true cell types exceeded those of their most similar counterparts by roughly three orders of magnitude, indicating that Deconformer-p can resolve these closely related populations (Supplementary Fig. S8B).

As preeclampsia (PE) is partly caused by placental dysfunction, we applied Deconformer-p to a plasma cfRNA dataset comprising early-onset PE (EOPE) and late-onset PE (LOPE) pregnancies and their matched controls [51]. The inferred cellular origin profiles were, as anticipated, less dramatically altered than in COVID-19 or liver cancer (Fig. 5A, Supplementary Data S4); the third-trimester syncytiotrophoblast (late SCT) contributed ~7%–12% of cfRNA in these pregnancies. Because each subgroup comparison involved only 12 cases and 12 controls, Benjamini–Hochberg adjustment across the 66 tested cell types is powered to detect only near-complete group separations (effect size ; see Supplementary Methods), and no cell type passed this threshold (Supplementary Data S5). We therefore report nominal P values together with effect sizes, interpret the differing cell types as exploratory signals.

Nominally significant cell types were more frequent in EOPE than in LOPE (10 versus 1 of 66; Fig. 5B, Supplementary Data S5), consistent with the more severe placental pathology typically associated with EOPE. In EOPE, the fraction of first-trimester syncytiotrophoblasts (early SCT) was increased (P = .012, effect size r = 0.51), a change that is biologically plausible given the central role of syncytiotrophoblast stress and senescence in EOPE [52, 53]. Neutrophils were increased (P = .033, r = 0.44) and platelets decreased (P = .020, r = 0.47), directions consistent with immune and vascular alterations reported in PE [54–57]. Seven additional cell types (intestinal epithelial progenitor cells, intestinal enterocytes, intrahepatic cholangiocytes, mesothelial cells, smooth muscle cells, lung ciliated cells, and salivary gland cells) also differed (P < .05, r = 0.45–0.65); such changes are broadly compatible with systemic involvement in EOPE [9, 58, 59], although their tissue origins cannot be verified from cfRNA alone. In LOPE, only T cells differed (P = .014, r = 0.50), close to the number expected by chance among 66 tests, and this single signal should therefore be regarded as tentative. SVM classifiers evaluated by LOOCV distinguished cases from controls in both subgroups (EOPE: AUROC = 0.875, 95% CI 0.714–1.000, AUPRC = 0.808; LOPE: AUROC = 0.813, 95% CI 0.629–0.996, AUPRC = 0.765; Supplementary Fig. S9A, Supplementary Data S8); given the small cohorts and the lack of independent validation, this indicates only that the differing cell types carry group-discriminative information, not diagnostic utility. Unsupervised clustering of the standardized differential [CLS] attention scores likewise separated cases from controls in both subgroups (Fig. 5C, Supplementary Data S6). Finally, the top 100 differential pathway pairs were dominated by negative regulation of epithelial cell differentiation and response to catecholamine in EOPE, and by regulation of tolerance induction in LOPE (Supplementary Fig. S9B and C, Supplementary Data S7).

Deconformer-p model tracks cellular dynamics during pregnancy

To evaluate whether Deconformer-p could capture gestational dynamics, we recruited 233 pregnant individuals at SZMCHH, collecting 389 peripheral blood samples at different gestational stages, and analyzed their plasma cfRNA. After quality control and pipeline analysis, the expression profiles of these samples were analyzed using Deconformer-p (Fig. 6A, Supplementary Fig. S10, Supplementary Table S1, Supplementary Data S4, Materials and Methods). The inferred cell-type components showed gestational changes across tissues and organs (Fig. 6B, Supplementary Fig. S11, and Supplementary Data S10). The components of early extravillous trophoblasts (EVT; Spearman’s R = −0.31, = 7.6 × 10−8), syncytiotrophoblasts (SCT; R = −0.15, = 7.7 × 10−3), and villous cytotrophoblasts (VCT; R = −0.14, = 9.9 × 10−3) decreased progressively during gestation, while late EVT (R = 0.17, = 6.8 × 10−3) and SCT (R = 0.13, = 2.4 × 10−2) fractions showed corresponding increases, demonstrating Deconformer’s capability to resolve placental developmental trajectories through maternal cfRNA profiling. Notably, late VCT fractions did not show the expected upward trend, possibly reflecting the higher transcriptomic similarity between early and late VCT populations (Supplementary Fig. S8A). Fractions of B cells, NK cells, macrophages, innate lymphoid cells, plasmablasts, erythroid progenitors, and myeloid progenitors all declined over gestation, consistent with the systemic immune adaptation described in normal pregnancy [60, 61]. The gestational changes of T-cell, endothelial-cell, and platelet fractions were in the same direction as those reported in a previous maternal cfRNA study [9].

As an orthogonal check that the inferred fractions carry measurable physiological signal, we compared them with routine clinical laboratory tests performed within 15 days of sampling (Fig. 6C, Supplementary Data S11). Hepatocyte fractions correlated positively with serum alanine aminotransferase (ALT; Spearman’s correlation coefficient R = 0.33, = 1.6 × 10−4) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST; R = 0.34, = 1.2 × 10−4), the standard clinical markers of hepatocellular injury, but not with total protein ( = 0.24). Neutrophil fractions correlated positively with the absolute neutrophil count (R = 0.18, = 0.044) and neutrophil percentage (R = 0.19, = 0.044), with a similar but nonsignificant trend for white blood cell count ( = 0.075). These correlations support the interpretation that Deconformer-p fractions carry signals related to independently measurable properties of peripheral physiology.

Deconformer-p model characterizes gestational diabetes mellitus

To further evaluate whether Deconformer-p detects cell-type-specific changes associated with pregnancy complications, we defined gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) cases and controls in the SZMCHH cohort by the 24–28-week oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT), yielding 63 cfRNA samples from 35 GDM women and 212 samples from 129 controls, with comparable sampling ages within each trimester (Wilcoxon P > .37; Fig. 6D and E, Supplementary Fig. S12A, Supplementary Tables S1 and S2, Materials and Methods). Comparisons of the 66 cell-type fractions within each trimester (two-sided Wilcoxon; Fig. 6F, Supplementary Data S5) identified no cell type passing Benjamini–Hochberg adjustment (smallest = 0.29), indicating that GDM-associated compositional shifts are subtle; we therefore report nominal P values with rank-biserial effect sizes and interpret them as exploratory signals.

Late EVT fractions were increased in the first trimester (P = 1.9 × 10−3, effect size r = 0.33) and early SCT fractions decreased in the second (P = .024, r = 0.23), directions consistent with altered placental development reported in GDM [62–65]. Pancreatic islet cell fractions were decreased in both the first and second trimesters (P = .043 and .026, r = 0.22–0.23), the clearest recurring signal and biologically plausible given the impaired β-cell compensation associated with GDM [66, 67]. Pancreatic acinar, skeletal muscle, hematopoietic stem, plasma, duodenum glandular and salivary gland cells, and third-trimester hepatocytes also differed nominally (|r| = 0.20–0.32; Supplementary Data S5).

An SVM classifier based on the 66 first-trimester fractions, evaluated by LOOCV, yielded an AUROC of 0.861 (95% CI 0.783–0.938) and an AUPRC of 0.48 (Supplementary Fig. S12B, Supplementary Data S8); given the small sample size and the lack of independent validation, this indicates only that the inferred composition carries GDM-related information, not diagnostic utility. First-trimester risk scores correlated weakly with second-trimester OGTT measurements (Spearman R = 0.24–0.27, = 0.031 for OGTT-0 h, −1 h and -AUC; Supplementary Fig. S12C, Supplementary Data S11), and again differed between groups, including platelet activation, leukocyte cell–cell adhesion, and vitamin D metabolism (Supplementary Fig. S12D–F, Supplementary Data S7).

Discussion

Deconformer was developed to address a practical challenge in cfRNA analysis: estimating cell-type contributions across a broad range of potential cellular sources while keeping computation feasible for routine use. Instead of operating directly on individual genes, Deconformer embeds each expression profile through a GOBP pathway mask, models pathway-level interactions with a Transformer encoder, and uses the [CLS] representation to infer the relative contribution of each cell type to the plasma cfRNA pool. This design is intended not to recover absolute cellular abundance in vivo, nor to infer an anatomical cfRNA-origin signature, but to provide a scalable and biologically informed representation of cfRNA composition for between-sample comparison.

We evaluated Deconformer in two ways. First, controlled internal comparisons showed that the pathway-integration module contributes to model performance: Deconformer outperformed the gene-level variant Deconformer-gene under cross-platform evaluation, and perturbing or randomizing the pathway mask consistently reduced performance. These results indicate that the pathway mask is not merely an arbitrary dimensionality-reduction device, but provides useful biological structure for prediction (Fig. 1). Second, in benchmarks against established deconvolution methods, Deconformer achieved the most consistent performance on cross-platform Smart-seq2-derived mixtures, particularly under negative-binomial noise settings that best reproduced several statistical properties of real plasma cfRNA. Across all simulation settings, its accuracy was close to that of BayesPrism, while its runtime and memory requirements were substantially lower. Thus, the main advantage of Deconformer is not only accuracy, but also the combination of competitive accuracy, broad cell-type coverage, and markedly improved computational efficiency.

The real cfRNA analyses provide additional evidence that the inferred fractions capture biologically associational variation. In pregnancy, Deconformer-p recapitulated known gestational trends, including the transition from early to late trophoblast signals and broader immune changes during gestation [9, 10]. The inferred fractions also showed concordance with independent clinical measurements: hepatocyte fractions correlated with ALT and AST, and neutrophil fractions correlated with neutrophil counts. These orthogonal associations support the physiological relevance of the inferred cfRNA contributions. In disease cohorts, Deconformer identified compositional shifts associated with COVID-19 severity, liver cancer, preeclampsia, and gestational diabetes. These shifts are associational rather than mechanistic or diagnostic: they were robust after multiple-testing correction in COVID-19 and liver cancer, and remain exploratory (nominal significance with effect sizes) in the smaller preeclampsia and gestational diabetes cohorts.

Taken together, four properties distinguish Deconformer from existing tools. First, efficiency: inference incurs negligible cost on standard hardware and training is feasible on a consumer-grade GPU (Table 2), so that 60-plus-cell-type analysis no longer trades coverage against runtime. Second, rigor of evaluation: the accuracy advantage was obtained on out-of-domain, cross-platform mixtures generated by independent benchmarking frameworks, with the noise model best reproducing 368 real plasma cfRNA samples and every method run under its officially recommended configuration. Third, tested interpretability: the contribution of the pathway-integration module was verified by controlled ablation and perturbation (Fig. 1), and attention analyses across four clinical cohorts further showed that the model consistently engages group-relevant pathways, indicating that the module’s predictions are associated with biologically organized pathway information. Fourth, usability: pretrained models, command-line tools, and step-by-step guides allow new users to go from installation to a first analysis in about 10 min on an ordinary laptop (Table 3). Together, these properties make panoramic cell-type composition analysis of cfRNA practical as a routine assay rather than a specialized undertaking.

Why Deconformer tolerates these challenging conditions warrants comment. Conventional deconvolution methods remain powerful when the reference and target mixtures are closely matched [68, 69], but plasma cfRNA represents a more diffuse and noisy mixture whose potential sources span many tissues and cell types, and marker-based discrimination can deteriorate as candidate cell types become increasingly similar [70]. By aggregating gene-level expression into pathway-level representations and learning how to combine these signals across samples, Deconformer reduces reliance on individual marker genes while retaining biologically organized prior structure. The controlled comparison with Deconformer-gene and the pathway-mask perturbation experiments support the contribution of this pathway structure, while the cross-platform benchmark shows competitive accuracy at substantially lower runtime and memory cost than established methods. Together, these design choices explain how Deconformer maintains competitive accuracy across broad cell-type panels while keeping computation practical; its outputs should still be interpreted as relative cfRNA contribution rather than absolute cellular abundance.

Despite the advantages above, several boundaries of the present work should be noted. Deconformer estimates relative cfRNA contributions, not absolute cellular abundance, because plasma cfRNA is shaped by cell-type-specific RNA release, degradation, stability, and technical factors [1]. The current training simulations model technical noise with dropout only and do not fully reproduce the distributional properties of real cfRNA. All analyses also rely on a single reference atlas (TSP) with a specific cell-merging scheme [7] whose influence has not been systematically tested. Deconformer does not explicitly correct batch effects, so inferred compositions may reflect technical differences between studies as well as biology [71]. For this reason, the present analyses were performed within individual cohorts against their own controls, and direct cross-cohort comparisons should be avoided without appropriate correction. In addition, the classification analyses were based on small cohorts and leave-one-out cross-validation without independent validation, and the attention analyses remain associational rather than mechanistic.

These boundaries point to several directions for future work. A central need for the field is the development of ground-truth cfRNA materials or experimental spike-in systems with known cellular contributions, which would allow evaluation beyond simulation-based benchmarks. Future versions of Deconformer could also incorporate more realistic cfRNA noise models, test alternative reference atlases and cell-type merging schemes, and integrate additional priors such as tissue-specific regulatory or epigenetic information [72]. Larger independent cohorts will be required to validate the disease-associated composition patterns reported here.

As cfRNA assays and processing pipelines continue to standardize, Deconformer provides a practical foundation for routine panoramic cfRNA composition analysis: it is efficient enough to run on standard hardware, broad enough to cover the major cellular sources of plasma cfRNA, and supported by pretrained models, a concise practical guide (Table 3), detailed Supplementary Methods and a continuously updated project repository, enabling the community to readily adopt, evaluate, and extend the framework.
