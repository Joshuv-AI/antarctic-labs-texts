Density fluctuation - Mach number scaling in compressible, high plasma beta turbulence: in-situ space observations and high-Reynolds number simulations
Riddhi Bandyopadhyay †, James R. Beattie ‡, Amitava Bhattacharjee

Abstract
Understanding the nature of compressible fluctuations in a broad range of turbulent plasmas, from the intracluster medium to the solar wind, has been an active field of research in the past decades. Theoretical frameworks for weakly compressible MHD turbulence in an inhomogeneous background magnetic field predict a linear scaling of the normalized mass density fluctuation ( δ ​ ρ / ρ 0 \delta\rho/\rho_{0} ), as a function of the turbulent Mach number ( ℳ t \mathcal{M}_{t} ), δ ​ ρ / ρ 0 ∝ ℳ t \delta\rho/\rho_{0}\propto\mathcal{M}_{t} . However, so far the scaling relation has been tested only using moderate to low plasma beta ( β ≲ 1 \beta\lesssim 1 ) solar wind observational data where the compressibility is weak δ ​ ρ / ρ 0 ∼ 0.1 \delta\rho/\rho_{0}\sim 0.1 . Here, we combine NASA’s Magnetospheric Multiscale Mission data in Earth’s magnetosheath, where β ∼ 10 \beta\sim 10 is high, and β ∼ 1 \beta\sim 1 highly-compressible magnetohydrodynamic turbulence simulations at unprecedented resolutions. Both show that δ ​ ρ / ρ 0 ∝ ℳ t \delta\rho/\rho_{0}\propto\mathcal{M}_{t} holds across a broad range of δ ​ ρ / ρ 0 \delta\rho/\rho_{0} , ℳ t \mathcal{M}_{t} and β \beta , demonstrating that δ ​ ρ / ρ 0 ∝ ℳ t \delta\rho/\rho_{0}\propto\mathcal{M}_{t} is a robust compressible turbulence relation, going beyond the asymptotics of the weakly compressible theory. We discuss the findings in the context of understanding the nature of strongly compressible turbulent fluctuations and the driving parameter in astrophysical and space plasmas.

I
Introduction

Most of the visible Universe is made of ionized plasmas, and most naturally occurring plasmas exist in a turbulent state
[
50
]
, ranging from the interstellar medium
[
21
,
12
,
4
]
, intracluster medium
[
54
,
45
]
to solar wind and planetary magnetospheres
[
79
]
. Turbulent fluctuations in plasmas have important effects on heating, transport, and overall evolution and structure in these systems
[
76
,
1
]
. An important parameter controlling the dynamics in turbulent plasmas is the plasma beta
\beta
, defined as

\beta=\frac{n\,k_{B}\,T}{B^{2}/\left(2\,\mu_{0}\right)},

(1)

where
n
is the plasma number density,
k_{B}
the Boltzmann constant,
T
is the temperature,
B
is the magnetic field, and
\mu_{0}
the vacuum permeability. Physically,
\beta
is the ratio of the thermal pressure, to the magnetic pressure
P_{\rm mag}
. Although the interplanetary solar wind is mostly characterized by
\beta\sim 1
, high-beta plasmas
(\beta\gg 1)
are prevalent in many astrophysical environments, e.g., in the warmer phases of the interstellar medium
[
28
]
and the intracluster medium
[
45
]
, where
\beta\gg 1
plays an essential role in facilitating the conditions for mirror and firehose instabilities.

For an isothermal plasma,
\beta=2c_{s}^{2}/v_{A}^{2}
where
c_{s}
is the sound speed, and
v_{A}
the Alfvén speed. Hence, in some ways,
\beta
, which controls the speed of the fast wave relative to the Alfvén speed, characterizing how compressible a plasma is. The properties and origins of compressible fluctuations in turbulent space plasmas are still not well understood
[
40
,
41
,
70
,
19
]
. Density fluctuation
(\delta\rho=\left\langle(\rho-\left\langle\rho\right\rangle)^{2}\right\rangle^{1/2})
in the solar wind is typically small compared to background density
(\rho_{0}=\langle\rho\rangle)
. Consequently, theoretical frameworks
[
48
,
49
,
82
]
have been developed for small-amplitude compressive fluctuations
(\delta\rho/\rho_{0}\ll 1)
, assuming a small turbulent Mach number, which is defined

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{t}=\frac{\left\langle(u-\left\langle u\right\rangle)^{2}\right\rangle^{1/2}}{\left\langle c_{s}\right\rangle}=\frac{\delta u}{c_{s}},

(2)

with
\delta u
as the rest-frame rms velocity fluctuation and
c_{s}
as the mean sound speed. For a homogeneous background field, the nearly-incompressible MHD (NI-MHD) theories predict that the normalized density fluctuations scale with the square of
\mathcal{M}_{t}
,

\displaystyle\delta\rho/\rho_{0}\propto\mathcal{M}_{t}^{2}.

(3)

However, a further generalization of NI-MHD theory, referred to here as the weakly compressible MHD theory for the case of an inhomogeneous background field
[
10
,
9
,
37
]
predicts a linear relationship of normalized density fluctuations scaling with
\mathcal{M}_{t}
,

\displaystyle\delta\rho/\rho_{0}\propto\mathcal{M}_{t}.

(4)

For supersonic turbulence,
\mathcal{M}_{t}\gg 1
, ubiquitous in the star-forming, colder phases of the interstellar medium
[
44
]
, with large magnetic field
[
5
]
and mass density inhomogeneities
[
23
,
3
]
, a further model has been proposed, based on the Rankine-Hugoniot shock-jump conditions. For isothermal, hydrodynamic shocks
Padoan et al. [58]
and
Passot & Vázquez-Semadeni [61]
showed that the density variance can be related to
\mathcal{M}_{t}
by

\displaystyle\delta\rho/\rho_{0}=b\mathcal{M}_{t},

(5)

where
b
is the so-called ‘driving parameter,’ which depends nonlinearly on how the turbulence is driven, i.e., if energy is injected with compressible modes (
b=1
for purely
|\bm{\nabla}\times\bm{u}|=0
modes) or incompressible modes (
b=1/3
for purely
\bm{\nabla}\cdot\bm{u}=0
modes)
[
25
]
. This was later generalized to high-
\beta
shocks in
Molina et al. [55]
, non-isothermal shocks in
Nolan et al. [56]
and low-
\beta
shocks in
Beattie et al. [6]
.

Numerous studies have been conducted to verify the relation of density fluctuation and turbulent Mach number, using both observational space and astrophysical data
[
49
,
75
,
2
,
27
,
51
,
52
,
69
,
33
,
17
,
83
]
and numerical simulations
[
58
,
61
,
42
,
25
,
63
,
15
,
56
,
59
,
53
,
6
,
18
,
19
]
, and mostly support the linear relation (
Equation 4
and
Equation 5
). Observationally in space plasmas, to the best of our knowledge, the
\delta\rho-\mathcal{M}_{t}
scaling relations have not been tested for high-
\beta
regime, and nor have they been tested on a scale-by-scale basis in simulations, far away from the modes that drive the turbulence. Our goal in this work is to then further examine the scaling relation between
\delta\rho-\mathcal{M}_{t}
for high-beta plasma. To test this, we utilize
in-situ
data in Earth’s magnetosheath, where the effect of spatial inhomogeneities is strong and the typical
\beta\sim 10
. Along with the
in-situ
data, we make use of an unprecedentedly high-resolution MHD simulation at Reynolds number
\rm{Re}\gtrsim 10^{6}
and
\beta\sim 1
. Regardless of
\beta
, within
1\sigma
uncertainties, both data support a linear scaling
\delta\rho/\rho_{0}\propto\mathcal{M}_{t}
, even sharing (statistically) the same proportionality constants. This potentially demonstrates the importance of inhomogeneous fluctuations in compressible, turbulent plasmas, and supports the weakly compressible MHD turbulence theory in
Bhattacharjee et al. [10]
.

This paper is organized as follows. In
section II
we discuss the observational and simulation data that we use in this study. In
section III
we calculate scale-dependent
\delta\rho/\rho_{0}
and
\mathcal{M}_{t}
curves from the simulation and combine them with the observational data to reveal that
\delta\rho/\rho_{0}\propto\mathcal{M}_{t}
over a broad range of
\delta\rho/\rho_{0}
and
\mathcal{M}_{t}
, regardless of
\beta
. Finally, in
section IV
we discuss the implications our results have for weakly compressible MHD theory and space and astrophysical plasmas, and in particular driving parameter measurements that may be contaminated by cascade effects.

II
Methods

II.1
MMS Observations

We use
in-situ
data collected by NASA’s Magnetospheric Multiscale (MMS) mission
[
13
,
14
]
in Earth’s magnetosheath. The magnetosheath consists of the shocked solar wind plasma downstream of the bow shock. Unlike the interplanetary solar wind, the magnetosheath plasma has a high
\beta
and is relatively compressible. The MMS consists of four identical spacecraft, but here we do not utilize any multi-spacecraft technique. For each interval, we average over the values obtained from four spacecraft. We make use of the ion and electron plasma moments from the Fast Plasma Instrument
[
62
]
. We use ion velocity and temperature data to evaluate
\mathcal{M}_{t}
. But, whenever available, we use the electron number density to measure background density and density fluctuation. This is because the number density of electrons and ions are almost equal due to the quasi-neutrality of the plasma, and due to a larger thermal speed the electron density measurements are usually more accurate
[
34
]
. Magnetic field data are obtained from the fluxgate magnetometer (FGM)
[
65
,
74
]
and combined with the plasma moments to obtain
\beta
values. For example, we show the burst resolution MMS data (MMS1) obtained in the turbulent magnetosheath on 2017 January 18 from 00:45:53 to 00:47:43 UTC in
Figure 1
. The interval shows strong turbulent fluctuations with considerable density variations throughout the interval. The interval has
\beta\approx 19
.

We eliminate intervals with very low average number density
n_{e}<5
cm
-3
, or very high average density
n_{e}>50
cm
-3
because of larger uncertainties in such intervals. The length of every interval spans many times that of the typical correlation length (equivalently time) of the magnetosheath, which is about
\tau_{c}\sim 100
s
[
72
, e.g.,]
.

II.2
Numerical Simulation

MHD plasma model

Along with the MMS observational data, we use a heavily modified version of the magnetohydrodynamical (MHD) code
flash

[
32
,
20
]
that has recently been run on
10,\!080^{3}
grid scales
[
4
]
. Our code uses a highly-optimized, hybrid-precision
[
24
]
, positivity-preserving, second-order MUSCL-Hancock HLL5R Riemann scheme
[
11
,
81
]
to solve the compressible, ideal, MHD fluid equations in three dimensions,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho+\nabla\cdot\left(\rho\bm{u}\right)=0,

(6)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\!\left(\rho\bm{u}\right)+\nabla\cdot\left(\rho\bm{u}\!\otimes\!\bm{u}+p\mathbb{I}-\frac{1}{\mu_{0}}\bm{b}\!\otimes\!\bm{b}\right)=\rho\bm{f},

(7)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\bm{b}+\nabla\cdot(\bm{u}\otimes\bm{b}-\bm{b}\otimes\bm{u})=0,

(8)

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot\bm{b}=0,

(9)

\displaystyle p=c_{s}^{2}\rho+\frac{1}{2\mu_{0}}\bm{b}\cdot\bm{b},

(10)

where
\rho
,
\bm{u}
,
\bm{b}
and
\mu_{0}
are the gas density, the velocity and magnetic fields, and the magnetic permittivity, respectively. Equation
10
relates the scalar pressure
p
to
\rho
via the isothermal equation of state with constant sound speed
c_{s}
, as well as the pressure contribution from the magnetic field. We work in units
c_{s}=\rho_{0}=\mu_{0}=L=1
, where
\rho_{0}
is the mean gas density and
L
is the characteristic length scale of the system, such that
L^{3}=\mathcal{V}=1
is the volume. We discretize the equations over a triply periodic domain of
[-L/2,L/2]
in each dimension, with grid resolution
10,\!080^{3}
– the largest grid in the world for simulations of this fluid turbulence regime. In order to drive turbulence, a turbulent forcing term
\bm{f}
is applied in the momentum equation (details below).

Our numerical model is an implicit large eddy simulation (ILES), which relies upon the spatial discretisation to supply the numerical viscosity and resistivity as a fluid closure model.
Malvadi Shivakumar &
Federrath [47]
provide a detailed characterization of the numerical viscous and resistive properties of this solver by comparing the ILES model with direct numerical simulations (DNS), which have explicit viscous and resistive operators. They derived empirical models for transforming grid resolution. Using their relations, for our resolution,
N_{\rm grid}=10,\!080
,
\mathrm{Re}\in[1.4\times 10^{6},5.3\times 10^{6}]
and
\mathrm{Rm}\in[1\times 10^{6},4.5\times 10^{6}]
.

Turbulent driving

We drive the turbulence with a turbulent Mach number of
u_{0}/c_{s}=\mathcal{M}_{t}=4.32\pm 0.18\approx 4
. This enables us to obtain scaling results in regimes where the simulation overlaps with both NI-MHD and weakly compressible MHD models, but also covers regimes in which the asymptotic approximations tend to break down. We apply a non-helical stochastic forcing term
\bm{f}
in Equation
7
, following an Ornstein-Uhlenbeck stochastic process
[
22
,
68
,
25
]
, using the
TurbGen
turbulent forcing module
[
25
,
26
]
. The forcing is constructed in Fourier space such that kinetic energy is injected at the smallest wavenumbers, peaking at
\ell_{0}^{-1}=k_{0}L/2\pi=2
and tending to zero parabolically in the interval
1\leq kL/2\pi\leq 3
. To replenish the large-scale compressible modes and shocks, we decompose
\bm{f}
into its incompressible (
\nabla\cdot\bm{f}=0
) and compressible (
|\nabla\times\bm{f}|=0
) modes
[
25
]
, and drive the turbulence with equal amounts of energy in each of the modes.

We set the correlation time of
\bm{f}
to
t_{0}=\ell_{0}/u_{0}
, such that the correlation time and turnover time of the largest eddy are equal. We drive the simulation into a statistically steady state, such that all of the first moments of underlying field variables no longer vary on average. We sample the turbulence 20 times across an
2t_{0}
interval, averaging all statistics (e.g.,
\mathcal{M}_{t}
and
\delta\rho/\rho_{0}
spectra, as discussed in
section III
) to ensure that our results are not sensitive to any intermittent events. We refer to
Beattie et al. [4]
for further information about the integral quantities and the time-evolution of the simulation.

Initial conditions & steady-state magnetic field

We initialize
\rho(x,y,z)=\rho_{0}
and
\bm{u}=\bm{0}
. For our simulations,
B_{0}=0
, and only the turbulent
\delta\bm{b}
remains.
\delta\bm{b}
is maintained in a statistically stationary state via the turbulent dynamo
[
67
,
64
,
43
]
. The saturated state of our magnetic fields results in an Alfvén Mach number,
\mathcal{M}_{\rm A}=u_{0}/\left\langle{v_{A}^{2}}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{V}}^{1/2}=2.03\pm 0.04\approx 2
, where
\left\langle{v_{A}^{2}}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{V}}^{1/2}
is the rms Alfvén velocity. On volume-average, this provides
\beta\sim 1/8\sim 1
.

Results

For each magnetosheath interval, we calculate the relative density fluctuation and turbulent Mach number,
Equation 2
. We approximately use 1200 magnetosheath intervals, creating a joint probability distribution function between
\delta\rho/\rho_{0}
and
\mathcal{M}_{t}

p(\mathcal{M}_{t},\delta\rho/\rho_{0})

\delta\rho/\rho_{0}=\theta_{1}\mathcal{M}_{t}^{\theta_{0}}
to the data and determine that the best fit parameters yield

\displaystyle\delta\rho/\rho_{0}=(0.83^{+0.54}_{-0.58})\mathcal{M}_{t}^{0.92^{+0.30}_{-0.29}},

\displaystyle(\text{MMS data)}

(11)

consistent within
1\sigma
to the
\delta\rho/\rho_{0}\propto\mathcal{M}_{t}
relation predicted by the weakly compressible MHD turbulence model
[
10
]
, even in regimes where the asymptotic expansion in powers of the
\mathcal{M}_{t}

Appendix A
.

For the simulation, we calculate the scale-dependent
\mathcal{M}_{t}
and
\rho/\rho_{0}
directly from the one-dimensional power spectrum of
\bm{u}
and
\rho/\rho_{0}-1
. For example, the
\bm{u}
spectrum is,

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{u}(k)=\int\operatorname{d}\!{\Omega_{k}}\;4\pi k^{2}\bm{u}(\bm{k})\bm{u}^{\dagger}(\bm{k}),

(12)

where
\Omega_{k}
is the integral over solid angle (see
Beattie et al. 4
for more information on
\mathcal{P}_{u}(k)
), such that the one-dimensional spectrum has a normalization,

\displaystyle\left\langle{u^{2}}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{V}}=\int\operatorname{d}\!{k}\;\mathcal{P}_{u}(k),

(13)

from Parseval’s theorem. Hence, the scale-dependent
\mathcal{M}_{t}
is simply,

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{t}(\ell/L)=\frac{1}{c_{s}}\left(\int_{k}^{k_{u}}\operatorname{d}\!{k^{\prime}}\;\mathcal{P}_{u}(k^{\prime})\right)^{1/2},

(14)

where
k_{u}
is the microscale, or inner scale of the turbulence, which we define directly from the spectrum

\displaystyle k_{u}=\left(\frac{1}{\left\langle{u^{2}}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{V}}}\int\operatorname{d}\!{k}\;k^{2}\mathcal{P}_{u}(k)\right)^{1/2},

(15)

such that the integral is not contaminated with modes in the viscous dissipation range. We similarly construct the scale-dependent rms for the mass density,

\displaystyle\frac{\delta\rho(\ell/L)}{\rho_{0}}=\left\langle\left(\frac{\rho(\ell/L)}{\rho_{0}}\right)^{2}\right\rangle=\left(\int_{k}^{k_{u}}\operatorname{d}\!{k^{\prime}}\;\mathcal{P}_{\rho/\rho_{0}-1}(k^{\prime})\right)^{1/2}.

(16)

Plotting
Equation 16
as a function of
Equation 14
allows us to directly compute the rms mass density as a function of
\mathcal{M}_{t}
for each
\ell/L
in the simulation. This process is equivalent to splitting the domain with volume
\mathcal{V}
into sub-domains
\mathcal{V}=\bigcup_{n}\mathcal{V}_{n}
and computing the rms for each collection of
\mathcal{V}_{n}
. Performing this analysis for all
\mathcal{V}_{n}
means that instead of running multiple simulations with different plasma parameters (e.g., different
\mathcal{M}
, as in
Beattie & Federrath 3
,
Beattie et al. 7
), we can use our single highly-resolved simulation to study multiple combinations of
\delta\rho/\rho_{0}
and
\mathcal{M}_{t}
. This is only possible with very high-resolution simulation data, with large amounts of dynamical range within the turbulence cascade, and allows us to test how robust
\delta\rho/\rho_{0}\propto\mathcal{M}_{t}
is, far away from the
k
modes influenced by the driving mechanism.

The computed
\delta\rho/\rho_{0}
and
\mathcal{M}_{t}
values are plotted with red, open markers in
Figure 3
. Using the same maximum likelihood fitting process and model as in the MMS data, we find

\displaystyle\delta\rho/\rho_{0}=(0.97^{+0.23}_{-0.23})\mathcal{M}_{t}^{0.94^{+0.15}_{-0.15}},

\displaystyle(\text{simulation data)}

(17)

again showing that the simulation data is 1
\sigma
consistent with the weakly compressible model, with a constant that is very close to unity. We discuss the proportionality constant further in
section IV
, and show the corner plots for the fits in
Appendix A
.

Both observation and simulation results shown here follow closely the scaling prediction from weakly compressible MHD theory with an inhomogeneous background field. Note that this is independent of the underlying
\beta
(
\beta\sim 1
for the simulation and
\beta\sim 10
for the MMS data), suggesting that the prediction of the weakly compressible MHD theory is robust with respect to
\beta

IV
Discussion & Conclusions

Due to the weak
\delta\rho/\rho_{0}
, the interplanetary solar wind behaves very close to an incompressible fluid. Indeed, until the recent advent of Parker Solar Probe
[
31
, PSP;]
data in the near-Sun solar wind, the relative amplitude of
\delta\rho/\rho_{0}
has, for the majority of cases, remained much smaller than unity
[
49
, e.g.,]
. Therefore, weakly compressible MHD is usually adequate to describe the interplanetary solar wind fluctuations. However, in several astrophysical settings, where
in-situ
measurements are not yet feasible, the compressive fluctuations can be significant, e.g.,
\delta\rho/\rho_{0}
varies by many orders of magnitude, as is the case in our simulations. Furthermore,
\beta
is another parameter which is limited to a rather narrow band of values in the solar wind, but could be large in many astrophysical plasmas. Earth’s magnetosheath provides an excellent natural laboratory to test high
\beta
, strongly compressive plasma environment using
in-situ
data
[
66
]
.

The proportionality constant,
b
, from
Equation 5
, has been measured in a number of galactic
[
52
, e.g.,]
and even extra-galactic sources
[
69
,
33
, e.g.,]
. In
Figure 3
we show a very interesting effect. By measuring
\delta\rho/\rho_{0}\propto\mathcal{M}_{t}
across a broad range of scales in the turbulence (red markers) we find
\delta\rho/\rho_{0}\approx 1/3\mathcal{M}_{t}
on the outer scale (
b=1/3)
, indicating incompressible driving, and then it changes to
\delta\rho/\rho_{0}\approx\mathcal{M}_{t}
(
b=1)
on smaller scales (smaller
\mathcal{M}_{t}
and
\delta\rho/\rho_{0}
). This value of
b
is theorized to be a signature of when the driving mechanism (
\bm{f}
in
Equation 7
) for the turbulent fluctuations is compressive. This means that deep within the cascade
\delta\rho/\rho_{0}=\mathcal{M}_{t}
, regardless of the nature of
\bm{f}
(a mix of compressible and incompressible modes in our simulation). Based on the compressible / incompressible mode decomposition for the simulation in
Beattie et al. [4]
, on the scales that
\delta\rho/\rho_{0}=\mathcal{M}_{t}
the turbulence is dominated by incompressible modes, so this demonstrates that even if the momentum modes are mostly incompressible, if one measures
b
deep in the cascade, where there is no driving source, one gets the compressible-driving relation
\delta\rho/\rho_{0}=\mathcal{M}_{t}
.

In this paper, we investigate the scaling of
\delta\rho/\rho_{0}
with the turbulent Mach number in high
\beta
, highly-compressible regime. This has been accomplished using a large number of MMS measurements (over 1200 intervals) in Earth’s magnetosheath and an unprecedentedly high-resolution

Maximum Likelihood Fits

We fit the simple model
\delta\rho/\rho_{0}=\theta_{1}\mathcal{M}_{t}^{\theta_{0}}\iff\log_{10}\delta\rho/\rho_{0}=\theta_{0}\log_{10}\mathcal{M}_{t}+\log_{10}\theta_{1}
to both the MMS and simulation data, to primarily test if there is statistical agreement between the data and the weakly incompressible theory from
Bhattacharjee et al. [10]
. Naturally, we are able to also probe the similarities and differences between the MMS data and the simulation, too. We use a maximum likelihood approach, utilizing the
Foreman-Mackey et al. [30]
sampler. We have very weakly constrained priors, with
-4\leq\theta_{0}\leq 4
and
-2\leq\log_{10}\theta_{1}\leq 1
, ensuring that we do not over-constrain the fits. We use a regular likelihood function, and we sample the posterior with 32 walkers, for 5000 steps, getting rid of the first 500 steps as a burn-in stage. We show the posterior for the MMS (a) and simulation (b) panels in
Figure 4

\sigma
)
\delta\rho/\rho_{0}=\mathcal{M}_{t}
, as predicted from weakly compressible MHD theory
[
10
]
, and secondly, both the MMS and simulation data agree with one another.
