Phylogenetic study and accurate identification of Tamarix species in China based on complete chloroplast genomes
Authors: Haiwen Li, Jiaxin Jian, Yanlei Liu, Zhaoping Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTamarix species play a significant ecological role in desert transformation and climate improvement, representing nearly 75% of Tamaricaceae. However, due to their high morphological variability and susceptibility to interspecific hybridization, Tamarix remains one of the most poorly classified genera among flowering plants, which has significantly impeded the utilization of Tamarix species in ecological restoration projects across desert regions. China is among the countries most severely affected by desertification. Therefore, this study aims to compare chloroplast genomes among Tamarix species distributed in China, clarify their phylogenetic relationships, and identify molecular markers for species identification.ResultsIn this study, we obtained the complete chloroplast genomes (CPGs) of 16 Tamarix species (33 CPGs), 24 of which were newly generated. A total of 1,650 non-SSR-related indels and 1,428 non-indel-related SSRs were identified, with mononucleotide SSRs being the most abundant. Comparative analyses using mVISTA revealed high sequence similarity across Tamarix chloroplast genomes, indicating strong conservation, except for Tamarix androssowii (OR619632). Overall, inverted repeat (IR) and coding regions were more conserved than single-copy and noncoding regions. Several intergenic regions showed higher variability than commonly used universal DNA markers. Phylogenetic analyses strongly supported the monophyly of Tamarix with two Myricaria samples as outgroups.ConclusionUsing 35 CPGs, we reconstructed a well-resolved phylogeny of Tamarix. Variations in branch lengths and support values provide new insights into intrageneric relationships within the genus. The genomic resources generated in this study offer a valuable foundation for future research on population genetics, species identification, and conservation of Tamarix.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-08959-z.Keywords: Tamarix, Phylogeny, Chloroplast genome, Identification, Next generation sequencing

Introduction

The family Tamaricaceae represents an ancient lineage of flowering plants, originating in the Tertiary period. Comprising four genera and approximately 120 species, the family is primarily distributed in grasslands and desert regions of the Old World [1]. Within this family, Tamarix Linn. is the dominant genus, encompassing over 90 species (about 75% of the total species diversity of the family) and forming a distinct natural group [2]. Species of Tamarix are predominantly xerophytes, adapted to arid and saline environments. They are widely utilized as windbreaks, for sand stabilization, afforestation, and soil and water conservation, thereby playing a central role in desert reclamation and climate regulation, especially in Northwest China. Owing to their ecological importance, extensive research has focused on the adaptation strategies of Tamarix to harsh environments. Previous studies have explored salt tolerance [3–5], drought resistance [6–8], seed germination mechanisms and traits [7, 9, 10], and environmental adaptation mechanisms [11–13]. Collectively, these investigations underscore the substantial variation among Tamarix species in their responses to extreme climatic stresses. In addition, Tamarix has considerable economic and cultural value, providing resources for fuel, fertilizer, food, dyes, spices, and medicinal uses in arid regions [14, 15].

These adaptive traits, particularly tolerance to high salinity, aridity, and low temperatures, make Tamarix species among the preferred taxa for desertification control and ecological restoration in arid and saline regions. However, only a subset of species within Tamarix play important roles in ecological restoration. Owing to the currently confused species relationships and difficulties in species identification within the genus, the effective application of Tamarix in ecological restoration, soil and water conservation, and related fields has been hindered [16]. For example, Tamarix austromongolica and T. chinensis, two closely related and frequently utilized restoration species, diverged relatively recently and exhibit highly similar morphological characteristics. Despite their morphological similarity, the two species already show clear differences in environmental adaptability. In practice, they are often misidentified and consequently used interchangeably, which can compromise restoration performance and reduce the effectiveness of species-site matching [17]. This challenge is largely attributable to the fact that Tamarix represents one of the most taxonomically complex genera among flowering plants. Its extensive morphological variability and frequent interspecific hybridization have led to hundreds of species names being reported [18]. In addition, the lack of reliable fossil records has hindered comprehensive and systematic research on the origin, distribution, and spread of this genus.

A monograph entitled ‘The genus Tamarix’ published by Baum offers only a brief overview of the origin and distribution of Tamarix [18]. Fifty-one species of Tamarix were identified, with 21 of them being recorded for the first time in 1852 [19]. By 1895, the number of recognized Tamarix species had risen to 67, and later editions saw this number increase to 78 [20]. Over the following century, more Tamarix species were described, eventually leading to the classification of 200 species names into 54 distinct species, grouped into three sections and nine series [18]. This classification effort helped clarify the previously chaotic taxonomy and the uncertain species count within Tamarix. However, Baum’s work was largely based on research involving specimens and included limited data from regions like China, Mongolia, and Pakistan. Later studies, such as such as the research by Qaiser in Pakistan in 1981 and the publication of floras from China, Mongolia, and other regions, have further refined the taxonomic grouping of Tamarix. Today, the genus is classified into nine series, encompassing 68 species and seven varieties [21]. These efforts underscore the difficulty of accurately classifying and identifying Tamarix species using traditional taxonomic characteristics.

In addition to traditional taxonomic identification, several studies have employed DNA fragments for species delimitation and identification within the genus Tamarix. These studies have shown that the use of a limited number of chloroplast or nuclear markers, such as the internal transcribed spacer (ITS), trnL-ndhF, and rbcL, is insufficient to draw firm conclusions regarding interspecific relationships in Tamarix [22–24]. Fortunately, the advent of next-generation sequencing (NGS) has provided unprecedented opportunities to address these challenges. Genomic and transcriptomic resources, particularly CPGs, have greatly improved phylogenetic resolution and species identification in Tamarix [25–34]. Some researchers have proposed treating the CPG as a “super-barcode” that enables precise discrimination among closely related species. Compared with traditional morphological approaches, ‘super DNA barcodes’ provide more objective results with minimal subjective interference. In addition, compared with traditional single or a few conventional DNA barcodes, super DNA barcodes contain a much larger number of informative sites, resulting in a higher probability of successful species identification [25–33].

China harbors 18 species and one variety of Tamarix, accounting for roughly 20% of the global species diversity of the genus. These taxa are mainly concentrated in northwestern China, particularly Xinjiang province, as well as Inner Mongolia and North China, where they thrive in saline and sandy soils of desert and semi-desert ecosystems [2, 18, 35, 36]. China is among the countries most severely affected by desertification, with vast arid regions, such as the Taklamakan Desert, in urgent need of ecological restoration. In this context, accurate identification and robust taxonomic resolution of Tamarix species are of both theoretical and practical significance. Clarifying their phylogenetic relationships will not only advance systematic botany but also provide a solid scientific basis for selecting species best suited to ecological engineering in arid regions. Therefore, the present study aims to compare chloroplast genomes of Tamarix species distributed in China, elucidate their phylogenetic relationships, and identify effective molecular markers for species discrimination, thereby supporting taxonomic research and facilitating the application of Tamarix in desertification control.

Materials and methods

Molecular material collection and DNA extraction

Leaves from 24 samples representing 13 Tamarix species were obtained from the Herbarium of Tarim University. Combined with data from public databases, the 16 species selected in this study are all distributed in China, representing 16 of the 19 Tamarix species in the country (84.2%) and covering the major distribution regions of Tamarix in China. The remaining species are currently extremely difficult to locate in the field, and therefore the present dataset represents the maximum number of Tamarix species that could be obtained for this study (Table 1, Fig. S1 & S2). This dataset is therefore well suited for investigating interspecific relationships within Tamarix in China. Molecular materials were collected from healthy, disease-free leaves, and sampling was not restricted to mature individuals. Sampling sites represented the core distribution areas or typical habitats of the target species. All sampled individuals were subjected to careful morphological evaluation before being used in subsequent analyses. Phylogenetic studies do not necessarily require multiple replicates for each species, although multiple individuals were included whenever possible. For rare species, only a single individual could be obtained despite extensive sampling efforts. Total genomic DNA was extracted following the procedure described by Li et al. [37] and subsequently purified using the Wizard DNA Clean-Up System (Promega, Madison, WI, USA). DNA quality was assessed via spectrophotometry, and DNA integrity was examined using 1.5% (w/v) agarose gel electrophoresis.

Complete chloroplast genome acquisition

Total DNA was fragmented into ~ 350 bp fragments using ultrasound with the Covaris M220. A paired-end library was prepared using the NEBNext Ultra™ DNA Library Prep Kit, and sequencing was performed in a PE150 (paired-end 150 bp) mode on the Illumina HiSeq X ten platform. Adapters sequences were removed by the sequencing service provider. For quality control, low-quality reads were filtered using the default settings of the NGS QC Toolkit (https://github.com/mjain-lab/NGSQCToolkit). Specifically, reads were retained only if at least 70% of bases had a Phred quality score ≥ 20, and reads containing more than 5% ambiguous bases (N) were discarded. Adapter trimming and base-level read trimming were not performed by NGS QC Toolkit, as this software conducts read-level filtering only. High-quality paired-end reads were assembled into contigs using SPAdes 3.6.1 (K-mer value = 95) [38]. Chloroplast genome contigs were identified using the Blast v2.6+ (≥ 60% sequence similarity), with the chloroplast genome of T. gracilis (OR619630) as the reference [39]. The selected contigs were subsequently assembled using Sequencher v5.4.5 (Gene Codes Corporation). To assess the accuracy of the assembly, all reads were aligned to the assembled chloroplast genome sequence using Geneious Prime v2022.2.1 [40]. The CPGs were annotated using Plann, with using T. gracilis (OR619630) as a reference [41]. A circular genome map was generated using Organellar Genome DRAW v1.1 (https://chlorobox.mpimp-golm.mpg.de/OGDraw.html) and further edited in Adobe Illustrator CC 2018 [42].

Analysis of microstructural mutation events

To investigate microstructural mutations in the chloroplast genomes of Tamarix species, including variable sites, parsimony-informative sites and simple sequence repeats (SSRs), we analyzed 33 chloroplast genomes. Nine of these genomes were downloaded from NCBI, with detailed information provided in Table 1. The genomes were first aligned using MAFFT v7 online tool [43] and then manually refined in BioEdit v7 [44]. Variable sites and parsimony-informative sites were identified using MEGA v10.0.5 [45]. SSRs were predicted using the GMATA (Genome-wide Microsatellite Analyzing Tool Package), with the following thresholds: mononucleotide SSRs were required to have at least ten repeat units, dinucleotides at least five repeat units, trinucleotides at least four repeat units, and tetranucleotides, pentanucleotides, and hexanucleotides at least three repeat units [46]. Based on the aligned sequence matrix, indels were manually verified and categorized as either SSR-related or non-SSR-related (normal) indels using Sequencher v5.4.5. T. gracilis (OR619630) was used as the reference genome to determine the size and position of the indel events.

Highly variable regions for Tamarix species identification

To identify the most variable regions in chloroplast genome of Tamarix, CPGs were compared using the mVISTA tool in Shuffle-LAGAN mode (http://genome.lbl.gov/vista/mvista/submit.shtml), with the chloroplast genome of Myricaria bracteata serving as the reference. Nucleotide diversity across the chloroplast genome was assessed using a sliding window analysis implemented in DnaSP v6.12.03 [47]. The analysis was performed with a window size of 600 bp and a step size of 200 bp.

Phylogenetic reconstruction of Tamarix in China

All available CPG data for Tamarix were retrieved from public databases. After data deduplication, 33 CPG sequences representing 16 Tamarix species, together with two Myricaria samples used as outgroups, were included in the phylogenetic reconstruction (Table 1). All chloroplast genome sequences were aligned using the online version of MAFFT (https://mafft.cbrc.jp/alignment/server/), and ambiguous regions in the alignment were trimmed using Gblocks v0.91b [48]. Phylogenetic analysis was conducted using both the Maximum Likelihood (ML) and Bayesian Inference (BI) methods. The optimal model (TVM + F + I + G4) was selected by using ModelFinder based on the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) [49]. ML analysis was performed using IQ-TREE with 1,000 bootstrap replicates [50]. For BI, phylogenetic trees were inferred using MrBayes, with Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) analyses run for 100,000,000 generations and trees sampled every 1,000 generations [51]. The first 25% of the samples were discarded as burn-in, and the average standard deviation of split frequencies was checked to ensure it was below 0.01.

Results

Chloroplast genome sequencing and features of Tamarix species

In this study, an average of 99,158,328 paired-end (PE150) reads were generated per sample, with a mean Q20 score of 95.07%. CPGs were obtained for 24 samples representing 13 Tamarix species. The average sequencing depth was 3,742 ×, and chloroplast genome reads accounted for approximately 3.9% of the total sequencing data (Table S1). Combined with chloroplast sequences retrieved from GenBank, a total of 33 samples representing 16 Tamarix species were used for further analyses (Table 1). The total chloroplast genome size of Tamarix ranged from 156,267 bp (T. taklamakanensis TTA1) to 155,076 bp (T. arceuthoides TAR), excluding T. androssowii (OR619632), which possesses an incomplete chloroplast genome. The Tamarix chloroplast genome exhibited a typical quadripartite structure, consisting of a pair of inverted repeat (IR) regions (22,871–26,578 bp), a large single copy (LSC) region (84,700–88,478 bp), and a small single copy (SSC) region (17,868–21,957 bp). The average GC content of the Tamarix chloroplast genome was 36.5%, with values of 42.5% in IR regions, 34.2% in LSC regions, and 29.6% in SSC regions.

The CPG of Tamarix species includes 120 to 131 genes, including 36 to 37 transfer RNA (tRNA) genes, eight ribosomal RNA (rRNA) genes, and 76 to 86 protein-coding genes (Fig. 1, Table 1). Seven tRNA genes (trnI-CAU, trnL-CAA, trnV-GAC, trnI-GAU, trnA-UGC, trnR-ACG, and trnN-GUU), six protein-coding genes (ndhB, rpl23, rps7, rps12, ycf2, and rpl2), and all four rRNA genes are duplicated in the IR regions. Fourteen genes (atpF, rpoC1, ndhB, petB, rpl2, ndhA, rps12, rps16, trnA-UGC, trnI-GAU, trnK-UUU, trnL-UAA, trnG-GCC, and trnV-UAC) contain a single intron, and two genes (clpP and ycf3) have two introns. The rps12 gene is a trans-spliced gene with the 5’-end located in the LSC region and the 3’-end located in the IR region. The trnK-UUU gene has the largest intron, which contains the matK gene.

Repeats and SSRs in CPGs

A total of 1,650 repeats were identified in the 33 Tamarix chloroplast genomes, comprising 42 complement repeats (C), 620 forward repeats (F), 714 palindromic repeats (P), and 274 reverse repeats (R). Among the samples, T. androssowii (OR619632) contained the highest number of complement repeats (4), with counts ranging from zero to four across accessions. T. taklamakanensis (TTA1) exhibited the highest number of forward repeats (28), varying from 15 to 28 among samples. T. hispida (71) contained the highest number of palindromic repeats (24), with counts ranging from 17 to 24 across accessions. In contrast, T. laxa (THI), T. laxa (ON920700), and T. karelinii (TKA1) contained the highest number of reverse repeats (12), with counts ranging from five to 12 across samples (Fig. 2).

Repeats situation in the complete chloroplast genome region among Tamarix species

Mononucleotide SSRs were present exclusively as A- and T-type, which were the most abundant among all SSR types, accounting for 59.0% and 33.5% of the total SSR number, respectively (Fig. 3A). No C- and G-type SSRs were found. The predominant dinucleotide SSR types were AT and TA, representing 0.1% and 7.5% of the total SSRs, respectively. No trinucleotide SSR types were identified (Fig. 3A). Mononucleotide SSRs were predominant both across all samples and within individual species (1,321, 92.5%), followed by dinucleotide SSRs (7.5%) (Fig. 3B).

Sequence divergence and highly variable region

A comparative analysis using mVISTA was performed on 33 chloroplast genomes of Tamarix species to assess the level of divergence (Fig. S3). The mVISTA revealed high sequence similarities across the chloroplast genome of Tamarix, indicating that these chloroplast genomes are highly conserved. An exception was observed for T. androssowii (OR619632), which represents an incomplete chloroplast genome. The IR regions and coding regions were more conserved than the single-copy and noncoding regions.

Additionally, nucleotide diversity among the 33 Tamarix chloroplast genomes was assessed. These genomes were aligned into a matrix of 158,652 bp, revealing an average nucleotide diversity value (pi) of 0.0014. The IR regions exhibited the lowest nucleotide diversity, containing 22 genes, whereas the SSC regions showed the highest diversity, (containing 17 genes. DnaSP v6.12.03 was used to estimate nucleotide diversity and identify mutation hotspot regions (highly variable region) across the complete Tamarix chloroplast genomes (Fig. 4). The Ycf1 region located in SSC region exhibited the highest variability (bigger than 0.008), followed by psbM-trnD, trnL-ndhF, psbE-petL, trnS-GCU-trnS-CGA, trnK-trnQ, trnL-UAA-trnF-GAA, ycf4-cemA, rps15-ycf1, and ndhF regions, which were identified as the most variable regions. Notably, ycf1 has also been previously reported as a universal chloroplast DNA barcode. The variability of the ten identified mutation hotspot regions was further evaluated in comparison with three universal chloroplast DNA barcodes (matK, rbcL, and trnH-psbA). The newly identified markers exhibited higher variability among Tamarix species than the universal DNA barcodes.

Phylogenetic results of Tamarix

Phylogenetic analyses indicated that Tamarix species form a well-supported monophyletic group (Fig. 5). Within the Tamarix lineage, which is the primary focus of this study, T. aphylla is positioned at the base of the genus as the earliest diverging lineage. It forms Clade I and represents the sister group to all other Tamarix species. Clade II comprises T. hispida, T. leptostachya, T. laxa, and T. elongata, which together form a sister clade to Clade III. Clade III includes T. taklamakanensis, T. androssowii, T. gracilis, T. tarimensis, T. ramosissima, T. arceuthoides, T. chinensis, T. austromongolica, T. gansuensis, T. hohenackeri, T. karelinii, as well as multiple accessions of T. arceuthoides, T. chinensis, and T. ramosissima. Clade III contained the largest number of species among the three clades. Most Tamarix species formed well-supported monophyletic groups; however, the four samples of T. ramosissima did not cluster together (Fig. 5).

Discussion

The chloroplast genome of Tamarix

In this study, the chloroplast genomes of 24 Tamarix samples were sequenced using NGS. The ycf15 and ycf68 genes were not annotated because several internal stop codons were detected in these regions, a phenomenon that has also been reported in previous studies [52]. The chloroplast genome of Tamarix is highly conserved, similar to that of most plant species, and no genome rearrangement events were detected in any Tamarix species. The mVISTA results and nucleotide diversity analyses revealed high similarities among the chloroplast genomes, indicating that chloroplast genome divergence in Tamarix is lower than that reported for many other plant taxa, consistent with previous studies [53–55]. Variation in chloroplast genome length among Tamarix species is mainly attributable to changes at the junctions of the quadripartite chloroplast structure (Fig. S4). These regions are generally noncoding and are prone to nucleotide substitutions and insertion-deletion events, without affecting the functional integrity of the chloroplast genome. Repetitive sequences represent another important category of genetic variation and also contribute to differences in the overall chloroplast genome length. Extensive variation in simple sequence repeats (SSRs) was also identified within Tamarix, highlighting substantial genomic changes that have occurred during the evolutionary history of the genus. These findings provide a molecular basis for the precise identification of Tamarix species in future studies.

Phylogenetic relationships of Tamarix

Although the chloroplast genome of T. androssowii (OR619632) is incomplete, it covers more than 80% of the complete chloroplast genome. Based on the phylogenetic relationships obtained, no abnormal phylogenetic placement was detected for this species. Therefore, it remains necessary to present the most comprehensive phylogenetic analysis of Tamarix species currently available based on our sampling. The phylogenetic results confirm the monophyly of Tamarix, which is consistent with its circumscription in traditional taxonomy (Fig. 5). However, owing to the low genetic divergence and high morphological similarity, the systematic relationships within Tamarix remains unclear [22–24]. At present, accurate species identification within Tamarix based on morphological traits remains challenging. In addition, the limited number of researchers and insufficient research investment in this genus have further constrained the application of CPGs in studies of Tamarix phylogenetic relationships. Fortunately, the large-scale application of NGS technology has greatly improved access to chloroplast genome data, thereby enabling the resolution of closely related species [56–58]. Moreover, a recent study employed CPG data from a limited number of Tamarix species to investigate their phylogenetic relationships [34]. In this study, the CPGs were used to assess phylogenetic relationships within Tamarix, resulting in a robust phylogenomic tree for the genus. Based on the chloroplast genome data, the phylogenetic relationships among Tamarix species still exhibit very short branch lengths and minimal differences within clade III (Fig. 5). From the perspective of CPGs, the species status of T. aphylla, T. hispida, T. leptostachya, T. laxa, T. elongata, T. taklamakanensis, and T. karelinii is well supported. Although represented by only a single sample, species including T. hohenackeri, T. gansuensis, T. austromongolica, T. tarimensis, T. gracilis, T. androssowii, and T. aphylla exhibited distinct genetic differences from other species, mainly reflected in branch lengths and support values of the phylogenetic tree.

Although CPG data have been widely to investigate relationships among Tamarix species, challenges remain, including incongruent clustering of conspecific samples. For example, the four samples of T. ramosissima did not form a single clade, the three samples of T. arceuthoides were not recovered as monophyletic, and one T. chinensis sample from this study did not cluster with the publicly available T. chinensis accession. These inconsistencies primarily occurred within Clade III. Several explanations may account for these results, and the following scenarios are proposed. (1) due to the limited number of diagnostic morphological characters and the high degree of morphological similarity among Tamarix species, errors may have occurred in morphology-based species identification; and (2) incomplete lineage sorting during the speciation process. During the formation of new species, ancestral polymorphisms may persist among lineages, and because chloroplast genomes are maternally inherited, incomplete lineage sorting can result in conspecific samples failing to cluster into a single clade. This issue could be addressed in future studies by incorporating additional single-copy nuclear genes. If the second scenario applies, chloroplast genome or specific chloroplast regions may provide indirect evidence of historical hybridization among Tamarix species. Overall, with the inclusion of more samples and additional single-copy nuclear genes, species-level classification of Tamarix is expected to be further improved in future studies. Thus, this study represents an important step toward reconstruct the phylogenetic relationships of Tamarix using CPGs. Compared with previous studies, the inclusion of 16 species and 33 samples allowed for more comprehensive phylogenetic inferences.

The identification of Tamarix species remains challenging. Previous studies have shown that species identification based on conventional morphological traits remains limited. To address this issue, and building on the phylogenetic relationships inferred from CPGs, we examined ten key morphological traits of Tamarix species to evaluate whether their combinations could enable accurate species identification (Fig. 6). For most samples within the same species, the combinations of these traits were consistent, providing morphological support for the phylogenetic relationships inferred from chloroplast genomes. Based on these results of morphological identification, our morphology-based species identifications are generally consistent with the phylogenetic findings. Therefore, the failure of samples from the same species (e.g., T. ramosissima, T. arceuthoides, and T. chinensis) to cluster together is more likely attributable to incomplete lineage sorting during speciation. Although these ten morphological traits can help resolve current issues in Tamarix species identification, most are apparent only during the flowering and fruiting stages. This limits their practical applicability, as seedling propagation is typically completed before these stages. Moreover, morphological identification is inherently subjective, and even trained professionals may misidentify species, particularly when frontline personnel lack sufficient botanical expertise. Therefore, although chloroplast genome alone does not provide sufficient resolution for species-level identification, nuclear genomic data offer greater potential, and molecular approaches represent a more reliable and appropriate strategy for the accurate identification of Tamarix species.

Identification of Tamarix species using key morphological traits

Potential highly variable chloroplast barcodes for species identification

Increasing evidence indicates that universal DNA barcodes exhibit low sequence divergence and limited discriminatory power, which is also confirmed in this study (Fig. 4) [59]. In Tamarix, these regions show limited variability, potentially leading to unsuccessful species identification and ambiguous phylogenetic relationships (Fig. 4). Tamarix species are important pioneer plants in desert ecosystems, playing a significant role in ecological protection and habitat restoration. However, the limited availability of genomic resources for Tamarix species remains a major obstacle for taxonomy, genetic studies, species identification, and conservation. Chloroplast genome sequences offer an opportunity to study genome evolution and provide valuable genetic resources for further research. Mutational events in the chloroplast genome are not randomly distributed but are concentrated in specific regions, forming so-called “hotspot” regions [59]. Comparative analysis of chloroplast genome sequences is an effective strategy to identify these mutation hotspots, which can serve as specific DNA barcodes. In this study, ten hypervariable regions were identified. Comparative analysis of phylogenetic relationships inferred from these ten regions showed that their combined use enables accurate identification of eight Tamarix species (Fig. S5), whereas CPGs allow accurate identification of ten species (Fig. 5), corresponding to an identification efficiency of 80% relative to the CPGs. Moreover, the chloroplast genome analysis revealed distinct major clades among different samples of T. ramosissima and T. chinensis, indicating a higher resolution compared with the ten hypervariable regions. Nevertheless, these markers have not yet been extensively applied in plant phylogeny or DNA barcoding. In this study, specific amplification primers were not designed for these ten regions. Primer design requires extensive data from multiple species to ensure universality. Therefore, this study only proposes these ten highly variable regions as candidate barcodes, which can be readily obtained via NGS sequencing.

Conclusions

In this study, we generated and analyzed an extensive chloroplast genome dataset for Tamarix, including 33 CPGs from 16 species, 24 of which were newly sequenced. Comparative analyses revealed a highly conserved chloroplast genome structure across the genus, with greater conservation in inverted repeat and coding regions than in single-copy and noncoding regions, while several intergenic regions exhibited high sequence variability exceeding that of commonly used universal DNA barcodes. Phylogenetic analyses based on CPGs robustly supported the monophyly of Tamarix and resolved intrageneric relationships with strong support. In addition, morphological analyses based on key diagnostic traits were largely congruent with the chloroplast genome-based phylogeny, further validating the reliability of the inferred relationships. Together, the variable regions identified here represent promising candidates for species identification, and the genomic resources generated provide a solid foundation for future studies on population genetics, taxonomy, and conservation biology of Tamarix.
