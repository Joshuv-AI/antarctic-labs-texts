Polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbon (PAH) abundances in the disk around T Chamaeleontis (T Cha)
Rahul Bandyopadhyay, Simon Casassus

Abstract
Context. The T Tauri star T Chamaeleontis (T Cha) is known to have a protoplanetary disk (PPD) with a dust gap separating the inner and outer disk regions. The mid-infrared (mid-IR) JWST spectrum of T Cha show multiple prominent aromatic infrared bands (AIBs) around 6.2, 8.1, and 11.3 μ \mu m. AIBs are commonly accepted as the emission stemming from polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbon (PAH) molecules. Although PAHs may be instrumental for disk processes involving gas heating, thermochemistry, and photoevaporation, their abundances in PPDs are not well-understood so far. Aims. We aim to characterize the PAHs giving rise to the AIBs observed in the JWST spectrum of T Cha. Our objective is to estimate the PAH abundances, in terms of their sizes, ionization fraction, and mass, in the disk of T Cha. Methods. We perform spectral fitting of the AIBs observed in the archival mid-IR JWST spectrum of T Cha to identify the possible underlying PAH emission components. We transfer the stellar radiation through a parametric disk model of T Cha in order to reproduce the mid-IR JWST spectrum, optical photometric fluxes, and millimetre continuum band fluxes of T Cha. We include stochastically heated PAH dust grains in our model to simulate the AIBs, and hence estimate the PAH abundances from the modelling. We use the results from previous observations and modelling efforts to reduce our model degeneracies. Results. We estimate the PAH abundances in T Cha self-consistently, with other important disk parameters, from our modelling. The overall disk morphology - an inner and an outer disk separated by a dust gap - derived in this work is consistent with the previous results from Spitzer, VLT, and ALMA observations. PAHs are located only in the outer disk in our model. Provided our best fiducial model, we estimate a population of small PAHs of ≲ 30 \lesssim 30 C atoms, with an ionized PAH fraction of ∼ \sim 0.15. We also obtain a PAH-to-dust mass ratio of 6.5 ​ ( ± 0.1 ) × 10 − 3 6.5(\pm 0.1)\times 10^{-3} , which amounts to 16 ​ ( ± 0.3 ) % 16(\pm 0.3)\% of the PAH-to-dust mass ratio observed in the ISM. We predict that the outer disk should have a frontal wall with smaller dust grains limited up to μ \mu m-order to fit the slope of the continuum within 14 − 15 14-15 μ \mu m. We propose a possibility of sub-micron dust grains within the gap to justify an observed plateau around ∼ \sim 10 μ \mu m in the JWST spectrum. Conclusions. While our model have degeneracies, we can predict a population of smaller and mostly neutral PAHs in the disk of T Cha. We also suggest that the disk might be undergoing FUV photoevaporation noting a high PAH-to-dust mass ratio.

Introduction

Infrared (IR) spectra of most astrophysical objects depict a group of emission features, commonly around 3.3, 6.2, 7.7, 8.6, and 11.3
\mu
m. It has been long established that a family of very small (predominantly) aromatic carbonaceous species are responsible for the emission
(
Leger & Puget 1984
;
Allamandola et al. 1989
)
, and hence the features are generally called aromatic IR bands (AIBs, e.g.,
Verstraete et al. 2001
). The small species have lower heat capacity so that they are transiently heated and undergo steep temperature excursions following the absorption of single ultraviolet (UV) photon
(
Sellgren 1984
)
. Such a stochastic nature of heating can reach temperatures of about one order higher than that in case of equilibrium heating, even far from the radiation source, and thus may significantly influence processes within any astrophysical medium.

Polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbon (PAH) molecules
are widely considered to be the principal carriers of AIBs, noting their C-C and C-H vibrational modes coincide with the observed AIB wavelengths
(e.g.,
Tielens 2008
;
Joblin & Tielens 2011
;
Li 2020
;
Peeters et al. 2021
;
Hrodmarsson et al. 2025
).
For example, the 3.3
\mu
m band correspond to the C-H stretching within a PAH molecule, while the 6.2 and 7.7
\mu
m bands correspond to the C-C stretching. The 8.6
\mu
m band arise due to C-H in-plane bending, and the 11.3 and 12.7
\mu
m bands generate due to C-H out-of-plane bending (e.g.,
Peeters et al. 2002
). However, so far it not have been possible to identify individual PAH molecules responsible for an AIB (e.g.,
Li 2020
).
PAHs are instrumental in the physics and chemistry of their environment through photoprocessing of the far-UV (FUV) photons
(
Bakes & Tielens 1994
;
Hollenbach & Tielens 1997
)
.

In protoplanetary disks (PPDs), PAHs are dynamically coupled to the gas and traces the disk surfaces (e.g.,
Lagage et al. 2006
). Emission from the transiently heated PAHs are usually extended up to the outer disks (e.g.,
Habart et al. 2006
;
Yoffe et al. 2023
).
Thus, PAHs could play a critical role in gas heating and overall disk thermodynamics, including chemistry propagation, through the FUV irradiated disk surfaces, even in the outermost disk regions (e.g.,
Kamp & Dullemond 2004
;
Visser et al. 2007
;
Kokoulina et al. 2021
).
PAHs could further influence the photoevaporative evolution of the disk through kinetic heating of gas, as models predict that the FUV photoevaporation efficiency increases in the presence of PAHs
(
Gorti & Hollenbach 2009
;
Gorti et al. 2009
)
.
Therefore, knowledge of the PAH abundances in PPDs is important for a complete understanding of the disk processes during planet formation stages.

From Spitzer observations, one or more AIB features were detected in
\sim
70
\%
of the PPDs around Herbig Ae/Be stars
(
Acke et al. 2004
;
Acke et al. 2010
)
. However, only
\sim
10
\%
of the disks around T Tauri stars have shown such features
(
Geers et al. 2006
;
Geers et al. 2009
)
, which might be because of the UV faintness of the T Tauri stars, in general. However, the major non-detections in T Tauri disks as well as those in Herbig Ae/Be disks may also be due to PAH cluster formation and subsequent adsorption into larger grain surfaces, that can suppress the PAH emission, and hence the AIBs
(
Bakes et al. 2001
;
Lange et al. 2023
)
. Further, PAHs could also be destroyed by EUV or X-rays
(
Siebenmorgen & Krügel 2010
)
.
Geers et al. 2006
used a standard radiative transfer model to fit the 11.3
\mu
m band flux of a sample of T Tauri disks. They find a better fit to the overall sample considering
\sim 10-100
times depletion of the PAH-to-dust mass ratio in their model compared to that in the ISM.
Whether the PAH emissions were not detected, or the PAHs were depleted due to processes related to disk evolution, is not well-known so far. This makes the PAH abundances among PPDs difficult to constrain (e.g.,
Seok & Li 2017
).

In this paper, we study the PPD around the T Tauri star T Chamaeleontis (T Cha), which have shown very prominent AIB features in its recent JWST observations.
We interpret the AIBs as PAH emission profiles, and aim to model them to estimate the abundances of PAHs in the disk of T Cha.
In general, the interstellar AIBs have been frequently interpreted in terms of dust emission from a population of very small PAH grains included within the interstellar dust models (e.g.,
Desert et al. 1990
;
Li & Draine 2001
;
Compiègne et al. 2011
).
These models thus provide optical properties of the PAH grains that can be fed into dust radiative transfer calculations to model the observed AIBs.

We note a few studies that provide estimates of the PAH properties in PPDs through 3D dust radiative transfer modelling. These studies attempt to fit the spectral energy distributions (SEDs) and AIB features simultaneously in individual PPDs. For example,

Woitke et al. 2019
constrained PAH mass and ionization fraction in 14 disks assuming single sized population of PAH grains, which were considered to be at an equilibrium temperature different than the rest of the dust population. Their modelling included satisfactory fits to the prominent AIBs in HD 97048 and HD 169142. They found a PAH-to-dust mass ratios in a range of
\sim 0.1-25\%
1
1

1

This value depends on the reference value of the ISM PAH-to-dust mass ratio, which differs among papers. To avoid ambiguity, whenever discussing the fraction of PAH-to-dust mass ratio compared to that of the ISM, we consider an ISM PAH-to-dust mass ratio of
\sim 4\times 10^{-2}
according to
Draine & Li 2007
of the ISM value of the PAH-to-dust mass ratio, and ionization fractions of the PAHs within
\sim 0.6-0.98
, among their modelled disks.

Sturm et al. 2024
followed a simple treatment of PAHs in radiative equilibrium to match the observed strength of the AIB features in the edge-on disk HH48 NE. They considered fixed size distribution of the PAHs and obtained a PAH mass consistent with that of the ISM.
The models by
Maaskant et al. 2014
focus on studying the PAH ionization across the disk gaps using a single-size PAH population. They predict the ionization fraction might be larger in the optically thin gas within the gaps, by modelling HD 97048, HD 169142, HD 135344 B, and Oph IRS 48.

Habart et al. 2021
modelled the AIBs observed in HD 100546 in terms of aromatised hydrocarbon nano-grains that show emission similar to PAH grains
(
Jones et al. 2013
)
. The nano-grains are stochastically heated in their model and have a fixed size distribution and a mass equivalent to
\sim
10
\%
of the ISM PAHs.
Devinat et al. 2022
performed a similar treatment for HD 169142.

Therefore, focused studies of estimating PAH sizes, ionization fraction, and mass, simultaneously through models of individual disks, are still rare in the literature. Most studies so far used generalized assumptions while interpreting the PAH abundances. Particularly, we note that the effects of varying PAH size distribution in individual models were not explored well.
We aim to estimate detailed PAH abundances, defined in terms of PAH sizes, ionization fraction, and mass, within the disk of T Cha. We plan to obtain these self-consistently by reproducing the mid-IR dust continuum and the AIBs observed in the JWST spectrum T Cha through tailored thermal dust radiative transfer modelling.

.

;
Schisano et al. 2009
)

Gaia Collaboration et al. 2023

\epsilon

\sim
7 Myr

)
. T Cha is one of the rare T Tauri disks, in which at least one AIB was detected from Spitzer observations
(
Geers et al. 2006

The Spitzer spectrum depicted a dip around
\sim
10
\mu

gap
.

(

Further models improved the characterization of the disk

T Cha demonstrated a high variability of

\sim
3 magnitude in optical photometric fluxes
(
Alcalá et al. 1993
;
Walter et al. 2018

. Its emission line (e.g., H
\alpha

Recent JWST mid-IR spectrum of T Cha depicts a significant variation compared to its Spitzer spectra, as

\sim

\sim

This simultaneously allows a greater amount of stellar radiation to reach the outer disk, increasing the outer disk SED in longer wavelengths.

suggested an outburst event have reduced the inner disk dust mass in the case of T Cha.

The AIBs are also much prominently observed in the JWST spectrum compared to the Spitzer spectra (see discussion in Sec.
4

Bajaj et al. 2024a

Analyses of the AIB profiles

A group of prominent AIBs could be seen on top of the mid-IR thermal continuum in the JWST spectrum of T Cha. The 11.3
\mu
m band was prominent in the Spitzer spectra
(
Geers et al. 2006
)
.
Arun 2025
recently reanalysed the Spitzer spectra and reported detection of AIBs at 6.2, 7.7, and 8.6
\mu
m. From the JWST spectrum, we can clearly identify the bands around 6.2 and 11.3
\mu
m, while the 7.7 and 8.6
\mu
m bands seem to have formed a complex centred around
\sim
8.1
\mu
m. The 6.2
\mu
m band is preceded by the weaker 6.0
\mu
m band, which is also observed in many other astrophysical sources
(
Peeters et al. 2002
)
.

4.1
PAH spectral type

The AIB features vary in different astrophysical objects/environments, which led into their classification as
\mathcal{A}
,
\mathcal{B}
, and
\mathcal{C}
by
Peeters et al. 2002
5
5

5

Peeters et al. 2002
used the term ’PAH’ for classification
, while later
Matsuura et al. 2014
added another class
\mathcal{D}
.
The classes are mainly based on the shapes and peak-positions of the 6.2, 7.7, and 11.3
\mu
m profiles as well as the relative location of the 7.7 and 8.6
\mu
m bands in terms of their central wavelengths.
The class
\mathcal{A}
and class
\mathcal{B}
profiles show a clear separation between the 7.7 and 8.6
\mu
m band profiles, while the peak of the 7.7
\mu
m class
\mathcal{B}
profile is
\sim
0.2
\mu
m red-ward compared that in class
\mathcal{A}
. Classes
\mathcal{C}
and
\mathcal{D}
exhibit a single profile in the 7-9
\mu
m range, with the peaks located around 7.7 and 8.22
\mu
m, respectively. Additionally, the class
\mathcal{D}
category is suggested to have an aliphatic feature around 6.9
\mu
m
(
Matsuura et al. 2014
)
.

The JWST spectrum of T Cha depicts a single AIB profile within 7-9
\mu
m peaking beyond 8
\mu
m, which predominantly identifies with an overall spectral class
\mathcal{C}
. The exact peak location at 8.1
\mu
m is slightly blueward compared to the typical class
\mathcal{C}
profile peak.
The profile also indicates a partial separation of the 7.7 and 8.6
\mu
m bands by showing a red wing at 8.6
\mu
m. This, along with a blue wing at 7.6
\mu
m tentatively indicate towards a class
\mathcal{A}
/
\mathcal{B}
contribution.
The peak-position of the 6.2
\mu
m band, at 6.27
\mu
m, and also the profile shape,
further relate to class
\mathcal{B}
profiles. We notice a very faint 6.9
\mu
m feature, which are sometimes also present in classes
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
as well, hence we cannot conclude of a class
\mathcal{D}
contribution. Hence, T Cha may be qualitatively characterised as of PAH spectral class
\mathcal{C}
with a contribution of class
\mathcal{B}
signatures.

An association of the class
\mathcal{A}
profiles to highly UV processed environments, such as galaxies and ISM, in general, can observed, compared to the prevalence of class
\mathcal{B}
,
\mathcal{C}
, and
\mathcal{D}
profiles in circumstellar materials with lower UV exposure (e.g.,
Li 2020
;
Peeters et al. 2021
). It is known that the aliphatic components of the hydrocarbon mixes are more susceptible to dissociation under higher UV irradiation than their more stable aromatic counterpart.
Hence, objects depicting class
\mathcal{C}
profiles, such as T Cha, could have a higher amount of aliphatic-to-aromatic ratio, which may be in form of larger number of aliphatic sub-groups attached to PAH molecules (e.g.,
Sloan et al. 2007
;
Dartois et al. 2020
, see further discussion in Sec.
4.3
).

The AIBs from the Herbig Ae/Be disks are mostly known to show class
\mathcal{B}
profiles
(
Keller et al. 2008
;
Acke et al. 2010
)
. Note that T Cha falls among the earliest spectral types among the T Tauri stars, and marginally later to Herbig Ae/Be stars. Hence, the occurrence of both class
\mathcal{B}
and
\mathcal{C}
spectral features in T Cha is a clear example of the correlation between UV hardness of the central star and the PAH emission, as also pointed out earlier (e.g.,
Sloan et al. 2007
;
Keller et al. 2008
;
Acke et al. 2010
).

4.2
Profile fitting of the AIBs: spectral components

Despite the common understanding of PAH molecules giving rise to the AIBs, the individual molecules responsible for a particular AIB feature have not been identified so far (e.g.,
Li 2020
).
However, detailed spectral fitting of the AIBs had allowed a better understanding of the features as combination of vibrational modes of PAHs
(
Boulanger et al. 1998
;
Verstraete et al. 2001
;
Smith et al. 2007
)
.
Further, such analyses also formed the observational basis of deriving empirical opacities for PAH grains, for example, the ‘astro-PAH’ opacities that are widely used to model dust emission
(
Li & Draine 2001
;
Draine & Li 2007
)
.

However, the spectral fitting has been so far focussed on class
\mathcal{A}
profiles (e.g.,
Smith et al. 2007
).
Due to the major differences within 7-9
\mu
m, the class
\mathcal{A}
profile components are not applicable for T Cha with strong class
\mathcal{C}
profile signatures. Hence, we attempt a spectral decomposition of the PAH profiles observed in the JWST spectrum of T Cha, in order to predict the underlying components in terms of their central wavelengths and width, and estimate the AIB fluxes (
I_{\mathrm{PAH}}
). We later utilize this information to derive PAH opacities that could be specifically applied to model T Cha (see further discussion on PAH opacities in Sec.
5.6
).

To fit the AIBs in T Cha and find out the probable underlying profile components, we mostly follow the technique by
(
Smith et al. 2007
)
, including the assumption that the vibrational modes of PAH molecules can be theoretically approximated as that of a harmonic oscillator. Hence, we assume the individual AIB profiles to be a combination of one or more Drude profiles of the functional form:

I_{\mathrm{D},j}(\lambda)=\frac{b_{j}\gamma_{j}^{2}}{\left(\lambda/\lambda_{j}-\lambda_{j}/\lambda\right)^{2}+\gamma_{j}^{2}}.\\

(1)

\lambda_{\mathrm{j}}
and
\gamma_{\mathrm{j}}
are the central wavelength and the fractional FWHM of the
j
th profile, where
\mathrm{FWHM}_{\mathrm{j}}=\lambda_{\mathrm{j}}\gamma_{\mathrm{j}}
. The peak strength of the function is measured as
b_{\mathrm{j}}
.

We fit each AIB profile in absolute flux scale with a function

I(\lambda)=C(\lambda)+I_{\mathrm{PAH}}(\lambda);\\
\\
I_{\mathrm{PAH}}(\lambda)=\sum_{j}I_{\mathrm{D},j}(\lambda)\\

(2)

C(\lambda)
represents the underlying continuum under the AIB profile.
C(\lambda)
is the sum of the stellar spectrum,
S_{*}(\lambda)
, and the thermal dust continuum, which we assume to be a modified black body spectrum. Hence,

C(\lambda)=S_{*}(\lambda)+\sum_{k}f_{k}B_{\lambda}(T_{k})(\lambda/\lambda_{0})^{-2},\\

(3)

where
f_{\mathrm{k}}>0
,
B_{\lambda}(T)
denotes the black body function and
\lambda_{0}
is the normalization wavelength assumed as 9.7
\mu
m.

We employ Bayesian inference technique to estimate the most probable Drude functions fitting the AIB profiles. For this we use the
NAUTILUS
code
(
Lange 2023
)
that runs a nested sampling algorithm to reach the maximum likelihood of the Drude profile parameters. We evaluate the likelihood function
L
defined as

\log{L}=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n}\left[\frac{(I_{\mathrm{obs},n}-I_{\mathrm{mod},n})^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}-\log(2\pi\sigma^{2})\right],\\

(4)

where the term within the square brackets is summed over
n
number of observed points on the spectrum.
I_{\mathrm{obs},n}
and
I_{\mathrm{mod},n}
denote the observed and the modelled flux densities at the
n
th observed point, respectively.
Using this procedure, we find the best-fit Drude profile parameters,
\lambda_{\mathrm{j}}
,
b_{\mathrm{j}}
, and
\gamma_{\mathrm{j}}
. More details of the fitting procedure is described in Appendix
A
.

The 6.2
\mu
m AIB in T Cha is fitted with two components at 6.184 and 6.27
\mu
m,
closely similar to the suggestion by
Peeters et al. 2002
that a class
\mathcal{B}
feature could be a mix of class
\mathcal{A}
and class
\mathcal{C}
profiles. Note that the analysis of 6.2
\mu
m band includes a fit to the 6.0
\mu
m band since it is partially blended with the 6.2
\mu
m profile. The 8.1
\mu
m band in T Cha is found to be composed of four components, centred at 7.58, 7.89, 8.14, and 8.65
\mu
m, from our analyses (see Sec.
4.3
). We used three components at 11.22, 11.286, and 11.42
\mu
m to satisfactorily fit the profile structure of the 11.3
\mu
m AIB. The fit to each profile is shown in Fig.
2
. The components derived for each AIB from this analyses are summarized in Table
1
with their defining parameters.

4.3
Implications on aliphatic hydrocarbon bonds in T Cha

The 7.7
\mu
m band was earlier explained observationally as the combination of the sub-features at
\sim
7.6 and
\sim
7.8
\mu
m by
(
Peeters et al. 2002
)
, where the dominance of the bluer sub-feature produces a class
\mathcal{A}
profile while class
\mathcal{B}
results when the redder component outshines.
Since an aliphatic sub-group shifts the purely aromatic vibrational modes to longer wavelengths, the observed shift of the 7.7
\mu
m band peak from class
\mathcal{A}
to B has been explained in terms of an aliphatic contribution to the pure C-C modes that generate the 7.7
\mu

(

;

)
.
When the
\sim
7.8
\mu
m sub-feature largely dominates the
\sim
7.6
\mu

\mu
m band nearly merge with the 8.6
\mu
m band and might generate a class
\mathcal{C}
profile (e.g.,
Sloan et al. 2007
).

From our analyses, we propose that the components of the 8.1
\mu
m profile at 7.58 and 7.89
\mu
m represent specifically the class
\mathcal{A}
/B signatures of the 7.7
\mu
m band, and may correspond to purely C-C modes. The 8.65
\mu

\mu
m C-H in-plane bending mode. The component at 8.14
\mu
m may be interpreted as the link between the 7.7 and 8.6
\mu

\mu
m. We hypothesize that the 8.14
\mu
m feature collectively represents the emission from the aliphatic sub-groups attached to the PAHs in T Cha.

5
Radiative transfer modelling

To estimate the PAH abundances in the disk of T Cha in a self-consistent way, we perform dust radiative transfer modelling of the observed data using the code
MCFOST

(
Pinte et al. 2006
)
. The code considers propagation of a large number (
\sim 10^{5}
) of photon packets, originated from a radiation source, through an input 3D density grid, adopting a Monte Carlo algorithm. It solves the radiative transfer problem at each grid cell and generates a temperature grid, which is further processed by the code to produced synthetic data, including images and/or SED that can be compared to the observational data. We model only the dust emission from the disk of T Cha and do not focus on the line emission from gas. This section discusses the general modelling procedure with a few basic model assumptions applicable for T Cha.

5.1
Modelling aim

We aim to model the mid-IR spectrum and the overall SED of T Cha ranging from optical to millimetre wavelengths.
We specifically attempt a best-fit of the JWST spectrum within
5-25

\mu
m, that includes the mid-IR thermal dust continuum emission along with the AIBs around 6.2, 8.1, and 11.3
\mu
m, and the optical (
0.44-0.9

\mu
m) and millimetre (
0.85-3.3
mm) band fluxes. Noting the ”see-saw” variability of the disk, we exclude all the photometric data points within
1-500

\mu
m from our modelling procedure.

It is trivially assumed that the IR excess in
\sim 5-9

\mu
m continuum mostly comes from the inner disk region, while the outer disk region mainly contributes to the continuum
>14

\mu
m.
It is important to note that PAHs are assumed as dust grains and their emission is approximated as Drude profiles, which have extended wings (Sec.
4
). Hence, they significantly contribute to the thermal continuum in the
5-14

\mu
m region. In order to find the best model-fit to the observed data,
we vary all the relevant parameters including dust abundances as well as the physical disk structure that governs the thermal emission and thus shape the disk SED. To reduce the degeneracies involved with the model, we intend to utilize all the relevant information from the previous studies, mainly from O13, H15, P17, and H18.

5.2
Input stellar spectrum

It is important to well-quantify the radiation source for the radiative transfer model. To obtain a suitable stellar spectrum, we refer to the database ‘The Castelli AND Kurucz 2004 Stellar Atmosphere Models’
6
6

6

https://archive.stsci.edu/hlsps/reference-atlases/cdbs/grid/ck04models/

(
Castelli & Kurucz 2003
)
. Given that the central star of T Cha is of G8 spectral type, we assume a 5500 K stellar temperature, and choose the corresponding atmospheric model, with the stellar gravity given as log
(g\>\mathrm{cm}\>\mathrm{s^{-2}})=3.5
. Then we calculate the input stellar spectrum with absolute fluxes considering a stellar radius of
1.3

R_{\sun}
and use that spectrum as the code input in our entire modelling process.

Since the disk of T Cha has a high inclination close to
\sim
70
∘
, as reported in literature (see Sec.
5.4
), some of the stellar radiation might get extinct while transmitting through the inner disk and the outer disk surface layers towards the observer.
Xie et al. 2025
reports that the JWST observations have been taken during the brightest phase of the variable photosphere of the central star. Therefore, we aim to match the highest photometric fluxes in the variability curve of the stellar photosphere. For this, we take care that the modelled disk should not block more than
\sim
1% of the stellar irradiation, as approximately calculated by noting the flux around 0.5
\mu
m in the stellar model SED.

5.3
Density structure

The adopted density structure is that of a disk in a vertical hydrostatic equilibrium with an axisymmetric density profile

\rho(r,z)=\frac{\Sigma(r)}{\sqrt{2\pi}H(r)}\mathrm{exp}\left(-\frac{1}{2}\left[\frac{z}{H(r)}\right]^{2}\right);\\
\Sigma(r)=\Sigma_{0}\left(\frac{r}{R_{0}}\right)^{\gamma}.

(5)

\Sigma(r)
denotes the surface density as a function of the radius having an exponent
\gamma
, with a reference value given by
\Sigma_{0}
at the reference radius
R_{0}
.
\rho(z)
has a Gaussian form with the maximum at the midplane and distribution along the vertical axis (
z
). The
1\sigma
deviation of the midplane density along
z
is referred to as the scale height that has radial form of

H(r)=H_{0}\left(\frac{r}{R_{0}}\right)^{\beta},

(6)

where the radial exponent
\beta
is called the flaring index;
H_{0}
is the reference scale height at
R_{0}
.

To calculate radiative transfer in
MCFOST
, the density structure is defined over a mesh-grid that can be radially divided in multiple components. For each component, we specify inner and outer radii, total dust mass (
M_{\mathrm{d}}
) and scale height, in terms of
H_{0}
,
R_{0}
, and
\beta
, as input parameters. Thus, we parametrize the radial dust surface density (
\Sigma_{\mathrm{d}}(r)
) and scale height (
H(r)
) in the model assuming the density form of Equation
5
.

Since it is well-accepted that T Cha has a gap in the dust disk, we primarily consider two components within the density grid - an inner and an outer disk, radially separated by a gap with
\Sigma_{\mathrm{d}}=0
.
However, the ALMA CO line observations did not infer a gap in the gas disk (H15;
Wölfer et al. 2023
). Also, spatially resolved ALMA continuum observations have so far only confirmed a gap in the larger millimetre size grains H18. Hence,
we also explore the possibility of smaller dust grains in the so-called dust gap, where we add another component within the grid representing the ‘gap’ region with non-zero dust mass.

The inner edge of the inner disk is directly illuminated by the central star and achieves a very high temperature. This results in puffing-up of the inner disk scale height within a thin rim at its inner edge
(
Dullemond et al. 2001
)
. Hence, we assume the inner disk geometry in our model to have such a ‘puffed-up’ inner rim component having a higher scale height than the rest of the inner disk.

5.4
Disk orientation

The knowledge of disk orientation in the sky is important for the PPDs, since the observables may depend sensitively on the inclination (
i
) and position angle (
PA
) of the disk. Hence it is crucial to properly set the disk orientation in the radiative transfer models to compare with the observations. In our modelling, we set
PA=90^{\circ}
arbitrarily since we only fit the total spectrum from the disk and do not compare any disk images. The inclination of the disk of T Cha has been previously estimated in the range of
\sim 58^{\circ}-75^{\circ}
(
Brown et al. 2007
; O13; H15; P17; H18). The inclination of 73
∘
obtained by H18 from the ALMA 3.3 mm continuum image corresponds mostly to the settled dust. H15 obtained
i=67^{\circ}
by fitting the ALMA CO line emission, while the modelling of the VLT-SPHERE 1.6
\mu
m scattered light observations by P17 resulted in
i=69^{\circ}
, and hence a lower estimation of inclination (average
i=68^{\circ}
) is obtained from the observations tracing the disk surface.
Since the main scope of this work is to model the PAH emission that mainly traces the disk surfaces, we set
i=68^{\circ}
for our T Cha disk model. Presuming the inclination to be well-constrained by these resolved observations of the outer disk, we choose not to vary the inclination to reduce our model degeneracies.

5.5
Dust properties

We consider both carbonaceous and silicate dust for the modelling. The carbonaceous dust include PAHs and amorphous carbon. Stochastic heating of PAHs are considered in the radiative transfer calculations. We adopt the opacities for amorphous carbon calculated from the optical properties given by
Zubko et al. 1996
for BE
7
7

7

BE sample refers to the carbon grains produced by burning benzene in air under normal conditions
(
Zubko et al. 1996
)
amorphous carbon materials. The amorphous silicate opacities considered are derived from that of the astronomical silicates described in
Draine 2003a
;
Draine 2003b
. We discuss the PAH opacities used in this work in Sec.
5.6
. We consider all the dust species to follow a power-law size distribution.

Earlier radiative transfer models of T Cha did not consider dust settling, due to the absence of relevant constraints, which simplifies the model assumptions. However, completely excluding such effect in the model calculations might deviate the model parameters compared to the realistic solutions. Dust settling can considerably affect the emission from small dust, especially PAHs, even with high turbulence levels (
\alpha=0.01
) as shown by
Dullemond et al. 2007
.
Hence, in our model calculations, we allow settling of dust grains according to their sizes and radial position in the disk as prescribed in
Dubrulle et al. 1995
.

5.6
PAH opacities

Similar to astrophysical dust models, we interpret PAHs as dust grains instead of molecules, and their opacities in our radiative transfer model is incorporated in terms of dust opacities.
We utilize the PAH opacities corresponding to the astro-PAH model, originally described in
Li & Draine 2001
(also refer to
Draine & Li 2007
and
Draine et al. 2021
). These are empirical opacities derived in order to replicate the AIBs observed mainly in the spectra of galaxies, nebulae, and ISM, in general, and also were guided by the laboratory spectra of PAHs. The opacities were further modified based on the Spitzer spectra of a large number of galaxies
(
Smith et al. 2007
;
Draine & Li 2007
)
.
As a result, astro-PAH opacities account for a generalized PAH spectral template,
where the 7.7 and 8.6
\mu
m bands are well separated, mostly corresponding to class
\mathcal{A}
and class
\mathcal{B}
spectral types (see previous discussion in Sec.
4
).

Therefore, it is not possible to generate more specific class
\mathcal{C}
signatures of a PAH profile directly using the astro-PAH model opacities.
A close replication of the observed positions and shapes of the AIB profiles in the model spectrum allows more accurate estimation of the modelled PAH characteristics.
Hence, we calculate the required PAH opacities based on the original formalism described in
Li & Draine 2001
, in order to properly reproduce the profile structure of each PAH band observed in the JWST spectrum of T Cha. For this, we use our PAH profile analyses described in Sec.
4
to modify the relevant parameters within the astro-PAH model that modulates the position and shape of the PAH profiles.
Details of this modification procedure are described in Appendix
B
.
We use the modified PAH ‘opacity files’ as the input for the code to run the dust radiative transfer calculations.

We consider both neutral and ionized PAHs with opacities derived for a size range of
\sim 2.88-50
Å. The size range correspond to PAHs with
\sim 10-50000
C atoms PAHs, referring to the expression in
Draine et al. 2021
: number of C atoms

N_{C}=418\left(\frac{a}{10\AA}\right)^{3},

(7)

where,
a
is the radius of the assumed PAH grain. This considers a carbon mass density of
2.0
g cm
-3
for the grain material. Note that smaller PAHs of
<20
C atoms were in general excluded in the astro-PAH model since they are photolytically unstable under interstellar radiation conditions
(
Li & Draine 2001
;
Draine et al. 2021
)
. Since the environment of a T Tauri star as in the disk of T Cha is expected to have a milder UV radiation, we allow the PAHs to be smaller (also see
Allamandola et al. 1989
for a similar argument for the Red Rectangle). We assume an MRN
(
Mathis et al. 1977
)
type grain size distribution (power-law index
p=-3.5
) for the PAHs.

In our model, we include PAHs only in the outer disk (see discussion in Sec.
6.1
). The PAH size range and ionization fraction (
\phi
), i.e., the ionized-to-neutral PAH ratio, and the ratio of the total PAH mass to the total dust mass (PAH-to-dust mass ratio) are varied in the model and constrained by fitting the AIB fluxes and the their flux ratios.

6
Model optimization: results and discussion

We compute a model of the PPD around T Cha that satisfactorily fits the observed JWST spectrum, including the dust continuum and the AIBs, and also the optical and millimetre band fluxes, as shown in Fig.
3
.
The main parameters defining the model are summarized in Table
2
.
The model involves a large number of parameters, which often influence the SED inter-dependently. Thus, we run several sets of model-grids, and apply both
\chi^{2}
minimization and visual interpretation at different modelling stage. Thereby we attempt to narrow down the tentative parameter ranges and improve the model iteratively.
This section discuss the best fiducial model obtained from this work, along with assumptions and optimization process related to specific model parameters and possible model degeneracies.

6.1
Disk morphology and dust population

We reinstate the basic morphology of the T Cha disk as found by the earlier studies, which is of a typical transition disk with an inner and an outer disk separated by a dust gap. However, we also propose the possible presence of smaller dust grains within the so-called dust gap.
Our model predicts a total dust mass of
\sim 8\times 10^{-4}

M_{\sun}
in the disk of T Cha.
The distribution of different dust species among the modelled disk components are summarized in Table
3
in terms of their mass (in Solar mass) and sizes.
Fig.
4
shows the radial profiles of the disk scale height and the dust surface density obtained from our modelling.

6.1.1
Inner disk dust mass

The only model parameter that we vary for the inner disk is the dust mass, while the rest of the inner disk parameters were kept fixed to the values guided by the literature, mostly from O13, which considerably reduce the degeneracies in our model. We take this approach since the main scope of this work is to address the PAH emission from T Cha, which presumably arise from outer disk region, as discussed later.

In order to fit their VLTI interferometric data, O13 found a very thin inner disk located at
0.07
au from the central star and extended only up to
0.11
au. However, they further proposed a ‘hidded-mass’, in the shadow of the optically thick inner disk, may be present and may extend up to 5 au.
From subsequent ALMA observations, H18 reported an unresolved inner disk with an extent
<1
au in thermal emission from mm size dust.
Based on these results, we parametrize the inner disk in terms of two regions: a thin ‘puffed-up rim’, located at the inner disk edge, having an extent of
0.07-0.11
au with a scale height of
0.2
; and the rest of the inner disk with a lower scale height of 0.05 au at 1 au, extending from 0.11 to 1 au, which we refer to as the ‘shadowed region’.
The flaring index of the inner disk (
0.07-1
au) is set to 1.0, also following O13.

We consider a mix of amorphous carbon and silicate grains in a mass ratio of
1:3
for the inner disk. The absence of the 10
\mu
m silicate emission band in the Spitzer spectra of T Cha was earlier interpreted as the absence of the small silicate grains in the inner disk. O13 found a lower limit of 5
\mu
m for the size of the silicate grains in their modelling. We directly adopt the size range for the silicate grains as
5-1000

\mu
m and that for the amorphous carbon grains as
0.01-1000

\mu
m in our model from O13. Both the grain population follow an MRN type power-law size distribution with a slope of
-3.5
.
We also do not include PAHs in the inner disk since O13 found no PAH emission from the inner disk in their mid-IR interferometric data. Also, from the analyses of the JWST MIRI-IFU slices,
Bajaj et al. 2024a
reported the PAH emission to be more extended than the continuum and seems to trace the outer regions of the disk.

We reduce the dust mass in the inner disk compared to that in O13 in order to match the
5-9

\mu
m continuum in the JWST spectrum, which is a factor of
\sim
3 lower than that in the Spitzer spectrum. The reduction of the dust mass in the inner disk results in an effective lowering of the scale height, due to a decrease in the opacity and thus increased the outer disk irradiation, which is expected to interpret the observed ‘see-saw’ variability. Note that the puffed-up rim may no longer be optically thick, due to its reduced dust mass, and hence the shadowed region may contribute considerably to the visible thermal emission in
5-9

\mu
m. Hence, we varied the dust mass in both the puffed-up rim and the shadowed region. We find that dust masses of
1.2\times 10^{-12}

M_{\sun}
within the puffed-up rim and of
1.9\times 10^{-11}

M_{\sun}
in the shadowed region of the inner disk accounts well for the 5-9
\mu
m JWST continuum.

6.1.2
Outer disk geometry and dust distribution

We presume that the ‘see-saw’ variability would also affect the far-IR
Herschel
photometric fluxes in the range (
70-500

\mu
m). However, we also expect that the variability would decrease with increasing wavelength since the larger dust must be more settled and less affected by the increased radiation on the outer disk. Hence, we use the mid-IR continuum in the
14-25

\mu
m range and the millimetre fluxes at ALMA
0.85
and
3
mm, SEST
1.3
mm, and ATCA
3.3
mm bands
in order to constrain the outer disk characteristics.

Our approach to fit the
14

\mu
m to 3.3 mm SED is to match the slope of the observed SED in
14-25

\mu
m and
0.85-3.3
mm regions, while simultaneously reproducing the observed peak strength of the continuum around
\sim
25
\mu
m.
We include amorphous carbon and silicate dust with a fixed standard ratio of
1:3
in the outer disk.
Variation of the amorphous carbon-to-silicate mass ratio does not have any major physical consequences to the scope of this work.
PAHs are included in the outer disk and will be discussed in detail in Sec.
6.2
. Also note that the inclusion of PAHs does not noticeably vary the modelling of the SED range focused in this section.

We note that larger grains close to the orders of centimetres and a shallower size distribution than the MRN law is needed to better fit the slope of the millimetre fluxes
>1
mm. We set a minimum grain size of 0.01
\mu
m, while varying the maximum sizes range from
\sim 1-10
mm, and the slope (
p
) of the power-law grain size distribution from
-2.5
to
-3.9
. The rest of the outer disk parameters while running these models are chosen from a standard range discussed below. We obtain a good match to the slope of the
0.85-3.3
mm SED with dust grains of sizes
0.01-5000

\mu
m with
p=-3.3
.

The outer disk geometry, including its radial extent and the scale height, and the total dust mass is constrained mainly by matching the strength and slope of the
14-25

\mu
m continuum.
The outer disk of T Cha was resolved in the 3.3 mm ALMA image by H18.
We truncate the outer disk at 50 au, which matches the radius where the millimetre dust emission from the outer disk falls to a value of
\sim
10% of the peak intensity, as derived from the ALMA image. The outer edge of the proposed dust gap, i.e, the inner radius of the outer disk has been largely differed in earlier works, with a range
\sim 12-30
au. Hence, we vary the inner edge of the outer disk in a wide range up to 32 au.
We vary the scale height (
H_{0}/R_{0}
) of the outer disk within a range of
1.5
to
3
au calculated at
R_{0}=25
au, while the flaring index
\beta
is varied from 1.1 to 1.5.

The outer disk dust mass is also needed to be varied alongside the geometrical parameters to match the
14-25

\mu
m continuum strength. However, the peak flux around
\sim
25
\mu
m is overestimated if we try to generate the strength of the
14-25

\mu
m continuum by increasing the dust mass.
We also note that including a single dust population of
0.01-5000

\mu
m in the entire outer disk generates a flatter slope in the
14-25

\mu
m model SED than in the observed.
Therefore, we propose the outer disk to be consisting of two regions. A region with smaller dust population with only up to micron-sized grains, located at the inner part of the outer disk, accounts well for the steeper rise of
14-25

\mu
m continuum. The rest of the outer disk region accommodates the wider grain population up to the millimetre sized dust. This results in a much better simultaneous fit in qualitative terms to the observed
14-25

\mu
m continuum slope and the SED peak around
\sim 25

\mu
m. We refer to the smaller dust region as outer disk ‘wall’, where the inner edge of the wall is understood as the inner edge of the outer disk in our discussion. The rest of the outer disk is referred as the ‘outer region’. The wall and the outer region is assumed to have the same scale height.

From modelling of the 1.6
\mu
m scattered light observations, P17 estimated the outer disk at
\sim
30 au.
While the modelling of 3 mm ALMA observations by P17 revealed a peak radial intensity of the outer disk at
\sim
35 au. Therefore, the two zones in the our model with two dust populations are also justified from separation of
\sim
5 au between the larger and the smaller dust distribution reported from those observations.

Hence, we assume a wall width of 5 au for simplicity. Further, changing of the wall width will be degenerate with varying the dust mass within the wall, considering the opacity of the medium.
We keep the minimum grain size in the wall at 0.01
\mu
m and vary the maximum grain size from
0.1-10

\mu
m. The grain size distribution has a power-law index
p=-3.5
.
We explore a wide range of dust mass for the wall in a qualitative fitting approach. For the best model calculations, we vary the dust mass in the wall in a range of
1\times 10^{-7}-8\times 10^{-7}

M_{\sun}
. Similarly, governed by a visual fit to the millimetre fluxes and literature ranges (e.g., O13, P17), the dust mass in the outer region is tuned between
1\times 10^{-5}
to
5\times 10^{-4}

M_{\sun}
for the best model calculations.

Finally, from
\chi^{2}
minimization of the fit to the
14-25

\mu
m continuum range, we estimate the outer disk of T Cha to have a radial extent of
31-50
au, with a scale height measuring
1.9
au at 25 au.
The location of the wall is at
31-36
au. We find a maximum grain size of 4
\mu
m in the wall and estimate dust masses of
7\times 10^{-7}
and
7.5\times 10^{-4}

M_{\sun}
in the wall and outer region of the outer disk, respectively.

We find the inner radius of the outer disk from our independent modelling is quite similar to that predicted from resolved outer disk observations by P17 and H18.
Therefore, the gap width
of
\sim
30 au in our model also further supports the possible presence of a planet of
\sim 1.2

M_{\mathrm{Jup}}
or the possibility of multiple planets within the gap, as proposed earlier (
Huélamo et al. 2011
; P17, H18).

6.1.3
Small silicates in the gap?

The JWST spectrum of T Cha clearly depicts a plateau-like shape of the continuum around
\sim
10
\mu
m as compared to a sharper dip in case of the Spitzer spectra. A presence of smaller dust grains within the gap of T Cha was not interpreted during the modelling of the Spitzer observations (e.g.,
Brown et al. 2007
;
Cieza et al. 2011
; O13). The regular PAH spectral templates depict a plateau between the 8.6 and 11.3
\mu
m bands. However, the observed JWST continuum around
\sim
10
\mu
m in is much shallower compared to that generally found between those bands. Further, the ALMA CO line observations have not so far reported any gap in the gas disk (H15;
Wölfer et al. 2023
). A proof of the presence of dust in the gap may support the possibility of replenishment of the inner disk after its destruction as suggested by
Xie et al. 2025
.

Therefore, we propose a faint 10
\mu
m silicate emission arising from the gap that compensates the otherwise deeper plateau within the 8.6 and 11.3
\mu
m bands and hence, may explain the JWST continuum plateau around
\sim
10
\mu
m. We assume smaller dust grains that might be coupled with the accreting gas, and hence fill the dust gap formed in terms of the larger millimetre sized grains.
Thus, in our models, we simply consider sub-micron amorphous carbon and silicate dust grains in
1:3
ratio, having a size range of
0.01-1

\mu
m with an MRN type size-distribution, within the gap spanning
1-31
au. We define the density distribution of matter within the gap roughly following
Maaskant et al. 2014
.
The scale height is kept at
H_{0}/R_{0}=0.05
defined at 1 au, the same as that for the shadowed region of the inner disk. We assume flaring of the less dense matter within the gap with
\beta=1.1
. The surface density profile is considered to be flat with
\gamma=0
.
By exploring a range of dust mass, we find that a dust mass of
6\times 10^{-10}

M_{\sun}
considered within the gap can closely match the strength of the continuum around
\sim
10
\mu
m.
Fig.
5
clearly shows an improvement of the overall model-fit to the JWST spectrum considering the abundance of small dust within the gap.

We do not include PAHs in the gap as well, since we note that the dust mass
\sim 5
orders lower in the gap, adding PAHs with same PAH-to-dust ratio as found in our model (see Sec.
6.2
) does not make any noticeable difference in the modelled PAH fluxes. A prevalence of ionized PAHs in the gaps of PPDs were earlier predicted by
Maaskant et al. 2014
considering a higher PAH-to-dust mass ratio equalling that of the ISM.
However, that is still
\sim
4 orders lower than that of the outer disk and does not anyway affect the main conclusions of this paper. A higher gas-to-dust ratio in the gap could have enhanced the PAH emission, although such observations are not so far available for T Cha, and could be investigated in the future.

6.2
PAH abundances

From laboratory and observational studies, it is found that the PAH emission bands are sensitive to PAH sizes (expressed here in terms of the number of C atoms,

) and ionization fraction (

Emission from the smaller PAHs is stronger in shorter wavelength bands. Hence, the 6.2/7.7, 6.2/11.3, and 7.7/11.3

\mu
m band ratios are proportional to
\phi

\mu

\sim
10%

(e.g.,
Draine et al. 2021

Therefore, the observation of multiple prominent AIBs in the JWST spectrum of T Cha is helpful in predicting
N_{\mathrm{C}}
and
\phi

M_{\mathrm{PAH}}/M_{\mathrm{dust}}

6.2.3
Implication of the estimated PAH abundances

From our modelling, we finally predict that the disk of T Cha has a population of smaller PAHs of
\sim 10-30
C atoms, with
\sim
85% neutral PAHs, and a PAH-to-dust mass ratio amounting to
\sim
16% of the ISM ratio of PAH-to-dust mass. Hence, our estimated PAH-to-dust mass ratio is consistent with earlier predictions while points to a high value among disks.
Our prediction of small PAH sizes and low ionization fraction largely follows the results from a few previous studies. The analyses of AIBs using the laboratory and theoretical PAH database suggested that the fitting of class
\mathcal{C}
profiles might need a random distribution of smaller (
N_{\mathrm{C}}<30
) and larger (
N_{\mathrm{C}}\geq 30
) PAHs, as well as a higher fraction of neutral PAHs
(
Cami 2011
;
Bauschlicher et al. 2018
)
.
Arun 2025
also predicted a population of smaller PAHs with
N_{\mathrm{C}}<30
in T Cha comparing to a theoretical size-charge grid for PAHs computed by
Maragkoudakis et al. 2020
.
In the context of PAH abundance uncertainties due to their cluster formation and adsorption into the larger grains,
models by
Lange et al. 2023
have predicted that the PAHs could be re-processed by UV irradiation as the larger grains reach the disk surface due to vertical mixing within a turbulent disk. A prevalence of smaller PAHs, with
N_{C}\leq 54
, towards the disk surface has been predicted as they are easier to get liberated due to such re-processing
(
Lange et al. 2021
;
Lange et al. 2023
)
.

Theoretical models have shown that the presence of PAHs on the disk surfaces can be very important in driving FUV phtoevaporative wind and limiting the gas lifetime in the disks
(
Gorti et al. 2009
;
Gorti & Hollenbach 2009
)
.

Nakatani et al. 2023
showed that if PAH mass is depleted to
\sim 0.1-1\%
of that in the ISM, the disk would evolve into a gas rich debris disk due to the lack of FUV photoevaporation. However, a higher PAH abundance larger than this critical value can drive H
2
wind driven by FUV photons, and reach a mass loss rate up to
\sim 10^{-8}

M_{\sun}
yr
-1
, which is comparable to the X-ray photoevaporation rate and much higher than EUV photoevaporation rate of
\sim 10^{-10}

M_{\sun}
yr
-1

(
Gorti et al. 2009
)
.
The higher PAH-to-dust mass ratio in T Cha might be indicative of a high efficiency of FUV photoevaporation active on the disk surface.

6.3
Model evaluation

Our model is not physically unique, since the explored parameter space might be limited due to generalized assumptions and the model may have certain degeneracies if explored in further detail. However, based on the literature we have been able to reduce the degeneracies in this particular work.

The emission from the PAHs depend on the opacities of the other dust species within the environment in a dust radiative transfer calculation. Hence, a properly reproduced thermal continuum ensures a proper estimation of the PAH abundances. From Fig
10
, we can understand the contribution of the PAHs over the thermal continuum produced by the other dust species. Our model satisfactorily reproduce the JWST continuum, except the
9-14

\mu
m region. The PAH abundances derived in this work mainly depend on the proper thermal balance in the outer disk, which may have been reasonably achieved, noting the fit of the
14-25

\mu
m continuum and the millimetre band fluxes. We further note that our modelled values of the outer disk parameters are in good agreement with the previous studies, which performed robust analyses of the resolved outer disk data (e.g. H15, P17, H18).

By exploring the models with minor deviation from our best fiducial model, we find that some degeneracies may exist between, for example, the scale height and the dust mass of the wall, as well as the flaring index
\beta
.
Note that the variation in the flaring index
\beta
also simultaneously changes the effective scale height at the inner edge of the outer disk.
To observe the dependency of the model on a particular outer disk parameter, we have shown the variation of the model spectrum by changing one parameter of the outer disk at a time in Appendix
D
. In Fig.
12
, we explore the
\beta
dependency of the model (also adjusting the respective scale heights, see figure caption) to find that there is no significant difference among the model fit to the optical, mid-IR and millimetre continua. Differences are visible in the PAH band strength and in the far-IR continuum within
30-500

\mu
m that was mainly covered by
Herschel
, and which is supposed to be affected by the ‘see-saw’ variability, and hence we could not fit.

Therefore, an important source of uncertainty in estimating the PAH-to-dust mass ratio might be the degeneracy affected modelled value of the flaring index, which influences the PAH emission.
However, we find the
\beta
values from the previous studies to be within
1.0-1.1
, particularly the results from the observations in gas emission and scattered light (e.g., H15, P17) that traces the disk surface well. Hence, we expect our estimation of
\beta=1.1
is very close to the actual value, and hence may rely on the PAH-to-dust mass ratio estimated considering that value.

7
Summary and conclusions

We present a parametric dust radiative transfer model of the disk around T Cha that reproduces the mid-IR JWST spectrum of the object obtained using MIRI-MRS instrument. Our fitting of the JWST spectrum re-establishes the geometric structure of the disk - an inner and an outer disk separated by a dusk gap - as inferred from earlier Spitzer, VLT, and ALMA observations, while also proposes the possible presence of smaller dust grains within the gap region. We satisfactorily fit the observed AIBs around 6.2, 8.1, and 11.3
\mu
m using our modelled PAH bands generated with our best fiducial model spectrum. The prominent AIBs in the JWST spectrum allows
a optimized estimation of the PAH abundances in terms of their sizes, ionization fraction, and mass from our modelling.
Our main conclusions are as follows:

-

We predict a population of small PAHs of
\lesssim 30
C atoms in the disk of T Cha. The population is of
\sim 85\%
neutral PAHs. Since the disk PAH emission is mainly observed from the surface layers, the PAH population more likely represent that of the disk surface. This is in line with theory of reprocessing of the clustered PAHs by the FUV irradiation that liberates only the smaller PAHs as the clusters arrive the disk surfaces due to turbulence
(
Lange et al. 2023
)
.

-

We estimate a PAH-to-dust mass ratio of
6.5(\pm 0.1)\times 10^{-3}
in the disk of T Cha, which corresponds to
16(\pm 0.3)\%
of the ISM value of the PAH-to-dust mass ratio. This value points towards a higher value found among disks. Such a higher PAH mass within the disk, coupled with a preferred population of smaller PAHs in the disk surface suggest that FUV photoevaporation might be very efficient in case of T Cha, which might drive a mass-loss close to the order of
\sim 10^{-9}-10^{-8}

M_{\sun}
yr
-1
as calculated theoretically (e.g.,
Nakatani et al. 2023
).

-

We predict the presence of an outer disk wall having smaller dust grains of
0.01-4

\mu
m in order to better reproduce the
14-25

\mu
m JWST spectral continuum. The outer region of outer disk comprise grains of sizes 0.01
\mu
m to 5 mm, with a shallower size-distribution with a power-law index of
-3.3
, which are necessary to reproduce the slope of the millimetre fluxes. This indicates an efficient grain growth in the outer region of the outer disk of T Cha.

-

We propose an abundance of sub-micron dust grains in the gap that might be coupled with the accreting gas within the gap in order to justify the JWST continuum plateau around
\sim
10
\mu
m, hence also supporting the possibility of replenishment of the inner disk after its destruction as suggested by
Xie et al. 2025
.

Our model is not unique owing to the probable degeneracies, which may be improved by future observations, further constraining the models. Simultaneous line and dust emission models can potentially reduce the degeneracies, which needs a more complex treatment which may be addressed in future works.

Appendix A
Additional details on the AIB profile fitting procedure described in Sec.
4

Smith et al. 2007
obtained individual fits to each observed galaxy spectrum using a model, assumed to be the sum of the stellar spectrum, modified blackbody SEDs representing the dust continuum, and the Drude profiles. However, we note that a full spectral fit using a simplified assumption of modified blackbodies representing the dust continuum, similar to that for the galaxy spectra in
Smith et al. 2007
, is not possible to achieve in case of the T Cha spectrum. This is due to the reason that the mid-IR spectrum of T Cha has a relatively complicated shape with the presence of the dip and the plateau around 10
\mu
m. Since our main scope in this paper is to use the AIB profile components to derive the modified PAH opacities (see Sec.
B
), we assume that can be achieved from individual fits to the PAH profiles at 6.2, 8.1, and 11.3
\mu
m as well.

First, we define the stellar flux using a fixed stellar spectrum of a star of
T_{\mathrm{eff}}=5500
K, with log
(g\>\mathrm{cm}\>\mathrm{s^{-2}})=3.5

(
Castelli & Kurucz 2003
)
. Next, we define the modified black body continuum that will represent the thermal dust emission.
In general, the inner disk region consists of hot dust, with
T\sim 1000
K,
accounting for the NIR excess, and the rest of the inner disk SED contribution is due to colder dust of
T=100-300
K. Thus, we make a reasonable assumption that the dust continuum under both the 6.2 and 8.1
\mu
m AIBs can be represented by the combination of modified blackbodies of
T=1000
, 300, and 100 K. For the continuum under the 11.3
\mu
m band, we separately define a single modified blackbody of
T=235
K, by visually inspecting from a set of
T=200-300
K modified blackbodies. The flux contribution (strength) of each black body component is obtained during the optimization procedure discussed below.

We perform a two-step optimization process to obtain the best-fit profile components for each AIB. We describe here the example of 6.2
\mu
m band for clarity. In first step, we assume a single Drude profile representing the observed 6.2
\mu
m AIB profile. We fit the combined spectrum of the star
+
modified black bodies (three in case of 6.2
\mu
m band)
+
the single Drude profile, to the observed JWST spectrum within the region covering the 6.2
\mu
m band, in absolute flux units. We optimize the Drude profile parameters:
\lambda_{\mathrm{1}}
,
b_{\mathrm{1}}
, and
\gamma_{\mathrm{1}}
of that single profile and the fluxes of each black body in this step. Note that this provides a good approximation of the relative contribution of the wings the AIB profile and the dust continuum emission. The second optimization step assumes multiple Drude profiles for the 6.2
\mu
m band, with varying profile parameters
\lambda_{\mathrm{j}}
,
b_{\mathrm{j}}
, and
\gamma_{\mathrm{j}}
, with fixed stellar spectrum, as well as fixing the fluxes strengths of the combined modified black bodies obtained in the first step. Similar approaches are taken to obtain the profile components of the 8.1 and 11.3
\mu
m bands.

\mu

The class
\mathcal{A}
template significantly differs from the class
\mathcal{C}
, particularly in the 7-9
\mu
m region. Therefore, to replicate the class
\mathcal{C}
signatures in the AIB profiles of T Cha, we modify the resonance parameters of the relevant Drude components in Table 4 of
Draine et al. 2021
. Note that we only modulate the parameters that alters the shape of the generated profiles, and not the original astro-PAH model.

Based on
Li & Draine 2001
, the absorption cross section per carbon atom contributed by the
j
th Drude component is

C^{\mathrm{PAH}}_{\mathrm{abs},j}(\lambda)/N_{\mathrm{C}}=\frac{2}{\pi}\frac{\gamma_{j}\lambda_{j}\sigma_{\mathrm{int},j}}{\left(\lambda/\lambda_{j}-\lambda_{j}/\lambda\right)^{2}+\gamma_{j}^{2}},\\

(9)

where,
\sigma_{\mathrm{int},j}
is the strength of the profile,
N_{\mathrm{C}}
denotes the number of C atoms, and the rest of the symbols are the same as in Equation
1
. Note that
\sigma_{\mathrm{int},j}
can be tuned for a number of Drude profile components such that it can replicate a given PAH template. Further, the choice of the
\sigma_{\mathrm{int},j}
may be guided by
\lambda_{\mathrm{j}}
,
b_{\mathrm{j}}
, and
\gamma_{\mathrm{j}}
corresponding to each profile.
Comparing Equation
1
and Equation
9
, we derive
\sigma_{\mathrm{int},j}
for all the Drude components calculated for the 6.2, 8.1 and 11.3
\mu
m AIBs listed in Table
1
, in a relative scale, using the values of
\lambda_{\mathrm{j}}
,
b_{\mathrm{j}}
, and
\gamma_{\mathrm{j}}
.
Then, we scale the total
\sigma_{\mathrm{int},j}
values individually summed for the 6.2, 8.1 and 11.3
\mu
m bands to the absolute
\sigma_{\mathrm{int},j}
scales corresponding to the 6.2
\mu
m band, the 7.7+8.6 complex, and the 11.3
\mu
m band, respectively, using the values given by
Draine et al. 2021
. The scaling is first applied to get the
\sigma_{\mathrm{int},j}
for the neutral PAHs in the required absolute units, and then the values were further scaled with appropriate factors to obtain the
\sigma_{\mathrm{int},j}
values for ionized PAHs. Since some components carries the dependency on the hydrogen-to-carbon ratio (
\mathrm{H/C}
, see
Draine & Li 2007
;
Draine et al. 2021
), during the scaling process, we adopt
\mathrm{H/C}=0.375
(also see below).

Following this process, we obtain the cross sections for 30 different sizes of PAHs spanning the size range of
\sim 3-50
Å, corresponding to
N_{\mathrm{C}}\sim 10-50000
. The cross sections for each PAH sizes are calculated in a wavelength range of 1 nanometre to 1 millimetre. Fig.
8
shows a comparison of the neutral and ionized PAH absorption cross section per C atom given by
Draine et al. 2021
and that calculated in this work corresponding to
N_{\mathrm{C}}=24
. We finally configure the files including the cross section data following the required format by
MCFOST
in order for them to be read by the code during radiative transfer calculations. To run the calculations, it is also needed to supply the size distribution, mass fraction of the dust species and the total dust mass in a region, from which the code can internally calculate the opacities for individual species. Thus, the calculated PAH cross sections are are translated to opacities within the code to generate the PAH emission bands in the modelled spectrum that can be compared to the observed AIBs.

Appendix C
Peak-to-peak flux estimation of the AIBs

To estimate the peak-to-peak flux of each AIB profile, we first define two base points towards the blue and red wings of the profile, where the profile meets the continuum, and measure their fluxes. We join the two points with a straight line that approximates the continuum (base) level. We then use linear interpolation between the two base points and obtain the base flux corresponding to the peak wavelength of the profile. Hence, the difference between the peak absolute flux and the base flux gives the peak-to-peak flux of the profile.
