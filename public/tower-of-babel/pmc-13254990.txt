Cinematic rendering-enhanced computed tomography of right intracardiac thrombi: A case report
Authors: Elias Lugo-Fagundo, Connor W Smith, Taha M Ahmed, Elliot K Fishman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntracardiac thrombi of the right heart are a rare entity, often presenting a significant risk for pulmonary embolism (PE). Two subtypes of intracardiac thrombi have been described, namely floating right heart thrombi and mural thrombi. The first-line imaging modality for the diagnosis of intracardiac thrombi is transthoracic echocardiography, but its sensitivity for detecting right heart thrombi remains limited. Advanced post-processing techniques, including cinematic rendering (CR) and black blood cinematic rendering (BBCR), applied to computed tomography (CT) have emerged with enhanced visualization in the evaluation of right heart thrombi. In this article, we report the case of a 46-year-old male who presented with progressive dyspnea, bilateral lower extremity swelling, and hemoptysis. We highlight the role of CT in the evaluation of intracardiac thrombi and the potential of CR as an adjunctive visualization tool to further clarify radiological features of this entity.Keywords: Black blood imaging, Cardiac, Cardiovascular, Cinematic Rendering, Computed Tomography, Emboli, Thrombi

Introduction

Intracardiac thrombi of the right heart are an uncommon, yet clinically significant manifestation of venous thromboembolic (VTE) disease [1]. Broadly, intracardiac thrombi may be categorized into two main subtypes based on their pathogenesis: floating right heart thrombi (FRHTS) and mural thrombi [2]. FRHTS, often termed “thrombi in transit,” originate within the peripheral venous system and migrate into the right atrium or ventricle, where their mobility and instability confer a high propensity for pulmonary embolism (PE) and, ultimately, increase the risk of mortality [3,4]. In contrast, mural or sessile thrombi arise within the heart itself, typically in the setting of ventricular dysfunction, chamber dilation, or localized stasis [5].

Right-sided thrombi not only present a difficult clinical picture but also pose a significant diagnostic challenge. Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) is typically the first-line imaging modality when an intracardiac thrombus is suspected; however, its sensitivity for detecting right-sided thrombi is limited [6]. On the other hand, transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) offers improved visualization, yet its minimally invasive nature and residual difficulty in detecting thin mural thrombi or differentiating different stages of thrombus organization constrain its utility [6,7]. Consequently, these limitations have contributed to the expanding role of computed tomography (CT) in the evaluation of suspected intracardiac thrombi. When performed with electrocardiographic-gating (ECG) and appropriate contrast timing, CT demonstrates excellent sensitivity and specificity for FRHTS, while delivering high spatial and temporal resolution, comprehensive assessment of the pulmonary vasculature and right ventricular function, and, most critically, reliable identification of thrombi with complex morphology [[8], [9], [10]].

Recent advances in 3-dimensional (3D) post-processing methods have further improved the visualization of intraluminal thrombi by generating photorealistic reconstructions [12,13]. For example, cinematic rendering (CR) is a 3-dimensional (3D) post-processing technique applied to volumetric CT datasets that utilizes a global illumination lighting model to produce photorealistic images with enhanced depiction of complex anatomical relationships [12]. In contrast to conventional volume rendering (VR), which relies on simplified ray-casting algorithms, CR incorporates complex light propagation, including scattering and interactions with adjacent voxels, resulting in improved depth perception and more realistic visualization of tissue interfaces [12]. Similarly, black-blood cinematic rendering (BBCR) is a recently described derivative technique in which the contrast-enhanced blood pool is rendered dark and transparent through selective manipulation of voxel opacity, thereby improving visualization of intraluminal structures and endocardial interfaces [13]. By supressing the signal from intravascular contrast, BBCR facilitates improved delineation of intracardiac thrombi, masses, and device-related structures relative to the surrounding blood pool. These techniques are particularly valuable when echocardiographic windows are suboptimal or when magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is not feasible.

In this article, we present an uncommon case of both right-sided “thrombi in transit” and mural thrombi in a 46-year-old man and discuss the corresponding imaging findings. This report highlights the role of CR as an adjunctive visualization tool in the assessment of intracardiac thrombi.

Case report

A 46-year-old man with polysubstance use disorder presented with progressive dyspnea, bilateral lower extremity swelling, and hemoptysis. Transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated severely reduced biventricular systolic function with multiple large, independently mobile right atrial thrombi. Contrast-enhanced CT of the chest demonstrated extensive thrombi within the right atrium, right ventricle, and pulmonary arteries (Fig. 1). The patient was admitted for decompensated heart failure complicated by intracardiac and pulmonary thrombi and symptomatically improved following diuresis and anticoagulation. Evaluation for an underlying hypercoagulable state or deep venous thrombosis was unrevealing, and cross-sectional imaging showed no evidence of malignancy. While the mural thrombi were hypothesized to result from flow stasis due to dilated atria and ventricles in the setting of a reduced ejection fraction, their unusual location and unprovoked nature prompted initiation of lifelong anticoagulation at discharge. One month following discharge, the patient suffered a cardiac arrest and was pronounced dead after unsuccessful resuscitation.

Discussion

Right-sided intracardiac thrombi are rare but represent a severe manifestation of VTE with significant associated risk [1]. While the clinical implications of these entities are well established, this case also highlights the role of advanced CT post-processing techniques, including CR and BBCR, as adjunctive tools for improved visualization of intracardiac pathology. In contrast to left-sided thrombi, right-sided thrombi exhibit greater morphologic heterogeneity and arise through 2 primary pathogenic mechanisms: FRHTS and mural thrombi. FRHTS originate in the deep veins of the lower extremities and may embolize into the right atrium or ventricle, where their marked mobility confers significant lethality. Anfinogenova et al. report mortality rates ranging from 80% to 100% for FRHTS, attributed to their mobility and high propensity to embolize [3]. As seen in this case, approximately 85% of patients with FRHTS have concurrent PE, and 7-18% of patients with PE harbor a coexistent floating thrombus [2,3]. Other risk factors include concomitant deep vein thrombosis (DVT), prior VTE, immobility, malignancy, and acquired thrombophilia [4]. Diagnosis of these “thrombi in transit” is typically made via TTE, which characteristically demonstrates a mobile, serpiginous structure traversing the tricuspid valve [1,2]. Sessile thrombi, by comparison, are immobile lesions that form within the right heart in situ, most commonly in the setting of ventricular dysfunction, chamber dilation, or regional stasis, and may also occur in association with indwelling catheters and device leads [1,5]. Despite their reduced motility, mural thrombi still carry meaningful embolic potential.

The coexistence of both floating and mural thrombi within the right heart, as in this case, occurring in the absence of DVT or known coagulopathy presents a unique challenge. Severe biventricular dysfunction with global chamber dilation contributes to flow stasis and endothelial remodeling, creating conditions favorable for in situ mural thrombus formation. In contrast, the origin of the floating thrombi remains unexplained due to the absence of identifiable venous sources of embolization. This combination of morphologies, coupled with the absence of a demonstrable FRHT origin, underscores the diagnostic uncertainty and therapeutic complexity presented by this case.

Although TTE is the diagnostic study of choice for suspected cardiac thrombi, it identifies right-sided thrombi in only 30-60% of cases, often missing smaller lesions, thrombi within the right atrial appendage, or thrombi adherent to device leads [6]. Conversely, TEE offers markedly improved sensitivity, detecting right atrial thrombi in 100% of cases, making it the most reliable imaging modality; however, important limitations persist [6]. For example, though minimally invasive, TEE cannot distinguish subacute thrombi, which carry a higher risk of embolization, from organized thrombi [7]. In addition, differentiating thrombus from adjacent myocardium can be challenging, rendering thin mural thrombi particularly difficult to identify.

CT, although adjunctive to echocardiography, provides complementary and clinically actionable information, particularly when performed with electrocardiographic-gating (ECG) and optimized contrast timing [8,9]. ECG gating markedly reduces cardiac motion artifact, improves depiction of intrathoracic structures, and enhances visualization of intracardiac thrombi [10]. Under these conditions, CT demonstrates excellent diagnostic accuracy, with reported sensitivity and specificity of 100% and 97%, respectively, in the detection of free-floating right heart thrombi [11], which often demonstrate a serpiginous configuration centered on the tricuspid valve [9]. On CT, acute cardiac thrombi typically appear as homogeneous, low-attenuation, non-enhancing intracardiac filling defects [9]. In contrast, chronic thrombi may demonstrate a more heterogeneous appearance due to organization and fibrosis, may develop a fibrous capsule, and can occasionally exhibit areas of high attenuation consistent with dystrophic calcification [9].

CR and BBCR further extend the utility of CT by providing enhanced 3D visualization of intracardiac structures and their spatial relationships. Rather than improving detection per se, these techniques facilitate improved depiction of thrombus morphology, attachment points, and relationships to adjacent cardiac structures, which may be difficult to fully appreciate on conventional 2-dimensional or standard volume rendered images. In this case, the combination of CR and ECG gating yielded high-fidelity visualizations of the systemic and pulmonary arteries with marked reduction in motion-related artifact [12]. Rowe et al. demonstrated that CR enhances visualization of small thrombi, including those on prosthetic or bioprosthetic valves, chronic PE adherent to vessel walls, and partially thrombosed aortic aneurysms [12].

More recently, BBCR, a preset that renders the contrast-enhanced blood pool dark and transparent in a manner analogous to black-blood MRI, has been shown to enhance visualization of cardiovascular intraluminal structures [13]. By selective supressing the signal from intravascular contrast, BBCR allows improved delineation of thrombus relative to the surrounding blood pool and may better demonstrate endoluminal detail, particularly in complex or multilobulated lesions. Brookmeyer et al. reported improved detection and characterization of intraluminal thrombi compared to standard CT techniques [13]. However, it is important to note that intracardiac thrombi are often readily identifiable on standard CT imaging, and CR and BBCR should be regarded as adjunctive tools that enhance visualization rather than primary diagnostic modalities. These advances highlight the potential role of CR and BBCR as valuable adjuncts in situations where conventional imaging is limited and MRI is not feasible, particularly in the acute care setting.
