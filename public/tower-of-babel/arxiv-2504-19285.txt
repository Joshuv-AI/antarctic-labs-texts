Control of active field theories at minimal dissipation
Artur Soriani, Elsen Tjhung, Étienne Fodor, Tomer Markovich

Abstract
Advances in experimental techniques enable the precise manipulation of a large variety of active systems, which constantly dissipate energy to sustain nonequilibrium phenomena without any equilibrium equivalent. To design novel materials out of active systems, an outstanding challenge is to rationalize how material properties can be optimally controlled by applying external perturbations. However, equilibrium thermodynamics is inadequate to guide the control of such nonequilibrium systems. Therefore, there is a dire need for a novel framework to provide a systematic toolbox for the thermodynamic control of active matter. Here, we build an optimization procedure for generic active field theories within a thermodynamically consistent formulation. Central to our approach is the distinction between the protocol heat , which is dissipated only during manipulation, and the total heat , which also accounts for the post-manipulation dissipation. We demonstrate that the latter generically features a global minimum with respect to the protocol duration. We deploy our versatile approach to an active theory of phase separation, and examine the scalings of the optimal protocol duration with respect to activity and system size. Remarkably, we reveal that the landscape of steady-state dissipation regulates the crossover between optimal control strategies for a finite duration.

In this paper, we search for generic control strategies in field theories of active matter. We propose a versatile framework to predict the optimal protocols which change material properties by tuning some experimentally accessible parameters. Inspired by a previous optimization procedure, originally proposed for passive systems
[
54
]
and recently adapted to active matter
[
1
]
, we focus on minimizing the heat generated by smooth and slow protocols. In contrast with
[
54
,
1
]
, we argue that the
protocol heat

Q_{\rm p}
, which is dissipated only during the protocol, is less adequate for optimization than the
total heat

Q_{\rm t}
, which also accounts for the post-protocol dissipation [Fig.
1
(a)]. For passive systems,
Q_{\rm t}
is optimal at long protocol duration, as expected from standard thermodynamics
[
1
]
, while it reaches a minimum at a finite duration for active systems [Fig.
1
(b)]. Instead,
Q_{\rm p}
is always zero for a vanishing protocol duration, and need not feature any other local minimum even for active systems [Fig.
1

We apply our framework to a thermodynamically consistent field theory, referred to as Chemical Model B (CMB), which describes nonequilibrium phase separation. Using dynamical response theory, we provide analytical predictions for protocols driving some homogeneous configurations of the system, and examine the scalings of the optimal duration with respect to system size and activity. Remarkably, we reveal a crossover between control strategies, and rationalize it based on the landscape of steady-state dissipation. Overall, our results demonstrate the potential of our novel approach to guiding control in a large class of active systems.

Results

The role of relaxation: Protocol vs total heat

We consider the external manipulation of a system at constant temperature
T
through a finite-time protocol varying the parameter
a(t)
from
a_{0}=a(0)
to
a_{\tau}=a(\tau)
. The system is assumed to be initially (
t=0
) at steady state [Fig.
1
(a)]. For any finite protocol duration
\tau
, the protocol is not quasi-static, so that the system is not in steady state at any time
t\in(0,\tau)
. After the protocol (
t>\tau
), the parameter
a(t>\tau)=a_{\tau}
is constant, and the system relaxes towards the stationary configuration given by
a_{\tau}
. We define the post-protocol time
\tau_{\rm r}
, which is far larger than the relaxation of the slowest mode, such that the system reaches a steady state at time
\tau+\tau_{\rm r}
.

We denote by
Q_{\rm p}
the
protocol heat
dissipated by the system during the protocol. During the subsequent relaxation, an additional contribution, referred to as the
relaxation heat

Q_{\rm r}
is dissipated, although the parameter
a(t>\tau)=a_{\tau}
is constant. The
total heat

Q_{\rm t}
, which is dissipated during the protocol and the post-protocol relaxation, follows as
Q_{\rm t}=Q_{\rm p}+Q_{\rm r}
. Clearly,
(Q_{\rm p},Q_{\rm r},Q_{\rm t})
all strongly depend on the shape of the protocol
a(t)
and its duration
\tau
. Conservation of energy states that
Q_{\rm t}
is given by the sum of the total work
W_{\rm t}
and the change in the system energy
E
:

Q_{\rm t}=W_{\rm t}+E(t=0)-E(t={\tau+\tau_{\rm r}})\,,

(1)

where
W_{\rm t}
can be decomposed in terms of the contributions produced by the external manipulation (
W_{\rm ext}
, accumulated only during the protocol) and by any non-conservative active forces (
W_{\rm act}
, accumulated during both the protocol and the subsequent relaxation):

W_{\rm t}=W_{\text{ext}}+W_{\text{act}}\,.

(2)

In the absence of external manipulation, the quantity
W_{\text{act}}
is the energetic cost needed to sustain non-equilibrium steady states
[
55
]
.
In the presence of fluctuations, Eqs. (
1
) and (
2
) take the same form for the stochastic definitions of heat, work, and energy
[
13
,
51
]
. In what follows, we focus on average values; in particular, the averages of
E
at times
t=0
and
t=\tau+\tau_{\rm r}
are evaluated in steady state, and thus are independent of the protocol.

For passive systems (
W_{\text{act}}=0
), Eq. (
1
) reduces to the first law of thermodynamics in its standard form, so minimizing either
Q_{\rm t}
or
W_{\rm ext}
leads to the same optimal protocol. The second law of thermodynamics states that
W_{\rm ext}
can only be larger than the difference between the Helmholtz free energy evaluated at
a_{0}
and
a_{\tau}

[
1
]
. Likewise,
Q_{\rm t}
is bounded from below by the difference of the system entropy at
a_{0}
and
a_{\tau}
, and the bound is only saturated for quasi-static protocols. For instantaneous protocols (
\tau=0
),
Q_{\rm p}
vanishes since there is no sufficient time to dissipate heat during the protocol [Fig.
1
(b)], whereas
Q_{\rm t}
has a non-zero value as a signature of the dissipation ensued during the post-protocol relaxation [Fig.
1
(c)].

The situation is markedly different for active systems. In steady state,
W_{\text{act}}
increases extensively with time to sustain the internal processes at the basis of activity. Then, at large duration
\tau
, both
Q_{\rm t}
and
Q_{\rm p}
linearly increase with
\tau
, in contrast with passive systems, whereas
W_{\rm ext}
saturates as in the passive case. It follows that
Q_{\rm t}

always
features a global minimum at finite
\tau
[Fig.
1
(c)], which achieves the best trade-off between the dissipation coming from internal processes and that stemming from external manipulation. Remarkably,
Q_{\rm p}
vanishes at
\tau=0
as in passive systems, and it may feature a local minimum
\tau_{\rm p}
under certain conditions [Fig.
1
(b)], in contrast with passive systems. For instance, for a scalar field theory, these conditions require a small activity (and also small system sizes, if the field is conserved), as we discuss in detail in the next sections.

In this paper, minimizing
Q_{\rm t}
(rather than
W_{\text{ext}}

[
56
]
or
Q_{\rm p}

[
1
]
) is the objective of our optimization. In particular, our aim is to quantify the optimal duration
\tau_{\rm t}
at minimal
Q_{\rm t}
, which separates the two regimes of dissipation inherent to active systems [Fig.
1
(b)]. Specifically, we deploy a systematic optimization framework
[
54
,
1
]
in a thermodynamically consistent formulation of active field theories
[
3
]
, examine the corresponding scalings of
\tau_{\rm t}
, and unveil a crossover between optimal protocols.

Energetics of active field theories

We describe the energy exchange between a given active system and its surrounding reservoirs within linear irreversible thermodynamics
[
57
,
3
]
; the energy reservoir absorbs the heat dissipated by the system, and the internal active driving is sustained by some active driving reservoir (typically chemical fuel). In practice, we consider here the coupling of two scalar fields: the density of active particles
\phi({\bf r},t)
, and the concentration of fuel molecules
n({\bf r},t)
. The specific nature of active driving is unimportant
1
1

1

What is important is the time-reversal signature of the active driving. For instance, a flux of photons has a different time-reversal signature than chemical driving
[
3
]
.
, and we focus on the case of chemical reactions for illustration purposes. The interactions between active particles are described by the free energy functional
F([\phi],a)
, which should not be confused with the Helmholtz free energy bounding
W_{\rm ext}
in passive systems
[
1
]
. The system is maintained away from equilibrium by setting the difference of some chemical potentials
\Delta\mu
constant; for instance, involving reactant and product molecules whose reaction fuels the particle activity
[
3
]
.

Given that the total number of active particles is conserved,
\phi
obeys a conservation law in terms of the diffusive current
{\boldsymbol{J}}
:

\dot{\phi}+\boldsymbol{\nabla}\cdot\boldsymbol{J}=0\,,

(3)

where
\dot{\phi}=\partial_{t}\phi
.
We describe the stochastic coupling of
(\phi,n)
through a linear relation between thermodynamic fluxes
\mathbb{J}=(\boldsymbol{J},\dot{n})
and forces
\mathbb{F}=(-\boldsymbol{\nabla}(\delta F/\delta\phi),\Delta\mu)

[
3
]
:

\mathbb{J}=\mathbb{L}\cdot\mathbb{F}+T^{1/2}\mathbb{\Lambda}+T\bbnu\,.

(4)

where the noise term
\mathbb{\Lambda}
is Gaussian with zero mean and correlations given by:

\left\langle\mathbb{\Lambda}({\bf r},t)\mathbb{\Lambda}^{\intercal}({\bf r}^{\prime},t^{\prime})\right\rangle=2\mathbb{L}\delta({\bf r}-{\bf r}^{\prime})\delta(t-t^{\prime})\,.

(5)

Here,
⊺
refers to vector transpose, and we have set the Boltzmann constant to unity. Whenever
\mathbb{L}
depends on
\phi
and its gradients, the noise
\mathbb{\Lambda}
is multiplicative. Then, interpreting Eq. (
4
) within the Stratonovitch convention
[
59
,
4
]
, the spurious drift
\bbnu
can be explicitly calculated for any given
\mathbb{L}
(see Ref.
[
61
]
, section VI).

For
d
spatial dimensions,
\mathbb{J}
and
\mathbb{F}
are vectors in
d+1
dimensions. The Onsager matrix
\mathbb{L}
is symmetric and semi-positive definite:

\mathbb{L}=\begin{pmatrix}\lambda_{\phi}\boldsymbol{1}_{d}&\boldsymbol{C}(\phi,\boldsymbol{\nabla}\phi)\\
\boldsymbol{C}^{\intercal}(\phi,\boldsymbol{\nabla}\phi)&\lambda_{n}\end{pmatrix}\,,

(6)

where
(\lambda_{\phi},\lambda_{n})
are mobility constants and
\boldsymbol{1}_{d}
is the
d\times d
identity matrix. Physically,
\delta F/\delta\phi
represents the free-energy cost of adding a particle, while
\Delta\mu
corresponds to the energy gain per fuel molecule during the chemical reaction. The diagonal terms in Eq. (
6
) indicate that the negative gradient of
\delta F/\delta\phi
drives the diffusive current
\boldsymbol{J}
, while
\Delta\mu
drives the chemical reaction rate
\dot{n}
. The off-diagonal terms in Eq. (
6
) couple the chemical reaction to the diffusive dynamics. In general, the coupling term
\boldsymbol{C}
is a function of
\phi
and its gradients. Through this coupling,
\Delta\mu
indirectly drives
\phi
to operate away from equilibrium.

Considering a given protocol
a(t)
[Fig.
1
], the expression of
Q_{\rm t}
follows from the product between the thermodynamic fluxes and forces
[
51
]
:

Q_{\rm t}=\left\langle\int_{0}^{\tau+\tau_{\rm r}}\int_{V}\mathbb{J}\cdot\mathbb{F}\,\mathrm{d}{\bf r}\mathrm{d}t\right\rangle\,,

(7)

where
\langle\cdot\rangle
denotes an average over noise realizations, and
V
refers to the system size. The conservation of energy [Eq. (
1
)] relates
Q_{\rm t}
to the variation of the system energy
E=\left\langle F\right\rangle
and to the total work
W_{\rm t}
; see stochastic thermodynamics in Methods. The work produced by externally varying
a(t)
during the protocol reads

W_{\text{ext}}=\int_{0}^{\tau}\dot{a}\left\langle\partial_{a}F\right\rangle\mathrm{d}t\,.

(8)

The work produced by the chemical reaction over the entire process is given by

W_{\text{act}}=\left\langle\int_{0}^{\tau+\tau_{\rm r}}\int_{V}\Delta\mu\dot{n}\,\mathrm{d}{\bf r}\mathrm{d}t\right\rangle\,,

(9)

which can also be written as

W_{\text{act}}=(\tau+\tau_{\rm r})P_{0}+\int_{0}^{\tau+\tau_{\rm r}}\langle P\rangle\mathrm{d}t\,,

(10)

where the power
P_{0}=\lambda_{n}V\Delta\mu^{2}
is a background contribution, independent of the coupling between the diffusive and chemical sectors of the dynamics, and

\displaystyle P=\int_{V}p\,\mathrm{d}{\bf r}\,,\quad p=\frac{\Delta\mu}{\lambda_{\phi}}\boldsymbol{C}\cdot\left(\boldsymbol{J}-\Delta\mu\,\boldsymbol{C}\right)

(11)

embodies how the
\phi
-dynamics affects the dissipation rate. It is worth noting that
W_{\text{act}}\geq 0
, as discussed in
[
3
]
. The expressions of heat and work [Eqs. (
7
-
11
)] straightforwardly extend to more complex field theories
[
3
,
51
]
, beyond the simple case of a single conserved scalar field [Eq. (
3
)].

The relation between the protocol heat
Q_{\rm p}
, the energy
E
, and the total work
W_{\rm t}
is identical to that for
Q_{\rm t}
in Eq. (
1
), with two key modifications: (i)
E(\tau+\tau_{\rm r})
is replaced by
E(\tau)
, where
E(\tau)
denotes a non-stationary average of
F
at time
\tau
; and (ii) in the definition of
W_{\rm act}
[Eq. (
10
)], the time
\tau+\tau_{\rm r}
is replaced by
\tau
.

Importantly, the averages in Eqs. (
8
-
11
) are not stationary, and depend on the details of the protocol
a(t)
. Whenever
a
is fixed (
\dot{a}=0
and
W_{\rm ext}=0
), these averages reduce to their steady-state values, so that
W_{\rm act}
scales like
\tau+\tau_{\rm r}
. In such a case, we have
E(0)=E(\tau)=E(\tau+\tau_{\rm r})
, and we deduce from Eq. (
1
) that both
(Q_{\rm p},Q_{\rm t})
diverge with the protocol duration. This regime reproduces the scaling of slow protocols [Figs.
1
(b-c)], and is a direct consequence of the permanent fuel consumption, which is present even when the parameter
a
is kept constant.

Perturbative treatment for slow protocols

We offer a systematic approach to express the heats
(Q_{\rm p},Q_{\rm t})
as functionals of the protocol
a(t)
for a generic active field theory [Eqs. (
3
-
6
)]. Inspired by previous works
[
54
,
1
]
, we rely on a perturbative expansion around the quasistatic limit (
\Omega\tau\gg 1
, where
\Omega
is the slowest relaxation frequency of the system (see Ref.
[
61
]
, section IV.B), and assume that the protocol is smooth by neglecting any discontinuity in
a(t)
; see dynamical response theory (DRT) in Methods. Within DRT, we obtain an implicit expression of
(Q_{\rm p},Q_{\rm t})
for an arbitrary protocol
a(t)
as

\displaystyle Q_{\rm t}

\displaystyle=(\tau+\tau_{\rm r})P_{0}+\tau_{\rm r}\langle P\rangle_{\text{s}}(a_{\tau})+B_{\rm t}+\int_{0}^{\tau}L(a,\dot{a})\mathrm{d}t\,,

(12)

\displaystyle Q_{\rm p}

\displaystyle=\tau P_{0}+B_{\rm t}+\dot{a}_{\tau}B_{\rm p}+\int_{0}^{\tau}L(a,\dot{a})\mathrm{d}t\,,

where
\langle\cdot\rangle_{\text{s}}
denotes a stationary average, and
(B_{\rm t},B_{\rm p})
are boundary terms set by
(a_{0},a_{\tau})
(see Ref.
[
61
]
, section I). The Lagrangian
L
is the only contribution depending on the full protocol:

L(a,\dot{a})=m(a)\dot{a}^{2}+\langle P\rangle_{\text{s}}(a)\,.

(13)

Within Lagrangian mechanics,
L
maps into the Lagrangian of a particle with position
a
and effective mass
m(a)
subject to the potential
-\langle P\rangle_{\text{s}}(a)
. We relate
m(a)
to some response functions (or, alternatively, some correlation functions
[
1
]
) measured at fixed
a
(see Ref.
[
61
]
, section I).

At large
\tau
,
L
is dominated by the term
\langle P\rangle_{\text{s}}(a)
, so that
(Q_{\rm p},Q_{\rm t})
scale like
\tau
. At short
\tau
,
L
is dominated by the term
m(a)\dot{a}^{2}
, so that
(Q_{\rm p},Q_{\rm t})
scale like
1/\tau
; although such a scaling deviates from the expectations for
Q_{\rm p}
[Fig.
1
], it can still help capture the location of any local minimum. In fact, the crossover between the DRT predictions at large and small
\tau
always entails a minimum for
(Q_{\rm p},Q_{\rm t})
at finite
\tau
. For a given
a(t)
, we deduce from Eqs. (
12
-
13
) the location of the optimal durations:

\tau_{\rm p}^{2}=\frac{\dot{a}_{s=1}B_{\rm p}+\int_{0}^{1}\dot{a}^{2}m(a)\mathrm{d}s}{P_{0}+\int_{0}^{1}\langle P\rangle_{\text{s}}(a)\mathrm{d}s}\,,\quad\tau_{\rm t}^{2}=\frac{\int_{0}^{1}\dot{a}^{2}m(a)\mathrm{d}s}{P_{0}+\int_{0}^{1}\langle P\rangle_{\text{s}}(a)\mathrm{d}s}\,,

(14)

where we have changed variable
s=t/\tau
, and
\dot{a}=da/ds
. Therefore,
Q_{\rm p}
and
Q_{\rm t}
are not minimum at the same duration
\tau
in general. Again, let us emphasize that DRT predicts that
Q_{\rm p}
always features a local minimum: we examine below in detail the conditions under which this prediction can actually fail. On the contrary,
Q_{\rm t}
always features a minimum and DRT will give good predictions for it close to the quasistatic limit.

In short, the DRT predictions [Eqs. (
12
-
14
)] hold for a generic active field theory within our thermodynamically-consistent formulation [Eqs. (
3
-
6
)], yet they rely on assuming that the protocol is close to quasistatic (
\Omega\tau\gg 1
). To deploy DRT on a specific dynamics, the main challenge is to explicitly evaluate the Lagrangian in terms of
a(t)
. In what follows, we demonstrate how DRT can inform the optimization of protocols for a new model of active phase separation.

Field theory of active phase separation

We now introduce a case study, referred to as Chemical Model B (CMB), which corresponds to a specific active field theory for nonequilibrium phase separation. To this end, we consider the canonical
\phi^{4}
free-energy functional
[
36
]

F([\phi],a)=\int_{V}\bigg[\frac{a(t)}{2}\phi^{2}+\frac{b}{4}\phi^{4}+\frac{\kappa}{2}(\boldsymbol{\nabla}\phi)^{2}\bigg]\mathrm{d}x\,,

(15)

where
b,\kappa>0
. We focus on the case of one spatial dimension (
d=1
), for which the spurious drift
\bbnu
[Eq. (
4
)] vanishes
[
3
]
. Here, the parameter
a(t)
is related to the second virial coefficient, as obtained from coarse-graining procedures

]

[
64
]
).

To leading order in powers of
\phi

C
[Eq. (
6

C=\gamma\partial_{x}\phi\,,

(16)

where
\gamma
is constant. The expression in Eq. (
16

vanishes for a constant

4

\displaystyle\dot{\phi}

\displaystyle+\partial_{x}J=0\,,

(17)

\displaystyle J

\displaystyle=-\lambda_{\phi}\partial_{x}\bigg[\bigg(a-\frac{\gamma\Delta\mu}{\lambda_{\phi}}+b\phi^{2}-\kappa\partial_{x}^{2}\bigg)\phi\bigg]+T^{1/2}\Lambda_{\phi}\,,

where the noise term
\Lambda_{\phi}
[Eqs. (

6

\langle\Lambda_{\phi}(x,t)\Lambda_{\phi}(x^{\prime},t^{\prime})\rangle=2\lambda_{\phi}\delta(x-x^{\prime})\delta(t-t^{\prime})\,.

Therefore, at the level of the
\phi

16

in the free energy

) regions [Fig.
2
(a)]. Unlike other models of active phase separation
[
39
,
65
,
51
]
, the activity in CMB does not necessarily stem from microscopic self-propulsion. More generally, activity here results from a coupling of the system with a fuel reservoir; for instance, ATP molecules powering nonequilibrium phase separation in living cells
[
66
,
67
]
.

For a constant
a(t)
, the active work
W_{\rm act}
quantifies the dissipation required to sustain steady-state activity, and follows from Eqs. (
10
-
11
) as

W_{\rm act}=(\tau+\tau_{\rm r})(P_{0}+\langle P\rangle_{\text{s}})\,,

(19)

where now

P=\int_{V}p\,\mathrm{d}x\,,\quad p=\frac{\gamma\Delta\mu}{\lambda_{\phi}}(\partial_{x}\phi)\left(J-\gamma\Delta\mu\,\partial_{x}\phi\right)\,,

(20)

and
J
is given in Eq. (
17
). At the mean-field level (
T=0
), the phase-separated profile
\phi(x)
is known analytically
[
36
]
, from which we deduce
p(x)
(see Ref.
[
61
]
, section IV.A): it vanishes in the bulk phases, and is negative at the interface [Fig.
2
(b) inset]. Therefore, the dissipation is maximal in the homogeneous state, where all the fuel energy is wasted in the thermostat, and is reduced for phase separation, where part of the fuel energy serves to shape the density profile [Fig.
2
(b)]. In the homogeneous state, our analytical results (see Ref.
[
61
]
, section IV.B) show that the finite-temperature value of
\langle P\rangle_{\text{s}}
is negative, and its absolute value reduces closer to the phase boundary [Fig.
2
(a)], while maintaining
W_{\text{act}}\geq 0
.

The thermodynamically consistent structure of our active field theory [Eqs. (
3
-
6
)] is built to properly account for all sources of dissipation coming from the diffusive and chemical sectors of the dynamics. For CMB [Eqs. (
15
-
18
)], the dissipation is locally reduced at interfaces, in line with similar results for another model of active phase separation
[
3
]
. As discussed below, the behavior of the steady-state dissipation across the phase diagram determines the shape of the optimal protocol.

Scalings of optimal protocol duration

For CMB dynamics [Eqs. (

[Fig.

\langle F(a_{\tau})-F(a_{0})\rangle

a_{\rm lin}(t)=a_{0}+(t/\tau)(a_{\tau}-a_{0})\,.

(21)

Using numerical simulations (see Ref.
[

[Eqs. (

Crossover between optimal protocols

We continue by examining the DRT predictions for the optimal protocol
a_{\text{op}}(t)
minimizing
(Q_{\rm p},Q_{\rm t})
[Eq. (
12
)] at a given duration
\tau
. This amounts to optimizing the Lagrangian
L
[Eq. (
13
)], since the rest of the terms in
Q_{\rm t}
[Eq. (
12
)] are independent of the protocol shape
a(t)
. The optimization of
L
is obtained explicitly by solving the corresponding Euler-Lagrange equation (see Ref.
[
61
]
, section IV.E). Since
Q_{\rm p}
and
Q_{\rm t}
[Eq. (
12
)] have the same functional dependence on
a(t)
, we attribute them the same optimal protocol
a_{\rm op}(t)
. Again, we focus here on the CMB dynamics in the weak-noise regime [Eqs. (
15
-
18
)] with
a(t)
evolving within the homogeneous state, see Fig.
5
(a).

In general, the shape of the optimal protocol changes with
\tau
, that is
a_{\rm op}=a_{\rm op}(t/\tau,\tau)
. Interestingly, we observe a crossover between two types of optimal protocols: at small
\tau
,
a_{\text{op}}
tends to the master curve
\alpha_{0}(t/\tau)
, which monotonically increases from
a_{0}
to
a_{\tau}
; at large
\tau
,
a_{\text{op}}
tends to the master curve
\alpha_{\infty}(t/\tau)
, which has a non-monotonic behavior [Fig.
5
(a)]. We rationalize this crossover by analyzing how
L
scales with
\tau
. Given that the optimal protocol takes the form
a_{\rm op}(t)=\alpha(t/\tau)
at large or small
\tau
, the corresponding contribution to
(Q_{\rm p},Q_{\rm t})
can be written as

\int_{0}^{\tau}L(a_{\rm op},\dot{a}_{\rm op})\mathrm{d}t=\int_{0}^{1}\bigg[\frac{m(\alpha)\dot{\alpha}^{2}}{\tau}+\tau\langle P\rangle_{\text{s}}(\alpha)\bigg]\mathrm{d}s\,.

(22)

The first term in Eq. (
22
) dominates at small
\tau
: it has the same functional form as for a passive system
[
54
]
, and accordingly yields the monotonic protocol
\alpha_{0}(t/\tau)
. The second term in Eq. (
22
) dominates at large
\tau
: it is optimal when the protocol minimizes the steady-state power
\langle P\rangle_{\text{s}}
[Eq. (
20
)]. For CMB,
\langle P\rangle_{\text{s}}
is minimal at the phase boundary [Fig.
2
(a)]. Therefore, the corresponding optimal protocol consists of setting
a(t)
close to this boundary for as long as possible, leading to the master curve
\alpha_{\infty}(t/\tau)
[Fig.
5
(a)].

The predictions for the optimal heat
Q_{\rm t}
, which minimize heat for all protocol shapes and durations, follow by substituting
a_{\rm op}
into Eq. (
12
). The agreement with numerical measurements breaks down at small
\Omega\tau
, as expected, yet the discrepancy remains negligible close to the (global) optimal duration
\tau_{\rm t}
[Fig.
5
(b)]. By matching the asymptotic behaviors of
Q_{\rm t}
at large and small
\tau
, we approximate
\tau_{\rm t}
[Eq. (
14
)] as

\tau_{\rm t}^{2}\approx\frac{\int_{0}^{1}\dot{\alpha}_{0}^{2}m(\alpha_{0})\mathrm{d}s}{P_{0}+\int_{0}^{1}\langle P\rangle_{\text{s}}(\alpha_{\infty})\mathrm{d}s}\,.

(23)

In practice, Eq. (
23
) reproduces the value estimated numerically (see Ref.
[
61
]
, section IV.F for more details) for our specific parameters [Fig.
5
(b)]: DRT predictions quantitatively capture the global minimum of
Q_{\rm t}
whenever their regime of validity contains
\tau_{\rm t}
.

Therefore, we conclude that the crossover between optimal protocols is governed by the competition between the dissipation stemming from either external driving or internal activity, respectively dominant at small and large
\tau
. This crossover is expected to appear generically when controlling homogeneous states in a broad class of active systems, beyond the specific case of CMB (see Ref.
[
61
]
, section IV.E).

Discussion

In this work, we have examined how to quantify and optimize the heat dissipated by complex active systems under external perturbation. Specifically, for thermodynamically consistent field theories of active matter, we have distinguished two definitions of heat. The protocol heat
Q_{\rm p}
is dissipated only during the protocol, and may feature a
local
minimum as a function of the protocol duration
\tau
only under specific conditions. In contrast, the total heat
Q_{\rm t}
is dissipated during both the protocol and its post-relaxation, and systematically features a global minimum. Therefore, our study goes beyond previous works which mostly focused on
Q_{\rm p}

[
15
,
1
]
: we have here highlighted the essential role of post-relaxation in quantifying the energetics of active systems.

We have put forward a systematic framework, inspired by previous works
[
54
,
1
]
, to optimize the external perturbation using dynamical response theory. Other frameworks unrealistically assume that the operator has full control over the system (namely, by completely changing the rules of the dynamics)
[
69
,
70
]
; this assumption is particularly useful to derive bounds on dissipation
[
71
,
72
,
73
]
. In contrast, our predictions apply here to the manipulation of some experimentally accessible parameters (namely, by changing the shape of a given free-energy). Remarkably, our framework allows one to evaluate: (i) the optimal duration of operation for a given protocol, (ii) the optimal protocol for a fixed duration, and (iii) the overall duration that gives minimal heat and the corresponding optimal protocol.

For the dynamics of a conserved scalar field that exhibits a nonequilibrium phase-separation, our scaling analysis predicts that
Q_{\rm p}
does not feature any minimum at large activity
\Delta\mu
and volume
V
. A similar analysis for non-conserved scalar fields yields a different scaling for
V
(see Ref.
[
61
]
, section V), so that
Q_{\rm p}
may show a local minimum even at large
V
. These results pave the way towards a systematic scaling analysis to inform protocol optimization in a broader class of active field theories. For instance, our approach could be deployed in polar active theories of the flocking transition
[
40
]
, for which a thermodynamically-consistent description has been recently proposed
[
49
]
.

When driving homogeneous active systems, we have revealed a crossover between two families of optimal protocols. In the specific case of a conserved scalar field, we have shown that this crossover embodies the competition between the internal and external contributions to dissipation, stemming respectively from activity and perturbation. Remarkably, since this competition is inherent to a broad class of active systems
[
1
]
, optimizing protocols between homogeneous states should generically yields a similar crossover. To go beyond our results, it remains to address protocols with multiple control parameters, for instance to build thermodynamic cycles
[
74
,
75
,
76
]
. Moreover, it would be interesting to examine how our framework can be extended to protocols crossing phase transitions.

Methods

Stochastic thermodynamics

Stochastic thermodynamics provides a way to calculate the average heat dissipated into the environment during the time
\tau+\tau_{\rm r}
as
[
13
,
4
]
:

Q_{\rm t}=T\left\langle\ln\frac{\mathcal{P}_{+}(\tau+\tau_{\rm r})}{\mathcal{P}_{-}(\tau+\tau_{\rm r})}\right\rangle\,,

(24)

where
\mathcal{P}_{+}
and
\mathcal{P}_{-}
are the probabilities of observing a given trajectory with the forward and the time-reversed dynamics, respectively. These probabilities take the form
\mathcal{P}_{\pm}(t)\sim e^{-A_{\pm}(t)}
in terms of the dynamical action:

A_{\pm}(t)=\frac{1}{4T}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{V}\left(\mathbb{L}\cdot\mathbb{F}\mp\mathbb{J}\right)\cdot\mathbb{L}^{-1}\cdot\left(\mathbb{L}\cdot\mathbb{F}\mp\mathbb{J}\right)\mathrm{d}{\bf r}\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}\,,

(25)

where
\mathbb{L}^{-1}
is the inverse of the Onsager matrix
\mathbb{L}
. The expression in Eq. (
25
) is given in the Stratonovitch convention
[
77
,
78
]
, where we have omitted some terms which are invariant under time-reversal. This connection between path probabilities and the average heat dissipation is quite general and extends even to systems in which the Einstein relation does not hold
[
50
]
.

Substituting Eq. (
25
) into Eq. (
24
) yields the total heat
Q_{\rm t}
in terms of the flux
\mathbb{J}
and force
\mathbb{F}
[Eq. (
7
)], which can also be written as

Q_{\rm t}=\left\langle\int_{0}^{\tau+\tau_{\rm r}}\int_{V}\bigg(\Delta\mu\,\dot{n}-\frac{\delta F}{\delta\phi}\dot{\phi}\bigg)\mathrm{d}{\bf r}\mathrm{d}t\right\rangle\,,

(26)

where we have used the definitions
\mathbb{J}=(\boldsymbol{J},\dot{n})
and
\mathbb{F}=(-\boldsymbol{\nabla}(\delta F/\delta\phi),\Delta\mu)
. A related expression has been derived for the heat in the absence of perturbation (
\dot{a}=0
) in terms of steady-state averages
[
3
]
. For a given protocol
a(t)
, the standard chain rule, valid within the Stratonovitch convention
[
59
]
, yields

\int_{V}\frac{\delta F}{\delta\phi}\dot{\phi}\,\mathrm{d}{\bf r}=\dot{F}-\dot{a}\partial_{a}F\,,

(27)

from which we deduce

Q_{\rm t}=W_{\text{ext}}+W_{\text{act}}+E(t=0)-E(t=\tau+\tau_{\rm r})\,,

(28)

where we have used
E=\langle F\rangle
, along with the definitions of the external work
W_{\text{ext}}
[Eq. (
8
)] and the active work
W_{\text{act}}
[Eq. (
9
)]. Substituting the dynamics [Eqs. (
4
-
6
)] into the definition of
W_{\text{act}}
, we obtain

W_{\rm act}=(\tau+\tau_{\rm r})P_{0}+\left\langle\int_{0}^{\tau+\tau_{\rm r}}(P+P_{\text{spu}})\mathrm{d}t\right\rangle\,,

(29)

in terms of

\displaystyle P_{\text{spu}}

\displaystyle=T^{1/2}\frac{\Delta\mu}{\lambda_{\phi}}\int_{V}(\lambda_{\phi}\Lambda_{n}-\boldsymbol{C}\cdot\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{\phi})\mathrm{d}{\bf r}

(30)

\displaystyle+T\frac{\Delta\mu}{\lambda_{\phi}}\int_{V}(\lambda_{\phi}\nu_{n}-\boldsymbol{C}\cdot\boldsymbol{\nu}_{\phi})\mathrm{d}{\bf r}\,.

The stochastic integrals in Eq. (
29
) are interpreted within the Stratonovitch convention. Consequently, we demonstrate in Ref.
[
61
]
, section VI that
\langle\int P_{\text{spu}}\mathrm{d}t\rangle=0
, so Eq. (
10
) indeed corresponds to
W_{\rm act}
.
In fact, the spurious drift
\bbnu
ensures thermodynamic consistency of the dynamics [Eq. (
4
)], but the active work [Eq. (
10
)] does not explicitly depend on
\bbnu
, as expected for noise-averaged quantities.

Dynamical response theory

Evaluating the expressions for heat [Eq. (
7
)] and work [Eqs. (
8
-
10
)] requires computing the non-steady-state average
\left\langle O\right\rangle(t)
of some observables
O
at an arbitrary time
t
. In general,
\left\langle O\right\rangle(t)
depends on the entire history of the protocol
a(t^{\prime})
for
t^{\prime}\in[0,t]
. Inspired by previous studies
[
54
,
1
]
, in the regime of slow protocols (
\Omega\tau\ll 1
), we demonstrate how to express
\left\langle O\right\rangle(t)
in terms of some specific response functions, or equivalently in terms of unperturbed correlation functions. Importantly, our approach assumes that the change in
a
is slow compared with the system relaxation time
\Omega^{-1}
, yet the overall change
a(\tau)-a(0)
can be large.

Let us split the protocol
a(t)=\sum_{n}\Delta a(t_{n},t_{n-1})
as a series of discrete jumps
\Delta a(t_{n},t_{n-1})=a(t_{n})-a(t_{n-1})
with durations
\delta t\ll\tau
, so that
t_{n}=n\delta t

[
79
]
. We approximate
\left\langle O\right\rangle(t_{n})
close to
\left\langle O\right\rangle(t_{n-1})
as
[
80
]

\displaystyle\left\langle O\right\rangle(t_{n})

\displaystyle\approx\left\langle O\right\rangle(t_{n-1})

(31)

\displaystyle+\int^{t_{n}}_{t_{n-1}}\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}\,\Delta a(t^{\prime},t_{n})\bigg[R_{O}^{(1)}(t_{n}-t^{\prime})

\displaystyle+\int^{t_{n}}_{t_{n-1}}\mathrm{d}t^{\prime\prime}\,\Delta a(t^{\prime},t_{n})R_{O}^{(2)}(t_{n}-t^{\prime},t_{n}-t^{\prime\prime})\bigg]\,,

where
R_{O}^{(1)}
and
R_{O}^{(2)}
are the first- and second-order response functions, given respectively by

\displaystyle R^{(1)}_{O}(a(t);t-t_{1})

\displaystyle=\left.\frac{\delta\left\langle O(t)\right\rangle}{\delta a(t_{1})}\right|_{a(t_{1})\to a(t)},

(32a)

\displaystyle R^{(2)}_{O}(a(t);t-t_{1},t-t_{2})

\displaystyle=\left.\frac{\delta^{2}\left\langle O(t)\right\rangle}{\delta a(t_{2})\delta a(t_{1})}\right|_{\begin{subarray}{c}a(t_{1})\to a(t)\\
a(t_{2})\to a(t)\end{subarray}}.

(32b)

Provided that the system relaxes faster than the rate of change of
a
within each timestep (
\Omega\delta t\gg 1
), we approximate
\langle O\rangle(t_{n-1})\approx\langle O\rangle_{\rm ss}(a(t_{n-1}))
as a steady-state average, and we extend the lower limits of integration in Eq. (
31
) as
t_{n-1}\to-\infty
. Additionally, assuming that the protocol
a(t)
is smooth, we expand

\Delta a(t^{\prime},t_{n})\approx(t^{\prime}-t_{n})\dot{a}(t_{n})+(t^{\prime}-t_{n})^{2}\ddot{a}(t_{n})/2\,.

(33)

Substituting this expansion in the response framework [Eq. (
31
)], we deduce

\displaystyle\left\langle O\right\rangle(t)

\displaystyle\approx\left\langle O\right\rangle_{\text{s}}\bigl(a(t)\bigr)+\dot{a}(t)\zeta_{O}^{(1,1)}\bigl(a(t)\bigr)

(34)

\displaystyle+\ddot{a}(t)\zeta_{O}^{(2,1)}\bigl(a(t)\bigr)+\dot{a}^{2}(t)\zeta_{O}^{(2,2)}\bigl(a(t)\bigr)\,,

where we have taken
t\equiv t_{n}\approx t_{n-1}
, and introduced

\displaystyle\zeta_{O}^{(1,1)}

\displaystyle=-\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{\prime}R_{O}^{(1)}(t^{\prime})\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}\,,\quad\zeta_{O}^{(2,1)}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{t^{\prime 2}}{2}R_{O}^{(1)}(t^{\prime})\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}\,,

(35)

\displaystyle\zeta_{O}^{(2,2)}

\displaystyle=\iint_{0}^{\infty}\frac{t^{\prime}t^{\prime\prime}}{2}R_{O}^{(2)}(t^{\prime},t^{\prime\prime})\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}\mathrm{d}t^{\prime\prime}\,.

Note that the observable-specific quantities in the rhs of Eq. (
34
), namely
\left\langle O\right\rangle_{\text{s}}
and
\zeta_{O}^{(m,n)}
, depend only on
a(t)
and not on its derivatives
(\dot{a},\ddot{a})
. Finally, substituting Eq. (
34
) into the expression of heat [Eq. (
7
)] yields the decomposition given in Eq. (
12
); see Ref.
[
61
]
, section I for details.

I
Protocol and total heat using dynamic response theory

In this section, we use Dynamic Response Theory (DRT) to derive Eq. (12) of the main text, which contain the protocol and total heat for slow processes.
Our starting point is Eq. (1) of the main text, the first law, where we can use
E=\langle F\rangle
and the expressions for the work (Eq. (2)), subdivided into external work
W_{\text{ext}}
(Eq. (8)) and active work
W_{\text{act}}
(Eq. (10)), to find expressions for the protocol heat
Q_{\text{p}}
and the total heat
Q_{\text{t}}
.
This leads to

\displaystyle Q_{\text{p}}

\displaystyle=\tau P_{0}+\int_{0}^{\tau}\langle P\rangle(t)\,\mathrm{d}t+\int_{0}^{\tau}\dot{a}(t)\langle\partial_{a}F\rangle(t)\,\mathrm{d}t-\big(\langle F\rangle(\tau)-\langle F\rangle_{\text{s}}(a_{0})\big)

(I.1a)

and

\displaystyle Q_{\text{t}}

\displaystyle=(\tau+\tau_{\text{r}})P_{0}+\int_{0}^{\tau}\langle P\rangle(t)\,\mathrm{d}t+\int_{\tau}^{\tau+\tau_{\text{r}}}\langle P\rangle(t)\,\mathrm{d}t+\int_{0}^{\tau}\dot{a}(t)\langle\partial_{a}F\rangle(t)\,\mathrm{d}t-\big(\langle F\rangle_{\text{s}}(a_{\tau})-\langle F\rangle_{\text{s}}(a_{0})\big),

(I.1b)

where
P_{0}=\lambda_{n}V\Delta\mu^{2}
,
\langle\cdot\rangle_{\text{s}}
denotes steady-state averages, while all the other averages are non-stationary.
In Eq. (
I.1b
), the integral of
\langle P\rangle
was split into two because its expressions in the manipulation process (
0\leq t\leq\tau
) and in the relaxation process (
\tau\leq t\leq\tau+\tau_{\text{r}}
) are fundamentally different.

Let us first calculate the averages during the manipulation process.
This is where DRT applies, and we can use Eq. (34) of the main text for the three relevant observables, that is,

\displaystyle\langle F\rangle(t)

\displaystyle=\langle F\rangle_{\text{s}}(a(t))+\dot{a}(t)\zeta_{F}^{(1,1)}(a(t))+\mathcal{O}(\ddot{a},\dot{a}^{2}),

(I.2a)

\displaystyle\langle\partial_{a}F\rangle(t)

\displaystyle=\langle\partial_{a}F\rangle_{\text{s}}(a(t))+\dot{a}(t)\zeta_{\partial_{a}F}^{(1,1)}(a(t))+\mathcal{O}(\ddot{a},\dot{a}^{2}),

(I.2b)

and

\displaystyle\langle P\rangle(t)

\displaystyle=\langle P\rangle_{\text{s}}(a(t))+\dot{a}(t)\zeta_{P}^{(1,1)}(a(t))+\ddot{a}(t)\zeta_{P}^{(2,1)}(a(t))+\dot{a}^{2}(t)\zeta_{P}^{(2,2)}(a(t))+\mathcal{O}(\dddot{a},\ddot{a}\dot{a},\dot{a}^{3}).

(I.2c)

Here, we kept the terms necessary to make Eqs. (
I.1
) correct up to
\mathcal{O}(\tau^{-2})
, assuming
\dot{a}\sim\tau^{-1}
and
\ddot{a}\sim\tau^{-2}
.
The expressions for the integrated response functions
\zeta_{O}^{(m,n)}
are given in Eq. (35) of the main text.

To make Eqs. (
I.2
) valid for
t=0
, we need to enforce
\zeta_{O}^{(m,n)}(a_{0})\equiv 0
for any
m
and
n
(because we assumed
\langle O\rangle(t_{0})=\langle O\rangle_{\text{s}}(a_{0})
from the beginning).
Once this is done, placing Eqs. (
I.2
) into Eqs. (
I.1
) and rearranging leads us to

\displaystyle Q_{\text{p}}

\displaystyle=\tau P_{0}+B_{\text{t}}+\dot{a}(\tau)B_{\text{p}}+\int_{0}^{\tau}L\big(a(t),\dot{a}(t)\big)\,\mathrm{d}t

(I.3a)

and

\displaystyle Q_{\text{t}}

\displaystyle=(\tau+\tau_{\text{r}})P_{0}+B_{\text{t}}+\dot{a}(\tau)\zeta_{P}^{(2,1)}(a_{\tau})+\int_{\tau}^{\tau+\tau_{\text{r}}}\langle P\rangle(t)\,\mathrm{d}t+\int_{0}^{\tau}L\big(a(t),\dot{a}(t)\big)\,\mathrm{d}t,

(I.3b)

where we defined

\displaystyle B_{\text{t}}

\displaystyle\equiv\int_{a_{0}}^{a_{\tau}}\left(\langle\partial_{a}F\rangle_{\text{s}}(a)+\zeta_{P}^{(1,1)}(a)\right)\,da-\Big(\langle F\rangle_{\text{s}}(a_{\tau})-\langle F\rangle_{\text{s}}(a_{0})\Big),

(I.4a)

\displaystyle B_{\text{p}}

\displaystyle\equiv\zeta_{P}^{(2,1)}(a_{\tau})-\zeta_{F}^{(1,1)}(a_{\tau}),

(I.4b)

\displaystyle L(a,\dot{a})

\displaystyle\equiv m(a)\dot{a}^{2}+\langle P\rangle_{\text{s}}(a),

(I.4c)

and

\displaystyle m(a)

\displaystyle\equiv\zeta_{\partial_{a}F}^{(1,1)}(a)+\zeta_{P}^{(2,2)}(a)-\partial_{a}\zeta_{P}^{(2,1)}(a).

(I.4d)

Equation (
I.3a
) is already in the form shown in Eqs. (12) of the main text.
However, Eq. (
I.3b
) still needs the calculation of the integral of
\langle P\rangle
in the relaxation process, which we turn to now.
In Sec.
III.4
we prove, using the relaxation dynamics of the system, that

\displaystyle\dot{a}(\tau)\zeta^{(2,1)}_{P}(a_{\tau})+\int_{\tau}^{\tau+\tau_{\text{r}}}\left\langle P(t)\right\rangle\mathrm{d}t=\tau_{\text{r}}\left\langle P\right\rangle_{\text{s}}(a_{\tau}),

(I.5)

Finally, placing Eq. (
I.5
) into Eq. (
I.3b
), we reach

\displaystyle Q_{\text{t}}

\displaystyle=(\tau+\tau_{\text{r}})P_{0}+\tau_{\text{r}}\left\langle P\right\rangle_{\text{s}}(a_{\tau})+B_{\text{t}}+\int_{0}^{\tau}L\big(a(t),\dot{a}(t)\big)\,\mathrm{d}t,

(I.6)

which is the expression for the total heat we see in Eq. (12) of the main text.

II
Response functions

The goal in this section is to obtain practical expressions for the response functions, given our active field dynamics.
From Eqs. (32) of the main text, the formal definitions of the first and second-order response functions of a generic observable
O
are

\displaystyle R^{(1)}_{O}(a(t);t-t_{1})

\displaystyle\equiv\left.\frac{\delta\left\langle O(t)\right\rangle}{\delta a(t_{1})}\right|_{a(t_{1})\to a(t)},

(II.1a)

and

\displaystyle R^{(2)}_{O}(a(t);t-t_{1},t-t_{2})

\displaystyle\equiv\left.\frac{\delta^{2}\left\langle O(t)\right\rangle}{\delta a(t_{2})\delta a(t_{1})}\right|_{\begin{subarray}{c}a(t_{1})\to a(t)\\
a(t_{2})\to a(t)\end{subarray}},

(II.1b)

where
t_{1}
and
t_{2}
are dummy variables, with no relation to the discretized times
t_{n}
.
To avoid any confusions, we only consider situations in which the time arguments are in decreasing order, i.e.,
R^{(1)}_{O}(a;t-t_{1})
for
t\geq t_{1}
and
R^{(2)}_{O}(a;t-t_{1},t-t_{2})
for
t\geq t_{1}\geq t_{2}
.

These definitions can be used to arrive at more practical expressions for the response functions
[
1
]
.
We start from the path integral definition of the average over noise realizations,

\displaystyle\left\langle O(t)\right\rangle

\displaystyle\equiv\int O(t)N^{-1}(t)e^{-A_{+}[\mathbb{J}](t)}\mathcal{D}\Lambda,

\displaystyle N(t)

\displaystyle\equiv\int e^{-A_{+}[\mathbb{J}](t)}\mathcal{D}\Lambda,

where
A_{+}
is the forward dynamical action given in Eq. (25) of the main text.
The dependence on
a
lies only on
N
and
e^{-A_{+}}
, so that Eq. (
II.1a
) gives

\displaystyle R^{(1)}_{O}(a(t);t-t_{1})

\displaystyle=\int O(t)\left[\frac{\delta N^{-1}(t)}{\delta a(t_{1})}e^{-A_{+}[\mathbb{J}](t)}+N^{-1}(t)\frac{\delta e^{-A_{+}[\mathbb{J}](t)}}{\delta a(t_{1})}\right]_{a(t_{1})\to a(t)}\mathcal{D}\Lambda

\displaystyle=\int O(t)\left[-N^{-2}(t)\frac{\delta N(t)}{\delta a(t_{1})}e^{-A_{+}[\mathbb{J}](t)}-N^{-1}(t)e^{-A_{+}[\mathbb{J}](t)}\frac{\delta A_{+}[\mathbb{J}](t)}{\delta a(t_{1})}\right]_{a(t_{1})\to a(t)}\mathcal{D}\Lambda

\displaystyle=\int\left[O(t)N^{-1}(t)\left\langle\frac{\delta A_{+}[\mathbb{J}](t)}{\delta a(t_{1})}\right\rangle e^{-A_{+}[\mathbb{J}](t)}-O(t)\frac{\delta A_{+}[\mathbb{J}](t)}{\delta a(t_{1})}N^{-1}(t)e^{-A_{+}[\mathbb{J}](t)}\right]_{a(t_{1})\to a(t)}\mathcal{D}\Lambda

\displaystyle=\left\langle\left\langle O(t)G(t_{1})\right\rangle\right\rangle_{a(t_{1})\to a(t)},

(II.2)

where we used

\frac{\delta N(t)}{\delta a(t_{1})}=-N(t)\left\langle\frac{\delta A_{+}[\mathbb{J}](t)}{\delta a(t_{1})}\right\rangle,

from the second to the third line, and we defined the generalized force (omitting functional dependencies)

G(t_{1})\equiv-\frac{\delta A_{+}}{\delta a(t_{1})}

(II.3)

and the two-entry correlation

\left\langle\left\langle O_{1}O_{2}\right\rangle\right\rangle\equiv\left\langle O_{1}O_{2}\right\rangle-\left\langle O_{1}\right\rangle\left\langle O_{2}\right\rangle.

(II.4)

Similarly, for the second-order response function, Eq. (
II.1b
) gives

R^{(2)}_{O}(a(t);t-t_{1},t-t_{2})=\left\langle\left\langle O(t)\frac{\delta G(t_{1})}{\delta a(t_{2})}\right\rangle\right\rangle_{\begin{subarray}{c}a(t_{1})\to a(t)\\
a(t_{2})\to a(t)\end{subarray}}+\left\langle\left\langle O(t)G(t_{1})G(t_{2})\right\rangle\right\rangle_{\begin{subarray}{c}a(t_{1})\to a(t)\\
a(t_{2})\to a(t)\end{subarray}},

(II.5)

with the three-entry correlation defined as

\left\langle\left\langle O_{1}O_{2}O_{3}\right\rangle\right\rangle\equiv\left\langle O_{1}O_{2}O_{3}\right\rangle+2\left\langle O_{1}\right\rangle\left\langle O_{2}\right\rangle\left\langle O_{3}\right\rangle-\left\langle O_{1}\right\rangle\left\langle O_{2}O_{3}\right\rangle-\left\langle O_{2}\right\rangle\left\langle O_{3}O_{1}\right\rangle-\left\langle O_{3}\right\rangle\left\langle O_{1}O_{2}\right\rangle.

(II.6)

Looking at Eq. (31) of the main text, we see that making
a(t_{1})\to a(t)
is the same as taking a steady-state average.
This allows us to write the response function of Eq (
II.2
) as a steady-state correlation function, i.e.,

R^{(1)}_{O}(a;t-t_{1})=\left\langle\left\langle O(t)G(t_{1})\right\rangle\right\rangle_{\text{s}}(a),

(II.7)

and the same is valid for higher orders.
Furthermore, Eq. (25) of the main text shows that the action is a functional of
a(t_{1})
(through
\mathbb{F}
), but the integrand does not depend on time derivatives of
a(t_{1})
, which simplifies the functional derivative as simply the partial derivative of the integrand.
Combining Eqs. (25) of the main text and (
II.3
), we get

G(t)=-\frac{1}{4T}\frac{\partial}{\partial a}\int_{V}\left\{\left[\mathbb{J}^{\intercal}-\mathbb{F}^{\intercal}\cdot\mathbb{L}^{\intercal}\right]\cdot\left[\mathbb{L}^{-1}\cdot\mathbb{J}-\mathbb{F}\right]+\mathcal{O}(T)\right\}\,\mathrm{d}{\bf r},

(II.8)

where
\mathcal{O}(T)
stands for contributions that vanish at
T=0
. Here, we refrain from writing these terms explicitly, because they are irrelevant in the following small noise treatment (see Sec.
III
).

Equation (
II.8
) shows a product of two binomials and thus four terms, only three of which depend explicitly on
a
(through
\mathbb{F}
).
In two of these three terms, the Onsager matrix completely disappears (because
\mathbb{L}^{\intercal}=\mathbb{L}
), and thus the dynamics of matter and fuel are uncoupled.
Since the fuel dynamics only depends on
a
through the coupling to active system, its contribution vanishes in these two terms.
The only remaining term is
\mathbb{F}^{\intercal}\cdot\mathbb{L}\cdot\mathbb{F}
, whose derivative is

\frac{\partial}{\partial a}\left[\mathbb{F}^{\intercal}\cdot\mathbb{L}\cdot\mathbb{F}\right]=\frac{\partial\mathbb{F}^{\intercal}}{\partial a}\cdot\mathbb{L}\cdot\mathbb{F}+\mathbb{F}^{\intercal}\cdot\mathbb{L}\cdot\frac{\partial\mathbb{F}}{\partial a}.

(II.9)

Take the first term in the RHS of Eq. (
II.9
).
In explicit matrix notation, it reads

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathbb{F}^{\intercal}}{\partial a}\cdot\mathbb{L}\cdot\mathbb{F}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{\partial}{\partial a}\boldsymbol{\nabla}\frac{\delta F}{\delta\phi},&0\end{pmatrix}\cdot\begin{pmatrix}\lambda_{\phi}\boldsymbol{\nabla}\frac{\delta F}{\delta\phi}-\boldsymbol{C}\Delta\mu\\
\boldsymbol{C}\cdot\boldsymbol{\nabla}\frac{\delta F}{\delta\phi}-\lambda_{n}\Delta\mu\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\left[\lambda_{\phi}\boldsymbol{\nabla}\frac{\delta F}{\delta\phi}-\Delta\mu\,\boldsymbol{C}\right]\cdot\frac{\partial}{\partial a}\boldsymbol{\nabla}\frac{\delta F}{\delta\phi},

and once again the contribution from the fuel dynamics vanishes.
Obviously, the same happens in the second term in the RHS of Eq. (
II.9
).
The complete generalized force is

G(t)=-\frac{1}{2T}\int_{V}\left\{\left[\boldsymbol{J}+\lambda_{\phi}\boldsymbol{\nabla}\frac{\delta F}{\delta\phi}-\Delta\mu\,\boldsymbol{C}\right]\cdot\boldsymbol{\nabla}\frac{\partial}{\partial a}\frac{\delta F}{\delta\phi}+\mathcal{O}(T)\right\}\,\mathrm{d}{\bf r}\,\mathrm{d}t^{\prime},

the same result we would have obtained if we used the dynamic action of only the system of interest from the beginning.
We interpret this result as a consequence of the fact that the active system dynamics can be solved without solving the fuel dynamics.
The term containing
\boldsymbol{J}
can be integrated by parts using the divergence theorem, and we arrive at

G(t)=-\frac{1}{2T}\int_{V}\Bigg\{\left[\lambda_{\phi}\boldsymbol{\nabla}\frac{\delta F}{\delta\phi}-\Delta\mu\,\boldsymbol{C}\right]\cdot\boldsymbol{\nabla}\partial_{a}\frac{\delta F}{\delta\phi}+\dot{\phi}\partial_{a}\frac{\delta F}{\delta\phi}+\mathcal{O}(T)\Bigg\}\,\mathrm{d}{\bf r},

(II.10)

and its functional derivative

\frac{\delta G(t)}{\delta a(t^{\prime})}=-\frac{1}{2T}\delta(t-t^{\prime})\int_{V}\Bigg\{\lambda_{\phi}\left[\boldsymbol{\nabla}\partial_{a}\frac{\delta F}{\delta\phi}\right]^{2}+\left[\lambda_{\phi}\boldsymbol{\nabla}\frac{\delta F}{\delta\phi}-\Delta\mu\,\boldsymbol{C}\right]\cdot\boldsymbol{\nabla}\partial_{a}^{2}\frac{\delta F}{\delta\phi}+\dot{\phi}\partial_{a}^{2}\frac{\delta F}{\delta\phi}+\mathcal{O}(T)\Bigg\}\,\mathrm{d}{\bf r}.

(II.11)

III
Small noise expansion

In this section, we introduce the basics of the small noise expansion, a field approximation for low noise strength that allows for analytic results.
We start from the dynamics of
\phi
(Eqs. (3) and (4) of the main text), which reads

\displaystyle\dot{\phi}+\boldsymbol{\nabla}\cdot

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{J}=0

(III.1a)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{J}=-\lambda_{\phi}\boldsymbol{\nabla}\frac{\delta F}{\delta\phi}+\boldsymbol{C}\Delta\mu+T^{1/2}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{\phi}+T\boldsymbol{\nu}_{\phi},

(III.1b)

with noise correlations given by (Eq. (5) of the main text)

\displaystyle\left\langle\Lambda_{\phi}^{\alpha}({\bf r},t)\Lambda_{\phi}^{\alpha^{\prime}}({\bf r}^{\prime},t^{\prime})\right\rangle=2\lambda_{\phi}\delta^{\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}}\delta({\bf r}-{\bf r}^{\prime})\delta(t-t^{\prime})

(III.2)

and
\alpha
and
\alpha^{\prime}
represent each of the
d
dimensions.

Note that the global density of the system,

\bar{\phi}\equiv\frac{1}{V}\int_{V}\phi({\bf r},t)\,\mathrm{d}{\bf r},

(III.3)

is a conserved quantity in the dynamics, which is easily proven by taking the time derivative of this definition and using Eq. (
III.1a
).
Thus, as long as we stay in the homogeneous state of the system, and as long as the noises are small enough to only have perturbative effects around this homogeneous state, we can carry out a small noise expansion in
\phi
, as in

\phi({\bf r},t)=\phi_{0}({\bf r},t)+T^{1/2}\phi_{1}({\bf r},t)+T\phi_{2}({\bf r},t)+\mathcal{O}(T^{3/2}).

(III.4)

The reason we use
T^{1/2}
as the perturbative parameter here is that it is precisely the pre-factor of the noise
\mathbb{\Lambda}
in Eq. (
III.1b
), thus serving as a proxy to the noise strength.

Since we want Eq. (
III.4
) to represent small effects around the homogeneous state, for consistency we must have

\displaystyle\phi_{0}({\bf r},t)

\displaystyle=\bar{\phi}

(III.5a)

and

\displaystyle\int_{V}\phi_{1}({\bf r},t)\,\mathrm{d}{\bf r}

\displaystyle=0=\int_{V}\phi_{2}({\bf r},t)\,\mathrm{d}{\bf r},

(III.5b)

and the same for higher orders.
Then, the space integral of
\phi^{n}
, where
n
is an integer, becomes

\displaystyle\int_{V}\phi^{n}\,\mathrm{d}{\bf r}

\displaystyle=\int_{V}\left[\phi_{0}^{n}+n\phi_{0}^{n-1}\left(T^{1/2}\phi_{1}+T\phi_{2}\right)+\frac{n(n-1)}{2}T\phi_{0}^{n-2}\phi_{1}^{2}\right]\,\mathrm{d}{\bf r}+\mathcal{O}(T^{3/2})

\displaystyle=V\bar{\phi}^{n}+\frac{n(n-1)}{2}T\bar{\phi}^{n-2}\int_{V}\phi_{1}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}{\bf r}+\mathcal{O}(T^{3/2}),

(III.6)

where we used Eq. (
III.5
) in the second line.
Thus, any integer power of
\phi
is approximated by
\phi_{1}^{2}
to linear order in
T
.
Similarly, the space integral of
(\boldsymbol{\nabla}\phi)^{n}
for
n\geq 2
becomes

\displaystyle\int_{V}(\boldsymbol{\nabla}\phi)^{n}\,\mathrm{d}{\bf r}=T^{n/2}\int_{V}(\boldsymbol{\nabla}\phi_{1})^{n}\,\mathrm{d}{\bf r}+\mathcal{O}(T^{(n+1)/2}).

(III.7)

Thus, only a term proportional to
(\boldsymbol{\nabla}\phi)^{2}
contributes linearly in
T
.

To proceed, define the Fourier transform

\tilde{\phi}_{1}({\bf k},t)\equiv\frac{1}{\sqrt{V}}\int_{V}\phi_{1}({\bf r},t)e^{-i{\bf k}\cdot{\bf r}}\,\mathrm{d}{\bf r}\quad\Rightarrow\quad\phi_{1}({\bf r},t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{V}}\sum_{{\bf k}}\tilde{\phi}_{1}({\bf k},t)e^{i{\bf k}\cdot{\bf r}}.

(III.8)

Since
\phi_{1}
is real, we have
\tilde{\phi}_{1}(-{\bf k},t)=\tilde{\phi}_{1}^{*}({\bf k},t)
.
Consequently,

\displaystyle\int_{V}\phi_{1}^{2}({\bf r},t)\,\mathrm{d}{\bf r}=\sum_{{\bf k}}|\tilde{\phi}_{1}({\bf k},t)|^{2}\quad\text{and}\quad\int_{V}\big(\boldsymbol{\nabla}\phi_{1}({\bf r},t)\big)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}{\bf r}=\sum_{{\bf k}}{\bf k}^{2}|\tilde{\phi}_{1}({\bf k},t)|^{2}.

(III.9)

Now, consider an observable
O
(possibly dependent on the external parameter
a
) written as

\displaystyle O([\phi],a)=\int_{V}o(\phi,a)\,\mathrm{d}{\bf r},\qquad o(\phi,a)=\sum_{n=2}^{N}c_{n}(a)\frac{\phi^{n}}{n}+\sum_{n=2}^{M}d_{n}(a)\frac{(\boldsymbol{\nabla}\phi)^{n}}{n},

(III.10)

where
c_{n}
and
d_{n}
are
\phi
-independent, while
N
and
M
are finite.
Every observable in this paper can be written in this form.
Equations (
III.6
), (
III.7
) and (
III.9
) can be used to show that, up to first non-trivial order in the small noise expansion, we can write

\displaystyle O([\phi],a)=O(\bar{\phi},a)+\frac{T}{2}\sum_{{\bf k}}\tilde{o}_{1}(a;{\bf k})|\tilde{\phi}_{1}({\bf k},t)|^{2}+\mathcal{O}(T^{3/2}),

(III.11)

where

\displaystyle\tilde{o}_{1}(a;{\bf k})=\sum_{n=2}^{N}(n-1)\bar{\phi}^{n-2}c_{n}(a)+d_{2}(a){\bf k}^{2}.

(III.12)

Equation (
III.11
) allows us to calculate the average of any observable through the average
\langle|\tilde{\phi}_{1}|^{2}\rangle
.

III.1
Steady-state averages

Our goal in this section is to calculate the steady-state average
\langle\tilde{\phi}_{1}({\bf k},t)\tilde{\phi}_{1}^{*}({\bf k}^{\prime},t^{\prime})\rangle_{\text{s}}
, which serves to calculate every other steady-state average in the paper.
As such, in this section, we assume
a
to be constant.

Much like the small noise expansion makes the observables quadratic in
\tilde{\phi}_{1}
(see Eq. (
III.11
)), it also linearizes quantities such as
\delta F/\delta\phi
and
\boldsymbol{C}
in
\tilde{\phi}_{1}
.
Therefore, to find the
\tilde{\phi}_{1}
dynamics, we place the small noise expansion for
\phi
(Eq. (
III.4
)) into the dynamics for
\phi
(Eqs. (
III.1
)), collect every term proportional to
T^{1/2}
in both sides of the equation, and then take the Fourier transform defined in Eq. (
III.8
).
Doing so leads to

\dot{\tilde{\phi}}_{1}({\bf k},t)=-\omega_{1}(a;{\bf k})\tilde{\phi}_{1}({\bf k},t)-i{\bf k}\cdot\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}_{\phi}({\bf k},t),

(III.13)

where the noise correlations in Fourier space reads

\displaystyle\left\langle\tilde{\Lambda}_{\phi}^{\alpha}({\bf k},t)[\tilde{\Lambda}_{\phi}^{\alpha^{\prime}}({\bf k}^{\prime},t^{\prime})]^{*}\right\rangle=2\lambda_{\phi}\delta^{\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}}\delta_{{\bf k},{\bf k}^{\prime}}\delta(t-t^{\prime}),

(III.14)

as derived from Eq. (
III.2
).
In Eq. (
III.13
),
\omega_{1}(a;{\bf k})
is the relaxation frequency of mode
{\bf k}
for a given model, only calculable once the free energy
F
and the coupling
\boldsymbol{C}
are specified.

Now, we define a new Fourier transform on the time variable,

\widetilde{\phi}_{1}({\bf k},\omega)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\tilde{\phi}_{1}({\bf k},t)e^{-i\omega t}\mathrm{d}t\quad\Rightarrow\quad\tilde{\phi}_{1}({\bf k},t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\widetilde{\phi}_{1}({\bf k},\omega)e^{i\omega t}d\omega.

(III.15)

It is important to note that such a Fourier transform on the time variable can only be done on
\phi_{1}
(and not on the whole field
\phi
), because
\phi_{1}
is expected to vanish as
t\to\pm\infty
.
Equation (
III.13
) can be shown to be equivalent to

\widetilde{\phi}_{1}({\bf k},\omega)=-\frac{i{\bf k}\cdot\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}_{\phi}({\bf k},\omega)}{\omega_{1}(a;{\bf k})+i\omega},

(III.16)

whereas Eq. (
III.14
) is equivalent to

\displaystyle\left\langle\widetilde{\Lambda}_{\phi}^{\alpha}({\bf k},\omega)[\widetilde{\Lambda}_{\phi}^{\alpha^{\prime}}({\bf k}^{\prime},\omega^{\prime})]^{*}\right\rangle=2\lambda_{\phi}\delta^{\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}}\delta_{{\bf k},{\bf k}^{\prime}}\delta(\omega-\omega^{\prime}).

(III.17)

Using the inverse transform of Eq. (
III.15
), we get

\displaystyle\left\langle\tilde{\phi}_{1}({\bf k},t)\tilde{\phi}_{1}^{*}({\bf k}^{\prime},t^{\prime})\right\rangle_{\text{s}}(a)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left\langle\widetilde{\phi}_{1}({\bf k},\omega)\widetilde{\phi}_{1}^{*}({\bf k}^{\prime},\omega^{\prime})\right\rangle_{\text{s}}(a)e^{i\omega t}e^{-i\omega^{\prime}t^{\prime}}d\omega d\omega^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\left\langle\left[{\bf k}\cdot\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}_{\phi}({\bf k},\omega)\right]\left[{\bf k}^{\prime}\cdot\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}_{\phi}({\bf k}^{\prime},\omega^{\prime})\right]^{*}\right\rangle}{\left[\omega_{1}(a;{\bf k})+i\omega\right]\left[\omega_{1}(a;{\bf k}^{\prime})-i\omega^{\prime}\right]}e^{i\omega t}e^{-i\omega^{\prime}t^{\prime}}d\omega d\omega^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}2\lambda_{\phi}{\bf k}^{2}\delta_{{\bf k},{\bf k}^{\prime}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{e^{i\omega[t-t^{\prime}]}}{\omega_{1}^{2}(a;{\bf k})+\omega^{2}}d\omega

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda_{\phi}{\bf k}^{2}}{\omega_{1}(a;{\bf k})}e^{-\omega_{1}(a;{\bf k})|t-t^{\prime}|}\delta_{{\bf k},{\bf k}^{\prime}}.

(III.18)

We used Eq. (
III.16
) in the second line and Eq. (
III.17
) in the third line.

III.2
Non-steady-state averages

In this section, we assume
a
to be time-dependent and intend to calculate the non-stationary average
\langle\tilde{\phi}_{1}({\bf k},t)\tilde{\phi}_{1}^{*}({\bf k}^{\prime},t^{\prime})\rangle
.
We start from Eq. (
III.13
) with
a=a(t)
, i.e.,

\dot{\tilde{\phi}}_{1}({\bf k},t)=-\omega_{1}(a(t);{\bf k})\tilde{\phi}_{1}({\bf k},t)-i{\bf k}\cdot\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}_{\phi}({\bf k},t),

(III.19)

Now we have time-dependent dynamics, which means that a Fourier transform in the time variable does not help.
Instead, we define an auxiliary function for arbitrary
t_{0}
,

\hat{\phi}_{1}({\bf k},t)\equiv\tilde{\phi}_{1}({\bf k},t)e^{\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\omega_{1}(a(t^{\prime});{\bf k})\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}},

for which the differential equation, derived from Eq. (
III.19
), is

\dot{\hat{\phi}}_{1}=-i{\bf k}\cdot\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}_{\phi}e^{\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\omega_{1}\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}}.

This equation is easily integrated to give, after returning to
\tilde{\phi}_{1}
,

\tilde{\phi}_{1}({\bf k},t)=e^{-\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\omega_{1}\mathrm{d}t_{1}}\tilde{\phi}_{1}({\bf k},t_{0})-i{\bf k}\cdot\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}_{\phi}({\bf k},t_{2})e^{-\int_{t_{2}}^{t}\omega_{1}\mathrm{d}t_{1}}\mathrm{d}t_{2}.

(III.20)

From Eq. (
III.20
), we can write

\left\langle\tilde{\phi}_{1}({\bf k},t)\tilde{\phi}_{1}^{*}({\bf k}^{\prime},t^{\prime})\right\rangle=e^{-\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\omega_{1}\mathrm{d}t_{1}}e^{-\int_{t_{0}}^{t^{\prime}}\omega_{1}^{\prime}\mathrm{d}t_{1}^{\prime}}\left\langle\tilde{\phi}_{1}({\bf k},t_{0})\tilde{\phi}_{1}^{*}({\bf k}^{\prime},t_{0})\right\rangle\\
+i{\bf k}^{\prime}\cdot\int_{t_{0}}^{t^{\prime}}\left\langle\tilde{\phi}_{1}({\bf k},t_{0})\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}_{\phi}^{*}({\bf k}^{\prime},t_{2}^{\prime})\right\rangle e^{-\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\omega_{1}\mathrm{d}t_{1}}e^{-\int_{t_{2}^{\prime}}^{t^{\prime}}\omega_{1}^{\prime}\mathrm{d}t_{1}^{\prime}}\mathrm{d}t_{2}^{\prime}-i{\bf k}\cdot\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\left\langle\tilde{\phi}_{1}^{*}({\bf k}^{\prime},t_{0})\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}_{\phi}({\bf k},t_{2})\right\rangle e^{-\int_{t_{0}}^{t^{\prime}}\omega_{1}^{\prime}\mathrm{d}t_{1}^{\prime}}e^{-\int_{t_{2}}^{t}\omega_{1}\mathrm{d}t_{1}}\mathrm{d}t_{2}\\
+\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\int_{t_{0}}^{t^{\prime}}\left\langle\left[{\bf k}\cdot\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}_{\phi}({\bf k},t_{2})\right]\left[{\bf k}^{\prime}\cdot\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}_{\phi}^{*}({\bf k}^{\prime},t_{2}^{\prime})\right]\right\rangle e^{-\int_{t_{2}}^{t}\omega_{1}\mathrm{d}t_{1}}e^{-\int_{t_{2}^{\prime}}^{t^{\prime}}\omega_{1}^{\prime}\mathrm{d}t_{1}^{\prime}}\mathrm{d}t_{2}^{\prime}\mathrm{d}t_{2},

with
t\geq t^{\prime}
.
The second line of this equation, representing the cross terms coming from the squaring of Eq. (
III.20
), contain correlations between
\phi
at
t_{0}
and noises at later times (because
t_{2}\geq t_{0}
).
Since the noise cannot influence the fields at previous times, these correlations are null.
The third line contain pure noise correlations, which are given in Eq. (
III.14
), leading us to

\displaystyle\left\langle\tilde{\phi}_{1}({\bf k},t)\tilde{\phi}_{1}^{*}({\bf k}^{\prime},t^{\prime})\right\rangle

\displaystyle=e^{-\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\omega_{1}\mathrm{d}t_{1}}e^{-\int_{t_{0}}^{t^{\prime}}\omega_{1}^{\prime}\mathrm{d}t_{1}^{\prime}}\left\langle\tilde{\phi}_{1}({\bf k},t_{0})\tilde{\phi}_{1}^{*}({\bf k}^{\prime},t_{0})\right\rangle+2\lambda_{\phi}{\bf k}^{2}\delta_{{\bf k},{\bf k}^{\prime}}\int_{t_{0}}^{t^{\prime}}e^{-\int_{t_{2}}^{t}\omega_{1}\mathrm{d}t_{1}}e^{-\int_{t_{2}}^{t^{\prime}}\omega_{1}^{\prime}\mathrm{d}t_{1}^{\prime}}\mathrm{d}t_{2}.

(III.21)

Now, to the specific result
\langle|\tilde{\phi}_{1}({\bf k},t)|^{2}\rangle
, from which the non-steady-state average of every observable can be calculated in small noise (see Eq. (
III.11
)).
This can easily be obtained from Eq. (
III.21
) by setting
{\bf k}^{\prime}={\bf k}
and
t^{\prime}=t
, giving us

\left\langle|\tilde{\phi}_{1}({\bf k},t)|^{2}\right\rangle=e^{-2\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\omega_{1}(a(t_{1});{\bf k})\mathrm{d}t_{1}}\left\langle|\tilde{\phi}_{1}({\bf k},t_{0})|^{2}\right\rangle+2\lambda_{\phi}{\bf k}^{2}\int_{t_{0}}^{t}e^{-2\int_{t_{2}}^{t}\omega_{1}(a(t_{1});{\bf k})\mathrm{d}t_{1}}\mathrm{d}t_{2}.

(III.22)

Thus, combining Eqs. (
I.1
), Eq. (
III.11
) and Eq. (
III.22
)
we can calculate the heats
Q_{\text{p}}
and
Q_{\text{t}}
exactly in small noise.
Nevertheless, the integrals in Eq. (
III.22
) cannot be solved analytically in most cases.
Thus, we solved them numerically for a linear protocol, a result used in Figs. (3) and (4) of the main text.

The one case where the integrals can be solved trivially is when
a
is constant.
In this case, Eq. (
III.22
) gives us

\left\langle|\tilde{\phi}_{1}({\bf k},t)|^{2}\right\rangle=e^{-2\omega_{1}(a;{\bf k})[t-t_{0}]}\left\langle|\tilde{\phi}_{1}({\bf k},t_{0})|^{2}\right\rangle+\frac{\lambda_{\phi}{\bf k}^{2}}{\omega_{1}(a;{\bf k})}\left[1-e^{-2\omega_{1}(a;{\bf k})[t-t_{0}]}\right],\qquad\text{if }\dot{a}=0.

(III.23)

This equation depicts a classic relaxation process.
No matter the average at
t_{0}
, this average always tends exponentially to a steady-state average when the system is left alone, i.e., when
\dot{a}(t)=0
for any
t
.
The relaxation time scale of each mode
{\bf k}
is given exactly by the inverse of the relaxation frequency
\omega_{1}(a;{\bf k})
.
Additionally, if the system is already in its steady state at
t_{0}
, then the average in Eq. (
III.23
) does not evolve, because the system is already relaxed.

III.3
Integrated response functions

This section is dedicated to the calculation of the integrated response functions defined in Eqs. (35) of the main text, which for a generic observable
O
read

\displaystyle\zeta_{O}^{(1,1)}(a)

\displaystyle=-\int_{0}^{\infty}tR_{O}^{(1)}(a;t)\mathrm{d}t,

(III.24a)

\displaystyle\zeta_{O}^{(2,1)}(a)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{2}R_{O}^{(1)}(a;t)\mathrm{d}t,

(III.24b)

and

\displaystyle\zeta_{O}^{(2,2)}(a)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{t}^{\infty}tt^{\prime}R_{O}^{(2)}(a;t,t^{\prime})\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}\mathrm{d}t,

(III.24c)

where
R_{O}^{(1)}
and
R_{O}^{(2)}
are defined in Eqs. (
II.1
).

Similarly to how the averages of observables simplify to the averages of
|\tilde{\phi}_{1}|^{2}
in small noise, the same is true for
\zeta_{O}^{(m,n)}
.
More specifically, for a given observable given by Eq. (
III.11
), its integrated response functions obey

\displaystyle\zeta_{O}^{(m,n)}(a)=\frac{T}{2}\sum_{{\bf k}}\tilde{o}_{1}(a;{\bf k})\zeta_{|\tilde{\phi}_{1}|^{2}}^{(m,n)}(a;{\bf k})+\mathcal{O}(T^{3/2}).

(III.25)

Thus, we can focus on the integrated response functions of
|\tilde{\phi}_{1}|^{2}
.

Before we start, it must be noted that the response functions (e.g. Eq. (
II.7
)) contain averages of products of observables at different times.
These can be written as averages of products of
|\tilde{\phi}_{1}|^{2}
at different times, which can be simplified noting that
\tilde{\phi}_{1}
has Gaussian statistics (see Eq. (
III.16
)) and using Isserli’s theorem (also known as classical Wick’s theorem).
Then, with the definitions of the two- and three-entry correlations of Eqs. (
II.4
) and (
II.6
), we can write

\left\langle\left\langle|\tilde{\phi}_{1}({\bf k},t)|^{2}|\tilde{\phi}_{1}({\bf k}_{1},t_{1})|^{2}\right\rangle\right\rangle_{\text{s}}=\left|\left\langle\tilde{\phi}_{1}({\bf k},t)\tilde{\phi}_{1}^{*}({\bf k}_{1},t_{1})\right\rangle_{\text{s}}\right|^{2}+\left|\left\langle\tilde{\phi}_{1}({\bf k},t)\tilde{\phi}_{1}^{*}(-{\bf k}_{1},t_{1})\right\rangle_{\text{s}}\right|^{2}

(III.26)

and

\left\langle\left\langle|\tilde{\phi}_{1}({\bf k},t)|^{2}|\tilde{\phi}_{1}({\bf k}_{1},t_{1})|^{2}|\tilde{\phi}_{1}({\bf k}_{2},t_{2})|^{2}\right\rangle\right\rangle_{\text{s}}=2\Re\bigg\{\left\langle\tilde{\phi}_{1}({\bf k},t)\tilde{\phi}_{1}^{*}({\bf k}_{1},t_{1})\right\rangle_{\text{s}}\left\langle\tilde{\phi}_{1}({\bf k}_{1},t_{1})\tilde{\phi}_{1}^{*}({\bf k}_{2},t_{2})\right\rangle_{\text{s}}\left\langle\tilde{\phi}_{1}({\bf k}_{2},t_{2})\tilde{\phi}_{1}^{*}({\bf k},t)\right\rangle_{\text{s}}\\
+\left\langle\tilde{\phi}_{1}(-{\bf k},t)\tilde{\phi}_{1}^{*}({\bf k}_{1},t_{1})\right\rangle_{\text{s}}\left\langle\tilde{\phi}_{1}({\bf k}_{1},t_{1})\tilde{\phi}_{1}^{*}({\bf k}_{2},t_{2})\right\rangle_{\text{s}}\left\langle\tilde{\phi}_{1}({\bf k}_{2},t_{2})\tilde{\phi}_{1}^{*}(-{\bf k},t)\right\rangle_{\text{s}}\\
+\left\langle\tilde{\phi}_{1}({\bf k},t)\tilde{\phi}_{1}^{*}(-{\bf k}_{1},t_{1})\right\rangle_{\text{s}}\left\langle\tilde{\phi}_{1}(-{\bf k}_{1},t_{1})\tilde{\phi}_{1}^{*}({\bf k}_{2},t_{2})\right\rangle_{\text{s}}\left\langle\tilde{\phi}_{1}({\bf k}_{2},t_{2})\tilde{\phi}_{1}^{*}({\bf k},t)\right\rangle_{\text{s}}\\
+\left\langle\tilde{\phi}_{1}({\bf k},t)\tilde{\phi}_{1}^{*}({\bf k}_{1},t_{1})\right\rangle_{\text{s}}\left\langle\tilde{\phi}_{1}({\bf k}_{1},t_{1})\tilde{\phi}_{1}^{*}(-{\bf k}_{2},t_{2})\right\rangle_{\text{s}}\left\langle\tilde{\phi}_{1}(-{\bf k}_{2},t_{2})\tilde{\phi}_{1}^{*}({\bf k},t)\right\rangle_{\text{s}}\bigg\}.

(III.27)

To start, we need the generalized force
G
and its functional derivative
\delta G/\delta a
.
Writing the free energy in the form of Eq. (
III.11
),

\displaystyle F([\phi],a)=F(\bar{\phi};a)+\frac{T}{2}\sum_{{\bf k}}\tilde{f}_{1}(a;{\bf k})|\tilde{\phi}_{1}({\bf k},t)|^{2}+\mathcal{O}(T^{3/2}),

(III.28)

the generalized force from Eq. (
II.10
) can be written as

G(t_{1})=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{{\bf k}_{1}}\partial_{a}\tilde{f}_{1}(a;{\bf k}_{1})\Bigg\{\omega_{1}(a,{\bf k}_{1})+\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt_{1}}\Bigg\}|\tilde{\phi}_{1}({\bf k}_{1},t_{1})|^{2}+\mathcal{O}(T^{1/2}),

(III.29)

while its its functional derivative from Eq. (
II.11
) becomes

\frac{\delta G(t_{1})}{\delta a(t_{2})}=-\frac{1}{2}\delta(t_{1}-t_{2})\sum_{{\bf k}_{1}}\left\{\partial_{a}^{2}\tilde{f}_{1}(a;{\bf k}_{1})\left[\omega_{1}(a,{\bf k}_{1})+\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt_{1}}\right]+\partial_{a}\tilde{f}_{1}(a;{\bf k}_{1})\partial_{a}\omega_{1}(a,{\bf k}_{1})\right\}|\tilde{\phi}_{1}({\bf k}_{1},t_{1})|^{2}+\mathcal{O}(T^{1/2}).

(III.30)

As mentioned before, the
\mathcal{O}(T)
terms in Eqs. (
II.10
) and (
II.11
) are dwarfed in our small noise treatment, and thus we put them together with the
\mathcal{O}(T^{1/2})
terms in Eqs. (
III.29
) and (
III.30
).
Then, the response functions follow straight from the results in Eqs. (
II.7
) and (
II.5
).
For the first order with
t\geq t_{1}
, we get (omitting
\mathcal{O}(T^{1/2})
)

\displaystyle R_{|\tilde{\phi}_{1}|^{2}}^{(1)}(a;{\bf k},t-t_{1})

\displaystyle=\left\langle\left\langle|\tilde{\phi}_{1}({\bf k},t)|^{2}G(t_{1})\right\rangle\right\rangle_{\text{s}}(a)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{{\bf k}_{1}}\partial_{a}\tilde{f}_{1}(a;{\bf k}_{1})\Bigg\{\omega_{1}(a,{\bf k}_{1})+\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt_{1}}\Bigg\}\left\langle\left\langle|\tilde{\phi}_{1}({\bf k},t)|^{2}|\tilde{\phi}_{1}({\bf k}_{1},t_{1})|^{2}\right\rangle\right\rangle_{\text{s}}(a)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{{\bf k}_{1}}\partial_{a}\tilde{f}_{1}(a;{\bf k}_{1})\Bigg\{\omega_{1}(a,{\bf k}_{1})+\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt_{1}}\Bigg\}\left|\left\langle\tilde{\phi}_{1}({\bf k},t)\tilde{\phi}_{1}^{*}({\bf k}_{1},t_{1})\right\rangle_{\text{s}}(a)\right|^{2}

\displaystyle=-\partial_{a}\tilde{f}_{1}(a;{\bf k})\Bigg\{\omega_{1}(a,{\bf k})+\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt_{1}}\Bigg\}\left(\frac{\lambda_{\phi}{\bf k}^{2}}{\omega_{1}(a;{\bf k})}\right)^{2}e^{-2\omega_{1}(a;{\bf k})[t-t_{1}]}

\displaystyle=-\partial_{a}\tilde{f}_{1}(a;{\bf k})\frac{2\lambda_{\phi}^{2}{\bf k}^{4}}{\omega_{1}(a;{\bf k})}e^{-2\omega_{1}(a;{\bf k})[t-t_{1}]},

(III.31)

where we used Eq. (
III.29
) in the second line, Eq. (
III.26
) in the third line, and Eq. (
III.18
) (the steady-state average
\langle|\tilde{\phi}_{1}|^{2}\rangle_{\text{s}}
) in the fourth line.
Similarly, the second order response function is (for
t\geq t_{1}\geq t_{2}
)

R_{|\tilde{\phi}_{1}|^{2}}^{(2)}(a;{\bf k},t-t_{1},t-t_{2})=\big(\partial_{a}\tilde{f}_{1}(a;{\bf k})\big)^{2}\frac{4\lambda_{\phi}^{3}{\bf k}^{6}}{\omega_{1}(a;{\bf k})}e^{-2\omega_{1}(a;{\bf k})[t-t_{2}]}\\
-\delta(t_{1}-t_{2})\left[2\omega_{1}(a,{\bf k})\partial_{a}^{2}\tilde{f}_{1}(a;{\bf k})+\partial_{a}\tilde{f}_{1}(a;{\bf k})\partial_{a}\omega_{1}(a,{\bf k})\right]\left(\frac{\lambda_{\phi}{\bf k}^{2}}{\omega_{1}(a;{\bf k})}\right)^{2}e^{-2\omega_{1}(a;{\bf k})[t-t_{1}]},

(III.32)

where we also used Eqs. (
III.27
) and (
III.30
).

Next, we use the definitions in Eqs. (
III.24
) to calculate the integrated response functions.
From Eq. (
III.31
), we get

\displaystyle\zeta_{|\tilde{\phi}_{1}|^{2}}^{(1,1)}(a;{\bf k})

\displaystyle=-\int_{0}^{\infty}tR_{|\tilde{\phi}_{1}|^{2}}^{(1)}(a;{\bf k},t)\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\partial_{a}\tilde{f}_{1}(a;{\bf k})\frac{2\lambda_{\phi}^{2}{\bf k}^{4}}{\omega_{1}(a;{\bf k})}\int_{0}^{\infty}te^{-2\omega_{1}(a;{\bf k})t}\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\partial_{a}\tilde{f}_{1}(a;{\bf k})\frac{\lambda_{\phi}^{2}{\bf k}^{4}}{2\omega_{1}^{3}(a;{\bf k})},

(III.33)

and

\displaystyle\zeta_{|\tilde{\phi}_{1}|^{2}}^{(2,1)}(a;{\bf k})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{2}R_{|\tilde{\phi}_{1}|^{2}}^{(1)}(a;{\bf k},t)\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=-\partial_{a}\tilde{f}_{1}(a;{\bf k})\frac{\lambda_{\phi}^{2}{\bf k}^{4}}{\omega_{1}(a;{\bf k})}\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{2}e^{-2\omega_{1}(a;{\bf k})t}\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=-\partial_{a}\tilde{f}_{1}(a;{\bf k})\frac{\lambda_{\phi}^{2}{\bf k}^{4}}{4\omega_{1}^{4}(a;{\bf k})}.

(III.34)

Finally, from Eq. (
III.32
), we arrive at

\displaystyle\zeta_{|\tilde{\phi}_{1}|^{2}}^{(2,2)}(a;{\bf k})

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{t}^{\infty}tt^{\prime}R_{|\tilde{\phi}_{1}|^{2}}^{(2)}(a;t,t^{\prime})\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\left[3\lambda_{\phi}{\bf k}^{2}\big(\partial_{a}\tilde{f}_{1}(a;{\bf k})\big)^{2}-2\omega_{1}(a,{\bf k})\partial_{a}^{2}\tilde{f}_{1}(a;{\bf k})-\partial_{a}\tilde{f}_{1}(a;{\bf k})\partial_{a}\omega_{1}(a,{\bf k})\right]\frac{\lambda_{\phi}^{2}{\bf k}^{4}}{4\omega_{1}^{5}(a;{\bf k})}.

(III.35)

III.4
Active work in the relaxation process

Our aim in this section is to prove Eq. (
I.5
), which includes the active work in the relaxation process.
The active power
P
, when written in the form of Eq. (
III.11
), is

P(t)=\frac{T}{2}\sum_{{\bf k}}\tilde{p}_{1}({\bf k})|\tilde{\phi}_{1}({\bf k},t)|^{2}+\mathcal{O}(T^{3/2}),

(III.36)

where
\tilde{p}_{1}
must be determined once
\boldsymbol{C}
is specified (see Eq. (
IV.11
) for the CMB case).
Thus, we can focus only on the relaxation of
|\tilde{\phi}_{1}|^{2}
, which is described in Eq. (
III.23
) with
t_{0}=\tau
and
a=a_{\tau}
,

\left\langle|\tilde{\phi}_{1}({\bf k},t)|^{2}\right\rangle=\left\langle|\tilde{\phi}_{1}({\bf k},\tau)|^{2}\right\rangle_{\text{s}}+\left(\left\langle|\tilde{\phi}_{1}({\bf k},\tau)|^{2}\right\rangle-\left\langle|\tilde{\phi}_{1}({\bf k},\tau)|^{2}\right\rangle_{\text{s}}\right)e^{-2\omega_{1}(a_{\tau};{\bf k})[t-\tau]},

(III.37)

where
\omega_{1}
is the relaxation frequency of the specific model, and we identified
\langle|\tilde{\phi}_{1}|^{2}\rangle_{\text{s}}=\lambda_{\phi}{\bf k}^{2}/\omega_{1}
.
Note that the
t
-dependence in Eq. (
III.37
) is only found in the arguments of the exponentials.

Although DRT does not apply to the full relaxation process (
t\geq\tau
), it does apply to
t=\tau
, and thus we can use Eq. (34) of the main text up to first order to write

\left\langle|\tilde{\phi}_{1}({\bf k},\tau)|^{2}\right\rangle=\left\langle|\tilde{\phi}_{1}({\bf k},\tau)|^{2}\right\rangle_{\text{s}}(a_{\tau})+\dot{a}(\tau)\zeta_{|\tilde{\phi}_{1}|^{2}}^{(1,1)}(a_{\tau};{\bf k})+\mathcal{O}(\ddot{a},\dot{a}^{2}),

(III.38)

where
\zeta^{(1,1)}_{|\phi_{1}|^{2}}
is the first-order integrated response function of
|\tilde{\phi}_{1}|^{2}
(see Eq. (
III.33
)).

Combining Eqs. (
III.36
-
III.38
), we get

\displaystyle\int_{\tau}^{\tau+\tau_{\text{r}}}\left\langle P(t)\right\rangle\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\frac{T}{2}\sum_{{\bf k}}\tilde{p}_{1}({\bf k})\left[\left\langle|\tilde{\phi}_{1}({\bf k},\tau)|^{2}\right\rangle_{\text{s}}\tau_{\text{r}}+\dot{a}(\tau)\frac{\zeta_{|\tilde{\phi}_{1}|^{2}}^{(1,1)}(a_{\tau};{\bf k})}{2\omega_{1}(a_{\tau};{\bf k})}\left(1-e^{-2\omega_{1}(a_{\tau};{\bf k})\tau_{\text{r}}}\right)\right]+\mathcal{O}(T^{3/2}).

(III.39)

Now, looking at the total heat in Eq. (
I.3b
), we see that we also need
\dot{a}(\tau)\zeta^{(2,1)}_{P}(a_{\tau})
, which for the generic form of
P
in Eq. (
III.36
) reads

\displaystyle\dot{a}(\tau)\zeta^{(2,1)}_{P}(a_{\tau})=\dot{a}(\tau)\frac{T}{2}\sum_{{\bf k}}\tilde{p}_{1}({\bf k})\zeta_{|\tilde{\phi}_{1}|^{2}}^{(2,1)}(a_{\tau};{\bf k})+\mathcal{O}(T^{3/2}).

(III.40)

Thus, summing Eqs. (
III.39
) and (
III.40
), we find

\dot{a}(\tau)\zeta^{(2,1)}_{P}(a_{\tau})+\int_{\tau}^{\tau+\tau_{\text{r}}}\left\langle P(t)\right\rangle\mathrm{d}t=\tau_{\text{r}}\frac{T}{2}\sum_{{\bf k}}\tilde{p}_{1}({\bf k})\left\langle|\tilde{\phi}_{1}({\bf k},\tau)|^{2}\right\rangle_{\text{s}}\\
+\dot{a}(\tau)\frac{T}{2}\sum_{{\bf k}}\tilde{p}_{1}({\bf k})\left(\frac{\zeta_{|\tilde{\phi}_{1}|^{2}}^{(1,1)}(a_{\tau};{\bf k})}{2\omega_{1}(a_{\tau};{\bf k})}+\zeta_{|\tilde{\phi}_{1}|^{2}}^{(2,1)}(a_{\tau};{\bf k})\right)\\
-\dot{a}(\tau)\frac{T}{2}\sum_{{\bf k}}\tilde{p}_{1}({\bf k})\frac{\zeta_{|\tilde{\phi}_{1}|^{2}}^{(1,1)}(a_{\tau};{\bf k})}{2\omega_{1}(a_{\tau};{\bf k})}e^{-2\omega_{1}(a_{\tau};{\bf k})\tau_{\text{r}}}+\mathcal{O}(T^{3/2}).

(III.41)

Comparing Eqs. (
III.33
) and (
III.34
), we can see that
\zeta_{|\tilde{\phi}_{1}|^{2}}^{(1,1)}=-2\omega_{1}\zeta_{|\tilde{\phi}_{1}|^{2}}^{(2,1)}
.
Therefore, the second term on the RHS of Eq. (
III.41
) is null.
This leaves us with two terms: the first scales linearly with
\tau_{\text{r}}
, while the last decreases exponentially.
In fact, since we assumed
\tau_{\text{r}}
to be the time required for the system to reach its steady state, then we must have
\omega_{1}(a_{\tau};{\bf k})\tau_{\text{r}}\gg 1
for every
{\bf k}
by definition.
Thus, the last term can be neglected, leading us to

\displaystyle\dot{a}(\tau)\zeta^{(2,1)}_{P}(a_{\tau})+\int_{\tau}^{\tau+\tau_{\text{r}}}\left\langle P(t)\right\rangle\mathrm{d}t=\tau_{\text{r}}\left\langle P\right\rangle_{\text{s}}(a_{\tau}),

(III.42)

where we identified
\left\langle P\right\rangle_{\text{s}}
in the first term on the RHS of Eq. (
III.41
) (compare to Eq. (
III.36
)).
Equation (
III.42
) is the same as Eq. (
I.5
), proving the result.

In conclusion, the non-functional (but still process-dependent) part of the protocol heat (Eq. (
I.1a
)), namely
\dot{a}(\tau)B_{\text{p}}
, is compensated in the following relaxation process, and thus should not affect the optimization procedure.
It must be noted that, while proved here only for the first order in the small noise expansion, this result is true for all orders, as long as all quadratic correlation functions between the many orders of the expansion obey a relaxation process such as Eq. (
III.37
), possibly with different relaxation frequencies.
Therefore, the expression for the total heat
Q_{\text{t}}
assuming DRT (Eq. (
I.6
)) holds true for any strength of the noise.

IV
Chemical Model B

This section is dedicated to the active field theory we employed in the main text to test our optimization procedures, which we call Chemical Model B (CMB).
It is defined by the dynamics of Eqs. (
III.1
) with the following free energy and coupling:

\displaystyle F

\displaystyle=\int_{V}\left(a\frac{\phi^{2}}{2}+b\frac{\phi^{4}}{4}+\kappa\frac{(\boldsymbol{\nabla}\phi)^{2}}{2}\right)\,\mathrm{d}{\bf r}

(IV.1)

and

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{C}

\displaystyle=\gamma\boldsymbol{\nabla}\phi,

(IV.2)

where
b
,
\kappa
and
\gamma
are constants.
For future reference, the active power (Eq. (11) of the main text) is

\displaystyle P=\frac{\Delta\mu}{\lambda_{\phi}}\int_{V}\left(\boldsymbol{C}\cdot\boldsymbol{J}-\Delta\mu\,\boldsymbol{C}^{2}\right)\,\mathrm{d}{\bf r}.

(IV.3)

IV.1
Phase-separation

In this section, we present the phase-separated solution to the one-dimensional CMB in its steady state.
At zero temperature, the dynamic equation for the field
\phi
is, taken from Eq. (17) of the main text,

\dot{\phi}(x,t)=\lambda_{\phi}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x^{2}}\left[\left(a-\frac{\gamma\Delta\mu}{\lambda_{\phi}}\right)\phi(x,t)+b\phi^{3}(x,t)-\kappa\frac{\partial^{2}\phi(x,t)}{\partial x^{2}}\right].

(IV.4)

Since there are no thermal fluctuations, the steady state solution is easily found by setting
\dot{\phi}=0
.
In that case, a possible solution of Eq. (
IV.4
), when
a+b\bar{\phi}^{2}<\gamma\Delta\mu/\lambda_{\phi}
, is

\phi(x)=\phi_{\text{het}}\tanh\left(k_{\text{het}}x-A_{\text{het}}\right),

(IV.5)

where
\phi_{\text{het}}\equiv\sqrt{(\gamma\Delta\mu/\lambda_{\phi}-a)/b}
,
k_{\text{het}}\equiv\sqrt{(\gamma\Delta\mu/\lambda_{\phi}-a)/(2\kappa)}
and

A_{\text{het}}\equiv\frac{1}{2}\log\left(\frac{\sinh\left[\frac{k_{\text{het}}V}{2}\left(1-\frac{\bar{\phi}}{\phi_{\text{het}}}\right)\right]}{\sinh\left[\frac{k_{\text{het}}V}{2}\left(1+\frac{\bar{\phi}}{\phi_{\text{het}}}\right)\right]}\right).

The field of Eq. (
IV.5
), plotted in the inset of Fig. 2(b) of the main text, contains an interface between two regions of opposite densities
\phi_{\pm}=\pm\phi_{\text{het}}
, signaling a coexistence of phases.
While
\phi_{\text{het}}
and
k_{\text{het}}
are necessary for Eq. (
IV.5
) to be a solution of Eq. (
IV.4
),
A_{\text{het}}
only comes from normalization (
\int_{-V/2}^{V/2}\phi(x)dx=V\bar{\phi}
), and it is only real when
a+b\bar{\phi}^{2}<\gamma\Delta\mu/\lambda_{\phi}
.
The curve
\tilde{a}=a-\gamma\Delta\mu/\lambda_{\phi}=-b\bar{\phi}^{2}
is what defines the first order transition line in Fig. 2(a) of the main text.

The local active power
p
(see Eq. (20) of the main text) can easily be obtained for this heterogeneous solution by using Eq. (
IV.5
),

\displaystyle p(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{\Delta\mu}{\lambda_{\phi}}\left[\boldsymbol{C}\cdot\boldsymbol{J}-\Delta\mu\,\boldsymbol{C}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{\gamma\Delta\mu}{\lambda_{\phi}}\left[\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial\phi^{2}(x)}{\partial t}-\gamma\Delta\mu\left(\frac{\partial\phi(x)}{\partial x}\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=-\frac{(k_{\text{het}}\phi_{\text{het}}\gamma\Delta\mu)^{2}}{\lambda_{\phi}}\text{sech}^{4}\left(k_{\text{het}}x-A_{\text{het}}\right).

(IV.6)

The first term inside the square brackets, coming from
\boldsymbol{C}\cdot\boldsymbol{J}
, vanishes, owning to the passive-like nature of CMB.
Equation (
IV.6
) is also plotted on the inset of Fig. 2(b) of the main text.

From Eq. (
IV.6
), it is straightforward to obtain the global active power.
In the homogeneous state
\phi(x)=\bar{\phi}
, the only valid steady-state solution at zero temperature when
a+b\bar{\phi}^{2}>\gamma\Delta\mu/\lambda_{\phi}
, the local and global active power are evidently zero.
Thus, we can write the global active power in steady state as a function of
\Delta\mu

P(\Delta\mu)=\begin{cases}0,&a+b\bar{\phi}^{2}>\gamma\Delta\mu/\lambda_{\phi};\\
\displaystyle{-\frac{(\phi_{\text{het}}\gamma\Delta\mu)^{2}}{\lambda_{\phi}}\frac{2k_{\text{het}}}{3}\frac{\cosh(2k_{\text{het}}V)+6\cosh(k_{\text{het}}V)\cosh(2A_{\text{het}})+5}{[\cosh(k_{\text{het}}V)+\cosh(2A_{\text{het}})]^{3}}\sinh(k_{\text{het}}V)},&a+b\bar{\phi}^{2}<\gamma\Delta\mu/\lambda_{\phi}.\end{cases}

(IV.7)

Equation (
IV.7
) is plotted on Fig. 2(b) of the main text.

IV.4
Scalings with volume and activity

In this section, we analyze the scalings of the quantities appearing in the heats (Eqs. (
I.3
)).
Although the Fourier transform defined in Eq. (
III.8
) is discrete, it can be extrapolated in the limit of large system sizes.
More specifically, in the thermodynamic limit
V\to\infty
, the sums over Fourier modes can be cast as integrals.
For an arbitrary function
g(k)
, we can write

\sum_{{\bf k}}g(k)\to\frac{V}{[2\pi]^{d}}\int_{k_{\mathrm{min}}}^{k_{\mathrm{max}}}g(k)\left(\frac{2\pi^{d/2}}{\Gamma(d/2)}k^{d-1}\right)dk,

(IV.16)

where
d
is the dimension of the system,
k_{\mathrm{min}}=2\pi/V^{1/d}
is the lower cutoff,
k_{\mathrm{max}}=\pi/\ell
is the upper cutoff,
\ell
is the lattice constant and
\Gamma
is the standard gamma function.
This allows us to study the scaling of the steady-state averages and of the integrated response functions with
V
when
V\to\infty
and with
\Delta\mu
when
\Delta\mu\to 0
.

For the rest of this section, we take
d=1
to represent the 1D CMB of the main text.
Starting with the steady-state averages (Eqs. (
IV.14
)), after defining
\bar{a}\equiv a+3b\bar{\phi}^{2}
, we get

\displaystyle\left\langle F\right\rangle_{\text{s}}(a)

\displaystyle=\left[a\frac{\bar{\phi}^{2}}{2}+b\frac{\bar{\phi}^{4}}{4}\right]V+\frac{TV}{2}\left[\frac{k_{\text{max}}}{\pi}+\frac{1}{2}\frac{\gamma\Delta\mu}{\{\lambda_{\phi}\kappa[\lambda_{\phi}\bar{a}-\gamma\Delta\mu]\}^{1/2}}\right]+\mathcal{O}(T^{3/2},V^{0}),

(IV.17a)

\displaystyle\left\langle\partial_{a}F\right\rangle_{\text{s}}(a)

\displaystyle=\frac{\bar{\phi}^{2}}{2}V+\frac{TV}{2}\left[\frac{1}{2}\frac{\lambda_{\phi}}{\{\lambda_{\phi}\kappa[\lambda_{\phi}\bar{a}-\gamma\Delta\mu]\}^{1/2}}\right]+\mathcal{O}(T^{3/2},V^{0})

(IV.17b)

and

\displaystyle\left\langle P\right\rangle_{\text{s}}(a)

\displaystyle=-\frac{T\gamma^{2}\Delta\mu^{2}V}{\lambda_{\phi}\kappa}\left\{\frac{k_{\text{max}}}{\pi}-\frac{1}{2}\left[\frac{\lambda_{\phi}\bar{a}-\gamma\Delta\mu}{\lambda_{\phi}\kappa}\right]^{1/2}\right\}+\mathcal{O}(T^{3/2},V^{0}).

(IV.17c)

From these, it is clear all these averages scale as
V
in the thermodynamic limit.
We can also see that
\langle P\rangle_{\text{s}}\sim\Delta\mu^{2}
, giving it the exact same
V
and
\Delta\mu
scalings as
P_{0}
of Eqs. (
I.1
).

Next, we move on to the continuum limit of the integrated response functions (Eqs. (
IV.15
)), which leads to

\displaystyle\zeta_{F}^{(1,1)}(a)

\displaystyle=\frac{T\lambda_{\phi}V}{4[\lambda_{\phi}\bar{a}-\gamma\Delta\mu]^{3}}\left\{\frac{\lambda_{\phi}\bar{a}}{\pi k_{\text{min}}}-\frac{3}{16}\Big[4\lambda_{\phi}\bar{a}+\gamma\Delta\mu\Big]\left[\frac{\lambda_{\phi}\kappa}{\lambda_{\phi}\bar{a}-\gamma\Delta\mu}\right]^{1/2}\right\}+\mathcal{O}(T^{3/2},V^{0}),

(IV.18a)

\displaystyle\zeta_{\partial_{a}F}^{(1,1)}(a)

\displaystyle=\frac{T\lambda_{\phi}^{2}V}{4[\lambda_{\phi}\bar{a}-\gamma\Delta\mu]^{3}}\left\{\frac{1}{\pi k_{\text{min}}}-\frac{15}{16}\left[\frac{\lambda_{\phi}\kappa}{\lambda_{\phi}\bar{a}-\gamma\Delta\mu}\right]^{1/2}\right\}+\mathcal{O}(T^{3/2},V^{0}),

(IV.18b)

\displaystyle\zeta_{P}^{(1,1)}(a)

\displaystyle=-\frac{T\gamma\Delta\mu V}{32}\frac{\lambda_{\phi}\Big[4\lambda_{\phi}\bar{a}-\gamma\Delta\mu\Big]}{\sqrt{\lambda_{\phi}\kappa[\lambda_{\phi}\bar{a}-\gamma\Delta\mu]^{5}}}+\mathcal{O}(T^{3/2},V^{0}),

(IV.18c)

\displaystyle\zeta_{P}^{(2,1)}(a)

\displaystyle=\frac{T\gamma\Delta\mu\lambda_{\phi}V}{4[\lambda_{\phi}\bar{a}-\gamma\Delta\mu]^{4}}\left\{\frac{\lambda_{\phi}\bar{a}}{\pi k_{\text{min}}}-\frac{15}{96}\Big[6\lambda_{\phi}\bar{a}+\gamma\Delta\mu\Big]\left[\frac{\lambda_{\phi}\kappa}{\lambda_{\phi}\bar{a}-\gamma\Delta\mu}\right]^{1/2}\right\}+\mathcal{O}(T^{3/2},V^{0})

(IV.18d)

and

\displaystyle\zeta_{P}^{(2,2)}(a)

\displaystyle=-\frac{T\gamma\Delta\mu\lambda_{\phi}^{2}V}{2[\lambda_{\phi}\bar{a}-\gamma\Delta\mu]^{5}}\left\{\frac{\lambda_{\phi}\bar{a}}{\pi k_{\text{min}}}-\frac{105}{768}\Big[8\lambda_{\phi}\bar{a}+\gamma\Delta\mu\Big]\left[\frac{\lambda_{\phi}\kappa}{\lambda_{\phi}\bar{a}-\gamma\Delta\mu}\right]^{1/2}\right\}+\mathcal{O}(T^{3/2},V^{0}).

(IV.18e)

Because
k_{\text{min}}=2\pi/V
, all of these (except for
\zeta_{P}^{(1,1)}
) scale as
V^{2}
in the thermodynamic limit.
Additionally,
\zeta_{F}^{(1,1)}
and
\zeta_{\partial_{a}F}^{(1,1)}
scale as
\Delta\mu^{0}
in the low activity limit.
Consequently,
B_{\text{p}}
and
m
of Eqs. (
I.4

V^{2}
and

IV.5
Optimal protocols

The goal of this section is to find an expression for the optimal protocols of CMB.
The functional part of the heats in Eqs. (
I.3
) reads, after using the Lagargian in Eq. (
I.4c
),

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\tau}\left[m(a(t))\dot{a}^{2}(t)+\left\langle P\right\rangle_{\text{s}}(a(t))\right]\mathrm{d}t=\int_{0}^{1}\left[m(a(s))\frac{\dot{a}^{2}(s)}{\tau}+\tau\left\langle P\right\rangle_{\text{s}}(a(s))\right]ds,

(IV.19)

where we changed variables to
s=t/\tau
and abused notation by writing
\dot{a}(s)=da/ds
.
Thus, in the dimensionless variable
s
, the Lagrangian can be written as

L(a,\dot{a})=m(a)\frac{\dot{a}^{2}}{\tau}+\tau\left\langle P\right\rangle_{\text{s}}(a).

(IV.20)

This Lagrangian can be readily placed in the Euler-Lagrange equation,

\frac{\partial L}{\partial a_{\text{op}}}=\frac{d}{ds}\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot{a}_{\text{op}}}\quad\Rightarrow\quad\ddot{a}_{\text{op}}=\frac{\tau^{2}\partial_{a}\left\langle P\right\rangle_{\text{s}}(a_{\text{op}})-\dot{a}_{\text{op}}^{2}\partial_{a}m(a_{\text{op}})}{2m(a_{\text{op}})},

(IV.21)

to furnish the optimal protocol for a given
\tau
.
Equation (
IV.21
) is a non-linear second-order equation and thus computationally intensive.
Here, we show how to use the form of Eq. (
IV.20
) to more efficiently solve Eq. (
IV.21
).

Since the Lagrangian of Eq. (
IV.20
) does not depend explicitly on
s
, the optimal protocol exhibits a first integral of motion given by

I\equiv\frac{1}{\tau}\left(\dot{a}_{\text{op}}\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot{a}_{\text{op}}}-L\right)=m(a_{\text{op}})\frac{\dot{a}_{\text{op}}^{2}}{\tau^{2}}-\left\langle P\right\rangle_{\text{s}}(a_{\text{op}}),

(IV.22)

which can be rewritten as

\left(\frac{1}{\tau}\frac{da_{\text{op}}}{ds}\right)^{2}=\frac{I+\left\langle P\right\rangle_{\text{s}}(a_{\text{op}})}{m(a_{\text{op}})}.

(IV.23)

Without further information, we might assume the optimal protocol to be monotonic, in which case Eq. (
IV.23
) can be readily solved with a single integration to give
a_{\text{op}}(s)
implicitly, as in

\tau s=\left|\int_{a_{0}}^{a_{\text{op}}(s)}\sqrt{\frac{m(a)}{I+\left\langle P\right\rangle_{\text{s}}(a)}}da\right|,

(IV.24)

once
I
is determined as a function of
\tau
,
a_{0}
and
a_{\tau}
from

\tau=\left|\int_{a_{0}}^{a_{\tau}}\sqrt{\frac{m(a)}{I+\left\langle P\right\rangle_{\text{s}}(a)}}da\right|.

(IV.25)

It must be noted that, because the LHS of Eq. (
IV.23
) is non-negative, there might be restrictions on the values of
I
that limit the applicability of Eqs. (
IV.24
) and (
IV.25
), arising from the properties of
\langle P\rangle_{\text{s}}
and
m
.
For example, in the homogeneous phases of the systems treated in this paper,
m(a)
is positive and monotonically decreasing, while
\langle P\rangle_{\text{s}}(a)
is negative and monotonically increasing (see sections
IV.2
and
IV.3
).
With these assumptions, we can see that Eq. (
IV.23
) implies a lower bound on the first integral, i.e.,
I\geq-\min_{a}\left\langle P\right\rangle_{\text{s}}(a).

This minimum happens at
a_{\text{min}}\equiv\min\{a_{0},a_{\tau}\}
, and thus the lower bound on
I
is
-\left\langle P\right\rangle_{\text{s}}(a_{\text{min}})
.
This, in turn, sets an upper bound on the protocol duration
\tau
that can be put into Eq. (
IV.25
), given by

\hat{\tau}\equiv\left|\int_{a_{0}}^{a_{\tau}}\sqrt{\frac{m(a)}{\left\langle P\right\rangle_{\text{s}}(a)-\left\langle P\right\rangle_{\text{s}}(a_{\text{min}})}}da\right|.

(IV.26)

Notwithstanding,
\tau
is a predetermined parameter of the protocol and can not be limited in any way by the optimization procedure.
We can then infer that, when
\tau
is bigger than
\hat{\tau}
, the assumption of monotonicity of the optimal protocol must be false, and its derivative must be zero at some point
s_{1}
.
Consequently, we need to integrate Eq. (
IV.23
) with different signs in different regions of the integration range, invalidating Eqs. (
IV.24
) and (
IV.25
) in this scenario.
Given the assumptions on
\langle P\rangle_{\text{s}}
and
m
, Eq. (
IV.21
) reveals that
\ddot{a}_{\text{op}}>0
, which means that
\dot{a}_{\text{op}}
changes sign only once and we need to split the integration region in two.
Then, Eq. (
IV.23
) shows that zero derivative can happen at
a_{1}\equiv a_{\text{op}}(s_{1})
given implicitly by
I=-\langle P\rangle_{\text{s}}(a_{1})
.
Moreover, since
I\geq-\langle P\rangle_{\text{s}}(a_{\text{min}})
, we must have
a_{1}\leq a_{\text{min}}
, otherwise
I
would bigger than its lower bound.
Thus, the optimal protocol attains zero derivative only once when it equals
a_{1}
, which is below the original range
[a_{0},a_{\tau}]
, and its initial derivative is always negative.

We can then conclude that, when
\tau>\hat{\tau}
, the optimal protocol is given implicitly by

\tau s=\begin{cases}-\displaystyle\int_{a_{0}}^{a_{\text{op}}(s)}\sqrt{\dfrac{m(a)}{\left\langle P\right\rangle_{\text{s}}(a)-\left\langle P\right\rangle_{\text{s}}(a_{1})}}da,&0\leq s\leq s_{1};\\
-\displaystyle\int_{a_{0}}^{a_{1}}\sqrt{\dfrac{m(a)}{\left\langle P\right\rangle_{\text{s}}(a)-\left\langle P\right\rangle_{\text{s}}(a_{1})}}da+\displaystyle\int_{a_{1}}^{a_{\text{op}}(s)}\sqrt{\dfrac{m(a)}{\left\langle P\right\rangle_{\text{s}}(a)-\left\langle P\right\rangle_{\text{s}}(a_{1})}}da,&s_{1}<s\leq 1;\end{cases}

(IV.27)

after
a_{1}
is determined as a function of
\tau
,
a_{0}
and
a_{\tau}
from

\tau=-\int_{a_{0}}^{a_{1}}\sqrt{\frac{m(a)}{\left\langle P\right\rangle_{\text{s}}(a)-\left\langle P\right\rangle_{\text{s}}(a_{1})}}da+\int_{a_{1}}^{a_{\tau}}\sqrt{\frac{m(a)}{\left\langle P\right\rangle_{\text{s}}(a)-\left\langle P\right\rangle_{\text{s}}(a_{1})}}da.

(IV.28)

Some of the non-monotonic solutions in fact become smaller than
a_{\text{1st}}
, the first-order transition line, but merely because the small noise expansion around the homogeneous solution does not take into account the presence of the transition line.
To compensate, we forcefully alter a posteriori all optimal protocols to never cross the phase transition.

It is worth noting that we split the integration region in two because, in our case,
\ddot{a}_{\text{op}}(s)
has a constant sign.
With different
a
-dependence on
\langle P\rangle_{\text{s}}
and
m
, one might need to split the region in more parts.

IV.6
Iterative method of obtaining optimal durations for the optimal protocols

In the main text, we estimated the position of the minimum
\tau_{\text{t}}
of the total heat
Q_{\text{t}}
for the optimal protocol by extrapolating the asymptotic results of the two master curves
\alpha_{0}
and
\alpha_{\infty}
into the intermediate region and finding their intersection.
There is, however, a more accurate numerical method of of finding
\tau_{\text{t}}
, which we describe in this section.

Our starting point is Eq. (14) of the main text,

\displaystyle\tau_{\text{t}}^{2}=\frac{\int_{0}^{1}\dot{a}^{2}m(a)ds}{P_{0}+\int_{0}^{1}\langle P\rangle_{\text{s}}(a)ds}.

(IV.29)

This equation provides us with the optimal duration
\tau_{\text{t}}
for a protocol that can be written as a function solely of
s=t/\tau
, thus it does not apply to the optimal protocols
a_{\text{op}}(\tau;s)
.
Nonetheless, we can pick an arbitrary duration
\tau_{0}
and generate its associated protocol
a_{\text{op}}(\tau_{0};s)
, as is done in Sec. (
IV.5
).
This gives us a curve in the range
0\leq s\leq 1
, and despite this curve not being optimal for all values of
\tau
, we can still use it for any
\tau
.
Doing so gives us the total heat of this curve (Eq. (
I.6
)) and a well-defined minimum point given by the RHS of Eq. (
IV.29
); call this
\tau_{1}
.
We can then repeat the steps, generating
a_{\text{op}}(\tau_{1};s)
and finding its minimum
\tau_{2}
.
This procedure converges to
\tau_{\text{t}}
because the total heat always displays a global minimum.
Equation (23) of the main text, which estimates
\tau_{\text{t}}
from asymptotics, is in agreement with the procedure just described for all simulations done in this paper.

IV.7
Numerical simulations

In this section, we present the basics of the numerical simulations shown in the main text, whose source code can be found in Ref.
[
2
]
.
Working in 1D, we discretize time as
t=j\delta t
where
j
is a natural number, and space as
x=n\delta x
, where
n=0,1,\cdots,N_{x}-1
and
N_{x}
is the number of lattice points, obeying
N_{x}\delta x=V
.
Labeling the field as
\phi_{n}^{j}
, we take its discrete Fourier transform
\mathcal{F}[\phi_{n}^{j}]_{k}\equiv\tilde{\phi}_{k}^{j}
(where
k=0,2\pi/(N_{x}\delta x),\cdots\pi/\delta x
) and update it in each time step
\delta t
using a pseudo-spectral scheme, such that space derivatives can be calculated exactly.
The current is calculated using the discretized form of Eq. (17) of the main text,

\tilde{J}_{k}^{j}=-i\lambda_{\phi}k\left\{\left[a(j\delta t)-\frac{\gamma\Delta\mu}{\lambda_{\phi}}+\kappa k^{2}\right]\tilde{\phi}_{k}^{j}+b\mathcal{F}[(\phi_{n}^{j})^{3}]_{k}\right\}+\sqrt{\frac{2T\lambda_{\phi}}{\delta x\delta t}}\tilde{\xi}_{k}^{j},

(IV.30)

where
\langle\xi_{k}^{j}\xi_{k^{\prime}}^{j^{\prime}}\rangle=\delta_{k,k^{\prime}}\delta^{j,j^{\prime}}
and the spurious drift vanishes in 1D
[
3
]
.
Then, the field gets updated as

\phi_{n}^{j+1}=\phi_{n}^{j}-\mathcal{F}^{-1}[ik\tilde{J}_{k}^{j}]_{n}\delta t,

(IV.31)

where we returned to position space because we need to calculate
\mathcal{F}[(\phi_{n}^{j})^{3}]_{k}
for Eq. (
IV.30
) in every time step.
Note that Eqs. (
IV.30
) and (
IV.31
) do not assume small noise, and thus are valid for any finite
T
.

Initially, we set
\phi_{n}^{j=0}=\bar{\phi}
and let it evolve with
\dot{a}=0
, until it reaches a steady state.
During the manipulation, the protocol and total heats are calculated as follows.
At each time step, we calculate the field at half time step,

\phi_{n}^{j+1/2}=\phi_{n}^{j}-\mathcal{F}^{-1}[ik\tilde{J}_{k}^{j}]_{n}\delta t/2,

(IV.32)

and
\tilde{J}_{k}^{j+1/2}
is calculated accordingly, with Eq. (
IV.30
).
The observables
\partial_{a}F
and
P
are retrieved from

\displaystyle\partial_{a}F^{j+1/2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n}\frac{(\phi_{n}^{j+1/2})^{2}}{2}\delta x

(IV.33)

and

\displaystyle P^{j+1/2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\Delta\mu}{\lambda_{\phi}}\sum_{k}\left[i\gamma k\tilde{\phi}_{k}^{j+1/2}\right]^{*}\left[\tilde{J}_{k}^{j+1/2}-i\gamma\Delta\mu k\tilde{\phi}_{k}^{j+1/2}\right].

(IV.34)

Then, the external and active works are calculated from discretized Stratonovich integrals,

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{\text{ext}}=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{\tau/\delta t}\dot{a}\big((j+1/2)\delta t\big)\partial_{a}F^{j+1/2}\delta t

(IV.35)

and

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{\text{act}}^{j^{\prime}}=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{j^{\prime}}\left[\lambda_{n}V\Delta\mu^{2}+P^{j+1/2}\right]\delta t,

(IV.36)

where the upper limit
j^{\prime}
in Eq. (
IV.36
) is chosen based on the heat one is trying to calculate.
The free energy, on the other hand, can be simply evaluated at any point with

F^{j}=\sum_{n}\left[a(j\delta t)\frac{(\phi_{n}^{j})^{2}}{2}+b\frac{(\phi_{n}^{j})^{4}}{4}\right]\delta x+\kappa\sum_{k}k^{2}\frac{|\tilde{\phi}_{k}^{j}|^{2}}{2}.

(IV.37)

Finally, the protocol and total heats, defined as fluctuating quantities in each noise realization, are obtained from

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{\text{p}}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{W}_{\text{ext}}+\mathcal{W}_{\text{act}}^{\tau/\delta t}-\left[F^{\tau/\delta t}-F^{0}\right]

(IV.38)

and

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{\text{t}}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{W}_{\text{ext}}+\mathcal{W}_{\text{act}}^{[\tau+\tau_{\text{r}}]/\delta t}-\left[F^{[\tau+\tau_{\text{r}}]/\delta t}-F^{0}\right].

(IV.39)

The heats shown in Figs. 3 and 5 of the main text are then mean values of Eqs. (
IV.38
) and (
IV.39
) over many realizations of the same protocol, i.e.,
Q_{\text{p}}=\langle\mathcal{Q}_{\text{p}}\rangle
and
Q_{\text{t}}=\langle\mathcal{Q}_{\text{t}}\rangle
.

VI
Evaluation of the spurious work

In this section, we provide the correct way of evaluating the stochastic integral present in Eq. (29) of the main text.
As mentioned before, this entails careful consideration of the Stratonovich discretization and the correlations between the noise and other factors.

Before approaching the integral itself, a discussion about the spurious drift is in order.
Within the matrix notation introduced in the main text, we can express the matrix
\bbnu
as follows.
Let
\mathbb{P}
be the square matrix where each line is an eigenvector of
\mathbb{L}
and let
\mathbb{D}
be the square diagonal matrix where each non-zero entry is the square root of an eigenvalue of
\mathbb{L}
.
Now, define
\mathbb{M}\equiv\mathbb{P}^{-1}\cdot\mathbb{D}\cdot\mathbb{P}
.
Consequently,
\mathbb{M}\cdot\mathbb{M}^{\intercal}=\mathbb{L}
, meaning that
\mathbb{M}
is essentially the “square root” of
\mathbb{L}
.
Then, defining the differential matrix operator

\frac{\delta}{\delta\mathbb{n}}\equiv\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{\nabla}\frac{\delta}{\delta\phi}\\
\frac{\delta}{\delta n}\end{pmatrix},

(VI.1)

the
u
-th element of the spurious drift matrix can be written as
[
3
]

\bbnu_{u}=\sum_{vw}\mathbb{M}_{uv}\frac{\delta}{\delta\mathbb{n}}_{w}\mathbb{M}_{wv},

(VI.2)

where the subscripts
u
,
v
and
w
run from
1
to
d+1
.
The same matrix
\mathbb{M}
can be used to redefine the noise
\mathbb{\Lambda}
into an uncorrelated form.
More specifically, if we set

\mathbb{\Lambda}({\bf r},t)\equiv\mathbb{M}({\bf r},t)\cdot\bbxi({\bf r},t)

(VI.3)

and require that

\left\langle\bbxi({\bf r},t)\bbxi^{\intercal}({\bf r}^{\prime},t^{\prime})\right\rangle=2\mathbb{1}\delta({\bf r}-{\bf r}^{\prime})\delta(t-t^{\prime}),

(VI.4)

where
\mathbb{1}
is the identity matrix, then

\left\langle\mathbb{\Lambda}({\bf r},t)\mathbb{\Lambda}^{\intercal}({\bf r}^{\prime},t^{\prime})\right\rangle=2\mathbb{L}(\phi({\bf r},t))\delta({\bf r}-{\bf r}^{\prime})\delta(t-t^{\prime})

(VI.5)

is guaranteed.
However, unlike
\mathbb{\Lambda}
, each element of the column matrix
\bbxi
is uncorrelated from one another, i.e.,
\left\langle\bbxi_{u}\bbxi_{v}\right\rangle=0
for
u\neq v
.

Now, note that the time integral of
P_{\text{spu}}
(see Eq. (30) of the main text) between arbitrary times
t_{1}
and
t_{2}
, can be written in more compact form using this matrix notation,

\left\langle\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}P_{\text{spu}}(t)\mathrm{d}t\right\rangle=T^{1/2}\frac{\Delta\mu}{\lambda_{\phi}}\left\langle\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\int_{V}\mathbb{K}^{\intercal}\cdot\left[\mathbb{M}\cdot\bbxi+T^{1/2}\bbnu\right]\,\mathrm{d}{\bf r}\mathrm{d}t\right\rangle,

(VI.6)

where we used Eq. (
VI.3
) and we defined

\mathbb{K}\equiv\begin{pmatrix}-\boldsymbol{C}\\
\lambda_{\phi}\end{pmatrix}.

(VI.7)

Here we treat stochastic time integrals involving the uncorrelated noise
\bbxi
, such as the first term inside the integral in Eq. (
VI.6
), for a generic matrix
\mathbb{K}
.
Only in the end we use the specific definition given by Eq. (
VI.7
).

Now, we follow reference
[
4
]
.
Consider the following time integral and its Stratonovich-discretized form:

\left\langle\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\int_{V}g(\phi({\bf r},t),n({\bf r},t))\bbxi_{v}({\bf r},t)\,\mathrm{d}{\bf r}\mathrm{d}t\right\rangle\equiv\left\langle\sum_{i}\int_{V}g\Big(\phi^{i+1/2}({\bf r}),n^{i+1/2}({\bf r})\Big)\bbxi_{v}^{i}({\bf r})\,\mathrm{d}{\bf r}\,\delta t^{1/2}\right\rangle,

(VI.8)

where
g
is a generic function of the fields
\phi
and
n
,
\bbxi_{v}
is the
v
-th element of the uncorrelated gaussian noise introduced in Eq. (
VI.3
),
i
indexes time steps (with size
\delta t
), and the fields on the RHS are evaluated at
i+1/2
(the mid-point between
i
and
i+1
), as dictated by the Stratonovich convention.
The noise correlations in discretized form read
\left\langle\bbxi_{u}^{i}\bbxi_{v}^{j}\right\rangle=2\delta_{uv}\delta^{ij}
and, because of this,
\bbxi({\bf r},t)\to\delta t^{-1/2}\bbxi^{i}({\bf r})
, leading to the
\delta t^{1/2}
factor in the RHS of Eq. (
VI.8
).

Because
\phi^{i+1/2}
and
\bbxi_{v}^{i}
in Eq. (
VI.8
) are not evaluated at the same point in time, it is unclear how to calculate this average as is.
For small
\delta t
, we can Taylor expand
g
as

g(\phi^{i+1/2},n^{i+1/2})\approx g(\phi^{i},n^{i})+\frac{\delta g}{\delta\mathbb{n}}^{i}\cdot\frac{1}{2}\left[T^{1/2}\mathbb{\Lambda}^{i}\delta t^{1/2}\right],

where
\frac{\delta}{\delta\mathbb{n}}
is the matrix defined in Eq. (
VI.1
) and the term inside the square brackets came from the discretized form of the dynamics (see Eqs. (
IV.30
) and (
IV.31
)), keeping only lowest order terms in
\delta t
.
Placing this in Eq. (
VI.8
) gives

\left\langle\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\int_{V}g(\phi({\bf r},t),n({\bf r},t))\bbxi_{v}({\bf r},t)\,\mathrm{d}{\bf r}\mathrm{d}t\right\rangle\approx\left\langle\sum_{i}\int_{V}g(\phi^{i},n^{i})\bbxi_{v}^{i}({\bf r})\,\mathrm{d}{\bf r}\,\delta t^{1/2}\right\rangle\\
+\left\langle\sum_{i}\int_{V}\frac{\delta g}{\delta\mathbb{n}}^{i}\cdot\frac{1}{2}\left[T^{1/2}\mathbb{\Lambda}^{i}\delta t^{1/2}\right]\bbxi_{v}^{i}({\bf r})\,\mathrm{d}{\bf r}\,\delta t^{1/2}\right\rangle,

(VI.9)

The sums in the RHS of Eq. (
VI.9
) have all its terms evaluated at the same point in time — these are known as Itô integrals.
The averages of the integrands are now calculable straightforwardly, and thus we proceed by commuting the averaging
\langle\cdot\rangle
with the sum over time steps.
In the case of the first sum,
\phi^{i}
and
\bbxi_{u}^{i}
are statistically independent, and the fact that
\langle\bbxi\rangle=0
means that this whole sum vanishes.
After using
\mathbb{\Lambda}=\mathbb{M}\cdot\bbxi
, we arrive at

\displaystyle\left\langle\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\int_{V}g(\phi({\bf r},t),n({\bf r},t))\bbxi_{v}({\bf r},t)\,\mathrm{d}{\bf r}\mathrm{d}t\right\rangle

\displaystyle\approx\sum_{i}\left\langle\int_{V}\frac{\delta g}{\delta\mathbb{n}}^{i}\cdot\frac{1}{2}\left[T^{1/2}\mathbb{\Lambda}^{i}\delta t^{1/2}\right]\bbxi_{v}^{i}\,\mathrm{d}{\bf r}\right\rangle\delta t^{1/2}

\displaystyle=T^{1/2}\sum_{i}\sum_{uw}\int_{V}\left\langle\frac{\delta g}{\delta\mathbb{n}}^{i}_{u}\mathbb{M}^{i}_{uw}\right\rangle\frac{\left\langle\bbxi_{w}^{i}\bbxi_{v}^{i}\right\rangle}{2}\,\mathrm{d}{\bf r}\delta t,

where we once again used that fields and noises are uncorrelated at same instant
i
.
Using
\left\langle\bbxi_{w}^{i}\bbxi_{v}^{i}\right\rangle=2\delta_{wv}
finally leads us to

\displaystyle\left\langle\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\int_{V}g(\phi({\bf r},t),n({\bf r},t))\bbxi_{v}({\bf r},t)\,\mathrm{d}{\bf r}\mathrm{d}t\right\rangle

\displaystyle\approx T^{1/2}\sum_{i}\sum_{u}\int_{V}\left\langle\frac{\delta g}{\delta\mathbb{n}}^{i}_{u}\mathbb{M}^{i}_{uv}\right\rangle\,\mathrm{d}{\bf r}\delta t

\displaystyle=T^{1/2}\sum_{w}\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\left\langle\int_{V}\mathbb{M}_{wv}\frac{\delta g}{\delta\mathbb{n}}_{w}\,\mathrm{d}{\bf r}\right\rangle\mathrm{d}t,

(VI.10)

where we returned to the continuum limit in the last line and renamed the dummy variable
u\to w
.
Equation (
VI.10
), which translates a stochastic Stratonovich integral into a standard Riemannian integral, is the equivalent of Eq. (46) in reference
[
4
]
.

Armed with Eq. (
VI.10
), we can now carry out the stochastic integral in Eq. (
VI.6
).
More specifically, setting
g=\sum_{u}\mathbb{K}_{u}\mathbb{M}_{uv}
and summing Eq. (
VI.10
) over
v
, we get

\displaystyle\left\langle\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\int_{V}\mathbb{K}^{\intercal}\cdot\mathbb{M}\cdot\bbxi\,\mathrm{d}{\bf r}\mathrm{d}t\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{uv}\left\langle\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\int_{V}\mathbb{K}_{u}\mathbb{M}_{uv}\bbxi_{v}\,\mathrm{d}{\bf r}\mathrm{d}t\right\rangle

\displaystyle=T^{1/2}\sum_{uvw}\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\left\langle\int_{V}\mathbb{M}_{wv}\frac{\delta}{\delta\mathbb{n}}_{w}\left[\mathbb{K}_{u}\mathbb{M}_{uv}\right]\,\mathrm{d}{\bf r}\right\rangle\mathrm{d}t.

(VI.11)

This result, together with the spurious drift integral in the RHS of Eq. (
VI.6
) and spurious drift definition in Eq. (
VI.2
), allow us to write

\displaystyle\left\langle\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}P_{\text{spu}}(t)\mathrm{d}t\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{T\Delta\mu}{\lambda_{\phi}}\sum_{uvw}\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\left\langle\int_{V}\left\{\mathbb{M}_{wv}\frac{\delta}{\delta\mathbb{n}}_{w}\left[\mathbb{K}_{u}\mathbb{M}_{uv}\right]+\mathbb{K}_{u}\mathbb{M}_{uv}\frac{\delta}{\delta\mathbb{n}}_{w}\mathbb{M}_{wv}\right\}\,\mathrm{d}{\bf r}\right\rangle\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\frac{T\Delta\mu}{\lambda_{\phi}}\sum_{uvw}\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\left\langle\int_{V}\frac{\delta}{\delta\mathbb{n}}_{w}\left[\mathbb{K}_{u}\mathbb{M}_{uv}\mathbb{M}_{wv}\right]\,\mathrm{d}{\bf r}\right\rangle\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\frac{T\Delta\mu}{\lambda_{\phi}}\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\left\langle\int_{V}\frac{\delta}{\delta\mathbb{n}}^{\intercal}\cdot\left[\mathbb{L}\cdot\mathbb{K}\right]\,\mathrm{d}{\bf r}\right\rangle\mathrm{d}t,

(VI.12)

where the last line follows from
\mathbb{L}=\mathbb{M}\cdot\mathbb{M}^{\intercal}
.
Up until here, we have not used the specific form of
\mathbb{K}
, and thus the simple form of Eq. (
VI.12
) is valid for any column matrix.
However, when we invoke the definitions of
\mathbb{L}
(see Eq. (6) of the main text) and of
\mathbb{K}
in Eq. (
VI.7
), we see that their dot product is very simple, with a single non-zero entry in its last element.
This ultimately takes us to

\left\langle\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}P_{\text{spu}}(t)\mathrm{d}t\right\rangle=\frac{T\Delta\mu}{\lambda_{\phi}}\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\left\langle\int_{V}\frac{\delta}{\delta n}\left[\lambda_{n}\lambda_{\phi}-\boldsymbol{C}^{2}\right]\,\mathrm{d}{\bf r}\right\rangle\mathrm{d}t=0.

(VI.13)

The last equality follows from the fact that
\boldsymbol{C}
does not depend on
n
.
