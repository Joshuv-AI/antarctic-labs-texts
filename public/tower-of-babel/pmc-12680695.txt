Autophagy modulates the mechanism of flow-mediated dilation upstream of telomerase
Authors: William E. Hughes, Shelby N. Hader, Kate Astbury, Lukas Brandt, Karima Ait-Aissa, David D. Gutterman, Andreas M. Beyer
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The non-canonical functions of telomerase reverse transcriptase (TERT), the catalytic subunit of telomerase play a critical role in maintaining microvascular homeostasis utilizing both human and rodent models. Previously, we have demonstrated that intact autophagic flux is necessary for the beneficial effects of TERT to maintain microvascular function and redox status in human resistance arterioles. The purpose of this investigation was to examine (1) whether loss of TERT function in vivo resulted in reductions in autophagy/mitophagy and concomitant changes in the mediator of microvascular FMD; (2) whether restoration of autophagy can reverse this pathological switch in dilator mechanism, reduce shear-induced mitochondrial H2O2 production while enhancing NO production. TERT mutant rats were generated and compared to their WT counterparts. Rats were given an autophagy activator (2% trehalose) for 28-days. Isolated mesenteric arteries were used for videomicroscopy, and aortic tissue was collected for immunoblotting. FMD and autophagic flux were measured in arteries in all groups. Loss of TERT function resulted in a switch from NOS-dependent to H2O2-dependent FMD, repressed microvascular shear-induced autophagic flux and NO production, and increased mitochondrial H2O2 production. Activation of autophagy restored NO-mediated dilation in TERT mutant rats, and enhanced shear-induced autophagic flux. We provide evidence that autophagy is necessary for the beneficial role of TERT within maintaining microvascular function, positioning this pathway as a modifiable target to maintain microvascular health by rescuing the endothelial dysfunction caused by loss of TERT signaling.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00395-025-01146-5.

Introduction

Vascular function is dependent upon adequate release of nitric oxide (NO) in endothelial cells to vascular smooth muscle in a paracrine manner to maintain vasodilation in response to shear stress (flow-mediated dilation, FMD). Our lab and others have demonstrated that microvascular function is maintained by nitric oxide (NO) in healthy adults, however, this mechanism of vasodilation switches with the onset of coronary artery disease (CAD), with hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) produced from the mitochondria predominating to maintain vasodilation [18, 19, 22]. This switch to H2O2 is associated with vascular senescence and a pro-inflammatory environment [11].

Previously, we demonstrated that the non-canonical functions of telomerase reverse transcriptase (TERT), the catalytic subunit of telomerase plays a critical role in maintaining microvascular homeostasis utilizing both human and mouse models. Primarily, TERT works independently of telomere length to modulate the redox environment within the microcirculation, holding a crucial role in the switch to mitochondria-derived H2O2 as the mechanism of vasodilation [5]. In addition, mutation of TERT in rodent models increases the susceptibility of the cardiac tissue to injury, increasing infarct size in response to angiotensin II, delaying recovery following ischemia–reperfusion injury, generation reactive oxygen species and impairs endothelial-dependent vasodilation [1, 2]. More recently, we have demonstrated that TERT works in concert with autophagy to mediate these effects microvascular effects. Indeed, intact autophagic flux is necessary for the beneficial effects of TERT to maintain microvascular function and redox status (i.e. reduce H2O2, maintain NO) in human resistance arterioles [13] and loss of TERT function can be rescued by activation of autophagy. However, these methods relied mostly on pharmacological modulation of TERT in ex vivo studies, bringing the question of whether genetic loss or inactivation of TERT in vivo would promote similar effects and have larger and systemic consequences. Furthermore, data from our lab and others have demonstrated a key role of the mitochondria being associated with this switch in microvascular function and redox status. A form of autophagy that is specific to recycling of mitochondria (mitophagy) has been demonstrated to play a role in neuronal pathologies, however, the role of mitophagy in maintenance of microvascular function is unclear.

The purpose of this investigation was to examine (1) whether loss of systemic TERT function resulted in reductions in autophagy and concomitant changes in the mediator of microvascular FMD in an in vivo/whole organism level; (2) whether restoration of autophagy can reverse this pathological switch in dilator mechanism, reduce mitochondria-derived H2O2 production and enhance shear-induced NO production; and (3) to investigate the role of mitophagy within the context of TERT mutation and autophagy activation. This was investigated using pressure myography, fluorescent microscopy, and analysis of protein expression via Western blot. We hypothesized that a mutation in TERT impairs autophagy, and more specifically mitophagy, resulting in a switch from NO to mitochondria-derived H2O2, and that activation of autophagy for 28 days would rescue the microvascular effects of TERT mutation, reverting the mechanism of dilation from H2O2 to NO.

Methods

Design of rat model of TERT deficiency

All protocols were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of the Medical College of Wisconsin and conformed to the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals published by the National Institutes of Health. TERT loss-of-function rats (TERT mutant) were maintained as an outbred strain on the Sprague Dawley (SD) background. Experimental animals were generated from heterozygote breeding pairs to avoid transgenerational telomere shortening. Wild type (WT) littermates were used when possible and otherwise substituted with SD-rats (Charles River) (15–20 weeks-old) and TERT mutant rats (15–20 weeks-old) as previously published [1, 6]. All rats were housed in groups of five (or less at their adult age), maintained at a temperature of 23 °C with 12 h light/dark cycles and fed a solid standard diet (Na+ content 1%) and water.

Tissue collection

Following euthanasia (CO2 asphyxiation) aorta, middle cerebral (MCA) and mesenteric arteries (MA) were dissected, and excess blood was rinsed off with HEPES buffer. Aorta were split into two groups for measurement of autophagic flux (± autophagy inhibition with 10 nM bafilomycin A1, BAFA1; Sigma-Aldrich, Catalog #B1793). MCAs or 3rd order mesenteric arteries were dissected for measurement of vascular function.

Videomicroscopy

Isolated mesenteric (180.7 ± 48 µm; range 97.2–290.0 µm) and MCA (213.9 ± 61.0 µm; range: 124.0–318.0 µm) were cannulated at both ends with glass micropipettes in an organ chamber filled with physiological saline solution, pressurized to 60 mmHg and pre-constricted with norepinephrine (NE, 1 mM; Sigma-Aldrich,) or developed spontaneous myogenic tone (MCA) to achieve a stable 30–50% reduction in passive diameter [7]. Flow through the cannulated arteries was achieved by changing the height of each reservoir in equal amounts in opposite directions without changes in intraluminal pressure. In response to flow, internal diameters were measured using on-screen calibrated calipers after 5 min at a given pressure gradient. Data are reported as % change in diameter at a given pressure gradient. Two flow-response curves were generated; first with vehicle, then a second with inhibitor(s), including L-NAME (100 µM; NOS inhibition; Cayman Chemical Catalog #80210) or PEG-catalase (500U/mL; scavenger of H2O2; Quanta BioDesign, Catalog #22501 or Sigma-Aldrich; Catalog #C4963). At the end of each experiment papaverine (100 µM) was added to assess vasodilator capacity/smooth muscle function [7, 14]. In addition, dose–response curves to the endothelial-dependent agonist, acetylcholine (10−9–10−4, Sigma-Aldrich, Catalog #A6625), were also performed. All vehicles, inhibitors, or pharmacological agents were administered by addition to the organ chamber containing the pressurized arteriole.

Autophagy activation intervention

Previous studies have demonstrated that the addition of trehalose, a non-reducing disaccharide in drinking water (2%) is sufficient to activate autophagy within the cardiovascular system [8, 15, 16, 21]. Trehalose (2%; Trehalose Dihydrate, Research Products International, Catalog # T82000) was added to normal drinking water for 28 days for rats to consume ad libitum. Water was changed daily. Age-matched littermate control rats consumed normal drinking water. Experimental and control animals did not differ in the volume of water consumed.

Western blot analysis

Western blot analysis was performed on lysates from aortas in RIPA buffer (Sigma-Aldrich, Catalog # 89900) with 1 × phosphatase/protease inhibitor cocktail. Analysis on lysates from mesenteric vessels used TPER buffer (ThermoFisher, Catalog #78510) with 1 × phosphatase and protease inhibitor cocktails (Roche cOmplete Mini Protease inhibitor cocktail tablet (#11836153001), Sigma Phosphatase Inhibitor cocktail 2 (#P5726), and Sigma Phosphatase Inhibitor cocktail 3 (#P0044). Protein concentration determined by BCA assay. 20–50 µg of protein were loaded into BioRad precast criterion gel 4–20% tris–HCl (p62, GAPDH), BioRad precast criterion 4–20% TGX gel (LC3B), and BioRad Criterion TGX Stain Free Precast Midi gels, 4–15% (p-eNOS, eNOS, B-Actin). For aorta lysates, after separation by electrophoresis (90 V for 30 min, then 160 V for 70 min), proteins were transferred to nitrocellulose membranes using 7:2:1 v/v/v Water/Methanol/tris–glycine (100 V for 70 min) using a cold tank transfer. For mesenteric artery lysates, after separation by electrophoresis (150 V for 70 min), proteins were transferred to PVDF membranes included in the Trans-Blot Turbo Transfer Pack using BioRad's Trans-Blot Turbo System with the 7 min mixed MW program. Membranes were washed with TBS-T (TBS + 0.1% Tween) and then blocked 5% non-fat milk (GAPDH and p62) or 5% BSA (LC3B), for 1 h or BioRad EveryBlot blocking buffer for 30 min at room temperature. After blocking, membranes were quickly rinsed 3 times using TBS-T. Primary antibodies were diluted in 5% IgG-free BSA (1:250 p-eNOSS1177, BD Biosciences #612392, 1:1000 eNOS, Cell Signaling #32027, 1:1000 β-actin, Abcam ab-8227, 1:1000, LC3B; Cell Signaling Technologies #2775S; p62/SQSTM1, 1:1000 Abnova; GAPDH, 1:20000; Abcam ab8245; Pink1 Abcam ab23707; Parkin Cell Signaling #2132; TFEB Bethyl Laboratories A303-673; TERT Bioss bs-1019R-FR) at 4 °C overnight. The following day, membranes were rinsed 3 times in TBS-T for 5–8 min, and then secondary antibodies were added (5% BSA, 1:80000 GAPDH; 1:2500 LC3B; 1:5000 p62; Millipore goat anti-rabbit or anti-mouse IgG-HRP). Clarity (BioRad) substrate was then applied to membranes (GAPDH and p62). The Clarity substrate was spiked with SuperSignal West Femto (ThermoFisher, 2% of the volume of the Clarity substrate). Bands were quantified using ImageJ. All proteins were normalized to housekeeping proteins (GAPDH, β-actin).

Measurement of autophagy within mesenteric arteries

LysoTracker Red DND-99 (ThermoFisher Scientific, Catalog # L7528; 50 nM) was used to quantify shear-induced autophagic flux in the presence and absence of lysosome inhibitor (10 nM; BAFA1 Sigma-Aldrich, Catalog #B1793) [13]. Images were captured every 15 min for 30 min using a fluorescent microscope (model Olympus IX73 with X-cite FIRE LED lamp).

Flow-induced mitochondria hydrogen peroxide and nitric oxide production

Mesenteric arteries were cannulated in HEPES buffer (pH: 7.4, 37 °C). Following pressurization, baseline images were captured using fluorescence microscopy. Mitochondrial H2O2 production was quantified using the fluorescent probe Mito Peroxy Yellow 1 (MitoPY1, Tocris Bioscience, Catalog # 4428; 10 μM, 1 h). NO production was quantified using the NO Detection Kit (Enzo Life Sciences; ENZ-51013-200). MitoPY1 and NO Detection Kit were added prior to pressurization to HEPES buffer-filled reservoirs so the probes were perfused intraluminal. Flow was induced across the arteries by moving the reservoirs in equal but opposite directions (100 cmH2O). Images were captured at baseline and 5 min of flow using fluorescence microscopy (model Olympus IX73 with X-cite FIRE LED lamp). The fold increase in fluorescence intensity from static values to 5 min of max flow was compared between groups using ImageJ [13].

Statistical analysis

All data are expressed as mean ± SD. Normality of data was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test, and non-parametric analysis was performed when necessary FMD is expressed as a percentage (%) of maximal dilation. 2-way repeated measures ANOVA was used with pressure gradient and intervention as parameters to establish statistical significance for vascular function studies. When a significant difference was observed between control and inhibitor curves, responses at individual concentrations/pressure gradients were compared using a Tukey post hoc analysis. For fluorescent analysis of H2O2 or NO, the % increase from static was compared between groups (e.g. WT vs. TERT mutant) using unpaired t-tests. Within groups (e.g. 5 min vs. static), data were compared using paired t-tests. Changes in protein expression were compared by one-way ANOVA across groups. Potential outliers were analyzed using ROUT analysis (Q = 1%). Analyses were performed using Graphpad Prism. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.

Discussion

This study sought to further determine the crosstalk between TERT and autophagy on microvascular function using a genetic rodent mutant model. There are a number of salient findings from these experiments. Primarily, genetic mutation of TERT results in a switch in the mechanism of microvascular vasodilation from NOS to H2O2, similar to that observed in arterioles from patients with CAD. This switch in vasodilator mechanism is paralleled by an increase in mitoPY1 fluorescent signal, a reduction in shear-induced NO production, and a reduction in shear-induced autophagic flux relative to WT rats. Importantly, activation of autophagy with the natural disaccharide, trehalose, for 28 days reversed the switch in dilator mechanism, abolished differences in both shear-induced H2O2 and NO production, in addition to differences in shear-induced autophagy. Taken together, these findings highlight the crucial independent and synergistic roles of autophagy and TERT in maintaining healthy microvascular endothelial function.

Role of TERT and autophagy in microvasculature

Our lab has previously established the important role that TERT holds in maintaining microvascular function in both animal and isolated human vessels. We have previously established that genetic loss of the catalytic subunit of telomerase (TERT), but not the RNA component (TERC) is associated with a pathological switch in the mechanism of vasodilation in a murine model [2]. Pharmacological inhibition of TERT in otherwise healthy isolated human adipose and coronary resistance arterioles [5] results in a pathological switch in the mechanism of microvascular vasodilation to shear stress from NO to H2O2, similar to what is observed in adipose resistance and atrial arterioles from patients with CAD. On the other hand, pharmacological activation of TERT rescues NO-mediated vasodilation in patients with CAD [5]. Additionally, genetic inactivation of TERT in a murine model is associated with a greater formation of ROS, exacerbation of microvascular dysfunction, and larger infarct sizes in response to angiotensin-II relative to WT or matched littermates that lack the RNA component of TERT [1, 2], while the location of TERT is important in mediating this response as mitochondrial translocation of TERT suppresses ROS formation and loss of mitochondrial TERT results in a significant reduction of ATP production [1, 4, 5]. In this context, TERT is positioned as a crucial regulator of microvascular and mitochondrial health.

Previously, we demonstrated that microvascular function in isolated human resistance arterioles is in part regulated by a crosstalk between autophagy and telomerase [13]. This was demonstrated by manipulating both autophagy and telomerase independently and in conjunction with one another. In the context of CAD, both autophagy and telomerase are reduced, resulting in a microvascular phenotype wherein H2O2 predominates as the primary mechanism of vasodilation to shear stress. This was corroborated with a reduction in shear-induced autophagic flux [13]. Within the current study, the pathological phenotype typically observed with CAD was mimicked using a rodent model of TERT inactivation. This was paralleled by an overall reduction in TERT mutant rats of the fluorescent signal of Lysotracker Red DND-99, used to track autophagy in response to shear stress. This is taken as evidence of a reduction in autophagic flux within TERT mutant rats. Furthermore, in agreement with our previous work, we observed an increase in mitochondrial H2O2 production in response to shear stress, and a significant reduction in shear-induced NO production in TERT mutant rats relative to WT. These findings were corroborated by a reduced protein expression of eNOS within mesenteric arteries from TERT Mutant rats. This is taken as further evidence that TERT mutation/inactivation impairs autophagic flux, and in turn, NOS signaling within the microvasculature, impacting functional responses to shear stress. Interestingly, when separated by sex, there were no differences in shear-induced mitochondrial H2O2 or NO production.

Similar to our previous work in isolated lungs of WT and TERT mutant rats [6], there were no differences in markers of autophagy or mitophagy (LC3B, p62, Beclin 1, Pink 1, Parkin) within isolated aortas. However, within the current study we specifically measured autophagic flux, or the rate of autophagy via the addition of BAFA1 to aortas (protein expression). This is in contrast to the Lysotracker Red DND-99 findings within the mesenteric arteries and can be taken as evidence that while there are no differences between groups in static conditions, TERT mutation impacts the resiliency or responsiveness of the vasculature to respond to dynamic conditions (e.g. shear stress).

TERT deficiency FMD phenotype is ameliorated by activation of autophagy

Given that inactivation of TERT resulted in a switch from NO to H2O2, and impaired shear-induced autophagic flux, we hypothesized that activation of autophagy would rescue this phenotype. Previous evidence in both mice and humans has demonstrated that activation of autophagy with the natural disaccharide trehalose ameliorates age-associated vascular dysfunction [15, 16]. It is important to note that trehalose does have caloric content (~ 16 kJ/g). In the context of previous human studies examining vascular function, the beneficial vascular effects were dependent upon the amount of weight gained across the supplementation period, as subjects ingested 100 g of trehalose per day, while maintaining their regular diet [15]. This resulted in an excess of approximately 400 kcal per day over the course of 12 weeks, and the beneficial effects on vascular function were isolated to those subjects who gained no more than 2.3 kg over the course of 12 weeks. The amount given within the current study (2% w/v, 10 g in 500 mL) was well below what previous human studies have administered but is in line with previous animal studies [8, 16, 21, 24]. The novel finding of the current study is that activation of autophagy with trehalose rescued NO-mediated microvascular FMD despite a genetic inactivation of TERT. Furthermore, activation of autophagy with trehalose did not impart negative effects in WT rats, as NO-mediated vasodilation was maintained. Interestingly, and in contrast to our hypothesis, activation of autophagy did not alter mitochondrial H2O2 production or NO production in response to shear stress. Taken together, autophagy regulates vasomotor responses to shear stress and can improve pathological phenotypes as a result of TERT deficiency.

Limitations and experimental considerations

There are several experimental considerations that warrant discussion. First, as mentioned previously, the genetic inactivation of TERT was not a total abolition. Instead, the protein is still apparent while telomerase activity as measured by the TRAP assay is significantly reduced below background. Second, it was not within the scope of the study to determine the mechanism by which trehalose activates autophagy, however, strong evidence from other laboratories has indicated that trehalose supplementation results in nuclear translocation of Transcription Factor EB, which is the master regulator of transcription of autophagy-related genes and lysosomal function [9, 10, 20, 23]. Third, the present experiments focused on isolated resistance arteries, they were performed in a systemic TERT loss-of-function model combined with chronic in vivo autophagy modulation. The switch from NO to H₂O₂ as the mediator of FMD recapitulates what is observed in patients with coronary artery disease, underscoring translational relevance [13]. Moreover, previous studies have established in vivo consequences of TERT deficiency or enhancement in cardiovascular disease. For example, telomerase deficiency predisposes to larger infarcts and impaired recovery after ischemia–reperfusion injury [1, 3], while mitochondrial TERT confers cardioprotection by improving complex I activity and reducing infarct size [4]. Take together, these reinforce the physiological importance of TERT in cardiovascular pathology. Nonetheless, we acknowledge that our study did not directly assess outcomes in atherosclerosis or myocardial infarction models, and future work is needed to determine whether autophagy activation can translate into protection in these disease contexts. Finally, while both male and female rats were included, the study was not designed or powered to detect sex-based differences in microvascular responses. The observed effect sizes for mitochondrial H₂O₂ (MitoPY1) and nitric oxide (NO) fluorescence were 0.45 and 0.73, respectively. Achieving 80% statistical power at α = 0.05 would require approximately 158 and 62 animals for these outcomes, which was beyond the ethical and logistical scope of the present investigation. Accordingly, analyses stratified by sex are presented as exploratory and should be interpreted with caution. Importantly, the primary outcomes regarding the mechanism of flow-mediated dilation and the effects of autophagy activation were adequately powered to test the central hypothesis of the study.

Perspectives

This study demonstrated the important role of autophagy and telomerase in maintaining microvascular function. Primarily, genetic inactivation of TERT is associated with a pathological switch in the mechanism of microvascular vasodilation and reduced production of NO to shear stress, while activation of autophagy rescues these detriments. These findings align with recent reviews highlighting the central role of microvascular dysfunction and redox imbalance in the pathogenesis and progression of heart failure [12, 17]. Taken together, these results, along with our previous human data strongly support a governing role for autophagy in microvascular function in health and disease.
