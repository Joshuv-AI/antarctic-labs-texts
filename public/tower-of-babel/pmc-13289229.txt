Integration of traditional medicine services into public health centers in Indonesia: a qualitative study
Authors: Sri Mumpuni Yuniarsih, Retna Siwi Padmawati, Ema Madyaningrum, Siwi Sri Widhowati, Yodi Mahendradhata
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMany people are still using traditional medicine in various countries. This is a challenge for governments because they must effectively manage services, maintain quality, and protect patients. Integrating traditional medicine into public health centers provides safe and quality services. This study aimed to explore the perceptions and experiences of traditional medicine users and program implementers in Indonesian public health centers.MethodsThis study used an exploratory qualitative research design. Data were collected from program implementers and traditional medicine users. Data were collected through focus group discussions and in-depth interviews from March to June 2023. The study had 16 participants which included 10 implementers in focus group discussions and six users in in-depth interviews. Analysis using an inductive approach was employed.ResultsThree key themes emerged: user preferences, success factors for integration, and challenges for improvement. Users preferred traditional medicine at public health centers over traditional medicine practitioners due to trust, accessibility, and therapeutic effects. Implementers highlighted the importance of human resource readiness and community support for successful integration, despite obstacles like limited training and resources.ConclusionThe integration of traditional medicine services into public health centers is essential to provide easy access and preserve traditional health services that are in line with local wisdom, effective, and safe.Keywords: Integrated medicine, Primary care, Qualitative, Traditional medicine, Public health center

Background

Traditional medicine (TM) has been a vital part of healthcare globally, particularly in regions where it is deeply embedded in cultural practices [1–3]. The Alma-Ata Declaration of 1978 formally recognized the potential of traditional medicine in primary health care and called for its integration to achieve equitable and accessible health services [4]. In recent decades, this integration has gained traction as part of efforts to achieve Universal Health Coverage (UHC), particularly in Asia and the Western Pacific, where TM is a fundamental component of healthcare for many populations [5].

Countries such as China, South Korea, and Taiwan have developed various models for integrating TM within modern healthcare. In China, traditional Chinese medicine has been comprehensively integrated into the national healthcare system through strong regulatory frameworks, practitioner education, and inclusion in national health insurance [6]. South Korea operates a dual system where traditional Korean medicine and modern medical practices coexist independently, while Taiwan permits modern healthcare providers to provide traditional therapies such as acupuncture, reflecting another pathway toward integration [7, 8].

In Indonesia, traditional medicine holds significant cultural and healthcare importance. Practices such as Jamu (herbal medicine), manual therapies like cupping and acupuncture, and spiritual healing have been integral to community health for generations [9–15]. Despite the increasing availability of modern medical services under the national health insurance program, TM remains a preferred choice for many Indonesians, with nearly 70% of households reporting its use [16]. However, TM services in Indonesia are often self-funded and operate outside the formal healthcare system due to gaps in regulation, limited modern healthcare providers’ training in TM, and the exclusion of TM from national health insurance coverage [3, 17, 18].

The Indonesian Ministry of Health regulates the provision of TM, focusing on four main activities: [1] registration and licensing of traditional medicine practitioners (laypeople who practice traditional healing, either through inherited skills or short-term training) [2], community empowerments in developing traditional medicine [3], the establishment of green open spaces, and [4] providing TM services in healthcare facilities, delivered by traditional healthcare providers. However, the delivery of TM services in healthcare facilities is still suboptimal. The challenges include limited policy understanding, resource constraints, lack of traditional healthcare providers, and regulatory gaps, as evidenced by the experiences of Halmahera Public Health Center in Semarang and Made Public Health Center in Surabaya [19–23]. The number of graduates in traditional healthcare compared to modern healthcare graduates is 0.01% (63 out of 334,194) [24]. The lack of government regulations on the recruitment of traditional health providers to public health centers makes it difficult to speed up the integration of traditional medicine [25].

A pilot project integrating traditional medicine services into public health centers has been developed in Boyolali, Indonesia, which offering acupressure and cupping as part of the services delivered by modern healthcare providers who are trained in TM services. This project demonstrates the success of the Ministry of Health’s program from 2020 to 2024 [26]. Although this integration shows potential, challenges remain in improving access and service quality. User experience and satisfaction are crucial factors in evaluating program improvement [27, 28]. This study aimed to explore user experiences with traditional medicine services at a public health center and to investigate the perceptions of program implementers regarding the integration of these services, providing valuable insight for further improvements and development.

Methods

Study design and context

An exploratory qualitative research design was used because the experience and perception of the integration of traditional medicine services into public health centers has not been previously explored and is a novel insight that has not been clearly understood [29]. This research was conducted at the Public Health Centers in Boyolali Regency from March to June 2023. Boyolali is the largest milk and beef producer on the island of Java. Boyolali is also listed as a production center for raw materials for the traditional herbal medicine industry. Several areas in Boyolali have ginger cultivation and traditional herbal medicine processing industries. The integration of traditional medicine services at a public health center in Boyolali is a model considered successful by the Indonesian Ministry of Health. Boyolali Regency has become an internationally achieving regency and its health campaign innovation received appreciation from the International Healthcare Innovation Award in 2020.

Data were collected through a focus group discussion (FGD) and in-depth interviews (IDIs). The researcher utilized the COREQ Checklist to ensure comprehensive reporting of qualitative research [30]. FGD was used to identify the need for the integration of traditional medicine services into public health centers based on the perceptions of traditional medicine program implementers. IDIs were used to explore the experiences of users who received traditional medicine services at the public health center.

Samples and sampling procedure

According to the inclusion criteria for FGD participants involved in the provision of traditional medicine services at public health centers—such as active involvement in services, at least one year of experience, relevant roles, adequate knowledge, consent, and availability—a purposive sampling technique was used to select participants. Ten participants were involved in the FGD, consisting of program managers (n = 7), and modern healthcare providers (n = 3). The program manager is responsible for planning, organizing, and overseeing the implementation of the program, ensuring that all components align with the established objectives, budget, and timeline. In this context, modern healthcare providers are healthcare professionals who have received specialized training in traditional health services, such as herbal medicine, acupressure, massage, and cupping. They are tasked with delivering these services to patients as part of their role.

The inclusion criteria for IDIs were patients who had used traditional medicine services at the public health center at least once. The users were randomly selected from data available at the public health center by the researcher and then invited to participate in an interview. The researcher and participants did not have any personal relationship; the participants could freely express their opinions and feelings according to the actual conditions. After receiving an explanation of the study’s purpose, process, and objectives, all research participants gave their written consent.

Data collection

FGD and IDI guidelines were used as the data collection instruments. All FGDs and interviews were audio-visually recorded. The first stage of data collection was to conduct face-to-face FGDs. The researcher started the FGD by asking some trigger questions regarding the participants’ opinions about the integration of traditional medicine services into public health centers, barriers, and success factors. The FGD was led by a researcher with expertise and research interest in traditional medicine and lasted for 90 min. The FGD was conducted through informal interactions and given sufficient time to encourage the participants of the FGD to feel comfortable, allowing them to freely express their opinions. The researcher was also assisted by an assistant researcher to make field notes. These field notes were used as a cross-correction of the audiovisual transcripts. The second stage of the data collection was to conduct IDIs with users of traditional medicine services. As the interviewer, the researcher was trained in qualitative data collection and is experienced in conducting interviews. The researchers collected data via WhatsApp video calls that lasted approximately 45 min each. The interview guide was developed through an iterative process. Four questions explored the patient’s experience in using traditional medicine services in public health centers. These questions include positive or negative experiences, patient satisfaction, and the necessity and future expectation of the integration of traditional medicine services into public health centers (Table 2). Data collection through IDIs was conducted with six participants, with analysis running after each interview. Saturation was reached after the fourth interview and two additional interviews were conducted. However, as no new themes emerged, it was determined that sufficient data had been collected and data collection was concluded. Interviews may be repeated with participants who need clarification of data. However, in this study, no interview was repeated.

Data analysis

The data were transcribed and then analyzed inductively using thematic analysis. Two researchers (principal investigator and independent coder) read the transcripts to gain a better understanding. Important ideas were manually processed into codes, categories, and themes using Microsoft Excel. Data coding was conducted by the principal investigator and validated by an independent coder. If coding, determining categories, or themes were different, a joint discussion was held, and the principal investigator provided a more detailed explanation of the phenomenon or condition according to the context during the interview process and research context. After the discussion, the principal investigator and independent coder made an agreed decision. If both could not find an agreement, then an agreement was made by involving the supervisor in the discussion. After identifying the themes, the principal investigator conducted face-to-face member-checking with all participants, communicating whether the data findings were intended by the participants. All participants agreed with the findings. After completing all these processes, the final analysis was performed.

Trustworthiness

The researchers tested the credibility of the data by building a relationship and mutual trust with the participants; explaining the research objectives, informed consent, and importance of answering the interview questions as objectively as possible; and conveying that all the information obtained was kept confidential and used only for research. Interviews were conducted via WhatsApp Video Call to ensure good call quality, and the researchers told the participants to prepare a special time for the interview activities, prepare an Internet connection, and be in a comfortable and quiet place. The researchers also conducted face-to-face member-checking with all participants. Transferability criteria were met by selecting the participants according to the context and research topic. Dependability criteria were met through intensive discussions on the findings and data analysis with supervisors. In addition, confirmability criteria were met through peer review with expert researchers or a literature review.

As illustrated in Table 4, the primary theme of user preferences is characterised by an inclination towards traditional medicine, which is shaped by three underlying subthemes: individual beliefs, trust in healthcare, and therapeutic effects. With regard to the subtheme of individual beliefs, participants indicated a preference for natural remedies over modern medicine, citing concerns about potential side effects. This preference was exemplified by the following statement: “I don’t like medicine, so I prefer acupressure” (P16/IDI). The subtheme of trust in healthcare encompassed the significance of certified healthcare providers, sterilization of tools, and affordable services. Participants highlighted the accessibility and cost-effectiveness of treatment at community health centers (P13/IDI). Finally, the therapeutic effects subtheme emphasized the user-perceived benefits of traditional therapies, such as pain relief and improved health. Participants shared experiences, including the following: ‘After cupping, my body feels very light’ (P12/IDI). Collectively, these subthemes illustrate the factors that shape users’ preferences towards traditional health practices.

Table 5 outlines the key themes and subthemes related to the success factors for integration of traditional medicine into public health services. The central theme, success factors for integration, is explored through two subthemes: benefit of integration and success factors.

Under the subtheme benefit of integration, participants highlighted several advantages of integrating traditional medicine, such as the availability of alternative treatments and the opportunity to protect local wisdom. For example, one participant (P11/IDI) emphasized the importance of offering alternative treatments to encourage more visits to public health centers. Another participant (P2/FGD) pointed out that providing traditional medicine is vital for preserving cultural practices. Additionally, service innovation in public health centers was noted as a significant benefit, with services like cupping and acupressure attracting more patients (P12/IDI). However, challenges such as insufficient staffing, as mentioned by P13/IDI, were also acknowledged.

The second subtheme, success factors, identifies the key elements contributing to the effective integration of traditional medicine. These include funding and mentoring programs for traditional medicine services, regional regulations that support traditional health services, and the presence of good infrastructure. Participants emphasized that support from local governments, such as funding and licensing programs, played a critical role in ensuring the safety and legitimacy of traditional medicine (P1/FGD). Moreover, the increasing demand for herbal medicine was highlighted, with one participant (P3/FGD) noting the success of herbal treatments in gaining widespread popularity. The establishment of legal frameworks, such as regulations from the Ministry of Health and local governments, was also crucial in legitimizing traditional medicine in public health centers (P7/FGD). In addition, the establishment of associations of traditional medicine practitioners helped to promote knowledge sharing and strengthen links with public health centers, contributing to the successful integration and acceptance of traditional health services in PHCs by traditional medicine practitioners (P7/FGD).

Table 6 outlines the challenges and the need for improvement in the integration of traditional medicine into public health services. The central theme, challenges, and need for improvement of existing integration, are examined through two subthemes: barriers and program improvement efforts.

Under the sub-theme of barriers, several issues hinder the full integration of traditional medicine. Competition between public health centers and traditional medicine practitioners is a significant challenge (P6/FGD). Additionally, traditional medicine services are not yet fully covered by health insurance schemes, limiting accessibility (P2/FGD). There are also concerns regarding human resource capacity and qualifications, with only a few trained modern healthcare providers in traditional medicine available (P4/FGD). Time constraints for delivering services, such as acupressure, which requires significant time per patient, also contribute to delays and inefficiencies (P8/FGD, P13/IDI).

The subtheme program improvement efforts focus on strategies to address these challenges. Key improvements suggested include integrating traditional medicine into all public health centers to increase accessibility (P4/FGD), providing ready-to-use traditional medicines to simplify consumption (P13/IDI), and offering traditional medicine training for modern healthcare providers to expand expertise (P4/FGD). Participants also emphasized the need for better socialization of traditional medicine services to the public (P12/IDI, P7/FGD), including educational campaigns to raise awareness of the benefits of traditional medicine practices (P7/FGD, P16/IDI). Additionally, promoting a healthy lifestyle through proper diet, stress management, and sleep patterns, alongside optimized media for program socialization, were proposed as essential steps for improving the integration and effectiveness of traditional medicine services (P11/FGD, P15/FGD).

Discussion

This study explored the integration of traditional medicine services into public health centers, uncovering several key themes: user preferences, factors contributing to successful integration, and challenges to further improvement. The integration of TM was facilitated through a TM clinic, alongside other services such as maternal and child health, dental health, and general medicine clinics.

TM in Indonesia has rapidly developed, particularly after the issuance of a new omnibus health law (Law No. 17 of 2023). Initially focused on promotive and preventive care, it now also includes curative treatments. Traditional healthcare providers are now authorized to provide services at public health centers [31]. A traditional healthcare provider is any person who has completed higher education in the field of traditional medicine, including skills and herbs, both domestic and foreign graduates and is recognized by the government following the provisions of laws and regulations [32]. However, the number of traditional healthcare providers is still limited to about 447 (3%) out of a total of 1,499,959 healthcare providers in Indonesia [33]. In their absence, modern healthcare providers are still allowed to provide traditional health services. These modern healthcare providers are trained by the Ministry of Health with a specific curriculum covering standardized and safe therapeutic modalities in herbal medicine, acupressure, and massage [34–37]. This study revealed that the integration of traditional medicine into public health centers has various benefits, such as the availability of alternative treatments, service innovation in public health centers, and an increase in the number of traditional medicine users. The integration of traditional medicine offers an alternative for individuals who avoid chemical drugs due to side effects or are seeking a more natural healthcare option. The use of traditional medicine as the sole treatment in Indonesia is still quite high [38]. Another study in Indonesia on health-seeking behavior found that, in the treatment process, most people prefer to combine modern medicine with traditional medicine [39].

One of the primary reasons for user preference is the safety and competency of service providers. The integration of traditional medicine into public health centers ensures that services are regulated, standardized, and provided in a safe manner. This includes the guarantee of sterilized equipment, which is a critical safety concern for users. Although cupping is widely practiced in Indonesia, it carries potential risks, including mild discomfort, pain, chills, itching, and, in rare cases, infections such as hepatitis and HIV/AIDS [40, 41]. Caution is essential to ensure patient safety and well-being, as stated in the regulations on traditional healthcare services, any traditional healing practices that involve invasive procedures must be carried out by modern healthcare providers [42]. Studies from other countries such as Burkina Faso and Korea, documented adverse events related to traditional medicine practices [43, 44]. The high prevalence of traditional medicine use in Burkina Faso (85%) reflects its integral role in healthcare, yet 14.7% of users reported adverse events, mainly gastrointestinal (57.7%), nervous system (15.2%), and skin disorders (7%). In Korea, 9,624 adverse event records linked to traditional medicines were identified, with 522 related to herbal remedies. Liver issues were the most common adverse events, and only eight cases were recorded by the Food and Drug Administration’s pharmacovigilance system. Additionally, 1,389 events were related to physiotherapy (285 cases) or acupuncture/moxibustion (1,104 cases), primarily involving infections, pneumothorax, and burns. Pharmacovigilance, which involves the detection, assessment, and prevention of adverse effects, is crucial for ensuring the safe integration of traditional medicine within public health centers [45].

In Indonesia, TM is a cultural heritage that reflects local wisdom. Practices such as using plants for medicinal purposes are deeply ingrained in local communities and are part of the country’s ethnomedicine [46]. The Sundanese people, for example, use a variety of plants such as cat’s whiskers for kidney issues and cucumber for hypertension [47]. Public health centers are uniquely positioned to offer services that are both effective and culturally relevant. Despite the rapid advancements of modern medicine, many people continue to rely on traditional medicine due to its alignment with cultural practices and personal preferences [2, 16, 48]. These centers must offer affordable and culturally appropriate services to ensure that individuals feel comfortable choosing the treatment that suits their needs and preferences.

User satisfaction plays a critical role in the success of healthcare services. The study found high levels of satisfaction with the delivery of traditional medicine services in public health centers. Patients expressed positive feedback about the service providers and the outcomes of treatment. This aligns with research suggesting that patient satisfaction is closely linked to organizational performance and the overall quality of healthcare [49, 50].

However, challenges remain. The lack of comprehensive government policies, financial limitations, and insufficient training for healthcare providers in TM are significant barriers [51]. Furthermore, the availability of traditional healthcare providers remains limited, necessitating the training of modern healthcare providers in traditional medicine practices, such as acupressure and herbal remedies. This is essential to ensure continuity of care and provide culturally competent services to patients [52].

This study faced several limitations, including a restricted range of experiences due to purposive sampling, as only participants with at least one experience using traditional medicine services were selected. This may exclude valuable insights from individuals who have never used these services. Additionally, purposive sampling can introduce selection bias, narrowing the scope of perspectives and potentially limiting the representativeness of the findings. Future studies should explore this experience in greater depth, particularly by targeting populations who have not utilized traditional medicine services. This would provide insight into why some individuals avoid these services, which could inform the development of more effective integration programs. Another limitation of this study is the lack of comparison between the outcomes and services provided by modern healthcare providers offering traditional medicine (TM) and those provided by traditional healthcare providers. While this study focuses on TM services offered by modern healthcare providers, it does not explore how these services compare to those provided by traditional healers. Future research could address this gap by comparing the outcomes and quality of TM services between modern and traditional healthcare providers to gain a more comprehensive understanding of the effectiveness and patient satisfaction with each approach.

Conclusions

The integration of traditional medicine into public health centers in Indonesia has the potential to contribute to safer and better-quality healthcare services while preserving local wisdom. Based on the findings, several users of traditional healthcare services at the public health center cited trust in the services, ease of access, and perceived therapeutic effects as key reasons for their choice. While these factors were frequently mentioned, individual experiences and preferences may vary. Therefore, these findings provide insights into the use of traditional healthcare services, although further research is needed to explore other influencing factors. The success factors for integration include government funding, strong demand for herbal medicine, supportive regulations, and positive collaboration between traditional medicine practitioners and public health centers. However, challenges remain, including the lack of insurance coverage and limited human resources, which pose obstacles to the integration of traditional medicine.

The findings suggest that strengthening training for modern healthcare providers in traditional medicine, expanding service coverage, and increasing public awareness could be strategic steps to address these challenges. This integration program could serve as a potential model for broader implementation at the national level, contributing to more inclusive and sustainable healthcare in line with the goals of the National Health Insurance and the achievement of Universal Health Coverage (UHC).
