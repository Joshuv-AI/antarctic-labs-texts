Maintenance intravenous immunoglobulin in chronic inflammatory demyelinating polyneuropathy: trends from an Australian tertiary centre
Authors: Kristina T Pidd, Anthony Khoo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChronic inflammatory demyelinating polyneuropathy (CIDP) is commonly treated with long‐term intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIg). Individual dosing requirements and duration of IVIg differ among patients, leading to significant heterogeneity in IVIg utilisation.AimsWe aimed to report on IVIg treatment trends in CIDP patients at our centre and sought to determine whether any clinical or demographic factors were predictive of a successful treatment wean.MethodsThis retrospective observational study included adults receiving maintenance IVIg for CIDP over a 5‐year period across a hospital network in South Australia. Overall, IVIg maintenance requirements were described across the cohort. Patient characteristics, investigations and IVIg usage were recorded. These factors were compared between those who had successfully weaned IVIg and those who had not.ResultsTwenty‐two patients were included, with 82% meeting European Academy of Neurology/Peripheral Nerve Society (EAN/PNS) criteria for definite CIDP. Seventeen had typical CIDP, with a mean age of 58 and a male‐to‐female ratio of 2:1. Average body mass index was 30 kg/m 2 . Mean cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) protein was 0.8 g/L. Mean maintenance IVIg was 0.6 g/kg every 4 weeks, with a mean duration of 64 months (range 3–152). Five out of six who had an IVIg wean successfully withdrew from IVIg completely. Exploratory analysis was limited by a small sample size; however, no significant association between clinical factors and successful IVIg wean were identified.ConclusionClinical stability was achieved for most patients on ≤0.6 g/kg/4 weeks of IVIg. Many individuals did not undergo an IVIg wean, highlighting the need for more proactive consideration of dose reduction or treatment cessation trials.Keywords: CIDP, IVIg, maintenance therapy, autoimmune neuropathy, peripheral neuropathy

Introduction

Chronic inflammatory demyelinating polyneuropathy (CIDP) is a rare autoimmune disease which targets the myelin sheath of peripheral nerves. It has a low mortality rate of 3.3%, but long‐term disability is common, affecting approximately half of all CIDP patients. 1

The diagnosis of CIDP is primarily clinical and relies on a concordant history, physical examination and abnormal nerve conduction studies (NCS). 2 Additional supportive findings include raised protein in cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), thickening and enhancement of nerve roots on imaging and, rarely, nerve biopsy. 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 Once CIDP has been diagnosed, management usually involves one of three first‐line treatments: corticosteroids, plasma exchange or intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIg). The vast majority of individuals with CIDP receive long‐term treatment with IVIg infusions at maintenance doses, which are effective in limiting disability progression. 9

According to the National Blood Authority, immunoglobulin (Ig) expenditure accounted for $1.0 billion in health costs in 2023–2024, of which CIDP accounted for 21.2% of the total grams administered. 10 Over 3000 CIDP patients were treated with Ig nationwide in 2023–2024, making it the most prevalent neurological condition requiring this scarce resource. 10 There is, however, a paucity of published clinical data on CIDP treatment practices with IVIg in Australia. According to national guidelines, maintenance dosing range is recommended from 0.4 to 1.0 g/kg every 2–6 weeks. 11 This is in keeping with the recommendation for individualised dosing as per the European Academy of Neurology/Peripheral Nerve Society (EAN/PNS) 2021 guidelines and includes the standard 1 g/kg every 3 weeks dosing that has been used in clinical trials. 12 , 13

Monitoring for treatment efficacy typically involves a combination of qualitative and quantitative scoring tools such as the Inflammatory Rasch‐built Overall Disability Scale (I‐RODS), 10‐m timed walk, overall neuropathy limitations scale (ONLS), manual grip strength and Medical Research Council (MRC) sum score to ensure that IVIg is effective for any given individual. 14 Once clinical stability has been established, it is recommended that patients undergo a period of IVIg withdrawal. 2 , 11 , 14 Disease remission does occur for a significant proportion of CIDP patients, likely reflecting immunological senescence, although the timing of this is difficult to predict. 4 , 15 No validated predictors of successful wean have been identified thus far. 16 However, variables such as delay to effective treatment, steroid resistance and associated multifocal deficit have been identified as predictors of poor outcome. 17 , 18 , 19 Without clear indicators for IVIg cessation, many CIDP patients are often on prolonged treatment, which increases resource burden and the risk of side effects, including thromboembolism. 15 , 20 , 21 , 22 , 23

There is substantial heterogeneity in how IVIg is maintained and weaned worldwide, with a lack of published Australian CIDP clinical treatment data. We aimed to describe maintenance IVIg treatment trends over a 5‐year period at our centre, with comparison to international practices. We also sought to determine whether any clinical or demographic factors were associated with higher IVIg requirements or predictive of a successful treatment wean.

Methods

Study design

We conducted a retrospective observational audit of all individuals receiving IVIg for CIDP from January 2019 to January 2024 at Flinders Medical Centre and Noarlunga Hospital, a 593‐bed tertiary referral centre and 140‐bed hospital that comprises part of the Southern Adelaide Local Health Network (SALHN) in South Australia. Patients who had received IVIg at the treatment centres were identified through admission records. The indication for IVIg was reviewed, and patients were included if they were adults (≥18 years old) and receiving IVIg for a diagnosis of probable or confirmed CIDP. Individuals were excluded if they had less than 3 months of treatment data or a diagnosis other than CIDP.

Data collection

Electronic record data of case notes for each patient were accessed. Detailed pre‐treatment data were collected for each patient, including demographic information, CIDP phenotype in line with 2021 EAN/PNS CIDP classification 2 and pre‐treatment investigation results (NCS, CSF analysis, imaging findings and, where performed, nerve biopsies).

We recorded any alternative therapies used before IVIg and information on induction and maintenance dose, change in dose or frequency over time and whether an IVIg wean was attempted and/or successful.

Patients who trialled weaning off IVIg were categorised as ‘successful’ if they no longer required IVIg to manage their symptoms and remained off IVIg for >6 months. Patients were classified as non‐responders if they had no clear clinical benefit from IVIg and were excluded from the successful IVIg wean group. Patients were categorised as having ‘failed’ an IVIg wean if they required re‐treatment to manage symptoms after a relapse within 6 months of treatment cessation.

Statistical analysis

Patient characteristics, pre‐treatment investigation results and IVIg usage were summarised using descriptive statistics.

Comparative analysis for ongoing IVIg versus successfully weaned was conducted through IBM SPSS Statistics software, with a P‐value of <0.05 deemed statistically significant. Fisher's exact test was applied for analysis of dichotomous variables, and Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables.

Ethics approval

This study was approved as a service evaluation into IVIg use at the Southern Adelaide Local Health Network (registration ID 4828). As a quality improvement study posing no risk, the need for individual informed consent was waived.

Results

Patient characteristics

A total of 41 patients received maintenance IVIg for a neurological condition at SALHN over the study period. Of these, 22 (54%) were treated over the 5‐year period for CIDP, with other frequent neurological diagnoses utilising IVIg including myasthenia gravis (16%) and multifocal motor neuropathy (8%). Three patients initially treated as CIDP were excluded due to a subsequent diagnosis of paraproteinemic neuropathy with positive anti‐MAG antibodies.

Typical CIDP comprised 17/22 (77%) of the cohort (Table 1). Of the remainder, two had distal acquired demyelinating symmetric polyneuropathy (DADS), two had sensory CIDP, and one had pure motor CIDP. The ratio of male to female patients was 2:1, with a mean age of 58 years (range 23–79) at time of diagnosis. Average body mass index (BMI) was 30 kg/m 2 (recorded in 19/22). Ten (45%) had a history of smoking, and five (23%) had a diagnosis of diabetes mellitus.

CSF protein was recorded for 14/22 patients. The mean CSF protein was 0.8 g/L (range: 0.3–1.9 g/L). Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) with contrast was performed in five patients, with 2/5 (40%) yielding supportive findings for CIDP. A nerve biopsy was performed in five patients, with histological findings in all cases consistent with the diagnosis of CIDP. Eighteen (82%) met EAN/PNS consensus criteria for definite CIDP, with 4/22 (18%) with possible CIDP. 2

IVIg maintenance treatment

IVIg was the initial treatment in 20/22 (91%) cases, with two patients (9%) undergoing treatment with steroids prior to transitioning to IVIg. The average maintenance dose of IVIg was 0.6 g/kg (range 0.4–1.2 g/kg) every 4 weeks. Fifteen (68%) patients required ≤0.6 g/kg/4 weeks (Table 1). The mean duration of maintenance treatment was 64 months (range 3–152 months). Of the three patients who had less than 6 months of IVIg treatment, one was clinically stable after an early trial of treatment cessation, and two had no clear clinical benefit (non‐responders).

Assessment for response to IVIg was recorded in the patient clinic documentation. Additional supportive evidence from NCS and objective qualitative tools such as ONLS and MRC sum score was used to monitor disease progression. However, scores were not consistently recorded for patients across electronic records, likely as a result of varied documentation styles among clinicians.

Across the 22 patients, 14 received treatment without a dose reduction or cessation, six underwent a wean, and two were IVIg non‐responders (Table 1); 45% of patients had their dose increased over time. The most common frequency of treatment was every 4 weeks. Six (27%) had a subsequent change in maintenance dosing frequency. Four patients were on combination therapy, with two on prednisolone, one on rituximab and one with both rituximab and prednisolone together with IVIg.

Weaning data trends

Six patients (27%) underwent a wean of IVIg treatment during the study period. Of those, five (83%) successfully withdrew from IVIg, and one relapsed within 6 months of stopping IVIg. There was otherwise limited documentation in the electronic medical record as to why treatment weans had not been attempted for most patients at our centre.

IVIg weaning was conducted by stopping treatment (no incremental dose reduction) in all six cases. Of those that were successful, IVIg was ceased after 3 to 32 months of maintenance treatment (mean 16.6 months). All patients were reviewed in clinic to ensure stability.

For the patient who relapsed, they had been on IVIg for 89 months. Evidence of a prior wean had not been documented. IVIg was stopped for 3 months before presentation to clinic with clinical deterioration and subsequent stabilisation on re‐induction of IVIg. Treatment was ceased 3 months afterwards despite effectiveness due to concurrent medical complications.

Given the small sample size (with only five individuals having a successful wean), this study was not adequately powered to identify predictors of successful treatment withdrawal. Exploratory analysis did not reveal any significant association between clinical or demographic variables (typical phenotype, sex, smoking status, diabetes mellitus status, age, IVIg maintenance dose, frequency, BMI and CSF protein) and a successful IVIg wean (Table 2).

Discussion

This real‐world study describes IVIg usage for CIDP at a single tertiary centre in Australia over a period of 5 years.

At our institution, people diagnosed with CIDP treated with IVIg commenced a single course of induction treatment at 2 g/kg over 3–5 days before transitioning to maintenance therapy between 0.4 and 1 g every 3 to 4 weeks, in accordance with a national administration framework facilitating IVIg usage. 11 Clinicians guided subsequent trials of dose escalation and weaning, as has previously been described. 2 , 9 , 24 Despite these guidelines, there was variation in clinical practice and a low rate of attempted treatment withdrawals, reflecting the difficulty in accurately predicting maintenance IVIg requirements.

All patients undergoing IVIg treatment withdrawal over the study period underwent cessation of therapy with close monitoring rather than gradual dose de‐escalation or extension of the dosing interval. This method of identifying IVIg responsiveness has the benefit of more clearly highlighting any treatment effect and aiding prompt decision‐making regarding re‐institution of treatment. 24 In our experience, a more protracted trial of dose reduction can lead to gradual deterioration that is more challenging to identify.

Notably, most individuals in our cohort were clinically stable at a maintenance dose of ≤0.6 g/kg every 4 weeks. This is lower than has been described elsewhere and highlights the need to carefully titrate IVIg to avoid potential under‐ or overdosing this scarce resource. 2 , 25 , 26 Our findings reinforce the benefit in adopting an individualised dosing framework. These frameworks allow for a minimum effective dose to be established using stepwise reductions after IVIg induction. 24 , 27 , 28 Use of subjective and quantitative markers like functional impairment measures (I‐RODS, ONLS, MRC sum score) aids in determining clinical response and stability. 29

Once stability has been established for 12 months, it is recommended IVIg be weaned and patients monitored for clinical deterioration. 11 , 24 Subsequently, international guidelines suggest periodic IVIg dose reduction attempts or decreasing treatment frequency every 6–12 months (years 0–3) or 1–2 years thereafter. 2

Despite these recommendations, our findings indicate that a large proportion of individuals do not undergo IVIg weaning despite clinical stability, which may be a contributing factor to excessive IVIg usage in Australia. This underscores the need for clinicians to proactively consider periodic treatment trials in accordance with best practice guidelines. Rigorous use of validated clinical scores aids in increasing confidence when undergoing weaning attempts and understanding when reinstituting treatment may be necessary. Addressing overtreatment is essential in reducing the resource and financial burden associated with IVIg prescription. 15 Multiple studies have established the safety of weaning IVIg, with effective reinstatement for those who subsequently deteriorate off treatment. 15 , 20 , 30

Consistent with demographic data worldwide, our CIDP patients were mostly male and between 40 and 60 years old. 23 , 31 , 32 This trend differs from the majority of autoimmune conditions, which more commonly affect younger females. The majority of our patients had an elevated BMI, and a quarter had diabetes mellitus. There are limited reports of CIDP affecting those with increased BMI, and this may reflect the high rates of obesity seen in the Australian general population. 33 Although it is an autoimmune condition, risk factors for CIDP remain poorly defined, and there are likely to be both genetic and environmental influences. An increased prevalence of diabetes in patients with CIDP was previously reported. 23 , 34

Given the limited sample size and relatively small proportion of individuals who underwent treatment withdrawal, we were unable to draw meaningful conclusions around predictors of a successful IVIg wean. In addition, due to the retrospective nature of the study, clinical data and investigation results were not always available, limiting potential analyses.

Predicting IVIg treatment duration remains a significant challenge worldwide. While measuring disease activity using clinical outcome measures is helpful, these have their own limitations – particularly given an emphasis on patient‐reported factors that are prone to the well‐documented placebo responses to IVIg. 35 Additional analysis on predictive factors of successful weaning, including serum and CSF biomarkers like NfL and peripherin that are not routinely assessed in clinical care, may be avenues for future research. 36

There were several limitations and potential sources of bias in our study which evaluated a single hospital network in South Australia. Because this was a single‐centre cohort, it finding have limited generalisability. Our patient group was captured based on those receiving IVIg treatment and remained in the public hospital system, introducing risk for selection bias. The retrospective design meant data collection relied on pre‐existing records, where certain information (such as outcome measures at each timepoint and detailed rationale for dosing adjustments) was not always available. Nonetheless, our study provides real‐world data regarding IVIg requirements in an Australian CIDP population over a 5‐year period and highlights the need for more proactive attempts at IVIg withdrawal. Future research should include larger, multicentre prospective cohort studies exploring alternative practices for IVIg maintenance and withdrawal.

Conclusion

Our study provides real‐world insights into IVIg use in CIDP patients. Prolonged treatment is common, and more proactive consideration of IVIg weaning is required. The majority of patients at our centre achieved prolonged clinical stability on ≤0.6 g/kg/4 weeks of IVIg. Further research exploring better ways to individualise maintenance treatment, predict disease activity and the likelihood of a successful IVIg wean is required.
