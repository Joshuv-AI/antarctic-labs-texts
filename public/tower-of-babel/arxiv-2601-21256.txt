Earth-Density Effects in LBL Experiments: A Comprehensive Review of Theory, Observations, and Future Directions
Tia Pandit, Bipin Singh Koranga

Abstract
Earth matter density uncertainties play a non-trivial role in three-flavour neutrino oscillations in matter, particularly for the ν μ → ν e \nu_{\mu}\rightarrow\nu_{e} appearance channel. This underpins CP-violation measurements in long-baseline experiments. We demonstrate that when realistic spatial variations of the Earth’s density are taken into account, oscillation probabilities acquire additional energy-dependent structure that cannot be captured by path-averaged density approximations. We discuss that mismodelling of matter profile can introduce degeneracies that obscure genuine leptonic CP-violating effects, thereby degrading parameter sensitivity and biasing CP-phase inference. Identifying energy regions where CP sensitivity remains robust against density uncertainties is therefore essential. These considerations show that marginalization over a single effective density is insufficient for next-generation precision measurements. They also motivate the incorporation of spatially resolved Earth density treatments in the analysis frameworks of future long-baseline neutrino oscillation experiments.



1
Introduction

Neutrinos are electrically neutral, spin 1/2 fermions that interact only via weak nuclear force & gravity. They’re produced as a result of nuclear processes in the Sun, supernovae, reactors and accelerators.

Neutrinos exist in three flavours; electron neutrino
(\nu_{e})
, muon neutrino
(\nu_{\mu})
and tau neutrino
(\nu_{\tau})
which undergo flavour oscillations
[
1
]
during propagation, implying non-zero mass and physics beyond the Standard Model.

When neutrinos propagate through matter such as the Earth’s crust, electrons in matter interact only with electron neutrinos (
\nu_{e}
) via charged-current processes, while muon and tau neutrinos (
\nu_{\mu}
,
\nu_{\tau}
) do not experience this interaction channel. This coherent, forward scattering of
\nu_{e}
creates an additional effective potential that shifts the relative energies, effective masses, and mixing angles of the neutrino states. Since oscillations depend on these quantities, even a modest matter-induced modification can substantially alter the flavour evolution. This phenomenon is known as the
Mikheyev–Smirnov–Wolfenstein (MSW) matter effect
[
15
,
14
]
.

In long-baseline (LBL) neutrino experiments
[
6
]
such as T2K, NO
\nu
A
[
3
]
, and DUNE
[
7
]
, neutrinos travel hundreds to thousands of kilometres through Earth. Over such distances, the MSW effect has enough baseline to produce observable changes in oscillation probabilities
[
10
]
. For an initial
\nu_{\mu}
beam, the matter environment alters both the amplitude and phase of its oscillation into
\nu_{e}
by the time it reaches the detector
[
2
]
[
13
]
. CP-violating effects in neutrino oscillations are fundamental, coming from the laws of particle physics themselves
[
5
]
[
11
]
. If CP violation is present, neutrinos and antineutrinos behave differently even in empty space. This difference is intrinsic and does not depend on where the neutrinos travel.
In contrast, matter-induced effects are environmental. They arise because neutrinos pass through matter, like the Earth, which contains electrons but not positrons. This uneven background enhances the
\nu_{\mu}\rightarrow\nu_{e}
appearance probability through the MSW effect, while for antineutrinos the effective matter potential changes sign, leading to a suppression of the
\bar{\nu}_{\mu}\rightarrow\bar{\nu}_{e}
transition. This neutrino–antineutrino asymmetry is hence a consequence of matter-induced effects rather than a direct signal of CP violation.

The matter-induced asymmetry has a predictable energy and baseline dependence that LBL experiments capitalize on. They differentiate it from the symmetry breaking caused by intrinsic CP violation. This is done by measuring oscillation probabilities across a range of energies and running in both neutrino and antineutrino modes. In this way, the Earth itself becomes a useful probe, allowing long-baseline experiments to determine the neutrino mass ordering while simultaneously constraining the CP-violating phase.

1.1
Three-Flavour Formulation

The three flavour states are quantum superpositions of mass eigenstates and the relation between them is given by the Pontecorvo–Maki–Nakagawa–Sakata (PMNS) matrix
U
[
12
]
,

|\nu_{\alpha}\rangle=\sum_{i=1}^{3}U_{\alpha i}\,|\nu_{i}\rangle,\qquad\alpha=e,\mu,\tau.

(1)

This mismatch between flavor and mass eigenstates leads to neutrino flavor oscillations during propagation. The oscillation phenomenon depends on three mixing angles
(\theta_{12},\theta_{23},\theta_{13})
, a CP-violating phase
\delta_{\rm CP}
, and the mass-squared differences
\Delta m_{21}^{2}
and
\Delta m_{31}^{2}
, and its observation provides direct evidence for physics beyond the Standard Model.

U=\begin{pmatrix}c_{12}c_{13}&s_{12}c_{13}&s_{13}e^{-i\delta_{\rm CP}}\\[3.99994pt]
-s_{12}c_{23}-c_{12}s_{23}s_{13}e^{i\delta_{\rm CP}}&\;\;c_{12}c_{23}-s_{12}s_{23}s_{13}e^{i\delta_{\rm CP}}&s_{23}c_{13}\\[3.99994pt]
\;\;s_{12}s_{23}-c_{12}c_{23}s_{13}e^{i\delta_{\rm CP}}&-c_{12}s_{23}-s_{12}c_{23}s_{13}e^{i\delta_{\rm CP}}&c_{23}c_{13}\end{pmatrix}

where
c_{ij}\equiv\cos\theta_{ij}
and
s_{ij}\equiv\sin\theta_{ij}
.

Neutrino oscillations are described as the quantum mechanical time evolution of a three-component state vector. In natural units
(\hbar=c=1)
, it is governed by a Schrödinger-like equation,

i\,\frac{d}{dt}\,|\nu(t)\rangle=H\,|\nu(t)\rangle,

(2)

where
H
is the effective Hamiltonian. In the relativistic limit, the propagation time
t
is identified with the baseline
L
.
In vacuum, the Hamiltonian is diagonal in the mass basis,

H_{\rm vac}^{(m)}=\begin{pmatrix}E_{1}&0&0\\
0&E_{2}&0\\
0&0&E_{3}\end{pmatrix}

(3)

with
E_{a}\simeq E+m_{a}^{2}/(2E)
, while in the flavor basis it is given by

H_{\rm vac}^{(f)}=UH_{\rm vac}^{(m)}U^{-1}

(4)

When neutrinos propagate through matter, the MSW effect induces an additional potential
[
12
]
that is diagonal in the flavor basis,

V_{f}=\begin{pmatrix}A&0&0\\
0&0&0\\
0&0&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad A=\sqrt{2}\,G_{F}N_{e},

(5)

where
N_{e}
denotes the electron number density.
The total Hamiltonian in matter is therefore

H_{f}=H_{\rm vac}^{(f)}+V_{f},

(6)

or, equivalently, in the mass basis,

H_{m}=H_{\rm vac}^{(m)}+U^{-1}V_{f}U.

(7)

In the three-flavor case, the Hamiltonian is non-diagonal in both bases, and the oscillation
probabilities are obtained by diagonalizing
H_{m}
and constructing the corresponding
evolution operator. Matter effects thus modify both the effective mass-squared differences
and mixing angles, leading to resonant enhancement of oscillations.

2
Role of Earth Density Uncertainties

The electron number density is determined by the Earth matter profile:

N_{e}(x)=Y_{e}(x)\,\frac{\rho(x)}{m_{p}}

(8)

where
\rho(x)
is the Earth density along the neutrino trajectory,

Y_{e}(x)
is the electron fraction (having a standard value of 0.5 for terrestrial matter) , and
m_{p}
is the proton mass.
It is clear that the electron number density is directly proportional to Earth density, rendering the MSW potential
(A=\sqrt{2}\,G_{F}N_{e})
position-dependent.

The Earth is radially stratified, in essence density increases with depth and neutrinos crossing hundreds to thousands of kilometers sample multiple layers, not a uniform medium. For long baselines, neutrinos mostly travel through the mantle, not the core but even within the mantle the density is not constant. Constant density approximations make use of the fact that Earth density varies slowly along the path, keeping the propagation mostly adiabatic but as LBL experiments enter precision era, “mostly” is not “exactly”. Earth density is no longer a background detail but a systematic uncertainty that competes with CP sensitivity
[
9
]
.
Even modest mismodelling of the matter profile can introduce biases that imitate or obscure genuine CP-violating effects.

Cervera-Freund Approximation

For analytic insight into matter effects in long-baseline experiments, it is common to employ
the Cervera–Freund approximation, in which the three-flavor
\nu_{\mu}\to\nu_{e}
appearance
probability in matter of constant density is expanded perturbatively in the small hierarchy
parameter
\alpha\equiv\Delta m^{2}_{21}/\Delta m^{2}_{31}
and in
\sin\theta_{13}

[
4
,
8
]
.
Retaining terms up to
{\cal O}(\alpha^{2})
, the probability can be written as a sum of four
physically transparent contributions,

P_{\mu e}\simeq P_{0}+P_{\sin\delta}+P_{\cos\delta}+P_{3},

(9)

where
P_{0}
is the leading atmospheric-driven term exhibiting MSW enhancement,

P_{\sin\delta}
and
P_{\cos\delta}
encode genuine and matter-induced CP effects,
and
P_{3}
is the dominant solar-scale contribution at small
\theta_{13}
.

In this framework, matter effects enter through the dimensionless parameter

\hat{A}\equiv A/\Delta m^{2}_{31}\propto\rho E_{\nu}
, implying that uncertainties in the
average Earth density primarily rescale the oscillation amplitude rather than shifting the
oscillation phase.

This behavior is reflected in both, Fig. 1 and Fig. 2.

first oscillation maximum, while the overall spectral shape remains unchanged. Although the absolute differences are small, they correspond to percent-level modulations of the signal rate and underpin the sensitivity of long-baseline
experiments to matter effects.
In Fig. 2,
\pm(5

10)\%
variations in the assumed density
lead to an absolute change in the appearance probability of

\Delta P_{\mu e}\sim 5\times 10^{-3}
near the first oscillation maximum. Although small in absolute terms, this corresponds to a
{\cal O}(10\%)
relative modulation
of the signal and is comparable to subleading CP-violating contributions, demonstrating that Earth-density uncertainties must be included in precision analyses of long-baseline experiments.

3
Accurate Matter Density Profiles

The assumption of a constant (or averaged) Earth density is not fundamental and breaks down when precision reaches the percent level, degeneracies between matter effects and CP violation matter and non-adiabatic or stochastic effects become relevant. To clearly separate genuine CP-violating effects from distortions caused by the Earth’s matter profile, Earth density needs to be considered as a spatially varying quantity. This can be done in a number of ways, some of which are covered in the following sub-sections.

3.1
Two-Layer Model:

In this approach, the neutrino trajectory is divided into segments with piecewise-constant matter density, typically characterized by
\rho_{\rm crust}
and
\rho_{\rm mantle}
.

\rho(x)=\begin{cases}\rho_{\mathrm{crust}},&x<L_{c}\\
\rho_{\mathrm{mantle}},&x\geq L_{c}\end{cases}

(10)

where
L_{c}
is the distance for which neutrino propagation remains within the crust.

Compared to a single-density approximation, the two-layer profile captures the dominant density discontinuity encountered by long-baseline neutrinos and provides a better estimate of the effective matter potential experienced
along the path. This leads to more accurate predictions of the appearance probability, particularly for experiments with baselines traversing both geological regions.

3.2
Multi-layer Model (Piecewise constant):

The two-layer approximation can be systematically generalized by subdividing the Earth into multiple shells with piecewise-constant densities.

\rho(x)=\rho_{i}\quad\text{for }x_{i}<x<x_{i+1}

(11)

In this multi-layer framework, neutrino propagation is treated as a sequence of evolution operators, each corresponding to a distinct density layer. This allows
the cumulative matter effect to be reconstructed along the baseline.
The multi-layer description provides improved accuracy and
a more faithful representation of density variations.

3.3
Preliminary Reference Earth Model-based profile

The multi-layer description can be further refined by adopting the
Preliminary Reference Earth Model (PREM), in which the Earth is represented
by a sequence of concentric shells with radially varying density.
In this approach, the matter potential becomes a function of position along the neutrino trajectory and the total evolution operator thus obtained is an ordered product,

U(L)=\prod_{i}\exp\!\left[-i\,H(\rho_{i})\,\Delta x_{i}\right],

(12)

where
\rho_{i}
and
\Delta x_{i}
denote the density and thickness of the

i
th PREM layer.

While simpler approximations already capture the qualitative impact of matter effects, the PREM-based treatment provides the most realistic estimate of the effective matter potential.

3.4
Perturbed PREM (uncertainty-aware):

For precision studies, the PREM profile can be treated as a reference density configuration with small perturbations,

\rho(x)=\rho_{\rm PREM}(x)+\delta\rho(x),\qquad|\delta\rho|\ll\rho_{\rm PREM}.

(13)

Correspondingly, the matter potential is written as

A(x)=A_{\rm PREM}(x)+\delta A(x)
, inducing a perturbation

\delta H(x)
in the effective Hamiltonian.
To first order, the evolution operator can be expressed as

U(L)\simeq U_{\rm PREM}(L)-i\int_{0}^{L}dx\,U_{\rm PREM}(L,x)\,\delta H(x)\,U_{\rm PREM}(x,0),

(14)

where
U_{\rm PREM}
denotes the evolution operator computed using the unperturbed PREM profile. This formulation provides a systematic way to quantify the impact of
realistic Earth-density uncertainties on oscillation probabilities.

4
Discussion & Future Directions

This review demonstrates that treating Earth matter effects with a constant or path-averaged density is no longer sufficient for precision long-baseline neutrino experiments. Although such approximations capture the leading MSW enhancement, they miss the energy-dependent distortions produced by realistic spatial variations in the Earth’s density.

A key consequence of matter-density mismodelling is the introduction of energy-dependent degeneracies between intrinsic CP-violating effects and matter-induced contributions in the
\nu_{\mu}\to\nu_{e}
appearance channel. These degeneracies cannot be eliminated by marginalizing over a single density normalization, and they lead to a CP sensitivity that varies strongly with energy. In some regions, the limiting factor is no longer detector performance or exposure, but uncertainty in the Earth itself.

To address this, future long-baseline analyses must incorporate spatially resolved Earth density profiles directly into oscillation fits. Doing so enables a transparent assessment of bias, degeneracy structure, and the robustness of CP inference. Ultimately, Earth matter effects should be treated as a precision-limiting systematic rather than a secondary correction. This ensures that observed neutrino–antineutrino asymmetries can be confidently attributed to fundamental CP violation in DUNE and other next-generation experiments.
