Effect of external evaluation and training on the incomplete resection rate in cold snare polypectomies for colonic lesions <10 mm: a prospective Spanish study (POLIPEVA)
Authors: Marina de Benito Sanz, Jorge Feito, Luis Hernández, Javier Tejedor-Tejada, Pilar Diez-Redondo, María Henar Núñez Rodríguez, Raúl Honrubia-López, Cristina Fernández, Victoria Busto, Sofia Parejo, Marta Aicart-Ramos, Marina Solano, Silvia Patricia Ortega, Natalia Marcos, Francisco Javier García-Alonso, on behalf of the POLIPEVA study group
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background/AimsCold snare polypectomy (CSP) technique for polyps <10 mm varies widely among endoscopists. This study aimed to evaluate an online educational intervention for incomplete resections.MethodsThis multicenter trial was performed between 2021 and 2024. After a baseline assessment of incomplete resection rates for CSP of lesions <10 mm was completed, participants undertook a 1-hour online course led by an expert senior endoscopist. A second assessment was performed following course completion. Resections were evaluated using two marginal biopsies obtained after CSP, assessed by a single pathologist blinded to polyp histology. Follow-up was conducted by telephone 4 weeks after colonoscopy.ResultsA total of 29 endoscopists participated in the baseline assessment, yielding a baseline 4.8% incomplete resection rate, with a median individual rate of 4.3% (interquartile range [IQR], 2.9%–5.7%; range, 0%–15.6%). Thirteen endoscopists completed the course and participated in the second study phase, which included 1,271 polyps in phase 1 and 826 in phase 2. No significant improvement in incomplete resection rate was observed (4.8% in phase 1 vs. 4.0% in phase 2; difference, 0.8%; 95% confidence interval [CI], –1.1% to 2.6%; p=0.4). Among 3,087 polypectomies, intraprocedural bleeding occurred in 288 (9.3%) cases; most were self-limited, with only 45 (1.5%) requiring clipping. Delayed bleeding was observed in five (0.5%) patients, four of whom had mild bleeding and one severe. No perforations were observed.ConclusionsThe utility of online endoscopic technique courses appears limited. CSP demonstrates an excellent safety profile (ClinicalTrials.gov number: NCT04636619).

INTRODUCTION

Over 80%–90% of polyps found during colonoscopy are diminutive (≤5 mm) or small (6–9 mm)1; their removal is the bread and butter for every practicing endoscopist. Although the importance of small lesions is frequently underestimated, a prospective registry including over 5,000 lesions showed that 4.0% of adenomas <6 mm and 16% of those between 6 and 10 mm exhibit villous architecture or severe dysplasia,2 results confirmed in other larger series.3,4 It has been estimated that 40% of small polyps will continue growing,5 with an estimated growth rate of 0.6 mm/year,6 further supporting the clinical significance of these lesions.

Cold snare polypectomy (CSP) for polyps <10 mm, compared with piecemeal biopsy forceps resection, is associated with a decreased risk of residual neoplastic tissue (relative risk, 0.40; 95% confidence interval [CI], 0.26–0.62).7 Hot snare polypectomy (HSP) has an incomplete resection rate of 6.8% for 5–9 mm nonpedunculated polyps.8 Studies comparing HSP and CSP have reported incomplete resection rates ranging between 1.8%–8.5% for CSP and from 2.6% to 3.7% for HSP.9-11 In a meta-analysis12 including 1,485 polyps randomized to HSP (n=741) or CSP (n=744), the overall incomplete resection rates for HSP (2.4%) and CSP (4.7%) showed no differences (odds ratio [OR], 0.51; 95% CI, 0.13–1.99; p=0.33). Because CSP reduces procedure time13,14 and may shorten and reduce the intensity of post-polypectomy abdominal pain,15 CSP is the recommended technique to remove <10 mm colorectal polyps.16

Compared to HSP, which presents a relatively narrow range of incomplete resection rates, CSP exhibits wider incomplete resection rates among endoscopists, suggesting that the polypectomy technique may be more important for CSP than for HSP. Multiple endoscopy curricula and competency assessment tools,17 including video-based assessments without personal interactions,18 have been developed to improve training programs, standardize skills, and evaluate endoscopic competence. Educational interventions aimed at improving adenoma detection rates (ADR) have been successful. In the EQUIP study,19 endoscopists randomized to receive endoscopic quality training increased their ADR from 36% to 47%, while those not receiving training remained unchanged. These improvements persisted for at least 5 months after completing the training program.20 Similarly, a simple educational intervention may improve the proximal serrated polyp detection rate from 9.3% to 15.6%.21

Therefore, we hypothesized that an online educational session specifically aimed to improve CSP technique would reduce incomplete resection rates. To our knowledge, the effects of such educational interventions have not been studied.

METHODS

This multicenter prospective study evaluated the effectiveness of an online educational intervention on the incomplete resection rate of CSP for colorectal polyps <10 mm. The study was conducted between 2021 and 2024 at 14 Spanish centers.

Selection of endoscopists

Participating endoscopists had a minimum of 1 year of experience in colonoscopy (excluding the formation period) and had performed >300 colonoscopies in the previous year. These inclusion criteria were established to ensure minimum baseline level of experience.

Selection of patients

Patients aged >18 years who were referred for colonoscopy, regardless of the indications, were eligible. Exclusion criteria included pregnancy, contraindications to polypectomy due to non-discontinuation of anticoagulant or antithrombotic agents (except aspirin), or uncorrected severe coagulopathy/thrombocytopenia. Patients with one or more polyps measuring 1–9 mm were enrolled. Polyps lost to histological analysis or those labelled as normal mucosa in the polypectomy specimen were excluded after enrollment.

Intervention

The first phase of the study consisted of a baseline assessment of incomplete resection rate.

First, the protocol was designed as a randomized study: after completing the baseline assessment, endoscopists were randomized to receive either an educational intervention or a complete report of their results, with comparison made to the remaining participants. Due to the small number of participating endoscopists, a protocol amendment was adopted, offering the educational intervention to all endoscopists and comparing pre- and post-intervention incomplete resection rates.

The educational intervention consisted of a 1-hour online course conducted by an expert endoscopist (L.H.) on CSP technique. This single-session online format was selected to enhance accessibility and engagement among potential participants. A voiceover video with slides and polypectomy clips was made available. The course was structured to include a brief introduction to the theoretical framework, a detailed technical description covering resection edge assessment and tips and tricks, and live questions.

After completing the educational intervention, endoscopists proceeded to the second study phase, undergoing a new estimation of their incomplete resection rates.

Procedures

All colonoscopies were performed under sedation according to the usual clinical practice at each center. Standard videocolonoscopy was used throughout the study; high-definition, magnification, and image-enhancement functions are not mandatory. If macroscopic residual tissue were observed after CSP, fragmented polypectomy was performed. After polypectomies completion and careful evaluation of the mucosal defect, two random biopsies were obtained from the margins at both ends of the defect.

Post-endoscopic monitoring

Four weeks after colonoscopy, participants were contacted by centralized telephone and completed a standardized questionnaire. If any potential adverse events were identified, a complete report was requested from the local investigators.

Histological analysis

Polypectomy specimens were analyzed at each center according to local protocols. Biopsy samples obtained from the base of the polyp were centrally assessed by a pathologist with over 5 years of experience in digestive pathology (J.F.), who was blinded to the histological diagnosis of the removed polyp.

Outcomes and definitions

The primary endpoint of this study was incomplete resection rate before and after the educational intervention. Complete resection was defined as cases in which biopsies from the margins of the mucosal defect contained only normal mucosa.

Secondary endpoints were the proportion of fragmented polypectomies, defined as piecemeal resection, and the polypectomy-related adverse event rate. These included intraprocedural delayed bleeding, perforation, postpolypectomy syndrome, and delayed hemorrhage, which was defined as any rectal bleeding between discharge and follow-up. The severity of adverse events was defined according to American Society for Gastrointestinal Endoscopy recommendations.22

Polypectomies that could not be completed with CSP and subsequently required HSP were excluded.

Sample size calculation

The sample size was estimated using Stata ver. 16 (StataCorp.). Based on the results from our previous study15 we observed an intrasubject correlation coefficient (with endoscopists as subjects) of 0.017.

The initial design conditioned sample size on the total number of endoscopists. When the study began with 11 endoscopists, 100 samples were estimated for phase I.

After the protocol amendment, the number of lesions/endoscopists needed after the educational intervention was estimated at 67 polyps. This calculation assumed a baseline incomplete polypectomy rate of 6.5% and aimed to detect an absolute risk reduction of 3.5% with an alpha risk of 5%, and power of 80%, and a 2.5% of loss rate due to normal or inflamed mucosa on histological analysis.

Data retrieval and statistical analysis

Data were collected and managed using the Spanish Digestive Endoscopy Society Research Electronic Data Capture (RedCap) tool. This is a secure, web-based application created to support data capture for research studies providing semiautomatic data quality control.23

Statistical analysis was performed using Stata ver. 16. Continuous variables were summarized using mean and standard deviation or, if they did not present a normal distribution according to the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test, median and interquartile range (IQR). Categorical variables were expressed as percentages.

The difference in the proportions of incomplete polypectomies (primary outcome) and adverse events was assessed using regression models based on generalized estimating equations to account for intrasubject correlation.

For secondary objectives, the proportion of fragmented polypectomies and adverse events were compared using the χ2 test and the homogeneity z-test without Yates correction.

To evaluate factors associated with incomplete polypectomies, multivariate logistic regression techniques based on the generalized estimating equation, accounting for intrasubject correlations, were used. Directed acyclic graphs24 (Fig. 1) were employed to identify confounding variables requiring conditioning when estimating causal effects. Variables assessed were selected based on scientific evidence and literature review and included the snare type, polypectomy method (en bloc vs. piecemeal), polyp size, and morphology. The interaction between polyp size and polypectomy type was also considered. These variables were evaluated using multivariable logistic regression models to determine adjusted OR.

Ethical statements

The institutional review boards of all the centers approved the study protocol. The trial was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT04636619).

RESULTS

A total of 2,444 patients were recruited between 2021 and 2024 from 14 centers by 29 endoscopists. Of these, 976 participants were excluded because they did not present polyps measuring 1–9 mm. Sixteen endoscopists only completed the first phase of study; therefore, the 492 patients recruited were excluded from the primary outcome analysis. Patients’ baseline characteristics are shown in Table 1. Recruitment was considered complete when all endoscopists participating in both phases reached a predetermined sample size. A complete study flowchart is shown in Figure 2.

Baseline assessment

A total of 29 endoscopists participated in the study, resecting 3,088 lesions, with a median of 126 lesions/endoscopist (IQR, 33–139). Detailed characteristics of all lesions are summarized in Table 2. Fourteen biopsies (1.1%) in phase 1 and nine (1.1%) in phase 2 were non-assessable and were subsequently excluded.

The overall baseline incomplete resection rate was 4.8%. The median individual incomplete resection rate was 4.3% (IQR, 2.9%–5.7%; range, 0%–15.6%). No correlation was observed between incomplete resection rate and endoscopist experience (p=0.85). However, endoscopists who participated in the educational intervention had a lower baseline incomplete resection rate than those who did not (4.5% vs. 6.8%; difference, –2.3%; 95% CI, –4.1% to –0.5%; p=0.01).

Effect of the educational intervention

Only patients from 13 endoscopists (eight centers) who completed both study phases were included in this analysis. The interval between the end of phase I and the beginning of phase II was 315 (IQR, 305–370) days. In phase 1, 1,271 polyps from 598 patients were included. In phase 2, 826 lesions from 357 patients were included, as shown in Table 3. The incomplete resection rate was 4.8% in phase 1 and 4.0% in phase 2 (difference, 0.8%; 95% CI, –1.1% to 2.6%; p=0.44).

The median individual incomplete resection rate was 4.3% in phase 1 (IQR, 1.5%–6.7%, range, 0%–8%), and 4% in phase 2, ≤5 mm (IQR, 1.6%–6.2%, range, 0.7%–8.1%). Seven endoscopists (53.8%) improved their results, while six (46.2%) had worse results. The results for each endoscopist are shown in Figure 3.

Incomplete resection risk factors

Risk factor analysis was performed using pooled data from both phases. Among the variables selected for multivariable regression analysis, type of polypectomy (en bloc vs. fragmented) and interaction between polypectomy type and size (categorized as <or=5 mm and >5 mm) has statistically significant association with incomplete polypectomy (Supplementary Table 1). Fragmented polypectomy (OR, 5.93; 95% CI, 2.3–15.27) and en bloc resection of polyps measuring 5–10 mm (OR, 6.15; 95% CI, 1.33–28.49) were identified as risk factor for incomplete polypectomy.

Adverse events

Across both study phases, a total of 3,087 polypectomies were analyzed. Intraprocedural bleeding was observed in 288 polypectomies (9.3%). Most episodes were self-limited; only 45 (1.5%) required endoscopic treatment, all of them were managed with clips.

Delayed adverse events were assessed in the 953 patients (99.8%) who completed the follow-up telephone calls. Delayed bleeding occurred in five patients (0.5%): four cases were mild, and one was severe. Mild abdominal pain was reported in eight patients (0.8%), and 13 patients (1.4%) experienced other minor adverse events, such as one cardiac arrhythmia, four hemorrhoidal complications, one hypertension episode, three mild respiratory infections, one case of peripheral sensory neuropathy, one renal colic, one transient ischemic attack, and one episode of vertigo.

DISCUSSION

We performed a multicenter randomized trial to assess the efficacy of an educational intervention on incomplete CSP resection rates. Based on our results, the utility of online endoscopic technique courses was limited.

In recent years, online training sessions have become commonplace and, in many cases, receive funding from private entities. However, their usefulness and relevance in clinical practice have rarely been evaluated. Computer-based colonoscopy simulation (CBCS) as a training method for novice endoscopists, has been evaluated in a comparative study with non-CBCS-trained endoscopists,25 demonstrating a relative increase in competency during the early stages of patient-based colonoscopy, which equalized after a short period of real-world practice. Similarly, a one-day colonoscopy skills improvement hands-on course26 did not increase ADR, colonoscopy completion rate, or withdrawal time. In our study, approximately half of the participating endoscopists improved their incomplete resection rates, while the other half had worse results. Interestingly, courses focused on improving ADR19,20 or serrated polyps detection rate21 have been proven effective, even when purely theoretical. This difference may be explained by the course objective. Improving ADR does not require specific mechanical skills but rather emphasizes the importance of a thorough examination. In contrast, performing colonoscopy or polypectomy may require the physical presence of a tutor to observe and refine movements, body positioning, and scope handling. Non-presential assessments, such as the Direct Observation of Polypectomy Skills (DOPyS),27,28 may be more effective than online courses while avoiding the costs of presential formation. Unfortunately, the items assessed in DOPyS have not been associated with clinically relevant outcomes, such as lower incomplete resection rates.

All participants had a baseline incomplete polypectomy rate of 7%, and after the online course, only one participant reached 7.8%. Available clinical trials have reported incomplete resection rates ranging from 1.8% to 8.5% for CSP.9-11 Our rates are consistent with those of previous retrospective CSP studies29,30 with no improvement observed in phase 2, making Hawthorne effect bias questionable and highlighting the limited utility of the course and the agreement of the results with real clinical practice. In contrast to expectations, we did not observe differential improvement in resection rates among endoscopists with worse baseline performance. Notably, endoscopists who participated in the educational intervention had a lower baseline incomplete resection rate than those who did not (4.5% vs. 6.8%; difference, –2.3; 95% CI, –4.1 to –0.5; p=0.01). A potential explanation for this difference is self-selection bias, with those who were more interested in improving their polypectomy techniques completing the whole study. Only four endoscopists (30.8%) reported a >3% decrease. Presential courses might achieve these results for a higher proportion of endoscopists; however, whether the additional resources required to attain a small overall improvement remains debatable.

A relevant finding, considering the large number of patients included in our study, is the very low rate of adverse events. Considering both immediate and delayed episodes, bleeding rates were below 1%, concordant with previous meta-analysis and further emphasizing the safety of CSP for this size colonic polyps.12

Our study has several strengths. We designed it to reflect clinical practice as closely as possible, including primary, secondary, and tertiary centers. We also included endoscopists with a wide range of experiences (years of practice, participation in previous CSP studies, or training courses) and recalculated the sample size after the protocol amendment. Nevertheless, certain limitations should be acknowledged. First, the long interval between the first and second study phases, as the educational intervention took place once all endoscopists had completed the baseline assessment, may have contributed to selection bias and, together with the protocol amendment, affects the study’s interval validity. Second, the online format of the course may have impacted participant attention. Despite these limitations, our study provides important evidence regarding the limited utility of online technical courses. In many cases, both online and in-person courses on the technical aspects of endoscopy require substantial financial investment for design and dissemination, which does not translate into meaningful practical improvements. Therefore, their cost-effectiveness is debatable. The small number of participating endoscopists precluded subgroup analysis; online educational interventions might be useful in selected groups, namely, trainees or endoscopists with below-average complete resection rates. Regarding adverse event analysis, the lack of data on comorbidities and baseline pharmacological treatments hindered the interpretation of our results.

Unfortunately, the large number of endoscopists who withdrew from the study, necessitated a design change. However, to maintain internal validity, we modified the study protocol before the training intervention, ensuring internal consistency in the revised protocol.

In conclusion, endoscopists who participated in an online CSP course did not demonstrate improvement in their CSP incomplete resection rates.
