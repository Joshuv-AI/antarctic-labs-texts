Normal approximation for subgraph counts in random intersection graphs: the case of edges
Katarzyna Rybarczyk, Grzegorz Serafin

Abstract
We investigate the subgraph count in the random intersection graph model 𝒢 ⁡ ( n ​ ; ​ m ​ ; ​ p ) \mathcal{G}(n\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}m\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}p) . Due to substantial edge dependencies, studying even such fundamental statistics is significantly more challenging than in the classical binomial model 𝒢 ⁡ ( n ​ ; ​ p ) \mathcal{G}(n\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}p) . This article establish new normal approximation bounds in both the Wasserstein and Kolmogorov distances for general non-degenerate U U -statistics. We then illustrate how these results can be potentially applied to the standardized number of subgraphs in the random intersection graph model. Finally, as a basic example, we derive necessary and sufficient conditions for the asymptotic normality of the number of edges in 𝒢 ⁡ ( n ​ ; ​ m ​ ; ​ p ) \mathcal{G}(n\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}m\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}p) .



Keywords
: random intersection graph, subgraph count, normal approximation, convergence rates.

This abundance of cliques, along with the edge correlations and other structural properties of random intersection graphs have resulted in the model and its generalisations finding many applications such as: “gate matrix layout” for VLSI design

Due to the edge dependencies, studying random intersection graph in the context of small subgraphs turns out to be much more challenging comparing to the binomial model. Even the analysis of the distribution of the number of edges in
{\mathcal{G}}(n\mathchar 59\relax m\mathchar 59\relax p)
is non-trivial, whereas in the

Only recently some results concerning the normal distribution of the number of triangles in a random intersection graph have been proved by Dong and Hu

17

is a substantial contribution to the solution of the problem, it

In particular, this concerns the case when

The aim of this paper is to develop a new, more efficient method for finding an upper bound on the distance between a standardised random variable counting the number of small subgraphs in
\mathcal{G}(n\mathchar 59\relax m\mathchar 59\relax p)
and the standard normal distribution. We achieved this by developing and combining some probabilistic as well as combinatorial techniques. The probabilistic ingredient of the approach builds on ideas from
[

,
32
,
33

, where the Stein method was combined with Malliavin calculus to obtain normal approximation bounds for functionals of independent random variables distributed uniformly on the interval
(-1\mathchar 59\relax 1)
. These methods have been already used in the context of small subgraphs in other random graph models
[

,
31
,
32

{\mathcal{G}}(n\mathchar 59\relax m\mathchar 59\relax p)

-statistics of random vectors.
On the other hand, what is essential for establishing accurate estimates of the distance,
is the estimation of the central moments of subgraphs existence indicators. We believe that omitting centralization was one of the reasons for the gap in the result from
[
17
]
. We resolve this problem by very subtle analysis of so called clique covers, that were introduced in
[
26

and describe subgraphs existence conditions. Namely, we developed new techniques for comparing clique covers of two not necessarily isomorphic graphs. This allows us to estimate specific types of central moments that are crucial for the final results. Additionally, the probabilistic approach described above effectively limits the number of cases considered.
Namely, analyzing the higher central moments of a random variable counting the number of edges in
{\mathcal{G}}(n\mathchar 59\relax m\mathchar 59\relax p)
poses a significant challenge and requires a thorough examination of clique covers of certain subgraphs associated with these moments. Theorem

The method we present is not only applicable across the entire range of traditionally considered parameters

Let

In the random intersection graph
{\mathcal{G}}(n\mathchar 59\relax m\mathchar 59\relax p)

\mathcal{V}
,

, and a set of attributes
\mathcal{A}=\{a_{1}\mathchar 59\relax\ldots\mathchar 59\relax a_{m}\}

a_{i}
,

(we say that vertex

. Then, two vertices

are connected by an edge in
{\mathcal{G}}(n\mathchar 59\relax m\mathchar 59\relax p)
whenever they chose at least one common attribute. The existence probability of any edge will be denoted by

and is given by

\displaystyle\mathbf{p}=1-(1-p^{2})^{m}\asymp 1\wedge(mp^{2}).

(2.1)

Note that for

\mathbf{p}\sim 1-e^{-mp^{2}}

Furthermore, for any vertex
v_{k}

and all the attributes, by

where

Hence, the distribution

of any

\mu_{m\mathchar 59\relax p}(x)=p^{|x|}(1-p)^{m-|x|}\mathchar 59\relax\ \ \ \ x\in\{0\mathchar 59\relax 1\}^{m}\mathchar 59\relax

where

as in the multi-index notation. In particular, we have

|A^{(k)}|\ \ \sim\ \ \mathcal{B}(m\mathchar 59\relax p).

Many of the local statistics on

\displaystyle X=\sum_{1\leq k_{1}<\ldots<k_{r}\leq n}h(A^{(k_{1})}\mathchar 59\relax\ldots\mathchar 59\relax A^{(k_{r})})\mathchar 59\relax

Our first goal is to examine the asymptotic normality of the standardised random variable

by establishing bounds on its distance to a normally distributed random variable

\displaystyle d_{W}(X\mathchar 59\relax Y):=\sup_{\text{Lip}(h)\leq 1}\big|\mathbf{E}[h(X)-h(Y)]\big|\mathchar 59\relax

\displaystyle d_{K/W}(X\mathchar 59\relax Y)\leq 2.

(2.3)

\displaystyle\frac{n^{4}\left\|\varrho_{2}\ast_{1}^{1}\varrho_{2}\right\|_{2}^{2}}{(\mathrm{Var}[X^{(2)}_{n}])^{2}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{n^{4}\big((1-p)^{4}+4p(1-p)^{3}+2p^{2}(1-p)^{2})\big)^{m}}{\big(n^{3}(1-2p^{2}+p^{3})^{m}\big)^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n^{2}(1-\mathbf{p})}\left(1-\Big(\frac{p}{1+p-p^{2}}\Big)^{2}\,\right)^{m}\leq\frac{1}{n^{2}(1-\mathbf{p})}\mathchar 59\relax

which ends the proof.
∎

The last term in (
7.7
) diverges at
p=1
, which makes the bound ineffective for values of
p
close to
1
. A more detailed discussion of this issue has already been provided in Section
2
, following formula (
2.10
). On the other hand, for
p
close to zero one can effectively bound the right-hand side of (
7.7
) in various ways. We propose the following one, which correspond to the necessary condition for the asymptotic normality of
\widetilde{X}^{(2)}_{n}
.

Corollary 7.1
.

For
mp^{3}\geq 1
and
p\leq 0.1
we have

\displaystyle d_{K/W}(\widetilde{X}^{(2)}_{n}\mathchar 59\relax\mathcal{N})

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{1}{\big({n^{2}(1-\mathbf{p})}\big)^{1/4}}.

Proof.

From Proposition
7.2
, Lemma
7.1
and estimate (
5.2
) we deduce for
mp^{3}\geq 1

\displaystyle d_{K/W}(\widetilde{X}^{(2)}_{n}\mathchar 59\relax\mathcal{N})\lesssim\left[\frac{1}{n^{2}\mathbf{p}(1-\mathbf{p})}+\frac{1}{n}+\frac{1}{n}\left(\frac{1-p+p(1-p)^{4}}{(1-2p^{2}+p^{3})^{2}}\right)^{m}\right]^{1/2}.

(7.9)

Thus, by virtue of (
2.3
) and the fact that
\mathbf{p}
is bounded away from zero for
mp^{3}\geq 1
, it suffices to show that for
p\leq 0.1

Q(m\mathchar 59\relax p):=\left(\frac{1-p+p(1-p)^{4}}{(1-2p^{2}+p^{3})^{2}}\right)^{m}\leq\frac{1}{{(1-\mathbf{p})}^{1/2}}.

First, we rewrite

\displaystyle Q(m\mathchar 59\relax p)=\left(1+4p^{3}-p^{4}\,\frac{8-13p-4p^{2}+12p^{3}-4p^{4}}{(1-p)(1+p-p^{2})^{2}}\right)^{m}.

One can see that for
p<1/2
the last ratio is positive and hence

Q(m\mathchar 59\relax p)\leq\left(1+4p^{3}\right)^{m}
,
p\in(0\mathchar 59\relax\tfrac{1}{2})
. Furthermore, it holds that

\displaystyle(1+4p^{3})\sqrt{1-p^{2}}=\sqrt{1-p^{2}\left(1-8p+8p^{3}-16p^{4}+16p^{6}\right)}\mathchar 59\relax

which is clearly less than one for
p\leq 0.1
(numerical verification shows that it stays true for
p\leq 0.126521\ldots
). This gives us

\displaystyle Q(m\mathchar 59\relax p)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-p^{2}}^{\,m}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\mathbf{p}}}\mathchar 59\relax\ \ \ \ \ p\leq 0.1\mathchar 59\relax

as required.
∎

8
Asymptotic normality conditions

Eventually, we turn our attention to the necessary and sufficient conditions for asymptotic normality of
\widetilde{X}^{(2)}_{n}=(X^{(2)}_{n}-\mathbf{E}[X^{(2)}_{n}])/\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}[X^{(2)}_{n}]}
.
Let us recall that whenever the Kolmogorov or Wasserstein distance between random variables
X_{n}
and a random variable
X
tends to zero then
X_{n}
converges to
X
in distribution. Thus, in view of Lemma
6.2
, Corollary
7.1
and the assumptions
n\mathchar 59\relax m\rightarrow\infty
, the condition
n^{2}\mathbf{p}(1-\mathbf{p})\rightarrow\infty
ensures
\widetilde{X}^{(2)}_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{D}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathcal{N}
whenever
p\leq 0.1
. It turns out that the opposite implication holds true without any additional conditions on
p
.

Lemma 8.1
.

If
\widetilde{X}^{(2)}_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{D}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathcal{N}
, then
n^{2}\mathbf{p}(1-\mathbf{p})\rightarrow\infty
.

Proof.

We adapt the argument from
[
28
, proof of Theorem 3.5]
. First of all,
\mathrm{Var}[X^{(2)}_{n}]
tends to infinity or else the limit would not be a continuous random variable.
Since

\widetilde{X}^{(2)}_{n}=\frac{X^{(2)}_{n}-\mathbf{E}[X^{(2)}_{n}]}{\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}[X^{(2)}_{n}]}}\geq-\frac{\mathbf{E}[X^{(2)}_{n}]}{\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}[X^{(2)}_{n}]}}\mathchar 59\relax

and the normal distribution is supported on the whole real line, the last expression has to tend to minus infinity. Thus, due to
\mathrm{Var}[X^{(2)}_{n}]\rightarrow\infty
, we get
\mathbf{E}[X^{(2)}_{n}]={n\choose 2}\mathbf{p}\asymp n^{2}\mathbf{p}\rightarrow\infty
.

On the other hand, denoting
Z^{(2)}_{n}={n\choose 2}-X^{(2)}_{n}
we obtain
\widetilde{Z}^{(2)}_{n}=-\widetilde{X}^{(2)}_{n}\rightarrow\mathcal{N}
and consequently

\widetilde{Z}^{(2)}_{n}=\frac{Z^{(2)}_{n}-\mathbf{E}[Z^{(2)}_{n}]}{\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}[Z^{(2)}_{n}]}}\geq-\frac{\mathbf{E}[Z^{(2)}_{n}]}{\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}[X^{(2)}_{n}]}}.

Repeating the previous argument, we conclude
\mathbf{E}[Z^{(2)}_{n}]={n\choose 2}(1-\mathbf{p})\asymp n^{2}(1-\mathbf{p})\rightarrow\infty
. Eventually, the observation
\mathbf{p}\wedge(1-\mathbf{p})\asymp\mathbf{p}(1-\mathbf{p})
completes the proof.
∎

Remark 8.2
.

Theorem
2.2
follows now from Lemma
6.2
, Corollary
7.1
, and Lemma
8.1
.

At the end of this section we present a threshold-like phenomenon for asymptotic normality, that gives us some understanding what happens when
p
is bounded away from zero and from one, without restricting it to be less than
0.1
.

Proposition 8.3
.

Let
p=p(n)\in\left(\varepsilon\mathchar 59\relax 1-\varepsilon\right)
for some fixed
\varepsilon\in(0\mathchar 59\relax\tfrac{1}{2})
.

1.

If
\frac{m}{\ln n}\rightarrow 0
, then
\widetilde{X}^{(2)}_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{D}}}{{\rightarrow}}\mathcal{N}
.

2.

If
\frac{m}{\ln n}\rightarrow\infty
, then
\widetilde{X}^{(2)}_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{D}}}{{\not\rightarrow}}\mathcal{N}
.

Proof.

Ad.
1.
The assumption
p>\varepsilon
implies
mp^{3}\rightarrow\infty
, so it suffices to
show that both of the terms in (
7.9
) tend to zero. Indeed, we have

\frac{1}{n^{2}\mathbf{p}(1-\mathbf{p})}=\frac{1}{1-(1-p^{2})^{m}}n^{-\frac{m}{\ln n}\ln\left(1-p^{2}\right)-2}\\
\leq\frac{1}{1-(1-\varepsilon^{2})^{m}}n^{-\frac{m}{\ln n}\ln\left(1-(1-\varepsilon)^{2}\right)-2}\rightarrow 0.

(8.1)

Furthermore,

\frac{1}{n}\left(\frac{1-p+p(1-p)^{4}}{(1-2p^{2}+p^{3})^{2}}\right)^{m}=\frac{1}{n}\left(\frac{1+p(1-p)^{3}}{(1-p)\big(1+p(1-p)\big)^{2}}\right)^{m}\\
\leq\frac{1}{n}\left(\frac{2}{\varepsilon}\right)^{m}=n^{\frac{m}{\ln n}\ln\left(2/\varepsilon\right)-1}\rightarrow 0\mathchar 59\relax

as required.

Ad.
2.
If
\frac{m}{\ln n}\rightarrow\infty
, then the expression in (
8.1
) tends to
\infty
, and thus
{n^{2}\mathbf{p}(1-\mathbf{p})}\rightarrow 0
. Consequently,
the necessary condition from Lemma
8.1
is not satisfied.
∎
