Recurrent Primary Intracranial Ewing Sarcoma With Parietal Bone Involvement: A Case Report
Authors: Junaid Imran, Sadiq Ur Rehman, Muhammad Osama, Hania Kalsoom, Pawan Kumar Thada
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionEwing sarcoma is a rare, aggressive malignancy of small round blue cells that typically arises from bone or soft tissue. Primary intracranial involvement is exceedingly uncommon, particularly in adults, and is associated with diagnostic challenges, high recurrence rates, and poor prognosis. Limited data exist regarding optimal management strategies for recurrent intracranial Ewing sarcoma in adult patients.Case SummaryWe report the case of a 24-year-old pregnant female who presented with progressive headaches, vomiting, and left parietal swelling. MRI revealed a ring-enhancing lesion in the left parietal region with surrounding edema. She underwent surgical resection, and histopathology demonstrated sheets of small round blue cells with strong CD99 positivity. Fluorescence in situ hybridization using an EWSR1 break-apart probe confirmed EWSR1 gene rearrangement; however, fusion partner identification was not available. Based on the morphologic and immunohistochemical features in conjunction with EWSR1 rearrangement, a diagnosis of EWSR1-rearranged round cell sarcoma consistent with Ewing sarcoma was made. Adjuvant therapy was delayed due to pregnancy. Despite subsequent multimodal treatment including surgery, VAC-IE chemotherapy, and radiotherapy, the patient developed local recurrence involving the parietal bone within one year and died four months after recurrence.ConclusionThis case highlights the aggressive nature and high recurrence risk of primary intracranial Ewing sarcoma in adults. Diagnostic confirmation requires histopathological and molecular analysis, as radiological findings may mimic other central nervous system tumors. Early diagnosis, timely multimodal therapy, and close surveillance are critical; however, outcomes remain poor, underscoring the need for improved and individualized treatment strategies for adult intracranial Ewing sarcoma.

Introduction

Ewing sarcoma (ES) is a rare and highly malignant tumor of small round blue cells that most commonly arises in the bones or soft tissues. It is a member of the Ewing family of tumors, a group of malignancies with the EWSR1 gene rearrangement, commonly with FLI1, driving aggressive tumor behavior. 1 This family includes Ewing sarcoma and peripheral primitive neuroectodermal tumors (PNET), both characterized by similar genetic markers but differing in levels of neuroectodermal differentiation. ES is now estimated to represent about 1% of pediatric cancers, with an annual incidence of 3 cases per million in people younger than 19 years of age. 2 However, ES in adults is particularly rare, comprising only 5-10% of all ES cases, and exhibits a distinct prognosis and therapeutic challenge because of reduced responsiveness to standard pediatric treatment protocols. 3 Intracranial manifestations are very rare, constituting 1–2% of ES cases while being at higher risk for recurrence and metastasis due to the diagnostic and therapeutic challenges of the intracranial manifestations. 4

Intracranial ES typically is diagnosed by a combination of radiological imaging, often showing a dense extra-axial mass, and histopathological analysis, including immunohistochemistry (CD99 positivity) and EWSR1 translocation molecular testing. This distinction is critical in distinguishing ES from other central nervous system (CNS) tumors with similar morphology. 4 Although advancements in molecular diagnostics and multimodal therapies have contributed to better outcomes, the prognosis remains cautious, especially in recurrent cases where standardized treatment strategies are still lacking. 1

We herein present a rare and challenging case of recurrent primary intracranial Ewing sarcoma involving the left parietal bone, focusing on the diagnostic complexities, therapeutic decisions, and recurrence management.

Case Summary

A 24-year-old female, during her fifth month of pregnancy, was admitted to the Neurosurgery Department with progressively worsening, throbbing headaches and recurrent episodes of vomiting. Over several weeks, her headaches had increased in both frequency and severity and were often accompanied by sudden, unprovoked bouts of vomiting. She also reported persistent dizziness, fatigue, episodes of diplopia, and intermittent blurring of vision due to left eye deviation. Despite these symptoms, she remained fully conscious and exhibited no motor or sensory deficits. Physical examination revealed a compressible 5 cm swelling on the left posterolateral aspect of the skull, centered over the left parietal bone. Neurological assessment showed intact cranial nerves and preserved motor and sensory function. Her Glasgow Coma Scale score was 15 out of 15.

MRI demonstrated a ring-enhancing lesion in the left parietal lobe with a heterogeneously enhancing mural nodule and significant surrounding edema, as shown in (Figure 1). Routine laboratory investigations, including complete blood counts and biochemical parameters, were within normal limits. The combination of headaches, vomiting, a visible parietal swelling, and a ring-enhancing intracranial lesion suggested several possible diagnoses. Histopathological examination revealed sheets of uniform small round blue cells with hyperchromatic nuclei and scant cytoplasm (Figure 3). Immunohistochemical analysis demonstrated strong, diffuse membranous CD99 positivity in more than 90% of tumor cells (Figure 4). The tumor cells were negative for GFAP, Olig2, synaptophysin, SF1, CD56, SSTR2, desmin, myogenin, and NKX2.2, excluding glial, neuroendocrine, and myogenic differentiation. Fluorescence in situ hybridization using an EWSR1 break-apart probe demonstrates a split-signal pattern characterized by one fused signal and spatially separated red and green signals within tumor nuclei, consistent with EWSR1 gene rearrangement (Figure 5). Additional immunohistochemical staining performed on the archived tumor specimen demonstrated strong nuclear positivity for FLI1 and ERG. These findings support ETS family involvement and, in conjunction with CD99 positivity and EWSR1 rearrangement, are highly suggestive of Ewing sarcoma. Additional staging investigations, including bone scans and computed tomography of the chest, abdomen, and pelvis, showed no evidence of metastatic disease.

After receiving the final diagnosis, the patient was discharged with symptomatic management that included corticosteroids and analgesics. Given the patient’s pregnancy, treatment planning was conducted through a coordinated multidisciplinary approach involving neurosurgery, oncology, obstetrics, anesthesiology, radiology, and intensive care teams. Surgical intervention was prioritized to relieve intracranial pressure and obtain definitive diagnosis, while adjuvant chemotherapy and radiotherapy were deferred after careful interdisciplinary discussion to balance optimal maternal management with fetal safety. Six months after the operation and now in the postpartum period, she began a regimen of VAC- IE chemotherapy consisting of vincristine, doxorubicin, cyclophosphamide, ifosfamide, and etoposide, along with radiotherapy.

Approximately one year after her initial surgery, the patient returned with swelling in the left parietal region and recurrent headaches. MRI confirmed a recurrent mass measuring (4.7 x 1.9 x 3 cm) involving the previously treated parietal bone flap, as shown in (Figure 6). In the postoperative period, she was monitored in the intensive care unit. Her vital signs and neurological examination remained stable, and motor and sensory function were preserved throughout her stay. Early mobilization and physical therapy were initiated to reduce the risks associated with prolonged immobility. She was discharged on antibiotics and antiepileptic medication. Although the second surgery achieved adequate local tumor removal and the patient initially recovered well, her disease continued to progress aggressively in the postoperative period. She subsequently developed worsening neurological decline due to rapid intracranial tumor progression, and despite supportive management, she died four months later as a result of advanced disease.

Discussion

Primary intracranial Ewing sarcoma (ES) is an uncommon malignant neoplasm arising from the central nervous system and is classified under the Ewing family of tumors. According to current WHO classification criteria, definitive diagnosis of Ewing sarcoma requires confirmation of an EWSR1–ETS family fusion. 5 In our case, break-apart FISH confirmed EWSR1 rearrangement; however, fusion partner testing was not available. Further immunohistochemical evaluation demonstrated strong nuclear positivity for FLI1 and ERG. These markers are associated with ETS family gene rearrangements and provide supportive evidence for an underlying EWSR1–ETS fusion. Although NKX2.2 is regarded as a relatively sensitive and specific marker for Ewing sarcoma, with reported positivity in approximately 90–95% of cases, NKX2.2-negative Ewing sarcomas have been described in the literature. Therefore, absence of NKX2.2 expression does not exclude the diagnosis, particularly when supported by other findings.6,7 In this context, the combined findings of CD99 positivity, EWSR1 rearrangement, and FLI1 and ERG positivity strongly support the diagnosis of Ewing sarcoma.

While arising primarily from the bone, ES can be seen confined to soft tissues and more rarely it can present as an intracranial tumor without any evidence of primary bony pathology. These cases are rare and thus difficult to diagnose. Intracranial ES is often misdiagnosed as glioma or other CNS tumor solely by imaging. 8 Our case illustrates these diagnostic difficulties since the lesion was believed to be glioma until molecular analysis was performed and ES was identified.

Intracranial ES cases are most common in children while the condition is extremely rare in adults. In general, children with ES are more favorable to the traditional multimodal treatment plans including surgery, chemotherapy, and radiotherapy. 9 It is quite the opposite in adult patients such as ours, where there is decreased treatment compliance. This case exemplifies this trend, as the patient experienced tumor recurrence within a year of initial treatment despite an aggressive approach with surgery, VAC-IE chemotherapy, and radiotherapy.

One important feature of this case is the pregnancy of the patient at the time of the first visit, which exacerbated and postponed therapy. There are few reports of treatment delay in ES because of pregnancy in the literature. Timing is critical in treating malignant tumors in pregnant patients, as any delay can compromise outcomes. 10 This underscores the necessity for personalized therapeutic plans in such complex scenarios to reduce delay without compromising maternal or fetal health.

The tumor in our case demonstrated the classic small, round blue cell morphology of ES, as well as CD99 reactivity, and EWSR1 rearrangement by FISH. Consistent with previous literature, immunohistochemical markers such as CD99 are considered essential for differentiating ES from other CNS tumors that appear similar morphologically. 11 Histopathological findings in our case support the utility of molecular diagnostics for confirming the diagnosis and guiding management strategies.

The clinical course of our patient, particularly the recurrence of the tumor, aligns with reports in the literature, suggesting that intracranial ES exhibits higher rates of local recurrence and metastasis compared to osseous ES. Adult recurrences are frequent and conventional chemotherapy responses are poor. 12 Consistent with our patient, conventional therapies for long- term remission in adult intracranial ES patients showed limited efficacy.

The VAC-IE chemotherapy regimen is standard for treatment of ES, but its efficacy in adult intracranial and recurrent cases is unclear. Novel targeted therapies are studied to determine if they may benefit adults with ES, as standard chemotherapy usually lacks long term success in this population. 13 Lack of large clinical trials in adult intracranial ES, however, leaves the treatment options limited, and there is a need for alternative, targeted approaches to improve outcomes.

Conclusion

Primary intracranial Ewing sarcoma in adults demonstrates aggressive behavior and high recurrence despite multimodal therapy. In this case, delayed adjuvant treatment due to pregnancy further complicated management. Early diagnosis, maximal safe resection, careful trimester-based risk assessment, and timely coordinated care are critical, although overall prognosis remains poor.
