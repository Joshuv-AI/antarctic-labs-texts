Evaluating machine learning approaches for multiple attack classification with improved computational efficiency in IoT networks
Authors: Maher Alharby
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe rapid expansion of Internet of Things (IoT) devices has introduced significant security challenges, making IoT networks increasingly vulnerable to cyber-attacks. This research study develops and evaluates a comprehensive approach for detecting and classifying Denial of Service, Distributed Denial of Service, and Mirai attacks in IoT environments using five supervised machine learning algorithms: Random Forest, Gradient Boosting, Naive Bayes, Decision Tree, and K-Nearest Neighbors, applied to the CICIoT2023 dataset. Our methodology incorporates data preprocessing techniques, including undersampling for class imbalance, and implements three feature selection methods: Chi-square, Principal Component Analysis, and Random Forest Regressor. We thoroughly evaluate model performance by measuring accuracy, precision, sensitivity, and F1-score metrics. In addition, we assess computational efficiency by considering training and prediction times. Our results demonstrate state-of-the-art performance, achieving the highest reported accuracy of 99.99% for the CICIoT2023 dataset, surpassing results reported in existing literature. Furthermore, our best-performing Decision Tree model exhibits significantly improved computational efficiency, with a 98.71% reduction in training time and a 99.53% reduction in prediction time compared to previous studies, while maintaining superior accuracy. This research contributes valuable insights into the effectiveness and efficiency of various machine learning approaches for IoT security, offering practical implications for developing robust and computationally efficient intrusion detection systems in resource-constrained IoT environments.Keywords: IoT security, DoS attacks, Machine learning, Cyber attacks, CICIoT2023 datasetSubject terms: Computer science, Information technology

Introduction

The Internet of Things (IoT) has emerged in our increasingly connected world, integrating various devices and systems to enhance efficiency and convenience across various sectors. IoT technology has enhanced how we interact with our environment and manage resources, from smart homes to industrial applications. This rapid expansion of IoT devices has led to unprecedented growth in data traffic, with global networks experiencing exponential increases in data streams from smart devices1. However, this rapid growth and increasing interconnectivity have introduced significant cybersecurity challenges2,3.

The expanding IoT ecosystem faces cyber threats that exploit the interconnected nature of these devices. These attacks pose risks to individual privacy and data security and have significant consequences for businesses, critical infrastructure, and national security. The complexity of IoT ecosystems, along with the limited processing power and storage capacity of IoT devices, creates unique challenges for implementing effective security measures4.

Recent research in IoT security has made significant progress in developing intrusion detection systems. Studies have explored various machine learning approaches5,6, from traditional algorithms to deep learning techniques. Researchers have explored several datasets, such as Bot-IoT, CICDDoS2019, and the recent CICIoT2023. Their investigations have focused on different types of attacks, specifically targeting Denial of Service (DDoS) detection and the broader classification of multiple attack types.

Despite these advances, several critical limitations persist in existing IoT security research. Many previous studies focus solely on DDoS attacks, neglecting other critical threats such as Denial of Service (DoS) and Mirai attacks. This limited focus restricts a thorough understanding of the diverse threat landscape in IoT security. The issue of class imbalance is often neglected, which can lead to biases in models and compromise the reliability of the results. Additionally, many studies tend to rely on just one feature selection technique or overlook feature selection altogether, which restricts the understanding of how effective different methods can be. There is also a significant gap in considering computational efficiency, as many studies focus mainly on accuracy metrics without assessing training and prediction times. These factors are essential for practical applications in resource-constrained IoT environments.

In this study, we address the critical security challenges faced by IoT ecosystems by developing and evaluating advanced intrusion detection systems. Unlike previous research that often focuses solely on DDoS attacks, our approach encompasses a range of threats, including DoS and Mirai attacks alongside DDoS. Our methodology employs traditional machine learning algorithms, including Random Forest, Gradient Boosting, Naive Bayes, Decision Tree, and K-Nearest Neighbors, to classify network traffic into normal, DoS, DDoS, and Mirai categories using the CICIoT2023 dataset. To enhance the robustness of our approach, we implement three distinct feature selection techniques: Chi-square, Principal Component Analysis (PCA), and Random Forest Regressor (RFR), comparing their impact on model performance and efficiency. Furthermore, our study addresses the often-overlooked issue of class imbalance in IoT security datasets by employing undersampling techniques, thereby improving the reliability and generalizability of our models.

This research makes four primary contributions to the field of IoT security. First, we provide a comprehensive evaluation of traditional machine learning algorithms across multiple attack types using the recent CICIoT2023 dataset. This addresses the limitation of existing studies that focus on a single attack type. Second, we systematically compare three distinct feature selection methods to identify the most effective approach for IoT intrusion detection, offering insights into their relative performance and theoretical advantages. Third, we address the critical research gap in computational efficiency by measuring and analyzing both training and prediction times alongside classification accuracy, providing practical insights for resource-constrained IoT environments. Finally, we implement undersampling techniques to tackle class imbalance issues, ensuring the development of more reliable and generalizable models.

Our comprehensive analysis revealed significant findings that advance the state-of-the-art in IoT intrusion detection. The RFR feature selection method consistently outperformed previous results in the literature. Among the machine learning algorithms evaluated, Random Forest, Decision Tree, and Gradient Boosting stood out for their exceptional performance, all achieving 99.99% accuracy with RFR feature selection across various attack types. Furthermore, our approach demonstrated remarkable improvements in computational efficiency. The Decision Tree model achieved a 98.71% reduction in training time and a 99.53% reduction in prediction time compared to the best reported results in existing studies, while maintaining superior accuracy. The study also revealed important insights into attack classification challenges, particularly the inherent difficulty in distinguishing between DoS and DDoS attacks due to their shared network traffic characteristics, while Mirai attacks were generally well-classified due to their distinct operational patterns. These findings, combined with our superior accuracy and computational efficiency, provide valuable contributions to the field of IoT security.

Particular attention is given to five key supervised learning algorithms: Random Forest, Gradient Boosting, Naive Bayes, Decision Tree, and K-Nearest Neighbors. Each algorithm’s fundamental principles, strengths, and potential applications in IoT security are examined. IoT attacks

The IoT security includes various potential threats and attack vectors. While it is not possible to cover all types of attacks in a single study, our research focuses on three widely recognized and impactful categories in IoT environments: DoS, DDoS, and Mirai botnet attacks. We selected these categories because of their significant effects on IoT networks, their diverse methods of operation, and their representation of both traditional and emerging threats. By concentrating on these specific attacks, we aim to provide a clear and thorough analysis of the critical security challenges facing IoT systems today. In this section, we will explore the characteristics, mechanisms, and potential impacts of these attacks, providing essential background information for our subsequent analysis and findings.

DDoS attacks are a significant threat to internet-based applications, aiming to disrupt services by overwhelming target systems with excessive traffic7,8. These attacks utilize compromised devices to send large volumes of packets, causing systems to become unresponsive to legitimate user requests. The CICIoT2023 dataset provides detailed subcategories of DDoS attacks, including various flood and fragmentation types. Common flood attacks include SYN flood, which exhausts IoT device resources with half-open TCP connections7; UDP flood, involving large unauthorized UDP packets7; and ICMP flood, which aims to take networks offline9. HTTP flood attacks generate traffic resembling normal network activity, making detection challenging10. Slowloris attacks gradually increase open connection requests, leading to system congestion10. Synonymous IP flood attacks target DNS servers with high-volume requests for non-existent domains11. Fragmentation attacks involve sending packets that exceed the allowed maximum size. These include ACK fragmentation, ICMP fragmentation, and UDP fragmentation attacks. These attacks aim to consume victim resources by forcing systems to process fragmented packets containing random or irrelevant information12.

DoS attacks are a persistent threat to network security, designed to cause services unavailable to legitimate users. Unlike DDoS attacks, which use multiple sources, DoS attacks typically originate from a single source13. The CICIoT2023 dataset includes several types of DoS attacks, each exploiting vulnerabilities in network protocols and services. TCP flood attacks, a common type of DoS, inundate the target system with a large number of TCP packets, often with forged source IP addresses. This flood of packets consumes server resources, making it unable to process legitimate requests14. HTTP flood attacks target web servers by sending multiple HTTP requests, exhausting the server’s ability to respond to honest user traffic. These attacks are particularly challenging to detect as they mimic normal user behavior15. SYN flood attacks target vulnerabilities in the TCP three-way handshake mechanism to disrupt network communication. An attacker initiates numerous SYN requests without completing the TCP handshake, resulting in multiple half-open connections that drain its resources16. On the other hand, UDP flood attacks leverage the UDP protocol’s connectionless nature. By sending many UDP packets to random ports on the target system, the attacker forces the system to repeatedly check for applications listening on those ports, potentially leading to system unresponsiveness17. These DoS attack types highlight the diverse techniques employed by attackers to disrupt network services.

The Mirai botnet attack represents a significant threat to IoT security, explicitly targeting vulnerable Internet-connected devices. Unlike traditional DoS or DDoS attacks, Mirai-based attacks exploit many poorly secured IoT devices to create a powerful botnet capable of launching massive attacks18. The CICIoT2023 dataset includes several Mirai attack variants, each employing different techniques to overwhelm target systems. A variant of the Mirai attack, known as GREIP flood, combines the use of GRE (Generic Routing Encapsulation) protocol packets with IP flooding techniques. This attack type encapsulates malicious traffic within GRE packets, potentially bypassing standard traffic filters and overwhelming the target’s network infrastructure19. Greeth flood attacks, another Mirai variant, specifically target Ethernet frames. By flooding the network with a high volume of Ethernet traffic, this attack can flood network bandwidth and overload network equipment19. The UDPPlain attack, a more straightforward but effective Mirai attack type, floods the target with a high volume of UDP packets. These packets often have spoofed source IP addresses, making it challenging to filter out malicious traffic. Due to the connectionless nature of UDP, the attack can quickly overwhelm the target’s ability to process incoming data19. These Mirai attack variants demonstrate the evolving complexity of IoT-based attacks, highlighting the need for comprehensive security measures that can detect and mitigate a wide range of attack strategies in IoT environments.

Supervised machine learning

Supervised machine learning algorithms play a crucial role in intrusion detection systems, particularly in IoT environments where the ability to accurately classify network traffic is essential. These algorithms learn from labeled training data, where each instance is paired with a known outcome, to create models capable of predicting the class of new instances. In the context of IoT security, supervised learning algorithms are trained on datasets containing both normal network traffic and various types of attacks, enabling them to distinguish between benign and malicious activities. The effectiveness of these algorithms lies in their ability to generalize patterns learned from historical data to new, potentially evolving threats. Our study focuses on five widely used supervised machine learning algorithms, each with its unique approach to classification:

Random Forest (RF)20 is an ensemble method that constructs multiple decision trees and aggregates their predictions. It operates by building many decision trees at training time and outputting the class of the individual trees. RF is particularly good at handling high-dimensional data and provides a measure of feature importance, making it valuable for understanding which network characteristics are most expressive of attacks.

Gradient Boosting (GB)21 builds a series of weak learners, typically decision trees, in a stage-wise fashion. It works by iteratively training new models to correct the errors of the existing ensemble, effectively learning from its mistakes. GB is known for its high predictive accuracy and excellent performance, making it a strong candidate for complex classification tasks such as intrusion detection.

Naive Bayes (NB)22 is a probabilistic classifier that outperforms in scenarios with limited training data. Despite its simplicity and the “naive” assumption that all features are independent, NB often performs exceptionally well in text classification and spam filtering. In the context of network security, its ability to handle high-dimensional data efficiently can be beneficial.

Decision Tree (DT)23 creates models based on simple decision rules inferred from data features. It builds classification or regression models as a tree structure, breaking down a dataset into smaller subsets while incrementally developing an associated decision tree. The process used by DTs to make decisions can be easily visualized and understood. This quality is essential in security applications where transparency is crucial.

K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN)24 is an instance-based learning algorithm that classifies objects according to the majority class among their nearest neighbors. The algorithm assumes that data points with similar features are assigned similar labels. KNN is conceptually simple yet effective, especially when the decision boundary is irregular. In network security, it can be beneficial for detecting abnormalities based on the similarity of traffic patterns to known attacks or benign samples.

By comparing these diverse algorithms, we aim to thoroughly evaluate their performance in detecting and categorizing DoS, DDoS, and Mirai attacks in IoT networks, offering insights into their respective strengths and limitations in this critical application domain. This comparison will shed light on which algorithms are most effective for different aspects of IoT security, potentially guiding future developments in intrusion detection systems.

The emergence of new IoT protocols often results in numerous daily attacks, which can compromise computing processes. Unlike local network attacks, assaults on IoT devices can spread over large areas with severe consequences. The diversity and distribution of IoT applications further complicate IoT security, making it more challenging and complicated than traditional cybersecurity approaches25. This study is dedicated to IoT security and aims to adapt well-established techniques such as anomaly detection and network behavior analysis to enhance IoT network security. The unique characteristics and limitations of IoT devices, including limited processing power and diverse communication protocols, justify the focus on IoT-specific literature. These distinctive features require customized security solutions to address the unique challenges within the IoT ecosystem. Almaraz-Rivera et al.26 developed an intrusion detection system for IoT networks using the Bot-IoT dataset. They addressed the class imbalance problem by randomly selecting consecutive flows for each attack type. They created three feature subsets to evaluate the impact of temporal data. These subsets were evaluated using seven algorithms: RF, DT, Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM), Multilayer Perceptron (MLP), Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU), Recurrent Neural Network (RNN), and Support Vector Machine (SVM) for both binary and multiclass classification of DDoS and DoS attacks. The study achieved an average accuracy of over 99% across the experiments, with binary classification reaching 99.89-99.972% and multiclass 99.862-99.945%. Decision Tree and MLP emerged as the best-performing models, with Decision Tree capable of processing over 30,000 flows per second, demonstrating real-time feasibility.

Alzahrani et al.27 explore the application of various machine learning algorithms to detect DDoS attacks within network traffic. The study uses the CICDDoS2019 dataset to evaluate six machine learning models: KNN, SVM, NB, DT, RF, and Logistic Regression (LR). The RFR method was employed as a feature selection technique, which improved the accuracy of machine learning methods in identifying attack traffic. The DT and RF algorithms showed the highest accuracy, both achieving 99%. However, DT outperformed RF concerning computation time, making it a more efficient option for real-time detection.

Kumar et al.28 proposed an artificial neural network algorithm incorporating DT, RF, KNN, and NB algorithms, utilizing the Bot-IOT dataset. They subsequently evaluated this algorithm using traffic data from a custom testbed comprising 20 IoT devices. The resulting dataset contained 3 million DoS, 5 million DDoS, and 2 million normal traffic samples. The results revealed that KNN and the hybrid approach achieved the highest accuracies at 99.466% and 99.611%, respectively. The artificial neural network was also independently tested with various activation functions, yielding the best accuracy at 99.529%. These comparative analyses concluded that machine learning techniques perform better in resource-constrained systems, while deep learning algorithms excel in environments with extensive data and computational resources.

Patil et al.8 present a real-time system for detecting DDoS attacks using Spark Streaming and Kafka. The system employs distributed machine learning algorithms from Spark MLlib, including RF, DT, Multinomial Logistic Regression (MLR), and NB. It was evaluated using the CICDDoS2019 dataset, including benign traffic and six DDoS attacks. The RF algorithm achieved the best results with an average classification accuracy of 89.05% for seven classes.

Jia et al.29 developed FlowGuard, an intelligent edge defense mechanism against IoT DDoS attacks. The system has two main components: a flow filter and a flow handler. The flow filter filters malicious flows based on rules created by the flow handler and detects attacks based on traffic variations. The flow handler identifies and classifies malicious flows using two machine learning models: LSTM for attack identification and Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) for attack classification. The Information Gain (IG) feature selection method was employed to enhance the efficiency and effectiveness of the proposed models. FlowGuard was evaluated using both simulated data and the CICDDoS2019 dataset. When evaluated on the CICDDoS2019 dataset, the system reached 99.9% accuracy in detecting attacks and 98.9% accuracy in classifying attack types.

Seifousadati et al.30 investigate using machine learning algorithms to detect DDoS attacks on IoT devices using the CICDDoS2019 dataset. The study emphasizes feature selection and preprocessing to enhance the detection capabilities of the models. Specifically, they employed a feature selection method that removed columns with zero variance and low correlation to improve model performance. Several algorithms were evaluated, with Adaptive Boosting (ADA) and Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGB) emerging as the most effective, achieving 100% accuracy in identifying DDoS attacks.

Becerra-Suarez et al.31 provide an in-depth evaluation of machine learning methods for DDoS detection using the CICDDoS2019 dataset. They evaluated six algorithms: RF, DT, ADA, XGB, MLP, and Dense Neural Network (DNN). The initial 80 features were reduced to 22 through the Pearson correlation coefficient (PCC) method. For hyperparameter optimization, the authors applied the Tree of Parzen Estimators (TPE), finding it more efficient than conventional grid search approaches. The RF classifier emerged as the top performer, achieving 99.97% accuracy in detecting DDoS attacks. XGB closely followed with 99.95% accuracy, while DT achieved 99.88%. The other algorithms (ADA, MLP, DNN) showed accuracies ranging from 99.59% to 99.70%.

The CICIoT2023 dataset, introduced by Neto et al.32, simulates large-scale cyber-attacks on a network of 105 IoT devices. It covers seven attack categories: DDoS, DoS, reconnaissance, web-based, brute force, spoofing, and Mirai. To demonstrate its utility, the dataset was divided into training (80%) and testing (20%) sets, then normalized for machine learning applications. Five machine learning methods were evaluated: LR, Perceptron (PER), ADA, RF, and DNN. While overall performance decreased when classifying multiple attack groups, RF and DNN maintained relatively high accuracy and F1 scores. The models performed well in classifying attacks with numerous occurrences, such as DDoS, DoS, and Mirai. However, they struggled with less common attacks like web-based and brute force, often misclassifying them as benign or other attack types. The multiclass classification showed decreased performance for benign traffic, recon, and spoofing, possibly due to similar traffic patterns. This phenomenon deserves further investigation in future research.

Abbas et al.33 focuses on applying deep learning models to detect and classify cyber-attacks within IoT networks. The study explores various deep learning models, including DNN, CNN, and RNN, to identify cyber-attacks in IoT environments. Using the CICDIoT2023 dataset, the study implements data preprocessing techniques such as robust scaling and label encoding for categorical variables to enhance model performance. The experimental results show that among the different models tested, the RNN model achieved the highest accuracy at 96.56%, indicating its effectiveness in detecting cyber-attacks compared to other methods.

Jony et al.34 explore the application of LSTM networks for intrusion detection in IoT environments. Utilizing the extensive CIC-IoT2023 dataset, the study demonstrates that the LSTM-based model can detect known and evolving cyber-attack patterns. The model achieved an accuracy rate of 98.75% and an F1 score of 98.59%.

Wang et al.35 introduced the Deep Learning-Bidirectional Long Short-Term Memory (DL-BiLSTM) algorithm, a lightweight model for detecting IoT attacks. This approach combines DNN and BiLSTM techniques. The research utilized CICIDS2017, N-BaIoT, and CICIoT2023 datasets. They employed Incremental Principal Component Analysis (IPCA) as a feature selection technique to reduce the number of dataset features. The modelś computational complexity was further decreased through dynamic quantization. When evaluated against other deep learning algorithms using the CICIoT2023 dataset, the DL-BiLSTM algorithm achieved the highest accuracy rate of 93.13% in detecting attack types.

Yaras et al.36 present a new hybrid deep learning algorithm for detecting DDoS attacks in IoT environments. The algorithm combines one-dimensional CNN and LSTM networks. It was evaluated using two recent datasets: CICIoT2023 and TON_IOT. The main contributions include developing a high-accuracy intrusion detection system for big data, excelling in both binary and multiclass classification. Using the CICIoT2023 dataset, the model achieved 99.995% accuracy for binary classification and 99.96% for multiclass classification. On the TON_IOT dataset, the model achieved an accuracy of 98.75% for binary classification.

Nkoro et al.37 utilize DNN for detecting cyber threats in Metaverse learning platforms. The study uses recent IoT cybersecurity datasets to model and evaluate the DNN’s performance. The study uses the Synthetic Minority Over-sampling (SMOTE) technique for data balancing and a Decision Tree Classifier (DTC) for feature selection. The DNN model achieves high binary classification accuracy across different datasets: 99.9% for EdgeIIoT, 99.10% for CICIoT2023, and 99.9% for UNSW-NB15. For multiclass classification, the model achieves 71.22% accuracy on EdgeIIoT (15 classes), 70.53% on CICIoT2023 (34 classes), and 84.74% on UNSW-NB15 (9 classes).

Khan et al.38 present a study on anomaly detection in IoT-based healthcare using the CICIoT2023 dataset. The research evaluates five machine learning algorithms: RF, ADA, LR, PER, and DNN. The dataset was preprocessed and balanced using the SMOTE technique. Features were selected using the PCC selection method. RF emerged as the best-performing algorithm, achieving an accuracy of 95.55% for the 8-Class experiment. The prediction times were decreased from 13.08s to 6.64s after applying the feature selection method.

Recent advancements highlight the trend towards more sophisticated and specialized models. Nandanwar and Katarya39 developed a deep learning intrusion detection system for Industrial IoT. Their method addresses real-time processing and high accuracy needs for critical infrastructure. The system detects various attacks efficiently and is suitable for resource-constrained IoT devices.

Saheed et al.40 proposed the IoT-Defender framework, which combines a Modified Genetic Algorithm (MGA) for feature selection with a fine-tuned LSTM network for intrusion detection in edge-enabled IoT networks. This approach achieved 99.41% accuracy on the BoT-IoT dataset, demonstrating the effectiveness of hybrid models in resource-constrained environments. In another work, Saheed et al.41 introduced a privacy-preserving framework for CPS-IIoT networks that uses a Bidirectional LSTM (BiLSTM) with a scaled dot-product attention mechanism. This model, which incorporates Pearson Correlation Coefficient (PCC) and agglomerative clustering for privacy, achieved outstanding performance on the UNSW-NB15 and X-IIoTID datasets. Further pushing the boundaries of explainability and privacy, Saheed et al.42 developed a privacy-preserving DNN framework that employs Shapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) to interpret model decisions. This model achieved near-perfect accuracy on the Edge-IIoTset and X-IIoTID datasets while maintaining fast training times, offering a robust solution for real-time threat detection in CPS-IoT networks.

However, existing studies in attack detection for IoT environments have notable limitations. Many of these studies focus solely on DDoS attacks, overlooking other critical threats such as DoS and Mirai attacks. Furthermore, many researchers have not addressed the crucial issue of class imbalance in their datasets, which can lead to biased models. Another common drawback is the insufficient exploration of feature selection methods, as many studies rely on a single approach or skip this step altogether. Furthermore, while some studies employ advanced deep learning models, they often fail to consider important practical factors, such as training and prediction times.

Our work addresses these limitations and makes several unique contributions to the field. Unlike many previous studies, we extended our scope to include DoS and Mirai attacks alongside DDoS, providing a more comprehensive approach to IoT security. We utilize the CICIoT2023 dataset, which is among the latest and most comprehensive datasets for IoT security studies. We employ an undersampling technique for data balancing to address the common class imbalance issue. Our feature selection approach is more robust, combining PCA, RFR, and Chi-square methods, offering a more diverse and potentially more effective strategy than the single-method approaches used in most existing studies. In addition, we focus on training and prediction times, which are crucial factors for real-world implementation. We comprehensively evaluate different approaches using a combination of traditional machine learning algorithms (RF, GB, NB, DT, and KNN). This approach significantly enhances the current understanding of IoT security and provides practical insights for implementing effective attack detection systems in real-world situations.

Research methodology

This section outlines the comprehensive approach adopted in this study for classifying IoT network attacks using machine learning techniques. Our methodology encompasses several essential stages, including dataset acquisition, data preprocessing, feature selection, and evaluating various machine learning classifiers. This systematic approach ensures a thorough exploration of the CICIoT2023 dataset’s potential for identifying and categorizing IoT-based cyber threats. Figure 1 presents the study’s methodological framework, depicting a flowchart that outlines the workflow from data preprocessing to model evaluation, highlighting the interconnected steps and logical progression.

Proposed methodological framework.

CICIoT2023 dataset

The CICIoT2023 dataset, produced by Neto et al.32, is constructed from an IoT topology consisting of 105 devices (67 IoT devices and 38 Zigbee and Z-Wave devices). This arrangement is intended to replicate a realistic smart home environment. The testbed includes smart home devices, cameras, and sensors, all configured to execute and record multiple types of attacks. Researchers generated 33 distinct attack types, which are organized into seven categories: DDoS, DoS, reconnaissance, web-based, brute-force, spoofing, and Mirai. The dataset includes both benign and malicious network traffic, with 47 features extracted from each network flow. Regarding sample distribution, DDoS attacks are the most prevalent, accounting for 73% of the dataset, followed by DoS attacks at 17%. In comparison, Web-based and Brute-force attacks represent less than 1% of the total data. This study concentrates on three primary types of attacks: DoS, DDoS, and Mirai. It also considers benign traffic, as these categories provide the most substantial number of data samples. Other attack classes, such as spoofing, web-based attacks, and brute force attacks, have limited data samples, which could compromise the performance and reliability of machine learning models if included in the analysis.

Data preprocessing

The preprocessing operation handles the dataset before using it in the suggested models. It involved handling null and infinite records, dropping all duplicate records, balancing the different classes in the dataset using the undersampling technique, and transforming all non-numeric values using Label Encoder. Finally, the data was scaled to convert the feature vector to a more suitable format for machine learning models.

Data cleaning: Our data preparation began with examining the dataset to ensure its integrity and suitability for machine learning algorithms. We systematically reviewed all rows, identifying and removing any rows that contained null or infinite values, which could potentially skew our analysis. We also conducted a duplicate check, removing any redundant entries to avoid bias in our models.

Data balancing: Given that the number of attack samples significantly outweighs the benign samples in our dataset, it is crucial to balance the data to achieve optimal results. For this purpose, we employed an undersampling technique to equalize the number of samples across all classes.

Undersampling involves reducing the number of samples in the majority classes (attack samples) to match the number in the minority class (benign samples). This approach helps prevent bias towards the majority classes and ensures that our machine learning models can learn equally from all classes43. In our study, we employed random undersampling, which randomly removes samples from the majority classes until their sizes match that of the minority class.

While undersampling effectively addresses class imbalance and reduces computational overhead, it has potential drawbacks that should be considered. The main limitation is the loss of potentially valuable information from the majority classes, which can diminish the model’s ability to capture the full diversity of attack patterns. In addition, undersampling may lead to overfitting if the remaining samples are not representative of the entire population. However, we chose undersampling over alternative techniques like SMOTE for several reasons: (1) it preserves the authenticity of real network traffic data without introducing synthetic samples that might not accurately reflect actual IoT attack characteristics; (2) it significantly reduces computational complexity and training time, which is crucial for resource-constrained IoT environments; and (3) given the large size of the CICIoT2023 dataset, there are still sufficient samples remaining after undersampling to maintain model generalizability.

After applying the undersampling technique to our imbalanced dataset, we achieved equal samples for each class. Specifically, each class (DoS, DDoS, Mirai, and benign) was reduced or maintained to contain exactly 302,896 records. This resulted in a total of 1,211,584 records in our balanced dataset. This balanced distribution ensures that our machine learning models are not biased towards any particular class during training. The choice of these three feature selection methods is justified by their complementary approaches to feature evaluation in the context of IoT security. Chi-square testing provides a statistical framework for assessing categorical features, making it particularly effective for detecting attacks in IoT systems, where many features relate to discrete network states and protocols. Its primary strength lies in identifying features that show strong independence from attack classes46. On the other hand, PCA provides dimensionality reduction while preserving variance. This method is useful for analyzing high-dimensional IoT network traffic data that often includes correlated attributes, such as packet sizes and timing patterns47. However, a notable drawback of PCA is the potential loss of interpretability, making it challenging to trace how original features contribute to attack detection. RFR employs ensemble learning with multiple decision trees to evaluate feature importance, effectively capturing non-linear relationships and interactions in network traffic patterns20. It handles mixed data types and reveals complex dependencies, but can be computationally intensive and may favor features with more categories.

Chi-Square48,49 evaluates the independence between a feature and the class label, selecting the top 15 features with the highest chi-square scores. To enhance the performance of the proposed machine learning models, the Chi-square feature selection method was applied to determine the most relevant features in our dataset. The features with the strongest correlations were identified by computing the Chi-square statistic for each feature in relation to the target variable. Machine learning classifier

The study focuses on implementing and evaluating various supervised machine learning models for classification, including RF, DT, KNN, GB, and NB.

We utilized the Train-Test Split method to divide the dataset into training (80%) and testing (20%) subsets. We then conducted model tuning using a random search technique to optimize model performance. This approach allowed us to identify the best parameter values for each model, thereby maximizing their performance.

We evaluate the effectiveness of our proposed model using the CICIoT2023 dataset. The assessment employs six key performance metrics: Accuracy, Precision, Sensitivity, F1-score, Training Time, and Prediction Time. These metrics comprehensively evaluate the model’s performance and computational efficiency, offering a robust framework for evaluating and classifying IoT network attacks. To validate the performance improvements, we conducted statistical significance testing using t-tests. Accuracy: This metric reflects the proportion of correct predictions made by the model:

FP (False Positives) are negative cases that the model incorrectly labels as positive. FN (False Negatives) are positive cases that the model incorrectly labels as negative.

From a methodological perspective, our study stands out for its comprehensive evaluation of multiple machine learning algorithms and feature selection methods. Although many previous studies typically focus on a single algorithm or a limited set of techniques, we compare five different classifiers (RF, GB, NB, DT, KNN) and three feature selection techniques (Chi-square, PCA, RFR). Regarding data preprocessing, our study addresses the critical issue of class imbalance through undersampling, a consideration often overlooked in previous research. Our use of undersampling offers an alternative approach to handling imbalanced datasets in IoT intrusion detection.

From a practical deployment perspective, our study is one of the few to report training and prediction times, with our best-performing model (DT) demonstrating superior computational efficiency. Our model achieves a training time of 9.1s and a prediction time of 0.03s, significantly outperforming the results reported by Wang et al.35. This emphasis on computational efficiency is crucial for real-world IoT intrusion detection systems, where resource constraints and real-time processing requirements are essential considerations.

Analyzing the classification patterns in our results, we identified important insights into IoT attack detection challenges. The observed confusion patterns in our study, particularly between DDoS and DoS attacks, reveal the inherent difficulty in distinguishing these attack types. The frequent misclassification between these two attack categories can be attributed to their similar characteristics in network traffic. Both DDoS and DoS attacks aim to overwhelm target systems, resulting in comparable patterns in packet transmission rates, connection establishment frequencies, and bandwidth usage. This observation reflects a broader challenge in cybersecurity, where similar attack mechanisms often generate overlapping traffic signatures, making precise classification challenging even for advanced machine learning models. In contrast, Mirai attacks demonstrated superior classification accuracy across all models due to their unique operational characteristics. These attacks specifically target IoT devices through distinct propagation patterns, unique payload signatures associated with the exploitation of IoT devices, and particular communication protocols used for coordinating the botnet.

In conclusion, our study achieves the highest accuracy in multi-class IoT attack detection and provides a more comprehensive evaluation of machine learning techniques. It addresses data imbalance and considers computational efficiency. These contributions offer valuable insights for developing robust and efficient intrusion detection systems for IoT environments.

Practical deployment challenges

The transition from laboratory-validated intrusion detection models to real-world IoT environments introduces challenges beyond computational efficiency. Although our decision tree approach achieves 99.99% accuracy in controlled settings, real deployment must address scalability, security, and privacy.

Deploying decision trees in IoT requires adaptability to diverse devices and evolving threats5. Their interpretability and low retraining cost are beneficial, but frequent updates are needed to counter new attack vectors. Scalability remains a central challenge for large IoT networks. Our lightweight model supports edge deployment, but effective model distribution and synchronization demand robust hierarchical strategies. Adversarial attacks on machine learning models are a major concern53. Although decision trees are more interpretable than black-box models, strong resilience requires adversarial training, input validation, and secure updates. Privacy-preserving techniques in IoT security offer both challenges and opportunities. Our approach analyzes raw network traffic, which may expose sensitive information. Incorporating federated learning or differential privacy could enhance detection while protecting user data. However, maintaining 99.99% accuracy with these methods is challenging due to the added noise or computational overhead54,55.

Conclusion

The rapid expansion of IoT devices has introduced significant security challenges, making these interconnected systems increasingly vulnerable to cyber-attacks. The complexity of IoT ecosystems, combined with limited computational resources and diverse communication protocols, necessitates innovative approaches to intrusion detection that balance effectiveness with efficiency. Our research has explored machine learning techniques to detect and classify DoS, DDoS, and Mirai attacks in IoT environments. The main contributions of this study include: (1) a comprehensive evaluation of traditional machine learning algorithms across multiple attack types using the recent CICIoT2023 dataset, (2) systematic comparison of three distinct feature selection methods (Chi-square, PCA, and RFR) with theoretical justification for their selection, (3) addressing critical research gaps in computational efficiency by measuring both training and prediction times, and (4) implementation of undersampling techniques to handle class imbalance issues while maintaining model generalizability.

Our analysis revealed several key findings that advance the state of the art in IoT intrusion detection. With RFR feature selection, the RF, DT, and GB algorithms consistently outperformed existing studies, achieving a near-perfect accuracy of 99.99% across multiple attack types. Notably, our DT model achieved the highest accuracy and exhibited remarkable computational efficiency, offering a 98.71% reduction in training time and a 99.53% reduction in prediction time compared to the best-reported results in existing studies. Furthermore, we identified specific challenges in attack classification, particularly the complexity involved in differentiating between DDoS and DoS attacks due to their similar network traffic characteristics. These results confirm the importance of algorithmic choice and feature selection in developing efficient and accurate intrusion detection systems for IoT environments.

For our future work, we plan to expand our research in several key areas. We intend to implement deep learning models, such as CNN and LSTM networks, to identify more complex attack patterns. Additionally, we see potential in integrating our high-efficiency traditional machine learning methods with emerging edge computing architectures. This integration could lead to the development of distributed IoT security systems that maintain real-time responsiveness while ensuring detection accuracy. We also aim to enhance real-time detection capabilities by developing streaming data processing techniques that can analyze actual network traffic. In addition, we plan to create privacy-preserving extensions of our methodology by incorporating approaches such as federated learning, differential privacy, and homomorphic encryption. Furthermore, we will expand our study to encompass a wider range of IoT attack types and assess the performance of our models on diverse IoT datasets to ensure their generalizability. Exploring the scalability and adaptability of our approach across various IoT ecosystems is another critical research direction. We intend to examine how our methodology can be tailored to specialized IoT domains, including industrial control systems, smart city infrastructures, and healthcare monitoring networks, where attack patterns and resource constraints may differ significantly from those in general IoT environments. Finally, based on our findings, we expect to develop and deploy a lightweight user-friendly intrusion detection system suitable for resource-constrained IoT devices.
