Co-sleeping as a contributor to early childhood caries: Behavioral pathways and clinical outcomes
Authors: Gloria Bayón-Hernández, Patricia Solano-Mendoza, Beatriz Solano-Mendoza, Juan Gómez-Salgado, Javier Fagundo-Rivera, Luis El Khoury-Moreno, David Ribas-Pérez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Early childhood caries (ECC) is a multifactorial chronic disease strongly influenced by feeding behaviors and oral hygiene practices. Co-sleeping is a common caregiving practice that may increase ECC risk by facilitating nocturnal feeding and limiting adequate nighttime oral care. This study aimed to evaluate the association between co-sleeping, related behavioral factors, and the presence and severity of ECC in children aged 1 to 6 years. A retrospective observational study was conducted using clinical and radiographic records from a pediatric dentistry clinic. The sample included 150 children aged 12 to 72 months. Parents completed a validated questionnaire on feeding practices, dietary habits, oral hygiene routines, sugar intake, and co-sleeping behaviors. ECC was assessed using the decayed, missing, and filled teeth index according to World Health Organization criteria. Logistic regression analyses were performed to identify associations between co-sleeping, behavioral variables, and ECC outcomes. Co-sleeping was reported in 18.7% of children. Co-sleeping children showed significantly higher mean decayed, missing, and filled teeth index scores than non–co-sleepers (7.14 vs 3.49). Co-sleeping was significantly associated with nighttime breastfeeding on demand, higher carbohydrate consumption, and inadequate oral hygiene practices (P < .05). In most co-sleeping cases (89.3%), children fell asleep with milk remaining in the oral cavity. Co-sleeping was associated with an approximately 2-fold increase in ECC risk, and behaviors that may increase the risk and severity of ECC, particularly nocturnal feeding and insufficient oral hygiene. Targeted parental education on nighttime feeding and oral care practices may help reduce ECC risk. Prospective studies are warranted to confirm these findings.

1. Introduction

The American Academy of Pediatric Dentistry defines early childhood caries (ECC) as the presence of 1 or more tooth surfaces with decay, cavitated or not, teeth lost from decay, or fillings on any surface of children’s primary teeth from birth to 71 months of age. Its first clinical manifestation is the appearance of a “white spot” on the tooth surface.[1–5]

When these lesions occur in infants under 3 years of age, they are classified as severe ECC.[6] The etiology of caries has been widely studied. Early research identified 3 main factors: diet, dental plaque, with Streptococcus mutans as the primary causative agent, and host susceptibility.[7] Later, time was recognized as a fourth important factor.[8,9] Among these, diet is considered the main determinant in the development of cavities.[10,11] Caries is also influenced by sociocultural factors, such as caregivers’ knowledge and behaviors.[12,13] More recently, caries has been discussed as a dysbiosis rather than a strictly infectious disease. A longitudinal study showed that oral microbial dysbiosis can precede dental caries by up to 3 years. Higher levels of Prevotella and Leptotrichia were associated with future caries, highlighting the importance of studying dental plaque to detect early dysbiotic changes.[14]

Breastfeeding provides numerous benefits, including strengthening the infant’s immune system, protecting against various diseases,[15] and promoting oral and functional development through the act of sucking.[15,16] While breastfeeding remains highly recommended, evidence suggests that prolonged breastfeeding beyond 12 months, especially when combined with an increased number of erupted teeth at 18 months, may be associated with a higher risk of dental caries later in childhood. These findings highlight the importance of early dental visits and guidance on breastfeeding practices, diet, and oral hygiene beginning with the eruption of primary teeth, which may help support oral health throughout childhood.[17–19] From a preventive perspective, and in order to preserve oral health, it is advisable to gradually reduce night feedings after the eruption of the first tooth. It is also essential to clean the child’s teeth after each feeding and leave intervals between feedings to promote dental remineralization after the acid attack induced by food intake.[20]

Several authors have described a close relationship between the presence of carious lesions and co-sleeping, defined as the habit of infants and young children sleeping with 1 or both parents.[21] Co-sleeping promotes breastfeeding on demand, which can contribute to the development of ECC. The suction directs the milk to the upper teeth and, when the child falls asleep with milk in the mouth, exposure to cariogenic bacteria is prolonged. In addition, decreased salivary flow during sleep reduces the protective action of saliva.[21,22]

Co-sleeping seeks to promote the baby’s well-being and facilitate their peace of mind during sleep.[23] A nocturnal feeding pattern is common that begins with the baby crying, followed by breastfeeding, after which the child falls asleep again with milk in the mouth.[21,22] The problem lies in the fact that many mothers who practice co-sleeping are unaware of the risks associated with this behavior. However, when they receive adequate information and remain awake during nighttime breastfeeding, it is possible to maintain proper oral hygiene and reduce the risk of cavities.[21]

Recommendations on oral hygiene after breastfeeding, which seek to avoid prolonged exposure of teeth to milk residues, are becoming more and more widespread. Establishing proper brushing and cleaning habits before bed can decrease susceptibility to nocturnal caries.[11] Taking simple measures, such as keeping a clean towel and water next to the bed to clean the tooth surface after each feeding, can remove milk residue without interrupting the baby’s sleep.[21,24] It is important to note that co-sleeping is not necessarily associated with breastfeeding on demand, although this is considerably facilitated.[21,25]

The aim of this study was to analyze the relationship between co-sleeping and caries in early childhood, considering the duration of co-sleeping, nocturnal breastfeeding on demand, and family factors such as the number of siblings.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Study design and sample

A retrospective observational study was conducted involving children aged 1 to 6 years who received care within the framework of the Master’s Degree in Paediatric Dentistry during 2017. The study was carried out in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki, and the protocol was approved by the corresponding Ethics Committee.

Due to its retrospective design, no a priori restrictions were placed on the sample size, as the aim was to include as many eligible patients as possible, taking into account the relatively low prevalence of ECC. Patients and the general public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting, or dissemination of this research.

2.2. Inclusion criteria

Children between the ages of 1 and 6 (up to 72 months), with written informed consent given by parents or legal caregivers.

2.3. Exclusion criteria

Patients not evaluated in the context of the Master’s Degree in Paediatric Dentistry.

2.4. Study and analysis variables

A general medical history was prepared for each patient, and the parents or caregivers completed a questionnaire validated by experts in pediatric dentistry. The presence of caries was determined retrospectively from the radiographs and the clinical evaluation recorded in the records, calculating the decayed and filled teeth (dft) score. Radiographic and clinical evaluation and data collection were performed by a single examiner under standardized conditions, in accordance with the World Health Organization Oral Health Survey Manual.[26]

The variables included demographic data, systemic history, oral hygiene habits, type of diet, sugar consumption, use of sweetened pacifiers, and co-sleeping practice associated with nocturnal breastfeeding.

The statistical analysis, performed with the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences version 27 program (IBM), combined descriptive and inferential statistics, using 95% confidence intervals. The personal information was processed in accordance with current data protection regulations.

Regarding caries analysis, as the International Caries Detection and Assessment System could not be applied due to the retrospective nature of the study, the dft index was used. The detection was carried out by a single calibrated operator by visual inspection and exploratory probe, following the recommendations of the World Health Organization; doubtful cases were classified as not representative of ECC.[26]

3. Results

The final sample of the study was composed of 150 children (94 males, 63%; 56 females, 37%), with an age range of 12 to 72 months and a mean of 52 ± 13.47 months. Of this total, 28 patients practiced co-sleeping (19 men and 9 women).

Variables such as carbohydrate consumption were evaluated, classified into 3 levels (low, medium, and high) according to the weekly intake of sugary substances. Most mothers reported offering sugary products (juices, pastries, or sweets) at least once a day. Oral hygiene was assessed based on daily brushing frequency and the use of fluoride toothpastes. The regression model identified that the child’s birth order, age, type of breastfeeding (breastfeeding or artificial), and oral hygiene habits were covariates significantly associated with co-sleeping (P < .05), so they can be considered possible predictors of this practice. In addition, the risk of co-sleeping increased proportionally to the time during which this practice was maintained, and it was observed that 15 of the 28 children who slept with their parents continued with this practice at the time of the study, regardless of their age.

It was observed that the number of siblings and the position of the child within the family were identified as relevant factors in the practice of co-sleeping. Specifically, greater parental attention was noted in younger infants and only children, compared to older siblings (Table 3). In terms of health, no statistically significant correlation was found between the presence of systemic diseases and co-sleeping (P > .05), although most of the participants did not have systemic diseases and only a small percentage suffered from respiratory problems (Table 2).

Carbohydrate consumption and oral hygiene habits were also significantly associated with co-sleeping (P < .05). A correlation was found between increased carbohydrate intake and poor oral hygiene, which in turn was associated with higher dft scores.

The data obtained indicated that, in all cases of co-sleeping, breastfeeding was on demand (P < .05; Table 2). From the first year of life, approximately 17% of children were breastfed on demand during the night. In addition, in 25 of the 28 cases of co-sleeping, mothers reported that the child fell asleep with the pacifier in his mouth, with milk present in the oral cavity. The main reason for consultation in the sample was trauma or dental caries.

Finally, when comparing the risk of caries (using the dft score) between children who practiced co-sleeping and those who did not, it was found that the risk was approximately twice as high among infants who slept in co-sleeping (dft 7.14 vs dft 3.49, respectively). This difference, which is related to poor oral hygiene and the practice of nocturnal breastfeeding on demand, was reflected in the distribution of patients according to the number of teeth affected by caries (Table 4). A dft score >5 was recorded in 64.3% of infants, and caries severity was classified into 3 categories according to age and number of teeth affected: no caries, ECC, and severe early caries.

4. Discussion

The importance of breastfeeding in infancy is unquestionable. Both the American Academy of Pediatrics and the American Academy of Pediatric Dentistry recognize breast milk as the ideal source of nutrition, as it protects children against multiple pathologies and provides numerous benefits for growth and development.[1] Nevertheless, prolonged breastfeeding may contribute to the development of ECC, with the associated risk increasing as the number of erupted teeth rises.[10,15,27,28] These findings may reflect overprotective caregiving behaviors during sleep, more frequently observed among parents of only or younger children, which could favor prolonged co-sleeping practices and, consequently, increase the risk of ECC.

Nakayama et al[10] reported that infant feeding practices are closely associated with family socioeconomic status, concluding that breastfeeding duration tends to be longer in households with lower educational and economic levels. In contrast, the study conducted by Wert et al[21] found no association between breastfeeding, co-sleeping, and maternal socioeconomic or educational level, as most participants had university education and high income. Consistent with these findings, our results indicate that nighttime, on demand breastfeeding increases the risk of developing ECC, and that this risk appears to be proportional to the duration of this practice.

A more recent study showed that breastfeeding beyond 12 months and nocturnal breastfeeding are associated with an increased risk of ECC. This systematic review of 31 studies found fewer caries in children breastfed for shorter durations, while nighttime feeding significantly raised ECC risk, highlighting the need for preventive oral health strategies.[27]

We also observed that breastfeeding per se does not necessarily promote the development of ECC. Rather, the determining factor appears to be the absence of adequate oral hygiene practices, particularly when breastfeeding is prolonged, on demand, and maintained during nighttime hours. In this regard, Tashiro et al[29] suggested that breast milk alone may not be involved in cavity formation; however, in the presence of carbohydrate-rich dental plaque, human milk may increase the risk of caries in children.

This perspective is consistent with our findings, as all children who were breastfed on demand in our sample consumed at least 1 sugary product per day, including industrial fruit juices, pastries, or sweets. Furthermore, evidence from a prospective cohort study of 3666 children indicates that breastfeeding beyond 12 months is associated with a higher risk of ECC and increased decayed-missing-filled per surface of deciduous teeth scores at 3 years of age, whereas exclusive breastfeeding for at least 6 months was associated with reduced disease severity. Consequently, the implementation of early preventive dental strategies is recommended, while further research is needed to adequately adjust for sugar intake as a confounding factor.[30]

Nowadays, parents demonstrate greater concern for their children’s health than in previous decades; however, work-related stress and daily responsibilities often limit the time available to address essential aspects such as nutrition, general health, and oral hygiene. This situation highlights parental stress as a significant barrier to adequate oral healthcare in children and is associated with physical, functional, and behavioral consequences that negatively affect their quality of life.[31]

In line with our findings, most parents reported brushing their children’s teeth only once daily, typically at night, which coincided with the only time they were at home during the working week. During weekends, greater attention was paid to children’s oral hygiene, likely due to increased availability of free time. Moreover, the growing prevalence of single-parent households represents an additional vulnerability factor, as limitations in time and resources may further compromise children’s oral health care.[32]

4.1. Limitations

One of the main limitations of this study is its retrospective design, which precludes the establishment of causal relationships among the variables analyzed. In addition, although the sample size was adequate for descriptive purposes, it may limit the generalizability of the findings to other populations. Data collected through questionnaires relied largely on parental or caregiver recall and self-perception, which may have introduced recall bias and social desirability bias. Furthermore, certain diet- and oral hygiene-related factors, such as the precise amount of sugar intake or toothbrushing technique, were not objectively measured.

4.2. Implications

The results of this study underline the need to strengthen health education aimed at parents and caregivers about the risks associated with co-sleeping combined with on demand night breastfeeding and the absence of adequate oral hygiene. From clinical practice, pediatric dentistry and primary care professionals should incorporate the prevention of early caries in routine checkups, promoting the early establishment of healthy oral hygiene and eating habits. At the public health level, it is essential to develop promotion and education programs that include information on the importance of tooth brushing from the eruption of the first tooth, the reduction of nighttime exposure to sugars, and parental supervision during prolonged breastfeeding. The implementation of multidisciplinary preventive strategies can significantly contribute to reducing the incidence of caries in early childhood and improving children’s oral health in the general population.

5. Conclusions

Co-sleeping may increase the risk of dental caries in early childhood due to its association with on demand nocturnal feeding, whether breastfeeding or bottle-feeding, with inadequate oral hygiene acting as the main mediating factor.

These findings highlight the need to strengthen parental education on oral health, particularly in families practicing co-sleeping or nighttime breastfeeding, by promoting appropriate hygiene habits and professional guidance regarding nocturnal feeding practices to reduce the incidence of ECC. Further prospective studies with larger samples are recommended to confirm these results and to explore the behavioral and sociocultural factors involved.
