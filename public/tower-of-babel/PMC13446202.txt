Neurodegeneration in the olfactory system in Niemann Pick type C1 disease
Authors: Maria Grazia Fioriello, Donatella Lobraico, Raffaella Pia Gatta, Lucia Iannuzzi, Nicola Quaranta, Simona Lobasso, Johannes Reisert, Antonio Frigeri, Michele Dibattista
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryNiemann-Pick type C1 (NPC1) disease is a rare neurodegenerative disorder linked to defective cholesterol biosynthesis. Because olfactory dysfunction frequently precedes clinical manifestations in neurodegenerative conditions, we investigated whether the olfactory system might provide early insights into disease processes in NPC1. Using a knock-in mouse model carrying the human I1061T mutation, we investigated the olfactory epithelium in wild-type, heterozygous, and mutant mice to analyze neurodegeneration at 36 and 60 days after birth. Mutant mice showed reduced density of mature olfactory sensory neurons, increased apoptosis, and infiltration of macrophage/microglia. These structural alterations were accompanied by reduced odor-evoked responses and impaired odor-guided behavior.Finally, we assessed olfaction in a family with a child carrying biallelic NPC1 mutations and two heterozygous carrier parents, finding hyposmia of different severity in all three individuals. Our work extensively characterized the OE structurally and functionally, proposing it as a sentinel to monitor disease progression.Keywords: Olfactory epithelium, Neurodegeneration, Sense of smell

Introduction

Niemann-Pick type C (NPC) disease is an autosomal recessive neurodegenerative disease caused by mutations in the Npc1 gene (in 95% of patients) or Npc2 gene.1 Both genes encode proteins required for cholesterol export from lysosomes.2 The NPC1 glycoprotein is involved in the efflux of low-density lipoprotein (LDL) derived unesterified cholesterol from late endosomes and lysosomes to other intracellular compartments.3,4 The NPC2 protein binds cholesterol5 and mutations in Npc2 gene produce accumulations of unesterified cholesterol, sphingolipids, and complex gangliosides in late endosomes and lysosomes of most cell types of the body indistinguishable from those of Npc1 mutations.6

Loss-of-function mutations in either of these proteins lead to deficient lipid trafficking, abnormal regulation of cholesterol biosynthesis, and intracellular accumulation of unesterified cholesterol and gangliosides GM2 and GM3 (sialic acid-containing sphingolipids, major constituents of neuronal membranes) in late endosomes/lysosomes,7,8 due to impaired mobilization and re-esterification of LDL cholesterol throughout the body, especially in liver, spleen, lung, and brain.9

The most prevalent mutation in the Npc1 gene, I1061T, accounts for approximately 15–20% of all disease-associated alleles in humans.10,11,12 This variant leads to the misfolding of the NPC1 protein, which is subsequently directed toward ER-associated degradation. This mutation predisposes patients to the classic NPC1 disease clinical phenotype,10,13 which includes hepatosplenomegaly, ataxia, dystonia, and progressive neurodegeneration14 (in late-infantile and juvenile forms between 3 and 15 years of life, 60–70% of cases).6 Neurodegeneration can even appear later with onset in adolescence and adulthood, when it is characterized by cognitive, auditory, and coordination impairments.15 Animal models for NPC1 disease reported age-dependent neuronal loss in the prefrontal cortex, thalamus, and brainstem, as well as degeneration of cerebellar Purkinje cells, astrogliosis, upregulation of inflammatory genes, and accumulation of cholesterol-derived compounds and sphingolipids within hepatic and neural tissues. These changes closely parallel those described in human NPC1 disease.16,17,18,19

Except for retinal degeneration,20 sensory systems such as olfactory, trigeminal, or auditory pathways in NPC1 disease have not been studied so far. Neurodegenerative diseases, e.g., Parkinson’s disease, show early olfactory impairment, which occurs at least two years before motor symptoms become evident.21,22,23 The same applies to Alzheimer’s disease.22,24,25,26,27 The most peripheral part of the olfactory system is the olfactory epithelium (OE) located in the upper nasal cavity, and it consists of several cell types: the olfactory sensory neurons (OSNs), the sustentacular cells (SUSs) and two populations of reserve stem cells located along the basal lamina of the OE, named horizontal basal cells (HBCs) and globose basal cells (GBCs). OSNs are bipolar neurons responsible for the detection of odorant molecules that enter the nasal cavity via breathing or sniffing; SUSs share properties with both glial and epithelial cells.28 Basal cells in the OE support neurogenesis that leads to the formation of new functional OSNs throughout life and supports a continuous turnover of these cells. This turnover is very remarkable and is unique in the nervous system of mammals.29,30,31 Both types of basal cell populations are multipotent, with HBCs considered reserve stem cells, whereas GBCs are responsible for continuous replacement of OSNs and other cell types in the OE.32,33 Because of these unique features, we hypothesized that the olfactory system and, in particular, the OE could be exploited to better understand the ongoing maintenance of a sensory system and the potentially ensuing neurodegeneration. Therefore, we studied whether structural and functional changes in the OE of a mouse model carrying the Npc1I1061T mutation occur and what their time course might be in similarity to other neurodegenerative diseases where olfactory decline precedes other symptoms. We found that the cell composition and functionality of the OE are impaired in Npc1I1061T mutant (MUT) mice as early as postnatal day 36. In particular, we found that the density of mature OSNs as well as the odorant response decreased in MUT mice compared to heterozygous (HET) and wild-type (WT) mice. We found infiltrated macrophages in the OE and increased number of apoptotic cells. In addition, we found that even HET mice showed altered features similar to MUT. In addition, we could test two parents heterozygous for one of the mutations of the Npc1 gene and their young child carrying both variants. We found that all three individuals, although to varying degrees, had impaired olfactory function.

Results

NPC1 mutant mice exhibit early neurodegeneration

In NPC1 disease, impairment of lipid trafficking affects the CNS, resulting in neuron loss throughout the brain.18 We hypothesized that in mice with the Npc1 I1061T mutation, neurodegeneration could also be observed in the OE, an outpost of the brain located in the nasal cavity. In this mouse model, neurological progression is characterized by increasing ataxia, dysarthria, tremor, impaired motor coordination, and progressive weight loss, with overt neurological signs becoming evident between 2 and 3 months of age.7,34 Based on this time course, P60 was selected as a symptomatic stage, whereas P36 was chosen as a presymptomatic stage to assess whether olfactory alterations could be detected before the onset of the motor deficits. We performed immunofluorescence on OE slices at P36 and at a late time point P60 (Figure 1) by using an antibody against olfactory marker protein (OMP), a marker of mature OSNs35,36,37,38,39 and a mouse anti β-III tubulin (Tuj1) antibody as a highly specific marker for neurons.40 For the purpose of quantification, OSNs were classified into two populations: Tuj1+/OMP− cells and OMP+ cells, irrespective of Tuj1 co-expression. Since the OE undergoes continuous proliferation and generation of new OSNs, some OSNs are not yet fully mature (aka immature). We clearly observed Tuj1+/OMP− neurons just above the basal layer of the OE and we considered them immature (Tuj1+/OMP−, Figures 1A, 1D, 1G, 1J, 1M, and 1P). Immature cells were not significantly different between the three genotypes at P36 (Figures 1A, 1D, 1G, summary plot in s upper panel). In addition, when OMP+ (irrespective of Tuj1 co-labeling) cells were quantified, we could observe a decreased density in MUT (Figure 1H) compared to WT (Figure 1B). Interestingly, although the mature cell population decreases (see OMP+ in Figure 1 and summary in Figure 1S upper panel), immature cells seem to remain constant in their density across genotypes. The same staining was performed at P60 and found the same patterns: a decrease in OMP+ density in the MUT mice (Figures 1K, 1N, 1Q and summary in the S lower panel) and no change in Tuj1+/OMP− cell density across genotypes (Figures 1J, 1M, and 1P). At P60, we noticed the OMP signal was restricted to a more apical region of the OE (closer to the ciliary layer) in MUT mice (Figure 1Q). We asked whether the altered cell density was accompanied by structural changes in the OE. Therefore, we measured OE thickness and found a trend toward a reduction in MUT mice at both P36 and P60, although the differences were not statistically significant (Figure 1T, lower panels).

OSNs undergo apoptosis during neurodegeneration

Apoptosis is a hallmark of many neurodegenerative diseases, and we used cleaved Caspase3 (cCasp3), a marker for activated caspase-3,41,42 to assess whether apoptotic cells are present in the OE. Apoptotic cells increased at P36 in MUT mice (Figure 2C, summary in G) compared to HET (Figure 2B) and WT (Figure 2A). At P60, apoptosis is already enhanced in HET (Figure 2E) and MUT (Figure 2F) mice compared to WT (Figure 2D, summary in G bottom panel). These results may explain the decreased density of OMP+ cell types that we observed in the OE of MUT mice.

NPC1 mutant mice exhibit neuroinflammation in OE

To investigate what triggers apoptosis in the OE of MUT mice we investigated microgliosis, a common feature of NPC1 disease models.7,43,44 We monitored microglia/macrophage activation by using antibodies against Iba1, the ionized calcium-binding adapter molecule 1, a widely recognized microglia/macrophage marker.45

Microglia/macrophages recruitment is more visible in MUT (Figures 3C, 3F, 3I, and 3L) and HET (Figures 3B, 3E, 3H, and 3K) mice compared to WT (Figures 3A, 3D, 3G, and 3J) as early as P36 (Figures 3C and 3I). While Iba1+ cells in WT are mostly localized below the basal lamina or lying over it (Figures 3A, 3D, 3G, and 3J), in HET (Figures 3B, 3E, 3H, and 3K) and MUT (Figure 3C, 3F, 3I, and 3L) they penetrate the epithelium. At both lower and higher magnification, we compared the distribution of Iba1+ cells in the OE of HET (Figures 3B, 3E, 3H, and 3K) and MUT (Figures 3C, 3F, 3I, and 3L) with WT. In addition, macrophage activation in MUT mice (Figures 3I and 3L) was confirmed by their morphology, which became amoeboid and hypertrophic, characterized by large cell bodies with shorter thicker processes (Figures 3M–3R). In HET mice, macrophages appeared to display a more intermediate phenotype (Figures 3N and 3Q). In summary, a decrease of OMP+ OSNs may be triggered/exacerbated by neuroinflammatory processes in the OE of MUT mice.

Altered phospholipid profiles in OE isolated from NPC1 MUT mice

Having established microglia activation in the OE of MUT mice at P36, we aimed to identify potential lipid biomarkers of inflammation. We therefore performed a comparative lipid analysis of OE isolated from WT, HET and MUT mice using positive-ion mode MALDI-TOF/MS.

Figure 4A illustrates the representative lipid profiles of WT and MUT OE at P60, where the main peaks in the mass spectra correspond to the [M + H]+ molecular ions of choline headgroup-containing glycerophospholipids. By comparing the two panels (upper and lower in Figure 4A, both the lipid profiles, although not perfectly overlapping, share many similarities, and no qualitative differences can be observed between the phospholipid patterns in OE of WT and MUT mice. In both mass spectra, major signals are detected in the higher m/z range, at m/z 734.5, 760.5, 782.6, and 810.5, attributable to the [M + H]+ molecular ions of four phosphatidylcholine (PC) species (PC 32:0, PC 34:1, 36:4, 38:4, respectively). Smaller MALDI signals at m/z 496.6 and 524.4 are visible in both the OE mass spectra and are assigned to the [M + H]+ molecular ions of lysophosphatidylcholine (LPC) species (Figure 4A). At m/z 496.6, the signal is attributable to the lysophospholipid LPC with a palmitic acid (LPC 16:0), while that at m/z 524.4 is assigned to LPC containing a stearic acid (LPC 18:0). MALDI lipid profiles of OE isolated from HET mice at P60 as well as those obtained from WT, HET, and MUT mice at P36 (not shown) have similar profiles as the WT and MUT at P60 (shown in Figure 4A).

The histograms in Figure 4B show the statistical analysis of MALDI signal intensities obtained by comparing replicates of OE mass spectra from WT, HET and MUT mice at P36 and P60, revealing alterations in acyl composition of phospholipids between the groups. No significant differences in signals assigned to different PC and LPC species were found, except for the peak diagnostic for LPC 16:0 that was significantly higher in the MUT lipid profile than in WT at P60 (Figure 4B). Interestingly, LPC levels are generally higher in many inflammatory states.46 Also, the signal assigned to PC 38:4 was significantly higher in the HET, but not the MUT, lipid profile than in WT at P60 (Figure 4B).

Figure 4C illustrates the PC/LPC ratios calculated from the various MALDI OE mass spectra of WT, HET and MUT at P36 and P60. This ratio in OE from the MUT mice at P60 was significantly lower than that calculated in WT. Notably, a lower PC/LPC ratio is generally a sign of increased inflammation.47,48,49

In conclusion, comparative lipid analyses of OE obtained from different groups of animals revealed a significant enrichment in LPC species containing palmitic acid and a markedly lower PC/LPC marker in OE from MUT mice at P60 compared with WT.

Stem cell niche of the olfactory epithelium

HBCs are the OE multipotent reserve stem cells. They are flat cells that directly contact the basal lamina and define the boundary between the OE and the underlying lamina propria. Under physiological conditions, HBCs are mitotically quiescent, whereas in pathological conditions, they become active and can regenerate diverse cell types of the OE.50,51,52,53 We used antibodies against cytokeratin 14 (CK14) as a marker of HBCs and found that HBC density tended to increase in MUT mice at P60 (Figure 5F) compared with WT mice. A similar trend was observed in HET mice at P60 (Figure 5E; summary in G). However, these differences were not statistically significant.

Ki67 is a marker of actively cycling cells54 and, although it is not specific for the GBCs, Ki67+ cells located in the basal layer are expected to include a substantial fraction of proliferating GBCs. Additional Ki67+ cells were also observed among duct/gland cells and SUSs (Figure 6, yellow arrowheads).55 The Ki67+ basal cell density does not change at both the time points between genotypes (Figures 6A–6C for P36; Figures 6D–6F for P60).

EOG responses are reduced in MUT mice

We then asked whether the structural differences we observed were associated with functional alteration of the OE. Therefore, we performed air-phase electro-olfactogram (EOG) recordings from the OE of WT, HET and MUT mice at both P36 and P60. When stimulated with isoamyl acetate (IAA) at 10−1 M, we observed an average response of approximately −11 mV in WT, which decreased to about half in MUT mice at P36 (Figure 7A) and P60 (Figure 7C). When stimulating the OE with additional concentrations of IAA (10−3 M, 10−5 M, 10−7 M), we again observed a decrease in response amplitude across concentrations in MUT animals. Dose-response plots showed that the amplitude of the response was overall smaller in the MUT mouse models compared to the WT at both P36 (Figure 7B) and at P60 (Figure 7D). The reduction in odorant responses may be due to the decreased density of OMP+ neurons in the OE of MUT mice.

Olfactory-driven behaviors are impaired in MUT mice

We next asked whether our findings are behaviorally relevant. We sought to investigate how the observed changes might combine to alter naive behavior such as tracking odors to find hidden food. To this end, we performed an odor-guided food-seeking test consisting of a hidden piece of cookie under the cage bedding.56,57,58,59 The mouse is free to explore the cage, and the latency to find the cookie is measured. MUT mice took more time to find the cookie than WT and HET at P36 (Figure 8 left panel). A similar increase in latency was observed in MUT mice at P60 (Figure 8 right panel) compared to WT. No differences across the genotypes were observed when the cookie was placed on top of the bedding, allowing the animal to reach it without relying on olfaction. This finding excludes gross motor impairments or alterations in the motivation to find the food.

Altered sense of smell in patients with NPC1 and their heterozygous parents

Olfactory function in NPC1 patients has not been reported yet, and to our knowledge, no data are currently available. We had the opportunity to assess olfactory performance in a family in which the child was carrying two pathogenic mutations of the Npc1 gene each inherited from a heterozygous parent. None of the three individuals reported obvious sensory deficits.

To evaluate olfactory function, we administered the Sniffin’ Sticks test60 and compared the results with the normative dataset published by Oleszkiewicz et al. (2019),61 in which subjects with a TDI score <30.75 are classified as hyposmic.

We first evaluated the olfactory ability of the child (age 21). She had a TDI (Threshold, Discrimination, Identification) score of 19.5, which was both below the 5th percentile for their age range (age 21–30, 5th perc. = 29.96) and the general cut-off for hyposmia.61 All three subtest scores (T = 5.5, D = 5, I = 9) were also below the 10th percentile for the corresponding age group (T = 5.75, D = 11, I = 11), supporting the classification of the patient as hyposmic.

Interestingly, the mother (50 years old, heterozygous for one of the mutations in the Npc1 gene) scored 26.25, which is below the cut-off for hyposmia. In her age group, the 10th percentile TDI score is 28.7. Her subtest results were T = 5.25, D = 11, and I = 10. Compared with the 10th percentiles for her age group (T = 5.5, D = 10, I = 11), T and I were below the corresponding values, while D was slightly above.

The father (51 years old) had a TDI score of 30.5 (T = 5.5, D = 13, I = 12). Although close to the threshold, this value was still below the 30.75 cut-off for hyposmia as defined by Oleszkiewicz et al. (2019).61 The 10th percentile TDI score for his age group is 26, while the median is = 32.38. Discussion

NPC1 is a rare autosomal recessive lipid storage disease that causes progressive neurodegeneration. We decided to use the B6.129-Npc1tm(I1061T)Dso mouse model with the I1061T missense mutation in the Npc1 gene as it represents the most frequent MUT observed in human patients.10,62,63 The murine NPC1 I1061T protein has a reduced half-life in vivo, consistent with protein misfolding and rapid ER-associated degradation. Here, our results showed that neurodegeneration in OE is impairing olfactory functions as early as P36, making it potentially a sensitive marker for NPC1 disease progression.

Early OSNs neurodegeneration in NPC1 disease

We found that, in the Npc1tm(I1061T)Dso mutant mouse model, neuronal degeneration rather than a failure to generate immature neurons accounts for the significant reduction of OMP+ mature OSN density observed at P36 in MUT mice compared with WT, while the density of Tuj1+ immature OSNs is preserved.

This extends prior NPC1 work demonstrating structural and functional deficits in the olfactory system in a knock out mouse model.7,34,57,64,65 We showed that NPC1 protein stability is essential to maintain cellular integrity in the OE. Cholesterol-trafficking defects can selectively impair mature OSNs while leaving the regenerative abilities of the OE largely intact. The persistence of immature neurons suggests that, although neurogenesis remains active, newly generated OSNs either fail to mature or survive long-term. This trajectory mirrors the progressive cell loss described in other neural regions in NPC models, particularly Purkinje cells in the cerebellum43 and other sensory systems affected by NPC, including the retina66 and auditory pathways.67,68 Our results suggest that apoptosis in the OE may be largely driven by cell-autonomous mechanisms and may in turn contribute to neuroinflammatory processes that further exacerbate cell loss. Crucially, these results align with findings from other neurodegenerative diseases69,70,71 such as Alzheimer’s disease72,73 and Parkinson’s disease,74,75 which also show early olfactory decline. This further supports the notion that mature OSNs are vulnerable targets across neurodegenerative diseases, regardless of the etiology. OMP-based readouts may be applicable in humans and could provide a sensitive and accessible tool to investigate early neurodegenerative change in the OE.

The density of the proliferating basal cells, likely corresponding in large part to proliferative GBCs, does not change in MUT mice compared to WT, indicating that the OE stem cell niche behaves differently from other neurogenic systems, such as the hippocampal dentate gyrus or the subventricular zone, where neurodegeneration often results in progenitor depletion and impaired regenerative capacity.76,77 The preservation of proliferative activity in the basal layer of the OE further suggests that this niche may be relatively resistant to cell-autonomous damage associated with impaired cholesterol trafficking, even during advanced disease stages.

HBCs are immunoreactive for CK14+,78 and by P60 we observed a trend toward increased density in HET and MUT mice. The persistence of the HBC pool in Npc1 MUTs preserves the regenerative potential of the OE.52 Our results are in agreement with previous studies using Npc1 mouse models, where the OE showed increased proliferation (GAP43/BrdU) despite severe OSN loss, indicating that the GBC/HBC niche can be active.34,79 The decreased density of mature OSNs, together with the altered odorant responses measured by EOG, indicate that in MUT mice the rate of apoptosis exceeds the rate of neuronal turnover. Our data further demonstrate that apoptosis in the OE increases across disease stages in the Npc1 mouse, with a clear rise in cCasp3+ OSNs at P36 and P60 in MUT. Our results on cCasp3 confirm that the cell death pathway by effector caspases is a recurring theme of neurodegeneration-linked olfactory decline, as reported for NPC1 disease or eosinophilic chronic rhinosinusitis (CRS).80,81

NPC1 misfolded protein causes increased neuroinflammation and microglia activation during disease progression

It has been suggested that neurodegeneration in NPC1 disease begins in a neural cell-autonomous and microglia-independent manner.82,83 Therefore, the apoptotic events triggered by the impaired egress of cholesterol and sphingolipids from late endosomes/lysosomes in neurons can induce mitochondrial stress in neurons,84 each of which can drive caspase activation.85,86,87,88 Nevertheless, in the OE of the Npc1 mouse model used here, microglia activation/macrophages recruitment and infiltration is more visible in MUT and HET mice compared to WT. Iba1+ cells infiltrating the OE may result from OSN cell death, and in this context, inflammation might exacerbate NPC1 disease.89 However, given the limited sample size, these findings should be considered preliminary and interpreted with caution. Our results agree with previous studies reporting that microgliosis is a common finding in the olfactory bulb of olfactory-deficient animals, including Npc1 mouse models.7,44

Olfactory functions are impaired in MUT mice as early as one month of age

The structural changes we observed in the OE are consistent with the functional impairments detected in our EOG experiments. Dose-response EOG recordings revealed a reduction in response amplitudes at different odorant concentrations, rather than a shift in sensitivity, consistent with the reduced density of mature OSNs in MUT mice. Previous reports7,57 showed deficits in EOG recordings in for the Npc1 knock out mouse model, but lacked both the dose responses and investigation during disease progression, focusing instead on a single time windows.

In our behavioral assay, MUT mice took longer to find the cookie compared to WT. The contribution of the peripheral olfactory system to this behavior is crucial, as altered transduction in OSNs negatively alters the ability of mice to perform the test.65,90,91 Since the test does not rely on an operant-conditioning task, mice must use their innate sense of smell to locate the odor source food. Previous studies investigating olfaction in Npc1 knock out mouse models showed altered olfactory behavior.34,57,92,93

Regarding our phospholipid profiling of OE from Npc1 mice, it is well known that LPC levels increase in many inflammatory diseases, and a decrease in the PC/LPC ratio is often used as an indicator of membrane integrity disruption and neuronal damage.47,48,94 LPC acts as a pro-inflammatory mediator, attracting immune cells such as neutrophils and T cells, and by stimulating the release of inflammatory factors, whereas high PC levels are generally associated with anti-inflammatory effects.46,95,96 In our study, lipid analysis revealed a significant enrichment of an LPC species and a markedly lower PC/LPC ratio in OE from MUT mice at P60 compared to WT. These findings support the rationale to extend this test to patients with NPC1 and to their parents by performing MALDI-TOF lipid analysis on biopsies taken from their noses.

Smell test as part of the tools for neurological evaluations in NPC1 disease

We described the pathophysiological and functional changes occurring in the olfactory system in a mouse model closely resembling NPC1 in humans.7,10,57,97,98 We then sought to investigate whether olfactory impairments are present in patients. We performed olfactory testing with a family of three, with the heterozygous parents and their child carrying both parental variants. The child exhibited a TDI score indicative of severe hyposmia (Table 3). Similar results were found in at least one of the parents although less severe than that of the child. Interestingly, all three subjects showed low scores in the Threshold test, which is more suggestive of peripheral rather than central damage along the olfactory pathways. These observations may suggest that peripheral damage is the first to occur not only in the patient but also in the HET subjects. Therefore, if the periphery olfactory system is altered, we may look for biomarkers in i.e., nasal biopsies that are easily accessible.

Conclusions

In our Npc1 mouse model, abnormalities in intracellular cholesterol transport with subsequent accumulation of lipids in the OE cause OSN neurodegeneration, microglia activation, and increased apoptosis very early during the disease progression. These alterations at a cellular level have deep consequences on olfactory abilities, as demonstrated by electrophysiological recordings and behavioral tests, the latter phenotype being similar to those observed in human NPC1 disease. Thus, the loss of the sense of smell could be used as an early and easily accessible sentinel symptom to diagnose the disease and guide treatments and approaches that prevent/slow neurodegeneration while simultaneously ameliorating olfactory dysfunction.

Finally, we showed that patients with NPC1 may be affected by olfactory impairment even in the absence of any other clear sensory and neurological signs of the disease. In addition, heterozygosity may also alter the sense of smell.

Limitations of the study

A limitation of our study is given by the low number of our subjects, but it is worth considering that NPC1 is a rare disease, and it is not easy to recruit young or young adults who could complete the tests. Nonetheless, our results from parents should suggest that olfactory testing should be included in the panel of neurological examinations since heterozygosity itself has been implicated in fostering neurodegeneration, adding another layer of complexity to the disease’s understanding.
