Tensor decomposition technique for qubit encoding of maximal-fidelity Lorentzian orbitals in real-space quantum chemistry Preprint: APS/123-QED
Taichi Kosugi, Xinchi Huang, Hirofumi Nishi, Yu-ichiro Matsushita

Abstract
To simulate the real- and imaginary-time evolution of a many-electron system on a quantum computer based on the first-quantized formalism, we need to encode molecular orbitals (MOs) into qubit states for typical initial-state preparation. We propose an efficient scheme for encoding an MO as a many-qubit state from a Gaussian-type solution that can be obtained from a tractable solver on a classical computer. We employ the discrete Lorentzian functions (LFs) as a fitting basis set, for which we maximize the fidelity to find the optimal Tucker-form state to represent a target MO. For n prod n_{\mathrm{prod}} three-dimensional LFs, we provide the explicit circuit construction for the state preparation involving 𝒪 ⁡ ( n prod ) \mathcal{O}(n_{\mathrm{prod}}) CNOT gates. Furthermore, we introduce a tensor decomposition technique to construct a canonical-form state to approximate the Tucker-form state with controllable accuracy. Rank- R R decomposition reduces the CNOT gate count to 𝒪 ⁡ ( R ​ n prod 1 / 3 ) . \mathcal{O}(Rn_{\mathrm{prod}}^{1/3}). We demonstrate via numerical simulations that the proposed scheme is a powerful tool for encoding MOs of various quantum chemical systems, paving the way for first-quantized calculations using hundreds or more logical qubits.

Among the various fields in which quantum computation is expected to outperform the classical computation,
quantum chemistry is a particularly active research field where quantum algorithms are developed and run on current hardware.
In fact, the second-quantized formalism for finding ground states and calculating excitation properties

is suitable for quantum computation not only for sophisticated methodologies

;
Fedorov et al. 2022
but also for requiring few resources to encode a many-electron state.
Quantum chemistry in the first-quantized formalism (or equivalently quantum chemistry in real space)
Wiesner 1996
;
Zalka 1998
;
Kassal et al. 2008
;
Jones et al. 2012
;
Hirai et al. 2022
;
Kosugi et al. 2022

;

;
Kosugi et al. 2023b
;

;
Horiba et al. 2024
;
Nishiya et al. 2024
, on the other hand,
requires more qubits than in the second-quantized formalism for performing a meaningful calculation on a quantum computer even for a small molecule.
This fact has been hindering realization of first-quantized calculations despite their favorable features compared to second-quantized calculations.
Looking at the recent growth in the number of high-fidelity qubits on hardware
Bluvstein et al. 2024
;
Acharya et al. 2024
and realized computation using logical qubits
Nishi et al. 2024
, however,
we can expect quantum computation using hundreds of logical qubits to be available not far in the future.
This situation highlights the need to develop efficient techniques for all the phases throughout the procedure of a first-quantized calculation, or more broadly a grid-based calculation
Huang et al. 2024
;
Huang et al. 2024a
;
Huang et al. 2024b

When performing a first-quantized calculation for simulating the real-time dynamics or finding the ground state
Kosugi et al. 2022
;
Nishi et al. 2023
;
Nishiya et al. 2024
of a many-electron state,
one has to prepare an initial state.
The first-quantized formalism for a quantum computer requires explicit antisymmetrization of an initial state for a many-fermion system

;
Berry et al. 2018
;
Horiba et al. 2024

in contrast to the second-quantized formalism.
It is noted that there also exists a work

The efficient antisymmetrization technique proposed by Berry et al.
Berry et al. 2018
assumes that each of the occupied molecular orbitals (MOs) have been encoded in the corresponding data register.
Effective schemes for encoding individual MOs, however, have not been developed yet.

The generic scheme for efficient qubit encoding based on the discrete Lorentzian functions (LFs) was recently proposed
Kosugi et al. 2024
based on linear combination of unitaries (LCU).
This scheme is designed for a target state given as a linear combination of localized functions in one- or multi-dimensional space.
We adopt it in the present study and specialize it for practical use of the real-space quantum chemistry by establishing a numerical procedure for constructing maximal-fidelity Lorentzian orbitals (MFLOs),
which can be used as the input to the antisymmetrization technique to prepare a many-electron state.
In addition, we introduce tensor decomposition technique that allows for reduction of gate operations in the circuit to encode an MFLO.
The workflow of our scheme is displayed in Fig.

We demonstrate via numerical simulations that the proposed scheme is a powerful tool for encoding MOs of various quantum chemical systems.
It is noted here that there is already a work
Huggins et al. 2024

II
Methods

II.1
Preliminaries

II.1.1
Gaussian basis functions in continuous space

Modern quantum chemistry calculation software on classical computers often uses the so-called Cartesian Gaussian (CG) functions
Helgaker et al. 2000
.
The
\mu
th CG function among adopted ones is
defined to be of the form

\displaystyle\chi_{\mu 0}(\bm{r})=\overbrace{\left(\sum_{s}^{n_{\mathrm{G}}}b_{\mu s}e^{-\gamma_{\mu s}r^{2}}\right)}^{\mathrm{Radial\ part}}\overbrace{x^{m_{\mu x}}y^{m_{\mu y}}z^{m_{\mu z}}}^{\mathrm{Cartesian\ part}},

(1)

localized at the origin.
The radial part is made up of contracted
n_{\mathrm{G}}
Gaussian functions having radial exponents

\{\gamma_{\mu s}\}_{s}
via the coefficients
\{b_{\mu s}\}_{s}.

Each MO in a target molecule is expanded in terms of atomic orbitals (AOs)
\{\chi_{\mu}\}_{\mu}
that comprise the basis set defined by locating
\{\chi_{\mu 0}\}_{\mu}
at the positions
\{\bm{\tau}_{\mu}\}_{\mu}
of individual atoms:

\chi_{\mu}(\bm{r})\equiv\chi_{\mu 0}(\bm{r}-\bm{\tau}_{\mu}).

The Cartesian exponents
m_{\mu\nu}\ (\nu=x,y,z)
in each
\chi_{\mu}
specify the orbital angular momentum conveyed by an electron accommodated in the AO.

\ell_{\mu}\equiv m_{\mu x}+m_{\mu y}+m_{\mu z}
is the magnitude of the orbital angular momentum.
While the number of possible combinations of the three exponents is equal to the dimension of multiplet for each of
s
-type (
\ell_{\mu}=0
) and
p
-type (
\ell_{\mu}=1
) orbitals,
that for
d
-type (
\ell_{\mu}=2
) is different from the corresponding dimension of the multiplet.
Specifically, the following six
d
-type CG functions exist:

d_{xx}\propto x^{2},\ d_{yy}\propto y^{2},\ d_{zz}\propto z^{2},\ d_{xy}\propto xy,\ d_{yz}\propto yz,\ d_{zx}\propto zx,

despite the five-dimensional irreducible representation of the rotation group.
That is similarly the case for higher (
\ell_{\mu}>2
) orbital angular momenta.
Although this fact makes a basis set constructed from CG functions redundant,
it brings about a striking feature, that is,
each of the
n_{\mathrm{G}}
terms in
Eq. (
1
)
is factorizable with respect to the three directions as

\exp(-\gamma_{\mu s}x^{2})x^{m_{\mu x}}\cdot\exp(-\gamma_{\mu s}y^{2})y^{m_{\mu y}}\cdot\exp(-\gamma_{\mu s}z^{2})z^{m_{\mu z}}.

It is already known that such factorizability in combination with tensor decomposition techniques allows one to efficiently perform HF calculations on a classical computer

Khoromskij et al. 2011
;
Khoromskaia and Khoromskij 2015
.
We will see in the present study that the factorizability is also favorable for efficiently encoding of MOs.

In what follows, we assume that the coefficients of contracted Gaussian functions in
\chi_{\mu 0}
are normalized in infinite continuous space such that the squared norm is unity:
\int d^{3}r|\chi_{\mu 0}(\bm{r})|^{2}=1.

II.1.2
Circuit for a generic LCU

The qubit encoding technique
Kosugi et al. 2024
that the present study is based on employs a generic LCU to a many-qubit system
Möttönen et al. 2005
;
Kosugi and Matsushita 2020b
.
The relevant part of the scheme for the present study is the unitary operation
U_{\mathrm{amp}}[\bm{c}]
to generate desired relative amplitudes
\bm{c}
on the computational bases of an
n
-qubit system entangled with a data register as

\displaystyle U_{\mathrm{amp}}[\bm{c}]\sum_{j=0}^{2^{n}-1}|\psi_{j}\rangle|j\rangle_{n}

\displaystyle\propto\sum_{j=0}^{2^{n}-1}c_{j}|\psi_{j}\rangle|0\rangle_{n}+(\mathrm{other\ states}),

(2)

where
|\psi_{j}\rangle
is an arbitrary state of the data register.
Since this operation implements amplitude encoding probabilistically in the most generic way,
it requires an exponential cost with respect to
n.

Composed of many multiply controlled
y
rotation gates, the circuit is depicted in Fig.
2
.
Since the expressions for the circuit parameters are known for given amplitudes
\bm{c}
,
the success probability can also be calculated on a classical computer.

U_{\mathrm{amp}}[\bm{c}]
used for generic relative-amplitude encoding of an
n
-qubit system in

2
).
The single-qubit
y
rotation gate

\mathcal{R}^{(m)}_{\lambda}\equiv R_{y}(-2\theta^{(m)}_{\lambda}),

where
\lambda

The explicit expressions for the rotation angles

\theta^{(m)}_{\lambda}
as functions of the amplitudes
\bm{c}

Kosugi and Matsushita 2020b
.

n
-qubit system from the beginning.

II.1.3
Discrete LFs for qubit encoding

The qubit encoding technique based on discrete LFs
Kosugi et al. 2024
was proposed so that
a given linear combination of localized functions in continuous space
is mapped to grid points represented by many-qubit states.
For an
n
-qubit system,
the normalized discrete LF localized at the origin in one-dimensional (1D) space is defined as

\displaystyle L_{k}(n,a)\equiv\frac{C_{S}(n,a)}{\sqrt{N}}\frac{(1-e^{-2a})(1-(-1)^{k}e^{-aN/2})}{1-2e^{-a}\cos(2\pi k/N)+e^{-2a}},

(3)

for
N\equiv 2^{n}
and
k=0,\dots,N-1.

a
is the width and
C_{S}(n,a)
is the normalization constant.
The normalized LF state centered at an integer coordinate
k_{\mathrm{c}}
is defined as

\displaystyle|L;a,k_{\mathrm{c}}\rangle\equiv\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}L_{k-k_{\mathrm{c}}}(n,a)|k\rangle_{n},

(4)

where
|k\rangle_{n}
is the computational basis.
A linear combination of LFs can be efficiently encoded by first generating the discrete Slater functions (SFs)
Klco and Savage 2020
;
Kosugi et al. 2024
with appropriate phase factors
and then performing the quantum Fourier transform (QFT)
Nielsen and Chuang 2011
.
For details, see the original paper.

II.2
MFLO in Tucker form

II.2.1
Mapping MO to grid points

Consider an MO
\phi
given as a linear combination

\displaystyle\phi(\bm{r})=\sum_{\mu}^{n_{\mathrm{bas}}}c_{\mu}\chi_{\mu}(\bm{r})

(5)

using known MO coefficients
\{c_{\mu}\}_{\mu}
of the
n_{\mathrm{bas}}
CG basis functions in continuous space.
Our goal is to amplitude encode
\phi
as a many-qubit state as accurately as possible under practical restrictions.
To this end, we define a simulation cell as a cube having a sufficiently large edge length
L.

We locate the cube at
\bm{r}_{\mathrm{orig}}
so that it contain the entirety of the target molecule.
We then introduce
n_{qe}
qubits for each direction to define

N_{qe}\equiv 2^{n_{qe}}
equidistant grid points in each direction in the cube.
The spacing between neighboring points is
\Delta x\equiv L/N_{qe}.

The computational basis

|\bm{k}\rangle_{3n_{qe}}
specified by three integers
\bm{k}

then encodes the position eigenstate of an electron at

\bm{r}^{(\bm{k})}\equiv\bm{r}_{\mathrm{orig}}+\Delta x(k_{x}\bm{e}_{x}+k_{y}\bm{e}_{y}+k_{z}\bm{e}_{z}).

From
h(\xi;\gamma,m)\equiv\xi^{m}e^{-\gamma\xi^{2}}
and

\widetilde{\bm{\tau}}_{\mu}\equiv\bm{\tau}_{\mu}-\bm{r}_{\mathrm{orig}},

we define

\displaystyle h_{\mu s}^{(\bm{k})}\equiv\prod_{\nu=x,y,z}h(k_{\nu}\Delta x-\widetilde{\tau}_{\mu\nu};\gamma_{\mu s},m_{\mu\nu}),

(6)

where
\gamma_{\mu s}
and
m_{\mu\nu}
are the radial exponent and the Cartesian exponent of
\chi_{\mu 0},
respectively.
[See Eq. (
1
)].
The
3n_{qe}
-qubit state representing the MO inside the simulation cell is then written as

\displaystyle|\phi_{\mathrm{ideal}}\rangle

\displaystyle\equiv\mathcal{N}\sqrt{\Delta V}\sum_{\bm{k}}\phi(\bm{r}^{(\bm{k})}+\bm{r}_{\mathrm{orig}})|\bm{k}\rangle_{3n_{qe}}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{N}\sqrt{\Delta V}\sum_{\bm{k}}\sum_{\mu,s}c_{\mu}b_{\mu s}h_{\mu s}^{(\bm{k})}|\bm{k}\rangle_{3n_{qe}},

(7)

where
\Delta V\equiv(\Delta x)^{3}
is the volume element.

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}\equiv\left(\Delta V\sum_{\bm{k}}|\phi(\bm{r}^{(\bm{k})}+\bm{r}_{\mathrm{orig}})|^{2}\right)^{-1/2}

(8)

is a dimensionless constant for

\langle\phi_{\mathrm{ideal}}|\phi_{\mathrm{ideal}}\rangle=1

to hold precisely.

\mathcal{N}
is close to 1 when
L
and
n_{qe}
are sufficiently large for encoding
\phi.

The index
\bm{k}
of computational basis in
Eq. (
7
)
measures the position eigenvalue from
\bm{r}_{\mathrm{orig}}.

II.2.2
Trial state and fidelity

To establish an efficient scheme for amplitude encoding
the target state
|\phi_{\mathrm{ideal}}\rangle,

we propose to make use of a Lorentzian basis set.
Let us assume that,
for each direction
\nu=x,y,z,

the number
n_{\mathrm{L}\nu}
of available LFs is fixed.
Let
a_{\nu\ell}
and
k_{\mathrm{c}\nu\ell}
be the width and center, respectively, of the
\ell
-th LF in the
\nu
direction.
We then define a trial state as a linear combination of all the possible

n_{\mathrm{prod}}\equiv n_{\mathrm{L}x}n_{\mathrm{L}y}n_{\mathrm{L}z}

product basis states,

\displaystyle|\phi_{\mathrm{Tucker}}[\bm{d},\bm{a},\bm{k}_{\mathrm{c}}]\rangle

\displaystyle\equiv\sum_{\bm{\ell}}^{n_{\mathrm{prod}}}d_{\ell_{x}\ell_{y}\ell_{z}}|L;a_{x\ell_{x}},k_{\mathrm{c}x\ell_{x}}\rangle|L;a_{y\ell_{y}},k_{\mathrm{c}y\ell_{y}}\rangle|L;a_{z\ell_{z}},k_{\mathrm{c}z\ell_{z}}\rangle.

(9)

We refer to this state as a Tucker-form trial state since
Eq. (
9
)
follows the contraction pattern of the Tucker decomposition
Tucker 1966
;
Kolda and Bader 2009
,
which is one of the most famous tensor decomposition techniques.
The coefficients
\bm{d}
in
Eq. (
9
)
are called the core tensor.

We need to find the optimal combination of
the core tensor
\bm{d},
the widths
\bm{a},

and the centers
\bm{k}_{\mathrm{c}}
of the LFs.
To this end, we define the fidelity of the Tucker-form trial state as

\displaystyle F(\bm{d},\bm{a},\bm{k}_{\mathrm{c}})\equiv|\langle\phi_{\mathrm{ideal}}|\phi_{\mathrm{Tucker}}[\bm{d},\bm{a},\bm{k}_{\mathrm{c}}]\rangle|^{2}-P(\bm{a},\bm{k}_{\mathrm{c}}),

(10)

where the first term on the RHS is the squared overlap between the trial and ideal states.
The second term,

\displaystyle P(\bm{a},\bm{k}_{\mathrm{c}})\equiv\frac{\alpha_{\mathrm{pen}}}{n_{\mathrm{prod}}}\mathrm{Tr}\left(\left(S(\bm{a},\bm{k}_{\mathrm{c}})-I\right)^{2}\right),

(11)

introduces a penalty term with a strength parameter
\alpha_{\mathrm{pen}}\geq 0.

S(\bm{a},\bm{k}_{\mathrm{c}})

is the
n_{\mathrm{prod}}
-dimensional overlap matrix between the three-dimensional (3D) LFs appearing in
Eq. (
9
)
and
I
is the identity matrix.
We maximize the fidelity while obeying the normalization condition

\||\phi_{\mathrm{Tucker}}[\bm{d},\bm{a},\bm{k}_{\mathrm{c}}]\rangle\|^{2}=1
to find the MFLO.
The penalty term vanishes if and only if all the 3D LFs are orthogonal to each other.
The larger
\alpha_{\mathrm{pen}}
is,
the closer the LFs are to an orthonormalized set during the maximization process.
The penalty term may increase the success probability of LCU at the cost of overlap, as will be demonstrated later.

The overlap between the trial and ideal states can be written as

\displaystyle\langle\phi_{\mathrm{ideal}}|\phi_{\mathrm{Tucker}}[\bm{d},\bm{a},\bm{k}_{\mathrm{c}}]\rangle=\sum_{\bm{\ell}}^{n_{\mathrm{prod}}}T_{\bm{\ell}}(\bm{a}_{\bm{\ell}},\bm{k}_{\mathrm{c}\bm{\ell}})d_{\bm{\ell}}.

(12)

The definition of

T_{\bm{\ell}}(\bm{a}_{\bm{\ell}},\bm{k}_{\mathrm{c}\bm{\ell}}),

which we call the
T
tensor,
and the derivation of
Eq. (
12
)
are described in Appendix
A
.
Due to the factorizable terms in the AO in
Eq. (
1
)
and the Tucker-form trial state,
the required spatial integrals for evaluating the
T
tensor are one dimensional.
By using the overlap between two LFs

S_{\ell\ell^{\prime}}^{(\nu)}(\bm{a}_{\nu},\bm{k}_{\mathrm{c}\nu})\equiv\langle L;a_{\nu\ell},k_{\mathrm{c}\nu\ell}|L;a_{\nu\ell^{\prime}},k_{\mathrm{c}\nu\ell^{\prime}}\rangle

in the
\nu
direction,
we can write the overlap matrix between the product bases as

\displaystyle S_{\bm{\ell},\bm{\ell}^{\prime}}(\bm{a},\bm{k}_{\mathrm{c}})=\prod_{\nu=x,y,z}S_{\ell_{\nu}\ell_{\nu}^{\prime}}^{(\nu)}(\bm{a}_{\nu},\bm{k}_{\mathrm{c}\nu}).

(13)

This matrix is clearly symmetric.

S_{\bm{\ell},\bm{\ell}^{\prime}}(\bm{a},\bm{k}_{\mathrm{c}})=S_{\bm{\ell}^{\prime},\bm{\ell}}(\bm{a},\bm{k}_{\mathrm{c}}).

The normalization condition to be respected by the trial state is then written as

\displaystyle\sum_{\bm{\ell},\bm{\ell}^{\prime}}d_{\bm{\ell}}S_{\bm{\ell},\bm{\ell}^{\prime}}(\bm{a},\bm{k}_{\mathrm{c}})d_{\bm{\ell}^{\prime}}=1.

(14)

II.2.3
Maximization of fidelity

For fixed
\bm{a}
and
\bm{k}_{\mathrm{c}},

the stationarity condition for the fidelity with the optimal core tensor
\widetilde{\bm{d}}
becomes

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\partial F(\bm{d},\bm{a},\bm{k}_{\mathrm{c}})}{\partial d_{\bm{\ell}}}-\kappa\frac{\partial\||\phi_{\mathrm{Tucker}}[\bm{d},\bm{a},\bm{k}_{\mathrm{c}}]\rangle\|^{2}}{\partial d_{\bm{\ell}}}\right)_{\bm{d}=\widetilde{\bm{d}}}

\displaystyle=2\sum_{\bm{\ell}^{\prime}}G_{\bm{\ell},\bm{\ell}^{\prime}}(\bm{a},\bm{k}_{\mathrm{c}})\widetilde{d}_{\bm{\ell}^{\prime}}-2\kappa\sum_{\bm{\ell},\bm{\ell}^{\prime}}S_{\bm{\ell},\bm{\ell}^{\prime}}(\bm{a},\bm{k}_{\mathrm{c}})\widetilde{d}_{\bm{\ell}^{\prime}},

(15)

where we defined the matrix

G(\bm{a},\bm{k}_{\mathrm{c}})
by

\displaystyle G_{\bm{\ell},\bm{\ell}^{\prime}}(\bm{a},\bm{k}_{\mathrm{c}})\equiv T_{\bm{\ell}}(\bm{a}_{\bm{\ell}},\bm{k}_{\mathrm{c}\bm{\ell}})T_{\bm{\ell}^{\prime}}(\bm{a}_{\bm{\ell}^{\prime}},\bm{k}_{\mathrm{c}\bm{\ell}^{\prime}}).

(16)

\kappa
is the Lagrange multiplier.
Since the fidelity is quadratic with respect to the core tensor,
the stationarity condition in
Eq. (
15
)
can be rewritten as the following
n_{\mathrm{prod}}
-dimensional generalized eigenvalue problem for the matrices:

\displaystyle G(\bm{a},\bm{k}_{\mathrm{c}})\widetilde{\bm{d}}=\kappa S(\bm{a},\bm{k}_{\mathrm{c}})\widetilde{\bm{d}}.

(17)

For any solution
\widetilde{\bm{d}}
of this problem,
Eqs. (
12
),
(
14
),
and
(
16
)
allow us to write the corresponding eigenvalue as

\displaystyle\kappa

\displaystyle=\widetilde{\bm{d}}\cdot G(\bm{a},\bm{k}_{\mathrm{c}})\widetilde{\bm{d}}

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{\bm{\ell}}T_{\bm{\ell}}(\bm{a}_{\bm{\ell}},\bm{k}_{\mathrm{c}\bm{\ell}})\widetilde{d}_{\bm{\ell}}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=F(\widetilde{\bm{d}},\bm{a},\bm{k}_{\mathrm{c}})+P(\bm{a},\bm{k}_{\mathrm{c}}),

(18)

which indicates that the eigenvalue is equal to the fidelity plus the penalty.
Let

\{\kappa_{p}(\bm{a},\bm{k}_{\mathrm{c}}),\widetilde{\bm{d}}_{p}(\bm{a},\bm{k}_{\mathrm{c}})\}_{p=0}^{n_{\mathrm{prod}}-1}

be the combinations of the eigenvalues and corresponding eigenvectors of this problem.
Although every combination among them satisfies
Eq. (
18
),
we would like to find the combination that achieves the highest fidelity.
Since the second term on the right-hand side of
Eq. (
18
)
does not depend on
\widetilde{\bm{d}},

the first term achieves its maximum when
\kappa
is maximized.
Specifically,
the globally optimal core tensor

\bm{d}(\bm{a},\bm{k}_{\mathrm{c}})

for the fixed
\bm{a}
and
\bm{k}_{\mathrm{c}}
is nothing but
the eigenvector belonging to the largest eigenvalue

\kappa_{\mathrm{max}}(\bm{a},\bm{k}_{\mathrm{c}})\equiv\max_{p}\kappa_{p}(\bm{a},\bm{k}_{\mathrm{c}}).

That is,

\displaystyle\bm{d}(\bm{a},\bm{k}_{\mathrm{c}})=\widetilde{\bm{d}}_{p}(\bm{a},\bm{k}_{\mathrm{c}})\ \mathrm{with}\ p=\mathop{\rm arg~max}\limits_{p}\kappa_{p}(\bm{a},\bm{k}_{\mathrm{c}}).

(19)

This core tensor achieves the fidelity

\displaystyle F(\bm{a},\bm{k}_{\mathrm{c}})\equiv F(\bm{d}(\bm{a},\bm{k}_{\mathrm{c}}),\bm{a},\bm{k}_{\mathrm{c}})=\kappa_{\mathrm{max}}(\bm{a},\bm{k}_{\mathrm{c}})-P(\bm{a},\bm{k}_{\mathrm{c}}).

(20)

Considering the classical-computational cost
\mathcal{O}(n_{\mathrm{prod}}^{1/3}N_{qe})
with respect to
n_{\mathrm{prod}}
and
N_{qe}
for evaluating the
T
tensor,
that for obtaining the fidelity in the equation above is

\mathcal{O}(\max(n_{\mathrm{prod}}^{3},n_{\mathrm{prod}}^{1/3}N_{qe})).

This efficient scaling is because

n_{\mathrm{prod}}^{3}
does not involve the size of data register and

n_{\mathrm{prod}}^{1/3}N_{qe}
has the low scaling thanks to the separability of CG functions and 3D LFs.

Next, we need to maximize the reduced fidelity
F(\bm{a},\bm{k}_{\mathrm{c}}),

which no longer involves the variable
\bm{d}.

Despite the lack of an explicit expression for the reduced fidelity,
it is possible to calculate numerically its gradient with respect to
\bm{a}
as

\displaystyle\frac{\partial F(\bm{a},\bm{k}_{\mathrm{c}})}{\partial a_{\nu\ell_{\nu}}}=2f(\bm{a},\bm{k}_{\mathrm{c}})g_{\nu\ell_{\nu}}(\bm{a},\bm{k}_{\mathrm{c}})

\displaystyle-\kappa_{\mathrm{max}}(\bm{a},\bm{k}_{\mathrm{c}})\bm{d}(\bm{a},\bm{k}_{\mathrm{c}})\cdot\frac{\partial S(\bm{a},\bm{k}_{\mathrm{c}})}{\partial a_{\nu\ell_{\nu}}}\bm{d}(\bm{a},\bm{k}_{\mathrm{c}})-\frac{\partial P(\bm{a},\bm{k}_{\mathrm{c}})}{\partial a_{\nu\ell}},

(21)

where we defined

f(\bm{a},\bm{k}_{\mathrm{c}})\equiv\sum_{\bm{\ell}}T_{\bm{\ell}}(\bm{a},\bm{k}_{\mathrm{c}})d_{\bm{\ell}}(\bm{a},\bm{k}_{\mathrm{c}})

and

\displaystyle g_{\nu\ell_{\nu}}(\bm{a},\bm{k}_{\mathrm{c}})

\displaystyle\equiv\sum_{\bm{\ell}^{\prime}}\frac{\partial T_{\bm{\ell}^{\prime}}(\bm{a},\bm{k}_{\mathrm{c}})}{\partial a_{\nu\ell_{\nu}}}d_{\bm{\ell}^{\prime}}(\bm{a},\bm{k}_{\mathrm{c}}).

(22)

The derivation of
Eq. (
21
)
is described in Appendix
B
.
From the results of numerical diagonalization for
Eq. (
17
)
and the known explicit expressions for the overlap matrix in
Eq. (
13
) and the
T
tensor,
we can evaluate all the terms on the RHS of
Eq. (
21
).
We can thus use any gradient-based optimization scheme to maximize

F(\bm{a},\bm{k}_{\mathrm{c}})
for fixed
\bm{k}_{\mathrm{c}}
which further reduces the fidelity:

F(\bm{k}_{\mathrm{c}})\equiv\max_{\bm{a}}F(\bm{a},\bm{k}_{\mathrm{c}}).

We should keep in mind that
F(\bm{k}_{\mathrm{c}})

depends on initial values of
\bm{a}
in general.

To complete the optimization of the trial state,
we need to maximize
F(\bm{k}_{\mathrm{c}}).

Since
\bm{k}_{\mathrm{c}}
are discrete variables,
we cannot employ gradient-based optimization.

II.3
Circuit for encoding a Tucker-form state

The circuit construction for qubit encoding of a Tucker-form state in
Eq. (
9
)
directly uses the techniques proposed in Ref.
Kosugi et al. 2024
.
To be specific,
the circuit
\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{Tucker}}^{(\mathrm{prob})}
for probabilistic preparation is shown in
Fig.
3
.
We use

\displaystyle n_{\mathrm{A}\nu}\equiv\lceil\log_{2}n_{\mathrm{L}\nu}\rceil

(23)

Lorentzian ancillae for each direction
\nu
to discern the ancillary states that designate the indices
\ell_{\nu}
of

|L;a_{\nu\ell_{\nu}},k_{\mathrm{c}\nu\ell_{\nu}}\rangle.

The total number of ancillae is

n_{\mathrm{A}}^{(\mathrm{L})}\equiv n_{\mathrm{A}x}+n_{\mathrm{A}y}+n_{\mathrm{A}z}

The partial circuit
U_{\mathrm{S-ph}}[\bm{a},\bm{k}_{\mathrm{c}}]

generates the SFs with phase factors according to the widths and centers of the LFs,
which are then provided with the desired amplitudes by

U_{\mathrm{amp}}[\bm{d}]

up to overall scaling.
Therefore, the normalized state of the entire system immediately before the measurement is

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{s_{\mathrm{Tucker}}}{n_{\mathrm{prod}}}}|\phi_{\mathrm{Tucker}}[\bm{d},\bm{a},\bm{k}_{\mathrm{c}}]\rangle|0\rangle_{n_{\mathrm{A}}^{(\mathrm{L})}}+(\mathrm{other\ states}),

(24)

where
s_{\mathrm{Tucker}}
is some value that can be calculated
Kosugi and Matsushita 2020b
from the expressions for

U_{\mathrm{amp}}[\bm{d}].

The success probability for encoding the Tucker-form state is thus

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{Tucker}}=\frac{s_{\mathrm{Tucker}}}{n_{\mathrm{prod}}}.

(25)

The QFT operations can be applied to the data register after the measurement is successful since the order of QFT and the measurement does not affect the result of the probabilistic encoding
Kosugi et al. 2024
.

II.4
Canonical-form state from tensor decomposition

Here we employ the canonical decomposition technique for finding more efficient qubit encoding of a known Tucker-form state.
Since the concept of this generic technique for tensor decomposition was repeatedly reinvented historically,
there are many names for the technique.
For details, see Ref.
Kolda and Bader 2009
and references therein.

Here, we assume that we already know the optimal parameters for the MFLO in the Tucker form.
For a specified rank
R,

the numerical machinery of canonical decomposition allows us to approximate the three-leg core tensor
\bm{d}
as

\displaystyle d_{\ell_{x}\ell_{y}\ell_{z}}\approx\sum_{r=0}^{R-1}v^{(x)}_{r\ell_{x}}v^{(y)}_{r\ell_{y}}v^{(z)}_{r\ell_{z}},

(26)

where the RHS is represented by the three two-leg tensors
v^{(\nu)}\ (\nu=x,y,z).

The RHS of the equation just above has a canonical form of rank
R.

For a generic tensor, canonical decomposition with a higher rank leads to a more accurate approximation.
While exact decomposition is possible for

R=n_{\mathrm{prod}}
in the present case,
it is desirable to find
R
as small as possible to achieve practical accuracy.
We refer to
v^{(\nu)}
as the canonical tensors.

Using the normalization constant

\displaystyle N_{r}^{(\nu)}\equiv\left(\sum_{\ell,\ell^{\prime}}v^{(\nu)}_{r\ell}v^{(\nu)}_{r\ell^{\prime}}S_{\ell\ell^{\prime}}^{(\nu)}(\bm{a}_{\nu},\bm{k}_{\mathrm{c}\nu})\right)^{1/2},

(27)

we define the normalized canonical tensor
u^{(\nu)}
via

u_{r\ell}^{(\nu)}\equiv v_{r\ell}^{(\nu)}/N_{r}^{(\nu)}.

We also define the normalized linear combination of LFs as

\displaystyle|\phi_{r}^{(\nu)}\rangle\equiv\sum_{\ell=0}^{n_{\mathrm{L}\nu}-1}u^{(\nu)}_{r\ell}|L;a_{\nu\ell},k_{\mathrm{c}\nu\ell}\rangle

(28)

and the canonical coefficients

\lambda_{r}\equiv N_{r}^{(x)}N_{r}^{(y)}N_{r}^{(z)}.

Substituting
Eq. (
26
) into
Eq. (
9
), we obtain the following approximation to the Tucker-form state:

\displaystyle|\phi_{\mathrm{Tucker}}[\bm{d},\bm{a},\bm{k}_{\mathrm{c}}]\rangle

\displaystyle\approx\sum_{r=0}^{R-1}\lambda_{r}\underbrace{|\phi_{r}^{(x)}\rangle|\phi_{r}^{(y)}\rangle|\phi_{r}^{(z)}\rangle}_{\equiv|\phi_{\mathrm{canon},r}\rangle}

\displaystyle\equiv|\phi_{\mathrm{canon}}\rangle.

(29)

We refer to
|\phi_{\mathrm{canon}}\rangle
as the MFLO in the canonical form of rank
R.

We can assume the canonical coefficients to be in descending order without loss of generality.

Circuit for encoding a canonical-form state

The circuit for qubit encoding of a canonical-form state in
Eq. (

)
can be constructed by modifying the techniques in
Ref.
Kosugi et al. 2024

while considering the contraction pattern for the canonical coefficients and the canonical tensors.
The circuit
\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{canon}}^{(\mathrm{prob})}
for probabilistic preparation is shown in

Besides the Lorentzian ancillae in
Eq. (

),
we use

\displaystyle n_{\mathrm{A}}^{(\mathrm{c})}\equiv\lceil\log_{2}R\rceil

(30)

canonical ancillae to discern the
R
terms in the canonical decomposition of the core tensor.

The normalized state of the entire system immediately after the relative-amplitude encoding gates for the normalized canonical tensors
u
(blue boxes in Fig.

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{Rn_{\mathrm{prod}}}}\sum_{r}\left(\bigotimes_{\nu=x,y,z}\sqrt{s_{r}^{(\nu)}}|\phi_{r}^{(\nu)}\rangle\right)|0\rangle_{n_{\mathrm{A}}^{(\mathrm{L})}}|r\rangle_{n_{\mathrm{A}}^{(\mathrm{c})}}

\displaystyle+(\mathrm{other\ states}),

(31)

where
s_{r}^{(\nu)}
is some value that can be calculated
Kosugi and Matsushita 2020b
from the expressions for

U_{\mathrm{amp}}[\bm{u}_{r}^{(\nu)}]
for each

To resolve the discrepancy between the coefficients in the equation above and those desired,
we modify the canonical coefficients as

\displaystyle\widetilde{\lambda}_{r}\equiv\frac{\lambda_{r}}{\sqrt{s_{r}^{(x)}s_{r}^{(y)}s_{r}^{(z)}}}.

(32)

The application of

U_{\mathrm{amp}}[\widetilde{\bm{\lambda}}]

to the state in
Eq. (

)
gives

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{s_{\mathrm{canon}}}{Rn_{\mathrm{prod}}}}\sum_{r}\lambda_{r}|\phi_{r}^{(x)}\rangle|\phi_{r}^{(y)}\rangle|\phi_{r}^{(z)}\rangle|0\rangle_{n_{\mathrm{A}}^{(\mathrm{L})}}|0\rangle_{n_{\mathrm{A}}^{(\mathrm{c})}}

\displaystyle+(\mathrm{other\ states}),

(33)

where
s_{\mathrm{canon}}
is some value that can be calculated analytically.
The state of the data register coupled to the all-zero ancillary state in
Eq. (
33
)
is
|\phi_{\mathrm{canon}}\rangle,

meaning that
\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{canon}}^{(\mathrm{prob})}
implements the desired probabilistic encoding.
The success probability is thus

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{canon}}=\frac{s_{\mathrm{canon}}}{Rn_{\mathrm{prod}}}.

(34)

II.6
CNOT gate counts

The CNOT gate count in a given circuit is often used to quantify its complexity and susceptibility to errors.
While the circuits depicted in
Figs.
3
and
4

are direct applications of the circuit in the original paper
Kosugi et al. 2024
,
we rearrange the gate operations to allow for the implementation of a uniformly controlled rotation (UCR)
Möttönen et al. 2005
and a diagonal unitary
Welch et al. 2014
to minimize the CNOT gate count.
For details, see Appendix
C
.
The gate count in the state preparation circuit for a Tucker-form state excluding QFT is

\displaystyle N_{\mathrm{C}X}(\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{Tucker}}^{(\mathrm{prob})})

\displaystyle=2^{n_{\mathrm{A}}^{(\mathrm{L})}}-11+3n_{qe}\sum_{\nu}2^{n_{\mathrm{A}\nu}},

(35)

while that for a canonical-form state is

\displaystyle N_{\mathrm{C}X}(\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{canon}}^{(\mathrm{prob})})

\displaystyle=-2^{n_{\mathrm{A}}^{(\mathrm{c})}+1}-11+(3n_{qe}+2^{n_{\mathrm{A}}^{(\mathrm{c})}})\sum_{\nu}2^{n_{\mathrm{A}\nu}}.

(36)

For a typical case where the LFs are defined with

n_{\mathrm{L}x}=n_{\mathrm{L}y}=n_{\mathrm{L}z},

the scaling of the gate counts derived above in terms of
R
and
n_{\mathrm{prod}}
is

N_{\mathrm{C}X}(\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{Tucker}}^{(\mathrm{prob})})=\mathcal{O}(n_{\mathrm{prod}})

and

N_{\mathrm{C}X}(\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{canon}}^{(\mathrm{prob})})=\mathcal{O}(Rn_{\mathrm{prod}}^{1/3}).

The canonical-form state thus reduces the gate count compared to the Tucker-form state when a low-rank decomposition of the target state is permissible.
Conversely, high-rank decomposition may require more gates than the Tucker-form state preparation.

While we focus on the CNOT gate counts in the present study,
efficient implementation of controlled unitaries with few
gate counts of
T
or Toffoli operations have been proposed in literature
Babbush et al. 2018
;
Boyd 2023
;
Yoshioka et al. 2024
;
Kobori et al. 2024
.

II.7
Adoption in an initial many-electron state

As will be seen in the examples below,
the fidelity between an ideal state and an encoded state may not be close to 1 for the few LFs compared to the system size.
When such an encoded state is used in an initial guess for imaginary-time evolution (ITE) (see, e.g., Ref.
Kosugi et al. 2022
),
the deviation of the encoded state from the ideal one may not lead to large additional cost thanks to the exponential decay of the weights of undesired states.
For real-time evolution (RTE), on the other hand,
the deviation can lead to distinct dynamics than the ideal one.
One possible workaround for that is to perform a small number of steps of variational or nonvariational ITE before starting the RTE so that the undesired states diminish.

IV
Conclusions

In summary,
we proposed an efficient scheme for encoding an MO as a many-qubit state referred to as the MFLO,
numerically constructed via maximization of the fidelity of a trial state.
Since the proposed scheme exploits the fact that a typical MO consists of a small number of localized orbitals, independently of the grid resolution for encoding,
the CNOT gate count is not affected largely by the size of a data register.
We demonstrated that the scheme works and the tensor decomposition technique can reduce the gate count.
This scheme will be a powerful tool for performing quantum chemistry in real space on a quantum computer using hundreds of or more logical qubits in the future.

We mention here the Fourier interpolation scheme
Ramos-Calderer 2022
;
Moosa et al. 2023
for encoding an MO.
One of its fascinating features against the present scheme is the unitarity
Möttönen et al. 2005
;
Moosa et al. 2023
.
For a case in which the determination of circuit parameters
from the Fourier components on the coarse grid for interpolation can be completed within practical time,
the interpolation scheme will be a good option.

Appendix D
Simple explanation of a low success probability for an antibonding overlap

In order for discussion to be simple,
we consider here two atoms
A
and
B
in 1D space.
We assume that we have a trial state

|\psi\rangle=(\cos\theta U_{A}+\sin\theta U_{B})|0\rangle

as a linear combination of the LFs with an angle parameter
\theta.

U_{A}
and
U_{B}
are the unitaries that generate the discrete LFs centered at the position of
A
and
B,
respectively.
The success probability for generating this state via LCU for the two unitaries is

\mathbb{P}=1/2+\sin(2\theta)\langle L_{A}|L_{B}\rangle/2.

Since the LFs are positive from their definitions, we can write

\langle L_{A}|L_{B}\rangle=1-\Delta
with some
\Delta\geq 0.

If the trial state is of bonding nature, we can parametrize it as
\theta=\pi/4+\delta
with a small
\delta.

The success probability in this case is

\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{bonding}}\approx 1-\Delta/2-\delta^{2}(1-\Delta).

If the trial state is of antibonding nature, on the other hand,
the success probability with an angle parameter
\theta=-\pi/4+\delta
is

\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{antibonding}}\approx\Delta/2+\delta^{2}(1-\Delta).

This analysis tells us that the success probability for a bonding MO between
A
and
B
tends to be higher than that for an antibonding MO between the same atoms.
In particular,
as the two centers get closer to each other,
\Delta
becomes smaller and the tendency is more pronounced.
This discussion may apply basically to generic trial states consisting of more than two LFs in 3D space.
