The circadian clock component NPAS2 is modulated by AMPK and regulates substrate utilization and mitochondrial function in human skeletal muscle cells
Authors: David Rizo-Roca, Logan A Pendergrast, Prasad Kamble, Dimitrius Santiago PSF Guimarães, Lucile Dollet, Marie Björnholm, Juleen R Zierath
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe molecular circadian clock coordinates cellular and systemic metabolism across the day, yet how it integrates with energy-sensing pathways in human skeletal muscle remains incompletely understood. AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) functions as a cellular energy sensor and has been implicated in circadian regulation; however, its role in coupling clock components to metabolic programming in human muscle has not been established.Here, we investigated the interaction between AMPK signaling and the circadian machinery in primary human myotubes, focusing on the downstream clock components NPAS2 and DBP. Silencing of the AMPKα subunit altered core clock gene expression, increasing CRY1 and CRY2 while reducing NPAS2 and DBP expression, with rhythmicity preserved. To determine the functional relevance of these changes, we examined metabolic phenotypes following siRNA-mediated silencing of NPAS2. NPAS2 knockdown reduced basal oxidative metabolism, decreased abundance of electron transport chain subunits, and increased β-oxidation flux, while maximal mitochondrial respiratory capacity remained intact. These alterations occurred independently of canonical AMPK–ACC signaling but were associated with reduced HIF1A expression. In contrast, DBP silencing reduced glucose oxidation but did not recapitulate the broader metabolic remodeling observed with NPAS2 knockdown.Together, these findings identify NPAS2 as a downstream mediator linking AMPK-dependent circadian signaling to skeletal muscle metabolic regulation. Disruption of this axis alters substrate utilization and impairs mitochondrial function, revealing a mechanism by which circadian clock components contribute to metabolic regulation in human skeletal muscle.keywords: Skeletal muscle, Circadian clock, Metabolic regulation, Glucose and lipid metabolism

Introduction

The molecular circadian clock governs metabolic homeostasis and is present in nearly all cells of the body, operating through a transcription-translation feedback loop initiated by heterodimerization of the transcription factors brain and muscle ARNT-like 1 (BMAL1), circadian locomotor output cycles kaput (CLOCK); and neuronal PAS domain protein 2 (NPAS2). This transcriptional process drives expression of the repressor proteins period (PER) and cryptochrome (CRY), which, upon translation, translocate to the nucleus and repress the clock transcription factor heterodimers [1]. The core molecular clock also drives rhythmic expression of clock-controlled genes, including albumin D-site-binding protein (DBP), a transcription factor that relays circadian information to downstream targets, such as PER1, thereby contributing to fine-tuning circadian gene networks [2]. In peripheral tissues, circadian clocks coordinate expression of genes involved in glucose and energy homeostasis, enabling temporal organization of metabolic processes across the day [3,4]. Disruption of this system is associated with metabolic diseases, including impaired lipid and glucose homeostasis in type 2 diabetes [5,6].

Circadian timing in peripheral tissues is regulated by systemic and local signals, including nutrients, hormones, and cellular energy status, promoting coherent and efficient temporal gating of metabolic processes [[7], [8], [9], [10], [11]]. Among these, AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) functions as a key cellular energy sensor that promotes catabolic metabolism and coordinates glucose and lipid utilization [[12], [13], [14]]. AMPK also interfaces with the circadian clock: AMPK activation destabilizes CRY proteins and alters clock gene expression in cultured mouse fibroblasts [15] and in mouse skeletal muscle [16], while circadian transcription factors, including the basic helix-loop-helix transcription factor differentiated embryo-chondrocyte 1 (DEC1), and oscillatory cAMP-protein kinase A signaling reciprocally modulate AMPK activity [17,18]. Together, these findings indicate reciprocal regulation between the circadian molecular clock and AMPK.

The mechanism by which AMPK regulates specific skeletal muscle clock components and influences metabolic programming remain incompletely understood. In particular, whether AMPK signaling is required to maintain expression of core clock genes such as NPAS2 and NPAS2-dependent downstream metabolic programs in human skeletal muscle remains unresolved. Here, we test the hypothesis that AMPKα regulates skeletal muscle circadian gene expression and investigate whether AMPK-regulated clock components influence downstream metabolic programming, focusing on NPAS2 and DBP as representative core and clock-controlled components linking energy sensing to metabolism.

Methods and materials

Human primary muscle cell culture

Primary cells were obtained from vastus lateralis biopsies collected from 7 male donors as approved by the Ethical Committee in Stockholm. Clinical characteristics of the donors are shown in Table 1. Myoblasts were proliferated in growth medium (F12/DMEM, 25 mM glucose; no. 31331; Thermo Fisher Scientific) supplemented with 100 U/mL penicillin, 100 μg/mL of streptomycin, and 0.25 μg/mL of amphotericin B and 20% fetal bovine serum (FBS). Differentiation was performed in DMEM with GlutaMAX (no. 31966; Gibco), supplemented with 20% medium 199 (no. 31150; Gibco), HEPES buffer (0.02 M; no. 15630; Gibco), zinc sulfate (0.03 μg/mL), vitamin B12 (1.4 μg/mL; Sigma–Aldrich), insulin (10 μg/mL; Actrapid; Novo Nordisk), and apo-transferrin (100 μg/mL; no. T100-5; BBI Solutions). On day 4, cultures were switched to post-fusion medium, containing DMEM/M199, HEPES, zinc sulfate, vitamin B12, and 2% FBS until day 6. Differentiation was evaluated microscopically and by the analysis of myogenic markers (Fig. S1a).

siRNA transfection and synchronization

On day 6 of differentiation, myotubes were simultaneously treated with predesigned validated Silencer Select siRNAs towards protein kinase AMP-activated catalytic subunit alpha 1 (5 nM, PRKAA1; no. 4390824, Thermo Fisher) and protein kinase AMP-activated catalytic subunit alpha 2 (PRKAA2) (5 nM; no. 4392420, Thermo Fisher), DBP (10 nM; no. 114134, Thermo Fisher), NPAS2 (10 nM; no. 106844, Thermo Fisher), or Negative control No. 2 (10 nM; no. 4390847, Thermo Fisher). Each siRNA was diluted to 10 nM in OPTI-MEM (50 μL), then combined with Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen, Sweden) diluted in OPTI-MEM according to the manufacturer's instructions. The reagent mixtures were combined and placed on a mechanical agitator for 30 min. For transfection, myotubes were rinsed twice with PBS, and 1 mL of DMEM was added per well. Next, 100 μl of siRNA transfection complex was added to the media for 5 h. Myotubes were then washed twice with PBS and maintained in growth medium until day 8, at which point this process was repeated identically. Serum shock was performed utilizing 50% serum DMEM, with samples collected in 6-hour intervals from 24 to 54 h post-shock (Figure 1A). For DBP and NPAS2-siRNA-treated cells, serum shock was performed using 50% serum DMEM, with samples collected at 48 h post-shock, as AMPKα knockdown effects on circadian gene expression were generally most robust at this time point while minimizing potential acute effects of the synchronization procedure.

Palmitate oxidation assay

At 48 h post synchronization, myotubes treated with DBP-siRNA, NPAS2-siRNA, or scramble control siRNA were serum-starved for 1 h in low-glucose DMEM (5.5 mM). Myotubes were then incubated for 6 h in 1 mL of low-glucose DMEM supplemented with 25 μM palmitate, including radioactive palmitate ([9,10-3H(N)] palmitate) at a concentration of 0.078 μM (specific activity of 5 mCi/mL and 53.7 Ci/mmol). After incubation, the supernatant was collected and mixed with 0.8 mL of charcoal buffer (10% activated charcoal in a 0.02 M Tris–HCl solution, adjusted to a pH of 7.5) and shaken for 30 min. Samples were subjected to centrifugation (13,000 g for 15 min), and 0.2 mL of the cleared supernatant was taken for analysis by liquid scintillation counting. Myotubes were processed separately for protein determination using a Bradford assay.

Glucose oxidation assay

At 48 h post synchronization, myotubes treated with DBP-siRNA, NPAS2-siRNA, or scrambled control siRNA were serum-starved for 1 h in low-glucose DMEM (5.5 mM). Myotubes were then incubated with 2 mCi/mL d-[U–14C] glucose in the absence or presence of 120 nM insulin. A small empty cup was placed onto the medium in each well, after which the wells were sealed with a plastic film and incubated for 4 h at 37 °C in 0% CO2. Subsequently, [14C]-CO2 was liberated from the medium by addition of 150 μL of 2 M HCl and trapped by addition of 300 μL of 2 M NaOH, followed by a further 1-hour incubation at 37 °C in 0% CO2. Trapped [14C]-CO2 was quantified by liquid scintillation counting. Cells were then lysed in 0.5 M NaOH, neutralized with 2 M HCl, and protein content was determined using a BCA protein assay.

Lactate assay in cell media

Cell supernatant was collected from all silencing conditions at 48 h post-synchronization. Lactate concentration in the medium was determined using a colorimetric assay [19], with modifications as described [20].

Determination of gene expression

RNA was isolated using a trizol-chloroform extraction procedure (Fisher Scientific) [21] and cDNA was synthesized using a High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Thermo Fisher). Quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) was performed on a ViiA 7 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosciences) using TaqMan Fast Universal PCR Master Mix (Thermo Fisher). Cycling conditions were 95 °C for 40 cycles for 1 s and 60 °C for 20 s for 40 cycles. Cycle threshold was analyzed using Quantstudio Real-time PCR software (Thermo Fisher; version 1.3). All results were normalized to expression of the ribosomal subunit (18 S).

Determination of protein abundance

Protein abundance was determined by Western blot. Cells were lysed in radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and homogenized by sonication. Lysates were clarified by centrifugation at 16,000×g, and protein concentration in the supernatant was determined using a Bradford assay. Samples were prepared in Laemmli buffer and resolved in duplicates by SDS–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis on 4–12% gradient gels. Proteins were transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes by wet transfer and stained with Ponceau S to verify transfer efficiency and for subsequent normalization. Membranes were blocked for 1 h in 5% skimmed milk in TBS-T [20 mM Tris–HCl, 150 mM NaCl, 0.02% (v/v) Tween 20, pH 7.5], followed by overnight incubation with diluted primary antibodies (1:1000) against ACC (Cell Signaling Technology, #3662 S, RRID), phospho-ACCSer79 (Cell Signaling Technology, #3661, RRID:AB_330337), AMPK alpha (Cell Signaling Technology, #2532, RRID:AB_330331), phospho-AMPKThr172 (Cell Signaling Technology, #2531, RRID:AB_330330), CPT1B (abcepta, #AP2532b, RRID:AB_2292207), GLUT1 (Merck, CBL242, RRID:AB_327046), and subunits of the electron transport chain (Total OXPHOS human WB Antibody Cocktail, abcam, ab110411, RRID:AB_2756818). After washing, membranes were incubated for 1 h with the appropriate horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:25,000). Signals were detected by enhanced chemiluminescence and visualized on X-ray film, which was subsequently scanned using a GS-800 Densitometer (Bio-Rad). After blot analysis for phospho-AMPKThr172 and phospho-ACCSer79, membranes were stripped and re-probed with additional antibodies against total AMPK and total ACC. Band intensities were quantified using ImageLab software (Bio-Rad).

Due to the number of experimental conditions, duplicate samples were analyzed across two membranes. All conditions from the same donor were loaded on the same gel to preserve paired comparisons. Band intensities were normalized to the corresponding lane Ponceau staining and subsequently median-centered within each membrane by dividing each normalized value by the median normalized intensity of all samples on that membrane. This procedure standardized signal intensity across membranes, while preserving relative differences between samples. Median-centered values were used for statistical analyses.

Seahorse XF96 bioenergetic assay

Mitochondrial function was assessed by an extracellular flux analyzer (Seahorse XFe96, Agilent), which measures both oxygen consumption rate (OCR) and extracellular acidification rate (ECAR). Myoblasts (5 × 103 per well) were seeded in 96-well Seahorse plates, differentiated, transfected with the indicated siRNAs as described above, and synchronized. Plates were loaded into the instrument following a 40-minute equilibration in Seahorse DMEM (Agilent #103575–100) supplemented with 5.5 mM d-Glucose, 1X, GlutaMAX (Gibco #35050061) and 1 mM sodium pyruvate. Mitochondrial parameters were assessed by sequential injection of 1 μM oligomycin, 4 μM FCCP (added in two steps) and 0.5 μM rotenone/0.5 μM antimycin. For normalization, cells were fixed with 5% trichloroacetic acid (TCA) and stained with sulforhodamine B; the dye was resuspended in 20 mM Tris base, and absorbance was read at 565 nm. Rotenone and antimycin A, which inhibit electron transfer through complexes I and III, respectively, were used to calculate non-mitochondrial OCR, which was subtracted from all other parameters. The oligomycin-induced decrease in OCR was used to calculate ATP-linked respiration and proton leak. Maximal respiration was induced by the uncoupler FCCP. The FCCP-induced peak OCR was used to calculate spare respiratory capacity and maximal OCR.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Analyses were performed using one- (main effect of siRNA) or two-way repeated measures ANOVA (main effects of siRNA and time) followed by Dunnett's or Tukey's test, respectively. Data from experiments with n = 3 were analyzed using Friedman's test followed by Dunn's post-hoc test. Rhythmic oscillations in gene expression were analyzed using the RAIN package in R (Rhythmicity Analysis Incorporating Non-parametric Methods, release 3.15) [22], with period set to 24 h, and period delta set to 12 h. Outcomes with P < 0.05 were considered rhythmic.

Results

Effects of AMPK silencing on expression of genes involved in energy metabolism

To characterize the influence of AMPK on the circadian expression of metabolic genes, primary human skeletal muscle cells were doubly transfected with siRNA targeting PRKAA1 and PRKAA2 (encoding AMPKα1 and AMPKα2, respectively) and subsequently synchronized via serum shock (Figure 1A). Silencing was validated by Western blot (Figure 1B) and, at mRNA level, efficiency for PRKAA1 ranged from 60 to 80% across all time points from circadian time (CT) 24–54 h (time after serum shock; Figure 1C), while silencing efficiency for PRKAA2 exceeded 90% across all time points (Figure 1D). This degree of knockdown was sufficient to achieve sustained AMPKα suppression exceeding the magnitude of diurnal oscillation. We next evaluated the expression of genes central to glucose and fatty acid metabolism. Expression of glucose metabolism markers, including solute carrier family 2 member 1 (SLC2A1, encoding GLUT1) and hexokinase 2 (HK2), was unaffected by AMPKα silencing (Figure 1E–F). Similarly, pyruvate dehydrogenase kinase 4 (PDK4), an enzyme involved in regulating substrate preference, varied with time but was not affected by PRKAA1/2-silencing (Figure 1G). Fatty acid binding protein 3 (FABP3) expression also varied with time, independent of AMPKα silencing (Figure 1H). In contrast, PRKAA1/2-silencing increased fatty acid transporter CD36 mRNA expression by approximately 300% (Figure 1I). Expression of peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma coactivator 1-alpha (PPARGC1A) was increased in PRKAA1/2-silenced myotubes, with additional time-dependent variation (Figure 1J); this increase may partially explain the elevated CD36 expression, as the encoded protein, PGC-1α, mediates the expression of genes involved in fatty acid oxidation. Expression of the PPARGC1A paralogue, peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma coactivator 1-beta (PPARGC1B), was unaltered (Figure 1K). Despite the increased expression of PPARGC1A, citrate synthase (CS) expression was unaffected by PRKAA1/2-silencing, suggesting that mitochondrial content was not altered (Figure 1L).

Effects of AMPK silencing on circadian gene expression

AMPKα silencing altered expression of core clock genes across the time course studied. Successful synchronization was confirmed for most clock genes in the scramble control condition, as DBP, BMAL1, CRY1, CRY2, nuclear receptor subfamily 1 group D member 1 (NR1D1), PER1, PER2, and PER3 all displayed rhythmicity, as determined using RAIN package in R [22]. In PRKAA1/2-silenced myotubes, only NR1D1 lost rhythmicity. Expression of the transcriptional clock activators CLOCK, NPAS2, and DBP was reduced in PRKAA1/2-silenced myotubes (Figure 2A–C), suggesting that AMPK influences regulation of molecular clock function. Gene silencing had no effect on the transcriptional clock activator BMAL1 (Figure 2D). Expression of the transcriptional clock repressors CRY1 and CRY2 was increased in PRKAA1/2-silenced myotubes (Figure 2E–F), consistent with a previous reported interaction between AMPK and CRY [15]. Expression of NR1D1 and PER1-3 was unaffected by PRKAA1/2-silencing (Figure 2G–J). We next investigated whether the clock genes downregulated by AMPKα silencing, NPAS2 and DBP, were associated with the expression of metabolic genes (Figure 2K). NPAS2 expression was positively associated with PPARGC1A and CS mRNA levels (Figure 2L-M), whereas DBP as negatively correlated with PPARGC1A and CD36 expression (Figure 2N-O).

Effect of gene silencing on glucose and palmitate oxidation

To characterize the influence of DBP and NPAS2 on glucose and lipid metabolism in human skeletal muscle cells, we transfected primary human myotubes with siRNA targeting either DBP or NPAS2. Myotubes were synchronized via serum shock, and gene expression and substrate utilization were subsequently determined. Expression of DBP and NPAS2 was effectively downregulated by the respective siRNAs (Figure 3A–B). In DBP-silenced myotubes, expression of both PRKAA1 and PRKAA2 was increased (Figure 3C–D), suggesting a bidirectional transcriptional relationship between DBP and AMPK; this effect did not reach statistical significance in NPAS2-silenced myotubes. Nevertheless, these transcriptional alterations did not translate into changes at the protein level, as assessed by Western blot 48 h post-synchronization (Figure 3E–F). Expression of genes involved in glucose metabolism was unaffected by any of the silencing conditions (Figure 3G–I), although protein content of the glucose transporter GLUT1 was increased in NPAS2-silenced myotubes (Figure 3J). Despite the absence of changes in AMPK or related metabolic gene expression, glucose oxidation rates were reduced under both basal and insulin-stimulated conditions (Figure 3K), despite increased GLUT1 protein abundance, and lactate production was unchanged (Figure 3L). Collectively, these data suggest that DBP and NPAS2 may regulate oxidative metabolism independently of detectable alterations in proximal AMPK signaling. We therefore assessed whether DBP and NPAS2 silencing altered substrate partitioning by enhancing fatty acid metabolism. Expression and phosphorylation of the AMPK downstream target acetyl-CoA carboxylase alpha (ACC) as well as the rate-limiting step of mitochondrial β-oxidation, carnitine palmitoyltransferase 1B (CPT1B), were unaffected by DBP and NPAS2 silencing (Figure 3M-O). Nevertheless, relative mRNA levels of FABP3 and CD36 were upregulated in NPAS2- and DBP-silenced myotubes, respectively (Figure 3P-Q), whereas expression of ACAA2 (Figure 3R), PPARA (Figure 3R), and RORA (Figure 3T), genes involved in fatty acid oxidation and circadian-metabolic regulation, was reduced. Furthermore, hypoxia inducible factor 1 subunit alpha (HIF1A) was downregulated upon NPAS2 silencing (Figure 3U). NPAS2 interacts with HIF-1α, a transcription factor that reprograms fuel preference towards glycolytic energy metabolism and suppresses fatty acid oxidation [23,24]. Consistent with these findings, β-oxidation flux increased following NPAS2 silencing (Figure 3V). Together with the observed reduction in glucose oxidation, these findings suggest that DBP and NPAS2 differentially impact substrate utilization (Figure 3W), at least in part independently of the AMPK-ACC axis (Figure 3E–F, M-O, X).

Effect of gene silencing on mitochondrial function

We next assessed whether the reduction in glucose oxidation reflected impaired mitochondrial function. NPAS2 silencing increased expression of PPARGC1A (Figure 4A), a key regulator of mitochondrial biogenesis. However, this was not accompanied by a corresponding increase in CS abundance (Figure 4B,G). Notably, NPAS2-silenced myotubes exhibited reduced abundance of multiple electron transport chain subunits, including complex I (NADH:ubiquinone oxidoreductase subunit A8, NDUFA8), complex III (ubiquinol-cytochrome c reductase core protein 2, UQCRC2), and complex IV (Mitochondrially Encoded Cytochrome C Oxidase II, COXII) components (Figure 4C-E,G), whereas complex V (ATP synthase F1 subunit alpha, ATP5A) remained unchanged (Figure 4F, G). These data suggest that NPAS2 silencing is associated with selective impairment of respiratory chain composition rather than a global reduction in mitochondrial abundance. To functionally assess these alterations, we performed Seahorse mitochondrial respirometry (Figure 4H). NPAS2 silencing reduced basal respiration (Figure 4I) and ATP-linked respiration (Figure 4J), consistent with diminished oxidative phosphorylation capacity. In contrast, spare respiratory capacity (Figure 4K), maximal respiration (Figure 4L), and proton leak (Figure 4M) were not significantly altered. Extracellular acidification rate (ECAR) was measured in parallel (Figure 4N). NPAS2 silencing reduced basal ECAR (Figure 4O) and oligomycin-stimulated ECAR (Figure 4P), whereas ΔECAR (oligomycin-stimulated ECAR – basal ECAR) remained unchanged (Figure 4Q). Because ECAR was obtained during a Mito Stress Test rather than a dedicated Glycolysis Rate Assay, the contribution of glycolytic and respiratory sources to extracellular acidification cannot be determined. Considering lactate production was unchanged (Figure 3L), reduced ECAR may largely reflect reduced respiratory acidification and/or lower overall metabolic activity rather than a clear reduction in glycolytic flux. Combined basal OCR and ECAR measurements are summarized in the energy phenotype map (Figure 4R), illustrating a shift toward a lower basal metabolic flux state without compromising maximal bioenergetic capacity upon NPAS2 silencing.

Discussion

AMPK functions as a central cellular energy sensor that coordinates metabolic adaptation to energetic stress and has emerged as an important regulator of circadian timing mechanisms [14,25]. Consistent with our previous observations that pharmacological activation of AMPK modulates clock gene expression in skeletal muscle [16,26], we found that AMPKα silencing alters the expression of core clock genes CRY1, CRY2, CLOCK, and NPAS2, as well as the clock-controlled gene DBP. Increased CRY1 and CRY2 expression is consistent with previous evidence that AMPK regulates CRY stability [15]. Despite these changes in clock gene expression, rhythmicity appeared preserved, suggesting that AMPK influences circadian transcriptional output without disrupting core oscillatory mechanisms. Together, these findings support a role for AMPK in linking energetic status to skeletal muscle circadian gene regulation and identify NPAS2 and DBP as downstream components of AMPK signaling (Figure 5). The reduction in NPAS2 and DBP expression following AMPKα silencing suggests that AMPK-dependent signaling converges on both core and output components of the circadian network. Given that NPAS2 and DBP occupy distinct regulatory levels within this hierarchy, we next asked whether AMPK-regulated clock factors contribute to skeletal muscle metabolic regulation beyond effects on circadian gene expression alone.

AMPK-dependent regulation of circadian gene expression and metabolic consequences of NPAS2 silencing in human skeletal muscle cells. A) Silencing of AMPKα in primary human skeletal muscle cells altered expression of circadian genes, increasing expression of the core clock repressors CRY1 and CRY2, while reducing expression of NPAS2 and the clock-controlled transcription factor DBP. B) Proposed model summarizing the metabolic effects of NPAS2 silencing. Reduction of NPAS2 expression decreased glucose oxidation while increasing fatty acid utilization. NPAS2 silencing was additionally associated with reduced abundance of electron transport chain (ETC) complexes I, III, and IV and reduced basal oxidative phosphorylation, whereas spare respiratory capacity and maximal respiratory capacity were preserved. Reduced HIF1A expression may represent a link between NPAS2 knockdown and the suppression of glucose metabolism. Collectively, these findings support a role for NPAS2 in coordinating substrate utilization and mitochondrial metabolism in skeletal muscle cells.

NPAS2 is a CLOCK paralog that can compensate for CLOCK deficiency in circadian regulation, whereas combined loss of both CLOCK and NPAS2 disrupts circadian behavior [27]. Although the role of NPAS2 in human skeletal muscle metabolism remains poorly understood, NPAS2 has been reported to promote aerobic glycolysis while suppressing mitochondrial oxidative metabolism in hepatocellular carcinoma cells [24]. Here, NPAS2 silencing increased PPARGC1A expression, suggesting activation of transcriptional programs typically associated with oxidative metabolism and mitochondrial adaptation. However, expression of RORA, a known regulator of NPAS2 and lipid metabolism [28], and ACAA2, a mitochondrial β-oxidation enzyme, was reduced, indicating broader metabolic transcriptional remodeling rather than a coordinated induction of oxidative pathways. We found that NPAS2 silencing reduced basal and ATP-linked mitochondrial respiration, accompanied by reduced abundance of multiple electron transport chain subunits and a relative increase in β-oxidation flux. Despite these changes, maximal respiratory capacity was preserved, consistent with preserved mitochondrial oxidative capacity and the presence of excess the electron transport chain capacity [29]. Thus, the increased PPARGC1A expression induced by NPAS2 silencing may represent a compensatory adaptation to the metabolic perturbations associated with NPAS2 deficiency.

The altered substrate utilization and relative increase in fatty acid utilization following NPAS2 silencing is consistent with the well-established reciprocal relationship between lipid and glucose metabolic pathways, whereby increased fatty acid oxidation is associated with reduced glucose utilization [30]. Consistent with this concept, experimentally induced circadian misalignment in humans promotes fatty acid metabolism gene signatures and reduces skeletal muscle glucose disposal [31], compatible with a potential role for NPAS2 in regulating substrate preference. Alternatively, the reduction in glucose oxidation may reflect the broader suppression of basal oxidative metabolism observed following NPAS2 silencing, suggesting that impaired mitochondrial utilization of glucose-derived substrates contributes to the phenotype, despite preserved maximal respiratory capacity. In this context, the modest increases in GLUT1 abundance and fatty acid utilization may represent secondary and transient adaptations to reduced oxidative metabolism, rather than primary drivers of the observed metabolic phenotype.

These metabolic alterations were not associated with basal changes in the AMPK-ACC axis, a major regulator of skeletal muscle fatty acid oxidation, nor with altered transcription of the metabolic regulators HK2 and PDK4. Instead, NPAS2 silencing reduced HIF1A expression, suggesting an alternative transcriptional mechanism underlying the observed metabolic phenotype. Disruption of other core clock components, such as BMAL1 and CLOCK, has also been linked to impaired HIF-1α signaling [32,33], supporting the existence of functional interactions between the molecular clock and hypoxia-responsive transcriptional programs. Direct causal testing of this pathway, for example through HIF1A knockdown or overexpression, will be necessary to establish this mechanism definitively. HIF-1α is a central transcriptional regulator of glycolytic metabolism that promotes expression of genes involved in glucose uptake and glycolysis while suppressing oxidative metabolism [34]. In skeletal muscle, circadian clock proteins interact with HIF-1α to coordinate glycolytic gene expression and oxygen-dependent metabolism, and disruption of this signaling axis impairs glycolytic metabolic programs [33,35]. Accordingly, reduced HIF1A expression may contribute to the diminished glucose utilization observed in NPAS2-silenced myotubes.

Silencing of the clock-controlled transcription factor DBP also reduced glucose oxidation. However, DBP knockdown did not consistently alter mitochondrial respiration or β-oxidation flux, although directional trends were observed. Together, these findings suggest that the metabolic effects of NPAS2 are not unique among circadian transcriptional regulators expressed in skeletal muscle. Consistent with this interpretation, silencing of CLOCK in primary human myotubes reduces glucose uptake, decreases the expression of HIF1-α-target genes, and increases the expression of genes involved in lipid handling and fatty acid metabolism [32]. These findings support a broader role for the skeletal muscle clock in regulating substrate utilization and metabolic homeostasis.

Collectively, NPAS2 appears to contribute to the coordination of substrate utilization and mitochondrial metabolism in skeletal muscle. NPAS2 silencing was associated with preserved maximal respiratory capacity, reduced basal oxidative metabolism, increased β-oxidation flux, and altered respiratory chain composition. These findings suggest a role for NPAS2 in integrating the core clock machinery with skeletal muscle metabolic regulation. Although NPAS2 and DBP are circadian regulators, we did not assess clock gene rhythmicity following silencing of these genes. Therefore, we cannot determine whether the observed metabolic phenotypes arise from altered circadian function.

In conclusion, these findings support the concept that molecular clock components are active regulators of skeletal muscle metabolic phenotype rather than passive timekeepers. Given the well-established association between circadian disruption and metabolic disease [36,37], a deeper understanding of the molecular pathways linking peripheral clocks to substrate utilization and mitochondrial function may reveal new opportunities to improve metabolic health. Further studies are warranted to determine whether targeting components of the AMPK–circadian clock axis can mitigate metabolic dysfunction associated with circadian disruption.

Limitations

A limitation of this study is the use of cultured primary human myotubes, which do not fully recapitulate in vivo endocrine, neural, and behavioral regulation of metabolism. Moreover, only male donor-derived myotubes were studied. Given established sex differences in skeletal muscle metabolism, potential sex-specific effects on circadian regulation and metabolic responses could not be assessed. In addition, metabolic flexibility was inferred from basal measurements rather than directly assessed using dynamic substrate-switching experiments. Additionally, the molecular mechanisms linking AMPK, NPAS2, HIF-1α, and glycolytic metabolism were beyond the scope of the present work but warrant further investigation. Finally, although DBP silencing produced effects on glucose oxidation, the limited sample size may have reduced sensitivity to detect additional metabolic changes.
