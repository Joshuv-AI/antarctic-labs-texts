Evaluation of Deep Learning-based Scatter Correction on a Long-axial Field-of-view PET scanner
Baptiste Laurent, Alexandre Bousse, Thibaut Merlin, Axel Rominger, Kuangyu Shi, Dimitris Visvikis

Abstract
Objective: Long-axial field-of-view positron emission tomography systems allow higher sensitivity, with an increased number of detected lines of response induced by a larger angle of acceptance. However, this extended angle increases the number of multiple scatters and the scatter contribution within oblique planes. As scattering affects both quality and quantification of the reconstructed image, it is crucial to correct this effect with more accurate methods than the state-of-the-art single scatter simulation that can reach its limits with such an extended field-of-view. In this work, which is an extension of our previous assessment of deep learning-based scatter estimation carried out on a conventional PET system, we aim to evaluate the DLSE method performance on LAFOV total-body PET. Approach: The proposed DLSE method based on a convolutional neural network U-Net architecture uses emission and attenuation sinograms to estimate scatter sinogram. The network was trained from Monte-Carlo simulations of XCAT phantoms [ 18 {}^{\text{18}} F]-FDG PET acquisitions using a Siemens Biograph Vision Quadra scanner model, with multiple morphologies and dose distributions. We firstly evaluated the method performance on simulated data in both sinogram and image domain by comparing it to the MC ground truth and SSS scatter sinograms. We then tested the method on 7 [ 18 {}^{\text{18}} F]-FDG and seven [ 18 {}^{\text{18}} F]-PSMA clinical datasets, and compare it to SSS estimations. Results: DLSE showed superior accuracy on phantom data, greater robustness to patient size and dose variations compared to SSS, and better lesion contrast recovery. It also yielded promising clinical results, improving lesion contrasts in [ 18 {}^{\text{18}} F]-FDG datasets and performing consistently with [ 18 {}^{\text{18}} F]-PSMA datasets despite no training with [ 18 {}^{\text{18}} F]-PSMA. Significance: LAFOV PET scatter can be accurately estimated from raw data using the proposed DLSE method.

Introduction

The emergence of long-axial field-of-view positron emission tomography/computed tomography systems has led to significant advancements in nuclear medicine, providing opportunities to new applications
Alberts et al. 2023
;
Slart et al. 2021
;
Surti et al. 2020
;
Alberts et al. 2021
;
Prenosil et al. 2021
. These systems provide extended body coverage and improved sensitivity, enabling reductions in either radiopharmaceutical doses or acquisition times
Mingels
et al. 2023
. However, despite these advantages, the geometry of long-axial field-of-view positron emission tomography systems is more prone to an increased impact of scatter coincidences due to the larger solid angle of acceptance resulting from the extended field-of-view. This presents challenges for data correction algorithms in terms of both qualitative and quantitative image accuracy.

Scatter coincidences, caused by Compton single or multiple interactions of one or both of the annihilation gamma photons with the attenuation medium, negatively impact quantification and degrade positron emission tomography image quality. Therefore, scatter must be corrected during the reconstruction process. Traditionally, this is achieved through methods such as single scatter simulation
Ollinger 1996
;
Watson et al. 1996
;
Watson 2000
and Monte-Carlo simulations
Barret et al. 2005
;
Holdsworth et al. 2002
;
Levin et al. 1995
. The single scatter simulation approach, which is the most popular and widely used scatter correction in clinical practice, requires an additional scaling step for the single scatter estimation to account for the presence of multiple scatters. However, since the distribution of multiple scatters differs from that of single scatters
Tsoumpas et al. 2005
, scaling the single scatter distribution cannot accurately account for multiple scatters nor activity from outside the FOV, leading to inaccuracies. To address this, double scatter simulation algorithms have been developed, extending the simulation to include second-order scatters
Watson et al. 2020
;
Tsoumpas et al. 2005
. However, these methods are generally limited to scatter estimation in direct planes or make less accurate estimation of oblique plane scatter contributions, which are much more prominent in long-axial field-of-view positron emission tomography systems. In addition to variations in scatter distribution along the axial length of the system
Adam
et al. 1999
, it has been demonstrated that, in long-axial field-of-view positron emission tomography systems, the scatter distribution also varies with the axial angle of the plane, as does the single-to-multiple scatter ratio
Zhang
et al. 2017
. Incorporating oblique planes into scatter estimation can therefore improve the robustness and accuracy of the scatter correction process
Bal et al. 2021
.

Monte-Carlo-based methods provide highly accurate scatter estimations
Teuho et al. 2014
, but they are computationally intensive for clinical applications, particularly with long-axial field-of-view systems, where scatter estimation can take up to an hour per iteration
Bayerlein et al. 2024
.

Recent deep learning post-processing techniques aim at correcting scatter in the image domain by transforming uncorrected images into scatter-free ones
Jahangir
et al. 2024
;
Guo et al. 2022
. These methods benefit from shorter computation times and show promising results. However, they disregard important spatial information inherent to positron emission tomography imaging physics, such as Compton scattering, and may introduce artefacts or bias directly into the final image. In addition, as with most deep learning-based image generation approaches, such techniques lack in terms of generalisation and scanner independent applicability.

Within this context, the deep learning-based scatter estimation approach we proposed in
Laurent et al. 2023
estimates three-dimensional scatter sinograms from three-dimensional emission and attenuation sinograms. It has proven effective on conventional positron emission tomography systems, delivering more robust and accurate results than single scatter simulation. The goal of this study was to evaluate its performance in addressing the specific challenges of scatter correction in long-axial field-of-view scanners.

The rest of this paper is organised as follows. Section
2
summarises the basics of positron emission tomography image reconstruction with sinogram-based scatter correction using deep learning-based scatter estimation, as well as our evaluation strategy. Section
3
compares deep learning-based scatter estimation and single scatter simulation on simulated and patient data and the results are discussed in Section
4
. Section
5
concludes this work.

2
Materials and Methods

2.1
Background on Scatter-corrected Image Reconstruction

Image reconstruction consists in estimating the radioactivity distribution image
\bm{x}=[x_{1},\dots,x_{J}]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{J}
from a measurement sinogram vector
\bm{y}=[y_{1},\dots,y_{I}]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{I}
, with
I
being the number of positron emission tomography detector bins, and
J
the number of voxels in the final image. Given an activity distribution
\bm{x}
, the measurement vector
\bm{y}
follows a Poisson distribution with independent entries with expectation

\mathbb{E}[\bm{y}]=\bar{\bm{y}}(\bm{x})

(1)

where
\bar{\bm{y}}(\bm{x})
is the positron emission tomography forward model, traditionally defined as

\bar{\bm{y}}(\bm{x})=\tau\bm{A}\bm{P}\bm{x}+\bar{\bm{r}}+\bar{\bm{s}}

(2)

where
\tau
is the acquisition time,
\bm{A}=\mathrm{diag}[\bm{a}]\in\mathbb{R}^{I\times I}
is a diagonal matrix defined by the attenuation factors
\bm{a}=[a_{1},\dots,a_{I}]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{I}
,
\bm{P}\in\mathbb{R}^{I\times J}
is the positron emission tomography system matrix defined as
[\bm{P}]_{i,j}=p_{i,j}
for all
i,j
,

\bar{\bm{r}}=[\bar{r}_{1},\dots,\bar{r}_{I}]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{I}
is the expected random coincidences and
\bar{\bm{s}}=[\bar{s}_{1},\dots,\bar{s}_{I}]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{I}
is the expected scatter. The reconstruction of the image is achieved by finding an image
\bm{x}
such that
\bar{\bm{y}}(\bm{x})\approx\bm{y}
in the sense of
\bm{x}
is a maximiser of the Poisson log-likelihood given the measurement sinogram
\bm{y}
.

The reconstruction is commonly achieved using the maximum-likelihood expectation-maximisation algorithm
Shepp and Vardi 1982
or its accelerated version ordered-subset expectation-maximisation
Hudson and Larkin 1994
. This require an accurate knowledge of the hyper parameters of
\bar{\bm{y}}
in (
2
): the attenuation factors
\bm{a}
, the expected randoms
\bar{\bm{r}}
and scatter
\bar{\bm{s}}
. The attenuation factors
\bm{a}
are computed with a forward projection of the 511-keV attenuation map that is usually derived from a computed tomography scan. Randoms can be corrected for by real-time subtraction of a delayed coincidence channel
Knoll 2010
, and the scatter estimation in clinical systems is widely performed using the SSS approach.
Ollinger 1996
;
Watson et al. 1996
;
Watson 2000
.

The deep learning-based scatter estimation method we introduced in our previous paper
Laurent et al. 2023
aims to leverage the relations between emission, attenuation and scatter in order to estimate
\bar{\bm{s}}
from the measured sinogram data
\bm{y}
and attenuation factors
\bm{a}
using a convolutional neural network
\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}}
. For practical purposes, we exploit the attenuation correction factors
\bm{b}=[1/a_{1},\dots,1/a_{I}]^{\top}
instead of the attenuation factors
\bm{a}
. The network
\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}}\colon\mathbb{R}^{I}\times\mathbb{R}^{I}\to\mathbb{R}^{I}
is then trained such that

\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{y},\bm{b})\approx\bar{\bm{s}}

(3)

where
\bm{\theta}
is the vector of weights to be trained.

In the rest of this paper we assume that the emission data
\bm{y}
is random-free, i.e.,
\bar{\bm{r}}=\bm{0}
.

2.2
DLSE Method

2.2.1
Network Architecture

The deep learning-based scatter estimation network
\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}}
is based on a U-Net architecture
Ronneberger et al. 2015
. It processes concatenated positron emission tomography emission (without randoms, as stated in Section
2.1
) and attenuation correction factor slices derived from a three-dimensional sinogram with a dimension of
520\times 50\times 11559
(respectively corresponding to bin displacement, bin angle, and axial slice index) and produces predicted scatter sinogram slices. Each of the five layers of the network contains two
3\times 3
convolutional layers followed by a rectified linear unit activation function and
2\times 2
max pooling. To prevent overfitting, dropout layers are added at the end of the contraction path. The expansion path is similar to the contraction path but replaces max pooling with
2\times 2
nearest-neighbour up-sampling to preserve the initial sinogram dimensions. The network ensures that the output dimensions match those of the input sinograms. The architecture is shown in Figure
1
.

Figure 1:
Proposed deep learning-based scatter estimation architecture based on a convolutional neural network U-Net architecture. The network takes emission and attenuation sinograms as input and predicts the scatter sinogram.

The convolutional neural network was implemented using the Keras framework
Chollet et al. 2015
with a TensorFlow back-end
Abadi
et al. 2015
.

2.2.2
Training

The data used for training the network consists exclusively of Monte-Carlo simulations generated from radiotracer distribution and morphologies. For each experiment, we used a collection of
P
Monte-Carlo-simulated random-free positron emission tomography emission sinograms
\bm{y}
and scatter sinograms
\bm{s}
as well as the corresponding attenuation correction factors
\bm{b}
. The training of
\bm{\theta}
is achieved via the minimisation problem

\min_{\bm{\theta}}\,\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{L}\left(\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{y},\bm{b}),\bm{s}\right)\right]

(4)

where the expectation is taken over the
P
realisations of
(\bm{y},\bm{b},\bm{s})
and
\mathcal{L}
is a loss function we defined as the mean squared error, i.e.,

\mathcal{L}(\bm{u},\bm{v})=\frac{1}{I}\|\bm{u}-\bm{v}\|_{2}^{2}\,,

(5)

\|\cdot\|_{2}
being the Euclidian norm.

Note that the forward model (
2
) utilises the
expected
scatter sinograms
\bar{\bm{s}}
while we train our model with Monte-Carlo-simulated sinograms
\bm{s}
. In principle, the model should be trained with the expected scatter sinograms
\bar{\bm{s}}=\mathbb{E}[\bm{s}]
obtained by averaging several Monte-Carlo instances of
\bm{s}
. However, due to the high computational time, we only used a single instance of
\bm{s}
and we assumed
\bm{s}\approx\bar{\bm{s}}
. In the following
\bm{s}
will be referred to as the ground truth.

The sinograms were normalised for training such that
\bm{y}
,
\bm{b}
and
\bm{s}
range between 0 and 1. The output of deep learning-based scatter estimation were then denormalised. We also resized using zero padding so that their dimension is a multiple of
2^{5}
, five being the number of convolution steps of the network.

The Adam optimiser
Kingma and
Ba 2014
was used to train the model by solving (
4
) for 10 epochs and with a learning rate of
10^{-5}
as well as a batch size of eight.

used to perform the network training (

Phantom Generation

We used the extended cardiac-torso phantom

et al. 2011

312\times 312\times 200

2\times 2\times 2

For each labelled organ, we added a

2.3
Evaluation

2.3.1
Simulated Data

The phantoms used for evaluation were not included in the training.
Deep learning-based scatter estimation scatter sinograms were compared to the Monte-Carlo ground truth and single scatter simulation scatter sinograms. In addition to visualising scatter distributions and profile lines, we used normalised root mean squared error to evaluate its robustness according to patient size and injected dose variations, with normalised root mean squared error defined as

\mathrm{NRMSE}(\hat{\bm{s}},\bm{s})=\frac{\mathcal{L}(\hat{\bm{s}},\bm{s})}{\hat{\bm{s}}_{\max}-\hat{\bm{s}}_{\min}}

(6)

where
\bm{s}
is the Monte-Carlo ground truth sinogram and
\hat{\bm{s}}
is the estimated one (by either deep learning-based scatter estimation or single scatter simulation),
\hat{\bm{s}}_{\max}
and
\hat{\bm{s}}_{\min}
being respectfully the maximal and minimal values of
\hat{\bm{s}}
, and
\mathcal{L}
is the mean squared error loss defined in (
5
).

We then performed an evaluation in the image domain, after a reconstruction of the sinograms with ordered-subset expectation-maximisation (3 iterations, 24 subsets) followed by post-reconstruction filtering (4-mm-full width at half maximum Gaussian filter) using Siemens e7tools toolkit. Images were reconstructed into a
440\times 440\times 645
matrix with 1.65-mm cubic voxels. Monte-Carlo ground truth and deep learning-based scatter estimation sinograms were smoothed by a 2-pixel-full width at half maximum Gaussian filter before being injected into the forward model
\bar{\bm{y}}
used for reconstruction (i.e., the expected scatter
\bar{\bm{s}}
in (
2
)).

We evaluated the deep learning-based scatter estimation performance in various organs, by defining four 50-mm-diameter spherical regions of interest, in which we compute the mean standardised uptake value activity error. We finally simulated a phantom incorporating six spherical lesions of various sizes (10 mm, 20 mm and 40 mm) and locations (three in the lungs, three in the liver) to assess the deep learning-based scatter estimation performance when faced with cold or hot abnormal activities. The simulation was performed for three lesion contrasts, 0:1, 3:1 and 6:1, where the contrast is computed as

\text{lesion contrast}=\frac{\text{mean lesion activity}}{\text{mean organ activity}}

3.3
Computational Speed

The deep learning-based scatter estimation method was run on an Intel Xeon 3.70 Ghz 10-core CPU and NVIDIA RTX A6000 GPU. The learning step took two hours per epoch resulting in 20 hours for the whole training process. The prediction of a whole three-dimensional scatter sinogram of 11,559 slices takes 381 seconds (33 ms per sinogram slice).

4
Discussion

This study aims to assess the performance of a previously proposed deep learning scatter estimation method, deep learning-based scatter estimation, using raw positron emission tomography data in the context of long-axial field-of-view systems. It is worth noting that the training of the proposed deep learning-based scatter estimation approach is entirely based on the use of simulated datasets, hence removing the need for clinical datasets in the training process. The first step involved evaluating the method on simulated images, allowing for comparison with the ground truth in both the sinogram and image domains. In the second step, deep learning-based scatter estimation was tested on 14 clinical datasets, encompassing a range of patient morphologies, tumour sizes, and locations, as well as two different radiopharmaceuticals.

The sinograms produced by deep learning-based scatter estimation from simulated datasets are visually similar to the ground truth and exhibit comparable distributions (Figure
2
). Deep learning-based scatter estimation also demonstrates better scatter quantification than the single scatter simulation sinogram, particularly for larger phantoms, where single scatter simulation tends to underestimate scatter levels. This underestimation is more likely in cases of large morphologies that occupy a significant portion of the system’s field-of-view, leading to inaccuracies in the tail-fitting scale factor used to account for multiple scatter events. The results shown in Figure
3
confirm that the scatter sinograms produced by deep learning-based scatter estimation are more accurate and less sensitive to phantom size and dose than single scatter simulation-produced sinograms.

Reconstruction of simulated data corrected with deep learning-based scatter estimation closely resembles the reference image, exhibiting no significant artefacts in various organs or around the lesions (Figure
4
). In addition to demonstrating better robustness to phantom size, the quantitative analysis reveals that deep learning-based scatter estimation is more resilient to variations in injected dose levels, showing lower normalised root mean squared error disparity compared to single scatter simulation in the reconstructed positron emission tomography images (Figure
5b
). In addition to demonstrating better robustness to phantom size, deep learning-based scatter estimation provides better accuracy than single scatter simulation on all considered doses. The performance of deep learning-based scatter estimation consistently improves as the patient activity increases but it decreases as the patient size increases (Figure
5a
and Figure
5b
). Furthermore, the analysis of different organ activities (Figure
6a
) indicates an improved accuracy for low-activity regions, such as lungs, as well as for high-activity brain region. Deep learning-based scatter estimation also proved to be more accurate, providing closer contrast recovery than single scatter simulation, when compared to reference image lesion contrasts (Figure
6b
).

The study conducted on clinical [
{}^{\text{18}}
F]-fluorodeoxyglucose acquisitions demonstrated consistent results for deep learning-based scatter estimation, producing visually comparable outcomes to single scatter simulation-corrected images (Figure
7
). The method appeared to be robust against significant variations in patient morphology, with weights ranging from 52 to 98 kg. In two of the three cases, deep learning-based scatter estimation-corrected images exhibited slightly higher activity levels, while the single scatter simulation method showed higher activity in the large morphology patient. This discrepancy may be attributed to inaccuracies in the tail-fitting algorithm used to estimate the scaling factor for multiple scatters, as the tails could potentially be too small for larger morphology patients. In all three examples, the lesion contrasts were found to be greater than those obtained with the single scatter simulation-based scatter correction.

It is also worth noting that the acquisition duration for these clinical datasets is twice as long as that of the simulated training data (six minutes compared to three minutes). These longer acquisition times naturally result in a higher number of detected coincidences, with a mean of
1.2\pm 0.55\times 10^{9}
for the simulated dataset, compared to a mean of
3.6\pm 0.6\times 10^{9}
coincidences for the [
{}^{\text{18}}
F]-fluorodeoxyglucose datasets. Consequently, the method is capable of adapting to different statistical levels in the data, which may arise from variations in radiopharmaceutical doses or acquisition durations.

Finally, Figure
9
shows visually similar results between deep learning-based scatter estimation and single scatter simulation-corrected images. Furthermore, maximum standardised uptake values were found to be similar between the two methods (Figure
10
). These results suggest that the method could adapt from one radiopharmaceutical to another without the need for retraining.

The prediction of the three-dimensional scatter sinogram takes about 380 seconds. In comparison, the single scatter simulation three-dimensional scatter estimation requires approximately 100 seconds, while the double scatter simulation can take an average of 5.9 times longer than the single scatter simulation on systems with a large number of time-of-flight bins
Watson et al. 2020
. The speed performance of deep learning-based scatter estimation can be significantly improved by reducing the resolution of the input scatter sinograms. Specifically, the output scatter sinogram is smoothed with a Gaussian filter before being incorporated into the reconstruction. For this study and the resulting qualitative and quantitative sinogram analysis, we opted to maintain the original sinogram dimensions. However, the pre-reconstruction smoothing step could be replaced with sinogram downsampling prior to network training without losing significant information in the final scatter sinogram. Reducing the resolution by a factor of 2 in each dimension would yield a level of detail comparable to that of the Gaussian-smoothed sinogram, and would decrease the scatter estimation duration to less than 50 seconds.

Realistic Monte-Carlo positron emission tomography simulations are computationally demanding; however, in the context of a deep learning-based method that utilises simulated data for training, the simulation processes only need to be run once.

Deep learning-based scatter estimation was tested on relatively high-count data. Additional experiments should include low-count positron emission tomography configurations such as ultra low-dose acquisitions or dynamic studies involving short time frames.

In this study, the sinograms were generated using a full-angle acceptance configuration with an maximum ring difference of 322. Since the method is applied independently to each sinogram slice, it can be readily used with maximum ring difference 85 data as they represent a subset of the maximum ring difference 322 sinograms.

The presented work has been applied to non-time-of-flight positron emission tomography data. A straightforward approach for adapting the method to time-of-flight data, which represents a potential direction for future investigations, involves applying the method to each time bin sinogram.

Finally, we compared deep learning-based scatter estimation method to single scatter simulation as it is the most common sinogram-based scatter correction method. However, a comparative study with the state-of-the-art image-based strategies would be valuable.

5
Conclusions

In the present study we assessed the deep learning-based scatter estimation method on a clinical long-axial field-of-view positron emission tomography system, representing more challenging scatter conditions compared to standard field-of-view positron emission tomography scanners. This method offers the advantage of directly incorporating multiple scatters and oblique planes in the data correction process. Similarly with the performance previously shown on conventional positron emission tomography systems, deep learning-based scatter estimation demonstrated higher accuracy on phantom data, demonstrating better robustness to variations in patient size and injected dose levels in comparison to single scatter simulation, also providing better lesion contrast recovery.
deep learning-based scatter estimation also yielded high-quality results on clinical data, showing improved lesion contrasts on [
{}^{\text{18}}
F]-fluorodeoxyglucose datasets and consistent results on [
{}^{\text{18}}
F]-prostate-specific membrane antigen-11 datasets, despite [
{}^{\text{18}}
F]-prostate-specific membrane antigen-11 activity distributions not being used during the deep learning-based scatter estimation training process. This study indicates that deep-learning methods applied to raw positron emission tomography data are effective for scatter estimation and correction in long-axial field-of-view PET systems. Future investigations could focus on deep learning-based scatter estimation generalisation across multiple scanner geometries without requiring retraining.
