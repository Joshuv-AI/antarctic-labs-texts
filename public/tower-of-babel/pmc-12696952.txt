Analysis of pathogen spectrum in bronchoalveolar lavage fluid, T lymphocyte depletion, and prognosis in patients with severe pneumonia complicated by ARDS
Authors: Dong-Xue Rao, Xin-Yue Sun, Rong Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo investigate differences in alveolar lavage fluid pathogen spectrum, T lymphocyte subsets, and prognosis between patients with severe pneumonia with and without acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS).MethodsA retrospective cohort study was conducted, enrolling 160 patients with severe pneumonia admitted to our hospital between April 1, 2021, and April 1, 2025. Among them, 80 patients had ARDS (ARDS group) and 80 did not (Non-ARDS group). Bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) was collected for t-NGS pathogen detection, T lymphocyte subset analysis, and 28-day prognosis assessment.Results1. Pathogens Profile: The ARDS group exhibited significantly higher BALF prevalence of Escherichia coli (15.0% vs 2.5%, P = 0.005), Enterococcus faecium (22.5% vs 5.0%, P = 0.001), and Candida albicans (25.0% vs 10.0%, P = 0.013) were significantly higher than in the Non-ARDS group. 2. Immunological Characteristics: In the entire cohort, the ARDS group exhibited significantly lower counts of total T lymphocytes, CD4+, and CD8+ T cells. After excluding patients with viral infections, only the CD8+ T cell count remained significantly different (P = 0.041). 3. Prognosis: The 28-day mortality rate was significantly higher in the ARDS group than in the Non-ARDS group (Log-rank P < 0.001), with a 3.77-fold increased risk of death (HR = 3.77, 95% CI: 1.98–7.15). 4. Risk Factors: Multivariable Cox regression analysis across the entire cohort identified vasoactive drug use, Escherichia coli and Candida albicans infections, and elevated CD4+/CD8+ ratios as independent risk factors for mortality. After excluding patients with viral infections, the independent risk factors shifted to vasoactive drugs, Escherichia coli, CRP, and AST. Detailed hazard ratios with confidence intervals are presented in the Results section.ConclusionPatients with severe pneumonia complicated by ARDS exhibit a distinct gut-derived pathogen spectrum and T-cell immune exhaustion, associated with poorer outcomes. Mortality risk in the full cohort was independently associated with vasoactive drug use, specific pathogen infections (E. coli, Candida albicans), and immune dysregulation (elevated CD4+/CD8+ ratio). In the non-viral infection subgroup, core immune deficiency manifested as specific depletion of CD8+ T cells, and mortality drivers shifted to vasoactive drug use, E. coli infection, systemic inflammation (CRP), and liver injury (AST). This study provides evidence for precise risk stratification and personalized treatment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40001-025-03589-x.

Acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) is a life-threatening non-cardiac pulmonary edema that can be triggered by various intrapulmonary factors (pneumonia, aspiration, etc.) or extrapulmonary factors (sepsis, acute pancreatitis, trauma, etc.), leading to severe hypoxemia, decreased lung compliance, increased arteriovenous shunting, and enlarged physiological dead space [1]. Severe pneumonia is one of the most common triggers of ARDS, and the pandemic of coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) has further increased its incidence [2,3]. The role of pulmonary microbial dysbiosis in the pathogenesis of ARDS has garnered increasing attention [4,5]. T-cell dysfunction, particularly the depletion of CD4+ and CD8+ T-cell subsets, has been demonstrated to be closely associated with the severity and poor prognosis of ARDS [6–13]. However, systematic comparative studies examining the pulmonary pathogen spectrum characteristics, T-cell immune status, and prognostic implications between patients with severe pneumonia complicated by ARDS and those with severe pneumonia alone (without ARDS) remain relatively scarce. This retrospective cohort study employed t-NGS technology to conduct an in-depth comparative analysis of pathogen profiles in bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) and peripheral blood T lymphocyte subset characteristics between two patient groups. It further explored key factors influencing 28-day outcomes, aiming to provide new insights for precise diagnosis and personalized treatment in this patient population.

Materials and methods

General data

This study was reviewed and approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Nanchong Central Hospital (Approval No.: 2025 Review (130)).

This retrospective cohort study enrolled eligible patients with severe pneumonia who were admitted to Nanchong Central Hospital between April 1, 2021, and April 1, 2025. According to the presence or absence of ARDS, the patients were divided into two groups: the ARDS group, which included 80 patients with "severe pneumonia complicated by acute respiratory distress syndrome" as the primary diagnosis, and the control group (non-ARDS group), which consisted of 80 patients with severe pneumonia without ARDS. Case data were selected and collected based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria.Inclusion criteria: (1) All patients were community-acquired cases at the time of admission. Additionally, The diagnosis of severe pneumonia in both groups complied with the clinical diagnostic criteria of the Chinese Guidelines for the Diagnosis and Treatment of Community-Acquired Pneumonia in Adults (2016 Edition) [14]; (2) The diagnosis of ARDS met the 2012 Berlin Definition [15]; (3) ARDS was induced by severe pneumonia; (4) Hospitalization duration > 24 h, age > 18 years, and complete clinical data. Exclusion criteria: (1) ARDS caused by non-infectious factors; (2) Hospitalization duration < 24 h, age < 18 years, or incomplete clinical data.

Methods

Specimen collection and management

Patients underwent bedside fiberoptic bronchoscopy in the Respiratory Intensive Care Unit (RICU) or general ICU at Nanchong Central Hospital. Following intravenous anesthesia, bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) was collected from the lesion site. It should be noted that fiberoptic bronchoscopy and bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) constitute standard diagnostic procedures for managing critically ill patients with community-acquired pneumonia. The decision to perform these procedures is based solely on clinical necessity and does not constitute an intervention for this research project. The samples were immediately stored in a − 80 °C constant temperature freezer and uniformly transported to the collaborating laboratory (Sichuan KingMed Center) for testing.

Analytical methods

Targeted multiplex high-throughput sequencing (t-NGS) was employed to analyze the BALF samples and compare the composition and structure of the pulmonary microbiota between the two patient groups. The principle involves multiplex targeted amplification of specific gene regions, followed by high-throughput sequencing of the amplified products to obtain detailed information on the microbial community. The procedure specifically includes: nucleic acid extraction, target gene enrichment, high-throughput sequencing, and data analysis. This method offers advantages such as high sensitivity, high resolution, and good reproducibility. The detection panel covers 231 common pathogens associated with respiratory tract infections, including 96 bacteria,82 viruses, 43 fungi, and 10 atypical pathogens.

Data collection

The following data were collected through the hospital's electronic medical record system: patient demographic information (gender, age), underlying diseases, clinical symptoms and signs, laboratory test indicators (including absolute counts of T lymphocyte subsets), treatment regimens (antibiotics, invasive mechanical ventilation, glucocorticoids, vasoactive drugs, etc.), initial antibiotic treatment efficacy (defined as meeting all four criteria: improvement in body temperature within 72 h; stable or improved SOFA score; stable or improved oxygenation; no mortality within 72 h), BALF t-NGS results, ICU length of stay, total length of hospital stay, total hospitalization costs, and 28-day survival status (the primary endpoint was all-cause mortality within 28 days).

Statistical methods

Continuous quantitative data were described using mean ± standard deviation if normally distributed, and compared between groups using the t-test. For data not following a normal distribution, median [P25, P75] was used for statistical description, and the rank-sum test was applied for intergroup comparisons. Categorical data were described as counts (percentages) and compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. The association between ARDS and 28-day mortality as well as in-hospital mortality was analyzed by plotting cumulative mortality curves using the Kaplan–Meier (K-M) method, with between-group differences compared by the log-rank test. Hazard ratios (HR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated based on Cox regression analysis. To explore factors influencing 28-day mortality and in-hospital mortality, univariate Cox regression analysis was first performed with the outcome as the dependent variable to identify potential predictors (P < 0.05). Variables selected from this step were then included in a multivariate Cox regression analysis (bidirectional stepwise method) to identify independent influencing factors (P < 0.05). Group differences in the distribution of fungi, bacteria, and other pathogens under ARDS grouping were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, and visualized using population pyramid plots. All statistical analyses and related visualizations were performed in R language (version 4.4.1), with graphs generated using the ggplot2 package. A two-sided P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Analysis of baseline characteristics and comparison of laboratory indicators by box plots

A total of 160 patients were included in this study (80 in the ARDS group and 80 in the Non-ARDS group). There were no statistically significant differences between the two groups in terms of age, gender, or underlying diseases (such as hypertension and diabetes) (P > 0.05, Table 1). The proportion of patients with fever (98.8% vs. 88.8%, P < 0.05) and dyspnea (100% vs. 57.5%, P < 0.05) was significantly higher in the ARDS group than in the Non-ARDS group. Regarding treatment, the ARDS group received a higher proportion of invasive mechanical ventilation (76.2% vs. 33.8%, P < 0.001), glucocorticoids (62.5% vs. 38.8%, P < 0.05), and vasoactive drugs (68.8% vs. 32.5%, P < 0.001) compared to the Non-ARDS group. There was a significant difference in the distribution of reasons for glucocorticoid use between the two patient groups (P < 0.001). In the ARDS group, the primary indications for glucocorticoid use were treatment of ARDS itself (35.0%) and infectious shock/sepsis (20.0%). In contrast, the main reason for glucocorticoid use in the Non-ARDS group was acute exacerbation of COPD/asthma (20.0%). The durations of invasive mechanical ventilation [6.00 (2.00, 10.00) days vs. 0.00 (0.00, 5.25) days, P < 0.001], ICU length of stay [11.00 (7.00, 15.00) days vs. 5.50 (0.00, 12.25) days, P < 0.001], and total hospitalization costs [66,263.78 (40,670.87, 116,238.81) RMB vs. 36,615.87 (18,549.60, 72,864.21) RMB, P < 0.001] were all significantly higher in the ARDS group compared to the Non-ARDS group. However, there were no statistically significant differences between the two groups in the proportion and duration of non-invasive ventilation/high-flow oxygen therapy or total hospitalization time (P > 0.05). All patients received antibiotic treatment, and the rate of initial treatment success in the ARDS group (31.2%) was lower than that in the non-ARDS group (45.0%), but the difference between the two groups did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.073) (Tables 1 and 2.) Comparison of treatment measures between the ARDS and non-ARDS groups

As shown in Supplementary Figs. 1–6, laboratory indicators in the ARDS group exhibited significant alterations in inflammatory and immune function markers. Regarding inflammatory response, procalcitonin (PCT) levels were significantly elevated in the ARDS group (P < 0.001). In terms of immune function, both total T lymphocyte counts and CD4 + and CD8 + T cell counts showed a downward trend in both the ARDS and non-ARDS groups. However, the decline was more pronounced in the ARDS group, with statistically significant differences (P < 0.05). However, after excluding viral infection, the difference in CD8 + T cell counts remained significant (P = 0.041) (Supplementary Table 1). Liver function analysis revealed significantly elevated aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) levels in the ARDS group (P < 0.001 and P = 0.014, respectively). Conversely, no statistically significant differences were observed between groups for neutrophil percentage (Neu), C-reactive protein (CRP), creatinine (Cr), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), white blood cell count (WBC), red blood cell count (RBC), hemoglobin (Hb), platelet count (PLT), lymphocyte count (Lym), monocyte count (Mon), potassium (K), or sodium (Na) levels (P > 0.05).

Comparison of BALF t-NGS pathogen profiles between the ARDS group and the control group

Pyramid plots revealed no statistically significant differences in the overall detection rates of bacteria, fungi, or viruses between the two groups. In the ARDS group, the detection rates of Enterococcus faecium (22.5% vs. 5.0%, P = 0.001) and Escherichia coli (15.0% vs. 2.5%, P = 0.005) were significantly higher. A rising trend was also observed in the detection of Staphylococcus aureus (8.8% vs. 1.2%, P = 0.070). The infection rate of Candida albicans was higher in the ARDS group than in the Non-ARDS group (25.0% vs. 10.0%, P = 0.013). For other common pathogens—such as Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Acinetobacter baumannii, Haemophilus influenzae, Streptococcus pneumoniae, Aspergillus flavus, Epstein-Barr virus (EBV), and cytomegalovirus (CMV)—no statistically significant differences in detection rates were observed between the two groups (P > 0.05) (see Figs. 1, 2, 3).Fig. 1Bacterial distribution pyramid plot: ARDS group vs. non-ARDS groupFig. 2Fungal distribution pyramid plot: ARDS group vs. non-ARDS groupFig. 3Viral distribution pyramid plot: ARDS group vs. non-ARDS group

28-Day survival analysis

Kaplan–Meier survival analysis revealed that the 28-day cumulative mortality rate was significantly higher in the ARDS group (58.7%) compared to the Non-ARDS group (23.1%), and the difference was statistically significant (Log-rank P < 0.001). The risk of 28-day mortality in ARDS patients was 3.77 times that of Non-ARDS patients (HR = 3.77, 95% CI: 1.98–7.15).

Mortality risk varied over time: by day 5, the cumulative mortality reached 7.5% (95% CI: 1.5–13.1) in the ARDS group and 2.5% (95% CI: 0.0–5.9) in the Non-ARDS group; by day 15, it increased sharply to 37.9% (95% CI: 26.2–47.8) in the ARDS group and 9.2% (95% CI: 2.4–15.5) in the Non-ARDS group; by day 28, the cumulative mortality reached 58.7% (95% CI: 45.0–69.0) in the ARDS group and 23.1% (95% CI: 8.7–35.2) in the Non-ARDS group. Univariate and multivariate Cox regression analysis of 28-day mortality

Univariate Cox regression analysis of 28-day mortality

The results demonstrated that in both the ARDS and Non-ARDS groups, invasive mechanical ventilation (HR = 3.54, 95% CI: 1.78–7.05, P < 0.001), use of vasoactive drugs (HR = 14.37, 95% CI: 5.19–39.78, P < 0.001), and the symptom of dyspnea (HR = 13.38, 95% CI: 1.85–96.78, P = 0.010) were significant risk factors for 28-day mortality. In terms of etiology, infection with Acinetobacter baumannii (HR = 1.95, 95% CI: 1.15–3.31, P = 0.014), Escherichia coli (HR = 3.15, 95% CI: 1.58–6.27, P = 0.001), and Candida albicans (HR = 2.05, 95% CI: 1.13–3.71, P = 0.018) significantly increased the risk of death. Among laboratory indicators, elevated levels of AST (P < 0.001) and ALT (P < 0.001) were significantly associated with mortality. Glucocorticoid use (P = 0.067), CD4/CD8 ratio (P = 0.011), and creatinine level (P = 0.066) showed borderline significance. Other factors, such as age, gender, and underlying diseases (e.g., hypertension, diabetes), had no significant impact on 28-day mortality (Supplementary Table 2).

Multivariate Cox regression analysis of 28-day mortality

The results showed that after adjusting for potential confounding factors, the use of vasoactive drugs (HR = 14.45, 95% CI: 4.80–43.49, P < 0.001), Escherichia coli infection (HR = 2.87, 95% CI: 1.35–6.13, P = 0.006), and Candida albicans infection (HR = 2.34, 95% CI: 1.20–4.56, P = 0.012) were independent risk factors for 28-day mortality. In addition, an elevated CD4/CD8 ratio (HR = 1.00, 95% CI: 1.00–1.00, P = 0.002) was significantly associated with an increased risk of death, while invasive mechanical ventilation did not retain significance in the multivariate analysis (P = 0.129). This analysis confirmed that, after adjusting for other clinical factors, the use of vasoactive drugs, specific pathogen infections, and the CD4/CD8 ratio are independent risk factors for predicting 28-day mortality (Table 3). Notably, in the subgroup analysis excluding individuals with viral infections, the structure of independent risk factors underwent significant changes: vasoactive drug use (HR = 9.05, 95% CI: 2.63–31.14, P < 0.001) and Escherichia coli infection (HR = 7.42, 95% CI: 2.67–20.60, P < 0.001) remained significant, whereas C-reactive protein (CRP) (HR = 1.01, 95% CI: 1.00–1.01, P = 0.009) and liver injury marker (AST) (HR = 1.00, 95% CI: 1.00–1.00, P = 0.008) emerged as new independent predictors (Supplementary Table 3). Discussion

This study systematically revealed specific alterations in pathogen spectrum, immune status, and prognosis among patients with severe pneumonia complicated by ARDS, by comparing 160 patients (80 with ARDS and 80 without ARDS). The main findings include: (1) significantly higher detection rates of Escherichia coli, Enterococcus faecium, and Candida albicans in the BALF of the ARDS group; (2) In the entire cohort, ARDS patients exhibited significantly reduced counts of total peripheral blood T lymphocytes, CD4 + T cells, and CD8 + T cells; however, after excluding patients with viral infections, only the significant reduction in CD8 + T cell count remained; (3) ARDS was a strong predictor of 28-day mortality (HR = 3.77); (4) In the full cohort analysis, vasoactive drug use, Escherichia coli infection, Candida albicans infection, and elevated CD4 + /CD8 + T cell ratios were independent risk factors for mortality. However, in the non-viral infection subgroup, the independent risk factors shifted to vasoactive drug use, Escherichia coli infection, systemic inflammation (CRP), and liver injury (AST).

T lymphocyte exhaustion and immune imbalance

This study confirms that patients with severe pneumonia complicated by ARDS exhibit more severe T lymphocyte depletion. Our key novel finding is that, after excluding interference from viral infections, this immunodeficiency precisely manifests as specific depletion of CD8 + T cells, consistent with previous studies [7–13]. We support the mechanisms proposed in the literature: excessive inflammatory response (TNF-α, IL-6) inducing T cell apoptosis (via Fas/FasL and mitochondrial pathways), bone marrow suppression impairing T cell neogenesis, T cell exhaustion (upregulation of PD-1 and TIM-3), and negative impacts of treatments such as glucocorticoids and mechanical ventilation [6,16–18]. An elevated CD4+/CD8+ ratio was identified as an independent predictor of mortality across the entire cohort, likely reflecting primarily a marked depletion of CD8+ T cells. This finding was supported by our sensitivity analysis: in the non-viral subgroup, the ratio ceased to be an independent factor, while the intergroup difference in CD8+ T cell depletion remained significant. Dynamic monitoring of T-cell subsets, particularly CD8+ T-cell counts, exploring immunomodulatory interventions (such as attempts with PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors in sepsis-induced immune paralysis, IL-7, etc.), and optimizing existing therapies may represent potential avenues for improving the immune status and prognosis of such patients. However, these approaches require validation through rigorously designed clinical trials.

Characteristics of pulmonary pathogen spectrum and gut microbiota translocation

Utilizing t-NGS technology, this study identified a distinct pulmonary pathogen profile in patients with severe pneumonia complicated by ARDS, characterized by enrichment of gut-associated bacteria (Escherichia coli, Enterococcus faecium) and Candida albicans. Notably, Escherichia coli emerged as a consistent independent risk factor for mortality across both the entire cohort and the non-viral subgroup, underscoring its pivotal role in disease causation. This finding aligns with the conclusions of Dickson et al. [19,20] and Zhang et al. [21], strongly supporting the hypothesis of "gut microbiota translocation" in the alteration of pulmonary microecology and the pathogenesis of ARDS. Potential mechanisms include: intestinal barrier dysfunction due to severe inflammation and hypoxia; disruption of normal flora balance and selection of drug-resistant bacteria by broad-spectrum antibiotics; increased translocation risk from invasive procedures such as mechanical ventilation; and an overall immunosuppressed state facilitating colonization or overgrowth of these opportunistic pathogens in the lungs. Notably, Staphylococcus aureus did not show a significant intergroup difference in this study, which slightly differs from the findings of Zhang et al. [21]. This discrepancy may be attributed to variations in study populations, infection sources (community-acquired vs. nosocomial), or detection methodologies. The identification of Escherichia coli and Candida albicans as independent risk factors for mortality underscores their adverse impact on prognosis, potentially due to their virulence, ability to induce intense inflammatory responses, tendency to form biofilms, or existing drug resistance. This suggests that clinicians should pay heightened attention to patients with severe pneumonia and ARDS, especially those with detected presence of these pathogens, and select potent antimicrobial/antifungal agents with good lung tissue penetration based on drug susceptibility testing. While modulating pulmonary microecology—such as through selective digestive decontamination (SDD) (though controversial) or the exploration of probiotics/prebiotics [22]—has a theoretical basis as an adjunctive therapeutic strategy, its efficacy and safety in ARDS require further high-quality evidence.

Liver injury and systemic inflammation

This study found that after excluding the confounding effects of viral infection, both AST and CRP emerged as independent risk factors for 28-day mortality. This study found that after excluding the confounding effects of viral infection, AST and CRP jointly became independent risk factors for 28-day mortality. This reveals that in non-viral severe pneumonia, intense systemic inflammatory response and liver injury are the core pathophysiological processes driving prognosis. Analysis of baseline data provided preliminary evidence: patients in the ARDS group exhibited significantly higher levels of inflammatory markers (PCT) and liver injury markers (AST) compared to the Non-ARDS group. This disparity indicates that ARDS patients endure more severe systemic inflammatory storms and greater organ damage from the early stages of the disease. Elevated AST levels likely stem from multiple mechanisms: first, systemic hypoperfusion and hypoxic liver injury, where shock and severe hypoxemia cause inadequate hepatic blood flow; second, inflammation-mediated hepatocyte damage, with uncontrolled cytokine storms exerting direct hepatotoxicity; additionally, drug-induced liver injury cannot be overlooked. Thus, elevated AST serves not only as a marker of hepatocyte injury but also as a critical warning sign of developing multi-organ dysfunction. Its correlation with CRP jointly indicates that the body is not only engulfed in a severe inflammatory storm but that functional reserves in vital organs (such as the liver) are beginning to collapse. This underscores the paramount importance of dynamically monitoring AST alongside inflammatory markers in the management of non-viral ARDS for the early identification of such critically ill patients.

Invasive mechanical ventilation and ventilation duration

Invasive mechanical ventilation was a strong risk factor in univariate analysis but lost independence after adjusting for other factors (particularly vasoactive drugs and specific pathogens), indicating that its risk is partially mediated by disease severity and complications. Concurrently, ARDS patients experienced significantly longer invasive mechanical ventilation durations than non-ARDS patients. This disparity itself holds significant clinical importance. Longer ventilation duration directly reflects disease severity, indicating the intractability of lung injury and delayed recovery in ARDS patients. On the other hand, it also serves as a key mediator linking severe disease to poor outcomes. Although we excluded ARDS status from the multivariate model due to its strong collinearity with ventilation duration, it is crucial to recognize that prolonged mechanical ventilation significantly extends the patient's exposure window to iatrogenic complications such as ventilator-associated lung injury and ventilator-associated pneumonia, thereby indirectly contributing to increased mortality. Therefore, in clinical management, any strategy that safely shortens mechanical ventilation duration may yield benefits in improving outcomes for ARDS patients.

Prognostic factors and clinical implications

This study confirms that ARDS itself is one of the strongest predictors of 28-day mortality in critically ill pneumonia patients (HR = 3.77). Multivariate analysis identified key independent risk factors and revealed shifts in their composition across different patient cohorts: 1. Vasoactive drug use: As the strongest predictor (HR = 14.45 across the entire cohort), it directly reflects the presence of shock/circulatory failure—the terminal manifestation of uncontrolled systemic inflammation and multiple organ dysfunction—and correlates with extremely poor prognosis. 2. Pathogenic characteristics: Across the entire cohort, detection of Escherichia coli and Candida albicans infections independently increased mortality risk by approximately 2–threefold, underscoring the importance of precise pathogen diagnosis and targeted antimicrobial therapy. The risk ratio for E. coli infection surged dramatically in the non-viral subgroup, highlighting its central role in driving the prognosis of bacterial ARDS. 3. Immunological and Inflammatory Markers: Our stratified analysis provided a more nuanced perspective. Across the entire cohort, an elevated CD4 + /CD8 + ratio—a marker of immune dysregulation—was associated with mortality. In non-viral pneumonia patients, however, mortality was more directly driven by intense systemic inflammation (CRP) and liver injury (AST). This shift suggests distinct immunopathological phenotypes between viral and non-viral ARDS. Furthermore, after excluding viral infections, elevated CD4/CD8 ratios were primarily driven by extreme depletion of CD8 + T cells. As the cornerstone of anti-intracellular pathogen defense and immune surveillance [23], severe impairment of CD8 + T cell function may trigger comprehensive immune collapse, increasing susceptibility to secondary infections and ultimately leading to poor outcomes.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths, including its comparative design, comprehensive assessment of the pathogen spectrum using t-NGS technology, relatively large sample size, and integrated analysis of microbiological, immunological, and prognostic data.

However, several limitations should be acknowledged: First, the retrospective design carries inherent risks of selection bias and information bias. Second, as a single-center study, the generalizability of the findings may be limited. Third, t-NGS detects nucleic acids and cannot differentiate between live/dead bacteria or colonization and invasive infection, which may lead to an overestimation of the infection burden. Furthermore, it provides no information on bacterial viability or antibiotic susceptibility. In this study, the t-NGS findings were not validated by traditional culture or alternative methods (such as mNGS, antigen/antibody testing). Additionally, the cost of t-NGS is relatively high compared to conventional microbiological cultures, which may limit its routine application in primary care hospitals. Fourth, T-cell analysis was limited to quantitative measures and lacked functional assessments (e.g., cytokine secretion, activation/exhaustion marker expression). Fifth, detailed data on antibiotic exposure (types, duration) and its potential impact on microbial findings were not comprehensively collected or analyzed. Sixth, while the CD4 + /CD8 + ratio was included as a continuous variable in the model, its optimal cutoff value or clinically meaningful stratification for practical use remains unexplored.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that compared to critically ill pneumonia patients without acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), those with ARDS exhibit unique pulmonary pathogen profiles (enrichment of Escherichia coli, Enterococcus faecium, and Candida albicans) and more severe immune depletion, with significantly poorer outcomes. Comparative analysis across the entire cohort and non-viral subgroups further revealed that in non-viral patients, risk was more directly associated with specific CD8 + T cell depletion, infection with highly virulent bacteria (Escherichia coli), intense systemic inflammation (CRP), and liver injury (AST). These findings underscore the importance of enhancing precise pathogen diagnosis (particularly focusing on gut-derived bacteria and Candida), assessing immune function status (especially CD8 + T cell subsets), and actively preventing and treating shock in patients with severe pneumonia complicated by ARDS. Future research should focus on prospectively validating these findings, delving into the mechanisms and intervention strategies for gut microbiota translocation, evaluating the potential of immunomodulatory therapies (such as targeting T cell exhaustion), and developing personalized treatment and prognostic prediction models based on risk factors. These efforts aim to ultimately improve clinical outcomes for critically ill patients in this category.
