Impact of dietary transitions on overweight and obesity burden and future trends among Chinese adults
Authors: Pan Zhuang, Xiaohui Liu, Yang Ao, Zhen Yan, You Wu, Xuzhi Wan, Yin Li, Yuqi Wu, Yu Zhang, Jingjing Jiao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPrevious studies have examined dietary shifts and their impact on metabolic health in China, but the burden of overweight and obesity attributable to dietary risk factors remains unknown. Here, we found high intakes of both red meat and refined grains were the leading dietary factors responsible for the overweight/obesity burden. Although increasing intake trends were detected for whole grains, fruits, dairy products, legumes, nuts, and fish, their average intakes were still below optimal levels. The overall suboptimal dietary quality was attributable for 50.6% (214.9 million) of the total overweight/obesity burden in 2011, resulting in a medical cost of ¥32.5 billion, and was expected to be attributable for 477.3 million overweight/obesity cases in 2031, causing a medical cost of ¥313.6 billion. Obesity-related dietary guidelines and agriculture and food policies are warranted to improve diet quality and fight the obesity epidemic in China.Subject terms: Nutrition, Obesity

Introduction

Obesity has become a major public health crisis affecting 670 million adults globally1. In China, about half of adults are overweight or obese2, and the prevalence is expected to reach 65% in 20303, which was estimated from linear models assuming static dietary and policy interventions. Obesity substantially increases the risk of chronic diseases, including cardiometabolic diseases, dementia, and cancers4, thereby leading to decreased life quality and expectancy and bringing a heavy economic burden3. The main cause of obesity is thought to be overeating, which leads to chronic positive energy balance4. However, in addition to quantity, diet quality also determines the obesogenic effects of foods and affects metabolic health through a diversity of biological pathways5. Evidence has consistently shown that consumption of fruits, vegetables, legumes, and whole grains could protect against long-term weight gain, whereas increased intakes of red meats, refined grains, and sugar-sweetened beverages (SSBs) are associated with chronic weight gain5–9. Aligned with these effects conferred by individual foods, adopting healthy dietary patterns, such as Mediterranean diet patterns, has been reported to reduce body weight and visceral adiposity in controlled trials10,11. Therefore, promoting a healthy diet represents a high priority to reduce the global crisis of obesity.

In the past several decades, China has experienced drastic changes in dietary patterns, resulting in an overall poor diet quality12. Influenced by the Western diet, the transition towards diets high in calories and rich in animal-derived foods has remarkably expanded the obesogenic environment in China12. It has been reported that the suboptimal diet was the leading cause of mortality, responsible for 3.1 million deaths in 201713 and accounted for 51% of cardiometabolic deaths nationwide in 2010–201214. Such inadequate dietary quality could also have contributed to the increasing burden of overweight/obesity in China, but to what extent is unknown. Although poor dietary habits have been reported to be associated with cardiometabolic disease burden in China15,16, the overweight/obesity burden related to dietary factors has not been reported yet, based on nationwide data.

The pandemic of obesity in China has substantial health and economic consequences3, which impose a tremendous burden on the healthcare system. The State Council released the Healthy China Initiative 2019–2030 including the Healthy Diet Campaign as the second campaign to tackle obesity and related chronic diseases17, which aligned with the target to significantly slow the growth rate of overweight/obesity in 2030 as one of the targets of the ‘Healthy China 2030 national strategy’18. However, these policies and programs are inadequate to address the rapid growth of obesity incidence3,19,20. More awareness of the overweight/obesity burden attributable to dietary factors is warranted to combat the epidemic in China.

In this study, we assess the transition of dietary components and examine the related overweight and obese burden among Chinese adults over the period of 1997–2011, and project the trend to 2031. Medical costs attributable to the diet-related overweight/obese burden were also estimated. The results can help guide obesity-related dietary guidelines, agriculture and food policies, and facilitate formulating effective and sustainable solutions in China.

Results

Time trends of dietary risk factors

Dietary factors were classified into two groups: moderation components (lower consumption is preferred), including refined grains, red meat, and SSBs; and adequacy components (higher consumption is preferred), including whole grains, vegetables, fruits, legumes, nuts, dairy products, and fish. Between 1997 and 2011, fruit intake increased significantly over time among the Chinese population (P for trend <0.001 in all groups), more prominently in females, younger adults, and rural areas (all P-values < 0.05 for interaction) (Supplementary Tables 1 and 2). Consumption of whole grains, dairy products, and nuts had been growing significantly, especially in urban areas (P < 0.001 for interaction). Meanwhile, significant increasing trends were observed for red meat and SSB consumption, which were more pronounced among younger adults (P < 0.001 for interaction). Notably, red meat consumption among rural residents increased by 50% from 1997 to 2011. In 2011, the mean intake levels were 74.3 g/day for red meat [theoretical minimum-risk exposure distribution (TMRED): 14.3], 424.5 g/day for refined grains (TMRED: 150), 8.4 g/day for whole grains (TMRED: 100), 81.7 g/day for fruits (TMRED: 300), 12.0 g/day for dairy (TMRED: 400), 54.8 g/day for legumes g/day (TMRED: 70), 286.9 g/day for vegetables (TMRED: 400), 4.7 g/day for nuts (TMRED: 10), 29.8 g/day for fish (TMRED: 40), and 18.7 g/day for SSBs (TMRED: 0) (Table 1 and Supplementary Table 1). Overall, although increases in most of the adequacy components were observed, average intakes remained below the TMRED levels, especially for whole grains, fruits, and dairy products (far below the TMRED levels) (Fig. 1a). In contrast, the steadily high intakes of red meat and refined grains far exceeded the TMRED levels.

Burden of overweight and obesity

In 2011, the estimated number of overweight/obesity cases among Chinese adults (aged ≥20 y) was 427.9 and 327.8 million, according to Chinese and WHO definitions, respectively. High red meat intake was the leading dietary factor for overweight/obesity, followed by high refined grain intake. In 2011, high red meat intake was attributable for 20.5% of the total overweight/obesity burden, accounting for 87.7 and 67.4 million cases referring to Chinese and WHO definitions, respectively (Fig. 1b). The high consumption of refined grains was associated with 55.9 and 42.8 million cases of overweight/obesity [population-attributable fraction (PAF) = 13.1%] according to Chinese and WHO definitions, respectively. Insufficient intakes of whole grains (PAF = 11.4%), fruits (PAF = 9.2%), dairy products (PAF = 3.5%), legumes (PAF = 3.0%), vegetables (PAF = 2.2%), nuts (PAF = 1.3%), and fish (PAF = 1.1%) were also important risk factors for overweight/obesity among the Chinese population, responsible for 48.8, 39.5, 14.9, 12.8, 9.4, 5.5, and 4.9 million cases of overweight/obesity (Chinese definition), respectively (Fig. 1b). The estimated number of overweight/obesity cases attributable to SSB consumption was 2.1 million (PAF = 0.5%) in 2011. Suboptimal levels of all ten dietary factors combined were attributable for 50.6% of the total overweight/obesity burden (Supplementary Table 3), accounting for 214.9 cases in 2011. This estimation of 50.6% represents the theoretical proportional reduction in the prevalence of overweight and obesity that would be expected if the entire population shifted its diet to the theoretical minimum-risk levels, holding all other factors constant.

Time trends of the estimated burden of overweight and obesity

In 1997, the overweight and obesity burden associated with the high intakes of red meat, refined grains, and SSBs, and the insufficient intakes of whole grains, vegetables, legumes, fruits, nuts, dairy products, and fish were 71.3, 51.4, 0.2, 48.9, 11.7, 17.8, 44.4, 8.5, 17.2, and 5.5 million, respectively. Consumption of red meat and SSBs increased with more corresponding attributable overweight/obesity cases over time (Fig. 2 and Supplementary Fig. 1), and our model projected that mean intakes of red meat and SSBs will continue to increase to 98.2 g/day and 43.1 g/day in 2031, respectively, which would produce a further increase in related overweight and obesity burden. The increase in red meat intake was estimated to relate to 16.4 (Chinese definition) or 12.5 (WHO definition) million increased overweight/obesity cases during 1997–2011, and will be associated with a further substantial increase of 160.1 (Chinese definition) or 122.2 (WHO definition) million overweight/obesity cases from 2011 to 2031 (Table 2). The increasing trend in SSB consumption was associated with 1.9 million (Chinese definition) increased overweight/obesity cases during 1997–2011 and is expected to be responsible for triple the events comparing 2031 with 2011 (Supplementary Table 3). The constantly high intake of refined grains has been related to 4.5 million increased overweight/obesity cases during 1997–2011, and an additional 26.4 million cases are expected from 2011 to 2031 (Table 2). The “low carbohydrate quality” cluster (high in refined grains and SSBs) accounted for 13.5% of the overweight/obesity burden, with this share projected to rise to 20.4% by 2031 (Supplementary Table 4).

As the mean intake of fruits rose from 18.6 g/d to 81.7 g/d, the related overweight/obesity events went from 44.4 million in 1997 to 39.5 million in 2011 (Fig. 3 and Supplementary Table 3). In 2031, the related overweight/obesity burden is expected to be further reduced to 9.7 million due to higher fruit intake. In 1997, the gap between the average whole-grain intake (3.9 g/d) and the recommended level (100 g/d) resulted in a total of 48.9 million overweight/obesity events; in 2011, Chinese adults on average consumed 8.4 g/d of whole grains, accounting for only 0.1 million fewer events compared with those in 1997, whereas in 2031 another 26.3 million reductions in overweight/obesity burden are expected attributable to increased whole-grain intake (Table 2). The number of overweight/obesity attributable to low vegetable intake decreased from 11.7 to 9.4 million during 1997–2011, and afterwards the trend was projected to be stable at about 152 thousand cases annually (Fig. 3 and Supplementary Table 3). With the growing trend of intakes of legumes, nuts, dairy products, and fish, the attributable overweight/obesity burden reduced by 4.9, 3.0, 2.2, and 0.6 million from 1997 to 2011, respectively, and was expected to further decrease by 8.9, 2.9, 5.5, and 3.0 million from 2011 to 2031, respectively. Taken together, suboptimal dietary factors combined were attributable for 49.6% of the total overweight/obesity burden in 1997 and were projected to be responsible for 70.7% of the burden in 2031 (Fig. 4 and Supplementary Fig. 2). Subgroup analyses of PAF by age, sex, and urban/rural status are presented in Supplementary Table 5. These analyses revealed consistent differences across age groups for all dietary factors. Notably, the PAFs for low dairy and nut consumption decreased more sharply in urban residents, while PAFs for high red meat consumption rose markedly in rural areas and for high SSB intake in younger adults (Supplementary Table 5).

In sensitivity analyses using RRs from Chinese or Asian studies, the overall PAF for suboptimal diet remained stable at approximately 50% (Supplementary Tables 6 and 7). This stability was driven by a marked decline in the PAF attributable to low whole-grain intake, which counterbalanced the comparatively smaller increases observed for high refined-grain and red meat consumption. When the TMRED was defined as consumption at or above the 90th percentile for healthful foods or below the 10th percentile for unhealthful foods in the CHNS data, the analysis yielded similar but modestly lower PAFs compared to the original results (Supplementary Table 8). Additionally, under a moderate policy scenario (annual change of 5%), the total PAF for overweight/obesity attributable to suboptimal diet could be reduced from 64.0% in 2025 to 50.3% by 2031. A strong policy implementation scenario (annual change of 10%) projects a more substantial reduction, lowering the PAF to 37.1% in 2031 (Supplementary Table 9).

Medical costs of overweight and obesity due to suboptimal dietary factors

In 2011, the suboptimal dietary quality was attributable for a medical cost of ¥32.5 billion for 214.9 million overweight/obesity cases (Fig. 5). Assuming that the increasing trends in suboptimal dietary factors and prevalence of overweight/obesity would continue, we projected that the suboptimal diet would be attributable for 477.3 million overweight/obesity cases in 2031, producing a medical cost of ¥313.6 billion (US$46.4 billion) which was approximately 16.5% of total national medical costs (Fig. 5). Correspondingly, the per-event medical cost for diet-related overweight/obesity was projected to increase from 151.2 Yuan in 2011 to 657.0 Yuan in 2031.

Discussion

In this nationwide study of a large Chinese population, we comprehensively described the time trend in ten dietary risk factors and assessed the related overweight/obesity burden in China. High intakes of red meat and refined grains were identified as the top two contributors to the overweight/obesity burden in China. Although intakes of adequate components, including fruits, whole grains, dairy, nuts, and fish, increased over time, the overall levels were still below the optimal goals and would remain inadequate even for the next 20 years. If no further measures are taken, suboptimal diet quality is projected to be associated with hundreds of millions of future overweight/obesity cases, which would be linked to substantial medical costs.

High BMI has been estimated to be responsible for 4.72 million deaths and 148 million disability-adjusted life-years (DALYs) in the Global Burden of Disease Study (GBD) 201721. In China, among the leading risk factors for death, the high body mass index increased the most by 185% in summary exposure values (SEVs) from 1990 to 201713, highlighting the urgent need for effective solutions. Changes in lifestyles, including decreased physical activity and the westernized diet, have probably driven the soaring rates of overweight and obesity in China. Here, our results showed that currently over 50% of the total overweight/obesity burden was attributable to the suboptimal diet in China under the stated assumptions of the comparative risk assessment framework. Despite some dietary improvements, the diet quality would remain poor and contribute to over 70% of the burden in 2031. In addition, previous time trend analyses of lifestyles reported that suboptimal dietary factors were also responsible for millions of obesity-related chronic diseases, including diabetes16 and CVD15. Therefore, dietary strategies targeting overweight and obesity should be prioritized in the expanding Chinese healthcare system.

We observed that the dietary pattern continued to be westernized during 1997‒2011, which was mostly driven by the substantial increase in red meat consumption. This dietary transition likely reflects synergistic effects of rising disposable incomes, cultural preferences associating meat with prosperity, and broader socioeconomic shifts, including rapid urbanization and market liberalization. These forces collectively enhanced rural access to meat markets while normalizing energy-dense diets amid China’s nutrition transition. A global analysis also showed that the absolute increases in red meat intakes (5.9 servings/week) were the largest in China across 185 countries between 1990 and 201822. With economic growth and global trading development, meat production increased by 43.8 million tons during 1995‒2014 in China, and up to 78% of the meat production was accounted for by red meat in 201423. Meanwhile, the importation of red meat has also increased sharply to meet the domestic demands24. Although red meat is a rich source of high-quality protein, iron, zinc, Vitamin B6, and Vitamin B12, it also contains a high amount of saturated fat, cholesterol and heme iron and was considered a high-energy density food25. Red meat consumption has also been linked with an increased risk of obesity-related cardiometabolic diseases26,27. Additionally, a meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials (RCTs) supported that substituting red meat with plant protein sources could improve blood indicators of lipid metabolism28. However, to date, most of the Chinese people do not perceive excessive red meat consumption as a risk factor for obesity and related chronic disease, and the Chinese dietary guideline also does not yet recommend limiting the intake of red meat. Another characteristic of the westernization of the diet in China was the increased consumption of SSBs, especially in young adults, despite the overall low intake level. The detrimental role of SSBs in weight gain and chronic disease development has been supported by a robust body of evidence29. Therefore, more intensified policy efforts are warranted to reduce SSB consumption.

The high consumption of refined grains provides a high intake of refined carbohydrates that have been proposed to increase weight by the “carbohydrate-insulin model”30. The overabundance of carbohydrates induces endocrine dysregulation that drives fuel partitioning, with carbohydrates directed to adipose tissue, leading to adaptive increases in intake and decreases in energy expenditure. In contrast, whole grains contain dietary fibers and phenolic phytochemicals that beneficially influence glucose and lipid metabolism31,32, probably via modulating gut microbiota33. A meta-analysis of RCTs has suggested that whole-grain consumption may impact body weight by suppressing appetite and energy intake34. Therefore, dietary guidance should focus on carbohydrate quality over quantity. An 8-week dietary intervention study reported that a diet rich in whole grains significantly reduced weight and relieved obesity-related inflammation compared with the refined grain diet35. Our findings suggest that population-level adherence to recommendations for substituting whole grains for refined grains could contribute to a substantial reduction in the future burden of obesity in China, as most of the carbohydrates consumed are refined in the Chinese diet14 despite a slight increase in whole-grain intake over time.

Notable improvements in the intake of fruits and dairy products were observed in our study. However, the current intake levels are still far from optimal. The mean intakes were estimated to be 150.4 g/day for fruits (TMRED = 300 g/day) and merely 25.8 g/day for dairy products (TMRED = 400 g/day) by 2031. A summary of evidence from 8 RCTs has demonstrated that increased intake of fruits and vegetables could benefit weight maintenance or loss36. Recently, a gene-diet interaction study showed that the consumption of fruits and vegetables could attenuate the genetic predisposition to obesity, indicating that people with a high genetic risk of obesity could benefit more from increased fruit and vegetable consumption37. Although dairy products are rich in saturated fat, they also contain calcium and fat-soluble vitamins like vitamin D. Current evidence from human studies suggests that overall dairy consumption reduces body fat while increasing lean mass, and whole-fat dairy consumption does not increase weight38. An animal study suggested that dairy could limit weight gain by increasing lipid excretion in the feces39. Thus, fruits and dairy products are important parts of a healthy diet for obesity prevention. However, similar to our findings, the China National Nutrition Surveys also showed increased intakes of fruits and vegetables during 1982-2012, but less than 10% of Chinese people meet the recommended intake levels14. Greater efforts are needed to advocate for increasing fruit and vegetable consumption for Chinese people.

We also observed improvements in intakes of fish and nuts over time. Fish is the main source of dietary marine n-3 polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs), which have been evidenced to reduce fat mass by regulating adipokines secretion and lipid metabolism, alleviating adipose inflammation, and promoting the browning of white fat40,41. Evidence from RCTs also supports that fish or fish oil could lower waist circumference42. Nuts are high in protein, fiber, and PUFAs. Although they are energy dense, evidence to date suggests that nuts can increase satiety and lead to inefficiency in energy absorption43. In addition, PUFAs in nuts could promote postprandial thermogenesis44. A 12-week RCT showed that consuming Pistachio nuts as snacks significantly reduced body weight and triglycerides compared with a refined carbohydrate snack45. In our study, increased intake of fish and nuts was associated with a reduction equivalent to millions fewer overweight/obesity cases in the population. Dietary guidelines should continue to recommend consuming fish and choosing nuts as healthy snacks for obesity prevention.

While our findings underscore the urgency of addressing obesity, the feasibility of policy interventions warrants further consideration. Taxation of SSBs represents a promising approach, evidenced by its implementation in numerous countries, including the United States, Spain, Chile, France, and Mexico—though China currently lacks a nationwide SSB tax. Empirical evidence suggests a 10% SSB tax equivalent correlates with a 10.0% reduction in beverage purchases and dietary intake46. Complementary evidence indicates that lower fruit and vegetable prices are associated with improved body weight outcomes47. Thus, a dual strategy combining subsidies to reduce prices of healthy foods (e.g., whole grains) with taxation of less healthy options (e.g., SSBs) may constitute a viable pathway for obesity prevention and control in the Chinese context. Additionally, reducing the intake of risk factors (like red meat and refined grains) should be achieved not merely through caloric restriction, but ideally by simultaneously increasing the intake of protective foods (like legumes, fish, and whole grains) to maintain energy balance. While this study focuses on dietary drivers, it is critical to view these findings as part of a complex system. The rise in overweight and obesity in China coincides with profound shifts not only in diet but also in physical activity patterns, urbanization, and the broader food environment. Therefore, dietary interventions should be considered as one essential element within a comprehensive strategy that addresses these multiple determinants.

Our study has notable strengths. The CHNS is a large-scale longitudinal study of a nationwide population with a high response rate (88%), which is the only study of its kind in China and allows for modeling the trajectory of dietary intakes. The comprehensive measurement of the individual dietary intakes and other confounders, such as sex, age, and location, enabled us to precisely estimate PAFs for each stratum. Additionally, our estimates of the etiology effect were based on the most recent and best available meta-analysis on diet-obesity associations. Some limitations merit attention. First, although 3-day 24-h dietary recalls inevitably contain measurement errors and recall bias and may not represent long-term dietary intake, these were supplemented with a weighing method whose accuracy was previously validated48. Second, although estimation was based on the original data and the extracted RRs were multivariable-adjusted, potential residual confounding could not be eliminated. Third, conclusions about causality cannot be drawn from our study because our estimation is based on the theoretically ideal level of each dietary risk factor, with the effect size estimated from meta-analyses of observational cohort studies. However, the protective/detrimental effects and underlying physiological mechanisms of the selected dietary factors are also supported by evidence from RCTs and fundamental experiments. Fourth, single nutrients such as marine n-3 PUFAs and fiber were not included as risk factors because they could overlap with other dietary risk factors, such as fish and whole grains. Processed meat was not considered in our estimation because there is a lack of prospective studies supporting the adverse effect on obesity development7, and also it may overlap with red meat. Fifth, our projection for medical costs might be underestimated because we used the lower end of published cost estimates as historical estimates without taking into account the increases in healthcare service costs over time. Sixth, our data source, the CHNS, does not cover all of China’s provinces (e.g., several northwestern and western provinces are absent) and, critically, lacks sampling weights for national calibration. Therefore, the absolute estimates generated in this study are not statistically representative of the entire Chinese population and should be interpreted as reflecting patterns within the diverse but specific set of provinces included in the survey. Furthermore, our PAF estimates are presented as point estimates without confidence intervals. While we derived our relative risks from meta-analyses that provided uncertainty measures, a full probabilistic uncertainty analysis (e.g., via Monte Carlo simulation) that simultaneously samples from the distributions of RRs, exposures, and TMREDs was not feasible due to a lack of robust distributional parameters for all inputs. Therefore, the uncertainty around our absolute PAF estimates should be considered in their interpretation. However, the primary focus of this paper—comparing the relative contribution of dietary factors and the relative effect of policy scenarios—is less affected by this limitation. Finally, since the induction period of dietary factors was not considered, the estimated PAF in a given year could apply to a few years later. Nonetheless, it would be the same for the projected trends, emphasizing the importance of early dietary modifications to prevent overweight/obesity.

In conclusion, the population-attributable burden of overweight and obesity associated with suboptimal dietary patterns grew substantially in China between 1997 and 2011. This rise was particularly pronounced for factors characterized by high intakes of red meat, refined grains, and SSBs. Several improvements have been observed in dietary intakes of fruits, whole grains, dairy products, nuts, and fish. However, current dietary patterns remain suboptimal and are projected to contribute to a substantial future burden of overweight/obesity. Our findings indicate that a shift to optimal dietary patterns could prevent up to 70% of this burden in 2031. This prevention of cases translates to substantial economic savings, potentially reducing national medical expenditures by 16.5%. Public health nutrition strategies are in urgent need to improve diet quality to overcome the obesity epidemic in China.

Methods

Study population

The study population was composed of participants from the China Health and Nutrition Survey (CHNS), which is a nationwide longitudinal household-based study jointly conducted by the Carolina Population Center, University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill, and the National Institute of Nutrition and Food Safety, China Center for Disease Control and Prevention. The detailed information on CHNS has been reported previously49. CHNS aimed to assess the urbanization and economic change and the resulting effects on the health and nutritional status in China. The survey uses a multistage random-cluster sampling process to select samples in nine provinces (Guizhou, Henan, Hubei, Hunan, Guangxi, Heilongjiang, Liaoning, Jiangsu, and Shandong), representing 47% (553 million people) of China’s population according to the 2010 census. The selected provinces/municipal cities generally represent variations in geography, economy, and health indicators throughout China. Trained interviewers interviewed individuals within each household to collect demographic, physical activity, socioeconomic, health, and diet information. All individuals were recruited with written informed consent. Since 1989, nine waves have been completed. By 2011, CHNS covered more than 27,000 individuals from 288 communities and 5884 households. The response rates were reported to be approximately 88% at the individual level and 90% at the household level49. The study was approved by the institutional review committees of the University of North Carolina (No. 07-1963) and the National Institute of Nutrition and Food Safety from the Chinese Center for Disease Control and Prevention (No. 201524). All participants provided informed consent. We included participants in 6 waves of the survey (1997, 2000, 2004, 2006, 2009, and 2011) and excluded persons who were pregnant or under the age of 20 in each wave of the survey. For the comparison of time trends, we further excluded the data for three megacities that were available only in 2011. The detailed flowchart and characteristics of the study population in each wave are shown in Supplementary Table 10 and Supplementary Fig. 3.

Dietary assessment and covariates

Dietary information was collected by trained interviewers using 3-day 24-hour dietary recalls with a weighing method in a household food inventory50,51. Participants were asked to report individual consumption of all foods consumed in-home and away from home. Meanwhile, at the beginning and the end of each 3-D home inventory, all stocked foods (including cooking oils and condiments), food purchased from markets or produced at home, and food waste were carefully weighed and recorded. The China Food Composition Tables were used to identify food items and convert food consumption data to nutrient and total energy intakes (Table-1991 version for 1997-2000 and Table-2002/2004 version for 2004–2011). The accuracy of total energy intake calculated by this dietary assessment method has been validated48. We calculated energy-adjusted intake for each food group using the residual method to 2000 kcal/day52.

The other personal information was self-reported and also collected, including sex, age, weight and height, occupation, education level, smoking, physical activity, medical history, and region (urban or rural).

Ascertainment of overweight/obesity cases

Cases of overweight and obesity were calculated based on the Monitoring Report on Nutrition and Health Status of Chinese People53 and the National Population Census. Data on age-, sex-, and urban/rural-specific rate of overweight/obesity in 2011 were extracted from the Monitoring Report on Nutrition and Health Status of Chinese People and were combined with the corresponding population data from the 2010 Population Census of the People’s Republic of China to obtain overweight/obesity cases for each group. Overweight and obesity were defined according to the Chinese (BMI ≥ 24.0 kg/m² for overweight/obesity) and WHO (BMI ≥ 25.0 kg/m² for overweight/obesity) criteria, respectively.

Etiological effects of dietary factors on overweight/obesity

We selected dietary factors for which 1) intakes were recommended in the 2022 Chinese Dietary Guideline (CDG); 2) sufficient food-based evidence was available on the presence and magnitude of probable causal relationships with overweight and obesity; 3) available intervention strategies existed to protect against weight gain; and 4) data were available in the CHNS without systematic bias. Ten foods were selected as the major dietary factors for overweight/obesity, including whole grains, vegetables, fruits, nuts, legumes, dairy, fish, refined grains (grain foods with fiber to carbohydrate ratio <0.1), red meat and SSBs. Processed meat was not included because there is a lack of prospective studies supporting the adverse effect on obesity development7, and also to avoid the potential overlap with red meat. We extracted the relative risks (RRs) associated with overweight/obesity risk for each food group from the most recent meta-analysis of prospective studies (Table 1)7,54,55, which used multivariable adjustment for multiple risk factors to reduce confounding bias. The same RR was used for both genders across ages and urban/rural groups.

Data analyses

In each survey wave, the mean and standard error of each dietary factor were calculated by sex, regions (urban/rural), and 10-year age groups. We applied a multivariate general linear mixed regression model to calculate the covariate-adjusted mean intake of each food group, which accounted for the repeated measurements of the same person over time using a random intercept. To describe the time trends, we standardized the distributions of dietary factors in each wave to the 2010 Chinese Population Census data. Future trends from 2012 to 2031 were projected using a random-effects model within each stratum of age, sex, and urban/rural based on data from individuals with three or more repeated measurements during the follow-up. The proportion of >3 repeated dietary measurements was 53.8%.

Assuming a causal relationship between each dietary risk factor and overweight/obesity incidence, we calculated the PAF to indicate the proportional reduction in overweight/obesity cases that would occur in the period of analysis if the distributions of intakes of dietary risk factors had been at an optimal level56. To show how policy assumptions influence the attribution, the TMRED for healthful foods (whole grains, vegetables, legumes, fruits, nuts, dairy, and fish) was defined as consumption at or above the 90th percentile in the CHNS data; for unhealthful foods (refined grains, red meat, and sugar-sweetened beverages), the TMRED was set as consumption at or below the 10th percentile. Additionally, we modeled two alternative policy scenarios beginning in 2025: moderate and strong implementation. For healthful foods (e.g., fruits, fish), moderate and strong implementation were defined as an annual consumption increase of 5% and 10%, respectively. Conversely, for unhealthful foods (e.g., red meat, SSBs), the scenarios were defined as an annual decrease of 5% (moderate) and 10% (strong).

The detailed methodology was described in Supplementary Notes. Statistical analyses were performed with SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA). Tests were two-sided, and the statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05.

Projection analysis of the medical cost of diet-related overweight and obesity

Future overweight/obesity burden and medical costs attributable to the overall suboptimal diet were further projected, considering the changes in prevalence over time. In this analysis, the prevalence rates of overweight/obesity used were obtained from the Chinese National Nutrition Surveys (2002, 2010–2012, and 2015–2019) and the China Chronic Disease and Risk Factor Surveillance (2013–2014). Linear models were fitted using the year as the independent variable and the overweight and obesity prevalence corresponding to the year as the dependent variable to predict the prevalence in 20303. This approach assumes static socioeconomic and environmental drivers. The models demonstrated excellent fit (R²: 0.97–0.99)3. Our projections of future obesity medical costs were based on historical cost estimates from a published study by Qin and Pan57, which estimated the overweight/obesity attributable costs for 2000-2009 using an improved two-part model based on the CHNS data. With the assumption that the trends in costs of overweight and obesity observed in the CHNS would continue, we fitted linear regression models using the historical overweight/obesity attributable cost estimates as dependent variables, and year and year squared (to capture the nonlinear trend) as independent variables to predict overweight/obesity-related costs in China from 2010 to 20313. The number of overweight/obesity cases and related medical expenditure attributable to the suboptimal diet were then calculated by multiplying the joint PAF by the total cases and medical costs. This projection model did not adjust for future inflation or purchasing power parity, likely resulting in conservative estimates, as it neither accounts for the well-documented rise in healthcare costs nor utilizes higher-end historical estimates.
