Exercise attenuates stress-related signaling as sensed by higher phosphorylation of small heat shock proteins in skeletal muscle from older individuals
Authors: Noni T Frankenberg, Victoria L Wyckelsma, Dion Ellul, Aaron C Petersen, Itamar Levinger, Michael J McKenna, Robyn M Murphy
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Muscle fibers from young people have higher HSP72, HSP27, and αB-crystallin in type I compared to type II fibers which is not seen in muscle from older people.•Compared to younger adults, older adults display elevated small heat shock protein phosphorylation, suggesting higher muscle stress.•High-intensity training reduces muscle stress markers to levels seen in young adults.•Exercise may help counter age-related muscle decline by modulating stress proteins.Keywords: Single muscle fibers, Aging, HSP, High-intensity training (HIT), Fiber type

Introduction

Cellular stresses are conditions that disrupt normal cell function, such as oxidative damage, protein misfolding, or a lack of oxygen and nutrients. These stresses may arise from external sources such as heat and toxins or internal factors such as DNA damage and endoplasmic reticulum (ER) or sarcoplasmic reticulum (SR) dysfunction.1 In response, cells activate protective mechanisms to restore protein homeostasis or, if the damage is irreparable, initiate programmed cell death. Heat shock proteins (HSPs) are ubiquitously expressed molecular chaperones that play a central role in maintaining homeostasis and cellular function during stress by stabilizing proteins or targeting them for degradation.2 In mammalian skeletal muscle, 3 well-characterized HSPs include HSP72 and the small HSPs (sHSPs), HSP27, and αB-crystallin. While HSP72 abundance is commonly used as a marker of a cellular stress response, the activity of sHSPs is primarily regulated by phosphorylation—specifically, at Ser15 and Ser82 on HSP27 and Ser59 and Ser45 on αB-crystallin.3,4 Phosphorylation at one of these sites alters the oligomeric structure of the core protein, resulting in a more active form of sHSPs.5 This structural change enhances the chaperone activity of the sHSPs, their cytoskeletal interactions and anti-apoptotic functions, allowing for rapid and dynamic cellular responses to stress.5 Phosphorylation of sHSPs serves as a sensitive indicator of various cellular stressors, including ER/SR stress,6 ionic imbalance,7 ischemia,8 heat shock,9 oxidative stress,10 and proteotoxic conditions.11 Among the signaling pathways involved in the phosphorylation of sHSPs, the p38 mitogen-activated protein kinase (p38 MAPK) cascade is particularly well-characterized. Stress-dependent activation of p38 MAPK in a variety of cells leads to phosphorylation of HSP27 via MAPK-activated protein kinase 2 (MK2), providing a mechanistic link between stress sensing and sHSP modification.12 Similarly, p38 MAPK mediates phosphorylation of αB-crystallin at Ser59, while the extracellular-regulated kinase 1/2 (ERK1/2) pathway—activated by growth factors, hyperosmotic stress, and mitogens—phosphorylates αB-crystallin at Ser45.13 The phosphorylation status of sHSPs therefore reflects the activation of specific stress–response pathways and serves as a valuable molecular indicator of cellular stress. Pertinent to the current study, there is growing interest in the interplay between HSP phosphorylation, aging, and adaptations to exercise training.

Relevant to the study here, in skeletal muscle there are many cellular stresses that result in the induction of HSPs. These can be induced by acute exercise, particularly that involving eccentric contractions,14 exposure to heat,9 aging,15,16 and oxidative stress from reactive oxygen species.17 Together, these data indicate that the HSPs are responsive to cellular stresses in skeletal muscle, and so it is of interest to examine them under times of chronic stress, such as aging. There is an inevitable loss of muscle mass with aging, and the induction of HSPs have been implicated with age-related muscle atrophy.18 The abundances of HSP72, HSP27, and αB-crystallin have been variably reported as being either higher or no different in skeletal muscle from young compared with older adults.19, 20, 21, 22, 23 It is important to match for physical activity levels when comparing HSP abundances in an age-dependent manner because even acute non-damaging exercise alters the abundance of HSP72, HSP27, and αB-crystallin in skeletal muscle in both older (∼70 years) and young (∼24 years) adults.24,25 Further, despite the broad age range included in the cohorts, phosphorylated HSP27 and αB-crystallin were reported to be higher in muscle from older (51–79 years) compared with younger (15–38 years) adults,23 indicating a higher level of cellular stress in the older cohort.

The heterogeneous nature of skeletal muscle should be considered for quantitative analyses. Data from numerous animal studies indicated HSP72, HSP27, and αB-crystallin proteins were more abundant in type I oxidative compared to type II glycolytic muscle fibers at whole muscle level when comparing muscles rich in either fiber type26,27 and shown in individual fibers.28 In contrast, in humans, HSP72 abundance is similar between fiber types in young adults,29,30 but whether this was the case in muscle from older adults is unknown.

Aging is associated with impaired cellular stress responses, leading to disrupted proteostasis and higher susceptibility to sarcopenia.31 High-intensity training (HIT) has emerged as a potent intervention to counteract these declines, as it imposes positive mechanical and metabolic stress on skeletal muscle, activating protective pathways such as the HSP response. In the same cohorts used in the current study, we have previously reported elevated levels of mitochondrial dynamics proteins—mitofusin-2 (Mfn2) and mitochondrial dynamics protein 49 (MiD49)—in skeletal muscle from older vs. younger adults, suggesting higher mitochondrial turnover is a compensatory response to cellular stress.32 After 12 weeks of HIT, Mfn2 and MiD49 levels in older adults decreased to levels comparable with younger individuals, indicating a restored mitochondrial balance. We proposed that training reduced the cellular stress burden, thereby lowering the need for elevated mitochondrial turnover. In line with this, a similar adaptation may occur in the heat shock response, where aging may result in higher levels of phosphorylation of small HSPs (pHSPs), including HSP27 and αB-crystallin relative to young adults and that chronic HIT could lead to reduced pHSPs, reflecting a more stable, less stressed cellular environment. A reduction in pHSPs may serve as a biomarker of improved muscle homeostasis and a shift toward a molecular phenotype more similar to a younger cohort. Here, we explore the response of HSPs to HIT in a cohort of older adults to identify whether the HSPs offer a pathway that could be targeted therapeutically to promote healthy aging. The aim of this study was to compare the abundances of HSP72, HSP27, and αB-crystallin proteins along with phospho-HSP27 at Serine15 (pHSP27 Ser15), pHSP27 Ser82, and phospho-αB-crystallin at Serine59 (pαB-crystallin Ser59) in type I and type II muscle fibers from young and older adults. In the latter group, the effect of HIT and the fiber type abundance of the HSPs were also examined. We hypothesized that compared with the young adults, muscles from the older adults would have higher abundances of the pHSPs and that these would be reversed with 12 weeks of HIT.

Methods

Participants

Young (n = 7, 25.0 ± 3.7 years) and older (n = 7, 70.0 ± 4.1 years, mean ± SD) adults were a subset from a cohort previously described,33 maximizing the use of human samples. Self-reported regular physical activity in the larger cohort was 8.2 ± 2.6 h per week and the average was not different between the young and older cohorts.32 Table 1 shows subset details of participants in this study. All participants signed informed consent, and the original study was approved by the Victoria University Human Research Ethics Committee (HRETH 11/221) and conformed to the Declaration of Helsinki.

Exercise protocol

Older adults trained under supervision 3 times per week for 12 weeks on a mechanically braked cycle ergometer (Monark 868; Monark Exercise, Vansbro, Sweden), as previously described.34 A standardized 3-min warm up was conducted prior to every training session, after which participants had 1 min of passive rest on the cycle ergometer. The training protocol was based on a previous method35 and comprised 4 bouts of 4-min exercise intervals performed at ∼90%–95% of peak heart rate (HRpeak); each interval was interspersed by 4 min of active recovery, where participants cycled at 50%–60% HRpeak. Each training session was followed by a 5-min cool down, and all training sessions were fully supervised. The training program undertaken had no effect on resting systolic blood pressure or resting diastolic blood pressure or body mass.34 For this subset of participants, HIT increased peak work rate (watt) (25% ± 23%), total work (J) (60% ± 46%), peak oxygen uptake (VO2peak) (16% ± 12%), and HRpeak (6% ± 8%), (p < 0.05) and tended to increase exercise time until a rating of perceived exertion (RPE)-17 was attained (p = 0.057) during the incremental test.

Muscle biopsy technique

The resting muscle biopsy was taken prior to commencement of the training program and at 48–72 h following completion of the final training session for older adults. All muscle biopsies were completed by the same experienced medical doctor. All participants refrained from strenuous physical activity, alcohol, and caffeine for at least 24 h prior to the biopsy procedures. Muscle biopsies were obtained using a biopsy needle with suction36 from the vastus lateralis after an injection of a local anesthetic into the skin and fascia (1% Xylocaine; AstraZeneca, Sydney, Australia) and a small incision were made. The muscle was blotted on filter paper to remove excess blood and quickly frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at –80°C until analysis.

Whole muscle homogenate preparation

Whole muscle homogenates were prepared as described previously.37 Briefly, a portion of whole muscle (15–30 mg) was weighed and homogenized on ice (weight:volume = 1:20) in an Na-ethylene glycol-bis(β-aminoethyl ether)-N,N,N’,N’-tetraacetic acid (EGTA), (Merck, Darmsadt, Germany) solution (containing in mM, 165 Na+, 50 EGTA, 90 HEPES, 1 Mg2+(10.3 total Mg2+), 8 total ATP, 10 creatine phosphate, pH7.10) with a protease inhibitor cocktail (PIC, Complete; Roche Diagnostics, Sydney, Australia). The homogenate was then diluted to 33 μg muscle wet weight/μL using a 3× sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) denaturing solution (0.125 M Tris-HCl, 10% glycerol, 4% SDS, 4 M urea, 10% mercaptoethanol, and 0.001% bromophenol blue, pH6.8). Finally, samples were further diluted to 2.5 μg muscle wet weight/μL using 3× SDS solution diluted 2:1 with 1× Tris.HCl (pH6.8).

The whole muscle homogenate served 2 primary purposes. Firstly, whole muscle homogenates were utilized for Westen blotting analysis. Secondly, a small portion of each homogenate sample was used to create a calibration mix of approximately 2 mL. The calibration mix was utilized in all Westen blotting analyses, including the single fiber analyses, by loading a 4-point calibration curve onto every gel (see below). This provided the relationship between input loading amount and density, which was used to determine the relative amount of each protein in a given sample. Notably, all samples fell within the established upper and lower detection limits of the calibration curve.

Single fiber collection, fiber-typing, and pooling

As previously described, segments of single fibers were collected from freeze-dried muscle33,38,39 and placed in 10 µL of 3× SDS denaturing solution. Fibers remained at room temperature for 1 h post collection and were vortexed 2–3 times to aid solubilization; they were thereafter stored at –80°C until dot blotting.

The fiber type of individual single fiber segments was determined using the dot blotting method previously described and now termed MyDoBID (Myosin heavy chain detection by Dot Blotting for IDentification of muscle fiber type).40,41 Briefly, a polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membrane was activated in 96% ethanol for 120 s and equilibrated in transfer buffer (Bio-Rad Laboratories Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA) for 120 s. Following this, 1-µL (10%) of each fiber was spotted on activated PVDF membrane. After a series of steps involving drying, re-activating, washing, and blocking, membranes were incubated in antibodies (for full details, see Latchman et al.,40): firstly, anti-myosin heavy chain IIa (MHCIIa) (mouse, monoclonal IgG, A4.74; Developmental Studies Hybridoma Bank (DSHB), Iowa city, IA, USA), followed by stripping and then anti-MHCI (mouse monoclonal IgM, A4.840; DSHB). Both antibodies were diluted 1:200 in 1% bovine serum albumin (BSA) in phosphate-buffered saline with 0.025% Tween (PBST) with 0.02% sodium azide (NaN3). Chemiluminescent substrate (ECL, Clarity; Bio-Rad Laboratories, Sydney, Australia) enabled imaging of dots using a Chemidoc MP and Image Lab software (Bio-Rad Laboratories). Fibers were identified as type II (MHCIIa), type I (MHCI), or hybrid (MHCII and MHCI), and fibers identified as type I or type II were used for further analyses.

The remaining 9-µL of each fiber were then pooled with other fibers of the same fiber-type from a given participant for a given time point. Each pool consisted of 4–11 fibers, and pooled fibers were stored at –80°C until Westen blotting. In total ∼2700 fibers were collected for this study. Between 85 and 103 type I fibers and 85–99 type II fibers were isolated and analyzed from each young adult (n = 7); and between 91 and 98 type I fibers and 76–101 type II fibers (n = 7) were isolated and analyzed from each older adult for HSP72, HSP27, pHSP27 Ser15, αB-crystallin, and pαB-crystallin Ser59.

Westen blotting

Calibrated Westen blots were performed to determine the abundance of HSP proteins in whole muscle homogenates and pools of single fibers using techniques previously described.32,38, 39, 40 Each 26-well gel contained the denatured samples, molecular weight markers (Page RulerTM, cat 26616; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), and a 3–4-point calibration curve comprising a mix of all whole muscle samples.42 Total protein was separated on 10% or 4%–15% Criterion TGX stain-free gels (Bio-Rad Laboratories) and run for 45 min at 200 V. Using a wet transfer protocol, protein was transferred to nitrocellulose membranes at 100 V for 30 min. Membranes were incubated in MiserTM solution (ThermoFisher Scientific) and blocked in 5% skim milk powder in tris-buffered saline-tween (TBST). Membranes were cut horizontally to minimize the number of probes for a section, and each section was incubated with antibodies diluted in 1% BSA in PBST. Details of antibodies used are as follows: HSP72 (1 in 500 mouse monoclonal, SMC100A; StressMarq Biosciences, Victoria, Canada); HSP27 (1 in 1000 mouse monoclonal, G3.1 ab2790; Abcam, Cambridge, UK); pHSP27 Ser15 (1 in 2000 monoclonal rabbit, ab76313; Abcam), pHSP27 Ser82 (1 in 2000 polyclonal mouse, ADI-SPA-524; Enzo Biochem, Farmingdale, NY, USA), αB-crystallin (1 in 1000 mouse monoclonal, SPA-222; StressGen Biotechnologies), pαB-crystallin Ser59 (1 in 1000 rabbit polyclonal, SPA-227; StressGen Biotechnologies). Membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C and for 2 h at room temperature, all with rocking. After washing and incubating with an appropriate horse radish peroxidase (HRP) secondary antibody (HRP goat anti-mouse 51782504 and HRP goat anti-rabbit 17065156; Bio-Rad Laboratories) and following TBST washes, the membranes were coated with chemiluminescent substrate (West Femto; Thermo Fisher Scientific). Images were acquired using Image Lab software (Bio-Rad Laboratories). The positions of molecular mass markers were captured under white light prior to chemiluminescent imaging without moving the membrane.

Data analysis and statistical tests

For data analysis, the linear relationship of the calibration curve was used to convert the density of a given protein to an amount of muscle and then normalized to the total protein in the respective lane, which was also expressed relative to the calibration curve. The same calibration curve was run on all gels, and data are expressed relative to the average of a given sample type present on the same gel (i.e., old pre in whole muscle analyses, old type I fibers in pooled fiber analyses). Each sample was run on at least 2 different gels on separate occasions, and values are presented as the mean of all runs. The old pre samples were used for normalizing the data, as they were common across all analyses undertaken.

Linear regression equations (y = mx + c) for the calibration curves were determined in Excel. Data was analyzed using GraphPad Prism (Version 10.3.2; GraphPad, San Diego, CA, USA) and IBM SPSS Statistics (Version 29; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). An overall Power G analysis using SPSS for one-way analysis of variance revealed that a minimum sample size of 7 subjects per group was needed to achieve a power of 0.80 when testing effects of three different interventions on a specific outcome (p = 0.05, high effect size (f = 0.7), and equal sample size across groups); for a paired t-test, a minimum sample size of four was needed to achieve actual power size of 0.81 and an effect size of 2.167 (generated using data from Frankenberg et al.,30 Larkins et al.,28 prior to experimental analysis). No significant outliers were identified at Q = 1% using the ROUT test. The normality of distribution of data was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk normality test. To assess the homogeneity of variance assumption for the one-way analysis of variance, Levene’s test was conducted. Any data that was not normally distributed or did not meet Levene’s assumptions were analyzed for significance using Brown-Forsye and Welch's analysis of variance with Games-Horwell’s post hoc analysis to allow for unequal variance and small sample group comparison. Homogenate data comparing young and old pre and old post group HSP70, pHSP27 Ser15, and pαB-crystallin Ser59 were analyzed using this method. HSP27 and αB-crystallin were analyzed using ordinary one-way analysis as homogeneity of variances assumptions tested by Levene’s test were met. Effect sizes are described using partial eta-squared (ηp2) along with their 95% confidence intervals (95%CIs, lower limit, upper limit) for these significant measures. A mixed effects model univariant test along with the appropriate multiple comparisons test was performed to test for young and older individual fiber type differences. To assess the homogeneity of variance assumption for the mixed effects univariant, Levene’s test was conducted. If data passed these assumptions, a Tukey post hoc was used to measure significance (young compared to older individuals’ fiber type difference for HSP27, pHSP27 Ser15, and pHSP27Ser82). If assumptions were not met, a Games-Horwell’s post hoc analysis was conducted, which allows for unequal variance (young compared to older individuals’ fiber type difference for HSP72, αB-crystallin, and pαB-crystallin Ser59). A Student’s paired t-test (two-sided) was performed to compare protein abundances of HSP70, HSP27, αB-crystallin, and pαB-crystallin Ser59 pre and post HIT for older individuals in type I and type II fibers. The magnitudes of difference for normally distributed data were determined by calculating the unbiased standardized mean difference, which is known as Cohen’s d (Cd), along with their 95%CIs (lower limit, upper limit). Data that were not normally distributed (pHSP27 Ser15 type I and type II fibers in pre and post HIT comparisons) were analyzed using a Wilcoxen signed paired rank test. The magnitudes of difference for data showing non-normal distribution were determined by the Wilcoxen paired rank test results, by determining the Z value and effect size Cohen’s ds, (or biased-corrected ds.biased, where ds is the SD of the control group) for independent samples (2 × 2 factor), which was calculated using the supplementary spreadsheet that accompanies the article by Pautz and colleagues.43 Individual data are expressed as mean ± SD and are color-coded the same for a given participant across all plots. Significance was accepted at p ≤ 0.05.

Results

The basal abundances of HSP72, HSP27, and αβ-crystallin were not significantly different in muscle between younger and older adults (p > 0.05, Fig. 1C, 1D, and 1F). However, both basal pHSP27 Ser15 (∼1.8-fold, Levene’s test F(2,16) = 6.782, p = 0.007; Brown-Forsye and Welch's (F(2,8.391) = 7.082, p = 0.015; Games-Horwell post hoc p = 0.05; 95%CI: –0.92 to 0.04; ηp2 = 0.43) and pαB-crystallin Ser59 were relatively higher in 4 older individuals and showed significantly different (Levene’s test F(2,16) = 35.2, p < 0.001; Brown-Forsye and Welch’s (F(2,8.391)= 3.9, p = 0.05; Games-Horwell’s post hoc p = 0.06) in older compared to younger adults (Fig. 1E and 1G). Effect of HIT on HSP abundances in pooled muscle fibers

Twelve weeks of HIT in older adults resulted in: increased HSP72 protein in type I fibers (∼1.2-fold, t(6) = –2.49, p = 0.04; 95%CI: –1.80 to 0.00; effect size Cd = 0.30) but not type II fibers (Fig. 3A and 3B); decreased HSP27 protein in both type I (∼1.6-fold, t(6) = 5.62, p = 0.01; 95%CI: 0.72–3.48; effect size Cd = 0.20) and type II fibers (∼1.4-fold, t(6) = –2.67, p = 0.04; 95%CI: 0.62–3.03; effect size Cd = 0.20) (Fig. 3C and 3D); decreased pHSP27 Ser15 in type I (∼2-fold, Z = –2.37, p = 0.01; 95%CI: 0.11–2.02; effect size Cds = 1.68) and type II fibers (∼2-fold, Z = –2.37, p = 0.05; 95%CI: 0.50–1.89; effect size Cds = 1.32) (Fig. 3E and 3F). Protein abundances of αB-crystallin were lower post HIT in type I fibers (∼1.4-fold, t(6) = 3.68, p = 0.01; 95%CI: 0.23–2.43; effect size Cd = 0.20) and type II fibers (∼1.8-fold, t(6) = 3.05, p = 0.02; 95%CI: 0.15–1.82; effect size Cd = 0.50) (Fig. 3G and 3H). Also, pαB-crystallin Ser59 was decreased in both type I fibers (∼3-fold, t(6) = 3.47, p = 0.01; 95%CI: 0.25–2.32; effect size Cd = 0.40) and type II fibers (∼2-fold, t(6) = 3.65, p = 0.01; 95%CI: 0.29–2.42; effect size Cd = 0.30) (Fig. 3I and 3J). There were no differences in fiber type abundances of pHSP27 Ser82 with HIT in older adults (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Discussion

We reported that skeletal muscle in older adults exhibited a greater level of HSP stress response compared with younger adults, indicated by the higher relative abundances of pαB-crystallin Ser59 and pHSP27 Ser15. Furthermore, 12 weeks of HIT decreased muscle pαB-crystallin Ser59 and pHSP27 Ser15 abundances in older adults to levels not different from those of young adults. This indicates the potential capacity of HIT to protect muscle from age-associated cellular stress, which could slow the inevitable age-related decline in muscle mass and function diagnosed as sarcopenia.44

Effect of age on protein abundance of HSPs

We have previously discussed the ability to improve relative quantification of Westen blotting by the inclusion of calibration curves, which allow even small differences in protein abundances to be detected.40,42 Using calibrated Westen blotting, we found that the level of HSPs were not different in the muscles from our 68–72-year-old adult cohort compared with our 18–30-year-old cohort of younger adults. In previous studies, aging comparisons have reported higher HSP72 (aged 76 ± 6 years),16 HSP27, and αB-crystallin content with age (51–79 years old),23 similar to rodent models.45 We suggest that we did not find this in our assessment due to methodological differences or because our older adults were moderately active, although specific physiological measurements are not reported for further comparisons. Older adults involved in this study self-reported ∼6–8 h of moderate activity per week, as previously reported.34 It has been suggested that higher αB-crystallin levels were related to reduced functional capacity, which is considered a hallmark of frailty in the elderly.46 Nevertheless, in the current study, higher abundances of pHSP27 Ser15 and pαB-crystallin Ser59 were found in muscle fibers from older adults compared to younger individuals, indicating higher cellular stress stimuli, most likely pertaining to various molecular changes related to age. These higher levels of phosphorylation of sHSPs have been observed in several different aging models (for reviews47). The phosphorylation-induced changes in sHSPs are linked to their ability to respond to cellular stress and maintain protein homeostasis, which allows them to modulate cellular stress responses48,49 and protein changes, as previously reported.34 Phosphorylation of sHSPs is typically reversible, allowing this post-translational modification to drive dynamic cellular changes, specifically in altering the oligomeric distribution and chaperone ability of sHSPs in response to stress.48 In addition to preserving protein structures and guiding refolding, the sHSPs have been implicated with apoptosis, which results from hyperphosphorylation of sHSPs and from which the cell cannot recover.48 An absolute level of phosphorylation that leads to this fatal end point is not clear.

Fiber type specific protein abundance of HSPs in young and older individuals

Skeletal muscle heterogeneity is an important consideration, as muscle fiber type composition can vary with both age and exercise.50,51 In the current study, single muscle fibers were isolated from biopsies and pooled based on the presence of MHCI or MHCII. Comparison of the abundance of HSPs in the specific fiber types by calibrated Westen blotting revealed greater amounts of HSP72, HSP27, and αB-crystallin in type I fibers isolated from young individuals, which was similar to fiber type-specific abundances of pαB-crystallin in older adults, although no fiber type difference in HSP72 and HSP27 was seen in the older adults (Fig. 2). Previous studies in young adults reported a higher abundance of both HSP27 and αB-crystallin26,52,53 in type I compared to type II fibers in young adults, similar to the findings here.

When the phosphorylated sHSPs were compared between the 2 cohorts, there was more pHSP27 Ser15 and pαB-crystallin Ser59 in type I fibers from older adults, with no difference between type II fibers present in older and younger adults. These results were broadly congruent with the whole muscle analyses; however, they revealed that differences in the whole muscle measurements for the phosphorylated sHSPs were specifically due to changes in type I fibers. Interestingly, despite the consistent differences in the abundance of pHSP27 Ser15 and pαB-crystallin Ser59 in whole muscle and pooled muscle fibers, pHSP27 at Ser82 was unchanged in specific fiber types between young and older adults or following HIT. This suggests that pHSP27 Ser82 phosphorylation is not a stress marker for age related cellular changes.

Effect of 12-week HIT in older adults on the fiber type specific protein abundance of HSPs

Following 12 weeks of HIT in the older adults, total HSP72, HSP27, and αB-crystallin were not different in whole muscle homogenates. In the same samples, pHSP27 Ser15 and pαB-crystallin Ser59, which were elevated when comparing older with young adults, were decreased in response to the HIT intervention and were no longer different from the levels seen in muscle from young adults. These findings demonstrate that the muscle cells had not reached an irreversible level of hyperphosphorylation, suggesting that a ∼2–3-fold higher level of phosphorylation of pHSP27 Ser15 and pαB-crystallin Ser59 in muscle is below the threshold for irrecoverable apoptotic events. The decreases in total HSP27, total αB-crystallin, pHSP27 Ser15, and pαB-crystallin Ser59 were seen in both type I and type II fibers, which is perhaps not surprising given the high-intensity nature of the training protocol that would likely have recruited both fiber types during the exercise bouts.54 Despite this, type I but not type II muscle fibers resulted in an ∼1.2-fold increase in HSP72. Both these findings indicate that cellular-specific changes that were not measurable at the level of whole muscle homogenates can provide insight into the cellular events that would otherwise not be discernible in whole muscle analyses.

With respect to HSP72, previous research indicated that inhibiting HSP72 led to muscle atrophy, while overexpression of HSP72 promoted enhanced preservation of muscle function.55,56 Also, HSP72 maintains the correct protein folding of SR/ER Ca2+ ATPases in the SR,57 which is critical to muscle function in maintaining cellular Ca2+ homeostasis. This suggests that the 12-week HIT intervention, sufficient to increase HSP72 abundance at least in type I muscle fibers, confers a protective effect on muscle function.34

Higher levels of pHSP27 Ser15 and pαB-crystallin Ser59 in the muscle fibers of older adults compared with younger adults likely indicated a higher level of cellular stress in the older individuals. The robust adaptation to the 12-week HIT intervention in whole muscle homogenate and both type I and type II muscle fibers in the older adults provides strong evidence of positive adaptation in cellular homeostasis from the exercise training intervention. We have previously reported increased pHSP27 Ser15 and pHSP27 Ser82 abundances following acute eccentric exercise in young humans,14 and while we have not measured the same in older individuals, it suggests that chronic exercise training does not promote a change in pHSP27 Ser82, yet it does for pHSP27 Ser15.

In a similar pattern to the phosphorylated sHSPs, we found higher abundance of mitochondrial dynamics proteins Mfn2 and MiD49 in the older compared to the young adults,32 which decreased following HIT. We also reported robust increases in mitochondrial content markers such as cytochrome c oxidase subunit IV and citrate synthase activity following HIT.32 We interpreted those findings as the muscles needing to increase mitochondrial turnover and, therefore, adapting by increasing both fusion (with Mfn2 as the marker) and fission (with MiD49 as the marker) in the older adults, which, following the stabilizing exercise training, were no longer required.32 Furthermore, in that earlier work, we found there was a type II fiber type-specific response to Mfn2. Together with the changes reported here specific to type I fibers, we can conclude that the sHSP response plays an important cellular role in the type I oxidative fibers, which could indicate that a greater level of oxidative stress occurs in type 1 fibers, given their higher mitochondrial content.32

Limitations

This study shows that HIT is associated with altered phosphorylation of sHSPs in aged skeletal muscle, potentially reflecting improved cellular stress adaptation. While consistent with literature linking sHSPs to proteostasis and exercise benefits, our exploratory findings are associative and do not confirm causality, the underlying signaling event and their functional significance remain speculative and warrant mechanistic studies to further investigate. A limitation of the study is the absence of a non-training older control group. Even so, it is unlikely that the higher HSP stress response seen in older individuals would reverse without intervention. Another limitation is that the younger individuals did not undergo the 12-week HIT program; however, the younger group was used as a comparator for the older individuals before they underwent training, and we would hypothesize that an exercise training program would result in different HSP responses. The baseline differences in VO₂peak and heart rate between age groups may have influenced outcomes; however, it is difficult to match for these parameters when disparately aged humans are studied. Finally, we did not measure phosphorylation at Ser45 on αB-crystallin due to the lack of a reliable antibody to enable calibrated Westen blotting, and hence, the development of quantitative measures. These constraints limit mechanistic interpretation, highlighting the need for future studies.

Conclusion

Following a comparison between young and older adults matched for self-reported physical activity, we explored the impact of 12 weeks of HIT on HSP content and phosphorylation in muscle from older individuals. We found that the HIT resulted in a reduction of HSP27, αB-crystallin, pαB-crystallin Ser59, and pHSP27 Ser15 levels. Moreover, distinct protein abundance profiles were observed across different muscle fiber types, which was consistent across both young and older participants. Collectively, our results signify a distinctive cellular response following 12 weeks of HIT in older adults, which may be indicative of a potential attenuated stress response as a consequence of the exercise training.
