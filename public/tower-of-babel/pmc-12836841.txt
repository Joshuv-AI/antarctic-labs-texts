CPA3 drives the development of asthma by promoting the activation of the JAK/STAT6 pathway
Authors: Ke Wang, Ruhao Wu, Mingwei Fan, Ruohan Jia, Yize Liu, Yue Li, Pengfei Li, Yu Wang, Tianci Jiang, Zhe Cheng
Published: 2025

Introduction

Asthma is a prevalent chronic respiratory disease that affects approximately 300 million people worldwide [1]. This condition is characterized by airway hyperresponsiveness (AHR), eosinophilic inflammation, excessive mucus secretion, and airway remodeling [2, 3]. The pathogenesis of asthma is multifactorial and involves genetic, environmental, and immune system factors [4, 5]. Despite ongoing advancements in therapeutic strategies, controlling asthma remains a considerable challenge [6]. Therefore, comprehending the underlying mechanisms that drive the onset and progression of asthma is crucial for developing effective therapies.

Recently, high-throughput sequencing technology has been extensively adopted in clinical practice, facilitating the investigation of disease mechanisms, identification of biomarkers, and discovery of therapeutic targets [7–9]. With the reduction in sequencing costs, high-throughput sequencing demonstrated its advantages in both throughput and clinical utility [10, 11]. However, the precise extraction of disease-relevant signals from the vast information provided by high-throughput sequencing data posed a significant challenge for clinical researchers.

With the rise of computer science, machine learning (ML) methods have become increasingly familiar to researchers [12, 13]. By combining computational efficiency with data-driven insights, ML enables to detect complex patterns in large datasets that are imperceptible to clinical researchers. Therefore, the integration of high-throughput sequencing and ML represents an efficient methodological portfolio for addressing clinical challenges. For instance, Ren et al. applied ML algorithms to develop a prognostic evaluation tool for lung adenocarcinoma patients based on lncRNA sequencing [14]. Yachida et al. employed an integrated approach, combining the Random Forest algorithm with metagenomic and metabolomic high-throughput sequencing, to identify microbiome-derived cancer biomarkers [15]. Zaslavsky et al. developed a machine learning framework for the simultaneous screening of multiple diseases [16].

In present study, we performed ML algorithms to identify significant biomarker of asthma, CPA3 from high-throughput RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) data. Afterward, we further explore and validate vital role of CPA3 in asthma from validation dataset, cell lines, and mice model. The findings suggest that upregulation of CPA3 in airway epithelial cells promotes asthma progression by activating the JAK/STAT6 pathway.

Materials and methods

Data collection

GSE67472 [17, 18] and GSE158752 [19, 20] datasets were downloaded form the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/), provided by the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI). The GSE67472 dataset (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE67472) includes transcriptomic data from bronchial epithelial cells of 43 healthy individuals and 62 asthma patients, while the GSE158752 dataset (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE158752) comprises bronchial epithelial cell samples from 17 healthy individuals and 50 asthma patients.

Weighted correlation network analysis (WGCNA)

After preprocessing, weighted gene co-expression network analysis was conducted on the GSE67472 dataset based on the “WGCNA” package [21]. A soft-thresholding power of six was selected to ensure scale-free topology (R2 > 0.85). Subsequently, we constructed a topological overlap matrix (TOM) to generate co-expression modules and quantify their associations with asthma. The most asthma-associated module (showing the strongest positive correlation) was selected for hub gene identification, applying dual criteria of module membership (MM > 0.7) and gene significance (GS > 0.5).

Differential expressed gene analysis and gene significance assessment

After performing differential gene expression analysis through the “limma” package, differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified meeting the threshold of adjusted p-value of < 0.05 and |log2 Fold Change (log2 FC)|> 1 [22]. The intersection of DEGs and previously identified hub genes were defined as key genes. Subsequently, key genes were incorporated to evaluate importance to distinguish asthma and control group using the Random Forest algorithm.

Cell culture and treatment

Normal human bronchial epithelial BEAS-2B cells (ATCC catalog number: CRL-3588) were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium (Solarbio, China), supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Solarbio). The cells were maintained in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO2. BEAS-2B cells were passaged every two days using TrypLE™ Express (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) for cell detachment. After culturing in complete medium for 24 h, the cells were treated with various concentrations of HDM (50 µg/mL, 100 µg/mL, 150 µg/mL, Dermatophagoides Pteronyssinus, Greer Laboratories, USA) or an equal volume of PBS. Following 24 h of treatment, cell samples were collected for subsequent quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) and Western blot analysis to determine the optimal concentration of HDM. CPA3 siRNA and overexpression plasmid transfections were performed using Lipofectamine™ 3000 reagent. After 24 h, the transfection mixture was replaced with complete culture medium (RPMI-1640 supplemented with 10% FBS). The cells were then stimulated with or without HDM (at the optimal concentration) and with or without leflunomide (50 µmol/L or 100 µmol/L) for an additional 24 h. For further details, refer to Appendix S1.

qRT‑PCR

qRT-PCR was conducted using the SYBR Green Premix Pro Taq HS qPCR Kit (Rox Plus) (Accurate Biology, China) on the QuantStudio™ 5 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems™, Thermo Fisher Scientific). RNA expressions were calculated by using the 2−ΔΔCt method, with GAPDH serving as the reference gene for normalization [23]. For further details, refer to Appendix S1.

Western blotting

Cell and tissue lysates were subjected to SDS-PAGE electrophoresis, and Western blotting was performed using primary antibodies against GAPDH, CPA3, total STAT6 (tSTAT6), and phosphorylated STAT6 (pSTAT6). Densitometric analysis of the blots was conducted using ImageJ software. For additional details, refer to Appendix S1.

Establishment of an allergic airway inflammation mouse model

Female C57/BL6N wild-type mice, aged 6–8 weeks (weight 20 ± 1 g, n = 20), were purchased from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. The mice were housed in a specific pathogen-free facility under a controlled temperature of 23 ± 2 °C, with free access to food and water. The room lighting was maintained on a 12-h light/dark cycle. The mice were allowed to acclimate for at least one week before the start of the experiment.

The mice were randomly assigned to four groups: the control group, the allergic airway inflammation group, the leflunomide treatment group, and the dexamethasone treatment group, with 5 mice in each group. Mice in the allergic airway inflammation, leflunomide treatment, and dexamethasone treatment groups were sensitized with 100 µg of house dust mite (HDM) (Dermatophagoides pteronyssinus, Greer Laboratories, USA) via intranasal administration on day 0, followed by daily intranasal exposure to 10 µg of HDM from days 7 to 12. The leflunomide treatment group received 5 mg/kg of leflunomide via intraperitoneal injection daily from days 7 to 12, 30 min prior to HDM stimulation. The dexamethasone treatment group was given a 2 mg/kg intraperitoneal injection of dexamethasone on days 7, 9, and 11, also 30 min before HDM stimulation. Mice in the control group were sensitized with an equivalent volume of PBS. All mice were sacrificed on day 14 for further analysis, which included the collection of lung tissues for subsequent histological examination [24].

Histology

Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and Periodic Acid-Schiff (PAS) staining were performed to evaluate airway inflammation and mucus secretion. Peribronchiolar and perivascular inflammation was scored as follows: 0, normal; 1, few inflammatory cells; 2, inflammatory cell infiltration extending to one cell layer; 3, infiltration extending to 2–4 cell layers; and 4, infiltration extending beyond 4 cell layers. PAS staining of the epithelial cells was scored as follows: 0, < 5% goblet cells; 1, 5–25%; 2, 25–50%; 3, 50–75%; 4, > 75%, with the scores of 1–4 indicating positive PAS staining in the bronchi [25]. For each mouse, six random fields containing airways were selected, and the scores for each field were recorded. The final score for each mouse was the average of the scores from these fields.

Immunohistochemistry was performed to assess the CPA3 expression. The sections were first incubated with a primary anti-CPA3 antibody and then with a horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-labeled secondary antibody. The antigen–antibody complex was visualized using a DAB chromogenic solution. Six random fields containing airways were selected from each mouse, and the CPA3 expression in airway epithelial cells was analyzed. Specifically, we delineated the airway epithelial region and measured the optical density (OD) of the stained areas using ImageJ software. The data were then normalized to the epithelial area (i.e., average optical density, AOD) to ensure accurate comparisons across experimental groups. The final mean AOD for each mouse was subsequently used for further analysis.

Statistical analysis

Bioinformatics statistical analysis and data visualization were performed using RStudio software (version 4.4.1; https://www.r-project.org/). Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism version 10.3.0 (GraphPad Software, Boston, Massachusetts, USA). The results were presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). For comparisons between two groups of continuous variables, an independent samples t-test was used, while multiple group comparisons were performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). p < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance, with the following notation: *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001.

Results

Identification of key genes for asthma differentiation

Through systematic correlation analysis between gene co-expression modules and clinical variables, the red module demonstrated the strongest positive association with asthma (Pearson r = 0.56), making it the most clinically relevant module (Fig. 1A-D). In contrast, the brown module exhibited a strong negative correlation with the asthma trait (r = −0.6), but was intentionally excluded from the primary analysis due to its inverse relationship with the disease. Since our primary aim was to identify upregulated genes associated with asthma pathogenesis, we focused on modules that exhibited positive correlations with the trait. Relying on dual criteria of module membership (MM > 0.7) and gene significance (GS > 0.5), 15 module genes were finally identified (SERPINB2, CPA3, CLCA1, POSTN, SCGB3A1, CST1, TMEM45A, PCAT19, CST4, PRR4, TPSAB1, SHISA2, LTF, ITLN1, BPIFA1). DEG analysis revealed 19 DEGs between two groups. The volcano plot indicated that 13 genes were upregulated (CLCA1, CST1, SERPINB2, POSTN, CPA3, CEACAM5, PRR4, ITLN1, CDC42EP5, CST4, PCAT19, TMEM200A, and TPSAB1) and six genes were downregulated (LTF, FHOD3, SHISA2, TMEM45A, SCGB3A1, and BPIFA1) (Fig. 1E). Subsequently, an intersection analysis was performed between module genes and the DEGs, resulting in the selection of four key genes: CPA3, CST1, CST4, and TPSAB1 (Fig. 1F). The results of Random Forest analysis indicated that CPA3 was the most important key gene in distinguishing between the asthma group and the control group (Fig. 1G). Hence, we focused on CPA3 for further validation and exploration.

Verification of CPA3 expression

First, we collected an independent asthma cohort (GSE158752) for validation, which showed significantly elevated CPA3 levels in asthma patients compared to controls (Fig. 2A). Next, the BEAS-2B cells were stimulated with different concentrations of HDM. Both qRT-PCR and western blot analyses demonstrated significantly elevated CPA3 expression following HDM stimulation compared with controls, peaking at 100 µg/mL HDM concentration (Figs. 2B-C). Following this observation, a mouse allergic airway inflammation model was established, and pathological staining was performed. Immunohistochemical results exhibited that CPA3 expression in the airway epithelial cells of the allergic airway inflammation mice was significantly higher than that in the control group (Fig. 2D-E). Consistently, qRT-PCR and Western blot analysis of lung tissues from HDM-stimulated allergic airway inflammation model mice showed a significant increase in CPA3 expression in the allergic airway inflammation group compared to the control group (Figs. 2F-H). Additionally, BEAS-2B cells were stimulated with papain, a model proteolytic allergen. The results demonstrated that, compared to the control group, the expression of CPA3 significantly increased after papain stimulation. (Figure S1A-B).

The CPA3 expression regulates inflammation and mucus secretion in airway epithelial cells

Knockdown of CPA3 expression reduces inflammation and mucus secretion in airway epithelial cells

To examine the role of CPA3 in airway epithelial cells, BEAS-2B cells were transfected with three different CPA3 siRNAs to knock down its expression. PCR analysis showed that mRNA levels of CPA3 were substantially reduced in all siRNA-transfected groups compared with the control (Fig. 3A). However, western blot analysis revealed a significant decrease in CPA3 expression compared with the control group only in the siRNA-2 group (Fig. 3B-C). Thus, siRNA-2 was selected for further experiments to establish the CPA3 knockdown model.

CPA3 promotes the development of asthma by activating the JAK/STAT6 pathway

The effect of leflunomide on the CPA3 expression and STAT6 induced by HDM

Leflunomide is a JAK/STAT6 inhibitor [26]. After 24 h of HDM stimulation in BEAS-2B cells, we observed a significant increase in the expression of phosphorylated STAT6 in the cell lysates. However, treatment with 50 µmol/L or 100 µmol/L leflunomide significantly attenuated this effect. Additionally, both concentrations of leflunomide (50 µmol/L and 100 µmol/L) substantially decreased the HDM stimulation-induced production of CPA3 in BEAS-2B cells (Figs. 5A-C).

CPA3 regulates the phosphorylation level of STAT6

To ascertain the role of CPA3 in the JAK/STAT6 pathway, the phosphorylation levels of STAT6 were studied in the CPA3 knockdown and overexpression models of BEAS-2B cells. The results revealed that CPA3 knockdown led to a decrease in the phosphorylation of STAT6 in the BEAS-2B asthma model (Fig. 5D-E). In contrast, its overexpression enhanced the phosphorylation of STAT6 in airway epithelial cells (Fig. 5F-G).

Leflunomide alleviates airway epithelial cell inflammation and mucus secretion

Considering the increased phosphorylation of STAT6 following CPA3 overexpression, the effect of JAK/STAT6 inhibition on inflammation and mucus segregation was subsequently explored in the CPA3 overexpression model. qRT-PCR analysis indicated that CPA3 overexpression led to elevated mRNA levels of the cytokines IL-25, IL-33, and TSLP and the chemokines CCL11 and CCL24 in HDM-stimulated BEAS-2B cells. Additionally, the mRNA expression of the mucus secretion marker MUC5AC was increased, while the expression levels of the cytokine IL-8 and the chemokine RANTES were also elevated. Nevertheless, treatment with leflunomide substantially decreased the CPA3 overexpression-induced elevated mRNA expressions of these cytokines, chemokines, and mucus secretion markers (Figs. 5H-M, Figures S4A-B).

Leflunomide alleviates airway inflammation and mucus secretion in a mouse allergic airway inflammation model

Mice treated with leflunomide demonstrated a reduced expression of CPA3 in lung tissue and a decreased phosphorylation level of STAT6

Based on the allergic airway inflammation mouse model, we treated the mice with leflunomide and used dexamethasone as a positive control. First, we assessed CPA3 expression and STAT6 phosphorylation levels in lung tissue from the different groups of mice using Western blotting. The results were consistent with previous findings, showing a significant increase in both CPA3 expression and STAT6 phosphorylation in the lung tissue of the allergic airway inflammation group. However, both the leflunomide and dexamethasone treatment groups exhibited a marked reduction in CPA3 expression and STAT6 phosphorylation compared to the allergic airway inflammation group (Fig. 6A-C).

Mice in the leflunomide treatment group exhibited reduced airway inflammation and decreased mucus secretion

H&E staining showed that both leflunomide and dexamethasone treatments significantly reduced the extent of inflammatory cell infiltration around the airways in the lung tissue of allergic airway inflammation mice (Fig. 6D-E). PAS staining results indicated that, similar to dexamethasone treatment, leflunomide treatment could alleviate the hyperplasia of goblet cells in the airway epithelium. Discussion

Asthma is a prevalent chronic respiratory disease that affects lots of people all over the world. Characterized by multiple complex mechanisms, asthma poses substantial challenges for both patients and healthcare providers in clinical management. It was crucial to investigate underlying molecular pathogenesis of asthma to provide helpful insight to clinical management and treatment. Fortunately, the integration of high-throughput sequencing and machine learning algorithms provided an efficient tool. In this study, we identified four key genes from DEGs and module genes from WGCNA based on RNA-seq data of asthma. Applying random forest algorithm, we focus on CPA3 as the most significant gene in asthma diagnosis.

As a key inflammatory mediator, CPA3 plays a critical role in allergic reactions. Primarily secreted by MCs and eosinophils, CPA3 regulates local inflammatory responses and is closely associated with allergic reactions and the pathological alterations noted in asthma [27]. Numerous studies have shown that intraepithelial mast cells in asthmatic airways express elevated levels of CPA3, and that epithelial-adjacent mast cells are a dominant anatomical compartment for CPA3 in type-2–high asthma [28–30]. This supports the biological rationale that elevated CPA3 in the airway epithelium can participate in epithelial-driven inflammatory signaling. As CPA3 is a specific marker for MCs, its elevation suggests mast cell activation and the subsequent release of inflammatory mediators, such as histamine and cytokines [31, 32]. CPA3 regulates the release of these mediators and potentially plays a central role in the chronic inflammatory processes characteristic of asthma.

Allergens, such as those from dust mites, can bind to receptors on immune cells, including airway epithelial cells, dendritic cells, and MCs. This binding triggers a cascade of immune responses that exacerbate inflammation [33]. Several studies suggest that HDM exposure can lead to increased activation of mast cells, which may stimulate the release and activity of CPA3. This is likely due to the cross-linking of IgE receptors (FcεRI) on mast cells, which, upon activation, triggers degranulation and the subsequent release of proteases such as CPA3 [34, 35]. HDM-derived proteolytic enzymes, such as Der p 1 and Der f 1, have been shown to contribute to the activation of various immune cells, including mast cells and dendritic cells [36, 37], which may, in turn, affect CPA3 activity. Der p 1, for example, has been demonstrated to interact with tight junction proteins in epithelial cells, disrupting the epithelial barrier and potentially enhancing the accessibility of CPA3 to its substrate in the airway [38]. Papain, a protease known to be allergenic to humans and a common cause of occupational asthma, is frequently used as a model proteolytic allergen. Subcutaneous injection of papain into mice induces a Th2 cell-mediated immune response [39, 40]. In this study, we found that the expression of CPA3 increased with the rising concentration of papain. Given papain’s proteolytic activity, these findings suggest that proteolytic activity may play a direct role in modulating CPA3 expression. Key signaling pathways, including the NF-κB, JAK/STAT, and MAPK pathways, are activated in response to these allergens, which facilitates the release of inflammatory mediators [41, 42]. These pathways increase the production of cytokines (e.g., IL-4, IL-5, and IL-13) and chemokines (e.g., CCL11 and CCL24), which further activate immune cells such as eosinophils, MCs, and T cells. This activation drives the expansion of allergic reactions and promotes airway remodeling. Of the various pathways, the JAK/STAT6 pathway plays a crucial role in the pathogenesis of asthma, particularly in increasing AHR, inflammation, mucus secretion, and smooth muscle hyperplasia [26]. Activation of this pathway induces Th2 cell differentiation and dendritic cell activation, leading to the persistence and exacerbation of allergic responses. This ongoing activation fuels chronic inflammation and airway remodeling in asthma.

Airway epithelial cells play a vital role in the immune response of the respiratory system. These cells not only form a physical barrier but also secrete a range of cytokines, including IL-25, IL-33, and TSLP. These cytokines activate Th2 cells and dendritic cells, amplifying the allergic inflammatory response. IL-25, IL-33, and TSLP are critical mediators of allergic inflammation that promote Th2 cell differentiation and dendritic cell activation, initiating a series of immune reactions [43–45]. Additionally, chemokines such as CCL11 and CCL24 recruit eosinophils to the airway and the bronchial mucosa, thereby contributing to the process. These chemokines enable the migration and accumulation of eosinophils by binding to receptors on these cells. Eosinophils release various proinflammatory mediators, including leukotrienes, cytokines, and proteases, exacerbating the local inflammation. Moreover, eosinophils form positive or negative feedback loops that amplify the pathological progression of asthma by interacting with airway epithelial cells and other immune cells [46, 47]. MUC5AC is a major mucin in the airways that plays an essential role in airway defense by forming a viscous barrier that traps and clears pathogens, allergens, and pollutants [48, 49]. In diseases such as asthma, excessive mucus production leads to increased MUC5AC expression, resulting in airway obstruction and impaired respiratory function, which are common symptoms in patients with asthma. As classic chemokines, IL-8 and RANTES are well-known for their roles in asthma, particularly in promoting leukocyte recruitment and mediating inflammatory reactions [50, 51]. The findings from this study demonstrated that CPA3 regulates airway inflammation and mucus secretion in airway epithelial cells. While CPA3 overexpression exacerbates inflammation and mucus production, its knockdown alleviates these effects, suggesting its potential value as a therapeutic target in asthma management.

The findings from this study further demonstrated that the overexpression of CPA3 significantly increases the phosphorylation level of STAT6. Thus, the activation of the JAK/STAT6 pathway appears to be a critical mechanism by which CPA3 regulates airway inflammation. STAT6 plays a central role in allergic inflammation [52], regulating the production of specific cytokines, such as IL-25, IL-33, and TSLP, and triggering immune responses that promote the expansion and exacerbation of inflammation [53, 54]. By activating the JAK/STAT6 pathway, CPA3 enhances the immune response in airway epithelial cells, resulting in increased expression of cytokines and chemokines that contribute to allergic inflammation and airway remodeling. Leflunomide, a JAK/STAT6 inhibitor, significantly reduced the HDM-induced phosphorylation levels of CPA3 and STAT6. The inhibitor effectively alleviated the excessive expression of cytokines, chemokines, and MUC5AC associated with CPA3 overexpression. These findings underscore the significance of the JAK/STAT6 pathway in asthma development, suggesting the therapeutic potential of leflunomide in reducing inflammation and mucus secretion in asthma. By inhibiting STAT6 phosphorylation, leflunomide may help restore immune balance in the airways, reduce allergic responses, and mitigate airway obstruction and inflammation in patients with asthma.

Although this study provides valuable insights into the role of CPA3 in asthma, several limitations should be considered. First, while the use of bioinformatics and RNA-seq data from asthma patients provides a broad overview, the findings may be limited by the quality and heterogeneity of the datasets, which could introduce biases in gene expression analysis. Additionally, while the results from the BEAS-2B cell line and the house dust mite–induced asthma mouse model are promising, these in vitro and in vivo models may not fully replicate the complexity of human asthma. The therapeutic potential of leflunomide in inhibiting CPA3-driven asthma progression was demonstrated, but further studies are needed to explore its clinical efficacy and safety in humans. Lastly, although we focused on the JAK/STAT6 pathway, other signaling pathways may also contribute to CPA3-mediated asthma progression, and future research should explore these potential interactions. Overall, while this study advances our understanding of CPA3's role in asthma, further validation in human studies and exploration of additional molecular mechanisms are required.

Conclusion

The findings from this study assert that CPA3 exacerbates inflammation and mucus secretion in the epithelial cells of patients with asthma by activating the JAK/STAT6 pathway. Furthermore, leflunomide, a JAK/STAT6 pathway inhibitor, significantly mitigates the inflammatory response and mucus secretion induced by CPA3 overexpression. Based on these findings, CPA3 is a potential therapeutic target, and the use of leflunomide may offer a promising new approach for asthma treatment.
