Synthetic embryos: current progress, application and challenges
Authors: Luyao Wang, Fucheng Guo, Yurong Chen, Xiangpeng Dai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSynthetic embryos, generated using stem cells or reprogrammed somatic cells, could recapitulate embryo development in vitro and provide unique opportunities for in-depth study of embryogenesis, particularly human embryogenesis. To date, the synthetic embryos for human, mouse, non-human primates and bovine are successfully generated. Here we summarize and discuss the research progresses of synthetic embryos, including the materials used for generating synthetic embryos, the potential applications, and current challenges for the generation and application of synthetic embryos. In this review, we discuss the function of synthetic embryos in the study of embryo development, drug screening, genetic disease and organ transplantation.Keywords: Synthetic embryo, Stem cells, Embryogenesis, Disease model, Drug screening

Introduction

The formation of zygotes is the beginning of human embryonic development. Zygotic genome activation (ZGA), the important biological event for early embryo development, occurs at the 8-cell stage in human embryos [1, 2]. At 8-cell stage to 16-cell stage, the embryo experiences compaction and polarization [3], followed by morula formation. Then the outer cells of morula develop to the trophectoderm (TE), and the inside cells develop to inner cell mass (ICM). Between the first 5 to 6 days after fertilization and before implantation, human embryo develops to a blastocyst, which is composed of TE and ICM cells [4]. The TE will develop to the trophoblast cells of the placenta, while the ICM contains epiblast and hypoblast (commonly referred to the primitive endoderm in rodents). The epiblast will develop to cells and tissues of the embryo proper, and will develop to extraembryonic membranes of the conceptus together with hypoblast. Then the blastocyst hatches from zona pellucida (ZP) and attaches to the uterus at embryonic day 6–7 (E6–E7) [5]. During E7 to E9, the epiblast no longer maintains pluripotent state and becomes radially oriented cells surrounding a small primordial cavity, which is the proamniotic cavity [5]. The primitive endoderm (PE) cells grow underneath the TE to form parietal PE (parPE) and arrange below the ventral side of the epiblast to form the ventral endoderm (VE). During E10 to E11, proamniotic cavity expands and forms the amniotic cavity (AC) [6, 7]. The epidermal cells on the dorsal side of the proamniotic cavity develop to a layer of squamous amnion epithelium, while cells on the ventral side maintain goblet shape and give rise to late epiblasts. Moreover, together with distal PE cells, VE cells develop to the primary yolk sac. Around E12–E13, the epiblast expands into an asymmetric plate morphology [5]. The VE rearranges to form the secondary yolk sac composed of a plate shape on the ventral part of the epiblast, which is named the ‘bilaminar disc’. After the peri-implantation period, embryos experience the events of gastrulation at E14. Gastrulation is an important event during early embryonic development period as it lays vital foundation for three germ layers and the body plan [5]. Recently, benefiting from the emerging and rapid development of the new in vitro culture system of human embryo, our understanding of early human embryonic development has expanded to embryonic day 13/14 (E13/14) [6–8].

The thorough understanding of key mechanisms which regulate early human development is urgent need for fundamental research in developmental biology, infertility, artificial reproductive technology and genetic diseases. The pre-implantation development of embryo, which is from zygote to blastocyst stage, has high conservation across species, making it possible to study these initial stages in animal models. However, some obvious differences in the development between mouse and human embryos can be observed [9]. Notably, the implantation time of embryos (E5 in mouse and E7 in human), the characteristics of the morphogenesis and lineage development are not identical for mice and humans embryos [9]. Generally, the human embryos used in researches are mainly donated by patients receiving assisted reproductive therapy, and many embryos may have abnormalities such as chromosomal translocations, aneuploidies, or gene mutations which make them not suitable for the study of normal embryo development [10]. Fortunately, synthetic embryos, which were generated using stem cells or reprogrammed somatic cells, could mimic early embryos in morphological and gene expression aspects and faithfully recapitulate embryo development (Fig. 1). Therefore, the synthetic embryos of human is expected to benefit the further exploration of the factors affecting human pre-implantation and post-implantation embryo development.

Using individual blocks (such as stem cells) to construct in vitro embryo models could help verify the principles established based on experimental embryology techniques [11]. The synthetic embryos generated with stem cells highly simulate embryonic development and are rapidly becoming an important experimental tool to promote the study of mammalian embryos development. In 2023, Pedroza et al. established a system, human extra-embryoids (hEEs) to facilitate the self-organization of human pluripotent stem cells to 3D structures, which could recapitulate important features of human embryonic development, such as the human extra-embryonic tissue-level interactions [12]. The hEE system offers spatially and temporally organized 3D model, enabling researchers to capture various complex cell-state transitions. Moreover, by considering multiple parameters from gene expression to spatial patterning, hEE provides opportunities to address the unexplored development stages of human embryos.

In the past, our understanding of human embryogenesis during peri-implantation to gastrulation mainly come from researches on existing anatomical and histological samples of human embryos [13]. In 2020, a three-dimensional embryo culture system which could support human blastocysts develop to the primitive striped period has been reported [8]. These embryos from the 3D culture system recapitulate developmental markers and 3D structures of the natural developed embryos [8]. In the past, to culture human embryos in vitro must obey the 14-day rule. Recently, the International Society for Stem Cell Research (ISSCR) released novel guidelines to relax the 14-day rule [14]. Although the great progress of new in vitro embryo culture systems benefits the in deep investigation of embryonic development, there remain tons of challenges to explore human natural embryonic development only based on in vitro culture system, including the scarcity of embryonic materials and the difficulties of in vitro experimental techniques.

Here, we summarized and discussed the current research advances of synthetic embryos, the cell resources used for generating synthetic embryos, the potential applications, and current challenges of synthetic embryos (Fig. 2). This review also summarized and discussed the application of synthetic embryos in the study of embryo development, drug screening, genetic disease and organ transplantation.

Cell sources used for the construction of synthetic embryos

Embryonic stem cells (ESCs)

Embryonic stem cells are derived from the ICM of the blastocyst. ESCs could differentiate into all fetal tissues, and their potential in the fields of regenerative medicine and cell replacement therapy has gained wide recognition. Previous studies have reported the successful induction of human ESCs (hESCs) into multiple kinds of cells, such as cardiomyocytes [15], neurons [16], pancreatic-like cells [17], and vascular cells [18]. Microinjection of naive mouse ESCs (mESCs) into blastocysts can produce chimeric embryos, demonstrating the potential of these cells to develop to all parts of the embryo [19].

Study on human ESCs has shown that they could form spherical aggregates within a few hours in low adhesion plates with the supplementation of Chiron. The hESCs were treated by Chiron flowed by the treatment of WNT agonist for one day under two-dimension culture condition [20]. Then the aggregates broke symmetry and exhibited an elongated morphology [20]. These three-dimensional multicellular aggregates are further confirmed as human gastruloids, which could produce derivatives of the three germ layers which occurs in early human development [20]. Importantly, the human gastruloids can reduplicate the early-mid stages of human gastrulation on extracellular matrix micro-discs upon treatment by BMP4 [21]. Therefore, these human embryo-like structures are novel in vitro models for researches on the underlying mechanisms regulating human embryonic development.

Altogether, the ESCs have extraordinary plasticity and self-organizing ability, and are capable of establishing embryo-like structures. The ESCs derived embryonic models provide promising models for further researches on embryo development of mammals in vitro.

Trophoblast stem cells (TSCs)

Trophoblastic stem cells are derived from the trophoblast of blastocyst and have the ability to differentiate into various trophoblast cell types of the placenta in vivo, which are considered as important cellular models for studying the molecular mechanisms underlying placental development. Mouse TSCs have been isolated from mouse blastocysts or early post-implantation trophoblasts 20 years ago [22], while human TSCs were not successfully isolated until recent years [23] (Fig. 3).

In 2018, human TSCs were successfully generated from human villous cytotrophoblast (CT) cells and human blastocysts [23]. Subsequently, new methods for the differentiation of hESCs into trophoblast-like stem cells have been developed [24]. Given the successful generation of a more complicated stem cell-derived embryo model in mice, the isolation of TSC has raised concerns about the possibility of constructing a human pre-gastrulation models which includes the epiblast, hypoblast, and trophoblast-like lineages [25]. The development of stem cell models that include all three cell types precursors of embryos (fetuses and their supporting tissues) will provide a powerful tool for studying lineage crosstalk or differential lineage responses to disturbances which are challenging to study when using natural human embryos. However, this development necessitates the need for revisiting and refining existing ethical guidelines to ensure appropriate regulation of stem cell-based embryo models.

Extended pluripotent stem cells (EPSCs)

Mouse EpSCs are derived from mouse gastrulation stage embryos, which represent the initiation state of pluripotency [26–28]. In 2017, mouse EPSCs was first derived from 8 cell embryos and the EPSCs were also used to establish mouse synthetic embryos [29]. Meanwhile, both human EPSCs and mouse EPSCs have been established with a specific chemical cocktail which includes hLIF, CHIR 99,021, DiM, and MiH (LCDM) [30]. A single mouse EPSC has robust chimeric contribution to both embryonic and extraembryonic lineages in vivo and allows for the production of an entire mouse through tetraploid complementation [30]. Human EPSCs also showed interspecies chimeric ability in mouse conceptus [30]. Therefore, the EPS cell lines pave a novel pathway for further deep understanding of initial cell fate commitments at the molecular level and provide a model to study basic questions concerning development of embryonic and extraembryonic lineages.

Although it is not possible to produce human blastocyst-like structures from human EPSCs using the same method as mouse EPSCs, the human EPSCs could be induced to establish human blastocyst-like structures (EPS-blastoids) by an optimized 3D, two-step system [31]. In this system, hEPS cells were firstly exposed to BMP4 for 3 days to prepare for the formation of TE-like cells [31]. The TE-like cells were then combined with EPSCs to establish blastocyst-like structures [31]. Based on the results of immunofluorescence analysis, morphological analysis and single-cell transcriptomic analyses, these human EPS-blastoids are proved to contain three cell lineages of blastocysts and have similar transcriptome landscape with natural early blastocysts [31]. Moreover, human EPS-blastoids which were cultured in vitro for eight to ten days exhibited similar cell lineages and transcriptome landscape with natural human embryos [31]. The human EPS-blastoids provided a potential tool for the in deep investigation of human embryos development during preimplantation and postimplantation stages. Further study on human blastoids reconstruction showed that TE-like cells differentiated from human EPSCs had similar transcriptome characteristics to TE cells of natural embryos [32]. Moreover, the TE-like cells derived from human EPS cells showed enhanced signaling pathways related to the TE lineage upon epidermal growth factor (EGF) treatment [32]. Therefore, the TE-like cells differentiated from human EPSCs showed enhanced human blastoid construction and PE lineage differentiation capability upon EGF treatment [32].

Human naïve pluripotent stem cells (hnPSCs)

Naïve and primed pluripotent stem cells represent the cellular states of the pre-implantation and post-implantation embryo, respectively. Different from the naïve pluripotency of mouse ESCs, human ESCs exhibited primed pluripotency. Until now, several groups reported the established naïve human pluripotent cells using various methods which have similar molecular characteristics and functional properties to mouse naive ES cells [33–38]. Furthermore, the human naïve PSCs were found to have the potential to differentiate into trophoblast-like cells without forced expression of transcription factors [39, 40].

In recent years, human naïve PSCs have been applied to successfully establish human embryo-like structures. For example, in 2021, Yu and colleagues firstly described their generated human blastocyst-like structures based on human naïve PSCs [41]. In their study, a three-dimensional sequential differentiation strategy was used to enable the human naïve PSCs self-organize into human blastocyst-like structures in vitro, which were termed as ‘human blastoids’ [41]. Results of immunofluorescence analysis and single-cell transcriptome analysis demonstrated that these ‘human blastoids’ have similar spatial organization, cell-lineage allocation and transcriptional characteristics with human blastocysts [41]. Subsequently, several groups also reported their human blastocyst-like structures generated from human naïve PSCs [42–45]. Human 8-cell-like-cells (8CLCs) were discovered from human naïve PSCs in 2023, which is the earliest human embryonic cell state captured in cultured cells to date [46]. 8CLCs have the potential to become an ideal model system for in vitro study of human genome activation as they have ZGA-, totipotency-like transcriptional, epigenetic, and developmental properties [46]. Therefore, the human embryo-like structure generated from human naïve PSCs provided a useful model for the study of human blastocysts development and could expand our knowledge for the exploration of better medical support for reproduction-related diseases in the future.

Reprogrammed fibroblasts

In 2021, Liu and colleagues reprogrammed the human dermal fibroblasts (HDFs) and firstly generated the iBlastoids, which is the first in vitro three-dimensional model of human blastocyst [47]. Results of further molecular and functional experiments indicated that iBlastoids could model the overall architecture of human blastocysts and have transcriptional similarity to human blastocysts [47]. Compared to pluripotent stem cells, trophoblast stem cells and naive extraembryonic endoderm cells, cells from the EPI-, TE-, and PE-like clusters in the iBlastoids exhibited more similar transcriptional features to the EPI, TE, and PE cells of blastocysts [47]. Moreover, stem cells, including naive PS cells, primed PS cells and TS cells, could be isolated from iBlastoids [47]. Overall, the iBlastoids may provide an advanced in vitro model for researches on human embryogenesis, early developmental diseases and the exploration of effective treatment methods for developmental diseases. More importantly, generating human embryonic-like structures by reprogramming somatic cells is a novel strategy which avoids the damage of human embryos.

The potential role and application of synthetic embryos

Synthetic embryos provide a new strategy for exploring the embryo development events

The abnormalities during the transition from blastocyst to gastrula can result in implantation failure or pregnancy loss, which are main reasons for the failure of in vitro fertilization (IVF), and are main limitations for assisted reproductive technology (ART) [48]. Due to ethical issues and limitations in sample accessibility, the key mechanisms of implantation failure and pregnancy loss remain in a “black box”. Moreover, little is known about environmental factors, such as potential toxins, which may be the cause of developmental failure during human embryo implantation [49, 50]. Fortunately, the synthetic embryos of human provided opportunities for deconstructing post-implantation human embryonic development by avoiding the technical, ethical, and legal limitations of natural human embryos [51]. Moreover, the synthetic embryos are easy for genetic manipulation and lineage tracing, which helps to understand the genetic and molecular mechanisms of human embryo development. Therefore, the synthetic embryos can be use to improve the basic understanding of human embryonic development and provide scientific basis for preventing miscarriage, birth defects, and teratogenicity.

Synthetic embryos provide new strategy for researches on human diseases

In the past decades, animal models and immortalized two-dimensional (2D) cultured cell lines (usually derived from tumor specimens) have been important tools for researches on human diseases. However, due to specie-specific differences in various aspects such as physiology, metabolism, and genetics, experimental animal models are less perfect tools for the study of human physiology and pathophysiology. Furthermore, traditional human cell lines cannot simulate the cell-cell interactions in vivo, which is a limitation to fully understand underlying mechanisms of human diseases. Notably, the synthetic embryos provide new opportunities as research models for researches on human diseases.

Congenital heart disease (CHD) is one of the most common causes of neonatal death [52]. The gastruloid models contain multiple cell types of all germ layers and have the advantage of being able to more accurately depict the development of cardiogenesis development. Rossi et al. captured cardiogenesis in gastruloids [53], which could depict crucial steps of early cardiac development in vitro. In their study, three cardiogenic factors including fibroblast growth factor, ascorbic acid, and vascular endothelial growth factor 165 could induce cardiac differentiation from pluripotent stem cells. This model not only mimics embryonic development, but also supports the generation of cardiovascular progenitors, including the first heart field (FHF) and the second heart field (SHF). The cardiac progenitors self-organize into a cardiac crescent-like region and form a beating cardiac tissue [53].

Novel models which include cardiac muscle and neurons have been developed to depict neuro-cardiac lineages. The human EMLOC model which replicates both cardiogenesis and neurogenesis was established by optimizing the protocol of “elongating multi-lineage organized” (EMLO) gastruloids to include angiocrine and pro-cardiogenic factors [54, 55]. This model recapitulates developmental features which are similar to in vivo heart, including the formation of the heart tube and putative outflow tract, the morphogenesis of ventricular wall and epicardium, and the differentiation of cardiomyocytes [55]. This neural cardiac model can provide a potential in vitro model for future researches on heart diseases, such as neural-based heart arrhythmia diseases.

Huntington’s disease (HD) is a progressive and fatal neurodegenerative disorder which caused by an expansion of a CAG repeat in the huntingtin gene (HTT) [56]. The pathogenesis of HD and the normal function of the huntingtin protein have not been well understood. There remains no effective therapy to treat or delay the progression of HD until now [56]. To investigate how early the HTT-CAG expansion can cause human embryonic defects, Galgoczi and colleagues established the 2D human gastruloids derived from human ESCs with a specific and highly reproducible CAG length-dependent phenotypic signature [57]. The results of this study showed that huntingtin CAG expansion disrupted the spatial restriction of activator response in the context of the polarized epithelium of the 2D gastruloid, which first demonstrated that HTT-CAG expansion has an influence on human gastrulation. This study reported the earliest developmental time point be altered by the HD mutation.

In a study of SARS-CoV-2, the expression patterns of the ACE2 and TMPRSS2 genes were examined in human pre-gastrulation embryos which grow up to day 14 on an in vitro culture platform [58]. The co-expression of these two genes in the trophoblast of the blastocyst and syncytiotrophoblast and hypoblast of the implantation stages demonstrated the possibility for vertical transmission, which required in deep investigation to understand potential risks of SARS-CoV-2 infection to implantation, placental health and fetal health using human stem-cell-derived embryo models [58]. Moreover, the diseases that occur at specific time points can be studied using reconstructing embryo-like structures, such as blastoids.

Synthetic embryos as key materials for drug screening and toxicological testing

Some teratogenic drugs and chemicals which present in food, beverages, and the environment can possibly lead to early miscarriage [59–61]. Drugs which proved to be safe in animal models may also cause severe embryonic toxicity in human. For example, the studies on mice indicated that the thalidomide had no adverse effects to the organism, and it was once considered safe. However, in 1961, Widukin Lenz and William McBride noted that the fetuses and newborns which were exposed to thalidomide in the uterus exhibited obvious abnormalities. Other studies found that the exposure of thalidomide can cause defects in limb, eyes, ears, face and vertebral column [62]. This devastating case suggests that some compounds may have potential human specific-susceptibility during development, highlighting the necessity of developing models using human cells to screen drugs and perform toxicological testing. Fortunately, the synthetic embryo models have the advantage of mass production, which can meet the sample requirements for drug screening and toxicological assays.

Excipients, such as coloring agents and preservatives, are commonly used pharmacologically inactive ingredients in many drugs, foods, and cosmetics. However, the concentrations of most excipients that may have adverse effects on embryo development are largely unknown. Researchers established the stem cell-based in vitro morphogenesis models to evaluate and confirm the lowest observed adverse effect level (LOAEL) of developmental toxicity caused by three coloring agents (allura red, brilliant blue, and tartrazine) and three preservatives (butylated hydroxyanisole, metabisulfite, and methylparaben) [63]. Results of gene expression analysis showed that treatment of excipient lead to significant changes in morphology and gene expression profile. This study demonstrates the possibility of using synthetic embryo models to explore the potential developmental toxicity of commonly used excipients.

Embryo models have also been used to explore the teratogenic potential of industrial compounds. Methoxyacetic acid (MAA) is a primary metabolite of methoxyethanol and dimethoxyethyl phthalate, which are commonly used organic solvent and plasticizer, respectively. Animal studies have shown that MAA is a teratogen which causes various abnormalities in developing embryos [64]. Researchers further explored the molecular mechanisms underlying the teratogenic effects of MAA using mouse gastruloids, which are 3D aggregates of pluripotent stem cells [65]. The results indicate that MAA may impair the axial elongation morphogenesis of mouse gastruloids in a RA signaling-dependent manner by inhibiting histone deacetylase.

The amniotic membrane ectoderm is the first tissue that differentiates from the ectoderm during human embryo implantation. In 2017, in vitro human amnion-like tissue was generated through the self-organization of human pluripotent stem cells [66]. Subsequently, the post-implantation amniotic sac embryoid (PASE) was also generated using the human pluripotent stem cells, which depicts various post-implantation embryogenic events centered around amniotic sac development [67]. In 2019, a microengineered human amniotic ectoderm (AM) tissue array has been established to detect the toxicity of six clinically relevant drugs on amniotic ectoderm development [68]. These drugs are Penicillin [69], Doxorubicin [70, 71], Ibuprofen and Acetaminophen [72], Dexamethasone and Citalopram. Results indicated that Penicillin showed no significant toxicity on the AM development, while Doxorubicin had significant toxicity on the AM development. Moreover, the acetaminophen is less toxic to AM tissue than ibuprofen, which is consistent with clinical experience that pregnant women are recommended to use Acetaminophen instead of Ibuprofen. However, Dexamethasone and Citalopram are potential teratogens for human embryo development [73]. Therefore, the human amniotic ectoderm tissue array was considered as a powerful tool for drug research and toxicity screening [74]. Human embryo models may provide powerful platforms for detecting potential teratogenic effects of newly discovered drugs.

Synthetic embryos are alternatives for animal models in basic research and preclinical trials

Our understanding of transcriptional regulation of human embryo development primarily comes from animal models (mainly in mice). However, there are many significant differences between human and mouse embryos in terms of the morphological, gene expression patterns, and the developmental time scale (Fig. 4) [75]. For example, FGF8, an essential gene required for cell migration during mouse gastrulation [76], was not expressed in human embryo [77]. FGF2 was not expressed in mouse embryo, but was expressed during human embryo development. Although the morphological and molecular investigation on non-human primate embryos have offered valuable insights of human gastrulation, study on non-human primates is limited by material scarcity, technical difficulties, and ethical issues [78, 79]. The human embryo models have shown advantages in simulating the human natural embryonic development process and are considered as promising alternatives to animal models for researches on human embryonic development.

Clinical trials are time-consuming and costly, therefore reliable pre-clinical studies are needed to improve their success rate. Many treatment strategies proved to be effective in pre-clinical rodent models failed in human clinical trials [80]. The developed human synthetic embryos may achieve better performance in predicting drug toxicity and can alternative models for some animal experiments.

Factors affecting the generation of synthetic embryos

The successful reconstruction of high-fidelity synthetic embryos is affected by various factors. Three important factors, the starting cells, the biochemical conditions, as well as the physical environment, dramatically affect the establishment of synthetic embryos [25].

The cell types used for constructing synthetic embryos

The cells types exhibited different capability to facilitate the construction of an synthetic embryo model. Although the human blastocyst-like structures can be constructed by reprogrammed fibroblasts, most synthetic embryos are established based on stem cells [47]. Since the first mESCs derived from the ICM of mouse blastocyst were established [81, 82], many different types of stem cells representing various developmental stages have been generated from early embryos [83]. These stem cells have the potential ability to be transformed into embryonic or extraembryonic stem cell states through genetic modification or chemical inductions [84] although they exhibit varying differentiation ability [85].

The roles, benefits, and limitations of diverse cell types in constructing synthetic embryo have been thoroughly discussed in Sect. 2: “Cell sources used for the construction of synthetic embryos”.

The signaling molecules

Signaling molecules secreted by the cells are crucial for regulating cell fate in embryos. For example, morphogens are secreted signaling molecules from a localized source and determine the arrangement and fate of responding cells in a concentration dependent manner [86]. The Fibroblast Growth Factor (FGF) signaling plays a vital role in the specification of PE and EPI [87], while the Bone Morphogenetic Protein (BMP) signaling regulates the correct development of extra-embryonic lineages, the TE and PE [88]. During mouse gastrulation, Wingless-INT (WNT), BMP, Nodal and FGF serve as morphogens which diffuse from their sources to establish concentration gradients, thus directing cell fate [89]. Therefore, it is feasible to induce cell fate transitions in vitro by adding appropriate chemical molecules to the culture medium. It has been indicated that XEN cells derived from the primitive endoderm of mouse blastocysts can differentiate into the extraembryonic visceral endoderm with the induction of BMP4 [90]. Human ES cells can differentiate into trophoblasts when exposed to BMP4 [91], while FGF2 showed inhibition effect on this differentiation [92].

Signaling molecules have been proved to be vital in the establishment of synthetic embryos. For example, the establishment of the anteroposterior polarity and the development of a primitive streak-like region in embryonic bodies derived from ESCs depend on local activation of the Wnt pathway [93]. In this study, it has been shown that the supplementation of exogenous Wnt proteins could posteriorize the embryoid body and result in mesendodermal fate, while Wnt antagonists could promote anterior character and neurectodermal differentiation. Notably, Wnt signaling agonist has been used in helping to establish human gastruloids derived from hESCs [20]. In detail, the human ES cells which were treated with WNT agonist Chiron for one day in two-dimensional culture were seeded in low-adherence plates with Chiron and then formed compact and spherical aggregates in a few hours. These aggregates gradually formed elongated structures. Prior to aggregation, although the pre-treated human ES cells expressed pluripotency markers, the expression of mesendodermal markers, including BRA, MIXL1, EOMES, and membrane-localized N-cadherin (CDH2) was increased which indicate that upon Chiron pre-treatment, human ES cells were partially activated to a primitive-streak-like state. Moreover, in the in vitro model of human gastrulation, the signaling cascade of BMP, WNT, and Nodal pathways was found to control the fate patterning of human gastruloids [94]. In this study, the inhibition of WNT signaling completely abolishes mesodermal differentiation, while inhibiting NODAL signaling reduces mesoderm differentiation, proving that WNT signaling initiates mesoderm differentiation and NODAL signaling promotes mesoderm differentiation. Moreover, WNT and NODAL signaling synergize to maintain high NANOG levels at the colony center. BMP signaling plays a role in initiating waves of WNT and NODAL signaling which move toward the colony center at constant rates, and functions in extra-embryonic differentiation at the colony edge.

The biomaterial platform used in the generation of synthetic embryos

Biomaterials are critical for the generation of synthetic embryos by supporting and directing the stem cell growth and determining the stem cell fate [95, 96]. Therefore, in order to better simulate the in vivo natural microenvironment of stem cells, various biomaterials have been developed. Currently, the microwells, micropatterns, hydrogels and microfluidic devices are commonly used culture platforms to facilitate the establishment of synthetic embryos in vitro.

In 2018, Rivron and colleagues developed the mouse blastocyst-like structures (blastoids) by sequentially culturing ESCs and TSCs in a microwell array [97]. Moreover, mouse ETX embryos derived from ES, TS and XEN cells, mouse EPS-blastoids derived from EPSCs and TSCs, mouse EPS-blastoids EPS cells, as well as the mouse iETX embryos derived from ESCs, TSCs and XEN cells, have been generated using the microwells [98–101]. Notably, spliceosomes-repressed totipotent blastomere-like cells (TBLCs) can develop to mouse blastocyst-like structures on the reported microwells [102]. Human blastoids have also been successfully generated on hydrogel microwells [103].

Micropatterned technology could precisely control the size and geometry of stem cells in colonies. In 2014, Warmflash and colleagues used micropatterns to provide the cultured human embryonic stem cells with geometrical constraints, and used bone morphogenetic protein 4 (BMP4) as a differentiation trigger in conditioned media, thus recapitulated early embryonic spatial patterning in human ESCs [104]. In 2018, the micropattern has been used as the platform to establish hPSC-based neuroectoderm developmental model which could mimic neuroectoderm regionalization during early neurulation [105]. Furthermore, in 2020, Minn et al.. generated the human gastruloids to model primate-specific features of embryogenesis through the 2D micropatterned gastruloid system [21].

The surrounding ECM of stem cells make valuable contribution in stem cell behavior and can direct cell differentiation into a specific lineage [106]. Hydrogels have become powerful tools to regulate stem cell fate by providing desired matrix mechanics, microscale structure, and cell-cell interactions [96, 107]. Hydrogel-based platforms have been developed to support the establishment of embryo-like structures from stem cells. For example, the mouse EPI aggregates and mouse TS aggregates were respectively established from ESCs and TSCs using hydrogel microwell array technology. Then EPI aggregates and TSC aggregates can fuse together and develop into mouse EpiTS embryoids in U-bottom low-attachment wells of a 96-well plate [108].

Hydrogel microwell can also be used to form human blastoids [103]. The initial number of cells per microwells plays a vital role for the successful establishment of human blastoids. The hydrogel microwells can be applied to control the initial cell number and generate human blastoids resembling preimplantation embryos. Notably, a 3D implantation-like niche (Gel-3D) was developed to establish human in vitro amnion-like tissue through self-organization of hPSCs [66]. When hPSCs are cultured in this 3D biomimetic native hydrogel environment, most of the hPSC clusters gradually differentiate into uniform, squamous amniotic tissues. Further study showed that a subset of these hPSC clusters could develop to post-implantation amniotic sac embryoid which is similar to the in vivo human amniotic sac [67]. However, the developmental efficiency of asymmetric embryonic cystic tissue in Gel-3D is not high, hindering its wide application for detailed mechanism studies as a tractable tool.

Stem cells are highly sensitive to the surrounding microenvironment. Microfluidic devices could accurately control various soluble and physical factors simultaneously in a spatiotemporal manner, providing a platform to simulate the in vivo microenvironment for stem cells [109]. The microfluidic device has been used as the platform to establish a hPSC-based model which could recapitulate key landmarks of early human post-implantation development [110]. Compared to the Gel-3D based culture system, the controllability and efficiency of the microfluidic human embryoid system significantly improved. Microfluidics can be conveniently used to generate dynamic and graded morphogenetic signals, which is crucial for controlling patterning and multicellular self-organization. However, the embryonic sac-like tissues produced by the microfluidic human embryoid system lack primitive endoderm and trophoblast, and therefore cannot form yolk sacs and placenta. For ethical perspectives, the incompleteness of this human embryo model is beneficial to alleviate ethical concerns on human embryo research. Notably, the microfluidic human embryoid system is capable of recapitulating important early post-implantation developmental markers in a highly controllable and scalable manner. These makers include the lumen formation of EPI, formation of the bipolar embryonic sac, primordial germ cell (PGC) specification, and development of gastrulating cells (or mesendoderm cells) with controlled anterior/posterior polarity in the EPI. This microfluidic human embryoid system could be easily used in cell biology laboratories as it does not need the fluid processing equipment typically required for more complex microfluidic devices, including tubing, syringe pumps, and valves [111, 112]. Moreover, this system is compatible with live imaging, immunofluorescence staining, fluorescent in situ hybridization (FISH), and single-cell sequencing. However, the microfluidic human embryoid system can only be maintained for about 4–5 days, as the limited space inside microfluidic devices restricts the sustained growth and development of human embryoids and the disseminating cells from human embryoids mimicking gastrulation and mesendoderm induction cause collapse and disintegration of human embryoid structure. Moreover, the EPI-like compartment of this microfluidic human embryoid can only be either posteriorized or anteriorized, thus lacking an anterior-posterior axis. Further studies need to focus on establishment of an optimized system to extend the culture time of human embryoids and facilitate self-organization of gastrulating cells to recapitulate the emergent trilaminar germ disk formation of human embryo.

Challenges for the synthetic embryos generation

Technical challenges

Despite the rapid development of embryo-like structures in recent decades, some technical challenges affect the successful establishment of synthetic embryos. The exploration of better starting cells for downstream differentiation and for assembling into embryo-like structures is a hot research area [113]. For instance, recent study has reported the successful generation of hypoblast cells (naive hPSC-derived hypoblast-like cells (nHyCs)), which could cooperate with naive hPSCs to form a 3D bilaminar structure (bilaminoids) with a pro-amniotic-like cavity [114]. The bilaminoids could recapitulate the patterning of the anterior-posterior axis and the formation of cells which could reflect the pregastrula stage. In the future, attention should be paid to multiple cell types, such as trophoblast cells, amniotic cells, and mesenchymal cells, in order to better form “assembloids” involving various cell types. Moreover, establishing well-characterized and highly controllable cell culture conditions is critical to achieve efficient and repeatable embryo model generation because small deviations in the culture process may lead to significant downstream effects [113].

How to keep the pluripotency of synthetic embryos during long-term cultivation is a significant challenge for human synthetic embryos. In 2016, Deglincerti et al.. established a system to culture human attached embryos in vitro until day post-fertilization 14 (d.p.f.14) [6]. Non-human primate natural embryos can grow in vitro for up to 20 days [79, 115]. Knowledge from in vitro culture of human embryos and non-human primate embryos may pave the way for long term culture of human synthetic embryos.

Furthermore, there are several differences between current developed synthetic embryos and natural human embryos. Therefore, further understanding of the underlying mechanisms for embryonic development is still required to help to generate synthetic embryos more similar to the in vivo embryos. It has been reported that several in vitro generated embryo models have different cell compositions from their in vivo counterparts according to the RNA sequencing data [116, 117]. Currently, single cell RNA sequencing is a commonly used method to evaluate the fidelity of in vitro generated embryo models. The number of genes considered for each cell in the single-cell transcriptome ranges from as few as 200 genes to an average of 2000–6000 genes. Considering that there are approximately 30,000 genes in the human genome, relying solely on single-cell transcriptomics to compare the similarity between synthetic embryos and natural embryos is insufficient. Novel tools that can more accurately distinguish the subtle differences need to be developed to help to establish in vitro synthetic embryo models more similar to natural embryos. Moreover, the comparison of molecular similarity between synthetic embryos and natural embryos is usually conducted by evaluating the expression levels of individual lineage markers. Zhao et al. raised that due to the sharing of molecular markers among various cell lineages in early embryo development, cell types and their states are not always associated with a single or limited number of markers [117]. They developed a reference dataset which covers developmental stages from the zygote to the gastrula through integrating six published human datasets [117]. This study identified markers that encompass all lineages during early embryo development and provided distinct clusters resolving different lineages at various embryonic development stages. The use of this reference tool may potentially reduce the risk of mis-annotation of synthetic embryos. Therefore, further studies on molecular mechanisms of embryo development and further technological improvements are required for annotating synthetic embryos, thereby promoting the construction of synthetic embryos with more similarities in gene expression profile with natural embryos.

The rapid development of artificial intelligence (AI) has provided opportunities for the improvement in generating synthetic embryos. Deep learning tools can help analyze datasets more quickly and thoroughly of single-cell sequencing for mouse and human embryos, obtaining more information about the embryonic development process, which will aid in the construction of synthetic embryos [118]. Moreover, deep learning could help to reliably classify and predict embryonic development [119]. In future, deep learning may help to improve the reproducibility of stem cell-derived embryo models and contribute to the standardization of stem cell-derived embryo model research [119]. The application of AI in image analysis, developmental prediction, multi-omics integration, and optimization of culture systems in synthetic embryo-related research will significantly enhance the efficiency, fidelity, and standardization of embryo development.

Ethical challenges

Synthetic embryos which could recapitulate the early embryos development in vitro provided convenient alternatives for researches on human embryos [120]. However, rapid development of synthetic embryos challenged current legal definitions of embryos, and some ethical concerns about their use emerged [120, 121]. Therefore, it is essential to set up ethical guidelines for generation and use of human synthetic embryos. In 2021, the ISSCR updated the Guidelines for Stem Cell Research and Clinical Translation which includes ethical guidelines for using embryo models [122]. The non-integrated models and integrated models are the two distinct embryo models recognized by the Guidelines. Non-integrated models lack the potential to generate fetuses. Researches on such embryo models need to be reported to the entity responsible for the specialized scientific and ethics oversight process, but these researches typically do not need further or ongoing review. Integrated models contain both embryonic and extraembryonic tissues and have the potential to form fetuses. Researches on such embryo models can only be allowed after being reviewed by a specialized oversight process. Moreover, transferring human embryo models to any uterus, either animal or human, is prohibited [122, 123].

Although the use of iPSCs to establish human synthetic embryos has achieved success, the current cell sources for synthetic embryos are mostly stem cells. Due to the fact that stem cells are obtained by destroying a human embryo, this may still pose ethical issues. Future research directions should focus more on using iPSCs to establish human synthetic embryos or directly reprogramming cells to establish synthetic embryos without going through the iPSC stage, lowering the ethical threshold for cell acquisition, and making the entire process of generating synthetic embryos unrelated to human natural embryos.

Conclusion and future perspective

The in deep understanding of human embryo development is of significance to the study and treatment of human disease related to embryo development. However, studies on human embryo development is limited by the complexity of the embryo development process, the lack of suitable models, and ethical issues. With the research progress of synthetic embryos, the investigation of embryo development will be promoted quickly [116]. Here we thoroughly summarized and discussed the generation, application, and challenges of synthetic embryo models as research models for human and other species.

The initial synthetic embryos were mouse embryo-like structures established using mouse stem cells (Fig. 5). After that, the synthetic embryos for human, non-human primates and bovine are gradually generated. Synthetic embryos could recapitulate the key events of embryo development, thus provide a platform for studying many development related diseases and their underlying mechanisms. Unlike the scarcity of natural embryo samples (especially human embryo samples), synthetic embryo models can be mass-produced to provide compatibility with systems requiring a large number of samples, such as drug screening and toxicology analysis. Moreover, the use of synthetic embryos as substitutes for experimental animals could reduce the injury and death of animals.

Nevertheless, the establishment of synthetic embryos still faces some technical challenges, such as the select of appropriate starting cell and the design of better culture platforms. More advanced tools for verifying cell lineage integrity of synthetic embryos also need to be developed. Moreover, the combination of synthetic embryology with bioengineering and material engineering will enable us to have deeper insights of the early stage of life by controlling embryo models, thus paving the way for basic biology research, new drug development, personalized medicine and regenerative therapy [124, 125].

Future development of biotechnology tools, such as micro-fabricated chips [126], optogenetics [127], and stimuli-responsive biomaterials [128], will open up novel avenues for the generation of synthetic embryo models. Organ-on-chip culture technology has been applied to develop various organ models, including but not limited to intestines [129], livers [130], kidneys [131], heart and reproductive tissues [132, 133]. The organ-on-chip model has the advantage of independent control of biomechanical, biochemical, and cellular parameters, and could faithfully simulate the cellular microenvironment, with unique advantages in organ modeling. Optogenetics refers to a technique which combines genetics and optics to perform protein function control using light. Optogenetic manipulation of signaling in vivo have been applied to reveal the signaling dynamics in specific cell populations during embryonic development [134, 135]. Optogenetics enables us to decode mechanisms that control cell fate determination and morphogenesis, thereby contributing to the generation of synthetic embryos. Moreover, 3D printing is a technology which uses tissue-like or cell materials to generate biological structures for tissue engineering [136]. However, the printed structures generated in this way are static and may not be able to promote necessary biological reactions, and cannot actively respond to environmental changes. Therefore, stimuli-responsive biocompatible materials that can respond to stimuli (physical, chemical, and/or biological) and lead to microstructures modifications have been widely studied for tissue engineering applications [137, 138]. The method of using stimuli-responsive materials and/or cells to provide predicted dynamic transformations for printed structures in a controllable manner is called 4D printing [137, 138]. Only a few responsive materials have been designed for 4D printing in tissue engineering field. Moreover, responsive materials will be used in the presence of cells or in vivo, the stimulus on materials must be safe and easily controlled. For example, extreme pH values and high temperatures need to be avoided. In addition, tissues are subjected to a variety of stimuli in vivo, however, most materials currently studied only respond to one stimulus. Thus more efforts are needed to develop new multifunctional materials to improve 4D printing technology, and the organization and biological functionality of printed organs and tissues. The 4D printing technology may provide a novel platform for the construction of synthetic embryos.

The development of uterus-like microenvironment and the uterus organoids provide opportunities for the integration of extraembryonic tissue-containing embryo models to understand the embryo-maternal interface and the implantation [139, 140]. In 2023, researchers developed a uterus-like microenvironment called 3E-uterus (ex vivo engineered uterine environment with 3D geometrically patterned hydrogels), which contributes to the first success of culturing the whole peri-implantation mouse embryo ex vivo, recapitulating the interaction between embryos and uterus [139]. This system can monitor cell dynamics and disrupt cellular processes through genetic, pharmacological, and biophysical methods, thereby providing opportunities for the deep exploration on potential mechanisms of embryo-uterus interaction. Tian and colleagues reported a novel ALI-EnAo (endometrial assembloids) model by combining endometrial epithelial cells (EnEC) and stromal cells (EnSC) based on an improved matrix and air-liquid interface (ALI) culture method [140]. ALI EnAos recapitulates the anatomical structure, cellular composition, hormone-induced menstrual cycle changes, gene expression profiles, as well as dynamic ciliary generation of the human endometrium, which will be helpful for studies on embryo implantation, endometrial growth, differentiation, and disease. The rapid development of embryo models and maternal environment modeling platform will provide great opportunity for understanding the mechanisms underlying developmental pathologies in the future.
