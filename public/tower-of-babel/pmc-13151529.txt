Subclinical atherosclerosis in women after pre-eclampsia: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Jessica Victoria Yao, Benjamen Wang, Anand Ganes, Stacey Peters, Andrew Taylor, Shaun Brennecke, Ingrid Winship, Dominica Zentner
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWomen with pre-eclampsia are at increased risk of later-life cardiovascular disease (CVD). Despite suggestions that women affected by pre-eclampsia should undergo routine CVD screening, uniform recommendations are lacking. Subclinical atherosclerosis provides a window of opportunity to identify and treat those at risk of CVD. Coronary artery calcium scoring (CAC), carotid intima–media thickness (CIMT) and ankle brachial index (ABI) are effective methods of detecting subclinical atherosclerosis. This systematic review sought to explore associations between timing of measurement and presence of subclinical atherosclerosis in women with a history of pre-eclampsia.MethodsWe searched the MEDLINE, EMBASE and CINAHL databases for all studies which reported subclinical atherosclerosis in women with a history of pre-eclampsia. Common and random effects models were used to examine the associations between pre-eclampsia and subclinical atherosclerosis.Results35 case–control studies comprising 20 235 women were included in the final analysis. 3376 had a history of pre-eclampsia. Among the 26 studies assessing CIMT, pre-eclampsia was associated with increased CIMT (standardised mean difference 0.63 (95% CI 0.32, 0.93)). This difference was present during pregnancy (0.65 (95% CI 0.33, 0.98)) and persisted for 12 months post partum (0.84 (95% CI 0.20, 1.47)) and beyond (0.50 (95% CI 0.01, 0.99)). Pooled analysis of the eight CAC studies also demonstrated that the odds of having subclinical atherosclerosis were 1.57 (95% CI 1.39, 1.77) times higher in women with a history of pre-eclampsia.ConclusionsAmong women with a history of pre-eclampsia, subclinical atherosclerosis can be seen during pregnancy and persists long term. Our review supports the importance of early follow-up in patients who have had pre-eclampsia.

Introduction

Pre-eclampsia (PE) is defined as the onset of hypertension after 20 weeks’ gestation associated with either proteinuria, maternal organ dysfunction or uteroplacental dysfunction.1 PE affects around 2%–4% of all pregnancies globally, with the incidence increasing over the last two decades in several countries.2 PE is a leading cause of maternal morbidity and mortality. In recent years, it has become apparent that pregnancy complications including hypertensive disorders of pregnancy (gestational hypertension and PE) and gestational diabetes increase the risk of later-life cardiovascular disease (CVD).3 PE is associated with a twofold increased risk of coronary artery disease (CAD) and a fourfold increased risk of heart failure.4 Despite suggestions that PE should be incorporated into traditional CVD risk models, uniform clinical guidance is lacking. Current international guidelines differ with respect to timing and method of screening women following PE.

The British National Institute for Health and Care Excellence guidelines recommend CVD risk-reduction strategies post partum but do not suggest regular screening.5 The American College of Obstetricians and Gynaecologists guidelines recommend lifestyle modifications but do not recommend screening.6 The American Heart Association recommends monitoring blood pressure (BP) and body mass index (BMI) as well as lifestyle counselling at 6 and 12 weeks, 6 and 12 months post partum.7 In contrast, the Australian Society of Obstetric Medicine of Australia and New Zealand guidelines recommend long-term monitoring: BP, urine protein, BMI and metabolic profile at 3–6 months post partum and annually.8 Similarly, the International Society for the Study of Hypertension in Pregnancy (ISSHP) recommends BP monitoring at 3 and 6 months and annual reviews.1 The disparity in international recommendations reflects poor understanding of the relationship between PE and CVD and highlights the need for further research in this area.

Atherosclerosis is a progressive inflammatory condition beginning early in life that can eventually lead to CAD.9 Subclinical atherosclerosis is defined as atherosclerosis in asymptomatic individuals. Exact prevalence is unknown. However, it is more common in those with traditional CVD risk factors including diabetes mellitus, dyslipidaemia and family history of premature CAD.9 PE is known to cause vascular damage, thus increasing risk for subclinical atherosclerosis.10 Coronary artery calcium scoring (CAC), carotid intima–media thickness (CIMT) and ankle brachial index (ABI) are effective methods of detecting subclinical atherosclerosis that enhance CAD risk stratification.11,13

Several studies have shown that early-stage subclinical atherosclerosis can be reversed.14 Thus, subclinical atherosclerosis provides a window of opportunity to identify and treat those at risk of CAD. The aim of this systematic review and meta-analysis was to explore associations between timing of assessment and presence of subclinical atherosclerosis in women with a history of PE.

Methods

This systematic review was conducted according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses guidelines. The study was registered on the PROSPERO International prospective register of systematic reviews (ID:CRD42024526268).

Eligibility criteria

This review included all case–control studies which explored subclinical atherosclerosis in women with a history of PE. As per ISSHP, PE was defined as a systolic BP of ≥140 mm Hg and/or diastolic BP ≥90 mm Hg with either proteinuria, as assessed by dipstick urinalysis and/or urine protein/creatinine ratio ≥30 mg/mmol, or maternal organ or uteroplacental dysfunction after 20 weeks’ gestation.1 There was no restriction on age, PE severity, gestation at PE diagnosis, delivery gestation, parity nor timing of follow-up. All studies evaluating subclinical atherosclerosis with CIMT, CAC, CT coronary angiogram or ABI were included. Studies needed at least two groups—a group with a history of PE and a control group defined as women who had had a pregnancy unaffected by PE.

Case reports, case series, conference abstracts, systematic reviews and narrative reviews were excluded but used for review of reference lists. Studies that included women with other pregnancy complications, where subgroup analysis for women with PE was not performed, were excluded. Furthermore, studies assessing functional, rather than anatomical, tests for atherosclerosis were excluded. Studies which did not indicate timing of investigation were excluded. Studies written in a language other than English were also excluded.

Information source and search strategy

The search was conducted in MEDLINE, EMBASE and CINAHL, with assistance from a librarian and included all articles from inception of databases until March 2024. The search strategy (see online supplemental data) was devised to capture all women with a history of PE and a subclinical atherosclerosis assessment. Reference lists of the included articles were assessed for relevant review articles.

Selection of articles

Articles were selected using the Covidence systematic review software, Veritas Health Innovation (covidence.org). After removing duplicated articles, two reviewers (JVY and AG) independently screened all articles by title and abstract. 43 conflicts were resolved through discussion between the two reviewers. Consensus was reached with the aid of a third reviewer for three articles (BW). Full texts were then reviewed by two reviewers (JVY and AG). Conflicts were again resolved by discussion between the two reviewers. Remaining articles were included for data extraction.

Data extraction

Independent data extraction was performed by JVY and BW using a prespecified data collection proforma. This included year, country, study design, PE definition, number of participants, participant characteristics, timing of assessment post partum, PE severity, control group and results.

Study quality assessment

Study quality and risk of bias were assessed by JVY and BW independently, based on the Newcastle-Ottawa Quality Assessment Scale (NOS) for case–control studies. Our gold standards were selection of women with a history of PE confirmed in medical records according to set criteria, selection of a control cohort from the same population and studies which controlled for age and at least one other factor.

Data synthesis and analysis

The R ‘meta’ package’ was used to conduct both common and random effects models. The inverse variance method was used for pooling means and ORs. Between study variances were assessed using I2 and τ2 estimates. Subgroup analyses were based on investigation modality (CIMT and CAC) and timing of assessment. Short-term and long-term assessment was defined as less and more than 1 year post index pregnancy, respectively. Further sensitivity analysis was conducted on studies with more than 10 years follow-up. Funnel plots were also conducted to assess for possible publication bias. Egger’s regression and trim-and-fill analyses were performed to account for publication bias. Statistical significance and heterogeneity were considered as p<0.05 and <0.01, respectively.

Results

Search results and characteristics of studies

After duplicate removal, 1715 titles and abstracts were identified. 60 full texts were assessed for eligibility, of which 35 case–control studies were included in the final analysis (figure 1). 26 studies evaluated CIMT, 8 studies evaluated CAC scores and 1 study assessed ABI. 20 235 women were included in the studies, of whom 3376 had a history of PE. In the control groups without PE (n=16 859), 14 001 had a prior normotensive pregnancy, 390 had a non-proteinuric hypertensive pregnancy, 358 had normotensive proteinuria in pregnancy, 292 had gestational diabetes, 17 had chronic hypertension, 122 women were nulligravid, 1153 had a preterm delivery and 526 had a small-for-gestational-age infant. Details of demographics and study characteristics are shown in online supplemental eTable 1. Analyses were only performed in those with a history of PE vs those with normotensive pregnancies.

PRISMA flow chart. The study selection process was conducted according to the PRISMA guidelines. PRISMA, Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses.

Quality assessment

Using the NOS, 30 studies were assessed as good quality, 1 study15 had inadequate confounding control and 4 studies16,19 were of fair quality due to unclear exposure ascertainment (see onlinesupplemental eTable 2, data table 1).

CIMT and PE

Pooled analysis of 26 CIMT studies using the random effects model in 4403 women (1335 PE, 3068 controls) showed a standardised mean difference (SMD) of 0.63 (95% CI: 0.32, 0.93) between the 2 groups (figure 2). However, significant heterogeneity (97%) was observed using the I2 method.

Sensitivity analysis

Sensitivity analysis was conducted by analysing subgroups according to timing of CIMT measurement in relation to the index pregnancy. Overall follow-up time ranged between a mean or median of 18 weeks’ gestation to 35 years post partum. Studies were stratified into three subgroups: (1) during pregnancy, (2) 1 year post partum and (3) more than 1 year post partum (see onlinesupplemental data table 2).

In 6 studies (n=812), CIMT was performed during pregnancy (see online supplemental data, figure 1). Pooled analysis (314 PE, 498 controls) showed a significant SMD of 0.65 (95% CI: 0.33, 0.98) using the random effects model with substantial heterogeneity (I2=73%).

In 6 studies (n=351), CIMT was performed within 1 year after pregnancy (see online supplemental data, figure 2). Pooled analysis (192 PE, 159 controls) again demonstrated a significant SMD of 0.84 (95% CI: 0.20, 1.47) using the random effects model with substantial heterogeneity (I2=89%).

In 14 studies (n=3240), CIMT was performed more than 1 year after pregnancy (see online supplemental data, figure 3). Pooled analysis (829 PE, 2411 controls) again showed a significant SMD of 0.50 (95% CI: 0.01, 0.99) using the random effects model with substantial heterogeneity (I2=98%).

Further sensitivity analyses excluding three studies deemed at higher risk of bias according to the NOS were performed. One study15 did not adequately control for confounding factors and two studies16 17 had unclear exposure ascertainment. Analyses of the remaining 23 studies (see online supplemental data, figure 4) demonstrated a lower SMD of 0.46 (95% CI: 0.21, 0.71) using the random effects model with high heterogeneity (I2=82%).

Publication bias

Funnel plot (figure 3) of all 26 CIMT studies demonstrated significant asymmetry suggesting publication bias. This was corroborated by statistical significance on Egger’s regression analysis (p=0.03). Additional analysis was performed with the exclusion of four studies which contributed to asymmetry on the funnel plot.15 16 20 21 Egger’s regression analysis remained significant (p=0.03); however, trim-and-fill analysis adjusting for publication bias demonstrated a lower SMD (0.23), which remained significant (p=0.05) (see online supplemental data, figure 5). In the 14 studies which assessed CIMT more than 1 year after pregnancy, Egger’s regression analysis demonstrated statistical significance (p=0.02). Further analysis of this subgroup excluding one study15 due to asymmetry demonstrated statistical non-significance (p=0.33) on Egger’s regression. However, exclusion of this study15 from the pooled analysis demonstrated loss of significance (SMD 0.31 (95% CI: −0.03, 0.64)) with persistent heterogeneity (I2=83%) (see online supplemental data, figure 6).

Funnel plot of all studies assessing CIMT in women with and without a history of pre-eclampsia (PE). CIMT, carotid intima–media thickness.

CAC score and PE

Of the eight studies that explored CAC score, five explored the presence/absence of any atherosclerosis (CAC score >0) and three used ordinal regression models to determine if those who had PE had greater CAC scores. Pooled analysis in all 8 CAC studies (n=12 841 women; 1943 PE, 10 898 controls) demonstrated an OR of 1.57 (95% CI: 1.39, 1.77) using the common effects model (figure 4) with low heterogeneity (I2=10%). A random effects model was not conducted due to low between-study variance (τ2<0.0001). The funnel plot (figure 5) also showed an asymmetric distribution suggesting publication bias. Follow-up ranged between a mean or medium of 10–4.9 years post partum. Age less than or greater than 50 did not influence the CAC results (see online supplemental data, figures 7 and 8).

Sensitivity analysis

To account for two studies18 19 that were judged as high risk due to unclear exposure ascertainment, further sensitivity analyses excluding these studies were performed (see online supplemental data, figure 9). This demonstrated an OR of 1.63 (95% CI: 1.36, 1.94) using the random effects model with low heterogeneity (I2=11%).

ABI and PE

Only one study22, n=157 women (122 PE, 35 controls) assessed ABI 3 months post partum. ABI values were significantly lower in patients who had had PE (ABI 1.04 (IQR 0.9–1.26)) compared with the control group (ABI 1.16 (IQR 1–3)), p<0.001.

Discussion

Identifying subclinical atherosclerosis offers a window of opportunity to treat patients at increased risk of CAD. The aim of this study was to explore associations between timing of measurement and presence of subclinical atherosclerosis in women with a history of PE. CIMT is recognised as a surrogate marker for atherosclerosis and a strong predictor of future cardiovascular events.11 CAC scores are readily available and highly specific for coronary atherosclerosis.23 Guidelines recommend the addition of CAC scores to cardiovascular prediction tools to improve risk stratification in intermediate-risk patients.24

This systematic review included 35 studies comprising more than 20 000 women. Overall, a history of PE was associated with presence of subclinical atherosclerosis, as assessed by CIMT and CAC score. SMD in CIMT between women with and without a history of PE was significant. This difference was present during pregnancy, 1 year post partum and beyond. Similarly, pooled analysis of CAC scores 10–35 years post partum demonstrated that the odds of having subclinical atherosclerosis were higher in women with a history of PE. The odds were higher after exclusion of studies that were judged as high risk of bias. Notably, the odds were higher in both those above and below the age of 50 years (OR 1.61). CAC scoring is known to be a poor screening test in patients under 50 years of age.23 Current guidelines suggest reserving CAC scores for those over the age of 45 years due to lack of calcified atherosclerotic plaque in younger patients.13 Our findings suggest that accelerated atherosclerosis may result in better utility of CAC scoring in a younger population with a past history of PE.

Two studies performed CIMT in the same group of women both intrapartum and post partum. Yuan et al25 found that CIMT was substantially greater in women with a history of PE both intrapartum and 18 months postpartum. Akhter et al26 found that women who had PE had substantially higher intima thickness and intima/media ratio compared with controls. 1 year post partum, CIMT decreased in both PE and controls but remained substantially higher in the PE group. This suggests a transient increase in CIMT in the intrapartum period. Proposed mechanisms for this include shear stress from increased BP causing an exaggerated inflammatory response, leading to vascular wall oedema.16 Indeed, previous studies have demonstrated acute CIMT changes with increased BP in otherwise healthy individuals.27 Furthermore, Blaauw et al28 found that pregnancy itself was associated with increased CIMT. In their study, both normotensive and pre-eclamptic pregnancies were associated with a substantial increase in CIMT compared with nulliparous women. Our review demonstrates that these changes are also present postpartum in women with a history of PE. It is possible that vascular changes precede pregnancy in women predisposed to develop both PE and CVD. Future studies should explore CIMT prior to pregnancy, during pregnancy and subsequent long-term cardiovascular outcomes.

There has been some suggestion that women with a history of PE should be further risk-stratified according to PE severity and gestational age at onset. Early-onset PE has been associated with a higher risk of CVD mortality compared with late-onset PE.29 CVD risk has also been found to correlate with the severity of PE.29 Of the four studies that assessed their cohort according to gestational age at onset, two studies found no meaningful difference in CIMT between women with early-onset PE and late-onset PE at a mean follow-up time of 1 and 11 years, respectively.26 30 In contrast, Christensen et al31 found that early-onset PE was associated with higher odds of subclinical atherosclerosis at a mean follow-up period of 12 years. Stergiotou et al32 found that early-onset PE was associated with increased intrapartum CIMT and arterial stiffness, whereas late-onset PE was associated with substantially increased CIMT and less change in arterial stiffness. In studies (n=5) that examined PE severity, no meaningful difference in CIMT was found in three studies.20 26 33 Hauge et al34 found that those with severe PE tended to have a higher prevalence of coronary atherosclerosis and a CAC score >0. These conflicting results highlight the need for further large-scale studies to determine the utility of risk stratification.

Strengths of our study included a large sample size of more than 20 000 women from contemporary case control studies. Most studies were of good quality. However, four studies were deemed ‘fair’ quality and one study did not adequately control for confounding factors. Sensitivity analyses excluding these studies demonstrated a lower pooled estimate among CIMT studies and a higher OR among CAC studies. Another inherent limitation was publication and small study bias as demonstrated by funnel plot asymmetry. This was corroborated by statistical significance on Eggers’ regression analysis. Additional analysis excluding studies that contributed to asymmetry was performed. Asymmetry was not totally corrected. Furthermore, trim-and-fill analyses demonstrated less significant results. Another limitation of our study was the presence of confounders. While all studies controlled for age, less than half of the studies accounted for BMI (n=18) and none of the studies adequately adjusted for all relevant risk factors such as obstetric history and cardiovascular risk factors. Furthermore, meta-analysis of CIMT studies demonstrated substantial heterogeneity. Potential causes of heterogeneity include a wide range of follow-up durations, different study populations and research methodologies. Out of the 25 studies exploring CIMT, 9 studies were conducted in Europe, 6 studies in Scandinavia, 4 in the USA/Canada, 3 in the Middle East, 2 in Asia and 1 in South America. Four studies28 30 35 36 only included women with severe PE, whereas other studies either did not specify the severity of PE or included those with less severe PE. Finally, sensitivity analyses were conducted by analysing CIMT with respect to follow-up time. Follow-up periods were inconsistently reported across the included studies. Some studies reported ‘mean’, while others reported ‘median’ and ‘range’ of time postpartum. Moreover, it is possible that study populations overlapped. Akhter et al published three studies in 2013,26 201430 and 2019.21 Blaauw et al also published three studies—two in 200628 and one in 2014.35 Only one of their studies published in 200628 was included in this review to minimise potential for overlap.

Conclusions

Our review suggests that a history of PE is associated with increased subclinical atherosclerosis compared to those with previous normotensive pregnancies. These changes are present during pregnancy and persist long term. It is possible that these changes precede pregnancy. However, results should be interpreted with caution given significant publication bias and heterogeneity among the studies. Our review supports the importance of early follow-up in patients who have had PE and the need for further robust studies in this area.
