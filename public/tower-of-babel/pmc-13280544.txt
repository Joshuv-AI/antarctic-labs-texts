Advances in neuroimaging studies of thalamic abnormalities in children with attention deficit hyperactivity disorder
Authors: Jiaqi Zhou, Ruilin Jin, Teng Zhu, Jiayun Yu, Xiaolei Cen, Qiqiong Feng, Yinan Mo, Mengjie Zhang, Wanying Zheng, Yanchi Wang, Rui Huang, Hanlin Li, Chuang Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAttention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) is a prevalent neurodevelopmental disorder whose core pathological mechanism is deeply rooted in the dysfunction of the cortico-striato-thalamo-cortical circuit. This review summarizes recent advances in magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) studies investigating thalamic abnormalities in children with ADHD, highlighting multidimensional pathological changes within the thalamus. Structurally, children with ADHD exhibit delayed overall thalamic volume development and significant atrophy in specific subregions, such as the ventral anterior nucleus, mediodorsal nucleus, and pulvinar. Regarding white matter microstructure, projection pathways from the thalamus to regions such as the motor cortex and striatum are impaired. Both resting-state and task-fMRI consistently demonstrate weakened cortical connectivity between the thalamus and both the default mode and dorsal attention networks. Furthermore, current evidence suggests that mainstream pharmacological treatments, particularly methylphenidate and atomoxetine, effectively promote structural remodeling and the “normalization” of network functions in the aberrant thalamus. Additionally, multidimensional neuroimaging metrics of the thalamus demonstrate substantial potential as objective biomarkers for assessing genetic risk, classifying clinical subtypes, and predicting treatment outcomes. Ultimately, this review emphasizes that the thalamus is not merely a passive sensory relay station, but an active gatekeeper regulating cognition and behavior. Its structural and functional abnormalities constitute a central hub in the pathogenesis of ADHD. Supported by future large-scale machine learning and longitudinal studies, the thalamus holds promise as a crucial biomarker for the precise diagnosis and personalized targeted intervention of ADHD.

Introduction

Attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) is a prevalent childhood neurodevelopmental disorder, with an estimated global prevalence ranging from 5.9 to 7.1% among children and adolescents (Willcutt, 2012; Wang et al., 2017). Clinically, based on the predominance of core symptoms, the DSM-5 classifies ADHD into distinct presentations: the predominantly inattentive presentation (ADHD-I), characterized primarily by distractibility and difficulty sustaining attention; the predominantly hyperactive/impulsive presentation (ADHD-HI), marked by excessive motor activity and impaired behavioral inhibition; and the combined presentation (ADHD-C), which encompasses both symptom domains. In investigating the pathogenesis of these symptoms, dysfunction within the cortico-striato-thalamo-cortical (CSTC) circuit has been widely acknowledged as the core pathophysiological foundation (Zhu et al., 2018). However, while an extensive body of prior neuroimaging research has predominantly focused on the cerebral cortex and basal ganglia, the critical nodal role of the thalamus within the pathogenic network of ADHD remains inadequately elucidated.

The thalamus is not merely a passive information relay station; rather, it functions as an active information gatekeeper within the CSTC circuit, regulating higher cognitive, emotional, and motor processes (Wilke et al., 2010; Wolff and Vann, 2019). Current physiological models and non-human primate research indicate that normal thalamic function serves as the physiological foundation for maintaining attention and behavioral control (Lanciego et al., 2012; Huang and Barber, 2021; Córdoba-Claros et al., 2025). Consequently, structural or functional abnormalities within the thalamus theoretically map directly onto the core clinical symptoms of ADHD.

First, mechanistically, this proposed link is supported by a growing body of evidence highlighting the role of specific thalamic subregions in attentional regulation. Specifically, the pulvinar and lateral geniculate body are deeply involved in visual processing and sensory integration (Li et al., 2012; Vann and Nelson, 2015). Research indicates that the pulvinar integrates environmental salience signals from the superior colliculus and precisely projects them to the parietal and prefrontal cortices (Huang and Barber, 2021; Córdoba-Claros et al., 2025), which is critical for filtering out irrelevant stimuli and maintaining sustained attention (Li et al., 2012). Theoretically, impairment of this bottom-up sensory gating function allows unfiltered sensory information to flood the cortex, which explains why individuals with ADHD are highly susceptible to visual distraction and auditory overload.

Second, regarding hyperactivity, the thalamus serves as a critical relay for transmitting inhibitory signals from the basal ganglia to the cortex. The ventral anterior nucleus (VA) and ventral lateral nucleus (VL) nuclei receive potent inhibitory inputs from the internal segment of the globus pallidus and the substantia nigra pars reticulata, subsequently projecting the integrated signals to the primary motor cortex and the supplementary motor area (Lanciego et al., 2012; Xia et al., 2012). This top-down prefrontal-basal ganglia-thalamic network exerts a strict inhibitory gating effect on motor output. Consequently, disruption of this pathway physiologically impairs the inhibition of prepotent motor responses, thereby providing a neuroanatomical basis for hyperactive symptoms.

Finally, concerning impulse control and executive function, the medial and anterior thalamic nuclei govern the transmission of higher-order information. These nuclei integrate reward and memory signals from the mesolimbic system and project them to the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC)—a pathway critical for working memory, higher-order cognition, and delayed gratification (Tsanov et al., 2011; Vann and Nelson, 2015; Mathiasen et al., 2017; Parnaudeau et al., 2018; Sweeney-Reed et al., 2021). Consequently, aberrations within thalamo-prefrontal projection mechanisms are posited as the theoretical root underlying the heightened impulsivity and executive dysfunction observed in ADHD. In light of the aforementioned theoretical framework, identifying specific structural and functional abnormalities of the thalamus in children with ADHD plays an indispensable role in elucidating its pathological mechanisms.

Central nervous system stimulants, predominantly methylphenidate, and non-stimulant medications, such as atomoxetine, currently constitute the frontline pharmacological treatments for ADHD. Beyond ameliorating overt clinical symptoms via the modulation of dopaminergic or noradrenergic systems, these medications also macroscopically regulate and promote the normalization of thalamic structure and function through neuroplastic mechanisms (Ivanov et al., 2010; Rubia et al., 2011a; Schweren et al., 2013).

Consequently, this review systematically synthesizes recent advancements in magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) research, focusing on the delayed structural development, microstructural impairments of white matter tracts, and functional network dysregulation of the thalamus in children with ADHD. Furthermore, it explores the potential role of the thalamus in pharmacological response, efficacy evaluation, and overall prognosis. Ultimately, this review seeks to provide a theoretical foundation for establishing the thalamus as a viable biomarker for ADHD.

Search method

This study aims to systematically review the progress of neuroimaging research on thalamic abnormalities in children with ADHD. The literature search was finalized in August 2025. We strictly restricted the publication timeframe of the included literature to the period from January 2010 to August 2025. This specific timeframe was chosen because, prior to 2010, neuroimaging studies investigating thalamic structures in ADHD were relatively sparse and primarily focused on traditional regions of interest, such as the cortex and basal ganglia. The first high-resolution structural MRI study specifically examining thalamic morphological characteristics in adolescents with ADHD was not published until 2010, after which high-quality studies in this area gradually began to emerge. Therefore, this temporal restriction effectively ensures the consistency of data quality and imaging methodologies across the included studies. The search covered multiple English and Chinese databases, including PubMed, Web of Science, and CNKI. Boolean operators and precise terminology were employed to ensure comprehensive and accurate results. Core search terms such as “Attention Deficit Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD),” “thalamus,” “MRI,” “neuroimaging,” and “children” were used. Boolean operators AND and OR were employed to construct the search strategy, for example: “ADHD” AND “thalamus” AND (“MRI” OR “neuroimaging”) AND “children,” ensuring the inclusion of literature related to ADHD, the thalamus, neuroimaging methods, and pediatric populations. Additionally, synonyms and variant terms, such as “Thalamic Abnormalities” and “Functional MRI,” were used to increase the flexibility and comprehensiveness of the search. Inclusion criteria focused on original research articles published in peer-reviewed journals, investigating ADHD in children using neuroimaging techniques to assess thalamic structure or function. To ensure the review focused on primary data, reviews and meta-analyses were excluded, as were studies solely involving adult ADHD. These exclusion criteria ensured the scientific rigor, timeliness, and relevance of the review. The selection process followed the PRISMA flowchart, and the detailed content can be seen in Fig. 1. Ultimately, a total of 64 studies were included in the review. Data extraction from all included studies focused on research methods, neuroimaging techniques, thalamic abnormalities observed, sample sizes, and findings. The selected studies provide a comprehensive perspective on neuroimaging methods and the neurobiological mechanisms of thalamic dysfunction in children with ADHD, reflecting the latest research trends and developments in this field over the past 15 years.

Developmental abnormalities in thalamic volume

The thalamus is primarily composed of gray matter, white matter, and other tissue components. Gray matter, consisting of aggregated neuron cell bodies, serves as the core structural foundation for the thalamus’s diverse physiological and neurological functions. In current research, the anatomical segmentation and volumetric measurement of the thalamus primarily utilize automated tools, such as FreeSurfer (Qiu et al., 2011; Batty et al., 2015), FSL (Xia et al., 2012), and volBrain (Wang et al., 2018). Based on these tools, researchers typically focus on two key metrics. First, total thalamic volume, derived by extracting the entire thalamus as a region of interest using automated segmentation based on anatomical landmarks. This metric reflects a comprehensive anatomical structure encompassing both gray matter nuclei and internal white matter fiber bundles (Batty et al., 2015; Wang et al., 2018). Second, thalamic gray matter volume (GMV), which is generally derived from voxel-based morphometry analysis. This approach involves segmenting whole-brain structural images into gray matter, white matter, and cerebrospinal fluid probability maps, followed by calculating the sum of gray matter voxels within the thalamic region using a gray matter mask (Fu et al., 2021). Changes in GMV are more likely to reflect variations in neuron cell body density or synaptic density. Overall, abnormalities in total thalamic volume suggest alterations in the general anatomical development of the thalamic nuclei, whereas changes in thalamic GMV are more sensitive to microstructural plastic adjustments at the neuronal level.

Current findings regarding total thalamic volume in children with ADHD remain inconsistent. Some studies report no significant differences in thalamic volume between children with ADHD and typically developing controls (Ivanov et al., 2010; Wang et al., 2021), whereas others indicate that thalamic volume in the ADHD group is significantly smaller (Xia et al., 2012; Batty et al., 2015; Rosch et al., 2018; Wang et al., 2018).

This discrepancy largely stems from the delayed trajectory of thalamic development in children with ADHD. In typically developing children, thalamic volume generally peaks around 10–11 years of age, whereas in children with ADHD, this peak is significantly delayed until approximately 13 years of age (Wang et al., 2021). Consequently, in strictly age-matched cross-sectional studies conducted before reaching this developmental peak, children with ADHD frequently exhibit smaller thalamic volumes (Batty et al., 2015; Wang et al., 2018). Conversely, in studies reporting no significant differences (Ivanov et al., 2010), the mean age of the ADHD group was 12.6 years compared to 11.1 years for the control group. Because both groups were approaching their respective peak developmental ages at the time of assessment, inter-group differences were negligible. Furthermore, sex imbalance serves as a critical confounding factor that cannot be overlooked. Research indicates that school-aged boys with ADHD exhibit significant reductions in bilateral thalamic volume, a phenomenon not observed in girls with the disorder (Wang et al., 2018). Given the higher prevalence of ADHD in males, some studies failed to adequately account for sex differences when matching control groups. This oversight often results in an overrepresentation of males in the ADHD cohort, while the control group maintains a near 1:1 sex ratio. Such imbalanced sampling may skew research outcomes, thereby amplifying the bias toward concluding that thalamic volume is consistently reduced in ADHD. White matter is primarily composed of nerve fibers that transmit information between brain regions. Its integrity is typically assessed via diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) metrics, including fractional anisotropy (FA), mean diffusivity (MD), and axial diffusivity (AD). Specifically, FA reflects the degree of myelination, axonal density, and directional consistency of fibers; MD represents the overall level of water diffusion, where elevations often suggest reduced tissue density or increased extracellular space; and AD primarily reflects the developmental status and integrity of the axons themselves (Alexander et al., 2007).

Research has demonstrated a significant decrease in the nodal centrality of the thalamus as an information hub in children with ADHD (Wu et al., 2024). However, microstructural indices, such as FA, MD, and AD, within the thalamus itself do not show widespread abnormalities (Qiu et al., 2011; Fall et al., 2015; Ercan et al., 2016; Bouziane et al., 2018). This suggests that the white matter pathology in ADHD is not concentrated within the internal thalamic structure but rather manifests in the specific projection pathways connecting the thalamus to external regions.

In children with ADHD, both the volume and FA values of white matter tracts connecting the bilateral thalamus to the striatum, hippocampus, motor cortex, and prefrontal cortex are significantly reduced (Rubia et al., 2011b; Xia et al., 2012). These declines signify insufficient myelination or loosely organized fiber bundles, directly resulting in inefficient information transmission. Clinically, impaired white matter connectivity between the thalamus and the motor cortex may lead to dysfunctions in motor integration, execution, and regulation, thereby contributing to inhibitory deficits and hyperactive behavior. Furthermore, the connectivity strength between the thalamus and the right precentral gyrus is negatively correlated with the severity of ADHD symptoms (Wu et al., 2020).

Notably, certain pathways exhibit anomalous increases in microstructural metrics. The ATR in children with ADHD shows atypical elevations in FA, MD, and AD (Xia et al., 2012; Lawrence et al., 2013; Svatkova et al., 2016). This phenomenon may reflect aberrant axonal pruning during early development or a localized compensatory mechanism activated in response to chronic executive dysfunction. These microstructural alterations directly interfere with executive control networks, correlating with lower scores on Stroop tasks and longer reaction times with higher variability during Flanker tasks (Fall et al., 2015; Svatkova et al., 2016).

It should be noted that, similar to morphological studies of the thalamus, current research on thalamic white matter microstructure is also generally limited by sex imbalances within study samples. Relevant study cohorts often exclusively include male patients (Qiu et al., 2011; Bouziane et al., 2018). Other studies have included female patients, but their representation remains low (Fall et al., 2015; Ercan et al., 2016; Svatkova et al., 2016; Wu et al., 2022). In such cases, relying solely on statistical covariate control is insufficient to isolate the independent influence of sex on microstructural abnormalities within the thalamocortical projection pathways (Wu et al., 2022). Consequently, current conclusions regarding white matter pathological alterations in ADHD may predominantly reflect the neuroanatomical characteristics of male child.

Resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging (rs-fMRI) studies have further corroborated the functional impairment of the thalamus as a global information hub. These aberrant connectivity patterns can be categorized into four core levels.

Large-scale network connectivity is undergoing widespread attenuation. There is a generalized inefficiency in information transmission between the thalamus and higher-order cortical networks. Research indicates that patients with ADHD exhibit significantly diminished thalamocortical connectivity within the default mode network, the cingulo-opercular network, the dorsal attention network (DAN), and the somatomotor network (Qiu et al., 2011; Hua et al., 2021; Hong, 2023). This pervasive weakening of large-scale network connectivity has been found to negatively correlate with the severity of core ADHD symptoms (Hua et al., 2021; Hong, 2023), highlighting the pivotal role of thalamocortical connectivity deficits in the pathogenesis of ADHD.

Thalamic interaction is impaired within emotional and social cognitive networks. Children with ADHD exhibit a significant reduction in dynamic functional connectivity (dFC) between the thalamus and the dorsal anterior insula (Fateh et al., 2022). Because the insulo-thalamic pathway is pivotal for social information processing, integration, and the formation of social working memory (Meyer and Lieberman, 2012), this attenuated connectivity likely contributes to the social dysfunction and working memory deficits observed in these patients. Furthermore, diminished functional connectivity between the thalamus and the right basolateral amygdala further suggests the involvement of emotional processing networks in the pathophysiology of the disorder (Fall et al., 2015).

A dissociation between auditory processing and attentional regulation was observed. The left superior temporal gyrus (STG), a critical node for processing auditory information, forms an integral component of the CSTC circuit through its connectivity with the thalamus (Zhou et al., 2019). Children with ADHD exhibit not only significantly diminished functional connectivity between the thalamus and the left STG (Zhou et al., 2019), but also abnormal auditory brainstem responses (ABR) within the thalamic region (Claesdotter-Hybbinette et al., 2015). This functional disconnection between the auditory cortex and the thalamic hub likely leads to the omission of critical auditory cues, manifesting as comprehension difficulties and a susceptibility to information overload. Ultimately, these impairments precipitate auditory attentional deficits and executive dysfunction.

A typical hyperconnectivity exists within the thalamo-striatal circuit. In contrast to the pervasive connectivity deficits described above, an anomalous pattern of enhancement is observed between the thalamus and the basal ganglia. Specifically, resting-state functional connectivity between the prefrontal–thalamic pathway and the putamen is significantly elevated in patients with ADHD compared to healthy controls (Mills et al., 2012). Notably, this hyperconnectivity correlates negatively with spatial working memory performance (Mills et al., 2012). While the thalamus typically modulates cognitive control and behavioral inhibition through its interactions with the basal ganglia, such atypical connectivity may underlie the dysregulation of cognitive control (Mills et al., 2012). This likely represents a compensatory mechanism emerging in response to the functional inefficiency of the CSTC circuit.

It is noteworthy that existing research on rs-fMRI similarly suffers from sex bias in study samples. Several studies have relied exclusively on male participants (Qiu et al., 2011; Zhou et al., 2019; Fateh et al., 2022; Kaiser et al., 2022). The few studies that have included female children with ADHD also failed to analyze the factor of gender (Fall et al., 2015). Such sampling designs imply that the aforementioned conclusions regarding widespread thalamic network dysregulation may primarily reflect the pathological characteristics of male child.

Results from task-based fMRI studies consistently demonstrate that, compared to healthy controls, children with ADHD exhibit significantly reduced thalamic activation during sustained attention tasks (Christakou et al., 2013; Kowalczyk et al., 2019), interference inhibition tasks (Rubia et al., 2011a, 2011b), and the cued reaction time paradigm (Clerkin et al., 2013). Specifically during visual attention tasks, children with ADHD show significantly diminished activation in the bilateral pulvinar of the thalamus (Li et al., 2012). The activation intensity of the left pulvinar negatively correlates with the DSM-IV Inattention Index (Li et al., 2012). Furthermore, functional connectivity between the pulvinar and the right prefrontal cortex is markedly attenuated, shifting instead toward an anomalously enhanced local connectivity with the bilateral occipital lobes (Li et al., 2012).

Moreover, children with ADHD display pronounced deficits within the reward circuitry. Thalamic neural activity is notably blunted, particularly during the reward anticipation phase and under conditions of low monetary reward (Mizuno et al., 2013; Chen et al., 2022). Paradoxically, they exhibit stronger thalamic activation in response to non-reward cues than to reward cues (Metin et al., 2018). This aberrant reward processing mechanism directly undermines the patients’ capacity for delayed gratification.

Similarly, fMRI studies are frequently limited by a disproportionately high representation of male samples (Rubia et al., 2011a, 2011b; Christakou et al., 2013; Mizuno et al., 2013; Kowalczyk et al., 2019). This implies that the current consensus—specifically, that children with ADHD exhibit significantly reduced thalamic activation during cognitive tasks—may primarily reflect the neuropathological characteristics of male patients. Although a few studies utilizing sex-balanced samples observed no significant sex differences during reward anticipation tasks (Li et al., 2012), the generalizability of existing conclusions warrants cautious evaluation.

The effects of ADHD medications on the thalamus

Although children with ADHD exhibit a significant delay in the age of peak thalamic development, this abnormal developmental trajectory is reversible. Research indicates that pharmacological interventions can effectively promote the normalization of both the structure and function of the thalamus.

Structurally, individuals with ADHD typically present with reduced total thalamic volume and smaller specific subregions. However, long-term medication use can reverse this trend. Studies have demonstrated that the long-term administration of methylphenidate can restore thalamic volume to near-normal levels (Schweren et al., 2013). Atomoxetine exerts a similar effect: in adolescents with ADHD, the volume of the thalamic pulvinar is notably smaller and positively correlates with the severity of hyperactive symptoms (Ivanov et al., 2010). Following atomoxetine treatment, a relative increase in the volume of this region is observed (Ivanov et al., 2010). Furthermore, both medications facilitate the structural recovery of the thalamus and significantly reduce the abnormal volumetric asymmetry often seen in patients with ADHD (Kowalczyk et al., 2019; Kim et al., 2025).

At the level of brain functional connectivity and networks, the modulatory effects of pharmacological interventions are equally substantial. Methylphenidate can upregulate task-specific thalamic hypoactivation in patients with ADHD, eliminating significant activation differences compared to healthy controls and thereby achieving functional normalization of the brain (Rubia et al., 2011b, 2011a; Mizuno et al., 2013). During working memory tasks, a single therapeutic dose of methylphenidate has also been shown to enhance connectivity between the right middle frontal gyrus and the right thalamus (Kowalczyk et al., 2023). However, rs-fMRI studies have yielded somewhat heterogeneous results: the acute administration of a single dose of methylphenidate has been observed to decrease regional cerebral blood flow in the thalamus of pediatric patients and significantly attenuate network metrics such as connection strength (CS), eigenvector centrality (EC), and betweenness centrality (BC) (Schrantee et al., 2017; Kaiser et al., 2022). The decline in these indicators may be related to the study sample, such as the inclusion of treatment-naive participants, which rules out the long-term adaptation mechanism of the dopamine system, and may also be associated with individual differences in brain development. This suggests that the mechanisms by which different medications affect local and global brain network metrics may vary (Kaiser et al., 2022).

The two medications have differences in their therapeutic profiles. For instance, methylphenidate uniquely upregulates ventral frontotemporal regions and improves sustained attention deficits (Kowalczyk et al., 2019). Nevertheless, both effectively alleviate core ADHD symptoms by upregulating hypoactive regions and normalizing brain function.

Importantly, this recovery does not contradict the aforementioned developmental delay. Rather, its underlying biological mechanism is likely rooted in enhanced neuroplasticity. By modulating dopamine and norepinephrine levels, these medications improve signal transduction efficiency within the CSTC circuit (Mallas et al., 2025). Through neuroplastic mechanisms, this sustained functional improvement may promote the normalization of synaptic pruning or dendritic arborization (Contreras et al., 2022), manifesting macroscopically as volumetric normalization. The functional recovery of the thalamus is not an isolated event. It involves the dynamic reorganization of network connectivity between the thalamus and other brain regions, such as the prefrontal cortex and the basal ganglia. Future research should focus on the dynamic changes in network connectivity specific to thalamic subregions and investigate how different therapeutic interventions alleviate the core symptoms of ADHD by modulating these specific neural circuits.

Clinical potential of thalamic as biomarkers in ADHD

Beyond serving as a core neuropathological structure in ADHD, the morphological and functional indices of the thalamus demonstrate significant potential as biomarkers for genetic risk, subtype prediction, treatment response, and prognostic assessment. A large-scale study (n = 606) based on the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development (ABCD) cohort revealed that children with familial ADHD exhibit significantly larger right thalamic gray matter volume compared to those with non-familial ADHD (Baboli et al., 2022). This independent, large-sample analysis provides robust evidence that structural abnormalities in the thalamus have a strong genetic origin. Preliminary progress has also been made regarding subtype characterization and clinical interventions. A research found that patients with the predominantly inattentive subtype of ADHD show significantly higher FA in the bilateral ATR (Svatkova et al., 2016). Regarding treatment response, a study noted that among adolescents receiving methylphenidate, responders had significantly smaller right thalamic volumes than non-responders (Kim et al., 2022). Furthermore, evidence from prospective cohorts suggests that adults with remitted ADHD exhibit stronger task-based functional connectivity between the right thalamus and the prefrontal cortex (Clerkin et al., 2013).

However, with the exception of the ABCD cohort, most aforementioned studies are limited by small sample sizes. Future research should leverage large-scale clinical cohorts and incorporate machine learning algorithms to conduct rigorous independent out-of-sample validation. Such efforts are essential to definitively establish the reliability of these neuroimaging indices for precision diagnosis and personalized treatment in clinical settings.

Discussion

The role of the thalamus in the pathogenesis of ADHD

Rather than serving merely as a passive information relay, the thalamus functions as an active gatekeeper within the CSTC circuit, modulating cognition and behavior. The thalamus serves as a central hub of the CSTC circuitry, integrating sensory inputs, modulating cortical excitability, and coordinating cognitive and behavioral responses (Sherman, 2012; Svatkova et al., 2016). Current neuroimaging evidence demonstrates a significantly delayed developmental trajectory of the thalamus in children with ADHD (Wang et al., 2021). These developmental abnormalities, along with concomitant structural and functional impairments, directly map onto the core clinical symptoms of ADHD via distinct neural circuits.

Regarding attention deficits, the thalamus exhibits severe impairments in filtering and integrating multimodal sensory information. In the visual domain, significant atrophy of the pulvinar and its abnormal connectivity with the prefrontal and parietal cortices compromise the efficiency of visual information transmission (Li et al., 2012; Xia et al., 2012). Consequently, this leads to dispersed visual attention, rendering children highly susceptible to visual distractions in environments such as classrooms. In the auditory domain, abnormal ABR are observed alongside impaired connectivity between the thalamus and auditory processing regions (Claesdotter-Hybbinette et al., 2015), including the left STG (Zhou et al., 2019), prefrontal cortex, and hippocampus (Xia et al., 2012). These deficits elucidate why affected children frequently experience auditory information overload and comprehension difficulties. Furthermore, weakened thalamocortical connections within the DAN lead to insufficient thalamic activation during sustained attention tasks (Hong, 2023). Particularly during visual tasks (Li et al., 2012), this impairment prevents children with ADHD from effectively filtering out irrelevant stimuli, ultimately resulting in inattentiveness and mind-wandering.

Impulsive behavior stems from a dual dysregulation of inhibitory control and reward processing. Impaired white matter connectivity between the thalamus and the prefrontal cortex compromises the top-down inhibitory control network (Cubillo et al., 2010), rendering affected children unable to suppress prepotent responses during interference tasks. Concurrently, thalamic hypoactivation during the reward anticipation phase, coupled with an exaggerated response to non-reward cues (Chen et al., 2022), disrupts typical reward processing mechanisms, thereby fundamentally undermining the patients’ capacity for delayed gratification.

Regarding hyperactive symptoms, the core pathological mechanism stems from dysregulation and impaired integration within the thalamo-motor cortical circuit. In children with ADHD, significant atrophy of the VA nucleus (Ivanov et al., 2010), coupled with compromised white matter tracts projecting from the thalamus to the motor cortex (Xia et al., 2012), severely impairs the “inhibitory gating” function of the prefrontal-basal ganglia network on motor output. This disruption in the top-down integration and regulation of motor signals provides a physiological explanation for the patients’ inability to suppress prepotent responses, which directly manifests behaviorally as uncontrollable hyperactivity and restlessness

In terms of executive dysfunction, children with ADHD exhibit significant structural atrophy in the VA and MD (Xia et al., 2012), as well as abnormalities in the microstructure of the ATR (Svatkova et al., 2016). These alterations impede the effective transmission of higher-order cognitive information to the DLPFC. Furthermore, an atypical hyperconnectivity between the thalamus and the putamen is observed, which negatively correlates with working memory performance (Mills et al., 2012). Additionally, impaired connectivity within the insula-thalamic pathway disrupts the processing and integration of social information, as well as the formation of social working memory (Fateh et al., 2022). This results in working memory deficits and reduced cognitive flexibility.

Notably, mainstream pharmacological interventions not only ameliorate overt clinical symptoms but also promote the restoration of thalamic volume (Ivanov et al., 2010), upregulate hypoactive regions (Rubia et al., 2011a; Kowalczyk et al., 2019), and facilitate the normalization of thalamocortical connectivity (Kowalczyk et al., 2023). This evidence of neuroplasticity provides compelling converse validation, strongly reinforcing the central role of thalamic structural and functional abnormalities in the pathophysiology of ADHD.

An integrative analysis of the specific abnormalities of thalamic subregions

Although imaging evidence regarding the structural and functional heterogeneity of thalamic subregions in ADHD is gradually emerging, there remains a lack of systematic and integrated research targeting individual nuclei. Current literature preliminarily reveals specific abnormalities in the following key subregions.

First, the VA serves as a critical node for inhibitory gating within the prefrontal-basal ganglia network, governing higher-order motor planning and behavioral inhibition (McFarland and Haber, 2002). Neuroimaging studies reveal significant volumetric atrophy of the VA in patients with ADHD (Xia et al., 2012). This reduction may attenuate its inhibitory regulation over the motor cortex, thereby contributing to behavioral inhibition deficits and hyperactivity. Concurrently, significant volume reduction is also observed in the MD, which forms dense reciprocal circuits with the DLPFC and orbitofrontal cortices to mediate emotional regulation and cognitive control (Kuramoto et al., 2017). This dual atrophy likely severely compromises the integrity of the prefrontal-thalamo-striatal circuit.

At the neural circuit level, abnormalities are also evident in the prefrontal-thalamic tract, which primarily consists of axonal bundles from the VA and MD nuclei (Phillips et al., 2021). Rs-fMRI studies have revealed that, compared to healthy controls, children with ADHD exhibit abnormally elevated functional connectivity between this tract and the putamen (Mills et al., 2012). Furthermore, this increased connectivity is negatively correlated with spatial working memory performance (Mills et al., 2012). Given the structural atrophy of the VA and MD, this functional “hyperconnectivity” may represent a compensatory neuroplastic response aimed at mitigating the inefficiency of the prefrontal-thalamo-striatal circuit.

Secondly, the pulvinar nucleus plays a central role in integrating the visual-parieto-frontal network, multisensory integration, and attentional reorienting (Huang and Barber, 2021). Research indicates that patients with ADHD not only exhibit significant volumetric reduction of the pulvinar but also demonstrate hypoactivation in this region during visual attention tasks (Li et al., 2012; Xia et al., 2012). This hypoactive state is significantly negatively correlated with clinical scores for inattention and hyperactivity/impulsivity, and is accompanied by aberrant functional connectivity between the pulvinar and the prefrontal and parietal cortices (Li et al., 2012). Notably, pulvinar volume in these patients can normalize following atomoxetine treatment (Ivanov et al., 2010), suggesting that structural alterations in this region could serve as an objective neuroimaging biomarker for evaluating pharmacological efficacy.

In summary, pathological abnormalities in the pulvinar and other thalamic subregions collectively impair the sustainability of visual attention and behavioral control in individuals with ADHD. Beyond medication, cognitive training targeting visual attention offers a promising way to support functional recovery in the brain (Janmohammadi et al., 2020).

Deficiencies and prospects

While neuroimaging has revealed significant thalamic abnormalities in pediatric ADHD, research is currently limited by small sample sizes and a lack of cross-cultural validation. Existing research is largely constrained by severe sex imbalances in study samples and the statistical simplification of sex as a variable, suggesting that current findings on thalamic abnormalities may primarily reflect male-specific pathological features. Future studies should utilize large-scale, sex-matched prospective cohorts and treat sex as a core variable to elucidate sex-specific differences and their underlying neural mechanisms in ADHD. Future studies should employ large-scale clinical cohorts and machine learning for independent validation to establish reliable neuroimaging biomarkers. Integrating multimodal imaging and longitudinal designs is essential to clarify the functional connectivity between thalamic subregions and other brain networks, ultimately determining how therapeutic interventions modulate these circuits to improve ADHD symptoms.
