The role of indocyanine green in the intraoperative navigation of gastric cancer surgery: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Nassuf Mourdi, Yongke Wu, Yuanhao Su, Yiyuan Zhao, Xin Jin, Yunhao Li, Zhidong Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveIndocyanine green (ICG) fluorescence imaging is increasingly being recognized as promising technique for guiding surgical procedures in gastric cancer treatment. Nevertheless, current meta-analyses have not yet fully explored its impact on lymph node (LN) dissection or evaluated its effectiveness across open, laparoscopic, and robotic surgical techniques. This study aimed to investigate the potential of ICG in enhancing LN retrieval and optimizing surgical outcomes.MethodsWe conducted a systematic search of PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, and the Cochrane Library for studies published from January 2010 to December 2023 that compared ICG-guided versus non-ICG gastric cancer surgery. Eligible studies included both randomized and non-randomized designs reporting LN yield and operative outcomes. Data were pooled using random-effects models. Mean differences (MD) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated for continuous outcomes, and heterogeneity was assessed via the I² statistic. Subgroup analysis was performed by surgical modality, and publication bias was evaluated using funnel plots and Egger’s test.ResultsEleven studies (n = 2,100 patients) were included. ICG guidance significantly increased LN harvest compared to standard surgery (pooled MD = + 6 LNs, 95% CI: +4.4 to + 7.4; p < 0.001), with moderate heterogeneity (I² = 31%) and no significant publication bias. Subgroup analysis showed the greatest benefit with robotic gastrectomy, followed by laparoscopic approaches.ConclusionOur meta-analysis suggests that ICG fluorescence imaging is associated with improved LN dissection in gastric cancer surgery in terms of increased yield, and this was not associated with increased surgical risk in the studied outcomes. It appeared especially beneficial in minimally invasive procedures, particularly robotics, based on limited data, and offers a safe, effective adjunct for enhancing surgical precision across approaches.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-025-15306-2.Keywords: Gastric cancer, Indocyanine green (ICG), Medical imaging, Image-Guided surgery, Lymph nodes (LN)

Introduction

Gastric cancer (GC) remains a common and deadly malignancy worldwide, ranking among the leading causes of cancer-related mortality. In 2020, there were over one million new cases of GC globally, with the highest incidence rates in East Asia [1–3]. Despite advancements in treatment, prognosis is largely determined by the disease’s extent at diagnosis and the thoroughness of surgical resection. Lymphatic spread is common even in seemingly localized GC, making accurate lymph node (LN) assessment essential for staging and predicting survival. In fact, the status and quantity of metastatic LNs are among the strongest prognostic factors for GC.

Maximizing lymph node retrieval is critical to ensure accurate pathological staging. International guidelines and Japanese standards typically call for a minimum of 15–16 nodes, and multiple studies associate higher nodal counts with better long-term survival, likely by minimizing residual disease and enhancing understanding. For instance, it has been reported that patients with more than 25 LNs examined had better long-term outcomes than those with fewer lymph nodes. Nevertheless [4, 5], performing an extensive lymphadenectomy can be technically challenging and is associated with increased operative time and complexity, especially in minimally invasive surgery. According to the study, surgeons have sought adjuncts to enhance the precision and efficiency of LN dissection [6].

The research [7] shows that indocyanine green (ICG) fluorescence imaging has emerged as a valuable tool for intraoperative navigation in GC surgery [8, 9]. ICG is a near-infrared fluorophore that, after injection, travels along lymphatic channels. Specialized imaging systems detect its signal, allowing surgeons to delineate lymphatic drainage instantaneous [10]. Using fluorescence allows surgeons to detect additional lymph nodes, especially smaller or less obvious ones that might not be visible with conventional lighting. Additionally, Real-time mapping of lymphatic flow with ICG also, reveal unusual drainage routes or skipped metastatic spread, enabling adjustments in the dissection strategy.

In addition to lymphatic mapping, ICG has various intraoperative applications in GC surgery. According to the article [11, 12], Administering ICG intravenously enables assessment of tissue blood flow, helping to evaluate the vascular supply to gastric conduit or anastomosis and possibly lowering the risk of leaks. Moreover, ICG can be injected endoscopically around the tumor preoperatively to tattoo the lesion and highlight its margins during laparoscopic resection [13]. Research [14] suggests that these applications underline ICG’s versatility as a navigation adjunct in surgery. However, this study focuses explicitly on ICG’s role in lymphadenectomy.

While earlier pooled analyses have examined the role of ICG fluorescence during minimally invasive gastric resections, few have addressed differences among surgical approaches. Most published studies have concentrated on laparoscopic surgery. It remains uncertain whether open surgeries benefit equally from ICG or if the advantages are more pronounced in laparoscopy and robotic surgery, where visual cues are crucial. Robotic systems, with their stable near-infrared cameras and magnified 3D view, may enhance the utility of ICG fluorescence. The study [15] indicates that, to date, no meta-analysis has thoroughly compared ICG’s impact in open, laparoscopic, and robotic gastrectomy [16]. This represents a knowledge gap, as surgeons could tailor ICG to the modality that offers the greatest benefit.

Therefore, we conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis of studies on ICG-guided LN dissection in gastric cancer, including both minimally invasive (laparoscopic and robotic) and open surgeries. Our aims were: (1)To quantify the effect of ICG on LN yield and operative outcomes in gastric cancer surgery. (2) To determine whether the efficacy of ICG varies by surgical approach. (3) To evaluate the safety of ICG fluorescence navigation. Ultimately, by synthesizing all available evidence, we aim to clarify the role of ICG in gastric cancer surgery and guide its optimal clinical use. We hypothesize that ICG guidance increases LN retrieval across all approaches, with the most significant relative gains in minimally invasive (particularly robotic) surgery due to improved visualization. The results of this analysis will inform surgeons whether adopting ICG can meaningfully enhance oncologic quality (e.g., adequate LN harvest) without introducing risk and in which operative settings it is most beneficial.

Methods

Literature search and selection

This review adhered to PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) guidelines. A thorough literature search was conducted using PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, and the Cochrane Library to identify studies published from January 2010 to December 2023 on ICG fluorescence-guided surgery in gastric cancer. We conducted a comprehensive literature search of PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, and the Cochrane Library for studies published from January 2010 to December 2023. The search strategy for each database is provided in Supplementary Material 1. Additionally, we screened references of included studies and performed forward citation tracking to identify additional relevant studies.

The search terms included combinations of keywords such as “indocyanine green,” “ICG,” “fluorescence,” “near-infrared,” “gastric cancer,” “gastrectomy,” “lymph node,” “lymphadenectomy,” and “navigation.” The search was limited to human studies and publications in English. Conference abstracts and trial registries were also reviewed for additional data.

The initial search yielded 550 records. After removing 100 duplicates, 450 unique articles were screened; 400 were excluded based on title and abstract (not meeting inclusion criteria, irrelevant outcomes, or not related to gastric cancer surgery). Fifty full-text articles were assessed for eligibility. Thirty-nine were further excluded due to the lack of a control group (ICG vs. non-ICG), insufficient data on outcomes, or incomplete details regarding the ICG protocol. Finally, 11 studies were included in the qualitative and quantitative synthesis.

Data extraction

Key variables extracted from each study included lymph node retrieval (mean ± SD of nodes detected in ICG versus non-ICG groups). Sample sizes (number of patients per group), surgical modality (open, laparoscopic, or robotic surgery), ICG protocol details (dosage ranging from 0.1 to 0.5 mg/kg and injection timing, either preoperative or intraoperative), and perioperative outcomes, which encompassed pre- and postoperative data such as bleeding volume (mL), operation time (minutes), and complication rates (including lymphatic leaks, infections, and anastomotic issues). Two reviewers NM, YW independently extracted data from the included studies using a standardized form. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion or consultation with a third reviewer ZW when necessary. Extracted data included study characteristics, patient demographics, surgical details, ICG protocols, and outcomes.

Quality assessment

Risk of bias assessment was conducted separately for randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and non-randomized studies. For RCTs, we used the Cochrane Risk of Bias tool (RoB 2.0), evaluating selection, performance, detection, attrition, and reporting bias. For non-randomized studies, we used the Risk of Bias in Non-randomized Studies of Interventions (ROBINS-I) framework, assessing bias due to confounding, selection of participants, classification of interventions, deviations from intended interventions, missing data, measurement of outcomes, and selection of reported results (Table 1). Each study was classified as having low, moderate, or high risk of bias, and the results were summarized in Tables 2 and 3.

Statistical analysis

We conducted a meta-analysis using RevMan and R’s Meta package (v4.2.0). For continuous outcomes (LN count, operative time, blood loss, length of stay), we calculated mean differences (MD) since all studies utilized the same scale; medians were converted to means when necessary. For binary outcomes (e.g., complication rates, LN harvest success), we pooled odds ratios (OR) or risk ratios (RR). All results were reported with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). A random-effects model (DerSimonian-Laird method) addressed heterogeneity, and the Hartung-Knapp adjustment provided estimates that are more conservative.

A random-effects model chosen due to anticipated clinical and methodological heterogeneity among studies, including differences in surgical techniques, ICG protocols, and patient populations. To assess the robustness of our findings, we also conducted sensitivity analyses using a fixed-effects model.

Heterogeneity quantified using the I² statistic and Cochran’s Q test. We interpreted I² values of < 25%, 25–75%, and > 75% as indicative of low, moderate, and high heterogeneity, respectively. A p-value of < 0.10 for the Q test indicated significant heterogeneity. When substantial heterogeneity was present, we examined potential sources through subgroup and sensitivity analyses.

To explore sources of heterogeneity, we performed meta-regression analyses examining the influence of study characteristics (publication year, sample size), clinical factors (tumor stage, surgical extent), and technical factors (ICG dose, injection timing) on the observed outcomes.

We planned the following subgroup analyses: (1) surgical approach (open, laparoscopic, robotic); (2) study design (RCT vs. observational); (3) surgery intent (curative vs. palliative); (4) ICG dose and timing; (5) geographic region (East Asia vs. other); (6) surgical extent (D1 vs. D2 dissection). Additionally, we conducted a subgroup analysis by tumor stage (if studies primarily included early GC vs. advanced GC) based on the hypothesis that advanced cases might have more LN metastases and potentially different outcomes.

Publication bias was assessed for the primary outcome (LN count) through visual inspection of a funnel plot and Egger’s regression test for funnel plot asymmetry. A roughly symmetric funnel and Egger’s test p > 0.05 were regarded as indicators of no significant publication bias. We also analyzed funnel plots for secondary outcomes when at least 8 to 10 studies reported them. Additionally, we performed sensitivity analyses for the LN yield outcome by omitting one study at a time (leave-one-out analysis) to ensure that no single study unduly impacted the results. The influence analysis was presented in a forest plot of omitted studies (Figs. 1 and 2). All reported p-values are two-tailed, and we deemed p < 0.05 to be statistically significant for effect estimates.

Results

Study characteristics

This meta-analysis included 11 studies (published 2017–2024) involving n = 2,097 patients (Fig. 3). The studies comprised 2 randomized trials and 9 observational comparative studies. Eleven studies were included, comprising two RCTs [24, 25] and nine observational studies [5, 14, 17, 26–29]. Six studies were conducted in East Asia (China, South Korea, Japan), representing regions with high GC volumes, while 5 originated from other countries (including at least one multi-center cohort). Sample sizes varied from 40 to 514 patients. All studies compared ICG fluorescence-guided gastrectomy (ICG group) to conventional gastrectomy without ICG (control group) (Tables 4 and 5). We carefully checked for overlapping patient populations across studies. If multiple reports from the same center with overlapping times were identified, only the most comprehensive report was included to avoid duplication.

A summary of 11 studies (published 2017–2024)

All studies in laparoscopic and robotic settings utilized advanced NIR imaging systems integrated into surgical platforms. In open surgeries, a photodynamic eye device or a wearable goggle system was used to visualize ICG fluorescence. Patients in the control group did not receive ICG. The primary outcome of total LN count was reported across all studies. The baseline mean number of LNs retrieved in control groups varied significantly, ranging from approximately 24 to 43 LNs per patient, reflecting differences in the extent of dissection and pathology practices.

Data on postoperative complications (classified by Clavien-Dindo grade), conversion to open surgery rates, and length of hospital stay were extracted when available. However, these outcomes were not consistently reported across all included studies. Complication rates ranged from 10% to 20% in both ICG and control groups, with no significant differences observed in individual studies. Conversion rates and length of stay were reported in fewer than half of the studies, precluding a meaningful meta-analysis of these outcomes.

In contrast, on average, the ICG groups retrieved between 40 and 53 LNs. Thus, all studies noted varying LN yields with ICG. Several studies also documented the number of metastatic (positive) LNs, the adequacy of nodal staging, and LN station analysis. Operative time was recorded in 10 of the 11 studies, while intraoperative blood loss was noted in 8. Postoperative complications were classified according to the Clavien-Dindo system; most studies reported 10–20% overall complication rates, with no significant differences identified between groups in individual series.

The risk of bias was low in the two RCTs included (one by Chen et al. and one by Kwon et al.), both of which ensured proper randomization and outcome assessment. The observational studies displayed moderate quality; most were propensity score-matched or adjusted for multiple variables to mitigate selection bias. However, since patients in some non-RCT studies chose ICG over not opting for it (or it was introduced later in the study period), some baseline differences were noted.

The data showed that tumor stage and other factors were generally similar between groups. Publication bias was assessed.

Overall, the studies provided a broad sample that reflects diverse surgical practices and ICG protocols, enhancing the generalizability of the meta-analysis findings (Figs. 4 and 5).

Heterogeneity analysis

Meta-regression analysis identified surgical approach as a significant source of heterogeneity (p = 0.02), with robotic surgery showing greater benefits than laparoscopic or open approaches. Heterogeneity was moderate (I² = 31%), with the smallest effect size observed in Zhang et al. [29] (MD = + 1.4 LNs, p >0.05). Other factors including publication year, sample size, tumor stage, and ICG protocol did not significantly explain the heterogeneity.

Lymph node retrieval outcomes

All 11 studies reported the total number of LNs harvested in the ICG and control groups. The meta-analysis revealed a significant increase in LN yield with ICG fluorescence guidance. Using a random-effects model, the pooled mean difference was MD = 5.9 additional LNs in the ICG group (95% CI: +4.4 to + 7.4 LNs, p < 0.0001). As shown in.

Figure 6, every individual study in our analysis favored the ICG group (all MDs > 0), although the effect sizes varied. The smallest difference was approximately + 1.4 LNs (in a study by Zhang et al., which was not statistically significant), while the largest difference exceeded + 8 LNs in other studies. Notably, the two RCTs found substantial benefits: the randomized trial by Kwon et al. (in robotic surgery) reported a mean of 50.5 nodes with ICG vs. 42.0 without (MD = + 8.5), and the trial by Chen et al. (laparoscopic D2) found a mean of 53 vs. 45 nodes (MD + 8, p < 0.001). Several retrospective series also showed 5–7 more nodes with ICG. Combined results showed an I² of 31.5% for lymph node counts (p = 0.15), which reflects a moderate level of heterogeneity among the included studies.

Subgroup analysis by surgical approach

We stratified the LN yield outcomes by surgical approach to examine whether the benefit of ICG differed among laparoscopic and robotic surgeries. Studies on robotic surgery showed the largest mean difference in LN count when using ICG. A pooled analysis of two robotic cohorts (including one RCT and one observational) yielded a mean difference of + 7.8 LNs (95% CI ~ + 5 to + 10), favoring ICG. It is important to note that this finding is based on only two studies (n = 112 patients), limiting the robustness of this estimate. In laparoscopic surgery, the pooled LN gain with ICG was slightly lower but still significant, with a mean difference of + 5.5 LNs (95% CI ~ + 3 to + 8). This overall effect encompassed a degree of variation among the included studies. For instance, a laparoscopic study by Roh et al. found a marked increase of + 7 nodes with ICG (43.0 vs. 36.0), while another study showed a more modest gain of + 4 nodes. Most laparoscopic studies utilized ICG injected preoperatively and found improved LN dissection (Fig. 7).

Subgroup analyses

Subgroup analysis by study design showed similar effects in RCTs (MD = + 5.8 LNs, 95% CI: +3.9 to + 7.7) and observational studies (MD = + 6.1 LNs, 95% CI: +4.0 to + 8.2). Analysis by surgery intent showed greater benefits in curative intent surgeries (MD = + 6.5 LNs) compared to palliative (MD = + 3.2 LNs). Subgroup analyses by ICG dose and timing, geographic region, and surgical extent limited by data availability, and showed no significant interactions. The robotic subgroup, while showing the largest effect size, was based on only two studies (n = 112 patients).

Operative time vs. blood loss

An important consideration is whether using ICG prolongs the surgery or causes any operative hindrance. Interestingly, our meta-analysis found that, on average, ICG-guided surgeries had significantly shorter operative times (Fig. 8). The pooled mean difference in operative duration was MD = − 21.1 min (95% CI: − 29.2 to − 13.1, p < 0.0001)), as demonstrated in studies like Dong et al. [28] (240.5 ± 38.3 vs. 275.4 ± 43.5 min). favoring the ICG group (a negative value indicates ICG cases were faster). This unexpected finding implies that ICG mapping may make lymphadenectomy more efficient, as the highlighted nodes are easier to identify, potentially limiting the need for prolonged dissection in ambiguous regions (Figs. 4 and 5).

Mean difference in operative time between ICG-guided and conventional gastric cancer surgery

In summary, the results of the meta-analysis indicate that ICG fluorescence imaging significantly enhances lymph node dissection in gastric cancer surgery without adversely affecting operative time or compromising safety based on the reported complication rates. The findings should be interpreted in the context of the included studies’ limitations, but they support further investigation into the integration of this technique into surgical practice.

Discussion

This comprehensive systematic review and meta-analysis is the first to evaluate ICG fluorescence-guided lymphadenectomy in gastric cancer across the primary minimally invasive surgical modalities. Our findings provide evidence suggesting that ICG guidance enhances the oncologic quality of surgery by increasing lymph node yield. On average, the use of ICG resulted in an additional 5–6 LNs retrieved per patient compared to standard surgery, a significant improvement consistent among diverse studies [22].Crucially, the improvement in lymph node retrieval did not come at the expenditure of longer surgery or higher morbidity. In fact, pooled data show a modest reduction in operative duration and blood loss in the ICG group, likely reflecting greater efficiency in identifying and clearing nodal tissue [21, 32]. These results affirm that ICG is a valuable tool in gastric cancer surgery and support its broader adoption to improve surgical outcomes.

Our analysis confirms and expands upon previous findings in the literature. Earlier meta-analyses on laparoscopic gastrectomy indicated that ICG increased LN harvest by approximately seven nodes without negatively impacting morbidity. The superiority of ICG in robotic surgery, as shown by Kwon et al. [25], aligns with our subgroup analysis. We observed a similar magnitude of effect and demonstrated that the benefit is apparent in both robotic and open surgeries. Our subgroup analyses revealed that the benefits of ICG were most pronounced in robotic surgery, followed by laparoscopic approaches. This finding aligns with the enhanced visualization capabilities of robotic systems. However, the robotic subgroup was based on limited data (two studies), and this should be considered a limitation of our analysis. Meta-regression confirmed surgical approach as a significant source of heterogeneity, suggesting that the type of surgery may modify the effect of ICG on lymph node yield. Among the surgical modalities, robotic gastrectomy illustrated the largest relative increase in lymph node yield roughly eight more nodes per case in our pooled analysis. This effect may be related to the integrated near-infrared capabilities and stable visualization that robotic systems provide. Robotic platforms often include built-in near-infrared (NIR) capability, seamlessly incorporating ICG imaging into the surgeon’s console view [21, 32]. In one comparative series, Jeon et al. observed that every patient undergoing robotic ICG-guided surgery achieved and complete D2 dissection, whereas a proportion of those treated laparoscopically without fluorescence did not meet this benchmark. Our subgroup findings support the idea that technology-enhanced surgery (robotics) enhances the benefits of ICG. Laparoscopic surgery also experienced significant gains: for instance, a primary phase III trial in China (IGCLC trial by Chen et al.) showed that ICG not only increased LN yield but also found lower locoregional recurrence rates (17.8% vs. 31.0%) and better three-year disease-free survival (81.4% vs. 69.9%) with ICG guidance. Consistent with our findings, Zhong et al. [27] reported a significant increase in LN yield (49.9 ± 14.8 vs. 42.0 ± 10.3) in their large cohort of 514 patients. These clinical outcome improvements emphasize that the extra nodes retrieved with fluorescence mapping may translate into more complete tumor clearance and more accurate pathological staging, which can translate into improved survival [13].

While our analysis did not formally include studies on open gastrectomy due to a lack of eligible comparative studies, evidence from the literature suggests that ICG could potentially enhance node dissection in open procedures by illuminating lymph nodes that might be overlooked under normal vision [26]. Stations (e.g., along with the lesser curvature or posterior gastric artery) might be overlooked under normal vision. Since open surgery is still common in low-resource settings and for advanced cases requiring multi-visceral resection, introducing ICG could help maintain high oncologic standards, though dedicated studies are needed to confirm this [33, 34]. The only caveat is that open surgeons need access to a fluorescence detection device [35]. This is increasingly feasible with the proliferation of affordable laparoscopic NIR systems.

A salient point from our study [11] is that ICG guidance did not compromise safety within the scope of our analysis; if anything, it may improve it. The pooled operative time was shorter in the ICG groups by roughly 20 min. At first glance, this is unexpected, as adding fluorescence imaging could be assumed to prolong the procedure. However, this time reduction, while statistically significant, should be interpreted with caution regarding its clinical relevance, and it likely reflects efficiency gains: surgeons can directly target glowing lymphatic channels and nodes instead of spending time searching through adipose tissue. Surgeons have described the fluorescence signal as a visual road map of lymphatic network, enabling targeted dissection of relevant nodal basins. Especially in obese patients or those with dense tissue, ICG can save time by clearly highlighting nodes that would otherwise require meticulous fat clearance to identify [24]. Additionally, by delineating lymphatic anatomy, ICG may help avoid unnecessary dissection in areas without drainage, thereby shortening the operation. Our findings concur with Wei et al., who observed significantly reduced dissection time (3–5 min less for the lymphadenectomy portion) in the ICG group of their trial.

Regarding blood loss, although the absolute difference was slight, a consistently lower trend with ICG suggests more controlled dissection. This is plausible because ICG can assist surgeons in clipping or coagulating lymphatic vessels before cutting, which reduces oozing [17, 31]. Furthermore, enhanced intraoperative visualization helps minimize unintended injury to small blood vessels. The absence of differences in major complication rates (anastomotic leaks, pancreatic fistula, etc.) indicates that ICG is not causing harm. Conversely, multiple studies have reported lower leak rates with ICG, presumably because enhanced perfusion assessment leads to more judicious decisions regarding resection margins and the vascularity of the anastomotic site. While our meta-analysis did not focus on perfusion outcomes, it is notable that ICG’s additional benefits (like confirming gastric conduit perfusion) can enhance surgical safety in GC and related surgeries [10].

An important consideration in adopting ICG is the learning curve and practical aspects. Surgeons require training to interpret fluorescence images, including differentiating strong signals from the background and understanding timing (ICG can diffuse over time). However, the learning curve is generally short. Studies report that after just a few cases, surgical teams become proficient in the technique [18]. Proper injection timing (typically 12 to 24 h before surgery for submucosal injection or 10 to 15 min before dissection for intraoperative sub serosal injection) is crucial for optimal node visualization. All included studies had well-defined ICG protocols; no significant protocol deviations were noted. The dosage of ICG (usually 5 to 10 mg per quadrant around the tumor, totaling approximately 25 to 50 mg) was safe and effective in generating fluorescence for up to two to three hours during surgery. We did not encounter issues with ICG signal decay during typical operation lengths. A booster injection is theoretically possible in prolonged cases but was not needed in these series [24].

The clinical implications of our results are significant but require validation through long term studies. By enhancing LN yield, the use of ICG has the potential to lead to stage migration. Some patients in the control group with inadequately stated nodes could be accurately upstaged in the ICG group (if metastases were present in the additional nodes retrieved). This can impact adjuvant treatment decisions. Accurate nodal staging is vital in gastric cancer, as it directly influences chemotherapy planning and survival predictions [19, 20].

Additionally, eliminating nodal non-compliance could improve outcomes, as suggested by the survival differences in the Chen trial. It’s noteworthy that Chen’s 3-year DFS improved by approximately 12% points with ICG; this is comparable to, if not greater than, some chemotherapy benefits in GC. These findings highlight how the quality of surgery remains a key determinant within a multidisciplinary treatment plan. Although long-term follow-up is not available for all studies, the promising evidence from limited number of trials suggests that ICG supported surgery can contribute to improved survival outcomes [23].

Clinical implications

Based on our findings, we recommend the integration of ICG fluorescence imaging in gastric cancer surgery, particularly in robotic and laparoscopic approaches. The technique improves lymph node yield without increasing operative time or complications. Surgeons should be aware of the learning curve associated with interpreting fluorescence images and the need for appropriate imaging equipment. For open surgery, ICG may still provide benefits, but the evidence is less robust. Future research should focus on standardizing ICG protocols and evaluating long-term outcomes.

Limitations

Several limitations of our study should be acknowledged. First, the robotic subgroup included only two studies, which severely limits the precision and generalizability of our estimates for this approach. Conclusions regarding the superiority of robotic surgery should therefore be considered hypothesis-generating rather than definitive. Second, data on certain outcomes such as complications, conversion rates, and length of stay were inconsistently reported, preventing comprehensive analysis of these important safety and efficiency endpoints. Third, while we performed subgroup analyses, some subgroups (e.g., by ICG dose, tumor stage) had limited data, reducing our ability to draw firm conclusions about these factors.

Fourth, and importantly, this systematic review was not prospectively registered in a public repository such as PROSPERO. While we adhered to PRISMA guidelines throughout the review process, the absence of prospective registration may introduce potential bias in outcome reporting and protocol transparency. We acknowledge this as a key methodological limitation and recommend that future systematic reviews in this field be prospectively registered to enhance reproducibility and minimize bias.

Fifth, the included studies varied in their ICG protocols (dosage, timing), which may have contributed to clinical heterogeneity. Finally, we did not pool long-term oncologic outcomes such as overall survival and recurrence due to limited data availability. Consequently, the critical question of whether the increased nodal retrieval translates into a survival benefit remains largely unanswered by the current analysis.

Perspective

Future research should prioritize: (i) head-to-head trials comparing ICG dosing regimens (e.g., preoperative vs. intraoperative injection); (ii) cost-effectiveness analyses of ICG implementation across resource settings; (iii) long-term survival impact of ICG-enhanced lymphadenectomy; and (iv) integration of ICG with emerging technologies like AI-guided fluorescence imaging. Our study provides the foundation for these inquiries by definitively establishing ICG’s differential efficacy across surgical modalities.

Conclusion

Our meta-analysis suggests that ICG fluorescence imaging is associated with improved LN dissection in gastric cancer surgery in terms of increased yield, and this was not associated with increased surgical risk in the studied outcomes. It appeared especially beneficial in minimally invasive procedures, particularly robotics, based on limited data, and offers a safe, effective adjunct for enhancing surgical precision across approaches.
