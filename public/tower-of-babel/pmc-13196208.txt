Associations between media gun violence exposure (GVE) and mental health: a national cross-sectional study
Authors: Devon Ziminski, Rachel Suresky, Esther Lee, Daniel C Semenza
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGun violence represents a major public health concern not only due to direct exposure but also because of its widespread presence in local news and social media. Despite this ubiquity, less is known about how media-based exposure to real-world gun violence relates to mental health outcomes.MethodsUsing a nationally representative sample of U.S. adults, this cross-sectional study examined the relationship between real-world media gun violence exposure (GVE) and mental health outcomes.ResultsFindings highlight the association between greater cumulative media gun violence exposure and various mental health outcomes, with greater exposure associated with a greater number of poor mental health days per month (IRR = 1.51; CI, 1.14–1.70) and a higher depression score (IRR = 1.21; CI, 1.07–1.36). Similar trends were found across four types of media GVE (local traditional media, local social media, outside traditional media, and outside social media), with the most associations between outside traditional media and outside social media, and poorer mental health outcomes.ConclusionsThese findings support existing research suggesting that repeated exposure to gun violence may negatively affect well-being, and that real-world media exposure may also have negative implications. Implications from this study contribute to the growing body of research that identifies the potential harms associated with social media and how incessant real-world violence may negatively affect well-being.Keywords: Gun violence exposure, Media, Social media, Traditional media, Mental health, Depression

Background

Gun violence represents a pervasive public health problem in the United States, resulting in substantial loss of life each year. More than 40,000 Americans died annually in 2023 and 2024 due to gun-related incidents [1, 2]. Gun violence exposure (GVE) includes direct victimization (e.g., being shot or threatened with a firearm), indirect victimization through witnessing violence (e.g., seeing someone shot), indirect vicarious exposure through close others (e.g., knowing a friend or family member shot), indirect proximity-based exposure (e.g., hearing gunshots in the neighborhood), and indirect exposure through real-world media (e.g., in newspapers and via online and social media).

Prior research has documented how real-world violent exposures have negative implications for post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), depression, and related adverse mental health outcomes [3]. Existing evidence suggests that psychological distress from firearm violence exposure may be higher compared to other types of violence exposures [3]. Additional studies have shown that exposure to lethal weapons (e.g., knives or guns) is associated with increased reports of mental health challenges [4], and that individuals injured by guns are at a higher risk of PTSD and depression compared to those injured through other lethal means [5].

The physical effects on the body related to stress, trauma response, and fear induced by gun-related violence can wear on the physical body and mental load, generating conditions for mental health to decline, or for new mental health issues to arise following exposure [6]. Indeed, it has been established that direct and indirect gun violence exposure is associated with adverse mental health effects [3], including anxiety [7], depression [8, 9], suicidal ideation [10, 11], post-traumatic stress disorder [12, 13], and substance use disorder [14, 15]. Cumulative exposures (e.g., experiencing multiple gun violence incidents directly and/or indirectly) have also shown compounded negative associations with mental health and physical health [16, 17].

Although research has examined the mental health effects of direct and indirect community-based GVE [11], as well as media violence more broadly, much existing media violence work has focused on fictional violence depicted in television, film, and video games, often in relation to aggressive behavior [18–22]. In contrast, comparatively fewer studies have examined indirect exposure to real-world gun violence through media sources, including local news and social media, and how such exposure may relate to mental health outcomes. We consider real-world media gun violence to be traditional and social media outlets that report on, discuss, or share information about actual gun violence incidents. We distinguish real-world media gun violence from fictional media violence (e.g., gun violence depicted in fictional or simulated TV shows or video games). We acknowledge the large body of literature that has assessed associations between fictional media violence and various outcomes, such as aggression and mental health [18, 19, 21, 22], with mixed reviews [20, 23]. We posit that real-world GVE through media may be associated with mental health outcomes based on prior literature outlining adverse effects of GVE through direct and indirect means [3, 8–11]. News coverage of real-world violent events may also elicit stronger perceptions of threat and/or mental distress due to its real-life significance, compared to fictional violent media portrayals. Given the widespread accessibility of media platforms (e.g., X, Instagram, TikTok, online news), real-world media-based exposure may represent a common but understudied pathway through which gun violence may be associated with mental health [6].

Mass media play an important role in shaping public perception and interpretation of societal events, including violent incidents and perceived threat. Frequent news reporting on gun violence coupled with sustained media coverage, particularly following high-profile mass shootings, may increase real-world media GVEs. Research conducted in the aftermath of such events suggests that news media coverage is associated with public emotional responses [24, 25], with greater consumption of shooting-related news linked to elevated stress, anxiety, and perceptions of personal threat among adults—even among individuals not directly affected by the events [16, 19, 20, 26–28]. Fear following mass shootings may be particularly pronounced among certain religious, LGBTQ+, and other minoritized communities who perceive themselves to be at higher risk of potential victimization [28–30].

Beyond individuals not directly affected by violent events, prior literature [31] has suggested that repeated media coverage of shootings may be associated with the reactivation of trauma-related symptoms among individuals with prior exposure to violence, including flashbacks and nightmares. Those with trauma histories or pre-existing anxiety disorders may be more vulnerable to experiencing acute or chronic distress in response to repeated exposures to media content compared to those without such histories [31]. For example, a study comparing newsroom employees exposed daily to video footage of violent events with a control group of journalists found no differences in overall mental health; however, employees with prior trauma and greater exposure to violent footage exhibited more severe mental health symptoms [32].

In recent years, online social networks have become a primary source through which individuals encounter information about gun violence [33]. Social media platforms facilitate the rapid dissemination and amplification of violent news content, thereby increasing the opportunities for repeated exposure [34, 35]. Information related to public mass shootings often receives substantial media attention, and other forms of gun violence, such as those involving families, communities, and self-inflicted injury, are also frequently represented across online platforms [36]. As a result, individuals may encounter multiple forms of real-world gun-related violence through media over time, and the potential psychological implications of such exposures are yet to be fully understood. In the aftermath of the 2022 Robb Elementary School shooting in Uvalde, Texas, analyses of crisis text line data revealed significant increases in gun-related conversations featuring stress and mental health concerns [37]. Using national data, researchers found that mass shootings are associated with short-term declines in community wellbeing and emotional health, particularly following deadlier events and among parents of young children [38].

Current study

Taken together, prior research indicates that direct and indirect GVE are associated with adverse mental health outcomes, fear, and distress, and may be amplified through persistent media coverage. Relatively fewer studies have examined how indirect exposure to real-world gun violence incidents through different media sources—such as local news (e.g., on TV and in newspapers) and social media platforms (e.g., TikTok, Instagram)—may be associated with mental health. While much of the existing literature on media GVE has focused on mass shootings, individuals are consistently exposed to a broader range of gun-related violence through the media. Understanding how exposure to this range of media content relates to mental health outcomes remains an important area of inquiry [36].

Building on literature that links indirect and direct GVE to various adverse physical and mental health outcomes, the present study examines the relationship between media GVE and mental health among a representative sample of U.S. adults. We hypothesize that greater exposure to real-world gun violence through traditional and social media sources will be associated with poorer mental health outcomes. We also hypothesize that cumulative real-world media gun violence exposure will be associated with poorer mental health outcomes.

Methods

Data

A national survey was conducted in collaboration with the probability-based online panel, Ipsos KnowledgePanel. Participants were drawn from the Ipsos panel and consented to the survey, each receiving compensation in exchange for entering an Ipsos sweepstakes. A pretest was conducted, followed by the dissemination of 12,822 surveys, of which 8,657 were completed, and 8,009 were deemed fully completed and included in the dataset. Data weights were created, fitted to the study sample, and used in all analyses to support representativeness. Weights were generated through an iterative proportional fitting (raking) procedure for the geodemographic distributions of individuals aged 18 and above by gender, race/ethnicity, census region, education level, household income, and political identification. Distributions were generated from the U.S. Census Current Population Survey and the American Community Survey. All study procedures were reviewed and approved by the Rutgers University Institutional Review Board.

Measures

Media gun violence exposure

Drawing on prior studies [39], media gun violence exposure was assessed through four items that asked participants how often they see stories, images, or videos about gun violence across four media contexts: (1) local community on traditional news media (e.g., cable news, newspapers), (2) local community on social media (e.g., Instagram, Tik Tok, Facebook, X), (3) outside local community on traditional news media (cable news, newspaper), and (4) outside local community on social media. Response options ranged from never (0) to all the time (5). Cumulative media gun violence exposure was assessed as the sum of the four media contexts (range 0 to 16), split into exposure levels: no exposure (0; 0 exposure level), mild exposure (1;1–5 exposure level), moderate exposure (2; 6–9 exposure level), and high exposure (4; 10–16 exposure level). Binary indicators of high media gun violence exposure were created for each of the four exposure types (high local traditional, high local social, high outside traditional, high outside social) by summing each exposure item (4 or 5 vs. low/no exposure, 0–3).

Mental health outcomes

Poor mental health days (past 30 days) were assessed through the number of days in the past 30 days during which respondents’ mental health was deemed not good. Depression (past two weeks) was measured through the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) which asked respondents how often from 0 (not at all) to 3 (nearly every day) they felt regarding a range of prompts (e.g., “Little interest or pleasure in doing things,” “Feeling down, depressed, or hopeless,” “Feeling tired or having little energy”) in the past two weeks [11, 40].

Suicidal ideation (ever) was measured from eight items from the Self-Injurious Thoughts and Behaviors Interview (SITBI) [41]. Respondents were asked to choose all items that applied to the prompt “Have you ever had any of the following thoughts for more than a few minutes? Items included: “I wish I could disappear or not exist,” “I wish I was never born,” “My life is not worth living,” “I wish I could go to sleep and never wake up,” “I wish I were dead,” “Maybe I should kill myself,” “I should kill myself.” All eight items were summed, and a binary indicator of any suicidal ideation was created by coding “1” if respondents said “yes” to any of the eight items. SITBI is a comprehensive measure of suicide-specific behaviors and thoughts (e.g., ideation, plans) and is helpful in understanding suicide-behavior history.

Models accounted for covariates including excessive prescription drug use (never, monthly or less, 2-4x a month, 2-3x a week, 4 + a week), binge drinking (> 6 drinks in one occasion, never, < monthly, monthly, weekly, daily or almost daily), marital status (currently married, widowed, divorced, separated, or never married), current employment status (working full-time, working part-time, not working), household income (>$10,000, $10,000- $24,999, $25 000-$49,999, $50,000- $74,999, $75 000-$99 999, $100,000-$149,999, or ≥$150 000), age (18–29, 30–44, 45–59, or ≥ 60 years), race/ethnicity (White non-Hispanic, Black non-Hispanic, Hispanic, or other non-Hispanic), sexual orientation (heterosexual, gay or lesbian, bisexual, or other), sex (male, female), firearm access (yes, no), sense of safety in neighborhood (0–12, higher numbers indicating a greater sense of safety), cumulative in-person GVE (none, low, moderate, high), health insurance coverage (yes, no), and poor physical health days (past 30 days).

Analysis

We calculated weighted descriptive statistics for all variables included in the study. Two main mental health outcomes of poor mental health days and depression were viewed as count data, and/or their ordinal indices could be summed to a count-like scale [42, 43].

We regressed these mental health outcomes on all media GVE variables (individual and cumulative) using a negative binomial model. Binary variables of suicide thought/ideation and suicide risk were regressed on all media GVE variables (individual and cumulative) using logistic regression. Models all included control variables. Missing data (< 5%) were handled using listwise deletion across all variables included in the models. Statistical analysis was performed using Stata 18. This study adheres to the STROBE guidelines for reporting cross-sectional research [44].

Discussion

This study examined the relationship between real-world media GVE and mental health outcomes, helping broaden our understanding of whether indirect exposure to actual gun violence incidents through media consumption may have associations similar to those of direct and indirect in-person GVE [10, 11]. We observed associations between high cumulative media GVE and mental health outcomes. Both outside traditional media and outside social media GVE were independently associated with a greater number of poor mental health days and depression. While our results are quite modest, they potentially support other research that has shown that when such traumatic violence-related media content is encountered repeatedly, it is linked to heightened psychological distress and threat sensitivity, potentially contributing to depressive symptoms and poorer overall mental health [16, 45–47]. As posited by Felix [16], repeated exposures to violent incidents, even through media, can heighten emotional responses and stress loading, contributing to adverse mental health changes.

Comprehensive media coverage around gun violence often occurs following a public mass shooting, and individuals can often see a wide range of incidents of gun-related violence (e.g., related to gun suicide, intimate partner violence involving a gun, and community gun violence) across their news feeds. These varied accounts, chronicling events from both local and faraway locations, can quickly compound and contribute to repeat GVEs [39]. Cumulative media GVE, therefore, could aggregate to a potential psychological toll on mental health and well-being, and additional research could help further explore this association [3, 16, 36]. Additionally, public perceptions of gun violence are shaped not only by the volume of coverage but also by the perceived credibility and framing of media sources [47, 48]. Repeated exposure to sensationalized or decontextualized reporting may contribute to narratives that emphasize individual culpability and negative portrayals of individuals, neighborhoods, or communities, particularly when coverage centers on episodic accounts of violent events without broader contextualization [49–51].

Beyond cumulative exposure, our finding that outside media coverage was associated with poor mental health outcomes could be explained by the nature of this content. Distal media coverage often emphasizes severe or highly publicized incidents, such as public mass shootings or interpersonal gun violence occurring outside one’s community, which may reinforce narratives of threats to safety and potential loss, irrespective of local gun violence rates [46, 52]. This consideration might add to our understanding of real-world media GVE, particularly given that traditional news sources tend to focus on local events while social media platforms can simultaneously deliver both local and outside gun violence-related content. The overlapping nature of these exposures may complicate traditional geographical delineations, suggesting that distinctions between local and non-local violence may be less straightforward in the current media landscape than previously presumed.

Our study distinguishes indirect media-based GVE from direct and indirect in-person GVE, with cumulative exposure across multiple media contexts associated with mental health burdens. This distinction is noteworthy, as much of the existing gun violence literature focuses on direct and indirect victimization [10, 12], while comparatively less attention has been paid to the potential mental health impacts of persistent real-world GVE through traditional news and social media [16, 25–27]. Work from other fields has highlighted negative associations between frequent intense news reporting and mental health, including following highly publicized and reported anti-Black violence [53], and after traumatic COVID-19 news reporting [54].

While prior work on social media use and mental health has yielded mixed results [55, 56], our findings suggest that media content may have distinct relevance beyond media type use and media dosage. Specifically, gun-related content across both traditional and social media platforms may pose specific considerations related to mental health, requiring a more differentiated, content-focused understanding of potential real-world media-related harms. Other research has posited that violence on social media may be more emotionally resonant than traditional media [57]. Future research can extend these findings by examining how different types of gun violence content, e.g., interpersonal, intimate partner, or politically based gun violence, may have differential associations with mental health, while also examining the dosage and type of media exposure [58].

Additionally, building on research that has identified positive aspects of social media, circumscribed examination of how platforms and engagement behaviors can potentially contribute to positive mental health outcomes, including through solution-oriented narratives and credible information, may have salubrious impacts on communities affected by gun violence [59]. Finally, our findings point to potential considerations for media practices that focus on context, prevention, and resilience, which may support individuals’ mental health. Specifically, situating violence within broader structural or social contexts rather than presenting isolated incidents may help prevent stigmatization and deterministic assumptions about communities disproportionately portrayed as sites of violence, and provide individuals with solutions-oriented action steps after seeing incidents of violence in the media [49–51].

Limitations

This study has multiple limitations. Foremost, although nationally representative, the cross-sectional study design limits causal interpretation of the relationship between media GVE and mental health outcomes. We are cautious not to overstate our findings, recognize their limited interpretability, and are aware of effect sizes that may be perceived as small [60]. We note that our large sample size may have led to false positive correlations.

Second, the self-reported nature of this study creates potential limitations related to under-reporting or the specificity of exposures and experiences, particularly in relation to our chosen mental health outcomes. Our measures were self-report and do not constitute clinical diagnoses. Due to the study’s cross-sectional nature, we are also unable to account for pre-existing mental health and trauma histories, which could affect results, with individuals with past trauma histories and mental health conditions potentially having a stronger reaction to media GVE and more pronounced potential associations with their mental health. As noted in research from the fictional media violence field [18–23], potential associations with violence exposure are more pronounced among ill media consumers, and this comparison is well-suited to be examined longitudinally or among individuals with varying mental health diagnoses and trauma histories exposed to media gun violence.

Third, we could not account for any changes in respondents’ media consumption habits (e.g., frequency, dosage, or duration of media use). Additionally, we could not account for differences across specific media sources (e.g., Facebook vs. Instagram). Fourth, our data collection process with Ipsos KnowledgePanel could have introduced selection bias towards those with internet access. Sample weights are used to adjust for this concern. Fifth, there are other geographic and neighborhood confounders that should be considered in future research to expand our understanding of the relationships between our main independent and outcome variable; future research can include constructs of sense of safety and social support as mediators. Even with these limitations, this study helps broaden our understanding of the ways in which U.S. adults may be touched by gun violence, warranting future research into this topic.

Conclusion

Understanding GVE requires looking beyond direct, in-person experiences to examine how traditional and social media GVE may be related to mental health. Our findings show that cumulative media exposure to gun violence, and independently outside media exposure across both traditional and social media platforms, were associated with poorer mental health. This pattern suggests that the psychological burden of gun violence may extend beyond those directly affected or living in high-violence communities, reaching individuals through repeated media encounters with violent content. Given that 84% of U.S adults use social media and 44% prefer to watch the news, [61] real-world media GVE poses an underexamined yet important public health concern. As gun violence remains a persistent crisis and media consumption continues to evolve, understanding and mitigating any psychological toll of media GVE will become exigent for efforts to support population well-being.
