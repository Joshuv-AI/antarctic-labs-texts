Diagnostic Performance of Machine Learning Models for Predicting Bloodstream Infections in Large Cohorts: A Systematic Review and Meta-analysis
Authors: Daniele Roberto Giacobbe, Elena Ceccardi, Marco Muccio, Mariano Nobile, Lucia Scarfone, Alberto Enrico Maraolo, Ivan Gentile, Matteo Bassetti
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionMachine learning (ML) models have been increasingly applied to support the diagnosis of bloodstream infections (BSI), particularly through the analysis of large routinely collected healthcare datasets. However, it remains uncertain whether large sample sizes alone are sufficient to achieve high diagnostic accuracy. We performed a systematic review and meta-analysis to evaluate the diagnostic performance of ML-based models for BSI in large cohorts.MethodsMEDLINE, Embase, and Web of Science were systematically searched from inception to December 31, 2025 (Prospero registration CRD420251080948). We included observational studies evaluating ML models for BSI diagnosis with ≥ 1000 patients or BSI episodes and reporting sufficient data to reconstruct diagnostic accuracy measures. Pooled sensitivity and specificity were estimated using a bivariate random-effects Reitsma model. Secondary analyses included pooled area under the summary receiver operating characteristic curve (SROC-AUC), predictive values, subgroup analyses, and meta-regression.ResultsTwenty-four retrospective studies were included. Most studies evaluated adult hospitalized patients and used routinely collected structured data, including laboratory, vital sign, and administrative variables. Tree-based ensemble algorithms were the most commonly used architectures. The pooled sensitivity and specificity were 76.6% (95% CI 66.1–84.7%) and 84.5% (95% CI 75.4–90.7%), respectively, with an SROC–AUC of 0.87. The pooled prevalence of BSI was 9.5%, corresponding to pooled positive and negative predictive values of 34.2% and 97.2%, respectively. Significant inter-study heterogeneity was observed (I2 = 74%).ConclusionsML-based models demonstrated good overall diagnostic performance for predicting BSI, but accuracy remains insufficient to solidly support clinical decision-making at the present time. These findings may suggest that increasing dataset size alone could not overcome limitations related to the limited clinical granularity of routinely extracted structured data.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s40121-026-01421-5.Keywords: Machine learning, Artificial intelligence, Bloodstream infections, Diagnosis, Prediction, Data extraction

Key Summary Points

Why carry out this study? This systematic review and meta-analysis assessed the diagnostic performance of machine learning (ML) models for bloodstream infection prediction in large cohorts.Conceptually, the study aimed to investigate whether large datasets based on routinely extracted healthcare data could provide sufficient performance. What was learned from this study? Although ML models showed good overall discrimination, their overall performance seems to remain insufficient for reliable standalone clinical decision-making.The findings suggest that increasing sample size alone may not be sufficient to overcome limitations, possibly imposed by the lack of clinical granularity in routinely extracted structured healthcare data.

Introduction

Bloodstream infections (BSI) are a major cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide, requiring prompt recognition and timely initiation of appropriate therapy to improve patient outcomes [1]. However, early diagnosis remains challenging due to the nonspecific clinical presentation and the limited sensitivity and turnaround time of conventional microbiological methods [2]. In this context, there has been growing interest in the application of machine learning (ML) techniques to enhance diagnostic accuracy in BSI and support clinical decision-making [3–5].

ML refers to a class of computational methods that enable algorithms to learn patterns from data and generate predictions without being explicitly programmed with predefined rules [6–8]. By leveraging large and complex datasets, ML models can integrate multiple variables simultaneously and potentially identify subtle, non-linear relationships that may not be apparent through traditional statistical approaches [6, 9]. However, a key premise underlying the success of ML models is the availability of large, high-quality datasets for training and validation. In clinical research, assembling such datasets often requires extensive manual extraction of detailed information from electronic health records, which is time-consuming, resource-intensive, and not always feasible at scale. Consequently, many studies rely on large cohorts constructed from automatically extractable data sources, such as laboratory test results or administrative discharge codes [3, 4, 10].

Given these considerations, it remains uncertain whether ML models trained on large, routinely collected datasets can achieve high diagnostic performance for predicting BSI. To address this question, we conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis aimed at evaluating the diagnostic accuracy of ML-based models in large cohorts, as a necessary premise to assess, in future research, if and how data source characteristics may influence their performance.

Methods

Protocol and Reporting Standards

This systematic review and meta-analysis followed the guidelines for transparent reporting of multivariable prediction models for individual prognosis or diagnosis (TRIPOD-SRMA) [11]. The related checklist is available in Supplementary Table S1. The protocol was prospectively registered in PROSPERO (CRD420251080948).

Literature Search and Study Selection

A systematic search was performed in MEDLINE (via PubMed), Embase, and Web of Science from database inception to June 15, 2025, with an update on December 31, 2025. The strategy utilized MeSH terms and free-text keywords (e.g., “bloodstream infection,” “bacteremia,” “machine learning,” “artificial intelligence”). The full strategy is available via PROSPERO and in Supplementary Table S2. Titles and abstracts were independently screened for eligibility by three reviewers (EC, MN, LS) using Rayyan software. Potentially eligible studies underwent full-text assessment, with any disagreements resolved by consensus among the research group. Eligibility was restricted to original studies published in the English language.

For studies evaluating multiple machine learning models or reporting performance across various validation frameworks (e.g., cross-validation, independent test sets), the optimal model was selected based on the highest Youden’s Index. This approach was adopted in line with the purpose of the study, i.e., to assess whether these models could maintain sufficient diagnostic accuracy even under optimistic assumptions when applied to large-scale datasets. In cases where performance was reported for both internal and external validation, the internal validation results were prioritized for the primary analysis to ensure methodological consistency (assessment under most optimistic assumptions), and to remain in line with the baseline research question and study aim (i.e., if pooled performance is suboptimal even in the best case scenario, this would more strongly support the need for further research to identify the underlying reasons).

Study Outcome Definition

The primary outcomes of this study were the diagnostic performance measures of ML models for predicting bloodstream infections (BSI), specifically: (a) sensitivity, (b) specificity, (c) PPV and NPV, (d) LR+ and LR−, (e) DOR, and (f) AUC.

Secondary outcomes included the assessment of feature importance (where reported), comparative performance across different machine learning architectures, and the evaluation of model robustness across predefined clinical subgroups.

To explore potential sources of heterogeneity and assess the stability of the primary pooled estimates, we pre-specified subgroup analyses, univariable meta-regressions, and sensitivity analyses (including leave-one-out and training-set-restricted analyses), as detailed in the Statistical analysis section.

Risk of Bias Assessment

The methodological quality and risk of bias of the included studies were independently assessed by at least two reviewers using the PROBAST + AI tool, an extension of the Prediction model Risk Of Bias Assessment Tool, specifically developed for artificial intelligence-based prediction models. Any disagreements were resolved through discussion or by involving a third reviewer.

Statistical Analysis

For each included study, both general characteristics and diagnostic performance metrics of the internal validation or test set were reported at the study level.

Diagnostic accuracy metrics were reconstructed from reported 2 × 2 contingency tables (TP, FP, TN, FN) and corresponding sample sizes. When raw counts were not available, values were derived hierarchically from: (1) TP, FN, FP, TN and sample sizes (including positive/negative or diseased/non-diseased patients); (2) sensitivity and specificity; and (3) PPV and/or NPV, only when necessary. In studies using k-fold cross-validation or similar, reported performance metrics and sample sizes were considered as aggregated estimates across folds. For meta-analytic purposes, these values were assumed to reflect a single effective confusion matrix, and the size of the test set was approximated as 1/k of the total sample size. This pragmatic approximation was chosen to avoid treating fold-averaged performance as if it had been derived from a single pooled set of out-of-fold predictions across the entire cohort, which could have overstated study precision and meta-analytic weight.

Pooled sensitivity and specificity were estimated using a bivariate random-effects model according to the Reitsma approach, which jointly synthesizes sensitivity and false-positive rate (FPR) while accounting for within-study variability, between-study heterogeneity (quantified by the I2 statistic and the estimated between-study variance, τ2), and the correlation between the two measures (ρ). Pooled specificity (1 − FPR) was derived from the model-based estimate of the false-positive rate. A summary receiver operating characteristic (SROC) curve was generated to represent overall diagnostic performance across studies with the area under the SROC curve that reflects their global accuracy; a 95% prediction region around the pooled estimate was also provided. Potential publication bias was assessed using Deeks’ funnel plot asymmetry test and visually inspected through the corresponding funnel plot. Forest plots of sensitivity and specificity were also produced; here, confidence intervals of the single studies were computed using Wald method on the logit scale.

In addition, pooled prevalence was estimated using a mixed-effects binomial generalized linear mixed model (GLMM) with logit link, with between-study heterogeneity quantified by I2 statistic and τ2. Pooled study-specific AUC was also assessed for descriptive purposes; AUCs were logit-transformed to account for their bounded nature and analyzed using a linear mixed-effects model, with between-study heterogeneity quantified by the I2 statistic and τ2. Based on pooled sensitivity, specificity and, where needed, prevalence, derived pooled diagnostic measures including LR+, LR−, DOR, PPV, and NPV were recalculated at the meta-analytic level. Confidence intervals for these derived measures were obtained via parametric bootstrap: 20,000 pairs of logit-sensitivity and logit-FPR were simulated from a multivariate normal distribution defined by the pooled estimates and their variance–covariance in the primary Reitsma model, and 95% confidence intervals were computed from the empirical distributions. Furthermore, expected consequences of testing were estimated for a hypothetical cohort of 1000 patients under alternative prevalence scenarios (0.05, 0.20, and the pooled prevalence), using pooled sensitivity and specificity from the primary bivariate Reitsma model; also here, confidence intervals were obtained via parametric bootstrap.

To explore heterogeneity and its impact on sensitivity and specificity, subgroup analyses were conducted for studies not judged to have high-quality concerns or high risk of bias, studies including only adult patients, studies performed in a single clinical care setting (inpatient or outpatient only), studies reporting patient-based rather than encounter-based data, and studies without cross-validation in the test set. In particular, the latter analysis was intended to assess the robustness of the pooled estimates to the approximation used for studies in which the 2 × 2 table could not be directly reconstructed. A leave-one-out analysis was additionally performed, excluding the study with the largest validation/test sample size. Univariable meta-regressions with the Reitsma model were also conducted to evaluate the impact of year of publication and clinical setting on sensitivity and FPR. Finally, to assess consistency of the primary results, an additional analysis of training set performance (including sensitivity, specificity, and training set sample size) was performed using the same bivariate model and Deeks’ test. Forest plots of sensitivity and specificity were also produced in the same way as in the primary analysis.

A p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Analyses were conducted using SAS software (version 9.4, SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA), and R Statistical Software (version 4.2.1, R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Ethics

As this research is a systematic review and meta-analysis of previously published data, it was exempt from institutional review board approval.

Results

Study Selection

A comprehensive literature search identified a total of 3402 records across three major databases. The initial search phase, conducted up to June 15, 2025, yielded 2978 records, which were reduced to 2132 articles following the removal of duplicates. A secondary search round, spanning from June 16, 2025, to December 31, 2025, identified an additional 424 initial records, resulting in 326 unique articles after de-duplication. Ultimately, after screening against eligibility criteria, 24 studies met the final inclusion criteria for this systematic review and meta-analysis [3, 5, 12–33]. The complete selection process is documented in the PRISMA flow diagram (Fig. 1).

Characteristics of the Included Studies

The systematic screening process identified 24 studies, all characterized by a retrospective observational design [3, 5, 12–33]. Research in this domain exhibits a robust global trajectory, with a significant concentration of high-quality evidence published between 2021 and 2025 [3, 5, 12–25, 27, 30, 31, 33]. Geographically, the evidence base is predominantly from Asia (n = 9), with studies from China [3, 16, 23, 24], Taiwan [17, 21, 31], and South Korea [18, 19], alongside significant contributions from the USA (n = 4; [26, 27, 30, 33]). Additional evidence originated from Israel [13, 29], the United Kingdom [20, 25], and several European countries (n = 7), including the Netherlands [14], Ireland [15], Turkey [12], Spain [22], Italy [5], Austria [28], and Belgium [32].

Regarding the target populations, the vast majority of investigations (n = 20) focused exclusively on adult cohorts [3, 5, 12–14, 16–21, 23–29, 31, 32]. Specialized pediatric research was conducted in three studies [15, 30, 33], while a mixed-age population was evaluated in one investigation [22]. Study setting was primarily centered in acute care environments: the emergency department (ED) was the most frequent setting (n = 9; [13–15, 17–19, 21, 31, 33]), followed by mixed hospital-wide settings (n = 8; [3, 5, 12, 22, 25, 27, 28, 30]), with the remaining studies conducted in intensive care unit (ICU) (n = 7; [16, 20, 23, 24, 26, 29, 32]).

Outcome Definitions and Computational Methodology

Definitions for BSI exhibited notable heterogeneity. While most studies targeted general blood culture positivity (n = 21; [3, 5, 12–22, 24, 25, 28–33]), a specific subset (n = 3) focused exclusively on Central Line-Associated Bloodstream Infections (CLABSI; [23, 26, 27]).

The computational landscape was dominated by tree-based ensemble architectures (n = 11), with a clear preference for eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) and random forest. Advanced deep learning approaches, specifically long short-term memory (LSTM) and bidirectional LSTM (BiLSTM) networks, were also utilized. While validation on an internal test set was performed in all studies, external validation across independent institutions was performed in seven studies (n = 7; [3, 12, 15, 16, 18, 29, 31]).

Explainability and Feature Importance

Regarding explainability, SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) were utilized in 50% of the studies (n = 12). Across the different architectures, laboratory and microbiological markers consistently emerged as the most contributing predictors. Vital signs showed high predictive importance in six investigations, followed by demographics and administrative data. The criteria for establishing the optimal cut-off thresholds were determined independently among the included studies and varied significantly. While the Youden Index was explicitly reported in some cases (n = 2), other studies utilized fixed probability thresholds ranging from 0.03 to 0.50 (n = 6) or did not explicitly state the optimization method.

Overall Diagnostic Accuracy, Predictive Values, and Subgroup Analyses

The diagnostic performance analysis across the 24 included studies revealed marked variability in individual model results, as detailed in Supplementary Table S3. Sensitivity demonstrated an extensive range, with values spanning from 0.05 [26] to 1.00 [32], although the majority of cohorts maintained performance levels above 0.65 (Fig. 2). Similarly, specificity exceeded 0.80 in most cases, with peak values (≥ 0.96) reported by [12, 16, 17, 25, 26] (Fig. 3). The global statistical synthesis using the bivariate Reitsma model, presented in Table 2, yielded a pooled sensitivity of 76.6% (95% CI 66.1–84.7%) and a pooled specificity of 84.5% (95% CI 75.4–90.7%). This discriminative capacity is corroborated by the SROC curve in Fig. 4, indicating good-to-excellent overall performance (SROC-AUC 0.87).

A critical finding emerged regarding the influence of disease prevalence on clinical utility: the pooled prevalence was 9.5% (95% CI 5.6–15.6%). The pooled estimates of PPV and NPV were 34.2% (95% CI 25.1–45.4%) and 97.2% (95% CI 96.0–8.1%), reflecting the characteristics of the included study populations. Given the known dependence of predictive values on disease prevalence, these estimates should not be interpreted as universally applicable predictive values, and a simulation in a hypothetical cohort of 1000 patients was also performed (Supplementary Table S4). This scenario analysis showed that increasing prevalence from 5 to 20% was associated with a marked increase in PPV (from 0.207 to 0.553), along with a corresponding increase in the number of false negatives (from 12 to 47). Regarding factors contributing to heterogeneity, the subgroup analyses (including the leave-one-out analysis) presented in Table 2 show that the estimates remained largely consistent across all subgroups; while the univariate meta-regression reported in Table 3 demonstrated that neither the year of publication nor the clinical setting had a statistically significant impact on performance. However, a trend toward higher specificity was observed in ICU compared to ED (p = 0.076).

Methodological Quality and Risk of Bias

A summary of methodological quality and risk of bias assessment for all included studies is available as supplementary Table S6. Regarding development of models, quality concern was judged as low, high, and unclear in 12/24 (50%), 11/24 (46%), and 1/24 (4%) studies, respectively, whereas applicability concern was judged low, high, and unclear in 21/24 (88%), 2/24 (8%), and 1/24 (4%) studies, respectively. Complete reporting of methodological quality and risk of bias assessments through the PROBAST + AI tool for all included studies is available as appendix in the supplementary material.

Discussion

In this systematic review and meta-analysis of 24 studies evaluating ML-based models for the diagnosis of BSI in large cohorts, we found that overall diagnostic performance was good but remained suboptimal for clinical implementation as a stand-alone diagnostic strategy. The pooled sensitivity and specificity were 76.6% and 84.5%, respectively, with an SROC-AUC of 0.87. Although these findings suggest that ML models are capable of identifying clinically meaningful patterns associated with BSI, their performance generally did not reach the high levels of diagnostic accuracy that would be expected for reliable clinical decision-making, particularly in a condition where missed diagnoses may have severe consequences. Notably, their diagnostic performance appears broadly comparable to, or only marginally better than, that reported in traditional predictive studies of BSI developed one or more decades ago using substantially smaller datasets [34–37]. Furthermore, in line with the aim of the study, we assessed the best case scenario (performance on internal validation cohorts) thus diagnostic accuracy could be expected to be even lower in external real world cohorts.

Therefore, a key finding of the present study is that the availability of very large datasets alone did not result in near-optimal predictive performance, nor did it substantially improve upon the performance of older scoring systems derived from smaller cohorts. One of the most plausible explanations is that many of the included studies, differently from old scoring systems, relied predominantly only on routinely available structured data, such as laboratory values, vital signs, microbiological parameters, and administrative or coding-based variables automatically extracted from electronic health records. While these variables are scalable and easily retrievable across large populations, they may insufficiently capture the full clinical complexity underlying BSI. In particular, several potentially informative dimensions of patient assessment, such as nuanced clinical examination findings, temporal evolution of symptoms, antimicrobial exposure patterns, and detailed information on indwelling devices, were generally absent or only partially represented in the analyzed datasets. This issue highlights a critical distinction between dataset size and dataset granularity. ML algorithms are highly dependent not only on the quantity of data available for training, but also on the richness and clinical relevance of the predictive variables included. Large cohorts built from automatically extractable administrative or laboratory data may therefore provide breadth without sufficient depth. Consequently, even sophisticated ML architectures may be unable to fully discriminate between true infections and conditions that mimic BSI or inflammatory responses. This interpretation, as a first step to nurture further research in this direction, may also be supported by the observation that tree-based ensemble models and deep learning approaches, despite their methodological complexity, did not consistently achieve markedly superior performance compared with simpler architectures across studies.

The relatively modest pooled positive predictive value (34.2%) also deserves consideration. Given the low pooled prevalence of BSI (9.5%), even models with acceptable sensitivity and specificity generated a substantial number of false-positive predictions. From a clinical perspective, this limitation may reduce the utility of these models as rule-in tools, particularly if their implementation leads to unnecessary blood cultures, excessive antimicrobial use, or alert fatigue. Conversely, the high pooled negative predictive value (97.2%) suggests that these approaches may be more suitable as rule-out or triage-support tools, helping clinicians identify patients at lower probability of bloodstream infection. However, even under this interpretation, the residual false-negative rate remains clinically relevant because delayed recognition of BSI can significantly worsen outcomes.

Another relevant observation is the substantial heterogeneity across studies. Variability was present in patient populations, clinical settings, outcome definitions, prevalence of infection, model architectures, feature selection strategies, and validation frameworks. Although subgroup analyses and meta-regressions did not identify statistically significant modifiers of diagnostic performance, residual heterogeneity remained high, and the power of these secondary analyses was also limited, calling for caution in the interpretation of the lack of statistically significance of results. In addition, definitions of BSI were not entirely uniform, with some studies focusing on general bacteremia and others specifically evaluating CLABSI, potentially influencing model behavior and performance estimates.

Several methodological limitations of the present meta-analysis should be acknowledged. First, it should be acknowledged that the choice of a cutoff of 1000 patients for defining large cohort is arbitrary. While this may reflect a reasonable threshold based on our personal experience of the amount of data at which it becomes difficult to perform manual extraction in a timely way with available resources, this may not be true elsewhere and also may not hold in collaborative multicenter studies. Second, all included studies had retrospective observational designs, which inherently introduce risks of selection bias, missing data, and unmeasured confounding. Third, the included studies exhibited considerable methodological heterogeneity regarding ML algorithms, predictor selection, outcome definitions, and threshold optimization strategies. Fourth, for some investigations, diagnostic contingency tables had to be reconstructed indirectly from reported performance metrics, which may have introduced approximation errors. Fifth, although in line with the study aim, our primary analysis intentionally selected the best-performing model within each study based on the highest Youden Index and prioritized internal validation results, representing a relatively optimistic estimate of achievable performance. This should lead to caution in interpreting the generalizability of our estimates, as real-world performance may be lower than the pooled estimates reported here. Sixth, because many studies reported only structured variables, we were unable to evaluate the incremental contribution of unstructured clinical data, such as narrative notes or imaging findings, which could substantially improve future models. Seventh, although most included investigations implemented cross-validation or internal test sets, only a minority incorporated robust external validation across independent institutions. This limitation is particularly important in ML research, where models may perform well within the original data environment but fail to generalize adequately to different patient populations, laboratory practices, or electronic health record systems. Finally, the English-language restriction of our literature search may represent a potential source of language bias.

Despite these limitations, this study also has some strengths. To our knowledge, it represents one of the largest and most comprehensive meta-analyses specifically focused on ML-based diagnostic models for BSI in large-scale cohorts. The study applied rigorous inclusion criteria requiring reconstructable diagnostic accuracy measures, utilized a bivariate random-effects framework appropriate for diagnostic meta-analysis, and explored multiple sources of heterogeneity through subgroup analyses, meta-regression, and sensitivity analyses. Importantly, our findings also provide a broader conceptual contribution by questioning the assumption that increasing sample size alone is sufficient to guarantee high-performing ML systems in clinical medicine. From this perspective, future research may therefore require not only larger datasets, but also richer and more clinically granular data integration, to verify that this actually leads to performance improvements (since this cannot be taken for granted a priori). It is possible, combining structured laboratory and administrative variables with longitudinal clinical trajectories, natural language processing of physician notes, microbiological trends, imaging data, and treatment-response dynamics may allow more comprehensive and granular characterization of features. In addition, prospective multicenter validation studies on external cohorts and standardized reporting frameworks will be essential to establish generalizability, reproducibility, and clinical utility before widespread implementation.

Conclusions

In conclusion, ML-based models for predicting bloodstream infections demonstrated overall good diagnostic accuracy across large cohorts, but, at the present time, their performance remains insufficiently robust to support clinical decision-making. The findings suggest that increasing dataset size alone may not overcome the limitations imposed by insufficient clinical granularity of routinely collected data. Future advances in this field may depend on the integration of more detailed, multimodal clinical information alongside rigorous prospective external validation.
