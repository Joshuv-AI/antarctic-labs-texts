Abstract
. In this work we study quantitative existence results for genus- 2 2 curves over ℚ {\mathbb{Q}} whose Jacobians have Mordell-Weil rank at least 1 1 or 2 2 , ordering the curves by the naive height of their integral Weierstrass models. We use geometric techniques to show that asymptotically the Jacobians of almost all integral models with two rational points at infinity have rank r ≥ 1 r\geq 1 . Since there are ≍ X 13 2 \asymp X^{\frac{13}{2}} such models among the X 7 X^{7} curves y 2 = f ⁡ ( x ) y^{2}=f(x) of height ≤ X \leq X , this yields a lower bound of logarithmic density 13 / 14 13/14 for the subset of rank r ≥ 1 r\geq 1 . We further present a large explicit subfamily where Jacobians have ranks r ≥ 2 r\geq 2 , yielding an unconditional logarithmic density of at least 5 / 7 5/7 . Independently, we give a construction of genus- 2 2 curves with split Jacobian and rank 2 2 , producing a subfamily of logarithmic density at least 2 / 21 2/21 . Finally, we analyze quadratic and biquadratic twist families in the split-Jacobian setting, obtaining a positive proportion of rank- 2 2 twists. These results have implications for Regev’s quantum algorithm in hyperelliptic curve cryptography.



1.
Introduction

The Mordell-Weil theorem implies that for any smooth projective curve
C
of genus
g\geq 1
defined over
{\mathbb{Q}}
, the group of rational points on its Jacobian admits a decomposition
\mathrm{Jac}(C)({\mathbb{Q}})\simeq T\times\mathbb{Z}^{r}
, where
T
is a finite group and
r\geq 0
is an integer called the (Mordell-Weil) rank of
\mathrm{Jac}(C)({\mathbb{Q}})
. A fundamental problem in arithmetic geometry is to understand how these ranks are distributed in families of curves, and in particular among hyperelliptic curves of a fixed genus
g
.

To ask precise distribution questions and give quantitative answers, one must first choose an ordering of the family, a notion of arithmetic
size

H(C)
and then count curves
C
with
H(C)\leq X
as
X\to\infty
. Common choices include ordering by conductor, discriminant, or by various height functions on coefficients of integral models. Since counting by conductor is notoriously difficult (see
[
14
]
), here we enumerate integral models by the height of the coefficients of their models.

In particular, for a model of the form
C:y^{2}=\sum_{i=0}^{2g+2}f_{i}x^{i}
with
f_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}
, we define the height by
H_{1}(C)=\max\{|f_{i}|\}
as in
[
6
]
. This gives rise to the following box

(1)

\mathcal{C}_{1}^{(g)}(X)=\left\{C:y^{2}=\sum_{i=0}^{2g+2}f_{i}x^{i}\in{\mathbb{Z}}[x]\middle|H_{1}(C)\leq X\right\}

of cardinality
(\gamma+o(1))X^{2g+3}
, for an explicit constant
\gamma>0
. This ordering was also used by Booker et al. in
[
9
]
for creating a database of genus-
2
curves (see Section 3.1).

Another height function considered in
[
5
]
for models of the form
C:y^{2}=x^{2g+1}+\sum_{k=2}^{2g+1}c_{k}x^{2g+1-k}
with
c_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}
is given by
H_{2}(C)=\max\{|c_{k}|^{\frac{2g(2g+1))}{k}}\}
, which gives rise to the box

(2)

\mathcal{C}_{2}^{(g)}(X)=\left\{C:y^{2}=x^{2g+1}+\sum_{k=2}^{2g+1}c_{k}x^{2g+1-k}\in{\mathbb{Z}}[x]\middle|H_{2}(C)\leq X\right\}.

By
[
5
]
*Theorem 44 the cardinality of
\mathcal{C}_{2}^{(g)}(X)
is
(\gamma+o(1))X^{\frac{2g+3}{4g+2}}
for an explicit constant
\gamma
. This ordering has been used as well in
[
9

*Sec 3.1.

If
S
is a subset of
\bigcup\limits_{X\geq 1}\mathcal{C}^{(g)}(X)
, where
\mathcal{C}^{(g)}(X)
denotes one of the boxes defined above, we set
S(X):=S\bigcap\mathcal{C}^{(g)}(X)
. We say that
S
has proportion
\delta\in{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}\bigcup\{\infty\}
if
\lim_{X\rightarrow\infty}\frac{|S(X)|}{|\mathcal{C}^{(g)}(X)|}=\delta
. In many arithmetic situations of interest, such as hyperelliptic curves whose Jacobian has large Mordell–Weil rank, one expects this proportion to be zero. To capture the asymptotic size of such thin subsets,
we introduce a logarithmic notion of density.

Definition 1
.

A set
S
of hyperelliptic curves of genus
g
has logarithmic density
\alpha
with respect to
\mathcal{C}^{(g)}(X)
if

|S(X)|=|\mathcal{C}^{(g)}(X)|^{\alpha+o(1)},

or, equivalently,

\lim_{X\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\log|S(X)|}{\log|\mathcal{C}^{(g)}(X)|}=\alpha.

For example, in the case of elliptic curves, in
[
39
]
it is proven that the random matrix model predicts that the set of curves of rank
r\geq 2
has logarithmic density
\frac{19}{24}
with respect to
\mathcal{C}_{2}^{(1)}(X)
.

The main results of this article focus on the genus-
2
case. Accordingly,
when referring specifically to genus-
2
curves, we omit the superscript

(g)
and write
\mathcal{C}(X)
in place of
\mathcal{C}^{(g)}(X)

Remark 1.1

(Brute-force search complexity)
.

A naive brute-force search that draws curves uniformly at random from
\mathcal{C}_{1}(X)
would succeed in finding a curve of rank
r\geq 1
in time
|\mathcal{C}_{1}(X)|^{1-\alpha+o(1)}

\mathcal{C}_{1}(X)

1.1.
Cryptographic consequences

Hyperelliptic curve cryptography (HECC) is the genus-
2
analogue of elliptic curve cryptography (ECC) with the difference that one works in the Jacobian group
\mathrm{Jac}(C)(\mathbb{F}_{q})
of a genus-2 curve
C/\mathbb{F}_{q}
. To ensure that the security of the desired cryptographic construction relies on the average case difficulty of the discrete logarithm problem, Köblitz
[
31
]
proposed to sample a random prime
q
and a random model
C:y^{2}+h(x)y=f(x),\,f,h\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[X]
.

A recent algorithm of Regev
[
42
]
gives a quantum approach to the discrete logarithm problem. This was initially designed for integer factorization, but was extended in
[
4
]
to HECC. As explained in Section
3.1
the curves obtained by solving Problem
1
are suited for Regev’s algorithm.

At a theoretical level, part (3) of Theorem
1
gives an unconditional lower bound on the density of rank-
2
Jacobians in our height ordering, and hence an upper bound on the expected number of trials needed to solve Problem
1
with
r=2
. Part (1) tackles the case of curves with split Jacobian. At a practical level, one finds high rank curves using the examples in the literature. We estimate the Néron-Tate height of the generators of the Mordell-Weil group (see
3.2
).

In cryptography one uses a fixed list of curves (see
[
4
]
). We tackle Problem
2
for a list of genus-2 curves used in cryptography. Recall that the Poincaré decomposition theorem states that for any genus-2 curve
C
, its
\mathrm{Jac}(C)
is isogenous, after some extension of the base field, either to a simple variety, to a split variety of the form
E_{1}\times E_{2}
for two non-isogenous curves
E_{1}
and
E_{2}
or of the form
E\times E
for an elliptic curve
E
.

Genus-
2
curves with split Jacobian or with complex multiplication form structured particular subclasses of curves within HECC. Although these special subclasses have received significant attention, we are not aware of any dedicated algorithmic treatment tailored to the present setting prior to this work. Remarkably, these two subclasses of genus-
2
curves appear in several cryptographic works, e.g.
[
15
]
,
[
13
]
,

[
29
]
and
[
16
]
.

At a theoretical level, Theorem
2
asserts that if
\mathrm{Jac}(C)\simeq E_{1}\times E_{2}
or
\mathrm{Jac}(C)\simeq E\times E
one can find a twist of rank
r=2
in a constant number of trials. At a practical level, it is easier to find twists of high rank for the curves of split Jacobian (Section
3.4
).

2.
Main results

2.1.
Informal presentation of the results

Let
C:\quad y^{2}+h(x)y=f(x)
be the Weierstrass model of a genus-
2
hyperelliptic curve defined over a field
k
of characteristic
\neq 2
. We restrict to the case where
f
is sextic. We call
\infty_{+}
and
\infty_{-}
the two points at infinity, which are potentially in a quadratic extension of
k
. We write
[D]
for the class of a degree-zero divisor
D
in
\mathrm{Pic}^{0}(C)=\mathrm{Jac}(C)
. When
\infty_{\pm}
are conjugate (possibly non-rational), the divisor
(\infty_{+})+(\infty_{-})
is rational, so for
P\in C(k)
the class
[2(P)-(\infty_{+})-(\infty_{-})]
defines a
k
-rational point of
\mathrm{Jac}(C)
.

To see when
\infty_{+}
and
\infty_{-}
are rational, one completes the square:
(2y+h(x))^{2}=g(x):=4f(x)+h(x)^{2}
.
When
\deg g=6
and the leading coefficient of
g
is a square in
k
, the weighted projective closure of
C
has two
k
-rational points at infinity, which we denote by
\infty_{+}
and
\infty_{-}
.
The image of
\infty_{+}
by the Abel-Jacobi map is a
k
-rational point

\alpha_{C}:=[\infty_{+}-\infty_{-}]\in\mathrm{Jac}(C)(k).

We study the density of genus-2 curves
C\in\mathcal{C}_{1}(X)
where
\alpha_{C}
is non-torsion.
This theme goes back at least to a question of Serre: in a real-analytic family of
real
hyperelliptic curves
with two real points at infinity, how frequently does the divisor class
[\infty_{+}-\infty_{-}]
become torsion?
Lawrence
[
32
]
proved that the divisor is torsion when specialized at a
dense
set of parameters in the
usual Euclidean topology.
This is a useful motivating viewpoint, but it is important to stress that “dense over
\mathbb{R}
” is a statement of

analytic
nature, and it does not predict how often torsion occurs among curves defined over
\mathbb{Q}
and ordered
arithmetically (e.g. by the size of integral coefficients).
A good mental model is that
\mathbb{Q}
is dense in
\mathbb{R}
but still has density
0
in any counting problem.
Our results in this section are on the arithmetic side: we order curves by height in explicit boxes of integral models and
show that, in this sense, torsion specializations of
[\infty_{+}-\infty_{-}]
are rare.

More concretely, the authors of
[
9
]
observed in their search for genus-
2
curves of small discriminant that

small coefficients make it more likely
that
g(x)=4f(x)+h(x)^{2}
has square leading coefficient, in which case

\infty_{\pm}
are
\mathbb{Q}
-rational. Moreover, the divisor class
[\infty_{+}-\infty_{-}]
“typically has infinite order”

[
9
]
*Remark A.1.
Our first goal is to turn this heuristic into a conceptual and quantitative statement.
We do this by working over a universal parameter space and viewing
[\infty_{+}-\infty_{-}]
as a universal section

\alpha_{\mathrm{univ}}
of the universal Jacobian.
We show:
(i) for each
n\geq 1
, the locus where
n\alpha_{\mathrm{univ}}=0
is Zariski closed;
(ii)
\alpha_{\mathrm{univ}}
is not torsion (by exhibiting a single specialization of infinite order);
and (iii) using torsion bounds for Jacobians in terms of the height of the model, we show that torsion specializations of
\alpha_{univ}
contribute negligibly in the relevant boxes.
A box-counting argument then shows that among the many integral models for which the two points at infinity are

\mathbb{Q}
-rational, the proportion for which
[\infty_{+}-\infty_{-}]
is torsion tends to
0
.
Equivalently, “most” such curves have
\rank\mathrm{Jac}(C)(\mathbb{Q})\geq 1
.
We also produce an unconditional source of rank
r\geq 2
by constructing an explicit two-section subfamily and applying Néron specialisation (Corollary
2.11
). This offers a partial explanation for the abundance of rank-
2
examples in databases built from small-coefficient
or small-discriminant searches (such as the current genus-
2
data in the
LMFDB
).

Finally, we study a different, more rigid source of extra rank: curves whose Jacobian splits up to isogeny as a product
of elliptic curves.
For curves of the form
C:y^{2}=f(x)
, the condition
\mathrm{Jac}(C)\simeq E_{1}\times E_{2}
can be related to writing
f
as a
product of two cubics, and we reduce the relevant counting problem to counting suitable pairs of elliptic curves with
full rational
2
-torsion.
This provides a second family of explicit rank phenomena in genus-
2
and leads to the bounds proved in
Section
2.3
.

We complement the theoretical arguments above with explicit computational
experiments supporting the rank phenomena discussed in this and in the next
section. For transparency and reproducibility, the scripts used in these
computations are publicly available on our GitHub Repository

3
]

p>3
be prime. Then

\rank E_{p}(\mathbb{Q})=\begin{cases}0&\text{ if }p\equiv 5(\bmod 12),\\
1&\text{ if }p\equiv 3(\bmod 4).\end{cases}

We are now ready to prove the above proposition.

Proof of Proposition
2.15
.

Fix
X\geq 1
. Consider all pairs of primes
(d,m)
with

d\leq X^{1/3},\quad m\leq X^{1/3},\quad d\equiv m\equiv 3\quad(\bmod 4),\quad d,m>3.

For each such pair, define the polynomial
f_{d,m}(x)=d^{3}x^{6}+m^{3}
. By construction,
\left|f_{d,m}\right|=\max\left\{\left|d^{3}\right|,\left|m^{3}\right|\right\}\leq X
so
C_{d,m}
is a smooth hyperelliptic curve lying in
\mathcal{C}_{1}(X)
.

By Lemmas
2.16
and
2.17
,

\rank\mathrm{Jac}\left(C_{d,m}\right)(\mathbb{Q})=\rank E_{d}(\mathbb{Q})+\rank E_{m}(\mathbb{Q})=2.

Using the Prime Number Theorem for primes in arithmetic progressions, we have

\left|\left\{p\leq X^{1/3}:p\;\text{prime},\,p\equiv 3\bmod 4\right\}\right|\sim\frac{3}{2}\frac{X^{1/3}}{\log X}

hence the number of pairs
(d,m)
as above is asymptotically
\frac{9}{4}\frac{X^{2/3}}{(\log X)^{2}}
.
∎

3.
Cryptographic motivation: Regev’s quantum algorithm

In this section we develop the arguments in Section
1.1
. We recall the mathematical assumptions which support the security of the hyperelliptic curve cryptography. We first recall Regev’s recent algorithm. By solving Problem
1
we produce hyperelliptic curves on which the algorithm is particularly fast. In practice HECC is implemented on a very small list of curves. We explain why the security of each curve relies on the difficulty to find twists of high rank. This is an application of Problem
2
.

The discrete logarithm problem (DLP) plays a key role in cryptography. Let
(G,+)
be a group, e.g. an elliptic curve or the Jacobian of a genus-2 curve. If
g
and
h
are two elements of
G
such that
h=[x]g
, the discrete logarithm of
h
in base
g
, denoted
\log_{g}h
, is
x
. Elliptic curve cryptography (ECC) and hyperelliptic curve cryptography (HECC) rely on the difficulty to compute discrete logarithms (see
[
4
]
).

The quantum algorithms offer a new paradigm: Shor’s algorithm
[
48
]
can compute discrete logarithms in polynomial time, which compromises the security of ECC and HECC. Recently Regev
[
42
]
proposed a quantum algorithm for the DLP which has a parameter
d
. The step which dominates its gate complexity of Regev’s algorithm consists of
d+\frac{n}{d}
additions and doublings in the group
G
. We recall that the gate complexity of Shor’s algorithm is
n
additions and doublings in
G
. Hence, from an asymptotic point of view, the advantage of Regev’s algorithm with respect to Shor’s algorithm is
\min(d,\frac{n}{d})
.

3.1.
Regev’s algorithm in a nutshell

One uses
d-2
elements of
G
,
g_{1},\ldots,g_{d-2}
and sets
g_{d-1}=h
and
g_{d}=g
.

In Regev’s algorithm one considers
the lattice

L=\left\{(z_{1},\ldots,z_{d})\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}\mid\sum_{i=1}^{d}[z_{i}]g_{i}=0\right\}.

One computes in superposition all the sums
\sum_{i=1}^{d}[z_{i}]g_{i}
, called multi-scalar multiplications, where
z_{1},\ldots,z_{d}
are non-negative
\frac{n}{d}
-bit integers. A quantum procedure computes a basis of the lattice
L
. Finally, one solves a linear system to find a vector of
L
of the form
(0,\ldots,0,-1,*)
. The last coordinate of this vector is
\log_{g}h
.

To obtain the best complexity of the algorithm, one must have a classical algorithm which computes any scalar product with coefficients of
\frac{n}{d}
bits. This can be done using Pippenger’s algorithm
[
41
]
if one can store a table with all the multi-scalar multiplications with coefficients
0
and
1
. In the paradigm of quantum algorithms one cannot store large tables and Regev
[
42
]
proposed to select the elements
g_{1},\ldots,g_{d-2}
which can be represented on few bits.

If
C
is a hyperelliptic curve defined over a finite field
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
, the method proposed in
[
2
]
and extended in
[
4
]
consists of using a curve
\overline{C}
defined over
{\mathbb{Q}}
whose reduction modulo
q
is isomorphic to
C
. A set of generators of
\mathrm{Jac}_{{\mathbb{Q}}}(\overline{C})
modulo its torsion allows to produce points on
\mathrm{Jac}_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}(C)
by reduction modulo
q
. These are used in the role of
g_{1},\ldots,g_{d-2}
in Regev’s algorithm.

By solving Problem
1
one finds curves on which Regev’s algorithm can be run with a large parameter
d
.

Consider now the case of a curve
C
which has only lifts of small rank. Assume that one found an integer
d
or a pair of integers
(d_{1},d_{2})
which are squares modulo
q
, one can use any curve
C^{\prime}
defined over
{\mathbb{Q}}
which is isomorphic to
C
over
{\mathbb{Q}}(\sqrt{d})
or
{\mathbb{Q}}(\sqrt{d_{1}},\sqrt{d_{2}})
. Hence, by solving Problem
2
one can run Regev’s algorithm on the curve

.
