Implementing digital patient-reported outcomes in routine cancer care: barriers and facilitators
Authors: SG Aanes, S Wiig, C Nieder, EC Haukland
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIn cancer care research, there exists a gap between patient-reported outcomes (PROs) and health care personnel-reported outcomes. PROs have shown significant benefits in terms of symptoms, quality of life, reduced hospital admissions and increased overall survival. However, throughout the past decade, PROS have been used mainly in clinical trials and slowly implemented in routine cancer care. We wanted to review specific published experiences identified as barriers to and facilitators of the implementation of digital PROs in routine cancer care.ResultsA literature search was conducted in PubMed and Ovid Medline Evidence-Based Medicine Reviews (EBMRs) from 1 January 2017 to 29 August 2023. A total of 313 records were screened, of which 15 records were included. Facilitators identified were a user-friendly electronic PRO (ePRO) solution routinely used by care providers in a revised workflow that engaged key stakeholders and provided sufficient support and infrastructure. Common barriers were lack of information about benefits, time constraints, literacy or lack of access to text or information technology (IT) together with a negative impact on workflow, inadequate IT infrastructure, not engaging staff and costs.ConclusionsSuccessful implementation of ePRO systems needs to address identified barriers and leverage facilitators. Using implementation frameworks and guidelines with quality improvement methods can enhance successful implementation as they address both local barriers and facilitators in a system thinking perspective. There is still an unmet need for real-world evidence on how sustainable PROs can be implemented most efficiently over time, thus highlighting the need for a bridge between medical and implementation science.Key words: patient-reported outcome measures, ePROM, ePRO, implementation, routine cancer care, remote symptom monitoring

Introduction

Great progress has been made in the field of oncology throughout the 2020s, with precision medicine tailoring anticancer treatments according to the type of molecular profile the patient’s cancer expresses.1 At the same time, being diagnosed with cancer as a life-threatening disease affects patients and their families psychologically, socioeconomically and physically. With an increasing jungle of new systemic anticancer treatments that for many patients will prolong their lives even in palliative phases, there is also a call for patient-centered care throughout the patient’s journey, emphasizing shared decision making between the patient and the care provider about which road to choose between treatments, benefits and possible outcomes.

Patient-reported outcomes (PROs) describe patient experiences including physical, somatic and psychological symptoms, functional status, overall quality of life, and subjective well-being.2 When the patient self-assesses these experiences, they are called PRO measures (PROMS). PROS can be collected by paper or electronically (ePROs). PROs have been studied against clinician-reported outcomes, revealing a gap between what health care personnel (HCP) describe compared to the outcomes reported by the patients regarding side-effects, physical symptoms and psychological issues, and this gap has not diminished throughout the past 20 years.3 The United States Food and Drug Administration, the European Medicines Association and the European Society of Medical Oncology have published guidelines on how PRO or PROMs should be integrated into clinical trials to fully investigate how new systemic treatments affect patients, contributing to a shift from disease-centered care to more patient-centered care.4, 5, 6, 7, 8 In addition, PROs have shown a positive effect on communication, symptoms, health-related quality of life and patient satisfaction when providing feedback to patients and/or HCP. An increase in overall survival has been reported in some studies.9,10 Furthermore, PROs can detect adverse events earlier than standard follow-up due to follow-up in real time, leading to a faster response from care providers, which may prevent further deterioration of the patient’s health. Despite this, PROS have been mainly used in clinical trials and implemented at a much slower pace in routine cancer care due to uncertainty especially regarding how to implement it successfully.11 In this review, we analyze the experiences related to implementation of PROs in routine cancer care that have been published during recent years, focusing particularly on facilitators of and barriers for a successful implementation of PRO/PROMs for patients, HCP and the health care system.

The review addresses the research question ‘What are identified as barriers and facilitators for implementing digital PROs in routine cancer care for patients, HCP and the health care system?’

Materials and methods

A literature search was conducted in PubMed and Ovid Medline Evidence-Based Medicine Reviews (EBMR), which included six databases (ACP Journal Club, Cochrane database of Systematic reviews, Cochrane Clinical Answers, Database of Abstracts of reviews of Effects, Health Technology Assessment and Embase). The chosen search terms were ‘PROMS’, ‘ePRO’, ‘ePROMs’, ‘patient reported outcome measures’, ‘PRO’, ‘patient reported outcome’, ‘implementation’ and ‘routine cancer care’, limited to full-text English reviews from 2017 to 29 August 2023. The articles included experiences with implementation in routine cancer care for all cancer types in both the adjuvant and palliative stages with systemic anticancer medication and radiotherapy for pediatric and adult oncology patients. Studies describing both the implementation of PROMs and ePROs were included due to scarce literature on the specific theme of implementation, as most publications have studied the clinical effects of PROs and not specifically the implementation. Different types of PRO and PROMs solutions with different follow-up intervals were included, as long as there was a description of the implementation process in the paper. The Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) checklist was used. Results were collected and summarized in Microsoft Excel, where extracts from the papers included specifically regarding the implementation process where grouped and divided into factors acting as facilitators or barriers to the process.

The exclusion criteria were articles describing PRO or PROMs use in trials, cancer survivorship, cancer registries and rehabilitation, surgery and the specific development and validation of PROM/PRO measurement tools to be able to compare more easily between different implementation experiences among more similar areas of use and departments. Results

The literature search revealed 308 articles in total, of which after reading of abstracts 10 reviews were included. Another five articles were added from the references, including in total 15 studies. Facilitators and barriers for patients

Facilitators

For patients and caregivers an important facilitator for using PROs in routine cancer care was that they address relevant and actionable symptoms, are easy to understand and interpret and do not take too much time to complete. Survey fatigue could be a potential threat for patient adherence, buy-in and burden in an already difficult life situation. They felt that it increased communication with their care team about which symptoms they felt were most bothersome and of concern, and if they perceived benefits as improved care it increased completion of PROs.11 Abejas et al. highlighted that PROs help patients and the care provider to not only focus on the medical or physical problems, but also draw attention to psychosocial, spiritual and financial burdens, and concerns about relatives and issues related to palliative care and end-of-life dilemmas.12 When PROs were conceived as a routine part of the clinical visit, it increased the motivation to use the program regularly. Furthermore, when physicians emphasized the importance of completing PROs to the patient and acknowledged the patient’s responses during subsequent consultations, it supported engagement over time. In contrast, if their self-reports were not used at clinical visits, engagement to complete PROs decreased as a consequence.13, 14, 15 Patients felt more involved in their care and obtained a better overview of their symptoms over time by tracking symptoms. In addition, PROs provided a sense of security to patients and caregivers which helped them engage in the program over time.12,16,17 Montgomery et al. found during their development of a national PRO implementation program that patients with insight into specific disease site/symptoms were fundamental as a key stakeholder of testing before the selection and implementation of relevant PROs.17 Available and sufficient support and information to understand the value of using PROs, the selected PRO solution and technologically was another crucial facilitator for patients to adhere to and use PROs actively.17,18

Barriers

Hindering use of PROMs were patients’ lack of awareness about the service, patients not being sufficiently informed about the purpose or the perception of no benefit. Time constraints when completing a questionnaire that was too long could prevent patients from using it. Patient literacy to both text and technology, digital literacy, access to technology and digital privacy and security concerns about the reported data are other barriers that were described.12,14,19,20 Van Egdom et al. found a relationship between less willingness to fulfil PROs and age, together with differences in use by gender (women > men), acknowledging that the study populations were dominated by Caucasians and mostly English-speaking participants.19 In areas with greater cultural diversity, the PRO tool needed to be translated into a wider range of languages to not exclude non-native patients.15 For the individual patient, high symptom burden and mild cognitive impairment were the main reasons for not completing PROs at an in-patient department.21 As mentioned earlier, if patients felt that the PRO reports were not used actively by the care providers, they may become frustrated and wonder why they should report data at all.15,16

Facilitators and barriers for health care personnel

Facilitators

For HCPs, the perceived relevance, specificity and evidence of the PRO, introduced together with concise, clinically relevant PROs in a user-friendly program, are central points to facilitate the implementation of a PRO program. The PRO scores and symptoms should be easy to interpret, and the program should be amenable to change and fit within work practice. Having sufficient resources to support the solution with enough staff, hardware, education and symptom guides available is another measure that needs to be included.11,17,18 Clinicians experiencing the benefit of a patient’s response to questions that they otherwise would most likely not address during a routine visit; nevertheless, describing serious concerns that are often downplayed, and data on symptom severity, tracking over time and giving focus to the consultation increased engagement.14, 15, 16 In addition, the tool impacted engagement when HCPs experienced that the tool facilitated communication between providers and shared understanding and decision making with the patient, and increased efficiency by focusing the visit time on symptoms of most concern/severity and appropriate intervention.20 A review of the PROs response should be included in the workflow with clear roles and responsibilities of who should follow-up, as well as an available combined patient- and provider-facing symptom management platform. Continuous optimization and evaluation should be carried out to adapt procedures and programs throughout the process.13,14,16,21 Moreover, HCP involvement as a key stakeholder throughout development and implementation, education about PROs and quality of life can increase awareness and engagement about how PROs can streamline and improve care if integrated in the local context.22,23

Barriers

For HCPs, the important barriers mentioned in the articles are concerns regarding the relevance of a PRO and issues relating to its feasibility and impact on workflows, not as an additional task on top of other demands imposing time constraints during consultations and hindering use.11,12 Van Egdom et al. reported a decreased value of patient perception when the patient–provider relationship had been established.19 Several studies found low physician engagement and low compliance to use the PRO tools.10, 11, 12, 13, 14,18, 19, 20,22 Kiderlen et al. found that only one-third of the care providers looked at ePRO reports during clinical visits, which remained at a low level after 1 year. Their ePRO system was not integrated into the electronic health record (EHR), which caused additional effect on time used, suggesting that additional time and work effort from ePROs are often reported in feasibility studies, but not in trials.15 The presence of technical issues and an increase in workload can lead to frustration among clinical staff members. Resistance to change and hesitation to adopt new technologies, as well as previous unsuccessful attempts to implement ePROs can also be barriers.12,24

Facilitators and barriers at a system level

Facilitators

From a system perspective, the importance of technological support, either in-house or through partnerships, easily available throughout development, implementation and management was a crucial facilitator. Furthermore, ePROs are preferable to paper PROs due to better data quality, faster completion times, lower cost and facilitating clinical decision making as they are readily available between clinical visits.25 Having ePROs fully integrated into existing patient management systems is highlighted by all the included articles, because it facilitates alert management in data collection, eases integration in the clinical workflow and makes data actionable at the point of care with clear instructions. Thus, the care process can be enhanced and not slowed.15

Moreover, how to engage and change the health care culture towards a patient-centered culture by actively embracing and using patients’ self-reports? A review of the ePRO response should be embedded in the work plan underpinning the standard of care with a clear deployment, as well as a combined patient- and provider-facing symptom management. Adaptation, continuous optimization and evaluation should be carried out to adapt procedures and programs throughout the process.14,16,19,24 Ongoing support and appreciation from the institution, leaders and management throughout the process of allocating sufficient human and technical resources seems substantial. Furthermore, face-to-face support and ongoing collaborative efforts are needed to customize the tool as well as the PRO measures and data.12 In addition, involving stakeholders from different professions such as administrative, technical, medical and from the patients’ side to form interdisciplinary teams during the process of development, implementation and continuous improvement helps in addressing and solving issues and problems (both technically and logistically).13,16 The incorporation of staff training, education sessions and guidelines into the early stages of implementation with transparent policies to ensure safe and secure use of the PROs was a clear facilitator.12,18,25 In several reviews, a local clinical champion and coordinators were essential for success.14,16,20,24 Including users from patients, front-desk staff and HCP, and mapping current and new workflows while eliminating redundant steps and continuous optimizations of the solution helped Nordan et al. succeed with their implementation in multiple medical departments.24 Basch et al. emphasized the need for central support of change management and identifying local change champions, as well as not taking the steps of implementation too quickly, as the HCP can fall off and not be engaged.16

Barriers

A common obstacle described in all the articles was the need to solve how the ePRO follow-up could be integrated into daily clinical practice as a new standard of care. Alert-fatigue can evolve if there is no sufficient time to follow-up the patient’s responses. The influence of the PROMs on the workflow with a focus on avoiding increases in work load and time used is covered in all the articles, recognizing that these systems will influence the clinical routine on both the technical and procedural sides. To have a bottom-up approach when implementing a PROM solution involving an interdisciplinary team and key stakeholders, together with strong commitment from management at all levels, learning from the implementation process by starting small and then spreading the implementation, and mapping current workflows, eliminating redundant steps and defining new workflows were described as important steps increasing staff buy-in over time.20,24

All the studies reported challenges regarding technical barriers. There were concerns regarding the security of patient data protection, information technology (IT) issues (unstable wireless local area network, log in, user friendliness, not integrating data and programs into EHRs) and technical difficulties of implementation. Nordhausen et al. recommended that multicenter implementation should start with a single center to realize complex technical integration, gain experience and make adaptations used by subsequent centers.21

Another barrier mentioned was the cost of PRO assessment, including the initial economic investment and maintenance, additional staff, tools and running costs—concluding that we do not know so much about the cost-effectiveness of PROs in routine cancer care.12,25

Discussion

When planning evidence-based interventions in our complex health care system, it is important to obtain knowledge about how it can be done more efficiently, and learn from other previous experiences. This review compiles experiences from five studies and 10 reviews including 4 systematic reviews, covering a broad range of cancer diagnoses and centers from both Europe, Australia and Northern America. Knowledge about the obstacles others have met throughout their implementation journey can make administrators and clinicians more prepared and vigilant about their own health care systems’ local barriers and facilitators. Barriers and facilitators varied in the different studies and reviews included, possibly due to how different health care trusts, hospitals and systems are organized. The main purpose of the review is to embrace the complexity of the system by including the knowledge of what others have experienced, in addition to discussing some of the tools and guidelines now published, which hopefully can make it easier for others to find their own successful way of implementation.

The health care system is complex, and when implementing new interventions, the studies included show how many different areas can be affected. Communication between the patient and the HCP, work and patient culture, clinical workflows, IT infrastructures and logistical problems are all processes that were addressed. Several of the studies used implementation frameworks before and during the intervention—including the International Society for Quality of Life Research (ISOQOL), European Organisation for Research and Treatment of Cancer (EORTC) and Medical Research Council frameworks for development and evaluation of complex interventions which helped them prepare and adapt to different challenges throughout the process of implementing a sustainable solution.12,13,16,21,25, 26, 27, 28 To progress from diffusion and dissemination, via implementation and adoption to sustainability can require appropriate resources and knowledge, time and support from implementation science experts, change agents and stakeholder groups.29 Broad support is needed from policy makers and leaders, who recognize the organization’s complexity and readiness for change, as well as a longer-term view for PRO implementation to be sustainable together with an evaluation framework to secure a solution that is truly embedded in clinical practice.29 To use the expertise from local change management with a quality improvement approach including key stakeholders (end users) throughout the whole process, mapping current and new workflows and continuing to optimize and learn from the steps before were important facilitators for several of the studies. The ESMO guideline ‘The role of patient-reported outcome measures in the continuum of cancer care’ (2022) recommends several PRO implementation guidelines to ensure that both barriers and implementation solutions are mapped before implementing PROMS in clinical practice.7 The guideline concluded that barriers are consistent between different departments and even among countries, but facilitating factors are more specific for each hospital. Another important guideline is PROTEUS, Patient-Reported Outcomes Tools: Engaging Users and Stakeholders, consortiums’ practice guideline,30 published in 2023. The guideline divides barriers and facilitators for patients with accessibility, lack of buy-in and burden of reporting PROs. At the clinician level, challenges with technology and logistics are important to focus on the disruptions of workflow, time and resource constraints and uncertainty on how to integrate data into clinical care. Cost, lack of shared values/purpose, uncertainty on how to assess the impact of PRO collection on quality as well as legal and regulatory concerns are crucial at an administrator level. Lastly, at a system level, barriers and facilitators are the no ‘one-size-fits-all’ approach, technical capacity and lack of in-house expertise. This is similar to the findings in this review.

A possible variable at a system level could be how the health system is organized economically. We did not find any clear evidence around differences in implementation between different health care organizations in our review, but one could think that maybe in a private organized system it could be more economical incentives and resources to implement digital health care, and cost of the software was an important barrier mentioned in several papers.19,25

In our review we did not find any studies describing the implementation of a co-existing ePRO solutions or replacing a dysfunctional solution for a new and improved solution. Having invested a lot of time, energy and money could be a possible barrier to stop a non-functioning system, in addition to the threat of ‘intervention-fatigue’ and resistance for new digital interventions for both HCP and patients. For instance, Taarnhøj et al.13 used the results from their feasibility study with low physician compliance affecting decreased patient adherence into a national study, urging for continuous feasibility testing before implementation of the software. All the included papers reported doing smaller or larger changes throughout the implementation process to either patient or clinical logistics, software and integration, education of staff or management commitment. This highlights the need for an institution to be able to continuously monitor, evaluate, act and improve the digital solution as well as all the processes concerned for a sustainable solution over time.

All the different studies included in this paper were published on the implementation of PROs in clinical cancer care before or during the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic (2017-2023). Throughout the years 2020-2021, the health care system rapidly, at a much larger extent than earlier, used digital solutions and technology to maintain contact with patients. It is possible that experiences from the ‘COVID-19 years’ can facilitate increased use of technology in the following years, as both our culture and access to secure IT solutions have changed. Increased use and availability of technology during recent years could mean that elderly patients are more familiar with using ePROs because they already have the technological skills required, contrary to previous belief that older age could be associated with lower use of technology. There is a need to investigate and focus more on possible differences in the routine use of digital interventions for individuals of different gender, socioeconomic status, cultures, races and also different age groups (overwhelmingly our studies included mostly Caucasians with higher education who were English-speaking).19 Focus on equity in digital health care is especially important as many of the factors mentioned above could only be fully investigated and revealed through research on use in standard cancer care.

Other publications not included in the review due to the paper not being a systematic review could limit the generalizations of our findings, although the experiences included across different studies are quite similar including systematic reviews, scoping reviews, randomized controlled trials and feasibility studies. In addition, we have included different ePRO tools with different intervals of follow-up as well as ePROs covering different types of measurement (although mostly symptoms under systemic cancer treatment) which can make it hard to compare the different implementation processes. Despite this, many of the same facilitators and barriers identified in the included papers illustrate how implementation processes for digital interventions touch upon the same core domains—patients, health care providers and the health care system itself. Not including any publications after August 2023 can additionally be a limitation, as there have been tremendous developments during this short period of time in the field of digital health care.

Furthermore, we did not include ‘remote symptom monitoring’ in our search as the term is not so common still in Europe, leading us to not find and include at least two other important papers.31,32

Lastly, it is not possible to exclude a possible publication bias towards the experiences with unsuccessful implementations of ePROs as the standard of care, as one could imagine that negative results are less likely published. Important barriers could be hidden in these experiences, and a lot of important learnings can be drawn from both positive and negative experiences especially due to the general lack of information about how to best achieve a successful and sustainable implementation over time in the real health care setting.

Conclusion

In the past few years there is increasing literature about experiences from efficient implementation of PROs in routine clinical cancer care after a decade of promising results from research studies regarding quality of life, reduced symptoms and even increasing overall survival. This has led to newly developed frameworks for implementation and guidelines.7,30

Knowledge about possible facilitators and barriers at the patient, HCP and system level is crucial when planning and implementing ePROs, and can vary from institution to institution.

Without an operational implementation of ePROs functioning for patients, health care providers and the health care system, there is a major risk that the benefits of ePRO will be diluted in routine use. Using known implementation frameworks as ISOQOL, EORTC and Medical Research Council frameworks.26, 27, 28 or the PROTEUS guidelines30 together with quality improvement methods can enhance how ePROs can be efficiently implemented in clinical cancer care, addressing and helping solve local barriers and enhance facilitators. There is still an unmet need for real-world evidence on how PROs can be implemented and maintained efficiently and sustainably over time, thus highlighting the need for a bridge between medical and implementation science.
