An analytical joint prior for effective spins for inference on the spin distribution of binary black holes
Masaki Iwaya, Kazuya Kobayashi, Soichiro Morisaki, Kenta Hotokezaka, Tomoya Kinugawa

Abstract
We derive an analytical form of the joint prior of effective spin parameters, χ eff \chi_{\text{eff}} and χ p \chi_{\text{p}} , assuming an isotropic and uniform-in-magnitude spin distribution. This is a vital factor in performing hierarchical Bayesian inference for studying the population properties of merging compact binaries observed with gravitational waves. In previous analyses, this was evaluated numerically using kernel density estimation (KDE). However, we find that this numerical approach is inaccurate in certain parameter regions, where both | χ eff | |\chi_{\text{eff}}| and χ p \chi_{\text{p}} are small. Our analytical approach provides accurate computations of the joint prior across the entire parameter space and enables more reliable population inference. Employing our analytic prior, we reanalyze binary black holes in the Gravitational-Wave Transient Catalog 3 (GWTC-3) by the LIGO-Virgo-KAGRA collaboration. While the results are largely unchanged, log-likelihood errors due to the use of the inaccurate prior evaluations are 𝒪 ⁡ ( 1 ) \mathcal{O}(1) . Since these errors accumulate with the increasing number of events, our analytical prior will be crucial in the future analyses.

Hierarchical Bayesian inference is commonly used to extract the population property of GW sources
[
36
,
37
,
38
]

The likelihood function in this inference involves the evidence for each event and selection function that characterizes observational selection effects.
Each component requires the evaluation of a high-dimensional integral over
\theta
.
These integrals are computed using Monte-Carlo methods, where random samples of
\theta
are drawn from reference distributions, and Monte-Carlo sums are calculated with appropriate reweighting of them.
For evaluating the evidence, posterior samples are obtained from the parameter estimation of each event, and their weights in the reweighting process are inversely proportional to the prior distribution assumed in that analysis.
For evaluating the selection function, typically, a large number of simulated signals are injected into data to study the search sensitivities, and their weights are inversely proportional to the distribution from which the simulated signals are drawn.

For inferring the distribution of
\chi_{\text{eff}}
and
\chi_{\text{p}}
, those reweighting processes require their joint distribution conditioned on
q
, denoted by
\pi(\chi_{\text{eff}},\chi_{\text{p}}|q)
, computed from the prior distribution or the distribution populating the simulated signals.
Typically, these distributions are isotropic with respect to component spins and uniform in their magnitudes.
In the GWTC-3 analysis, it was evaluated numerically by drawing samples from the isotropic and uniform-in-magnitude spin distribution and applying kernel density estimation (KDE).

In this paper, we derive an analytical formula of
\pi(\chi_{\text{eff}},\chi_{\text{p}}|q)
.
By comparing our analytical method with the conventional numerical method, we find that the latter method fails to capture certain structures of
\pi(\chi_{\text{eff}},\chi_{\text{p}}|q)
, introducing numerical errors in the population inference. Furthermore, this analytical approach is faster and eliminates the stochastic uncertainty inherent in the numerical methods.
To investigate potential biases in the GWTC-3 analysis, we reanalyzed the GWTC-3 BBHs with our analytic formula of
\pi(\chi_{\text{eff}},\chi_{\text{p}}|q)
.

The structure of the paper is as follows: In Section

In section
III
, we re-analyze GWTC-3 BBH events with a model presented in
[
15

IV

II
Joint distribution of effective spins

In this section, we derive an analytic formula of
\pi(\chi_{\text{eff}},\chi_{\text{p}}|q)
and compare it with that computed with the conventional numerical method.
It is required to numerically compute the likelihood function of the population inference, which is given by

p(\left\{d_{i}\right\}|\Lambda)\propto\prod_{i=1}^{N_{\mathrm{ev}}}\frac{\mathcal{Z}_{i}(\Lambda)}{\alpha(\Lambda)}.

(3)

\mathcal{Z}_{i}(\Lambda)
represents the evidence for the
i
–th event, given by

\mathcal{Z}_{i}(\Lambda)=\int p_{\mathrm{pop}}(\theta_{i}|\Lambda)p(d_{i}|\theta_{i})\differential{\theta_i},

(4)

where
p_{\mathrm{pop}}(\theta|\Lambda)
represents a parameterized model distribution of
\theta
,
\theta_{i}
and
d_{i}
represent the source parameters and data for the
i
–th event respectively, and
p(d_{i}|\theta_{i})
represents the likelihood function.

\alpha(\Lambda)
represents the selection function, given by

\alpha(\Lambda)\coloneqq\int\differential\theta p_{\mathrm{det}}(\theta)p_{\mathrm{pop}}(\theta|\Lambda),

(5)

where
p_{\mathrm{det}}(\theta)
is the probability of detecting a GW signal with the source parameter values of
\theta
.

As explained in the previous section, those integrals are computed with Monte-Carlo methods.

\mathcal{Z}_{i}(\Lambda)
is computed with posterior samples from the parameter estimation of the
i
–th event,
\{\theta_{i}^{j}\}_{j=1}^{N^{i}_{\mathrm{samp}}}
:

\mathcal{Z}_{i}(\Lambda)\propto\frac{1}{N^{i}_{\mathrm{samp}}}\sum_{j=1}^{N^{i}_{\mathrm{samp}}}\frac{p_{\mathrm{pop}}(\theta_{i}^{j}|\Lambda)}{\pi_{\mathrm{PE}}(\theta_{i}^{j})},

(6)

where
\pi_{\mathrm{PE}}(\theta)
is the prior distribution assumed in the parameter estimation.

\alpha(\Lambda)
is computed with simulated signals injected into data.
They are analyzed by search pipelines, and the Monte-Carlo sum is evaluated over the subset of signals successfully recovered by the pipelines:

\alpha(\Lambda)=\frac{1}{N_{\mathrm{inj}}}\sum_{k:\mathrm{found}}\frac{p_{\mathrm{pop}}(\theta_{k}|\Lambda)}{p_{\mathrm{inj}}(\theta_{k})},

(7)

where
N_{\mathrm{inj}}
is the total number of injections,
p_{\mathrm{inj}}(\theta)
is the probability density from which the injection source parameters are drawn,
\theta_{k}
is the
k
–th recovered injection during the injection campaign.

In the LVK analyses, both
\pi_{\mathrm{PE}}(\theta)
and
p_{\mathrm{inj}}(\theta)
are isotropic in spin orientations and uniform in spin magnitudes.
Let
\bm{a}_{i}=(a_{i},\cos\vartheta_{i},\phi_{i})
, where
\phi_{i}
is the azimuthal angle of the
i
–th spin around the orbital angular momentum.
The isotropic and uniform-in-magnitude spin prior is given by

\pi(\bm{a}_{i})=\frac{1}{4\pi a_{\rm max}},

(8)

where
a_{\rm max}
denotes the maximum spin magnitude.
Here, we assume that the spin magnitudes of both compact objects share the same range, such that
0\leq a_{1},a_{2}\leq a_{\rm max}
.

In population inference which focuses exclusively on
\chi_{\text{eff}}
and
\chi_{\text{p}}
, the other spin components are marginalized over, resulting in the following expression for the joint prior:

\displaystyle\pi(\chi_{\text{eff}},\chi_{\text{p}}|q)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\differential{\bm{a}_1}\differential{\bm{a}_2}\pi(\bm{a}_{1})\pi(\bm{a}_{2})

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\delta\quantity(\Xeff-\frac{a_1\cos\vartheta_1+qa_2\cos\vartheta_2}{1+q})

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\delta\quantity(\Xp-\max\qty(a_1\sin\vartheta_1,\qty(\frac{3+4q}{4+3q})qa_2\sin\vartheta_2)).

(9)

In the GWTC-3 analysis, this integral was numerically computed using KDE.
We instead calculate this integral analytically to evaluate this joint prior more accurately.

II.1
KDE prior

We begin by reviewing the numerical method employed in the GWTC-3 analysis. The joint distribution is decomposed as

\pi(\chi_{\text{eff}},\chi_{\text{p}}|q)=\pi(\chi_{\text{p}}|\chi_{\text{eff}},q)\pi(\chi_{\text{eff}}|q).

(10)

The term
\pi(\chi_{\text{eff}}|q)
is evaluated with its analytical expression given by Eq. (10) in Ref.
[
39
]
, while
\pi(\chi_{\text{p}}|\chi_{\text{eff}},q)
is calculated numerically.

For the numerical evaluation of
\pi(\chi_{\text{p}}|\chi_{\text{eff}},q)
, KDE is applied to random samples drawn from the prior. Since this probability is conditioned by
\chi_{\text{eff}}
, the actual degree of freedom for this draw is three
1
1

1

azimuthal angles are marginalized
. Samples of
a_{1},a_{2}
, and
\cos\vartheta_{2}
are drawn from the uniform priors, and the values of
\cos\vartheta_{1}
are determined by Eq. (
1
) Unphysical samples with
\absolutevalue{\cos\vartheta_1}\geq 1
are rejected. This procedure is repeated until a sufficient number, 10,000 by default, of samples are obtained. Once a sufficient number of samples is obtained,
\chi_{\text{p}}
samples are generated according to (
2
), and KDE with the Gaussian kernel is applied to them. Due to the Jacobian of the transformation to
\chi_{\text{eff}}
from
\cos\vartheta_{1}
, each sample has the weight,

\displaystyle\partialderivative{\cos\vartheta_1(a_1,a_2,\Xeff,\cos\vartheta_2)}{\Xeff}=\frac{1+q}{a_{1}}.

(11)

The boundary conditions must be set carefully because KDE, in general, tends to have biases near the boundaries
[
41
]
. In the numerical evaluation of
\pi(\chi_{\text{p}}|\chi_{\text{eff}},q),
the boundary condition is
\pi(0|\chi_{\text{eff}},q)=\pi(\chi_{\mathrm{p,max}}|\chi_{\text{eff}},q)=0.
Here,
\chi_{\mathrm{p,max}}
is the physically maximum value for
\chi_{\text{p}}
given
\chi_{\text{eff}}
and
q
:

\displaystyle\chi_{\mathrm{p,max}}\coloneqq\max_{q,\chi_{\text{eff}}}(\chi_{\text{p}})=

\displaystyle\begin{cases}a_{\mathrm{max}}&\frac{\absolutevalue{\Xeff}}{a_{\mathrm{max}}}\leq\frac{q}{1+q}\\
\sqrt{a_{\mathrm{max}}^{2}-\quantity((1+q)\abs{\Xeff}-a_\mathrm{max}q)^{2}}&\frac{\absolutevalue{\Xeff}}{a_{\mathrm{max}}}>\frac{q}{1+q}.\end{cases}

(12)

To prevent the density from spilling out of
[0,\chi_{\mathrm{p,max}}]
, KDE for grids in
[\varepsilon\chi_{\mathrm{p,max}},(1-\varepsilon)\chi_{\mathrm{p,max}}]
are evaluated first, where
\varepsilon
is a small constant;
\varepsilon=0.02
is chosen in the GWTC-3 inference. Then the boundary conditions are manually provided; other values are calculated by interpolation. Finally, the function is normalized to achieve

\int_{0}^{\chi_{\mathrm{p,max}}}\pi(\chi_{\text{p}}|\chi_{\text{eff}},q)=1.

(13)

An implementation of this method can be found at
[
42
]
, though with a different choice of
\varepsilon
.

\pi(\chi_{\text{p}}|\chi_{\text{eff}},q)
as constructed above will henceforth be referred to as “the KDE prior” in this paper.

This numerical method has several limitations. First, the use of KDE may obscure certain structures in the prior distribution.
For example, the KDE prior may not capture the discontinuous derivative behavior due to the max function in equation (
9
).
Second, this method can be computationally expensive when dealing with a large number of events.
In particular, this method is slow if we give large
|\chi_{\text{eff}}|
because many random samples are rejected during the sampling process.
Third, this method introduces an additional source of uncertainty to the final results from the probabilistic nature of KDE. Because KDE relies on random samples, the results have statistical errors, but this is not an inherent property of the prior distribution.

II.2
Analytical prior

To overcome the limitations of the KDE prior, here we present an analytical joint prior.
The difficulty to compute equation (
9
) arises from the large number of conditional branches. In Ref.
[
39
]
, the author first specifies the branching cases and then provides primitive functions. However, the conditional expressions to be considered for computing
\pi(\chi_{\text{eff}},\chi_{\text{p}}|q)
are complex depending on
q,\chi_{\text{eff}},
and
\chi_{\text{p}}
. Furthermore, the number of cases to be considered is much larger than
\pi(\chi_{\text{eff}}|q)
, and it is not easy to consider in advance how many cases will be needed.

Instead, we adopt an approach that we first identify the primitive function of the integral and then assign the appropriate integral domain. To achieve this procedure, we give the integral domain
a_{i}\in(0,1)
and
\vartheta_{i}\in[0,\pi]
by a step function:

\Theta(x)=\begin{cases}1&x>0\\
0&x<0.\end{cases}

(14)

The following scaling relation with respect to
a_{\mathrm{max}}
holds:

\pi\left(\left.\chi_{\text{eff}},\chi_{\text{p}}\right|q,a_{\mathrm{max}}=a\right)=\frac{1}{a^{2}}\pi\left(\left.\frac{\chi_{\text{eff}}}{a},\frac{\chi_{\text{p}}}{a}\right|q,a_{\mathrm{max}}=1\right).

(15)

Thus, without loss of generality, we assume
a_{\mathrm{max}}=1
hereafter.
By the simple transformation of the integral variables
z_{i}\coloneqq a_{i}\cos\vartheta_{i}
and
x_{i}\coloneqq a_{i}\sin\vartheta_{i}
, we get

\displaystyle\pi\left(\left.\chi_{\text{eff}},\chi_{\text{p}}\right|q\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\int\differential{z_1}\int\differential{z_2}\int\differential{x_1}\int\differential{x_2}\frac{x_{1}}{x_{1}^{2}+z_{1}^{2}}\frac{x_{2}}{x_{2}^{2}+z_{2}^{2}}\Theta(x_{1})\Theta(x_{2})

\displaystyle\times\delta\quantity(\Xeff- \frac{z_1+qz_2}{1+q})\delta\quantity(\Xp-\max\qty(x_1,\frac{3+4q}{4+3q}qx_2))\Theta\quantity(1-x_1^2-z_1^2)\Theta\quantity(1-x_2^2-z_2^2).

(16)

The integral can be reduced to the sum of one-dimensional integrals as (see appendix
A
for details)

\pi(\chi_{\text{eff}},\chi_{\text{p}}|q)=I_{1}+I_{2}+I_{3}+I_{4},

(17)

where

\displaystyle I_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1+q}{8q}\Theta(\chi_{\text{p}})\Theta\quantity(q-\frac{4+3q}{3+4q}\Xp)\Theta(x_{1}^{\mathrm{max}}-x_{1}^{\mathrm{min}})

\displaystyle\times\quantity[F\qty(x_1^\mathrm{max}\left|(1+q)\Xeff,\Xp,\frac{4+3q}{3+4q}\Xp,q\right.)-F\qty(x_1^\mathrm{min}\left|(1+q)\Xeff,\Xp,\frac{4+3q}{3+4q}\Xp,q\right.)]

(18)

\displaystyle I_{2}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1+q}{8q}\Theta(\chi_{\text{p}})\Theta\quantity(1-\Xp)\Theta(x_{2}^{\mathrm{max}}-x_{2}^{\mathrm{min}})\quantity[F\qty(x_2^\mathrm{max}\left|(1+q)\Xeff,\Xp,0,q\right.)-F\qty(x_2^\mathrm{min}\left|(1+q)\Xeff,\Xp,0,q\right.)]

(19)

\displaystyle I_{3}

\displaystyle=\frac{1+q}{8q}\frac{4+3q}{3+4q}\Theta(\chi_{\text{p}})\Theta\quantity(q-\frac{4+3q}{3+4q}\Xp)\Theta(x_{3}^{\mathrm{max}}-x_{3}^{\mathrm{min}})

\displaystyle\times\quantity[F\qty(x_3^\mathrm{max}\left|(1+q)\Xeff,\frac{4+3q}{3+4q}\Xp,\Xp,1\right.)-F\qty(x_3^\mathrm{min}\left|(1+q)\Xeff,\frac{4+3q}{3+4q}\Xp,\Xp,1\right.)]

(20)

\displaystyle I_{4}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1+q}{8q}\frac{4+3q}{3+4q}\Theta(\chi_{\text{p}})\Theta\quantity(q-\frac{4+3q}{3+4q}\Xp)\Theta(x^{\mathrm{max}}_{4}-x^{\mathrm{min}}_{4})

\displaystyle\times\quantity[F\qty(x^\mathrm{max}_4\left|(1+q)\Xeff,\frac{4+3q}{3+4q}\Xp,0,1\right.)-F\qty(x^\mathrm{min}_4\left|(1+q)\Xeff,\frac{4+3q}{3+4q}\Xp,0,1\right.)]

(21)

and

\displaystyle\begin{cases}x_{1}^{\mathrm{max}}=\min\quantity(\sqrt{q^2-\qty(\frac{4+3q}{3+4q})^2\Xp^2},(1+q)\Xeff+\sqrt{1-\Xp^2})\\
x_{1}^{\mathrm{min}}=\max\quantity(-\sqrt{q^2-\qty(\frac{4+3q}{3+4q})^2\Xp^2},(1+q)\Xeff-\sqrt{1-\Xp^2})\end{cases}

(22)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}x_{2}^{\mathrm{max}}=\min\quantity(q,(1+q)\Xeff+\sqrt{1-\Xp^2})\\
x_{2}^{\mathrm{min}}=\max\quantity(-q,(1+q)\Xeff-\sqrt{1-\Xp^2})\end{cases}

(23)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}x_{3}^{\mathrm{max}}=\min\quantity(\sqrt{1-\Xp^2},(1+q)\Xeff+\sqrt{q^2-\qty(\frac{4+3q}{3+4q})^2\Xp^2})\\
x_{3}^{\mathrm{min}}=\max\quantity(-\sqrt{1-\Xp^2},(1+q)\Xeff-\sqrt{q^2-\qty(\frac{4+3q}{3+4q})^2\Xp^2})\end{cases}

(24)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}x_{4}^{\mathrm{max}}=\min\quantity(1,(1+q)\Xeff+\sqrt{q^2-\qty(\frac{4+3q}{3+4q})^2\Xp^2})\\
x_{4}^{\mathrm{min}}=\max\quantity(-1,(1+q)\Xeff-\sqrt{q^2-\qty(\frac{4+3q}{3+4q})^2\Xp^2}).\end{cases}

(25)

These expressions have roots whose contents can be negative, but they are greater than or equal to zero because of step functions.
The one-dimensional function
F(x|a,b,c,d)
is defined as

F(x|a,b,c,d)\coloneqq\int_{0}^{x}\differential{x}\frac{b}{(x-a)^{2}+b^{2}}\log(\frac{x^2+c^2}{d^2}),

(26)

and has an analytical form:

\displaystyle F(x|a,b,c,d)=G\left(\left.\frac{x}{b}\right|\frac{a}{b},\frac{c}{b}\right)+\log(\frac{b^2}{d^2})\quantity[\arctan(\frac{x-a}{b})+\arctan(\frac{a}{b})],

(27)

\displaystyle G(x|\alpha,\beta)=\mathrm{Im}[g(x|\alpha,\beta)+g(x|\alpha,-\beta)-g(0|\alpha,\beta)-g(0|\alpha,-\beta)],

(28)

\displaystyle g(x|\alpha,\beta)=\begin{cases}\log(x-\beta i)\log(\frac{\alpha-x+i}{\alpha+i- \beta i})+\Dilog\quantity(\frac{x-\beta i}{\alpha+i-\beta i})&|\beta|<1\\
\frac{1}{2}\quantity(\log(x-\alpha-i))^{2}+\Dilog\quantity(\frac{-\alpha}{x-\alpha-i})&\beta=1,\alpha\leq 0\\
\log(\alpha+i-\beta i)\log(\alpha-x+i)-\Dilog\quantity(\frac{\alpha-x+i}{\alpha+i-\beta i})&\mathrm{otherwise},\\
\end{cases}

(29)

\displaystyle\Dilog(z)=-\int_{0}^{z}\differential{z'}\frac{\log(1-z')}{z^{\prime}}.

(30)

The branching of
g(x|\alpha,\beta)
arises in order to avoid transcending branch cuts of complex logarithm function and the dilogarithm function denoted by
\Dilog(z)
, which are both on the real axis. The more detailed derivation of an analytical form of
F(x|a,b,c,d)
is given in Appendix
A
. These expressions provide the fully analytical formulae to compute
\pi(\chi_{\text{eff}},\chi_{\text{p}}|q).
We will refer to this approach as “the analytical prior”.

The analytical prior in two-dimensional plane is shown in Figure

. In both cases, the joint distributions are symmetric around
\chi_{\text{eff}}=0
. The two-dimensional distribution takes its supremum at the limit of
\chi_{\text{eff}}=0,\chi_{\text{p}}\to 0
.
The larger the value of

is, the slower the rise of
\pi(\chi_{\text{p}}|\chi_{\text{eff}},q)
at
\chi_{\text{p}}=0
is. The contours are nearly parallel to the
\chi_{\text{p}}=\mathrm{const.}
line near the
\chi_{\text{p}}=1
limit, suggesting the steep decline at the
\chi_{\text{p}}=1
boundary. In addition, the right panel (
q=0.5
) illustrates that the contours have cusps around
\chi_{\text{p}}\approx 0.4
, which is resulted from the behavior of the
\max
function.

\displaystyle\chi_{\text{p}}=

0.2\lesssim\chi_{\text{p}}\lesssim 0.7
through the normalization procedure (

This bias can be reduced by increasing the number of samples, though this comes at the expense of higher computational costs.
Such discrepancies are generally unavoidable for small values of
|\chi_{\text{eff}}|
and can have a substantial impact on population inference when the underlying distribution is concentrated in that parameter region.

IV
Conclusions

We derive an analytical expression for the joint prior distribution of effective spin parameters
\chi_{\text{eff}}
and
\chi_{\text{p}}
given isotropic and uniform-in-magnitude spins.
By comparing our analytical prior with the KDE prior, computed with the numerical approach employed in Refs
[
14
,
15
]
, we found that the latter approach causes inaccurate assessments of
\pi(\chi_{\text{eff}},\chi_{\text{p}}|q)
in a certain parameter region, especially in the area of
\absolutevalue{\Xeff}\lesssim 0.01
. With the analytical prior, we re-analyze the GWTC-3 BBHs reported by LVK and estimate their spin distribution by adopting the
Gaussian Spin Model
employed in the GWTC-3 analysis. The results are largely unchanged from those obtained with the KDE prior.
However, the errors in likelihood caused by using the KDE prior are non-negligible. Since they are expected to grow as the number of events increases, our analytical prior will play an essential role in future analyses incorporating new GW events.
While this paper only examines and discusses the application of the analytical joint prior to the
Gaussian Spin Model
, this prior distribution is widely required in hierarchical inference for models that incorporate the two spin parameters
\chi_{\text{eff}}
and
\chi_{\text{p}}
.
