1.
Introduction

A (finite, abstract)
simplicial complex
is a non-empty finite
set
\mathcal{S}
of finite sets that is closed under taking subsets,
meaning that for all
S^{\prime}\subseteq S\in\mathcal{S}
, we have
S^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}
.
According to this convention, the empty set
\varnothing
is an element of every
simplicial complex.
We call
V(\mathcal{S})=\bigcup_{S\in\mathcal{S}}S
the
vertex set

of
\mathcal{S}
and the members of
\mathcal{S}

edges
.
The
degree

d(x)
of a vertex
x\in V(\mathcal{S})
is the number of edges
containing
x
, that is,
d(x)=|\{e\in\mathcal{S}\colon x\in e\}|
.
The
minimum degree
of
\mathcal{S}
is defined
as
\delta(\mathcal{S})=\min\{d(x)\colon x\in V(\mathcal{S})\}
. The minimum
degree of the
trivial complex

\mathcal{S}=\{\varnothing\}
is
\delta(\mathcal{S})=\infty
.

For
d\in{\mathds{N}}_{0}
, let
\alpha(d)
be the largest real number such that
every simplicial complex
\mathcal{S}

with
|\mathcal{S}|\leq\alpha(d)|V(\mathcal{S})|

satisfies
\delta(\mathcal{S})\leq d
.
In other words,
\alpha(d)
is the largest constant for which the lower
bound
|\mathcal{S}|>\alpha(d)|V(\mathcal{S})|
can be inferred from
\delta(\mathcal{S})>d
.
(For the existence of this maximum we refer to Section
2
).
Currently, no explicit formula for
\alpha(d)
is in sight.
In this work, we determine
\alpha(2^{d}-m)
for all integers
d
and
m

with
d\geq m\geq 1
.
As we shall see, this means that we solve this problem for the first natural
regime of parameters.

Historically, the investigation of
\alpha(d)
began in the context of traces of
families of finite sets.
Frankl
[
1
]
reduced a certain problem on traces to the minimum
degree problem for simplicial complexes and obtained the first general result,
namely that for all
d\in{\mathds{N}}
,

(1.1)

\displaystyle\alpha(2^{d}-1)=\frac{2^{d+1}-1}{d+1}\,.

In addition, Frankl and Watanabe
[
7
]
proved that for all
d\in{\mathds{N}}
,

(1.2)

\displaystyle\alpha(2^{d}-2)=\frac{2^{d+1}-2}{d+1}

and
\alpha(2^{d})=\frac{2^{d+1}-1}{d+1}+\frac{1}{2}
.
Piga and Schülke
[
6
]
extended these results to values further away from
powers of two by showing that for all
d,c\in{\mathds{N}}
with
d\geq 4c
,
we have
\alpha(2^{d}-c)=\frac{2^{d+1}-c}{d+1}
.
We refer the reader to the introduction of
[
6
]
and the references therein
for further early results. Our main contribution is the following.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
d
and
m
be integers with
d\geq m\geq 1
.
Then
\alpha(2^{d}-m)=\frac{2^{d+1}-m}{d+1}
.

As we will see in Section
2
, this formula is not correct
for
m>d
. However, we illustrate that our methods can be used to push past this
natural barrier by showing the following result, which solves a conjecture by
Frankl and Watanabe
[
7
]
.

Theorem 1.2
.

We have
\alpha(11)=\frac{53}{10}
.

Overview

In Section
2
we establish the existence of
\alpha(d)
and discuss upper bound constructions.
The main part of this work concerns the lower bound:
Given a simplicial complex with a certain minimum degree, we need to show that
it has many edges.
Very roughly speaking, our proof follows the strategy developed in
[
6
]
,
which has a local and a global part.
Instead of counting the edges directly, it is convenient to instead consider weights
of vertices, which are terms similar to
\sum_{x\in F\in\mathcal{S}}\frac{1}{|F|}
.
The minimum degree condition gives some lower bound for these weights.
However, this lower bound is too small to finish directly.
For this, we group vertices together into “conglomerates” (which are similar to
“clusters” in
[
6
]
) with the intention that the average weight in each
conglomerate is sufficiently large.
To bound the average weight in each conglomerate, we need local results about
simplicial complexes on a constant number of vertices with a certain number of edges.

The improvements over
[
6
]
come from several places.
One is that the local considerations here are much more precise; we will discuss these
in Section
4
.
Another improvement comes from the fact that conglomerates are more flexible than
clusters—for instance, two conglomerates might intersect, albeit in at most one
vertex.
Further, we introduce an auxiliary function on the vertices and a more general setting
with potentially several simplicial complexes on the same vertex set.
Together, this allows us to perform an induction on the number of edges of one of the
simplicial complexes, which normally would disturb the minimum degree condition.
The global part of our argument is given in Section
5
.
There we prove a more general result (Theorem
5.1
) and deduce
Theorem
1.1
from it.
In Section
6
we prove Theorem
1.2
.
Section
3
serves as a collection of several tools, starting with inequalities, continuing with a weighted version of the Kruskal-Katona theorem, which is at the core of the local considerations in Section
4
, and culminating in a first illustration of our method (see Lemma
3.13
).

2.
Upper bounds

Let us first check that for every
d\in{\mathds{N}}_{0}
the maximum
\alpha(d)
does indeed exist. Given
d
we consider the set

M=\{\alpha\in\mathds{R}\colon\text{Every simplicial complex $\mathcal{S}$ with }|\mathcal{S}|\leq\alpha|V(\mathcal{S})|\text{ satisfies }\delta(\mathcal{S})\leq d\}\,,

which is bounded from above, as there are non-trivial complexes
\mathcal{S}

with
\delta(\mathcal{S})>d
.
Thus the supremum
\alpha_{\star}=\sup(M)
exists and it remains to
show
\alpha_{\star}\in M
.
Let
\mathcal{S}
be any simplicial complex
with
|\mathcal{S}|\leq\alpha_{\star}|V(\mathcal{S})|
,
take an isomorphic copy
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
of
\mathcal{S}

with
{V(\mathcal{S})\cap V(\mathcal{S}^{\prime})=\varnothing}
, and observe
that
\varnothing\in\mathcal{S}\cap\mathcal{S}^{\prime}

yields
|\mathcal{S}\cup\mathcal{S}^{\prime}|<\alpha_{\star}|V(\mathcal{S}\cup\mathcal{S}^{\prime})|
.
Hence, there is some
\alpha^{\prime}\in M
such
that
|\mathcal{S}\cup\mathcal{S}^{\prime}|\leq\alpha^{\prime}|V(\mathcal{S}\cup\mathcal{S}^{\prime})|
.
Since
\mathcal{S}\cup\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
is a simplicial complex,
the definition of
M
leads us to
\delta(\mathcal{S})=\delta(\mathcal{S}\cup\mathcal{S}^{\prime})\leq d
,
which proves
\alpha_{\star}\in M
.

It follows straight from the definition of
\alpha(d)
that every non-trivial simplicial
complex
\mathcal{S}
with
\delta(\mathcal{S})>d
gives rise to an upper
bound
\alpha(d)\leq|\mathcal{S}|/|V(\mathcal{S})|
. We can again improve upon this by working
with many disjoint copies of
\mathcal{S}
.

Lemma 2.1
.

If
d\in{\mathds{N}}_{0}
and
\mathcal{S}
denotes a non-trivial simplicial complex
with
\delta(\mathcal{S})>d
,
then

\alpha(d)\leq\frac{|\mathcal{S}|-1}{|V(\mathcal{S})|}\,.

Proof.

For any
n\in{\mathds{N}}
, let
\mathcal{S}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{S}_{n}
be pairwise
vertex-disjoint copies of
\mathcal{S}
.
Their union
\mathcal{S}_{\star}=\mathcal{S}_{1}\cup\dots\cup\mathcal{S}_{n}

is a simplicial complex with
\delta(\mathcal{S}_{\star})>d
, whence

\alpha(d)\leq\frac{|\mathcal{S}_{\star}|}{|V(\mathcal{S}_{\star})|}=\frac{n(|\mathcal{S}|-1)+1}{n|V(\mathcal{S})|}\,.

The result follows by letting
n
go to infinity.
∎

Now we can easily generate some (well-known) upper bounds on
\alpha(\cdot)
.

Construction 1
.

For
d\in{\mathds{N}}_{0}
, the power set
\mathcal{S}=\powerset([d+1])
is a simplicial
complex with
|\mathcal{S}|=2^{d+1}
,
|V(\mathcal{S})|=d+1
,
and
\delta(\mathcal{S})=2^{d}
.
Thus, Lemma
2.1

yields
\alpha(2^{d}-1)\leq\frac{2^{d+1}-1}{d+1}
.

This can be generalised by omitting some ‘large’ sets.

Construction 2
.

For integers
d
and
m
with
d\geq m\geq 2
, the set system

\mathcal{S}=\powerset([d+1])\smallsetminus\Big(\{[d+1]\}\cup\big\{[d+1]\smallsetminus\{i\}\colon i\in[m-2]\big\}\Big)

is a simplicial complex with
|\mathcal{S}|=2^{d+1}-m+1
,
|V(\mathcal{S})|=d+1
, and
\delta(\mathcal{S})=2^{d}-m+1
.
Using Lemma
2.1
,
we get
\alpha(2^{d}-m)\leq\frac{2^{d+1}-m}{d+1}
.

Construction 3
.

For
d\geq 2
, the simplicial complex

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}=

\displaystyle\{A\subseteq[d+1]\colon|A|\leq d-1\}

\displaystyle\cup\{A\subseteq[d+2,2d+2]\colon|A|\leq d-1\}

\displaystyle\cup\{\{1,d+2\},[2,d+1],[d+3,2d+2]\}

satisfies
|V(\mathcal{S})|=2(d+1)
,
|\mathcal{S}|=2(2^{d+1}-d-1)
,
and
\delta(\mathcal{S})=2^{d}-d
,
whence

(2.1)

\alpha(2^{d}-d-1)\leq\frac{2^{d+1}-d-3/2}{d+1}\,.

This means that for
m=d+1
, the formula in Theorem
1.1
does not hold.
Let us also note that we have already shown all upper bounds promised in
the introduction (as the case
d=4
of (
2.1
)
reads
\alpha(11)\leq\frac{53}{10}
), so that it remains to establish the
corresponding lower bounds in the remainder of this article.

3.
Preliminaries

3.1.
Inequalities

In this subsection we collect four estimates, which will be required later.

Fact 3.1
.

Given integers
d\geq 3
and
t\geq 1
, let
n_{1},\dots,n_{t}\in{\mathds{N}}_{0}

be such that
\sum_{\tau\in[t]}n_{\tau}\leq d
.
If
\sum_{\tau\in[t]}(2^{n_{\tau}}-1)\geq 2^{d}-d
,
then there is an index
\tau^{\star}\in[t]
such that
n_{\tau^{\star}}=d

and
n_{\tau}=0
for all
\tau\in[t]\smallsetminus\{\tau^{\star}\}
.

Proof.

For all
a,b\geq 0
, we have
(2^{a}-1)(2^{b}-1)\geq 0
and,
thereby,
(2^{a}-1)+(2^{b}-1)\leq 2^{a+b}-1
.
By an induction on the number of summands this yields

(2^{a_{1}}-1)+\dots+(2^{a_{m}}-1)\leq 2^{a_{1}+\dots+a_{m}}-1

for all
a_{1},\dots,a_{m}\geq 0
.

Now assume for the sake of contradiction that there is a
partition
[t]=A\mathbin{\mathchoice{\vtop{\halign{\hfil$\m@th\displaystyle#$\hfil\cr\cup\cr\cdot\crcr}}}{\vtop{\halign{\hfil$\m@th\textstyle#$\hfil\cr\cup\cr\cdot\crcr}}}{\vtop{\halign{\hfil$\m@th\scriptstyle#$\hfil\cr\cup\cr\cdot\crcr}}}{\vtop{\halign{\hfil$\m@th\scriptscriptstyle#$\hfil\cr\cup\cr\cdot\crcr}}}}B
such that
both sums
a=\sum_{i\in A}n_{i}
and
b=\sum_{i\in B}n_{i}
are positive integers.
Using the condition in the statement and the above observation, we get

\displaystyle 2^{d}-d

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i\in[t]}(2^{n_{i}}-1)\leq(2^{a}-1)+(2^{b}-1)

\displaystyle=2\big((2^{a-1}-1)+(2^{b-1}-1)\big)+2\leq 2(2^{a+b-2}-1)+2

\displaystyle=2^{a+b-1}\leq 2^{d-1}\,.

But this gives
d\geq 2^{d-1}
, contrary to
d\geq 3
.

We have thereby shown that there is some
\tau^{\star}\in[t]
with
n_{\tau}=0

for all
\tau\in[t]\smallsetminus\{\tau^{\star}\}
.
Finally,
2^{d-1}<2^{d}-d\leq 2^{n_{\tau^{\star}}}-1
yields
n_{\tau^{\star}}=d
.
∎

Fact 3.2
.

For all integers
d\geq 4
, we have

\frac{2^{d+1}-1}{2(d+1)}\geq\frac{1}{2}+\frac{2d-2}{3}+\frac{(d-2)(d-3)}{4}\,.

Proof.

First note that

2^{d+1}\geq 1+\binom{d+1}{1}+\binom{d+1}{2}+\binom{d+1}{3}+\binom{d+1}{4}

yields

\frac{2^{d+1}-1}{2(d+1)}\geq\frac{1}{2}+\frac{d}{4}+\frac{1}{6}\binom{d}{2}+\frac{1}{8}\binom{d}{3}\,.

Together with

\binom{d}{2}=\binom{d-2}{2}+2\binom{d-2}{1}+1

and

\binom{d}{3}=\binom{d-3}{3}+3\binom{d-3}{2}+3\binom{d-3}{1}+1\geq 3\binom{d-2}{2}+1

this leads to

\displaystyle\frac{2^{d+1}-1}{2(d+1)}\geq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}+\frac{d}{4}+\frac{1}{6}\binom{d-2}{2}+\frac{2d-3}{6}+\frac{3}{8}\binom{d-2}{2}+\frac{1}{8}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\binom{d-2}{2}+\frac{1}{24}\Big(\binom{d-2}{2}+14d-9\Big)\,.

Again using

\binom{d-2}{2}=\binom{d-4}{2}+2\binom{d-4}{1}+1\geq 2(d-4)+1=2d-7

leaves us with

\frac{2^{d+1}-1}{2(d+1)}\geq\frac{1}{2}+\frac{(d-2)(d-3)}{4}+\frac{2d-2}{3}\,.\qed

Fact 3.3
.

If
d
,
m
, and
s
are integers with
d\geq m\geq 3
and
m-2\geq s\geq 0
,
then the numbers
r^{\prime}
,
r
defined by

\displaystyle r^{\prime}=

\displaystyle\max\{a\in{\mathds{N}}_{0}\colon a\leq s\text{ and }2^{a}\leq m-1\}\,,

\displaystyle r=

\displaystyle\max\{a\in{\mathds{N}}_{0}\colon a\leq s+1\text{ and }2^{a}\leq m\}

satisfy

\frac{m-1-s}{d+1-r}-\frac{1}{2}\leq\frac{m-2-s}{d+1-r^{\prime}}

and

(s+1)\Big(\frac{s}{d}+\frac{m-2-s}{d+1-r^{\prime}}\Big)+(d-s)\Big(\frac{s+1}{d}+\frac{m-2-s}{d+1-r}-\frac{1}{2}\Big)\leq m-2\,.

Proof.

Setting
x=m-2-s
and
y=d+1-r
, we have
x\geq 0
and

y-x=(d-m)+(s+1-r)+2\geq 2\,.

This gives

\displaystyle\frac{x+1}{y}

\displaystyle=\frac{x}{y+1}+\frac{x}{y(y+1)}+\frac{1}{y}\leq\frac{x}{y+1}+\frac{x}{2y}+\frac{1}{y}

\displaystyle=\frac{x}{y+1}+\frac{x+2}{2y}\leq\frac{x}{y+1}+\frac{1}{2}\,,

and together with
r\leq r^{\prime}+1
we obtain

\frac{m-1-s}{d+1-r}-\frac{1}{2}\leq\frac{m-2-s}{d+2-r}\leq\frac{m-2-s}{d+1-r^{\prime}}\,,

which proves the first estimate.

Because of

\frac{(s+1)s}{d}+\frac{(d-s)(s+1)}{d}=s+1\,,

the second one reduces to

(m-2-s)\Big(\frac{s+1}{d+1-r^{\prime}}+\frac{d-s}{d+1-r}\Big)\leq m-s-3+\frac{d-s}{2}\,.

Due to
d\geq m
, the right side is at least
\frac{3}{2}(m-2-s)

and, thus, it is sufficient to show

(3.1)

\displaystyle\frac{s+1}{d+1-r^{\prime}}+\frac{d-s}{d+1-r}\leq\frac{3}{2}\,.

Suppose first that
(d,r)=(4,2)
.
Now
2^{r}\leq m\leq d
yields
m=4
, while
r\leq s+1
and
2^{r^{\prime}}\leq m-1

give
r^{\prime}\leq 1\leq s
.
Hence, we have indeed

\frac{s+1}{d+1-r^{\prime}}+\frac{d-s}{d+1-r}\leq\frac{s+1}{4}+\frac{4-s}{3}=\frac{19-s}{12}\leq\frac{18}{12}=\frac{3}{2}\,.

Now let
(d,r)\neq(4,2)
.
Clearly,
\frac{s+1}{d+1-r^{\prime}}+\frac{d-s}{d+1-r}\leq\frac{d+1}{d+1-r}
,
and so to prove (
3.1
) it is enough to
show
r\leq\frac{d+1}{3}
, i.e.,
d\geq 3r-1
.
If
r\neq 2
, then
d\geq 2^{r}\geq 3r-1
.
Otherwise,
r=2
holds, and so
d\neq 4
and
d\geq 2^{r}
show
d\geq 3r-1
.
∎

Corollary 3.4
.

Let
d
,
m
,
r
, and
s
be nonnegative integers. If
d\geq m\geq 3
,

m-1\geq s+1\geq r
, and
m\geq 2^{r}
, then

\frac{s+1}{d}+\frac{m-2-s}{d+1-r}-\frac{1}{2}\leq\frac{m-2}{d+1}\,.

Proof.

For reasons of monotonicity, we can assume that
r
is the number defined
in Fact
3.3
. In particular, we have
r\geq 1
and
the first estimate from that result yields

\frac{s+1}{d}+\frac{m-2-s}{d+1-r}-\frac{1}{2}\leq\frac{s}{d}+\frac{m-1-s}{d+1-r}-\frac{1}{2}\leq\frac{s}{d}+\frac{m-2-s}{d+1-r^{\prime}}\,,

for
r^{\prime}
defined as in Fact
3.3
.
Combined with the second part of Fact
3.3
this proves
our claim.
∎

3.2.
Variants of Katona’s theorem

We shall often need a weighted version of the classical Kruskal-Katona
theorem applicable to generalised complexes (see Lemma
3.8
below).
Let us first fix some notation.
For two finite subsets
A
,
B
of
{\mathds{N}}
, we write
A\strictif B
if

\max(A\,\triangle\,B)\in B
, or, equivalently, if

\sum_{i\in A}2^{i-1}<\sum_{i\in B}2^{i-1}
. The latter description of
\strictif

shows that the linear orders
\bigl(\bigcup_{m\geq 0}{\mathds{N}}^{(m)},\strictif\bigr)

and
({\mathds{N}},<)
are isomorphic.

Definition 3.5
.

Given
\ell\in{\mathds{N}}
and
M\subseteq{\mathds{N}}_{0}
, let
\mathcal{R}_{M}(\ell)

denote the set consisting of the first
\ell
elements
of
\bigl(\bigcup_{m\in M}{\mathds{N}}^{(m)},\strictif\bigr)
.
In the special case
M={\mathds{N}}_{0}
we drop the subscript and if
m\in{\mathds{N}}_{0}
,
we write
\mathcal{R}_{m}(\ell)
instead of
\mathcal{R}_{\{m\}}(\ell)
.

Next, for a collection
\mathcal{F}
of finite sets and
\ell\in{\mathds{N}}
,
the
\ell
-shadow of
\mathcal{F}
is defined by

\partial_{\ell}\mathcal{F}=\{F^{\prime}\subseteq F\colon F\in\mathcal{F}\text{ and }|F^{\prime}|=\ell\}\,.

Let us recall that Kruskal
[
4
]
and Katona
[
3
]
proved independently
that if
m\geq\ell
are nonnegative integers and
\mathcal{F}\subseteq{\mathds{N}}_{0}^{(m)}
is
finite, then
|\partial_{\ell}(\mathcal{F})|\geq|\partial_{\ell}(\mathcal{R}_{m}(|\mathcal{F}|))|
.
The following concept generalises simplicial complexes.

Definition 3.6
.

Given
M\subseteq{\mathds{N}}_{0}
, an
M
-complex
is a non-empty finite
collection of sets
\mathcal{K}
such that

(1)

|x|\in M
for all
x\in\mathcal{K}
,

(2)

and for all
x\subseteq y\in\mathcal{K}
with
|x|\in M
,
we have
x\in\mathcal{K}
.

For instance,
{\mathds{N}}_{0}
-complexes are the same as simplicial complexes.

Remark 3.7
.

If
M\subseteq{\mathds{N}}_{0}

and
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\bigcup_{m\in M}{\mathds{N}}^{(m)}
is an
M
-complex,
then, by the aforementioned Kruskal-Katona theorem, so
is
\bigcup_{m\in M}\mathcal{R}_{m}(|\mathcal{F}\cap{\mathds{N}}^{(m)}|)
.
Indeed, for any
\ell,m\in{\mathds{N}}
with
\ell\leq m
this result
yields
|\partial_{\ell}\bigl(\mathcal{R}_{m}(|\mathcal{F}\cap{\mathds{N}}^{(m)}|)\bigr)|\leq|\partial_{\ell}(\mathcal{F}\cap{\mathds{N}}^{(m)})|\leq|\mathcal{F}\cap{\mathds{N}}^{(\ell)}|
.
As
\partial_{\ell}\bigl(\mathcal{R}_{m}(|\mathcal{F}\cap{\mathds{N}}^{(m)}|)\bigr)
is an initial
segment of
({\mathds{N}}^{(\ell)},\strictif)
, it is thus a subset
of
\mathcal{R}_{\ell}(|\mathcal{F}\cap{\mathds{N}}^{(\ell)}|)
.

The following lemma is essentially due to Katona
[
2
]
, but we will prove it
for the sake of completeness.

Lemma 3.8
.

If
M\subseteq{\mathds{N}}_{0}
and
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\bigcup_{m\in M}{\mathds{N}}^{(m)}

is an
M
-complex, then for every family of reals
(w_{m})_{m\in M}

satisfying
w_{m}\geq w_{m^{\prime}}
whenever
m\leq m^{\prime}
, we have

(3.2)

\displaystyle\sum_{m\in M}w_{m}\big|\mathcal{F}\cap{\mathds{N}}^{(m)}\big|\geq\sum_{m\in M}w_{m}\big|\mathcal{R}_{M}(|\mathcal{F}|)\cap{\mathds{N}}^{(m)}\big|\,.

Proof.

By Remark
3.7
,
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}=\bigcup_{m\in M}\mathcal{R}_{m}(|\mathcal{F}\cap{\mathds{N}}^{(m)}|)

is an
M
-complex and, therefore, we can assume without loss of generality
that
\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
, i.e., that for every
m\in M
the set
\mathcal{F}\cap{\mathds{N}}^{(m)}

is an initial segment of
({\mathds{N}}^{(m)},\strictif)
.
Setting
f_{m}=|\mathcal{F}\cap{\mathds{N}}^{(m)}|

and
r_{m}=|\mathcal{R}_{M}(|\mathcal{F}|)\cap{\mathds{N}}^{(m)}|
, we of course have

(3.3)

\sum_{m\in M}f_{m}=\sum_{m\in M}r_{m}\,.

Hence, unless
f_{m}=r_{m}
holds for all
m
(in which case we would be done),
there has to be some maximal
m^{\star}\in M
such
that
f_{m^{\star}}>r_{m^{\star}}
.

Claim 3.9
.

For all
m\in M
with
m\leq m^{\star}
, we have
f_{m}\geq r_{m}
.

Proof.

Since both
\mathcal{F}\cap{\mathds{N}}^{(m)}
and
\mathcal{R}_{M}(|\mathcal{F}|)\cap{\mathds{N}}^{(m)}

are initial segments of
({\mathds{N}}^{(m)},\strictif)
, it suffices to find
an element
y\in\mathcal{F}\cap{\mathds{N}}^{(m)}
such that for
the successor
y^{+}
of
y
in
({\mathds{N}}^{(m)},\strictif)
,
we have
y^{+}\not\in\mathcal{R}_{M}(|\mathcal{F}|)
.

To this end, let
x=\{x_{1},\dots,x_{m^{\star}}\}\in(\mathcal{F}\cap{\mathds{N}}^{(m^{\star})})\smallsetminus\mathcal{R}_{M}(|\mathcal{F}|)
, where
x_{1}<\dots<x_{m^{\star}}
, and consider
y=\{x_{m^{\star}-m+1},\dots,x_{m^{\star}}\}
.
Since
\mathcal{F}
is an
M
-complex, we know that
y\in\mathcal{F}\cap{\mathds{N}}^{(m)}
.
Let
y^{+}
be the successor of
y
in
({\mathds{N}}^{(m)},\strictif)
.
Then
\max(y^{+}\triangle\,x)=\max(y^{+}\triangle\,y)\in y^{+}
and,
thus,
x\strictif y^{+}
.
Since
\mathcal{R}_{M}(|\mathcal{F}|)
is an initial segment
of
(\bigcup_{\mu\in M}{\mathds{N}}^{(\mu)},\strictif)
and
x\notin\mathcal{R}_{M}(|\mathcal{F}|)
,
this entails
y^{+}\notin\mathcal{R}_{M}(|\mathcal{F}|)
, and so
y
is as desired.
∎

The claim and the maximality of
m^{\star}
mean that we have
f_{m}\geq r_{m}

for all
m\in M
with
m\leq m^{\star}
, while
f_{m}\leq r_{m}
for all
m\in M

with
m>m^{\star}
.
So when we rewrite (
3.3
) in the form

\sum_{m\in M\cap[0,m^{\star}]}(f_{m}-r_{m})=\sum_{m\in M\cap(m^{\star},\infty)}(r_{m}-f_{m})\,,

then both sides involve nonnegative summands only.
Owing to the monotonicity of
(w_{m})_{m\in M}
we can conclude

\sum_{m\in M\cap[0,m^{\star}]}w_{m}(f_{m}-r_{m})\geq\sum_{m\in M\cap(m^{\star},\infty)}w_{m}(r_{m}-f_{m})\,,

which implies the desired estimate
\sum_{m\in M}w_{m}f_{m}\geq\sum_{m\in M}w_{m}r_{m}
.
∎

To explain how this lemma is going to be utilised, we recapitulate an
elegant argument of Frankl
[
1
]
, which provides a lower bound
on
\alpha(m)
in terms of the function

\beta(m)=\sum_{F\in\mathcal{R}(m+1)}\frac{1}{|F|+1}\,.

Lemma 3.10

(Frankl)
.

For every
m\in{\mathds{N}}_{0}
, we have
\alpha(m)\geq\beta(m)
.

Proof.

Given a simplicial complex
\mathcal{S}
with
\delta(\mathcal{S})\geq m+1
, we need to
show
|\mathcal{S}|>\beta(m)|V|
, where
V=V(\mathcal{S})
.
To this end, we consider for every vertex
x\in V
the
sum
q(x)=\sum_{x\in F\in\mathcal{S}}\frac{1}{|F|}
.
Lemma
3.8
applied to
M={\mathds{N}}_{0}
, the simplicial complex

\mathcal{A}=\{F\smallsetminus\{x\}\colon x\in F\in\mathcal{S}\}\,,

and the weights
w_{j}=\frac{1}{j+1}
,
discloses
q(x)=\sum_{F\in\mathcal{A}}\frac{1}{|A|+1}\geq\beta(|\mathcal{A}|-1)\geq\beta(m)

for every
x\in V
.
By double counting this leads to

|\mathcal{S}|=1+\sum_{x\in V}q(x)>\beta(m)|V|\,.\qed

Example 3.11
.

For any nonnegative integer
d
, we have
\mathcal{R}(2^{d})=\powerset([d])
, whence

\beta(2^{d}-1)=\sum_{i=0}^{d}\binom{d}{i}\frac{1}{i+1}=\frac{2^{d+1}-1}{d+1}\,.

Using Construction
1
and the previous lemma,
we obtain
\alpha(2^{d}-1)=\frac{2^{d+1}-1}{d+1}
.

Example 3.12
.

For every positive integer
d
,
we have
\mathcal{R}(2^{d}-1)=\powerset([d])\smallsetminus\{[d]\}
and

\beta(2^{d}-2)=\beta(2^{d}-1)-\frac{1}{d+1}=\frac{2^{d+1}-2}{d+1}\,.

In view of Construction
2
and Lemma
3.10
,
this yields
\alpha(2^{d}-2)=\frac{2^{d+1}-2}{d+1}
.

3.3.
A special case

We now turn to an illustration of our method and establish a slight
generalisation of the well-known lower bound
\alpha(5)\geq\frac{13}{4}
,
which corresponds to the case
(d,m)=(3,3)
of Theorem
1.1
.
For the sake of comparison, we point out that a direct application of
Lemma
3.10
only gives the inferior bound
\alpha(5)\geq\frac{19}{6}
,
so that some additional averaging argument is needed.
An analogue of the following statement addressing the case
d\geq 4
of
Theorem
1.1
will be formulated
as Theorem
5.1
below.

Lemma 3.13
.

Let
\mathcal{S}
be a simplicial complex with vertex set
V(\mathcal{S})=V

and let
f\colon V\longrightarrow{\mathds{N}}_{0}
be a map.
If for all
x\in V
, we have
f(x)+d(x)\geq 6
, then

\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x\in V}f(x)+|\mathcal{S}|>\frac{13}{4}|V|\,.

Proof.

Assume that the assertion does not hold, and for a fixed vertex set
V

let
(\mathcal{S},f)
be a counterexample which minimises
|\mathcal{S}|
.
We commence by observing that
\mathcal{S}
cannot contain sets with more
than
3
elements.
Indeed, if
\mathcal{S}
would contain such sets, let
F\in\mathcal{S}

with
|F|=m\geq 4
be maximal.
By deleting
F
from
\mathcal{S}
, we obtain a simplicial complex
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}

such that
f(x)+d_{\mathcal{S}^{\prime}}(x)\geq 6
for all
x\in V\smallsetminus F

and
d_{\mathcal{S}^{\prime}}(x)\geq 2^{m-1}-1>6
for all
x\in F
.
Hence,
(\mathcal{S}^{\prime},f)
would be a counterexample
with
|\mathcal{S}^{\prime}|<|\mathcal{S}|
, a contradiction.

Similarly, one can argue that

(3.4)

\text{ if a vertex $x$ belongs to a $3$-set of\penalty\ $\mathcal{S}$, then $d(x)\leq 6$}.

Assume contrariwise that
d(x)\geq 7
but there is some
xyz\in\mathcal{S}
.
Let
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}=\mathcal{S}\smallsetminus\{xyz\}
and define
f^{\prime}\colon V\longrightarrow{\mathds{N}}_{0}

by setting
f^{\prime}(y)=f(y)+1
,
f^{\prime}(z)=f(z)+1
, and
f^{\prime}(v)=f(v)
for
all
v\in V\smallsetminus\{y,z\}
. Clearly, we then still
have
f^{\prime}(v)+d_{\mathcal{S}^{\prime}}(v)\geq 6
for all
v\in V
and our minimal choice of
\mathcal{S}

entails

\frac{13}{4}|V|<\frac{1}{2}\sum_{v\in V}f^{\prime}(v)+|\mathcal{S}^{\prime}|=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{v\in V}f(v)+|\mathcal{S}|\,,

which is absurd.

Having thus proved (
3.4
), we observe that any two
3
-sets in
\mathcal{S}

cannot intersect in a single vertex (because this vertex then had degree at
least
7
), and that no vertex can be in three or more
3
-sets of
\mathcal{S}
.

For
x\in V
, we
set
k(x)=\frac{1}{2}f(x)+\sum_{x\in F\in\mathcal{S}}\frac{1}{|F|}
.
Note that this is the ‘contribution of
x
’ to the term we want to find
a lower bound for, which can be rewritten as
1+\sum_{x\in V}k(x)
.

Define a
quartette
to be a quadruple
Q\in V^{(4)}
such
that
|Q^{(3)}\cap\mathcal{S}|\geq 2
.
We contend that every vertex
x
which is not contained in any quartette
satisfies
k(x)>\frac{13}{4}
.
Our discussion of triples in
\mathcal{S}

shows that
x
can belong to at most one triple.
Let us view
k(x)

as a sum of
f(x)
terms equal to
\frac{1}{2}
and
d(x)
terms of the
form
\frac{1}{|F|}
, so that there are altogether at least
6
summands.
Among them there are
1
, at least four equal to
\frac{1}{2}
, and a sixth one
that is at least
\frac{1}{3}
.
For this reason, we have indeed

k(x)\geq 1+\frac{4}{2}+\frac{1}{3}=\frac{10}{3}>\frac{13}{4}
.

Next we consider any quartette
Q
. Again by our discussion of triples in
\mathcal{S}
,
there are two vertices in
Q
, say
u
and
v
, which belong to exactly two
triples, while the two other ones, say
x
and
y
, are in exactly one triple.
Consequently,
Q
is disjoint to all other quartettes. Moreover, the argument
from the previous paragraph discloses
k(x),k(y)\geq\frac{10}{3}
.
Similarly, we obtain
k(u),k(v)\geq 1+\frac{3}{2}+\frac{2}{3}=\frac{19}{6}
, and a simple
addition leads to
\sum_{z\in Q}k(z)\geq 13
.

Let
\mathcal{Q}
be the set of all quartettes, and let
H
be the set of vertices
which are not contained in any quartette. Recalling that the quartettes are
disjoint, we obtain

\sum_{v\in V}k(v)=\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}}\sum_{v\in Q}k(v)+\sum_{v\in H}k(v)\geq 13|\mathcal{Q}|+\frac{13}{4}|H|=\frac{13}{4}|V|\,,

whence

\frac{1}{2}\sum_{v\in V}f(v)+|\mathcal{S}|=1+\sum_{v\in V}k(v)>\frac{13}{4}|V|\,.

But this contradicts the fact that
(\mathcal{S},f)
is a counterexample.
∎

Corollary 3.14
.

We have
\alpha(5)=\frac{13}{4}
.

Proof.

The upper bound
\alpha(5)\leq\frac{13}{4}
can be seen by plugging
d=m=3

into Construction
2
. Now suppose conversely that
\mathcal{S}

is a simplicial complex with
\delta(\mathcal{S})\geq 6
.
Lemma
3.13
, applied to
\mathcal{S}
and the constant
function
f\colon V(\mathcal{S})\longrightarrow\{0\}
,
yields the desired bound
|\mathcal{S}|>\frac{13}{4}|V|
.
∎

4.
Local lemmata

In order to adapt Frankl’s Lemma
3.10
to the situation considered in
Theorem
1.1
, we need lower bounds
on
\sum_{F\in\mathcal{A}}\frac{1}{|F|+1}
for simplicial complexes
\mathcal{A}

of size
|\mathcal{A}|\geq 2^{d}-m+1
, where
d\geq m\geq 3
are given. We commence with the case
that the ground set
V(\mathcal{A})
has size
d
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
d
,
m
,
s
be nonnegative integers such that
d\geq\max\{s,2\}

and
m\geq s+2
. Further, let
\mathcal{A}
be a simplicial complex
with
|V(\mathcal{A})|=d

and
|\mathcal{A}|\geq 2^{d}-m+1
. If
\mathcal{A}
contains exactly
d-s
sets
of size
d-1
, then

(4.1)

\displaystyle\sum_{F\in\mathcal{A}}\frac{1}{|F|+1}\geq\frac{2^{d+1}-2}{d+1}-\Big(\frac{s}{d}+\frac{m-2-s}{d+1-r}\Big)

holds for some nonnegative integer
r
such that
2^{r}\leq m-1
and
r\leq s
.

Proof.

We can assume that the vertex set
V=V(\mathcal{A})
is not in
\mathcal{A}
, because
removing
V
from
\mathcal{A}=\powerset(V)
would keep the assumptions valid
and decrease the left side of our estimate.

Now we consider the simplicial
complex
\mathcal{B}=\{F\subseteq V\colon|F|\leq d-2\text{ or }F\in\mathcal{A}\}
,
which arises from
\powerset(V)
by removing
V
itself and
s
sets of size
d-1
.
As in Example
3.12
we see that

\displaystyle\sum_{F\in\mathcal{B}}\frac{1}{|F|+1}=\frac{2^{d+1}-2}{d+1}-\frac{s}{d}\,.

So due to
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathcal{B}
, it remains to show
that
\sum_{F\in\mathcal{B}\smallsetminus\mathcal{A}}\frac{1}{|F|+1}\leq\frac{m-2-s}{d+1-r}

for some nonnegative integer
r
with
2^{r}\leq m-1
and
r\leq s
.

In the special case
\mathcal{B}=\mathcal{A}
, this is clear, so we can take a
set
A_{\star}\in\mathcal{B}\smallsetminus\mathcal{A}
such that
|A_{\star}|
is minimal.
Write
|A_{\star}|=d-r
with
r\geq 2
and notice
that
\sum_{F\in\mathcal{B}\smallsetminus\mathcal{A}}\frac{1}{|F|+1}\leq\frac{|\mathcal{B}\smallsetminus\mathcal{A}|}{d-r+1}
.
Further, one readily computes

(4.2)

\displaystyle|\mathcal{B}\smallsetminus\mathcal{A}|

\displaystyle=|\mathcal{B}|-|\mathcal{A}|\leq(2^{d}-s-1)-(2^{d}-m+1)=m-s-2\,.

Thus, we can finish the proof by showing
r\leq s
and
2^{r}\leq m-1
.
To this aim, we analyse the sets in
\powerset(V)
which must be missing
from
\mathcal{A}
because they are supersets of
A_{\star}
.
First, each of the
r
elements
x\in V\smallsetminus A_{\star}
gives
a
(d-1)
-set
V\smallsetminus\{x\}
containing
A_{\star}
; together with the
hypothesis
|V^{(d-1)}\smallsetminus\mathcal{A}|=s
this proves
r\leq s
.
The second part is shown by

2^{r}=\big|\{F\colon A_{\star}\subseteq F\subseteq V\}\big|\leq|\powerset(V)\smallsetminus\mathcal{A}|\leq m-1\,.\qed

The following lemma will later be used to argue that if a simplicial complex uses a ground set larger
than necessary, then its weight is sufficiently large.

Lemma 4.2
.

Given integers
d\geq m\geq 3
, let
\mathcal{A}
be a simplicial complex
with
|V(\mathcal{A})|\geq d+1
and
|\mathcal{A}|\geq 2^{d}-m+1
.

(

)

We have
\sum_{F\in\mathcal{A}}\frac{1}{|F|+1}\geq\frac{2^{d+1}-m}{d+1}
.

(

)

If
\mathcal{A}
contains exactly
d-s-1
sets of size
d-1

for some
s\in[0,m-2]
, then there is an integer
r

with
0\leq r\leq s+1
,
2^{r}\leq m
, and

\sum_{F\in\mathcal{A}}\frac{1}{|F|+1}\geq\frac{2^{d+1}-2}{d+1}-\Big(\frac{s+1}{d}+\frac{m-2-s}{d+1-r}-\frac{1}{2}\Big)\,.

Proof.

Arguing for fixed
d
,
m
by induction on
|\mathcal{A}|+|V(\mathcal{A})|
, we suppose that both
statements hold for all simplicial complexes
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
satisfying
|V(\mathcal{A}^{\prime})|\geq d+1
,

|\mathcal{A}^{\prime}|\geq 2^{d}-m+1
,
and
|\mathcal{A}^{\prime}|+|V(\mathcal{A}^{\prime})|<|\mathcal{A}|+|V(\mathcal{A})|
. If some set
F\in\mathcal{A}
has size at
least
d
, then
|\mathcal{A}|\geq 2^{d}
, so that we can simply delete a maximal such
set and apply induction. Therefore, we can assume
{|F|<d}
for all
F\in\mathcal{A}
.
Remark
3.7
allows us to suppose further, that

(4.3)

\mathcal{A}\cap{\mathds{N}}^{(\mu)}=\mathcal{R}_{\mu}(|\mathcal{A}\cap{\mathds{N}}^{(\mu)}|)

for all
\mu\in{\mathds{N}}
.

Define the integer
s\leq d-1
by
|\mathcal{A}\cap{\mathds{N}}^{(d-1)}|=d-s-1
. Let us first look
at the special case
s<0
, where
( )
‣
4.2
is not applicable and we only have
to deal with
( )
‣
4.2
. The case
\mu=d-1
of (
4.3
)
yields
([d]\smallsetminus\{i\})\in\mathcal{A}
for all
i\in[d]
,
whence
\powerset([d])\smallsetminus\{[d]\}\subseteq\mathcal{A}
. As we saw in Example
3.12
,
this entails
\sum_{F\in\mathcal{A}}\frac{1}{|F|+1}\geq\frac{2^{d+1}-2}{d+1}
, which is
stronger than necessary.

It remains to study the case
s\geq 0
, where (
4.3
) reveals

\mathcal{A}\cap{\mathds{N}}^{(d-1)}=\{[d]\smallsetminus\{i\}\colon s+2\leq i\leq d\}\,.

Moreover, when applied to
\mu=1
(
4.3
) tells
us
V(\mathcal{A})=[D]
for some
D>d
. Now we set

M=\{2,\dots,d-2\}\quad\text{ as well as }\quad\mathfrak{M}=\bigcup_{\mu\in M}{\mathds{N}}^{(\mu)}\,,

and try to ‘simplify’
\mathcal{A}\cap\mathfrak{M}
.
To this end, we set
\mathcal{T}=\mathcal{R}_{M}(|\mathcal{A}\cap\mathfrak{M}|)
and consider the set system

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}=\{F\in\mathcal{A}\colon|F|\in\{0,1,d-1\}\}\cup\mathcal{T}\,.

We contend that
\mathcal{B}
is a simplicial complex. Since
\powerset([D])\cap\mathfrak{M}

is an initial segment of
(\mathfrak{M},\strictif)
which contains
\mathcal{A}\cap\mathfrak{M}
, it
contains
\mathcal{T}
as well, and therefore we have
\mathcal{B}\subseteq\powerset([D])
.
Due to the choice of
\mathcal{T}
, it is only left to argue that for all
F^{\prime}\subseteq F\in\mathcal{A}\cap{\mathds{N}}^{(d-1)}
with
|F^{\prime}|\geq 2
, it follows that
F^{\prime}\in\mathcal{T}
.
For this, observe that also
\mathcal{Q}=\{F\in\powerset([d])\cap\mathfrak{M}\colon[s+2,d]\not\subseteq F\}

is an initial segment of
(\mathfrak{M},\strictif)
.
Thus,
\mathcal{Q}\subseteq\mathcal{A}
implies
\mathcal{Q}\subseteq\mathcal{T}
, which completes the proof that
\mathcal{B}
is a simplicial
complex.
As Lemma
3.8

tells us
\sum_{F\in\mathcal{A}}\frac{1}{|F|+1}\geq\sum_{F\in\mathcal{B}}\frac{1}{|F|+1}
,
we can henceforth assume that
\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{B}
.
The main gain of this transformation is
that now the set
\mathcal{T}=\mathcal{A}\cap\mathfrak{M}
satisfies the trichotomy

\mathcal{T}\subsetneq\powerset([d])\cap\mathfrak{M}\quad\text{ or }\quad\mathcal{T}=\powerset([d])\cap\mathfrak{M}\quad\text{ or }\quad\mathcal{T}\supsetneq\powerset([d])\cap\mathfrak{M}\,,

which due to
|\powerset([d])\cap\mathfrak{M}|=2^{d}-2d-2
suggests the following

First case:
|\mathcal{T}|\geq 2^{d}-2d-2
and
|\mathcal{T}|+D\geq 2^{d}-d
.

Assume first that
|\mathcal{A}|>2^{d}-m+1
. If
|\mathcal{T}|>2^{d}-2d-2
, then

\mathcal{T}\smallsetminus(\powerset([d])\cap\mathfrak{M})
is non-empty and we can simply
delete an inclusion-maximal element of this set and apply induction.
On the other hand, if
|\mathcal{T}|=2^{d}-2d-2
, or in other
words
\mathcal{T}=\powerset([d])\cap\mathfrak{M}
, then

, so that

and apply induction.

Thus it remains to consider the case
|\mathcal{A}|=2^{d}-m+1
. As

\displaystyle d-s-1

\displaystyle=|\mathcal{A}\cap{\mathds{N}}^{(d-1)}|=|\mathcal{A}|-(|\mathcal{T}|+D+1)

\displaystyle\leq(2^{d}-m+1)-(2^{d}-d+1)=d-m

yields
s\geq m-1
, part
( )
‣
4.2
is mute and we only need to

( )
‣
4.2
.

To this end, we observe that
\mathcal{E}=\{F\subseteq[d]\colon|F|\leq d-2\}

\sum_{F\in\mathcal{E}}\frac{1}{|F|+1}=\frac{2^{d+1}-2}{d+1}-1\,.

As the set system

contains

and

\sum_{F\in\mathcal{A}}\frac{1}{|F|+1}\geq\frac{2^{d+1}-2}{d+1}-1+\frac{1}{2}+\frac{d-m+1}{d+1}=\frac{2^{d+1}-m}{d+1}+\frac{1}{2}-\frac{2}{d+1}\,.

Because of

, this implies the desired estimate.

Second case:
|\mathcal{T}|\leq 2^{d}-2d-3
or
|\mathcal{T}|+D\leq 2^{d}-d-1
.

Suppose first that

. Now
|\mathcal{T}|\leq 2^{d}-2d-3
is surely true, which in turn implies
\mathcal{T}\subsetneq\powerset([d])\cap\mathfrak{M}
.
Thus, we can remove
the set
\{D\}
from
\mathcal{A}
and add an inclusion-minimal set from

with

.
Since
|\mathcal{C}|=|\mathcal{A}|
and

,
the induction hypothesis applies to
\mathcal{C}
, and due to
\sum_{F\in\mathcal{A}}\frac{1}{|F|+1}\geq\sum_{F\in\mathcal{C}}\frac{1}{|F|+1}
, we are done.

It remains to study the case

.
Now
|\mathcal{T}|\leq 2^{d}-2d-2
, whence
\mathcal{T}\subseteq\powerset([d])\cap\mathfrak{M}
and
|\mathcal{T}|+D\leq 2^{d}-d-1
. Due to

\displaystyle d-s-1

\displaystyle=|\mathcal{A}\cap{\mathds{N}}^{(d-1)}|=|\mathcal{A}|-(|\mathcal{T}|+D+1)

\displaystyle\geq(2^{d}-m+1)-(2^{d}-d)=d-m+1

we have

. Thus part
( )
‣
4.2
follows from Lemma
4.1

applied to the numbers

,

, and the simplicial

\mathcal{A}\smallsetminus\{\{d+1\}\}
here in place of

,

, and
\mathcal{A}
there.
Moreover, Corollary
3.4
yields part
( )
‣
4.2
.

On some occasions the following very weak estimate will suffice.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
d\geq 4
be an integer and let
\mathcal{A}
be a simplicial complex.
If
|\mathcal{A}|\geq 2^{d}-2d+2
, then

\sum_{F\in\mathcal{A}}\frac{1}{|F|+1}\geq\frac{2^{d+1}-1}{d+1}-\frac{2d-2}{3}\,.

Proof.

The case
M={\mathds{N}}_{0}
of Lemma
3.8
allows us to assume that

\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{R}(2^{d}-2d+2)
. As
d\geq 4
yields
2^{d}-2d+2\in(2^{d-1},2^{d})
,
we then have
V(\mathcal{A})=[d]
. It remains to
recall
\sum_{F\subseteq[d]}\frac{1}{|F|+1}=\frac{2^{d+1}-1}{d+1}

and to observe that each of the at most
2d-2
sets
in
\powerset([d])\smallsetminus\mathcal{A}
has size at least
2
.
∎

5.
Mountains

We call
{\mathds{N}}_{\geq 2}
-complexes (cf. Definition
3.6
)

.
The following result on mountains generalises Theorem
1.1
.

Theorem 5.1
.

Given integers
d
and
m
with
d\geq 4
and
d\geq m\geq 3
, let

•

V
be a set of vertices,

•

\mathfrak{B}_{1},\dots,\mathfrak{B}_{t}\subseteq\powerset(V)
be mountains,

•

and let
f\colon V\longrightarrow{\mathds{N}}_{0}
be a map.

If for all
x\in V
, we have

\displaystyle f(x)+\sum_{\tau=1}^{t}d_{\mathfrak{B}_{\tau}}(x)\geq 2^{d}-m\,,

and for all
F\in\bigcup_{\tau\in[2,t]}\mathfrak{B}_{\tau}
, we have
|F|<d
,
then

\displaystyle\sum_{\tau=1}^{t}|\mathfrak{B}_{\tau}|+\sum_{x\in V}\Big(\frac{f(x)}{2}+1\Big)\geq\frac{2^{d+1}-m}{d+1}|V|\,.

Let us first derive Theorem
1.1
from this result.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
assuming Theorem
5.1
.

Let integers
d\geq m\geq 1
be given.
Recall that Construction
2
provides the upper bound, so it
remains to show
\alpha(2^{d}-m)\geq\frac{2^{d+1}-m}{d+1}
.
Further note that due to Examples
3.11
and
3.12
,
we may assume
d\geq m\geq 3
, and because of Corollary
3.14
,
we can even restrict ourselves to
d\geq 4
.

Now let
\mathcal{S}
be a simplicial complex with
\delta(\mathcal{S})\geq 2^{d}-m+1
.
We are to prove the lower
bound
|\mathcal{S}|>\frac{2^{d+1}-m}{d+1}|V(\mathcal{S})|
.
Theorem
5.1
applied to

•

V=V(\mathcal{S})
,

•

t=1
and the mountain
\mathfrak{B}_{1}=\{F\in\mathcal{S}:|F|\geq 2\}
,

•

and the zero function
f\colon V\longrightarrow\{0\}

yields

|\mathfrak{B}_{1}|+|V|\geq\frac{2^{d+1}-m}{d+1}|V|\,.

Since
|\mathcal{S}|=|\mathfrak{B}_{1}|+|V|+1
, we are done.
∎

The rest of this section is dedicated to the proof of Theorem
5.1
.
Fix
d
,
m
, and
V
as there and assume that the theorem does not
hold. Choose a counterexample consisting of
mountains
\mathfrak{B}_{1},\dots,\mathfrak{B}_{t}\subseteq\powerset(V)
and a
map
f\colon V\longrightarrow{\mathds{N}}_{0}
such that
|\mathfrak{B}_{1}|
is minimal.
The argument which gave (
3.4
) generalises as follows.

Claim 5.2
.

If
M\in\mathfrak{B}_{1}
is inclusion-maximal, then there are at least three
vertices
x\in M
with
d_{\mathfrak{B}_{1}}(x)\leq 2^{d}-m
.
In particular,
|F|\leq d
for all
F\in\mathfrak{B}_{1}
.

Proof.

Let
M^{\prime}=\{x\in M\colon d_{\mathfrak{B}_{1}}(x)\leq 2^{d}-m\}
and
define
\mathfrak{B}_{1}^{\prime}=\mathfrak{B}_{1}\smallsetminus\{M\}
and

\displaystyle f^{\prime}(x)=\begin{cases}f(x)+1&\text{ if }x\in M^{\prime}\\
f(x)&\text{ if }x\in V\smallsetminus M^{\prime}\,.\end{cases}

Now
\mathfrak{B}_{1}^{\prime},\mathfrak{B}_{2},\dots,\mathfrak{B}_{t}
satisfy the conditions in the statement of
the theorem. Since we chose the counterexample such that
|\mathfrak{B}_{1}|
is
minimal and
|\mathfrak{B}_{1}^{\prime}|<|\mathfrak{B}_{1}|
, we infer that

\displaystyle\Big(\frac{|M^{\prime}|}{2}-1\Big)+\sum_{\tau=1}^{t}|\mathfrak{B}_{\tau}|+\sum_{x\in V}\Big(\frac{f(x)}{2}+1\Big)\geq\frac{2^{d+1}-m}{d+1}|V|\,.

Thus, if
|M^{\prime}|\leq 2
, then the desired inequality would hold
for
\mathfrak{B}_{1},\dots,\mathfrak{B}_{t}
and
f
, contradicting that these form a counterexample.
∎

For every
x\in V
, set

\displaystyle\mu(x)=f(x)+|\{(y,\tau)\in V\times[t]\colon xy\in\mathfrak{B}_{\tau}\}|

and

\displaystyle k(x)=1+\frac{f(x)}{2}+\sum_{\tau=1}^{t}\sum_{x\in F\in\mathfrak{B}_{\tau}}\frac{1}{|F|}\,.

The left side of the inequality we are dealing with rewrites as
\sum_{x\in V}k(x)
.
Following the basic strategy of §
3.3
, we analyse how the individual summands
k(x)
compare to the alleged average of
\frac{2^{d+1}-m}{d+1}
.

Claim 5.3
.

For
x\in V
, the following statements hold.

(1)

We have
\mu(x)\geq d
.

(2)

If
\mu(x)=d
, then

(a)

f(x)=d_{\mathfrak{B}_{2}}(x)=\dots=d_{\mathfrak{B}_{t}}(x)=0
,

d_{\mathfrak{B}_{1}}(x)\geq 2^{d}-m
,

(b)

and there are integers
r
and
s

with
0\leq r\leq s\leq m-2
and
2^{r}\leq m-1
such that

k(x)\geq\frac{2^{d+1}-2}{d+1}-\Big(\frac{s}{d}+\frac{m-2-s}{d+1-r}\Big)\geq\frac{2^{d+1}-m}{d+1}-\frac{(d-2)(d-3)}{4d}

and the number of
d
-sets in
\mathfrak{B}_{1}
containing
x
is exactly
d-s
.

(3)

If
\mu(x)\geq d+1
, then

(a)

k(x)\geq\frac{2^{d+1}-m}{d+1}
,

(b)

and if there is some
s\in[0,m-2]

such that the number of
d
-sets in
\mathfrak{B}_{1}
containing
x
is
exactly
d-s-1
, then there is an integer
r
with
0\leq r\leq s+1

and
2^{r}\leq m
satisfying

k(x)\geq\frac{2^{d+1}-2}{d+1}-\Big(\frac{s+1}{d}+\frac{m-2-s}{d+1-r}-\frac{1}{2}\Big)\,.

Proof.

We commence by defining vertex-disjoint simplicial
complexes
\mathcal{A}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{A}_{t+f(x)}
such that

•

\mathcal{A}_{\tau}\cong\{\varnothing\}\cup\{F\smallsetminus\{x\}\colon x\in F\in\mathfrak{B}_{\tau}\}
for
\tau\in[t]
,

•

\mathcal{A}_{t+i}\cong\powerset([1])
for
i\in[f(x)]
.

Then
\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{A}_{1}\cup\dots\cup\mathcal{A}_{t+f(x)}
is a simplicial complex
with
|V(\mathcal{A})|=\mu(x)
,

|\mathcal{A}|=1+f(x)+\sum_{\tau\in[t]}d_{\mathfrak{B}_{\tau}}(x)\geq 2^{d}-m+1\,,

and
\sum_{F\in\mathcal{A}}\frac{1}{|F|+1}=k(x)
.
Due to
|F|<d
for all
F\in\bigcup_{\tau\in[2,t]}\mathfrak{B}_{\tau}

and Claim
5.2
, we have

|\mathcal{A}\cap V^{(d-1)}|=|\{F\in\mathfrak{B}_{1}\cap V^{(d)}\colon x\in F\}|\quad\text{ and }\quad\mathcal{A}\cap V^{(d)}=\varnothing\,.

Therefore, Lemma
4.2
implies clause (
3
) and we can
assume
\mu(x)\leq d
throughout the remainder of the argument.

Set
n_{i}=|V(\mathcal{A}_{i})|
for
i\in[t+f(x)]
.
Owing to
\sum_{i\in[t+f(x)]}n_{i}=\mu(x)\leq d
and

\sum_{i\in[t+f(x)]}(2^{n_{i}}-1)\geq\sum_{i\in[t+f(x)]}(|\mathcal{A}_{i}|-1)=|\mathcal{A}|-1\geq 2^{d}-m\geq 2^{d}-d\,,

Fact
3.1
guarantees that there is an index
i\in[t+f(x)]
such
that
n_{i}=d
and
n_{j}=0
for all
j\in[t+f(x)]\smallsetminus\{i\}
.
This immediately implies
f(x)=0
and
\mu(x)=d
;
so (
1
) is proved and for (
2a
)
it suffices to show
i=1
, which is what we shall do next.

Define
s
such that

d-s=|\{F\in\mathfrak{B}_{i}\colon x\in F\text{ and }|F|=d\}|\,.

In other words,
d-s
is the number of
(d-1)
-sets in
\mathcal{A}
,
whence
d-s\leq\binom{|V(\mathcal{A})|}{d-1}=\binom{d}{d-1}=d
and
s\geq 0
.
Moreover,
\mathcal{A}\cap V^{(d)}=\varnothing
yields

2^{d}-m+1\leq|\mathcal{A}|\leq\sum_{j=0}^{d-2}\binom{d}{j}+(d-s)=2^{d}-s-1\,,

whence
m\geq s+2
and
d-s\geq d-m+2\geq 2
. In particular,
\mathfrak{B}_{i}
contains a
d
-set,
which proves
i=1
.

Furthermore, we have now shown that
\mathcal{A}
satisfies all the conditions to apply
Lemma
4.1
, which in turn implies (
2b
) apart from the
second inequality. To confirm this final part, we observe
that
2^{r}\leq m-1\leq d-1<2^{d-2}
gives
r\leq d-3
, wherefore

\displaystyle\frac{s}{d}+\frac{m-2-s}{d+1-r}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{s}{4}+\frac{m-2-s}{4}=\frac{m-2}{4}=\frac{m-2}{d+1}+\frac{(m-2)(d-3)}{4(d+1)}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{m-2}{d+1}+\frac{(d-2)(d-3)}{4d}\,.\qed

In particular, all ‘problematic vertices’
x
with
k(x)<\frac{2^{d+1}-m}{d+1}

satisfy
\mu(x)=d
. This allows us to study their ‘neighbourhoods’, which are
completely contained in
\mathfrak{B}_{1}
. We are thereby led to the notion of a
conglomerate, which can be thought of as an adaptation of quartettes
from §
3.3
to the present circumstances.

Definition 5.4
.

A
conglomerate
is a set
K\in V^{(d+1)}
for which there is a
vertex
x\in K
with

|\{A\colon x\in A\subseteq K\}\smallsetminus\mathfrak{B}_{1}|\leq m\,.

Roughly speaking, ‘most’ subsets of a conglomerate are in
\mathfrak{B}_{1}
. This can be made precise as follows.

Claim 5.5
.

If
K
denotes a conglomerate, then

(1)

|K^{(d)}\cap\mathfrak{B}_{1}|\geq d-m+2\geq 2
,

(2)

|\powerset(K)\smallsetminus\mathfrak{B}_{1}|\leq d+2m
,

(3)

for every
z\in K
,
we have
|\{A\colon z\in A\subseteq K\}\smallsetminus\mathfrak{B}_{1}|\leq 2m-1
,

(4)

and
K^{(2)}\subseteq\mathfrak{B}_{1}
.

Proof.

Pick
x\in K
such that setting
\mathcal{A}=\{A\colon x\in A\subseteq K\}

we have
|\mathcal{A}\smallsetminus\mathfrak{B}_{1}|\leq m
(which is possible by
Definition
5.4
).
Using that
\mathfrak{B}_{1}
does not contain any set of size
d+1
, we obtain

\displaystyle 2^{d}-m

\displaystyle\leq|\mathcal{A}\cap\mathfrak{B}_{1}|=\sum_{i=2}^{d}|\mathcal{A}\cap\mathfrak{B}_{1}\cap K^{(i)}|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=2}^{d-1}\binom{d}{i-1}+|\mathfrak{B}_{1}\cap K^{(d)}|

\displaystyle=2^{d}-d-2+|\mathfrak{B}_{1}\cap K^{(d)}|\,,

which proves (
1
).

Now we consider the decomposition

\displaystyle\powerset(K)\smallsetminus\mathfrak{B}_{1}=\{A\subseteq K\colon|A|\leq 1\}

\displaystyle\mathbin{\mathchoice{\vtop{\halign{\hfil$\m@th\displaystyle#$\hfil\cr\cup\cr\cdot\crcr}}}{\vtop{\halign{\hfil$\m@th\textstyle#$\hfil\cr\cup\cr\cdot\crcr}}}{\vtop{\halign{\hfil$\m@th\scriptstyle#$\hfil\cr\cup\cr\cdot\crcr}}}{\vtop{\halign{\hfil$\m@th\scriptscriptstyle#$\hfil\cr\cup\cr\cdot\crcr}}}}\{A\subseteq K\colon|A|\geq 2\text{ and }x\in A\not\in\mathfrak{B}_{1}\}

\displaystyle\mathbin{\mathchoice{\vtop{\halign{\hfil$\m@th\displaystyle#$\hfil\cr\cup\cr\cdot\crcr}}}{\vtop{\halign{\hfil$\m@th\textstyle#$\hfil\cr\cup\cr\cdot\crcr}}}{\vtop{\halign{\hfil$\m@th\scriptstyle#$\hfil\cr\cup\cr\cdot\crcr}}}{\vtop{\halign{\hfil$\m@th\scriptscriptstyle#$\hfil\cr\cup\cr\cdot\crcr}}}}\{A\subseteq K\colon|A|\geq 2\text{ and }x\notin A\not\in\mathfrak{B}_{1}\}\,.

The first set on the right side contains
d+2
elements, and the second one at
most
m-1
(by the choice of
x
). Moreover, since
\mathfrak{B}_{1}
is a mountain,
the map
A\longmapsto A\mathbin{\mathchoice{\vtop{\halign{\hfil$\m@th\displaystyle#$\hfil\cr\cup\cr\cdot\crcr}}}{\vtop{\halign{\hfil$\m@th\textstyle#$\hfil\cr\cup\cr\cdot\crcr}}}{\vtop{\halign{\hfil$\m@th\scriptstyle#$\hfil\cr\cup\cr\cdot\crcr}}}{\vtop{\halign{\hfil$\m@th\scriptscriptstyle#$\hfil\cr\cup\cr\cdot\crcr}}}}\{x\}
is an injection from the third set into

m-1
elements.
Combining these bounds we
infer
|\powerset(K)\smallsetminus\mathfrak{B}_{1}|\leq(d+2)+2(m-1)=d+2m
,
which proves (
2
).

Given any
z\in K
, among the at most
d+2m
sets in
\powerset(K)\smallsetminus\mathfrak{B}_{1}

there are at least
d+1
not containing
z
, namely
\varnothing
and the
d
sets
\{y\}
with

.
Thus, we indeed have
|\{A\colon z\in A\subseteq K\}\smallsetminus\mathfrak{B}_{1}|\leq(d+2m)-(d+1)=2m-1
,
which proves (
3
).

Finally, assume that contrary to (
4
) there is some
pair
yz\in K^{(2)}\smallsetminus\mathfrak{B}_{1}
. Since none of the
2^{d-1}
sets
A

with

can belong to
\mathfrak{B}_{1}
, we then obtain

|\{A\colon z\in A\subseteq K\}\smallsetminus\mathfrak{B}_{1}|\geq 2^{d-1}>2d-1\geq 2m-1\,,

which contradicts (
3
).
∎

Claim 5.6
.

If a vertex
x\in V
satisfies
\mu(x)=d
, then there is a unique conglomerate
containing
x
, namely the set
K=\{x\}\cup\{y\in V\colon xy\in\mathfrak{B}_{1}\}
.

Proof.

Claim
5.3
(
2a
) discloses
|K|=d+1
.
As every set
A\in\mathfrak{B}_{1}
with
x\in A
needs to be a subset of
K
,
we also
have
|\{A\colon x\in A\subseteq K\}\smallsetminus\mathfrak{B}_{1}|=2^{d}-d_{\mathfrak{B}_{1}}(x)\leq m
,
which proves that
K
is indeed a conglomerate.
The uniqueness of
K
is immediate from
Claim
5.5
(
4
).
∎

Claim 5.7
.

Let
K
be a conglomerate and
D\in\mathfrak{B}_{1}\cap V^{(d)}
. If
D\not\subseteq K
,
then
|D\smallsetminus K|\geq 3
and every
x\in D\cap K
satisfies

k(x)\geq\frac{2^{d+1}-1}{d+1}+\frac{1}{2}
.

Proof.

Assume for the sake of contradiction that
|D\smallsetminus K|\in\{1,2\}
.
Since
\mathfrak{B}_{1}
contains no sets of size
d+1
, we know that
D
is inclusion-maximal
in
\mathfrak{B}_{1}
and Claim
5.2
guarantees that some
x\in D\cap K

satisfies
d_{\mathfrak{B}_{1}}(x)\leq 2^{d}-m
. On the other hand,
Claim
5.5
(
3
) informs us
that there are at least
2^{d}-(2m-1)
sets in
\powerset(K)\cap\mathfrak{B}_{1}

containing
x
. Therefore, the set

Q=\{A\subseteq D\colon x\in A\text{ and }|A|\geq 2\text{ and }A\not\subseteq K\}

satisfies
|Q|\leq m-1
. But if
|D\smallsetminus K|=1
,
then
|Q|=2^{d-2}\geq d\geq m
, while
|D\smallsetminus K|=2

would imply
|Q|=3\cdot 2^{d-3}\geq 2^{d-2}\geq m
.
This contradiction concludes the proof of
|D\smallsetminus K|\geq 3
.

Now let
x\in D\cap K
be arbitrary.
Using again that Claim
5.5
(
3
) states

|\{A\colon x\in A\subseteq K\}\cap\mathfrak{B}_{1}|\geq 2^{d}-2m+1\,,

we learn that the simplicial complex

\mathcal{A}=\{\varnothing\}\cup\{A\smallsetminus\{x\}\colon x\in A\in(\mathfrak{B}_{1}\cap\powerset(K))\}

For these reasons, we have indeed

\displaystyle k(x)

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{A\in\mathcal{A}}\frac{1}{|A|+1}+\sum_{A\in\mathcal{A}^{\prime}}\frac{1}{|A|+1}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{2^{d+1}-1}{d+1}-\frac{2d-2}{3}+\frac{3}{2}+\frac{2d-5}{3}

Claim 5.8
.

If a conglomerate
K
is disjoint to all other conglomerates, then

\sum_{x\in K}k(x)\geq 2^{d+1}-m\,.

Proof.

Set

Q=\{x\in K\colon K\smallsetminus\{x\}\not\in\mathfrak{B}_{1}\}

x\in K\smallsetminus Q
satisfies
\mu(x)=d
.
Due to Claim
5.6
, we know
K=\{x\}\cup\{y\in V\colon xy\in\mathfrak{B}_{1}\}

and, therefore, Claim
5.3
(
2a
) tells us that at least

contain
x
. Further,
K\smallsetminus\{x\}\in\mathfrak{B}_{1}
and

\powerset(K)\cap\mathfrak{B}_{1}

.
We infer that

|\powerset(K)\cap\mathfrak{B}_{1}|\geq 2^{d+1}-d-m-1\,.

Since
k(z)\geq 1+\sum_{z\in F\in\powerset(K)\cap\mathfrak{B}_{1}}\frac{1}{|F|}

for every

, we have

\sum_{z\in K}k(z)\geq|K|+|\powerset(K)\cap\mathfrak{B}_{1}|\geq 2^{d+1}-m\,,

as desired. So from now on we may focus on the case that
\mu(x)\geq d+1

for all
x\in K\smallsetminus Q
.

Our claim is clear if
k(z)\geq\frac{2^{d+1}-m}{d+1}
holds for every
z\in K
, whence we may assume that
k(z)<\frac{2^{d+1}-m}{d+1}
holds for some
z\in K
.
Claim
5.3
(
3a
) yields
\mu(z)=d

and thus we have

. Summarising this paragraph,

(5.1)

there is some
z\in Q
with
\mu(z)=d
.

In particular,
Q
is non-empty. Now
Claim
5.5
(
1
) informs
us that the nonnegative integer
s

can be bounded by
s\leq(d+1)-(d-m+2)-1=m-2
.

Setting

\displaystyle r^{\prime}=

\displaystyle\max\{a\in{\mathds{N}}_{0}\colon a\leq s\text{ and }2^{a}\leq m-1\}\,,

\displaystyle r=

\displaystyle\max\{a\in{\mathds{N}}_{0}\colon a\leq s+1\text{ and }2^{a}\leq m\}\,,

we contend that

k(x)\geq\begin{cases}\frac{2^{d+1}-2}{d+1}-\big(\frac{s}{d}+\frac{m-2-s}{d+1-r^{\prime}}\big)&\text{ if }x\in Q\cr\frac{2^{d+1}-2}{d+1}-\big(\frac{s+1}{d}+\frac{m-2-s}{d+1-r}-\frac{1}{2}\big)&\text{ if }x\in K\smallsetminus Q.\end{cases}

-set
D\in\mathfrak{B}_{1}

satisfies

, then Claim
5.7

provides a stronger bound. So we may assume that such

Next, for
x\in K\smallsetminus Q
we have

by the above discussion.
Moreover, due to the definition of

, all but one of the

sets

contain
x
and, therefore,

(

) leads to the desired estimate.
If
x\in Q
and
\mu(x)=d
, we can argue

5.3
(
2b
)
instead.
Lastly, if
x\in Q
and

, then (

) gives

, and there are still

containing
x
.
By Claim

(

) applied to

, we obtain

k(x)\geq\frac{2^{d+1}-2}{d+1}-\Big(\frac{s}{d}+\frac{m-1-s}{d+1-r}-\frac{1}{2}\Big)\,.

Combined with the first part of Fact

this concludes the

).

Lastly, the second part of Fact

\displaystyle\sum_{x\in K}k(x)

\displaystyle\geq(2^{d+1}-2)-(s+1)\Big(\frac{s}{d}+\frac{m-2-s}{d+1-r^{\prime}}\Big)

\displaystyle\;\;\;\;\;-(d-s)\Big(\frac{s+1}{d}+\frac{m-2-s}{d+1-r}-\frac{1}{2}\Big)

\displaystyle\geq 2^{d+1}-m\,.\qed

If we could show that the conglomerates are mutually disjoint, then the proof
of Theorem
5.1
could easily be completed as in §
3.3
.
The route followed in the remaining pages of this section, however, is slightly
different. Specifically, we shall only show that distinct conglomerates cannot
intersect in two or more vertices (cf. Claim
5.11
below).
It will then turn out that a slightly more subtle version of our argument can
still be pushed through. Let us start with a special case.

Claim 5.9
.

If
d=4
