Primary cardiac tumors are rare with an estimated incidence of 200 cases per million people (0.02%) according to autopsy series.1,2 Approximately 75% are benign, of which myxomas are the most common.2 Whereas left atrial myxomas account for the vast majority of cases, right atrial myxomas comprise only 8% to 15% of myxoma presentations.2, 3, 4, 5 Clinical manifestations are often nonspecific and can mimic other cardiovascular conditions, including right-sided heart failure, tricuspid valve dysfunction, pulmonary embolism, or arrhythmias,6 or they may present asymptomatically as an incidental finding. Given the risk of obstruction, embolization, and cardiopulmonary collapse, prompt surgical excision remains the treatment of choice and is associated with excellent early and long-term outcomes.4, 5, 6, 7 We present a rare case of right atrial myxoma and highlight multimodality diagnostic imaging and surgical considerations. Written informed consent was provided by the patient.

History of presentation, past medical history

A timeline of events is presented in Table 1. A 62-year-old woman with hyperlipidemia, a 40 pack-year ex-smoking history, and previous myocardial infarction with drug-eluting stent to the left circumflex coronary artery underwent routine follow-up transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) with her cardiologist. The TTE demonstrated an incidental finding of a 20 × 17 mm heterogenous, globular, gelatinous-appearing, pedunculated right atrial mass attached to the interatrial septum and an otherwise unremarkable echocardiogram. At the time of this TTE, the patient was asymptomatic. Differential diagnoses

For an adult patient presenting with a right atrial mass, differential diagnoses include benign (eg, myxomas) and malignant tumors (eg, sarcomas and metastatic deposits), as well as thrombi and other pathological formations (eg, cysts). It is crucial to also consider potential complications relating to the right atrial mass, including hemodynamic obstruction, embolic events (eg, pulmonary embolus), valvular pathology (eg, tricuspid valve dysfunction), arrhythmias, and cardiac failure.

Further investigations

Over the next 3 months, the patient underwent diagnostic coronary angiography, which demonstrated a 70% mid left anterior descending artery stenosis (fractional flow reserve: 0.79), and she was referred to cardiac surgery for excision of right atrial mass and coronary artery bypass grafting. Intercurrently, she developed worsening exertional dyspnea and self-presented to the hospital emergency department. Baseline electrocardiography was normal with a sinus rhythm, heart rate of 60 beats/min, normal axis, and no conduction abnormalities or ischemic changes. There were no significant findings on clinical examination. Given the reported dyspnea symptoms, computed tomography (CT) pulmonary angiography was performed to rule out pulmonary embolism and lung pathology. This redemonstrated the right atrial mass, now measuring 23 × 25 × 25 mm, with mean CT attenuation of 68 Hounsfield units (HU) and no pulmonary emboli or lung pathology (Figure 1, Video 1). A repeat TTE (Figure 2, Video 2A and B) demonstrated that the right atrial mass had increased in size over the 3 months, from 20 × 17 mm to 25 × 22 mm. The mass remained attached to the interatrial septum and was mobile within the right atrial chamber with each cardiac contraction but not in proximity to the tricuspid valve. Biatrial and biventricular size were within normal limits, and biventricular function was preserved. Management, outcome, and follow-up

The patient underwent excision of the right atrial mass and coronary artery bypass grafting with in situ left internal thoracic artery to the left anterior descending artery. Preoperative transesophageal echocardiography confirmed the known right atrial findings (Figure 3, Video 3A and B). A median sternotomy was performed, and cardiopulmonary bypass was instituted via the distal ascending aorta and bicaval venous cannulation. After single aortic cross-clamp and cardioplegic arrest with antegrade blood cardioplegia, a transverse atriotomy was performed. The right atrial mass was identified with the pedicle attached to the fossa ovalis and no other attachments (Figure 4). The mass was carefully resected at the pedicle with a scalpel. The fossa ovalis was then repaired with bovine pericardial patch. Discussion

Primary cardiac tumors are rare, with myxomas representing the predominant histological subtype. Although the majority occur within the left atrium, right atrial myxomas account for 8% to 15% of cases.2, 3, 4, 5 Their rarity, coupled with the often subtle or nonspecific clinical manifestations, renders timely diagnosis challenging. The case presented here underscores several important aspects: the spectrum of clinical presentation, the role of multimodality imaging, operative management strategies, and late follow-up outcomes.

Clinical presentation and diagnostic challenges

The spectrum of clinical presentation for right atrial myxomas ranges from entirely asymptomatic to severe hemodynamic compromise. Obstruction of tricuspid valve inflow causing heart failure, pulmonary embolization, arrhythmias, and constitutional symptoms can all present.4,5 Echocardiography remains the gold standard for detection and evaluation. Complementary modalities such as CT and cardiac magnetic resonance imaging can further enhance assessment of morphology, mobility, and tissue characterization8 and assess for other differentials. The overlap with alternative diagnoses, such as thrombus, metastatic deposits, primary malignant cardiac tumors, or other benign cardiac tumors, adds to the diagnostic complexity. Indeed, compared to left heart masses, right-sided lesions demonstrate a higher prevalence for malignancies (eg, sarcomas, lymphomas) as well as metastatic involvement9 and should prompt suspicion, especially in the setting of salient clinical factors such as smoking and constitutional symptoms. In our patient, given the presence of new respiratory symptoms, a CT pulmonary angiogram was performed to exclude pulmonary embolism and other sinister thoracic lesions. This also provided characterization of the isolated right atrial mass, including size, morphology, relationship to adjacent structures, and attenuation by mean Hounsfield units. In a CT case series, the mean attenuation for atrial myxoma has been reported at 74 ± 46 HU,10 whereas it is usually lower for thrombus, at 50 to 70 HU. If there are clinical or imaging features that are suspicious for malignancy, further investigation with whole-body position emission tomography may be warranted.9,11 In our case, CT and echocardiography provided sufficient diagnostic clarity to guide operative planning, reaffirming their central role. Surgical excision remains the treatment of choice for cardiac myxomas, regardless of symptom burden, because of the potential for imminent and unexpected embolization, obstruction, and circulatory collapse. The operative course and recovery detailed in this report highlights the favorable risk-benefit profile of surgical management when performed in a timely and controlled setting.

Myxoma growth rate, histopathology, and recurrence risk

In the case presented, the approximately 20 × 17 mm right atrial mass was detected incidentally during routine TTE and had developed within a 29-month interval period since the last echocardiogram. The mass had then increased to 25 × 22 mm in 3 months' time. Reports with documented growth rate are rare, given that the finding of a cardiac myxoma usually necessitates prompt surgical excision to prevent embolic events. Growth rates for atrial myxomas in adults have previously been reported ranging from 0.1 to 5.5 mm per month, and rates based on area from 3.4 to 250 mm2 per month.12,13

Histopathological analysis remains essential for definitive diagnosis, typically demonstrating stellate myxoma cells embedded within a myxoid stroma. The risk of recurrence after complete resection is low, generally <5%, or about 0.5 cases per 1,000 person-years of follow-up,5,14 though higher rates are described in familial or syndromic contexts such as Carney complex.15 Long-term surveillance with periodic echocardiography is warranted, as late recurrences have been reported even after complete excision.7

Outcomes and prognosis

The long-term outlook after right atrial myxoma excision is excellent, with low perioperative morbidity and mortality and durable symptom resolution. Our patient achieved uneventful postoperative recovery and was free of residual tumor on follow-up echocardiography. This favorable trajectory aligns with contemporary series demonstrating operative mortality rates of 1% to 2% and long-term survival comparable to age-matched controls.5,6 Importantly, our case reaffirms that even incidentally detected, asymptomatic lesions warrant prompt surgical referral given the potential for rapid growth and sudden complications.

Broader implications and learning points

This case adds to the limited body of literature on right atrial myxomas, highlighting several practical lessons. First, incidental findings of intracardiac masses on routine imaging should be urgently referred, particularly when lesions are mobile or pedunculated. Second, multimodality imaging, though not always necessary, can be invaluable when clinical presentation is ambiguous. Third, surgical excision of right atrial myxomas can be performed safely with tailored operative planning. Finally, vigilance in postoperative and long-term surveillance is required to detect recurrence.

From a broader perspective, right atrial myxomas remind clinicians of the diagnostic breadth of cardiovascular practice. Presentations can masquerade as common conditions such as pulmonary embolism or heart failure, underscoring the importance of considering structural cardiac pathology in atypical cases. The rarity of these tumors also emphasizes the need for case reporting, as collective experience refines our understanding of their behavior and optimal management.

Conclusions

Right atrial myxomas are rare benign cardiac tumors that may present incidentally or with nonspecific symptoms as well as embolic or obstructive complications. Echocardiography remains the cornerstone of diagnosis, with surgery providing definitive treatment and excellent outcomes. Our case illustrates the importance of early recognition, multimodal imaging, exercising the exclusion of other potential differentials and complicating factors, careful operative technique, and structured follow-up. It further highlights that even rare entities can pose significant risks if not addressed promptly, reinforcing the principle of proactive management in cardiac care.
